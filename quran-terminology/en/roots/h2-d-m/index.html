Hadm: demolishing a building and dismantling an erected structure

1. Meaning in early Arabic

The principal use of hā–dāl–mīm concerns demolishing a building or dismantling a structure previously constructed and erected. هَدَمَ يَهْدِمُ هَدْمًا describes destroying a wall, house, or building. In this sense, hadm contrasts with bināʾ, building.

When a house is built, its parts are arranged to support the roof, walls, and other elements. Hadm disrupts that arrangement and removes its stability and function.

A wall of mud bricks ceases to stand when its bricks are pulled out and brought down. Some early Arabic linguists accordingly explain hadm through removing a building’s mud bricks.

Hadm therefore means more than moving an object: it changes an erected structure out of its former state.

It does not necessarily destroy all the building materials. A house may be demolished while its stones, bricks, and wood remain, perhaps to be reused elsewhere.

Arabic sources also record uses such as part of a well wall collapsing and its material falling inside. These belong to the same field of lost structural stability.

Historical background and early witnesses

Building demolition is clearly attested in the Quran, a text from the period of revelation. Early Arabic sources also define hadm in opposition to bināʾ.

For this precise meaning, the present study does not establish an independently datable pre-Islamic poetic witness. Certain attestation in pre-Islamic poetry should therefore not be claimed without independent evidence.

Another form in the family, hidm, does occur in poetry attributed to pre-Islamic poets.

A poetic witness from Aws ibn Ḥajar

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Aws ibn Ḥajar reads:

وَذَاتُ هِدْمٍ عَارٍ نَوَاشِرُهَا

wa-dhātu hidmin ʿārin nawāshiruhā

تُصْمِتُ بِالْمَاءِ تَوْلَبًا جَدِعًا

tuṣmitu bi-al-māʾi tawlaban jadiʿā

“A woman in a worn garment, her forearms bare, quiets a wasted child with water.”

Hidm means a worn, patched garment here. The poet portrays an impoverished woman whose clothing is old and who gives her child water to quiet him in her poverty.

Some transmissions attribute the verse to Bishr ibn Abī Khāzim instead. Both poets belong to the pre-Islamic period, but the attribution dispute and lack of an independent date for this verse must remain part of its assessment.

The witness shows that the family was not limited to demolishing buildings; certain forms also denoted worn objects.

A semantic resemblance can be seen between a garment wearing out and a building losing its structure, but resemblance alone does not prove one meaning developed from the other.

Two recognized branches must therefore be distinguished: hadm as dismantling an erected structure, and hidm as a worn garment.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important recorded forms include:

  • هَدَمَ: demolished a building.
  • يَهْدِمُ: demolishes.
  • هَدْم: demolition or collapse of a building.
  • هَدَّمَ: demolished; sometimes emphasizing the intensity or extent of destruction.
  • تَهْدِيم: demolition, the form-II verbal noun.
  • اِنْهَدَمَ: collapsed or was demolished.
  • اِنْهِدَام: collapse or destruction.
  • تَهَدَّمَ: collapsed or fell into ruin.
  • تَهَادَمَ: used of walls and buildings undergoing collapse.
  • مَهْدُوم: demolished.
  • هِدْم: an old, patched garment.
  • أَهْدَام: old garments, a recorded plural of hidm.

These forms do not all occur in the Quran. Its sole derivative is huddimat with an attached lām.

Hadama, haddama, and inhadama

Hadama is transitive: in ordinary use, an agent demolishes something.

For example:

هَدَمَ الْبَيْتَ means “he demolished the house.”

Haddama is form II. Lexicons sometimes explain its doubled consonant through extensive or intense destruction.

The form alone, however, does not require repeated action or a building being reduced entirely to fine particles. It allows such nuances, but the actual intensity and extent must follow from context.

Inhadama, by contrast, describes the thing that collapses.

For example, اِنْهَدَمَ الْجِدَارُ means “the wall collapsed.”

The sentence alone does not identify human attack, deterioration, an earthquake, or another cause.

The act of demolishing, the occurrence of collapse, and its cause must therefore be distinguished.

The semantic core

In the building-related branch, the core is:

Removing the integrity and stability of a building previously constructed and erected.

Three features stand out:

First, something has already been built and has a defined structure.

Second, an action or event damages that structure and brings it out of its standing state.

Third, all or part of the building’s former stability and function is lost.

This does not imply the absolute destruction of matter or specify the method of demolition.

Nor should the worn-garment branch be reduced to this definition without separate investigation.

3. Quranic usage: Al-Ḥajj 22:40

The root occurs once, as لَهُدِّمَتْ.

Al-Ḥajj 22:40

الَّذِينَ أُخْرِجُوا مِنْ دِيَارِهِمْ بِغَيْرِ حَقٍّ إِلَّا أَنْ يَقُولُوا رَبُّنَا اللَّهُ ۗ وَلَوْلَا دَفْعُ اللَّهِ النَّاسَ بَعْضَهُمْ بِبَعْضٍ لَهُدِّمَتْ صَوَامِعُ وَبِيَعٌ وَصَلَوَاتٌ وَمَسَاجِدُ يُذْكَرُ فِيهَا اسْمُ اللَّهِ كَثِيرًا ۗ وَلَيَنْصُرَنَّ اللَّهُ مَنْ يَنْصُرُهُ ۗ إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَقَوِيٌّ عَزِيزٌ

alladhīna ukhrijū min diyārihim bi-ghayri ḥaqqin illā an yaqūlū rabbunā allāhu wa-lawlā dafʿu allāhi al-nāsa baʿḍahum bi-baʿḍin la-huddimat ṣawāmiʿu wa-biyaʿun wa-ṣalawātun wa-masājidu yudhkaru fīhā ismu allāhi kathīran wa-la-yanṣuranna allāhu man yanṣuruhu inna allāha la-qawiyyun ʿazīz

Al-Ḥajj 22:40

“Those who were expelled from their homes without right, only for saying: ‘Our Lord is The God.’ Had The God not checked some people through others, monasteries, churches, synagogues, and mosques in which the name of The God is much remembered would have been demolished. The God will surely help those who help Him; The God is indeed Strong and Invincible.”

Hadm here concerns places of worship: buildings erected for worship and remembrance of The God.

The structure of la-huddimat

The expression has two parts:

La- is the lām introducing the result of the preceding lawlā construction, with emphatic force.

Huddimat is a third-person feminine singular passive perfect verb in form II.

An active construction presents an agent performing an action. A passive construction focuses on what happens to its subject without naming the agent within the verb.

Ṣawāmiʿ is the passive subject; biyaʿ, ṣalawāt, and masājid are coordinated with it.

The subject is therefore a range of places of worship threatened with demolition if checking and restraint were absent.

The feminine singular verb is compatible with that range, since Arabic allows feminine singular agreement with nonhuman plurals.

The passive does not imply the absence of a human agent. This part of the sentence highlights the demolition of places of worship without naming the prospective demolishers.

The role of lawlā

وَلَوْلَا دَفْعُ اللَّهِ النَّاسَ بَعْضَهُمْ بِبَعْضٍ is decisive for understanding la-huddimat.

Lawlā introduces an existing circumstance that prevents the stated consequence.

The meaning is:

Had The God not restrained some people through others, these places of worship would have been demolished.

La-huddimat thus does not simply report a particular past demolition; it states what would occur without checking and restraint.

“They were demolished,” detached from the preceding condition, would change the sentence’s meaning.

“Would have been demolished” following “had ... not” better conveys the Arabic construction.

Why are places of worship named differently?

The verse does not name mosques alone. It gives four distinct designations:

Ṣawāmiʿ concerns monasteries and places of worship and religious seclusion.

Biyaʿ denotes churches and Christian places of worship.

Ṣalawāt appears among names for worship sites and is rendered “synagogues” in the translation above.

Masājid names places of prostration and worship.

These terms are historically and linguistically distinct, but all belong to a sentence about preventing the destruction of places of worship.

The passage continues with يُذْكَرُ فِيهَا اسْمُ اللَّهِ كَثِيرًا.

Fīhā can refer to the entire set. Reference to the nearest term, masājid, has also been proposed. This study favors the inclusive reading in view of the enumeration of different worship sites, without treating the pronoun’s reference as undisputed.

Either way, hadm’s meaning remains unchanged: demolition of buildings and places erected for worship.

The relationship between hadm and dafʿ

The verse contrasts demolition with The God checking some people through others.

Dafʿ comes from dāl–fāʾ–ʿayn and here concerns restraining or countering a force capable of harming others.

Preventing demolition thus becomes connected with restraining aggressors.

The connection is clearer alongside Al-Ḥajj 22:39, where those subjected to fighting and injustice are granted permission to defend themselves.

Al-Ḥajj 22:40 describes people unjustly driven from their homes merely for declaring The God their Lord.

The passage therefore concerns violence and aggression against people and their worship sites, rather than permission to wage war simply because beliefs differ.

Lexical meaning must nevertheless be distinguished from contextual implications.

Hadm does not inherently mean religious persecution or aggression. Demolishing one’s dilapidated house to build a new one is also lexically hadm.

The sentence and surrounding verses connect demolition here with injustice and aggression.

4. Demolition of worship sites and protection of freedom to worship

Al-Ḥajj 22:40 places prevention of worship-site demolition alongside defending people persecuted for their faith and speech.

The connection shows how aggression against people and destruction of their worship sites can belong to the same oppressive conduct.

The verse does not discuss protecting only a mosque or a worship site belonging to Muhammad’s community; it lists places with different names and religious backgrounds.

This has a clear social implication: protecting places of worship should not be confined to followers of one religion or denomination.

Someone defending their own right to worship cannot commend destroying others’ worship sites merely because their beliefs differ.

This does not endorse every belief or practice that may occur within a worship site. Respect for people’s right to worship remains distinct from judging a belief’s truth.

Al-Baqarah 2:114 likewise describes those who prevent remembrance of The God’s name in mosques and strive for their ruin.

That verse places preventing remembrance alongside efforts to ruin the place of worship.

A building can be physically demolished, or deprived of its function by preventing people from attending and worshipping. Both belong to the broader discussion of destroying or disabling worship sites, but hadm in Al-Ḥajj 22:40 directly depicts demolition of the building.

For a fuller discussion, Forqan’s principle “Defending Churches, Synagogues, Mosques, and Other Places of Worship” addresses this verse.

5. Comparison with related roots

Hā–dāl–mīm and hā–dāl–dāl: demolition and severe breaking

The roots share their first two letters but differ in their final letter and range of use.

Hadm principally concerns demolishing a building and dismantling its erected structure.

Hadd concerns violently breaking and collapsing something solid.

In Maryam 19:90, haddan accompanies mountains falling and depicts their severe breaking and collapse.

In Al-Ḥajj 22:40, huddimat concerns monasteries, churches, synagogues, and mosques.

One occurrence emphasizes mountains collapsing; the other, erected buildings being demolished.

The Quranic contrast is clear, but the roots overlap in severe destruction. It would be wrong to claim that hadd never describes buildings or hadm never describes anything else.

Hā–dāl–mīm and khāʾ–rāʾ–bāʾ: demolition and ruin

Quranic khāʾ–rāʾ–bāʾ describes both a place’s ruin and the act of destroying it.

Al-Baqarah 2:114 mentions people striving for the ruin of mosques.

Al-Ḥashr 59:2 uses يُخْرِبُونَ بُيُوتَهُمْ for people destroying their houses.

Kharāb can describe a place, settlement, or building losing its inhabited, usable state.

A house might retain walls and a roof yet become a ruin after years of abandonment.

Hadm, by contrast, principally emphasizes dismantling and bringing down the structure.

Ruin and demolition may nevertheless coincide: a demolished building may also be ruined.

The difference is chiefly one of emphasis. Hadm focuses on structural demolition, while kharāb also denotes the resulting ruined or uninhabited condition.

Hā–dāl–mīm and nūn–qāf–ḍād: dismantling and undoing

Naqḍ concerns undoing, opening, and disrupting a joined structure.

The Quran uses the root for both material things and covenants.

Al-Kahf 18:77 describes a wall on the verge of collapse and then its being set upright. The common analysis assigns yanqaḍḍa to qāf–ḍād–ḍād, although another morphological analysis connects it with nūn–qāf–ḍād.

This verse should therefore not be presented without qualification as a secure witness for nūn–qāf–ḍād. A clear material example of naqḍ is undoing spun thread in Al-Naḥl 16:92.

In other uses, such as breaking a covenant, the meaning extends to violating a commitment and undoing a previously established bond.

Quranic hadm concerns building demolition, whereas naqḍ ranges more broadly over undoing material and nonmaterial structures.

This does not mean naqḍ always involves slow, piece-by-piece dismantling or hadm always sudden, violent destruction. Neither root alone invariably determines speed or method.

Hā–dāl–mīm and dāl–mīm–rāʾ: building demolition and extensive destruction

Quranic dāl–mīm–rāʾ describes severe destruction and devastation.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:137, for example, speaks of destroying what Pharaoh and his people had made and erected.

Tadmīr can encompass the loss of a society’s constructions, possessions, or power.

The Quranic use of hadm concerns demolition of built places.

This does not require tadmīr to destroy every member of a society or hadm to affect only part of a building. The object and context determine the extent in each case.

Hā–dāl–mīm and bāʾ–nūn–yāʾ: demolishing and building

Early Arabic definitions oppose hadm to bināʾ.

Bināʾ concerns constructing and erecting something; hadm dismantles the erected structure.

Arranging bricks into a wall produces a stable construction. Separating those bricks and bringing the wall down removes its former state.

The contrast clearly illustrates the root’s physical meaning.

Building is not always good, however, nor demolishing always bad.

Someone may build a safe home, or demolish a dangerous structure about to collapse in order to preserve life.

An act’s ethical value depends on its purpose, legitimacy, circumstances, and consequences. Hadm alone does not determine the final ethical judgment.

6. Challenges in understanding and translation

Does hadm mean absolute annihilation?

No. It concerns loss of an erected structure, not necessarily destruction of all matter.

A demolished house’s materials may remain.

La-huddimat in Al-Ḥajj 22:40 therefore does not require the places of worship to vanish without a trace.

“Would have been demolished” is sufficient and precise.

Does hadm always involve an intention to destroy?

In transitive hadama, an agent normally demolishes something.

Intransitive forms such as inhadama can simply describe a building collapsing without identifying its cause.

Quranic huddimat is passive and does not name the agent in the verb itself.

The verse’s context nevertheless concerns aggression and some people being checked through others. Demolition here therefore relates to social circumstances and resistance to aggression.

Does form II necessarily mean intense or repeated destruction?

Huddimat is form II, whose doubled consonant can in some uses accompany multiplicity or intensity.

That alone does not prove that every worship site was demolished repeatedly or that all would be utterly annihilated.

The verse names a plurality of worship sites. That breadth can suit the verb’s form, but specifying intensity or the number of events requires further evidence.

The base translation therefore need not replace “would have been demolished” with “would have been utterly annihilated” or “would have been demolished repeatedly.”

Does “would have been demolished” report a historical demolition?

No. In Al-Ḥajj 22:40, the verb occurs as the result of lawlā.

The sentence states that without The God checking some people through others, worship sites would have been demolished.

This differs from simply reporting a past event.

Quoting the verb independently should therefore not erase its condition and turn it into an unconditional report of a completed demolition.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Proposed equivalents include:

هَدَمَ: demolished or destroyed.

هَدْم: demolition of a building.

هَدَّمَ: demolished; where context supports it, demolished extensively or severely.

تَهْدِيم: demolition.

اِنْهَدَمَ: collapsed or was demolished.

تَهَدَّمَ: collapsed or fell into ruin.

مَهْدُوم: demolished.

هِدْم: an old, patched garment in that particular branch.

For the sole Quranic occurrence, la-huddimat, the most suitable equivalent within the sentence is:

“Would have been demolished.”

The translation given above for Al-Ḥajj 22:40 preserves this sense.

The Persian equivalent of “would have been destroyed” also conveys it, but the plainer wording for “would have been demolished” reads more naturally.

A more formal Persian equivalent is possible, but offers general readers no advantage over the straightforward rendering.

“Would have been utterly annihilated” introduces more than the word and sentence require.

8. Conclusion

The principal branch of hā–dāl–mīm concerns building demolition and dismantling an erected structure. Other Arabic forms describe collapse, a demolished building, and old, worn garments.

Quranic usage establishes building demolition in Arabic of the revelation period, while some poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period illustrates another branch, hidm as worn clothing. Without sufficient historical evidence, the branches should not be reduced to one certain semantic origin.

Only la-huddimat occurs in the Quran. It is a form-II passive verb within the result of a lawlā construction.

The passage concerns preventing demolition of monasteries, churches, synagogues, and mosques in connection with checking aggression and defending the oppressed.

The important principle of protecting worship sites of different religions follows from that context, but this ethical and social conclusion should not replace the root’s lexical meaning.

Final definition for Forqan:

In its principal branch, hā–dāl–mīm concerns demolishing and dismantling a building previously constructed and erected. It may remove the building’s stability and function without requiring the absolute destruction of its materials.

Its sole Quranic form, la-huddimat, means “would have been demolished,” in a context connecting prevention of worship-site destruction with restraining aggression and protecting the oppressed.
