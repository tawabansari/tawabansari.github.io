The Root N Q DH: Rescuing, Delivering, and Recovering

A study of naqdh in early Arabic and the Qur’an: from rescuing people and recovering property to deliverance from fire and drowning, and supposed deities’ inability to rescue others

The three-letter root N Q DH concerns escaping, rescuing another, or releasing someone or something from a predicament or another’s possession. A person saved from death, a horse recovered from an enemy, and an object released from someone else’s control can all be described through this family.

In familiar Arabic usage, anqadha means rescuing or delivering, istanqadha releasing or recovering, and naqīdh something rescued or retrieved from another’s possession.

The root is closely connected with rescue, but should not be confined to saving people from death. Its object may be preserving life, delivering someone from distress, or recovering property held by another.

The root occurs five times in five Qur’anic verses: four Form IV uses and one Form X. The passages concern deliverance from fire, supposed deities’ inability to save others, rescue from drowning, and recovering something taken by a fly.

All involve deliverance or recovery, but the rescuer, danger, predicament, and final outcome differ by verse.

1. Semantic Core and Forms

Important Arabic forms include:

  • نَقَذَ: To escape or be saved; in some uses, to rescue another.
  • نَقْذ: Safety or deliverance.
  • نَقَذ: Something recovered from another’s possession.
  • نَقِيذ: Rescued, delivered, or retrieved from another’s control.
  • نَقِيذَة: Another form for something recovered; in some early uses, a coat of armour.
  • نَقَائِذ: A plural of naqīdh or naqīdhah, including horses recovered from an enemy.
  • أَنْقَذَ: To rescue or deliver another.
  • نَقَّذَ: To deliver, close in meaning to anqadha.
  • تَنَقَّذَ: To release someone or something from distress or another’s control.
  • اِسْتَنْقَذَ: To undertake rescue or recovery; in familiar uses, to retrieve something from another.
  • مُنْقِذ: A rescuer or deliverer.
  • مُنْقَذ: Rescued or delivered.

Some Arabic dictionaries also attest al-anqadh as a name for the hedgehog. Placement in the same lexical entry does not establish a historical relationship with rescue.

Only two verbal formations from this family occur in the Qur’an:

Form IV anqadha, in fa-anqadhakum, yunqidhūni, yunqadhūna, and tunqidhu.

Form X istanqadha, in yastanqidhūhu.

The semantic core: release from distress or possession

One lexical analysis identifies the core as extracting or releasing something from a situation in which it is caught or held by another.

This fits the family’s principal uses.

When someone at risk of drowning is rescued, they are released from danger.

When an enemy has captured a horse and its owner recovers it, the animal is taken out of the other party’s control.

Both involve release from a prior condition, but the difference matters: the first saves a life, while the second retrieves something from another’s possession.

Deliverance or recovery from a situation in which someone or something is caught therefore aptly describes the family’s semantic field.

Not every departure, movement, or separation should nevertheless be called naqdh. Its familiar uses prominently involve release from distress, danger, or another’s control.

Whether rescue succeeds also follows from the sentence. A negative verb such as lā yastanqidhūhu expresses inability to recover, rather than an accomplished rescue.

2. N Q DH in Early Arabic

Deliverance from danger and distress

Familiar expressions include:

أَنْقَذَهُ مِنَ الْهَلَاكِ

anqadhahu mina al-halāki

Translation: “He saved him from destruction.”

أَنْقَذَهُ مِنَ الْعَدُوِّ

anqadhahu mina al-ʿaduwwi

Translation: “He rescued him from the enemy.”

Min identifies the condition or party from which someone is delivered.

Anqadha itself denotes rescue; the accompanying expression identifies the danger or predicament.

Anqadhahu mina al-gharaq concerns drowning, whereas anqadhahu mina al-ʿaduww may concern captivity or enemy control.

Some uses also express being saved through the Form I verb naqadha.

Being saved must therefore be distinguished from saving another, despite belonging to the same family.

A horse recovered from the enemy

A particular early use concerns horses and other property captured by an enemy and subsequently recovered.

Lexical accounts attest:

فَرَسٌ نَقَذٌ

farasun naqadhun

Translation: “A horse recovered from another’s possession.”

Khaylun naqāʾidhu likewise denotes horses retrieved from an enemy or another group.

Rescue here need not mean saving a horse’s life. It may be perfectly healthy while held by those who captured it.

Returning it to an owner or another group is a kind of istinqādh.

N Q DH therefore extends beyond rescue from physical danger.

Istinqādh in this setting may concern recovering something previously lost or no longer under its owner’s control.

This does not establish that everything recovered necessarily belonged to the person recovering it. Ownership and entitlement follow from the circumstances, rather than from the root alone.

Naqīdhah: recovered or protective armour

Early Arabic also attests naqīdhah for a coat of armour.

The pre-Islamic poet Yazīd ibn al-Ṣaʿiq says:

أَعْدَدْتُ لِلْحِدْثَانِ كُلَّ نَقِيذَةٍ
أُنُفٍ كَلَائِحَةِ الْمُضِلِّ جَرُورِ

aʿdadtu li-al-ḥidthāni kulla naqīdhatin / unufin ka-lāʾiḥati al-muḍilli jarūri

Translation: “For the turns of fortune I prepared new, shining armour, gleaming like a mirage in the desert.”

Naqīdhah here denotes armour.

Lexical tradition offers two explanations for the designation.

The first identifies armour recovered from an enemy. The name then reflects the passive sense: something delivered or retrieved.

The second connects the name with armour protecting its wearer from sword blows and delivering them from injury.

This explanation emphasizes the armour’s protective function.

Both explanations are recorded, but neither should be treated as the certain historical origin without independent evidence.

The example matters because the family describes both recovered property and a means of protection in early Arabic.

Not every derivative means protecting, however. Protection and rescue may be related without being identical.

Rescue and preventing danger

Someone may keep another person away from danger before it arrives. Prevention or protection is then more prominent.

Someone may instead already be in danger and be brought out of it. Rescue is clearer in that situation.

The distinction is not absolute in language. People may be said to have been rescued even before the danger causes its final injury.

Naqdh therefore need not mean extraction after injury has already occurred.

What matters is release from a threatening situation, or recovery from circumstances in which someone or something is caught.

3. Qur’anic Uses: Delivering People

Āl ʿImrān 3:103: rescue from the brink of a fiery pit

وَكُنْتُمْ عَلَىٰ شَفَا حُفْرَةٍ مِّنَ النَّارِ فَأَنقَذَكُم مِّنْهَا

wa-kuntum ʿalā shafā ḥufratin mina al-nāri fa-anqadhakum minhā

Translation: “You were on the brink of a pit of fire, and He rescued you from it.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:103

This is the root’s first Qur’anic occurrence.

The verse begins:

وَاعْتَصِمُوا بِحَبْلِ اللَّهِ جَمِيعًا وَلَا تَفَرَّقُوا

wa-iʿtaṣimū bi-ḥabli Allāhi jamīʿan wa-lā tafarraqū

Translation: “Hold fast together to the rope of The God, and do not divide.”

It then recalls a divine blessing: the audience had been enemies, but The God joined their hearts and made them brothers through His favour.

Their previous condition is then portrayed as standing on the brink of a fiery pit.

Fa-anqadhakum is a Form IV perfect: The God rescued them.

Kum refers to the same audience, and minhā to the fiery pit or fire in the image.

Is the fire literal?

The image appears amid enmity, division, and hearts being joined.

The context therefore does not justify assuming that the whole audience physically stood beside an actual pit of fire.

The image conveys their proximity to a dangerous, destructive condition.

Exegetical history also relates the fire to the threat of punishment in the Hereafter. This fits Qur’anic warning, but the same verse also concerns their previous hostility and division.

The social and ethical dimension should therefore remain visible, rather than restricting deliverance to rescue from physical fire.

Nor should N Q DH be redefined here as creating brotherhood.

Joining hearts is expressed through fa-allafa; fa-anqadhakum expresses deliverance from danger.

Within one context, the two verbs present related aspects of the same divine blessing.

Rescue from falling differs from rescue after a fall

Another detail matters in the image.

The audience stood on shafā ḥufrah, the brink of a pit.

The text does not state that they had already fallen into the fire and were subsequently pulled out.

Anqadha should therefore not be restricted to rescue after falling.

Someone may be saved from a precipice before falling, and the rescue is real.

Deliverance can thus precede the final injury.

Preservation from danger is only one possible rescue situation, however; not every use necessarily occurs before someone becomes caught in a predicament.

Yā Sīn 36:23: supposed deities cannot rescue

أَأَتَّخِذُ مِن دُونِهِ آلِهَةً إِن يُرِدْنِ الرَّحْمَٰنُ بِضُرٍّ لَّا تُغْنِ عَنِّي شَفَاعَتُهُمْ شَيْئًا وَلَا يُنقِذُونِ

a-attakhidhu min dūnihi ālihatan in yuridni al-raḥmānu bi-ḍurrin lā tughni ʿannī shafāʿatuhum shayʾan wa-lā yunqidhūni

Translation: “Should I take deities apart from Him? If al-Raḥmān intends harm for me, their intercession will avail me nothing, nor will they rescue me.”

Yā Sīn 36:23

The verse belongs to the speech of a man urging his people to follow the messengers and worship the Creator.

He speaks of deities people have taken apart from The God.

He denies their effectiveness in two respects: their intercession avails nothing, and they cannot rescue him.

Yunqidhūni is Form IV and concerns rescuing the speaker.

Naqdh here concerns the capacity to rescue from harm and danger.

Intercession and rescue

Intercession and rescue stand together without sharing an identical meaning.

Intercession here concerns mediation or intervention to obtain benefit or avert harm.

Rescue concerns release from danger or harmful circumstances.

The verse thus challenges two claims about supposed deities: that they can benefit through mediation, or possess power to rescue.

N Q DH should not be translated as intercession, just as intercession is not always identical with rescue.

The verse concerns deities taken apart from The God. It should not, without independent examination, be generalized into denial of every kind of assistance or intercession discussed elsewhere in the Qur’an.

What is clear here is the supposed deities’ inability to rescue and the futility of their claimed intercession before al-Raḥmān’s will.

Yā Sīn 36:43: rescue from drowning

وَإِن نَّشَأْ نُغْرِقْهُمْ فَلَا صَرِيخَ لَهُمْ وَلَا هُمْ يُنقَذُونَ

wa-in nashaʾ nughriqhum fa-lā ṣarīkha lahum wa-lā hum yunqadhūna

Translation: “If We will, We drown them; then they have no one to answer their cries, nor are they rescued.”

Yā Sīn 36:43

The preceding verses describe a sign concerning human offspring carried in a ship and means of transport on which people ride.

They are then warned that The God may drown them, leaving no one to answer their cries.

Yunqadhūna is a Form IV passive imperfect.

The contrast with the earlier use matters.

Yā Sīn 36:23 concerns supposed deities lacking the ability to rescue.

In Yā Sīn 36:43, people themselves are the recipients of rescue. The passive emphasizes their condition.

فَلَا صَرِيخَ لَهُمْ and وَلَا هُمْ يُنقَذُونَ

The verse combines the absence of someone to answer their cries with their not being rescued.

Ṣarīkh, from Ṣ R KH, here denotes someone responding to another’s cry and helping them.

Rescue concerns deliverance actually taking place.

Someone may try to help but fail to save another. Assistance and rescue therefore do not always have the same result.

The verse denies both a rescuer responding to their cries and deliverance from drowning.

Together, the expressions portray human powerlessness before a danger that, if The God wills it, no one can independently remove.

Yā Sīn 36:44: the exception of mercy

Yā Sīn 36:44 states:

إِلَّا رَحْمَةً مِّنَّا وَمَتَاعًا إِلَىٰ حِينٍ

illā raḥmatan minnā wa-matāʿan ilā ḥīnin

Translation: “Except as mercy from Us and enjoyment for a time.”

Yā Sīn 36:44

The exception makes deliverance or preservation in that situation dependent on divine mercy.

Enjoyment for a time also emphasizes the limited duration of their benefit.

Rescue from one danger does not mean permanent immunity from every danger or death.

Someone may survive drowning while remaining mortal.

This follows from context, rather than making temporary release the meaning of naqdh itself.

4. Rescue from Fire and Recovering Something from a Fly

Al-Zumar 39:19: can the Prophet rescue someone in the Fire?

أَفَمَنْ حَقَّ عَلَيْهِ كَلِمَةُ الْعَذَابِ أَفَأَنتَ تُنقِذُ مَنْ فِي النَّارِ

a-fa-man ḥaqqa ʿalayhi kalimatu al-ʿadhābi a-fa-anta tunqidhu man fī al-nāri

Translation: “Is someone against whom the sentence of punishment has become due… can you rescue someone in the Fire?”

Al-Zumar 39:19

The verse concerns someone facing the fulfilment of a sentence of punishment.

The Prophet is asked whether he can rescue someone in the Fire.

Tunqidhu is a Form IV second-person singular imperfect.

It means “you deliver” or “you rescue.”

In context, the question challenges the Prophet’s independent ability to deliver such a person.

The meaning of مَنْ فِي النَّارِ

Man fī al-nār denotes someone in the Fire, or described as caught in its punishment.

Early explanations offer two closely related approaches.

One understands someone against whom punishment has become due, whose condition is as though already in the Fire.

The other emphasizes the person’s outcome and asks about rescue from divine punishment.

Both principally concern the limits of the Prophet’s ability to save someone facing punishment.

Tunqidhu itself does not establish a discussion of the physical method of pulling someone out of fire.

Fī al-nār identifies the person’s condition. The verse specifies neither a method of extraction nor a particular physical means of rescue.

Does the verse deny every human role in guiding others?

No.

The Qur’an identifies calling, warning, teaching, and showing the way as messengers’ responsibilities.

Guiding people and guaranteeing their final deliverance from their actions’ consequences are nevertheless different matters.

The Prophet may call, warn, and show the way, but cannot guarantee everyone’s salvation independently of divine will and judgment.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:56 states:

إِنَّكَ لَا تَهْدِي مَنْ أَحْبَبْتَ وَلَٰكِنَّ اللَّهَ يَهْدِي مَن يَشَاءُ

innaka lā tahdī man aḥbabta wa-lākinna Allāha yahdī man yashāʾu

Translation: “You do not guide whomever you love; The God guides whomever He wills.”

This helps distinguish the Prophet’s calling from guidance’s final outcome.

N Q DH should nevertheless not be restricted to guidance. Al-Zumar 39:19 concerns rescue from fire; its relationship to guidance follows from context and other passages.

Nor should a sentence of punishment being fulfilled be generalized into making efforts at reform and return pointless. The verse concerns the limits of the Prophet’s power to save, rather than cancelling human responsibilities for calling, reform, or choice.

Al-Ḥajj 22:73: recovering something from a fly

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّاسُ ضُرِبَ مَثَلٌ فَاسْتَمِعُوا لَهُ إِنَّ الَّذِينَ تَدْعُونَ مِن دُونِ اللَّهِ لَن يَخْلُقُوا ذُبَابًا وَلَوِ اجْتَمَعُوا لَهُ وَإِن يَسْلُبْهُمُ الذُّبَابُ شَيْئًا لَّا يَسْتَنقِذُوهُ مِنْهُ ضَعُفَ الطَّالِبُ وَالْمَطْلُوبُ

yā ayyuhā al-nāsu ḍuriba mathalun fa-istamiʿū lahu inna alladhīna tadʿūna min dūni Allāhi lan yakhluqū dhubāban wa-lawi ijtamaʿū lahu wa-in yaslubhumu al-dhubābu shayʾan lā yastanqidhūhu minhu ḍaʿufa al-ṭālibu wa-al-maṭlūbu

Translation: “People, a likeness is presented, so listen to it. Those you call upon apart from The God will never create a fly, even if they gather for it. If the fly snatches something from them, they cannot recover it from it. Weak are both seeker and sought.”

Al-Ḥajj 22:73

This is the root’s only Qur’anic Form X use.

Yastanqidhūhu forms the response to the condition in yaslubhum and is negated by lā. It is jussive, marked by deletion of the nūn from yastanqidhūna:

لَا يَسْتَنْقِذُوهُ مِنْهُ

lā yastanqidhūhu minhu

“They cannot recover it from it.”

The subject is not rescuing a person from death or fire, but recovering something a fly has taken.

This matters in defining N Q DH’s semantic core.

Defining it only as saving people from destruction would not adequately encompass this verse.

Understanding the core as release or recovery from a predicament or from another’s control makes the application clear.

يَسْلُبْهُمُ and يَسْتَنْقِذُوهُ

The verse sets two actions against one another.

Salaba concerns taking or snatching something from another.

Istanqadha here recovers something lost and now held by another.

The fly takes something, and the supposed deities cannot even recover it.

Deliverance here therefore concerns releasing an object from another’s possession.

Form X can express seeking or effort in some verbs. Its morphology alone does not, however, restrict istanqadha here to requesting rescue.

Arabic also uses it for the act of recovery itself.

The verse denies the ability to accomplish that act.

Why does the Qur’an use a fly?

The supposed deities’ powerlessness is illustrated in two respects.

First, they cannot create even a fly, despite gathering together for the task.

Second, if the fly takes something from them, they cannot recover it.

The contrast combines inability to create with inability to retrieve something from a small creature.

In this context, rescue forms part of an argument against the supposed deities’ independent power.

N Q DH itself does not mean power, creation, or divinity. Those concepts follow from the whole verse.

The Qur’an also does not specify what the fly takes. Some exegetical accounts mention food or perfume placed on idols.

Such explanations may illuminate the example’s historical setting, but should not be inserted into the base translation without evidence.

The Qur’an says only shayʾan, “something.”

This indefiniteness matters to the argument: the supposed deities are powerless even when a fly takes something from them.

5. N Q DH and Related Roots

N Q DH and N J W

The roots are very close in rescue and deliverance.

Anjā and other N J W derivatives frequently describe people being delivered from danger and punishment in the Qur’an.

For example:

فَأَنجَيْنَاهُ وَأَصْحَابَ السَّفِينَةِ

fa-anjaynāhu wa-aṣḥāba al-safīnati

Translation: “We saved him and the people of the ship.”

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:15

The verse concerns Noah and his companions being saved.

N Q DH can likewise convey rescue, as in Āl ʿImrān 3:103 and Yā Sīn 36:43.

The recovered horse and Al-Ḥajj 22:73 instead emphasize release from another’s possession.

N J W thus has a wide Qur’anic field of deliverance and survival, while familiar N Q DH uses emphasize rescue or retrieval from a particular condition.

There should not, however, be an absolute distinction making najāh always occur without another’s intervention and inqādh always require an outside agent.

The Qur’an itself uses anjaynā for saving others. The difference is contextual and a tendency of usage, rather than an exceptionless rule.

Study of N J W

N Q DH and KH L Ṣ

Khalaṣ concerns separation, release from mixture, or emerging from a condition; some uses involve purity from admixture.

Arabic sometimes explains khallaṣahu and anqadhahu through one another.

That proximity does not make rescue mean moral purification or sincerity of intention.

Qur’anic forms such as mukhliṣ and mukhlaṣ belong to this field and require study under KH L Ṣ. The verbal noun ikhlāṣ does not itself occur in that form in the Qur’anic text.

Inqādh in the passages under discussion concerns rescue from danger or recovery of an object.

Someone may therefore escape danger without any statement about their moral purity or sincerity.

Study of KH L Ṣ

N Q DH and ʿ Ṣ M

ʿAṣm has familiar uses involving holding, preventing injury, and preserving.

Hūd 11:43 states:

لَا عَاصِمَ الْيَوْمَ مِنْ أَمْرِ اللَّهِ إِلَّا مَن رَّحِمَ

lā ʿāṣima al-yawma min amri Allāhi illā man raḥima

Translation: “Today there is no protector from The God’s command, except one on whom He has mercy.”

The context concerns protection against danger.

Rescue may likewise precede final injury, as when someone is delivered from the brink of a fiery pit in Āl ʿImrān 3:103.

Clear N Q DH uses emphasize release from distress or recovery, whereas ʿaṣm may emphasize preventing involvement in danger and preserving safety.

The distinction is relative. ʿAṣm does not invariably precede danger, nor inqādh invariably follow injury.

N Q DH and GH W TH

Ghawth concerns help and relief in need or distress.

Someone asking another for help may seek ghawth.

Requesting or providing assistance does not necessarily accomplish rescue.

Someone may try to help but fail to deliver the other person.

Yā Sīn 36:43 combines the absence of someone responding to cries with not being rescued. Assistance and deliverance are related without being identical.

Inqādh often emphasizes deliverance taking place, whereas seeking or attempting help may not yet yield rescue.

N Q DH and N F DH

The roots look similar but differ in meaning.

N Q DH concerns delivering, rescuing, and recovering.

N F DH concerns passing through and reaching completion.

Someone may cross an obstacle to rescue another, but passage and rescue are not one action.

Someone can cross a river without saving anyone, or throw rescue equipment to someone without crossing anywhere.

Similar letters or a possible practical relationship do not make the roots identical.

Study of N F DH

6. Translation Difficulties and Proposals for Forqan

The main translation point is that both rescuing and recovering belong to this family.

Always choosing “saving from death” would obscure Al-Ḥajj 22:73.

Restricting the root to releasing something from another’s control would likewise require extra explanation for drowning or fire.

The definition should preserve both branches.

Suitable Qur’anic equivalents

Āl ʿImrān 3:103

فَأَنقَذَكُم مِّنْهَا

fa-anqadhakum minhā

“Then He rescued you from it.”

This retains the verb and its relationship to the preceding danger.

Yā Sīn 36:23

وَلَا يُنقِذُونِ

wa-lā yunqidhūni

“Nor do they rescue me.”

The verse denies the supposed deities’ ability to save the speaker.

Yā Sīn 36:43

وَلَا هُمْ يُنقَذُونَ

wa-lā hum yunqadhūna

“Nor are they rescued.”

The passive should remain visible in translation.

Al-Zumar 39:19

أَفَأَنتَ تُنقِذُ مَن فِي النَّارِ

a-fa-anta tunqidhu man fī al-nāri

“Can you rescue someone in the Fire?”

The question is retained. The Prophet’s limited capacity follows from context and should not become added content in the verb’s own meaning.

Al-Ḥajj 22:73

لَّا يَسْتَنقِذُوهُ مِنْهُ

lā yastanqidhūhu minhu

“They cannot recover it from it.”

“Recover” is more natural and precise than “rescue” here, since the object is something taken by a fly.

Brief root meaning and meanings in the verses

Brief root meaning: Escaping, delivering, and releasing someone or something from distress, danger, or another’s possession.

Āl ʿImrān 3:103: Delivering the audience from a dangerous condition portrayed as standing at a fiery pit’s brink.

Yā Sīn 36:23: Rescuing from harm and danger; a capacity the supposed deities lack.

Yā Sīn 36:43: Being rescued from drowning, expressed in the passive.

Al-Zumar 39:19: Deliverance from fire and punishment, in a question challenging the Prophet’s independent power to save.

Al-Ḥajj 22:73: Recovering something taken by a fly, an act the supposed deities cannot accomplish.

7. Conclusion

N Q DH concerns deliverance, rescue, and recovery in Arabic.

Early uses concern both people rescued from danger and horses, armour, or other things retrieved from another. The meaning therefore cannot be confined to saving human lives.

The Qur’an uses the root five times in five verses.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:103, The God delivers the audience from danger portrayed as a fiery pit’s brink, amid their previous enmity and division.

Yā Sīn 36:23 denies supposed deities’ ability to rescue. Yā Sīn 36:43 describes not being saved from drowning, and Yā Sīn 36:44 states the exception of divine mercy and enjoyment for a time.

Al-Zumar 39:19 questions the Prophet’s ability to save someone against whom punishment has become due. Calling and warning must here be distinguished from guaranteeing final deliverance.

Al-Ḥajj 22:73 concerns recovering something a fly has taken, rather than saving a person. Release and recovery from another’s control therefore also belong to the root’s semantic field.

Rescue itself does not mean guidance, intercession, permanent protection, or independent power. Those concepts may relate to deliverance in some contexts without becoming part of the root’s definition.

Final definition: N Q DH principally denotes escaping, delivering, and recovering someone or something from distress, danger, or another’s possession. Form IV anqadha concerns rescuing another; Form X istanqadha may concern release or retrieval from another’s control. Qur’anic uses apply both to people delivered from danger and punishment and to recovery of a stolen object. The particular danger, rescuer, success or failure, and final outcome follow from each sentence’s construction and context.
