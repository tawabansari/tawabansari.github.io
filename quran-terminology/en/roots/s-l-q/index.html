Harsh speech, verbal assault, and harm through words

S L Q has several branches in Arabic. In speech, it concerns force, intensity, loud or powerful expression, and sometimes verbally assailing or hurting another. Such force may appear in capable oratory or in hostile language used to humiliate, reproach, or distress its audience.

The root occurs only once in the Quran, as salaqūkum in Al-Aḥzāb 33:19. The passage describes people who shrink from confrontation when danger arrives but assail others with sharp tongues once their fear subsides.

Here salq is more than speaking loudly: it is directing forceful, harsh speech against another. Its harmful quality becomes clear through bi-alsinatin ḥidādin, “with sharp tongues,” and the surrounding account of these people's conduct.

1. Early Arabic usage and evidence attributed to the pre-Islamic period

Forceful speech and oratorical ability

An early witness is a verse attributed to Al-Aʿshā, a poet of the late pre-Islamic period:

فِيهِمُ الْخِصْبُ وَالسَّمَاحَةُ وَالنَّجْدَةُ
فِيهِمْ وَالْخَاطِبُ الْمِسْلَاقُ

fīhimu al-khiṣbu wa-al-samāḥatu wa-al-najdatu / fīhim wa-al-khāṭibu al-mislāq

“Among them are abundance and generosity; among them are courage and the powerful orator.”

Al-mislāq describes a speaker with ability, fluency, and force of expression. It appears alongside praised qualities and need not mean an abusive or foul-mouthed person in this witness.

This matters because force of speech in this family is not inherently negative. Language can be strong, resonant, and effective without being used to harm others.

Transmitted versions vary in some words and in sallāq and mislāq. The verse is attributed to Al-Aʿshā and recorded in early literary works, but its exact date is not independently certain. It supports early usage, rather than dating the emergence of every sense of the root.

Expressions such as khaṭīb sallāq, khaṭīb mislāq, and lisān mislaq are also recorded. They describe a capable orator, a sharp tongue, or forceful speech.

Salaqahu bi-lisānihi, meanwhile, describes someone addressing another with harsh, unpleasant words and treating them severely through speech.

Here forceful expression becomes a verbal confrontation with an addressee.

Two related directions can therefore be distinguished: expressive strength and oratorical ability, and the use of such strength to assail, reproach, or hurt another.

Physical uses

Classical Arabic records other forms for physical actions and conditions.

Salaqa is used for striking, especially with a whip. In some uses, the intensity of the blow is connected with injury or skin being stripped.

Salaqa al-shayʾa bi-al-māʾi al-ḥārri concerns treating something with hot water or boiling it. Cooking vegetables and eggs in water are familiar applications.

Salīqah can denote the place or mark of a strap on a camel's side; sulāq is also recorded for an affliction of the tongue or mouth. These are historical lexical descriptions and do not identify a precise modern medical diagnosis.

Al-silq names an edible plant. Al-salaq can denote level or low-lying terrain. Salīqah is also known in the sense of natural disposition.

The family is therefore not confined to speech.

Some senses, such as striking, injuring, forceful contact, and verbal assault, may be related through imagery. Harmful speech can be compared to a blow directed at its recipient.

That similarity does not establish a single historical origin. Early lexical discussions also recognize the family's diverse meanings.

One should therefore not declare skinning, boiling, striking, or raising the voice the historically original meaning of every derivative. The evidence does not establish such a definite sequence.

For the Quranic use, forceful speech, strong address, and verbal harm provide the clearest linguistic basis.

2. The Quranic form and its construction

The root occurs once in the Quran:

Salaqūkum — Al-Aḥzāb 33:19

It derives from the basic verb salaqa.

Salaqū is a third-person masculine plural perfect verb, and -kum is the attached plural object pronoun, “you.”

The verb thus describes a group acting directly upon the people addressed. Although perfect in form, within the verse's idhā construction it describes what happens when fear departs; English can naturally use “they assail you.”

Bi-alsinatin ḥidādin explains the means of the action. The preposition bi- marks the instrument: their tongues.

Ḥidād is the plural of ḥadīd in the sense “sharp.” It belongs to Ḥ D D, not S L Q.

The harsh verbal action becomes clear through three connected elements:

  • Salaqū: the act of assailing or treating harshly.
  • -Kum: the people against whom it is directed.
  • Bi-alsinatin ḥidādin: the sharp tongues used to carry it out.

The construction cannot be reduced to “they spoke.” Nor should the independent description “sharp tongues” be treated as the verb's entire lexical definition; it specifies the action's quality here.

Sallāq, mislāq, salīqah, and salīq are known outside the Quran but do not occur in these forms in it. The basic verb salaqa does occur, inflected as salaqūkum; it should not be classified as absent from the Quran.

3. The occurrence in Al-Aḥzāb 33:19

The sole Quranic occurrence appears in this context:

أَشِحَّةً عَلَيْكُمْ ۖ فَإِذَا جَاءَ الْخَوْفُ رَأَيْتَهُمْ يَنْظُرُونَ إِلَيْكَ تَدُورُ أَعْيُنُهُمْ كَالَّذِي يُغْشَىٰ عَلَيْهِ مِنَ الْمَوْتِ ۖ فَإِذَا ذَهَبَ الْخَوْفُ سَلَقُوكُمْ بِأَلْسِنَةٍ حِدَادٍ أَشِحَّةً عَلَى الْخَيْرِ ۚ أُولَٰئِكَ لَمْ يُؤْمِنُوا فَأَحْبَطَ اللَّهُ أَعْمَالَهُمْ ۚ وَكَانَ ذَٰلِكَ عَلَى اللَّهِ يَسِيرًا

ashiḥḥatan ʿalaykum fa-idhā jāʾa al-khawfu raʾaytahum yanẓurūna ilayka tadūru aʿyunuhum ka-alladhī yughshā ʿalayhi mina al-mawti fa-idhā dhahaba al-khawfu salaqūkum bi-alsinatin ḥidādin ashiḥḥatan ʿalā al-khayri ulāʾika lam yuʾminū fa-aḥbaṭa Allāhu aʿmālahum wa-kāna dhālika ʿalā Allāhi yasīrā

Al-Aḥzāb 33:19

“Stingy toward you. When fear comes, you see them looking at you, their eyes revolving like someone overcome by death. But when fear departs, they assail you with sharp tongues, grasping for good. Those have not believed, so The God has rendered their deeds fruitless; and that is easy for The God.”

The preceding context

Al-Aḥzāb 33:18 describes people who obstruct others and tell their brothers to join them, while themselves coming to battle only a little.

The next verse contrasts their behavior during danger with their behavior after it passes.

Their fear is so intense that their eyes are compared with those of someone overcome by death.

Yet once the danger subsides, these same people assail others with sharp tongues.

This contrast clarifies salaqūkum: they evade responsibility and confrontation in danger, then turn their speech into a means of pressure and attack when safe.

Does salaqūkum mean only insulting?

Lexical explanations include harsh speech, verbal injury, reproach, fault-finding, and severe address.

The verb should not be confined to any one of those instances.

Insulting may be one form, but its field is wider. Persistent reproach, humiliation, threats, harsh words, or oppressive demands can assail someone without explicit profanity or insults.

Conversely, merely speaking loudly or possessing oratorical skill does not establish the condemned sense here.

The accompanying conduct, direction of speech, and character of those described are decisive.

“They assail you with sharp tongues” therefore preserves more than “they speak to you” or the narrower “they insult you.”

The relation to ashiḥḥatan ʿalā al-khayr

The phrase ashiḥḥatan ʿalā al-khayr follows salaqūkum bi-alsinatin ḥidādin.

Ashiḥḥatan expresses intense stinginess and grasping. After danger in a war setting, it can relate to eagerness for the benefits of victory, including spoils.

Al-khayr is not, however, an exclusive lexical name for war booty. Its broader meaning of good or benefit should not be confined without evidence to a single historical instance.

The verse connects harsh speech with these people's grasping attitude toward benefits. They withhold support and responsibility in danger, then become forceful in speech and demands afterward.

Some historical explanations connect this with disputes over shares of spoils. That is an intelligible contextual application, but salq must not be translated lexically as “demanding spoils.”

The verb concerns the manner of verbal treatment; motive and subject come from context.

4. Sharp tongues, powerful speech, and moral responsibility

Salaqūkum shows that words can be used to attack others without a physical blow.

“Sharp tongues” offers a clear image: as a sharp implement can cut and injure, speech can be used to hurt and humiliate.

The image must be distinguished from a physical description. The verse does not say that tongues literally cut bodies; it describes harsh, harmful conduct.

Expressive power does not establish correctness

The verse attributed to Al-Aʿshā uses this family to praise a capable speaker.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:19 connects the same field of forceful speech with wrongful conduct.

One person may speak fluently, powerfully, and persuasively to convey truth; another may use that ability to deceive, humiliate, threaten, or demand what is unjust.

The root alone does not establish a statement's truth or falsehood.

What turns powerful expression into harmful assault is not volume alone, but the manner and direction of speech, its relation to right, and the speaker's conduct.

This prevents treating every frank criticism, serious objection, or firm statement as the condemned salq merely because it is forceful.

Other Quranic descriptions of speech

Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:2 describes hands and tongues extended toward others with evil. Both can serve hostility, though physical and verbal actions differ.

Al-Fatḥ 48:11 describes people saying with their tongues what is not in their hearts.

Neither verse uses S L Q, but both contribute to the broader treatment of verbal conduct.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:19 emphasizes harsh verbal assault; Al-Fatḥ 48:11 the mismatch between speech and inward conviction; Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:2 the hostile use of hands and tongues.

Wrongful speech is thus described through more than one verb or image.

Speech and action

The problem in Al-Aḥzāb 33:19 is not limited to a harsh tongue.

The surrounding verses describe obstructing others, evading responsibility, and reluctance in confrontation. Harsh speech follows once the danger passes.

The contrast exposes a gap between verbal claims and actual conduct.

Someone unwilling to accept responsibility in hardship but verbally attacking others when safe may use speech as a substitute for action and a means of pressure.

The verse judges a particular group through a combination of behaviors. It does not establish that every frightened person, forceful speaker, or outspoken critic is a hypocrite.

Fear in danger is not itself hypocrisy. The context condemns a combination of obstruction, evasion of responsibility, greed, and verbal aggression.

5. Distinctions from related roots

S L Q and Ṣ L Q

Arabic lexical accounts relate salq and ṣalq in some uses concerning sound and shouting.

Ṣalaqa can mean crying out or making a loud, intense sound. The s/ṣ variation is sometimes explained as a variation in linguistic form.

This does not make every branch of the two roots identical.

The Quranic text followed here, in the transmission of Ḥafṣ, has salaqūkum with sīn. Ṣalaqūkum with ṣād is reported in some reading and linguistic accounts; that report should not silently replace the base reading or be treated as having the same transmission status.

S L Q and S L Ṭ

These are close in sound and certain speech-related uses, but are distinct roots.

S L Ṭ concerns power, authority, proof, and predominance in the Quran. Lisān salīṭ is also known in Arabic for sharp, forceful speech.

S L Q in Al-Aḥzāb emphasizes the harsh verbal action itself.

Salīṭ can qualify a tongue or speaker, while salaqūkum describes what people do to others through their tongues.

Semantic closeness does not establish a historical derivational relationship or identity of roots.

S L Q and Q W L

Qawl broadly denotes saying and speech. Speech may be true or false, good or bad, calm or harsh.

Salaqa in its verbal application carries a quality of force, severity, and intense address.

Simply rendering it “they said” in Al-Aḥzāb 33:19 loses a decisive element.

S L Q and SH T M

Shatm concerns insults and abusive language.

Salq may include insults but is not confined to them. Harsh words, severe reproach, aggressive address, and verbal injury can occur without an explicit insult.

“They insulted” therefore conveys a possible instance, not the verb's entire range.

S L Q and B S Ṭ

Basṭ concerns extending and spreading. Extending the tongue can describe certain verbal behaviors.

The verbal assault in Al-Aḥzāb 33:19 can be understood as extending one's tongue in harsh confrontation, but basṭ alone does not imply injury or aggression.

Explaining salq this way is adequate only if the force and direction of the verbal assault are also made clear.

6. Translation suggestions for Forqan

Several equivalents can be considered for salaqūkum in Al-Aḥzāb 33:19.

“They assailed you” preserves forceful action directed at an addressee. With bi-alsinatin ḥidādin, it becomes:

“They assailed you with sharp tongues.”

“They attacked you with harsh tongues” conveys the general sense, though “attacked” may initially suggest physical conflict.

“They hurt you with harsh words” makes the harmful result explicit but weakens the image of forceful assault.

The Persian expression for speaking insolently to someone is natural, but may narrow the verse to impertinence or bad manners.

“They spoke to you,” “they raised their voices,” and “they insulted you” are insufficient alone: the first loses intensity, the second reduces it to volume, and the third selects one form of verbal harm.

A suitable Forqan rendering is:

“But when fear departs, they assail you with sharp tongues.”

This preserves the force of salaqūkum and the image of sharp tongues without adding a particular instance such as insults, threats, or demands for spoils.

Conclusion

S L Q has different Arabic semantic branches that cannot all be reduced to a single physical image or established historical definition.

Its speech-related witnesses attest forceful oratory and powerful address. These may praise an able speaker or, in a critical context, describe verbal injury and assault.

The sole Quranic occurrence is Al-Aḥzāb 33:19: salaqūkum describes people assailing others with sharp tongues after danger passes. The accompanying phrases about sharp tongues and grasping for good make this harmful confrontation rather than mere speaking ability.

The Quranically relevant branch can be defined as follows:

In speech, S L Q concerns forceful, intense expression that may take the form of harsh address, verbal assault, and harm through words.

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:19 specifically, it means assailing or treating another harshly through speech.

Across these Quranic passages, language can serve truth, justice, and clarification, or become a means of aggression, humiliation, and pressure. Its value depends on truth, conduct, and how it is used, not merely on eloquence or volume.
