Overturning, returning to a former condition, and falling back again

Brief meaning: Turning something back or overturning it; returning it to a former condition, especially one it had left. Extended uses describe falling back into difficulty, deviation, or an unwelcome condition.

1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

The three-consonant Arabic root R K S concerns turning back, overturning, and returning to an earlier condition. Its derivatives can describe something being reversed or a person becoming caught again in a situation previously left behind.

Recorded uses of رَكَسَ and أَرْكَسَ express turning back or overturning. ارْتَكَسَ can describe falling back into an affair or condition from which someone had escaped.

In the Qur’an, the root occurs only in Form IV. Both occurrences are in Al-Nisāʾ and concern groups whose professed allegiance, actual conduct, and return to fitnah are at issue.

In the first, The God has overturned them because of what they earned. The second describes people who, whenever returned to fitnah, are plunged back into it.

The uses share a semantic core, but their grammatical constructions and contexts differ significantly.

Forms examined and Qur’anic derivatives

R K S occurs twice in the Qur’an, in two forms:

  • أَرْكَسَهُمْ: Active perfect verb, Form IV, with the object pronoun “them”; Al-Nisāʾ 4:88.
  • أُرْكِسُوا: Passive perfect verb, Form IV, third-person masculine plural; Al-Nisāʾ 4:91.

Other Arabic forms include رَكَسَ, يَرْكُسُ, رَكْس, إِرْكَاس, ارْتَكَسَ, ارْتِكَاس, مَرْكُوس, رَكِيس, and رِكْس.

رَكِيس and مَرْكُوس can describe something turned back or overturned. A recognized use of irtikās expresses returning to, or falling back into, an unwelcome condition.

These forms help explain the lexical family, but only أَرْكَسَهُمْ and أُرْكِسُوا occur in the Qur’an.

2. The semantic core in early Arabic

Turning back and overturning

One early lexical explanation identifies a principal meaning for R K S:

قَلْبُ الشَّيْءِ عَلَى رَأْسِهِ وَرَدُّ أَوَّلِهِ عَلَى آخِرِهِ

qalbu al-shayʾi ʿalā raʾsihi wa-raddu awwalihi ʿalā ākhirihi

Translation: “Turning something upside down upon its head, and returning its beginning upon its end.”

This explanation presents related images of overturning and reversing the order of parts. Returning to a former condition is also established in other uses of the root; the quoted definition alone need not express every aspect of that meaning.

Early Arabic also records رَكَسْتُ الشَّيْءَ and أَرْكَسْتُهُ for turning something back. The basic verb and Form IV can therefore have closely related meanings.

The added hamzah in أَرْكَسَ does not by itself establish greater intensity than رَكَسَ or a more deliberate act. Morphology must be considered alongside actual usage.

The physical image is clear: something is turned over, changing its orientation or condition.

In human and ethical settings, this does not mean a person’s body literally turns upside down. Their standing, direction of conduct, or condition may have changed.

Returning to a condition previously escaped

Lexical records contain the following expression:

ارْتَكَسَ فُلَانٌ فِي أَمْرٍ قَدْ كَانَ نَجَا مِنْهُ

irtakasa fulānun fī amrin qad kāna najā minhu

Translation: “Someone became caught again in an affair from which he had previously escaped.”

This clearly illustrates extending the root’s physical meaning to human circumstances.

The issue is more than backward movement. A person has been in a situation, left or escaped it, and then become caught in it again.

Irtikās can thus express a return involving renewed entanglement.

Someone might abandon harmful conduct and then return to it. In such a use, the word emphasizes returning to that behavior or condition.

This example does not establish that every form necessarily expresses repeated recurrence or moral failure.

Repetition is identified through constructions such as كلما or through context. Negative value likewise depends on the condition in which the person becomes caught.

Returning something into another substance

Arabic records أَرْكَسَ الثَّوْبَ فِي الصِّبْغِ for dipping a cloth into dye again.

The cloth has previously been in contact with the dye and is immersed in it once more.

This shows that irkās can describe returning something into a medium or substance.

The action is not necessarily undesirable. Dyeing cloth again may be ordinary and useful.

Raks therefore cannot initially be defined as sin, corruption, or deviation.

The negative character of its Qur’anic uses comes from the people’s circumstances and the verses’ contexts, not from every act of returning being intrinsically bad.

Riks: filth or something unpleasant

Some lexical records also use رِكْس for something filthy, unclean, or unpleasant.

An early explanation compares it with رجيع, material discharged from the body.

Some lexicographers explain this connection through food being transformed into excreted material.

This may suggest an image linking a changed condition with the naming of unpleasant matter.

It should not be treated as the certain historical origin of all the root’s meanings.

رِكْس for filth and رَكْس for turning back also differ in form and usage.

A sense of filth in one branch does not make Qur’anic أَرْكَسَهُمْ mean physically defiling people.

The Qur’anic verbs concern people’s condition and conduct, not bodily contamination.

Other recorded uses

Arabic also records رِكْس for a large group of people and a bridge. Some lexical accounts additionally relate the word to a structure rebuilt after collapse; such usages should be distinguished from the Qur’anic verbs.

One use of الرَّاكِس names the ox stationed in the middle of a threshing floor while other cattle circle around it.

الرِّكاس is also recorded for a tether restricting a camel’s movement.

These usages show that dictionaries collect words with different referents under R K S.

Listing words under one entry does not alone prove that all originated in one historical image.

Rākis also occurs as a place-name in poetry by al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī. A proper place-name should not be converted into a descriptive verbal meaning without independent evidence.

For Qur’anic study, turning back and overturning remain central, since both Qur’anic verbs fit that field.

3. Poetry and early usage

Poetry attributed to Umayyah ibn Abī al-Ṣalt, an Arab poet living near the emergence of Islam, contains the expression:

أُرْكِسُوا فِي جَهَنَّمٍ

urkisū fī jahannamin

Translation: “They were overturned and cast into Hell.”

The rest of the verse speaks of their disobedience and false speech.

Early exegetical and lexical works transmit this example, but the complete wording varies. Some versions have حميم النار and others جهنم; other parts of the verse also differ.

أركسوا في جهنم is transmitted in versions of Umayyah’s poetry, but the date of this particular composition is not firmly established.

The example matters because irkās describes casting people into an unwelcome condition.

Hell is not contained in أركسوا itself: the sentence expressly names the destination.

Nor are everlasting confinement, fire, or punishment in the hereafter intrinsic to the lexical definition of irkās.

The verse illustrates the verb in an image of overthrow and entrapment.

Early usage also records the root for returning to difficulty. Two features thus stand out: reversing a condition and falling into a situation previously left or one in which a person becomes caught.

These features also appear in the Qur’anic uses.

4. أَرْكَسَهُمْ: overturned because of their own deeds

Al-Nisāʾ 4:88

Verse text:

فَمَا لَكُمْ فِي الْمُنَافِقِينَ فِئَتَيْنِ وَاللَّهُ أَرْكَسَهُمْ بِمَا كَسَبُوا ۚ أَتُرِيدُونَ أَنْ تَهْدُوا مَنْ أَضَلَّ اللَّهُ ۖ وَمَنْ يُضْلِلِ اللَّهُ فَلَنْ تَجِدَ لَهُ سَبِيلًا

fa-mā lakum fī al-munāfiqīna fiʾatayni wa-Allāhu arkasahum bi-mā kasabū a-turīdūna an tahdū man aḍalla Allāhu wa-man yuḍlili Allāhu fa-lan tajida lahu sabīlan

Al-Nisāʾ 4:88

Translation:

“What is the matter with you that you have become two groups concerning the hypocrites, when The God has overturned them because of what they earned? Do you wish to guide someone whom The God has led astray? Whoever The God leads astray, you will never find a way for him.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:88

The meaning of أَرْكَسَهُمْ

أَرْكَسَهُمْ is an active perfect verb in Form IV.

Its subject is الله, and “them” refers to the hypocrites mentioned at the beginning of the verse.

Overturning or returning to an unwelcome condition fits this construction and context.

The verse concerns a group about whom the addressees disagree.

فِئَتَيْنِ describes the addressees dividing into two groups in their assessment. It should not be translated as two independent groups of hypocrites without regard for the sentence’s structure.

The God then describes their condition through أَرْكَسَهُمْ.

Early interpretation often explains this as their being returned to unbelief or their former condition.

The verb’s lexical meaning should nevertheless be distinguished from identifying historical details.

أركس does not itself name a religious group, a change in legal status, or a specific penal ruling. It expresses overturning and returning; the context identifies the setting of that change.

بِمَا كَسَبُوا: conduct and consequence

بِمَا كَسَبُوا is a particularly significant part of the verse.

It connects their overthrow with their own conduct and what they earned.

The verse therefore does not merely describe something imposed on people without connection to their actions.

كَسَبُوا concerns what they themselves did or acquired.

In this construction, بما expresses causation.

أركسهم should not be translated or analyzed apart from this qualification, which would suggest that their overthrow was entirely independent of their conduct.

At the same time, أركسهم is an act of The God in this verse.

Both relations appear together: The God overturned them, and this happened because of their own deeds.

Divine agency and human responsibility should therefore not replace one another in the explanation.

The verse describes a consequence connected to people’s conduct, not an absence of responsibility for the condition they have reached.

The connection with misguidance

The verse continues:

أَتُرِيدُونَ أَنْ تَهْدُوا مَنْ أَضَلَّ اللَّهُ

a-turīdūna an tahdū man aḍalla Allāhu

Al-Nisāʾ 4:88

This places their overthrow in the field of guidance and misguidance.

Returning to unbelief or deviation fits this context.

Irkās and iḍlāl nevertheless remain distinct words.

Irkās evokes overturning and returning, while iḍlāl expresses leading astray, causing departure from the right way.

Both here concern the same people’s condition.

Their association places overthrow within guidance and misguidance without making the two roots entirely synonymous.

Does overthrow mean losing every future opportunity to reform?

The verse speaks of misguidance and the impossibility of finding a way for someone whom The God leads astray.

The root R K S alone, however, does not establish that everyone who slips once or returns to error is permanently deprived of reform.

Returning from error and correcting conduct are also explicit Qur’anic themes.

For example, Al-Nisāʾ 4:146 presents repentance, reform, holding fast to The God, and making religion sincere as a way back for hypocrites.

Irkās should therefore not become an absolute rule making every lapse or return to error determine an eternal, unchangeable fate.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:88 concerns a particular group with particular conduct and circumstances.

Nor should it become a means of definitively determining the intentions and fate of anyone others call a hypocrite.

Knowing observable conduct, determining practical responsibility, and judging hidden intentions are different matters.

Historical setting and the limits of the ruling

Historical and exegetical accounts differ about the people associated with the verse’s revelation.

Some describe disagreement over people who withdrew from military accompaniment. Others mention groups moving between two communities whose conduct became disputed.

These accounts help establish historical background but do not determine the lexical meaning of أركسهم.

The following verses also discuss hostile relations, agreements, withdrawing from fighting, and offering peace.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:90 expressly states that no way of acting against those who withdraw from fighting and offer peace has been granted.

أركسهم alone is therefore no command for coercive action against every opponent or person accused of hypocrisy.

The practical ruling must be understood with the conditions and restrictions of the relevant verses, rather than turning a lexical verb into a general license for treating people.

5. أُرْكِسُوا: recurrent return to fitnah

Al-Nisāʾ 4:91

Verse text:

سَتَجِدُونَ آخَرِينَ يُرِيدُونَ أَنْ يَأْمَنُوكُمْ وَيَأْمَنُوا قَوْمَهُمْ كُلَّ مَا رُدُّوا إِلَى الْفِتْنَةِ أُرْكِسُوا فِيهَا ۚ فَإِنْ لَمْ يَعْتَزِلُوكُمْ وَيُلْقُوا إِلَيْكُمُ السَّلَمَ وَيَكُفُّوا أَيْدِيَهُمْ فَخُذُوهُمْ وَاقْتُلُوهُمْ حَيْثُ ثَقِفْتُمُوهُمْ ۚ وَأُولَٰئِكُمْ جَعَلْنَا لَكُمْ عَلَيْهِمْ سُلْطَانًا مُبِينًا

sa-tajidūna ākharīna yurīdūna an yaʾmanūkum wa-yaʾmanū qawmahum kulla mā ruddū ilā al-fitnati urkisū fīhā fa-in lam yaʿtazilūkum wa-yulqū ilaykumu al-salama wa-yakuffū aydiyahum fa-khudhūhum wa-uqtulūhum ḥaythu thaqiftumūhum wa-ulāʾikum jaʿalnā lakum ʿalayhim sulṭānan mubīnan

Al-Nisāʾ 4:91

Translation:

“You will find others who wish to be safe from you and from their own people. Whenever they are returned to fitnah, they are overturned in it. If they do not withdraw from you, offer you peace, and restrain their hands, seize them and kill them wherever you find them. Against these We have given you clear authority.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:91

The construction of أُرْكِسُوا

أُرْكِسُوا is a Form IV passive perfect verb, third-person masculine plural.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:88, أَرْكَسَهُمْ is active and its subject is expressly The God.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:91, the verb is passive and its agent is unnamed.

This difference matters.

Sharing a root does not automatically make the first verse’s specified agent the grammatical subject of the second.

The second focuses on the condition into which the people are plunged.

أُرْكِسُوا فِيهَا suitably means that they are overturned in fitnah or plunged back into it.

كُلَّ مَا: recurring return

Before أركسوا, the verse says:

كُلَّ مَا رُدُّوا إِلَى الْفِتْنَةِ

kulla mā ruddū ilā al-fitnati

Al-Nisāʾ 4:91

That is, “Whenever they were returned to fitnah.”

كُلَّ مَا expresses recurrence.

Repeated return is therefore established by this construction, not by assuming that R K S necessarily expresses multiple repetitions in every use.

This distinction matters for lexical study.

Something may be overturned once and still be described with raks.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:91, however, describes a recurring pattern: whenever the people are returned to fitnah, they become caught in it again.

The distinction between رُدُّوا and أُرْكِسُوا

The sentence places two related verbs together:

رُدُّوا إِلَى الْفِتْنَةِ

ruddū ilā al-fitnati

Al-Nisāʾ 4:91

أُرْكِسُوا فِيهَا

urkisū fīhā

Al-Nisāʾ 4:91

The first belongs to R D D and expresses their being returned toward fitnah.

The second belongs to R K S and describes their overturning or falling into that same fitnah.

The prepositions also differ.

إِلَى in the first expression identifies the direction of return toward fitnah.

فِيهَا in the second describes their entanglement within it.

The verse has therefore not repeated two verbs without a distinction.

One presents being returned toward fitnah; the other presents falling into that condition.

The distinction arises from the verbs together with their prepositions. It does not establish an absolute rule that radd always concerns external movement and raks exclusively internal entrapment.

Fitnah in this verse

Fitnah has a broad Qur’anic field: depending on context, trial, pressure, persecution, misleading, or a condition of turmoil and conflict.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:91 concerns people seeking safety from both parties yet becoming caught again in fitnah when returned to it.

The continuation addresses withdrawal from fighting, offering peace, and restraining hands.

Fitnah here is therefore connected with hostility and conflict.

It should not be reduced to any intellectual disagreement or personal trial without considering that context.

Nor does أُرْكِسُوا establish that everyone entering a harmful environment inevitably adopts all its behavior.

The verse concerns particular people and their pattern of conduct.

Seeking security from both sides

The opening describes people seeking safety from both the addressees and their own community.

Seeking security is not in itself objectionable.

Someone may be caught between two groups and wish to avoid conflict.

The preceding verse also identifies people who avoid fighting either party. When they withdraw and offer peace, attacking them is not permitted.

The issue in Al-Nisāʾ 4:91 is therefore more than wanting safety: their conduct on returning to fitnah and their failure to withdraw from hostility matter.

This prevents أُرْكِسُوا from becoming a general accusation against neutral people or those seeking peace.

Three behavioral conditions in the continuation

Al-Nisāʾ 4:91 then addresses withdrawal from confrontation, offering peace, and restraining hands.

These are observable criteria of conduct.

Merely claiming someone is inwardly hypocritical or holds mistaken beliefs does not establish the applicability of all the practical rulings in this passage.

The verses distinguish those who withdraw from fighting from those who do not.

For root study, irkās describes a condition; practical responsibility and how people are treated depend on other clauses and the actual circumstances of their conduct.

The meaning of أُرْكِسُوا should therefore not be equated with every ruling and consequence later in the verse.

6. Related roots and translation proposals

R K S (raks) and N K S (naks)

These roots are closely related in Arabic meaning.

Naks also concerns overturning or reversing something.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:65 contains نُكِسُوا عَلَىٰ رُءُوسِهِمْ.

Yā Sīn 36:68 uses نُنَكِّسْهُ فِي الْخَلْقِ for changes in a person’s condition with advancing age.

Lexical explanations sometimes treat markūs and mankūs as closely related.

This does not establish that the roots are identical in every use.

The Qur’anic uses of R K S highlight return to an unwelcome condition and renewed entanglement in fitnah.

N K S in the cited verses can emphasize reversed direction or a change of condition.

This is a tendency arising from particular Qur’anic usages, not an absolute boundary throughout Arabic.

R K S (raks) and R D D (radd)

R D D covers a broad field of returning and responding.

Both roots occur in one sentence in Al-Nisāʾ 4:91.

رُدُّوا إِلَى الْفِتْنَةِ expresses being returned toward fitnah; أُرْكِسُوا فِيهَا expresses being caught in it again.

Irkās in such a setting can therefore go beyond simple return and highlight overturning or plunging.

Yet lexical explanations sometimes define raks through radd, so an absolute, exceptionless distinction would be unjustified.

R K S (raks) and R J S (rijs)

Qur’anic rijs concerns filth and impurity.

Al-Māʾidah 5:90 applies it to a set of practices and activities.

Lexical records also sometimes use riks for filth or something unpleasant.

R J S and R K S are nevertheless independent roots.

Overlap between riks and rijs does not make Qur’anic أركسهم directly mean “He physically defiled them.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:88 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:91 concern overturning and returning to a wrongful condition.

Impurity may feature in ethical analysis of certain conditions, but the verb’s direct meaning should not be converted into bodily impurity.

R K S (raks) and N K Ṣ (nukūṣ)

Nukūṣ concerns retreat and backward movement.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:66 contains عَلَىٰ أَعْقَابِكُمْ تَنْكِصُونَ.

This can evoke stepping back or turning away.

Irkās may likewise concern return to an earlier condition, but its Qur’anic use highlights overturning and becoming caught in the condition described.

Not every retreat is therefore irtikās, and not every irkās describes bodily movement backward.

R K S (raks) and R K Z (rakz)

These roots differ only in their last consonant, but their main semantic fields differ.

One branch of R K Z concerns planting and securing something; another concerns a faint sound.

R K S concerns turning back, overturning, and falling back into a condition.

Something might physically turn over and sink into the ground, but that combined image does not establish identical roots or verbs.

Qur’anic رِكْزًا in Maryam 19:98 means a faint sound, whereas أركسهم in Al-Nisāʾ 4:88 concerns people’s condition being overturned.

Translation proposals for Forqan

أَرْكَسَهُمْ — Al-Nisāʾ 4:88

Suitable equivalents include “He overturned them,” “He returned them to their former condition,” or, as a contextual explanation, “He returned them to their former misguidance because of their conduct.”

The supplied translation reads:

“The God has overturned them because of what they earned.”

This rendering is lexically defensible and preserves the verb’s principal image.

“Overturned” has the advantage of not substituting the interpretive conclusion of return to unbelief directly for the word itself.

أُرْكِسُوا — Al-Nisāʾ 4:91

Suitable equivalents include “they were overturned,” “they were plunged back,” and “they became caught in it again.”

In أُرْكِسُوا فِيهَا, “they were overturned in it” closely follows the Arabic construction.

The supplied translation reads:

“Whenever they are returned to fitnah, they are overturned in it.”

This preserves the difference between رُدُّوا and أُرْكِسُوا.

Where further explanation is needed, the article or word box can clarify that falling back into fitnah is meant.

Suggested word-box wording for Forqan

Root: R K S

Brief root meaning: Overturning, turning back, and returning to an earlier condition; sometimes falling back into difficulty.

Word: أَرْكَسَهُمْ — Al-Nisāʾ 4:88

Meaning here: He overturned them or returned them to misguidance, because of what they themselves earned.

Word: أُرْكِسُوا — Al-Nisāʾ 4:91

Meaning here: They were plunged back into fitnah or overturned in it after being returned toward it.

These explanations keep the root meaning aligned with the active and passive constructions. They are proposed wording for the study, not grounds for replacing existing authored verse explanations.

7. Conclusions and final definition

Arabic R K S concerns turning back, overturning, and returning to a former condition.

Physically, something can be inverted or returned to its former position or state. Extended uses can describe a person becoming caught again in circumstances previously left or escaped.

Recorded derivatives such as markūs, rakīs, and irtikās illustrate this field.

Another branch records riks for filth or something unpleasant, but this should not define every derivative.

The Qur’an uses R K S only twice, both in Form IV.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:88, أَرْكَسَهُمْ is active and describes hypocrites being overturned because of their own conduct.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:91, أُرْكِسُوا is passive and concerns people who are plunged back into fitnah whenever returned to it.

In the first, بِمَا كَسَبُوا connects conduct and consequence. In the second, كُلَّ مَا expresses recurring return, and فِيهَا identifies entanglement within fitnah.

Ethical significance and recurrence therefore arise from the full sentences, not the root alone.

Final definition of R K S:

Its principal field is turning something back or overturning it, or returning it to a former condition.

In human and extended uses, it can describe falling back into a situation, becoming caught again, or returning to a condition previously left.

Such return may occur in a negative setting, but the root alone does not mean unbelief, sin, bodily impurity, or punishment in the hereafter.

The two Qur’anic uses connect overturning with human conduct and return to fitnah. Understanding this requires attention to the verb’s form, sentence qualifications, and the people’s behavior.

For Forqan, “overturning” in Al-Nisāʾ 4:88 and “being overturned in fitnah” in Al-Nisāʾ 4:91 are suitable equivalents. Returning to an earlier condition and falling back into it should be clarified through lexical and contextual explanation.
