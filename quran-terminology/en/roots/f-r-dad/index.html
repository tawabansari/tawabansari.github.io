From Marking and Assigning a Share to an Appointed Matter

The root F R Ḍ in the Qur’an is not used only in the familiar juristic sense of “making obligatory.” Its uses extend from determining a financial right and separating out a known share, to undertaking Hajj, appointing the rulings of a surah, placing a responsibility upon someone, and appointing a way to release oaths. In one use, the form fāriḍ is used for an old cow; this branch should not be forced into the other uses without evidence.

The common axis visible in most Qur’anic uses is bringing something out of an open and undefined state and placing it within a definite limit, share, relation, or condition. Obligation may be the result of such determination and stabilization, but the root itself is not equal to compulsion in every context. Sometimes what is appointed is a duty upon a person, sometimes a right for him or her, sometimes a financial share, and sometimes a way of releasing a commitment.

This root appears eighteen times in the Qur’an in four forms. This limited but clear variety shows that mechanically translating every occurrence as “made obligatory” or “imposed” narrows the semantic field of the verses.

Important Forms and Derivatives of the Root

The Qur’anic forms of this root are:

  • فَرَضَ and its conjugations, nine times: determined, appointed, placed upon, or assigned for someone, according to context.
  • فَرِيضَة, six times: an appointed matter, right, share, or amount.
  • مَفْرُوض, two times: appointed, fixed, or separated out as a share.
  • فَارِض, once: old or advanced in age, about the cow in Al-Baqarah 2:68.

The noun farḍ itself does not occur in the Qur’an. The well-known plural farā’iḍ is also not a Qur’anic form, although it later became common, especially in the science of inheritance division. Likewise iftaraḍa, muftaraḍ in the sense “something assumed,” and the modern Persian/Arabic scientific word “hypothesis” are not Qur’anic forms. These later forms may be studied in the history of the word, but their later meanings should not be read back into Qur’anic usage.

Among these forms, farīḍah can structurally indicate something that has been appointed and determined. However, the morphological pattern by itself does not tell us whether that thing is a legal obligation, a financial amount, an inheritance share, or a right arising from an agreement. The context of each verse clarifies that difference.

Semantic Core of the Root

In most uses of the root, three elements appear close together: determination, delimitation, and stabilization. What is made farḍ is not merely imagined in the mind; a recognized limit, share, relation, path, or responsibility is set for it. For this reason, the root can be used for a woman’s financial right, an inheritance share, the directions of ṣadaqāt, the undertaking of Hajj, the Prophet’s responsibility before the Qur’an, or the appointed way to release an oath.

This core should not be equated with one juristic result. “Obligation” is a real part of the meaning in some verses, but in verses such as فَرَضَ اللَّهُ لَهُ or فَرَضَ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ تَحِلَّةَ أَيْمَانِكُمْ, the root appears in a context of removing constraint and providing an appointed way. Thus the root is not inherently heavy or light, positive or negative. Its determinative quality comes from the root itself; direction, addressee, and result come from the full structure of the verse.

To specify and appoint a matter, share, or relation so that it comes out of an open and undefined state.

“Placing upon” and “making obligatory” are important branches of this core, but they do not cover the whole root.

Material Image of the Root

In early lexical usage, farḍ can refer to making a mark, cut, notch, or groove in something. A clear example is the notch made at the end of a bow so the string can rest in its determined place. In wood or a tool, a mark or cut separates a point from the rest and gives it a defined function. In the same lexical field, usages such as marking, grooving, separating a share, and determining an amount are also found.

The value of this image is that it shows how an unclear matter comes to have a definite boundary and place. The notch of the bow is not a random cut; it is the fixed place where the string rests. The string sits there so it does not slip and the bow can function properly. Thus the material image of the root is not merely “cutting,” but making a definite mark so something can be placed in its proper location. From this angle, one may cautiously see a connection between this image and uses such as “appointed share,” “determined right,” or “fixed matter.”

At the level of contextual reflection, rights that the Qur’an calls farīḍah or mafrūḍ are taken out of the discretionary control of those with power and given a recognized place. This conclusion comes from the combined verses of inheritance, financial rights, and ṣadaqāt, not from the bow-notch alone. Therefore, one may speak of the social function of determination and stabilization, but one should not compare society to a bow or place elaborate social consequences inside the lexical definition of the root.

This image must not become an absolute theory. The Qur’an does not use the root for physical cutting in any occurrence. Therefore every farīḍah should not be translated as “a cut-off piece,” nor should every ruling be called a groove or cut. The material image helps understand the motion of the root, but translation must remain natural and contextual.

Use of the Root in Early Arabic

In early Arabic, فَرَضَ الخَشَبَةَ could refer to making a groove or mark in wood, and farḍ al-qaws was the determined place of the string on the bow. From this field, uses connected to separating and assigning a share expanded: فَرَضَ لَهُ في العَطاء means that a known share was appointed for someone in a grant or payment. The root was also used for maintenance or appointed payments, because the amount came out of uncertainty and became a recognized right or share.

These data show that the meaning “to appoint” was connected with determining a share, amount, place, and limit before becoming a purely juristic term. However, the exact historical transition from material notch to ruling and share cannot be reconstructed with certainty, nor can one lexical image be asserted as the source of all meanings. Semantic similarity by itself does not prove historical derivation.

The use of fāriḍ in the sense of old or aged is recorded within this lexical family, but even some lexicographers treated it as outside the central analogy of “mark and groove.” Some lexical and exegetical explanations say that an old animal has, as it were, “cut through” its years and reached their end. This may be an attempt to connect the branch of old age with the meaning of cutting, but it does not establish the history of derivation. Therefore the direct meaning of age should be preserved, and this connection should be mentioned only as a lexical possibility, not as the certain origin of the word. Explanations such as the cutting off of reproductive power or the splitting of years of life should not enter the base definition without independent evidence.

Challenges of Understanding and Translation

The most important problem in understanding this root is restricting it to the juristic term “farḍ.” In later fiqh usage, farḍ often refers to an act whose performance is binding. Later, some schools distinguished between farḍ and wājib, while others treated the two as synonyms. These distinctions belong to the history of fiqh and legal theory, not to the direct definition of the root in the Qur’an.

The word wājib as a legal or devotional category does not occur in the Qur’an. The root W J B appears in the Qur’an only once, in the expression فَإِذَا وَجَبَتْ جُنُوبُهَا, about the sides of an animal falling down. Therefore later technical differences between farḍ and wājib cannot be extracted from Qur’anic uses of the two words.

The second problem is the modern Persian meaning of “farz kardan.” In contemporary Persian this often means “to suppose,” “to assume temporarily,” or to set up a possibility for discussion. The scientific word “hypothesis” also refers to a proposition that still requires testing. This semantic field almost moves in the opposite direction from the main Qur’anic uses, because F R Ḍ in the Qur’an usually takes something out of indeterminacy and makes it specified, rather than merely placing it as a mental possibility.

The third problem is translating every case as “made obligatory.” Such a translation loses part of the meaning in نَصِيبًا مَفْرُوضًا, فَرَضَ اللَّهُ لَهُ, and فَرَضَ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ تَحِلَّةَ أَيْمَانِكُمْ. In these verses, appointed share, placed right, removal of constraint, and the provision of an appointed way are present in the context.

On the other side, reacting against the juristic translation should not lead to the opposite excess. One cannot say the root never indicates obligation. In verses related to the Qur’an, the rulings of a surah, financial rights, and appointed shares, the binding aspect can be quite real. The more precise statement is that obligation is one important result of determination and stabilization, not the only possible meaning of the root.

Methodological Note

To understand F R Ḍ, four levels must be distinguished.

  • First, the base lexical meaning: determining, placing a limit, or bringing something out of indeterminacy.
  • Second, the subject appointed in the verse, such as Hajj, a financial right, an inheritance share, the rulings of a surah, or the way to release an oath.
  • Third, the syntactic relation: whether something is appointed for someone, upon someone, or within a certain time.
  • Fourth, the legal or ethical result that comes from the full context.

These four levels should not be collapsed into one compressed translation. For example, in فَرَضَ اللَّهُ لَهُ, one cannot conclude from the root alone that the matter is “obligatory,” “permitted,” or “special.” The letter lām, the removal of constraint in the sentence, and the context of the verses are involved. Likewise, in فَرَضَ فِيهِنَّ الْحَجَّ, the person’s commitment to Hajj is understood from the human subject and the context of the rites, not from the root alone.

The adjective mafrūḍ is not inherently positive. In one verse it describes the appointed share of women and men in inheritance, and in another verse it is the speech of Satan about the share he wants to take from the servants. Thus the root only indicates that the share is specified; the justice or corruption of that share is understood from the speaker, the source of appointment, and the context.

Qur’anic Uses of the Root

Undertaking Hajj in the Known Months

الْحَجُّ أَشْهُرٌ مَعْلُومَاتٌ فَمَنْ فَرَضَ فِيهِنَّ الْحَجَّ فَلَا رَفَثَ وَلَا فُسُوقَ وَلَا جِدَالَ فِي الْحَجِّ

Hajj is in known months; so whoever undertakes Hajj in them, then in Hajj there is to be no sexual approach or speech, no fusūq, and no disputing.

Al-Baqarah 2:197

In this verse the subject of فَرَضَ is the human being himself. The verse does not say who made the original Hajj obligatory for everyone; it says that whoever makes Hajj definite for himself in the known months enters a state whose behavioral boundaries must be observed. Therefore translations such as “undertook Hajj,” “committed himself to Hajj,” or “made Hajj binding upon himself” are closer to the structure than the vague “made Hajj farḍ.”

The fī in فِيهِنَّ places this commitment within those known months. One should not build a theory beyond the verse from this preposition, but its presence shows that farḍ of Hajj here is tied to conscious entry into a determined time and state.

Determining a Financial Right in the Marital Bond

مَا لَمْ تَمَسُّوهُنَّ أَوْ تَفْرِضُوا لَهُنَّ فَرِيضَةً

So long as you have not touched them or appointed for them a fixed financial due.

Al-Baqarah 2:236

وَقَدْ فَرَضْتُمْ لَهُنَّ فَرِيضَةً فَنِصْفُ مَا فَرَضْتُمْ

While you have appointed for them a fixed due, then half of what you appointed.

Al-Baqarah 2:237

The expression تَفْرِضُوا لَهُنَّ فَرِيضَةً shows that a financial right is determined and recognized for the women. Farīḍah here is not merely the name of an abstract duty; it is an amount about which the verse can later say “half of what you appointed.” Thus determination, amount, and contractual responsibility are all present together.

The lām in لَهُنَّ shows the direction of the right: this amount is appointed for the women. Therefore “you made it obligatory upon them” would be an inverted and incorrect translation. The one who appoints the amount accepts a financial responsibility through that determination and establishes a right for the other party.

فَآتُوهُنَّ أُجُورَهُنَّ فَرِيضَةً وَلَا جُنَاحَ عَلَيْكُمْ فِيمَا تَرَاضَيْتُمْ بِهِ مِنْ بَعْدِ الْفَرِيضَةِ

So give them their financial dues as a fixed right; and there is no blame upon you in what you mutually agree to after the fixed due.

An-Nisa 4:24

In this verse, farīḍah first appears with the payment of ujūrahunna, then in the definite form al-farīḍah returns to that recognized right. The phrase مِنْ بَعْدِ الْفَرِيضَةِ shows that after the initial right has been stabilized, mutual consent still has a place. Thus “being appointed” does not necessarily negate later agreement; the verse itself brings together the fixed right and mutual agreement after it.

Determined Shares in Inheritance

لِلرِّجَالِ نَصِيبٌ ... وَلِلنِّسَاءِ نَصِيبٌ ... نَصِيبًا مَفْرُوضًا

For men is a share, and for women is a share; a share appointed and fixed, whether what is left is little or much.

An-Nisa 4:7

Mafrūḍ here is the adjective of naṣīb. Before all numerical ratios are stated, the verse removes the share of women and men from discretionary and uncertain treatment and calls it an appointed share. More exact amounts come in later verses. Therefore mafrūḍ here is better translated as “appointed” or “determined,” not as if all later inheritance details were contained inside this adjective.

آبَاؤُكُمْ وَأَبْنَاؤُكُمْ لَا تَدْرُونَ أَيُّهُمْ أَقْرَبُ لَكُمْ نَفْعًا فَرِيضَةً مِنَ اللَّهِ

Your fathers and your sons: you do not know which of them is nearer to you in benefit; an appointed ordinance from The God.

An-Nisa 4:11

After several inheritance ratios are mentioned, farīḍatan min The God states the source and firmness of this division. Here “farīḍah” includes both determination and obligation, because the shares have not been left to human preference or tribal custom. Still, the word should not be understood merely as a “required act of worship”; the subject in this verse is the division of financial rights among heirs.

Appointed Directions for Ṣadaqāt

إِنَّمَا الصَّدَقَاتُ لِلْفُقَرَاءِ ... وَابْنِ السَّبِيلِ فَرِيضَةً مِنَ اللَّهِ

The ṣadaqāt are only for the poor and the needy and the other named groups through the wayfarer; an appointed ordinance from The God.

At-Tawbah 9:60

Here farīḍatan min The God comes after the directions for the distribution of ṣadaqāt are named. The root indicates that the path of distribution is determined and restrained: these funds should not be spent without rule or according to the desire of someone in power. Yet farīḍah by itself does not state a percentage, collection time, governmental structure, or specific mechanism of execution. These details should not be inserted into the translation of the root without text.

The Share Satan Claims for Himself

وَقَالَ لَأَتَّخِذَنَّ مِنْ عِبَادِكَ نَصِيبًا مَفْرُوضًا

And he said: I will surely take from Your servants an appointed share for myself.

An-Nisa 4:118

This verse is a clear example of the ethical neutrality of the root. The same expression نَصِيبًا مَفْرُوضًا can indicate a fixed share, but here it occurs in Satan’s speech. Mafrūḍ points to his claim about a specified or separated share; it does not guarantee the legitimacy or realization of that claim. The negative quality comes from the speaker and his intention, not from the root F R Ḍ.

Appointing the Content of a Surah

سُورَةٌ أَنْزَلْنَاهَا وَفَرَضْنَاهَا وَأَنْزَلْنَا فِيهَا آيَاتٍ بَيِّنَاتٍ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَذَكَّرُونَ

A surah that We sent down and appointed, and in it We sent down clear signs, so that you may remember.

An-Nur 24:1

The pronoun hā in فَرَضْنَاهَا returns to the surah. The root here does not present the surah as a merely advisory text; its content contains appointed boundaries and matters. However, translating it as “We made all of it obligatory” may say more than the wording, because the verse itself does not explain that every sentence of the surah has one kind of ruling. “We appointed it” or “We made its boundaries and matters clear and appointed” is more cautious; the latter is an explanation and should not replace the base translation.

The Qur’an as an Appointed Responsibility upon the Prophet

إِنَّ الَّذِي فَرَضَ عَلَيْكَ الْقُرْآنَ لَرَادُّكَ إِلَى مَعَادٍ

Indeed, the One who appointed the Qur’an upon you will surely return you to a place of return.

Al-Qasas 28:85

The expression فَرَضَ عَلَيْكَ الْقُرْآنَ establishes a responsible relation between the Prophet and the Qur’an. The preposition ʿalā shows that the Qur’an has been placed upon him. But whether that responsibility includes receiving, reciting, conveying, acting, or all of these together must be understood from the wider context. Translation should not put one of these results in place of the base meaning without explanation.

Likewise, maʿād at the end of the verse is not determined in this article. The root F R Ḍ clarifies only the first part and should not be used to settle the location or quality of the return.

What Has Been Appointed for the Prophet or upon Other Believers

مَا كَانَ عَلَى النَّبِيِّ مِنْ حَرَجٍ فِيمَا فَرَضَ اللَّهُ لَهُ

There was no constraint upon the Prophet in what The God appointed for him.

Al-Ahzab 33:38

This use prevents us from translating فَرَضَ always as “placed a heavy burden upon someone.” Here the structure لَهُ and the phrase مَا كَانَ عَلَى النَّبِيِّ مِنْ حَرَجٍ show that the appointed matter works in the direction of removing constraint and defining what is permitted for him. “Appointed for him” is more natural than “made obligatory upon him.”

قَدْ عَلِمْنَا مَا فَرَضْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ فِي أَزْوَاجِهِمْ وَمَا مَلَكَتْ أَيْمَانُهُمْ

We know well what We appointed upon them concerning their spouses and what their right hands possessed.

Al-Ahzab 33:50

In this verse, عَلَيْهِمْ highlights relation of duty and responsibility. The difference between فَرَضَ اللَّهُ لَهُ in verse 38 and فَرَضْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ in verse 50 shows that preposition and context play a central role in the direction of meaning. This difference should not be turned into a mechanical rule for all Arabic, but in this context لَهُ harmonizes with the removal of constraint for the Prophet, while عَلَيْهِمْ harmonizes with regulations concerning other believers.

The Appointed Way to Release Oaths

قَدْ فَرَضَ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ تَحِلَّةَ أَيْمَانِكُمْ وَاللَّهُ مَوْلَاكُمْ

The God has appointed for you the way of releasing your oaths; and The God is your protector.

At-Tahrim 66:2

The object of فَرَضَ here is تَحِلَّةَ أَيْمَانِكُمْ: the way of release and opening from oaths. Thus farḍ in this verse does not create a dead end; it appoints a way to open a commitment formed by an oath. Translating it as “made the release of your oaths obligatory upon you” can weaken the direction of لَكُمْ and the releasing function of taḥillah.

Fāriḍ: An Old Cow

إِنَّهَا بَقَرَةٌ لَا فَارِضٌ وَلَا بِكْرٌ عَوَانٌ بَيْنَ ذَلِكَ

It is a cow neither old nor young, but middle-aged between that.

Al-Baqarah 2:68

The meaning of فَارِض is made clear by its contrast with بِكْر and by the explanation عَوَانٌ بَيْنَ ذَلِكَ. Here it is not “one who obligates,” “one who determines,” or “one with a groove”; it is an age-description of the cow. Some lexical explanations say that the animal has, as it were, cut through its age and reached its end, but that is a possible internal lexical connection, not the direct meaning of the verse or the certain history of derivation. Since this use occurs only once in the Qur’an, the translation should remain independent and simple: old, aged, or advanced in years.

Examination of Important Qur’anic Derivatives

Faraḍa

Faraḍa occurs in the Qur’an only in Form I. Its subject is sometimes a human being and sometimes The God. When a human being is the subject, he may determine a financial right or undertake Hajj. When the verb is attributed to The God, its object may be a surah, the Qur’an, family regulations, a matter specific to the Prophet, or the way to release oaths.

Therefore the meaning of the verb should not be determined solely by the subject. Even in divine usage, faraḍa sometimes comes with ʿalā and sometimes with li-. Divine power and knowledge are not like human action, but the linguistic core remains determination and appointment. Attributing this verb to The God does not require a bodily image of cutting or marking.

Farīḍah

Farīḍah in the Qur’an appears mostly in the field of financial rights and shares: the appointed right in the marital bond, inheritance shares, and the directions of ṣadaqāt. This pattern shows that farīḍah is not merely mental; it affects real human relations and brings a right or responsibility out of ambiguity.

However, farīḍah in the Qur’an is not the general name for all acts of worship. The Qur’an does not call ṣalāt, ṣiyām, zakāt, or Hajj by the noun farīḍah in these eighteen uses. From this fact one should not conclude that these matters are not responsible obligations; one should only distinguish between Qur’anic linguistic data and later fiqh classification.

Mafrūḍ

Mafrūḍ in both Qur’anic uses is an adjective of naṣīb. In An-Nisa 4:7 it describes an appointed inheritance share, and in An-Nisa 4:118 it describes Satan’s claimed share from the servants. The morphological structure is the same in both, but the ethical direction differs. This example shows that the meaning of the derivative should be studied apart from the moral judgment of the context.

Fāriḍ

Fāriḍ occurs only in Al-Baqarah 2:68 and means old or aged. Formally it is an active participle, but in the verse it has become an established age-description. The active-participle pattern alone does not permit giving it the meaning “one who makes something farḍ.”

Grammatical and Structural Notes

One important feature of this root in the Qur’an is its changing relation with prepositions:

  • Faraḍa + li- in these uses can indicate direction of right, benefit, specification, or opening: the financial right for women, the appointed matter for the Prophet, and the way of releasing oaths for believers.
  • Faraḍa + ʿalā in the verses studied highlights something being placed upon the addressee: the Qur’an upon the Prophet and family regulations upon other believers.
  • Faraḍa + fī in the verse of Hajj places the commitment within the known months.

This formulation is the pattern of these Qur’anic uses and should not become a mechanical rule for the whole Arabic language. Nor does lām have one identical translation in every verse: in one place it is a financial right for women, in another a matter specific to the Prophet, and in another the way of releasing oaths for believers. The precise meaning still comes from the link between verb, preposition, object, and context.

In فَرَضَ فِيهِنَّ الْحَجَّ, the feminine plural pronoun in فِيهِنَّ returns to the known months. This structure places the undertaking of Hajj within its time-container. Translation should preserve this connection and should not leave fīhinna without effect.

In نَصِيبًا مَفْرُوضًا, mafrūḍan is the adjective of the share and describes how specified it is. In فَرِيضَةً مِنَ اللَّهِ, the accusative farīḍah can be explained grammatically in different ways, but its semantic role is clear: the division or directions just mentioned are presented as an appointed matter from The God. Possible disagreement over the grammatical label should not be turned into major theological differences.

In تَفْرِضُوا لَهُنَّ فَرِيضَةً, the closeness of the verb and same-root noun emphasizes the act of determining and the thing determined. Still, one should not call farīḍah a cognate accusative immediately only because of the shared root and then extract a special meaning from that. Its role in context is a measurable financial right.

Comparison with Nearby Roots

F R Ḍ Compared with Q D R; Appointment and Measuring

The root Q D R has a wide field of measure, capacity, proportion, assessment, and determination. F R Ḍ can also involve determining an amount, but its Qur’anic uses lean more toward appointing a share, right, matter, or specified relation.

Al-Baqarah 2:236 brings both roots together: أَوْ تَفْرِضُوا لَهُنَّ فَرِيضَةً concerns determining the financial right, while عَلَى الْمُوسِعِ قَدَرُهُ وَعَلَى الْمُقْتِرِ قَدَرُهُ concerns a measure suited to each person’s capacity. This pairing shows that farḍ and qadr are not identical: one appoints a right or matter, while the other can express proportional measure and capacity. This is a tendency, not an absolute rule for all Arabic usage.

Al-Ahzab 33:38 also brings فَرَضَ اللَّهُ لَهُ beside وَكَانَ أَمْرُ اللَّهِ قَدَرًا مَقْدُورًا. With two different roots, the verse expresses both the appointed matter for the Prophet and the measured certainty of The God’s command. The two should not be reduced to one translation.

F R Ḍ Compared with K T B; Appointing and Recording

K T B expands from writing, joining letters, and recording, and the Qur’an often uses it for appointed matters in the structure كُتِبَ عَلَيْكُمْ. F R Ḍ leans more toward specifying a share, limit, or definite matter, while K T B can emphasize recording, being written, and stabilization within a system or book.

In An-Nisa 4:24, the two nearby fields appear together: كِتَابَ اللَّهِ عَلَيْكُمْ, then فَآتُوهُنَّ أُجُورَهُنَّ فَرِيضَةً. The first expression points to the divine written and appointed matter in the overall structure of the verse, while the second highlights the specific financial right. Yet from one verse one cannot conclude that kataba is always general and faraḍa always particular; the group of uses only shows this tendency in this context.

F R Ḍ Compared with W Ṣ Y; Appointed Share and Continuing Instruction

W Ṣ Y in the language is connected with joining and conveying speech or responsibility to another. In the Qur’an, this root is used both for divine instruction and guidance and for a bequest a human being leaves concerning wealth or responsibilities after himself. Therefore waṣiyyah cannot simply be “flexible human will” while farīḍah is merely “decisive divine assignment” opposite to it; the Qur’an itself speaks of يُوصِيكُمُ اللَّهُ and even وَصِيَّةً مِنَ اللَّهِ.

An-Nisa 4:11 uses both roots in one structure. The verse begins with يُوصِيكُمُ اللَّهُ فِي أَوْلَادِكُمْ, states the appointed shares, makes their execution after وَصِيَّةٍ يُوصِي بِهَا أَوْ دَيْنٍ, and ends with فَرِيضَةً مِنَ اللَّهِ. Thus waṣiyyah and farīḍah do not erase each other in this verse; they stand together in one system.

In this context, waṣiyyah is a word or decision that must be carried from its owner to after him and executed, while farīḍah emphasizes that the stated shares are appointed by The God. The human will is not unlimited or immune from judgment; An-Nisa 4:12 mentions غَيْرَ مُضَارٍّ, and Al-Baqarah 2:182 recognizes the possibility of crookedness or sin in a bequest and the need for correction. On the other hand, the appointed inheritance shares are executed after bequest and debt. Thus the difference between the two is not a difference between “unrestricted human matter” and “divine ruling”; the main difference is their function in the sentence: waṣiyyah conveys something for execution, while farīḍah stabilizes a share or matter in its appointed place.

F R Ḍ Compared with W J B; Qur’anic Root and Juristic Term

In fiqh language, “farḍ” and “wājib” are sometimes synonyms and sometimes two different degrees of obligation. But this terminological comparison should not be attributed to the Qur’an itself. The root W J B appears in the Qur’an only with the meaning of falling down, and the word wājib as a legal-ruling title does not exist in the Qur’an.

Therefore, the Qur’anic difference between F R Ḍ and W J B is not that one is “definitive” and the other “probabilistic.” That distinction belongs to later legal systems. In Qur’anic root study, F R Ḍ should be understood from its own uses, not from the later classification of farḍ and wājib.

Translation Suggestions for Forqan

One fixed English equivalent is not suitable for every verse. The following suggestions should be chosen according to context:

  • فَرَضَ فِيهِنَّ الْحَجَّ: “undertook Hajj,” “committed himself to Hajj,” or “made Hajj binding upon himself.”
  • تَفْرِضُوا لَهُنَّ فَرِيضَةً: “appoint for them a fixed right or amount.”
  • فَرَضْتُمْ لَهُنَّ فَرِيضَةً: “you have appointed for them a fixed financial right.”
  • سُورَةٌ أَنْزَلْنَاهَا وَفَرَضْنَاهَا: in base translation, “a surah that We sent down and appointed”; explanation of boundaries and rulings can come separately.
  • فَرَضَ عَلَيْكَ الْقُرْآنَ: “appointed the Qur’an upon you,” or in explanation, “placed the responsibility of the Qur’an upon you.”
  • فَرَضَ اللَّهُ لَهُ: “The God appointed for him”; not automatically “made obligatory upon him.”
  • فَرَضْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ: “We appointed upon them.”
  • فَرَضَ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ تَحِلَّةَ أَيْمَانِكُمْ: “The God appointed for you the way of releasing your oaths.”
  • فَرِيضَة in financial rights: “appointed right,” “fixed amount,” “appointed share,” or “ordinance,” depending on the sentence.
  • فَرِيضَةً مِنَ اللَّهِ: “an appointed ordinance from The God”; where the binding aspect is prominent, “an appointed ruling from The God.”
  • نَصِيبًا مَفْرُوضًا: “an appointed share” or “a determined share.”
  • لَا فَارِضٌ وَلَا بِكْرٌ: “neither old nor young.”

The Persian expression “farz kard” should be used less in base translation, because for the modern reader it often means “assumed” or “supposed.” If the word “farḍ” is preserved, a brief explanation should accompany it so it is not confused with hypothesis or possibility.

Conclusion

The root F R Ḍ in most Qur’anic uses indicates determining, delimiting, and appointing. The material image of a groove or mark in something firm helps us understand how a place, share, or limit comes out of the unknown; but this image should not make translation physical or artificial.

From this core arise branches such as determining a financial right, stabilizing an inheritance share, specifying the directions of ṣadaqāt, undertaking Hajj, appointing the rulings of a surah, placing the responsibility of the Qur’an, and providing the way to release oaths. Obligation is real in several of these uses, but the whole root is not equal to “making obligatory.” The structures li-, ʿalā, and fī, the object of the verb, and the context of the verse determine the exact direction of meaning.

Comparison with Q D R, K T B, W Ṣ Y, and W J B also shows that farḍ is not merely measuring, not merely recording, not a replacement for bequest, and not the same as the later fiqh category “wājib.” In the inheritance verses, bequest, debt, and appointed shares stand together; farīḍah stabilizes the appointed position of the shares without removing the principle of bequest from the structure of the verse.

The form fāriḍ in the meaning of old is a special branch and should not be forcibly dissolved into the central image of groove and determination. Likewise, the modern meaning “hypothesis” and the juristic distinction between farḍ and wājib should not be imposed upon the Qur’an.

F R Ḍ: to specify and appoint a matter, share, right, or relation so that it comes out of an open and undefined state; and in some contexts, to place it as a binding responsibility.
