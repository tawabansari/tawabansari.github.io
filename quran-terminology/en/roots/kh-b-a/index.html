The Qur’anic Root KH B A

Qur’anic and lexical forms

Hiding, keeping something concealed, and what remains out of sight

The Arabic root KH B A concerns covering something and keeping it hidden. Its derivatives are used when someone puts property somewhere others cannot see it, sets something aside for future use, or hides from other people.

The word «خَبْء» (khabʾ) denotes something concealed or remaining out of sight. It may be stored property, an object inside a vessel, a seed beneath the soil, or something else that has not yet become apparent.

In early Arabic, hiding something did not always mean deception or improper secrecy. Someone might store food for the future, put property in a safe place for protection, or remain screened from view inside a tent. Being hidden does not itself determine the moral value of what is concealed.

The Qur’an uses this root in Al-Naml 27:25, where God is described as the one who brings forth «الْخَبْءَ» (al-khabʾa), what is hidden, in the heavens and the earth.

This use draws attention both to the existence of hidden things and to their being brought forth. Something concealed from human beings, or situated somewhere beyond their sight, can become apparent through God’s command and power.

Understanding the verse requires distinguishing “the hidden thing,” “bringing it forth,” and “knowing hidden matters.” These three concepts are connected in the verse, but expressed through different words and constructions.

1. Forms and semantic core of the root

The root KH B A occurs only once in the Qur’an, as «الْخَبْءَ» (al-khabʾa), in Al-Naml 27:25.

This word is a noun and functions as the object of «يُخْرِجُ» (yukhriju), “He brings forth.”

Other forms associated with this family are also recorded in Arabic:

  • خَبَأَ ـ يَخْبَأُ / يَخْبَؤُ (khabaʾa–yakhbaʾu / yakhbaʾu): to cover or hide something, or set it aside for later use.
  • خَبْء (khabʾ): hiding; as a noun, something hidden.
  • خَبِيء (khabīʾ): something hidden or concealed.
  • خَبِيئَة (khabīʾah): something concealed or stored.
  • خَبَايَا (khabāyā): the plural of khabīʾah; hidden or stored things.
  • خَبَّأَ (khabbaʾa): to hide something or keep it concealed.
  • أَخْبَأَ (akhbaʾa): to hide, preserve, or store something.
  • اِخْتَبَأَ (ikhtabaʾa): to hide oneself or conceal something somewhere.
  • تَخَبَّأَ (takhabbaʾa): to become hidden. The form «تَخَبَّى» (takhabbā) is also recorded for pitching a tent; this form without a hamzah and its relationship to the tent family require separate examination.
  • مَخْبَأ (makhbaʾ): a hiding place or a place for keeping something out of sight.
  • مُخَبَّأ (mukhabbaʾ): something or someone kept concealed.
  • خَابِيَة (khābiyah): a large vessel for storing water or other substances, whose hamzah has been omitted in common usage.
  • خِبَاء (khibāʾ): a tent or shelter of wool, hair, or animal fleece; its derivational relationship to KH B A is disputed.

These forms are recorded in Arabic dictionaries, but only al-khabʾa occurs in the Qur’an. The imperfect is also recorded with a fatḥah as «يَخْبَأُ» (yakhbaʾu), while «يَخْبَؤُ» appears in some early dictionary transmissions. These transmitted spellings should not be reduced to a categorical error without acknowledging the variation.

Semantic core

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris explains this lexical material as follows:

الْخَاءُ وَالْبَاءُ وَالْحَرْفُ الْمُعْتَلُّ وَالْهَمْزَةُ يَدُلُّ عَلَى سَتْرِ الشَّيْءِ

al-khāʾu wa-al-bāʾu wa-al-ḥarfu al-muʿtallu wa-al-hamzatu yadullu ʿalā satri al-shayʾi

That is, this lexical material indicates covering something.

He then explains «خَبَأْتُ الشَّيْءَ» (khabaʾtu al-shayʾa), “I concealed the thing,” in this sense.

This definition establishes a clear relationship between the act of hiding and what has been hidden.

For example, when someone puts an object under a cloth, the action is concealment. Once covered, the object is called khabʾ or khabīʾ.

Lisān al-ʿArab also states:

الْخَبْءُ: مَا خُبِئَ

al-khabʾu: mā khubiʾa

That is, khabʾ is something that has been hidden.

Here a verbal noun is used to denote the thing upon which the act of hiding has been performed.

Two aspects should therefore be distinguished: khabaʾa is the verb “to hide,” whereas al-khabʾ in the Qur’anic use denotes the hidden thing itself.

Taken together, the evidence suggests the following central meaning: covering something or keeping it out of sight and, in nominal use, what remains hidden or concealed.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Lisān al-ʿArab: covering and what is concealed

Being hidden does not necessarily mean being nonexistent. Something may exist without having been seen, or without its location being apparent to someone.

Nor has every hidden thing necessarily been concealed to deceive. Nature, distance, a covering, or the limitations of human knowledge may keep something out of sight.

2. Uses in early Arabic and everyday life

Keeping property and food for the future

One established use of khabaʾa is setting something aside and storing it.

Dictionaries explain this verb not only through «سَتَرَهُ» (satarahu), “he covered it,” but also through «حَفِظَهُ» (ḥafiẓahu) and «ادَّخَرَهُ» (iddakharahu): he preserved it or stored it for the future.

Someone might, for example, keep some food in a vessel for later use.

The food is hidden or stored in this situation, but concealing it is not improper.

In «خَبِيئَةٌ لِيَوْمِ الْحَاجَةِ» (khabīʾatun li-yawmi al-ḥājah), something is set aside for a day of need.

This use shows that hiding and storing may occur in the same action. Nevertheless, the two meanings are not identical. Something may be stored for the future in a location known to others, or hidden without any intention of using it later.

Storage is therefore one use of the root, rather than a definition encompassing all its forms.

A vessel that conceals and preserves

In Arabic, «خَابِيَة» (khābiyah) denotes a large vessel in which water or other things are kept.

Lexicographers explain that its earlier form involved a hamzah, which was omitted through frequent usage.

Here the vessel provides a means of preserving and keeping what is inside it.

«خِبَاء» (khibāʾ) is also used for a tent or shelter.

A tent separates its interior from its surroundings and can screen people and belongings from view.

Ibn Fāris includes this use in a lexical entry encompassing both hamzated and weak-letter forms. The precise morphological origin of khibāʾ is disputed: some lexicographers examine it under KH B W and others consider a yāʾ origin. His wording therefore does not mean that KH B A itself is a four-consonant root.

A semantic relationship between covering and a tent is apparent. Without historical investigation, however, it cannot be asserted that all tent-related forms necessarily derive from khabaʾa.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: differing hamzah, wāw, and yāʾ analyses of khibāʾ

A pre-Islamic poetic witness: someone screened from view

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Muhalhil ibn Rabīʿah states:

عَلَى أَنْ لَيْسَ يُوفَى مِنْ كُلَيْبٍ
إِذَا بَرَزَتْ مُخَبَّأَةُ الْخُدُورِ

ʿalā an laysa yūfā min kulaybin / idhā barazat mukhabbaʾatu al-khudūri

A rendering close to the sense:

“Even so, vengeance for Kulayb is not fully satisfied when the woman secluded behind curtains comes forth.”

The line occurs in a context of vengeance and remembrance of Kulayb’s killing.

«مُخَبَّأَةُ الْخُدُورِ» (mukhabbaʾatu al-khudūri) refers to a woman kept within a screened, curtained space.

Mukhabbaʾah belongs to this family and describes someone’s being screened or kept concealed.

Concealment here does not mean destruction or nonexistence. The person is present but hidden from outsiders; «بَرَزَتْ» (barazat), “she came forth,” then expresses her emergence.

This contrast between being concealed and coming forth is also linguistically useful for understanding «يُخْرِجُ الْخَبْءَ» (yukhriju al-khabʾa), “He brings forth what is hidden,” in the Qur’an.

This witness alone, however, makes no judgment about the moral value of seclusion or women’s social position. For root research, what matters is the meaning of mukhabbaʾah within the poetic construction.

The poem is attributed to Muhalhil, a pre-Islamic poet, and appears in collections of poetry attributed to him. This study accepts the attribution as a transmitted attribution in the poetic collection, rather than independent evidence establishing the exact date of each line.

Khibāʾ in the poetry of Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd

One transmitted version of the Muʿallaqah of Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd, a pre-Islamic poet, reads:

وَتَقْصِيرُ يَوْمِ الدَّجْنِ وَالدَّجْنُ مُعْجِبٌ
بِبَهْكَنَةٍ تَحْتَ الْخِبَاءِ الْمُعَمَّدِ

wa-taqṣīru yawmi al-dajni wa-al-dajnu muʿjibun / bi-bahkanatin taḥta al-khibāʾi al-muʿammadi

A rendering close to the sense:

“And shortening a cloudy day—a delightful day—beside a well-proportioned woman beneath a pillared tent.”

In this line, al-khibāʾ al-muʿammad is a tent supported by poles. Another widely transmitted version has «الطِّرَافِ» (al-ṭirāfi) in place of «الْخِبَاءِ» (al-khibāʾi). Early commentaries record both versions; citing khibāʾ in this line therefore depends on this particular transmission.

Sharḥ al-Muʿallaqāt al-Tisʿ: khibāʾ as a transmitted alternative to ṭirāf

This use illustrates the presence of tents in everyday Arab life and their role as screened spaces.

Since the derivation of khibāʾ is disputed, this line alone cannot establish that all meanings of KH B A originated from the name of a tent.

The witnesses involving mukhabbaʾah and khibāʾ illustrate two related matters: keeping a person screened from view, and a shelter separating its interior from the outside.

Khabāyā al-arḍ: what the earth holds hidden

Arabic uses «خَبَايَا الْأَرْضِ» (khabāyā al-arḍ) for things hidden in the earth.

A poem attributed to Ibn Shihāb al-Zuhrī states:

تَتَبَّعْ خَبَايَا الْأَرْضِ وَادْعُ مَلِيكَهَا
لَعَلَّكَ يَوْمًا أَنْ تُجَابَ وَتُرْزَقَا

tatabbaʿ khabāyā al-arḍi wa-udʿu malīkahā / laʿallaka yawman an tujāba wa-turzaqā

Translation:

“Seek out the earth’s hidden things and call upon its Sovereign; perhaps one day your prayer will be answered and you will receive provision.”

Lexicographical accounts relate this expression to cultivation and agriculture.

A farmer places a seed in the soil. It becomes hidden from view, but under suitable conditions it can grow and a plant can emerge from the earth.

Other explanations allow khabāyā al-arḍ to include such things as materials and reserves hidden in the ground.

This breadth shows that khabāyā is not exclusively the name of a particular agricultural product or mineral.

The attribution to Ibn Shihāb al-Zuhrī and its transmission in Islamic-period sources also prevent presenting it with certainty as pre-Islamic poetry. Its value lies in documenting an established classical Arabic expression. Some sources transmit «فَتُرْزَقَا» (fa-turzaqā), “and consequently receive provision,” instead of «وَتُرْزَقَا» (wa-turzaqā), “and receive provision,” at the end of the second hemistich. This variation does not change the meaning of khabāyā.

Ibn ʿAbd al-Barr, Al-Tamhīd: attribution to al-Zuhrī and the fa-turzaqā wording

3. Qur’anic use: bringing forth what is hidden in the heavens and the earth

أَلَّا يَسْجُدُوا لِلَّهِ الَّذِي يُخْرِجُ الْخَبْءَ فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ وَيَعْلَمُ مَا تُخْفُونَ وَمَا تُعْلِنُونَ

allā yasjudū li-llāhi alladhī yukhriju al-khabʾa fī al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍi wa-yaʿlamu mā tukhfūna wa-mā tuʿlinūna

Proposed translation for this study:

“So that they do not prostrate to The God, who brings forth what is hidden in the heavens and the earth, and knows what you conceal and what you disclose.”

Al-Naml 27:25

This is the only Qur’anic occurrence of the root KH B A.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the sole occurrence, Al-Naml 27:25, word six

The verse occurs within the account of Solomon and the hoopoe.

In Al-Naml 27:22, the hoopoe brings news from the land of Sabaʾ. In Al-Naml 27:23, it describes a woman who rules the people and possesses a magnificent throne.

In Al-Naml 27:24, the hoopoe explains that it found the woman and her people prostrating to the sun instead of God. The account then mentions their deeds being made attractive to them and their being diverted from the path of guidance.

The expression concerning prostration to God and the description «الَّذِي يُخْرِجُ الْخَبْءَ» (alladhī yukhriju al-khabʾa), “who brings forth what is hidden,” then follow. In the quoted reading «أَلَّا يَسْجُدُوا» (allā yasjudū), the phrase is read as an lā and connected to the preceding verse. “So that they do not prostrate” therefore continues that context; it is not an independent command to refrain from prostration. An imperative reading, «أَلا يَا اسْجُدُوا» (alā yā usjudū), is also reported in the sources, but its grammatical analysis and translation must remain distinct.

Al-Ṭabarī: distinguishing the an lā reading from the imperative reading

This setting matters because the contrast is between prostration to the sun and prostration to God.

The sun is a visible phenomenon of the world. The verse, however, directs attention to God, whose power extends beyond visible phenomena to what is hidden in the heavens and the earth.

Al-khabʾa: the hidden thing itself, rather than the act of hiding

In yukhriju al-khabʾa, al-khabʾ is the object of yukhriju.

In other words, God brings forth something that is hidden.

Al-khabʾ here is not the name of the act of concealment. Yukhriju, moreover, comes from KH R J and means bringing forth.

Two different concepts thus stand together: what is concealed, and bringing it forth from concealment.

Persian can render this expression as “He brings forth the hidden thing” or “He makes what is hidden apparent.”

The first is closer to the construction of the two Arabic words, because it retains the movement from concealment to emergence.

“Makes apparent” can nevertheless convey the result of bringing something forth in some settings.

The translation should not be limited to uncovering mental secrets or hidden thoughts. Al-khabʾ can also denote material things.

“In the heavens and the earth”: where are the hidden things?

The verse places al-khabʾ alongside «فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ» (fī al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍi), “in the heavens and the earth.”

The preposition fī identifies the location of what is hidden: in the heavens and the earth.

It should not be automatically replaced with min, “from,” without justification, as though the verse spoke only of something emerging from a physical container.

At the same time, yukhriju indicates that something moves from a hidden condition to another state. In ordinary language, “bringing forth from concealment” may therefore also be an appropriate explanation.

Al-Ṭabarī identifies rain and vegetation as instances of khabʾ in his explanation of the verse. Early exegetical reports apply “the hidden thing of the sky” to rain and “the hidden thing of the earth” to vegetation and provision concealed in the ground.

These examples are understandable in terms of ordinary experience of nature.

Before rain falls, a person does not see it as water descending onto the earth. A seed placed in the soil is also hidden from view before germination.

Rain and vegetation can consequently serve as examples of things emerging after having been concealed.

But al-khabʾ does not exclusively mean “rain” or “plants” in Arabic. These are exegetical examples falling within the broader meaning of concealment.

Nor should the verse be taken to mean that all plants lie fully formed beneath the soil and are simply pulled out. Plant growth is a process in which the seed develops and new parts form.

The Qur’an is not discussing the biological details of growth or the mechanism of rainfall here. It expresses God’s ability to bring forth hidden things.

Al-Ṭabarī: rain and vegetation as instances of khabʾ

Is al-khabʾ related to the unseen?

Some early explanations apply al-khabʾ to anything concealed and absent from view.

This reading fits the root’s lexical meaning: something concealed from human sight or awareness may also fall within the semantic field of the unseen.

Nevertheless, “the hidden thing” and “the unseen” should be distinguished.

Someone may know that some seeds are hidden in the soil without seeing them. The seeds are concealed from that person’s eyes, but their existence is not necessarily unknown.

Conversely, something may lie beyond human knowledge without anyone having deliberately hidden it.

Khabʾ thus emphasizes the hidden thing, whereas ghayb can encompass a broader field of matters beyond someone’s observation or awareness.

In Al-Naml 27:25, “what is hidden” accommodates material examples such as seeds and rain, while allowing a scope extending beyond them.

Restricting it to one particular kind of hidden thing requires further contextual evidence.

4. Bringing forth hidden things and knowing hidden matters

After yukhriju al-khabʾa, the verse states:

«وَيَعْلَمُ مَا تُخْفُونَ وَمَا تُعْلِنُونَ» (wa-yaʿlamu mā tukhfūna wa-mā tuʿlinūna), “and He knows what you conceal and what you disclose.”

That is, God knows what you hide and what you make apparent.

This part relates to the first expression but does not repeat the same meaning.

The first expression concerns bringing forth something hidden.

The second concerns knowing hidden and apparent matters.

These two abilities are conceptually different.

A human being might know that something hidden exists but be unable to bring it out of its hiding place.

Conversely, someone might discover and dig something out of the soil without previously knowing that it existed or where exactly it was located.

The verse describes God both as bringing forth hidden things and as knowing what is concealed and apparent.

This pairing places divine power and knowledge together without making the two verbs synonymous.

Tukhfūna and tuʿlinūna

In this phrase, tukhfūna comes from KH F Y, and tuʿlinūna from ʿ L N.

The verb expressing concealment in the second part of the verse therefore does not come from KH B A.

This lexical distinction matters. The Qur’an uses a separate verb for “what you conceal,” while using the noun al-khabʾ for “the hidden thing that God brings forth.”

In mā tukhfūna wa-mā tuʿlinūna, “what you conceal and what you disclose,” the acts of hiding and making apparent are attributed to the addressees.

In yukhriju al-khabʾa, however, God is the agent of bringing forth. The verse does not require every hidden thing to have been hidden by a human being.

Two situations can thus be distinguished: something that someone deliberately conceals, and something remaining out of human sight because of its concealed location.

Both may fall within the field of hiddenness, but the nature of their concealment differs.

The connection with prostration to the sun

In the hoopoe’s account, the people of Sabaʾ prostrated to the sun, whose light and visible presence are tangible in human life.

In contrast, the verse presents God as the one who brings forth hidden things and knows the concealed and the apparent.

This contrast shows that the verse’s argument does not rest merely on a phenomenon’s visibility or outward effects.

Despite its size and natural role, the sun is not presented in the Qur’an as the true deity. God is the one deity to whom creation, power, and encompassing knowledge are attributed.

This point emerges from the combined context of Al-Naml 27:23, Al-Naml 27:24, Al-Naml 27:25, and Al-Naml 27:26. It does not mean that al-khabʾ alone signifies divine oneness, prostration, or a deity.

Nor does the verse explain the physical manner in which all hidden things in the heavens are brought forth. A specific scientific theory about the universe’s structure or all phenomena yet to be discovered cannot be extracted from it.

5. Distinctions from related roots

KH B A (khabʾ) and GH Y B (ghayb): the hidden thing and what is absent from view

Ghayb concerns what lies beyond someone’s observation or awareness.

In its established use, khabʾ denotes something hidden or kept concealed.

Property concealed somewhere is khabʾ, for example, even if its owner knows where it is.

The same property can lie beyond the awareness of someone who knows neither of its existence nor of its location.

These concepts therefore overlap without being identical.

In Al-Naml 27:25, al-khabʾ principally denotes what God brings forth. Al-Anʿām 6:59 discusses the keys of the unseen and God’s knowledge of hidden matters.

The two verses are related in their treatment of God’s knowledge and power, but their different words should be preserved.

Study of GH Y B

KH B A (khabʾ) and KH F Y (khafiyy): hiddenness and concealment

Khafiya and its derivatives are used in the Qur’an for being hidden or hiding something.

In Al-Naml 27:25, both families occur in the same verse: al-khabʾ from KH B A and tukhfūna from KH F Y.

Al-khabʾ there denotes the hidden thing itself, whereas tukhfūna expresses the act of hiding.

This distinction partly arises from the words’ grammatical forms, since one is a noun and the other a verb. Their occurrence together alone should not establish an absolute boundary between the roots throughout Arabic.

When translating this particular verse, however, distinguishing the hidden thing from the act of hiding is essential.

KH B A (khabʾ) and K T M (katm): hiding something and withholding information

In the Qur’an, kitmān commonly concerns concealing a truth, testimony, or something known that a person could disclose.

Al-Baqarah 2:159 condemns concealing clear signs and guidance after they have been explained in the Scripture.

Khabaʾa, however, can concern hiding a material object, such as property stored for the future.

Withholding information can therefore involve a kind of concealment, but not every khabʾ necessarily entails concealing truth or deceiving others.

Nor should the moral weight of knowingly suppressing the truth be transferred to every use of KH B A.

Study of K T M

KH B A (khabʾ) and DH KH R (iddikhār): keeping concealed and storing

Iddikhār concerns keeping something for future use. Its root is DH KH R, with dhāl; that consonant changes and assimilates in iddakhara. It should not be confused with D KH R, which concerns abasement and humility.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: DH KH R in taddakhirūna, Āl ʿImrān 3:49

Āl ʿImrān 3:49 speaks of informing people about what they eat and what they store in their houses.

This use is related to khabaʾa because something set aside for the future may be kept hidden somewhere.

Storage is not always concealment, however. Someone can keep food for the future in a visible place.

Conversely, someone may hide something without intending to use it later.

Iddikhār thus highlights the purpose of keeping something, whereas khabʾ emphasizes concealment and hiddenness.

KH B A (khabʾ) and KH B W (khubūw): concealment and a fire’s subsiding

KH B W resembles KH B A in spelling and pronunciation, but has a different Qur’anic meaning.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:97, «خَبَتْ» (khabat) concerns the subsiding of a fire’s flame.

The subject there is a flame’s diminishing or dying down, rather than concealing an object.

Lexicographical sources do discuss overlap and differing morphological analyses of some forms associated with these two families, including khibāʾ.

Nevertheless, al-khabʾ in Al-Naml 27:25 cannot be translated as a fire’s subsiding.

This example shows that similarity between Arabic forms, or alternation between hamzah and a weak letter in certain derivatives, does not justify merging two roots.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

In Al-Naml 27:25, the Qur’anic form is al-khabʾa.

Suitable Persian equivalents for the word include “the hidden thing,” “what is hidden,” and “what has remained concealed.”

In yukhriju al-khabʾa, “He brings forth what is hidden” is closer to the Arabic construction.

“He makes what is hidden apparent” is also fluent, but expresses the result of bringing forth in place of the movement itself.

“He brings rain out of the sky and vegetation out of the earth” can serve as an explanation through examples. It should not replace a direct translation of al-khabʾ, however, since these two examples are not explicitly named in the verse.

“He reveals the secrets of the heavens and the earth” may also restrict the meaning too heavily to hidden information.

The principal proposal is therefore to translate khabʾ as “what is hidden” in the base translation, while explaining rain, vegetation, and other hidden things separately.

Brief root meaning and meaning of the word in the verse

الْخَبْءَ (al-khabʾa) — Al-Naml 27:25

  • Root: KH B A.
  • Grammatical form: A definite, accusative noun; the object of yukhriju, “He brings forth.”
  • Brief root meaning: Covering and keeping concealed; khabʾ: something hidden or concealed.
  • Meaning in this verse: What is hidden in the heavens and the earth and which God brings forth.

This distinction keeps the root’s lexical meaning separate from the act of bringing forth and its object in the verse.

Rain and vegetation are also excluded from the word’s brief definition, since they are examples to be examined within the broader meaning of al-khabʾ.

7. Conclusion

The Arabic root KH B A concerns covering, hiding, and keeping something out of sight. As a noun, khabʾ denotes something that has remained hidden.

Early Arabic evidence illustrates this meaning through keeping property and food, a person screened behind curtains, vessels and shelters, and things concealed in the earth.

Concealment in this family does not necessarily carry a negative moral meaning. Something may be kept for protection or future use, or simply remain out of human sight because of its natural location.

The Qur’an uses this root only in Al-Naml 27:25. There God is described as the one who brings forth what is hidden in the heavens and the earth, and knows what human beings conceal or disclose.

The two parts of the verse are connected but not synonymous: the first concerns bringing forth hidden things, and the second God’s knowledge of the concealed and the apparent.

Rain and vegetation are established examples of al-khabʾ in early explanations, but the word itself is not limited to them.

Final definition: The root KH B A indicates covering something and keeping it concealed; khabʾ denotes what has remained hidden or covered. In Al-Naml 27:25, it refers to the hidden thing that God brings forth in the heavens and the earth. Its meaning is confined neither to mental secrets nor to rain and vegetation; the precise referent is identified through context. Brief root meaning: Covering and keeping concealed; khabʾ: something hidden or concealed.
