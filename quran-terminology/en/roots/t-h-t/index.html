Linguistic Note

ت ح ت (T Ḥ T) is mostly a directional and spatial word in Arabic. Its base meaning is “under” or “beneath.” In the Qur’an, however, this being-under is not always a simple geometric position. Sometimes it marks foundation, something on which another thing stands. Sometimes it marks hiddenness, something unseen yet effective. Sometimes it stands opposite فوق (fawq), “above.” Sometimes it marks a surrounding field of support, as in rivers flowing beneath gardens and feeding their life from below.

So T Ḥ T should not always be flattened into simple “under.” The question is: under what? Under a garden, under feet, under a tree, under a wall, beneath soil, or within a social and family relation?

Central Meaning

T Ḥ T in the Qur’an points to under, beneath, lower position, lower side, or a foundational level of something. Sometimes it marks physical location, such as under a tree, under the earth, under feet, or beneath gardens. At other times it marks structural and relational placement, such as being within the bond, guardianship, or household relation of another person.

Material Image

The material image is something located below: roots under soil, water beneath a garden, treasure under a wall, earth under the feet, or a person standing beneath the shade of a tree. Being lower can be hidden, but it is not therefore unimportant. Sometimes what is below is the foundation of what appears above.

In the Qur’an, تَجْرِي مِن تَحْتِهَا الْأَنْهَارُ (tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhār) is a key image. The garden is visible, but the rivers run beneath it. The visible life of the garden is fed by a lower flow. In the story of the wall and the treasure, the wall is seen, but the value is hidden beneath it. Here taḥt points to a lower protected layer, where something important remains hidden until the right time.

Classical Language Note

In classical Arabic, تحت (taḥt) stands opposite فوق (fawq). Fawq marks above, over, upper side, or higher level; taḥt marks below, under, lower side, or lower level. This contrast is used in orientation, buildings, earth, movement, ownership, authority, and social relation.

The Qur’an keeps this contrast alive. It speaks of above the heads, beneath the feet, beneath gardens, under the tree, beneath soil, and under the wall. The word is simple, but in Qur’anic contexts it gains strong visual and structural force.

Beneath Gardens: The Hidden Flow of Life

One of the most frequent uses of taḥt in the Qur’an is the phrase تَجْرِي مِن تَحْتِهَا الْأَنْهَارُ (tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhār). In 2:25, the Qur’an says: أَنَّ لَهُمْ جَنَّاتٍ تَجْرِي مِن تَحْتِهَا الْأَنْهَارُ (anna lahum jannātin tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhār).

Min taḥtihā shows that the rivers flow from beneath the gardens or through their lower level. The image is not only decorative. It is an image of sustained life. A garden beneath which rivers flow is alive from its foundation.

In 9:72, the same image appears with abiding: جَنَّاتٍ تَجْرِي مِن تَحْتِهَا الْأَنْهَارُ خَالِدِينَ فِيهَا (jannātin tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhār khālidīna fīhā). Blessing is not merely on the surface; it has life from below.

Under the Tree: Place of Pledge and Presence

In Sūrat al-Fatḥ, the Qur’an says: لَقَدْ رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنِ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ إِذْ يُبَايِعُونَكَ تَحْتَ الشَّجَرَةِ (laqad raḍiya Allāhu ʿani al-mu’minīna idh yubāyiʿūnaka taḥta al-shajarah) in 48:18.

Taḥta al-shajarah marks the physical place of the pledge. Yet within the verse, that place becomes a place of covenant, tranquility, and divine pleasure. Being under the tree is not merely a geographic note; it is the setting of an act of faith.

Under the Feet: Earthly Foundation, Provision, and Punishment

The Qur’an sometimes brings taḥt into relation with feet. In 5:66, it says: لَأَكَلُوا مِن فَوْقِهِمْ وَمِن تَحْتِ أَرْجُلِهِم (la-akalū min fawqihim wa-min taḥti arjulihim). If they had upheld what was revealed, they would have eaten from above them and from beneath their feet.

Here min fawqihim and min taḥti arjulihim form an image of provision from every direction, from the sky and from the earth. In contrast, punishment can also be pictured from these directions. In 6:65, the Qur’an says: عَذَابًا مِّن فَوْقِكُمْ أَوْ مِن تَحْتِ أَرْجُلِكُمْ (ʿadhāban min fawqikum aw min taḥti arjulikum).

What lies beneath human feet can be the place of stability, provision, and settlement. But if the divine order turns against a people, that same lower ground may become the direction of upheaval and punishment.

Beneath Soil and Beneath al-Tharā

In Sūrat Ṭā Hā, the Qur’an says: لَهُ مَا فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَمَا فِي الْأَرْضِ وَمَا بَيْنَهُمَا وَمَا تَحْتَ الثَّرَىٰ (lahu mā fī al-samāwāti wa-mā fī al-arḍi wa-mā baynahumā wa-mā taḥta al-tharā) in 20:6.

Al-tharā refers to moist soil or the soil layers of the earth. Mā taḥta al-tharā means what lies beneath the soil, in the hidden depths of the earth. The verse extends the ownership of The God to the lowest and most hidden layers. Nothing, even what lies beneath soil and beyond human sight, is outside His dominion.

Beneath the Wall: Hidden Foundation and Preserved Trust

In the story of Moses and the righteous servant, the Qur’an says: وَأَمَّا الْجِدَارُ فَكَانَ لِغُلَامَيْنِ يَتِيمَيْنِ فِي الْمَدِينَةِ وَكَانَ تَحْتَهُ كَنزٌ لَّهُمَا (wa-ammā al-jidāru fa-kāna li-ghulāmayni yatīmayni fī al-madīnah wa-kāna taḥtahu kanzun lahumā) in 18:82.

Taḥtahu points to beneath the wall, a hidden place rather than an exposed one. The treasure is under the wall, and the wall protects it. Being below here does not mean being worthless. The treasure is below. A valuable truth or trust may remain hidden beneath the visible structure until its time arrives.

Beneath the Person: Support and Calm

In the story of Maryam, the Qur’an says: فَنَادَاهَا مِن تَحْتِهَا أَلَّا تَحْزَنِي قَدْ جَعَلَ رَبُّكِ تَحْتَكِ سَرِيًّا (fa-nādāhā min taḥtihā allā taḥzanī qad jaʿala rabbuki taḥtaki sariyyā) in 19:24.

Both min taḥtihā and taḥtaki appear in this verse. A call comes from beneath her, and a stream is placed beneath her. In this setting, taḥt comes close to support, calm, and provision. In a moment of pain and isolation, Maryam finds a sign of life and relief below her.

Taḥt in Family and Social Relation

In Sūrat al-Taḥrīm, the Qur’an says: كَانَتَا تَحْتَ عَبْدَيْنِ مِنْ عِبَادِنَا صَالِحَيْنِ فَخَانَتَاهُمَا (kānatā taḥta ʿabdayni min ʿibādinā ṣāliḥayni fa-khānatāhumā) in 66:10.

In this verse, taḥta ʿabdayn is not simple physical placement. It means that the two women were in marital and household relation with two righteous servants. A literal rendering such as “under two servants” is misleading and may create an unintended tone of domination or contempt. The meaning is better carried by “in marital bond with,” “within the household bond of,” or “in relation with.”

At the same time, the verse warns that being connected to righteous people does not automatically save a person. Outward relation is not enough; the truth of a person is shown through faith, action, and loyalty.

Taḥt and Fawq: Directions of Surrounding

The Qur’an often places above and below together. This contrast is not merely geometric; it marks surrounding, totality, and the structure of the world. In 5:66, provision appears from above and below. In 6:65, punishment is pictured from above or beneath the feet. In 20:6, the dominion of The God includes the heavens, the earth, what is between them, and what lies beneath the soil.

Thus taḥt often gains meaning within a network of relations: fawq, maʿ, bayn, earth, rivers, feet, tree, soil, and wall.

Hidden and Supporting Level

One important feature in Qur’anic uses of this word is that taḥt sometimes points to a hidden and supporting level. What appears above may rest on what is below.

In jannah, the garden is visible, but the rivers flowing beneath it sustain its life. In the story of al-Kahf, the wall is visible, but the treasure is hidden in the lower layer. In the story of Maryam, pain and isolation are visible, but a stream of divine support is placed below her.

This meaning should not be forced onto every use of taḥt. Sometimes taḥt is simply a place adverb, such as under the tree. But in some contexts, the Qur’an uses this simple word to reveal a hidden supporting foundation.

Distinguishing Qur’anic Uses

In 2:25 and 9:72, taḥt appears in تَجْرِي مِن تَحْتِهَا الْأَنْهَارُ (tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhār), pointing to beneath the gardens and the foundational flow of rivers. In 48:18, تَحْتَ الشَّجَرَةِ (taḥta al-shajarah) is the place of pledge. In 5:66, مِن تَحْتِ أَرْجُلِهِمْ (min taḥti arjulihim) marks earthly provision and comprehensive blessing. In 6:65, مِن تَحْتِ أَرْجُلِكُمْ (min taḥti arjulikum) marks punishment from below.

In 20:6, مَا تَحْتَ الثَّرَىٰ (mā taḥta al-tharā) points to the hidden lower layers of the earth. In 18:82, تَحْتَهُ كَنزٌ (taḥtahu kanzun) points to the hidden treasure beneath the wall. In 19:24, تَحْتَكِ سَرِيًّا (taḥtaki sariyyā) points to a stream below and near Maryam. In 66:10, تَحْتَ عَبْدَيْنِ (taḥta ʿabdayn) marks family and household relation, not physical underness.

Grammatical and Structural Notes

In تَجْرِي مِن تَحْتِهَا الْأَنْهَارُ (tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhār), min marks the source or direction of flow, and taḥtihā marks the lower place beneath the gardens. In تَحْتَ الشَّجَرَةِ (taḥta al-shajarah), taḥt is a place adverb: the pledge occurred under the tree.

In مِن تَحْتِ أَرْجُلِهِمْ (min taḥti arjulihim), the connection with feet marks the lowest human point of contact with the earth. In مَا تَحْتَ الثَّرَىٰ (mā taḥta al-tharā), taḥt extends divine ownership beneath the soil. In كَانَ تَحْتَهُ كَنزٌ (kāna taḥtahu kanzun), the pronoun refers back to the wall, and the treasure is hidden beneath it.

In قَدْ جَعَلَ رَبُّكِ تَحْتَكِ سَرِيًّا (qad jaʿala rabbuki taḥtaki sariyyā), taḥtaki marks a lower and near position in relation to Maryam. In كَانَتَا تَحْتَ عَبْدَيْنِ (kānatā taḥta ʿabdayn), taḥt expands from physical place into social and marital relation.

Difference from Nearby Words

T Ḥ T is close to أسفل (asfal), but they are not the same. Taḥt is a spatial or relational position: something is beneath or lower than something else. Asfal more often points to the lowest or basest level, and in some contexts carries the value of decline or abasement.

T Ḥ T differs from دون (dūn). Dūn can mean lower than, other than, less than, or apart from something. Taḥt more directly marks lower spatial direction or under-position. T Ḥ T is also close to سفل (S F L), but S F L can carry a negative value of lowness or fall; taḥt does not have a fixed negative value.

T Ḥ T differs from بطن (baṭn). Baṭn points to inside, belly, inwardness, or interior. Taḥt points to under or below. Something may be below without being inside; something may be inside without being below.

T Ḥ T may come close to قاعدة (qāʿidah) or أساس (asās) in some contexts, but it is not identical to them. Qāʿidah and asās directly mean base or foundation. Taḥt does not always mean foundation, though in some contexts, such as rivers beneath gardens or treasure beneath a wall, it approaches the sense of a supporting lower level.

Forqan Translation Method

In Forqan translation, T Ḥ T should be translated according to context. In many places, “under” or “beneath” is suitable. In other places, “below,” “from below,” “beneath the feet,” “beneath the soil,” “within the bond,” or “in marital relation with” may be more accurate.

Not every use of taḥt should be heard negatively. In the Qur’an, being beneath can point to blessing, the flow of life, calm, hidden treasure, or the place of covenant. Only in some contexts does the lower direction carry punishment, abasement, or threat.

Translation Suggestions for Forqan

  • For تَجْرِي مِن تَحْتِهَا الْأَنْهَارُ (tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhār): rivers flow beneath them; or in root explanation: rivers flow beneath their foundation.
  • For تَحْتَ الشَّجَرَةِ (taḥta al-shajarah): under the tree.
  • For مِن تَحْتِ أَرْجُلِهِمْ (min taḥti arjulihim): from beneath their feet.
  • For وَمَا تَحْتَ الثَّرَىٰ (wa-mā taḥta al-tharā): and what lies beneath the soil; or: and what is hidden beneath the layers of soil.
  • For وَكَانَ تَحْتَهُ كَنزٌ (wa-kāna taḥtahu kanzun): and beneath it was a treasure; or: and in its lower hidden layer was a treasure.
  • For قَدْ جَعَلَ رَبُّكِ تَحْتَكِ سَرِيًّا (qad jaʿala rabbuki taḥtaki sariyyā): your Lord has placed a stream beneath you; or: your Lord has placed a stream below you within reach.
  • For كَانَتَا تَحْتَ عَبْدَيْنِ (kānatā taḥta ʿabdayn): the two were in marital bond with two servants; or: they were within the household bond of two servants from among Our servants.

Summary

T Ḥ T in the Qur’an is a directional word that points to under, beneath, lower side, or a foundational level of something. This simple word gains important layers in Qur’anic contexts: beneath gardens is the place of flowing rivers; under the tree is the place of covenant; beneath the feet may indicate provision or punishment; beneath the soil marks the reach of divine dominion; beneath the wall is the place of hidden treasure; and being taḥt in family relation marks position and bond.

The main difference between T Ḥ T and asfal is that asfal more often points to the lowest or basest level, while taḥt marks the under-relation between two things. Its difference from dūn is that dūn can mean other than or less than, while taḥt is primarily spatial or positional. Its difference from baṭn is that baṭn is inside, while taḥt is below.

In summary: T Ḥ T in the Qur’an means being under or placed on a lower or foundational level. Sometimes it marks physical location, sometimes a hidden supporting layer, and sometimes a social or relational position.
