The Qur’anic Root Q Ṣ M

Shattering, breaking continuity, and bringing destruction

Qur’anic forms

قَصَمْنَا

Qaṣamnā

We shattered; 21:11

  2. 2. Qaṣm in the Qur’an: shattering wrongdoing communities

In early Arabic, Q Ṣ M denotes breaking something, particularly a severe break that disrupts the connection between its parts. Breaking a back, a tooth, or a spear are recorded uses of this family.

Classical Arabic lexicons record this meaning, and the Qur’anic occurrence itself attests the sense of shattering in the language of revelation. This investigation did not establish an independently dated pre-Islamic witness.

The following line is transmitted with differences in wording. Kitāb al-Afʿāl attributes it to Kaʿb ibn Zuhayr, while the accompanying editorial note records uncertainty in the commentary on his collected poems about the line and the poem to which it belongs:

كَأَنْ لَمْ يُلَاقِ الْمَرْءُ عَيْشًا بِنِعْمَةٍ / إِذَا نَزَلَتْ بِالْمَرْءِ قَاصِمَةُ الظَّهْرِ

It is as though a person had never lived in comfort when a back-breaking calamity descends upon him.

Qāṣimat al-ẓahr, “that which breaks the back,” describes a crushing calamity that breaks a person’s strength and resilience.

This transmitted use illustrates the extension from breaking bodies to devastating calamity. Its attribution and precise date remain uncertain; it is not presented here as securely dated pre-Islamic evidence.

Forms examined: قَصَمَ, يَقْصِمُ, قَصْم, قَصِم, قَاصِم, قَاصِمَة, مَقْصُوم, اِنْقَصَمَ, اِنْقِصَام, تَقَصَّمَ, and أَقْصَم.

Qur’anic form: قَصَمْنَا. The root occurs once, in 21:11. Other quoted verses provide context or comparison.

1. Core meaning and the material image

Qaṣama l-shayʾa means to break something apart, while qaṣama ẓahrahu means to break someone’s back.

Inqaṣama means to be broken; maqṣūm describes something broken; and qaṣim describes something brittle or a person weak and readily overcome.

Aqṣam al-thaniyyah describes someone with a broken front tooth.

Many lexical explanations emphasize breaking something so that its parts separate. A spear may crack while remaining in one piece, or break so severely that its two parts come apart.

The second situation more closely illustrates the prominent sense of qaṣm.

Some definitions use the broader gloss “breaking” without specifying the manner of separation. That alone does not prove a distinct usage in which there is no separation; the details must be determined from a particular occurrence.

Figuratively, breaking someone’s back can mean destroying their strength or capacity to withstand a blow. Qāṣimat al-ẓahr draws on this image.

Expressions such as “breaking a ruler’s power” and “a back-breaking calamity” help convey the metaphor: bodily strength becomes an image of the ability to endure or act.

2. Qaṣm in the Qur’an: shattering wrongdoing communities

وَكَم قَصَمنا مِن قَريَةٍ كانَت ظالِمَةً وَأَنشَأنا بَعدَها قَومًا ءاخَرينَ

Wa-kam qaṣamnā min qaryatin kānat ẓālimatan wa-anshaʾnā baʿdahā qawman ākharīn.

How many a settlement that was doing wrong We shattered, and after it We brought another people into being.

Al-Anbiya 21:11

Qaṣamnā is a form I perfect verb in the first-person plural: “We shattered.”

Kam here expresses abundance. The verse recalls many wrongdoing settlements rather than a single exceptional event.

The settlement is presented as the object of shattering.

A qaryah is not merely a collection of buildings. It is a place inhabited by a community. Calling it wrongdoing concerns the people’s conduct and relationships, not the moral conduct of walls.

In this context, qaṣm presents the destruction of a community through an image of shattering.

The verse then mentions bringing another people into being. Together with the following verses, this points to the end of the communities described, rather than a temporary setback.

The following verses speak of the people fleeing, confessing their wrongdoing, and reaching their final condition. Political or social collapse may be discussed as an application, but qaṣm alone specifies neither those mechanisms nor the manner of destruction.

The following verses develop the picture:

فَلَمّا أَحَسّوا بَأسَنا إِذا هُم مِنها يَركُضونَ

Fa-lammā aḥassū baʾsanā idhā hum minhā yarkuḍūn.

When they sensed Our might, suddenly they were running away from it.

Al-Anbiya 21:12

Those who had lived in their settlements and enjoyed their resources now run to save themselves.

Eventually they acknowledge their wrongdoing:

قالوا يٰوَيلَنا إِنّا كُنّا ظٰلِمينَ

Qālū yā waylanā innā kunnā ẓālimīn.

They said, “Woe to us! We were indeed wrongdoers.”

Al-Anbiya 21:14

فَما زالَت تِلكَ دَعوىٰهُم حَتّىٰ جَعَلنٰهُم حَصيدًا خٰمِدينَ

Fa-mā zālat tilka daʿwāhum ḥattā jaʿalnāhum ḥaṣīdan khāmidīn.

That remained their cry until We made them like a harvested crop, extinguished.

Al-Anbiya 21:15

The passage begins with shattering and ends with a harvested, extinguished condition.

Qaṣm emphasizes the force of the destruction; ḥaṣīd and khāmidīn depict the people’s final state.

3. Wrongdoing and the collapse of a community

In 21:11, ẓālimah, “wrongdoing,” describes the settlements that The God shattered.

As a reflection on the verse, wrongdoing need not be confined to individual relationships. Violations of rights, corruption, seizure of property, suppression of justice, and abuse of power can weaken collective life. These examples explain the social implications of wrongdoing, not the lexical definition of qaṣm.

A society may possess strong buildings, great wealth, a powerful army, and extensive resources while losing trust among its people and in its administration.

Outward strength does not guarantee its survival.

Elsewhere the Qur’an says:

وَما كانَ رَبُّكَ لِيُهلِكَ القُرىٰ بِظُلمٍ وَأَهلُها مُصلِحونَ

Wa-mā kāna rabbuka li-yuhlika l-qurā bi-ẓulmin wa-ahluhā muṣliḥūn.

Your Lord would not destroy settlements unjustly while their people are putting things right.

Hud 11:117

This translation takes bi-ẓulmin to mean “unjustly,” denying injustice in the destruction. Muṣliḥūn describes people who actively put things right. This is a contextual reading of the verse, not a new meaning of qaṣm.

There is a meaningful difference between people working to remedy corruption and establish justice and a community in which wrongdoing is accepted and entrenched.

Nevertheless, a natural disaster or social collapse cannot, without evidence, be declared divine punishment or proof that all its victims were wrongdoers. In 21:11, the Qur’an itself identifies the settlements in question as wrongdoing.

4. Comparison with related roots

Q Ṣ M (qaṣm) and F Ṣ M (faṣm)

These roots are close in sound and meaning.

A familiar lexical distinction associates faṣm with cracking or breaking without complete separation, and qaṣm with a severe break that separates the parts.

A metal ring may crack while remaining in one piece, illustrating faṣm in this distinction. If it breaks apart into separate pieces, qaṣm is the closer description.

F Ṣ M likewise occurs once in the Qur’an:

لا إِكراهَ فِى الدّينِ قَد تَبَيَّنَ الرُّشدُ مِنَ الغَىِّ فَمَن يَكفُر بِالطّٰغوتِ وَيُؤمِن بِاللَّهِ فَقَدِ استَمسَكَ بِالعُروَةِ الوُثقىٰ لَا انفِصامَ لَها وَاللَّهُ سَميعٌ عَليمٌ

Lā ikrāha fī l-dīn. Qad tabayyana l-rushdu mina l-ghayy. Fa-man yakfur bi-l-ṭāghūti wa-yuʾmin bi-llāhi fa-qadi stamsaka bi-l-ʿurwati l-wuthqā lā nfiṣāma lahā. Wa-llāhu samīʿun ʿalīm.

There is no compulsion in religion. Sound guidance has become distinct from error. Whoever rejects taghut and has faith in The God has grasped the firmest handhold, for which there is no break. The God is All-Hearing, All-Knowing.

Al-Baqarah 2:256

Here the Qur’an denies any infiṣām of the firmest handhold. In 21:11, it speaks of qaṣm, the shattering of wrongdoing settlements.

The distinction is useful for explaining the image. It should not become an exceptionless rule imposed on every occurrence solely from the spelling of the root; the actual usage and context must still be examined.

Q Ṣ M (qaṣm) and Q Ṣ F (qaṣf)

Both roots concern breaking and shattering.

Prominent uses of qaṣf include breaking wood and branches, a wind’s breaking force, and a formidable sound.

Qaṣm particularly emphasizes a severe break and the loss of continuity or structural strength.

In 17:69, qāṣif describes a shattering wind; in 21:11, qaṣm concerns the destruction of wrongdoing settlements.

Q Ṣ M (qaṣm) and Ḥ Ṭ M (ḥaṭm)

Ḥaṭm denotes breaking and crushing. Fragmentation and the reduction of something to broken remnants are prominent images in this family.

Qaṣm emphasizes a severe break that disrupts continuity.

A branch broken into two parts can be described as maqṣūm. A branch crushed under pressure into small fragments more closely illustrates ḥaṭm.

The roots nevertheless overlap in expressing forceful breaking.

Q Ṣ M (qaṣm) and H L K (halāk)

Halāk has a broader range, referring to perishing, destruction, and the end of something’s existence or ability to survive.

Qaṣm may bring destruction, but carries the image of breaking apart and losing structural strength.

Al-Ḥajj 22:45 speaks of the destruction of wrongdoing settlements, and al-Anbiyāʾ 21:11 of their shattering.

Both concern the destruction of wrongdoing communities, but qaṣm conveys that destruction through a particularly forceful, tangible image.

Q Ṣ M (qaṣm) and K S R (kasr)

Kasr is a more general word for breaking.

In many uses, qaṣm expresses a severe break that disrupts continuity or structural strength.

“Shattering” therefore often conveys qaṣm more closely than the unqualified “breaking.”

5. Translation choices for Forqan

Render qaṣama as “shattered.” In an explanation of the destruction of communities, its contextual result can be clarified as “shattered and destroyed.”

For 21:11, “How many a settlement that was doing wrong We shattered” preserves the root’s image more directly than the general “We destroyed.”

Brief root meaning: Breaking something severely and disrupting its continuity or strength; figuratively, breaking power or bringing devastating destruction to a person or community.

Conclusion

Q Ṣ M concerns shattering and the loss of continuity or strength.

The Qur’an uses the root only in 21:11, concerning the destruction of wrongdoing settlements.

Qaṣm is close to faṣm, qaṣf, ḥaṭm, and halāk, with severe breaking and the loss of structural strength especially prominent.

In its Qur’anic context, wrongdoing accompanies the destruction of the communities described. As an ethical reflection, a society founded on violations of people’s rights undermines its own foundations, however powerful it appears. This is not a warrant to diagnose every historical collapse as the same divine judgment.
