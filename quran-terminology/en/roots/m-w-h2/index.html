Qur’anic Root

The Qur’anic Root M W H

Water: the carrier of life, cleansing, growth, flow, and the continuation of living existence

Important Forms from This Root

مَاء (māʾ)

water; the basic Qur’anic form of this root.

مَاء طَهُور (māʾ ṭahūr)

pure and purifying water.

مَاء مَعِين (māʾ maʿīn)

flowing, visible, accessible water.

مَاء مَهِين / مَاء دَافِق (māʾ mahīn / māʾ dāfiq)

the lowly-looking beginning fluid of the human being; or a gushing fluid.

مَاء غَدَق / مَاء حَمِيم (māʾ ghadaq / māʾ ḥamīm)

abundant water; or, in punishment contexts, boiling burning water.

Method Note

The root M W H appears in the Qur’an almost entirely through the noun مَاء (māʾ), water. This matters. The Qur’an does not mainly unfold this root through many different verbs; it places water itself in different scenes: water sent down from the sky, purifying water, flowing and accessible water, reproductive fluid, abundant water, sweet water, and even boiling water in punishment.

“Water” is usually the right translation. But if it is heard only as a natural liquid, part of the Qur’anic weight is missed. In the Qur’an, water is one of the clearest signs that life depends on The God’s governing care. Dry land becomes living through water, the body is cleansed through water, provision emerges through water, and the human being begins from a small fluid. So māʾ is not merely a substance. It is a field where life, purity, provision, dependence, and trial become visible.

Central Meaning

The central meaning of māʾ in the Qur’an is life-bearing, flowing effectiveness. Water enters the earth, breaks dryness, moves the hidden seed, washes impurity, and preserves the thirsty body from collapse. For this reason, water in the Qur’an often stands opposite dryness, stagnation, impurity, and death.

Yet water is not an independent agent. Water itself is created, sent down, measured, and under The God’s command. Its value lies precisely in showing that the life of the human being and the earth depends on something the human being does not finally own. If water sinks away, if rain stops, if sweet water turns salty, human power and planning are exposed as fragile.

Material Image

The material image is dry cracked earth where no motion is visible. When water descends, the soil softens, seeds split, roots move, and the surface of the earth slowly returns to color and life. The Qur’an repeatedly uses this image to bring the idea of resurrection and reviving after death close to human perception.

Another image is clear water poured over a tired, dusty body. Water dissolves impurity, carries it away, and makes cleanliness and calm possible. From here, māʾ is connected both with the revival of the earth and with human purification.

Classical Arabic Note

In the Arabian environment, water was not merely a natural need. It was a condition for settlement, travel, herding, security, and communal life. A well, spring, or rainfall could change the fate of a tribe or a trade route. Arabic therefore has precise vocabulary for different qualities and appearances of water: sweet water, salty water, running water, hidden water, abundant water, scarce water, and boiling water.

The Qur’an uses this concrete experience, but it does not leave the reader at the level of instinctive need. It moves from drinking water to the governance behind it; from bodily thirst to existential dependence; and from the greening of land to the possibility of life after death.

Qur’anic Usage

Water as the General Field of Life

The Qur’an says: وَجَعَلْنَا مِنَ الْمَاءِ كُلَّ شَيْءٍ حَيٍّ (wajaʿalnā mina al-māʾi kulla shayʾin ḥayy) 21:30. Water is placed as one of the general foundations of living things. Every living thing is meaningful in relation to water.

This is not only a natural observation. It is a sign for faith, because life in its most delicate layers depends on a fluid the human being neither originates from himself nor finally owns.

Water and the Revival of the Earth After Death

Rain is repeatedly joined to the revival of the earth: وَأَحْيَا بِهِ الْأَرْضَ بَعْدَ مَوْتِهَا (wa-aḥyā bihi al-arḍa baʿda mawtihā) 45:5. Dead earth does not mean annihilated earth. It means earth whose active growth has gone silent.

Water breaks that silence and reveals hidden life. This image is one of the clearest Qur’anic ways of understanding resurrection: what has become still and inactive can re-enter the field of life by The God’s command.

Water, Provision, and the Link Between Sky and Earth

Surat al-Baqarah says: وَأَنزَلَ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً فَأَخْرَجَ بِهِ مِنَ الثَّمَرَاتِ رِزْقًا لَّكُمْ (wa-anzala mina al-samāʾi māʾan fa-akhraja bihi mina al-thamarāti rizqan lakum) 2:22. Water is the link between sky, earth, and provision.

Water descends from above, activates the spread-out earth, and its result reaches the human being as fruits and provision. Provision is therefore not merely the product of human work. Human effort takes place inside a field already prepared through water, soil, light, seasons, and The God’s measure.

Māʾ Ṭahūr: Pure and Purifying Water

The Qur’an says: وَأَنزَلْنَا مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً طَهُورًا (wa-anzalnā mina al-samāʾi māʾan ṭahūrā) 25:48. Ṭahūr does not only mean that the water is pure; it also points to its purifying effect.

Surat al-Anfāl gives the same meaning with a practical and inward dimension: وَيُنَزِّلُ عَلَيْكُم مِّنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً لِّيُطَهِّرَكُم بِهِ (wayunazzilu ʿalaykum mina al-samāʾi māʾan liyuṭahhirakum bihi) 8:11. In that scene, water is not only for washing the body. Calm, firmness, and the removal of anxiety are also present in the verse’s setting.

Sweet Water and Salty Water

The Qur’an distinguishes two kinds of water: هَٰذَا عَذْبٌ فُرَاتٌ وَهَٰذَا مِلْحٌ أُجَاجٌ (hādhā ʿadhbun furātun wahādhā milḥun ujāj) 25:53. Both are water, but their effect for life is not the same.

ʿAdhb furāt is sweet, pleasant, and life-suitable water. Milḥ ujāj is salty and biting. This shows that, in Qur’anic logic, the mere existence of water is not enough. Quality, proportion, and usability are also part of the blessing.

Māʾ Maʿīn: Flowing and Accessible Water

At the end of Surat al-Mulk, a basic question is asked: قُلْ أَرَأَيْتُمْ إِنْ أَصْبَحَ مَاؤُكُمْ غَوْرًا فَمَن يَأْتِيكُم بِمَاءٍ مَّعِينٍ (qul araʾaytum in aṣbaḥa māʾukum ghawran faman yaʾtīkum bimāʾin maʿīn) 67:30.

If your water sinks into the ground and disappears from reach, who will bring you flowing and visible water? Here maʿīn is essential. The issue is not only that water exists. The issue is access. Water may exist deep underground, but for a thirsty human being, life-giving water is water that is visible, flowing, and usable.

Māʾ Mahīn and Māʾ Dāfiq: The Humble Beginning of the Human Being

The Qur’an returns the human being to his biological beginning in order to break arrogance: ثُمَّ جَعَلَ نَسْلَهُ مِن سُلَالَةٍ مِّن مَّاءٍ مَّهِينٍ (thumma jaʿala naslahu min sulālatin min māʾin mahīn) 32:8; and: خُلِقَ مِن مَّاءٍ دَافِقٍ (khuliqa min māʾin dāfiq) 86:6.

Māʾ mahīn should be translated carefully. It should not dishonor creation itself, but should show the outward smallness and vulnerability of the human being’s biological beginning. The human being who imagines power, lineage, wealth, or authority as superiority began from a fluid that appears lowly and fragile from the material point of view.

Māʾ Ghadaq: Abundant Water and Human Trial

Surat al-Jinn says: وَأَن لَّوِ اسْتَقَامُوا عَلَى الطَّرِيقَةِ لَأَسْقَيْنَاهُم مَّاءً غَدَقًا ۝ لِّنَفْتِنَهُمْ فِيهِ (wa-an lawi istaqāmū ʿalā al-ṭarīqati la-asqaynāhum māʾan ghadaqā linftinahum fīh) 72:16 to 72:17.

Abundant water is not always only a sign of ease. It can also become a field of trial. Abundance reveals gratitude, justice, and the human ability to keep measure. Just as drought tests the human being, abundant water may also test him through excess, waste, and forgetfulness.

Māʾ Ḥamīm: When a Blessing Becomes Pain

In some verses, water appears in the setting of punishment: وَسُقُوا مَاءً حَمِيمًا (wasuqū māʾan ḥamīmā) 47:15. Here water is no longer comfort and life; it appears as boiling, burning water.

This use shows that the value of water depends on its relation to mercy, measure, and proper place. The same substance that is life-giving in the world can become painful when placed in a context of punishment.

Difference from Nearby Words

Maṭar often appears in the Qur’an in the context of punitive rain or a falling punishment, as in وَأَمْطَرْنَا عَلَيْهِم مَّطَرًا (wa-amṭarnā ʿalayhim maṭarā). This should be said carefully: often, not absolutely always.

Ghayth is rain that comes after distress, dryness, or despair, carrying the shade of relief. Ṣayyib is a heavy pouring rain, especially in a scene of darkness, thunder, and lightning. Wābil is heavy rain, while ṭall is light rain, dew, or gentle moisture.

Sharāb is any drink, not only water. Water may be a drink, but every drink is not māʾ. Baḥr, nahr, and ʿayn each show a place or form in which water appears: sea, river, and spring. Māʾ is water itself before it takes a particular geographic form.

Translation Method

In most cases, “water” is the best translation of māʾ. But the translator must preserve the adjective and context. مَاء مَعِين (māʾ maʿīn) is not just water; it is flowing, visible, accessible water. مَاء مَهِين (māʾ mahīn) may be rendered “a lowly-looking fluid” or “a fluid of little outward worth.” مَاء طَهُور (māʾ ṭahūr) should convey both purity and purifying power. In punishment verses, “water” should not be translated in a way that hides the painful force of ḥamīm.

Forqan Translation Suggestions

وَجَعَلْنَا مِنَ الْمَاءِ كُلَّ شَيْءٍ حَيٍّ (wajaʿalnā mina al-māʾi kulla shayʾin ḥayy): We made every living thing from water; We tied the life of every living thing to water.

مَاءً طَهُورًا (māʾan ṭahūrā): pure and purifying water.

بِمَاءٍ مَّعِينٍ (bimāʾin maʿīn): flowing and accessible water; visible and running water.

مَاءٍ مَّهِينٍ (māʾin mahīn): a lowly-looking fluid; a fluid of little outward worth.

مَاءٍ دَافِقٍ (māʾin dāfiq): a gushing fluid.

مَاءً غَدَقًا (māʾan ghadaqā): abundant and overflowing water.

مَاءً حَمِيمًا (māʾan ḥamīmā): boiling, burning water.

عَذْبٌ فُرَاتٌ (ʿadhbun furāt): sweet and pleasant.

مِلْحٌ أُجَاجٌ (milḥun ujāj): salty and biting; salty and bitter.

Summary

The root M W H, through the word مَاء (māʾ), places before the human being one of the simplest and deepest signs of divine governance. Water is a carrier of life, but it does not own life. It cleanses, but its purifying power belongs to an order The God has established. It revives the earth, but revival is by The God’s command. It reminds the human being of his humble beginning, while also revealing the complete dependence of civilization, body, and provision on The God’s mercy.

In Forqan’s reading, māʾ is not only the name of a natural element. It reminds the human being that his life rests on something he uses every day but never finally owns. If water sinks away, if rain stops, if sweet water becomes salty, the human being realizes how deeply his life depends on the mercy, measure, and governing care of his Lord.
