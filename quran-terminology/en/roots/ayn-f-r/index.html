Central meanings: soil and dust on the ground, and becoming covered in them; in another branch, strength, toughness, mastery, and exceptional capability. In its Quranic occurrence, ʿifrīt denotes a powerful, capable being from among the jinn.

1. The root and its meanings in early Arabic

The three-consonant root ʿ F R has several semantic branches in Arabic. The principal ones include soil and the ground's surface, becoming covered in soil, an earthy color, physical strength, toughness, and exceptional capability.

In early Arabic, ʿafar and ʿafr denote soil and the exposed surface of the ground. The expression ʿafaru al-arḍ refers to surface soil.

ʿAfarahu fī al-turāb means that he rubbed someone in the dust or rolled him in it. ʿAffarahu taʿfīran also describes covering something in soil.

For example, when two people wrestle and one throws the other down so that his body or face touches the soil, the action may be described using this family.

Here, soil is a physical substance, and rubbing in soil is an action performed on a person or object.

In the same semantic field, ʿufrah denotes a color resembling earth, described in lexical accounts sometimes as between gray and red, and sometimes as between white and red.

Aʿfar also describes animals whose color resembles pale soil or reddish earth. Some gazelles are described this way in Arabic.

Another branch of the family concerns power and toughness.

ʿIfr has been used for a powerful, hardy, bold, or shrewd person. ʿIfriyah and ʿifrīt belong to this field too: a being or person distinguished by strength, agility, toughness, or ability to act.

In some uses, these qualities accompany audacity, rebelliousness, cunning, or misconduct.

Thus, ʿifrīt cannot invariably mean a good and powerful person; nor should every Arabic use be restricted to an inherently evil, deceptive being.

The important point is that soil and dust, on the one hand, and strength and toughness, on the other, are recognized branches of this family.

Lexical explanations propose a connection: someone who throws an opponent down and rubs him in the dirt may be described as strong and formidable.

The image is understandable, but does not by itself prove that all senses of power and toughness arose from rubbing someone in dirt.

A historical account must therefore distinguish these branches rather than declare one the certain origin of all the others without sufficient evidence.

2. Early Arabic evidence and its historical setting

Soil, dust, and the color of earth

One established use of ʿ F R in early Arabic names surface soil.

ʿAfaru al-arḍ denotes soil on the ground, while taʿfīr concerns covering something in soil or becoming covered in it.

Early poets also use this family for wrestling, throwing an opponent down, and rubbing him in the dust.

A verse attributed to Abū Dhuʾayb al-Hudhalī reads:

أَلْفَيْتُ أَغْلَبَ مِنْ أُسْدِ الْمَسَدِّ حَدِيدَ النَّابِ أَخْذَتُهُ عَفْرٌ فَتَطْرِيحُ

alfaytu aghlaba min usdi al-masaddi ḥadīda al-nābi akhdhatuhu ʿafrun fa-taṭrīḥ

“I found him like a thick-necked, sharp-fanged lion of al-Masadd: his grip drags an opponent down, then flings him to the ground.”

Here, ʿafr accompanies taṭrīḥ, throwing to the ground. Akhdhatuhu is a noun phrase meaning his grip or manner of seizing, rather than a verb meaning it seized him.

Two lexical explanations of ʿafr in this expression are recorded.

One understands it as rubbing an opponent in soil and covering him in dust.

The other relates ʿafr to seizing and dragging the opponent, which can lead to throwing him down. The translation here follows this explanation.

This difference shows why the sequence of actions and sentence structure matter when interpreting even a single poetic witness.

Both explanations nevertheless concern seizing, overpowering, and throwing down.

Abū Dhuʾayb lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. This study has not independently established the date of this verse. It supports an early use of the root, but cannot without further evidence be presented as certainly predating Quranic revelation.

The branch concerning plants

ʿAfār is the Arabic name of a tree whose wood was used to kindle fire.

A verse attributed to al-Aʿshā reads:

زِنَادُكَ خَيْرُ زِنَادِ الْمُلُوكِ خَالَطَ مِنْهُنَّ مَرْخٌ عَفَارَا

zināduka khayru zinādi al-mulūki khālaṭa minhunna markhun ʿafārā

“Your firesticks are the finest of kings' firesticks, in which markh wood meets ʿafār.”

Markh and ʿafār name two plants whose wood was used in traditional fire-making.

This witness establishes ʿafār as an early Arabic plant name.

The existence of this tree name, however, does not mean that an ʿifrīt is made of fire or that the word directly means fire.

Sharing a root does not make every lexical branch identical.

Al-Aʿshā lived in the late pre-Islamic period and into the emergence of Islam. This study has not independently established this verse's date, but its two tree names remain useful evidence for early usage.

Exceptional strength and the ʿifrīt

A witness closer to the Quranic sense appears in the poetry of Dhū al-Rummah, an Umayyad poet:

كَأَنَّهُ كَوْكَبٌ فِي إِثْرِ عِفْرِيَةٍ
مُسَوَّمٌ فِي سَوَادِ اللَّيْلِ مُنْقَضِبُ

ka-annahu kawkabun fī ithri ʿifriyatin / musawwamun fī sawādi al-layli munqaḍib

“As though he were a star pursuing an ʿifriyah, sent forth through the darkness of night, breaking away.”

Describing a wild bull's rapid movement, the poet employs the image of a star and an ʿifriyah.

ʿIfriyah is a form close to ʿifrīt, used for a powerful, unruly, or exceptionally agile being or person.

Speed and force of movement stand out in this poetic image.

Dhū al-Rummah lived after Quranic revelation. The verse therefore shows the continued use of ʿifriyah in early Arabic, rather than independently proving its pre-Islamic circulation.

Taken together, these witnesses attest soil, color, and certain plant names in early Arabic; strength and toughness also appear clearly in the Quran and early lexical sources.

This study has not established sufficient independently datable evidence that the exact form ʿifrīt circulated before Islam with all its later associations.

That limitation does not erase the clear Quranic use. It prevents an unsupported claim about the word's precise date of emergence.

3. Lexical and Quranic forms

Important forms in the Arabic family include ʿafar, ʿafr, ʿafara, ʿaffara, taʿfīr, taʿaffara, inʿafara, iʿtafara, ʿufrah, aʿfar, ʿafrāʾ, ʿifr, ʿifriyah, ʿifrīt, ʿafārīt, and ʿafār.

These forms belong to several semantic branches.

ʿAfar and ʿafr: soil and the earthy surface of the ground.

ʿAfarahu and ʿaffarahu: rubbing someone or something in soil, or rolling them in it.

Taʿfīr: covering with soil or rubbing in the dust.

Taʿaffara and inʿafara: becoming covered in soil or falling into it.

Iʿtafara: in some uses, falling to the ground or throwing another down.

ʿUfrah: an earthy or dusty color, or a mixture of white and red.

Aʿfar and ʿafrāʾ: descriptions of an earth-like color, including that of certain animals.

ʿIfr: in the strength branch, a hardy, powerful, and sometimes unruly person.

ʿIfriyah: a being or person distinguished by strength, agility, or toughness, sometimes with shrewdness and cunning.

ʿIfrīt: a powerful, bold, capable being or person, in some uses unruly or crafty.

ʿAfārīt: the plural of ʿifrīt.

ʿAfār: a tree whose wood was used to kindle fire.

Other recorded forms include ʿafarnā for exceptionally powerful beings and yaʿfūr for certain earth-colored animals.

These forms show that ʿ F R is not a family with only one branch.

Not every member should be translated as soil or dust, just as not every derivative denotes a powerful or rebellious being.

The Quranic form

The Quran contains only the form ʿifrītun from this family:

Al-Naml 27:39

It is an indefinite singular noun in the nominative, functioning as the subject of qāla, said.

In the usual morphological analysis, ʿifrīt follows the pattern fiʿlīt.

On that analysis, ʿ F R are its three root consonants, while yāʾ and tāʾ are additional letters in its formation.

The final tāʾ in ʿifrīt should not be treated as an ordinary feminine ending. The word can describe a male person or being, and its established plural is ʿafārīt.

Lexical sources also record ʿifriyah, ʿifāriyah, and ʿifrīn in the same semantic field.

These related forms matter morphologically, but they do not all occur in the Quran.

The Quran uses only ʿifrītun; Quranic analysis must therefore focus on this form and its context.

4. ʿIfrītun mina al-jinn: a powerful being from among the jinn

The sole Quranic occurrence of ʿ F R appears in Solomon's story.

In Al-Naml 27:38, Solomon asks the chiefs before him which of them can bring the ruler of Sabaʾ's throne before her people arrive.

In the next verse, one of the jinn responds:

قَالَ عِفْرِيتٌ مِنَ الْجِنِّ أَنَا آتِيكَ بِهِ قَبْلَ أَنْ تَقُومَ مِنْ مَقَامِكَ ۖ وَإِنِّي عَلَيْهِ لَقَوِيٌّ أَمِينٌ

qāla ʿifrītun mina al-jinni anā ātīka bihi qabla an taqūma min maqāmika wa-innī ʿalayhi la-qawiyyun amīn

Al-Naml 27:39

Al-Naml 27:39

“A powerful one from among the jinn said: I will bring it to you before you rise from your place, and I am indeed capable of it and trustworthy.”

Here, ʿifrītun introduces someone responding to Solomon's request.

Mina al-jinn specifies that he belongs to the jinn.

This matters because Arabic does not restrict ʿifrīt to jinn: a powerful, unruly, or very shrewd human could also receive this description.

By adding mina al-jinn, the Quran identifies the being's affiliation.

The word ʿifrīt alone therefore does not establish that everyone so called in Arabic must be a jinn.

In this Quranic verse he is one of the jinn because the text explicitly says so.

The meaning of ʿifrīt in this context

In Arabic, ʿifrīt may describe a being or person distinguished by strength, boldness, toughness, and ability to act.

Here, that capability accompanies a specific proposal.

The ʿifrīt says he can bring the throne of Sabaʾ's ruler before Solomon rises from his place.

He thus presents himself as able to undertake a task requiring considerable capability.

In context, powerful is a suitable equivalent for introducing him.

Yet ʿifrīt should not simply be equated with qawī, strong: its Arabic range can include agility, toughness, and shrewdness as well as strength.

Nor should all these qualities be inserted into the translation as established traits of this particular jinn.

Calling someone an ʿifrīt may emphasize exceptional capability in one situation, or rebelliousness and harsh behavior in another.

Context determines the relevant sense.

In Al-Naml 27:39, the focus is ability to perform a task. Exceptional strength and capability are consequently the most relevant features for a basic rendering.

5. Distinguishing ʿifrīt from qawī and amīn

The ʿifrīt continues:

وَإِنِّي عَلَيْهِ لَقَوِيٌّ أَمِينٌ

wa-innī ʿalayhi la-qawiyyun amīn

Al-Naml 27:39

That is: “And I am indeed capable of it and trustworthy.”

Two independent descriptions occur in this expression.

Qawī belongs to Q W Y and concerns strength and capability.

Amīn belongs to ʾ M N and concerns trustworthiness and reliability.

These descriptions must be translated separately. The strength expressed by qawī overlaps with ʿifrīt, but trustworthiness is not part of the meaning of ʿifrīt itself.

ʿIfrīt is the designation or description introducing the speaker.

Qawī and amīn are qualities he claims concerning his capability and trustworthiness.

Strength to perform the task

By saying la-qawiyyun, the ʿifrīt emphasizes his ability to carry out the assignment.

The claim alone does not specify the kind of power involved.

It may concern carrying, transporting, or accomplishing something beyond ordinary human ability.

The verse does not, however, explain how the throne would be transported.

Neither ʿifrīt nor his claim establishes that the method necessarily involved flying, vanishing, converting matter into energy, or any particular scientific mechanism.

Those details are absent from the text.

Trustworthiness

The ʿifrīt also describes himself as amīn.

For such an assignment, trustworthiness may concern delivering what has been entrusted to him without betrayal or improper appropriation.

In this expression, however, the Quran reports his own words.

A distinction is therefore needed between claiming trustworthiness and demonstrating it in practice.

Someone may describe himself as highly capable and reliable, while assessing that claim requires further evidence.

Al-Naml 27:39 does not say that the ʿifrīt lied; nor does this verse separately verify all his claims.

His speech is reported, followed in the narrative by another person's proposal.

He should not therefore be declared a traitor or deceiver solely because some lexical uses of the word carry negative associations.

Nor does his use of amīn alone establish his unfailing moral integrity.

This distinction avoids assigning a fixed moral verdict to the root.

6. The ʿifrīt and the one who had knowledge from the Book

Al-Naml 27:40 introduces another person as possessing ʿilmun mina al-kitāb, knowledge from the Book.

He too offers to bring the throne, but gives a shorter interval: “before your glance returns to you.” The pronoun in ilayka refers to Solomon, the addressee, rather than the person making the offer.

The Quran then reports that the throne stood before Solomon.

This connection matters for understanding the ʿifrīt's role.

In Al-Naml 27:39, a jinn of exceptional capability offers to undertake the task.

In Al-Naml 27:40, another person, identified by knowledge from the Book, offers to do it faster.

This difference does not imply that the ʿifrīt inherently lacks knowledge or that all jinn act solely through physical strength.

Al-Naml 27:39 says nothing about the extent of his knowledge.

Nor does Al-Naml 27:40 give the second person's name or precise identity. Identifying him as a particular human or jinn without explicit evidence goes beyond that text.

Capability and knowledge both matter in these verses, but they do not establish a universal theory of knowledge's absolute superiority to physical strength or a fixed division of beings into two groups.

The two proposals do nevertheless show that one being's exceptional capability does not mean no other can perform the same task faster.

This inference arises from comparing the verses, not from the lexical meaning of ʿifrīt.

Solomon's response

Later in Al-Naml 27:40, after seeing the throne, Solomon attributes what has happened to his Lord's favor and calls it a test of gratitude or ingratitude.

His response shifts the narrative's attention from extraordinary feats alone to a person's relationship with divine favor and power.

That lesson should not be inserted into the meaning of ʿifrīt itself.

The ʿifrīt in Al-Naml 27:39 is a powerful, capable jinn. Gratitude, testing, and divine favor are concepts expressed later in the account.

7. Must an ʿifrīt be a satan or an evil being?

Some lexical sources explain ʿifrīt with descriptions such as rebellious, audacious, cunning, and intensely powerful.

These uses show that the word can carry negative associations.

But established linguistic usage must be distinguished from a judgment about the particular character mentioned in the Quran.

Al-Naml 27:39 speaks of ʿifrītun mina al-jinn.

The verse itself does not call him a shayṭān, disbeliever, wrongdoer, or misleader.

This word alone cannot therefore establish that he was then disobeying The God or Solomon.

Nor does his being a jinn mean that every jinn has the qualities of an ʿifrīt.

Al-Jinn 72:11 mentions righteous individuals and others among the jinn. Al-Jinn 72:14 also distinguishes those who submit from those who deviate from what is right.

Being a jinn does not itself impose one moral assessment on all members of that group.

ʿIfrīt in ordinary language

In Persian and some other languages, ʿifrīt sometimes denotes a frightening being, a very powerful demon, or an evil person.

These uses belong to the word's later cultural and narrative developments.

Storytelling images of bodily size, horns, wings, frightening features, or particular legendary powers should not be inserted into its Quranic definition.

This verse does not describe the ʿifrīt's appearance.

Familiar images of demons and ʿifrīts in popular culture do not establish the appearance of the being mentioned here.

Does the root's connection with soil mean that the ʿifrīt was created from soil?

One possible error is to infer from the earthy sense of ʿafar that an ʿifrīt must have been created from earth.

That inference is not lexically sound.

A being's name may derive from behavior, color, strength, or another feature without identifying the material of its creation.

Al-Naml 27:39 identifies the ʿifrīt as one of the jinn.

Al-Rahman 55:15 describes the creation of jinn from mārijin min nār.

A possible connection between the name ʿifrīt and throwing an opponent into the dirt does not overturn the Quran's statement about the creation of jinn.

This illustrates that a name's derivation does not by itself determine its referent's material nature or manner of creation.

8. Distinguishing ʿ F R from related roots

ʿ F R (ʿifrīt) and Q W Y (qawī)

Q W Y concerns strength, capability, and power to act.

The Quranic ʿifrīt likewise denotes a powerful being.

Arabic ʿifrīt can also convey toughness, agility, audacity, or exceptional shrewdness.

In Al-Naml 27:39, the ʿifrīt himself uses qawī about himself.

Both words appear in the verse, but serve different functions.

ʿIfrīt introduces the person; qawī forms part of his claim to be capable of the assignment.

Powerful is therefore suitable as a brief rendering, while the root study may explain the broader lexical field.

ʿ F R (ʿifrīt) and M R D (mārid)

M R D concerns rebelliousness and exceeding limits, and in other uses, bareness and smoothness.

Al-Saffat 37:7 contains shayṭānin mārid.

Mārid there describes a rebellious satan.

ʿIfrīt in Al-Naml 27:39 introduces a jinn speaking of his ability to accomplish a task.

Although lexical sources sometimes associate ʿifrīt with rebelliousness and audacity, it should not invariably be equated with mārid.

The words serve different roles in these two verses.

ʿ F R (ʿifrīt) and J N N (jinn)

J N N concerns concealment and being hidden.

Al-jinn names a group of created beings in the Quran.

In Al-Naml, ʿifrīt designates or describes a particular member of that group.

Jinn and ʿifrīt are consequently not complete synonyms.

In ʿifrītun mina al-jinn, one expression identifies the person by a distinguishing description, while the other specifies his membership among the jinn.

The verse does not imply that all jinn are ʿifrīts or that Arabic applies ʿifrīt exclusively to jinn.

ʿ F R (ʿifrīt) and SH Ṭ N (shayṭān)

The Quran uses shayṭān for beings or agents associated with misguidance and opposition to truth.

Al-Anam 6:112 speaks of satans among humans and jinn.

This shows that the Quranic designation is not confined to a single kind of being.

Arabic ʿifrīt can also describe a human or a jinn.

That similarity does not make the words identical.

In its Quranic occurrence, ʿifrīt denotes the being's exceptional capability. Shayṭān's associations with misguidance and hostility to truth require their own examination.

Translating ʿifrīt as satan in Al-Naml 27:39 without explanation may import a moral judgment not explicitly stated in the phrase.

ʿ F R (ʿafar) and T R B (turāb)

Turāb names soil or dust.

In one branch, ʿafar likewise refers to soil and the ground's earthy surface.

The two words sometimes denote the same substance, but belong to different lexical families.

ʿ F R also includes covering with soil, earthy color, and the distinct branch of strength and toughness.

Sharing a referent in the case of soil does not make their roots or all their derivatives identical.

ʿ F R (ʿafar) and Ṣ R ʿ (ṣaraʿa)

Ṣ R ʿ concerns throwing someone to the ground and overcoming him in wrestling or conflict.

Some uses of ʿ F R also concern rubbing an opponent in the dust and throwing him down.

Both actions may occur in a single event.

A wrestler may, for example, throw his opponent down so that his body rolls in the soil.

Ṣaraʿa emphasizes throwing down and overcoming, whereas ʿaffara in its soil-related branch concerns covering with soil.

Not every fall or throw is taʿfīr, and not every taʿfīr implies victory in wrestling.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

The sole Quranic form is ʿifrītun in Al-Naml 27:39.

The current Persian Forqan translation renders the phrase as a powerful one from among the jinn.

That rendering fits the context: the person claims the capacity to undertake a difficult task.

The root study should nevertheless clarify that lexical ʿifrīt may convey toughness, agility, and exceptional shrewdness alongside strength.

A powerful one from among the jinn is therefore suitable for the basic translation, while the lexical commentary can explain the wider range.

Word: عِفْرِيتٌ

Root: ʿ F R

Word type: indefinite singular noun, nominative; in the usual analysis, pattern fiʿlīt; subject of qāla.

Brief root meaning: soil and rubbing in the dust; in another branch, strength, toughness, and exceptional capability.

Meaning in this verse: a powerful, capable being from among the jinn.

Proposed rendering of the phrase:

قَالَ عِفْرِيتٌ مِنَ الْجِنِّ

qāla ʿifrītun mina al-jinn

Al-Naml 27:39

“A powerful one from among the jinn said.”

The complete translation should also preserve the distinction between the introduction of the ʿifrīt and his own claims.

Accordingly, wa-innī ʿalayhi la-qawiyyun amīn must remain his reported speech:

“And I am indeed capable of it and trustworthy.”

Trustworthiness should not be added to the lexical meaning of ʿifrīt: it belongs to ʾ M N and appears as an independent description in his speech.

Renderings such as evil demon, rebellious satan, or sorcerer jinn are unsuitable for the basic text, since each adds a specific trait or judgment not explicitly stated in the phrase.

Powerful conveys the principal feature needed to understand his role here.

Conclusion

ʿ F R has several semantic branches in Arabic: surface soil and dust, covering something in soil, earthy color, intensity and power, toughness, and certain plant and animal names.

In the soil branch, ʿafar names surface soil and taʿfīr means covering something in it.

In the strength branch, ʿifr, ʿifriyah, and ʿifrīt describe powerful, tough, bold, or exceptionally capable beings or people. Some lexical uses also involve shrewdness, rebelliousness, or cunning.

These senses should not all be reduced to one certain historical origin. Connecting the ʿifrīt's power with throwing an opponent into the dust is an etymological explanation to examine, not conclusive proof of the word's history.

The Quran contains only ʿifrītun, in Al-Naml 27:39.

It introduces a jinn who responds to Solomon's request by declaring his ability to bring the ruler of Sabaʾ's throne.

In that verse, the ʿifrīt calls himself qawī and amīn. These independent descriptions must be retained in translation: qawī overlaps with his description as powerful, while amīn concerns trustworthiness and is not part of the meaning of ʿifrīt.

The verse does not explicitly call him a satan, wrongdoer, or traitor. Negative associations of some lexical uses and later legendary images should not be transferred to this particular character without evidence.

The two principal branches, soil and rubbing in the dust, and exceptional strength and toughness, should therefore be distinguished. In Al-Naml 27:39, ʿifrītun denotes a powerful and capable being from among the jinn, who makes claims about the speed with which he can act and his trustworthiness.

For Forqan, a powerful one from among the jinn is an appropriate rendering faithful to context. Other qualities, such as shrewdness, audacity, rebelliousness, or particular extraordinary powers, should enter the explanation only as far as lexical evidence and the Quranic text support them.
