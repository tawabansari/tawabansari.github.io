1. Introducing the root and its semantic field

The three-letter Arabic root Ṭ W Q has two prominent semantic fields: a ring or collar that encircles something, and a person’s capacity to perform or bear an activity.

Physically, ṭawq is a ring encircling a neck or something else. It may be ornamental, like a necklace; natural, like a colored ring around some birds’ necks; or a heavy burden or constraint placed around the neck.

In the second field, ṭāqah denotes capacity to act. Aṭāqa means being able to do something or having it within one’s capacity. Ability need not make an act easy: it may demand considerable effort and take someone to the limits of endurance.

The root occurs four times in four Qur’anic verses, in three morphological forms: yuṭīqūnahu in Al-Baqarah 2:184, ṭāqata in Al-Baqarah 2:249 and Al-Baqarah 2:286, and sa-yuṭawwaqūna in Āl ʿImrān 3:180.

Qur’anic counts and forms: Quranic Arabic Corpus

These uses show two important aspects: the extent of human capacity and endurance, and something placed upon people like a neck-ring on the Day of Resurrection.

The neck-ring image does not make every use a reference to restriction or entrapment. Capacity to act can be positive, and a ṭawq need not be punitive. Each form’s meaning depends on the word and its context.

2. The semantic core and physical image

Encircling and encompassing

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris explains Ṭ W Q through something surrounding something else:

فَكُلُّ مَا اسْتَدَارَ بِشَيْءٍ فَهُوَ طَوْقٌ

fa-kullu mā istadāra bi-shayʾin fa-huwa ṭawqun

“Everything that goes around something is a ṭawq.”

He connects capacity with the same image: someone able to do something has, as it were, encompassed it and brought it within the bounds of their capacity.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: Ṭ W Q

This explains a relationship between the semantic fields, but its limits matter.

Physical encircling means something surrounds something else. Capacity involves no actual ring around a neck or body: an activity lies within someone’s ability.

This connection can illuminate the lexical family without becoming a conclusive account of how all its derivatives historically arose.

A neck-ring: ornament and constraint

A necklace around the neck gives a clear image of the root.

A gold or silver ring may be ornamental. A rope or chain around someone’s neck can use the same encircling shape for restraint or imposing a burden.

Encircling is common to both, while value and function depend on material, purpose, and circumstances.

Arabic also calls a bird with a ring of colored feathers around its neck muṭawwaq. A ṭawq need not be human-made: a ringlike shape around something can belong to the same field of naming.

From ṭawq to ṭāqah

For capacity, imagine an act a person can perform: lifting a load, covering a distance, or bearing a particular difficulty.

An act within someone’s ability can be said to lie within their ṭāqah. Beyond their actual capacity, they cannot perform or bear it.

Three situations must nevertheless be distinguished: an easy act, an act achievable with effort and difficulty, and an act beyond the person’s capacity.

Ṭ W Q can describe capacity and its limits, but the word alone does not always identify which situation applies.

Al-Rāghib al-Isfahānī’s Mufradāt defines ṭāqah as the amount a person can do with difficulty. Lisān al-ʿArab similarly connects it with the furthest extent of ability and what can be achieved with effort.

Al-Rāghib’s Mufradāt: capacity and difficulty

Having ṭāqah therefore need not mean acting comfortably or effortlessly. Yet every occurrence of aṭāqa does not necessarily place someone on the verge of collapse.

Capacity differs from ease, just as capacity differs from absolute inability.

3. Important forms and early Arabic usage

Ṭawq: a ring and what surrounds something

Ṭawq (طَوْق) is the family’s familiar noun for a ring, necklace, or something encircling something else.

Its plural is aṭwāq. Form II ṭawwaqa means placing a ring on something or around someone’s neck; taṭawwaqa concerns putting one on or being encircled by it.

Ṭawwaqahu al-qilādata (طَوَّقَهُ الْقِلَادَةَ), for example, describes placing a necklace on someone. In taṭawwaqati al-ḥayyatu ʿalā ʿunuqihi (تَطَوَّقَتِ الْحَيَّةُ عَلَى عُنُقِهِ), a snake coils around his neck like a ring.

Ṭawq is therefore not always a separate metal object: an encircling shape can also motivate the expression.

The independent noun ṭawq does not occur in the Qur’an, but sa-yuṭawwaqūna in Āl ʿImrān 3:180 draws on this field.

Ṭāqah: capacity and endurance

In the capacity field, ṭāqa al-shayʾa and aṭāqahu concern being able to do something or managing it.

The verbal nouns ṭawq and iṭāqah and the noun ṭāqah are also recognized in this usage.

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

وَالطَّوْقُ وَالْإِطَاقَةُ: الْقُدْرَةُ عَلَى الشَّيْءِ

wa-al-ṭawqu wa-al-iṭāqatu: al-qudratu ʿalā al-shayʾi

“Ṭawq and iṭāqah mean ability concerning something.”

It also states of capacity:

وَالطَّوْقُ الطَّاقَةُ أَيْ أَقْصَى غَايَتِهِ

wa-al-ṭawqu al-ṭāqatu ay aqṣā ghāyatihi

“Ṭawq is ṭāqah: the furthest extent of one’s ability.”

The continuation explains this limit as the amount someone can do with difficulty.

Lisān al-ʿArab: ṭawq and iṭāqah

Ṭāqah can thus denote the limit of ability, particularly when human difficulty and limitation are at issue.

The word’s meaning must nevertheless be distinguished from a situation’s intensity. Lifting a load may require slight effort from one person and another’s utmost capacity. Both may be able to do it, with different amounts of difficulty.

Capacity alone establishes neither complete ease nor absolute inability.

Ṭawwaqa: placing a neck-ring and imposing a responsibility

Beyond placing a physical neck-ring, Arabic ṭawwaqa also appears in expressions concerning the imposition of duties and responsibilities.

An imposed responsibility is likened to something encircling a person and laid upon them as a burden.

Lexical sources record ṭawwaqtuka al-shayʾa (طَوَّقْتُكَ الشَّيْءَ) for placing a task upon someone or charging them with it.

Ṭawwaqa does not consequently imply a task beyond someone’s capacity in every sentence. Sometimes an actual ring is meant; sometimes an imposed responsibility or burden.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:180, miserliness and resurrection provide the context decisive for the verb’s meaning.

Ṭāqah: a strand or individual portion

Arabic also records ṭāqah for a strand of hair or thread, or a sprig or bunch of plants, as in ṭāqatun min rayḥānin, a sprig or bunch of basil.

Ibn Fāris regards this use as distant from the main encircling sense and treats its derivation from ṭawq cautiously.

A plant strand therefore does not establish that human capacity originally meant “a strand of force.” Such an explanation lacks sufficient support.

Some sources identify architectural ṭāq, an arch, as an Arabicized Persian word. Its resemblance to Ṭ W Q alone cannot establish a common origin for every use.

Maqāyīs and Mukhtār al-Ṣiḥāḥ: strands and architectural ṭāq

An early poetic witness: neck or capacity?

A verse attributed to ʿAmr ibn Umāmah reads:

كُلُّ امْرِئٍ مُقَاتِلٌ عَنْ طَوْقِهِ
كَالثَّوْرِ يَحْمِي جِلْدَهُ بِرَوْقِهِ

kullu imriʾin muqātilun ʿan ṭawqihi / ka-al-thawri yaḥmī jildahu bi-rawqihi

“Each person fights to defend his neck and life, like a bull protecting its skin with its horn.”

In this transmission, ṭawqihi concerns the neck and defense of life.

Another transmission of the same verse gives:

كُلُّ امْرِئٍ مُجَاهِدٌ بِطَوْقِهِ

kullu imriʾin mujāhidun bi-ṭawqihi

“Each person strives according to his capacity.”

Ṭawq in this reading concerns ability and capacity.

Lexical accounts record both readings, whose difference determines the interpretation. They must not be conflated and then used to make one conclusive evidence for the other’s sense.

The verse is attributed to early Arabic poetry, but both its textual and attributional differences must remain visible. Lisān al-ʿArab, quoting Ibn Barrī, names ʿAmr ibn Umāmah; Tāj al-ʿArūs also reports an attribution to ʿĀmir ibn Fuhayrah. These accounts alone do not settle its date or authorship.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: poetic readings and attributions

The witness illustrates two recognized uses of ṭawq: the neck and its surrounding ring, and a person’s capacity or ability.

It does not make ability and neck synonymous throughout the root’s uses.

4. Qur’anic uses of the root

A. Yuṭīqūnahu: ability and the question of difficulty

The first occurrence appears in the fasting verses:

أَيَّامًا مَعْدُودَاتٍ ۚ فَمَنْ كَانَ مِنْكُمْ مَرِيضًا أَوْ عَلَىٰ سَفَرٍ فَعِدَّةٌ مِنْ أَيَّامٍ أُخَرَ ۚ وَعَلَى الَّذِينَ يُطِيقُونَهُ فِدْيَةٌ طَعَامُ مِسْكِينٍ ۖ فَمَنْ تَطَوَّعَ خَيْرًا فَهُوَ خَيْرٌ لَهُ ۚ وَأَنْ تَصُومُوا خَيْرٌ لَكُمْ ۖ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ تَعْلَمُونَ

ayyāman maʿdūdātin; fa-man kāna minkum marīḍan aw ʿalā safarin fa-ʿiddatun min ayyāmin ukhara; wa-ʿalā alladhīna yuṭīqūnahu fidyatun ṭaʿāmu miskīnin; fa-man taṭawwaʿa khayran fa-huwa khayrun lahu; wa-an taṣūmū khayrun lakum in kuntum taʿlamūna

“A counted number of days. Whoever among you is ill or traveling shall make up the number on other days. For those who bear it with difficulty, there is a ransom: feeding a needy person. Whoever voluntarily does more good, it is better for them; and that you fast is better for you, if you know.”

Al-Baqarah 2:184

Yuṭīqūnahu is a Form IV imperfect verb, from aṭāqa yuṭīqu.

The final wāw is the plural subject pronoun; -hu is its object.

In context, -hu refers to the ṣiyām prescribed in the preceding verse, Al-Baqarah 2:183.

The subject is therefore ability to fast.

Does yuṭīqūnahu mean they can fast easily?

The wording does not impose that restriction.

Aṭāqa denotes ability to act and can describe ability involving difficulty.

Someone may cover a distance comfortably while another covers it with considerable pain and effort. Both managed the act, with different abilities and difficulty.

This distinction matters for yuṭīqūnahu, since the verse concerns fasting and compensation.

A lexical boundary must nevertheless remain: yuṭīqūnahu is affirmative and does not by itself mean “they cannot do it.”

A statement such as uṭīqu hādhā, “I can bear this,” affirms some ability or endurance rather than absolutely denying it.

This ability may involve such difficulty that the person cannot sustain it. That situation requires contextual and circumstantial evidence; negation must not be silently inserted into the verb itself.

Immediate ability and sustained endurance

Understanding the verse requires distinguishing doing something once from being able to continue it for the prescribed duration.

Someone might fast a single day, while repeating it on consecutive days causes harmful and unbearable strain.

A person might also be physically able to abstain from food and drink briefly, while their overall circumstances do not permit continuing it consistently with health and essential responsibilities.

These illustrate real differences in human capacity.

Yuṭīqūnahu itself specifies neither endurance’s duration nor a medical threshold of incapacity. The worship is prescribed for ayyāman maʿdūdātin, a counted number of days, so assessment must relate to the act and its duration.

Three situations can therefore be distinguished: tolerating fasting ordinarily, performing it with severe difficulty near the limit of endurance, and being unable to tolerate it at all.

The distinctions matter to understanding the prescription, but the verb alone must not be said to express this entire classification.

Yuṭīqūnahu and fidyah

Wa-ʿalā alladhīna yuṭīqūnahu is followed by fidyatun ṭaʿāmu miskīnin.

Fidyah belongs to F D Y and concerns compensation or a payment prescribed in exchange for a stated circumstance.

Study of F D Y

The relationship between capacity, difficulty, and compensation emerges from the complete construction.

Does the verse prescribe compensation for people fasting with extreme difficulty, or did this stage of prescription offer those able to fast a choice between fasting and feeding?

Early explanations offer both approaches.

Some take yuṭīqūnahu as ability to fast and understand an initial choice between fasting and feeding, then relate the prescription to Al-Baqarah 2:185 through an abrogation analysis.

Other explanations concern people for whom fasting is extremely difficult, such as elderly people unable to sustain it.

Lexical reports also transmit readings such as yuṭawwaqūnahu and yaṭṭawwaqūnahu, emphasizing a heavy imposed duty or undertaking it with difficulty. They do not replace yuṭīqūnahu in the familiar received text and must be examined separately as historical and lexical evidence.

Several points are clear from the Qur’anic text itself.

First, illness and travel accompany making up days later in the same verse.

Second, alladhīna yuṭīqūnahu are mentioned separately, with feeding a needy person as compensation.

Third, wa-an taṣūmū khayrun lakum affirms fasting’s value.

Fourth, Al-Baqarah 2:185 emphasizes ease and the denial of undue hardship.

A reading attentive to extreme difficulty and inability to sustain fasting can therefore be defended through ṭāqah’s semantic field and the principle of ease. This conclusion draws on combined contextual indications, rather than turning affirmative yuṭīqūnahu into negative lā yuṭīqūnahu.

Al-Qurṭubī: interpretations of yuṭīqūnahu and reported readings

Root study alone also cannot settle whether the verse is abrogated. Such judgment requires examining the verses’ actual compatibility or incompatibility. Different circumstances addressed by two verses do not alone establish that one cancels the other.

B. Lā ṭāqata lanā: perceived inability before Goliath

The second occurrence appears in Saul’s story:

فَلَمَّا فَصَلَ طَالُوتُ بِالْجُنُودِ قَالَ إِنَّ اللَّهَ مُبْتَلِيكُمْ بِنَهَرٍ فَمَنْ شَرِبَ مِنْهُ فَلَيْسَ مِنِّي وَمَنْ لَمْ يَطْعَمْهُ فَإِنَّهُ مِنِّي إِلَّا مَنِ اغْتَرَفَ غُرْفَةً بِيَدِهِ فَشَرِبُوا مِنْهُ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا مِنْهُمْ فَلَمَّا جَاوَزَهُ هُوَ وَالَّذِينَ آمَنُوا مَعَهُ قَالُوا لَا طَاقَةَ لَنَا الْيَوْمَ بِجَالُوتَ وَجُنُودِهِ قَالَ الَّذِينَ يَظُنُّونَ أَنَّهُمْ مُلَاقُو اللَّهِ كَمْ مِنْ فِئَةٍ قَلِيلَةٍ غَلَبَتْ فِئَةً كَثِيرَةً بِإِذْنِ اللَّهِ وَاللَّهُ مَعَ الصَّابِرِينَ

fa-lammā faṣala ṭālūtu bi-al-junūdi qāla inna Allāha mubtalīkum bi-naharin fa-man shariba minhu fa-laysa minnī wa-man lam yaṭʿamhu fa-innahu minnī illā mani ightarafa ghurfatan bi-yadihi fa-sharibū minhu illā qalīlan minhum; fa-lammā jāwazahu huwa wa-alladhīna āmanū maʿahu qālū lā ṭāqata lanā al-yawma bi-jālūta wa-junūdihi; qāla alladhīna yaẓunnūna annahum mulāqū Allāhi kam min fiʾatin qalīlatin ghalabat fiʾatan kathīratan bi-idhni Allāhi wa-Allāhu maʿa al-ṣābirīna

“When Saul set out with the troops, he said: The God will test you with a river. Whoever drinks from it is not of me, and whoever does not taste it is of me, except one who takes a single handful. They drank from it, apart from a few. When he and those who believed with him crossed it, they said: Today we have no capacity to face Goliath and his troops. Those who thought they would meet The God said: How many a small group has overcome a large group by The God’s permission! And The God is with the patient.”

Al-Baqarah 2:249

Ṭāqata appears within lā ṭāqata lanā.

Lā here categorically negates the class, with ṭāqata as its noun. The statement emphasizes lack of capacity in the situation at issue.

What is the subject of this inability?

Bi-Jālūta wa-junūdihi shows that it concerns facing Goliath and his troops.

The use extends beyond bodily capacity to lift an object: confrontation and steadfastness in battle are also at issue.

Did they truly have no ability?

Their statement receives a response from another group in the story.

Those who believed they would meet The God recall how often a small group has overcome a larger one by His permission.

Lā ṭāqata lanā thus expresses one group’s assessment and sense of inability before the approaching circumstances, rather than necessarily reporting that victory is impossible.

Facing great danger, someone may judge their capacity insufficient, while perseverance, planning, and circumstances not yet known could allow another outcome.

Ṭāqah here concerns capacity to confront, denied within some soldiers’ speech. The Qur’an reports it without establishing that every sense of inability proves actual, conclusive incapacity.

Capacity also extends beyond muscular strength. Preparedness, morale, numbers, and a mission’s difficulty may affect assessment of the ability to confront.

These factors explain the situation; ṭāqah itself does not mean fear, courage, or faith.

C. Mā lā ṭāqata lanā bihi: limits of endurance and responsibility

The third occurrence appears at Al-Baqarah’s conclusion:

لَا يُكَلِّفُ اللَّهُ نَفْسًا إِلَّا وُسْعَهَا لَهَا مَا كَسَبَتْ وَعَلَيْهَا مَا اكْتَسَبَتْ رَبَّنَا لَا تُؤَاخِذْنَا إِنْ نَسِينَا أَوْ أَخْطَأْنَا رَبَّنَا وَلَا تَحْمِلْ عَلَيْنَا إِصْرًا كَمَا حَمَلْتَهُ عَلَى الَّذِينَ مِنْ قَبْلِنَا رَبَّنَا وَلَا تُحَمِّلْنَا مَا لَا طَاقَةَ لَنَا بِهِ وَاعْفُ عَنَّا وَاغْفِرْ لَنَا وَارْحَمْنَا أَنْتَ مَوْلَانَا فَانْصُرْنَا عَلَى الْقَوْمِ الْكَافِرِينَ

lā yukallifu Allāhu nafsan illā wusʿahā; lahā mā kasabat wa-ʿalayhā mā iktasabat; rabbanā lā tuʾākhidhnā in nasīnā aw akhṭaʾnā; rabbanā wa-lā taḥmil ʿalaynā iṣran kamā ḥamaltahu ʿalā alladhīna min qablinā; rabbanā wa-lā tuḥammilnā mā lā ṭāqata lanā bihi; wa-ʿfu ʿannā wa-ighfir lanā wa-irḥamnā; anta mawlānā fa-unṣurnā ʿalā al-qawmi al-kāfirīna

“The God charges no self beyond its capacity. What it has earned is to its benefit, and what it has acquired is against it. Our Lord, do not hold us accountable if we forget or err. Our Lord, do not lay a heavy burden upon us as You laid upon those before us. Our Lord, do not burden us with what we cannot bear; pardon us, forgive us, and have mercy on us. You are our Protector, so help us against the disbelieving people.”

Al-Baqarah 2:286

The verse is especially important for ṭāqah because it contains two close but independent concepts: wusʿ and ṭāqah.

The verse begins lā yukallifu Allāhu nafsan illā wusʿahā.

The prayer later says wa-lā tuḥammilnā mā lā ṭāqata lanā bihi.

Wusʿ belongs to W S ʿ and concerns spaciousness and breadth of capacity.

Ṭāqah belongs to Ṭ W Q and concerns ability and the limit of endurance.

Both approach human capacity while remaining lexically distinct.

Is ṭāqah always less than wusʿ?

Some lexical explanations distinguish them by treating wusʿ as capacity within which action is possible, while ṭāqah concerns a limit of ability sometimes involving severe difficulty.

This distinction is useful for examining the verse.

Their occurrence together does not, however, establish a fixed numerical scale in which wusʿ always means an easy stage and ṭāqah always the final limit of endurance throughout Arabic.

That precise classification requires independent examination of both roots’ full usage.

Al-Baqarah 2:286 explicitly states that divine obligation does not exceed a self’s wusʿ; the prayer then asks The God not to impose a burden beyond its ṭāqah.

Both statements attend to human capacity.

Tuḥammilnā and ṭāqah have distinct meanings

Tuḥammilnā in wa-lā tuḥammilnā mā lā ṭāqata lanā bihi belongs to Ḥ M L.

The verb concerns placing a burden or responsibility upon a person.

Ṭāqah identifies a person’s capacity to bear such a burden.

The noun ṭāqah in this sentence therefore does not mean “imposing”; that meaning comes from the separate verb tuḥammilnā. This concerns ṭāqah’s role in this construction, without denying ṭawwaqa’s use for imposing a duty in other Arabic expressions.

This matters in translation: rendering ṭāqah directly as “an imposed burden” confuses two independent functions in the sentence.

Responsibility and actual human capacity

The prayer does more than ask The God for escape from difficult tasks: it concerns what lies beyond endurance.

A difficult act differs from an act beyond one’s ability.

A responsibility may require effort while remaining within someone’s capacity. Another may so exhaust health, safety, or essential strength that the person can no longer fulfill it.

Ṭāqah therefore should not mean complete comfort, just as a small ability to start a task does not establish the capacity to perform it fully and sustainably.

This distinction matters in worship, travel, work, family responsibilities, and other areas of life.

Assessing someone’s actual situation nevertheless requires honesty and knowledge of circumstances. Feeling difficulty alone does not prove incapacity, just as briefly tolerating pressure does not prove that continuing it will remain harmless and within endurance.

D. Sa-yuṭawwaqūna: something placed upon people like a neck-ring

The fourth occurrence appears in Āl ʿImrān:

وَلَا يَحْسَبَنَّ الَّذِينَ يَبْخَلُونَ بِمَا آتَاهُمُ اللَّهُ مِنْ فَضْلِهِ هُوَ خَيْرًا لَهُمْ بَلْ هُوَ شَرٌّ لَهُمْ سَيُطَوَّقُونَ مَا بَخِلُوا بِهِ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ وَلِلَّهِ مِيرَاثُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ وَاللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ خَبِيرٌ

wa-lā yaḥsabanna alladhīna yabkhalūna bi-mā ātāhumu Allāhu min faḍlihi huwa khayran lahum; bal huwa sharrun lahum; sa-yuṭawwaqūna mā bakhilū bihi yawma al-qiyāmati; wa-li-Allāhi mīrāthu al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍi wa-Allāhu bi-mā taʿmalūna khabīrun

“Those who are miserly with what The God has given them from His bounty must not think it better for them; rather, it is worse for them. On the Day of Resurrection, what they withheld will be placed upon them like a neck-ring. To The God belongs the inheritance of the heavens and earth, and The God is fully aware of what you do.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:180

The verse uses sa-yuṭawwaqūna.

The prefix sa- relates the verb to the future.

Yuṭawwaqūna is a Form II passive imperfect verb. Its plural wāw is the passive subject, referring to the people under discussion.

It means a neck-ring being placed upon them, or their being encircled by something like one.

What becomes their neck-ring?

The verse states:

“مَا بَخِلُوا بِهِ” (mā bakhilū bihi).

That is, what they withheld through miserliness.

It refers to bounty The God placed at their disposal, which they withheld from giving and from fulfilling its associated due.

The subject is not simply possessing wealth, but being miserly with what one has received.

A lexical translation should not restrict mā bakhilū bihi to one particular type of property or payment without evidence.

The setting may concern financial rights and withheld giving, while the expression itself refers to what they were miserly with.

An actual neck-ring or an image of sin’s burden?

Ṭawwaqa can denote placing an actual ring around a neck. Here, what people withheld through miserliness is placed upon them as a neck-ring on the Day of Resurrection.

The image expresses a clear relationship: what someone refused to give and retained for themselves now becomes a burden around their neck.

The verse does not detail how this image is realized in the hereafter.

It should therefore not be restricted, without evidence, to a particular material, animal, or physical mechanism.

Nor should it be reduced to an indistinct psychological feeling, as though the verse said nothing about a real consequence of miserliness at resurrection.

The wording emphasizes the withheld thing being placed upon a person as a neck-ring. Its ultimate manner of realization is not established by this expression alone.

Does ṭawwaqa always mean imposing a task beyond capacity?

No.

The verb here draws on placing a neck-ring and encircling.

Yuṭīqūnahu in Al-Baqarah 2:184 belongs to the field of ability and endurance.

A shared root does not make sa-yuṭawwaqūna simply “they will be charged with a difficult task.”

Certain Arabic expressions do connect a ring with imposition, but the ring image directly fits Āl ʿImrān 3:180’s construction and subject of miserliness.

Preserving that image is more precise than replacing the verb with “they will be punished”: punishment arises from context without exhausting the verb’s meaning.

5. Distinctions from related roots

Ṭ W Q (ṭāqah) and W S ʿ (wusʿ)

Study of W S ʿ

W S ʿ concerns breadth, room, and capacity.

Ṭ W Q in its capacity use concerns what a person can perform or bear.

Together in Al-Baqarah 2:286, they approach human capacity through two aspects: room or scope, and ability to bear.

Wusʿ should nevertheless not always be made effortless capacity, nor ṭāqah a near-fatal inability.

Both concern actual limits of human capacity in Qur’anic usage, with the precise distinction depending on the sentence.

Ṭ W Q (iṭāqah) and Q D R (qudrah)

Study of Q D R

Q D R concerns measure, determining an amount, and ability.

Qudrah can denote ability to act, while iṭāqah approaches the act through having it within one’s endurance or capacity.

Someone may be able to perform an act, yet find repeating it very difficult.

Endurance then concerns more than the bare possibility of performing it.

The distinction is not absolute: qudrah and iṭāqah overlap in many Arabic uses.

Ṭ W Q (ṭāqah) and ʿ J Z (ʿajz)

Study of ʿ J Z

ʿ J Z concerns inability and failing to manage something.

Its semantic field stands opposite ability.

Yet lacking ṭāqah and being incapable are not identical in every situation.

Someone may be unable to perform one particular task while having sufficient ability for others.

Someone may likewise have sound general capacity while a particular activity exceeds their endurance under present conditions.

Judgments of ability or inability must therefore relate to a specified activity and actual circumstances.

Ṭ W Q (iṭāqah) and Ṭ W ʿ (ṭawʿ)

Study of Ṭ W ʿ

These roots have similar letters but remain independent.

Ṭ W Q concerns the neck-ring, capacity, and endurance.

Ṭ W ʿ concerns compliance, agreement, and acting willingly or voluntarily.

Both occur in Al-Baqarah 2:184: yuṭīqūnahu and fa-man taṭawwaʿa khayran.

Yuṭīqūnahu concerns capacity to bear fasting.

Taṭawwuʿ concerns voluntarily doing further good.

Being able to act therefore differs from willingly undertaking it. Someone may have ability without willingness, or desire to do something they cannot perform.

This distinction matters to the verse and should not disappear because ṭāqah and ṭāʿah look similar.

Ṭ W Q (ṭāqah) and K L F (taklīf)

Study of K L F

K L F in Qur’anic usage concerns charging someone with a duty or placing a responsibility upon them.

Ṭāqah concerns capacity to bear that responsibility.

The fields appear together in Al-Baqarah 2:286.

Taklīf concerns the prescribed responsibility itself; ṭāqah concerns a person’s capacity to perform or bear it.

An obligation can appear quantitatively identical for two people while demanding different proportions of their capacity, given their bodies, living conditions, and resources.

This matters to understanding responsibility proportionate to human capacity.

Ṭ W Q (ṭawq) and GH L L (ghull)

Study of GH L L

One use of ghull denotes a shackle or chain placed around someone’s neck or hands.

The Qur’an mentions aghlāl in various contexts, including punishment and imposed restraints.

Ṭawq may likewise surround a neck, but need not bind or restrain. It can be ornamental or a natural marking.

Ghull as a shackle or chain has a clearer connection with binding and restricting.

Āl ʿImrān 3:180 uses the neck-ring image for miserliness’s consequence, without making ṭawq and ghull identical in every usage.

6. Morphology and interpretive challenges

Yuṭīqūnahu and yuṭawwaqūna

The verbs share a root, but differ in form and meaning.

Yuṭīqūnahu in Al-Baqarah 2:184 is a Form IV active imperfect with an attached object.

Sa-yuṭawwaqūna in Āl ʿImrān 3:180 is a Form II passive imperfect.

In the first, people have capacity to perform or bear something.

In the second, something is placed upon them like a neck-ring.

Translating both simply as “they bear” loses the image and morphological distinction.

Ṭāqah and iṭāqah

Ṭāqah names ability and capacity; lexical sources describe it as a noun also used in place of the verbal noun iṭāqah.

Lisān al-ʿArab: ṭāqah in place of a verbal noun

Iṭāqah is the Form IV verbal noun, concerning managing something or having capacity to act.

The two approach each other in the field of ability, while their grammatical roles differ.

Al-Baqarah 2:286 uses the noun ṭāqah; Al-Baqarah 2:184 uses the Form IV verb.

Morphological study must preserve this difference, without inferring that ṭāqah always means fixed capacity and iṭāqah always means exhausting endurance.

Does ṭāqah always involve difficulty?

Some early definitions emphasize difficulty and the furthest limit of ability.

Aṭāqa can also refer generally to ability to act.

The more precise account is that the root expresses capacity to bear or perform, with some contexts emphasizing severe difficulty and the limit of ability.

This preserves lexical evidence without making intense suffering an obligatory component of every derivative.

Does a little ability amount to sufficient ability?

Not necessarily.

Ability to begin an act differs from ability to complete it under prescribed conditions.

This matters particularly for worship such as fasting, involving duration and repetition.

Study of Ṣ W M

Determining whether a person has sustainable capacity requires examining their circumstances.

A lexical definition alone cannot give one ruling for elderly people, sick people, travelers, and people with different life responsibilities.

Conversely, an act’s difficulty alone does not place it beyond capacity.

The boundary requires study of the prescription and actual circumstances.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

For physical ṭawq, “neck-ring,” “ring,” or “necklace” is suitable. Where restraint or pressure is involved, “a ring around the neck” better preserves the image.

For ṭāqah, “capacity” or “ability” is central. “Capacity to bear” is more suitable when a difficult task or heavy burden is at issue.

For aṭāqa, “was able,” “managed,” or, in a suitable context, “could bear” can be used.

Physical ṭawwaqa can mean “placed a ring around someone’s neck” or “put a ring on something.” With responsibilities, “imposed” can be used when context supports it.

The main proposals for the four Qur’anic occurrences follow.

Al-Baqarah 2:184: yuṭīqūnahu means “they can bear it.” A rendering emphasizing this context’s severe difficulty, “they bear it with difficulty,” can be defended. “They cannot do it at all” is not a direct translation of this affirmative verb; any such conclusion in interpreting the prescription must come from sustainable capacity and other contextual indications.

Al-Baqarah 2:249: lā ṭāqata lanā al-yawma bi-Jālūta wa-junūdihi means “Today we have no capacity to face Goliath and his troops.” “Face” clarifies the subject of ability from the confrontation with the army.

Al-Baqarah 2:286: mā lā ṭāqata lanā bihi means “what we cannot bear.” This is natural while preserving the difference between capacity to bear and an imposed burden.

Āl ʿImrān 3:180: sa-yuṭawwaqūna mā bakhilū bihi means “What they withheld will be placed upon them like a neck-ring.” This preserves the root’s image rather than reducing it to the general “they will be punished.”

The basic translation should keep the neck-ring image and capacity meaning in their respective places. Discussion of their historical and semantic relationship belongs in the root study.

8. Summary

Ṭ W Q concerns encircling and encompassing in Arabic. Physical ṭawq is a ring surrounding a neck or something else. In the capacity field, ṭāqah and iṭāqah concern ability to perform or bear an activity.

The root occurs four times in the Qur’an. Three concern human capacity and endurance’s limits: yuṭīqūnahu in fasting and compensation, and lā ṭāqata lanā in Saul’s story and the closing prayer of Al-Baqarah.

The fourth, sa-yuṭawwaqūna in Āl ʿImrān 3:180, uses the neck-ring image to express miserliness’s consequence on the Day of Resurrection.

Ease, severe difficulty, and actual inability must be distinguished. Iṭāqah can involve a difficult act, without itself meaning absolute inability. A denial of capacity must also be understood in relation to the particular activity.

This matters especially in Al-Baqarah 2:184. Yuṭīqūnahu affirms capacity to bear, while the compensation context and principle of ease require considering those who tolerate fasting only with severe difficulty. The verb’s form alone does not determine sustainable capacity.

Sa-yuṭawwaqūna in Āl ʿImrān 3:180 is understood through the neck-ring branch, rather than directly through ability: what people withheld is placed upon them like a ring around the neck.

The brief meaning of Ṭ W Q is encircling and encompassing; in the branch concerning people, capacity to perform or bear an activity.

The fields are related, but Qur’anic translation must preserve their distinction. An actual or depicted neck-ring, ability to act, endurance’s limits, and imposing responsibility each have a specified meaning within their construction and context.
