Qur’anic Root B GH T

Arriving suddenly: an event whose time of occurrence is unexpected by those facing it

The Arabic root ب غ ت denotes something arriving or happening suddenly, so that a person did not expect it at that moment or was unaware of its approach. It applies in ordinary life, such as someone’s unexpected arrival, and to larger events that overturn human preparedness.

The family’s central feature is the relationship between an event and the expectations or awareness of those facing it. An event may have been developing for a long time, while its arrival remains sudden for its audience. Baghtah therefore does not necessarily mean something without preceding causes, circumstances, or signs.

The root occurs 13 times in the Qur’an, always as بَغْتَةً. Some verses describe punishment arriving, while others concern the Hour. Two verses describe communities being seized after periods of hardship and prosperity before punishment.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: thirteen occurrences of baghtatan

These uses show that suddenness does not conflict with prior warning. A person may know that something is possible or even promised, yet not know when it will actually arrive, or behave as though it never will.

1. The root’s semantic core in Arabic

Ibn Fāris identifies one principle for this root in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

«الْبَغْتُ، وَهُوَ أَنْ يَفْجَأَ الشَّيْءُ»

That is, baght is something happening or arriving suddenly.

He treats it as a principle from which additional, extended meanings should not be drawn without grounds.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise explains بَغْت and بَغْتَة through فَجْأَة: something happening or arriving suddenly.

Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī adds an important qualification in Al-Mufradāt:

«مُفَاجَأَةُ الشَّيْءِ مِنْ حَيْثُ لَا يُحْتَسَبُ»

That is, something arriving from a direction or in circumstances where it is not expected.

Al-Mufradāt, Lisān al-ʿArab, and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: sudden and unexpected

This shows that the word is not merely about speed. What matters is the unexpected arrival of an event for the person encountering it.

Someone might arrive quickly while others already know and await the arrival time. Speed alone does not make that arrival baghtatan.

Conversely, someone might reach home at an entirely unexpected time after a long, unhurried journey. Although their movement was not fast, their arrival surprises the household.

The distinction clarifies the core: baghtatan describes an event’s arrival in relation to human expectations and awareness before it describes speed of movement.

A sudden event can of course occur very quickly, but speed and surprise remain distinct features that should not always be equated.

2. Lexical forms and early Arabic witnesses

The family’s basic triliteral verb is بَغَتَ. Arabic says:

«بَغَتَهُ الْأَمْرُ»

Meaning that the event came upon him suddenly or took him by surprise.

The verbal nouns بَغْت and بَغْتَة are recorded for sudden occurrence.

Form III بَاغَتَهُ likewise means arriving unexpectedly upon someone or surprising them; its verbal noun is مُبَاغَتَة.

Other uses have بَاغِت for what arrives suddenly and مَبْغُوت for someone taken by surprise.

Plural بَغَتَات also appears in expressions for sudden events. بَغَتَاتُ الْعَدُوِّ, for example, concerns an enemy’s unexpected actions.

The family is therefore not confined to punishment or events of the hereafter. Any occurrence whose arrival is unexpected by its audience may be described through it.

A poetic witness

A poem attributed to Yazīd ibn Ḍabbah al-Thaqafī says:

وَلَكِنَّهُمْ بَانُوا وَلَمْ أَدْرِ بَغْتَةً
وَأَفْظَعُ شَيْءٍ حِينَ يَفْجَؤُكَ الْبَغْتُ

wa-lākinnahum bānū wa-lam adri baghtatan / wa-afẓaʿu shayʾin ḥīna yafjaʾuka al-baghtu

Translation: “But they departed suddenly, without my knowing; most dreadful is what strikes you by surprise.”

Al-Ḥarbī, Gharīb al-Ḥadīth: the Yazīd ibn Ḍabbah witness

The line concerns people departing and separating from the poet when he did not expect or know of their departure.

The lexical point is that baghtatan describes people leaving, rather than punishment or a supernatural event.

Suddenness is thus a general linguistic meaning that the Qur’an applies in its particular settings.

Early sources transmit variants. Some have مَاتُوا instead of بَانُوا, and أَعْظَمُ or أَنْكَأُ instead of أَفْظَعُ.

Lisān, Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, and Al-Muḥkam: variants of the witness

These variants do not alter the use of baght for an unexpected event. They do show why one wording should not be presented as uniquely certain without examination.

The composition date is also insufficiently clear to establish the line confidently as pre-Qur’anic. It is an early Arabic witness rather than definitive evidence of when the word originated.

Al-Rāghib also quotes the following line:

إِذَا بَغَتَتْ أَشْيَاءُ قَدْ كَانَ مِثْلُهَا
قَدِيمًا فَلَا تَعْتَدَّهَا بَغَتَاتِ

idhā baghatat ashyāʾu qad kāna mithluhā / qadīman fa-lā taʿtaddahā baghatāti

Translation: “If things happen suddenly whose like existed long before, do not count them among wholly unexpected events.”

Al-Rāghib, Al-Mufradāt: the witness on similar events

The line relates an event to previous knowledge of its possibility. Something may surprise an individual in the moment while seeming less unexpected to someone familiar with earlier examples.

The witness is semantically instructive, but its attribution and date are not clear enough to establish an independent historical characteristic of the root.

3. Qur’anic بَغْتَةً and its grammatical structure

Every Qur’anic occurrence has the accusative form بَغْتَةً.

It is a verbal noun expressing sudden arrival, used to clarify how the action occurs.

أَخَذْنَاهُمْ بَغْتَةً, for example, describes a group being seized suddenly.

In تَأْتِيَهُمُ السَّاعَةُ بَغْتَةً, the suddenness concerns the Hour’s arrival.

In some sentences it can be analyzed as an accusative verbal noun functioning circumstantially: the event occurs in a manner sudden for those facing it.

Al-Darwīsh: circumstantial or cognate-accusative analysis

This analysis fits the Persian adverb “nāgahān,” or English “suddenly.”

Some constructions may also be understood as “a sudden coming” or “a sudden seizing,” though natural Persian and English generally need only the adverb.

The accusative form itself does not supply additional meanings such as extreme speed, deliberate concealment, or the complete absence of preceding signs.

The verbs بَغَتَ, بَاغَتَ, and other derivatives do not occur in the Qur’an. This reports the attested forms; it does not itself establish a particular rhetorical intention behind not selecting those verbs.

4. Sudden punishment in accounts of communities

One important Qur’anic use describes a severe encounter arriving upon a community after warnings and changing living conditions.

Al-Anʿām 6:44

Verse text:

فَلَمَّا نَسُوا مَا ذُكِّرُوا بِهِ فَتَحْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ أَبْوَابَ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا فَرِحُوا بِمَا أُوتُوا أَخَذْنَاهُمْ بَغْتَةً فَإِذَا هُمْ مُبْلِسُونَ

fa-lammā nasū mā dhukkirū bihi fataḥnā ʿalayhim abwāba kulli shayʾin ḥattā idhā fariḥū bi-mā ūtū akhadhnāhum baghtatan fa-idhā hum mublisūna

Translation:

When they forgot what they had been reminded of, We opened to them the doors of everything. When they rejoiced in what they had been given, We seized them suddenly, and there they were, despairing and helpless.

Al-Anʿām 6:44

Baghtatan comes after the description of a process.

The group first turns away from reminders, then receives opened doors of opportunity and prosperity. They rejoice in what they have received; after that, the severe encounter arrives suddenly.

This sequence shows that the sudden ending of a process does not mean there were no preceding circumstances.

The group had already been reminded and had passed through several stages. Yet they lacked the necessary expectation and preparedness when the final encounter arrived.

أَخَذْنَاهُمْ expresses seizing, while بَغْتَةً clarifies its sudden and unexpected arrival.

Baghtatan should therefore not be redefined as seizing or punishing. Those meanings come from the verb and context, rather than the root ب غ ت itself.

Nor does the verse authorize treating every prosperous society as close to punishment or economic well-being itself as a sign of divine displeasure. The subject is a community with a particular history concerning reminders and responsibility.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:95

Verse text:

ثُمَّ بَدَّلْنَا مَكَانَ السَّيِّئَةِ الْحَسَنَةَ حَتَّىٰ عَفَوْا وَقَالُوا قَدْ مَسَّ آبَاءَنَا الضَّرَّاءُ وَالسَّرَّاءُ فَأَخَذْنَاهُمْ بَغْتَةً وَهُمْ لَا يَشْعُرُونَ

thumma baddalnā makāna al-sayyiʾati al-ḥasanata ḥattā ʿafaw wa-qālū qad massa ābāʾanā al-ḍarrāʾu wa-al-sarrāʾu fa-akhadhnāhum baghtatan wa-hum lā yashʿurūna

Translation:

Then We replaced the adverse condition with a favorable one until they increased and said, “Hardship and ease also touched our forefathers.” We then seized them suddenly, while they were unaware.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:95

This verse likewise concerns a community’s changing circumstances.

They experience hardship followed by more favorable conditions. They then regard adversity and ease as events their predecessors had also experienced.

In that situation, the severe encounter arrives suddenly.

وَهُمْ لَا يَشْعُرُونَ is particularly significant: at the moment of occurrence, they did not perceive its arrival.

Baghtatan describes sudden occurrence, and lā yashʿurūn further clarifies the audience’s awareness.

The expressions are closely related without either being wholly redundant. The first describes how the event arrives; the second emphasizes the unawareness of those encountering it.

The comparison with Al-Anʿām 6:44 is instructive. Both describe a severe encounter after changing conditions and prosperity, but each has its own details.

Baghtatan is therefore not restricted to a particular stage of prosperity or kind of economic behavior. The shared feature is the unexpected moment of arrival.

5. Baghtatan in contrast with jahratan

Al-Anʿām 6:47

Verse text:

قُلْ أَرَأَيْتَكُمْ إِنْ أَتَاكُمْ عَذَابُ اللَّهِ بَغْتَةً أَوْ جَهْرَةً هَلْ يُهْلَكُ إِلَّا الْقَوْمُ الظَّالِمُونَ

qul a-raʾaytakum in atākum ʿadhābu Allāhi baghtatan aw jahratan hal yuhlaku illā al-qawmu al-ẓālimūna

Translation:

Say, “Tell me: if The God’s punishment came upon you suddenly or openly, would any but the wrongdoing people be destroyed?”

Al-Anʿām 6:47

This verse is especially important for the word’s semantic range because it sets baghtatan against جَهْرَةً.

Jahratan comes from ج ه ر and concerns openness and visibility.

Baghtatan concerns something arriving when the audience did not expect it at that moment.

In this context, suddenness therefore involves unawareness of an event’s approach, whereas jahratan foregrounds an open, observable arrival.

This opposition does not make baghtatan synonymous with “secretly” throughout Arabic.

Suddenness and secrecy are related but distinct concepts.

Someone may appear openly before other people’s eyes while arriving unexpectedly. Something may also be done secretly while those informed know exactly when it will occur.

The contrast here depends on how punishment arrives: one mode is unexpected, the other open and visible.

Early explanations relate jahratan to people seeing punishment approach. Some accounts also propose a contrast between night and day.

Al-Ṭabarī: sudden versus open arrival

Baghtatan itself is not confined to night, however, nor is jahratan necessarily a name for daytime. Something sudden may happen in daylight, and something apparent may be visible at night.

The analysis should therefore begin with the words’ meanings, rather than turning them into fixed terms for night and day.

The verse concerns divine punishment, but that subject does not restrict the root’s meaning to punishment, since Arabic also uses it for ordinary events.

6. Suddenness and inability to respond

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:40

Verse text:

بَلْ تَأْتِيهِمْ بَغْتَةً فَتَبْهَتُهُمْ فَلَا يَسْتَطِيعُونَ رَدَّهَا وَلَا هُمْ يُنْظَرُونَ

bal taʾtīhim baghtatan fa-tabhatuhum fa-lā yastaṭīʿūna raddahā wa-lā hum yunẓarūna

Translation:

Rather, it comes upon them suddenly and bewilders them. They cannot repel it, nor will they be granted respite.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:40

Baghtatan and فَتَبْهَتُهُمْ stand beside each other here.

They come from different roots and should not be identified as one.

Baghtatan describes the event’s sudden arrival.

Fatabhatuhum comes from ب ه ت and describes the audience becoming bewildered and helpless before it.

The relationship is clear: the event arrives suddenly, and encountering it leaves the audience bewildered.

Bewilderment is thus a consequence of sudden occurrence in this sentence, rather than baghtatan directly meaning to bewilder.

The continuation describes their inability to repel the event and the absence of respite.

These features belong to the full scene. The root itself does not make every sudden event necessarily deadly, irresistible, or bewildering.

An unexpected encounter with a friend, for example, can be described with baghtatan without immobilizing the person or exposing them to danger.

The verse expresses several related stages through distinct words: unexpected arrival, bewilderment, and inability to repel what has come.

7. Suddenness and a predetermined time

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:53

Verse text:

وَيَسْتَعْجِلُونَكَ بِالْعَذَابِ وَلَوْلَا أَجَلٌ مُسَمًّى لَجَاءَهُمُ الْعَذَابُ وَلَيَأْتِيَنَّهُمْ بَغْتَةً وَهُمْ لَا يَشْعُرُونَ

wa-yastaʿjilūnaka bi-al-ʿadhābi wa-lawlā ajalun musamman la-jāʾahumu al-ʿadhābu wa-la-yaʾtiyannahum baghtatan wa-hum lā yashʿurūna

Translation:

They ask you to hasten punishment. Were it not for an appointed term, punishment would already have come to them. It will certainly come upon them suddenly, while they are unaware.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:53

This clarifies the difference between an event’s actual time and human awareness of it.

On one side is أَجَلٌ مُسَمًّى, an appointed term.

On the other, the same punishment is described as baghtatan.

A time being fixed with The God therefore does not conflict with its arrival being sudden for the audience.

An event can occur at a specified time while those encountering it remain unaware of its approach.

Baghtatan should therefore not be defined as an event without order, cause, or a determined time.

Here, people ask the Prophet to bring punishment sooner. Yet when what they requested occurs, its arrival will surprise them.

Discussing the possibility of an event, or even demanding its hastening, does not necessarily mean readiness to face it.

The verse does not establish that every sudden event in human life is divine punishment with a particular appointed time announced in the Qur’an. Such a conclusion requires independent evidence.

8. The Hour’s sudden arrival

Many Qur’anic uses of baghtatan concern السَّاعَة, the Hour.

In Al-Anʿām 6:31, those who denied meeting The God face the Hour’s sudden arrival and lament their previous neglect.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:187 discusses a question about the Hour’s timing and reserves knowledge of it to The God, before stating that it arrives only suddenly.

Yūsuf 12:107 asks about the possibility of an enveloping punishment from The God or the Hour arriving suddenly while the audience is unaware.

Al-Ḥajj 22:55 places the Hour’s sudden arrival alongside punishment on a harsh day.

Al-Zukhruf 43:66 joins the Hour’s sudden arrival with the audience’s unawareness.

Despite differing contexts, the uses share a meaning: people do not know the exact time of the Hour and cannot arrange their encounter with it by relying on a known date.

Muḥammad 47:18

Verse text:

فَهَلْ يَنْظُرُونَ إِلَّا السَّاعَةَ أَنْ تَأْتِيَهُمْ بَغْتَةً فَقَدْ جَاءَ أَشْرَاطُهَا فَأَنَّىٰ لَهُمْ إِذَا جَاءَتْهُمْ ذِكْرَاهُمْ

fa-hal yanẓurūna illā al-sāʿata an taʾtiyahum baghtatan fa-qad jāʾa ashrāṭuhā fa-annā lahum idhā jāʾat-hum dhikrāhum

Translation:

Do they await anything but the Hour coming upon them suddenly, when its signs have already come? How will their remembrance avail them when it reaches them?

Muḥammad 47:18

This is one of the most important passages for defining the limits of baghtatan.

The same sentence that describes the Hour as sudden announces that its signs have already come.

Defining baghtatan as an event with no preceding sign or warning would therefore conflict with this sentence.

The verse brings preceding signs and sudden arrival together.

Understanding the relationship requires distinguishing awareness of possibility, recognition of signs, and knowledge of the actual arrival time.

A person may observe signs or receive warnings without knowing the event’s definite timing.

The Hour’s suddenness therefore does not mean an absolute absence of preliminaries.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:187 likewise reserves knowledge of the Hour’s timing to The God. Reading the verses together shows that warnings and signs do not supply certain knowledge of the time.

The basic meaning here is arrival at a time not known or anticipated by the audience, even though the event itself was previously announced.

Suddenness and regret

Al-Anʿām 6:31 joins the Hour’s sudden arrival with the regret of those who denied it.

Regret, however, is not part of baghtatan’s lexical meaning; it is the human reaction in that scene.

Muḥammad 47:18 also concerns the narrowing of the opportunity for remembrance and response after occurrence.

Sudden events can thus have grave consequences, particularly when people previously had opportunities to understand and reform their conduct but disregarded them.

Suddenness describes the timing and manner of arrival. Regret, inability, and lost opportunity come from the event’s circumstances and consequences.

9. The root’s other Qur’anic uses

For completeness, the occurrences mentioned earlier should also be considered in their particular settings.

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:202, the painful punishment described in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:201 comes upon its audience suddenly, while they are unaware. Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:203 then describes their request for more time. The subject is thus more than unexpected arrival: it is an encounter after which people wish for another opportunity.

Al-Zumar 39:55 commands people to follow the best of what their Lord has sent down before punishment reaches them suddenly. Warning before occurrence and the possibility of preventive action are prominent. Baghtatan therefore cannot mean that prior warning is useless.

Al-Zukhruf 43:66 joins suddenness with the audience’s unawareness. It clearly relates to Yūsuf 12:107 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:187, while each addresses human responsibility and the future in a different context.

Al-Ḥajj 22:55 places the Hour’s sudden arrival beside continuing doubt and dispute concerning revelation. The audience’s doubt and the event’s timing remain distinct: baghtatan does not itself mean doubt, but describes the arrival of what they doubted.

All 13 Qur’anic occurrences can thus be understood in context. They share one family and form, but the event and the audience’s response are not identical in every passage.

10. Distinguishing related roots

ب غ ت (baght) and ف ج أ (fajʾah)

Fajʾah is baghtah’s closest familiar equivalent in Arabic dictionaries.

Both describe an occurrence or arrival that is sudden and unexpected for someone.

Some later explanations propose precise distinctions, for example restricting one to an event without any preliminaries and the other to an event with preceding signs.

Early lexical evidence is insufficient for such a fixed, universal division. Lexicographers repeatedly use one to explain the other.

The Qur’anic uses concern unexpected arrival, but proposed distinctions from fajʾah should not become fixed linguistic rules without independent evidence.

ب غ ت (baght) and غ ف ل (ghaflah)

Study of GH F L

Ghaflah concerns inattention, unawareness, or the mind’s neglect of something deserving attention.

Baghtatan describes how an event arrives.

The concepts can be related: someone heedless of an event may be surprised when it arrives.

Heedlessness and suddenness are nevertheless different.

Someone may know of and watch for a possible event without knowing its exact time, and still be surprised at its arrival.

Someone may also neglect a matter without any sudden event occurring.

Baghtatan should therefore not always be translated as “in a state of heedlessness.” Such a condition, if present, must come from the context or other words.

ب غ ت (baght) and ش ع ر (shuʿūr)

Study of SH ʿ R

Several verses pair baghtatan with وَهُمْ لَا يَشْعُرُونَ.

Shuʿūr concerns perception and awareness. The Qur’anic phrase emphasizes the audience’s unawareness of the event’s approach.

Baghtatan itself describes the manner of its arrival.

The meanings are close and reinforce each other within the context, while coming from different roots.

Rendering both merely as “unaware” may weaken the distinction between the adverb of suddenness and the clause describing the audience’s condition.

“Suddenly, while they were unaware” therefore preserves the distinction.

ب غ ت (baght) and ب ه ت (baht)

Study of B H T

These roots differ in their letters and arrangement.

Baht concerns bewilderment, helplessness before something, and, in some formations, attributing false statements.

Baght concerns sudden arrival.

Both roots’ presence in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:40 shows why they should not be equated.

The event arrives suddenly and then bewilders its audience.

This is a relationship between an event and a human response, rather than identity of root or complete meaning.

ب غ ت (baght) and ج ه ر (jahr)

Study of J H R

Jahr concerns openness and visibility.

Al-Anʿām 6:47 contrasts baghtatan with jahratan, foregrounding unexpected arrival against open, observable arrival in that context.

Visibility and suddenness are nevertheless not always logically incompatible.

The contrast must therefore be understood in its own setting, rather than defining baght as complete concealment or jahr as slow, gradual arrival.

ب غ ت (baght) and ب ي ت (bayāt)

Study of B Y T

In uses concerning time and nighttime events, bayāt relates to night and passing the night.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:97 presents the danger of a severe encounter arriving at night while people sleep. Al-Aʿrāf 7:98 describes its daytime arrival.

These verses clarify that surprise is not confined to one hour of night or day.

An event may occur at night during sleep, or during ordinary daytime activity.

Baghtatan therefore has a broader temporal meaning and should not be restricted to night attack, darkness, or secrecy.

11. Translation proposals for Forqan

For general Arabic بَغَتَ, “came upon someone suddenly” or “took by surprise” is suitable.

For بَاغَتَ, “arrived unexpectedly upon someone” is possible, or “surprised them” where deliberate action is involved.

For بَغْت and بَغْتَة, the principal equivalents are “suddenness” and “suddenly.”

“Suddenly” is proposed as the basic rendering in all 13 Qur’anic uses. It is brief and natural and preserves the principal meaning without adding interpretive claims.

In Al-Anʿām 6:44 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:95, for example, أَخَذْنَاهُمْ بَغْتَةً may be rendered “We seized them suddenly.”

For the Hour, تَأْتِيَهُمُ السَّاعَةُ بَغْتَةً may be rendered “the Hour comes upon them suddenly.”

In Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:53, where baghtatan accompanies wa-hum lā yashʿurūn, a suitable translation is: “It will come upon them suddenly, while they are unaware.”

In Al-Anʿām 6:47, بَغْتَةً أَوْ جَهْرَةً may be rendered “suddenly or openly,” with their relationship explained separately.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:40, بَلْ تَأْتِيهِمْ بَغْتَةً فَتَبْهَتُهُمْ is best rendered “Rather, it comes upon them suddenly and bewilders them,” preserving the two roots’ distinction.

Secretly, without any preceding sign, extremely quickly, in the blink of an eye, and without warning should not replace baghtatan’s basic meaning unless the context itself supports that added explanation.

Nor should punishment passages turn baghtatan into a separate technical category of “sudden punishment” with fixed characteristics. It remains an expression of manner, while punishment’s nature comes from the verse’s other components.

12. Conclusion

The Arabic root ب غ ت concerns something arriving or occurring suddenly, when a person did not expect it at that moment or was unaware of its approach.

Ordinary usage includes unexpected meetings and departures. The Qur’an applies the word to punishment arriving or the Hour coming.

All 13 Qur’anic occurrences use بَغْتَةً, describing how an event happens or arrives.

The verses show that suddenness need not conflict with preceding circumstances. Al-Anʿām 6:44 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:95 describe sudden encounters after reminders and changing conditions. Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:53 places an appointed time for punishment beside its sudden arrival.

Muḥammad 47:18 even mentions that the Hour’s signs have come while still describing its arrival as baghtatan.

The core is therefore not the absence of causes or preliminaries, but the audience’s unanticipated moment of arrival and actual encounter.

Baghtatan’s proximity to wa-hum lā yashʿurūn and fatabhatuhum also shows that suddenness, unawareness, and bewilderment are related yet distinct. Each has its own word and construction.

Final root definition: ب غ ت denotes something arriving or happening suddenly at a time when its audience did not expect it or was unaware of its approach.

This meaning is not restricted to speed, secrecy, punishment, or an absolute absence of prior warning. Those features, if present, come from the event and sentence context.

The distinctive feature is the relationship between an event and human expectation. Something may have a fixed time, known causes, or preceding signs, yet arrive suddenly for someone who does not know its timing or has not prepared to face it.
