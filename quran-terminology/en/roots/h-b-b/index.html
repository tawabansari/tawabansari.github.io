The Qur’anic Root Ḥ B B (ح ب ب)

Grain and love: from attraction and attachment to preference, loyalty, and being loved in relations between humanity and The God

Arabic Ḥ B B has two important branches: ḥabb (حَبّ) and ḥabbah (حَبَّة), grain and a seed; and ḥubb (حُبّ) and maḥabbah (مَحَبَّة), love and affection. The initial vowel matters: unvocalized writing makes both look alike, although their semantic fields differ.

Love concerns attraction, attachment, and affection toward someone or something. People may love someone for family ties, beauty, kindness, or virtue; desire wealth, comfort, power, or something useful; or love The God and express that love in choices and conduct.

The Qur’an discusses love among people, for worldly desires and possessions, and between The God and His servants. Some loves are praised, some described as human realities, and some criticized when they lead to injustice, error, or preferring falsehood over truth.

Ḥubb itself therefore names neither a virtue nor a sin. Love can motivate generosity and loyalty or harmful attachment. Its moral character follows from its object, place in someone’s life, and resulting actions.

1. Root meanings in earlier Arabic

The root occurs 95 times in 85 Qur’anic verses, in nine derived or nominal forms. Lexicons contrast ḥubb with bughḍ (بُغْض), hatred. Aḥabba (أَحَبَّ) means loving, muḥibb (مُحِبّ) a person who loves, and maḥbūb (مَحْبُوب) someone or something loved.

Earlier Arabic also records ḥababtuhu (حَبَبْتُهُ) for loving, although lexicographers differ over its currency and formation. Aḥbabtuhu (أَحْبَبْتُهُ) is more familiar.

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris distinguishes branches involving grain, adherence and constancy, and shortness or smallness.

He explains love through adherence and continuity: someone loving something may remain attached, stay with it, or repeatedly incline toward it.

This explanation also invokes muḥibb for a camel remaining in place through exhaustion or refusal to move, highlighting staying and not leaving its position.

This lexical explanation must not become a psychological rule covering every love. Loving need not mean bodily proximity or inability to separate: someone may love a distant person or something unattainable.

Nor does adherence establish that love historically developed from a camel’s behavior or standing in one place. These illustrate semantic connections, rather than a certain history of the word’s origin.

Love in pre-Islamic poetry

ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād’s Muʿallaqah states:

وَلَقَدْ نَزَلْتِ فَلَا تَظُنِّي غَيْرَهُ
مِنِّي بِمَنْزِلَةِ الْمُحَبِّ الْمُكْرَمِ

wa-la-qad nazalti fa-lā taẓunnī ghayrahu / minnī bi-manzilati al-muḥabbi al-mukrami

“You hold with me the place of an honored beloved; do not imagine otherwise.”

ʿAntarah describes his beloved’s standing through manzilah, someone’s place with another person.

The use shows love exceeding a fleeting response to sensory pleasure. The beloved may enjoy special esteem in the lover’s heart and judgment.

Grammatical discussions examine al-muḥabb (الْمُحَبّ), its passive formation, and its distinction from muḥibb. The line’s overall meaning remains clear: the beloved occupies an honored place with the lover.

In the same Muʿallaqah, ʿAntarah describes camels and their food:

مَا رَاعَنِي إِلَّا حَمُولَةُ أَهْلِهَا
وَسْطَ الدِّيَارِ تَسُفُّ حَبَّ الْخِمْخِمِ

mā rāʿanī illā ḥamūlatu ahlihā / wasṭa al-diyāri tasuffu ḥabba al-khimkhimi

“Nothing alarmed me but her people’s burden-bearing camels, picking khimkhim seeds from the ground amid the dwellings.”

Ḥabb here concerns a plant’s seed, rather than love.

Both uses in poetry attributed to one poet illustrate their familiar distinction in earlier Arabic: a beloved’s emotional standing versus seed eaten by an animal.

Seed, heart, and concrete imagery

Ḥabb can name grain such as wheat and barley; ḥabbah is one grain or seed.

Some sources record ḥibb and ḥibbah, with kasrah, for seeds of wild plants and greenery. Lexicographers differ over their exact scope: some associate ḥabb more with cereals, others allow a broader range.

Ḥabbatu al-qalbi (حَبَّةُ الْقَلْبِ), the heart’s inner or central part, is also known. Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī explores a semantic connection between grain and love through this image.

Some explanations liken love to something penetrating the heart’s center. The comparison is intelligible but does not prove that love historically derived from the name for seed.

Earlier Arabic also uses ḥabab (حَبَب) for teeth arranged like grains. Some commentaries additionally discuss strands and droplets of moisture in the mouth. Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd describes his beloved’s smile:

وَإِذَا تَضْحَكُ تُبْدِي حَبَبًا
كَرُضَابِ الْمِسْكِ بِالْمَاءِ الْخَصِرْ

wa-idhā taḍḥaku tubdī ḥababan / ka-ruḍābi al-miski bi-l-māʾi al-khaṣir

“When she smiles, she reveals beadlike things [teeth or moisture in her mouth], like fragments of musk in cold water.” Commentaries discuss both the ordered teeth and strands or droplets of oral moisture; the witness should not be confined to either without acknowledging the different explanations.

Small parts resembling grains and lying together provide the comparison’s setting.

Ḥubāb (حُبَاب) also describes bubbles or small raised forms on water. Ibn Fāris offers more than one possible connection with grain or smallness.

The family therefore has several historical branches. An invented unifying image, such as a seed planted in the heart, must not explain every meaning. Literary appeal does not replace etymological research.

2. Ḥabb and ḥabbah: grain in nature and Qur’anic imagery

Fāliqu al-ḥabbi wa-l-nawā: splitting grain and fruit stones

Al-Anʿām states:

إِنَّ اللَّهَ فَالِقُ الْحَبِّ وَالنَّوَىٰ يُخْرِجُ الْحَيَّ مِنَ الْمَيِّتِ وَمُخْرِجُ الْمَيِّتِ مِنَ الْحَيِّ ذَٰلِكُمُ اللَّهُ فَأَنَّىٰ تُؤْفَكُونَ

inna llāha fāliqu al-ḥabbi wa-l-nawā yukhriju al-ḥayya mina al-mayyiti wa-mukhriju al-mayyiti mina al-ḥayyi dhālikumu llāhu fa-annā tuʾfakūna

“The God splits the grain and the fruit stone. He brings the living from the dead and brings the dead from the living. That is The God; how then are you turned away?” (Al-Anʿām 6:95)

Al-ḥabb stands beside al-nawā (النَّوَىٰ).

Ḥabb concerns grain; nawā here concerns a fruit stone. Both can participate in germination and plant growth.

Ḥabb does not mean love here. The verse concerns splitting grain and fruit stones and bringing forth life.

Al-Anʿām 6:99 also mentions grain growing in clusters. Yā Sīn 36:33, Qāf 50:9, Al-Raḥmān 55:12, Al-Nabaʾ 78:15, and ʿAbasa 80:27 likewise use ḥabb for plants, produce, and human provision.

Ḥabbu al-ḥaṣīdi (حَبُّ الْحَصِيدِ) in Qāf 50:9 concerns harvestable grain. Al-ḥabbu dhū al-ʿaṣfi (الْحَبُّ ذُو الْعَصْفِ) in Al-Raḥmān 55:12 presents grain alongside accompanying plant parts.

In its natural branch, ḥabb can thus be plant produce or seed important for growth or food.

Ḥabbah: a single seed

Al-Baqarah states:

مَثَلُ الَّذِينَ يُنْفِقُونَ أَمْوَالَهُمْ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ كَمَثَلِ حَبَّةٍ أَنْبَتَتْ سَبْعَ سَنَابِلَ فِي كُلِّ سُنْبُلَةٍ مِائَةُ حَبَّةٍ وَاللَّهُ يُضَاعِفُ لِمَنْ يَشَاءُ وَاللَّهُ وَاسِعٌ عَلِيمٌ

mathalu alladhīna yunfiqūna amwālahum fī sabīli llāhi ka-mathali ḥabbatin anbatat sabʿa sanābila fī kulli sunbulatin miʾatu ḥabbatin wa-llāhu yuḍāʿifu li-man yashāʾu wa-llāhu wāsiʿun ʿalīmun

“The likeness of those spending their wealth in the way of The God is a seed growing seven ears, with a hundred seeds in each ear. The God multiplies for whom He wills; The God is encompassing in bounty and knowing.” (Al-Baqarah 2:261)

Ḥabbah occurs twice: first the planted seed, then the seeds produced within the ears.

The Qur’an uses the seed’s growth and multiplication to illustrate multiplied results of spending.

Ḥabbah itself does not mean multiplied reward. Reward and increase emerge from the parable and the verse’s continuation.

Nor is this a fixed agricultural rule guaranteeing seven hundred seeds from every seed in every condition. The verse illustrates multiplied benefit and connects it with divine willing and bounty.

A mustard seed: smallness and precise reckoning

Al-Anbiyāʾ states:

وَنَضَعُ الْمَوَازِينَ الْقِسْطَ لِيَوْمِ الْقِيَامَةِ فَلَا تُظْلَمُ نَفْسٌ شَيْئًا وَإِنْ كَانَ مِثْقَالَ حَبَّةٍ مِنْ خَرْدَلٍ أَتَيْنَا بِهَا وَكَفَىٰ بِنَا حَاسِبِينَ

wa-naḍaʿu al-mawāzīna al-qisṭa li-yawmi al-qiyāmati fa-lā tuẓlamu nafsun shayʾan wa-in kāna mithqāla ḥabbatin min khardalin ataynā bihā wa-kafā binā ḥāsibīna

“We set up the just scales for the day of resurrection, so no soul is wronged at all. Even if it is the weight of a mustard seed, We bring it; We suffice as reckoners.” (Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:47)

Ḥabbah is qualified by mustard, identifying a small seed.

Luqmān 31:16 likewise describes a mustard seed’s weight, beyond neither the knowledge nor power of The God even within a rock, the heavens, or the earth.

Both uses emphasize smallness and precision in knowledge or reckoning.

Ḥabbah does not fundamentally mean a tiny particle. Seeds vary in size; these verses highlight smallness through the kind of seed and the construction.

Al-Anʿām 6:59 also describes a seed in the earth’s darkness, exemplifying something not hidden from the knowledge of The God.

Ḥabbah means seed throughout: growth and multiplication provide one image, smallness or concealment others.

3. Ḥubb: affection toward people and desired things

Loving desirable worldly things

Āl ʿImrān states:

زُيِّنَ لِلنَّاسِ حُبُّ الشَّهَوَاتِ مِنَ النِّسَاءِ وَالْبَنِينَ وَالْقَنَاطِيرِ الْمُقَنْطَرَةِ مِنَ الذَّهَبِ وَالْفِضَّةِ وَالْخَيْلِ الْمُسَوَّمَةِ وَالْأَنْعَامِ وَالْحَرْثِ ذَٰلِكَ مَتَاعُ الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا وَاللَّهُ عِنْدَهُ حُسْنُ الْمَآبِ

zuyyina li-l-nāsi ḥubbu al-shahawāti mina al-nisāʾi wa-l-banīna wa-l-qanāṭīri al-muqanṭarati mina al-dhahabi wa-l-fiḍḍati wa-l-khayli al-musawwamati wa-l-anʿāmi wa-l-ḥarthi dhālika matāʿu al-ḥayāti al-dunyā wa-llāhu ʿindahu ḥusnu al-maʾābi

“Love of desirable things has been made attractive to people: women, sons, heaped stores of gold and silver, marked horses, livestock, and cultivated land. These are the enjoyment of worldly life; with The God is the good destination of return.” (Āl ʿImrān 3:14)

Ḥubb here concerns desires and desirable things in human life.

People may love family relationships, children, possessions, transport, livestock, and cultivated land. Such attachment belongs to human life.

The word love itself does not make all these desires intrinsically prohibited or bad. Elsewhere the Qur’an presents children, wealth, and life’s resources as blessings or responsibilities.

Conversely, loving something does not prove it good for someone in every situation.

Al-Baqarah 2:216 makes the distinction clearer.

Loving is not a conclusive criterion of good

Al-Baqarah states:

كُتِبَ عَلَيْكُمُ الْقِتَالُ وَهُوَ كُرْهٌ لَكُمْ وَعَسَىٰ أَنْ تَكْرَهُوا شَيْئًا وَهُوَ خَيْرٌ لَكُمْ وَعَسَىٰ أَنْ تُحِبُّوا شَيْئًا وَهُوَ شَرٌّ لَكُمْ وَاللَّهُ يَعْلَمُ وَأَنْتُمْ لَا تَعْلَمُونَ

kutiba ʿalaykumu al-qitālu wa-huwa kurhun lakum wa-ʿasā an takrahū shayʾan wa-huwa khayrun lakum wa-ʿasā an tuḥibbū shayʾan wa-huwa sharrun lakum wa-llāhu yaʿlamu wa-antum lā taʿlamūna

“Fighting is prescribed for you, although unwelcome to you. You may dislike something good for you and love something bad for you. The God knows, while you do not know.” (Al-Baqarah 2:216)

Love stands against dislike here.

Someone may love something harmful, or find an action unwelcome although beneficial under particular conditions.

Love is a real state or inclination, but does not independently determine a thing’s ultimate worth.

Ordinary language likewise allows loving food whose excessive consumption is harmful. The affection is real but does not establish the benefit of every quantity consumed.

Conversely, someone may dislike a difficult treatment needed in particular circumstances to preserve their health.

These examples illustrate love’s distinction from good; the root itself does not concern bodily benefit and harm.

Aḥabba therefore retains its loving meaning. Whether the beloved is good or bad requires reasons and context.

Family affection: love and perceptions of love

In Yūsuf 12:8, Joseph’s brothers say Joseph and his brother are dearer to their father than they are.

Aḥabbu (أَحَبُّ) is comparative here: more loved or dearer.

The word does not prove every detail of the brothers’ judgment correct. The Qur’an reports their words and perception.

People may infer another’s affection from behavior, yet confuse their own interpretation with the full truth of that person’s feelings and intentions.

The story continues with jealousy and wrongful conduct. Those are not aḥabb’s lexical meaning, but the brothers’ reactions to the situation as they understood it.

Intense love: shaghafahā ḥubban

Yūsuf states:

وَقَالَ نِسْوَةٌ فِي الْمَدِينَةِ امْرَأَتُ الْعَزِيزِ تُرَاوِدُ فَتَاهَا عَنْ نَفْسِهِ قَدْ شَغَفَهَا حُبًّا إِنَّا لَنَرَاهَا فِي ضَلَالٍ مُبِينٍ

wa-qāla niswatun fī al-madīnati imraʾatu al-ʿazīzi turāwidu fatāhā ʿan nafsihi qad shaghafahā ḥubban innā la-narāhā fī ḍalālin mubīnin

“Women in the city said, ‘The official’s wife seeks to seduce her young servant; love has penetrated her heart. We see her in clear error.’” (Yūsuf 12:30)

Ḥubban (حُبًّا) accompanies shaghafahā (شَغَفَهَا).

Shaghaf belongs to SH GH F and here conveys love’s intense penetration of the heart.

The combination establishes her attachment’s intensity. Ḥubb alone need not mean overpowering, unrestrained passion.

The sentence is the city women’s speech: they report her conduct and judge it.

Intense affection accompanies wrongdoing here, but this does not make every intense love or emotional attachment inherently misguided.

The woman’s conduct must be assessed through events, ethical boundaries, and narrative context; Arabic love does not itself name a sin.

Aḥabbu ilayya: an ethical preference expressed through love

Yūsuf states:

قَالَ رَبِّ السِّجْنُ أَحَبُّ إِلَيَّ مِمَّا يَدْعُونَنِي إِلَيْهِ وَإِلَّا تَصْرِفْ عَنِّي كَيْدَهُنَّ أَصْبُ إِلَيْهِنَّ وَأَكُنْ مِنَ الْجَاهِلِينَ

qāla rabbi al-sijnu aḥabbu ilayya mimmā yadʿūnanī ilayhi wa-illā taṣrif ʿannī kaydahunna aṣbu ilayhinna wa-akun mina al-jāhilīna

“He said, ‘My Lord, prison is dearer to me than what they invite me to. Unless You avert their scheme from me, I may incline toward them and become one of the ignorant.’” (Yūsuf 12:33)

Aḥabbu means dearer or preferable.

Joseph prefers prison to the behavior he is being invited to accept.

Love’s language is therefore not confined to pleasure and comfort. Someone may choose the harder option for its value.

This does not make prison inherently pleasurable for Joseph. The comparison is relative: prison is more acceptable than participating in wrongdoing.

Aḥabb can therefore approach practical preference and evaluation in some constructions without entirely losing its connection to love.

4. Love, preference, and the beloved’s place in decisions

Istaḥabbū: preferring one thing over another

Fuṣṣilat states:

وَأَمَّا ثَمُودُ فَهَدَيْنَاهُمْ فَاسْتَحَبُّوا الْعَمَىٰ عَلَى الْهُدَىٰ فَأَخَذَتْهُمْ صَاعِقَةُ الْعَذَابِ الْهُونِ بِمَا كَانُوا يَكْسِبُونَ

wa-ammā thamūdu fa-hadaynāhum fa-staḥabbū al-ʿamā ʿalā al-hudā fa-akhadhathum ṣāʿiqatu al-ʿadhābi al-hūni bi-mā kānū yaksibūna

“As for Thamūd, We guided them, but they preferred blindness over guidance. The thunderbolt of humiliating punishment seized them for what they earned.” (Fuṣṣilat 41:17)

Istaḥabbū (اسْتَحَبُّوا) is form X.

The construction uses ʿalā to prefer one thing over another. “Preferred” is therefore natural.

Al-Tawbah 9:23 uses the same verb for preferring disbelief over faith. Ibrāhīm 14:3 and Al-Naḥl 16:107 describe preferring worldly life over the Hereafter.

Love’s family can thus enter the field of choice and priority.

Such istaḥabba need not express intense emotion. Someone may give priority to an option for its benefit, comfort, or expected outcome.

In Fuṣṣilat 41:17, blindness supplies the misguidance meaning and al-hudā supplies guidance. Istaḥabbū itself expresses preferring.

The form X pattern alone does not establish a particular psychological process producing passionate attachment to the chosen option.

Here preference follows from the relationship between alternatives and the practical choice.

Family and property: the question of priority

Al-Tawbah states:

قُلْ إِنْ كَانَ آبَاؤُكُمْ وَأَبْنَاؤُكُمْ وَإِخْوَانُكُمْ وَأَزْوَاجُكُمْ وَعَشِيرَتُكُمْ وَأَمْوَالٌ اقْتَرَفْتُمُوهَا وَتِجَارَةٌ تَخْشَوْنَ كَسَادَهَا وَمَسَاكِنُ تَرْضَوْنَهَا أَحَبَّ إِلَيْكُمْ مِنَ اللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ وَجِهَادٍ فِي سَبِيلِهِ فَتَرَبَّصُوا حَتَّىٰ يَأْتِيَ اللَّهُ بِأَمْرِهِ وَاللَّهُ لَا يَهْدِي الْقَوْمَ الْفَاسِقِينَ

qul in kāna ābāʾukum wa-abnāʾukum wa-ikhwānukum wa-azwājukum wa-ʿashīratukum wa-amwālun iqtaraftumūhā wa-tijāratun takhshawna kasādahā wa-masākinu tarḍawnahā aḥabba ilaykum mina llāhi wa-rasūlihi wa-jihādin fī sabīlihi fa-tarabbaṣū ḥattā yaʾtiya llāhu bi-amrihi wa-llāhu lā yahdī al-qawma al-fāsiqīna

“Say: If your fathers, children, brothers, spouses, clan, acquired wealth, commerce whose decline you fear, and dwellings you enjoy are dearer to you than The God, His Messenger, and striving in His way, then wait until The God brings His command. The God does not guide the defiantly disobedient.” (Al-Tawbah 9:24)

Aḥabba ilaykum min (أَحَبَّ إِلَيْكُمْ مِنْ) is comparative.

The issue is more than affection for parents, children, spouses, relatives, wealth, or homes. Such attachments become dearer and take precedence over The God, the Messenger, and striving in His way.

Love can reveal itself through decisions and conduct.

Someone may deeply love their family and work for their welfare. This is not inherently wrong.

But taking bribes, oppressing others, or violating their rights for family comfort gives that attachment a role in wrongdoing.

The example applies the ethical question of priority; ḥubb itself does not mean bribery, corruption, or disobedience.

Nor does the verse command lovelessness toward family. Elsewhere the Qur’an commands kindness to parents, care for spouses and children, and relatives’ rights.

The problem arises when human attachment replaces truth and responsibility as the criterion.

Yuḥibbūnahum ka-ḥubbi llāh: love and setting up rivals

Al-Baqarah states:

وَمِنَ النَّاسِ مَنْ يَتَّخِذُ مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ أَنْدَادًا يُحِبُّونَهُمْ كَحُبِّ اللَّهِ وَالَّذِينَ آمَنُوا أَشَدُّ حُبًّا لِلَّهِ وَلَوْ يَرَى الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا إِذْ يَرَوْنَ الْعَذَابَ أَنَّ الْقُوَّةَ لِلَّهِ جَمِيعًا وَأَنَّ اللَّهَ شَدِيدُ الْعَذَابِ

wa-mina al-nāsi man yattakhidhu min dūni llāhi andādan yuḥibbūnahum ka-ḥubbi llāhi wa-alladhīna āmanū ashaddu ḥubban li-llāhi wa-law yarā alladhīna ẓalamū idh yarawna al-ʿadhāba anna al-quwwata li-llāhi jamīʿan wa-anna llāha shadīdu al-ʿadhābi

“Some people take rivals besides The God, loving them as The God is loved; believers are stronger in love for The God. If only the wrongdoers, when seeing the punishment, could recognize that all power belongs to The God and that The God is severe in punishment.” (Al-Baqarah 2:165)

Three forms occur: yuḥibbūnahum, the noun ḥubb in ka-ḥubbi llāhi, and ḥubban in ashaddu ḥubban li-llāhi.

Love can therefore be compared with another love in intensity and standing.

The issue is not merely strong attachment to a person or thing. The verse first identifies rivals taken besides The God, then describes their being loved.

Love here accompanies setting up rivals and assigning another reference point the standing belonging to The God.

Yuḥibbūnahum does not make loving anyone besides The God associationism. Someone may deeply love parents, a spouse, children, or friends without treating them as divine rivals.

The beloved’s standing is decisive. When their will, command, or approval becomes a superior authority against divine truth, attachment shapes fundamental orientation and loyalty.

One sin or wrong choice nevertheless does not independently establish someone’s inward associationism. Ethical failure, unhealthy attachment, and consciously assigning rival authority must remain distinct.

Lexical study clarifies affection and its intensity. The complete boundaries of andād and shirk require independent study.

Love and giving what one loves

Āl ʿImrān states:

لَنْ تَنَالُوا الْبِرَّ حَتَّىٰ تُنْفِقُوا مِمَّا تُحِبُّونَ وَمَا تُنْفِقُوا مِنْ شَيْءٍ فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ بِهِ عَلِيمٌ

lan tanālū al-birra ḥattā tunfiqū mimmā tuḥibbūna wa-mā tunfiqū min shayʾin fa-inna llāha bihi ʿalīmun

“You will not attain righteousness until you spend from what you love. Whatever you spend, The God knows it.” (Āl ʿImrān 3:92)

Tuḥibbūna concerns things someone values.

Giving only worthless or surplus things is not the instruction’s whole point. Giving from what one loves shows that attachment to possessions does not control every decision.

Al-Baqarah 2:177 also describes giving wealth ʿalā ḥubbihi. Interpretations identify love of wealth or love of The God. Either way, love and spending are connected.

Al-Insān 76:8 likewise states:

وَيُطْعِمُونَ الطَّعَامَ عَلَىٰ حُبِّهِ مِسْكِينًا وَيَتِيمًا وَأَسِيرًا

wa-yuṭʿimūna al-ṭaʿāma ʿalā ḥubbihi miskīnan wa-yatīman wa-asīrā

“They give food, despite loving it, to someone in need, an orphan, and a captive.” (Al-Insān 76:8)

Here too the pronoun permits interpretive discussion. Nearby al-ṭaʿām supports love of food; the next verse’s divine orientation also matters for considering love of The God.

Ḥubb does not mean giving in these verses. Giving comes from the separate verbs anfaqū, ātā, and yuṭʿimūna.

The verses connect love with practical choice: someone may love something and nevertheless relinquish it for another’s good.

Al-Ḥashr 59:9 likewise connects affection for migrants with preferring them over oneself, even in personal need.

Love need not mean keeping the beloved possession for oneself. It can accompany generosity, selflessness, and prioritizing others’ rights.

5. Divine love, love of The God, and making something dear

Yuḥibbuhum wa-yuḥibbūnahu: love in both directions

Al-Māʾidah states:

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا مَنْ يَرْتَدَّ مِنْكُمْ عَنْ دِينِهِ فَسَوْفَ يَأْتِي اللَّهُ بِقَوْمٍ يُحِبُّهُمْ وَيُحِبُّونَهُ أَذِلَّةٍ عَلَى الْمُؤْمِنِينَ أَعِزَّةٍ عَلَى الْكَافِرِينَ يُجَاهِدُونَ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ وَلَا يَخَافُونَ لَوْمَةَ لَائِمٍ ذَٰلِكَ فَضْلُ اللَّهِ يُؤْتِيهِ مَنْ يَشَاءُ وَاللَّهُ وَاسِعٌ عَلِيمٌ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū man yartadda minkum ʿan dīnihi fa-sawfa yaʾtī llāhu bi-qawmin yuḥibbuhum wa-yuḥibbūnahu adhillatin ʿalā al-muʾminīna aʿizzatin ʿalā al-kāfirīna yujāhidūna fī sabīli llāhi wa-lā yakhāfūna lawmata lāʾimin dhālika faḍlu llāhi yuʾtīhi man yashāʾu wa-llāhu wāsiʿun ʿalīmun

“You who believe: whoever turns back from their religion, The God will bring a people whom He loves and who love Him, humble toward believers and firm toward disbelievers. They strive in the way of The God and fear no critic’s blame. That is the bounty of The God, given to whom He wills; The God is encompassing in bounty and knowing.” (Al-Māʾidah 5:54)

Love runs in both directions: The God loves them, and they love Him.

The verbs share a family but exchange subject and object.

Divine love must not automatically inherit every psychological and bodily feature of human love. Need, longing, excitement, fear of separation, and dependence do not thereby become established of The God.

Conversely, yuḥibbu with The God as subject should not unnecessarily become an unrelated meaning such as merely rewarding. The Qur’an speaks of divine love; its relations to reward, mercy, approval, and conduct require broader study.

Al-Māʾidah 5:54 connects love with the beloved people’s conduct. Context establishes those qualities, which are not ḥubb’s entire lexical meaning.

In kuntum tuḥibbūna llāh: love and following

Āl ʿImrān states:

قُلْ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ تُحِبُّونَ اللَّهَ فَاتَّبِعُونِي يُحْبِبْكُمُ اللَّهُ وَيَغْفِرْ لَكُمْ ذُنُوبَكُمْ وَاللَّهُ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ

qul in kuntum tuḥibbūna llāha fa-ttabiʿūnī yuḥbibkumu llāhu wa-yaghfir lakum dhunūbakum wa-llāhu ghafūrun raḥīmun

“Say: If you love The God, follow me; The God will love you and forgive your sins. The God is forgiving and merciful.” (Āl ʿImrān 3:31)

Love of The God is connected with following the Messenger.

Tuḥibbūna and ittabiʿūnī are nevertheless separate verbs: loving and following.

The Qur’an brings professed love into relation with commands and conduct without changing love’s lexical meaning into obedience.

Someone may claim love yet refuse to follow a path recognized as true when personal wishes conflict with it.

Conduct can reveal how far professed love shapes decisions.

The direct address to Muḥammad and his mission as Messenger also matter. The construction alone cannot turn every action later attributed to him in historical reports into an enduring command without independent evidence.

Ḥubb retains its loving meaning. Following’s conditions and scope require the mission’s context and the relevant obedience passages.

Whom does The God love?

Divine love is connected with varied qualities and conduct throughout the Qur’an.

The God loves those doing good in Al-Baqarah 2:195 and Āl ʿImrān 3:134; the mindful in Āl ʿImrān 3:76 and Al-Tawbah 9:4; the steadfast in Āl ʿImrān 3:146; the trusting in Āl ʿImrān 3:159; and those repenting and seeking purity in Al-Baqarah 2:222.

Al-Māʾidah 5:42, Al-Ḥujurāt 49:9, and Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:8 likewise say The God loves al-muqsiṭīn, the equitable.

Conversely, The God does not love wrongdoers, transgressors, corrupters, the treacherous, and the arrogant; examples include Al-Baqarah 2:190, Al-Baqarah 2:205, Āl ʿImrān 3:57, Al-Anfāl 8:58, and Al-Naḥl 16:23.

Divine love is therefore not detached from justice and conduct.

Lā yuḥibbu must nevertheless not become an unexamined judgment on someone’s entire being or eternal fate. Denying love toward transgressors condemns transgression, while repentance passages present possibilities of returning and reform.

Some verses negate love toward an act itself, such as corruption in Al-Baqarah 2:205; others identify people through conduct-related descriptions.

Translation and analysis should preserve that grammatical distinction.

Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:8 does not forbid kindness and justice toward people who neither fought believers over religion nor expelled them, concluding that The God loves the equitable.

Divine love of justice is therefore not confined to relations within one religious community.

Ḥabbaba ilaykumu al-īmān: making faith dear

Al-Ḥujurāt states:

وَاعْلَمُوا أَنَّ فِيكُمْ رَسُولَ اللَّهِ لَوْ يُطِيعُكُمْ فِي كَثِيرٍ مِنَ الْأَمْرِ لَعَنِتُّمْ وَلَٰكِنَّ اللَّهَ حَبَّبَ إِلَيْكُمُ الْإِيمَانَ وَزَيَّنَهُ فِي قُلُوبِكُمْ وَكَرَّهَ إِلَيْكُمُ الْكُفْرَ وَالْفُسُوقَ وَالْعِصْيَانَ أُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الرَّاشِدُونَ

wa-ʿlamū anna fīkum rasūla llāhi law yuṭīʿukum fī kathīrin mina al-amri la-ʿanittum wa-lākinna llāha ḥabbaba ilaykumu al-īmāna wa-zayyanahu fī qulūbikum wa-karraha ilaykumu al-kufra wa-l-fusūqa wa-l-ʿiṣyāna ulāʾika humu al-rāshidūna

“Know that the Messenger of The God is among you. Were he to obey you in many affairs, you would suffer. But The God made faith dear to you and adorned it within your hearts, and made disbelief, defiance, and disobedience hateful to you. Those are the rightly directed.” (Al-Ḥujurāt 49:7)

Ḥabbaba is form II, making something loved or dear.

The God makes faith dear to the addressees.

Aḥabba expresses loving; ḥabbaba here expresses making something dear to someone else.

Zayyanahu fī qulūbikum also follows, from a separate root, describing faith being adorned in the hearts.

Endearing and adorning are related expressions but lexically distinct.

Karraha conversely means making something hateful. The contrast gives importance to the emotional orientation toward faith and disbelief.

Ḥabbaba alone cannot establish the full relationship among divine guidance, human choice, and belief formation. That requires wider study of guidance and responsibility.

Maḥabbatan minnī: love in Moses’ story

Ṭā Hā states:

أَنِ اقْذِفِيهِ فِي التَّابُوتِ فَاقْذِفِيهِ فِي الْيَمِّ فَلْيُلْقِهِ الْيَمُّ بِالسَّاحِلِ يَأْخُذْهُ عَدُوٌّ لِي وَعَدُوٌّ لَهُ وَأَلْقَيْتُ عَلَيْكَ مَحَبَّةً مِنِّي وَلِتُصْنَعَ عَلَىٰ عَيْنِي

ani iqdhifīhi fī al-tābūti fa-iqdhifīhi fī al-yammi fa-l-yulqihi al-yammu bi-l-sāḥili yaʾkhudhhu ʿaduwwun lī wa-ʿaduwwun lahu wa-alqaytu ʿalayka maḥabbatan minnī wa-li-tuṣnaʿa ʿalā ʿaynī

“Place him in the chest and cast it into the river; let the river cast him onto the bank, where an enemy of Mine and his takes him. I cast upon you love from Me, so that you might be nurtured under My eye.” (Ṭā Hā 20:39)

Maḥabbah is a noun of love.

The God says He cast love from Himself upon Moses.

Two readings of its direction can be considered.

One understands special love from The God toward Moses.

Another understands Moses being made dear to people encountering him. His placement with people potentially dangerous to him supports this reading.

The senses are related but grammatically and conceptually distinct: divine love versus love arising toward Moses in other hearts through divine care.

Maḥabbatan minnī does not confine love’s exact character to one psychological explanation.

The close rendering “I cast upon you love from Me” therefore leaves both readings available for examination.

Aḥibbāʾuhu: claiming special beloved status

Al-Māʾidah states:

وَقَالَتِ الْيَهُودُ وَالنَّصَارَىٰ نَحْنُ أَبْنَاءُ اللَّهِ وَأَحِبَّاؤُهُ قُلْ فَلِمَ يُعَذِّبُكُمْ بِذُنُوبِكُمْ بَلْ أَنْتُمْ بَشَرٌ مِمَّنْ خَلَقَ يَغْفِرُ لِمَنْ يَشَاءُ وَيُعَذِّبُ مَنْ يَشَاءُ وَلِلَّهِ مُلْكُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ وَمَا بَيْنَهُمَا وَإِلَيْهِ الْمَصِيرُ

wa-qālati al-yahūdu wa-l-naṣārā naḥnu abnāʾu llāhi wa-aḥibbāʾuhu qul fa-lima yuʿadhdhibukum bi-dhunūbikum bal antum basharun mimman khalaqa yaghfiru li-man yashāʾu wa-yuʿadhdhibu man yashāʾu wa-li-llāhi mulku al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍi wa-mā baynahumā wa-ilayhi al-maṣīru

“The Jews and Christians said, ‘We are sons of The God and His beloved ones.’ Say: Why then does He punish you for your sins? Rather, you are humans among His creation. He forgives whom He wills and punishes whom He wills. Dominion over the heavens, earth, and what lies between belongs to The God; to Him is the return.” (Al-Māʾidah 5:18)

Aḥibbāʾ is a plural related to ḥabīb, naming beloved people.

“We are sons of The God and His beloved ones” is a reported claim, followed by a question about sin and punishment.

Claiming special beloved status therefore does not establish immunity from ethical responsibility.

Nor should the reported historical claim be generalized uncritically to every Jewish or Christian person in every period. The Qur’an quotes and criticizes a particular claim.

Aḥibbāʾ shows the family expressing being loved as well as loving.

Calling oneself or one’s group beloved does not independently establish that relationship’s reality or its supposed privileges.

6. Distinctions from nearby roots and words

Ḥ B B (ḥubb) compared with W D D (wudd)

Study of W D D

Wudd and mawaddah concern love and friendship, sometimes wanting or wishing.

Ḥubb likewise concerns love, and the families overlap in many uses.

W D D also clearly includes wishing: wadda in constructions such as waddū law expresses wanting an event to occur.

Ḥ B B, alongside love, expresses preferring one thing over another through form X.

This does not make wudd invariably practical affection and ḥubb invariably inward emotion. Such an absolute division fails to fit their ranges.

Al-Wadūd describes The God in Hūd 11:90 and Al-Burūj 85:14. Al-Ḥabīb does not occur as a divine name or nominal description in the Qur’an; this naming distinction matters.

Ḥ B B (ḥubb) compared with R Ḥ M (raḥima)

Study of R Ḥ M

Mercy concerns kindness, conveying good, and merciful relations in the Qur’an.

Love and mercy can coexist, as in parents’ affection and care for children.

They nevertheless differ lexically. Someone may love another yet fail in conduct, or treat someone mercifully and responsibly without close emotional attachment.

Yuḥibbu should not always become shows mercy, just as every raḥmah need not mean emotional affection.

Ḥ B B (ḥubb) compared with SH GH F (shaghafa)

Study of SH GH F

Shaghaf stands beside ḥubban in Yūsuf 12:30.

There it concerns love’s intense penetration into the heart.

Ḥubb admits different intensities, from mild affection to powerful attachment.

Shaghaf therefore specifies the attachment’s intensity and quality in that combination; ḥubb alone does not always mean overwhelming infatuation.

Ḥ B B (ḥubb) compared with H W Y (hawā)

Study of H W Y

Qur’anic hawā concerns personal inclination and desire, criticized in some contexts when wrongful desire is followed.

Ḥubb can likewise concern desires but has a broader field: love of The God, justice, doing good, family, and wealth.

Hawā in some constructions emphasizes the pull and direction of personal desire.

Every love is therefore not condemned desire; conversely, everything loved is not thereby true or good.

Ḥ B B (ḥubb) compared with ʿ SH Q (ʿishq)

Arabic ʿishq is known for intense love or passion, but the word itself does not occur in the Qur’an.

Modern Persian uses it for romantic love and intense attachment to The God, a person, or a goal.

Every Qur’anic ḥubb should not become passionate love: its range extends from liking conduct or wealth to relations between The God and His servants.

Nor should intensity be denied. Ashaddu ḥubban li-llāh in Al-Baqarah 2:165 shows ḥubb expressing powerful love without needing replacement by another word.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Love and affection are primary equivalents for ḥubb and maḥabbah. Attachment, liking, and sometimes passion can fit parts of their range according to subject.

Love suits Al-Baqarah 2:165, concerning attachment to supposed divine rivals and comparisons of love for The God.

“Love of desirable things” conveys ḥubbu al-shahawāt in Āl ʿImrān 3:14. “Worshipping desires” adds excessive interpretation without independent contextual evidence for such dependence.

Ḥubban accompanies shaghafahā in Yūsuf 12:30. “Love has penetrated her heart” conveys the combination’s intensity, which comes from both words together.

For the perfect verb aḥabba, loved or liked can fit. Aḥabbu in Yūsuf 12:33, however, is an elative rather than a perfect verb: aḥabbu ilayya means dearer or preferable to me. The accusative elative in Al-Tawbah 9:24 is also aḥabba, so unvocalized spelling or the final vowel alone cannot establish its role.

Aḥabbu ilā abīnā in Yūsuf 12:8 is dearer to our father. Preserve the comparative construction’s distinction from a simple loving verb.

Istaḥabba with ʿalā naturally means preferred. Fuṣṣilat 41:17 has “preferred blindness over guidance,” rather than “fell passionately in love with blindness.”

Ḥabbaba means made dear or made lovable. “Made faith dear to you” clearly conveys Al-Ḥujurāt 49:7.

Maḥabbatan minnī in Ṭā Hā 20:39 is best kept close: “I cast upon you love from Me.” Explain its expression through Moses’ relationships separately.

Aḥibbāʾuhu in Al-Māʾidah 5:18 means His beloved ones. Preserve the reported claim and the immediate criticism following it.

Ḥabb in nature passages is grain or seed, with harvested grain fitting agricultural settings. Clarify ḥabbu al-ḥaṣīd’s connection with harvestable produce.

Ḥabbah is one seed. It suits both occurrences in Al-Baqarah 2:261; mustard seed in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:47 and Luqmān 31:16 preserves the image of smallness.

Spelling resemblance between ḥabb and ḥubb does not make agricultural verses discuss affection or make love passages fundamentally refer to planting seeds.

Khayr in expressions such as ḥubbu al-khayr requires context. Wealth fits Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:8. Al-khayr in Ṣād 38:32 concerns horses, but love’s relationship with remembrance of the Lord has different interpretations requiring examination there. These uses do not make every Qur’anic khayr mean wealth or horses.

Conclusion

Arabic Ḥ B B has several branches: ḥabb and ḥabbah concern grain and seed, while ḥubb and maḥabbah concern love, affection, and attachment.

Earlier sources connect love with adherence and constancy. This can illuminate one feature of affection without proving every love historically developed from one material image.

Qur’anic love can concern people, family, wealth, comfort, good conduct, faith, and The God. Love itself does not determine the beloved’s moral value.

The elative aḥabb expresses preference or being dearer; istaḥabba with ʿalā expresses prioritizing an option over another.

Love is not always a feeling isolated from conduct. People can sacrifice for the beloved, give treasured wealth, or prioritize right over personal wishes. Misplaced attachment can instead prioritize someone’s advantage or approval over truth and justice.

Those behavioral consequences must not become love’s lexical definition. Loving, obeying, giving, loyalty, and setting up rivals are distinct concepts connected in some contexts.

The Qur’an describes both human love for The God and divine love for people. It connects this with justice, doing good, mindfulness, patience, trust, and returning from wrongdoing, without requiring reduction to human emotion or material reward alone.

Nor does merely claiming membership of a people or community establish being loved by The God.

Ḥ B B therefore requires distinguishing grain and seed in nature from love and attachment in relationships and inclinations. Love’s branch extends from liking to preference, beloved status, and reciprocal relations between humanity and The God. The object loved, sentence construction, and Qur’anic context establish its kind, intensity, and worth.
