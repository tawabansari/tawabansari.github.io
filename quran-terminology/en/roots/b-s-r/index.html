The Qur’anic Root B S R (Busūr and Bāsir)

From unripe dates and acting before the proper time to a grim, displeased face; a study of عَبَسَ وَبَسَرَ and وُجُوهٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ بَاسِرَةٌ

1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

B S R has several established uses in older Arabic. Busr denotes dates that have not yet reached the soft-ripe ruṭab stage. Related expressions concern hastening to do something before its appropriate time. In another use, basara and busūr concern drawing the face into a scowl, looking sour, and showing displeasure or grimness in the face.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an, in two verses of two surahs:

  • بَسَرَ (basara) in Al-Muddaththir 74:22: a form I perfect verb.
  • بَاسِرَةٌ (bāsiratun) in Al-Qiyāmah 75:24: a feminine active participle of the same verb.

Both uses concern facial expression. In the first, someone scowls after considering and weighing what to say. In the second, a group of faces on the Day of Resurrection is described as bāsirah.

Other forms, including busr, busūr, busrah, ibtisār, absara, and tabassara, occur in lexicons but not in the Qur’an.

This family raises an important research question: did the sense of scowling develop directly from unripeness and acting prematurely, or are these distinct but neighboring branches within a lexical family?

Older lexicographers do not fully agree on the relationship. Actual usages must therefore be established first; possible connections can then be examined cautiously.

2. Semantic cores in older Arabic

Unripeness and being before the proper time

In Maqāyīs al-lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies two underlying senses for B S R:

«أَحَدُهُمَا الطَّرَاءَةُ وَأَنْ يَكُونَ الشَّيْءُ قَبْلَ إِنَاهُ، وَالْأَصْلُ الْآخَرُ وُقُوفُ الشَّيْءِ وَقِلَّةُ حَرَكَتِهِ»

aḥaduhumā al-ṭarāʾatu wa-an yakūna al-shayʾu qabla ināhu wa-al-aṣlu al-ākharu wuqūfu al-shayʾi wa-qillatu ḥarakatihi

Translation: “One is freshness and something being before its time of maturity; the other is something remaining still and having little movement.”

In the first branch, he records busr for something fresh and not yet mature, and nabātun busrun for plants at an early stage of growth.

Related uses concern doing something before its appropriate time.

In Arabic, بَسَرَ الرَّجُلُ حَاجَتَهُ can mean that someone acts to obtain what he wants before the suitable time.

Likewise, بَسَرَ غَرِيمَهُ is used of a creditor demanding payment from a debtor before the debt falls due.

These usages show that part of the family concerns more than doing something quickly: acting before the appropriate time is central.

Busr: dates that are not yet fully ripe

One of the root’s clearest physical uses is busr as the name of a stage in dates ripening.

Lisān al-ʿArab records stages of the fruit’s development and ripening: ṭalʿ, khalāl, balaḥ, busr, ruṭab, and tamr.

In this sequence, busr is a stage at which the date has grown and may have acquired color, but has not yet reached the soft-ripe ruṭab stage or full ripeness.

Its singular unit is busrah. A date palm whose fruit has reached that stage is described by أَبْسَرَتِ النَّخْلَةُ.

Here the word names a natural stage of fruit growth. A date being unripe does not necessarily make it spoiled, worthless, or inedible.

This matters when using the date’s busr stage as an image for understanding busūr of the face: unripeness should not be equated with rotting or corruption.

Fruit may not yet be fully ripe while its present condition remains a natural part of its growth.

Is basara meaning “to scowl” also connected with unripeness?

In al-Mufradāt, al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī attempts to connect these branches.

He first explains basr through hastening to do something before its proper time. Concerning Al-Muddaththir 74:22, he then says that the person displayed his frown prematurely.

Al-Rāghib seeks to retain the same semantic direction in explaining وَوُجُوهٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ بَاسِرَةٌ. In his account, the faces show displeasure and apprehension before reaching their eventual punishment.

This proposed connection deserves examination, but has limitations.

First, lexicons independently record basara in the sense of scowling.

Second, Al-Muddaththir 74:22 contains no explicit word meaning “before the proper time.”

Third, in Al-Qiyāmah 75:24, the meaning of grim or scowling faces is clear without positing premature timing.

Al-Rāghib’s explanation therefore cannot be treated as the certain and necessary meaning of both Qur’anic occurrences.

A semantic relationship between unripeness and a grim face may exist. Establishing that one historically generated the other, however, requires further evidence.

Standing still and ceasing to move

Ibn Fāris’s second branch concerns stillness and reduced movement.

Lexicons record أَبْسَرَ الْمَرْكَبُ of a vessel that has stopped moving.

In discussing Al-Muddaththir 74:22, al-Qurṭubī likewise reports this usage among the people of Yemen: forms of the family are used when a vessel moves neither forward nor backward.

One can imagine a resemblance between ceasing to move and a state of contraction or grimness. That resemblance alone does not prove that Arabic scowling necessarily developed from a ship stopping.

The two senses reported by Ibn Fāris and the independently established sense of a scowling face are best recognized on their own terms, without reducing them to an invented historical origin.

3. Busūr: a grim or displeased face in Arabic

The meaning of basara wajhahu

Under B S R, Kitāb al-ʿAyn connects busūr with frowning.

It explains that someone may look grim and frowning because of worry or thought about something.

In some uses, the same book also connects al-bāsir with overpowering and domination. That sense should not automatically be equated with facial expression itself.

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

«بَسَرَ الرَّجُلُ وَجْهَهُ بُسُورًا أَيْ كَلَحَ»

basara al-rajulu wajhahu busūran ay kalaḥa

Translation: “The man performed busūr with his face: he drew it into a scowl and looked sour.”

Al-Zajjāj is also quoted as explaining basara in Al-Muddaththir 74:22 through looking with intense aversion or displeasure.

This use describes the outward manifestation of a condition.

A person may scowl because of anger, worry, fear, pain, dissatisfaction, or burdensome thought. The verb itself does not determine the exact cause.

Basara should therefore not always be restricted to “became angry” or “became afraid.”

What it directly describes is visible grimness and displeasure in the face. The inward state is understood from context.

A witness from Arabic poetry

A poem by Tawbah ibn al-Ḥumayyir reads:

وَقَدْ رَابَنِي مِنْهَا صُدُودٌ رَأَيْتُهُ

wa-qad rābanī minhā ṣudūdun raʾaytuhu

وَإِعْرَاضُهَا عَنْ حَاجَتِي وَبُسُورُهَا

wa-iʿrāḍuhā ʿan ḥājatī wa-busūruhā

Translation: “The aloofness I saw in her troubled me, as did her turning away from my request and her scowling.”

The verse appears in the collected poetry of Tawbah and in older literary works, including al-Amālī. Al-Ṭabarī also cites it when explaining basara in Al-Muddaththir 74:22.

The verse presents three close but distinct matters: aloofness, disregard for a request, and scowling.

Busūruhā concerns the woman’s face and outward behavior. Iʿrāḍuhā is separately mentioned and concerns turning away.

Their conjunction shows that busūr and iʿrāḍ can occur together, but are not complete synonyms.

Someone may look grimly at another without turning away. Equally, someone may turn away without scowling at that moment.

The witness matters for Al-Muddaththir 74:22, since the next verse separately uses adbara, “turned away,” after basara.

Tawbah ibn al-Ḥumayyir belongs to the early Islamic period. The verse is therefore a witness to classical Arabic close to the age of revelation, rather than securely pre-Islamic poetry.

A witness attributed to Bishr ibn Abī Khāzim

In his commentary on Al-Muddaththir 74:22, al-Qurṭubī attributes another verse to Bishr ibn Abī Khāzim:

صَبَّحْنَا تَمِيمًا غَدَاةَ الْجِفَارِ

ṣabbaḥnā tamīman ghadāta al-jifāri

بِشَهْبَاءَ مَلْمُومَةٍ بَاسِرَةْ

bi-shahbāʾa malmūmatin bāsirah

Sense translation: “We attacked Tamīm on the morning of al-Jifār, with a densely gathered, grey, fearsome force.”

Here bāsirah describes a fighting force, not an individual’s face. It can evoke the force’s grim and frightening appearance. Since bāsir is also recorded as “overpowering,” however, the witness does not establish that this military description necessarily developed from the facial sense.

The wording varies across sources, including the place-name and some other words. Attribution to a pre-Islamic poet is thus reported, but a definitive judgment about this hemistich’s wording and date requires comparison with a reliable edition of his Dīwān.

As transmitted in the commentaries, the verse shows bāsirah describing the formidable, forbidding appearance of a fighting force. It does not by itself establish a historical progression from a facial sense to a battle scene.

Both Qur’anic uses, however, concern the face directly. This extended descriptive use need not become their basic meaning.

Immature plants in Dhū al-Rummah’s poetry

Dhū al-Rummah uses busrah to describe a stage of plant growth.

His Dīwān reads:

رَعَتْ بَارِضَ الْبُهْمَى جَمِيمًا وَبُسْرَةً

raʿat bāriḍa al-buhmā jamīman wa-busratan

وَصَمْعَاءَ حَتَّى آنَفَتْهَا نِصَالُهَا

wa-ṣamʿāʾa ḥattā ānafathā niṣāluhā

Sense translation: “They grazed the newly emerging buhmā, through its young growth, seed-setting, and filling seed-heads, until the sharp tips of its heads irritated the animals’ noses and deterred them from grazing it.”

The Dīwān’s commentary identifies busrah as a growth stage: fresh growth that has not yet opened. Ṣamʿāʾ describes the seed-head’s enclosure filling before it opens. Other lexical explanations identify jamīm as the stage before seed-setting and busrah as seed-setting. The verse thus names successive stages, rather than merely a plant’s general softness and freshness.

The witness shows that Arabic freshness and immaturity were not confined to dates.

It does not follow that bāsirah in Al-Qiyāmah 75:24 means a young, fresh, or immature face. The Qur’anic facial use belongs to the established branch of scowling.

4. Al-Muddaththir 74:22: thumma ʿabasa wa-basara

Al-Muddaththir 74:22

ثُمَّ عَبَسَ وَبَسَرَ

thumma ʿabasa wa-basara

Translation: “Then he frowned and drew his face into a scowl.”

This is the root B S R’s first Qur’anic occurrence in the order of the text.

The verse is short but belongs to a connected sequence. Al-Muddaththir 74:18, Al-Muddaththir 74:19, Al-Muddaththir 74:20, Al-Muddaththir 74:21, Al-Muddaththir 74:22, Al-Muddaththir 74:23, Al-Muddaththir 74:24, and Al-Muddaththir 74:25 describe someone considering, weighing, looking, scowling, turning away, and acting arrogantly, then making a statement about the Qur’an.

The facial expression forms part of a broader course of behavior.

Are ʿabasa and basara synonyms?

ʿAbasa belongs to ʿ B S and concerns frowning and drawing the face into a contracted expression.

Basara occupies the same semantic field, but lexical explanations highlight facial grimness, sourness, intense aversion, and sometimes a change in facial color.

Al-Ṭabarī explains ʿabasa here as contracting the area between the eyes, and basara through كَلَحَ وَجْهَهُ: making the face sour and grim.

Both verbs therefore concern an unpleasant facial expression, while their focus need not be identical.

ʿAbasa evokes a frown and contraction of the face, particularly the forehead and the area between the brows.

Basara can express a broader appearance of sourness and grimness.

This should not become a fixed sequence in which everyone first frowns and then necessarily changes facial color or enters a more intense stage of anger.

Lexicons show considerable overlap and sometimes explain one word through the other.

Their conjunction in one sentence invites examination of a distinction, but does not establish two definite, invariable physical stages.

Does basara mean frowning prematurely?

As noted above, al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī proposed this explanation.

The verse’s context does not require it.

The person scowls after considering and looking. The verse itself does not say that the behavior occurred before its proper time.

By contrast, scowling is independently established in lexicons and known in poetry.

Al-Rāghib’s account can therefore be examined as an attempt to connect the root’s branches in language history, but “he frowned prematurely” should not be treated as the certain translation of basara.

Does basara mean turning away?

The following verse, Al-Muddaththir 74:23, says ثُمَّ أَدْبَرَ وَاسْتَكْبَرَ.

This expression separately states turning away and acting arrogantly.

It is therefore inappropriate to translate basara alone as “he turned away.”

Basara concerns facial expression. Idbār belongs to D B R and concerns turning away or turning one’s back.

Study of D B R

A person may, of course, turn away with a grim face. The conjunction of two behaviors does not make them lexically identical.

Can a person’s intention be inferred from the face?

Here the Qur’an places facial behavior alongside considering, weighing, turning away, arrogance, and what the person says.

The grim face is one outward sign within the situation described by the passage as a whole.

Basara itself does not establish that every person who looks sour is arrogant or opposed to truth.

Illness, pain, worry, or grief may make someone scowl. Such an expression is not itself proof of moral corruption.

In Al-Muddaththir, moral assessment emerges from the person’s complete behavior and subsequent words, rather than facial expression alone.

The distinction prevents an outward description from becoming an absolute measure of human intention.

5. Al-Qiyāmah 75:24: grim faces confronting a dreadful future

Al-Qiyāmah 75:24

وَوُجُوهٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ بَاسِرَةٌ

wa-wujūhun yawmaʾidhin bāsiratun

Translation: “And faces on that Day will be grim and scowling.”

Here bāsiratun is a feminine nominative active participle functioning as the predicate that states the condition of wujūhun, “faces.” Feminine singular agreement is compatible with this nonhuman plural. It should not be mistaken for an attributive adjective leaving the sentence without a predicate.

In Al-Muddaththir 74:22, basara expressed someone’s behavior as a verb. Here the active participle describes the faces’ condition.

This morphological difference does not establish that those faces remain in the same condition forever without change. The participle itself does not determine the condition’s duration or manner.

What is directly stated is that the faces have a grim and unpleasant expression on the Day in question.

The contrast between nāḍirah and bāsirah

Two verses earlier, the Qur’an introduces faces with a different description.

Al-Qiyāmah 75:22

وُجُوهٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ نَاضِرَةٌ

wujūhun yawmaʾidhin nāḍiratun

Translation: “Faces on that Day will be fresh and radiant.”

By contrast, Al-Qiyāmah 75:24 speaks of faces that are bāsirah.

The contrast matters linguistically.

Nāḍirah belongs to N Ḍ R and concerns freshness, vitality, and facial radiance.

Bāsirah belongs to B S R and here concerns grimness and scowling.

The Qur’an thus presents two contrasting conditions through distinct descriptions: fresh, radiant faces and grim, unpleasant-looking faces.

The distinction emerges from the words and their contextual placement.

The contrast alone should not become a new definition of B S R, as though the root necessarily meant deprivation of mercy or certain condemnation.

Those ideas are understood from the resurrection scene as a whole and the following verses.

Taẓunnu an yufʿala bihā fāqirah

Al-Qiyāmah 75:25

تَظُنُّ أَنْ يُفْعَلَ بِهَا فَاقِرَةٌ

taẓunnu an yufʿala bihā fāqiratun

Translation: “It expects that a backbreaking calamity will befall it.”

This verse explains the expectation confronting the grim faces.

Taẓunnu belongs to Ẓ N N, and fāqiratun to F Q R. Here fāqirah concerns an overwhelming, backbreaking calamity.

Study of Ẓ N N; Study of F Q R

Fear or anticipation of something dreadful is thus understood from the following sentence.

Bāsirah itself does not mean knowing the future, expecting punishment, or being certain of torment.

Al-Ṭabarī describes these faces in his commentary on Al-Qiyāmah 75:24 as grim, frowning, and altered. He transmits kāshirah from Mujāhid and kāliḥah from Qatādah.

These explanations fit the established Arabic meaning of busūr.

A face being kāliḥ or bāsir does not alone determine that someone’s skin literally becomes a specific color or assumes a fixed physical shape.

Some commentaries also mention darkening or a change in facial color. That may form part of interpreting the scene, but should not all be put into the brief word-box meaning of bāsirah.

“Grim and scowling” preserves the direct sense.

Does a grim face mean inward sorrow?

Grimness of face and sorrow are naturally related, but are not identical.

Sorrow may alter a person’s face. Conversely, a grim face may result from anger, pain, or apprehension.

In Al-Qiyāmah 75:24 and Al-Qiyāmah 75:25, the Qur’an itself supplies the cause of apprehension in context.

It is therefore preferable to translate the outward condition through bāsirah first, then understand the inward state and dreadful expectation from the next verse.

This prevents bāsirah from being restricted without explanation to “sorrowful,” “hopeless,” “sinful,” or “condemned to punishment.”

6. Distinctions from related roots

B S R (busūr) and ʿ B S (ʿubūs)

ʿUbūs and busūr are closely related in describing the face.

They occur together in Al-Muddaththir 74:22.

In its clear use, ʿabasa concerns frowning and contracting the face. Basara likewise concerns grimness, sourness, and visible displeasure in the face.

Differences in the kind or extent of the facial expression can be examined, but one verse does not establish that busūr is always a later and more intense stage of ʿubūs.

The overlap in lexicons is sufficient for basara sometimes to be explained through ʿabasa.

Study of ʿ B S

B S R (busūr) and K L Ḥ (kulūḥ)

Kulūḥ is among the closest words to busūr in descriptions of the face.

كَلَحَ وَجْهُهُ concerns a face becoming sour and drawn into a scowl.

Al-Ṭabarī likewise explains basara through kalaḥa.

Not every detail of kulūḥ should, however, be transferred to busūr. Some older descriptions connect kulūḥ with exposed teeth, but bāsirah in Al-Qiyāmah 75:24 does not necessarily state that teeth are visible.

The words overlap in describing an unpleasant face, but are not completely interchangeable in every use.

Study of K L Ḥ

B S R (busūr) and Q Ṭ B (taqṭīb)

Taqṭīb concerns drawing the brows together and contracting the forehead.

In Arabic, this can accompany ʿubūs and busūr.

In commentary usage, however, bāsir can describe the entire face more broadly.

Someone who frowns may therefore also have a bāsir face, but bāsir alone does not establish every detail of muscular contraction in the forehead or face.

B S R (busūr) and N Ḍ R (naḍārah)

The two stand in contrast in Al-Qiyāmah 75:22 and Al-Qiyāmah 75:24.

Naḍārah concerns freshness and vitality; busūr concerns facial grimness and displeasure.

The contrast is clear, but N Ḍ R must also be distinguished from N Ẓ R. Nāẓiratun in Al-Qiyāmah 75:23 belongs to N Ẓ R, a different root from nāḍiratun in Al-Qiyāmah 75:22.

Resemblance in form should not make facial radiance and looking into one meaning.

Study of N Ḍ R; Study of N Ẓ R

B S R (busūr) and B SH R (bishr)

In Arabic, bishr, with i after b, concerns an open, cheerful expression and visible gladness of face.

Busūr, conversely, concerns grimness and scowling.

Older lexical reports also mark this difference, explaining bishr through an open expression and basr through frowning and grimness.

The root consonants differ clearly: B SH R has shīn, whereas B S R has sīn.

Busūr cannot therefore be treated as a derivative of B SH R.

Study of B SH R

B S R (busūr) and B Ṣ R (baṣar)

Baṣar, from B Ṣ R, concerns seeing and sight.

The Qur’anic use of B S R concerns facial expression.

Al-Zajjāj is quoted as explaining basara in Al-Muddaththir 74:22 through looking with intense aversion. That usage does not make the roots identical.

An unpleasant look can be one manifestation of a bāsir face, but B S R itself does not mean the capacity for sight or observation.

Study of B Ṣ R

B S R (busr) and R Ṭ B (ruṭab)

In agricultural language, busr names a stage in dates ripening before ruṭab.

Ruṭab is another stage of the fruit’s ripening.

They are related within date growth, but the Qur’an does not use busr for fruit.

Unripe dates should therefore not be read into the two verses describing scowling faces.

Study of R Ṭ B

Bāsūr and bawāsīr

Arabic lexicons record bāsūr and its plural bawāsīr as names for a medical condition, commonly hemorrhoids.

Some lexicographers, including in Kitāb al-ʿAyn and Lisān al-ʿArab, identify bāsūr as an Arabicized loanword.

The similarity between bāsūr and bāsir therefore does not prove direct derivation from one historical root.

Neither Qur’anic occurrence of B S R concerns hemorrhoids.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

For the sense used in the Qur’an, “scowling,” “drawing the face into a scowl,” and “facial grimness” are suitable equivalents.

The choice should nevertheless distinguish the verb from the active participle.

In Al-Muddaththir 74:22, basara is a verb expressing the person’s behavior. “He drew his face into a scowl” or “he scowled” is natural.

Since ʿabasa also occurs in that verse, both verbs should be rendered without empty repetition, while avoiding a distinction greater than the evidence supports.

Proposed translation:

“Then he frowned and drew his face into a scowl.”

In Al-Qiyāmah 75:24, bāsiratun describes faces. “Grim,” “scowling,” and “frowning” are therefore appropriate.

Principal proposal:

“And faces on that Day will be grim and scowling.”

The rendering need not add “frightened,” “blackened,” “hopeless,” or “expecting punishment” to bāsirah itself. Where needed, such ideas can be explained from Al-Qiyāmah 75:25.

For non-Qur’anic busr, “unripe dates” is appropriate. For ibtisār concerning premature action, “doing something ahead of its proper time” or “premature haste” is natural.

Preserving an image of unripe dates should not lead to invented renderings such as “an unripe face,” “a premature face,” or “he frowned before the proper time” in the two Qur’anic verses.

The lexical image belongs in the research explanation; translation should convey the expression’s natural, established meaning.

8. Conclusion

B S R has several Arabic semantic branches. One concerns freshness, unripeness, and acting prematurely; another, as reported by Ibn Fāris, concerns stillness and reduced movement. Alongside them stands the established use basara wajhahu for facial grimness and scowling.

In older Arabic, busr names a stage of date growth before ruṭab. Basara al-ḥājah concerns pursuing a request at an inappropriate time, and basara al-dayn demanding a debt prematurely.

Some lexicographers, particularly al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī, seek to connect these uses with facial busūr through prematurity. The explanation deserves examination, but lacks sufficient support to become the certain meaning of the two Qur’anic verses.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an, both times concerning the face.

In Al-Muddaththir 74:22, basara accompanies ʿabasa and describes a grim, scowling expression within the person’s behavior.

In Al-Qiyāmah 75:24, bāsiratun describes faces contrasting with nāḍiratun faces. The next verse speaks of expecting a severe, backbreaking event, but this expectation and apprehension do not derive from bāsirah itself.

The visible condition of the face must therefore be distinguished from its inward cause.

Nor does a frowning face in a particular context itself prove moral corruption. In Al-Muddaththir, assessment of the person’s behavior emerges from his thinking, words, turning away, and arrogance together. In Al-Qiyāmah, the faces’ condition is described within a scene concerning their fate in the Hereafter.

Final definition for Forqan:

B S R is an Arabic family with uses concerning unripeness, acting before the proper time, and drawing the face into a scowl. In Qur’anic use, it denotes grimness, scowling, and visible displeasure in the face. Basara in Al-Muddaththir 74:22 expresses scowling as a behavior, while bāsiratun in Al-Qiyāmah 75:24 describes grim, frowning faces. Its historical connection with unripe dates or premature haste is uncertain and should not be imposed on translation. The cause of facial grimness, the degree of apprehension, and its moral or otherworldly consequence are likewise understood from context, rather than from the root alone.
