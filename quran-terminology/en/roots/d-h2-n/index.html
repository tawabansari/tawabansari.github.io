Oil, anointing and softening: from the physical properties of oil to accommodation and compromise in conduct

Arabic D H N includes words concerning oil, anointing, softness and certain forms of accommodation in speech and conduct. Duhn names an oily substance; dahana means to apply oil. Related idhān and mudāhanah also developed behavioral uses, where accommodation can mean yielding, compromising or concealing actual opposition.

The root occurs five times in four Qur’anic verses: al-duhni in Al-Muʾminūn 23:20, al-dihāni in Al-Raḥmān 55:37, mudhinūna in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:81, and the two verbs tudhinu and fa-yudhinūna in Al-Qalam 68:9.

These uses have two important branches: physical oil and its properties, and behavioral accommodation or compromise. Not every behavioral use necessarily entails lying or hypocrisy, nor does every physical property of oil transfer to all derivatives.

1. The semantic core and physical uses

Oil and anointing

Duhn is a general Arabic name for an oily substance. It can refer to vegetable oils and other fatty materials.

Lisān al-ʿArab records:

الدُّهْنُ مَعْرُوفٌ

al-duhnu maʿrūfun

That is, “oil is well known.” It then explains dahana as applying oil to the head or another object.

Three things should be distinguished: oil as a substance, applying it, and the condition it produces on a surface.

Oil can make a surface greasy, soft, slippery or shiny. These qualities are not all present in every use of the word. Slipperiness or brilliance need not be the main point when edible oil is mentioned.

Ibn Fāris’s account of the root

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris explains D H N through līn, suhūlah and qillah: softness, ease or smoothness, and smallness of amount.

He discusses physical duhn and behavioral idhān within this framework.

Softness and ease of movement or interaction may thus connect anointing with mild conduct.

Smallness of amount also appears in some family uses, including scant rainfall and an animal’s limited milk supply.

The root should therefore not be confined to “softening.” Ibn Fāris seeks connections among uses; his explanation does not prove that every derivative arose from a single physical image.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: softness, ease and smallness of amount

Anointing and slipperiness

Arabic dahana means applying oil to something; tadahhana and iddahana are recorded for applying oil to oneself.

Anointing can reduce friction and make a surface softer or more slippery. This physical property offers an intelligible explanation for extension into conduct.

Historically, however, two claims differ: Arabs associated anointing with softness, and all figurative meanings arose from this image at a particular time.

Lexical evidence supports the first; the second requires further historical examination.

2. Early Arabic uses

Oil and brilliance: a verse by Al-Aʿshā

A verse by the pre-Islamic poet Al-Aʿshā reads:

وَأَجْرَدَ مِنْ فُحُولِ الْخَيْلِ طِرْفٍ
كَأَنَّ عَلَى شَوَاكِلِهِ دِهَانَا

wa-ajrada min fuḥūli al-khayli ṭirfin / ka-anna ʿalā shawākilihi dihānā

Translation: “And a noble, short-coated stallion, as though oil had been applied to his flanks.” Ajrad describes the horse’s sparse or short coat; ṭirf its nobility and select quality. This is not ṭarf meaning eye or gaze.

The poet uses dihān to describe the horse’s appearance.

Oil on the horse’s body may evoke softness, smoothness and brilliance. Dihān here is something applied to a surface, or something that makes its appearance resemble an oiled surface.

This witness matters for the physical and visual sense of dihān: early poetry also used it to describe a surface’s appearance and sheen.

It does not establish that every Arabic dihān necessarily means transparent oil. Lexical sources record other meanings for the same form.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab: dihān and Al-Aʿshā’s witness

Accommodation in conduct: a verse attributed to Zuhayr

A verse attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā reads:

وَفِي الْحِلْمِ إِدْهَانٌ وَفِي الْعَفْوِ دُرْبَةٌ
وَفِي الصِّدْقِ مَنْجَاةٌ مِنَ الشَّرِّ فَاصْدُقِ

wa-fī al-ḥilmi idhānun wa-fī al-ʿafwi durbatun / wa-fī al-ṣidqi manjātun mina al-sharri fa-uṣduqi

Translation following Abū Al-Haytham’s explanation: “In forbearance there is accommodation and weakness; in pardon, habituation [of the other party to being forgiven]; and in firmness and steadfastness there is escape from harm, so stand firm.”

The verse shows that literary idhān could denote accommodation involving reduced firmness.

It may warn that forbearance and pardon can displace firmness if they become weakness or accustom the other party to forgiveness. In Tahdhīb al-Lughah, Abū Al-Haytham explains ṣidq here as courage and firmness in confrontation. It should therefore not be treated simply as an ethical contrast between accommodation and truth-telling: “truthfulness” is a lexical reading, but this particular commentary emphasizes steadfastness.

Attribution varies: some sources name Zuhayr, while others, including a transmission in Tahdhīb al-Lughah, name Kaʿb ibn Zuhayr. Dursah also occurs instead of durbah and is explained through training or habituation. The particular verse cannot consequently be dated definitively before Islam without independent examination.

Its value here is to illustrate the established behavioral use of idhān.

Tahdhīb al-Lughah: Abū Al-Haytham, firmness and the dursah reading

Light rain and slight moisture

Lisān al-ʿArab records dahana al-maṭaru al-arḍa: rain moistened the ground slightly.

Duhn also denotes enough rain merely to wet the earth’s surface.

This relates to the smallness-of-amount branch in Ibn Fāris’s account.

The point here is not oil but a small amount of moisture settling on the surface.

A thin film of oil may resemble a lightly moistened surface; that resemblance alone does not establish the use’s historical origin.

Other uses

Dahīn is recorded for a weak person and for a she-camel giving little milk.

Mudhun names an oil container. It also denotes a hollow in rock or a mountain where rainwater collects.

Dahanahu bi-al-ʿaṣā means striking someone with a stick; the expression itself does not specify a light blow. Al-Rāghib regards it as a figurative, mocking use.

The family thus extends beyond oil and compromise, although physical oil and behavioral accommodation are most relevant to the four Qur’anic verses.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Rāghib: rain, weakness, vessel and striking

3. The Qur’anic uses

Al-Muʾminūn 23:20: tanbutu bi-al-duhni

وَشَجَرَةً تَخْرُجُ مِنْ طُورِ سَيْنَاءَ تَنْبُتُ بِالدُّهْنِ وَصِبْغٍ لِلْآكِلِينَ

wa-shajaratan takhruju min ṭūri saynāʾa tanbutu bi-al-duhni wa-ṣibghin li-al-ākilīna

Translation: And a tree coming forth from Mount Sinai, growing with oil and a relish for those who eat.

The verse concerns a tree supplying oil and material for food.

Its description and established Arabic uses clearly point to the olive tree, whose fruit is eaten and yields oil.

Direct word meaning should nevertheless be distinguished from identifying the referent.

Al-duhn means oil in the verse. The expression does not name the olive, although context and linguistic evidence support identifying the tree as an olive.

The role of bi- in bi-al-duhni

Tanbutu bi-al-duhni is a noteworthy construction.

“Grows with oil” stays close to the Arabic wording, but should not suggest that the tree needs oil supplied externally to grow.

The tree provides oil in its produce.

Contextually, it can therefore be explained as a tree whose growth and fruit-bearing also yield oil.

This clarifies bi- and the relation between oil and the tree; it does not create an independent root meaning for D H N.

Al-duhn and ṣibgh distinguished

The verse continues:

وَصِبْغٍ لِلْآكِلِينَ

wa-ṣibghin li-al-ākilīna

That is, “and a relish for those who eat.” Ṣibgh belongs to Ṣ B GH, concerning dyeing and coating.

In food contexts it can denote something into which food or bread is dipped, or with which it is coated, such as oil or a sauce.

Al-duhn identifies the oily substance; ṣibghin li-al-ākilīna highlights its use as a relish or dip.

Their coordination alone does not establish that the tree supplies two completely separate substances. One substance may be described from two perspectives: oil in nature, relish or food coating in use.

Duhn here is therefore oil, rather than bodily anointing, moral gentleness or compromise.

Al-Ṭabarī: olive tree, oil and relish

Study of Ṣ B GH

Al-Raḥmān 55:37: ka-al-dihāni

فَإِذَا انْشَقَّتِ السَّمَاءُ فَكَانَتْ وَرْدَةً كَالدِّهَانِ

fa-idhā inshaqqati al-samāʾu fa-kānat wardatan ka-al-dihāni

Translation following the oil interpretation: Then, when the sky splits and becomes rosy, like oil.

The verse describes a transformation of the sky through wardatan and al-dihāni.

Arabic records several uses of dihān that should be distinguished when examining the verse.

The first interpretation: oil or an oily substance

Lexicons record dihān for the substance used in anointing, and also as a plural of duhn.

The sky at its splitting is accordingly compared to oil or an oily substance.

Smoothness, brilliance, fluidity or oil’s visual color may contribute to this comparison.

Al-Ṭabarī prefers the interpretation concerning oil and the brilliance of its color.

Dihān alone, however, does not specify which quality supplies the comparison. The wording identifies neither the oil’s type nor its exact color or physical state.

The second interpretation: red leather

Lexical sources also record al-dihān as al-adīmu al-aḥmaru: red leather.

The comparison may accordingly concern color: the sky becomes red or rosy and resembles red leather.

This directly relates to wardatan, which can express a rosy or red appearance in this context.

Red leather should therefore not be dismissed without examination merely because it is farther from the familiar oil meaning.

The third interpretation: differing colors

Al-Farrāʾ explains the verse through the sky’s resemblance to oil’s differing colors.

This focuses on changing or varied color effects.

It fits the comparison’s visual possibilities but should not become a precise theory of the sky’s physical layers or particular colors.

Which interpretation is preferable?

Oil or an oily substance has clear support in the central D H N family. Dihān for red leather is also lexically attested.

Two principal readings are thus linguistically open to consideration: an oily substance with its visible qualities, and red leather as a color comparison.

Wardatan is an important color clue, but does not by itself require that al-dihān means red leather. The second comparison might also concern brilliance or another visible condition.

Dihān should not be translated categorically as molten metal, hot gas or a substance of a particular chemical composition. These are possible explanations of an image, not the root’s fixed direct meanings.

The verse presents the sky’s transformation as a comparison. Dihān alone does not conclusively establish its physical nature.

Al-Ṭabarī: oil, brilliance and the red-leather reading

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:81: mudhinūna

أَفَبِهَٰذَا الْحَدِيثِ أَنْتُمْ مُدْهِنُونَ

a-fa-bi-hādhā al-ḥadīthi antum mudhinūna

Translation: Do you treat this discourse with laxity and accommodation?

Mudhinūna is the sound masculine plural active participle of adhana.

Here the subject is human conduct toward a discourse, rather than physical oil.

Al-ḥadīth refers to the discourse whose standing and origin the preceding verses explain.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:77 mentions a noble Qur’an; Al-Wāqiʿah 56:80 describes its coming down from the Lord of all worlds.

Verse 81 therefore questions the audience’s response to this discourse.

What does mudhinūna mean?

Lexical idhān and mudāhanah concern accommodation, appeasement and compromise.

In some uses this involves hiding one’s real position, deception or yielding concerning something that should be taken seriously.

Lexicographers sometimes explain mudhin through concealed opposition, outward accommodation or showing something contrary to what is held inwardly.

Exegetical reports include denial, turning away, accommodation and treating the discourse lightly. Al-Ṭabarī’s own explanation emphasizes softening speech toward deniers and siding with them in denial. A behavioral reading may consequently concern accommodating deniers, rather than merely personal laxity toward the discourse.

These explanations do not all operate at the same level.

Denial may describe the audience’s conduct or its result, but idhān does not simply mean declaring false in every lexical use.

Nor should the technical sense of hypocrisy, concealing disbelief while professing faith, be imposed on every mudhin without supporting evidence.

Under the behavioral reading preferred in this study, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:81 focuses on a lax, compromising or unserious response to the discourse; this is not the only transmitted interpretation. Al-Wāqiʿah 56:82 then mentions denial, clarifying the passage’s critical direction.

The proposed “treat with laxity and accommodation” foregrounds the root’s behavioral branch. “Accommodate those who deny it” must also remain in view; reports explaining the word through denial should not be discredited merely because the root has a wider range.

The participle itself does not establish that every addressee inwardly believes and deliberately hides that belief. Assessing individuals’ knowledge and intentions requires independent evidence.

Al-Ṭabarī: accommodating deniers and other reports

Al-Qalam 68:9: tudhinu fa-yudhinūna

وَدُّوا لَوْ تُدْهِنُ فَيُدْهِنُونَ

waddū law tudhinu fa-yudhinūna

Translation: They wish that you would accommodate and compromise, so that they too would accommodate.

The verse contains two verbal forms of D H N.

Tudhinu is a second-person masculine singular imperfect Form IV verb addressed to the Prophet.

Fa-yudhinūna is likewise Form IV imperfect, but third-person masculine plural, referring to the opposing group.

Both express accommodation and yielding; context determines the nature and value of that accommodation.

Why is this accommodation criticized?

Al-Qalam 68:8 reads:

فَلَا تُطِعِ الْمُكَذِّبِينَ

fa-lā tuṭiʿi al-mukadhdhibīna

That is, “So do not obey those who deny.”

Verse 9 then states their desire that the Prophet accommodate them so they too would accommodate.

The connection shows that this is more than good manners, courtesy or gentle speech. Obeying deniers and yielding to their demands are at issue.

Idhān in this setting concerns accommodation that may lead to compromise over truth or reduced firmness on a principled position.

This follows from context, rather than from every kind of gentleness being objectionable in Arabic.

Ṭā Hā 20:44 commands Moses and Aaron to speak gently to Pharaoh; Āl ʿImrān 3:159 praises the Prophet’s gentleness.

Gentleness in dealing with others should therefore be distinguished from yielding over truth to gain an opponent’s approval.

The first may be an ethical virtue; the second is criticized in the context of Al-Qalam 68:9.

Does the verse describe mutual compromise?

Waddū law tudhinu fa-yudhinūna shows the opposing group’s desire for the Prophet’s accommodation, with their own accommodation envisaged in response.

Fa- connects the two verbs.

The verse does not state that, if the Prophet accommodated them, they would certainly believe the truth or abandon denial.

It states their desire for mutual accommodation, rather than a certain, realized outcome of an agreement.

Nor should details of a particular bargain or political proposal enter the translation without evidence.

Al-Ṭabarī: reciprocal accommodation in a denial context

4. The connection between oil and compromise

At first sight, physical oil and behavioral idhān appear to be different uses.

Arabic lexical sources nevertheless explain a connection.

Ibn Fāris links accommodating conduct with anointing. As oil can soften and smooth a surface, compromising behavior can make an encounter milder and apparently free of tension.

Tahdhīb al-Lughah explains idhān through drawing close in speech and softening one’s words.

Mudāhanah is also explained as muṣānaʿah: accommodation that may preserve a relationship, gain approval or conceal opposition.

This semantic connection should not be extended excessively.

Physical anointing is an actual material action; behavioral mudāhanah is a social use. Shared roots do not establish that every instance is deception or that every mild, flexible response constitutes blameworthy compromise.

Compromise and commendable gentleness

Gentleness can take several forms in language and ethics.

Someone may act gently to prevent violence, communicate clearly or preserve another’s dignity. That is not intrinsically abandoning truth.

Another person may hide a position they consider correct, withhold truth or show agreement contrary to their belief to obtain approval.

Gentleness in the latter situation may become mudāhanah or compromising conduct.

Outward appearance does not always establish the boundary. The disputed matter, intention, content and practical consequences also matter.

Al-Qalam 68:9 concerns deniers and pressure for the Prophet’s accommodation. Yielding relates here to his responsibility to convey truth.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:81 likewise concerns responding to a discourse whose standing has been established in the preceding verses.

The criticism follows from the behavioral sense combined with each passage’s context.

5. Important forms and morphology

Duhn

Duhn is a substance noun meaning oil.

In Al-Muʾminūn 23:20 it appears with bi-: bi-al-duhni.

It denotes the oily substance supplied by the tree.

Dihān

Arabic records dihān both for an oily substance and as a plural of duhn.

Other reported uses include red leather and a smooth or polished surface.

In Al-Raḥmān 55:37 it follows the comparative kāf: ka-al-dihāni.

The construction is a comparison, and its precise sense should be considered together with wardatan.

Dahana, tadahhana and iddahana

Dahana is a Form I verb primarily used for applying oil to something.

Physical tadahhana and iddahana are recorded for applying oil to oneself.

These forms do not occur in the Qur’an but help explain the lexical family.

Adhana and mudhin

Adhana is Form IV and, in behavioral use, concerns accommodation, compromising conduct and sometimes concealed opposition.

Mudhin is its active participle.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:81 has plural mudhinūna.

Al-Qalam 68:9 has the two imperfect forms tudhinu and fa-yudhinūna.

Form IV alone does not establish hypocrisy, lying or disbelief. Such meanings accompany particular behavioral uses and contexts.

Mudhun and mudhin

These forms should be distinguished.

Mudhun, with ḍammah on the hāʾ, names a vessel or implement for holding oil.

Mudhin, with kasrah on the hāʾ, is the active participle of adhana, describing a person who accommodates or compromises in the behavioral use.

Their unvocalized spelling may look identical, but their patterns and meanings differ.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: five occurrences, two nouns and three Form IV forms

6. Comparison with nearby roots

D H N (dahn) and Z Y T (zayt)

Arabic zayt means oil and, in Qur’anic usage, is particularly associated with olive oil.

Duhn also denotes oil but is a more general name for oily substances.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:20 clearly points to olive oil, without restricting duhn to olive oil in every Arabic use.

Al-Nūr 24:35 uses zayt of the olive tree’s oil.

The words may thus share a referent without identical ranges of use.

D H N (dahn) and L Y N (līn)

Līn concerns softness in physical and behavioral settings.

D H N also concerns accommodation behaviorally, while retaining its oil and anointing family.

Gentleness may be positive or negative, depending on its object and context.

The accommodation in Al-Qalam 68:9 concerns deniers’ wishes and possible compromise over truth. Gentle speech in Ṭā Hā 20:44, by contrast, is part of Moses and Aaron’s mission to Pharaoh.

A gentle manner alone is therefore not equivalent to mudāhanah.

D H N (dahn) and KH D ʿ (khadaʿa)

KH D ʿ concerns deception and presenting a false appearance to mislead another.

Behavioral idhān starts from accommodation and appeasement, which may in some uses involve concealing one’s position or deception.

Compromise and deception can overlap without being fully synonymous.

Someone may withhold truth from fear of opposition. Adhana alone does not establish whether the intention was deception or merely avoiding conflict.

This matters especially when judging individuals’ intentions.

Study of KH D ʿ

D H N (dahn) and N F Q (nafaqa)

Lexicons explain some accommodating conduct through showing something contrary to an inward position.

This may approach certain uses of hypocrisy.

Qur’anic nifāq has its own semantic family and contexts, however, and should not automatically replace idhān.

Mudhinūna in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:81 may convey concealment or accommodation, but translating it as “hypocrites” without sufficient evidence imports another technical designation.

7. Challenges of understanding and translation

The main difficulty is that the physical and behavioral branches are connected without meaning the same thing.

In Al-Muʾminūn 23:20, al-duhn means oil. Rendering it softness or accommodation would be incorrect.

Al-dihān in Al-Raḥmān 55:37 forms a comparison, with a significant lexical alternative between oil and red leather. Familiarity with modern “oil” should not erase historical possibilities.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:81 and Al-Qalam 68:9 concern human conduct, rather than actual oil use.

Even there, idhān should not always be equated with lying, disbelief or hypocrisy.

Denial and disbelief may be part of the context, while the word itself concerns accommodation, appeasement and yielding.

Compromise over truth should also be distinguished from gentleness. The Qur’an praises gentleness and mild speech in their proper contexts; the accommodation in Al-Qalam 68:9 concerns deniers’ wish that the Prophet yield.

Direct translation should convey the word as clearly and naturally as possible. Intention, causes and ethical consequences belong in the analysis.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

Al-Muʾminūn 23:20: bi-al-duhni

Rendering: “with oil.”

A full verse translation should clarify the relation between the tree and its oily produce, without substituting “olive” for duhn.

Al-Raḥmān 55:37: ka-al-dihāni

Rendering following the oil sense: “like oil.”

Rendering following the alternative: “like red leather.”

Both have lexical support. Oil is clearer and more general within D H N, while the attested red-leather sense and its relation to wardatan must also be considered.

A final translation requires close consideration of the verse’s comparison.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:81: mudhinūna

Proposed rendering: “treat with laxity and accommodation.”

This conveys the behavioral branch without replacing the word with particular labels such as hypocrite or disbeliever.

Al-Qalam 68:9: tudhinu fa-yudhinūna

Proposed rendering: “you would accommodate and compromise, so that they too would accommodate.”

This preserves the repeated verb. Contextual explanation should clarify its relation to deniers’ desire that the Prophet yield to them.

9. Conclusion

D H N has two important Arabic branches: physical oil and anointing, and behavioral accommodation, appeasement and compromise. Lexicons also record narrower uses such as scant rainfall, limited milk and expressions of weakness.

Softness from anointing and accommodating conduct have an intelligible semantic connection, without establishing a definite historical origin for every derivative.

Al-duhn in Al-Muʾminūn 23:20 names oil. Al-dihān compares the sky in Al-Raḥmān 55:37, with the important alternatives of oil and red leather. Mudhinūna in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:81 concerns a lax or accommodating response to the discourse. Tudhinu fa-yudhinūna in Al-Qalam 68:9 concerns reciprocal accommodation and yielding.

Short definition: Physical D H N denotes oil and anointing; behaviorally, accommodation that may, depending on context, become compromising conduct or concealment of one’s position. Brief root meaning: Oil and anointing; softness and accommodating conduct, with the sense determined by context.

Oil should not be imposed on behavioral uses, nor should mudāhanah be equated with every kind or gentle response. The criticism in the two behavioral Qur’anic settings follows from dealings with truth and deniers, rather than from softness being intrinsically objectionable.
