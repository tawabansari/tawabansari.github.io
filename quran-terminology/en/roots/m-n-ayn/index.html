The Qur’anic Root M N ʿ

Blocking access: from withholding to protection

The Qur’anic root م ن ع concerns a clear relationship: something intervenes between two sides, preventing one from reaching the other or an action from taking place. When access to something desired is blocked, the result is deprivation, withholding, or restraint. When access by a danger is blocked, the same relationship becomes protection and defence.

The Qur’an uses this family in the verb مَنَعَ, the intensive descriptions مَنَّاع and مَنُوع, the active participle مَانِعَة, and the passive participle مَمْنُوعَة. In some verses a person is prevented from acting; in others something is withheld from their reach, good is denied to others, or a fortress or force is expected to prevent danger from reaching someone. There are seventeen occurrences: twelve Form I verbs, two instances of mannāʿ, and one each of manūʿ, māniʿah, and mamnūʿah.

The semantic core may be expressed as follows:

M N ʿ: intervening between someone and something they desire, can access, or might encounter. This shared relationship gives rise to prevention, withholding, obstruction of access, and, in another direction, protection and defence.

Early Arabic: prevention can deprive or protect

Arabic lexicons explain manʿ as intervening between a person and something they want. Accordingly, one part of its range is described as the opposite of giving: something could be given to someone, but its delivery is blocked.

Within the same family, مَنِيع and مَنَعَة describe strength, security, and support that make an enemy’s approach difficult. A manīʿ fortress is not easily entered; a person or community possessing manaʿah has protective strength against attack.

The Muʿallaqah transmitted under the name of the pre-Islamic poet ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm, as preserved in al-Zawzanī’s commentary, includes:

يَقُتْنَ جِيَادَنَا وَيَقُلْنَ لَسْتُمْ
بُعُولَتَنَا إِذَا لَمْ تَمْنَعُونَا

“They feed our horses and say: You are not our husbands if you do not protect us.”

Here tamnaʿūnā means protecting the women from captivity at enemy hands, as al-Zawzanī explains; it does not mean depriving them of something.

Two Safaitic inscriptions provide material evidence for the protective sense. In KRS 1693, OCIANA reads w mnʿ s¹fry l- ġnṯ as an account of protecting two inscriptions belonging to Ġnṯ. The interpretation of the following words is disputed, but the protective reading of mnʿ is retained. In HNSD 123, the phrase w mnʿ h- ʾnfs¹ forms part of a request to protect monuments from someone who comes there. These belong to the pre-Islamic Safaitic corpus; the catalogue entries do not establish a precise year for either text. Safaitic evidence should also be distinguished from transmitted Classical Arabic poetry.

Protection is therefore not a sense invented by interpreting Qur’anic verses. Early evidence also uses the family for preventing harm from reaching its target. This establishes the antiquity of the usage, not an exact chronological sequence between its protective and withholding senses.

The evidence helps clarify the shared relationship: blocking access underlies these uses; deprivation and protection are different outcomes. Which outcome arises depends on what lies on the other side of the barrier. This is a semantic account, not proof that every use developed from a literal wall.

When an obstacle prevents an action

Several Qur’anic verses use manʿ of whether an action takes place.

Iblīs is asked:

قَالَ مَا مَنَعَكَ أَلَّا تَسْجُدَ إِذْ أَمَرْتُكَ ۖ قَالَ أَنَا خَيْرٌ مِنْهُ خَلَقْتَنِي مِنْ نَارٍ وَخَلَقْتَهُ مِنْ طِينٍ

“He said: What prevented you from prostrating when I commanded you? He said: I am better than him. You created me from fire and created him from clay.”

Al-Aʿraf 7:12

No physical barrier stands between Iblīs and prostration. His answer discloses his reason for refusing: his judgment of himself and Adam has obstructed obedience. In أَلَّا تَسْجُدَ, translating lā as a separate English negation after “prevented” would reverse the question. A familiar grammatical analysis treats it as an added particle for emphasis; other analyses consider the force of the whole construction. The context asks why he did not prostrate, not what prevented him from refusing.

An obstacle need not therefore be a wall, door, military force, or external object. A thought, decision, or situation can also prevent an action from occurring.

A comparable construction appears in Al-Isra:

وَمَا مَنَعَ النَّاسَ أَنْ يُؤْمِنُوا إِذْ جَاءَهُمُ الْهُدَىٰ إِلَّا أَنْ قَالُوا أَبَعَثَ اللَّهُ بَشَرًا رَسُولًا

“Nothing prevented people from believing when guidance came to them except that they said: Has The God sent a human being as a messenger?”

Al-Isra 17:94

Again, the obstacle is not an object: their understanding of the messenger’s humanity is what leads them not to believe.

These verses support a nonphysical preventing cause within the range of manʿ. Expressions such as “psychological wall,” “epistemological blockage,” or “inner fortress” remain explanatory metaphors; they should not become the root’s lexical definition.

In Al-Tawbah, disbelief is identified as preventing contributions from being accepted:

وَمَا مَنَعَهُمْ أَنْ تُقْبَلَ مِنْهُمْ نَفَقَاتُهُمْ إِلَّا أَنَّهُمْ كَفَرُوا بِاللَّهِ وَبِرَسُولِهِ وَلَا يَأْتُونَ الصَّلَاةَ إِلَّا وَهُمْ كُسَالَىٰ وَلَا يُنْفِقُونَ إِلَّا وَهُمْ كَارِهُونَ

“Nothing prevented their contributions from being accepted except that they disbelieved in The God and His messenger; they come to Salat only sluggishly and spend only unwillingly.”

Al-Tawbah 9:54

Here too, manʿ describes a prevented outcome: the contributions do not reach acceptance.

The remaining verb occurrences are Al-Isra 17:59, Al-Kahf 18:55, Ta-Ha 20:92, and Sad 38:75. These also belong in the complete count, although the detailed discussion here focuses on the examples above.

Access withheld: from measured provisions to fruit

Another branch concerns something a person wants to receive but cannot access.

Joseph’s brothers say:

فَلَمَّا رَجَعُوا إِلَىٰ أَبِيهِمْ قَالُوا يَا أَبَانَا مُنِعَ مِنَّا الْكَيْلُ فَأَرْسِلْ مَعَنَا أَخَانَا نَكْتَلْ وَإِنَّا لَهُ لَحَافِظُونَ

“When they returned to their father, they said: Our father, further measured provisions have been withheld from us. Send our brother with us so that we may obtain our measure, and we will surely guard him.”

Yusuf 12:63

مُنِعَ مِنَّا الْكَيْلُ concerns their future access to measured provisions. The previous verse says that they received their supplies; the condition concerns a further allocation unless they bring their brother. Al-kayl here means the measuring out of provisions or the measured allocation, not merely an empty measuring vessel.

Al-Maʿun also says:

وَيَمْنَعُونَ الْمَاعُونَ

“And they withhold al-māʿūn.”

Al-Maʿun 107:7

The precise range of al-māʿūn is a separate lexical question. The verb here is clear: something that could reach another person is withheld.

The passive participle expresses a related relationship in the description of the Garden’s fruit:

وَفَاكِهَةٍ كَثِيرَةٍ ۝ لَا مَقْطُوعَةٍ وَلَا مَمْنُوعَةٍ

“And abundant fruit, neither exhausted nor withheld.”

Al-Waqiʿah 56:32–33

Being مَمْنُوعَة is not the same as being مَقْطُوعَة. Fruit might exist while a barrier prevents someone from reaching it. Negating mamnūʿah means that access is not barred.

The word alone cannot establish a theory of ownership, resource scarcity, garden guards, or the Garden’s economic arrangements. These are possible human circumstances of prevention; the verse states that the fruit is not withheld.

Nor does mamnūʿ itself mean ḥarām. Something may be kept out of reach without being the subject of a religious ruling that forbids it.

مَنَّاعٍ لِلْخَيْرِ and مَنُوعًا: the person who withholds

Qaf says:

مَنَّاعٍ لِلْخَيْرِ مُعْتَدٍ مُرِيبٍ

“Persistently withholding good, transgressing, and steeped in doubt.”

Qaf 50:25

And Al-Qalam:

مَنَّاعٍ لِلْخَيْرِ مُعْتَدٍ أَثِيمٍ

“Persistently withholding good, transgressing, and sinful.”

Al-Qalam 68:12

Mannāʿ, on the pattern faʿʿāl, presents withholding good as a marked characteristic. The pattern can express intensity or repetition, but it does not specify a numerical frequency or degree. In Qaf 50:25, murīb is rendered here in the sense of being in doubt, a reading reported by al-Ṭabarī; it need not be restricted to causing other people to doubt.

Al-Maʿarij also says:

إِنَّ الْإِنْسَانَ خُلِقَ هَلُوعًا ۝ إِذَا مَسَّهُ الشَّرُّ جَزُوعًا ۝ وَإِذَا مَسَّهُ الْخَيْرُ مَنُوعًا

“The human being was created fretful: when harm touches him, deeply distressed; and when good touches him, intensely withholding.”

Al-Maʿarij 70:19–21

Here good has reached the person, but he holds it back. In this context manūʿ, on the intensive pattern faʿūl, approaches the sense of being grudging or withholding.

One may picture the person preventing good from passing to someone else, but this should not become a theory of a “flow of grace.” The nature of the good must be determined from context; manʿ describes withholding it.

Nor must مَنَّاعٍ لِلْخَيْرِ always mean only “financially miserly.” The expression says “good,” and the root manʿ does not specify its kind. A particular setting may foreground wealth and financial obligations, but the definition should not restrict good to wealth in advance.

The same barrier, now protective

When the barrier stands between a person and danger, the same root expresses protection.

Al-Anbiya says:

أَمْ لَهُمْ آلِهَةٌ تَمْنَعُهُمْ مِنْ دُونِنَا ۚ لَا يَسْتَطِيعُونَ نَصْرَ أَنْفُسِهِمْ وَلَا هُمْ مِنَّا يُصْحَبُونَ

“Or do they have deities who protect them against Us? They cannot even help themselves, nor are they given protection from Us.”

Al-Anbiya 21:43

تَمْنَعُهُمْ here does not mean depriving them. The supposed deities would have to serve as a barrier keeping danger away from their followers.

Al-Nisa also reports the hypocrites’ claim:

الَّذِينَ يَتَرَبَّصُونَ بِكُمْ فَإِنْ كَانَ لَكُمْ فَتْحٌ مِنَ اللَّهِ قَالُوا أَلَمْ نَكُنْ مَعَكُمْ وَإِنْ كَانَ لِلْكَافِرِينَ نَصِيبٌ قَالُوا أَلَمْ نَسْتَحْوِذْ عَلَيْكُمْ وَنَمْنَعْكُمْ مِنَ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ ۚ فَاللَّهُ يَحْكُمُ بَيْنَكُمْ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ ۗ وَلَنْ يَجْعَلَ اللَّهُ لِلْكَافِرِينَ عَلَى الْمُؤْمِنِينَ سَبِيلًا

“Those who wait to see what befalls you: if victory comes to you from The God, they say, ‘Were we not with you?’ And if the disbelievers gain a share, they say, ‘Did we not gain the upper hand over you and protect you from the believers?’ The God will judge between you on the Day of Resurrection, and The God will never grant the disbelievers a way over the believers.”

Al-Nisa 4:141

In Al-Hashr 59:2, some suppose that their fortresses will protect them: مَانِعَتُهُمْ حُصُونُهُمْ. The fortresses are imagined as barriers between them and danger.

These uses share the protective relationship also attested in the Safaitic evidence: a barrier prevents an opposing force from reaching its target.

Manʿ is therefore not intrinsically positive or negative. Depriving a person in need and protecting someone against danger can both be expressed through this root; the moral value comes from context.

Al-Baqarah 2:114: prevention alongside ruin

The root appears in the same verse discussed in the study of خ ر ب:

وَمَنْ أَظْلَمُ مِمَّنْ مَنَعَ مَسَاجِدَ اللَّهِ أَنْ يُذْكَرَ فِيهَا اسْمُهُ وَسَعَىٰ فِي خَرَابِهَا ۚ أُولَٰئِكَ مَا كَانَ لَهُمْ أَنْ يَدْخُلُوهَا إِلَّا خَائِفِينَ ۚ لَهُمْ فِي الدُّنْيَا خِزْيٌ وَلَهُمْ فِي الْآخِرَةِ عَذَابٌ عَظِيمٌ

“Who is more unjust than one who prevents the name of The God from being remembered in His mosques and strives for their ruin? Such people should not enter them except in fear. Disgrace awaits them in this world, and a tremendous punishment in the Hereafter.”

Al-Baqarah 2:114

مَنَعَ مَسَاجِدَ اللَّهِ أَنْ يُذْكَرَ فِيهَا اسْمُهُ concerns preventing remembrance of the name of The God in mosques. The root does not specify whether this is achieved by locking doors, intimidation, expulsion, or another means.

The two roots approach the situation differently: manʿ concerns preventing access or an action; kharāb concerns the loss of a place’s maintained and inhabited condition. The conjunction itself does not establish that preventing worship and ruining a mosque are identical.

Preventing a mosque’s religious use can thus be connected with its ruin, but the two words are not synonyms.

M N ʿ and ʿ Ṭ W: withholding and giving

Classical lexicons also define manʿ as the opposite of giving. The contrast is especially clear where something might pass from one person to another: giving enables its receipt, while withholding prevents it.

Yet manʿ cannot always be defined solely as the opposite of giving. Its protective branch makes this too narrow. A fortress defending its inhabitants does not withhold something from them; it prevents an enemy from reaching them.

The Qur’an does not make ʿaṭāʾ / manʿ an obligatory lexical pair. In Sad 38:39, concerning the gift to Solomon, it says:

هَٰذَا عَطَاؤُنَا فَامْنُنْ أَوْ أَمْسِكْ بِغَيْرِ حِسَابٍ

“This is Our gift; so bestow or retain, without account.”

Sad 38:39

Here imsāk, retaining, stands opposite bestowing; the word manʿ is not used. فَامْنُنْ belongs to م ن ن, not م ن ع; the similarity of spelling should not obscure the distinction.

Al-Layl similarly places أَعْطَىٰ, “gave,” on one side of its description and بَخِلَ, “withheld miserly,” on the other (92:5, 92:8). The lexical contrast between manʿ and giving is valid and useful for the withholding branch, but the Qur’an employs several words for not giving, retaining, and miserliness.

It is therefore too restrictive to define manʿ invariably as “blocking the flow of giving.” That image fits some uses, but not the whole root.

M N ʿ compared with N H Y and Ḥ R M

Prevention, prohibition, and declaring something forbidden may lead to similar practical outcomes, but their perspectives differ.

Nahy commonly concerns an instruction: someone is told not to act. They may be prohibited while remaining physically able to do so.

Manʿ foregrounds obstruction of an action or access. The obstacle can be a person, fortress, decision, situation, or other cause; it need not take the form of a verbal command.

Taḥrīm has its own lexical range, involving what is made forbidden. But مَمْنُوعَة in Al-Waqiʿah 56:33 concerns access to fruit. The form itself does not supply a legal ruling.

Something forbidden may also be kept out of reach in practice, but manʿ and taḥrīm are not lexically identical.

Translation proposals for Forqan

For مَا مَنَعَكَ and related constructions, “What prevented you?” is natural and precise. The complement must then be translated according to the whole sentence, particularly in Al-Aʿraf 7:12.

For مُنِعَ مِنَّا الْكَيْلُ, “further measured provisions have been withheld from us” or “we have been denied another allocation” conveys the context more clearly than wording suggesting that a measuring vessel was taken away.

For وَيَمْنَعُونَ الْمَاعُونَ, “they withhold al-māʿūn” preserves the verb while leaving the separate lexical question open.

For مَنَّاعٍ لِلْخَيْرِ, “persistently withholding good” or “one who obstructs good” can be chosen according to context. Good should not be restricted without evidence to wealth, guidance, or another particular instance.

مَنُوعًا in Al-Maʿarij can be “intensely withholding” or “grudging.”

For لَا مَقْطُوعَةٍ وَلَا مَمْنُوعَةٍ, “neither exhausted nor withheld” makes the distinction accessible in English. “Forbidden” risks introducing a legal or religious prohibition that the context does not require.

For تَمْنَعُهُمْ مِنْ دُونِنَا, “protect them against Us,” and for مَانِعَتُهُمْ حُصُونُهُمْ, “their fortresses would protect them,” are natural. The root study clarifies the shared relationship of blocking access behind these renderings.

Conclusion

The root م ن ع centres on intervention that prevents something from reaching its target.

Early evidence attests both withholding and protection. Lexicographers contrast manʿ with giving, while recording manīʿ places and manaʿah as expressions of strength and defence. The Safaitic records KRS 1693 and HNSD 123 also attest protection of inscriptions and monuments; their exact dates are not established by the catalogue entries. The transmitted poem of ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm supplies a further witness to protection.

In the Qur’an, an obstacle sometimes stands between someone and an action: Iblīs is prevented from prostrating, people from believing, and contributions from being accepted. The cause need not be physical; a judgment, decision, or situation can stop an action from taking place.

Elsewhere, access to something is blocked: further measured provisions are withheld from Joseph’s brothers, al-māʿūn is withheld from others, and mamnūʿah describes something kept out of reach.

In مَنَّاعٍ لِلْخَيْرِ and مَنُوعًا, the person is the agent of withholding, preventing good from reaching where it otherwise might. This does not establish a theory of a “flow of grace”; the word describes prevention or withholding.

In the other direction, the same root expresses protection. Supposed deities are expected to shield their followers, hypocrites claim to have provided protection, and fortresses are imagined as defensive barriers. The shared relationship of intervention explains the apparent contrast: blocking access to good deprives; blocking the approach of danger protects.

The lexical contrast with giving helps explain withholding but does not define the whole root. The Qur’an also contrasts giving with retaining or miserliness. Manʿ is thus one expression of obstructed transfer and access, not the only word available for not giving.

M N ʿ: intervening between someone and something they desire, can access, or might encounter. Where the barrier separates a person from something desired, the result is prevention, deprivation, or withholding; where it separates them from danger, the result is protection and defence. The obstacle may be physical or nonphysical, but the root alone does not specify its nature, cause, or moral value.
