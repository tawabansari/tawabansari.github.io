Linguistic Note

The core of ج ر ي (J R Y) is smooth and continuous movement in a channel or course. This movement is usually unbroken, directed, and bound to a path. Water flows in a river; a ship runs upon the sea; the sun and moon move in their courses; time and appointed term also proceed within the order of The God.

So J R Y is not merely “going.” Going may be any kind of motion, but jary carries the feel of flow, extension, continuity, and movement in a channel. In this root, motion is joined to order, path, continuity, and sometimes destination.

For this reason, J R Y is one of the Qur’anic roots that helps reveal dynamic order. The Qur’anic world is not static, yet its movement is not chaotic. River, ship, sun, moon, and time each run in their own course.

Central Meaning

The central meaning of J R Y is flowing, running, continuous movement, moving along a path, and something proceeding in its own channel, whether water, ship, sun and moon, divine command, time, or appointed term.

Material Image

The material image is water flowing in a river. It is not standing still, dry, or interrupted. It comes from a source, passes through a channel, and moves toward a destination or wider expanse. This image then extends to ship, wind, sun, moon, stars, time, divine command, and the life of the next world.

In the Qur’an, تَجْرِي مِن تَحْتِهَا الْأَنْهَارُ (tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhār) is among the most beautiful images of this root: gardens beneath which rivers flow. Blessing is not dry or static; it is flowing, living, and continuous.

Classical Language Note

In classical Arabic, J R Y concerns the flowing of water, continuous running, passing along a path, and the ongoing course of a matter. Jāriyah can refer to a ship because it runs upon water. Majrā is the place or course of flow. These uses show that the root considers both the channel and the movement within it.

Jary is also used for smooth, continuous movement of a horse, different from broken or interrupted movement. For understanding the Qur’an, the key point is this: jary is not merely mechanical motion; it is ordered movement along an appointed path.

Rivers That Flow

One of the most frequent Qur’anic uses of this root concerns rivers flowing in gardens. In 2:25, the Qur’an says: جَنَّاتٍ تَجْرِي مِن تَحْتِهَا الْأَنْهَارُ (jannātin tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhār).

Tajrī is not only information about the presence of water. It points to life, freshness, movement, and continuity of blessing. A garden with rivers flowing through it is living and sustained. In 9:72, the flowing of rivers appears with abiding and the peace of the next life.

Water Flow and the Channel of Life

The Qur’an treats the movement of water through the earth as a sign of divine governance, even in verses where the root J R Y itself does not appear. In 39:21, water is sent down from the sky, made to travel as springs in the earth, and then brings forth crops.

In 18:33, the Qur’an says: وَفَجَّرْنَا خِلَالَهُمَا نَهَرًا (wa-fajjarnā khilālahumā naharā). The verb is fajjarnā, not J R Y, but the river is the channel of flow. Nahr is the bed or watercourse; jary is the flowing motion within it.

Ships That Run

One major field of J R Y is the movement of ships at sea. In 10:22, the Qur’an says: وَجَرَيْنَ بِهِم بِرِيحٍ طَيِّبَةٍ (wa-jarayna bihim bi-rīḥin ṭayyibah). The ships carried them smoothly with a good wind.

In 45:12, it says: لِتَجْرِيَ الْفُلْكُ فِيهِ بِأَمْرِهِ (li-tajriya al-fulku fīhi bi-amrih). Ships run in the sea by His command. Jary in the Qur’an is not meaningless natural movement; it is movement within the order of divine command and governance.

Jāriyah: The Running Vessel

The word الْجَارِيَة (al-jāriyah) appears in the Qur’an for a ship running upon water. In 69:11, the Qur’an says: حَمَلْنَاكُمْ فِي الْجَارِيَةِ (ḥamalnākum fī al-jāriyah).

Al-jāriyah here refers to the ship of Nūḥ: that which runs upon water. In some later or historical usage, jāriyah may call other meanings to mind, but in this verse its meaning is the running vessel on water.

Majrā and Mursā: Movement and Rest

In the story of Nūḥ, the Qur’an says: بِسْمِ اللَّهِ مَجْرَاهَا وَمُرْسَاهَا (bismi Allāhi majrāhā wa-mursāhā) in 11:41. Majrā is from J R Y and points to running, course, or movement. Mursā, from resting and anchoring, stands opposite it.

The verse places two states of the ship side by side: running and coming to rest. Both are in the name of The God. The ship in the flood is not abandoned or ownerless; its movement and rest are understood in relation to the name of The God and His lordship.

The Sun: Running Toward a Resting Place

The Qur’an also uses J R Y for the sun. In 36:38, it says: وَالشَّمْسُ تَجْرِي لِمُسْتَقَرٍّ لَّهَا (wa-al-shamsu tajrī li-mustaqarrin lahā).

The sun moves in its appointed course. This movement is not disorderly; it is تَقْدِيرُ الْعَزِيزِ الْعَلِيمِ (taqdīru al-ʿazīzi al-ʿalīm). The phrase tajrī li-mustaqarrin lahā is subtle: movement is joined to settled limit. The sun runs, but not aimlessly; it is related to an appointed station, limit, or order.

The Sun and Moon: Running Until a Named Term

In 39:5, the Qur’an says: وَسَخَّرَ الشَّمْسَ وَالْقَمَرَ كُلٌّ يَجْرِي لِأَجَلٍ مُّسَمًّى (wa-sakhkhara al-shamsa wa-al-qamara kullun yajri li-ajalin musamman). In 31:29, it says: كُلٌّ يَجْرِي إِلَىٰ أَجَلٍ مُّسَمًّى (kullun yajri ilā ajalin musamman).

This expression shows that jary is tied to appointed term and limit. Cosmic movement is not open-ended and chaotic; it runs until a named term within an ordered decree. Here jary is not only spatial movement, but movement through time and decree.

All Swimming in an Orbit

In 21:33, the Qur’an says: كُلٌّ فِي فَلَكٍ يَسْبَحُونَ (kullun fī falakin yasbaḥūn). The root J R Y does not appear here, but the semantic field is close. Yasbaḥūn pictures floating or swimming in an orbit, while the jary verses about the sun and moon present the same ordered motion from another angle.

The two images complete one another. The sun and moon are swimming in an orbit from one angle, and running along an appointed course and time from another.

Jawār: Ships Running on the Sea

In the Qur’an, الْجَوَار (al-jawār) is used for running ships. In 42:32, it says: وَمِنْ آيَاتِهِ الْجَوَارِ فِي الْبَحْرِ كَالْأَعْلَامِ (wa-min āyātihi al-jawāri fī al-baḥri ka-al-aʿlām).

In 55:24, it says: وَلَهُ الْجَوَارِ الْمُنشَآتُ فِي الْبَحْرِ كَالْأَعْلَامِ (wa-lahu al-jawāri al-munshaʾātu fī al-baḥri ka-al-aʿlām). Al-jawār is the plural of jāriyah: ships running on the sea. The Qur’an treats their movement as a sign of lordship and blessing.

Jāriyāt: Smooth and Easy Movement

In 51:3, the Qur’an says: فَالْجَارِيَاتِ يُسْرًا (fa-al-jāriyāti yusrā). The precise referent is discussed in tafsīr, but the root sense is clear: things that run smoothly, easily, and continuously.

The verse emphasizes the quality of motion: yusrā, an easy and unhindered flowing.

The Flow of Blessing Against Dryness and Stillness

In Qur’anic imagery, jary often signals life, mercy, and continuity. A flowing river keeps the garden alive. A running ship carries the human being across the sea. The running sun and moon maintain the order of night and day.

In this sense, jary stands against dryness, stagnation, interruption, and disorder. What flows is part of a living and active order.

Distinguishing Qur’anic Uses

In 2:25 and 9:72, J R Y concerns rivers flowing in the gardens of the next life. In 10:22 and 45:12, it concerns ships at sea. In 69:11, al-jāriyah is the vessel running on water. In 11:41, majrāhā stands opposite mursāhā.

In 36:38, the sun runs in its appointed course. In 39:5 and 31:29, the sun and moon run until a named term. In 42:32 and 55:24, al-jawār are ships running on the sea. In 51:3, al-jāriyāt points to things running easily.

Grammatical and Structural Notes

In تَجْرِي مِن تَحْتِهَا الْأَنْهَارُ (tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhār), the verb tajrī refers to the rivers, and min taḥtihā marks the direction and location of the flow. In جَرَيْنَ بِهِم بِرِيحٍ طَيِّبَةٍ (jarayna bihim bi-rīḥin ṭayyibah), the feminine plural verb refers to ships; bihim shows that the ships carry the passengers, and bi-rīḥin ṭayyibah gives the means and condition of motion.

In لِتَجْرِيَ الْفُلْكُ فِيهِ بِأَمْرِهِ (li-tajriya al-fulku fīhi bi-amrih), fīhi marks the channel of motion, the sea. Bi-amrih shows that the running occurs within divine command. In بِسْمِ اللَّهِ مَجْرَاهَا وَمُرْسَاهَا (bismi Allāhi majrāhā wa-mursāhā), majrā and mursā are two opposite states: running and coming to rest.

In وَالشَّمْسُ تَجْرِي لِمُسْتَقَرٍّ لَّهَا (wa-al-shamsu tajrī li-mustaqarrin lahā), the lām marks direction or end. In كُلٌّ يَجْرِي لِأَجَلٍ مُّسَمًّى (kullun yajri li-ajalin musamman) and كُلٌّ يَجْرِي إِلَىٰ أَجَلٍ مُّسَمًّى (kullun yajri ilā ajalin musamman), jary is joined to appointed time.

Difference From Nearby Words

J R Y is close to S Y R, but they are not identical. Sayr points to going, moving, and traveling a path. Jary is smooth, continuous flow in a channel or course. Sayr may describe humans traveling in the earth; jary carries more of the feel of flow, extension, and running.

J R Y also differs from M SH Y, S B Ḥ, M R R, N H R, and F Y Ḍ. Mashy is walking, especially step-by-step movement of a living being. Sabḥ gives the image of floating or swimming in an expanse or orbit. Murūr is passing by or crossing. Nahr is the river or watercourse, while jary is the act of flowing. Fayḍ is close to overflowing and abundance, while jary points to ordered flowing in a course.

J R Y is completely separate from A J R. Ajr concerns reward and wage, not flowing. In phrases such as إِنْ أَجْرِيَ إِلَّا عَلَى اللَّهِ (in ajriya illā ʿalā Allāh), ajriya comes from A J R, not J R Y. Translation and root study must not mix them.

Forqan Translation Method

Forqan should translate J R Y according to the channel. With water, “flow” or “run” fits. With ships, “run,” “sail,” or “move upon the water” may be more precise. With the sun and moon, “run in an appointed course” or “move until a named term” preserves the Qur’anic sense. With majrā, the link between movement and course should remain visible.

J R Y must also be kept fully distinct from A J R. Ajrī meaning “my reward” belongs to A J R, not J R Y.

Forqan Translation Suggestions

  • tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhār: rivers flow beneath it; or beneath them rivers run.
  • jarayna bihim bi-rīḥin ṭayyibah: they carried them smoothly with a good wind.
  • li-tajriya al-fulku fīhi bi-amrih: so that the ships may run in it by His command.
  • al-jāriyah: the running vessel; or the vessel running upon water.
  • majrāhā wa-mursāhā: its running and its coming to rest; or its running course and its anchoring place.
  • wa-al-shamsu tajrī li-mustaqarrin lahā: and the sun runs toward its resting place; or the sun runs in its appointed course.
  • kullun yajri li-ajalin musamman: each runs until a named term; or each remains in flow until an appointed end.
  • al-jawāri fī al-baḥr: the ships running on the sea.
  • fa-al-jāriyāti yusrā: by those that run easily; or by those that flow smoothly and easily.

Summary

The Qur’anic root J R Y points to flowing, running, and continuous movement in a course or channel. It begins from the image of flowing water, then extends in the Qur’an to ships, rivers, sun, moon, appointed term, path, and divine order.

Jary is not aimless or scattered movement. The river flows in its bed, the ship runs upon the sea, the sun and moon move until a named term, and every flow belongs within a system of decree and divine command.

In short: J R Y in the Qur’an points to smooth and continuous movement in a path; it begins with river and ship, then expands to cosmic order, appointed term, and the flow of blessing and divine governance.
