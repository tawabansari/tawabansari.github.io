Qur’anic root ش ط ر

Half, part, side and direction: from dividing and moving away to facing towards the Sacred Mosque

The three-letter Arabic root ش ط ر has two established semantic fields: half or part of something, and side, direction and distance. When something is divided into two halves, each half is a شَطْر. In another use, the same word denotes the direction towards which someone goes or from which something comes. Only the second use appears in the Qur’an: the command to face towards the Sacred Mosque.

شَطْر occurs five times in Al-Baqarah 2:144, 2:149 and 2:150. The destination is the Sacred Mosque in every instance, and the command is expressed through وَلِّ وَجْهَكَ or وَلُّوا وُجُوهَكُمْ. Understanding شطر therefore matters for orientation in these verses. Its lexical meaning, however, should not be equated with the entire concept of the qiblah, face, Sacred Mosque or wisdom of this command. Each word and subject makes its own contribution to the verse’s construction.

The only Qur’anic forms are شَطْرَ and شَطْرَهُ, occurring three and two times respectively. شَطْرَهُ is not an independent derivative: it is شطر with the pronoun هُ attached. Arabic has verbs such as شَطَرَ, شَطَّرَ and شَاطَرَ and nouns such as شَطِير, شَطُور, شَاطِر and مَشْطُور, but these do not occur in the Qur’an in these forms.

Qur’anic forms

شَطْرَ
(shaṭra)
Directional adverb in a construct phrase: three occurrences

شَطْرَهُ

1. The semantic core and usage in early Arabic

Shaṭr meaning a half or part

Arabic شَطْرُ الشَّيْءِ means half of something. شَطَرَ الشَّيْءَ means dividing it into two halves, while شَاطَرَ فُلَانًا مَالَهُ means dividing property into halves with someone else.

For example, someone who gives another half their property and keeps the other half can be described as having shared it through مُشَاطَرَة. Here شطر emphasises dividing something into two portions.

Lexical accounts also explain this meaning through Arab livestock keeping. A she-camel has four teats; some methods of milking involved taking milk from two and leaving the other two. Each pair of the four was considered a شطر.

Arabic also records شَطُور for a sheep with one of its two teats dry, or with teats of unequal size. This use concerns the body’s division into two sides.

The established expression حَلَبَ الدَّهْرَ أَشْطُرَهُ also draws on livestock keeping. It describes someone who has tested life and experienced its hardship and ease, as though they had milked every part of the udder and gained benefit or experience from each.

أَشْطُر here concerns different portions, and the resulting expression denotes extensive experience. This figurative use nevertheless does not make شطر mean experiencing in every sentence.

Poetry and prosody also use شطر for part of a verse. Each of its two halves can be called its شطر; some prosodic terminology uses مَشْطُور for a metre from which part of the usual structure is omitted. This use remains connected to a part or half. Prosodic terminology developed after the period of revelation is not itself dated evidence for the word’s meaning before the Qur’an.

Shaṭr meaning side or direction

The root’s other field concerns direction and side. Arabic شَطْرُ الشَّيْءِ can denote the direction of something, and قَصَدْتُ شَطْرَهُ means turning or going towards it.

A traveller going towards a city from far away can be said to take their course شَطْرَ that city. This means that the city is the destination and شطر indicates its direction, rather than that the traveller is going to half of the city.

Lexical sources explain this use through نَحْو, جِهَة and تِلْقَاء. In sentences about movement and turning towards something, these can be close in meaning: towards something, in its direction or facing it.

شطر meaning direction does not necessarily imply proximity to the destination. Someone can face towards a city from a great distance without having reached it.

Direction therefore needs to be distinguished from arrival. شطر can indicate the direction of movement or facing without itself meaning reaching, entering or being present at the place.

Did the directional meaning arise from half?

One can imagine a relation between the two fields. When something is divided into halves, each half lies on one side; شطر might have extended from a part of something to its direction.

This is only a possible explanation. Authoritative lexical sources also recognise two separate fields under ش ط ر: dividing into halves, and distance and facing a direction. This classification in dictionaries compiled after revelation does not itself prove an etymological separation or connection between the branches.

The distinction shows that neither meaning should be made the definite origin of the other without historical evidence. The existence of “half” does not itself prove that Arabs first imagined a city divided into two whenever they spoke of its شطر.

Nor should “direction” replace the clear meaning of half in sentences about dividing property or objects.

The Qur’anic sentence construction identifies the appropriate meaning. فَوَلِّ وَجْهَكَ شَطْرَ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ concerns facing towards a known place, rather than dividing the mosque into halves.

2. Shaṭr in pre-Islamic poetry and Arab life

Evidence for direction and distance

A poem attributed to Laqīṭ ibn Yaʿmur al-Iyādī, a pre-Islamic Arab poet, says:

«وَقَدْ أَظَلَّكُمْ مِنْ شَطْرِ ثَغْرِكُمْ
هَوْلٌ لَهُ ظُلَمٌ تَغْشَاكُمْ قِطَعًا»

“From the direction of your frontier, a great danger has overshadowed you, whose darkness covers you in successive masses.”

The poet warns his people of a danger approaching from outside. This section of the poem also describes the enemy’s preparations and the unawareness of people who are still living at ease.

مِنْ شَطْرِ ثَغْرِكُمْ means from the direction of your frontier or border region. شطر identifies the direction from which the danger comes, rather than half of the frontier.

The preposition مِنْ also matters. The danger comes from that direction. شطر therefore was used both for movement towards a destination and for a region from whose side something approaches.

This witnesses the directional use of شطر in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period. It need not be treated as a meaning created solely to explain the qiblah verses.

Turning towards a destination

Another poetic witness, transmitted under Sāʿidah ibn Juʾayyah al-Hudhalī’s name, says:

«أَقُولُ لِأُمِّ زِنْبَاعٍ أَقِيمِي
صُدُورَ الْعِيسِ شَطْرَ بَنِي تَمِيمِ»

“I say to Umm Zinbāʿ: set the camels’ breasts straight towards Banū Tamīm.”

الْعِيس denotes camels, and شَطْرَ بَنِي تَمِيمِ indicates their direction of movement. The speaker wants them to travel towards Banū Tamīm’s territory or dwelling place.

This is clear evidence for direction, but its attribution differs between sources. Al-Shāfiʿī’s Al-Risālah gives Sāʿidah ibn Juʾayyah, whereas Lisān al-ʿArab attributes it to Abū Zinbāʿ al-Judhāmī. Its exact date of composition is also insufficiently secure. It is therefore better treated as evidence preserved in early works, rather than as a definite pre-Islamic witness.

In both verses, شطر concerns the direction of movement or of something approaching. Translating these uses only as half would lose the sentence’s meaning.

شَطِير: someone who has become distant

Arabic also calls someone far away or a stranger شَطِير. مَنْزِلٌ شَطِيرٌ can denote a distant dwelling, and نَوًى شُطُورٌ distance and separation.

A poem attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays also uses the plural الشُّطُر for people distant from or dispersed away from their community.

Distance is prominent in this use. Someone separated from their group or home is called شطير because they are distant or estranged, rather than because half of them has been detached.

Lexical accounts also use شَاطِر for someone who has moved away from their family or community and adopted opposition or rebellious conduct. Later Arabic uses for cleverness and skill should not become the root’s primary Qur’anic meaning. Some lexicographers also consider this rebellious use of شاطر a later formation; its inclusion in a dictionary therefore does not alone establish that it predates the Qur’an.

These uses place distance, being on another side and having a direction within the root’s semantic field, while good or bad moral value is not a fixed feature of them all.

3. All Qur’anic occurrences of shaṭr

فَوَلِّ وَجْهَكَ شَطْرَ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ

The first two occurrences of this root appear in Al-Baqarah 2:144:

«قَدْ نَرَىٰ تَقَلُّبَ وَجْهِكَ فِي السَّمَاءِ فَلَنُوَلِّيَنَّكَ قِبْلَةً تَرْضَاهَا فَوَلِّ وَجْهَكَ شَطْرَ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ وَحَيْثُ مَا كُنْتُمْ فَوَلُّوا وُجُوهَكُمْ شَطْرَهُ وَإِنَّ الَّذِينَ أُوتُوا الْكِتَابَ لَيَعْلَمُونَ أَنَّهُ الْحَقُّ مِنْ رَبِّهِمْ وَمَا اللَّهُ بِغَافِلٍ عَمَّا يَعْمَلُونَ»

“We indeed see the turning of your face in the sky. We will surely turn you towards a qiblah that pleases you. Therefore, turn your face towards the Sacred Mosque; and wherever you are, turn your faces towards it. Those who were given the Book certainly know that it is the truth from their Lord. The God is not unaware of what they do.”

(Al-Baqarah 2:144)

شَطْرَ first occurs in شَطْرَ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ and then as شَطْرَهُ. Both concern turning towards a particular destination.

The first sentence addresses the Prophet through فَوَلِّ وَجْهَكَ. The command then extends through the plural فَوَلُّوا وُجُوهَكُمْ.

This difference shows that the command concerns more than one person. Wherever the other people addressed may be, they too must turn their faces towards the same destination.

The pronoun هُ in شَطْرَهُ refers back to the Sacred Mosque. It introduces no new destination, but expresses the previous one without repeating its name.

Shaṭr alongside وَلِّ وَجْهَكَ

Each word’s contribution must be distinguished to understand the sentence.

وَلِّ comes from و ل ي and expresses the command to turn in this construction. وَجْهَكَ identifies what is to face the destination. شَطْرَ indicates the direction of this turning, and الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ identifies the destination.

The sentence’s meaning therefore arises from these elements together. شطر itself means neither turning the face nor the Sacred Mosque.

Nor does شطر alone mean worship, faith, following or surrender in this verse. These may belong to the broader qiblah context, but should not be presented as its lexical meaning.

شطر المسجد الحرام expresses an actual direction in Arabic. The Sacred Mosque is a known place and the command concerns facing towards it. The word nevertheless does not say that all the qiblah’s meaning and wisdom are confined to bodily movement.

The command’s direct meaning must therefore be distinguished from the concepts the Qur’an associates with it, without denying either level because of the other.

وَمِنْ حَيْثُ خَرَجْتَ

Al-Baqarah 2:149 says:

«وَمِنْ حَيْثُ خَرَجْتَ فَوَلِّ وَجْهَكَ شَطْرَ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ وَإِنَّهُ لَلْحَقُّ مِنْ رَبِّكَ وَمَا اللَّهُ بِغَافِلٍ عَمَّا تَعْمَلُونَ»

“Wherever you depart from, turn your face towards the Sacred Mosque. It is indeed the truth from your Lord. The God is not unaware of what you do.”

(Al-Baqarah 2:149)

The same فَوَلِّ وَجْهَكَ شَطْرَ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ recurs here, preceded by وَمِنْ حَيْثُ خَرَجْتَ.

This emphasises that the command does not depend on the particular place someone occupies. They may have departed from one place and be in another; the commanded direction remains the same.

Lexically, شطر again means direction. A change in the starting place or the location of those addressed does not change that meaning.

This also clarifies that شطر does not mean reaching the Sacred Mosque. Someone can remain elsewhere and still turn their face towards it.

وَإِنَّهُ لَلْحَقُّ مِنْ رَبِّكَ emphasises the command’s truth and its subject in context, rather than making شطر lexically mean truth.

The command repeated in Al-Baqarah 2:150

The next verse says:

«وَمِنْ حَيْثُ خَرَجْتَ فَوَلِّ وَجْهَكَ شَطْرَ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ وَحَيْثُ مَا كُنْتُمْ فَوَلُّوا وُجُوهَكُمْ شَطْرَهُ لِئَلَّا يَكُونَ لِلنَّاسِ عَلَيْكُمْ حُجَّةٌ إِلَّا الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا مِنْهُمْ فَلَا تَخْشَوْهُمْ وَاخْشَوْنِي وَلِأُتِمَّ نِعْمَتِي عَلَيْكُمْ وَلَعَلَّكُمْ تَهْتَدُونَ»

“Wherever you depart from, turn your face towards the Sacred Mosque; wherever you are, turn your faces towards it, so that people may have no argument against you, except those among them who do wrong. Do not fear them, but fear Me, and so that I may complete My favour upon you and that you may be guided.”

(Al-Baqarah 2:150)

شَطْرَ and شَطْرَهُ recur here. First the Prophet and then the group addressed are commanded to face towards the same place.

The continuation discusses people’s argument, not fearing opponents, fearing The God, the completion of favour and guidance.

These subjects place the qiblah command within a broader set of religious and social relations. Changing or establishing direction is also connected to others’ opposition and the place of the divine command.

This connection should not remove the directional meaning of شطر. فَلَا تَخْشَوْهُمْ وَاخْشَوْنِي is a separate command about fear and reverence; وَلَعَلَّكُمْ تَهْتَدُونَ concerns guidance; and شَطْرَهُ indicates the direction of facing.

The verse’s full meaning arises from these expressions together, rather than from putting them all inside one root.

4. Direction, destination and turning in Qur’anic construction

Does shaṭr mean half of the mosque?

Half is a real and established Arabic meaning of شطر, but does not apply to شَطْرَ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ in the Qur’an.

Translating it as half here would mean that the Prophet must turn his face towards half of the mosque. The verse neither divides the mosque nor identifies which half is intended.

Direction, by contrast, accords with the sentence construction. وَلِّ وَجْهَكَ concerns facing, and the Sacred Mosque is the destination towards which that facing takes place.

Early Arabic evidence also shows an independent, established directional use of شطر. Preferring direction here therefore does not require a new hypothetical explanation derived from half.

The syntax of shaṭr

All five Qur’anic occurrences are accusative. In the usual syntactic analysis, شطر in فَوَلِّ وَجْهَكَ شَطْرَ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ is an adverbial of place, indicating the direction towards which the action of turning applies.

Another analysis treats شطر as a second object of ولّى. On this reading, وجه and شطر complete the verb’s relation: what is turned and the direction towards which it is placed.

These analyses differ over grammatical function without changing the word’s principal meaning. Both identify the direction or destination of orientation.

The accusative ending itself also establishes neither distance, proximity, geometric precision nor a restricted angle. These properties do not come from the word’s case.

Direction is not arrival

Arabic قَصْدُ الشَّيْءِ can mean going towards something or aiming at it. Some lexicographers therefore explain شطر through قصد.

قصد in this explanation does not necessarily mean an inward intention without a spatial direction. A traveller going towards a city aims at that city even while remaining many miles away.

Al-Baqarah 2:144, 2:149 and 2:150 command through وَلِّ وَجْهَكَ and وَلُّوا وُجُوهَكُمْ. The direct meaning is therefore orienting the face towards a destination.

شطر itself nevertheless determines neither the distance nor how to find the direction, nor the required precision for someone unable to see the destination close by.

These questions may matter in further discussions of the qiblah, but cannot be resolved through the lexical meaning of شطر alone.

The difference between shaṭr and qiblah

Al-Baqarah 2:144 first says فَلَنُوَلِّيَنَّكَ قِبْلَةً تَرْضَاهَا, then فَوَلِّ وَجْهَكَ شَطْرَ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ.

قِبْلَة comes from ق ب ل and concerns what someone faces. شطر belongs to another root and indicates the destination’s direction.

The sentence introduces a qiblah and then expresses the direction of facing towards its destination. The concepts are connected without being lexically identical.

Likewise, وجه and turning have their own meanings. وجه identifies what faces a direction, while ولّى expresses the act of turning.

شطر itself therefore does not contain the entire qiblah concept or every dimension of religious orientation. That question requires studying several roots together and the relevant verses.

5. Spatial direction and the broader meaning of the qiblah

The qiblah verses both command turning one’s face towards the Sacred Mosque and connect the subject to faith, following the Messenger, guidance and relations between communities.

Al-Baqarah 2:142, discussing the question about the change of qiblah, gives this response:

«قُلْ لِلَّهِ الْمَشْرِقُ وَالْمَغْرِبُ يَهْدِي مَنْ يَشَاءُ إِلَىٰ صِرَاطٍ مُسْتَقِيمٍ»

“Say: the east and west belong to The God. He guides whom He wills to a straight path.”

(Al-Baqarah 2:142; part of the verse)

Al-Baqarah 2:148 also says:

«وَلِكُلٍّ وِجْهَةٌ هُوَ مُوَلِّيهَا فَاسْتَبِقُوا الْخَيْرَاتِ»

“Everyone has a direction towards which they turn. Compete, then, in good deeds.”

(Al-Baqarah 2:148; part of the verse)

These expressions show that the Qur’an discusses orientation beyond a place’s name. It also concerns The God’s ownership of east and west, guidance and competing in good deeds.

These concepts do not erase the spatial meaning of شطر. The command to face towards the Sacred Mosque is explicit in verses 144, 149 and 150, and its destination is named.

Conversely, the reality of that destination does not reduce the entire value of devotion to bodily facing towards a place. Al-Baqarah 2:148 discusses competing in goodness, and 2:177 describes righteousness beyond merely facing east or west.

These levels should not be opposed. A spatial direction can have a real place in a community’s worship and identity while also being connected to broader responsibilities and values.

For the root study, the conclusion is more limited and clear: شطر expresses the destination’s direction. Why this destination was appointed, its relation to Abraham’s way and its significance for believers’ collective life concern the qiblah concept and other verses, rather than meanings to place within the lexical definition of شطر.

Does shaṭr specify an exact direction or a general bearing?

Arabic شطر can indicate the direction in which something lies. Someone who can see the destination faces it through direct sight; if it is distant, its direction must be found another way.

The word itself prescribes neither observation, natural signs, calculation nor another method for finding the direction. Nor does it alone establish a precise margin of acceptable error.

شطر therefore establishes neither that only one completely calculated geometric angle is acceptable nor that any approximate direction suffices without regard to the destination.

The verse names the destination and commands facing it. How this direction is found and observed requires further evidence and rules.

6. Comparison with related roots and translation choices

ش ط ر (شَطْر) and ن ص ف (نِصْف)

نصف in its clear use denotes one of two equal portions of something. شطر can also denote half of something, overlapping with نصف in this field.

Arabic شطر also means direction or side, a use that cannot be replaced with نصف in every sentence.

شطر المال can mean half of the property, for example, whereas شطر بني تميم in early poetry means towards Banū Tamīm.

The second meaning suits Al-Baqarah 2:144. A dictionary’s inclusion of half therefore does not justify translating Qur’anic شطر as half.

ش ط ر (شَطْر) and و ج ه (وَجْه)

وجه concerns face, the front-facing side and orientation. This field can approach شطر in some uses.

In فَوَلِّ وَجْهَكَ شَطْرَ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ, however, وَجْهَكَ and شَطْرَ have different roles. The face is what turns towards a direction, and شطر expresses that direction.

شطر therefore should not be translated as وجه here, nor should the words’ association make their roots and meanings identical.

ش ط ر (شَطْر) and ق ب ل (قَبِلَ)

ق ب ل concerns facing, being before something and turning towards it. This family also supplies the Qur’anic قِبْلَة.

Qur’anic شطر indicates the direction of the Sacred Mosque. قبلة identifies a destination that someone faces.

These concepts are close in Al-Baqarah, but neither contains the other’s entire meaning. ش ط ر itself means neither a place of worship nor a religious institution or community.

ش ط ر (شَطْر) and ن ح و (نَحْو)

Arabic نحو can mean towards something or facing in its direction. Lexicographers also explain شطر in the qiblah verses through نحو.

Other uses of نحو concern manner, likeness and the way of speaking. Its closeness to شطر in spatial sentences therefore does not make all uses of the two words synonymous.

“Towards” and “in the direction of” can both convey شطر in translating Al-Baqarah.

ش ط ر (شَطْر) and ش ط أ (شَاطِئ)

شاطئ, meaning edge or shore, comes from ش ط أ and ends with hamzah. Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:30 mentions شَاطِئِ الْوَادِ الْأَيْمَنِ, the valley’s right bank.

شاطئ and شطر can both occur in spatial sentences, but differ in root and direct meaning.

شَطَط and تُشْطِطْ come from ش ط ط and concern departing from rightness and exceeding a limit in Qur’anic use. Resemblance of the letters should not mix this family with ش ط ر either.

Translation choices for Forqan

“Towards” or “in the direction of” is the most appropriate rendering of شطر in all five Qur’anic occurrences.

فَوَلِّ وَجْهَكَ شَطْرَ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ can be translated: “So turn your face towards the Sacred Mosque.”

For وَحَيْثُ مَا كُنْتُمْ فَوَلُّوا وُجُوهَكُمْ شَطْرَهُ, “And wherever you are, turn your faces towards it” is appropriate.

شَطْرَهُ here is towards it, with هُ referring to the Sacred Mosque.

The same equivalent should be retained in Al-Baqarah 2:149 and 2:150, since the construction and subject of the command are identical. Superficial variety does not require translating it as direction in one verse, half in another and spiritual orientation in a third.

In a detailed discussion of the qiblah, religious orientation or the command’s relation to Abraham’s way should be explained separately from the basic translation.

شطر should not become “go,” “enter,” “reach the Sacred Mosque” or “worship the Sacred Mosque.” It indicates direction and contains none of these verbs.

7. Conclusion

Arabic ش ط ر has two established semantic fields. One concerns half or part of something, such as dividing property into halves or milking part of a she-camel’s udder. The other concerns side, direction and distance, such as travelling towards a community, danger approaching from a frontier or someone becoming distant from home.

These fields can approach each other in some verbal images, but the available evidence does not establish either as the definite historical origin of the other.

Only شَطْر occurs in the Qur’an, five times in Al-Baqarah 2:144, 2:149 and 2:150. Each means side or direction, accompanying the command to turn the face towards the Sacred Mosque.

Two points matter here. First, شطر does not mean half of the mosque in these verses, but its direction. Second, the word itself is neither the entire qiblah concept nor a complete account of the wisdom of facing that destination. The qiblah is understood through the words and verses together.

This passage names a real spatial destination and explicitly commands facing it. At the same time, the Qur’an connects the subject with faith, following, guidance, not fearing opponents and competing in good deeds. Spatial meaning and these broader connections can be understood together, without either replacing the other.

The semantic core of ش ط ر needs to retain two branches: half or part of something, and side, direction and distance relative to a destination or reference point.

The second branch is clear and preferable in Qur’anic use: شطر المسجد الحرام means towards the Sacred Mosque. This identifies the destination, but alone explains neither every spiritual dimension of the qiblah nor the precise method of finding its direction at each geographical location.

Preserving this boundary keeps the verses’ direct meaning clear and allows the broader discussion of qiblah and religious orientation to be pursued in its own place, without imposing it on the lexical meaning of شطر.
