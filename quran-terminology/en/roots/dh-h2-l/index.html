Root DH H L (dhahala)

Qur’anic and lexical forms

تَذْهَلُ
(tadhhalu)
Becomes oblivious; sole Qur’anic form; third person feminine singular imperfect

ذَهَلَ
(dhahala)
Became oblivious; lexical Form I

ذَهِلَ
(dhahila)
Another vocalization; in the poetic variant

ذُهُول
(dhuhūl)
Diverted attention; non-Qur’anic verbal noun

ذَهْل
(dhahl)
Leaving aside or forgetting; non-Qur’anic verbal noun

ذَاهِل
(dhāhil)
Someone distracted; non-Qur’anic active participle

أَذْهَلَ
(adhhala)
Distracted someone; Form IV; non-Qur’anic

إِذْهَال
(idhhāl)
Causing distraction; Form IV verbal noun; non-Qur’anic

Attention diverted from something; forgetting or neglecting it through preoccupation; leaving it aside

DH H L concerns attention failing to remain on something, or a person forgetting and neglecting it through preoccupation with another matter. Severe fear, grief, mental preoccupation, or another cause may produce this state. Some Arabic uses also include deliberately setting something aside.

In Persian, dhuhūl usually suggests amazement, bewilderment, or being overwhelmed by a tremendous event. This fits the Qur’anic use without exhausting its Arabic range. Early Arabic could describe someone distracted by another task without a terrifying event necessarily occurring.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as تَذْهَلُ in Al-Ḥajj 22:2. The verse describes the terror and magnitude of the Hour’s shaking through a nursing woman becoming oblivious even to the one she is nursing.

A mother’s attention to an infant, especially during nursing, is among the closest and most natural human concerns. An event powerful enough to interrupt even that attention vividly communicates its magnitude.

Dhuhūl should therefore not simply mean “being afraid.” Fear causes it here, while dhuhūl itself concerns attention diverted from something previously attended to and cared for.

1. Core meaning and forms

Dhuhūl: attention diverted from something

One lexical analysis states the family’s core meaning as follows:

أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى شُغْلٍ عَنْ شَيْءٍ بِذُعْرٍ أَوْ غَيْرِهِ

aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā shughlin ʿan shayʾin bi-dhuʿrin aw ghayrih

Meaning: One semantic principle concerns being preoccupied away from something through fright or another cause.

Two elements matter: the object no longer attended to, and the factor diverting attention from it.

Arabic uses the following expressions:

ذَهَلَ عَنِ الشَّيْءِ: he became oblivious to something or forgot it.

ذَهِلَ عَنْهُ: he ceased attending to it, his attention turning away.

أَذْهَلَهُ الْأَمْرُ: the matter occupied him so strongly that he became oblivious to something else.

أَذْهَلَهُ الْخَوْفُ عَنْهُ: fear distracted him from attending to it.

الذُّهُول is a verbal noun of the family, denoting becoming oblivious, forgetting, or a lapse of attention.

A broader definition reads:

الذَّهْلُ تَرْكُكَ الشَّيْءَ تَتَنَاسَاهُ عَلَى عَمْدٍ أَوْ يَشْغَلُكَ عَنْهُ شَاغِلٌ

al-dhahlu tarkuka al-shayʾa tatanāsāhu ʿalā ʿamdin aw yashghaluka ʿanhu shāghil

Meaning: Dhahl is leaving something aside, either deliberately putting it out of mind or being distracted from it by another concern.

The Arabic field was therefore not confined to one cause or psychological condition.

Sometimes someone deliberately turns from a matter; sometimes a preoccupation overwhelms attention to what previously concerned them.

This breadth does not mean that the nursing woman in Al-Ḥajj 22:2 deliberately decides to abandon her infant. The context concerns overwhelming terror and intensity, with diverted attention resulting from that condition.

Dhahala and adhhala

ذَهَلَ and ذَهِلَ are both recorded for becoming oblivious and forgetting.

ذَهَلْتُ عَنْهُ describes the condition of someone oblivious to something.

أَذْهَلَنِي عَنْهُ كَذَا highlights the diverting cause: something distracted me from attending to that matter.

The first construction concerns the person’s condition; the second also includes its cause.

The distinction concerns Form I and the causative Form IV, without establishing an absolute rule about intention. The sentence and context show how dhuhūl occurs.

Only تَذْهَلُ occurs in the Qur’an: a Form I imperfect verb in the third person feminine singular.

ذَهْل, ذُهُول, ذَاهِل, and أَذْهَلَ are known in Arabic but do not occur in those forms in the Qur’an.

2. Early Arabic usage

A poetic witness from Labīd ibn Rabīʿah

Labīd ibn Rabīʿah, who lived through the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, describes in his famous Muʿallaqah a female wild bovine that has lost its calf and wandered in search of it.

One verse in this passage reads:

حَتَّى إِذَا يَئِسَتْ وَأَسْحَقَ حَالِقٌ
لَمْ يُبْلِهِ إِرْضَاعُهَا وَفِطَامُهَا

ḥattā idhā yaʾisat wa-asḥaqa ḥāliqun / lam yublihi irḍāʿuhā wa-fiṭāmuhā

Translation: Until she despaired of finding her calf and her udder wasted and lost its milk—an udder that nursing and weaning her calf had not worn away.

One transmission substitutes ذَهِلَتْ for يَئِسَتْ:

حَتَّى إِذَا ذَهِلَتْ وَأَسْحَقَ حَالِقٌ

ḥattā idhā dhahilat wa-asḥaqa ḥāliq

Meaning: Until she ceased attending to and searching for her calf and her udder wasted and lost its milk. The image is explained through grief and cessation of feeding causing the milk to diminish. Ḥāliq is also explained either through the udder’s previous fullness or its drawing upward after milk decreased.

Early commentaries record the variant. They explain ذَهِلَتْ here with سَلَتْ وَنَسِيَتْ: she ceased being occupied with it and forgot.

Dhahilat is a transmitted variant; it should not replace yaʾisat in every version without explanation.

The animal ceases pursuing its lost calf after searching and grieving. Dhuhūl concerns attention moving away from what had intensely occupied its mind and behavior.

It is therefore not confined to sudden fear; grief and exhaustion can also provide its setting.

This does not make every dhuhūl an end to affection or permanent forgetting. Labīd’s poem presents a particular subject within a narrative after a period of time.

Kuthayyir ʿAzzah: forgetting an attachment

The Umayyad poet Kuthayyir ʿAzzah opens a poem:

صَحَا قَلْبُهُ يَا عَزَّ أَوْ كَادَ يَذْهَلُ
وَأَضْحَى يُرِيدُ الصَّرْمَ أَوْ يَتَبَدَّلُ

ṣaḥā qalbuhu yā ʿazza aw kāda yadhhalu / wa-aḍḥā yurīdu al-ṣarma aw yatabaddal

Translation: His heart has recovered, ʿAzzah, or nearly ceased being absorbed in that attachment; now he wishes to sever the bond or seek a replacement.

The verse describes a changing heart and the wish to withdraw from an earlier attachment. Kāda expresses approaching that state, rather than necessarily its complete and lasting realization.

يَذْهَلُ concerns ceasing attention to an attachment that had occupied his heart.

It illustrates sulūw, finding release from an earlier attachment, which early lexical explanations also associate with dhuhūl.

Kuthayyir is an Umayyad poet. His verse is a post-Qur’anic witness and should not be assigned to the pre-Islamic period.

Together with Labīd’s variant and early lexical explanations, it nevertheless illustrates situations other than fear.

Dhahl of the night and other limited uses

ذَهْلٌ مِنَ اللَّيْلِ is recorded for a portion or hour of the night.

Its consonant is disputed: some transmissions use dhāl, others dāl, دَهْل. Some lexical explanations prefer the latter.

This use cannot therefore be connected confidently with the principal DH H L sense without caution.

ذُهْلُول is also recorded for a slender, swift horse, a use that some lexical analyses find difficult to explain through the core meaning described above.

Dhuhl also names certain Arab tribal groups. Proper names do not by themselves authorize extracting meanings of heedlessness or forgetting from the people’s or tribes’ names.

Such limited branches show that not every dictionary form reduces to one simple semantic definition.

3. “Every nursing woman becomes oblivious”: the Qur’anic occurrence

The root’s only occurrence is in Al-Ḥajj 22:2:

يَوْمَ تَرَوْنَهَا تَذْهَلُ كُلُّ مُرْضِعَةٍ عَمَّا أَرْضَعَتْ وَتَضَعُ كُلُّ ذَاتِ حَمْلٍ حَمْلَهَا وَتَرَى النَّاسَ سُكَارَىٰ وَمَا هُمْ بِسُكَارَىٰ وَلَٰكِنَّ عَذَابَ اللَّهِ شَدِيدٌ

yawma tarawnahā tadhhalu kullu murḍiʿatin ʿammā arḍaʿat wa-taḍaʿu kullu dhāti ḥamlin ḥamlahā wa-tarā al-nāsa sukārā wa-mā hum bi-sukārā wa-lākinna ʿadhāba allāhi shadīd

Translation: On the day you see it, every nursing woman becomes oblivious to what she nurses, and every pregnant one lays down her burden. You see people as drunk, though they are not drunk; The God’s punishment is severe.

The preceding verse, Al-Ḥajj 22:1, addresses all people and describes the magnitude of زَلْزَلَةَ السَّاعَةِ, the shaking of the Hour.

Al-Ḥajj 22:2 places three images together: a nursing woman becoming oblivious to what she nurses, pregnancy’s burden being laid down, and people appearing drunk although they are not.

They share one theme: an event intense enough to transform ordinary human attention and behavior.

Why murḍiʿah?

Early grammatical explanations distinguish مُرْضِع from مُرْضِعَة.

Murḍiʿ can describe a woman in the nursing phase or with a nursing infant, even when not nursing at that instant.

In a familiar lexical explanation, murḍiʿah with its final tāʾ emphasizes someone actually performing the act of nursing.

Several grammatical explanations of Al-Ḥajj 22:2 highlight this distinction.

On this reading, murḍiʿah strengthens the image: someone in the closest relationship of care with her infant at that moment becomes distracted from it by the event’s intensity.

The final tāʾ alone should not become an absolute rule that murḍiʿah always means nursing at that instant or murḍiʿ always excludes current nursing.

The distinction between a standing description and performing an act can depend on sentence structure and the speaker’s intention.

What is clear here is the close relationship with the nursing infant, attention to which is disrupted even so.

“What she has nursed”: the infant or nursing itself?

Two supported grammatical analyses of عَمَّا أَرْضَعَتْ are recorded.

On the first, mā is a relative pronoun referring to the one she has nursed: the infant.

The nursing woman thus becomes oblivious to the child she has nursed.

An object pronoun is understood after أَرْضَعَتْ: أَرْضَعَتْهُ.

On the second, mā forms a verbal noun construction referring to nursing itself.

The woman then ceases attending to nursing and its continuation.

Both analyses appear in early grammatical explanations and fit the overall image.

The first gives a particularly clear image of the event’s intensity: becoming oblivious to a being she is directly caring for at that moment.

The verbal noun analysis also has support, emphasizing interruption of an act she is performing.

The grammatical distinction does not change تَذْهَلُ’s core meaning. Both involve attention distracted from something very close and important.

Permanent forgetting or diverted attention?

Some lexical explanations gloss tadhhalu with tansā: she forgets.

This does not mean permanent destruction of memory or loss of recognition of her child.

Arabic dhuhūl may result from intense preoccupation, fear, or grief. Someone can become oblivious to a normally important concern without its information disappearing from memory forever.

The context of Al-Ḥajj 22:2 clearly concerns terror and an overwhelming event.

Tadhhalu describes its intensity diverting the nursing woman’s attention from her infant or nursing.

“Becomes oblivious,” “forgets,” and “her attention is diverted” can each convey aspects of the meaning.

“She no longer recognizes her child” or “forgets the child forever” go beyond the wording and require independent evidence.

4. Dhuhūl and the event’s intensity in Al-Ḥajj

Dhuhūl, fear, and distress

Al-Ḥajj 22:2 combines dhuhūl with pregnancy’s burden being laid down and the people’s unusual condition.

وَتَضَعُ كُلُّ ذَاتِ حَمْلٍ حَمْلَهَا describes every pregnant one laying down what she carries.

Early explanations sometimes interpret this as miscarriage through extreme terror.

That reading relates to the intensity of the image, but neither medical details nor the pregnancy’s precise stage follow from DH H L itself.

The verse continues:

وَتَرَى النَّاسَ سُكَارَىٰ وَمَا هُمْ بِسُكَارَىٰ

wa-tarā al-nāsa sukārā wa-mā hum bi-sukārā

Meaning: You see people as drunk, although they are not drunk.

Their appearance resembles drunkenness, which the Qur’an expressly denies being their actual condition.

The resemblance in behavior should not be identified with the actual cause of intoxication.

The severity of divine punishment follows, and the complete scene expresses the event’s terror.

Dhuhūl is one sign of ordinary attention being transformed: someone becomes oblivious to something normally important.

Dhuhūl itself does not mean drunkenness, unconsciousness, insanity, or total loss of cognition. Each condition requires its own words and evidence.

An actual event or an image expressing overwhelming terror?

Explanations of Al-Ḥajj 22:1 and Al-Ḥajj 22:2 differ on the shaking’s precise timing and how the scene occurs.

One reading places it at the threshold of resurrection, before earthly life ends. Nursing and pregnant women may then form part of people’s actual situation.

Another understands the verse to express the terror of resurrection through images revealing its magnitude. Nursing and pregnancy can illustrate the degree of terror even when the details of their realization in that world are unclear to us.

DH H L alone cannot establish either reading conclusively.

Both retain tadhhalu’s meaning: attention diverted from an important concern by the event’s intensity.

Lexical study should not change the word to establish a predetermined theory about the timing or structure of resurrection.

Nor should the Qur’an’s clear imagery be removed. Nursing, pregnancy, and the people’s condition belong in the translation; how and when they occur belongs to contextual interpretation.

5. Distinctions from related roots

DH H L and N S Y: becoming oblivious and forgetting

Study of N S Y

Nisyān can denote forgetting or leaving something neglected.

Dhuhūl can overlap with forgetting; early explanations sometimes gloss ذَهِلَ through نَسِيَ.

Dhuhūl often emphasizes attention distracted from something, particularly when another matter intensely occupies the mind.

Nisyān can occur without such an evident cause.

Someone may forget a name or subject without intense fear or preoccupation at that moment. Al-Ḥajj 22:2 instead connects the nursing woman’s diverted attention with a tremendous event.

This is a semantic tendency, rather than a rule that every nisyān is calm and every dhuhūl fearful.

DH H L and GH F L: heedlessness and diverted attention

Study of GH F L

Ghaflah has a broad Qur’anic field, including inattention, unawareness, or neglect of truth and responsibility.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:179 describes a group as الْغَافِلُونَ in a context of disregarding faculties of understanding and turning away from guidance.

Tadhhalu in Al-Ḥajj 22:2 describes a response to an overwhelming event’s terror.

The nursing woman’s inattention is not presented as ordinary moral weakness or irresponsibility. The event’s intensity diverts her attention from something important.

Every dhuhūl should therefore not be treated as blameworthy heedlessness or irresponsibility.

Some Qur’anic ghaflah, by contrast, concerns moral responsibility: people turn away despite opportunities to attend and understand.

Context establishes that distinction, rather than dhuhūl always being involuntary and ghaflah always intentional.

DH H L and S H W: distracted attention and sahw

Sahw concerns inattention, heedlessness, or the mind overlooking something.

Al-Māʿūn 107:5 reads:

الَّذِينَ هُمْ عَنْ صَلَاتِهِمْ سَاهُونَ

alladhīna hum ʿan ṣalātihim sāhūn

Meaning: Those who are heedless of their ṣalāh.

Sāhūn belongs to S H W and appears with the preposition ʿan.

Dhahala can likewise take ʿan, but a shared preposition does not make every implication of the roots identical.

The Qur’anic dhuhūl highlights terror and overwhelming intensity, while sahw in Al-Māʿūn appears within conduct and religious responsibility.

Their shared inattention does not make the nursing woman’s dhuhūl in Al-Ḥajj 22:2 identical with the censured sahw in Al-Māʿūn 107:5.

DH H L and F Z ʿ: diverted attention and alarm

Fazaʿ concerns fear, alarm, and disturbance at danger or a sudden event.

Dhuhūl focuses more on the resulting condition of attention and mental preoccupation.

Someone may feel afraid while continuing to attend to surrounding matters. Very intense fear may instead prevent attention to other concerns.

The event’s terror supplies the setting for dhuhūl in Al-Ḥajj 22:2.

Fear can therefore cause dhuhūl without fully translating tadhhalu.

“Every nursing woman becomes afraid” does not adequately convey the image: the verse describes her attention being diverted from her infant or nursing.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

The root’s sole Qur’anic form is تَذْهَلُ in Al-Ḥajj 22:2.

“Becomes oblivious,” “forgets,” and “her attention is diverted” are possible equivalents depending on sentence structure.

Forqan’s current Persian translation means:

“On the day you see it, every nursing woman becomes oblivious to what she nurses…”

This preserves the verb’s basic meaning and closely follows the construction عَمَّا أَرْضَعَتْ.

If mā is read as a relative pronoun, another rendering is:

“On the day you see it, every nursing woman becomes oblivious to the infant she is nursing…”

This is clearer in Persian and identifies the referent, but “infant” results from the grammatical analysis of mā and its reference.

If the aim is to remain close to the wording while leaving grammatical explanation for the reflection, Forqan’s current translation remains defensible.

“Forgets” may also convey the verse, provided the explanation does not imply permanent memory loss or inability to recognize the child.

“Is terrified” or “becomes afraid” alone is insufficient, substituting the cause for the state of diverted attention.

For a general Forqan translation, preserving becoming oblivious and attention being diverted is preferable.

7. Conclusion

DH H L concerns attention diverted from something, forgetting it, or leaving it aside. Fear, grief, intense mental preoccupation, or another cause may produce this state. Some Arabic uses also include deliberately leaving something aside.

Early poetry does not confine dhuhūl to sudden fear. Labīd’s transmitted variant concerns ceasing attention to a lost calf, while Kuthayyir’s verse concerns moving away from an earlier attachment.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, in Al-Ḥajj 22:2.

The Hour’s shaking is so terrifying that even a nursing woman becomes oblivious to what she is nursing. The image conveys intensity through disruption of one of the closest human concerns.

Tadhhalu alone does not establish permanent forgetting, loss of recognition, or a conscious decision to abandon the infant.

Final definition: DH H L concerns attention diverted from something, forgetting it, or leaving it aside, whether through preoccupation or, in some Arabic uses, deliberate withdrawal. The Qur’anic occurrence describes diverted attention through overwhelming terror and distress.

The root’s meaning should be distinguished from its cause. Fear, grief, and intense preoccupation may cause dhuhūl, but dhuhūl names the condition of attention distracted from a concern, rather than the fear or grief itself.
