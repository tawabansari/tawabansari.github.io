Apprehension with concern and care, compassion for another, and the reddish light on the horizon after sunset

Early Arabic SH F Q has related but distinct uses. Chief among them are shafaq, the light and redness remaining on the horizon after sunset, and ishfāq, apprehension, concern, and serious regard for something that may have unwelcome consequences.

In human experience, ishfāq is more than simply fearing a danger. It can describe taking the possibility of something unwelcome seriously, with concern and sensitivity. When the concern involves oneself, fear and caution stand out; when it involves someone loved, it may become compassion, care, and goodwill.

The same family includes shafaqah, compassion and tenderness mingled with concern. A shafīq person does not merely love another: they are also concerned about harm reaching that person and wish them well.

The Qur’anic occurrences fall into three groups: شَفَق for the afterglow, forms of أَشْفَقَ for apprehension, and forms of the active participle مُشْفِق for someone in that state.

Ishfāq describes believers and obedient servants of The God as well as offenders and wrongdoers afraid of their deeds’ consequences. The word itself therefore establishes neither faith nor guilt. The object and cause of concern, and the resulting conduct, determine its significance and value.

1. Early Arabic usage and lexical witnesses

Apprehension and concern about unwelcome consequences

أَشْفَقَ مِنَ الشَّيْءِ is used when someone fears something and takes its possible harm or unwelcome outcome seriously.

إِشْفَاق names this state, and مُشْفِق describes someone experiencing it.

For example, someone preparing for a difficult journey may worry about its dangers without becoming terrified or incapacitated. They consider possible harm and decide with greater care.

A serious responsibility may provoke the same response. Someone may fear accepting work with important consequences because failing to do it properly could harm themselves or others.

Ishfāq is related to fear in these uses, but does not necessarily mean flight, sudden terror, or loss of the ability to decide.

A poetic witness attributed to the pre-Islamic period

An early Arabic collection attributes the following verse to Jābir ibn Qaṭan al-Nahshalī, a poet associated with the pre-Islamic period:

فَإِنِّي ذُو مُحَافَظَةٍ هَضُومٌ

fa-innī dhū muḥāfaẓatin haḍūmun

إِذَا شَفِقَتْ عَلَى الرِّزْقِ الْعِيَالُ

idhā shafiqat ʿalā al-rizqi al-ʿiyālu

Translation:

“I uphold honor and am ready to forgo my share and give, even when households anxiously guard their provisions.”

Here, شَفِقَتْ عَلَى الرِّزْقِ concerns anxiety about provisions and reluctance to consume or give them away.

The poet depicts people guarding their livelihood for fear of its diminishing. Against this background, he speaks of his own generosity and readiness to give.

The witness shows that SH F Q was not confined to fear of enemies, warfare, or immediate danger: concern over losing something valued could also be expressed through this root.

Here, concern accompanies restraint and tight control. But such restraint or miserliness is not essential to every use of ishfāq; it emerges from the provisions and behavior described in this particular setting.

The verse has been transmitted in different forms. Variants include أَبِيٌّ or لِقَوْمِي in the first hemistich and الزاد in place of الرزق. This article retains the quoted wording and treats the variants as differences in transmission.

The verb شَفِقَتْ is also recorded with different vowels on fāʾ. The basic verb’s use in this sense was disputed among lexicographers, so it should not be presented as uniformly accepted in every form.

The attribution to Jābir ibn Qaṭan is recorded in an early collection of Arabic poetry and usage, but an independent date for this verse is not established here. It is a useful witness to usage attributed to the pre-Islamic period, without conclusively dating the verb’s first appearance or all its meanings.

The difference between أَشْفَقَ مِنْهُ and أَشْفَقَ عَلَيْهِ

The preposition accompanying the verb can clarify the direction of concern.

أَشْفَقَ مِنْهُ commonly describes fear of something or its consequences.

أَشْفَقَ عَلَيْهِ, by contrast, may describe concern for someone: fear that harm will reach them.

For example, a mother may worry about her child’s illness. أَشْفَقَتْ عَلَى وَلَدِهَا naturally conveys concern and compassion for her child, rather than fear of the child.

In أَشْفَقَ مِنَ الْخَطَرِ, however, the danger is what the person fears.

This distinction matters for the Qur’anic occurrences, most of which use min.

It must not become an absolute rule. Early شَفِقَ عَلَى الرِّزْقِ also concerns the possible loss of provisions. Thus ʿalā does not always imply love for a human being, and min alone does not determine fear’s exact intensity or character.

Shafaqah: compassion mingled with concern

شَفَقَة describes compassion and tenderness accompanied by concern for another.

شَفِيق likewise describes someone who values another’s welfare and fears that harm may reach them.

A familiar Arabic proverb says:

إِنَّ الشَّفِيقَ بِسُوءِ الظَّنِّ مُولَعٌ

inna al-shafīqa bi-sūʾi al-ẓanni mūlaʿun

Translation:

“The caring, concerned person is much given to anticipating misfortune.”

The proverb concerns someone who takes possible harm or loss seriously because the person or thing matters to them.

Parents whose child is traveling may worry more than others about dangers on the road. This need not reflect distrust of the child; it may arise from how much the child’s safety matters to them.

Love and concern come together in this usage.

The proverb does not establish that all compassion entails groundless suspicion, or that severe anxiety always proves genuine love.

It is recorded in early proverb collections, but its independent date of origin is not established here. It is a witness to recognized Arabic usage, not conclusive proof that this exact expression dates to the pre-Islamic period.

Shafaq in coverings and woven cloth

Lexical records also use شَفَق for a covering or cloth whose weave is thin, flimsy, or poor in quality.

ثَوْبٌ شَفَقُ النَّسْجِ describes a garment with a loose or sparse weave.

شَفَّقَ الثَّوْبَ concerns making a cloth’s weave thin or poor in quality. Separately, أَشْفَقَ الْعَطَاءَ is recorded for reducing a gift or giving little. These uses should not all be assigned to the same derived form.

They are connected with thinness and delicacy.

Some lexical explanations seek a shared core linking this thinness, the faint redness of twilight, and tenderness of heart in ishfāq.

That semantic explanation is understandable, but does not itself establish that every branch developed from one original physical meaning.

Recording different uses under one root is not the same as proving their historical order of development.

2. Shafaq: the afterglow on the horizon

A distinct, familiar meaning is شَفَق: light remaining on the horizon after the sun sets.

When the sun’s disk passes below the horizon as seen from the ground, the sky does not immediately become completely dark. Light and colors remain for a time; redness and related shades are among their conspicuous features.

Early Arabic calls this remaining redness and light shafaq.

Lexical descriptions include حُمْرَةُ الأُفُقِ, “the horizon’s redness,” and بَقِيَّةُ ضَوْءِ الشَّمْسِ, “the remaining sunlight.”

Some accounts restrict shafaq to the red glow; others also include the whitish light remaining afterward.

Its prominent, familiar meaning is the reddish light as night begins.

Shafaq, sunrise, and sunset

Shams names the sun, ghurūb describes its setting, and shafaq denotes the light remaining on the horizon afterward.

These concepts are related but distinct.

The sun may have set while shafaq remains visible.

As the afterglow disappears, night’s darkness spreads further.

Shafaq is therefore neither the sun itself nor the setting of its disk. It is a visible luminous phenomenon during the transition from day to night.

This Qur’anic use occurs in Al-Inshiqāq 84:16:

فَلَا أُقْسِمُ بِالشَّفَقِ

fa-lā uqsimu bi-l-shafaq

Al-Inshiqāq 84:16

Translation:

“No indeed; I swear by the afterglow.”

Al-Inshiqāq 84:17 then mentions the night and what it gathers, while Al-Inshiqāq 84:18 mentions the moon when it becomes full and its light complete.

Al-Inshiqāq 84:19 continues:

لَتَرْكَبُنَّ طَبَقًا عَنْ طَبَقٍ

la-tarkabunna ṭabaqan ʿan ṭabaq

Al-Inshiqāq 84:19

Translation:

“You will surely pass from state to state.”

In this sequence, shafaq forms part of a group of celestial phenomena and visible transitions. The rendering of lā in “No indeed; I swear” reflects a grammatical interpretation of the oath formula, rather than part of shafaq’s meaning.

Daylight diminishes, night arrives, and the moon appears in different states. Such changes can invite reflection on changing conditions.

This contextual association should not turn the lexical meaning of shafaq into “human transformation,” “passing through life’s stages,” or “the destiny of the Hereafter.”

The word itself denotes the reddish afterglow. Its relation to changes in the world and human life is understood from the oaths and the verses’ message together.

Does shafaq mean fear or concern?

Because shafaq and shafaqah belong to the same linguistic family, someone might try to derive a meaning about fear or compassion from the horizon’s redness.

For example, a literary similarity can be imagined between the delicate remaining light and the tenderness of a concerned heart.

That similarity is not the verse’s lexical definition.

In Al-Inshiqāq 84:16, shafaq denotes a celestial phenomenon, not human fear, divine compassion, or sorrow at day’s end.

A shared root also does not prove that the red afterglow historically generated the meaning of compassion, or that concern gave the afterglow its name.

Both usages are known in Arabic, but the historical sequence of their relationship is not certain from the available evidence.

3. Qur’anic forms and semantic core

SH F Q occurs eleven times in the Qur’an, in three morphological groups.

شَفَق is the noun for the afterglow, occurring in Al-Inshiqāq 84:16.

أَشْفَقَ is a Form IV verb, appearing as أَشْفَقْنَ in Al-Aḥzāb 33:72 and أَأَشْفَقْتُمْ in Al-Mujādilah 58:13.

مُشْفِق is the Form IV active participle, occurring in the plural forms مُشْفِقُونَ and مُشْفِقِينَ.

مُشْفِقُونَ occurs in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:28, Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:49, Al-Muʾminūn 23:57, Al-Shūrā 42:18, and Al-Maʿārij 70:27.

مُشْفِقِينَ occurs in Al-Kahf 18:49, Al-Shūrā 42:22, and Al-Ṭūr 52:26.

The difference between mushfiqūn and mushfiqīn concerns syntax and grammatical case, not two entirely different kinds of concern.

In وَأَشْفَقْنَ, the final nūn is the feminine plural subject pronoun. In أَأَشْفَقْتُمْ, the first hamzah marks a question and the second belongs to the Form IV verb.

Forms including شَفَقَة, شَفِيق, شَفِقَ, تَشْفِيق, and مُشَفَّق are recorded in Arabic but do not appear in these forms in the Qur’an.

The Qur’anic uses require two main branches to be defined separately:

Shafaq: the remaining, usually reddish light on the horizon during the transition from day to night.

Ishfāq: apprehension and concern involving serious regard for something with potentially unwelcome consequences.

In human usage, ishfāq may lead to self-protection or concern for another. The word itself determines neither the kind of danger nor the concern’s moral value.

4. Believers’ apprehension: concern about responsibility and accountability

Awe of the Lord and apprehension of the Hour

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:49 says:

الَّذِينَ يَخْشَوْنَ رَبَّهُمْ بِالْغَيْبِ وَهُمْ مِنَ السَّاعَةِ مُشْفِقُونَ

alladhīna yakhshawna rabbahum bi-l-ghaybi wa-hum mina al-sāʿati mushfiqūn

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:49

Translation:

“Those who stand in awe of their Lord in the unseen and are apprehensive of the Hour.”

The verse contains two words from different roots: يَخْشَوْنَ from KH SH Y and مُشْفِقُونَ from SH F Q.

Khashyah concerns the Lord, while ishfāq concerns the Hour.

This difference matters to the sentence’s structure.

Awe of the Lord may relate to recognizing His greatness, authority, and one’s responsibility before Him. Apprehension of the Hour, by contrast, concerns an event associated with accountability and the manifestation of deeds’ consequences.

Being mushfiq here does not mean denying resurrection; it characterizes people who take it seriously.

A person can believe an event will occur while remaining concerned about its consequences.

Ishfāq alongside khashyah

Al-Muʾminūn 23:57 says:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ هُمْ مِنْ خَشْيَةِ رَبِّهِمْ مُشْفِقُونَ

inna alladhīna hum min khashyati rabbihim mushfiqūn

Al-Muʾminūn 23:57

Translation:

“Indeed, those who are apprehensive out of awe of their Lord.”

مِنْ خَشْيَةِ رَبِّهِمْ supplies the basis for their apprehension.

Ishfāq may therefore be a state arising from khashyah.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:58 describes belief in the Lord’s signs, Al-Muʾminūn 23:59 rejects associating partners with Him, and Al-Muʾminūn 23:60 describes giving with apprehensive hearts.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:60 describes people who give what they give while their hearts are وَجِلَة, apprehensive, because they are returning to their Lord. The giving and the heart’s concern must both be preserved in explaining the verse.

This sequence connects the praised apprehension with doing good and accepting responsibility.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:61 states that these people hasten to good deeds.

Their ishfāq therefore does not mean loss of the capacity to act.

They are neither unaware of nor careless about responsibility and their ultimate outcome; that awareness accompanies faith and effort toward good.

Apprehension of punishment

Al-Maʿārij 70:27 says:

وَالَّذِينَ هُمْ مِنْ عَذَابِ رَبِّهِمْ مُشْفِقُونَ

wa-alladhīna hum min ʿadhābi rabbihim mushfiqūn

Al-Maʿārij 70:27

Translation:

“And those who are apprehensive of their Lord’s punishment.”

Al-Maʿārij 70:28 then states that their Lord’s punishment is not something from which one can assume complete security.

The connection clarifies ishfāq in this context.

The point is attention to deeds’ consequences, rather than a requirement to experience incapacitating terror at every moment.

Al-Maʿārij 70:24 and Al-Maʿārij 70:25 describe recognizing a share of one’s wealth for those in need, while Al-Maʿārij 70:26 describes affirming the Day of Recompense; the surrounding verses also describe responsible conduct.

This concern accompanies social and moral action.

In such a setting, ishfāq can guard against an unfounded sense of immunity from accountability.

Apprehension among one’s family

In Al-Ṭūr 52:26, the inhabitants of Paradise say in conversation:

قَالُوا إِنَّا كُنَّا قَبْلُ فِي أَهْلِنَا مُشْفِقِينَ

qālū innā kunnā qablu fī ahlinā mushfiqīn

Al-Ṭūr 52:26

Translation:

“They said: ‘Previously, among our families, we were apprehensive.’”

فِي أَهْلِنَا identifies the setting of their former lives.

It should not be changed without evidence into “we were worried about our families”: fī expresses being among the family, not necessarily the family as the direct object of concern.

Their words continue in Al-Ṭūr 52:27:

فَمَنَّ اللَّهُ عَلَيْنَا وَوَقَانَا عَذَابَ السَّمُومِ

fa-manna allāhu ʿalaynā wa-waqānā ʿadhāba al-samūm

Al-Ṭūr 52:27

Translation:

“Then The God favored us and protected us from the scorching punishment.”

The continuation relates their former concern to their ultimate outcome and punishment.

In their earthly family life they were attentive to accountability, and now speak of deliverance.

The verse does not imply that their lives were always bitter, joyless, or without ease. Being apprehensive among one’s family does not mean being deprived of affection and life’s blessings.

Apprehension of angels and honored servants

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:28 says:

يَعْلَمُ مَا بَيْنَ أَيْدِيهِمْ وَمَا خَلْفَهُمْ وَلَا يَشْفَعُونَ إِلَّا لِمَنِ ارْتَضَىٰ وَهُمْ مِنْ خَشْيَتِهِ مُشْفِقُونَ

yaʿlamu mā bayna aydīhim wa-mā khalfahum wa-lā yashfaʿūna illā li-mani irtaḍā wa-hum min khashyatihi mushfiqūn

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:28

Translation:

“He knows what is before them and what is behind them; they intercede only for whom He approves, and they are apprehensive out of awe of Him.”

This continues the description of The God’s honored servants, who do not precede Him in speech and act by His command.

مُشْفِقُونَ describes these servants.

It therefore does not establish their guilt; here it accompanies awe, obedience, and recognition of the limits of their position.

The verse also restricts intercession by divine approval.

The position of intercessor cannot therefore be turned into independent, unlimited authority: those whose intercession is discussed are themselves described as apprehensive and obedient.

This topic connects with Forqan’s separate study, “Intercession in the Qur’an.”

5. Offenders’ apprehension after their deeds are disclosed

The same word applied to obedient servants and believers also describes offenders and wrongdoers.

Al-Kahf 18:49 says:

وَوُضِعَ الْكِتَابُ فَتَرَى الْمُجْرِمِينَ مُشْفِقِينَ مِمَّا فِيهِ

wa-wuḍiʿa al-kitābu fa-tarā al-mujrimīna mushfiqīna mimmā fīh

Al-Kahf 18:49

Translation:

“The record is set down, and you see the offenders apprehensive of what it contains.”

They then express astonishment that small and large deeds have been recorded, and find what they did present before them.

The verse concludes by affirming that the Lord wrongs no one.

Here, apprehension arises from what is recorded in the record of deeds.

The offenders are concerned because they confront their conduct’s consequences.

Linguistically, this belongs to the same family as believers’ ishfāq, but the circumstances and cause differ.

Believers are attentive to responsibility in earthly life, before the Hour and the disclosure of their outcome. In this scene, offenders confront the record and consequences of their deeds.

An offender’s apprehension therefore does not establish the same moral quality as that of reverent believers.

Wrongdoers’ apprehension about what they have earned

Al-Shūrā 42:22 says:

تَرَى الظَّالِمِينَ مُشْفِقِينَ مِمَّا كَسَبُوا وَهُوَ وَاقِعٌ بِهِمْ

tarā al-ẓālimīna mushfiqīna mimmā kasabū wa-huwa wāqiʿun bihim

Al-Shūrā 42:22

Translation:

“You see the wrongdoers apprehensive of what they have earned, while it will fall upon them.”

مِمَّا كَسَبُوا specifies the object of their concern.

They fear the consequence of what they have earned.

وَهُوَ وَاقِعٌ بِهِمْ emphasizes that the consequence in question will reach them.

This is therefore not merely vague uncertainty about the future: they confront their deeds’ outcome.

The continuation contrasts them with believers who do righteous deeds, in the gardens of Paradise.

The contrast again shows that ishfāq does not itself determine positive or negative moral value.

An offender may fear punishment without having repaired the past or truly repented.

A righteous person may also fear failing a responsibility without having committed an offense.

Fear of an action’s consequence is not identical to remorse, repentance, or repairing harm. Each must be assessed through the person’s words and conduct.

Believers’ apprehension of the Hour

Al-Shūrā 42:18 says:

يَسْتَعْجِلُ بِهَا الَّذِينَ لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ بِهَا وَالَّذِينَ آمَنُوا مُشْفِقُونَ مِنْهَا وَيَعْلَمُونَ أَنَّهَا الْحَقُّ

yastaʿjilu bihā alladhīna lā yuʾminūna bihā wa-alladhīna āmanū mushfiqūna minhā wa-yaʿlamūna annahā al-ḥaqq

Al-Shūrā 42:18

Translation:

“Those who do not believe in it seek to hasten it; those who believe are apprehensive of it and know that it is the truth.”

The verse distinguishes two responses to the Hour.

Those without belief demand that it arrive quickly. Believers, by contrast, are concerned about it and know it to be a reality that will occur.

Their apprehension here does not arise from denial or doubt that the Hour will happen.

They take its arrival seriously and are attentive to its consequences.

Knowing and being certain that an event is real does not necessarily eliminate concern about one’s own outcome in it.

Someone may believe in the establishment of justice while fearing the disclosure of their own shortcomings.

Here concern is associated with understanding and responsibility, rather than denying the truth.

6. Apprehension before responsibility and expense

The heavens, earth, and mountains’ apprehension of the trust

Al-Aḥzāb 33:72 says:

إِنَّا عَرَضْنَا الْأَمَانَةَ عَلَى السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ وَالْجِبَالِ فَأَبَيْنَ أَنْ يَحْمِلْنَهَا وَأَشْفَقْنَ مِنْهَا وَحَمَلَهَا الْإِنْسَانُ إِنَّهُ كَانَ ظَلُومًا جَهُولًا

innā ʿaraḍnā al-amānata ʿalā al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍi wa-l-jibāli fa-abayna an yaḥmilnahā wa-ashfaqna minhā wa-ḥamalahā al-insānu innahu kāna ẓalūman jahūlā

Al-Aḥzāb 33:72

Translation:

“We offered the trust to the heavens, the earth, and the mountains, but they refused to bear it and were apprehensive of it; the human being bore it. Indeed, he was exceedingly unjust and ignorant.”

The pronoun hā in أَشْفَقْنَ مِنْهَا refers to the trust.

The heavens, earth, and mountains respond to bearing the trust with refusal and apprehension, whereas the human being accepts it.

This use links ishfāq with the weight of responsibility.

Taking a responsibility’s consequences seriously may cause concern about one’s ability to fulfill it.

Ashfaqna itself does not identify precisely what the trust is or its nature. That must be examined through the study of ʾ M N and the surrounding verses.

Attributing apprehension to the heavens, earth, and mountains does not, without further evidence, establish human emotions or nervous systems for them.

The verse describes their response to the trust without detailing the mode of its existence.

Nor should the expression be declared meaningless or devoid of reality merely because its manner is unknown to us.

The linguistic point is clear: ishfāq here involves apprehension and serious regard for a responsibility whose acceptance has consequences.

Concern about offering charity

Al-Mujādilah 58:13 says:

أَأَشْفَقْتُمْ أَنْ تُقَدِّمُوا بَيْنَ يَدَيْ نَجْوَاكُمْ صَدَقَاتٍ

a-ashfaqtum an tuqaddimū bayna yaday najwākum ṣadaqāt

Al-Mujādilah 58:13

Translation:

“Were you apprehensive about offering charitable gifts before your private consultation?”

This follows Al-Mujādilah 58:12, which instructed believers to offer charity before private consultation with the Messenger.

Al-Mujādilah 58:13 questions their apprehension about doing so.

أَأَشْفَقْتُمْ here concerns apprehension about a specific action.

The subject is giving charity before the consultation.

Ishfāq here consequently relates to the financial burden or difficulty of fulfilling a duty.

Someone may hesitate because they fear diminished wealth or consider a cost burdensome. But ashfaqtum alone does not establish that all the addressees acted from miserliness, poverty, or the same intention.

Al-Mujādilah 58:12 also provides relief for someone without the means. Al-Mujādilah 58:13 mentions their failure to make the offering and the return of divine mercy, then commands establishing ṣalāh, giving zakāh, and obeying The God and the Messenger.

The passage concerns a particular duty and its circumstances; it does not make every concern about living expenses inherently sinful.

Here too, ishfāq names the concern. Judgment of conduct depends on the command, capacity, circumstances, and response.

7. Distinctions from related roots

SH F Q and KH W F: apprehension and fear

Khawf expresses the general field of fear and concern about danger or unwelcome consequences.

Ishfāq is also connected with fear, but its familiar uses emphasize sensitivity and concern about something taken seriously.

Someone may be startled by a sudden sound without having previously considered or worried about that matter.

By contrast, a person apprehensive of an important decision’s outcome may act calmly and deliberately while keeping possible harm in mind.

This is a semantic tendency, not an absolute division.

Khawf can also be informed and cautious, while ishfāq can approach intense fear in some circumstances.

SH F Q and KH SH Y: apprehension and awe

Khashyah and ishfāq sometimes occur together in a Qur’anic verse.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:49, khashyah concerns the Lord and ishfāq the Hour.

In Al-Muʾminūn 23:57, awe of the Lord provides the basis for ishfāq.

Qur’anic khashyah often involves recognizing its object and taking authority or consequences seriously.

Ishfāq tends to emphasize concern about the outcome.

This must not become an absolute rule that khashyah is always knowledge and ishfāq always feeling. Both may involve understanding and inward response.

Forqan’s study of KH SH Y examines the distinction more extensively.

Study of KH SH Y

SH F Q and Ḥ DH R: apprehension and caution

Ḥadhar concerns vigilance and caution before danger, frequently emphasizing practical readiness or avoidance.

Ishfāq is a state of apprehension about something or its consequences.

It may lead someone to exercise caution, but the concepts are not identical.

Someone concerned about a relative’s illness may be moved to care for them and seek treatment.

The concern relates to ishfāq, while precautionary measures may belong to the field of ḥadhar.

The word ishfāq itself does not establish that any particular action has been taken.

SH F Q and R ʾ F: concerned compassion and tenderness

Raʾfah concerns kindness and tenderness toward another’s situation.

Shafaqah may likewise involve love and compassion, but its familiar use prominently includes concern that harm might reach someone.

A person may treat someone suffering with kindness and work to ease their pain. Raʾfah and shafaqah can coincide in that situation.

Raʾfah does not necessarily mean fear of a future event.

Ishfāq, conversely, may exist without affectionate compassion, as when offenders fear accountability or someone worries about accepting a weighty responsibility.

Not every Qur’anic mushfiq can therefore be translated “kind and compassionate.”

SH F Q and W J L: apprehension and inward fear

Wajal concerns inward fear and unease and is associated in the Qur’an with the heart’s state.

In Al-Muʾminūn 23:60, وَقُلُوبُهُمْ وَجِلَةٌ describes those apprehensively aware of their return to their Lord.

Ishfāq may accompany such fear, but does not necessarily describe visible trembling or agitation of the heart.

In uses concerning responsibility and ultimate outcomes, ishfāq may emphasize continuing concern and sensitivity, while wajal highlights inward fear and emotional response.

This distinction too should not be converted into fixed, necessary stages.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

For شَفَق in Al-Inshiqāq 84:16, Persian shafaq is appropriate, since it is also a familiar Persian name for the red glow after sunset. In English, “afterglow” is suitable.

A lexical explanation can say: “the reddish light remaining on the horizon after the sun sets.”

For إِشْفَاق and its derivatives, suitable equivalents include “to fear,” “to be concerned,” and “to be apprehensive.”

Modern Persian shafaqat commonly suggests kindness and compassion. Using it for ishfāq in every verse may therefore mislead the reader.

For example, مُشْفِقِينَ مِمَّا فِيهِ in Al-Kahf 18:49 does not mean offenders feel compassion for the contents of their record. They fear what it contains.

Likewise, the wrongdoers in Al-Shūrā 42:22 are concerned about their deeds’ consequences, not affectionate toward those deeds.

مِنَ السَّاعَةِ مُشْفِقُونَ in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:49 can be rendered:

“They are apprehensive of the Hour.”

In Al-Muʾminūn 23:57:

“They are apprehensive out of awe of their Lord.”

In Al-Ṭūr 52:26:

“Previously, among our families, we were apprehensive.”

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:72:

“They were apprehensive of it.”

In Al-Mujādilah 58:13:

“Were you apprehensive about offering charitable gifts before your private consultation?”

And in Al-Inshiqāq 84:16:

“No indeed; I swear by the afterglow.”

These renderings preserve the core meaning; accompanying expressions provide the cause or object of concern.

“Responsible concern,” “fear of accountability,” “fear of punishment,” and “compassion for another” may explain particular contexts, but should not all become invariant meanings of the root itself.

9. Conclusion

Early Arabic SH F Q has two prominent semantic fields: the celestial afterglow and inward apprehension.

Shafaq denotes the redness and light remaining after sunset. In Al-Inshiqāq 84:16 it appears alongside the night and moon in a series of Qur’anic oaths.

Ishfāq concerns apprehension about something whose possible harm or unwelcome consequences a person takes seriously.

Early poetic witnesses show concern about diminishing provisions. In human usage, such concern may also accompany love and compassion for another.

But shafaqah as kindness does not cover the root’s entire Qur’anic range.

Mushfiq describes believers and obedient servants as well as offenders and wrongdoers.

Believers are not careless about the Hour, accountability, or punishment. The God’s honored servants are apprehensive out of awe of Him. Offenders and wrongdoers, meanwhile, fear their deeds and the consequences.

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:72, apprehension relates to bearing a weighty trust, and in Al-Mujādilah 58:13 to fulfilling a particular duty.

Ishfāq can thus arise before responsibility, danger, accountability, or an unwelcome outcome. The word alone does not conclusively establish whether the concern is justified, the person’s moral worth, or their subsequent behavior.

The difference between أَشْفَقَ مِنْهُ and أَشْفَقَ عَلَيْهِ also shows that the family can express fear of something and concern for another. Compassion is one use, not the exclusive meaning of every derivative.

The two branches can therefore be defined as follows:

Shafaq is the light and redness remaining on the horizon after sunset. Ishfāq is apprehension and concern involving serious regard for something with potentially unwelcome consequences. The concern may involve oneself, another person, a heavy responsibility, or the outcome of deeds; in some uses it accompanies compassion and care.

For Forqan’s Persian translation, shafaq for the celestial phenomenon and “being fearful and concerned” for Qur’anic ishfāq are clear primary equivalents. Shafaqat in the sense of compassion should be used only where the direction of concern and context support it.
