Qur’anic root ك ظ م

Restraint, containing anger, and bearing inward pressure and grief

Qur’anic forms

الْكَاظِمِينَ
كَظِيمٌ
مَكْظُومٌ

1. Meaning in early Arabic

ك ظ م concerns holding in, blocking an outlet, containing anger, silence, and being overwhelmed by pressure or grief.

Ibn Faris identifies holding and gathering as basic meanings. Restraining anger resembles preventing something gathered inside from escaping.

Lexicons record these expressions:

  • كَظَمَ السِّقَاءَ: tied the mouth of a filled waterskin.
  • كَظَمَ الْبَابَ: shut the door and blocked passage.
  • كَظَمَ غَيْظَهُ: contained his anger and prevented its eruption.
  • كَظَمَ الْبَعِيرُ: the camel stopped chewing its cud.
  • أَخَذَ بِكَظَمِهِ: seized his throat and restricted his breathing.

A verse attributed to Ziyad ibn ʿUlba al-Hudhali describes a woman:

كَظِيمَ الْحَجْلِ وَاضِحَةَ الْمُحَيَّا عَدِيلَةَ حُسْنِ خَلْقٍ فِي تَمَامِ

With a silent anklet, a bright face, and beauty of proportion in a complete form.

Lexical commentary explains كَظِيمَ الْحَجْلِ as an anklet whose space is filled by the leg, preventing movement and sound.

This connects fullness, restricted movement, and prevented sound.

The verse’s exact date has not been independently established; the evidence considered here does not securely date the family’s use before the Qur’an.

The Qur’an itself attests containing anger, bearing grief, and inward pressure as recognized meanings at revelation.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important Arabic forms include:

  • كَظَمَ ـ يَكْظِمُ: held in; contained; blocked an outlet.
  • كَظْم: holding in; containment; restraint.
  • كَاظِم: someone holding something in or preventing its expression.
  • كَاظِمُونَ and كَاظِمِينَ: plurals of كَاظِم.
  • كَظِيم: containing anger or grief; also filled with inward distress.
  • مَكْظُوم: afflicted by pressure, grief, or severe constraint.
  • كُظُوم: silence or containment; also a camel’s cessation of cud-chewing.
  • كَظَم: throat or passage for breath.
  • كِظَامَة: a means of binding or restraint; also an underground water conduit in some usages.
  • كَظَائِم: plural of كِظَامَة.

Qur’anic forms examined:

الْكَاظِمِينَ ـ كَاظِمِينَ ـ كَظِيمٌ ـ مَكْظُومٌ

The Qur’an uses the active participle كَاظِم, adjective كَظِيم, and passive participle مَكْظُوم. The verb كَظَمَ and verbal noun كَظْم do not occur.

3. Core meaning and material imagery

A clear material image is a waterskin tied shut after filling so its contents do not escape.

The water remains inside, but its outlet is closed.

This helps explain uses concerning anger and grief.

Anger can build inward pressure toward speech or action. Restraining it and avoiding an impulsive response accords with كَظْم.

Containment is not always deliberate. Grief, fear, or distress may exert such pressure that breathing or emotional expression seems blocked.

Two distinct situations therefore occur:

Deliberate restraint of an inward state, and subjection to overwhelming pressure or constraint.

This distinction matters for كَاظِم, كَظِيم, and مَكْظُوم.

4. Qur’anic usages

الْكَاظِمِينَ الْغَيْظَ: deliberate restraint of anger

The verse presents restraining anger as a characteristic of the mindful.

الْكَاظِمِينَ is a plural active participle; الْغَيْظَ is its object.

غَيْظ denotes intense anger and its inward pressure; كَظْم is the restraint of that anger.

The Qur’an does not require that people never feel anger. Injustice, betrayal, or wrongdoing can provoke it; what matters is their response.

Someone deceived in a transaction may become intensely angry. They could insult, attack, or harm the other person, or contain the anger, consider the situation, and pursue their right justly.

In the latter case, anger has not vanished, but has not been allowed to govern conduct.

كَظْمُ الْغَيْظ concerns this capacity to regulate one’s response.

Restraining anger and forgiving others

After those who restrain anger, Al-Imran 3:134 mentions those who pardon people.

These are distinct actions.

Someone may restrain anger while still seeking restoration of a violated right. In other circumstances they may choose to pardon the offender.

كَظْم concerns restraint; عَفْو concerns pardon or foregoing accountability.

Al-Shura also clarifies this distinction:

Restraining anger does not mean surrender to oppression, compulsory abandonment of rights, or endless tolerance of misconduct.

People can restrain anger while firmly and justly resisting wrongdoing.

كَظِيمٌ: Jacob’s contained grief

كَظِيم describes Jacob enduring intense grief.

Three semantic families describe this state:

  • أَسَف: sorrow and grief.
  • حُزْن: sadness associated with loss and affliction.
  • كَظِيم: someone bearing grief gathered within.

Jacob’s grief was severe enough to affect his physical condition.

Containing grief does not mean absolute silence or withholding every expression of feeling. Jacob speaks of his sorrow in this very verse, and later says:

Jacob does not deny his grief. He suffers, speaks of it, and brings it before his Lord.

The context shows that contained grief is compatible with expressing pain, praying, and requesting help.

A Prophet’s intense grief also shows that faith and patience do not eliminate ordinary human feelings.

كَظِيمٌ: anger at the birth of a daughter

The same adjective used of Jacob describes someone displeased by news of a daughter’s birth.

The origins and moral significance of the two states differ.

Jacob grieved separation from his son; this person’s grief and anger arise from considering a daughter’s birth humiliating.

The next verse describes the consequences:

The Qur’an criticizes the judgment that treats a daughter’s birth as calamity and disgrace instead of good news.

وَهُوَ كَظِيمٌ conveys intense displeasure and inward pressure.

كَظِيم is therefore not inherently a commendation. Someone may grieve a real loss, or resent something good because of a mistaken judgment.

كَظِيمٌ: contradiction between belief and conduct

The verse’s ending matches al-Nahl 16:58.

Al-Zukhruf exposes the contradiction of attributing daughters to The God while resenting having daughters oneself.

They disliked for themselves what they attributed to Him.

كَظِيم here describes inward pressure and displeasure, not praiseworthy self-control.

In both verses the contained feeling nevertheless affects the face.

كَظْم therefore does not imply that every trace of anger or grief is invisible.

كَاظِمِينَ: constriction on the Day of Reckoning

كَاظِمِينَ accompanies the image of hearts reaching throats.

Intense fear and anxiety can make someone feel breathless and unable to speak.

The image conveys the severity of dread and pressure at the reckoning.

Unlike Al-Imran 3:134, anger is not expressly named as the object. “Restraining their anger” is therefore unsuitable here.

The word describes people overwhelmed by constriction and fear.

This also accords with كَظَم meaning the throat or passage for breath.

The verse does not detail the physical mechanism. Translation should preserve hearts at throats and people in severe constraint.

مَكْظُومٌ: Jonah in severe distress

مَكْظُوم is the passive participle of كَظَمَ, describing someone overwhelmed by pressure and distress.

The verse concerns Jonah calling out in affliction.

The account in al-Anbiya helps explain this condition:

Al-Qalam describes Jonah as مَكْظُوم; al-Anbiya calls his distress الْغَمّ.

The descriptions illuminate one another.

Overwhelmed by severe constraint, Jonah called upon his Lord and was delivered.

Again, كَظْم does not mean absolute silence: the Qur’an expressly says that he called while مَكْظُوم.

“Someone restraining anger” does not fully convey this occurrence, where constraint and distress are more prominent.

5. Morphology and syntax

كَاظِم, كَظِيم, and مَكْظُوم

كَاظِم is an active participle on the pattern فَاعِل.

Al-Imran 3:134 supplies the explicit object الْغَيْظَ and attributes restraint to the people.

Ghafir 40:18 uses the same participle without an explicit object to describe constriction and pressure.

كَظِيم follows فَعِيل, which can have active-like or passive-like meanings in different Arabic words.

Lexicons use كَظِيم both for someone containing anger or grief and for someone afflicted by intense inward distress.

كَاظِمِينَ is accusative and circumstantial.

It may describe the owners of the hearts, understood from الْقُلُوبُ.

Another analysis treats it as describing the hearts themselves with a human attribute.

Both convey severe pressure and constriction that day.

Clauses containing كَظِيم and مَكْظُوم

فَهُوَ كَظِيمٌ describes Jacob’s state in Yusuf 12:84.

In al-Nahl 16:58 and al-Zukhruf 43:17, وَهُوَ كَظِيمٌ describes the hearer of the news as his face darkens.

وَهُوَ مَكْظُومٌ in al-Qalam 68:48 describes Jonah as he calls.

These forms describe conditions, not necessarily enduring character traits.

6. Comparison with related roots

ك ظ م compared with غ ي ظ

غَيْظ denotes intense anger and its inward pressure.

كَظْم concerns containing what is within.

In الْكَاظِمِينَ الْغَيْظَ, غَيْظ is what is restrained; كَظْم is the restraint.

كَظْم therefore does not itself mean anger and can concern grief or other inward pressures.

ك ظ م compared with غ ض ب

غَضَب concerns anger and intense displeasure.

Containing anger means preventing it from becoming an impulsive, harmful response.

Someone may feel anger yet restrain it, or express anger without necessarily acting unjustly.

Feeling anger, expressing it, and acting while angry are distinct matters.

ك ظ م compared with ك ت م

كَتْم concerns hiding or not revealing something.

People can conceal truth, news, knowledge, or a secret.

كَظْم also involves preventing expression, but its Qur’anic uses emphasize anger, grief, and inward pressure.

A person can conceal truth without emotional strain, or contain grief without hiding its existence, as Jacob spoke of his sorrow.

The terms are therefore related but not identical.

ك ظ م compared with ح ز ن

حُزْن names grief and heaviness of heart.

كَظْم may describe how that grief is borne and contained.

In Yusuf 12:84, حُزْن causes Jacob’s eyes to whiten; كَظِيم describes him bearing that grief.

People may express grief freely or hold it within.

حُزْن names the feeling; كَظْم describes one state of bearing or expressing it.

ك ظ م compared with ك ر ب

كَرْب denotes severe distress and crushing pressure.

مَكْظُوم can describe someone subjected to such distress.

كَرْب principally names the affliction; مَكْظُوم describes the afflicted person.

ك ظ م compared with ص ب ر

صَبْر concerns perseverance, self-restraint, and steadfastness under difficulty.

كَظْم concerns containing something gathered within.

Restraining anger can express صَبْر, but patience extends beyond emotional restraint.

Someone may persevere in pursuing justice, fulfilling a difficult responsibility, or remaining committed to truth without being angry.

Al-Qalam 68:48 places the command to persevere beside Jonah’s description as مَكْظُوم, distinguishing deliberate steadfastness from overwhelming distress.

ك ظ م compared with ع ف و

عَفْو concerning wrongdoing means pardon or foregoing accountability.

كَظْم concerns restraint of anger.

Al-Imran 3:134 expresses these through separate descriptions.

Someone can contain anger, avoid injustice, and then decide whether to pardon or pursue their right justly.

7. Suggested renderings for Forqan

كَظِيمٌ — Yusuf 12:84containing his grief
كَظِيمٌ — al-Nahl 16:58filled with anger and grief

Rendering every derivative as “restraining anger” is unsuitable: some concern grief, others fear and distress.

The modern expression “emotional suppression” should not replace all these meanings. Deliberate behavioral restraint, bearing grief, and overwhelming psychological pressure are distinct states.

Conclusion

ك ظ م concerns holding in, blocking an outlet, containment, and restraint.

The Qur’an uses it for deliberate restraint of anger, Jacob’s contained grief, resentment at daughters’ births, dread at reckoning, and Jonah’s severe distress.

Moral value depends on context. Restraining anger to avoid injustice is commendable; resentment arising from mistaken judgment is not a virtue.

Nor does restraining anger mean denying feelings, accepting oppression, or compulsorily abandoning human rights.

The common meaning is holding something in and restricting its expression or outlet, whether through deliberate restraint or overwhelming affliction.
