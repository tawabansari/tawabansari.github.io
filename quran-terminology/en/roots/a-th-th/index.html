The Qur’anic Root A TH TH

Athāth: household goods, accumulated possessions, and abundance; from thick hair and dense vegetation to material provision

Qur’anic and lexical forms

أَثَاثًا
(athāthan)
The only Qur’anic form: an accusative noun, twice, Al-Naḥl 16:80 and Maryam 19:74

أَثَّ
(aththa)
Lexical Form I verb: became abundant or dense; absent from the Qur’an

يَأَثُّ / يَئِثُّ / يَؤُثُّ
(yaʾaththu / yaʾiththu / yaʾuththu)
Lexical imperfect vocalizations; absent from the Qur’an

أَثّ / أَثَاثَة
(athth / athāthah)
Lexical verbal nouns for abundance; athāthah is also a disputed proposed singular of athāth

أَثِيث
(athīth)
Lexical adjective: thick or dense, illustrated in poetry

أَثِيثَة
(athīthah)
Lexical feminine of athīth; absent from the Qur’an

أَثَائِث / أَثَايِث
(athāʾith / athāyith)
Lexical adjectival plurals; absent from the Qur’an

أَثَّثَ
(aththatha)
Lexical Form II verb: to make soft or prepare for use

تَأَثَّثَ
(taʾaththatha)
Lexical Form V verb: to acquire goods or material means

1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

The Arabic root A TH TH concerns abundance, density, and things gathering together. أَثَّ الشَّعْرُ (aththa al-shaʿru) describes hair becoming plentiful and thick; نَبَاتٌ أَثِيثٌ (nabātun athīthun) describes vegetation growing densely and becoming intertwined.

The same family includes أَثَاث (athāth), the goods and furnishings of daily life, particularly things assembled in a home. In broader early Arabic usage, it could also encompass a person’s accumulated property and possessions.

Qur’anic athāth should therefore not be confined to the narrower modern sense of “furniture.” Clothing, carpets, mats, blankets, and other useful belongings can also fall within its range.

The root occurs twice in the Qur’an, both times as أَثَاثًا (athāthan):

  • Al-Naḥl, 16:80: goods and articles made from livestock wool, soft hair, and hair.
  • Maryam, 19:74: the material resources and belongings of earlier generations, alongside their outward appearance and social standing.

In the first verse, athāth is a blessing obtained from natural materials. In the second, superior material provision appears alongside an impressive appearance and social position, but the Qur’an shows that these alone establish neither the rightness of one’s path nor immunity from destruction.

The two usages complement each other. Goods and possessions can be useful blessings, while their abundance is no certain measure of their owner’s moral worth or truthfulness.

Forms recorded in Arabic

A TH TH is a doubled root: its final two consonants are identical. In أَثَّ (aththa), the two thāʾ consonants are expressed through doubling.

Important forms in this family include:

  • أَثَّ (aththa): became plentiful or dense, or its parts intertwined.
  • يَأَثُّ (yaʾaththu), يَئِثُّ (yaʾiththu), and يَؤُثُّ (yaʾuththu): imperfect forms recorded in lexical sources.
  • أَثّ (athth) and أَثَاثَة (athāthah): abundance and density, especially of hair and vegetation.
  • أَثِيث (athīth): thick, abundant, or dense, as with long, thick hair or intertwined vegetation.
  • أَثِيثَة (athīthah): the feminine of athīth.
  • أَثَائِث (athāʾith) and أَثَايِث (athāyith): recorded plurals of athīth and athīthah in some uses.
  • أَثَاث (athāth): household and everyday goods; more broadly, accumulated property and possessions.
  • أَثَّثَ (aththatha): prepared or made something comfortable with furnishings, coverings, or soft padding.
  • تَأَثَّثَ (taʾaththatha): acquired household goods, property, or material means.

These verbal and adjectival forms do not occur in the Qur’an. Only athāthan appears, in the two verses.

Lexicographers also disagree about the singular of athāth. Al-Farrāʾ regards it as a collective expression with no known singular from the same letters; others give أَثَاثَة (athāthah) as its singular.

Athāthah should therefore not be presented without qualification as an undisputed singular.

2. Density and abundance in early Arabic

Thick hair and dense vegetation

A clear use of A TH TH describes hair and vegetation that are plentiful and intertwined.

Arabic uses شَعْرٌ أَثِيثٌ (shaʿrun athīthun) for thick, abundant hair.

Likewise, nabātun athīthun describes vegetation growing plentifully, with its parts close together or intertwined.

These usages concern not only quantity but also density.

Plants scattered far apart across the ground present a different image from vegetation growing closely together and intertwining.

Thickness and density are therefore important features of athīth.

This does not mean that every derivative in A TH TH must refer to something living or growing.

Household goods can be athāth without growing like hair or plants.

A pre-Islamic witness from Imruʾ al-Qays

The Muʿallaqah of Imruʾ al-Qays contains this verse:

وَفَرْعٍ يَزِينُ الْمَتْنَ أَسْوَدَ فَاحِمٍ
أَثِيثٍ كَقِنْوِ النَّخْلَةِ الْمُتَعَثْكِلِ

wa-farʿin yazīnu al-matna aswada fāḥimin / athīthin ka-qinwi al-nakhlati al-mutaʿathkili

“And long hair adorning the back, black and coal-dark, thick like a date-palm cluster with its interwoven fruit-bearing strands.”

Athīth describes hair here.

The poet compares long, thick hair to a date cluster whose many strands and fruits lie together.

This witness matters because it shows abundance and density in pre-Islamic poetry, independently of the Qur’anic use of athāth.

The distinction between the two usages must nevertheless be preserved.

Athīth describes something thick and dense; Qur’anic athāth names goods and possessions.

A semantic relationship can be recognized between density and assembling the goods of daily life, but “very abundant” should not be inserted into every translation of athāth.

Making something soft and ready for use

Lexical sources also record أَثَّثَ الشَّيْءَ (aththatha al-shayʾa) for preparing or softening something, especially by furnishing it with a carpet, covering, or soft layer.

This has an understandable connection with everyday goods, mats, and bedding.

A sleeping place, for example, can be prepared with coverings and blankets to make sitting or sleeping more comfortable.

Softness is not, however, an essential feature of every item of athāth.

Household goods can be made from wood, stone, metal, fabric, or other materials, and need not all be soft.

Athāth should therefore not be confined to “soft articles” or “bedding.”

Is abundance the root’s original meaning?

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies a principal field for the family involving gathering and softness.

He relates abundant vegetation, thick hair, and assembled household goods within this field.

In Al-Mufradāt, Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī likewise explains athāth as abundant household goods, connecting it with quantity and density.

Discussing Al-Naḥl 16:80, Al-Ṭabarī also suggests that goods gathering together and becoming plentiful may explain the name athāth, just as thick hair is called athīth.

These are defensible accounts of a semantic connection.

Recorded meaning should nevertheless be distinguished from a certain history of naming.

Athāth and athīth clearly belong to one lexical family and relate to abundance. That relationship alone does not establish exactly when the name for household goods arose, or which particular use produced it.

For Forqan, this is best treated as a lexical explanation rather than a proven historical derivation.

3. Athāth: household goods or all possessions?

Early Arabic athāth had a wider range than the familiar modern sense of furniture.

Household goods and furnishings

One principal meaning of athāth is the goods assembled in a home for daily use.

Carpets, mats, blankets, clothing, and comparable articles fall within this field.

Early explanations define athāth as مَتَاعُ الْبَيْتِ (matāʿ al-bayt), household goods.

This matters especially in Al-Naḥl 16:80, which mentions livestock wool, soft hair, and hair.

These materials can be used to make clothing, carpets, mats, coverings, and other necessities.

Athāth in that verse therefore means useful goods made from natural materials, rather than ornamental objects alone.

Accumulated property and possessions

The early lexicographer Abū Zayd al-Anṣārī also explains athāth more broadly as a person’s entire property.

In that use, property can include livestock, goods, and other belongings.

Early sources name camels, sheep, and enslaved people among the categories of property recognized in that historical setting.

This records the word’s historical and social usage; it does not morally endorse slavery or human ownership of other human beings.

The broader usage also prevents translating athāth simply as “furniture” in every verse.

In Maryam 19:74, athāth concerns the material provision and outward display of earlier generations. “Goods and possessions” is more appropriate there than “furniture.”

Does athāth always mean abundant wealth?

Some sources explain athāth as abundant property.

This relates to the family’s field of abundance and density.

It should not make a very large quantity a necessary condition in every use.

Al-Naḥl 16:80 concerns producing goods from materials obtained from livestock. It specifies neither their number nor their exact monetary value.

In Maryam 19:74, the comparative construction أَحْسَنُ أَثَاثًا (aḥsanu athāthan) expresses superiority in goods and possessions. Greater quantity is one lexical explanation of that material advantage, not the necessary meaning of aḥsan itself.

Athāth itself can therefore denote goods and possessions, while their quantity or quality emerges from context.

4. “Goods and provision”: useful articles from livestock fibers

Al-Naḥl, 16:80

وَاللَّهُ جَعَلَ لَكُمْ مِنْ بُيُوتِكُمْ سَكَنًا وَجَعَلَ لَكُمْ مِنْ جُلُودِ الْأَنْعَامِ بُيُوتًا تَسْتَخِفُّونَهَا يَوْمَ ظَعْنِكُمْ وَيَوْمَ إِقَامَتِكُمْ ۙ وَمِنْ أَصْوَافِهَا وَأَوْبَارِهَا وَأَشْعَارِهَا أَثَاثًا وَمَتَاعًا إِلَىٰ حِينٍ

wa-allāhu jaʿala lakum min buyūtikum sakanan wa-jaʿala lakum min julūdi al-anʿāmi buyūtan tastakhiffūnahā yawma ẓaʿnikum wa-yawma iqāmatikum; wa-min aṣwāfihā wa-awbārihā wa-ashʿārihā athāthan wa-matāʿan ilā ḥīn

“The God has made your houses places of rest for you, and from livestock hides He has made dwellings you find light to carry on the day of your departure and the day of your stay. And from their wool, soft hair, and hair He has provided household goods and articles for use for a time.”

This is the first Qur’anic occurrence of athāth.

The God here recalls the blessing of shelter and the means of daily life.

The verse first mentions houses in which people find rest.

It then mentions portable dwellings, such as tents and coverings made from livestock hides.

It proceeds to wool, soft hair, and hair: materials from which people can make useful goods.

Three materials, three different words

The verse places أَصْوَاف (aṣwāf), أَوْبَار (awbār), and أَشْعَار (ashʿār) together.

These words belong to different lexical families.

Aṣwāf is the plural of ṣūf and is familiarly associated with sheep’s wool.

Awbār is the plural of wabar, used for the soft hair or hairy covering of certain animals, especially camels.

Ashʿār is the plural of shaʿr and here concerns livestock hair, including that of goats.

This classification should not be made absolute, as though only one animal could produce each material.

The verse names three groups of useful animal materials employed in making everyday goods.

Athāth denotes the articles produced from those materials.

It does not mean the wool, soft hair, or hair itself, but the goods that can be made from them.

Athāth and matāʿ: do they mean the same thing?

The verse coordinates أَثَاثًا وَمَتَاعًا (athāthan wa-matāʿan).

The concepts are close, but their occurrence together does not justify treating them as complete synonyms without examining their use.

Athāth emphasizes the assembled goods and belongings themselves.

Matāʿ has a broader Arabic range and can denote something used or enjoyed, including for a period of time.

A particular item can therefore be athāth and also serve as matāʿ.

A mat, for example, is an article among one’s belongings, and a person makes use of it.

Both words can apply to the same thing from different perspectives.

They should not be divided by an artificial rule such as athāth always meaning fixed furnishings and matāʿ always meaning portable articles.

This very verse concerns life during travel and residence, and articles made from animal fibers can be portable.

The more defensible distinction is that athāth emphasizes assembled goods and possessions, while matāʿ emphasizes their use and benefit, with overlap between the two words.

Ilā ḥīn: use for a time

The verse ends with إِلَىٰ حِينٍ (ilā ḥīn).

This places human enjoyment within a temporal limit.

Early explanations sometimes relate ḥīn to the end of a person’s life, and sometimes to the time when the goods wear out.

Both are understandable in ordinary experience.

A person may die before their possessions perish, or their belongings may wear out before their own life ends.

Ḥīn itself does not name an exact date or duration here.

“For a time” is therefore suitable; the expression should not be restricted without evidence to either “until death” or “until the goods wear out.”

It also shows that material enjoyment in this world is neither permanent nor unlimited.

That limitation does not make everyday goods worthless. The verse presents them among the useful blessings of The God.

Does athāth mean only luxury and comfort?

In Al-Naḥl 16:80, athāth is made from natural materials and serves everyday needs.

It should therefore not be treated solely as a sign of luxury or aristocratic living.

Clothing, carpets, coverings, and other simple goods can also fall within its range.

Nor does the verse confine itself to individual use. Articles made from wool, soft hair, and hair can have economic value and be traded.

Commerce and monetary value are not independent meanings of athāth; they are ways of making use of goods.

The central concern here is the usefulness of the goods and material means provided for human life.

5. “Better in goods and appearance”: material provision does not establish truth

Maryam, 19:73

وَإِذَا تُتْلَىٰ عَلَيْهِمْ آيَاتُنَا بَيِّنَاتٍ قَالَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا لِلَّذِينَ آمَنُوا أَيُّ الْفَرِيقَيْنِ خَيْرٌ مَقَامًا وَأَحْسَنُ نَدِيًّا

wa-idhā tutlā ʿalayhim āyātunā bayyinātin qāla alladhīna kafarū li-alladhīna āmanū ayyu al-farīqayni khayrun maqāman wa-aḥsanu nadiyyā

“When Our clear signs are recited to them, those who disbelieve say to those who believe, ‘Which of the two groups has a better dwelling place and a finer assembly?’”

Maryam, 19:74

وَكَمْ أَهْلَكْنَا قَبْلَهُمْ مِنْ قَرْنٍ هُمْ أَحْسَنُ أَثَاثًا وَرِئْيًا

wa-kam ahlaknā qablahum min qarnin hum aḥsanu athāthan wa-riʾyā

“How many generations before them We destroyed who were finer in goods, possessions, and outward appearance.”

This is the second Qur’anic occurrence of athāth.

The preceding verse is essential to understanding its role.

Claiming superiority through social position

In Maryam 19:73, those who disbelieve respond to the clear signs of The God by appealing to their material and social situation.

They speak of their dwelling place and assembly.

Their question is not simply about having a home or sitting in a gathering; they compare the two groups’ positions to assert their own superiority.

Material and social advantage becomes a means of judging whose position is right.

The following verse challenges that argument.

The Qur’an’s response: better-provided predecessors were also destroyed

Maryam 19:74 recalls earlier generations with better goods and outward appearance who were nevertheless destroyed.

The comparison shows that abundant resources and an impressive appearance guarantee neither the rightness of one’s path nor protection from the consequences of one’s actions.

A community may possess extensive property, grand houses, and splendid assemblies while its conduct and beliefs involve injustice or denial.

Material provision alone cannot therefore be the final measure of moral and religious judgment.

The verse nevertheless does not condemn possessing goods itself.

Al-Naḥl 16:80 presented athāth as a blessing of life.

In Maryam 19:74, the issue is misusing material provision as evidence of superiority and truth.

The verses are therefore compatible. One describes the usefulness of everyday goods; the other shows that better goods do not prove the rightness of a person’s position.

Aḥsanu athāthan: better or more?

Aḥsan has a comparative construction in the verse.

Athāthan is an accusative specification, or tamyīz, identifying the respect in which the comparison is made.

The phrase does more than say that they had goods: it describes a better situation in goods and possessions. Greater quantity can be one expression of that advantage, but the comparative construction does not itself specify the number of objects.

Early explanations interpret the phrase both as more goods and property and as finer furnishings.

Quantity and quality can coincide, but they are distinct.

Someone may own many articles that are old or of little value; someone else may have fewer goods of greater quality and beauty.

Aḥsanu athāthan should therefore not be confined to a greater number of objects.

The context concerns the visible material advantages of earlier generations.

Athāth and riʾy

The phrase أَحْسَنُ أَثَاثًا وَرِئْيًا (aḥsanu athāthan wa-riʾyan) places two words together.

Athāth concerns goods and possessions.

In the reading with hamzah, رِئْي (riʾy) belongs to the family of seeing and concerns what meets the eye, outward appearance, and visible display.

This distinction matters.

A person may have considerable property without a particularly impressive appearance or social display.

Another may create a striking appearance with limited resources.

The verse addresses both material goods and outward appearance.

Athāth alone should therefore not be translated as “outward beauty,” since the second word expresses that field more directly.

Reading variants of wa-riʾyan

There are recognized differences in reading the second word.

In the reading وَرِئْيًا (wa-riʾyan), including Ḥafṣ’s transmission from ʿĀṣim, visible appearance and what is seen are prominent.

Qālūn from Nāfiʿ, Ibn Dhakwān from Ibn ʿĀmir, and Abū Jaʿfar read وَرِيًّا (wa-riyyan), with a doubled yāʾ and no hamzah. Two lexical analyses are transmitted: changing hamzah to yāʾ and assimilating the two yāʾs, or a connection with riyy and the image of being well-watered, flourishing, and enjoying abundance.

Al-Qurṭubī also reports وَزِيًّا (wa-ziyyan), with zāy, from Ibn ʿAbbās and others, relating it to appearance and beauty. This report should not be presented without qualification as having the same standing as the two preceding forms in the ten readings.

These differences do not belong to A TH TH itself.

Athāth continues to mean goods and possessions here.

They require detailed study under R ʾ Y or in an examination of the readings of Maryam 19:74, rather than incorporation into the lexical definition of athāth.

Does wealth prove that one’s path is wrong?

Maryam 19:74 does not establish that every wealthy or well-provided person is in the wrong according to the Qur’an.

That is not the verse’s argument.

It shows that earlier people had better goods and appearance, yet their advantages did not prevent their destruction.

Thus neither wealth nor an impressive appearance proves that a position is right or, by itself, that it is wrong.

Judgment concerns conduct, belief, responsibility, and other criteria stated throughout the Qur’an.

This matters in translation: defining athāth inherently as “deceptive luxury” or “blameworthy wealth” would overlook its positive use in Al-Naḥl 16:80.

6. Neighboring words and roots

Athāth (A TH TH) and matāʿ (M T ʿ): assembled goods and their use

Study of M T ʿ

These words occur together in Al-Naḥl 16:80.

Athāth concerns everyday goods and, more broadly, accumulated possessions.

Matāʿ denotes something people use and benefit from.

An article can be both athāth and matāʿ.

Their emphasis differs: one concerns assembled things, the other their use and enjoyment.

Matāʿ should nevertheless not always be regarded as something of little value or merely temporary. Those features depend on context and accompanying qualifications.

In Al-Naḥl 16:80, ilā ḥīn supplies the temporal limitation.

Athāth (A TH TH) and māl (M W L): everyday goods and property

Study of M W L

Arabic māl encompasses a broad range of property.

Athāth can form part of it.

Early usage sometimes also applies athāth to all one’s property, so the words overlap.

Not all property, however, consists of household goods.

Land, livestock, food stores, and other possessions can also be māl.

“Goods and possessions” suits athāth in Maryam 19:74, while “household and everyday goods” is more precise in Al-Naḥl 16:80.

Athāth (A TH TH) and rīsh (R Y SH): material means and adornment

Study of R Y SH

Alongside birds’ feathers, Arabic rīsh can concern adornment, clothing, and material provision.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:26 places لِبَاسًا (libāsan) and رِيشًا (rīshan) together.

Here rīsh concerns human clothing and adornment.

Athāth can also include clothing and goods that make life more comfortable and attractive.

The two words should not be equated in every use.

Rīsh fundamentally denotes feathers, while athāth concerns assembled goods and possessions.

Clothing may also be both a necessity and an ornament; the two functions need not be separate.

Athāth (A TH TH) and zukhruf (Z KH R F): everyday goods and ornament

Study of Z KH R F

Arabic zukhruf concerns ornament and decoration, and can also denote gold and glittering display.

Athāth may be beautiful, but ornament is not essential to its meaning.

A plain blanket or ordinary mat is also among one’s everyday goods without being luxurious.

In Maryam 19:74, athāth accompanies outward appearance, so both material provision and adornment may be involved.

That association should not turn zukhruf into a general equivalent for athāth.

Athīth (A TH TH) and kathrah (K TH R): density and abundance

Study of K TH R

Kathrah has the broad Arabic meaning of abundance.

Athīth likewise concerns abundance and thickness when applied to hair and vegetation.

Not everything plentiful is athīth.

Many scattered objects can be numerous without creating an image of thickness or density.

Athīth emphasizes density and closeness between the parts.

This helps explain its semantic relationship with athāth, but athīth itself does not occur in the Qur’an.

Athāth (A TH TH) and firāsh (F R SH): a collection of goods and bedding

Study of F R SH

F R SH concerns spreading out and can be used for mats and bedding.

A floor covering may be one part of a home’s athāth.

Athāth is not restricted to floor coverings.

Clothing, blankets, and other articles can also form part of it.

Athāthan in Al-Naḥl 16:80 should therefore not be translated only as “carpets.”

Athāth (A TH TH) and riʾy (R ʾ Y): possessions and appearance

Study of R ʾ Y

Maryam 19:74 places athāth and riʾy together.

Athāth refers to goods and possessions.

In the reading with hamzah, riʾy concerns outward appearance and what is seen.

The concepts are related, since possessions can affect the appearance of a person’s life.

They are nevertheless distinct.

Someone may possess considerable property without displaying it outwardly.

Conversely, someone may create an attractive appearance with limited means.

A translation should therefore preserve both aspects.

7. Translation choices for Forqan

The basic Arabic meaning of athāth is household and everyday goods. More broadly, it can include accumulated property and possessions.

The translation should therefore fit each verse’s context.

Al-Naḥl, 16:80

The relevant phrase:

وَمِنْ أَصْوَافِهَا وَأَوْبَارِهَا وَأَشْعَارِهَا أَثَاثًا وَمَتَاعًا إِلَىٰ حِينٍ

wa-min aṣwāfihā wa-awbārihā wa-ashʿārihā athāthan wa-matāʿan ilā ḥīn

“And from their wool, soft hair, and hair He has provided household goods and articles for use for a time.”

For athāth here, “household and everyday goods” is suggested.

This includes carpets, clothing, blankets, and other articles made from animal fibers.

“Furniture” is too narrow to cover all the products concerned.

“Abundant property” is too general here, since the verse names particular materials and the articles made from them.

Maryam, 19:74

The verse:

وَكَمْ أَهْلَكْنَا قَبْلَهُمْ مِنْ قَرْنٍ هُمْ أَحْسَنُ أَثَاثًا وَرِئْيًا

wa-kam ahlaknā qablahum min qarnin hum aḥsanu athāthan wa-riʾyā

“How many generations before them We destroyed who were finer in goods, possessions, and outward appearance.”

For athāth here, “goods and possessions” is suggested.

This is broader than household furnishings and fits the comparison of the groups’ material provision.

For a smoother translation, one could say:

“How many generations before them We destroyed who had greater possessions and a finer appearance.”

This moves aḥsan somewhat toward “more.” A research discussion should therefore clarify that the verse concerns superiority in goods and provision, potentially involving both quantity and quality.

Brief meanings for word-by-word boxes

أَثَاثًا (athāthan), Al-Naḥl 16:80: household and everyday goods, including carpets, clothing, and other articles made from wool, soft hair, and hair.

أَثَاثًا (athāthan), Maryam 19:74: goods and possessions; the material belongings and resources expressing a person’s or group’s provision.

The root explanation can also note the connection with athīth, “thick” or “dense.”

That connection should not turn athāth into “density,” “thickness,” or “very abundant property” in both verses.

8. Conclusion

The Arabic root A TH TH concerns abundance, density, and things gathering together.

Athīth describes thick hair and dense vegetation. Its use in Imruʾ al-Qays’s poetry attests this field in pre-Islamic Arabic.

The same family records athāth for everyday goods and, more broadly, accumulated property and possessions.

Lexical explanations recognize a clear relationship between density and assembled goods, but that relationship alone is not a proven history of the word’s naming.

Athāth occurs twice in the Qur’an.

In Al-Naḥl 16:80, everyday goods are among the blessings people obtain from livestock wool, soft hair, and hair. They can be necessary and useful articles, rather than luxuries alone.

In Maryam 19:74, athāth concerns the material provision and impressive appearance of earlier generations. The Qur’an recalls that people with better goods and possessions were also destroyed. Greater resources alone therefore prove neither the rightness of a person’s path nor their ultimate outcome.

The uses are compatible. Goods and property can be valuable blessings without becoming certain measures of human moral worth or truth.

Athāth should also remain distinct from matāʿ, māl, rīsh, zukhruf, and riʾy. They overlap in some settings but emphasize different aspects of possessions, use, and adornment.

For Forqan, the basic definition is: Athāth is the assembled goods of daily life; more broadly, it can encompass a person’s property and possessions. It relates to the lexical field of abundance and gathering, but neither great quantity nor luxury is a necessary condition of its meaning.

“Household and everyday goods” in Al-Naḥl 16:80 and “goods and possessions” in Maryam 19:74 are preferable equivalents.

This definition preserves both Qur’anic uses without restricting athāth to modern furniture or making abundant possessions inherently good or bad.
