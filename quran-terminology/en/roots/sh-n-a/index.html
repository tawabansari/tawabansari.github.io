Enmity, hatred and aversion toward another: from early Arabic usage to the command to treat enemies justly

The Arabic root SH N ʾ concerns hatred, aversion and strong dislike of someone or something. Shaniʾa describes a person’s hostility or aversion toward another; shāniʾ denotes the person who bears that hatred.

Shanaʾān names this state of hostility and aversion between people or groups. It may arise from disagreement, rivalry, earlier hostility, harmful conduct or other causes, but none of these causes is an essential part of the root’s meaning.

Disliking something and hating a particular person are distinguishable. A person may abhor misconduct without personal hostility toward its perpetrator. Conversely, disagreement or resentment may lead someone to hate a person who is in the right on a particular issue.

The Quran uses two forms of this root: shanaʾān and shāniʾ. In two verses of Al-Māʾidah, shanaʾān is hatred that must not lead to aggression or injustice. In Al-Kawthar, shāniʾ denotes someone who hates Muḥammad.

A key distinction in these Quranic uses is between the feeling of hostility and conduct arising from it. Hatred may exist within a person, but it does not authorize distorting the truth, violating others’ rights or exceeding the limits of justice.

1. The root in early Arabic

Hating and feeling aversion

Classical Arabic records shaniʾtuhu as “I hated him” or “I felt aversion toward him.” Verbal nouns include shanʾ, shinʾ, shanaʾān and shanʾān within the same semantic field.

Shanaʾa, with a fatḥah on the nūn, is also recorded, though shaniʾa is among the principal attested verb forms.

Shāniʾ is an active participle denoting someone who hates another.

Mashnūʾ is a passive participle describing someone who is hated or regarded with aversion.

These forms must be distinguished: shāniʾ is the person who hates; mashnūʾ is the person who is hated.

The central meaning concerns a negative relation between a person and the object of hatred. The word alone does not determine when the hostility began, its cause, how long it will last or which party is right.

Shanūʾah: aversion and keeping away

Shanūʾah is also recorded for aversion and keeping away from something regarded as unpleasant or impure.

This use is not restricted to active hostility against a person: it includes repugnance and avoidance of an unwanted object.

For example, someone may recoil from something dirty or deeply unpleasant and wish to keep away. This differs from personal enmity, though both involve rejection and distance from what is disliked.

Azd Shanūʾah is also the traditional name of a known branch of the Arab tribes, traced in genealogical accounts to the period before Islam.

Different explanations of the name have been transmitted. Some connect it with hostility between groups; others with keeping clean and avoiding impurity.

The tribal name alone does not establish any one explanation as its certain historical origin. A proper name may survive while the initial reason for naming remains unclear.

Linguistic witnesses and the limits of historical proof

Early lexical works cite a verse by al-Aḥwaṣ, an Umayyad poet, for shanaʾān:

وَمَا الْعَيْشُ إِلَّا مَا تَلَذُّ وَتَشْتَهِي

wa-mā al-ʿayshu illā mā taladhdhu wa-tashtahī

وَإِنْ لَامَ فِيهِ ذُو الشَّنَانِ وَفَنَّدَا

wa-in lāma fīhi dhū al-shanāni wa-fannadā

Translation:

“Life is only what you enjoy and desire, even if one who bears hatred blames and censures you for it.”

Dhū al-shanān here denotes someone who bears hostility or resentment and criticizes another from that position. Shanān is transmitted without the hamzah and is explained as a variant of shanaʾān; it is not a separate root.

The word thus occurs in a human relationship: someone hates another, and this hostility appears in judgment and speech.

Censure is not an essential meaning of shanaʾān. The poet describes a situation in which hatred accompanies blame.

Al-Aḥwaṣ is an Islamic-period poet, so this line is not evidence of pre-Islamic usage. Transmissions also differ in the verb of censure without changing the basic sense of dhū al-shanān.

This study has not established independently dated pre-Quranic witnesses for every form in this family. The old tribal name Azd Shanūʾah and the uses recorded in Arabic dictionaries help clarify the background and semantic field without dating the emergence of each meaning.

The Quran’s use of shanaʾān and shāniʾ clearly attests this family as vocabulary for human hatred and aversion in the Arabic of its revelation.

Does shanaʾān necessarily mean extremely intense hatred?

The root SH N ʾ expresses hatred and aversion in its Quranic uses; all three occurrences involve a clearly negative relationship between people or groups.

The word’s structure alone does not yield a fixed, measurable degree of hatred.

In some contexts hatred may be intense and accompanied by harm or violence. Elsewhere it may remain inward hostility or aversion.

“Hatred” and “enmity” are therefore appropriate equivalents, but descriptions such as “deadly hatred,” “endless hatred” or “irreconcilable resentment” require contextual evidence rather than automatic inclusion.

2. Core meaning and Quranic forms

The root occurs three times in three Quranic verses, in two forms:

Shanaʾān in Al-Māʾidah 5:2 and Al-Māʾidah 5:8, and shāniʾaka in Al-Kawthar 108:3.

Shanaʾān is a noun and verbal noun of hatred or aversion. It is nominative in both Al-Māʾidah verses, in the construction shanaʾānu qawmin.

Recognized readings include shanaʾān with a fatḥah on the nūn and shanʾān with a sukūn. The discussion here follows the vocalized form in the installed Quran text.

Lexical analyses commonly treat the first as a verbal noun of hatred. Both verbal-noun and descriptive analyses have been proposed for the second.

The variation does not change the basic semantic field of hatred and aversion. Grammatical analysis must nevertheless distinguish the state of hostility from the person who bears it.

Shāniʾaka combines the active participle shāniʾ with the attached pronoun -ka. In Al-Kawthar it is the accusative noun governed by inna.

The attached pronoun addresses Muḥammad and marks him as the object of the person’s hatred.

The verbs shaniʾa and yashnaʾu and other nouns such as mashnūʾ exist in Arabic but do not occur in the Quran.

The core meaning in Quranic usage may be stated as follows:

Hatred or aversion toward a particular person or group: a state of disliking another and bearing hostility or hatred toward them.

This defines the presence of hostility. Whether its cause is justified, its intensity and duration, and the conduct arising from it must be examined separately.

3. Shanaʾān in Al-Māʾidah 5:2: hatred must not become aggression

Al-Māʾidah 5:2 states:

وَلَا يَجْرِمَنَّكُمْ شَنَآنُ قَوْمٍ أَنْ صَدُّوكُمْ عَنِ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ أَنْ تَعْتَدُوا

wa-lā yajrimannakum shanaʾānu qawmin an ṣaddūkum ʿani al-masjidi al-ḥarāmi an taʿtadū

Al-Māʾidah 5:2

Translation:

“Do not let hatred of a people, because they barred you from the Sacred Mosque, drive you to transgress.”

This passage occurs among commands to respect the sacred rites, the sacred month, sacrificial offerings and those making their way to the Sacred House.

The context recalls the believers being barred from the Sacred Mosque. Such conduct can provoke anger, aversion and hostility.

Yet the Quran draws a boundary between that hatred and the believers’ subsequent conduct: hatred must not lead them to transgress.

Distinguishing hatred from transgression

Shanaʾān names hatred; taʿtadū concerns transgressing.

They do not mean the same thing.

A person may be angry at a group’s conduct and hostile toward them. Deciding how to act toward them is a further step.

For example, if a group previously obstructed others’ movement or legitimate access to a place, those harmed may develop hostility toward them.

That hostility does not entitle them to attack people’s lives, property or rights unjustly.

The verse gives a clear command governing conduct, beyond an exhortation to be kind to an enemy.

The meaning of lā yajrimannakum

Yajrimannakum belongs to J R M. In this construction it concerns driving or impelling someone to act.

The statement therefore warns against hatred driving people to aggression, rather than merely saying “do not feel hatred.”

This distinction matters.

Anger and hatred can influence decisions, but the influence of a feeling does not authorize every action arising from it.

Nor does the verse prohibit every form of defense against an enemy. Legitimate defense and aggression differ. Al-Baqarah 2:190 likewise prohibits transgression alongside the command to fight those who fight.

Al-Māʾidah 5:2 does not require passivity in the face of every harm; it prevents hatred from erasing the boundary between right and transgression.

Hatred of a people and individual responsibility

The verse uses shanaʾānu qawmin: hatred associated with a group.

This must not become permission to condemn every member of a people, religion, family or community.

Particular members of a group may have committed wrongdoing. Their responsibility does not transfer without independent grounds to everyone sharing a name or affiliation.

Individual responsibility is also emphasized in verses such as Al-Anʿām 6:164.

Displeasure with some members of a group cannot justify violating the rights of innocent or unrelated people.

Cooperation in goodness, not aggression

Al-Māʾidah 5:2 continues:

وَتَعَاوَنُوا عَلَى الْبِرِّ وَالتَّقْوَىٰ وَلَا تَعَاوَنُوا عَلَى الْإِثْمِ وَالْعُدْوَانِ

wa-taʿāwanū ʿalā al-birri wa-l-taqwā wa-lā taʿāwanū ʿalā al-ithmi wa-l-ʿudwān

Al-Māʾidah 5:2

Translation:

“Cooperate in goodness and taqwā, and do not cooperate in sin and aggression.”

This continuation clarifies the standard for collective action.

Hatred of a group does not legitimize cooperation in oppressing it. Belonging to a favored group likewise does not make cooperation in its wrongdoing right.

The verse’s standard is goodness, taqwā and avoidance of transgression, not friendship or enmity alone.

4. Shanaʾān in Al-Māʾidah 5:8: justice even toward an enemy

Al-Māʾidah 5:8 states:

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا كُونُوا قَوَّامِينَ لِلَّهِ شُهَدَاءَ بِالْقِسْطِ وَلَا يَجْرِمَنَّكُمْ شَنَآنُ قَوْمٍ عَلَىٰ أَلَّا تَعْدِلُوا اعْدِلُوا هُوَ أَقْرَبُ لِلتَّقْوَىٰ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū kūnū qawwāmīna li-llāhi shuhadāʾa bi-l-qisṭi wa-lā yajrimannakum shanaʾānu qawmin ʿalā allā taʿdilū iʿdilū huwa aqrabu li-l-taqwā

Al-Māʾidah 5:8

Translation:

“You who have believed, stand firmly for The God as witnesses with equity. Do not let hatred of a people cause you to act unjustly. Be just; that is nearer to taqwā.”

This verse also uses shanaʾānu qawmin, but the prohibited conduct differs from Al-Māʾidah 5:2.

Al-Māʾidah 5:2 warns against aggression; Al-Māʾidah 5:8 warns against failing to act justly.

Hatred can thus have different consequences, from overt aggression to distorted testimony or judgment.

Justice is independent of affection and hostility

A person may love someone who is wrong in a particular dispute.

They may also hate someone who is right on a particular issue.

When affection and hostility determine what counts as right, partiality replaces justice.

Suppose two people dispute ownership of property. A judge or witness may like one and hate the other.

Those feelings do not change the facts of ownership.

If documents and evidence support the disliked person, hostility does not justify disregarding them.

Al-Māʾidah 5:8 establishes this boundary: hostility toward people must not distort judgment about their rights.

Justice differs from moral indifference

Acting justly toward an enemy does not mean approving all their conduct.

An enemy may actually have committed oppression. Justice requires establishing it through reliable evidence and assigning responsibility according to the conduct actually committed.

Justice does not permit lying about a wrongdoer, fabricating unsupported charges or attributing a crime they did not commit in order to condemn them.

Justice toward an enemy means remaining faithful to what is right even amid hostility; it does not abandon the pursuit of rights.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:135 likewise commands believers to uphold equity and truthful testimony, even against themselves and those close to them.

The two verses complement one another: affection must not divert people from truth, and hatred must not lead them to violate another’s rights.

No transgression, even against enemies

Study of ʿ D L

Does shanaʾān mean mutual hostility here?

Shanaʾānu qawmin can concern hatred of a group or hostility associated with it.

Grammatically, the genitive construction does not by itself settle every detail about who initiated the hostility. In Al-Māʾidah 5:2, the obstruction of access to the Sacred Mosque supplies a more specific background.

Al-Māʾidah 5:8 focuses on the effect of hostility on the addressees’ conduct.

The phrase alone therefore does not prove equal hostility on both sides or identify one side as necessarily its initiator.

The clear command is that hostility toward a group must not prevent the addressees from acting justly.

Hatred and taqwā

Within this passage, the verse commands:

اعْدِلُوا هُوَ أَقْرَبُ لِلتَّقْوَىٰ

iʿdilū huwa aqrabu li-l-taqwā

Al-Māʾidah 5:8

“Be just; that is nearer to taqwā.”

Taqwā is thus not confined to inward feelings or individual rites: conduct toward people one dislikes also matters.

Someone may be kind and fair to friends while setting aside those principles when facing an opponent.

Such justice depends on personal affection rather than commitment to a stable standard.

The Quranic command breaks that dependence. Even where hostility exists, what is right must be recognized on its merits.

5. Shāniʾ: the person who hates

Al-Kawthar 108:3 states:

إِنَّ شَانِئَكَ هُوَ الْأَبْتَرُ

inna shāniʾaka huwa al-abtar

Al-Kawthar 108:3

Translation:

“Surely the one who hates you is himself cut off.” “Without a lasting outcome” is an explanatory rendering here; the intended kind of severance requires a separate study of abtar and the surah’s context.

Shāniʾaka is an active participle from SH N ʾ denoting someone who hates Muḥammad.

Unlike the two Al-Māʾidah occurrences, this word refers to the person to whom hatred is attributed, rather than the state of hatred itself.

The distinction between verbal noun and active participle must be retained.

Shanaʾān is hatred; shāniʾ is the person who hates.

The relation between shāniʾ and abtar

Al-Kawthar has three verses. Al-Kawthar 108:1 speaks of the gift of al-kawthar to Muḥammad; Al-Kawthar 108:2 commands him to perform ṣalāh for his Lord and to perform naḥr; Al-Kawthar 108:3 identifies the one who hates him as al-abtar.

Abtar belongs to B T R and concerns being severed or cut off.

Arabic uses the description for something with a part cut off and, in human contexts, someone deprived of continuation or extension.

Historical and exegetical reports sometimes connect the verse with mockery of Muḥammad following the loss of his sons, or with the hostility of particular individuals.

Shāniʾ itself does not name a specific person, and the surah does not give the enemy’s name.

The word alone therefore cannot establish the historical identity of a particular individual.

Nor should abtar be restricted to having no son without examining the root and context. Severance can also concern a person’s eventual standing and its continuation.

The surah highlights the contrast between the divine gift to Muḥammad and the outcome for the person who hates him.

Is every opponent a shāniʾ?

Shāniʾ denotes someone who bears hatred or aversion.

Disagreeing with a statement or interpretation does not automatically make someone a shāniʾ.

A person may doubt a claim, ask for evidence or offer another understanding based on their knowledge. These situations do not themselves prove hatred.

Opposition may also be accompanied by insults, harm or overt hostility. In that case, the description of hatred can be examined on the evidence.

Al-Kawthar 108:3 speaks of someone who hates Muḥammad; it does not classify everyone who differed with him on any matter under this description.

Applying shāniʾ to condemn every critic or questioner extends the term beyond what its lexical meaning supports.

6. Distinctions from related roots

SH N ʾ and B GH Ḍ

Bughḍ concerns dislike, hatred or aversion.

Shanaʾān belongs to the same field and concerns human hostility and aversion in its Quranic uses.

The roots may have similar meanings in many sentences, but semantic proximity does not make all their uses identical.

Bughḍ may describe dislike of a person or thing. In the Quranic passages examined here, shanaʾān is hostility that can affect relationships and conduct among people or groups.

This is a tendency of usage, not an absolute rule that one is always mild and the other always intense.

Al-Māʾidah 5:14 and Al-Māʾidah 5:64 place al-ʿadāwah and al-baghḍāʾ together. This pairing shows the Quran’s use of different words for hostility and hatred, each to be understood in context.

SH N ʾ and ʿ D W

ʿAdāwah belongs to ʿ D W and denotes enmity or hostility in human relationships.

Shanaʾān focuses on the state of hating or feeling aversion toward a person or group.

ʿAdāwah can appear in hostile relationships and conduct, but it need not always become physical action or war.

Al-Māʾidah 5:2 distinguishes hostility from aggression. The former may lead to the latter, yet they do not have identical meanings.

SH N ʾ and K R H

K R H concerns disliking or finding something unwelcome.

A person may dislike an action without regarding its performer as an enemy. They may also dislike difficult circumstances without directing that feeling at a person.

The Quranic uses of shanaʾān concern hostility and aversion toward people or groups.

Karāhah can therefore cover a wider range of objects and intensities and need not lead to interpersonal hostility.

For example, disliking a difficult task does not establish that someone is hostile toward another person.

SH N ʾ and GH Y Ẓ

Ghayẓ concerns anger and intense inward agitation.

Shanaʾān is hatred or aversion toward a person or group.

The states may coexist but are not identical.

Someone may become intensely angry at another’s conduct, then calm down after clarification without retaining lasting hostility.

Conversely, a person may hate another for years without visibly expressing anger at every moment.

Āl ʿImrān 3:134 places restraining anger alongside pardoning people. This concerns controlling harmful reactions, but ghayẓ should not be equated with shanaʾān.

SH N ʾ and SH M T

Shamātah belongs to SH M T and denotes pleasure at another’s misfortune or failure.

Shanaʾān belongs to SH N ʾ and expresses hatred or aversion.

Someone may hate another and rejoice in their defeat or distress. Both states then occur together.

But hatred does not necessarily entail delight in an enemy’s misfortune.

For example, a person may hate someone for earlier oppression yet take no pleasure in their child’s illness or the suffering of innocent relatives.

Shanaʾān names hatred; shamātah concerns a particular kind of pleasure.

Similarity of letters should not cause the meanings of the two roots to be confused.

SH N ʾ and SH ʾ N

Shaʾn belongs to the separate root SH ʾ N and concerns an affair, matter, situation or undertaking.

Shanaʾān belongs to SH N ʾ and refers to hatred and aversion.

The roots share letters in a different order and have different meanings. Shāniʾ should not be linked derivationally to shaʾn on the basis of visual similarity.

7. Questions of understanding and social application

Hatred does not alter the truth

A central implication of the two shanaʾān passages in Al-Māʾidah is that hostility must not replace standards of knowledge and judgment.

For example, after years of conflict, two people or groups may readily accept any damaging report about the other and reject every favorable one.

Hatred of someone does not prove an accusation against them true.

Affection likewise does not erase a person’s mistake or crime.

An allegation must therefore be assessed by distinguishing emotional relations with the person from the strength of the evidence about their conduct.

This especially matters for those empowered to judge or decide about others: judges, witnesses, administrators and anyone making decisions about people’s rights.

The greater the authority and consequences of a decision, the greater the significance of the risk that personal hostility becomes injustice.

Does hatred always arise from ignorance?

Hatred may arise from rumor, prejudice or misunderstanding. It may also follow actual injury inflicted by another’s oppressive conduct.

These situations should not be treated as identical.

Al-Māʾidah 5:2 places the obstruction of the believers’ access to the Sacred Mosque in the background of hostility. The hatred discussed is thus connected with a specific harmful act.

Even such hatred does not authorize aggression.

Defending justice does not require first describing all hostility as groundless or mistaken.

There may be a real reason for anger and hostility, but a reason for anger is not permission to oppress.

Condemning conduct without condemning identity

Hostility toward people’s conduct sometimes becomes hostility toward their name, ethnicity, religion or family.

For example, someone harmed by a particular member of a group may subsequently hold every member responsible.

Such generalization disregards people’s actual responsibility.

Al-Māʾidah 5:8 maintains the command of justice even toward a disliked group. Group affiliation cannot replace examination of the individual’s conduct.

If a member of an ethnic or religious group acts rightly, hostility toward that group cannot make the action wrong.

Justice here means assessing each person and action by the relevant standard, rather than by earlier affection or hatred.

Distinguishing frank criticism from hostility

Criticizing a statement, theory, law, conduct or performance does not necessarily mean hating its author.

A person may argue clearly and criticize someone’s conduct frankly without bearing personal resentment toward them.

Conversely, someone may speak gently while violating another’s rights out of hostility.

The force of someone’s tone alone does not establish shanaʾān.

Assessing hostility requires examining the subject, demonstrable intent, conduct and actual relationship between the parties.

This distinction allows frank criticism of oppression and corruption without treating every criticism as personal hostility or disguising every hostility as legitimate criticism.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

The main Persian equivalents for shanaʾān are “enmity,” “resentment” and “aversion.”

“Enmity” is the most natural choice in the two Al-Māʾidah verses, where relationships between people and groups and their effects on social conduct and justice are at issue.

“Resentment” may also fit, but its Persian counterpart kīnah often suggests accumulated, lasting displeasure. Since shanaʾān alone does not establish a particular duration, this should not be an automatic equivalent everywhere.

For shāniʾ, “one who bears hatred” and “one who hates” are lexically suitable.

“Enemy” may convey the meaning naturally, but “the one who hates you” is more precise when distinguishing it from mutual enmity or merely political opposition matters.

The proposed renderings are as follows. They do not change the installed verse translations:

Al-Māʾidah 5:2

“Do not let hatred of a people, because they barred you from the Sacred Mosque, drive you to transgress.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:8

“Do not let hatred of a people cause you to act unjustly. Be just; that is nearer to taqwā.”

Al-Kawthar 108:3

“Surely the one who hates you is himself cut off.”

In the first two passages, hatred must remain distinct from aggression and injustice. In the third, shāniʾ describes the one who hates, while abtar describes that person’s condition or outcome; the meaning of abtar must not be inserted into the lexical definition of shāniʾ.

Concluding synthesis

SH N ʾ concerns hating, feeling aversion and finding someone or something unwelcome. In human relationships, shanaʾān names hostility, and shāniʾ identifies the person to whom that hostility is attributed.

Arabic also records related uses of aversion and keeping away. Early witnesses and the historical name Azd Shanūʾah help clarify the background without establishing when every semantic branch arose.

Shanaʾān occurs in Al-Māʾidah 5:2 and Al-Māʾidah 5:8. In the first, hatred must not turn into aggression; in the second, it must not obstruct justice.

The verses distinguish feeling from action. Hostility toward a person or group does not permit lying, violating rights or exceeding the limits of justice.

In Al-Kawthar 108:3, shāniʾ describes someone who hates Muḥammad. The description establishes hatred without itself identifying the person, all their motives or the details of their outcome.

The central conclusion is that hatred, even when arising from real harm, does not alter truth and justice. People may condemn wrongdoing and pursue their rights, but cannot make hatred an independent criterion for deciding right and wrong.

The final definition in Quranic usage is therefore:

SH N ʾ denotes hatred or aversion toward a person or group. Shanaʾān names that state; shāniʾ is the person who hates another. The root identifies hostility itself, not its certain cause, its justification or every action that might arise from it.

For Forqan’s Persian translation, “enmity” for shanaʾān and “the one who hates you” for shāniʾaka provide clear equivalents.
