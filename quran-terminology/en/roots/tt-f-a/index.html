Going out and extinguishing: from putting out fire to quelling war and attempts to extinguish the light of The God

The Arabic root ṭ f ʾ concerns fire going out and the disappearance of its flame and light. طَفِئَتِ النَّارُ means “the fire went out,” while أَطْفَأَ النَّارَ means someone extinguished it. In the first construction, the fire ceases burning; in the second, an agent causes it to do so.

The family extends beyond extinguishing an actual fire. Putting out the fire of war, quelling hostility, and preventing turmoil from spreading are among its uses. War or enmity is pictured as a fire that has been kindled and can spread; extinguishing it means ending its blaze and preventing its continuation.

Ṭ f ʾ occurs in three Quranic verses. Once, The God extinguishes the fire of war; twice, people are described as wanting to extinguish His light with their mouths.

Despite their different objects, these uses share a semantic core: putting out a light or a blaze. The fire of war and divine light must nevertheless be distinguished. In one verse, fire pictures warfare and hostility; in the other two, light pictures guidance and truth that opponents seek to extinguish.

Wanting to extinguish something must also be distinguished from succeeding. The verse about warfare attributes extinguishing to The God; the verses about divine light first describe opponents’ intention, then announce the completion of His light.

All three Quranic occurrences are Form IV verbs: أَطْفَأَهَا in Al-Māʾidah 5:64, يُطْفِئُوا in Al-Tawbah 9:32, and لِيُطْفِئُوا in Al-Ṣaff 61:8.

1. Historical meaning and early Arabic usage

A fire whose flame disappears

Classical Arabic uses طَفِئَتِ النَّارُ تَطْفَأُ for a fire going out. The verbal nouns طَفْء and طُفُوء are also recorded for this meaning.

Early lexical definitions explain this with ذَهَبَ لَهَبُهَا: “its flame disappeared.”

This is a starting point for understanding the root. A fire previously blazing and producing light and heat ceases to flame.

Arabic distinguishes a fire without visible flame but with live embers from one whose flame and remaining ember heat have disappeared.

Some older explanations distinguish خامدة, with embers remaining, from هامدة and طافئة, whose embers have cooled. This is a recorded lexical distinction, not an absolute condition on every use of طُفُوء.

These distinctions help clarify vocabulary, but should not become absolute rules governing every Arabic use. In ordinary language, “going out” can accompany different degrees of reduced flame and heat.

The clear meaning of ṭ f ʾ is the ending of a blaze or the disappearance of its flame and light.

Going out and putting out

طَفِئَتِ النَّارُ and أَطْفَأَ الرَّجُلُ النَّارَ have different constructions.

In the first, the fire is the grammatical subject and its going out is described. The sentence supplies no separate explanation of what caused it.

In the second, the person is the subject and the fire the object. أَطْفَأَ indicates that the person caused the fire to go out.

The different roles of the agent and the affected thing therefore matter.

انْطَفَأَتِ النَّارُ is also recorded for a fire going out.

The simple forms طَفِئَ and يَطْفَأُ, and the form انْطَفَأَ, do not occur in the Quran. All three Quranic occurrences derive from Form IV أَطْفَأَ.

Thus every Quranic occurrence attributes extinguishing to an agent, whether The God or those seeking to extinguish His light.

The absence of the simple verb does not show that it was unknown in Arabic or that the Quran avoided it for a particular rhetorical purpose. It is an established Arabic form; these three Quranic passages use Form IV.

Figurative use for quelling hostility

In early Arabic, fire was more than a natural phenomenon: it also described war, discord, and hostility.

Conflict between groups could be compared to a fire kindled by enmity and spread by continuing violence.

Conversely, ending discord and quelling hostility could be expressed through إطفاء.

A verse attributed to Ziyād al-Ṭammāḥī states:

وَكَانَتْ بَيْنَ آلِ أَبِي أُبَيٍّ
رَبَاذِيَةٌ فَأَطْفَأَهَا زِيَادُ

wa-kānat bayna āli abī ubayyin / rabādhiyyatun fa-aṭfaʾahā ziyād

“Discord arose among the family of Abī Ubayy, and Ziyād quelled it.”

رَبَاذِيَة here concerns trouble and discord arising among people.

فَأَطْفَأَهَا means that he extinguished or quelled that discord.

What is extinguished is not an actual fire but hostility. The poet uses extinguishing to describe ending a situation pictured as fire.

This illustrates the root’s figurative use. The family’s name, however, is transmitted in different readings, including آل بني عدي and آل أبي أبيّ. The precise date of composition has not been independently established.

It can therefore serve as an early lexical witness recorded in Arabic dictionaries, but should not be declared certainly pre-Islamic without historical evidence.

مُطْفِئُ الْجَمْرِ: a name for a day of intense cold

Arabic dictionaries record مُطْفِئُ الْجَمْرِ, “extinguisher of embers.”

The name was used for one of the intensely cold days associated with winter’s end.

Its image is clear: cold so severe that it seems to extinguish even glowing embers.

مُطْفِئ is the Form IV active participle, describing something that causes extinction.

The idea of extinguishing could thus enter names for days and descriptions of severe cold.

This does not make ṭ f ʾ mean cold or winter. Cold is the imagined cause of extinction here; extinguishing remains the word’s meaning.

The precise origin of the name and every associated account are not certain. Its recorded use does not establish a pre-Islamic date without independent evidence.

A defensible historical meaning

Lexical and literary evidence records ṭ f ʾ for fires going out or being extinguished, and figuratively for quelling conflict and warfare.

The evidence reviewed here does not establish when each usage first arose or exactly when particular extensions developed.

A historical definition should therefore remain within what the evidence supports:

Ṭ f ʾ denotes a flame going out and a blaze ending; in the transitive construction, causing a fire or light to go out. Figurative uses extend this to quelling war and hostility.

2. Morphological forms and the Quranic semantic core

Important Arabic forms include طَفِئَ, يَطْفَأُ, طَفْء, طُفُوء, أَطْفَأَ, يُطْفِئُ, إِطْفَاء, مُطْفِئ, and انْطَفَأَ.

Of these, the Quran uses only the Form IV verb.

أَطْفَأَهَا in Al-Māʾidah 5:64 consists of the perfect verb أَطْفَأَ and the attached pronoun هَا.

The God is the subject; هَا refers back to نَارًا.

The construction therefore means that The God extinguished that fire.

يُطْفِئُوا in Al-Tawbah 9:32 is the plural imperfect Form IV verb following أَنْ.

لِيُطْفِئُوا in Al-Ṣaff 61:8 combines lām with the plural imperfect verb.

In both verses, the intended agents are people wanting to extinguish the light of The God.

In Al-Tawbah, the extinguishing verb is governed by an expression of desire: the verse concerns their intention and objective, not their success.

Al-Ṣaff conveys the same objective through a different construction.

All three uses concern extinguishing, but their objects differ.

In Al-Māʾidah, the object is the fire of war.

In Al-Tawbah and Al-Ṣaff, divine light is the object of attempted extinguishing.

The core meaning remains, while the particular sense follows from the object and context.

3. أَطْفَأَهَا اللَّهُ: extinguishing the fire of war

Al-Māʾidah 5:64 states:

وَقَالَتِ الْيَهُودُ يَدُ اللَّهِ مَغْلُولَةٌ غُلَّتْ أَيْدِيهِمْ وَلُعِنُوا بِمَا قَالُوا بَلْ يَدَاهُ مَبْسُوطَتَانِ يُنْفِقُ كَيْفَ يَشَاءُ وَلَيَزِيدَنَّ كَثِيرًا مِنْهُمْ مَا أُنْزِلَ إِلَيْكَ مِنْ رَبِّكَ طُغْيَانًا وَكُفْرًا وَأَلْقَيْنَا بَيْنَهُمُ الْعَدَاوَةَ وَالْبَغْضَاءَ إِلَىٰ يَوْمِ الْقِيَامَةِ كُلَّمَا أَوْقَدُوا نَارًا لِلْحَرْبِ أَطْفَأَهَا اللَّهُ وَيَسْعَوْنَ فِي الْأَرْضِ فَسَادًا وَاللَّهُ لَا يُحِبُّ الْمُفْسِدِينَ

wa-qālati al-yahūdu yadu allāhi maghlūlatun ghullat aydīhim wa-luʿinū bi-mā qālū bal yadāhu mabsūṭatāni yunfiqu kayfa yashāʾu wa-la-yazīdanna kathīran minhum mā unzila ilayka min rabbika ṭughyānan wa-kufran wa-alqaynā baynahumu al-ʿadāwata wa-l-baghḍāʾa ilā yawmi al-qiyāmati kullamā awqadū nāran li-l-ḥarbi aṭfaʾahā allāhu wa-yasʿawna fī al-arḍi fasādan wa-allāhu lā yuḥibbu al-mufsidīn

Al-Māʾidah 5:64

“The Jews said: The hand of The God is bound. May their hands be bound, and they were cursed for what they said. Rather, His two hands are outstretched: He gives as He wills. What has been sent down to you from your Lord will surely increase many of them in transgression and unbelief. We have cast enmity and hatred among them until the Day of Resurrection. Whenever they kindle a fire for war, The God extinguishes it. They strive to spread corruption on earth, and The God does not love the corrupters.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:64

Two families stand opposite one another here: w q d and ṭ f ʾ.

أَوْقَدُوا belongs to w q d and means kindling a fire or making it blaze.

أَطْفَأَهَا belongs to ṭ f ʾ and describes extinguishing that fire.

The construction presents a clear contrast: they kindle a fire, and The God extinguishes it.

This is not an ordinary fire for warmth or illumination. نَارًا لِلْحَرْبِ identifies it as a fire for war.

The fire of war as an image of spreading hostility

War can arise from discord, enmity, aggression, incitement, and a decision to use violence, then affect growing numbers of people.

Fire is a natural image for this: a flame may begin at one point and burn increasingly large areas, just as limited conflict can become widespread devastation.

إيقاد نار الحرب means starting or inflaming war.

Conversely, إطفاء نار الحرب means quelling war and preventing its blaze from continuing.

In this context, أَطْفَأَهَا اللَّهُ primarily concerns quelling warfare or frustrating its ignition, rather than merely putting out a physical fire.

The use remains connected to the root’s physical meaning but is figurative in context.

Quelling warfare, corruption, and hostility

Before mentioning the fire of war, the verse mentions enmity and hatred.

After extinguishing, it says that they strive to spread corruption on earth.

War, hostility, and corruption thus form a connected pattern of conduct, rather than an isolated military encounter.

These actions should not be generalized indiscriminately to every member of a people throughout history.

The verse says كَثِيرًا مِنْهُمْ specifically in connection with increased transgression and unbelief. Nearby verses distinguish different conduct among the People of the Book; Al-Māʾidah 5:66 also mentions a moderate group among them. The qualification’s particular grammatical scope should be distinguished from the wider contextual argument against indiscriminate generalization.

Blameworthy conduct belongs to those who actually speak and act that way. Religious or ethnic identification alone does not establish every individual’s corruption, warmongering, or unbelief.

Does quelling the fire of war mean that no war ever occurs?

كُلَّمَا أَوْقَدُوا نَارًا لِلْحَرْبِ أَطْفَأَهَا اللَّهُ describes the frustration of warmongering within this context.

It does not establish that no war has ever begun, that no oppressive group has achieved temporary victory, or that no innocent person has suffered from warfare.

The passage describes divine action against warmongering. How that action occurs and which events the expression encompasses require separate historical and Quranic examination.

Attributing extinguishing to The God does not remove human responsibility for starting war or spreading corruption.

The agents who kindle the fire are identified. The God attributes extinguishing to Himself, while those pursuing warfare and corruption remain responsible for their actions.

The distinction between agents of corruption and the One who quells it is central to the verse’s image.

4. Attempts to extinguish The God's light

The other two Quranic occurrences move from extinguishing the fire of war to attempts to extinguish The God's light.

Here light and extinction express the conflict between guidance and attempts to prevent its spread.

Al-Tawbah 9:32: the desire to extinguish the light

Al-Tawbah 9:32 states:

يُرِيدُونَ أَنْ يُطْفِئُوا نُورَ اللَّهِ بِأَفْوَاهِهِمْ وَيَأْبَى اللَّهُ إِلَّا أَنْ يُتِمَّ نُورَهُ وَلَوْ كَرِهَ الْكَافِرُونَ

yurīdūna an yuṭfiʾū nūra allāhi bi-afwāhihim wa-yaʾbā allāhu illā an yutimma nūrahu wa-law kariha al-kāfirūn

Al-Tawbah 9:32

“They want to extinguish The God's light with their mouths, but The God refuses anything except to complete His light, even though the disbelievers dislike it.”

Al-Tawbah 9:32

Here يُطْفِئُوا is an imperfect verb of Form IV.

نُورَ اللَّهِ is its object: what the opponents want to extinguish.

The sentence, however, begins with يُرِيدُونَ.

The Quran first describes their desire and intention: they want to extinguish The God's light.

Wanting this does not mean achieving it.

The verse then says that The God completes His light despite the opponents' displeasure.

The central contrast is therefore between the desire to extinguish and the divine will to complete the light.

What is The God's light in this context?

In its physical usage, نُور means light. Light makes things visible and enables people to see them.

In passages about guidance, however, light is more than a physical phenomenon.

Al-Tawbah 9:32 concerns opposition to the divine message and religious claims. Al-Tawbah 9:33 then speaks of sending the Messenger with guidance and the religion of truth.

Thus نُورَ اللَّهِ here relates to guidance, truth, and the divine message.

Extinguishing it means preventing guidance from becoming manifest, being accepted, or spreading.

The root itself, however, does not mean denial, distortion, concealment, or religious opposition. These are behaviors that may form part of an attempt to extinguish the light.

The verb still means extinguishing; its object, The God's light, carries the image from physical fire and illumination into the sphere of guidance.

بِأَفْوَاهِهِمْ: an attempt made with mouths

A striking feature of this verse is the expression بِأَفْوَاهِهِمْ.

أَفْوَاه is the plural of فَم and means mouths.

Extinguishing light with one's mouth can evoke blowing on a flame, as someone might do to put out a small flame.

Here, however, the subject is The God's light in the context of guidance and the divine message.

The mouth is also an instrument of speech.

The preceding verses discuss false religious statements and attributions. Al-Tawbah 9:30 uses قَوْلُهُمْ بِأَفْوَاهِهِمْ in this connection.

Thus “with their mouths” can evoke blowing out a flame while also referring to speech used to oppose guidance.

Speech in such conduct can serve concealment of truth, misleading publicity, distortion, or incitement to opposition.

This does not mean that every verbal disagreement, critical question, or difference of interpretation is an attempt to extinguish The God's light.

The text speaks of people intending to extinguish the light. A question or criticism does not by itself establish such an intention.

What matters is the relationship of the conduct to truth, together with the person's awareness and intention.

The promise of completing the light

The verse ends by stating that The God completes His light despite the disbelievers' displeasure.

This indicates that the destiny of the divine light is not governed by its opponents' wishes.

People may oppose truth, obstruct its spread, mislead others, or temporarily prevent a community from accessing guidance. Such conduct does not establish their power to destroy truth before The God.

Nor should completion of the divine light be taken to mean that oppression, concealment, distortion, or deprivation of access to truth never occurs.

The Quran itself describes such conduct and holds people responsible for it.

The promise does not deny temporary oppression or opposition; it expresses the superiority of the divine will over the wishes of those seeking to extinguish the light.

5. لِيُطْفِئُوا: the aim of extinguishing in Al-Ṣaff

Al-Ṣaff 61:8 states:

يُرِيدُونَ لِيُطْفِئُوا نُورَ اللَّهِ بِأَفْوَاهِهِمْ وَاللَّهُ مُتِمُّ نُورِهِ وَلَوْ كَرِهَ الْكَافِرُونَ

yurīdūna li-yuṭfiʾū nūra allāhi bi-afwāhihim wa-allāhu mutimmu nūrihi wa-law kariha al-kāfirūn

Al-Ṣaff 61:8

“They want to extinguish The God's light with their mouths, and The God is the completer of His light, even though the disbelievers dislike it.”

Al-Ṣaff 61:8

This verse closely resembles Al-Tawbah 9:32 in subject and imagery.

Both describe a desire to extinguish the divine light and the use of mouths.

Their grammatical structures, however, differ.

Al-Tawbah says:

يُرِيدُونَ أَنْ يُطْفِئُوا

yurīdūna an yuṭfiʾū

Al-Tawbah 9:32

They want to extinguish.

Al-Ṣaff says:

يُرِيدُونَ لِيُطْفِئُوا

yurīdūna li-yuṭfiʾū

Al-Ṣaff 61:8

They want to extinguish.

In the first construction, أَنْ introduces a verbal clause and puts the imperfect verb in the subjunctive; the clause functions as the object of يُرِيدُونَ.

In the second, لِـ is attached to the verb. Grammarians offer different accounts of the precise function of this lām.

The construction can be understood in relation to their intention and aim: their actions and efforts are directed toward extinguishing The God's light.

The difference between أَنْ and lām alone does not establish that one verse describes a mere wish while the other necessarily describes the successful execution of a plan.

Both verses describe a desire to extinguish; neither confirms that it succeeds.

The different endings of the two verses

Al-Tawbah 9:32 uses يَأْبَى followed by يُتِمَّ.

Al-Ṣaff 61:8 uses وَاللَّهُ مُتِمُّ نُورِهِ.

The first construction emphasizes refusing anything except completion of the light.

The second describes The God as مُتِمّ: the completer of His light.

This difference in expression matters, but it should not be turned into two contradictory assertions.

Both verses say that the opponents' desire does not determine the destiny of the divine light.

مُتِمّ belongs to ت م م and concerns completion. Its meaning should not be transferred to ط ف أ.

ط ف أ expresses extinguishing; ت م م expresses completing.

Their contrast shapes the central image of the verse.

The context of Al-Ṣaff

Al-Ṣaff 61:6 speaks of Jesus announcing a Messenger to come after him.

Al-Ṣaff 61:7 condemns inventing a lie against The God while being invited to submission.

Following the verse about light, Al-Ṣaff 61:9 speaks of sending the Messenger with guidance and the religion of truth.

In this sequence, divine light relates to guidance and the truth of the message; attempts to extinguish it occur in the context of opposition to that message.

Extinguishing therefore draws on the physical image of putting out light, while its subject is truth and guidance.

In contemporary life, someone may fabricate news, distort statements, conceal evidence, or misuse power to obstruct knowledge of the truth.

When undertaken with a conscious intention to suppress truth, such actions can offer examples for reflection on the verse's theme.

They should not become a new definition of the root itself. Extinguishing remains its meaning; the truth concerned, the method of opposition, and the agents' responsibility are understood from context and other evidence.

6. Distinguishing ط ف أ from related roots

ط ف أ and و ق د: extinction and kindling

و ق د concerns fire catching, being kindled, or being set ablaze.

The primary usage of ط ف أ describes a flame ending or being extinguished.

Al-Māʾidah 5:64 places these two families in opposition.

أَوْقَدُوا describes kindling the fire; أَطْفَأَهَا describes putting it out.

This contrast shows two different directions of change within a single situation.

Kindling should not always be equated with corruption, nor extinguishing with goodness.

Lighting a fire for warmth or illumination can be useful; extinguishing a fire essential to life can be harmful.

In Al-Māʾidah the subject is the fire of war, and the conduct is evaluated through that context.

ط ف أ and خ م د: extinguishing and subsiding

خ م د concerns a flame subsiding, activity quieting, and, in some usages, extinction or the end of movement.

Some lexical explanations distinguish خمود from طُفُوء: a خامدة fire may have no visible flame while retaining embers, whereas a طافئة fire has gone out and its embers have cooled.

This distinction is not absolute across all Arabic usage.

In the Quran, خَامِدِينَ in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:15 and خَامِدُونَ in Yā Sīn 36:29 describe groups who have perished.

By contrast, ط ف أ occurs only in Form IV verbs concerning the fire of war or the desire to extinguish light.

The roots overlap semantically, but their forms and Quranic applications differ.

ط ف أ and ه م د: extinction and stillness

ه م د also concerns fire going out and activity subsiding.

In Al-Ḥajj 22:5, هَامِدَة describes earth without movement or growth, transformed when water reaches it.

This illustrates a state of stillness and absence of visible activity.

The primary meaning of طُفُوء concerns fire going out.

Their similarity does not mean that every instance of همود describes extinguished light, or that every instance of طُفُوء necessarily describes death and complete immobility in a living being.

ط ف أ and ط ف و: going out and floating

These roots look very similar in writing, but their meanings differ.

طَفِئَ, with a final hamzah, concerns fire going out.

طَفَا, with a weak final radical, concerns rising and floating on water.

طَفَا الشَّيْءُ فَوْقَ الْمَاءِ means that something floated on the water.

Extinguishing fire and floating on water must therefore not be equated because of similar spelling.

Some older sources also record forms for extinguishing without the hamzah in spelling or pronunciation. Such dialectal or orthographic differences do not by themselves establish a semantic or historical relationship between the two families.

The three Quranic forms examined here are likewise classified under ط ف أ in the morphological index.

ط ف أ and ن و ر: extinguishing and light

ن و ر concerns light and illumination.

ط ف أ expresses extinguishing.

The two families appear together in Al-Tawbah 9:32 and Al-Ṣaff 61:8.

يُطْفِئُوا is what the opponents want to do; نُورَ اللَّهِ is what they want to extinguish.

Here light relates to guidance and truth.

Extinguishing does not itself mean misleading or disbelieving, just as light is not restricted to a single religious concept throughout Arabic and the Quran.

Extinguishing light is also distinct from destroying truth itself. A person can seek to conceal truth or obstruct its spread without necessarily succeeding.

Both verses emphasize this distinction between the opponents' efforts and completion of the divine light.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

The principal Persian equivalents are “to go out” and “to extinguish.”

طَفِئَتِ النَّارُ in Arabic means “the fire went out.”

أَطْفَأَ النَّارَ means “he extinguished the fire.”

إِطْفَاء means “extinguishing.”

مُطْفِئ means “one who extinguishes.”

انْطَفَأَ means “it went out.”

Figuratively, أَطْفَأَ الْحَرْبَ can be rendered “he quelled the war” or “he extinguished the fire of war.”

The following renderings are proposed for the three Quranic occurrences:

Al-Māʾidah 5:64 — أَطْفَأَهَا اللَّهُ

“The God extinguished it.”

In the full construction: “Whenever they kindled a fire for war, The God extinguished it.”

Retaining the image of the fire of war makes the verse's imagery clear.

Al-Tawbah 9:32 — يُرِيدُونَ أَنْ يُطْفِئُوا نُورَ اللَّهِ بِأَفْوَاهِهِمْ

“They want to extinguish The God's light with their mouths.”

“They want” must be preserved: the verb describes the opponents' intention, not successful extinction.

Al-Ṣaff 61:8 — يُرِيدُونَ لِيُطْفِئُوا نُورَ اللَّهِ بِأَفْوَاهِهِمْ

“They want to extinguish The God's light with their mouths.”

The Persian rendering can match that of Al-Tawbah because the two constructions concern the same aim. Their grammatical distinction between أَنْ and لِـ should nevertheless be retained in the analysis.

Replacing يُطْفِئُوا with “distort,” “deny,” or “conceal” is unsuitable in the direct translation. These may be means of attempting to extinguish the light, but the Arabic verb uses the specific image of extinguishing.

Nor should the basic translation restrict The God's light without explanation to one instance, such as a particular book, religious institution, or governmental system.

Light is retained in the translation; its relationship to guidance and truth is explained in the contextual discussion.

8. Summary

ط ف أ describes fire going out and its flame and illumination ending. The transitive أَطْفَأَ expresses causing something to go out.

Older Arabic applies this family to quelling hostility and war as well as physical fire. The verse attributed to Ziyād al-Ṭammāḥī illustrates ending conflict between groups. Its precise date is unclear, so it cannot be presented as certainly pre-Islamic.

The root occurs three times in the Quran, exclusively as Form IV verbs.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:64, The God extinguishes a fire kindled for war. Fire images warfare and hostility, while extinguishing concerns quelling or frustrating them.

Al-Tawbah 9:32 and Al-Ṣaff 61:8 describe people seeking to extinguish The God's light with their mouths. Light occurs in the context of guidance and the divine message; attempts to extinguish it relate to opposing truth's manifestation or spread.

Neither verse confirms that the opponents succeed. Both instead speak of completion of the divine light.

Extinguishing is not inherently good or bad. Putting out the fire of war and preventing corruption can be good; attempting to extinguish the light of guidance is condemned in these passages.

Evaluation depends on what is extinguished, the agent's purpose, and the conduct's consequences, rather than the root alone.

A final definition of ط ف أ is: a flame or light going out and the state of burning ending; transitively, extinguishing or causing that state to end. Figurative usage extends to quelling war or hostility, or attempting to prevent light from exercising its effect.

This explains all three Quranic occurrences without equating lexical meaning with the verses' religious or social subject matter.

In Al-Māʾidah, the fire kindled is that of war, and The God puts it out. In Al-Tawbah and Al-Ṣaff, the opponents seek to extinguish divine light, but their wishes do not determine its destiny.

The root thus brings together two contrasting images: the fire of war that The God extinguishes, and the light of truth that opponents seek to extinguish but The God brings to completion.

The contrast extends beyond a flame going out: people can direct their efforts toward kindling war or suppressing truth, yet remain accountable for their actions. Divine truth is not governed by the wishes of those seeking to eliminate it.
