From oaths and covenants to commitment, alliance, and concealing the truth

The Arabic root ح ل ف concerns swearing an oath and the bond established between people through an oath or covenant. حَلَفَ means “to swear”; حِلْف can denote a covenant or alliance; and حَلِيف describes someone who has entered a covenant with another and stands in a relationship of companionship and commitment.

In ordinary use, an oath is a statement made to affirm the truth of a claim or strengthen a commitment. Swearing does not, however, establish that the claim is true or that the speaker will fulfil the commitment. A person can swear truthfully, affirm a mistaken statement under oath, or knowingly swear falsely to deceive others.

In the Qur’an, this root mostly describes people who swear to justify their conduct, win others’ approval, or conceal the truth. Nevertheless, Al-Māʾidah 5:89 discusses oaths and their attendant responsibility without presenting the act of swearing itself as objectionable.

Qur’anic forms: twelve occurrences of inflected Form I verbs from حَلَفَ and one occurrence of the intensive noun حَلَّاف. They occur in Al-Nisāʾ 4:62, Al-Māʾidah 5:89, Al-Tawbah 9:42, Al-Tawbah 9:56, Al-Tawbah 9:62, Al-Tawbah 9:74, Al-Tawbah 9:95, Al-Tawbah 9:96, Al-Tawbah 9:107, Al-Mujādilah 58:14, Al-Mujādilah 58:18, and Al-Qalam 68:10. In Al-Mujādilah 58:18, يَحْلِفُونَ occurs twice.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: thirteen occurrences in twelve verses

Forms such as حَلْف, حَلِف, حِلْف, حَلْفَة, حَالِف, حَلِيف, حُلَفَاء, حَالَفَ, تَحَالَفَ, اسْتَحْلَفَ, and مَحْلُوف are recorded in Arabic but do not occur in these forms in the Qur’an.

1. Core Meaning and Usage in Early Arabic

حَلَفَ: swearing an oath

حَلَفَ يَحْلِفُ means “to swear an oath.” Its verbal noun is recorded as حَلْف and حَلِف, while حَلْفَة denotes a single act of swearing.

In Al-ʿAyn, حلف is explained through قسم, and مَحْلُوفَة is also recorded with the meaning “oath.”

Kitāb al-ʿAyn: ḥalf, maḥlūfah, and ḥallāf

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris proposes one underlying principle for this root: المُلازَمَة, companionship and adherence.

He explains that a covenant binds people to one another, while through an oath a person binds himself to uphold what he has said.

This analysis establishes an intelligible connection between oaths and alliances. It does not imply that every oath actually results in fidelity. Adherence here concerns the structure and function of an oath, rather than a guarantee of the speaker’s honesty.

Someone may swear and fail to fulfil the oath. The act of swearing has still occurred, even if the commitment or claim does not accord with reality or subsequent conduct.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: adherence and exceptional usages

حَلِف and حِلْف: oath and covenant

In early Arabic, حَلْف and حَلِف were used for an oath. حِلْف, with kasrah on the first consonant, is also known in the sense of a covenant or alliance between individuals or groups.

Two people could swear to assist one another, provide mutual protection, or uphold an agreement.

In such circumstances, an oath was more than an emphatic sentence: it could establish a social relationship carrying obligations.

Accordingly, حِلْف was also applied to covenants between tribes and groups. Those joined by such a bond were called حُلَفَاء.

Three concepts must be distinguished here: the oath that is spoken, the agreement established between the parties, and their practical conduct in carrying it out.

These can occur together, but their meanings are distinct.

حَالَفَ and حَلِيف: alliance and companionship

حَالَفَ فُلَانٌ فُلَانًا means that one person entered a covenant with another.

حَلِيف denotes an ally, with حُلَفَاء or أَحْلَاف as its plural.

Early dictionaries record the word in connection with tribal alliances and groups bound by covenants.

Al-ʿAyn and Lisān al-ʿArab, for example, discuss bonds between tribes and the designation of certain groups as أَحْلَاف.

This usage shows that حلف in Arab social relations was not confined to verbally affirming the truth of a report.

A covenant does not inherently possess positive moral value, however. Two groups can unite to defend the oppressed or to violate others’ rights.

The legitimacy of a covenant therefore depends on its purpose, conditions, and the parties’ conduct, rather than the word حلف itself.

حَلِيف also has an extended Arabic use. حَلِيفُ الْجُودِ, for example, describes someone as though he were constantly allied with generosity.

No formal covenant or actual oath is involved in this expression. The quality of companionship and adherence is transferred from human relationships to a personal trait.

حَلَّاف: someone who swears frequently

حَلَّاف, on the pattern فَعَّال, is an intensive form of this root.

Al-ʿAyn records حَلَّاف and حَلَّافَة for someone who swears frequently.

Beyond the basic act of swearing, this form emphasizes frequency or a habitual tendency to swear.

The pattern فَعَّال does not by itself establish that the oaths are false.

A person may swear frequently and some of his statements may be true. When حَلَّاف is used reproachfully in a verse, the accompanying descriptions and context must also be examined.

This distinction matters in Al-Qalam 68:10.

مُحْلِف: something that prompts disagreement and oaths

Lexical sources also record مُحْلِف for something disputed about which people swear to establish their own judgement.

For example, people may disagree over whether a horse’s colour is كُمَيْت, reddish with a dark mane and tail, or أَشْقَر, a different reddish colouring.

Disagreement over identification can then lead the parties to swear.

In this usage, كُمَيْتٌ مُحْلِفَةٌ describes a horse whose precise colouring prompts disagreement and oaths. Lexicographers transmit both مُحْلِف and مُحْلِفَة in this field. The expression concerns disputed identification of a horse’s colouring, rather than the name of a separate colour.

Swearing in this situation does not necessarily mean that either party is knowingly lying. Two people may genuinely perceive a colour or situation differently.

Conflicting oaths, without examination of the parties’ knowledge and intentions, do not conclusively show that one has knowingly lied.

Al-Rāghib’s Mufradāt: muḥlif and disputed horse colouring

Other uses: the ḥalfāʾ plant and sharpness of speech

حَلْفَاء is also the Arabic name of a plant growing in moist places or beside water. Early sources describe its leaves or other pointed parts.

حَلِيفُ اللِّسَانِ is also used of someone with a sharp tongue and penetrating speech.

Some lexicographers connect this expression with the sharpness of the ḥalfāʾ plant, whereas Ibn Fāris regards it as a usage remote from the principle of adherence.

These meanings show that every word recorded under a lexical family cannot be translated as “oath” or “covenant.”

The historical relationship between the plant’s name and swearing or alliance is also unclear. Shared letters alone do not establish a derivational connection.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: alliance, ḥalfāʾ, and ḥalīf al-lisān

2. Early Poetry: Oaths and Covenantal Relationships

The dīwān of Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī, the well-known pre-Islamic poet, contains the following:

حَلَفْتُ يَمِينًا غَيْرَ ذِي مَثْنَوِيَّةٍ
وَلَا عِلْمَ إِلَّا حُسْنَ ظَنٍّ بِصَاحِبِ

ḥalaftu yamīnan ghayra dhī mathnawiyyatin / wa-lā ʿilma illā ḥusna ẓannin bi-ṣāḥibi

The sense is: I swore an oath without exception or retraction, although my knowledge rested on nothing beyond a favourable opinion of a companion.

حَلَفْتُ يَمِينًا clearly combines the verb “to swear” with the noun “oath.”

غَيْرَ ذِي مَثْنَوِيَّةٍ emphasizes the absence of an exception or withdrawal from the oath’s substance.

This witness shows that حلف was used for swearing in Arabic before the Qur’an and could be combined with يَمِين.

The oath in this verse does not by itself establish that the poet is lying. Its content and circumstances matter when assessing its truthfulness.

Sharḥ Abyāt Sībawayh: Al-Nābighah’s oath and transmitted readings

Another ode by the same poet contains:

حَلَفْتُ فَلَمْ أَتْرُكْ لِنَفْسِكَ رِيبَةً
وَهَلْ يَأْثَمَنَّ ذُو أُمَّةٍ وَهُوَ طَائِعُ

ḥalaftu fa-lam atruk li-nafsika rībatan / wa-hal yaʾthamanna dhū ummatin wa-huwa ṭāʾiʿu

Meaning: I swore and left you no room for doubt; would a person of religion and faith sin while acting obediently? Here ذُو أُمَّةٍ denotes a person of religion and faith, rather than merely someone bound by a covenant.

The verse occurs in the poet’s defence of himself and his attempt to remove suspicion.

The same ode speaks of an oath of innocence proving unavailing: the poet seeks the addressee’s trust through an oath but fears it will not be accepted.

These verses have transmitted variants. The first is quoted with بِصَاحِبِ and بِغَائِبِ, and with different case readings of حُسْنَ ظَنٍّ; the second also has variants. The dīwān’s text and short lexical quotations must therefore be distinguished, rather than treating all transmissions as identical.

Majmaʿ al-Bayān: dhū ummah as a person of religion and faith

These examples show that oaths have long served to affirm a claim, defend oneself, and dispel suspicion.

In Abū Dhuʾayb al-Hudhalī’s poetry, حَلِيف also occurs in connection with a pledge. The poet asks whether a person has upheld the pledge or committed wrongdoing and betrayal in it.

This literary use shows that حَلِيف can extend beyond a tribal ally to a person bound to another by a pledge of fidelity.

Abū Dhuʾayb was a mukhaḍram poet who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. Without dating the particular verse, it should not be presented as a securely pre-Islamic witness.

Tāj al-ʿArūs: Abū Dhuʾayb’s witness linking ḥalīf with a pledge

Taken together, the early witnesses support two principal uses: an oath that strengthens a claim and a covenant that establishes an obligating relationship between people.

3. Oaths in Qur’anic Usage: Claims, Justification, and Seeking Trust

In most Qur’anic uses of حلف, the speakers’ behaviour or claims are criticized in the surrounding passage.

This should not change the meaning of the verb itself into “to lie” or “to act hypocritically.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:62: an oath to justify conduct

فَكَيْفَ إِذَا أَصَابَتْهُمْ مُصِيبَةٌ بِمَا قَدَّمَتْ أَيْدِيهِمْ ثُمَّ جَاءُوكَ يَحْلِفُونَ بِاللَّهِ إِنْ أَرَدْنَا إِلَّا إِحْسَانًا وَتَوْفِيقًا

fa-kayfa idhā aṣābat-hum muṣībatun bi-mā qaddamat aydīhim thumma jāʾūka yaḥlifūna bi-llāhi in aradnā illā iḥsānan wa-tawfīqan

Translation: How will it be when a misfortune strikes them because of what their hands have sent ahead, and they then come to you swearing by The God, “We intended only goodness and reconciliation”?

Here يَحْلِفُونَ describes people who swear to justify their conduct after encountering its consequences.

They claim that their purpose was nothing but goodness and reconciliation.

This shows that an oath can emphasize a claim about a person’s intention.

The verb يَحْلِفُونَ alone does not disclose their actual intention. The discrepancy between their claim and the reality of their conduct emerges from the surrounding verses.

This distinction matters: swearing about an intention neither establishes the claim’s truth nor independently proves its falsity.

Al-Tawbah 9:42 and Al-Tawbah 9:56: oaths about excuses and affiliation

Al-Tawbah 9:42 describes people who failed to accompany the Prophet and will swear that they would have gone out with the others had they been able.

The verse then confronts their claim with divine testimony to their lying.

Here the oath serves to substantiate an excuse and justify their failure to accompany the others.

In Al-Tawbah 9:56, a group swears by The God that they belong to the believers’ community, although the verse rejects this claim and speaks of their fear.

The subjects differ. In Al-Tawbah 9:42, the oath concerns the ability to do something; in Al-Tawbah 9:56, affiliation and membership in a community.

In both cases, however, the speaker seeks to make the claim more acceptable to the audience through an oath.

يَحْلِفُونَ therefore cannot simply be translated as “making excuses” or “claiming faith.” These are subjects of the oaths in the respective verses, rather than the verb’s own meaning.

Al-Tawbah 9:62 and Al-Tawbah 9:74: oaths to gain approval and deny speech

In Al-Tawbah 9:62, a group swears by The God to obtain the addressees’ approval.

The stated purpose of the oath is to please others.

In Al-Tawbah 9:74, however, an oath denies a statement that the verse expressly says they uttered.

The subject of an oath can thus be a social aim, a claim about conduct, or denial of an earlier statement.

In Al-Tawbah 9:74, an explicit statement in the verse makes clear that the oath does not accord with the truth.

Its falsity is established by the sentence concerning their actual speech, rather than by the use of حلف.

Al-Tawbah 9:95 and Al-Tawbah 9:96: oaths to alter others’ reactions

In Al-Tawbah 9:95, a group will swear when the addressees return to them, so that others turn away from examining their situation.

Al-Tawbah 9:96 speaks of swearing to gain approval.

These purposes differ. Someone may want others to leave him alone or may want their approval and acceptance.

In either case, the oath can influence the audience’s reaction.

An oath does not itself release someone from responsibility for his actions, however, nor does others’ approval necessarily establish that his conduct is right.

This conclusion emerges from the verses’ context, rather than the lexical meaning of حلف.

4. Oaths and the Distance Between Claim and Truth

Al-Tawbah 9:107: an oath about good intentions

وَالَّذِينَ اتَّخَذُوا مَسْجِدًا ضِرَارًا وَكُفْرًا وَتَفْرِيقًا بَيْنَ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَإِرْصَادًا لِمَنْ حَارَبَ اللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُ مِنْ قَبْلُ وَلَيَحْلِفُنَّ إِنْ أَرَدْنَا إِلَّا الْحُسْنَىٰ وَاللَّهُ يَشْهَدُ إِنَّهُمْ لَكَاذِبُونَ

wa-alladhīna ittakhadhū masjidan ḍirāran wa-kufran wa-tafrīqan bayna al-muʾminīna wa-irṣādan li-man ḥāraba allāha wa-rasūlahu min qablu wa-la-yaḥlifunna in aradnā illā al-ḥusnā wa-allāhu yashhadu innahum la-kādhibūna

Translation: Those who established a mosque for harm, disbelief, division among the believers, and preparation for one who had previously fought The God and His messenger will certainly swear, “We intended only goodness”; and The God testifies that they are liars.

وَلَيَحْلِفُنَّ is an emphatic imperfect verb stressing that they will swear.

The construction presents their swearing emphatically, but morphological emphasis does not guarantee an oath’s truthfulness.

The verse concerns people who provided a building called a mosque, while their harmful purposes are also named in the verse itself.

Nevertheless, they swear that they intended only goodness.

The discrepancy between an action’s appearance and its actual purpose is prominent here. Building a mosque may outwardly appear religious and commendable, but if its purpose is harm and division, the building’s religious designation does not alter the reality of the conduct.

An oath cannot by itself remove that discrepancy.

In this verse, The God expressly testifies to the falsity of their claim.

This example does not establish that everyone claiming good intentions or swearing to substantiate a statement must be lying. The verse concerns a particular group and conduct whose nature the text makes clear.

The lexical point is that حلف remains the act of swearing; falsity arises from the oath’s content failing to accord with the truth.

Al-Mujādilah 58:14: knowingly swearing to a lie

أَلَمْ تَرَ إِلَى الَّذِينَ تَوَلَّوْا قَوْمًا غَضِبَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِمْ مَا هُمْ مِنْكُمْ وَلَا مِنْهُمْ وَيَحْلِفُونَ عَلَى الْكَذِبِ وَهُمْ يَعْلَمُونَ

a-lam tara ilā alladhīna tawallaw qawman ghaḍiba allāhu ʿalayhim mā hum minkum wa-lā minhum wa-yaḥlifūna ʿalā al-kadhibi wa-hum yaʿlamūna

Translation: Have you not seen those who have allied themselves with people upon whom The God is angry? They belong neither to you nor to them, and they knowingly swear to falsehood.

This is one of the clearest occurrences for understanding the distinction between swearing and lying.

يَحْلِفُونَ expresses the act of swearing, عَلَى الْكَذِبِ specifies the oath’s false content, and وَهُمْ يَعْلَمُونَ states their knowledge.

The verse therefore contains three distinct matters: the oath, its falsity, and the person’s awareness of that falsity.

These accompanying expressions distinguish the act of swearing from its content and the speakers’ knowledge. Their presence alone does not prove that awareness or falsity could never be part of a word’s meaning: an expression may also provide emphasis. Here the lexical sources and the non-condemnatory use in Al-Māʾidah 5:89 also support the distinction.

The verse shows that falsity and awareness should be identified from the verb’s complement and accompanying evidence.

Not every incorrect oath should therefore be treated as deliberate lying without examination. A person may mistakenly be certain of something and swear to affirm it.

Assessing such a person’s moral responsibility differs from assessing someone who knows the truth and swears against it.

Al-Ṭabarī, Al-Mujādilah 58:14: knowingly swearing to falsehood

Al-Mujādilah 58:18: repeated swearing at the reckoning

يَوْمَ يَبْعَثُهُمُ اللَّهُ جَمِيعًا فَيَحْلِفُونَ لَهُ كَمَا يَحْلِفُونَ لَكُمْ وَيَحْسَبُونَ أَنَّهُمْ عَلَىٰ شَيْءٍ أَلَا إِنَّهُمْ هُمُ الْكَاذِبُونَ

yawma yabʿathuhumu allāhu jamīʿan fa-yaḥlifūna lahu ka-mā yaḥlifūna lakum wa-yaḥsabūna annahum ʿalā shayʾin a-lā innahum humu al-kādhibūna

Translation: On the day when The God raises them all, they will swear to Him just as they swear to you, thinking that they stand upon something. Indeed, they are the liars.

يَحْلِفُونَ appears twice in this verse.

The first occurrence concerns their swearing at the resurrection; the second recalls the same group’s behaviour toward their worldly audience.

The repetition depicts one mode of conduct—resorting to oaths—in two different settings.

It does not mean that The God needs their oaths to discover the truth. The context concerns divine knowledge and judgement, and the verse itself identifies them as liars at its conclusion.

وَيَحْسَبُونَ أَنَّهُمْ عَلَىٰ شَيْءٍ also shows that people may imagine their claim has standing when it has none in reality.

The distinction between the standing a speaker imagines and the truth of his words comes from the context, rather than the direct meaning of حلف.

5. Binding Oaths and the Reproach of Frequent Swearing

Al-Māʾidah 5:89: responsibility for an oath

لَا يُؤَاخِذُكُمُ اللَّهُ بِاللَّغْوِ فِي أَيْمَانِكُمْ وَلَٰكِنْ يُؤَاخِذُكُمْ بِمَا عَقَّدْتُمُ الْأَيْمَانَ فَكَفَّارَتُهُ إِطْعَامُ عَشَرَةِ مَسَاكِينَ مِنْ أَوْسَطِ مَا تُطْعِمُونَ أَهْلِيكُمْ أَوْ كِسْوَتُهُمْ أَوْ تَحْرِيرُ رَقَبَةٍ فَمَنْ لَمْ يَجِدْ فَصِيَامُ ثَلَاثَةِ أَيَّامٍ ذَٰلِكَ كَفَّارَةُ أَيْمَانِكُمْ إِذَا حَلَفْتُمْ وَاحْفَظُوا أَيْمَانَكُمْ كَذَٰلِكَ يُبَيِّنُ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ آيَاتِهِ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَشْكُرُونَ

lā yuʾākhidhukumu allāhu bi-l-laghwi fī aymānikum wa-lākin yuʾākhidhukum bi-mā ʿaqqadtumu al-aymāna fa-kaffāratuhu iṭʿāmu ʿasharati masākīna min awsaṭi mā tuṭʿimūna ahlīkum aw kiswatuhum aw taḥrīru raqabatin fa-man lam yajid fa-ṣiyāmu thalāthati ayyāmin dhālika kaffāratu aymānikum idhā ḥalaftum wa-ḥfaẓū aymānakum ka-dhālika yubayyinu allāhu lakum āyātihi laʿallakum tashkurūna

Translation: The God does not hold you accountable for idle speech in your oaths, but holds you accountable for the oaths you have bound firmly. Its expiation is feeding ten needy people from the average food you give your families, or clothing them, or freeing a person in bondage. Whoever cannot find the means is to fast for three days. That is the expiation of your oaths when you have sworn; and safeguard your oaths. Thus The God makes His signs clear to you, so that you may give thanks.

This verse is the most important use of ح ل ف concerning the legal and moral responsibility of an oath.

حَلَفْتُمْ appears toward the end of the ruling and denotes the act of swearing itself.

Other words in the verse also contribute to identifying the kind of oath and its attendant responsibility.

أَيْمَان is the plural of يَمِين and denotes oaths in this context.

لَغْو concerns speech lacking the requisite validity and firmness.

عَقَّدْتُمُ comes from ع ق د and concerns firmly establishing and binding a commitment.

Study of ʿ Q D

These words should not be equated with حلف. The latter means “to swear,” while the verse distinguishes unbinding oaths from those firmly undertaken as commitments.

Not every oath-like expression therefore creates identical responsibility irrespective of its circumstances.

The verse does not condemn swearing categorically. It discusses responsibility and expiation for binding oaths and ends by commanding that oaths be safeguarded. The mere act of swearing should not be treated as requiring expiation in every case: breaking a binding oath, حِنْث, is a separate issue relevant to expiation. The detailed conditions and timing of expiation require their own legal examination and do not follow from the root حلف alone.

حلف does not itself mean breaking an oath. Breaching or failing to fulfil a commitment is a separate matter assessed according to the oath’s content and circumstances.

Al-Baqarah 2:224 and Al-Baqarah 2:225 also discuss oaths. Al-Naḥl 16:91 emphasizes fulfilling covenants and confirmed oaths, while Al-Naḥl 16:94 warns against making oaths a means of deception and undermining relations between people.

These verses matter for understanding the rulings concerning oaths, but the root حلف alone does not contain all those rulings.

Al-Kashshāf, Al-Māʾidah 5:89: swearing, breach, and expiation

Al-Qalam 68:10: حَلَّاف, a frequent swearer

وَلَا تُطِعْ كُلَّ حَلَّافٍ مَهِينٍ

wa-lā tuṭiʿ kulla ḥallāfin mahīnin

Translation: And do not obey any contemptible, frequent swearer.

This is the only nominal occurrence of ح ل ف in the Qur’an.

حَلَّاف, on the pattern فَعَّال, denotes someone who swears frequently.

مَهِينٍ is a separate description. The following verses introduce other traits, including fault-finding, malicious tale-bearing, withholding good, and aggression.

The reproach cannot therefore be understood from the number of oaths alone; the full set of contextual descriptions also contributes to identifying the person.

Nevertheless, the choice of حَلَّاف makes frequent swearing a prominent characteristic of this figure.

Someone who constantly resorts to oaths to gain acceptance may be compensating for the audience’s lack of trust. This is a possible explanation of human behaviour, rather than the fixed lexical meaning of حَلَّاف.

The word alone does not establish that all the person’s oaths are false. Establishing a lie requires knowledge of the oath’s content and the relevant reality.

حَلَّاف should consequently be understood as “someone who swears frequently,” with its moral assessment explained through the verse’s descriptions.

Al-Ṭabarī, Al-Qalam 68:10: frequent swearing

6. Distinctions from Related Roots and Translation Proposals

ح ل ف (ḥalaf) and ق س م (qasam)

قَسَم denotes an oath in Arabic, and أَقْسَمَ also means “to swear.”

أَقْسَمُوا بِاللَّهِ appears in several Qur’anic verses, just as يَحْلِفُونَ بِاللَّهِ occurs among the uses of ح ل ف.

Both therefore share the field of swearing.

ق س م also has another branch concerning division, allocation of shares, and separating portions.

The features of that branch cannot be transferred into the meaning of حلف.

Nor does the predominantly reproachful setting of حلف in the Qur’an establish an absolute rule that حلف always denotes a false oath and قسم always a truthful one.

That value distinction is not established within the two roots themselves. Both can express swearing, whose truthfulness must be determined from its content and context.

ح ل ف (ḥalaf) and ي م ن (yamīn)

يَمِين means “right hand” in Arabic and, in another use, denotes an oath.

حلف expresses the act of swearing, whereas يَمِين can name the oath itself.

Both families appear in Al-Māʾidah 5:89: أَيْمَان names the oaths, while حَلَفْتُمْ expresses the act of swearing.

Different words can thus express different components of a linguistic and legal relationship.

This does not imply that every يَمِين must be performed with the right hand or that swearing in every culture must involve a particular bodily gesture.

In this Qur’anic context, يَمِين names an oath; its manner of performance is not determined by the word itself.

ح ل ف (ḥalaf) and ع ه د (ʿahd)

عَهْد concerns a covenant, commitment, or relationship undertaken between people or before The God.

حلف can strengthen such a covenant, but not every pledge necessarily involves a spoken oath.

Nor does every oath necessarily create a contract between two people.

Someone can swear to establish the truth of a report without entering a covenant of cooperation with the audience.

Conversely, two groups can establish a covenantal relationship through agreement and mutual commitment.

Oaths and covenants thus overlap in some circumstances while differing in meaning and function.

Study of ʿ H D

ح ل ف (ḥalaf) and ح ن ث (ḥinth)

One established use of حِنْث concerns breaking an oath and failing to fulfil it.

Ṣād 38:44 contains the command وَلَا تَحْنَثْ: “Do not break your oath.”

Swearing and breaking an oath are thus expressed through different lexical families.

حلف concerns the oath itself; in an oath context, حِنْث denotes its violation or breach.

حلف should therefore not be translated as “breaking a covenant” throughout the Qur’an, even where a person’s oath accompanies misconduct.

ح ل ف (ḥalaf) and ك ذ ب (kadhib)

كَذِب concerns lying and a report’s failure to accord with the truth.

حلف denotes swearing.

They appear together in several Qur’anic verses, including Al-Tawbah 9:107 and Al-Mujādilah 58:14.

Their conjunction illustrates two distinct concepts. Someone can affirm a lie under oath, but falsity characterizes the statement’s content, while the oath is a means of emphasizing it.

Al-Mujādilah 58:14 also states the speakers’ awareness. Swearing, falsity, and knowledge of falsity are therefore three concepts that can occur together in one act.

Study of K DH B

ح ل ف (ḥalaf) and ل ز م (lazima)

Ibn Fāris explains the principle of ح ل ف through adherence.

ل ز م concerns accompanying, adhering to, and not separating from something.

This proximity does not make the two roots identical.

In an alliance, people can bind themselves to one another; through an oath, a person commits himself to a statement.

Nevertheless, companionship and adherence cannot always be treated as oaths. Two people may cooperate for years without any oath between them.

Adherence thus explains a semantic connection among the derivatives; it is not the direct equivalent of حلف in every sentence.

Translation proposals for Forqan

In the twelve verbal Qur’anic occurrences, “to swear an oath” is the basic, natural equivalent.

يَحْلِفُونَ بِاللَّهِ translates as “they swear by The God.”

حَلَفْتُمْ in Al-Māʾidah 5:89 is “you swore,” while وَلَيَحْلِفُنَّ in Al-Tawbah 9:107 is “and they will certainly swear.”

فَيَحْلِفُونَ لَهُ in Al-Mujādilah 58:18 is “then they swear to Him”; later in the same verse, كَمَا يَحْلِفُونَ لَكُمْ is “just as they swear to you.”

حَلَّاف in Al-Qalam 68:10 is “a frequent swearer.” Using “a lying swearer” without a separate explanation merges the word’s meaning with an ethical judgement derived from the context.

In non-Qur’anic usage, حِلْف is “covenant” or “alliance,” حَلِيف an “ally,” حَالَفَ “entered an alliance,” تَحَالَفَ “entered a covenant with one another,” and اسْتَحْلَفَ “asked someone to swear.”

For مُحْلِف in the disputed-identification use, “something about which conflicting oaths are sworn” is an appropriate explanation.

Conclusion

The Arabic root ح ل ف concerns oaths and relationships established through an oath or covenant.

حَلَفَ means “to swear”; حِلْف can denote a covenant or alliance; حَلِيف describes an ally bound by covenant; and حَلَّاف someone who swears frequently.

Ibn Fāris proposes adherence as the family’s common principle. This connects swearing with commitment to a statement or covenant, without implying that everyone who swears actually remains faithful to his words.

The root occurs thirteen times in the Qur’an, mostly describing people who swear to justify conduct, establish affiliation, gain approval, or conceal the truth.

Nevertheless, Al-Māʾidah 5:89 distinguishes idle oaths from binding commitments and explains responsibility and expiation; the occurrence of an oath alone does not determine all the conditions of that responsibility. Al-Qalam 68:10 places frequent swearing alongside the reproachable traits of a particular figure.

Brief root meaning: Swearing an oath to affirm a claim or strengthen a commitment; حَلَّاف describes someone who swears frequently.

An oath itself, however, is neither conclusive proof of truth nor conclusive evidence of falsehood.

In Al-Mujādilah 58:14, the Qur’an states falsity and awareness through independent expressions. In Al-Tawbah 9:107, the context itself reveals the discrepancy between a claim of good intentions and harmful conduct.

This distinction is decisive for translation and understanding the root: swearing is a person’s speech act; the truth or falsity of its content and the person’s knowledge of reality are separate matters.

حلف should consequently not become “lying,” “acting hypocritically,” “breaking a covenant,” or “deceiving” in Qur’anic translation. Such conduct can accompany an oath but is not the root’s direct meaning.

For Forqan’s translation, “swearing an oath” and “someone who swears frequently” suit the Qur’anic forms. Commentary should also clarify who swears, the oath’s content and audience, and whether the Qur’an independently judges the person’s honesty or awareness.
