Arabic A F F concerns expressing impatience, displeasure, dislike, and aversion. أُفٍّ (uffin) is a short exclamation uttered when someone finds something unpleasant, is irritated by a situation, or disapproves of another’s words or conduct. It may arise from weariness and impatience, or express strong aversion to something considered wrong and objectionable.

The Qur’an uses this root three times. Al-Isrāʾ 17:23 forbids saying uff to one’s parents. In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:67, Ibrāhīm addresses the same expression to his people and what they worship apart from The God. In Al-Aḥqāf 46:17, a child denying resurrection says to both parents: أُفٍّ لَكُمَا (uffin lakumā), “Uff to you both!”

These uses show that the word itself does not mean sin, an abusive insult, or disrespect. Saying it to parents is forbidden in one setting; Ibrāhīm uses it to condemn idol worship in another. Its lexical meaning must therefore remain distinct from moral judgments concerning speaker and audience.

Uff does not always express mild irritation. Sometimes it conveys impatience; elsewhere, strong aversion and reproach. Tone, audience, and context establish the reaction’s intensity and direction.

1. Qur’anic forms and semantic core

A F F occurs three times in three ayahs, with one principal form in the reading transmitted by Ḥafṣ:

  • أُفٍّ (uffin) in Al-Isrāʾ 17:23.
  • أُفٍّ (uffin) in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:67.
  • أُفٍّ (uffin) in Al-Aḥqāf 46:17.

In all three, uffin is an independent expression of displeasure or aversion.

The root is geminate: its final two radicals are identical. In Qur’anic uffin, the two f letters appear as one letter bearing shaddah, the doubling sign.

Arabic records أَفَّ (affa) for saying uff and expressing impatience or dislike. Its imperfect forms include يَؤُفُّ (yaʾuffu) and يَئِفُّ (yaʾiffu).

أَفَّفَ (affafa) and تَأَفَّفَ (taʾaffafa) also concern expressing displeasure and uttering this reaction. The verbal nouns تَأْفِيف (taʾfīf) and تَأَفُّف (taʾaffuf) belong to the same field.

أَفَّاف (affāf) and أُفُوفَة (ufūfah) describe someone who frequently expresses impatience and keeps saying uff.

Forms such as affa, affafa, taʾaffafa, and affāf are known in Arabic but do not occur in the Qur’an in those forms.

Arabic grammar sometimes classifies uffin as an ism fiʿl, a verbal expression with nominal form, close in meaning to أَتَضَجَّرُ (ataḍajjaru), “I express impatience and displeasure.” Lexical explanations also describe it as a sound of dislike or disgust. In the familiar present-tense ism-fiʿl analysis, uffin has a fixed kasrah ending and an implicit first-person subject, anā, “I.” The kasrah is then part of its fixed form, with tanwīn marking indefiniteness; it should not be treated as an ordinary genitive noun merely because it ends in kasrah.

These explanations approach the word differently: one identifies grammatical function, the other its role in speech.

Unlike an ordinary sentence, uffin needs no explicit verb and subject to convey the speaker’s feeling. The exclamation itself can form a complete reaction to a person, action, or situation.

Its Qur’anic semantic core is:

Expressing displeasure, impatience, or aversion toward someone or something through a short exclamation revealing the speaker’s dislike and irritation.

The reaction ranges from slight irritation to strong reproach; its intensity is not a fixed part of the word’s meaning.

2. Early Arabic usage: aversion, dirt, and expressions of impatience

أُفٍّ (uffin): reacting to something unpleasant

Early Arabic uses uffin when confronting something unpleasant.

A person might react with dislike to an offensive smell, difficult work, irritating behavior, or an intolerable situation.

Arabic expressions include أُفٍّ لَهُ (uffin lahu), expressing displeasure or aversion toward someone, and أُفٍّ لِهَذَا (uffin li-hādhā), reacting against a matter.

The word itself does not explain why the speaker is displeased. The situation or subsequent speech supplies the reason.

This distinguishes uffin from words directly naming an action or moral judgment. The exclamation first conveys the speaker’s reaction.

Arabic also knows أُفٍّ وَتُفٍّ (uffin wa-tuffin). Tuffin accompanies uffin as a close expression emphasizing dislike and displeasure.

The pairing shows how spoken uff could combine with another expression to intensify or highlight the reaction.

Tuffin belongs to T F F, however, and is not an A F F derivative.

Did أُفٍّ (uffin) originally mean dirt?

Early lexicons also record dirt or grime as meanings of uff.

One explanation identifies uff as dirt around a fingernail and tuff as dirt beneath it. Another identifies uff as ear dirt and tuff as fingernail dirt.

Some lexicographers infer that people first said uff when encountering something dirty and repellent, then extended it to anything unpleasant or irritating.

The semantic connection is intelligible: dirt and unpleasant smells can provoke a short sound of aversion.

The disagreement about the supposed original referent nevertheless matters.

Was it ear dirt or fingernail dirt? Did the exclamation develop from a name for dirt, or the name for dirt from the sound of disgust?

Recording these meanings in dictionaries does not establish their historical sequence.

Dirt should therefore be presented as one recorded use, rather than the proven origin of every other use.

Nor does understanding the Qur’an require translating uffin directly as “dirt” or “filth.” All three ayahs use it to express dislike and displeasure.

Blowing dust away as an explanatory image

Another Arabic explanation connects uff with blowing away dust or ash.

In this account, someone blows unwanted material off themselves or a place to sit; the reaction then extends into a sound of rejection or dislike toward unpleasant things.

The image can clarify a connection between sound, bodily reaction, and impatience.

A conceivable explanation must nevertheless remain distinct from demonstrated historical etymology.

A sound resembling a puff of air does not itself establish that uff necessarily originated in that action.

Nor should blowing or removing dust be added to every Qur’anic occurrence.

The Qur’anic speech reaction is clear; knowing its exact historical origin is not necessary to understand the sentence.

Smallness and insignificance

Some Arabic dictionaries also record uff and أَفَف (afaf) for small quantity or slightness.

This can connect with belittling or dismissing something. Someone saying uff may find a person or action so objectionable as to consider it unworthy of attention.

That connection does not make uffin directly mean “a negligible thing” or “worthless” in every sentence.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:23 concerns neither quantity nor material value, but a person’s conduct toward parents.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:67, context conveys contempt for idol worship, while uffin remains an exclamation of aversion.

أُفٍّ (uffin) in a transmitted literary witness

A literary account attributed to a woman of Al-Nuʿmān ibn al-Mundhir’s family transmits:

فَأُفٍّ لِدُنْيَا لَا يَدُومُ نَعِيمُهَا
تَقَلَّبُ تَارَاتٍ بِنَا وَتَصَرَّفُ

fa-uffin li-dunyā lā yadūmu naʿīmuhā / taqallabu tārātin binā wa-taṣarrafu

“Uff to a world whose pleasures do not last; time and again it changes with us and shifts its condition.”

Uffin here does not address a human being.

The speaker reacts against worldly pleasures’ instability. The line thus illustrates a reaction to a situation or condition of life, rather than simply insulting another person.

Literary accounts differ in attribution, naming different women of Al-Nuʿmān’s family. The reported setting also falls after Islam’s beginnings. The line is therefore a transmitted witness to early Arabic usage, not conclusive evidence of pre-Qur’anic composition. A later account of an early Islamic scene is not itself a contemporary record. In the wording above, تَقَلَّبُ (taqallabu) and تَصَرَّفُ (taṣarrafu) omit one of the two initial tāʾ letters of tataqallabu and tataṣarrafu. No independently dated pre-Qur’anic witness for this exclamation was established in this investigation.

Different forms of أُفٍّ (uffin)

Arabic usage and Qur’anic readings transmit uff in several forms.

Ḥafṣ from ʿĀṣim and Nāfiʿ read أُفٍّ (uffin), with kasrah and tanwīn.

Ibn Kathīr and Ibn ʿĀmir read أُفَّ (uffa), with fatḥah and no tanwīn. Abū ʿAmr, Ḥamzah, Al-Kisāʾī, and Shuʿbah from ʿĀṣim read أُفِّ (uffi), with kasrah and no tanwīn. These are readings of the same three locations, not additional occurrences to count.

Lexicographers record further Arabic forms including أُفٌّ (uffun), أُفًّا (uffan), أُفْ (uf), and أُفَّة (uffah).

The differences concern pronunciation and grammatical construction. The basic expression of dislike and displeasure remains.

Neither the vowel nor tanwīn establishes that one form means only sadness and another only anger or contempt.

3. فَلَا تَقُلْ لَهُمَا أُفٍّ: honoring one’s parents

The root’s first Qur’anic occurrence is in Al-Isrāʾ:

وَقَضَىٰ رَبُّكَ أَلَّا تَعْبُدُوا إِلَّا إِيَّاهُ وَبِالْوَالِدَيْنِ إِحْسَانًا إِمَّا يَبْلُغَنَّ عِنْدَكَ الْكِبَرَ أَحَدُهُمَا أَوْ كِلَاهُمَا فَلَا تَقُلْ لَهُمَا أُفٍّ وَلَا تَنْهَرْهُمَا وَقُلْ لَهُمَا قَوْلًا كَرِيمًا

wa-qaḍā rabbuka allā taʿbudū illā iyyāhu wa-bi-l-wālidayni iḥsānan immā yablughanna ʿindaka al-kibara aḥaduhumā aw kilāhumā fa-lā taqul lahumā uffin wa-lā tanharhumā wa-qul lahumā qawlan karīman

“Your Lord has decreed that you worship none but Him and show goodness to parents. If one or both of them reach old age with you, do not say even ‘Uff’ to them, do not rebuke them, and speak to them with honorable, respectful words.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:23

Uffin exemplifies an expression of impatience or displeasure that a person might utter when dealing with parents.

The ayah particularly foregrounds their old age.

Parents at this stage may need more help. Daily tasks may become difficult, requiring their child to devote more time and effort to their care.

Weariness may arise, but the Qur’an forbids turning it into speech that hurts and belittles one’s parents.

Why does the ayah name أُفٍّ (uffin) separately?

The ayah does more than prohibit severe harm or harsh conduct.

Fa-lā taqul lahumā uffin addresses one of the shortest expressions of displeasure.

Someone may neither insult nor shout at a parent, yet respond to a request for help with an uff that treats the parent’s presence or need as an unwelcome burden.

In that setting, a brief reaction can send a clear message: “I am tired of you and your needs,” or “I do not want to care for you.”

The significance lies not in the expression’s brevity, but in what even a short reaction communicates to its recipient.

The ayah does not, however, equate every natural feeling of weariness or psychological pressure with saying uff.

Fa-lā taqul, “do not say,” directly concerns speech. It calls on a person not to express irritation through contemptuous treatment of parents.

This distinction does not diminish the command; it clarifies its direct scope.

أُفٍّ (uffin) and نَهْر (nahr) distinguished

After prohibiting uffin, the ayah says:

وَلَا تَنْهَرْهُمَا (wa-lā tanharhumā), “and do not rebuke them.”

Tanhar belongs to N H R. Here it concerns harshness, driving someone away through verbal aggression, and an overbearing, hurtful manner.

The prohibitions are close, but not identical.

Uffin can convey impatience or dislike through a single short exclamation.

Nahr is more overt conduct in which someone harshly drives away or rebukes another.

The ayah thus prohibits both a brief contemptuous reaction and openly harsh treatment.

This should not become an absolute scale in which uffin is always insignificant and nahr always more severe. A short expression can also cause deep hurt.

وَقُلْ لَهُمَا قَوْلًا كَرِيمًا: beyond refraining from disrespect

The two prohibitions are followed by a positive command:

وَقُلْ لَهُمَا قَوْلًا كَرِيمًا (wa-qul lahumā qawlan karīman), “and speak to them with honorable words.”

A person must do more than avoid uff and aggression: the command is to speak worthily and honor one’s parents.

The distinction matters.

Someone might utter no insult yet act coldly and indifferently. Avoiding hurtful words alone does not fulfill the entire meaning of goodness toward parents.

The ayah restricts harmful behavior while also prescribing a positive manner of speech.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:24 continues this with humility arising from mercy and a prayer for one’s parents.

Within this passage, uffin is one example of conduct incompatible with goodness and honoring parents.

Does honoring parents require unconditional obedience?

The prohibition of uffin should not be equated with absolute obedience to every parental demand.

In Luqmān 31:15, even when parents press someone to associate partners with The God, the Qur’an forbids obeying that demand while commanding kind companionship with them in worldly life.

Refusing a demand and disrespecting the person making it are therefore distinct matters.

One can disagree with a parent’s request and still speak respectfully.

Fa-lā taqul lahumā uffin does not authorize imposing every parental command on a child. It concerns conduct and expressions of displeasure within a relationship for which the Qur’an prescribes goodness and respect.

4. أُفٍّ لَكُمْ وَلِمَا تَعْبُدُونَ: Ibrāhīm’s aversion to idol worship

Al-Anbiyāʾ says:

أُفٍّ لَكُمْ وَلِمَا تَعْبُدُونَ مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ أَفَلَا تَعْقِلُونَ

uffin lakum wa-li-mā taʿbudūna min dūni allāhi a-fa-lā taʿqilūna

“Uff to you and to what you worship apart from The God! Will you not reason?”

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:67

This is the Qur’an’s second uffin occurrence.

Ibrāhīm speaks to people worshipping objects of devotion apart from The God.

The immediate context concerns idols unable to defend themselves or answer.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:66, he asks why they worship something apart from The God that can neither benefit nor harm them.

He then says: أُفٍّ لَكُمْ وَلِمَا تَعْبُدُونَ مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ (uffin lakum wa-li-mā taʿbudūna min dūni allāhi).

Uffin reacts to a practice and belief Ibrāhīm considers false.

The meaning is therefore more than impatience arising from fatigue. He expresses aversion to worshipping objects he considers unworthy of worship.

Is Ibrāhīm hurling insults?

Uffin lakum is forceful and reproachful, but should not be equated indiscriminately with every form of personal abuse.

Ibrāhīm explicitly reproaches his audience and their objects of worship.

The preceding ayah, however, supplies his reason: worshipping things he has shown can neither benefit nor harm.

He then asks:

أَفَلَا تَعْقِلُونَ (a-fa-lā taʿqilūna), “Will you not reason?”

His speech is thus more than an emotional shout. The aversion continues an argument about the supposed gods’ powerlessness.

Here uffin expresses aversion to falsehood and reproaches a religious practice.

This use shows that the expression is not objectionable or forbidden in every situation.

Otherwise its appearance in Ibrāhīm’s speech, within the argument the Qur’an reports, would require a different explanation.

The sound uff alone does not determine the speech’s value: its subject, the speaker’s situation, and his reasons also matter.

Is أُفٍّ (uffin) always mild?

Ibrāhīm’s use is an important witness for answering this question.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:23, uffin exemplifies one of the briefest expressions of impatience.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:67, the same exclamation accompanies explicit reproach of the audience and their objects of worship.

A brief linguistic form should not be confused with invariably mild meaning.

A short word can express weariness in one setting and strong aversion in another.

To preserve Ibrāhīm’s tone, “Uff to you and to what you worship” fits better than “I am a little displeased with you.”

There is no need to translate uffin directly as “Perish!” either. Destruction or a curse goes beyond aversion and requires independent support before being added.

أَفَلَا تَعْقِلُونَ (a-fa-lā taʿqilūna) is not from this root

Two superficially similar forms appear in the ayah:

أُفٍّ (uffin) and أَفَلَا (a-fa-lā).

They do not belong to the same root.

Uffin is an independent word grouped under A F F.

A-fa-lā combines the interrogative hamzah, the connective fa, and lā, the negative particle.

A-fa-lā taʿqilūna thus means “Will you not, then, reason?” and has no derivational connection with uffin.

This matters for counting: not every word beginning with the letters hamzah and fāʾ belongs to A F F.

How this differs from Ibrāhīm’s address to his father

In Maryam 19:42, Ibrāhīm addresses his father as يَا أَبَتِ (yā abati), “O my father,” and asks about worshipping something that neither hears, sees, nor benefits him.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:67, he addresses his people and what they worship.

The audience and manner of dialogue differ.

The conversation with his father foregrounds respectful address; the confrontation with idol worshippers expresses explicit aversion to their supposed gods.

The difference in tone should not become an absolute permission to belittle or attack every religious opponent.

Each scene reports particular speech in a specific setting. Applying it to other relationships requires considering ayahs on justice, good speech, and conduct toward opponents.

5. أُفٍّ لَكُمَا (uffin lakumā): a child denying resurrection

The root’s third occurrence is in Al-Aḥqāf:

وَالَّذِي قَالَ لِوَالِدَيْهِ أُفٍّ لَكُمَا أَتَعِدَانِنِي أَنْ أُخْرَجَ وَقَدْ خَلَتِ الْقُرُونُ مِنْ قَبْلِي وَهُمَا يَسْتَغِيثَانِ اللَّهَ وَيْلَكَ آمِنْ إِنَّ وَعْدَ اللَّهِ حَقٌّ فَيَقُولُ مَا هَٰذَا إِلَّا أَسَاطِيرُ الْأَوَّلِينَ

wa-alladhī qāla li-wālidayhi uffin lakumā a-taʿidāninī an ukhraja wa-qad khalati al-qurūnu min qablī wa-humā yastaghīthāni allāha waylaka āmin inna waʿda allāhi ḥaqqun fa-yaqūlu mā hādhā illā asāṭīru al-awwalīna

“And the one who said to his parents: Uff to you both! Do you promise me that I will be brought out again, though generations before me have passed away? The two seek The God’s help and say: Woe to you, believe! The God’s promise is indeed true. But he says: This is nothing but tales of the ancients.”

Al-Aḥqāf 46:17

Uffin lakumā directly addresses both parents.

Its main formal difference from uffin lakum in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:67 is the addressee pronoun.

Lakum addresses a plural audience; lakumā addresses two people.

In Al-Aḥqāf, those two are the parents introduced by لِوَالِدَيْهِ (li-wālidayhi) at the ayah’s beginning.

Uffin lakumā therefore directly concerns the child’s relationship with the parents.

What is disputed?

The parents ask their child to accept the promise of resurrection.

The child responds by questioning how he can be brought out again after death when so many generations before him have passed away.

He then calls their words أَسَاطِيرُ الْأَوَّلِينَ (asāṭīru al-awwalīna), “tales of the ancients.”

This is more than a family irritation or weariness with a conversation.

The child denies resurrection while answering his parents in a tone filled with aversion.

Uffin lakumā expresses impatience and aversion toward their words and invitation.

The root itself should not be defined as “denying resurrection,” however. The question and subsequent sentences establish that denial.

Uffin conveys the manner and tone of the child’s reaction.

Contrast with the preceding call to goodness

Al-Aḥqāf 46:15 enjoins goodness toward parents.

It recalls the mother’s difficulties in pregnancy and childbirth, then presents an example of a grateful, upright person.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:16 speaks of accepting such people’s good deeds and passing over their bad ones.

After this description, Al-Aḥqāf 46:17 presents a child saying uffin lakumā to his parents.

The sequence contrasts two kinds of conduct.

One side shows gratitude, goodness, and attention to parental rights; the other, a child rejecting the parents’ invitation with aversion and derision.

The contrast also connects with Al-Isrāʾ 17:23, which forbids addressing parents with this expression.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:17 thus gives a concrete example of speech incompatible with honoring parents.

Is every question about resurrection the same as this child’s conduct?

No. The question here cannot be detached from its tone and what follows.

He does not merely ask how resurrection occurs: he expresses aversion through uffin lakumā, then calls the divine promise ancient tales.

Seeking truth, asking how something happens, and contemptuous denial must remain distinct.

The ayah does not establish that every critical question about parental beliefs constitutes disrespect or disbelief.

At the same time, this particular speech’s content must not be obscured. The Qur’an describes someone rejecting resurrection and answering parents with impatience and contempt.

Two matters therefore coexist: the person’s position concerning belief and the manner of speaking.

Uffin concerns the latter, although the context connects them.

وَهُمَا يَسْتَغِيثَانِ اللَّهَ (wa-humā yastaghīthāni allāha)

The ayah continues with the parents seeking The God’s help and urging the child to believe, since The God’s promise is true.

This describes their response to the child’s denial.

For A F F, the difference between the two sides’ speech matters.

The child reveals aversion and impatience through uffin lakumā.

The parents seek The God’s help and call the child to belief.

Their speech also includes وَيْلَكَ (waylaka), an expression capable of conveying warning and deep concern.

Waylaka and uffin lakumā do not mean the same thing. Shared emotional intensity does not make them synonyms.

Nor does the ayah establish that every parental action is right simply because the actors are parents. It describes a particular situation in which a child rejects their invitation to accept the divine promise.

6. A F F and related roots and expressions

A F F (أُفٍّ, uffin) and Ḍ J R (ضَجَر, ḍajar)

Arabic ḍajar concerns impatience, vexation, and distress at a continuing situation.

Uffin is a familiar way to express such a state.

They are close, but approach the subject from different angles.

Ḍajar can name the state of impatience; uffin is an exclamation revealing it in speech.

Someone can also show impatience through behavior without saying uff.

Uffin can moreover go beyond impatience to strong aversion, as Ibrāhīm’s speech illustrates.

A F F (أُفٍّ, uffin) and K R H (كَرِهَ, kariha)

Study of K R H

Karāhah concerns finding something unpleasant or not wanting it.

Uffin can express that dislike without being identical with it.

Someone can be displeased by an action without voicing the feeling.

Saying uff, by contrast, usually reveals the speaker’s dislike or irritation.

The direct prohibition in Al-Isrāʾ 17:23 concerns uttering such a reaction. It accompanies a broader command of goodness toward parents, but uffin’s lexical meaning should not be extended to every inward state of dislike.

A F F (أُفٍّ, uffin) and N H R (نَهَرَ, nahara)

Study of N H R

Nahara, in human conduct, concerns harshly rebuking or driving someone away through verbal aggression.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:23 includes both expressions:

فَلَا تَقُلْ لَهُمَا أُفٍّ وَلَا تَنْهَرْهُمَا (fa-lā taqul lahumā uffin wa-lā tanharhumā).

Here uffin is a brief reaction of impatience or displeasure, while nahr describes harsh, aggressive treatment.

Both can hurt the recipient, but their manner of expression differs.

Prohibiting both shows that respect means more than avoiding shouting: even brief contemptuous expressions of irritation matter.

A F F (تَأَفُّف, taʾaffuf) and A DH Y (أَذَى, adhā)

Study of A DH Y

Adhā concerns hurt, distress, and harm.

Taʾaffuf concerns expressing impatience and dislike.

They can occur together. Saying uff to an elderly parent, for example, can cause hurt and distress.

Not all harm involves taʾaffuf, however. Belittling speech, harsh conduct, or depriving another of a right can also cause harm.

Nor does every uff necessarily mean harming another person. The literary witness reacts to worldly life, while Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:67 expresses aversion to idol worship.

Taʾaffuf is a kind of expressed reaction; adhā covers a broader field of hurt and harm.

A F F (أُفٍّ, uffin) and T F F (تُفٍّ, tuffin)

Uffin and tuffin occur together in the familiar expression uffin wa-tuffin.

In speech, both concern expressing dislike and displeasure.

Their roots nevertheless differ.

Uffin belongs to A F F, tuffin to T F F.

Lexical explanations connecting the two with ear or fingernail dirt are also inconsistent.

Their pairing does not establish that they were historically one word or that one certainly arose from the other.

A F F (أُفٍّ, uffin) and W Y L (وَيْل, wayl)

Study of W Y L

Arabic wayl expresses warning, a bad outcome, lament, or strong reproach.

Both expressions appear in Al-Aḥqāf 46:17.

The child says: uffin lakumā.

The parents say: وَيْلَكَ آمِنْ (waylaka āmin), “Woe to you, believe!”

Both carry strong emotion without sharing one meaning.

Uffin directly reveals the speaker’s dislike and aversion.

Wayl can warn of a bad outcome or express strong reproach.

Rendering both with one exclamation can therefore erase part of their difference.

Preserving uff as “Uff,” while translating waylaka as “Woe to you” or “Woe betide you,” better distinguishes them.

7. Speaker, audience, and context across the three Qur’anic uses

The three uffin occurrences clearly illustrate the difference between lexical meaning and the moral value of a use.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:23 forbids saying it to parents.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:67, Ibrāhīm uses it to express aversion toward his people and their supposed gods.

In Al-Aḥqāf 46:17, a child denying resurrection addresses the same word to his parents.

The exclamation’s core meaning remains: expressing dislike and displeasure.

Morally, however, the situations differ.

One places the word within a prohibition; another within Ibrāhīm’s argument; the third within a denier’s disrespectful address to parents.

Two important conclusions follow.

First, uffin cannot be inherently sinful in every use. That would fail to explain Ibrāhīm’s use adequately.

Second, his use does not establish that expressing aversion and contempt is commendable or permissible everywhere. The Qur’an itself forbids this expression when addressing parents.

A word with the same lexical meaning can thus have different moral values in different situations.

The root alone does not establish that difference; the audience, subject, reason, and setting do.

Nor should uffin always be considered insignificant simply because it is brief.

In a human relationship, the short exclamation can communicate contempt or disregard; in an argument, it can express decisive aversion to a false belief.

Meaning together with context is therefore the appropriate criterion, rather than a judgment based on the word’s brevity.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

For uffin, retaining the exclamation itself as “Uff” is suitable in these passages.

Explaining it as an expression of impatience, displeasure, or aversion makes its function accessible to English readers. Retaining the sound preserves its brevity and emotional character better than a long paraphrase.

Its intensity and direction must nevertheless be established contextually in translation and explanation.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:23

For fa-lā taqul lahumā uffin, the proposed rendering is:

“Then do not say even ‘Uff’ to them.”

“Even” conveys the prohibition’s reach to this short expression of displeasure; it does not correspond to a separate Arabic particle.

For a rendering closer to the sentence’s form:

“Then do not say ‘Uff’ to them.”

The explanation can clarify that this means refraining from expressing impatience and dislike in a way that violates parental dignity.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:67

For uffin lakum wa-li-mā taʿbudūna min dūni allāhi, the proposal is:

“Uff to you and to what you worship apart from The God!”

This preserves Ibrāhīm’s strong reproach without adding a curse or threat absent from the exclamation itself.

“How I recoil from you and your objects of worship!” conveys the explanatory meaning, but preserves less of the original’s brief exclamatory form.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:17

For uffin lakumā, the proposal is:

“Uff to you both!”

“Both” preserves the dual pronoun, showing the child addresses both parents.

“I am tired of you both!” can approximate the reaction in some English conversations, but may convey weariness alone and weaken the context’s aversion and contempt.

Retaining uff is therefore preferable here.

Unsuitable equivalents

Uffin should not invariably be translated as “insult,” “curse,” “punishment,” or “sin.”

“Ouch,” primarily a response to physical pain, is likewise unsuitable as a general equivalent.

“Alas” can convey surprise, sorrow, or lament, while a warning exclamation can blur uffin’s distinction from wayl in some contexts.

In these Qur’anic uses, “Uff” accompanied by a brief contextual explanation preserves the exclamation’s form and intended reaction.

9. Conclusion

Arabic A F F concerns expressing impatience, dislike, irritation, and aversion.

Uffin is a short exclamation revealing that someone finds a thing or another’s behavior unpleasant. Arabic explains this reaction through verbs such as affa and taʾaffafa, and nouns such as taʾaffuf.

Early sources also record ear or fingernail dirt, small quantities, and temporal expressions within this family. Their precise historical origins remain unclear; a hypothetical image such as blowing dust away should not establish their derivation. A recorded temporal expression such as عَلَىٰ إِفِّ ذَاكَ (ʿalā iffi dhāka), “at that very time,” is distinct from Qur’anic uffin.

In Ḥafṣ’s reading, the root occurs only three times, always as uffin.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:23 forbids expressing displeasure to parents. Even a short exclamation can be incompatible with goodness and respect in that setting.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:67, Ibrāhīm uses the same expression to declare aversion to idol worship, showing that the word itself does not inherently mean sin or forbidden conduct.

In Al-Aḥqāf 46:17, a child denying resurrection answers his parents this way. Uffin reveals his disrespectful tone alongside denial of the divine promise; denial of resurrection is not the word’s lexical meaning.

These uses also show that uffin is not invariably mild. It can convey simple impatience or strong aversion, with intensity and value established contextually.

Forqan’s final definition of A F F:

“Expressing impatience, dislike, irritation, or aversion through a short exclamation revealing the speaker’s displeasure toward a person, action, or situation. Qur’anic uffin expresses this spoken reaction, sometimes as impatience and sometimes as strong reproach and aversion. The word is neither inherently an abusive insult nor invariably sinful: audience, subject, and context establish its intensity and moral value.”
