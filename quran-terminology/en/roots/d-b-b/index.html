Qur’anic root د ب ب — D B B

Moving, creeping and the moving creature: from ants and human footsteps to the range of living creatures, their provision and two different earthly creatures in the Qur’an

The three-letter Arabic root د ب ب (dāl bāʾ bāʾ) concerns movement and progress over the ground. In older usage, دَبَّ (dabba) particularly expresses quiet, slow movement or closely spaced steps. It can describe an ant moving over soil, a child learning to walk or an elderly person taking slow steps.

دَابَّة (dābbah) denotes a creature that moves. Its broad Arabic use can encompass various animals and even humans, though some customary uses restrict it to quadrupeds and riding animals. In the Qur’an, it does not name only one particular animal: it describes earthly creatures, beings moving in different ways, humans in an ethical judgement, a creature eating Solomon’s staff and a creature brought forth from the earth in another verse.

This range clarifies an important point: dābbah is fundamentally a description of a moving creature, rather than the name of a narrow zoological group. Accompanying expressions identify the creature’s kind, habitat, behaviour and place in the verse.

The root occurs 18 times in 18 Qur’anic verses: 14 times as singular دَابَّة (dābbah) and four times as plural دَوَابّ (dawābb). All occurrences are nominal; verbs such as دَبَّ (dabba) and يَدِبُّ (yadibbu) do not occur in the Qur’an. Differences among دَابَّةٍ (dābbatin), دَابَّةً (dābbatan), دَابَّةُ (dābbatu), الدَّوَابِّ (al-dawābbi) and الدَّوَابُّ (al-dawābbu) concern definiteness or grammatical role, rather than separate roots or independent derivatives.

1. The root’s meaning and early Arabic usage

Dabba: gentle, continuous movement

دَبَّ يَدِبُّ دَبِيبًا (dabba yadibbu dabīban) concerns moving, walking or creeping gently. It can describe ants, small animals, humans, groups of people and even the gradual advance of something through a body or environment.

Ibn Fāris places movement over the earth at the root’s centre, describing a motion lighter than ordinary walking. This fits many older physical uses, but should not be restricted to crawling on the belly or moving extremely slowly.

دَبَّ النَّمْلُ (dabba al-namlu) describes ants moving. Ants have legs and walk, yet Arabic calls their movement dabb. Dabb therefore does not imply having no legs.

دَبَّ الشَّيْخُ (dabba al-shaykhu) describes an elderly man moving slowly and with difficulty. He is not crawling on his belly: the manner and rhythm of his steps are at issue.

دَبَّ الْقَوْمُ إِلَى الْعَدُوِّ (dabba al-qawmu ilā al-ʿaduwwi) means that a group advanced slowly and cautiously towards the enemy. The movement may be quiet to avoid revealing their presence.

These uses share movement from one place to another, though its speed and precise manner differ. Lexical accounts explain the forms’ meanings but do not independently establish when every form was current before revelation.

Evidence from poetry before Islam

The pre-Islamic Arab poet Aws ibn Ḥajar says in one of his poems:

«وَإِنَّكُمَا يَا ابْنَيْ جَنَابٍ وُجِدْتُمَا
كَمَنْ دَبَّ يَسْتَخْفِي وَفِي الْحَلْقِ جُلْجُلُ»

“You two, sons of Janāb, have proved like someone creeping in concealment with a bell at his neck.”

The poet compares the two to someone seeking to move secretly, while a bell around his neck sounds and reveals his presence.

دَبَّ (dabba) expresses slow, cautious movement; يَسْتَخْفِي (yastakhfī), “seeks concealment,” identifies his intention to remain hidden.

Secrecy is therefore not an independent meaning of dabba itself. It comes from the second verb and the image of the bell.

The line shows that pre-Islamic poetic language could use dabba of humans, rather than only the movement of insects or quadrupeds.

Elsewhere in the same poem, Aws ibn Ḥajar describes someone advancing slowly in another scene:

«يَدُبُّ إِلَيْهِ خَاتِيًا يَدَّرِي لَهُ
لِيَفْقُرَهُ فِي رَمْيِهِ وَهْوَ يُرْسِلُ»

The passage concerns moving cautiously and approaching a target. يَدُبُّ (yadibbu) expresses movement; the image’s other words complete its sense of slowness or caution.

These two examples from one poet show that older Arabic could use dabb for human movement, particularly someone advancing slowly or cautiously.

From bodily movement to gradual spread

Lexical sources record دَبَّ السُّقْمُ فِي الْجِسْمِ (dabba al-suqmu fī al-jismi) for illness spreading through the body. دَبَّ الْبِلَى فِي الثَّوْبِ (dabba al-bilā fī al-thawbi) can likewise describe wear gradually advancing through cloth.

Illness and wear have no legs like humans and animals. Movement is used figuratively for a condition spreading gently and gradually.

Arabic also has expressions for malicious gossip and discord gradually spreading among people. دَبَّتْ عَقَارِبُهُ (dabbat ʿaqāribuhu), literally “his scorpions crept,” describes someone whose harm and tale-bearing circulate among others.

These extensions are understandable. Just as a creature moves quietly over the earth, illness, wear or discord can gradually spread through an environment.

These figurative uses should not change dābbah’s direct Qur’anic meaning. When the Qur’an discusses earthly creatures, their food or their manner of movement, it concerns living beings, rather than illness or social discord.

How is dābbah formed?

Dābbah belongs to the family of dabba and is morphologically related to an active description, “one that moves.”

The second and third root consonants are both bāʾ. This is a doubled root: in forms such as دَبَّ (dabba) and دَابَّة (dābbah), the two bāʾ consonants ordinarily appear as one letter marked with shaddah.

Dābbah is grammatically feminine, but this does not necessarily mean a female animal. It can describe male or female creatures.

Its plural is دَوَابّ (dawābb). Qur’anic الدَّوَابّ (al-dawābb) is consequently the plural of dābbah, rather than a word from another root.

Customary Arabic sometimes predominantly uses dābbah for a riding animal, such as a horse or mule. This restriction in everyday usage is real, but does not make the word originally exclusive to riding quadrupeds.

Broader uses remain present in the Qur’an.

2. Dābbah: the range of moving creatures in creation

Wa-baththa fīhā min kulli dābbatin

Al-Baqarah 2:164 says:

«وَبَثَّ فِيهَا مِنْ كُلِّ دَابَّةٍ»

“And dispersed throughout it creatures of every kind.”

(Al-Baqarah 2:164, excerpt)

The same construction occurs in Luqmān 31:10:

«وَبَثَّ فِيهَا مِنْ كُلِّ دَابَّةٍ»

“And dispersed throughout it creatures of every kind.”

(Luqmān 31:10, excerpt)

بَثَّ (baththa) concerns dispersing and spreading; دَابَّة (dābbah) names the creature dispersed.

The roots should remain distinct. Bathth expresses the action of spreading; D B B concerns the moving creature.

In both verses, فِيهَا (fīhā), “in it,” refers to the earth. The subject is therefore creatures dispersed throughout the earth.

مِنْ كُلِّ دَابَّةٍ (min kulli dābbatin), “creatures of every kind,” expresses their breadth, but does not list all species or explain how each originated.

Al-Jāthiyah 45:4 says:

«وَفِي خَلْقِكُمْ وَمَا يَبُثُّ مِنْ دَابَّةٍ آيَاتٌ لِقَوْمٍ يُوقِنُونَ»

“And in your creation and the creatures He disperses are signs for people who are certain.”

(Al-Jāthiyah 45:4)

Human creation and the dispersal of creatures occur alongside one another.

Naming humans separately does not categorically exclude them from dābbah’s lexical range. The Qur’an may name humans separately for a verse’s particular subject, even where a broader word can linguistically include them.

Kulla dābbatin min māʾin: different modes of movement

Al-Nūr 24:45 says:

«وَاللَّهُ خَلَقَ كُلَّ دَابَّةٍ مِنْ مَاءٍ فَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ يَمْشِي عَلَىٰ بَطْنِهِ وَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ يَمْشِي عَلَىٰ رِجْلَيْنِ وَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ يَمْشِي عَلَىٰ أَرْبَعٍ يَخْلُقُ اللَّهُ مَا يَشَاءُ إِنَّ اللَّهَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ قَدِيرٌ»

“And The God created every moving creature from water. Some move on their bellies, some walk on two legs and some on four. The God creates whatever He wills. Indeed, The God has power over all things.”

(Al-Nūr 24:45)

This verse is especially important for understanding dābbah’s range.

After كُلَّ دَابَّةٍ (kulla dābbatin), “every moving creature,” it mentions three modes of movement: on the belly, on two legs and on four.

The construction would make little sense if dābbah were lexically restricted to quadrupeds. The verse explicitly includes creatures moving in different ways.

Nor can dābbah be restricted to creeping or crawling animals. A creature moving on two legs belongs to the same collection.

Are humans also dābbah?

Humans move over the earth and can accordingly fall within dābbah’s broad Arabic range.

Mentioning movement on two legs in Al-Nūr 24:45 allows this inclusion, although مَنْ يَمْشِي عَلَىٰ رِجْلَيْنِ (man yamshī ʿalā rijlayni), “one who walks on two legs,” is not exclusively human and can also include birds and other bipeds.

Other verses clarify the range further. Al-Anfāl 8:22 and Al-Anfāl 8:55 use dawābb of groups of humans.

Restricting dābbah to nonhuman animals in every verse is therefore inconsistent with actual Qur’anic usage.

Conversely, humans need not be intended whenever dābbah occurs. In Sabaʾ 34:14, it describes a creature eating Solomon’s staff. In that context, the behaviour identifies the kind of creature.

Does the verse list every mode of movement?

Al-Nūr 24:45 names movement on the belly, two legs and four. Nature also contains creatures with more legs or other modes of movement in different environments.

The verse itself continues يَخْلُقُ اللَّهُ مَا يَشَاءُ (yakhluqu Allāhu mā yashāʾu), “The God creates whatever He wills.” Its three examples should therefore not be made a denial of every other mode of movement.

مِنْ مَاءٍ (min māʾin), “from water,” concerns the origin of creation. D B B itself supplies no biological theory about creatures’ bodily composition.

Dābbah means a moving creature. How it is created is the subject of a separate expression in the verse.

Earthly creatures and birds: neighbouring descriptions

Al-Anʿām 6:38 says:

«وَمَا مِنْ دَابَّةٍ فِي الْأَرْضِ وَلَا طَائِرٍ يَطِيرُ بِجَنَاحَيْهِ إِلَّا أُمَمٌ أَمْثَالُكُمْ مَا فَرَّطْنَا فِي الْكِتَابِ مِنْ شَيْءٍ ثُمَّ إِلَىٰ رَبِّهِمْ يُحْشَرُونَ»

“There is no creature on the earth, nor any bird flying with its two wings, except that they are communities like you. We have neglected nothing in the Book; then to their Lord they will be gathered.”

(Al-Anʿām 6:38)

دَابَّةٍ فِي الْأَرْضِ (dābbatin fī al-arḍi), “a creature on the earth,” and طَائِرٍ يَطِيرُ بِجَنَاحَيْهِ (ṭāʾirin yaṭīru bi-janāḥayhi), “a bird flying with its two wings,” occur separately.

The distinction draws attention to movement and location: creatures on the ground and birds flying through the air.

It does not establish that a bird can never linguistically be called dābbah. Birds can also walk on the earth and then have terrestrial movement.

The verse introduces two groups alongside one another, rather than constructing a biological classification with no possible overlap.

Umamun amthālukum: communities like you

إِلَّا أُمَمٌ أَمْثَالُكُمْ (illā umamun amthālukum), “except that they are communities like you,” shows that the Qur’an does not present living creatures merely as disconnected, scattered objects.

They are communities and groups with their place in creation.

أَمْثَالُكُمْ (amthālukum), “like you,” does not make every human and animal characteristic identical. The verse does not say that all creatures speak equally, have the same legal responsibility or possess identical cognitive capacities.

The conclusion also describes their gathering to their Lord. This goes beyond dābbah’s lexical meaning and requires the verse itself and other passages about the final gathering.

Dābbah identifies the moving creature; community and gathering are meanings attributed to it in this particular context.

3. Creatures, provision and The God’s power

Wa-mā min dābbatin fī al-arḍi illā ʿalā Allāhi rizquhā

Hūd 11:6 says:

«وَمَا مِنْ دَابَّةٍ فِي الْأَرْضِ إِلَّا عَلَى اللَّهِ رِزْقُهَا وَيَعْلَمُ مُسْتَقَرَّهَا وَمُسْتَوْدَعَهَا كُلٌّ فِي كِتَابٍ مُبِينٍ»

“There is no creature on the earth whose provision does not rest with The God. He knows its settled place and its place of deposit. All is [recorded] in a clear Book.”

(Hūd 11:6)

Dābbah here is associated with provision and The God’s knowledge of creatures’ circumstances.

وَمَا مِنْ دَابَّةٍ (wa-mā min dābbatin), “there is no creature,” is general and unrestricted to one kind of animal.

The feminine singular pronoun in رِزْقُهَا (rizquhā), “its provision,” refers back to dābbah. It agrees with the grammatically feminine word, rather than making every creature female.

The verse concerns creatures’ provision in relation to The God, but dābbah itself does not mean “one receiving provision.” The moving creature is the subject; provision is a separate concept attributed to it.

Nor should the verse become a claim that no living creature ever dies of hunger. It concerns provision and The God’s knowledge, without denying deaths, food shortages or the loss of animal populations.

Creatures that do not carry their provision

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:60 says:

«وَكَأَيِّنْ مِنْ دَابَّةٍ لَا تَحْمِلُ رِزْقَهَا اللَّهُ يَرْزُقُهَا وَإِيَّاكُمْ وَهُوَ السَّمِيعُ الْعَلِيمُ»

“And how many a creature does not carry its provision! The God provides for it and for you, and He is the Hearing, the Knowing.”

(Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:60)

Here dābbah concerns creatures that do not have their provision with them, or do not store and carry it.

The expression begins وَكَأَيِّنْ مِنْ (wa-ka-ayyin min), “and how many.” It therefore does not say that no creature carries or stores food.

Some animals gather and store food; others do not. An unjustified generalisation should not put the verse in conflict with that reality.

It speaks of many creatures without provision prepared and carried with them, then recalls The God providing for them and for humans.

This does not change dābbah’s meaning, but shows that moving creatures differ in how they obtain food.

Mā min dābbatin illā huwa ākhidhun bi-nāṣiyatihā

In Hūd 11:56, Hūd says:

«إِنِّي تَوَكَّلْتُ عَلَى اللَّهِ رَبِّي وَرَبِّكُمْ مَا مِنْ دَابَّةٍ إِلَّا هُوَ آخِذٌ بِنَاصِيَتِهَا إِنَّ رَبِّي عَلَىٰ صِرَاطٍ مُسْتَقِيمٍ»

“Indeed, I have placed my trust in The God, my Lord and your Lord. There is no creature whose forelock He does not grasp. Indeed, my Lord is upon a straight path.”

(Hūd 11:56)

آخِذٌ بِنَاصِيَتِهَا (ākhidhun bi-nāṣiyatihā), “grasping its forelock,” depicts holding and controlling. نَاصِيَة (nāṣiyah) concerns the front of the head or forehead hair; grasping it can express control over another in Arabic.

This image does not mean that every creature without exception has forehead hair identical to that of humans or horses.

Dābbah itself does not mean a being under domination. Being within The God’s power comes from ākhidhun bi-nāṣiyatihā.

Facing his people, Hūd places his trust in The God and uses the image to express The God’s encompassing power.

Dābbah’s range thus connects with divine power, while its lexical meaning remains a moving creature.

4. Creatures and ethical responsibility: are humans included in dawābb?

Inna sharra al-dawābbi ʿinda Allāhi

Al-Anfāl 8:22 says:

«إِنَّ شَرَّ الدَّوَابِّ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ الصُّمُّ الْبُكْمُ الَّذِينَ لَا يَعْقِلُونَ»

“Indeed, the worst of moving creatures in The God’s sight are the deaf and mute who do not use reason.”

(Al-Anfāl 8:22)

الدَّوَابِّ (al-dawābbi) is dābbah’s plural, but the subject here is not nonhuman animals.

The preceding verses describe people who say they have heard while failing to respond to what they have heard.

الصُّمُّ الْبُكْمُ (al-ṣummu al-bukmu), “the deaf and mute,” should not therefore be understood here as condemning people who are physically deaf or unable to speak.

It accompanies الَّذِينَ لَا يَعْقِلُونَ (alladhīna lā yaʿqilūna), “who do not use reason,” within a context of failing to hear and accept truth.

The judgement concerns the group’s cognitive and ethical behaviour, rather than physical disability.

Dawābb can therefore include humans in Qur’anic usage. That does not make D B B inherently derogatory.

Disparagement and condemnation come from شَرّ (sharr), “worst,” and the following descriptions, rather than dawābb alone.

Alladhīna kafarū fa-hum lā yuʾminūna

Al-Anfāl 8:55 says:

«إِنَّ شَرَّ الدَّوَابِّ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا فَهُمْ لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ»

“Indeed, the worst of moving creatures in The God’s sight are those who have committed kufr and do not believe.”

(Al-Anfāl 8:55)

The following verse says:

«الَّذِينَ عَاهَدْتَ مِنْهُمْ ثُمَّ يَنْقُضُونَ عَهْدَهُمْ فِي كُلِّ مَرَّةٍ وَهُمْ لَا يَتَّقُونَ»

“Those among them with whom you made a covenant, who then break their covenant every time and do not practise taqwa.”

(Al-Anfāl 8:56)

Dawābb here describes people whose behaviour is condemned.

Al-Anfāl 8:55 speaks of kufr—rejection and denial—and not believing. The following verse identifies the repeated covenant-breaking of a group among them and their lack of taqwa, mindful care to avoid wrongdoing.

The two verses cannot be detached from context to produce a general judgement of physical or human worthlessness against everyone who does not share a religious community’s designation.

Nor does dābbah itself mean someone committing kufr, sin or covenant-breaking. Separate expressions in the verses establish those characteristics.

These uses both establish the word’s human range and show that the Qur’an’s ethical judgement concerns behaviour, rather than an intrinsic meaning of being a moving creature.

Wa-al-dawābbu wa-kathīrun mina al-nāsi

Al-Ḥajj 22:18 says:

«أَلَمْ تَرَ أَنَّ اللَّهَ يَسْجُدُ لَهُ مَنْ فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَمَنْ فِي الْأَرْضِ وَالشَّمْسُ وَالْقَمَرُ وَالنُّجُومُ وَالْجِبَالُ وَالشَّجَرُ وَالدَّوَابُّ وَكَثِيرٌ مِنَ النَّاسِ وَكَثِيرٌ حَقَّ عَلَيْهِ الْعَذَابُ وَمَنْ يُهِنِ اللَّهُ فَمَا لَهُ مِنْ مُكْرِمٍ إِنَّ اللَّهَ يَفْعَلُ مَا يَشَاءُ»

“Have you not seen that to The God prostrate whoever is in the heavens and whoever is on the earth, the sun, the moon, the stars, the mountains, the trees, the moving creatures and many people? For many, too, punishment has become due. Whoever The God humiliates has none to honour him. Indeed, The God does whatever He wills.”

(Al-Ḥajj 22:18)

Al-dawābb appears alongside trees, mountains and other beings, after which كَثِيرٌ مِنَ النَّاسِ (kathīrun mina al-nāsi), “many people,” is named separately.

This shows humans being addressed separately in this context with regard to their different responsibilities and conditions.

As Al-Anfāl 8:22 and Al-Anfāl 8:55 show, this does not mean humans can never linguistically belong within dābbah’s range.

Al-Ḥajj concerns prostration and creatures’ relationship with The God. The meaning of human, animal, tree and mountain prostration cannot be derived from D B B alone.

The word identifies the moving creature; prostration is a relationship attributed to it in the verse.

The variety of creatures and their colours

Fāṭir 35:28 says:

«وَمِنَ النَّاسِ وَالدَّوَابِّ وَالْأَنْعَامِ مُخْتَلِفٌ أَلْوَانُهُ كَذَٰلِكَ إِنَّمَا يَخْشَى اللَّهَ مِنْ عِبَادِهِ الْعُلَمَاءُ إِنَّ اللَّهَ عَزِيزٌ غَفُورٌ»

“And people, moving creatures and livestock likewise have diverse colours. Of The God’s servants, only those with knowledge fear Him with awe. Indeed, The God is Mighty, Forgiving.”

(Fāṭir 35:28)

People, dawābb and livestock are named alongside one another.

الْأَنْعَام (al-anʿām) concerns livestock such as camels, cattle and sheep. Through movement they can belong to the general range of moving creatures, while the verse names them separately for their particular standing.

Naming livestock separately does not restrict dābbah’s lexical definition to animals other than livestock.

Nor should dawābb here be treated exclusively as riding animals. The subject is diversity of colours and creatures; the expression has a broader range.

5. Two different uses of dābbat al-arḍ

A creature that speaks to people

Al-Naml 27:82 says:

«وَإِذَا وَقَعَ الْقَوْلُ عَلَيْهِمْ أَخْرَجْنَا لَهُمْ دَابَّةً مِنَ الْأَرْضِ تُكَلِّمُهُمْ أَنَّ النَّاسَ كَانُوا بِآيَاتِنَا لَا يُوقِنُونَ»

“And when the word comes to pass against them, We will bring forth for them a creature from the earth that speaks to them, [saying] that people were not certain of Our signs.”

(Al-Naml 27:82)

دَابَّةً مِنَ الْأَرْضِ (dābbatan mina al-arḍi) is indefinite: a creature from the earth.

أَخْرَجْنَا (akhrajnā) expresses bringing it forth; تُكَلِّمُهُمْ (tukallimuhum) in the prevalent recitation means speaking to people.

Dābbah is therefore not necessarily a being without communication or speech. Here the Qur’an describes a creature speaking to people.

The verse itself does not specify its bodily form, size, exact place of emergence or other characteristics.

Exegetical accounts transmit various descriptions of its shape, place of appearance and actions. Those accounts should not replace dābbah’s lexical meaning or the Qur’an’s explicit description.

Some transmitted readings also have تَكْلِمُهُمْ (taklimuhum), meaning wounding or marking them. This differs from the prevalent tukallimuhum, concerning speech. The report must remain distinct from the prevalent recitation: transmission alone does not establish equal standing for every form within the recitational tradition.

That difference concerns the verb ك ل م (K L M), rather than D B B. In either case, dābbah names the creature the Qur’an describes being brought forth.

A creature that eats Solomon’s staff

Sabaʾ 34:14 says:

«فَلَمَّا قَضَيْنَا عَلَيْهِ الْمَوْتَ مَا دَلَّهُمْ عَلَىٰ مَوْتِهِ إِلَّا دَابَّةُ الْأَرْضِ تَأْكُلُ مِنْسَأَتَهُ فَلَمَّا خَرَّ تَبَيَّنَتِ الْجِنُّ أَنْ لَوْ كَانُوا يَعْلَمُونَ الْغَيْبَ مَا لَبِثُوا فِي الْعَذَابِ الْمُهِينِ»

“Then, when We decreed death for him, nothing indicated his death to them except a creature of the earth eating his staff. When he fell, it became clear to the jinn that, had they known the unseen, they would not have remained in the humiliating suffering.”

(Sabaʾ 34:14)

دَابَّةُ الْأَرْضِ (dābbatu al-arḍi) here denotes a creature eating Solomon’s staff.

Older lexical and exegetical explanations generally identify it as الْأَرَضَة (al-araḍah), a wood-eating insect commonly identified today as a termite.

That explanation fits the described behaviour: a creature eats wood, gradually undermining the staff’s firmness.

The Qur’an itself does not give a scientific name or precise insect species. “Termite” is therefore an established identification, rather than the direct meaning of D B B.

The creature’s role comes from تَأْكُلُ (taʾkulu), “eats,” and its object مِنْسَأَتَهُ (minsaʾatahu), “his staff.” Minsaʾah means a staff and belongs to another root.

Are the two earthly creatures identical?

Similar wording in Al-Naml 27:82 and Sabaʾ 34:14 should not lead us to identify them as one creature.

Al-Naml has dābbatan mina al-arḍi: a creature from the earth, brought forth and speaking to people.

Sabaʾ has dābbatu al-arḍi: a creature of the earth eating Solomon’s staff.

The former concerns a word coming to pass and an encounter with people who lacked certainty about the signs. The latter concerns Solomon’s death and the exposure of the jinn’s ignorance of the unseen.

Their constructions and contexts genuinely differ. What they share is dābbah and its connection to the earth.

Just as dābbah describes many different creatures elsewhere, its referents in these two verses should not be assumed identical without evidence.

In Solomon’s story, an event involving a small wood-eating creature reveals a truth about ignorance of the unseen. Dābbah itself does not mean “revealer of the unseen”; that outcome comes from the complete narrative.

6. Related roots and suggested translations

D B B (dabba) and M SH Y (mashā)

مَشَىٰ (mashā), from م ش ي (mīm shīn yāʾ), concerns walking and moving. Older dabba likewise concerns movement over the ground, but can foreground gentle, gradual motion.

Al-Nūr 24:45 mentions dābbah, then uses يَمْشِي (yamshī), “moves” or “walks,” for the movement of some creatures.

Their occurrence together shows that dābbah and walking are distinct concepts. Dābbah names the moving being; yamshī expresses something it does.

Dābbah should therefore not always be translated as merely “one who walks,” just as every Qur’anic use of walking does not belong to D B B.

D B B (dabba) and S AYN Y (saʿā)

سَعَىٰ (saʿā), from س ع ي (sīn ʿayn yāʾ), can concern movement, effort and pursuing an aim.

By contrast, dabba’s primary use particularly concerns movement over the earth, without making purpose or conscious effort an invariable part of its meaning.

An ant that moves is dābbah, but that description alone supplies no particular conclusion about its intentions or conscious effort.

Conversely, someone may strive at something without changing location, as in working to settle a dispute.

D B B (dābbah) and N AYN M (anʿām)

أَنْعَام (anʿām), from ن ع م (nūn ʿayn mīm), concerns a recognised group of livestock in the Qur’an. Dābbah has a broader field and can include animals outside that group.

Fāṭir 35:28 names both. This distinction can foreground different groups, without implying that livestock are not moving creatures.

Translate anʿām as “livestock”; dawābb may be “moving creatures” or “animals,” according to context.

D B B (dabba) and D ʾ B (daʾaba)

د أ ب (dāl hamzah bāʾ), with middle hamzah, is a separate root. دَأْب (daʾb) concerns habit, practice and persistence in an activity.

Yūsuf 12:47 says:

«تَزْرَعُونَ سَبْعَ سِنِينَ دَأَبًا»

“You will cultivate for seven years continuously, according to established practice.”

(Yūsuf 12:47, excerpt)

دَأَبًا (daʾaban) belongs to D ʾ B and should not be identified with D B B.

دَائِبَيْنِ (dāʾibayni) in Ibrāhīm 14:33, concerning the sun and moon continuing their courses, likewise belongs to D ʾ B rather than D B B.

Sound resemblance between daʾb and dabb does not establish identical meanings or derivation.

D B B (dabba) and D B R (dabara)

د ب ر (dāl bāʾ rāʾ) concerns the rear or back and, in some constructions, directing and ordering.

Qur’anic يُدَبِّرُ (yudabbiru) belongs to this root and concerns directing affairs.

Dabba, however, concerns movement. The roots share their first two consonants, but differ in their third.

Similar letters therefore do not connect dābbah with تَدْبِير (tadbīr), directing affairs, or allow that meaning to be inserted into the root merely because creatures move.

Suggested translations for Forqan

The basic rendering “moving creature” is appropriate for dābbah. It conveys the broad Arabic range better than “quadruped” or “riding animal.”

In Al-Baqarah 2:164, Luqmān 31:10, Al-Jāthiyah 45:4, Hūd 11:6 and Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:60, “creature” or “moving creature” is natural and precise.

In Al-Nūr 24:45, “every moving creature” fits its inclusion of movement on the belly, two legs and four.

In Al-Anʿām 6:38, “no creature on the earth, nor any bird flying with its two wings” preserves the two descriptions.

In Al-Anfāl 8:22 and Al-Anfāl 8:55, translate dawābb as “moving creatures,” rather than “quadrupeds.” Humans are being judged; “quadrupeds” could introduce a new disparaging comparison in place of the original word.

In Sabaʾ 34:14, use “a creature of the earth eating his staff,” then explain that termites are the established identification in lexical accounts.

In Al-Naml 27:82, retain “a creature from the earth.” Its shape and identity do not follow from dābbah itself.

For al-dawābb in Fāṭir 35:28 and Al-Ḥajj 22:18, “moving creatures” or “animals” is appropriate. Since other uses include humans, however, the root should not be permanently defined as “nonhuman animal.”

“Moving creature” is somewhat more explicit than “animal,” but useful in a root study: it retains movement without unjustifiably restricting the word to one group.

7. Conclusion

D B B concerns moving over the earth. Older dabba often expresses gentle, gradual movement. Its family includes dābbah, a creature that moves.

All Qur’anic occurrences are dābbah and its plural dawābb. They concern creatures spreading across the earth, their different movements, provision and settled places, their relationship to The God’s power, varied colours, prostration and human ethical responsibility.

Al-Anfāl 8:22 and Al-Anfāl 8:55 show that dābbah is not restricted to nonhuman animals. These concern humans whose conduct is condemned. Dābbah itself does not mean disparagement or sin; the negative judgement comes from descriptions and context.

Al-Nūr 24:45 likewise shows that dābbah does not name creatures with a fixed number of legs. It describes movement on bellies, two legs and four. The word should therefore not be restricted to reptiles or quadrupeds.

In Al-Naml 27:82, dābbah is brought forth from the earth and, in the prevalent recitation, speaks to people. In Sabaʾ 34:14, it eats Solomon’s staff. A shared name does not make the two beings identical; their particular identity requires the behaviour and context of each verse.

Generally, dābbah identifies a moving creature. That name alone does not determine intelligence, faith, ethical value, number of legs, food or manner of creation.

D B B’s semantic core is movement and progress over the earth, with an established association with gentle, gradual motion. Dābbah names a moving creature: a broad designation whose particular kind and qualities must be understood from sentence and context.

This explains ants’ and humans’ movement in older language and the Qur’an’s varied uses of dābbah and dawābb, without restricting all moving creatures to one animal type, manner of movement or ethical standing.
