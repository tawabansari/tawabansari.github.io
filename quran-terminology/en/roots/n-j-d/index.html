Elevated ground and a clear path: from high terrain to the two ways before the human being

1. Introducing the root and its semantic core

Arabic N J D has several branches, principally elevated ground, a raised or clear path, courage and strength, and assistance in difficulty.

Geographical najd names land higher than its surroundings. It need not be a tall mountain or difficult summit: it can be broad, level terrain elevated relative to neighboring plains or lowlands.

The word also names a path across high ground. Such a path may be visible to a traveler; Arabic accordingly uses najd for a clear, distinct way as well as an elevated one.

Another branch concerns courage, strength, and confronting difficulty. Najdah can mean bravery or ability, but also hardship, severity, and distress in other uses. Istanjada means seeking help.

Elevation, prominence, strength, and overcoming difficulty can be conceptually related. Yet all branches should not be derived from one certain material image. Shared root letters do not prove that early Arabic speakers necessarily pictured courage or assistance as climbing a mountain.

The root’s sole Qur’anic form is al-najdayn. This study prefers the contextual reading of two ways and examines the other reading separately.

Qur’anic forms

N J D occurs only once in the Qur’an:

النَّجْدَيْنِ — Al-Balad 90:10

It is the dual of najd. The rendering preferred in this study is “two ways.”

It is definite, dual, and accusative. Its accusative marker is yāʾ, which marks both accusative and genitive dual forms in Arabic.

Non-Qur’anic forms include nujūd, anjud, and nijād as geographical plurals; anjād belongs to the branch concerning brave people. Najdah can mean bravery or hardship; najīd describes a courageous person; anjada concerns entering high ground or providing help; istanjada means requesting assistance. The plurals should not all be assigned indiscriminately to one sense.

None of these forms, apart from al-najdayn, occurs in the Qur’an.

2. Early Arabic usage and branches of meaning

Najd: high ground contrasted with lowlands

Geographical Arabic contrasts najd with ghawr.

Ghawr concerns low or sunken ground; najd names land elevated relative to it.

This distinction mattered for identifying places and routes. Someone moving from lowlands into higher country could be described with anjada.

The Arabian region named Najd is associated with this geographical feature. The general word is not restricted to that region: other suitable high ground can also be called najd. This semantic relationship alone does not establish the precise historical sequence of the common noun and place name.

Najd is not confined to very high elevations. It can also describe firm, level ground raised above its surroundings.

The core of this branch is relative elevation, rather than necessarily difficult passage, severe roughness, or danger.

Najd: an elevated and visible path

An important use names a path across elevated ground, apparent to a traveler.

The path-related use can be explained through this Arabic expression:

النَّجْدُ: الطَّرِيقُ الْمُرْتَفِعُ الْبَيِّنُ الْوَاضِحُ

al-najdu: al-ṭarīqu al-murtafiʿu al-bayyinu al-wāḍiḥu

Meaning:

“Najd is the elevated, distinct, clear path.”

Elevation and visibility appear together in this explanation.

This does not establish that every high path is clear, or that every clear path must cross high ground. These features come together in an attested use of najd.

In Arabia’s terrain, a path across high ground could be recognizable from a distance and serve as a visible guide.

Visibility does not mean ease: a recognizable path can still require effort to traverse.

This distinction matters for the Qur’anic use.

A witness attributed to pre-Islamic poetry: an elevated route

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays says:

فَرِيقَانِ مِنْهُمْ جَازِعٌ بَطْنَ نَخْلَةٍ
وَآخَرُ مِنْهُمْ قَاطِعٌ نَجْدَ كَبْكَبِ

farīqāni minhum jāziʿun baṭna nakhlatin / wa-ākharu minhum qāṭiʿun najda kabkabi

Translation:

“They formed two groups: one crossing the valley of Nakhlah, the other traversing the high route of Kabkab.”

Baṭn Nakhlah and najd Kabkab identify two different routes.

Baṭn names the interior or lower part of a place, including a valley. Najd Kabkab describes a route in Kabkab’s high terrain.

Najd here is geographical rather than a name for moral virtue, courage, or assistance.

The verse has variant wording. Some versions begin غَدَاةَ غَدَوْا فَسَالِكٌ بَطْنَ نَخْلَةٍ; qāṭiʿ and jāziʿ also vary in the other portion.

The variants do not fundamentally change najd’s geographical use. The quoted version should nevertheless be treated as one transmitted wording rather than the sole certain text. Attribution also does not independently establish its precise composition date.

Najd and najdah: courage, strength, and hardship

Another branch uses najd and najīd for a brave, capable person.

Najdah is a familiar word for courage. Facing danger or showing ability in difficult affairs can be described through this family.

In other early uses, however, najdah means severity, difficulty, or distress.

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd says:

تَحْسَبُ الطَّرْفَ عَلَيْهَا نَجْدَةً
يَا لَقَوْمِي لِلشَّبَابِ الْمُسْبَكِرِّ

taḥsabu al-ṭarfa ʿalayhā najdatan / yā la-qawmī li-al-shabābi al-musbakarri

Approximate sense:

“She finds even moving her gaze a hardship; how remarkable is that fully blossomed youth!”

Najdah means difficulty or burden here, rather than courage. The poet pictures the described woman’s delicacy and languid gaze as though even looking were an effort; al-ṭarf should not be restricted to the specific action of raising the gaze.

One form of the family can therefore have a meaning distinct from its geographical branch.

Courage or distress should consequently not be transferred into the Qur’anic al-najdayn without evidence.

Assistance and other uses

Istanjada means asking for help, while anjadahu means helping someone.

This is intelligibly related to the ability to confront difficulty: someone seeking assistance needs another’s capacity to address a problem.

It does not prove that istinjād originated from the image of going up to high ground.

Other uses include nijād, the strap or baldric of a sword, and tanjīd, furnishing or decorating a home with fabrics, carpets, and ornaments.

Some lexical explanations connect the sword strap with elevation because it rests over the shoulder. Such accounts should not become certain historical origins for every word in the family.

The root thus has varied uses. Understanding the Qur’an requires starting with its actual form and context, rather than transferring an entire dictionary entry into one word.

3. Qur’anic usage: Al-Balad 90:10

وَهَدَيْنَاهُ النَّجْدَيْنِ

wa-hadaynāhu al-najdayni

Translation:

“We showed him the two ways.”

Al-Balad 90:10

This is the root’s sole Qur’anic occurrence.

Al-najdayn is dual and refers to two ways in the preferred reading. The lexical image involves elevated or visible paths.

The verse alone does not name the two ways. Their referents require the surah’s context and related verses about guidance.

A. The preceding verses: abilities granted to the human being

The preceding verses say:

أَلَمْ نَجْعَلْ لَهُ عَيْنَيْنِ

a-lam najʿal lahu ʿaynayni

“Have We not given him two eyes?”

Al-Balad 90:8

وَلِسَانًا وَشَفَتَيْنِ

wa-lisānan wa-shafatayni

“And a tongue and two lips?”

Al-Balad 90:9

وَهَدَيْنَاهُ النَّجْدَيْنِ

wa-hadaynāhu al-najdayni

“We showed him the two ways.”

Al-Balad 90:10

These three verses mention eyes, tongue, lips, and showing two ways.

Eyes enable sight, while tongue and lips relate to speech and other functions. Guidance to two ways introduces something beyond merely possessing bodily organs.

This sequence can emphasize the human ability to recognize, receive, and respond to guidance.

Their juxtaposition does not make eyes, tongue, or intellect the lexical meaning of najd.

The preceding verses describe bodily organs; verse 10 concerns the two ways shown to the person.

B. Two ways: good and evil, or geographical routes?

Najd can mean an elevated, clear path in Arabic, yet Al-Balad 90:10 specifies no geographical destination, mountain, or journey.

The surah concerns the human being and human conduct.

It begins with human creation amid hardship, assertions of power, and the assumption that one’s conduct goes unseen.

It then recalls the abilities and guidance granted to the person.

Immediately after verse 10 come:

فَلَا اقْتَحَمَ الْعَقَبَةَ

fa-lā iqtaḥama al-ʿaqabata

“Yet he did not brave the difficult pass.”

Al-Balad 90:11

وَمَا أَدْرَاكَ مَا الْعَقَبَةُ

wa-mā adrāka mā al-ʿaqabatu

“What will make you know what the difficult pass is?”

Al-Balad 90:12

The following verses explain the pass through concrete acts: freeing someone from bondage, feeding during hunger, attending to an orphaned relative and someone in destitution, faith, and mutually urging patience and mercy.

This sequence relates the two ways contextually to the course of life and human conduct.

The reading of good and evil, or guidance and error, therefore clearly fits the continuation.

Two levels should nevertheless remain distinct:

The lexical meaning in the path reading: two elevated or clear ways.

The contextual meaning in Al-Balad: two ways before the person, significant for ethical and practical choice.

This preserves the root without inserting the entire interpretive conclusion into the word from the outset.

C. Must one way be easy and the other difficult?

Najd alone does not establish that one must be easy and the other difficult.

It names elevated or clear ground and paths, rather than necessarily a steep, dangerous, or exhausting route.

The next verse, however, introduces al-ʿaqabah: a difficult pass.

This change of expression matters.

After mentioning two ways, the Qur’an describes a pass the person has not crossed, then explains crossing it through particular human actions.

Responsible, commendable conduct may therefore require effort and generosity.

Freeing a person in bondage, feeding during hunger, and helping people in need can require money, time, and strength.

This does not mean every good deed is always difficult or every easy act belongs to evil.

Al-ʿaqabah introduces difficulty into the passage; that difficulty is not an independent definition of najd.

D. Are both ways equally approved by The God?

Wa-hadaynāhu al-najdayn speaks of showing the two ways.

Showing a way does not necessarily mean approving its pursuit.

Another Qur’anic verse helps clarify this:

إِنَّا هَدَيْنَاهُ السَّبِيلَ إِمَّا شَاكِرًا وَإِمَّا كَفُورًا

innā hadaynāhu al-sabīla immā shākiran wa-immā kafūran

Translation:

“We showed him the way, whether grateful or ungrateful.”

Al-Insān 76:3

Guidance to the way is followed by two different human responses.

The way is shown, but gratitude and ingratitude do not have identical outcomes.

Fuṣṣilat 41:17 also says:

وَأَمَّا ثَمُودُ فَهَدَيْنَاهُمْ فَاسْتَحَبُّوا الْعَمَىٰ عَلَى الْهُدَىٰ

wa-ammā thamūdu fa-hadaynāhum fa-istaḥabbū al-ʿamā ʿalā al-hudā

Translation:

“As for Thamūd, We guided them, but they preferred blindness to guidance.”

Fuṣṣilat 41:17, opening part of the verse

This clearly distinguishes showing the way from accepting it.

Al-Balad 90:10 therefore does not establish equal divine approval of good and evil. Showing two ways concerns awareness of different courses; their evaluation becomes clear through other verses.

E. Does guidance mean compelling a choice?

Hadaynāhu belongs to H D Y.

Here it is associated with two ways and conveys showing the way.

The verse does not say that the person has traversed both or has been forced to choose without the capacity to decide.

Verse 11 criticizes the failure to brave the pass, emphasizing conduct and responsibility.

Qur’anic guidance can concern showing and clarifying a way; other constructions can involve bringing someone to a destination or broader divine guidance.

The entire theological debate about freedom, compulsion, and destiny should not be inserted into one word’s meaning.

The passage clearly presents guidance being granted and human responses to it being judged.

4. Another reading of al-najdayn: the two breasts

Early interpretation also transmits a reading of al-najdayn as the mother’s two breasts, alongside the ways of good and evil.

This reading concerns the child’s natural guidance toward the mother’s breasts and access to nourishment.

It can be considered from two perspectives.

Al-Balad 90:8 and Al-Balad 90:9 mention bodily organs: eyes, tongue, and lips. Guiding a child toward nourishment can likewise relate to the gifts of creation.

The breasts’ physical prominence also offers an imaginable connection with najd’s material meaning.

Resemblance alone, however, does not establish preference for this reading.

Why does the two-ways reading fit the surah more strongly?

Verse 10 uses hadaynāhu and is immediately followed by the failure to cross the pass.

The following verses explain ethical and social conduct rather than infant feeding or further bodily abilities.

Al-Insān 76:3, combining guidance to the way with gratitude and ingratitude, supplies an independent Qur’anic parallel.

Although the two-breasts reading belongs to interpretive history and can be examined alongside bodily blessings, the ways of good and evil fit the surah’s context and parallel Qur’anic uses of guidance more closely.

That preference does not make good or evil the inherent meaning of najd throughout Arabic.

Good and evil identify the ways contextually, rather than defining N J D independently.

5. Linguistic construction and comparison with related roots

The dual in al-najdayn

Al-najdayn is dual and refers to two instances of najd.

In wa-hadaynāhu al-najdayn, hu is an object pronoun and al-najdayn is accusative. A familiar grammatical analysis makes it the verb’s second object; another supplies an understood ilā.

Both analyses allow the sense of showing the two ways to the person.

Their dual number alone does not establish that every human action can be sorted into two simple categories without complexity or distinctions.

The surah can present two principal, opposed orientations, while the following verses clarify particular deeds and responsibilities.

The dual does not mean that only two visible options exist at every moment. It concerns two main directions of guidance rather than counting every detailed choice in life.

N J D (نَجْد) and S B L (سَبِيل): an elevated path and a way to follow

S B L has broad Qur’anic use; sabīl names a way, course, or means of reaching a destination.

Geographical najd evokes an elevated or visible route; sabīl concerns the course itself without necessarily implying high ground.

Al-Insān 76:3 uses al-sabīl, while Al-Balad 90:10 uses al-najdayn.

Both can concern human guidance, but their linguistic images differ.

Al-Balad evokes prominent, clear ways; Al-Insān speaks of the way shown to the human being.

This is not an absolute rule that sabīl is always easy and najd always difficult.

Study of S B L

N J D (نَجْد) and Ṣ R Ṭ (صِرَاط): two ways and the path of guidance

Ṣirāṭ often occurs in Qur’anic expressions concerning a correct path or direction of guidance.

Al-Fātiḥah 1:6 says:

اهْدِنَا الصِّرَاطَ الْمُسْتَقِيمَ

ihdinā al-ṣirāṭa al-mustaqīma

“Guide us to the straight path.”

Ṣirāṭ is qualified by mustaqīm, and the speaker requests guidance to the right way.

Al-Balad 90:10 uses the dual of najd to express two ways.

The principal difference lies in construction and context: one requests the straight path; the other presents two ways before the person.

This does not make ṣirāṭ inherently singular throughout Arabic or najd inherently two. The dual form, rather than the root, supplies the number in Al-Balad.

Study of Ṣ R Ṭ

N J D (نَجْد) and ʿ Q B (عَقَبَة): an elevated way and a difficult pass

This comparison is particularly useful for Al-Balad.

Geographical najd concerns elevated ground or an elevated route.

ʿAqabah concerns a difficult pass whose traversal can require effort.

Its introduction after the two ways moves the surah’s image from a route to overcoming difficulty.

The words should nevertheless not be treated as synonyms.

Not every najd is necessarily an ʿaqabah, nor is every ʿaqabah described as najd.

Here the pass is linked to acts requiring responsibility and generosity. The context conveys that ethical meaning, while difficulty remains distinct from najd’s fixed lexical meaning.

Study of ʿ Q B

N J D (نَجْد) and GH W R (غَوْر): high and low ground

Najd and ghawr form a clear geographical contrast in Arabic.

Najd names high ground, and ghawr low or sunken ground.

This illuminates the root’s material image.

It does not establish that everything high in the Qur’an is good and everything low is bad.

Elevation is geographical; it does not itself determine moral value.

Both ways in Al-Balad 90:10 are called najd. Elevation alone therefore cannot identify one as true and the other false.

Their ethical distinction requires the context and the Qur’anic discussion of guidance.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

The root’s general Arabic meanings should be distinguished from its Qur’anic use.

For geographical najd, “high ground,” “an elevation,” or “upland” are suitable.

For its path sense, “elevated way” or “an elevated, clear route” better conveys the lexical image.

Najdah can be “courage” or “bravery” in one context, and “difficulty,” “severity,” or “distress” in another.

For istanjada, “sought help” is natural.

Several renderings of al-najdayn in Al-Balad 90:10 are possible:

RenderingAssessment
Two elevationsPreserves the geographical image without clarifying the path sense
Two elevated waysClose to the lexical image, but may suggest only geographical routes in Persian
Two clear waysConveys both the path and its visibility
The ways of good and evilStates the contextual reading, but good and evil are not explicit in the word
Two waysBrief and natural, without adding an interpretive identification

The preferred basic translation for Forqan is:

“We showed him the two ways.”

This leaves good and evil out of the basic translation and explains the contextual identification separately.

A lexical note can mention the image of an elevated, clear route.

A reflection can then explain the two orientations of good and evil through the surah’s continuation and other guidance verses.

“Two difficult ways” is unsuitable as a basic rendering because difficulty does not necessarily follow from najd itself.

“Two ways of spiritual ascent,” “two peaks of awareness,” or “two stages of human evolution” should likewise not enter the translation without independent evidence.

7. Conclusion

N J D has several branches in Arabic. Its geographical branch concerns high ground, elevated terrain, and a clear route; other branches concern courage, strength, hardship, assistance, objects, and home decoration.

The poetry attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays illustrates geographical elevation and routes. The verse attributed to Ṭarafah also shows najdah meaning difficulty or burden, rather than always courage.

The Qur’an uses this root only once: al-najdayn in Al-Balad 90:10.

In the path reading, the direct rendering is two ways with an image of elevation and clarity. The surah relates them contextually to human orientation and responsibility.

The following verses connect the pass with freeing someone from bondage, feeding people in need, faith, patience, and mercy. They present responsibility toward guidance and a right course that can require effort and generosity.

The two-breasts interpretation is historically attested. Yet the continuation’s emphasis on choice and responsibility, together with parallels such as Al-Insān 76:3 and Fuṣṣilat 41:17, strengthens the ways-of-good-and-evil reading.

Good and evil should nevertheless remain contextual referents, rather than part of najd’s lexical definition.

Proposed final definition for Forqan

N J D’s geographical branch denotes elevated or prominent ground, with najd naming upland terrain or an elevated, clear route. Other branches concern courage, ability, hardship, and assistance, but should not all be assigned one unproven historical material origin.

In this study’s preferred contextual reading, Qur’anic al-najdayn means “the two ways,” evoking elevated, visible routes. Al-Balad and the parallel guidance verses connect these with good and evil in human life.

“Two ways” is therefore suitable for the basic translation; “the ways of good and evil” supplies the contextual explanation.
