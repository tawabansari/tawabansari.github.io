Qur’anic root ك س ف

Pieces, fragments, and distinct masses; obscuration and diminished light

1. Meaning in early Arabic

The Arabic root ك س ف has two main semantic branches: cutting and separating part of something, and a change through which something loses its former brightness, freshness, or visibility.

In the first branch, كِسْف and كِسْفَة denote a piece or portion of something. They describe pieces of cloth, sections of clouds, and other things perceived as distinct fragments or masses.

Al-Farra, an early Arabic linguist, reports the following expression from a Bedouin speaker:

أَعْطِنِي كِسْفَةً مِنْ ثَوْبِكَ

Give me a piece of your garment.

Here كِسْفَة denotes a piece of fabric separated from a larger whole.

Classical Arabic also records كَسَفَ الثَّوْبَ for cutting cloth into pieces.

Ibn Faris identifies cutting part from something and an unwelcome change in its condition as meanings of this family. Eclipses of the sun and moon, and changes in a person’s expression, belong to the second branch.

Arabic describes someone with a gloomy, frowning face as كَاسِفُ الْوَجْهِ and someone in an unhappy state as كَاسِفُ الْبَالِ.

The evidence reviewed here does not establish an independently datable pre-Islamic example. The Qur’an itself nevertheless shows that كِسْف and كِسَف were understood as pieces, fragments, and parts of a whole at the time of revelation.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important Arabic forms include:

  • كَسَفَ ـ يَكْسِفُ: cut or separated; also obscured or diminished the light of something.
  • كَسْف: cutting and separating part of something.
  • كِسْف: a piece, fragment, or portion.
  • كِسْفَة: a piece of cloth, cloud, or something else.
  • كِسَف: pieces or fragments.
  • أَكْسَاف: one of the family’s plural forms.
  • كُسُوف: an eclipse or obscuration of the sun’s or moon’s light.
  • كَاسِف: obscuring or becoming dark; also gloomy or frowning.
  • تَكْسِيف: cutting into pieces.
  • اِنْكَسَفَ: became eclipsed or dimmed.
  • اِنْكِسَاف: eclipse or obscuration of light.

Forms examined in the Qur’an:

كِسْفًا ـ كِسَفًا

In Hafs’s transmission from ʿAsim, كِسَفًا occurs in al-Isra 17:92, al-Shuʿara 26:187, al-Rum 30:48, and Saba 34:9; كِسْفًا occurs in al-Tur 52:44.

The family’s verbs and the verbal noun كُسُوف do not occur in the Qur’an.

3. Core meaning and material imagery

The shared meaning in Qur’anic usage is distinct pieces or parts of a whole.

When cloth is cut into pieces, each may be called كِسْفَة. Likewise, clouds seen as distinct masses or sections can be described by the plural كِسَف.

Distinct parts do not necessarily result from cutting with a tool or breaking a solid object.

A cloud is not a solid, rigid body, yet it may appear as masses and sections of varying shapes and sizes.

In these nominal usages, the important feature is the presence of distinct parts, rather than the particular process that produced them.

In the family’s other branch, كُسُوف concerns the obscuration or reduction of the sun’s or moon’s light. Related expressions describe a gloomy face or an unhappy condition.

Although some lexicographers connect obscuration, interruption of light, and separation of a part, it cannot be established that all these uses historically developed from one material image.

The Qur’an employs the branch relating to pieces and fragments.

4. Qur’anic usages

كِسَفًا: Muhammad’s opponents demand that the sky fall

This verse quotes opponents who made acceptance of Muhammad’s call conditional on the fulfillment of various demands.

The surrounding verses mention bringing forth a spring, possessing gardens, owning a golden house, and ascending into the sky.

One demand was that he make the sky fall upon them in pieces.

تُسْقِطَ, from س ق ط, expresses bringing down; كِسَفًا specifies the condition of what is brought down.

Falling and being in pieces are therefore distinct ideas expressed by separate words.

The verse does not report such an event taking place; it quotes the opponents’ demand.

The reply comes in the next verse, where Muhammad is instructed to emphasize that he is a human being and a Messenger.

كِسَفًا: the demand of Shuʿayb’s people

The demand voiced by Muhammad’s opponents in al-Isra 17:92 appears here in the speech of Shuʿayb’s opponents.

They make his truthfulness conditional on fulfilling their demand and ask him to bring down pieces from the sky upon them.

The following verses state:

The Qur’an describes their end as عَذَابُ يَوْمِ الظُّلَّةِ, the punishment of the day of the overshadowing cloud.

Although these verses are connected, the people’s demand and the account of their end should not simply be treated as identical events.

كِسَف itself does not specify the material, size, or exact nature of what falls from the sky. Nor does the word alone establish that the punishment of the overshadowing day took the form of falling stones, celestial bodies, or any other particular natural phenomenon.

كِسَفًا: clouds formed into masses

This verse is especially useful for understanding ك س ف: كِسَف describes clouds and rainfall rather than punishment or threat.

The Qur’an expresses several aspects of this natural occurrence with distinct words:

  • تُثِيرُ, from ث و ر: the winds stirring up clouds.
  • يَبْسُطُهُ, from ب س ط: spreading the cloud through the sky.
  • كِسَفًا, from ك س ف: masses and sections of cloud.
  • الْوَدْقَ, from و د ق: rain emerging from within the cloud.

Here كِسَف refers to masses of cloud.

Clouds may be scattered across the sky or appear as distinguishable masses within a broad formation. كِسَف does not necessarily imply complete separation between them.

Its application to clouds shows that the word is not restricted to broken solid objects.

Punishment, destruction, and danger are therefore not inherent in its meaning. Here it occurs in a context of mercy, rainfall, and human rejoicing.

كِسَفًا: danger from the sky alongside sinking into the earth

Two similar-sounding lexical families occur side by side:

نَخْسِفْ belongs to خ س ف, while كِسَفًا belongs to ك س ف.

The first part concerns the possibility of people being swallowed into the earth; the second concerns pieces falling upon them from the sky.

People live with earth beneath their feet and sky above them. Familiarity with this apparently stable environment can produce a feeling of absolute security.

The verse draws attention to the limits of that security and the power governing creation.

Again, كِسَف names the pieces, while the idea of bringing them down comes from نُسْقِطْ.

The condition إِنْ نَشَأْ presents the event as a possibility, rather than reporting that it has occurred to a specified group.

كِسْفًا: a piece of the sky and denial of what is seen

Unlike the preceding instances in Hafs’s reading, كِسْفًا here has a vowelless sin.

سَاقِطًا describes the piece as falling.

The observers respond by calling what they see an accumulated cloud.

مَرْكُوم is the passive participle of ر ك م and describes something whose parts are heaped upon one another.

سَحَابٌ مَرْكُومٌ is the observers’ statement, not The God’s direct description of the falling object.

The Qur’an depicts a situation in which even witnessing a sign need not change the deniers’ position: they may explain what they see in terms of prior beliefs.

This connects with al-Hijr 15:14 and al-Hijr 15:15, which also describe the deniers’ response to an extraordinary sign.

Lexically, كِسْف in al-Tur 52:44 means a piece of the sky; the word does not inherently mean cloud, stone, or celestial body.

5. Morphology and syntax

كِسْف and كِسَف

One established meaning of كِسْف, with a vowelless sin, is a piece or portion of something.

كِسَف, with fatha on the sin, is a plural of كِسْفَة: pieces or fragments, comparable to قِطْعَة and قِطَع.

Some lexicographers also regard كِسْف as plural or collective. A vowelless sin therefore does not invariably indicate a singular in Arabic.

Recognized Qur’anic readings transmit forms with either fatha or sukun on the sin in al-Isra 17:92, al-Shuʿara 26:187, al-Rum 30:48, and Saba 34:9.

In al-Tur 52:44, the ten canonical readings agree on sukun on the sin.

The singular masculine description سَاقِطًا in this verse is consistent with understanding كِسْفًا as a single piece.

These morphological differences do not change the family’s shared semantic core.

Syntactic functions in the verses

In al-Isra 17:92, كِسَفًا can be read as a circumstantial expression describing السَّمَاءَ: the sky falling in pieces.

In al-Shuʿara 26:187 and Saba 34:9, كِسَفًا is the object of the verb of bringing down, denoting pieces coming from the sky.

In al-Rum 30:48, كِسَفًا is the second object of يَجْعَلُ, specifying the state into which the cloud is formed.

In al-Tur 52:44, كِسْفًا is the object of يَرَوْا, while سَاقِطًا describes its condition when seen.

These constructions show that the noun itself does not denote falling. Motion or a change of condition comes from accompanying verbs and descriptions.

6. Comparison with related roots

ك س ف compared with خ س ف

These families sound similar and overlap in usages concerning eclipses of the sun and moon.

Their distinction is nevertheless clear in Qur’anic usage.

خَسْف concerns sinking, being swallowed up, or diminished visibility; كِسْف denotes a piece of something.

Saba 34:9 places sinking into the earth alongside pieces falling from the sky.

Classical Arabic uses both families for solar and lunar eclipses, though كُسُوف is more commonly associated with the sun and خُسُوف with the moon.

The Qur’an says of the moon:

This verb belongs to خ س ف, not ك س ف.

In modern astronomical terminology, كُسُوف usually means a solar eclipse and خُسُوف a lunar eclipse. This technical usage must not restrict all earlier and Qur’anic meanings of either family to eclipses.

ك س ف compared with ق ط ع

قَطْع concerns cutting, separating, or breaking continuity.

The verbal use of كَسْف overlaps with قَطْع. The nouns كِسْف and كِسْفَة, however, identify a piece or portion.

قَطْع can describe cutting a rope, ending a relationship, or interrupting a flow. Qur’anic كِسْف presents the image of distinct pieces or parts.

ك س ف compared with ك س ر

كَسْر means breaking and disrupting an object’s integrity.

A vessel may break into pieces when struck. Here كَسْر names the breaking, and the resulting fragments exemplify separate pieces.

Not every كِسْف, however, results from breaking a solid object.

The use of كِسَف for clouds in al-Rum 30:48 makes this distinction clear.

ك س ف compared with س ق ط

سُقُوط concerns falling or descending.

كِسَف denotes pieces that may fall, but falling is not essential to their meaning.

The pairing of نُسْقِطْ with كِسَفًا in Saba 34:9, and كِسْفًا with سَاقِطًا in al-Tur 52:44, illustrates the distinction.

One word identifies the thing’s form or nature; the other identifies its movement.

ك س ف compared with ر ك م

رَكْم concerns gathering and heaping parts upon one another.

Al-Nur states:

رُكَامًا emphasizes the cloud’s accumulation, whereas كِسَفًا in al-Rum 30:48 identifies its sections and masses.

Both describe clouds, but from different perspectives: accumulation on the one hand, distinct sections or masses on the other.

The descriptions are compatible: a cloud formation can have both distinguishable sections and an accumulated structure.

ك س ف compared with س ح ب

سَحَاب names a cloud; كِسَف can describe its sections and masses.

In al-Rum 30:48, the Qur’an first mentions a cloud and then describes it as كِسَف.

In al-Tur 52:44, however, people seeing a piece of the sky falling call it an accumulated cloud.

A cloud may thus contain several masses or sections, but not every كِسْف is a cloud.

“Piece” and “fragment” suit most Qur’anic occurrences of this family.

In al-Rum 30:48, “masses” is more natural because the referent is cloud, avoiding the impression of a broken solid object.

Uniformly translating the word as “punishment,” “celestial stone,” “meteorite,” or “cloud” is unsuitable: each adds a particular meaning or referent not inherent in the word.

Conclusion

ك س ف has two main branches in Arabic: cutting and separating part of something, and obscuration or a change in its former condition and brightness.

The Qur’an uses the family for pieces, fragments, and distinguishable masses.

Some verses concern pieces falling from the sky; al-Rum 30:48 uses the same family for cloud masses.

This contextual variety shows that كِسْف does not inherently mean punishment, falling, or cloud. The material, movement, and consequences are specified by other words and by context.

The common Qur’anic meaning is a piece or distinct parts of a whole.
