The root f-z-z — Inciting, unsettling, and compelling movement

Qur’anic and lexical forms

وَاسْتَفْزِزْ
(wa-stafziz)
Incite with your voice

لَيَسْتَفِزُّونَكَ
(la-yastafizzūnaka)
Pressure to dislodge you

يَسْتَفِزَّهُمْ
(yastafizzahum)
Intending to drive them out

The three-consonant Arabic root f-z-z concerns inciting, unsettling, disturbing stability, and prompting someone to move or react. In established uses, فَزَّهُ and أَفَزَّهُ mean frightening, disturbing, or unsettling someone. اِسْتَفَزَّهُ can also mean provoking, dislodging, or using pressure or deception to induce unwanted movement.

This incitement may be psychological, as when threatening speech disturbs another person’s composure. It may also lead to actual movement and displacement, as when threats and pressure drive people from their land.

F-z-z occurs three times in the Qur’an, all in al-Isrāʾ: وَاسْتَفْزِزْ in 17:64, لَيَسْتَفِزُّونَكَ in 17:76, and يَسْتَفِزَّهُمْ in 17:103. All are Form X, but occur in three different situations: Satan’s incitement of people, opponents’ efforts to drive Muhammad from his land, and Pharaoh’s intention to drive out the Israelites.

The shared semantic core of these Qur’anic uses is disturbing a person’s or group’s composure and settled position, and prompting movement through provocation, pressure, or threats. Whether the intended result is wrongful behavior or expulsion from a land is established by syntax and context.

1. The root in early Arabic

Inciting and unsettling

In established Arabic usage, فَزَّهُ and أَفَزَّهُ mean frightening, disturbing, or inciting someone.

Older lexical accounts explain this usage through expressions such as أَفْزَعَهُ and أَزْعَجَهُ.

أَفْزَعَهُ concerns frightening or alarming someone.

أَزْعَجَهُ can refer to unsettling someone and making them move from their place.

These meanings often occur together. Faced suddenly with danger, someone may lose their composure, rise from their place, or flee.

Fear and movement are nevertheless distinct. Someone may be afraid yet remain where they are; another may be drawn into hasty action through provocation, anger, or pressure without intense fear.

فَزَّ قَلْبُهُ describes a heart becoming agitated through fear.

اِسْتَفَزَّهُ الْخَوْفُ likewise describes fear unsettling a person and disturbing their steadiness.

Fear can cause agitation in these uses, but the root is not restricted to fear.

Evidence from early Arabic poetry

A verse attributed to al-Shammākh ibn Ḍirār, a poet who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, reads:

يُطِيعُ سَفِيهَ الْقَوْمِ إِذْ يَسْتَفِزُّهُ
وَيَعْصِي حَلِيمًا شَيَّبَتْهُ الْهَزَاهِزُ

Yuṭīʿu safīha l-qawmi idh yastafizzuhu / wa-yaʿṣī ḥalīman shayyabat-hu l-hazāhizu.

Translation: “He obeys the community’s fool when that man provokes him, but disobeys a forbearing, wise man whom hardships have made gray-haired.”

Here يَسْتَفِزُّهُ describes provoking someone to react and follow another person’s words.

The speaker describes a person stirred to action by a fool’s provocation, while rejecting the words of someone forbearing and experienced.

Istifzāz here does not necessarily involve physical expulsion. Someone’s decisions and behavior can be influenced so that they undertake an action they had not previously intended.

This example helps establish the sense of provocation and disturbance of steadiness.

Al-Shammākh, however, lived partly in the Islamic period. Moreover, lexical and literary sources transmit the verse with differences in wording and attribution. Without establishing when it was composed, it cannot serve as conclusive pre-Qur’anic evidence.

What the verse itself clearly demonstrates is istifzāz as inciting someone and drawing them into a reaction under another person’s influence.

Pre-Islamic evidence: فَزّ as a wild cow’s calf

The pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā says:

كَمَا اسْتَغَاثَ بِسَيْءٍ فَزُّ غَيْطَلَةٍ
خَافَ الْعُيُونَ فَلَمْ يُنْظَرْ بِهِ الْحَشَكُ

Kamā staghātha bi-sayʾin fazzu ghayṭalatin / khāfa l-ʿuyūna fa-lam yunẓar bihi l-ḥashaku.

Translation: “Like a wild cow’s calf seeking a little milk from its mother, afraid of watchful eyes, with no time to wait for her udder to fill.”

In this verse, فَزّ names a wild cow’s calf.

One lexical explanation identifies غَيْطَلَة as a female wild cow, while سَيْء denotes the small amount of milk present before the fuller flow of milk.

The verse shows fazz as an animal name in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

This point should remain distinct from the meaning of istifzāz.

Some lexicographers relate naming the calf fazz to its agility and light movement. This is an understandable association, but the verse itself does not establish the name’s historical origin.

Zuhayr’s example is thus evidence for the animal name; alone, it does not establish that pre-Islamic istifzāz had precisely the Qur’anic senses of incitement and expulsion.

Other recorded uses

Lexical sources also use فَزّ to describe a light, agile person.

فَزَّ عَنْهُ is also recorded for turning away and distancing oneself from someone.

فَزَّ الْجُرْحُ describes a wound exuding moisture or fluid.

These different uses show that several lexical branches are recorded under f-z-z. They should not all be traced to a single historical meaning through an imagined account.

For Qur’anic usage, the evidence concerning incitement, disturbance, and displacement is directly relevant.

2. Linguistic forms and semantic core

The main recognized forms in this family include:

  • فَزَّ: in some uses, became frightened or unsettled; also, distanced himself from someone.
  • فَزَّهُ: frightened, incited, or unsettled him.
  • أَفَزَّهُ: disturbed him and prompted movement or reaction.
  • اِسْتَفَزَّهُ: provoked him, disturbed his steadiness, or pressured him to move or leave.
  • اِسْتِفْزَاز: provocation, disturbance, and incitement to react.
  • فَزّ: in another branch, a light, agile person; also a wild cow’s calf.
  • أَفْزَاز: the plural of fazz in its use for a wild cow’s calf.
  • فَزَّة: a leap or sudden movement prompted by agitation.

Lexical sources record further forms, but not all occur in the Qur’an.

Only the Form X verb occurs in the Qur’an.

Its three forms are:

وَاسْتَفْزِزْ — An imperative addressed to one person.

لَيَسْتَفِزُّونَكَ — A plural imperfect verb concerning those who intended to unsettle and drive out Muhammad.

يَسْتَفِزَّهُمْ — A singular imperfect verb concerning Pharaoh’s intention toward a group of people.

These forms share a root and derivational pattern, but their agents, objects, and intended results differ in each verse.

Together, the uses suggest the following semantic core:

Through provocation, fear, or pressure, to disturb someone’s composure, steadiness, or position and draw them into reaction or movement.

This definition needs sufficient breadth to explain both psychological provocation and efforts at physical displacement.

Istifzāz should not, however, be equated with every invitation, movement, or change of place. In established uses, incitement and disturbance of the previous condition are significant.

3. وَاسْتَفْزِزْ: inciting people with Satan’s voice

Al-Isrāʾ 17:64

وَاسْتَفْزِزْ مَنِ اسْتَطَعْتَ مِنْهُمْ بِصَوْتِكَ وَأَجْلِبْ عَلَيْهِمْ بِخَيْلِكَ وَرَجِلِكَ وَشَارِكْهُمْ فِي الْأَمْوَالِ وَالْأَوْلَادِ وَعِدْهُمْ ۚ وَمَا يَعِدُهُمُ الشَّيْطَانُ إِلَّا غُرُورًا

Wa-stafziz mani staṭaʿta minhum bi-ṣawtika wa-ajlib ʿalayhim bi-khaylika wa-rajilika wa-shārik-hum fī l-amwāli wa-l-awlādi wa-ʿidhum; wa-mā yaʿiduhumu sh-shayṭānu illā ghurūrā.

Translation: “And incite whomever you can among them with your voice; rally against them your cavalry and infantry; share with them in wealth and children, and make them promises. Satan promises them nothing but delusion.”

This verse continues God’s exchange with Iblīs.

In al-Isrāʾ 17:62, Iblīs arrogantly states his intention to mislead Adam’s descendants.

In al-Isrāʾ 17:63, the divine response describes the fate of those who follow Iblīs.

Verse 64 then presents means of inciting and deceiving people.

What kind of incitement does استفزز express?

وَاسْتَفْزِزْ is a Form X imperative. Its imperative form occurs in a context of warning and the consequences of following Iblīs; it should not be understood as an ethical command to deceive or an endorsement of Satan’s conduct.

Iblīs is the addressee.

The object is identified by مَنِ اسْتَطَعْتَ مِنْهُمْ: whomever among them you can.

Here istifzāz concerns disturbing a person’s steadiness and drawing them into a reaction under the influence of Satan’s invitation.

This incitement may work through fear, greed, anger, deceptive promises, or the stirring of desires.

None of these examples alone exhausts the lexical meaning of istifzāz. The verse emphasizes incitement through Satan’s voice, then mentions other methods alongside it.

بِصَوْتِكَ: Satan’s voice

بِصَوْتِكَ identifies the instrument of incitement.

In its direct sense, ṣawt refers to sound or voice.

In this context, however, Satan’s voice appears within the framework of inviting and deceiving people.

Someone might incite another to wrongdoing through threats, promises, propaganda, or deceptive speech.

Such behavior can exemplify incitement through voice and invitation.

The phrase does not establish that every pleasant sound, song, musical performance, or sound-transmitting device is inherently Satan’s voice.

Such a ruling does not follow from f-z-z or ṣawt itself.

The central issue is how the voice is used and for what purpose.

A voice drawing someone into deception, oppression, and rejection of truth differs in purpose and consequences from one used for teaching, sound warning, or helping others.

Istifzāz and deceptive promises

The verse continues:

«وَعِدْهُمْ»

Wa-ʿidhum.

and then:

«وَمَا يَعِدُهُمُ الشَّيْطَانُ إِلَّا غُرُورًا»

Wa-mā yaʿiduhumu sh-shayṭānu illā ghurūrā.

Satan’s promises here are associated with deception.

In the hope of substantial gain, someone may do something they ordinarily recognize as wrong.

For example, promises of quick wealth, power, or social standing may draw a person into oppression, betrayal, or corruption.

Istifzāz does not simply mean making promises. A promise may be one means of drawing someone away from considered judgment toward a hasty or wrongful reaction.

The act of incitement should therefore be distinguished from its instruments.

Does Satan have absolute control over people?

Al-Isrāʾ 17:65 states that Satan has no authority over God’s servants.

In Ibrāhīm 14:22, Satan likewise says at the reckoning that he had no power over people: he invited them, and they responded.

These verses matter for understanding the limits of istifzāz.

Satan can incite, whisper, and invite, but this does not mean people have no choice in response.

Conversely, the limits on Satan’s power do not mean that provocation and deception have no real effects on human behavior.

The Qur’an takes deception seriously while preserving responsibility for human choice.

Istifzāz here therefore means inciting and drawing into reaction, not absolute possession of the human will.

4. لَيَسْتَفِزُّونَكَ: pressure to drive out the Messenger

Al-Isrāʾ 17:76

وَإِنْ كَادُوا لَيَسْتَفِزُّونَكَ مِنَ الْأَرْضِ لِيُخْرِجُوكَ مِنْهَا ۖ وَإِذًا لَا يَلْبَثُونَ خِلَافَكَ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا

Wa-in kādū la-yastafizzūnaka mina l-arḍi li-yukhrijūka minhā; wa-idhan lā yalbathūna khilāfaka illā qalīlā.

Translation: “They almost dislodged you from this land through pressure and threats, to drive you out of it; then they would have remained there after you only a little while.”

Here لَيَسْتَفِزُّونَكَ concerns a group’s efforts to compel Muhammad to leave his land.

This differs significantly from al-Isrāʾ 17:64.

Verse 64 concerned incitement through Satan’s voice and invitation.

Verse 76 concerns dislodging and driving someone from a particular land.

مِنَ الْأَرْضِ identifies the place from which flight or displacement is intended.

لِيُخْرِجُوكَ مِنْهَا makes the pressure’s purpose explicit: driving the Messenger from that land.

Distinguishing istifzāz from expulsion

Two verbs occur in the same verse:

يَسْتَفِزُّونَكَ

and

يُخْرِجُوكَ

The sentence permits a distinction between inciting and dislodging on the one hand, and the intended expulsion on the other, although these verbs are closely related in this context.

Istifzāz here concerns unsettling, pressuring, and dislodging.

Ikhrāj expresses taking or driving someone out of a place.

Threats and harassment may place someone under such pressure that they must leave their home or land.

In that situation, pressure and threats prompt movement, while departure from the land is the intended result.

Istifzāz may thus precede expulsion, but the words are not precisely identical.

Connection with the preceding verses

Al-Isrāʾ 17:73 describes opponents’ efforts to turn Muhammad away from what had been revealed to him.

In al-Isrāʾ 17:74, God speaks of making him firm.

Verse 76 then raises pressure to drive him out.

This sequence shows two forms of pressure.

First, efforts to change the message and make the Messenger attribute something else to God.

Second, efforts to drive him from his land.

These actions may be connected. Opponents might first try to persuade someone to abandon their position and, if unsuccessful, resort to threats and expulsion to remove their presence.

This pattern should not become the lexical meaning of istifzāz itself. The root describes incitement and dislodging; the details and purpose of the pressure come from context.

Had expulsion actually occurred?

وَإِنْ كَادُوا indicates that they had come close to doing this.

The construction does not establish that the particular action, with all its conditions and intended purpose, was fully accomplished.

Intending expulsion, nearing its execution, and departure occurring under other circumstances must therefore be distinguished.

The verse itself also does not name the group or give the historical details of the action. Identifying them requires contextual examination and independent evidence.

What the verse clearly describes is the effort to expel Muhammad and a warning about its consequences for his opponents.

5. يَسْتَفِزَّهُمْ: Pharaoh’s intention to drive out the Israelites

Al-Isrāʾ 17:103

فَأَرَادَ أَنْ يَسْتَفِزَّهُمْ مِنَ الْأَرْضِ فَأَغْرَقْنَاهُ وَمَنْ مَعَهُ جَمِيعًا

Fa-arāda an yastafizzahum mina l-arḍi fa-aghraqnāhu wa-man maʿahu jamīʿā.

Translation: “Then he intended to drive them from that land, but We drowned him and all those with him.”

Pharaoh is the subject of أَرَادَ in this verse.

The pronoun هُمْ in يَسْتَفِزَّهُمْ refers to the group he threatens.

Within the story of Moses and the Israelites, the passage concerns Pharaoh’s intention to dislodge and drive them out.

مِنَ الْأَرْضِ identifies the land from which he intends to expel them.

The construction resembles al-Isrāʾ 17:76.

Istifzāz occurs with مِنَ الْأَرْضِ in both verses.

In these contexts, the root thus concerns pressure to uproot people from their settled position and land.

Intention and accomplishment

The verse says:

«فَأَرَادَ أَنْ يَسْتَفِزَّهُمْ»

Fa-arāda an yastafizzahum.

That is, Pharaoh intended to do this.

It then describes his drowning and that of his companions.

His intention should therefore not be equated with successfully expelling the Israelites.

Elsewhere, the Qur’an describes the Israelites’ departure and their rescue from Pharaoh’s domination.

Here, however, Pharaoh’s purpose is contrasted with his own fate.

He intended to dislodge others, but he and his companions were themselves destroyed.

Does istifzāz mean killing here?

Some exegetical explanations relate istifzāz to killing or destruction.

This study favors “dislodging and driving out” for the construction; interpreting it as destruction is another reading of how they might be uprooted from the earth.

Killing and destruction can be means or consequences of oppressive conduct, but should not replace the word’s basic meaning without contextual evidence.

For Pharaoh’s destruction, al-Isrāʾ 17:103 uses a different verb:

«فَأَغْرَقْنَاهُ»

Fa-aghraqnāhu.

That is, “We drowned him.”

Istifzāz in this sentence expresses Pharaoh’s intention; drowning is the fate that befell him and his companions.

Distinguishing istifzāz from migration

Dislodging and expelling people may cause their migration.

Not every migration, however, results from istifzāz.

People may migrate for better opportunities, learning, trade, or a freely chosen place to live.

Someone driven from home and land through threats and harassment is in a different situation.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:76 and 17:103 emphasize pressure and the intention to expel.

These uses should therefore be distinguished from voluntary movement.

6. Morphology and syntax

F-z-z is a three-consonant geminate root: its second and third consonants are both z.

In اِسْتَفَزَّ, the two zāʾ consonants merge into a doubled consonant marked with shaddah.

Only this verb’s Form X occurs in the Qur’an.

وَاسْتَفْزِزْ: the imperative

In al-Isrāʾ 17:64, وَاسْتَفْزِزْ is the second-person masculine singular imperative.

In this imperative, the consonants are unassimilated and the two zāʾ letters appear separately; this is one of the inflectional forms of a geminate verb.

Iblīs is the addressee.

مَنِ اسْتَطَعْتَ مِنْهُمْ specifies the object and the range of people subject to incitement.

بِصَوْتِكَ identifies its instrument.

Together these elements convey inciting, by voice, those whom one is able to influence.

لَيَسْتَفِزُّونَكَ: the plural imperfect

The imperfect verb is plural in al-Isrāʾ 17:76.

The initial لَـ adds emphasis.

يَسْتَفِزُّونَ refers to plural agents.

كَ is the attached object pronoun referring to Muhammad.

The agents here are a group of opponents, rather than Satan as the sentence’s direct subject.

مِنَ الْأَرْضِ identifies the place from which they seek to dislodge the Messenger.

يَسْتَفِزَّهُمْ: the singular imperfect

In al-Isrāʾ 17:103, the imperfect verb is masculine singular.

Its subject in the sentence is Pharaoh.

هُمْ is the attached object pronoun, referring to those he intends to drive out.

The verb also follows أَنْ and is connected with أَرَادَ.

The sentence therefore describes an intention to act.

The differences between these forms do not change the basic semantic direction of istifzāz, but the agent, addressee, object, and situation differ.

7. Comparison with related roots

F-z-z (اِسْتَفَزَّ) and f-z-ʿ (فَزِعَ)

Fazaʿ concerns alarm and human or animal reactions to danger.

Istifzāz may cause such alarm, but is not restricted to it.

In al-Isrāʾ 17:76, pressure and threats can prompt someone to leave their land.

In al-Isrāʾ 17:64, Satan’s incitement may operate through desires and deceptive promises, beyond frightening alone.

Fazaʿ thus primarily concerns alarm and the reaction to it, whereas istifzāz emphasizes inciting and disturbing steadiness.

F-z-z (اِسْتَفَزَّ) and ʾ-z-z (أَزَّ)

Maryam 19:83 describes devils forcefully inciting disbelievers.

تَؤُزُّهُمْ belongs to the root ʾ-z-z.

In Qur’anic usage, this root concerns incitement, setting in motion, and persistent prompting.

Istifzāz can likewise refer to provoking someone and drawing them into a reaction.

The roots are semantically close here but are not identical.

Istifzāz emphasizes disturbing someone’s settled state and drawing them out of their previous condition. Azz also contains the sense of stirring and driving into action through pressure and incitement.

Their precise differences should be examined in context, rather than assuming that one is always more intense than the other.

F-z-z (اِسْتَفَزَّ) and n-z-gh (نَزَغَ)

Nazgh also occurs in the Qur’an for incitement and the creation of conflict.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:53 says that Satan stirs discord among people.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:200 also mentions Satan’s prompting and seeking refuge in God.

In these uses, nazgh is close to whispering and inciting discord or wrongful conduct.

Istifzāz has a broader range and can even describe driving a group from a land.

Nazgh can thus be a kind of incitement, but not every instance of istifzāz necessarily concerns discord between people.

F-z-z (اِسْتَفَزَّ) and w-s-w-s (وَسْوَسَ)

Waswasah concerns hidden, recurring suggestions of speech or thought.

Istifzāz emphasizes agitation and unsettling.

Whispering may be one instrument of istifzāz, but the meanings are not identical.

Someone may provoke another through an overt threat, without hidden whispering being involved.

A suggestion may also arise in a person’s mind without drawing them into the intended reaction.

F-z-z (اِسْتَفَزَّ) and kh-r-j (أَخْرَجَ)

These roots occur together in al-Isrāʾ 17:76.

Istifzāz concerns inciting and dislodging.

Ikhrāj means taking or driving someone out of a place.

Istifzāz may aim at expulsion, but not every act of taking someone out involves provocation or threats.

For example, one person may take another out of a place with their consent. Departure has occurred, but istifzāz is not necessarily involved.

The verse in al-Isrāʾ combines pressure and the purpose of expulsion in one sentence.

8. Challenges in understanding and translation

The first error is restricting istifzāz to making someone angry.

In contemporary Persian, istifzāz is sometimes understood as irritating or angering someone.

This is insufficient for its Qur’anic uses.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:76 and al-Isrāʾ 17:103 concern dislodging and driving people from a land.

Incitement may therefore concern forced movement and displacement, beyond causing anger alone.

The second error is equating istifzāz with frightening.

Fear may be one of its instruments, but al-Isrāʾ 17:64 describes incitement through Satan’s voice in a context of invitation and deceptive promises.

The third error is equating istifzāz with accomplished expulsion.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:76 describes opponents coming close to this action.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:103 describes Pharaoh’s intention.

The mere use of the verb should therefore not be treated as proof that expulsion was fully accomplished.

The fourth error is interpreting بصوتك as a particular instrument or art and then making a blanket judgment about it.

The verse describes incitement through Satan’s voice. Identifying particular instances requires examining the invitation’s content, purpose, and connection with deception.

The fifth error is assigning absolute power to Satan.

In the same context, the Qur’an describes the limits of Satan’s authority. Incitement should therefore not be understood as coercion that leaves people without choice.

Finally, not every effective appeal, passionate speech, or stirring of emotions should be labeled blameworthy istifzāz.

Words may awaken someone from indifference to oppression and encourage them to defend what is right.

Conversely, speech may use deception and threats to draw someone into corruption or abandonment of responsibility.

The lexical meaning of incitement alone does not determine every instance’s moral value. That value depends on the speech’s truth or falsity, the purpose of incitement, and the resulting conduct.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

For lexical uses of فَزَّهُ, suitable equivalents include “incited,” “disturbed,” and “unsettled.”

Depending on context, اِسْتَفَزَّهُ may be rendered “provoked him,” “made him react,” “dislodged him,” or “compelled him to move through pressure and threats.”

The term istifzāz may be retained in a Persian research discussion, but for general readers the sentence’s specific meaning is preferable.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:64

For وَاسْتَفْزِزْ مَنِ اسْتَطَعْتَ مِنْهُمْ بِصَوْتِكَ:

“And incite whomever you can among them with your voice.”

“Incite” is more natural here than “drive out,” because the passage concerns stirring people through voice.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:76

For لَيَسْتَفِزُّونَكَ مِنَ الْأَرْضِ:

“They almost dislodged you from this land through pressure and threats.”

This rendering preserves the distinction between pressure and expulsion and fits the continuation لِيُخْرِجُوكَ مِنْهَا.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:103

For فَأَرَادَ أَنْ يَسْتَفِزَّهُمْ مِنَ الْأَرْضِ:

“Then he intended to drive them from that land.”

Expulsion from the land is the prominent contextual meaning here.

Brief root meaning and contextual meaning

The following distinction suits a brief lexical explanation:

Brief root meaning: inciting, unsettling, and dislodging from a place or settled state.

Meaning in al-Isrāʾ 17:64: inciting people through Satan’s voice and invitation, drawing them into following and deception.

Meaning in al-Isrāʾ 17:76: pressuring Muhammad to dislodge and expel him from his land.

Meaning in al-Isrāʾ 17:103: Pharaoh’s intention to drive the Israelites from the land.

An occurrence’s explanation should emphasize the meaning of that form in context, keeping historical examination and comparison with other roots separate.

10. Conclusion

In Arabic, f-z-z concerns unsettling, frightening, inciting, and dislodging.

Early linguistic evidence uses this family for provoking someone and disturbing their steadiness. In the verse attributed to al-Shammākh, istifzāz means inciting a person and drawing them into a reaction under another’s influence.

Other uses, such as fazz for a wild cow’s calf, are recorded in pre-Islamic poetry, but the precise historical relationship among all these branches has not been established.

The Qur’an uses this root only in Form X, three times in al-Isrāʾ.

In al-Isrāʾ 17:64, Satan is addressed through a threatening imperative to incite whomever he can with his voice. This does not morally endorse deception, and the following verse makes the limits of his authority clear.

In al-Isrāʾ 17:76, istifzāz concerns pressure and threats intended to drive Muhammad from his land.

In al-Isrāʾ 17:103, the same verb describes Pharaoh’s intention to drive out the Israelites.

A defensible semantic core is disturbing someone’s composure and steadiness and prompting reaction or movement through provocation, pressure, or threats.

Istifzāz may disturb a person inwardly or lead to displacement from their position and land. These uses share a semantic field, but context determines the outcome in each verse.

Morally, too, not all incitement is alike. Provocation can serve deception, oppression, and expulsion, while a sound warning can prompt reconsideration and responsible action.

Understanding and translating the Qur’an therefore requires distinguishing the act of incitement, its instrument, the agent’s purpose, and the response of the person being incited.

This distinction prevents istifzāz from being restricted to anger and fear, equated with completed expulsion, or interpreted as removing human responsibility in the face of provocation and deception.
