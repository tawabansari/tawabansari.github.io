The root S ʿ R: kindling, blazing, and intensification

Qur’anic and lexical forms

The Arabic root S ʿ R concerns kindling fire, blazing, and increasing heat and burning. Saʿara al-nāra means to kindle or stir up a fire; saʿīr denotes blazing fire; saʿʿara means to kindle or intensify its flames.

The family extends beyond physical fire to stirring up war, intense hunger and thirst, and agitation or madness. Siʿr, the price of goods, is also recorded under the same root, although its historical connection with kindling has not been clearly established.

In the reading of Ḥafṣ, three main forms occur in the Qur’an: saʿīr, suʿur, and suʿʿirat.

Saʿīr generally denotes the blazing fire of punishment. Suʿʿirat describes the Blazing Fire being kindled. Suʿur in Al-Qamar admits a broader range of explanations, including agitation, madness, severe suffering, and blazing fires.

These uses require distinguishing physical fire, figurative intensity and heat, disturbed behavior, and the separate price sense of siʿr.

1. Meaning in early Arabic

Kindling and intensifying fire

Attested early Arabic uses saʿara al-nāra for lighting a fire, stirring its flames, or intensifying its burning.

The focus is not simply the existence of fire but its being kindled or made to burn more strongly.

Saʿʿara al-nāra also carries this meaning. Form II can emphasize intensity, but its pattern alone does not prove that every use denotes a more intense stage than Form I. Actual usage determines the sense.

Istaʿarati al-nāru describes a fire flaring up or blazing.

Saʿīr names blazing fire or its flames. Lexical descriptions cover the fire itself, its flame, and its intensity.

A useful initial definition is:

Kindling and stirring up fire, or an increase in its flames and burning heat.

This begins with a concrete use. It does not by itself establish claims about punishment in the hereafter, illness, war, or human mental states.

Evidence attributed to pre-Islamic poetry

A poem attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays includes:

وَسَالِفَةٍ كَسَحُوقِ اللِّيَانِ
أَضْرَمَ فِيهَا الْغَوِيُّ السُّعُرْ

wa-sālifatin ka-saḥūqi al-liyāni / aḍrama fīhā al-ghawiyyu al-suʿur

“And a neck like a tall palm, in which a reckless man has kindled flames.”

The verse describes a horse; sālifah names part of its neck. The image is a tall palm whose burning has stripped its covering and exposed its length, compared with the horse’s long neck and short hair.

Al-suʿur here concerns fire and blazing. The accompanying verb aḍrama, kindled, reinforces that interpretation.

It attests a fire-related use of suʿur in poetry attributed to pre-Islamic Arabic.

Variants of some words exist, and attribution to Imruʾ al-Qays does not independently settle the exact date of this verse. It supports the early semantic field without establishing the word’s entire history.

Kindling war

Saʿara al-ḥarba and asʿara al-ḥarba are recorded for stirring up or intensifying war.

War is pictured as a fire that begins, spreads, or grows more intense.

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Al-Asʿar al-Juʿfī states:

فَلَا تَدْعُنِي الْأَقْوَامُ مِنْ آلِ مَالِكٍ
إِذَا أَنَا لَمْ أُسْعِرْ عَلَيْهِمْ وَأَثْقُبِ

fa-lā tadʿuni al-aqwāmu min āli Mālikin / idhā anā lam usʿir ʿalayhim wa-athqubi

“Let the people not count me among the house of Mālik if I do not kindle and set a blaze against them.”

The verse invokes hostile and warlike action through the image of kindling fire. Athqub here likewise concerns ignition, not piercing an opening in the opponents. The verse alone does not specify a literal fire at a particular place.

This illustrates the early linguistic connection between fire and intensified conflict.

As with other transmitted early poetry, its wording and precise date require consideration apart from the poet’s historical classification. It attests the imagery without settling every historical detail.

Suʿār: intense hunger, thirst, and agitation

Arabic records suʿār for intense hunger and thirst.

Suʿāru al-jūʿi denotes hunger so severe that it seems to burn within a person.

An early poetic witness transmitted in lexical works states:

تُسَمِّنُهَا بِأَخْثَرِ حَلْبَتَيْهَا
وَمَوْلَاكَ الْأَحَمُّ لَهُ سُعَارُ

tusamminuhā bi-akhthari ḥalbatayhā / wa-mawlāka al-aḥammu lahu suʿāru

“You fatten her with the thicker of her two milkings, while your closest kinsman suffers burning hunger.”

Suʿār here denotes intense hunger rather than an external fire.

A pre-Islamic date for this verse has not been established sufficiently here. It is evidence of recorded Arabic usage, not independent proof that this precise expression circulated before Islam.

Masʿūr also describes someone affected by severe agitation or madness.

Nāqatun masʿūratun describes a she-camel so restless and swift that it seems to have lost its ordinary behavioral control.

These uses are associated with intensity and heat, without proving that all historically arose through a direct comparison with fire.

2. Other branches and Qur’anic forms

Siʿr: price

Siʿr is the price at which goods are traded; its plural is asʿār.

In economic usage, tasʿīr concerns setting or announcing prices.

Persian also uses borrowings such as siʿr, asʿār, and tasʿīr in financial contexts.

The branch shares the root letters, but its historical relationship to blazing fire is uncertain.

Some lexical explanations compare prices with a fire’s rising intensity. Such an analogy is not by itself proof of historical derivation.

The economic sense does not occur in the Qur’an. Verses containing saʿīr should therefore not be interpreted through prices.

Qur’anic forms

The root occurs nineteen times in nineteen verses: sixteen instances of saʿīr, two of suʿur, and one of suʿʿirat in the reading of Ḥafṣ.

Saʿīr: a noun denoting blazing fire, sometimes definite with al- and sometimes indefinite, as saʿīran.

Suʿur: explained through severe agitation or madness, suffering, or as the plural of saʿīr. It occurs in Al-Qamar 54:24 and Al-Qamar 54:47.

Suʿʿirat: a Form II passive past verb, third-person feminine singular, meaning was kindled or made to blaze. It occurs in Al-Takwīr 81:12 in the reading of Ḥafṣ.

Saʿara, asʿara, istaʿara, tasaʿʿara, siʿr, suʿār, and misʿar are established Arabic forms but do not occur in those forms in the Qur’an.

Saʿīr is the most frequent Qur’anic derivative.

3. Saʿīr: blazing fire and punishment in the hereafter

Blazing fire, not merely a place-name

Al-Nisāʾ 4:10 states:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ يَأْكُلُونَ أَمْوَالَ الْيَتَامَىٰ ظُلْمًا إِنَّمَا يَأْكُلُونَ فِي بُطُونِهِمْ نَارًا وَسَيَصْلَوْنَ سَعِيرًا

inna alladhīna yaʾkulūna amwāla al-yatāmā ẓulman innamā yaʾkulūna fī buṭūnihim nāran wa-sa-yaṣlawna saʿīran

Al-Nisāʾ 4:10

“Those who unjustly consume orphans’ property consume nothing but fire in their bellies, and they will burn in a blazing fire.”

The verse contains both nāran and saʿīran.

It first describes unjustly consuming an orphan’s property as consuming fire, then states the offenders’ outcome in blazing fire.

Saʿīran describes that fire as kindled and burning.

The word alone does not establish a location necessarily separate from the Fire or Jahannam. Its blazing and burning character matters here.

Nor does saʿīr itself mean injustice to orphans. That conduct is identified at the verse’s beginning, while saʿīr describes its stated outcome.

Lexical meaning must be distinguished from the verse’s ethical context.

Jahannam and saʿīr

Al-Nisāʾ 4:55 states:

فَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ آمَنَ بِهِ وَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ صَدَّ عَنْهُ وَكَفَىٰ بِجَهَنَّمَ سَعِيرًا

fa-minhum man āmana bihi wa-minhum man ṣadda ʿanhu wa-kafā bi-jahannama saʿīran

Al-Nisāʾ 4:55

“Some of them believed in it, and some turned away from it; Jahannam suffices as a blazing fire.”

Jahannam and saʿīran occur together.

Jahannam identifies the place of punishment, while saʿīr highlights its blazing character.

The construction does not establish two independent places named Jahannam and Saʿīr.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:97 also states:

وَمَنْ يَهْدِ اللَّهُ فَهُوَ الْمُهْتَدِ وَمَنْ يُضْلِلْ فَلَنْ تَجِدَ لَهُمْ أَوْلِيَاءَ مِنْ دُونِهِ وَنَحْشُرُهُمْ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ عَلَىٰ وُجُوهِهِمْ عُمْيًا وَبُكْمًا وَصُمًّا مَأْوَاهُمْ جَهَنَّمُ كُلَّمَا خَبَتْ زِدْنَاهُمْ سَعِيرًا

wa-man yahdi Allāhu fa-huwa al-muhtadi wa-man yuḍlil fa-lan tajida lahum awliyāʾa min dūnihi wa-naḥshuruhum yawma al-qiyāmati ʿalā wujūhihim ʿumyan wa-bukman wa-ṣumman maʾwāhum jahannamu kullamā khabat zidnāhum saʿīran

Al-Isrāʾ 17:97

“Whomever God guides is guided; for those He leaves astray you will find no protectors apart from Him. We will gather them on the Day of Resurrection upon their faces, blind, mute, and deaf. Their refuge is Jahannam; whenever it subsides, We increase the blaze for them.”

This verse is particularly useful for understanding saʿīr.

Khabat describes the fire subsiding; zidnāhum saʿīran stands opposite it.

Saʿīr here concerns blazing and increasing flame.

The word is thus not merely an arbitrary name for an otherworldly place. The sense of a blazing fire remains active.

The verse does not fully explain the physical mechanism of burning in the hereafter. It speaks of subsiding and intensified fire without detailing every condition of that world.

Saʿīr as a final destination

Al-Shūrā 42:7 states:

وَكَذَٰلِكَ أَوْحَيْنَا إِلَيْكَ قُرْآنًا عَرَبِيًّا لِتُنْذِرَ أُمَّ الْقُرَىٰ وَمَنْ حَوْلَهَا وَتُنْذِرَ يَوْمَ الْجَمْعِ لَا رَيْبَ فِيهِ فَرِيقٌ فِي الْجَنَّةِ وَفَرِيقٌ فِي السَّعِيرِ

wa-kadhālika awḥaynā ilayka qurʾānan ʿarabiyyan li-tundhira umma al-qurā wa-man ḥawlahā wa-tundhira yawma al-jamʿi lā rayba fīhi farīqun fī al-jannati wa-farīqun fī al-saʿīri

Al-Shūrā 42:7

“Thus We revealed to you an Arabic Qur’an, so that you may warn the Mother of Towns and those around it, and warn of the Day of Gathering, about which there is no doubt: a group in the Garden and a group in the blazing fire.”

Al-jannah and al-saʿīr stand opposite each other.

Saʿīr can designate the fire as a setting of punishment: fī describes a group being in it.

That locative use does not sever its connection with blazing fire.

It identifies the destination or condition of the group opposite those in the Garden while retaining its fire sense.

Fire as the outcome of punishment also appears in Al-Isrāʾ 17:97, Al-Furqān 25:11, Al-Aḥzāb 33:64, Al-Fatḥ 48:13, Al-Insān 76:4, and Al-Inshiqāq 84:12.

Saʿīr and following Satan

Al-Ḥajj 22:4 describes a devil who misleads his follower and leads him to the punishment of the blazing fire.

Luqmān 31:21 contrasts Satan’s invitation to that punishment with the call to follow revelation.

Fāṭir 35:6 identifies Satan as an enemy and says he summons his party to become companions of the blazing fire.

These verses connect misguidance, following, and outcomes. Saʿīr itself does not mean Satan, misguidance, or disobedience.

Sabaʾ 34:12 describes making jinn who deviate from the divine command taste the punishment of the blazing fire.

Al-Mulk 67:5 mentions punishment prepared for devils. In Al-Mulk 67:10 and Al-Mulk 67:11, the fire’s companions acknowledge their failure to listen or reason and admit their sin.

Saʿīr is not confined to one offense or one group. These settings concern varied deeds, rejection, allegiance, and responsibility, while the word retains its blazing-fire sense.

4. Suʿur: agitation, madness, suffering, or flames?

Suʿur occurs twice in Al-Qamar, with particular lexical and contextual significance.

Recorded explanations include severe agitation or madness, suffering, and the plural of saʿīr, blazing fires.

Translation must therefore consider who speaks, what condition is described, and the surrounding passage.

Thamūd’s accusation: error and suʿur

Al-Qamar 54:24 states:

فَقَالُوا أَبَشَرًا مِنَّا وَاحِدًا نَتَّبِعُهُ إِنَّا إِذًا لَفِي ضَلَالٍ وَسُعُرٍ

fa-qālū a-basharan minnā wāḥidan nattabiʿuhu innā idhan la-fī ḍalālin wa-suʿurin

Al-Qamar 54:24

“They said: Are we to follow one human from among ourselves? Then we would indeed be in error and madness.”

These are Thamūd’s words rejecting Ṣāliḥ’s call.

They object to following a single human from among themselves.

Ḍalāl denotes error, while suʿur can describe the derangement with which the speakers equate following Ṣāliḥ.

That reading fits their objection: from their perspective, following such a person would demonstrate irrationality and failed judgment.

The word is not a diagnosis in modern medical terms. Early language could use such expressions to disparage foolishness, agitation, or unreasonable conduct.

Some early explanations instead give suffering and hardship: following Ṣāliḥ would, in their claim, bring them distress.

The pairing with ḍalāl and their objection to following a human favor “error and madness” in the interpretation adopted here.

It remains the rejecters’ claim, not the Qur’an’s own judgment on following Ṣāliḥ.

The same expression applied to offenders

Al-Qamar 54:47 states:

إِنَّ الْمُجْرِمِينَ فِي ضَلَالٍ وَسُعُرٍ

inna al-mujrimīna fī ḍalālin wa-suʿurin

Al-Qamar 54:47

“The offenders are indeed in error and blazing fires.”

The verse uses the same combination previously spoken by Thamūd:

«فِي ضَلَالٍ وَسُعُرٍ».

fī ḍalālin wa-suʿurin

Al-Qamar 54:47

“In error and suʿur”—here read as error and blazing fires.

This time Thamūd is not speaking; the expression concerns offenders.

The next verse, Al-Qamar 54:48, states:

يَوْمَ يُسْحَبُونَ فِي النَّارِ عَلَىٰ وُجُوهِهِمْ ذُوقُوا مَسَّ سَقَرَ

yawma yusḥabūna fī al-nāri ʿalā wujūhihim dhūqū massa saqara

Al-Qamar 54:48

“On the day they are dragged through the Fire upon their faces: Taste the touch of Saqar.”

This continuation strongly supports fire and punishment as the sense of suʿur in the preceding verse.

On that reading, suʿur in Al-Qamar 54:47 can be rendered “blazing fires” or “burning punishment.”

Other lexical interpretations retain madness and agitation here. They understand the verse as describing the offenders’ worldly confusion, with their punishment explained next.

Both have linguistic support. The immediate continuation in Al-Qamar 54:48 favors the fire interpretation adopted in this article.

The contrast between the occurrences

The repeated phrase ḍalālin wa-suʿurin creates a significant correspondence within Al-Qamar.

In Al-Qamar 54:24, Ṣāliḥ’s opponents claim that following him would bring error and madness.

Al-Qamar 54:47 uses the same words of the offenders’ condition.

The repetition can create a reversal: those calling obedience to divine warning error and madness face error and a burning outcome themselves.

Recognizing that correspondence does not require identical referents for suʿur in both places. Different contexts can draw on different parts of its semantic range.

Nor does that possibility create two unrelated roots. Both belong to S ʿ R and relate to intensity, burning, or agitation.

For Forqan, “madness” in Al-Qamar 54:24 and “blazing fires” in Al-Qamar 54:47 are defensible choices, provided the contextual reasoning and alternate lexical readings remain clear.

5. Suʿʿirat: the Blazing Fire being kindled

Al-Takwīr 81:12 states:

وَإِذَا الْجَحِيمُ سُعِّرَتْ

wa-idhā al-jaḥīmu suʿʿirat

Al-Takwīr 81:12

“And when the Blazing Fire is kindled.”

In the reading of Ḥafṣ, suʿʿirat is a Form II passive past verb, third-person feminine singular. With idhā in this passage, it describes a future event, not a report of an already completed occurrence.

Its active counterpart is saʿʿara: to kindle or intensify fire.

The passive does not name the agent; al-jaḥīm is what the kindling is predicated of.

The construction describes an action undergone by the Blazing Fire: its being set ablaze.

Other recognized readings have suʿirat without gemination, a Form I passive. Both belong to the field of kindling and blazing.

The morphological difference alone does not establish two separate events or definite stages of punishment.

The surrounding passage

Al-Takwīr opens with major transformations of the familiar world and the human condition.

It describes the sun being folded up, stars falling or losing their light, mountains moving, animals being gathered, records being opened, and other events.

The Blazing Fire is then kindled and the Garden brought near.

Following these descriptions, Al-Takwīr 81:14 states:

عَلِمَتْ نَفْسٌ مَا أَحْضَرَتْ

ʿalimat nafsun mā aḥḍarat

Al-Takwīr 81:14

“A soul will know what it has brought.”

Suʿʿirat is part of this scene of judgment.

The verb alone does not specify the material of the Blazing Fire, its physical combustion mechanism, or the complete sequence of its kindling.

Its meaning is clear: being kindled or made to blaze.

Further details require consideration of other verses.

6. Comparison with related roots

S ʿ R and W Q D: blazing and kindling

W Q D concerns lighting or kindling fire.

Awqada al-nāra means to kindle fire; waqūd denotes what fuels it.

Saʿara can likewise mean kindling, with blazing intensity prominent in its attested uses.

The roots overlap. A fire that is lit may blaze, and added fuel or air may intensify it.

This does not establish that W Q D always denotes the beginning and S ʿ R a later stage. Usage does not support that absolute division.

S ʿ R and L H B: blazing and flame

Lahab denotes flame.

Dhāta lahabin occurs in Al-Masad 111:3.

Saʿīr can also refer to fire and its blazing flames.

Lahab more directly names the flame, while saʿīr denotes a blazing fire or its condition of burning.

The distinction is not complete separation: a saʿīr fire has flames, and flame manifests its burning.

S ʿ R and J Ḥ M: blazing fire and jaḥīm

Jaḥīm is used in the Qur’an for the burning fire of punishment.

Al-Takwīr 81:12 places the two families together:

«الْجَحِيمُ سُعِّرَتْ».

al-jaḥīmu suʿʿirat

Al-Takwīr 81:12

“The Blazing Fire is kindled.”

Al-jaḥīm is the subject under discussion, while suʿʿirat expresses its being kindled.

Their grammatical roles differ.

Both belong to fire and burning. Comparing them does not justify an unsupported classification of separate places or levels of punishment.

S ʿ R and S J R: kindling and sajr

Al-Takwīr 81:6 states:

وَإِذَا الْبِحَارُ سُجِّرَتْ

wa-idhā al-biḥāru sujjirat

Al-Takwīr 81:6

“And when the seas are set ablaze.”

Sujjirat belongs to S J R, not S ʿ R. The translation above follows the kindling interpretation; filling or overflowing also figures in explanations of the verse and belongs to that root’s separate study.

Arabic S J R includes filling, heaping up, and firing an oven or fire.

Sujjirat and suʿʿirat may thus receive similar translations in some contexts without being forms of one root.

The subject in Al-Takwīr 81:6 is the seas, while in Al-Takwīr 81:12 it is the Blazing Fire.

Both subject and root must remain distinct. Similar translations do not establish identity across their entire semantic fields.

S ʿ R and Ṣ L Y: blazing fire and exposure to it

Qur’anic Ṣ L Y concerns entering or being exposed to fire and experiencing its burning.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:10 combines the two families in wa-sa-yaṣlawna saʿīran.

Sa-yaṣlawna expresses what will happen to the people, while saʿīran identifies the blazing fire.

One describes human exposure to fire; the other describes the fire.

The whole meaning of a person’s burning should not be inserted into saʿīr’s lexical definition, although burning is the outcome stated here.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Translation should distinguish blazing fire, being kindled, and the contextual senses of suʿur in Al-Qamar.

“Blazing fire” is a natural base rendering for saʿīr.

“Kindled fire” or “burning fire” may also fit particular sentences.

“Flames” may be preferable where the passage highlights the flames and their intensity.

Suʿʿirat in Al-Takwīr 81:12 means “was kindled” or “was made to blaze.”

Within wa-idhā al-jaḥīmu suʿʿirat, “when the Blazing Fire is kindled” naturally conveys the future scene.

“Madness” or “severe derangement” fits Thamūd’s claim in Al-Qamar 54:24.

In Al-Qamar 54:47, the next verse’s dragging of offenders through fire favors “blazing fires” or “burning punishment.”

These are contextual readings of one form, not two independent roots.

Suitable equivalents outside the Qur’an include:

  • Saʿara al-nāra: kindled or stirred up the fire.
  • Asʿara al-ḥarba: provoked or kindled war.
  • Istaʿarati al-nāru: the fire blazed up.
  • Suʿāru al-jūʿi: burning or intense hunger.
  • Masʿūr: agitated or restless; in the madness sense, deranged.
  • Siʿr: the price of goods.
  • Tasʿīr: kindling or intensifying fire; in economic usage, setting a price.

Qur’anic saʿīr should not be rendered as high prices, madness, or war, since those senses do not fit its occurrences.

Conversely, the two instances of suʿur should not be forced into an identical referent merely because they share a written form.

Conclusion

S ʿ R concerns kindling fire, blazing, and intensified burning.

Related imagery appears in war, severe hunger, and agitation. That does not prove a single concrete historical origin for every branch.

Siʿr, price, is recorded under the same family, but its historical connection with fire is not secure enough to derive its economic meaning from burning.

The main Qur’anic field is the blazing fire of punishment.

Saʿīr denotes blazing fire and can identify punishment’s setting or outcome. Suʿʿirat describes the Blazing Fire being kindled.

Suʿur has two notable occurrences in Al-Qamar. In Al-Qamar 54:24, Thamūd calls following Ṣāliḥ error and madness; in Al-Qamar 54:47, the same combination describes offenders and is followed by punishment in fire.

The repetition creates a meaningful correspondence: language used to discredit following the messenger returns in the description of offenders’ outcome.

The principal branch can therefore be defined as follows:

S ʿ R concerns kindling and blazing fire and intensified burning. Other uses extend to severe agitation, derangement, or suffering; siʿr, price, is a lexical branch whose historical connection with kindling remains unproven.

“Blazing fire” is the best base rendering for saʿīr. Suʿur in each Al-Qamar occurrence requires attention to the speaker, context, and neighboring verses.

These distinctions retain the concrete sense of blazing while avoiding unrelated economic, medical, or philosophical meanings in Qur’anic interpretation.
