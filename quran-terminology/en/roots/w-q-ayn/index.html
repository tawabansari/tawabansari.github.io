Descending, occurring, coming to pass, and taking a position

The Arabic root w-q-ʿ denotes something descending, striking a place, an event occurring, or something coming to pass. Waqaʿa can describe an object falling, a bird alighting, an incident happening, harm reaching someone, or a promise or judgment being fulfilled.

Wuqūʿ is the verbal noun; wāqiʿ describes what descends or comes to pass; wāqiʿah names an event that occurs; and mawqiʿ denotes the place where something lands or settles.

In the Qur’an, this family appears in several settings: a mountain threatening to fall, falling in prostration, punishment arriving, a promise being fulfilled, truth becoming established, hostility being caused, the Resurrection occurring, and the positions of stars.

Understanding these uses requires distinguishing physical descent from the broader sense of happening or coming to pass. Not everything that “occurs” is a body falling from above.

1. The root in early and classical Arabic

In early Arabic, waqaʿa al-shayʾu describes something falling or descending. When a bird stops flying and settles on the ground or a branch, one says waqaʿa al-ṭāʾiru. Rain reaching the ground can also be described in terms of wuqūʿ.

Besides descent itself, waqʿ can refer to the effect of impact. The sound of hooves striking the ground is called waqʿ al-ḥawāfir. Waqʿ al-maṭar likewise refers in lexical usage to the sound of rain hitting the ground, particularly heavy rain.

These uses share a clear relationship: something moves, reaches a place, and leaves an effect through contact or impact.

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Aʿshā Bāhilah reads:

لَمْ تَرَ أَرْضًا وَلَمْ تَسْمَعْ بِسَاكِنِهَا
إِلَّا بِهَا مِنْ نَوَادِي وَقْعِهِ أَثَرُ

lam tara arḍan wa-lam tasmaʿ bi-sākinihā / illā bihā min nawādī waqʿihi atharu

Translation: “You see no land, nor hear of its inhabitants, without some trace of his arrivals remaining there.”

The poet praises the man commemorated in the elegy, whose presence and journeys left traces in different lands.

Waqʿihi here concerns his arrival or descent. Its association with athar, “trace,” highlights a presence that leaves a mark.

The verse is transmitted in an ode attributed to Aʿshā Bāhilah and appears in classical literary collections and lexical commentaries. Although the poet belongs to the pre-Islamic period, the ode survives through later transmission, with variant readings in some verses. It is therefore useful evidence for early usage, without establishing every historical or textual detail beyond doubt.

Another verse in the same ode concerns severe cold and its effect on animals. Some lexical transmissions give mawqūʿ al-ṣaqīʿ, referring to the effect of frost settling; others give mawḍūʿ al-ṣaqīʿ. Because the reading varies, that verse cannot serve as independent, textually certain evidence for w-q-ʿ.

In early Arabic, waqʿah and waqīʿah also name a fierce clash in battle. Waqāʾiʿ al-ʿArab is an expression for tribal battles and days of warfare. Here, the encounter between two groups and the resulting event are prominent.

Other uses are also recorded. Waqīʿah can mean speaking ill of someone or finding fault with them, while tawaqquʿ concerns expecting something to happen. Tawqīʿ has also been associated with marking, leaving an impression, or arranging rhythm.

These branches show uses extending beyond physical movement and impact. Historical evidence is still needed before claiming that they all arose at one time or from a single original image.

Three features are well supported in early usage: descending and arriving, impact and its effects, and an event occurring or coming to pass.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important forms recorded in Arabic include:

  • وَقَعَ (waqaʿa): fell, descended, happened, or came to pass.
  • وُقُوع (wuqūʿ): descent, occurrence, or realization.
  • وَقْع (waqʿ): descent, impact, or the sound it produces.
  • وَقْعَة (waqʿah): a single occurrence; also an incident or fierce encounter.
  • وَقِيعَة (waqīʿah): an incident or battle; elsewhere, disparagement or fault-finding.
  • وَاقِع (wāqiʿ): descending, occurring, or coming to pass.
  • وَاقِعَة (wāqiʿah): an event; sometimes a great and severe event.
  • مَوْقِع (mawqiʿ): a site of occurrence or landing; also a position or effect.
  • مَوَاقِع (mawāqiʿ): plural of mawqiʿ, positions or sites of occurrence.
  • أَوْقَعَ (awqaʿa): brought something down or caused something to happen.
  • إِيقَاع (īqāʿ): causing or bringing something about.
  • وَاقَعَ (wāqaʿa): encountered something or entered into a clash.
  • مُوَاقَعَة (muwāqaʿah): encounter or engagement; in some uses, sexual intercourse.
  • تَوَقَّعَ (tawaqqaʿa): expected an event or considered its likelihood.
  • تَوَقُّع (tawaqquʿ): expectation or anticipation of an occurrence.
  • تَوْقِيع (tawqīʿ): in later uses, marking, a signature, or arranging rhythm.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an.

Qur’anic forms include waqaʿa and its inflections, yūqiʿa, wāqiʿ, wāqiʿah, waqʿah, mawāqiʿ, and muwāqiʿūhā. The family occurs 24 times in 22 verses.

Waqaʿa is a Form I verb, yūqiʿa belongs to Form IV, and muwāqiʿūhā derives from Form III. The nominal forms have different functions as well.

For example, wāqiʿ can describe an event that has not yet happened but is presented as certain to occur. The active participle alone does not establish that an event happened in the past.

3. Qur’anic uses

1. Descending and settling: physical occurrence

Al-Ḥajj 22:65

أَلَمْ تَرَ أَنَّ اللَّهَ سَخَّرَ لَكُم مَّا فِي الْأَرْضِ وَالْفُلْكَ تَجْرِي فِي الْبَحْرِ بِأَمْرِهِ وَيُمْسِكُ السَّمَاءَ أَن تَقَعَ عَلَى الْأَرْضِ إِلَّا بِإِذْنِهِ ۗ إِنَّ اللَّهَ بِالنَّاسِ لَرَءُوفٌ رَّحِيمٌ

a-lam tara anna Allāha sakhkhara lakum mā fī al-arḍi wa-al-fulka tajrī fī al-baḥri bi-amrihi wa-yumsiku al-samāʾa an taqaʿa ʿalā al-arḍi illā bi-idhnihi inna Allāha bi-al-nāsi la-raʾūfun raḥīm

Al-Ḥajj 22:65

Translation: “Have you not seen that The God has subjected to you whatever is on earth, and the ships sailing on the sea by His command, and that He holds the sky back from falling upon the earth except by His permission? Indeed, The God is tenderly compassionate and merciful toward people.”

Here, taqaʿa ʿalā al-arḍ describes the sky falling upon the earth.

The preposition ʿalā specifies the relationship between what might fall and what it would fall upon.

This use is close to the root’s physical image. Nevertheless, the verse does not explain the sky’s physical structure, material, or the mechanism by which it is held. Those details cannot be extracted from taqaʿa alone.

A comparable use appears in Al-Aʿrāf 7:171: the mountain is raised above a group, and they think it will fall upon them.

Wāqiʿun bihim describes something that has not yet fallen at that moment, but whose fall they anticipate.

The active participle wāqiʿ can therefore refer to an expected event, not necessarily one already completed.

2. Fa-qaʿū: falling in prostration

Al-Ḥijr 15:29

فَإِذَا سَوَّيْتُهُ وَنَفَخْتُ فِيهِ مِن رُّوحِي فَقَعُوا لَهُ سَاجِدِينَ

fa-idhā sawwaytuhu wa-nafakhtu fīhi min rūḥī fa-qaʿū lahu sājidīn

Al-Ḥijr 15:29

Translation: “Then, when I have fashioned him and breathed into him of My spirit, fall down before him in prostration.”

The command is repeated in Ṣād 38:72.

In fa-qaʿū, fa- is a connecting particle and qaʿū is the plural imperative of waqaʿa. Here the verb is accompanied by sājidīn, describing the state of those addressed.

In ordinary usage, wuqūʿ can refer to a body descending; in this construction, prostration is specified by sājidīn.

Fa-qaʿū lahu sājidīn thus means falling down in a state of prostration.

Prostration should not be derived from w-q-ʿ alone: the same root describes a mountain falling, an event arriving, or a promise coming to pass.

Nor should the verb be reduced to “obey,” removing the explicit image of falling in prostration. Obedience can be inferred from the action’s context, but the construction directly expresses falling in prostration.

3. An affliction or harm reaching people

Al-Aʿrāf 7:134

وَلَمَّا وَقَعَ عَلَيْهِمُ الرِّجْزُ قَالُوا يَا مُوسَى ادْعُ لَنَا رَبَّكَ بِمَا عَهِدَ عِندَكَ ۖ لَئِن كَشَفْتَ عَنَّا الرِّجْزَ لَنُؤْمِنَنَّ لَكَ وَلَنُرْسِلَنَّ مَعَكَ بَنِي إِسْرَائِيلَ

wa-lammā waqaʿa ʿalayhimu al-rijzu qālū yā Mūsā udʿu lanā rabbaka bi-mā ʿahida ʿindaka la-in kashafta ʿannā al-rijza la-nuʾminanna laka wa-la-nursilanna maʿaka banī Isrāʾīl

Al-Aʿrāf 7:134

Translation: “When the affliction fell upon them, they said: ‘Moses, call upon your Lord for us by what He has covenanted with you. If you remove this affliction from us, we will surely believe you and send the Children of Israel with you.’”

Waqaʿa ʿalayhimu al-rijz describes the affliction reaching them and their becoming subject to it.

Al-rijz is the grammatical subject, naming what has befallen them; ʿalayhim identifies those affected.

Combining waqaʿa with ʿalā here presents something arriving upon a person or group and exposing them to its consequences.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:71, Hūd likewise tells his people that rijsun wa-ghaḍabun has befallen them from their Lord.

Rijs and rijz must be distinguished: they belong to separate roots. Their association with waqaʿa does not make them identical in meaning.

Nor does occurrence itself determine the kind, severity, or duration of the affliction. Those features come from other words and the context.

4. Truth becoming established and falsehood ending

Al-Aʿrāf 7:118

فَوَقَعَ الْحَقُّ وَبَطَلَ مَا كَانُوا يَعْمَلُونَ

fa-waqaʿa al-ḥaqqu wa-baṭala mā kānū yaʿmalūn

Al-Aʿrāf 7:118

Translation: “Thus the truth became established, and what they were doing came to nothing.”

This verse occurs in the account of Moses confronting the magicians.

The magicians perform something that affects people’s sight. Moses then casts his staff, and what they have contrived is undone.

Fa-waqaʿa al-ḥaqq does not describe a body falling from above. It concerns truth becoming manifest, established, and realized.

Wa-baṭala mā kānū yaʿmalūn clarifies the opposite outcome: the magicians’ work becomes ineffective and comes to nothing.

This use shows that wuqūʿ can convey establishment or realization.

Waqaʿa and ḥaqq are nevertheless not synonyms. The verb expresses occurrence or realization, while al-ḥaqq names what becomes established.

Goodness and truthfulness do not come from w-q-ʿ itself. Here, al-ḥaqq specifies what is realized and gives the statement its direction.

5. The word coming to pass: judgment and promise

Al-Naml 27:85

وَوَقَعَ الْقَوْلُ عَلَيْهِم بِمَا ظَلَمُوا فَهُمْ لَا يَنطِقُونَ

wa-waqaʿa al-qawlu ʿalayhim bi-mā ẓalamū fa-hum lā yanṭiqūn

Al-Naml 27:85

Translation: “The word came to pass against them because they had done wrong, and they do not speak.”

Waqaʿa al-qawlu ʿalayhim also appears in Al-Naml 27:82.

Al-qawl here is not merely a sound reaching someone’s ears. The expression concerns a declaration or judgment coming into effect against a group.

ʿAlayhim identifies those to whom its fulfillment pertains.

In Al-Naml 27:85, bi-mā ẓalamū gives the reason it applies to them. The word coming to pass is linked to their wrongdoing.

In such uses, waqaʿa can be rendered “came to pass,” “took effect,” or “became binding upon them,” according to context.

Yūnus 10:51 also concerns the occurrence of punishment whose arrival the opponents had previously urged. Waqaʿa refers to the stage when a threat ceases to be merely anticipated and becomes an experienced event.

The distinction between a promise before fulfillment and its actual occurrence matters. An event may be discussed without having happened. Using waqaʿa of it brings its realization or arrival into focus.

6. A reward becoming incumbent upon The God

Al-Nisāʾ 4:100

وَمَن يُهَاجِرْ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ يَجِدْ فِي الْأَرْضِ مُرَاغَمًا كَثِيرًا وَسَعَةً ۚ وَمَن يَخْرُجْ مِن بَيْتِهِ مُهَاجِرًا إِلَى اللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ ثُمَّ يُدْرِكْهُ الْمَوْتُ فَقَدْ وَقَعَ أَجْرُهُ عَلَى اللَّهِ ۗ وَكَانَ اللَّهُ غَفُورًا رَّحِيمًا

wa-man yuhājir fī sabīli Allāhi yajid fī al-arḍi murāghaman kathīran wa-saʿatan wa-man yakhruj min baytihi muhājiran ilā Allāhi wa-rasūlihi thumma yudrikhu al-mawtu fa-qad waqaʿa ajruhu ʿalā Allāhi wa-kāna Allāhu ghafūran raḥīmā

Al-Nisāʾ 4:100

Translation: “Whoever migrates in the way of The God will find many places of refuge and ample room on earth. Whoever leaves his home migrating to The God and His Messenger, and is then overtaken by death, his reward has become incumbent upon The God. The God is forgiving and merciful.”

Waqaʿa ajruhu ʿalā Allāh concerns the reward of someone who has begun migration but dies before reaching the destination.

The expression does not describe a reward physically falling. It indicates that the reward has become established and assured.

The situation is also significant: the person has begun the journey, but death prevents its completion. The Qur’an does not restrict the reward to physically reaching the destination.

This illustrates occurrence being used of an obligation that has become established within a specified relationship.

The root alone cannot determine the amount of the reward or how it is received. The verse does not specify these details.

Nor does ʿalā Allāh mean that an external party has imposed an obligation on The God against His will. The God Himself states this promise.

7. Yūqiʿa: bringing about hostility and hatred

Al-Māʾidah 5:91

إِنَّمَا يُرِيدُ الشَّيْطَانُ أَن يُوقِعَ بَيْنَكُمُ الْعَدَاوَةَ وَالْبَغْضَاءَ فِي الْخَمْرِ وَالْمَيْسِرِ وَيَصُدَّكُمْ عَن ذِكْرِ اللَّهِ وَعَنِ الصَّلَاةِ ۖ فَهَلْ أَنتُم مُّنتَهُونَ

innamā yurīdu al-shayṭānu an yūqiʿa baynakumu al-ʿadāwata wa-al-baghḍāʾa fī al-khamri wa-al-maysiri wa-yaṣuddakum ʿan dhikri Allāhi wa-ʿani al-ṣalāti fa-hal antum muntahūn

Al-Māʾidah 5:91

Translation: “Satan only wishes, through intoxicants and gambling, to cause hostility and hatred among you and to turn you away from the remembrance of The God and from prayer. Will you then desist?”

Yūqiʿa belongs to Form IV and here means causing or bringing something about.

With the simple waqaʿa, the event itself may happen. With awqaʿa, an agent can cause it to happen.

Al-ʿadāwata wa-al-baghḍāʾ supplies the verb’s objects: hostility and hatred are what Satan wishes to create among people.

Baynakum specifies the social relationship involved. The issue is hostility and hatred arising between people.

This does not mean that w-q-ʿ inherently signifies corruption or hostility. Those meanings come from the particular objects in the verse.

For example, a disagreement may arise between two people; someone who deliberately creates the conditions for it or intensifies it contributes to its occurrence. This distinction between an event happening and someone causing it is visible in waqaʿa and awqaʿa.

Besides hostility and hatred, Al-Māʾidah 5:91 mentions turning people away from remembrance of The God and prayer. These are distinct consequences and should not all be folded into yūqiʿa itself.

8. Wāqiʿ: an event presented as certain to occur

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:6

وَإِنَّ الدِّينَ لَوَاقِعٌ

wa-inna al-dīna la-wāqiʿ

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:6

Translation: “And indeed, the Recompense will surely occur.”

Wāqiʿ is the active participle of waqaʿa.

Here, occurrence concerns something announced whose realization is emphatically affirmed.

Al-dīn in this context concerns recompense and reckoning. Translating it as “religion will occur,” with religion understood as a belief system or religious law, would not convey the intended sense.

The same la-wāqiʿun construction describes the Lord’s punishment in Al-Ṭūr 52:7 and what has been promised in Al-Mursalāt 77:7.

Al-Maʿārij 70:1 also uses ʿadhābin wāqiʿin of a punishment presented as certain to occur.

These uses show that wāqiʿ need not describe a completed past event. It can concern a future occurrence presented as inevitable.

In Al-Shūrā 42:22, wa-huwa wāqiʿun bihim concerns a consequence feared by wrongdoers that will befall them.

Future certainty comes from the complete sentence, its emphatic features, and the context of the divine promise. The participle wāqiʿ does not by itself guarantee certain future occurrence in every Arabic usage.

9. Al-wāqiʿah: the great event of Resurrection

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:1

إِذَا وَقَعَتِ الْوَاقِعَةُ

idhā waqaʿati al-wāqiʿah

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:1

Translation: “When the Event occurs.”

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:2

لَيْسَ لِوَقْعَتِهَا كَاذِبَةٌ

laysa li-waqʿatihā kādhibah

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:2

Translation: “Its occurrence cannot be denied.”

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:15

فَيَوْمَئِذٍ وَقَعَتِ الْوَاقِعَةُ

fa-yawmaʾidhin waqaʿati al-wāqiʿah

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:15

Translation: “Then, on that day, the Event will occur.”

Wāqiʿah is the feminine active participle of waqaʿa, but in nominal use it can name the event itself.

In Arabic, wāqiʿah denotes an event that occurs and can refer to a great and severe event.

In Al-Wāqiʿah and Al-Ḥāqqah, the designation concerns the immense event of Resurrection.

In idhā waqaʿati al-wāqiʿah, the verb and noun share one root. Their combination emphasizes the occurrence of that very event.

The root w-q-ʿ itself does not mean Resurrection. Wāqiʿah is its designation here, as a general word can name a particular event in a specific context.

Waqʿatihā in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:2 is a noun of a single occurrence. The suffix -hā refers back to al-wāqiʿah in the preceding verse.

Laysa li-waqʿatihā kādhibah can be explained with emphasis on the impossibility of denying the event or on the absence of falsehood in the announcement of its occurrence. Either way, the expression affirms that it truly and undeniably occurs.

The expression should not be turned into a theory about celestial motion, the earth falling, or the physical structure of Resurrection. Any such details stated elsewhere must be examined in those passages.

10. Mawāqiʿ al-nujūm: the positions of the stars

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:75

فَلَا أُقْسِمُ بِمَوَاقِعِ النُّجُومِ

fa-lā uqsimu bi-mawāqiʿi al-nujūm

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:75

Translation: “So I swear by the positions of the stars.”

Mawāqiʿ is the plural of mawqiʿ: a place of occurrence, descent, or settlement.

In physical uses, mawqiʿ can be where a bird alights or rain reaches. More broadly, it can denote where something is situated or takes effect.

In mawāqiʿ al-nujūm, these positions are attributed to the stars.

Several interpretations are possible:

First, the stars’ positions and locations in the sky.

Second, the places or times at which they set and disappear from an earthly observer’s sight.

Third, the stations or positions they occupy in their apparent movement across the sky.

These possibilities arise from recognized uses of mawqiʿ and its relationship with nujūm. They should not all be inserted into the basic translation at once.

“The positions of the stars” is a natural, broad rendering that leaves room for further examination in the commentary.

Reflection may discuss the ordering of stellar positions, their apparent motion, and the vastness of the heavens. But a particular astronomical theory, stellar distances in light-years, or the physical mechanisms of stellar death should not be presented as the certain lexical meaning of mawāqiʿ.

Some exegetical interpretations connect nujūm with successive portions of Qur’anic revelation. That reading requires separate examination of the evidence for najm and the passage’s context. Its existence does not eliminate the direct reading concerning stellar positions.

The following verses speak of the greatness of the oath and of a noble Qur’an. This continuity matters when reflecting on signs in creation and revelation, while mawāqiʿ retains the sense of positions or sites of occurrence.

11. Muwāqiʿūhā: encountering and falling into

Al-Kahf 18:53

وَرَأَى الْمُجْرِمُونَ النَّارَ فَظَنُّوا أَنَّهُم مُّوَاقِعُوهَا وَلَمْ يَجِدُوا عَنْهَا مَصْرِفًا

wa-raʾā al-mujrimūna al-nāra fa-ẓannū annahum muwāqiʿūhā wa-lam yajidū ʿanhā maṣrifā

Al-Kahf 18:53

Translation: “The guilty saw the Fire and expected that they would fall into it, and they found no way of escape from it.”

Muwāqiʿūhā is a plural active participle from Form III, with -hā referring to the Fire.

Here, muwāqaʿah concerns encountering or becoming involved with something.

The same form can describe an encounter in warfare or a clash between two sides. Form III alone, however, does not establish equal participation by both parties.

In Al-Kahf 18:53, the guilty see the Fire and anticipate falling into it. Ẓann here has been understood as a strong expectation or as certainty; it should not automatically be reduced to a weak conjecture. This concerns ẓ-n-n and should be distinguished from the meaning of muwāqiʿūhā.

Wa-lam yajidū ʿanhā maṣrifā also indicates that they find no way to turn away or escape from it.

Muwāqiʿūhā therefore refers here to falling into or becoming caught in the Fire. The physical details of entry and encounter do not follow from the derivative alone.

It is semantically related to simple waqaʿa, while Form III and what is encountered bring confrontation and engagement into focus.

4. Interpretive challenges and changing usage

One challenge is the modern Persian word vāqeʿiyyat, “reality.”

It generally denotes what truly exists or corresponds to what is. This relates to some uses of wuqūʿ, but the full modern philosophical meaning of reality should not be projected onto Qur’anic w-q-ʿ.

For example, fa-waqaʿa al-ḥaqq in Al-Aʿrāf 7:118 concerns truth becoming established in a confrontation. It does not make waqaʿa and ḥaqq one root or one lexical concept.

Likewise, wāqiʿ in punishment passages primarily describes an event that comes to pass. The word alone offers no theory distinguishing subjective and objective reality.

In contemporary language, tawaqquʿ concerns expectation or prediction. It relates to w-q-ʿ because someone anticipates something not yet realized. But tawaqquʿ does not occur as a separate derivative of this root in the Qur’an.

Tawqīʿ also commonly refers to a signature in Persian and Arabic today. The history of this use should not be equated with the direct Qur’anic meaning of waqaʿa.

Something happening does not determine its ethical value. Truth becomes established, punishment occurs, a reward becomes incumbent, and Satan seeks to create hostility. The subject or object involved and the context determine each case’s value.

5. Comparison with related roots

W-q-ʿ and s-q-ṭ: occurrence and falling

Suqūṭ primarily concerns falling or moving downward.

Wuqūʿ can express this too, but has a wider range, including promises being fulfilled, events arising, and judgments taking effect.

In Al-Ḥajj 22:65, taqaʿa ʿalā al-arḍ concerns falling. But fa-waqaʿa al-ḥaqq in Al-Aʿrāf 7:118 cannot be translated as truth physically falling.

Suqūṭ and wuqūʿ overlap in some uses without being exact synonyms.

Study of s-q-ṭ

W-q-ʿ and n-z-l: occurrence and descent

Nuzūl concerns descending or coming down and is used in the Qur’an of revelation, rain, and other things.

Wuqūʿ can express arrival and descent, but many passages emphasize an event coming to pass or its effects reaching someone.

For example, waqaʿa ʿalayhimu al-rijz concerns affliction reaching them. It does not by itself specify a physical path of descent.

The distinction is a tendency, not an absolute boundary across every use.

Study of n-z-l

W-q-ʿ and ḥ-q-q: occurrence and truth becoming established

Ḥ-q-q concerns truth, right, firmness, and the establishment of something certain.

Wuqūʿ emphasizes something happening or becoming established.

In fa-waqaʿa al-ḥaqq, truth is what becomes established and manifest; occurrence expresses its realization.

The concepts can converge, but not everything that happens is “right” or “true” in an ethical or epistemic sense. An unjust event can also occur.

An event occurring and its being right are therefore two distinct judgments.

Study of ḥ-q-q

W-q-ʿ and w-j-b: realization and becoming binding

Wajaba relates in some early uses to falling or descending, and elsewhere to something becoming established or obligatory.

Waqaʿa can likewise express a judgment taking effect or becoming binding in constructions such as waqaʿa al-qawlu ʿalayhim.

This proximity does not equate all uses of the roots. Wuqūʿ describes a mountain falling or people falling in prostration, whereas legal or obligatory wujūb has another particular domain.

Study of w-j-b

W-q-ʿ and l-q-y: occurrence and encounter

Liqāʾ concerns meeting or encountering.

Muwāqaʿah can also approach the sense of a clash or encounter, but not every w-q-ʿ derivative means meeting.

In Al-Kahf 18:53, muwāqiʿūhā describes an encounter linked with becoming caught in the Fire. This is different from an ordinary or friendly meeting.

Study of l-q-y

W-q-ʿ and w-q-f: alighting and standing

Waqafa concerns standing or stopping movement.

Waqaʿa can describe something reaching or settling in a place, without itself implying a permanent stop.

A bird alighting on a branch may fly again. An event may occur and then end.

Settling and standing are thus related but distinct notions.

6. Translation recommendations for Forqan

For physical waqaʿa, “fell,” “descended,” or “settled upon” are suitable.

For events, “happened,” “occurred,” or “came to pass” are natural.

For a promise or judgment, “was fulfilled,” “took effect,” or “became established” may fit.

For fa-qaʿū sājidīn, “fall down in prostration” clearly preserves the construction.

For yūqiʿa baynakumu al-ʿadāwah, “cause hostility among you” is suitable.

Wāqiʿ may be “occurring” or “descending,” or, in contexts of promise and punishment, “bound to occur” or “certain to come to pass.”

For al-wāqiʿah, “the Event” or “that great event” is suggested. The familiar Persian title Vāqeʿah can also be retained in the Persian version.

Waqʿatihā can be rendered “its occurrence” or “its happening.”

For mawāqiʿ al-nujūm, “the positions of the stars” is preferable to a rendering that decides in advance on one particular movement or kind of setting.

For muwāqiʿūhā in Al-Kahf 18:53, “they will fall into it” or “they will be caught in it” is appropriate.

Translation should not be confined to one fixed equivalent. Physical impact, social events, judgments taking effect, and divine promises occur in different constructions, each calling for a contextually fitting rendering.

7. Conclusion

Arabic w-q-ʿ ranges from something descending and striking a place to an event happening or coming to pass.

Physically, something falls, descends, or settles somewhere. With events, something happens or its consequences reach someone. With promises and judgments, what was previously announced reaches fulfillment.

The Qur’an uses this family for the sky or a mountain falling, falling in prostration, punishment occurring, truth becoming established, a declaration taking effect, a reward becoming incumbent, hostility being caused, stellar positions, and the Resurrection occurring.

Its derivatives cannot all be reduced to “falling from above.” Equally, physical descent should not be erased where a passage actually concerns something falling.

Wāqiʿah in Al-Wāqiʿah and Al-Ḥāqqah names the great event of Resurrection, without making Resurrection the root’s meaning. Mawāqiʿ denotes positions or sites of occurrence, while muwāqiʿūhā in Al-Kahf concerns falling into and becoming caught in the Fire.

The root has no fixed ethical value in itself. Truth can become established, punishment can occur, and hostility can be brought about. The subject or object involved, the sentence structure, and the context determine each case’s value and character.

The root w-q-ʿ denotes something descending or settling and, more broadly, an event happening or coming to pass. Sometimes movement and impact are central; sometimes an effect reaching a person or place; and sometimes a promise, judgment, or anticipated event becoming realized.
