From the small mustard seed to precise accounting of deeds and bringing forth what human eyes cannot see

خَرْدَل (khardal) is the Arabic name of a familiar plant with small edible seeds. Persian likewise calls it khardal. Its seeds have long been known in food, condiments, and some historical medicinal uses.

Early Arabic used the mustard seed in speech and comparison because of its small size and weight. Someone describing a very slight quantity could compare it to a mustard seed. This comparison was especially relevant to justice, rights, and accounting, where even something as small as a seed could receive attention.

Khardal does not lexically mean “something very small,” however. It names a real plant with small seeds. Their recognized smallness allows the name to function in a comparison.

The Qur’an uses the word in two verses. Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:47 places the mustard seed within the just scales and the accounting on the Day of Resurrection. In Luqmān 31:16, Luqmān tells his son that even something as small as a mustard seed, within a rock, in the heavens, or in the earth, will be brought forth by God.

In the first verse, smallness does not prevent just consideration. In the second, smallness and concealment do not obstruct divine knowledge and power.

Both use the mustard seed as a comparison. Accounting, bringing forth, divine knowledge, and justice emerge from other words and the verses’ contexts, rather than from the plant name’s independent meaning.

1. The Qur’anic form and word family

The Qur’anic morphological index treats KH R D L as a four-letter lexical entry. Unlike common triliteral roots, it is represented by four basic consonants: khāʾ, rāʾ, dāl, and lām.

Only خَرْدَلٍ (khardalin) occurs in the Qur’an: once in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:47 and once in Luqmān 31:16.

Both place it in حَبَّةٍ مِنْ خَرْدَلٍ (ḥabbah min khardal), “a seed of mustard.”

Important Arabic forms in this family include:

  • خَرْدَل (khardal): the mustard plant and its seeds as a kind.
  • خَرْدَلَة (khardalah): one mustard seed or one unit of the plant.
  • خَرَادِل (kharādil): a recorded plural for pieces of meat; it must not be treated as the plant name’s plural without evidence.
  • خَرَادِيل (kharādīl): pieces of meat, the form occurring in this article’s poetic witness.
  • خَرْدَلَ ـ يُخَرْدِلُ (khardala–yukhardilu): in verbal uses, to cut into pieces, separate, or divide something into parts.
  • خَرْدَلَة (khardalah): the verb’s verbal noun; in another use, eating the choice portions of food.
  • مُخَرْدَل (mukhardal): something cut into pieces; in some uses, someone or something fallen or thrown down.
  • خُرْدُولَة (khurdūlah): in one lexical use, a relatively large piece of meat.

These forms are recorded in Arabic lexicons but must not be equated with the Qur’anic forms.

Neither the verb khardala nor the description mukhardal occurs in the Qur’an. Both Qur’anic instances concern the mustard plant’s name.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: two nominal occurrences in the quadriliteral entry

Does khardal have an abstract fundamental meaning?

For some Arabic roots, related forms can reveal a meaning such as splitting, gathering, cutting, or returning.

Qur’anic khardal, however, is first and foremost a plant name.

The seed’s smallness therefore does not establish that the four consonants KH R D L originally meant “making small,” “mincing,” or “dividing into particles.”

Lexicons record خَرْدَلَ اللَّحْمَ (khardala al-laḥma) for cutting meat into pieces. Its result may involve producing small pieces.

Similarity between the mustard seed’s smallness and chopping meat does not securely establish whether the plant’s name arose from the verb or the verb arose from the plant’s name.

Establishing that relationship requires independent historical evidence.

This article therefore retains the plant’s name as its established Qur’anic meaning, considering other verbal and specialized uses separately.

2. Mustard in early Arabic life and language

A plant with small seeds and a pungent taste

Arabic lexicons describe mustard as follows:

الْخَرْدَلُ ضَرْبٌ مِنَ الْحُرْفِ مَعْرُوفٌ

al-khardalu ḍarbun mina al-ḥurfi maʿrūfun

That is, mustard is a familiar kind of plant within the traditional group called ḥurf in early Arabic. This traditional designation must not be equated with modern botanical family classification.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Kitāb al-ʿAyn: the plant’s name and the reading ḥurf

In modern botany, mustard generally refers to a group of Brassicaceae plants whose seeds are used in food and condiments.

Their seeds are small, and some types have a pungent taste. Crushing or grinding mustard seeds and mixing them with other ingredients can produce a pungent condiment.

The old name need not have been confined to one particular species in modern botanical classification.

The Qur’anic mention of mustard alone therefore does not identify black mustard, white mustard, or another precise type.

Nor is such specialized identification necessary for understanding the two verses: the feature employed in both is the seed’s smallness.

Canadian Grain Commission: mustard seed types and botanical family

حَبَّةُ خَرْدَلٍ (ḥabbat khardal): a real seed

Arabic ḥabbah denotes one seed or grain.

حَبَّةٌ مِنْ خَرْدَلٍ (ḥabbah min khardal) means a seed of the mustard plant.

This construction differs from khardal alone.

Khardal can name the plant or its seeds as a kind, while ḥabbah identifies one unit.

Someone may use a substantial quantity of mustard in food, for example, or hold one seed between their fingers.

The seed’s smallness is more readily apparent in the second example.

This makes it suitable for comparison with a very slight quantity.

A mustard seed is nevertheless neither an official, fixed unit of weight nor naturally identical in weight to every other mustard seed.

The Qur’anic verses give no numerical weight for it.

Mithqāl ḥabbah min khardal must therefore not be converted into a fixed scientific or legal unit with a specified value.

The point is comparison with the weight of a very small, familiar seed.

An early witness: the mustard seed and justice in Al-Najāshī al-Ḥārithī’s poetry

A poem attributed to Al-Najāshī al-Ḥārithī, an early Islamic poet, states:

قَبِيلَةٌ لَا يَغْدِرُونَ بِذِمَّةٍ
وَلَا يَظْلِمُونَ النَّاسَ حَبَّةَ خَرْدَلِ

qabīlatun lā yaghdurūna bi-dhimmatin / wa-lā yaẓlimūna al-nāsa ḥabbata khardali

Translation:

“A tribe that does not betray a pledge, nor wrong people by so much as a mustard seed.”

Here, حَبَّةَ خَرْدَلِ (ḥabbat khardal) expresses a very small amount.

The poet speaks of injustice to the extent of a mustard seed: even a negligible amount of another person’s right.

The verse, however, originally belongs to a satire against Banū al-ʿAjlān. Within the tribal values invoked, inability to betray or encroach upon others is presented sarcastically.

The apparently positive sentences therefore do not establish that the poet intended to praise the tribe’s justice and loyalty.

Early literary reports associate the poem with an encounter involving ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb. In that account, ʿUmar reads its outward wording as praise, while the complainants explain that it was intended to humiliate their tribe.

For this lexical study, the verse matters in one respect: Arabic of that period used ḥabbat khardal to indicate a very slight amount.

This witness belongs historically to early Islam, rather than the pre-Islamic period. Some wording and attributions within the account vary, but wa-lā yaẓlimūna al-nāsa ḥabbata khardal is recorded in several early literary sources.

The relationship with Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:47 is clear. Both use the seed’s smallness to show how a tiny amount can matter in justice and rights.

Poem and verse nevertheless have different meanings and purposes. The poem is tribal satire; the Qur’anic verse concerns just divine accounting.

Al-Dīnawarī’s Al-Mujālasah wa-Jawāhir al-ʿIlm, report 988: Al-Najāshī’s satire

خَرْدَلَ اللَّحْمَ (khardala al-laḥma): cutting meat into pieces

Kitāb al-ʿAyn, Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, and Lisān al-ʿArab record the verb خَرْدَلَ اللَّحْمَ (khardala al-laḥma).

It means cutting meat into pieces.

Explanations differ about their size. Some lexicographers describe separating large portions of meat; others also record cutting it into small pieces.

This variation shows that the verb did not invariably mean mincing something down to mustard-seed size.

A verse attributed to Kaʿb ibn Zuhayr states:

يَغْدُو فَيَلْحَمُ ضِرْغَامَيْنِ عَيْشُهُمَا
لَحْمٌ مِنَ الْقَوْمِ مَعْفُورٌ خَرَادِيلُ

yaghdū fa-yulḥimu ḍirghāmayni ʿayshuhumā / laḥmun mina al-qawmi maʿfūrun kharādīlu

An approximate rendering of the sense:

“At daybreak he feeds two lions; their sustenance is dusty, dismembered flesh from those people.”

Here, خَرَادِيل (kharādīl) concerns separated pieces of meat.

This differs from the mustard seed in the two Qur’anic verses. The poem concerns meat cut apart; the Qur’an uses a real plant’s name to compare a very slight weight.

Kaʿb ibn Zuhayr lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. Versions of this line also differ. Without dating its composition and examining the variants, it cannot be called a securely pre-Qur’anic witness.

It helps identify another use within the word family but does not establish the historical origin of the plant name.

Lisān al-ʿArab, Al-ʿAyn, and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: cutting meat, piece sizes, and Kaʿb’s witness

Other lexical uses of khardal

Early sources record خَرْدَلَ الطَّعَامَ (khardala al-ṭaʿāma) for eating a meal’s choice, pleasant portions.

خَرْدَلَتِ النَّخْلَةُ (khardalat al-nakhlah) also describes a date palm shedding many unripe fruits while those remaining grow large.

These uses show that Arabic KH R D L extends beyond the plant’s name.

Their precise historical relationship is unclear, however. Eating the better portions of food need not derive from a mustard seed’s smallness, just as shedding dates cannot be made the mustard name’s original meaning without evidence.

Qur’anic usage should therefore center upon the plant’s name and small seed, recognizing the other recorded uses as separate branches.

Al-Qāmūs al-Muḥīṭ and lexical reports: shedding fruit and growth of what remains

3. Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:47: the mustard seed in just assessment

وَنَضَعُ الْمَوَازِينَ الْقِسْطَ لِيَوْمِ الْقِيَامَةِ فَلَا تُظْلَمُ نَفْسٌ شَيْئًا وَإِنْ كَانَ مِثْقَالَ حَبَّةٍ مِنْ خَرْدَلٍ أَتَيْنَا بِهَا وَكَفَىٰ بِنَا حَاسِبِينَ

wa-naḍaʿu al-mawāzīna al-qisṭa li-yawmi al-qiyāmati fa-lā tuẓlamu nafsun shayʾan wa-in kāna mithqāla ḥabbatin min khardalin ataynā bihā wa-kafā binā ḥāsibīna

Proposed translation in this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“We shall set up the just scales for the Day of Resurrection, so no person will be wronged in anything. Even if it is the weight of a seed of mustard, We shall bring it forth; We suffice as reckoners.”

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:47

Khardal appears alongside several other words here: mawāzīn, qisṭ, mithqāl, ḥabbah, and ḥāsibīn.

Each word’s role must be distinguished to understand the lexical entry precisely.

Mawāzīn concerns scales and assessment.

Qisṭ expresses justice.

Mithqāl expresses something’s weight or heaviness.

Ḥabbah identifies one seed.

Khardal identifies the kind of seed.

Ḥāsibīn concerns reckoning.

Justice and reckoning are therefore not meanings contained in khardal itself. The word supplies a small, familiar object for comparison.

مِثْقَالَ حَبَّةٍ مِنْ خَرْدَلٍ (mithqāl ḥabbah min khardal): what is being weighed?

Mithqāl belongs to TH Q L and concerns weight or an amount of heaviness.

Ḥabbah min khardal supplies the comparison for that weight.

The expression therefore means something whose weight or amount equals a mustard seed.

This matters because the verse does not say that God brings or weighs only actual mustard seeds at the Resurrection.

Its context concerns accounting and preventing injustice.

The seed therefore expresses a very small, negligible amount in ordinary experience, rather than identifying the smallest possible quantity in the universe.

Mithqāl ḥabbah must nevertheless not be treated as a particular scientific weight or fixed number in modern measurement.

The seed naturally has weight; its slight weight makes the comparison intelligible.

Are only bad deeds intended?

The verse states:

فَلَا تُظْلَمُ نَفْسٌ شَيْئًا (fa-lā tuẓlamu nafsun shayʾā).

That is, no person will be wronged in anything.

It then mentions bringing forth something the size of a mustard seed.

The construction does not restrict the subject to one particular kind of deed.

Human rights and good deeds, as well as wrongdoing and evil, fall within justice and accounting.

This also fits Al-Zalzalah 99:7 and Al-Zalzalah 99:8, which separately describe seeing good and evil of a particle’s weight.

The seed’s smallness therefore does not mean the verse concerns only minor sins.

Just as a very small evil must not be overlooked, a slight right or good deed must not be lost.

This conclusion comes from the context of justice and reckoning, rather than the plant’s name itself meaning a good or bad deed.

أَتَيْنَا بِهَا (ataynā bihā): bringing forth what is slight

After mentioning the mustard seed’s weight, the verse states:

أَتَيْنَا بِهَا (ataynā bihā).

That is, We bring it forth.

Atā belongs to ʾ T Y and concerns coming; with its construction here, it expresses bringing.

Bihā refers to what has been described through comparison with the mustard seed’s size.

Contextually, bringing it forth belongs within accounting and justice.

Khardal itself, however, does not mean becoming apparent, presenting something, or reckoning.

These acts and results come from other parts of the verse.

Nor does ataynā bihā alone establish a definite theory about the physical presentation of deeds, the form of the Resurrection’s scales, or the substance of what is weighed.

The Qur’an presents just assessment but does not explain every detail of how it occurs in this verse.

Al-Ṭabarī: accounting for good and evil, bihā, and bringing forth

Smallness does not justify disregarding a right

A clear implication is that something’s smallness does not make it unimportant in just accounting.

Someone might take a little money from another person and assume that its negligible amount means no right was violated.

Someone might also do a small good deed unknown to anyone, seemingly insignificant in people’s eyes.

These examples differ ethically, but both clarify the importance of precise assessment.

The verse’s central point is that no one is wronged, even regarding something as small as a mustard seed.

This principle should not become obsessive calculation of every everyday action or reduce human worth to mechanically counting small deeds. The verse concerns divine justice and accounting, rather than humans calculating their own or others’ final worth through a numerical formula.

4. Luqmān 31:16: something small, never beyond divine knowledge even when deeply concealed

يَا بُنَيَّ إِنَّهَا إِنْ تَكُ مِثْقَالَ حَبَّةٍ مِنْ خَرْدَلٍ فَتَكُنْ فِي صَخْرَةٍ أَوْ فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ أَوْ فِي الْأَرْضِ يَأْتِ بِهَا اللَّهُ إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَطِيفٌ خَبِيرٌ

yā bunayya innahā in taku mithqāla ḥabbatin min khardalin fa-takun fī ṣakhratin aw fī al-samāwāti aw fī al-arḍi yaʾti bihā allāhu inna allāha laṭīfun khabīrun

Proposed translation in this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“My son, if it is the weight of a seed of mustard and is within a rock, or in the heavens, or in the earth, The God will bring it forth; surely The God is subtle and fully aware.”

Luqmān 31:16

The verse occurs among Luqmān’s counsels to his son.

In Luqmān 31:13, he warns his son against associating partners with God, describing it as a great injustice.

Luqmān 31:15 concerns relations with parents, not following an invitation to associate partners with God, and the final return to Him.

Luqmān 31:16 then mentions something the size of a mustard seed which God brings forth even from a distant or concealed location.

The seed’s smallness matters here too, but unlike Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:47, a hidden or inaccessible location is added to the image.

The role of مِثْقَالَ حَبَّةٍ مِنْ خَرْدَلٍ (mithqāl ḥabbah min khardal)

This expression is identical in both verses.

Mithqāl denotes weight, while ḥabbah min khardal makes that very slight weight imaginable.

Luqmān 31:16 associates the small amount with three locations: within a rock, in the heavens, and in the earth.

The verse therefore concerns more than slight weight; it also presents the location’s distance or concealment.

Someone may see and touch a seed in their hand, but finding it becomes difficult if it lies in a deep crevice among rocks or somewhere beyond sight.

The image combines two limitations of human knowledge: the object’s smallness and its location’s remoteness or concealment.

إِنَّهَا (innahā): what does the pronoun refer to?

At the beginning of the speech, after يَا بُنَيَّ (yā bunayya), comes إِنَّهَا (innahā). Hā is a feminine pronoun whose referent is not explicitly named at that point.

Early explanations present several possibilities.

Some commentators relate it to human deeds, whether good or bad.

Others connect it with a quality or the subject Luqmān is discussing.

Another grammatical analysis treats hā in innahā as a pronoun of circumstance or story: it introduces a subject explained by the following clause.

The verse’s reminder of divine knowledge and power is clear, but the pronoun’s exact referent requires care.

A direct translation should therefore preferably retain “it,” without silently substituting “a good or bad deed” without an explanatory marker.

Commentary can identify human deeds as a strong reading in light of Luqmān’s counsels and other Qur’anic verses.

This distinguishes literal translation from exegetical interpretation.

Al-Ṭabarī: hā, the pronoun of circumstance, and good and bad deeds

Is the subject a real seed or a human deed?

The mustard seed is a real, familiar object employed in comparison.

The thing compared may nevertheless be more than a material seed.

If human deeds are intended, the verse indicates that no small, concealed act escapes divine knowledge and consideration.

Read more broadly, it emphasizes divine knowledge and power over something extremely small wherever it may be.

These two aspects need not conflict.

God’s knowledge and power concerning small, hidden matters also support understanding precise accounting of human deeds.

Their relationship does not make khardal itself mean a deed, sin, goodness, or secret.

Lexically it remains the plant’s name; the construction establishes its illustrative function.

فِي صَخْرَةٍ (fī ṣakhrah): a seed within a rock

The verse states:

فَتَكُنْ فِي صَخْرَةٍ (fa-takun fī ṣakhrah).

That is, the thing may be within a rock.

Ṣakhrah belongs to another root and denotes a hard, substantial rock.

Fī indicates location within or in the bounds of the rock.

In ordinary experience, hard, enclosed rock can make something inside difficult to see or extract.

This image sets divine power and knowledge against limited human knowledge and ability.

The verse itself, however, names no particular rock, geographical location, or special cosmic feature.

Some exegetical reports identify it with a rock beneath the earths or a rock on the back of an enormous creature.

Those details do not come from the verse’s own wording.

Nor should fī ṣakhrah become a theory about the earth’s or heavens’ structure, or a foundational cosmic rock.

The rock occurs alongside the heavens and earth to extend the range of places in which even something small does not escape divine knowledge.

أَوْ فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ أَوْ فِي الْأَرْضِ (aw fī al-samāwāt aw fī al-arḍ)

After the rock come the heavens and earth.

This sequence broadens the range of locations.

The thing might be in a confined, concealed place, or within the expanse of the heavens and earth.

Khardal here does not mean the sky, a star, a planet, or a fundamental particle of matter.

The seed supplies a comparison for smallness, while the heavens and earth identify possible locations of the thing concerned.

The verse therefore cannot establish a scientific theory of atomic size or the structure of matter.

Nor does it claim that a real mustard seed necessarily occupies each of these locations.

The conditional construction expresses divine power and knowledge even in such circumstances.

يَأْتِ بِهَا اللَّهُ (yaʾti bihā allāhu): bringing the thing forth

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:47 uses أَتَيْنَا بِهَا (ataynā bihā).

Luqmān 31:16 uses يَأْتِ بِهَا اللَّهُ (yaʾti bihā allāhu).

Both concern bringing something forth.

Al-Anbiyāʾ explicitly concerns the Day of Resurrection, just scales, and reckoning.

Luqmān’s sentence ends with the descriptions لَطِيفٌ خَبِيرٌ (laṭīf khabīr).

The difference clarifies the verses’ respective emphases.

Al-Anbiyāʾ emphasizes just accounting.

Within a father’s counsel to his son, Luqmān emphasizes divine knowledge and power over something however small and hidden.

Together, the verses indicate that no small deed should be dismissed as irrelevant to accounting. Nevertheless, all the phrases of one verse must not be silently inserted into the other’s translation.

اللَّطِيفُ الْخَبِيرُ (al-laṭīf al-khabīr): divine knowledge and concealed matters

Luqmān’s verse ends:

إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَطِيفٌ خَبِيرٌ (inna allāha laṭīfun khabīr).

Laṭīf belongs to L Ṭ F, and khabīr to KH B R.

Here the descriptions relate to divine knowledge and power concerning small, concealed matters.

Khardal itself does not mean subtlety, discernment, or knowledge, however.

The small seed provides an example placed alongside the descriptions.

Their conjunction reinforces the verse’s meaning: what is very small, remote, or hidden to humans does not escape God’s awareness.

Laṭīf must nevertheless not be confined to seeing tiny particles, nor khabīr to knowing a mustard seed’s location.

Both descriptions have broader semantic fields and occur with different subjects elsewhere.

Al-Ṭabarī: rock reports, bringing forth, and laṭīf and khabīr

5. Distinctions from related words and roots

KH R D L (khardal) and Ḥ B B (ḥabb): a particular plant and a seed

Arabic ḥabb concerns seeds and, in some uses, grain and plant seeds collectively.

Khardal instead names a particular recognized plant in Arabic.

حَبَّةٍ مِنْ خَرْدَلٍ (ḥabbah min khardal) makes the distinction clear.

Ḥabbah identifies one seed; khardal specifies its plant.

Khardal alone therefore does not mean every kind of seed, nor is ḥabbah restricted to mustard seeds.

Persian dāneh-ī az khardal, “a seed of mustard,” follows the Arabic construction more closely; “mustard seed” is shorter and natural.

Study of Ḥ B B

KH R D L (khardal) and DH R R (dharr): mustard seed and particle

Al-Zalzalah 99:7 and Al-Zalzalah 99:8 use مِثْقَالَ ذَرَّةٍ (mithqāl dharrah).

It overlaps with mithqāl ḥabbah min khardal in expressing a very small amount.

Dharrah and khardal nevertheless remain different words.

Khardal names the plant and its seed; early Arabic dharrah has uses concerning extremely small, negligible things.

Modern scientific usage may give “particle” a different technical meaning. That later meaning must not be imposed indiscriminately on Qur’anic usage.

Both expressions concern a small amount, but this does not make the mustard seed and dharrah equal units of weight.

Nor does smallness alone mean worthlessness.

In accounting passages, the slight amount appears alongside the importance of just consideration.

Study of DH R R

KH R D L (khardal) and TH Q L (thiql): the small object and its weight

Mithqāl belongs to TH Q L and concerns weight or an amount of heaviness.

Khardal names something whose weight supplies the comparison.

The seed therefore supplies the comparison in mithqāl ḥabbah min khardal, while mithqāl expresses weight.

Khardal must accordingly not be defined as weight or heaviness.

This distinction holds in both Qur’anic uses.

Study of TH Q L

KH R D L (khardal) and W Z N (wazn): mustard seed and assessment

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:47, mawāzīn belongs to W Z N.

Weight, justice, and reckoning are related within the verse’s construction.

Mustard itself, however, is neither a measuring instrument nor the name of a scale.

Here the seed simply makes a very small amount imaginable.

Saying that divine justice overlooks nothing even the size of a mustard seed is therefore a contextual conclusion, rather than KH R D L’s lexical definition.

Study of W Z N

KH R D L (khardal) and KH R D (khurd): smallness and resemblance between words

Persian khurd is familiar in the sense of small or tiny.

Arabic also records KH R D forms concerning virginity, bashfulness, and related meanings.

Outward similarity between khurd and khardal alone does not establish that one historically derives from the other.

Arabic khardal is an established plant name and a four-consonant lexical entry.

The Persian meaning of khurd alone therefore does not justify treating lām as an addition that turns an adjective meaning small into the plant’s name.

Such analysis requires independent historical and linguistic evidence.

Nor does the meat-cutting verb establish that the plant’s name means “chopped up.”

This article keeps the plant’s name distinct from meanings involving cutting and making small.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

For خَرْدَلٍ (khardalin), the principal equivalent in both verses is “mustard.”

The Persian word is familiar, so neither a new equivalent nor a lengthy explanation inside the translation is needed.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:47, مِثْقَالَ حَبَّةٍ مِنْ خَرْدَلٍ (mithqāl ḥabbah min khardal) may be translated:

“The weight of a seed of mustard.”

The same equivalent fits Luqmān 31:16.

The shorter “the size of a mustard seed” is natural, but a translation closely following the Arabic should retain mithqāl’s weight component.

“A tiny particle” is not precise in either verse: it removes the kind of seed and may conflate it with the independent word dharrah elsewhere.

“The smallest thing in the universe” is also unsuitable. The Qur’an names a real plant without claiming its seed is the smallest existing object.

Commentary can explain that the seed expresses a very small amount, but that explanation should not replace the original word in translation.

Brief root meaning and the meaning in each use

خَرْدَلٍ (khardalin): Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:47

Root or lexical entry: KH R D L.

Grammar: a noun singular in form, indefinite and genitive after مِنْ (min) in حَبَّةٍ مِنْ خَرْدَلٍ (ḥabbah min khardal).

Brief root meaning: mustard, a familiar plant with small seeds.

Meaning in this verse: mustard, whose seed’s slight weight supplies a comparison for a negligible amount in just accounting.

خَرْدَلٍ (khardalin): Luqmān 31:16

Root or lexical entry: KH R D L.

Grammar: a noun singular in form, indefinite and genitive after مِنْ (min) in حَبَّةٍ مِنْ خَرْدَلٍ (ḥabbah min khardal).

Brief root meaning: mustard, a familiar plant with small seeds.

Meaning in this verse: mustard, whose seed’s slight weight describes something small which God brings forth even from within a rock or from the heavens and earth.

In both uses, the basic meaning remains the plant’s name.

The surrounding sentences supply their different emphases: Resurrection accounting in one, Luqmān’s counsel and a reminder of divine knowledge and power in the other.

7. Conclusion

Arabic khardal names a familiar plant with small seeds. Early usage employed those seeds to describe a very slight amount.

Al-Najāshī al-Ḥārithī’s poetic witness shows ḥabbat khardal being used in speech concerning justice and others’ rights.

Lexicons record other KH R D L forms, including a verb for cutting meat into pieces. Their historical relationship to the plant’s name is uncertain, however; “mincing” cannot be declared its original meaning without evidence.

The Qur’an uses the word twice, solely in ḥabbah min khardal.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:47 places the seed’s slight weight within justice and accounting. No right or deed should be excluded from just consideration merely for being small.

Luqmān 31:16 adds a concealed or distant location to the smallness. Something that small remains within divine knowledge and power even inside a rock or in the heavens and earth.

In both verses, khardal remains the plant’s name; justice, reckoning, knowledge, and bringing forth emerge from construction and context.

Final definition: Qur’anic KH R D L names the mustard plant, whose small seeds supply a comparison for a very slight amount. Khardalin in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:47 and Luqmān 31:16 refers to that plant. The seed’s slight weight illustrates precise, just accounting in the first verse and divine knowledge and power concerning small, hidden matters in the second. Other recorded uses, such as cutting meat into pieces, do not establish a definite derivational relationship with the plant’s name. Brief root meaning: Mustard, the plant and its small seeds, used in the Qur’an for comparison with a very slight amount.
