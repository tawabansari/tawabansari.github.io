Hujūʿ: nighttime sleep, resting, and light sleep

1. Meaning in early Arabic

In Arabic, hā–jīm–ʿayn concerns sleeping and resting, with nighttime sleep prominent in its principal usage. هَجَعَ يَهْجَعُ هُجُوعًا means to sleep, particularly at night. Hujūʿ names that sleep, and hājiʿ describes someone asleep.

Early Arabic does not restrict the family to deep, prolonged sleep. Some witnesses also use it for light sleep, a brief doze, or even resting without falling fully asleep.

This breadth should not be confused with staying awake. Someone may lie down to rest without falling asleep, or sleep so lightly that they do not count it as real sleep. These conditions remain connected with sleeping, although its degree and quality differ.

Lexicons sometimes define hujūʿ as sleep generally and sometimes specifically as nighttime sleep. Early witnesses likewise show night and nighttime rest as familiar settings of the root’s use.

A poetic witness from Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā includes:

قَفْرٍ هَجَعْتُ بِهَا وَلَسْتُ بِنَائِمٍ

qafrin hajaʿtu bihā wa-lastu bi-nāʾimin

وَذِرَاعُ مُلْقِيَةِ الْجِرَانِ وِسَادِي

wa-dhirāʿu mulqiyati al-jirāni wisādī

“In a barren wilderness I rested, yet was not asleep; the foreleg of a she-camel with her neck laid down was my pillow.”

The poet describes a halt in a difficult wilderness. The camel has lain down, and he too reclines to rest, but real sleep does not come to him.

Combining hajaʿtu with “I was not asleep” is significant. If hujūʿ always meant falling fully asleep, the expressions would appear contradictory.

He may mean that he lay down to sleep without actually sleeping, or that his sleep was so light and unsettled that he did not count it as sleep.

This witness thus illustrates usage extending from full sleep to resting in a state close to sleep.

The verse is transmitted in Zuhayr’s collected poetry and early Arabic linguistic works. Zuhayr was a pre-Islamic poet, although the precise date of this particular verse is not independently established. With that limitation, it remains a valuable witness to early usage.

A poetic witness from Abū Qays ibn al-Aslat

A poem attributed to Abū Qays ibn al-Aslat, a poet of the late pre-Islamic period, reads:

قَدْ حَصَّتِ الْبَيْضَةُ رَأْسِي فَمَا

qad ḥaṣṣati al-bayḍatu raʾsī fa-mā

أَطْعَمُ نَوْمًا غَيْرَ تَهْجَاعِ

aṭʿamu nawman ghayra tahjāʿi

“The helmet has worn the hair from my head, and I taste no sleep beyond a brief doze.”

Bayḍah here means a battle helmet. The poet depicts a difficult life through the wear of warfare and scant sleep.

Tahjāʿ denotes brief, light sleep in the verse. He does not say that he never sleeps, but that his share of sleep is limited to a short doze.

This use shows that the family can also describe the quantity and quality of sleep.

Since the poem’s exact date is unclear, it cannot provide a precisely dated pre-Islamic attestation. Alongside Zuhayr’s witness and the lexical accounts, however, it helps clarify this family’s meaning.

Conclusion from the early witnesses: hā–jīm–ʿayn principally concerns sleep and resting, especially at night, and can describe light sleep or rest that does not become full sleep.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important recorded forms include:

  • هَجَعَ: slept or settled down to sleep.
  • يَهْجَعُ: sleeps.
  • هُجُوع: sleeping, especially at night.
  • هَجْعَة: a spell of sleep; sometimes a short sleep early in the night.
  • هَاجِع: asleep or resting.
  • هُجَّع and هُجُوع: plurals denoting sleepers.
  • هَوَاجِع: a feminine plural form for sleepers.
  • تَهْجَاع: brief, light sleep.
  • هَجَّعَ: put someone to sleep or induced sleep.
  • هَجِيع: in some recorded uses, a portion or hour of the night.

Not all these forms occur in the Quran. Its only derivative from this root is يَهْجَعُونَ.

The semantic core

The recognized core is sleeping or being in a state of sleep or rest, prominently used of nighttime sleep.

Several features can be distinguished within this field.

First is sleeping itself: a state in which a person temporarily leaves ordinary wakefulness.

Second is the familiar connection with night. Although sleep occurs at other times, principal Arabic uses of hujūʿ closely associate it with nighttime rest.

Third is variation in quantity and depth. Forms such as hajʿah and tahjāʿ can denote a spell of sleep or a brief sleep, whereas hujūʿ concerns sleeping itself and does not independently fix its duration.

Some lexicons also record هَجَعَ غَرَثُهُ for the subsiding of hunger’s intensity. Related descriptions can refer to an inattentive, easily deceived person. These uses do not occur in the Quran and should not expand the Quranic definition merely through a possible connection with settling or sleep.

Quranic hujūʿ should therefore first be examined through the clear meaning of sleeping.

3. Quranic usage

The root occurs once in the Quran, as يَهْجَعُونَ.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:17

كَانُوا قَلِيلًا مِنَ اللَّيْلِ مَا يَهْجَعُونَ

kānū qalīlan mina al-layli mā yahjaʿūn

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:17

“They used to sleep little of the night.”

The verse describes the God-conscious. Al-Dhāriyāt 51:15 speaks of their place among gardens and springs, and Al-Dhāriyāt 51:16 recalls their good deeds in their former life.

The Quran then describes one of their characteristics: they spent little of the night sleeping.

The structure of yahjaʿūn

يَهْجَعُونَ is an imperfect verb in the basic form of hā–jīm–ʿayn.

Its wāw marks a third-person masculine plural subject, while its final nūn marks the indicative in this verbal construction.

The form thus means “they sleep.”

With kānū, it describes their past condition and behavior. “They used to sleep” conveys that construction naturally.

Yahjaʿūn itself means neither sleeping little nor worship. The small amount comes from qalīlan in relation to the night. The connection between their wakefulness and worship likewise comes from the surrounding verses.

The roles of qalīlan and mā

Several analyses have been proposed for mā and the syntactic position of qalīlan.

One takes mā as emphatic, yielding the meaning that they slept for a small portion of the night.

Another takes mā as introducing a verbal noun, yielding the sense that their nighttime sleeping was little.

Despite their syntactic differences, these analyses reach similar meanings: sleep occupied little of their night.

Another reading treats mā as negative and understands them as not sleeping at night. Grammarians including al-Nasafī object to the particular analysis that makes qalīlan depend on a verb following negative mā: on their account, what follows that negative particle cannot govern an element placed before it.

Contextually, scant sleep clearly conveys the intended characteristic without requiring an absolute denial of sleep.

“They used to sleep little of the night” is therefore lexically appropriate and has strong grammatical support.

Connection with the following verses

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:18 describes the same group seeking forgiveness before dawn.

This connection shows that their scant sleep was not merely a bodily trait or lifestyle habit, but was associated with attention to The God and worship.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:19 then mentions the right of the petitioner and the deprived in their wealth.

Together, these features present a broader picture of doing good: attention and seeking forgiveness in their relationship with The God, and observance of the needy’s rights in their use of wealth.

Little sleep is therefore not, by itself, the measure of goodness in this passage. Its value emerges within the lives and deeds of those the Quran calls muḥsinīn, doers of good.

Someone might sleep little because of work, travel, illness, or worry. Scant sleep alone does not make that person an instance of the praised description.

4. Comparison with related roots

Hā–jīm–ʿayn and hā–jīm–dāl: hujūʿ and hujūd

These roots differ only in their last letter, and both have Arabic uses concerning nighttime sleep.

Hujūʿ from hā–jīm–ʿayn principally refers to sleeping.

Hujūd from hā–jīm–dāl likewise concerns sleeping, but some formations in that family also express nighttime wakefulness and leaving sleep behind.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:79, fa-tahajjad commands nighttime wakefulness in a context of worship.

In Al-Dhāriyāt 51:17, however, yahjaʿūn refers to sleeping itself.

Simply put, Al-Dhāriyāt describes the amount of sleep of the God-conscious, while Al-Isrāʾ commands wakefulness at night for worship.

This contrast between two Quranic uses must not become an absolute rule equating every Arabic use of hā–jīm–ʿayn with sleep and every derivative of hā–jīm–dāl with wakefulness.

Study of hā–jīm–dāl

Hā–jīm–ʿayn and nūn–wāw–mīm: hujūʿ and nawm

Nawm is a broader word for sleep, including sleep by day or night.

For example, Al-Rūm 30:23 presents human sleep by night and day as a divine sign.

Hujūʿ, however, is more prominently associated with nighttime sleep in its principal Arabic usage.

Both can be translated “sleep,” but lexical study should preserve nawm’s broader range and hujūʿ’s familiar connection with night.

Neither nawm nor hujūʿ independently labels sleep good or bad. The ethical value of behavior belongs to its circumstances and context; sleep is not inherently objectionable.

For a broader discussion of sleep in the Quran, see Forqan’s study of nūn–wāw–mīm.

Study of nūn–wāw–mīm

Hā–jīm–ʿayn and ḍād–jīm–ʿayn: sleeping and lying down

Ḍād–jīm–ʿayn concerns reclining and lying on one’s side. Lying down need not mean sleeping.

Al-Sajdah 32:16 describes people whose sides leave their beds as they call upon their Lord in fear and hope.

Maḍājiʿ there refers to beds or resting places, whereas yahjaʿūn in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:17 names sleeping itself.

Someone can lie on a bed while still awake or get up to undertake an activity. The name of a bed and the verb for sleeping are therefore not complete synonyms.

Despite the difference, the passages connect in describing people who relinquish the comfort of sleep to attend to their Lord.

Hā–jīm–ʿayn and rāʾ–qāf–dāl: hujūʿ and ruqūd

Ruqūd likewise concerns sleeping or being asleep.

Al-Kahf 18:18 describes the Companions of the Cave as appearing awake to an observer while they are asleep.

That usage emphasizes their sleeping state.

In Al-Dhāriyāt 51:17, yahjaʿūn instead occurs in a construction specifying the amount of nighttime sleep.

The contextual contrast is clear, but it does not establish that ruqūd always means prolonged sleep and hujūʿ always means brief sleep. The brevity of the God-conscious people’s sleep comes from qalīlan, not from the root independently.

5. Challenges in understanding and translation

Is hujūʿ always brief sleep?

No. Hujūʿ fundamentally concerns sleeping and does not itself specify duration.

Forms such as hajʿah and tahjāʿ can denote a spell of sleep or a brief, light sleep, but this feature must not be extended to every derivative.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:17 explicitly expresses the small amount through qalīlan.

If yahjaʿūn always meant sleeping little, the contribution of qalīlan to the sentence would be overlooked.

Can hujūʿ occur only at night?

Lexical accounts prominently associate the family with nighttime sleep, and some definitions restrict it to night.

Uses concerning rest and sleep without an explicit night qualification nevertheless caution against treating that restriction as an unquestioned rule for every branch.

In Al-Dhāriyāt, mina al-layl itself specifies the time.

There is therefore no need to expand yahjaʿūn into a lengthy phrase such as “they would fall into nighttime sleep at night.”

“They used to sleep” is natural and precise; “of the night” supplies the time qualification.

Does the verse require everyone to sleep little?

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:17 describes a group of doers of good; it does not issue a general command to deprive people of sleep.

The distinction between description and command matters.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:9 presents sleep as rest and cessation from activity, and Al-Furqān 25:47 likewise places night and sleep within divine provision and ordering.

Praising people who forgo some nighttime comfort for worship does not make sleep itself objectionable or sleeplessness necessary for everyone.

When discussing nighttime worship, Al-Muzzammil 73:20 also recognizes human limitations and differing circumstances.

A praised description should therefore not be turned into a binding rule about how much all people must sleep.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Proposed equivalents vary with derivative and context:

هَجَعَ: slept; in some poetic uses, rested.

هُجُوع: sleeping, especially at night.

هَجْعَة: a spell of sleep; in a suitable context, a short sleep.

هَاجِع: asleep or resting.

تَهْجَاع: light sleep or a brief doze.

هَجَّعَ: put someone to sleep.

يَهْجَعُونَ: they sleep.

For the root’s sole Quranic occurrence, the following rendering is suitable:

“They used to sleep little of the night.” — Al-Dhāriyāt 51:17

This preserves the three principal elements: qalīlan expresses the small amount, mina al-layl identifies its time, and yahjaʿūn denotes sleeping.

“They stayed awake a little” reverses the sentence’s direction. “They never slept at all” also does not follow the stronger grammatical reading adopted here.

“They kept night vigils” can explain the contextual implication of their scant sleep, but is not a direct equivalent of yahjaʿūn.

7. Conclusion

Early Arabic hā–jīm–ʿayn concerns sleeping and resting, with nighttime sleep among its most prominent settings.

Early poetry shows that the family can describe light sleep, brief rest, or resting without falling fully asleep. Its principal Quranic meaning nevertheless remains sleeping.

Its sole Quranic derivative is yahjaʿūn in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:17. The small amount of sleep of the God-conscious follows from the sentence as a whole and qalīlan, not from the root itself.

Final definition for Forqan:

Hā–jīm–ʿayn denotes sleeping and resting, prominently in relation to nighttime sleep. Certain forms can also describe brief or light sleep, but the amount of sleep is not a constant meaning of every derivative.

In the Quran, yahjaʿūn means “they sleep,” within a description of doers of good who spent little of the night sleeping.
