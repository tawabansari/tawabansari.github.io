The Arabic root S F Ḥ concerns pouring and causing liquids to flow. Safaḥa al-māʾa means to pour water; safaḥa al-dama means to shed blood; masfūḥ describes something poured out or made to flow.

The same family includes sifāḥ and musāfaḥah, used for sexual intercourse outside the marriage bond. Lexical explanations connect this usage with the discharge of sexual fluid, but its social and legal meaning exceeds the physical act of pouring.

The Qur’an uses the root in two settings: daman masfūḥan, shed blood, and musāfiḥīn and musāfiḥāt, concerning illicit sexual relations in passages about marriage.

The physical sense of pouring is evident in the first branch and appears in traditional explanations of the second. It cannot, however, determine every rule or characteristic of a sexual relationship. In the Qur’anic passages, musāfaḥah describes sexual conduct opposed to iḥṣān and the responsible bond of marriage.

1. The root in early Arabic

Safḥ: pouring and causing liquid to flow

Arabic lexical sources record safaḥa for pouring, causing liquid to flow, and pouring it out.

Safaḥa al-māʾa means to pour water, and safaḥa al-dama means to shed blood or pour it onto the ground.

This usage is close to ṣabba and arāqa, two verbs concerning pouring and causing liquid to flow.

One lexical definition gives the root’s basic meaning as irāqatu shayʾin, “pouring something out.”

The central feature is liquid coming out and flowing, rather than simply existing in a vessel or body.

Water in a vessel has not yet been poured out. Once it leaves the vessel and flows onto the ground or another surface, it may be described as masfūḥ.

A similar distinction applies to blood. Blood within vessels or tissues differs in condition from blood poured out of the body.

In this physical branch, safḥ thus names an action or its resulting condition, not merely a substance.

Early Arabic witnesses

Al-Ṭirimmāḥ ibn Ḥakīm says in a poem:

مُفَجَّعَةٌ لَا دَفْعَ لِلضَّيْمِ عِنْدَهَا
سِوَى سَفَحَانِ الدَّمْعِ مِنْ كُلِّ مَسْفَحِ

mufajjaʿatun lā dafʿa li-l-ḍaymi ʿindahā / siwā safaḥāni al-damʿi min kulli masfaḥi

“A bereaved woman, with no means to resist injustice except tears streaming from every outlet.”

Safaḥān al-damʿ in this couplet refers to tears flowing and pouring forth.

The poet describes a woman unable to counter her affliction, whose tears flow.

The sense is clear: tears emerge from the eyes as flowing liquid.

The witness shows that safḥ is not restricted to blood; it also describes tears.

Al-Ṭirimmāḥ was an Umayyad poet. His verse therefore witnesses classical Arabic after the Qur’an’s revelation, not pre-Islamic Arabic. It confirms independent use of the root for flowing liquid.

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd also contains al-safīḥ:

وَجَامِلٍ خَوَّعَ مِنْ نِيبِهِ
زَجْرُ الْمُعَلَّى أُصُلًا وَالسَّفِيحْ

wa-jāmilin khawwaʿa min nībihi / zajru al-muʿallā uṣulan wa-al-safīḥ

Explanatory rendering: “And many a camel herd whose aged she-camels were reduced by drawing the muʿallā and safīḥ arrows at evening”—an allusion to camels slaughtered in maysir.

Al-safīḥ here names one of the arrows used in maysir, belonging to the arrows assigned no share.

This attests the form safīḥ in transmitted pre-Islamic poetry, but the couplet does not use it for pouring water or blood. It therefore is not direct evidence for that physical meaning of safḥ.

This article presents no independently dated pre-Qur’anic poem or text that conclusively establishes both the pouring sense and the sexual usage in that earlier period.

Both meanings are nevertheless recorded in classical lexicons and occur in the Qur’an itself. Their existence in the Arabic of revelation is clear; the precise pre-Qur’anic history of their emergence and development requires further evidence.

Safḥ al-jabal: a mountain’s lower slope

Safḥ al-jabal denotes the side, slope, or lower part of a mountain.

Lexical accounts sometimes describe the sloping expanse over which water descends and sometimes restrict the term to the mountain’s foot or lower section.

Flowing water on a mountain slope offers a conceivable semantic connection with pouring and flowing.

There are nevertheless differing etymological explanations. One connects safḥ al-jabal with ṣafḥ and proposes an interchange of ṣād and sīn.

One therefore cannot claim without independent evidence that the slope’s name certainly arose from water flowing over it.

The recorded meaning is clear: the lower or sloping part of a mountain. The historical reason for its name is less certain.

From pouring liquid to musāfaḥah

Arabic uses sifāḥ and musāfaḥah for sexual intercourse outside marriage.

Lexical accounts explain this usage through the discharge of sexual fluid in intercourse without a marriage bond.

This explanation can be paraphrased as the discharge of sexual fluid without a marriage contract; it is an explanatory account of the word, not by itself a complete definition of every relevant rule.

In such explanations, māʾ, “water,” stands for sexual fluid.

The semantic connection is understandable, but lexical imagery must be distinguished from the conduct’s actual definition.

Socially, musāfaḥah does not mean every discharge of sexual fluid. Discharge within a marital relationship is not called musāfaḥah simply because of this physical feature.

Nor is the word merely a biological event: it concerns a relationship between people.

In the Qur’anic marriage passages, the relevant contrast is between lawful, chaste relations and illicit intercourse. A complete account of all the text’s legal categories requires examining the related verses.

Pouring fluid may thus explain the word’s imagery, but musāfaḥah in marriage passages cannot be reduced to a bodily process.

2. Qur’anic forms and derivatives

S F Ḥ occurs four times in four Qur’anic verses.

Its Qur’anic forms are:

Masfūḥan: the passive participle of the basic verb safaḥa, meaning poured out or shed, in Al-Anʿām 6:145.

Musāfiḥīn: the masculine plural active participle of form III, describing men engaging in illicit intercourse, in Al-Nisāʾ 4:24 and Al-Māʾidah 5:5.

Musāfiḥātin: the corresponding feminine plural active participle, describing women engaging in such relations, in Al-Nisāʾ 4:25.

Other Arabic forms include safaḥa, yasfaḥu, safḥ, sufūḥ, sāfiḥ, saffāḥ, and musāfaḥah.

These particular forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

Saffāḥ may describe someone who sheds much blood; it is also used for a very generous giver. In Persian, it is better known in the bloodshed sense.

This should not be equated with Qur’anic musāfiḥ.

Saffāḥ as “bloodshedder” concerns shedding blood, whereas musāfiḥ in the marriage passages denotes sexual conduct.

Form III in sāfaḥa can suggest a relationship between two parties, but the form alone establishes neither consent nor coercion, number of partners, or duration. These require independent evidence.

3. Daman masfūḥan: shed blood

Al-Anʿām 6:145 includes:

إِلَّا أَنْ يَكُونَ مَيْتَةً أَوْ دَمًا مَسْفُوحًا أَوْ لَحْمَ خِنْزِيرٍ

illā an yakūna maytatan aw daman masfūḥan aw laḥma khinzīrin

Al-Anʿām 6:145

“Unless it is carrion, or shed blood, or swine flesh.”

Masfūḥan modifies daman in this verse.

Dam names blood; masfūḥ describes its condition as poured out or shed.

Daman masfūḥan therefore says more than “blood.” Masfūḥ contributes an actual qualification.

Blood and shed blood

Blood circulates within animal bodies as part of their biological structure. Once poured out, it is in a different condition.

Masfūḥ describes that condition of being shed.

Lexically, blood remaining in meat’s tissues and blood poured out of a body do not have identical descriptions.

This matters because the Qur’an’s own qualifications must be preserved when examining permitted and prohibited foods.

Al-Baqarah 2:173, Al-Māʾidah 5:3, and Al-Naḥl 16:115 list al-dam among prohibited foods. Al-Anʿām 6:145 uses the more specific daman masfūḥan.

Assessing the ruling on blood therefore requires reading these passages together.

One cannot remove masfūḥ from Al-Anʿām 6:145 and imply that it simply says dam.

Conversely, the qualification alone does not resolve every question about blood remaining in tissues, foods made from blood, or slaughter conditions.

These concern the relevant food passages collectively, not the lexical meaning of a single adjective alone.

Does masfūḥ mean ritually impure blood?

Masfūḥ does not lexically mean ritually impure.

Nor does it mean forbidden, carrion, or spoiled blood.

It describes blood being shed.

The prohibition on consuming blood in Al-Anʿām 6:145 follows from the ruling’s structure and its inclusion among prohibited items, not from a claim that masfūḥ itself means forbidden.

This distinction matters in Qur’anic study. A substance can have a lexical description while a religious ruling about it is established by the statement in which it appears.

Carrion, swine flesh, and shed blood are distinct terms.

Replacing them all with a general word such as “impure” would erase those distinctions from the basic translation.

Forqan’s study “Halal and Haram in the Qur’an” examines these passages together and discusses the bounds of what is permitted and prohibited.

The present study’s lexical conclusion is clear: masfūḥ means poured out or shed, and in Al-Anʿām 6:145 it describes blood.

4. Musāfaḥah: sexual intercourse outside the marriage bond

The other three occurrences belong to marriage passages.

In each, musāfaḥah stands opposite iḥṣān.

This contrast is central: the issue is not merely a physical act but the framework of the sexual relationship.

Muḥṣinīna ghayra musāfiḥīna: Al-Nisāʾ 4:24

Al-Nisāʾ 4:24 includes:

أَنْ تَبْتَغُوا بِأَمْوَالِكُمْ مُحْصِنِينَ غَيْرَ مُسَافِحِينَ

an tabtaghū bi-amwālikum muḥṣinīna ghayra musāfiḥīna

Al-Nisāʾ 4:24

“That you seek with your wealth, maintaining chastity, not engaging in illicit intercourse.”

The phrase appears amid provisions about marriage and women with whom marriage is prohibited or permitted.

Muḥṣinīna belongs to Ḥ Ṣ N and here concerns chastity and lawful relations.

Ghayra musāfiḥīna contrasts with it, excluding illicit sexual relations.

Together, the expressions distinguish sexual relations within a lawful framework from those outside it.

The same verse then addresses enjoyment within the relationship and payment of ujūrahunna.

This connection matters because the marriage relationship entails responsibilities and financial rights.

Ujūr here should not be reduced to wages for purchasing sex. The verse discusses marriage provisions and women’s financial entitlements.

The distinction between iḥṣān and musāfaḥah is therefore not merely whether money is paid.

Someone may pay for intercourse outside marriage, but payment alone does not create marriage.

Marriage, conversely, is not simply a financial exchange: it involves a bond and specified responsibilities.

This follows from the passage and related marriage verses, not from S F Ḥ alone.

Musāfiḥāt and akhdān: Al-Nisāʾ 4:25

Al-Nisāʾ 4:25 includes:

فَانْكِحُوهُنَّ بِإِذْنِ أَهْلِهِنَّ وَآتُوهُنَّ أُجُورَهُنَّ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ مُحْصَنَاتٍ غَيْرَ مُسَافِحَاتٍ وَلَا مُتَّخِذَاتِ أَخْدَانٍ

fa-nkiḥūhunna bi-idhni ahlihinna wa-ātūhunna ujūrahunna bi-al-maʿrūfi muḥṣanātin ghayra musāfiḥātin wa-lā muttakhidhāti akhdānin

Al-Nisāʾ 4:25

“Marry them with the permission of their people and give them their financial dues honorably, as chaste women, not engaging in illicit intercourse or taking secret lovers.”

Musāfiḥātin is feminine plural here.

The Qur’an thus does not attribute musāfaḥah only to men: Al-Nisāʾ 4:24 addresses men, and Al-Nisāʾ 4:25 women.

This grammatical distinction shows that responsibility for sexual conduct is not restricted to one sex.

Another important feature is musāfiḥātin appearing beside muttakhidhāti akhdānin.

Akhdān belongs to KH D N and denotes close companions. In this sexual context, taking akhdān concerns secret relationships outside marriage.

Alongside excluding musāfaḥah, the passage thus also excludes taking secret lovers.

Must musāfaḥah be an open relationship?

Lexical and exegetical explanations sometimes distinguish two forms of illicit sexual relations:

Open, unrestricted relations described as musāfaḥah, and a secret relationship with a particular partner described as taking akhdān.

This distinction fits the expressions’ juxtaposition in Al-Nisāʾ 4:25.

A word’s general meaning must nevertheless be distinguished from a contrast understood through its pairing with another expression.

S F Ḥ itself does not mean being visible to the public. The social sense of musāfaḥah concerns illicit sexual intercourse.

When paired with akhdān, the context supports a distinction between unrestricted or open relations and a secret relationship with a particular partner.

It does not follow that every musāfaḥah occurred before spectators or that all secret relations were excluded from its broader meaning.

In either case, the central issue is the absence of a lawful framework for the relationship.

Musāfaḥah and taking akhdān are thus distinct expressions that can describe different forms of illicit relations without having entirely separate, non-overlapping meanings in every use.

Women’s rights and the relationship’s framework

Al-Nisāʾ 4:25 combines marriage, the permission of their people, honorable payment of financial dues, iḥṣān, and the exclusion of musāfaḥah and taking akhdān.

Sexual relations in this context are therefore not isolated from social and legal responsibilities.

The woman is not presented merely as an object of male sexual enjoyment: her dues and the marriage framework are expressly addressed.

Musāfaḥah should therefore not be defined solely as a relationship in which a man benefits while the woman has no role or responsibility. The feminine plural here does not support that one-sided limitation.

Nor does form III by itself establish freely given consent by both parties in every instance described. Consent and coercion require separate assessment through relevant evidence.

Al-Nūr 24:33 prohibits compelling women into prostitution. That concerns illicit intercourse, but coercion and the coercer’s responsibility should not be inferred from musāfaḥah’s lexical meaning alone.

Men, musāfaḥah, and secret lovers: Al-Māʾidah 5:5

Al-Māʾidah 5:5 includes:

إِذَا آتَيْتُمُوهُنَّ أُجُورَهُنَّ مُحْصِنِينَ غَيْرَ مُسَافِحِينَ وَلَا مُتَّخِذِي أَخْدَانٍ

idhā ātaytumūhunna ujūrahunna muḥṣinīna ghayra musāfiḥīna wa-lā muttakhidhī akhdānin

Al-Māʾidah 5:5

“When you give them their financial dues, maintaining chastity, not engaging in illicit intercourse or taking secret lovers.”

The phrase concerns marriage to chaste women among believers and those given the Scripture.

It combines payment of women’s financial dues, men’s chastity, and avoidance of illicit intercourse and secret lovers.

Its important grammatical difference from Al-Nisāʾ 4:25 concerns the people described.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:25, musāfiḥātin and muttakhidhāti akhdānin refer to women.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:5, musāfiḥīna and muttakhidhī akhdānin refer to men.

The Qur’an therefore does not attribute illicit intercourse and secret lovers solely to women.

This parallel matters: both men and women bear responsibility for chastity and the framework of their relationships.

Sexual desire and musāfaḥah

The existence of sexual needs and attraction is not itself musāfaḥah in the Qur’an.

Musāfaḥah does not name desire, love, or affection for another person; it describes sexual conduct and relations.

One should therefore not label every emotional attachment or bodily attraction musāfaḥah without conduct having occurred or circumstances being understood.

Avoiding musāfaḥah likewise does not mean denying sexual needs or devaluing the marital bond.

Al-Rūm 30:21 describes tranquility, affection, and mercy between spouses. Al-Baqarah 2:187 likens spouses to garments for one another.

These passages present marriage as more than a physical act.

Musāfaḥah in the passages under discussion, by contrast, concerns relations outside the lawful framework associated with marriage and iḥṣān.

The narrower lexical conclusion here is that musāfaḥah describes illicit sexual conduct and stands opposite iḥṣān in these verses.

5. Distinctions from nearby roots

S F Ḥ and S F K: pouring and bloodshed

S F K is especially associated with shedding blood in Arabic, although its lexical range is not necessarily confined to blood.

Al-Baqarah 2:30 includes:

وَيَسْفِكُ الدِّمَاءَ

wa-yasfiku al-dimāʾa

Al-Baqarah 2:30

“And sheds blood.”

Safk is directly associated with bloodshed in this usage.

Safḥ can also concern blood, but is not limited to it: water, tears, and other liquids can be described with this verb.

In Al-Anʿām 6:145, masfūḥan describes shed blood; yasfiku al-dimāʾa in Al-Baqarah 2:30 expresses the act of bloodshed.

The roots overlap here, but this does not make every use identical.

Safk al-dimāʾ in a passage about human conduct can concern killing and violence, whereas dam masfūḥ in Al-Anʿām 6:145 concerns blood as prohibited food.

The subject and ethical significance of each use must come from its own verse.

S F Ḥ and Ṣ B B: pouring and pouring down

Ṣabba means to pour or pour liquid down.

ʿAbasa 80:25 states:

أَنَّا صَبَبْنَا الْمَاءَ صَبًّا

annā ṣababnā al-māʾa ṣabban

ʿAbasa 80:25

“That We poured down water abundantly.”

Safaḥa in water- and blood-related uses belongs to the same general field.

The distinction is not absolute or identical in every case. Both can express pouring liquid and may explain each other in some contexts.

Safḥ, however, also has a specialized branch concerning illicit intercourse.

That branch does not give ṣabb the same social and legal sense.

Shared physical imagery therefore does not equate every derivative of the two families.

S F Ḥ and N K Ḥ: intercourse and the marriage bond

Qur’anic nikāḥ concerns the marital bond and the lawful framework of relations between women and men.

Musāfaḥah contrasts with that framework in Al-Nisāʾ 4:24, Al-Nisāʾ 4:25, and Al-Māʾidah 5:5.

Nikāḥ and musāfaḥah should not be treated merely as two names for physical intercourse.

Nikāḥ involves rights, commitment, and responsibility. Musāfaḥah in these passages denotes illicit relations outside that framework.

The key difference is therefore not merely whether intercourse occurs, but the framework within which the relationship exists.

Musāfaḥah and zinā

Z N Y is used for prohibited sexual intercourse in the Qur’an.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:32 states:

وَلَا تَقْرَبُوا الزِّنَىٰ إِنَّهُ كَانَ فَاحِشَةً وَسَاءَ سَبِيلًا

wa-lā taqrabū al-zinā innahu kāna fāḥishatan wa-sāʾa sabīlan

Al-Isrāʾ 17:32

“Do not approach zinā; it is an enormity and an evil way.”

Lexicons explain musāfaḥah through zinā, and its Qur’anic uses occupy the same behavioral field.

This proximity does not give all derivatives of Z N Y and S F Ḥ precisely the same imagery and semantic structure.

Zinā directly names prohibited intercourse; musāfaḥah is associated in lexical explanations with pouring and relations outside a lawful marriage bond.

In the marriage passages, musāfaḥah specifically contrasts with iḥṣān. The context highlights lawful, committed relations as against illicit ones.

Conversely, two roots do not establish two wholly independent categories of prohibited sexual conduct. Zinā and musāfaḥah clearly overlap semantically.

Musāfaḥah and khidn: unrestricted relations and a secret lover

KH D N concerns a close companion or friend.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:25 and Al-Māʾidah 5:5, taking akhdān refers to secret lovers in relations outside marriage.

Musāfaḥah concerns illicit intercourse in the same passages.

Their pairing can distinguish unrestricted sexual relations from secret relations with a particular partner.

Khidn is not itself synonymous with musāfaḥah, and musāfaḥah does not inherently require public visibility.

Both expressions should therefore remain in translation rather than being omitted or merged.

S F Ḥ and Ṣ F Ḥ: pouring and turning away

These roots look similar but have different first letters.

S F Ḥ begins with sīn and principally concerns pouring or causing liquid to flow.

Ṣ F Ḥ begins with ṣād and concerns overlooking, pardon, and turning away from wrongdoing in Qur’anic usage.

Al-Ḥijr 15:85 includes:

فَاصْفَحِ الصَّفْحَ الْجَمِيلَ

fa-ṣfaḥi al-ṣafḥa al-jamīla

Al-Ḥijr 15:85

“So overlook with gracious forbearance.”

Fa-ṣfaḥ belongs to Ṣ F Ḥ, not S F Ḥ.

Some lexical explanations propose a relationship or sound interchange in the name of a mountain slope. This does not make the roots identical in the Qur’an.

Masfūḥ in Al-Anʿām 6:145 therefore does not mean forgiven or overlooked blood, nor does ṣafḥ jamīl mean pouring something out.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Translation must distinguish physical pouring from the branch concerning sexual relations.

For masfūḥ, the basic equivalent is “poured out” or “shed.”

Daman masfūḥan in Al-Anʿām 6:145 is suitably rendered:

“Shed blood.”

“Blood poured out” is also lexically defensible. Persian “blood that has been shed” naturally conveys the resulting condition.

This should not be changed without grounds to impure blood, spoiled blood, or every kind of blood within the body.

For musāfiḥīna in Al-Nisāʾ 4:24 and Al-Māʾidah 5:5, Persian “those engaging in zinā” is natural.

Ghayra musāfiḥīna can be rendered:

“Not engaging in illicit intercourse,” or “not seeking sexual relations outside marriage.”

The first is shorter and suits a basic translation. The second explains the type of conduct and relationship lexically and contextually.

For musāfiḥātin in Al-Nisāʾ 4:25, “women engaging in illicit intercourse” or “women entering sexual relations outside marriage” are suitable.

Muḥṣanātin ghayra musāfiḥātin can naturally be rendered:

“Chaste, not engaging in illicit intercourse.”

“Sexual promiscuity” can also explain musāfaḥah in general language, but should not replace precise translation everywhere: readers may give it a vague scope broader than the prohibited conduct itself.

The following equivalents suit other lexical forms; the Qur’anic masfūḥ is included at the end for comparison:

  • Safaḥa al-māʾa: poured water or caused it to flow.
  • Safaḥa al-dama: shed blood.
  • Safaḥa al-damʿa: caused tears to flow.
  • Safḥ al-jabali: a mountain’s slope or lower part.
  • Sifāḥ: illicit sexual intercourse.
  • Musāfaḥah: engaging in sexual relations outside marriage.
  • Saffāḥ al-dimāʾi: a bloodshedder.
  • Masfūḥ: poured out or shed.

In the marriage passages, musāfaḥah should not become any sexual desire, loving someone, wanting marriage, or merely having an emotional relationship.

Nor should daman masfūḥan lose the qualification that the blood has been shed.

Conclusion

S F Ḥ concerns pouring and causing liquids to flow, clearly seen in its uses for water, blood, and tears.

Safḥ, sufūḥ, and masfūḥ relate to pouring and flowing. Safḥ al-jabal names a mountain’s slope or lower part, and safīḥ has other uses, including an arrow used in maysir.

The Qur’an uses the family in two principal fields.

In Al-Anʿām 6:145, masfūḥan describes shed blood. Its qualification is part of the Qur’anic wording and must not be overlooked in translation or discussion of food provisions.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:24, Al-Nisāʾ 4:25, and Al-Māʾidah 5:5, musāfiḥīn and musāfiḥāt concern illicit sexual relations in the context of marriage.

Musāfaḥah contrasts with iḥṣān and connects with marriage, chastity, and reciprocal rights.

Its pairing with taking akhdān also addresses secret lovers alongside illicit sexual relations. The expressions are close but should not be assumed identical without examination.

Masculine and feminine forms further show that the conduct is not restricted to one sex: both men and women bear responsibility for their relationships.

The root can therefore be defined as follows:

Physically, S F Ḥ denotes pouring and causing liquid to flow. Masfūḥ describes something shed or poured out. Musāfaḥah, in its social usage and the Qur’anic marriage passages, denotes illicit intercourse; lexical explanations connect it with pouring fluid, but its meaning is not confined to that bodily process.

The key Qur’anic distinction is between material condition and conduct: shed blood describes a liquid’s condition, while musāfaḥah describes human sexual behavior and relations.

For Forqan, “shed blood” for daman masfūḥan and “engaging in illicit intercourse” for musāfiḥ in the marriage passages are suitable basic equivalents. A fuller account of commitment, iḥṣān, and women’s and men’s rights must come from the relevant passages together, not from the root’s material imagery alone.
