From fruitless fire-kindling and a lot without a share to unsuccessful effort, disappointed hope, and the outcome of a wrongful course

The Arabic root KH Y B concerns failure and deprivation of an outcome that was expected or sought. Its words apply when someone tries to reach a goal but does not attain it, or misses a benefit they had hoped to receive.

In Arabic, «خَابَ سَعْيُهُ» means that his effort did not achieve the desired result; «خَابَ أَمَلُهُ» means that his hope was not fulfilled. A «خَائِب» is someone who has failed to attain the intended goal or expected benefit.

Khaybah does not necessarily mean losing something one previously possessed. Someone may lose neither property nor resources yet fail to obtain what they worked toward.

Khaybah is therefore close to khasārah, but they are not identical in every use. It can also accompany despair and sorrow, yet primarily emphasizes not attaining the desired goal and being deprived of the result, rather than merely an inward feeling of sadness.

The root occurs five times in the Qur’an: four instances of the verb «خَابَ» and one of the active participle «خَائِبِينَ».

All its Qur’anic uses concern the adverse outcome of human conduct and direction: the failure of defiant oppressors, fabrication against God, bearing wrongdoing, corrupting the self, and the unsuccessful return of a group opposing the believers.

Nevertheless, the lexical meaning of KH Y B is not restricted to disbelief, wrongdoing, or fabrication. These are instances the Qur’an associates with failure. The root itself expresses not attaining a result and remaining deprived of what is sought.

1. Forms and semantic core

KH Y B is a triliteral hollow root with medial yāʾ. Its second consonant is yāʾ, which becomes alif in certain inflected forms.

Its basic verb is «خَابَ ـ يَخِيبُ», and its familiar verbal noun is «خَيْبَة».

Important Arabic forms and derivatives include:

  • خَابَ ـ يَخِيبُ: to fail, remain deprived of what is sought, or not attain the expected result.
  • خَيْبَة: failure, deprivation of a desired outcome, and disappointed hope.
  • خَائِب: unsuccessful; someone who has not attained what they wanted or hoped for.
  • خَائِبُونَ / خَائِبِينَ: plural forms of the active participle khāʾib.
  • خَيَّبَ ـ يُخَيِّبُ: to cause someone to fail or disappoint their hope.
  • تَخْيِيب: causing failure or depriving someone of the expected result.
  • مُخَيِّب: something or someone that causes disappointment or failure.
  • مُخَيَّب: someone made unsuccessful or deprived of attaining a goal.
  • أَخْيَب: more unsuccessful, or marked by greater failure; in some early uses, a name for a lot yielding no share.
  • خَيَّاب: something that does not yield the expected result, including in the expression for fruitless fire-kindling.
  • خَيَّابِ بْنِ هَيَّابٍ: a proverbial name in an expression for fruitless, detrimental effort.
  • تَخَيَّبَ: in recorded later uses, to fail or have one’s hope disappointed.

These forms differ in antiquity and frequency. Some are well attested in early sources; certain extended forms became more common in later Arabic.

Only two morphological types occur in the Qur’an: the Form I past verb «خَابَ» and the active participle «خَائِبِينَ».

The past verb appears in Ibrāhīm 14:15, Ṭā Hā 20:61, Ṭā Hā 20:111, and Al-Shams 91:10. The active participle occurs in Āl ʿImrān 3:127.

The verbal noun «خَيْبَة», present verb «يَخِيبُ», and Form II «خَيَّبَ» do not occur in the Qur’an.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: five occurrences in four surahs

The fundamental meaning in Maqāyīs al-Lughah

Ibn Fāris writes in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

الْخَاءُ وَالْيَاءُ وَالْبَاءُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى عَدَمِ فَائِدَةٍ وَحِرْمَانٍ.

al-khāʾu wa-al-yāʾu wa-al-bāʾu aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā ʿadami fāʾidatin wa-ḥirmānin

Translation:

“Khāʾ, yāʾ, and bāʾ have one underlying principle indicating lack of benefit and deprivation.”

He then gives the image of fruitless fire-kindling: khayyāb describes a qadḥ that produces no fire. The reading «قِدْح» also occurs in transmissions. Lane understands «قَدْح», with fatḥah, as the effort of striking fire itself, and rejects identifying it conclusively with a particular implement.

He continues that someone strives in a matter and khāba when deprived of the intended result and obtaining no benefit.

This definition brings out two related elements.

First, a desired goal or expected result.

Second, remaining deprived of that result.

Both elements are clear in uses concerning human effort: someone has moved toward a goal without reaching it.

Nevertheless, not every use of khaybah necessarily involves prolonged, deliberate effort. Someone may simply have had a hope or expectation that was not fulfilled.

The expectation and desired result may thus arise through effort, hope, a request, or a situation from which an outcome is expected.

2. Material imagery and early Arabic uses

Qadḥ khayyāb: fruitless fire-kindling

One important early example is «الْقَدْحُ الْخَيَّابُ».

Qadḥ, with fatḥah, concerns the act of striking fire. Lane explains the expression as an effort to produce fire with a zand. Other sources and readings have qidḥ and an explanation involving a fire-kindling implement. It should therefore not be identified as a stone of a particular material and shape.

The shared image is an effort or implement from which the expected fire does not result, described as khayyāb.

In this image, someone works toward a specific result: producing fire.

Yet the result does not occur.

Failure thus concerns the expected outcome of an action or implement, rather than necessarily a defect in the shape, size, or material of a particular tool.

The example clearly illustrates khaybah as effort that does not yield the intended result.

No moral meaning is involved here either. A fruitless effort or ineffective implement does not, in itself, have a moral decision or will to refrain from work.

What occurs is the nonfulfilment of the human expectation of kindling fire.

This image helps explain khaybah concerning human effort and hope. However, Ibn Fāris’s explanation alone cannot establish that the fire-kindling use historically preceded all human uses.

Maqāyīs and Lane: fire-kindling imagery and the readings of qadḥ/qidḥ

A lot that yields no share

In another early Arabic use, «الْقِدْحُ الْأَخْيَبُ» refers to a lot among the maysir drawing arrows that yields its holder no portion.

This use must be distinguished from fruitless fire-kindling: qadḥ, with fatḥah, names the act of striking fire, whereas qidḥ, with kasrah, here denotes a maysir drawing arrow.

Although qidḥ also appears in fire-kindling transmissions, the similar written forms do not establish that the implements and activities are identical.

In the first use, the desired result is making fire.

In the second, someone expects to receive a share from a division or drawing of lots.

If the lot carries no share, the expectation of receiving a benefit is not fulfilled.

Lisān al-ʿArab mentions lots such as «الْمَنِيح», «السَّفِيح», and «الْوَغْد» that yield no share in the familiar maysir system.

The linguistic point is that al-akhyab can describe something that does not bring the expected benefit.

This does not mean that KH Y B itself means gambling or maysir.

Maysir is a particular social activity; khaybah is deprivation of a result, which can also occur within that activity.

Khaybah and disappointed hope

Arabic says:

خَابَ أَمَلُهُ

khāba amaluhu

That is, “His hope was not fulfilled.”

Also:

خَابَ سَعْيُهُ

khāba saʿyuhu

That is, “His effort proved fruitless.”

The first expression concerns hope; the second foregrounds effort and striving.

Someone may hope to receive good news that never arrives. Their hope has then been disappointed, even if they made no particular effort to obtain that news.

Conversely, someone may work for a long time toward a goal without achieving the desired result. Their effort has then failed.

Both situations involve not attaining what is sought, but the person’s relationship to that goal differs.

Khaybah therefore cannot be restricted to failure at work or psychological despair.

Khayyaba: causing another to fail

In Form II, «خَيَّبَهُ» means causing someone to fail or be deprived of what they sought.

For example:

خَيَّبَ أَمَلَهُ

khayyaba amalahu

That is, “He did not fulfil his hope,” or “He caused his hope to be disappointed.”

This construction differs from khāba.

In «خَابَ الرَّجُلُ», the man himself has failed.

In «خَيَّبَهُ», another agent is presented as causing his failure.

The transitive verb alone, however, does not establish that this agent acted unjustly.

Someone may hope to achieve a wrongful goal, and another may rightly prevent its realization. The failure is undesirable from the first person’s perspective, but may be a just outcome.

Causing another’s failure and wronging them are therefore not lexically identical.

Proverbial expressions in Arabic

Early sources record:

الْهَيْبَةُ خَيْبَةٌ

al-haybatu khaybatun

That is, “Fear and diffidence can cause deprivation of what is sought.”

The expression concerns someone who misses an opportunity through fear of asking or acting.

It does not mean that all fear or caution is blameworthy. The proverb applies where fear prevents the effort needed to attain a goal.

Another recorded expression is:

سَعْيُهُ فِي خَيَّابِ بْنِ هَيَّابٍ

saʿyuhu fī khayyābi bni hayyābin

That is, his effort follows a fruitless, detrimental course.

«وَادِي تُخَيِّبَ» is also used for a vain or fruitless situation. The variant «تُخُيِّبَ» appears in lexical transmissions as well.

These expressions show that khaybah in early Arabic was associated with fruitless effort, disappointed hope, and a course that does not reach its intended goal.

The precise origins of all these proverbs are unknown. Their presence in early dictionaries alone does not prove that they were all current before Islam.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Lisān al-ʿArab, and Tāj al-ʿArūs: proverbs and early uses

3. Evidence from Arabic poetry

Al-akhyab in poetry attributed to Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd says:

وَالصِّدْقُ يَأْلَفُهُ اللَّبِيبُ الْمُرْتَجَى
وَالْكَذِبُ يَأْلَفُهُ الدَّنِيُّ الْأَخْيَبُ

wa-al-ṣidqu yaʾlafuhu al-labību al-murtajā / wa-al-kadhibu yaʾlafuhu al-daniyyu al-akhyabu

Translation:

“The discerning man, hoped in and trusted, is drawn to truth; the lowly, deeply unsuccessful man is drawn to falsehood.”

Here al-akhyab describes someone whom the poet portrays as lowly and given to falsehood.

Akhyab has the afʿal pattern and can express greater failure or an intensified quality.

The poet places akhyab alongside danī and kadhib. In the first hemistich, truth is associated with a discerning, trusted person.

The moral contrast consequently arises from the words taken together.

Akhyab itself does not mean liar or lowly. Those qualities are conveyed separately by al-kadhib and al-danī.

This witness therefore does not establish lying or baseness as the meaning of KH Y B.

Transmissions of Ṭarafah’s Dīwān have both «اللَّبِيبُ الْمُرْتَجَى» and «الْكَرِيمُ الْمُرْتَجَى» in the first hemistich. The wording «الدني الأخيب» in the second is more stable across the transmissions consulted.

The line belongs to a poem attributed to Ṭarafah concerning the rights of his mother Wardah, also recorded in commentary on his Dīwān. It is credible evidence from poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period, although attribution within a Dīwān does not by itself establish the precise date of every line.

Commentary on the six poets: the reading al-labīb attributed to Ṭarafah

Khayyāb in rajaz transmitted by Thaʿlab

Thaʿlab transmits the following rajaz in Majālis Thaʿlab:

اسْكُتْ وَلَا تَنْطِقْ فَأَنْتَ خَيَّابْ
كُلُّكَ ذُو عَيْبٍ وَأَنْتَ عَيَّابْ

uskut wa-lā tanṭiq fa-anta khayyāb / kulluka dhū ʿaybin wa-anta ʿayyāb

Translation:

“Be silent and do not speak, for you are khayyāb, without benefit; you are full of faults, yet fault-finding toward others.”

The same rajaz continues:

إِنْ صَدَقَ الْقَوْمُ فَأَنْتَ كَذَّابْ
أَوْ نَطَقَ الْقَوْمُ فَأَنْتَ هَيَّابْ

in ṣadaqa al-qawmu fa-anta kadhdhāb / aw naṭaqa al-qawmu fa-anta hayyāb

Translation:

“If the people speak truth, you are a liar; if the people speak, you are fearful and helpless.”

Khayyāb is used here as a disparaging description of a person.

Lisān al-ʿArab offers two possible interpretations.

In the first, it is an intensive adjective from khaybah: someone very unsuccessful and without benefit.

In the second, the person is likened to the khayyāb fire-kindling implement that produces no fire: despite appearances and claims, nothing useful results from him.

These readings are close, but their lexical images differ.

In the first, the description directly concerns the person’s failure.

In the second, it involves comparison with an implement that does not perform its expected function.

Thaʿlab’s transmission does not identify the poet. Although the witness occurs in an early Arabic source, it should not be called definitively pre-Islamic without evidence.

Majālis Thaʿlab: the rajaz without a named poet

Alongside Ṭarafah’s line, it shows that KH Y B could describe fruitless conduct and personal failure as well as material deprivation.

4. Qur’anic uses: failure in opposition to truth

The opponents’ unsuccessful return: Āl ʿImrān 3:127

لِيَقْطَعَ طَرَفًا مِنَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا أَوْ يَكْبِتَهُمْ فَيَنْقَلِبُوا خَائِبِينَ

li-yaqṭaʿa ṭarafan mina alladhīna kafarū aw yakbitahum fa-yanqalibū khāʾibīna

Proposed translation:

“So that He may cut off a portion of those who disbelieved or subdue them, and they return unsuccessful.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:127

Khāʾibīn here is an active participle in the sound masculine plural, in the accusative case.

It describes a group opposing the believers with a goal, but failing to attain its intended result.

The preceding verses concern divine assistance and reassurance for the believers during confrontation.

Verse 127 mentions two possibilities: eliminating a portion of the opponents or subduing them, followed by their unsuccessful return.

Failure versus total military defeat

Khāʾibīn denotes deprivation of the desired goal, but does not itself specify the precise form of defeat.

A group may return from a confrontation without achieving its main objective. It is then unsuccessful even if its members have not all been killed or captured.

«فَيَنْقَلِبُوا خَائِبِينَ» should therefore not be translated as the complete destruction of every enemy without further evidence.

Yanqalibū conveys their return; khāʾibīn describes their condition on returning.

Grammatically, khāʾibīn is a circumstantial accusative referring to the subject of yanqalibū.

The verse thus distinguishes the return itself from its character.

Khaybah can therefore be the unsuccessful outcome of a political or military objective, but the root’s meaning is not restricted to warfare.

The verse concerns a result within divine assistance and confrontation. The active participle alone does not establish that every opposing group in every historical conflict necessarily and immediately meets the same outcome.

The failure of a defiant oppressor: Ibrāhīm 14:15

وَاسْتَفْتَحُوا وَخَابَ كُلُّ جَبَّارٍ عَنِيدٍ

wa-istaftaḥū wa-khāba kullu jabbārin ʿanīdin

Proposed translation:

“And they sought a decisive opening and judgment; every defiant tyrant failed.”

Ibrāhīm 14:15

Here khāba is a past verb, with «كُلُّ جَبَّارٍ عَنِيدٍ» as its subject.

Jabbār and ʿanīd are two independent descriptions.

In this context, jabbār describes someone acting through coercion and self-exaltation.

ʿAnīd denotes defiance and persistent opposition.

Khāba itself means neither. It states the outcome for someone possessing those qualities.

The preceding verses describe threats against the messengers by disbelievers who wanted to expel them from their land.

They then mention the promise to destroy the wrongdoers and replace them.

Within this setting, «وَخَابَ كُلُّ جَبَّارٍ عَنِيدٍ» concerns the failure of those who relied on force and defiance.

Is khaybah here only worldly defeat?

The verse presents the oppressors’ outcome in relation to divine judgment.

The following verse, Ibrāhīm 14:16, mentions Hell.

Failure in this context is thus not limited to losing temporary power or a particular confrontation; a wider outcome is involved.

This breadth comes from the connection between the verses, rather than the verb khāba alone.

An oppressor may acquire power, wealth, or dominance for a time. Such outward success does not contradict ultimate khaybah when measured against truth, since the goals and standards of outcome differ.

Khāba here should therefore be understood through the outcome of a person’s course in opposition to truth, rather than assuming that every tyrant fails to achieve every objective throughout life.

Early explanations also disagree over the subject of «وَاسْتَفْتَحُوا»: did the messengers seek judgment and victory, or did their opponents demand a decision while claiming to be right?

This matters for the opening clause’s context, but does not alter the subject or meaning of «خَابَ كُلُّ جَبَّارٍ عَنِيدٍ».

Under either reading, failure is the outcome of the defiant tyrant.

Al-Ṭabarī: readings of the subject of istaftaḥū

Fabrication against God and khaybah: Ṭā Hā 20:61

قَالَ لَهُمْ مُوسَىٰ وَيْلَكُمْ لَا تَفْتَرُوا عَلَى اللَّهِ كَذِبًا فَيُسْحِتَكُمْ بِعَذَابٍ وَقَدْ خَابَ مَنِ افْتَرَىٰ

qāla lahum mūsā waylakum lā taftarū ʿalā allāhi kadhiban fa-yusḥitakum bi-ʿadhābin wa-qad khāba mani iftarā

Proposed translation:

“Moses said to them: Woe to you! Do not fabricate a lie against The God, lest He destroy you with a punishment; whoever fabricated has indeed failed.”

Ṭā Hā 20:61

Khāba appears here at the end of a warning against fabrication.

Iftarā belongs to F R Y and in this context means inventing and attributing falsehood to God.

Khāba states the result of that conduct.

It must therefore not be translated directly as “fabricated” or “lied,” since the Qur’an uses the separate verb iftarā for that conduct.

Is every error in understanding religion a fabrication?

The verse itself concerns fabrication against God.

However, an unwitting mistake differs from attributing an invented statement to God.

Someone may misunderstand a verse or issue while sincerely seeking truth, then correct the mistake once it becomes clear.

Another may knowingly attribute an unsupported statement to God to pursue an objective or interest.

These situations cannot be equated merely because the resulting claim is incorrect.

The Qur’an also independently condemns speaking about God without knowledge, as in Al-Baqarah 2:169.

In Ṭā Hā 20:61, fabrication occurs in the setting of opposition to Moses and his warning.

Khaybah here is therefore the failure of someone fabricating against God, rather than an automatic judgment on everyone with questions, doubts, or mistaken interpretations.

The lexical distinction is clear: fabrication is the conduct; khaybah is its unsuccessful outcome.

The failure of one who bears wrongdoing: Ṭā Hā 20:111

وَعَنَتِ الْوُجُوهُ لِلْحَيِّ الْقَيُّومِ وَقَدْ خَابَ مَنْ حَمَلَ ظُلْمًا

wa-ʿanati al-wujūhu li-al-ḥayyi al-qayyūmi wa-qad khāba man ḥamala ẓulman

Proposed translation:

“Faces bow low before the Living, the Ever-Sustaining; whoever bears wrongdoing has indeed failed.”

Ṭā Hā 20:111

Here khāba accompanies «مَنْ حَمَلَ ظُلْمًا».

Ḥamala belongs to Ḥ M L, and ẓulman to Ẓ L M.

The expression presents wrongdoing as a burden someone carries.

Khāba itself, however, means neither carrying nor committing wrongdoing.

The verse states the outcome for a person standing before God with such a burden.

The surrounding verses concern the Day of Resurrection and humanity’s presence before God.

The next verse, Ṭā Hā 20:112, describes someone who does righteous deeds while believing, and denies that they need fear injustice or deprivation.

This connection clarifies the contrast between bearing wrongdoing and righteous action.

Does this verse deny the possibility of reform?

«مَنْ حَمَلَ ظُلْمًا» concerns someone described as bearing wrongdoing.

It does not establish that everyone who has ever done wrong remains forever in that condition, even after repentance, reform, and restitution.

Repentance, reform, and restoring rights are addressed in other Qur’anic verses.

This verse itself states the outcome of bearing wrongdoing on the Day of Reckoning.

Khaybah in this context therefore concerns someone confronted with their wrongdoing, rather than the mere record of a past error regardless of what followed it.

Nor does the verse specify a financial amount or visible form of loss.

Someone may have attained many worldly objectives yet ultimately fail because of the burden of wrongdoing.

Khaybah here thus concerns the true and final outcome, rather than merely outward worldly success or failure.

Falāḥ and khaybah in contrast: Al-Shams 91:9 and Al-Shams 91:10

Al-Shams 91:9 says:

قَدْ أَفْلَحَ مَنْ زَكَّاهَا

qad aflaḥa man zakkāhā

Proposed translation:

“The one who purified it has indeed prospered.”

Al-Shams 91:10 says:

وَقَدْ خَابَ مَنْ دَسَّاهَا

wa-qad khāba man dassāhā

Proposed translation:

“And the one who buried and corrupted it has indeed failed.”

These verses provide one of the clearest Qur’anic contrasts for understanding KH Y B.

Aflaḥa belongs to F L Ḥ, and khāba to KH Y B.

Here falāḥ and khaybah stand opposite one another.

Zakkāhā and dassāhā also describe two different actions concerning the self.

The preceding verses concern the self, its formation, and its awareness of transgression and reverence.

The two outcomes are then stated: falāḥ for one who purifies the self, and khaybah for one who debases it. This explanation and translation follow the reading of human agency transmitted from Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī and Qatādah. Another reading assigns purification and debasement to God, concerning a soul He purifies or abandons; Al-Ṭabarī preferred this reading. The disagreement over agency must be kept distinct from the semantic contrast between prosperity and failure.

Al-Ṭabarī: disagreement over the agent of purification and debasement

Is khaybah the same as sadness or depression?

The two verses primarily concern the outcome of a person’s course, rather than merely their feelings at a particular moment.

Someone acting wrongfully may feel satisfied, powerful, or even happy in worldly life.

Such feelings alone do not establish that their course has reached a sound outcome.

Conversely, someone pursuing reform and purification may experience difficulty and sorrow, without that temporary suffering necessarily constituting khaybah.

Khaybah here should therefore not be translated merely as becoming sad, becoming depressed, or losing hope.

The closer meaning is failing and remaining deprived of the desired outcome.

The difference between dassāhā and khāba

Dassāhā belongs to another root and requires its own independent study.

Early explanations associate it with concealing and burying, casting down, or corrupting the self.

Al-Baghawī: the two readings of agency and the meaning of tadsiyah

Explanations of tadsiyah, however, must not be transferred into the meaning of khaybah.

Under the reading of human agency, dassāhā states a person’s conduct or process concerning the self; the other reading assigns the action differently.

Khāba states its outcome and result.

The same distinction applies to the preceding verse: zakkāhā concerns purification, while aflaḥa states the outcome.

The two verses thus connect course and outcome, without making them lexically identical.

Forqan’s existing study of F L Ḥ also examines falāḥ through attaining sound fruit, purification, and a wholesome outcome.

That background helps explain the contrast between these verses. Nevertheless, khaybah must still be understood through independent evidence for KH Y B.

5. Khaybah and related Qur’anic words

KH Y B (khaybah) compared with KH S R (khusrān): failure and loss

Khasārah in Arabic concerns losing, suffering loss, and diminution of benefit or property.

Khaybah emphasizes not attaining what is sought and remaining deprived of an expected result.

These concepts coincide in some situations, but are not complete synonyms.

A merchant may, for example, lose their capital in a transaction and suffer a loss.

Someone else may lose no money yet unsuccessfully seek employment, a position, or a result. Failure has occurred without necessarily involving financial loss.

Qur’anic khusrān encompasses a broad field of loss and the ruin of the outcome of human life.

Al-ʿAṣr 103:2, for example, describes humanity as being in loss; Al-Shams 91:10 sets khaybah against falāḥ.

These fields are close regarding human outcomes, but translation must preserve the difference between loss and deprivation of what is sought.

Study of KH S R

KH Y B (khaybah) compared with F L Ḥ (falāḥ): failure and attaining fruit

Qur’anic falāḥ concerns prosperity and attaining a sound outcome.

In Al-Shams 91:9 and Al-Shams 91:10, the two stand in contrast.

Outward success must nevertheless be distinguished from true falāḥ.

Someone may achieve a wrongful objective, such as obtaining wealth and power through deception or oppression.

Relative to that immediate objective, they may not have failed.

Such outward success, however, is not falāḥ by Qur’anic standards.

Khaybah in the verses studied can instead describe someone ultimately deprived despite attaining temporary objectives.

The contrast between falāḥ and khaybah in Al-Shams therefore concerns the fruit and outcome of a human course, rather than merely a feeling of success or failure in an everyday situation.

Study of F L Ḥ

KH Y B (khaybah) compared with Y ʾ S (yaʾs): failure and loss of hope

Yaʾs concerns despair and abandoning hope.

Khaybah concerns an unfulfilled objective or hope.

Abū Hilāl al-ʿAskarī’s Muʿjam al-Furūq al-Lughawiyyah distinguishes them: khaybah follows hope or expectation of obtaining something, whereas yaʾs can precede hope or follow it.

This is a useful semantic distinction.

Someone may despair of succeeding in an undertaking before even beginning it.

Yaʾs is present, but their effort cannot necessarily be called unsuccessful, since they may not have made any effort yet.

Conversely, someone may strive without achieving their goal yet remain hopeful of succeeding at another opportunity.

Khaybah has occurred in the previous effort, but complete yaʾs is not necessarily present.

Failure and despair can accompany one another, but are not the same condition.

Al-Furūq al-Lughawiyyah: distinguishing yaʾs, qunūṭ, and khaybah

KH Y B (khaybah) compared with Q N Ṭ (qunūṭ): deprivation and intense despair

Qunūṭ commonly concerns despair and is described in some lexical explanations as more intense than yaʾs.

Khaybah primarily concerns an unrealized outcome.

Someone may experience qunūṭ after failure, but that psychological consequence is not a necessary component of khaybah.

The Qur’an forbids despair of God’s mercy, as in Al-Zumar 39:53.

This use concerns human hope in divine mercy.

By contrast, «وَقَدْ خَابَ مَنِ افْتَرَىٰ» in Ṭā Hā 20:61 concerns the failure of someone who fabricated, without necessarily describing an inward feeling of despair.

Khaybah must therefore not be translated as despair in every verse.

KH Y B (khaybah) compared with F SH L (fashal): faltering and failure

Fashal in Arabic and Qur’anic usage concerns faltering, weakness of resolve, or loss of steadfastness.

Āl ʿImrān 3:122, for example, mentions two groups that nearly faltered.

In such a context, fashal can be a condition that exposes someone to defeat.

Khaybah emphasizes the outcome and deprivation of what is sought.

Someone may remain very steadfast in their effort without obtaining the expected result.

Conversely, someone may falter, then correct their conduct, resume striving, and ultimately attain the result.

Fashal and khaybah therefore differ across situations.

In contemporary Persian, shekast, “defeat/failure,” may seem a possible equivalent for both. Precise Qur’anic translation must preserve the distinction between faltering along a course and failing in its outcome.

KH Y B (khaybah) compared with Ḥ R M (ḥirmān): deprivation and not attaining a goal

Ibn Fāris and Ibn Manẓūr use ḥirmān to explain khaybah.

Their closeness is clear: an unsuccessful person is deprived of the desired result or benefit.

Ḥirmān can describe deprivation even when someone has made no particular effort toward obtaining something.

Khaybah in its familiar use has a clearer connection to expectation, seeking, or hope.

They therefore overlap considerably, but khaybah places greater emphasis on failure regarding something expected.

Study of Ḥ R M

KH Y B (khaybah) compared with KH W B (khawbah): deprivation and destitution

Some early sources record «خَابَ يَخُوبُ» as becoming poor.

Khawbah is also recorded for hunger, destitution, and loss of resources.

Al-khawbah can additionally describe land that receives no rain although adjoining land has been watered.

These uses are close to deprivation and lack of benefit.

Dictionaries nevertheless record these forms under KH W B and KH Y B as two entries; some also give yakhūbu as a linguistic form close to yakhību. These phonetic and semantic connections alone do not establish their derivational history or the definitive priority of one over the other.

The Qur’anic forms studied belong to KH Y B. Poverty or rainless land must therefore not directly replace khāba in the verses.

Lexical sources: khawbah, poverty, and rainless land

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

“To fail” is accurate and natural for KH Y B in most Qur’anic uses.

It conveys not attaining what is sought without necessarily adding financial loss, psychological despair, or bodily destruction.

“To remain deprived of the result” is also a suitable explanation, though sometimes too lengthy for a fluent verse translation.

“To be defeated” may fit certain contexts, especially confrontation and dominance. Indiscriminate use, however, can restrict khaybah to defeat in a battle or competition.

In Al-Shams 91:10, “failed” is more precise than “despaired,” since the verse concerns the outcome of debasing the self, rather than a person’s feelings.

In Ṭā Hā 20:61, “failed” suits the person fabricating falsehood.

In Ṭā Hā 20:111, “failed” fits the condition of one bearing wrongdoing.

In Ibrāhīm 14:15, “failed” can be said of the defiant tyrant.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:127, khāʾibīn means “unsuccessful,” describing those who return without attaining their objective.

Brief root meaning

Failing and remaining deprived of the expected result or benefit; not attaining something one pursued or hoped for.

Meanings in Qur’anic uses

خَائِبِينَ — Āl ʿImrān 3:127: unsuccessful; those returning without achieving their objective.

خَابَ — Ibrāhīm 14:15: failed; the defiant tyrant’s outcome in relation to divine judgment and promise.

خَابَ — Ṭā Hā 20:61: failed; someone who fabricates against God.

خَابَ — Ṭā Hā 20:111: failed; someone confronted with the burden of wrongdoing.

خَابَ — Al-Shams 91:10: failed; someone who debases their self. This follows the reading of human agency, whose exegetical alternatives were explained above.

The root’s meaning remains consistent across these equivalents, while the kind of effort and cause of failure are established by each verse’s context.

Conclusion

KH Y B in Arabic concerns deprivation of an expected outcome and failure to attain what is sought.

Ibn Fāris relates it to lack of benefit and deprivation, illustrating it through fire-kindling that produces no result. In another use, a lot yielding no share is described by the same family.

Arabic poetic witnesses, including the line attributed to Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd, show that the family could also describe an unsuccessful, unbenefited person.

Its five Qur’anic uses concern the outcome of human conduct and direction. Tyranny, fabrication against God, bearing wrongdoing, and debasing the self are associated with failure; in Āl ʿImrān 3:127, returning without attaining the objective is described as khāʾibīn.

Khaybah itself, however, does not mean wrongdoing, disbelief, fabrication, or corruption of the self. The verses identify these actions independently; khāba states their unsuccessful outcome.

Khaybah is also related to yaʾs, qunūṭ, khusrān, and fashal, but is not a complete synonym of any. Someone can fail without losing all hope, or miss a goal without losing previously held property.

In the contrast between Al-Shams 91:9 and Al-Shams 91:10, falāḥ and khaybah are two different outcomes; under the reading of human agency, they concern how one treats one’s self. The ultimate criterion is thus not merely attaining a passing wish, but the outcome of the course one follows.

The fundamental meaning can therefore be expressed as:

“Not attaining what is sought and remaining deprived of an expected benefit or outcome.”

In Qur’anic usage, this deprivation becomes associated with the outcome of wrongful courses. Which conduct causes khaybah, however, is learned from the verses themselves, rather than the root’s lexical meaning alone. Brief root meaning: Failing and remaining deprived of the expected result or benefit; not attaining something one pursued or hoped for.
