Qur’anic Root GH TH W (غ ث و)

Ghuthāʾ: debris and weathered remains, from what a flood carries to vegetation that has lost its freshness

Qur’anic forms

غُثَاءً
(ghuthāʾan)
Noun; one form in two verses: Al-Muʾminūn 23:41 and Al-Aʿlā 87:5

1. The root and its Qur’anic form

The Arabic root GH TH W (غ ث و) concerns debris, weathered plant remains, and light materials that water and floods carry along or bear on their surface. In a familiar use, غُثَاء (ghuthāʾ) denotes decaying leaves, dry grasses, broken stalks, and small fragments moving with the foam and impurities on the water’s surface.

In Arabic, ghuthāʾ is not exclusively a name for dry vegetation. It can encompass an assortment of light, scattered remains collected along a flood’s course. It is also used for vegetation that has dried and deteriorated, losing its freshness.

In the Qur’an, this family occurs only as the noun ghuthāʾ. In one verse, a group of people is likened to ghuthāʾ after their destruction; in the other, pasture vegetation becomes ghuthāʾ after growing.

Form examined

The root GH TH W has just one nominal form in the Qur’an: غُثَاءً (ghuthāʾan), occurring in two verses:

  • غُثَاءً (ghuthāʾan): in Al-Muʾminūn 23:41, describing the end of a destroyed people.
  • غُثَاءً (ghuthāʾan): in Al-Aʿlā 87:5, describing vegetation that becomes dry, weathered remains after growing.

Other forms are also known in Arabic:

  • غَثَا، يَغْثُو (ghathā, yaghthū): said of a valley or water when ghuthāʾ becomes abundant in it.
  • غَثْو، غُثُوّ (ghathw, ghuthūw): recorded verbal nouns for this verb.
  • غُثَاء (ghuthāʾ): debris, foam, and light remains brought by a flood; also dry, weathered vegetation.
  • غُثَاءَة (ghuthāʾah): a single unit of ghuthāʾ.
  • أَغْثَاء (aghthāʾ): a recorded plural of ghuthāʾ; it has been considered irregular, and its acceptance is disputed.
  • أَغْثَى (aghthā): a verb used in connection with ghuthāʾ becoming abundant or collecting in water and floods.

None of these verbal or plural forms occurs in precisely that form in the Qur’an.

The root’s third radical also requires care. The usual morphological classification of the Qur’an places ghuthāʾ under GH TH W, since غَثَا يَغْثُو (ghathā yaghthū) is known in Arabic. Lexical sources, however, also report forms with yāʾ. A morphological index entry should therefore not be treated as settling every historical disagreement about the word’s origin.

2. Core meaning and early Arabic usage

Ghuthāʾ: what a flood carries

One of the clearest early Arabic uses of ghuthāʾ concerns debris visible on the surface of floodwater.

When heavy rain falls and water flows through valleys, it carries fallen leaves, dry grasses, broken branches, plant stalks, and scattered fragments. Some of these materials float, travel with the current, or gather along the banks and around obstacles.

This assortment is called ghuthāʾ in Arabic.

Lexical explanations record غَثَا الْوَادِي (ghathā al-wādī) for a valley in which ghuthāʾ has become abundant. غَثَا الْمَاءُ (ghathā al-māʾu) is likewise used when leaves, stalks, and scattered materials become plentiful in the water.

Ghuthāʾ therefore does not name the water or flood itself; it refers to materials the water carries or that appear on its surface.

This distinction matters. Ghuthāʾ is not a continuous portion of the flow in the way water itself is. It is an assortment of materials that the current has collected and carries along.

Nevertheless, movement in a flood should not be made an inseparable part of every use of ghuthāʾ. The same word also denotes dry, weathered vegetation, even when the sentence mentions no flood.

Ghuthāʾ and foam on water

Some Arabic sources also use ghuthāʾ for foam and impurities on water. Even the scum forming on the surface of liquid in a pot has been called by this name.

This usage shows that the word’s semantic range is broader than dry leaves and plant stalks.

It does not follow that ghuthāʾ always means pure water foam. Its familiar definition also includes plant remains, debris, and other materials that may be mixed with foam.

Translating ghuthāʾ simply as “foam” in every verse would therefore obscure part of its meaning.

Although their uses sometimes overlap, زَبَد (zabad) and ghuthāʾ are not exactly the same word. Zabad can refer to foam forming on liquids, whereas ghuthāʾ, in its familiar flood setting, includes the debris and remains carried along as well as foam.

Dry, weathered vegetation

Another important use of ghuthāʾ concerns vegetation that has passed its period of growth and freshness and become dry and weathered.

Fresh vegetation is green and moist. Over time, or as conditions change, it may lose its moisture, wither, and become dry, brittle stalks and leaves.

Such vegetation can be called ghuthāʾ.

There is a clear connection between plant ghuthāʾ and flood debris: much of what a flood carries consists of these dry, weathered, or detached plant remains.

This connection does not mean that every dry plant must previously have been in a flood.

In Arabic, the name of something familiar in one natural setting can also apply to similar instances. The use of ghuthāʾ for dry vegetation is thus compatible with its meaning of flood debris, without requiring water to be present in every sentence.

Evidence from pre-Islamic poetry

The Muʿallaqah attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays includes:

كَأَنَّ ذُرَى رَأْسِ الْمُجَيْمِرِ غُدْوَةً
مِنَ السَّيْلِ وَالْغُثَاءِ فَلْكَةُ مِغْزَلِ

“It is as though the summit of Mujaymir at daybreak, with floodwater and debris gathered around it, were the whorl of a spindle.”

In this verse, the poet depicts Mujaymir’s summit surrounded by floodwater and the debris it carries.

فَلْكَةُ مِغْزَل (falkatu mighzal) is a spindle’s round whorl. The poet makes the hill, surrounded by collected water and ghuthāʾ, resemble this implement.

This example matters because ghuthāʾ appears directly alongside a flood and refers to materials gathered in flowing water.

The verse has variant wordings. Its opening is transmitted both as ذرى رأس المجيمر (dhurā raʾsi al-Mujaymir) and as طمية المجيمر (Ṭamiyyatu al-Mujaymir). In its second half, الغثاء (al-ghuthāʾ), الغثّاء (al-ghuththāʾ), and الأغثاء (al-aghthāʾ) are reported. The transmission of aghthāʾ is accompanied by lexical disagreement over this plural; it should not be presented as an undisputed, regular plural.

Despite these textual variants, the word’s semantic field in the poetic image is clear: debris and remains collected with floodwater.

This evidence is valuable for Qur’anic study because it illustrates ghuthāʾ in early Arabic poetry independently of explanations of Qur’anic verses.

Figurative usage for people

Arabic also uses غثاء الناس (ghuthāʾ al-nās) for a group of people, particularly when a speaker wishes to express their supposed baseness, insignificance, or lack of the value the speaker seeks.

This use resembles the image of flood debris: materials carried away by flowing water and gathered as scattered remains after leaving their former place.

This figurative usage should not be made the primary meaning of ghuthāʾ, nor should the word be taken to depict people as inherently worthless in every instance.

In Qur’anic passages, we must first identify what has become ghuthāʾ or been compared with it, and then examine the contextual meaning.

3. “Fa-jaʿalnāhum ghuthāʾan”: the destruction of a people

Al-Muʾminūn 23:41

فَأَخَذَتْهُمُ الصَّيْحَةُ بِالْحَقِّ فَجَعَلْنَاهُمْ غُثَاءً فَبُعْدًا لِلْقَوْمِ الظَّالِمِينَ

“Then the mighty cry seized them in truth, and We made them like weathered flood debris. So away with the wrongdoing people!”

Here, ghuthāʾan describes the end of a group of people.

Context: a people after Noah

In the preceding verses of Al-Muʾminūn, the Qur’an describes the emergence of a community after Noah’s people. A Messenger from among them is sent to call the people to worship The God.

The leaders of that people, however, deny his mission. They reject his message because the Messenger is human and emphasize their social advantages and material circumstances against him.

In Al-Muʾminūn 23:39, the Messenger asks The God for help against their denial. Al-Muʾminūn 23:40 then announces their impending regret.

Verse 41 concludes the episode: a mighty cry seizes them, and they become ghuthāʾ.

In this passage the Qur’an does not explicitly name the people. This verse alone therefore does not establish with certainty that they are ʿĀd or Thamūd. Identifying them historically requires a separate examination of the relevant verses and evidence.

Ghuthāʾ: the end of a community’s power and cohesion

In Arabic, flood debris is an assortment of things detached from their former place and gathered by flowing water.

When the Qur’an says فَجَعَلْنَاهُمْ غُثَاءً (fa-jaʿalnāhum ghuthāʾan), this image describes a community’s destruction.

Before the event, they possessed life, social relations, power, and the ability to make decisions. After the mighty cry arrives, that life and power disappear.

Likening them to ghuthāʾ expresses this collapse through a tangible image: something that no longer has its former stability or position and remains as weathered remnants.

Nevertheless, the lexical meaning of ghuthāʾ is not “a destroyed people.” Its basic meaning is debris and weathered remains. In this verse, the destroyed people are likened to it.

Nor does ghuthāʾ imply that their bodies necessarily crumbled into plant particles or that an actual flood carried their corpses away.

The verse itself identifies الصيحة (al-ṣayḥah), the mighty cry, as the cause of destruction, rather than a flood.

Here ghuthāʾ depicts their end; it does not give a physical account of how they died.

Does ghuthāʾ mean that human beings are worthless?

The image of debris may suggest insignificance or loss of standing. But a person’s moral worth must be distinguished from the destruction of a people’s power.

The verse concerns a group that, within the narrative, denied the Messenger’s message and is finally called الْقَوْمِ الظَّالِمِينَ (al-qawmi al-ẓālimīn), “the wrongdoing people.”

The moral judgment concerning them therefore comes from their conduct and description as wrongdoers, rather than solely from the lexical meaning of ghuthāʾ.

Ghuthāʾ depicts their end here. Translating it from the outset as “worthless people” would both remove the natural image of debris and transfer the moral judgment into the root itself.

“Like weathered flood debris” better preserves this distinction.

The role of “fa-jaʿalnāhum”

The verb جَعَلْنَاهُمْ (jaʿalnāhum) also matters for understanding the verse.

In this construction, the pronoun هم (hum) refers to the people and is the first object; ghuthāʾ is the second object and expresses the state into which they were made.

The verse thus does more than say that they resembled debris; it describes a change in their condition after the mighty cry arrives.

Even so, the construction alone does not establish that the change must have been a material conversion of human bodies into debris. Arabic can also use “making” or “rendering” to express a change of condition or outcome.

In this context, destruction and collapse fit both the sentence’s construction and the preceding narrative.

4. “Fa-jaʿalahu ghuthāʾan aḥwā”: the end of pasture vegetation

Al-Aʿlā 87:4

وَالَّذِي أَخْرَجَ الْمَرْعَىٰ

“And the One who brought forth pasture vegetation,”

Al-Aʿlā 87:5

فَجَعَلَهُ غُثَاءً أَحْوَىٰ

“Then made it dry, dark debris.”

In these two verses, ghuthāʾ has a concrete, botanical application.

The pronoun’s referent and the plant’s transformation

Verse 4 contains الْمَرْعَىٰ (al-marʿā): vegetation that grows for grazing and feeding livestock.

In verse 5, the pronoun هُ (hu) in فَجَعَلَهُ (fa-jaʿalahu) refers back to this same marʿā.

The passage therefore concerns vegetation that was first brought forth and subsequently becomes ghuthāʾ.

This clear connection shows that ghuthāʾ in this context concerns plant remains.

Vegetation that was once green and fresh undergoes a transformation, reaching a state in which it no longer possesses its former freshness and vitality.

The verse does not directly present a flood image. Its basic translation should therefore not add that a flood necessarily carried the vegetation away or piled it beside a valley.

Such an image belongs to the lexical uses of ghuthāʾ, but the verse itself speaks only of vegetation growing and then becoming ghuthāʾ.

For this application, “dry, weathered debris” is consequently more suitable than “debris carried away by a flood.”

What does “aḥwā” mean?

In verse 5, ghuthāʾ is accompanied by أَحْوَى (aḥwā).

This word belongs to another root and, in Arabic usage, concerns dark coloration, blackness tinged with green, or an intensely dark color.

There are two major syntactic and semantic analyses of its relationship to ghuthāʾ. These are two interpretations of the same wording, rather than two different recitations of the verse.

First analysis: aḥwā describes ghuthāʾ itself.

On this understanding, the verse says that vegetation becomes dry, dark debris after growing.

This analysis fits the sentence’s surface order. فجعله غثاء (fa-jaʿalahu ghuthāʾan) comes first, followed by aḥwā as a description of the resulting state.

After withering, drying, and the passage of time, vegetation may lose its former color and become dark.

Second analysis: aḥwā concerns the earlier condition of the marʿā.

On this interpretation, The God brought forth pasture vegetation of a deep green tending toward darkness and then made it ghuthāʾ.

This understanding rests on the use of aḥwā for very dark green.

It requires explaining the description’s relationship to al-marʿā in the preceding verse, since aḥwā follows ghuthāʾ in the surface order.

Both interpretations occur in the tradition of Arabic linguistic analysis. The first, however, follows the direct sentence order more simply, because it does not require relating the description back to a noun in the preceding verse.

For a basic translation, “dry, dark debris” is appropriate, while the possibility that aḥwā refers to the vegetation’s dark green before drying should remain part of the fuller explanation.

Does ghuthāʾ itself mean blackening?

In this discussion, ghuthāʾ and aḥwā must be distinguished.

Ghuthāʾ names the debris and weathered remains. Aḥwā describes their color or, on the other analysis, the vegetation’s earlier stage of growth.

The definition of the root GH TH W should therefore not say that ghuthāʾ necessarily means “blackened vegetation.”

Nor should ghuthāʾ be understood as burning in fire. The verse mentions no fire.

Vegetation can become debris through drying, deterioration, and the passage of time without having burned.

Ghuthāʾ and the life of vegetation

These two verses place growth and deterioration side by side.

The same vegetation that emerges from the earth and serves for a time as livestock feed can become dry remains at the end of its growing period.

This transformation is part of the familiar order of nature.

Nevertheless, the lexical meaning of ghuthāʾ is not “the life cycle,” “death and resurrection,” or “the law of universal decay.” Such concepts may arise in reflection on the verses, but the word itself names weathered remains.

Nor should the image of dry vegetation be taken to mean the absolute annihilation of its matter. Debris may continue to exist in nature or take part in other processes.

What the verse shows is the loss of the marʿā’s former condition and its transformation into ghuthāʾ.

5. Two Qur’anic uses: one image in two contexts

In Al-Muʾminūn 23:41, ghuthāʾ describes a people’s end. In Al-Aʿlā 87:5, it refers to plant remains.

What these uses share is the loss of a former condition and transformation into remnants that no longer possess their previous freshness or stability.

This resemblance must not erase the difference between the contexts.

In Al-Muʾminūn, the subject is a human community destroyed by a mighty cry after denying the Messenger. Ghuthāʾ depicts their end.

In Al-Aʿlā, the subject is pasture vegetation. Ghuthāʾ concerns a state of plant deterioration and has a more concrete meaning.

The use in Al-Muʾminūn therefore does not mean that ghuthāʾ originally denotes “divine punishment.” Nor does its use in Al-Aʿlā establish that actual vegetation or floodwater must be the subject in Al-Muʾminūn.

The word itself supplies the image of debris and weathered remains; the context identifies what has reached this state.

Is ghuthāʾ always useless?

In lexical and figurative usage, ghuthāʾ is often associated with unwanted things of little value, particularly debris that water carries away.

Being of little value in a particular use does not mean having no possible role or benefit in nature.

Plant remains, for example, may decompose in soil after drying or participate in the natural cycling of matter.

This fact does not change the Qur’anic meaning of ghuthāʾ. In its familiar image, the word denotes weathered, scattered remains.

It is therefore better to use “debris and weathered remains” as the basic definition, reserving “something utterly worthless” for contexts that actually support that judgment.

6. Comparison with related roots

GH TH W (ghuthāʾ) and Z B D (zabad): flood debris and foam

These words are close in the flood image, but they are not identical.

In its familiar use, زبد (zabad) denotes foam and frothy material forming on a liquid’s surface.

Ghuthāʾ can include, in addition to foam, dry leaves, broken stalks, debris, and other things carried by water.

This distinction can be examined alongside the image in Al-Raʿd.

Al-Raʿd 13:17

أَنْزَلَ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً فَسَالَتْ أَوْدِيَةٌ بِقَدَرِهَا فَاحْتَمَلَ السَّيْلُ زَبَدًا رَابِيًا ۚ وَمِمَّا يُوقِدُونَ عَلَيْهِ فِي النَّارِ ابْتِغَاءَ حِلْيَةٍ أَوْ مَتَاعٍ زَبَدٌ مِثْلُهُ ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ يَضْرِبُ اللَّهُ الْحَقَّ وَالْبَاطِلَ ۚ فَأَمَّا الزَّبَدُ فَيَذْهَبُ جُفَاءً ۖ وَأَمَّا مَا يَنْفَعُ النَّاسَ فَيَمْكُثُ فِي الْأَرْضِ ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ يَضْرِبُ اللَّهُ الْأَمْثَالَ

“He sent water down from the sky, and valleys flowed according to their capacity, while the flood carried swelling foam. A similar foam arises from what they heat in the fire, seeking ornaments or useful goods. Thus The God sets forth truth and falsehood. The foam is cast aside and carried away, while what benefits people remains in the earth. Thus The God sets forth examples.”

This verse uses zabad, not ghuthāʾ.

The relationship between the images is nevertheless clear. Both ghuthāʾ and zabad can appear on floodwater or move with its flow.

In Al-Raʿd 13:17, however, the Qur’an uses zabad to illustrate truth and falsehood. That meaning should not be inserted directly into the definition of ghuthāʾ.

Furthermore, the same verse uses zabad for material heated in fire. Its range is therefore not confined to floods.

Ghuthāʾ and zabad consequently overlap in some applications, while each retains its own range of use.

GH TH W (ghuthāʾ) and H SH M (hashīm): weathered remains and broken vegetation

In the Qur’an, هشيم (hashīm) concerns dry, broken vegetation.

Al-Kahf 18:45

وَاضْرِبْ لَهُمْ مَثَلَ الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا كَمَاءٍ أَنْزَلْنَاهُ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ فَاخْتَلَطَ بِهِ نَبَاتُ الْأَرْضِ فَأَصْبَحَ هَشِيمًا تَذْرُوهُ الرِّيَاحُ ۗ وَكَانَ اللَّهُ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ مُقْتَدِرًا

“Set forth for them the example of worldly life: like water We sent down from the sky, with which the vegetation of the earth mingled; then it became dry, broken fragments scattered by the winds. The God has power over everything.”

Here, هشيما (hashīman) refers to dry, broken vegetation, while تذروه الرياح (tadhrūhu al-riyāḥ) describes the winds scattering it.

This image is clearly close to ghuthāʾ in Al-Aʿlā 87:5.

Still, it would be wrong to say that hashīm is always something the wind carries, while ghuthāʾ is always something water carries. Such a boundary does not hold across all lexical uses.

Ghuthāʾ has a prominent association with flood debris in its familiar usage, but it also denotes dry vegetation. Hashīm emphasizes vegetation’s broken, brittle condition.

The two words are thus very close in some contexts, without necessarily being completely identical.

GH TH W (ghuthāʾ) and Ḥ Ṭ M (ḥuṭām): weathered remains and broken fragments

حطام (ḥuṭām) comes from the root Ḥ Ṭ M (ح ط م), which concerns breaking and crushing.

In Al-Zumar 39:21, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:65, and Al-Ḥadīd 57:20, ḥuṭām denotes vegetation or crops reduced to dry, broken fragments.

In these applications, its resemblance to ghuthāʾ in Al-Aʿlā 87:5 is clear.

The root Ḥ Ṭ M primarily concerns breaking and being crushed, whereas ghuthāʾ, in its familiar use, evokes debris and weathered remains, particularly in connection with flowing water.

Nevertheless, these words should not be assigned fixed, separate stages: for example, that vegetation must become ḥuṭām before ghuthāʾ, or the reverse.

Both can apply to dry plant remains. Their distinction follows from their lexical ranges and contexts, rather than a fixed sequence of deterioration.

GH TH W (ghuthāʾ) and GH TH Y (ghathayān): debris and nausea

Arabic sources record غثيان (ghathayān) for nausea and an unsettled stomach.

This family resembles ghuthāʾ in its first two radicals, and some lexical discussions consider forms with wāw and yāʾ together.

There is even an explanation comparing nausea with something unpleasant rising, just as ghuthāʾ rises to the water’s surface.

This resemblance, however, does not establish a common historical origin.

The Qur’an’s morphological classification places ghuthāʾ under GH TH W, while some lexical sources also report forms with yāʾ.

It is therefore better to acknowledge the disagreement and avoid importing the meaning of nausea into the Qur’anic verses without grounds.

Neither Qur’anic use of ghuthāʾ concerns illness, nausea, or an unsettled stomach.

GH TH W (ghuthāʾ) and GH W TH (ghawth): two different roots

GH TH W (غ ث و) and GH W TH (غ و ث) consist of the same three letters in a different order, but they are not one root.

The root GH W TH concerns giving assistance and asking for help. Forms such as استغاثة (istighāthah) belong to this family.

By contrast, GH TH W in Qur’anic usage concerns debris and weathered remains.

Shared letters do not establish a historical or semantic connection between these families. Ghuthāʾ should therefore not be translated as aid, rescue, or relief from distress.

GH TH W (ghuthāʾ) and GH TH TH (ghathth): debris and poor quality

Arabic غَثّ (ghathth) belongs to another family. It contrasts with سَمِين (samīn), “fat” or “substantial,” and can indicate leanness, lack of substance, or inferior quality.

This usage may resemble the dismissive treatment of ghuthāʾ in some figurative expressions, but similarity of meaning does not establish a shared root.

Ghathth belongs to GH TH TH (غ ث ث), while the usual Qur’anic classification assigns ghuthāʾ to GH TH W.

Lack of substance or inferior quality should therefore not be made the primary meaning of ghuthāʾ in every use.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

For ghuthāʾ, no single Persian word equally conveys both flood debris and dry, weathered vegetation in every application.

The basic equivalent is therefore best chosen according to context.

In Al-Muʾminūn 23:41, the proposed Persian wording means “weathered flood debris.”

Proposed translation:

“Then the mighty cry seized them in truth, and We made them like weathered flood debris. So away with the wrongdoing people!”

The flood debris image matters here because ghuthāʾ figuratively describes a people’s end.

“Lifeless remains” could also explain the outcome, but using it in place of ghuthāʾ would preserve less of the Arabic word’s distinctive image.

In Al-Aʿlā 87:5, “dry, weathered debris” is more suitable.

Proposed translation:

“Then made it dry, dark debris.”

The pronoun here refers to pasture vegetation, so a flood need not be added to the translation.

To make the botanical image more explicit, one could say: “Then made it dry, dark plant remains.”

In that wording, however, “plant remains” explains the referent more directly while conveying less of the familiar debris image.

When translating غثاء أحوى (ghuthāʾan aḥwā), darkness must be recognized as coming from aḥwā rather than from ghuthāʾ itself.

Equivalents such as “ash,” “something burned,” “worthless rubbish,” “base people,” or “pure water foam” should likewise not replace the basic meaning of ghuthāʾ without attention to context.

Brief explanations for Forqan’s word boxes could read:

  • غُثَاءً (ghuthāʾan), Al-Muʾminūn 23:41: weathered debris; here, an image of a people’s destruction and collapse.
  • غُثَاءً (ghuthāʾan), Al-Aʿlā 87:5: dry debris; the remains of vegetation that has lost its freshness and vitality.

8. Conclusion

In Arabic, the root GH TH W is known through ghuthāʾ: debris and light remains that a flood carries or collects on its surface. Decaying leaves, dry grasses, broken stalks, and scattered fragments are familiar examples. In some uses the word also encompasses foam and impurities on water.

Ghuthāʾ occurs only twice in the Qur’an.

In Al-Muʾminūn 23:41, a people becomes ghuthāʾ after the mighty cry arrives. The image describes their destruction and the loss of their previous condition, rather than establishing that their bodies turned into plant matter or were carried away by an actual flood.

In Al-Aʿlā 87:5, ghuthāʾ concerns pasture vegetation that becomes dry, weathered remains after growing. Aḥwā further describes its color or condition, and two linguistic interpretations of this description’s precise relationship can be examined.

These two uses show that ghuthāʾ can name actual plant remains and also serve figuratively to describe a human community’s destruction.

Ghuthāʾ also resembles zabad, hashīm, and ḥuṭām semantically, but these should not all be collapsed into one word. Zabad is chiefly associated with foam, hashīm with dry, broken vegetation, and ḥuṭām with the result of breaking, whereas ghuthāʾ foregrounds debris and weathered remains.

For Forqan, the root’s basic definition is: ghuthāʾ, debris and light, weathered remains, particularly what a flood carries or gathers on the water’s surface; in its botanical use, vegetation reduced to debris after drying and losing its freshness.

This definition covers both Qur’anic applications without making destruction, punishment, moral worthlessness, blackening, or burning an invariant part of the root itself.
