Justice, respect for rights, and a rightful share: from judgment and measurement to the distinction between the just and the unjust

In early Arabic, the root ق س ط has two opposing semantic directions: justice and respect for rights on one side, and injustice and departure from what is right on the other. Word formation, vowels, and context distinguish these directions.

In its familiar use, قِسْط denotes justice, fairness, and respecting each person's share and rights. أَقْسَطَ means to act justly, and مُقْسِط describes someone who acts fairly.

By contrast, one principal use of the basic verb قَسَطَ means to act unjustly or depart from what is right. In this sense, قَاسِط describes an unjust person who deviates from justice.

This distinction is clear in the Quran as well. The God loves الْمُقْسِطِينَ, whereas in Al-Jinn, الْقَاسِطُونَ are among those whose end is the Fire.

Alongside these two directions, قِسْط in Arabic can mean a share, portion, or specified amount belonging to a person. Forms such as أَقْسَاط, for assigned shares and portions, belong to this usage.

The sense of a share must not, however, be imposed on every Quranic occurrence. The Quran discusses qisṭ in testimony, judgment, social relations, orphans' rights, transactions, treatment of opponents, and the final reckoning. Many of these concern preserving truth and preventing injustice, rather than merely distributing property.

The root occurs 25 times in 22 Quranic verses, in five morphological groups: قِسْط, أَقْسَطُ, Form IV verbs, مُقْسِطِين, and قَاسِطُون.

Understanding this family requires keeping three matters distinct: a share or portion, the practice of justice, and unjust conduct that departs from what is right.

1. The root in early Arabic

In early Arabic, qisṭ was used in judgment, distribution, and relations between people.

When several people have shares in property, respecting each person's entitlement can be expressed through قِسْط. Likewise, judging a dispute impartially and according to what is right can be called إِقْسَاط.

Conversely, someone who departs from what is right or judges unjustly is called قَاسِط in its familiar usage.

Early sources also distinguish nominal forms:

قِسْط, with initial kasrah, concerns justice and a share.

قَسْط and قُسُوط, in usages concerning injustice, denote departure from what is right.

Vocalization matters, but no exceptionless rule explains every historical use of the basic verb. Some early sources record قَسَطَ both for acting unjustly and for acting justly.

Thus, although the distinction between قَاسِط and مُقْسِط is clear in the Quran, this does not mean that every basic verbal form throughout Arabic history necessarily meant injustice.

A poetic witness attributed to Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd reads:

كَذَاكَ الْحُكْمُ يُقْسِطُ أَوْ يَجُورُ

kadhāka al-ḥukmu yuqsiṭu aw yajūru

Translation: “Such is judgment: it deals justly or acts unjustly.”

Here, يُقْسِطُ stands opposite يَجُورُ.

Jawr concerns injustice and departure from fairness. Its opposing verb in this sentence therefore denotes just conduct.

This usage shows that a form belonging to qisṭ could stand in opposition to injustice in the Arabic literary tradition.

It does not mean that قَاسِط in Al-Jinn denotes someone just. There, word formation and context determine a different meaning.

The poem is recorded in the collection attributed to Ṭarafah and in early literary sources, including works on Arabic proverbs. Like much poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period, however, its surviving text comes through later transmission; the precise date of this verse has not been established by independent contemporary evidence.

It helps establish the range of early usage, but cannot determine exactly when either meaning arose.

Qusūṭ: departure from what is right

Early dictionaries also quote this hemistich:

يَشْفِي مِنَ الضِّغْنِ قُسُوطُ الْقَاسِطِ

yashfī mina al-ḍighni qusūṭu al-qāsiṭi

Here, قُسُوطُ الْقَاسِطِ refers to an unjust person's wrongdoing and departure from what is right. The poetic image connects wrongdoing with resentment and hostility.

The poet and date are not securely identified; some versions have غيظ in place of ضغن.

This therefore cannot establish a securely dated pre-Quranic usage. Its value lies in the early dictionaries' recording of qusūṭ and qāsiṭ for injustice and departure from right.

Shares and portions in early language

In Arabic, قِسْط can also denote the share or portion that falls to someone.

For example, when property is divided among several people, each may receive their qisṭ.

Here, the word denotes an assigned amount or share.

Dictionaries also record قَسَّطَ for separating something into portions or distributing it.

A verse attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays reads:

إِذْ هُنَّ أَقْسَاطٌ كَرِجْلِ الدَّبَى
أَوْ كَقَطَا كَاظِمَةَ النَّاهِلِ

idh hunna aqsāṭun ka-rijli al-dabā / aw ka-qaṭā kāẓimata al-nāhili

Translation: “When they were in groups, like swarms of young locusts, or sandgrouse at Kāẓimah coming to drink.”

Here, أَقْسَاط refers to groups or separate portions.

This shows that the family was not confined to moral judgment; it could also describe division into sections.

It does not establish that every division or share is fair. Dividing is one action; respecting rights in the division is another matter.

Opposing meanings and historical origins

Early Arabic sources record two opposing meanings in this family: justice and injustice.

Some lexicographers explain the hamzah of أَقْسَطَ as removing the basic verb's sense of injustice: abandoning injustice and acting fairly.

This is a morphological explanation worth considering, but should not be presented as the established history of the two verbs.

A connection between shares, fair distribution, and unfair distribution is understandable, but the evidence does not determine which sense came first historically.

A precise definition must therefore retain the attested branches and then examine which meaning the Quran employs in each form.

2. Forms and derivatives

ق س ط is a three-consonant root with various Arabic forms.

Its main forms include:

قِسْط: justice, fairness, and respect for rights; in another use, an assigned share or portion.

قَسَطَ: he acted unjustly or departed from right, although some linguistic witnesses also record just conduct for the basic verb.

يَقْسِطُ: the imperfect of the basic verb; meaning depends on form and context.

قُسُوط: acting unjustly and departing from what is right.

قَاسِط: an unjust person who departs from right.

قَاسِطُون: the plural of qāsiṭ; unjust people.

أَقْسَطَ: he acted justly and respected rights.

يُقْسِطُ: he acts justly.

إِقْسَاط: practicing justice and fairness, especially in judgment and interpersonal conduct.

مُقْسِط: a just person.

مُقْسِطُون and مُقْسِطِين: plural forms of muqsiṭ; just people.

أَقْسَطُ: more just or fairer; the Quranic comparative form.

قَسَّطَ: he divided something into portions or distributed it among people.

تَقْسِيط: dividing something into specified shares or portions.

تَقَسَّطُوا: they divided something among themselves.

قِسْطَة: a share or specified amount in some lexical usages.

أَقْسَاط: assigned shares and portions.

In modern Arabic, قِسْط can denote a periodic payment toward a debt, such as a monthly loan installment.

This relates to a designated share, but the modern technical meaning of installment payments must not be transferred to every Quranic occurrence.

Physical usages and other names

Dictionaries record قَسَط for a particular crookedness in the lower leg or foot.

رِجْلٌ قَسْطَاءُ describes a leg with a particular curvature.

A connection with deviation is imaginable, but without historical evidence one cannot claim that injustice developed from the sense of a crooked leg.

قُسْط, with initial ḍammah, also names an aromatic medicinal plant substance recorded in early Arabic sources.

That name must not be equated with قِسْط meaning justice. Shared consonants alone do not establish a common historical origin.

Quranic forms

This family occurs 25 times in five morphological groups:

  • قِسْط: fifteen occurrences.
  • أَقْسَطُ: two occurrences.
  • Form IV verbs: three occurrences.
  • مُقْسِطِين: three occurrences.
  • قَاسِطُون: two occurrences.

These occur in 22 verses.

The basic verb قَسَطَ and derivatives such as تَقْسِيط and أَقْسَاط do not occur in the Quran.

Non-Quranic usages help explain the family's history and semantic range, but must be distinguished from forms actually present in the Quran.

Distinguishing أَقْسَطَ from أَقْسَطُ

Without vowel marks these forms look alike, but their grammatical structures differ.

أَقْسَطَ is a Form IV perfect verb meaning to act justly.

أَقْسَطُ in Al-Baqarah 2:282 and Al-Aḥzāb 33:5 is an elative: more just.

In these verses, it describes a way of acting as better in its respect for rights.

Translation should preserve that distinction.

3. Qisṭ in divine oneness, guidance, and the purpose of the mission

A broad Quranic use of قِسْط connects it with divine command and human responsibility for upholding justice.

The God upholds justice

Āl ʿImrān 3:18

شَهِدَ اللَّهُ أَنَّهُ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ وَالْمَلَائِكَةُ وَأُولُو الْعِلْمِ قَائِمًا بِالْقِسْطِ ۚ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ الْعَزِيزُ الْحَكِيمُ

shahida allāhu annahu lā ilāha illā huwa wa-al-malāʾikatu wa-ulū al-ʿilmi qāʾiman bi-al-qisṭi lā ilāha illā huwa al-ʿazīzu al-ḥakīm

Āl ʿImrān 3:18

Translation: “The God bears witness that there is no deity except Him, as do the angels and those possessing knowledge, while He upholds justice. There is no deity except Him, the Invincible, the Wise.”

Here, قَائِمًا بِالْقِسْطِ refers to The God.

Qāʾim belongs to ق و م, and qisṭ to ق س ط.

The expression describes justice as something The God upholds and maintains.

It does not restrict qisṭ to a financial share or distribution of property.

The verse concerns divine oneness and testimony to it; in this context it describes His upholding justice.

Justice is thus a foundational characteristic of divine action and judgment here.

Later sources also list al-Muqsiṭ among divine names. In the Quran itself, however, this form does not occur as a direct name of The God. What it explicitly gives is قَائِمًا بِالْقِسْطِ and other expressions of His just judgment.

The Quran's actual wording must be distinguished from later lists of divine names.

The divine command to uphold justice

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:29, Muḥammad is commanded to say that his Lord has commanded qisṭ.

Qisṭ is a broad principle here, not merely the distribution of wealth.

Āl ʿImrān 3:21 condemns those who kill the Prophets unjustly and kill people who call for qisṭ.

Calling for justice can thus encounter powers and conduct hostile to it.

Qisṭ here is a serious matter in human relations, not merely a virtue praised in speech.

The word itself does not mean struggle or being killed; other verbs in the verse express those events.

Upholding qisṭ as a purpose of sending Messengers

Al-Ḥadīd 57:25

لَقَدْ أَرْسَلْنَا رُسُلَنَا بِالْبَيِّنَاتِ وَأَنْزَلْنَا مَعَهُمُ الْكِتَابَ وَالْمِيزَانَ لِيَقُومَ النَّاسُ بِالْقِسْطِ وَأَنْزَلْنَا الْحَدِيدَ فِيهِ بَأْسٌ شَدِيدٌ وَمَنَافِعُ لِلنَّاسِ وَلِيَعْلَمَ اللَّهُ مَنْ يَنْصُرُهُ وَرُسُلَهُ بِالْغَيْبِ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ قَوِيٌّ عَزِيزٌ

laqad arsalnā rusulanā bi-al-bayyināti wa-anzalnā maʿahumu al-kitāba wa-al-mīzāna li-yaqūma al-nāsu bi-al-qisṭi wa-anzalnā al-ḥadīda fīhi baʾsun shadīdun wa-manāfiʿu li-al-nāsi wa-li-yaʿlama allāhu man yanṣuruhu wa-rusulahu bi-al-ghaybi inna allāha qawiyyun ʿazīz

Al-Ḥadīd 57:25

Translation: “We sent Our Messengers with clear proofs and sent down with them the Book and the balance so that people may uphold justice. We sent down iron, in which there is great might and benefits for people, and so that The God may know who supports Him and His Messengers unseen. Indeed, The God is Strong, Invincible.”

The phrase لِيَقُومَ النَّاسُ بِالْقِسْطِ is particularly important here.

The subject of يَقُومَ is the people.

Thus the verse does more than say that Messengers speak about justice: it associates upholding qisṭ with the people.

The Book and balance accompany the mission and clear proofs so that people can act and judge by sound standards.

Qisṭ is therefore a practical responsibility in human life.

Someone may praise justice in speech while acting against it in transactions, testimony, family life, or judgment.

Upholding qisṭ requires actually respecting rights and fairness.

The verse continues with iron, its great strength, and its benefits. Their juxtaposition does not make qisṭ mean imposing belief by force or establishing justice through any kind of violence.

Iron, the Book, the balance, and qisṭ each have their own place in the verse.

It cannot give every group or government unlimited authority to impose its interpretation on others in the name of justice.

The stated purpose is upholding qisṭ; whether an action accords with right and justice must be assessed against Quranic principles themselves.

4. Qisṭ in testimony, judgment, and human relations

The Quran does not confine qisṭ to financial relations. Testimony, judging disputes between opponents, and reconciliation must also rest on it.

Justice even against oneself and one's relatives

Al-Nisāʾ 4:135

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا كُونُوا قَوَّامِينَ بِالْقِسْطِ شُهَدَاءَ لِلَّهِ وَلَوْ عَلَىٰ أَنْفُسِكُمْ أَوِ الْوَالِدَيْنِ وَالْأَقْرَبِينَ ۚ إِنْ يَكُنْ غَنِيًّا أَوْ فَقِيرًا فَاللَّهُ أَوْلَىٰ بِهِمَا ۖ فَلَا تَتَّبِعُوا الْهَوَىٰ أَنْ تَعْدِلُوا ۚ وَإِنْ تَلْوُوا أَوْ تُعْرِضُوا فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ كَانَ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ خَبِيرًا

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū kūnū qawwāmīna bi-al-qisṭi shuhadāʾa li-llāhi wa-law ʿalā anfusikum awi al-wālidayni wa-al-aqrabīna in yakun ghaniyyan aw faqīran fa-allāhu awlā bihimā fa-lā tattabiʿū al-hawā an taʿdilū wa-in talwū aw tuʿriḍū fa-inna allāha kāna bi-mā taʿmalūna khabīrā

Al-Nisāʾ 4:135

Translation: “You who believe, uphold justice and bear witness for The God, even against yourselves, your parents, or close relatives. Whether someone is rich or poor, The God has greater claim concerning both. Do not follow desire and depart from justice. If you distort or turn away, The God is aware of what you do.”

Here, قَوَّامِينَ بِالْقِسْطِ is connected with testimony.

Qawwāmīn conveys persistence and commitment in upholding something.

This is not simply an occasional judgment: a person must remain committed to justice in testimony and conduct.

The verse names difficult situations: testimony against oneself, parents, relatives, and the rich or poor.

Qisṭ must not depend on family affection, personal interest, or people's economic position.

For example, someone might conceal a fact out of affection for family, to prevent harm to a relative.

But concealing it can violate justice when another person's right depends on it.

Someone might likewise give false testimony favoring a person in poverty out of sympathy.

Poverty does not justify false testimony. Assistance must be given through proper means.

Here, qisṭ is connected with truthfulness and trustworthiness in testimony.

Restricting it to equal distribution of property therefore loses an important part of its Quranic use.

Justice even in the face of hostility

Al-Māʾidah 5:8

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا كُونُوا قَوَّامِينَ لِلَّهِ شُهَدَاءَ بِالْقِسْطِ ۖ وَلَا يَجْرِمَنَّكُمْ شَنَآنُ قَوْمٍ عَلَىٰ أَلَّا تَعْدِلُوا ۚ اعْدِلُوا هُوَ أَقْرَبُ لِلتَّقْوَىٰ ۖ وَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ خَبِيرٌ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū kūnū qawwāmīna li-llāhi shuhadāʾa bi-al-qisṭi wa-lā yajrimannakum shanaʾānu qawmin ʿalā allā taʿdilū iʿdilū huwa aqrabu li-al-taqwā wa-ittaqū allāha inna allāha khabīrun bi-mā taʿmalūn

Al-Māʾidah 5:8

Translation: “You who believe, stand firm for The God as witnesses with justice. Let hatred of a people not lead you to act unjustly. Be just; that is nearer to mindfulness. Be mindful of The God. Indeed, He is aware of what you do.”

This verse is an important witness to the relationship between qisṭ and ʿadl.

It first commands testimony بِالْقِسْطِ.

Then it uses أَلَّا تَعْدِلُوا and اعْدِلُوا.

Both ق س ط and ع د ل thus occur in one verse concerning justice.

Their coexistence shows a shared semantic field.

It does not prove that they mean exactly the same in every use, or that one is always a higher stage than the other.

Here, qisṭ concerns testimony, while ʿadl appears in the broader command to avoid injustice amid hostility.

Differences of belief and the right to justice

Hostility toward a group does not itself permit wronging them.

This is among the clearest implications of Al-Māʾidah 5:8.

Disagreeing with a group's conduct or beliefs does not authorize lies about them, violation of their rights, or biased judgment against them.

For example, if two people differ in religion and one has a definite entitlement in a transaction, that disagreement should not deprive them of it.

This accords with qisṭ: respecting rights and fairness does not depend on liking the rights-holder.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:42, Muḥammad is told to judge with qisṭ if he judges between the people concerned. The verse ends by saying that The God loves the just.

Justice therefore applies to judging others, not only members of one's own believing community.

Justice in reconciliation

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:9

وَإِنْ طَائِفَتَانِ مِنَ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ اقْتَتَلُوا فَأَصْلِحُوا بَيْنَهُمَا ۖ فَإِنْ بَغَتْ إِحْدَاهُمَا عَلَى الْأُخْرَىٰ فَقَاتِلُوا الَّتِي تَبْغِي حَتَّىٰ تَفِيءَ إِلَىٰ أَمْرِ اللَّهِ ۚ فَإِنْ فَاءَتْ فَأَصْلِحُوا بَيْنَهُمَا بِالْعَدْلِ وَأَقْسِطُوا ۖ إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُحِبُّ الْمُقْسِطِينَ

wa-in ṭāʾifatāni mina al-muʾminīna iqtatalū fa-aṣliḥū baynahumā fa-in baghat iḥdāhumā ʿalā al-ukhrā fa-qātilū allatī tabghī ḥattā tafīʾa ilā amri allāhi fa-in fāʾat fa-aṣliḥū baynahumā bi-al-ʿadli wa-aqsiṭū inna allāha yuḥibbu al-muqsiṭīn

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:9

Translation: “If two groups of believers fight, reconcile them. If one transgresses against the other, fight the transgressing group until it returns to the command of The God. If it returns, reconcile them with justice and act fairly. Indeed, The God loves the just.”

أَقْسِطُوا is a Form IV imperative.

It commands action in the context of reconciling two groups.

The verse begins with reconciliation, then addresses one group's aggression, and after that group's return again stresses reconciliation with justice.

It ends with the separate command أَقْسِطُوا.

This sequence indicates that ending fighting or stopping aggression is not by itself sufficient to achieve justice.

Two groups may cease fighting while one group's rights remain violated.

Peace that merely stops violence may therefore still require a fair resolution of both parties' rights.

In context, أَقْسِطُوا concerns fairness in reconciliation and judgment.

This one sentence does not supply every detail of a legal or military system.

The command to fight the aggressing group also belongs to this specified context; it is not unlimited authorization for war, revenge, or imposing belief.

Kindness and justice toward followers of other religions

Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:8

لَا يَنْهَاكُمُ اللَّهُ عَنِ الَّذِينَ لَمْ يُقَاتِلُوكُمْ فِي الدِّينِ وَلَمْ يُخْرِجُوكُمْ مِنْ دِيَارِكُمْ أَنْ تَبَرُّوهُمْ وَتُقْسِطُوا إِلَيْهِمْ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُحِبُّ الْمُقْسِطِينَ

lā yanhākumu allāhu ʿani alladhīna lam yuqātilūkum fī al-dīni wa-lam yukhrijūkum min diyārikum an tabarrūhum wa-tuqsiṭū ilayhim inna allāha yuḥibbu al-muqsiṭīn

Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:8

Translation: “The God does not forbid you to show kindness and act justly toward those who have not fought you over religion or driven you from your homes. Indeed, The God loves the just.”

تُقْسِطُوا إِلَيْهِمْ concerns just treatment of others.

The preposition إِلَى marks the direction of the relationship: acting justly toward them.

Alongside it is تَبَرُّوهُمْ, from ب ر ر, concerning kindness.

These verbs are not identical.

Kindness can include help, compassion, and good treatment. Justice concerns respecting the other person's rights and fairness.

One may avoid wronging someone and also go beyond respecting their rights by helping or showing kindness.

The verse does not reject good and just treatment of people who have not fought its addressees over religion or driven them from their homes.

Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:9 gives a different ruling concerning relations and allegiance with those who fought over religion, expelled people, or assisted in their expulsion.

This distinction must be retained.

It does not mean abandoning justice toward enemies: Al-Māʾidah 5:8 explicitly requires justice even amid hostility.

Iqsāṭ in the Quran therefore does not depend on both parties sharing a belief.

5. Qisṭ in financial rights, family, and measurement

An important use of this root concerns justice in people's specific rights and obligations.

Here, its connection with shares, quantities, measurement, and judgment becomes clearer.

Orphans' rights

Al-Nisāʾ 4:3 uses تُقْسِطُوا when discussing fear of failing to treat orphans justly.

The verse then addresses marriage and the condition of justice.

Qisṭ here is thus connected with the rights of vulnerable people, especially orphans.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:127 also discusses orphaned females, vulnerable children, and upholding justice for orphans.

These verses should be considered together.

Being an orphan does not permit unjust use of someone's property or rights. Guardianship and decision-making power do not amount to unlimited ownership of another's rights.

Here, qisṭ concerns people vulnerable to exploitation because of age, family circumstances, or dependence.

The root alone cannot supply every rule of marriage, guardianship, or inheritance.

Recording debts and أَقْسَطُ

Al-Baqarah 2:282 commands recording financial obligations with a fixed term and gives specific directions about the scribe, debtor, witnesses, and preventing harm to them.

It then describes this procedure as أَقْسَطُ before The God.

Here, أَقْسَطُ means more just.

Justice therefore depends on more than good intentions; the means of recording and establishing rights also matter.

For example, two people may lend and borrow with good intentions but later disagree over the amount or repayment date.

A clear record reduces the risk of disagreement and violation of rights.

This accords with the verse, which also describes the procedure as firmer for testimony and more likely to prevent doubt.

أَقْسَطُ itself does not mean writing or recording an agreement. Other instructions specify the procedure; this word describes its greater fairness.

Respecting lineage and أَقْسَطُ

أَقْسَطُ also occurs in Al-Aḥzāb 33:5.

That verse concerns attributing people to their actual fathers.

The Quran describes this as more just before The God.

It then addresses those whose fathers are unknown and distinguishes mistakes from deliberate intent.

Justice can thus concern identity and family affiliation, not only property.

أَقْسَطُ itself does not imply that supporting, caring for, or showing kindness to a child whose parents are unavailable is wrong.

The issue is accurate attribution and preserving its truth.

Qisṭ in measure and weight

Al-Anʿām 6:152 and Hūd 11:85 command giving full measure and weight with qisṭ.

Both emphasize respecting rights in transactions and avoiding short delivery.

Qisṭ here concerns fairness in measurement.

For example, a seller who promises a kilogram but delivers less has not respected the buyer's right.

When two people agree on a specified quantity and that amount is delivered accurately, fairness in quantity is observed.

Yet transactional justice is not confined to weight. Price, quality, truthful description, and the parties' consent can also matter.

These matters should be studied alongside other verses concerning transactions and people's rights.

Al-Raḥmān 55:9

وَأَقِيمُوا الْوَزْنَ بِالْقِسْطِ وَلَا تُخْسِرُوا الْمِيزَانَ

wa-aqīmū al-wazna bi-al-qisṭi wa-lā tukhsirū al-mīzān

Al-Raḥmān 55:9

Translation: “Establish weight with justice and do not give short measure.”

Here, qisṭ accompanies wazn and mīzān.

Justice in measurement must be put into practice.

Someone may claim to be just but reduce another's rightful quantity when weighing goods.

That claim does not accord with their conduct.

The root qisṭ itself does not mean scales. Wazn and mīzān are distinct concepts linked here to justice.

6. Qisṭ in the final reckoning and judgment

Quranic justice is not confined to this world's social relations.

Divine judgment and the reckoning of deeds are also described through qisṭ.

Yūnus 10:4 speaks of rewarding those who believe and do good with qisṭ.

Yūnus 10:47 describes judgment between people after their Messenger's coming as being with qisṭ.

Yūnus 10:54 likewise speaks of judgment with qisṭ and people not being wronged.

Qisṭ thus concerns fair judgment and respect for rights in the outcome of actions.

Just balances at the Resurrection

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:47

وَنَضَعُ الْمَوَازِينَ الْقِسْطَ لِيَوْمِ الْقِيَامَةِ فَلَا تُظْلَمُ نَفْسٌ شَيْئًا ۖ وَإِنْ كَانَ مِثْقَالَ حَبَّةٍ مِنْ خَرْدَلٍ أَتَيْنَا بِهَا ۗ وَكَفَىٰ بِنَا حَاسِبِينَ

wa-naḍaʿu al-mawāzīna al-qisṭa li-yawmi al-qiyāmati fa-lā tuẓlamu nafsun shayʾan wa-in kāna mithqāla ḥabbatin min khardalin ataynā bihā wa-kafā binā ḥāsibīn

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:47

Translation: “We shall set up the balances of justice for the Day of Resurrection, so no soul will be wronged at all. Even if it is the weight of a mustard seed, We shall bring it; We suffice as reckoners.”

الْقِسْطَ here qualifies الْمَوَازِينَ.

It expresses the balances' justice.

Grammar describes this construction as qualification by a verbal noun.

The singular form القسط beside plural الموازين does not imply that there is only one balance.

The central point is justice in measurement and reckoning.

The verse continues by stating that no soul is wronged.

Even something the weight of a mustard seed is not disregarded.

This connects qisṭ with precision, respect for rights, and avoiding any shortfall in judgment.

The word cannot establish the physical nature of the balances, their material, or the precise mechanism of divine reckoning.

The verse clearly affirms justice and precision in judgment.

Qisṭ and human responsibility

These usages promise people that they will not be wronged in the final judgment.

That does not mean everyone's actions have identical outcomes.

People's deeds, intentions, and responsibilities may differ, and so may the outcome of their reckoning.

Justice does not disregard those differences or assign everyone the same result.

Qisṭ requires judgment consistent with truth and each person's due.

7. Qāsiṭūn and muqsiṭūn: injustice and justice

The family's most significant semantic distinction appears in قَاسِطُون and مُقْسِطِين.

They share root consonants but have opposing Quranic meanings.

Qāsiṭūn in Al-Jinn

Al-Jinn 72:14

وَأَنَّا مِنَّا الْمُسْلِمُونَ وَمِنَّا الْقَاسِطُونَ ۖ فَمَنْ أَسْلَمَ فَأُولَٰئِكَ تَحَرَّوْا رَشَدًا

wa-annā minnā al-muslimūna wa-minnā al-qāsiṭūna fa-man aslama fa-ulāʾika taḥarraw rashadā

Al-Jinn 72:14

Translation: “Among us are those who submit, and among us are those who depart from right. Those who submit have sought right guidance.”

الْقَاسِطُونَ stands opposite الْمُسْلِمُونَ.

Qāsiṭūn is the plural active participle of the basic verb قَسَطَ.

In context, it denotes people who depart from right and the proper path.

The following clause accords with this: those who submit are described as seeking right guidance.

Qāsiṭūn therefore does not mean the just here; it stands opposite submission and right guidance.

Al-Jinn 72:15

وَأَمَّا الْقَاسِطُونَ فَكَانُوا لِجَهَنَّمَ حَطَبًا

wa-ammā al-qāsiṭūna fa-kānū li-jahannama ḥaṭabā

Al-Jinn 72:15

Translation: “As for the unjust, they will be fuel for Hell.”

The same word recurs here.

This time, their final outcome is stated.

Together, the verses make clear that qāsiṭūn concerns deviation and wrongdoing.

This does not automatically make every difference of interpretation, human error, or person of another religious designation qāsiṭ.

The passage speaks of groups with specified circumstances and choices.

Judging other individuals requires separately considering their conduct, awareness, and responsibility.

Muqsiṭīn: the just, whom The God loves

Al-Māʾidah 5:42, Al-Ḥujurāt 49:9, and Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:8 state that The God loves الْمُقْسِطِينَ.

Muqsiṭīn is the plural of muqsiṭ, belonging to Form IV.

It denotes people who practice justice.

Al-Māʾidah 5:42 concerns judging between people.

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:9 concerns just reconciliation and confronting aggression.

Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:8 concerns just treatment of people who have not fought the addressees over religion or expelled them from their homes.

Being muqsiṭ is therefore not confined to one particular act.

One can act justly in judgment, dispute resolution, social relations, and respecting others' rights.

Merely claiming justice does not make someone muqsiṭ. Actual conduct and respect for rights establish the description.

Is the distinction only the presence of hamzah?

Qāsiṭ and muqsiṭ have opposite meanings in Quranic usage.

This does not establish an absolute rule that Form IV always reverses the basic verb's meaning.

Form IV has various Arabic functions; semantic changes must be examined for each root and usage.

Early sources also sometimes record the basic verb qasaṭa for justice.

The precise explanation is therefore:

In the Quran, qāsiṭūn denotes the unjust who depart from right, and muqsiṭīn denotes the just. This distinction rests on actual usage, not a mechanical rule applying to all Arabic verbs.

8. Comparison with related roots

ق س ط (Qisṭ) and ع د ل (ʿAdl)

ʿAdl and qisṭ concern justice and fairness in the Quran.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:135 connects qisṭ with just testimony and also speaks of not departing from justice.

Al-Māʾidah 5:8 places qisṭ and ʿadl together.

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:9 commands reconciliation with ʿadl and then iqsāṭ.

The roots share the field of justice, but should not be treated as identical in all usages.

Qisṭ usages emphasize rights and shares, fair judgment, and accurate measurement.

ʿAdl covers a broad field of balanced conduct and judgment, respecting rights, and putting matters in their proper place.

One cannot say that qisṭ is always only distributive justice and ʿadl only moral or judicial justice.

The Quran itself uses qisṭ in testimony, judgment, and the final reckoning.

Differences should therefore be expressed as contextual tendencies, not absolute boundaries.

ق س ط (Qisṭ) and ح ق ق (Ḥaqq)

Ḥaqq concerns established truth, what is due, and what truly belongs to a person or situation.

Qisṭ concerns justice in conduct, distribution, and judgment.

For example, knowing someone's entitlement to property is one matter; respecting it in distribution or judgment is another.

Someone may know another's right but fail to respect it.

Knowing the right is then not itself acting with qisṭ.

Conversely, just action requires identifying what each party is entitled to.

Ḥaqq and qisṭ are connected in practice, but are not one concept.

ق س ط (Qisṭ) and ق س م (Qismah)

ق س م concerns division and separating shares.

Qisṭ can express a share or fairness in distribution.

Not every distribution is necessarily fair.

Someone dividing common property may assign shares according to actual entitlements, or favor one person with more and give another less.

Division occurs in both cases; only the first clearly observes qisṭ.

Fair distribution also does not necessarily mean numerical equality of every share.

Shares may differ according to rights, agreements, or legitimate circumstances.

Qisṭ should not be limited to “equal division in every circumstance.”

ق س ط (Qisṭ) and و ز ن (Wazn)

Wazn concerns weighing and determining quantity or weight.

Mīzān is an instrument or standard of measurement.

In the relevant use, qisṭ expresses justice and fairness in measuring.

Al-Raḥmān 55:9 places these concepts together.

Weighing may be accurate or inaccurate. Observing qisṭ means measuring fairly without diminishing another's rightful quantity.

The balance also accompanies qisṭ in the final reckoning.

Qisṭ is therefore not itself the name of a weighing instrument in these verses, although dictionaries record a balance-related use for some forms.

ق س ط (Qisṭ) and ب خ س (Bakhs)

Bakhs concerns diminishing another's right or rightful amount.

Hūd 11:85 commands full measure and weight with qisṭ alongside forbidding diminution of people's goods and rights.

The concepts are connected in that context.

Qisṭ prevents rights being violated, while bakhs is a form of diminishing them.

Bakhs is only one way of violating justice.

Someone may give false testimony or judge with bias without directly reducing a quantity of property.

Such conduct can also conflict with qisṭ.

ق س ط (Qisṭ) and ظ ل م (Ẓulm)

Ẓulm concerns wrongdoing and unjust conduct across various Quranic fields.

In its positive usage, qisṭ opposes ẓulm.

For example, Yūnus 10:47 and Yūnus 10:54 accompany judgment with qisṭ with the statement that people are not wronged.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:47 likewise affirms after mentioning the just balances that no soul will be wronged.

Qisṭ is thus directly connected with the denial of injustice.

Yet ẓulm and qisṭ should not become mere moral labels requiring no examination.

Assessing justice or injustice requires considering rights, actions, circumstances, and outcomes.

ق س ط (Qisṭ) and ق س ط س (Qisṭās)

قِسْطَاس occurs in Al-Isrāʾ 17:35 and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:182, concerning a balance or instrument of accurate measurement.

Its consonants resemble qisṭ, and early lexicographers discuss a semantic connection.

The Quranic morphological index, however, classifies qisṭās under the four-consonant entry ق س ط س.

Views also differ about its precise history and origin.

It should not be treated as an ordinary derivative of the three-consonant ق س ط without separate investigation.

Qisṭās concerns a measuring instrument or standard in the Quran, whereas qisṭ in the verses examined denotes justice and fairness.

They may occupy a shared semantic field while differing in form and usage.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

“Justice,” “just conduct,” and “fairness” are suitable equivalents for Quranic قِسْط.

The choice depends on sentence structure.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:18, قَائِمًا بِالْقِسْطِ may be rendered “upholding justice.”

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:135, قَوَّامِينَ بِالْقِسْطِ means “steadfast upholders of justice.”

In Al-Māʾidah 5:8, شُهَدَاءَ بِالْقِسْطِ may be rendered “witnesses with justice” or “witnesses acting fairly.”

In Hūd 11:85 and Al-Raḥmān 55:9, بِالْقِسْطِ concerning measure and weight means “with justice” or “fairly.”

In Al-Ḥadīd 57:25, لِيَقُومَ النَّاسُ بِالْقِسْطِ means “so that people may uphold justice.”

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:47, الْمَوَازِينَ الْقِسْطَ may be rendered “the balances of justice” or “the just balances.”

أَقْسَطُ

أَقْسَطُ is comparative in Al-Baqarah 2:282 and Al-Aḥzāb 33:5.

“More just” is an appropriate equivalent.

In Al-Baqarah 2:282 it qualifies the procedure for recording and establishing financial obligations.

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:5 it concerns correctly attributing people to their fathers.

Rendering it simply “just” obscures part of its comparative meaning.

أَقْسِطُوا and تُقْسِطُوا

For Form IV verbs, “act justly,” “deal fairly,” and “respect rights” are appropriate.

In Al-Ḥujurāt 49:9, أَقْسِطُوا commands just action in reconciliation.

In Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:8, تُقْسِطُوا إِلَيْهِمْ means “act justly toward them.”

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:3, تُقْسِطُوا concerns justice toward orphans.

مُقْسِطِين

In Al-Māʾidah 5:42, Al-Ḥujurāt 49:9, and Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:8, muqsiṭīn means those who act justly.

“Those who practice fairness” is also suitable; “the just” is shorter and natural, as is the preferred concise Persian equivalent.

It must not be confused with qāsiṭūn.

قَاسِطُون

In Al-Jinn 72:14 and Al-Jinn 72:15, qāsiṭūn denotes the unjust or those who depart from right.

“The just” would be incorrect in these verses.

The root study can clarify the distinction as follows:

قَاسِط: unjust and departing from what is right.

مُقْسِط: just and practicing fairness.

Brief meanings for Forqan word boxes

For قِسْط in positive Quranic usages:

Brief root meaning: “Justice and respect for each person's rights and rightful share.”

For أَقْسَطُ:

Brief root meaning: “Justice and fairness.”

Meaning in this verse: “The greater fairness of the particular conduct or procedure discussed.”

For Form IV verbs:

Brief root meaning: “Acting justly and respecting others' rights.”

For الْمُقْسِطِينَ:

Brief root meaning: “Justice and respect for rights.” Meaning in this verse: “People who act justly and fairly.”

For الْقَاسِطُونَ:

Brief root meaning: “Injustice and departure from what is right.”

Meaning in this verse: “People who depart from right and act unjustly.”

Word boxes should not merge justice and injustice without regard to the word form. These are editorial proposals; installing this study does not authorize rewriting existing authored verse boxes.

Qisṭ meaning a share should likewise be translated according to its construction, without importing modern installment payments into verses about justice.

10. Conclusion

In early Arabic, ق س ط has several related and sometimes opposing usages.

قِسْط concerns justice, fairness, and rights; in another use it denotes a specified share or portion.

In its familiar Quranic sense, أَقْسَطَ means to act justly, and مُقْسِط denotes a just person.

Conversely, قَسَطَ in the injustice branch denotes departure from right, and قَاسِط denotes an unjust person in the Quran.

Early Arabic witnesses also record justice for the basic verb in some usages. The family's entire history cannot be reduced to a simple morphological rule.

Quranic qisṭ is not only about distributing shares.

Āl ʿImrān 3:18 describes The God as upholding qisṭ.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:135 and Al-Māʾidah 5:8 address justice in testimony and judgment, even against oneself and relatives or amid hostility.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:3 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:127 connect it with orphans' rights.

Al-Baqarah 2:282 and Al-Aḥzāb 33:5 use أَقْسَطُ for the greater fairness of two specific procedures.

Al-Anʿām 6:152, Hūd 11:85, and Al-Raḥmān 55:9 connect qisṭ with fair measure and weight.

In Al-Ḥujurāt 49:9, justice is part of the responsibility to reconcile disputes and end aggression.

Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:8 addresses just treatment of those who have not fought the addressees over religion or expelled them from their homes.

In Al-Ḥadīd 57:25, upholding qisṭ is one stated purpose of sending Messengers, the Book, and the balance.

Verses about final judgment connect qisṭ with accurate reckoning and no soul being wronged.

In contrast to these positive usages, Al-Jinn 72:14 and Al-Jinn 72:15 use الْقَاسِطُونَ for the unjust who depart from right.

Together, these usages show that Quranic justice is not merely a theoretical concept or equal shares for everyone.

A person may divide wealth fairly but give false testimony, treat friends justly but disregard enemies' rights, or end a dispute in the name of peace without restoring the injured person's right.

Each situation concerns respecting rights and preventing injustice.

The final definition must distinguish three branches:

ق س ط:

1. قِسْط: justice, fairness, and respect for each person's rights; in distribution, the share or portion belonging to them.

2. إِقْسَاط: practicing justice and respecting rights in judgment, distribution, and conduct toward others; Quranic muqsiṭ denotes a just person.

3. قُسُوط: injustice and departure from right; Quranic qāsiṭ denotes someone who departs from justice.

The branches are semantically related, but must not be reduced to vague definitions such as “distribution” or “social conduct.”

Taken together, the Quranic usages show that qisṭ is actual respect for rights, rather than merely claiming justice, numerical equality in every circumstance, or favoring those close to us.

A person practices qisṭ by preserving truth in testimony, judging without affection or hostility distorting the decision, respecting vulnerable people's rights, avoiding short delivery in trade, and considering both parties' rights in reconciliation.

Power, wealth, or religious standing does not itself make someone muqsiṭ. Justice is recognized through conduct and practical respect for rights.

Muqsiṭ respects what is right; qāsiṭ departs from it. This clearly illustrates the importance of word formation and context in understanding Quranic language.
