Driving and pushing with force; nails and fastenings joining a ship’s parts

1. Introducing the root and its semantic field

Arabic D S R concerns driving, pushing back, and applying force to move something. Dasara commonly means pushing or driving with force. Related words describe spear thrusts, a ship pushing water aside, and implements joining its planks.

An important word is disār, a nail or cord fastening a ship’s planks. Its plural is dusur.

A relationship with driving can be examined: pressure and blows drive a nail into wood to join parts; another shipbuilding method passes cord through holes in planks and binds them together.

This does not prove that every use originated in nailing. Ibn Fāris distinguishes driving from disār as a fastening and does not establish a straightforward derivational connection.

The root occurs once in the Qur’an, as dusur in Al-Qamar 54:13. The verse describes the vessel carrying Noah through its planks and dusur.

The principal Qur’anic question is therefore what dusur means in a ship’s construction.

Early sources also record interpretations involving the bow, crossbeams, and components reinforcing the hull.

These require separate examination. Rescue, flood, faith, and punishment belong to Noah’s wider story rather than the word’s lexical meaning.

2. Qur’anic forms and early Arabic meanings

The Qur’anic form

D S R occurs only once, in one lexical form:

Dusurin: plural of disār, in Al-Qamar 54:13.

The complete form is wa-dusurin. Wa- is a conjunction, and dusurin is a genitive broken-plural noun coordinated with alwāḥin.

Some dictionaries also record dasīr as a related singular; disār remains the familiar singular of dusur.

Other Arabic forms include:

Dasara–yadsuru: drove, pushed back, or propelled with force.

Dasr: driving, repelling, or applying force to propel something.

Disār: a nail, cord, or implement fastening a ship’s parts together.

Dusur: plural of disār; a ship’s nails or fastenings.

Dasīr: another recorded noun for a ship’s fastening implement.

Midsar: a spear or forceful thrusting implement in certain early uses.

Dawsar: a large, powerful camel; elsewhere, a military force advancing strongly against an enemy.

Dawāsir: a plural associated with some uses of dawsar.

Apart from dusur, the other listed verbal and nominal forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

The wider family should inform research while the specific constructional use remains central to the verse.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: sole occurrence in Al-Qamar 54:13

Semantic core: driving and repelling

Ibn Fāris identifies this principle in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى الدَّفْعِ

aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā al-dafʿi

“One principle indicating pushing or repelling.”

He then explains dasara as forceful driving.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise connects dasr with pushing and spear thrusting.

In this branch, an agent applies force to move something forward or away from its position.

The core should not be confined to one direction.

Pushing may be forward, sideways, or away; depending on the object, it may drive something into another material.

Blows drive a nail into wood, while a ship pushes water aside with its bow.

Both involve force, but they are physically different actions.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: driving and disār outside the main analogy

Disār: fastening a ship

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

الدِّسَارُ: خَيْطٌ مِنْ لِيفٍ يُشَدُّ بِهِ أَلْوَاحُهَا، وَقِيلَ: هُوَ مِسْمَارُهَا

al-disāru: khayṭun min līfin yushaddu bihi alwāḥuhā wa-qīla huwa mismāruhā

“Disār is a fiber cord fastening its planks; it is also said to be its nail.”

This preserves two distinct uses.

The first is a cord joining planks.

The second is a nail securing their connection.

Both join and fasten the ship’s parts.

Their materials and methods differ: a nail is hammered into wood, whereas cord can pass through holes and be tied to bind pieces.

Dasara al-safīnata also means fastening a ship’s components.

Al-Ṭabarī explains this through securing a ship with nails or other means.

One material or implement design should therefore not be assumed from the outset.

Lisān al-ʿArab: fiber cords and ship nails

Must disār be an iron nail?

Some commentaries explain dusur as nails fastening the planks.

This is a familiar, lexically supported meaning.

The word alone does not specify their material.

Wooden ships may use metal nails, wooden pegs, fiber lashings, or combined fastenings.

Dusur cannot establish iron, a particular metal or nail shape, or a specific construction method for Noah’s vessel.

Recorded fiber-cord uses likewise do not prove the vessel had no nails.

These concern possible material referents. The joining and reinforcing function is clearer from the lexical evidence.

3. Tangible uses and early Arabic poetic witnesses

Thrusting with a spear

Dasarahu bi-al-rumḥi describes spearing or thrusting a spear at someone.

Force and directed movement matter here.

The agent drives the spear forward, potentially penetrating its target.

Lisān al-ʿArab explains this construction through thrusting and pushing.

Dasr itself is not generally the name of a spear; the spear is the instrument.

Nor does the root invariably mean wounding or killing. Those outcomes depend on the object and circumstances.

An early poetic witness

Maqāyīs al-Lughah transmits a verse about a large, powerful being:

عَنْ ذِي قَدَامِيسَ لُهَامٍ لَوْ دَسَرْ
بِرُكْنِهِ أَرْكَانَ دَمْخٍ لَانْقَعَرْ

ʿan dhī qadāmīsa luhāmin law dasar / bi-ruknihi arkāna damkhin la-inqaʿar

“Of that massive, mighty creature: were it to push against Damkh’s foundations with its flank, it would uproot them.”

Dasara concerns applying great force to push and dislodge.

Ibn Fāris cites it to explain the root’s principle.

The poet and composition date are not securely identified in the transmission examined; it should not be called certainly pre-Islamic without independent evidence.

It witnesses forceful driving within the Arabic lexical tradition.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: the forceful-driving witness of uncertain attribution

A ship pushing water aside with its bow

Dictionaries record:

دَسَرَتِ السَّفِينَةُ الْمَاءَ بِصَدْرِهَا

dasarati al-safīnatu al-māʾa bi-ṣadrihā

“The ship pushed the water aside with its bow.”

The ship itself is the verb’s subject.

In motion, it displaces water before it and opens a passage.

This is clearly related to pushing or driving.

It does not make dusur in Al-Qamar 54:13 necessarily name that action: there it is a plural noun alongside planks.

The same family can describe a ship displacing water and name its fastening implements.

Their root is shared, but their referents and grammatical constructions differ.

A witness from Bishr ibn Abī Khāzim

The pre-Islamic poet Bishr ibn Abī Khāzim describes a ship:

مُعَبَّدَةِ السَّقَائِفِ ذَاتِ دُسْرٍ
مُضَبَّرَةٍ جَوَانِبُهَا رَدَاحِ

muʿabbadati al-saqāʾifi dhāti dusrin / muḍabbaratin jawānibuhā radāḥi

“With coated planking and firm fastenings, its broad sides compactly joined.”

Dusr occurs with saqāʾif here.

Saqāʾif describes the hull’s planks or broad components.

Lexical explanations take muʿabbadah as coated or caulked, with a substance such as pitch.

The verse therefore concerns a physical ship’s construction.

Planks and dusur appear together, particularly relevant because Al-Qamar 54:13 also pairs them.

This confirms the word’s early use in describing ship construction.

It does not identify every fastening as a nail or fiber cord. Fastenings fit the context and definitions, while precise material needs more evidence.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Bishr ibn Abī Khāzim’s ship witness

A witness of uncertain attribution

A lexical collection attributed to exchanges between Nāfiʿ ibn al-Azraq and Ibn ʿAbbās transmits:

سَفِينَةُ نُوتِيٍّ قَدْ أُحْكِمَ صُنْعُهَا
مُثْخَنَةُ الْأَلْوَاحِ مَنْسُوجَةُ الدُّسُرْ

safīnatu nūtiyyin qad uḥkima ṣunʿuhā / muthkhanatu al-alwāḥi mansūjatu al-dusur

“A sailor’s ship, firmly built, with thick planks and interwoven fastenings.”

Nūtiyy names a sailor or someone operating a vessel.

Mansūjatu al-dusur emphasizes interconnected construction.

Transmitted wording varies; the poet and composition date remain uncertain.

It should not be treated as securely pre-Islamic.

It helps explain the lexical tradition, while Bishr’s witness has a clearer historical attribution.

Al-Durr al-Manthūr: the muthkhanat al-alwāḥ transmission and Nāfiʿ collection witness

Dawsar and advancing force

Dictionaries use dawsar for a large, strong camel and a military force.

Ibn Fāris explains the latter through pushing enemies back.

The driving branch is therefore not restricted to ships.

That use should not be imposed on Al-Qamar 54:13.

The verse concerns a vessel with planks and dusur, not troops or a powerful animal.

Non-Qur’anic forms illuminate the family without independently determining this verse’s sense.

4. Qur’anic usage: Al-Qamar 54:13

Al-Qamar 54:13

وَحَمَلْنَاهُ عَلَىٰ ذَاتِ أَلْوَاحٍ وَدُسُرٍ

wa-ḥamalnāhu ʿalā dhāti alwāḥin wa-dusurin

“We carried him upon a vessel of planks and nails.”

This is the root’s sole Qur’anic occurrence.

Ḥamalnāhu describes carrying someone identified as Noah in the preceding context.

ʿAlā dhāti alwāḥin wa-dusurin introduces the means carrying him.

Dhāt describes something possessing planks and dusur.

Safīnah is not explicitly named here, but the story and parallel verses identify Noah’s ship.

Why components rather than the word safīnah?

Elsewhere the Qur’an names Noah’s vessel fulk or safīnah.

Hūd 11:37 commands building the fulk; Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:15 mentions safīnah.

Al-Qamar 54:13 instead gives two constructional features: planks and dusur.

This introduces the vessel through its constituent or reinforcing parts.

Alwāḥ are planks; dusur in the familiar interpretation are nails or fastenings joining them.

The image presents Noah’s means of rescue as a structure of connected parts.

It is not a complete inventory of its materials and tools.

Sail ropes, wood type, dimensions, number of decks, steering gear, and navigation are not specified.

Alwāḥ and dusur

Alwāḥ belongs to L W Ḥ and means planks here.

Dusur belongs to D S R and conventionally names their fastenings.

Their functions differ.

Planks form principal parts of the hull or structure.

Dusur conventionally secure those parts together.

Translating both as planks would erase that distinction.

Rendering dusur merely as strength may also obscure the physical fastenings.

It refers to a material component, though precise shape and substance are not settled by the verse.

Nails or cords?

Early sources offer two close explanations.

One is nails.

The other is cords or lashings binding the planks.

Nails are more familiar in early explanations of the verse and accepted by many lexicographers.

Reputable dictionaries nevertheless also record fiber cords.

Both secure the planks’ connection.

Ship fastenings therefore offer a broader explanation of the attested field.

For one referent in a base translation, nails are defensible through lexical support and the familiar Qur’anic interpretation.

Choosing nails should not categorically deny cord uses in early Arabic.

Could dusur mean the bow?

Some early explanations identify the ship’s bow or breast.

They relate it to dasara as driving and pushing aside.

The forward part displaces water and can be described through that action.

This has a clear connection with an attested use.

Yet dusur in Al-Qamar 54:13 is plural and paired with planks.

Planks and nails or fastenings coherently describe structural components.

The bow interpretation needs to account for the plural and its coordination here.

This does not conclusively refute it, but preference would require further linguistic and structural evidence.

Nails or connecting fastenings are therefore preferred on the available evidence.

Crossbeams and hull components

Early commentaries also give other explanations.

Some identify crossbeams.

Others name ribs or components reinforcing the hull.

These too concern construction.

They should not all become indistinguishable synonyms of nail.

A crossbeam, hull rib, and nail have different technical functions, though each may reinforce a structure.

The disagreement shows several constructional referents in the lexical and exegetical tradition.

The base translation need not include every possibility in a long phrase.

A preferred sense may be selected while established alternatives remain in the root explanation.

Al-Ṭabarī: nails, bow, crossbeams, and ship ribs

Does the word itself mean ship?

Some explanations associate dusur with the ship itself or a part because it drives water aside.

Dhāti alwāḥin wa-dusurin in Al-Qamar 54:13 describes a vessel possessing these components.

Translating dusur as the entire ship would blur the distinction between object and parts.

Dhāt also marks something possessing the named features or components.

Constructional components fit better here, avoiding a redundant identification of the whole vessel with its parts.

5. The verse within Noah’s story

Construction and flowing water

Al-Qamar 54:11 describes heaven’s gates opened with pouring water.

Al-Qamar 54:12 brings together waters rising from earth and descending from heaven.

Al-Qamar 54:13 then places Noah on a vessel of planks and dusur.

Al-Qamar 54:14 describes the vessel moving.

The sequence distinguishes structural components in Al-Qamar 54:13 from movement in Al-Qamar 54:14.

Dusur does not itself mean moving on water; that comes from tajrī in Al-Qamar 54:14.

It should not simply become floating, sailing, or cutting through waves.

Construction and divine command

Hūd 11:37 commands Noah to build the ship under divine oversight and revelation.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:27 also commands building the fulk.

These connect with Al-Qamar 54:13 by identifying Noah’s vessel as something constructed.

They do not provide exact carpentry, nailing, measurement, or design instructions.

Alwāḥ wa-dusur names structural parts without specifying technical details or quantities.

The verse informs the word’s meaning without becoming a complete engineering plan.

Does dusur mean a means of rescue?

Noah’s vessel rescues him and his companions in the story.

That follows from the wider narrative of being carried and delivered.

Dusur itself does not mean rescue.

A nail or cord may strengthen a rescue vessel, but its lexical meaning is not restricted to rescue.

Such fastenings may also serve trading, passenger, or other vessels.

Noah’s vessel’s particular narrative role should not become the root’s fixed meaning.

Does the verse identify a specific technology?

Planks and nails are sometimes taken to disclose a particular shipbuilding technology in Noah’s time.

The verse states only a vessel possessing planks and dusur.

It specifies neither nail material nor whether wooden and fiber fastenings could be combined.

It does not date shipbuilding’s origin, humanity’s first nails, or tools used before Noah.

Those require independent historical and archaeological research.

A construction of joined components fits the image; the root alone should not yield more.

6. D S R and neighboring words

D S R and D F ʿ: driving and repelling

D F ʿ is the verbal branch’s closest semantic neighbor.

Both can concern pushing and driving away.

Attested dasr also concerns forceful propulsion or penetration.

Pressure and blows drive a nail into wood; a vessel pushes water aside.

Qur’anic dafʿ has broader uses, including defense against aggression and averting harm.

This is a tendency, not an absolute distinction between forceful dasr and invariably gentle dafʿ.

Nor do all Qur’anic dafʿ uses concern nails, ships, or penetration.

D S R and D Ḥ R: driving and expulsion

Daḥr concerns driving something away from a position or situation.

The verbal dasr branch can likewise concern driving.

Dasr often emphasizes force propelling something, while daḥr more often emphasizes being driven away.

Qur’anic daḥr concerns expelling Iblīs or driving devils away.

Dusur in Al-Qamar 54:13 instead concerns ship components.

Close verbal meanings cannot make it mean expelled or banished beings here.

Study of D Ḥ R

D S R and SH D D: driving and fastening

SH D D concerns strength, intensity, and making firm.

Definitions of disār say tushaddu bihi alwāḥu al-safīnati: the ship’s planks are fastened with it.

Shadd and dasr are not identical.

Shadd describes fastening.

Disār names an implement used to secure ship components.

Dusur can be explained as securing fastenings here, without reducing the whole root to firmness.

Study of SH D D

D S R and R B Ṭ: joining and tying

Rabṭ concerns tying and connecting.

Ship cords and lashings can bind planks together.

Disār here names a particular fastening, whereas rabṭ covers many kinds of binding and connection.

Some results overlap, but morphology and semantic fields differ.

D S R and KH R Z: fastenings and stitching

Arabic kharz concerns piercing and stitching pieces with thread or cord.

Some disār explanations describe cords connecting ship planks.

That method can relate to kharz.

Kharz names the stitching action; disār may name the cord or fastening itself.

Not every disār must be sewn cord, since nails are also attested.

D S R and S R D: connected components and orderly construction

Sabaʾ 34:11 uses S R D in armor-making.

It concerns orderly joining, including armor links.

Joining armor components and securing ship planks have a comparable result.

Sard and dasr remain different roots.

Dusur concerns ship components in Al-Qamar 54:13.

Sard concerns armor construction in Sabaʾ 34:11.

Technical details should not be transferred between them to erase the distinction.

D S R and L W Ḥ: fastenings and planks

Al-Qamar 54:13 pairs alwāḥ and dusur.

Alwāḥ is the plural of lawḥ and means planks here.

Dusur preferably means nails or fastening implements.

Their constructional roles complement one another.

One names hull components; the other names implements or parts connecting and securing the structure.

Their pairing alone does not specify every technical detail.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Dusurin is the sole Qur’anic form.

Several Persian equivalents merit consideration.

Nails is the most familiar and preferred equivalent here.

Fastenings is broader, potentially covering nails and other joining implements.

Connecting cords makes the fiber branch clearer.

Reinforcing ship components is a broader explanation that can include crossbeams or hull ribs, but is less direct as translation.

Al-Qamar 54:13

The current Persian Forqan translation, rendered in English, reads:

“We carried him upon a vessel of planks and nails.”

This fits the familiar, lexically supported interpretation.

There is therefore no need to change it.

The article should nevertheless explain that early dusur is not restricted to iron nails and also names connecting cords.

Fastenings could be chosen more broadly in a future revision, but only with deliberate attention to direct translation versus lexical explanation.

Could dusur be translated as bonds?

A bond may convey the result of joining but is too abstract here.

The verse concerns material structural components.

Nails or fastenings present them more concretely.

Power, rescue, faith, and divine command are not the word’s own meanings.

Outside the Qur’an, dasara may be translated drove, pushed back, propelled forcefully, or thrust a spear, according to context.

Construction remains the preferred sense in Al-Qamar 54:13.

8. Conclusion

D S R concerns driving and applying force to propel something.

Ibn Fāris identifies the verbal core as pushing, illustrated by forceful driving and spear thrusting.

Early disār also names an implement connecting and fastening ship planks, explained as either a nail or fiber cord.

Driving a nail into wood offers a possible relationship with pushing, without proving every form’s historical origin.

The Qur’an uses the family once, as dusur in Al-Qamar 54:13.

Noah is carried on a vessel possessing planks and dusur.

Nails or connecting fastenings are the familiar, preferred interpretation.

Early explanations also include bow, crossbeams, and reinforcing hull components. These belong to the interpretation’s history, while the plural alongside planks and lexical disār evidence favor fastenings.

The word does not establish exact nail or cord material, vessel size, or a complete construction method.

Movement and rescue belong to the wider story rather than dusur’s lexical meaning.

Proposed final root definition: The verbal D S R branch concerns forcefully driving or propelling. In ship construction, disār and its plural dusur name nails or fastenings connecting and securing components. The preferred sense in Al-Qamar 54:13 is nails or implements joining ship planks, without the word determining their precise material or manufacture. Brief root meaning: Driving or propelling with force; dusur are a ship’s nails or connecting fastenings.
