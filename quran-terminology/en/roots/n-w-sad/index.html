Withdrawing, retreating, escaping, and seeking deliverance: from physical movement to “wa-lāta ḥīna manāṣ” in the Qur’an

In early Arabic, N W Ṣ concerns movement and displacement, particularly moving away from something, retreating, withdrawing from an encounter, or trying to get out of a difficult situation. Uses such as fleeing an opponent, seeking an escape route, and finding refuge belong to this semantic field.

The root does not simply mean “fleeing.” Arabic dictionaries also record movement, coming and going, moving backward, drawing aside, stirring, and even struggling to get free.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as مَنَاصٍ in Ṣād 38:3:

وَلَاتَ حِينَ مَنَاصٍ

wa-lāta ḥīna manāṣin

Meaning: “And it was no longer a time for escape.”

This use concerns the destruction of earlier generations: they cried out when the consequences of their behavior arrived, but no opportunity to escape remained.

Three matters should be distinguished: movement and withdrawal in Arabic, the escape and refuge-seeking expressed by manāṣ, and escape becoming impossible at a particular time, which is understood from the complete verse construction.

1. The semantic core in early Arabic dictionaries

One lexical explanation defines N W Ṣ as follows:

النُّونُ وَالْوَاوُ وَالصَّادُ أَصْلٌ صَحِيحٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى تَرَدُّدٍ وَمَجِيءٍ وَذَهَابٍ

al-nūnu wa-al-wāwu wa-al-ṣādu aṣlun ṣaḥīḥun yadullu ʿalā taraddudin wa-majīʾin wa-dhahābin

Meaning: “Nūn, wāw, and ṣād form an established root denoting coming and going and movement to and fro.”

This definition matters because it does not initially restrict the root to fleeing.

Movement back and forth may involve ordinary displacement, escape from danger, withdrawing from an opponent, or trying to get free of an obstacle. Its direction and the subject’s situation therefore matter in each use.

The expression نَاصَ عَنْ قِرْنِهِ is also recorded: someone withdrew or fled from an opponent.

Manāṣ is recorded both as a verbal noun and as a place of refuge.

The relationship between general movement and the particular use of fleeing is clear: someone facing danger tries to move, retreat, or change direction to get away from it.

This relationship does not mean that every movement or retreat in Arabic necessarily involves fleeing danger. The situation and construction determine that feature.

2. Forms and derivatives in Arabic

The family’s different forms concern movement, retreat, escape, and efforts to get free, although they do not all mean exactly the same thing.

نَاصَ يَنُوصُ: moved, drew aside, retreated, or moved away from something. نَاصَ عَنْ قِرْنِهِ concerns withdrawing or fleeing from an opponent.

نَوْص: a verbal noun of nāṣa, expressing movement, retreat, and escape in lexical uses.

مَنَاص: recorded both as a verbal noun denoting escape and retreat and as a place of escape or refuge. This is the word occurring in the Qur’an.

مَنِيص: another recorded verbal noun for movement, departure, or escape.

نَائِص: describes a creature that moves, draws back, or raises its head and stirs from its position.

اسْتَنَاصَ: in animal movement, raising the head, moving backward, or starting to move. Some lexical explanations also give “seeking an escape.”

نَاوَصَ and مُنَاوَصَة: struggling, grappling, resisting, or moving against another force to get free.

اِنْتَاصَ: in a recorded use concerning the sun, setting.

نَوِيص: in مَا بِهِ نَوِيصٌ, strength or ability to move; the expression means that someone does not even have the strength to stir or react.

These uses differ. نَاصَ عَنْهُ emphasizes moving away from someone or something, whereas نَاصَ إِلَيْهِ is also recorded for rising or moving toward a destination.

Nāṣa therefore cannot be translated as “fled” in every construction. The preposition and direction of movement help determine the sentence’s meaning.

This distinction between ʿanhu and ilayhi is recorded in lexical usage. It shows that the family’s general field of movement is broader than its particular use for escape.

Rare uses and caution about their origins

Some dictionaries also record al-nawṣ as generosity. Its connection to movement and escape is not transparent, and the evidence examined is insufficient to establish its historical origin.

Al-nawṣah is also recorded for a single washing. One lexical analysis treats it as originally mawṣah, with mīm changing to nūn. On that analysis, it should not uncritically be treated as an independently derived member of N W Ṣ.

These instances show that several words appearing in one dictionary entry do not necessarily establish a single historical family.

3. Early Arabic witnesses: from retreat to struggling for release

Moving backward and forward in a poem attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays

An important witness is attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays:

أَمِنْ ذِكْرِ لَيْلَى إِذْ نَأَتْكَ تَنُوصُ
وَتَقْصُرُ عَنْهَا خُطْوَةً وَتَبُوصُ

a-min dhikri laylā idh naʾatka tanūṣu / wa-taqṣuru ʿanhā khuṭwatan wa-tabūṣu

The sense: “Is it from remembering Laylā, when she has gone far from you, that you retreat, shorten your step in following her, and move forward again?”

Two verbs matter here: tanūṣu and tabūṣu.

A lexical explanation of this contrast states:

النَّوْصُ: التَّأَخُّرُ فِي كَلَامِ الْعَرَبِ، وَالْبَوْصُ: التَّقَدُّمُ

al-nawṣu: al-taʾakhkhuru fī kalāmi al-ʿarabi wa-al-bawṣu: al-taqaddumu

Meaning: “Nawṣ in Arabic is moving backward, and bawṣ is moving forward.”

The contrast between backward and forward movement is clear. The poet describes someone caught between drawing away and moving ahead.

This witness matters because nawṣ did not necessarily mean fleeing danger or an enemy in early Arabic. It could also express moving backward and drawing away.

The line has variant transmissions. One names Laylā, another Salmā. Tanūṣu remains in both, and the different name does not change the meaning for which the verse is cited.

Early lexical transmissions attribute the line to Imruʾ al-Qays, a pre-Islamic poet. That transmission alone, however, does not resolve every textual difference in the collected poetry.

An animal raising its head and moving

Lexical uses also employ this root for a horse’s movement when reined in or urged to run.

A line by Ḥārithah ibn Badr al-Ghudānī says:

غَمْرُ الْجِرَاءِ إِذَا قَصَرْتُ عِنَانَهُ
بِيَدِي اسْتَنَاصَ وَرَامَ جَرْيَ الْمِسْحَلِ

ghamru al-jirāʾi idhā qaṣartu ʿinānahu / bi-yadī istanāṣa wa-rāma jarya al-misḥali

The sense: “That horse, abundant in running—when I shorten its rein with my hand, it raises its head and seeks to run like a wild ass.”

Istanāṣa here concerns the animal’s reaction to a tightened rein.

It reacts to restricted movement: raising its head, drawing back, or trying to move again.

This fits the definition involving movement to and fro, but its details should not be generalized to every derivative.

Ḥārithah ibn Badr was a poet of the Umayyad period. The line is therefore evidence from early Arabic after Islam and should not be presented as pre-Islamic poetry.

A gazelle struggling with a trap: munāwaṣah

A clear physical use of the family appears in an Arabic proverb:

نَاوَصَ الْجَرَّةَ ثُمَّ سَالَمَهَا

nāwaṣa al-jarrata thumma sālamahā

Meaning: “It struggled with the jarrah, then became quiet before it.”

Jarrah here is not a jar; it is a device used in trapping gazelles.

The lexical explanation describes a trapped gazelle initially struggling to escape, moving to and fro and grappling with the trapping device. When it can no longer get free, its movement subsides.

Munāwaṣah here is more than simple movement: it involves resistance and efforts to escape confinement.

The proverb describes someone who initially opposes and resists others but eventually must accommodate them. Another explanation applies it to someone caught in a situation who struggles for a while and then settles down.

This use also helps explain manāṣ by showing that efforts to get free belong to the same semantic field.

The root itself does not necessarily mean “surrendering after defeat.” The gazelle’s becoming quiet is the outcome narrated by the proverb, not the inherent meaning of nāwaṣa.

The proverb is recorded in lexical and literary material, but the earliest date of its use is not established by the evidence examined.

Nawṣ as escape

A passage attributed to ʿAdī ibn Zayd says:

يَا نَفْسُ أَبْقِي وَاتَّقِي شَتْمَ ذَوِي الْـ
أَعْرَاضِ فِي غَيْرِ نُوصِ

yā nafsu abqī wa-ittaqī shatma dhawī al-aʿrāḍi fī ghayri nūṣi

“O soul, spare yourself and beware of reviling people of honor, where there is no escape.”

Nūṣ here concerns escape and withdrawal. The sense is an admonition to avoid a situation from whose consequences there is no escape.

ʿAdī ibn Zayd is known as a pre-Islamic poet, and lexical transmissions attribute this passage to him. Since the quotation is brief and the complete poem’s setting is not provided here, details of his situation should not be guessed from it.

Together, these witnesses establish movement, retreat, and escape as uses of the root. The value and outcome of that movement differ between examples.

4. Qur’anic manāṣ in Ṣād

Ṣād 38:3

كَمْ أَهْلَكْنَا مِنْ قَبْلِهِمْ مِنْ قَرْنٍ فَنَادَوْا وَلَاتَ حِينَ مَنَاصٍ

kam ahlaknā min qablihim min qarnin fa-nādaw wa-lāta ḥīna manāṣin

“How many generations We destroyed before them! They cried out, when it was no longer a time for escape.”

This is the only Qur’anic occurrence of N W Ṣ. The translation above is also consistent with Forqan’s installed text.

The form and meaning of manāṣ

Manāṣ is a noun from N W Ṣ on the pattern mafʿal.

This construction can function as a mīm-prefixed verbal noun or a place noun in Arabic. Manāṣ can therefore denote the act of escape and retreat or a place of escape and refuge.

In the verse, manāṣin is indefinite and genitive, forming a construct phrase with ḥīna.

Two closely related understandings can be considered:

  • “A time for escape”: a time when withdrawing and finding safety are still possible.
  • “A time to find an escape route or refuge”: a time when someone can find shelter by moving away from danger.

Both relate to recorded uses of manāṣ. In a natural translation of the verse, however, “escape” is sufficient and close to the intended sense.

Wa-lāta: negating the opportunity, not defining the root

The construction wa-lāta ḥīna manāṣ is decisive.

In a familiar grammatical analysis, lāta consists of negative lā with an added tāʾ. Here it negates the availability of the appropriate time.

Early grammarians discussed the construction and its case endings. In the widely used reading, ḥīna is accusative, although a nominative form is also reported in grammatical discussions and transmitted readings.

The central point is that lāta does not define escape as inherently impossible. It denies that the time in question is an appropriate time for escape.

Manāṣ means escape or deliverance; “there is no longer an opportunity to escape” is the meaning of the complete construction wa-lāta ḥīna manāṣ.

This distinction matters in translation. Defining manāṣ alone as “a lost opportunity” transfers part of lāta ḥīna into the root itself.

They cried out—but what did they say?

The verse says:

فَنَادَوْا

fa-nādaw

Meaning: “Then they cried out.”

The phrase itself does not state what they called for.

Early interpretations offer several explanations: appealing for help and rescue when punishment arrives, asking to return and repent, or crying out to flee.

These explanations relate manāṣ to escape and retreat.

The explicit wording and the interpretation must nevertheless remain distinct. This sentence says only that they cried out at a time when escape was no longer available.

A direct translation should therefore not silently replace fa-nādaw with “they cried out in repentance” or “they asked The God for forgiveness.”

These may be contextual interpretations, but nādaw itself does not specify such content.

5. The verse’s connection with the surah

The opening of Ṣād says:

صٓ ۚ وَالْقُرْآنِ ذِي الذِّكْرِ

ṣād wa-al-qurʾāni dhī al-dhikri

“Ṣād. By the Qur’an, possessed of reminder.” (Ṣād 38:1)

Then:

بَلِ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا فِي عِزَّةٍ وَشِقَاقٍ

bali alladhīna kafarū fī ʿizzatin wa-shiqāqin

“Rather, those who disbelieve are in haughtiness and opposition.” (Ṣād 38:2)

The verse under discussion then recalls earlier generations who were destroyed and cried out when the consequences arrived, but it was no longer a time for escape.

This sequence matters.

The second verse presents haughtiness and opposition to the message. The third recalls the fate of generations whose earlier opportunity had passed.

Wa-lāta ḥīna manāṣ is therefore more than an isolated description of escape. It warns people still being addressed by the message that continued opposition may bring a fate like that of their predecessors.

The root N W Ṣ itself, however, does not mean disbelief, arrogance, or enmity. These belong to the surrounding verses and should not be added to its lexical definition.

When movement is possible but deliverance is not

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:12, Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:13, Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:14, and Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:15 describe a related situation.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:12 says:

فَلَمَّا أَحَسُّوا بَأْسَنَا إِذَا هُمْ مِنْهَا يَرْكُضُونَ

fa-lammā aḥassū baʾsanā idhā hum minhā yarkuḍūna

“When they sensed Our severity, suddenly they were running away from it.”

The next verse, Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:13, continues:

لَا تَرْكُضُوا وَارْجِعُوا إِلَىٰ مَا أُتْرِفْتُمْ فِيهِ وَمَسَاكِنِكُمْ لَعَلَّكُمْ تُسْأَلُونَ

lā tarkuḍū wa-irjiʿū ilā mā utriftum fīhi wa-masākinikum laʿallakum tusʾalūna

“Do not run away; return to the luxury you enjoyed and your dwellings, so that you may be questioned.”

Flight has actually begun in this account, but it does not result in deliverance.

This helps clarify an important distinction: the ability to move is not the same as the possibility of finding safety.

A person may still run, change direction, or move away from danger, yet no longer be able to escape an outcome that has arrived.

In Ṣād, too, manāṣ occurs when the possibility of escape has been lost. The emphasis is on deliverance becoming impossible, not on every physical movement ceasing.

A way of escape and a way to reform

In Ghāfir 40:84 and Ghāfir 40:85, people profess faith when they see divine severity, but their faith does not benefit them in that situation.

These verses help explain why some interpretations of Ṣād understand the earlier peoples’ cry as a request to repent or return.

Repentance should not, however, become the lexical meaning of manāṣ. Escaping a consequence differs from correcting a past wrong, even if both requests may sometimes come after the opportunity has passed.

Comparing the verses shows that the timing of a human response matters for its outcome. That conclusion comes from the network of verses, not from adding “belated repentance” to N W Ṣ.

6. Comparison with related roots

N W Ṣ (nawṣ) and Ḥ Y Ṣ (ḥayṣ): retreat and escape

Ḥāṣa yaḥīṣu concerns drawing aside, turning away, and escaping in Arabic.

Its derivative maḥīṣ occurs in the Qur’an.

Qāf 50:36 says:

وَكَمْ أَهْلَكْنَا قَبْلَهُمْ مِنْ قَرْنٍ هُمْ أَشَدُّ مِنْهُمْ بَطْشًا فَنَقَّبُوا فِي الْبِلَادِ هَلْ مِنْ مَحِيصٍ

wa-kam ahlaknā qablahum min qarnin hum ashaddu minhum baṭshan fa-naqqabū fī al-bilādi hal min maḥīṣin

“How many generations We destroyed before them who were stronger in might than they! They ranged through the lands—is there any escape?”

This verse is very close to Ṣād 38:3 in construction and subject.

Both recall the destruction of earlier generations and end by raising the question of escape.

Maḥīṣ, however, belongs to Ḥ Y Ṣ, not N W Ṣ.

The roots are lexically close: both can concern withdrawing and escaping. Nāṣa and ḥāṣa also appear together in explanations of this semantic field.

The definition of N W Ṣ emphasizes coming and going and movement, whereas familiar uses of Ḥ Y Ṣ more often concern drawing aside or deviating from a course or encounter.

This is not an absolute boundary, since the families overlap in the meaning of escape.

The more important point is that the Qur’an uses two different roots to express related ideas. The lack of an escape in these verses is not produced by a single root alone; different Arabic constructions convey it.

Study of Ḥ Y Ṣ

N W Ṣ (nawṣ) and B W Ṣ (bawṣ): moving backward and forward

In the lexical explanation cited earlier, bawṣ denotes moving forward and stands opposite nawṣ as moving backward.

The verse attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays illustrates the contrast.

The roots are close in sound but are distinct. The poem expresses two directions of movement through different words.

Forward movement should therefore not replace retreat in explaining nawṣ without contextual evidence, although some derivatives have broader uses within the general field of movement.

N W Ṣ (nawṣ) and N W Ḍ (nawḍ)

Lexical material relates nāḍa and nāṣa to movement, departure, or moving away in some uses.

Their final letters differ: ṣād in one and ḍād in the other.

Proximity in meaning or interchangeability in some expressions does not make all their derivatives identical.

Evidence for N W Ḍ is therefore useful for understanding N W Ṣ only where their relationship in usage is clearly recorded.

N W Ṣ (nawṣ) and N W SH (nawsh): escape and attainment

N W SH concerns reaching and taking something, whereas the Qur’anic use of N W Ṣ concerns escaping and withdrawing from danger.

The roots are close in sound, but the direction of the relationship differs in their principal Qur’anic uses.

In Sabaʾ 34:52, al-tanāwush concerns an attempt to attain something:

وَأَنَّىٰ لَهُمُ التَّنَاوُشُ مِنْ مَكَانٍ بَعِيدٍ

wa-annā lahumu al-tanāwushu min makānin baʿīdin

“And how could they attain it from a distant place?”

In Ṣād 38:3, however, manāṣ concerns escaping or finding deliverance from a situation when that opportunity is no longer available.

Both verses concern an action becoming impossible in a particular situation. That contextual similarity should not merge the roots.

Tanāwush is directed toward something a person wants to reach; manāṣ in its Qur’anic use concerns movement away from a consequence from which a person seeks deliverance.

This distinction matters in determining each root’s meaning.

7. Translation proposal for Forqan

The principal and appropriate equivalent for manāṣ in Ṣād 38:3 is “escape.”

Forqan’s installed Persian translation is equivalent to:

“How many generations We destroyed before them! They cried out, when it was no longer a time for escape.”

This is defensible lexically and contextually and does not need to change.

A word explanation may add that manāṣ concerns escape, retreat, or finding a way to get free.

The translation itself should not turn it into “repentance,” “forgiveness,” “a cry for help,” or “a lost opportunity.” These belong either to the complete sentence or to contextual interpretation.

“Refuge” is another recorded meaning, but in ḥīna manāṣ, “a time for escape” is more natural than “a time of refuge.”

“Escape” is therefore the best choice for this Qur’anic use.

8. Conclusion

In early Arabic, N W Ṣ concerns movement, coming and going, retreat, and withdrawal. The lexical definition cited earlier centers on movement to and fro, while witnesses show this movement extending in some situations to fleeing an opponent and seeking deliverance.

The poem attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays contrasts tanūṣu with forward movement. Animal movement and a gazelle’s struggle with a trap also show that the family was not limited to people fleeing enemies.

Only manāṣ in Ṣād 38:3 occurs in the Qur’an. It denotes escape or a way of deliverance. Escape’s impossibility is not inherent in the root; it comes from wa-lāta ḥīna manāṣ.

The surah warns that earlier generations cried out when their outcome arrived, but no opportunity to escape remained. Comparison with Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:12, Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:13, Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:14, Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:15, and Qāf 50:36 clarifies this meaning.

Final definition for Forqan:

N W Ṣ concerns movement and displacement, particularly retreating, drawing aside, and moving away from something. In danger or confrontation, this extends to escape and seeking deliverance. Manāṣ in Ṣād 38:3 means “escape”; wa-lāta ḥīna manāṣ states that it was no longer a time for escape.
