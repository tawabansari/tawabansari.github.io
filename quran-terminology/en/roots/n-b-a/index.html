The Qur’anic Root N B ʾ

News, informing, and the title “Nabī”

The root ن ب أ in the Qur’an operates in the field of news and informing. Nabaʾ is news that reaches a recipient; anbaʾa and nabbaʾa mean to inform someone of something. In the morphological classification of Qur’anic vocabulary, nubuwwah and nabī also belong to this family.

The historical derivation of nabī, however, requires greater care than that of the other members of this family. Within the Arabic lexical tradition, some derive it from nabaʾ and anbaʾa, meaning news and informing, and cite the hamzated form nabīʾ as evidence of this connection. Another analysis connects it with a family relating to height and elevation. Both analyses are old; neither should be selected merely because it better suits a theological interpretation.

Hebrew and Aramaic evidence likewise shows that the cognate title nabī is very old and was established in other Semitic languages. This evidence strengthens the title’s connection with prophethood and prophetic speech, but does not by itself determine the precise direction of the word’s historical development.

Qur’anic forms

The family ن ب أ occurs 160 times in the Qur’an: Form II نَبَّأَ (nabbaʾa) 46 times, Form IV أَنْبَأَ (anbaʾa) four times, Form X once, نَبَأ (nabaʾ) and its plural 29 times, نُبُوَّة (nubuwwah) five times, and نَبِيّ (nabī) and its plurals 75 times.

This classification is consistent with a connection between nabī and N B ʾ, but a morphological count does not settle the historical question of derivation. It is therefore better to examine the clear meaning of nabaʾ and its verbs first, then assess nabī.

Nabaʾ: news, not necessarily prediction or certain information

The Qur’an uses nabaʾ for various kinds of news.

Concerning the past, it says:

وَاتْلُ عَلَيْهِمْ نَبَأَ ابْنَيْ آدَمَ بِالْحَقِّ

Wa-tlu ʿalayhim nabaʾa ibnay Ādama bi-l-ḥaqq.

“Recite to them, in truth, the report of Adam’s two sons.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:27

And:

وَاتْلُ عَلَيْهِمْ نَبَأَ نُوحٍ

Wa-tlu ʿalayhim nabaʾa Nūḥ.

“Recite to them the report of Noah.”

Yūnus 10:71

Concerning an existing situation, the hoopoe says:

وَجِئْتُكَ مِنْ سَبَإٍ بِنَبَإٍ يَقِينٍ

Wa-jiʾtuka min Sabaʾin bi-nabaʾin yaqīn.

“I have brought you certain news from Sheba.”

An-Naml 27:22

The Qur’an also speaks of:

النَّبَإِ الْعَظِيمِ

An-nabaʾi l-ʿaẓīm.

“The great news.”

And:

نَبَأٌ عَظِيمٌ

Nabaʾun ʿaẓīm.

“Great news.”

These, too, are expressions used in the Qur’an.

These combinations show that nabaʾ can be important news, but greatness and certainty are not obligatory components of the word’s own meaning. If certainty were inherent in nabaʾ, the qualification in nabaʾ yaqīn would lose much of its function.

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:6 makes this clearer:

إِنْ جَاءَكُمْ فَاسِقٌ بِنَبَإٍ فَتَبَيَّنُوا

In jāʾakum fāsiqun bi-nabaʾin fa-tabayyanū.

“If a transgressor brings you a report, investigate.”

Calling something nabaʾ therefore does not guarantee its truth. A report may require investigation and clarification.

Nor does nabaʾ inherently mean news to which a person has no ordinary means of access. The hoopoe’s news from Sheba is a report obtained through observation and travel. When the Qur’an speaks of:

مِنْ أَنْبَاءِ الْغَيْبِ نُوحِيهِ إِلَيْكَ

Min anbāʾi l-ghaybi nūḥīhi ilayk.

“From the reports of the unseen, which We reveal to you.”

The report’s unseen character comes from الْغَيْبِ (al-ghayb), not from anbāʾ itself.

Nabaʾ can therefore concern the past, the present, the future, or something hidden. Context determines its time reference and the recipient’s degree of access to it.

Anbaʾa and nabbaʾa: informing

The verbal meaning of this family is very clear in the Qur’an.

In the account of Adam, we read:

أَنْبِئُونِي بِأَسْمَاءِ هَٰؤُلَاءِ

Anbiʾūnī bi-asmāʾi hāʾulāʾ.

“Inform Me of the names of these.”

Al-Baqarah 2:31

And then:

يَا آدَمُ أَنْبِئْهُمْ بِأَسْمَائِهِمْ

Yā Ādamu anbiʾhum bi-asmāʾihim.

“O Adam, inform them of their names.”

Al-Baqarah 2:33

In At-Taḥrīm 66:3, several forms of this family occur in succession:

فَلَمَّا نَبَّأَهَا بِهِ قَالَتْ مَنْ أَنْبَأَكَ هَٰذَا قَالَ نَبَّأَنِيَ الْعَلِيمُ الْخَبِيرُ

Fa-lammā nabbaʾahā bihi qālat man anbaʾaka hādhā qāla nabbaʾaniya l-ʿalīmu l-khabīr.

“When he informed her of it, she said: Who informed you of this? He said: The Knowing, the Aware informed me.”

In these constructions, the word’s core is nothing more complicated than giving information and making someone aware.

The God also repeatedly says that He will inform people of their deeds or disagreements:

فَيُنَبِّئُكُمْ بِمَا كُنْتُمْ تَعْمَلُونَ

Fa-yunabbiʾukum bi-mā kuntum taʿmalūn.

“Then He will inform you of what you used to do.”

And:

فَيُنَبِّئُكُمْ بِمَا كُنْتُمْ فِيهِ تَخْتَلِفُونَ

Fa-yunabbiʾukum bi-mā kuntum fīhi takhtalifūn.

“Then He will inform you of that over which you used to differ.”

This informing can even concern something the person previously did. Nabaʾ and tanbiʾah should therefore not be equated with predicting the future.

The derivation of nabī

The connection between nabī and N B ʾ has substantial support in the Arabic tradition.

Lexicographers have described a nabī as someone who reports on behalf of The God. The hamzated form nabīʾ is also known in Arabic, and forms relating to nabī are pronounced with a hamzah in some Qur’anic readings. On this account, nabī can be a lightened pronunciation of nabīʾ, belonging to the same family as nabaʾ and anbaʾa.

This analysis is also natural semantically. The verbs of N B ʾ in the Qur’an concern reporting and informing, and a nabī is someone connected with divine news and announcement.

Another analysis, however, has also existed since early times. Arabic records a nearby family relating to height, prominence, and being elevated. Some lexicographers have assigned nabī to this field: someone whose standing has been raised.

The existence of this competing analysis should not be concealed. Nor can the semantic resemblance between nabī and news alone establish that the history of derivation has been completely resolved.

What can be said with greater confidence is that the connection between nabī and N B ʾ has real support in Arabic form, Qur’anic reading, and meaning; it is not merely a late play on words. Claims of a certain historical origin, however, require caution.

Hebrew and Aramaic: an ancient Semitic family

Comparative evidence deepens this discussion.

In Hebrew, a very close form, nābīʾ, titles a person speaking in a prophetic capacity. A verb of the same family is used in that language for prophesying.

In Aramaic, too, a close form is attested for a prophet; in some branches of the language, even the sense spokesperson for another appears. A related verb likewise means to prophesy, and terms for prophethood and a female prophet are formed from the same family.

This distribution shows that nabī is not a newly coined term of Islamic Arabic. It is a very ancient title within the Semitic language family.

That antiquity, however, does not allow us to say with certainty which form was originally derived from which. The noun for prophet may be very old, with verbs of prophesying formed from it later; the historical relationship may also be more complicated. Comparative evidence establishes the family’s antiquity and relatedness, not necessarily the direction of its development.

Nor should the disagreement among Arabic lexicographers between “news” and “height” be resolved simply by saying that the word entered Arabic from other Semitic languages. Such a claim requires independent historical evidence.

What the Semitic evidence shows more strongly is that the family of nabī and nubuwwah is independent of the field of R S L and sending. This bears directly on understanding the difference between Prophet and Messenger.

Prophethood, the Book, and judgment

In several verses, the Qur’an places prophethood alongside the Book and judgment.

Āl ʿImrān 3:79 reads:

مَا كَانَ لِبَشَرٍ أَنْ يُؤْتِيَهُ اللَّهُ الْكِتَابَ وَالْحُكْمَ وَالنُّبُوَّةَ

Mā kāna li-basharin an yuʾtiyahu llāhu l-kitāba wa-l-ḥukma wa-n-nubuwwah.

“It is not for any human being that The God should give him the Book, judgment, and prophethood…”

In Al-Anʿām 6:89:

أُولَٰئِكَ الَّذِينَ آتَيْنَاهُمُ الْكِتَابَ وَالْحُكْمَ وَالنُّبُوَّةَ

Ulāʾika lladhīna ātaynāhumu l-kitāba wa-l-ḥukma wa-n-nubuwwah.

“Those are the ones to whom We gave the Book, judgment, and prophethood.”

And in Al-Jāthiyah 45:16:

وَآتَيْنَاهُمُ الْكِتَابَ وَالْحُكْمَ وَالنُّبُوَّةَ

Wa-ātaynāhumu l-kitāba wa-l-ḥukma wa-n-nubuwwah.

“And We gave them the Book, judgment, and prophethood.”

The three terms—the Book, judgment, and prophethood—are separately coordinated.

In Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:27, too:

وَجَعَلْنَا فِي ذُرِّيَّتِهِ النُّبُوَّةَ وَالْكِتَابَ

Wa-jaʿalnā fī dhurriyyatihi n-nubuwwata wa-l-kitāb.

“And We placed prophethood and the Book among his descendants.”

The same two terms appear together in Al-Ḥadīd 57:26.

These constructions show a relationship between prophethood and the Book, but not their identity. If prophethood itself meant “having a Book,” the Qur’an could still mention both for emphasis. Taken together, however, the usages provide no reason to treat “the Book” as the definition of prophethood itself.

Al-Baqarah 2:213

The verse says:

فَبَعَثَ اللَّهُ النَّبِيِّينَ مُبَشِّرِينَ وَمُنْذِرِينَ وَأَنْزَلَ مَعَهُمُ الْكِتَابَ بِالْحَقِّ لِيَحْكُمَ بَيْنَ النَّاسِ فِيمَا اخْتَلَفُوا فِيهِ

Fa-baʿatha llāhu n-nabiyyīna mubashshirīna wa-mundhirīna wa-anzala maʿahumu l-kitāba bi-l-ḥaqqi li-yaḥkuma bayna n-nāsi fīmā khtalafū fīh.

“Then The God raised up the Prophets as bearers of good news and warners, and sent down with them the Book in truth, to judge between people concerning that over which they differed.”

The verse connects the Prophets with the Book, but does not say that every Prophet received a separate, new Book.

النبيين (an-nabiyyīn) is plural, while الكتاب (al-kitāb) is singular. The singular form of al-kitāb does not by itself prove that one particular historical Book is intended, since it may designate the category of scripture. Yet the construction does not establish a one-to-one relationship of “one Prophet = one Book” either.

More importantly, the sentence concerns judging between people in their disagreement. The verse explains the Book’s role within this relationship of revelation; it does not define a lexical condition of prophethood.

Al-Māʾidah 5:44 makes the issue clearer:

إِنَّا أَنْزَلْنَا التَّوْرَاةَ فِيهَا هُدًى وَنُورٌ يَحْكُمُ بِهَا النَّبِيُّونَ

Innā anzalnā t-tawrāta fīhā hudan wa-nūrun yaḥkumu bihā n-nabiyyūn.

“We sent down the Torah; in it were guidance and light; the Prophets judged by it.”

Here there is one Torah and there are multiple Prophets. Several Prophets can therefore judge within the framework of a Book revealed before them.

Concerning John, we also read:

يَا يَحْيَىٰ خُذِ الْكِتَابَ بِقُوَّةٍ

Yā Yaḥyā khudhi l-kitāba bi-quwwah.

“O John, take hold of the Book with strength.”

Maryam 19:12

Yet the Qur’an introduces no new Book called the Book of John.

Two propositions must therefore be kept distinct:

A Prophet is connected with the Book and revelation.

And:

Every Prophet has a separate, new Book.

The first is consistent with the Qur’an; the second does not follow from these verses.

Prophet and Messenger

Examining N B ʾ also clarifies the distinction discussed in the study of R S L.

Rasūl, Messenger, belongs to the family of sending and mission. A person is dispatched by a sender for a mission.

Nabī, Prophet, is another title. In the N B ʾ analysis, it relates to news and prophetic announcement. Even if the root analysis involving “height” is accepted, it still does not belong to R S L.

The Qur’an describes Moses as:

رَسُولًا نَبِيًّا

Rasūlan nabiyyā.

“A Messenger, a Prophet.”

These are the titles it gives him.

The same two descriptions occur for Ishmael, and concerning Muhammad we also read:

الرَّسُولَ النَّبِيَّ الْأُمِّيَّ

Ar-rasūla n-nabiyya l-ummiyy.

“The Messenger, the ummī Prophet.”

One person can be both Messenger and Prophet, since the two titles do not describe the same aspect.

Al-Ḥajj 22:52 even says:

وَمَا أَرْسَلْنَا مِنْ قَبْلِكَ مِنْ رَسُولٍ وَلَا نَبِيٍّ

Wa-mā arsalnā min qablika min rasūlin wa-lā nabiyy.

“We did not send before you any Messenger or Prophet…”

Both a Messenger and a Prophet can therefore be sent. Their difference is not that one is sent while the other is not.

Nor does the Book mark the boundary between them. Prophets can judge by the Torah, and Messengers can come in continuity with earlier revelation. Whether a figure has a new Book or a new law must be examined through the verses about that particular figure, rather than inserted into the definition of Prophet or Messenger.

Consequently, translating both titles with one English word erases part of the Qur’an’s structure.

Mary and the scope of the title Prophet

In Sūrat Maryam, Mary appears in a narrative sequence with figures about whose group it is subsequently said:

أُولَٰئِكَ الَّذِينَ أَنْعَمَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِمْ مِنَ النَّبِيِّينَ ... وَمِمَّنْ هَدَيْنَا وَاجْتَبَيْنَا

Ulāʾika lladhīna anʿama llāhu ʿalayhim mina n-nabiyyīna … wa-mimman hadaynā wa-jtabaynā.

“Those are the ones upon whom The God bestowed favor, from among the Prophets… and from among those whom We guided and chose.”

Before this collective reference, Mary is introduced with:

وَاذْكُرْ فِي الْكِتَابِ مَرْيَمَ

Wa-dhkur fī l-kitābi Maryam.

“Mention Mary in the Book.”

Jesus, Abraham, Moses, Aaron, Ishmael, and Idrīs then appear in the same narrative sequence.

From the sūrah’s structure, including Mary in the group to which ulāʾika, “those,” refers is a serious reading. The Arabic masculine plural does not exclude a woman from a mixed group.

The Qur’an does not, however, use the direct statement “Mary is a Prophet.” Contextual inclusion must therefore be distinguished from explicit designation.

Calling Mary ṣiddīqah does not rule out this possibility either. Concerning Abraham, the Qur’an says:

صِدِّيقًا نَبِيًّا

Ṣiddīqan nabiyyā.

“A man of truth, a Prophet.”

It likewise places the same two descriptions together for Idrīs. Ṣiddīq and nabī are therefore not incompatible titles in Qur’anic language.

This example again shows that the definition of Prophet cannot be restricted in advance by criteria such as gender, possessing a separate Book, or bringing a new law.

Translation

Depending on context, نَبَأ (nabaʾ) can be rendered “news,” “report,” or retained as nabaʾ. If the news is great, certain, or unseen, that feature should be translated from the qualification and construction of the particular verse, rather than placed inside the word in every occurrence.

نَبَّأَ (nabbaʾa) and أَنْبَأَ (anbaʾa) mean “gave news,” “informed,” or “made someone aware of something.”

Translating nabī raises a more substantial issue: it must remain distinct from rasūl. In English, “Messenger” identifies someone who carries a message and belongs naturally to the field of rasūl; “Prophet” preserves a separate title for nabī.

If rasūl and nabī are both translated “Messenger,” the expression:

رَسُولًا نَبِيًّا

Rasūlan nabiyyā.

“A Messenger, a Prophet.”

Is reduced to two synonymous terms, and the Qur’an’s distinction disappears.

Using Prophet for nabī and Messenger for rasūl preserves the distinction in natural English. In linguistic discussion, retaining nabī itself also keeps the Arabic title visible without merging its field with that of rasūl.

Nabī should not be rendered “foreteller,” either. The N B ʾ family concerns news, which can relate to the past, the present, the unseen, or the future.

Summary

The root ن ب أ in the Qur’an centers on news and informing. Nabaʾ is news that reaches a recipient, but the word itself guarantees neither truth nor greatness, an unseen character, nor reference to the future. The Qur’an says nabaʾ yaqīn and nabaʾ ʿaẓīm, speaks of investigating a report brought by a transgressor, and only in some instances identifies news as unseen by adding al-ghayb.

The verbs of this family likewise have a clear meaning: a human being or The God informs another of something. The news may concern something past, presently existing, hidden, or future.

The connection of nabī with this family has substantial support in Arabic, the hamzated form, and certain Qur’anic readings. Another ancient analysis connects it with height, however, and this disagreement should not be concealed.

Hebrew and Aramaic also show that the family of nabī and nubuwwah is very old and shared across Semitic languages. This evidence strengthens nabī’s independence from the field of “sending,” R S L, and shows its connection with prophetic speech. It does not, however, resolve the precise direction of derivation by itself, and the disagreement among Arabic lexicographers cannot be attributed to the word’s antiquity without historical evidence.

In the Qur’an, prophethood has a real relationship with the Book and judgment, but is not identical with them. Al-kitāb wa-l-ḥukm wa-n-nubuwwah places them separately alongside one another, and Al-Māʾidah 5:44 shows multiple Prophets judging by one Torah. The connection between Prophet and Book should therefore not be denied, but neither can it yield a rule that “every Prophet has a separate Book.”

Messenger and Prophet are likewise independent titles that can be united in one person. Messenger identifies someone in terms of being sent and entrusted with a mission; Prophet is a title from a different family. The Qur’an itself calls one person rasūlan nabiyyan and even speaks of “sending” both a Messenger and a Prophet.

A cautious conclusion for this root is:

N B ʾ: a family centered on news and informing. Nabaʾ is news that reaches a recipient and may concern the past, the present, the unseen, or the future. The connection of nabī with this family has substantial support in Arabic and Semitic evidence, but the precise history of derivation must remain open because of the ancient competing analysis. In Qur’anic usage, Prophet is a title independent of Messenger; neither possessing a separate Book nor bringing a new law is part of the title’s own meaning.
