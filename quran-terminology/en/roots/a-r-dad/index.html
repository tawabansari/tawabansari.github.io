Qur’anic Root

The Qur’anic Root A R Ḍ

Earth, land, and the ground beneath one’s feet

Arḍ denotes earth, land, and the ground beneath one’s feet. The Qur’an uses the same word in accounts of creation, life, movement, growth, and resurrection; context determines its extent and role.

Forms discussed

أَرْض / الأَرْض
Singular noun: earth, land, ground

أَرْضُ اللَّهِ
Possessive phrase, not a new derivative

Methodological note

Arḍ in the Qur’an denotes the earth in contrast with the heavens, a particular land, or the ground on which people walk and plants grow. The sentence determines its extent: al-arḍ does not necessarily mean the entire globe on every occurrence.

All 461 Qur’anic occurrences use the singular noun. Arabic plurals such as arāḍin and araḍūn, and the relational adjective arḍī, do not occur in the Qur’an. The singular form alone establishes neither a description of the earth’s shape nor a social theory.

Core meaning

The basic meaning of arḍ is earth or land. It may be the ground beneath one’s feet, the earth contrasted with the sky, or a particular territory inhabited by people. These uses are related, but they should not all be replaced by the abstract definition “field of responsibility.”

In passages about corruption, reform, migration, or power on earth, land is the setting of human conduct. The ethical and social meaning comes from the verbs, descriptions, and context. “The earth belongs to The God” states a relationship; ownership and responsibility are not independent meanings of the letters A R Ḍ.

The physical referent

The sensory reference is straightforward: the ground on which people stand, walk, build, and cultivate. In agricultural settings it dries out or supports vegetation with water. Geographically it may denote an extensive land or a limited area.

No invented story of the root’s origin is needed. Describing the earth as dead or alive, or as speaking at the resurrection, belongs to the wording of particular passages; these descriptions are not all part of the dictionary definition of arḍ.

Arabic before and around the Qur’an

Ibn Sīdah’s al-Muḥkam, under arḍ, attributes this verse to Imruʾ al-Qays:

بِلَادٌ عَرِيضَةٌ وَأَرْضٌ أَرِيضَةٌ
مَدَافِعُ مَاءٍ فِي فَضَاءٍ عَرِيضِ

Broad lands and fertile ground; courses of water in a wide open space.

Arḍ denotes land or ground, while arīḍah describes it as good and fertile. The verse attests the concrete use in poetry attributed to a pre-Qur’anic poet; ethical responsibility and political power are not meanings of the noun itself. Lexical citations transmit both “water” and “rain”; the wording here follows al-Muḥkam.

Qur’anic uses

Earth in contrast with the heavens

خَلَقَ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ بِالْحَقِّ

He created the heavens and the earth in truth.

64:3

The pairing with the heavens describes the scope of creation. “In truth” qualifies the creating; it does not make order or ethical purpose the lexical meaning of arḍ.

A place of settlement and enjoyment

وَلَكُمْ فِي الْأَرْضِ مُسْتَقَرٌّ وَمَتَاعٌ إِلَىٰ حِينٍ

And on earth you have a dwelling and enjoyment for a time.

2:36

Earth is the place of settlement; “for a time” limits the enjoyment. Temporariness comes from that qualification, not from the noun earth. The passage gives grounds for reflection on the limited duration of human residence.

The earth belongs to The God

إِنَّ الْأَرْضَ لِلَّهِ

Indeed, the earth belongs to The God.

7:128

Moses says this to his people; the passage goes on to speak of inheritance and the outcome for the mindful. Humans use land, but their possession is not treated here as unrestricted. Trust and responsibility are ethical implications of that relationship, not translations of the word earth.

Corruption and reform on earth

وَلَا تُفْسِدُوا فِي الْأَرْضِ بَعْدَ إِصْلَاحِهَا

Do not cause corruption on earth after its setting right.

7:56

Earth is where corruption occurs. Its effects may concern people, social relationships, and the environment according to context. That breadth matters in explanation, but “on earth” remains an appropriate translation of fī al-arḍ; “society” is not its universal replacement.

The growth of life

فَأَحْيَيْنَا بِهِ الْأَرْضَ بَعْدَ مَوْتِهَا

Then with it We brought the earth to life after its death.

35:9

Dryness and the absence of vegetation are expressed as death, and renewed growth as coming to life. The earth had not disappeared; its condition and vegetation change. The example should be read through the passage’s own imagery.

Movement and provision

فَامْشُوا فِي مَنَاكِبِهَا وَكُلُوا مِنْ رِزْقِهِ

So walk through its regions and eat of His provision.

67:15

The feminine pronoun refers to the earth. It is the expanse of movement and sustenance. Travel and seeking provision follow from the commands to walk and eat; the noun arḍ does not itself mean work or earning.

The extent of the earth and migration

أَلَمْ تَكُنْ أَرْضُ اللَّهِ وَاسِعَةً فَتُهَاجِرُوا فِيهَا

Was the earth of The God not spacious enough for you to migrate in it?

4:97

The passage addresses people who plead their weakness. The earth’s extent points to the possibility of leaving a situation. The following verses exempt those without the ability or means to leave; the rebuke should not be universalized without regard to actual capacity.

Inheritance by righteous servants

أَنَّ الْأَرْضَ يَرِثُهَا عِبَادِيَ الصَّالِحُونَ

That My righteous servants shall inherit the earth.

21:105

The verse speaks of this promise being written. Interpretations differ over the scope and reference of earth in the promise; the noun alone does not decide between a particular land, earth generally, or an eschatological fulfillment. Righteousness and inheritance are related by the text, but no particular scheme of government can be derived from the noun arḍ.

Pharaoh’s self-exaltation

إِنَّ فِرْعَوْنَ عَلَا فِي الْأَرْضِ

Indeed, Pharaoh exalted himself in the land.

28:4

The continuation describes dividing the population and oppressing a group among them. That context establishes the political character of Pharaoh’s conduct. Land remains the setting; domination comes from his self-exaltation and the actions described after it.

The earth bringing forth its burdens

إِذَا زُلْزِلَتِ الْأَرْضُ زِلْزَالَهَا وَأَخْرَجَتِ الْأَرْضُ أَثْقَالَهَا

When the earth is shaken with its shaking and brings forth its burdens.

99:1–2

The continuation says “يَوْمَئِذٍ تُحَدِّثُ أَخْبَارَهَا” (99:4): that day it will tell its news. Earth takes an active role in this depiction of resurrection. The role comes from the verbs of the passage and is not a permanent dictionary definition of earth.

Earth and its inhabitants

وَلِلَّهِ يَسْجُدُ مَا فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَمَا فِي الْأَرْضِ مِنْ دَابَّةٍ

To The God prostrates whatever creature is in the heavens and on earth.

16:49

The verse includes creatures other than humans among the inhabitants of earth. The relationship of service comes from the sentence and its verb of prostration. Reflection on human responsibility toward other creatures is compatible with this breadth but does not exhaust the lexical meaning of earth.

Related words

Turāb denotes dust or earth as material, while ṭīn is clay. Ṣaʿīd concerns the surface of the ground, and qāʿ level ground. Arḍ may denote the ground underfoot, a land, or earth contrasted with the heavens. Replacing all these words with a single definition erases differences between sentences.

Balad and qaryah relate to inhabited places and populations, and dār to a dwelling or abode. They may refer to a part of the same land called arḍ. Their distinction is not a mandatory historical progression from land to village to city, or a fixed modern urban/rural division.

Translation considerations

First identify the reference from context. “Earth” suits the contrast with the heavens, “land” a particular territory, and “ground” a physical surface; “soil” may fit a specifically material setting.

“Field of life,” “sphere of responsibility,” and “realm of action” may be interpretive descriptions, but they are not regular translation equivalents of arḍ. The definite article alone does not establish a reference to the whole globe; the land may be the territory already known from the narrative.

Suggested renderings

Al-arḍ beside the heavens: “the earth”; fī al-arḍ with a particular territorial reference: “in the land”; arḍ Allāh wāsiʿah: “the earth of The God is spacious.”

Lā tufsidū fī al-arḍ: “do not cause corruption on earth”; aḥyā al-arḍ baʿda mawtihā: “brought the earth to life after its death”; akhrajat al-arḍ athqālahā: “the earth brought forth its burdens.” Preserve the verbs’ imagery and place social or natural explanation outside the translation.

Summary

Arḍ denotes earth, land, and ground. The Qur’an uses it in passages about creation, settlement, growth, migration, corruption and reform, power, inheritance, and resurrection. Those contexts establish the reference and role of the land in each passage; they are not all separate lexical meanings of the noun. Accurate translation derives the scope from context and distinguishes a place from judgments about what happens there.
