Core meaning: breaking, crumbling, and disintegration into small fragments and broken remains.

The Arabic root ر ف ت concerns breaking, crushing, and disintegration, through which an object's intact structure is lost and it becomes fragments.

Transitive رَفَتَ means to break or crush something; رُفَات names the fragments and remains resulting from such disintegration.

The Quran uses this family for the body after death, when those denying resurrection ask how they could be raised as a new creation after becoming bones and crumbled remains.

The root occurs only as رُفَاتًا in Al-Isra 17:49 and Al-Isra 17:98.

1. Meanings in early Arabic

رَفَتَ: breaking and crushing

Arabic dictionaries record رَفَتَ الشَّيْءَ for breaking and crushing something, especially into smaller pieces.

رَفَتَ الْعَظْمَ, for example, describes crushing a bone.

The bone is broken into fragments rather than merely cracked.

The dictionaries also record رَفَتَ عِظَامَ الْجَزُورِ: breaking the bones of a slaughtered camel so they can be cooked to extract their fat.

This illustrates actual breaking and crushing, not only decay or gradual deterioration.

ارْفَتَّ الْحَبْلُ is also recorded for a rope that breaks apart.

The emphasis here is the loss of the rope's continuity.

The family therefore includes related uses for fragmentation and for the breaking of continuity.

These uses are recorded in classical dictionaries. Assigning each to pre-Islamic speech would require independent, datable evidence; without it, such dating cannot be asserted with certainty.

The Quran's own use of رُفَات establishes the sense of crumbling and disintegration in the Arabic of revelation.

رُفَات: fragments resulting from disintegration

رُفَات denotes fragments or remains of something broken, crushed, or worn down, which has lost its former integrity.

Dictionaries explain it using words such as فُتَات and حُطَام.

The meaning is not restricted to bones or human remains.

For example, lexical discussions mention مَدَر, clods of dried earth, becoming crumbled fragments.

In this example, رُفَات concerns earth broken into pieces rather than death or bodily decay.

Comparable broken fragments can fall within the word's semantic range; this does not establish every imaginable collocation as attested usage.

In the Quran, its reference to the human body after death brings the deterioration and disintegration of bodily remains into focus.

That application should not become the exclusive definition of the root.

Fragmentation and absolute annihilation

Breaking something into fragments does not mean the absolute disappearance of all its constituent material.

If a pottery vessel falls and shatters, its shape and structure are lost, but its fragments remain.

Further crushing may reduce those fragments to much smaller particles.

The vessel no longer exists as an intact container, but its material has not thereby vanished entirely.

رُفَات similarly denotes remains produced by breaking and disintegration.

This does not require every fragment to be visible or to have a particular size.

The word does not precisely quantify the degree of fragmentation.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important forms include:

رَفَتَ: broke or crushed something; in intransitive use, broke apart or crumbled.

رَفْت: breaking, crushing, or coming apart.

رُفَات: fragments and remains of something broken or disintegrated.

مَرْفُوت: something broken or crushed.

ارْفَتَّ: broke apart or crumbled into fragments.

تَرَفَّتَ: crumbled or disintegrated.

رُفَات is a singular noun with a collective sense: it names fragmented material or remains, rather than necessarily being the grammatical plural of another noun.

This matters in the verse: عِظَامًا is the plural of عَظْم, while رُفَاتًا itself names the crumbled remains.

Only وَرُفَاتًا occurs in the Quran: the initial wāw is a conjunction, and رُفَاتًا is accusative.

No verb or other derivative of this root occurs in the Quran.

3. Quranic uses

Al-Isra 17:49: bones and crumbled remains

وَقَالُوا أَئِذَا كُنَّا عِظَامًا وَرُفَاتًا أَئِنَّا لَمَبْعُوثُونَ خَلْقًا جَدِيدًا

wa-qālū a-idhā kunnā ʿiẓāman wa-rufātan a-innā la-mabʿūthūna khalqan jadīdā

Al-Isrāʾ 17:49

“They said: ‘When we have become bones and crumbled remains, will we really be raised as a new creation?’”

Al-Isra 17:49

رُفَات accompanies عِظَام.

عِظَام is the plural of عَظْم and means bones.

رُفَات denotes fragments and remains resulting from disintegration.

Together the words depict the body's condition after death.

After death, soft tissues may decompose; bones too may deteriorate and crumble over time.

Those denying resurrection appeal to this condition, asking whether they will be raised after their bodies have become bones and crumbled remains.

رُفَات here does not describe an intact body or untouched skeleton; it concerns loss of the former structure's integrity.

Does عِظَامًا وَرُفَاتًا specify two successive stages?

Bones may initially remain after death and later crumble.

The conjunction wāw, however, does not by itself establish a chronological sequence.

The phrase can place two conditions or types of remains together: bones and crumbled fragments.

It does not establish that every body becomes bones and then fine particles on the same timetable or in an identical sequence.

The central issue is denial of recreation after bodily disintegration.

The Quran's response

The next verse says:

Al-Isra 17:50: “Say: be stones or iron.”

Al-Isra 17:51 then mentions a state they consider even more formidable and answers that the One who first originated them will bring them back.

This sequence identifies divine power to recreate as the central question.

The deniers infer impossibility from the body's disintegration; the Quran recalls the power of the original Creator.

رُفَات itself describes the condition of fragmentation.

How people are recreated, the nature of the new creation, and its relationship to the former body cannot be determined from this word alone.

Al-Isra 17:98: the same question within the deniers' outcome

ذَٰلِكَ جَزَاؤُهُمْ بِأَنَّهُمْ كَفَرُوا بِآيَاتِنَا وَقَالُوا أَئِذَا كُنَّا عِظَامًا وَرُفَاتًا أَئِنَّا لَمَبْعُوثُونَ خَلْقًا جَدِيدًا

dhālika jazāʾuhum bi-annahum kafarū bi-āyātinā wa-qālū a-idhā kunnā ʿiẓāman wa-rufātan a-innā la-mabʿūthūna khalqan jadīdā

Al-Isrāʾ 17:98

“That is their recompense because they disbelieved in Our signs and said: ‘When we have become bones and crumbled remains, will we really be raised as a new creation?’”

Al-Isra 17:98

The expression from Al-Isra 17:49 is repeated.

Its surrounding context differs.

Al-Isra 17:49 reports the deniers' statement, followed by the Quran's response.

Al-Isra 17:98 recalls that statement while describing the outcome of those who rejected the divine signs.

Al-Isra 17:97 concerns their gathering on the Day of Resurrection and their destination.

Al-Isra 17:98 explains the recompense by recalling their rejection of the signs and their statement about being raised.

Al-Isra 17:99 then invokes the creation of the heavens and earth in affirming God's creative power.

رُفَات has the same sense in both occurrences: crumbled and disintegrated remains.

The sentence's role in its context changes.

The first passage foregrounds the deniers' question and its answer; the second recalls the denial in relation to their outcome.

This distinction comes from context and surrounding sentences, not a change in the meaning of رُفَات.

4. Comparison with related roots

رُفَات and فُتَات

The two words are very close in meaning.

فَتَّ means to crumble or reduce something to small pieces; فُتَات denotes the resulting fragments.

رَفَتَ likewise concerns breaking or crumbling, and رُفَات names the broken remains.

In some uses, the words can be almost synonymous.

A thoroughly crushed bone, for example, can be described through either family.

Similarity of consonants and meanings does not establish that one root historically arose from the other.

Nor can a fixed difference in particle size or degree of fragmentation be assigned to all their uses.

رُفَات and حُطَام

حُطَام denotes broken or crushed material.

The Quran uses it for dried, broken vegetation, as in Al-Hadid 57:20.

رُفَات also denotes broken, crumbled remains.

Both can name the result of breakage. In the cited Quranic examples, حُطَام concerns plants and رُفَات bodily remains; that contextual difference is not a permanent lexical division between process and result.

Their ranges overlap when describing broken material.

رُفَات and رَمِيم

Ya-Sin 36:78 describes bones as رَمِيم.

This word belongs to ر م م and concerns decay and deterioration in that passage.

رُفَات can also describe deteriorated remains, but foregrounds their crumbled condition.

A bone can become weak through age and decay and then crumble easily.

Deterioration and fragmentation are related in this situation without being identical concepts.

رَمِيم emphasizes the decayed state; رُفَات emphasizes the crumbled remains.

Preserving this distinction in translation is useful.

رُفَات and تُرَاب

تُرَاب means earth or dust.

رُفَات names fragmented remains and is not restricted to earth.

A crushed bone may become tiny fragments without those fragments being identical in material composition to ordinary soil.

In bodily-decay contexts, رُفَات can be explained as dust-like remains; its broader meaning nevertheless concerns fragmented material.

This distinction matters in resurrection passages.

Ar-Ra'd 13:5 speaks of becoming dust.

Al-Isra 17:49 and Al-Isra 17:98 speak of bones and crumbled remains.

Both images involve the former body's loss of structure, but the words do not present exactly the same image.

تُرَاب and رُفَات therefore need not receive identical translations in every context.

رُفَات and عِظَام

عِظَام, the plural of عَظْم, means bones.

A bone is a particular component of a human or animal body.

رُفَات does not name a particular organ or body part; it names fragmented remains.

The words therefore describe different aspects.

One names a bodily structure; the other identifies crumbled material.

Their combination in Al-Isra 17:49 and Al-Isra 17:98 gives a broader picture of bodily disintegration.

5. Later usage

In modern Arabic, رُفَات is commonly used for human remains.

رُفَاتُ الْمَيِّتِ, for example, refers to the remains of someone who has died.

Persian also uses the borrowed word in expressions concerning the transfer of remains. Adding another word meaning “remains” to it can be redundant.

This familiar use should not replace the broader lexical definition.

Arabic sources also apply the word to fragments of other materials.

The prevalence of human-remains usage does not prove that the word originally referred only to dead people; other uses remain possible in modern Arabic too.

Nor does رُفَات necessarily mean a complete corpse.

In discussions of the dead, it often concerns bodily remains, deteriorated bones, or fragments.

For these Quranic verses, “crumbled remains” preserves the fragmentation image. The broader modern expression “human remains,” however, need not specify an exact degree of bodily disintegration; its conventions should not be judged solely by etymology.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

رُفَاتًا — Al-Isra 17:49

Brief root meaning: breaking and crumbling into fragments and disintegrated remains.

Contextual meaning: the body's crumbled remains after death.

Proposed translation: “crumbled remains.”

Proposed rendering of the phrase:

“When we have become bones and crumbled remains, will we really be raised as a new creation?”

رُفَاتًا — Al-Isra 17:98

Brief root meaning: crumbling and structural disintegration.

Contextual meaning: the body's crumbled remains invoked by those denying resurrection.

Proposed translation: “crumbled remains.”

The phrase can be rendered as in Al-Isra 17:49.

Using the same equivalent supports consistency because the word and expression are identical in both verses.

“Decayed fragments” is understandable in context, but “decayed” emphasizes gradual deterioration.

“Scattered particles” conveys part of the image but may overemphasize dispersal or very small size.

“Crumbled remains” retains both what is left and its fragmented condition without prescribing the precise process of decomposition.

7. Conclusion

ر ف ت concerns breaking, crushing, and disintegration, through which an intact structure becomes fragments or small remains.

رُفَات names those crumbled remains.

The word is not restricted to human remains; dictionaries also apply it to other broken materials.

Its two Quranic occurrences are in Surah Al-Isra, both concerning the human body after death.

The combination of bones and crumbled remains illustrates the deniers' appeal to bodily deterioration as grounds for rejecting recreation.

رُفَات neither means the absolute annihilation of matter nor explains how people are recreated. It describes crumbling; recreation is discussed through the surrounding passages.

Final definition of ر ف ت:

“Breaking, crumbling, and coming apart, with the former structure reduced to fragments and disintegrated remains. رُفَات names these remains and, in its Quranic use, concerns the body's crumbled remains after death.”
