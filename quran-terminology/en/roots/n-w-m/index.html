Sleep, subsiding activity, dreaming, and the boundary between sleep and wakefulness in Arabic and the Qur’an

The Arabic root N W M is best known for sleeping and the state of sleep: ordinary activity decreases, interaction with the surroundings changes, and a person temporarily leaves ordinary wakefulness.

Its uses extend beyond bodily sleep. Early Arabic records related expressions for wind becoming calm, fire subsiding, a market becoming sluggish, sound falling silent, and even trusting another person.

One lexical definition presents the root’s core as follows:

أَصْلٌ صَحِيحٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى جُمُودٍ وَسُكُونِ حَرَكَةٍ

aṣlun ṣaḥīḥun yadullu ʿalā jumūdin wa-sukūni ḥarakatin

Meaning: “An established root denoting stillness and the subsiding of movement.”

This can explain many physical and figurative uses, but it does not mean that sleep completely switches off bodily activity or awareness. “Subsiding movement” is a lexical analysis, not, without further evidence, a scientific definition of sleep.

N W M occurs nine times in the Qur’an, in three principal forms: nawm three times, manām four times, and nāʾimūn twice. Forms such as niyām, naʾūm, manāmah, and the verb nāma do not occur, although they are known in Arabic.

The Qur’an discusses sleep in different settings: human life and rest, the setting for dreams, a state related to taking the self, and a condition in which people may be unaware of surrounding danger.

All concern sleep, but none alone permits reducing sleep to dreaming, death, moral heedlessness, or absolute stillness.

1. Important forms and derivatives

Nāma yanāmu: to sleep

Nāma is a Form I verb with a medial wāw radical. In the perfect, the middle wāw becomes alif; the imperfect is yanāmu.

Early dictionaries use nāma for sleeping or being asleep. The explanation النَّوْمُ النُّعَاسُ also occurs, but lexical proximity does not make every degree of drowsiness and full sleep identical in every use.

Two important verbal nouns are nawm and manām. Besides naming sleep, manām can sometimes denote a sleeping place or what someone sees in sleep.

Nāʾim is an active participle meaning asleep or a person sleeping. Arabic records the plurals niyām, nuwwam, and nuwwām.

Naʾūm describes someone who sleeps much. Nuwamah is recorded in the same sense, while nawmah in another vocalization describes an obscure, inconspicuous person. Lexical transmissions also contain overlapping vocalizations and meanings; each use should be assessed through its own wording and context.

Among derived forms, anāma and nawwama concern putting someone to sleep or causing sleep. Tanāwama may mean pretending to sleep: appearing asleep while actually awake.

Istanāma has two important uses: seeking or preparing for sleep, and, in istanāma ilayhi, becoming reassured and trusting another.

The latter establishes a semantic extension toward trust, but istanāma in that sense must not replace Qur’anic manām.

Manām: sleep, a sleeping place, and what is seen in sleep

Manām is an important derivative.

It can denote sleep itself, as in being asleep, or what someone sees while asleep—a dream.

The sentence clarifies the difference.

رَأَيْتُ فِي الْمَنَامِ naturally means “I saw in sleep.” But فِي مَنَامِهِ in describing someone’s condition may simply mean “during his sleep,” without referring to a particular dream.

This matters in the Qur’an, where manām sometimes accompanies seeing and sometimes concerns sleep itself.

The initial mīm is an added element of the word’s structure, not a root letter; its root remains N W M.

Manāmah: a sleeping place and sleeping cover

Early dictionaries record manāmah for a cover or cloth in which someone sleeps, and sometimes for a sleeping place or raised platform.

Manāmah is also explained as qaṭīfah, a thick cloth or cover.

The semantic relationship is understandable: the state’s name is transferred to something used for sleeping.

That does not mean manām in every Qur’anic verse denotes a physical sleeping place or bed.

2. Early Arabic uses

Wind, sea, and fire becoming still

Recorded lexical expressions include:

نَامَتِ الرِّيحُ: the wind became calm.

نَامَ الْبَحْرُ: the sea became calm.

نَامَتِ النَّارُ: the fire subsided.

Wind, sea, and fire do not sleep as people do. These expressions concern visible activity becoming calm or subsiding.

They relate to the definition involving movement subsiding.

Human sleep and a subsiding fire nevertheless differ. The metaphor “the fire slept” does not establish that human sleep ends all vital activity.

A sluggish market and worn clothing

Arabic dictionaries record:

نَامَتِ السُّوقُ: the market became sluggish.

Activity and trade have decreased or prosperity has subsided.

نَامَ الثَّوْبُ is also recorded for clothing becoming old and worn.

The uses differ: the market emphasizes reduced activity, while clothing concerns the effects of use and time.

Both are recorded under N W M, but appearing in one entry does not establish that every meaning developed from one original physical image.

Sound falling silent

نَامَ الْخَلْخَالُ describes an anklet’s sound ceasing.

A sounding anklet may make noise as the foot moves. When it falls silent, it is figuratively said to have “slept.”

Ṭurayḥ al-Thaqafī says:

نَامَتْ خَلَاخِلُهَا وَجَالَ وِشَاحُهَا
وَجَرَى الْإِزَارُ عَلَى كَثِيبٍ أَهْيَلِ

nāmat khalākhiluhā wa-jāla wishāḥuhā / wa-jarā al-izāru ʿalā kathībin ahyali

“Her anklets fell silent while her ornamental sash moved, and the garment flowed over a soft, rounded form.”

Nāmat concerns the anklets falling silent, not their literally having sleep and wakefulness.

Ṭurayḥ was an Umayyad-period poet. The line is evidence from early Arabic after Islam, not pre-Islamic poetry.

Sleep and heedlessness

نَامَ عَنْ حَاجَتِهِ may mean neglecting a need or task one should attend to.

Sleep figuratively expresses inattention or failure to act.

One vocalization of nawmah also describes an obscure, inconspicuous person little known in society.

These uses show stillness, inactivity, or being out of public attention described through sleep language.

Sleeping and moral heedlessness remain distinct. Sleep may metaphorically express heedlessness in some settings, but Qur’anic sleep should not become religious or moral heedlessness without evidence.

3. Early poetic witnesses

Sleep and sleeplessness in al-Khansāʾ

A line attributed to al-Khansāʾ concerns Hāshim ibn Ḥarmalah’s death and the easing of her grief:

كَمَا مِنْ هَاشِمٍ أَقْرَرْتُ عَيْنِي
وَكَانَتْ لَا تَنَامُ وَلَا تُنِيمُ

ka-mā min hāshimin aqrartu ʿaynī / wa-kānat lā tanāmu wa-lā tunīmu

The sense: “As through Hāshim’s fate I found rest for my eye, which neither slept itself nor let another sleep.”

Tanāmu expresses sleeping; tunīmu expresses causing another to sleep.

The verbs share a root but differ morphologically and in the subject’s relationship to the action. In tanāmu, the subject sleeps; in tunīmu, it causes or allows another to sleep.

Early literary transmissions record the line. Some have لَدَى الْمَنِيمِ or مَعَ الْمَنِيمِ instead of وَلَا تُنِيمُ.

The variant matters because the evidence for transitive tunīmu depends on this particular wording. The line should therefore retain notice of its variants.

Al-Khansāʾ lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. Historical reports place the event before Islam, but dating this particular line independently of the event requires more precise evidence.

Sleep figuratively attributed to lightning

A line attributed to Sāʿidah ibn Juʾayyah al-Hudhalī says:

حَتَّى شَآهَا كَلِيلٌ مُوهِنًا عَمِلٌ
بَاتَتْ طِرَابًا وَبَاتَ اللَّيْلَ لَمْ يَنَمْ

ḥattā shaʾāhā kalīlun mūhinan ʿamilun / bātat ṭirāban wa-bāta al-layla lam yanam

The sense: “Until a faint, persistent lightning in the night stirred those animals; they passed the night excited, and the lightning did not rest all night.”

Lam yanam here expresses lightning’s continuing illumination or activity.

Lightning does not literally sleep; the poet denies sleep to describe uninterrupted activity.

The witness illustrates a figurative extension in which sleep stands opposite continuing movement or activity.

The line is attributed to Sāʿidah ibn Juʾayyah, a Hudhalī poet who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, and occurs in early grammatical transmissions. Some wording and explanations vary, but lam yanam remains in the witnesses examined.

Since the composition date is unclear, it should not certainly be called pre-Qur’anic evidence.

The witnesses fit the recorded uses: literal sleep, causing another to sleep, and figurative calm or cessation of activity. None establishes that all Qur’anic uses must be figurative.

4. Qur’anic sleep as part of human life

Sleep, repose, and returning to activity

Al-Furqān 25:47

وَهُوَ الَّذِي جَعَلَ لَكُمُ اللَّيْلَ لِبَاسًا وَالنَّوْمَ سُبَاتًا وَجَعَلَ النَّهَارَ نُشُورًا

wa-huwa alladhī jaʿala lakumu al-layla libāsan wa-al-nawma subātan wa-jaʿala al-nahāra nushūran

“He is the One who made the night a covering for you, sleep a repose, and the day a time of rising and dispersal.”

Nawm and subāt stand together here.

Nawm denotes sleep itself; subāt belongs to the independent root S B T and here concerns rest and a temporary break from ordinary activity.

They should not be treated as mere repetition of one meaning.

Sleep appears alongside night and day: night is a covering, sleep affords repose and a break from activity, and day relates to nushūr.

This suggests a cycle of sleep and activity, but does not establish that sleeping is possible or permitted only at night.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:9

وَجَعَلْنَا نَوْمَكُمْ سُبَاتًا

wa-jaʿalnā nawmakum subātan

“We made your sleep a repose.”

The shorter verse expresses the same relationship between sleep and subāt.

The repeated subāt presents sleep among the blessings and arrangements of creation.

A particular medical theory about sleep stages, brain activity, or bodily repair should not be extracted from subāt itself. These belong to empirical science and require separate investigation.

Study of S B T

Does the Qur’an restrict sleep to night?

Al-Rūm 30:23

وَمِنْ آيَاتِهِ مَنَامُكُمْ بِاللَّيْلِ وَالنَّهَارِ وَابْتِغَاؤُكُمْ مِنْ فَضْلِهِ إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَآيَاتٍ لِقَوْمٍ يَسْمَعُونَ

wa-min āyātihi manāmukum bi-al-layli wa-al-nahāri wa-ibtighāʾukum min faḍlihi inna fī dhālika la-āyātin li-qawmin yasmaʿūna

“Among His signs are your sleep by night and day and your seeking His bounty. In this are signs for a people who listen.”

This is important evidence for manām’s meaning.

Manāmukum here concerns human sleep, not a particular dream.

Bi-al-layli wa-al-nahār explicitly mentions both night and day.

Two grammatical interpretations are offered: sleep and seeking provision both occur across night and day, or sleep mainly belongs to night and seeking provision mainly to day.

The first follows the sentence’s apparent structure more closely and requires no rearrangement.

Al-Furqān 25:47 therefore should not by itself imply that the Qur’an ignores or denies daytime sleep. Al-Rūm 30:23 clearly gives sleep a broader temporal range.

Nor does this verse set a precise sleep schedule or the amount people need.

5. Sleep and dreams: two uses of manām

Abraham’s dream

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:102

فَلَمَّا بَلَغَ مَعَهُ السَّعْيَ قَالَ يَا بُنَيَّ إِنِّي أَرَىٰ فِي الْمَنَامِ أَنِّي أَذْبَحُكَ فَانْظُرْ مَاذَا تَرَىٰ

fa-lammā balagha maʿahu al-saʿya qāla yā bunayya innī arā fī al-manāmi annī adhbaḥuka fa-unẓur mādhā tarā

“When he reached the stage of endeavor alongside him, he said: My dear son, I see in sleep that I slaughter you; consider what you think.”

Al-manām accompanies arā, “I see.”

The subject is therefore more than Abraham’s being asleep: what he sees is also at issue.

The continuation confirms this. Al-Ṣāffāt 37:105 says:

قَدْ صَدَّقْتَ الرُّؤْيَا

qad ṣaddaqta al-ruʾyā

“You have indeed fulfilled the vision.”

What was seen in manām is called ruʾyā.

Manām can thus be the setting for a dream; raʾā fī al-manām concerns seeing something during sleep.

This does not make manām mean a dream in every verse. Al-Rūm 30:23 uses it for ordinary human sleep.

Arā is imperfect, but that form alone does not establish how many times Abraham’s dream recurred. This lies outside N W M itself.

The Prophet’s dream in al-Anfāl

Al-Anfāl 8:43

إِذْ يُرِيكَهُمُ اللَّهُ فِي مَنَامِكَ قَلِيلًا وَلَوْ أَرَاكَهُمْ كَثِيرًا لَفَشِلْتُمْ وَلَتَنَازَعْتُمْ فِي الْأَمْرِ وَلَٰكِنَّ اللَّهَ سَلَّمَ إِنَّهُ عَلِيمٌ بِذَاتِ الصُّدُورِ

idh yurīkahumu allāhu fī manāmika qalīlan wa-law arākahum kathīran la-fashiltum wa-la-tanāzaʿtum fī al-amri wa-lākinna allāha sallama innahu ʿalīmun bi-dhāti al-ṣudūri

“When The God showed them to you as few in your sleep; had He shown them as many, you would have faltered and disputed over the matter. But The God brought safety. He knows what is within the breasts.”

Manām again relates to something being shown to someone.

The natural reading is that the Prophet saw the enemies as few in sleep.

Some interpretive and lexical reports suggest another possibility: manām as a sleeping place, meaning the eye, and thus seeing with the eyes.

In examining these alternatives, the dream reading is considered more suitable to the sequence of verses.

The following verse provides an important reason:

وَإِذْ يُرِيكُمُوهُمْ إِذِ الْتَقَيْتُمْ فِي أَعْيُنِكُمْ قَلِيلًا

wa-idh yurīkumūhum idhi iltaqaytum fī aʿyunikum qalīlan

“When, at your encounter, He showed them to you as few in your eyes.” (Al-Anfāl 8:44)

Al-Anfāl 8:44 explicitly describes seeing during the encounter “in your eyes”; Al-Anfāl 8:43 uses “in your sleep.”

The difference supports the sleep reading.

The alternative recorded in lexical tradition should nevertheless remain acknowledged.

Does every use of sleep involve a dream?

The last two verses concern seeing something in sleep.

That does not make every sentence with manām concern dreaming. A person may be asleep without the sentence saying anything about a dream experience.

Translation should therefore preserve the difference between sleep and dreams.

“In sleep” is appropriate in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:102 and Al-Anfāl 8:43; context establishes the dream experience.

In Al-Rūm 30:23, “your dreams by night and day” would unnecessarily restrict the meaning.

6. Sleep, taking the self, and the distinction from death

Al-Zumar 39:42

اللَّهُ يَتَوَفَّى الْأَنْفُسَ حِينَ مَوْتِهَا وَالَّتِي لَمْ تَمُتْ فِي مَنَامِهَا فَيُمْسِكُ الَّتِي قَضَىٰ عَلَيْهَا الْمَوْتَ وَيُرْسِلُ الْأُخْرَىٰ إِلَىٰ أَجَلٍ مُسَمًّى إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَآيَاتٍ لِقَوْمٍ يَتَفَكَّرُونَ

allāhu yatawaffā al-anfusa ḥīna mawtihā wa-allatī lam tamut fī manāmihā fa-yumsiku allatī qaḍā ʿalayhā al-mawta wa-yursilu al-ukhrā ilā ajalin musamman inna fī dhālika la-āyātin li-qawmin yatafakkarūna

“The God takes the selves at their death, and those that have not died during their sleep. He retains those for whom He has decreed death and releases the others until an appointed term. In this are signs for a people who reflect.”

This is an important Qur’anic use of manām.

The word concerns sleep, not a particular dream.

The verse distinguishes selves taken at death from those that have not died but whose sleep relates to tawaffī.

Allatī lam tamut matters: it explicitly concerns those who have not died.

Sleep and death are therefore not identified, although both relate to yatawaffā.

This prevents translating manām as death or claiming that everyone literally dies during sleep in the same sense as at life’s end.

Their shared relation to tawaffī should also remain. The Qur’an relates sleep, the self, and its being taken, retained, or released.

The nature of that relation, the self’s location, separation or return, and a bodily or biological mechanism cannot be extracted from N W M.

A detailed study must examine W F Y, N F S, and M W T independently as well.

Study of W F Y

Complementary evidence: Al-Anʿām 6:60

Al-Anʿām 6:60 says:

وَهُوَ الَّذِي يَتَوَفَّاكُمْ بِاللَّيْلِ وَيَعْلَمُ مَا جَرَحْتُمْ بِالنَّهَارِ ثُمَّ يَبْعَثُكُمْ فِيهِ لِيُقْضَىٰ أَجَلٌ مُسَمًّى

wa-huwa alladhī yatawaffākum bi-al-layli wa-yaʿlamu mā jaraḥtum bi-al-nahāri thumma yabʿathukum fīhi li-yuqḍā ajalun musamman

“He is the One who takes you by night and knows what you have done by day, then raises you in it so that an appointed term may be completed.”

Nawm itself does not occur, but context concerns nighttime sleep and returning to daytime activity.

Read with Al-Zumar 39:42, the verse relates sleep and wakefulness to human life, its continuation, and an appointed term.

This does not establish a definite physical model of the self leaving the body and spatially returning; the verses do not specify those details.

7. Nāʾimūn: being asleep when something happens

Two further occurrences use the plural active participle nāʾimūn.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:97: sleep and being caught unaware

أَفَأَمِنَ أَهْلُ الْقُرَىٰ أَنْ يَأْتِيَهُمْ بَأْسُنَا بَيَاتًا وَهُمْ نَائِمُونَ

a-fa-amina ahlu al-qurā an yaʾtiyahum baʾsunā bayātan wa-hum nāʾimūna

“Do the people of the settlements feel secure that Our severity will not come upon them by night while they sleep?”

Nāʾimūn describes their condition when the event occurs.

Wa-hum nāʾimūn is a circumstantial clause: they are sleeping when the severity arrives.

The next verse, Al-Aʿrāf 7:98, says:

أَوَأَمِنَ أَهْلُ الْقُرَىٰ أَنْ يَأْتِيَهُمْ بَأْسُنَا ضُحًى وَهُمْ يَلْعَبُونَ

a-wa-amina ahlu al-qurā an yaʾtiyahum baʾsunā ḍuḥan wa-hum yalʿabūna

“Or do the people of the settlements feel secure that Our severity will not come upon them in daylight while they play?” (Al-Aʿrāf 7:98)

The contrast presents two everyday situations: sleeping at night and being occupied with play by day.

Sleep may create the setting for being caught unaware, but nāʾimūn itself does not mean moral heedlessness.

Turning it into “those heedless of truth” requires evidence beyond the word.

Al-Qalam 68:19: the garden owners’ sleep

فَطَافَ عَلَيْهَا طَائِفٌ مِنْ رَبِّكَ وَهُمْ نَائِمُونَ

fa-ṭāfa ʿalayhā ṭāʾifun min rabbika wa-hum nāʾimūna

“An encompassing visitor from your Lord passed over it while they slept.”

This belongs to the garden owners’ story.

In the story, they plan to harvest the garden in the morning; their later conversation also speaks of excluding the needy.

While they sleep, an event overtakes the garden, whose condition has changed by morning.

Nāʾimūn again means that they are asleep.

Contextual reflection may discuss their unawareness of the nighttime event or their decision’s outcome. The word itself does not establish spiritual sleep or doctrinal ignorance.

Neither verse presents sleep itself as blameworthy. In al-Aʿrāf it is the people’s condition when the event arrives; in al-Qalam it is the owners’ condition as the garden’s fate changes.

8. Distinctions from related roots

N W M and W S N: sleep and drowsiness

Al-Baqarah 2:255 says:

لَا تَأْخُذُهُ سِنَةٌ وَلَا نَوْمٌ

lā taʾkhudhuhu sinatun wa-lā nawmun

“Neither drowsiness nor sleep overtakes Him.”

Sinah belongs to W S N; lexical explanations associate it with the onset of sleep, dozing, and drowsiness.

Sinah and nawm express nearby but distinct states.

One interpretive explanation likewise gives nuʿās for sinah and deeper sleep for nawm.

The verse denies both of The God.

Within “the Living, the Sustainer,” this negation relates to life and the continuous sustaining and preservation of creation.

It does not make human sleep a moral failing. Al-Furqān 25:47 and Al-Nabaʾ 78:9 present sleep as part of human life’s arrangement.

A condition can thus be natural and useful for humans while denied of The God, without nawm changing lexical meaning.

N W M and N ʿ S: sleep and dozing

Nuʿās also occurs in the Qur’an.

Āl ʿImrān 3:154 says:

ثُمَّ أَنْزَلَ عَلَيْكُمْ مِنْ بَعْدِ الْغَمِّ أَمَنَةً نُعَاسًا

thumma anzala ʿalaykum min baʿdi al-ghammi amanatan nuʿāsan

“After distress, He sent down upon you reassurance accompanied by drowsiness.”

Al-Anfāl 8:11 likewise uses النُّعَاسَ أَمَنَةً مِنْهُ.

Both associate drowsiness with security and reassurance amid anxiety and tension.

This is close to nawm, but does not express all sleep’s features in precisely the same way.

Nor does it establish that every doze signifies security or every sleep psychological calm. Those features belong to the particular contexts.

Study of N ʿ S

N W M and R Q D: sleeping and resting

Al-Kahf 18:18 says:

وَتَحْسَبُهُمْ أَيْقَاظًا وَهُمْ رُقُودٌ

wa-taḥsabuhum ayqāẓan wa-hum ruqūdun

“You would think them awake, while they are resting asleep.”

Ruqūd belongs to R Q D.

It concerns sleeping and lying at rest. The Companions of the Cave’s appearance contrasts with their actual condition.

Ruqūd and nawm remain separate words. Their shared reference to sleep does not identify their entire semantic ranges.

The story also mentions raising them and their discussion of how long they remained. Those connections belong to a separate study of the narrative.

N W M and R ʾ Y: sleep and what is seen in sleep

Nawm names sleep itself.

Ruʾyā concerns what someone sees, especially during sleep.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:102 and Al-Ṣāffāt 37:105 make the relationship clear: Abraham sees something in manām and that experience is then called ruʾyā.

Sleep and dreaming may occur together but are not complete synonyms.

The difference is important for translating manām.

Study of R ʾ Y

N W M and GH F L: bodily sleep and heedlessness

Heedlessness concerns inattention and unawareness; nawm denotes sleeping.

Arabic may metaphorically use sleep for heedlessness, as in نَامَ عَنْ حَاجَتِهِ.

In the Qur’anic occurrences of nāʾimūn, however, clear contextual evidence concerns bodily sleep.

The similarity between unawareness during sleep and moral inattention should not become one definition for both roots.

Study of GH F L

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

“Sleep” is the principal equivalent for nawm. It is natural and sufficient in Al-Furqān 25:47, Al-Nabaʾ 78:9, and Al-Baqarah 2:255.

Context must determine manām.

Manāmukum in Al-Rūm 30:23 is “your sleep”; fī manāmihā in Al-Zumar 39:42 is “during their sleep.”

Fī manāmika in Al-Anfāl 8:43 is “in your sleep,” and fī al-manām in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:102 is “in sleep.” In both, seeing establishes the experience of a dream.

Nāʾimūn in Al-Aʿrāf 7:97 and Al-Qalam 68:19 is appropriately “asleep” or “they are sleeping.”

A lexical explanation of manām can introduce sleep and what is seen during sleep, but translation should not automatically substitute one for the other.

Sinah must remain distinct from nawm in Al-Baqarah 2:255: “drowsiness” or “dozing” for the former, “sleep” for the latter.

10. Conclusion

N W M concerns sleeping and sleep. The lexical definition cited earlier centers on movement subsiding and stillness; calming wind, subsiding fire, a sluggish market, and trusting another belong to this semantic field.

These extensions are recorded, but biologically sleep should not be defined as complete bodily stillness or the cessation of awareness. Figurative uses should not change every verse’s direct meaning.

In the Qur’an, nawm names sleep, nāʾimūn describes sleeping people, and manām concerns sleep itself or the experience of seeing something in sleep, according to context.

The Qur’an relates sleep to repose, night and day, dreams, and continued life. Al-Zumar 39:42 connects sleep with taking the self while distinguishing it from death. Al-Baqarah 2:255 denies sleep and drowsiness of The God, while human sleep is presented as natural and a sign of creation’s arrangement.

A usable definition for Forqan is therefore:

N W M denotes sleeping and being asleep, when ordinary activity and interaction with the environment change. Figurative Arabic uses also express calm, subsiding activity, or its cessation. Qur’anic manām sometimes denotes sleep itself and sometimes the setting for a dream; context determines the precise meaning.
