1. Word Meaning and Structure

Arabic ḍifdiʿ (ضِفْدِع) names the animal called a frog. Its established plural is ḍafādiʿ (ضَفَادِع), the form the Qur’an uses in reporting the signs sent against Pharaoh’s people.

Frogs are closely associated with water and moist environments. Many species spend part of their lives in water and part on land. Swimming, jumping, and calling are familiar features of these animals.

Australian Museum: frog life cycles and characteristics

In Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Jawharī records الضِّفْدِعُ مِثَالُ الْخِنْصِرِ: the singular follows the vocalization of khinṣir (خِنْصِر). He then identifies it as the singular of ḍafādiʿ.

Lisān al-ʿArab records both ḍifdiʿ and ḍafdaʿ as eloquent forms, with ḍifdiʿah and ḍafdaʿah as feminine forms.

Different vowels in certain dialects do not therefore name different animals. In their principal use, the forms refer to the same familiar creature.

Qur’anic morphological indexing classifies Ḍ F D ʿ (ض ف د ع) as quadriliteral. Unlike Z L Z L or W S W S, its four letters do not repeat a pair.

More importantly, ḍifdiʿ is an animal name, rather than a word whose clearly established original verbal meaning can be shown to have produced the animal’s name.

Separate meanings should not be assigned to its four letters, nor should the frog’s name be derived without evidence from jumping, swimming, calling, or hiding in water.

These may be real biological features, but biological characteristics differ from a name’s historical origin.

Arabic uses other words for a frog’s call, such as naqīq and naqnaqah. Their existence does not establish that ḍifdiʿ originated as imitation of that call.

The family occurs only once in the Qur’an, in the plural al-ḍafādiʿ. Its Qur’anic meaning should therefore be examined first through the established animal name and the verse’s context.

2. Linguistic Forms and Early Arabic Evidence

Ḍifdiʿ, Ḍafādiʿ, and Ḍafādī

Lexical sources record several forms.

Ḍifdiʿ is the familiar singular; ḍafdaʿ is also accepted as an eloquent form. Ḍifdaʿ, with kasrah on ḍād and fatḥah on dāl, is reported too, but Al-Khalīl and others reject it, and some dictionaries call it rare or inadmissible. The recorded vocalizations are not all equally accepted.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Lisān al-ʿArab, and Al-Miṣbāḥ: vocalization variants

Ḍifdiʿah and ḍafdaʿah are recorded as feminine forms.

Ḍafādiʿ is the established broken plural and the Qur’anic form.

Certain witnesses also use ḍafādī (ضَفَادِي). Al-Azharī explains this as replacing the plural’s final ʿayn with yāʾ, comparable to arānī beside arānib in some Arabic uses.

The uncommon variant should not be presented as the principal or Qur’anic plural. The Qur’an uses ḍafādiʿ.

Arabic also records the verb ḍafdaʿa. Ḍafdaʿa al-māʾu means that frogs appeared in the water or that the water had frogs. The following poetic description muḍafdiʿāt is explained as waters abundant in frogs; abundance should not become an obligatory, independent meaning of every verbal use.

Ḍafdaʿa al-rajulu also describes a person drawing himself together or contracting, with other bodily actions recorded in some accounts.

The verbs do not occur in the Qur’an, and should not all be made the source of the animal name without evidence.

A verb concerning frogs becoming abundant in water may have been formed from the animal name, rather than the name arising from the verb.

Chronology and direction of derivation between ḍifdiʿ and related verbs therefore require caution.

A Witness from Labīd ibn Rabīʿah

An important witness appears in Labīd ibn Rabīʿah’s poetry:

يَمَّمْنَ أَعْدَادًا بِلُبْنَى أَوْ أَجَا
مُضَفْدِعَاتٍ كُلُّهَا مُطَحْلِبَهْ

yammamna aʿdādan bi-lubnā aw ajā / muḍafdiʿātin kulluhā muṭaḥlibah

“They headed toward watering places at Lubnā or Ajā, full of frogs and all covered in algae.”

Muḍafdiʿāt describes waters abundant in frogs.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: Labīd’s witness and waters full of frogs

Muṭaḥlibah likewise describes water on which algae have grown.

The witness matters for several reasons.

First, the frog’s name occurs in an environmental description: waters characterized by many frogs.

Second, muḍafdiʿāt shows Arabic forming from the animal name a description of its habitat.

Third, the use is independent of the Qur’anic account of the signs sent against Pharaoh’s people. Labīd’s frogs belong to an aquatic scene; punishment and divine sign are not meanings of the word itself.

Labīd lived across the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods. The lexical quotation does not establish this verse’s exact date. It attests early usage, but should not be declared certainly earlier than revelation without further examination.

The Frog’s Call in Arabic Expressions

Arabic calls the frog’s sound naqīq (نَقيق), with naqqa (نَقَّ) for its calling.

Naqnaqah (نَقْنَقَة) also concerns the repeated call of a frog.

Lisān al-ʿArab records naqqat ḍafādiʿu baṭnihi (نَقَّتْ ضَفَادِعُ بَطْنِهِ), literally “the frogs of his belly croaked.”

The expression concerns a hungry person, comparing the hungry belly’s sound with frog calls.

It does not mean that frogs are actually inside the person. The image rests on a similarity in sound.

The frog thus appears in literary images and everyday expressions as well as in its ordinary animal sense.

Naqīq belongs to another lexical entry; the frog’s call should not replace the meaning of ḍifdiʿ itself.

Frog names the animal; naqīq names its call.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab: naqīq and naqnaqah

Other Uses of the Name Ḍifdiʿ

Some early sources also record ḍifdiʿ for a part on the underside of a horse’s hoof. Historical anatomical terminology should not be identified directly with modern veterinary distinctions between tissues.

Here, a part of the hoof bears the same name as the frog.

Other languages likewise sometimes apply animal names to parts of animals’ bodies.

The naming alone proves neither that the frog’s name came from hoof anatomy nor the reverse.

Later sources also use ḍifdiʿ for certain stars. Those uses concern the history of astronomical naming, rather than the Qur’anic word’s meaning.

Lane’s citations of early sources: hunger, hoof terminology, and star names

The Qur’an supplies no indication that al-ḍafādiʿ refers to a horse’s body part, a star name, or a literary image.

The context, its company with tangible phenomena, and the established noun all point to actual frogs.

Possible Relationships in Semitic Languages

Biblical Hebrew uses צְפַרְדֵּעַ for a frog. Aramaic also has names for the same animal with some sounds comparable to the Arabic form.

Comparative dictionaries place these beside Arabic ḍifdiʿ.

The proximity can matter for word history, but does not prove a direct Arabic borrowing from Hebrew or an identical quadriliteral structure behind every form.

The names differ across languages, and reconstructing their history requires careful examination of sound changes.

Similarity alone therefore does not determine ḍifdiʿ’s origin with certainty.

Brown–Driver–Briggs: the Hebrew frog name and Semitic comparisons

The issue is not decisive for the Qur’anic use, since the animal meaning is clear and independently attested in Arabic.

3. Qur’anic Use in Al-Aʿrāf 7:133

Ḍ F D ʿ occurs only once in the Qur’an, in Al-Aʿrāf.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the sole occurrence in Al-Aʿrāf 7:133

Al-Aʿrāf 7:133

فَأَرْسَلْنَا عَلَيْهِمُ الطُّوفَانَ وَالْجَرَادَ وَالْقُمَّلَ وَالضَّفَادِعَ وَالدَّمَ آيَاتٍ مُفَصَّلَاتٍ فَاسْتَكْبَرُوا وَكَانُوا قَوْمًا مُجْرِمِينَ

fa-arsalnā ʿalayhimu al-ṭūfāna wa-al-jarāda wa-al-qummala wa-al-ḍafādiʿa wa-al-dama āyātin mufaṣṣalātin fa-stakbarū wa-kānū qawman mujrimīna

Translation quoted in this study, rendered here in English:

“So We sent against them the flood, locusts, lice, frogs, and blood as distinct signs; yet they grew arrogant and were a criminal people.”

The Grammatical Position of Wa-l-Ḍafādiʿa

Wa-l-ḍafādiʿa comprises the conjunction wa-, the definite article al-, and the broken plural ḍafādiʿ. Its singular is ḍifdiʿ; the technical grammatical label “collective noun” does not accurately identify this form.

Al-ḍafādiʿa is accusative, among the nouns following arsalnā, “We sent.”

Al-ṭūfāna comes first; al-jarāda, al-qummala, al-ḍafādiʿa, and al-dama are coordinated with it.

Frogs are therefore one of the things the sentence presents as sent against the stated people.

The definite article is part of the Qur’anic form. Definiteness does not establish a particular biological frog species.

A definite Arabic plural can refer to animals of a familiar kind without specifying their scientific name or exact species.

The direct meaning of al-ḍafādiʿa is therefore “the frogs.”

Frogs beside the Other Signs

The verse lists five items: ṭūfān, locusts, qummal, frogs, and blood.

They are not all of one kind.

Ṭūfān concerns a widespread phenomenon or event; locusts and qummal name animals; frogs are familiar animals, and blood is a different material.

The list therefore does not merely name several diseases, insects, or natural events of one type.

Biologically, frogs differ from locusts and lice: those are insects, while frogs are amphibians. Identifying qummal as lice is nevertheless only one lexical and exegetical explanation; its precise referent requires separate examination.

Al-Ṭabarī: differing identifications of qummal and the signs’ context

Al-ḍafādiʿ should not become an insect or small pest merely because it accompanies insects in the list.

Nor should every item be presumed in advance to share one natural mechanism.

The Qur’an presents them as signs sent against the stated people.

Āyātin mufaṣṣalātin expresses clear, distinct signs. The phrase alone does not specify the precise interval between events, their durations, or their entire historical sequence.

Ḍafādiʿ names the frogs; their role as a divine sign comes from the sentence and its context.

Why Are Frogs a Sign of Affliction Here?

In ordinary life, frogs have a role within their natural environments.

Their presence in ponds, rivers, and wetlands does not itself indicate punishment.

They may help control certain insect populations and belong to an ecosystem’s natural relationships.

Australian Museum: adult frogs feeding on insects

An animal’s presence in unusual circumstances, particularly in large numbers or over a wide area, can nevertheless disrupt human life and comfort.

Many animals entering living spaces, disrupted access to surroundings, or major ecological changes can create difficulties.

Such examples clarify the difference between an animal’s natural presence and a distressing event.

Verse 133 does not say exactly where the frogs appeared, how many there were, or what kind of harm they caused.

Details such as every house filling with frogs, frogs entering food vessels, changed water composition, or widespread animal deaths should therefore not be presented as explicit information in this verse.

Some other accounts of Moses include such details, but this Qur’anic verse does not state them.

What is clear is that frogs are among the signs sent against Pharaoh’s people and connected in the continuing narrative with requests for relief from affliction.

Connection with the Preceding Verses

Al-Aʿrāf 7:130 speaks of Pharaoh’s people experiencing years of scarcity and reduced crops.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:131 describes their response to good and hardship: prosperity was treated as their own, while misfortune led them to regard Moses and his companions as bad omens.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:132 records their declaration that no sign would make them believe Moses.

Verse 133 then lists the signs sent against them.

The sequence places frogs within Moses’ confrontation with Pharaoh’s power and the people’s leaders’ response to divine signs, rather than in an independent zoological report.

The verse ends with fa-stakbarū wa-kānū qawman mujrimīna.

It judges the conduct and moral condition of those people, rather than the frogs.

The frog’s inclusion among afflictions does not establish that the animal is inherently impure, evil, or blameworthy.

Human moral conduct and an animal’s role in an event are separate matters.

Requests for Relief and Broken Promises

Al-Aʿrāf 7:134 records that, when affliction strikes, they ask Moses to call upon his Lord. They promise belief and the release of the Children of Israel with him if the hardship is lifted.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:135 states that they broke their promise after the affliction was lifted.

These verses place the earlier signs, including frogs, within hardship, appeals for release, and a return to past conduct.

The continuation does not specify the frog event’s precise nature.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:134 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:135 do not, for example, state exactly how many days the frogs remained or where they went after relief came.

Those questions require independent historical and natural information.

Al-Ṭabarī: requests for relief and the limits of the reported details

For the root study, what matters is identifying the animal name and its contextual role in the account.

4. Differences from Related Words

Ḍifdiʿ and Jarād

Arabic jarād names locusts.

Locusts are insects; certain species can move in vast numbers and damage crops.

Ḍifdiʿ names a frog, an amphibian associated with water and moist habitats.

The words appear in one list in Al-Aʿrāf 7:133, but that combination makes them neither biologically nor lexically identical.

Ḍafādiʿ should therefore not be translated as “insects,” “locusts,” or “flying pests.”

Ḍifdiʿ and Qummal

Qummal also occurs in verse 133.

Lexical and exegetical sources offer different identifications of its precise referent.

That disagreement should not be transferred to ḍafādiʿ.

The frog has an independently established Arabic name with clear linguistic evidence.

Uncertainty about the kind of qummal does not justify changing ḍafādiʿ’s meaning.

Ḍifdiʿ and Naqīq

Naqīq names the frog’s call.

It belongs to N Q Q and is associated with the verb naqqa.

A frog can produce naqīq, but animal and call names are not synonymous.

The distinction also matters for word origin: an imagined similarity between frog calls and ḍifdiʿ does not establish a certain etymology.

Ḍifdiʿ and Amphibians

Modern biological classification places frogs among amphibians.

Amphibian is a broader group that also includes other animals.

Translating ḍafādiʿ as “amphibians” is therefore too general.

Traditional boundaries between names for frogs and neighboring animals such as toads have not always matched modern scientific classification.

“Frogs” is a clear, suitable Qur’anic equivalent, but the name alone does not identify the precise scientific species in the historical event.

Ḍifdiʿ and Figurative Uses

Arabic uses the frog’s name for a hungry belly’s sound and in certain other expressions.

Such uses concern similarities in sound, appearance, or behavior.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:133 gives no indication that al-ḍafādiʿ means belly sounds, vibration, psychological disturbance, or an entirely abstract phenomenon.

The tangible, direct animal meaning therefore takes precedence over an unsupported figurative reading.

5. Conclusion and Translation Proposals

Ḍ F D ʿ is a quadriliteral Arabic family whose principal familiar word, ḍifdiʿ, means frog.

Several singular vocalizations are recorded; the principal plural is ḍafādiʿ.

Some lexical uses form verbs or descriptions from the animal name, such as muḍafdiʿāt in Labīd’s verse for waters full of frogs.

The frog’s name is clear and independent in the linguistic evidence. Other words, such as naqīq, express its call; the animal name should not be derived from that call without evidence.

Other Semitic languages have comparable frog names, but the similarities do not alone establish the word’s definite origin or historical route.

The Qur’an uses Ḍ F D ʿ only once, as al-ḍafādiʿa in Al-Aʿrāf 7:133.

It accompanies ṭūfān, locusts, qummal, and blood, naming one of the signs sent against Pharaoh’s people.

The context connects the signs with hardship, requests for relief, and broken promises.

Ḍafādiʿ itself means neither punishment nor deception, disease, or corruption.

It names animals whose presence in that event formed part of the signs described in the Qur’an.

Brief root meaning: Frog; the familiar amphibian associated with water and moist environments.

Meaning of Qur’anic wa-l-ḍafādiʿa (وَالضَّفَادِعَ): And the frogs; animals listed among the signs sent against Pharaoh’s people.

Proposed translation: “And the frogs.”

The full translation of Al-Aʿrāf 7:133 should preserve this direct equivalent, without adding abundance, entry into houses, or mechanisms of harm to the word itself.

Such information, if reliably supported, may be discussed separately in historical examination or reflection on the verse.

The central conclusion is that Qur’anic al-ḍafādiʿ names frogs; their role as a sign and affliction comes from context, rather than the animal name’s lexical meaning.

The distinction allows the Qur’anic text to be understood without imposing added stories, unproven scientific hypotheses, or unsupported figurative meanings.
