Foundational Meaning

The root ز ل ل fundamentally points to slipping, losing stability, and sliding away from a firm place. Its basic image is a foot that should remain steady on the ground, but suddenly its connection to the stable place weakens and it slips from its position.

This root does not yet indicate a complete fall. It more specifically marks the moment when balance is lost and departure from stability begins.

Qur'anic Forms of the Root

The Qur'anic forms of this root are limited: زَلَل, أَزَلَّ, and اسْتَزَلَّ.

The simple verb زَلَّ points to the act of slipping itself. أَزَلَّ, in Form IV, means to make someone slip or to become the cause of slipping. اسْتَزَلَّ, in Form X, is a causing-to-slip that happens through seeking out, provoking, or exploiting a field of weakness: someone or something finds the point of slipping and makes the actor slip from there.

Linguistic Note

The linguistic center of ز ل ل is the breaking of the connection between a foot and a stable place. In the material sense, the foot does not hold on the ground or slides away from its place.

In the ethical and Qur'anic sense, the human being leaves a position where he should have remained firm: after clear signs, after covenant, after establishment, or at the moment of testing.

Material Image of the Root

The material image of this root is a foot on a surface where either the actor loses balance, or an outside factor makes him slip from the firm place.

This is where it differs from ز ل ق. Zalaq relates more to the quality of the surface: ground that is smooth, slippery, and without friction, as in صَعِيدًا زَلَقًا. Zalal relates more to the slipping of the foot or actor: the moment when the foot, despite a prior condition of stability, comes out of control.

Older and Classical Arabic Usage

In classical Arabic usage, zalal is connected with the slipping of the foot, error, and leaving a firm condition. From the same field, زَلَّة is used for a behavioral slip or mistake that moves a person out of the proper place.

In a final version, a reliable witness from early Arabic poetry or old usage may be added if one is found.

Adam and His Spouse Being Made to Slip

The first Qur'anic use of this root appears in the story of Adam and his spouse:

فَأَزَلَّهُمَا الشَّيْطَانُ عَنْهَا فَأَخْرَجَهُمَا مِمَّا كَانَا فِيهِ

Then Satan made the two of them slip from it, and brought them out from what they had been in. Al-Baqarah 2:36

In this verse, the verb is in Form IV: أَزَلَّهُمَا. Satan was not merely a witness to the slip; he caused it. The preposition عَنْ also matters: عَنْهَا means from that place, condition, or boundary.

So the slip here is not a small error with no effect. It is a slip that leads to departure from the previous state: فَأَخْرَجَهُمَا مِمَّا كَانَا فِيهِ.

Slipping after Clear Signs

The second use concerns slipping after clear signs have come:

فَإِن زَلَلْتُم مِّن بَعْدِ مَا جَاءَتْكُمُ الْبَيِّنَاتُ فَاعْلَمُوا أَنَّ اللَّهَ عَزِيزٌ حَكِيمٌ

So if you slip after the clear signs have come to you, then know that The God is Mighty, Wise. Al-Baqarah 2:209

Here zalal happens after the path has become clear. This gives the verse weight. Slipping in darkness is one matter; slipping after bayyinat is another. Once the way has become clear, the slip is no longer mere not-knowing. It is weakness in the very position that should have become firmer after seeing clear signs.

A Foot Slipping after Firmness

The third use keeps the material image of the root direct:

وَلَا تَتَّخِذُوا أَيْمَانَكُمْ دَخَلًا بَيْنَكُمْ فَتَزِلَّ قَدَمٌ بَعْدَ ثُبُوتِهَا

And do not take your oaths as a means of deception among yourselves, lest a foot slip after its firmness. An-Nahl 16:94

Here the subject is covenant, oath, and social trust. When oaths become tools of deception, the stable foot of society slips. The phrase بَعْدَ ثُبُوتِهَا is precise: the problem is not merely moving on weak ground. The problem is that something had become firm, but through deception and abuse of covenant, its firmness was lost.

Istizlal in the Event of Uhud

The fourth use appears in the event of Uhud:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ تَوَلَّوْا مِنكُمْ يَوْمَ الْتَقَى الْجَمْعَانِ إِنَّمَا اسْتَزَلَّهُمُ الشَّيْطَانُ بِبَعْضِ مَا كَسَبُوا

Indeed, those among you who turned away on the day the two groups met, Satan only made them slip because of some of what they had earned. Aal-Imran 3:155

Here اسْتَزَلَّهُم appears. This form shows that Satan did not create the slip from nothing. He used some of what they themselves had earned. In their inner state or prior behavior, there was a point of slipping. Istazalla in this context means that Satan found that weak point and disturbed their balance from there.

Distinguishing Similar Uses

These four uses should be distinguished. In Al-Baqarah 2:36, Satan made Adam and his spouse slip from their previous place, and the result was departure. In Al-Baqarah 2:209, slipping after clear signs is the issue. In An-Nahl 16:94, a foot slips after firmness in the space of oath and social trust. In Aal-Imran 3:155, slipping occurs through the exploitation of what people had previously earned.

All of these involve the loss of stability, but their level and cause are not the same.

Grammatical and Structural Notes

The preposition and verbal form matter in this root. أَزَلَّهُمَا عَنْهَا shows being made to slip away from a place. زَلَلْتُم مِّن بَعْدِ مَا جَاءَتْكُمُ الْبَيِّنَاتُ shows slipping after clarity and completion of proof.

فَتَزِلَّ قَدَمٌ بَعْدَ ثُبُوتِهَا joins firmness and slipping in one image. اسْتَزَلَّهُمُ الشَّيْطَانُ بِبَعْضِ مَا كَسَبُوا shows that slipping can be activated through a person's prior weak points.

Difference from Nearby Roots

ز ل ق relates more to a slippery surface: ground where the foot cannot hold. Zalal relates more to the slipping of the foot or actor. Falling is the stage of collapse and descent; zalal may lead to falling, but it is itself the beginning of sliding away from stability.

Dalal is losing the way and direction. A person may walk with firm steps on a wrong path, but zalal means that in the place where he should have remained firm, his foot slipped. Khata' points to error, but zalal has a more bodily and precise image: sliding away from a firm place. Zawal is the passing away or displacement of a state, while zalal is the moment of slipping from stability.

Translation Suggestions for Forqan

Zalal may be translated, depending on context, as "to slip," "to lose stability," "to slide from a firm place," or "to fall into a slip." أَزَلَّهُمَا is best rendered "he made the two of them slip" or "he made the two of them slip from that place."

اسْتَزَلَّهُم may be rendered "he drew them into slipping" or "he made them slip through their point of weakness." فَتَزِلَّ قَدَمٌ بَعْدَ ثُبُوتِهَا is best translated while preserving the image of the foot: "lest a foot slip after its firmness."

Linguistic Summary

The root ز ل ل points to slipping, losing stability, and sliding away from a firm place. Its material image is a foot whose connection to stable ground is lost.

In the Qur'an, this root appears for Adam and his spouse slipping from their previous place, slipping after clear signs, a foot slipping after firmness in the field of covenant, and Satan making people slip by exploiting what they had earned. Its difference from zalaq is that zalaq is more the quality of the slippery surface, while zalal is the actor's own slip from a position of stability.
