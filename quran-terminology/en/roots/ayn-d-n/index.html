1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

The Arabic root ع د ن (ʿayn dāl nūn) is associated with residence, settling and remaining in a place. عَدَنَ (ʿadana) is used of someone or something that settles somewhere and makes it a place of residence. The same family includes مَعْدِن (maʿdin), a place where a substance such as gold or silver is found and lodged.

In the Qur’an, this family appears only as عَدْن (ʿadn), always in جَنَّات عَدْن (jannāt ʿadn); the grammatical case of jannāt varies between passages. The expression concerns gardens promised as a place of lasting residence.

Forms examined:

  • عَدْن (ʿadn): residence and remaining in a place; the family’s only Qur’anic form, occurring in eleven verses.
  • عَدَنَ، يَعْدِنُ / يَعْدُنُ (ʿadana, yaʿdinu / yaʿdunu): resided, remained or settled somewhere. This verb does not occur in the Qur’an.
  • عَادِن (ʿādin): a resident or someone remaining in a place. This form does not occur in the Qur’an.
  • مَعْدِن (maʿdin): a place where a substance is lodged and found in the earth; also a location or source of something. This word does not occur in the Qur’an.
  • مَعَادِن (maʿādin): the recognized Arabic plural of maʿdin, absent from the Qur’an.

ʿAdn occurs in eleven verses across eleven surahs: Al-Tawbah 9:72, Al-Raʿd 13:23, Al-Naḥl 16:31, Al-Kahf 18:31, Maryam 19:61, Ṭā Hā 20:76, Fāṭir 35:33, Ṣād 38:50, Ghāfir 40:8, Al-Ṣaff 61:12 and Al-Bayyinah 98:8.

In every occurrence, ʿadn accompanies جَنَّات (jannāt), “gardens.” The Qur’an does not use this family’s verb of residing or the word maʿdin. Its Qur’anic use is therefore limited and consistent, although the wider lexical family is more extensive.

2. The semantic core and older Arabic usage

Residence and remaining in a particular place

Arabic lexicons record عَدَنَ (ʿadana) for residing in a place. عَدَنَ بِالْمَكَانِ (ʿadana bi-l-makān) means to reside there, while عَدَنْتُ الْبَلَدَ (ʿadantu al-balad) means making that land one’s home and place of residence.

Older lexical accounts also record this root in descriptions of camels. عَدَنَتِ الْإِبِلُ (ʿadanat al-ibil) describes camels remaining in a pasture without leaving it. One older explanation associates it with their remaining in pasture containing salt-tolerant plants.

These uses clarify the meaning of residence in ع د ن. Remaining in a pasture, living in a land and settling in a particular place all belong to this semantic field, although a dictionary report does not establish the precise date when each use arose.

Recording an expression in an older lexicon does not by itself date its origin. The account of camels remaining in pasture is evidence that this meaning was recognized in Arabic lexical tradition, but without independent evidence it cannot be assigned to a particular pre-Islamic verse or a specific date before the Qur’an.

A poem attributed to al-Aʿshā, a poet of the late pre-Islamic period, uses this root for firmness and rootedness in describing someone’s forbearance:

وَإِنْ يَسْتَضِيفُوا إِلَى حِلْمِهِ
يُضَافُوا إِلَى رَاجِحٍ قَدْ عَدَنْ

“If they turn to his forbearance, they are joined to a weighty, firmly established [man].”

Here قَدْ عَدَنْ (qad ʿadan) describes the person’s firmness and rootedness; the lexical explanation likewise emphasizes composure and not being easily unsettled. This connects physical settling with firmness of character, rather than speaking of gardens in the Hereafter.

The verse is also transmitted with حُكْمِهِ (ḥukmihi), “his judgment,” instead of حِلْمِهِ (ḥilmihi), “his forbearance.” Attribution to al-Aʿshā provides an older poetic witness but does not by itself establish the precise date of this particular verse.

Remaining rather than merely passing through

ʿAdn concerns residence rather than temporary passage. Someone may enter a place, remain for a time and then leave, but using ʿadn to characterize a place of habitation draws attention to settling and remaining there.

This lexical meaning alone does not establish that every Arabic use of the root must signify eternal, unending residence. Remaining in a pasture or a land may be lengthy and stable without necessarily being everlasting.

The distinction matters because some Qur’anic passages place jannāt ʿadn alongside the explicit expressions خَالِدِينَ فِيهَا (khālidīna fīhā), “abiding therein,” and أَبَدًا (abadan), “forever.” These expressions clarify unending permanence; their contribution should not disappear into the definition of ʿadn.

Maʿdin: a place where something is lodged

مَعْدِن (maʿdin) is a recognized derivative of this family. Arabic uses it for the natural location of gold, silver and other minerals.

Lexical explanations connect it with settling in two ways: sometimes with the place where a substance is lodged and remains, and sometimes with a location where people have taken up residence.

The word can also refer more broadly to the source of a quality. For example, “a maʿdin of goodness and generosity” describes a person or place in which goodness and generosity are rooted and recognized.

This use shows the family extending from remaining in a place to something being established in its original or recognized location.

Maʿdin must nevertheless not be identified with jannāt ʿadn. Their lexical relationship does not establish that the gardens are underground, contain mineral deposits, or have the structure of a mine. They are different uses of one linguistic family.

ʿAdan as a geographical name

عَدَن (ʿAdan, Aden) is also the recognized name of a city in the southern Arabian Peninsula. This geographical name should be distinguished from عَدْن (ʿadn) in the Qur’anic expression جَنَّاتُ عَدْنٍ (jannātu ʿadnin).

Similarity of letters and the family’s association with residence do not establish that these gardens are connected with the city, or that the city’s name explains the Qur’anic expression.

The Qur’an itself states no connection between jannāt ʿadn and that city.

3. Jannāt ʿadn: residence or a proper name?

جَنَّاتُ عَدْنٍ (jannātu ʿadnin) consists of جَنَّات (jannāt), the plural of جَنَّة (jannah), and عَدْن (ʿadn), its genitive complement in a construct phrase.

Jannah in its recognized use denotes a garden. Reading ʿadn through the Arabic meaning of residence characterizes these gardens in terms of remaining and settling. Jannāt ʿadn can therefore be understood as “gardens of lasting residence.”

Another possibility is that ʿAdn in this expression functions as the proper name of a place in Paradise. On that reading, “gardens of ʿAdn” more closely preserves the construction.

The Arabic construct phrase alone does not conclusively decide between these possibilities. The form ʿAdn also resembles the name Eden in other religious and Semitic linguistic traditions. That resemblance requires independent historical investigation: it neither proves borrowing nor by itself disproves the Arabic meaning of residence.

What the Qur’anic usage establishes more securely is that all eleven occurrences concern gardens of reward in the Hereafter, associated with entry, residence, blessings and remaining.

“Gardens of lasting residence” is therefore defensible from the Qur’anic context, while the history of the designation and its possible status as a proper name remain open to investigation.

In Adam’s story, the Qur’an speaks of الجَنَّة (al-jannah), “the Garden,” without explicitly calling it jannāt ʿadn. Their certain identification cannot therefore rest only on similar names or external narratives.

4. Jannāt ʿadn: lasting residence and the divine promise

Good dwellings in gardens of lasting residence

Al-Tawbah 9:72

وَعَدَ اللَّهُ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَالْمُؤْمِنَاتِ جَنَّاتٍ تَجْرِي مِنْ تَحْتِهَا الْأَنْهَارُ خَالِدِينَ فِيهَا وَمَسَاكِنَ طَيِّبَةً فِي جَنَّاتِ عَدْنٍ ۚ وَرِضْوَانٌ مِنَ اللَّهِ أَكْبَرُ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ هُوَ الْفَوْزُ الْعَظِيمُ

“The God has promised believing men and believing women gardens beneath which rivers flow, abiding therein, and good dwellings in gardens of lasting residence. And approval from The God is greater. That is the great success.”

This is one of the clearest uses of ʿadn because مَسَاكِنَ طَيِّبَةً (masākina ṭayyibah), “good dwellings,” accompanies it. The passage describes a place where believing men and women reside and enjoy good places of habitation.

“Good dwellings” is not the meaning of ʿadn itself. Masākin names places of habitation, while ṭayyibah describes them as good. In jannāt ʿadn, ʿadn emphasizes settled residence.

The same verse also has خَالِدِينَ فِيهَا (khālidīna fīhā), making the permanence of their remaining in the gardens more explicit.

The closing words must also be retained: وَرِضْوَانٌ مِنَ اللَّهِ أَكْبَرُ (wa-riḍwānun mina Allāhi akbar). After gardens and good dwellings, the Qur’an describes approval from The God as greater. Reward in this verse is therefore not confined to having a house or enjoying material blessings.

The same image in Al-Ṣaff

Al-Ṣaff 61:12

يَغْفِرْ لَكُمْ ذُنُوبَكُمْ وَيُدْخِلْكُمْ جَنَّاتٍ تَجْرِي مِنْ تَحْتِهَا الْأَنْهَارُ وَمَسَاكِنَ طَيِّبَةً فِي جَنَّاتِ عَدْنٍ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ الْفَوْزُ الْعَظِيمُ

“[In that case,] He will forgive you your sins and admit you to gardens beneath which rivers flow, and to good dwellings in gardens of lasting residence. That is the great success.”

This verse also uses مَسَاكِنَ طَيِّبَةً فِي جَنَّاتِ عَدْنٍ (masākina ṭayyibatan fī jannāti ʿadnin). Their association in two surahs connects these gardens, within the Qur’anic image of reward, with habitation and lasting enjoyment.

In Al-Ṣaff, the verse follows a call to faith and striving in the way of The God. In Al-Tawbah, the promise addresses believing men and women. Although the settings differ, the image of the place of reward is shared.

These uses do not establish that ʿadn is merely the name of a house or building. The verses speak of gardens and place good dwellings within them.

5. ʿAdn and khulūd: residence and unending permanence

One of the most important distinctions is between ʿadn and خُلُود (khulūd). The Qur’an places them together in several passages.

Residence and abiding in Ṭā Hā

Ṭā Hā 20:76

جَنَّاتُ عَدْنٍ تَجْرِي مِنْ تَحْتِهَا الْأَنْهَارُ خَالِدِينَ فِيهَا ۚ وَذَٰلِكَ جَزَاءُ مَنْ تَزَكَّىٰ

“Gardens of lasting residence beneath which rivers flow, abiding therein. That is the reward of whoever has purified himself.”

Jannāt ʿadn identifies the place of reward, while khālidīna fīhā expresses its inhabitants’ abiding there.

Their proximity shows a relationship between the meanings, but does not justify assuming complete synonymy.

In its Arabic semantic field, ʿadn emphasizes residence and settling in a place. Khulūd concerns the continuity of remaining. In the setting of reward in the Hereafter, they jointly present a lasting, unending residence.

The ending identifies this as جَزَاءُ مَنْ تَزَكَّىٰ (jazāʾu man tazakkā), the reward of whoever has pursued purification. The purity and fitness of those inhabiting the gardens follow from this clause and its setting, rather than from ʿadn’s lexical meaning.

Residence, abiding and eternity in Al-Bayyinah

Al-Bayyinah 98:8

جَزَاؤُهُمْ عِنْدَ رَبِّهِمْ جَنَّاتُ عَدْنٍ تَجْرِي مِنْ تَحْتِهَا الْأَنْهَارُ خَالِدِينَ فِيهَا أَبَدًا ۖ رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهُمْ وَرَضُوا عَنْهُ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ لِمَنْ خَشِيَ رَبَّهُ

“Their reward with their Lord is gardens of lasting residence beneath which rivers flow, abiding therein forever. The God is pleased with them, and they are pleased with Him. That is for whoever has reverent fear of his Lord.”

Three expressions stand together: ʿadn, khālidīna fīhā and abadan.

Translating ʿadn from the outset as the complete idea of “unending eternity” obscures some of the sentence’s linguistic distinctions. A more precise reading allows ʿadn to express the place of residence, khulūd the continuity of remaining, and abadan its unending character more explicitly.

This does not limit the duration of remaining in Paradise. The verse expressly states unending permanence. The issue is recognizing what each word contributes.

The mutual approval of The God and His servants also belongs to the description of this reward. It is not part of ʿadn’s lexical definition, but comes from the subsequent clauses.

6. Entry into jannāt ʿadn: reward and peace

The gardens and their inhabitants’ wishes

Al-Naḥl 16:31

جَنَّاتُ عَدْنٍ يَدْخُلُونَهَا تَجْرِي مِنْ تَحْتِهَا الْأَنْهَارُ ۖ لَهُمْ فِيهَا مَا يَشَاءُونَ ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ يَجْزِي اللَّهُ الْمُتَّقِينَ

“Gardens of lasting residence which they enter, beneath which rivers flow. There they have whatever they wish. Thus The God rewards those who uphold taqwa [mindful guarding of their conduct].”

يَدْخُلُونَهَا (yadkhulūnahā), “they enter them,” expresses entry into these gardens. Entry and residence are related but distinct: someone can enter a place without making it a home.

Here jannāt ʿadn accompanies a verb of entry, naming the place into which those who uphold taqwa are admitted.

لَهُمْ فِيهَا مَا يَشَاءُونَ (lahum fīhā mā yashāʾūn) expresses their enjoyment of what they wish. It is a separate part of the promise rather than a meaning extracted from ʿadn.

Open gates

Ṣād 38:50

جَنَّاتِ عَدْنٍ مُفَتَّحَةً لَهُمُ الْأَبْوَابُ

“Gardens of lasting residence, their gates opened to them.”

The verse is brief but spatially clear. These gardens are presented as a place whose gates are open to its inhabitants.

مُفَتَّحَة (mufattaḥah) belongs to ف ت ح (F T Ḥ) and expresses the gates’ being open. Openness and access therefore do not come directly from ʿadn.

Nor does the verse specify the gates’ number, material or the structures behind them. The image of open gates must be retained without deriving details the text does not state.

Together, these two passages present jannāt ʿadn as a place where those receiving reward enter and reside. The manner of entry and details of the place can be described only within what the Qur’an states.

7. Family relationships and the qualification of righteousness

In two verses, entry into these gardens is associated with righteous members of the family.

Al-Raʿd: righteous relatives joining them

Al-Raʿd 13:23

جَنَّاتُ عَدْنٍ يَدْخُلُونَهَا وَمَنْ صَلَحَ مِنْ آبَائِهِمْ وَأَزْوَاجِهِمْ وَذُرِّيَّاتِهِمْ ۖ وَالْمَلَائِكَةُ يَدْخُلُونَ عَلَيْهِمْ مِنْ كُلِّ بَابٍ

“Gardens of lasting residence which they enter, along with whoever is righteous among their fathers, spouses and descendants. And the angels enter upon them from every gate.”

Residence here is associated with family members accompanying them, but this is not stated without qualification. وَمَنْ صَلَحَ (wa-man ṣalaḥa), “and whoever is righteous,” introduces the qualification of righteousness.

Family relationship alone is therefore not presented as a guarantee of entry.

The distinction between ʿadn and ص ل ح (Ṣ L Ḥ) must also be maintained. ʿAdn concerns the place of residence; righteousness describes those fit to enter it.

The verse continues with angels entering from the gates. This image relates to hospitality and honour for the inhabitants, but such details do not come from ʿadn itself.

Ghāfir: the angels’ supplication for admission

Ghāfir 40:8

رَبَّنَا وَأَدْخِلْهُمْ جَنَّاتِ عَدْنٍ الَّتِي وَعَدْتَهُمْ وَمَنْ صَلَحَ مِنْ آبَائِهِمْ وَأَزْوَاجِهِمْ وَذُرِّيَّاتِهِمْ ۚ إِنَّكَ أَنْتَ الْعَزِيزُ الْحَكِيمُ

“Our Lord, admit them to the gardens of lasting residence which You have promised them, and whoever is righteous among their fathers, spouses and descendants. Indeed, You are the Mighty, the Wise.”

Here angels ask for the believers’ admission. As in Al-Raʿd, the relatives’ accompanying them is qualified by righteousness.

The two verses have closely related semantic structures: these gardens are the promised destination, entry is sought and prayed for, and the relatives’ inclusion depends on their righteousness.

These verses do not make family reunion or family life part of ʿadn’s lexical meaning. That feature follows from the context of reward and the righteous accompanying one another.

8. A promise still beyond human sight

Maryam 19:61

جَنَّاتِ عَدْنٍ الَّتِي وَعَدَ الرَّحْمَٰنُ عِبَادَهُ بِالْغَيْبِ ۚ إِنَّهُ كَانَ وَعْدُهُ مَأْتِيًّا

“Gardens of lasting residence which the Entirely Merciful has promised His servants, unseen [by them]. Indeed, His promise will be fulfilled.”

Jannāt ʿadn here is associated with the divine promise and the unseen.

بِالْغَيْبِ (bi-l-ghayb) concerns a promise whose realization lies outside present human sight and experience. The gardens’ form therefore cannot be determined through ordinary measurements or knowledge of earthly gardens.

Their unseen character does not authorize removing or making symbolic every Qur’anic description. The Qur’an itself speaks of gardens, rivers, dwellings, gates and blessings.

The more precise approach preserves the stated image without establishing its character as certain beyond what the Qur’an says.

مَأْتِيًّا (maʾtiyyan) also concerns the promise’s realization. The certainty of the promise follows from this clause and its context rather than from ʿadn itself.

9. Blessings, rest and habitation in the gardens

Enjoyment of blessings in Al-Kahf

Al-Kahf 18:31

أُولَٰئِكَ لَهُمْ جَنَّاتُ عَدْنٍ تَجْرِي مِنْ تَحْتِهِمُ الْأَنْهَارُ يُحَلَّوْنَ فِيهَا مِنْ أَسَاوِرَ مِنْ ذَهَبٍ وَيَلْبَسُونَ ثِيَابًا خُضْرًا مِنْ سُنْدُسٍ وَإِسْتَبْرَقٍ مُتَّكِئِينَ فِيهَا عَلَى الْأَرَائِكِ ۚ نِعْمَ الثَّوَابُ وَحَسُنَتْ مُرْتَفَقًا

“For them are gardens of lasting residence, with rivers flowing beneath them [the inhabitants]. There they are adorned with bracelets of gold and wear green garments of fine and heavy silk, reclining on adorned couches. Excellent is the reward, and beautiful the resting place!”

The gardens are described through an ensemble of blessings: flowing rivers, golden ornaments, fine clothing, places to recline and rest.

These features do not come from ʿadn itself. ʿAdn concerns the place of residence, while other words express the character of its inhabitants’ enjoyment.

Including every blessing mentioned here within the definition of ʿadn would confuse the root’s meaning with the description of one particular referent.

The gardens in Fāṭir

Fāṭir 35:33

جَنَّاتُ عَدْنٍ يَدْخُلُونَهَا يُحَلَّوْنَ فِيهَا مِنْ أَسَاوِرَ مِنْ ذَهَبٍ وَلُؤْلُؤًا ۖ وَلِبَاسُهُمْ فِيهَا حَرِيرٌ

“Gardens of lasting residence which they enter; there they are adorned with bracelets of gold and with pearls, and their clothing therein is silk.”

The same image of entry, ornaments and fine clothing appears here.

The Al-Kahf and Fāṭir passages show these gardens described through more than remaining: in the setting of reward, they also provide blessings and rest.

Later in the same surah, Fāṭir 35:35 speaks of دَار الْمُقَامَة (dār al-muqāmah), a dwelling of residence where its inhabitants, through The God’s favour, are free from toil and weariness.

Its connection with jannāt ʿadn matters because another word clarifies residence in the same setting. Nevertheless, the two are different expressions; semantic proximity must not erase their distinctions.

Rest and enjoyment in these verses therefore follow from the complete picture of reward in the Hereafter. The basic meaning of ʿadn remains residence and settling.

10. Related roots

ع د ن (ʿadn) and خ ل د (khuld): residence and duration

ʿAdn and khulūd approach one another in passages about Paradise, but are not identical.

In its lexical family, ʿadn concerns residence and settling in a place. Khulūd concerns duration and remaining.

In Arabic, residence does not necessarily mean eternity; nor does duration alone describe every feature of a place.

The Qur’an places jannāt ʿadn alongside khālidīna fīhā, adding abadan in Al-Bayyinah 98:8, to make the reward’s unending permanence clear.

“Everlasting gardens” is understandable as the overall contextual meaning, but “gardens of lasting residence” more precisely shows the specific contribution of ʿadn.

ع د ن (ʿadn) and س ك ن (sakan): residence and habitation

س ك ن (S K N) is associated with stillness, coming to rest and habitation. مَسْكَن (maskan) is a place of habitation.

ʿAdn also concerns residence, but psychological peace, having a house or comfort must not be made part of every lexical use.

In Al-Tawbah 9:72, good dwellings are situated within jannāt ʿadn. Their association shows these two families complementing one another in describing the place of reward.

ʿAdn emphasizes residence, while masākin names places of habitation.

ع د ن (ʿadn) and أ و ي (awā): residence and taking refuge

أَوَى (awā), from أ و ي, has important uses involving seeking refuge, finding lodging and giving shelter.

مَأْوَى (maʾwā) can therefore name a refuge or place of return, while the Arabic field of ʿadn places greater emphasis on residence and settling.

This is not an absolute boundary. A location can be both a refuge and a place of lasting habitation; context may unite both features in one referent.

ع د ن (ʿadn) and ق و م (qiyām and iqāmah): related semantic fields

ق و م (Q W M) has a broad family. Forms such as إِقَامَة (iqāmah) and مَقَام (maqām) can involve settling, establishing and remaining.

In Fāṭir 35:35, dār al-muqāmah is associated with the lasting residence of the people of Paradise.

Dār al-muqāmah and jannāt ʿadn are close in this setting, but muqāmah belongs to another root. Their semantic proximity must not lead to identifying their roots or all their uses.

ع د ن (ʿadn) and ج ن ن (jannah): residence and garden

In jannāt ʿadn, jannāt names the kind of place, while ʿadn, on a descriptive reading, clarifies its character as a place of residence.

ʿAdn by itself therefore does not mean a garden in every Arabic use.

This guards against reducing the Qur’anic expression to an undifferentiated word such as “Paradise” and overlooking its construction.

A fluent verse translation may still use “Paradise” where the aim is to convey the overall meaning. For Forqan’s detailed lexical study, preserving the distinction between jannāt and ʿadn is more useful.

11. Translation suggestions for Forqan

For جَنَّاتُ عَدْنٍ (jannātu ʿadnin), the basic suggested equivalent is “gardens of lasting residence.” It preserves both jannāt and the recognized Arabic meaning of ʿadn.

“Gardens of abiding” is a shorter, more fluent expression usable in some contexts.

“Everlasting gardens” is also defensible as the complete meaning of the promise in the Hereafter, particularly where abiding and eternity are explicit. It makes the distinction between ʿadn and khuld less visible, however.

“Gardens of ʿAdn” is appropriate if the translation is to leave open its possible status as a proper name. A brief explanation of the Arabic meaning of residence will then help the reader.

For direct translation and Forqan’s root study, “gardens of lasting residence” is the principal suggestion here, without treating the historical question of a proper name as conclusively settled.

“Palace,” “house,” “psychological peace,” “luxury,” “the highest Paradise,” or “the best rank of Paradise” must not be extracted from ʿadn alone. If a verse presents any such meaning, it requires independent evidence.

Nor must every translation contain the root’s entire lexical explanation. The basic translation should remain natural, with finer distinctions explained in the root study and the verse reflection.

12. Conclusion

In Arabic, ع د ن concerns residence, remaining and settling in a place. Camels remaining in pasture, living in a land and the word maʿdin help clarify the family’s semantic field.

The Qur’an uses only عَدْن (ʿadn), in jannāt ʿadn. All eleven occurrences concern gardens of reward in the Hereafter.

Arabic evidence and Qur’anic context make “gardens of lasting residence” defensible. The possible use of ʿAdn as a proper name and its historical relationship with Eden in other linguistic traditions nevertheless remain separate questions that must not be affirmed or denied without sufficient evidence.

These gardens are places of entry, residence, blessings and remaining for those rewarded. The Qur’an also speaks of flowing rivers, good dwellings, open gates, enjoyment of wishes and divine approval. Each feature follows from its own words and the context, rather than from ʿadn’s lexical meaning alone.

The distinction from khulūd must also remain: the Arabic field of ʿadn concerns residence and settling; khulūd expresses the duration of remaining; and expressions such as abadan can make its unending character explicit.

Understanding jannāt ʿadn precisely involves neither reducing it to a name without meaning nor importing every description of Paradise into the root’s definition. The starting point is the recognized meaning of residence, connected within the Qur’an’s verses with a place of lasting reward.
