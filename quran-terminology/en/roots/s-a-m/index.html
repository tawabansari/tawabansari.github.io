The Qur’anic Root S ʾ M

Qur’anic and lexical forms

Core meaning: becoming weary, bored, or fed up with something—a state in which continuing an activity, repeating conduct, or facing a situation becomes tiring or unwelcome. The Qur’an uses this root to prohibit weariness in recording people’s rights, to deny weariness in glorifying the Lord, and to describe human beings as never weary of asking for good.

1. The root and its meaning in pre-Islamic Arabic

S ʾ M denotes becoming weary, tiring of continuing an activity, or becoming bored with something.

Saʾima al-shayʾa means becoming weary or bored with something.

Saʾima mina al-shayʾ likewise means tiring of or becoming bored with something.

Al-saʾam and al-saʾāmah are verbal nouns of this family, denoting weariness and boredom.

Weariness here need not mean physical incapacity. Someone may remain physically able to act while finding its continuation or repetition unwelcome.

For example, a person may be able to write yet tire of recording lengthy transaction details.

Another may grow weary of repeating a request without losing the ability to speak or ask.

The issue is not merely bodily energy, but also the person’s relationship to continuing the activity or situation.

A pre-Islamic witness: weariness of life’s burdens in Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā’s poetry

The pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā says in the celebrated ode attributed to him:

سَئِمْتُ تَكَالِيفَ الْحَيَاةِ وَمَنْ يَعِشْ
ثَمَانِينَ حَوْلًا لَا أَبَا لَكَ يَسْأَمِ

saʾimtu takālīfa al-ḥayāti wa-man yaʿish / thamānīna ḥawlan lā abā laka yasʾami

Translation:

“I have grown weary of life’s burdens; whoever lives eighty years—mark my words—grows weary.”

Saʾimtu is a first-person singular perfect verb.

Takālīfa al-ḥayāh refers to life’s difficulties, hardships, and burdens.

Yasʾami belongs to the same root and expresses becoming weary. It is the conditional result of man yaʿish; its final kasrah in this transmitted verse follows the rhyme, rather than being the normal indicative ending.

Lā abā laka is a conventional address drawing attention here. Its force should not be reduced to a literal insult; “mark my words” conveys its interjectional function in this translation.

The poet speaks of long experience of life, in which difficulties, suffering, and recurring troubles can cause weariness.

He is not merely describing physical weakness in old age. Aging may reduce bodily strength, but saʾimtu emphasizes weariness of life’s burdens.

Eighty years evokes a long life. This poetic statement is neither a scientific law nor a categorical judgment about everyone reaching that age.

The verse appears in familiar transmissions and commentaries of Zuhayr’s ode. Its attribution supplies important evidence for pre-Qur’anic usage, although, like other pre-Islamic poems, it survives through later written transmission.

What the witness establishes

Here saʾam does not mean forgetting, becoming incapable, despairing, or abandoning life.

The poet is weary of life and its burdens. The word itself does not say that he is unable to live or has decided to end his life.

The root’s meaning should therefore remain distinct from possible consequences of weariness.

A person can tire of an activity and still continue it.

Someone may be dissatisfied with a situation yet unable to change it.

Or a person may continue despite weariness because of responsibility, interest, or an important goal.

Saʾam expresses weariness itself, not necessarily incapacity, stopping, or abandoning an action.

A historical definition

The poetic witness attributed to Zuhayr and recorded Arabic usage support this definition:

S ʾ M means becoming weary or bored with something, especially when continuing, repeating, or enduring it becomes burdensome and unwelcome.

This may arise in facing life’s difficulties.

It may result from repeated activity.

Or it may persist in relation to a person, behavior, or situation.

The same recognizable meaning appears in the Qur’an, while each verse identifies the object of weariness or its denial.

2. Principal forms and derivatives

Saʾima yasʾamu

The basic verb means becoming weary, bored, or fed up with something.

سَئِمْتُ مِنْهُ

saʾimtu minhu

“I became weary or bored with it.”

It can take a direct object or the preposition min.

In Zuhayr’s poem, saʾimtu takālīfa al-ḥayāh has no preposition.

Fuṣṣilat 41:49 uses yasʾamu … min duʿāʾi al-khayr.

Both constructions express weariness, but the complement and syntax differ.

Saʾam

A verbal noun of this family.

It means weariness, boredom, or being tired of something.

It describes a person’s state toward continuing an activity or situation.

Saʾmah

A recorded verbal noun denoting weariness and boredom. The lexical witness checked here gives the form with a vowelless hamzah.

Differences in the vocalization of verbal nouns should not be converted into fixed psychological distinctions or degrees of fatigue without evidence.

Saʾām and saʾāmah

These too are recorded verbal nouns.

Saʾāmah commonly means weariness and boredom.

Some lexical explanations use malal and ḍajar to explain it.

Their closeness does not make all their uses identical.

Saʾūm

A description of someone weary or frequently becoming weary.

The form is recorded in lexical works.

Its pattern can express intensity or recurrence, but does not quantify either on its own.

Asʾama

A Form IV verb, used transitively for making someone weary or bored.

أَسْأَمَهُ

asʾamahu

“He made him weary or bored.”

The subject causes weariness in another person.

This differs from saʾima, which attributes weariness to the person experiencing it.

Forms actually found in the Qur’an

S ʾ M occurs three times in the Qur’an:

  • Tasʾamū in Al-Baqarah 2:282.
  • Yasʾamūna in Fuṣṣilat 41:38.
  • Yasʾamu in Fuṣṣilat 41:49.

All three belong to the basic verb saʾima yasʾamu.

Nouns such as saʾam and saʾāmah, and transitive asʾama, do not occur in the Qur’an.

All three Qur’anic occurrences accompany lā.

Yet lā does not perform the same function in all three.

In Al-Baqarah 2:282 it introduces a prohibition.

In the two Fuṣṣilat verses it introduces negation.

This syntactic distinction matters for translation.

3. Wa-lā tasʾamū: not tiring of recording rights in Al-Baqarah 2:282

Al-Baqarah 2:282 discusses recording debts with specified terms, writing obligations, and obtaining testimony.

Part of the verse reads:

وَلَا تَسْأَمُوا أَنْ تَكْتُبُوهُ صَغِيرًا أَوْ كَبِيرًا إِلَىٰ أَجَلِهِ

wa-lā tasʾamū an taktubūhu ṣaghīran aw kabīran ilā ajalihi

Al-Baqarah 2:282

Translation:

“Do not tire of writing it down, whether small or large, with its due date.”

Tasʾamū is a second-person plural imperfect verb following prohibitive lā.

It is jussive, marked by deletion of the final nūn.

An taktubūhu specifies the action of which the addressees must not tire.

The suffix hu refers to the debt or right being recorded in context.

Ṣaghīran aw kabīran concerns the small or large amount of that right or debt.

The sentence concerns recording financial rights and obligations with specified terms, not every kind of writing or document in every situation.

Why mention weariness in writing?

Recording a debt can seem tiresome to the parties.

A small amount may seem unworthy of documentation.

Friends may assume that details need not be recorded.

Preparing a document, specifying the due date, or assembling witnesses may also take time.

A person may then tire of writing or attending to its details.

The verse addresses that state and calls on people not to neglect recording rights because of weariness.

The prohibition concerns becoming weary of writing. In its practical context, it calls for not allowing weariness to cause neglect of recording rights; it does not claim that feeling weary is psychologically impossible.

Someone may tire of an activity while remaining responsible for carrying it out properly.

Small and large rights

Ṣaghīran aw kabīran indicates that a small amount should not itself justify disregarding its record.

An amount trivial to one person may matter greatly to another.

Even a small transaction can lead to disputes about the debt or payment date.

The value of documentation is therefore not confined to large transactions.

This follows from the sentence and its context, not from saʾam lexically meaning financial justice.

Weariness, fairness, and testimony

After the prohibition, the verse continues:

ذَٰلِكُمْ أَقْسَطُ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ وَأَقْوَمُ لِلشَّهَادَةِ وَأَدْنَىٰ أَلَّا تَرْتَابُوا

dhālikum aqsaṭu ʿinda Allāhi wa-aqwamu li-l-shahādati wa-adnā allā tartābū

Al-Baqarah 2:282

That is fairer before The God, firmer for testimony, and more conducive to your having no doubts.

Three results are mentioned: greater fairness, stronger testimony, and less occasion for doubt.

Recording rights serves more than outward order.

Writing can preserve the right, the amount owed, and the time of payment.

If a dispute arises, a document can help establish what happened.

A clear record can also reduce a witness’s vulnerability to forgetfulness or error.

Personal weariness should not displace trustworthiness and protection of others’ rights in this setting.

The same verse later treats immediate trade conducted between the parties differently regarding omission of writing. This sentence cannot therefore be isolated as an identical requirement to document every human transaction.

The contextual meaning of tasʾamū

Wa-lā tasʾamū in Al-Baqarah 2:282 means not tiring of recording the debt and its details.

It calls on the addressees not to let difficulty or tedium lead to neglect of the record.

The root still means weariness; its object here is responsibility for recording financial rights.

4. Wa-hum lā yasʾamūn: no weariness in glorifying the Lord

Fuṣṣilat 41:38 reads:

فَإِنِ اسْتَكْبَرُوا فَالَّذِينَ عِنْدَ رَبِّكَ يُسَبِّحُونَ لَهُ بِاللَّيْلِ وَالنَّهَارِ وَهُمْ لَا يَسْأَمُونَ

fa-ini istakbarū fa-lladhīna ʿinda rabbika yusabbiḥūna lahu bi-l-layli wa-l-nahāri wa-hum lā yasʾamūn

Fuṣṣilat 41:38

Translation:

“If they act arrogantly, those with your Lord glorify Him night and day, and they do not grow weary.”

Yasʾamūna is a third-person plural imperfect verb.

Lā introduces negation here.

Unlike Al-Baqarah 2:282, this is a statement about those with the Lord, rather than a command not to tire.

Glorification and arrogance in context

Fuṣṣilat 41:37 mentions night, day, sun, and moon as divine signs.

It prohibits prostrating to the sun and moon and calls for prostration to their Creator.

The next verse says that if some act arrogantly, those with the Lord glorify Him night and day without weariness.

Glorification is set against arrogance in this context.

Pride and self-importance can cause a person to turn away from serving the Lord.

Those with the Lord, however, are described as continuously glorifying Him without weariness.

Lā yasʾamūn is therefore a positive description here.

The connection with Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:20

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:20 reads:

يُسَبِّحُونَ اللَّيْلَ وَالنَّهَارَ لَا يَفْتُرُونَ

yusabbiḥūna al-layla wa-l-nahāra lā yafturūn

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:20

Translation:

“They glorify night and day without flagging.”

Its subject is close to Fuṣṣilat 41:38.

Both mention glorification night and day.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:20, however, uses yafturūna.

Fuṣṣilat 41:38 uses yasʾamūna.

Futūr here concerns flagging or reduced activity or strength.

Saʾam concerns weariness or boredom.

The states can be related without being identical.

Someone may grow weary of an activity and continue it.

Another may flag because of reduced physical strength without losing interest in the activity.

The two Qur’anic expressions should not be conflated without grounds.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:19 also has wa-man ʿindahu, semantically related to alladhīna ʿinda rabbika in Fuṣṣilat 41:38.

This connection supports discussion of angels and favored servants, but lā yasʾamūn itself does not explain their bodily nature or constitution.

Does lā yasʾamūn mean having no limitations whatsoever?

The verse explicitly denies weariness of them.

That word does not disclose details of their existence, strength, need for rest, or physical constitution.

Absence of weariness in glorification is not itself a theory about the biology or nature of those with the Lord.

Nor should it become an unsupported command that every human must never feel tired or take time to rest.

The verse describes a particular group and its condition.

The contextual meaning of yasʾamūn

Wa-hum lā yasʾamūn means that they do not grow weary of glorifying the Lord.

The root’s meaning remains weariness; its occurrence is denied.

5. Lā yasʾamu al-insān: human beings do not tire of asking for good

Fuṣṣilat 41:49 reads:

لَا يَسْأَمُ الْإِنْسَانُ مِنْ دُعَاءِ الْخَيْرِ وَإِنْ مَسَّهُ الشَّرُّ فَيَئُوسٌ قَنُوطٌ

lā yasʾamu al-insānu min duʿāʾi al-khayri wa-in massahu al-sharru fa-yaʾūsun qanūṭ

Fuṣṣilat 41:49

Translation:

“The human being does not tire of asking for good; but if adversity touches him, he becomes deeply hopeless and despairing.”

Yasʾamu is a third-person singular imperfect verb.

Its subject is al-insān, the human being.

Min duʿāʾi al-khayr specifies its complement: asking for good.

The issue is weariness of asking, rather than bodily fatigue from work or movement.

Why does a person not tire of asking for good?

A person may continually desire good, comfort, provision, security, health, or greater benefits in life.

Obtaining one thing may give rise to another desire.

Satisfying one need can also reveal a new need or aspiration.

Asking for good can then continue without the person feeling there is nothing more to want.

Duʿāʾi al-khayr should not be restricted to economic greed or requests for wealth.

Duʿāʾ concerns asking here; khayr identifies what is sought.

A person may ask The God for health, safety, provision, or relief.

The instances of good follow from context and other uses of that word, not from S ʾ M.

Lā yasʾam does not itself mean being greedy, covetous, or ungrateful.

It expresses not becoming weary of continuing to ask.

The contrast with despair

The verse’s second part reads:

وَإِنْ مَسَّهُ الشَّرُّ فَيَئُوسٌ قَنُوطٌ

wa-in massahu al-sharru fa-yaʾūsun qanūṭ

Fuṣṣilat 41:49

But if adversity touches him, he becomes deeply hopeless and despairing.

This introduces another state.

A person who never tires of asking for good may despair when facing hardship or adversity.

Three independent lexical families appear:

S ʾ M concerns weariness and boredom.

Y ʾ S concerns hopelessness.

Q N Ṭ likewise belongs to the field of despair and lost hope.

These may be connected in experience, but their meanings differ.

Someone may tire of repeating a request while still hoping it will be fulfilled.

Someone may not be tired of an activity but may lose hope about its outcome.

Hardship can also bring both weariness and despair.

The juxtaposition of lā yasʾam and yaʾūs qanūṭ does not make saʾam mean despair.

Inconsistency in human behavior

The verse contrasts human responses to good and adversity.

While asking for good, people’s wants can appear endless.

Yet adversity may quickly lead them to despair.

The contrast portrays instability in judgment and response as life’s conditions change.

Al-insān does not imply that every person behaves this way to the same degree in every circumstance.

Elsewhere the Qur’an describes people who endure hardship patiently, retain hope, and give thanks for blessings.

Ibrāhīm 14:34 also discusses abundant divine gifts and human ingratitude.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:83 addresses changing responses to blessing and adversity.

These verses illuminate human conduct, while saʾam retains its lexical meaning of weariness and boredom.

The connection with Fuṣṣilat 41:50

After despair in adversity, Fuṣṣilat 41:50 describes another situation: receiving mercy after suffering, a person may regard it as an unquestionable entitlement and misjudge his eventual outcome.

Fuṣṣilat 41:49 and Fuṣṣilat 41:50 thus examine behavior under different conditions.

Fuṣṣilat 41:49 concerns asking for good and responding to adversity.

Fuṣṣilat 41:50 concerns the return of blessing and mercy.

A full analysis of the second verse should not displace the lexical study of lā yasʾam.

The contextual meaning of yasʾam

Lā yasʾamu al-insānu min duʿāʾi al-khayr means that the human being does not tire of asking for good.

Absence of weariness here concerns continued requesting and wanting good.

Its negative construction resembles Fuṣṣilat 41:38, but its subject and contextual evaluation differ.

6. The three Qur’anic uses and their differences

All three occurrences accompany negation or prohibition.

Each, however, concerns its own subject.

Al-Baqarah 2:282: preventing neglect of responsibility

وَلَا تَسْأَمُوا

wa-lā tasʾamū

Al-Baqarah 2:282

“Do not grow weary.”

The Qur’an warns the addressees against allowing weariness to interfere with recording financial rights.

This is a prohibition.

Its positive value follows from protecting rights, fairness, and avoiding dispute.

Fuṣṣilat 41:38: no weariness in glorification

وَهُمْ لَا يَسْأَمُونَ

wa-hum lā yasʾamūn

Fuṣṣilat 41:38

“And they do not grow weary.”

This is a statement.

It concerns those with the Lord who glorify Him night and day.

Fuṣṣilat 41:49: no weariness of asking for good

لَا يَسْأَمُ الْإِنْسَانُ مِنْ دُعَاءِ الْخَيْرِ

lā yasʾamu al-insānu min duʿāʾi al-khayr

Fuṣṣilat 41:49

“The human being does not tire of asking for good.”

This too is a statement.

Unlike Fuṣṣilat 41:38, it describes human behavior, contrasting the desire for good with despair in hardship.

Is weariness always condemned in the Qur’an?

S ʾ M has no fixed moral value in itself.

Becoming weary is a human state whose moral evaluation depends on its object and the person’s conduct.

Al-Baqarah 2:282 prohibits becoming weary of writing; in context, this calls for preventing weariness from leading to neglect of others’ rights.

In Fuṣṣilat 41:38, absence of weariness in glorification is positive.

In Fuṣṣilat 41:49, tireless requests for good appear beside despair in adversity, exposing inconsistent responses.

One use does not make every kind of tiredness or boredom sinful.

Nor does another make tirelessness in pursuing every desire a virtue.

A psychological state may indicate perseverance in one context and form part of unbalanced behavior in another.

Its value follows from the object, action, and context, not the root alone.

7. Distinctions from related roots

S ʾ M (saʾam) and M L L (malal)

These families are very close in meaning.

Saʾam means becoming weary or bored with something.

Malal likewise commonly concerns tiring of continued or repeated activity.

Lexical works directly explain saʾima through malla.

The closeness is clear, but fixed distinctions across all uses require separate investigation.

One cannot assert that saʾam is always stronger than malal, or that malal always results only from repetition.

Both belong to the field of boredom and mental weariness.

Al-Baqarah 2:282 uses tasʾamū to prohibit weariness in recording rights.

Natural Persian equivalents convey “do not grow weary” or “do not tire of writing it.”

The same verse uses yumlili for dictating the debt’s terms. It belongs to M L L in another recorded semantic branch and should not be confused with tasʾamū.

S ʾ M (saʾam) and F T R (futūr)

Futūr commonly concerns flagging, reduced strength, or diminished activity.

Saʾam concerns weariness and boredom.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:20 has lā yafturūn.

Fuṣṣilat 41:38 has lā yasʾamūn.

Both occur in the context of glorification night and day.

Their contextual closeness shows that not flagging and not becoming weary can be related descriptions.

Yet flagging and boredom are not necessarily the same state.

Someone may weaken physically or in performance while retaining strong interest in the activity.

Another may tire of an activity while retaining the strength to perform it.

S ʾ M (saʾam) and Ḥ S R (ḥasr)

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:19 contains wa-lā yastaḥsirūn.

In the context of service and glorification, it concerns becoming exhausted or worn out by activity.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:20 then has lā yafturūn, while Fuṣṣilat 41:38 has lā yasʾamūn.

All three descriptions relate to perseverance in action.

They should not be treated as completely synonymous without investigating their uses.

Saʾam emphasizes weariness and boredom, while istiḥsār here is closer to exhaustion.

S ʾ M (saʾam) and Ṣ B R (ṣabr)

Ṣabr concerns steadfastness and restraint of oneself.

Saʾam means weariness or boredom.

They can be related in experience without meaning the same thing.

Someone may tire of an activity yet persevere from responsibility or a sound purpose.

Weariness does not necessarily mean absence of patience in that situation.

Conversely, not being weary does not prove patience: the activity may simply be enjoyable, without requiring self-restraint against hardship.

Lā yasʾamūn should therefore not be directly translated as “they exercise patience.”

Perseverance and absence of weariness are related but distinct.

S ʾ M (saʾam) and Y ʾ S (yaʾs)

Yaʾs concerns despair.

Saʾam concerns becoming weary or bored.

Both families appear in Fuṣṣilat 41:49.

Their juxtaposition helps clarify the difference.

Someone may tire of requesting something while still hoping to obtain it.

Conversely, someone may despair of an outcome without tiring of the activity itself.

Weariness is therefore not itself despair.

S ʾ M (saʾam) and Q N Ṭ (qunūṭ)

Qunūṭ also belongs to the field of despair.

Fuṣṣilat 41:49 places yaʾūs and qanūṭ side by side.

They require separate studies of their own roots.

For S ʾ M, the essential point is that qunūṭ is not a necessary semantic consequence of saʾam.

Someone can tire of continuing a behavior without losing hope.

Someone may also despair in hardship without previously tiring of asking for good.

The three families should not be reduced to a single equivalent.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

Principal meanings

Saʾima: became weary, bored, or fed up.

Yasʾamu: grows weary, becomes bored, or tires of something.

Saʾam: weariness or boredom with continuation or repetition.

Saʾmah: weariness and boredom; a verbal noun with a vowelless hamzah.

Saʾām and saʾāmah: weariness, boredom, and being fed up.

Saʾūm: a weary person, or one frequently becoming weary.

Asʾama: made someone weary or bored.

Suggested renderings of Qur’anic forms

In Al-Baqarah 2:282:

wa-lā tasʾamū an taktubūhu ṣaghīran aw kabīran ilā ajalihi

Al-Baqarah 2:282

“Do not tire of writing it down, whether small or large, with its due date.”

In Fuṣṣilat 41:38:

وَهُمْ لَا يَسْأَمُونَ

wa-hum lā yasʾamūn

Fuṣṣilat 41:38

“And they do not grow weary.”

In Fuṣṣilat 41:49:

لَا يَسْأَمُ الْإِنْسَانُ مِنْ دُعَاءِ الْخَيْرِ

lā yasʾamu al-insānu min duʿāʾi al-khayr

A natural rendering such as “does not get tired” can also work, provided it is not understood solely as physical incapacity.

9. Conclusion

S ʾ M means weariness, boredom, and being fed up with something.

In the pre-Islamic poem attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā, it concerns weariness of life’s hardships.

This transmitted witness supports the root’s use in the familiar field of human weariness before its Qur’anic occurrences, subject to the transmission limits already noted.

Lexical forms such as saʾam, saʾāmah, and the family’s other derivatives likewise concern weariness.

The Qur’an has three occurrences.

Al-Baqarah 2:282 prohibits growing weary of recording debts and rights with specified terms.

Fuṣṣilat 41:38 denies weariness in the glorification of those with the Lord.

Fuṣṣilat 41:49 describes human beings as never weary of asking for good, yet liable to despair when adversity strikes.

The root’s central meaning remains in all three uses.

Their objects and contextual evaluations differ.

In one verse, weariness can lead to neglect of a responsibility concerning rights.

In another, absence of weariness is positive in glorification.

In the third, tireless requests for good stand beside despair in hardship.

The root itself thus names neither a virtue nor a sin.

S ʾ M means becoming weary or bored with something, so that continuing, repeating, or enduring it becomes tiring and unwelcome.

This does not necessarily mean physical incapacity, stopping action, abandoning responsibility, or losing hope.

Nor does absence of weariness itself mean patience, faith, reverence, or moral goodness; the object and context determine the evaluation.

Suitable Persian renderings express becoming weary, bored, tired of something, or, where appropriate, fed up.

“Becoming weary” captures the shared meaning of the three occurrences clearly: recording rights, glorifying, and asking for good each involve denial or prohibition of that same state.
