Arabic Ḥ D D has several related branches: a boundary separating things; prevention of passage or transgression; a sharp cutting edge; hardness and firmness; and opposition to another. In this family, حَدّ (ḥadd) denotes a boundary or endpoint, حُدُود (ḥudūd) boundaries, حَدِيد (ḥadīd) iron or sharpness, and حَادَّ (ḥādda) opposition and taking a stand against someone.

The Qur’an uses the root in varied settings. Ḥudūd Allāh concerns divine boundaries in fasting, family relations, inheritance, and other responsibilities. Yuḥāddūna Allāha wa-rasūlahu describes opposing The God and His Messenger. Al-ḥadīd names iron in several verses, whereas fa-baṣaruka al-yawma ḥadīdun describes keen vision. Alsinatin ḥidādin describes sharp, biting tongues.

One fixed equivalent cannot translate them all. Boundary, iron, keen sight, and opposition to The God belong to one lexical family but have distinct meanings and roles in Qur’anic sentences.

A key distinction is between Qur’anic ḥudūd Allāh and the specialized penal usage of ḥudūd in jurisprudence. In the Qur’an, divine boundaries are not merely punishments: they also concern conduct, rights, responsibilities, and relationships made clear for humanity.

1. Forms and derivatives

Ḥ D D consists of ḥāʾ and two dāls: a doubled Arabic root. In many derivatives, the two dāls merge into one consonant marked with shaddah.

Important forms include:

  • حَدَّ ـ يَحُدُّ (ḥadda–yaḥuddu): set a boundary, restrained, prevented, or limited something.
  • حَدّ (ḥadd): boundary, dividing limit, or endpoint; in legal uses, a prescribed restriction or punishment.
  • حُدُود (ḥudūd): plural of ḥadd: boundaries and limits.
  • حَدَّدَ ـ يُحَدِّدُ (ḥaddada–yuḥaddidu): defined a boundary; also sharpened something.
  • تَحْدِيد (taḥdīd): defining a limit, demarcating, or sharpening.
  • حَادَّ ـ يُحَادُّ (ḥādda–yuḥāddu): opposed or stood against another.
  • مُحَادَّة (muḥāddah): opposition and confrontation.
  • حَدِيد (ḥadīd): iron; adjectivally, sharp, cutting, hard, or penetrating.
  • حِدَاد (ḥidād): adjectival plural for sharp things, including biting tongues.
  • حِدَّة (ḥiddah): sharpness, intensity, or irascibility.
  • حَادّ (ḥādd): sharp, cutting, or intense.
  • أَحَدَّ (aḥadda): sharpened; in some uses, directed a keen or penetrating gaze.
  • مَحْدُود (maḥdūd): having a specified limit or being prevented from something.
  • حَدَّاد (ḥaddād): blacksmith; also doorkeeper or restrainer in other early uses.
  • حَدِيدَة (ḥadīdah): a piece of iron or an iron object.
  • حِدَاد / إِحْدَاد (ḥidād/iḥdād): in another usage, refraining from adornment during mourning.

The root occurs 25 times in 20 Qur’anic verses. These occurrences fall into four lexical groups:

  • 1. حُدُود (ḥudūd): 14 occurrences with its case and pronominal variations.
  • 2. حَدِيد (ḥadīd): six occurrences, denoting iron or sharpness.
  • 3. حَادَّ ـ يُحَادُّ (ḥādda–yuḥāddu): four verbal occurrences.
  • 4. حِدَاد (ḥidād): one occurrence describing tongues.

All four verbal occurrences belong to Form III. Yuḥādidi in Al-Tawbah 9:63 preserves the uncontracted structure, while yuḥāddūna in Al-Mujādilah 58:5 and Al-Mujādilah 58:20 merges the two dāls.

Ḥidād in Al-Aḥzāb 33:19 likewise functions as a plural adjective in alsinatin ḥidādin, describing sharp, biting tongues rather than mourning observance.

The singular ḥadd does not occur independently in the Qur’an, although its plural ḥudūd occurs fourteen times. Taḥdīd, ḥaddād as blacksmith, and iḥdād as refraining from adornment do not occur either.

2. Semantic cores and early Arabic usage

Boundary and obstruction: two basic meanings

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies two cores for Ḥ D D:

الْأَوَّلُ الْمَنْعُ، وَالثَّانِي طَرَفُ الشَّيْءِ

al-awwalu al-manʿu wa-al-thānī ṭarafu al-shayʾi

That is: “The first is prevention; the second, the edge or end of a thing.”

This distinction matters because it does not confine the family to one image.

In the first sense, ḥadd prevents passage or entry.

In the second, it denotes an edge, endpoint, or limit.

They are clearly related. A land boundary marks a property’s limits and may also prevent entry onto another property. That connection does not prove every derivative arose historically from one image.

In Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Jawharī likewise describes ḥadd as the barrier between two things and their limit.

Ḥadd al-dār concerns a house’s or property’s boundary, where one holding ends and another begins.

A limit therefore need not imprison someone within an enclosure: it may simply demarcate two domains.

Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs al-Lughah: prevention and the edge of a thing

Boundary: one thing’s end and another’s beginning

Arabic ḥadd al-shayʾ can mean a thing’s endpoint.

The endpoint may be material, like the edge of land or an object, or abstract, like someone’s authority or responsibility.

Here the limit defines something and prevents confusion with something else.

A land boundary, for example, does more than prohibit entry: it establishes the extent of each owner’s rights.

Boundaries in human relationships can likewise clarify each side’s duties, rights, and authority.

This matters for ḥudūd Allāh. Divine boundaries should not become merely a list of prohibitions, since some define rights and responsibilities to maintain and fulfill.

Ḥadda: restraining and preventing

Lexicons record ḥaddahu ʿan al-amr for preventing someone from an action.

The verb here denotes prevention itself.

Someone may be refused entry or prevented from something they have no right to do.

This field also explains early ḥaddād as doorkeeper, regulating admission and potentially preventing entry.

Ḥaddād also denotes a jailer. Lexicons offer two explanations: preventing a prisoner’s escape, or handling iron and iron restraints.

The alternatives show why a title alone does not always establish its precise naming history.

A poetic witness from Al-Aʿshā

In discussing prevention, Ibn Fāris quotes a line attributed to Al-Aʿshā:

فَقُمْنَا وَلَمَّا يَصِحْ دِيكُنَا
إِلَى جَوْنَةٍ عِنْدَ حَدَّادِهَا

fa-qumnā wa-lammā yaṣiḥ dīkunā / ilā jawnatin ʿinda ḥaddādihā

That is: “We rose before our rooster had crowed and went toward a wine jar in its keeper’s charge.”

Ḥaddād here is explained as the wine seller or keeper who holds the wine and controls access until its price is paid, rather than simply an unspecified doorkeeper.

Lisān al-ʿArab explains جَوْنَة (jawnah) in this witness as the wine jar or storage vessel.

The witness shows ḥaddād naming someone regulating access, rather than invariably a blacksmith.

It does not establish universal pre-Islamic use for every doorkeeper throughout Arabia. Its meaning should remain within the attested witness; poetic attribution likewise does not independently establish this line’s exact date.

Lisān al-ʿArab: ḥaddād as wine keeper and the wine jar

Al-Nābighah’s witness: preventing improper conduct

Lexicons quote a line attributed to Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī. Maqāyīs transmits al-malīk; the text below preserves Lisān al-ʿArab’s al-ilāh reading:

إِلَّا سُلَيْمَانَ إِذْ قَالَ الْإِلَهُ لَهُ
قُمْ فِي الْبَرِيَّةِ فَاحْدُدْهَا عَنِ الْفَنَدِ

illā sulaymāna idh qāla al-ilāhu lahu / qum fī al-bariyyati fa-uḥdudhā ʿani al-fanadi

That is: “Except Solomon, when the deity said to him: Rise among the people and restrain them from folly and misconduct.”

Fa-uḥdudhā here concerns prevention and restraint.

It does not set a geographical border for people; it prevents improper behavior.

This illustrates prevention extending beyond material boundaries in early Arabic.

The line is also transmitted with الْمَلِيكُ (al-malīk) instead of الْإِلَهُ (al-ilāh). Citations must acknowledge the variant.

Maqāyīs: Al-Nābighah’s witness with al-malīk

The sharp cutting edge

Ibn Fāris’s second core concerns a thing’s edge.

Ḥadd al-sayf denotes a sword’s cutting edge, and ḥadd al-sikkīn a knife’s sharp edge.

Here ḥadd is more than a boundary: it is an edge capable of cutting or penetrating.

Adjectival ḥadīd can likewise express sharpness.

Arabic accordingly uses baṣarun ḥadīdun for keen, penetrating sight, and lisānun ḥadīdun for a sharp, biting tongue.

Neither expression means the eye or tongue is made of iron.

Sharpness and penetration thus extend figuratively from cutting tools to seeing or speaking. This semantic relationship alone does not prove an exact historical sequence for every usage.

Al-Aʿshā’s witness to intensity

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ records a line attributed to Al-Aʿshā, whose first hemistich also appears in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

وَكَأْسٍ كَعَيْنِ الدِّيكِ بَاكَرْتُ حَدَّهَا
بِفِتْيَانِ صِدْقٍ وَالنَّوَاقِيسُ تُضْرَبُ

wa-kaʾsin ka-ʿayni al-dīki bākartu ḥaddahā / bi-fityāni ṣidqin wa-al-nawāqīsu tuḍrabu

That is: “A cup like the rooster’s eye, whose strength I met early with true companions while the bells were ringing.”

Ḥaddahā concerns the drink’s strength and pungency.

It concerns neither a geographical border nor an iron implement, but intensity of effect.

The witness illustrates sharpness applied beyond a material edge to a forceful effect, without independently establishing the historical direction of every extension.

Lisān and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: the drink’s strength and Al-Aʿshā’s witness

Iron: ḥadīd

Ḥadīd is the Arabic name for iron.

Lexicons connect this name with iron’s hardness, firmness, and resistance.

Al-Jawharī includes iron in the family, associating one naming explanation with strength and resistance.

A material’s established name should nevertheless be distinguished from an etymological explanation of it.

Where Qur’anic ḥadīd concerns metal and implements, its direct meaning is iron, rather than “something sharp” or “something that prevents.”

Iron can make sharp or resistant tools, but not every iron object is sharp.

A cutting edge should therefore not become a compulsory component of every use of ḥadīd.

Ḥiddah: sharpness and intensity of conduct

Arabic records حِدَّة (ḥiddah) for sharp anger, intense behavior, and biting speech.

Speaking with ḥiddah can mean speaking sharply, harshly, or hurtfully.

Sharpness is no longer a material feature of a knife or sword, but a quality or effect of speech or behavior.

It does not follow that every sharp statement is wrong or every gentle statement right.

The root expresses intensity and sharpness; assessing the speech’s truth requires its content and behavioral context.

3. Qur’anic ḥudūd Allāh: boundaries of conduct and responsibility

Al-Baqarah 2:187: boundaries in fasting

At Al-Baqarah 2:187’s end, the rules described for eating, drinking, intimacy, and retreat are called ḥudūd Allāh.

The verse then instructs that these boundaries not be approached.

Ḥudūd here concerns practical boundaries specified by the verse itself.

The verse distinguishes times permitting certain acts from times requiring abstention.

Intimacy with spouses, for example, is permitted on fasting nights, while a restriction remains during retreat in mosques.

Ḥudūd Allāh here therefore concerns more than one prohibition: the stated rules demarcate permitted and prohibited conduct.

Not approaching and not transgressing

Al-Baqarah 2:187 says fa-lā taqrabūhā: do not approach these boundaries.

Al-Baqarah 2:229, however, says fa-lā taʿtadūhā: do not transgress them.

The verbs differ.

Qaruba belongs to Q R B and concerns approaching.

Iʿtadā belongs to ʿ D W and concerns going beyond or transgressing.

Al-Baqarah 2:187 thus warns, within its setting, against approaching prohibited limits; Al-Baqarah 2:229 emphasizes not crossing established boundaries.

Translation should preserve that distinction.

It does not establish that every activity near any religious boundary is prohibited in every circumstance. Each limit and warning requires its own subject and context.

Al-Baqarah 2:229: rights and boundaries in family relations

Verse text:

الطَّلَاقُ مَرَّتَانِ فَإِمْسَاكٌ بِمَعْرُوفٍ أَوْ تَسْرِيحٌ بِإِحْسَانٍ وَلَا يَحِلُّ لَكُمْ أَنْ تَأْخُذُوا مِمَّا آتَيْتُمُوهُنَّ شَيْئًا إِلَّا أَنْ يَخَافَا أَلَّا يُقِيمَا حُدُودَ اللَّهِ فَإِنْ خِفْتُمْ أَلَّا يُقِيمَا حُدُودَ اللَّهِ فَلَا جُنَاحَ عَلَيْهِمَا فِيمَا افْتَدَتْ بِهِ تِلْكَ حُدُودُ اللَّهِ فَلَا تَعْتَدُوهَا وَمَنْ يَتَعَدَّ حُدُودَ اللَّهِ فَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الظَّالِمُونَ

al-ṭalāqu marratāni fa-imsākun bi-maʿrūfin aw tasrīḥun bi-iḥsānin wa-lā yaḥillu lakum an taʾkhudhū mimmā ātaytumūhunna shayʾan illā an yakhāfā allā yuqīmā ḥudūda Allāhi fa-in khiftum allā yuqīmā ḥudūda Allāhi fa-lā junāḥa ʿalayhimā fīmā iftadat bihi tilka ḥudūdu Allāhi fa-lā taʿtadūhā wa-man yataʿadda ḥudūda Allāhi fa-ulāʾika humu al-ẓālimūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Divorce is twice; then retention in a proper manner or release with kindness. You may not take back anything you gave them, unless the two fear they cannot uphold The God’s boundaries. If you fear they cannot uphold them, neither is blameworthy concerning what the woman gives for her release. These are The God’s boundaries: do not transgress them. Those who transgress The God’s boundaries are the wrongdoers.”

Ḥudūd occurs four times here, each associated with Allāh, with varying grammatical case.

Its repetition emphasizes divine boundaries in regulating the spouses’ relationship.

The important point is that these boundaries concern more than prohibitions.

The verse addresses proper retention, kind release, wrongful recovery of gifts, and ending a relationship under specified conditions.

Divine boundaries thus concern both parties’ rights and responsibilities.

Upholding The God’s boundaries

The verse contains yuqīmā ḥudūda Allāh.

Iqāmah belongs to Q W M and here concerns establishing or upholding boundaries.

The construction matters because boundaries are not merely things one must avoid transgressing.

Some must be upheld in human conduct and relationships.

If family relationships require proper treatment and kindness, preserving divine boundaries also involves respecting those rights and behaviors.

Ḥudūd Allāh therefore cannot become merely a line not to cross while positive responsibilities are neglected.

The following verse, Al-Baqarah 2:230, uses the expression in discussing return to a family relationship, conditional on upholding divine boundaries.

This continuity connects those boundaries with a just, responsible framework for family relations.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:13 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:14: boundaries, obedience, and transgression

Al-Nisāʾ 4:13

تِلْكَ حُدُودُ اللَّهِ وَمَنْ يُطِعِ اللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُ يُدْخِلْهُ جَنَّاتٍ تَجْرِي مِنْ تَحْتِهَا الْأَنْهَارُ خَالِدِينَ فِيهَا وَذَٰلِكَ الْفَوْزُ الْعَظِيمُ

tilka ḥudūdu Allāhi wa-man yuṭiʿi Allāha wa-rasūlahu yudkhilhu jannātin tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhāru khālidīna fīhā wa-dhālika al-fawzu al-ʿaẓīmu

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“These are The God’s boundaries. Whoever obeys The God and His Messenger, He will admit into gardens beneath which rivers flow, to remain there. That is the great success.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:14

وَمَنْ يَعْصِ اللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُ وَيَتَعَدَّ حُدُودَهُ يُدْخِلْهُ نَارًا خَالِدًا فِيهَا وَلَهُ عَذَابٌ مُهِينٌ

wa-man yaʿṣi Allāha wa-rasūlahu wa-yataʿadda ḥudūdahu yudkhilhu nāran khālidan fīhā wa-lahu ʿadhābun muhīnun

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Whoever disobeys The God and His Messenger and transgresses His boundaries, He will admit into a fire, to remain there; and he will have a humiliating punishment.”

These verses follow rules concerning inheritance and surviving relatives’ rights.

Ḥudūd Allāh accordingly concerns rules establishing people’s shares and rights.

Here too, the boundaries are more than names for punishments.

They provide a framework for determining entitlements.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:13 describes obedience; Al-Nisāʾ 4:14, disobedience and transgressing boundaries.

Respecting divine boundaries here therefore involves others’ rights and adherence to the stated rules.

Yataʿadda ḥudūdahu also connects crossing a boundary with disobedience.

Ḥudūd itself does not identify a punishment’s precise kind or implementation. The subsequent clauses describe the outcomes in these verses.

Al-Tawbah 9:97: knowing the boundaries of revelation

Al-Tawbah 9:97 describes Bedouin Arabs as stronger in disbelief and hypocrisy and more liable not to know ḥudūda mā anzala Allāhu ʿalā rasūlihi within the verse’s context.

The construction matters lexically.

Ḥudūd concerns the boundaries and rules of what The God revealed.

The subject is therefore not merely enforcing punishments, but knowing the revealed message’s boundaries.

The verse does not make every desert-dweller inherently ignorant or unbelieving. Al-Tawbah 9:99 describes Bedouins believing in The God and the last day.

Its assessment requires the stated characteristics and context.

For this root, the key point is that boundaries can be known or unknown; knowing them precedes conscious observance.

Al-Tawbah 9:112: preserving boundaries

After repentance, worship, praise, bowing, prostration, enjoining what is right, and forbidding wrong, Al-Tawbah 9:112 mentions al-ḥāfiẓūna li-ḥudūdi Allāh.

Ḥafiẓa concerns preserving and guarding.

Divine boundaries here are something to preserve.

This relates to upholding The God’s boundaries in Al-Baqarah 2:229, without being identical.

Upholding emphasizes observance and realization; preserving emphasizes continued protection and maintenance.

Neither expression establishes that preserving boundaries means only memorizing the wording of rules.

The context describes believers’ conduct and responsibilities.

Guarding divine boundaries therefore concerns practice and commitment.

Al-Mujādilah 58:4: boundaries in reparation and responsibility

Al-Mujādilah 58:4 says wa-tilka ḥudūdu Allāh after describing reparation for ẓihār according to a person’s ability.

Divine boundaries can thus concern reparatory obligations and conditions for fulfilling them.

The same context distinguishes ability from inability.

Divine boundaries do not therefore simply impose an obligation irrespective of human circumstances: the prescribed framework itself can provide different routes for different conditions.

This matters when stricter rulings are assumed to demonstrate greater adherence to divine boundaries.

If the Qur’an provides different options for different circumstances, removing them also changes the prescribed boundary.

Observance thus includes both refraining from prohibited acts and preserving exceptions and permitted options stated in the verse.

Al-Ṭalāq 65:1: boundaries also protecting women’s rights

Verse text:

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّبِيُّ إِذَا طَلَّقْتُمُ النِّسَاءَ فَطَلِّقُوهُنَّ لِعِدَّتِهِنَّ وَأَحْصُوا الْعِدَّةَ وَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ رَبَّكُمْ لَا تُخْرِجُوهُنَّ مِنْ بُيُوتِهِنَّ وَلَا يَخْرُجْنَ إِلَّا أَنْ يَأْتِينَ بِفَاحِشَةٍ مُبَيِّنَةٍ وَتِلْكَ حُدُودُ اللَّهِ وَمَنْ يَتَعَدَّ حُدُودَ اللَّهِ فَقَدْ ظَلَمَ نَفْسَهُ لَا تَدْرِي لَعَلَّ اللَّهَ يُحْدِثُ بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ أَمْرًا

yā ayyuhā al-nabiyyu idhā ṭallaqtumu al-nisāʾa fa-ṭalliqūhunna li-ʿiddatihinna wa-aḥṣū al-ʿiddata wa-ittaqū Allāha rabbakum lā tukhrijūhunna min buyūtihinna wa-lā yakhrujna illā an yaʾtīna bi-fāḥishatin mubayyinatin wa-tilka ḥudūdu Allāhi wa-man yataʿadda ḥudūda Allāhi fa-qad ẓalama nafsahu lā tadrī laʿalla Allāha yuḥdithu baʿda dhālika amran

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Prophet, when you divorce women, divorce them for their waiting period and count that period. Be mindful of The God, your Lord. Do not expel them from their homes, nor should they leave, unless they commit manifest indecency. These are The God’s boundaries. Whoever transgresses them has wronged himself. You do not know: The God may bring about something new afterward.”

The boundaries concern divorce, the waiting period, and women’s housing.

The verse explicitly prohibits expelling women from their homes.

These boundaries therefore constrain the husband’s conduct and responsibility as well as the woman’s behavior.

Divine boundaries can accordingly protect people’s rights.

The verse also says wa-man yataʿadda ḥudūda Allāhi fa-qad ẓalama nafsahu.

Transgression is thus connected with wronging oneself.

This does not mean only the transgressor suffers or the other party’s rights are unimportant. The rules themselves concern both parties’ relationship and living circumstances.

Violating these boundaries can therefore infringe another person’s rights too.

The final verb يُحْدِثُ (yuḥdithu) also matters: it belongs to Ḥ D TH, bringing about something new, rather than Ḥ D D. Similar writing must not merge the roots.

4. Qur’anic boundaries and the specialized penal term ḥadd

Jurisprudence uses ḥadd and ḥudūd for a particular category of prescribed punishments. This specialization should be distinguished from broader usage without claiming that every penal sense first arose in a single later period.

The technical term is established within Islamic legal systems.

Qur’anic ḥudūd Allāh has a wider meaning.

It appears concerning fasting, family relations, inheritance, reparation, rights, and responsibilities.

It cannot therefore be directly translated as “criminal punishments” wherever it occurs.

Conversely, a Qur’anic punishment or legal response is not necessarily named ḥudūd by the Qur’an itself.

Al-Baqarah 2:178 discusses retaliation, and Al-Māʾidah 5:38 concerns theft, but neither verse uses ḥudūd Allāh to name a penal category.

The lexical distinction must remain clear.

“Divine boundary” does not authorize inventing arbitrary punishments or prohibitions. An attribution to The God requires evidence from the text and reliable sources.

Understanding ḥudūd Allāh thus involves both respecting prescribed boundaries and avoiding unsupported boundaries attributed to The God.

Lisān al-ʿArab: legal boundaries and penal usage

5. Muḥāddah: opposing and taking the other side

The image of opposite sides of a boundary

Arabic ḥāddahu concerns opposition and confrontation.

Lexicographers explain it through two sides standing against each other.

One can picture each taking the opposite side of a boundary and moving in a different direction.

The image helps explain the relationship between ḥadd and muḥāddah.

It should not become a proven historical origin of the verb; the attested meaning is opposition and confrontation.

Al-Tawbah 9:63: opposing The God and His Messenger

Verse text:

أَلَمْ يَعْلَمُوا أَنَّهُ مَنْ يُحَادِدِ اللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُ فَأَنَّ لَهُ نَارَ جَهَنَّمَ خَالِدًا فِيهَا ذَٰلِكَ الْخِزْيُ الْعَظِيمُ

a-lam yaʿlamū annahu man yuḥādidi Allāha wa-rasūlahu fa-anna lahu nāra jahannama khālidan fīhā dhālika al-khizyu al-ʿaẓīmu

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Do they not know that whoever opposes The God and His Messenger will have the fire of Hell, remaining there? That is the great disgrace.”

Yuḥādidi is a Form III imperfect verb from Ḥ D D.

Here it means opposing or confronting.

The opposition concerns The God and His Messenger.

Al-Tawbah’s context connects this with groups taking a hostile stand against the message and Messenger.

The verb alone does not make every question, doubt, or disagreement about a verse an instance of opposing The God.

The opposition must be identified through the characteristics and conduct described in its Qur’anic context.

Nor does yuḥādidi itself name a particular military activity. Opposition may involve hostile practical action in some circumstances; context establishes its precise form.

Lisān al-ʿArab: muḥāddah, opposition, and Form III

Al-Mujādilah 58:5 and Al-Mujādilah 58:20: opposition and its outcome

Yuḥāddūna appears in Al-Mujādilah 58:5 and Al-Mujādilah 58:20.

Both concern opposing The God and His Messenger.

Al-Mujādilah 58:5 compares their outcome with earlier groups’ suppression and humiliation.

Al-Mujādilah 58:20 places them among the most abased.

Muḥāddah here describes opposition directed against The God and His message.

Lexically, these uses cannot all be restricted to armed fighting, nor can their oppositional force be reduced to an ordinary difference of opinion.

Al-Mujādilah 58:22: loyalty and opposition

Al-Mujādilah 58:22 uses ḥādda for those opposing The God and His Messenger.

The same verse addresses affection and loyalty toward them, even where close family relationships are involved.

Lexically, ḥādda describes a stance and conduct, rather than an ethnicity, family, or immutable identity.

The verse concerns loyalty in the face of opposition to The God.

The root alone does not establish severing every human relationship, abandoning justice toward opponents, or removing their rights.

The scope of loyalty and conduct toward opponents requires other verses too, including justice and treatment of those who have not fought or expelled people.

Translating ḥādda Allāha wa-rasūlahu as “opposed The God and His Messenger” preserves the verb; context establishes the opposition’s precise form.

6. Ḥadīd: iron, sharpness, and penetration

Al-Isrāʾ 17:50: iron in the context of bones and dust

Verse text:

قُلْ كُونُوا حِجَارَةً أَوْ حَدِيدًا

qul kūnū ḥijāratan aw ḥadīdan

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Say: Be stones or iron.”

Ḥadīdan here names the metal iron.

The context concerns resurrection and human re-creation.

Stone and iron appear as hard, resistant substances.

Ḥadīd does not mean “sharp” or “penetrating vision” here.

The usage illustrates the material’s established name.

Al-Kahf 18:96: iron pieces in building a barrier

Verse text:

آتُونِي زُبَرَ الْحَدِيدِ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا سَاوَىٰ بَيْنَ الصَّدَفَيْنِ قَالَ انْفُخُوا حَتَّىٰ إِذَا جَعَلَهُ نَارًا قَالَ آتُونِي أُفْرِغْ عَلَيْهِ قِطْرًا

ātūnī zubara al-ḥadīdi ḥattā idhā sāwā bayna al-ṣadafayni qāla unfukhū ḥattā idhā jaʿalahu nāran qāla ātūnī ufrigh ʿalayhi qiṭran

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Bring me pieces of iron. When he had leveled the space between the two mountain sides, he said: Blow. When he made it like fire, he said: Bring it to me, so I may pour molten copper over it.”

Al-ḥadīd denotes material used to construct a barrier.

زُبَر (zubar) denotes its substantial pieces or masses.

Iron here is a construction material shaped through heat and human work.

It should not be translated “boundary,” even though a barrier across a passage results from using it.

Using a material to create a boundary does not make its name mean “boundary” in that sentence.

Sabaʾ 34:10: iron softened for David

Verse text:

وَلَقَدْ آتَيْنَا دَاوُودَ مِنَّا فَضْلًا يَا جِبَالُ أَوِّبِي مَعَهُ وَالطَّيْرَ وَأَلَنَّا لَهُ الْحَدِيدَ

wa-la-qad ātaynā dāwūda minnā faḍlan yā jibālu awwibī maʿahu wa-al-ṭayra wa-alannā lahu al-ḥadīda

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“We indeed bestowed favor upon David from Us: Mountains, return with him, and birds too. We softened iron for him.”

Al-ḥadīd names iron, while أَلَنَّا (alannā) describes softening it.

Sabaʾ 34:11 then mentions making full coats of mail and proportioning their links.

Iron thus relates to craft and protective equipment in this setting.

Ḥadīd itself does not establish precisely how it became soft or the industrial method used.

The verse describes David’s bestowed advantage and its result; technical details require independent evidence.

Al-Ḥajj 22:21: implements of iron

Al-Ḥajj 22:21 mentions maqāmiʿu min ḥadīdin.

Maqāmiʿ concerns striking or subduing implements; min ḥadīdin identifies their material.

Iron is what the implements are made from.

Ḥadīd itself therefore does not mean striking or punishment; maqāmiʿ and context supply that information.

Al-Ḥadīd 57:25: iron’s strength and benefits

Verse text:

لَقَدْ أَرْسَلْنَا رُسُلَنَا بِالْبَيِّنَاتِ وَأَنْزَلْنَا مَعَهُمُ الْكِتَابَ وَالْمِيزَانَ لِيَقُومَ النَّاسُ بِالْقِسْطِ وَأَنْزَلْنَا الْحَدِيدَ فِيهِ بَأْسٌ شَدِيدٌ وَمَنَافِعُ لِلنَّاسِ وَلِيَعْلَمَ اللَّهُ مَنْ يَنْصُرُهُ وَرُسُلَهُ بِالْغَيْبِ إِنَّ اللَّهَ قَوِيٌّ عَزِيزٌ

la-qad arsalnā rusulanā bi-al-bayyināti wa-anzalnā maʿahumu al-kitāba wa-al-mīzāna li-yaqūma al-nāsu bi-al-qisṭi wa-anzalnā al-ḥadīda fīhi baʾsun shadīdun wa-manāfiʿu li-al-nāsi wa-li-yaʿlama Allāhu man yanṣuruhu wa-rusulahu bi-al-ghaybi inna Allāha qawiyyun ʿazīzun

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“We sent Our messengers with clear signs, and sent down with them the Book and the balance so people might uphold justice. We sent down iron, containing great strength and benefits for people, and so The God may know who supports Him and His messengers unseen. The God is strong and mighty.”

Al-ḥadīd here names iron.

The verse gives it two characteristics: baʾsun shadīdun and manāfiʿu li-al-nāsi.

Iron is described through both strength and its benefits for people.

The verse begins by identifying upholding justice as the purpose of sending messengers, the Book, and the balance.

The juxtaposition raises relationships between power, tools, and justice beyond iron as a natural material alone.

Ḥadīd itself does not make every military use legitimate, nor does it deny defensive or military applications.

Iron can make varied tools. The rightness of using them depends on purpose and circumstances.

Does anzalnā al-ḥadīd establish iron’s cosmic origin?

Modern natural science studies the formation of heavy elements through stellar processes and the arrival of some materials on Earth through celestial bodies.

Anzalnā al-ḥadīd alone nevertheless does not define a particular mechanism of iron production or how all terrestrial iron arrived.

Anzala has varied Qur’anic uses: Al-Aʿrāf 7:26 mentions sending down clothing, and Al-Zumar 39:6 sending down livestock.

Every use of anzala should therefore not imply direct physical movement from outer space to Earth without independent evidence.

This does not deny elements’ natural or cosmic origins, which empirical science examines through its own data.

For Ḥ D D, the direct meaning of al-ḥadīd remains iron; its origin is a separate question from lexical meaning.

Qāf 50:22: keen, penetrating sight

Verse text:

لَقَدْ كُنْتَ فِي غَفْلَةٍ مِنْ هَٰذَا فَكَشَفْنَا عَنْكَ غِطَاءَكَ فَبَصَرُكَ الْيَوْمَ حَدِيدٌ

la-qad kunta fī ghaflatin min hādhā fa-kashafnā ʿanka ghiṭāʾaka fa-baṣaruka al-yawma ḥadīdun

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“You were heedless of this; We have removed your covering from before you, and today your sight is keen and penetrating.”

Ḥadīdun here describes baṣaruka.

It therefore does not mean iron.

Baṣarun ḥadīdun denotes keen, penetrating sight in Arabic.

A covering has been removed, and the person now sees something more clearly.

The image relates to sharpness and penetration.

Ḥadīd itself does not mean knowing every truth or possessing unlimited knowledge.

The verse concerns a particular condition in which previously neglected reality becomes evident.

Nor should the description be confined without evidence to a physical alteration of the eye or biological visual capacity.

It expresses penetration and clarity of sight; the fuller experience requires the broader context.

7. Alsinatin ḥidādin: sharp, biting speech

Al-Aḥzāb 33:19

Verse text:

أَشِحَّةً عَلَيْكُمْ فَإِذَا جَاءَ الْخَوْفُ رَأَيْتَهُمْ يَنْظُرُونَ إِلَيْكَ تَدُورُ أَعْيُنُهُمْ كَالَّذِي يُغْشَىٰ عَلَيْهِ مِنَ الْمَوْتِ فَإِذَا ذَهَبَ الْخَوْفُ سَلَقُوكُمْ بِأَلْسِنَةٍ حِدَادٍ أَشِحَّةً عَلَى الْخَيْرِ أُولَٰئِكَ لَمْ يُؤْمِنُوا فَأَحْبَطَ اللَّهُ أَعْمَالَهُمْ وَكَانَ ذَٰلِكَ عَلَى اللَّهِ يَسِيرًا

ashiḥḥatan ʿalaykum fa-idhā jāʾa al-khawfu raʾaytahum yanẓurūna ilayka tadūru aʿyunuhum ka-alladhī yughshā ʿalayhi mina al-mawti fa-idhā dhahaba al-khawfu salaqūkum bi-alsinatin ḥidādin ashiḥḥatan ʿalā al-khayri ulāʾika lam yuʾminū fa-aḥbaṭa Allāhu aʿmālahum wa-kāna dhālika ʿalā Allāhi yasīran

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“They are severely grudging toward you. When fear comes, you see them looking at you, their eyes revolving like someone overcome by death. When fear passes, they lash you with sharp, biting tongues, grudging toward good. They have not believed, so The God has rendered their deeds fruitless; that is easy for The God.”

Ḥidādin describes alsinatin here.

It is an adjectival plural of ḥadīd, “sharp” or “cutting.”

The tongues are sharp, penetrating, and hurtful in speech.

Material sharpness is applied to forceful speech.

The verse compares conduct during fear with conduct after it passes.

Their terror is prominent during danger; once it passes, they attack others with sharp speech.

Ḥidād here therefore concerns more than eloquence or skillful speaking: the context emphasizes harsh, hurtful words.

The word itself does not name an iron weapon.

“Sharp tongue” is a familiar image comparing speech’s biting effect with a cutting tool.

Al-Ṭabarī and lexical sources: sharp speech distinguished from mourning

Adjectival ḥidād and mourning ḥidād

Arabic also uses ḥidād for refraining from adornment during mourning.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:19’s alsinatin ḥidādin, however, describes sharp, biting tongues.

The verse says nothing about mourning, black clothing, or avoiding adornment.

Identical written forms should not merge distinct meanings.

8. Comparison with related roots

Ḥ D D (ḥadd) and Ḥ J Z (ḥajaza): boundary and intervention

Ḥajaza concerns separating, preventing, and restraining.

Ḥadd can likewise be a boundary or barrier between two things.

Ḥadd also often denotes a specified endpoint or extent, while ḥajz emphasizes separating and preventing access or passage.

The families overlap in some uses without being fully synonymous.

Ḥ D D (ḥadd) and ʿ D W (iʿtadā): boundary and transgressing it

Study of ʿ D W

Ḥadd identifies the boundary.

Iʿtadā, when used with boundaries, describes wrongfully crossing it.

The families occur together in Al-Baqarah 2:229 and Al-Ṭalāq 65:1.

Defining a boundary and crossing it are different matters.

The boundary itself is not transgression; transgression occurs when someone violates the prescribed limit.

Ḥ D D (ḥadd) and Ḥ R M (ḥarrama): setting limits and prohibiting

Study of Ḥ R M

Ḥarrama concerns prohibition; related uses concern inviolability and protection.

Ḥadd can mark a boundary one must not cross.

Not all boundaries are merely prohibitions: some establish rights and responsibilities to uphold.

Ḥudūd Allāh should therefore not automatically become “The God’s prohibitions” in every verse.

Conversely, a Qur’anic prohibition can establish a binding boundary for conduct.

The fields are related but distinct.

Ḥ D D (ḥadd) and F Ṣ L (faṣl): boundary and separation

Study of F Ṣ L

Faṣala concerns separating and clarifying distinctions.

Ḥadd can likewise mark a boundary between two things.

Boundary and separation are related, but faṣl emphasizes separating or distinguishing, whereas ḥadd can denote the boundary or endpoint itself.

Ḥ D D (ḥadīd) and SH D D (shadīd): iron and intensity

Study of SH D D

Nominal ḥadīd names iron.

Shadīd belongs to SH D D and concerns intensity and firmness.

Al-Ḥadīd 57:25 describes iron with baʾsun shadīdun.

Material name and intensity are therefore distinct.

Although iron can be hard and resistant, ḥadīd should not become “intensity” in this verse.

Ḥ D D (ḥādda) and KH L F (khālafa): confrontation and disagreement

Study of KH L F

Arabic khālafa concerns opposition or taking a different course.

Qur’anic ḥādda likewise concerns opposing The God and His Messenger.

The families are close, while muḥāddah can emphasize an opposing stance and confrontation.

The root alone does not make every scholarly disagreement, question, or interpretive difference opposition to The God.

Ḥ D D (ḥadd) and Ḥ D TH (ḥadatha): boundary and coming into being

Study of Ḥ D TH

Ḥ D D and Ḥ D TH have similar letters.

Ḥ D TH, however, concerns coming into being, newness, and occurrence.

Al-Ṭalāq 65:1 contains both families: ḥudūd Allāh from Ḥ D D, and yuḥdithu from Ḥ D TH.

Their proximity within one verse makes careful root distinction necessary.

9. Interpretive challenges and technical usage

Are divine boundaries merely prohibitions?

No. Qur’anic usage includes both limits on prohibited conduct and frameworks of rights and responsibilities.

Al-Baqarah 2:229 calls for upholding boundaries.

Al-Tawbah 9:112 describes guarding them.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:13 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:14 address observance or transgression following inheritance and rights provisions.

Boundary therefore means more than “do not do this”: it can establish an entitlement to fulfill or responsibility to uphold.

Does making a rule stricter better preserve divine boundaries?

The root establishes no such principle.

A boundary is a specified limit. Moving it without evidence, even for greater strictness, does not preserve the original boundary.

Studying Qur’anic instructions requires distinguishing observance from adding restrictions in The God’s name.

This especially matters where the Qur’an specifies exceptions, accommodation, or reparatory options.

Does ḥudūd always mean penal rules?

No. That restriction reflects a specialized legal usage rather than every attested meaning. The lexical distinction alone does not date every penal application to one later period.

Qur’anic ḥudūd occurs in varied settings, not only punishment.

The juristic term must therefore be distinguished from broader, direct Qur’anic usage.

Is muḥāddah any difference of opinion?

No. Its Qur’anic uses concern opposition to The God and His Messenger.

Questions, ignorance, or scholarly differences alone do not establish the hostile stance described in those verses.

Identifying such opposition requires conduct and clear evidence.

Does ḥadīd always mean iron?

No.

Five Qur’anic uses name iron; Qāf 50:22 describes keen, penetrating sight.

Ḥidād in Al-Aḥzāb 33:19 likewise describes sharp, biting tongues.

The modified noun and context establish the meaning.

10. Translation proposals for Forqan

For ḥudūd Allāh, “The God’s boundaries” or “The God’s prescribed limits” is appropriate. Contextual explanation should identify the relevant rights, commands, or prohibitions.

For iqāmatu ḥudūdi Allāh, “upholding The God’s boundaries” or “observing and maintaining The God’s prescribed limits” is appropriate.

For al-ḥāfiẓūna li-ḥudūdi Allāh, “guardians of The God’s boundaries” is natural.

For yataʿadda ḥudūda Allāh, “transgresses The God’s boundaries” is appropriate.

For yuḥādidi Allāha wa-rasūlahu and yuḥāddūna Allāha wa-rasūlahu, “opposes The God and His Messenger” or “stands against The God and His Messenger” is appropriate.

For al-ḥadīd in Al-Isrāʾ 17:50, Al-Kahf 18:96, Al-Ḥajj 22:21, Sabaʾ 34:10, and Al-Ḥadīd 57:25, the direct meaning is “iron.”

For fa-baṣaruka al-yawma ḥadīdun in Qāf 50:22, “today your sight is keen and penetrating” is appropriate.

For bi-alsinatin ḥidādin in Al-Aḥzāb 33:19, “with sharp, biting tongues” preserves the adjective’s meaning.

Forqan’s word boxes should likewise retain two separate fields.

Brief root meaning: “Boundary and limit; prevention, sharpness, and cutting.”

Meaning in the verse: Depending on context: “divinely prescribed boundaries,” “opposition and confrontation,” “iron,” “keen sight,” or “sharp, biting speech.”

One explanation cannot serve every derivative. Each verse should preserve distinctions among noun, verb, adjective, and their constructions.

11. Conclusion

Arabic Ḥ D D has two basic axes: prevention and a thing’s edge or endpoint.

The field connects boundaries, setting limits, preventing transgression, sharpness, hardness, iron, and opposition.

The root occurs 25 times in the Qur’an.

Ḥudūd occurs fourteen times concerning divinely prescribed boundaries. These are not merely prohibitions or punishments: they also frame human rights, responsibilities, and conduct. Their grammatical constructions vary; not every instance literally says ḥudūd Allāh.

The Qur’an speaks of upholding, guarding, not approaching, and not transgressing boundaries. Each verb has a distinct role that translation and analysis should preserve.

Ḥādda and its forms describe opposing The God and His Messenger, without making every question, scholarly difference, or lack of knowledge such opposition.

Ḥadīd names iron in five verses and keen sight in Qāf 50:22; ḥidād describes sharp tongues in Al-Aḥzāb 33:19.

Thus boundary and prevention, alongside sharpness and cutting, clarify the family’s main field, while each derivative’s form and context establish its meaning.

The central point for Qur’anic ḥudūd Allāh is recognizing and observing boundaries as prescribed. Breaking them is transgression; adding unsupported limits in The God’s name cannot be justified merely as guarding them.

A boundary retains its meaning when its location is clear. Moving it does not preserve the original limit.

Such precision matters both for observing divine instruction and for avoiding violations of human rights in religion’s name.
