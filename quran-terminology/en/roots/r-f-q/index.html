Companionship, gentleness, support, and provision for comfort

Brief root meaning: gentleness and consideration in conduct; companionship and accompanying others; a means or condition allowing benefit, comfort, and support. In the bodily branch, mirfaq also names the elbow.

1. The root and its semantic field

The Arabic triliteral R F Q produces a family whose meanings initially seem diverse: a companion, gentleness, an elbow, a means of comfort, and a reclining place.

Rifq contrasts with harshness and roughness. Someone acting with rifq treats another gently and thoughtfully. Yet the family also concerns traveling together, leaning on an elbow, and having amenities available.

This range prevents reducing the family to the familiar modern sense of kindness. Rafīq is a companion in one verse; marāfiq names specific body parts in another; and murtafaq describes a place for reclining and resting in two verses.

Forms examined and Qur’anic derivatives

R F Q occurs five times in the Qur’an, in four lexical forms:

  • رَفِيقًا — rafīqan: a noun on the faʿīl pattern, companion; Al-Nisāʾ 4:69.
  • الْمَرَافِقِ — al-marāfiqi: plural of mirfaq, elbows; Al-Māʾidah 5:6.
  • مِرْفَقًا — mirfaqan: something providing comfort or benefit; Al-Kahf 18:16.
  • مُرْتَفَقًا — murtafaqan: a place for reclining, resting, or staying; Al-Kahf 18:29 and Al-Kahf 18:31.

Familiar Arabic forms such as rifq, rufaqāʾ, rifqah, murāfaqah, rāfaqa, and taraffaqa do not occur in the Qur’an in those exact forms.

Qur’anic analysis should therefore concentrate on the four attested forms. Other derivatives illuminate Arabic usage without artificially increasing the Qur’anic count.

2. Early Arabic meaning and linguistic witnesses

Rifq contrasted with harshness

Early lexical accounts contrast al-rifq with al-ʿunf. Rafaqa bihi and taraffaqa bihi describe gentle, considerate treatment. Arfaqahu can mean benefiting someone or providing what serves their needs.

Concord and closeness without harshness have been proposed as a central meaning, connecting companionship, the elbow, and means of comfort.

This helps explain many derivatives. A companion travels or works alongside another; considerate conduct accommodates another’s situation; an amenity facilitates benefit and action.

That relationship is not a proven history of every word’s derivation. An elbow does not itself mean kindness, and rafīq does not always describe someone’s moral gentleness.

Rafīq and companionship in Arab life

Rafīq names someone accompanying another. Rifqah or rufqah also named a party traveling together.

Travel companionship was more than two people incidentally occupying one place. Fellow travelers could depend on one another when moving, stopping, transporting loads, and confronting difficulties.

This explains the connection with cooperation and assistance. Rafīq alone nevertheless does not establish a close friend, faithful helper, or righteous person. Context and accompanying descriptions must supply those qualities.

Mirfaq: bodily support and a means of benefit

Early Arabic uses mirfaq and marfiq for the elbow, where upper arm and forearm meet. Mirfaq can also name something a person benefits from or relies on.

Mirfaqah names a cushion or support. Irtafaqa can mean reclining or leaning.

The bodily connection is clear: a seated person can rest an elbow on the ground, a cushion, or another support, transferring some bodily weight to it. Murtafaq for a place or situation of reclining is understandable in this setting.

This does not establish that all senses of gentleness, companionship, and comfort originated from the body-part name. The forms belong to one lexical family; their historical sequence requires further evidence.

Early poetic witnesses

An elegy attributed in early accounts to Aʿshā Bāhilah says:

فَبِتُّ مُرْتَفِقًا لِلنَّجْمِ أَرْقُبُهُ
حَيْرَانَ ذَا حَذَرٍ لَوْ يَنْفَعُ الْحَذَرُ

fa-bittu murtafiqan li-al-najmi arqubuhu / ḥayrāna dhā ḥadharin law yanfaʿu al-ḥadharu

“I spent the night reclining, watching the star, bewildered and fearful—if fear could be of any use.”

The line describes an anxious night. Murtafiqan, with kasrah on fāʾ, is an active participle describing the reclining person. It differs from Qur’anic murtafaqan, with fatḥah on fāʾ, a reclining place. Reclining here is a bodily posture, not psychological peace.

This witness shows that irtifāq can concern a reclining posture even for someone distressed or restless.

Another witness comes from the pre-Islamic poet Bishr ibn Abī Khāzim, in a poem complaining about the house of Āl Laʾm:

فَإِنِّي وَالشَّكَاةَ مِنْ آلِ لَأْمٍ
كَذَاتِ الضِّغْنِ تَمْشِي فِي الرِّفَاقِ

fa-innī wa-al-shakāta min āli Laʾmin / ka-dhāti al-ḍighni tamshī fī al-rifāqi

An approximate rendering: “I and my complaint against Āl Laʾm are like a she-camel longing to return home, walking in a rifāq tether.”

Rifāq here names a tether restricting a camel’s movement. Accounts describe fastening a lower limb to the upper limb or neck. It concerns physical restraint, not gentleness or kindness.

The witness demonstrates the family’s actual range. Historical or figurative connections between branches do not transfer rifq’s positive moral associations to every derivative.

3. Qur’anic uses

A. Rafīqan: good companionship in Al-Nisāʾ 4:69

Verse text:

وَمَنْ يُطِعِ اللَّهَ وَالرَّسُولَ فَأُولَٰئِكَ مَعَ الَّذِينَ أَنْعَمَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِمْ مِنَ النَّبِيِّينَ وَالصِّدِّيقِينَ وَالشُّهَدَاءِ وَالصَّالِحِينَ ۚ وَحَسُنَ أُولَٰئِكَ رَفِيقًا

wa-man yuṭiʿi Allāha wa-al-rasūla fa-ulāʾika maʿa alladhīna anʿama Allāhu ʿalayhim mina al-nabiyyīna wa-al-ṣiddīqīna wa-al-shuhadāʾi wa-al-ṣāliḥīna wa-ḥasuna ulāʾika rafīqan

Al-Nisāʾ 4:69

Translation:

“Whoever obeys The God and the Messenger will be with those whom The God has favored: the prophets, the thoroughly truthful, the witnesses, and the righteous. How excellent those are as companions!”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:69

Rafīqan here concerns companionship.

The verse has two related parts. Fa-ulāʾika maʿa alladhīna anʿama Allāhu ʿalayhim describes the obedient as being with those receiving divine favor. Wa-ḥasuna ulāʾika rafīqan then praises those companions.

Rafīqan is singular in form but can have collective reference here. Early grammatical and interpretive accounts compare it with nouns such as ṣadīq, whose singular form can also refer to a group in some uses.

The common grammatical analysis treats rafīqan as an accusative specification, a tamyīz: it identifies the respect in which they are excellent—as companions.

The positive value does not come solely from R F Q. The verse identifies prophets, the truthful, witnesses, and the righteous, then explicitly praises them with ḥasuna.

Rafīq here is therefore more than simply a kind person: it is a companion whose goodness is established by the identities and standing described in context.

The verse does not make every Arabic rafīq righteous or worthy. Companionship is a relationship; context determines the companion’s moral character.

B. Al-marāfiqi: elbows in Al-Māʾidah 5:6

Part of the washing verse says:

Qur’anic text:

فَاغْسِلُوا وُجُوهَكُمْ وَأَيْدِيَكُمْ إِلَى الْمَرَافِقِ

fa-ighsilū wujūhakum wa-aydiyakum ilā al-marāfiqi

Al-Māʾidah 5:6

Translation:

“Wash your faces and your hands and arms up to the elbows.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:6

Al-marāfiqi is the plural of mirfaq or marfiq and denotes elbows: the joints connecting the forearms with the upper arms.

This is a concrete bodily use. Ilā al-marāfiqi expresses a washing boundary through a familiar body part.

Here marāfiq does not mean service facilities, amenities, gentleness, or companions, although such senses and related terms occur within the same family.

Ilā contributes to specifying the limit. How that limit is applied in washing requires analysis of the full command and preposition; the root alone cannot determine it.

The verse clearly distinguishes family meaning from a particular derivative. Although rifq elsewhere concerns gentleness, al-marāfiq names body parts and should be translated accordingly.

C. Mirfaqan: provision of relief in Al-Kahf 18:16

Verse text:

وَإِذِ اعْتَزَلْتُمُوهُمْ وَمَا يَعْبُدُونَ إِلَّا اللَّهَ فَأْوُوا إِلَى الْكَهْفِ يَنْشُرْ لَكُمْ رَبُّكُمْ مِنْ رَحْمَتِهِ وَيُهَيِّئْ لَكُمْ مِنْ أَمْرِكُمْ مِرْفَقًا

wa-idhi iʿtazaltumūhum wa-mā yaʿbudūna illā Allāha fa-ʾwū ilā al-kahfi yanshur lakum rabbukum min raḥmatihi wa-yuhayyiʾ lakum min amrikum mirfaqan

Al-Kahf 18:16

Translation:

“When you have withdrawn from them and what they worship apart from The God, seek refuge in the cave; your Lord will spread some of His mercy over you and prepare for you comfort in your situation.”

Al-Kahf 18:16

Mirfaqan concerns something that makes difficulty more bearable or provides a means of benefit and comfort.

The youths withdraw from their people’s religious and social environment and seek refuge in a cave. Their needs extend beyond emotional calm to safety, shelter, and provisions for continuing life.

Mirfaqan names no particular object such as food, bedding, or water. Those may illustrate needed resources but should not replace the word’s meaning in translation.

Wa-yuhayyiʾ lakum matters too: hayyaʾa means preparing or providing. The verse describes something beneficial and comforting being prepared for their circumstances.

This connects with the youths’ prayer in Al-Kahf 18:10:

رَبَّنَا آتِنَا مِنْ لَدُنْكَ رَحْمَةً وَهَيِّئْ لَنَا مِنْ أَمْرِنَا رَشَدًا

rabbanā ātinā min ladunka raḥmatan wa-hayyiʾ lanā min amrinā rashadan

Al-Kahf 18:10

“Our Lord, grant us mercy from Yourself and prepare for us right guidance in our situation.”

Al-Kahf 18:10 emphasizes sound direction through rashadan; Al-Kahf 18:16 concerns comfort and practical benefit through mirfaqan amid that difficulty.

The concepts are compatible but not identical. Someone may choose the right path yet still need shelter, resources, and relief to follow it.

Mirfaqan expresses that aspect of relief and provision for comfort.

The Ḥafṣ reading has mirfaqan; recognized readings also transmit marfiqan. These do not create opposite meanings here: both concern benefit, comfort, and something by which people manage their affairs.

D. Murtafaqan: a resting place in Al-Kahf 18:29 and Al-Kahf 18:31

Al-Kahf uses murtafaqan twice in contrasting scenes.

The first describes the wrongdoers’ fate and a fire surrounding them:

Verse text:

وَقُلِ الْحَقُّ مِنْ رَبِّكُمْ ۖ فَمَنْ شَاءَ فَلْيُؤْمِنْ وَمَنْ شَاءَ فَلْيَكْفُرْ ۚ إِنَّا أَعْتَدْنَا لِلظَّالِمِينَ نَارًا أَحَاطَ بِهِمْ سُرَادِقُهَا ۚ وَإِنْ يَسْتَغِيثُوا يُغَاثُوا بِمَاءٍ كَالْمُهْلِ يَشْوِي الْوُجُوهَ ۚ بِئْسَ الشَّرَابُ وَسَاءَتْ مُرْتَفَقًا

wa-quli al-ḥaqqu min rabbikum fa-man shāʾa fa-l-yuʾmin wa-man shāʾa fa-l-yakfur innā aʿtadnā li-al-ẓālimīna nāran aḥāṭa bihim surādiquhā wa-in yastaghīthū yughāthū bi-māʾin ka-al-muhli yashwī al-wujūha biʾsa al-sharābu wa-sāʾat murtafaqan

Al-Kahf 18:29

Translation:

“Say: The truth is from your Lord. Whoever wills, let them believe; whoever wills, let them reject. We have prepared for the wrongdoers a fire whose enclosure surrounds them. If they cry for help, they will be answered with water like molten metal, scorching faces. How evil a drink, and how evil a resting place!”

Al-Kahf 18:29

Murtafaqan accompanies sāʾat and describes a place of punishment and distress instead of comfort.

The expression is contrasting and ironic: a supposed resting place proves terrifying.

The root itself does not acquire the meaning punishment or evil. Negativity comes from the fire, punishment, and sāʾat in context.

By contrast, Al-Kahf 18:31 describes the outcome for those who believe and do righteous deeds:

Verse text:

أُولَٰئِكَ لَهُمْ جَنَّاتُ عَدْنٍ تَجْرِي مِنْ تَحْتِهِمُ الْأَنْهَارُ يُحَلَّوْنَ فِيهَا مِنْ أَسَاوِرَ مِنْ ذَهَبٍ وَيَلْبَسُونَ ثِيَابًا خُضْرًا مِنْ سُنْدُسٍ وَإِسْتَبْرَقٍ مُتَّكِئِينَ فِيهَا عَلَى الْأَرَائِكِ ۚ نِعْمَ الثَّوَابُ وَحَسُنَتْ مُرْتَفَقًا

ulāʾika lahum jannātu ʿadnin tajrī min taḥtihimu al-anhāru yuḥallawna fīhā min asāwira min dhahabin wa-yalbasūna thiyāban khuḍran min sundusin wa-istabraqin muttakiʾīna fīhā ʿalā al-arāʾiki niʿma al-thawābu wa-ḥasunat murtafaqan

Al-Kahf 18:31

Translation:

“For them are gardens of lasting residence, with rivers flowing beneath them. There they are adorned with gold bracelets and wear green garments of fine silk and thick brocade, reclining on couches. How excellent the reward, and how excellent a resting place!”

Al-Kahf 18:31

Reclining is explicit here, since muttakiʾīna fīhā ʿalā al-arāʾiki precedes murtafaqan.

The closing murtafaqan thus fits the setting of ease, reclining, and enjoying blessings.

The basic meaning remains the same; the two places’ qualities differ. One is condemned with sāʾat, the other praised with ḥasunat.

Morphologically, murtafaq follows the muftaʿal pattern of Form VIII and can function here as a place noun: somewhere to recline, rest, or benefit from available amenities.

The couches in Al-Kahf 18:31 clearly support the resting sense. Al-Kahf 18:29 uses the same word in a contrasting scene to expose that place’s badness.

Resting place can preserve the image in both verses, provided the praise or condemnation is also conveyed clearly.

4. R F Q and related words

R F Q (rifq) and L Ṭ F (luṭf)

Both sometimes concern gentle conduct, but they are not identical.

Behavioral rifq emphasizes consideration and avoiding harshness; other branches concern companionship, benefit, and support.

Luṭf can concern delicacy, gentleness, precision, and thoughtful conduct. Al-Kahf 18:19 uses wa-l-yatalaṭṭaf for the person sent to obtain food, who must act carefully and discreetly.

Discreet and cautious conduct is prominent there; mirfaqan in Al-Kahf 18:16 concerns comfort and relief being provided.

Both can relate to gentleness, but their objects and functions in these verses differ.

R F Q (rifq) and Ṣ Ḥ B (ṣuḥbah)

Study of Ṣ Ḥ B

Ṣuḥbah and muṣāḥabah concern being together and companionship. Ṣāḥib may be a fellow traveler, associate, or someone with an established relationship to another.

Rafīq likewise names a companion, with accompanying another along a route or undertaking prominent in its lexical field.

No absolute rule makes ṣāḥib merely incidental company and rafīq invariably an intimate, faithful friend. Arabic usage overlaps, and context determines the precise distinction.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:69 establishes the companions’ goodness through their descriptions and ḥasuna, not through any claim that rafīq is inherently superior to ṣāḥib.

R F Q (rifq) and Y S R (yusr)

Study of Y S R

Yusr concerns ease in contrast to difficulty. Some R F Q uses concern something that enables comfort and benefit.

Mirfaqan in Al-Kahf 18:16 is more than a feeling of ease: it is provided for the youths in their situation and may bring practical relief.

Ease can be a result of irtifāq without being an exact equivalent of mirfaq in every use.

R F Q (rifq) and R F R F (rafraf)

Study of R F R F

Despite a resemblance in writing, these are separate roots.

R F Q is triliteral, with rafīq, mirfaq, and murtafaq. R F R F is quadriliteral and appears as rafrafin in Al-Raḥmān 55:76.

Some forms can overlap in contexts of comfort and support, but that does not establish a shared root or identical meaning.

5. Structural points and translation difficulties

A major difficulty is transferring one familiar meaning to every derivative. Reducing rifq to gentleness or kindness obscures al-marāfiq and murtafaq.

A second difficulty is equating singular form with singular reference. Rafīqan in Al-Nisāʾ 4:69 is morphologically singular yet concerns a group. Natural Persian or English may use companions without rewriting the Arabic as a plural.

A third distinction separates bodily reference from benefit. Mirfaq can name an elbow or a useful means or provision; neither sense should displace the other by force.

The contrast between Al-Kahf 18:29 and Al-Kahf 18:31 also matters. Resting place preserves part of the lexical image, but the punishment verse requires clear condemnation so that it is not mistaken for genuine comfort.

Conversely, translating murtafaq itself as the worst punishment removes its image. Punishment comes from other parts of the verse, not this word alone.

The family illustrates three levels requiring distinction: the broad lexical family, the specific derivative, and the value or consequence supplied by context.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

The following equivalents suit Qur’anic R F Q:

رَفِيقًا — Al-Nisāʾ 4:69: companion, collectively companions here. “How excellent those are as companions!” is natural and precise.

الْمَرَافِقِ — Al-Māʾidah 5:6: elbows. The verse names a specific body part without needing an interpretive replacement.

مِرْفَقًا — Al-Kahf 18:16: comfort, relief, or a means of comfort, according to the sentence. “Prepare for you comfort in your situation” is defensible. Practical resources and relief can clarify the explanation without necessarily entering the base translation.

مُرْتَفَقًا — Al-Kahf 18:29: resting place in a denunciatory construction. “How evil a resting place!” preserves the contrast.

مُرْتَفَقًا — Al-Kahf 18:31: a place for resting or reclining. Given the couches, “How excellent a resting place!” fits.

For the brief root meaning in Forqan’s index:

R F Q: gentleness and consideration; companionship; support and provision for comfort and benefit.

This introduces the family’s principal field while leaving each derivative’s meaning to its context.

7. Conclusion and final definition

R F Q has related but distinct branches. Rifq concerns gentle conduct; rafīq is a companion; mirfaq can name an elbow or a useful means or provision; murtafaq concerns a place for reclining and resting.

The Qur’anic uses clearly demonstrate these differences:

Al-Nisāʾ 4:69 concerns excellent companions. Al-Māʾidah 5:6 names elbows. Al-Kahf 18:16 concerns comfort and relief provided amid difficulty. Al-Kahf 18:29 and Al-Kahf 18:31 describe contrasting places with murtafaq: one condemned, one praised.

Early Arabic also extends beyond moral gentleness to leaning on an elbow and even a tether restricting a camel’s movement.

Final definition of R F Q:

The principal branches are gentleness and consideration in conduct, companionship and accompanying others, and relying on or benefiting from what enables comfort and action. The elbow and derivatives naming places of support belong to the same family.

Connections through companionship, compatibility, and reliance are recognizable, but should not collapse into one artificial, exceptionless meaning. Each Qur’anic derivative has its own sense, and positive or negative value depends on sentence and context.

Understanding the root requires preserving both its members’ linguistic relationship and their actual differences in use.
