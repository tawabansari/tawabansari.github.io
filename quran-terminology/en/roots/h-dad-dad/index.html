The Qur’anic Root Ḥ Ḍ Ḍ

Qur’anic and lexical forms

يَحُضُّ
(yaḥuḍḍu)
Al-Ḥāqqah 69:34 and Al-Māʿūn 107:3; form I imperfect

تَحَاضُّونَ
(taḥāḍḍūna)
Al-Fajr 89:18; second-person plural form VI in the reading of Ḥafṣ

(ḥaḍḍ)

Urging action: from individual encouragement to mutual social responsibility

The Arabic root ح ض ض concerns urging, encouraging, and moving someone toward attention and action in a matter. حَضَّ is used when one person encourages another to do something and directs him toward action. This encouragement may involve emphasis and insistence, but does not necessarily mean coercion.

The root occurs three times in the Qur’an, each time concerning food for the needy. Two verses describe someone who does not encourage others to attend to a needy person’s food; the third reproaches a group for not encouraging one another to do so.

These usages show that the Qur’an addresses not only feeding people directly, but also the ethical significance of encouraging others to do it. The root itself does not mean feeding, spending, or charity: these concepts come from the action toward which the encouragement is directed.

Qur’anic forms: يَحُضُّ in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:34 and Al-Māʿūn 107:3, and تَحَاضُّونَ in Al-Fajr 89:18.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: three occurrences in two morphological forms

1. Lexical meaning and early Arabic usage

Ḥaḍḍa: encouraging and urging

حَضَّهُ عَلَى الْأَمْرِ means to urge and encourage someone to undertake something.

حَضَّ يَحُضُّ حَضًّا is the family’s basic form I verb. In this construction, the person encouraging another is the subject, while the action encouraged is usually introduced by عَلَى.

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris explains this meaning through الْبَعْثُ عَلَى الشَّيْءِ: prompting someone toward something.

Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs al-Lughah: urging and low ground as separate principles

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise presents ḥaḍḍ as a kind of ḥathth. This indicates the core meaning of motivating and encouraging action.

The form II حَضَّضَ also means encouraging and urging. For example, حَضَّضَ الْقَوْمَ عَلَى الْقِتَالِ means that someone urged a group to fight.

This example matters because ḥaḍḍ does not inherently mean encouraging a good deed. Someone may be encouraged toward a beneficial or harmful action. Its ethical value comes from what is encouraged and the circumstances, not from the root itself.

The distinction between ḥaḍḍ and ḥathth

Explanations attributed to Al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad distinguish ḥaḍḍ from ḥathth.

According to this explanation, ḥathth has a broader range, including driving and setting people or animals in motion, whereas ḥaḍḍ more often concerns encouraging an action that does not necessarily involve physical movement.

For example, making an animal move faster can be expressed through ḥathth. Encouraging a person to help someone in need, fight, or undertake a social action are clear usages of ḥaḍḍ.

Lexical reports do not all express the distinction identically, however. Lisān al-ʿArab also brings ḥaḍḍ close to motion and driving in some usages. The distinction is therefore better understood as a tendency in usage than an absolute, exceptionless rule.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: the ranges of ḥaḍḍ and ḥathth

Taḥāḍḍa: mutual encouragement

تَحَاضَّ الْقَوْمُ means that members of a group encourage one another to do something.

This form VI construction can indicate participation and a reciprocal relationship between people.

The same relationship appears in مُحَاضَّة: each party encourages the other to act.

It differs from حَضَّ in that the simple form can describe one person encouraging another, whereas تَحَاضَّ presents encouragement among several people reciprocally.

This distinction matters for Al-Fajr 89:18, which concerns a group who do not encourage one another to attend to food for the needy.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: taḥāḍḍ and muḥāḍḍah

Ḥaḍīḍ: low ground and the foot of a mountain

Another branch of the family appears in حَضِيض.

Dictionaries use حَضِيض for low ground, the foot of a mountain, and a place contrasted with an elevation.

Ibn Fāris identifies this usage as a separate principle from urging. Al-Ṣiḥāḥ likewise describes ḥaḍīḍ as the lower part of a mountain and the ground where it ends.

A rajaz passage transmitted by Al-Azharī and then Lisān al-ʿArab concerning the difficulty of poetry contains:

زَلَّتْ بِهِ إِلَى الْحَضِيضِ قَدَمُهُ

zallat bihi ilā al-ḥaḍīḍi qadamuhu

Meaning: His foot slipped, sending him down to the bottom.

In this image, ḥaḍīḍ is a low place, not the action of encouraging or urging. In the rajaz passage, poetry is compared to a difficult ladder on which an unskilled climber slips; falling to the bottom thus forms part of a literary image.

The fragment’s attribution and date are not sufficiently clear to present it as certain evidence from before Islam. The spatial meaning of ḥaḍīḍ is nevertheless explicitly recorded in dictionaries.

Lisān al-ʿArab: the rajaz on poetry’s difficulty and falling to the bottom

Its existence does not establish that ḥaḍḍ in the sense of encouragement originally meant lowering or raising someone. Without independent historical and lexical evidence, that connection would remain an imagined possibility.

حُضُض was also a name for a medicinal substance in Arabic. Early sources give differing accounts of its nature, ranging from a plant extract to a substance prepared from animal products. The name does not occur in the Qur’an, and its derivational relationship to encouragement is unclear. Tāj al-ʿArūs also reports Al-Ṣaghānī’s rejection of the animal-origin account and preference for a plant extract; these lexical reports should not be treated as a single, certain identification of the substance.

Tāj al-ʿArūs: differing accounts of ḥuḍuḍ and Al-Ṣaghānī’s criticism

2. Wa-lā yaḥuḍḍu: not encouraging food for the needy

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:34: neglecting the needy alongside not believing

وَلَا يَحُضُّ عَلَىٰ طَعَامِ الْمِسْكِينِ

wa-lā yaḥuḍḍu ʿalā ṭaʿāmi al-miskīni

Translation: And he did not urge others to provide food for the needy.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:34

The verse continues the description of a person facing the consequences of his conduct in the scene of reckoning.

The preceding verse, Al-Ḥāqqah 69:33, says that he did not believe in The God, the Great. Immediately afterward comes the failure to encourage food for the needy.

This juxtaposition matters. The passage speaks of more than an inner belief or verbal declaration: it places human conduct toward people in need alongside faith.

يَحُضُّ is a third-person singular imperfect verb. The person reproached here does not encourage others toward an action.

The verse does not specify exactly whom he was to encourage: family, relatives, neighbors, or other members of society. The verb means encouraging another, but the recipient of that encouragement is not named in the sentence.

Ḥaḍḍ does not itself mean feeding someone directly. A person may provide food, encourage someone else to do so, or do both.

What is explicitly stated here is the absence of encouragement to attend to food for the needy. The broader context suggests disregard for the person in need, but the verb alone does not establish that he never gave anyone any food.

The Qur’an places this disregard alongside not believing. This combination does not mean that every person who fails on one occasion to encourage feeding others necessarily denies The God. The verse describes a particular person’s condition and conduct in a particular context.

Al-Māʿūn 107:3: social conduct and denying the dīn

وَلَا يَحُضُّ عَلَىٰ طَعَامِ الْمِسْكِينِ

wa-lā yaḥuḍḍu ʿalā ṭaʿāmi al-miskīni

Translation: And he does not encourage others to provide food for the needy.

Al-Māʿūn 107:3

The Arabic text is identical to Al-Ḥāqqah 69:34, but the context differs.

Al-Māʿūn 107:1 opens by asking about someone who denies the dīn. Al-Māʿūn 107:2 then describes his conduct toward the orphan, and Al-Māʿūn 107:3 his failure to encourage food for the needy.

Denying the dīn is connected here with social conduct. Someone who violently pushes the orphan away and does not encourage others to attend to a needy person’s food displays disregard for vulnerable people’s rights in practice.

As in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:34, يَحُضُّ is singular. Personal responsibility is therefore prominent.

The issue is not only whether the person himself feeds someone in need. The Qur’an speaks of an action capable of affecting others’ conduct: encouraging their care.

For example, someone with limited financial means may be able to encourage a wealthy person to help a needy person. Conversely, someone may have means yet neither help directly nor call on others to do so.

The example clarifies encouragement’s practical range. It does not establish that every person without means must, in every circumstance, make others give financial help. Ability, awareness of the need, and the possibility of influencing others also matter in assessing responsibility.

The surah continues by discussing people whose religious conduct involves showing off and who withhold māʿūn. Its verses thus connect religious claims with actual conduct toward others.

Ḥaḍḍ itself does not mean faith, prayer, māʿūn, or spending, however. Its lexical meaning remains encouraging and urging; the subject and ethical value of that encouragement come from context.

3. Wa-lā taḥāḍḍūna: mutual encouragement in society

Al-Fajr 89:18

وَلَا تَحَاضُّونَ عَلَىٰ طَعَامِ الْمِسْكِينِ

wa-lā taḥāḍḍūna ʿalā ṭaʿāmi al-miskīni

Translation: And you do not encourage one another to provide food for the needy.

Al-Fajr 89:18

The subject resembles the two preceding verses, but the morphological distinction matters.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:34 and Al-Māʿūn 107:3 use يَحُضُّ; Al-Fajr 89:18 uses تَحَاضُّونَ.

تَحَاضُّونَ is a second-person plural form VI verb. In the reading of Ḥafṣ, it conveys mutual encouragement: members of a community urging one another to do something. Its underlying morphological form is تَتَحَاضُّونَ, with one of the initial two tāʾ letters omitted.

Here the Qur’an addresses more than a lack of encouragement from one individual. It addresses a group among whom this reciprocal relationship is absent.

Context: the relationship with wealth and people in need

Al-Fajr 89:15 and Al-Fajr 89:16 describe someone who takes financial abundance as a sign of divine honor and restricted provision as a sign of his own humiliation.

كَلَّا then rejects this understanding, directing attention to the audience’s conduct toward vulnerable people and wealth.

Al-Fajr 89:17 discusses failing to honor the orphan. Al-Fajr 89:18 addresses the absence of mutual encouragement to provide food for the needy. Al-Fajr 89:19 describes consuming inheritance indiscriminately, and Al-Fajr 89:20 an intense love of wealth.

This continuity places the absence of encouragement alongside other conduct involving wealth, others’ rights, and social standing.

The context suggests more than forgetting an isolated good deed. The verses criticize a society in which an intense love of wealth and misconduct toward others’ rights accompany disregard for those in need.

The distinction between taḥāḍḍūna and yaḥuḍḍu becomes prominent here. Mutual encouragement toward good can carry motivation and responsibility from one person to another. Where such encouragement is absent, the needy person is deprived even of this social support.

This follows from the verb’s reciprocal construction together with context. It must not be treated as meaning that the word taḥāḍḍūna itself contains a particular administrative system or economic mechanism.

Variant readings

Early accounts of Qur’anic readings transmit different forms for Al-Fajr 89:18.

تَحَاضُّونَ emphasizes reciprocity: you do not encourage one another.

تَحُضُّونَ is a basic form I verb, meaning “you do not encourage.”

يَحُضُّونَ is also transmitted. Rather than direct address, it describes a group’s conduct in the third person.

Al-Ṭabarī reports these differences. He explains taḥāḍḍūna as mutual encouragement, whereas taḥuḍḍūna expresses encouraging others without a morphological emphasis on reciprocity.

Some accounts also transmit تُحَاضُّونَ, explained in terms approaching watchfulness and care. This reading should not be imposed without examination on the familiar تَحَاضُّونَ.

The differences affect how the action is expressed, but in all the well-known readings the verse’s main subject remains attending to food for the needy.

Al-Ṭabarī, Al-Fajr 89:18: taḥāḍḍūna, taḥuḍḍūna, and yaḥuḍḍūna

4. Ṭaʿām al-miskīn and the limits of what ḥaḍḍ conveys

In all three Qur’anic occurrences, the verb is followed by عَلَىٰ طَعَامِ الْمِسْكِينِ.

This matters because the three verses speak of ḥaḍḍ directed toward ṭaʿām, rather than using an explicit verb أَطْعَمَ.

طَعَام can refer to food itself. In some constructions it is also explained in the sense of feeding or iṭʿām.

Accordingly, two related analyses are proposed for ṭaʿām al-miskīn.

In the first, it means “the needy person’s food”: food provided for, or due to reach, that person.

In the second, ṭaʿām is understood as iṭʿām, giving food to the needy.

Both ultimately concern meeting a needy person’s need for food, but their difference in wording should be recognized.

In natural translation, “encouraging others to feed the needy” conveys the verse clearly. “Encouraging the provision of food for the needy” is also appropriate and keeps the connection with the noun ṭaʿām more visible.

عَلَى introduces the action or subject toward which encouragement is directed. It does not establish physical pressure, legal coercion, or domination over others.

Encouraging is different from feeding

Direct feeding is expressed in the Qur’an through terms such as إِطْعَام and the verb أَطْعَمَ. Al-Insān 76:8, for example, describes giving food to a needy person, an orphan, and a captive.

In Al-Muddaththir 74:44, a group likewise say they were not among those who fed the needy.

These verses share the concern for people in need found in the three occurrences of ح ض ض, but use different verbs.

Iṭʿām expresses giving food itself; ḥaḍḍ emphasizes encouragement and urging toward that action.

Someone encouraging another to give food has not necessarily performed the act of feeding himself. Likewise, someone giving food has not necessarily encouraged others to do so.

The two behaviors can occur in one person, but remain distinct actions.

This distinction matters for understanding the verses. In the root’s three occurrences, the Qur’an attends to conduct that may prompt others to act. Encouragement does not itself replace actual help, but may open a route for help to reach someone in need.

Does ḥaḍḍ mean commanding or coercing?

Some interpretive explanations bring يَحُضُّ close to يَأْمُرُ: the person does not command or advise others to provide food.

This may convey the sentence’s point in ordinary language, but ḥaḍḍ and amr are not lexically identical.

Amr concerns giving a command and may proceed from authority. Ḥaḍḍ concerns urging and encouraging, without necessarily requiring a commander and subordinate.

Two friends can encourage one another to help someone in need without either possessing authority to command the other.

In ordinary usage, ḥaḍḍ also does not mean physical coercion. Insistence and emphasis may accompany encouragement, but the degree of pressure, method of persuasion, and limits of others’ choice are not determined by the root itself.

“Encourage,” “urge,” and “prompt” are therefore more appropriate equivalents for yaḥuḍḍu and taḥāḍḍūna than “force.”

5. Distinctions from related roots

Ḥ Ḍ Ḍ (ḥaḍḍ) and Ḥ TH TH (ḥathth)

These roots are close in encouragement and setting in motion.

In Arabic, ḥathth can also concern accelerating movement. Al-Aʿrāf 7:54 uses حَثِيثًا to describe swift pursuit.

The Qur’anic uses of ḥaḍḍ concern encouraging a person toward a social action.

The distinction is not absolute, however. Both can be used in Arabic to encourage an action. The principal difference should be sought in each construction and context.

Study of Ḥ TH TH

Ḥ Ḍ Ḍ (ḥaḍḍ) and Ḥ R Ḍ (ḥaraḍ)

Taḥrīḍ likewise concerns urging and emphatic encouragement.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:84 and Al-Anfāl 8:65 use حَرِّضْ for encouraging believers to fight.

Lexical sources sometimes explain ḥaḍḍ and taḥrīḍ through one another. This relative synonymy does not make them the same root.

Ḥaḍḍ occurs in the Qur’an only concerning food for the needy, but this Qur’anic restriction does not mean that Arabic used it exclusively for good deeds.

Study of Ḥ R Ḍ

Ḥ Ḍ Ḍ (ḥaḍḍ) and D ʿ W (daʿw)

Daʿwah concerns calling someone: to attend, accept a statement, follow a path, or undertake an action.

Ḥaḍḍ emphasizes encouraging and urging action.

Calling may accompany urging, but not every call is emphatic encouragement. A behavior may also be encouraged during conversation without any formal invitation.

Study of D ʿ W

Ḥ Ḍ Ḍ (ḥaḍḍ) and W Ṣ Y (waṣī)

Al-ʿAṣr 103:3 mentions تَوَاصَوْا بِالْحَقِّ and تَوَاصَوْا بِالصَّبْرِ; Al-Balad 90:17 contains تَوَاصَوْا بِالْمَرْحَمَةِ.

Tawāṣī concerns mutual counsel, whereas taḥāḍḍ emphasizes mutually encouraging action.

The two may approach one another in social conduct, but neither should become a fixed translation of the other. Counseling truth and perseverance has a broader scope than encouraging one particular action.

Study of W Ṣ Y

Ḥ Ḍ Ḍ (ḥaḍḍ) and Ḥ Ḍ R (ḥaḍara)

ح ض ر is a distinct root, concerning presence, arriving, and becoming available.

حَضَّ means encouraging, while حَضَرَ means being present. Their similar beginnings do not justify transferring one root’s meaning to the other.

حُضُور and حَضّ likewise should not be conflated because of their visual resemblance.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

For both occurrences of وَلَا يَحُضُّ عَلَىٰ طَعَامِ الْمِسْكِينِ, “and he does not encourage others to provide food for the needy” is suitable.

In Al-Ḥāqqah 69:34, given the account of past conduct, “and he did not urge others to provide food for the needy” is also natural.

In Al-Māʿūn 107:3, “and he does not encourage feeding the needy” is clear and fluent. The lexical explanation should nevertheless state that the verb emphasizes encouragement, not the direct act of feeding.

In Al-Fajr 89:18, “and you do not encourage one another to provide food for the needy” is preferable, preserving the reciprocal relationship of تَحَاضُّونَ.

For non-Qur’anic Arabic usages, “emphatic encouragement,” “encouraging,” and “urging” are suitable equivalents for ḥaḍḍ. Taḥḍīḍ may likewise indicate strong encouragement, but retaining the Arabic term in translation is unnecessary.

In its spatial branch, ḥaḍīḍ means “the foot of a mountain,” “low ground,” or “the lowest part.” This usage has no direct semantic relationship to the three Qur’anic occurrences.

Conclusion

The Arabic root ح ض ض has two familiar lexical branches: ḥaḍḍ, encouraging and urging toward something, and ḥaḍīḍ, low ground or the foot of a mountain. Dictionaries record them as distinct semantic fields; they should not be derived from one image without evidence.

Only the encouragement branch occurs in the Qur’an. يَحُضُّ twice describes an individual who does not encourage others to attend to food for the needy. تَحَاضُّونَ occurs once, emphasizing, in the familiar reading, the absence of mutual encouragement among members of a group.

All three concern human conduct toward those in need. In Al-Ḥāqqah this stands alongside faith; in Al-Māʿūn, denying the dīn and treatment of the orphan; in Al-Fajr, the orphan’s rights, inheritance, and an intense love of wealth.

Brief root meaning: The Qur’anic branch’s semantic core is urging and encouraging another to do something. In taḥāḍḍ, this extends to mutual encouragement between people.

The root’s important feature is its distinction between doing something and encouraging others to do it. Feeding a needy person is one action; urging others to provide that person’s food is another. The Qur’an addresses both within the network of human responsibility.

Ḥaḍḍ does not itself mean feeding, charity, coercion, a governmental command, or an economic system. These concepts can be examined only where independently supported by context and other verses.

An accurate translation of ح ض ض should therefore preserve encouragement and urging and, in Al-Fajr 89:18, the reciprocity of taḥāḍḍ. This distinction clarifies the lexical meaning while conveying the verses’ social significance without adding an unwritten meaning to the word itself.
