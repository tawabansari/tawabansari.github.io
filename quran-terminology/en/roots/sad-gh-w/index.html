Inclining and turning toward one side: from tilting a vessel and listening attentively to the heart’s inclination and taking sides

The Arabic root Ṣ GH W concerns inclination, tilting, and turning in a particular direction. This may be physical, such as inclining the head, neck, or a vessel. It may also concern attention, hearing, interest, and inward inclination, such as listening to someone or the heart turning toward an idea or desire.

The family includes ṣaghā, to incline; aṣghā, to incline something and, in hearing, to listen attentively; ṣaghw, inclination; and ṣāghiyah, close associates and companions inclined toward someone.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an, in Al-Anʿām 6:113 and Al-Taḥrīm 66:4, as wa-li-taṣghā and ṣaghat. Both are Form I verbs, and both concern hearts inclining.

The central distinction is that inclining, listening attentively, and deviating from truth do not have one identical meaning. Attentive listening is one use arising from attention and inclination toward sound; the heart, however, may incline toward something right or wrong. The verb’s complement and context establish its direction and value.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: two Form I occurrences

1. The semantic core: inclining to one side

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris introduces the root’s basic meaning as:

أَصْلٌ صَحِيحٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى الْمَيْلِ

aṣlun ṣaḥīḥun yadullu ʿalā al-mayli

That is, the root denotes inclination.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: ṣaghw and the core meaning of inclination

Ibn Durayd’s Jamharat al-Lughah likewise says:

الصَّغْوُ: الْمَيْلُ

al-ṣaghwu al-maylu

That is, ṣaghw means inclination.

The definitions clarify the main semantic field: something oriented in one direction inclines toward another, or someone directs attention and inclination toward a person or thing.

Arabic records:

صَغَا إِلَى الشَّيْءِ

ṣaghā ilā al-shayʾi

It means inclining toward something.

Also:

صَغْوُ فُلَانٍ مَعَكَ

ṣaghwu fulānin maʿaka

It means that someone’s inclination or allegiance is with you.

The first use expresses movement or inclination toward something; the second concerns support and taking someone’s side.

Ṣaghw alone does not judge the rightness of the object of inclination.

Someone may listen to truthful speech or incline toward deceptive words. Both fit this family’s underlying attention and inclination, but the speech’s content and the person’s situation establish the behavior’s value.

Physical and inward inclination

Physically, something inclines away from its previous position or direction.

For example, a vessel is tilted aside, or a person’s or animal’s head turns to one side.

Inwardly, someone’s attention, interest, or inclination turns toward a subject.

The semantic connection is clear: both involve orientation.

This resemblance does not establish that every inward use necessarily arose historically after physical uses.

Arabic evidence establishes the presence of both in this family.

2. Physical and social uses in early Arabic

The sun and stars inclining

Maqāyīs al-Lughah records:

صَغَتِ النُّجُومُ

ṣaghat al-nujūmu

That is, the stars inclined toward setting.

Jamharat al-Lughah also uses al-shamsu ṣaghwāʾu for the sun inclining westward.

Ṣaghā here concerns an astronomical body’s apparent movement or position toward setting.

Setting, however, is not the root’s independent, constant meaning.

Ṣaghā denotes inclination; the direction toward setting follows from the particular subject and use.

The Qur’anic ṣaghat qulūbukumā therefore cannot mean hearts setting merely because ṣaghat al-nujūmu is recorded.

One concerns astronomical bodies’ apparent movement; the other concerns human inward inclination.

Jamharat al-Lughah: inclination, ṣaghw, and the sun described as ṣaghwāʾ

Tilting a vessel

Arabic records:

أَصْغَيْتُ الْإِنَاءَ

aṣghaytu al-ināʾa

It means tilting the vessel to one side.

Lisān al-ʿArab explains that a vessel may be tilted so its remaining liquid gathers on one side.

This clearly illustrates the root’s physical meaning.

The vessel’s tilt also directs its contents to one side.

Tilting does not necessarily mean emptying it completely.

The purpose may be gathering liquid in a corner, or some contents may spill out.

The manner of action and context determine the result.

A reduced share: muṣghan ināʾuhu

Figuratively and socially, aṣghā ināʾa fulānin also means reducing someone’s share or right.

The image relates to tilting a vessel as though someone’s contents had consequently diminished.

A poem attributed to Al-Namir ibn Tawlab says:

وَإِنَّ ابْنَ أُخْتِ الْقَوْمِ مُصْغًى إِنَاؤُهُ
إِذَا لَمْ يُزَاحِمْ خَالَهُ بِأَبٍ جَلْدِ

wa-inna ibna ukhti al-qawmi muṣghan ināʾuhu / idhā lam yuzāḥim khālahu bi-abin jaldi

Translation: “A people’s sister’s son has his share diminished if he lacks a strong father to support him in contending with his maternal uncle.”

Muṣghan ināʾuhu here does not describe an actual tilted vessel; it pictures someone’s reduced share and standing in tribal relationships.

Lexical sources attribute the witness to Al-Namir ibn Tawlab; some also attribute it to Ghassān ibn Waʿlah.

Lisān al-ʿArab, SH Ṭ R: the alternative attribution to Ghassān ibn Waʿlah

Al-Namir lived through the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, so without dating this particular line it cannot be treated as definitely preceding the Qur’an.

The recorded use nevertheless shows how tilting a vessel could express diminished entitlement in Arabic.

It does not make ṣaghw mean depriving others of their rights in every sentence.

Lisān al-ʿArab: tilting vessels and the witness attributed to Al-Namir ibn Tawlab

Attentive listening: inclining the head and ear

Iṣghāʾ, attentive listening, is one of the family’s best-known uses.

Lisān al-ʿArab records:

أَصْغَيْتُ إِلَى فُلَانٍ

aṣghaytu ilā fulānin

It means inclining one’s hearing toward someone.

The person directs attention to another’s voice or speech.

This is sometimes accompanied by inclining the head or ear.

Attentive listening is not merely bodily movement; it can mean conscious attention and careful hearing.

Aṣghā ilā al-ḥadīthi accordingly means listening attentively to speech.

The use relates to the basic inclination, but not all Ṣ GH W forms should be restricted to hearing.

A poetic witness from Dhū al-Rummah

The Umayyad poet Dhū al-Rummah describes his camel:

تُصْغِي إِذَا شَدَّهَا بِالْكُورِ جَانِحَةً
حَتَّىٰ إِذَا مَا اسْتَوَىٰ فِي غَرْزِهَا تَثِبُ

tuṣghī idhā shaddahā bi-al-kūri jāniḥatan / ḥattā idhā mā istawā fī gharzihā tathibu

A rendering close to the sense: “When he fastens the saddle upon her, she inclines her head and body; then, when the rider has settled into her stirrup, she leaps up.”

Tuṣghī here concerns the camel inclining.

Commentators explain that while the saddle is fastened she inclines her head aside as though listening to something.

The witness shows the familiar physical use of inclining the head.

Dhū al-Rummah’s poetry belongs to the Islamic period and must not be presented as pre-Islamic evidence.

The main action here is bodily inclination, not necessarily actually hearing speech.

Tahdhīb al-Lughah: Dhū al-Rummah’s witness and the camel’s inclined head

Ṣāghiyah: close associates and companions

Ṣāghiyatu al-rajuli in Arabic denotes close associates, companions, or those inclined toward someone who visit them.

Ibn Fāris likewise uses ṣāghiyah for someone’s inclined relatives and companions.

Inclination here extends beyond bodily movement or hearing a sound to social relationships and support.

Someone may be related to another, support them, or take their side in a matter.

Ṣāghiyah does not always mean an unquestioning follower or fanatical supporter.

The relationship’s nature and degree of loyalty depend on the situation.

Ṣaghā: crookedness of the mouth and face

Some sources also record al-ṣaghā for one side of the mouth or face being tilted.

Aṣghā here describes someone whose mouth or face is inclined.

Ṣaghwāʾ is its feminine form and is sometimes applied to a bird with a beak inclined to one side.

This branch shows inclination as a bodily characteristic rather than conscious behavior.

Without evidence, such crookedness must not be attributed to partisanship, moral corruption, or misguidance.

Al-Muḥīṭ, Asās al-Balāghah, and Tāj al-ʿArūs: inclination, associates, and bodily condition

3. The first Qur’anic use: Al-Anʿām 6:113

وَلِتَصْغَىٰ إِلَيْهِ أَفْئِدَةُ الَّذِينَ لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ بِالْآخِرَةِ وَلِيَرْضَوْهُ وَلِيَقْتَرِفُوا مَا هُمْ مُقْتَرِفُونَ

wa-li-taṣghā ilayhi afʾidatu alladhīna lā yuʾminūna bi-al-ākhirati wa-li-yarḍawhu wa-li-yaqtarifū mā hum muqtarifūna

Translation: So that the hearts of those who do not believe in the Hereafter may incline toward it, approve of it, and acquire what they acquire through their deeds.

Taṣghā here is an imperfect of the Form I ṣaghā.

The subject is afʾidatu alladhīna lā yuʾminūna bi-al-ākhirati: the hearts of those who do not believe in the Hereafter.

This concerns inward inclination, not inclining the head, neck, or ear.

Toward what do the hearts incline?

In wa-li-taṣghā ilayhi, the pronoun -hi refers to the embellished, deceptive speech introduced in the preceding verse.

Al-Anʿām 6:112 says that devils among humans and jinn communicate embellished, deceptive speech to one another.

The verse’s expression is zukhrufa al-qawli ghurūran.

In Al-Anʿām 6:113, the group’s hearts therefore incline toward that deceptive speech.

This matters because taṣghā alone does not determine whether the heart inclines toward truth or falsehood.

Ilayhi and the pronoun’s antecedent in the previous verse establish the direction.

Study of Z KH R F

Inclining, approving, and acting

Three verbs follow one another in this verse:

وَلِتَصْغَىٰ إِلَيْهِ

wa-li-taṣghā ilayhi

وَلِيَرْضَوْهُ

wa-li-yarḍawhu

وَلِيَقْتَرِفُوا مَا هُمْ مُقْتَرِفُونَ

wa-li-yaqtarifū mā hum muqtarifūna

The first denotes the heart’s inclination.

The second expresses approval and satisfaction.

The third concerns doing or acquiring something through deeds.

The verbs are connected but not synonymous.

Someone may hear speech and attend to it without yet accepting it.

Someone may incline toward an idea and approve of it without yet acting upon it.

Inward inclination may also become manifest in behavior after acceptance and approval.

The verse presents this connection between the heart’s inclination, approval, and deeds.

The verbs’ sequence should not, however, become an exceptionless psychological law requiring every human decision to follow these stages in this order.

The verse expresses their relationship in the context of encountering deceptive speech.

Does taṣghā here mean listening attentively?

Since iṣghāʾ can mean attentive listening, wa-li-taṣghā ilayhi afʾidatu may be associated with attending to deceptive speech.

The direct meaning of the verb must nevertheless be distinguished from the family’s auditory use.

The subject here is afʾidah, hearts, not ears.

“Their hearts may incline toward it” therefore better matches the construction and meaning.

“Listening with the heart” can be a literary explanation, but the more precise direct translation is the heart inclining.

The verse also does not equate merely hearing deceptive speech with accepting it.

The independent verbs taṣghā and yarḍawhu make the distinction clearer.

Study of F ʾ D

Is inclination inherently blameworthy?

No.

Here the heart inclines toward deceptive speech, giving it a blameworthy force.

Ṣ GH W itself does not carry that value in every use.

Someone may also incline toward truthful speech, good conduct, or a trustworthy person.

Ṣaghw basically means inclination; its rightness or wrongness follows from its object and situation.

The role of li- in wa-li-taṣghā

Li- precedes the verb here.

Grammatically, the construction relates to purpose or result.

Exegetical sources discuss the lām’s connection with the preceding sentence and whether it expresses that behavior’s purpose or outcome.

The previous verse describes devils among humans and jinn communicating deceptive speech, followed here by hearts inclining, approving the speech, and performing related deeds.

These three stages can therefore be understood as outcomes or purposes of the deceptive speech.

The lām alone cannot establish that God morally approves of human deception and misguidance.

Understanding the relationship requires distinguishing behavior’s occurrence, deceivers’ purposes, hearers’ responses, and moral judgment on the action.

These issues follow from the network of verses and complete wording, not Ṣ GH W alone.

Al-Ṭabarī: the heart’s inclination, approval, and acquiring deeds

4. The second Qur’anic use: Al-Taḥrīm 66:4

إِنْ تَتُوبَا إِلَى اللَّهِ فَقَدْ صَغَتْ قُلُوبُكُمَا وَإِنْ تَظَاهَرَا عَلَيْهِ فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ هُوَ مَوْلَاهُ وَجِبْرِيلُ وَصَالِحُ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَالْمَلَائِكَةُ بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ ظَهِيرٌ

in tatūbā ilā allāhi fa-qad ṣaghat qulūbukumā wa-in taẓāharā ʿalayhi fa-inna allāha huwa mawlāhu wa-jibrīlu wa-ṣāliḥu al-muʾminīna wa-al-malāʾikatu baʿda dhālika ẓahīrun

Translation: If you two turn to The God in repentance, the hearts of both of you have indeed inclined; and if you join together against him, then The God is his helper, and Gabriel, the righteous among the believers, and the angels are also supporters thereafter.

Ṣaghat here is a third-person feminine singular perfect of the Form I ṣaghā.

Its subject is qulūbukumā, the two people’s hearts.

Fa-qad ṣaghat qulūbukumā describes an inclination that has already occurred.

Unlike Al-Anʿām 6:113, however, no explicit preposition and complement such as ilayhi follows the verb.

The inclination’s direction must therefore be established from context.

In which direction have their hearts inclined?

Al-Taḥrīm 66:1 concerns prohibiting something God made lawful in seeking the wives’ approval.

Al-Taḥrīm 66:3 concerns a confidential statement and its disclosure.

Verse 4 calls two people to repentance and discusses joining together against the Prophet.

The sequence places ṣaghat qulūbukumā in a context of warning and correcting conduct.

“Your hearts have inclined away from the right direction” or “turned in a wrong direction” therefore fits the context.

Early exegetical accounts likewise explain ṣaghat through mālat and zāghat, inclined and deviated.

Al-Ṭabarī transmits explanations from Ibn ʿAbbās, Qatādah, and others concerning the hearts inclining away from the proper position.

Ṣaghā’s lexical meaning alone nevertheless does not make the verb always mean straying from truth.

The context and call to repentance establish the inclination’s undesirable direction here.

Could ṣaghat mean inclining toward repentance?

Some exegetical sources transmit another possibility: the hearts inclining toward repentance.

This is not impossible within the general meaning, since the root itself determines no positive or negative direction.

The verse, however, calls for repentance after an event discussed previously, while its continuation warns against joining together against the Prophet.

Inclining away from the proper position and consequently needing repentance thus has stronger contextual support.

Preferring inclination toward repentance would require evidence establishing that direction.

No such direct complement follows ṣaghat in the verse.

Although the lexical possibility of inclination toward repentance is recorded, the preferred contextual meaning remains the inclination prompting the call to repentance.

Al-Qurṭubī: inclination away from truth and the reported inclination toward repentance

Study of T W B

The difference between ṣaghat and taẓāharā

The verse continues:

وَإِنْ تَظَاهَرَا عَلَيْهِ

wa-in taẓāharā ʿalayhi

Taẓāharā belongs to Ẓ H R and here denotes two people joining together and helping one another.

Ṣaghat, however, concerns hearts inclining.

Inward inclination and practical cooperation therefore differ.

On one side, hearts have inclined in a particular direction.

On the other, the possibility of two people cooperating against someone is raised.

The connection shows that inward inclination and outward behavior may relate.

It does not make every passing inclination independently equivalent to concerted action.

The Qur’an itself expresses the conditions through different verbs.

Does the verse concern two hearts or several?

It says:

قُلُوبُكُمَا

qulūbukumā

The suffix -kumā refers to two people, while qulūb is plural.

Arabic can use a plural when attributing something to two people, each with one instance, as in expressions concerning their hands, eyes, or hearts.

Qulūbukumā therefore refers to the hearts of those same two addressees.

It does not establish that each has several hearts or that more than two people are addressed.

Ṣaghat is also feminine singular, agreeing with qulūb in this construction.

The grammar does not change the meaning of inclination.

Al-Ṭabarī: ṣaghat, the two addressees’ hearts, and cooperation

Study of Q L B

The role of fa-qad

Fa-qad ṣaghat describes an inclination already realized.

Two main grammatical analyses of the conditional in tatūbā are offered.

One understands fa-qad ṣaghat in connection with the conditional response: the inclination is the reason repentance is needed.

The other regards the response as omitted but recoverable, since the hearts’ inclination preceded the call to repentance.

Neither analysis turns the perfect ṣaghat into a definite inclination occurring only in the future after repentance.

This matters in translation, while ṣaghw itself continues to mean inclination.

Al-Sirāj al-Munīr: two conditional-response analyses and the plural qulūb

5. Important forms and morphology

Ṣaghā with wāw and yāʾ variants

Arabic records both wāw-based and yāʾ-based forms of this verb.

Ṣaghā yaṣghū and ṣaghā yaṣghā both concern inclination.

Ibn al-Sikkīt and Shamir transmit different forms in lexical accounts.

Some lexicographers consider the yāʾ-based form more frequent in certain dialects.

The variation concerns Arabic final-weak roots, ending in wāw or yāʾ and taking changing forms in inflection.

The Qur’anic morphological index places both occurrences under Ṣ GH W.

Recorded wāw and yāʾ forms nevertheless mean that a word’s spelling alone does not establish its precise derivational history.

Ṣaghw and ṣaghan

Ṣaghw is a known verbal noun of ṣaghā and denotes inclination.

Ṣaghan and related forms also occur in lexical sources.

Some uses concern bodily inclination, others inward inclination and taking sides.

Aṣghā and iṣghāʾ

Aṣghā is Form IV.

Physically, it concerns inclining something such as a vessel or head.

In hearing, aṣghā ilā al-ḥadīthi means listening attentively to speech.

Iṣghāʾ is its verbal noun.

These forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

The common modern Persian and Arabic use of iṣghāʾ should therefore not make wa-li-taṣghā in Al-Anʿām 6:113 merely hearing with the ear.

Ṣāghin and ṣāghiyah

Ṣāghin is the Form I active participle, denoting someone inclined in a direction.

Socially, ṣāghiyah describes companions and close associates inclined toward someone.

Closeness or support here does not always mean complete agreement or unquestioning adherence.

Aṣghā and ṣaghwāʾ

Adjectivally, aṣghā can describe a person or animal with a mouth or face inclined to one side.

Ṣaghwāʾ is its feminine form.

These uses do not occur in the Qur’an.

Ṣaghat and taṣghā

Ṣaghat in Al-Taḥrīm 66:4 is a feminine singular perfect.

Taṣghā in Al-Anʿām 6:113 is a feminine singular imperfect following lām.

Both verses have plural hearts as their subject but a feminine singular verb.

Arabic uses this construction with broken and nonhuman plurals and in certain collective constructions.

A singular verb therefore does not mean that only one heart inclined.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab: wāw and yāʾ variants

6. Comparison with related roots

Ṣ GH W (ṣaghā) and M Y L (māla)

Māla in Arabic concerns inclination and change of direction.

Ṣaghā likewise basically concerns inclination.

Ibn Fāris and other lexicographers explain ṣaghw through mayl.

The families are therefore very close in some uses.

The recorded Ṣ GH W uses, however, also concern inclining the ear, tilting vessels, and the inclination of hearts and associates.

M Y L has a broader range involving bodily movement, inward inclination, and changes in the direction of various things.

Different scope does not mean complete separation; each has its particular uses.

Ṣ GH W (ṣaghā) and Ṣ GH R (ṣaghura)

Despite similar letters, these roots are separate.

Ṣ GH W denotes inclination.

Ṣ GH R concerns small size, age, and quantity, with another branch for abasement and subordination.

Iṣghāʾ concerns attentive listening, whereas taṣghīr from Ṣ GH R means making small.

Similar pronunciation should not be treated as proof of kinship or equivalent meanings.

Study of Ṣ GH R

Ṣ GH W (ṣaghā) and S M ʿ (samiʿa)

Samiʿa concerns hearing.

Aṣghā also denotes attentive listening in its auditory use.

They are not identical, however.

Someone may hear a sound without paying particular attention.

The established auditory aṣghā, by contrast, can foreground attending and inclining one’s hearing toward sound.

Hearing and listening attentively may therefore express different aspects of encountering sound.

In Al-Anʿām 6:113, however, hearts are the subject of taṣghā, making inward inclination more direct.

Study of S M ʿ

Ṣ GH W (ṣaghā) and N Ṣ T (anṣata)

Anṣata concerns falling silent and listening.

Aṣghā can also concern attending to speech.

Remaining silent and directing attention toward something are nevertheless distinct.

Someone may be silent without following another’s speech.

Someone may also attend to speech while asking questions or responding.

The roots therefore overlap in hearing without being complete synonyms.

Ṣ GH W (ṣaghā) and Ṣ B W (ṣabā)

Ṣabā ilā in Arabic denotes inclination and attraction toward something.

Ṣaghā ilā can likewise express inclination toward something.

Ṣ B W, however, also includes the families of childhood and the east wind ṣabā.

In Yūsuf 12:33, aṣbu ilayhinna concerns Joseph inclining in a situation of pressure and trial.

In Al-Anʿām 6:113, taṣghā concerns hearts inclining toward deceptive speech.

The object of inclination matters in both, but these close uses do not make every derivative of the roots synonymous.

Study of Ṣ B W

Ṣ GH W (ṣaghā) and H W Y (hawiya)

Hawā can denote inward inclination and attraction in Arabic.

Ṣaghw also concerns inclination in uses about the heart.

An explanation of Al-Anʿām 6:113 transmitted from Ibn Zayd relates wa-li-taṣghā to inclination and liking.

Inclining and liking must nevertheless be distinguished.

A heart may attend to speech without yet liking or accepting it.

Al-Anʿām 6:113 likewise uses taṣghā and yarḍawhu as separate verbs.

Inclining is therefore more precise in direct translation; approval follows from the next verb.

Study of H W Y

Ṣ GH W (ṣaghā) and Z Y GH (zāgha)

Zāgha concerns inclination and deviation from a direction.

In Qur’anic uses, it frequently accompanies deviation from truth or the proper course.

Ṣaghā likewise denotes inclination, but its basic meaning fixes no positive or negative direction.

Ṣaghat in Al-Taḥrīm 66:4 occurs in a context conveying deviation from the proper position; early commentators accordingly explain it through zāghat.

Ṣaghw is nevertheless not necessarily undesirable deviation in every Arabic use.

The root’s definition must preserve this distinction.

Ṣ GH W (ṣaghā) and Ṣ D F (ṣadafa)

Ṣadafa ʿan concerns turning or inclining away from something.

The established construction ṣaghā ilā can concern inclining toward something.

Some constructions therefore contrast turning toward and turning away.

That opposition alone does not establish a shared historical origin.

The preposition and context also establish the inclination’s direction.

Ilayhi in Al-Anʿām 6:113 specifies the hearts’ direction, whereas ṣadafa ʿanhā in Al-Anʿām 6:157 expresses turning away from the signs.

7. Challenges of understanding and translation

Does ṣaghw always mean attentive listening?

No.

Attentive listening is one known use of aṣghā in this family.

Both Qur’anic verses, however, use the Form I ṣaghā with hearts as the subject.

Inward inclination is therefore more appropriate.

In Al-Anʿām 6:113, hearts incline toward deceptive speech.

In Al-Taḥrīm 66:4, they have inclined in a direction related to the call to repentance.

Restricting the entire root to hearing with the ear therefore removes part of its meaning.

Is ṣaghw inherently blameworthy?

No.

Both Qur’anic uses place the hearts’ inclination in a critical context.

That contextual feature is not the root’s general definition.

Someone may incline toward goodness, truth, or a worthy person.

Someone may also incline toward deceptive speech or an improper desire.

The inclination’s moral value must therefore be assessed from its object, the person’s awareness, and subsequent response.

Is the heart inclining equivalent to acting?

No.

Al-Anʿām 6:113 presents inclining, approving, and acting as three separate verbs.

Al-Taḥrīm 66:4 also uses different constructions for the hearts’ inclination and two people’s possible cooperation.

Attention, acceptance, decision, and action must therefore not all be merged into ṣaghw.

Inward inclination can also involve different degrees of awareness and choice.

The verb alone does not determine all those degrees.

Does ṣaghat qulūbukumā make every inward inclination definitely sinful?

The verse criticizes two people’s conduct and calls them to repentance.

The inclination discussed therefore relates to an improper situation.

It should not establish a general rule making every passing inclination equally morally responsible, even where someone does not act upon it or consciously resists it.

Judging inclination and action depends on their object, awareness, choice, and subsequent behavior.

Lexically, ṣaghw is inclination; its moral implications follow from context.

Does ṣāghiyah mean unquestioning adherence?

No.

Socially, ṣāghiyah can mean close associates, companions, or those inclined toward someone.

That inclination does not necessarily mean complete submission, absolute obedience, or approval of all their conduct.

Someone may support another while disagreeing on a particular matter.

Unlimited obedience should therefore not be inferred from companionship.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

Inclining, developing an inclination, and turning toward suit the root’s general meaning.

Tilting or being tilted is more natural for physical uses.

In auditory uses, listening attentively is appropriate for aṣghā.

For the heart, inclining or developing an inclination is more precise.

Al-Anʿām 6:113: wa-li-taṣghā ilayhi afʾidatu alladhīna lā yuʾminūna bi-al-ākhirati

Preferred rendering:

“So that the hearts of those who do not believe in the Hereafter may incline toward it.”

Context identifies ilayhi as referring to the preceding verse’s embellished, deceptive speech.

Al-Taḥrīm 66:4: fa-qad ṣaghat qulūbukumā

A rendering close to the wording:

“The hearts of both of you have indeed inclined.”

Context relates this inclination to deviation from the proper position and the need for repentance.

An interpretive translation can convey this as “The hearts of both of you have inclined away from the right course.” Away from the right course supplies the verb’s unexpressed complement from context, rather than belonging to the word itself.

For non-Qur’anic forms, ṣaghw can be rendered inclination; aṣghā ilā al-ḥadīthi, he listened attentively to speech; aṣghā al-ināʾa, he tilted the vessel; and ṣāghiyah, close associates and companions inclined toward someone.

9. Conclusion

The Arabic root Ṣ GH W denotes inclination and orientation toward one side.

This may be physical, as with a vessel, head, face, or the sun’s apparent movement toward setting.

It can also concern hearing, such as inclining the ear and attending to speech.

Inwardly, the heart and will incline toward a person, idea, or speech. Socially, close associates and companions inclined toward someone may be called ṣāghiyah.

Only two forms of this family occur in the Qur’an.

In Al-Anʿām 6:113, some hearts incline toward deceptive speech, followed by approval and the resulting behavior.

In Al-Taḥrīm 66:4, two addressees’ hearts have inclined within a context calling them to repentance and warning against joining together against the Prophet.

In both, the verb’s basic meaning is inclination, with its direction and value established by context.

Concise root definition: Ṣ GH W denotes inclining and developing an orientation toward one side, whether in bodily position, attention and hearing, or the heart’s inclination and support for others. Brief root meaning: Inclining toward one side; in attention, listening attentively; in the heart, inward inclination

The family’s central distinction is that inclining, listening attentively, approving, and acting are four different conditions.

Attentive listening is one use of directed attention. Approval and action may follow inclination, but ṣaghw itself does not necessarily entail them.

The heart inclining is therefore the most precise basic rendering for both Qur’anic uses. Its direction, toward deceptive speech or away from the proper position, must be established from the relevant verses and context.
