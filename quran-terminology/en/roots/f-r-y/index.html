In early Arabic, the three-consonant root f-r-y concerns cutting, splitting, and shaping something. In its physical use, فَرَى meant cutting leather, hide, or another material: an action that could serve to make or repair something, or result in damage.

From this family, اِفْتَرَى in Qur’anic language means fabricating a false statement or claim and attributing it to someone. Such attribution may concern God, Messengers, revelation, religious rulings, or other people.

The word فَرِيّ also describes a great, extraordinary, or unusual deed in Arabic, which may be praised or condemned. In Mary’s story, it occurs in the speech of people accusing her of wrongdoing.

The root f-r-y occurs 60 times in 54 Qur’anic verses. Most occurrences belong to the family of iftirāʾ and concern fabricating or attributing falsehood. By contrast, فَرِيًّا in Maryam 19:27 has a different use that should not be reduced to lying without examination.

The root’s historical meaning concerns cutting and shaping; in its predominant Qur’anic branch, however, iftirāʾ denotes fabricating and attributing a statement or claim contrary to truth. These levels must remain distinct, so that a physical image is not imposed on the verses and every derivative is not translated simply as “falsehood.”

1. The root in pre-Qur’anic Arabic

Cutting leather and shaping something

In early Arabic, فَرَى الشَّيْءَ means to cut or split something.

One familiar use was cutting hide and leather to make everyday necessities.

Making waterskins, shoes, straps, and other leather articles required measuring and cutting the hide correctly.

The craftsperson first determined the required shape and dimensions, then cut the hide accordingly.

Measuring and cutting are distinct tasks. Someone might devise an article’s design yet fail to cut and make it successfully.

This distinction appears in an important example from pre-Islamic poetry.

Evidence from Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā

The pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā says in praise of Harim ibn Sinān:

وَلَأَنْتَ تَفْرِي مَا خَلَقْتَ وَبَعْضُ
الْقَوْمِ يَخْلُقُ ثُمَّ لَا يَفْرِي

Wa-la-anta tafrī mā khalaqta wa-baʿḍu / l-qawmi yakhluqu thumma lā yafrī

Translation: “You carry out what you have measured and planned, whereas some people make plans but do not put them into practice.”

In some transmissions, the verse begins with فَلَأَنْتَ.

Two verbs are contrasted in this example:

خَلَقْتَ — You measured and determined a design.

تَفْرِي — You cut it to measure and put it into practice.

Here, khalq does not mean creation from nothing; it concerns measuring and preparing a design.

The poet uses leatherworking to describe the person he praises: he not only decides, but also carries out his decisions.

Thus فَرَى describes a positive action in this example and concerns doing a task successfully.

The verse is preserved in Zuhayr’s collected poetry and early lexical and literary sources. Its attribution to a pre-Islamic poet and its presence in older poetic transmissions provide important evidence for the root’s pre-Qur’anic meaning, although the text reaches us through later manuscripts and quotations.

The linguistic implication is clear: فَرَى in pre-Qur’anic Arabic could express measured cutting and the execution of a previously determined design.

This example therefore establishes an old sense unrelated to lying; by itself, however, it cannot determine the root’s earliest historical meaning.

The distinction between فَرَى and أَفْرَى

Some older dictionaries distinguish these verbs.

They explain فَرَى as cutting to repair or make something.

They describe أَفْرَى as cutting or splitting that damages or ruins an object.

This distinction is not uniform across all lexical accounts.

Some lexicographers record closely related senses of cutting and splitting for both forms, without an absolute boundary between repair and destruction.

We therefore cannot say that every use of فَرَى necessarily means successful construction, or that every use of أَفْرَى means destruction.

The object, verbal form, and context determine the action’s direction.

For example, cutting hide may be part of making a waterskin, or may tear and destroy it. Cutting alone does not establish the action’s full value without knowledge of its purpose and outcome.

يَفْرِي الْفَرِيَّ: performing an extraordinary deed

The expression يَفْرِي الْفَرِيَّ is also known in Arabic.

It describes someone who performs a great, extraordinary, or outstanding deed.

Lexical accounts sometimes associate such a deed with skill and mastery; the expression is not necessarily condemnatory.

In this use, the meaning extends from the concrete act of cutting to performing something remarkable.

Independent historical evidence is needed, however, to establish exactly when this development began and from which usage it arose.

The figurative relationship between skillful cutting and extraordinary achievement can be explained, but that explanation must not become a definitive history of every derivative’s origin.

Fabricating and attributing false statements

Early Arabic sources record فَرَى كَذِبًا and اِفْتَرَى الْكَذِبَ as meaning to fabricate and utter falsehood.

الفِرْيَة is also a term for a fabricated lie or false accusation.

In this use, someone produces a statement or fashions a claim without a truthful basis.

This does not mean that every lie is cut like a piece of leather, or that all the stages of making it are contained in the verb’s meaning.

A relationship between physical cutting and making, and the fabrication of speech, can be explained; that image alone does not establish the root’s entire semantic history.

The Qur’anic use of iftirāʾ itself clearly shows that fabricating and falsely attributing speech was a recognized meaning in the Arabic of the revelation period.

2. Linguistic forms and Qur’anic derivatives

Important forms in this Arabic word family include:

  • فَرَى: cut, split, or prepared something according to a design and measurement.
  • فَرْي: cutting and splitting; also the result or manner of this action.
  • أَفْرَى: split or cut; in some uses, with resulting damage.
  • فَرِيّ: a great, extraordinary, or unusual deed; in an appropriate context, something gravely objectionable.
  • فِرْيَة: a fabricated lie or false accusation.
  • اِفْتَرَى: fabricated a lie or attributed an unfounded claim to someone.
  • اِفْتِرَاء: the act of fabricating or falsely attributing a claim.
  • مُفْتَرٍ: someone who fabricates a false statement or claim.
  • مُفْتَرًى: something fabricated or falsely attributed.
  • مُفْتَرَيَات: fabricated things or statements.

Most Qur’anic forms belong to Form VIII.

اِفْتَرَى and its imperfect forms, such as يَفْتَرُونَ and تَفْتَرُونَ, express fabricating and attributing a false claim.

اِفْتِرَاءً is the verbal noun of this verb and occurs in al-Anʿām 6:138 and al-Anʿām 6:140.

مُفْتَرٍ and مُفْتَرُونَ refer to people to whom fabrication is attributed.

مُفْتَرًى and مُفْتَرَيَاتٍ describe a statement or thing presented as fabricated.

فَرِيًّا occurs in Maryam 19:27, with a meaning distinct from the forms relating to iftirāʾ.

The familiar noun فِرْيَة does not occur in this form in the Qur’an, although it is semantically related to the iftirāʾ family.

Is a single definition sufficient for every derivative?

Cutting leather, fabricating a false statement, and performing an extraordinary deed are three distinct uses.

Their relationship can be examined through cutting, shaping, and producing something.

But defining the root’s core merely as “making” erases its distinction from many other roots concerning making and producing.

Defining it only as “lying” fails to explain the historical cutting sense or فَرِيّ in Mary’s story adequately.

It is therefore preferable to retain two levels:

Historical physical meaning: cutting and splitting, especially cutting something to a design and measurement.

Established meaning in Form VIII: fabricating and attributing a false, unfounded statement or claim.

This distinction does not deny connections between the branches; it prevents one branch’s meaning from being imposed on every derivative.

3. شَيْئًا فَرِيًّا: astonishment and accusation in Mary’s story

Maryam 19:27

فَأَتَتْ بِهِ قَوْمَهَا تَحْمِلُهُ قَالُوا يَا مَرْيَمُ لَقَدْ جِئْتِ شَيْئًا فَرِيًّا

Fa-atat bihi qawmahā taḥmiluhu qālū yā Maryamu laqad jiʾti shayʾan fariyyā

Translation: “Then she brought him to her people, carrying him. They said: O Mary, you have indeed done something extraordinary and reprehensible.”

This is the only Qur’anic occurrence of فَرِيّ.

After Jesus’ birth, Mary brings her child to her people.

Seeing her and the child, they react with astonishment and reproach.

Here شَيْئًا فَرِيًّا refers to a great, unusual deed that the speakers regard as objectionable.

Older lexical sources explain fariyy in this use as الأمر العظيم: a great, extraordinary matter.

Yet a deed’s greatness does not always mean that it is bad.

Just as extraordinary achievement in يفري الفري may indicate skill and ability, in Maryam 19:27 the description occurs in a reproachful statement.

Its condemnatory force therefore comes from the context of Mary’s people’s speech.

Does the Qur’an itself accuse Mary?

No. The verse reports what Mary’s people say.

The speakers say:

«لَقَدْ جِئْتِ شَيْئًا فَرِيًّا»

Laqad jiʾti shayʾan fariyyā

This sentence expresses their judgment of Mary’s situation; it does not endorse the claim that she acted wrongly.

In Maryam 19:28, these same people mention Mary’s parents and say they were not immoral.

Their words show that they assess the child’s birth through an assumption about her sexual relations and moral conduct.

Earlier, however, Maryam 19:20 reports Mary’s statement that no human has had sexual contact with her and that she has not been unchaste.

In Maryam 19:30, Jesus also speaks and identifies himself as God’s servant.

The story thus distinguishes the event’s reality from people’s perception of it.

This matters for understanding fariyy.

Simply translating the word as “a lie” or “a definite sin” might turn the speakers’ accusation into an established fact.

The verse instead reports the words of people who did not know how Jesus had been born.

The distinction between فَرِيّ and فِرْيَة

فَرِيّ in this verse describes something great and extraordinary.

In familiar Arabic usage, however, فِرْيَة refers to a lie or fabricated accusation.

These words belong to the same family, but their meaning must be examined independently in each sentence.

If someone accuses Mary of something she has not done, that accusation itself may be a lie and slander.

This conclusion must not replace the direct meaning of فَرِيًّا.

In the verse, Mary’s people deem an action deeply objectionable from their perspective. The error in their judgment becomes clear from the story as a whole.

Thus, “an extraordinary and reprehensible deed” is more appropriate for شَيْئًا فَرِيًّا in this context than “a fabricated lie.”

4. Iftirāʾ: fabricating lies and attributing them to God

Many Qur’anic occurrences concern people who attribute statements or rulings to God without a truthful basis for that attribution.

The preposition عَلَى matters in اِفْتَرَى عَلَى اللَّهِ الْكَذِبَ.

افترى expresses the act of making or fashioning the claim.

الكذب identifies its false content.

على الله specifies that the statement is attributed to God.

The issue is therefore more than an incorrect sentence: someone advances a claim in God’s name.

A. Fabricating rulings on what is lawful and forbidden

Al-Naḥl 16:116

وَلَا تَقُولُوا لِمَا تَصِفُ أَلْسِنَتُكُمُ الْكَذِبَ هَٰذَا حَلَالٌ وَهَٰذَا حَرَامٌ لِتَفْتَرُوا عَلَى اللَّهِ الْكَذِبَ إِنَّ الَّذِينَ يَفْتَرُونَ عَلَى اللَّهِ الْكَذِبَ لَا يُفْلِحُونَ

Wa-lā taqūlū limā taṣifu alsinatukumu l-kadhiba hādhā ḥalālun wa-hādhā ḥarāmun li-taftarū ʿalā llāhi l-kadhiba inna lladhīna yaftarūna ʿalā llāhi l-kadhiba lā yufliḥūn

Translation: “And do not say, on the basis of the falsehood your tongues describe, ‘This is lawful and this is forbidden,’ thereby fabricating lies against The God. Indeed, those who fabricate lies against The God do not prosper.”

The root f-r-y appears twice in this verse:

لِتَفْتَرُوا

and

يَفْتَرُونَ

Both belong to Form VIII.

The verse concerns attributing rulings on what is lawful and forbidden to God on the basis of false claims.

Someone may offer an opinion on the benefits or harms of a food, behavior, or commodity. Such an opinion is not, in itself, a claim to divine legislation.

But declaring something lawful or forbidden as God’s ruling makes a claim about the statement’s divine authority.

If that attribution is fabricated, it falls within the meaning of iftirāʾ.

This distinction matters because a religious ruling is more than a personal opinion. It may affect other people’s lives, freedom, property, relationships, and rights.

Attributing a prohibition or obligation to God without valid support from revelation can subject people, in religion’s name, to restrictions God has not prescribed.

B. Fabricated restrictions on livestock and food

In al-Anʿām 6:138, the Qur’an describes groups who had imposed special restrictions on livestock and crops.

Some concerned eating the produce; others concerned using livestock.

The same verse describes their conduct with افْتِرَاءً عَلَيْهِ.

Al-Anʿām 6:140 also describes people who ignorantly killed their children and forbade what God had provided them.

Here افْتِرَاءً عَلَى اللَّهِ refers to fabricating lies and attributing them to God.

These occurrences show that iftirāʾ may concern practical and social behavior, beyond expressing an abstract belief.

When a fabricated claim in God’s name becomes binding law or custom, it may affect food, property, family, and even human life.

This does not mean that every disagreement over a ruling necessarily constitutes deliberate fabrication.

Someone may misunderstand a verse or reach an incorrect interpretation through ignorance.

That is different from knowing the truth but deliberately attributing a contrary statement to God.

In passages such as al-Anʿām 6:144, the Qur’an associates fabricating lies about God with misleading people and بِغَيْرِ عِلْمٍ, “without knowledge.”

Assessing responsibility therefore requires distinguishing the claim’s falsity, its evidential basis, the speaker’s conduct, and the statement’s social consequences.

C. Claiming to receive revelation

Al-Anʿām 6:93 describes those who fabricate lies against God or claim to have received revelation when nothing has been revealed to them.

This is among the clearest examples of iftirāʾ concerning religious authority.

Someone may have a thought, dream, feeling, or personal interpretation.

These are not, in themselves, divine revelation.

If someone invents a claim and presents it as a message revealed by God, the issue goes beyond whether an idea is correct: it concerns attributing that idea to God.

This distinction matters when examining claims to prophethood, revelation, and legislation.

Conversely, if someone has an inward experience and honestly presents it as personal, merely recounting it cannot be called fabrication without evidence.

The central question is what has been claimed and to whom it has been attributed.

D. Associating partners with God and assigning divine standing to others

After discussing association, al-Nisāʾ 4:48 says that whoever associates a partner with God has fabricated a tremendous sin.

Here افترى concerns more than inventing a false sentence.

Assigning a partner to God creates or accepts an attribution that the Qur’an regards as untrue.

Someone may give a being, office, or power a standing reserved for God.

This false attribution may appear in belief, obedience, or conduct.

The verse does not imply that every intense attachment, respect for a person, or following of an expert automatically constitutes association and fabrication.

Such a judgment requires establishing whether a specifically divine standing has actually been assigned to someone else.

Here too, iftirāʾ remains connected to fabricated, untrue attribution, while the verse’s context determines its precise application.

5. Accusing Messengers and divine scripture of falsehood

The root f-r-y is not used only to describe people who fabricate lies against God.

In the Qur’an, opponents of the Messengers sometimes accuse them of fabrication as well.

This distinction is crucial: an occurrence of افتراه does not necessarily mean that the Qur’an itself confirms fabrication has taken place.

Sometimes the word belongs to opponents’ speech as they make an accusation.

A. Accusing the Qur’an of being fabricated

Yūnus 10:38

أَمْ يَقُولُونَ افْتَرَاهُ قُلْ فَأْتُوا بِسُورَةٍ مِثْلِهِ وَادْعُوا مَنِ اسْتَطَعْتُمْ مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ صَادِقِينَ

Am yaqūlūna iftarāhu qul fa-ʾtū bi-sūratin mithlihi wa-dʿū mani staṭaʿtum min dūni llāhi in kuntum ṣādiqīn

Translation: “Or do they say, ‘He fabricated it’? Say: Then bring a surah like it, and call upon whomever you can apart from The God, if you are truthful.”

In this verse, افْتَرَاهُ belongs to the opponents’ speech.

They claim that Muhammad invented the Qur’an and attributed it to God.

The Qur’an reports this claim, then challenges them to produce a surah like it.

Thus افتراه means “he fabricated it,” but the speakers advance the allegation; the Qur’an does not endorse it.

This distinction also matters in Hūd 11:13, al-Sajdah 32:3, al-Shūrā 42:24, and al-Aḥqāf 46:8.

In each case, first identify who is speaking and what they are claiming.

B. مُفْتَرَيَات: fabricated surahs in an answer to an accusation

Hūd 11:13

أَمْ يَقُولُونَ افْتَرَاهُ قُلْ فَأْتُوا بِعَشْرِ سُوَرٍ مِثْلِهِ مُفْتَرَيَاتٍ وَادْعُوا مَنِ اسْتَطَعْتُمْ مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ صَادِقِينَ

Am yaqūlūna iftarāhu qul fa-ʾtū bi-ʿashri suwarin mithlihi muftarayātin wa-dʿū mani staṭaʿtum min dūni llāhi in kuntum ṣādiqīn

Translation: “Or do they say, ‘He fabricated it’? Say: Then bring ten fabricated surahs like it, and call upon whomever you can apart from The God, if you are truthful.”

مُفْتَرَيَاتٍ is the feminine plural of مُفْتَرَاة and here describes the surahs.

It refers to composed or fabricated texts within the framework of the opponents’ claim about the Qur’an.

The verse does not commend fabricating lies as morally good behavior.

The sentence is structured as a response to a claim.

If the opponents say Muhammad made the Qur’an himself, they are asked to produce something like it.

مفتريات is therefore part of an argument challenging their allegation.

Grammatically, مفتريات describes سور; the verb افتراه itself does not mean “write ten surahs.”

C. مُفْتَرٍ: a person accused of fabricating lies

In al-Naḥl 16:101, when one verse’s replacement by another is mentioned, opponents call Muhammad مُفْتَرٍ.

This is the active participle of Form VIII.

The word means “fabricator”; in this context, it is used as an accusation by opponents.

The Qur’an immediately rejects the accusation in context and describes the ignorance of many of its speakers.

By contrast, in Hūd 11:50, Hūd accuses his people of making unfounded claims about their objects of worship.

These uses show that, linguistically, muftarin can appear in the speech of either side of a dispute.

The word alone does not establish whether the accusation is true.

Judgment requires examining the claim, its evidence, and whether the speaker is telling the truth or lying.

D. مُفْتَرًى: describing something as fabricated

In al-Qaṣaṣ 28:36, Moses’ opponents call his signs سِحْرٌ مُفْتَرًى, “fabricated magic.”

In Sabaʾ 34:43, opponents also speak of إِفْكٌ مُفْتَرًى, “a fabricated falsehood.”

مُفْتَرًى is a passive participle.

Unlike muftarin, which refers to the person making the claim, it describes something presented as fabricated.

In both verses, however, this description belongs to the opponents’ accusation.

Without attending to speaker and context, it cannot be treated as the Qur’an’s own judgment of Moses or revelation.

This reveals the important distinction between reporting an accusation and proving it.

Someone may confidently call a statement “fabricated,” but that confidence alone is not evidence that it is fabricated.

6. Fabrication, slander, and accusations against people

Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:12

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّبِيُّ إِذَا جَاءَكَ الْمُؤْمِنَاتُ يُبَايِعْنَكَ عَلَىٰ أَنْ لَا يُشْرِكْنَ بِاللَّهِ شَيْئًا وَلَا يَسْرِقْنَ وَلَا يَزْنِينَ وَلَا يَقْتُلْنَ أَوْلَادَهُنَّ وَلَا يَأْتِينَ بِبُهْتَانٍ يَفْتَرِينَهُ بَيْنَ أَيْدِيهِنَّ وَأَرْجُلِهِنَّ وَلَا يَعْصِينَكَ فِي مَعْرُوفٍ فَبَايِعْهُنَّ وَاسْتَغْفِرْ لَهُنَّ اللَّهَ إِنَّ اللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ

Yā ayyuhā n-nabiyyu idhā jāʾaka l-muʾminātu yubāyiʿnaka ʿalā an lā yushrikna bi-llāhi shayʾan wa-lā yasriqna wa-lā yaznīna wa-lā yaqtulna awlādahunna wa-lā yaʾtīna bi-buhtānin yaftarīnahu bayna aydīhinna wa-arjulihinna wa-lā yaʿṣīnaka fī maʿrūfin fa-bāyiʿhunna wa-staghfir lahunna llāha inna llāha ghafūrun raḥīm

Translation: “O Prophet, when believing women come to pledge to you that they will associate nothing with The God, will not steal, will not commit adultery, will not kill their children, will not bring a slander they fabricate between their hands and feet, and will not disobey you in what is right, then accept their pledge and ask The God to forgive them. Indeed, The God is Forgiving, Merciful.”

The form يَفْتَرِينَهُ occurs here.

The verb refers to the women addressed in the pledge, while the pronoun هُ refers to the slander.

بُهْتَان belongs to another root and concerns a false accusation that can astonish its hearer or the accused person.

يَفْتَرِينَهُ expresses the act of fabricating that slander.

Despite their related meanings, the two words therefore perform different roles.

Buhtān identifies the kind of false statement; iftirāʾ identifies its fabrication and attribution.

بَيْنَ أَيْدِيهِنَّ وَأَرْجُلِهِنَّ

This expression deserves attention.

Historical and exegetical explanations sometimes associate it with falsely attributing a child to a husband or concealing the child’s actual parentage.

This may be one instance under discussion, but the wording itself should be examined independently of external narratives.

The verse describes slander that the women fabricate and bring. “Between their hands and feet” belongs within that construction.

A basic translation should therefore preserve the verse’s image, rather than replace the Qur’anic wording with possible historical details lacking conclusive evidence.

For f-r-y, the important point is that fabrication here is not confined to lying against God.

Fabricating an accusation against people can also be expressed with this verb.

This use illustrates the root’s semantic range.

Fabricated falsehood and social harm

An unfounded accusation can damage reputation, family relationships, social trust, and human rights.

Someone may invent a report about another person and publish it as truth.

If the report is fabricated, this conduct falls within the meaning of iftirāʾ.

Yet someone who deliberately invents an accusation must be distinguished from someone who misheard a report and repeated it without sufficient knowledge.

Both may cause harm, but their knowledge, intention, and responsibility are not identical.

This distinction does not trivialize spreading false reports: people are responsible for their speech.

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:6 commands verification when a report comes from a transgressor, so that people are not harmed unknowingly.

Al-Nūr 24:15 likewise describes people uttering something without knowledge and considering it trivial, although it is grave before God.

These verses matter for responsibility concerning reports, accusations, and spreading statements, but they do not themselves contain derivatives of f-r-y.

7. Distinguishing iftirāʾ from kadhib, buhtān, ifk, and ikhtilāq

F-r-y (اِفْتَرَى) and k-dh-b (كَذَبَ)

Kadhib concerns a statement or claim contrary to truth.

In its predominant Qur’anic use, iftirāʾ emphasizes fabricating and attributing such a statement.

Al-Anʿām 6:21 places two actions side by side: fabricating lies against God and rejecting His signs as false.

This distinction matters.

One person may invent a false claim in God’s name.

Another may dismiss a true statement as a lie.

These actions may be related, but they are not exactly the same.

A proposition’s falsity and proof that its speaker knowingly fabricated it are also distinct questions.

Analysis of iftirāʾ should therefore examine both the false attribution and the claimant’s conduct.

F-r-y (اِفْتَرَى) and ʾ-f-k (إِفْك)

Ifk is used in the Qur’an for falsehood and distortion of truth.

In al-Furqān 25:4, opponents call the Qur’an إِفْكٌ and say Muhammad fabricated it.

Ifk and iftarā occur together in this sentence.

Their combination shows that ifk can identify the alleged false content, while iftirāʾ refers to its fabrication.

A single verse should not establish an absolute boundary for every use of these roots.

Both concern falsehood and distortion of truth; their precise distinctions require examination of their individual uses.

F-r-y (اِفْتَرَى) and b-h-t (بُهْتَان)

Buhtān concerns false accusation and speech that causes astonishment or bewilderment.

Iftirāʾ expresses the act of fabricating or attributing a false claim.

The two occur in one sentence in al-Mumtaḥanah 60:12.

Someone may fabricate slander and attribute it to another.

In that case, buhtān is the kind of accusation and iftirāʾ the act of fashioning it.

Not all Qur’anic instances of iftirāʾ concern personal accusations: many concern invented religious rulings or claims.

F-r-y (اِفْتَرَى) and kh-l-q (اِخْتَلَقَ)

In early Arabic, khalq can concern measuring, shaping, and producing.

In some uses, ikhtilāq concerns inventing and fashioning unfounded statements.

In Ṣād 38:7, opponents say of the message إِنْ هَٰذَا إِلَّا اخْتِلَاقٌ, “This is nothing but an invention.”

These are their reported words, attributing fabrication to the message.

Ikhtilāq and iftirāʾ are semantically close.

Closeness alone does not prove that every aspect of their meaning is identical.

Nor does khalq, in uses concerning actual creation, mean fabricating a lie in itself.

The sense of fabrication in ikhtilāq follows from its particular form and usage.

F-r-y (اِفْتَرَى) and kh-r-ṣ (خَرَصَ)

In some Qur’anic uses, kharṣ concerns conjecture and speaking without sufficient knowledge.

Someone may guess about a subject and present that guess as truth.

This may overlap with iftirāʾ, but not every incorrect guess is necessarily deliberate fabrication.

Al-Anʿām 6:116 discusses following conjecture and kharṣ.

The distinction matters for understanding responsibility in claims to knowledge.

Someone who does not know should distinguish their ignorance from a claim to knowledge.

Someone who invents a statement and attributes it to God or another person assumes a different responsibility.

8. Morphology, syntax, and limits of meaning

F-r-y is a final-weak root whose third consonant is yāʾ.

Its basic verb appears as فَرَى يَفْرِي.

Form VIII appears as اِفْتَرَى يَفْتَرِي.

The final alif maqṣūrah in افترى does not indicate a separate root: it is the verb’s written form in this construction.

Verb and verbal noun

اِفْتَرَى is the perfect verb.

يَفْتَرِي is the imperfect verb.

اِفْتِرَاء is the Form VIII verbal noun.

In al-Anʿām 6:138 and al-Anʿām 6:140, افْتِرَاءً occurs in the accusative and expresses fabrication in connection with the claims and conduct described.

This verbal noun must not be equated with فَرِيًّا in Maryam 19:27, since their structures and meanings differ.

Active and passive participles

مُفْتَرٍ is the active participle: someone who fabricates a false statement or claim. Context establishes whether the description applies truthfully or is reported as a false accusation.

مُفْتَرًى is the passive participle: something presented as made up or fabricated.

The passive participle occurs in al-Qaṣaṣ 28:36 and Sabaʾ 34:43.

In al-Naḥl 16:101, the active participle appears in the opponents’ speech.

Translation should preserve this distinction.

“Fabricator” describes a person.

“Fabricated” describes the statement or thing being discussed.

The pronoun in افْتَرَاهُ

In an expression such as افْتَرَاهُ, هُ is the verb’s object pronoun.

In Yūnus 10:38, it refers to the Qur’an.

The sentence thus means that the opponents claim Muhammad fabricated it.

Elsewhere, the pronoun may refer to a statement, claim, or another object.

Its antecedent must be determined from the specific context.

يَفْتَرِينَهُ in al-Mumtaḥanah

In al-Mumtaḥanah 60:12, يَفْتَرِينَهُ has a feminine plural subject and an attached object pronoun.

The construction identifies the women addressed as the agents fabricating the slander, with the final pronoun referring to that slander.

Here, fabrication concerns making an accusation, rather than claiming revelation or legislation.

Does iftirāʾ always involve knowing that a claim is false?

In ordinary usage, iftirāʾ concerns fabricating and attributing false statements and is closely associated with claims a person invents.

When judging individuals, however, the verb’s meaning must be distinguished from proving their inner state.

If the Qur’an explicitly describes a group as committing iftirāʾ, that description holds within its context.

But encountering someone’s mistaken interpretation today does not, by itself, prove that they knowingly fabricated a lie.

Conversely, if someone clearly knows the truth yet publishes a contrary claim in God’s or another person’s name, their responsibility should not be reduced to a simple mistake.

The meaning of iftirāʾ should remain explicit, while applying it to particular people requires evidence about their claims and conduct.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

A single fixed equivalent is insufficient for f-r-y.

For the physical use of فَرَى, suitable equivalents include “cut,” “split,” and, where appropriate, “cut and made to measure.”

يَفْرِي الْفَرِيَّ may be rendered “performs an extraordinary, outstanding deed.”

For فَرِيّ in Maryam 19:27, “an extraordinary and reprehensible deed” conveys both its great, unusual character and the speakers’ condemnation.

For the predominant Qur’anic branch, the following equivalents are proposed:

اِفْتَرَى — fabricated a lie; falsely attributed; invented.

يَفْتَرُونَ — they fabricate lies; they invent unfounded claims.

اِفْتِرَاءً — falsely attributing; fabricating a false claim.

مُفْتَرٍ — fabricator; in a reported accusation, someone accused of fabrication.

مُفْتَرًى — fabricated; invented; falsely attributed.

مُفْتَرَيَاتٍ — fabricated statements or texts.

فِرْيَة in Arabic — a fabricated lie; a false accusation.

Renderings in important verses

In Maryam 19:27:

“O Mary, you have indeed done something extraordinary and reprehensible.”

In al-Naḥl 16:116:

“Thereby fabricating lies against The God.”

In al-Anʿām 6:140:

“By fabricating lies against The God.”

In Yūnus 10:38:

“Or do they say, ‘He fabricated it’?”

In Hūd 11:13:

“Then bring ten fabricated surahs like it.”

In al-Naḥl 16:101:

“They said: You are only a fabricator.”

In al-Mumtaḥanah 60:12:

“And will not bring a slander they fabricate between their hands and feet.”

The choice of equivalent should also account for the speaker.

Sometimes the Qur’an itself condemns fabrication.

Sometimes a Messenger warns against fabricating lies against God.

Sometimes the Messengers’ opponents accuse them of fabrication.

If translation obscures this distinction, a reported accusation may appear to be an endorsed fact.

A brief lexical explanation should also preserve two fields:

Brief root meaning: cutting and shaping; in the iftirāʾ branch, fabricating and attributing a false claim.

Meaning in this verse: the derivative’s contextual sense, such as lying against God, alleging that the Qur’an is fabricated, inventing slander, or describing an extraordinary deed.

Each occurrence’s explanation should emphasize the branch relevant to its context. A short explanation of a word need not repeat the root’s entire semantic history.

10. Conclusion

In early Arabic, f-r-y concerns cutting, splitting, and shaping. Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā’s verse shows that فَرَى could express proper cutting and executing a previously determined design.

Lexical sources record expressions from the same family for performing extraordinary deeds and fabricating false statements.

Without sufficient historical evidence, however, these branches should not all be treated as established stages in one development.

Two main Qur’anic uses can be distinguished.

First, فَرِيًّا in Maryam 19:27 describes a great, extraordinary deed that Mary’s people regard as objectionable. Their accusation must not be turned into the Qur’an’s own judgment of Mary.

Second, the iftirāʾ family, which in most Qur’anic occurrences concerns fabricating and attributing a false claim.

The Qur’an discusses fabrication in various contexts: attributing invented rulings to God, falsely claiming revelation, assigning divine standing to other objects of worship, accusing Messengers of inventing their message, and making accusations against people.

This range shows that iftirāʾ extends beyond lying in ordinary conversation; it may involve constructing a claim and attributing it to another authority.

At the same time, falsehood itself, fabricating falsehood, rejecting a true statement as false, and reporting an unproven accusation must be distinguished.

The root’s historical meaning concerns cutting and shaping; in its predominant Qur’anic branch, iftirāʾ means fabricating and attributing a statement or claim contrary to truth.

What matters in iftirāʾ is more than the statement’s surface content: the claimed attribution matters too.

Someone may invent a statement and attribute it to God. Another may attribute words to a person who never said them. Another may invent a claim about revelation and present it as truth.

In all these cases, the central issue is faithfulness in attributing statements and claims.

The Qur’anic root f-r-y thus connects with human responsibility for truth, speech, and the social effects of claims.

Preserving these distinctions is essential to Forqan’s translation: not every incorrect statement is necessarily deliberate fabrication, not every accusation of fabrication is true, and not everything people find extraordinary or unconventional is necessarily a lie or sin.
