The root ف ر د — Being single, alone, or separate from others

Qur’anic and lexical forms

فَرْدًا
(fardan)
Alone, according to relationship and context

فُرَادَى
(furādā)
Individually and separately

أَفْرَدَ / اِنْفَرَدَ
(afrada / infarada)
Lexical verbs not used in the Qur’an

The triliteral Arabic root ف ر د concerns being single, alone, or apart from others. فَرْد denotes someone or something without a companion or counterpart in the situation under discussion. This may mean having no companion, being separated from a group, having no partner in an undertaking, or being unmatched in a particular quality.

فُرَادَى likewise describes people considered separately, each as an individual.

The root occurs five times in the Qur’an, in two forms: فَرْدًا three times and فُرَادَى twice. Its uses range from Zechariah’s being alone and asking for a child to each person’s appearance before God and the invitation to independent reflection.

Crucially, فَرْد does not always mean someone socially isolated, bereft of others, or abandoned. A person may live among others yet act independently in a task. Someone with family and friends may also be individually accountable in a particular situation.

Thus, the root’s semantic core is being single or separate in a specified respect. Context determines whether this means physical solitude, lack of a companion, independence in a decision, or uniqueness.

1. The root in early Arabic and the historical standing of its evidence

In early Arabic, فَرْد described something that is single, without another accompanying or matching it.

In recorded Arabic usage, فَرَدَ and اِنْفَرَدَ concern becoming alone or standing apart from others. أَفْرَدَهُ means separating someone or something from a group and making it stand alone.

Several situations can be distinguished within this semantic field.

First, being one rather than several, such as one camel as opposed to several camels.

Second, being apart from a group, such as an animal that has moved away from its herd.

Third, having no companion in a situation, such as a traveler making a journey alone.

Fourth, being unmatched in a quality, such as a sword with no known equal in workmanship.

These uses belong to a common semantic field, but they should not all be equated with a single social or psychological condition.

Evidence attributed to pre-Islamic poetry

In a poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Muraqqish al-Akbar, he addresses his beloved:

وَإِذَا مَا رَأَيْتِ رَكْبًا مُخِبِّينَ
يَقُودُونَ مُقْرَبَاتٍ جِيَادَا

Wa-idhā mā ra’ayti rakban mukhibbīna / yaqūdūna muqrabātin jiyādā.

فَهُمُ صُحْبَتِي عَلَى أَرْحُلِ الْمَيْسِ
يُزَجُّونَ أَيْنُقًا أَفْرَادَا

Fa-humu ṣuḥbatī ʿalā arḥuli l-maysi / yuzajjūna aynuqan afrādā.

Translation: “If you see a company of riders hurrying along, leading fine, well-kept horses, they are my companions on saddles of mays wood, urging individual she-camels onward.”

In this poem, أَفْرَادَا describes the she-camels.

The poet describes riders traveling together, while the camels are called individual or separate.

This illustrates the relational sense of being separate: the camels can be distinguished individually while still belonging to a caravan. The adjective alone does not establish their exact spacing or manner of movement.

Being individual therefore does not necessarily mean being outside every group or having no relationships.

This poem is transmitted in al-Mufaḍḍaliyyāt among works attributed to al-Muraqqish al-Akbar. Its attribution to a pre-Islamic poet and preservation in an early anthology provide evidence for investigating usage before the Qur’an. Nevertheless, it reaches us through later transmission, rather than a surviving record contemporary with its composition.

With that historical qualification, the example supports the senses “separately” and “individually” and provides evidence for this semantic branch in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

Physical and social uses

Early dictionaries use ظَبْيَةٌ فَارِدٌ for a gazelle separated from its herd.

شَجَرَةٌ فَارِدَةٌ likewise means a tree standing apart from other trees.

In these two examples, singleness concerns spatial position or the relationship of one thing to a group of its own kind.

In رَاكِبٌ مُفْرَدٌ, the rider has no companion.

By contrast, descriptions of a sword as فَرْد or فَرِيد can concern its unmatched quality. The transmitted poetic expression سَيْفِ الصَّيْقَلِ الفَرَدِ also records فَرَد with a vowel on the middle consonant.

The latter use concerns being unmatched, rather than spatial solitude.

فَرِيد is also used for a pearl or precious bead separated from other beads or distinguished by its value and quality.

These uses show that singleness can describe both an observable situation and a comparative quality.

Yet the occasional meaning “unmatched” does not imply that every person who is alone is superior or more valuable than others.

Superiority, value, and distinction emerge from the comparison and its accompanying indications, not from the root alone.

2. Linguistic forms and the semantic core

Important forms in this Arabic family include:

  • فَرْد: single, alone, separate, or unmatched.
  • فُرَادَى: individually, separately, each alone.
  • أَفْرَاد: a plural of فَرْد; individual people or things.
  • فَرِيد: unique, unmatched, or distinguished; in some uses, a separate or precious pearl.
  • فَارِد: apart from others or solitary.
  • أَفْرَدَ: separated, made solitary, or assigned to one person.
  • إِفْرَاد: separating or setting apart.
  • اِنْفَرَدَ: became alone or separated from others.
  • تَفَرَّدَ: became the sole or unmatched participant in a matter.
  • اِسْتَفْرَدَ: found someone or something alone, isolated it, or selected it for oneself.
  • مُفْرَد: separated, solitary, or a single unit as opposed to a collection.

In later linguistic and grammatical disciplines, مُفْرَد also became a technical term for a form that is neither dual nor plural.

This technical use relates to being one, but a later grammatical definition should not replace the word’s meaning in the Qur’an.

Of this family, the Qur’an uses only فَرْدًا and فُرَادَى.

The shared semantic core can be defined as:

Being one or standing apart from others in a specified respect, without a companion, partner, or counterpart in that same respect.

This definition accommodates Zechariah’s solitude, each person’s coming before God, and individual reflection in Saba’.

The root itself does not determine whether solitude is desirable or undesirable, voluntary or imposed, temporary or permanent.

3. فُرَادَى: Each person’s appearance before God

Al-Anʿām 6:94

وَلَقَدْ جِئْتُمُونَا فُرَادَىٰ كَمَا خَلَقْنَاكُمْ أَوَّلَ مَرَّةٍ وَتَرَكْتُمْ مَا خَوَّلْنَاكُمْ وَرَاءَ ظُهُورِكُمْ ۖ وَمَا نَرَىٰ مَعَكُمْ شُفَعَاءَكُمُ الَّذِينَ زَعَمْتُمْ أَنَّهُمْ فِيكُمْ شُرَكَاءُ ۚ لَقَدْ تَقَطَّعَ بَيْنَكُمْ وَضَلَّ عَنْكُمْ مَا كُنْتُمْ تَزْعُمُونَ

Wa-laqad ji’tumūnā furādā kamā khalaqnākum awwala marratin wa-taraktum mā khawwalnākum warā’a ẓuhūrikum. Wa-mā narā maʿakum shufaʿā’akumu lladhīna zaʿamtum annahum fīkum shurakā’. Laqad taqaṭṭaʿa baynakum wa-ḍalla ʿankum mā kuntum tazʿumūn.

Translation: “You have indeed come to Us individually, as We created you the first time, leaving behind your backs what We had granted you. We do not see with you your intercessors whom you claimed were partners concerning you. The ties between you have been severed, and what you used to claim has deserted you.”

Here فُرَادَى describes people’s condition when they appear before God.

The verse concerns people who possessed wealth, resources, and relationships in worldly life and attributed intercession and a share in their destiny to other persons or beings.

When they appear before God, those claimed supports are no longer with them.

Here فُرَادَى means that each appears alone, apart from what they imagined would support them.

Being alone does not mean losing every human relationship

Solitude is connected with three matters in this verse.

First, a person has left behind what they possessed in the world.

Second, their supposed intercessors are not with them.

Third, the connections and claims on which they relied have fallen away.

The subject is therefore more than people’s physical separation: imagined supports collapse, revealing each person’s actual position before God.

Nevertheless, فُرَادَى should not be extended to mean the destruction of all human relationships or the absence of every kind of companionship in the hereafter. The verse emphasizes the failure of the companionship and partnerships claimed by its addressees.

Nor does كَمَا خَلَقْنَاكُمْ أَوَّلَ مَرَّةٍ imply that human beings were biologically independent of parents or others at the beginning of life.

The comparison concerns their appearance before God in relation to their initial creation and the loss of subsequently acquired advantages and possessions, rather than denying an infant’s natural dependence on others’ care.

The relationship between فُرَادَى and intercession

The same verse also contains شُفَعَاءَكُمُ.

The lexical meaning of شَفْع concerns joining and accompanying, placing it in semantic contrast with being single.

Yet intercession is more than two people standing together. In social usage, it involves joining in support of a request, person, or undertaking.

The verse criticizes those who claimed that their intercessors were partners in their affairs.

When the truth of that claim becomes apparent, those imagined intercessors are not with them.

This connects with human responsibility before God. No one can escape the reality of their actions and responsibility by assigning independent authority to someone else.

However, without examining other verses, this criticism of imagined intercessors should not become a denial of every form of permitted intercession.

The principle emphasized is that having relationships, followers, a leader, or a supposed intercessor creates no independent ownership of a person’s destiny.

4. فَرْدًا: Appearing without claimed supports

A. Maryam 19:80: Coming alone after boasting of wealth and children

Maryam 19:80

وَنَرِثُهُ مَا يَقُولُ وَيَأْتِينَا فَرْدًا

Wa-narithuhu mā yaqūlu wa-ya’tīnā fardā.

Translation: “We shall inherit from him what he speaks of, and he will come to Us alone.”

Understanding فَرْدًا here requires attention to the preceding verses.

Maryam 19:77 describes someone who rejects God’s signs and confidently claims that he will be given wealth and children.

Maryam 19:78 asks whether he has gained knowledge of the unseen or obtained a covenant from the Entirely Merciful.

Maryam 19:79 then rejects his claim and announces that his words will be recorded.

Maryam 19:80 describes his eventual condition with وَيَأْتِينَا فَرْدًا.

Here being alone concerns someone who can no longer secure a guaranteed position by claiming wealth, children, or knowledge of the future.

What he relied upon in the world does not possess final authority over his destiny.

Yet فَرْدًا does not itself mean poor, childless, or condemned. If these ideas enter the analysis, they must come from the preceding verses and the person’s circumstances.

Someone may have family and possessions in this world while appearing individually for the reckoning in the hereafter.

The root’s meaning is therefore not equivalent to lacking a family in worldly life.

B. Maryam 19:95: Each person’s coming on the Day of Resurrection

Maryam 19:95

وَكُلُّهُمْ آتِيهِ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ فَرْدًا

Wa-kulluhum ātīhi yawma l-qiyāmati fardā.

Translation: “Every one of them will come to Him on the Day of Resurrection alone.”

Two expressions appear together in this verse:

كُلُّهُمْ — every one of them.

فَرْدًا — individually, each alone.

Together they express a universal statement while describing each person’s manner of appearing individually.

Maryam 19:93 states that everyone in the heavens and earth comes to the Entirely Merciful as a servant.

Maryam 19:94 speaks of their precise enumeration.

Maryam 19:95 then states that all will come individually to God on the Day of Resurrection.

The verse thus joins the universality of their appearance with each person’s individuality.

However numerous people may be, none escapes divine knowledge and reckoning in this appearance.

Does individuality mean absolute solitude at the Resurrection?

Other Qur’anic verses describe groups, followers, leaders, witnesses, and conversations among people in the hereafter.

Therefore, فَرْدًا in Maryam 19:95 should not mean that no two people see each other at the Resurrection or that there is no interaction or conversation.

The word itself does not yield that conclusion.

The verse states that each person appears before God in their individual position.

This accords with personal responsibility, the principle expressed in al-Anʿām 6:164: no bearer of a burden bears another’s burden.

Individual responsibility does not mean that one person’s actions have no effect on others. Someone may influence others or invite them to good or evil and be accountable for their own contribution.

In any case, their responsibility does not become identical with another person’s responsibility.

5. فَرْدًا in Zechariah’s prayer: Having no child and successor

Al-Anbiyā’ 21:89

وَزَكَرِيَّا إِذْ نَادَىٰ رَبَّهُ رَبِّ لَا تَذَرْنِي فَرْدًا وَأَنْتَ خَيْرُ الْوَارِثِينَ

Wa-Zakariyyā idh nādā rabbahu rabbi lā tadharnī fardan wa-anta khayru l-wārithīn.

Translation: “And Zechariah, when he called upon his Lord: My Lord, do not leave me alone; You are the best of inheritors.”

Here Zechariah asks God not to leave him فَرْدًا.

Read without context, the word might suggest that he had no companion, family, or wife.

Other verses, however, make clear that he had a wife and that his request concerned a child and succession.

In Maryam 19:5, Zechariah expresses concern about his relatives after him, mentions his wife’s barrenness, and asks God to grant him a successor from Himself.

In Maryam 19:6, he asks that this successor inherit from him and the family of Jacob.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:38, he also asks for good offspring.

Within the story, لَا تَذَرْنِي فَرْدًا therefore expresses a request for a child and successor who will remain after him.

Being alone and having no one

Zechariah is not entirely bereft of others at this time. He has a wife and is in contact with people.

Yet he regards himself as alone in relation to the child and successor he desires.

Thus فَرْد here concerns the lack of a companion in a particular respect.

This distinction matters because solitude does not always mean having no social relationships.

Someone may have many relatives and friends but lack a successor to a particular responsibility.

Conversely, someone may live alone while possessing knowledge, social support, or other resources.

The precise meaning of being alone emerges from the relationship addressed by the sentence and story.

وَأَنْتَ خَيْرُ الْوَارِثِينَ

After making his request, Zechariah says: “You are the best of inheritors.”

This shows that, despite asking for a child and successor, he recognizes God’s ultimate ownership and authority.

In al-Anbiyā’ 21:90, the Qur’an states that his prayer was answered, John was granted to him, and his wife was made fit for him.

This response further clarifies the object of his request.

The word فَرْد itself does not mean childless; in this context, Zechariah’s solitude concerns the absence of a child and successor.

Nor does the verse imply that every childless person is deficient, worthless, or deprived of God’s approval.

Zechariah shares his personal desire with God. It neither defines everyone’s worth nor judges the destiny of all people without children.

6. فُرَادَى: Individual reflection apart from unexamined conformity

Saba’ 34:46

قُلْ إِنَّمَا أَعِظُكُمْ بِوَاحِدَةٍ ۖ أَنْ تَقُومُوا لِلَّهِ مَثْنَىٰ وَفُرَادَىٰ ثُمَّ تَتَفَكَّرُوا ۚ مَا بِصَاحِبِكُمْ مِنْ جِنَّةٍ ۚ إِنْ هُوَ إِلَّا نَذِيرٌ لَكُمْ بَيْنَ يَدَيْ عَذَابٍ شَدِيدٍ

Qul innamā aʿiẓukum bi-wāḥidatin an taqūmū li-llāhi mathnā wa-furādā thumma tatafakkarū. Mā bi-ṣāḥibikum min jinnatin. In huwa illā nadhīrun lakum bayna yaday ʿadhābin shadīd.

Translation: “Say: I counsel you in just one thing: that you stand for The God, in pairs and individually, and then reflect. Your companion has no madness. He is only a warner to you before a severe punishment.”

This verse illustrates a different use of فُرَادَى.

In al-Anʿām 6:94, people are described as appearing individually before God.

In Saba’ 34:46, however, people in worldly life are invited to reflect, both in pairs and individually.

Here مَثْنَىٰ and فُرَادَىٰ appear in contrast.

مَثْنَى means two by two.

فُرَادَى means one by one or individually.

The Qur’an thus does not command exclusively shared or exclusively individual reflection; it places the two modes together.

Why in pairs and individually?

The context concerns an accusation that opponents directed at Muhammad.

They accused Muhammad of madness.

The Qur’an asks them to stand for God and then reflect.

In a pair, a person can converse with another, present reasons, ask questions, and test an opinion.

Alone, a person can reflect on the truth of the matter without depending on others’ immediate reactions.

These two approaches can complement one another.

Someone who always follows the crowd may accept a statement because it is widespread rather than because it is well supported.

Conversely, someone who never tests an opinion with others may retain errors that conversation and joint examination could reveal.

By including both مَثْنَى and فُرَادَى, the verse allows its audience both forms of examination.

This is a reflection on the verse’s application; it does not mean that فُرَادَى itself signifies truth-seeking or intellectual independence.

The lexical meaning remains “individually.” Its direction and purpose emerge from لِلَّهِ and ثُمَّ تَتَفَكَّرُوا.

Is individual reflection superior to shared reflection?

The verse does not state such a ranking.

Mentioning مَثْنَى beside فُرَادَى shows that both modes are included; this alone establishes no absolute superiority of either one.

Likewise, the invitation to stand individually shows that shared examination is not the only valued mode here.

The emphasis is on examining the matter sincerely and thoughtfully.

One person may discover the truth in company, while another recognizes an error during solitary reflection.

In either case, evidence and truth should be the criterion, rather than the number of people who accept an opinion.

Another point is that بِوَاحِدَةٍ belongs to و ح د, not ف ر د.

It refers to one counsel, whereas فُرَادَى specifies how the audience stands and reflects.

Their shared association with being one does not make their roots or grammatical roles identical.

7. Singleness, uniqueness, and divine oneness

In Arabic, فَرْد can describe something with no equal.

Consequently, some later theological writings and prayers also use it in describing God.

In the Qur’an, however, none of the five occurrences of ف ر د is a direct name or description of God.

The Qur’an uses forms of و ح د to express God’s oneness.

For example, al-Ikhlāṣ 112:1 states:

«قُلْ هُوَ اللَّهُ أَحَدٌ»

Qul huwa llāhu aḥad.

Al-Baqarah 2:163 speaks of إِلَٰهٌ وَاحِدٌ, “one god.”

These two expressions directly concern God’s oneness.

فَرْد, وَاحِد, and أَحَد are semantically related, but semantic proximity does not make them all one root.

Nor is a person’s singleness in a particular situation identical in meaning or being to God’s oneness.

A person may travel alone, have no partner in an undertaking, or be considered unmatched in a quality. All these situations, however, are defined relative to circumstances, resources, and other beings.

God’s oneness in the Qur’an is a broader subject involving divinity, lordship, creation, ownership, and the absence of a divine equal.

A study of this root should therefore not automatically import every discussion of divine oneness into the meaning of فَرْد.

8. Comparison with related roots

ف ر د (فَرْد) and و ح د (وَاحِد)

Both roots can concern being one.

وَاحِد can indicate one thing or person as opposed to several. In counting, one is the first number.

فَرْد, however, often emphasizes being apart from others or lacking a companion or counterpart.

Someone may, for example, be one member of a group. Numerically, that person is one; when separated from the group, they may also be described as solitary or alone.

This distinction should not become an absolute rule that وَاحِد never expresses solitude or فَرْد never expresses the number one.

The roots share a semantic field, but their uses require separate examination.

ف ر د (فَرْد) and و ت ر (وَتْر)

In Arabic, وَتْر denotes something single or odd as opposed to paired or even.

Al-Fajr 89:3 states:

«وَالشَّفْعِ وَالْوَتْرِ»

Wa-sh-shafʿi wa-l-watr.

Here شَفْع and وَتْر stand in contrast.

In this use, وَتْر emphasizes being odd or single rather than paired.

فَرْد, however, can describe a person without a companion even when odd and even numbers are not at issue.

In al-Anbiyā’ 21:89, Zechariah uses فَرْدًا for his situation without a child and successor, rather than the numerical distinction between odd and even.

An odd or unpaired thing can thus be described as single in a particular respect, but not every use of فَرْد should be restricted to an odd number.

ف ر د (فَرْد) and ش ف ع (شَفَعَ)

شَفْع concerns pairing and joining.

In social usage, intercession can mean joining in support of a person, request, or undertaking.

In al-Anʿām 6:94, فُرَادَى and شُفَعَاءَكُمُ occur together.

This matters because people appear individually, without the intercessors they imagined would accompany them.

But that contrast does not establish that all human companionship or support is wrong.

People can assist one another in good actions. The issue is assigning ultimate authority and a guaranteed destiny to someone other than God.

ف ر د (فَرْد) and ع ز ل (عَزَلَ)

عَزْل concerns setting aside, removing, or separating something from a position or group.

فَرْد can describe the condition of someone standing alone.

عَزْل focuses more on the act of separating or removing, whereas being فَرْد describes the state of being single or alone.

Someone may choose solitude without having been excluded by anyone.

Conversely, someone may be removed from a responsibility while continuing to live among family and society.

Being removed and being alone are therefore different concepts.

ف ر د (فَرْد) and و ح ش (وَحْشَة)

In some Arabic uses, وَحْشَة concerns fear, unfamiliarity, and a sense of loneliness.

فَرْد itself, however, does not express fear or sadness.

Someone may be alone in a place and enjoy calm and concentration. Another person may feel lonely and alienated in a large crowd.

Objective solitude and the feeling of loneliness are thus distinct matters.

In Saba’ 34:46, فُرَادَى is associated with reflection, without describing the state as unpleasant or fearful.

By contrast, فَرْدًا in Zechariah’s prayer describes a situation he asks God to change.

This difference shows that the value and feeling associated with solitude must be understood from context.

ف ر د (فَرْد) and فِرْدَوْس

Despite its similar opening consonants, فِرْدَوْس is a distinct word and should not be treated as a triliteral derivative of ف ر د.

In al-Kahf 18:107 and al-Mu’minūn 23:11, فِرْدَوْس occurs in the context of the Garden.

Its historical etymology involves other languages and the word’s history and requires independent study.

The resemblance between فَرْد and فِرْدَوْس does not imply that Firdaws means a place for solitary or incomparable people.

9. Morphological and grammatical points

فَرْدًا: A person in a state of being alone

فَرْدًا is accusative in all three Qur’anic occurrences.

In Maryam 19:80:

«وَيَأْتِينَا فَرْدًا»

Wa-ya’tīnā fardā.

In Maryam 19:95:

«وَكُلُّهُمْ آتِيهِ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ فَرْدًا»

Wa-kulluhum ātīhi yawma l-qiyāmati fardā.

In al-Anbiyā’ 21:89:

«لَا تَذَرْنِي فَرْدًا»

Lā tadharnī fardā.

In the two verses of Maryam, فَرْدًا describes a person’s state when coming before God.

In al-Anbiyā’, it describes Zechariah’s state, in which he asks not to remain.

One lexical form thus appears in three sentences with different subjects.

The same case ending does not make Zechariah’s solitude and people’s solitude at the Resurrection identical in cause, time, or consequence.

فُرَادَى: A plural with an individualizing meaning

Arabic uses فُرَادَى for people or things situated separately.

Semantically, it can refer to a plurality of individuals while describing each member’s state individually.

This is clear in al-Anʿām 6:94.

The audience is addressed in the plural:

«جِئْتُمُونَا»

Ji’tumūnā.

Their condition, however, is described as:

«فُرَادَى»

Furādā.

That is, a collection of people, each of whom has appeared separately.

In Saba’ 34:46, فُرَادَى also contrasts with مَثْنَى and refers to standing individually.

The form فُرَادَى therefore does not mean that no group exists. It specifies how the members of that group are situated or act.

Being single and making something separate

In Qur’anic usage, فَرْد expresses a state or description.

In Arabic, however, أَفْرَدَ can describe the act of separating something or setting it alone.

For example, أَفْرَدَ الشَّيْءَ means that someone separated a thing from a collection.

This verbal form does not occur in the Qur’an.

The translation of فَرْدًا should therefore not automatically import the meaning “someone deliberately isolated.”

An event may leave someone alone, or a person may stand individually in a situation; the noun فَرْد itself does not specify the cause.

10. Challenges in understanding and translation

The first error is equating being alone with having no one in every occurrence.

Zechariah in al-Anbiyā’ 21:89 has a wife but regards himself as alone in relation to a child and successor.

In Saba’ 34:46, a person can voluntarily act individually for the purpose of reflection.

In Maryam 19:95, individuality concerns appearing at the Resurrection.

These three situations are semantically related but do not have identical causes or consequences.

The second error turns individuality into absolute human independence.

People depend on many factors, resources, and relationships in life. Even someone making an independent decision does not leave all human and natural connections behind.

Thus فُرَادَى in Saba’ 34:46 should not be translated as denying people’s need for knowledge, dialogue, and cooperation.

The third error equates individual appearance at the Resurrection with the absence of every relationship or conversation in the hereafter.

Other Qur’anic verses describe conversations between groups, testimony, and various relationships.

Being individual at the reckoning does not mean that no relationships exist; each person appears before God in their actual position.

The fourth error turns singleness into moral superiority.

فَرِيد can sometimes denote something unique and distinguished, but فَرْد in the Qur’an does not itself describe a superior person.

Zechariah asks to be delivered from remaining alone, whereas individual reflection in Saba’ forms part of an instruction.

Its moral value therefore emerges from the cause and subject of the situation.

The fifth error restricts responsibility to actions performed without anyone else.

Individuality at the reckoning does not conflict with responsibility for shared undertakings.

Several people may participate in a good or bad action, while each remains accountable for their own contribution, choice, and conduct.

Finally, فَرْد should not be confined to later philosophical and theological discussions. Its Qur’anic uses are clear and varied and should be examined before constructing technical definitions.

11. Suggested translations for Forqan

The distinction between being alone and being considered individually matters when translating this root’s forms.

In general usage, فَرْد can be translated “alone,” “single,” “solitary,” and, where appropriate, “unmatched.”

Suitable equivalents for فُرَادَى include “individually,” “separately,” “each alone,” and “one by one.”

In al-Anʿām 6:94:

“You have indeed come to Us individually, as We created you the first time.”

“Individually” is preferable here to “bereft of others,” because the audience is plural while each person’s condition is described individually.

In Maryam 19:80:

“And he will come to Us alone.”

In Maryam 19:95:

“Every one of them will come to Him on the Day of Resurrection alone.”

In al-Anbiyā’ 21:89:

“My Lord, do not leave me alone; You are the best of inheritors.”

Here solitude concerns the request for a child and successor. That explanation belongs in the verse’s analysis rather than in translating فَرْدًا directly as “childless.”

In Saba’ 34:46:

“In pairs and individually, and then reflect.”

This rendering preserves the contrast between مَثْنَى and فُرَادَى.

A word box can likewise distinguish the following:

Brief root meaning: Being single, alone, or apart from others.

Meaning in the verse: In al-Anʿām and Maryam, each person’s individual appearance; in al-Anbiyā’, Zechariah’s being alone in relation to a child and successor; in Saba’, individual reflection alongside reflection in pairs.

These are suggestions for distinguishing root meaning from contextual meaning. Installing this study does not replace the text of previously authored word boxes; revising them requires a separate review.

12. Conclusion

In early Arabic, ف ر د concerns being single, remaining alone, and standing apart from others.

Transmitted poetic evidence, particularly أَفْرَادَا in the poem attributed to al-Muraqqish al-Akbar, illustrates this family’s use for people or things considered separately, subject to the historical qualification discussed above.

In Arabic, this semantic field also extends to being unmatched, separating something from a collection, and acting independently in a task.

The Qur’an uses the root five times.

In al-Anʿām 6:94, people appear individually before God without their imagined supports.

Maryam 19:80 and Maryam 19:95 describe people’s individual appearance before God.

In al-Anbiyā’ 21:89, Zechariah asks God not to leave him without a child and successor.

In Saba’ 34:46, people are invited to stand and reflect, both in pairs and individually.

These uses show that being single can describe a person’s position before God, a family situation, or a way of acting and thinking.

The root’s semantic core is being one or alone in a specified respect and standing apart from others in that same respect.

Being single does not necessarily mean isolation, having no one, superiority, absolute independence, or a lack of social responsibility. Such features belong to a sentence’s meaning only when supported by context and other evidence.

In the Qur’an, a person can live, cooperate, and converse with others; ultimately, the reality of that person’s choices and deeds also belongs to them. Appearing individually before God does not remove responsibility within relationships. It shows that no relationship can replace truth and personal accountability.
