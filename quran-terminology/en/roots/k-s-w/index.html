Covering and providing clothing: from human dress to bones clothed with flesh

The Arabic root K S W concerns covering something with another material and providing clothing for someone. Its best-known use concerns human clothing: kasā describes giving someone a garment or clothing them. Its range extends beyond fabric to covering an object, vegetation covering the earth, or flesh covering bones.

The Quran uses this family in two main settings: providing clothing within family and social responsibilities and expiation; and clothing bones with flesh in accounts of creation and revival.

These apparently different settings share a core relationship: something covers something else. The covering is clothing in one case and flesh in the other.

K S W occurs five times in the Quran: three verbs and two occurrences of the noun كِسْوَة. The forms are وَكِسْوَتُهُنَّ in Al-Baqarah 2:233, نَكْسُوهَا in Al-Baqarah 2:259, وَاكْسُوهُمْ in Al-Nisāʾ 4:5, كِسْوَتُهُمْ in Al-Māʾidah 5:89, and فَكَسَوْنَا in Al-Muʾminūn 23:14.

These uses show that kasw concerns covering or providing clothing, rather than simply wearing clothes. Each verse requires attention to who covers, what is covered, and the covering material.

1. Historical meaning in early Arabic

Providing clothes and being clothed

In early Arabic, كَسَا يَكْسُو describes clothing someone or providing them with a garment.

كَسَوْتُهُ ثَوْبًا means “I clothed him with a garment” or “I gave him a garment.”

Both the recipient and the garment appear in the verb's construction.

كِسْوَة and كُسْوَة denote clothing. كِسَاء names a familiar covering, especially a garment thrown over the body or wrapped around oneself.

Kisāʾ can describe a garment or wrap, rather than necessarily a shirt or tailored item. Lack of stitching should not be made an indispensable part of its definition without evidence for the particular usage.

The family is consequently not restricted to one type of clothing in its attested uses. Covering the body or providing its covering is central.

Clothing itself, the act of clothing, and the condition of being clothed should nevertheless be distinguished. They are related but grammatically and semantically different.

Kasā, kasiya, and iktasā

The distinction between كَسَا and neighboring forms matters.

كَسَا ordinarily describes someone clothing another person or covering something.

كَسِيَ is recorded for becoming clothed or having clothes on.

اكْتَسَى likewise concerns putting on clothes or becoming covered.

In كَسَوْتُهُ ثَوْبًا, the speaker provides someone else with a garment or clothes them. In اكْتَسَى ثَوْبًا, the person puts on a garment.

The roles of the one covering and the one covered must therefore be distinguished.

كَاسٍ can describe someone who has clothing. Its active-participle form does not always mean that the person clothes others; attested usage also describes someone who is themselves clothed.

Early poetry and linguistic analysis discuss this feature.

A poetic witness from early Islamic Arabic

A poem attributed to Abū al-Aswad al-Duʾalī says:

كَسَانِي وَلَمْ أَسْتَكْسِهِ فَحَمِدْتُهُ
أَخٌ لِي يُعْطِينِي الْجَزِيلَ وَنَاصِرُ

kasānī wa-lam astaksihi fa-ḥamidtuhu / akhun lī yuʿṭīnī al-jazīla wa-nāṣir

“A brother who gives me generously and supports me clothed me without my asking him for clothing, and I praised him.”

Two forms of K S W occur here.

كَسَانِي means “he clothed me” or “he gave me a garment.”

أَسْتَكْسِهِ belongs to form X and concerns asking another person for clothing.

The poet distinguishes a gift requested from one given before any request. He praises someone who met his need without being asked.

Kasw here concerns clothing specifically, rather than every financial gift. Generosity and helpfulness are qualities of the action, not the verb's lexical meaning.

Abū al-Aswad lived in the early Islamic period. This witnesses Arabic close to the period of revelation, but is not independent pre-Islamic evidence.

Minor variants occur in transmission, including أَخٌ لِي and أَخٌ لَكَ. They do not change the root meaning under examination.

Kāsī in al-Ḥuṭayʾah's poetry

Al-Ḥuṭayʾah's famous verse says:

دَعِ الْمَكَارِمَ لَا تَرْحَلْ لِبُغْيَتِهَا
وَاقْعُدْ فَإِنَّكَ أَنْتَ الطَّاعِمُ الْكَاسِي

daʿi al-makārima lā tarḥal li-bughyatihā / wa-qʿud fa-innaka anta al-ṭāʿimu al-kāsī

“Leave noble deeds; do not travel in pursuit of them. Sit, for you are the one who is fed and clothed.”

The poet uses al-ṭāʿim al-kāsī reproachfully.

The target is content with ordinary necessities rather than pursuing nobility and virtue.

Lexicographers analyze kāsī in different ways. Some take it as someone possessing clothes, while in other uses its form can concern clothing another.

A derivative's pattern alone does not determine its meaning; the whole expression and established usage matter.

Al-Ḥuṭayʾah lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, but historical reports connect this verse to an incident under ʿUmar. It should therefore not be presented as pre-Islamic evidence.

The earth covered with vegetation

Classical Arabic also records اكْتَسَتِ الْأَرْضُ بِالنَّبَاتِ.

It means “the earth became covered with vegetation.”

The earth does not dress like a person; growing plants spread over its surface.

When plants grow and extend over large areas, the land can be described as covered by them.

The concept of covering thus extends beyond human dress.

The expression is recorded in dictionaries compiled after revelation; establishing its pre-Islamic currency requires independently datable evidence.

It would therefore be unjustified to claim that early Arabs necessarily explained everything through the earth putting on a garment.

The evidence clearly attests providing clothing and covering. The Quran itself records this family's use for bones covered with flesh.

The semantic core

Established Arabic and Quranic uses support the following core:

Covering a person or thing with another covering; for people, giving garments or providing clothing.

The covering may vary, but the relationship between the agent, the object covered, and the covering material remains.

Kasw does not itself mean complete concealment, guaranteed protection, adornment, warming, or meeting every need of life.

Those qualities may follow from the covering or context, but are not fixed components of the root.

2. Kiswah in the Quran: clothing as a right and responsibility

The two nominal occurrences concern social rights and responsibilities.

Both refer to actual human clothing, rather than a figurative covering.

Their roles differ. Al-Baqarah 2:233 concerns clothing mothers during nursing; Al-Māʾidah 5:89 lists clothing among the means of expiating an oath that requires expiation.

Al-Baqarah 2:233: responsibility for the mother's clothing

Al-Baqarah says:

وَعَلَى الْمَوْلُودِ لَهُ رِزْقُهُنَّ وَكِسْوَتُهُنَّ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ

wa-ʿalā al-mawlūdi lahu rizquhunna wa-kiswatuhunna bi-l-maʿrūf

Al-Baqarah 2:233

“The one for whom the child is born is responsible for their provision and clothing in a recognized, proper manner.”

Al-Baqarah 2:233

كِسْوَتُهُنَّ combines kiswah with the attached feminine plural pronoun hunna.

The pronoun refers to the mothers whose nursing the verse describes.

كِسْوَتُهُنَّ is coordinated with رِزْقُهُنَّ. Both are responsibilities assigned to الْمَوْلُودِ لَهُ, the child's father.

Provision and clothing are named separately.

رِزْقُهُنَّ concerns their sustenance and livelihood; كِسْوَتُهُنَّ their clothing.

Although clothing is part of livelihood, naming it independently brings this need into focus.

A nursing mother not only has responsibilities toward the child; she also has needs that must be met.

This is especially significant where the parents have separated while the child continues to need nursing and care.

The verse places provision for the mother alongside care for the child, giving no basis for treating the latter as a reason to overlook the former.

Clothing according to what is proper

The expression ends with:

بِالْمَعْرُوفِ

bi-l-maʿrūf

Al-Baqarah 2:233

“In a recognized, proper manner.”

This qualification concerns how the responsibility for provision and clothing is fulfilled.

Suitable clothing varies with geography and circumstances. Cold and warm climates create different needs; age, physical condition, activities, and social circumstances can also affect what someone needs.

These differences are not part of kiswah's lexical definition; they concern identifying appropriate clothing according to maʿrūf.

The verse also says:

لَا تُكَلَّفُ نَفْسٌ إِلَّا وُسْعَهَا

lā tukallafu nafsun illā wusʿahā

Al-Baqarah 2:233

“No soul is charged beyond its capacity.”

The responsibility should thus be considered alongside propriety and actual capacity.

Providing something unsuitable and worthless does not address the mother's real need; nor should capacity be ignored by imposing an unlimited obligation.

The verse also addresses harm involving either parent because of the child. In this context, clothing forms part of respecting people's rights while a child needs care, rather than being merely a financial transaction.

Kiswah itself should still not be translated as “all living expenses.” The separately named rizq and kiswah should retain their meanings.

Al-Māʾidah 5:89: providing clothes for people in need

Al-Māʾidah says:

فَكَفَّارَتُهُ إِطْعَامُ عَشَرَةِ مَسَاكِينَ مِنْ أَوْسَطِ مَا تُطْعِمُونَ أَهْلِيكُمْ أَوْ كِسْوَتُهُمْ أَوْ تَحْرِيرُ رَقَبَةٍ

fa-kaffāratuhu iṭʿāmu ʿasharati masākīna min awsaṭi mā tuṭʿimūna ahlīkum aw kiswatuhum aw taḥrīru raqabah

Al-Māʾidah 5:89

“Its expiation is feeding ten people in need from the average of what you feed your families, or clothing them, or freeing an enslaved person.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:89

The verse concerns expiation for an oath.

كِسْوَتُهُمْ combines kiswah with hum.

The pronoun refers to عَشَرَةِ مَسَاكِينَ, ten people in need.

Providing clothing appears alongside feeding and freeing an enslaved person.

It is thus one specified form of expiation.

Al-Baqarah 2:233 concerns responsibility for a nursing mother's needs; here clothing is an option for expiating an oath.

The lexical field remains clothing and its provision.

Does kiswah mean any financial assistance?

Kiswah cannot without justification be replaced by financial assistance or charity in general.

Assistance can include food, clothing, housing, and other needs, but this verse specifically names clothing.

أَوْ separates the options; they should not be merged into one action.

Feeding represents one option, while providing clothes represents another.

Conversely, the word itself does not specify a number of garments, fabric type, or fixed clothing design. This expression gives none of those details.

مِنْ أَوْسَطِ مَا تُطْعِمُونَ أَهْلِيكُمْ directly qualifies the feeding option. K S W itself cannot turn that phrase into a fixed numerical rule for clothing.

The wording clearly lists clothing ten people in need among the forms of expiation.

Clothing others can therefore serve a religious and social responsibility, extending beyond an individual's need.

That responsibility comes from the verse's command, not from kasw meaning expiation or worship.

3. Wa-ksūhum: clothing within responsibility for property

Al-Nisāʾ 4:5 says:

وَلَا تُؤْتُوا السُّفَهَاءَ أَمْوَالَكُمُ الَّتِي جَعَلَ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ قِيَامًا وَارْزُقُوهُمْ فِيهَا وَاكْسُوهُمْ وَقُولُوا لَهُمْ قَوْلًا مَعْرُوفًا

wa-lā tuʾtū al-sufahāʾa amwālakumu allatī jaʿala allāhu lakum qiyāman wa-rzuqūhum fīhā wa-ksūhum wa-qūlū lahum qawlan maʿrūfan

Al-Nisāʾ 4:5

“Do not hand over to those lacking sound judgment your property which The God has made a support for you; provide for them from it, clothe them, and speak to them in a recognized, proper manner.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:5

وَاكْسُوهُمْ is a plural imperative of كَسَا يَكْسُو.

اُكْسُوا means “clothe” or “provide clothing”; hum refers to the people under discussion. When beginning with the imperative, its connecting hamzah takes a ḍammah, not a kasrah.

It appears alongside two other commands:

وَارْزُقُوهُمْ فِيهَا: provide for them from it.

وَاكْسُوهُمْ: clothe them.

وَقُولُوا لَهُمْ قَوْلًا مَعْرُوفًا: speak to them in a recognized, proper manner.

The three commands are related but distinct.

Their livelihood must be provided, their clothing supplied, and speech toward them proper.

Managing property should not become deprivation

The verse concerns property management and entrusting property to those unable to administer it soundly.

Direct control may be withheld to prevent loss, but that restriction does not remove responsibility for their needs.

The same sentence commands provision and clothing for them.

Within this context, responsibility for managing property gives no basis for using another's limited ability as a reason to deprive them of necessities.

The social significance is clear: people with limited financial-management ability may be especially vulnerable to exploitation or deprivation.

Vulnerability is not part of kasw's lexical meaning. The root here expresses the duty to provide clothing.

Clothing and human dignity

Placing وَاكْسُوهُمْ between provision and proper speech shows that care extends beyond food.

Someone may have food but lack suitable clothing; the verse names clothing separately.

Someone may also receive food and clothing while suffering humiliation or hurtful speech. The verse joins material provision with proper speech.

Responsible treatment therefore involves both material needs and how people are addressed.

This remains a contextual conclusion. Kasw does not itself mean respect, affection, or dignity; providing clothing is one action placed alongside respectful treatment.

4. Naksūhā laḥman: clothing bones with flesh in Al-Baqarah 2:259

Al-Baqarah 2:259 uses K S W beyond human dress.

In the account of someone passing a ruined settlement and asking about its revival, the Quran says:

وَانْظُرْ إِلَى الْعِظَامِ كَيْفَ نُنْشِزُهَا ثُمَّ نَكْسُوهَا لَحْمًا

wa-nẓur ilā al-ʿiẓāmi kayfa nunshizuhā thumma naksūhā laḥman

Al-Baqarah 2:259

“Look at the bones, how We raise them, then clothe them with flesh.”

Al-Baqarah 2:259

نَكْسُوهَا is a first-person plural imperfect verb from K S W.

نَكْسُو means “We cover” or “We clothe.”

The pronoun hā refers to الْعِظَامِ, the bones.

لَحْمًا identifies the covering material.

The construction resembles كَسَوْتُهُ ثَوْبًا: a person is clothed with a garment in one case, bones with flesh in the other.

The root still concerns covering; the object and material change.

Why this matters for the definition

Defining kasw solely as providing clothes would make its translation here unsuitable.

Bones are not wearing human garments; flesh covers them.

Covering is therefore broader than providing clothing.

Ordinarily, a person clothes another with garments; in the verse, bones are clothed with flesh.

That distinction requires a definition broad enough for both.

Providing clothes is one use of kasw, not its entire meaning.

Nunshizuhā and naksūhā are different verbs

Before نَكْسُوهَا, the verse says:

كَيْفَ نُنْشِزُهَا

kayfa nunshizuhā

Al-Baqarah 2:259

“How We raise them.”

نُنْشِزُهَا belongs to N SH Z and concerns raising or setting upright.

ثُمَّ then introduces نَكْسُوهَا.

Raising the bones and covering them with flesh are distinct actions in this sentence.

The first verb's meaning should not be transferred to K S W. Kasw does not itself mean making bones, raising them, or bringing a being to life.

Revival belongs to the narrative as a whole; this root specifically describes clothing the bones with flesh.

Flesh as covering in an account of renewed creation

The person is raised after a hundred years and encounters signs of divine power.

The bones and their covering with flesh form part of the account of reconstruction and revival.

Covering here does not merely mean hiding something. It describes flesh covering bones in a process connected with creation and life.

Naksūhā should therefore not be translated as “We hide them” or “We make them disappear from view.”

The verb also does not specify the biological mechanism producing flesh or every stage of bodily reconstruction.

Such details do not follow from kasw's lexical meaning.

In the quoted reading, the statement is clear: the bones are raised and clothed with flesh.

5. Fa-kasawnā al-ʿiẓāma laḥman: covering in human creation

Al-Muʾminūn 23:14 uses the same root to describe human creation:

فَخَلَقْنَا الْمُضْغَةَ عِظَامًا فَكَسَوْنَا الْعِظَامَ لَحْمًا ثُمَّ أَنْشَأْنَاهُ خَلْقًا آخَرَ

fa-khalaqnā al-muḍghata ʿiẓāman fa-kasawnā al-ʿiẓāma laḥman thumma anshaʾnāhu khalqan ākhar

Al-Muʾminūn 23:14

“Then We made the lump into bones, then clothed the bones with flesh, then brought him forth as another creation.”

Al-Muʾminūn 23:14

فَكَسَوْنَا combines fa with the perfect verb كَسَوْنَا.

كَسَوْنَا means “We clothed” or “We covered.”

الْعِظَامَ names what is covered, and لَحْمًا the material covering it.

This construction clearly demonstrates the verb's meaning.

Unlike kiswah in Al-Baqarah and Al-Māʾidah, the covering is flesh rather than human clothing.

The shared meaning with Al-Baqarah 2:259

The two verses have an evident verbal parallel.

Al-Baqarah 2:259 says:

ثُمَّ نَكْسُوهَا لَحْمًا

thumma naksūhā laḥman

Al-Baqarah 2:259

“Then We clothe them with flesh.”

Al-Muʾminūn 23:14 says:

فَكَسَوْنَا الْعِظَامَ لَحْمًا

fa-kasawnā al-ʿiẓāma laḥman

Al-Muʾminūn 23:14

“Then We clothed the bones with flesh.”

Both describe bones covered with flesh.

Al-Baqarah places the image within revival and reconstruction; Al-Muʾminūn within human creation.

One recognizable linguistic expression serves two contexts associated with creation and life.

Their contextual distinction must nevertheless remain.

One displays the power to revive after death; the other describes stages of human creation.

A shared verb does not make every detail of the processes identical.

Kasw does not mean creating flesh

كَسَوْنَا states that the bones were covered with flesh.

The verb itself does not mean “We created flesh.”

It occurs among verbs of creation, each with a specific role.

خَلَقْنَا concerns creating.

كَسَوْنَا concerns covering.

أَنْشَأْنَاهُ concerns bringing forth another creation.

Rendering all of them generically as “We made” would lose their linguistic distinctions.

“We clothed” or “We covered” better preserves فَكَسَوْنَا.

Fa also links and orders the statements. Neither it nor kasw alone provides a detailed timetable for the development of bones and bodily tissues.

Turning the account of creation into a precise schedule for every embryonic tissue exceeds the lexical meaning of kasw.

Is flesh pictured as clothing?

The linguistic image suggests a resemblance: as clothes cover the body, flesh covers bones.

This does not make flesh merely an external layer unrelated to bodily structure.

Flesh is part of the living being; kasw foregrounds its covering relationship to bones.

Clothing helps explain the verb but does not define the complete physical nature of flesh and bone.

A root's material image should remain within its semantic scope rather than become a comprehensive theory of the verse's subject.

6. Comparison with neighboring roots

K S W and L B S: clothing someone and wearing clothes

The families overlap in clothing contexts but differ in the roles expressed by the verbs.

Kasā commonly concerns clothing someone or providing clothes for them.

Labisa concerns putting clothes on and wearing them.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:26 says:

يَا بَنِي آدَمَ قَدْ أَنْزَلْنَا عَلَيْكُمْ لِبَاسًا يُوَارِي سَوْآتِكُمْ وَرِيشًا

yā banī ādama qad anzalnā ʿalaykum libāsan yuwārī sawʾātikum wa-rīshan

Al-Aʿrāf 7:26

“Children of Adam, We have sent down to you clothing that covers your private parts, and adornment.”

لِبَاس names the clothing, while يُوَارِي describes its covering function.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:5 instead uses وَاكْسُوهُمْ: “clothe them” or “provide their clothing.”

One passage can emphasize clothing and its function; another the responsibility to provide it for someone else.

This is not an absolute boundary: some Arabic constructions overlap.

The distinction must be read from each verb's actual use in its sentence.

K S W and S T R: covering and concealing

S T R concerns covering and hiding from view.

Kasw often results in coverage, but wearing or providing a covering does not necessarily entail complete concealment.

Clothes may cover some body parts while leaving others visible.

Likewise, فَكَسَوْنَا الْعِظَامَ لَحْمًا foregrounds flesh covering bones, rather than merely their concealment from sight.

Kasw cannot therefore always be translated as hiding.

K S W and GH SH Y: covering and enveloping

GH SH Y also concerns covering and enveloping, describing something spreading over or overtaking another.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:54 says:

يُغْشِي اللَّيْلَ النَّهَارَ يَطْلُبُهُ حَثِيثًا

yughshī al-layla al-nahāra yaṭlubuhu ḥathīthan

Al-Aʿrāf 7:54

“He covers the day with the night, which pursues it swiftly.”

The covering concerns night and its darkness.

Quranic kasw concerns a specified covering applied to someone or something, such as clothes or flesh.

Ghashy can foreground encompassing or spreading over something.

This is a tendency, not a rule absolutely dividing all forms of the two roots.

K S W and R Z Q: clothing and provision

Two verses place kasw alongside provision.

In Al-Baqarah 2:233:

رِزْقُهُنَّ وَكِسْوَتُهُنَّ

rizquhunna wa-kiswatuhunna

Al-Baqarah 2:233

“Their provision and their clothing.”

And in Al-Nisāʾ 4:5:

وَارْزُقُوهُمْ فِيهَا وَاكْسُوهُمْ

wa-rzuqūhum fīhā wa-ksūhum

Al-Nisāʾ 4:5

“Provide for them from it and clothe them.”

The pairing shows the roles of provision and clothing in meeting human needs.

Rizq extends beyond food and can denote various gifts and benefits.

Here the nominal kiswah and verbal uksūhum specifically concern clothing.

Their pairing does not make rizq mean only food everywhere, nor kiswah every livelihood need.

Each contributes its own meaning.

K S W and TH W B: clothing as action and as garment

Thawb names a garment, while kasw can express the action of covering or providing clothing.

In كَسَوْتُهُ ثَوْبًا, kasawtu expresses the action and thawban the garment.

This distinction matters in translation.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:5 uses the imperative وَاكْسُوهُمْ, appropriately rendered “provide them with clothes” or “clothe them.”

Al-Baqarah 2:233 uses the noun كِسْوَتُهُنَّ, more directly “their clothing.”

Both concern clothing, but their nominal and verbal constructions differ.

K S W and R Y SH: clothing and adornment

Al-Aʿrāf 7:26 places لِبَاسًا alongside رِيشًا.

Rīsh here can concern adornment and visible well-being.

Kiswah in the Quran's normative contexts does not itself mean luxurious or ornamental clothes.

Clothes may be beautiful, but providing them for a person in need or a nursing mother does not necessarily mean supplying expensive, ostentatious garments.

Such qualities depend on the verse's criteria and the person's circumstances, not the root alone.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Translation should preserve the distinction between clothing as a noun and covering as an action.

كَسَا, of a person: clothed, gave a garment, or provided clothing.

يَكْسُو: covers or provides clothing, according to its object and context.

كِسْوَة: clothing or clothes.

كِسَاء in classical Arabic: garment, wrap, or covering, as appropriate.

اكْتَسَى: put on clothes, or in other uses became covered.

كَاسٍ: clothed or possessing clothing; in an appropriate construction, one who clothes another.

These equivalents preserve covering without inserting actual garments into the passages about bones and flesh.

Clothing is a natural equivalent of kiswah in Al-Baqarah and Al-Māʾidah. Al-Nisāʾ requires a verbal rendering. In the creation passages, covering with flesh preserves the verb's direct sense.

Kiswah should not without evidence become full maintenance, financial assistance, adornment, or a gift; nor should kasawnā al-ʿiẓāma laḥman become simply “We created flesh.”

8. Summary

K S W concerns covering and providing clothes. In ordinary human usage, someone gives another a garment or clothes them, but the family extends to covering an object with another material.

Classical Arabic records kasā for clothing or providing garments, kiswah for clothing, kisāʾ for a garment or wrap, and iktasā for dressing or becoming covered. Vegetation covering the earth further illustrates the range beyond human dress.

Early Islamic poetic witnesses attest providing clothes. They do not establish precise pre-Islamic dates for every semantic extension. The Quran itself is a contemporary witness to both clothing and bones covered with flesh.

The root occurs five times in the Quran.

Al-Baqarah 2:233 makes clothing part of provision for a nursing mother. Al-Nisāʾ 4:5 places it alongside sustenance and proper speech. Al-Māʾidah 5:89 lists clothing people in need among the options for expiating an oath.

These passages attend to a real human need. Clothing is not merely optional luxury; in specified circumstances, responsibility for providing it rests with others.

Al-Baqarah 2:259 and Al-Muʾminūn 23:14 describe bones covered with flesh, showing that the root extends beyond fabric and clothes.

Covering, providing clothing, becoming covered, and the result of coverage are related but distinct. Kasw expresses covering or supplying a covering; its material, function, and associated moral or social responsibility come from the rest of the verse.

The root K S W means covering a person or thing with another covering; for people, giving garments or providing clothing.

Covering is the broader meaning, and providing clothes a principal branch.

The distinction explains both social responsibilities and creation passages without restricting the root to human clothing or expanding it into absolute protection, complete concealment, or creation of every bodily component.

A Quranic root can address everyday needs and an aspect of creation while retaining its basic meaning. K S W illustrates this continuity: from clothing provided for another person to flesh covering bones.
