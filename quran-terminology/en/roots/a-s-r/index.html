The Qur’anic Root أ س ر

From binding and taking into custody to captives and bodily cohesion

The root أ س ر has a compact but important semantic range in the Qur’an. Most occurrences concern taking people captive and holding them in one’s power. In one verse, however, أَسْر refers to the constitution and cohesion of the human body. This use prevents us from reducing the root to imprisonment or captivity in war alone.

In Arabic, the concrete image behind this family involves binding, securing, and holding. A strap used to bind something was called إِسَار, and a person seized and put in bonds was called أَسِير. The latter use became established enough for a captured person to be called a captive even when not physically bound at that moment.

The Qur’an uses the verb تَأْسِرُونَ, the noun أَسْر, the singular أَسِير, and the plurals أَسْرَى and أُسَارَى. The root occurs six times: once as a verb, once as أَسْر, and four times as singular or plural forms of أَسِير.

Its semantic core can be expressed as follows:

أ س ر: taking and holding firmly through binding and connection. Taking a person and restricting their freedom gives rise to captivity; asr can also express the firmness and cohesion of a living being’s constitution.

Qur’anic forms and constructions

تَأْسِرُونَ
Form I verb; once, in 33:26

أَسِيرًا
Singular; once, in 76:8

أَسْرَى / أُسَارَى
Plurals of asīr; three occurrences

أَسْرَهُمْ
Noun with a pronoun; once, in 76:28

Binding: the concrete image in early Arabic

Classical Arabic dictionaries record أَسَرَ الشيءَ for firmly binding something. For example, أَسَرَ قَتَبَهُ describes securing a pack saddle with a leather thong; the thong itself was called إِسَار.

The same image is recorded in explanations of أَسِير. A person captured in war was bound with a thong and thus called asīr. Usage then extended to anyone held by an enemy, even if not actually bound with such a strap.

This connection is recorded in the vocabulary itself: إسار، أسر، أسير preserve the relationship between binding and taking captive. The dictionaries transmitting these explanations are later than the Qur’an; their entries should not themselves be treated as independently dated pre-Qur’anic documents.

Poetry attributed to pre-Islamic poets also provides evidence, though the reliability of each attribution needs separate consideration. Al-Shimshāṭī’s later compilation, Al-Anwār wa-Maḥāsin al-Ashʿār, attributes the following lines about Ḥudhayfah ibn Badr to ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm:

وَإِنِّي بِالذَّنَائِبِ يَوْمَ خَوٍّ
مَنَنْتُ عَلَى حُذَيْفَةَ بَعْدَ أَسْرِ
وَلَوْ غَيْرِي يَجِيءُ بِهِ أَسِيرًا
لَنَالَ بِهِ رَغِيبَةَ ذُخْرِ دَهْرِ

“At al-Dhanāʾib, on the day of Khaww, I spared Ḥudhayfah after taking him captive. Had someone else brought him in as a captive, he would have gained through him a coveted store of wealth for years to come.”

In the narrative accompanying these verses, Ḥudhayfah is captured in battle and offers a ransom for his freedom. The editor notes that these lines are absent from the edition of the poet’s collected poems consulted there. This is therefore evidence transmitted under a pre-Islamic poet’s name, rather than conclusive proof of the episode or the poem’s date. Within the transmitted account, أسر and أسير clearly concern capturing an enemy and depriving him of freedom of action.

The following expression is also transmitted from al-Aʿshā:

وَقَيَّدَنِي الشِّعْرُ فِي بَيْتِهِ
كَمَا قَيَّدَ الْآسِرَاتُ الْحِمَارَا

“Poetry bound me in its house, as those who bind restrain the donkey.”

The use is figurative, but the underlying image remains binding and restraint. Ibn Fāris cites the line in his discussion of this root; it is also transmitted in al-Ḥātimī’s Ḥilyat al-Muḥāḍarah.

These transmitted poetic examples support both binding and captivity as early uses of the family. Their preservation in later works, however, must be distinguished from independent verification of the date and circumstances of every verse.

“And you took a group captive”: the act of capture

The only verbal occurrence of this root in the Qur’an is in Al-Ahzab:

وَأَنْزَلَ الَّذِينَ ظَاهَرُوهُمْ مِنْ أَهْلِ الْكِتَابِ مِنْ صَيَاصِيهِمْ وَقَذَفَ فِي قُلُوبِهِمُ الرُّعْبَ فَرِيقًا تَقْتُلُونَ وَتَأْسِرُونَ فَرِيقًا

“He brought down from their fortresses those People of the Book who had supported them, and cast terror into their hearts: a group you were killing, and a group you were taking captive.”

Al-Ahzab 33:26

تَأْسِرُونَ stands opposite تَقْتُلُونَ. Some are killed; others are taken alive and brought under another party’s control.

Here, asr does not simply mean tying someone with a rope. Binding is the root’s underlying image, but the verb has acquired the broader sense of taking a person captive.

This distinction matters: a root can retain its original image while a word’s established use extends beyond the particular instrument involved.

أَسْرَى and أُسَارَى: two plurals of “captive”

The Qur’an uses two plurals of أَسِير: أَسْرَى and أُسَارَى.

Al-Baqarah says:

ثُمَّ أَنْتُمْ هَٰؤُلَاءِ تَقْتُلُونَ أَنْفُسَكُمْ وَتُخْرِجُونَ فَرِيقًا مِنْكُمْ مِنْ دِيَارِهِمْ تَظَاهَرُونَ عَلَيْهِمْ بِالْإِثْمِ وَالْعُدْوَانِ وَإِنْ يَأْتُوكُمْ أُسَارَىٰ تُفَادُوهُمْ وَهُوَ مُحَرَّمٌ عَلَيْكُمْ إِخْرَاجُهُمْ ۚ أَفَتُؤْمِنُونَ بِبَعْضِ الْكِتَابِ وَتَكْفُرُونَ بِبَعْضٍ ۚ فَمَا جَزَاءُ مَنْ يَفْعَلُ ذَٰلِكَ مِنْكُمْ إِلَّا خِزْيٌ فِي الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا وَيَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ يُرَدُّونَ إِلَىٰ أَشَدِّ الْعَذَابِ ۗ وَمَا اللَّهُ بِغَافِلٍ عَمَّا تَعْمَلُونَ

“Yet here you are, killing one another and expelling a group of your own people from their homes, supporting one another against them in sin and aggression. But if they come to you as captives, you ransom them, although their expulsion was forbidden to you. Do you then believe in part of the Book and reject part? What recompense is there for those among you who do this except disgrace in worldly life? On the Day of Resurrection they will be returned to the severest punishment. The God is not unaware of what you do.”

Al-Baqarah 2:85

أُسَارَىٰ here describes people held by the opposing party, whose release is secured through ransom. Captivity thus has a social and legal dimension: a person has been deprived of freedom, and that freedom can become the subject of a ransom.

The other plural appears in Al-Anfal:

مَا كَانَ لِنَبِيٍّ أَنْ يَكُونَ لَهُ أَسْرَىٰ حَتَّىٰ يُثْخِنَ فِي الْأَرْضِ ۚ تُرِيدُونَ عَرَضَ الدُّنْيَا وَاللَّهُ يُرِيدُ الْآخِرَةَ ۗ وَاللَّهُ عَزِيزٌ حَكِيمٌ

“It is not for a prophet to have captives until he has thoroughly subdued the enemy in battle in the land. You desire the passing goods of this world, while The God desires the Hereafter; The God is mighty and wise.”

Al-Anfal 8:67

A few verses later:

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّبِيُّ قُلْ لِمَنْ فِي أَيْدِيكُمْ مِنَ الْأَسْرَىٰ إِنْ يَعْلَمِ اللَّهُ فِي قُلُوبِكُمْ خَيْرًا يُؤْتِكُمْ خَيْرًا مِمَّا أُخِذَ مِنْكُمْ وَيَغْفِرْ لَكُمْ ۗ وَاللَّهُ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ

“O Prophet, say to the captives in your hands: If The God knows any good in your hearts, He will give you better than what was taken from you and will forgive you. The God is forgiving and merciful.”

Al-Anfal 8:70

In this verse, the expression

فِي أَيْدِيكُمْ مِنَ الْأَسْرَىٰ

describes the captive’s situation in very concrete terms: captives are “in the hands” of the opposing party.

Lexical sources transmit differing accounts of the distinction between أسرى and أسارى. Some distinguish a bound captive from one not yet firmly bound. This distinction is not consistently maintained, however, and other sources identify both as plurals of asīr.

The Qur’an itself does not supply enough evidence for a fixed technical distinction between the two plurals. أسارى in Al-Baqarah and أسرى in Al-Anfal both designate people held by another party. It is therefore sounder to treat both as plurals of “captive,” without importing a proposed finer distinction into the translation unless independent evidence supports it.

“And a captive”: captivity does not remove human need

The only singular occurrence of أَسِير is in Al-Insan:

وَيُطْعِمُونَ الطَّعَامَ عَلَىٰ حُبِّهِ مِسْكِينًا وَيَتِيمًا وَأَسِيرًا

“They give food, despite their love for it, to a needy person, an orphan, and a captive.”

Al-Insan 76:8

The captive appears alongside the needy person and the orphan, as someone to be fed.

The root itself identifies only the person’s situation: someone has been seized and deprived of freedom. The verse does not specify their ethnicity, religion, reason for capture, or previous moral standing.

Accordingly, أسير should not be narrowed in translation to “a Muslim captive,” “a convicted prisoner,” or a similarly restricted category. The word itself simply means a captive.

Including the captive among those who receive food also matters within the Qur’an’s pattern of usage: taking someone in war or holding them in one’s power does not erase their humanity or their needs.

This conclusion comes from the verse’s context, not from the lexical meaning of asīr alone.

“We strengthened their asr”: from external bonds to bodily cohesion

A different and particularly important occurrence appears in the same surah:

نَحْنُ خَلَقْنَاهُمْ وَشَدَدْنَا أَسْرَهُمْ ۖ وَإِذَا شِئْنَا بَدَّلْنَا أَمْثَالَهُمْ تَبْدِيلًا

“We created them and made their bodily frame and bonds firm; whenever We will, We replace them with others like them.”

Al-Insan 76:28

Here أَسْرَهُمْ cannot mean “their captivity.” The sentence concerns human creation:

خَلَقْنَاهُمْ

and then:

شَدَدْنَا أَسْرَهُمْ

The verb شَدَدْنَا itself means making firm. Asr here therefore concerns the firmness, connection, and cohesion of the human constitution.

Arabic records شديد الأسر for a person or animal whose limbs and joints are firmly knit together. Lexical sources explain asr in this use in terms of constitution, the connection of joints, and firmness of bodily structure.

A line attributed to Labīd uses this expression of a horse:

سَاهِمُ الْوَجْهِ شَدِيدٌ أَسْرُهُ
مُشْرِفُ الْحَارِكِ مَحْبُوكُ الْكَتِدِ

“Lean-faced, firmly built; high at the withers and tightly knit across the shoulders.”

Labīd lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. The date of each of his verses requires separate investigation, so this example should not be called definitively pre-Islamic without evidence. Its value here is to illustrate the early use of شديد الأسر for a firm bodily constitution.

Nor does the Qur’anic verse require us to restrict asr to one particular component, such as joints, nerves, or a bodily passage. These are explanations offered for how the body is held together. The expression itself is broader: The God created them and made their asr firm.

“Bodily frame and cohesion” is more natural here than restricting the expression to one particular part.

Are captivity and bodily asr unrelated meanings?

At first sight, taking a human being captive and the firmness of bodily constitution seem far apart. Yet the concrete image of binding and joining supplies a defensible connection.

In captivity, a person is seized and their freedom of movement restricted; in the early concrete image, they may be bound with an إسار.

In شددنا أسرهم, the subject is a set of components firmly bound and joined together, giving the human body its stability.

The shared element is therefore better understood as binding, holding, and forming a firm connection, rather than imprisonment alone.

This connection should not, however, become a claim of certain historical reconstruction. The two branches are intelligible in Arabic through the image of fastening and joining; that does not establish precisely when or how each meaning arose.

A definition of the root should accommodate both branches without forcing either:

In one branch, asr holds a person under another’s control and under restraint; in the other, it holds the components of a bodily structure firmly together.

أ س ر and و ث ق: taking captive and securing bonds

In its account of battle, Muhammad says:

فَإِذَا لَقِيتُمُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا فَضَرْبَ الرِّقَابِ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا أَثْخَنْتُمُوهُمْ فَشُدُّوا الْوَثَاقَ فَإِمَّا مَنًّا بَعْدُ وَإِمَّا فِدَاءً حَتَّىٰ تَضَعَ الْحَرْبُ أَوْزَارَهَا

“When you encounter those who have disbelieved, strike their necks until you have thoroughly subdued them; then secure the bonds. Thereafter, either release them freely or for ransom, until the war lays down its burdens.”

Muhammad 47:4, excerpt

The root أ س ر does not occur in this passage, but شُدُّوا الْوَثَاقَ describes securing the bonds of those taken in battle.

This helps distinguish the two semantic fields. Asr can express the act of capturing a person and placing them in captivity; وثاق emphasizes the bond and its secure fastening.

The fields overlap, but they are not fully synonymous. Someone can be called asīr even when the word is no longer restricted to physical binding at that moment.

The concrete image of a bond is therefore important for understanding the history of asīr, but “a bound person” cannot replace “a captive” in every verse.

Captivity, ransom, and freedom: what the root itself does not mean

Several Qur’anic verses associate captives with ransom or release. These rulings and actions should not be incorporated into the root’s lexical meaning.

Al-Baqarah 2:85 uses تفادوهم. Muhammad 47:4 mentions مَنًّا and فداءً after securing the bonds. Al-Anfal 8:70 also refers to what has been taken from the captives.

These belong to the legal and ethical setting of captivity, but أ س ر does not itself mean taking a ransom, releasing someone, or killing.

In this branch, the root describes taking and holding a person. What subsequently happens to the captive is conveyed by other verbs and rulings.

This distinction prevents military history, the jurisprudence of captives, or an entire theory of the laws of war from being inserted into a lexical definition.

Translation proposals for Forqan

In وَتَأْسِرُونَ فَرِيقًا, “and you were taking a group captive” is natural English. “You were binding” retains the root’s image but narrows the verb’s established meaning in a setting of war.

أَسْرَى، الْأَسْرَى and أُسَارَى can all be translated as “captives” in their Qur’anic occurrences. Different plural forms need not become a semantic distinction that the Qur’an itself does not establish.

For أَسِيرًا, “a captive” is likewise accurate and natural.

But وَشَدَدْنَا أَسْرَهُمْ should not become “We strengthened their captivity.” “We made their bodily frame firm,” “We strengthened their bodily bonds,” or “We made their bodily frame and bonds firm” fits the context of creation more closely.

For Forqan, “We made their bodily frame and bonds firm” can preserve the root’s image without restricting the verse to a single part of the body.

Summary

The Qur’anic root أ س ر concerns taking, binding, and holding firmly.

Classical dictionaries and poetry attributed to pre-Islamic poets preserve this image. An isār was a strap used to secure things; a person captured in war was called asīr. In the poem transmitted under ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm’s name, أسر and أسير concern battle, the capture of Ḥudhayfah, and the possibility of ransom, subject to the attribution cautions discussed above. Al-Aʿshā also uses a member of this family in an image of binding.

In وَتَأْسِرُونَ فَرِيقًا, the Qur’an expresses the act of taking captive. أسرى، أسارى and the singular أسير designate people held by another party and deprived of freedom of action. Asrā and usārā are two plurals of asīr; lexical disagreements about their precise distinction do not justify two separate Qur’anic definitions.

But وَشَدَدْنَا أَسْرَهُمْ reveals another branch of the same family. Asr here concerns the firmness and cohesion of human constitution. Early expressions such as شديد الأسر, used of a firmly built person or animal, support this use.

The root’s core therefore cannot be reduced to captivity alone. Captivity is one development of the underlying sense of binding and holding.

أ س ر: taking and holding firmly through binding and connection. From this core, taking a person and restricting their freedom gives rise to captivity; asr can also express the firmness and cohesion of a living being’s constitution. The root itself does not specify the kind of bond, the captive’s fate, or the details of bodily structure: each must be understood from its own context.
