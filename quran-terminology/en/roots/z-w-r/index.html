Core meaning: inclining, turning away from a direction or course, and turning toward another; in distinct extended uses, visiting someone and, in speech, departing from truth and expressing falsehood.

1. The root in early Arabic

The Arabic root ز و ر concerns inclination, a change of direction, and departure from a course. These meanings appear in bodily or spatial deviation, turning toward someone for a visit, and speech departing from truth.

In physical usage, زَوَر denotes crookedness or inclination. أَزْوَر describes something inclined away from a straight or balanced position; زَوْرَاء is its feminine form.

In the visiting branch, زَارَ فُلَانًا means visiting someone. A person goes from their own location or route toward another and meets them.

In speech, زُور denotes falsehood, lies, or untruth: speech that departs from reality.

One early lexical analysis describes the underlying meaning as follows:

الزَّاءُ وَالْوَاوُ وَالرَّاءُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى الْمَيْلِ وَالْعُدُولِ

al-zāʾu wa-l-wāwu wa-l-rāʾu aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā al-mayli wa-l-ʿudūl

Meaning: “Zāy, wāw, and rāʾ have one underlying principle, indicating inclination and departure.”

This definition explains a semantic connection between inclination, visiting, and false speech.

A plausible account of derivation must nevertheless be distinguished from proof of the actual history of words. Grouping these uses under inclination and departure does not establish that they arose together or developed from one another in a particular sequence.

Independent evidence from the period matters when studying Arabic before the Qur’an.

First example: inclination in the poetry of ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād

The pre-Islamic poet ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād describes his mount:

شَرِبَتْ بِمَاءِ الدُّحْرُضَيْنِ فَأَصْبَحَتْ
زَوْرَاءَ تَنْفِرُ عَنْ حِيَاضِ الدَّيْلَمِ

sharibat bi-māʾi al-duḥruḍayni fa-aṣbaḥat / zawrāʾa tanfiru ʿan ḥiyāḍi al-daylam

Translation:

“She drank at al-Duḥruḍayn and then turned aside, shunning the watering pools of al-Daylam.”

Al-Duḥruḍayn refers here to known watering places.

زَوْرَاء describes the poet’s mount and concerns inclination or turning aside.

Early explanations gloss زَوْرَاء as مائلة: inclined or turned aside.

Some explanations relate this to the posture of the animal’s body or neck, and others to its turning away from other watering places.

These differences of detail leave the central sense intact: some inclination away from a direction or straight position.

The example attests physical usage in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period; the meaning is not merely inferred from the Qur’anic تزاور.

Second example: visiting in poetry attributed to ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAjlān al-Nahdī

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAjlān al-Nahdī says of Hind:

خَلِيلَيَّ زُورَا قَبْلَ شَحْطِ النَّوَى هِنْدًا
وَلَا تَأْمَنَا مِنْ دَارِ ذِي لَطَفٍ بُعْدَا

khalīlayya zūrā qabla shaḥṭi al-nawā hindan / wa-lā taʾmanā min dāri dhī laṭafin buʿdan

Translation:

“My two companions, visit Hind before separation carries us far away; do not feel secure against distance from the dwelling of one dear to you.”

It continues:

وَمُرَّا عَلَيْهَا بَارَكَ اللَّهُ فِيكُمَا
وَإِنْ لَمْ تَكُنْ هِنْدُ لِوَجْهَيْكُمَا قَصْدَا

wa-murrā ʿalayhā bāraka Allāhu fīkumā / wa-in lam takun hindu li-wajhaykumā qaṣdan

Translation:

“And pass by her—may The God bless you both—even if Hind is not the destination of your journey.”

زُورَا is a dual imperative: “You two, visit.”

The person to be visited is Hind.

The poet asks his friends to visit her before separation and distance increase.

Visiting here means going to a person and meeting her.

The visit does not concern a religious ceremony, cemetery, worship, or sacred site.

The example therefore illustrates visiting in its general sense in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period, rather than an exclusively ritual act.

The poem is transmitted among verses attributed to ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAjlān and in early literary accounts. The poet belonged to the pre-Islamic period, but the wording and attribution of each transmitted verse require assessment separately from his lifetime.

Pre-Qur’anic meaning and the limits of the evidence

These examples illustrate two uses in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period:

First, inclination and deviation from a direction.

Second, turning toward someone to visit them.

Both involve a connection with direction and movement.

In the first, something inclines away from a straight position or course.

In the second, someone leaves a location or route to meet another person.

The meaning of زُور as false speech is clear in the Qur’an and early lexicons. The examples examined in this article, however, do not independently establish its pre-Islamic history to the same degree.

A general lexical definition alone cannot establish that every verbal use already circulated before Islam with precisely the same range.

These poetic examples illustrate inclination and changing direction, together with visiting another person. Their semantic evidence should be distinguished from the certainty of dating each transmitted line.

The speech-related زُور clearly denotes untruth in Qur’anic usage and can plausibly be connected with inclination or departure.

2. Important forms and derivatives

زَارَ يَزُورُ

This basic triliteral verb means visiting someone and meeting them.

زَارَ فُلَانًا

zāra fulānan

Meaning: “He visited someone.”

زَارَ أَهْلَهُ

zāra ahlahu

Meaning: “He visited his family.”

The verb itself specifies neither the visit’s duration nor its purpose or accompanying rituals.

A visit may be brief or lengthy, for an inquiry after someone’s welfare or another purpose, and take place in different locations.

The Qur’an uses this verb in زُرْتُمُ الْمَقَابِرَ.

زِيَارَة and زَوْر

زِيَارَة is a familiar verbal noun of زار, meaning visiting another.

زَوْر, with a fatḥah on zāy and a vowelless wāw, is also recorded for visiting.

In some Arabic uses this form also denotes a visitor or a group of visitors.

Its vocalization and meaning differ from زُور, falsehood.

زَائِر

The active participle of زار: someone who visits another.

زَائِرُ الْبَيْتِ

zāʾiru al-bayt

Meaning: “A visitor to the house.”

زَائِرُ الْمَرِيضِ

zāʾiru al-marīḍ

Meaning: “Someone visiting a sick person.”

The purposes of these visits may differ while the basic meaning remains.

زُوَّار and زُوَّر

These are recorded plurals of زائر.

They denote people visiting a person or place.

زُوَّارُ الْبَيْتِ

zuwwāru al-bayt

Meaning: “Visitors to the house.”

These forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

زَوْرَة

In the visiting branch, this denotes one visit.

زَوْرَةٌ وَاحِدَةٌ

zawratun wāḥidah

Meaning: “A single visit.”

A related form is also recorded for distance and deviation. Vocalization and context therefore matter in selecting the sense.

مَزَار

This can denote a place of visiting or the act of visiting itself.

مَزَارُ فُلَانٍ

mazāru fulān

It may refer to the place where someone is met or where people go to visit.

In later religious and social usage, مزار also denotes tombs that people visit.

This specialized use does not exhaust the word’s lexical range or establish its earliest meaning.

أَزَارَ يُزِيرُ

This is recorded for causing a visit or sending someone to visit another.

أَزَارَهُ فُلَانًا

azārahu fulānan

Meaning: “He sent him to visit someone, or brought about their meeting.”

The person arranging or causing the visit is distinct from the visitor.

اِزْدَارَ

This is recorded with the meaning “visited someone.”

It belongs to Form VIII and involves a sound change in its formation.

It should not be confused with اِزْدَرَى, from ز ر ي, meaning to despise.

اِسْتَزَارَ

Meaning: “He asked someone to visit him.”

اِسْتَزَارَهُ

istazārahu

This expresses a request for another person’s visit.

The verb does not occur in the Qur’an.

زَوَر

With a fatḥah on both zāy and wāw, this denotes inclination, deviation, and crookedness.

In bodily descriptions it can refer to asymmetry or inclination in part of the chest.

زَوِرَ صَدْرُهُ

zawira ṣadruhu

Meaning: “His chest became crooked or asymmetrical.”

This belongs to the physical branch of the root.

أَزْوَر and زَوْرَاء

أَزْوَر is masculine and زَوْرَاء its feminine counterpart.

They describe something crooked or inclined.

عُنُقٌ أَزْوَرُ

ʿunuqun azwar

Meaning: “An inclined or crooked neck.”

رَكِيَّةٌ زَوْرَاءُ

rakiyyatun zawrāʾ

Meaning: “A well not dug straight.”

This is the branch also present in ʿAntarah’s verse.

اِزْوَرَّ and اِزْوِرَار

اِزْوَرَّ عَنْهُ means turning away from something or deviating from its course.

اِزْوِرَار is its verbal noun: inclination, deviation, or turning away.

The preposition عن identifies that from which something turns away.

تَزَاوَرَ

This verb has two distinct uses.

First:

تَزَاوَرَ الْقَوْمُ

tazāwara al-qawm

Meaning: “The people visited one another.”

Mutual visiting is expressed here.

Second:

تَزَاوَرَ عَنِ الشَّيْءِ

tazāwara ʿani al-shayʾ

Meaning: “It inclined away from something or turned aside.”

The verb here concerns deviation in direction and turning aside.

The Qur’anic تَزَاوَرُ has this second meaning.

Form VI alone does not establish reciprocal visiting in every use.

زُور

With a ḍammah on zāy, this denotes false speech, lies, and untruth.

قَوْلُ الزُّورِ

qawlu al-zūr

Meaning: “False or untrue speech.”

شَهَادَةُ الزُّورِ

shahādatu al-zūr

Meaning: “False testimony.”

This sense occurs four times in the Qur’an.

زَوَّرَ يُزَوِّرُ and تَزْوِير

One recorded use of زَوَّرَ is preparing or arranging something, especially speech.

زَوَّرَ الْكَلَامَ

zawwara al-kalām

This can mean arranging, preparing, or polishing words before speaking.

Another use is fabricating false speech, distorting something, or presenting it contrary to reality.

From this branch, modern Persian and Arabic use تزوير for document forgery, falsifying signatures, and fabricating evidence.

This later administrative and legal sense should not be projected onto every early use.

Arranging words is not intrinsically lying. Falsehood arises when speech departs from reality or is deliberately constructed to deceive.

زَوْر

Another Arabic use of زَوْر denotes the chest or its upper part.

In descriptions of horses it can refer to the chest and surrounding bones.

Lexical explanations connect this use with a crooked chest, but this does not establish that visiting and falsehood both developed from the name of that body part.

Of these forms, the Qur’an uses only تَزَاوَرُ, زُرْتُمُ, and the noun زُور.

3. Inclination of the sun in the account of the cave

Al-Kahf 18:17

وَتَرَى الشَّمْسَ إِذَا طَلَعَت تَزَاوَرُ عَن كَهْفِهِمْ ذَاتَ الْيَمِينِ وَإِذَا غَرَبَت تَّقْرِضُهُمْ ذَاتَ الشِّمَالِ وَهُمْ فِي فَجْوَةٍ مِّنْهُ ذَٰلِكَ مِنْ آيَاتِ اللَّهِ مَن يَهْدِ اللَّهُ فَهُوَ الْمُهْتَدِ وَمَن يُضْلِلْ فَلَن تَجِدَ لَهُ وَلِيًّا مُّرْشِدًا

wa-tarā al-shamsa idhā ṭalaʿat tazāwaru ʿan kahfihim dhāta al-yamīni wa-idhā gharabat taqriḍuhum dhāta al-shimāli wa-hum fī fajwatin minhu dhālika min āyāti Allāhi man yahdi Allāhu fa-huwa al-muhtadi wa-man yuḍlil fa-lan tajida lahu waliyyan murshidan

Al-Kahf 18:17

Translation:

“You see the sun, when it rises, inclining away from their cave to the right, and when it sets, passing them on the left, while they are in an open space within it. That is among the signs of The God. Whoever The God guides is rightly guided; whoever He leaves astray, you will find no guiding protector for him.”

تَزَاوَرُ is a present-tense verb from ز و ر.

In the reading of Ḥafṣ, the ungeminated تَزَاوَرُ is explained by deleting one of the two tāʾs of تَتَزَاوَرُ. In the variant with a doubled zāy, the tāʾ is assimilated into the zāy. These are distinct morphological explanations.

Other transmitted readings also express inclination and turning aside.

The subject is the sun.

عَنْ كَهْفِهِمْ identifies that in relation to which the inclination occurs.

ذَاتَ الْيَمِينِ specifies its direction.

A suitable rendering is therefore:

“When the sun rises, it inclines away from their cave to the right.”

Here تزاور does not mean visiting the cave.

The preposition عن and the direction described make this clear.

The spatial picture

The next part says:

وَإِذَا غَرَبَت تَّقْرِضُهُمْ ذَاتَ الشِّمَالِ

wa-idhā gharabat taqriḍuhum dhāta al-shimāl

Al-Kahf 18:17

Meaning: “And when it sets, it passes them on the left.”

تَقْرِضُهُمْ comes from another root and here concerns passing by or to one side.

The verse describes the sun at sunrise and sunset as seen in different directions relative to the cave and its occupants.

It continues:

وَهُمْ فِي فَجْوَةٍ مِّنْهُ

wa-hum fī fajwatin minhu

Al-Kahf 18:17

Meaning: “While they are in an open space within it.”

Together these details describe their location in the cave and its relationship with sunlight.

تزاور alone identifies neither the country where the cave stood nor the exact geographical orientation of its entrance.

Such conclusions require independent geographical and historical evidence.

The verse describes the sun’s apparent movement relative to the observer and cave. The verb alone does not yield an independent astronomical theory about the motion of the earth or sun.

Inclination and visiting

In Arabic, تزاوروا can mean visiting one another.

In Al-Kahf 18:17, however, تزاور عن expresses inclination away from something.

Both belong to one lexical family, but sentence structure distinguishes their meanings.

In visiting, someone turns toward another.

In the cave account, the sun inclines to one side relative to the cave.

Thus “visit” cannot translate every form of this root.

4. Zūr: false speech and departure from truth

In its speech-related branch, زُور denotes lies and falsehood.

The connection with inclination is intelligible: words that fail to represent reality depart from it.

This does not make every change of direction or difference of opinion زُور.

Someone may understand an issue differently without deliberately distorting the truth.

Someone else may fabricate a statement, reverse the facts, or make a false attribution.

Such speech belongs to the semantic field of زُور.

The Qur’an uses the noun in four contexts.

Al-Ḥajj 22:30: avoiding false speech

Al-Ḥajj 22:30 says:

فَاجْتَنِبُوا الرِّجْسَ مِنَ الْأَوْثَانِ وَاجْتَنِبُوا قَوْلَ الزُّورِ

fa-jtanibū al-rijsa mina al-awthāni wa-jtanibū qawla al-zūr

Al-Ḥajj 22:30

Translation:

“So avoid the defilement of idols, and avoid false speech.”

الزُّورِ is the genitive complement of قَوْلَ.

The expression means speech characterized by falsehood: words departing from truth.

The context concerns sacred obligations and the rites of pilgrimage.

The juxtaposition of avoiding idols and avoiding false speech indicates that worship and religious rites should not be accompanied by untruths or groundless attributions.

Lexically, however, قول زور is not confined to one kind of religious speech.

Lies, false testimony, groundless claims about The God, and distortions of reality may fall within its scope according to the subject.

Nor should the expression be made the name of a particular entertainment, musical genre, or ritual without independent evidence.

The explicit word قول, speech, must remain visible in translation.

Al-Furqān 25:4: a false allegation about the Qur’an

وَقَالَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا إِنْ هَٰذَا إِلَّا إِفْكٌ افْتَرَاهُ وَأَعَانَهُ عَلَيْهِ قَوْمٌ آخَرُونَ فَقَدْ جَاءُوا ظُلْمًا وَزُورًا

wa-qāla alladhīna kafarū in hādhā illā ifkun iftarāhu wa-aʿānahu ʿalayhi qawmun ākharūna fa-qad jāʾū ẓulman wa-zūran

Al-Furqān 25:4

Translation:

“Those who disbelieved said: ‘This is only a falsehood he invented, and other people helped him with it.’ They have indeed brought injustice and falsehood.”

The Prophet’s opponents claim that he fabricated the Qur’an with others’ assistance.

The Qur’an characterizes their allegation as ظُلْمًا وَزُورًا.

ظلم and زور are distinct words.

ظلم concerns injustice, putting something in the wrong place, or violating a right.

زور emphasizes the statement’s falsity and departure from reality.

The allegation is therefore characterized as both false and unjust, beyond a mere linguistic mistake.

The root زور does not itself mean injustice.

That element is expressed separately by ظلم.

إِفْكٌ and افْتَرَاهُ occur in the opponents’ words: they call the Qur’an an invented lie.

The concluding statement then identifies this very allegation as false.

The structure distinguishes the opponents’ claim from its actual truth.

Al-Furqān 25:72: not bearing false witness

وَالَّذِينَ لَا يَشْهَدُونَ الزُّورَ وَإِذَا مَرُّوا بِاللَّغْوِ مَرُّوا كِرَامًا

wa-alladhīna lā yashhadūna al-zūra wa-idhā marrū bi-l-laghwi marrū kirāman

Al-Furqān 25:72

Translation:

“And those who do not bear false witness, and when they pass by idle speech, pass with dignity.”

الزُّورَ is the object of يَشْهَدُونَ.

شهد concerns presence and observation as well as bearing witness.

Accordingly, two related readings of لَا يَشْهَدُونَ الزُّورَ are discussed.

First, they do not give false testimony or distort truth in their witness.

Second, they do not participate in falsehood or gatherings characterized by it.

The first accords with the familiar sense of testifying; “do not bear false witness” is a clear basic translation.

The second draws on the sense of attending or witnessing, especially alongside the following description of passing by idle speech with dignity.

Neither reading should be imposed on the other without evidence.

The wording alone does not equate every presence at a gathering where wrongdoing occurs with giving false testimony.

Nor should زور be reduced to all idle talk.

لَغْو occurs separately in the same verse.

لغو can denote pointless or unprofitable speech, whereas زور concerns falsity.

Words may be pointless without being false.

Conversely, a statement may be very serious and socially consequential yet false.

The words therefore have distinct semantic fields.

Al-Mujādilah 58:2: false attribution in family relations

الَّذِينَ يُظَاهِرُونَ مِنكُم مِّن نِّسَائِهِم مَّا هُنَّ أُمَّهَاتِهِمْ إِنْ أُمَّهَاتُهُمْ إِلَّا اللَّائِي وَلَدْنَهُمْ وَإِنَّهُمْ لَيَقُولُونَ مُنكَرًا مِّنَ الْقَوْلِ وَزُورًا وَإِنَّ اللَّهَ لَعَفُوٌّ غَفُورٌ

alladhīna yuẓāhirūna minkum min nisāʾihim mā hunna ummahātihim in ummahātuhum illā al-lāʾī waladnahum wa-innahum la-yaqūlūna munkaran mina al-qawli wa-zūran wa-inna Allāha la-ʿafuwwun ghafūr

Al-Mujādilah 58:2

Translation:

“Those among you who pronounce ẓihār concerning their wives—their wives are not their mothers. Their mothers are only those who gave birth to them. They certainly utter objectionable speech and falsehood. The God is indeed Pardoning, Forgiving.”

زُورًا identifies the speech as false.

The subject is ẓihār: a pronouncement likening a wife to one’s mother in order to assert a prohibition of marital relations.

The Qur’an explicitly says that their wives are not their mothers.

It then calls this مُنْكَرًا مِّنَ الْقَوْلِ وَزُورًا.

Here زور concerns stating an unreal relationship.

A man cannot turn the marital relationship into motherhood by saying so.

This matters because false speech is not always merely a report about the past.

A statement may characterize a legal or family relationship in a way inconsistent with reality.

The verse rejects that attribution, with its consequences discussed in subsequent verses.

زور does not itself mean ẓihār. The latter is the practice under discussion, while زور characterizes the speech as false.

The attribution’s falsity does not alone establish the knowledge and intention of every person who uttered it.

Assessing awareness and deliberate intent is distinct from identifying the statement’s falsity.

5. Visiting graves in Al-Takāthur

Al-Takāthur 102:2

حَتَّىٰ زُرْتُمُ الْمَقَابِرَ

ḥattā zurtumu al-maqābir

Al-Takāthur 102:2

Translation:

“Until you visited the graves.”

زُرْتُمُ is the second-person plural past tense of زَارَ يَزُورُ.

الْمَقَابِرَ is its object: cemeteries or burial places.

The direct meaning of the verb is visiting.

The preceding verse must also be considered to understand the expression.

Al-Takāthur 102:1

أَلْهَاكُمُ التَّكَاثُرُ

alhākumu al-takāthur

Al-Takāthur 102:1

Translation:

“Rivalry in increase distracted you.”

Together, the verses describe distraction by rivalry continuing until the graves are reached.

Two familiar readings of زُرْتُمُ الْمَقَابِرَ are offered.

First reading: reaching death and the grave

In this reading, competition over wealth, numbers, or other advantages occupies people until life’s end.

Eventually a person dies and reaches the grave.

زُرْتُمُ thus expresses death and entry into the grave.

The language is intelligible because visiting involves going to a place, and the grave is pictured as a destination reached after earthly life.

The verb زار does not itself mean dying.

Death and life’s end are understood from the context of rivalry and reaching the graves.

This accords with the continuation: Al-Takāthur 102:3 and Al-Takāthur 102:4 announce future knowledge, followed by seeing the blazing fire and being questioned about blessings.

Second reading: visiting a cemetery to count the dead

Another reading refers to people who, competing over their group’s numbers and prestige, count even their dead.

Visiting graves then means actually going to cemeteries.

Their rivalry has extended to counting deceased members to claim superiority over others.

This fits the range of تكاثر, which can concern wealth, numbers, power, and social standing.

The verse itself specifies neither the tribe involved nor a ceremony for counting the dead.

Such details require independent historical evidence.

Is the visit a religious rite here?

Neither reading warrants restricting زُرْتُمُ to a particular ritual.

In the first, visiting graves pictures the end of earthly life.

In the second, going to the cemetery forms part of collective rivalry and boasting.

Neither reading by itself commands a cemetery ceremony or establishes a particular devotional practice.

Nor does the word “visit” alone yield a general ruling on every form of cemetery visitation.

The surah’s main subject is distraction by rivalry; visiting graves marks its end or culmination in the picture presented.

Does visiting imply a temporary stay in the grave?

Ordinarily, a visitor often returns or moves elsewhere afterward.

Using “visit” for the grave can therefore suggest its temporary character in relation to resurrection.

This reflection accords with Al-Muʾminūn 23:15 and Al-Muʾminūn 23:16, which mention death and subsequent resurrection.

Yet the temporary nature of the grave should not be proved solely from the lexical meaning of زار.

The word names visiting; it does not describe every posthumous stage or the length of a stay in the grave.

These questions require consideration of verses about death, the intervening state, and resurrection.

The direct sense is visiting, while the connection with death and resurrection comes from context and other Qur’anic passages.

6. Related roots and concepts

ز و ر (زُور) and ك ذ ب (كَذِب)

كذب concerns lies and statements contrary to fact.

زور likewise denotes false speech in its verbal branch.

The two overlap in many contexts.

Lexical explanations of زور particularly evoke departure from truth.

It can describe speech that distorts reality, makes a false attribution, or presents falsehood as truth.

This does not justify an absolute rule that كذب is always a simple lie and زور always an elaborate deliberate fabrication.

Such distinctions need contextual support in each use.

ز و ر (زُور) and أ ف ك (إِفْك)

إفك concerns turning or reversing and, in speech, falsehood and fabrication.

In Al-Furqān 25:4, opponents call the Qur’an إِفْكٌ افْتَرَاهُ, while the Qur’an calls their own allegation ظُلْمًا وَزُورًا.

Both words can thus concern lies and false attribution.

Each nevertheless has its own root and semantic field.

Their occurrence together does not establish complete synonymy.

ز و ر (زُور) and ب ه ت (بُهْتَان)

بهتان concerns falsely attributing something to a person, particularly a grave and shocking accusation.

زور has a broader field of false speech and departure from truth.

An accusation may be both بهتان and زور.

Not every instance of زور, however, accuses a particular person.

Al-Mujādilah 58:2 concerns an unreal family relationship, whereas Al-Ḥajj 22:30 prohibits false speech generally.

ز و ر (زُور) and ش ه د (شَهَادَة)

شهادة concerns presence, observation, and testimony.

زور characterizes speech as false.

Al-Furqān 25:72 brings these fields together in لَا يَشْهَدُونَ الزُّورَ.

In the testimony reading, شهادة names the act and زور characterizes its content as untrue.

This does not restrict زور to formal courtroom testimony.

False speech can occur in courts, families, social relationships, commerce, or religion.

ز و ر (زَوَر) and م ي ل (مَيْل)

ميل concerns inclination and turning from one direction toward another.

The physical branch of زور overlaps with this.

In Al-Kahf 18:17, تزاور describes the sun inclining away from the cave.

ميل has further Qur’anic uses, including inward inclination, desire, and deviation in conduct.

Not every inclination therefore means falsehood or visiting.

ز و ر (زِيَارَة) and ل ق ي (لِقَاء)

لقاء concerns encountering and meeting.

زيارة concerns going to visit someone or somewhere, whereas لقاء can describe the encounter itself.

Two people may meet accidentally without either having set out to visit the other.

Conversely, someone may set out intending a visit but fail to meet the person. Intending a visit should be distinguished from completing it.

An intention and journey toward a meeting are not identical to the encounter’s occurrence; they do not always justify saying the visit was completed.

ز و ر (زَوَر) and ج و ر (جَوْر)

جور concerns departure from justice and oppression.

The speech-related زور concerns departure from truth.

Their notions of deviation can overlap, but the roots differ.

False speech may have unjust consequences, yet زور is not simply synonymous with ظلم.

Al-Furqān 25:4 uses both words separately.

ز و ر (زُور) and و ز ر (وِزْر)

The roots contain similar letters but differ in order and meaning.

Qur’anic ز و ر concerns inclination, visiting, and false speech.

و ز ر concerns burdens and responsibility and, in some forms, refuge or a stronghold.

The Qur’an also uses وِزْر for the burden of responsibility or sin.

زور, falsehood, must therefore be distinguished from وزر, a burden of sin.

False speech may entail sin and responsibility; that moral consequence does not make the roots identical.

8. Conclusion

ز و ر concerns inclination, departure from a course, and turning in another direction.

The poetry discussed here illustrates two uses attributed to early Arabic.

In ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād’s verse, زَوْرَاء concerns inclination and turning aside.

In the poem attributed to ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAjlān al-Nahdī, زُورَا commands a visit.

These examples provide evidence for spatial inclination and visiting. Dating and attributing each transmitted line remain separate historical questions.

In speech, زُور denotes falsehood and departure from truth. The semantic connection is intelligible, but this article’s examples do not establish an equally certain pre-Islamic history for every form.

The root occurs six times in the Qur’an.

In Al-Kahf 18:17, تزاور describes the sun inclining away from the cave.

Al-Ḥajj 22:30 prohibits false speech.

Al-Furqān 25:4 characterizes the opponents’ false allegation about the Qur’an as injustice and falsehood.

Al-Furqān 25:72 describes avoiding false testimony; another reading concerns nonparticipation in falsehood.

Al-Mujādilah 58:2 characterizes the attribution made in ẓihār as objectionable and false speech.

Al-Takāthur 102:2 uses زرتم with graves, picturing in one contextual reading the end of distraction by rivalry at death.

These uses show that the root does not carry a negative moral value in every form.

Visiting is an ordinary human act.

The sun’s inclination describes direction and position.

Speech-related زُور, however, denotes falsehood and is condemned in the Qur’anic passages.

Inclination and departure provide a useful semantic connection among spatial deviation, visiting, and false speech. This connection does not by itself establish the historical order in which these distinct uses developed.

The three main uses must remain distinct: spatial inclination, visiting, and speech departing from truth.

A false statement should not automatically be treated as a deliberate lie without examining its speaker’s awareness and intention. Disagreement or an honest question is not intrinsically زور.

The subject and structure of each verse identify the relevant branch. This distinction prevents “visiting,” “deviation,” and “falsehood” from being interchanged without regard to context.
