Introduction to the Root

The root AYN W DH in the Arabic lexical family moves around the axis of seeking refuge, turning toward a safe place, holding fast to a shelter, and placing oneself within its protection.

In Arabic one says:

`adha bihi

He sought refuge in him; he turned toward him and held fast to his protection.

al-awdh / al-`iyadh

Seeking refuge and taking shelter.

al-ma`adh

Refuge-seeking or a place of refuge; and in the expression ma`adha The God, seeking refuge in The One God.

a`adhahu

He placed him in refuge, or sought refuge for him.

ista`adha

He sought refuge and asked for protection.

Ibn Faris gives the basic principle of this root as “al-iltija' ila al-shay'”, meaning to seek refuge in something and turn toward it, and then treats uses connected with clinging and remaining close as carried upon that principle. This order matters: “clinging” can be a tangible image for understanding refuge-seeking, but it should not replace the main core, which is iltija' and seeking refuge.

So, in the simplest wording:

AYN W DH is the relation of refuge-seeking in which, before a threat, one turns toward a safe place in order to come under its protection.

Qur'anic Use of the Root

The root AYN W DH appears seventeen times in the Qur'an, in four main forms:

Simple triliteral verb — ten times

Examples: a`udhu, `udhtu, ya`udhuna

Form IV — once

u`idhuha

Form X — four times

fasta`idh

ma`adh — twice

This morphological variety shows four directions:

seeking refuge oneself

placing another in refuge

asking for refuge

a compact expression of refuge-seeking in “ma`adha The God”

Short Meaning of the Root

Short meaning of the root:

To seek refuge, to take shelter, to turn toward a safe place, and to come under its protection.

In Form IV:

To place another in refuge.

At the practical level of the Qur'an:

Real refuge-seeking can be accompanied by moving away from the behavior, relationship, or channel that keeps a person exposed to that same harm; but this result comes from the context of the verses, not from the direct dictionary meaning of the root.

Tangible Image in the Lexical Family

The lexical family of AYN W DH has several tangible uses that make the image of refuge-seeking clearer.

Arabic sources mention:

al-`uwwadh min al-lahm

A part of meat that clings to the bone and remains close to it.

Forms from the same family are also used for a plant that grows at the foot of a stone, tree, or barrier and comes under its cover.

In another use, a newly delivered female animal may be called `a'idh, because her young takes refuge in her and remains close to her.

These witnesses strengthen one shared image:

A vulnerable being draws near to something, remains close to it, and comes under its protection.

But this image should not be reversed. Ibn Faris does not make “clinging” the principle and then derive “refuge” from it; he makes iltija' the principle and carries the related uses of remaining close and clinging upon it.

So adhesion is an explanatory image of the root, not the prior and decisive definition.

The Basic Construction “a`udhu bi-”

In Qur'anic verbal uses, the refuge to which a person turns usually comes with bi-:

أَعُوذُ بِاللَّهِ أَعُوذُ بِكَ أَعُوذُ بِالرَّحْمَٰنِ عُذْتُ بِرَبِّي فَاسْتَعِذْ بِاللَّهِ

This construction shows that bi- introduces the safe place and the one toward whom the refuge-seeker turns.

In Arabic grammar, bi- has several uses, including attachment, seeking help, and accompaniment. But from the preposition alone one should not conclude that the verse is speaking about “ontological connection to the divine essence.” Such an expression is beyond the syntactic capacity of the preposition bi-.

The agent or situation from which refuge is sought also does not always come in one single construction.

Sometimes it comes with min:

مِنَ الشَّيْطَانِ الرَّجِيمِ مِنْ هَمَزَاتِ الشَّيَاطِينِ مِنْ كُلِّ مُتَكَبِّرٍ

And sometimes with a clause of an...:

أَنْ أَكُونَ مِنَ الْجَاهِلِينَ أَنْ أَسْأَلَكَ مَا لَيْسَ لِي بِهِ عِلْمٌ أَنْ تَرْجُمُونِ

So the Qur'anic syntax is wider than a simple rule of “bi- for clinging and min for removing.”

Al-Baqarah 2:67; Refuge from Being among the Ignorant

وَإِذْ قَالَ مُوسَىٰ لِقَوْمِهِ إِنَّ اللَّهَ يَأْمُرُكُمْ أَن تَذْبَحُوا بَقَرَةً ۖ قَالُوا أَتَتَّخِذُنَا هُزُوًا ۖ قَالَ أَعُوذُ بِاللَّهِ أَنْ أَكُونَ مِنَ الْجَاهِلِينَ

“And when Moses said to his people: Indeed, The God commands you to slaughter a cow. They said: Do you take us in mockery? He said: I seek refuge in The God from being among the ignorant.”

Al-Baqarah 2:67

The subject of refuge here is not an external enemy.

Moses seeks refuge from:

أَنْ أَكُونَ مِنَ الْجَاهِلِينَ

So a human being can seek refuge from placing himself in a wrong moral and behavioral state.

This verse is important for the practical understanding of isti`adhah, because danger is not always something that attacks a person “from outside”; sometimes the person himself may enter a position from which he must seek refuge in The One God.

Ali Imran 3:36; Placing Another in Refuge

فَلَمَّا وَضَعَتْهَا قَالَتْ رَبِّ إِنِّي وَضَعْتُهَا أُنثَىٰ وَاللَّهُ أَعْلَمُ بِمَا وَضَعَتْ وَلَيْسَ الذَّكَرُ كَالْأُنثَىٰ وَإِنِّي سَمَّيْتُهَا مَرْيَمَ وَإِنِّي أُعِيذُهَا بِكَ وَذُرِّيَّتَهَا مِنَ الشَّيْطَانِ الرَّجِيمِ

“So when she delivered her, she said: My Lord, I have delivered her female — and The God knew best what she had delivered — and the male is not like the female; and I have named her Maryam, and I place her and her offspring in refuge with You from the expelled shaytan.”

Ali Imran 3:36

Here the form:

أُعِيذُهَا

appears.

The speaker herself is not the direct subject of refuge; she entrusts another to the safe place.

So Form IV in this use expands the field of the root from:

seeking refuge oneself

to:

placing another in refuge.

Hud 11:47; Refuge from Asking without Knowledge

قَالَ رَبِّ إِنِّي أَعُوذُ بِكَ أَنْ أَسْأَلَكَ مَا لَيْسَ لِي بِهِ عِلْمٌ ۖ وَإِلَّا تَغْفِرْ لِي وَتَرْحَمْنِي أَكُن مِّنَ الْخَاسِرِينَ

“He said: My Lord, I seek refuge in You from asking You for that about which I have no knowledge; and if You do not forgive me and have mercy on me, I will be among the losers.”

Hud 11:47

This verse shows that the subject of isti`adhah can be a person’s own behavior.

Noah seeks refuge from asking for something about which he has no knowledge.

Therefore, in the logic of the Qur'an, refuge is not only from a dangerous being or an external event; ignorance and acting on the basis of ignorance can also be channels of harm.

Yusuf 12:23; “ma`adha The God” and Practical Refusal

وَرَاوَدَتْهُ الَّتِي هُوَ فِي بَيْتِهَا عَن نَّفْسِهِ وَغَلَّقَتِ الْأَبْوَابَ وَقَالَتْ هَيْتَ لَكَ ۚ قَالَ مَعَاذَ اللَّهِ إِنَّهُ رَبِّي أَحْسَنَ مَثْوَايَ ۖ إِنَّهُ لَا يُفْلِحُ الظَّالِمُونَ

“And she in whose house he was sought him from himself, firmly closed the doors, and said: I am ready for you. He said: Refuge in The God! Indeed, He is my Lord; He has made my dwelling good. Indeed, the wrongdoers do not succeed.”

Yusuf 12:23

ma`adh in this construction is a verbal noun of the family AYN W DH.

In common syntactic analysis, ma`adha The God is a compact verbal-noun expression that stands in place of the act of seeking refuge; that is, it carries a meaning close to:

أَعُوذُ بِاللَّهِ

But one should not conclude from the omission of the verb alone that the syntax itself encodes “absolute immediacy” or “speed of reaction.” The decisiveness and immediacy of Yusuf become clear above all from the whole scene and his practical response.

The most important point is that Yusuf says ma`adha The God and rejects the very act from which he seeks refuge.

So the word and the behavior move in one direction.

Yusuf 12:79; Isti`adhah from Wrongdoing and Leaving the Wrongful Act

قَالَ مَعَاذَ اللَّهِ أَن نَّأْخُذَ إِلَّا مَن وَجَدْنَا مَتَاعَنَا عِندَهُ إِنَّا إِذًا لَّظَالِمُونَ

“He said: Refuge in The God from taking anyone except the one with whom we found our goods; indeed, then we would surely be wrongdoers.”

Yusuf 12:79

Here the link between isti`adhah and behavior is even clearer.

Yusuf seeks refuge from a specific act and immediately gives the reason:

إِنَّا إِذًا لَّظَالِمُونَ

So “refuge from wrongdoing” is accompanied by refusing to do that very wrongful act.

From this verse one cannot conclude that the lexical meaning of awdh is “leaving wrongdoing.”

But one can conclude that real isti`adhah from an error is incompatible with knowingly choosing that same error.

Maryam 19:18; Refuge and Establishing a Boundary

قَالَتْ إِنِّي أَعُوذُ بِالرَّحْمَٰنِ مِنكَ إِن كُنتَ تَقِيًّا

“She said: I seek refuge in The Merciful from you, if you are one who has taqwa.”

Maryam 19:18

Maryam, before someone who has suddenly appeared in her private space, says:

إِنِّي أَعُوذُ بِالرَّحْمَٰنِ مِنكَ

In this context, isti`adhah is at the same time the declaration of a boundary.

She does not ignore the possible danger and she clarifies her relation to the person before her.

But “boundary-setting” is not the lexical definition of awdh; it is a behavior that accompanies refuge-seeking in this situation.

Al-Muminun 23:97; Refuge from the Promptings of the Shaytans

وَقُل رَّبِّ أَعُوذُ بِكَ مِنْ هَمَزَاتِ الشَّيَاطِينِ

“And say: My Lord, I seek refuge in You from the promptings and proddings of the shaytans.”

Al-Muminun 23:97

This verse identifies the threatening factor as:

هَمَزَاتِ الشَّيَاطِينِ

Refuge-seeking here is from infiltrating pressures and impulses, before they necessarily become a final act.

Al-Muminun 23:98; Refuge Even from the Presence of the Threatening Factor

وَأَعُوذُ بِكَ رَبِّ أَن يَحْضُرُونِ

“And I seek refuge in You, my Lord, from their being present with me.”

Al-Muminun 23:98

This verse is important for understanding the preventive aspect of isti`adhah.

Refuge is not sought only from the result of influence; even:

أَن يَحْضُرُونِ

is the subject of refuge.

So isti`adhah can take place before the harm is completed.

This is an important witness for the reflection that Qur'anic refuge-seeking can be accompanied by preventing the threatening factor from drawing near or becoming established.

Al-Araf 7:200; The Command to Seek Refuge When Nazgh Occurs

وَإِمَّا يَنزَغَنَّكَ مِنَ الشَّيْطَانِ نَزْغٌ فَاسْتَعِذْ بِاللَّهِ إِنَّهُ سَمِيعٌ عَلِيمٌ

“And if a nazgh from the shaytan stirs you, then seek refuge in The God; indeed, He is Hearing, Knowing.”

Al-Araf 7:200

In this verse, isti`adhah is a response to:

نَزْغ

an impulse or provocation that can divert a person from the right path.

But to understand this command fully, the surrounding verses must also be seen.

Al-Araf 7:199–201; The Practical Network around Isti`adhah

خُذِ الْعَفْوَ وَأْمُرْ بِالْعُرْفِ وَأَعْرِضْ عَنِ الْجَاهِلِينَ

“Take up pardon, command what is recognized, and turn away from the ignorant.”

Al-Araf 7:199

وَإِمَّا يَنزَغَنَّكَ مِنَ الشَّيْطَانِ نَزْغٌ فَاسْتَعِذْ بِاللَّهِ إِنَّهُ سَمِيعٌ عَلِيمٌ

“And if a nazgh from the shaytan stirs you, then seek refuge in The God; indeed, He is Hearing, Knowing.”

Al-Araf 7:200

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ اتَّقَوْا إِذَا مَسَّهُمْ طَائِفٌ مِّنَ الشَّيْطَانِ تَذَكَّرُوا فَإِذَا هُم مُّبْصِرُونَ

“Indeed, those who have taqwa, when a passing touch from the shaytan touches them, remember; then suddenly they are seeing clearly.”

Al-Araf 7:201

In this set, isti`adhah stands among several practical directions:

turning away from the ignorant

recognizing nazgh

seeking refuge

taqwa

remembering

insight

These words are not synonyms of awdh, but they show that the Qur'an places the command to seek refuge inside a behavioral and cognitive network.

For this reason, isti`adhah in this context is not compatible with continuing the same provocation without restraint.

An-Nahl 16:98; Isti`adhah When Reciting the Qur'an

فَإِذَا قَرَأْتَ الْقُرْآنَ فَاسْتَعِذْ بِاللَّهِ مِنَ الشَّيْطَانِ الرَّجِيمِ

“So when you recite the Qur'an, seek refuge in The God from the expelled shaytan.”

An-Nahl 16:98

This verse is one of the most well-known cases of isti`adhah.

But to understand it, the verse should not be separated from the two verses that follow it.

An-Nahl 16:98–100; Isti`adhah, Faith, Tawakkul, and Wilayah

فَإِذَا قَرَأْتَ الْقُرْآنَ فَاسْتَعِذْ بِاللَّهِ مِنَ الشَّيْطَانِ الرَّجِيمِ

“So when you recite the Qur'an, seek refuge in The God from the expelled shaytan.”

An-Nahl 16:98

إِنَّهُ لَيْسَ لَهُ سُلْطَانٌ عَلَى الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَعَلَىٰ رَبِّهِمْ يَتَوَكَّلُونَ

“Indeed, he has no authority over those who have faith and who place their tawakkul upon their Lord.”

An-Nahl 16:99

إِنَّمَا سُلْطَانُهُ عَلَى الَّذِينَ يَتَوَلَّوْنَهُ وَالَّذِينَ هُم بِهِ مُشْرِكُونَ

“His authority is only over those who take him as wali and those who, through him, make partners.”

An-Nahl 16:100

Isti`adhah here is immediately accompanied by three other concepts:

faith

tawakkul

wilayah

These are not the meaning of isti`adhah, but the structure of the context shows that seeking refuge from the shaytan is not merely pronouncing a sentence. The human being’s relation to trust and wilayah also matters.

A person who seeks refuge from a threatening factor while knowingly taking that same factor as wali is, in practice, moving in two opposite directions.

Ghafir 40:27; Refuge from an Arrogant Threatener

وَقَالَ مُوسَىٰ إِنِّي عُذْتُ بِرَبِّي وَرَبِّكُم مِّن كُلِّ مُتَكَبِّرٍ لَّا يُؤْمِنُ بِيَوْمِ الْحِسَابِ

“And Moses said: I have sought refuge in my Lord and your Lord from every arrogant one who does not have faith in the Day of Account.”

Ghafir 40:27

In this verse, the threat is an arrogant human being.

So awdh in the Qur'an is not only about inner whispering or an unseen matter; a person can seek refuge in The One God from a human threat as well.

Ghafir 40:56; Isti`adhah before Arrogance and Argument without Authority

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ يُجَادِلُونَ فِي آيَاتِ اللَّهِ بِغَيْرِ سُلْطَانٍ أَتَاهُمْ إِن فِي صُدُورِهِمْ إِلَّا كِبْرٌ مَّا هُم بِبَالِغِيهِ ۚ فَاسْتَعِذْ بِاللَّهِ ۖ إِنَّهُ هُوَ السَّمِيعُ الْبَصِيرُ

“Indeed, those who argue about the signs of The God without any authority that has come to them, there is nothing in their chests except arrogance which they will not attain; so seek refuge in The God. Indeed, He is The Hearing, The Seeing.”

Ghafir 40:56

Here isti`adhah comes in the context of argument without authority and arrogance in the chests.

The verse does not say that awdh itself means struggling against arrogance, but it shows that intellectual and moral conditions can also be places where isti`adhah is needed.

Fussilat 41:36; Isti`adhah from Nazgh

وَإِمَّا يَنزَغَنَّكَ مِنَ الشَّيْطَانِ نَزْغٌ فَاسْتَعِذْ بِاللَّهِ إِنَّهُ هُوَ السَّمِيعُ الْعَلِيمُ

“And if a nazgh from the shaytan stirs you, then seek refuge in The God; indeed, He is The Hearing, The Knowing.”

Fussilat 41:36

This verse is very close in wording to Al-Araf 200, but its preceding context clarifies another point.

Fussilat 41:34–36; Isti`adhah and Restraining the Reaction

وَلَا تَسْتَوِي الْحَسَنَةُ وَلَا السَّيِّئَةُ ادْفَعْ بِالَّتِي هِيَ أَحْسَنُ فَإِذَا الَّذِي بَيْنَكَ وَبَيْنَهُ عَدَاوَةٌ كَأَنَّهُ وَلِيٌّ حَمِيمٌ

“The good deed and the bad deed are not equal. Repel with what is better; then the one between you and him there was enmity becomes as though he were a close wali.”

Fussilat 41:34

وَمَا يُلَقَّاهَا إِلَّا الَّذِينَ صَبَرُوا وَمَا يُلَقَّاهَا إِلَّا ذُو حَظٍّ عَظِيمٍ

“And none is granted it except those who were patient, and none is granted it except one with a great share.”

Fussilat 41:35

وَإِمَّا يَنزَغَنَّكَ مِنَ الشَّيْطَانِ نَزْغٌ فَاسْتَعِذْ بِاللَّهِ إِنَّهُ هُوَ السَّمِيعُ الْعَلِيمُ

“And if a nazgh from the shaytan stirs you, then seek refuge in The God; indeed, He is The Hearing, The Knowing.”

Fussilat 41:36

Before the command to seek refuge, the Qur'an says:

ادْفَعْ بِالَّتِي هِيَ أَحْسَنُ

and speaks of:

صَبَرُوا

Therefore, in this context, isti`adhah is accompanied by restraining the response produced by nazgh and choosing the better reaction.

This does not mean awdh = controlling anger; rather, controlling the reaction is one of the behaviors that, in this context, is compatible with the direction of refuge-seeking.

Ad-Dukhan 44:20; Refuge from Physical Harm

وَإِنِّي عُذْتُ بِرَبِّي وَرَبِّكُمْ أَنْ تَرْجُمُونِ

“And I have sought refuge in my Lord and your Lord from your stoning me.”

Ad-Dukhan 44:20

This use shows that the threat can be entirely physical and external.

Therefore, awdh should not be limited only to whispering and spiritual danger.

Al-Jinn 72:6; Seeking Refuge in a Wrong Shelter

وَأَنَّهُ كَانَ رِجَالٌ مِّنَ الْإِنسِ يَعُوذُونَ بِرِجَالٍ مِّنَ الْجِنِّ فَزَادُوهُمْ رَهَقًا

“And that there were men from humankind who used to seek refuge in men from the jinn, so they increased them in rahaq.”

Al-Jinn 72:6

This verse clarifies an essential point:

awdh by itself does not guarantee a correct or successful act.

They really were:

يَعُوذُونَ

but the result of their refuge-seeking was not protection:

فَزَادُوهُمْ رَهَقًا

So besides the act of seeking refuge, the nature of the refuge to which one turns is also decisive.

Al-Falaq 113; The Complete Structure of Refuge-Seeking

قُلْ أَعُوذُ بِرَبِّ الْفَلَقِ

“Say: I seek refuge in the Lord of the daybreak.”

Al-Falaq 113:1

مِن شَرِّ مَا خَلَقَ

“From the evil of what He created.”

Al-Falaq 113:2

وَمِن شَرِّ غَاسِقٍ إِذَا وَقَبَ

“And from the evil of darkness when it enters and becomes pervasive.”

Al-Falaq 113:3

وَمِن شَرِّ النَّفَّاثَاتِ فِي الْعُقَدِ

“And from the evil of those who blow into knots.”

Al-Falaq 113:4

وَمِن شَرِّ حَاسِدٍ إِذَا حَسَدَ

“And from the evil of an envier when he envies.”

Al-Falaq 113:5

Surah Al-Falaq shows the structure of refuge-seeking very clearly:

the refuge-seeker

the speaker.

the refuge

Lord of the daybreak.

that from which refuge is sought

different forms of evil.

Here min repeatedly introduces the danger or source of evil, but this use should not be turned into a general theory that the preposition min itself “removes the danger.” Its precise function in the sentence is to introduce that from which refuge is sought.

An-Nas 114; Refuge from Inner Infiltration

قُلْ أَعُوذُ بِرَبِّ النَّاسِ

“Say: I seek refuge in the Lord of the people.”

An-Nas 114:1

مَلِكِ النَّاسِ

“King of the people.”

An-Nas 114:2

إِلَٰهِ النَّاسِ

“The God of the people.”

An-Nas 114:3

مِن شَرِّ الْوَسْوَاسِ الْخَنَّاسِ

“From the evil of the whisperer who withdraws.”

An-Nas 114:4

الَّذِي يُوَسْوِسُ فِي صُدُورِ النَّاسِ

“The one who whispers in the chests of the people.”

An-Nas 114:5

مِنَ الْجِنَّةِ وَالنَّاسِ

“From the jinn and the people.”

An-Nas 114:6

In this surah, the danger comes close to the inner space of human perception and decision:

يُوَسْوِسُ فِي صُدُورِ النَّاسِ

This use shows that refuge in the Qur'an is not only from physical or external threat; inner infiltration is also the subject of isti`adhah.

Is Isti`adhah Only a Spoken Phrase?

The Qur'an itself teaches verbal forms of isti`adhah:

قُلْ أَعُوذُ

and:

فَاسْتَعِذْ بِاللَّهِ

So saying it and verbally asking for refuge is truly part of isti`adhah and should not be treated as unimportant.

But the network of verses shows that isti`adhah is not reduced to a sound formula independent of choice and behavior.

Yusuf says ma`adha The God and refrains from the wrong act.

Noah seeks refuge from asking without knowledge.

Maryam seeks refuge and announces her boundary.

In Al-Araf, isti`adhah stands beside taqwa, remembrance, and insight.

In Fussilat, it stands beside patience and repelling badness with what is better.

In An-Nahl, it stands beside faith, tawakkul, and not accepting the wilayah of the shaytan.

Therefore:

The spoken statement of isti`adhah can be the verbal expression of refuge-seeking; but Qur'anic isti`adhah in practice is compatible with moving in the direction of that very refuge.

“Blocking the Channels of Vulnerability”; Meaning or Practical Result?

This distinction must remain clear.

Lexically, one cannot say:

AYN W DH = blocking the channels of vulnerability

The core of the root is seeking refuge and turning toward a safe place.

But in the practical use of the Qur'an, refuge-seeking can require that a person not keep the channel of threat open.

If the danger is a wrongful act, he refrains from that act.

If nazgh is provoking a reaction, he restrains his reaction.

If a threatening factor seeks to gain wilayah, he does not accept its wilayah.

If the presence of the harmful factor is itself the danger, he seeks refuge from that very presence.

So the more precise expression is:

Practical isti`adhah in the Qur'an can be accompanied by recognizing and closing channels through which a threat enters a person’s behavior, decision, or condition.

This is a contextual reflection, not the direct lexical definition.

AYN W DH Compared with L W DH

The root L W DH is one of the closest semantic families to AYN W DH.

Lexical sources for:

لَاذَ بِهِ

also mention “he sought refuge in him” and even “`adha bihi.”

Therefore, one should not build an absolute boundary and say:

awdh always means clinging to a protector ladh always means hiding behind a barrier.

The two roots have real overlap in the field of refuge and iltija'.

Nevertheless, in some forms of L W DH, the element of concealment and hiding becomes prominent. In the Qur'an it says:

لَا تَجْعَلُوا دُعَاءَ الرَّسُولِ بَيْنَكُمْ كَدُعَاءِ بَعْضِكُم بَعْضًا ۚ قَدْ يَعْلَمُ اللَّهُ الَّذِينَ يَتَسَلَّلُونَ مِنكُمْ لِوَاذًا ۚ فَلْيَحْذَرِ الَّذِينَ يُخَالِفُونَ عَنْ أَمْرِهِ أَن تُصِيبَهُمْ فِتْنَةٌ أَوْ يُصِيبَهُمْ عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ

“Do not make the call of the Messenger among you like the call of some of you to others. The God certainly knows those of you who slip away covertly, taking shelter behind one another; so let those who oppose his command beware lest a trial afflict them or a painful punishment afflict them.”

An-Nur 24:63

In liwadha, taking refuge in one another in order to leave secretly is prominent.

So one may say:

AYN W DH in the Qur'an focuses on seeking refuge and holding fast to a safe place.

L W DH, in the use liwadha, highlights taking shelter in another for concealment and hiding.

This difference is tendential and usage-based, not an absolute boundary in Arabic.

AYN W DH Compared with W Q Y

W Q Y inclines toward protection, guarding, and creating a barrier between a being and harm.

AYN W DH is seen more from the angle of the refuge-seeker who turns toward a safe place.

In an initial way:

wiqayah

Protection and shielding from harm.

awdh

Seeking refuge and turning toward a safe place.

But the full boundary between the two should be examined in an independent study of the root W Q Y.

AYN W DH Compared with H S N

H S N is connected with a fortress, strength, protection, and being safe.

AYN W DH highlights the movement or relation of seeking refuge itself.

Therefore, one should not build from the image difference between these two roots a fixed system in which, for example, one always means “entering a fortress” and the other always means “clinging to a protector.” The precise meaning of each derivative is extracted from its actual use.

AYN W DH Compared with T W K L

Tawakkul concerns trust, reliance, and entrusting one’s affair.

awdh concerns seeking refuge before a threat.

The two are not synonyms, but An-Nahl 98–100 shows that they can stand together in one protective structure.

Isti`adhah says toward what refuge a human being turns before a threat.

Tawakkul states the relation of trust and reliance he has upon his Lord.

Morphological and Syntactic Notes

a`udhu

It is a present-tense simple triliteral verb:

أَعُوذُ بِاللَّهِ

The subject himself seeks refuge in the safe place.

u`idhuha

In Ali Imran 36 it is from Form IV:

أُعِيذُهَا بِكَ

The subject places another in refuge.

fasta`idh

It is an imperative from Form X.

In Qur'anic use it means seeking and asking for refuge.

ma`adha The God

ma`adh is a verbal noun.

In common syntactic analysis, this construction is understood as an accusative verbal noun from which the act of refuge-seeking is understood; a meaning close to:

أَعُوذُ بِاللَّهِ

But its precise rhetorical force must be determined from context, not only from the omission of the verb.

Challenges of Understanding and Translation

First, awdh should not be reduced only to pronouncing a sentence, although the verbal statement of isti`adhah is a real part of Qur'anic use.

Second, “blocking vulnerability” should not be made the dictionary meaning of the root. It can be a practical result of isti`adhah in some contexts.

Third, Ibn Faris gives the principle of the root as iltija', not adhesion. Remaining close and clinging come at the stage of explaining related uses.

Fourth, from the preposition bi- one should not extract a theory of “ontological connection to the divine essence.” In the verbal construction, bi- introduces the refuge, but its syntactic capacity is more than one fixed meaning.

Fifth, what refuge is sought from does not always come with min; the construction an... also appears in the Qur'an.

Sixth, between AYN W DH and L W DH there is real lexical overlap. “Clinging” versus “hiding” should not be made an absolute boundary between the two roots.

Seventh, ma`adha The God is a compact and decisive construction in the context of Yusuf, but “immediacy” should not be extracted only from the omission of the verb as a syntactic law.

Eighth, Al-Jinn 6 shows that every refuge-seeking does not necessarily bring protection; the destination of refuge also matters.

Ninth, isti`adhah can be from external danger, a moral state, behavior without knowledge, whispering, arrogance, the presence of a threatening factor, and wrongdoing. So it should not be limited only to “shaytan” or spiritual danger.

Translation Suggestions for Forqan

For:

أَعُوذُ بِاللَّهِ

“I seek refuge in The God.”

For:

أَعُوذُ بِكَ

“I seek refuge in You.”

For:

أُعِيذُهَا بِكَ

“I place her in refuge with You.”

For:

فَاسْتَعِذْ بِاللَّهِ

“So seek refuge in The God.”

For:

مَعَاذَ اللَّهِ

“Refuge in The God!”

This short equivalent preserves the compactness of the Arabic construction and does not unnecessarily turn it into a long explanation.

Short Meaning of the Root for Forqan

Short meaning of the root:

Seeking refuge, taking shelter, turning toward a safe place, and coming under its protection.

In Form IV:

Placing another in refuge.

Practical reflection:

Isti`adhah in the Qur'an is not limited to speech; real refuge-seeking in the relevant contexts is accompanied by distancing oneself from the behavior, relationship, or channel that keeps a person exposed to that same threat.

Conclusion

The root AYN W DH is fundamentally a root of iltija' and refuge-seeking.

The refuge-seeker, upon sensing or recognizing a threat, turns toward a safe place and wants to come under its protection.

The tangible uses of Arabic, such as meat remaining close to bone or a plant taking shelter beside a protective barrier, strengthen the image of nearness and remaining with the refuge; but the central core remains seeking refuge.

In the Qur'an, this root has a wide field.

Moses seeks refuge from being ignorant.

Noah seeks refuge from asking without knowledge.

Maryam seeks refuge from the possible danger of a human being.

Yusuf seeks refuge from falling into wrongdoing.

Refuge is sought from the promptings and presence of the shaytans.

Before nazgh, the command fasta`idh billah comes.

In Al-Falaq refuge is sought from different forms of evil, and in An-Nas from a whispering that reaches into the chests.

On the other hand, Al-Jinn 6 shows that merely seeking refuge is not enough; the refuge must also be real.

Most importantly, the network of verses shows that isti`adhah cannot be reduced to a spoken formula detached from behavior. Yusuf, with ma`adha The God, leaves that very act; Maryam sets a boundary; people of taqwa, when nazgh touches them, remember and see clearly; in Fussilat, the better response and patience are mentioned; and in An-Nahl, isti`adhah stands with faith, tawakkul, and not accepting the wilayah of shaytan.

Therefore, the final definition of this root can be stated as follows:

AYN W DH expresses seeking refuge and turning toward a safe place in order to come under its protection. Remaining close to the refuge is part of the lexical image of this family, but its principle is “iltija'.” In the practical use of the Qur'an, isti`adhah is not merely saying a request for refuge; the refuge-seeker moves in the direction of that refuge and, wherever the context requires, distances himself from the behavior, relationship, or channel that keeps the way of threat open. This practical layer is understood from the network of verses and should not be made identical with the direct lexical meaning of the root.
