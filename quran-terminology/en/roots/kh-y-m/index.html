From erecting shelter and staying in one place to human disposition: al-khiyām in the description of the Garden

The Arabic root KH Y M concerns dwelling, remaining, and settling. Its clearest instance is khaymah, a shelter erected in a place for habitation. Khayyama bi-al-makān means to remain in a place; takhayyama can mean pitching a tent or staying in it.

In early Arabic, a khaymah was more than fabric stretched over pegs. Some lexicographers described a structure of branches and wood covered with vegetation for shelter from sunlight. Others also included tents made with fabric and ropes.

Khīm also denotes human character, disposition, and nature. Ibn Fāris connects this with relative stability and a person’s return to their disposition.

Early dictionaries additionally record khāma, retreating in fear; khāmah, a fresh, tender plant stem; and khām, unprocessed material.

Shared consonants do not establish that every detail of these uses developed from one proven historical meaning. Proposed semantic cores, actual usage, and each derivative’s particular meaning must be distinguished.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an:

Al-khiyām in Al-Raḥmān 55:72.

It appears in ḥūrun maqṣūrātun fī al-khiyām, referring to tents or covered dwellings.

Khaymah names a kind of shelter and dwelling. The precise shape, materials, dimensions, and manner of living in the heavenly tents cannot be derived from the word alone.

1. Important forms and semantic core

KH Y M is a triliteral hollow root with medial yāʾ, which changes in certain inflected forms.

Important Arabic forms and derivatives include:

  • خَيْمَة: a tent, shelter, or dwelling erected with a supporting structure and covering.
  • خِيَام: a plural of khaymah; tents.
  • خِيَم، خَيْمَات: other plurals of khaymah.
  • خَيْم: in one use, the timbers or supports on which a tent stands.
  • خَيَّمَ ـ يُخَيِّمُ: to pitch a tent, enter one, or remain in a place.
  • تَخَيَّمَ: to pitch a tent or stay in one.
  • أَخَامَ، أَخْيَمَ: in recorded uses, to construct a tent.
  • خِيم: character, disposition, nature, or an enduring temperament.
  • خَامَ ـ يَخِيمُ: in one use, to retreat or hold back, especially through fear or weakness.
  • خَائِم: someone retreating through fear or weakness.
  • إِخَامَة: in bodily description, keeping a leg raised because injury or inability prevents placing it on the ground.
  • خَامَة: a fresh, tender plant stem or shoot; in some uses, an ear of grain.
  • خَام: in certain early uses, untanned skin or syrup not yet heated.
  • مُخَيَّم: concerning habitation, a place for pitching tents or an erected tent.

These differ in antiquity, frequency, and semantic relationship. Modern mukhayyam meaning camp belongs to the family, but modern camps’ organization and form must not be projected onto early Arabian tents.

Only the plural form al-khiyām occurs in the Qur’an.

It belongs to khaymah; the definite article and final kasrah relate to the verse’s syntax.

Khayyama, khīm meaning disposition, and khāma meaning retreat do not occur in the Qur’an.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: one occurrence in Al-Raḥmān 55:72

The semantic core in Maqāyīs al-Lughah

Ibn Fāris writes:

الْخَاءُ وَالْيَاءُ وَالْمِيمُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى الْإِقَامَةِ وَالثَّبَاتِ.

al-khāʾu wa-al-yāʾu wa-al-mīmu aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā al-iqāmati wa-al-thabāti

Translation:

“Khāʾ, yāʾ, and mīm have one underlying principle indicating dwelling and remaining established.”

He identifies the tent as an instance of that principle, then says:

خَيَّمَ بِالْمَكَانِ: أَقَامَ بِهِ

khayyama bi-al-makāni aqāma bihi

That is, “He remained in that place.”

The root is thus not restricted to the tent’s structure. Remaining in one location is a broader field, with a tent as one means of doing so.

Thabāt here must not mean everlasting permanence.

Someone may remain in a place for a night or several days and then move on.

A tent can likewise be pitched and later taken down.

Remaining is contrasted with moving away at that moment, without requiring the tent or its inhabitant to stay there forever.

This distinction matters for nomadic life: someone may reside in a place temporarily.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: dwelling and remaining

2. The tent in early Arab life

How was a tent constructed?

One early definition in Lisān al-ʿArab states:

بَيْتٌ مِنْ بُيُوتِ الْأَعْرَابِ مُسْتَدِيرٌ يَبْنِيهِ الْأَعْرَابُ مِنْ عِيدَانِ الشَّجَرِ.

baytun min buyūti al-aʿrābi mustadīrun yabnīhi al-aʿrābu min ʿīdāni al-shajari

Translation:

“A round dwelling among the dwellings of the Bedouin, which they build from tree branches.”

The tent here is a familiar shelter erected from wood and branches.

Another account describes three or four poles covered with thumām vegetation to provide shade from heat.

Such a shelter could stand near a livestock watering place, where herders paused while watering animals.

It need not be used only for sleeping: it could provide rest, shelter from the sun, and storage for certain belongings.

Early sources nevertheless differ over tent materials and construction.

Must a tent be made from wood and branches?

Ibn al-Aʿrābī restricted khaymah to four poles roofed with vegetation. Fabric shelters had a different precise designation in this account.

The explanation transmitted from Al-Aṣmaʿī likewise emphasizes wood and vegetation.

Other lexicographers also count fabric shelters erected with ropes and supports as khaymah.

Ibn Barrī discusses this disagreement, noting that some do not restrict the term to plant-built structures.

This matters for the Qur’anic meaning.

One lexicographer’s definition using tree branches does not prove that Arabic never called fabric structures khaymah.

Nor do fabric tents invalidate early descriptions of plant-built shelters.

The shared feature is an erected shelter used for dwelling and protection from environmental conditions.

Its materials and shape can vary.

The general meaning of khaymah is therefore not limited to one fixed building plan.

Lisān al-ʿArab: differing definitions and tent materials

Khaym: tent timbers and supports

Khaym sometimes denotes the timbers and supports on which a tent is built.

This differs from khaymah as the complete shelter.

When a tent is removed, for example, its supports or traces of its site may remain.

Khaym can then concern structural components, rather than an intact tent ready for habitation.

Early Arabic poetry illustrates the distinction.

A witness from Al-Muraqqish al-Akbar

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Al-Muraqqish al-Akbar says:

هَلْ تَعْرِفُ الدَّارَ عَفَا رَسْمُهَا
إِلَّا الْأَثَافِيَّ وَمَبْنَى الْخِيَمْ

hal taʿrifu al-dāra ʿafā rasmuhā / illā al-athāfiyya wa-mabnā al-khiyam

Translation:

“Do you recognize the dwelling whose traces have vanished, except the hearthstones and the place where the tents stood?”

The poet returns to a place where people once lived.

Its inhabitants have left, and most signs of life have disappeared.

Hearthstones and tent sites remain recognizable.

Athāfī are stones used to support a cooking pot over a fire.

Mabnā al-khiyam concerns the site or traces of erected tents.

This shows the tent’s practical relationship to dwelling in early Arab life.

Even after the inhabitants depart, the poet recognizes tent sites as traces of earlier habitation.

The line itself nevertheless establishes no single material or geometric shape for every tent.

A witness from Zuhayr: dwelling after reaching water

A poem attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā says:

فَلَمَّا وَرَدْنَ الْمَاءَ زُرْقًا جِمَامُهُ
وَضَعْنَ عِصِيَّ الْحَاضِرِ الْمُتَخَيِّمِ

fa-lammā waradna al-māʾa zurqan jimāmuhu / waḍaʿna ʿiṣiyya al-ḥāḍiri al-mutakhayyimi

Translation:

“When they reached water whose pools were clear and bluish, they laid down the staffs of a settled tent-dweller and alighted.”

Al-mutakhayyim refers to someone pitching a tent and dwelling in a location.

Waḍaʿna ʿiṣiyya al-ḥāḍir likewise pictures movement ending and habitation beginning.

Reaching water in nomadic life could prompt a halt and the establishment of shelter.

That halt need not be permanent.

The group might later move again as water, pasture, or other needs changed.

Takhayyum is thus dwelling in a place without lexically establishing permanence.

The witness is recorded in Zuhayr’s Dīwān and commentaries; mutakhayyim clearly concerns alighting and dwelling.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Asās al-Balāghah: Al-Muraqqish and Zuhayr

Tent and howdah

In some poetic uses, khiyām also denotes howdahs.

A howdah was an enclosed or covered seat placed on a camel’s back for travel.

Tent and howdah resemble one another through the protected, covered space they provide.

A howdah is nevertheless different from a tent pitched on the ground.

A tent commonly serves a halt or residence, while a howdah can be used during travel.

A witness from Labīd

The Muʿallaqah attributed to Labīd ibn Rabīʿah says:

شَاقَتْكَ ظُعْنُ الْحَيِّ يَوْمَ تَحَمَّلُوا
فَتَكَنَّسُوا قُطُنًا تَصِرُّ خِيَامُهَا

shāqatka ẓuʿnu al-ḥayyi yawma taḥammalū / fa-takannasū quṭunan taṣirru khiyāmuhā

Translation of the sense:

“The women’s departure on the day their people loaded up stirred your longing; then they entered their howdahs in groups, while the covered travel structures creaked.”

Khiyām concerns coverings associated with howdahs here.

Ẓuʿn concerns the caravan’s women and their seats during departure.

Early versions differ in some words, including taḥammalū and taḥammalat, and in the second hemistich.

Reputable lexical explanations nevertheless attest khiyām for howdah coverings.

A covered space need therefore not be fixed to the ground to be described through this family.

Dwelling and shelter matter, but must not become a requirement of absolute geographical immobility.

Al-Ṭabarī and the Abū ʿUbaydah transmission: Labīd, variants, and quṭun

3. Dwelling, remaining, and human disposition

Khayyama: remaining in a place

Arabic says:

خَيَّمَ بِالْمَكَانِ

khayyama bi-al-makāni

That is, “He remained in that place.”

Also:

تَخَيَّمَ

takhayyama

can mean pitching a tent or staying in one.

Another use is:

خَيَّمَ الْوَحْشِيُّ فِي كِنَاسِهِ

khayyama al-waḥshiyyu fī kināsihi

“The wild animal remained in its shelter and did not leave.”

This shows that khayyama was not used exclusively for people and human-made structures.

An animal remaining in its natural refuge could also be described through this family.

Khayyamat al-rāʾiḥatu bi-al-makān describes an odor lingering in a place rather than disappearing.

The odor has not literally built a tent: the verb khayyama describes its lingering presence.

The root’s semantic field thus extends beyond a structure’s name.

None of these instances necessarily means remaining forever.

Khīm: character and disposition

Khīm, with kasrah on the khāʾ, denotes character, nature, temperament, and disposition in early Arabic.

Abū ʿUbayd explains it through shīmah, ṭabīʿah, khuluq, and sajiyyah.

Ibn Fāris places it under dwelling and stability.

In his explanation, character is what human conduct rests upon and returns to. This is his analysis; Lisān al-ʿArab also transmits a possible Persian loan origin for khīm. The connection must not be treated as a proven historical derivation.

The explanation requires care.

A stable characteristic need not be unchangeable.

Someone may reform a longstanding habit through education, experience, or deliberate choice.

Khīm does not establish that every moral quality is fixed from the outset and incapable of change.

A relatively stable, recognizable disposition is foregrounded.

A poetic witness about disposition

A line attributed in early sources to Ḥātim al-Ṭāʾī, and elsewhere to Dhū al-Iṣbaʿ al-ʿAdwānī, says:

وَمَنْ يَبْتَدِعْ مَا لَيْسَ مِنْ خِيمِ نَفْسِهِ
يَدَعْهُ وَيَغْلِبْهُ عَلَى النَّفْسِ خِيمُهَا

wa-man yabtadiʿ mā laysa min khīmi nafsihi / yadaʿhu wa-yaghlibhu ʿalā al-nafsi khīmuhā

Translation:

“Whoever adopts conduct outside their established disposition will abandon it, and their longstanding nature will prevail over them.”

Khīm concerns personal disposition and nature.

The poet contrasts temporary conduct with a stable characteristic.

This is a poetic observation or judgment, rather than a definitive law making moral reform impossible.

Attribution also varies. It should be presented as an early attributed witness, rather than conclusively assigned to one poet without explanation.

Muḥāḍarāt al-Udabāʾ: attribution of the disposition witness to Dhū al-Iṣbaʿ

Khāma: retreat and fear

Khāma ʿan al-qitāl means retreating or holding back from action through fear and weakness.

Khāʾim is recorded for a fearful or retreating person.

Ibn Fāris connects this with remaining motionless, as though fear prevents advancing.

Ibn Sīdah proposes another connection: retreat resembles the folding back of a tent covering.

These are proposed semantic relationships, rather than proof of a historical pathway.

Poetry attributed to Junādah ibn ʿĀmir al-Hudhalī says:

لَعَمْرُكَ مَا وَنَى ابْنُ أَبِي أُنَيْسٍ
وَلَا خَامَ الْقِتَالَ وَلَا أَضَاعَا

la-ʿamruka mā wanā ibnu abī unaysin / wa-lā khāma al-qitāla wa-lā aḍāʿā

Translation of the sense:

“By your life, the son of Abū Unays did not falter, retreat from fighting, or fall short.”

Khāma concerns holding back or retreating before battle.

That meaning must not be transferred into Qur’anic al-khiyām, which names dwellings rather than describing fear or weakness.

Other uses: tender plants and unprocessed material

Arabic dictionaries also record khāmah for a plant’s fresh stem or growth.

Khām describes skin not yet tanned or material whose preparation is incomplete. Tāj al-ʿArūs also gives a Persian origin for khām in the sense of unprocessed material. Listing it under these consonants alone does not establish derivation from dwelling.

These uses are semantically distant from tents and dwellings.

Some might be connected through softness, natural condition, or incomplete preparation. Available evidence nevertheless does not establish a definitive historical origin for all of them.

Rawness, immaturity, and plant tenderness must therefore not enter the Qur’anic definition of al-khiyām.

They are distinct lexical uses recorded to show the material’s range.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: khīm, khām, and proposed origins

4. Qur’anic use: ḥūrun maqṣūrātun fī al-khiyām

حُورٌ مَقْصُورَاتٌ فِي الْخِيَامِ

ḥūrun maqṣūrātun fī al-khiyāmi

Current Forqan translation:

“fair ones with beautiful eyes, sheltered in tents.”

Al-Raḥmān 55:72

This is the root’s only Qur’anic occurrence.

Al-khiyām is a plural of khaymah, genitive after fī.

Fī al-khiyām concerns being in tents or covered dwellings.

Two other important words appear:

Ḥūr and maqṣūrāt.

Ḥūr belongs to Ḥ W R and here describes the heavenly ḥūr.

Maqṣūrāt belongs to Q Ṣ R and concerns being restricted, reserved, or kept.

The three components must therefore be distinguished.

Ḥūr identifies those described.

Maqṣūrāt describes their condition.

Fī al-khiyām identifies the associated location or space.

Neither ḥūr nor qaṣr can be derived from khiyām alone.

What does tent mean here?

In early Arabic, a khaymah is a covered dwelling.

Some uses concern wood and vegetation; others also call a fabric-covered structure khaymah.

The word could additionally designate a howdah or covered travel enclosure.

Al-Raḥmān 55:72, however, concerns heavenly gardens and their blessings.

Al-khiyām thus denotes the dwellings or covered spaces described within those gardens.

The use does not conclusively identify their material as fabric, branches, wood, or something else.

Nor does the word specify dimensions, door counts, geometry, or construction methods.

The more firmly established lexical meaning is a tent or shelter erected for dwelling.

Are the heavenly tents made of pearl?

Early exegetical sources contain reports describing heavenly tents as hollow pearls.

Some give dimensions of one or several farsakhs and large numbers of doors.

These reports must be distinguished from the Qur’anic text.

Al-Raḥmān 55:72 only says fī al-khiyām.

It contains neither luʾluʾ, pearl, nor dhahab, gold, nor numbers specifying tent dimensions and doors.

The surah mentions pearl in Al-Raḥmān 55:22 and ruby and coral in Al-Raḥmān 55:58. Their presence alone does not establish the material of the tents in verse 72.

Such reports can be recognized as external explanations, without incorporating them into the direct translation of al-khiyām.

This does not deny the possibility of tents unlike earthly tents.

Rather, their precise qualities are not determined by the word and verse themselves.

Does maqṣūrāt mean imprisoned?

This concerns Q Ṣ R rather than KH Y M, but brief discussion prevents narrowing the verse to a mistaken conception.

Early explanations interpret maqṣūrāt as kept within tents or reserved for their spouses.

Al-Ṭabarī reports both and seeks not to restrict the description to only one application.

Al-khiyām itself nevertheless means neither a prison nor a place of compulsory confinement.

A tent is a dwelling.

Someone may voluntarily remain in a tent, rest in it, or leave it.

Fī and al-khiyām alone therefore do not establish that the heavenly ḥūr are never allowed to leave.

Any such interpretation of maqṣūrāt requires independent study of Q Ṣ R and the surah’s context, rather than putting confinement inside the meaning of tent.

Nor does this heavenly description independently establish a general rule confining women to earthly homes.

It describes heavenly blessings rather than directly commanding rules for human social movement.

Al-Ṭabarī: maqṣūrāt, tents, and pearl reports

The tent as private, sheltered space

A tent could provide a covered space separated from the surrounding open environment in early Arab life.

Shelter, covering, and dedicated space consequently relate to its practical use alongside habitation.

The results of using a tent must nevertheless be distinguished from the word’s meaning.

A tent may provide some protection from sun, wind, or others’ gaze.

Lexically, however, it does not mean secrecy, imprisonment, or exclusive ownership.

This distinction also holds in Al-Raḥmān 55:72.

Fī al-khiyām identifies the dwelling space; maqṣūrāt is another description requiring separate understanding.

Connection with the surrounding verses

The context of Al-Raḥmān 55:62, Al-Raḥmān 55:63, Al-Raḥmān 55:64, Al-Raḥmān 55:65, Al-Raḥmān 55:66, Al-Raḥmān 55:67, Al-Raḥmān 55:68, Al-Raḥmān 55:69, Al-Raḥmān 55:70, Al-Raḥmān 55:71, Al-Raḥmān 55:72, Al-Raḥmān 55:73, Al-Raḥmān 55:74, Al-Raḥmān 55:75, and Al-Raḥmān 55:76 matters for understanding this use.

Al-Raḥmān 55:62 mentions two further gardens.

Subsequent verses describe their color and vegetation, springs, fruits, and blessings.

Al-Raḥmān 55:70 says:

فِيهِنَّ خَيْرَاتٌ حِسَانٌ

fīhinna khayrātun ḥisānun

That is, there are good and beautiful ones within them.

Al-Raḥmān 55:72 then mentions ḥūrun maqṣūrātun fī al-khiyām.

Al-Raḥmān 55:74 gives a further description of them.

The tents thus belong to the image of blessing and dwelling within heavenly gardens.

These verses themselves do not establish architectural plans, distances between tents and springs, or routes between them.

Nor does mentioning tents mean every heavenly dwelling is a tent and no other kind exists.

Elsewhere the Qur’an uses ghuraf, masākin, and other expressions concerning dwelling places.

Each must be understood in its own context.

5. Differences from related roots

KH Y M (khaymah) compared with B Y T (bayt)

Bayt denotes a house or dwelling.

Khaymah can also be a dwelling, but commonly concerns a shelter erected with a supporting structure and covering.

Some early lexicographers treat it as a kind of bayt; others distinguish plant-built tents from fabric houses or shelters.

They are therefore close within habitation, but not complete synonyms.

“Tents” better preserves the Arabic designation in Al-Raḥmān 55:72.

“Houses” conveys dwelling more generally, but obscures the distinction from other structures.

Study of B Y T

KH Y M (khaymah) compared with KH B ʾ (khibāʾ)

Khibāʾ concerns shelter and residential covering, made in certain familiar uses from fabric or wool.

Khaymah likewise denotes covered accommodation.

As the lexical reports show, some speakers distinguished a branch-built khaymah from a fabric khibāʾ.

Others did not maintain so strict a boundary.

No absolute rule should therefore make every khaymah plant-built and every khibāʾ fabric-built.

Precise distinctions depend on naming practices and context.

Study of KH B ʾ

KH Y M (takhyīm) compared with Q W M (iqāmah)

Iqāmah concerns standing, establishing, and remaining in a place.

Takhyīm can also concern dwelling.

It is commonly connected with pitching or occupying a tent, whereas iqāmah has a broader field.

Someone may reside in a stone house, a large city, or somewhere without a tent.

Takhyīm can thus be a kind of residence, but not every residence is necessarily takhyīm.

Nor must iqāmah always be translated as remaining forever.

Study of Q W M

KH Y M (khīm) compared with SH Y M (shīmah)

Shīmah denotes human character or disposition.

Early Arabic khīm also concerns this field.

Some dictionaries directly explain khīm through shīmah and sajiyyah.

Their closeness nevertheless does not make the roots identical.

SH Y M has its own lexical family; Qur’anic KH Y M concerns tents.

Disposition is a recorded meaning of khīm, but must not replace al-khiyām in Al-Raḥmān 55:72.

KH Y M (khaymah) compared with S K N (maskan)

S K N concerns resting and residing.

Maskan is a dwelling place.

A tent can be someone’s maskan, but not every maskan is a tent.

Conversely, a tent’s presence does not necessarily establish complete tranquility or absolute security.

It may stand in an unsafe environment, or its inhabitants may live in difficult circumstances.

Tranquility and security must therefore not enter the lexical definition without contextual evidence.

Al-Raḥmān 55:72 contains a setting of the Garden and its blessings, but those qualities come from the surrounding verses rather than KH Y M itself.

Study of S K N

KH Y M (khaymah) compared with Ẓ L L (ẓill)

Ẓill concerns shade; miẓallah can be an implement providing shade.

A tent may likewise be erected for protection from sunlight and to provide shade.

Its function is not limited to shade: it can provide residence and rest.

Nor does every shade arise from a tent. Trees, mountains, and buildings can also provide it.

Tent and shade are practically related, but not synonymous.

Study of Ẓ L L

KH Y M (khaymah) compared with S R D Q (surādiq)

Surādiq can denote a covering or broadly surrounding enclosure.

In Al-Kahf 18:29, surādiquhā describes the Fire.

It is conceptually connected to tents through covering and enclosing space, but differs in structure and usage.

Khaymah basically names an erected dwelling; surādiq can express a broader covering or enclosure.

Al-khiyām in Al-Raḥmān 55:72 should therefore not become surādiqs without evidence.

Study of S R D Q

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Qur’anic al-khiyām is clearly a plural for tents.

Its direct, natural Persian and Dari equivalent is khaymah-hā, “tents.”

The lexical explanation can add that this is a covered shelter or dwelling known in early Arabic with varied structures and materials.

Forqan’s current translation of Al-Raḥmān 55:72 is:

“fair ones with beautiful eyes, sheltered in tents.”

This retains al-khiyām through its direct equivalent.

The root study need not turn tents into pearl palaces, closed rooms, prisons, or permanent houses. None of those details follows from the word itself.

Maqṣūrāt also requires independent study under Q Ṣ R; its meaning must not be transferred into khiyām.

Brief root meaning

Dwelling and remaining in a place; in the noun’s main use, a tent as an erected shelter or dwelling.

Meaning in this verse

الْخِيَامِ — Al-Raḥmān 55:72: tents; covered dwellings in which the heavenly ḥūr are situated in this description.

This preserves the clear meaning without definitively specifying materials, shape, dimensions, or living conditions.

Conclusion

KH Y M concerns dwelling, remaining, and settling. Its clearest instance is the tent: a shelter someone erects and occupies.

Early poetic witnesses show tents as familiar in nomadic life. Tent sites, remaining supports, and coverings associated with travel and howdahs all occur within the family’s uses.

Lexical sources differ on materials and form. Some restrict khaymah to wood and vegetation, while others also accept fabric tents. Its basic meaning must therefore not be confined to one building material.

Khīm meaning disposition and khāma meaning retreat are also recorded under this family, but do not occur in the Qur’an and must not be added to al-khiyām.

The root appears only in Al-Raḥmān 55:72:

حُورٌ مَقْصُورَاتٌ فِي الْخِيَامِ

ḥūrun maqṣūrātun fī al-khiyāmi

Khiyām is a plural for tents, naming covered dwellings.

It does not itself establish the exact materials, dimensions, or structure of heavenly tents. Nor does a tent’s presence alone imply imprisonment or a ban on inhabitants leaving.

The fundamental field can therefore be expressed as dwelling and settling in a place, and Qur’anic al-khiyām as tents or covered dwellings.

A tent names a dwelling place. The inhabitants’ qualities, their degree of reservation or restriction, and the heavenly dwelling’s nature are separate matters established through other words and the verse’s context. Brief root meaning: Dwelling and remaining in a place; in the noun’s main use, a tent as an erected shelter or dwelling.
