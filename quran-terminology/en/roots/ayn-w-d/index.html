Returning, repetition, bringing back, and bringing into being again

1. The root in early Arabic

The Arabic root ʿ W D denotes returning to something, doing something again, the recurrence of a condition, or repetition after an initial occurrence.

ʿĀda means “returned,” and yaʿūdu “returns.” This may be spatial, as when someone comes home again, or behavioral, as when a discontinued action is resumed. Something may also return to its earlier condition after changing.

In early Arabic, ʿawd was closely related to badʾ, beginning: an action begins and is performed again, or something leaves a condition and returns to it.

This semantic field is also attested in poetry attributed to the period before the Quran.

Witnesses from pre-Islamic poetry

The Muallaqah attributed to Antarah ibn Shaddad describes a male ostrich tending its eggs:

صَعْلٍ يَعُودُ بِذِي الْعُشَيْرَةِ بَيْضَهُ
كَالْعَبْدِ ذِي الْفَرْوِ الطَّوِيلِ الْأَصْلَمِ

ṣaʿlin yaʿūdu bi-dhī al-ʿushayrati bayḍahu / ka-l-ʿabdi dhī al-farwi al-ṭawīli al-aṣlam

Translation: A small-headed male ostrich revisiting its eggs at Dhu al-Ushayrah, like an earless slave wearing a long fur garment.

Yaʿūdu here concerns returning or visiting the eggs again. The ostrich returns to their location to tend them. The witness illustrates revisiting and attending to something, without the subject being return from travel or a change of belief.

Another verse is attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Ubayd ibn al-Abras:

أَقْفَرَ مِنْ أَهْلِهِ عَبِيدُ
فَالْيَوْمَ لَا يُبْدِي وَلَا يُعِيدُ

aqfara min ahlihi ʿabīdu / fa-l-yawma lā yubdī wa-lā yuʿīd

Translation: Ubayd is deserted by his people and left alone; today he neither begins nor does anything again.

The verse is transmitted in accounts of the poet's final day and attributed to him in early sources and his collected poems, although its written transmission belongs to later periods.

The contrast between yubdī and yuʿīdu matters: the first concerns beginning, the second doing again or resuming. In the transmitted context, the whole expression can also convey helplessness or impending destruction; it need not refer exclusively to speech. The contrast between beginning and renewal also appears in Quranic creation passages.

Returning and repetition are therefore not restricted to Quranic usage. Early witnesses attributed to pre-Islamic poets illustrate revisiting, attending to something, and resuming an action; attribution and the precise date of a transmitted verse remain distinct questions.

Two distinct branches within ʿ W D

Alongside ʿawd, return, Arabic dictionaries record ʿūd, wood or a branch.

Despite sharing their root letters, the two forms do not have the same meaning:

ʿAwd concerns returning or repetition.

ʿŪd denotes wood or a tree branch.

ʿŪd also names fragrant wood and a musical instrument. Other uses of the family concern an aged animal or an old road.

Shared letters alone cannot establish that “return” arose from “wood,” or the reverse. The return and restoration branch is central to Quranic study because the Quranic verbs belong to that field.

2. Forms and the semantic core

Principal Quranic forms include:

  • ʿĀda, yaʿūdu, taʿūdūna, naʿūda, ʿudnā, and naʿud: returning, repeating an action, or coming back to a condition.
  • Yuʿīdu, nuʿīdu, sanuʿīdu, and uʿīdū: bringing back, bringing into being again, or being brought back.
  • ʿĀʾidūn: those who return or resume something.
  • ʿĪd: a day or occasion of gathering, celebration, or remembrance associated with recurrence.
  • Maʿād: a place, destination, or occasion of return.

Forms such as ʿawd, ʿawdah, ʿādah, iʿtiyād, muʿāwadah, taʿawwud, and ʿiyādah are also known in Arabic, but should not mistakenly be listed as forms occurring in the Quran.

ʿĀda is the simple verb and usually expresses the person's or thing's own return.

Aʿāda is Form IV and usually expresses bringing something back, repeating an act, or bringing something into being again. Returning and bringing back must therefore be distinguished.

The principal branch's semantic core can be defined as:

Coming again to something previously existing or experienced, resuming an action, or restoring something to a condition, location, or stage with which it has a prior relationship.

This does not require exact repetition of every detail. Someone may resume an action without all its original circumstances recurring. Something may regain a former condition without retracing the same process of change.

Understanding each derivative therefore requires identifying who or what returns, to what, and under what circumstances.

3. Quranic uses: returning to an earlier condition, behavior, or situation

Returning to a former condition

The Quran says of the moon:

وَالْقَمَرَ قَدَّرْنَاهُ مَنَازِلَ حَتَّىٰ عَادَ كَالْعُرْجُونِ الْقَدِيمِ

wa-l-qamara qaddarnāhu manāzila ḥattā ʿāda ka-l-ʿurjūni al-qadīm

Yā Sīn 36:39

We have appointed phases for the moon until it returns like an old, curved date-cluster stalk.

Yā Sīn 36:39

ʿĀda here concerns the moon's return to a condition in which, after passing through different phases, it again appears as a thin crescent.

Return does not mean backward movement in space. Through its phases, the moon's visible shape reaches a condition resembling an earlier stage.

The comparison ka-l-ʿurjūni al-qadīm explains its thin, curved shape. This comes from the simile, not the root's independent meaning.

The verse illustrates the recurrence of a condition within a natural process.

Resuming a behavior after leaving it

Al-Nur says:

يَعِظُكُمُ اللَّهُ أَنْ تَعُودُوا لِمِثْلِهِ أَبَدًا إِنْ كُنْتُمْ مُؤْمِنِينَ

yaʿiẓukumu allāhu an taʿūdū li-mithlihi abadan in kuntum muʾminīn

Al-Nūr 24:17

The God admonishes you never to return to anything like it, if you are believers.

Al-Nūr 24:17

The preceding verses concern spreading accusations and wrongful statements about others.

Taʿūdū li-mithlihi means returning to conduct of the same kind. Li-mithlihi matters: the warning covers similar behavior, not just repetition of that exact incident with the same people.

The return is behavioral repetition rather than spatial movement.

Returning to what was forbidden

بَلْ بَدَا لَهُمْ مَا كَانُوا يُخْفُونَ مِنْ قَبْلُ وَلَوْ رُدُّوا لَعَادُوا لِمَا نُهُوا عَنْهُ وَإِنَّهُمْ لَكَاذِبُونَ

bal badā lahum mā kānū yukhfūna min qablu wa-law ruddū la-ʿādū li-mā nuhū ʿanhu wa-innahum la-kādhibūn

Al-Anʿām 6:28

Rather, what they previously concealed has become apparent to them. If they were returned, they would surely resume what they were forbidden; indeed, they are liars.

Al-Anʿām 6:28

Two distinct roots appear together:

Ruddū belongs to R D D and expresses being returned.

La-ʿādū belongs to ʿ W D and expresses resuming earlier behavior.

Being restored to a situation and repeating past conduct are therefore distinct matters.

Someone may be brought back to a situation, while what they do there is a separate question. The verse states of this group that, if returned, they would resume the forbidden behavior.

Returning to riba after admonition

Al-Baqarah 2:275 contrasts two situations after declaring riba forbidden.

First comes someone who receives admonition from their Lord and desists; then someone who ʿāda, returns to it.

ʿAwd here concerns resuming previous conduct after encountering admonition.

ʿĀda does not itself define riba. The root expresses return; the verse identifies its object.

Al-Māʾidah 5:95 similarly discusses someone repeating the act after its ruling on hunting while in ihram.

In both cases, ʿawd concerns repetition, while the act, conditions of responsibility, and consequences come from each verse's rulings and qualifications.

Resuming wrongful behavior after warning

Al-Mujādilah 58:8 describes people forbidden certain secret conversation who nevertheless resume it.

Return here means repeating something previously forbidden.

Al-Mujādilah 58:3 also says thumma yaʿūdūna li-mā qālū, but its construction differs from verse eight. It concerns a statement about wives, followed by a ruling on that statement and renewed contact between spouses.

Two broad linguistic directions merit examination: returning to and repeating the statement, or reversing its effect and resuming the relationship limited by it. The second encompasses further explanations concerning intention, retaining the marriage, and resuming intimacy; these are not identical positions.

The root alone cannot settle the disagreement. Li-mā qālū, sentence order, and the ensuing ruling must be examined. Neither repetition of the words nor resumption of marital relations can be established from the root alone without the complete construction.

4. Returning to a religion, social condition, or earlier confrontation

Returning to a community's religion

The account of Shuayb says:

قَالَ الْمَلَأُ الَّذِينَ اسْتَكْبَرُوا مِنْ قَوْمِهِ لَنُخْرِجَنَّكَ يَا شُعَيْبُ وَالَّذِينَ آمَنُوا مَعَكَ مِنْ قَرْيَتِنَا أَوْ لَتَعُودُنَّ فِي مِلَّتِنَا قَالَ أَوَلَوْ كُنَّا كَارِهِينَ

qāla al-malaʾu alladhīna istakbarū min qawmihi la-nukhrijannaka yā shuʿaybu wa-alladhīna āmanū maʿaka min qaryatinā aw la-taʿūdunna fī millatinā qāla a-wa-law kunnā kārihīn

Al-Aʿrāf 7:88

The arrogant leaders of his people said: Shuayb, we will surely expel you and those who believe with you from our settlement, unless you return to our religion. He said: Even if we are unwilling?

Al-Aʿrāf 7:88

La-taʿūdunna fī millatinā here concerns returning to membership in or adherence to a religious community.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:89 also speaks, through Shuayb's response, of returning to their religion after deliverance from it.

The use of ʿawd alone does not establish that Shuayb personally once accepted all that community's beliefs.

The address includes Shuayb and his believing companions. Some companions may previously have followed that religion, with a collective address including everyone. In certain Arabic constructions, ʿāda can also express entering a condition rather than necessarily returning personally to a formerly held belief.

Ibrāhīm 14:13 likewise threatens the Messengers with expulsion or return to their opponents' religion.

Al-Kahf 18:20 uses Form IV: the opponents may compel the young men to return to their religion.

The distinction matters: one construction concerns people's return; the other their being brought back by others.

These verses concern social and religious movement, while each person's prior beliefs require separate evidence.

Repeated action and repeated consequences

عَسَىٰ رَبُّكُمْ أَنْ يَرْحَمَكُمْ وَإِنْ عُدْتُمْ عُدْنَا وَجَعَلْنَا جَهَنَّمَ لِلْكَافِرِينَ حَصِيرًا

ʿasā rabbukum an yarḥamakum wa-in ʿudtum ʿudnā wa-jaʿalnā jahannama li-l-kāfirīna ḥaṣīrā

Al-Isrāʾ 17:8

Your Lord may have mercy on you. If you return, We return; and We have made Hell an enclosing prison for the deniers.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:8

Both verbs in wa-in ʿudtum ʿudnā belong to ʿ W D.

Their objects differ: the addressees return to earlier behavior, while the second return concerns repeated divine response and punishment in the preceding context.

Sharing a root does not make the kinds of action identical.

Al-Anfāl 8:19 has a similar construction: renewed confrontation is met with a renewed response.

ʿAwd can therefore appear in a conditional relationship: if conduct recurs, the corresponding response recurs as well.

The root does not establish a fixed historical cycle or exact repetition of every event; such claims exceed its meaning.

5. Iʿādah: restoring and bringing into being again

Form IV aʿāda commonly means bringing something back, doing something anew, or bringing it into being again.

It is not restricted to reviving the dead. The Quran uses it for restoring an object, returning humans to the earth, placing them again in a situation, and renewed creation.

Restoring Moses' staff

قَالَ خُذْهَا وَلَا تَخَفْ سَنُعِيدُهَا سِيرَتَهَا الْأُولَىٰ

qāla khudhhā wa-lā takhaf sanuʿīduhā sīratahā al-ūlā

Ṭā Hā 20:21

He said: Take it and do not fear; We will restore it to its former condition.

Ta Ha 20:21

The preceding verses describe Moses' staff becoming a snake.

Sanuʿīduhā means “We will restore it”; sīratahā al-ūlā specifies its first condition.

Iʿādah here restores something to its prior condition rather than recreating humans or returning after death.

This is a particularly clear witness to iʿādah's range.

Returning humans to the earth

مِنْهَا خَلَقْنَاكُمْ وَفِيهَا نُعِيدُكُمْ وَمِنْهَا نُخْرِجُكُمْ تَارَةً أُخْرَىٰ

minhā khalaqnākum wa-fīhā nuʿīdukum wa-minhā nukhrijukum tāratan ukhrā

Ṭā Hā 20:55

From it We created you, into it We return you, and from it We bring you forth once more.

Ta Ha 20:55

Three different verbs occur:

Khalaqnākum expresses human creation from the earth.

Nuʿīdukum expresses returning humans to the earth.

Nukhrijukum expresses bringing them out again.

Nuʿīdukum should therefore not be equated with nukhrijukum.

Restoration here is to the earth; subsequent emergence is a separate event.

Nūḥ 71:18 follows the same sequence: return to the earth and then emergence from it.

Beginning creation and renewing it

اللَّهُ يَبْدَأُ الْخَلْقَ ثُمَّ يُعِيدُهُ ثُمَّ إِلَيْهِ تُرْجَعُونَ

allāhu yabdaʾu al-khalqa thumma yuʿīduhu thumma ilayhi turjaʿūn

Al-Rūm 30:11

The God begins creation, then brings it into being again; then you are returned to Him.

Al-Rūm 30:11

Two matters are distinguished:

First, yabdaʾu al-khalqa thumma yuʿīduhu: beginning creation and renewing it.

Then, ilayhi turjaʿūn: humans being returned to The God.

Yuʿīduhu belongs to ʿ W D, and turjaʿūna to R J ʿ. The verse thus uses different families for related aspects.

Renewed creation also appears in Yūnus 10:4, Yūnus 10:34, Al-Naml 27:64, Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:19, and Al-Rūm 30:27.

Here iʿādah concerns The God's power to bring life and creation into being again.

The root alone does not explain the bodily form, timing, or mechanism of renewed creation; those questions require the verses concerning resurrection, life, death, and emergence.

Iʿādah does not always mean renewed creation

Al-Isrāʾ 17:69 uses yuʿīdakum for bringing people back to the sea.

Al-Ḥajj 22:22 and Al-Sajdah 32:20 use passive uʿīdū for the people of the Fire being returned to it.

Restoration is therefore not restricted to one subject: it can concern a place, a previous condition, or renewed creation.

Beginning and renewal in contrast

إِنَّهُ هُوَ يُبْدِئُ وَيُعِيدُ

innahu huwa yubdiʾu wa-yuʿīd

Al-Buruj 85:13

Indeed, it is He who begins and brings into being again.

Al-Buruj 85:13

Yubdiʾu and yuʿīdu appear together, presenting beginning and renewal as related aspects of divine power.

Sabaʾ 34:49 uses the verbs negatively concerning falsehood: with truth's arrival, falsehood neither begins nor renews.

In context, this expresses falsehood's impotence and ineffectiveness; it should not be restricted to creating beings without contextual grounds.

The Quran clearly attributes yubdiʾu wa-yuʿīdu to The God, but the noun al-Muʿīd does not appear there as an independent divine name. That title is known in subsequent naming traditions; the Quranic form discussed here is a verb.

6. ʿĪd, maʿād, ʿāʾidūn, and the people named Ad

ʿĪd: an occasion whose remembrance returns

قَالَ عِيسَى ابْنُ مَرْيَمَ اللَّهُمَّ رَبَّنَا أَنْزِلْ عَلَيْنَا مَائِدَةً مِنَ السَّمَاءِ تَكُونُ لَنَا عِيدًا لِأَوَّلِنَا وَآخِرِنَا وَآيَةً مِنْكَ وَارْزُقْنَا وَأَنْتَ خَيْرُ الرَّازِقِينَ

qāla ʿīsā ibnu maryama allāhumma rabbanā anzil ʿalaynā māʾidatan mina al-samāʾi takūnu lanā ʿīdan li-awwalinā wa-ākhirinā wa-āyatan minka wa-urzuqnā wa-anta khayru al-rāziqīn

Al-Māʾidah 5:114

Jesus son of Mary said: O The God, our Lord, send down upon us a table spread from heaven to be a festival for the first and last of us and a sign from You. Provide for us; You are the best of providers.

Al-Māʾidah 5:114

ʿĪd belongs to this lexical family, with medial wāw changing to yāʾ in its formation.

Arabic uses ʿīd for a day or occasion of gathering, celebration, remembrance, or observance. Recurrence belongs to its familiar usage.

Jesus asks that the table spread be an ʿīd for the first and last members of his community.

The pairing of ʿīdan with wa-āyatan minka matters: festival describes the event's place in communal life and memory, while sign describes its relation to The God.

The word supports an occasion of celebration, gathering, and remembrance, but does not itself specify its calendar date, frequency, or rites.

Nor does ʿīd alone establish whether a ceremony is obligatory or forbidden; such rulings require separate commands and qualifications.

Maʿād: a place or destination of return

إِنَّ الَّذِي فَرَضَ عَلَيْكَ الْقُرْآنَ لَرَادُّكَ إِلَىٰ مَعَادٍ قُلْ رَبِّي أَعْلَمُ مَنْ جَاءَ بِالْهُدَىٰ وَمَنْ هُوَ فِي ضَلَالٍ مُبِينٍ

inna alladhī faraḍa ʿalayka al-qurʾāna la-rādduka ilā maʿādin qul rabbī aʿlamu man jāʾa bi-l-hudā wa-man huwa fī ḍalālin mubīn

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:85

The One who prescribed the Quran for you will surely bring you back to a destination of return. Say: My Lord knows best who brings guidance and who is in manifest error.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:85

Maʿād has a form capable of expressing a destination, place, or occasion of return.

La-rādduka belongs to R D D, while maʿād belongs to ʿ W D. La-rādduka is an active participle functioning predicatively, not a verb.

The construction expresses bringing the addressee back to a destination of return.

Arabic maʿād can concern a familiar place, a situation regained, or humanity's final destination. Its use for life after death and final return is also recognized.

The word maʿād itself is not a specific place name in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:85.

Interpretations include Muhammad's return to Mecca and final return in the Hereafter. The Mecca reading can be examined through the mission and separation from his birthplace, but Mecca is not named in this phrase. The final-return reading can likewise be examined through the word's range and return passages.

The lexical definition should therefore not begin by restricting maʿād to Mecca or Resurrection; the verse's intended reference requires contextual and Quranic evidence.

ʿĀʾidūn: those returning

إِنَّا كَاشِفُو الْعَذَابِ قَلِيلًا إِنَّكُمْ عَائِدُونَ

innā kāshifū al-ʿadhābi qalīlan innakum ʿāʾidūn

Al-Dukhān 44:15

We lift the punishment for a little while; surely you will return.

Al-Dukhān 44:15

ʿĀʾidūn is the plural active participle of ʿāda: those returning or those who return.

The preceding verses describe a plea for relief from punishment and a claim of belief, followed by the warning that they will return.

Returning to their former behavior and attitude toward truth is a clear, defensible contextual reading. The participle itself does not specify the object of return; the setting supplies it.

As elsewhere, ʿawd cannot be fully understood without identifying what the return concerns.

Ad: the proper name of a people

كَذَّبَتْ عَادٌ الْمُرْسَلِينَ

kadhdhabat ʿādun al-mursalīn

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:123

Ad denied the Messengers.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:123

Ad here is the proper name of a people.

It occurs in numerous Quranic verses, including Hud's story and accounts of his people's fate.

Its resemblance to the verb ʿāda does not justify translating the people's name as “those returning.”

A proper name can have its own naming history and origin. Placement under a root in some morphological indexes does not establish derivation from return.

Ad as a people's name should therefore be distinguished from the verb. Nor does ʿĀdan al-ūlā in Al-Najm 53:50 alone establish a definite history of several separate peoples bearing that name.

7. Neighboring roots and proposed translations

ʿ W D (ʿawd) compared with R J ʿ (rujūʿ)

Both belong to the field of return.

Quranic ʿ W D often emphasizes recurrence, a condition returning, or resumed behavior.

R J ʿ also has broad uses for return toward a reference point, direction, location, or condition.

The distinction is not absolute: both can express spatial or nonspatial return.

Al-Rūm 30:11 offers a clear contextual comparison: yuʿīduhu concerns renewed creation, and turjaʿūna concerns humans being returned to The God.

ʿ W D (ʿawd) compared with R D D (radd)

R D D often expresses bringing back, sending back, repelling, or restoring something to a place or condition.

ʿ W D can express the return itself or repeating an action.

Al-Anʿām 6:28 distinguishes ruddū and la-ʿādū: they are brought back, then resume earlier conduct.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:85, la-rādduka expresses bringing back, and maʿād the destination of return.

ʿ W D (ʿawd) compared with K R R (karr)

K R R also concerns return and repetition.

Some uses emphasize repeated movement, returning for renewed action, or another turn.

ʿ W D spans return, repeated conduct, restoration of a condition, and renewed creation.

These fields overlap, but each derivative's meaning must be determined from actual usage.

ʿ W D (ʿawd) compared with B D ʾ (badʾ)

B D ʾ concerns beginning and the appearance of a first stage.

The principal branch of ʿ W D concerns what occurs again after a beginning.

The contrast is clear in verses about beginning creation and renewing it.

Iʿādah alone does not specify the precise material details of restoration; any such claim requires contextual support.

Distinguishing similar roots

ʿ W D is distinct from ʿ D W. ʿĀdin meaning a transgressor and al-ʿādiyāt belong to ʿ D W, not ʿ W D.

Likewise, ʿīd from ʿ W D differs from waʿīd from W ʿ D. The former concerns an occasion or gathering; the latter belongs to promise and threat.

Similar spelling should not lead to merging their roots or meanings.

Proposed translations for Forqan

Depending on context, ʿāda can mean “returned,” “did again,” “resumed,” or “regained a former condition.”

Yaʿūdūna can mean “they return” or “they resume that action,” according to the object of return.

Aʿāda and its imperfect forms commonly mean “brought back,” “brought into being again,” “did anew,” or “restored to the previous condition.”

Uʿīdū is passive: “they were returned” or “they are returned,” not simply “they returned.”

ʿĀʾidūn means “those returning” or “those who return.”

Quranic ʿīd can be retained as “Eid” in Persian and rendered “festival” in English, with an explanation of gathering, celebration, and remembrance.

Maʿād can be “place of return,” “destination of return,” or contextually “final return.” Resurrection is a possible referent, not the invariant equivalent of every lexical use.

In creation passages, yuʿīdu al-khalqa can be rendered “brings creation into being again.” This conveys the meaning more naturally than cumbersome wording that merely mirrors the root's form.

8. Conclusion

The principal branch of ʿ W D denotes return and repetition, concerning a place, condition, behavior, religion, life stage, or recurring action.

Early Arabic witnesses attributed to pre-Islamic poets attest returning and doing again. The Quran applies this field to the moon's crescent, repeated conduct, religious affiliation, restoring Moses' staff, return to the earth, and renewed creation.

A major distinction is between ʿāda and aʿāda: the former generally expresses return or repetition itself, the latter bringing back or bringing into being again.

ʿĪd and maʿād share this field but have independent nominal uses: an occasion of gathering and remembrance, and a place or destination of return.

ʿŪd, wood, is a lexical branch that should not be made the historical source of return without evidence. Nor should the proper name Ad be interpreted as “those returning” merely because it resembles the verb.

Final definition of ʿ W D:

In its return branch, ʿ W D denotes coming again to a former place or condition, resuming a behavior or event, and restoring something to a state or stage related to the past. Form IV extends this to bringing back or bringing into being again. The kind and object of return, and its ethical or religious implications, are determined by the verse's construction and context rather than by the root alone.
