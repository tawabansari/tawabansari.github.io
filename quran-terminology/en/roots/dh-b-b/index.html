The fly; repelling and driving away; sharpness and the tips of objects; restless movement and going back and forth

Arabic DH B B has several branches. Dhubāb names the fly; dhabba ʿan concerns repelling harm and defending someone; dhubābu al-sayf denotes a sword’s tip or sharp part. Other uses concern restless movement, going back and forth, and not remaining settled.

The Qur’an uses this root only in dhubāb, the fly. Al-Ḥajj 22:73 names the fly twice to reveal the inability of those called upon apart from The God when measured against divine creative power. They cannot create even a fly, and if it takes something from them, they cannot recover it.

The meaning here is clear: a fly, a small winged creature. Understanding the lexical family nevertheless requires examining its other branches without replacing the animal’s name in the verse with defense, sharpness, or restlessness.

The root occurs twice in the Qur’an, both in Al-Ḥajj 22:73:

Dhubāban and al-dhubābu.

These are the same word, differing in definiteness and grammatical role rather than derivation or basic meaning.

Arabic uses dhubābah for an individual fly and dhubāb for flies as a kind, or an individual of that kind. Adhibbah and dhibbān are also attested plurals.

1. Semantic cores in early Arabic

Do all the meanings reduce to a single image?

One early lexical classification does not derive the whole family from a single meaning. It identifies three strands for the doubled root DH B B:

First, the small creature dhubāb and associated uses; second, sharpness and narrow or pointed parts of objects; third, restless movement and going back and forth.

Several meanings within one lexical family do not necessarily establish one as the definite historical origin of all the others.

Connections can be understood between a fly’s repeated movements, shooing it away, and restless motion. These similarities nevertheless do not establish the history of every word.

DH B B is therefore best treated as a family with several attested branches, each form examined first in its actual usage.

Dhubāb: the fly’s name

Dhubāb names a familiar insect found around human habitations, animals, plants, and food.

Early Arabic sometimes used the name more broadly than modern biological classifications, applying it to some other winged insects. Its usual meaning nevertheless remains the familiar fly.

In everyday life, a fly was a small, active, sometimes troublesome creature. People could shoo it from food, their bodies, or a place of rest; its quick movements and repeated return made repelling it a familiar activity.

Some verbal uses of the family have been explained against this background.

Smallness, nuisance, and repeated movement are descriptions of the animal. They do not replace the lexical meaning of dhubāb with “a nuisance” or “a restless creature.”

Dhabba: driving away and defending

Arabic uses these expressions:

Dhabba al-dhubāb: he shooed away the flies.

Dhabba ʿanhu: he defended him or warded harm off from him.

Study of D F ʿ

Dhabba ʿan ḥarīmihi: he defended his family, protected domain, or those under his protection.

In these uses, dhabba concerns repelling, preventing, and driving something away.

ʿAn matters in dhabba ʿanhu because it identifies the person from whom harm or aggression is being warded off.

Midhabbah names an implement used to shoo away flies: a fly whisk.

Early explanations connect these uses of repelling and defending with shooing away flies.

This does not establish that every Arabic use of defending necessarily arose from the image of shooing flies. The semantic connection is clear, but the historical direction of derivation requires independent evidence.

Defending with dhabba does not necessarily mean fighting or killing an opponent. Harm can be repelled in different ways, specified by context.

Sharpness and the tips of objects

Another branch describes sharp or terminal parts of objects.

Dhubābu al-sayf denotes the tip or sharp part of a sword.

Dhubābu asnāni al-ibil is also attested for the sharp edges of camels’ teeth.

Dhubābu al-ʿayn in some uses denotes the pupil or dark spot of the eye.

The word thus occurs beyond the insect’s name, designating parts of other things in particular combinations.

These meanings should not be transferred to Al-Ḥajj 22:73. Creating, taking something, and recovering it from the fly indicate the animal, rather than a sword’s tip or another part of an object.

Restless movement, dryness, and remainder

Dhabba yadhibbu is also attested for restless movement and not staying in one place.

Baʿīrun dhabb describes a camel that will not remain settled.

Dhabbat shafatuhu and dhabba al-nabāt also describe a lip drying or a plant withering.

Dhubābatu al-shayʾ can denote a small remainder, such as a little outstanding debt or residual thirst.

These meanings are attested, but their precise historical connections with the fly’s name or the verb of repelling are neither uniform nor certain.

One should not invent a sequence in which something first moved like a fly, then became sharp, then dried out, and finally became a small remainder. Without evidence, that is not sound etymology.

2. Pre-Qur’anic poetic witnesses

ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād: flies in a garden

The pre-Islamic poet ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād describes a green garden in his celebrated Muʿallaqah:

وَخَلَا الذُّبَابُ بِهَا فَلَيْسَ بِبَارِحٍ
غَرِدًا كَفِعْلِ الشَّارِبِ الْمُتَرَنِّمِ

wa-khalā al-dhubābu bihā fa-laysa bi-bāriḥin / gharidan ka-fiʿli al-shāribi al-mutarannimi

Translation: The flies have the garden to themselves and do not leave, sounding melodiously like a wine-drinker singing.

Al-dhubāb here denotes flies. The poet compares their buzzing with a person raising his voice in joy and intoxication.

The following verse compares a fly rubbing its front legs together with someone working with fire-making tools. One well-known explanation describes a person who has lost a hand or fingers and works the fire-making implement with his forearms. This is a literary comparison of the animal’s movement.

These images show that flies were familiar creatures in early Arabic poetry, with their behavior and movement available for literary description.

The fly here does not signify sin, inherent impurity, or a demonic force. The poet uses its presence and sound in a natural setting to enrich the image.

Its evaluative associations therefore depend on the setting and the speaker’s purpose.

Al-Muthaqqib al-ʿAbdī: a verse with two interpretations

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Muthaqqib al-ʿAbdī also states:

وَتَسْمَعُ لِلذُّبَابِ إِذَا تَغَنَّى
كَتَغْرِيدِ الْحَمَامِ عَلَى الْوُكُونِ

wa-tasmaʿu li-al-dhubābi idhā taghannā / ka-taghrīdi al-ḥamāmi ʿalā al-wukūni

Translation following the animal sense: You hear the fly when it sings, like the singing of doves upon their nesting places.

The verse is transmitted in his poetry and lexical accounts. Some versions have al-ghuṣūn instead of al-wukūn: branches rather than nesting places.

Dhubāb itself has also received different interpretations here. Some explain it as flies; others, given the ode’s description of a camel, understand the sharp parts of its teeth. Another version has al-nuyūb, “canine teeth,” in place of al-dhubāb.

The witness is useful for understanding the word’s range and variant uses, but should not be presented as undisputed evidence for a single meaning.

ʿAntarah’s description of buzzing and movement offers clearer evidence for the animal sense of dhubāb.

3. Qur’anic use: creating a fly and the inability of false objects of worship

The root’s only Qur’anic verse is Al-Ḥajj 22:73:

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّاسُ ضُرِبَ مَثَلٌ فَاسْتَمِعُوا لَهُ إِنَّ الَّذِينَ تَدْعُونَ مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ لَنْ يَخْلُقُوا ذُبَابًا وَلَوِ اجْتَمَعُوا لَهُ وَإِنْ يَسْلُبْهُمُ الذُّبَابُ شَيْئًا لَا يَسْتَنْقِذُوهُ مِنْهُ ضَعُفَ الطَّالِبُ وَالْمَطْلُوبُ

yā ayyuhā al-nāsu ḍuriba mathalun fa-stamiʿū lahu inna alladhīna tadʿūna min dūni allāhi lan yakhluqū dhubāban wa-lawi ijtamaʿū lahu wa-in yaslubhumu al-dhubābu shayʾan lā yastanqidhūhu minhu ḍaʿufa al-ṭālibu wa-al-maṭlūb

Translation: O people, a parable is presented, so listen to it. Those you call upon apart from The God will never create a fly, even if they gather for it. If the fly takes something from them, they cannot recover it from it. Weak are the seeker and the sought.

The two occurrences of dhubāb occupy different constructions.

Lan yakhluqū dhubāban: inability to create

In the first expression, dhubāban is an indefinite noun and the object of yakhluqū, “create.”

Those called upon apart from The God cannot create even a fly, however they gather for the task.

The choice of a fly matters. It is small compared with many living creatures and is not usually treated in everyday life as particularly impressive or majestic.

Smallness nevertheless does not make its life insignificant. The Qur’an uses this small creature to test a claim to creative power.

Wa-law ijtamaʿū lahu also prevents the inability being restricted to an isolated individual or force. Even collective effort by those called upon apart from The God cannot overcome it.

The subject is the inability of the invoked objects of worship in relation to divine creative power. It should not immediately be turned into a ruling about every future technology or the details of biological reproduction. The verse assesses beings that people place in the position of objects of worship.

Wa-in yaslubhumu al-dhubābu shayʾan: the fly as subject

In the second expression, al-dhubābu is definite and the subject of yaslubhumu, “takes from them.”

The fly is now the agent of taking, rather than the object of creating.

Wa-in yaslubhum is conditional: if the fly takes something from them, they cannot recover it from it.

The verse uses this possibility to reveal their inability. It need not be read as a report of one particular historical incident.

Shayʾan, “something,” is also indefinite and does not specify what is taken.

Some historical and interpretive accounts mention food, fragrant substances, or other things placed on stone objects of worship, which flies might take or consume.

These examples can make the image tangible, but the Qur’anic expression itself names no particular substance, food, or offering.

“If the fly takes something from them” is therefore more precise than a translation specifying the substance without the verse doing so.

Yastanqidhūhu belongs to N Q DH and concerns bringing something out or recovering it from the situation in question.

Study of N Q DH

Inability to recover what a fly takes reveals the weakness of false objects of worship from another angle.

The verse first presents their inability to create, then even their inability to recover something small.

Ḍaʿufa al-ṭālibu wa-al-maṭlūb: two sides of weakness

The verse concludes:

ضَعُفَ الطَّالِبُ وَالْمَطْلُوبُ

ḍaʿufa al-ṭālibu wa-al-maṭlūb

Translation: Weak are the seeker and the sought.

Early explanations offer several identifications of the seeker and the sought.

One reading identifies the seeker as the objects of worship unable to recover what the fly has taken, and the sought as the fly.

Another identifies the seeker as the worshipper asking something from a false object of worship, and the sought as that invoked object.

The first is more directly connected with the immediately preceding description of taking something and the failure to recover it.

Both readings nevertheless reveal weakness when measured against divine power.

The lexical point remains clear: al-dhubāb names the fly. Identifying the seeker and the sought concerns the parable’s complete construction, rather than a change in the animal name’s meaning.

The parable in the context of Al-Ḥajj

Al-Ḥajj 22:71 describes people worshipping apart from The God things for which they possess neither an authorizing proof nor knowledge establishing the worship’s validity.

After the fly parable in Al-Ḥajj 22:73, Al-Ḥajj 22:74 states that they have not esteemed The God as He deserves, although He is Strong and Invincible.

The parable is therefore more than an insect description. A small, familiar creature tests claims to power and worthiness of worship.

Apparent grandeur, social renown, or human attribution of sacred status does not itself give an object or being divine creative power and authority.

This conclusion comes from the verse and its context; dhubāb itself still means a fly.

4. Distinctions from related roots

DH B B (dhabba) and DH B DH B (dhabdhaba)

Study of DH B DH B

Dhabdhaba is a reduplicated four-consonant form associated with back-and-forth movement, oscillation, and instability.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:143 states:

مُذَبْذَبِينَ بَيْنَ ذَٰلِكَ لَا إِلَىٰ هَٰؤُلَاءِ وَلَا إِلَىٰ هَٰؤُلَاءِ

mudhabdhabīna bayna dhālika lā ilā hāʾulāʾi wa-lā ilā hāʾulāʾ

Translation: Wavering between the two, neither toward these nor toward those.

Qur’anic morphological classification places mudhabdhabīna under DH B DH B, rather than counting it among the two DH B B occurrences.

Lexical explanations connect dhabdhabah with restless movement in some DH B B forms. This does not make mudhabdhabīna another form of the fly’s name, or transfer wavering to dhubāb in Al-Ḥajj 22:73.

DH B B (dhabba) and DH B Ḥ (dhabaḥa)

Study of DH B Ḥ

DH B Ḥ is a separate root concerning slaughtering or cutting the throat.

Sharing two initial consonants does not make the roots identical.

Dhubāb names the fly, while dhabḥ concerns an action performed on a living creature.

No verb from DH B Ḥ occurs in Al-Ḥajj 22:73; the families should remain distinct.

Dhubāb, baʿūḍ, and naḥl: different insect names

The Qur’an also names baʿūḍah in Al-Baqarah 2:26 and naḥl in Al-Naḥl 16:68.

Study of B ʿ Ḍ; Study of N Ḥ L

Baʿūḍah concerns the mosquito and naḥl the honeybee. Although broad early Arabic uses sometimes applied dhubāb to other winged insects, the Qur’anic words require examination in their respective uses.

Without contextual evidence, dhubāb in Al-Ḥajj 22:73 should not become a mosquito or honeybee in translation. Its clear, usual equivalent is “fly.”

5. Translation proposals for Forqan

“Fly” is the most precise, natural equivalent for both dhubāban and al-dhubābu in Al-Ḥajj 22:73.

The first expression:

لَنْ يَخْلُقُوا ذُبَابًا

lan yakhluqū dhubābā

Translation: “They will never create a fly.”

The indefinite form makes an example of this creature the object of their inability to create.

The second expression:

وَإِنْ يَسْلُبْهُمُ الذُّبَابُ شَيْئًا

wa-in yaslubhumu al-dhubābu shayʾā

Translation: “And if the fly takes something from them.”

The definite form refers to the familiar kind of creature and functions as the subject.

There is no need to add “small and weak” to the first occurrence or “troublesome” to the second. These features may be discussed when explaining the parable, but are not separately written into the word.

“Defending,” “driving away,” “a sword’s tip,” and “restless movement” likewise do not belong in these two expressions.

Forqan’s current translation uses “fly” in both places, corresponding to Persian magas, and requires no change to that lexical choice.

6. Conclusion

Arabic DH B B has several branches: the fly’s name, repelling and defending, tips and sharp parts, and some uses concerning restless movement or small remainders.

Semantic connections have been proposed for some branches, such as shooing flies and dhabba, “repelling.” Historical evidence is nevertheless needed before deriving every meaning from a single image or origin.

Pre-Islamic poetic witnesses show that flies were familiar animals whose movement and sound contributed to literary description.

The Qur’an uses this root only in dhubāb, twice in Al-Ḥajj 22:73: first as the object of creating, then as the subject of taking something.

This small creature reveals the inability of those called upon apart from The God. They cannot create even a fly, and cannot recover something it takes from them.

Final definition: Dhubāb names the fly, a small winged insect. DH B B also has distinct uses concerning repelling, defending, sharpness, and restless movement, but only the animal sense of dhubāb occurs in the Qur’an.

Its meaning does not change in Al-Ḥajj 22:73. The construction and context make the fly a parable testing claims to power and revealing the inability of false objects of worship.
