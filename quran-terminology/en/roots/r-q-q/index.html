Thinness, delicacy, softness, and parchment

Brief root meaning: Thinness as opposed to thickness, and softness as opposed to coarseness and hardness. Extended uses include delicacy, tenderness, weakness, and reduced quantity. Qur’anic رَقّ names thin skin prepared for writing.

1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

The Arabic triliteral root ر ق ق is geminate: its second and third consonants are identical. The two qāfs are represented by a shaddah in forms such as رَقَّ and رَقّ.

Its principal field concerns thinness and softness. Something thin rather than thick is رقيق. The same word can describe a soft voice, gentle behavior, or a sensitive, tenderhearted person.

Thinness is not invariably positive. Thinning skin may prepare it for writing, whereas thinning, weakened bones indicate diminished strength. Tenderheartedness is also distinct from physical weakness. The precise meaning depends on what is being described as thin, soft, or weak.

The family occurs only once in the Qur’an, as رَقٍّ:

Al-Ṭūr 52:3 — فِي رَقٍّ مَنْشُورٍ

رَقّ here denotes a writing surface: in established Arabic usage, thin, prepared skin that bears writing.

Examined forms and Qur’anic derivatives

Important forms in Arabic include:

  • رَقَّ: Became thin or soft.
  • يَرِقُّ: Becomes thin or soft.
  • رِقَّة: Thinness, softness, delicacy.
  • رَقِيق: Thin, delicate, or soft; in a particular social use, an enslaved person.
  • رَقَّقَ: Made thin or soft.
  • أَرَقَّ: Made thin; in some constructions, became reduced in wealth or weakened in circumstances.
  • رُقَاق: Thin, flat bread.
  • رَقّ: Thin skin prepared for writing, or a writing sheet.
  • رُقُوق: Plural of رَقّ in the sense of writing skins.
  • رِقّ: In historical Arabic legal and social usage, enslavement.

Only رَقٍّ among these forms occurs in the Qur’an. Its Qur’anic frequency is limited, although its lexical field is broader.

2. Core meaning in early Arabic

Thinness in contrast to thickness

Early Arabic usage contrasts الرقيق with الغليظ and الثخين: thin as opposed to thick or bulky.

رَقَّ الشيءُ describes something becoming thin; رَقَّقَهُ describes someone making it thin.

This is clear with physical objects. Skin, cloth, bread, or a layer of material can be thin or thick. Thin skin concerns dimension and thickness, not its morality, feelings, or value.

رُقَاق also names thin, flat bread, illustrating the root’s role in everyday life, food names, and descriptions of material properties.

One central field of this family contrasts with coarseness and roughness. Its uses include soft ground, thin bread, weakened bones, and a writing surface.

Thinness and softness are semantically close but not always identical. Thinness concerns thickness; softness may concern texture, hardness, behavior, or response.

Tenderness in behavior and feeling

رِقَّة is not confined to material objects. رِقَّةُ القَلْب concerns tenderness, sensitivity, and freedom from hardheartedness.

رَقَقْتُ لَهُ can also mean feeling compassion or mercy toward someone.

This can be understood as a semantic extension from material softness to an emotional or ethical condition. Just as soft material responds differently to pressure than a hard body, a tenderhearted person is responsive to others’ suffering or circumstances.

This explanation does not prove the word’s historical development. One cannot establish that Arabs first understood thin skin and then created the expression for tenderness from that image. The evidence establishes both branches within the word family.

رقيق does not by itself mean a virtuous or just person. Sensitivity and tenderness may characterize someone, while the ethical value of their conduct requires other evidence.

Weakness or scantiness

In other uses, رَقَق concerns weak bones and reduced bodily strength.

Expressions such as رَقَّتْ عِظَامُهُ describe weakening bones. رَقَق is also recorded for scant wealth or reduced possessions.

Here thinness extends toward weakness or scarcity, but that extension should not define every derivative.

Thin bread is not necessarily weak or inferior. Thin skin may suit writing. A tenderhearted person does not necessarily lack strength of character.

Thinness, softness, scantiness, and weakness are thus related without being exact synonyms.

Other recorded branches

رَقاق describes soft, level ground, while رَقَّة, with fatḥah on the rāʾ, names land from which water has receded. It should be distinguished from رِقَّة, with kasrah, meaning softness.

Expressions such as تَرَقْرَقَ الدَّمْعُ and تَرَقْرَقَ السَّرَابُ concern the apparent movement, trembling, or shimmering of liquid or a mirage.

ترقرق has a repeated structure. Its inclusion in the same lexical discussion does not transfer all its features to Qur’anic رَقّ.

رَقّ is also recorded as the name of an aquatic animal, including a kind of turtle. Its historical relationship to thin skin or writing surfaces is unclear and should not be supplied through an invented derivation.

A lexical entry can therefore contain secondary branches and particular names that cannot all be derived from a single simple physical or moral definition.

3. Raqq in early Arab life and writing

Skin as a writing material

Before paper became widely available, prepared animal skin was a recognized writing material.

Arabic had several words for skin and writing materials, including أديم, قضيم, and رَقّ.

They were not exact synonyms. أديم often described tanned leather, while رَقّ was especially associated with thin skin prepared for writing. The precise boundaries between the names are not uniform across early witnesses.

What matters here is the clear association of رَقّ with thin skin usable as a writing surface.

It is no longer merely the adjective “thin,” but the name of a particular material or surface used to record writing.

A witness from Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd

A poem by the pre-Islamic poet Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd describes the traces of an old dwelling:

كَسُطُورِ الرَّقِّ رَقَّشَهُ
بِالضُّحَى مُرَقِّشٌ يَشِمُهُ

ka-suṭūri al-raqqi raqqashahu / bi-al-ḍuḥā muraqqishun yashimuhu

Translation: “Like lines on parchment, adorned in daylight by a maker of patterns who marks it.”

The remains of the dwelling are compared with written lines on skin.

The poet treats رَقّ as a familiar surface for writing and decoration. The comparison is intelligible because readers recognize the relationship between parchment and its recorded lines.

رَقَّشَهُ in the same verse belongs to a different root, ر ق ش, concerning marking or decoration. Similar spelling must not merge it with رَقّ.

A witness attributed to al-Mutalammis al-Ḍubaʿī

A verse attributed to another pre-Islamic poet, al-Mutalammis al-Ḍubaʿī, reads:

فَكَأَنَّمَا هِيَ مِنْ تَقَادُمِ عَهْدِهَا
رِقٌّ أُتِيحَ كِتَابُهَا مَسْطُورُ

fa-ka-annamā hiya min taqādumi ʿahdihā / riqqun utīḥa kitābuhā masṭūru

Approximate translation: “As though that place, through the passing of time, were parchment bearing writing set in lines.”

Here رق accompanies كتاب and مسطور and refers to a writing surface. Its vocalization varies between transmitted versions: both رَقّ and رِقّ appear.

The usual distinction is رَقّ, with fatḥah, for parchment and رِقّ, with kasrah, for enslavement. A less common kasrah pronunciation is also recorded for parchment. Meaning must therefore be identified from both the transmitted form and its context.

In the verse attributed to al-Mutalammis, كتاب and مسطور establish a writing skin or sheet, rather than enslavement.

These witnesses illustrate familiarity with writing on skin in poetry transmitted from pre-Islamic poets.

Riqq as enslavement

In a historical legal and social use, رِقّ denotes enslavement or the condition of a person treated as another’s property.

رَقيق can name an enslaved person; أَرِقّاء is its plural, and استرقاق concerns enslaving someone.

Some lexical explanations connect this branch with softness or weakness through a person’s subordination and lack of power under an owner.

That explanation should not be treated as certain historical etymology. Recording two uses in one family and proposing a semantic relationship does not establish their chronological order or the reason for naming.

رِقّ meaning enslavement does not occur in the Qur’an, and there is no linguistic basis for importing it into فِي رَقٍّ مَنْشُورٍ.

That verse concerns a book and its writing surface, rather than a person’s legal status.

4. Qur’anic usage: فِي رَقٍّ مَنْشُورٍ

Al-Ṭūr 52:2

Verse text:

وَكِتَابٍ مَسْطُورٍ

wa-kitābin masṭūrin

Al-Ṭūr 52:2

Translation:

“And by a book inscribed,”

Al-Ṭūr 52:3

Verse text:

فِي رَقٍّ مَنْشُورٍ

fī raqqin manshūrin

Al-Ṭūr 52:3

Translation:

“On parchment unfolded,”

The two verses form a continuous construction: an inscribed book on unfolded parchment.

Raqq in this verse

رَقّ names a writing material or surface. Its established meaning is thin skin prepared for writing.

This clearly relates to thinness. Thick, unprepared skin differs from a surface prepared for writing; its ability to receive writing and be used matters in preparation.

The word itself does not specify every preparation stage, processing method, or animal species.

“Skin prepared for writing” can therefore clarify the material, but should not be restricted to a particular kind of skin or technology.

“Unfolded parchment” also suits the verse concisely, since منشور describes its opened or spread-out state.

Kitāb, masṭūr, and raqq

Three words play different roles in these verses:

كِتَابٍ denotes a writing or book.

مَسْطُورٍ describes it as inscribed in lines.

رَقٍّ is the surface or material bearing the writing.

رَقّ itself does not mean writing, recording, or arranging lines. كتاب and مسطور supply those meanings in this construction.

Grammatically, فِي رَقٍّ is a prepositional phrase, commonly linked to مسطور: the book is inscribed on parchment. Another analysis treats it as an additional description of كتاب. Either way, it concerns the same inscribed book.

مَنْشُورٍ modifies رَقٍّ and shares its genitive case.

منشور belongs to ن ش ر, not ر ق ق. Opening or spreading comes from that adjective.

Manshūr: unfolded, not necessarily published

In modern Persian, “published” usually describes a book or text made available to others. English has a similar conventional use.

مَنْشُور here primarily concerns spreading or unfolding.

Opening a rolled skin or document exposes its written surface. رق منشور evokes an opened or spread-out writing surface.

Rendering it “published skin” would therefore mislead, suggesting modern printing or public distribution.

“Unfolded parchment” or “an unfolded writing skin” better preserves the image.

The description alone does not determine for whom, when, or how the book became accessible.

Which book is meant?

Al-Ṭūr opens with oaths by the mountain, an inscribed book, the inhabited house, the raised roof, and the sea described as مسجور. Al-Ṭūr 52:7 then states:

Verse text:

إِنَّ عَذَابَ رَبِّكَ لَوَاقِعٌ

inna ʿadhāba rabbika la-wāqiʿun

Al-Ṭūr 52:7

Translation:

“Your Lord’s punishment will surely occur.”

The inscribed book is thus one of the objects of the opening oaths.

Interpretations of its identity include revealed scriptures, the Torah, the Qur’an, or records of human deeds.

The mountain’s association with Moses invites consideration of his revelation. Elsewhere, passages about judgment also mention books and opened writings.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:13, for example, mentions a book a person finds open on resurrection day; Al-Takwīr 81:10 describes sheets being opened.

These support comparison of writing and unfolding imagery, but do not alone prove that the book in Al-Ṭūr 52:2 is necessarily a record of deeds.

Nor is رَقّ itself the name of the Torah, Qur’an, or any particular book.

The lexical meaning is therefore clearer than the book’s final identification: an inscribed book on an unfolded writing surface. Determining which book is meant requires broader contextual study.

Could raqq have a nonmaterial application?

Some interpretations associate this book with heavenly writings or realities beyond ordinary earthly books.

That discussion should remain distinct from lexical meaning.

Established Arabic usage gives رَقّ the meaning of a writing surface. Applying it here to a reality beyond material skin is an interpretive reading requiring independent contextual evidence.

Conversely, mentioning parchment does not establish that the book must possess every physical feature of the materials people use on earth. The verse uses a particular linguistic image without fully explaining the book’s ultimate physical nature.

The parchment image should therefore neither be erased nor used to import every material detail into the unseen without evidence.

5. Comparison with related roots

R Q Q (riqqah) and W R Q (waraq)

Their writing-related uses can overlap, but they are different roots.

The family و ر ق includes وَرَق for leaves and وَرِق for silver or silver money in the Qur’an. The vocalizations and senses should be distinguished.

Al-Anʿām 6:59 mentions a leaf, while وَرِق in Al-Kahf 18:19 concerns the money used by the cave companions.

Modern Persian ورق often also means paper or a page, but this modern Persian meaning should not be projected onto every Qur’anic Arabic use.

رَقّ in Al-Ṭūr 52:3 concerns skin or a prepared writing surface. Translators might sometimes render both words as a sheet or page, but this does not merge their roots.

R Q Q (riqqah) and R Q M (raqm)

رَقْم concerns marking, recording, and writing.

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:9 and Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:20 use كِتَابٌ مَرْقُومٌ: a book in which something is recorded or marked.

رَقّ in Al-Ṭūr 52:3 names the writing surface, rather than recording or marking itself.

A recorded text may be on parchment, but that possibility does not make the roots identical.

رَقّ denotes writing material or surface; رَقْم concerns the act or result of recording and marking.

This distinction matters especially in root study: similar letters must not transfer one root’s meaning to another.

R Q Q (riqqah) and Ṣ Ḥ F (ṣuḥuf)

صحيفة concerns a sheet or writing bearing recorded content; صحف is its plural.

Al-Takwīr 81:10 reads:

Verse text:

وَإِذَا الصُّحُفُ نُشِرَتْ

wa-idhā al-ṣuḥufu nushirat

Al-Takwīr 81:10

Translation:

“When the sheets are unfolded.”

The verse describes sheets being opened.

Al-Ṭūr 52:3 also uses منشور, but its subject is رق.

The shared unfolding image is useful for comparison, although صحيفة and رق are not lexically identical.

صحيفة emphasizes a sheet or writing as a recorded unit, while رق in its specific sense concerns the material or writing surface.

R Q Q (riqqah) and R Q B

ر ق ب is another root, with Qur’anic forms including رَقَبَة and رِقَاب.

In some passages these relate to freeing people from enslavement.

رقبة belongs to ر ق ب, not ر ق ق.

رِقّ meaning enslavement is established in Arabic but does not occur in the Qur’an.

The thematic relationship between enslavement and liberation, or shared letters, does not merge their morphological structures or lexical histories.

6. Interpretive challenges and translation proposals for Forqan

The main translation challenge is distinguishing the root’s general meaning from the particular word in the verse.

ر ق ق covers thinness, softness, and delicacy, but Al-Ṭūr 52:3 uses a specific name for writing material.

فِي رَقٍّ مَنْشُورٍ cannot therefore be rendered “in unfolded delicacy” or “in manifest softness.” These substitute the root’s general meaning for the derivative and erase the verse’s image.

“On an unfolded sheet” is broadly intelligible but can obscure the skin material and suggest ordinary paper to a modern reader.

“On unfolded parchment” more directly conveys the established image.

If more explanation is needed, the analysis can state:

“رَقّ named thin, prepared skin used for writing.”

This belongs in the article or lexical box, rather than placing every detail inside the brief verse translation.

Proposed Qur’anic translation

رَقٍّ — Al-Ṭūr 52:3

Primary equivalent: “skin prepared for writing.”

Brief equivalent: “parchment.”

With مَنْشُورٍ: “unfolded parchment” or “an unfolded writing skin.”

Proposed verse rendering:

“On parchment unfolded,”

This preserves the image of the existing Forqan Persian rendering; the installed verse translation can remain unchanged.

Proposed Forqan word box

Word: رَقٍّ

Root: R Q Q

Brief root meaning: Thinness and softness, contrasted with thickness and coarseness.

Meaning in this verse: Thin, prepared writing skin bearing the book’s inscription.

The full article can discuss other forms such as رقيق, رقة, and رقاق, while the verse box should remain brief and specific to this use.

7. Conclusion and final definition

ر ق ق forms a semantic family concerning thinness and softness. These may describe material thickness and texture, human behavior, tenderness, or reduced strength and quantity.

In early Arabic, thin skin prepared for writing was called رَقّ. Poetry transmitted from pre-Islamic poets attests familiarity with this material and comparisons between physical traces and writing on skin.

Other Arabic uses include رِقّ for enslavement and expressions of weakness or scarcity. They should not be transferred to the Qur’anic word without evidence.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an: رَقٍّ in Al-Ṭūr 52:3.

There it names a writing surface; مسطور describes inscription in lines, while منشور describes unfolding. The identity of the book invoked in the oath is not determined by رق itself.

Final definition of ر ق ق:

The root principally concerns thinness and softness as opposed to thickness, bulk, and coarseness. Extended uses describe gentle conduct, tenderness, weakness, or scantiness. In writing, رَقّ denotes thin skin prepared for inscription.

Its particular Qur’anic sense is parchment, rather than moral tenderness, weakness, enslavement, or the act of writing.

The concise rendering “on unfolded parchment” preserves the verse’s meaning. Root study places this name within the wider field of thinness and softness without reducing every branch to a single meaning.
