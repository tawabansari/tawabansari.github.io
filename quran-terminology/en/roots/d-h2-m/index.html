Blackness and darkness, covering and overwhelming: from dark color to deep green vegetation

Arabic D H M concerns blackness, darkness and overwhelming. It has been used for the color of horses and camels, night’s darkness, dark vegetation, crowds and events that suddenly befall someone.

In the Qur’an the root occurs only once, as mudhāmmatāni in Al-Raḥmān 55:64. It describes two gardens whose green is so deep and dark that it approaches blackness.

D H M is not generally confined to greenness. Blackness and darkness are central to this lexical family; applied to plants or gardens, however, the quality may express an intense, deep green.

The only Qur’anic form is mudhāmmatāni. Dahama, duhmah, adham, dahmāʾ, idhamma, idhāmma and duhm are known in Arabic, but these forms themselves do not occur in the Qur’an.

1. The semantic core

Blackness and darkness

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ records:

الدُّهْمَةُ: السَّوَادُ

al-duhmatu: al-sawādu

That is, duhmah means blackness.

Adham describes something black or very dark. Arabic uses farasun adhamu for a black horse and nāqatun dahmāʾu for a dark-colored she-camel.

In descriptions of animals, the word has also covered shades of very dark color, without invariably being limited to pure black.

This matters because Arabic color terms, like those in many other languages, do not always follow the precise boundaries of modern color classification. A very dark green may be described as approaching black without actually ceasing to be green.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Al-Muḥkam: blackness and animal colors

Covering and overwhelming

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris describes the root’s core as:

أَصْلٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى غِشْيَانِ الشَّيْءِ فِي ظَلَامٍ

aṣlun yadullu ʿalā ghishyāni al-shayʾi fī ẓalāmin

That is, a principle denoting something’s being covered in darkness.

His analysis seeks a connection between dark color and uses concerning overwhelming or assault.

When night’s darkness spreads, things are covered by it. Likewise, when a large body of people or riders descends upon a place, they can be said to overwhelm it.

This semantic connection should not become an unproven historical claim. Ibn Fāris’s explanation alone does not establish that every meaning necessarily developed from an original image of darkness.

Arabic records at least three clear branches: blackness and darkness, overwhelming and assault, and crowds. Their connections are intelligible, but usage and context determine the precise sense of each form.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: covering in darkness

2. Early Arabic uses

A black horse: a verse attributed to ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād

A verse attributed to the celebrated pre-Islamic poet ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād reads:

تُمْسِي وَتُصْبِحُ فَوْقَ ظَهْرِ حَشِيَّةٍ
وَأَبِيتُ فَوْقَ سَرَاةِ أَدْهَمَ مُلْجَمِ

tumsī wa-tuṣbiḥu fawqa ẓahri ḥashiyyatin / wa-abītu fawqa sarāti adhama muljami

Translation: “She [ʿAblah] spends evening and morning upon a soft mattress, while I pass the night upon the back of a bridled black horse.”

Adham describes the horse, referring to its dark or black color.

The poet contrasts the comfort of a person on a soft bed with his own life on a horse ready for movement and battle.

The verse appears in Sharḥ al-Qaṣāʾid al-Sabʿ al-Ṭiwāl al-Jāhiliyyāt. That source also records a reading of the second half-line with ajrad instead of adham.

Adham is therefore an established transmitted reading; the variant should nevertheless be considered when evaluating the witness.

The color sense is clear here. Assault, abundance and calamity do not follow from adham itself.

Ibn Al-Anbārī’s commentary: ʿAblah, the black horse and the ajrad variant

Assault and overwhelming: a verse attributed to Bishr ibn Abī Khāzim

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Bishr ibn Abī Khāzim reads:

فَدَهَمْتُهُمْ دَهْمًا بِكُلِّ طِمِرَّةٍ
وَمُقَطِّعٍ حَلَقَ الرِّحَالَةِ مِرْجَمِ

fa-dahamtuhum dahman bi-kulli ṭimirratin / wa-muqaṭṭiʿin ḥalaqa al-riḥālati mirjami

Translation: “I swept down upon them with every nimble mare and every mighty horse whose leaps break the saddle’s rings and whose hooves pound the ground.” Ṭimirrah denotes an agile mare; muqaṭṭiʿ ḥalaq al-riḥālah describes the force of the horse’s leaps, and mirjam its pounding of the ground with its hooves. Dictionaries transmit fa-dahamtuhum; Sharḥ al-Mufaḍḍaliyyāt also reports fa-dahimnahum, with the horses as subject.

Dahamtuhum does not describe black color here. It concerns overwhelming a group in an assault.

In this setting, dahama goes beyond color description to express an action in which something or a group comes upon another and overwhelms it.

Arabic dahamahumu al-amru also describes an event that suddenly befalls a group.

Suddenness may be understood from the event and its context. Not every use of the root, however, includes surprise or assault: adham as a color description does not.

Sharḥ al-Mufaḍḍaliyyāt: Bishr’s verse and the saddle rings

A large crowd

Arabic al-dahm is also used for a large body of people.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn uses al-dahm for a numerous group and dahamūnā for people arriving all at once in a body.

Jayshun dahmun is also recorded for a numerous army.

The focus is on numbers and the crowd’s pervasive presence, not necessarily its members’ color.

An image of filling the field of vision or covering a place may explain a semantic connection with other branches. It should not be presented as the proven historical origin of the crowd sense.

The darkness of night

Arabic dictionaries also record al-dahmāʾ for a very dark night.

In some expressions concerning the lunar month, forms of this root describe its final nights because of their greater darkness.

This is close to the color and darkness branch. Duhmah may describe a dark environment as well as the color of physical objects.

Hardship and calamity

Al-duhaymāʾ and al-duhaym occur in some Arabic uses for a calamity or a great, difficult event.

Lexical sources offer two explanations: a connection with darkness, and an association of certain proverbial expressions with a story about a she-camel named Al-Duhaym.

In the latter account, the camel was associated with a terrible event, and its name became proverbial for calamity.

The accounts differ in detail. Without independent examination, they cannot establish the origin of every use of duhaym.

Nor should calamity be generalized to every form of the root. Dahmāʾ, describing a night or animal, need not imply anything unfortunate or morally charged.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: assault, crowd, night and the Duhaym account

3. Dahm and dark vegetation

An important use of the root describes deeply green, well-watered vegetation.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn records:

وادْهَامَّ الزَّرْعُ، إذا عَلَاهُ السَّوَادُ رِيًّا

wa-idhāmma al-zarʿu idhā ʿalāhu al-sawādu riyyan

That is, a crop is described as idhāmma when its color becomes dark through being well watered.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise records ḥadīqatun dahmāʾu mudhāmmatun for a garden whose green is so deep and dark that it approaches blackness.

This use is crucial to understanding the Qur’anic word.

Thriving, well-watered plants may develop a very dark green color. Arabic could describe such darkness with the D H M family.

Applied to a plant or garden, mudhāmm therefore need not mean blackening through loss of greenness. It may express green so intense that it appears nearly black.

The distinction between ordinary blackness and very dark green follows from established uses, without inventing a new meaning for the root.

Al-ʿAyn: well-watered crops and dark green

Does mudhāmm directly mean dense vegetation?

Abundant vegetation may make a garden look green and dark. This does not establish that mudhāmm directly and necessarily means “having very dense vegetation.”

In descriptions of a garden, the central meaning concerns color and the darkness of its green.

Abundant growth, water and freshness can explain the appearance, but the number of trees, density of branches and precise quantity of water are not independent components of the adjective’s meaning.

This distinction especially matters in translating the Qur’an.

4. The Qur’anic occurrence: Al-Raḥmān 55:64

مُدْهَامَّتَانِ

mudhāmmatāni

Translation: Both have become deep, dark green.

The verse continues a description of heavenly gardens.

Al-Raḥmān 55:62 reads:

وَمِنْ دُونِهِمَا جَنَّتَانِ

wa-min dūnihimā jannatāni

Translation: And besides those two, there are two other gardens.

After the address to humans and jinn in Al-Raḥmān 55:63, verse 64 consists of a single word: mudhāmmatāni.

It describes those two gardens.

The morphology of mudhāmmatāni

Mudhāmmatāni is the feminine dual active participle of idhāmma.

Idhāmma follows ifʿālla, commonly designated Form XI in Arabic morphology. This pattern occurs particularly in certain verbs concerning color and visible condition.

Its masculine singular participle is mudhāmm, and its feminine singular is mudhāmmah.

The Qur’an uses the feminine dual mudhāmmatāni because the description concerns two gardens.

The word is nominative and refers syntactically to jannatāni in Al-Raḥmān 55:62. Fa-bi-ayyi ālāʾi rabbikumā tukadhdhibāni intervenes between the noun and its description. It may be analyzed as a delayed attribute of jannatāni; another grammatical analysis treats it as the predicate of an omitted subject, humā mudhāmmatāni. Both analyses refer the description to the same two gardens.

Verse syntax: description or predicate referring to the two gardens

The verses are thus connected: there are two further gardens, and both are very dark green.

Idhamma and idhāmma

Both idhamma and idhāmma are recorded in Arabic.

Idhamma follows ifʿalla, Form IX, and concerns becoming black or dark. Its verbal noun is idhimām.

Idhāmma follows ifʿālla, Form XI, and its recorded verbal noun is idhīmām.

Both can concern darkening, but their morphological patterns differ.

The Qur’anic form derives from idhāmma, not idhamma.

The pattern can express a change or establishment of a color condition. It cannot by itself quantify the darkness, water supply or density of vegetation.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: nominative feminine dual Form XI active participle

Are the two gardens black or green?

The question arises from the difference between the root’s general sense and its particular use of a garden.

Duhmah generally means blackness, but Arabic idhāmma al-zarʿu describes plants whose green has become very dark through ample watering.

Explaining Al-Raḥmān 55:64, Al-Ṭabarī writes that the gardens are blackish through the intensity of their green.

He transmits early lexical and exegetical explanations, some giving khaḍrāwāni, green, and others muswāddatāni, blackened.

These explanations are not necessarily contradictory in this context.

Khaḍrāwāni identifies the gardens’ vegetative color; muswāddatāni emphasizes that color’s darkness.

The description thus concerns gardens of exceptionally deep, dark green, rather than a black color unrelated to their vegetation.

Is ample watering part of the word itself?

Lexical explanations often associate idhāmma al-zarʿu with riyy, being well watered.

Exegetical reports likewise connect the gardens’ dark green with abundant water and flourishing plants.

The context supports this connection: Al-Raḥmān 55:66 mentions two gushing springs in the same gardens.

Direct meaning should nevertheless be distinguished from background explanation.

Mudhāmmatāni itself describes the gardens’ color and appearance. Ample watering may explain that color, but the word alone specifies neither the quantity of water nor the method of watering.

A direct translation can therefore say “Both have become deep, dark green,” while the study explains the Arabic association with fresh, well-watered vegetation.

Al-Ṭabarī: dark green, ample watering and transmitted explanations

The relation to the preceding gardens

Al-Raḥmān 55:46 mentions two gardens, and Al-Raḥmān 55:62 introduces two further gardens.

Al-Raḥmān 55:48 describes the first pair as dhawātā afnānin, whereas the later gardens are described as mudhāmmatāni.

The two sets are thus described through different qualities.

D H M alone does not establish that the second pair must be higher or lower in value, rank or blessings than the first.

Such a judgment requires consideration of the whole passage, min dūnihimā and the gardens’ other descriptions.

Lexical research should clarify the condition described by mudhāmmatāni, rather than derive a hierarchy of heavenly ranks from dark vegetation alone.

5. Important forms distinguished

Duhmah and adham

Duhmah is a noun denoting blackness or darkness.

Adham describes something with that color, such as a black horse.

These forms clearly express color; they need not also imply assault, calamity or abundance.

Dahmāʾ

Dahmāʾ is the feminine of adham, used of an animal or a dark night.

In certain expressions it also denotes a large crowd, a blackened cooking pot or a calamity.

Forms in one family may thus develop several uses without all those meanings being present in every construction.

Dahama and dahima

In expressions such as dahamahumu al-amru or dahamathumu al-khaylu, these verbs concern overwhelming, an event’s befalling someone, or an assault.

Their difference from the color adjective adham is clear: one expresses an action or occurrence, the other a visible quality.

Neither “black” nor “assault” can consequently serve interchangeably for every use of the root.

Idhāmma and mudhāmm

Idhāmma concerns darkening; applied to vegetation, it can express becoming very dark green.

Mudhāmm describes something in this condition.

The Qur’an uses the feminine dual of this participle for the two gardens.

6. Comparison with nearby roots

D H M (dahama) and S W D (sawida)

S W D concerns blackness and becoming black.

D H M also has a clear branch of blackness and darkness, alongside recorded uses concerning overwhelming, assault and crowds.

In color descriptions the roots may overlap: both adham and aswad can describe black color.

Applied to vegetation, however, D H M may express very dark green. Translating mudhāmmatāni simply as “two black gardens” may therefore give readers the wrong image without contextual explanation.

The breadth of dahama in assault contexts likewise does not make every S W D and D H M form synonymous.

D H M (dahama) and KH Ḍ R (khaḍira)

KH Ḍ R concerns greenness and appears in the Qur’an in descriptions of plants, clothing and certain blessings.

Applied to a garden, D H M expresses more than green color: it emphasizes its depth and darkness.

Khaḍrāʾ and mudhāmmah may describe a similar reality in a garden, but their semantic precision differs.

Khaḍrāʾ expresses green; mudhāmmah can describe a green so dark that it appears nearly black.

This matters for Al-Raḥmān 55:64: “two green gardens” loses a significant part of mudhāmmatāni’s particular quality.

Study of KH Ḍ R

D H M (dahama) and GH SH W (ghashiya)

In verbal uses such as ghashiya, GH SH W concerns covering and overwhelming.

This approaches the dahama branch concerning assault or an event’s befalling someone.

Ghashiya, however, covers a wider field of covering and overwhelming, without necessarily implying darkness or suddenness.

Dahama, in some uses, may particularly emphasize an overwhelming or sudden arrival.

The roots can therefore be close in certain sentences without being fully synonymous.

7. Challenges of understanding and translation

One possible error is confining mudhāmmatāni to “two black gardens.”

This attends to the root’s central darkness branch but does not adequately convey its established use of plants and gardens.

Conversely, “two green gardens” is insufficient because it weakens the emphasized depth of color.

Expressions such as “two gardens with dense trees,” “two thoroughly watered gardens” or “two gardens full of flourishing plants” may fit the broader image, but mix the adjective’s direct meaning with explanations of its cause or result.

Mudhāmmatāni primarily describes the gardens’ color and appearance.

Darkness here should not automatically be associated with grief, death, corruption or lack of light. It describes gardens and vegetation and concerns a deep green.

A color quality that may evoke darkness or calamity elsewhere here describes the gardens’ beauty and greenness.

The value and connotations of each use follow from the word together with its context, not from a different branch of the root.

8. Translation proposal for Forqan

For the Qur’anic word:

مُدْهَامَّتَانِ

mudhāmmatāni

The preferred rendering is:

“Both have become deep, dark green.”

“Both are very dark green” is also accurate and clear.

“Both gardens have become deep, dark green” suits a standalone explanation. A close translation can omit “gardens,” since the preceding verses establish the dual referent.

By contrast, “both are black” conveys part of the color sense but may suggest black gardens unrelated to greenness.

“Both are completely verdant” gives the general sense while omitting the darkness.

A rendering preserving both green and its darkness is therefore more suitable for Forqan.

9. Conclusion

Arabic D H M concerns blackness, darkness and overwhelming. Its family describes dark animal colors, night’s darkness, assault and sudden events, crowds, and very dark green vegetation.

These uses have semantic connections, but should not all be reduced to one restricted definition or unproven historical image.

The sole Qur’anic form is mudhāmmatāni, the feminine dual active participle of idhāmma. In Al-Raḥmān 55:64 it describes two gardens with exceptionally deep, dark green vegetation.

Short definition: D H M is a family of meanings concerning blackness and darkness, as well as overwhelming and assault. Applied to plants and gardens, it denotes very dark green approaching black. Brief root meaning: Blackness and darkness, overwhelming and assault; of a garden, very dark green approaching black.

The preferred Qur’anic sense of mudhāmmatāni is therefore “both have become deep, dark green,” rather than simply “two black gardens” or merely “two green gardens.”

Here darkness is not a sign of destruction or withering. It is the color of the gardens’ vegetation, associated in Arabic with deep green and ample watering.
