1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

The three-consonant Arabic root TH Q F concerns grasping something correctly, skill and agility in attaining it, and finding or gaining control of a person. In early usage, this family of words also describes straightening a spear and correcting its crookedness.

All Qur’anic occurrences belong to the verb ثَقِفَ and its inflected forms. In the verses it chiefly concerns finding, reaching, or gaining the upper hand over people. There is no direct Qur’anic use of thaqāfah meaning skill, knowledge, or culture.

TH Q F occurs six times in the Qur’an, in six verses across six surahs. All occurrences are Form I verbs.

Important forms of this family include:

  • ثَقِفَ / يَثْقَفُ (thaqifa / yathqafu): to grasp, gain access to something or someone, and in appropriate circumstances gain the upper hand over them. Qur’anic: yes, in various inflected forms.
  • ثَقِفْتُمُوهُمْ (thaqiftumūhum): you found or gained access to them. Qur’anic: yes, in Al-Baqarah 2:191 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:91.
  • ثُقِفُوا (thuqifū): they were found, brought within reach, or gained access to. Qur’anic: yes, in Āl ʿImrān 3:112 and Al-Aḥzāb 33:61.
  • تَثْقَفَنَّهُمْ (tathqafannahum): you gain access to them or the upper hand over them, with emphatic nūn. Qur’anic: yes, in Al-Anfāl 8:57.
  • يَثْقَفُوكُمْ (yathqafūkum): they gain access to you or the upper hand over you. Qur’anic: yes, in Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:2.
  • ثَقُفَ / ثَقِفَ (thaqufa / thaqifa) in the use describing skill: to become agile, perceptive, and skilled. Qur’anic: no, in this descriptive sense.
  • ثَقْف / ثَقِف / ثَقُف (thaqf / thaqif / thaquf): an agile, skilled, quick-witted person, able to grasp things rapidly. Qur’anic: no, in these adjectival forms.
  • ثَقِيف (thaqīf): skilled, perceptive, and quick in understanding. Qur’anic: no.
  • ثَقَافَة (thaqāfah): skill and agility in understanding or performing a task; for skill and practice with weapons, ثِقَافَة (thiqāfah), with an initial kasrah, is also recorded. Qur’anic: no.
  • ثِقَاف (thiqāf): an implement for straightening spears or bows; in another use, skill and practice with weapons. Qur’anic: no.
  • ثَقَّفَ / تَثْقِيف (thaqqafa / tathqīf): to straighten, correct crookedness, develop skill, or train. Qur’anic: no.
  • مُثَقَّف (muthaqqaf): something straightened and corrected, particularly a spear whose crookedness has been removed. Qur’anic: no.
  • ثَاقَفَ / مُثَاقَفَة (thāqafa / muthāqafah): competing in skill or combat, particularly with weapons. Qur’anic: no.
  • تَثَقَّفَ (tathaqqafa): to become skilled, educated, or trained. Qur’anic: no.

These forms show that the root’s Arabic family extends beyond its Qur’anic use.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: six occurrences of TH Q F

In the Qur’an itself, the prevailing sense is neither education and culture nor straightening implements, but gaining access to people, finding them in a particular situation, or gaining the upper hand over them.

2. The linguistic core: skill in grasping and attaining

Thaqifa: ordinary finding or skilled apprehension?

Arabic dictionaries record ثَقِفَ الشَّيْءَ for grasping something, learning it skillfully, and mastering it.

The expression رَجُلٌ ثَقِفٌ لَقِفٌ describes a person who learns quickly and correctly retains what has been grasped.

Two features stand out: agility in apprehension and the ability to retain or use what has been grasped.

In this field, thaqifa is therefore more than knowing a subject; skill in attaining and mastering it is also involved.

Someone may, for example, hear a statement without properly grasping its purpose. A person who understands it quickly and accurately can be described in Arabic as thaqif.

In another use, ثَقِفْتُ فُلَانًا means finding, reaching, or gaining control of a person.

This is not limited to mental apprehension. Its object is a real person who may be involved in pursuit, confrontation, or war.

An important distinction follows: grasping a statement differs from gaining access to an opponent, although both can belong to the field of attaining and mastering the object in question.

This overlap nevertheless does not establish that thaqifa must always mean domination. The object and context determine the kind of attainment.

Lisān al-ʿArab: apprehension, skill, and gaining access

Thaqāfah: skill and agility

In early Arabic, thaqāfah concerned skill, perceptiveness, and the ability to perform a task proficiently.

A thaqif person in this field grasps something quickly or is agile and practiced in performing a task.

This meaning has a historical connection with the modern concept of “culture,” but is not its exact equivalent.

In modern Arabic, thaqāfah also denotes a body of knowledge, beliefs, arts, values, ways of life, and a people’s intellectual and social achievements.

This semantic extension must not be projected back onto Qur’anic uses.

When the Qur’an says thaqiftumūhum, it is not discussing culture, general education, or social formation. In that context the verb concerns gaining access to people.

Familiarity with the modern meaning of thaqāfah must therefore not replace examination of the Qur’anic verb.

Does the root inherently mean domination?

Not without qualification.

In uses concerning knowledge and skill, thaqifa can mean correct apprehension and mastery of a craft. In uses concerning people, particularly in war, reaching someone and gaining the upper hand become prominent.

Mastery in this family therefore has two distinct forms: mastery of knowledge or skill, and gaining access to or the upper hand over a person in a confrontation.

These should not be confused.

Nor can a martial use of the verb establish that every derivative primarily means war or killing. Arabic also uses other forms for skill, quick apprehension, and correcting implements.

3. The material image: straightening a spear and skilled control

One familiar use is تَثْقِيفُ الرُّمْحِ: straightening a spear and correcting its crookedness.

Thiqāf names an implement used to apply pressure to a bent part of a spear or certain other implements, bringing it closer to the desired shape.

The material meaning is clear: a crooked object is controlled and corrected with an implement.

The image concerns agility, skill, and the ability to use an implement. Someone who can straighten a spear properly has mastery of the craft and its tools.

Can every meaning of TH Q F, however, be shown to have developed from straightening spears?

That conclusion is not certain.

Ibn Fāris’ analysis in Maqāyīs al-Lughah presents correcting an object’s crookedness as the root’s principal core, then connects skilled apprehension and gaining the upper hand over a person with it.

On this analysis, just as a craftsperson controls and straightens an object with an implement, a skilled person correctly grasps what is heard or pursued, and in confrontation gains access to an opponent.

This is an intelligible explanatory connection, but should not be treated as a demonstrated historical development of every use.

Other lexical explanations give greater prominence to agility in apprehension and attaining an object than to spear straightening.

Both fields should therefore be preserved: physical correction and straightening, and skilled apprehension and attainment.

Qur’anic usage belongs to the latter, particularly gaining access to people.

Early Arabic poetic witnesses

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm says of the spear-straightening implement:

إِذَا عَضَّ الثِّقَافُ بِهَا اشْمَأَزَّتْ
وَوَلَّتْهُ عَشَوْزَنَةً زَبُونَا

idhā ʿaḍḍa al-thiqāfu bihā ishmaʾazzat / wa-wallat-hu ʿashawzanatan zabūnan

Meaning: “When the straightening implement bit upon it, it recoiled and met it with stubborn resistance.”

Here al-thiqāf names the straightening implement. In the poem’s context, the image of the spear resisting this implement represents the poet’s people and their unyielding dignity; it is not merely a technical account of working on a spear.

Al-Zawzanī’s commentary on the Muʿallaqāt: the image of dignity in ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm

The witness shows that this family’s material use was recognized in early poetic Arabic.

In another verse, the Umayyad poet ʿAdī ibn al-Riqāʿ describes revising and refining a poem:

نَظَرَ الْمُثَقِّفِ فِي كُعُوبِ قَنَاتِهِ
حَتَّى يُقِيمَ ثِقَافُهُ مُنْآدَهَا

naẓara al-muthaqqifi fī kuʿūbi qanātihi / ḥattā yuqīma thiqāfuhu munʾādahā

Meaning: “[I examined the poem] with the scrutiny of a spear-straightener inspecting the joints of his shaft, until his implement straightens its crooked part.”

Here al-muthaqqif is the person who corrects and straightens. Naẓara is a verbal noun, with al-muthaqqifi as its genitive complement. The image of a craftsperson’s care explains the poet’s scrutiny of his poem; the line does not begin with an independent verb meaning “he looked” and a separate subject.

This witness postdates the Qur’an’s revelation, but illuminates an early technical use of the root.

Al-Bāqillānī’s Iʿjāz al-Qurʾān: ʿAdī’s witness in the context of refining a poem

By contrast, a poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAmr Dhū al-Kalb reads:

فَإِمَّا تَثْقَفُونِي فَاقْتُلُونِي
وَإِنْ أَثْقَفْ فَسَوْفَ تَرَوْنَ بَالِي

fa-immā tathqafūnī fa-uqtulūnī / wa-in athqaf fa-sawfa tarawna bālī

Meaning: “If you gain access to me, kill me; if I gain access to you, you will see what I do.”

The transmitted readings of this verse differ, particularly in the first half’s verbal construction and the second half’s wording. Its sense of confrontation and gaining access to an opponent nevertheless remains clear.

Tāj al-ʿArūs: readings of ʿAmr Dhū al-Kalb’s witness and thaqāfah versus thiqāfah

This witness is much closer to the Qur’anic uses than the modern sense “culture,” since the verb occurs in hostility and confrontation.

Together these witnesses show three established fields of TH Q F before the modern concept of thaqāfah became established: straightening and correcting implements, skill and rapid apprehension, and gaining access to an opponent in confrontation.

The fields are connected, but all three meanings should not be inserted simultaneously into the translation of a particular use.

4. Thaqiftumūhum: gaining access to an opponent in a context of war

Al-Baqarah 2:191

Verse text

وَاقْتُلُوهُمْ حَيْثُ ثَقِفْتُمُوهُمْ وَأَخْرِجُوهُمْ مِنْ حَيْثُ أَخْرَجُوكُمْ وَالْفِتْنَةُ أَشَدُّ مِنَ الْقَتْلِ وَلَا تُقَاتِلُوهُمْ عِنْدَ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ حَتَّىٰ يُقَاتِلُوكُمْ فِيهِ فَإِنْ قَاتَلُوكُمْ فَاقْتُلُوهُمْ كَذَٰلِكَ جَزَاءُ الْكَافِرِينَ

wa-uqtulūhum ḥaythu thaqiftumūhum wa-akhrijūhum min ḥaythu akhrajūkum wa-al-fitnatu ashaddu mina al-qatli wa-lā tuqātilūhum ʿinda al-masjidi al-ḥarāmi ḥattā yuqātilūkum fīhi fa-in qātalūkum fa-uqtulūhum kadhālika jazāʾu al-kāfirīna

English translation

Kill them wherever you gain access to them, and expel them from where they expelled you; fitnah is more severe than killing. Do not fight them at the Sacred Mosque unless they fight you there; if they fight you, then kill them. Such is the recompense of the disbelievers.

Examining the use

Thaqiftumūhum is a Form I perfect active verb with the subject pronoun tum and object pronoun hum.

The subject consists of those addressed by the verse; the object is the same group mentioned in wa-uqtulūhum, “kill them.”

The verb means gaining access to them, finding them in the situation concerned, or gaining the upper hand over them.

Thaqiftumūhum therefore does not by itself mean “kill.” The command to kill comes from the separate verb wa-uqtulūhum.

This distinction is fundamental.

Thaqiftumūhum alone cannot yield a ruling to kill, since the same verb also occurs in other verses without a command to kill.

Ḥaythu also matters here. It concerns the place or situation in which access is gained.

Ḥaythu thaqiftumūhum specifies where gaining access occurs, but does not itself determine which people the ruling concerns.

The surrounding verses must be consulted to identify those addressed and those concerned.

Immediately before this verse, Al-Baqarah 2:190 begins its command as follows:

وَقَاتِلُوا فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ الَّذِينَ يُقَاتِلُونَكُمْ وَلَا تَعْتَدُوا

wa-qātilū fī sabīli Allāhi alladhīna yuqātilūnakum wa-lā taʿtadū

English translation

Fight in the way of The God those who fight you, and do not transgress.

The group denoted by hum in Al-Baqarah 2:191 is therefore not detached from the context of confrontation and war.

In Al-Baqarah 2:191 itself, expelling them is also connected with their earlier expulsion of those addressed. This relationship indicates a particular conflict involving expulsion and aggression.

The continuation even makes fighting at the Sacred Mosque conditional on the other side fighting there.

Al-Baqarah 2:192 and Al-Baqarah 2:193 also discuss desisting from the conflict.

Ḥaythu thaqiftumūhum cannot therefore be turned into an independent command to kill every person who disagrees, anywhere in the world. That generalization does not follow from the verb or the verses’ context.

On the other hand, the severity of the command actually present must not be concealed by altering the verb’s meaning. Uqtulū is a command to kill; thaqiftumūhum describes gaining access to those whom that command concerns.

Lexical study neither removes the command nor extends it without limit; it identifies each word’s meaning and preserves the verse’s actual qualifications.

Does thaqiftumūhum mean only seeing them?

Seeing a person is one way to find them, but in this context the verb extends beyond mere observation.

Someone may see an opponent at a distance without having gained access to them.

In martial uses, thaqifa can instead describe the other person coming within reach or an opportunity to gain the upper hand over them.

“You gain access to them” is consequently more precise here than “you see them.”

“You find them” is also lexically defensible, but may not adequately convey to a Persian reader the difference between ordinary finding and gaining access during a confrontation.

5. Thaqiftumūhum in Al-Nisāʾ: those concerned and the verse’s conditions

Al-Nisāʾ 4:91

Verse text

سَتَجِدُونَ آخَرِينَ يُرِيدُونَ أَنْ يَأْمَنُوكُمْ وَيَأْمَنُوا قَوْمَهُمْ كُلَّ مَا رُدُّوا إِلَى الْفِتْنَةِ أُرْكِسُوا فِيهَا فَإِنْ لَمْ يَعْتَزِلُوكُمْ وَيُلْقُوا إِلَيْكُمُ السَّلَمَ وَيَكُفُّوا أَيْدِيَهُمْ فَخُذُوهُمْ وَاقْتُلُوهُمْ حَيْثُ ثَقِفْتُمُوهُمْ وَأُولَٰئِكُمْ جَعَلْنَا لَكُمْ عَلَيْهِمْ سُلْطَانًا مُبِينًا

sa-tajidūna ākharīna yurīdūna an yaʾmanūkum wa-yaʾmanū qawmahum kulla mā ruddū ilā al-fitnati urkisū fīhā fa-in lam yaʿtazilūkum wa-yulqū ilaykumu al-salama wa-yakuffū aydiyahum fa-khudhūhum wa-uqtulūhum ḥaythu thaqiftumūhum wa-ulāʾikum jaʿalnā lakum ʿalayhim sulṭānan mubīnan

English translation

You will find others who wish to be secure from you and from their own people. Whenever they are returned to fitnah, they are plunged back into it. If they do not withdraw from you, offer you peace, and restrain their hands, then seize them and kill them wherever you gain access to them. Over these We have given you clear authority.

Examining the use

The same form thaqiftumūhum occurs here as in Al-Baqarah 2:191.

Its lexical meaning remains unchanged: gaining access to a specified group.

The subject and conditions, however, are not identical to those of Al-Baqarah 2:191.

The verse describes a group seeking security in relation to both sides, but plunged back into fitnah whenever returned to it.

It then sets out conditions involving withdrawal, offering peace, and restraining their hands.

Yakuffū aydiyahum matters because it expresses restraining one’s hands from aggression.

The ruling follows these conditions, rather than preceding them.

Fa-khudhūhum and wa-uqtulūhum are also two independent verbs preceding ḥaythu thaqiftumūhum.

Akhdh denotes taking or seizing, and qatl denotes killing. Thaqifa, however, specifies the situation of gaining access to the people concerned.

These three verbs should not be treated as synonyms.

If thaqifa itself meant “seize and kill,” its distinction from the accompanying verbs would disappear.

In the same surah, the preceding verse, Al-Nisāʾ 4:90, describes people withdrawing from fighting and offering peace; in that situation the Qur’an grants no way to act against them.

This continuity matters. Ḥaythu thaqiftumūhum cannot authorize disregarding people’s peace and withdrawal.

Al-fitnah also requires examination in its own right. Thaqifa itself cannot establish that fitnah must mean holding a different belief or mere religious disagreement.

In this context fitnah concerns the group’s behavior, the security of both sides, withdrawal, and restraining hands from aggression.

Thaqifa’s lexical meaning therefore remains stable, but the accompanying ruling can be understood only with those concerned, its conditions, and neighboring verses in view.

6. Gaining access and the upper hand in Al-Anfāl and Al-Mumtaḥanah

Al-Anfāl 8:57: gaining access in war

Verse text

فَإِمَّا تَثْقَفَنَّهُمْ فِي الْحَرْبِ فَشَرِّدْ بِهِمْ مَنْ خَلْفَهُمْ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَذَّكَّرُونَ

fa-immā tathqafannahum fī al-ḥarbi fa-sharrid bihim man khalfahum laʿallahum yadhdhakkarūna

English translation

If you gain access to them in war, then through them scatter those behind them, so that they may take heed.

Examining the use

Tathqafannahum is a Form I imperfect verb with the heavy emphatic nūn attached.

This emphasis concerns the conditional construction, without altering the verb’s principal meaning.

Fī al-ḥarb explicitly specifies the kind of situation in which access is gained.

Of the root’s six Qur’anic uses, this is consequently one of the clearest witnesses for gaining the upper hand or gaining access during military confrontation.

Thaqifa here is not merely seeing someone on a road or encountering them by chance. It concerns finding and gaining control of an opponent in war.

The preceding verses must also be consulted to identify the pronoun hum.

Al-Anfāl 8:56 describes people who make a covenant and then repeatedly break it.

Tathqafannahum fī al-ḥarb therefore concerns a group in a context of covenant breaking and war.

Al-Anfāl 8:58 also addresses fearing treachery and openly terminating a covenant. This continuity shows that conduct toward the other side is not examined without regard to covenants and the circumstances of confrontation.

In Al-Anfāl 8:57 itself, tathqafannahum is followed by the command fa-sharrid bihim man khalfahum.

This command concerns scattering or deterring those behind the group in question.

The first verb’s meaning must nevertheless be distinguished from the second command’s intended result. Thaqifa does not mean frightening or scattering others; those concepts arise from fa-sharrid and its accompanying construction.

Nor does this verse alone prescribe unlimited aggression, torture, or harm to people unconnected with the conflict. The limits of wartime conduct require examining the verses concerning covenants, the prohibition of transgression, and people’s rights.

Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:2: if the enemies gain access to you

Verse text

إِنْ يَثْقَفُوكُمْ يَكُونُوا لَكُمْ أَعْدَاءً وَيَبْسُطُوا إِلَيْكُمْ أَيْدِيَهُمْ وَأَلْسِنَتَهُمْ بِالسُّوءِ وَوَدُّوا لَوْ تَكْفُرُونَ

in yathqafūkum yakūnū lakum aʿdāʾan wa-yabsuṭū ilaykum aydiyahum wa-alsinatahum bi-al-sūʾi wa-waddū law takfurūna

English translation

If they gain access to you, they will be your enemies and stretch out their hands and tongues against you with evil; they wish that you would disbelieve.

Examining the use

Yathqafūkum is an imperfect verb with a plural subject pronoun and the object kum.

Unlike Al-Baqarah and Al-Nisāʾ, the believing addressees here are those to whom others may gain access.

This reversal of roles matters greatly.

In Al-Baqarah 2:191, the Qur’an’s addressees may gain access to the opponent. In Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:2, enemies may gain access to the addressees.

The verb’s principal meaning remains stable despite this difference.

The verse then explains how the enemies would behave if they gained such access: they would use their hands and tongues against the addressees with evil.

Thaqifa here therefore describes a situation of access or dominance enabling hostile conduct by the other side.

The verb itself, however, does not mean enmity.

Yakūnū lakum aʿdāʾan expresses enmity, while yabsuṭū ilaykum aydiyahum wa-alsinatahum bi-al-sūʾ describes hostile behavior.

The whole sentence’s meaning should therefore not be compressed into yathqafūkum itself.

Another consideration is the surah’s context. Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:1 concerns enemies hostile to The God and the believers who have driven the believers out of their homes.

In Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:8 and Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:9, the Qur’an distinguishes those who have neither fought the addressees on account of religion nor expelled them from their homes from those who have fought, expelled, and assisted in expelling them.

The enmity described in Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:2 should consequently not be generalized to everyone with a different belief or religious affiliation.

The verse describes enemies who would behave with hostility if they gained access to the addressees. Yathqafūkum thus expresses gaining access to the other side; the enemy’s identity, the threat’s nature, and the enemy’s behavior emerge from the other expressions and the surah’s context.

This use is particularly important for determining the root’s meaning: thaqifa itself is neither a command to fight, nor a ruling to kill, nor even a description of enmity. The same verb can also describe enemies gaining access to believers.

The verb’s stable meaning is therefore gaining access or the upper hand; the situation, subject, object, and other sentence elements determine its evaluation and consequences.

7. Thuqifū: being found and a situation of humiliation

Āl ʿImrān 3:112

Verse text

ضُرِبَتْ عَلَيْهِمُ الذِّلَّةُ أَيْنَ مَا ثُقِفُوا إِلَّا بِحَبْلٍ مِنَ اللَّهِ وَحَبْلٍ مِنَ النَّاسِ وَبَاءُوا بِغَضَبٍ مِنَ اللَّهِ وَضُرِبَتْ عَلَيْهِمُ الْمَسْكَنَةُ ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمْ كَانُوا يَكْفُرُونَ بِآيَاتِ اللَّهِ وَيَقْتُلُونَ الْأَنْبِيَاءَ بِغَيْرِ حَقٍّ ذَٰلِكَ بِمَا عَصَوْا وَكَانُوا يَعْتَدُونَ

ḍuribat ʿalayhimu al-dhillatu ayna mā thuqifū illā bi-ḥablin mina Allāhi wa-ḥablin mina al-nāsi wa-bāʾū bi-ghaḍabin mina Allāhi wa-ḍuribat ʿalayhimu al-maskanatu dhālika bi-annahum kānū yakfurūna bi-āyāti Allāhi wa-yaqtulūna al-anbiyāʾa bi-ghayri ḥaqqin dhālika bi-mā ʿaṣaw wa-kānū yaʿtadūna

English translation

Humiliation has been laid upon them wherever they are found, except through a bond from The God and a bond from people. They incurred anger from The God, and destitution was laid upon them. That was because they denied the signs of The God and killed the prophets without right. That was because they disobeyed and transgressed.

Examining the use

Thuqifū is a Form I perfect passive verb. Unlike thaqiftumūhum in Al-Baqarah 2:191 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:91, the one who finds or gains access is not named here.

The passive shifts attention from the finder to those found or brought within reach.

Ayna mā thuqifū refers to various places: wherever they are found.

This verse nevertheless differs significantly from the martial uses. In Āl ʿImrān 3:112, thuqifū is not directly accompanied by a command to seize or kill. It describes humiliation, destitution, and the consequences of the group’s behavior.

The martial meaning of thaqiftumūhum in Al-Baqarah 2:191 must therefore not be transferred wholesale to this verse.

Here thuqifū can mean “they are found” or “access is gained to them,” but the verb itself cannot establish that war captivity, detention, or killing must be intended.

The role of illā bi-ḥablin mina Allāhi wa-ḥablin mina al-nās

Ayna mā thuqifū is followed by the exception illā bi-ḥablin mina Allāhi wa-ḥablin mina al-nās.

This matters to understanding the sentence because it does not leave the described situation unqualified and without exception.

Arabic ḥabl means a rope, but in extended uses can denote a bond, means of connection, covenant, or relationship on which a person relies.

Ḥablin mina Allāh and ḥablin mina al-nās occur together here. Identifying them precisely concerns Ḥ B L and the verse’s context, rather than thaqifa itself.

The clear point is that the exception cannot be removed and ayna mā thuqifū then made into an absolute, unqualified ruling.

Thuqifū itself does not mean humiliation either. Humiliation is expressed by ḍuribat ʿalayhimu al-dhillah; thuqifū introduces the situation in which this description applies.

Those concerned and the cause of the described situation

The preceding verses must establish whom hum refers to. The context discusses groups among the People of the Book and conduct such as denying the signs, killing prophets, disobedience, and transgression.

The verse itself explains the cause:

“ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمْ كَانُوا يَكْفُرُونَ بِآيَاتِ اللَّهِ وَيَقْتُلُونَ الْأَنْبِيَاءَ بِغَيْرِ حَقٍّ.”

Its explanation therefore rests on behavior and responsibility, rather than ethnic or genealogical membership alone being an inherent cause of humiliation.

The next verse makes the boundary clearer. Āl ʿImrān 3:113 explicitly states:

“لَيْسُوا سَوَاءً.”

That is, they are not all alike.

Āl ʿImrān 3:112 therefore cannot support a uniform, perpetual, unchangeable judgment upon every member of an ethnic group or all the People of the Book throughout history.

This caution does not change thuqifū’s meaning; it prevents an incorrect generalization about those concerned and the verse’s consequence.

Lexical conclusion: In Āl ʿImrān 3:112, thuqifū denotes people being found or brought within reach. Humiliation and destitution are expressed by other words, and should not be presented as independent meanings of thaqifa.

8. Thuqifū: finding, seizing, and killing in a connected construction

Al-Aḥzāb 33:61

Verse text

مَلْعُونِينَ أَيْنَمَا ثُقِفُوا أُخِذُوا وَقُتِّلُوا تَقْتِيلًا

malʿūnīna aynamā thuqifū ukhidhū wa-quttilū taqtīlan

English translation

Cast out and removed from mercy; wherever access is gained to them, they are seized and killed with severity.

Examining the use

Thuqifū again occurs as a perfect passive verb.

Unlike Āl ʿImrān 3:112, however, two further verbs follow:

“أُخِذُوا” and “قُتِّلُوا.”

The first belongs to ʾ KH DH and means seizing. The second belongs to Q T L and denotes killing.

The verse thus contains three connected but distinct concepts: gaining access to people, seizing them, and killing them.

This separation is essential to studying TH Q F.

If thuqifū itself meant “they were killed,” its distinction from quttilū would disappear. If it meant only “they were seized,” the independent role of ukhidhū would likewise be overlooked.

In this context thuqifū therefore denotes finding or gaining access to people; seizing and killing are actions or consequences expressed afterward.

Distinguishing thuqifū from ukhidhū

Finding or gaining access to a person in Arabic need not be identical to seizing and detaining them.

A person may be identified and brought within reach without having yet been seized. Seizing can also be a stage following access.

The same distinction is visible in Al-Aḥzāb 33:61.

Thuqifū expresses finding or gaining access to the people; ukhidhū expresses seizing them.

In some Arabic uses thaqifa can, of course, also convey dominance and gaining control. The verbs’ semantic fields are therefore close, but proximity does not make them wholly identical.

The role of quttilū taqtīlan

Quttilū is a passive Form II verb. Taqtīlan is its verbal noun and emphasizes the act of killing.

This construction intensifies the expression.

Form II and the emphatic verbal noun nevertheless cannot, by themselves, specify a particular method of killing, an exact number of repetitions, or physical details of the act.

The verse uses a severe expression about killing, without explaining the details of its execution in this phrase itself.

The methodological principle remains: a morphological pattern offers semantic possibilities, but conclusions must not go beyond contextual evidence.

Who are the people concerned?

Al-Aḥzāb 33:61 should not be read apart from the preceding verse.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:60 states:

لَئِنْ لَمْ يَنْتَهِ الْمُنَافِقُونَ وَالَّذِينَ فِي قُلُوبِهِمْ مَرَضٌ وَالْمُرْجِفُونَ فِي الْمَدِينَةِ لَنُغْرِيَنَّكَ بِهِمْ ثُمَّ لَا يُجَاوِرُونَكَ فِيهَا إِلَّا قَلِيلًا

la-in lam yantahi al-munāfiqūna wa-alladhīna fī qulūbihim maraḍun wa-al-murjifūna fī al-madīnati la-nughriyannaka bihim thumma lā yujāwirūnaka fīhā illā qalīlan

English translation

If the hypocrites, those in whose hearts is sickness, and those spreading alarm in the city do not desist, We will certainly stir you against them; then they will remain your neighbors there for only a little while.

The verse begins with a condition: la-in lam yantahi.

That is, if they do not desist from the behavior concerned.

It then identifies particular groups in the city and sets out a severe consequence.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:61 therefore continues that warning. It must not be detached from the preceding condition and turned into a general ruling about every opponent, critic, or person accused of hypocrisy.

Al-murjifūna fī al-madīnah also requires independent examination of its root and context. Reporting unwelcome news, disagreeing, or criticizing authority does not, without further evidence, establish a definite instance of this description.

In the same surah, Al-Aḥzāb 33:59 discusses believing women and preventing harm to them. Al-Aḥzāb 33:60 then threatens the groups mentioned.

This continuity matters for understanding the warning’s social background, but without further examination it should not restrict all the conduct of the groups in Al-Aḥzāb 33:60 to one kind of harassment.

Is aynamā thuqifū absolute in meaning?

Linguistically, aynamā expresses spatial scope: wherever the situation occurs.

Broad spatial scope does not make the subject of the ruling unlimited.

The plural wāw, which serves as the passive verb’s subject, refers back to the group identified in the preceding context. “Wherever” access occurs therefore does not change the people concerned or the warning’s condition.

This distinction matters when understanding Qur’anic commands and warnings.

An expression with broad spatial scope must not be turned into a ruling without limits on whom it concerns. Nor should the difficult content justify concealing or arbitrarily changing the explicit meaning of ukhidhū wa-quttilū taqtīlan.

Research preserves both the expression’s directness and the limits established by the context itself.

Lexical conclusion: In Al-Aḥzāb 33:61, thuqifū means being found or gained access to. Seizing and killing arise from the following two independent verbs; neither should be treated as a fixed component of TH Q F.

9. Morphology and syntax in the Qur’anic uses

Active and passive forms

All six Qur’anic occurrences are Form I verbs, but differ in person, tense, and active or passive voice.

In Al-Baqarah 2:191 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:91, thaqiftumūhum is active. The addressees are the subject, and hum is the object.

In Al-Anfāl 8:57, tathqafannahum has emphatic nūn. It concerns a singular addressee, and gaining access to an opponent is a possible event within a condition.

In Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:2, yathqafūkum is active, but the parties’ roles have changed. The enemies are the subject, while kum denotes those to whom the enemies may gain access.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:112 and Al-Aḥzāb 33:61, thuqifū is passive. Those to whom access is gained stand at the center of the clause, while the person gaining access is not named.

The root itself is consequently not restricted to one particular group. Depending on context, believers, their enemies, or others may be the subject or object of gaining access.

Ḥaythu and aynamā

Al-Baqarah 2:191 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:91 have ḥaythu thaqiftumūhum.

Āl ʿImrān 3:112 and Al-Aḥzāb 33:61 use أَيْنَ مَا ثُقِفُوا and أَيْنَمَا ثُقِفُوا.

Both constructions concern place, but are not syntactically identical.

Ḥaythu can function as a locative adverb, while conditional aynamā denotes any place where the condition is fulfilled.

This difference must not be overlooked. In neither construction, however, does the locative expression alone identify whom the ruling concerns.

The pronoun’s referent and the sentence’s context remain decisive.

In and immā

Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:2 has in yathqafūkum.

Al-Anfāl 8:57 has fa-immā tathqafannahum.

Both are conditional. Gaining access is presented as a situation whose fulfillment would bring the ensuing consequence or command into relation with it.

Neither expression should therefore be translated as though the sentence already declared that access had certainly been gained.

“If you gain access to them” differs from “when you have certainly gained access to them.”

Is thaqifa inherently a verb of violence?

The six Qur’anic uses occur amid hostility, war, threats, or severe consequences.

This distribution cannot establish that the verb itself means striking, punishing, or killing.

Some verses express these actions through independent verbs.

The verb’s Qur’anic field of use must therefore be distinguished from its semantic core. In the Qur’an it chiefly concerns gaining access to the other side, while its principal meaning remains finding, gaining access, or gaining the upper hand.

10. Distinguishing related roots

TH Q F (thaqifa) compared with W J D (wajada)

Study of W J D

Wajada has a broad field in Arabic and the Qur’an: finding, encountering something, discovering a situation, and in some uses feeling.

Thaqifa can also denote finding, but its Qur’anic uses emphasize gaining access to a person in confrontation or dominance.

It should nevertheless not be claimed that wajada never concerns an opponent or enemy, or that thaqifa always entails seizure and complete victory.

The distinction is a tendency, understood from each verse’s context.

TH Q F (thaqifa) compared with L Q Y (laqiya)

Study of L Q Y

Laqiya concerns meeting, encountering, and facing something or someone.

Two people can meet without either gaining the upper hand over the other.

In uses concerning an opponent, thaqifa can go beyond an ordinary encounter to gaining access or bringing the other side within reach.

This must not become an absolute rule equating laqiya with peaceful encounters and thaqifa with violence. Laqiya also occurs in martial contexts.

TH Q F (thaqifa) compared with ʾ KH DH (akhadha)

Study of ʾ KH DH

Akhadha concerns taking or gaining control of something or someone, and has extensive uses.

Thaqifa, in the use examined here, expresses gaining access to a person.

Both concepts occur together in Al-Aḥzāb 33:61:

“أَيْنَمَا ثُقِفُوا أُخِذُوا.”

This combination shows that finding or gaining access to people and seizing them are two connected stages or aspects, rather than necessarily one action with exactly the same meaning.

Translating thaqifa as “arrest” in every verse is consequently imprecise: arrest can be a consequence of gaining access, rather than the verb’s fixed meaning.

TH Q F (thaqifa) compared with Ẓ F R (ẓafira)

Study of Ẓ F R

Arabic ẓafira concerns attaining, succeeding, and gaining victory.

Dictionaries explain thaqiftuhu through ẓafirtu bihi. This proximity supports attainment and gaining the upper hand within thaqifa’s semantic field.

It should not establish, however, that thaqifa always means a complete and final victory has occurred.

The verb may concern only gaining access in a particular situation.

TH Q F (thaqifa) compared with GH L B (ghalaba)

Study of GH L B

Ghalaba concerns overcoming or prevailing over another.

Although thaqifa can involve dominance in some contexts, it does not invariably express the final outcome of a competition or war.

In Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:2, for example, it concerns the possibility that enemies might gain access to the addressees, followed by their likely behavior.

Yathqafūkum should therefore not always be restricted to “they defeat you,” although dominance can be an aspect of gaining access in this context.

TH Q F (thaqifa) compared with ʿ L M (ʿalima) and F H M (fahima)

Study of ʿ L M; Study of F H M

Early Arabic also uses thaqifa to describe someone skilled and perceptive.

This use concerns knowledge and understanding, without making the words absolute synonyms.

ʿIlm concerns knowing; fahm concerns understanding meaning; descriptive thaqifa can emphasize agility and skill in understanding or performing a task.

The six Qur’anic occurrences, however, do not concern learning or understanding speech. All concern finding or gaining access to people.

The lexical sense “quick-wittedness” should therefore not enter the direct translation of these verses.

11. Translation proposals for Forqan

Translation should distinguish “finding,” “gaining access,” and “gaining the upper hand” according to context.

Ḥaythu thaqiftumūhum in Al-Baqarah 2:191 can be translated as “wherever you gain access to them.”

The same equivalent is appropriate in Al-Nisāʾ 4:91, which concerns gaining access to a specified group during confrontation.

Fa-immā tathqafannahum fī al-ḥarb in Al-Anfāl 8:57 can be translated as “if you gain access to them in war” or “if you gain the upper hand over them in war.” The first preserves the verb’s broader meaning; the second gives more prominence to dominance in the martial context.

In yathqafūkum in Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:2 closely corresponds to “if they gain access to you.”

Ayna mā thuqifū in Āl ʿImrān 3:112 can be translated as “wherever they are found.” Adding arrest or killing to this expression has no direct support.

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:61, أَيْنَمَا ثُقِفُوا أُخِذُوا وَقُتِّلُوا تَقْتِيلًا is best translated with the three verbs distinguished: “Wherever access is gained to them, they are seized and killed with severity.”

“Access is gained,” “they are seized,” and “they are killed” should remain distinct.

“Culture,” “acculturation,” “teaching,” and “correcting crookedness” should not enter the direct translation of the Qur’anic verb in any of these cases.

These senses belong to the TH Q F family, but the forms and contexts used in the Qur’an draw on another field.

Thaqifa itself should also not replace qatl, akhdh, ghalaba, or sharrada. When those actions occur in a verse, they should be translated from their own words.

12. Final synthesis

Arabic TH Q F has a broad semantic field. Rapid and skilled apprehension, proficiency in a task, straightening crooked implements, and gaining access to a person or opponent are recorded uses of this family.

In the Qur’an, however, it occurs only six times, all as Form I verbs.

Every Qur’anic occurrence concerns finding or gaining access to people. Sometimes this takes place in war and direct confrontation; sometimes it concerns enemies possibly gaining the upper hand; sometimes a passive construction describes people found or brought within reach.

In Al-Baqarah 2:191 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:91, the verb accompanies commands concerning war. In Al-Anfāl 8:57, it has the explicit qualification fī al-ḥarb. In Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:2, it expresses enemies possibly gaining access to believers.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:112, thuqifū accompanies humiliation and destitution, without itself meaning humiliation. In Al-Aḥzāb 33:61, the same passive form accompanies ukhidhū and quttilū, without seizing and killing becoming fixed components of thaqifa.

Two fundamental conclusions follow.

First, the semantic core of Qur’anic TH Q F is finding, gaining access to, or gaining the upper hand over a person; it does not itself mean killing, punishment, or enmity. Where those actions occur, they are understood from other verbs and expressions.

Second, the root itself does not determine a ruling’s scope or the people’s identity. One must ask who gains access to whom, in what situation, and under what conditions in the surrounding verses.

The martial use must therefore not be generalized to all Arabic uses, just as the modern meaning of thaqāfah must not be imposed upon Qur’anic verses.

Final definition: In Qur’anic usage, TH Q F denotes finding or gaining access to a person in a particular situation, which may involve gaining the upper hand or control over them. The sentence’s construction and context determine the kind of access, degree of dominance, and actions that follow.

Preserving this boundary prevents both concealing the actual severity of certain verses and deriving rulings beyond the Qur’an’s wording and qualifications from a single verb.
