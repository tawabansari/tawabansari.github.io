Standing, stopping movement, and holding in place

The Arabic root w-q-f denotes standing, stopping, or preventing someone or something from continuing to move. Waqafa can describe someone who stops, or someone who brings another person or an animal to a halt. Wuqūf denotes standing or pausing; waqf denotes stopping something; and mawqūf describes someone or something held or stopped.

Stopping in this family does not always mean that a body stands upright. Someone’s movement may be halted, a person may be held for questioning, or an activity may be prevented from continuing. The construction and context determine the kind and purpose of the stop.

The root occurs four times in the Qur’an. All four concern people held or stopped in scenes of Resurrection: before the Fire, in their Lord’s presence, or for questioning.

1. The root in early and classical Arabic

In early Arabic, waqafa was used both for someone standing and for stopping a mount. Both uses appear in poetry attributed to pre-Islamic poets.

At the opening of his celebrated Muʿallaqah, Imruʾ al-Qays says:

قِفَا نَبْكِ مِنْ ذِكْرَى حَبِيبٍ وَمَنْزِلِ
بِسِقْطِ اللِّوَى بَيْنَ الدَّخُولِ فَحَوْمَلِ

qifā nabki min dhikrā ḥabībin wa-manzili / bi-siqṭi al-liwā bayna al-dakhūli fa-ḥawmali

Translation: “You two, stop, so that we may weep at the memory of a beloved and a dwelling at Siqṭ al-Liwā, between al-Dakhūl and Ḥawmal.”

Qifā is the dual imperative of waqafa. The poet asks for movement to stop, allowing time to remember and weep at the beloved’s former dwelling.

A few verses later, the poem continues:

وُقُوفًا بِهَا صَحْبِي عَلَيَّ مَطِيَّهُمْ
يَقُولُونَ لَا تَهْلِكْ أَسًى وَتَجَمَّلِ

wuqūfan bihā ṣaḥbī ʿalayya maṭiyyahum / yaqūlūna lā tahlik asan wa-tajammali

Translation: “My companions halted their mounts there beside me, saying: ‘Do not destroy yourself with grief; bear it with composure.’”

Here, the poet’s companions have halted their mounts beside him. Wuqūfan concerns this halt, and maṭiyyahum names the mounts they have stopped; it should not be rendered simply as standing on their mounts.

These uses show that stopping in early poetry could be part of purposeful conduct, beyond the mere absence of motion. Travelers interrupt their journey to allow someone time to do something.

The purpose—remembering the beloved and weeping—comes from the poem’s story, not from w-q-f itself.

Another example appears in the Muʿallaqah attributed to ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād:

فَوَقَفْتُ فِيهَا نَاقَتِي وَكَأَنَّهَا
فَدَنٌ لِأَقْضِيَ حَاجَةَ الْمُتَلَوِّمِ

fa-waqaftu fīhā nāqatī wa-ka-annahā / fadanun li-aqḍiya ḥājata al-mutalawwimi

Translation: “I halted my she-camel there, as though she were a palace, to satisfy the need of one who lingers.”

Waqaftu is transitive here, and nāqatī, “my she-camel,” is its object. The poet says that he himself stopped the animal.

This differs from qifā in Imruʾ al-Qays’s poem. There, people are told to stop; here, someone prevents another creature from continuing to move.

The distinction shows that the basic verb waqafa can be both intransitive and transitive. Form IV is not required to express stopping someone or something else.

These poems are attributed to poets who lived before Islam and are recognized as pre-Islamic figures in Arabic literary tradition. Their texts nevertheless survive through later transmission and manuscripts. Familiar attribution is not the same as independent proof of the composition date of every verse.

Arabic lexicons likewise record waqafa bi-al-makān for standing in a place and waqafa al-dābbata for stopping an animal.

The distinction can be stated as follows:

In waqafa al-rajulu, the man himself stops.

In waqaftu al-rajula, the speaker stops the man.

This matters for the Qur’anic passages, since most occurrences take a passive verb or passive participle form.

2. Core meaning and forms

Three aspects are closely related in the central uses of this family: standing, movement stopping, and stopping another.

Someone walking along a route who then stops has performed waqafa. If another person prevents that movement from continuing, they have stopped the traveler. In both cases, movement or progress halts at a point.

The stop need not be permanent. Someone may pause briefly and resume walking. Duration must be determined from context.

Important Arabic forms include:

  • وَقَفَ (waqafa): stood, stopped, or stopped someone else.
  • يَقِفُ (yaqifu): stands or stops something.
  • قِفْ (qif): stop; singular imperative.
  • قِفُوا (qifū): stop, or hold someone; plural imperative, depending on transitivity.
  • وَقْف (waqf): stopping or holding something.
  • وُقُوف (wuqūf): standing or pausing.
  • وَاقِف (wāqif): standing or stopped in a place.
  • مَوْقُوف (mawqūf): someone or something held or stopped.
  • مَوْقِف (mawqif): a place of standing or stopping.
  • وَقَّفَ (waqqafa): stopped something or held it in place.
  • تَوْقِيف (tawqīf): stopping; in other uses, informing someone about a matter.
  • تَوَقَّفَ (tawaqqafa): stopped, paused, or ceased continuing an activity.
  • اسْتَوْقَفَ (istawqafa): asked someone to stop or made them halt.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an.

Lexicons also record Form IV awqafa. Some early lexicographers preferred waqafa for stopping something, regarding awqafa in that sense as restricted or uncommon. Their disagreement does not justify erasing the recorded Form IV usage.

The transitive verb under discussion in the Qur’an belongs to basic Form I.

The actual Qur’anic forms are:

  • Wuqifū in Al-Anʿām 6:27 and Al-Anʿām 6:30.
  • Mawqūfūn in Sabaʾ 34:31.
  • Wa-qifūhum in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:24.

Wuqifū is a passive perfect verb, mawqūfūn a masculine plural passive participle, and wa-qifūhum a command to stop a group of people.

In the reading used here, wuqifū has u on the wāw and i on the qāf: “they were held.” It differs from waqafū, “they stood.” This is a distinction of grammatical voice, not merely pronunciation.

In wa-qifūhum, the initial wa- is a conjunction, and qifūhum consists of an imperative plus the object suffix -hum. It means “and hold them,” not “and they stood.”

3. Qur’anic uses

1. Wuqifū ʿalā al-nār: held before the Fire

Al-Anʿām 6:27

وَلَوْ تَرَىٰ إِذْ وُقِفُوا عَلَى النَّارِ فَقَالُوا يَا لَيْتَنَا نُرَدُّ وَلَا نُكَذِّبَ بِآيَاتِ رَبِّنَا وَنَكُونَ مِنَ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ

wa-law tarā idh wuqifū ʿalā al-nāri fa-qālū yā laytanā nuraddu wa-lā nukadhdhiba bi-āyāti rabbinā wa-nakūna mina al-muʾminīn

Al-Anʿām 6:27

Translation: “If only you could see when they are held before the Fire and say: ‘If only we could be sent back, not deny our Lord’s signs, and be among the believers!’”

Wuqifū is a passive perfect verb. The people described are held before the Fire.

The passive matters: the agent who stops them is not named in this construction, and attention falls on the condition of those being held.

ʿAlā al-nār specifies the relationship between this stopping and the Fire.

Waqafa ʿalā can describe standing on something, being stationed before it, or confronting it. The preposition ʿalā should therefore not be restricted to a picture of their feet necessarily resting on the Fire’s surface.

The central scene places them in a situation where the Fire is present and manifest before them, and they can no longer deny its reality as before.

Their next words clarify this encounter: they wish to return to their former life and no longer deny their Lord’s signs.

Wuqifū itself, however, does not mean remorse or a request to return. Those reactions come from what they say.

Al-Anʿām 6:28 continues the scene: what they formerly concealed has become manifest to them, and if returned, they would resume their former conduct.

Their words on confronting the Fire do not necessarily indicate a genuine reform of intention and conduct.

Waqf here expresses their position of confrontation; the surrounding verses disclose their inner condition and response.

2. Wuqifū ʿalā rabbihim: before their Lord

Al-Anʿām 6:30

وَلَوْ تَرَىٰ إِذْ وُقِفُوا عَلَىٰ رَبِّهِمْ ۚ قَالَ أَلَيْسَ هَٰذَا بِالْحَقِّ ۚ قَالُوا بَلَىٰ وَرَبِّنَا ۚ قَالَ فَذُوقُوا الْعَذَابَ بِمَا كُنْتُمْ تَكْفُرُونَ

wa-law tarā idh wuqifū ʿalā rabbihim qāla a-laysa hādhā bi-al-ḥaqqi qālū balā wa-rabbinā qāla fa-dhūqū al-ʿadhāba bi-mā kuntum takfurūn

Al-Anʿām 6:30

Translation: “If only you could see when they are held before their Lord! He says: ‘Is this not the truth?’ They say: ‘Yes, by our Lord.’ He says: ‘Then taste the punishment because you used to disbelieve.’”

The same verb, wuqifū, appears here, but its complement differs.

In Al-Anʿām 6:27, they are held before the Fire. In Al-Anʿām 6:30, they stand before their Lord.

This shows that waqf need not concern stopping before a material object. Here it places people in accountability before their Lord.

ʿAlā rabbihim should not be translated as people being physically above The God. The preposition does not require that interpretation in this construction.

Arabic can use this family of expressions for appearing before an authority, a judge, or someone entitled to pass judgment on a person.

For example, someone may be brought before an authority and held to answer for their conduct. The point is accountability, not measuring the physical distance between the parties.

In the verse, the dialogue begins immediately:

أَلَيْسَ هَٰذَا بِالْحَقِّ

a-laysa hādhā bi-al-ḥaqq

Al-Anʿām 6:30

They are asked whether what they now face is not the truth, and they answer affirmatively.

Wuqifū describes being held in attendance; the following dialogue shows that this entails truth becoming evident and people answering for themselves.

This verse does not make “knowing The God” the root’s basic meaning. Although people acknowledge a truth in the scene, wuqifū primarily places them in attendance, held before their Lord. Their recognition and acknowledgment are disclosed by what follows.

3. Mawqūfūn: held for the reckoning

Sabaʾ 34:31

وَقَالَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا لَنْ نُؤْمِنَ بِهَٰذَا الْقُرْآنِ وَلَا بِالَّذِي بَيْنَ يَدَيْهِ ۗ وَلَوْ تَرَىٰ إِذِ الظَّالِمُونَ مَوْقُوفُونَ عِنْدَ رَبِّهِمْ يَرْجِعُ بَعْضُهُمْ إِلَىٰ بَعْضٍ الْقَوْلَ يَقُولُ الَّذِينَ اسْتُضْعِفُوا لِلَّذِينَ اسْتَكْبَرُوا لَوْلَا أَنْتُمْ لَكُنَّا مُؤْمِنِينَ

wa-qāla alladhīna kafarū lan nuʾmina bi-hādhā al-qurʾāni wa-lā bi-alladhī bayna yadayhi wa-law tarā idhi al-ẓālimūna mawqūfūna ʿinda rabbihim yarjiʿu baʿḍuhum ilā baʿḍin al-qawla yaqūlu alladhīna ustuḍʿifū li-alladhīna istakbarū law-lā antum la-kunnā muʾminīn

Sabaʾ 34:31

Translation: “Those who disbelieved said: ‘We will never believe in this Qur’an or in what preceded it.’ If only you could see the wrongdoers held before their Lord, passing words back and forth! Those who were weakened say to those who acted arrogantly: ‘Had it not been for you, we would have been believers.’”

Mawqūfūn is the masculine plural passive participle of waqafa.

Unlike the two occurrences in Al-Anʿām, this is not a passive perfect verb. A passive participle describes the people’s condition.

Al-ẓālimūna mawqūfūn presents wrongdoers in a state of being held.

ʿInda rabbihim identifies the setting of their attendance before their Lord. Here, ʿinda does not by itself establish a particular physical distance or spatial coordinates for The God.

The next part of the verse shows this halt becoming a scene in which relationships between followers and misleading leaders are exposed.

Those who were weakened hold the arrogant responsible for their misguidance, saying that without them they would have believed.

In the following verses, the other group replies, and the exchange about each group’s role and responsibility continues.

Mawqūfūn therefore presents more than a motionless crowd. People are held where their statements and claims become manifest, and their own behavior and others’ influence become matters of accountability.

Mawqūfūn itself does not mean criminality, conviction, or questioning. Those aspects come from al-ẓālimūn and the setting of reckoning.

Nor does the word establish equal responsibility for followers and leaders. The verses present their conduct and claims separately.

The sense of being stopped and held remains, while the other parts of the verse disclose their ethical and judicial situation.

4. Wa-qifūhum: a command to halt for questioning

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:24

وَقِفُوهُمْ ۖ إِنَّهُمْ مَسْئُولُونَ

wa-qifūhum innahum masʾūlūn

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:24

Translation: “And hold them; they are to be questioned.”

This is the clearest Qur’anic example of the active transitive use of waqafa.

Wa-qifūhum commands that a group be stopped. The suffix -hum refers to those whose gathering and being led away were described in the preceding verses.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:22 commands the gathering of wrongdoers and their associates. Al-Ṣāffāt 37:23 speaks of leading them to the path of Hell. Then comes the command:

وَقِفُوهُمْ

wa-qifūhum

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:24

That is, stop and hold them.

The next sentence explains why:

إِنَّهُمْ مَسْئُولُونَ

innahum masʾūlūn

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:24

They must answer and undergo questioning.

This sequence distinguishes the root’s meaning from the purpose of the action.

Wa-qifūhum means stopping them; innahum masʾūlūn explains the reason for the stop.

W-q-f should therefore not be defined directly as questioning. Questioning belongs to s-ʾ-l and appears in the second sentence.

“Question them” conveys part of the scene’s general meaning but omits the act of stopping them.

A more precise translation is: “Hold them; they are to be questioned.”

Here, stopping provides the setting for accountability. Those being led along a path are held to face questions before their course continues.

The following verses continue the confrontation and dialogue. They are asked why they do not help one another; then some turn toward others and question them.

Verse 24 itself does not specify the stop’s exact location or duration, or the sequence of every stage of reckoning. Wa-qifūhum alone cannot supply those details.

4. Standing, holding, and questioning

The four Qur’anic occurrences are close in meaning but differ in structure and function.

In Al-Anʿām 6:27 and Al-Anʿām 6:30, wuqifū is passive: people are held, without the agent being explicitly named in the verb.

In Sabaʾ 34:31, mawqūfūn is a passive participle describing their condition.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:24, wa-qifūhum directly commands stopping others.

The family can thus express both the act of stopping and its result: being held in a particular setting.

All four concern people in scenes of reckoning and accountability. This comes from their contexts, not from a fixed meaning shared by every derivative.

Pre-Islamic poetry uses the same root for travelers stopping and a camel being held, without sin, punishment, or questioning being involved.

Waqf therefore cannot be defined inherently as the detention of criminals.

Stopping also need not mean movement ending permanently. In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:24, the command occurs within a scene of gathering and movement toward Hell. The verb alone does not establish an everlasting halt.

5. Waqafa ʿalā: standing before or learning about something

Classical Arabic also has another use of waqafa ʿalā.

For example, waqafa ʿalā al-amr can mean learning about a matter or understanding its reality.

The image of stopping before something and attending to it can help explain the connection with knowing. This is a semantic relationship, not a demonstrated history of how the usage arose.

This lexical use must be distinguished from choosing a translation for the Al-Anʿām passages.

“Held before the Fire” and “held before their Lord” preserve the scene of attendance and confrontation. However, classical explanations of Al-Anʿām 6:27 also include being made aware of the Fire’s reality; that possibility should not be dismissed outright.

For the basic translation here, “being held” better preserves the scene. “Becoming aware” should not replace it without discussion, although it can be presented separately as an interpretive possibility.

In waqafa ʿalā al-amr, the person is the subject of an active verb; the Al-Anʿām passages use the passive. That difference alone does not rule out a sense of being shown or made aware of something. The full construction and context must also be considered.

Grammar and context together therefore guide the choice of translation.

6. Later terminology: endowments and pauses in recitation

Arabic and Persian have other familiar uses of waqf that should not be projected unexamined onto these verses.

One is the endowment of property.

In this terminology, someone dedicates land, a building, or another asset to a particular use, restricting certain ordinary forms of disposal of the principal asset.

The connection with the root is clear: something is held so that it remains available for a defined purpose.

Waqf as a legal institution, with rules on ownership, management, and the use of revenue, has a broader technical meaning.

None of the four Qur’anic occurrences of w-q-f concerns endowing land, a building, or other property.

Legal definitions and rules for endowments cannot therefore be imported into the direct meaning of wuqifū or wa-qifūhum. Nor does the presence or absence of that terminology in the Qur’an by itself settle the legitimacy of every dedication of property to charitable use. That subject requires examination of passages on giving, ownership, bequests, and other relevant provisions.

Another use is pausing and resuming in recitation.

Here, waqf means stopping while reading, as when a reciter pauses at the end of a sentence or verse.

This fits the general meaning of stopping, but the technical rules, symbols, and classifications of recitational pauses are not part of the lexical meaning of the root in Resurrection passages.

Mawqif can also mean a position, social stance, or viewpoint today. These senses relate to standing in a position, but the noun mawqif does not occur in the Qur’an from this root.

7. Comparison with related roots

W-q-f and q-w-m: stopping and rising

Q-w-m has a broad Qur’anic range: rising, standing, establishing, supporting, and undertaking a responsibility.

Waqf focuses more on standing in a place or stopping movement.

Someone may rise and stand while continuing an activity. Someone may also stop while traveling without rising from a seated position.

Qur’anic waqf prominently describes people held in a particular setting. Qiyām can emphasize rising, remaining steadfast, or fulfilling a responsibility, depending on usage.

The two families overlap in standing, but their derivatives are not freely interchangeable.

Study of q-w-m

W-q-f and ḥ-b-s: stopping and holding back

Ḥabs concerns holding, confining, or preventing something from leaving or being released.

Waqf can likewise mean stopping someone else.

They can overlap when applied to people, but waqf does not necessarily mean imprisonment or a lengthy deprivation of freedom.

Someone stopped along the way to answer a question may resume the journey afterward.

Wa-qifūhum in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:24 commands holding people for questioning. The verb alone does not specify the duration or form of that holding.

Study of ḥ-b-s

W-q-f and ʿ-r-ḍ: holding and presenting

In uses concerning display, ʿarḍ means bringing something forward or exposing it to view.

Waqf emphasizes stopping and holding in a particular setting.

In Al-Anʿām 6:27, people are held before the Fire. Seeing and confronting it are part of the scene, but reducing wuqifū to merely showing something would omit the reading that emphasizes their being held.

Someone may be held before something to see it, or be shown something without being stopped.

Confrontation and stopping are related notions that can coincide without being identical.

Study of ʿ-r-ḍ

W-q-f and w-q-ʿ: stopping and occurring

These roots differ only in their final consonant.

W-q-ʿ concerns falling, descending, happening, and coming to pass.

W-q-f concerns standing and stopping movement.

Something can fall to the ground and then stop after impact, but these stages are not one meaning. Falling can be described as wuqūʿ; the subsequent halt can be described through w-q-f.

The distinction is also clear in Qur’anic use: waqaʿa al-qawl concerns a declaration coming to pass, whereas wa-qifūhum commands stopping people.

Their similar spelling must not lead to merging the roots.

Study of w-q-ʿ

W-q-f and s-ʾ-l: holding and asking

Suʾāl concerns asking, requesting, and, in certain contexts, questioning in accountability.

Waqf concerns stopping or holding.

The two appear together in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:24:

Wa-qifūhum commands holding, while masʾūlūn indicates that they are to be questioned.

This conjunction helps prevent a translation error: defining waqf directly as questioning erases the distinction between holding people and the questions they must face.

Study of s-ʾ-l

8. Translation recommendations for Forqan

For intransitive waqafa, “stood,” “stopped,” or “paused” is appropriate.

For transitive use, “stopped,” “held,” or “prevented from continuing to move” is clearer.

For wuqūf, “standing,” “stopping,” or in some contexts “pausing” is suitable.

For wāqif, “standing” or “someone who has stopped in a place” is suggested.

For mawqūf, “stopped,” “held,” or, in a specific detention context, “detained” may fit. “Detained” should not impose the modern legal sense of formal custody or imprisonment without contextual grounds.

Basic equivalents of mawqif are “a place of standing” or “a stopping place.”

For the four Qur’anic occurrences, the following are suggested:

Al-Anʿām 6:27

“When they are held before the Fire.”

Al-Anʿām 6:30

“When they are held before their Lord.”

Sabaʾ 34:31

“When the wrongdoers are held before their Lord.”

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:24

“And hold them; they are to be questioned.”

These renderings distinguish stopping from questioning while preserving attendance and accountability.

“Holding” is broader and more natural here than “making them stand upright,” since it does not dictate their exact bodily posture.

9. Conclusion

Arabic w-q-f denotes standing, pausing, and stopping movement. It can describe someone who stops or someone who stops another.

The poetic witnesses attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays and ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād show both uses in early literary tradition: travelers stopping and a camel held beside the beloved’s dwelling.

All four Qur’anic occurrences appear in Resurrection scenes. Wuqifū describes people held before the Fire or their Lord. Mawqūfūn describes wrongdoers held before their Lord, and wa-qifūhum commands stopping people for questioning.

Stopping is linked with reckoning and accountability in these passages, but the root itself does not mean questioning, conviction, or punishment. Those notions come from accompanying words and context.

Nor should these uses be made to establish precise physical coordinates for The God or the bodily details of people’s standing in the hereafter.

Property endowments and technical recitation pauses are historically and linguistically related uses, but they should not be presented as the direct meanings of these Qur’anic passages.

W-q-f denotes standing or stopping in a place and, transitively, preventing someone or something from continuing to move and holding them there. In the Qur’an, this occurs in scenes of attendance and accountability at Resurrection. The halt provides a setting for facing truth and questioning, while waqf itself expresses the action and state of being held.
