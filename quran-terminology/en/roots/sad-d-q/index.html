Method Note

The root ص د ق (Ṣ D Q) is foundational in the Qur’an because ṣidq is not limited to “telling the truth” in the ordinary ethical sense. Truthful speech is one expression of ṣidq, but it is not the whole meaning. In the Qur’an, ṣidq may appear in speech, covenant loyalty, firmness under test, fulfillment of promise, confirmation of revelation, and in forms of giving that reveal the truthfulness of a person’s inner commitment.

If ṣidq is always translated simply as “truthfulness,” part of its Qur’anic range is lost. Truthfulness often sounds like a matter of the tongue, while Qur’anic ṣidq can point to the harmony of tongue, intention, belief, and action. A ṣādiq is not merely someone who avoids lying; he is someone whose claim proves true when life tests it.

At the same time, ṣidq should not be made vague or overly philosophical. Its central meaning is clear: something is ṣādiq when it corresponds to truth and does not prove crooked, hollow, or false under test.

Central Meaning

The core meaning of Ṣ D Q is correspondence joined with firmness: a statement, claim, promise, or action agrees with reality and truth, and does not collapse under testing.

If a person speaks and his speech corresponds to reality, his speech has ṣidq. If a person has faith and stands by that faith in action, his faith has proven true. If a promise is given and then fulfilled, the promise has become true. If a book confirms earlier revelation, it confirms and makes clear the divine truth within it; it does not validate every later historical interpretation or every existing version without distinction.

So ṣidq is not only a linguistic quality. It is harmony between claim and reality, between tongue and action, between covenant and loyalty, and between faith and perseverance.

Material Image

The simple image behind this root is something that, when measured against reality or placed in the field of trial, does not come out crooked or hollow. Speech with ṣidq does not conflict with reality. A covenant with ṣidq is not abandoned under pressure. Faith with ṣidq is not a calm, cost-free claim; it shows itself in hardship.

Classical Arabic also uses images of firmness and straightness, such as رُمْحٌ صَدْقٌ (rumḥun ṣidq), a sound and straight spear that does not bend or break. This image should not dominate the root, but it helps show why Qur’anic ṣidq goes beyond verbal honesty and reaches firmness in faith, covenant, and action.

Ṣadaqa / Ṣadaqū: A Claim Proving True

The verbal forms of this root may be close to “telling the truth,” but in many verses they carry a wider sense: a person’s claim, covenant, or faith proves true in reality. Thus صَدَقُوا (ṣadaqū) does not always mean only “they spoke truthfully”; it may mean they made their claim true, proved true, or stood faithfully by what they had pledged.

Ṣidq Opposite Kadhib

One clear use of Ṣ D Q is its contrast with falsehood and baseless claims. The Qur’an repeatedly asks opponents to bring proof if they are truthful.

In 2:23, after challenging the doubters to bring a surah like the Qur’an, the verse ends with إِن كُنتُمْ صَادِقِينَ (in kuntum ṣādiqīn). This does not merely mean “if you are not lying.” It means: if your claim corresponds to reality, show it. Ṣidq here is tied to proving a claim and revealing that it is not empty.

Ṣidq and Practical Faith

Verse 2:177 is essential for this root. It connects ṣidq with faith, giving, establishing salat, giving zakat, fulfilling covenants, patience, and taqwa. After describing this whole field, the Qur’an says: أُولَٰئِكَ الَّذِينَ صَدَقُوا وَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْمُتَّقُونَ (ulāʾika alladhīna ṣadaqū wa-ulāʾika humu al-muttaqūn).

Here ṣidq is not merely truthful speech. The verse describes faith, social responsibility, giving, covenant loyalty, and patience under pressure, then says: these are the ones who proved true. Their faith did not remain a statement; it became visible through action, covenant, and endurance.

Ṣidq and Covenant Loyalty

In Surah Al-Ahzab, ṣidq is joined with covenant and firmness under severe testing: مِنَ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ رِجَالٌ صَدَقُوا مَا عَاهَدُوا اللَّهَ عَلَيْهِ (mina al-muʾminīna rijālun ṣadaqū mā ʿāhadū Allāha ʿalayhi) in 33:23.

This means they proved true in what they had pledged to The God. The issue is not verbal honesty alone, but practical loyalty and remaining firm under trial. The phrase وَمَا بَدَّلُوا تَبْدِيلًا (wa-mā baddalū tabdīlā) completes the meaning: they did not alter their covenant or become double in their path.

Being With the Truthful

The Qur’an commands believers: يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا اتَّقُوا اللَّهَ وَكُونُوا مَعَ الصَّادِقِينَ (yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū ittaqū Allāha wa-kūnū maʿa al-ṣādiqīn) in 9:119.

The ṣādiqīn here are not merely people who avoid lying. The context of Surah At-Tawbah involves testing, responsibility, truth and falsehood in claims of faith, and the exposure of hypocrisy. To be with the ṣādiqīn is to join those whose claim and action, inner and outer, faith and responsibility are not split.

Ṣādiq and Ṣādiqīn: True Ones in Claim and Action

A ṣādiq is one whose speech, faith, or covenant corresponds to reality and action. In the Qur’an, being ṣādiq is not only verbal manners; it is a condition revealed under test. This is why the ṣādiqīn often stand in contrast to claimants, hypocrites, or people who speak but do not prove their claim true.

Ṣidq and Faith Without Rayb

In 49:15, the truthful are those who believe in The God and His Messenger, do not fall into rayb, and strive with their wealth and selves in The God’s path: أُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الصَّادِقُونَ (ulāʾika humu al-ṣādiqūn).

Absence of rayb and practical striving show that ṣidq is firmness in a claim and loyalty to faith. If faith is only spoken and no trace of it appears in action, its ṣidq has not yet become clear.

Ṣidq and the Day of Judgment

In 5:119, ṣidq is something that benefits the truthful on the Day of Judgment: هَٰذَا يَوْمُ يَنفَعُ الصَّادِقِينَ صِدْقُهُمْ (hādhā yawmu yanfaʿu al-ṣādiqīna ṣidquhum).

Ṣidq here is not merely today’s claim. It is a reality that carries weight on the day when truth becomes manifest. A person may make claims in the world, but when realities are disclosed, only real ṣidq will benefit.

The God’s Truth and Fulfilled Promise

The Qur’an uses ṣidq for The God’s speech and for fulfilled divine promise. In 3:95, it says: قُلْ صَدَقَ اللَّهُ (qul ṣadaqa Allāh). The God’s truth is absolute because His knowledge and speech are free from ignorance, error, and falsehood.

In 36:52, when the promise becomes reality, it is said: هَٰذَا مَا وَعَدَ الرَّحْمَٰنُ وَصَدَقَ الْمُرْسَلُونَ (hādhā mā waʿada al-Raḥmān wa-ṣadaqa al-mursalūn). The messengers proved true because what they announced came to pass.

Taṣdīq and Harmony With Prior Revelation

One important Qur’anic use of this root is taṣdīq: confirming a truth that came before, not accepting every later religious claim without distinction.

In 10:37, the Qur’an is called تَصْدِيقَ الَّذِي بَيْنَ يَدَيْهِ (taṣdīqa alladhī bayna yadayhi). This means the Qur’an agrees with, confirms, and clarifies the divine truth of earlier revelation. It does not mean that every interpretation, alteration, translation, or historical tradition is approved without examination. The Qur’an confirms the divine truth and also stands as criterion and furqan.

Ṣiddīq and Ṣiddīqīn

Ṣiddīq is a person in whom truthfulness has reached a distinguished station: one who recognizes truth, confirms it, and remains firm in relation to it. Abraham is described as صِدِّيقًا نَّبِيًّا (ṣiddīqan nabiyyan) in 19:41.

Joseph is addressed as يُوسُفُ أَيُّهَا الصِّدِّيقُ (Yūsufu ayyuhā al-ṣiddīq) in 12:46. Here ṣiddīq means someone known for truth, trustworthiness, and firmness; Joseph is not merely an interpreter of dreams, but a person whose truthfulness has become visible in conduct, speech, and trust.

The Qur’an also places the ṣiddīqīn beside the prophets, witnesses, and righteous ones, as in 4:69. The ṣiddīqīn are not merely honest speakers; they are people of confirmed truth and loyalty to it.

Ṣiddīqah

The Qur’an calls Mary, the mother of Jesus, a ṣiddīqah: وَأُمُّهُ صِدِّيقَةٌ (wa-ummuhu ṣiddīqah) in 5:75.

Her being ṣiddīqah is set against exaggerated claims about Jesus and his mother. The Qur’an affirms Mary’s rank through truthfulness and purity, but does not move her outside the human station. For translation, ṣiddīqah means a deeply truthful woman, true-believing and faithful to truth, not a being beyond humanity.

Ṣidq in Station, Path, and Honorable Remembrance

Sometimes ṣidq is attached to things that sound unusual in English, such as لِسَانَ صِدْقٍ (lisāna ṣidq), مُدْخَلَ صِدْقٍ (mudkhala ṣidq), مُخْرَجَ صِدْقٍ (mukhraja ṣidq), and قَدَمَ صِدْقٍ (qadama ṣidq). In these expressions, ṣidq points to firmness, rightness, honor, and alignment with truth.

In 19:50, لِسَانَ صِدْقٍ عَلِيًّا (lisāna ṣidqin ʿaliyyan) means an elevated truthful remembrance, not false fame. In 17:80, mudkhal ṣidq and mukhraj ṣidq point to an entrance and exit that are true, sound, pure, and aligned with the truth. In 10:2, qadam ṣidq may point to a true and firm standing or station with the Lord.

Ṣadaqah and Ṣadaqāt

Ṣadaqah and ṣadaqāt also come from this root. In the Qur’an, ṣadaqah is not merely financial assistance; it is giving that should be joined with truthfulness of faith, social responsibility, and purity of intention.

In 9:103, the Prophet is told: خُذْ مِنْ أَمْوَالِهِمْ صَدَقَةً تُطَهِّرُهُمْ وَتُزَكِّيهِم بِهَا (khudh min amwālihim ṣadaqatan tuṭahhiruhum wa-tuzakkīhim bihā). Ṣadaqah here is connected with purification and growth. It is not only the transfer of wealth; it can reveal the truthfulness of faith and responsibility toward the community.

Ṣadaqah and zakat, however, should not be collapsed into one word everywhere. Their fields may overlap, but context must decide. Zakat is strongly connected with growth, purity, and ordered financial-social responsibility; ṣadaqah more directly shows the giving or payment through which inner truthfulness becomes outwardly visible.

Ṣaduqātihinna and Truthfulness in Marriage

One important and often under-read use of this root is صَدُقَاتِهِنَّ (ṣaduqātihinna) in the verse about women: وَآتُوا النِّسَاءَ صَدُقَاتِهِنَّ نِحْلَةً (wa-ātū al-nisāʾa ṣaduqātihinna niḥlah) in 4:4.

This payment is not the price of a woman or ownership over her. Its connection with Ṣ D Q shows that it should be a sign of truthful intention, seriousness of covenant, and real responsibility at the beginning of marriage.

The continuation of the verse shows that this right belongs to the woman. If she willingly gives up part of it, then benefiting from it is permitted. But the original due must be given to her. Therefore ṣaduqātihinna should not be understood as a sale transaction; it is closer to the truthfulness of covenant and respect for the woman’s right in marriage.

Difference From Nearby Words

Ṣidq is close to ḥaqq, but not identical with it. Ḥaqq points to truth, reality, and established right itself. Ṣidq more often concerns the harmony of our speech, belief, claim, or action with that truth. In simple terms, ḥaqq is the established reality; ṣidq is when the tongue, faith, promise, or conduct corresponds to that reality.

Ṣidq is not the same as birr. Birr is broad responsible goodness, while 2:177 shows ṣidq as the practical truth of such goodness. Ṣidq is close to wafāʾ, but wafāʾ means completing a covenant fully, while ṣidq means the claim or covenant proves true. Ṣidq also differs from amānah, though a trustworthy person should be truthful and a truthful person should not betray a trust.

Ṣidq is not identical with faith either. Faith is confirmation, trust, and orientation toward truth; ṣidq shows that faith has not remained a claim, but has proven true in action and perseverance. Ṣidq also stands in deep contrast with nifāq, because nifāq is a split between outer and inner, claim and reality, while ṣidq means that split is absent.

Forqan Translation Method

Forqan should not limit Ṣ D Q to “telling the truth” everywhere. That rendering is natural in some verses, but it is not enough in many others.

Sometimes ṣidq means truthful speech. Sometimes it means a promise proving true. Sometimes it means proving a claim through action. Sometimes it means confirming truth. Sometimes it indicates deep truthfulness and the rank of ṣiddīq. Sometimes it names a giving or payment that reveals truthfulness of intention and responsibility. In phrases like mudkhal ṣidq, lisān ṣidq, and qadam ṣidq, it can mean rightness, firmness, honor, and true standing.

Forqan Translation Suggestions

  • in kuntum ṣādiqīn: if you are truthful; or if you are true in your claim.
  • ulāʾika alladhīna ṣadaqū: those are the ones who proved true; or those are the ones who made their claim true.
  • kūnū maʿa al-ṣādiqīn: be with the truthful; or be with the true ones.
  • ṣadaqū mā ʿāhadū Allāha ʿalayhi: they proved true in what they had pledged to The God.
  • ṣadaqa Allāh: The God spoke truth; or The God’s speech is true.
  • wa-ṣadaqa al-mursalūn: the messengers spoke truth; or the word of the messengers proved true.
  • taṣdīq alladhī bayna yadayhi: confirming what was before it; or confirming the truth that came before it.
  • al-ṣiddīqīn: the ṣiddīqīn; or the deeply truthful ones.
  • wa-ummuhu ṣiddīqah: and his mother was a ṣiddīqah; or his mother was a deeply truthful woman.
  • lisān ṣidq: a tongue of truth; or truthful honorable remembrance.
  • mudkhal ṣidq / mukhraj ṣidq: an entrance of truth and an exit of truth; or a true and sound entrance and exit.
  • qadam ṣidq: qadam ṣidq; or a true and firm station with the Lord.
  • ṣadaqah: ṣadaqah; or truthful giving, if the context calls for explanation.
  • ṣaduqātihinna: the women’s ṣaduqāt; or the true due of women at the beginning of marriage. In smoother translation it may be rendered as marital due or mahr, with an explanation that the word comes from Ṣ D Q and must not be understood as a price for the woman.

Summary

The root Ṣ D Q in the Qur’an is not limited to verbal truthfulness. Its central meaning is correspondence with truth and firmness in claim. Truthful speech corresponds to reality. A truthful human being is one whose claim of faith, covenant, intention, and action are coherent. A true promise is one that is fulfilled. A confirming book is one that affirms and clarifies the truth of earlier revelation.

The Qur’an shows ṣidq in many fields: opposite falsehood, in practical faith, covenant loyalty, companionship with the truthful, the Day of Judgment, The God’s promise, confirmation of revelation, the rank of ṣiddīqīn, ṣadaqah, ṣaduqātihinna, and expressions such as lisān ṣidq, mudkhal ṣidq, mukhraj ṣidq, and qadam ṣidq.

For Forqan translation, this root should not be forced into one rigid equivalent. “Truthfulness” is a good center, but each verse must show where that truthfulness appears: in speech, promise, faith, action, covenant, giving, marriage, or confirmation of truth.
