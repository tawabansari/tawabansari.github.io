Fear, astonishment, and a powerful impression on the heart and mind

Brief meaning: Fear, alarm, and apprehension; frightening another. Another branch concerns the powerful impression of beauty, grandeur, or greatness. Rūʿ, with ḍammah on the rāʾ, also denotes the heart, mind, or inner self in early Arabic. Only al-rawʿ, meaning fear or alarm, occurs in the Qur’an.

1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic form

The three-consonant root R W ʿ concerns fear, alarm, astonishment, and an event’s deep effect on a person.

In rāʿahu al-shayʾu, something frightens or alarms someone. A sudden event, troubling news, or an unfamiliar situation may evoke fear.

Rawwaʿahu also means frightening or alarming another.

The family is not restricted to fear.

Early Arabic rāʿanī al-shayʾu can describe something that impresses a person through beauty, greatness, or grandeur.

A beautiful scene may so arrest someone’s attention that their ordinary composure momentarily changes. Fear need not be involved.

Rāʾiʿ describes something whose beauty or grandeur makes a striking impression.

In another usage, rūʿ with ḍammah denotes the heart, mind, or inner self.

The vowel matters: al-rawʿ in Hūd 11:74 means fear, not Abraham’s heart or intellect leaving him.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, in the account of Abraham and the divine messengers, when his fear departs and good news reaches him.

The form examined

الرَّوْعُ — Hūd 11:74

This consists of the definite article al- and the noun rawʿ.

It is a definite nominative noun and the subject of dhahaba.

Its meaning here is fear or alarm.

Other forms and derivatives are known in Arabic:

  • رَوْع: Fear or alarm, with fatḥah on rāʾ.
  • رُوع: Heart, mind, thoughts, or inner self, with ḍammah.
  • رَاعَ يَرُوعُ: Frightened or alarmed; in another usage, impressed or astonished.
  • رَوَّعَ: Frightened someone.
  • تَرْوِيع: Frightening or alarming.
  • ارْتَاعَ: Became frightened or alarmed.
  • تَرَوَّعَ: Became fearful or apprehensive.
  • رَوْعَة: An instance of fright; in another branch, an impressive touch of beauty.
  • رَوْعَات: Plural of rawʿah: frights or alarming experiences.
  • رَائِع: Impressive through beauty, grandeur, or strong effect; in some early usages, a frightened person or animal.
  • أَرْوَع: A man of striking appearance, stature, or presence.
  • رَوْعَاء: In some usages, a strikingly beautiful woman; of animals, an alert and sensitive female.
  • رُوَاع: A person of striking beauty; also used of a woman. This should be distinguished from descriptions of an animal’s sensitivity.

These occur in early Arabic records, but only al-rawʿ occurs in the Qur’an.

2. The semantic core in early Arabic

Fear and its seat in an early lexical analysis

An early lexical analysis proposes one principle for the root:

الرَّاءُ وَالْوَاوُ وَالْعَيْنُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى فَزَعٍ أَوْ مُسْتَقَرِّ فَزَعٍ

al-rāʾu wa-al-wāwu wa-al-ʿaynu aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā fazaʿin aw mustaqarri fazaʿin

Translation: “Rāʾ, wāw, and ʿayn have one principle indicating fright or the seat of fright.”

This definition has two aspects.

First, the experience of fear itself.

Second, where fear is experienced: the heart or inner self.

Arabic accordingly distinguishes two similarly written forms:

Rawʿ, with fatḥah: fear or alarm.

Rūʿ, with ḍammah: heart, mind, or inner self.

This distinction is also recorded in other early lexical explanations.

Arabic uses the expression:

وَقَعَ ذَلِكَ فِي رُوعِي

waqaʿa dhālika fī rūʿī

Meaning: “That entered my heart, mind, or thoughts.”

Rūʿī here means my mind or inner self, not my fear.

The unvocalized spelling should not always be translated “fear” without considering vowels and context.

Must rawʿ always be intense and sudden?

Many usages associate alarm with a sudden or unexpected event.

Someone may hear an unfamiliar sound, encounter an unexpected person, or perceive danger.

This can disturb their ordinary calm.

Such examples do not make every usage necessarily brief, intense, or caused by a completely sudden event.

The word itself belongs to the field of fear and alarm.

Context establishes its intensity, duration, and cause.

Hūd 11:74 describes fear that had arisen in Abraham and has now departed.

Nearby verses explain its cause.

Al-rawʿ itself specifies neither the exact intensity nor the duration.

Rāʿanī al-amru: something frightened me

An early lexical explanation gives:

رَاعَنِي الأَمْرُ يَرُوعُنِي رَوْعًا

rāʿanī al-amru yarūʿunī rawʿan

Meaning: “The matter frightened and alarmed me.”

The cause of fear is the grammatical subject and the frightened person the object.

For example, hearing troubling news may alarm someone.

The verb does not establish that the news represented an actual danger.

Someone may first be afraid and later discover that no danger existed.

Feeling fear and facing real danger are distinct matters.

This matters in Abraham’s story.

The unfamiliar guests’ behavior alarms him, but his fear departs after their mission becomes clear and good news arrives.

Rawwaʿahu: frightening another

Rawwaʿa is a Form II verb.

Arabic says:

رَوَّعْتُهُ فَارْتَاعَ

rawwaʿtuhu fa-irtāʿa

Meaning: “I frightened him, and he became frightened.”

Rawwaʿa here concerns causing fear in another.

Intending to frighten and actually producing fear are not always the same.

Someone may deliberately threaten another to frighten them.

An event may cause fear without anyone intending it.

Someone may also intend to frighten a person who remains unafraid.

An event’s agent, that agent’s intention, and the person’s reaction must therefore be distinguished.

Rawwaʿa does not occur in the Qur’an.

Hūd 11:74 speaks of fear itself and its departure.

Irtāʿa: becoming frightened

Irtāʿa is recorded as meaning becoming afraid or alarmed.

This construction foregrounds the condition of the person experiencing fear.

For example:

ارْتَاعَ مِنَ الصَّوْتِ

irtāʿa mina al-ṣawti

Meaning: “He was frightened by the sound.”

This relates to rāʿa but uses a different sentence structure.

In rāʿanī al-ṣawtu, the sound is the subject and “me” the object.

In irtaʿtu mina al-ṣawti, the frightened person is the subject.

This matters for identifying the morphological forms.

Irtāʿa may also express a strong reaction to news or an event without encoding every detail of its cause.

Rawʿah: an instance of fright

Rawʿah can denote a single fright or state of fear.

Its plural is rawʿāt.

It can describe an experience that alarms someone for a time.

The plural does not necessarily mean several actual dangers.

Someone may repeatedly fear things later found harmless.

Conversely, one dangerous incident may cause prolonged fear.

The number of frights is thus not identical to the number of dangers.

Rawʿah and rawʿāt do not occur in the Qur’an.

They nevertheless help explain the root’s lexical field.

Afrakha rawʿuhu: fear subsiding

Arabic records the familiar expression:

أَفْرَخَ رَوْعُهُ

afrakha rawʿuhu

It describes fear subsiding and a person becoming calm.

Some early explanations connect its imagery with a chick emerging from an egg.

Fear is pictured as leaving its dwelling, releasing the person from its pressure.

Lexicographers differ over the vowel on rāʾ in this expression.

The commonly reported reading is rawʿ with fatḥah: the fear itself has gone.

Another reading prefers rūʿ with ḍammah, referring to the heart or seat of fear.

An early discussion considers this explanation plausible while distinguishing it from the more commonly transmitted reading.

The disagreement shows attention to the relationship between fear and its inner seat.

The expression does not mean that a person’s heart physically leaves the body.

Its image concerns the departure of fear.

“ذَهَبَ عَنْ إِبْرَاهِيمَ الرَّوْعُ” in Hūd 11:74 belongs to the same field of fear subsiding.

Rūʿ: heart and inner self

An early lexical definition states:

وَالرُّوعُ بِالضَّمِّ: الْقَلْبُ وَالْعَقْلُ

wa-al-rūʿu bi-al-ḍammi al-qalbu wa-al-ʿaqlu

Translation: “Rūʿ, with ḍammah, is the heart and intellect.”

Waqaʿa fī rūʿī is also recorded as “entered my mind or thoughts.”

This usage clarifies an important point.

Heart and mind can function in ordinary language as seats of thought, feeling, and understanding, beyond references to organs or physical capacities.

This lexical usage does not establish a scientific theory of the mind’s physical location or the neurological mechanisms of emotion.

Rūʿ here names the inner self or mind.

Al-rawʿ in Hūd 11:74, by contrast, has fatḥah and denotes fear.

Pronunciation and meaning distinguish the two forms.

3. Wonder, beauty, and grandeur

Rāʿanī al-shayʾu: being impressed by beauty

An early explanation says that something impressive through beauty or abundance can be described as follows:

رَاعَنِي فَهُوَ رَائِعٌ

rāʿanī fa-huwa rāʾiʿun

Meaning: “It impressed me; it is impressive.”

This differs from fear of danger.

A beautiful view, magnificent building, or remarkable ability may deeply impress someone.

The response may involve admiration and happiness rather than fear.

Not every rāʾiʿ should therefore be translated “frightening.”

In modern Arabic, rāʾiʿ commonly describes something excellent, beautiful, or impressive.

That positive usage must not replace the Qur’anic meaning of al-rawʿ.

Hūd 11:74 clearly concerns Abraham’s fear.

Rawʿah and beauty

One branch uses rawʿah for an impressive touch of beauty or grandeur.

Something makes a striking impression on the observer.

This differs from rawʿah as a fright.

Similarity of form does not make the emotional value identical.

A person may fear danger or marvel at beauty.

Both experiences can be intense, while one is unpleasant and the other pleasant.

“Fear” alone cannot explain the entire family.

Conversely, making beauty its only meaning would obscure the Qur’anic al-rawʿ.

Arwaʿ: grandeur, stature, and presence

Arwaʿ can describe a man of striking stature, beauty, or imposing presence.

An early lexical explanation relates this to the impression his appearance makes on the observer.

Some usages also describe nobility, standing, and distinction.

A powerful impression can thus involve admiration and respect.

Beauty, nobility, and imposing presence are not identical qualities.

Someone physically beautiful may behave badly.

Someone noble and respected may lack striking physical features.

Context determines the particular sense of arwaʿ.

Rawʿāʾ: an animal’s alertness and sensitivity

Rawʿāʾ describes certain female animals, especially mares and she-camels.

One explanation concerns an alert, sensitive animal that reacts quickly to sounds and movements.

Another concerns beauty and striking appearance.

The explanations should not be conflated without evidence.

An animal may respond to the slightest sign of danger.

Another may stand out for its beauty or build.

The shared word does not make every beautiful animal timid or every alert animal magnificent in appearance.

This matters when reading early poetry.

4. Pre-Islamic and early Arabic poetic evidence

Alarm in ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād’s poetry

The pre-Islamic poet ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād says in his Muʿallaqah:

مَا رَاعَنِي إِلَّا حَمُولَةُ أَهْلِهَا
وَسْطَ الدِّيَارِ تَسُفُّ حَبَّ الْخِمْخِمِ

mā rāʿanī illā ḥamūlatu ahlihā / wasṭa al-diyāri tasuffu ḥabba al-khimkhimi

Translation: “Nothing alarmed me but her people’s pack-camels, eating khimkhim seeds amid the dwellings.”

The previous line describes preparations for departure by night.

His alarm concerns signs of separation from his beloved as her family leaves.

Mā rāʿanī here concerns what alarmed him; mā … illā expresses restriction, not an unconditional negation.

The danger is not an attack or death.

The prospect of separation disturbs him.

This usage shows that the family is not restricted to fear of bodily harm.

An emotional event or troubling news may also cause shock and apprehension.

Rāʿanī itself does not mean romantic separation; the poem supplies that context.

The line is recorded in ʿAntarah’s Muʿallaqah and provides evidence from pre-Islamic poetry.

An animal’s fear of a hunter in al-Nābighah’s poetry

The pre-Islamic poet al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī describes an animal hearing a hunter:

فَارْتَاعَ مِنْ صَوْتِ كَلَّابٍ فَبَاتَ لَهُ
طَوْعُ الشَّوَامِتِ مِنْ خَوْفٍ وَمِنْ صُرَدِ

fa-irtāʿa min ṣawti kallābin fa-bāta lahu / ṭawʿu al-shawāmiti min khawfin wa-min ṣaradi

Approximate translation, following the nominative ṭawʿu reading: “It took fright at the dog-handler’s voice and spent the night in fear and cold, in a condition that would please its gloating enemies.” Kallāb denotes the hunter or dog-handler, not the plural “dogs”; ṭawʿ also has a transmitted accusative reading with another explanation.

Fa-irtāʿa describes the animal’s fearful reaction.

The cause is named in the poem: the hunter’s voice.

The passage places irtāʿa in a concrete, perceptible situation.

The verb itself does not mean hearing the hunter’s voice.

Hearing causes the fear; irtāʿa describes the reaction.

Its proximity to khawf also shows their semantic closeness.

That co-occurrence does not make them interchangeable throughout Arabic.

Lā turaʿ: reassurance in Abū Khirāsh’s poetry

Abū Khirāsh al-Hudhalī says:

رَفَوْنِي وَقَالُوا يَا خُوَيْلِدُ لَا تُرَعْ
فَقُلْتُ وَأَنْكَرْتُ الْوُجُوهَ هُمُ هُمُ

rafawnī wa-qālū yā Khuwaylidu lā turaʿ / fa-qultu wa-ankartu al-wujūha humu humu

Translation: “They reassured me and said, ‘Khuwaylid, do not fear.’ Finding the faces unfamiliar or troubling, I said, ‘It is them!’” Ankartu must not simply be rendered “recognized.”

The line concerns the poet’s encounter with people he feared.

Lā turaʿ means “Do not fear.”

The root again belongs to the field of fear and alarm.

Rafawnī concerns calming or reassuring him.

The reassurance does not remove his suspicion of the faces before him.

Verbal reassurance and actual relief from fear are distinct.

This also matters in Abraham’s story: saying lā takhaf and achieving inner calm are not linguistically or experientially identical.

Abū Khirāsh lived across the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods.

The poem’s exact date is uncertain, so it should not unconditionally be assigned to the period before the Qur’an.

Alertness in Imruʾ al-Qays’s poetry

In a poem attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays, describing a traveling she-camel:

تَخْدِي عَلَى الْعِلَّاتِ سَامٍ رَأْسُهَا
رَوْعَاءُ مَنْسِمُهَا رَثِيمٌ دَامِي

takhdī ʿalā al-ʿillāti sāmin raʾsuhā / rawʿāʾu mansimuhā rathīmun dāmī

Approximate translation: “She strides on despite hardships, her head held high: a sensitive she-camel with bruised and bleeding footpads.”

Rawʿāʾ describes the she-camel.

Lexical explanations associate it with sensitivity and quick reactions.

Such an animal may rapidly respond to something seen or heard.

That should not be equated with cowardice or incapacity.

The poem itself depicts continued movement despite the journey’s hardships.

Sensitivity and endurance can coexist in one animal.

This pre-Islamic poetic attribution illustrates the family’s use for animal behavior and sensitivity.

Relief from distress

The Umayyad poet Dhū al-Rummah says:

جَذْلَانَ قَدْ أَفْرَخَتْ عَنْ رُوعِهِ الْكُرَبُ

jadhlāna qad afrakh-at ʿan rūʿihi al-kurabu

Translation: “Joyful, the afflictions have departed from his heart.”

Rūʿihi, with ḍammah, refers to the heart or inner self.

Al-kurab denotes afflictions and heavy distress.

The image concerns release from inner pressure and the return of joy.

Lexical discussions also use this line to distinguish rawʿ from rūʿ.

Dhū al-Rummah lived in the Umayyad period; his poetry is evidence for early Arabic after the Qur’an’s revelation.

It remains useful for understanding literary usage.

What the poems establish

The poems illustrate several distinct usages.

ʿAntarah’s rāʿanī concerns alarm at signs of separation.

Al-Nābighah’s irtāʿa describes an animal’s fear of a hunter’s voice.

Abū Khirāsh’s lā turaʿ addresses a frightened person reassuringly.

Imruʾ al-Qays’s rawʿāʾ concerns an animal’s sensitivity and responsiveness.

Dhū al-Rummah’s rūʿ names the inner seat of feeling and thought.

Not every derivative should be reduced to a single experience such as fear of death, terror, or striking beauty.

5. Qur’anic usage: Abraham’s fear departs

Hūd 11:74

Verse:

فَلَمَّا ذَهَبَ عَنْ إِبْرَاهِيمَ الرَّوْعُ وَجَاءَتْهُ الْبُشْرَىٰ يُجَادِلُنَا فِي قَوْمِ لُوطٍ

fa-lammā dhahaba ʿan Ibrāhīma al-rawʿu wa-jāʾathu al-bushrā yujādilunā fī qawmi Lūṭin

Hūd 11:74

Translation:

“Then, when fear had departed from Abraham and the good news had reached him, he was pleading with Us about Lot’s people.”

Al-rawʿ in the verse

Al-rawʿ here means fear or alarm.

It follows dhahaba as its subject.

Dhahaba ʿan Ibrāhīma al-rawʿu means that Abraham’s fear departed.

Fear is presented as something previously experienced and now removed.

This does not describe an object’s physical relocation.

Fear is an inner state; dhahaba ʿanhu here describes its ending.

Al-rawʿ does not mean Abraham’s heart or intellect.

It has fatḥah and means fear.

Confusing it with rūʿ, “heart,” would distort the sentence.

Hūd 11:69: Abraham’s guests arrive

Verse:

وَلَقَدْ جَاءَتْ رُسُلُنَا إِبْرَاهِيمَ بِالْبُشْرَىٰ قَالُوا سَلَامًا ۖ قَالَ سَلَامٌ ۖ فَمَا لَبِثَ أَنْ جَاءَ بِعِجْلٍ حَنِيذٍ

wa-laqad jāʾat rusulunā Ibrāhīma bi-al-bushrā qālū salāman qāla salāmun fa-mā labitha an jāʾa bi-ʿijlin ḥanīdhin

Hūd 11:69

Translation:

“Our messengers came to Abraham with good news. They said, ‘Peace.’ He said, ‘Peace,’ and soon brought a roasted calf.”

This introduces the divine messengers’ arrival.

They greet Abraham, and he returns their greeting.

He then prepares food for them.

The scene initially resembles ordinary hospitality.

The next verse describes behavior that alarms him.

Hūd 11:70: the cause of fear

Verse:

فَلَمَّا رَأَىٰ أَيْدِيَهُمْ لَا تَصِلُ إِلَيْهِ نَكِرَهُمْ وَأَوْجَسَ مِنْهُمْ خِيفَةً ۚ قَالُوا لَا تَخَفْ إِنَّا أُرْسِلْنَا إِلَىٰ قَوْمِ لُوطٍ

fa-lammā raʾā aydiyahum lā taṣilu ilayhi nakirahum wa-awjasa minhum khīfatan qālū lā takhaf innā ursilnā ilā qawmi Lūṭin

Hūd 11:70

Translation:

“When he saw their hands did not reach for it, he found them strange and felt fear of them within himself. They said, ‘Do not fear; we have been sent to Lot’s people.’”

This directly clarifies al-rawʿ in Hūd 11:74.

Abraham has brought food but sees that the guests do not touch it.

Their behavior seems unusual to him.

The verse then says:

“نَكِرَهُمْ”

He found them strange or unfamiliar.

And:

“أَوْجَسَ مِنْهُمْ خِيفَةً”

He felt fear of them within himself.

The Qur’an itself thus explains the cause of his alarm.

Their behavior did not fit his ordinary expectations of guests.

This worried him.

The verse does not say they intended to harm him.

Perceiving danger and an actual intention to harm are distinct.

His fear arises before their mission becomes clear.

Did Abraham’s fear show weak faith?

The Qur’an explicitly reports his fear.

It does not label it sin, disbelief, or weak faith.

He encounters something unusual and responds humanly.

Rather than condemning his fear, the messengers say:

“لَا تَخَفْ”

Meaning: “Do not fear.”

They then explain their mission.

This sequence matters.

A person may have faith and trust in The God yet fear an unfamiliar event or sign of danger.

Fear itself does not prove a wrong decision or lack of faith.

Understanding reality, responding once it becomes clear, and fulfilling one’s responsibilities matter.

Abraham’s fear also departs as the situation becomes clearer.

Al-rawʿ here should not be made into a negative moral judgment about him.

“Do not fear” and “the fear departed”

In Hūd 11:70, the messengers say:

“لَا تَخَفْ”

In Hūd 11:74, the Qur’an says:

“ذَهَبَ عَنْ إِبْرَاهِيمَ الرَّوْعُ”

The sentences are clearly connected.

Abraham first experiences fear.

He is then reassured.

After the good news, the narrative reports the departure of his fear.

The sentences nonetheless differ in type.

Lā takhaf addresses him with an instruction not to fear.

Dhahaba al-rawʿu reports that fear has ended.

They are not complete synonyms.

A person told not to fear may remain worried for a time.

Fear actually departing signifies a changed inner state.

The story expresses both stages separately.

Good news reaches him

Hūd 11:74 pairs the departure of fear with good news:

“وَجَاءَتْهُ الْبُشْرَىٰ”

Hūd 11:71 announces Isaac and, after him, Jacob.

In Hūd 11:72, Abraham’s wife expresses astonishment because she and her husband are old.

In Hūd 11:73, the messengers speak of The God’s command, mercy, and blessings.

The good news concerns Abraham’s family’s future.

The messengers have also explained in Hūd 11:70 that they were sent to Lot’s people.

This information also relates to relief concerning Abraham’s own safety and household.

Early explanations offer two readings of the precise good news in Hūd 11:74.

Some identify it as the announcement of a child.

Others emphasize Abraham learning that the messengers had not come to harm him.

The nearby verses explicitly announce a child and also clarify the messengers’ mission.

One aspect should not be emphasized so exclusively that the other disappears from the context.

Lexically, al-rawʿ itself does not mean good news.

Fear is expressed through R W ʿ and good news through B Sh R.

Two events joined in the opening clause

Hūd 11:74 opens with two events:

Abraham’s fear departing.

The good news reaching him.

His pleading about Lot’s people follows.

This narrative sequence matters.

While the guests remain unfamiliar, his concern relates to possible danger.

After their mission becomes clear and good news arrives, the discussion turns to Lot’s people.

This does not mean that people must overcome all their fears before speaking about others’ rights or fate.

The verse recounts a particular event, not a universal psychological law.

Dhahaba al-rawʿu also does not establish the exact duration of the inner change.

6. After fear: concern for others

Pleading about Lot’s people

Hūd 11:74 continues:

“يُجَادِلُنَا فِي قَوْمِ لُوطٍ”

Meaning: “He was pleading with Us about Lot’s people.”

The verb belongs to J D L and concerns discussion and argument.

Some early explanations understand Abraham as discussing the matter with the divine messengers; because they came by The God’s command, the discussion is attributed to The God.

That explanation can be considered contextually, since the messengers are present.

The Qur’anic wording itself nevertheless says:

“يُجَادِلُنَا”

A direct translation should not silently replace this with “he argued with the angels.”

Explaining the messengers’ mediation belongs to contextual interpretation.

Jidāl here need not signify hostility, disrespect, or denial of the divine command.

The following verse helps clarify the discussion’s character.

Hūd 11:75: forbearance and turning back

Verse:

إِنَّ إِبْرَاهِيمَ لَحَلِيمٌ أَوَّاهٌ مُنِيبٌ

inna Ibrāhīma la-ḥalīmun awwāhun munībun

Hūd 11:75

Translation:

“Abraham was indeed forbearing, oft-sighing, and ever turning back.”

This follows the mention of his pleading about Lot’s people.

Three qualities are named:

“Ḥalīm.”

“Awwāh.”

“Munīb.”

These qualities have moral and spiritual significance.

Ḥalīm concerns forbearance.

Awwāh relates to sighing, lamentation, and deep feeling.

Munīb concerns returning and turning toward The God.

The context portrays Abraham as forbearing, deeply moved, and turning back.

His pleading should therefore not be characterized without evidence as contentiousness or selfish opposition to The God.

These qualities are not al-rawʿ’s lexical meaning.

That word names his earlier fear; its departure is expressed by dhahaba.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:32: concern for Lot

Verse:

قَالَ إِنَّ فِيهَا لُوطًا ۚ قَالُوا نَحْنُ أَعْلَمُ بِمَنْ فِيهَا ۖ لَنُنَجِّيَنَّهُ وَأَهْلَهُ إِلَّا امْرَأَتَهُ كَانَتْ مِنَ الْغَابِرِينَ

qāla inna fīhā Lūṭan qālū naḥnu aʿlamu bi-man fīhā la-nunajjiyannahu wa-ahlahu illā imraʾatahu kānat mina al-ghābirīna

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:32

Translation:

“He said, ‘Lot is there.’ They said, ‘We know best who is there. We will certainly save him and his family, except his wife; she was among those who remained behind.’”

This clarifies another part of Abraham’s conversation with the messengers.

He points out that Lot is in the settlement.

They reply that they know its inhabitants and will save Lot and his family except his wife.

Lot’s fate is therefore one clear subject of Abraham’s concern.

Some explanatory narratives give extended conversations about possible numbers of believers in the settlements.

This verse states the specific point about Lot’s presence and the promise to save him.

Unstated numerical details should not be added to the Qur’anic text.

The verse clarifies Hūd 11:74 without changing R W ʿ’s lexical meaning.

Hūd 11:76: the discussion ends

Verse:

يَا إِبْرَاهِيمُ أَعْرِضْ عَنْ هَٰذَا ۖ إِنَّهُ قَدْ جَاءَ أَمْرُ رَبِّكَ ۖ وَإِنَّهُمْ آتِيهِمْ عَذَابٌ غَيْرُ مَرْدُودٍ

yā Ibrāhīmu aʿriḍ ʿan hādhā innahu qad jāʾa amru rabbika wa-innahum ātīhim ʿadhābun ghayru mardūdin

Hūd 11:76

Translation:

“Abraham, turn away from this. Your Lord’s command has come, and a punishment that cannot be repelled is coming upon them.”

This instructs him to end the discussion.

His pleading occurs in a particular situation and has a limit.

Its ending does not make his concern for others inherently blameworthy.

The preceding verse describes him positively.

His forbearance and concern for people can be distinguished from the execution of an irrevocable command.

That understanding comes from the passage, not al-rawʿ alone.

Did every concern end when his fear departed?

In Hūd 11:74, his fear concerning the guests subsides.

He then speaks about Lot’s people.

These are different concerns.

Fear for one’s own safety may end while concern for others remains.

Once the guests’ situation becomes clear, Abraham’s attention turns elsewhere.

Dhahaba al-rawʿu does not mean all his feelings and concerns ceased in every situation.

It concerns a particular fear arising when the guests arrived.

Al-rawʿ names that alarm, not all Abraham’s inner states.

7. Parallel passages and related roots

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:28: inwardly felt fear

Verse:

فَأَوْجَسَ مِنْهُمْ خِيفَةً ۖ قَالُوا لَا تَخَفْ ۖ وَبَشَّرُوهُ بِغُلَامٍ عَلِيمٍ

fa-awjasa minhum khīfatan qālū lā takhaf wa-bashsharūhu bi-ghulāmin ʿalīmin

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:28

Translation:

“He felt fear of them within himself. They said, ‘Do not fear,’ and gave him good news of a knowledgeable boy.”

This presents the guests’ scene from another angle.

Awjasa concerns sensing something inwardly.

Khīfah names the fear.

The messengers then say:

“لَا تَخَفْ”

The announcement of a child follows.

This directly clarifies al-rawʿ in Hūd 11:74.

One verse describes fear arising.

The other describes its departure.

Awjasa and rawʿ do not share a root.

One concerns inward perception in this construction; the other names the emotion.

Al-Ḥijr 15:52: wajilūn

Verse:

إِذْ دَخَلُوا عَلَيْهِ فَقَالُوا سَلَامًا قَالَ إِنَّا مِنْكُمْ وَجِلُونَ

idh dakhalū ʿalayhi fa-qālū salāman qāla innā minkum wajilūna

Al-Ḥijr 15:52

Translation:

“When they entered upon him and said, ‘Peace,’ he said, ‘We are afraid of you.’”

The word wajilūn occurs here.

Wajal belongs to W J L and concerns apprehension and anxiety.

Abraham describes his household’s condition with “إِنَّا مِنْكُمْ وَجِلُونَ”.

In Al-Ḥijr 15:53, the messengers answer:

“لَا تَوْجَلْ”

Meaning: “Do not be afraid.”

The Qur’an uses several related words to describe this situation.

This does not make wajal, khawf, and rawʿ identical in every usage.

They may describe a similar experience here while differing in form and semantic range.

R W ʿ (rawʿ) and Kh W F (khawf)

Khawf occurs widely in the Qur’an for fear of danger, harm, consequences, or the future.

Rawʿ also means fear in its Qur’anic use.

The messengers say lā takhaf in Hūd 11:70.

Hūd 11:74 reports the departure of fear.

The two words are connected within one event.

Khawf, however, appears in many contexts, while rawʿ occurs only once.

This single occurrence cannot establish a rigid rule that khawf is always calm fear and rawʿ always sudden fright.

A distinction found in some usages should not become an exceptionless definition of both roots.

R W ʿ (rawʿ) and F Z ʿ (fazaʿ)

Fazaʿ concerns fear, fright, and agitation.

An early lexical definition explains rawʿ through fazaʿ.

Their closeness is clear.

Fazaʿ nevertheless has its own Qur’anic uses, as in Al-Naml 27:87 concerning fear when the Trumpet is sounded.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:103 also mentions al-fazaʿ al-akbar.

Their context and intensity differ from Abraham’s story.

Every characteristic of Resurrection terror should not be transferred to al-rawʿ in Hūd 11:74.

Here rawʿ is Abraham’s alarm at the guests, not the name of the greatest terror of Resurrection.

R W ʿ (rawʿ) and W J L (wajal)

Wajal concerns inner apprehension in Qur’anic usage.

Al-Ḥijr 15:52 uses it in Abraham’s story.

It can describe fear felt in the heart.

Rawʿ also concerns fear and its inner effect.

Closeness in the field of emotions does not make the roots identical.

Other verses use wajal of believers whose hearts feel awe when The God is mentioned.

Wajal should therefore not be confined to encounters with unfamiliar guests.

Rawʿ, by contrast, occurs only in this scene in the Qur’an.

R W ʿ (rawʿ) and W J S (ījās)

Awjasa comes from W J S.

It accompanies khīfah in Hūd 11:70 and Al-Dhāriyāt 51:28.

The construction expresses inwardly felt fear.

W J S is not restricted to naming fear; it also concerns hidden perception or sensation.

Rawʿ in Hūd 11:74 directly names alarm.

In awjasa minhum khīfatan, khīfah names the fear and awjasa expresses sensing it inwardly.

Dhahaba ʿanhu al-rawʿu expresses that state’s ending in different wording.

This illustrates why each root’s meaning must be distinguished from the whole sentence’s message.

R W ʿ (rawʿ) and R W Ḥ (rūḥ)

The first two consonants match, but the third differs.

R W ʿ concerns fear, alarm, and powerful impressions.

R W Ḥ is an independent, extensive family including Qur’anic rūḥ and other derivatives.

Some discussions associate rūʿ, the inner self, with rūḥ.

Similarity in meaning or writing does not establish one root.

Al-rūʿ ends in ʿayn.

Al-rūḥ ends in ḥāʾ.

They should not be merged in the root index or Qur’anic translation.

R W ʿ (rawʿ) and R W Gh (rawgh)

Rawgh concerns turning aside, moving toward something, and sometimes going quietly or unobtrusively.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:26 uses fa-rāgha ilā ahlihi for Abraham going to his household to prepare food.

The verb comes from R W Gh, not R W ʿ.

In the same story, the guests’ behavior later alarms Abraham.

The roots occur in one narrative but describe different actions.

Rāgha concerns movement or turning aside.

Rawʿ concerns alarm and its inner effect.

Sharing two consonants must not lead to conflation.

R W ʿ (rawʿ) and B Sh R (bushrā)

Al-rawʿ and al-bushrā occur together in Hūd 11:74.

One denotes fear and the other good news.

Both may relate to a changed inner state.

Someone may first be worried, then become calm upon receiving good news.

Rawʿ and bushrā are not invariably lexical opposites.

Fear departing does not necessarily mean good news arriving.

Nor does every good announcement follow fear.

The two events coincide in this particular narrative.

Their relationship should be read there without turning it into a universal rule.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan and conclusions

Rendering the Qur’anic form

Word: الرَّوْعُ — Hūd 11:74

Suitable equivalents include:

“Fear.”

“Alarm.”

“Fright.”

In the verse’s construction:

“فَلَمَّا ذَهَبَ عَنْ إِبْرَاهِيمَ الرَّوْعُ”

A suitable translation is:

“Then, when fear had departed from Abraham…”

This preserves the familiar direct meaning of al-rawʿ.

The proposed wording is lexically clear and defensible.

“Alarm” can emphasize the disturbed condition, while “fear” is also natural and accurate.

There is no need to replace a suitable existing equivalent merely for novelty.

Would “anxiety of the heart” be better?

The Persian expression for “anxiety of the heart” can foreground fear’s inner effect.

It may also suggest ongoing worry in some contexts.

Hūd 11:74 concerns a particular fear arising with the guests and now departing.

“Fear” or “alarm” is therefore more suitable for direct translation.

Al-rawʿ should not be translated “heart,” since the Qur’anic form has fatḥah and means fear.

Proposed word-box explanation

Word: الرَّوْعُ

Root: R W ʿ

Brief root meaning: Fear and alarm; frightening or impressing. Rūʿ in another usage denotes the heart or inner self.

Meaning here: Abraham’s fear of the unfamiliar guests, which departed once their mission became clear and good news arrived.

These two fields should remain separate.

The brief root field presents the family’s lexical range.

The contextual field identifies Abraham’s particular fear.

Beauty, imposing presence, animal reactions, poetry, and the distinction between rawʿ and rūʿ belong in the full article. These proposals do not replace existing authored verse explanations.

Conclusion and final definition

R W ʿ concerns fear, alarm, and an event’s strong impression on a person.

Rawʿ in the first branch names fear itself.

Rāʿa and rawwaʿa can concern frightening another.

Irtāʿa and tarawwaʿa concern becoming frightened.

Rāʾiʿ and related forms in another branch describe an impressive effect of beauty, greatness, or grandeur.

A strong impression is not invariably unpleasant.

Rūʿ with ḍammah, meanwhile, names the heart, mind, or inner self.

An early analysis explains these fields through fear and its seat.

The feeling, its inner seat, and its cause must nevertheless be distinguished.

Pre-Islamic and early Arabic poetry also illustrates the family’s range.

ʿAntarah’s rāʿanī concerns alarm at signs of separation.

Al-Nābighah’s irtāʿa describes the animal’s fear of a hunter’s voice.

Imruʾ al-Qays’s rawʿāʾ concerns an animal’s sensitivity.

Dhū al-Rummah’s rūʿ denotes the heart or inner self.

The family cannot be explained solely through an image of extreme terror.

Its only Qur’anic occurrence is al-rawʿ in Hūd 11:74.

The verse describes fear previously felt by Abraham and now gone.

Hūd 11:70 explains its cause: the guests did not touch the food, and their behavior alarmed him.

They told him not to fear and explained their mission.

The following verses announce Isaac and, after him, Jacob.

Hūd 11:74 then reports fear departing and good news arriving before turning to Abraham’s pleading about Lot’s people.

The context presents a response to unfamiliar circumstances, not something labeled sin or weak faith.

Once his fear subsides, Abraham speaks about others’ fate.

These contextual messages should not be added to al-rawʿ’s lexical definition.

Final definition of R W ʿ:

The family’s main field concerns fear and alarm, and an event or sign’s impression on the inner self.

Some derivatives extend to the powerful, astonishing impression of beauty, greatness, or grandeur.

Rūʿ with ḍammah also names the heart or inner self.

Three important uses are therefore:

1. Fear and frightening: Rawʿ, rāʿa, and rawwaʿa.

2. Wonder and striking impressions: Rāʾiʿ describing beauty or grandeur.

3. Heart and inner self: Rūʿ in waqaʿa fī rūʿī.

These belong to one family without having identical meanings in every construction.

Only the first sense occurs in the Qur’an.

Al-rawʿ in Hūd 11:74 means fear or alarm.

The word alone specifies neither exact intensity, cause, duration, nor the reaction’s moral value.

Other clauses establish those details.

In Abraham’s story, Hūd 11:70 explains the fear, Hūd 11:71 announces the good news, and Hūd 11:74 describes the fear’s departure.

Forqan’s study and translation should distinguish fear arising, understanding its cause, receiving reassurance, and fear departing.

The stages are connected but expressed through separate words and clauses.

A suitable brief definition is:

“Fear and a powerful inner impression; in other usages, wonder at beauty or grandeur, and with a different pronunciation, the heart or inner self.”

This preserves al-rawʿ’s clear Qur’anic meaning without merging fear, beauty, the heart, and human reaction into an invented uniform sense.
