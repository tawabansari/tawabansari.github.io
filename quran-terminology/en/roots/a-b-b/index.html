The Qur’anic Root A B B

ʾAbb: pasture vegetation and animal fodder, from grasslands and plant cover to readiness and turning toward something in early Arabic

1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

The Arabic root أ ب ب has two recognized semantic branches. The first concerns pasture vegetation and fodder eaten by livestock. In this usage, أَبّ (ʾabb) denotes grass, plant cover available for grazing, and plants that feed animals.

The second branch concerns intending, preparing to move, and turning toward something. Here أَبَّ لِلسَّيْرِ (ʾabba li-al-sayri) means preparing to leave or travel; أَبَّ إِلَى وَطَنِهِ (ʾabba ilā waṭanihi) is used of someone drawn toward their homeland or intending to return to it.

Some lexical explanations propose a connection between the branches: pasture vegetation is called ʾabb because it is ready for grazing or cutting. Without sufficient historical evidence, however, this explanation should not be treated as the certain origin of the name.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an:

ʿAbasa, 80:31

وَفَاكِهَةً وَأَبًّا

wa-fākihatan wa-ʾabban

“And fruits and pasture vegetation.”

Here أَبًّا (ʾabban) refers to plants and animal feed. The following verse, ʿAbasa 80:32, speaks of provision for people and their livestock, reinforcing that meaning.

Forms examined

Important forms of the Arabic family أ ب ب include:

  • أَبّ (ʾabb): grass, pasture vegetation, and plant cover used by livestock; the family’s only Qur’anic form.
  • أَبَّ، يَئِبُّ، يَؤُبُّ (ʾabba, yaʾibbu, yaʾubbu): prepared to move, intended to leave, or turned toward something; lexical verbal usages absent from the Qur’an.
  • أَبّ (ʾabb): in the verbal branch, a verbal noun meaning intending or preparing.
  • أَبِيب (ʾabīb): a recorded verbal noun for preparing and intending to move.
  • أَبَاب، أَبَابَة (ʾabāb, ʾabābah): readiness and making preparations to move; in some uses, a longing toward one’s homeland.
  • إِبَابَة (ʾibābah): readiness to leave or turn toward a destination.
  • اِئْتَبَّ (iʾtabba): prepared or intended to move.

Other forms are also recorded: أَبَاب (ʾabāb), with fatḥah on the initial hamzah, for water or a mirage; and أُبَاب (ʾubāb), with ḍammah, for the mass of a flood or waves. Their historical relationship to the root’s principal branches requires examination.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an. Knowing them is useful for lexical study, but meanings should not be devised for the Qur’anic ʾabban that lack support from the verse and the evidence concerning plants.

أ ب ب is a triliteral root with identical second and third consonants. In أَبّ (ʾabb), these two bāʾs are represented by the shaddah.

Pasture vegetation أَبّ (ʾabb) also differs from أَب (ʾab), “father,” commonly classified under أ ب و. They differ in morphological structure as well as contextual meaning.

2. The semantic core of ʾabb: vegetation available for grazing

ʾAbb in early Arabic

Authoritative Arabic lexical sources explain الأَبُّ (al-ʾabbu) through الْكَلَأ (al-kalaʾ) and الْمَرْعَى (al-marʿā).

Kalaʾ names plants growing from the ground on which livestock graze.

Marʿā likewise concerns a pasture or vegetation available for grazing.

Explaining ʾabb through these words therefore establishes its main semantic field: plants used to feed livestock.

Al-Zajjāj explains ʾabb as all vegetation on which livestock feed. Al-Farrāʾ likewise explains it through animal feed.

This usage directly concerns Arab pastoral and agricultural life. Camels, sheep, goats, and other livestock needed pasture vegetation; access to grass and water mattered to communities’ survival and movements.

ʾAbb thus named more than something ornamental or insignificant in nature: it concerned animal nourishment and people’s economic life.

That social setting does not, however, make wealth, livestock farming, trade, or the economy the lexical meaning of ʾabb.

The word denotes pasture vegetation; its economic importance is understood through its use in people’s lives.

The vegetation itself or the place where it grows?

Arabic sources sometimes explain ʾabb through kalaʾ and sometimes through marʿā.

This difference matters.

Kalaʾ points more directly to pasture vegetation itself, whereas marʿā can include both grazable vegetation and the place where animals graze.

Accordingly, not every use of ʾabb should be restricted to a piece of land or a grassland.

In ʿAbasa 80:31, ʾabb accompanies fruits and other growing things. Pasture vegetation therefore fits its context more directly.

Translating it simply as “pasture” may lead a reader to think chiefly of grazing land rather than the plants growing there.

“Pasture vegetation” or “fodder” is accordingly more suitable for the verse.

The lexical explanation may nevertheless note that these plants collectively form the vegetation covering a pasture.

Does ʾabb mean only wild vegetation?

Some dictionaries restrict ʾabb to plants that people do not sow and on which livestock feed.

On that understanding, it may denote wild grasses and a pasture’s natural vegetation.

Yet not every source makes this restriction.

Some lexicographers use the word more broadly for plants that feed livestock without requiring them to grow wild.

Some explanations even extend ʾabb to everything that grows from the earth.

This last definition is very broad; adopting it without contextual attention can blur the distinction between ʾabb and the other plants named in ʿAbasa.

Taken together, the evidence more clearly supports pasture vegetation and animal feed.

Growing wild may therefore characterize many instances of ʾabb, but should not become an essential, exceptionless part of its definition.

Does ʾabb mean only dried grass?

Some explanations distinguish fresh from dried vegetation and associate ʾabb more closely with dried animal feed.

Yet dryness is not a fixed condition in the principal lexical definitions.

Pasture vegetation may be green and fresh, with animals grazing on it where it grows. It may also be cut, dried, and subsequently used as feed.

Both situations can belong to the field of fodder and animal nourishment.

Thus, “dried grass” may be intelligible in some explanations but is too restrictive as a translation of the Qur’anic verse.

ʿAbasa 80:31 gives ʾabb no description such as dry, yellow, or withered.

Such features should therefore not be added to the basic translation.

3. Witnesses from early Arabic poetry

ʾAbb alongside pasture and a watering place

A verse transmitted by Ibn Durayd and later lexicographers reads:

جِذْمُنَا قَيْسٌ وَنَجْدٌ دَارُنَا
وَلَنَا الْأَبُّ بِهِ وَالْمَكْرَعُ

jidhmunā qaysun wa-najdun dārunā / wa-lanā al-ʾabbu bihi wa-al-makraʿ

“Qays is the root of our lineage, and Najd our homeland; there the pasture vegetation and the watering place are ours.”

Here al-ʾabb accompanies الْمَكْرَع (al-makraʿ).

Makraʿ concerns a place of access to water or a watering place.

Their association matters for understanding ʾabb: pasture and water are closely connected needs in pastoral life.

Speaking of ʾabb and makraʿ in his own land, the poet pictures the natural resources supporting life and the keeping of livestock.

Here ʾabb means pasture vegetation, rather than preparing to travel or intending.

The poet is not conclusively identified in the transmission examined. The verse is a witness from the Arabic poetic tradition, but should not be assigned without evidence to a particular pre-Islamic poet.

ʾAbb in poetry attributed to Abū Duʾād al-Iyādī

Poetry attributed to Abū Duʾād al-Iyādī, the well-known pre-Islamic poet, contains this expression:

يَرْعَى بِرَوْضِ الْحَزْنِ مِنْ أَبِّهِ

yarʿā bi-rawḍi al-ḥazni min ʾabbihi

“It grazes in the meadow of the rugged upland, feeding on its pasture vegetation.”

The verb يَرْعَى (yarʿā) directly concerns grazing, and ʾabb is what the animal eats.

Semantically, the expression is clearly consistent with the recognized meaning of pasture vegetation.

Sources transmit differing readings of the verse’s following portion. Moreover, a criticism transmitted from Abū Ḥanīfah al-Dīnawarī’s Kitāb al-Nabāt regards the verse as fabricated and challenges its attribution to Abū Duʾād. The issue is therefore not only variant wording: the attribution’s reliability also matters.

Attribution to a pre-Islamic poet does not by itself establish the precise date of every verse, especially when that attribution has itself been challenged.

Thus, ʾabb’s association with grazing clarifies the plant-related semantic field within this transmission; given the criticism of its attribution, however, it should not be counted as certain evidence of the word in pre-Islamic poetry.

The word’s rarity in early evidence

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris transmits Abū Zayd al-Anṣārī’s statement that he had heard ʾabb mentioned only in the Qur’an.

This report matters for the history of knowledge of the word.

Yet it does not establish that ʾabb was certainly unknown throughout all pre-Qur’anic Arabic.

First, Abū Zayd’s report concerns the limits of what he himself had heard.

Second, other lexicographers transmit poetic witnesses and pasture-related usages.

The more precise conclusion is therefore that, compared with commoner terms for plants and grass, ʾabb was less frequent or less familiar within part of the lexical transmission tradition.

That rarity does not mean the word was meaningless or lacked Arabic usage.

Nor should fabricated verses or uncertain attributions be introduced into pre-Islamic poetry to compensate for limited evidence.

4. The second branch: readiness, intention, and turning toward something

أَبَّ لِلسَّيْرِ: preparing to move

Early Arabic uses أَبَّ لِلسَّيْرِ (ʾabba li-al-sayri) of someone preparing to travel or move.

Here the person makes preparations to leave and directs their intention toward a destination.

أَبَّ إِلَى وَطَنِهِ (ʾabba ilā waṭanihi) is also recorded for longing toward one’s homeland or intending to return there.

Another example, أَبَّ إِلَى سَيْفِهِ (ʾabba ilā sayfihi), describes someone reaching toward their sword to draw it.

Here readiness and intention appear in a concrete action.

Yet these uses do not mean that every ʾabb in Arabic denotes movement.

In the verse of ʿAbasa, ʾabb names vegetation rather than the act of preparing.

A witness from al-Aʿshā’s poetry

Poetry attributed to al-Aʿshā reads:

صَرَمْتُ وَلَمْ أَصْرِمْكُمُ وَكَصَارِمٍ
أَخٌ قَدْ طَوَى كَشْحًا وَأَبَّ لِيَذْهَبَا

ṣaramtu wa-lam aṣrimkumu wa-ka-ṣārimin / akhun qad ṭawā kashḥan wa-ʾabba li-yadhhabā

“I have broken with you, though I have not yet completely severed the bond, like a companion who has turned away and prepared to leave.”

أَبَّ لِيَذْهَبَا (ʾabba li-yadhhabā) here means preparing to leave.

The witness therefore belongs to the branch of intention and readiness, rather than pasture vegetation.

This distinction shows both usages to be known within the Arabic poetic tradition.

Their presence in poetry nevertheless does not establish the precise historical derivational relationship between the branches.

Is plant-related ʾabb derived from being ready?

In Mufradāt, al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī explains ʾabb as pasture ready for grazing and cutting, connecting it with the verb ʾabba, “to prepare.”

This explanation is semantically intelligible.

Vegetation that has grown and become suitable for livestock nourishment can be regarded as ready for grazing or cutting.

Yet that resemblance alone does not prove that the plant name was historically derived from the verb for preparing.

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris also identifies two separate principles for the root: pasture, and intention or readiness.

The evidence therefore allows both branches to be recognized without requiring either to be the other’s certain origin.

Accordingly, ʾabb in ʿAbasa 80:31 cannot be translated as “something that is ready.”

There the word denotes familiar pasture vegetation. Readiness concerns a possible lexical relationship and should not replace the verse’s direct meaning.

5. وَفَاكِهَةً وَأَبًّا: the word’s place in ʿAbasa

ʿAbasa, 80:31

وَفَاكِهَةً وَأَبًّا

wa-fākihatan wa-ʾabban

“And fruits and pasture vegetation.”

The verse belongs to a continuous sequence concerning rain, earth, growth, and nourishment for people and livestock.

Understanding ʾabb requires attention to this context.

The opening subject: human food

ʿAbasa, 80:24

فَلْيَنْظُرِ الْإِنْسَانُ إِلَىٰ طَعَامِهِ

fa-l-yanẓuri al-insānu ilā ṭaʿāmihi

“Let the human being look at their food.”

Here the human being is invited to attend to and reflect upon their food.

The Qur’an goes on to discuss rain, the opening of the earth, and the growth of various crops.

ʾAbb therefore occurs in a setting chiefly concerned with food and benefit from what grows from the earth.

This prevents ʾabb from being turned, without contextual evidence, into an abstraction such as spiritual readiness, intending to travel, or human movement.

Rain and growth

ʿAbasa, 80:25

أَنَّا صَبَبْنَا الْمَاءَ صَبًّا

annā ṣababnā al-māʾa ṣabban

“We poured down water abundantly.”

ʿAbasa, 80:26

ثُمَّ شَقَقْنَا الْأَرْضَ شَقًّا

thumma shaqaqnā al-arḍa shaqqan

“Then We split the earth open.”

ʿAbasa, 80:27

فَأَنْبَتْنَا فِيهَا حَبًّا

fa-anbatnā fīhā ḥabban

“And made seeds and grain grow in it.”

These verses discuss the relationship between water, earth, and the growth of crops.

Yet صَبّ (ṣabb), شَقّ (shaqq), and نَبْت (nabt) each belong to a different root.

ʾAbb subsequently names one of the growing things in this collection.

ʾAbb itself should therefore not be defined as rain, splitting the earth, or the act of making something grow.

Those are events preceding the list of produce and explaining the setting in which it comes into being.

Grapes and plants available for cutting

ʿAbasa, 80:28

وَعِنَبًا وَقَضْبًا

wa-ʿinaban wa-qaḍban

“And grapes and plants available for gathering and cutting.”

Here قَضْب (qaḍb) belongs to another root and concerns cutting or gathering plants.

Lexical explanations allow qaḍb to denote greens and plants cut for human or animal food, including fodder that grows again after cutting.

In some applications, qaḍb is therefore close to ʾabb.

Yet that closeness does not make them complete synonyms.

Qaḍb highlights cutting and gathering, whereas ʾabb more clearly concerns pasture vegetation and animal feed.

This distinction should nevertheless not become a rigid division in which qaḍb is always cultivated and ʾabb always wild, or qaḍb exclusively human food and ʾabb exclusively animal food.

The evidence as a whole does not establish such exceptionless boundaries.

Gardens, fruits, and pasture vegetation

ʿAbasa, 80:29

وَزَيْتُونًا وَنَخْلًا

wa-zaytūnan wa-nakhlan

“And olives and date palms.”

ʿAbasa, 80:30

وَحَدَائِقَ غُلْبًا

wa-ḥadāʾiqa ghulban

“And gardens with dense, substantial trees.”

ʿAbasa, 80:31

وَفَاكِهَةً وَأَبًّا

wa-fākihatan wa-ʾabban

“And fruits and pasture vegetation.”

These verses introduce a collection of plants and produce.

Olives and date palms are two familiar kinds of useful trees. Ḥadāʾiq denotes gardens, while ghulb describes the density or substantial growth of the trees.

Fākihah and ʾabb follow.

The two words occur together in context, both belonging to what The God has brought forth from the earth.

Their association in one verse does not, however, make them identical.

The distinction between fākihah and ʾabb

Arabic فَاكِهَة (fākihah) is familiar for fruit and enjoyable plant food: something people benefit from and enjoy eating.

In its established meaning, أَبّ (ʾabb) denotes pasture vegetation and animal feed.

Early explanations likewise state that fākihah is for people and ʾabb for livestock.

This contrast is clear and useful within the verses’ context.

It should not, however, become an absolute botanical classification.

Animals may eat some fruits, while some pasture plants may under certain conditions have food or other uses for people.

The distinction between fākihah and ʾabb therefore chiefly concerns their familiar uses, rather than two botanical groups separate in every respect.

In ʿAbasa, fākihah represents some produce used by people, while ʾabb represents vegetation used by livestock.

That relationship is understood through the words’ meanings and context, rather than through an absolute law about which creature can eat which plant.

6. مَتَاعًا لَكُمْ وَلِأَنْعَامِكُمْ: provision for people and livestock

ʿAbasa, 80:32

مَتَاعًا لَكُمْ وَلِأَنْعَامِكُمْ

matāʿan lakum wa-li-anʿāmikum

“As provision and a means of benefit for you and your livestock.”

This verse plays an important role in understanding ʾabb.

The preceding verses mention seeds and grain, grapes, plants for cutting, olives, date palms, gardens, fruits, and pasture vegetation.

The Qur’an then says these are provision for people and their livestock.

Pasture vegetation thus also belongs among the earth’s gifts, even when people do not necessarily eat it themselves.

How animal feed relates to human life

People benefit from plant products in various ways.

Some are eaten directly; others feed the livestock that play a role in human life.

Livestock can provide milk, meat, wool, transport of loads, and other benefits.

Pasture vegetation therefore matters to the system of human nourishment and livelihood even when it is not directly human food.

In ʿAbasa, too, the list of growing things is followed by mention of both people and livestock.

This connection shows that human benefit from the earth extends beyond food eaten directly.

Yet all these economic benefits should not be extracted from ʾabb itself. ʾAbb names pasture vegetation; the benefits of livestock keeping are understood through these plants’ role in life and the broader context.

Does the verse assign each plant to one group?

مَتَاعًا لَكُمْ وَلِأَنْعَامِكُمْ (matāʿan lakum wa-li-anʿāmikum) concerns the gifts collectively named in the preceding verses.

It should therefore not be read as a rigid legal or botanical division assigning all fākihah exclusively to people and all ʾabb exclusively to animals.

The subject here is benefiting from these things.

Linguistically, such a division can make each group’s predominant role clear without denying all its other uses.

Another Qur’anic verse is consistent with this point.

Al-Sajdah, 32:27

أَوَلَمْ يَرَوْا أَنَّا نَسُوقُ الْمَاءَ إِلَى الْأَرْضِ الْجُرُزِ فَنُخْرِجُ بِهِ زَرْعًا تَأْكُلُ مِنْهُ أَنْعَامُهُمْ وَأَنْفُسُهُمْ ۖ أَفَلَا يُبْصِرُونَ

a-wa-lam yaraw annā nasūqu al-māʾa ilā al-arḍi al-juruzi fa-nukhriju bihi zarʿan taʾkulu minhu anʿāmuhum wa-anfusuhum; a-fa-lā yubṣirūn

“Have they not seen that We drive water to dry, barren land and bring forth through it a crop from which their livestock and they themselves eat? Will they not see?”

Here a crop can feed both people and livestock.

Thus, ʿAbasa does not establish that every plant product has only one kind of consumer.

At the same time, in its particular usage ʾabb remains familiar as pasture vegetation.

The recurrence of matāʿan lakum wa-li-anʿāmikum

Al-Nāziʿāt uses the same expression after speaking of water and pasture.

Al-Nāziʿāt, 79:31

أَخْرَجَ مِنْهَا مَاءَهَا وَمَرْعَاهَا

akhraja minhā māʾahā wa-marʿāhā

“He brought forth from the earth its water and its pasture.”

Al-Nāziʿāt, 79:33

مَتَاعًا لَكُمْ وَلِأَنْعَامِكُمْ

matāʿan lakum wa-li-anʿāmikum

“As provision for you and your livestock.”

Here مَرْعَاهَا (marʿāhā) explicitly denotes the earth’s pasture, and people and livestock are subsequently identified as its beneficiaries.

This connection is especially important for understanding ʿAbasa 80:31 and ʿAbasa 80:32.

ʿAbasa uses ʾabb and Al-Nāziʿāt marʿā. Both occur alongside water, earth, and the benefit people and livestock receive.

The usage in Al-Nāziʿāt therefore gives further Qur’anic support for understanding ʾabb within the field of pasture vegetation.

Yet this does not make ʾabb and marʿā completely identical in every Arabic usage.

Marʿā can also convey the place of grazing, whereas ʾabb in ʿAbasa points more directly to the vegetation itself.

This distinction assists precise translation.

7. Was ʾabb an unknown word?

Early reports relate that ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb asked about the meaning of ʾabb when reading وَفَاكِهَةً وَأَبًّا (wa-fākihatan wa-ʾabban).

The wording and details vary among transmissions. In some, he refrains from pursuing the question after asking; others report a statement about unnecessary overexertion in inquiry.

These reports matter for the history of understanding the word, but do not permit several conclusions.

First, one person asking about a word does not establish that it had no recognized Arabic meaning.

Second, other lexical reports explain ʾabb as pasture vegetation and transmit relevant poetic witnesses.

Third, disagreement over the word’s scope does not make its meaning impossible to know.

For example, lexicographers may disagree over whether ʾabb means only wild plants or includes all vegetation available for grazing. Both definitions nevertheless remain within animal feed.

Some interpretive reports also associate ʾabb very broadly with plants, or even with fresh fruits.

These narrower or different possibilities should be recorded, but their mere presence in a report should not give them priority over the meaning supported by independent lexical evidence and Qur’anic context.

Overall, pasture vegetation has stronger support, although the precise range of plants called ʾabb remains disputed.

8. Comparison with related roots and words

A B B (ʾabb) and R ʿ Y (marʿā): pasture vegetation and grazing land

Study of R AYN Y (R ʿ Y)

Arabic raʿy concerns grazing.

Marʿā may denote grazing land and the plants on which an animal feeds.

In its familiar usage, ʾabb likewise concerns pasture vegetation.

Many lexical explanations bring these words close together.

Yet in translating ʿAbasa 80:31, “pasture vegetation” is more precise than “pastures,” since the verse belongs to a list of growing things.

In Al-Nāziʿāt 79:31, marʿā accompanies the earth’s water and can encompass a broader field of vegetation and grazing places.

Their closeness should therefore not make all their usages identical.

A B B (ʾabb) and K L A (kalaʾ): plant food for livestock

Study of K L A

Arabic kalaʾ is familiar for the plants and grasses on which livestock feed.

Lexical sources repeatedly define ʾabb through kalaʾ.

The words are consequently close in meaning.

Yet this does not mean that all Arabic names for pasture vegetation were used interchangeably without distinction.

Each word may have a more specific scope in some dialects, regions, or specialized pastoral uses.

ʾAbb occurs only once in the Qur’an; this limitation prevents all its semantic boundaries with kalaʾ from being derived from Qur’anic usage alone.

A B B (ʾabb) and Q Ḍ B (qaḍb): pasture vegetation and plants for cutting

Study of Q Ḍ B

These words occur close to one another in ʿAbasa.

Qaḍb belongs to a family concerning cutting and gathering. In plant usage, it can denote greens or fodder that are gathered and cut.

In its established usage, ʾabb denotes pasture vegetation and animal feed.

A plant may therefore be ʾabb as animal feed and also fall within qaḍb insofar as it is cut.

This closeness does not erase either word’s meaning.

Yet an absolute boundary should not be imposed, such as qaḍb always being human food and ʾabb always animal food, or qaḍb exclusively cultivated and ʾabb exclusively wild.

Such a division requires further historical and usage evidence.

A B B (ʾabb) and F K H (fākihah): animal feed and fruit

Study of F K H

Fākihah and ʾabb occur together in ʿAbasa 80:31.

Fākihah concerns fruits and enjoyable plant food.

ʾAbb denotes pasture vegetation and livestock feed.

This distinction matters to the verse’s construction, since the following verse brings people and livestock together.

Yet fākihah and ʾabb should not become two completely separate, nonoverlapping botanical groups.

Animals may eat some fruits, and some pasture plants may have uses for people.

The principal distinction therefore concerns their familiar uses.

A B B (ʾabb) and N B T (nabāt): vegetation and growth

Study of N B T

Arabic nabāt concerns plant growth and can broadly name vegetation.

ʾAbb is one plant category familiar within grazing and animal feed.

In a general sense, ʾabb may therefore be regarded as nabāt, but not every plant is necessarily ʾabb.

For example, a fruit tree, an ornamental plant, and pasture grass are all plants, yet their familiar uses differ.

In ʿAbasa 80:27, فَأَنْبَتْنَا (fa-anbatnā) expresses making something grow; ʾabb subsequently names one result of growth.

ʾAbb should therefore not be translated as the act of making something grow itself.

A B B (ʾabb) and Ḥ B B (ḥabb): pasture vegetation and grain

Study of Ḥ B B

Ḥabb in ʿAbasa 80:27 concerns seeds and grain.

ʾAbb in ʿAbasa 80:31 denotes pasture vegetation.

They are close in letters and sound, but do not share one root.

Grain and grass are also different concepts, although one plant may produce grain while its stems and leaves feed livestock.

Their closeness in the list of produce should therefore not make them complete synonyms.

A B B (ʾabb) and A B W (ʾab): pasture vegetation and father

Study of A B W

This distinction matters especially in writing without vowel marks.

أَبّ (ʾabb), with doubled bāʾ, means pasture vegetation in ʿAbasa 80:31.

أَب (ʾab), “father,” belongs to a different morphological family.

The word for father also occurs in the same sūrah.

ʿAbasa, 80:35

وَأُمِّهِ وَأَبِيهِ

wa-ummihi wa-abīhi

“And from his mother and his father.”

أَبِيهِ (ʾabīhi) here denotes a person’s father and does not share a root with ʾabb in ʿAbasa 80:31.

A resemblance in writing should therefore not transfer fatherhood into pasture vegetation or pasture into the word for father.

A B B (ʾabb) and A B L (ʾabābīl): similar forms from different families

Study of A B L

أَبَابِيل (ʾabābīl) occurs in Al-Fīl 105:3.

In the common morphological classification, it belongs under أ ب ل rather than أ ب ب.

Its resemblance in sound to ʾabb should therefore not assign the words to a single root.

The historical formation of ʾabābīl also has its own lexical discussions, but none permits ʾabb in ʿAbasa to be translated as flocks of birds or scattered groups.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

Three English equivalents merit consideration for ʾabb in ʿAbasa 80:31.

“Fodder” is brief and familiar, conveying plant food for livestock. Yet it may suggest chiefly vegetation cut or stored as animal feed.

“Pasture grasses” is close to the lexical meaning, but may restrict the range of grazable plants to ordinary, low-growing grasses.

“Pasture vegetation” is natural and broader. It can encompass different grasses and plant cover used by livestock without making dryness, freshness, wild growth, or cultivation certain.

Accordingly, “pasture vegetation” is Forqan’s principal proposed equivalent.

Proposed translation of the verse

ʿAbasa, 80:31

وَفَاكِهَةً وَأَبًّا

wa-fākihatan wa-ʾabban

“And fruits and pasture vegetation.”

This rendering preserves the distinction between fākihah and ʾabb.

Where greater brevity and fluency are needed, another translation can say:

“And fruits and fodder.”

In that case, the lexical explanation should clarify the broader meaning of vegetation available for grazing.

A brief meaning for the word-by-word box

أَبًّا (ʾabban): pasture vegetation; grasses and other plants on which livestock feed.

For contextual explanation:

“It accompanies fruits and other products of the earth; the following verse discusses benefit for people and livestock.”

The basic translation should not adopt “dried grass,” “wild vegetation,” “all plants of the earth,” or “food specifically for camels” as the word’s sole meaning without qualification or explanation.

Nor should ʾabb be translated as preparing, intending to travel, father, or spiritual nourishment. Those meanings do not adequately fit its actual use in ʿAbasa.

10. Conclusion

The Arabic root أ ب ب has two important branches: pasture vegetation and animal feed on one hand, and intending and preparing to move on the other.

In the first branch, ʾabb denotes grasses and plants on which livestock feed. Early lexical sources clearly record this meaning, supported by transmitted Arabic poetry concerning pasture and watering places, with due caution about the reliability and attribution of some poetic transmissions.

The word’s precise scope is disputed. Some restrict it to wild vegetation; others include all grazable plants; still others propose a broader definition of vegetation.

Yet pasture vegetation and animal feed have clearer support.

In the second branch, ʾabba is used for preparing, intending to move, and turning toward a destination. Some lexical explanations connect the plant name with readiness for grazing or cutting. That semantic connection is intelligible, but its derivational history is not certain.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an: ʿAbasa 80:31.

There ʾabb is named after grain, grapes, plants for cutting, olives, date palms, gardens, and fruits.

The next verse speaks of provision for people and livestock.

This context identifies ʾabb as one of the earth’s useful growing things, with a particular role in feeding livestock.

Thus, the word cannot be turned into an abstraction such as readiness or intending to move, nor should its usefulness be restricted to direct human food.

ʾAbb’s closeness to marʿā, kalaʾ, and qaḍb is real, but does not make them complete synonyms. Marʿā may denote grazing land, kalaʾ broadly names pasture vegetation, and qaḍb concerns cutting and gathering plants.

For Forqan, the family’s final definition is this: In Qur’anic usage, ʾabb is pasture vegetation and fodder that provide feed for livestock. It can encompass fresh or dried plants and various forms of grazable plant cover without requiring them all to grow wild or belong to one botanical kind. The other branch, intending and preparing to move, is known in Arabic but should not replace ʾabb’s direct meaning in ʿAbasa.

This definition clarifies the root’s sole Qur’anic occurrence and preserves its place among the earth’s gifts, which provide both direct human food and what livestock need.
