Departing from right and justice: from inclining to one side to unfair judgment and depriving others of their rights

The Arabic root Ḥ Y F is associated with inclining or turning away from an even or proper course. When people depart from justice in judgment, distributing wealth, or dealing with others, favoring one party at another’s expense, this conduct is called ḥayf.

In early Arabic, ḥāfa ʿalayhi meant wronging someone or treating them unjustly. This sense relates to the broader idea of inclination and deviation, but not every inclination is ḥayf. People may lean spatially to one side without infringing anyone’s rights. In human rights and relationships, ḥayf concerns an inclination that departs from justice and harms the rightful party.

Arabic also uses this family for taking away from something’s edges. Removing material from the sides of property or an object and diminishing it can be expressed by taḥayyafa. This provides a tangible image of reducing and changing something’s extent.

The Qur’an uses this root only in Al-Nūr 24:50, which discusses the possibility of injustice in judgment. It concerns people who turn to adjudication when a ruling favors them, but turn away from accepting it when they think the other party may be in the right.

Here, ḥayf is neither mere disagreement nor ordinary fear of losing a dispute. It concerns unfair judgment and departure from right at one party’s expense.

1. Forms of the root and its semantic core

The Qur’an uses Ḥ Y F only in yaḥīfa. This verb derives from ḥāfa–yaḥīfu and occurs in Al-Nūr 24:50.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the sole occurrence, Al-Nūr 24:50, word 9

Important forms in classical Arabic include:

  • حَافَ ـ يَحِيفُ (ḥāfa–yaḥīfu): to depart from justice, wrong someone, or act unfairly against them.
  • حَيْف (ḥayf): deviation from right in judgment or conduct; wrongdoing and injustice.
  • حَائِف (ḥāʾif): someone who departs from justice and wrongs another.
  • حَافَة، حُيَّف and حُيُف (ḥāfah, ḥuyyaf, and ḥuyuf): recorded plurals of ḥāʾif in the sense of an unjust person.
  • تَحَيَّفَ (taḥayyafa): to take from something’s edges and diminish it.
  • تَحَيُّف (taḥayyuf): taking away from something’s edges or portions; in some uses, diminishing it gradually.
  • حِيفَة (ḥīfah): in one lexical use, a side or lateral part; in another, a tool for shaving and reducing wood.
  • أَحْيَف and حَيْفَاء (aḥyaf and ḥayfāʾ): describing land or a place that has received no rain.

These are not all Qur’anic derivatives. Only yaḥīfa appears in the Qur’an. The other forms help clarify the root’s semantic field and history of use.

The semantic core

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies a single principle for this root:

أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ، وَهُوَ الْمَيْلُ

aṣlun wāḥidun wa-huwa al-maylu

That is, the root has one principle: inclining. (Aṣlun wāḥidun, wa-huwa al-maylu.)

He then explains ḥāfa ʿalayhi yaḥīfu and includes taḥayyaftu al-shayʾa in the same family: I took something from its sides.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: inclination and taking from the sides

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise explains ḥayf through inclination in judgment, unjust deviation, and wrongdoing.

These explanations place inclination at the family’s center. In ethical and legal usage, however, a particular inclination is meant: departing from justice at another person’s expense.

For example, two people may dispute a right, and the adjudicator may overlook one party’s right because of the other’s kinship, wealth, or influence. Such judgment is an instance of ḥayf.

The issue is not merely liking one party. It is allowing that preference to affect the assessment of right and produce an improper inequality.

In human relationships, therefore, ḥayf denotes departing from the standard of right and justice at someone else’s expense.

This does not make every difference in the treatment of two people unjust. Their situations, needs, or rights may differ, so fair treatment need not be identical. The criterion for ḥayf is the loss of right and justice, rather than a difference in outcomes alone.

2. The physical image and Arabic usage

Taking away from something’s edges

One recognized use is taḥayyafa al-shayʾa.

The lexicons record it for removing material from something’s sides or edges and diminishing it.

For example, taking parts off the edges of a piece of wood reduces its size. What matters here is the removal from the edges.

Ibn Fāris connects this use with inclination: a person turns from the object’s full breadth toward its sides and takes from them.

Lisān al-ʿArab also records taḥayyafa mālahu for diminishing someone’s property and taking from its edges.

This is semantically related to ḥayf in human rights, since both may involve something being diminished from its extent or rightful share.

Yet the physical image must be distinguished from the moral meaning. Not every act of shaving or reducing is unjust. A carpenter shaving wood to make an object is not necessarily committing ḥayf.

Injustice arises when someone’s right is reduced or given to another contrary to a proper standard.

Taking from the edges can thus illuminate a semantic connection, but Qur’anic ḥayf should not be confined to physically cutting or taking material away.

Ḥāfah: an edge or side

The lexicons use ḥāfatu al-shayʾi for something’s edge or side.

Ḥāfatā al-wādī are a valley’s two sides, while ḥāfatā al-lisāni denotes the tongue’s two lateral edges.

These uses concern direction and edges. Yet caution is needed about the precise relation of all forms of ḥāfah to Ḥ Y F and Ḥ W F: lexicographers place some forms in neighboring families and analyze them differently.

Resemblance between ḥayf and ḥāfah alone therefore does not establish that the legal sense of ḥayf historically arose from the name of a valley’s or stone’s edge.

What is clear is a semantic connection between inclining to one side, edges, and taking from the sides in recorded Arabic usage.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: ḥāfah, ḥīfah, and possible wāw-based analysis

A shaving tool

In one lexical use, ḥīfah names a tool for shaving and smoothing an arrow shaft or bow.

It removes excess wood to produce the intended shape.

Taking away from the sides is prominent here.

The tool is not an instrument of injustice merely because it reduces wood. No moral judgment is involved in this example; the word identifies a kind of action or tool.

This reminds us that a word family may be morally neutral in physical use and carry a negative sense in matters of rights.

Ḥayf in distribution and giving

One clear Arabic use of ḥayf concerns improper inequality in distribution and giving.

The lexicons record ḥāfa of someone who gives property among children and prefers one over another without a fair standard.

The point is not simply that the amounts differ. It is an unfair preference violating right and equity.

For example, a father may have two children, one of whom is ill and needs additional help with treatment. Giving that child more is not ḥayf merely because the amounts differ.

If, however, personal preference or pressure from one child leads the father to violate the other’s right without a fair criterion, the conduct may be an instance of ḥayf.

Thus, ḥayf is not identical with every outwardly unequal distribution. Justice and respect for rights are the criteria.

Some early sources explain ḥayf chiefly through a judge’s or ruler’s decision. Al-Azharī, however, disputes restricting it to rulers: it may be committed by anyone who wrongs another.

This disagreement matters. Although adjudication is an important field of use, the Arabic root was not limited to the official office of a judge.

Lisān al-ʿArab, J N F: Al-Azharī rejects restricting ḥayf to a ruler

A witness from Arabic poetry

Ibn al-Rūmī, an Abbasid-era poet, says:

مِنَ الْحَيْفِ تَخْسِيسُ النَّوَالِ وَمَطْلُهُ
فَعَجِّلْ خَسِيسًا أَوْ فَأَجِّلْ مُوَفَّرًا

mina al-ḥayfi takhsīsu al-nawāli wa-maṭluhu / fa-ʿajjil khasīsan aw fa-ajjil muwaffaran

Translation:

“It is unfair for a gift to be both paltry and delayed; so give a small gift promptly, or delay a plentiful one.”

Ibn al-Rūmī’s verse: a small gift promptly or a plentiful gift later

Here, the poet speaks of ḥayf in giving.

He means that if a gift is both paltry and delayed, the giver’s conduct is, in his view, unfair.

Here, ḥayf is not confined to a court’s ruling. It concerns how another person is treated and whether fairness is observed in giving.

The verse shows that Arabic literature also uses ḥayf for unfairness in social relationships.

Ibn al-Rūmī, however, is an Abbasid-era poet. This is evidence from literary Arabic after the Qur’an, rather than a pre-Islamic witness.

The witnesses presented in this article do not include an independent verse for this form with a securely established pre-Islamic attribution and date. A later verse or lexicon example therefore cannot be presented as conclusive evidence of pre-Islamic usage.

Nevertheless, early lexicons’ recording of ḥayf and the Qur’an’s direct use of yaḥīfu establish this use in Arabic at the time of revelation.

3. The Qur’anic use: fear of unfair judgment

أَفِي قُلُوبِهِمْ مَرَضٌ أَمِ ارْتَابُوا أَمْ يَخَافُونَ أَنْ يَحِيفَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِمْ وَرَسُولُهُ بَلْ أُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الظَّالِمُونَ

a-fī qulūbihim maraḍun ami irtābū am yakhāfūna an yaḥīfa allāhu ʿalayhim wa-rasūluhu bal ulāʾika humu al-ẓālimūna

Proposed translation for this study:

“Is there sickness in their hearts, or have they fallen into doubt, or do they fear that The God and His Messenger will wrong them? Rather, they themselves are the wrongdoers.”

Al-Nūr 24:50

This is the sole Qur’anic occurrence of Ḥ Y F.

Yaḥīfa here concerns the possibility of unfair judgment. Yet the verse speaks through a question, rather than reporting wrongdoing by The God or the Messenger.

The preceding verses are essential to understanding it.

The setting: accepting adjudication only when it is advantageous

Al-Nūr 24:47 describes people who claim to believe in The God and the Messenger and to obey, but some then turn away after this claim.

The next verse explains:

وَإِذَا دُعُوا إِلَى اللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ لِيَحْكُمَ بَيْنَهُمْ إِذَا فَرِيقٌ مِنْهُمْ مُعْرِضُونَ

wa-idhā duʿū ilā allāhi wa-rasūlihi li-yaḥkuma baynahum idhā farīqun minhum muʿriḍūna

Proposed translation for this study:

“And when they are called to The God and His Messenger so that he may judge between them, suddenly a group of them turns away.”

Al-Nūr 24:48

This verse concerns an invitation to adjudication to resolve a dispute.

Al-Nūr 24:49 then reveals the contrast in their conduct:

وَإِنْ يَكُنْ لَهُمُ الْحَقُّ يَأْتُوا إِلَيْهِ مُذْعِنِينَ

wa-in yakun lahumu al-ḥaqqu yaʾtū ilayhi mudhʿinīna

Proposed translation for this study:

“But if the right is on their side, they come to him in submission.”

Al-Nūr 24:49

Together, these verses show a particular pattern of conduct.

When they think the right is on their side, they accept adjudication. When the result may not favor them, however, they turn away from the same adjudication.

The issue is not simply disagreement about a ruling. Their willingness to accept judgment changes with their personal interests.

Al-Nūr 24:50 questions this group and this conduct.

Am yakhāfūna an yaḥīfa: do they fear injustice in judgment?

Al-Nūr 24:50 contains several successive questions:

«أَفِي قُلُوبِهِمْ مَرَضٌ» (A-fī qulūbihim maraḍun.)

Is there sickness in their hearts?

«أَمِ ارْتَابُوا» (Am irtābū.)

Or have they fallen into doubt?

«أَمْ يَخَافُونَ أَنْ يَحِيفَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِمْ وَرَسُولُهُ» (Am yakhāfūna an yaḥīfa allāhu ʿalayhim wa-rasūluhu.)

Or do they fear that The God and His Messenger will wrong them?

Here, yaḥīfa means wronging someone or departing from justice.

The construction ʿalayhim also identifies those at whose expense the possible injustice is being discussed.

In ḥāfa ʿalayhi, ʿalā connects the unfair conduct with the person who suffers it.

The sentence therefore does not mean merely that The God and His Messenger might incline toward them. Its question concerns possible unfair judgment against them.

This possibility is raised in a critical question, however. The verse does not say that The God or the Messenger actually committed ḥayf.

Nor can it be asserted that every member of the group inwardly believed The God to be unjust. The successive questions challenge possible motives for their conduct.

The verse’s ending makes its judgment clear:

«بَلْ أُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الظَّالِمُونَ» (Bal ulāʾika humu al-ẓālimūna.)

Rather, they themselves are the wrongdoers.

This is the verse’s explicit conclusion concerning the conduct of the group being discussed.

The relation between ḥayf and al-ḥaqq

Al-Nūr 24:49 contains al-ḥaqqu, while the next verse discusses possible ḥayf.

This juxtaposition establishes a clear contrast.

Right is what judgment must recognize and respect. In matters of rights, ḥayf occurs when judgment or conduct departs from it.

Put simply, if a person is in the right, fair adjudication must recognize it. If the other party is in the right, the same justice requires the first person to relinquish the unfounded claim.

Fair judgment therefore does not necessarily favor everyone who comes to court.

A ruling may displease one party yet remain just. Conversely, it may appear favorable to that party but be ḥayf because the other’s right was ignored.

Understanding ḥayf thus depends on the standard of right, rather than satisfaction or dissatisfaction with the outcome.

Is every concern about adjudication ḥayf or wrongdoing?

No.

A genuine concern about unfair judgment must be distinguished from the conduct described in Al-Nūr 24:47, Al-Nūr 24:48, Al-Nūr 24:49, and Al-Nūr 24:50.

People may encounter evidence of corruption, bribery, bias, or judicial error in a human legal system and rightly fear losing their rights.

Such concern is not in itself wrongdoing.

Someone may also have doubts about a judgment, interpretation, or report attributed to religion and ask questions to clarify it. Questioning or asking for evidence is not equivalent to the selective acceptance of adjudication described here.

Al-Nūr 24:48 and Al-Nūr 24:49 identify the conduct: they come when the right favors them, but turn away otherwise.

Their censure thus rests on a pattern of behavior, rather than the mere presence of questions, fear, or doubt in the mind.

This distinction matters for moral responsibility. Not everyone who questions a judge’s, leader’s, or religious interpreter’s ruling can be included in bal ulāʾika humu al-ẓālimūna.

Is yaḥīfu used only of a ruler?

Here, yaḥīfu occurs in the setting of adjudication, so injustice in judgment is prominent.

This Qur’anic use does not establish that the root is restricted to official adjudication throughout Arabic.

As the lexical evidence shows, ḥayf can concern distributing wealth, giving, family conduct, and other human relationships.

The specific setting in Al-Nūr 24:50 is adjudication between disputing parties.

Its subject therefore cannot be extended to every objectionable act or personal preference. In this context, departure from justice in resolving a dispute is central.

Wrongdoing and responsibility at the verse’s conclusion

Al-Nūr 24:50 ends with al-ẓālimūna, rather than another form of Ḥ Y F.

This choice shows how the Qur’an can use two closely related word families in one sentence.

Yaḥīfu in the question concerns possible injustice in judgment.

Al-ẓālimūna in the response condemns the group’s actual conduct.

The concepts are related, but their positions in the sentence differ.

The first part raises the possibility of unfair judgment; the second states a conclusion about that group’s conduct.

Semantically, their own wishes and interests replace the standard of right in their acceptance of adjudication.

This does not justify going further and making definitive claims about individual intentions whose details the text does not supply.

4. Morphological and structural points

Yaḥīfa is an imperfect verb from ḥāfa–yaḥīfu, Form I.

Ḥ Y F is a hollow root with yāʾ as its middle radical.

In Al-Nūr 24:50, yaḥīfa follows an and is therefore subjunctive.

The subject is allāhu, with rasūluhu coordinated to it.

These two names appear as the possible agents of unfair judgment within a question. The interrogative construction does not assert that they performed the act.

The preposition ʿalā in ʿalayhim also plays an important role.

In established usage, ḥāfa ʿalayhi means wronging someone. Ignoring this relationship can reduce the verb to inclining and lose the verse’s sense of injustice.

Yakhāfūna in the verse belongs to Kh W F and means fearing.

Fear should therefore not enter the definition of yaḥīfu. Yakhāfūna describes their possible response; yaḥīfu describes the act raised by the question.

Three matters must remain distinct: fear, unfair judgment, and the moral assessment of the group’s conduct.

5. Distinctions from related roots

Ḥ Y F (ḥayf) and Ẓ L M (ẓulm): unfair judgment and wrongdoing more broadly

Qur’anic ẓulm has a wide semantic field, including wronging others, violating rights, improper conduct, and wronging oneself.

Ḥayf is also a kind of wrongdoing, but its recognized use emphasizes departing from right and justice at someone’s expense.

For example, overlooking another’s right in distributing property can be both ẓulm and ḥayf.

Not every act of ẓulm, however, takes the form of distribution or adjudication.

Al-Nūr 24:50 places the words together: yaḥīfu first concerns a possible judgment, then al-ẓālimūna concerns the group whose conduct does not observe the standard of right.

They thus belong to a shared ethical field, without being complete synonyms in every instance.

Study of Ẓ L M

Ḥ Y F (ḥayf) and J W R (jawr): departing from right

Arabic jawr concerns deviating from the proper path and, morally, wrongdoing and departure from justice.

In Al-Naḥl 16:9, wa-minhā jāʾirun describes paths deviating from the right course.

This shows that jawr can also describe a route.

Ḥayf likewise concerns inclination, but its Qur’anic use involves unfair judgment against a disputing party.

Lisān al-ʿArab explains ḥayf through jawr and ẓulm, establishing genuine overlap.

Yet no absolute boundary should be drawn whereby jawr always means only a deviating path and ḥayf only courtroom injustice. The lexicons also record closer uses.

Their main distinction in Qur’anic study is understood from each word’s context.

Study of J W R

Ḥ Y F (ḥayf) and J N F (janaf): two ways of expressing deviation from right

Al-Baqarah 2:182 states:

فَمَنْ خَافَ مِنْ مُوصٍ جَنَفًا أَوْ إِثْمًا فَأَصْلَحَ بَيْنَهُمْ فَلَا إِثْمَ عَلَيْهِ إِنَّ اللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ

fa-man khāfa min mūṣin janafan aw ithman fa-aṣlaḥa baynahum fa-lā ithma ʿalayhi inna allāha ghafūrun raḥīmun

Proposed translation for this study:

“But whoever fears deviation from right or sin on the part of a testator and makes reconciliation among them bears no sin. Indeed, The God is forgiving and merciful.”

Al-Baqarah 2:182

Janaf belongs to J N F and concerns inclination or deviation from a course or from right.

Here, the topic is a will and possible deviation from right in it.

Some lexical sources propose that janaf is broader while ḥayf is restricted to adjudication.

Al-Azharī rejects that restriction, explaining that ḥayf may also be committed by someone other than a judge.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn and Lisān al-ʿArab: proposed distinction between janaf and ḥayf and its critique

The proposed distinction therefore cannot become an absolute rule for all uses.

The Qur’anic settings also differ: Al-Baqarah 2:182 concerns a will and reconciliation, while Al-Nūr 24:50 concerns accepting or rejecting adjudication.

Both roots approach departure from right, but their precise distinction must be examined in each construction and context.

Nor do janaf or ḥayf always determine whether conduct was intentional or inadvertent. Intention requires independent evidence.

Study of J N F

Ḥ Y F (ḥayf) and B Kh S (bakhs): injustice and diminishing a right

Qur’anic bakhs concerns giving short measure, diminishing a right, or reducing something’s value.

Hūd 11:85 instructs that people’s possessions and rights should not be diminished.

This is close to lexical taḥayyafa in the sense of taking from something’s edges.

Bakhs, however, concerns diminishing or devaluing directly, while ethical ḥayf can involve unfair partiality and departure from equity.

For example, a judgment favoring one party without valid grounds is ḥayf. If it thereby reduces another’s property or right, bakhs may also apply.

Both concepts can occur in one act, without all their uses being identical.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Suitable equivalents for Qur’anic Ḥ Y F include wronging, acting unjustly, and departing from justice.

In Al-Nūr 24:50, an yaḥīfa allāhu ʿalayhim wa-rasūluhu may be rendered:

“That The God and His Messenger will wrong them.”

This is concise, natural, and close to the verb’s recognized meaning.

“That The God and His Messenger will act unjustly toward them” is also defensible and brings out the setting of adjudication.

“That The God and His Messenger will incline toward them” is unsuitable, since it fails to convey the verb’s ethical and legal meaning.

“That The God and His Messenger will consume their rights” may express a consequence of ḥayf in some situations, but is not a precise direct translation: the word is not limited to consuming or appropriating property.

“That The God and His Messenger will give them less in distributing property” likewise adds a restricted instance not specified by the verse.

For Forqan’s basic rendering, “will wrong them” is therefore suitable. The explanation “unfair judgment and deviation from right at another’s expense” belongs in the root study and reflection.

Brief root meaning and the word’s meaning in the verse

يَحِيفَ (yaḥīfa) — Al-Nūr 24:50

  • Root: Ḥ Y F.
  • Grammar: subjunctive imperfect verb, third-person masculine singular, Form I.
  • Brief root meaning: departing from right and justice; wronging another.
  • Meaning in this verse: to wrong them in adjudication or rule unfairly against them.

This word box keeps the general root meaning distinct from the contextual meaning.

The act is raised within a question. The box must therefore not imply that the verse reports wrongdoing by The God or the Messenger.

7. Conclusion

Arabic Ḥ Y F concerns inclination and deviation. In ethical matters and human rights, it denotes departing from right and justice at another’s expense.

The lexical witnesses for taking from something’s edges illuminate the root’s physical background. Yet not every reduction or inclination is ḥayf in the sense of injustice.

The root occurs only in Al-Nūr 24:50. It raises a question about possible unfair judgment by The God and the Messenger, while the preceding context describes people whose acceptance of adjudication depends on their own advantage or disadvantage.

The verse concludes that they themselves are the wrongdoers.

Two points follow: ḥayf here concerns injustice in judgment, and a fair judgment need not favor the person seeking it.

Right is the criterion of justice, rather than personal preference, the parties’ power, or satisfaction on the part of someone ruled against.

Final definition: Ḥ Y F denotes inclination and deviation. In human rights, it concerns departing from right and justice at another’s expense. Yaḥīfa in Al-Nūr 24:50 means wronging or judging unfairly: an act raised in a critical question, rather than reported as conduct of The God or the Messenger. Brief root meaning: Inclination and deviation; in rights and judgment, departing from right and justice at another’s expense.
