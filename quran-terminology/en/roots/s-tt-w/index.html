The root S Ṭ W: assault, attack, and force used to overpower

The Arabic root S Ṭ W concerns attacking another and using force to overpower or compel submission. Saṭā means to assault or attack; saṭwah can denote a forceful assault, the intensity of exercised power, or power used to dominate another.

It occurs only once in the Qur’an, as yasṭūna in Al-Ḥajj 22:72. The verse describes people whose displeasure becomes visible when they hear clear divine signs and who almost attack those reciting them.

The passage moves from displeasure and opposition to the threshold of assault. Rejecting a statement or becoming angry does not itself mean saṭw. Yasṭūna expresses assault; its proximity rather than confirmed occurrence comes from the preceding yakādūna.

The root does not always mean injustice or an unlawful attack. Arabic also records animal behavior, exercises of power, and bodily interventions. Whether a particular action is justified depends on its subject and context.

1. Meaning in early Arabic

Core definition: overpowering through assault and force

Arabic lexical usage explains saṭw through forceful seizure and domination by applying strength.

Saṭā ʿalayhi and saṭā bihi describe attacking someone, assaulting them, or overpowering them through force.

Two elements stand out: an aggressive action directed at another and the use of force against that person.

One central lexical explanation is qahr wa-ʿuluww: overpowering and gaining the upper hand. Saṭā ʿalayhi yasṭū is likewise explained as subduing someone by forceful action.

ʿUluww here need not mean occupying a physically higher position. It can mean gaining superiority through force.

Some concrete uses involving stallions or male camels include upward or forward bodily movement. They do not establish that every saṭw proceeds from above downward or must involve hands and arms.

A practical definition is thus attacking or taking forceful action against another in order to overpower or affect them through strength.

A witness attributed to pre-Islamic poetry

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Aws ibn Ḥajar includes this half-line:

فَفَاءُوا وَلَوْ أَسْطُو عَلَى أُمِّ بَعْضِهِمْ

fa-fāʾū wa-law asṭū ʿalā ummi baʿḍihim

“They drew back; and if I were to assault the mother of one of them…”

The continuation describes someone remaining silent before such conduct and saying nothing.

The verse belongs to boasting, threat, and claims of power over opponents. The poet portrays himself as so dominant that even an assault on an opponent’s close relative would go unanswered.

Asṭū here is not mere disagreement or harsh speech. It describes an aggressive act associated with force and threat.

The witness matters because assault and domination are not meanings inferred only from Qur’anic interpretation; they also occur in poetry attributed to pre-Islamic Arabic.

Variants of the verse exist. Attribution to a pre-Islamic poet does not independently settle the exact date of this particular verse. It remains important early linguistic evidence, without proving every detail of the root’s history.

Animal behavior and the concrete image of saṭw

Saṭā al-faḥlu is recorded for vigorous or aggressive behavior by a male animal. Saṭā al-faḥlu ʿalā ṭarūqatihi can also describe mounting the female; animal uses should not all be reduced to fighting.

Farasun sāṭin has several explanations: a horse rearing on its hind legs and striking with its forelegs, a long-striding horse, or one raising its tail while running. These recorded descriptions need not all apply to one animal at once.

Some of these uses provide a concrete image of vigorous forward movement or bodily force against another. Long strides or a raised tail do not themselves necessarily indicate an assault.

Animal behavior should not automatically be equated with moral injustice. Such movement may occur in defense, competition, or other natural behavior.

The assault branch therefore involves vigorous aggressive action, while the root alone does not determine the action’s moral value.

Other Arabic uses

Saṭā al-māʾu is recorded for water becoming abundant or rising.

This does not describe one person attacking another. Increasing or rising water may be associated with advancing or overwhelming force, but that association alone does not establish the sense’s historical origin.

A specialized husbandry use concerns manual intervention in a female animal’s reproductive tract. Accounts mention removing a dead fetus or semen after mating. These are records of historical terminology, not a general name for every reproductive intervention.

Such accounts concern direct bodily intervention that could have a particular remedial or husbandry purpose. The name alone neither makes it injustice nor establishes that every historical practice was appropriate treatment.

The family is therefore not confined to human assault. The principal sense needed for the Qur’anic occurrence, however, is attacking with force.

2. Forms and derivatives

S Ṭ W occurs only once in the Qur’an:

يَسْطُونَ — Al-Ḥajj 22:72

It is a Form I present verb, third-person masculine plural, from saṭā yasṭū.

The Arabic family includes several forms:

Saṭā: attacked, assaulted, or overpowered by force.

Yasṭū: attacks or assaults.

Saṭw: a verbal noun for assault, attack, or forceful action.

Saṭwah: a single assault, and also the intensity of coercive force; broader uses include the awe and influence arising from power.

Saṭawāt: plural of saṭwah; assaults or manifestations of force.

Sāṭin: an active participle, someone attacking another with force.

Masṭuwwun ʿalayhi: someone subjected to an assault.

Sāṭā: recorded in some Arabic uses for treating someone harshly or severely.

Among these forms, only yasṭūna occurs in the Qur’an.

Saṭwah, also borrowed into Persian, does not occur in the Qur’anic text. Neither sāṭin, saṭawāt, nor the other derivatives should be presented as Qur’anic words simply because Arabic records them.

This matters because the broader Persian use of saṭwah could otherwise replace the precise meaning of yasṭūna in the verse.

The Qur’anic occurrence does not concern political power or influence in general. It describes attacking people, with the larger phrase stating that the speakers are close to doing so.

3. Qur’anic usage: attacking those who recite the signs

Al-Ḥajj 22:72 states:

وَإِذَا تُتْلَىٰ عَلَيْهِمْ آيَاتُنَا بَيِّنَاتٍ تَعْرِفُ فِي وُجُوهِ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا الْمُنْكَرَ يَكَادُونَ يَسْطُونَ بِالَّذِينَ يَتْلُونَ عَلَيْهِمْ آيَاتِنَا قُلْ أَفَأُنَبِّئُكُم بِشَرٍّ مِنْ ذَٰلِكُمُ النَّارُ وَعَدَهَا اللَّهُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا وَبِئْسَ الْمَصِيرُ

wa-idhā tutlā ʿalayhim āyātunā bayyinātin taʿrifu fī wujūhi alladhīna kafarū al-munkara yakādūna yasṭūna bi-alladhīna yatlūna ʿalayhim āyātinā qul a-fa-unabbiʾukum bi-sharrin min dhālikumu al-nāru waʿadahā Allāhu alladhīna kafarū wa-biʾsa al-maṣīru

Al-Ḥajj 22:72

“When Our clear signs are recited to them, you recognize displeasure in the faces of those who disbelieve. They almost attack those reciting Our signs to them. Say: Shall I tell you of something worse than that? The Fire: The God has promised it to those who disbelieve, and what an evil destination!”

The verse describes a group’s response to the recitation of clear divine signs.

Several elements can be distinguished in its construction.

First, the divine signs are recited to them.

Second, displeasure and a negative reaction become visible in their faces.

Third, the reaction is so intense that they are close to attacking the reciters.

Yasṭūna names the assault in this third element; yakādūna places them near its occurrence. Neither word alone expresses the complete movement from inner displeasure to threatened assault.

Displeasure and assault are distinct

The verse says:

تَعْرِفُ فِي وُجُوهِ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا الْمُنْكَرَ

taʿrifu fī wujūhi alladhīna kafarū al-munkara

Al-Ḥajj 22:72

“You recognize displeasure in the faces of those who disbelieve.”

This describes visible signs of a negative reaction.

Al-munkara here is not itself saṭw. The facial response is described first; proximity to assault is then expressed separately.

S Ṭ W should therefore not be translated simply as becoming angry, frowning, or disliking what is said.

A person may dislike a statement without assaulting anyone. Saṭw describes force directed against another, not mere displeasure.

Those reciting the signs are the prospective targets here.

The importance of yakādūna

Yasṭūna is preceded by yakādūna.

Kāda is a verb of proximity; in this construction it indicates an action approaching occurrence.

Yakādūna yasṭūna thus means “they almost attack,” not “they attacked.”

This distinction is essential in translation.

The sentence conveys intensity and proximity to assault without reporting that the assault definitely occurred.

Rendering it simply as “they attack the reciters” removes the meaning contributed by yakādūna.

Conversely, the absence of a definite report of assault does not make the threat negligible. The reaction is described as approaching assault.

The real danger is conveyed while the distinction between nearing an act and performing it remains intact.

Bi- in yasṭūna bi-alladhīna

The Qur’anic expression is:

يَسْطُونَ بِالَّذِينَ

yasṭūna bi-alladhīna

Al-Ḥajj 22:72

“They attack those who…”

Saṭā can take either ʿalā or bi- in Arabic.

Here bi- introduces those targeted by the assault.

Bi-alladhīna does not mean that the reciters are being used as instruments of attack. They are the people against whom it is directed.

“Attack those who…” is a natural rendering of this construction.

Without independent evidence, saṭā ʿalayhi and saṭā bihi should not be divided into necessarily physical attack in one case and merely verbal threat in the other.

Both constructions can express assault; context determines the precise action.

Answering a statement or threatening its speaker

The preceding verse, Al-Ḥajj 22:71, describes people worshiping something besides God for which no authority has been sent down and about which they have no knowledge.

Al-Ḥajj 22:72 then describes their negative response to clear signs.

The connection matters: the passage first addresses evidence and knowledge, then portrays people who almost assault the reciters instead of responding through argument.

This distinguishes answering a statement from attacking its speaker.

One may reject an argument and provide reasons. Assaulting the person presenting it does not replace a reasoned answer.

A comparable behavior can occur when someone responds to questions or criticism with threats and intimidation rather than evidence.

That is a contemporary analogy, not a lexical definition of saṭw as intellectual or logical inability. The root still means forceful assault.

The scope of the verse’s criticism

Al-Ḥajj 22:72 describes a hostile response approaching assault when clear signs are heard.

It does not justify equating every dissenter, questioner, or critic with those people simply because their views differ.

Nor does a negative facial expression alone prove a definite intention to attack. In this verse, proximity to assault is stated separately.

Applications should distinguish disagreement, anger, threat, and actual assault.

The relevant evidence is the conduct itself, not merely disagreement with our belief or interpretation.

4. Assault, authority, and the moral assessment of power

Saṭwah can describe the intensity of exercised power or the intimidating presence through which someone influences others.

Persian also uses it for the conspicuous power or influence of a ruler, army, or powerful person.

That broad use should not be equated with every use of the verb saṭā.

Possessing power does not necessarily mean attacking others. Someone may hold lawful authority or defensive strength without using it for attack or repression.

Uses of force also differ morally. Protecting life or preventing aggression is different from silencing truth or depriving others of their rights.

Purpose, subject, and circumstances establish that distinction, not S Ṭ W alone.

In the Qur’anic occurrence, the targets are people reciting clear divine signs. The context makes the hostile behavior’s blameworthiness clear.

Saṭw should consequently not be defined only as doing injustice, although its occurrence in Al-Ḥajj 22:72 belongs to an unjust, hostile setting.

This distinction preserves lexical meaning while grounding moral assessment in evidence.

5. Comparison with related roots

S Ṭ W and B Ṭ SH: assault and forceful seizure

B Ṭ SH concerns seizing, grasping, and applying force intensely.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:130 states:

وَإِذَا بَطَشْتُمْ بَطَشْتُمْ جَبَّارِينَ

wa-idhā baṭashtum baṭashtum jabbārīna

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:130

“When you seize with force, you do so as tyrants.”

Baṭsh accompanies jabbārīn, emphasizing the people’s harsh, domineering conduct.

Saṭw and baṭsh overlap in assault and force; lexical explanations also use baṭsh to explain saṭw.

In clear assault uses, saṭw highlights attacking or advancing against someone, while baṭsh also emphasizes forceful seizure.

This is a tendency, not an absolute boundary. One attack may be described by both.

Baṭsh alone likewise need not always mean injustice; its object and Qur’anic context determine the action’s moral character.

S Ṭ W and Q H R: assault and overpowering

Qahr concerns overcoming or subduing another.

Al-Ḍuḥā 93:9 states:

فَأَمَّا الْيَتِيمَ فَلَا تَقْهَرْ

fa-ammā al-yatīma fa-lā taqhar

Al-Ḍuḥā 93:9

“As for the orphan, do not oppress him.”

The verse prohibits oppressive treatment of the orphan.

Qahr can emphasize domination or the other person’s subjection to pressure, while saṭw in its assault sense highlights aggressive action.

Someone may attack without succeeding in defeating the other person. An assault has occurred, but final domination has not been achieved.

Saṭw should therefore not always be rendered as successful overpowering. It can describe an attack or aggressive attempt.

Arabic also uses saṭw in association with domination, so the semantic fields overlap.

S Ṭ W and Ẓ L M: force and violation of rights

Qur’anic ẓulm concerns injustice, misplacing things, and violating rights.

Saṭw itself describes a type of action: assault or aggressive force.

Injustice can occur without direct physical assault, for example through loss of property, denial of rights, or unfair judgment.

Conversely, force may under some circumstances prevent injustice or defend a right.

Saṭw and ẓulm are not synonyms, although both can occur in an unjust act.

In Al-Ḥajj 22:72, assault against reciters of clear signs reveals the blameworthy character of the conduct. That judgment comes from context rather than a fixed definition of every derivative.

S Ṭ W and ʿ D W: assault and transgression

ʿ D W has a broad Qur’anic range, including hostility and crossing limits.

Iʿtidāʾ can describe various violations, from attacks on life and property to transgressing specified boundaries.

Saṭw as assault names a particular manner of aggressive action.

Attacking someone to silence them may constitute both saṭw and iʿtidāʾ.

Not every transgression is saṭw, however. Someone can violate another’s rights without directly attacking them.

This matters for translation: merely rendering yasṭūna as “transgress” weakens the verse’s image of assault.

S Ṭ W and Ṣ W L: assault and charging

Ṣāla yaṣūlu concerns attacking, charging, and advancing forcefully.

It has been used particularly for attacking animals and people assaulting others intensely.

Ṣawl and saṭw are close in this field and sometimes explain one another in dictionaries.

Overlapping usage does not make their historical roots identical.

Qur’anic yasṭūna belongs to S Ṭ W and should not be assigned to Ṣ W L because of semantic proximity.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Translation should retain aggressive action and its forceful character.

Suitable equivalents include attacking, assaulting, charging against someone, and in some contexts forcibly laying hands on another.

For Al-Ḥajj 22:72, this rendering is appropriate:

“They almost attack those reciting Our signs to them.”

It preserves three main features of the statement.

First, “almost” conveys yakādūna without adding a definite occurrence of assault.

Second, “attack” preserves the aggressive action.

Third, those reciting the signs remain the targets.

“They are close to assaulting them” is also a natural equivalent.

“They hate them,” “they disagree with them,” or “they want to condemn them” do not adequately convey saṭw’s direct meaning.

“They kill them” goes too far: saṭw alone does not establish killing.

Non-Qur’anic forms can be rendered as follows:

Saṭā ʿalayhi: attacked or assaulted him.

Saṭā bihi: assaulted him forcefully.

Saṭw: assault, attack, or forceful action.

Saṭwah: a forceful assault, intense exertion of power, or the intimidating presence arising from it, according to context.

Sāṭin: an attacker or someone assaulting another with force.

Saṭā al-māʾu: water became abundant or rose.

Animal and husbandry uses require the particular established sense. Rendering them all as doing injustice or unlawful attack is inaccurate.

The Persian borrowing saṭwah should likewise not always be replaced with power or sovereignty. Its characteristic uses involve intensity, intimidating presence, and the capacity to exercise force; context determines its relation to legitimate or illegitimate power.

Conclusion

S Ṭ W concerns assault and the use of force against another. Early poetry, animal descriptions, and expressions of exercised power attest this field.

More restricted uses describe increasing water and bodily intervention. They should be acknowledged, but do not establish a single certain historical image underlying every branch.

The family occurs once in the Qur’an. Yasṭūna in Al-Ḥajj 22:72 describes those so displeased by clear divine signs that they almost attack the reciters.

Yakādūna indicates proximity rather than confirmed assault. Yasṭūna names an attack, not mere disagreement, anger, or displeasure.

The verse distinguishes disagreement expressed through reasons and dialogue from attacking the person presenting the statement.

The resulting definition is:

In its principal use, S Ṭ W denotes assault or forceful aggressive action against another, using strength to overpower them.

Al-Ḥajj 22:72 applies this to hostility against those reciting divine signs.

Possessing power, disagreeing, or becoming angry is not itself saṭw. The word names aggressive force; in the verse, yakādūna supplies the additional meaning of nearing that action.

The root alone does not settle whether force is justified. That judgment depends on purpose, circumstances, and the parties’ rights. The verse’s context identifies the threatened targets as reciters of clear divine signs: the speaker is targeted instead of the truth of the message being addressed.
