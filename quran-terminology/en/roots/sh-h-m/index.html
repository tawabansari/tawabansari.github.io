Animal fat and fatness: from the material sense in early Arabic to the dietary restrictions of the Children of Israel in the Quran

In early Arabic, SH Ḥ M denotes fat in an animal's body. Shaḥm names the fatty substance found around internal organs, beneath the skin, and, in some animals, in distinct deposits. Shaḥmah denotes a piece of it; shuḥūm is the plural of shaḥm.

From this material sense come words for becoming fat, abundant body fat, food prepared with fat, and someone who sells animal fat or feeds it to others.

The family is attested in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period. Besides being edible and distinguishable from meat, fat is associated with plentiful food, well-fed livestock, and comfortable living. These social and figurative associations must not replace the basic meaning.

The root occurs once in the Quran, as shuḥūmahumā in Al-Anam 6:146. The verse concerns the fat of cattle and sheep or goats, and a particular dietary restriction imposed on the Jewish people described there.

Three matters must be distinguished: the lexical meaning of fat, the portions excepted from the prohibition, and the people to whom the ruling applied.

1. Shaḥm in early Arabic

Fat as a familiar edible substance

Early Arabic shaḥm names animal body fat, distinguished from laḥm, meat, although the two can remain attached in an animal's body and during food preparation.

This distinction had everyday uses: when cutting meat, cooking, or describing an animal's condition, the amount of fat could indicate bodily condition and food value.

Lexical examples include a camel's hump and white abdominal fat. Shaḥm is therefore not restricted to one organ.

This breadth does not make every oily substance, vegetable fat, or soft body part shaḥm irrespective of linguistic usage.

First witness: Imru al-Qays on meat and fat

In the Muʿallaqah attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imru al-Qays, the speaker recalls slaughtering his mount for a group of young women who shared its meat.

He says:

فَظَلَّ الْعَذَارَى يَرْتَمِينَ بِلَحْمِهَا
وَشَحْمٍ كَهُدَّابِ الدِّمَقْسِ الْمُفَتَّلِ

fa-ẓalla al-ʿadhārā yartamīna bi-laḥmihā / wa-shaḥmin ka-huddābi al-dimaqsi al-mufattali

The verse conveys:

“The young women spent the day tossing its meat and fat to one another—fat like the hanging fringes of twisted white silk.”

Laḥm and shaḥm appear side by side.

The poet refers to two edible substances from the same animal but names them separately.

Shaḥm is the substance accompanying the meat, its appearance compared to hanging white silk fringes.

The comparison relies on the audience recognizing fat's color, texture, and appearance.

The witness supports an ordinary food sense in early Arabic, without requiring a religious technical meaning. Attribution to a pre-Islamic poet alone does not establish the exact date of composition.

The basic sense is clear: shaḥm is animal fat, a substance that can be separated alongside meat, prepared, and eaten.

Second witness: al-Asha and “kidney fat”

The poet al-Asha describes people living comfortably before experiencing war:

وَكَانُوا بِشَحْمِ الْكُلَى قَبْلَهَا
فَقَدْ جَرَّبُوهَا لِمُرْتَادِهَا

wa-kānū bi-shaḥmi al-kulā qablahā / fa-qad jarrabūhā li-murtādihā

The verse conveys:

“Before it they lived in ease and plenty; now they have experienced it for whoever would seek it.”

Shaḥm al-kulā literally refers to fat around the kidneys, but the expression here does more than name food.

Kidney fat portrays prosperity, contrasted with the hardships of war.

This shows how fat could be associated with good food and abundance in the language and life represented by the poem.

Comfort and prosperity are not the literal meanings of shaḥm: they arise through the figurative expression and its context.

The underlying substance remains animal fat, now used to portray human circumstances.

What the early witnesses show

These two witnesses attributed to pre-Islamic poets illustrate two levels of usage. Their attribution does not itself supply precise dates for the individual lines.

In Imru al-Qays, shaḥm directly names edible animal fat. In al-Asha, the same substance contributes to an image of prosperity through its associations with food and abundance.

The defensible basic meaning is animal fat. Wealth, plenty, rich food, and comfort may accompany figurative uses but are not fixed parts of its lexical definition.

2. Important forms and derivatives

The family has several principal forms.

Shaḥm: animal body fat; the basic name of the substance.

Shuḥūm: the plural, fats; it can refer to deposits in different body regions, or kinds and masses of fat.

Shaḥmah: a piece or portion of fat, also used in certain anatomical and figurative expressions.

Shaḥīm: fat or well supplied with body fat.

Shaḥuma: to become fat. Shaḥima: dictionaries also record craving fat or eating much of it; some usages associate it with becoming fat after leanness. The two vocalizations should not be assigned one meaning without qualification.

Shaḥama al-qawm: to feed people fat or serve them food containing it.

Ashḥama: in one recorded use, to come to possess abundant fat.

Shaḥḥām: a seller of fat, or someone who frequently feeds it to others; context determines the sense.

Mashḥūm: food into which fat has been put.

All belong to this family, but dictionary inclusion does not establish independent, datable pre-Quranic evidence for every form.

Only shuḥūmahumā occurs in the Quran: shuḥūm plus the attached dual pronoun humā.

Shaḥmah and figurative applications

Shaḥmat al-udhun denotes the soft lower earlobe, from which an earring is commonly hung.

This applies the name to a body part resembling fat in softness or appearance.

Shaḥmat al-rummānah is recorded for the pale tissue separating pomegranate seeds; shaḥmat al-nakhlah denotes the soft palm heart.

These expressions do not identify animal fat with soft plant tissue. Naming can draw on resemblance, softness, or structure.

Their recording in later dictionaries does not prove that every expression dates to the pre-Islamic period.

The family's main direct meaning remains animal fat; other uses require their own contextual evidence.

3. Quranic usage: Al-Anam 6:146

The Quran states:

وَعَلَى الَّذِينَ هَادُوا حَرَّمْنَا كُلَّ ذِي ظُفُرٍ ۖ وَمِنَ الْبَقَرِ وَالْغَنَمِ حَرَّمْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ شُحُومَهُمَا إِلَّا مَا حَمَلَتْ ظُهُورُهُمَا أَوِ الْحَوَايَا أَوْ مَا اخْتَلَطَ بِعَظْمٍ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ جَزَيْنَاهُم بِبَغْيِهِمْ ۖ وَإِنَّا لَصَادِقُونَ

wa-ʿalā alladhīna hādū ḥarramnā kulla dhī ẓufurin wa-mina al-baqari wa-al-ghanami ḥarramnā ʿalayhim shuḥūmahumā illā mā ḥamalat ẓuhūruhumā awi al-ḥawāyā aw mā ikhtalaṭa bi-ʿaẓmin dhālika jazaynāhum bi-baghyihim wa-innā la-ṣādiqūn

Al-Anʿām 6:146

“For those who were Jews, We prohibited every clawed animal. Of cattle and sheep or goats, We prohibited their fat, except what their backs or entrails bear, or what is mingled with bone. Thus We recompensed them for their transgression; indeed, We are truthful.”

Shuḥūmahumā refers to the fat of cattle and ghanam. The latter includes sheep and goats and should not be restricted without reason to wool-bearing sheep.

The dual pronoun humā refers to the two categories, al-baqar and al-ghanam.

The subject is consequently the fat of two livestock categories, not of every animal in the world.

The meaning of shuḥūmahumā

Shuḥūm is the plural of shaḥm and denotes animal body fats.

The plural concerns the fatty substance in these animals, without by itself treating every bodily location identically.

The following exceptions show that not all their fat has the same status under the ruling.

Shuḥūmahumā should not be translated as “their meat”: the Quran uses laḥm for meat elsewhere.

Nor should it be restricted to melted fat, dairy fat, or one particular organ's fat. These may be related instances or products, but the word has a broader range.

Some lexical and exegetical accounts identify tharb, the thin fat covering parts of the abdominal organs, and fat around the kidneys as important instances of the fat at issue.

These explanations help identify possible referents, but the verse does not use “tharb” or “kidneys.” Translating shuḥūmahumā simply as “their kidney fat” or “their abdominal fat” would insert interpretation without explanation.

First exception: fat associated with the back

إِلَّا مَا حَمَلَتْ ظُهُورُهُمَا

illā mā ḥamalat ẓuhūruhumā

Al-Anʿām 6:146

“Except what their backs bear.”

This excepts some fat from the preceding ruling.

Ḥamalat belongs to Ḥ M L and means bearing or carrying. Ẓuhūr is the plural of ẓahr, back.

The back is presented as the bearer or location of fat.

The phrase thus concerns fat associated with these animals' backs.

It cannot be included in the prohibited category merely because it is fat: it is expressly excepted.

Precise identification requires attention to anatomy and the relevant use of ẓahr. The existence of an exception remains clear even without settling every anatomical detail.

Second exception in the adopted reading: al-ḥawāyā

أَوِ الْحَوَايَا

awi al-ḥawāyā

Al-Anʿām 6:146

“Or the entrails.”

Ḥawāyā belongs to Ḥ W Y and refers to coiled internal abdominal parts, including intestines and related structures.

One recognized grammatical analysis coordinates al-ḥawāyā with ẓuhūruhumā. The sense then is:

“Except fat borne by their backs or entrails.”

This fits the sentence's subject: excepting certain fats from prohibition.

On this reading, al-ḥawāyā is not itself a name for fat; it names organs that can bear fat on or around them.

Other analyses coordinate al-ḥawāyā with the excepted relative expression, or even with shuḥūm, making it part of the prohibition in the latter analysis. The present translation adopts the exception reading. None makes SH Ḥ M mean intestines or digestive organs.

Shaḥm names a fatty substance; ḥawāyā names internal organs. They belong to different roots.

Third exception: fat mingled with bone

أَوْ مَا اخْتَلَطَ بِعَظْمٍ

aw mā ikhtalaṭa bi-ʿaẓm

Al-Anʿām 6:146

“Or what is mingled with bone.”

Ikhtalaṭa belongs to KH L Ṭ and means becoming mixed.

ʿAẓm denotes bone.

The phrase concerns fat mingled with or attached to bone.

It states another exception to the preceding ruling.

Some early explanations include fat in parts such as the fatty tail and flank associated with bone.

Such examples should not be added to the direct translation of ʿaẓm: the text itself says only “bone.”

What the exceptions establish

Under the adopted reading, these three expressions show that even for the historical recipients, the ruling did not prohibit all fat of cattle and sheep or goats without exception.

Fat associated with the back, entrails, or bone is excepted on this interpretation.

Ignoring the exceptions and saying that all their fat was prohibited to Jews would therefore be insufficiently precise.

The verse distinguishes portions of animal fat, and interpretation and translation should preserve those distinctions.

4. A particular restriction and general dietary prohibition

Al-Anam 6:146 identifies the recipients at its opening:

وَعَلَى الَّذِينَ هَادُوا

wa-ʿalā alladhīna hādū

Al-Anʿām 6:146

“And for those who were Jews.”

This qualification is essential to the ruling's scope.

The verse does not declare cattle and sheep or goat fat forbidden to everyone. It reports restrictions imposed on a specified group.

Its ending also gives the reason:

ذَٰلِكَ جَزَيْنَاهُم بِبَغْيِهِمْ

dhālika jazaynāhum bi-baghyihim

Al-Anʿām 6:146

“Thus We recompensed them for their transgression.”

The restriction is linked to the recipients' transgression, rather than to a claim that such fat has always been inherently impure or forbidden to all humanity.

An-Nisa 4:160 further illuminates this distinction:

فَبِظُلْمٍ مِنَ الَّذِينَ هَادُوا حَرَّمْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ طَيِّبَاتٍ أُحِلَّتْ لَهُمْ

fa-bi-ẓulmin mina alladhīna hādū ḥarramnā ʿalayhim ṭayyibātin uḥillat lahum

Al-Nisāʾ 4:160

“Because of wrongdoing by those who were Jews, We prohibited for them good things that had been permitted to them.”

It explicitly describes good things previously permitted but subsequently prohibited because of the wrongdoing concerned.

A prohibition in a particular law or historical setting cannot automatically be treated as proof of inherent impurity without considering its reason and recipients.

In Aal Imran 3:50, Jesus likewise says:

وَلِأُحِلَّ لَكُم بَعْضَ الَّذِي حُرِّمَ عَلَيْكُمْ

wa-li-uḥilla lakum baʿḍa alladhī ḥurrima ʿalaykum

Āl ʿImrān 3:50

“And to permit for you some of what had been prohibited to you.”

Some earlier restrictions were therefore not necessarily everlasting and identical for every community and period.

Connection with Al-Anam 6:145

Immediately before the verse about fats, Al-Anam 6:145 commands the messenger:

قُل لَّا أَجِدُ فِي مَا أُوحِيَ إِلَيَّ مُحَرَّمًا عَلَىٰ طَاعِمٍ يَطْعَمُهُ

qul lā ajidu fī mā ūḥiya ilayya muḥarraman ʿalā ṭāʿimin yaṭʿamuhu

Al-Anʿām 6:145

“Say: In what has been revealed to me, I find nothing prohibited to someone who eats it…”

The verse then specifies prohibited foods.

Al-Anam 6:146 describes particular restrictions imposed on the Jewish recipients concerned.

The sequence matters: first comes what was revealed to the messenger concerning prohibited food, then an example of particular restrictions on earlier people.

The ruling in Al-Anam 6:146 should not be detached from its recipients and reason and extended to everyone without evidence.

Dietary rulings should likewise not be derived from a detached word or verse. Other relevant passages, including Al-Maidah 5:3 and An-Nahl 16:115, belong in that inquiry.

For the root study, the main result is clear: shaḥm names a substance; permissibility or prohibition comes from the text and scope of legislation, not from the root's lexical meaning.

Assigning a universal prohibition to fat without Quranic grounds requires scrutiny, just as disregarding an explicit divine prohibition does.

5. Distinctions from related roots

SH Ḥ M and L Ḥ M: fat and meat

Laḥm denotes meat; shaḥm denotes animal fat.

Their juxtaposition in the poem attributed to Imru al-Qays shows that they could name distinguishable portions of animal food in early Arabic.

Meat and fat are not always completely separate in an animal: meat can contain fat, and fat can adhere to other tissues.

The lexical distinction alone consequently cannot determine every ruling concerning animal parts.

For example, the use of “swine flesh” in Al-Anam 6:145 and shuḥūmahumā in Al-Anam 6:146 does not suffice to derive new rulings for every animal part merely from the difference between the nouns.

Such judgments require consideration of the animal, the prohibition's context and object, and the relevant passages together.

The linguistic distinction is real but does not alone yield a comprehensive legal ruling.

SH Ḥ M and S M N: fatness and dairy fat

S M N concerns both bodily fatness and a fatty dairy product.

Siman denotes bodily fatness; samn denotes butter-derived fat, such as clarified butter.

Shaḥm, by contrast, directly names fat in an animal's body.

The families overlap in fatness, but bodily fat and dairy-derived fat are not the same substance.

An animal with abundant body fat can be called shaḥīm or samīn. This overlap does not make all derivatives interchangeable.

Al-Anam 6:146 concerns bodily fat of cattle and sheep or goats, not their milk, butter, or dairy products.

SH Ḥ M and D H N: fat and oil

Duhn has a broader range, including oily substances used for rubbing, consumption, and other purposes.

It can be of plant or animal origin.

Shaḥm in its basic sense is animal body fat.

Fat can be melted to yield an oily substance, but the resulting product and bodily fatty tissue differ in form and use.

Olive oil does not become shaḥm in its basic sense merely because it is fatty.

Thus “the fat of the two” is preferable to “their oil” for shuḥūmahumā. “Oil” could suggest an extracted liquid and obscure the bodily substance.

SH Ḥ M and W D K: fat and rendered grease

Wadak denotes the fatty substance or grease obtained from meat or fat. Some uses concern rendered fat or the grease accompanying meat.

Shaḥm primarily names the bodily substance itself; wadak can emphasize its resulting grease.

Their meanings overlap without making them exact substitutes everywhere.

Fat in an animal's body is shaḥm; after rendering, its greasy product may also be described as wadak.

This helps explain traditional food preparation but should not become an unsupported absolute distinction across all Arabic usage.

SH Ḥ M compared with SH Ḥ Ḥ and SH Ḥ N

Despite their resemblance, SH Ḥ M differs from SH Ḥ Ḥ and SH Ḥ N.

SH Ḥ Ḥ concerns miserliness, greed, and withholding.

SH Ḥ N concerns filling and loading in forms such as mashḥūn, as in the Quranic description of a laden ship.

SH Ḥ M is the family of animal fat.

Sharing two initial consonants does not by itself establish a historical or semantic connection.

6. Morphology, translation, and conclusion

Shuḥūmahumā in Al-Anam 6:146 consists of shuḥūm, the plural of shaḥm, and the attached dual pronoun humā, referring to the two livestock categories. Grammatically it is the object of ḥarramnā and is accusative. That syntactic role is not a third component of the word.

The construction reads:

وَمِنَ الْبَقَرِ وَالْغَنَمِ حَرَّمْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ شُحُومَهُمَا

wa-mina al-baqari wa-al-ghanami ḥarramnā ʿalayhim shuḥūmahumā

Al-Anʿām 6:146

“Of cattle and sheep or goats, We prohibited their fat to them.”

Min relates the discussion to cattle and ghanam. The pronouns in ʿalayhim and shuḥūmahumā have different referents: the former refers to the recipients of the ruling; the latter to the two animal categories.

Translation must preserve this distinction.

The plural shuḥūm does not specify a fixed number of individual animals. It is compatible with fat in different bodily regions and the two categories concerned.

Translation proposals for Forqan

Shaḥm: “animal fat”; Persian peyh is the proposed basic equivalent.

Shuḥūm: “fats” or “animal fats.” In natural Persian, peyh can also serve collectively according to context.

Shaḥmah: “a piece of fat”; in particular compounds such as shaḥmat al-udhun, “earlobe.”

Shaḥīm: “fat” or “well supplied with body fat.”

Shaḥuma: “became fat” or “increased in body fat.”

Shaḥḥām: “fat seller,” or in another use, someone who feeds fat to others.

Mashḥūm: “containing fat” or “prepared with fat.”

The proposed basic rendering of shuḥūmahumā is:

“The fat of the two.”

The Persian peyh is more specific here than the broader charbī, which can also refer to oils, milk fat, and other fatty substances. In English, “animal fat” supplies the relevant clarification.

Peyh directly evokes bodily animal fat and fits the verse's exceptions.

For readers unfamiliar with that Persian term, a lexical note can explain: “fat stored in an animal's body.”

Conclusion

Early Arabic SH Ḥ M denotes animal fat. Poetry attributed to pre-Islamic poets shows it as a recognized food substance, also used in images of fatness, prosperity, and abundance.

The family includes words for a piece of fat, bodily fatness, food containing fat, and related activities. Figurative applications to an earlobe or soft plant tissue are recorded, but should not be asserted as the original meaning without evidence.

Its sole Quranic form is shuḥūmahumā in Al-Anam 6:146. The verse concerns cattle and sheep or goat fat and excepts certain portions from a particular prohibition.

The prohibition applies to the recipients identified and is linked to their transgression. Shaḥm itself therefore carries neither a moral judgment nor a prohibition, and cannot establish a universal ban on eating fat.

The defensible semantic core is fat or fatty substance in an animal's body. Related fatness, fat-containing food, and figurative applications connect with this familiar substance.

Forqan's proposed Persian equivalent is peyh in the context of Al-Anam 6:146. The lexical meaning, bodily location, and particular ruling are related but distinct matters that research and translation should keep separate. These proposals do not alter the installed verse translations.
