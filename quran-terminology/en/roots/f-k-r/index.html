Qur’anic root ف ك ر (fakara)

Qur’anic and lexical forms

(fakkara)

(yatafakkarūna)
They reflect

(tatafakkarūna)

Thinking, mentally examining, and weighing a subject to reach understanding; from signs of creation to recognizing truth and evaluating conduct

1. The root in early Arabic

The three-letter root ف ك ر in Arabic concerns using the mind to think about a subject, examine it, and arrive at an understanding or conclusion. فَكَرَ فِي الْأَمْرِ means that someone thought about a matter and considered it mentally. تَفَكَّرَ likewise denotes thinking and reflecting on a subject.

Classical lexicons explain this family through expressions such as إِعْمَالُ الْخَاطِرِ فِي الشَّيْءِ and تَرَدُّدُ الْقَلْبِ فِي الشَّيْءِ: exercising thought about something and turning it over in the mind for further consideration.

“Heart” in this lexical definition refers to the sphere of understanding and thought. It should not be turned into a claim about the biological location of thinking in the body.

This meaning in Arabic close to the period of revelation is also consistent with the Qur’an’s own usage. To attribute it precisely to the pre-Islamic period, however, independent historical evidence must be distinguished from reports in later dictionaries. This study does not present independently dated poetic or inscriptional evidence on which to establish the pre-Qur’anic meaning with certainty. The recognized classical Arabic meaning can therefore be explained without projecting it onto the entire pre-Islamic period in the absence of independent evidence.

Some lexical writings also refer to the root ف ر ك when explaining thought; that root concerns rubbing and abrasion. This image can illustrate repeated examination of a subject, but rearranged letters or similar imagery do not establish that فكر historically originated from فرك.

Unlike many roots with a clear material image, the principal recognized meaning of ف ك ر directly concerns mental activity. There is no need to invent a hypothetical physical image to understand it.

A central point is that thinking, in its lexical meaning, does not necessarily mean thinking correctly. People can use thought to recognize truth, solve a problem, examine signs, or even devise justification and deception. Its value and outcome depend on the subject, intention, method, and context, rather than on the root alone.

2. Forms and semantic core

Arabic recognizes such forms as فَكَرَ, فَكَّرَ, تَفَكَّرَ, أَفْكَرَ, فِكْر, فِكْرَة, تَفْكِير, تَفَكُّر, and مُفَكِّر within this family.

Only two verbal patterns of this root occur in the Qur’an:

  • فَكَّرَ: a past-tense Form II verb, in al-Muddaththir 74:18.
  • تَفَكَّرَ: Form V, appearing in imperfect forms such as يَتَفَكَّرُونَ and تَتَفَكَّرُونَ, the jussive يَتَفَكَّرُوا after لَمْ, and the subjunctive تَتَفَكَّرُوا in Sabaʾ 34:46, coordinated with تَقُومُوا after أَنْ.

The root occurs 18 times in 18 Qur’anic verses: once in Form II and 17 times in Form V.

The nouns فِكْر, فِكْرَة, تَفْكِير, تَفَكُّر, and the designation مُفَكِّر do not occur in these nominal forms in the Qur’an. Rather than naming a discipline or a class of thinkers through these forms, the Qur’an speaks of the act of thinking itself.

Form II in فَكَّرَ can express engaging in thought or exercising one’s thinking. Form V in تَفَكَّرَ also concerns, in its Qur’anic uses, a person’s own reflection on and examination of a subject.

The difference in morphological pattern does not establish that فَكَّرَ always expresses negative thinking or تَفَكَّرَ always positive thinking. Such a distinction is not intrinsic to the two patterns.

The semantic core can be expressed as follows:

Exercising thought and directing the mind to examine a matter in order to grasp meaning, recognize relationships, weigh possibilities, or reach a conclusion.

This definition includes reflection on nature, examination of statements and claims, consideration of the consequences of conduct, and the use of thought to arrive at an incorrect conclusion.

3. Reflection on creation: from observing signs to examining meaning

Reflection on creation is one of the broadest contexts in which this root occurs in the Qur’an.

The Qur’an does not confine people to seeing phenomena. It calls them to think about what they see and examine how phenomena relate to creation, order, life, and truth.

Āl ʿImrān 3:191: reflecting on the creation of the heavens and earth

الَّذِينَ يَذْكُرُونَ اللَّهَ قِيَامًا وَقُعُودًا وَعَلَىٰ جُنُوبِهِمْ وَيَتَفَكَّرُونَ فِي خَلْقِ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ رَبَّنَا مَا خَلَقْتَ هَٰذَا بَاطِلًا سُبْحَانَكَ فَقِنَا عَذَابَ النَّارِ

Alladhīna yadhkurūna Allāha qiyāman wa-quʿūdan wa-ʿalā junūbihim wa-yatafakkarūna fī khalqi al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍi rabbanā mā khalaqta hādhā bāṭilan subḥānaka fa-qinā ʿadhāba al-nār.

“Those who remember The God while standing, sitting, and on their sides, and reflect on the creation of the heavens and earth: Our Lord, You have not created this in vain. Glory be to You; so protect us from the punishment of the Fire.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:191

In this verse, يَتَفَكَّرُونَ occurs with the preposition فِي, and its object is خَلْقِ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ, “the creation of the heavens and earth.”

Instead of merely seeing the heavens and earth, people reflect on their creation. The conclusion voiced by these thinkers in the verse rejects the purposelessness of creation and turns toward God.

The root فكر itself, however, does not mean discovering the purpose of creation or proving God’s existence. That conclusion comes from the subject and the continuation of the verse. Reflection is a mental activity; recognizing wisdom in creation is the result expressed in this context.

Remembrance and reflection also appear together here. Remembering God and reflecting on creation are distinct acts that accompany one another in the lives of the same people. Their association does not mean that every remembrance is necessarily reflection or that every reflection means verbally remembering God.

Signs in the earth, plants, and life

Al-Raʿd 13:3 describes the spreading of the earth, mountains, rivers, fruits, and the covering of day with night. It presents these phenomena as signs for people who reflect.

Al-Naḥl 16:11 connects the growth of crops, olives, date palms, grapes, and other fruits with reflection. Al-Naḥl 16:69 speaks of bees, their paths, and the drink of differing colors that emerges from within them and contains healing for people.

Yūnus 10:24 portrays worldly life through rain and the growth of plants, followed by their transformation and destruction. Reflecting on this example involves more than understanding plant growth; the instability of worldly circumstances is also part of the verse’s message.

These verses show that observing nature can begin the process of thinking, but observation and reflection are not identical.

Seeing a tree is observation. Examining how it grows from water and soil, how it relates to other living things, and what determines its survival or destruction is thinking about it. When the Qur’an describes these phenomena as signs, it also directs people to consider what they signify.

This contemporary example is not a lexical definition of “reflection.” Nor do these verses provide a complete theory of botany or biology; they make actual phenomena objects of attention and thought.

Reflection on oneself and on life

أَوَلَمْ يَتَفَكَّرُوا فِي أَنْفُسِهِمْ مَا خَلَقَ اللَّهُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ وَمَا بَيْنَهُمَا إِلَّا بِالْحَقِّ وَأَجَلٍ مُسَمًّى وَإِنَّ كَثِيرًا مِنَ النَّاسِ بِلِقَاءِ رَبِّهِمْ لَكَافِرُونَ

A-wa-lam yatafakkarū fī anfusihim mā khalaqa Allāhu al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍa wa-mā baynahumā illā bi-al-ḥaqqi wa-ajalin musamman wa-inna kathīran mina al-nāsi bi-liqāʾi rabbihim la-kāfirūn.

“Have they not reflected within themselves? The God created the heavens and earth and what lies between them only in truth and for an appointed term. Yet many people indeed deny the meeting with their Lord.”

Al-Rūm 30:8

The expression فِي أَنْفُسِهِمْ can refer to thinking within oneself or considering one’s own existence. In either case, the continuation connects the subject with the creation of the heavens and earth, creation in truth, and an appointed term. Restricting the verse to knowledge of human anatomy therefore fails to encompass its full context.

Al-Rūm 30:21 also presents the creation of spouses from among human beings themselves, finding tranquility together, and the establishment of affection and mercy between them as signs for people who reflect.

Al-Zumar 39:42 makes the taking of souls, death, sleep, and release until an appointed term subjects of reflection. It concerns a reality whose effects people encounter daily in part, while its full nature remains unclear to them. Reflection on it should not become the invention of a definitive theory of the soul’s nature or the unknown mechanisms of life after death.

Al-Jāthiyah 45:13 speaks of what is in the heavens and earth being subjected for human beings, presenting this whole as signs for those who reflect.

The object of thought differs in all these cases. Their common feature is that a person moves beyond a passing encounter with a phenomenon and makes it a subject of examination.

4. Reflection on speech, examples, and practical responsibility

Reflection in the Qur’an is not limited to knowing nature. People must also think about statements, decisions, benefits and harms, and the results of their conduct.

Al-Baqarah 2:219: weighing benefit and harm

يَسْأَلُونَكَ عَنِ الْخَمْرِ وَالْمَيْسِرِ قُلْ فِيهِمَا إِثْمٌ كَبِيرٌ وَمَنَافِعُ لِلنَّاسِ وَإِثْمُهُمَا أَكْبَرُ مِنْ نَفْعِهِمَا وَيَسْأَلُونَكَ مَاذَا يُنْفِقُونَ قُلِ الْعَفْوَ كَذَٰلِكَ يُبَيِّنُ اللَّهُ لَكُمُ الْآيَاتِ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَتَفَكَّرُونَ

Yasʾalūnaka ʿani al-khamri wa-al-maysiri qul fīhimā ithmun kabīrun wa-manāfiʿu li-al-nāsi wa-ithmuhumā akbaru min nafʿihimā wa-yasʾalūnaka mādhā yunfiqūna quli al-ʿafwa kadhālika yubayyinu Allāhu lakumu al-āyāti laʿallakum tatafakkarūn.

“They ask you about wine and gambling. Say: In both there is great sin and benefits for people, but their sin is greater than their benefit. And they ask you what they should spend. Say: What is surplus to need. Thus The God makes the signs clear to you, so that you may reflect.”

Al-Baqarah 2:219

Placing benefit and harm together matters here. The Qur’an does not deny that benefit exists, but weighs it against the greater sin, clarifying the basis for judgment.

Reflection in this context can invite people to evaluate conduct from several angles, rather than regard the mere existence of a benefit as sufficient to justify it.

The second question, about spending, also concerns a practical decision. People must think about their possessions, their needs, and giving from what they have.

Reflection is therefore not merely an activity detached from life; it can shape everyday choices and conduct.

Al-Baqarah 2:266: reflecting on an example

Al-Baqarah 2:266 imagines someone with a flourishing garden of date palms, grapes, and every kind of fruit. When old age has overtaken him and he has vulnerable children, a fiery whirlwind destroys the garden.

The verse ends with لَعَلَّكُمْ تَتَفَكَّرُونَ, “so that you may reflect.”

Readers must imagine themselves in that situation, weigh the relationship between possessions, need, vulnerability, and the timing of loss, and understand the example within the context of the verses on giving.

Reflection here is more than observing an actual garden: it examines a hypothetical situation to grasp a truth about human conduct.

In al-Aʿrāf 7:176, after the example of someone who turned away from the signs and followed his desire, the instruction to recount narratives is also connected with the audience’s reflection.

Al-Ḥashr 59:21 similarly links reflection with the example of a mountain that, if the Qur’an had been sent down upon it, would have humbled itself and split apart in awe of God.

Qur’anic examples therefore do more than embellish speech. An example can invite the mind to compare, examine relationships, and grasp a meaning beyond the visible image.

Al-Naḥl 16:44: clear explanation and the audience’s reflection

بِالْبَيِّنَاتِ وَالزُّبُرِ وَأَنْزَلْنَا إِلَيْكَ الذِّكْرَ لِتُبَيِّنَ لِلنَّاسِ مَا نُزِّلَ إِلَيْهِمْ وَلَعَلَّهُمْ يَتَفَكَّرُونَ

Bi-al-bayyināti wa-al-zuburi wa-anzalnā ilayka al-dhikra li-tubayyina li-al-nāsi mā nuzzila ilayhim wa-laʿallahum yatafakkarūn.

“With clear proofs and writings; and We sent down to you the Reminder so that you may make clear to people what has been sent down to them, and so that they may reflect.”

Al-Naḥl 16:44

Here تُبَيِّنَ and يَتَفَكَّرُونَ are distinct verbs.

The first part states the responsibility to clarify what has been revealed. The second concerns people’s reflection.

Their association shows that conveying and clarifying the message does not replace the audience’s own thinking. People must also examine what has reached them and understand its meaning.

Conversely, the verb “reflect” does not itself signify a right to legislate independently or invent arbitrary meanings for revelation. Thinking must address the actual subject under examination.

In this connection, al-Naḥl 16:35, al-Naḥl 16:82, al-Māʾidah 5:92, and al-Taghābun 64:12 also matter for examining the relationship between the Messenger’s mission, clear delivery, and the audience’s responsibilities.

5. Reflection, independent judgment, and the possibility of mistaken thought

One significant use of this root occurs in Sabaʾ 34:46:

قُلْ إِنَّمَا أَعِظُكُمْ بِوَاحِدَةٍ أَنْ تَقُومُوا لِلَّهِ مَثْنَىٰ وَفُرَادَىٰ ثُمَّ تَتَفَكَّرُوا مَا بِصَاحِبِكُمْ مِنْ جِنَّةٍ إِنْ هُوَ إِلَّا نَذِيرٌ لَكُمْ بَيْنَ يَدَيْ عَذَابٍ شَدِيدٍ

Qul innamā aʿiẓukum bi-wāḥidatin an taqūmū li-Allāhi mathnā wa-furādā thumma tatafakkarū mā bi-ṣāḥibikum min jinnatin in huwa illā nadhīrun lakum bayna yaday ʿadhābin shadīd.

“Say: I counsel you only one thing: that you stand for The God, in pairs and individually, then reflect. There is no madness in your companion. He is only a warner to you before a severe punishment.”

Sabaʾ 34:46

The verse places reflection after the call to stand for God, in pairs and individually.

The immediate object of reflection is also clear: examining the accusation made against their companion.

The verse shows that a widespread claim can be examined anew. Public belief, a collective accusation, or repetition of a statement cannot by themselves replace its evaluation.

The reference to مَثْنَىٰ وَفُرَادَىٰ brings reflection with another person and reflection individually into view in this context. It should not become a rule that all thinking is valid only in solitude or in pairs.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:184 likewise asks why people have not reflected on the accusation of madness against their companion. In al-Anʿām 6:50, after the Messenger states that he does not possess God’s treasuries, does not independently know the unseen, and is not an angel, the question whether the blind and the seeing are equal accompanies an invitation to reflect.

These uses show that reflection can help examine claims, recognize the limits of people’s abilities, and distinguish truth from supposition.

Al-Muddaththir 74:18: when thinking serves a mistaken conclusion

إِنَّهُ فَكَّرَ وَقَدَّرَ

Innahu fakkara wa-qaddar.

“Indeed, he thought and calculated.”

Al-Muddaththir 74:18

This is the Qur’an’s only occurrence of the Form II verb فَكَّرَ.

The continuation criticizes how he calculated and decided. His conclusion appears in al-Muddaththir 74:24:

فَقَالَ إِنْ هَٰذَا إِلَّا سِحْرٌ يُؤْثَرُ

Fa-qāla in hādhā illā siḥrun yuʾthar.

“Then he said: This is nothing but magic handed down.”

Al-Muddaththir 74:24

In al-Muddaththir 74:25, he attributes it to human speech.

Taken together, these verses show that the person has exercised thought and reached a conclusion that the Qur’an condemns.

This matters for understanding the root: a conclusion reached after thinking is not necessarily correct.

Someone may think extensively about a subject yet arrive at an incorrect result because of prejudice, self-interest, fear of accepting truth, or faulty reasoning. These are recognizable possibilities in human conduct. But the motives of the person in al-Muddaththir must be assessed through the evidence of those verses, rather than extracting all such motives from the word فَكَّرَ itself.

We therefore cannot say that Form II means deceptive thinking and Form V means guiding thought.

Both patterns concern mental activity. Moral judgment comes from the context, subject, and outcome.

6. Distinguishing ف ك ر from related roots

Reflection in the Qur’an is related to reasoning, attentive consideration, examination, knowledge, and recollection. These activities may occur together in a cognitive process, but their lexical meanings are not identical.

ف ك ر (fakara) and ع ق ل (ʿaqala): thinking and reasoning

The Arabic root ع ق ل has a physical branch involving binding and restraint. In the sphere of cognition, it concerns understanding, weighing, and exercising reason.

ف ك ر directly denotes thinking and mentally examining a subject.

In Qur’anic usage, reflection and reasoning can lead to similar results. A person reflects on a sign, weighs its relationships, and reaches understanding. Yet reflection cannot invariably be placed before reasoning, nor reasoning treated as its inevitable final stage.

For a fuller examination of this family, see Forqan’s study of the root ع ق ل.

ف ك ر (fakara) and د ب ر (dabara): reflection and attentive consideration

The physical sense of د ب ر concerns the back or rear of something. In the branch تَدَبَّرَ, the focus is careful examination of a subject and attention to its connections and implications.

Qur’anic occurrences of tadabbur directly concern the Qur’an, speech, or the Book’s signs, whereas the objects of tafakkur are more varied: the creation of the heavens and earth, narratives, examples, human conduct, and examination of a claim.

This difference in Qur’anic usage is real, but it should not be made an absolute rule for all Arabic. Tafakkur can also be deep, sustained, and precise; tadabbur does not simply mean predicting how an undertaking will end.

For a fuller account of this distinction, see the study of the root د ب ر.

ف ك ر (fakara) and ن ظ ر (naẓara): thinking and looking or examining

In Arabic, ن ظ ر can concern looking, paying attention, examining, or waiting.

Naẓar sometimes begins with sensory sight and sometimes itself constitutes intellectual examination. These families therefore overlap in some uses.

Tafakkur in Qur’anic usage denotes the activity of thinking and examining a subject, whereas naẓar can also denote direct looking or waiting.

Every naẓar cannot be treated as superficial seeing, nor every tafakkur as profound understanding. The construction and context determine the distinction.

The study of the root ن ظ ر is available for examining this family.

ف ك ر (fakara) and ع ل م (ʿalima): thinking and knowing

عِلْم concerns knowing and awareness. Tafakkur refers to the mental activity of examining a subject.

People can use prior knowledge to think about a new question. They may also acquire new understanding through reflection.

Knowing a truth does not mean that a person is reflecting on it at that moment. Thinking about something likewise does not guarantee knowledge of its truth.

Reflection and knowledge are consequently related, but are not complete synonyms.

ف ك ر (fakara) and ذ ك ر (dhakara): thinking and remembering

Dhikr, in its various uses, concerns remembering, recalling, and bringing a subject into awareness.

Tafakkur concerns mentally examining a subject encountered or recalled.

The two verbs appear together in Āl ʿImrān 3:191, showing that remembrance of God and reflection on creation can accompany one another in a person’s life.

Dhikr should not be reduced to thinking, nor tafakkur to mere recollection.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

For the Qur’anic forms of ف ك ر, the basic natural equivalents in Persian/Dari express “thinking,” “reflecting,” “becoming absorbed in thought,” and, where context warrants, “thinking carefully.” These senses also guide English translation.

فَكَّرَ: “he thought” or “he reflected.”

In al-Muddaththir 74:18, “he thought and calculated” is appropriate because فَكَّرَ and قَدَّرَ occur together, and both verbs must remain in the translation.

يَتَفَكَّرُونَ: “they reflect” or “they think about it.”

Where the object of thought is clear in the sentence, the translation need not incorporate a lengthy explanation of the root.

تَتَفَكَّرُونَ: “you reflect.”

In لَعَلَّكُمْ تَتَفَكَّرُونَ, “so that you may reflect” or “perhaps you will reflect” preserves the expression of hope or purpose.

أَفَلَا تَتَفَكَّرُونَ: “Will you not reflect?”

In al-Anʿām 6:50, this rendering conveys both the verb’s meaning and the sentence’s questioning, reproachful form.

أَوَلَمْ يَتَفَكَّرُوا: “Have they not reflected?”

In al-Aʿrāf 7:184 and al-Rūm 30:8, this backward-looking form asks why the people addressed have not reflected.

تَتَفَكَّرُوا: “reflect.”

In Sabaʾ 34:46, the expression follows ثُمَّ and concerns reflection after standing for God.

Basic translations should not replace the verb in every verse with “deep thinking,” “scientific thinking,” “philosophical thinking,” or “critical thinking.” These can be kinds or applications of thinking, but they do not follow from the root alone.

Although the borrowed word tafakkur is familiar in Persian, the native equivalent meaning “to think” is generally clearer and more fluent in natural Qur’anic translation.

Conclusion

ف ك ر belongs to the semantic field of thinking and exercising the mind to examine a subject. Through reflection, people raise an issue mentally, weigh its aspects, and consider it to understand meaning or reach a conclusion.

The Qur’an does not restrict this activity to one subject. Signs of creation, plants, human life, sleep and death, affection and mercy, statements and claims, the benefits and harms of conduct, narratives, and examples can all become objects of reflection.

Most Qur’anic uses invite people to recognize signs and truth. Al-Muddaththir 74:18 nevertheless shows that exercising thought does not itself guarantee a correct conclusion. Thinking can serve understanding or justify a mistaken result.

Reflection should therefore neither be confined to thinking about nature nor expanded to mean every kind of cognition, knowledge, reasoning, or attentive consideration. Each family has its own semantic range, with connections in certain uses.

Final definition for Forqan:

ف ك ر: exercising thought to examine and weigh a subject in order to grasp meaning, recognize relationships, or reach a conclusion, without the correctness or incorrectness of that conclusion being intrinsic to the root’s meaning.

In Qur’anic Form V usage, this meaning appears chiefly as considered reflection on verses, signs, events, and statements. Its moral direction and outcome are determined by each verse’s context.
