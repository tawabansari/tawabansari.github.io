The Arabic root S GH B concerns hunger and the hardship caused by insufficient food. Saghiba means to become hungry, sāghib means hungry, and masghabah means hunger or famine.

Lexical sources sometimes explain the root through jūʿ, hunger, and sometimes emphasize hunger accompanied by fatigue and suffering. This shows that saghab is not confined to an ordinary feeling of needing food: it can describe the greater hardship of food deprivation.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as masghabah in Al-Balad 90:14, where feeding others on a day of hunger is presented among the deeds associated with crossing the difficult pass.

Notably, hunger is not attributed simply to one individual. The Qur’an speaks of yawmin dhī masghabatin: a day characterized by hunger and scarce food. Providing nourishment to those in need takes on particular significance in such circumstances.

1. The root in early Arabic

Saghab: hunger and the hardship it brings

Arabic lexical sources define saghiba al-rajulu through jāʿa: the person became hungry.

Al-saghab names this condition, and al-saghbah also denotes hunger.

Alongside the general meaning, another explanation is recorded: al-jūʿu maʿa al-taʿabi, “hunger with fatigue.”

This explanation distinguishes passing hunger from hunger accompanied by suffering and exhaustion.

Someone may become hungry after several hours without eating while retaining access to food and ordinary strength. Someone deprived of adequate food for a prolonged period, however, may experience weakness, fatigue, and difficulty performing everyday tasks.

In its broad usage, saghab denotes hunger and can include this distressing condition.

The explanation “hunger with fatigue” should not nevertheless be taken to make fatigue indispensable in every Arabic use. Lexical accounts identify hunger as the basic meaning, with some emphasizing accompanying fatigue.

Other lexical explanations likewise relate the root directly to hunger and explain masghabah through majāʿah, hunger or famine.

The semantic center is therefore clear: hunger, especially when accompanied by hardship caused by insufficient food.

The status of historical evidence

To understand the root in pre-Islamic Arabic, one must distinguish dictionary-recorded usage from an independent witness whose pre-Qur’anic date can be established.

Saghab, sāghib, and masghabah are clearly associated with hunger in Arabic sources. The Qur’an itself also shows that masghabah was intelligible as hunger and food scarcity in the Arabic of its revelation.

This article presents no independent verse or text with an established pre-Islamic date that determines the root’s precise earlier usage.

That limitation does not mean the word was unknown before the Qur’an. It means that its exact historical currency cannot be dated solely through dictionaries compiled later.

Sāghib in Arabic poetry

A couplet transmitted without a poet’s name in early lexical sources states:

فَلَوْ كُنْتَ حُرًّا يَا ابْنَ قَيْسِ بْنِ عَاصِمٍ
لَمَا بِتَّ شَبْعَانًا وَجَارُكَ سَاغِبُ

fa-law kunta ḥurran yā ibna Qaysi bni ʿĀṣimin / la-mā bitta shabʿānan wa-jāruka sāghibu

“Had you been noble, son of Qays ibn ʿĀṣim, you would not have spent the night full while your neighbor was hungry.”

Here, sāghib stands opposite shabʿān.

Shabʿān describes someone who is full, and sāghib someone hungry.

The contrast makes the word’s direct, tangible meaning clear. The poet reproaches someone who sleeps with a full stomach while his neighbor goes hungry.

The ethical reproach and the neighbor’s claim arise from the whole couplet; sāghib alone describes hunger.

Lexical transmissions vary in wording and form of address. This article does not establish the poet’s identity or the date of composition with certainty, so the couplet should not be presented without qualification as a securely dated pre-Islamic witness.

Jarīr, a poet of the Umayyad period, also says:

تُعَلِّلُ وَهْيَ سَاغِبَةٌ بَنِيهَا
بِأَنْفَاسٍ مِنَ الشَّبِمِ الْقَرَاحِ

tuʿallilu wa-hiya sāghibatun banīhā / bi-anfāsin mina al-shabimi al-qarāḥi

“Hungry herself, she soothes her children with draughts of cool, pure water.”

The couplet depicts poverty and a shortage of food. The mother is hungry and uses water to calm her children instead of sufficient nourishment.

Sāghibah describes the hungry woman. The word alone does not establish that her children are equally hungry, although the whole scene depicts the family’s difficult circumstances.

This witness belongs to the Umayyad period and should not be called pre-Islamic poetry. It nevertheless clearly illustrates sāghib in early Arabic independently of commentary on the Qur’anic verse.

Saghab and thirst

Some lexical accounts report occasional use of saghab for thirst.

Those accounts also describe this use as uncommon.

The root’s basic meaning should therefore not be broadened into the body’s general need for food and drink. Its recognized usage concerns hunger.

Thirst may accompany hunger, and both can cause weakness and suffering, but their coexistence does not make them identical concepts.

In Al-Balad, masghabah also appears with iṭʿām, feeding. This association confirms hunger; there is no reason here to translate it as thirst.

2. Forms and derivatives

Arabic records several forms in the S GH B family.

Saghiba yasghabu: to become hungry. Saghaba yasghubu is also recorded.

Saghab: hunger; a verbal noun naming the condition of being hungry.

Sughūb and saghābah: recorded verbal nouns for hunger.

Sāghib: an active participle describing someone hungry.

Saghib and saghbān: recorded adjectives for a hungry person.

Saghbā: a feminine adjective describing hunger.

Sighāb: a recorded plural for hungry people.

Asghaba: in one usage, to enter a condition of hunger or famine.

Musghib: someone who has entered a condition of hunger or famine.

Masghabah: hunger or famine; a condition of food scarcity and its associated hardship.

Of these forms, only masghabah appears in the Qur’an.

It occurs once, as masghabatin in Al-Balad 90:14.

Masghabah follows the pattern mafʿalah and functions here as a verbal noun, expressing a condition of hunger or famine.

Its initial mīm does not by itself make it the name of a specific place or time. In the Qur’anic phrase, yawm names the time, while masghabah identifies the condition associated with that day.

Nor does masghabah name a hungry person. Arabic has forms such as sāghib for that description.

This distinction matters for understanding the verse accurately.

3. Masghabah in the Qur’an: feeding in a time of hunger

Al-Balad 90:14 states:

أَوْ إِطْعَامٌ فِي يَوْمٍ ذِي مَسْغَبَةٍ

aw iṭʿāmun fī yawmin dhī masghabatin

Al-Balad 90:14

“Or feeding on a day of hunger.”

This is the root’s only Qur’anic occurrence.

Here, the phrase dhī masghabatin describes a day or time characterized by hunger and food scarcity; masghabah names that condition.

Two parts of the verse deserve attention: iṭʿāmun and fī yawmin dhī masghabatin.

Iṭʿām belongs to Ṭ ʿ M and means feeding.

Masghabah identifies the condition of hunger and insufficient food.

The verse thus describes an action that answers people’s real needs in difficult circumstances.

The significance of yawmin dhī masghabatin

In the Arabic sentence, yawmin follows fī, and dhī modifies it.

Dhī is the first term of a possessive construction, while masghabatin is its genitive complement. Together they describe yawmin.

The structure means a day possessing masghabah: a day characterized by hunger and food scarcity.

This construction does not itself say that everyone in a town or region is equally hungry.

Nor does “day” necessarily restrict the condition to twenty-four hours. Arabic yawm can situate a condition or event in time without the verse specifying its exact duration.

“A day of hunger” and “a time of food scarcity” are therefore conceptually close, although retaining “day” in the basic translation follows the Arabic structure more closely.

Does masghabah mean only a general famine?

Lexical sources explain masghabah through majāʿah.

Majāʿah can denote famine or widespread hunger in a community or region, where food becomes scarce and access to nourishment difficult.

The verse itself, however, specifies neither the geographical extent nor the number of hungry people.

A region may suffer scarcity through drought, war, blocked food routes, or lost livelihoods.

People in a generally food-supplied society may also lack adequate nourishment because of severe poverty.

Such situations can instantiate hunger and food scarcity, but these causes are not part of masghabah’s lexical meaning.

What the verse explicitly presents is hunger and the importance of feeding others in that situation.

Must the person giving food also be hungry?

In Al-Balad 90:14, dhī masghabatin describes the day, not the person providing food.

The phrase alone therefore does not require the giver to be hungry for the action to qualify as feeding on a day of hunger.

Someone with sufficient food may feed the needy during famine.

Someone else may face scarcity personally yet share what is available.

Both can be instances of feeding others in a time of hunger.

The act’s special value in the chapter’s context relates to difficult circumstances and recipients’ actual need. A condition absent from the wording should not be added.

The difficult pass and responsibility toward the needy

Al-Balad 90:11 states:

فَلَا اقْتَحَمَ الْعَقَبَةَ

fa-lā iqtaḥama al-ʿaqabata

Al-Balad 90:11

“Yet he did not cross the difficult pass.”

Al-Balad 90:12 states:

وَمَا أَدْرَاكَ مَا الْعَقَبَةُ

wa-mā adrāka mā al-ʿaqabatu

Al-Balad 90:12

“And what has made you know what the difficult pass is?”

The next verse, Al-Balad 90:13, states:

فَكُّ رَقَبَةٍ

fakku raqabatin

Al-Balad 90:13

“Freeing a neck.”

Feeding on a day of hunger follows.

The connection matters. Earlier, in Al-Balad 90:6, the Qur’an describes someone claiming to have squandered great wealth.

In describing the crossing of the difficult pass, however, attention turns to actions that free others and meet their needs.

Feeding in a time of hunger is a tangible act connected with human life and well-being.

When food is scarce or inaccessible, supplying it to someone in need can prevent suffering, weakness, and harm from worsening.

The act’s importance thus lies not merely in the amount spent, but in answering a need present in those circumstances.

This conclusion comes from the chapter’s context. Masghabah still names hunger or famine, not generosity or crossing the difficult pass.

4. Who needs food in a time of masghabah?

Al-Balad 90:15 states:

يَتِيمًا ذَا مَقْرَبَةٍ

yatīman dhā maqrabatin

Al-Balad 90:15

“An orphan who is a relative.”

Al-Balad 90:16 states:

أَوْ مِسْكِينًا ذَا مَتْرَبَةٍ

aw miskīnan dhā matrabatin

Al-Balad 90:16

“Or a destitute person reduced to the dust.”

These verses identify recipients of the feeding mentioned immediately before them.

Yatīman and miskīnan function as recipients of iṭʿām in the sentence.

There is a distinction between dhī masghabatin in Al-Balad 90:14 and dhā maqrabatin and dhā matrabatin in the next two verses.

Dhī masghabatin refers to the day; dhā maqrabatin describes the orphan, and dhā matrabatin the needy person.

The Qur’an thus first states the difficult condition of hunger and then identifies two examples of people needing food in it.

An orphan who is a relative

Yatīm concerns family status and the loss of a father; it should not automatically be translated as poor.

An orphan may nevertheless be vulnerable in access to food, property, and family support.

Dhā maqrabatin emphasizes kinship and family closeness.

The recipient is thus an orphan with whom there is also a family relationship.

This relationship may intensify relatives’ ethical responsibility and concern, but does not change the meaning of masghabah.

The verse alone does not establish that every orphan is poor or that all orphans need food equally.

A destitute person reduced to the dust

Miskīn denotes a needy person facing material constraint and difficulty securing a livelihood.

Dhā matrabatin gives a stronger image of deprivation: someone seemingly brought down to the dust or deprived of ordinary means of living.

The expression should not always be limited to literally living on bare earth or possessing no shelter whatsoever.

In the feeding context, it concerns a needy person whose severe deprivation makes assistance especially important and urgent.

Masghabah and matrabah thus meet in a social setting but are not synonyms.

Masghabah describes hunger and food scarcity, while matrabah emphasizes a person’s severe poverty and deprivation.

Someone may be very poor yet have food at a particular time. Conversely, a crisis or interrupted food access may leave someone hungry who was not previously among society’s poorest.

Poverty, hunger, and famine are therefore related but distinct concepts.

Faith, perseverance, and compassion

Al-Balad 90:17 states:

ثُمَّ كَانَ مِنَ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَتَوَاصَوْا بِالصَّبْرِ وَتَوَاصَوْا بِالْمَرْحَمَةِ

thumma kāna mina alladhīna āmanū wa-tawāṣaw bi-al-ṣabri wa-tawāṣaw bi-al-marḥamati

Al-Balad 90:17

“Then be among those who believe and urge one another to patience and urge one another to compassion.”

The verse places faith and mutual encouragement to patience and compassion alongside the preceding deeds.

Feeding someone in need is thus not isolated from human relationships: it connects with ethical responsibility, concern for others’ suffering, and reciprocal encouragement to compassion.

This connection does not make masghabah itself mean compassion, divine testing, or spiritual poverty.

Masghabah remains hunger and food scarcity. The Qur’an responds to that condition with an act meeting real human need.

5. Distinctions from nearby roots

S GH B and J W ʿ: hunger and the hardship of insufficient food

J W ʿ concerns hunger and the body’s need for food.

Jūʿ may describe passing hunger or prolonged, severe hunger. Its intensity depends on context and circumstances.

Saghab also concerns hunger, with lexical explanations highlighting accompanying fatigue and hardship.

Masghabah likewise commonly denotes a condition of hunger or famine.

This should not become an absolute rule that every jūʿ is mild hunger and every saghab life-threatening starvation.

Quraysh 106:4 states:

الَّذِي أَطْعَمَهُمْ مِنْ جُوعٍ وَآمَنَهُمْ مِنْ خَوْفٍ

alladhī aṭʿamahum min jūʿin wa-āmanahum min khawfin

Quraysh 106:4

“Who fed them against hunger and secured them against fear.”

Here, jūʿ denotes an unmet need for food, and aṭʿamahum describes meeting that need.

In Al-Balad 90:14, masghabah names hunger and food scarcity, and iṭʿām is the practical response.

In both, hunger relates to supplying food, although one uses jūʿ and the other masghabah.

A fuller comparison should likewise distinguish the meaning of hunger from the various circumstances in which it arises; neither word alone fixes its cause or severity.

S GH B and KH M Ṣ: painful hunger and an empty belly

KH M Ṣ concerns hollowness and reduced volume in Arabic.

Makhmaṣah belongs to this family and denotes hunger and food scarcity in the Qur’an.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:3, makhmaṣah appears in the context of necessity; in Al-Tawbah 9:120, it stands alongside thirst and fatigue.

Masghabah and makhmaṣah are close in referring to hunger and insufficient food.

The basic field of khamṣ concerns hollowness and an empty belly, whereas saghab relates directly to hunger and its associated hardship.

This is a tendency rather than an absolute rule.

One cannot say that every makhmaṣah is more severe than masghabah or that masghabah always follows makhmaṣah. Qur’anic usage establishes no such fixed sequence.

Makhmaṣah in Al-Māʾidah 5:3, through its association with necessity, belongs to a legal discussion of food; masghabah in Al-Balad 90:14 occurs in a passage about feeding those in need.

Beyond their lexical fields, the verses therefore differ in subject: one concerns someone facing hunger and necessity, the other feeding people in a time of hunger.

S GH B and L GH B: hunger and fatigue

Laghab concerns fatigue and exhaustion in Arabic.

The expression sāghibun lāghibun is recorded for someone hungry and tired.

Their pairing shows that hunger and fatigue can coexist.

They nevertheless do not mean the same thing.

Someone may tire from work or a long journey without being hungry. Conversely, someone may be hungry without yet displaying obvious exhaustion.

Sāghib therefore concerns hunger and lāghib fatigue.

Where saghab is explained as hunger with fatigue, that association does not merge the two roots.

S GH B and KH Ṣ Ṣ: hunger and need

Khaṣāṣah concerns need and a lack of means.

In Al-Ḥashr 59:9, it describes people who prefer others to themselves despite their own need.

Khaṣāṣah can lead to hunger, but does not itself mean being hungry.

Someone may need clothing, housing, tools, or other essentials without being hungry at that moment.

Saghab, by contrast, directly concerns hunger.

Economic need may thus cause insufficient food, but is not identical to hunger itself.

S GH B and Ṭ ʿ M: a condition of need and its response

Ṭ ʿ M concerns food, eating, tasting, and feeding.

Iṭʿām in Al-Balad 90:14 means feeding.

Masghabah identifies the condition of need and insufficient food.

The two families thus express two sides of a relationship: hunger and a practical response to it.

The presence of food somewhere does not prove that hungry people’s needs are met. Food may be in warehouses, markets, or some people’s possession while those who need it cannot obtain it.

Providing food and delivering it to people in need is then an important response to hunger.

This conclusion fits iṭʿām and the verse’s context, but the whole question of economic distribution, social justice, or food supply systems should not be inserted into masghabah’s lexical meaning.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

The basic equivalent for S GH B is “hunger.”

Where severity and suffering are prominent, “severe hunger” or “hunger with hardship” may fit.

Several renderings of masghabah in Al-Balad 90:14 merit consideration:

“A day of hunger” conveys the general meaning and difficult circumstances.

“A day of famine” emphasizes widespread scarcity but may restrict the verse to a general crisis.

“A day of severe hunger” is also natural and close to the lexical meaning.

A suitable basic rendering for Forqan is:

“Or feeding on a day of hunger.”

This preserves masghabah’s basic meaning while conveying the difficult condition addressed.

The following equivalents suit the non-Qur’anic forms; masghabah, the Qur’anic form, is included for comparison:

  • Saghiba: became hungry.
  • Saghab: hunger.
  • Sāghib: hungry.
  • Saghbān: hungry; in some uses, severely hungry.
  • Saghbā: a hungry woman.
  • Asghaba: entered a state of hunger or famine.
  • Masghabah: hunger or famine; a difficult condition of insufficient food.

Masghabah should not be translated here as poverty, oppression, drought, an economic boycott, or famine-related deaths.

Such conditions may accompany or cause hunger, but are not the root’s direct meaning.

Nor should masghabah in Al-Balad 90:14 become spiritual hunger or deficient faith. Iṭʿām, the orphan, and the needy person indicate a real need for food.

Conclusion

S GH B concerns hunger and the hardship of insufficient food.

Lexical sources explain it through jūʿ and sometimes emphasize hunger’s association with fatigue and difficulty.

Early poetic witnesses likewise depict actual bodily hunger: a neighbor spending the night hungry and a mother soothing her children with draughts of water amid food scarcity.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as masghabah in Al-Balad 90:14.

The verse describes feeding others on a day characterized by hunger and scarce food.

The chapter places this alongside freeing a neck and caring for a related orphan and a destitute person reduced to the dust. Faith, perseverance, and mutual encouragement to compassion follow.

Feeding during masghabah matters not merely as giving away a possession, but as answering the real need of someone deprived of adequate nourishment in difficult circumstances.

This ethical message must nevertheless not replace the lexical definition.

The semantic center of S GH B is hunger, especially hunger accompanied by hardship. Masghabah describes hunger and food scarcity, which may also take the form of famine or a crisis of subsistence.

In Al-Balad 90:14, this condition is associated with people’s time and circumstances. Rather than praising hunger’s suffering, the verse presents feeding and meeting need among the deeds of crossing the difficult pass.

The distinction is fundamental: hunger is not itself a virtue; the valued act here is responding practically to others’ hunger and deprivation.

For Forqan, “hunger” is the basic meaning, and “a day of hunger” a suitable rendering of yawmin dhī masghabatin.
