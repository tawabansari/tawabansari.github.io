The root Ḥ B K (ح ب ك)

Firm weaving, interconnected parts, and ordered patterns and paths

The Arabic root Ḥ B K concerns making something firm and orderly, especially through weaving and joining its parts. حَبَكَ الثَّوْبَ (ḥabaka al-thawba) is used when cloth is woven well and firmly. مَحْبُوك (maḥbūk) describes something whose parts are well joined or whose structure is firm and orderly. From the same family, حُبُك (ḥubuk) also denotes continuous patterns and tracks visible on water, sand, hair, cloth, or mail.

Two aspects matter in this word family: soundness and order in construction, and the patterns and extensions produced by such construction or arrangement. They are related but distinct. Something may be firmly woven without a conspicuous pattern; patterns may appear on water or sand without anyone weaving them like cloth.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an: الْحُبُكِ (al-ḥubuki) in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:7, in وَالسَّمَاءِ ذَاتِ الْحُبُكِ (wa-al-samāʾi dhāti al-ḥubuki).

The verse describes the sky as possessing ḥubuk. Understanding this expression requires first establishing the word’s Arabic meanings: ordered patterns and paths, soundness and beauty of construction, or a characteristic encompassing both aspects.

1. Forms and derivatives

Ḥ B K consists of ḥāʾ, bāʾ, and kāf. It is a sound triliteral Arabic root, without a weak, doubled, or hamzated radical.

Important forms in this family include:

  • حَبَكَ ـ يَحْبِكُ / يَحْبُكُ (ḥabaka–yaḥbiku/yaḥbuku): wove something well and firmly; made its parts firm and orderly.
  • حَبْك (ḥabk): weaving and making firm; also the resulting firmness and order.
  • حُبُك (ḥubuk): ordered patterns, tracks, and extensions, including those on water, sand, hair, and other arrangements.
  • حَبِيكَة (ḥabīkah): one pattern or track among such ordered patterns and tracks.
  • حِبَاك (ḥibāk): a pattern or extension on a surface or in a structure; one singular form associated with ḥubuk in lexicons.
  • حَبَائِك (ḥabāʾik): plural of ḥabīkah: ordered patterns and extensions.
  • حَبِيك (ḥabīk): as an adjective, woven or firmly constructed; in the field of patterns, also a collective form associated with ḥabīkah.
  • مَحْبُوك (maḥbūk): well and firmly woven; also firmly constructed with well-connected parts.
  • حَبِيكُ الشَّعْرِ (ḥabīku al-shaʿri): patterns and extensions formed by the curves of hair.
  • احْتَبَكَ (iḥtabaka): made something firm and well, or secured its parts.
  • حُبْكَة (ḥubkah): in some early uses, a cord or something used to bind parts firmly.

Lexicons record several morphological analyses of ḥubuk. Some treat it as the plural of حَبِيكَة (ḥabīkah), others as the plural of حِبَاك (ḥibāk). حَبَائِك (ḥabāʾik) and ḥibāk also belong to this family.

These differences should be acknowledged. In its Qur’anic use, however, الْحُبُكِ is clearly a plural form associated with ordered patterns or extensions.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: sole occurrence in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:7

Only this noun occurs in the Qur’an. The verb ḥabaka, the verbal noun ḥabk, maḥbūk, ḥabīkah, and the family’s other forms do not occur in the Qur’anic text.

2. Semantic core and early Arabic usage

Making firm and joining parts

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris describes the root’s core as follows:

إِحْكَامُ الشَّيْءِ فِي امْتِدَادٍ وَاطِّرَادٍ

iḥkāmu al-shayʾi fī imtidādin wa-iṭṭirādin

That is, making something sound and orderly in extension and continuity.

This definition concerns more than firmness alone: extension and regularity within the construction also matter.

Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs al-Lughah: extension, soundness, and two explanations of the sky

In cloth, for example, warp and weft are arranged together to form a continuous structure. When their connections are sound and firm, the cloth is well woven.

Arabic therefore says حَبَكَ الثَّوْبَ (ḥabaka al-thawba): he wove the cloth well and firmly.

In Lisān al-ʿArab, this expression is explained through good weaving and the visible quality of workmanship.

Ḥabk therefore involves more than merely placing parts together; the quality and firmness of their connection also belong to its meaning.

This should not become a claim that every use of Ḥ B K necessarily concerns cloth, warp and weft, or human manufacture.

The same root also describes arrangements that arise in nature.

Ordered patterns and paths

In early Arabic, ḥubuk describes patterns and extensions visible on a surface.

These patterns may be straight, curved, rippled, or interconnected.

When wind passes over still water, for example, it produces wave-like patterns. On sand it can create rows and ridges. Curly hair can likewise show ordered, curving extensions.

Arabic uses ḥubuk in these settings.

In Lisān al-ʿArab, Ibn Manẓūr explains حُبُك الرَّمْل (ḥubuk al-raml) through the shapes and extensions of sand, and reports similar usage for water and curly hair.

Al-Farrāʾ likewise explains ḥubuk through patterns formed by wind on sand or water, comparing them with the configurations of mail and curly hair.

These examples show that ḥubuk does not simply mean a road along which a person travels.

طَرَائِق (ṭarāʾiq), when used to explain this word, can mean patterns, rows, and extensions on a surface or within a structure.

Translating ḥubuk merely as “paths,” without explanation, may therefore suggest routes for travel alone, although the word’s lexical range is broader.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab: water, sand, hair, and weaving

A witness attributed to pre-Islamic poetry: patterns on water

Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā, the well-known pre-Islamic poet, is quoted as describing water patterned by the wind:

مُكَلَّلٌ بِأُصُولِ النَّجْمِ تَنْسُجُهُ
رِيحٌ خَرِيقٌ لِضَاحِي مَائِهِ حُبُكُ

mukallalun bi-uṣūli al-najmi tansujuhu / rīḥun kharīqun li-ḍāḥī māʾihi ḥubuku

That is: “Water encircled by low-growing plants; a strong wind weaves patterns across its exposed surface.”

Here ḥubuk refers to the rippling patterns that wind creates on water.

The poet also uses تَنْسُجُهُ (tansujuhu): the wind seems to weave the water’s surface.

This image matters because the weaving is not literally the work of a weaver. The poet likens the wind’s movement and the formation of ripples to weaving.

Within this image, ḥubuk expresses the order and extension of the patterns appearing on the water.

This is an important early witness to the word’s meaning: the verse is transmitted as Zuhayr’s and describes water outside the Qur’anic setting. Its attribution and textual transmission should nevertheless be distinguished from independent proof of the exact date of this particular line.

The line has textual variants. Some sources give بِعَمِيمِ النَّبْتِ (bi-ʿamīmi al-nabti) instead of بِأُصُولِ النَّجْمِ (bi-uṣūli al-najmi); the wording concerning the wind also varies in some transmissions.

Lexicons and commentaries transmit the wording بِأُصُولِ النَّجْمِ and رِيحٌ خَرِيقٌ (rīḥun kharīqun) in a description of water. These variants do not change the basic sense of ḥubuk as patterns on its surface.

Ibn ʿAṭiyyah, Al-Muḥarrar al-Wajīz: Zuhayr’s witness and textual variants

Ḥubuk in sand

In sandy terrain, wind can arrange the surface into ordered rows and ridges.

These patterns may run alongside one another or appear curved and rippled.

Lexicons use ḥabīkah for one such extension and ḥubuk for their plurality.

The image is close to patterns on water.

In both cases, wind gives a previously more uniform surface visible patterns and extensions.

There is also a real difference: water ripples move and continually change, whereas patterns in sand may remain for a time after the wind subsides.

Their lexical common ground is therefore the shape and order of the patterns, rather than identical physical structures in water and sand.

Cloth and its patterns

With cloth, ḥabk concerns weaving well and firmly.

كَسَاءٌ مُحَبَّكٌ (kasāʾun muḥabbakun) is recorded for a cloth bearing patterns or ordered stripes.

This use brings two features together: sound construction and visible order in the weave.

A cloth may be structurally strong and also beautifully patterned.

These features should not be equated in every use of the root. Maḥbūk can emphasize the quality and firmness of construction even when no particular visible pattern is intended.

Nor is every ordered pattern produced by weaving cloth, as the examples of water and sand show.

Mail and its linked rings

Early Arabic also records ḥubuk in connection with mail made from iron rings.

The rings of mail form a continuous construction. Their interconnection can produce regular visible patterns.

Both the joining of parts and their ordered configuration matter here.

Mail and cloth nevertheless have different structures: one consists of metal rings, the other of threads and strands.

Their shared relevance to this root lies in ordered, interconnected parts, rather than material or an identical method of manufacture.

Curly hair and ordered curves

Lexicons also record ḥubuk for curly hair.

Curly hair can form strands and curves resembling continuous rows.

This usage likewise concerns pattern and extension.

No literal weaving has occurred, but the hair’s curves create an image comparable to patterns in cloth, water, or sand.

The example shows that ḥubuk need not mean knotting or fastening.

Something can have ordered patterns and extensions without actually being knotted.

Sound construction of bodies and objects

Lexicons also use maḥbūk for a horse or other animal whose bodily construction is firm, proportionate, and strong.

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, بَعِيرٌ مَحْبُوكُ الْقَرَى (baʿīrun maḥbūku al-qarā) describes a camel with a strong, firm back.

Arabic poetry likewise uses maḥbūk to describe a horse’s body.

This differs from patterns on water: strength, proportion, and cohesion of construction are more prominent than visible lines.

Forms from this family are also recorded for work performed well and firmly.

Ḥ B K is therefore not confined to outward shapes; quality and soundness of construction form another important branch.

3. Qur’anic usage

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:7: the sky possessing ordered patterns and paths

Verse text:

وَالسَّمَاءِ ذَاتِ الْحُبُكِ

wa-al-samāʾi dhāti al-ḥubuki

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“By the sky possessing ordered patterns and paths.”

This is the root’s only occurrence in the Qur’an.

الْحُبُكِ is a broken plural in the genitive, following ذَاتِ (dhāti).

Dhāti means possessing or having something; here it qualifies السَّمَاءِ (al-samāʾi), the sky.

The verse thus describes the sky as possessing ḥubuk.

Grammatically, وَالسَّمَاءِ (wa-al-samāʾi) introduces an oath, while ذَاتِ الْحُبُكِ states the sky’s attribute.

The construction shows that ḥubuk is an attribute of the sky. It does not itself explain precisely which physical phenomena constitute these patterns or extensions.

The word’s meaning must be established from known Arabic usage.

Ḥubuk: ordered patterns and extensions

Early Arabic records ḥubuk for patterns on water and sand, extensions in hair, the weave of cloth, and the arrangement of mail rings.

In all these uses, interrelated shapes or extensions form a recognizable order.

“Ordered patterns and paths” therefore has clear lexical support as an interpretation of ḥubuk.

Applied to the sky, the word attributes such a characteristic to it.

Examples involving water and sand should not be taken to mean that the sky ripples exactly like them, or that its construction is identical to cloth or mail.

These examples establish the word’s semantic range rather than identical physical structures across different phenomena.

A sky of firm and sound construction

Alongside ordered patterns and paths, some lexicographers and commentators explain ذَاتِ الْحُبُكِ through the sky’s sound and firm construction.

This reading relates to ḥabaka al-thawba and maḥbūk.

Good, firm weaving is desirable in cloth. In describing a horse’s body, maḥbūk can likewise express proportionate, sound bodily construction.

On this basis, ḥubuk as an attribute of the sky can also suggest ordered, firm construction.

The two explanations need not conflict.

Ordered patterns and extensions may manifest orderly construction, and sound construction may possess an order visible in its patterns or extensions.

Translation nevertheless requires distinguishing lexical meaning from interpretive conclusions.

For this study’s proposed translation, “possessing ordered patterns and paths” is preferred. “Possessing sound and firm construction” emphasizes another attested aspect of the lexical range and an early interpretation of the verse; it should not be treated as linguistically baseless.

Al-Ālūsī, Rūḥ al-Maʿānī: paths and sound, well-ordered construction

Does ḥubuk refer to the paths of stars?

Lexicons and commentaries sometimes explain حُبُك السَّمَاءِ (ḥubuk al-samāʾ) as طَرَائِقُ النُّجُومِ (ṭarāʾiqu al-nujūmi): the paths or courses of stars.

This explanation relates to the meaning ṭarāʾiq, but should not turn the word into a technical name for stellar orbits.

The Qur’anic expression does not contain نُجُوم (nujūm), “stars.”

Al-ḥubuk alone therefore does not directly establish that only celestial orbits are intended.

The orderly movement of celestial bodies may be considered one possible application of this image. Establishing that the verse specifically concerns astronomical orbits, however, requires further Qur’anic and contextual evidence.

Nor must “path” here mean a physical track inscribed in the sky.

Just as patterns and extensions are described on water and sand, order and extensions consistent with the image of ḥubuk can be considered in describing the sky.

The oath and the following verse

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:7 opens with an oath by the sky.

The next verse, Al-Dhāriyāt 51:8, says:

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:8

إِنَّكُمْ لَفِي قَوْلٍ مُخْتَلِفٍ

innakum la-fī qawlin mukhtalifin

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Indeed, you are engaged in conflicting speech.”

This sentence is the response to the oath.

The first verse describes the sky through ḥubuk; the second reports the conflicting speech of those addressed.

Their juxtaposition can suggest a contrast between celestial order and the diversity of human assertions.

That contrast alone, however, does not determine the lexical meaning of ḥubuk.

The meaning must be established from Arabic usage and the verse’s construction; the rhetorical relationship between the two verses can then be considered.

Nor should this oath be taken as a complete theory of the universe’s physical structure. The expression directly characterizes the sky through ḥubuk within an oath.

Can ḥubuk relate to known cosmic structures?

Modern inquiry examines phenomena such as the courses of celestial bodies, large cosmic structures, filaments and concentrations of matter, and various natural patterns.

Some of these may visually resemble ordered patterns, extensions, or networks.

Such resemblance can provide a basis for contemporary reflection on the verse.

Resemblance in imagery nevertheless differs from proof of the verse’s definitive meaning.

The use of ḥubuk for water, sand, or cloth does not directly establish that the Qur’an specifically describes the cosmic web, dark-matter filaments, galactic orbits, or a particular structure in modern cosmology.

None of these scientific names or mechanisms appears in the verse’s wording.

Conversely, reflection on known natural structures need not be rejected merely because the Qur’an’s earliest audience could not observe them using modern scientific instruments.

The necessary boundary is to preserve the established lexical meaning and present any scientific application supported by adequate evidence as a possibility or reflection.

A defensible interpretation here concerns order, patterns, and extensions associated with the sky’s construction. The word alone does not identify an exclusive physical referent.

4. Comparison with related roots

Ḥ B K (ḥabaka) and N S J (nasaja): weaving and firmness of weave

Arabic نَسَجَ (nasaja) concerns weaving and interlacing strands.

Ḥabaka can likewise describe weaving cloth, while emphasizing the weave’s quality, order, and firmness.

In Zuhayr’s line, the wind is described with تَنْسُجُهُ (tansujuhu), while the resulting water patterns are called ḥubuk.

The witness clearly illustrates the proximity of these word families.

Nasj foregrounds weaving and joining parts; ḥabk, in its textile use, foregrounds the quality and firmness of that work.

This tendency should not become an absolute rule that every nasj is weak and every ḥabk strong.

Both families can describe good, firm weaving.

Their lexical connection also does not establish that the sky in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:7 must be woven from material strands like cloth.

Ḥ B K (ḥabaka) and Ḥ W K (ḥāka): good weaving and the act of weaving

حَاكَ (ḥāka) and the Ḥ W K family concern weaving.

حَائِك (ḥāʾik) is the Arabic word for a weaver.

Ḥabaka also concerns weaving in textile contexts, although many witnesses give greater prominence to the resulting firmness and order.

The roots are close in usage but distinct.

In early Arabic, a weaver may weave cloth whose quality is then described through Ḥ B K.

The bāʾ in Ḥ B K and wāw in Ḥ W K also identify them as two morphologically distinct roots.

Semantic proximity should not be taken as proof of historical kinship or an otherwise unestablished sound change between them.

Ḥ B K (ḥubuk) and Ṭ R Q (ṭarīq): patterns and paths

Study of Ṭ R Q

Arabic طَرِيق (ṭarīq) concerns a road or path.

Some uses of ḥubuk are likewise explained through ṭarāʾiq.

In descriptions of water, sand, or hair, however, ṭarāʾiq need not denote routes for travel. It can describe patterns and extensions running across a surface like paths.

Ḥubuk and ṭarāʾiq are therefore close in this setting without sharing their entire semantic ranges.

A road on land may enable human passage, whereas a rippled pattern on water does not serve that function.

In Al-Dhāriyāt 51:7, “paths” without explanation may suggest travel routes too narrowly.

“Ordered patterns and paths” better preserves this breadth.

Ḥ B K (ḥabk) and Ḥ B L (ḥabl): firm connection and rope

Arabic حَبْل (ḥabl) denotes a rope or means of fastening and connection.

Some uses of Ḥ B K also concern tying firmly or securing a connection.

The two families thus approach one another through the imagery of connection and firmness.

Ḥabl, however, names a rope or connecting means, whereas ḥabk concerns the act and quality of making firm, or patterns resulting from ordered construction.

Ḥubuk in the verse therefore does not establish that the sky possesses literal ropes.

If ropes or strands are used to explain natural structures, they must be identified as analogy or application rather than direct translations of ḥubuk.

Ḥ B K (ḥabk) and R B Ṭ (rabaṭa): securing and tying

Arabic رَبَطَ (rabaṭa) concerns tying and joining.

Figuratively, it can also mean making firm or keeping steady.

Ḥabk relates to this field but also concerns ordered weaving and extended patterns in its attested uses.

The roots can therefore overlap in making firm without being synonymous in all their applications.

The imagery of patterns and ordered construction matters for understanding Qur’anic al-ḥubuk; “tying” alone does not convey its full semantic range.

Ḥ B K (ḥabk) and S B K (sabk): firm weaving and casting

Arabic سَبَكَ (sabaka) concerns melting and pouring material, especially metal, into a mold to make a coherent object.

In its familiar use, ḥabaka concerns weaving and joining parts.

Quality of construction may matter in both cases, but the methods differ.

In sabk, material can acquire a coherent shape through melting and casting; in ḥabk, the joining and arrangement of parts, or their resulting patterns, are prominent.

This distinction helps prevent confusion between two roots that are close in writing.

5. Interpretive challenges and semantic development

Does ḥubuk mean astronomical orbits?

Some commentaries explain ḥubuk as the paths of stars.

This is one possible interpretation of paths and extensions.

Ḥubuk itself, however, is not a technical name for an orbit.

If an orbit means a celestial body’s course, that scientific definition should not be treated as inherent in the word.

Other Qur’anic verses discuss the movement of celestial bodies, but their existence alone does not establish that al-ḥubuk in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:7 specifically names those courses.

Astronomical orbits may therefore be considered a possible application, while preserving the word’s general, attested meaning.

Does ḥubuk mean breaks or cracks in the sky?

Al-Farrāʾ explains ḥubuk through angular or curving configurations visible in sand, water, or hair.

تَكَسُّر (takassur) in this use does not mean physical breakage or destruction.

It concerns lines, folds, and curves that give an otherwise uniform surface varied patterns.

This lexical explanation therefore does not establish that Al-Dhāriyāt 51:7 describes the sky cracking, splitting, or suffering damage.

The Qur’an uses different words and roots for the sky’s splitting in other verses.

Here ḥubuk belongs to the field of patterns and orderly construction, rather than destruction and collapse.

Does ḥubuk mean a woven network?

In contemporary Persian, “network” can describe interconnected links, strands, or paths.

Because Ḥ B K concerns weaving and ordered parts, network imagery can help explain some of its uses.

In modern scientific language, however, “network” may suggest a specific structure with nodes, connections, and defined geometrical relationships.

Ḥubuk itself has no such technical definition.

Translating the verse as “By the sky possessing the cosmic web” or “the sky woven from strands of matter” therefore gives it a much more specific meaning than its Arabic wording establishes.

Any such reading should be examined as a possible reflection supported by appropriate evidence, rather than used in the direct translation.

Does ḥubuk refer only to beauty?

Some early explanations relate ḥubuk to sound and beautiful construction.

This meaning connects with the root’s use for well-woven cloth and firm construction.

Translating it solely as “beauty,” however, can obscure an important part of its semantic field: patterns, extensions, and ordered construction.

“Beautiful” is a broad description and may apply to something without any particular pattern or ordered structure.

By contrast, ḥubuk in water, sand, and hair describes recognizable configurations and patterns.

For this study’s translation, patterns and ordered paths are therefore preferred, with beauty and sound construction discussed in the lexical explanation. This does not invalidate the attested early interpretation through good and sound creation.

Iḥtibāk in rhetoric

In Arabic rhetoric, اِحْتِبَاك (iḥtibāk) describes reciprocal omission: something is omitted from each of two expressions while its counterpart appears in the other, so that each supplies evidence for reconstructing what the other leaves unstated.

Its imagery can be associated with weaving and joining the parts of discourse.

The technical rhetorical term iḥtibāk is nevertheless distinct from the direct meaning of ḥubuk in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:7.

The term’s existence in later disciplines does not establish that the verse concerns omitted or implied sentence elements, or a particular literary device.

Later technical usage should be distinguished from lexical meaning and Qur’anic usage.

Al-Suyūṭī, Al-Itqān: reciprocal omission in iḥtibāk

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

For وَالسَّمَاءِ ذَاتِ الْحُبُكِ in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:7, this study proposes the following direct rendering:

“By the sky possessing ordered patterns and paths.”

This translation has several features.

First, it preserves dhāti as “possessing,” attributing the characteristic to the sky itself.

Second, it does not restrict ḥubuk to “paths,” since early uses are also recorded for patterns and extensions on water, sand, cloth, and hair.

Third, it preserves order and interrelation among patterns without inserting a particular physical structure, such as astronomical orbits or cosmic filaments.

“By the sky possessing ordered paths” is also defensible, although it conveys less of the patterned or woven aspect.

“By the sky possessing firm construction” emphasizes soundness and quality of construction, but does not fully convey the attested sense of ḥubuk as patterns and extensions.

“By the sky possessing beautiful patterns” is another possibility, though it conveys firmness and extension less clearly than “ordered patterns and paths.”

Forqan’s word box should retain two separate fields:

Brief root meaning: “Weaving and making firm; ordered patterns and extensions.”

Meaning in this verse: “Ordered patterns and paths associated with the sky’s construction.”

A reflection on the verse can explain the early Arabic uses for patterns on water and sand, curves in hair, the arrangement of cloth, and firmness of construction.

All these explanations need not be placed within the direct translation of a short verse.

7. Conclusion

In Arabic, Ḥ B K concerns sound construction, the ordered connection of parts, and the patterns and extensions visible in such construction or arrangement.

Ḥabaka al-thawba means weaving cloth well and firmly. Maḥbūk can describe something whose parts are firmly and orderly constructed. Ḥubuk is also recorded for patterns and ordered paths in water, sand, hair, mail, and other arrangements.

Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā’s line about patterns formed by wind on water is an important early Arabic witness to the meaning of ḥubuk.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an: وَالسَّمَاءِ ذَاتِ الْحُبُكِ in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:7.

The verse attributes ḥubuk to the sky. Ordered patterns and paths have support in attested Arabic usage; soundness and beauty of construction also connect with another branch of this word family.

The verse nevertheless names no specific physical structure. It should therefore not be restricted without sufficient evidence to astronomical orbits, celestial waves, the cosmic web, or strands of matter.

These phenomena may be examined in separate study or reflection, but they do not themselves establish the lexical meaning of ḥubuk.

This study favors understanding ḥubuk in Al-Dhāriyāt as highlighting ordered patterns and extensions in the sky, connected with the family’s senses of sound and good construction. This is a preferred interpretation, while early sources also attest the explanation through good and sound creation.

“By the sky possessing ordered patterns and paths” thus preserves an attested meaning without restricting it to an unproven scientific identification or material image.
