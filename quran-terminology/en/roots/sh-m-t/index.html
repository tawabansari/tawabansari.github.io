Rejoicing at another’s misfortune, an enemy’s failure, and giving others cause to gloat

In its principal early Arabic use, SH M T concerns joy or satisfaction at trouble, defeat or calamity befalling another, particularly an enemy or rival. Shamātah names this condition; a shāmit takes pleasure in another’s misfortune or failure.

This differs from ordinary happiness. What pleases the person is another’s suffering, defeat or adverse situation. The pleasure may appear in words, laughter or humiliation, or remain inward. An outward display is therefore not always necessary to the meaning.

Arabic also distinguishes shamita bihi from ashmata bihi ʿaduwwahu. The first concerns someone rejoicing at another’s misfortune; the second concerns causing an enemy to rejoice at a person’s plight.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as the verb tushmit in Al-Aʿrāf 7:150. Aaron asks Moses not to make the enemies rejoice at his situation. Understanding this requires attention to the root, the verbal construction and the circumstances between the brothers.

1. The root in early Arabic

Joy at an enemy’s calamity or failure

Shamita bi-fulānin means taking pleasure in another person’s calamity or failure.

Shamātah and shamāt name this satisfaction. Early lexical definitions frequently concern a calamity befalling an enemy, giving hostility and rivalry a prominent place in the word’s usage.

For example, if two people are enemies and one falls ill or suffers defeat, the other may delight in the suffering or humiliation itself, rather than in the ending of injustice. That can be called shamātah.

It should therefore not be equated with every joy at victory or the removal of danger. Someone may welcome the end of oppression or people’s rescue without enjoying another person’s pain. In shamātah, the other’s misfortune itself becomes the object of pleasure.

A witness attributed to al-Shanfarā

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Shanfarā contains:

وَبَاضِعَةٍ حُمْرِ الْقِسِيِّ بَعَثْتُهَا

wa-bāḍiʿatin ḥumri al-qisiyyi baʿathtuhā

وَمَنْ يَغْزُ يَغْنَمْ مَرَّةً وَيُشَمَّتِ

wa-man yaghzu yaghnam marratan wa-yushammat

Translation:

“I sent out a band of raiders with red bows; one who raids sometimes gains booty and sometimes comes away disappointed.”

The verse concerns the risks and uncertain outcome of raiding. Someone who sets out seeking booty does not always return victorious: sometimes the objective is achieved, and sometimes the expedition fails.

Failure of this kind may give opponents cause to rejoice. Here, however, the lexical explanation of the verb emphasizes returning unsuccessful; actual gloating by an enemy is not directly reported in the line.

The transmitted form and explanations of the verb vary, and some emphasize failure. This verse alone should not be used to reduce every SH M T usage either to an enemy’s pleasure or to failure itself.

Although attributed to al-Shanfarā, conventionally classified as pre-Islamic, the precise date of this line has not been independently established here. It illuminates the early semantic field without proving the historical order of its meanings.

Failure and returning empty-handed

Classical Arabic also records forms such as shamātā for people who return unsuccessful from an undertaking, especially war or an expedition for booty.

This belongs to the field of defeat and failure, but must be distinguished semantically from an enemy’s rejoicing.

The unsuccessful person has failed to achieve a goal; the person who gloats takes pleasure in someone else’s failure.

Both can occur in one event: one person is defeated and an enemy rejoices. Yet being defeated and gloating are different.

Their coexistence does not establish, without historical evidence, that either meaning necessarily originated in the other.

Other Arabic uses

Shammata al-ʿāṭisa means offering a good wish or prayer for someone who has sneezed.

Tashmīt here is a prayer for the person’s well-being and mercy. Some lexical explanations connect it with keeping misfortune and gloating away: wishing someone well so that they do not fall into a condition that delights others.

This may explain a semantic relationship, but does not prove the word’s historical origin.

Tasmīt, with sīn, is also attested. Similar usage does not justify treating the entire root families SH M T and S M T as identical.

Some lexical accounts also record al-shawāmit for an animal’s legs. It occurs in a verse attributed to al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī, but the reading and interpretation of that passage are disputed. It cannot establish the definite origin of shamātah.

These uses matter to the lexical family’s history, but none should be imported into the Qur’anic occurrence without evidence.

2. Central meaning and Qur’anic forms

The Qur’an uses SH M T only in tushmit.

This is from ashmata, yushmitu, Form IV, addressed to a singular addressee in the imperfect after prohibitive lā.

The basic verb shamita, the nouns shamātah and shamāt, the active participle shāmit and the form tashmīt are known in Arabic but absent from the Qur’anic text.

Two constructions must be distinguished:

Shamita bihi: He rejoiced at that person’s calamity or failure.

Ashmata bihi ʿaduwwahu: He made the person’s enemy rejoice at their calamity or failure, or gave that enemy cause to gloat.

The Qur’an uses the second construction.

The principal meaning in the gloating branch is pleasure at another’s misfortune. Form IV can express causing that pleasure in someone else or giving them occasion for it.

Aaron is not telling Moses that Moses delights in his suffering. He asks Moses not to act in a way that gives the enemies pleasure at his situation.

3. Shamātah in Moses and Aaron’s exchange

The root’s sole Qur’anic occurrence is Al-Aʿrāf 7:150:

وَلَمَّا رَجَعَ مُوسَىٰ إِلَىٰ قَوْمِهِ غَضْبَانَ أَسِفًا قَالَ بِئْسَمَا خَلَفْتُمُونِي مِنْ بَعْدِي أَعَجِلْتُمْ أَمْرَ رَبِّكُمْ وَأَلْقَى الْأَلْوَاحَ وَأَخَذَ بِرَأْسِ أَخِيهِ يَجُرُّهُ إِلَيْهِ قَالَ ابْنَ أُمَّ إِنَّ الْقَوْمَ اسْتَضْعَفُونِي وَكَادُوا يَقْتُلُونَنِي فَلَا تُشْمِتْ بِيَ الْأَعْدَاءَ وَلَا تَجْعَلْنِي مَعَ الْقَوْمِ الظَّالِمِينَ

wa-lammā rajaʿa mūsā ilā qawmihi ghaḍbāna asifan qāla biʾsamā khalaftumūnī min baʿdī a-ʿajiltum amra rabbikum wa-alqā al-alwāḥa wa-akhadha bi-raʾsi akhīhi yajurruhu ilayhi qāla ibna umma inna al-qawma istaḍʿafūnī wa-kādū yaqtulūnanī fa-lā tushmit biya al-aʿdāʾa wa-lā tajʿalnī maʿa al-qawmi al-ẓālimīn

Al-Aʿrāf 7:150

Translation:

“When Moses returned to his people, angry and sorrowful, he said: How badly you succeeded me in my absence! Did you hasten your Lord’s command? He cast down the tablets and seized his brother’s head, pulling him toward himself. He said: Son of my mother, the people deemed me weak and nearly killed me; do not make the enemies rejoice at my plight, and do not place me with the wrongdoing people.”

The setting

Returning to his people, Moses encounters a situation of deviation: some Israelites had turned to the calf in his absence.

He responds with anger and sorrow, casts down the tablets and seizes his brother Aaron’s head, pulling him toward himself.

Aaron explains that the people had deemed him weak and nearly killed him.

He then says:

فَلَا تُشْمِتْ بِيَ الْأَعْدَاءَ

fa-lā tushmit biya al-aʿdāʾ

Al-Aʿrāf 7:150

Translation:

“Do not make the enemies rejoice at my plight.”

Aaron is concerned about the enemies’ interpretation of, and response to, Moses’ treatment of him.

The grammar of fa-lā tushmit biya al-aʿdāʾ

Fa links the sentence to the preceding explanation.

Lā is prohibitive, and tushmit is a jussive imperfect in Form IV, addressed to a masculine singular addressee: Moses.

Biya refers to Aaron as the person whose condition may become the object of gloating.

Al-aʿdāʾ is the verb’s object: the people who may be made to rejoice at that condition.

Moses is asked not to create the occasion for gloating; Aaron is the person whose plight may delight the enemies; and the enemies are the potential rejoicers.

This differs from lā tashmat bī. With the basic verb directed at the person who gloats, it could mean “Do not rejoice at my plight yourself.” In the site’s base reading, Ḥafṣ, the verse concerns making the enemies rejoice.

How does an occasion for gloating arise?

Aaron has already faced opposition and threats from the people. The verse narrates Moses seizing his brother’s head and then Aaron’s explanation. This order of narration does not establish every detail of conversations that may have occurred before it.

In this situation, Moses’ conduct could please Aaron’s enemies. They might enjoy the confrontation or interpret it as supporting their own position.

Aaron’s request therefore concerns more than personal suffering: it also concerns how conduct may affect opponents’ attitudes. The verse does not separately enumerate those present at the encounter.

The verse does not say that the enemies actually gloated. Aaron asks Moses not to give them cause to do so.

The distinction between a possible response and an established occurrence must be retained.

Was Aaron complicit in the people’s deviation?

Aaron continues:

وَلَا تَجْعَلْنِي مَعَ الْقَوْمِ الظَّالِمِينَ

wa-lā tajʿalnī maʿa al-qawmi al-ẓālimīn

Al-Aʿrāf 7:150

Translation:

“And do not place me with the wrongdoing people.”

This is related to the previous request, but does not repeat it.

The first concerns the enemies’ gloating; the second asks Moses not to count Aaron among the wrongdoers.

Passages in Ṭā Hā help explain this request.

Ṭā Hā 20:90 explicitly states that Aaron had already told the people they were being tested through the calf and should follow him and obey his command.

Ṭā Hā 20:91 gives their response: they would not cease their devotion to the calf until Moses returned.

In Ṭā Hā 20:94, Aaron tells Moses he feared dividing the Israelites and being accused of disregarding his instruction.

These verses show that Aaron had not invited the people to worship the calf: he opposed it and encountered resistance.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:150 likewise reports that he was treated as weak and threatened with death.

His failure to stop the people cannot therefore be turned into complicity without examining the circumstances.

Nor should al-aʿdāʾ and al-qawmi al-ẓālimīn be assumed, without evidence, to be wholly separate groups or exactly identical. Their referents may overlap, while each designation has a distinct role in the sentence.

Moses’ response after Aaron’s explanation

After the brothers’ exchange, Al-Aʿrāf 7:151 reports Moses saying:

قَالَ رَبِّ اغْفِرْ لِي وَلِأَخِي وَأَدْخِلْنَا فِي رَحْمَتِكَ وَأَنْتَ أَرْحَمُ الرَّاحِمِينَ

qāla rabbi ighfir lī wa-li-akhī wa-adkhilnā fī raḥmatika wa-anta arḥamu al-rāḥimīn

Al-Aʿrāf 7:151

Translation:

“He said: My Lord, forgive me and my brother, and admit us into Your mercy; You are the most merciful of those who show mercy.”

Moses places himself and his brother together in this prayer, following his earlier forceful conduct and Aaron’s explanation.

The verse expressly asks forgiveness and mercy for both, but does not describe all their inner emotions or every detail of reconciliation.

For SH M T, the key is that Aaron’s request occurs amid disagreement, threats, being treated as weak and his brother’s forceful conduct. The setting shows how one person’s action can give another person’s opponents cause to gloat.

4. Shamātah and related concepts

SH M T (shamātah) and F R Ḥ (faraḥ)

Faraḥ concerns joy and satisfaction. In the Qur’an, its value depends on the object and context of the joy.

Shamātah in its principal use is a particular kind of joy: delight at another’s calamity, failure or defeat.

Every instance of shamātah is thus a kind of pleasure, but not every pleasure is shamātah.

Enjoying a blessing, rescue from danger or the end of oppression is not gloating merely because it involves joy. The decisive issue is what causes the pleasure.

SH M T (shamātah) and S KH R (mockery)

In its mocking sense, S KH R concerns ridicule and belittling others through speech or conduct.

Shamātah concerns pleasure at another’s misfortune.

Someone may rejoice at an enemy’s defeat and also ridicule them. Gloating and mockery then occur together.

Gloating can nevertheless remain inward without mocking words. Someone may also ridicule another without any new misfortune having befallen them.

The roots are related but not identical in meaning.

SH M T (shamātah) and SH T M (insult)

SH T M concerns insults and abusive speech. Its consonant order differs from SH M T; similarity in sound does not make them one root.

An insult is a linguistic act; shamātah is pleasure at another’s plight.

A person who gloats may also insult, but insulting is not required for gloating.

SH M T (shamātah) and Ḥ S D (envy)

Ḥasad concerns an adverse attitude toward a blessing or advantage another possesses.

Shamātah concerns delight at another’s misfortune or failure.

An envious person may rejoice when another loses a blessing, bringing envy and gloating together.

Not every instance of envy proves actual gloating, and not all gloating necessarily arises from envy. Hostility, rivalry, resentment and other motives can play a role.

SH M T (shamātah) and GH Ḍ B (anger)

Ghaḍab concerns anger; shamātah concerns pleasure at another’s plight.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:150, Moses is angry and Aaron asks him not to make the enemies gloat.

The states belong to different parties: Moses’ anger at the people’s deviation, and the enemies’ possible pleasure at Aaron’s situation.

Anger is not itself gloating, though conduct driven by anger can give others cause to gloat.

5. Interpretive issues and social application

A central distinction is between gloating and giving others cause to gloat.

In the first case, someone personally enjoys another’s misfortune. In the second, someone may act in a way that pleases others at a person’s plight without personally sharing that pleasure.

This distinction is also observable in social life.

For example, someone may severely humiliate a colleague before that colleague’s opponents. Their own intention may not be to gloat, yet their conduct may delight the opponents and let them enjoy the humiliation.

Actual or possible gloating does not by itself prove that public criticism or holding a wrongdoer accountable is improper. Exposing wrongdoing, defending rights and administering justice may be necessary. Preventing opponents’ pleasure should not become an excuse to conceal truth or suspend accountability.

In Moses and Aaron’s story, the issue is specific: Aaron says he was overpowered by opposition and even threatened with death; Moses’ forceful response may now humiliate him in his enemies’ eyes.

A social implication may therefore be found in attention to how one’s conduct affects another’s situation, especially when that person is already under pressure or threat.

This application must remain distinct from lexical meaning. SH M T does not itself mean protecting reputation, preventing family disagreement or pursuing political expediency. Such concerns emerge from the setting and the consequences of conduct.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Persian shamātat is a familiar equivalent, but it can also suggest reproach. Its meaning here should be made explicit as “rejoicing at another’s trouble or defeat.” In English, “gloating” is suitable when this particular kind of pleasure is understood.

Shāmit can be translated as “one who gloats” or “someone who delights in another’s misfortune.”

For fa-lā tushmit biya al-aʿdāʾ, the causative meaning of Form IV must be preserved.

Suitable renderings include:

“Do not make the enemies rejoice at my plight.”

Or:

“Do not give the enemies cause to gloat over me.”

The first is more immediately explicit for a general audience; the second foregrounds the connection with gloating.

“Do not gloat over me” is inaccurate for this construction: Moses is not presented as personally enjoying Aaron’s suffering. Aaron asks him not to make the enemies take such pleasure.

“Do not make the enemies happy” conveys part of the result but does not adequately specify pleasure at someone’s misfortune.

For Forqan, the proposed rendering is:

“Do not make the enemies rejoice at my plight.”

Reflection may explain that Aaron fears the enemies’ pleasure at Moses’ treatment of him, rather than accusing Moses of gloating. These proposals do not change the installed verse translations.

Conclusion

SH M T principally concerns satisfaction at another’s calamity, defeat or failure, especially an enemy’s. Arabic distinguishes the person who gloats from someone whose conduct gives others cause to do so.

Early witnesses connect the family with failure, defeat and opponents’ pleasure. Other uses, including returning unsuccessful and offering a good wish after a sneeze, are attested; they should not all be reduced without evidence to one historical meaning.

The root occurs only in Al-Aʿrāf 7:150, as tushmit.

After describing pressure and threats from the people, Aaron asks Moses not to make the enemies rejoice at his plight and not to place him among the wrongdoers.

This shows that gloating concerns not only an enemy’s inward pleasure: others’ conduct can give occasion for it. Shamātah in this Qur’anic use does not itself mean humiliation, insult, mockery or defeat. These are related concepts or possible circumstances requiring separate examination.

The principal sense can therefore be defined as follows:

SH M T denotes joy or satisfaction at another’s calamity, trouble or failure. In causative ashmata, it denotes making someone else rejoice at such a plight or giving them cause to gloat.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:150 uses this second meaning: Aaron asks Moses not to make the enemies rejoice at his situation through his conduct.
