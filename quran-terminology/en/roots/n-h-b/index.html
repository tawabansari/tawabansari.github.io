An undertaking assumed: from keeping a pledge to life’s end, and the separate branch of wailing and weeping

1. Introduction and semantic core

Arabic N Ḥ B has two established semantic branches. The first concerns vows, undertakings, assuming an obligation, and fulfilling it. Related uses concern accepting danger, strenuous effort, an appointed time, and the end of life.

The second concerns sound, particularly audible weeping and wailing, and an animal’s cough.

In the first branch, naḥb denotes a vow or undertaking assumed by a person. In qaḍā naḥbahu, it accompanies qaḍā and can convey fulfilling an undertaking or reaching life’s end.

The expression is also used for death. This does not make naḥb mean death in every occurrence, or imply that everyone who has “completed his naḥb” was necessarily killed.

Distinguishing a word’s independent meaning from a phrase’s meaning is essential. Just as completing a pledge concerns fulfilling an undertaking, completing a naḥb can concern discharging an assumed obligation. In another use, the same phrase expresses a person’s life ending.

Naḥīb, by contrast, denotes loud, audible weeping. Its shared consonants with the undertaking sense do not justify making weeping the primary meaning of a vow or reducing every branch to one emotional image.

Qur’anic forms

N Ḥ B occurs only once in the Qur’an: naḥbahu in Al-Aḥzāb 33:23.

This consists of the noun naḥb and the attached pronoun -hu.

In qaḍā naḥbahu, naḥba is the accusative object of qaḍā. The pronoun -hu refers to the person whose undertaking or naḥb is being discussed.

Other Arabic forms include naḥaba for making a vow or assuming an obligation; naḥḥaba for obligating oneself or exerting serious effort; tanāḥaba and nāḥaba for making arrangements, taking risks, or submitting a dispute for judgment; and naḥīb and intaḥaba for audible weeping.

None of those other verbs or derivatives occurs in the Qur’an; the sole form is naḥbahu.

The Qur’anic focus is therefore naḥb in qaḍā naḥbahu. Other Arabic uses illuminate the semantic field without becoming Qur’anic forms themselves.

2. Early Arabic usage and semantic branches

Naḥb: a vow or undertaking assumed

One established Arabic meaning of naḥb is a vow.

The person makes an action binding upon himself and accepts an obligation concerning it.

Naḥaba naḥban is recorded for making a vow, and qaḍā naḥbahu for fulfilling what one has undertaken.

A vow here is more than a wish or hope. Wishing to perform something differs from taking responsibility for performing it.

Naḥb in this use therefore involves commitment and responsibility.

Later technical jurisprudential definitions of a vow should not be imposed on every early use. An undertaking could concern an action, accepting danger, participating in confrontation, or keeping a promise one had assumed.

A poetic witness is transmitted without a securely identified poet:

فَإِنِّي وَالْهِجَاءَ لِآلِ لَأْمٍ
كَذَاتِ النَّحْبِ تُوفِي بِالنُّذُورِ

fa-innī wa-al-hijāʾa li-āli laʾmin / ka-dhāti al-naḥbi tūfī bi-al-nudhūri

Translation:

“In satirizing the family of Laʾm, I am like a woman bound by a vow who fulfils her vows.”

Naḥb clearly relates to a vow and its fulfilment here. The speaker compares his commitment to an action with someone discharging a vow.

The witness is recorded in lexical transmission, but its attribution and composition date are uncertain. It should not be called an unqualified pre-Islamic witness.

It nevertheless illustrates an established sense in Arabic linguistic tradition: naḥb can be something a person undertakes and then fulfils.

Naḥb: commitment, risk, and strenuous effort

Arabic also associates naḥb with taking risks and accepting danger.

Nāḥabahu ʿalā al-amri is recorded for making an arrangement or staking something against another person.

Tanāḥaba al-qawmu can concern a group agreeing to confront one another at an appointed time.

A person or group then commits to an undertaking and accepts an outcome potentially involving danger or loss.

In this field, sāra ʿalā naḥbin describes travelling with strenuous effort and determination, as though committed to reaching a destination.

Jarīr, a poet of the Islamic period, says:

بِطَخْفَةَ جَالَدْنَا الْمُلُوكَ وَخَيْلُنَا
عَشِيَّةَ بِسْطَامٍ جَرَيْنَ عَلَى نَحْبِ

bi-ṭakhfata jāladnā al-mulūka wa-khaylunā / ʿashiyyata bisṭāmin jarayna ʿalā naḥbi

Translation:

“At Ṭakhfah we fought the kings, and our horses, on Bisṭām’s evening, raced into great danger.”

ʿAlā naḥbin here concerns the great danger faced by horses and warriors.

This does not make naḥb invariably mean warfare or mortal danger. Risk is one use, and its object can arise in other settings.

The verse also belongs to the Islamic period and is not a direct witness from poetry predating the Qur’an.

Naḥb: an appointed term, life’s end, and death

Qaḍā naḥbahu is also recorded in Arabic as an expression for a person’s death.

Naḥb then relates to an appointed term, lifespan, or its conclusion.

Alongside a vow, Arabic records death, an appointed term, and duration among the uses of naḥb. Construction and context distinguish these meanings.

Dhū al-Rummah, a poet of the Islamic period, says:

عَشِيَّةَ فَرَّ الْحَارِثِيُّونَ بَعْدَمَا
قَضَىٰ نَحْبَهُ فِي مُلْتَقَى الْقَوْمِ هَوْبَرُ

ʿashiyyata farra al-ḥārithiyyūna baʿdamā / qaḍā naḥbahu fī multaqā al-qawmi hawbaru

Translation:

“That evening the Ḥārithīs fled, after Hawbar had met his end where the two peoples confronted one another.”

An explanation transmitted with the witness identifies Hawbar as Yazīd ibn Hawbar, killed in battle. This identification comes from the verse’s explanation; qaḍā naḥbahu itself does not specify his name or manner of death.

Here qaḍā naḥbahu concerns life ending and death on a battlefield.

The witness matters because it shows the phrase functioning as a recognized expression for death.

Dhū al-Rummah lived after Qur’anic revelation, however. His verse alone cannot establish that the same phrase had the same settled sense before the Qur’an.

Death is explicitly connected with warfare in this poem, but qaḍā naḥbahu should not therefore be restricted to being killed in battle. Arabic also records it for death and life’s end generally.

A witness from Labīd: naḥb as something to be fulfilled

Labīd ibn Rabīʿah, who lived through the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, opens one poem:

أَلَا تَسْأَلَانِ الْمَرْءَ مَاذَا يُحَاوِلُ
أَنَحْبٌ فَيُقْضَى أَمْ ضَلَالٌ وَبَاطِلُ

a-lā tasʾalāni al-marʾa mādhā yuḥāwilu / a-naḥbun fa-yuqḍā am ḍalālun wa-bāṭilu

Translation:

“Will you two not ask the man what he seeks: an undertaking to fulfil, or error and futility?”

Naḥb appears with yuqḍā in this verse.

The question contrasts something to be fulfilled with a futile pursuit.

This fits a vow or undertaking whose performance a person has assumed.

Since Labīd lived across both periods and this poem’s exact date is not securely established, it should not be presented as an unqualified pre-Qur’anic witness.

This and other uses show naḥb’s connection with fulfilling undertakings or bringing a matter to an end. The Qur’anic verse’s own context remains decisive in identifying its sense.

Naḥb and naḥīb: sound and weeping

In another branch, naḥīb denotes loud, audible weeping, wailing, and lamentation.

Intaḥaba likewise means weeping with wailing.

The emphasis falls on the sound produced while crying.

Nuḥāb is also recorded for a camel’s cough.

Weeping and coughing share audible sound and movements of breathing. This resemblance does not establish a common historical origin for every use of N Ḥ B.

It is useful to distinguish two fields: vows, undertakings, and risk on one side, and sound on the other. Their existence alone does not reveal their historical order of development.

Methodologically, a vow should not be derived from crying, or crying from a vow, through an unsupported story.

Qur’anic naḥbahu occurs in a context of pledges and fulfilment, rather than crying or wailing.

There is therefore no clear linguistic or contextual basis for reading qaḍā naḥbahu in Al-Aḥzāb 33:23 as ending one’s wailing or weeping.

3. Qur’anic usage: Al-Aḥzāb 33:23

مِنَ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ رِجَالٌ صَدَقُوا مَا عَاهَدُوا اللَّهَ عَلَيْهِ ۖ فَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ قَضَىٰ نَحْبَهُ وَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ يَنْتَظِرُ ۖ وَمَا بَدَّلُوا تَبْدِيلًا

mina al-muʾminīna rijālun ṣadaqū mā ʿāhadū allāha ʿalayhi fa-minhum man qaḍā naḥbahu wa-minhum man yantaẓiru wa-mā baddalū tabdīlan

Translation:

“Among the believers are men who remained true to what they pledged to The God. Some have fulfilled their undertaking, and others are waiting; and they have made no alteration.”

Al-Aḥzāb 33:23

This is the root’s sole Qur’anic occurrence.

First examine the verse’s structure. It begins with men who remained faithful to their pledge to God, then distinguishes those who have fulfilled their naḥb from those still waiting.

Its ending stresses that they have not altered their pledge and course.

The whole verse therefore concerns truthfulness, pledges, and steadfast commitment.

A. Ṣadaqū mā ʿāhadū allāha ʿalayhi: truthfulness in action

The first important phrase is:

صَدَقُوا مَا عَاهَدُوا اللَّهَ عَلَيْهِ

ṣadaqū mā ʿāhadū allāha ʿalayhi

Ṣadaqū comes from Ṣ D Q, and ʿāhadū from ʿ H D.

Truthfulness here exceeds speaking truthfully. Their conduct remained consistent with their pledge.

A person might make a promise and reaffirm it in comfort, then retreat from it when fulfilment becomes difficult.

Those praised in the verse, by contrast, remained faithful in practice to what they accepted.

The central concern is consistency between speech and action.

This matters because naḥb occurs in a sentence already discussing pledges and truthfulness in keeping them.

The undertaking and its fulfilment in qaḍā naḥbahu consequently have direct contextual support.

B. Fa-minhum man qaḍā naḥbahu: fulfilling an undertaking or life ending?

The central phrase is:

فَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ قَضَىٰ نَحْبَهُ

fa-minhum man qaḍā naḥbahu

Depending on object and context, Arabic qaḍā can mean completing, performing, judging, or spending a period of time.

Its object here is naḥbahu.

The lexical witnesses show naḥb as a vow or undertaking, and qaḍā naḥbahu as an Arabic expression for life ending.

Two principal readings therefore arise.

First reading: fulfilling an undertaking

This refers to people who completed what they had undertaken.

It connects directly with the opening, which says they remained true to their pledge to God.

Naḥbahu then concerns an obligation accepted and steadfastly pursued.

Second reading: life ending in faithfulness

The other reading concerns people whose lives ended while they remained faithful to their pledge.

It fits the established Arabic death idiom qaḍā naḥbahu.

The historical and military setting of Al-Aḥzāb also supports it: some believers had died in confrontations while others remained alive.

The readings need not contradict one another.

Someone can remain faithful until death, so that life’s conclusion and the fulfilment of an undertaking coincide in one person.

For each use, distinguish the noun’s meaning, the phrase’s meaning, and the contextual referent. Death should not become the only meaning of the whole family, nor should the phrase’s established use for life’s end be ignored.

C. Does qaḍā naḥbahu necessarily mean martyrdom?

Some translations and interpretations directly render the phrase as “was martyred” or “was killed in God’s cause.”

This fits some historical instances associated with the verse.

The Arabic phrase itself, however, uses neither qatala nor ustushhida.

Qaḍā naḥbahu can concern life’s end, but death and being killed in battle are not identical. The vow and undertaking senses are also recorded.

Early interpretations likewise distinguish these possibilities. Some concern people killed in battle; another transmitted reading allows fulfilment of the pledge whether a person is killed or remains alive.

The interpretive tradition thus did not restrict the phrase to one form of death.

Martyrdom can be an instance of fulfilling one’s naḥb here, but should not be presented as its sole lexical meaning.

For the basic translation, this study prefers retaining fulfilment of an undertaking, with life’s end or martyrdom discussed contextually.

D. Wa-minhum man yantaẓiru: waiting for what?

After the first group, the verse says:

وَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ يَنْتَظِرُ

wa-minhum man yantaẓiru

“And others are waiting.”

The object of yantaẓiru is not explicitly stated.

The preceding phrase supports understanding them as waiting to fulfil their naḥb, or for what will come along the course of their pledge.

In the life-ending reading, this can mean awaiting life’s conclusion in faithfulness, or a fate such as martyrdom.

In the undertaking reading, it can concern a remaining opportunity or occasion to complete what they have assumed.

Without a contextual indication, waiting should not be restricted exclusively to death or to a future battle.

More importantly, waiting here is not inactivity.

A person may not yet have completed an undertaking but remain steadfast throughout that time.

The ending clarifies this: they made no alteration.

E. Wa-mā baddalū tabdīlan: steadfastness in the pledge

The final phrase is:

وَمَا بَدَّلُوا تَبْدِيلًا

wa-mā baddalū tabdīlan

“And they made no alteration.”

Baddalū and its verbal noun tabdīlan come from B D L.

The verbal noun following the verb emphasizes the negation of alteration.

The sentence follows both groups: those who fulfilled their naḥb and those waiting.

Contextually, it is appropriate to relate the emphasis on not altering to all these faithful believers.

Their distinction is therefore not between faithfulness and unfaithfulness.

Both share truthfulness and steadfastness; one has reached completion or its final outcome, while the other is still waiting.

This is among the verse’s most important interpretive keys.

Having reached the end and still being on the way are different situations, but truthfulness and faithfulness can exist in both.

This comes from the sentences together, rather than belonging wholly to naḥb’s independent definition.

4. Al-Aḥzāb’s context and other Qur’anic passages

The setting: pledges under difficulty

The preceding verses help identify the pledge in Al-Aḥzāb 33:23.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:15 says:

وَلَقَدْ كَانُوا عَاهَدُوا اللَّهَ مِنْ قَبْلُ لَا يُوَلُّونَ الْأَدْبَارَ ۚ وَكَانَ عَهْدُ اللَّهِ مَسْئُولًا

wa-la-qad kānū ʿāhadū allāha min qablu lā yuwallūna al-adbāra wa-kāna ʿahdu allāhi masʾūlan

Translation:

“They had indeed previously pledged to The God not to turn their backs; and the pledge to The God will be questioned.”

Al-Aḥzāb 33:15

This concerns people who had earlier pledged but withdrew from fulfilment under difficulty.

The following context describes people’s different responses to danger and confrontation with the allied forces.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:22 also says:

وَلَمَّا رَأَى الْمُؤْمِنُونَ الْأَحْزَابَ قَالُوا هَٰذَا مَا وَعَدَنَا اللَّهُ وَرَسُولُهُ وَصَدَقَ اللَّهُ وَرَسُولُهُ ۚ وَمَا زَادَهُمْ إِلَّا إِيمَانًا وَتَسْلِيمًا

wa-lammā raʾā al-muʾminūna al-aḥzāba qālū hādhā mā waʿadanā allāhu wa-rasūluhu wa-ṣadaqa allāhu wa-rasūluhu wa-mā zādahum illā īmānan wa-taslīman

Translation:

“When the believers saw the allied forces, they said: This is what The God and His Messenger promised us, and The God and His Messenger spoke truly. It increased them only in faith and submission.”

Al-Aḥzāb 33:22

The next verse discusses men who remained faithful to their pledge.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:23 therefore concerns more than people dying: it belongs to a test of faithfulness under danger and hardship.

Some withdraw from their undertaking when difficulty arrives; others remain steadfast.

In this context, completing one’s naḥb relates to faithfulness reaching its outcome.

The reward for truthfulness: Al-Aḥzāb 33:24

Al-Aḥzāb 33:24 says:

لِيَجْزِيَ اللَّهُ الصَّادِقِينَ بِصِدْقِهِمْ وَيُعَذِّبَ الْمُنَافِقِينَ إِنْ شَاءَ أَوْ يَتُوبَ عَلَيْهِمْ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ كَانَ غَفُورًا رَحِيمًا

li-yajziya allāhu al-ṣādiqīna bi-ṣidqihim wa-yuʿadhdhiba al-munāfiqīna in shāʾa aw yatūba ʿalayhim inna allāha kāna ghafūran raḥīman

Translation:

“So that The God may reward the truthful for their truthfulness, and punish the hypocrites if He wills, or turn toward them. Indeed, The God is forgiving and merciful.”

Al-Aḥzāb 33:24

Bi-ṣidqihim states the basis of reward: their truthfulness.

The preceding verse had distinguished those who completed their naḥb from those still waiting.

Reward is now connected with truthfulness, rather than merely dying or remaining alive.

Those who died therefore cannot be treated as the only faithful people, with survivors excluded until death.

The evident criterion across the verses is truthfulness and keeping the pledge.

This also fits the ending of Al-Aḥzāb 33:23: وَمَا بَدَّلُوا تَبْدِيلًا.

Pledges and fulfilment in Al-Fatḥ 48:10

Another passage says:

فَمَنْ نَكَثَ فَإِنَّمَا يَنْكُثُ عَلَىٰ نَفْسِهِ ۖ وَمَنْ أَوْفَىٰ بِمَا عَاهَدَ عَلَيْهُ اللَّهَ فَسَيُؤْتِيهِ أَجْرًا عَظِيمًا

fa-man nakatha fa-innamā yankuthu ʿalā nafsihi wa-man awfā bi-mā ʿāhada ʿalayhu allāha fa-sa-yuʾtīhi ajran ʿaẓīman

Translation:

“Whoever breaks the pledge breaks it only to his own detriment; and whoever fulfils what he pledged to The God, He will soon give him a tremendous reward.”

Al-Fatḥ 48:10, excerpt

Keeping the pledge is contrasted with breaking it.

Although N Ḥ B does not occur here, faithfulness to an undertaking connects this with Al-Aḥzāb 33:23.

Within the Qur’anic semantic network, truthfulness in a pledge thus exceeds a spoken declaration.

Pledge, fulfilment, and naḥb should nevertheless not all be made synonyms; each word has its own role.

5. Questions about completing one’s naḥb

Does naḥb only mean death?

Persian sometimes uses the Arabic expression qaḍā-ye naḥb kard for passing away or dying.

This fits one recorded Arabic meaning.

Extending that idiom to the whole root family would overlook vows, undertakings, risk, and strenuous effort.

Conversely, removing death entirely from qaḍā naḥbahu because Al-Aḥzāb 33:23 concerns pledges would discard actual Arabic evidence.

A more precise approach distinguishes three levels:

First, naḥb as a noun meaning a vow or undertaking.

Second, qaḍā naḥbahu as a phrase conveying fulfilment of an undertaking or life’s end.

Third, the verse’s referents, potentially including those who died while keeping their pledge.

The levels are related without being identical.

Does naḥb mean wishing for death or seeking to be killed?

That does not follow from the root itself.

In the vow sense, naḥb is an obligation assumed by a person, but its object must come from context or accompanying explanation.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:23 concerns truthfulness in a pledge and not altering it.

The surah’s historical setting involves warfare and possible death, but the verse does not explicitly say they vowed to be killed or wished for death.

Someone might pledge steadfastness in danger and die while keeping it.

Steadfastness under danger differs from seeking death as an independent objective.

Qaḍā naḥbahu should therefore not be directly translated as “achieved his wish to be killed.”

Is a pledge fulfilled only through death?

The verse establishes no such universal rule.

Arabic qaḍā can describe completing a task without life necessarily ending.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:23 likewise distinguishes some who have fulfilled their naḥb from others waiting.

This does not make a living person’s faithfulness deficient or worthless.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:23 says they made no alteration, and Al-Aḥzāb 33:24 connects reward with truthfulness.

Someone may fulfil a specific undertaking before death, while another undertaking may extend throughout life.

This depends on the pledge’s object and circumstances, rather than a fixed rule supplied by N Ḥ B itself.

6. Comparison with related roots

N Ḥ B (نَحْب) and N Dh R (نَذْر): undertaking and vow

One Arabic sense of naḥb is explained through nadhr.

Nadhr concerns something a person makes binding upon himself. The Qur’an also discusses vows and their fulfilment:

يُوفُونَ بِالنَّذْرِ وَيَخَافُونَ يَوْمًا كَانَ شَرُّهُ مُسْتَطِيرًا

yūfūna bi-al-nadhri wa-yakhāfūna yawman kāna sharruhu mustaṭīran

Translation:

“They fulfil the vow and fear a day whose evil is widespread.”

Al-Insān 76:7

Vow and fulfilment are distinct: the vow is what was assumed, and fulfilment is performing it.

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:23, naḥb similarly accompanies qaḍā and relates to fulfilling an undertaking.

Naḥb and nadhr are nevertheless not identical in every Arabic use.

Naḥb has other branches involving an appointed term, death, risk, and sound. Nadhr also has its own range in Arabic and the Qur’an.

A vow is one recorded meaning of naḥb, without the two families being interchangeable everywhere.

Study of N Dh R

N Ḥ B (نَحْب) and ʿ H D (عَهْد): undertaking and pledge

ʿAhd has a broad Qur’anic range, including a covenant, undertaking, instruction, or responsibility established between parties.

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:23, ʿāhadū precedes qaḍā naḥbahu.

This creates a clear semantic connection.

The pledge introduces the relationship and commitment accepted; naḥb here relates to fulfilling or concluding that commitment.

This distinction is not an absolute definition of either root.

Naḥb itself can mean a vow or undertaking, and a pledge can be fulfilled or broken.

It would therefore be wrong to make ʿahd invariably the beginning of responsibility and naḥb invariably its end. That fixed sequence is unsupported by linguistic evidence.

In this particular verse, qaḍā foregrounds completion.

Study of ʿ H D

N Ḥ B (نَحْب) and Q Ḍ Y (قَضَىٰ): undertaking and completion

Understanding the Qur’anic phrase requires distinguishing these two roots.

Naḥb here relates to an undertaking or assumed obligation.

Qaḍā conveys performing or completing it in this construction.

Completion therefore comes from qaḍā and should not become an independent component of naḥb’s definition.

The phrase’s death idiom also does not make every qaḍā concern death or life’s end.

Qur’anic qaḍā accompanies different subjects; construction and object determine the precise sense.

Study of Q Ḍ Y

N Ḥ B (نَحْب) and W F Y (وَفَىٰ): undertaking and fulfilment

W F Y concerns paying or performing fully, completing, and fulfilling what is owed.

Al-Insān 76:7 combines fulfilment with a vow, and Al-Fatḥ 48:10 concerns keeping a pledge.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:23 does not use the verb wafā, but ṣadaqū mā ʿāhadū and qaḍā naḥbahu convey keeping a pledge in practice.

Naḥb names the undertaking or accepted obligation, while fulfilment concerns performing it and remaining faithful to it.

Translation should preserve this distinction.

Study of W F Y

N Ḥ B (نَحْب) and ʾ J L (أَجَل): life’s end and an appointed term

In the death usage, naḥb connects with ajal.

Ajal nevertheless also denotes a specified time or deadline in Arabic and the Qur’an, rather than death alone.

The Qur’an says:

فَإِذَا جَاءَ أَجَلُهُمْ لَا يَسْتَأْخِرُونَ سَاعَةً وَلَا يَسْتَقْدِمُونَ

fa-idhā jāʾa ajaluhum lā yastaʾkhirūna sāʿatan wa-lā yastaqdimūna

Translation:

“Then when their appointed term comes, they neither delay it by an hour nor advance it.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:34, excerpt

This concerns the arrival of an appointed term.

Study of ʾ J L

N Ḥ B (نَحْب) and B K Y (بَكَىٰ): weeping and wailing

This comparison concerns the root’s second branch.

Bakā means weeping; naḥīb particularly denotes audible weeping and wailing.

Every naḥīb is a form of weeping, but not all weeping is necessarily loud or accompanied by wailing.

This branch does not directly explain Qur’anic naḥb in Al-Aḥzāb 33:23.

Shared consonants alone therefore do not justify putting grief, wailing, or lamentation into the translation of naḥbahu.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Translation must first distinguish the undertaking branch from sound.

For naḥb in the vow branch, suitable equivalents include “vow,” “undertaking,” or “what a person has assumed.”

Natural renderings of naḥīb include “audible weeping,” “wailing,” and “sobbing.”

Animal nuḥāb can be “coughing.”

Uses involving risk may call for “risk” or “great danger.”

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:23, however, the main translation issue is qaḍā naḥbahu.

Several renderings merit examination:

RenderingAssessment
Discharged his vowFits the lexical meaning and pledge context, but may evoke a technical vow
Fulfilled his undertakingNatural and consistent with the verse’s opening
Kept his pledgeConveys the sense, but makes the distinction between qaḍā and naḥb less visible
His life endedAn established Arabic use, but hides the undertaking sense
DiedPossible in the historical context, but narrower than the Arabic expression
Was martyredAn interpretive instance, rather than the word’s necessary direct equivalent

The preferred proposal for Forqan’s basic translation is:

“Some have fulfilled their undertaking, and others are waiting.”

This directly fits the opening phrase ṣadaqū mā ʿāhadū allāha ʿalayhi.

It also leaves room to explain the established death idiom and those who died in the reflection.

If the reflection emphasizes life’s end, it can explain the Arabic death usage and how faithfulness unto death is a clear instance in this historical setting.

Death or martyrdom should nevertheless not simply replace naḥb without explanation.

8. Conclusion

Arabic N Ḥ B has two established semantic branches.

The first concerns vows, undertakings, assumed responsibility, risk, and completion, with related uses for a due date, appointed term, and life’s end.

The second concerns sound: naḥīb for audible weeping and wailing, and nuḥāb for an animal’s cough.

The two should not be reduced to an invented single image without historical evidence.

Arabic poetic witnesses illustrate vows and undertakings, risk, and life’s end. Some have uncertain attribution or dates; others belong to Islamic-period poets. They are not all conclusive pre-Qur’anic witnesses.

The Qur’an uses the root once: naḥbahu in Al-Aḥzāb 33:23.

The principal concern is truthfulness and keeping the pledge. Some believers have completed their naḥb and others wait, but both share steadfastness and refusal to alter their pledge.

Qaḍā naḥbahu is also recognized Arabic for life ending. Dying in faithfulness can therefore be a clear instance in the verse’s historical setting.

The phrase is nevertheless not restricted to being killed or martyred; nor does the second group’s waiting imply inactivity or a lack of truthfulness.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:24 likewise connects reward with truthfulness, rather than merely dying.

Proposed final definition for Forqan

In its first branch, N Ḥ B concerns a vow, undertaking, or obligation assumed by a person, with further uses involving risk, an appointed time, and life’s end. In a separate branch, naḥīb denotes audible weeping and wailing.

Qur’anic naḥbahu occurs in qaḍā naḥbahu. This study’s preferred reading, guided by the pledge and truthfulness context, centres on an undertaking a person fulfils. The Arabic phrase can also convey life’s end, and faithfulness unto death is a defensible instance here.

This study therefore prefers “fulfilled his undertaking” as the basic rendering in Al-Aḥzāb 33:23, while retaining life’s end and dying as an established Arabic use and contextual reading in the explanation.
