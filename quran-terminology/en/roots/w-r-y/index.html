The Qur’anic Root W R Y

Covering and concealment; and, in another branch, the emergence and production of fire

The root W R Y (و ر ي) presents two clear semantic branches in the Qur’an. In one, something is covered, disappears from sight, or someone conceals themselves. In the other, fire emerges from a fire-making implement or is produced and kindled by an agent. An imagery-based resemblance can be seen between these branches, but the available evidence is insufficient to turn that resemblance into a definitive theory about the historical origin of all the root’s forms.

Warāʾ (وَرَاء) complicates this picture. Some Qur’anic morphological indexes also place it under W R Y, but its origin was disputed in the early lexical tradition. Warāʾ therefore belongs in this study, yet should not be used from the outset as conclusive evidence for defining the semantic core of W R Y.

If warāʾ is included under this entry, W R Y is indexed 32 times in the Qur’an, with warāʾ accounting for 24 instances. Outside this group, eight verbal and participial occurrences remain: four from the wārā family, two from tawārā, one tūrūna (تُورُونَ), and one al-mūriyāt (الْمُورِيَاتِ). These eight occurrences clearly display the two principal, undisputed Qur’anic branches.

Two branches and a cautious conceptual connection

In the first branch, wārā (وارى) denotes covering something and removing it from sight, while tawārā (توارى) denotes becoming concealed or withdrawing from view. The relationship between these forms is clear: in one, an agent covers something; in the other, the agent themselves disappears from sight.

In the fire branch, warā z-zand (ورى الزند) describes fire emerging from the fire-making implement, and awrā z-zand (أورى الزند) describes bringing its fire forth. Here the semantic movement appears to run in the opposite direction to the first branch: in muwārāh, something visible is covered; in īrāʾ, something previously unseen becomes manifest.

A cautious conceptual connection can be seen here. Striking a fire-making implement begins with something whose fire is not yet visible and ends with the appearance of a flame or spark. In muwārāh, the movement is from visibility to concealment; in īrāʾ, from invisibility to appearance. Both branches operate at the boundary between being seen and not being seen.

This is only one way to understand the symmetry between the branches. It does not establish that ancient Arabs regarded fire as a physical entity hidden inside wood, or that both branches necessarily arose from a single historical image. A conceptual resemblance cannot replace derivational evidence.

The use of covering is also clear in early language. A verse attributed to ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād says:

وَأَغُضُّ طَرْفِي مَا بَدَتْ لِي جَارَتِي

حَتَّى يُوَارِي جَارَتِي مَأْوَاهَا

Wa-aghuḍḍu ṭarfī mā badat lī jāratī / ḥattā yuwāriya jāratī maʾwāhā.

“I lower my gaze until my neighbor’s dwelling conceals her from view.”

Yuwārī (يواري) here presents a very simple image: a place conceals someone from the observer’s eyes. This use shows that covering and removing from sight were recognized meanings in early language. Nevertheless, the attribution and date of each verse must be assessed independently of the poet’s fame; a poetic witness should not bear more evidential weight than it can support.

Muwārāh and tawārī in the Qur’an

The clearest uses of the covering branch occur in Al-Māʾidah 5:31:

كَيْفَ يُوَارِي سَوْءَةَ أَخِيهِ

Kayfa yuwārī sawʾata akhīh.

“How to cover his brother’s sawʾah.”

And then:

فَأُوَارِيَ سَوْءَةَ أَخِي

Fa-uwāriya sawʾata akhī.

“So that I may cover my brother’s sawʾah.”

In these constructions, yuwārī says no more, in itself, than covering and removing from sight. The nature of what is covered comes from sawʾata akhīhi (سوأة أخيه), “his brother’s sawʾah,” and from the story’s context.

The same relationship appears in Al-Aʿrāf 7:26:

لِبَاسًا يُوَارِي سَوْآتِكُمْ

Libāsan yuwārī sawʾātikum.

“Clothing that covers your sawʾāt.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:20 uses the passive form of the same family:

مَا وُورِيَ عَنْهُمَا مِنْ سَوْآتِهِمَا

Mā wūriya ʿanhumā min sawʾātihimā.

“What had been concealed from them of their sawʾāt.”

The association of W R Y with sawʾah (سوأة) in these verses is significant. The Qur’an repeatedly uses this verb where the object of covering is sawʾah: something whose remaining exposed is made undesirable by the context. This association gives greater depth to the Qur’anic use of muwārāh, but the relationship must not be reversed. W R Y does not itself mean shame, ugliness, vulnerability, or sin. That semantic coloring comes from sawʾah and the situation described in the verse. If the same verb is applied to a neutral object, the root does not itself make that object shameful.

This distinction matters: the root expresses only the act of covering, while its object and context determine the reason for covering and its value.

Tawārā (توارى) presents another direction within this branch. In An-Naḥl 16:59 we read:

يَتَوَارَى مِنَ الْقَوْمِ مِنْ سُوءِ مَا بُشِّرَ بِهِ

Yatawārā mina l-qawmi min sūʾi mā bushshira bih.

“He hides from his people because of the unpleasantness of what he has been told.”

Here the agent no longer covers something else; he withdraws from the people’s sight and presence. In this use, form VI, the tafāʿul pattern, clearly shows the action returning to the agent: the person places himself in a position where he cannot be seen.

The tafāʿul pattern itself should not be made to establish more than this. Yatawārā (يتوارى) does not alone prove that concealment must happen slowly, like a shadow, gently, or step by step. If such qualities are perceived in a scene, they must be established from that scene’s context, not from the morphological pattern alone.

In Ṣād 38:32 we also read:

حَتَّىٰ تَوَارَتْ بِالْحِجَابِ

Ḥattā tawārat bi-l-ḥijāb.

“Until it disappeared behind the veil.”

Again, the direct meaning of tawārat (توارت) is becoming hidden from sight. Bi-l-ḥijāb (بالحجاب) also indicates the means or boundary of that concealment. The verb itself, however, specifies neither whether the disappearance was rapid or gradual nor even the identity of its subject. Those questions must be examined through the verse’s construction and context.

The wārā/tawārā branch can therefore be defined concisely as placing something out of sight, or the agent themselves withdrawing from view.

Īrāʾ an-nār: the emergence of fire

The second branch appears in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:71:

أَفَرَأَيْتُمُ النَّارَ الَّتِي تُورُونَ

A-fa-raʾaytumu n-nāra llatī tūrūn.

“Have you considered the fire that you kindle?”

Tūrūna (تُورُونَ) comes from awrā (أورى). Arabic usage distinguishes fire emerging from a fire-making implement from someone bringing it forth. Warā z-zand (ورى الزند) means that the implement’s fire appeared and came out; awrā z-zand (أورى الزند) means that someone brought that fire forth or caused it to appear.

“Kindle” is a natural English translation, but the lexical image is more specific than simply lighting an already prepared fire. It concerns fire appearing from the means used to produce it.

This image becomes clearer in Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:2:

فَالْمُورِيَاتِ قَدْحًا

Fa-l-mūriyāti qadḥā.

Two lexical roots stand together here: al-mūriyāt (الموريات) belongs to W R Y, and qadḥan (قدحا) to Q D Ḥ. They should not be treated as having the same meaning.

Qadḥ is an action performed to create fire or a spark, such as striking or rubbing a fire-making implement. Īrāʾ refers to the stage at which fire or a spark actually appears. One can therefore perform qadḥ without obtaining the desired result, whereas when awrā has taken place, fire has appeared.

This distinction helps prevent us from treating al-mūriyāt merely as “those who strike” or “spark-strikers.” Qadḥan itself contributes the striking action to the image. Al-mūriyāt denotes those that bring forth fire, while qadḥan clarifies how this happens within the verse’s construction.

Accordingly, a rendering such as:

“And those that bring forth sparks by striking”

can better preserve the relationship between the two words.

At the same time, W R Y itself does not identify the referents of al-mūriyāt. The root explains only the act of īrāʾ. Who or what performs this act is a contextual question and should not be extracted from the root’s meaning alone.

This branch also permits the same conceptual comparison: in muwārāh, something disappears from sight; in īrāʾ, fire comes into view. One produces concealment, the other appearance. This opposition is useful for understanding the lexical family, but still should not become a definitive derivational history.

Warāʾ: linguistic proximity and disputed derivation

A large share of what some indexes place under W R Y consists of warāʾ (وراء). This word occurs 24 times in the Qur’an; if all these instances are counted under the root, they make up most of its occurrences. Its derivational connection to W R Y, however, is not certain.

Two analyses existed in the early lexical tradition. One regarded the final hamzah of warāʾ as a transformation of a weak consonant and connected it with the tawārā family. The other regarded the hamzah as original and assigned warāʾ to a separate root. The existence of this disagreement is sufficient reason not to use warāʾ as conclusive evidence for constructing the semantic core of W R Y.

Qur’anic warāʾ is also broader than the simple equivalent “behind.” Sometimes the spatial direction behind something is clear:

وَرَاءَ ظُهُورِهِمْ

Warāʾa ẓuhūrihim.

“Behind their backs.”

In other constructions, however, the word denotes something beyond a limit, on the other side of something, after something, or lying ahead. For example:

مِنْ وَرَائِهِ جَهَنَّمُ

Min warāʾihi jahannam.

Ibrāhīm 14:16

In such a construction, “Hell is behind him” does not necessarily convey the appropriate image; the reference may be to something that lies ahead as he continues along his course.

This feature shows why warāʾ should not be confined in translation to one fixed geometrical direction. Its working semantic core is closer to being on the other side of a boundary or outside the reference domain; context determines the precise direction.

Al-Baqarah 2:91 says:

وَيَكْفُرُونَ بِمَا وَرَاءَهُ

Wa-yakfurūna bi-mā warāʾah.

Here “what is beyond it” or “what lies further than it” is more natural than “what is behind it.” The issue is not physical direction but something outside the limits of what they accept.

Nevertheless, the breadth of warāʾ’s spatial meaning does not by itself establish its derivational independence. Semantic difference and historical difference are separate questions. Two forms may be historically related yet develop different semantic fields over time, just as semantic resemblance alone does not establish a shared root. The disagreement over warāʾ’s derivation must therefore remain open.

Forms outside Qur’anic usage

In later language, tawriyah (توریه) became a technical term for speech whose apparent meaning suggests one thing while the speaker intends another. This usage is understandable through covering and concealment, but its technical rhetorical meaning should not be imposed upon Qur’anic W R Y.

Likewise, at-Tawrāh (التوراة), the Torah, should not be counted without caution as a definite Qur’anic derivative of W R Y merely because of morphological resemblance or later derivational analyses. A word’s presence in the Qur’an does not establish its definite membership in every root subsequently proposed for it.

Other lexical forms are also recorded under entries close to W R Y. Since they do not occur in the Qur’an in these uses, however, they should not occupy the center of a Qur’anic root study. Such a study should begin above all with the forms the Qur’an itself uses.

Translation proposals for Forqan

In the covering branch, yuwārī (يُوَارِي) can be translated, depending on the sentence, as “covers” or “conceals from sight.” Wūriya (وُورِيَ) can be “had been covered” or “had been concealed from view.” Yatawārā (يَتَوَارَى) can be “hides himself” or, more simply, “hides,” while tawārat (تَوَارَتْ) will usually be “became hidden” or “disappeared from sight.”

These forms do not require shame, deception, fear, or impurity to be incorporated into the translation of the root itself. If such meanings are present in a verse, they come from other words and the context.

In the fire branch, “you kindle” is natural English for tūrūna (تُورُونَ), while the root discussion can clarify its image of bringing fire forth and making it visible.

In Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:2, “فَالْمُورِيَاتِ قَدْحًا” (fa-l-mūriyāti qadḥā) can be understood as “And those that bring forth sparks by striking,” preserving the relationship between the two roots without putting the whole meaning of qadḥ inside al-mūriyāt.

No single fixed equivalent is suitable for warāʾ either. “Behind,” “beyond,” “on the other side of,” “further than,” and, in some contexts, an expression for what lies ahead should be selected according to the verse’s construction. In Al-Baqarah 2:91, “what is beyond it” or “what lies further than it” is more suitable than the spatial rendering “behind it.”

Summary

The undisputed Qur’anic forms of W R Y show two main branches. In wārā and tawārā, the semantic movement is toward concealment: something is covered from sight or the agent themselves withdraws from view. In warā and awrā concerning fire, the movement is toward appearance: fire emerges or an agent brings it forth.

A conceptual symmetry can be seen between these branches: one conceals what is visible, and the other reveals what is unseen. This symmetry is useful for explaining how the meanings relate, but not for proving that they all arose from a single historical image.

The repeated association of muwārāh with sawʾah in the Qur’an is also significant. Yet unpleasantness, shame, or vulnerability comes from sawʾah and context, not from W R Y itself. Similarly, the tafāʿul pattern in yatawārā and tawārat expresses becoming hidden, but does not alone determine gradualness, gentleness, or the physical manner of concealment.

In the fire branch, the distinction between īrāʾ and qadḥ must be preserved. Qadḥ is the action of producing a spark, while īrāʾ denotes the actual emergence of fire. Their juxtaposition in “فَالْمُورِيَاتِ قَدْحًا” (fa-l-mūriyāti qadḥā) completes the image without making the two words identical in meaning.

Warāʾ also has a broad Qur’anic range and should not invariably be translated as “behind.” Nevertheless, the early disagreement over its origin prevents the 24 instances of warāʾ from serving, without qualification, as the basis for defining W R Y.

Concise definition: in its undisputed Qur’anic uses, W R Y denotes, in one branch, covering and concealing from sight or becoming hidden, and, in the other, the emergence or production of fire. A conceptual opposition between concealment and appearance can be seen between these branches, but their historical unity is uncertain. Warāʾ’s derivational membership in the same root must likewise remain an open and disputed question.
