A study of nakf and istinkāf in early Arabic and the Qur’an: from wiping away tears and turning away from an undertaking to disdaining service to The God and its distinction from arrogance

The three-letter root N K F has several recognized branches in Arabic. One physical use concerns setting something aside or removing it, as with wiping tears from the cheek, leaving a course, or the interruption of something previously continuing. Another branch uses nakf and istinkāf for refusing an action that someone regards as objectionable or beneath their dignity.

In human behavior, istinkāf differs from simple refraining. People may not do something because of inability, danger, ignorance, or a belief that it is wrong. Istinkāf, however, usually involves a sense of indignity, unwillingness to accept the action, or regarding oneself as above it.

Istinkāf may therefore accompany arrogance, but the words are not identical in meaning. Istinkāf highlights refusing and rejecting an action or status, while arrogance emphasizes magnifying oneself and claiming superiority.

N K F occurs only three times in the Qur’an, in two consecutive verses of Al-Nisāʾ. All three occurrences belong to the verb istankafa.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:172, the Qur’an says that the Messiah and the angels brought near do not disdain serving The God. It then warns that whoever disdains His service and is arrogant will face divine judgment. The following verse contrasts those who disdain and are arrogant with believers who do righteous deeds.

Understanding the root requires distinguishing the lexical meaning of setting aside, refusal arising from a sense of indignity, self-importance, and the moral consequences of such behavior.

1. Forms and semantic core

Important forms of this family in Arabic include:

  • نَكَفَ: set aside, removed, or cut off; in some uses, turned away from something.
  • نَكِفَ: regarded an action as beneath one’s dignity or refused it.
  • نَكْف: setting aside and removing; in other uses, refraining and unwillingness.
  • نَكَف: in some uses, disgrace or reproach that someone seeks to avoid.
  • نَاكِف: someone turning away from something or refusing an action.
  • اِسْتَنْكَفَ: refused an action or status because of a sense of indignity or unwillingness.
  • اِسْتِنْكَاف: refusal arising from a sense of indignity or self-importance.
  • اِنْتَكَفَ: left a course, changed direction, or turned from one undertaking to another; in another use, wiped or removed something.
  • تَنَاكَفَ: in certain uses, two people exchanged speech between them.
  • أَنْكَفَ: in one lexical use, declared something or someone free of what is regarded as disgraceful.
  • نَكْفَة: in another branch, a part or gland in the jaw and neck region.
  • نُكَاف: a disease or swelling in that region.

Forms such as mankūf are also recorded for a person or animal affected in the bodily region just mentioned.

These physical uses should not be imposed on the Qur’anic meaning of istinkāf.

Qur’anic forms

Only three forms of Form X occur in the Qur’an:

يَسْتَنْكِفَ in Al-Nisāʾ 4:172, after lan.

يَسْتَنْكِفْ in Al-Nisāʾ 4:172, after conditional man.

اِسْتَنْكَفُوا in Al-Nisāʾ 4:173, as a plural perfect verb.

These are not three independent derivatives, but grammatical forms of one verb.

The basic forms nakafa and nakifa, the noun istinkāf, and the family’s other recognized derivatives do not occur in the Qur’an.

Is setting aside the root’s only core meaning?

Two lexical branches can be distinguished in examining this family.

The first concerns cutting something off and setting it aside.

The second concerns parts of the body, particularly the jaw and neck region.

One proposed derivational explanation relates feeling affronted and refusing to turning away and exposing the side of the jaw.

Another explanation connects istinkāf with wiping tears from the cheek, as though someone pushes away something they do not wish to accept.

These explanations help identify possible relationships among meanings, but should not be treated as the certain historical origin of every use.

Two clear semantic fields are setting something aside or removing it, and refusal accompanied by a sense of indignity or unwillingness.

The relationship can be understood through setting aside and avoiding something, but the established meaning of istinkāf should not be reduced to a bodily movement of the hand or neck.

2. N K F in early Arabic: from tears and rain to turning away

Wiping tears from the cheek

One recognized physical use of this root concerns wiping away tears.

نَكَفَ الدَّمْعَ

nakafa al-damʿa

Translation: “He wiped the tears away.”

Early explanations describe someone using a finger to remove tears from the cheek.

Nakf here is not merely the tears drying, but moving and removing them from where they were.

An expression in a verse transmitted by early lexicographers, whose author is unknown, reads:

لَمْ يُنْكَفْ لِعَيْنَيْكَ مَدْمَعُ

lam yunkaf li-ʿaynayka madmaʿu

Translation: “No tear was wiped away from your eyes.”

It occurs in a verse about separation and remembering an earlier pledge.

Yunkaf in it concerns wiping away or removing tears.

The full verse varies in transmission: some have fa-bānū, others fa-mātū; the wording concerning the pledge also varies between al-ḥilf and al-khulf.

The author and exact date of this witness are unknown.

It helps establish attested early Arabic usage, but cannot be presented as definitively pre-Islamic poetry.

Rain ceasing and a flow coming to an end

The expression nakaftu al-ghaytha describes reaching the limit of the rain and leaving its area: the rain ceases in relation to the person, rather than the person themselves stopping the natural phenomenon.

In the expression:

غَيْثٌ لَا يُنْكَفُ

ghaythun lā yunkafu

Translation: “Rain that does not cease.”

Nakf here concerns a flow coming to an end or being cut off.

Water not easily exhausted is likewise described as māʾun lā yunkaf.

In another use, baḥrun lā yunkaf describes a sea whose water cannot all be drawn out or exhausted.

This approaches the general use of removing and cutting off, but is not identical to wiping tears from the cheek.

Some lexical accounts also apply the expression to an immense army whose number or end is not readily determined.

This figurative use concerns inexhaustibility and inability to reach the end of a body of things.

Such examples should not make the Qur’anic istinkāf mean “cutting off” or “bringing to an end.” In its social and ethical use, that verb has a more specific meaning.

Leaving a course and changing direction

Intakafa is also attested for leaving one course and turning to another route or undertaking.

For example:

أَرَادَ هَذَا ثُمَّ انْتَكَفَ إِلَىٰ غَيْرِهِ

arāda hādhā thumma intakafa ilā ghayrihi

Translation: “He intended this undertaking, then turned away from it toward another.”

The person moves away from a previous direction or decision.

Nakafa ʿani al-shayʾi in some uses likewise means moving aside or turning away from something.

This branch approaches istinkāf, since both involve a person distancing themselves from something.

Changing course, however, need not arise from a sense of indignity or self-importance.

People may abandon a wrong decision after receiving new information. Such a change is not itself istinkāf in the sense of arrogant refusal.

Poetic evidence: the heart’s return from its previous course

Abū al-Najm al-ʿIjlī, an Umayyad poet, says in a rajaz poem:

مَا بَالُ قَلْبٍ رَاجَعَ انْتِكَافًا
بَعْدَ التَّعَزِّي اللَّهْوَ وَالْإِيجَافَا

mā bālu qalbin rājaʿa intikāfan / baʿda al-taʿazzī al-lahwa wa-al-ījāfā

Translation: “What has happened to a heart that, after finding consolation and letting go, has turned back again to diversion and haste?”

Intikāf here concerns the heart returning and changing direction.

The subject is not a person’s bodily movement between places, but a change in inclination and inner condition.

The verse shows that N K F can describe turning back from a direction or inclination.

This use does not mean arrogance.

The poet’s heart may have abandoned an earlier resolve without self-importance or refusal of a duty being involved.

Abū al-Najm belongs to the Umayyad period. This is therefore a witness to classical Arabic after the beginning of Islam, not the pre-Islamic period.

Istinkāf: refusing as beneath one’s dignity

Arabic uses the expression:

اِسْتَنْكَفَ مِنَ الْأَمْرِ

istankafa mina al-amri

Or:

اِسْتَنْكَفَ عَنِ الْأَمْرِ

istankafa ʿani al-amri

Translation: “He regarded the undertaking as beneath his dignity and refused it.”

This differs from simply refraining.

Some lexical explanations use anifa for istinkāf: regarding an action as objectionable to one’s standing or position.

Another explanation relates istinkāf to rejecting what is regarded as disgraceful or disparaging and saying “no,” distinguishing it from arrogance.

The distinction matters.

Istinkāf concerns the act of rejecting and refusing.

Why someone does so, how highly they regard themselves, and whether pride motivates them must be established from usage and context.

In the established expression istankafa ʿani al-shayʾi, a sense that the action is undignified or objectionable is prominent. The word alone does not describe every psychological and moral detail of the person.

Nakfah: the bodily branch

In early Arabic lexicons, nakfah names a part or gland beneath the jaw, near the ear and the two sides of the throat.

Descriptions of its exact location differ.

Some describe small glands near the jaw, others parts on either side of the throat, and others a prominent part of the neck and jaw region.

Nukāf also names a swelling or disease of that region.

The word’s present medical use should not turn all early descriptions into one specific medical diagnosis.

This branch belongs in a study of N K F, but nakfah as a bodily part does not occur in the Qur’an.

Some proposed connections between istinkāf and turning the neck to expose the side of the jaw are lexical analyses, not definitive historical proof that refusal developed from the name of that bodily part.

3. Al-Nisāʾ 4:172: the Messiah does not disdain serving The God

لَنْ يَسْتَنْكِفَ الْمَسِيحُ أَنْ يَكُونَ عَبْدًا لِلَّهِ وَلَا الْمَلَائِكَةُ الْمُقَرَّبُونَ وَمَنْ يَسْتَنْكِفْ عَنْ عِبَادَتِهِ وَيَسْتَكْبِرْ فَسَيَحْشُرُهُمْ إِلَيْهِ جَمِيعًا

lan yastankifa al-masīḥu an yakūna ʿabdan lillāhi wa-lā al-malāʾikatu al-muqarrabūna wa-man yastankif ʿan ʿibādatihi wa-yastakbir fa-sa-yaḥshuruhum ilayhi jamīʿan

Forqan’s rendering:

“The Messiah will never disdain being a servant of The God, nor will the angels brought near. Whoever disdains His worship and is arrogant, He will gather them all to Himself.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:172

This verse contains two occurrences of N K F.

The first part concerns the Messiah and the angels brought near.

The second states a general principle about those who disdain serving The God and are arrogant.

«لَنْ يَسْتَنْكِفَ الْمَسِيحُ»

Yastankifa is an imperfect verb of Form X.

It follows lan here and is subjunctive.

The sentence denies that the Messiah disdains being a servant of The God.

It thus does more than state that the Messiah is in practice a servant. It explicitly denies that he regards accepting this status as disgraceful or humiliating.

The distinction matters.

Someone may occupy a position while resenting its acceptance or wishing to distance themselves from it.

The verse, by contrast, denies the Messiah’s istinkāf: being a servant of The God is not something he refuses.

The role of «أَنْ يَكُونَ عَبْدًا لِلَّهِ»

The object of istinkāf is expressed through the construction an yakūna.

The subject of refusal is being a servant of The God.

Istinkāf is thus not always restricted to failing to perform a particular action. Someone may disdain accepting a position or relationship.

The position of servanthood is at issue here.

N K F does not itself mean servanthood. That concept comes from ʿabdan lillāhi.

Servanthood in the Qur’an also requires an independent examination of ʿ B D and its uses. Istinkāf alone cannot define every aspect of obedience, worship, and the relationship between a creature and the Creator.

Context: the Messiah’s position and rejection of exaggeration

The preceding verse says:

يَا أَهْلَ الْكِتَابِ لَا تَغْلُوا فِي دِينِكُمْ وَلَا تَقُولُوا عَلَى اللَّهِ إِلَّا الْحَقَّ

yā ahla al-kitābi lā taghlū fī dīnikum wa-lā taqūlū ʿalā allāhi illā al-ḥaqqa

Translation: “People of the Book, do not exaggerate in your religion, and say nothing about The God except the truth.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:171

The same verse goes on to describe the Messiah, Jesus son of Mary, as The God’s messenger and rejects attributing a child to The God.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:172 therefore continues the discussion of the Messiah’s position.

By saying that the Messiah does not disdain being a servant, the Qur’an clarifies his relationship to The God.

Istinkāf here concerns refusing the position of servanthood out of a sense of indignity or self-importance. The Qur’an denies such a condition of the Messiah.

This does not make every expression of respect, affection, or honor toward the Messiah an instance of istinkāf.

The verse concerns his position before The God, not a denial of his mission, dignity, or worth.

Someone questioning or researching a verse’s meaning or the Messiah’s position is also not a mustankif merely because they ask questions.

Istinkāf concerns refusal and rejection, not the effort to understand.

«وَلَا الْمَلَائِكَةُ الْمُقَرَّبُونَ»

The Qur’an mentions the angels brought near after the Messiah.

The construction shows that the denial of istinkāf also applies to them.

They too do not disdain serving The God.

Their nearness and special standing before The God do not lead them to consider servanthood unsuitable for themselves.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:19 likewise says:

وَمَنْ عِنْدَهُ لَا يَسْتَكْبِرُونَ عَنْ عِبَادَتِهِ وَلَا يَسْتَحْسِرُونَ

wa-man ʿindahu lā yastakbirūna ʿan ʿibādatihi wa-lā yastaḥsirūna

Translation: “Those in His presence do not act arrogantly toward His service, nor do they grow weary.”

Describing those in The God’s presence, this expresses the same theme through K B R.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:26 also says:

بَلْ عِبَادٌ مُّكْرَمُونَ

bal ʿibādun mukramūna

“Rather, they are honored servants.”

Servanthood and being honored are thus not opposed in the Qur’an.

The order in which the Messiah and the angels are mentioned in Al-Nisāʾ 4:172 does not alone establish a definitive hierarchy between angels and prophets.

That question needs independent evidence; N K F cannot resolve it.

«وَمَنْ يَسْتَنْكِفْ عَنْ عِبَادَتِهِ»

The second part of the verse says:

وَمَنْ يَسْتَنْكِفْ عَنْ عِبَادَتِهِ وَيَسْتَكْبِرْ

wa-man yastankif ʿan ʿibādatihi wa-yastakbir

“Whoever disdains His worship and is arrogant.”

Yastankif is an imperfect verb in the jussive within a conditional construction.

The preposition ʿan identifies what the person refuses: His worship.

In the first part, the object of istinkāf was expressed through an yakūna ʿabdan lillāhi.

Here, the same concept is expressed through a different construction, ʿan ʿibādatihi.

This difference should not become a rule that istinkāf without a preposition always means refusing a position, while istinkāf with ʿan always means abandoning a bodily action.

Both constructions involve rejection and refusal; the accompanying phrase identifies their object.

4. Istinkāf and istikbār: close but distinct concepts

Al-Nisāʾ 4:172 says:

وَمَنْ يَسْتَنْكِفْ عَنْ عِبَادَتِهِ وَيَسْتَكْبِرْ

wa-man yastankif ʿan ʿibādatihi wa-yastakbir

Al-Nisāʾ 4:173 says:

وَأَمَّا الَّذِينَ اسْتَنْكَفُوا وَاسْتَكْبَرُوا

wa-ammā alladhīna istankafū wa-istakbarū

Istinkāf and istikbār appear together in both expressions.

Their co-occurrence helps explain the distinction between the roots.

Istinkāf: refusal and a sense of indignity

In recognized use, istinkāf concerns rejecting or refraining from an action that someone regards as beneath their dignity or unsuitable.

The object may be an activity, responsibility, or position.

Someone may refuse a useful task not because of inability or because it is wrong, but because they consider it beneath their standing.

This is a clear example of istinkāf.

Istikbār: magnifying oneself

Istikbār belongs to K B R and concerns regarding oneself as great or claiming superiority.

In blameworthy Qur’anic uses, an arrogant person considers themselves above accepting truth or a divine command.

Istikbār can thus prepare the ground for istinkāf.

Someone who considers themselves above others may refuse an activity they regard as suitable for those others.

That relationship does not make the words fully synonymous.

Istinkāf emphasizes rejection and refusal; istikbār emphasizes self-importance and claims of superiority.

Is every act of refraining istinkāf?

No.

Someone may refrain from an action because they consider it wrong.

They may refuse a command because it demands injustice or something contrary to truth.

Such refusal is not inherently blameworthy istinkāf.

Someone may also fail to do something because of genuine inability. Inability differs from disdain and self-importance.

Qur’anic istinkāf concerns serving The God: refusing a relationship the verse regards as right and fitting.

Its blameworthiness follows from the combination of its meaning, object, and context.

Not everyone resisting a human command should therefore be called disdainful or arrogant.

This distinction matters particularly in social relationships: obedience to The God is not unconditional obedience to every human authority.

Does joining the words imply complete separation?

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:172 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:173, istinkāf and istikbār are joined by wa, “and.”

The conjunction shows that the Qur’an uses two different expressions.

Their separate appearance does not establish that they have no semantic overlap.

The conjunction alone also cannot create two entirely separate groups: one that only disdains and another that is only arrogant.

Both descriptions characterize the behavior being criticized.

Their distinction must be established by examining meanings and uses, not merely the presence of a conjunction.

Ghāfir 40:60 says:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ يَسْتَكْبِرُونَ عَنْ عِبَادَتِي سَيَدْخُلُونَ جَهَنَّمَ دَاخِرِينَ

inna alladhīna yastakbirūna ʿan ʿibādatī sa-yadkhulūna jahannama dākhirīna

Translation: “Those who are arrogant toward My worship will enter Hell in humiliation.”

Istikbār can therefore also appear directly with ʿan ʿibādatī.

Istinkāf and istikbār are close in this setting, but each highlights an aspect of the behavior.

5. Al-Nisāʾ 4:173: the consequences of disdain and arrogance

فَأَمَّا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَعَمِلُوا الصَّالِحَاتِ فَيُوَفِّيهِمْ أُجُورَهُمْ وَيَزِيدُهُمْ مِنْ فَضْلِهِ وَأَمَّا الَّذِينَ اسْتَنْكَفُوا وَاسْتَكْبَرُوا فَيُعَذِّبُهُمْ عَذَابًا أَلِيمًا وَلَا يَجِدُونَ لَهُمْ مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ وَلِيًّا وَلَا نَصِيرًا

fa-ammā alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti fa-yuwaffīhim ujūrahum wa-yazīduhum min faḍlihi wa-ammā alladhīna istankafū wa-istakbarū fa-yuʿadhdhibuhum ʿadhāban alīman wa-lā yajidūna lahum min dūni allāhi waliyyan wa-lā naṣīran

Forqan’s rendering:

“As for those who believe and do righteous deeds, He will give them their rewards in full and add to them from His bounty. But those who disdain and are arrogant, He will punish with a painful punishment, and they will find for themselves neither a guardian nor a helper besides The God.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:173

Istankafū here is a third-person masculine plural perfect verb of Form X.

This is the third and final Qur’anic occurrence of N K F.

Istinkāf appears beside istikbār, contrasted with belief and righteous deeds.

Two contrasting situations

The verse first speaks of those who believe and do righteous deeds.

They are promised full payment of reward and an addition from divine bounty.

The contrasting group is described as having disdained and acted arrogantly.

The refusal criticized is thus more than a passing feeling or inner discomfort. It concerns the human relationship with truth and service to The God.

Istankafū itself does not establish precisely which act these people abandoned or in what specific situation they refused a divine command.

The preceding verse clarifies the object of their behavior: serving The God.

Details of each person’s or group’s behavior require other evidence.

Does istinkāf mean lack of faith?

Those who believe and do righteous deeds are contrasted here with those who disdain and are arrogant.

Arrogantly refusing to serve The God is thus placed in opposition to belief and righteous action.

N K F itself, however, does not mean disbelief or lack of faith.

Someone may doubt a truth or not yet understand it sufficiently without regarding its acceptance as undignified.

Conversely, someone may recognize something’s truth but refuse to accept it out of pride or self-importance.

The situations differ in intention, knowledge, and moral responsibility.

Istinkāf should therefore not mean merely rejecting a claim or holding a different belief from others.

The setting of servanthood and self-importance is decisive in the Qur’anic usage.

Does istinkāf always lead to punishment?

Al-Nisāʾ 4:173 describes punishment for people who, in context, disdained serving The God and acted arrogantly.

That judgment must be understood with those descriptions and the object of their behavior.

The Arabic root N K F does not always describe behavior deserving punishment.

Wiping tears from the cheek or changing course, for example, are uses of the family without a fixed moral judgment.

Even in the human act of refusal, people may rightly refrain from wrongdoing.

Punishment therefore does not come from the root itself, but from the particular behavior and divine judgment in the verse.

Repentance and reform

The verse discusses the fate of people described by istinkāf and istikbār.

This does not establish that everyone who once refused truth has permanently lost the opportunity to return and reform.

Other Qur’anic verses discuss repentance, forgiveness, and returning.

Judging people’s ultimate responsibility therefore also requires examining behavior, knowledge, persistence in error, and the possibility of reform.

This belongs to a wider study of repentance, disbelief, arrogance, and human responsibility.

The central point here is that the istinkāf criticized in these two verses concerns refusing to serve The God and its association with arrogance.

6. N K F and related roots

N K F and ʾ B Y

Abā in Arabic concerns rejecting and refusing.

Istinkāf is also a kind of refusal, but in recognized use involves considering an action or position undignified or objectionable.

Al-Baqarah 2:34 says:

إِلَّا إِبْلِيسَ أَبَىٰ وَاسْتَكْبَرَ وَكَانَ مِنَ الْكَافِرِينَ

illā iblīsa abā wa-istakbara wa-kāna mina al-kāfirīna

Translation: “Except Iblīs: he refused, was arrogant, and was among the disbelievers.”

Abā and istakbara appear together here.

Their relationship between refusal and arrogance resembles Al-Nisāʾ 4:172.

That similarity should not make abā and istankafa fully synonymous.

Abā can emphasize refusal itself, while istankafa commonly also highlights a sense of indignity or unwillingness to accept.

Refusal may be right or wrong, depending on its object and cause.

Study of ʾ B Y

N K F and K B R

These families appear together in Al-Nisāʾ 4:172 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:173.

Istinkāf emphasizes refusal and rejection.

Istikbār emphasizes self-importance and claims of superiority.

Istikbār may cause istinkāf: considering oneself above accepting something may lead to refusing it.

Arrogance can also appear in speech, behavior, and other relationships without a particular act of refusal occurring at that moment.

Istinkāf, conversely, concerns someone’s relationship to an action or position they do not accept.

The meanings are close, but one should not be absorbed into the other.

Study of K B R

N K F and ʾ N F

Anifa mina al-shayʾi in Arabic concerns regarding something as beneath one’s dignity.

Istinkāf has often been explained through anifa.

The words are thus very close in the field of disdain and unwillingness to accept an action.

Semantic closeness does not establish historical identity between the roots.

Early explanations also depict disdain through raising the nose or turning the face away.

That image can make arrogant behavior tangible, but does not alone establish a word’s historical origin.

Study of ʾ N F

N K F and ʿ R Ḍ: turning away

Aʿraḍa is a Form IV verb from ʿ R Ḍ, not a four-letter root. In familiar uses, it concerns turning away and distancing oneself from something.

Istinkāf may also accompany turning away.

Not every act of turning away is istinkāf.

Someone may turn away from a statement because they consider it irrelevant or harmful, without disdain or self-importance motivating them.

Istinkāf gives greater prominence to rejecting something as objectionable or beneath one’s standing.

Turning away may therefore be an outward manifestation of istinkāf, but has a broader independent meaning.

Study of ʿ R Ḍ

N K F and N K B

Nakaba ʿani al-ṭarīqi concerns deviating from a road and leaving a course.

This approaches some forms of N K F, such as intikāf in the sense of changing course.

Similar sounds and a shared idea of moving aside do not alone establish a direct historical relationship between the roots.

Istinkāf in Al-Nisāʾ 4:172 does not mean physical movement away from a road, but refusal to serve The God.

A spatial meaning from a neighboring root should not be imposed on another root’s moral and social use.

7. Proposed translations for Forqan

Physical uses should be distinguished from uses concerning refusal.

For nakafa al-damʿa, “wiped away the tears” or “removed the tears from the cheek” is suitable.

For intakafa concerning a course, “left the course” or “changed direction” is natural.

For nakifa ʿani al-amri, “regarded the undertaking as beneath his dignity” and “refused it” are appropriate.

For istankafa, “refused,” “disdained accepting it,” or “considered himself above it and refused” can be used, according to context.

In Persian Qur’anic translation, “refusing” is best retained as the basic equivalent: it is natural and conveys rejection without placing the entire psychological analysis inside one word.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:172: first occurrence

لَنْ يَسْتَنْكِفَ الْمَسِيحُ أَنْ يَكُونَ عَبْدًا لِلَّهِ

lan yastankifa al-masīḥu an yakūna ʿabdan lillāhi

Forqan’s rendering:

“The Messiah will never disdain being a servant of The God.”

This rendering is appropriate.

Explanation may add that the Messiah does not consider the position undignified or unsuitable for himself.

Adding “out of arrogance” to the verb’s independent meaning is unnecessary, since the Qur’an mentions istikbār separately in the following part.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:172: second occurrence

وَمَنْ يَسْتَنْكِفْ عَنْ عِبَادَتِهِ وَيَسْتَكْبِرْ

wa-man yastankif ʿan ʿibādatihi wa-yastakbir

Forqan’s rendering:

“Whoever disdains His worship and is arrogant.”

This preserves the distinction between the verbs.

“Disdains” renders istinkāf, while “is arrogant” renders istikbār.

Both should not be rendered simply as “is arrogant,” since that would eliminate one Qur’anic expression.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:173

وَأَمَّا الَّذِينَ اسْتَنْكَفُوا وَاسْتَكْبَرُوا

wa-ammā alladhīna istankafū wa-istakbarū

Forqan’s rendering:

“But those who disdain and are arrogant.”

This rendering is also appropriate.

Context clarifies that the refusal concerns serving The God, as stated in the preceding verse.

Brief root meaning and meanings in the verses

Brief root meaning: setting aside and removing; in the human use of istinkāf, refusing an action or position because it is regarded as undignified or objectionable.

Lan yastankifa in Al-Nisāʾ 4:172: will not disdain; the Messiah does not regard being a servant of The God as undignified or unsuitable.

Yastankif in Al-Nisāʾ 4:172: disdains; in context, turns away from His worship and does not accept it.

Istankafū in Al-Nisāʾ 4:173: disdained; describes people whose refusal also accompanies arrogance.

These explanations should preserve the distinction between refusal, the motive of disdain, and the separate description of arrogance.

8. Conclusion

N K F has several semantic branches in Arabic.

The physical branch concerns setting aside, removing, or cutting something off, as with wiping tears from the cheek and the ending of a flow of water or rain.

In movement and behavior, it describes leaving a course or turning toward another undertaking.

The established human use of istinkāf concerns refusing an action or position regarded as objectionable or beneath one’s dignity.

Nakfah and nukāf also name bodily parts and a disease of that region. Without historical evidence, this branch should not be turned into the Qur’anic meaning of istinkāf.

The Qur’an uses only Form X of this root. All three occurrences are in Al-Nisāʾ 4:172 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:173.

The first verse denies that the Messiah and the angels brought near disdain serving The God. Their standing and dignity are thus compatible with servanthood.

The Qur’an then discusses people who disdain serving The God and are arrogant. The following verse contrasts this behavior with belief and righteous deeds.

The co-occurrence of istinkāf and istikbār shows their relationship, but does not make their meanings identical.

Istinkāf emphasizes rejection and refusal; istikbār emphasizes self-importance and claims of superiority.

Istinkāf should therefore not be translated merely as arrogance, nor should every refusal or disagreement be called istinkāf.

Someone may refrain from an action because of inability, ignorance, caution, or a belief that a request is wrong. That differs from refusing truth out of disdain or self-importance.

The Qur’anic criticism concerns disdaining service to The God and its association with arrogance, not every act of refraining or questioning a claim.

Final definition: in its physical branch, N K F denotes setting aside, removing, and in some uses cutting something off. In human behavior, istinkāf concerns refusing an action or position because it is regarded as undignified, objectionable, or beneath oneself. The Qur’an uses the root only in this human sense, concerning refusal to serve The God. Istinkāf may accompany istikbār, but they should not be fully equated: refusal and self-importance are related yet distinct aspects of human behavior.
