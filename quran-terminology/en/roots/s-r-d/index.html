Central meaning: bringing things successively and linking components into a continuous whole. Physical uses include piercing and stitching leather, especially making armor from linked rings. In speech, it denotes successive, continuous narration. The Quran uses this root only once, in God’s command to David concerning armor: wa-qaddir fī al-sard, “observe measure in linking the rings.”

1. The root in early and pre-Quranic Arabic

Arabic S R D concerns continuity, succession, and connection between components.

In familiar uses, separate things follow one another to form a continuous string, weave, or whole.

The connection may be physical, as in stitching leather, joining metal rings, or making armor.

It may also occur in speech or time, as sentences follow one another or actions continue successively.

Early Arabic records several familiar expressions:

Sarada al-dirʿa: he joined an armor’s rings and made it.

Sarada al-adīma: he pierced and stitched leather.

Sarada al-ḥadītha: he delivered speech continuously, one part after another.

Sarada al-shayʾa: he brought one thing after another.

Continuity and succession are shared features.

Continuity does not invariably mean strength or correctness.

Sentences may follow rapidly while their contents are false.

Parts may likewise follow one another without sufficiently strong connections.

Continuity itself must therefore be distinguished from its quality.

This matters in the Quran: wa-qaddir fī al-sard concerns not only making armor but also measure and proportion in its construction.

Pre-Islamic evidence: misrad in Tarafa ibn al-ʿAbd’s Muʿallaqa

The pre-Islamic poet Tarafa ibn al-ʿAbd describes a camel in his celebrated Muʿallaqa:

كَأَنَّ جَنَاحَيْ مَضْرَحِيٍّ تَكَنَّفَا
حِفَافَيْهِ شُكَّا فِي الْعَسِيبِ بِمِسْرَدِ

ka-anna janāḥay maḍraḥiyyin takannafā / ḥifāfayhi shukkā fī al-ʿasībi bi-misradi

Translation:

“As though the two wings of a great bird flanked its tail, fastened into the tailbone with an awl.”

He describes the camel’s tail through an image of a bird’s wings.

Maḍraḥiyy names or describes a large bird; early explanations identify a vulture or a long-winged bird of prey.

Ḥifāfayhi denotes the two sides of something.

Al-ʿasīb is the tailbone or central part of the tail.

Shukkā here concerns piercing, fastening, and joining.

Misrad denotes a piercing and stitching tool.

The image therefore concerns something pierced and fastened with a pointed implement.

Misrad here is a tool, not armor itself.

Nor does it mean a bird’s wing or a tailbone.

Its connection with S R D lies in piercing and joining.

The example matters historically because it belongs to Tarafa’s established Muʿallaqa and the Arabic poetic tradition attributed to pre-Islamic times.

Although written collections of pre-Islamic poetry were compiled later, its transmission in established versions and early commentaries provides substantial evidence for this use of misrad.

What does the example establish historically?

Tarafa’s example attests the family’s association with piercing and joining in early Arabic.

With leather, piercing usually prepares a passage for thread or a thong to join its parts.

Some armor likewise requires parts or rings to be connected into a continuous covering.

The verse alone does not establish armor-making as the origin of every use of sard.

It attests a piercing tool, not the precise history of every meaning.

A fuller definition also requires other uses and the attested armor-making sense.

Evidence near the revelation period: masrūd armor

Abu Dhuʾayb al-Hudhali, who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, says:

وَعَلَيْهِمَا مَسْرُودَتَانِ قَضَاهُمَا
دَاوُدُ أَوْ صَنَعُ السَّوَابِغِ تُبَّعُ

wa-ʿalayhimā masrūdatāni qaḍāhumā / Dāwūdu aw ṣanaʿu al-sawābighi Tubbaʿu

Translation:

“Upon the two were two linked coats of mail, completed by David or by Tubbaʿ, the skilled maker of ample armor.”

Masrūdatāni is the feminine dual passive participle.

Its noun is omitted; context supplies two coats of armor.

Masrūd describes them as made from linked rings or connected components.

This concerns their structure, not merely their iron material or weight.

Because Abu Dhuʾayb also lived in the Islamic period, the verse cannot be treated as independent pre-Islamic evidence without establishing its date.

Nor does its mention of David independently prove the history of armor-making in his time. Poetic attribution of an object to a celebrated figure is not itself a historical document, and the poem’s precise date requires separate evidence.

Its lexical value is in showing masrūd used of armor.

Historical definition

Taken together, Tarafa’s example, armor imagery in early poetry, and recorded Arabic meanings support this account:

Physically, S R D concerns piercing, stitching, and linking components in a continuous string or weave. In armor-making, it concerns joining rings and components. More broadly, it also expresses successive speech or action.

Continuity and connection provide a common semantic field.

The available evidence alone does not establish the precise historical order of stitching, armor-making, and continuous narration.

2. Important forms and derivatives

Sarada, yasrudu

The basic Form I verb.

Physically, it denotes piercing, stitching, and joining components.

سَرَدَ الدِّرْعَ

sarada al-dirʿa

“He made armor from linked components.”

سَرَدَ الأَدِيمَ

sarada al-adīma

“He pierced and stitched leather.”

In speech, sarada al-ḥadītha denotes bringing utterances one after another.

This verb form does not occur in the Quran.

Sard

A verbal noun and noun in this family.

Generally, it concerns continuity and succession of parts.

In armor-making, it denotes making and joining rings and sometimes the rings or connections themselves.

It is the only form used in the Quran.

Sarrada, yusarridu

The Form II verb.

In leatherwork and armor-making, it concerns piercing and joining components.

Form II can foreground the making and joining activity.

Its form alone does not establish great speed, intense pressure, or a specific tool in every case.

Tasrīd

The Form II verbal noun.

With leather, it concerns piercing and stitching.

In armor-making, it can concern joining and arranging rings.

This verbal noun does not occur in the Quran in this form.

Misrad

A piercing and stitching tool.

It can be translated as an awl or a piercing tool for stitching.

It is the form found in Tarafa’s Muʿallaqa.

There it denotes the tool, not armor or its collection of rings.

Sirād

Also recorded for a piercing and stitching tool.

The relevant lexical explanation identifies sirād with a misrad or awl: an implement used to stitch leather.

The expression “that with which stitching is done” does not, by itself, establish a separate meaning of thread or thong.

Without independent evidence, the documented tool sense should be preserved rather than adding thread as an established meaning.

Sarrād

Someone who makes armor or joins its rings.

Some uses also concern a leather stitcher.

The occupational title therefore depends on the person’s work.

Masrūd

The passive participle of Form I.

With armor, it describes a coat whose rings or components have been joined.

دِرْعٌ مَسْرُودَةٌ

dirʿun masrūdatun

“A coat of armor with interlinked rings.”

With leather, it can describe something pierced and stitched.

Musarrad

The passive participle of Form II.

In stitching and armor-making, it describes parts joined through stitching or connection.

Masrūd and musarrad are close in many uses.

Asrada

A Form IV verb also recorded in the date-palm branch.

Asrada al-nakhlu concerns the condition of the palm’s fruit: dates affected by thirst that dry before ripening. It should not be automatically treated as the verb for joining armor rings.

This verb does not occur in the Quran.

Sarāda

Sarāda, without a doubled rāʾ, is recorded in the date-fruit vocabulary. Al-khalāla al-ṣulba here describes a hard, unripe date, not necessarily a piercing or stitching implement.

Other descriptions concern a date becoming sweet before coloring, falling while still green, or drying from thirst before ripening.

These botanical uses should not be identified with a stitching tool without evidence, and they are absent from the Quran.

Speech and succession

Sard al-ḥadīth means bringing speech successively.

Sard al-ṣawm concerns fasting on successive days.

Spatial or physical continuity gives way to temporal succession in these uses.

Words follow one another, or fasting days continue in sequence. This does not by itself mean omitting the nightly breaking of the fast.

The root itself does not establish the truth of a narrative or an obligation to fast continuously.

Those questions require independent evidence.

The actual Quranic form

S R D occurs only once in the Quran:

Al-sardi in Sabaʾ 34:11.

It is a definite noun in the genitive after fī.

Al-sard occurs in an armor-making context.

The expressions for narration, leather stitching, and stitching tools do not appear in the Quran in those forms.

3. Wa-qaddir fī al-sard: observing measure in making armor

Sabaʾ 34:11 says:

أَنِ اعْمَلْ سَابِغَاتٍ وَقَدِّرْ فِي السَّرْدِ وَاعْمَلُوا صَالِحًا إِنِّي بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ بَصِيرٌ

ani iʿmal sābighātin wa-qaddir fī al-sardi wa-iʿmalū ṣāliḥan innī bi-mā taʿmalūna baṣīrun

Sabaʾ 34:11

Translation:

“Make ample coats of mail and observe measure in linking the rings; and do righteous work. Indeed, I see what you do.”

The singular command iʿmal addresses David.

Sābighāt denotes ample, fully covering coats of mail.

Qaddir is an imperative from Q D R.

Fī al-sard identifies the work in which measure and proportion must be observed.

Al-sard in this verse

In Arabic, the sard of armor concerns joining its rings.

Mail consists of small metal components arranged into a continuous body covering.

Its function depends on their connections.

Separate rings do not form a continuous covering.

Weak connections may come apart in use.

Poorly arranged parts can make armor heavy, ill-fitting, or ineffective.

Sard thus concerns an important aspect of manufacture: joining and arranging the components of the finished covering.

The word itself does not express every engineering feature of armor.

Measure, proportion, and precision are understood from qaddir and the nature of the work.

What does qaddir add?

Qaddir concerns measuring and observing an appropriate amount.

God commands David not merely to make armor but to observe measure in its sard.

This distinction matters.

Producing an object is one consideration.

Making it properly with appropriate dimensions and proportions is another.

Ring size, metal thickness, joining technique, and overall proportions can all affect mail’s performance.

The verse does not specify the rings’ diameter, thickness, or exact connection type.

It expresses the principle of measure and proportion in the work.

It supplies no numerical specifications or detailed manufacturing method.

Rings and rivets in lexical explanations

Early explanations give several related readings of wa-qaddir fī al-sard.

Some connect sard with armor rings.

Others foreground their holes and joining points.

Others relate it to the rivets or pins connecting them.

One technical explanation says an overly thick pin and a very narrow hole may break the ring.

An overly thin pin and wide hole, conversely, leave the connection loose.

This accords with observing measure and proportion.

These are technical explanations of a particular construction method.

Sabaʾ 34:11 does not explicitly name rivets, hole diameters, or ring thickness.

Technical explanation should therefore not replace the verse’s wording in translation.

Does al-sard mean only a rivet?

Restricting it to rivets is one lexical and technical reading.

The family’s range is broader than one metal component.

Sard is recorded for stitching leather, making armor, and joining rings.

“Linking the rings” or “constructing the armor’s rings” therefore provides an appropriately broader rendering in Sabaʾ 34:11.

Rivets and holes may belong to that process without exhausting the word’s meaning.

Does al-sard mean weaving cloth?

Lexical explanations sometimes describe making mail with nasj, weaving.

The image is intelligible because armor rings form a continuous arrangement like elements of a weave.

Mail need not be constructed like fabric made from thread, warp, and weft.

Its construction concerns metal connections.

If sard is translated as “weaving armor,” the reader should understand interlinked metal rings rather than ordinary cloth.

“Linking the rings” can therefore be clearer for Persian readers.

4. Connections with other verses about David

Two other verses bear directly on al-sard in Sabaʾ 34:11.

Sabaʾ 34:10: iron made soft for David

Sabaʾ 34:10 says:

وَأَلَنَّا لَهُ الْحَدِيدَ

wa-alannā lahu al-ḥadīda

Sabaʾ 34:10

Translation:

“And We made iron soft for him.”

This immediately precedes the command to make armor.

The context clearly connects softened iron with armor-making.

Iron can be used for tools and protective coverings.

It must be shaped appropriately to produce rings or other armor components.

Iron being made soft for David helps explain his ability to make armor.

The verse does not describe the precise means of softening it.

The phrase alone does not establish shaping without heat, without tools, or by a particular technique.

Such details require other reliable evidence.

Alannā itself belongs to L Y N.

It means neither cooling nor linking rings. Arabic sard should also be distinguished from Persian sard, “cold.”

Al-sard in the next verse names the work of connecting and constructing armor.

Softening iron and constructing mail are distinct expressions connected within one process.

Al-Anbiyaʾ 21:80: teaching the making of protective clothing

Al-Anbiyaʾ 21:80 says:

وَعَلَّمْنَاهُ صَنْعَةَ لَبُوسٍ لَكُمْ لِتُحْصِنَكُمْ مِنْ بَأْسِكُمْ فَهَلْ أَنْتُمْ شَاكِرُونَ

wa-ʿallamnāhu ṣanʿata labūsin lakum li-tuḥṣinakum min baʾsikum fa-hal antum shākirūna

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:80

Translation:

“And We taught him the making of protective clothing for you, to shield you from the violence of your fighting. Will you then be grateful?”

This verse concerns David being taught to make labūs.

Labūs belongs to L B S and relates to what is worn.

Context also specifies this covering’s function.

Its purpose is protection from harm in fighting.

Labūs here therefore concerns protective clothing or armor.

It directly relates to Sabaʾ 34:11.

One verse concerns teaching the manufacture of protective clothing.

The other concerns ample coats of mail and observing measure in their construction.

Was David the first armor-maker?

The Quran states that David was taught to make protective clothing and that iron was made soft for him.

It does not explicitly say that no one before him had made armor or any military protection.

Nor does it say that every historical type of armor was first invented by him.

These verses alone cannot settle the worldwide history of armor’s invention.

They state his possession of this ability and the command to make suitable armor.

Distinguishing an instrument of war from its purpose

Al-Anbiyaʾ 21:80 identifies protecting people from the harm of fighting as its purpose.

Armor here is a means of defense and protection.

The noun armor and the action sard alone cannot establish every rule concerning war, defense, or military equipment.

Those questions require their relevant verses.

Making protective equipment is nevertheless a real, material activity.

Al-sard should not be reduced here to arranging speech, writing laws, or spiritual bonds: iron, armor, and protective clothing establish a physical referent.

In Sabaʾ 34:11, al-sard concerns making and joining armor rings. Al-Anbiyaʾ 21:80 clarifies the protective function of this craft.

5. From linked rings to continuous speech

Arabic sard also applies to successive speech and action, beyond armor-making.

The semantic connection is intelligible as an image.

In armor, one ring connects to another.

In speech, one sentence follows another.

In continuous activity, one stage follows another.

The uses must nevertheless remain distinct.

Continuous speech and narration

Sarada al-ḥadītha means delivering speech successively.

In narration, sard concerns the flow of expression and continuity between sentences.

“Narration” or “successive telling” captures this Arabic use; Persian sard meaning “cold” should not be confused with it.

Sard does not itself establish a narrative’s truth.

Someone may tell a real event coherently.

They may tell a false account with equal skill.

Continuity of expression and truth of content are different questions.

Continuous speech is not necessarily beautiful or precise either.

Those qualities require separate evidence.

Does Quranic sard mean narration?

No.

Sabaʾ 34:11 concerns armor-making.

Iron and ample armor occur in the same context.

Importing modern “story narration” into it substitutes another use for the contextual meaning.

Continuity belongs to both fields, but its kind differs.

Narration concerns connected speech.

Armor concerns metal connections and a protective covering’s structure.

Succession without proportion

The general meaning gives importance to successive components.

Succession alone does not ensure quality.

Armor rings may be connected yet poorly sized.

Speech may be continuous yet ordered in a way that causes ambiguity or misunderstanding.

Qaddir matters in Sabaʾ 34:11 because it calls for measure and proportion in sard.

The verse joins continuity with measurement.

Their association does not make precise measurement part of every use of sard.

Measurement is expressed by Q D R, not S R D itself.

6. Distinctions from related roots

S R D (sard) and N S J (nasj)

Nasj concerns weaving or interlacing strands or components.

Armor-making sard concerns connecting rings.

Both can concern forming a continuous whole.

Hence the lexical expression nasj ḥalaq al-dirʿ, “weaving the rings of armor.”

Not every weave should be called sard.

Nasj can apply to cloth and soft threads.

Sard in Sabaʾ 34:11 concerns armor rings.

The subject and material help determine the sense.

S R D (sard) and KH R Z (kharz)

Kharz concerns piercing and stitching leather or other parts.

Sard too is recorded for piercing and joining leather.

The families overlap here.

With armor, sard can also denote the rings and their arrangement of connections.

Kharz is therefore not a complete substitute in every use.

Tarafa’s pre-Islamic example uses misrad for a piercing and stitching tool.

It illustrates this semantic proximity.

S R D (sard) and Z R D (zard)

Arabic zard also concerns armor rings.

Zarrād denotes an armor-maker.

Sarrād and zarrād are sometimes treated as closely related forms.

Some lexical analyses explain the difference through alternation of sīn and zāy.

This matters for the occupational term and armor-making vocabulary.

It does not establish that all forms with s or z across languages share one certain historical development.

The Quran has al-sard with sīn.

That form should be preserved in Quranic root study.

S R D (sard) and S B GH (sābigh)

With coverings, S B GH concerns fullness and ample coverage.

Sard concerns joining armor components.

Both families occur in Sabaʾ 34:11.

Sābighāt expresses the armor’s ample coverage.

Al-sard concerns making and joining its rings.

Their meanings differ.

Armor may cover the body amply while having poor connections.

Its rings may conversely be well joined while leaving necessary body areas insufficiently covered.

Coverage and proportionate construction are thus distinct aspects of the verse.

S R D (sard) and Q D R (taqdīr)

Q D R here concerns measurement and determining appropriate amounts.

Sard concerns constructing and joining rings.

Qaddir fī al-sard requires measure to be observed during armor-making.

Taqdīr concerns how the work is performed; sard identifies the work.

The construction does not make measurement the lexical meaning of sard itself.

S R D (sard) and S R D Q (surādiq)

Surādiq occurs in al-Kahf 18:29.

It concerns a curtain, enclosing canopy, or surrounding barrier.

The morphological index treats S R D Q separately from S R D.

The shared first three letters do not establish identical roots or meanings.

Al-sard in Sabaʾ 34:11 should therefore not be translated as a canopy or enclosure of curtains.

7. The verse-specific meaning and conclusion

In Sabaʾ 34:11, an appropriate rendering of:

وَقَدِّرْ فِي السَّرْدِ

wa-qaddir fī al-sardi

Sabaʾ 34:11

is:

“And observe measure in linking the rings.”

This has two advantages.

It preserves al-sard’s armor-making context.

It attributes measurement and proportion to qaddir, not sard itself.

It avoids inserting exact rivet dimensions, hole diameters, or a particular construction method into the direct translation.

For a Forqan word box:

Exact word: al-sardi (السَّرْدِ).

Root: S R D.

Brief root meaning: “Bringing successively and linking components; physically, stitching and joining armor rings.”

Meaning in this verse: “Joining and arranging armor rings during manufacture, with measure and proportion required by the accompanying command.”

Findings

Early Arabic S R D concerns piercing, stitching, connecting parts, and forming a continuous whole.

Misrad in Tarafa’s Muʿallaqa denotes a piercing and stitching tool.

It shows the physical branch in poetry attributed to pre-Islamic times.

Abu Dhuʾayb’s masrūdatāni describes two linked coats of mail, although it cannot be treated without qualification as independent pre-Quranic evidence.

Continuous speech and successive actions also belong to the family’s recorded uses.

The Quran uses S R D only once, for armor-making.

Sabaʾ 34:10 mentions iron being made soft for David.

Sabaʾ 34:11 commands ample armor and measure in joining its rings.

Al-Anbiyaʾ 21:80 describes teaching protective manufacture and its function against the harm of fighting.

Together they establish a real, material craft involving iron, protective clothing, and manufacturing skill.

The Quran does not specify the exact method, ring dimensions, or tools.

Nor do the verses explicitly establish David as the first maker of all historical armor.

In Sabaʾ 34:11, wa-iʿmalū ṣāliḥan, “do righteous work,” follows the commands about armor and measure. The wording shifts from singular commands to a plural address.

Technical ability and manufacture thus occur in a context of responsibility and righteous action.

Righteous action is not the lexical meaning of sard.

Making and connecting rings comes from sard, measure and proportion from qaddir, and righteousness from the separate command wa-iʿmalū ṣāliḥan.

S R D concerns bringing components successively and joining them into a continuous string or whole. Its Quranic use concerns joining and arranging armor rings.

The verse’s point is clear: connecting parts alone is insufficient; their dimensions and proportions should suit effective armor.

For Forqan, “linking the armor’s rings” accurately conveys al-sard. Details about rivets, holes, and joining methods belong in study and interpretation rather than the direct verse translation.
