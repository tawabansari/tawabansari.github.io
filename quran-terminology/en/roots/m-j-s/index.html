Root م ج س — Majūs, the name of a religious community

Qur’anic and lexical forms

الْمَجُوسَ
(al-Majūsa)
Community name; al-Ḥajj 22:17

From the Magi of ancient Iran to the Qur’an’s identification of the Majūs, and the distinction between a community’s name and judgment of its members

In Arabic, Majūs names a religious community historically associated with the religions of ancient Iran, particularly the Zoroastrian tradition. Unlike many Qur’anic roots, this word is originally a group name, rather than a verb from which a general meaning such as going, knowing, worshipping, or denying can be derived.

Majūs occurs only once in the Qur’an, in al-Ḥajj 22:17. They are named alongside believers, Jews, Sabians, Christians, and those who associate partners, and God assigns the final judgment between these groups to the Day of Resurrection.

The important point is that the Qur’an identifies the Majūs here by their recognized name without explaining their beliefs, rituals, scriptures, or the origins of their religion.

Understanding the word therefore requires keeping two questions separate: whom the name designated in language and history, and what the Qur’an says about that community.

Historically, م ج س also differs from native Arabic derivational roots. Majūs is a word of Iranian origin that entered Arabic, where verbs and other derivatives were subsequently formed from it.

1. The historical meaning of Majūs

In ancient Iranian languages, forms resembling maguš and magu designated people holding religious office and conducting religious rites.

Historical forms of the name are attested in Old Persian and Mesopotamian documents. One of its earliest datable attestations is the Behistun inscription from the reign of Darius I in the sixth century BCE.

In that inscription, a person named Gaumāta bears a title belonging to this word family.

Greek accounts likewise use magos for people with religious roles among the Medes and Iranians.

These attestations show that the name had a long history before Islam and was initially associated with a religious and social title in ancient Iran.

Who were the Magi?

Historical accounts of the Medes and Achaemenids describe the Magi as a group involved in religious ceremonies, the preservation of religious traditions, and certain court activities.

During the Parthian and Sasanian periods, the name also designated priests of Iranian religious traditions, particularly Zoroastrianism.

The Persian word mugh remains familiar. The title mōbad also developed from the ancient family of names associated with the Magi and designates a priestly office in Zoroastrian tradition.

Nevertheless, historical study must distinguish a magus as a religious officeholder from Majūs as the name of a broader community.

Earlier uses could refer to a particular group of priests; in Arabic, however, Majūs also became a designation for adherents of a religious tradition.

This extension of usage does not prove that every member’s beliefs changed. A name can expand from a particular group to a wider society without all its members holding the same office or performing the same duties.

Is Majūs an Arabic word?

Arabic lexicons identify Majūs as a familiar community name and also note its non-Arabic origin.

Related forms are attested in Iranian languages, Aramaic, and Syriac.

The evidence places the Arabic name within an ancient word family from the cultural environment of Iran and its neighboring lands.

The precise route into Arabic is not entirely clear. It may have entered directly from an Iranian language or through an intermediary such as Aramaic.

Similar forms in several languages do not by themselves establish the direction or sequence of borrowing.

Some older Arabic dictionaries relate a story about a man with small ears and derive the name from him. This account lacks adequate historical support and cannot replace independent, earlier Iranian evidence.

A defensible historical definition of Majūs in Arabic near the period of revelation is therefore: the name of a religious community associated with the Magi of ancient Iran and Iranian religious traditions, particularly Zoroastrianism.

This definition identifies the community and its historical setting. It does not, by itself, judge the truth or falsehood of every belief and practice of its members.

2. Majūs in early Arabic poetry

Early Arabic poetry also associates the name Majūs with fire.

An account of a poetic exchange attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays and al-Tawʾam al-Yashkurī preserves this verse:

«أَصَاحِ أُرِيكَ بَرْقًا هَبَّ وَهْنًا
كَنَارِ مَجُوسَ تَسْتَعِرُ اسْتِعَارَا»

Aṣāḥi urīka barqan habba wahnan / ka-nāri majūsa tastaʿiru istiʿārā.

“Friend, I show you lightning that rose in the night, like the fire of the Majūs blazing fiercely.”

The account attributes the first hemistich to Imruʾ al-Qays and the second to al-Tawʾam al-Yashkurī.

The poet compares the lightning’s brightness and flaring to the fire of the Majūs.

The comparison shows that Arabic literary tradition associated the Majūs with a recognizable fire, suitable for describing brightness and blazing.

The exchange’s attribution and precise date are not certain, however. Its account survives in later compilations, and some details differ between versions.

This verse alone therefore cannot establish that this exact wording was composed before the Qur’an’s revelation.

It nevertheless attests the name in Arabic literary tradition and shows that its association with fire was known in historical Arabic usage.

Does Majūs mean “fire-worshipper”?

This question requires distinguishing a community’s name from descriptions of its rites.

Fire has played an important ritual role in Zoroastrianism and has been associated with light and purity.

The preservation of fire in fire temples and its presence in religious ceremonies are also well attested in the history of this tradition.

Consequently, some outsiders have called its adherents “fire-worshippers.”

Zoroastrians generally reject this label if it means worshipping fire itself as an independent deity. In their explanation of their beliefs, fire can be a sacred sign and a means of directing attention toward divine reality.

Moreover, ancient Iranian religions have a long and varied history. The beliefs of every group associated with the Magi and Majūs should not be assumed identical in every period.

Thus, “fire-worshipper” is not a lexical translation of Majūs.

Majūs is a community name. The connection between some of its rites and fire is a historical and religious question requiring separate study.

The distinction matters in Qur’anic study as well: al-Ḥajj 22:17 neither mentions fire alongside the Majūs nor calls them fire-worshippers.

3. Forms and derivatives in Arabic

The Qur’an contains only one form from the family م ج س:

الْمَجُوسَ — al-Ḥajj 22:17

This is the name of a religious community.

Classical Arabic also formed other words from it.

Majūsī designates an individual affiliated with the Majūs.

Majūsiyyah denotes their religion or religious affiliation.

Tamajjasa is recorded in the sense of becoming a Majūsī or joining the religion of the Majūs.

Majjasa is also used of someone who brings another person into that religion or raises them in it.

These verbs exemplify the formation of Arabic words and verbs from an established name.

Similarly, Arabic can form verbs from religious or social names to indicate joining a community or adopting its religion.

This process does not mean that the community’s original name necessarily arose from the Arabic verb.

For Majūs, historical attestations of the Iranian name predate the recorded Arabic verbs in this family.

Is م ج س a native triliteral root?

Qur’anic morphological indexes place الْمَجُوسَ under the letters م ج س.

This classification helps organize vocabulary and locate related forms.

Historical etymology nevertheless requires distinguishing morphological classification from a word’s actual origin.

Majūs entered Arabic from a non-Arabic linguistic tradition. Arabic verbs such as majjasa and tamajjasa were formed from this name.

An assumed Arabic verb therefore cannot be presented as the established origin of Majūs.

Nor should its appearance alone lead us to classify Majūs as a passive participle meaning “someone upon whom the action of majs has been performed.”

In the Qur’anic verse, it names a community rather than describing a passive action.

Arabic uses Majūs as a collective designation and Majūsī for an individual.

This distinction matters for understanding the word’s formation.

Why the form al-Majūs?

In the Qur’an, Majūs occurs with the definite article al-:

“الْمَجُوسَ”

This form refers to a recognized community.

The article does not mean that every member has identical beliefs, conduct, or ultimate destiny.

It identifies the community within the sentence without passing moral judgment on each individual.

In al-Ḥajj 22:17, the word is coordinated with the preceding groups and appears in the accusative case.

Its accusative case follows its coordination with the noun of inna; it does not make the word a direct object. In a natural translation of the sentence, وَالْمَجُوسَ means “and the Majūs.”

Grammatically, it stands alongside the other groups named.

4. Qur’anic usage: the Majūs among religious communities

Al-Ḥajj 22:17

«إِنَّ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَالَّذِينَ هَادُوا وَالصَّابِئِينَ وَالنَّصَارَىٰ وَالْمَجُوسَ وَالَّذِينَ أَشْرَكُوا إِنَّ اللَّهَ يَفْصِلُ بَيْنَهُمْ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ إِنَّ اللَّهَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ شَهِيدٌ»

Inna alladhīna āmanū wa-alladhīna hādū wa-al-ṣābiʾīna wa-al-naṣārā wa-al-majūsa wa-alladhīna ashrakū inna Allāha yafṣilu baynahum yawma al-qiyāmati inna Allāha ʿalā kulli shayʾin shahīd.

“Indeed, those who have believed, those who became Jews, the Sabians, the Christians, the Majūs, and those who associated partners—The God will judge between them on the Day of Resurrection. Indeed, He is Witness over all things.”

The Qur’an names several groups in this verse.

Some designations identify people through belief or action, such as الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا and الَّذِينَ أَشْرَكُوا.

Others are established names of religious communities, such as Jews, Sabians, Christians, and Majūs.

This difference should not be overlooked.

Majūs here is a community name, whereas alladhīna ashrakū identifies people through the act of associating partners.

The name Majūs therefore does not in itself have the same lexical meaning as shirk.

Why does the Qur’an name the Majūs separately?

The verse distinguishes the names of different groups recognized by their beliefs or social identity.

This indicates that the Majūs were an identifiable community in the society and language addressed by the Qur’an.

Naming a group separately does not, however, define all its doctrinal boundaries.

For example, their separate mention from الَّذِينَ أَشْرَكُوا does not establish that no member ever held a belief or engaged in a practice involving shirk.

Nor does their presence in this list establish that all Majūs were necessarily associators of partners.

Such judgments require knowledge of the actual beliefs and conduct of the people or community concerned.

The verse does not undertake that investigation here. It names the groups and assigns final judgment to God.

Are the Majūs the same as Zoroastrians?

Historically, Majūs is strongly connected with Iranian religious traditions, particularly Zoroastrianism.

Zoroastrians may therefore be mentioned in a historical explanation of the term.

Translating Majūs as “Zoroastrians” in every historical context without qualification may, however, narrow the name’s scope too much.

In earlier periods, the name of the Magi designated religious officeholders; in Arabic it acquired a broader use for a religious community.

For the Qur’anic text itself, retaining Majūs is consequently more precise and faithful.

An explanation can add its historical association, especially with Zoroastrianism.

Likewise, the word alone does not establish that the Qur’an names Zoroaster or rules on his status as a Prophet.

Al-Ḥajj 22:17 does not name Zoroaster, the Avesta, or any specific religious text belonging to the community.

Attributing such information to the verse itself goes beyond its wording.

Final judgment belongs to God

After naming the groups, the Qur’an states:

«إِنَّ اللَّهَ يَفْصِلُ بَيْنَهُمْ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ»

Inna Allāha yafṣilu baynahum yawma al-qiyāmah.

Judgment and the separation of truth from falsehood on the Day of Resurrection are attributed to God.

This sentence shows that religious names are not the endpoint of the verse’s argument.

After identifying the groups, the Qur’an states the final judgment.

The verse concludes:

«إِنَّ اللَّهَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ شَهِيدٌ»

Inna Allāha ʿalā kulli shayʾin shahīd.

That is, God is Witness over all things.

This description relates to the preceding judgment. Divine judgment rests on full knowledge of what people have said, believed, and done.

A community’s name alone should therefore not determine our verdict on every member’s ultimate destiny.

People may be born into a religious community without being equal in knowledge, conduct, intention, or responsibility.

Elsewhere, the Qur’an also addresses human knowledge, action, choice, and responsibility in moral judgment. Relevant references include al-Nisāʾ 4:40, al-Zalzalah 99:7, and al-Zalzalah 99:8.

Nevertheless, al-Ḥajj 22:17 alone does not pronounce uniform salvation or condemnation upon all the named groups. It emphasizes the occurrence of divine judgment and God’s witness.

5. Community names and descriptions of belief

Studying religious vocabulary in the Qur’an requires distinguishing a community’s historical name, an individual’s beliefs, and their actual conduct.

For example, “Jews,” “Christians,” and “Majūs” can name groups recognized through particular histories and religious traditions.

By contrast, “faith,” “kufr,” and “shirk,” in their Qur’anic uses, concern people’s relationship to truth, belief, and conduct.

These two kinds of designation do not always have identical boundaries.

A community’s name may endure throughout history while its members’ beliefs and practices vary.

Two people affiliated with the same community may also understand a doctrinal matter differently.

A lexical study of Majūs should therefore not become an attempt to establish the truth or falsehood of every belief held by its adherents.

Does Majūs name an action?

No. In Qur’anic usage, Majūs names a group.

By contrast, أَشْرَكُوا is a verb expressing the act of associating partners.

This difference is important.

Linguistically, being a Majūsī means affiliation with a recognized community. Determining a particular person’s beliefs or conduct requires further evidence.

Majūs should not be translated directly as “magicians” either.

In European linguistic history, some words relating to magic developed from the title of the Magi and related names. This historical development shows a change of usage in those languages; it does not make “magicians” the direct meaning of Majūs in the Qur’an.

Al-Ḥajj 22:17 neither discusses magic nor defines the Majūs by such a practice.

Majūs and najas: two different words

Although م ج س and ن ج س resemble one another in writing, they are not the same root.

In Arabic and the Qur’an, ن ج س relates to impurity.

In Qur’anic usage, م ج س forms the name Majūs.

The different initial letter reflects a real difference in the words’ structure and history.

Their superficial resemblance does not make Majūs lexically mean “the impure.”

Nor can the name alone, without independent investigation, establish a ruling about the bodily, moral, or doctrinal impurity of every adherent.

If studied, such claims require separate, clear evidence rather than phonetic similarity between roots.

The Majūs and Sabians: names of two communities

“Sabians” likewise names a recognized group in the Qur’an.

This designation occurs in al-Baqarah 2:62, al-Māʾidah 5:69, and al-Ḥajj 22:17.

The Majūs, however, are mentioned only in al-Ḥajj 22:17.

The two names appearing in one verse does not make their religions, histories, or beliefs identical.

Their separate mention does not explain all their doctrinal and social differences either.

Each name must first be studied through its own historical and linguistic evidence.

In lexical research, this principle prevents transferring one community’s characteristics to another.

6. Challenges of interpretation and translation

The main challenge in translating الْمَجُوسَ is the temptation to replace a religious community’s name with historical interpretations of its beliefs.

Directly translating it as “fire-worshippers” introduces a doctrinal definition absent from the verse itself.

Translating it only as “Zoroastrians” identifies an important historical association but may overly restrict its scope in early Arabic.

Rendering it as “Magi” may suggest that the verse speaks only of priests and religious officeholders, whereas al-Majūs functions here as a religious community’s designation.

Likewise, equivalents such as “magicians,” “dualists,” or “followers of fire” cannot replace the Qur’anic name without independent examination.

Suggested translation for Forqan

Al-Ḥajj 22:17

«وَالْمَجُوسَ»

Wa-al-majūsa.

“And the Majūs.”

The suggested translation of the complete verse is:

“Indeed, those who have believed, those who became Jews, the Sabians, the Christians, the Majūs, and those who associated partners—The God will judge between them on the Day of Resurrection. Indeed, The God is Witness over all things.”

A lexical note may state:

“الْمَجُوسَ: the name of a religious community historically associated with the Magi of ancient Iran and especially the Zoroastrian tradition.”

For the brief root meaning, rather than inventing an inaccurate derivational meaning, one can write:

“The historical name of a religious community, with an Iranian linguistic origin.”

This explanation is sufficient for the Qur’anic use.

The separate root study explains the name’s history and its difference from doctrinal terms; the basic translation should not incorporate the entire history of Iranian religions.

Non-Qur’anic forms

Arabic Majūsī denotes a person affiliated with the Majūs.

Majūsiyyah names their religion or religious affiliation.

Tamajjasa means becoming a Majūsī or joining the religion of the Majūs.

Majjasa ghayrahu means bringing someone else into that religion or raising them in it.

These derivatives are established in Arabic, but none occurs in precisely these forms in the Qur’an.

They must therefore not be used to invent additional Qur’anic occurrences for this family.

7. Conclusion

In Qur’anic root indexes, م ج س corresponds to الْمَجُوسَ. Historically, however, this family differs from many native Arabic roots.

Majūs is a name of Iranian origin, with ancient forms attested in Iranian documents and neighboring languages.

Initially it was associated with Magi and religious officeholders in Iranian traditions. In Arabic, Majūs became the name of a religious community associated particularly with Zoroastrianism.

The verse attributed to an exchange between Imruʾ al-Qays and al-Tawʾam al-Yashkurī shows the name’s association with fire in Arabic literary tradition, though its attribution and date are uncertain.

That historical association does not authorize defining Majūs lexically as “fire-worshippers,” or assuming identical beliefs among every member in every period.

The name appears in the Qur’an only in al-Ḥajj 22:17.

The verse names the Majūs alongside other groups and assigns final judgment between them to God.

It neither names Zoroaster, explains their religious scripture, nor defines all their beliefs and rituals.

Final definition for Forqan: In Qur’anic usage, م ج س corresponds to Majūs, a religious community historically connected with the Magi of ancient Iran and the Zoroastrian tradition. The word is originally a group name, not an Arabic verb with a general meaning. Arabic verbs such as majjasa and tamajjasa were formed from this name and should not be assumed to be its historical origin.

الْمَجُوسَ in al-Ḥajj 22:17 refers to that recognized community without its designation alone constituting a moral or doctrinal judgment on each individual.

Majd, najas, Majūs, and other words resembling one another in sound or writing must not be confused: each has its own origin and meaning.

Ultimately, the verse clarifies an essential distinction: a religious community’s name is not identical to the full reality of every member’s beliefs, knowledge, and conduct.

People may be known by names and traditions inherited from earlier generations, yet their responsibility toward truth and their actions requires actual knowledge of them and just judgment.

After mentioning different communities, al-Ḥajj 22:17 assigns final judgment to God, who is Witness over all things. Majūs should be understood in that setting: a religious community’s name among others, rather than a predetermined verdict on every adherent’s destiny.
