The Arabic root Ḥ J B concerns a barrier placed or standing between two things, restricting or preventing sight, arrival, entry, or direct contact. The barrier may be material, like a curtain dividing a house, or nonmaterial, like something preventing understanding, access to someone, or enjoyment of something.

In this family, حِجَاب (ḥijāb) denotes a screen or partition, حَاجِب (ḥājib) someone controlling entry and access, and مَحْجُوب (maḥjūb) someone or something screened off or denied access.

The root occurs eight times in the Qur’an: seven as ḥijāb and once as مَحْجُوبُونَ (maḥjūbūna). Its uses range from spatial screens and partitions to impediments to understanding, separation in communication, and being barred from access to the Lord.

In contemporary Persian and Arabic, ḥijāb is especially associated with women’s covering. That technical usage does not exhaust the root’s meaning. Qur’anic ḥijāb denotes a barrier or partition; none of its seven occurrences by itself names a particular garment such as a headscarf or chador.

This distinction does not deny the Qur’an’s references to clothing. It requires separating the word’s meaning from other terminology and rules, which must be examined through their own wording and verses.

1. Forms and derivatives

Ḥ J B consists of ḥāʾ, jīm, and bāʾ. It is a sound triliteral root without weak, doubled, or hamzated radicals.

Important forms in this family include:

  • حَجَبَ ـ يَحْجُبُ (ḥajaba–yaḥjubu): covered, obstructed, or prevented seeing, entry, or arrival.
  • حَجْب (ḥajb): covering, separating, or preventing access.
  • حِجَاب (ḥijāb): screen, covering, or any barrier intervening between two sides.
  • حُجُب (ḥujub): plural of ḥijāb: screens and barriers.
  • حَاجِب (ḥājib): someone preventing or controlling entry and access; also eyebrow.
  • حَجَبَة / حُجَّاب (ḥajabah/ḥujjāb): established plurals of ḥājib for doorkeepers and access officials.
  • حِجَابَة (ḥijābah): responsibility for guarding and regulating access; historically, custody of a particular doorway or place.
  • مَحْجُوب (maḥjūb): screened off, separated by a barrier, or denied encounter and access.
  • حَجَّبَ ـ يُحَجِّبُ (ḥajjaba–yuḥajjibu): covered or established a barrier between things.
  • تَحَجَّبَ (taḥajjaba): went behind a screen or covered oneself.
  • اِحْتَجَبَ (iḥtajaba): became hidden, went behind a barrier, or remained beyond others’ sight and access.
  • مُحْتَجِب (muḥtajib): someone behind a screen or barrier, beyond sight and access.
  • اِسْتَحْجَبَ (istaḥjaba): appointed someone to guard a doorway or regulate access.
  • حَجَبَات (ḥajabāt): in early anatomical vocabulary, prominent parts of an animal’s hip or pelvic bones.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an.

The Qur’anic forms are حِجَابٌ (ḥijābun) in Al-Aʿrāf 7:46 and Fuṣṣilat 41:5; حِجَابًا (ḥijāban) in Al-Isrāʾ 17:45 and Maryam 19:17; حِجَابٍ (ḥijābin) in Al-Aḥzāb 33:53 and Al-Shūrā 42:51; بِالْحِجَابِ (bi-al-ḥijābi) in Ṣād 38:32; and لَمَحْجُوبُونَ (la-maḥjūbūna) in Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:15.

The Qur’an thus uses only the noun ḥijāb and the passive participle maḥjūb from this family. The verb ḥajaba and the forms تَحَجَّبَ (taḥajjaba) and اِحْتَجَبَ (iḥtajaba) do not occur in its text.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: seven ḥijāb nouns and one passive participle

2. Semantic core and early Arabic usage

Obstruction: the root’s semantic core

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris writes concerning Ḥ J B:

أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ، وَهُوَ الْمَنْعُ

aṣlun wāḥidun wa-huwa al-manʿu

That is: “It has one core: preventing or obstructing.”

He then explains حَجَبْتُهُ عَنْ كَذَا (ḥajabtuhu ʿan kadhā) as preventing someone from something.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise explains ḥijāb through سِتْر (sitr), stating that anything intervening between two things may be called ḥijāb.

Together these explanations establish the basic meaning: intervening or creating a barrier so that sight, arrival, or direct access is restricted.

The barrier need not be a cloth curtain.

A wall, door, covering, or something else may obstruct sight or entry. The obstacle may also concern relationship, authority, or understanding.

Someone denied admission to a ruler’s presence, for example, may be maḥjūb without any curtain hanging directly before his eyes.

Ḥajb therefore has a wider semantic range than covering the body.

Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs al-Lughah: prevention and bodily analogies

Ḥijāb: what stands between two sides

Arabic ḥijāb names something used to screen or establish a barrier between two sides.

Materially, a curtain between two rooms can be a ḥijāb.

Its function need not merely be covering a person. It may separate private and public space, prevent seeing into a room, or restrict direct access.

In each case, ḥijāb concerns something altering the direct relationship between its two sides.

This matters in Qur’anic usage, where ḥijāb frequently accompanies expressions such as بَيْنَ (bayna), مِنْ وَرَاءِ (min warāʾi), and عَنْ (ʿan).

These expressions clarify the relationship between the barrier and its two sides.

Ḥājib: a doorkeeper

Early Arabic uses ḥājib for someone regulating admission to a ruler or dignitary.

A ḥājib need not physically cover someone; he prevents direct access.

In Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Jawharī explains ḥājib through بَوَّاب (bawwāb), “doorkeeper.”

This records prevention of access as an established branch of the root’s lexical range, although the attestation alone does not date that sense’s origin.

Someone may remain outside a doorway because the ḥājib refuses entry. Here the obstruction concerns admission.

حِجَابَة (ḥijābah) likewise names responsibility for guarding access and, historically, custody of the Kaʿbah’s door.

Here ḥijābah is an administrative or social responsibility, rather than a garment.

Ḥājib al-ʿayn: the eyebrow

Ḥājib also means eyebrow in Arabic.

Ibn Fāris associates this use with obstruction, likening the eyebrows to something preventing material from reaching the eyes. His account is a lexical analogy, rather than a comprehensive anatomical explanation of their function.

An eyebrow is not an absolute barrier: a person can still see beneath it.

Ḥajb therefore need not sever every form of contact. Something may restrict only part of an effect or access.

The example shows obstruction imagery applied to a natural bodily structure.

Ḥijāb al-jawf: an internal bodily partition

Lexicons record حِجَابُ الْجَوْف (ḥijāb al-jawf) for an internal membrane or tissue separating bodily regions.

Al-Azharī describes tissue intervening between internal parts of the body.

Here ḥijāb is neither clothing nor a means of hiding someone from others.

It is a natural partition between two bodily regions.

The example again shows that ḥijāb can denote an intervening structure even within the body, beyond ordinary sight.

Ḥājib al-shams: the sun’s edge

Early Arabic also records حَاجِبُ الشَّمْس (ḥājib al-shams) for the sun’s edge or the portion first appearing at the horizon.

Its imagery relates to the eyebrow and something partially appearing or remaining screened.

It does not establish that early Arabs imagined the sun as possessing an actual bodily eyebrow.

Natural language uses familiar images to describe other shapes and phenomena.

Nor does this usage alone explain every Qur’anic meaning of ḥijāb.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: doorkeeper, eyebrow, internal partition, and sun’s edge

A witness attributed to pre-Islamic poetry

Ibn Qutaybah’s Al-Shiʿr wa-al-Shuʿarāʾ records a line attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays describing a horse:

سَلِيمُ الشَّظَى عَبْلُ الشَّوَى شَنِجُ النَّسَا
لَهُ حَجَبَاتٌ مُشْرِفَاتٌ عَلَى الْفَالِ

salīmu al-shaẓā ʿablu al-shawā shaniju al-nasā / lahu ḥajabātun mushrifātun ʿalā al-fāli

That is: “With sound shank bones, stout limbs, and taut leg sinews; its hip points rise above the flesh beside them.” This is an approximate rendering of early descriptive anatomical vocabulary, rather than a modern anatomical classification.

Ḥajabāt here concerns bony projections near the horse’s flank and pelvis.

This usage is also recorded in Lisān al-ʿArab and Tahdhīb al-Lughah.

The witness matters because early poetry uses Ḥ J B beyond household curtains or human covering: the family also names certain prominent animal body parts.

The line does not directly attest ḥijāb as a curtain. That sense requires independent lexical evidence and the Qur’an’s own uses.

Although sources attribute the line to Imruʾ al-Qays, attribution and later transmission should be distinguished from definitive proof of its composition date.

Ibn Qutaybah, Al-Shiʿr wa-al-Shuʿarāʾ: witness attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays

The relationship between covering and preventing

Some uses combine covering and prevention.

A curtain between two people may also obstruct their sight.

This is not always so.

Someone may be refused entry without being unable to see the place. Something may also remain unseen while information about it is obtained another way.

Ḥajb should therefore not always be equated with blindness, inability to hear, or severance of every form of communication.

Context establishes the barrier’s nature and what it prevents.

The Qur’anic uses clearly illustrate this distinction.

3. Qur’anic ḥijāb as barrier and partition

Al-Aʿrāf 7:46: a partition between two groups

Verse text:

وَبَيْنَهُمَا حِجَابٌ وَعَلَى الْأَعْرَافِ رِجَالٌ يَعْرِفُونَ كُلًّا بِسِيمَاهُمْ وَنَادَوْا أَصْحَابَ الْجَنَّةِ أَنْ سَلَامٌ عَلَيْكُمْ لَمْ يَدْخُلُوهَا وَهُمْ يَطْمَعُونَ

wa-baynahumā ḥijābun wa-ʿalā al-aʿrāfi rijālun yaʿrifūna kullan bi-sīmāhum wa-nādaw aṣḥāba al-jannati an salāmun ʿalaykum lam yadkhulūhā wa-hum yaṭmaʿūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Between the two is a screen and partition. On the heights are men who recognize each group by their marks, and they call to the people of the Garden: Peace be upon you. They have not yet entered it, although they hope to.”

Ḥijābun stands between two groups identified in the preceding context as the people of the Garden and the Fire.

بَيْنَهُمَا (baynahumā) clarifies its role: something intervenes between those two sides.

Ḥijāb is not a garment here. The verse describes a partition between two groups.

The word itself does not establish its material or physical form.

وَعَلَى الْأَعْرَافِ رِجَالٌ (wa-ʿalā al-aʿrāfi rijālun) also introduces a group on the heights who recognize others by their marks.

This description shows that a barrier need not prevent every form of recognition or communication. The same scene includes recognition and calling.

Without evidence, ḥijāb should therefore not become a barrier absolutely cutting off all sight, hearing, and communication.

Its clear meaning here is a partition between two sides.

Maryam 19:17: a screen between Mary and others

Verse text:

فَاتَّخَذَتْ مِنْ دُونِهِمْ حِجَابًا فَأَرْسَلْنَا إِلَيْهَا رُوحَنَا فَتَمَثَّلَ لَهَا بَشَرًا سَوِيًّا

fa-ittakhadhat min dūnihim ḥijāban fa-arsalnā ilayhā rūḥanā fa-tamaththala lahā basharan sawiyyan

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Mary placed a screen between herself and them. Then We sent Our spirit to her, and it appeared to her as a well-formed human being.”

In the preceding verse, Maryam 19:16, Mary withdraws from her family to an eastern place.

Maryam 19:17 then says she adopted min dūnihim ḥijāban.

Ḥijāb here concerns a partition Mary established between herself and others.

This fits seclusion and withdrawal from others’ direct sight and access.

The verse does not say Mary put on a headscarf, chador, or particular garment.

Ittakhadhat min dūnihim ḥijāban describes establishing a barrier or screen between herself and others, without specifying its exact kind.

The distinction matters: clothing and a partition can both conceal something, but differ in kind and function.

The spirit subsequently appears to Mary as a human being. The screen therefore does not make every entry, encounter, or communication impossible under all circumstances.

The event’s details require reflection on the story itself.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:53: a screen between the sides of a conversation

Verse text:

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَا تَدْخُلُوا بُيُوتَ النَّبِيِّ إِلَّا أَنْ يُؤْذَنَ لَكُمْ إِلَىٰ طَعَامٍ غَيْرَ نَاظِرِينَ إِنَاهُ وَلَٰكِنْ إِذَا دُعِيتُمْ فَادْخُلُوا فَإِذَا طَعِمْتُمْ فَانْتَشِرُوا وَلَا مُسْتَأْنِسِينَ لِحَدِيثٍ إِنَّ ذَٰلِكُمْ كَانَ يُؤْذِي النَّبِيَّ فَيَسْتَحْيِي مِنْكُمْ وَاللَّهُ لَا يَسْتَحْيِي مِنَ الْحَقِّ وَإِذَا سَأَلْتُمُوهُنَّ مَتَاعًا فَاسْأَلُوهُنَّ مِنْ وَرَاءِ حِجَابٍ ذَٰلِكُمْ أَطْهَرُ لِقُلُوبِكُمْ وَقُلُوبِهِنَّ وَمَا كَانَ لَكُمْ أَنْ تُؤْذُوا رَسُولَ اللَّهِ وَلَا أَنْ تَنْكِحُوا أَزْوَاجَهُ مِنْ بَعْدِهِ أَبَدًا إِنَّ ذَٰلِكُمْ كَانَ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ عَظِيمًا

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū lā tadkhulū buyūta al-nabiyyi illā an yuʾdhana lakum ilā ṭaʿāmin ghayra nāẓirīna ināhu wa-lākin idhā duʿītum fa-udkhulū fa-idhā ṭaʿimtum fa-intashirū wa-lā mustaʾnisīna li-ḥadīthin inna dhālikum kāna yuʾdhī al-nabiyya fa-yastaḥyī minkum wa-Allāhu lā yastaḥyī mina al-ḥaqqi wa-idhā saʾaltumūhunna matāʿan fa-isʾalūhunna min warāʾi ḥijābin dhālikum aṭharu li-qulūbikum wa-qulūbihinna wa-mā kāna lakum an tuʾdhū rasūla Allāhi wa-lā an tankiḥū azwājahu min baʿdihi abadan inna dhālikum kāna ʿinda Allāhi ʿaẓīman

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Believers, do not enter the Prophet’s houses unless you are permitted for a meal, without waiting for its preparation. When invited, enter; when you have eaten, disperse without lingering for conversation. That troubled the Prophet, and he felt shy before you, but The God is not shy of the truth. When you ask them for something, ask from behind a screen and partition. That is purer for your hearts and theirs. You must not hurt The God’s Messenger, nor ever marry his wives after him. That would indeed be grave before The God.”

Here ḥijābin occurs in min warāʾi ḥijābin.

Warāʾ clarifies the spatial relationship: asking or requesting occurs from the other side of a partition.

Ḥijāb is therefore a barrier between the sides of a conversation.

The verse’s subject and audience

The verse begins with etiquette for entering the Prophet’s houses.

It addresses unauthorized entry, waiting for food, lingering for conversation, and troubling the Prophet.

It then instructs that requests to the women concerned be made from behind a screen.

The feminine plural pronoun in سَأَلْتُمُوهُنَّ (saʾaltumūhunna) relates to the household and the Prophet’s wives. The verse’s ending explicitly mentions أَزْوَاجَهُ (azwājahu), “his wives.”

Extending the command from the Prophet’s wives to all women in every setting therefore requires separate contextual argument.

The verse nevertheless specifies a way of maintaining privacy in the situation under discussion.

The lexical meaning of ḥijāb does not automatically turn the partition into headwear.

A partition and personal covering

The verse does not say that women should wear something named ḥijāb over their heads, faces, or bodies.

Its command is فَاسْأَلُوهُنَّ مِنْ وَرَاءِ حِجَابٍ (fa-isʾalūhunna min warāʾi ḥijābin).

That is, requests are made from behind a partition.

The partition may have been a curtain or another household barrier. The verse itself specifies neither material, size, nor shape.

This distinction matters when studying the term ḥijāb historically.

In later religious and social usage, ḥijāb became a broad designation for covering and certain conventions of women’s social presence. This distinction alone does not establish a precise date or a single course for the terminology’s development.

Its contemporary usage nevertheless does not prove that ḥijāb in Al-Aḥzāb 33:53 names that clothing.

Investigating rules of women’s covering also requires separate study of verses such as Al-Nūr 24:31 and Al-Aḥzāb 33:59, where different words occur.

Dhālikum aṭharu li-qulūbikum wa-qulūbihinna

After the instruction to ask from behind a screen, the verse says this is purer for both sides’ hearts.

The screen thus relates to privacy and interpersonal conduct within this context.

“Purer hearts” is not the lexical meaning of ḥijāb; it is the result the verse attributes to the instruction.

The expression also does not establish that every curtain or separation necessarily purifies hearts in every situation.

The verse addresses a particular setting; extending its instruction elsewhere requires separate examination.

4. Ḥijāb in understanding, communication, and encounter

Al-Isrāʾ 17:45: ḥijāban mastūran

Verse text:

وَإِذَا قَرَأْتَ الْقُرْآنَ جَعَلْنَا بَيْنَكَ وَبَيْنَ الَّذِينَ لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ بِالْآخِرَةِ حِجَابًا مَسْتُورًا

wa-idhā qaraʾta al-qurʾāna jaʿalnā baynaka wa-bayna alladhīna lā yuʾminūna bi-al-ākhirati ḥijāban mastūran

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“When you recite the Qur’an, We place a hidden and concealed barrier between you and those who do not believe in the hereafter.”

Here the barrier is placed between Muhammad and those who do not believe in the hereafter.

The verse concerns reciting the Qur’an, so the barrier belongs to the setting of conveying and receiving its message.

The verse alone does not establish that every recitation produced a visible material curtain between the Prophet and his opponents.

The meaning of mastūran

مَسْتُور (mastūr) is a passive participle from S T R, describing something covered or concealed.

In ordinary Arabic morphology, ḥijāban mastūran means a barrier itself concealed or hidden.

Some early grammarians and commentators also explain it as حِجَابًا سَاتِرًا (ḥijāban sātiran), a barrier that conceals.

The explanations are distinct.

In the first, the barrier itself is hidden; the second emphasizes its concealing function.

Al-Ṭabarī reports both explanations and prefers the barrier itself being concealed as the more apparent construction.

The base translation should therefore render mastūr as “concealed” or “hidden,” leaving discussion of its function to contextual explanation.

Al-Ṭabarī: mastūr versus sātir and preference for a hidden barrier

A barrier to receiving the message

The following verse, Al-Isrāʾ 17:46, mentions coverings over hearts and heaviness in ears.

This continuity connects the barrier in Al-Isrāʾ 17:45 with an obstacle to receiving and understanding the message.

The same verses nevertheless describe recitation, turning away, and the audience’s reactions.

The barrier need not mean total inability to hear the Qur’an’s sounds. The issue may concern its message failing to reach understanding and acceptance.

Nor does the expression establish a definitive theory of the heart’s or brain’s physical structure.

On this contextual reading, ḥijāb obstructs the audience’s relationship with the message. The root alone does not establish its precise nature. Commentaries also transmit other applications, including protection of the Prophet from opponents’ sight; lexical meaning alone does not decide among them.

Al-Qurṭubī: reported readings concerning understanding and protection from sight

Fuṣṣilat 41:5: a barrier in the opponents’ speech

Verse text:

وَقَالُوا قُلُوبُنَا فِي أَكِنَّةٍ مِمَّا تَدْعُونَا إِلَيْهِ وَفِي آذَانِنَا وَقْرٌ وَمِنْ بَيْنِنَا وَبَيْنِكَ حِجَابٌ فَاعْمَلْ إِنَّنَا عَامِلُونَ

wa-qālū qulūbunā fī akinnatin mimmā tadʿūnā ilayhi wa-fī ādhāninā waqrun wa-min bayninā wa-baynika ḥijābun fa-iʿmal innanā ʿāmilūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“They said: Our hearts are within coverings against what you invite us to; there is heaviness in our ears, and between us and you a barrier. So do your work; we too are doing ours.”

Ḥijābun here is quoted from the opponents’ speech.

They describe three obstacles: coverings over their hearts, heaviness in their ears, and a barrier between themselves and the Prophet.

Together these indicate separation in accepting the message and responding to the Prophet’s invitation.

The speech’s meaning should nevertheless be distinguished from the truth of the speakers’ claim.

The Qur’an quotes them; this does not endorse every part of their assertion.

Ḥijāb here depicts separation between two sides, asserted by the opponents themselves.

The word names neither clothing nor a specified wall.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:45 and Fuṣṣilat 41:5

Both verses concern the relationship between the Prophet and his opponents.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:45 attributes placing the barrier to The God.

In Fuṣṣilat 41:5, the opponents themselves assert its existence.

The difference in speaker and construction matters.

The verses should not be equated so completely that a divine statement and the opponents’ claim become indistinguishable.

Both nevertheless allow ḥijāb to be understood as a nonmaterial impediment in the relationship with the message and its understanding.

5. Ḥijāb in relation to The God

Al-Shūrā 42:51: speaking from behind a screen

Verse text:

وَمَا كَانَ لِبَشَرٍ أَنْ يُكَلِّمَهُ اللَّهُ إِلَّا وَحْيًا أَوْ مِنْ وَرَاءِ حِجَابٍ أَوْ يُرْسِلَ رَسُولًا فَيُوحِيَ بِإِذْنِهِ مَا يَشَاءُ إِنَّهُ عَلِيٌّ حَكِيمٌ

wa-mā kāna li-basharin an yukallimahu Allāhu illā waḥyan aw min warāʾi ḥijābin aw yursila rasūlan fa-yūḥiya bi-idhnihi mā yashāʾu innahu ʿaliyyun ḥakīmun

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“It is not for any human being that The God should speak to him except by revelation, from behind a screen, or by sending a messenger who reveals, with His permission, whatever He wills. He is indeed exalted and wise.”

Min warāʾi ḥijābin here describes one form of divine communication with a human being.

Warāʾ concerns what lies beyond or on the other side of a boundary or partition.

The subject here, however, is communication between The God and humanity, rather than two people conversing across a room.

Ḥijāb alone therefore does not establish that The God sits in a material location with a cloth curtain hanging before Him.

The verse describes modes of communication and conveying a message, rather than the unseen world’s physical architecture or a divine location.

Does the screen prevent hearing too?

Speaking from behind a screen remains a form of communication in this verse.

A screen therefore does not sever every form of contact.

Direct encounter or a particular access may be restricted while speech and message still reach the recipient.

This fits the root’s core: a barrier restricts a form of access without necessarily eliminating every avenue of communication.

The screen is accordingly compatible with speaking here.

Ḥ J B alone nevertheless cannot establish that communication’s exact nature.

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:15: being barred from the Lord

Verse text:

كَلَّا إِنَّهُمْ عَنْ رَبِّهِمْ يَوْمَئِذٍ لَمَحْجُوبُونَ

kallā innahum ʿan rabbihim yawmaʾidhin la-maḥjūbūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“No indeed; on that day they will certainly be screened off and kept from their Lord.”

Maḥjūbūna is the masculine plural passive participle of Ḥ J B.

The emphatic lām in la-maḥjūbūna reinforces the statement’s certainty.

عَنْ رَبِّهِمْ (ʿan rabbihim) identifies whom they are barred or kept from: their Lord.

Unlike household-screen passages, the verse names no material curtain.

Its subject is deprivation of a kind of access or benefit in relation to the Lord.

Is the verse only about not seeing The God?

Theological traditions cite this verse in discussing seeing The God in the hereafter.

Some take it as evidence that a group is denied the vision of The God.

Lexically, maḥjūbūna ʿan rabbihim concerns being barred from access or encounter with their Lord.

The expression alone does not determine the physical or nonphysical nature of that encounter, its perceptual means, a divine location, or humanity’s mode of presence in the hereafter.

Seeing The God therefore requires separate study alongside other verses.

The expression’s force should nevertheless remain clear.

In Al-Muṭaffifīn’s context, this deprivation forms part of the condemned outcome of those denying the day of judgment.

Maḥjūbūna thus concerns more than not seeing an ordinary scene: it describes serious deprivation in relation to the Lord.

The root itself does not establish that deprivation’s precise nature.

Al-Ṭabarī: deprivation and differing explanations of maḥjūbūn

6. Ḥijāb in Solomon’s story

Ṣād 38:32: disappearing behind a screen

Verse text:

فَقَالَ إِنِّي أَحْبَبْتُ حُبَّ الْخَيْرِ عَنْ ذِكْرِ رَبِّي حَتَّىٰ تَوَارَتْ بِالْحِجَابِ

fa-qāla innī aḥbabtu ḥubba al-khayri ʿan dhikri rabbī ḥattā tawārat bi-al-ḥijābi

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“He said: I preferred the love of good over remembrance of my Lord, until it disappeared behind the screen.”

Al-ḥijābi here is preceded by bi-.

تَوَارَتْ (tawārat) belongs to W R Y and concerns becoming hidden.

Ḥijāb is thus what something disappears behind, or by means of which it becomes hidden.

An important issue remains: the feminine singular subject of tawārat is not explicitly named in this sentence.

Did the sun disappear, or the horses?

The preceding verse, Ṣād 38:31, describes fine horses presented to Solomon.

Many early commentators identify tawārat’s subject as the sun, disappearing from sight at day’s end.

This reading relates to bi-al-ʿashiyyi in Ṣād 38:31.

The sun nevertheless is not explicitly named in Ṣād 38:32.

Another reading relates the pronoun to the horses, since Arabic nonhuman plurals can take feminine singular verbal agreement.

On that reading, the horses disappeared from sight, and Solomon orders them brought back in Ṣād 38:33.

Both readings require assessment against context and the pronoun’s construction.

Ḥ J B alone definitively establishes neither.

In either case, ḥijāb is a screen behind which something disappears from sight.

Identifying what disappeared therefore concerns the pronoun’s referent and interpretation of the story, rather than ḥijāb’s lexical meaning.

Is the screen a mountain or the horizon?

Some early explanations relate the sun’s disappearance behind a screen to the horizon, a mountain, or a natural barrier.

The image fits general usage: a natural barrier can also hide something from sight.

The verse itself names no mountain, specific horizon, or particular physical structure.

The base translation should therefore preserve “disappeared behind the screen,” leaving the referent’s identification to contextual reflection.

This preserves both the word’s meaning and the pronoun’s genuine ambiguity.

Al-Thaʿālibī: sun and horses as readings of tawārat

7. Comparison with related roots

Ḥ J B (ḥajaba) and S T R (satara): obstructing and covering

Satara concerns covering and concealing.

Ḥajaba can likewise concern covering, while also emphasizing obstruction and separation.

The families overlap in many uses.

A curtain concealing something can also be a ḥijāb.

Not every ḥajb completely conceals something from sight, however. Someone denied admission to a ruler may be maḥjūb without the entire surrounding space being hidden from him.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:45 brings both families together in ḥijāban mastūran.

The combination shows semantic proximity alongside distinct forms and roles.

Ḥ J B (ḥajaba) and M N ʿ (manaʿa): intervening and preventing

Study of M N ʿ

Arabic manaʿa concerns preventing.

Ibn Fāris likewise explains Ḥ J B through manʿ.

Manʿ has a broad range: someone may be prevented from acting without a curtain or spatial barrier separating them from the intended outcome.

Many uses of ḥajb preserve the image of something intervening between two sides or blocking access.

In nonmaterial uses, the obstacle need not have a bodily form.

The roots are therefore close, without being interchangeable throughout.

Ḥ J B (ḥijāb) and Ḥ J Z (ḥājiz): partition and obstruction

Ḥ J Z likewise concerns separating, preventing, and keeping apart.

In Al-Ḥāqqah 69:47, حَاجِزِينَ (ḥājizīna) concerns those who could intervene, while their ability to do so is denied.

Ḥijāb likewise denotes something intervening between two sides.

The families overlap in obstruction and separation.

Ḥājiz, however, is an active-participle form identifying an intervening person or thing; ḥijāb normally names a barrier or partition. The action itself may be expressed through ḥajz.

Ḥ J B and Ḥ J Z also remain distinct lexical families.

Ḥ J B (ḥijāb) and B R Z KH (barzakh): barrier and interval between domains

Study of B R Z KH

Qur’anic barzakh concerns an interval or barrier between two regions or conditions.

Ḥijāb can likewise separate two sides.

Not every ḥijāb is necessarily a barzakh, nor can every barzakh be likened to a material curtain.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:46, for example, concerns a partition between two groups.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:100 uses barzakh in relation to the human condition after death.

Their separation imagery is comparable, but the passages concern different subjects.

Ḥ J B (ḥijāb) and K N N (akinnah): an intervening barrier and enclosing coverings

In Fuṣṣilat 41:5, أَكِنَّة (akinnah) describes coverings associated with hearts, while ḥijāb describes a barrier between the speakers and the Prophet.

The sentence’s imagery makes the distinction clear.

Akinnah concerns something within which the hearts are situated.

Ḥijāb concerns something between two sides.

This does not make akinnah invariably internal to the body or ḥijāb invariably external.

This verse uses two different images for refusing the message.

Ḥ J B (ḥijāb) and KH M R (khimār): barrier and clothing

خِمَار (khimār) belongs to KH M R. In clothing usage it denotes a covering, especially a head covering, whose extent requires attention to its use and context.

Al-Nūr 24:31 contains بِخُمُرِهِنَّ (bi-khumurihinna).

The seven Qur’anic instances of ḥijāb do not use it as that name for clothing.

The linguistic distinction matters.

Ḥijāb primarily names a barrier or partition; khimār names a type of covering.

A garment may in practice obstruct sight, but this shared function does not make the words synonymous in the Qur’an.

The instruction in Al-Nūr 24:31 therefore requires examining khumur, جُيُوب (juyūb), and the verse’s construction, rather than beginning with contemporary ḥijāb terminology.

Ḥ J B (ḥijāb) and J L B (jilbāb): partition and garment

Study of J L B

Al-Aḥzāb 33:59 contains جَلَابِيبِهِنَّ (jalābībihinna).

جِلْبَاب (jilbāb) names a garment. In the Qur’anic morphological classification used by Forqan and the Quranic Arabic Corpus, jalābīb is listed under J L B; the repeated bāʾ in its form does not by itself establish an independent quadriliteral root here.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: jalābīb under J L B

Ḥijāb in Al-Aḥzāb 33:53, however, occurs in min warāʾi ḥijābin and concerns a partition between the sides of a conversation.

The verses belong to one surah but use different words in different settings.

The rules and characteristics of either should therefore not be transferred wholesale to the other without separate examination.

Nor does contemporary use of ḥijāb for women’s covering change the historical meanings of jilbāb or khimār.

8. Interpretive challenges and terminological change

Ḥijāb today and in the Qur’an

Contemporary Persian and Arabic commonly use ḥijāb for women’s covering, especially of hair and body.

This is familiar in modern language and religious culture.

The Qur’an nevertheless uses ḥijāb in varied settings: a partition between two groups, Mary’s secluded space, privacy in the Prophet’s household, an impediment to receiving the message, a screen behind which something disappears, divine communication, and being barred from the Lord.

Making ḥijāb exclusively the name for women’s covering therefore restricts its wider Qur’anic meaning.

That technical usage may be established socially and legally, but should not replace lexical meaning in a root study.

Conversely, understanding ḥijāb alone does not resolve every issue concerning clothing, privacy, modesty, or women’s social presence.

Each requires examination of its own relevant verses and terms.

Is every ḥijāb material?

No.

In Maryam 19:17 and Al-Aḥzāb 33:53, ḥijāb concerns a partition within human living space.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:45 and Fuṣṣilat 41:5 concern a barrier in relation to message and understanding.

Al-Shūrā 42:51 uses ḥijāb in divine communication.

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:15 concerns deprivation in relation to the Lord.

These uses show that ḥijāb can be material or nonmaterial.

The root alone therefore does not establish every barrier’s shape, material, or physical location.

Does a screen always prevent all communication?

No.

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:53, people can make requests from behind a screen.

Al-Shūrā 42:51 presents speech from behind a screen as a form of communication.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:46, a partition coexists with recognition and calling within the same scene.

A screen therefore need not sever every avenue of communication.

One must identify what it prevents: sight, entry, approach, receiving the message, or another kind of access.

Is being maḥjūb always punishment?

Maḥjūb itself means screened off or barred from access.

This may happen for privacy, protection, regulation of access, or deprivation.

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:15 places it in a context of condemnation and punishment.

Mary’s adopting a screen in Maryam 19:17, however, concerns seclusion and separation from others.

The root alone therefore does not determine the barrier’s moral value.

The same kind of obstacle can preserve privacy in one setting and deny access to something good in another.

Does a divine screen establish a physical divine location?

Al-Shūrā 42:51 mentions speaking from behind a screen.

It does not say The God is inside a room or behind a material curtain.

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:15 likewise describes a group being barred from their Lord.

Both concern the human relationship with The God, without defining divine being, location, or human perception in the hereafter.

Spatial imagery or metaphor should therefore not yield an unevidenced physical architecture of the unseen.

The relationship actually expressed must also remain intact. The mode of communication in Al-Shūrā 42:51 and deprivation in Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:15 are real parts of the respective statements’ meanings.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

For ḥijābun in Al-Aʿrāf 7:46, “screen” or “partition” is appropriate. Contextual explanation can identify a barrier between two groups.

For ḥijāban mastūran in Al-Isrāʾ 17:45, “a concealed and hidden barrier” preserves mastūr’s passive form. The impediment to receiving the message belongs in reflection.

For fa-ittakhadhat min dūnihim ḥijāban in Maryam 19:17, “she placed a screen between herself and them” is appropriate.

For min warāʾi ḥijābin in Al-Aḥzāb 33:53, “from behind a screen” or “from behind a partition” preserves the Arabic construction.

For tawārat bi-al-ḥijābi in Ṣād 38:32, “disappeared behind the screen” is appropriate. The pronoun’s referent should not be added to the base translation without contextual examination.

For wa-min bayninā wa-baynika ḥijābun in Fuṣṣilat 41:5, “between us and you is a barrier” is direct. Reflection can clarify the intellectual and communicative separation.

For min warāʾi ḥijābin in Al-Shūrā 42:51, “from behind a screen” is appropriate, without inserting a material divine location.

For la-maḥjūbūna in Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:15, “certainly screened off and kept away” or “certainly denied access to their Lord” can be used. The former is closer to the passive-participle form; the latter makes its contextual meaning more explicit.

Forqan’s word boxes should likewise keep two fields separate.

Brief root meaning: “Covering, obstructing, and separating; preventing sight or access.”

Meaning in each verse: Depending on context, a partition, an impediment to receiving the message, separation in conversation, disappearance from sight, or deprivation of access.

One fixed explanation such as “women’s covering” is inaccurate across these forms.

10. Conclusion

Arabic Ḥ J B concerns obstructing, covering, and separating.

Ibn Fāris identifies prevention as its core; other sources describe ḥijāb as anything intervening between two sides.

The barrier may be material, such as a curtain, door, or partition, or nonmaterial, such as an impediment to understanding, access, or communication.

The root occurs eight times in the Qur’an.

Ḥijāb separates two groups in Al-Aʿrāf 7:46; marks Mary’s seclusion in Maryam 19:17; concerns conversational privacy in the Prophet’s household in Al-Aḥzāb 33:53; and hides something from sight in Ṣād 38:32.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:45 and Fuṣṣilat 41:5 use it in connection with receiving the message and separation between the Prophet and his opponents.

Al-Shūrā 42:51 describes divine speech to a human being from behind a screen; maḥjūbūna in Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:15 expresses a group’s deprivation of access to their Lord.

Thus Qur’anic ḥijāb primarily denotes a barrier or partition, rather than exclusively naming a particular garment.

Women’s covering is an established contemporary technical use, but it should not restrict every Qur’anic meaning.

In clothing passages, the Qur’an uses other terms such as khumur and jalābīb. Their meanings and associated instructions require independent examination.

The shared feature across Ḥ J B is a barrier altering the direct relationship between two sides.

It may prevent sight, entry, access, or reception of a message, or mark deprivation of an important relationship or benefit.

Understanding this range preserves Qur’anic distinctions and prevents later religious and social terminology from replacing the verses’ direct meanings without lexical and contextual evidence.
