Unwillingness and imposition, opposition and separation, and finding a way out of constraint

Arabic R Gh M has several uses concerning dust, abasement, displeasure, opposition, and release from an imposed situation. Raghām denotes earth or fine dust; raghm, abasement or something happening against someone’s wishes; murāghamah, opposition and separation; and murāgham, an escape route, refuge, or place to go to escape pressure.

The familiar expression raghima anfuhu evokes a nose being rubbed in dust. In Arabic usage, this image conveys humiliation, frustration, and displeasure.

Another use concerns someone leaving their people and choosing a different course against their wishes. Rāghama qawmahu can mean opposing one’s people and leaving them.

In the spatial use of this family, murāgham denotes a place someone can go to distance themselves from pressure or an unwelcome situation. This semantic connection alone does not establish the historical direction of derivation.

The distinction matters: one use describes someone subjected to abasement against their will; another describes someone leaving those who seek to constrain them, thereby acting against their wishes.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as murāghaman.

The verse concerns someone emigrating in The God’s way and finding many routes or places on earth for departure, release, and settlement.

Here murāgham appropriately means a way out of constraint or a place for migration and release from pressure, rather than abasement or humiliating another person.

Qur’anic form: مُرَاغَمًا, murāghaman (Al-Nisāʾ 4:100).

Other recorded Arabic forms include raghām, raghm, rughm, righm, raghama, raghima, raghuma, rāghim, raghīm, arghama, irghām, raghghama, targhīm, rāghama, murāghamah, murāghim, murāgham, margham, marghamah, and taraghghama.

Apart from murāgham, which occurs with accusative nunation, the other listed forms do not occur in the Qur’an. Their lexical range should be examined independently of the Qur’anic use.

1. Two principal branches in early Arabic

Al-raghām: earth and fine dust

One lexical analysis identifies two semantic branches:

الرَّاءُ وَالْغَيْنُ وَالْمِيمُ أَصْلَانِ: أَحَدُهُمَا التُّرَابُ، وَالْآخَرُ الْمَذْهَبُ

al-rāʾu wa-al-ghaynu wa-al-mīmu aṣlāni: aḥaduhumā al-turābu wa-al-ākharu al-madhhabu

“Rāʾ, ghayn, and mīm have two semantic foundations: earth, and a route or place to go.”

The first branch is explained as follows:

فَالْأَوَّلُ الرَّغَامُ، وَهُوَ التُّرَابُ

fa-al-awwalu al-raghāmu wa-huwa al-turābu

“The first is raghām, meaning earth.”

Other lexical accounts record raghām for soft earth, dust, or sand mixed with soil.

The precise material described nevertheless differs among explanations.

Some describe soft earth.

Others mention sand mixed with soil.

Another explanation emphasizes the fineness of the particles of earth.

These differences do not support a precise modern definition in terms of particle size or geological composition.

The clearer meaning is earth or dust familiar in early Arabic usage.

Raghima anfuhu: a nose rubbed in dust

Arabic has the familiar expression:

رَغِمَ أَنْفُهُ

raghima anfuhu

Its literal meaning is:

“His nose was rubbed in dust.”

In social usage, however, it conveys abasement, defeat, frustration, or displeasure.

Arabic imagery can associate the nose with dignity and pride.

Its contact with dust pictures a fall from dignity or being forced to accept an unwelcome situation.

The expression therefore need not report a physical event.

Saying raghima anfuhu does not require the person’s nose actually to have touched earth.

The intended meaning may be that they were frustrated or humbled against their wishes, or could not prevent something from happening.

Arghamahu likewise means humbling someone or forcing them into a situation contrary to their wishes.

Not every use of the family should be confined to the nose-and-dust image.

Qur’anic murāghaman concerns routes or places for migration, not rubbing someone’s nose in dust.

Raghm: something contrary to someone’s wishes

Lexically, raghm is associated with something happening unwillingly and with abasement.

Arabic records:

فَعَلَهُ عَلَى رَغْمِهِ

faʿalahu ʿalā raghmihi

“He did it against his wishes.”

Persian also uses expressions such as be-raghm-e khāst-e ū and ʿalā-raghm-e mokhālefat-e ū: despite his wishes or opposition.

The root’s social meaning can thus concern an event being unwelcome to someone.

Two situations must nevertheless be distinguished.

Someone may be compelled to act against their will.

Or another person may do something that displeases them without necessarily violating their rights.

Leaving an oppressive group, for example, may go against its leaders’ wishes.

Their displeasure alone does not prove that the person leaving has done wrong.

Raghm and murāghamah may therefore be understood in terms of people’s wishes and opposition, while the act’s moral worth is not determined by the word alone.

Raghama: becoming humbled or unable to obtain redress

Raghama and raghima are recorded for someone humbled, placed unwillingly in a situation, or unable to recover their rights.

The usage is explained as follows:

وَرَغَمَ فُلَانٌ، إِذَا لَمْ يَقْدِرْ عَلَى الِانْتِصَافِ

wa-raghama fulānun idhā lam yaqdir ʿalā al-intiṣāfi

“A person raghama when unable to obtain redress.”

Abasement or frustration can thus concern an inability to resist others’ pressure.

Inability and humiliation are not always identical.

Someone may be physically or economically weak yet honorable in character and conduct.

Conversely, a powerful person may become disgraced through their own conduct.

R Gh M should therefore not be confined to poverty, physical weakness, or low social status.

The point here is experiencing abasement, pressure, or something occurring against one’s wishes.

2. Murāghamah: opposition, separation, and departure

Murāghamah is an important use of this family.

A lexical definition states:

وَالْمُرَاغَمَةُ: الْهِجْرَانُ

wa-al-murāghamatu al-hijrānu

“Murāghamah means separation and leaving a relationship.”

The explanation allows someone to leave their family or group for a time and later return.

Murāghamah therefore need not mean permanent separation.

A disagreement between individuals or groups may lead one party to leave the other temporarily.

In another use, separation can be lasting and involve relocation.

Rāghama qawmahu

This construction is explained as follows:

وَيُقَالُ رَاغَمَ فُلَانٌ قَوْمَهُ: نَابَذَهُمْ وَخَرَجَ عَنْهُمْ

wa-yuqālu rāghama fulānun qawmahu: nābadhahum wa-kharaja ʿanhum

“Someone rāghama his people: he opposed them and departed from among them.”

Two events are connected in this expression.

First, the person opposes their group.

Second, they separate from it.

The two should not always be treated as a single act.

A person may disagree with a group while continuing to live among them.

Someone may also leave town for trade, work, or a family visit without hostility or disagreement with its inhabitants.

Murāghamah here concerns separation accompanied by opposition and displeasure.

Not every journey or relocation can therefore be called murāghamah.

Rāghamahum: leaving against others’ wishes

Another definition states:

وَرَاغَمَ قَوْمَهُ: نَبَذَهُمْ وَخَرَجَ عَنْهُمْ وَعَادَاهُمْ

wa-rāghama qawmahu: nabadhahum wa-kharaja ʿanhum wa-ʿādāhum

“He rāghama his people: he abandoned them, left them, and opposed or became hostile to them.”

Murāghamah can thus concern frustrating the former group’s wishes or control.

Opposition must nevertheless be distinguished from violence.

Simply leaving a group may constitute acting against its wishes.

The former group may want the person to remain and follow its belief or command, while they choose another path.

Separation itself can then be practical opposition.

Murāghamah should therefore not always be translated as fighting or directly harming another person.

Opposition can also appear in conduct, choice of direction, and leaving an environment.

Is every act of opposition right?

No.

Lexically, opposition and separation can serve different purposes.

Someone may leave a group to escape injustice and preserve freedom.

Someone else may abandon a relationship to evade responsibility, betray others, or commit a wrong.

Rāghama itself does not establish either party’s rightness.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:100 specifies the migration’s direction through fī sabīli Allāh.

Its positive orientation comes from that qualification and the context, not from every murāghamah being inherently commendable.

3. Murāgham: a way out, refuge, and place of migration

After earth and the related uses concerning abasement, the other branch is described as follows:

وَالْأَصْلُ الْآخَرُ الْمُرَاغَمُ، وَهُوَ الْمَذْهَبُ وَالْمَهْرَبُ

wa-al-aṣlu al-ākharu al-murāghamu wa-huwa al-madhhabu wa-al-mahrabu

“The other foundation is murāgham: a route or place to go, and an escape.”

Madhhab here means a place or route someone can take.

Mahrab likewise concerns a route or place of escape.

Murāgham thus expresses an opportunity to leave the present situation and go elsewhere.

Mā lī ʿan dhāka al-amri murāghamun

Arabic records this expression:

مَا لِي عَنْ ذَاكَ الْأَمْرِ مُرَاغَمٌ

mā lī ʿan dhāka al-amri murāghamun

“I have no way out of that matter.”

It shows that murāgham can denote an exit from a situation.

It need not mean a mountain route or a specific passageway.

Living circumstances, obligations, or existing pressure may leave someone seeing no alternative.

Murāgham can therefore concern release from constraint in different uses.

It should not be generalized to solving every problem or succeeding in every undertaking.

The Qur’anic use concerns migration and finding routes or places to go.

Murāgham: refuge and fortress

Murāgham is also recorded lexically for a fortress or refuge.

This concerns a place where someone can take shelter or remain beyond opponents’ pressure and reach.

The word does not always mean a fortress built of walls and stone.

A secure setting could be a town, region, or social environment where a person can live and act.

Murāghaman in Al-Nisāʾ 4:100 should therefore not simply become “many castles.”

A fortress is one possible lexical instance; the verse concerns abundant opportunities for migration on earth.

Is murāgham always a secure place?

Many lexical and interpretive explanations connect it with refuge or escape.

Having a way out must nevertheless be distinguished from guaranteed security.

Someone may leave danger and find another route while migration remains difficult.

A new destination may escape the former pressure yet present other economic or social problems.

Murāgham emphasizes going elsewhere and distancing oneself, not an absolute guarantee against every danger or difficulty.

This distinction matters in Al-Nisāʾ 4:100.

4. Early Arabic poetic witnesses

ʿAlā raghmihi in poetry by Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā

A verse by the pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā reads:

فَرَدَّ عَلَيْنَا الْعَيْرَ مِنْ دُونِ إِلْفِهِ
عَلَىٰ رَغْمِهِ يَدْمَى نَسَاهُ وَفَائِلُهْ

fa-radda ʿalaynā al-ʿayra min dūni ilfihi / ʿalā raghmihi yadmā nasāhu wa-fāʾiluh

“He drove the wild ass back toward us, away from its mate and against its will, with parts of its leg bleeding.”

Al-ʿayr denotes a male wild ass here.

Ilfuhu is its mate or familiar companion.

Al-nasā and al-fāʾil concern parts of the animal’s leg and associated tissue.

The poet describes a hunt in which the animal is unwillingly separated from its mate and driven back toward the hunters.

The key expression is:

عَلَىٰ رَغْمِهِ

ʿalā raghmihi

That is, “against its will.”

This witness matters for early usage: raghm expresses the unwelcome character of an event in language attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

The line says nothing about migration or finding refuge.

It illuminates unwillingness and imposition, rather than directly explaining Qur’anic murāgham.

Nor does it establish that raghm always means hunting or wounding an animal.

Those events belong to the poem’s scene; ʿalā raghmihi expresses their unwelcome character.

“Like a mountain whose sides offer refuge”: al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī

A verse by al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī reads:

كَطَوْدٍ يُلَاذُ بِأَرْكَانِهِ
عَزِيزِ الْمُرَاغَمِ وَالْمَهْرَبِ

ka-ṭawdin yulādhu bi-arkānihi / ʿazīzi al-murāghami wa-al-mahrabi

“Like a mountain whose sides offer refuge, with a formidable, secure retreat and shelter.”

Ṭawd denotes a great mountain.

Yulādhu bi-arkānihi concerns taking refuge along its sides or slopes.

Al-murāgham and al-mahrab appear together.

Their combination connects murāgham in the poem with escape or refuge.

The precise sense of ʿazīz requires context: it may suggest strength, inaccessibility, or the position’s firmness.

These two hemistichs alone do not establish the location’s exact geography or construction.

The line is cited as evidence in lexical and interpretive explanations of murāgham.

Al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. The line’s precise date is unclear.

It is significant early Arabic evidence, but should not be declared definitively pre-Qur’anic without independent evidence.

“To a land whose location is not near”

A lexical and grammatical explanation of the verse cites this line:

إِلَىٰ بَلَدٍ غَيْرِ دَانِي الْمَحَلِّ
بَعِيدِ الْمُرَاغَمِ وَالْمُضْطَرَبْ

ilā baladin ghayri dānī al-maḥalli / baʿīdi al-murāghami wa-al-muḍṭarab

“To a land whose location is not near, whose place of retreat and movement is far away.”

Al-murāgham accompanies al-muḍṭarab here.

Muḍṭarab concerns a place of movement and activity.

This combination supports the spatial meaning of murāgham.

The poet and exact date are unclear in the transmissions examined.

It cannot therefore be assigned to a particular poet or treated as certain pre-Islamic evidence.

Its citation in linguistic explanation illustrates murāgham as a route or place to go and seek escape.

What the poetic witnesses show

Zuhayr’s ʿalā raghmihi means against the wishes of the person or creature concerned.

Al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī’s murāgham accompanies mahrab and concerns refuge or escape.

In the third witness, murāgham likewise accompanies a place of movement and activity.

The two domains—unwillingness and finding a way out—were therefore familiar in early Arabic.

They should not be reduced incautiously to one historical image.

5. The structure of Qur’anic murāghaman

The root’s sole Qur’anic form is:

مُرَاغَمًا

murāghaman

Al-Nisāʾ 4:100

“An escape route or place to go elsewhere.”

Its structure is related to Form III rāghama.

Rāghama and Form III

Rāghama belongs to Form III, the mufāʿalah pattern.

Lexical usage connects it with opposition, separation, and leaving a group or person.

Form III can denote reciprocal action in some verbs, but reciprocity is not a fixed requirement for all of them.

In rāghama qawmahu, for example, disagreement may exist between a person and their people.

That does not mean every murāghamah necessarily involves two-sided warfare or equally matched reciprocal action.

Merely departing from the group can run against its wishes.

Rāghama must therefore be understood through indications of opposition, separation, and the person’s circumstances.

Murāgham: place noun or passive participle?

Murāgham has the same morphological pattern as a Form III passive participle and a place noun derived from a non-basic verb.

The structure can be a Form III passive participle; place nouns and mīm-prefixed verbal nouns from non-basic verbs may also share this pattern.

In lexical usage and the verse’s context, a place or route of departure and escape fits the sentence. This should not be replaced by a literal passive-participle translation.

Arabic place nouns derived from non-basic verbs can have the same outward structure as passive participles.

A morphological label must therefore be distinguished from contextual meaning.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:100, murāghaman does not simply mean a person who has been opposed.

It denotes a route or place a migrant can find to leave pressure and settle elsewhere.

The spatial and contextual sense matters for translation.

Murāghaman: an indefinite accusative noun

Murāghaman is indefinite and accusative in the verse.

It is the object of yajid.

Kathīran modifies it and means abundant.

The construction is:

يَجِدْ فِي الْأَرْضِ مُرَاغَمًا كَثِيرًا

yajid fī al-arḍi murāghaman kathīran

Al-Nisāʾ 4:100

“He finds on earth many routes and places for departure and release.”

Murāgham is outwardly singular, but kathīran can convey abundant places, routes, or opportunities associated with it.

Natural translation may therefore speak of many routes or places.

The construction does not specify a particular number of destinations.

Kathīran expresses abundance, not an exact count.

6. “He finds on earth abundant murāgham and spaciousness”: Al-Nisāʾ

The root’s sole Qur’anic occurrence appears in this verse:

وَمَنْ يُهَاجِرْ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ يَجِدْ فِي الْأَرْضِ مُرَاغَمًا كَثِيرًا وَسَعَةً وَمَنْ يَخْرُجْ مِنْ بَيْتِهِ مُهَاجِرًا إِلَى اللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ ثُمَّ يُدْرِكْهُ الْمَوْتُ فَقَدْ وَقَعَ أَجْرُهُ عَلَى اللَّهِ وَكَانَ اللَّهُ غَفُورًا رَحِيمًا

wa-man yuhājir fī sabīli Allāhi yajid fī al-arḍi murāghaman kathīran wa-saʿatan wa-man yakhruj min baytihi muhājiran ilā Allāhi wa-rasūlihi thumma yudrikhu al-mawtu fa-qad waqaʿa ajruhu ʿalā Allāhi wa-kāna Allāhu ghafūran raḥīman

Al-Nisāʾ 4:100

“Whoever emigrates in The God’s way will find on earth many routes and places for release, and spaciousness. Whoever leaves home emigrating toward The God and His Messenger, then death overtakes him, his reward has become due from The God. The God is forgiving and merciful.”

Murāghaman is directly connected with migration here.

Understanding it requires attention to migration, the circumstances before departure, and finding another place.

Wa-man yuhājir fī sabīli Allāh

Yuhājir belongs to H J R.

Here it concerns leaving one’s former location and going elsewhere in The God’s way.

It accompanies conditional man and is jussive.

Yajid is the conditional result and is also jussive.

The sentence links migration in The God’s way with finding other routes or places on earth.

Hijrah and murāgham are nevertheless different words.

Hijrah expresses leaving and going away.

Murāgham denotes a route or place where one can distance oneself from previous pressure.

Both should not receive one mechanical translation, “migration.”

Movement and the possibility of finding a destination are related but distinct parts of the expression.

Fī al-arḍ: the geographical breadth of choice

The verse says:

فِي الْأَرْضِ

fī al-arḍi

Al-Nisāʾ 4:100

“On earth.”

This identifies the geographical field in which another route or place can be sought.

The preceding verses also mention the breadth of The God’s earth for migration.

The verse’s meaning is therefore not confined to one town or destination.

It does not mean that every person at every time can move anywhere without legal, geographical, economic, or security obstacles and live there.

Many possible routes on earth differ from equal practical access for everyone.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:98 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:99 also address this distinction.

Murāghaman kathīran: ways out of constraint

Lexical and interpretive accounts offer several explanations:

Mutaḥawwal: a place to which one can relocate.

Madhhab: a route or place to go.

Mahrab: a way of escape.

Mandūḥah: an alternative or release from something unwelcome.

Mahājar: a place of migration.

These explanations are semantically close.

They share the opportunity to leave a previous situation and find somewhere else to continue life or preserve one’s chosen course.

They do not, however, express precisely the same concept.

Mahrab emphasizes escape from danger or pressure.

Mutaḥawwal emphasizes relocation between situations.

Mandūḥah emphasizes an available alternative and room for relief.

Mahājar emphasizes a place of migration.

These possibilities fit the verse, but none should be declared its sole certain meaning without evidence.

Wa-saʿatan: spaciousness alongside murāgham

The expression continues:

وَسَعَةً

wa-saʿatan

Al-Nisāʾ 4:100

“And spaciousness.”

Saʿah belongs to W S ʿ, not R Gh M.

This distinction matters.

Murāghaman concerns a route or place for migration and release from pressure.

Saʿatan denotes relief and expanded possibilities.

Some interpretations connect saʿah with ample provision.

Others include broader living opportunities, freedom of action, and release from social or religious constraint.

These senses can coexist; saʿah need not be restricted to wealth.

This reading fits the context: someone leaving pressure may gain greater freedom, security, and opportunity to live.

Saʿah itself nevertheless does not mean certain enrichment.

The verse should not be translated as guaranteeing wealth to every migrant at their destination.

Are murāgham and saʿah complete synonyms?

No.

They can complement one another in the same sentence, but belong to different roots.

Murāgham emphasizes finding an exit and another destination.

Saʿah expresses accompanying or resulting spaciousness and relief.

Someone may find a way to leave a restrictive environment yet need further time to improve their financial and social circumstances.

Finding an exit and achieving fuller relief can thus be two stages or aspects.

The verse mentions both together without erasing their difference.

7. The preceding verses: opportunity and genuine inability

To understand murāghaman in Al-Nisāʾ 4:100, consider Al-Nisāʾ 4:97, Al-Nisāʾ 4:98, and Al-Nisāʾ 4:99 as well.

“Was The God’s earth not spacious?”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:97 describes people questioned by the angels about their circumstances at death.

They reply:

كُنَّا مُسْتَضْعَفِينَ فِي الْأَرْضِ

kunnā mustaḍʿafīna fī al-arḍi

Al-Nisāʾ 4:97

“We were held in weakness on earth.”

The response is:

أَلَمْ تَكُنْ أَرْضُ اللَّهِ وَاسِعَةً فَتُهَاجِرُوا فِيهَا

a-lam takun arḍu Allāhi wāsiʿatan fa-tuhājirū fīhā

Al-Nisāʾ 4:97

“Was The God’s earth not spacious enough for you to emigrate within it?”

Remaining under pressure and subjugation must, in some circumstances, be weighed against the possibility of leaving.

The Qur’an immediately distinguishes those with an exit from those genuinely unable to leave.

“Unable to devise a means or find a way”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:98 states:

إِلَّا الْمُسْتَضْعَفِينَ مِنَ الرِّجَالِ وَالنِّسَاءِ وَالْوِلْدَانِ لَا يَسْتَطِيعُونَ حِيلَةً وَلَا يَهْتَدُونَ سَبِيلًا

illā al-mustaḍʿafīna mina al-rijāli wa-al-nisāʾi wa-al-wildāni lā yastaṭīʿūna ḥīlatan wa-lā yahtadūna sabīlan

Al-Nisāʾ 4:98

“Except the men, women, and children held in weakness, unable to devise a means or find a way.”

This exception is essential.

The Qur’an does not disregard genuine inability.

Physical, financial, family, or security circumstances, or lack of access to a migration route, may prevent departure.

An apparent route may also exist without the person being capable of using it.

The verse identifies two limitations:

لَا يَسْتَطِيعُونَ حِيلَةً

lā yastaṭīʿūna ḥīlatan

Al-Nisāʾ 4:98

“They are unable to devise a means.”

And:

وَلَا يَهْتَدُونَ سَبِيلًا

wa-lā yahtadūna sabīlan

Al-Nisāʾ 4:98

“They do not find a way.”

The theoretical existence of a route is not the same as a person’s real ability to use it.

Murāghaman kathīran should not therefore imply that every constrained person can certainly migrate regardless of their actual circumstances.

“These The God may pardon”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:99 states:

فَأُولَٰئِكَ عَسَى اللَّهُ أَنْ يَعْفُوَ عَنْهُمْ وَكَانَ اللَّهُ عَفُوًّا غَفُورًا

fa-ulāʾika ʿasā Allāhu an yaʿfuwa ʿanhum wa-kāna Allāhu ʿafuwwan ghafūran

Al-Nisāʾ 4:99

“These The God may pardon; The God is pardoning and forgiving.”

This continuation shows the importance of real inability in assessing a person’s position.

The migration verse should not become a rule assigning equal responsibility to everyone remaining in an unsuitable environment irrespective of their actual options.

Capacity, access, and constraints form part of the context.

Murāghaman: release, not an assurance of absolute ease

Within this passage, verse 100 describes finding multiple routes and places for migration.

This can counter the belief that every route is closed.

It does not deny migration’s difficulties.

Verse 100 itself raises the possibility of death overtaking a person during migration.

It thus describes release and spaciousness while recognizing difficulty and an unfinished journey.

Both aspects belong together.

8. “Then death overtakes him”: effort’s value before arrival

The second part of Al-Nisāʾ 4:100 states:

وَمَنْ يَخْرُجْ مِنْ بَيْتِهِ مُهَاجِرًا إِلَى اللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ ثُمَّ يُدْرِكْهُ الْمَوْتُ فَقَدْ وَقَعَ أَجْرُهُ عَلَى اللَّهِ

wa-man yakhruj min baytihi muhājiran ilā Allāhi wa-rasūlihi thumma yudrikhu al-mawtu fa-qad waqaʿa ajruhu ʿalā Allāhi

Al-Nisāʾ 4:100

“Whoever leaves home emigrating toward The God and His Messenger, then death overtakes him, his reward has become due from The God.”

This matters for the verse’s message.

Beginning migration and reaching a destination

Yakhruj min baytihi describes departure from home.

Muhājiran describes the person’s state on leaving.

Thumma yudrikhu al-mawtu shows that death may overtake them before arrival.

The Qur’an nevertheless speaks of their reward.

Here the value of effort is not confined to successful arrival.

This comes from the verse’s second part, not from R Gh M itself.

Murāgham concerns a route or place of escape and migration.

Ajr concerns reward.

Khurūj concerns going out.

Mawt concerns death.

These concepts are related yet lexically independent.

Does the verse guarantee every migrant’s material success?

No.

Such a reading would have to account for the second part, which explicitly allows death during the journey.

Verse 100 describes finding routes and relief while also allowing that a migrant may not reach the destination.

Their reward is nevertheless presented as secured with The God.

The promise should not be restricted to economic success, complete bodily security, or everyone’s certain arrival at a desired destination.

Its contextual message is broader.

Responsibility in choosing a route and genuine constraints

Together, Al-Nisāʾ 4:97, Al-Nisāʾ 4:98, Al-Nisāʾ 4:99, and Al-Nisāʾ 4:100 present the possibility of leaving pressure while recognizing real human limitations.

They neither absolutely justify remaining without reason in harmful circumstances nor condemn those lacking capacity and a route without considering their situation.

This does not mean everyone should set out on an unknown, dangerous route without assessing security, legal passage, finances, or acceptance at the destination.

Verse 98 explicitly mentions lacking a means and a way.

Real capability and access therefore matter in practical assessment.

R Gh M itself does not authorize forged documents, violations of others’ rights, or disregard of ethical and social responsibilities.

Such matters require independent Qur’anic criteria and attention to actual circumstances.

9. Does murāghaman mean humiliating an enemy?

The question arises from the relation between raghm and murāghamah.

Some interpretations explain that leaving an oppressive environment frustrates opponents by acting against their wishes.

This is understandable in relation to rāghama qawmahu.

Two matters must nevertheless be distinguished.

Leaving pressure may frustrate those imposing their wishes and control.

The person’s main aim, however, may be preserving freedom, faith, security, and life rather than humiliating others.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:100 describes migration in The God’s way and finding abundant murāgham and spaciousness on earth.

The expression itself commands neither humiliation of opponents nor revenge.

Frustrating oppressors may result from departure, but should not become the word’s sole direct meaning.

Release from pressure and permanent hostility

Arabic murāghamah can accompany anger and opposition.

Such opposition need not produce permanent hostility.

Someone may leave a group to preserve their rights and later establish just relations if circumstances change.

Not every migration means eternally severing all ties or becoming hostile to everyone in the former community.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:100 concerns finding another route and place on earth.

Murāgham itself does not determine the migrant’s later relationship with former neighbors.

10. Other verses about migration and spaciousness

“My earth is spacious”

Al-ʿAnkabūt states:

يَا عِبَادِيَ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِنَّ أَرْضِي وَاسِعَةٌ فَإِيَّايَ فَاعْبُدُونِ

yā ʿibādiya alladhīna āmanū inna arḍī wāsiʿatun fa-iyyāya fa-uʿbudūni

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:56

“My servants who believe, My earth is spacious; worship Me alone.”

This is conceptually connected with Al-Nisāʾ 4:97, Al-Nisāʾ 4:98, Al-Nisāʾ 4:99, and Al-Nisāʾ 4:100.

Both passages connect earth’s breadth with worship and human orientation.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:56 does not itself contain murāgham.

It informs the context and concept of migration, rather than providing another occurrence of R Gh M.

“Those who emigrated for The God after being wronged”

Al-Naḥl states:

وَالَّذِينَ هَاجَرُوا فِي اللَّهِ مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا ظُلِمُوا لَنُبَوِّئَنَّهُمْ فِي الدُّنْيَا حَسَنَةً

wa-alladhīna hājarū fī Allāhi min baʿdi mā ẓulimū la-nubawwiʾannahum fī al-dunyā ḥasanatan

Al-Naḥl 16:41

“Those who emigrated for The God after being wronged—We shall certainly give them a good place in this world.”

Migration follows the experience of injustice here.

This fits murāghaman as a route or place of release from pressure.

It does not make R Gh M itself mean being oppressed.

Oppression comes from min baʿdi mā ẓulimū, and migration from hājarū.

This verse helps illuminate migration, while murāgham’s independent meaning still rests on lexical evidence and Al-Nisāʾ 4:100.

“Those who emigrated and strove in The God’s way”

Al-Baqarah states:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَالَّذِينَ هَاجَرُوا وَجَاهَدُوا فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ أُولَٰئِكَ يَرْجُونَ رَحْمَتَ اللَّهِ

inna alladhīna āmanū wa-alladhīna hājarū wa-jāhadū fī sabīli Allāhi ulāʾika yarjūna raḥmata Allāhi

Al-Baqarah 2:218

“Those who believe, and those who emigrated and strove in The God’s way: they hope for The God’s mercy.”

Migration accompanies faith and striving here.

These three words do not have identical meanings.

Migration concerns leaving a previous setting or location.

Striving concerns effort in the relevant cause.

Faith concerns acceptance and belief.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:100 concerns a route or place for migration.

These verses can be studied together in an independent inquiry into migration, but their meanings should not all be transferred to R Gh M.

11. R Gh M and related roots

R Gh M (murāgham) and H J R (hijrah)

Arabic H J R concerns leaving and distancing oneself.

Murāgham concerns a route or place found for escape, separation, and release from pressure.

Both concepts occur in Al-Nisāʾ 4:100.

Yuhājir describes emigrating.

Murāghaman denotes a route or place of departure and release.

Rāghama qawmahu also emphasizes opposition to a group and departure from it.

Not every migration is necessarily hostile murāghamah.

People may move for study, work, or family visits without disagreeing with their former community.

Murāghamah in its oppositional use, by contrast, commonly involves separation against the other group’s wishes.

The roots are related but not complete synonyms.

R Gh M (murāgham) and F R R (firār)

Farra concerns fleeing and moving away from danger or something unwelcome.

Murāgham can denote a route or place of escape.

They are close in the domain of release from pressure.

Firār emphasizes fleeing itself, while Qur’anic murāgham emphasizes finding another route or place.

Murāghamah can also involve opposition and separation, which flight need not involve.

Someone may flee a natural danger without opposing anyone.

R Gh M should therefore not be confined to fear or fleeing an enemy.

R Gh M (murāgham) and W S ʿ (saʿah)

Study of W S ʿ

Saʿah concerns spaciousness and relief.

It directly accompanies murāghaman in Al-Nisāʾ 4:100.

The roots nevertheless differ.

Murāgham denotes an escape route or place of relocation.

Saʿah denotes breadth and spaciousness.

Finding murāgham may make saʿah possible, but this relationship does not make the words identical.

Translating murāghaman kathīran wa-saʿatan simply as “great relief” loses part of their distinction.

R Gh M (raghm) and K R H (karh)

Karh concerns unpleasantness and something contrary to a person’s wishes.

Social uses of raghm likewise concern displeasure and unwillingness.

For example, ʿalā raghmihi and ʿalā karāhiyatihi can have similar meanings in some sentences.

Other R Gh M uses concern earth, abasement, opposition, and escape.

Not every form can therefore be translated as compulsion.

In particular, murāghaman in Al-Nisāʾ 4:100 does not mean being forced; it occurs in a setting of departure and finding alternatives.

R Gh M (raghm) and Dh L L (dhull)

Study of Dh L L

Dhull concerns abasement and lowliness.

One use of R Gh M connects a nose rubbed in dust with abasement.

The roots overlap in this domain.

Dhull does not lexically denote a migration route or escape location.

Murāgham in Al-Nisāʾ concerns leaving pressure, not the migrant’s humiliation.

The abasement branch alone should not lead to translating the Qur’anic form as a place of humiliation.

R Gh M (raghm) and R Gh B (raghbah)

Study of R Gh B

The roots look similar but differ in their third letter.

R Gh M ends in mīm and has familiar uses concerning earth, displeasure, opposition, and escape.

R Gh B ends in bāʾ and concerns desire, inclination, and turning toward or away in different constructions.

The Qur’an states:

وَإِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ فَارْغَبْ

wa-ilā rabbika fa-irghab

Al-Sharḥ 94:8

“Turn your desire toward your Lord.”

This belongs to R Gh B, not R Gh M.

Murāghaman should not become “a desirable place” merely through resemblance to raghbah.

A migrant may desire a destination, but desirability is not part of murāgham’s derivational meaning.

R Gh M (raghm) and Gh R M (ghurm)

Ghurm and gharām belong to Gh R M.

This family concerns debt, loss, financial liability, and in some uses punishment or intense attachment.

The Qur’an states:

إِنَّ عَذَابَهَا كَانَ غَرَامًا

inna ʿadhābahā kāna gharāman

Al-Furqān 25:65

“Its punishment is indeed enduring and inescapable.”

Gharām belongs to Gh R M, not R Gh M.

Their similar letters should not cause confusion.

Murāgham in Al-Nisāʾ 4:100 means neither debt nor abiding punishment.

12. Translation recommendations for Forqan

This family requires distinctions among earth, abasement, opposition, and escape.

Raghām: earth; fine dust; sometimes sand mixed with soil.

Raghm / rughm / righm: abasement; displeasure; something occurring against someone’s wishes.

Raghima anfuhu: his nose was rubbed in dust; figuratively, he was humbled or frustrated.

Raghama / raghima: became humbled or unwillingly placed in a situation; in another use, could not obtain redress.

Arghamahu: humbled or compelled him against his wishes; rubbed his nose in dust, literally or figuratively.

Irghām: compulsion against one’s wishes; imposition; humiliation, depending on context.

Rāghama: opposed; separated from someone or one’s group; left them.

Murāghamah: opposition and angry disagreement; separation and departure. Reciprocal or equally matched action is not necessary in every use.

Murāghim: someone who opposes or separates from a group.

Murāgham: an escape route; place of migration; refuge; somewhere to go to escape pressure.

Margham / marghim: the nose in a recorded usage; margham in another construction concerns an opportunity to prevent or repel. These should not be equated with Qur’anic murāgham.

Marghamah: abasement and displeasure; in some uses, resentment and opposition.

Raghghama: humbled someone, acted against their wishes, or compelled them.

Targhīm: humbling, frustrating, or compelling against someone’s wishes.

Taraghghama: became angry or displeased; in some uses, expressed resentment.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:100

وَمَنْ يُهَاجِرْ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ يَجِدْ فِي الْأَرْضِ مُرَاغَمًا كَثِيرًا وَسَعَةً

wa-man yuhājir fī sabīli Allāhi yajid fī al-arḍi murāghaman kathīran wa-saʿatan

Al-Nisāʾ 4:100

Suggested translation:

“Whoever emigrates in The God’s way will find on earth many routes and places for release, and spaciousness.”

A concise equivalent for murāghaman itself is:

“A route or place for escaping pressure and going elsewhere.”

For a very concise translation, “many places for migration” is also defensible.

That wording makes release from pressure less explicit.

“Many escape routes” also conveys part of the sense, but may overemphasize flight and fear.

“Many refuges” is possible, provided it does not imply a fortress or guaranteed security.

A natural translation can therefore use “many routes and places for release,” explaining that murāgham concerns opposition, leaving a former environment, and finding another opportunity for life.

A distinction concerning wa-saʿatan

Since wa-saʿatan follows murāghaman kathīran, the final translation should preserve the two words’ distinction.

For example:

“He will find on earth many routes and places for release, and spaciousness.”

This more clearly distinguishes the words.

The prose may still benefit from stylistic smoothing.

In any event, murāghaman and saʿatan should not merge as though one were absent from the Arabic.

Murāghaman should not be translated as abundant wealth or certain victory over enemies.

Neither follows from the word alone.

13. Conclusion

One lexical analysis identifies two principal branches of R Gh M: earth, and a route or place to go or escape.

The first includes raghām, earth, and raghima anfuhu, a nose being rubbed in dust.

Social usage extends this image to abasement, frustration, and something happening against someone’s wishes.

Rāghama can likewise denote opposing and leaving a group that rejects one’s decision.

The same analysis treats murāgham, a route or place of departure and escape, as a separate branch.

Without sufficient historical evidence, every use should not be declared derived from the nose-and-dust image.

Early poetic witnesses also reveal different uses.

Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā uses ʿalā raghmihi for something against the creature’s wishes.

Al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī pairs murāgham with mahrab for escape or refuge.

The third witness pairs murāgham with a place of movement and activity.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as murāghaman.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:100 describes someone emigrating in The God’s way and finding many routes or places on earth for departure and release from pressure.

This accords with the lexical explanations madhhab, mahrab, mutaḥawwal, and mandūḥah.

Murāghaman should not be restricted to humiliating an enemy, taking revenge, or degrading another person.

Leaving pressure may frustrate those imposing it, but the verse directly concerns finding another route and place on earth.

Murāghaman and wa-saʿatan are related but not complete synonyms.

The former concerns a route or place of escape and migration.

The latter concerns spaciousness and expanded possibilities.

The preceding verses distinguish those able to leave from those lacking a means or route.

Abundant migration opportunities do not establish unrestricted access to a safe destination for every person in every circumstance.

The second part of Al-Nisāʾ 4:100 also raises death during migration.

Here the value of effort in The God’s way is not confined to successful arrival.

Murāghaman kathīran wa-saʿatan should therefore not become a promise of certain wealth, absolute security, or guaranteed material success for every migrant.

Final definition: Arabic R Gh M concerns earth, abasement, and something happening against someone’s wishes, as well as opposition, separation, and finding escape. Rāghama can describe opposing and leaving a group; murāgham denotes a route or place for escaping pressure and moving to another environment. Its sole Qur’anic occurrence, Al-Nisāʾ 4:100, concerns abundant opportunities for migration on earth. Resisting others’ impositions may characterize this situation, but humiliation, revenge, wealth, guaranteed security, and migration’s outcome do not follow from the word alone. They must be investigated through sentence structure and the verses’ context.
