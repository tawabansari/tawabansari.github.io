Qur’anic Root N Z F (نَزَفَ)

Qur’anic and lexical forms

يُنْزَفُونَ
(yunzafūna)
Passive imperfect; Al-Ṣāffāt 37:47

يُنْزِفُونَ
(yunzifūna)
Active Form IV imperfect; Al-Wāqiʿah 56:19

نَزَفَ
(nazafa)
Drawing out and exhausting; not Qur’anic in this form

أَنْزَفَ
(anzafa)
A supply running out or awareness being lost; lexical form

نَزِيف
(nazīf)
Intoxicated or severely weakened; not Qur’anic

مَنْزُوف
(manzūf)
Drained or deprived of awareness; not Qur’anic

Depletion and exhaustion: from draining a well and losing blood to the loss of reason through intoxicating drink

1. Introducing the root and its semantic core

Arabic N Z F denotes depletion, outflow, and exhaustion, especially when a source, reserve, or capacity diminishes or is lost through withdrawal or consumption.

One clear use concerns drawing water from a well until it becomes low or runs out. «نَزَفَ الْبِئْرَ» means drawing out the well’s water, while «أَنْزَفَتِ الْبِئْرُ» can mean that its water ran out.

The family also concerns blood. «نَزْفُ الدَّمِ» denotes blood flowing out and can involve blood loss and resulting weakness.

In another use, someone who loses awareness and discernment through wine is called «نَزِيف» or «مَنْزُوف», as though their reason or discernment has been drained away.

Arabic also uses «أَنْزَفَ الْقَوْمُ» of people whose drink or reserves have run out.

These uses share the loss or exhaustion of something. What is lost differs: water, blood, drink, physical strength, an argument, or human awareness.

Thus, “diminishing toward depletion or loss” is a defensible semantic core. Not every use necessarily means complete exhaustion; the family can also express severe depletion and its effects.

The Qur’anic occurrences concern the effect of drink on people. Both verses describe the drink of the Garden and emphasize the absence of a harmful effect on the drinkers’ awareness.

Qur’anic forms

N Z F occurs twice in two Qur’anic verses:

  • يُنْزَفُونَ: Al-Ṣāffāt 37:47; passive, understood here as their awareness not being taken away.
  • يُنْزِفُونَ: Al-Wāqiʿah 56:19; active Form IV, understood as not becoming intoxicated or losing awareness through the drink.

In Ḥafṣ’s transmission from ʿĀṣim, the forms have the same consonantal spelling but differ in the vowel on zāy and their verbal construction.

Arabic records other forms, including «نَزَفَ», «نَزْف», «أَنْزَفَ», «نَزِيف», «مَنْزُوف», «نَزُوف», «نُزْفَة», and «مِنْزَفَة». None of these nominal forms or verbal nouns occurs in the Qur’an.

The perfect «نَزَفَ» and verbal noun «نَزْف» do not occur in the Qur’an. However, the usual analysis treats «يُنْزَفُونَ» as a Form I passive imperfect. Absence of the perfect form should therefore not be confused with absence of a Form I verb.

The Qur’anic study thus centers on the two verbal forms and their relationship to the drink of the Garden.

2. Early Arabic usage and semantic range

Nazf: draining a well

Drawing water from a well is one principal Arabic use of nazf.

One lexical definition explains its core as follows:

أَصْلٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى نَفَادِ شَيْءٍ وَانْقِطَاعٍ

aṣlun yadullu ʿalā nafādi shayʾin wa-inqiṭāʿin

Meaning:

“A root denoting the exhaustion and cessation of something.”

In its use for well water, nazf can concern drawing it out gradually until the supply is exhausted.

Water leaves its source, so its quantity diminishes or runs out.

The act of drawing and its result must nevertheless be distinguished.

«نَزَفْتُ الْبِئْرَ» concerns drawing out the well’s water.

«أَنْزَفَتِ الْبِئْرُ», however, can mean that the well’s water has run out.

This shows how the verbal construction and its subject help determine the precise meaning.

A witness from Labīd’s poetry

Labīd ibn Rabīʿah describes rain-bearing clouds:

أَرَبَّتْ عَلَيْهِ كُلُّ وَطْفَاءَ جَوْنَةٍ
هَتُوفٍ مَتَى يُنْزَفْ لَهَا الْمَاءُ تَسْكُبِ

arabbat ʿalayhi kullu waṭfāʾa jawnatin / hatūfin matā yunzaf lahā al-māʾu taskubi

Translation:

“Dark, rain-laden clouds lingered above it, thundering clouds that pour whenever their water is drawn forth.”

«يُنْزَفْ» here concerns drawing out water.

The poet’s image connects rainfall with water emerging from a watery reserve.

This shows the root’s association with extracting water and depleting its supply.

It does not establish that nazf always means immediately and completely emptying a source. Gradual outflow is also involved in many uses.

Labīd lived in both the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. Without dating this particular verse, it should not be presented as certain pre-Qur’anic evidence.

Transmissions of this witness differ in vocalizing the zāy of «يُنْزَفْ». The quotation above has a fatḥah and a passive construction; variant wording should not be erased by silently changing the witness.

Blood loss: outflow and depletion

Arabic also records N Z F for the outflow of blood.

«نُزِفَ دَمُهُ» means that someone’s blood flowed out or was lost.

«نَزَفَهُ الدَّمُ» can describe someone weakened by blood loss.

Semantically this is close to well water: something present in a source or body leaves it.

Yet losing some blood must be distinguished from losing all of it.

Ordinary usage can describe substantial bleeding without implying that all the body’s blood has been lost.

Modern medical usage also employs nazf for bleeding. This relates to earlier usage, but a modern technical term should not replace the definition of every Qur’anic use of the root.

Neither Qur’anic occurrence concerns bleeding.

Tears: exhaustion through weeping

Lexical usage also records this root for tears:

نَزَفَ عَبْرَتَهُ

nazafa ʿabratahu

Meaning:

“He exhausted his tears.”

The issue is tears flowing gradually and coming to an end.

Rajaz verse attributed to al-ʿAjjāj says:

وَأَنْزَفَ الْعَبْرَةَ مَنْ لَاقَى الْعِبَرْ

wa-anzafa al-ʿabrata man lāqā al-ʿibar

A rendering of the sense:

“Whoever encountered sorrows and chastening experiences exhausted his tears.”

Nazf here does not mean sorrow or weeping. Sorrow may cause weeping, but the verb expresses exhausting the tears.

This shows the root’s use for the outflow and depletion of different liquids.

Al-ʿAjjāj was a poet of the Islamic period; this verse is not certain evidence of pre-Islamic usage.

Loss of reason and awareness

One particularly important use concerns losing reason and awareness through wine.

Arabic uses «نَزِيف» for an intoxicated person.

A lexical explanation says:

السَّكْرَانُ نَزِيفٌ إِذَا نُزِفَ عَقْلُهُ

al-sakrānu nazīfun idhā nuzifa ʿaqluhu

Meaning:

“An intoxicated person is called nazīf when his reason has been drained away.”

Reason is not a liquid physically leaving the body here. “Draining reason” is an image for losing awareness and discernment.

This matters for Qur’anic study because both occurrences concern the drink of the Garden.

A witness attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays says:

وَإِذْ هِيَ تَمْشِي كَمَشْيِ النَّزِيفِ
يَصْرَعُهُ بِالْكَثِيبِ الْبُهُرْ

wa-idh hiya tamshī ka-mashyi al-nazīfi / yaṣraʿuhu bi-al-kathībi al-buhur

Translation:

“As she walks, she walks like an intoxicated, weakened person whom breathlessness brings down on the sand dune.”

Lexical explanations take «النَّزِيف» here as an intoxicated person or someone who has lost strength and balance.

The poet compares someone’s gait to the movement of a person lacking adequate strength or normal balance.

This shows that nazīf in early poetry extended beyond water or blood leaving a source to weakness or loss of control.

Yet the verse alone does not assign every feature of intoxication to the root. The scene concerns movement and physical incapacity; identifying its cause depends on lexical evidence and poetic context.

Inzāf: running out of drink

Arabic uses «أَنْزَفَ الْقَوْمُ» of people whose drink has run out.

In this use, the drink supply is exhausted, rather than the drinkers’ reason or awareness.

A verse by al-Ubayrid al-Riyāḥī says:

لَعَمْرِي لَئِنْ أَنْزَفْتُمُ أَوْ صَحَوْتُمُ
لَبِئْسَ النَّدَامَى كُنْتُمُ آلَ أَبْجَرَا

la-ʿamrī la-in anzaftumu aw ṣaḥawtumu / la-biʾsa al-nadāmā kuntumu āla abjarā

Translation:

“By my life, whether drunk or sober, you, the family of Abjar, have been wretched drinking companions.”

The contrast between «أَنْزَفْتُمُ» and «صَحَوْتُمُ» clearly supports becoming intoxicated as opposed to being sober here. Arabic also records “running out of drink” for anzafa, but that should not be made this witness’s principal sense without regard to the contrast.

This witness has also been discussed in lexical explanations of the reading «يُنْزِفُونَ».

Al-Ubayrid al-Riyāḥī belonged to the Islamic period and the early Umayyad era. His verse is not independent evidence of pre-Qur’anic usage.

Anzafa has two related uses concerning drink: running out of drink and losing awareness through drinking.

Each sentence must therefore be examined to identify whether the drink supply or the drinker’s discernment is exhausted.

Other uses of the root

Nazīf can also describe a very thirsty person weakened and parched by thirst.

Some usages also apply it to someone suffering from fever.

In discussion and dispute, «نَزَفَ الرَّجُلُ فِي الْخُصُومَةِ» means that someone’s argument has run out.

This clearly shows the root’s application to something nonphysical.

The person’s reasoning and grounds for argument are exhausted; no liquid has left their body.

«نُزْفَة» denotes a small amount of water or drink, while «مِنْزَفَة» names an implement for drawing water from a well.

These uses illuminate the root’s semantic field but are not Qur’anic forms.

3. The first Qur’anic passage: Al-Ṣāffāt 37:47

لَا فِيهَا غَوْلٌ وَلَا هُمْ عَنْهَا يُنْزَفُونَ

lā fīhā ghawlun wa-lā hum ʿanhā yunzafūna

Translation:

“There is no hidden harm or injury in it, nor do they lose awareness through it.”

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:47

This is the first Qur’anic occurrence of N Z F in the order of the muṣḥaf.

The two preceding verses are important to understanding it.

A. The drink of the Garden in the preceding verses

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:45 says:

يُطَافُ عَلَيْهِمْ بِكَأْسٍ مِنْ مَعِينٍ

yuṭāfu ʿalayhim bi-kaʾsin min maʿīnin

Translation:

“A cup from a flowing source is passed around among them.”

The next verse says:

بَيْضَاءَ لَذَّةٍ لِلشَّارِبِينَ

bayḍāʾa ladhdhatin li-al-shāribīna

Translation:

“White and bright, delightful to those who drink.”

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:46

Verse 47 adds another important feature: this drink causes neither harm nor the loss of awareness.

«يُنْزَفُونَ» therefore concerns drinking the liquid described just before it.

The verb cannot be detached from that setting and translated as bleeding, a well running dry, or someone running out of arguments.

B. The relationship between ghawl and nazf

The verse says:

لَا فِيهَا غَوْلٌ

lā fīhā ghawlun

Then:

وَلَا هُمْ عَنْهَا يُنْزَفُونَ

wa-lā hum ʿanhā yunzafūna

«غَوْل» belongs to GH W L and concerns harm or a damaging effect inflicted on a person. In some uses, that harm may be hidden or sudden.

In the context of drink, ghawl can concern its harmful effects, including bodily harm or impaired reason.

«يُنْزَفُونَ», however, concerns something being lost by the drinkers; reason and awareness are its most fitting semantic referent here.

The expressions are close but should not be translated so that their distinction disappears.

Ghawl can concern harm and the drink’s damaging effects.

Nazf here emphasizes losing awareness or discernment.

The meanings can overlap, but their combination highlights the absence of both harm and impaired awareness.

C. Does «يُنْزَفُونَ» mean that they do not become intoxicated?

Yes. Arabic has an established use of nazf for losing reason through wine.

The word is read passively in this verse.

In the familiar analysis, the drinkers’ reason is not taken away by this drink.

“They do not become intoxicated” is therefore a natural, defensible rendering.

This is a contextual equivalent, however. “Their awareness is not lost” makes the image of the root clearer and explains the use of N Z F.

Fluent translation and lexical explanation can therefore be distinguished:

“They do not become intoxicated” gives the clear result of the statement.

“Their awareness is not lost” makes its connection to nazf more apparent.

D. The role of «عَنْهَا»

The pronoun hā in «وَلَا هُمْ عَنْهَا يُنْزَفُونَ» refers to the previously mentioned drink.

Here, ʿan connects an effect with its source: they do not lose awareness through that drink.

A mechanically literal “they are not drained of it” is therefore unclear, since it could suggest that they do not lose the drink itself.

In the awareness reading, the statement means that the drink does not take away their reason.

Yet ʿan should not be made an absolute rule meaning cause in every Arabic usage.

Its function follows from this particular construction, the verb, and the pronoun’s referent.

4. The second Qur’anic passage: Al-Wāqiʿah 56:19

لَا يُصَدَّعُونَ عَنْهَا وَلَا يُنْزِفُونَ

lā yuṣaddaʿūna ʿanhā wa-lā yunzifūna

Translation:

“They suffer no headache from it, nor do they lose awareness.”

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:19

The same root appears in a different form here.

To understand it, consider the two preceding verses:

يَطُوفُ عَلَيْهِمْ وِلْدَانٌ مُخَلَّدُونَ

yaṭūfu ʿalayhim wildānun mukhalladūna

“Everlasting youths circulate among them.”

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:17

بِأَكْوَابٍ وَأَبَارِيقَ وَكَأْسٍ مِنْ مَعِينٍ

bi-akwābin wa-abārīqa wa-kaʾsin min maʿīnin

“With cups, pitchers, and a cup from a flowing source.”

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:18

Verse 19 then denies two harmful effects of the drink.

A. «لَا يُصَدَّعُونَ عَنْهَا»

«يُصَدَّعُونَ» belongs to Ṣ D ʿ.

In this context it concerns suffering a headache.

Arabic ṣudāʿ means headache, while the family also concerns splitting and cleaving apart.

The verse does not mean that the drinkers’ heads literally split. It concerns the headache that may follow drinking certain beverages.

“They suffer no headache” is thus the natural equivalent.

B. «وَلَا يُنْزِفُونَ»

In Ḥafṣ’s reading, the zāy in «يُنْزِفُونَ» has a kasrah.

This is an active Form IV imperfect.

Arabic «أَنْزَفَ الرَّجُلُ» can mean becoming intoxicated and losing reason through wine.

“They do not lose awareness” or “they do not become intoxicated” therefore fits this context.

The same construction also has another recorded meaning: a person or group running out of drink.

This distinction matters when studying the readings.

C. Does the verse also concern drink never running out?

Arabic «أَنْزَفَ الْقَوْمُ» can mean that the people’s drink has run out.

Some lexical and exegetical explanations therefore take «يُنْزِفُونَ» here as the people of the Garden not running out of drink.

This reading has lexical support.

But lexical possibility should be distinguished from the preferred contextual sense.

Verse 19 places two statements together:

First, the drinkers do not suffer headaches.

Second, «يُنْزِفُونَ» is denied of them.

Since the first clause denies an unpleasant effect of drinking, understanding the second as not losing reason and awareness fits the construction clearly.

Conversely, «مِنْ مَعِينٍ» in the preceding verse describes drink from a flowing source and can provide a setting for its abundance and availability.

The inexhaustible-drink reading is thus not groundless. Yet absence of intoxication has stronger contextual support alongside the denial of headaches and the parallel Al-Ṣāffāt passage.

The senses should not be artificially combined as “they neither become intoxicated nor run out of drink,” as if one word necessarily established both equally within that construction.

It is better to select one reading for the base translation and preserve the other in the lexical explanation.

D. The distinction between Al-Ṣāffāt and Al-Wāqiʿah

The two verses concern closely related subjects, but their accompanying expressions differ.

Al-Ṣāffāt says:

لَا فِيهَا غَوْلٌ

lā fīhā ghawlun

Al-Wāqiʿah says:

لَا يُصَدَّعُونَ عَنْهَا

lā yuṣaddaʿūna ʿanhā

Both deny nazf of the drinkers.

Al-Ṣāffāt states the absence of the drink’s harmful effects.

Al-Wāqiʿah denies one specific unpleasant effect: headache.

Both also deny the loss of awareness.

The connection shows that the principal subject is enjoying a delightful drink without the harmful effects of intoxicating beverages familiar in earthly life.

This description does not establish that these verses explain the drink’s chemical composition, production, or effects on the nervous system.

Those details are not stated in the text.

5. The variant readings «يُنْزَفُونَ» and «يُنْزِفُونَ»

These two forms have an important place in the Qur’anic reading tradition.

In Ḥafṣ’s transmission from ʿĀṣim, the two verses differ:

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:47 has «يُنْزَفُونَ» with fatḥah on zāy.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:19 has «يُنْزِفُونَ» with kasrah on zāy.

Not all recognized readings make this distinction between the verses.

Ḥamzah, al-Kisāʾī, and Khalaf read the kasrah form in both.

Nāfiʿ, Ibn Kathīr, Abū ʿAmr, Ibn ʿĀmir, Abū Jaʿfar, and Yaʿqūb read the fatḥah form in both.

ʿĀṣim, as transmitted by Ḥafṣ and Shuʿbah, retains fatḥah in Al-Ṣāffāt and kasrah in Al-Wāqiʿah.

«يُنْزَفُونَ»: the passive form

With fatḥah on zāy, the verb is passive.

The usual analysis treats «يُنْزَفُونَ» as a Form I passive imperfect related to «نُزِفَ الرَّجُلُ». The Form IV passive has the same outward form, but that identity alone does not establish identical analysis and meaning in every use.

The familiar interpretation is:

“Their awareness is not taken away.”

Reason and awareness are what the statement denies being lost.

The word “reason” itself is not present in the Qur’anic phrase. Its semantic referent follows from established Arabic usage and the context of drinking.

«يُنْزِفُونَ»: the active Form IV

With kasrah on zāy, this is the active imperfect of anzafa.

The verb has two established Arabic uses:

Becoming intoxicated and losing awareness.

Running out of drink or a reserve in one’s possession.

The form therefore allows two lexical interpretations.

Two recorded meanings do not establish that both are simultaneously intended to the same degree in a particular verse.

In both surahs, the awareness reading clearly fits the denial of the drink’s harmful effects.

Do the variant readings introduce two different drinks?

No.

Both verses concern the drink of the Garden. The different vowel on zāy does not by itself establish two drinks with different natures.

Nor does it follow that Al-Ṣāffāt denies only impaired reason while Al-Wāqiʿah denies only the exhaustion of drink: other readings use the same form in both places.

Variant readings can broaden the possibilities for study, but should not become a definite system that the text and linguistic evidence do not require.

“They do not become intoxicated” or “they do not lose awareness” is therefore defensible in both verses, while the morphological difference and possibility of inexhaustible drink remain part of the root explanation.

6. Comparing related roots

N Z F (نَزَفَ) and N Z Ḥ (نَزَحَ): depletion and drawing out

N Z Ḥ is among the closest roots to N Z F.

Arabic nazḥ concerns drawing water from a well and, in another use, moving away or becoming distant.

For well water, «نَزَفَ الْبِئْرَ» and «نَزَحَ الْبِئْرَ» can be very close in meaning.

N Z F also emphasizes a reserve’s depletion and exhaustion in its established uses.

This is a tendency rather than an absolute boundary fixed across every sentence and dialect.

The Qur’an does not use N Z F for well water. This comparison illuminates the lexical field rather than directly identifying the verses’ subject.

N Z F (نَزَفَ) and N F D (نَفِدَ): loss and exhaustion

N F D concerns something running out or coming to an end.

The Qur’an says:

مَا نَفِدَتْ كَلِمَاتُ اللَّهِ

mā nafidat kalimātu allāhi

“The words of The God would not run out.”

Luqmān 31:27, part of the verse

N Z F can likewise concern water, drink, or a person’s capacity being exhausted.

N F D focuses on exhaustion itself, while many physical uses of N Z F involve something flowing out or being consumed.

This distinction is not absolute: anzafa can mean that drink runs out without stating an act of withdrawal.

In the “their drink never runs out” reading of Al-Wāqiʿah 56:19, N Z F approaches N F D.

In the awareness reading, however, reason is what is not lost, rather than the supply of drink.

N Z F (نَزَفَ) and S K R (سَكِرَ): impaired awareness and intoxication

Arabic S K R concerns intoxication and blocked or impaired perception.

The Qur’an uses «سُكَارَىٰ» for intoxicated people.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:43 says:

لَا تَقْرَبُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَأَنْتُمْ سُكَارَىٰ حَتَّىٰ تَعْلَمُوا مَا تَقُولُونَ

lā taqrabū al-ṣalāta wa-antum sukārā ḥattā taʿlamū mā taqūlūna

Translation:

“Do not approach ṣalāh while intoxicated, until you know what you are saying.”

Here, intoxication is connected with lacking proper awareness of one’s own speech.

This is close to nazf used for losing reason through drink.

But sakr and nazf are not synonymous throughout Arabic.

N Z F has a wider field involving water, blood, tears, drink, and arguments.

Sakr in the drinking context directly expresses intoxication and impaired awareness.

“They do not become intoxicated” therefore suits the contextual use in Al-Ṣāffāt and Al-Wāqiʿah, without restricting the root’s general definition to intoxication.

N Z F (نَزَفَ) and GH W L (غَالَ): loss of capacity and harmful effects

The two roots occur together in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:47.

Ghawl concerns harm or a damaging effect inflicted on a person.

Nazf in this context concerns the drinker’s loss of awareness.

Loss of awareness can be one instance of a drink’s harm, so the expressions overlap semantically.

That overlap does not make ghawl and nazf identical in every use.

Ghawl also concerns other harms, while nazf can concern the exhaustion of water, blood, or a reserve.

N Z F (نَزَفَ) and Ṣ D ʿ (صَدَعَ): loss of awareness and headache

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:19 places two verbs together:

لَا يُصَدَّعُونَ

lā yuṣaddaʿūna

And:

وَلَا يُنْزِفُونَ

wa-lā yunzifūna

The first concerns headache in this context; the second concerns impaired awareness.

These effects may accompany one another in experiences of drinking certain beverages, but they are not identical.

Someone may have a headache without being intoxicated, and impaired awareness need not be accompanied by a headache.

The verse therefore denies two different characteristics.

Translation should preserve this distinction.

Study of Ṣ D ʿ

N Z F (نَزَفَ) and KH M R (خَمَرَ): the drink and its effect

In earthly usage, khamr names an intoxicating drink; other uses of its family also concern covering. The same name occurs in descriptions of the Garden, but the verses under discussion deny intoxication and loss of awareness of its drink.

Muḥammad 47:15 says:

وَأَنْهَارٌ مِنْ خَمْرٍ لَذَّةٍ لِلشَّارِبِينَ

wa-anhārun min khamrin ladhdhatin li-al-shāribīna

Translation:

“And rivers of wine delightful to those who drink.”

However, the word khamr does not occur directly in the two verses under study in Al-Ṣāffāt and Al-Wāqiʿah; their descriptions speak of «كَأْسٍ مِنْ مَعِينٍ».

Other verses about the drink of the Garden make the connection with khamr understandable.

The drink’s name must nevertheless be distinguished from its effect.

Khamr concerns the drink; nazf concerns something being lost, here awareness.

The roots should not be merged.

Nor should descriptions of the Garden’s drink yield new rulings about earthly intoxicants without an independent study of the relevant verses.

These verses describe enjoyment in the Hereafter and its difference from familiar harmful drinks.

Study of KH M R

7. Interpretive questions and proposed Forqan renderings

Does N Z F mean only intoxication in the Qur’an?

Intoxication or loss of awareness clearly fits both Qur’anic contexts.

That should not restrict the root’s general core to “becoming intoxicated.”

Arabic also uses nazf for water, blood, tears, arguments, and reserves.

The family’s general meaning should therefore be distinguished from its particular Qur’anic use.

General meaning: something diminishing, flowing out, or being exhausted.

Qur’anic use: losing reason or awareness through drink, an effect denied in both verses.

Must the Garden’s drink have no effect whatsoever on perception?

Al-Ṣāffāt and Al-Wāqiʿah speak of the absence of ghawl, headaches, and impaired awareness.

These descriptions deny familiar harmful effects of intoxicating drinks of the Garden’s drink.

They do not establish that drinking in the Garden produces no feeling, pleasure, or experience.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:46 itself describes the drink as «لَذَّةٍ لِلشَّارِبِينَ».

Pleasure and the preservation of awareness thus occur together.

The distinction matters: the drink is delightful, but loss of reason and harmful effects are denied of it.

The precise character of that pleasure and how the drink acts in the Hereafter are not explained.

Should the “drink never running out” meaning be removed?

No.

Arabic has this established sense of anzafa, which also appears in explanations of the kasrah reading.

But its existence should not make it the verse’s only possible meaning regardless of context.

Both surahs’ denial of harm and impaired awareness strongly supports the intoxication reading.

The inexhaustible-drink interpretation should therefore remain a valid lexical possibility, while the base translation selects the preferred contextual sense.

Proposed renderings

Suitable equivalents for general Arabic uses include:

«نَزَفَ مَاءَ الْبِئْرِ»: he drew out and exhausted the well’s water.

«أَنْزَفَتِ الْبِئْرُ»: the well’s water ran out.

«نَزَفَ دَمُهُ»: his blood flowed out or he suffered blood loss.

«نَزَفَ عَبْرَتَهُ»: he exhausted his tears.

«نَزِيف»: intoxicated and deprived of awareness in the drinking context; in other uses, extremely weak or thirsty.

«أَنْزَفَ الْقَوْمُ»: the people’s drink or reserves ran out.

«نَزَفَ فِي الْخُصُومَةِ»: his grounds for argument ran out in a dispute.

For the two Qur’anic forms:

VerseQur’anic formProposed rendering
Al-Ṣāffāt 37:47وَلَا هُمْ عَنْهَا يُنْزَفُونَNor do they lose awareness through it
Al-Wāqiʿah 56:19وَلَا يُنْزِفُونَNor do they lose awareness

“They do not become intoxicated” is also concise and natural in both verses.

“They do not lose awareness” conveys the root’s semantic image more clearly and better suits Forqan’s research purpose.

In Al-Ṣāffāt, ghawl and nazf should not both become an identical expression in translation.

Al-Wāqiʿah should likewise preserve the distinction between headache and loss of awareness.

The inexhaustible-drink meaning belongs in the explanation of readings and Arabic usage; it need not be added as a second meaning within the translated sentence itself.

8. Conclusion

Arabic N Z F denotes something diminishing, flowing out, and being exhausted.

Physical uses include drawing out well water, losing blood, and exhausting tears.

Other uses concern running out of drink or arguments, and losing awareness through wine.

Poetic witnesses, including verse attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays, show nazīf describing someone with impaired awareness or balance. Labīd’s verse also illustrates drawing out water.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an, both times concerning the drink of the Garden.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:47, «يُنْزَفُونَ» accompanies the denial of ghawl and concerns the drinkers not losing awareness.

In Al-Wāqiʿah 56:19, «يُنْزِفُونَ» accompanies the denial of headache; the same awareness reading fits the context strongly.

Arabic anzafa can also mean running out of drink, so this interpretation should remain part of lexical study and discussion of the readings.

A possible meaning, however, should not automatically enter the translation as certain and equal to the preferred interpretation.

The fatḥah–kasrah distinction matters morphologically, but does not by itself establish two different drinks or completely separate characteristics in the two surahs.

Together, the verses describe the Garden’s drink as delightful while denying familiar harmful effects of intoxicating drinks, including harm, headaches, and impaired awareness.

This does not provide a medical or chemical explanation of the Garden’s drink.

Proposed final definition for Forqan

N Z F denotes something diminishing, flowing out, and being exhausted, particularly a source, reserve, or capacity depleted or lost through withdrawal, consumption, or another influence.

Arabic applies this meaning to water, blood, tears, drink, arguments, and human awareness.

The Qur’anic «يُنْزَفُونَ» and «يُنْزِفُونَ» concern the drink of the Garden; their preferred meaning is not losing reason or awareness through drinking.

With kasrah on zāy, inexhaustible drink is also lexically defensible. Yet the two contexts make the denial of intoxication and impaired awareness more suitable for the base translation.

Thus, “something being depleted or lost” is the root’s general semantic core, and “loss of awareness” its specific Qur’anic application. Both verses deny that effect of the drinkers.
