Binding, fettering, and giving: two semantic branches in early Arabic, from captives’ chains to gifts and generosity

The Arabic root Ṣ F D has two established branches: binding and securing a person or thing with bonds and chains, and giving something to another person.

The first includes ṣafada, to bind or fetter; ṣafd and ṣifād, a bond or fetter; aṣfād, bonds and chains; and maṣfūd, bound or fettered.

In the second branch, aṣfada commonly means giving, while iṣfād denotes bestowing a gift.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an, in Ibrāhīm 14:49 and Ṣād 38:38. Both use the plural al-aṣfād, clearly meaning bonds and chains.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: two nominal occurrences of aṣfād

The essential distinction is that binding and giving are both recorded, but neither should be declared the original meaning and the other its definite consequence without sufficient evidence. Ibn Fāris identifies two independent foundations. Some lexicographers relate giving to binding through the connection and obligation a gift may create, but this is a semantic explanation, not proof of historical origin.

1. The root’s semantic foundations in Arabic

Two independent foundations in Maqāyīs al-Lughah

Ibn Fāris writes:

الصَّادُ وَالْفَاءُ وَالدَّالُ أَصْلَانِ صَحِيحَانِ: أَحَدُهُمَا عَطَاءٌ، وَالْآخَرُ شَدٌّ بِشَيْءٍ

al-ṣādu wa-al-fāʾu wa-al-dālu aṣlāni ṣaḥīḥāni: aḥaduhumā ʿaṭāʾun, wa-al-ākharu shaddun bi-shayʾin

The root thus has two valid semantic foundations: giving, and fastening something with a means of binding.

This matters methodologically. Unlike families whose branches can demonstrably be traced to one central meaning, Ibn Fāris records two foundations here.

Each branch should therefore first be understood through its actual uses before assessing any possible connection.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: two independent principles, gifts and binding

Ṣafada: binding and fettering

Lisān al-ʿArab records:

صَفَدَهُ يَصْفِدُهُ صَفْدًا وَصُفُودًا ... أَوْثَقَهُ وَشَدَّهُ وَقَيَّدَهُ

ṣafadahu yaṣfiduhu ṣafdan wa-ṣufūdan ... awthaqahu wa-shaddahu wa-qayyadahu

It means securing, binding, and fettering someone.

A person is secured by a bond so that freedom of movement is restricted.

The bond may be a chain, a shackle, a leather strap, or another means of fastening.

Ṣafadtuhu bi-al-ḥadīdi means fettering someone with iron.

Iron, however, is not a necessary component of the verb itself. The sentence and situation establish the means.

Ṣafada basically expresses binding and fettering, not a metal type, chain shape, or bodily location.

Ṣafd, ṣafad, and ṣifād

Lexical sources record ṣafd and ṣafad, with different vocalizations, for bonds and fetters.

Ṣifād likewise denotes a means of binding someone.

In Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Jawharī explains ṣifād as what secures a captive, whether a leather strap, fetter, or shackle.

Ṣifād therefore does not name just one tool of a fixed shape; it can refer to different kinds of restraints.

Nor should every ordinary tie be called ṣifād without regard to usage. Established witnesses particularly concern firmly binding and restraining people.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab: binding, ṣifād, and kinds of restraint

Aṣfād: plural bonds and fetters

Aṣfād is plural, identified in lexical sources as the plural of ṣafad.

The Qur’an uses it with the definite article: al-aṣfād.

Both verses describe individuals restrained by these bonds.

Bonds, fetters, or chains are therefore suitable Persian equivalents.

Chains creates a clear image, but bonds and fetters has a broader lexical scope because the word itself does not necessarily specify the material.

2. The second branch: gifts and giving

Aṣfadahu: he gave him something

Lisān al-ʿArab records:

أَصْفَدْتُهُ إِصْفَادًا أَيْ أَعْطَيْتُهُ مَالًا

aṣfadtuhu iṣfādan ay aʿṭaytuhu mālan

It means giving someone wealth.

Other uses may concern giving a servant, goods, or another gift.

Iṣfād is the verb’s verbal noun in the sense of giving.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ also records a distinction between Form I ṣafadahu, commonly binding, and Form IV aṣfadahu, giving.

The distinction is not absolute across all dialects.

The Cairo Arabic Language Academy records both binding and giving for aṣfada; some early sources also use ṣafada for giving.

Morphology helps identify meaning but does not replace examining context.

Lisān al-ʿArab and the Cairo Academy dictionary: both uses of aṣfada

Is giving a kind of bond?

In Al-Mufradāt, Al-Rāghib al-Isfahānī explains ṣafad as a gift through the image of feeling captive to another’s kindness.

Arabic expressions such as being captive to someone’s generosity can describe indebtedness and obligation toward the giver.

Al-Zamakhsharī offers a concise expression in Asās al-Balāghah:

الْعَطَاءُ قَيْدٌ

al-ʿaṭāʾu qaydun

A gift is a bond.

Figuratively, giving may create a relationship accompanied by gratitude, obligation, or loyalty.

Three matters must nevertheless be distinguished.

First, literally restraining someone with a fetter.

Second, giving wealth or a gift.

Third, the literary image of a connection that giving may create.

The image does not prove that giving historically derived from chains.

Nor does it mean every gift aims to make its recipient dependent or captive. Giving may involve no expectation of reciprocation or obligation.

Al-Mufradāt and Asās al-Balāghah: giving pictured as a bond

Istiṣfād: requesting a gift

Arabic also uses istaṣfada for requesting a gift.

It is Form X.

In its established use, someone asks another to give something.

Istiṣfād in this branch therefore means requesting generosity, not asking to be chained.

The form does not occur in the Qur’an.

3. Early Arabic poetic witnesses

Bonds and chains in ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm’s Muʿallaqah

The pre-Islamic poet ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm says in his Muʿallaqah:

فَآبُوا بِالنِّهَابِ وَبِالسَّبَايَا
وَأُبْنَا بِالْمُلُوكِ مُصَفَّدِينَا

fa-ābū bi-al-nihābi wa-bi-al-sabāyā / wa-ubnā bi-al-mulūki muṣaffadīnā

Translation: “They returned with plunder and captives; we returned with kings in fetters.”

Muṣaffadīnā denotes people bound and restrained.

The poet describes warfare and captivity, presenting the fettered, defeated kings as a sign of victory.

Al-Zawzanī’s commentary explains taṣfīd through taqyīd, fettering.

The witness shows the family’s use for binding captives in pre-Islamic poetry.

Muṣaffad itself, however, means neither king nor warrior; those identities follow from other words in the line.

Al-Zawzanī’s commentary: ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm’s witness and taṣfīd

Ṣifād in poetry about captivity

Ibn Fāris and Ibn Manẓūr record another line:

هَلَّا كَرَرْتَ عَلَى ابْنِ أُمِّكَ مَعْبَدٍ
وَالْعَامِرِيُّ يَقُودُهُ بِصِفَادِ

hallā kararta ʿalā ibni ummika maʿbadin / wa-al-ʿāmiriyyu yaqūduhu bi-ṣifādi

Translation: “Why did you not turn back to help your brother Maʿbad, while the ʿĀmirī led him with a fetter?”

Bi-ṣifādi denotes the means by which the captive is bound and led.

The meaning of bond or fetter is therefore clear.

Literary sources differ in attribution, including attribution to ʿAwf ibn al-Khariʿ.

It can therefore illuminate early ṣifād, but its attribution should not be presented as certain without acknowledging variation.

Ansāb al-Ashrāf: Maʿbad’s captivity witness attributed to ʿAwf ibn al-Khariʿ

Giving in Al-Aʿshā’s poetry

The well-known pre-Islamic poet Al-Aʿshā says:

تَضَيَّفْتُهُ يَوْمًا فَقَرَّبَ مَقْعَدِي
وَأَصْفَدَنِي عَلَى الزَّمَانَةِ قَائِدَا

taḍayyaftuhu yawman fa-qarraba maqʿadī / wa-aṣfadanī ʿalā al-zamānati qāʾidā

Translation: “One day I visited him as a guest; he brought my seat close and, in my infirmity, gave me a guide.”

Aṣfadanī here means giving.

The poet recalls someone honoring him and providing a person to guide or assist him.

Chaining does not fit the context: qāʾidan, a guide, is the gift received.

This is a particularly significant pre-Islamic use of Form IV for giving.

The gift branch is therefore not merely an invention of later dictionaries.

Al-Nihāyah: Al-Aʿshā’s wording and the gift of a guide

Giving in Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī’s poetry

A line attributed to Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī says:

هَذَا الثَّنَاءُ فَإِنْ تَسْمَعْ لِقَائِلِهِ
فَمَا عَرَّضْتُ أَبَيْتَ اللَّعْنَ بِالصَّفَدِ

hādhā al-thanāʾu fa-in tasmaʿ li-qāʾilihi / fa-mā ʿarraḍtu abayta al-laʿna bi-al-ṣafadi

Translation: “This is my praise; if you heed its speaker, I have not offered it—may you be spared a curse—to seek a gift.”

Sources also record variants such as fa-mā ʿaraḍtu and fa-lam uʿarriḍ.

Al-ṣafad here means a gift or reward.

The poet emphasizes that his praise was not offered to obtain a gift.

This also supports giving as a pre-Islamic Arabic meaning.

Al-Ṭabarī: Al-Nābighah’s witness and the meaning of a gift

What the poetic witnesses establish

Two points emerge.

First, Ṣ F D was familiar for binding and restraint in warfare and captivity.

Second, the same family expressed gifts and giving in praise and social relationships.

Both branches thus have early evidence.

The witnesses do not definitively establish which meaning came first historically.

Retaining the recorded branches is more accurate than inventing an unsupported derivational story.

4. The first Qur’anic use: Ibrāhīm 14:49

وَتَرَى الْمُجْرِمِينَ يَوْمَئِذٍ مُقَرَّنِينَ فِي الْأَصْفَادِ

wa-tarā al-mujrimīna yawmaʾidhin muqarranīna fī al-aṣfādi

Translation: On that day you will see the criminals bound together in bonds and chains.

Al-aṣfād is a definite broken plural, genitive after fī.

The verse concerns a condition on the Day of Resurrection.

Ibrāhīm 14:48 describes the earth and heavens changing and everyone appearing before God.

Verse 49 then pictures the criminals restrained in bonds and fetters.

Ibrāhīm 14:50 describes their garments and fire covering their faces.

Al-aṣfād therefore appears in a context of punishment and restriction.

Muqarranīna versus al-aṣfād

The words belong to different roots.

Muqarranīna comes from Q R N and concerns being placed together or bound to one another.

Al-aṣfād comes from Ṣ F D and names the bonds and restraints used.

The combination therefore expresses more than chains being present: individuals are restrained in them.

Translating only “in chains” diminishes muqarranīna.

Translating only “bound together” removes the means of binding expressed by al-aṣfād.

“Bound together in bonds and chains” conveys both.

Are their limbs bound together, or are people bound to one another?

Exegetical sources offer two main explanations of muqarranīna.

One understands a person’s hands and feet bound with chains or shackles.

The other understands criminals joined and bound to one another.

Al-Ṭabarī emphasizes restrained limbs, while Ibn Kathīr and others attend to similar groups gathered and individuals bound together.

Ibn Kathīr: groups bound together

Lexically, muqarranīna concerns joining and bringing together; al-aṣfād specifies the means of binding.

The wording does not definitively establish detailed arrangements, such as the number of chains or how many people each connects.

The main meaning of binding and restraint should therefore remain, with exegetical details presented as possible explanations.

Al-Ṭabarī: restrained limbs and the meaning of aṣfād

Must al-aṣfād mean iron chains?

Binding with iron is an established lexical use.

Ṣifād and ṣafad, however, may denote different means of restraint.

Ibrāhīm 14:49 does not specify their material.

Iron chains may be a familiar image, but iron should not be added to the direct translation without evidence.

Must these bonds be understood as purely symbolic?

The verse pictures criminals in bonds and chains in a resurrection scene.

It clearly conveys restriction, restraint, and lack of freedom of movement.

Ṣ F D itself does not determine whether those bonds’ precise nature in the Hereafter matches familiar earthly chains.

Conversely, uncertainty about the Hereafter does not authorize removing bonds and substituting a wholly abstract expression such as being trapped by one’s own thoughts.

Such a symbolic reading needs independent Qur’anic evidence before it can be accepted.

In lexical research, the word’s clear meaning remains bonds and restraints; the manner in which that meaning is realized is a separate question.

5. The second Qur’anic use: Ṣād 38:38

وَآخَرِينَ مُقَرَّنِينَ فِي الْأَصْفَادِ

wa-ākharīna muqarranīna fī al-aṣfādi

Translation: And others who were bound together in bonds and chains.

The verse belongs to Solomon’s story.

Ṣād 38:35 contains Solomon’s prayer for forgiveness and a distinctive dominion.

Ṣād 38:36 describes the wind subjected to him.

Ṣād 38:37 mentions devils employed in building and diving.

Verse 38 then describes others restrained in bonds and chains.

The nearest referent of ākharīna is therefore the previously discussed group: other devils subjected within Solomon’s dominion.

Does ākharīna refer to human beings?

Ākharīna alone in verse 38 does not identify the group.

The preceding verse, however, discusses devils.

The natural contextual reading therefore makes others another group of those devils.

Al-Ṭabarī likewise accepts this reading and transmits reports about a group of devils restrained in chains.

This explanation must not, however, become a claim that al-aṣfād itself means devils.

The word denotes only bonds and restraints; the identity of those restrained emerges from the context.

Why were some of them bound?

Some exegetical reports describe these devils as disobedient or rebellious and explain that they were restrained for that reason.

This explanation fits the setting of rule and subjection in the narrative.

Ṣād 38:38 itself, however, does not state the particular reason for binding them.

The verse does not say what each had done or why each needed to be restrained.

Disobedience or rebellion may therefore be examined as an explanation transmitted in exegetical sources, but must not become part of the meaning of al-aṣfād or an explicit statement of the verse itself.

Al-Ṭabarī: another group of devils in bonds

How this scene differs from Ibrāhīm 14:49

Ibrāhīm 14:49 concerns the guilty in a scene of resurrection.

Ṣād 38:38, however, concerns a restrained group within the account of Solomon’s rule.

Both verses contain the same expression, muqarranīna fī al-aṣfād.

They therefore share the image of restraint and binding.

The identities of those involved and the circumstances of the event nevertheless differ.

This difference shows that the lexical meaning of al-aṣfād is not restricted to resurrection, Hell, or one kind of offender.

Bonds may describe captivity in this world or a condition in the hereafter.

Its relation to ʿaṭāʾunā in the following verse

Ṣād 38:39 speaks of the divine gift bestowed on Solomon.

Since the root Ṣ F D has another Arabic branch meaning a gift, the proximity of these two verses merits attention in lexical study.

Care is needed, however: in verse 38, al-aṣfād means bonds, whereas the following verse expresses giving with a word from the root ʿ Ṭ W.

The mere proximity of the verses therefore does not establish that the Qur’an simultaneously intends the meaning of gifts in al-aṣfād.

If proposed as a literary device, that possibility requires an independent argument and should not enter the direct translation.

6. Distinguishing the principal forms

Ṣafada and ṣafd

Ṣafada is a Form I verb. In the familiar binding branch, it refers to fastening and putting in bonds.

Ṣafd is the verbal noun of this use.

Some recorded vocalizations also use ṣafad for the bond itself or for a gift.

The vowels and the word’s combination with the other parts of the sentence therefore matter in identifying its meaning.

Ṣufūd

Ṣufūd is recorded in lexical sources as a verbal noun of binding.

This form does not occur in the Qur’an.

Ṣifād

Ṣifād denotes a bond or an implement used to fasten a person.

The poetic witnesses concerning captivity make this meaning clear.

Aṣfād

Aṣfād is the plural associated with bonds and restraints.

It is the only form of the Ṣ F D family used in the Qur’an.

Some lexicons also record this plural in the gift branch. In the two Qur’anic occurrences, however, the construction muqarranīna fī makes the meaning of bonds and restraints clear.

Ṣaffada and taṣfīd

Ṣaffada is a Form II verb denoting restraining and putting in bonds.

In some uses, this construction emphasizes fastening firmly or more intensely.

Taṣfīd is its verbal noun.

Form II does not, however, invariably specify a particular number of chains or a particular binding implement.

Maṣfūd and muṣaffad

Maṣfūd is the passive participle of the Form I verb: a person or thing that has been restrained.

Muṣaffad is formed from Form II and likewise refers to someone put in bonds.

In ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm’s verse, the plural muṣaffadīnā describes captive, restrained kings.

Aṣfada and iṣfād

In its familiar use, aṣfada means to give a gift.

Iṣfād is its verbal noun.

Some sources nevertheless also record binding as a meaning of this verb.

It is therefore better to examine the particular use in the sentence than to determine the meaning solely from the morphological form.

Istaṣfada

Istaṣfada is a Form X verb associated with requesting a gift.

This form does not occur in the Qur’an.

7. Comparison with related roots

Ṣ F D (ṣafada) and W TH Q (wathaqa)

Wathaqa in Arabic is associated with firmness, confidence, and making secure.

Wathāq can denote a bond that secures a thing or a person.

Muḥammad 47:4 speaks of making captives’ bonds secure.

Ṣafada, in the principal branch represented in the Qur’an, likewise concerns restraint.

W TH Q, however, has a wider family concerning firmness and trust, while Ṣ F D also has a separate branch of gifts and giving alongside bonds and restraints.

The two thus overlap in binding but are not complete synonyms.

Study of W TH Q

Ṣ F D (ṣafada) and Q Y D (qayyada)

Qayd in Arabic denotes an implement that restricts movement, especially a fetter for the feet.

Ṣafad can likewise refer to a restraint or bond.

Lexicographers sometimes explain one through the other.

The use of ṣafad, however, does not necessarily restrict the place of binding to the feet.

Sources also discuss bonds for the hands and neck and other forms of restraint.

Translating al-aṣfād solely as ankle fetters may therefore narrow the word’s scope excessively.

Ṣ F D (ṣafada) and GH L L (ghull)

Ghull in Arabic denotes a restraint that can bind the neck and hands.

This word occurs in various Qur’anic verses in contexts of captivity and punishment.

Ṣafad may also denote such a bond and is explained through ghull in lexical sources.

Not every ṣafad, however, necessarily has the particular form of a ghull.

In Ibrāhīm 14:49, the more general bonds and restraints stays closer to the wording.

Ṣ F D (ṣafada) and S L S L (silsilah)

Silsilah denotes a chain.

This family appears in contexts of restraint and punishment in Al-Insān 76:4 and Al-Ḥāqqah 69:32.

Al-aṣfād in its two verses can likewise include chains.

Silsilah, however, evokes a more specific implement made of connected links, whereas ṣafad can refer to various kinds of bonds and restraints.

Chains is therefore a natural equivalent for al-aṣfād, although restraints has a broader lexical scope.

Study of S L S L

Ṣ F D (ṣafada) and Q R N (qarana)

This comparison is particularly important for understanding the Qur’anic verses because both roots occur in the same expression.

Qarana concerns joining and placing together.

Ṣafada concerns binding and restraint.

In muqarranīna fī al-aṣfād, joining and the means of restraint occur together.

The two verbs, however, are not complete synonyms.

Two things may be joined without being bound with a chain.

A person may likewise be held in a restraint without necessarily being attached to another person.

Both aspects must therefore be considered when translating the Qur’anic expression.

Ṣ F D (ṣafada) and M N N (manna)

In some Arabic uses, manna refers to giving and doing good.

Aṣfada in the gift branch likewise concerns giving.

M N N, however, has other meanings, including reminding someone of a favor and recounting a blessing.

Iṣfād in its familiar use focuses on giving a gift.

The two families thus overlap in giving, but their derivatives are not all identical.

8. Challenges in understanding and translation

Does every use of Ṣ F D mean a chain?

No.

The meaning of gifts and giving is recorded through reliable lexical evidence and pre-Islamic poetry.

A definition restricted to chains and bonds would therefore be incomplete.

In the Qur’an, however, both existing occurrences refer to bonds and restraints.

The existence of the gift meaning must not become a reason to translate al-aṣfād in these two verses as gifts.

Is giving inherently a way of making someone dependent?

No.

Some lexicographers liken giving to a kind of bond because it can create a sense of indebtedness.

This literary explanation does not determine the nature of every gift.

A giver may give without expecting a return, and a recipient may remember the giver gratefully without becoming bound to unlimited obedience or dependence.

A possible relationship between the two branches must therefore not become a theory that gift recipients are subject to moral coercion.

Does being restrained always indicate guilt?

No.

In some contexts, putting someone in bonds is part of an offender’s punishment.

In some historical circumstances, prisoners of war were also put in bonds.

Maṣfūd itself, however, states only the condition of being restrained. It does not by itself establish whether the person is guilty, innocent, oppressed, or deserving of punishment.

In Ibrāhīm 14:49, the description al-mujrimīn establishes the moral and punitive setting.

In Ṣād 38:38, the group’s identity is understood from the narrative context.

The moral judgment therefore comes from the expression and its context, not from the root’s independent meaning.

Does al-aṣfād specify a particular kind of chain?

No.

In Arabic, this family can include bonds and restraints made of various materials.

The two Qur’anic verses specify neither the number of bonds, their material, nor the precise way they are connected.

Direct translation should therefore not add unstated physical details.

Can muqarranīna be omitted?

No.

It comes from another root and adds an independent meaning to the sentence.

Al-aṣfād identifies the means of binding, whereas muqarranīna specifies being bound and joined together.

Translating the complete expression as bound in bonds and chains or bound together with restraints is therefore more precise than simply in chains.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

In Ibrāhīm 14:49:

وَتَرَى الْمُجْرِمِينَ يَوْمَئِذٍ مُقَرَّنِينَ فِي الْأَصْفَادِ

wa-tarā al-mujrimīna yawmaʾidhin muqarranīna fī al-aṣfādi

Preferred rendering:

“And on that day you will see the guilty bound together in bonds and chains.”

In Ṣād 38:38:

وَآخَرِينَ مُقَرَّنِينَ فِي الْأَصْفَادِ

wa-ākharīna muqarranīna fī al-aṣfādi

Preferred rendering:

“And others who had been bound together in bonds and chains.”

In both expressions, al-aṣfād means bonds and restraints.

For non-Qur’anic forms, the following equivalents are suitable:

  • Ṣafadahu: he bound him or put him in bonds.
  • Ṣifād: a bond or restraint.
  • Aṣfād: bonds and chains.
  • Ṣaffadahu: he bound him firmly.
  • Maṣfūd: someone put in bonds.
  • Muṣaffad: someone restrained and chained.
  • Aṣfadahu in the gift use: he gave him a gift.
  • Iṣfād: giving a gift.
  • Istaṣfada: he requested a gift.

These equivalents require care: some forms, particularly aṣfada, have more than one use in lexical sources. The exact equivalent must therefore be determined from the sentence and context.

10. Conclusion

The root Ṣ F D has two well-attested Arabic branches: binding and restraint, and gifts and giving.

In the first branch, ṣafad and ṣifād denote bonds and restraints, while taṣfīd denotes putting in bonds.

In the second branch, iṣfād is giving something to another person.

Some lexicographers see an illustrative relationship between the branches: just as a chain restricts movement, giving may create a bond of gratitude and commitment between people.

This illustrative relationship does not establish the historical origin of one meaning from the other. Ibn Fāris also presents them as two separate principles.

Pre-Islamic Arabic poetry attests both uses. In ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm’s Muʿallaqah, muṣaffadīnā describes captive, restrained kings. In the poetry of Al-Aʿshā and Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī, forms of the same family concern giving and gifts.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an, both times as al-aṣfād and both times meaning bonds and restraints.

In Ibrāhīm 14:49, the guilty are described as bound in restraints in a scene of resurrection.

In Ṣād 38:38, another group is put in bonds within the account of Solomon’s rule.

Concise root definition: Ṣ F D in one branch denotes binding and fastening with bonds and restraints, and in another denotes gifts and giving. In all its Qur’anic uses, however, al-aṣfād means bonds and chains. Brief root meaning: Binding and restraining with bonds; in another branch, gifts and giving; Qur’anic aṣfād: bonds and chains

The essential distinction is that shared root letters do not license merging two independent meanings in every use. In understanding the Qur’anic verses, the meaning of bonds and restraints must be preserved. The meaning of gifts, despite its reliable linguistic basis, must not be added to the verses.
