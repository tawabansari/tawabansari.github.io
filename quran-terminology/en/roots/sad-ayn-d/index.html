Ascending, rising, and the difficulty of ascent: from moving upward to the exposed surface of the earth and arduous hardship

The Arabic root Ṣ ʿ D concerns ascending, moving upward, rising, and the difficulty of an uphill route. In this family, ṣuʿūd means ascent, ṣaʿūd a difficult path or mountain pass, ṣaʿīd the exposed surface of the earth, and taṣaʿʿud ascent and, in some uses, a matter becoming difficult.

In the Qur’an, this family extends beyond bodily movement. Good speech ascending toward God, breathless difficulty, severe punishment, people moving along a route, and the ground in the verses on tayammum are all expressed through forms of this root.

The root occurs nine times in nine verses, in six morphological and lexical forms: tuṣʿidūna in Āl ʿImrān 3:153, yaṣṣaʿʿadu in Al-Anʿām 6:125, yaṣʿadu in Fāṭir 35:10, ṣaʿadan in Al-Jinn 72:17, ṣaʿūdan in Al-Muddaththir 74:17, and ṣaʿīdan four times, in Al-Nisāʾ 4:43, Al-Māʾidah 5:6, Al-Kahf 18:8, and Al-Kahf 18:40.

The crucial distinction is that ascent, ṣaʿīd, and arduous ascent do not have an identical meaning simply because they share a root. The verb of ascent foregrounds upward movement; ṣaʿīd denotes the ground’s surface; and ṣaʿūd and some uses of ṣaʿad emphasize hardship.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: nine occurrences in six morphological categories

1. The semantic core: elevation and difficulty

Ibn Fāris writes about this family in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

أَصْلٌ صَحِيحٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى ارْتِفَاعٍ وَمَشَقَّةٍ

aṣlun ṣaḥīḥun yadullu ʿalā irtifāʿin wa-mashaqqatin

That is, the root denotes elevation and difficulty.

He then contrasts ṣuʿūd with ḥudūr, going down a slope.

This explanation brings out two aspects: movement or a condition associated with height, and the difficulty of certain uphill routes.

These two features should not be assumed equally present in every derivative.

Someone may climb a short step with little difficulty, whereas traversing a high, difficult pass may demand considerable strength.

Ascending is therefore the verb’s familiar meaning, while difficulty may characterize the route or result from the action.

Ṣaʿida: moving upward

In Arabic, ṣaʿida al-jabala or ṣaʿida fī al-jabali means climbing a mountain.

Ṣaʿida fī al-sullami is also used for climbing a ladder or stairs.

These uses emphasize movement from a lower level to a higher one.

The verb may accompany fī, ilā, or ʿalā; each construction specifies the movement’s relation to its route or destination.

Ṣaʿida ilā foregrounds movement toward a higher destination.

Ṣaʿida ʿalā can express climbing onto something.

In references to mountains or stairs, ṣaʿida fī indicates movement along an ascending route.

These distinctions do not, however, have entirely fixed boundaries throughout Arabic usage.

Ṣuʿūd: the act of ascending

Ṣuʿūd is the Form I verbal noun and denotes the act of ascending.

Hubūṭ and ḥudūr, by contrast, are used for descending or going downhill.

In Arabic, ascent may concern a person, an animal, or something else moving from a lower level to a higher one.

Ṣuʿūd alone does not specify the destination’s altitude, the movement’s duration, or its degree of difficulty.

Those features must be established from the route, its descriptions, and other parts of the sentence.

2. Ṣaʿūd and ṣaʿdāʾ: a difficult route and the hardship of climbing

One important branch of this family names a route that is difficult to traverse.

Lisān al-ʿArab explains al-ṣaʿūd as al-ʿaqabatu al-kaʾūdu: a difficult, arduous mountain pass.

Ṣaʿūdāʾ likewise denotes an ascent or passage requiring considerable effort to climb.

In this use, direction is no longer the only concern; the route’s difficulty is also foregrounded.

A poetic witness from Tamīm ibn Muqbil

A poem attributed to Tamīm ibn Muqbil says:

وَحَدَّثَهُ أَنَّ السَّبِيلَ ثَنِيَّةٌ
صَعُودَاءُ تَدْعُو كُلَّ كَهْلٍ وَأَمْرَدَا

wa-ḥaddathahu anna al-sabīla thaniyyatun / ṣaʿūdāʾu tadʿū kulla kahlin wa-amradā

Translation: “He told him that the way was a steep, difficult pass, calling every mature man and youth to traverse it.”

Here ṣaʿūdāʾ describes thaniyyah, a passage or mountain pass.

In this combination, the upward slope and the difficulty of traversing it are connected.

Tamīm ibn Muqbil was a mukhaḍram poet, one who lived through both the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. This is therefore a witness to early Arabic, but without independent evidence its precise composition cannot be dated before the Qur’an’s revelation.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Tamīm ibn Muqbil’s poetic witness

Difficulty in nonphysical matters

Arabic also records taṣaʿʿadanī al-amru: a matter became difficult and burdensome for me.

The person does not literally climb a mountain or stairs; the task’s difficulty is compared with that of ascent.

This extension is understandable. Climbing a difficult route may require great strength, and progress in a demanding task may create a similar experience.

The resemblance does not mean that every use of ascent necessarily denotes suffering and hardship.

Difficulty has become established in some forms and combinations of this family, but each use requires separate examination.

Ṣuʿadāʾ: difficult breathing

Arabic sources also record ṣuʿadāʾ for prolonged, labored breathing.

In yatanaffasu al-ṣuʿadāʾa, a person draws a long, heavy breath, sometimes because of pain, grief, fatigue, or difficulty.

Some explanations relate the expression to the breath rising and being difficult to release.

This use shows that the Ṣ ʿ D family can describe the bodily experience of difficulty.

It should not become the basic meaning of every derivative. Ṣaʿīd in the verses concerning the ground does not mean shortness of breath.

3. Ṣaʿīd: the exposed surface of the earth

Ṣaʿīd is one of this root’s most important forms.

It occurs four times in the Qur’an, always concerning the earth and its surface.

Lexical sources disagree about its precise scope.

Some define it as soil, while others give the broader meaning of the upper, exposed surface of the earth.

Is ṣaʿīd the same as soil?

In an account transmitted by Ibn Durayd in Jamharat al-Lughah, Abū ʿUbaydah explains ṣaʿīd as soil unmixed with sand or saline material.

Al-Zajjāj and other lexicographers, by contrast, understand it as the earth’s surface, whether that surface is soil or something else.

In explaining Al-Nisāʾ 4:43, Al-Ṭabarī also transmits both views and several intermediate interpretations.

It is therefore incorrect to claim that all Arabic lexicographers agreed that ṣaʿīd means only soft soil.

The claim that no lexicographer understood it as soil is equally inconsistent with early sources.

Jamharat al-Lughah: Abū ʿUbaydah’s view and the ground-surface definition

Taken together, the uses make one point clearer: ṣaʿīd concerns the surface of the earth. Soil may be one instance of that surface, but restricting every use to soft, dusty soil requires supporting evidence.

Why is this word connected with ascent?

Lexical explanations sometimes identify ṣaʿīd as the upper, visible surface of the earth: the part on which people stand and with which they come into contact.

This allows a connection with the family of height and rising: the exposed surface stands in contrast to the layers beneath it.

The explanation does not mean that ṣaʿīd is always elevated or mountainous ground.

The surface of a level plain can also be ṣaʿīd.

The possible connection with rising and the upper surface thus clarifies the spatial definition; it does not authorize turning every instance into a hill or elevation.

Ṣaʿīd and pathways

Arabic also records ṣaʿīd for a path or surface on which people move about.

Plurals such as ṣuʿud and ṣuʿudāt are recorded for this use.

In these combinations, ṣaʿīd is a traversable surface or route, not necessarily soil that can be gathered by hand.

This does not establish that ṣaʿīd means a road or public thoroughfare in every Qur’anic verse.

The meaning must be determined within each verse’s context.

4. Ṣaʿīd in the verses on tayammum

Al-Nisāʾ 4:43: fa-tayammamū ṣaʿīdan ṭayyiban

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَا تَقْرَبُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَأَنْتُمْ سُكَارَىٰ حَتَّىٰ تَعْلَمُوا مَا تَقُولُونَ وَلَا جُنُبًا إِلَّا عَابِرِي سَبِيلٍ حَتَّىٰ تَغْتَسِلُوا وَإِنْ كُنْتُمْ مَرْضَىٰ أَوْ عَلَىٰ سَفَرٍ أَوْ جَاءَ أَحَدٌ مِنْكُمْ مِنَ الْغَائِطِ أَوْ لَامَسْتُمُ النِّسَاءَ فَلَمْ تَجِدُوا مَاءً فَتَيَمَّمُوا صَعِيدًا طَيِّبًا فَامْسَحُوا بِوُجُوهِكُمْ وَأَيْدِيكُمْ إِنَّ اللَّهَ كَانَ عَفُوًّا غَفُورًا

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū lā taqrabū al-ṣalāta wa-antum sukārā ḥattā taʿlamū mā taqūlūna wa-lā junuban illā ʿābirī sabīlin ḥattā taghtasilū wa-in kuntum marḍā aw ʿalā safarin aw jāʾa aḥadun minkum mina al-ghāʾiṭi aw lāmastumu al-nisāʾa fa-lam tajidū māʾan fa-tayammamū ṣaʿīdan ṭayyiban fa-imsaḥū bi-wujūhikum wa-aydīkum inna allāha kāna ʿafuwwan ghafūran

Translation: O you who have believed, do not approach ṣalāh while intoxicated, until you know what you are saying; nor in a state of janābah, except as passers along a way, until you wash. If you are ill or traveling, or one of you has come from relieving himself, or you have had contact with women and find no water, then turn toward a clean surface of the earth and wipe your faces and hands. Surely The God is Pardoning, Forgiving.

Here ṣaʿīdan is an accusative noun, and ṭayyiban qualifies it.

Fa-tayammamū comes from Y M M and denotes intending or turning toward something.

The construction fa-tayammamū ṣaʿīdan ṭayyiban therefore concerns turning toward a clean surface of the earth.

Ṣaʿīd itself is not the name of the act of tayammum; it is the object toward which the action is directed.

The role of ṭayyiban

Ṭayyiban comes from Ṭ Y B and here expresses cleanliness and suitability.

Cleanliness should therefore not be made part of ṣaʿīd’s independent meaning.

Ṣaʿīd generally may mean the earth’s surface; in this command, ṭayyiban restricts it to a surface with the quality specified in the verse.

Nor should every soil or surface be assumed equally clean and suitable simply because it lies on the ground.

That quality requires separate consideration.

Study of Ṭ Y B

Is tayammum necessarily restricted to soft soil?

There is a real disagreement in lexical sources about the scope of ṣaʿīd.

Restricting ṣaʿīd to soil narrows the range of material used.

Understanding it as the exposed surface of the earth gives it a broader linguistic scope, potentially including soil, rock, or another natural ground surface.

The verse itself does not say turāb; it says ṣaʿīdan ṭayyiban.

Directly translating this as “soft, dusty soil” therefore adds features not independently specified by ṣaʿīd.

The lexical meaning alone does not settle every practical detail of tayammum. Its relation to wiping, the surface’s material, and the conditions of use must be examined through the complete command and related verses.

Al-Māʾidah 5:6: the repeated command and the role of minhu

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِذَا قُمْتُمْ إِلَى الصَّلَاةِ فَاغْسِلُوا وُجُوهَكُمْ وَأَيْدِيَكُمْ إِلَى الْمَرَافِقِ وَامْسَحُوا بِرُءُوسِكُمْ وَأَرْجُلَكُمْ إِلَى الْكَعْبَيْنِ وَإِنْ كُنْتُمْ جُنُبًا فَاطَّهَّرُوا وَإِنْ كُنْتُمْ مَرْضَىٰ أَوْ عَلَىٰ سَفَرٍ أَوْ جَاءَ أَحَدٌ مِنْكُمْ مِنَ الْغَائِطِ أَوْ لَامَسْتُمُ النِّسَاءَ فَلَمْ تَجِدُوا مَاءً فَتَيَمَّمُوا صَعِيدًا طَيِّبًا فَامْسَحُوا بِوُجُوهِكُمْ وَأَيْدِيكُمْ مِنْهُ مَا يُرِيدُ اللَّهُ لِيَجْعَلَ عَلَيْكُمْ مِنْ حَرَجٍ وَلَٰكِنْ يُرِيدُ لِيُطَهِّرَكُمْ وَلِيُتِمَّ نِعْمَتَهُ عَلَيْكُمْ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَشْكُرُونَ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū idhā qumtum ilā al-ṣalāti fa-ighsilū wujūhakum wa-aydiyakum ilā al-marāfiqi wa-imsaḥū bi-ruʾūsikum wa-arjulakum ilā al-kaʿbayni wa-in kuntum junuban fa-iṭṭahharū wa-in kuntum marḍā aw ʿalā safarin aw jāʾa aḥadun minkum mina al-ghāʾiṭi aw lāmastumu al-nisāʾa fa-lam tajidū māʾan fa-tayammamū ṣaʿīdan ṭayyiban fa-imsaḥū bi-wujūhikum wa-aydīkum minhu mā yurīdu allāhu li-yajʿala ʿalaykum min ḥarajin wa-lākin yurīdu li-yuṭahhirakum wa-li-yutimma niʿmatahu ʿalaykum laʿallakum tashkurūna

Translation: O you who have believed, when you rise for ṣalāh, wash your faces and hands up to the elbows, and wipe over your heads and feet up to the ankles. If you are in a state of janābah, purify yourselves. If you are ill or traveling, or one of you has come from relieving himself, or you have had contact with women and find no water, then turn toward a clean surface of the earth and wipe your faces and hands from it. The God does not intend to place hardship upon you; rather, He intends to purify you and complete His blessing upon you, so that you may give thanks.

The same combination, ṣaʿīdan ṭayyiban, occurs here.

Compared with Al-Nisāʾ 4:43, however, minhu follows fa-imsaḥū bi-wujūhikum wa-aydīkum.

The distinction matters because the pronoun -hu refers back to ṣaʿīd, making the wiping’s relation to it more explicit.

What does minhu specify?

The Arabic preposition min has several uses, including origin, partitivity, and something’s relation to its source.

Here minhu indicates that wiping takes place with what comes from, or is related to, ṣaʿīd.

Whether min necessarily means gathering soil particles and transferring them to the skin is a grammatical and practical interpretation that must be assessed alongside other evidence.

This preposition alone cannot establish that every surface lacking visible dust is necessarily excluded from ṣaʿīd.

Conversely, minhu cannot be ignored, removing the wiping’s relation to the specified surface.

A direct translation appropriately retains “from it”; the practical manner belongs in a specialized study of the command.

Ṣaʿīd and the removal of hardship

Al-Māʾidah 5:6 concludes:

مَا يُرِيدُ اللَّهُ لِيَجْعَلَ عَلَيْكُمْ مِنْ حَرَجٍ

mā yurīdu allāhu li-yajʿala ʿalaykum min ḥarajin

That is, God does not intend to place hardship upon you.

This statement occurs among commands concerning purity and the absence of water.

The option of turning toward ṣaʿīd in the stated circumstances thus belongs within the removal of hardship and provision of a practical alternative.

This message emerges from the whole verse, not because ṣaʿīd itself means leniency.

Nor can this clause, without examining all related commands, establish new details about the number of movements, every surface’s material, or exceptional circumstances.

The lexical meaning of ṣaʿīd clarifies the word’s scope; the practical ruling must be assessed through the Qur’an’s complete wording and commands.

5. Ṣaʿīd in descriptions of the earth’s transformation

Al-Kahf 18:8: ṣaʿīdan juruzan

وَإِنَّا لَجَاعِلُونَ مَا عَلَيْهَا صَعِيدًا جُرُزًا

wa-innā la-jāʿilūna mā ʿalayhā ṣaʿīdan juruzan

Translation: We will indeed turn what is upon the earth into a barren, vegetationless ground surface.

Ṣaʿīdan and juruzan describe the earth’s condition after its adornments have disappeared.

Al-Kahf 18:7 describes what is on the earth as adornment and a means of testing people.

Verse 8 then presents the transformation of that condition.

Ṣaʿīdan denotes the earth’s surface.

Juruzan comes from J R Z and here expresses barrenness and the absence of vegetation.

Thus being vegetationless is not part of ṣaʿīd’s independent meaning. It follows from juruzan and the connected verses.

Study of J R Z

Is ṣaʿīd always dry, barren land?

No.

In this verse ṣaʿīd is qualified by juruzan.

If ṣaʿīd always independently meant barren land, the qualifier juruz would contribute less to the combination.

In Arabic, ṣaʿīd may be the ground’s surface, whether or not vegetation grows on it.

Here, however, the earth loses its previous adornment and covering.

“A barren ground surface” therefore suits the complete combination; “barren” should not become part of ṣaʿīd’s fixed definition.

Al-Kahf 18:40: ṣaʿīdan zalaqan

فَعَسَىٰ رَبِّي أَنْ يُؤْتِيَنِ خَيْرًا مِنْ جَنَّتِكَ وَيُرْسِلَ عَلَيْهَا حُسْبَانًا مِنَ السَّمَاءِ فَتُصْبِحَ صَعِيدًا زَلَقًا

fa-ʿasā rabbī an yuʾtiyani khayran min jannatika wa-yursila ʿalayhā ḥusbānan mina al-samāʾi fa-tuṣbiḥa ṣaʿīdan zalaqan

Translation: Perhaps my Lord will give me something better than your garden and send upon it a measured event from the sky, so that it becomes a smooth, slippery ground surface.

Here ṣaʿīdan describes the ground’s condition after the garden is lost.

Zalaqan comes from Z L Q and concerns slipperiness.

Lexical and exegetical explanations understand the combination as a smooth, bare, uncovered ground surface.

The components of meaning must nevertheless be kept distinct.

Ṣaʿīd concerns the earth’s surface.

Zalaq foregrounds slipperiness or slippery smoothness.

The garden’s destruction and loss of vegetation follow from the verses’ context.

The difference between ṣaʿīdan juruzan and ṣaʿīdan zalaqan

Both combinations occur in the same surah and concern the earth after it loses its covering or produce.

Their qualifiers differ, however.

In Al-Kahf 18:8, juruzan emphasizes barrenness and the absence of vegetation.

In Al-Kahf 18:40, zalaqan expresses the surface’s slipperiness.

In both, ṣaʿīd remains the ground’s surface.

It should therefore not be translated “soil” in one and “stone” in the other unless context or linguistic evidence requires that distinction.

Ṣaʿīd is not independently the name of a particular soil type; combined with different qualifiers, it can describe different conditions of the ground’s surface.

6. Physical ascent, difficulty, and figurative meaning in the Qur’an

Āl ʿImrān 3:153: idh tuṣʿidūna

إِذْ تُصْعِدُونَ وَلَا تَلْوُونَ عَلَىٰ أَحَدٍ وَالرَّسُولُ يَدْعُوكُمْ فِي أُخْرَاكُمْ فَأَثَابَكُمْ غَمًّا بِغَمٍّ لِكَيْلَا تَحْزَنُوا عَلَىٰ مَا فَاتَكُمْ وَلَا مَا أَصَابَكُمْ وَاللَّهُ خَبِيرٌ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ

idh tuṣʿidūna wa-lā talwūna ʿalā aḥadin wa-al-rasūlu yadʿūkum fī ukhrākum fa-athābakum ghamman bi-ghammin li-kaylā taḥzanū ʿalā mā fātakum wa-lā mā aṣābakum wa-allāhu khabīrun bi-mā taʿmalūna

Translation: When you were moving away and pressing onward, paying attention to no one, while the messenger called you from behind; then grief after grief befell you, so that you would not grieve over what you had lost or what had befallen you. The God is aware of what you do.

Tuṣʿidūna here is a Form IV imperfect, distinct from the Form I taṣʿadūna.

Early lexical sources attend to this distinction.

The difference between ṣaʿida and aṣʿada

In a familiar Arabic use, ṣaʿida denotes climbing a mountain, stairs, or an elevated place.

Aṣʿada fī al-arḍi, however, can denote advancing across land, setting out on a journey, or moving away along a route, even when the movement is not necessarily upward.

Al-Farrāʾ and several lexicographers report this distinction.

Explaining Āl ʿImrān 3:153, Al-Ṭabarī likewise distinguishes climbing a mountain from moving away along the ground or a valley.

On this basis, tuṣʿidūna in the widely transmitted reading concerns moving away and continuing along a route in flight.

Were they climbing a mountain?

Some exegetical reports say that a group headed toward the mountain.

Other reports describe their movement in a valley or along level ground.

A reading attributed to Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī also transmits taṣʿadūna, which is more directly compatible with climbing.

The Ḥafṣ reading, however, has tuṣʿidūna.

Lexically, this form can express advancing and moving away along a route without requiring a mountain ascent in the verb itself.

The verse also says wa-lā talwūna ʿalā aḥadin, describing paying attention to no one or not turning back toward others.

The main image is therefore hurried movement away in difficult circumstances.

A direct translation, “you were moving away and pressing onward,” is more cautious than “you were climbing the mountain,” because the route’s details must be established from other evidence.

Al-Anʿām 6:125: ka-annamā yaṣṣaʿʿadu fī al-samāʾi

فَمَنْ يُرِدِ اللَّهُ أَنْ يَهْدِيَهُ يَشْرَحْ صَدْرَهُ لِلْإِسْلَامِ وَمَنْ يُرِدْ أَنْ يُضِلَّهُ يَجْعَلْ صَدْرَهُ ضَيِّقًا حَرَجًا كَأَنَّمَا يَصَّعَّدُ فِي السَّمَاءِ كَذَٰلِكَ يَجْعَلُ اللَّهُ الرِّجْسَ عَلَى الَّذِينَ لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ

fa-man yuridi allāhu an yahdiyahu yashraḥ ṣadrahu li-al-islāmi wa-man yurid an yuḍillahu yajʿal ṣadrahu ḍayyiqan ḥarajan ka-annamā yaṣṣaʿʿadu fī al-samāʾi ka-dhālika yajʿalu allāhu al-rijsa ʿalā alladhīna lā yuʾminūna

Translation: Whoever The God intends to guide, He opens his breast to Islam; and whoever He intends to lead astray, He makes his breast tight and distressed, as though he were struggling to ascend into the sky. Thus The God places defilement upon those who do not believe.

Yaṣṣaʿʿadu here is Form V.

Its original form is yataṣaʿʿadu; the tāʾ assimilates into ṣād, producing the doubled form.

Other readings transmit yaṣṣāʿadu and yaṣʿadu.

These variations concern the formation of ascent and the effort to climb; in this context they do not remove the simile’s overall meaning.

Ascent and the constriction of the breast

The verse contrasts two conditions.

On one side stands the opening of the breast to Islam.

On the other stands tightness and constriction.

The latter condition is then compared to someone attempting to ascend into the sky.

The difficulty and arduousness of ascent matter in this simile.

Al-Ṭabarī explains the image as attempting something extremely difficult and beyond the person’s capacity.

The verse thus concerns more than simple upward movement: it depicts ascent accompanied by constriction and difficulty.

Is the verse about reduced oxygen at altitude?

At high altitudes, reduced air pressure and oxygen partial pressure can make breathing difficult. This is an established scientific fact.

The verse itself, however, does not name air pressure, oxygen, a numerical altitude, or the biological mechanism of breathing.

The Qur’anic simile primarily compares constriction and difficulty with the effort to ascend into the sky.

One may reflect on the image’s compatibility with bodily experience at altitude, but that reflection should not be treated as the definitive lexical meaning of yaṣṣaʿʿadu or proof that the verse presents a complete scientific theory.

The verse’s semantic focus also concerns guidance and misguidance. The verb of ascent alone cannot resolve every question of human choice and divine will.

Fāṭir 35:10: ilayhi yaṣʿadu al-kalimu al-ṭayyibu

مَنْ كَانَ يُرِيدُ الْعِزَّةَ فَلِلَّهِ الْعِزَّةُ جَمِيعًا إِلَيْهِ يَصْعَدُ الْكَلِمُ الطَّيِّبُ وَالْعَمَلُ الصَّالِحُ يَرْفَعُهُ وَالَّذِينَ يَمْكُرُونَ السَّيِّئَاتِ لَهُمْ عَذَابٌ شَدِيدٌ وَمَكْرُ أُولَٰئِكَ هُوَ يَبُورُ

man kāna yurīdu al-ʿizzata fa-li-llāhi al-ʿizzatu jamīʿan ilayhi yaṣʿadu al-kalimu al-ṭayyibu wa-al-ʿamalu al-ṣāliḥu yarfaʿuhu wa-alladhīna yamkurūna al-sayyiʾāti lahum ʿadhābun shadīdun wa-makru ulāʾika huwa yabūru

Translation following a common interpretation: Whoever desires might—all might belongs to God. Good speech ascends toward Him, and righteous action raises it. Those who contrive evil deeds face severe punishment, and their scheming will perish.

Yaṣʿadu here is a Form I imperfect.

Its subject is al-kalimu al-ṭayyibu, good speech.

Unlike the physical uses, ascent here concerns speech.

Does speech ascending mean the bodily movement of words?

Arabic can use ascent for a nonphysical matter.

Here ilayhi yaṣʿadu expresses good speech’s relation to God.

The expression can signify speech reaching the divine presence and being accepted.

The verb yaṣʿadu does not itself specify the physics of words moving, their path through the universe, or a location for God with spatial coordinates.

Conversely, the image of ascent should not be removed altogether by translating only “God accepts speech” without explanation: the verse itself uses an upward image.

Wa-al-ʿamalu al-ṣāliḥu yarfaʿuhu

The verse continues:

وَالْعَمَلُ الصَّالِحُ يَرْفَعُهُ

wa-al-ʿamalu al-ṣāliḥu yarfaʿuhu

In one common interpretation, righteous action raises good speech.

On this analysis, al-ʿamalu al-ṣāliḥu is the subject of yarfaʿu, while the pronoun -hu refers back to al-kalimu al-ṭayyibu.

Good speech is thus closely connected with righteous action; speech detached from action does not convey everything the verse expresses.

Exegetical sources also present other analyses of the pronoun’s referent and the relation between speech and action.

This disagreement concerns the second clause’s construction; yaṣʿadu in the first clause still means ascending.

The raising expressed by yarfaʿu should therefore not be equated with the independent meaning of yaṣʿadu.

7. Ṣaʿad and ṣaʿūd: severe, arduous punishment

Al-Jinn 72:17: ʿadhāban ṣaʿadan

لِنَفْتِنَهُمْ فِيهِ وَمَنْ يُعْرِضْ عَنْ ذِكْرِ رَبِّهِ يَسْلُكْهُ عَذَابًا صَعَدًا

li-naftinahum fīhi wa-man yuʿriḍ ʿan dhikri rabbihi yaslukhu ʿadhāban ṣaʿadan

Translation: So that We may test them through it; whoever turns away from the remembrance of his Lord, He causes him to enter a severe, arduous punishment.

Ṣaʿadan here qualifies ʿadhāban, punishment.

Lexical sources record ṣaʿad for difficulty and severe hardship.

Al-Ṭabarī likewise explains this verse’s punishment as severe and difficult.

The point here is therefore not a person literally climbing a mountain; the punishment’s severity and arduousness are foregrounded.

Is ṣaʿadan an actual mountain pass?

Some exegetical reports explain ṣaʿad as a mountain in Hell.

The verse itself, however, uses ṣaʿadan to qualify ʿadhāban and contains no independent word for a mountain, stone, or specified route.

“A severe, arduous punishment” is therefore a clearer basic translation in terms of construction and lexical usage.

Reports of a particular mountain in Hell should be distinguished from the word’s direct meaning.

This qualifier also does not determine the punishment’s extent, duration, or bodily characteristics.

Al-Ṭabarī: severe, arduous punishment

Al-Muddaththir 74:17: sa-urhiquhu ṣaʿūdan

سَأُرْهِقُهُ صَعُودًا

sa-urhiquhu ṣaʿūdan

Translation: I will burden him with arduous hardship.

Ṣaʿūd in Arabic names a difficult mountain pass or steep ascent.

Figuratively, it can denote a task requiring great effort and suffering.

Urhiqu is also important here, since it expresses burdening or imposing something heavy.

The condition’s severity and weight therefore emerge from the two words combined.

The difference between ṣaʿadan and ṣaʿūdan

Ṣaʿadan in Al-Jinn 72:17 qualifies punishment.

Ṣaʿūdan in Al-Muddaththir 74:17 names or evokes the arduousness of an uphill struggle imposed upon someone.

Both concern hardship and difficulty, but their construction and role differ.

Lexical and exegetical sources also report ṣaʿūd as a mountain in Hell.

Those details cannot, however, be established definitively from the verse’s wording alone.

The preceding context in Al-Muddaththir describes someone who, despite receiving blessings, stubbornly resists the divine signs.

Ṣaʿūdan here therefore concerns a severe punishment following that behavior.

The word’s lexical meaning does not become the behavior itself, such as arrogance or denial.

Arduous ascent creates an image of hardship; the context establishes what kind of punishment that hardship constitutes.

8. Other forms of the root in Arabic

Ṣaʿida and ṣuʿūd

Ṣaʿida is the Form I verb and ṣuʿūd its verbal noun.

Their familiar use denotes ascending and moving upward.

Aṣʿada and iṣʿād

Aṣʿada is Form IV.

Some uses concern advancing across land, setting out on a journey, and moving away; others can concern upward movement.

Iṣʿād should therefore not be restricted to climbing a mountain in every sentence.

Ṣaʿʿada and taṣʿīd

Ṣaʿʿada is Form II.

In some uses it denotes raising or ascending.

Taṣʿīd is likewise known in Arabic for raising and in some technical uses, such as the sublimation of a substance through heating.

Modern chemical and technical meanings must not be imposed on Qur’anic uses.

Taṣaʿʿada and taṣaʿʿud

Taṣaʿʿada concerns ascending and, in some combinations, becoming difficult.

Al-Anʿām 6:125 uses the assimilated form yaṣṣaʿʿadu.

In context, this form depicts an arduous, difficult ascent.

Ṣaʿūd and ṣaʿdāʾ

Ṣaʿūd and ṣaʿdāʾ denote a route, hill, or mountain pass that is difficult to climb.

Figuratively, they can also express a task’s hardship and difficulty.

Ṣaʿīd

In Qur’anic usage, ṣaʿīd denotes the earth’s surface.

Arabic also records it for pathways and certain exposed ground surfaces.

Without supporting evidence, it should not be restricted to elevated ground or soft soil.

Ṣuʿadāʾ

Ṣuʿadāʾ in some uses denotes a long, labored breath.

Tanaffasa al-ṣuʿadāʾa means drawing a heavy, prolonged breath.

This use does not occur in the Qur’an.

Ṣāʿid and maṣʿad

Ṣāʿid is the Form I active participle and denotes one who ascends.

Maṣʿad can denote a place, route, or direction of ascent.

In later Arabic, forms of this family are also used for devices for ascending.

These newer uses do not create a particular meaning for the Qur’anic verses.

9. Comparison with related roots

Ṣ ʿ D (ṣaʿida) and ʿ L W (ʿalā)

ʿAlā concerns being high and spatial or ranked superiority.

Ṣaʿida, in its primary use, expresses movement from a lower level to a higher one.

ʿUluww may therefore denote the state of being high, while ṣuʿūd more strongly foregrounds movement upward itself.

This distinction is not absolute across all derivatives of the two roots.

Ṣ ʿ D (ṣaʿida) and R F ʿ (rafaʿa)

Rafaʿa in Arabic denotes raising.

Ṣaʿida concerns ascending.

Both roots occur together in Fāṭir 35:10.

Yaṣʿadu expresses good speech moving or ascending, while yarfaʿuhu expresses raising it.

A distinction can thus be drawn between something ascending and another agent raising it.

This should not become an absolute rule that every ascent lacks an agent and every raising involves only an external agent.

Study of R F ʿ

Ṣ ʿ D (ṣaʿida) and R Q Y (raqiya)

Raqiya in Arabic concerns ascending and advancement.

Ṣaʿida is very close to it in uses concerning mountains and stairs.

R Q Y also concerns rank, degree, and progress in some uses.

The Ṣ ʿ D family, by contrast, also has the distinct ṣaʿīd branch for the earth’s surface and ṣaʿūd for hardship.

The roots therefore overlap in ascent, but not all their derivatives are synonymous.

Ṣ ʿ D (ṣaʿida) and H B Ṭ (habaṭa)

Habaṭa concerns coming down and descending.

In its direct meaning, ṣaʿida moves in the opposite direction.

This opposition does not mean that every use of either root necessarily involves physical vertical movement.

Both Arabic families can have broader uses, with their direction and destination established by context.

Study of H B Ṭ

Ṣ ʿ D (ṣaʿīd) and T R B (turāb)

Turāb names soil as an earthly material.

In its broad use, ṣaʿīd denotes the exposed ground surface.

Turāb thus concerns the material, while ṣaʿīd can concern its position and surface.

This distinction matters in Al-Nisāʾ 4:43 and Al-Māʾidah 5:6, where the Qur’anic word is ṣaʿīd, not turāb.

Restricting ṣaʿīd to soft, loose soil therefore fails to convey its full recorded scope.

Study of T R B

Ṣ ʿ D (ṣaʿīd) and ʾ R Ḍ (arḍ)

Arḍ is the general name for the earth as an expanse, place, and domain.

In its terrestrial use, ṣaʿīd more strongly foregrounds the upper, exposed surface.

In Al-Kahf 18:8, this surface becomes barren after the earth’s adornment is removed.

In Al-Kahf 18:40, the garden becomes a slippery surface.

Ṣaʿīd may therefore occupy part of the earth’s semantic field without being identical to every use of arḍ.

Study of ʾ R Ḍ

Ṣ ʿ D (ṣaʿida) and Ṣ B B (ṣabba)

In its familiar use, ṣabba denotes pouring and causing something to flow downward.

Such movement often involves flow from above to below.

Ṣaʿida, by contrast, primarily concerns ascending.

Their directions may oppose one another, but ṣabb does not necessarily mean descending from a height, and ascent does not always concern a physical substance.

10. Challenges of understanding and translation

Do all derivatives of Ṣ ʿ D mean ascending?

No.

Ascent is clear in yaṣʿadu and yaṣṣaʿʿadu.

Ṣaʿīd in Qur’anic uses, however, denotes the earth’s surface.

Ṣaʿadan and ṣaʿūdan also foreground hardship and difficulty in the context of punishment.

One fixed Persian equivalent therefore cannot serve every form.

Is ṣaʿīd always soft soil?

No.

Early sources record both soil and the ground’s surface as definitions.

The uses in Al-Kahf 18:8 and Al-Kahf 18:40 nevertheless show ṣaʿīd’s connection with the ground surface, which can be qualified by different properties such as barrenness or slipperiness.

The general translation “the ground’s surface” is therefore more cautious than restricting the word to a particular soil type.

Is every ascent difficult?

Not necessarily.

Ascending may be easy or difficult.

Hardship is an important feature in ṣaʿūd and some uses of ṣaʿad.

In Fāṭir 35:10, however, yaṣʿadu concerns good speech, and the difficulty of ascending is not the verse’s subject.

Hardship therefore cannot be generalized to every form of the root.

Does tuṣʿidūna in Āl ʿImrān necessarily mean climbing a mountain?

In the widely transmitted reading, the verb is Form IV.

Arabic can use this form for moving away and advancing across land.

The image of hurried movement away is therefore clear in the verse, but the exact terrain should not be determined from the verb alone.

Does good speech’s ascent have a specified physical path?

In Fāṭir 35:10, the Qur’an speaks of good speech ascending toward God.

This expression alone does not determine speech’s bodily route, God’s spatial location, or the nature of the unseen world.

The upward image can be retained in translation, with divine acceptance discussed separately in the analysis.

Is ṣaʿūdan necessarily the name of a mountain in Hell?

Some exegetical reports offer that identification.

In Al-Muddaththir 74:17 itself, however, ṣaʿūd appears alongside urhiqu, forming an image of arduous hardship.

Turning it directly into the name of a mountain with a specified height, material, and nature therefore goes beyond the verse’s wording.

“You were moving away and pressing onward.”

The context clearly shows hurried movement and departure from the site of confrontation.

“A clean surface of the earth.”

This rendering retains ṣaʿīd’s broader meaning and the independent qualifier ṭayyib.

For non-Qur’anic uses, ṣuʿūd can be rendered “ascending”; ṣaʿūd, depending on context, “a difficult mountain pass” or “severe hardship”; ṣuʿadāʾ, “a long, labored breath”; and iṣʿād, in travel, “advancing and moving away along a route.”

12. Conclusion

The Arabic root Ṣ ʿ D concerns elevation, ascent, and the difficulty of moving upward.

In the verbal branch, ascent denotes movement from a lower level to a higher one. Some early uses of iṣʿād, however, also denote moving away and advancing along a route.

In the ṣaʿīd branch, the word denotes the earth’s exposed surface, not necessarily a mountain, elevated ground, or a particular soil type.

In the ṣaʿad and ṣaʿūd branch, the difficulty of an uphill route extends to describing suffering and severe punishment.

The Qur’an clearly displays these distinctions: people move away along a route, good speech ascends, arduous ascent becomes an image of tightness and constriction, the ground surface is described in different conditions, and severe punishment is expressed through forms associated with a difficult uphill struggle.

Concise root definition: In its movement branch, Ṣ ʿ D denotes ascending and moving upward; ṣaʿīd denotes the upper, exposed surface of the earth; and forms such as ṣaʿad and ṣaʿūd denote arduous difficulty and hardship. Brief root meaning: Ascending and moving upward; ṣaʿīd: the exposed surface of the earth; ṣaʿad and ṣaʿūd: hardship and arduous difficulty

Its derivatives therefore cannot all be understood solely as “ascending” or solely as “difficulty.”

The central distinction is that direction of movement, the ground’s surface, and the hardship of ascent are three related but different uses of this family. Each form’s precise meaning must be established from its morphology, grammatical construction, and the verse’s context.
