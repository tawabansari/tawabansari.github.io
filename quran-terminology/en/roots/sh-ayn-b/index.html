Branching, separation, and joining: from mountain passes and tree branches to human communities and mutual recognition in the Qur’an

Early Arabic records two familiar branches of SH ʿ B: splitting, separation, and branching; and gathering, joining, and bringing separated parts together.

Shaʿaba can mean separating or dispersing. Shuʿbah denotes a branch extending from a larger whole, without necessarily being detached from it. Shiʿb denotes a cleft or passage between mountains or elevations.

The family also describes repairing broken objects and gathering scattered parts. Shaʿaba al-ināʾa can mean repairing a vessel by joining its broken sections.

Some early lexicographers classified these opposing senses as contronyms. Another explanation attributes them to different tribal usages, rather than necessarily opposite senses within a single dialect. This is a lexical explanation, not a historical conclusion established here.

Both separation and joining are recorded, but their relationship does not prove which came first or how one developed from the other.

The two common-noun occurrences are shuʿūban in Al-Ḥujurāt 49:13 and shuʿabin in Al-Mursalāt 77:30. This count excludes the proper name Shuʿayb.

The first concerns peoples and tribes formed for mutual recognition. The second describes a three-branched shadow in a scene of judgment and punishment.

The family can thus describe branching within a whole and name human groups with shared connections and belonging.

1. The root in early Arabic

Shiʿb: a mountain cleft or passage

Shiʿb denotes a cleft or passage between mountains or elevations.

It may carry people, animals, or rainwater.

Some lexical accounts describe a narrow channel bounded by higher sides.

The prominent feature is an opening between parts of the terrain.

Imagine a passage between two mountain ranges. It may divide into smaller routes, or several paths may meet in it.

Such terrain can illustrate both separation and the meeting of routes.

Shiʿb does not itself require bifurcation. A passage can have a single clear course; branching is a possible feature, not an invariant part of its meaning.

A poetic witness attributed to ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAntarah reads:

أَرْضُ الشَّرَبَّةِ شِعْبٌ وَوَادِي
رَحَلْتُ وَأَهْلُهَا فِي فُؤَادِي

arḍu al-sharabbati shiʿbun wa-wādī / raḥaltu wa-ahluhā fī fuʾādī

Its general sense is:

“The land of al-Sharabbah is pass and valley; I have departed, but its people remain in my heart.”

Shiʿb appears alongside wādī.

Both are geographical terms. Wādī can denote a valley or watercourse, while shiʿb here highlights a passage between elevations.

The poem appears in collections attributed to ʿAntarah. The attribution and date of this particular verse have not been independently established here, so it cannot prove when the word first appeared.

Its geographical sense is a passage or cleft, rather than a human group or tree branch.

Shuʿbah: a tree branch

Shuʿbah can denote a branch growing from a trunk or larger branch.

Branches are distinguishable from one another while remaining connected to a common source.

A trunk may divide into main branches, each producing smaller branches.

Looking outward from the trunk emphasizes branching and differentiation.

Following the branches inward reveals their common connection.

The image helps explain the semantic relationship, but does not establish the historical origin of every usage.

Labīd ibn Rabīʿah: shuʿbat al-sāq

Labīd, who lived through the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods, describes his camel’s speed:

تَسْلُبُ الْكَانِسَ لَمْ يُؤْرَ بِهَا
شُعْبَةَ السَّاقِ إِذَا الظِّلُّ عَقَلْ

taslubu al-kānisa lam yuʾra bihā / shuʿbata al-sāqi idhā al-ẓillu ʿaqal

“The camel drives the sheltered animal from the branch’s cover before it becomes aware of her, when the shadow has come to rest.”

Al-kānis is an animal sheltered in its cover; shuʿbat al-sāq denotes a tree branch. In the conventional explanation, the swiftly approaching camel causes the sheltered animal to leave its cover beneath the branch.

The camel approaches before the animal becomes aware of it.

The transmitted wording of lam yuʾra bihā varies, while the lexical explanation identifies shuʿbat al-sāq as a tree branch.

The verse attests the botanical use of shuʿbah in early poetry.

Labīd spans both periods, and this verse’s precise date is not established here. It should not be assigned categorically to his pre-Islamic years.

Shaʿaba: splitting and separating

Shaʿaba al-shayʾa is recorded for splitting or separating something.

Shaʿaba amrahu can mean scattering or fragmenting one’s affairs.

Something potentially united becomes divided.

A group may begin with one aim and later divide into factions pursuing different courses.

Early poetry also uses shuʿab for different branches, directions, or purposes.

This should not automatically be equated with blameworthy discord.

Tree branching is natural growth, and a path’s division is not inherently bad. The object and consequences determine the evaluation.

Shaʿaba: gathering and repair

The other semantic branch appears opposite.

In repairing a vessel, shaʿaba al-ināʾa means joining its broken parts.

An artisan might connect sections of a cracked or broken vessel with suitable material, restoring its use.

Such a repairer is called shaʿʿāb, and the craft shiʿābah.

Shuʿbah can also name a piece used to join broken sections.

Here the action reunites separated parts.

The Umayyad poet al-Kumayt ibn Zayd says:

وَقَالَتْ لِيَ النَّفْسُ اشْعَبِ الصَّدْعَ وَاهْتَبِلْ
لِإِحْدَى الْهَنَاتِ الْمُضْلِعَاتِ اهْتِبَالَهَا

wa-qālat liya al-nafsu ishʿabi al-ṣadʿa wa-htabil / li-iḥdā al-hanāti al-muḍliʿāti ihtibālahā

“My soul said to me: Mend the breach, and prepare and devise a response to one of the burdensome matters.”

Ishʿab al-ṣadʿ means repairing or closing a breach.

Al-Kumayt lived after Islam, so this is not pre-Islamic evidence. It nevertheless illustrates the joining and repair sense.

Does shaʿaba have opposite meanings?

Early lexical discussions offer two accounts.

One treats separation and gathering as opposite meanings of the same word.

Another assigns them to differing Arab usages, without assuming both necessarily belonged to one dialect. It is a proposed explanation of the contrast.

Both senses are recorded in Arabic.

An extremely vague definition such as “changing the arrangement of parts” would fail to distinguish this root from many others.

Both branches should therefore remain explicit:

Separation: splitting, branching, and dispersing parts.

Joining: gathering, connecting, and repairing separated parts.

The two Qur’anic common-noun uses concern differentiated groups or branches.

2. Important forms and derivatives

The following forms belong to the family.

شَعَبَ: shaʿaba; separated, split, or dispersed; alternatively, gathered and joined.

شَعْب: shaʿb; a cleft or division; also a large human group encompassing smaller groups or tribes.

شُعُوب: shuʿūb; the plural of shaʿb for peoples, occurring in the Qur’an.

شِعْب: shiʿb; a mountain passage, cleft, or route between elevations.

شِعَاب: shiʿāb; the plural of shiʿb, mountain passes.

شُعْبَة: shuʿbah; a branch or subdivision; also a piece used in joining broken parts.

شُعَب: shuʿab; the plural of shuʿbah, branches, occurring in the Qur’an.

شَعَّبَ: shaʿʿaba; made something branch, divided it, or dispersed it.

تَشَعَّبَ: tashaʿʿaba; branched or divided.

اِنْشَعَبَ: inshaʿaba; separated, branched off, or took a different route.

شَعَّاب: shaʿʿāb; a repairer who joins broken parts of vessels or other objects.

شِعَابَة: shiʿābah; the craft of joining and repairing broken parts.

مَشْعَب: mashʿab; a route or branching way. مِشْعَب: mishʿab, with a different first vowel, is a boring tool used in repair; the forms should be distinguished.

مَشْعُوب: mashʿūb; split or branched; alternatively, joined or repaired.

أَشْعَب: ashʿab; in animal descriptions, having widely separated horns or certain other bodily features.

شَعْبَان: Shaʿbān, the name of an Arabic calendar month. Lexical explanations connect it with dispersal in search of water or other movements, but the original naming event is not securely established.

شَعُوب: Shaʿūb, a name for death understood as separating people. Its vowels and usage differ from shuʿūb, peoples.

شُعُوبِيّ and شُعُوبِيَّة: shuʿūbī and shuʿūbiyyah; later terms associated with debates over ethnic standing and Arabs and non-Arabs. They should not replace the Qur’anic meaning of shuʿūb.

Shuʿayb also occurs as a prophet’s proper name. Its form resembles an Arabic diminutive of shaʿb and has been connected with it lexically, but that resemblance alone cannot establish the name’s historical meaning or equate it with a common noun.

This study counts the two common-noun occurrences, shuʿūban and shuʿabin; Shuʿayb is treated separately as a proper name.

3. Al-Ḥujurāt 49:13: peoples and mutual recognition

The Qur’an says:

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّاسُ إِنَّا خَلَقْنَاكُمْ مِنْ ذَكَرٍ وَأُنْثَىٰ وَجَعَلْنَاكُمْ شُعُوبًا وَقَبَائِلَ لِتَعَارَفُوا ۚ إِنَّ أَكْرَمَكُمْ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ أَتْقَاكُمْ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ عَلِيمٌ خَبِيرٌ

yā ayyuhā al-nāsu innā khalaqnākum min dhakarin wa-unthā wa-jaʿalnākum shuʿūban wa-qabāʾila li-taʿārafū inna akramakum ʿinda allāhi atqākum inna allāha ʿalīmun khabīr

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:13

“O people, We created you from a male and a female and made you peoples and tribes so that you may know one another. The most honored of you before The God is the most mindful of Him. The God is Knowing, Aware.”

This is a clear social use of the word family.

Shaʿb: a large human group

Shuʿūb is the plural of shaʿb.

Shaʿb could designate a large group containing smaller groups and tribes.

Some early genealogical classifications placed shaʿb above qabīlah: several tribes belonged to a people, with further subdivisions within each tribe.

The classification is not uniform. Some explanations call it a large tribe, others a wider collection of tribes.

A single exceptionless hierarchy should not be imposed on all early Arabic usage.

For the verse, peoples alongside tribal groups are what matters.

Are shuʿūb modern nations?

The modern Persian word mellat often suggests a country, national identity, culture, or political organization.

Shaʿb in the Qur’anic period did not necessarily name a state, country, or citizen body.

A people could share ancestry, history, or social connections without modern borders or a single government.

“Nations” can be understandable in translation, but may convey more modern political structure than the historical term requires.

“Peoples” or “large human communities” avoids imposing modern statehood.

The word itself does not specify language, skin color, territory, or political organization as an exclusive classification criterion.

Such features may help identify communities without being fixed components of the word’s meaning.

Peoples and tribes

The Qur’an says:

وَجَعَلْنَاكُمْ شُعُوبًا وَقَبَائِلَ

wa-jaʿalnākum shuʿūban wa-qabāʾil

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:13

“We made you peoples and tribes.”

Qabāʾil, from Q B L, denotes tribes with shared ties.

The two terms occur together.

Their pairing recognizes more than one form of human grouping.

A person can belong simultaneously to larger and smaller groups.

For example, one may belong to a people, a tribe within it, and a family or branch within that tribe.

These memberships need not exclude one another.

The pairing does not prescribe one universal genealogical system.

Nor does the verse make knowledge of tribal ancestry or adherence to tribal organization a condition of legitimate social standing.

Its subject is human difference and social connection.

Li-taʿārafū: mutual recognition

After peoples and tribes, the verse says:

لِتَعَارَفُوا

li-taʿārafū

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:13

“So that you may know one another.”

This expresses a purpose of the groupings mentioned.

Taʿārafū is Form VI of ʿ R F and denotes mutual recognition.

Recognition here is reciprocal: people become known to and recognize one another.

For example, encounters between groups with different languages or histories can promote understanding of their identities, needs, experiences, and relationships. This illustrates a broader application; the word alone does not contain all these details.

It does not mean acquiring superiority over another group.

Nor does it require erasing every difference to form a uniform society.

The verse acknowledges different groups and associates them with mutual recognition.

Difference serves recognition rather than authorizing contempt, hostility, or exclusion.

Ethnic difference and honor

The verse continues:

إِنَّ أَكْرَمَكُمْ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ أَتْقَاكُمْ

inna akramakum ʿinda allāhi atqākum

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:13

“The most honored of you before God is the most mindful of Him.”

This states the criterion of honor before God.

Membership in no particular people or tribe is named as the basis of superiority.

The criterion is taqwā.

Ethnic, tribal, family, or communal membership is therefore not itself proof of superior standing before God.

A prominent family or a long historical lineage does not automatically make someone more mindful of God than another person.

A large or powerful group likewise does not confer moral superiority on each member.

Someone from a small, unfamiliar, or disadvantaged group may uphold truth, justice, and responsibility.

The verse grounds honor in taqwā rather than inherited affiliation.

It ends:

إِنَّ اللَّهَ عَلِيمٌ خَبِيرٌ

inna allāha ʿalīmun khabīr

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:13

“God is Knowing, Aware.”

This ending matters: social titles, dress, claims, and ancestry do not by themselves establish taqwā.

People can assess visible conduct, while complete knowledge of inward states and final standing before God belongs to Him.

Claims to piety should therefore not become another instrument of contempt or self-exaltation.

The surrounding context in Al-Ḥujurāt

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:11 prohibits one group ridiculing another:

لَا يَسْخَرْ قَوْمٌ مِنْ قَوْمٍ عَسَىٰ أَنْ يَكُونُوا خَيْرًا مِنْهُمْ

lā yaskhar qawmun min qawmin ʿasā an yakūnū khayran minhum

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:11

“Let no people ridicule another people; they may be better than them.”

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:12 commands avoiding much suspicion and prohibits spying and backbiting.

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:13 then broadens the address from believers to all people:

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّاسُ

yā ayyuhā al-nās

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:13

“O people.”

This change of address is significant.

It concerns all humanity, rather than one tribe or religious community alone.

In this context peoples and tribes are discussed alongside rejection of contempt and superiority claims.

Collective differences do not authorize ridicule, disrespect, or moral judgments based solely on ancestry.

Shuʿūb names human groups; the verse’s full message comes from its relationship with li-taʿārafū and atqākum.

The root itself does not mean moral equality or taqwā. Those principles arise from the surrounding clauses and context.

4. Al-Mursalāt 77:30: a three-branched shadow

The second common-noun occurrence is in Al-Mursalāt.

Al-Mursalāt 77:29 says:

انْطَلِقُوا إِلَىٰ مَا كُنْتُمْ بِهِ تُكَذِّبُونَ

inṭaliqū ilā mā kuntum bihi tukadhdhibūn

Al-Mursalāt 77:29

“Proceed to what you used to deny.”

Al-Mursalāt 77:30 continues:

انْطَلِقُوا إِلَىٰ ظِلٍّ ذِي ثَلَاثِ شُعَبٍ

inṭaliqū ilā ẓillin dhī thalāthi shuʿab

Al-Mursalāt 77:30

“Proceed to a shadow with three branches.”

Al-Mursalāt 77:31 says:

لَا ظَلِيلٍ وَلَا يُغْنِي مِنَ اللَّهَبِ

lā ẓalīlin wa-lā yughnī mina al-lahab

Al-Mursalāt 77:31

“Neither offering shade nor protecting against the flame.”

Al-Mursalāt 77:32 then describes great sparks.

Shuʿab: branching parts of a phenomenon

Shuʿab is the plural of shuʿbah.

Shuʿbah denotes a branch or offshoot of a whole.

Here it describes three branches of a shadow.

It does not mean human groups.

The same root that names peoples in Al-Ḥujurāt describes branches here.

The object of the word determines its particular sense.

People and tribes call for the social sense.

A shadow with three branches calls for branches or projecting divisions.

Is this a shadow of smoke?

Early explanations identify it as the shadow of smoke from fire, rising and dividing into three branches.

This accords with the nearby mention of flame and large sparks.

Al-Mursalāt 77:30 itself, however, does not use dukhān, smoke.

It names a shadow with three branches; Al-Mursalāt 77:31 denies protection from flame.

Smoke is an intelligible contextual explanation, not the lexical definition of shuʿab.

The word means branches. Their material and physical character require contextual evidence.

Why three branches?

Thalāth means three.

It specifies the number of branches.

SH ʿ B itself contains no particular number.

A tree or route can divide into two, three, or more branches.

The number comes from thalāth, not from the root.

Without evidence, these should not be equated with three classes of people, religious groups, stages of punishment, or sins.

Questions prompted by the image do not justify adding unsupported details.

The explicit description is a shadow with three branches.

A shadow that provides no refuge

Ordinary shade offers shelter from light and heat.

Al-Mursalāt 77:31 denies such protection here.

The word ẓill in Al-Mursalāt 77:30 cannot alone establish a safe, cool place.

Al-Mursalāt 77:31 rules out that expectation.

By contrast, Al-Mursalāt 77:41 says:

إِنَّ الْمُتَّقِينَ فِي ظِلَالٍ وَعُيُونٍ

inna al-muttaqīna fī ẓilālin wa-ʿuyūn

Al-Mursalāt 77:41

“The mindful are among shades and springs.”

The contrast within the surah matters.

One passage denies shelter and comfort; the other places shade alongside springs and blessings.

Shade does not have identical implications in every verse.

The same principle applies to shuʿab.

Branching is neither intrinsically good nor bad.

In Al-Mursalāt it belongs to punishment; in Al-Ḥujurāt human grouping relates to mutual recognition.

The positive or negative implications come from context, not the root alone.

5. Separation and connection in SH ʿ B

The two common-noun occurrences initially seem very different.

Shuʿūb in Al-Ḥujurāt names peoples.

Shuʿab in Al-Mursalāt names branches.

A semantic relationship can nevertheless be recognized.

When a whole divides, its parts become distinguishable.

They may still belong to a common source.

Tree branches are distinct while connected to one trunk.

Human groups can have different identities while sharing humanity and historical or social connections.

This image helps explain the relationship between branching and connection.

It does not prove that every people split from a single earlier group or retained identical ties in every period.

Repair, meanwhile, joins parts that were broken apart.

Three situations can thus be distinguished:

A whole dividing into branches.

Distinct branches or groups sharing an origin or bond.

Separated parts being joined again.

Their imagery overlaps without proving one historically undifferentiated meaning.

Al-Ḥujurāt concerns grouping and collective identity; Al-Mursalāt concerns branching.

Repairing a broken vessel is recorded Arabic usage but does not occur independently in these Qur’anic common-noun forms.

6. Comparison with related roots

SH ʿ B and Q B L: peoples and tribes

Shuʿūb and qabāʾil stand together in Al-Ḥujurāt 49:13.

Shaʿb often denotes the larger human grouping.

A tribe is recognized through ancestry or particular social ties.

Some early classifications place several tribes within one people.

A fixed hierarchy should not govern every occurrence of either term.

Their naming boundaries vary, and some sources treat them as close in meaning.

“Peoples and tribes” is therefore preferable to inserting a detailed genealogical chart into the translation.

SH ʿ B and ʾ M M: peoples and communities

Ummah, from ʾ M M, can denote humans or other beings sharing a direction, condition, or connection.

The social shaʿb more specifically names a people or large human grouping.

Several ethnic groups may share one ummah through religion, purpose, or social responsibility.

Conversely, members of one people need not share the same beliefs.

The concepts are not identical.

Every people is not necessarily a separate religious community, nor every community a single people.

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:13 addresses humanity without assigning each people a separate religion or deity.

SH ʿ B and F R ʿ: branches

Farʿ denotes a branch or derivative part of an original whole.

Shuʿbah likewise can denote a branch.

The terms overlap botanically.

SH ʿ B also encompasses mountain clefts, peoples, and repairs.

Its full family cannot be reduced to tree branches.

The overlap also does not establish identical semantic histories.

SH ʿ B and F R Q: separation

Farq concerns separating or distinguishing things.

One branch of SH ʿ B also concerns separation and dispersal.

Branching and offshoots are especially salient in SH ʿ B.

Dividing a group into two illustrates separation.

Describing many branches within a whole particularly suits the imagery of SH ʿ B.

Farq is not invariably the act and shaʿb its result: both families have verbal and nominal uses.

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:13 does not condemn human grouping itself, whereas passages about dividing religion condemn particular conduct.

Difference and division are not automatically blameworthy discord.

SH ʿ B and J M ʿ: gathering

Jamʿ means bringing parts together.

SH ʿ B records both separation and joining.

Repair overlaps with gathering as separated sections are reunited.

Branching, conversely, highlights differentiation.

Overlap in one branch does not make the entire family mean gathering.

Each sentence must establish whether branching, dispersal, or repair is intended.

SH ʿ B and SH T T: dispersal

SH T T concerns scattering, separation, and diversity.

Ashtātan in Al-Zalzalah 99:6 describes people emerging separately.

SH ʿ B can additionally emphasize branches of a whole or connected human groups.

A tree’s branches remain attached to one trunk despite being distinct.

Shatāt may emphasize parts located in different places or directions.

This is a useful tendency, not an absolute boundary.

Both overlap in dispersal, but branching and grouping are especially prominent in SH ʿ B.

SH ʿ B, SH ʿ R, and SH ʿ L

These are independent roots.

SH ʿ R concerns awareness and perception, as well as hair.

SH ʿ L appears in blazing and ignition.

SH ʿ B concerns branching, grouping, separation, and joining.

Two shared initial radicals do not prove one meaning or history.

In Al-Mursalāt 77:30, shuʿab means branches, not shuʿal, flames.

The fiery context does not change the word’s lexical meaning into flame.

7. Morphology and suggested Forqan renderings

Shuʿūban in Al-Ḥujurāt 49:13

The form appears in:

وَجَعَلْنَاكُمْ شُعُوبًا وَقَبَائِلَ

wa-jaʿalnākum shuʿūban wa-qabāʾil

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:13

“We made you peoples and tribes.”

Shuʿūb is the broken plural of shaʿb.

It is indefinite and accusative here.

Jaʿalnā is a perfect verb; kum is its first object, shuʿūban its second, with qabāʾila coordinated to it.

The construction describes people being constituted as peoples and tribes.

Shuʿūb is a noun, not an act of breaking a vessel or a tree branch.

Its social sense comes from shaʿb and the context of human creation and grouping.

“Large human communities” is explicit; “peoples” is more natural while less explicit about scale.

“Nations” is possible if readers are told that modern states or political nationalities are not necessarily intended.

Shuʿabin in Al-Mursalāt 77:30

The form appears in:

إِلَىٰ ظِلٍّ ذِي ثَلَاثِ شُعَبٍ

ilā ẓillin dhī thalāthi shuʿab

Al-Mursalāt 77:30

“Toward a shadow with three branches.”

Shuʿab is the plural of shuʿbah.

It is genitive as the dependent term of thalāthi.

Dhī qualifies ẓillin and indicates possession of three branches.

Shuʿab therefore denotes branching divisions or extensions.

It does not by itself specify their substance, direction, or geometry.

“Three branches” is natural; “three branching divisions” is explanatory but heavier.

“Three columns of smoke” may fit an interpretation but adds smoke, which the verse does not explicitly name.

Suggested equivalents for the family

شَعَبَ: shaʿaba; separated or branched; alternatively, gathered and joined.

شَعْب: shaʿb; a people or large human group; also a cleft or separation.

شُعُوب: shuʿūb; peoples or large human communities; nations in an appropriate sense.

شِعْب: shiʿb; a mountain pass, cleft, or narrow valley.

شِعَاب: shiʿāb; mountain passes or clefts.

شُعْبَة: shuʿbah; branch or subdivision.

شُعَب: shuʿab; branches or subdivisions.

شَعَّبَ: shaʿʿaba; caused to branch or divided into parts.

تَشَعَّبَ: tashaʿʿaba; branched or subdivided.

اِنْشَعَبَ: inshaʿaba; separated, branched off, or took another route.

شَعَّاب: shaʿʿāb; a repairer of broken vessels or a joiner of broken parts.

شِعَابَة: shiʿābah; repair and joining of broken sections.

مَشْعُوب: mashʿūb; split or branched; alternatively, repaired.

These equivalents are not interchangeable without context.

“A large human group” fits Al-Ḥujurāt 49:13 but changes the meaning of Al-Mursalāt 77:30.

Conversely, branches fits Al-Mursalāt, but translating shuʿūb in Al-Ḥujurāt only as branches could obscure its social meaning.

Translation should convey the particular derivative’s meaning rather than impose one artificial equivalent on every form. These proposals do not replace installed verse translations.

8. Conclusion

SH ʿ B records both separation and branching, and gathering and joining separated parts.

Poetic witnesses illustrate mountain passes and tree branches; lexical accounts also record peoples, clefts, separation, and repair.

A single original image cannot be established for all these usages. Contronymy and differing dialectal usage are two explanations offered for the opposing senses.

The two common-noun Qur’anic occurrences are counted separately from the proper name Shuʿayb.

Shuʿūb in Al-Ḥujurāt 49:13 names peoples alongside tribes. Mutual recognition is their stated purpose, and taqwā rather than ethnic or inherited affiliation determines honor before God.

Shuʿab in Al-Mursalāt 77:30 describes three branches of a shadow in punishment; Al-Mursalāt 77:31 denies protection from flame.

Neither branching nor differentiation has a fixed moral value in itself.

Human grouping can serve mutual recognition, while branching can also appear in a frightening scene.

Evaluation comes from context and consequences, not the root alone.

The two defensible branches are division into distinct parts or branches, and joining or gathering separated parts.

Socially, shaʿb names a people that may encompass smaller groups. Physically, shuʿbah is a branch or offshoot.

“Peoples” or “large human communities” suits Al-Ḥujurāt 49:13; “branches” suits Al-Mursalāt 77:30.

A further distinction emerges: different identities do not in themselves imply hostility or superiority.

Al-Ḥujurāt situates human differences within mutual recognition. Peoples and tribes should not become a basis for inherent privilege, contempt, or exclusive claims to divine honor.

People can differ in ancestry, language, territory, and social ties without those affiliations determining their standing before God.

This conclusion comes from the relationship of shuʿūb with li-taʿārafū and atqākum, not from the root itself meaning equality, taqwā, or virtue.
