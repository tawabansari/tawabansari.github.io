Qur’anic Root Q M Ṣ (ق م ص)

A shirt and putting it on; a separate branch concerning leaping, bucking, and sudden movement

Qur’anic forms

قَمِيص

Qamīṣ

1. Basic meaning and linguistic background

The Arabic family ق م ص (Q M Ṣ) has two distinct semantic branches. One concerns a shirt, wearing a garment, and figuratively assuming a role or responsibility. The other concerns leaping, bucking, and sudden movement.

In poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period, قَمِيص (qamīṣ) names a familiar garment. Lexical definitions generally describe a sewn garment with sleeves that a person wears; its length and shape may vary.

Ibn Fāris distinguishes wearing and entering into something as one branch, and leaping and movement as another. The historical evidence is insufficient to reduce both to a single shared semantic origin.

Qur’anic form examined: قَمِيص (qamīṣ), a shirt. This noun occurs six times in six verses: Yūsuf 12:18, Yūsuf 12:25, Yūsuf 12:26, Yūsuf 12:27, Yūsuf 12:28, and Yūsuf 12:93. Yūsuf 12:84, Yūsuf 12:94, and Yūsuf 12:96, quoted below, explain the narrative context; they do not themselves contain qamīṣ.

Other Arabic forms in this family include قُمْصَان (qumṣān) and أَقْمِصَة (aqmiṣah), shirts; تَقْمِيص (taqmīṣ), dressing someone in a shirt; تَقَمُّص (taqammuṣ), wearing a shirt or assuming a role or office; and قُمَاص (qumāṣ), leaping and bucking. These forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

2. Evidence from early Arabic usage

Qamīṣ as clothing

Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd, a pre-Islamic poet, describes the marks of leather straps on his she-camel’s body in his Muʿallaqah:

كَأَنَّ عُلُوبَ النَّسْعِ فِي دَأْيَاتِهَا مَوَارِدُ مِنْ خَلْقَاءَ فِي ظَهْرِ قَرْدَدِ

تَلَاقَى وَأَحْيَانًا تَبِينُ كَأَنَّهَا بَنَائِقُ غُرٌّ فِي قَمِيصٍ مُقَدَّدِ

“As though the marks of the leather straps on her back were watercourses across smooth rock on hard, elevated ground; sometimes they meet and sometimes they separate, like white pieces of cloth in a torn shirt.”

The poet compares the marks of the leather straps on his she-camel’s body to lines and pieces of cloth in a shirt.

This verse attributed to Ṭarafah supports the use of qamīṣ for clothing in pre-Qur’anic Arabic, although the surviving text reached us through later transmission and compilation. قَمِيصٍ مُقَدَّدِ (qamīṣin muqaddadi), a torn shirt, is lexically close to the tearing of Joseph’s shirt: qamīṣ names the garment and ق د د (Q D D) expresses its splitting.

The historical origin of qamīṣ

Similar forms have been reported in other languages, and borrowing has been proposed; the source language and transmission route remain uncertain. Derivatives such as taqammuṣ and taqmīṣ do not establish that the noun originated in Arabic, since borrowed nouns can also generate verbs. The verse attributed to Ṭarafah supports early usage, not a definitive historical etymology.

Leaping and bucking

In the other branch, قَمَصَ (qamaṣa) describes a camel or horse leaping or moving suddenly; descriptions of the camel mention raising its two forelegs and bringing them down together. Such movement may dislodge a rider, but the rider’s falling is not a necessary part of the verb’s meaning.

Arabic also records قَمَصَ الْبَحْرُ بِالسَّفِينَةِ (qamaṣa l-baḥru bi-s-safīnah), describing a ship being tossed by the sea’s waves.

Here the ship’s movement is compared to an animal suddenly leaping and shaking its body.

This branch is known in classical Arabic lexicons but is absent from the Qur’anic uses of the root.

3. Qamīṣ in the Qur’an: Joseph’s shirt

All Qur’anic occurrences of qamīṣ are in Sūrat Yūsuf. The shirt has a role at three stages in Joseph’s life: first as an instrument of deception, then as evidence revealing the truth, and finally as something accompanying glad news and the restoration of his father’s sight.

The lexical meaning of qamīṣ is the same at all three stages. What changes is the shirt’s role in the events of the narrative.

The bloodstained shirt: an instrument of deception

After separating Joseph from his father, his brothers brought his shirt to their father with false blood on it, seeking to make their claim believable:

وَجاءو عَلىٰ قَميصِهِ بِدَمٍ كَذِبٍ قالَ بَل سَوَّلَت لَكُم أَنفُسُكُم أَمرًا فَصَبرٌ جَميلٌ وَاللَّهُ المُستَعانُ عَلىٰ ما تَصِفونَ

Wa-jāʾū ʿalā qamīṣihi bi-damin kadhib. Qāla bal sawwalat lakum anfusukum amran fa-ṣabrun jamīl. Wa-llāhu l-mustaʿānu ʿalā mā taṣifūn.

“They brought false blood upon his shirt. He said: Rather, your souls have made a matter seem attractive to you. So, beautiful patience; and The God is the One whose help is sought against what you describe.”

Yusuf 12:18

قَمِيصِهِ (qamīṣihi) consists of the noun qamīṣ and the attached pronoun هِ (hi), referring to Joseph.

By presenting the bloodstained shirt, Joseph’s brothers sought to make their claim that a wolf had eaten him believable.

The shirt’s belonging to Joseph gave their claim an appearance of credibility; yet the object’s being real did not establish the truth of the story told with it.

دَمٍ كَذِبٍ (damin kadhibin), false blood, refers to blood used to convey a false claim. The Qur’an does not identify its source, and this verse does not say whether the shirt was torn or intact.

Jacob did not accept their claim. The event shows that a real object can be used to construct a false account of an incident.

The torn shirt: evidence for discerning the truth

At another stage in Joseph’s life, when the wife of the Egyptian dignitary sought sexual intimacy with him, Joseph moved toward the door:

وَاستَبَقَا البابَ وَقَدَّت قَميصَهُ مِن دُبُرٍ وَأَلفَيا سَيِّدَها لَدَا البابِ قالَت ما جَزاءُ مَن أَرادَ بِأَهلِكَ سوءًا إِلّا أَن يُسجَنَ أَو عَذابٌ أَليمٌ

Wa-stabaqā l-bāba wa-qaddat qamīṣahu min duburin wa-alfayā sayyidahā ladā l-bāb. Qālat mā jazāʾu man arāda bi-ahlika sūʾan illā an yusjana aw ʿadhābun alīm.

“They raced to the door, and she tore his shirt from behind. They found her master at the door. She said: What should be the recompense of someone who intended evil against your household, except imprisonment or a painful punishment?”

Yusuf 12:25

Here قَمِيصَهُ (qamīṣahu) is the object of قَدَّتْ (qaddat), she tore. The verb belongs to ق د د (Q D D), denoting cutting or splitting something, especially along its length.

مِنْ دُبُرٍ (min duburin) identifies where the shirt was torn: from behind.

The woman tried to place responsibility for what had happened on Joseph. He replied that she had sought intimacy with him. A witness from her family then proposed a way to assess the conflicting claims:

قالَ هِىَ رٰوَدَتنى عَن نَفسى وَشَهِدَ شاهِدٌ مِن أَهلِها إِن كانَ قَميصُهُ قُدَّ مِن قُبُلٍ فَصَدَقَت وَهُوَ مِنَ الكٰذِبينَ

Qāla hiya rāwadatnī ʿan nafsī. Wa-shahida shāhidun min ahlihā in kāna qamīṣuhu qudda min qubulin fa-ṣadaqat wa-huwa mina l-kādhibīn.

“He said: She sought to seduce me. A witness from her family testified: If his shirt was torn from the front, she has spoken the truth and he is among the liars.”

Yusuf 12:26

وَإِن كانَ قَميصُهُ قُدَّ مِن دُبُرٍ فَكَذَبَت وَهُوَ مِنَ الصّٰدِقينَ

Wa-in kāna qamīṣuhu qudda min duburin fa-kadhabat wa-huwa mina ṣ-ṣādiqīn.

“But if his shirt was torn from behind, she has lied and he is among the truthful.”

Yusuf 12:27

In these two verses, the shirt itself is examined. The distinction between مِنْ قُبُلٍ (min qubulin), from the front, and مِنْ دُبُرٍ (min duburin), from behind, forms the basis of the witness’s reasoning.

A tear from behind was consistent with Joseph’s moving away and the woman grasping the back of his shirt to stop him.

فَلَمّا رَءا قَميصَهُ قُدَّ مِن دُبُرٍ قالَ إِنَّهُ مِن كَيدِكُنَّ إِنَّ كَيدَكُنَّ عَظيمٌ

Fa-lammā raʾā qamīṣahu qudda min duburin qāla innahu min kaydikunna inna kaydakunna ʿaẓīm.

“When he saw that his shirt was torn from behind, he said: This is of your scheming; indeed, your scheming is great.”

Yusuf 12:28

In the first incident, Joseph’s brothers used the shirt to conceal the truth. In this incident, its condition helped reveal the truth.

In both cases, the shirt is a material object. In the first, it was deliberately prepared to suggest a false claim; in the second, it bore traces of the incident.

This part of Joseph’s story illustrates the importance of examining material evidence when judging conflicting claims. The direction of a tear is not, by itself, a universal rule for identifying truth and falsehood in every similar incident. The reasoning derives its validity from the relationship between the shirt’s condition and the particular event under consideration.

Joseph’s shirt and the restoration of Jacob’s sight

Years after Joseph’s separation from his family, Jacob’s grief continued:

وَتَوَلّىٰ عَنهُم وَقالَ يٰأَسَفىٰ عَلىٰ يوسُفَ وَابيَضَّت عَيناهُ مِنَ الحُزنِ فَهُوَ كَظيمٌ

Wa-tawallā ʿanhum wa-qāla yā asafā ʿalā Yūsufa wa-byaḍḍat ʿaynāhu mina l-ḥuzni fa-huwa kaẓīm.

“He turned away from them and said: Alas for Joseph! His eyes became white from grief, while he held his sorrow within.”

Yusuf 12:84

After the brothers recognized Joseph and acknowledged their wrongdoing, he told them:

اذهَبوا بِقَميصى هٰذا فَأَلقوهُ عَلىٰ وَجهِ أَبى يَأتِ بَصيرًا وَأتونى بِأَهلِكُم أَجمَعينَ

Idhhabū bi-qamīṣī hādhā fa-alqūhu ʿalā wajhi abī yaʾti baṣīran waʾtūnī bi-ahlikum ajmaʿīn.

“Take this shirt of mine and cast it upon my father’s face; he will regain his sight. And bring all your family to me.”

Yusuf 12:93

بِقَمِيصِي (bi-qamīṣī) consists of the preposition bi, the noun qamīṣ, and the attached first-person singular pronoun. Earlier, Joseph’s shirt was mentioned by others or in the narrative account; here Joseph speaks of his own shirt.

The demonstrative هَٰذَا (hādhā), this, identifies the particular shirt Joseph has in view.

Before the shirt arrived, Jacob spoke of sensing Joseph’s scent:

وَلَمّا فَصَلَتِ العيرُ قالَ أَبوهُم إِنّى لَأَجِدُ ريحَ يوسُفَ لَولا أَن تُفَنِّدونِ

Wa-lammā faṣalati l-ʿīru qāla abūhum innī la-ajidu rīḥa Yūsufa lawlā an tufannidūn.

“When the caravan departed, their father said: Indeed, I sense Joseph’s scent, if you do not consider me feeble-minded.”

Yusuf 12:94

What Joseph had said then came to pass:

فَلَمّا أَن جاءَ البَشيرُ أَلقىٰهُ عَلىٰ وَجهِهِ فَارتَدَّ بَصيرًا قالَ أَلَم أَقُل لَكُم إِنّى أَعلَمُ مِنَ اللَّهِ ما لا تَعلَمونَ

Fa-lammā an jāʾa l-bashīru alqāhu ʿalā wajhihi fa-rtadda baṣīrā. Qāla a-lam aqul lakum innī aʿlamu mina llāhi mā lā taʿlamūn.

“When the bearer of glad news arrived, he cast it upon his face, and he regained his sight. He said: Did I not tell you that I know from The God what you do not know?”

Yusuf 12:96

The Qur’an connects the whitening of Jacob’s eyes with his grief and describes his sight returning after the shirt was cast upon his face.

The verses do not specify the nature of his eye condition, the mechanism by which his sight returned, or the shirt’s material properties. One cannot therefore derive a particular therapeutic property from qamīṣ itself or turn this event into a general prescription for treating eye disease with people’s clothing.

Nor does the Qur’an specify whether the shirts mentioned at the three stages were the same physical shirt or different shirts. What matters for understanding the narrative is the relationship between these events, not proving the objects identical.

4. Taqammuṣ and semantic extension

In its direct Arabic sense, تَقَمَّصَ (taqammaṣa) means to put on a shirt.

Figuratively, the verb can describe assuming an office, responsibility, or role; for example, تَقَمَّصَ الْإِمَارَةَ (taqammaṣa l-imārah), assuming rulership.

As a person puts on a garment, so a person may assume a social role or responsibility and appear in that capacity.

In modern Arabic, taqammuṣ can also mean taking on another person’s role, as an actor portrays a character.

In some religious and philosophical systems, taqammuṣ has become a term for the soul’s passage from one body to another. The body is then compared to a garment that the soul wears and later exchanges for another.

This technical meaning does not follow from the Qur’anic use of qamīṣ. The Qur’an does not use taqammuṣ, and none of its occurrences of qamīṣ presents the shirt as another name for the human body.

The theory of a soul’s passage between bodies therefore requires an independent study; the lexical meaning of qamīṣ or its uses in Joseph’s story cannot establish it.

5. Comparison with related roots

Q M Ṣ (qamīṣ) and L B S (libās): shirt and covering

Libās has a broader semantic range: what covers, protects, or adorns a person.

Alongside material clothing, the Qur’an uses libās in expressions concerning the garment of taqwā, spouses as garments, and the night as a garment.

Qamīṣ names a particular kind of clothing. Every shirt is clothing, but not every garment is a shirt.

In Joseph’s story, qamīṣ preserves the image of a specific object that can be worn, torn, stained with blood, or sent to someone else.

Q M Ṣ (qamīṣ) and TH W B (thawb): shirt and garment

Thawb denotes a garment in Arabic. Its Qur’anic plural ثِيَاب (thiyāb) appears in various contexts.

Thiyāb can encompass different kinds of garments, whereas qamīṣ refers to a more specific kind of clothing.

Al-Nūr 24:58 mentions setting aside garments at midday, and Al-Muddaththir 74:4 commands the purification of garments.

Rendering thiyāb as “shirts” in these contexts would narrow its range without justification, just as rendering qamīṣ simply as “clothing” would make its specificity less apparent.

Q M Ṣ (qamīṣ) and S R B L (sirbāl): shirt and body garment

Sirbāl denotes a garment covering the body and overlaps with qamīṣ in some Arabic usages.

Al-Naḥl 16:81 speaks of garments protecting people against heat and harm in battle. Ibrāhīm 14:50 describes the offenders’ garments as made of pitch.

The protective or punitive function of these garments follows from their material, description, and verse context, not from sirbāl itself.

Qamīṣ and sirbāl can refer to similar clothing in Arabic. In the Qur’anic use of qamīṣ, however, the shirt’s belonging to Joseph and its role in the narrative are prominent.

6. Translation recommendations for Forqan

“Shirt” is recommended for every Qur’anic occurrence of قَمِيص (qamīṣ).

“Garment” is defensible but broader. Expressions such as “sacred clothing,” “healing garment,” or “spiritual covering” introduce qualities not contained in the independent meaning of qamīṣ.

قَمِيصِهِ (qamīṣihi) and قَمِيصَهُ (qamīṣahu) mean “his shirt”; بِقَمِيصِي هَٰذَا (bi-qamīṣī hādhā) means “with this shirt of mine.” The full verse translation should follow the natural syntax of the receiving language.

In non-Qur’anic usage, appropriate equivalents include putting on a shirt, assuming a role or responsibility, leaping, bucking, and being tossed, according to the relevant semantic branch.

7. Conclusion

The Arabic family Q M Ṣ has two branches: a shirt and wearing a garment; and leaping and sudden movement. A shared historical origin has not been conclusively established.

The Qur’an uses only the noun qamīṣ from this family. Joseph’s shirt serves the presentation of a lie early in the story, helps reveal the truth in its middle, and accompanies the glad news that Joseph is alive and the restoration of Jacob’s sight near its end.

Despite these different narrative roles, the lexical meaning of qamīṣ remains constant throughout its Qur’anic occurrences.

Proposed definition for Forqan: A shirt; putting on a garment and, figuratively, assuming a role or responsibility; in a separate branch, leaping, bucking, and sudden movement. The direct meaning of qamīṣ in the Qur’an is “shirt.”
