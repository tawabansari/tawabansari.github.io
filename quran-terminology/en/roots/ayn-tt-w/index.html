Core meaning: reaching out to take or obtain something, or giving something and placing it at another's disposal. Quranic uses include taking action, giving, and the gift or grant itself.

1. The root and its meaning in early Arabic

The Arabic root ʿ ṭ w is weak-final: its third consonant is wāw, which changes to alif or yāʾ in some derivatives.

It concerns reaching out, obtaining, taking, giving, and conveying something to another. Direction matters: someone may reach toward an object to take it, or extend something toward another to give it.

Early Arabic ʿaṭā yaʿṭū means reaching out and taking or picking something up. ʿAṭw names the act of reaching and taking.

Reaching out to pick up a vessel, for example, can be expressed with ʿaṭā. The same family can describe an animal raising its head and forelimbs to reach high foliage.

The Form IV verb aʿṭā means giving or placing something at another's disposal. ʿAṭāʾ denotes giving, a gift, or what is given.

Taking and reaching are prominent in ʿaṭā; giving and transferring something to another in aʿṭā.

These actions can belong to one interaction: when one person gives something to another, one is the giver and the other the recipient.

Not every use, however, describes transferring a material object between two people. What is given may be wealth, ability, creation, opportunity, or another blessing. A person may also undertake an action without any gift being mentioned.

The family's central semantic field can therefore be described as follows:

Reaching for something to take it, or giving and placing something at another's disposal, extending from material transfer to bestowing blessings, capacities, and opportunities.

Taking and giving are related directions, but derivatives must not all be translated alike in both directions.

2. Early poetic evidence and its historical setting

An important example occurs in the Muʿallaqah of the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays:

وَتَعْطُو بِرَخْصٍ غَيْرِ شَثْنٍ كَأَنَّهُ
أَسَارِيعُ ظَبْيٍ أَوْ مَسَاوِيكُ إِسْحِلِ

wa-taʿṭū bi-rakhṣin ghayri shathnin ka-annahu / asārīʿu ẓabyin aw masāwīku isḥil

“She reaches with soft, delicate fingers, neither thick nor coarse, like slender worms or tooth-cleaning twigs of the isḥil tree.”

Taʿṭū here means reaching out to take something.

The poet describes a woman's soft, delicate fingers, comparing their shape and suppleness with familiar slender objects.

Here taʿṭū does not mean bestowing a gift. She reaches out to take or pick something up.

The example illustrates a physical use of ʿaṭā yaʿṭū before this study turns to giving in other derivatives.

The verse belongs to the celebrated Muʿallaqah and is prominent evidence of poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period. Like other transmitted poems, its attribution and wording reach us through later transmission; a poet's early date does not independently establish the date of every word.

Another example appears in early lexical works:

تَحُكُّ بِقَرْنَيْهَا بَرِيرَ أَرَاكَةٍ
وَتَعْطُو بِظِلْفَيْهَا إِذَا الْغُصْنُ طَالَهَا

taḥukku bi-qarnayhā barīra arākatin / wa-taʿṭū bi-ẓilfayhā idhā al-ghuṣnu ṭālahā

“She rubs the fruit of an arāk tree with her two horns, and reaches with her two forehooves when the branch is above her.”

Taʿṭū describes an animal lifting its forelimbs to reach foliage or a branch.

Here too the sense is reaching and taking, not giving.

The poet has not been securely identified in this study. The verse illustrates recorded classical usage, but should not be presented as conclusively pre-Islamic without independent evidence.

What the historical examples establish

One clear early use of this root is reaching out to take something.

A person or animal approaches the object or extends a limb toward it.

This image must not be imposed on every derivative.

When the Quran speaks of The God's giving, for example, the root does not entail a human-like bodily hand transferring an object between places.

The physical image helps explain semantic relationships; it is not a bodily description of divine action.

3. Lexical and Quranic forms

Important Arabic forms include ʿaṭā, yaʿṭū, ʿaṭw, aʿṭā, yuʿṭī, iʿṭāʾ, ʿaṭāʾ, ʿāṭā, muʿāṭāh, taʿāṭā, taʿāṭin, ʿāṭin, and miʿṭāʾ.

They share a root without having identical meanings.

ʿAṭā yaʿṭū: reaching out, taking something, or seeking to reach it.

ʿAṭw: the act of reaching and taking.

Aʿṭā yuʿṭī: giving something to another or placing it at their disposal.

Iʿṭāʾ: the act of giving.

ʿAṭāʾ: giving, a gift, or what has been given.

ʿĀṭā: handing something to another, sometimes in an interaction involving both giving and taking.

Muʿāṭāh: handing over, exchanging, or conveying something between people.

Taʿāṭā: reaching for something, taking it, or undertaking an action.

Miʿṭāʾ: someone who gives generously or frequently.

ʿĀṭin: a person or animal extending a hand or forelimbs to reach something.

Aʿṭā al-baʿīr is also recorded for a camel becoming tractable and offering no resistance. Lexical explanation relates this to the animal yielding its head rather than resisting its handler.

That use must not be generalized to every occurrence of aʿṭā. Quranic meanings depend on subject, object, and context.

Quranic forms

The root occurs fourteen times in twelve Quranic verses, in three morphological branches:

First, Form IV aʿṭā, including aʿṭā, aʿṭaynāka, yuʿṭīka, yuʿṭū, uʿṭū, and yuʿṭaw.

Second, the noun ʿaṭāʾ, occurring as ʿaṭāʾan, ʿaṭāʾi, ʿaṭāʾu, and ʿaṭāʾunā.

Third, taʿāṭā in the form fa-taʿāṭā.

Although related lexically, these branches differ in structure and meaning.

Aʿṭā foregrounds giving.

ʿAṭāʾ concerns the act of giving or what is given.

Taʿāṭā concerns reaching, taking, or undertaking an action.

The basic verb ʿaṭā yaʿṭū does not occur in the Quran, although it matters for the family's lexical background.

4. Aʿṭā: giving and placing at another's disposal

Quranic aʿṭā occurs in several settings.

Sometimes The God gives something to a creature or servant.

Sometimes a person gives or pays something to another.

Sometimes the passive foregrounds receiving or not receiving something.

This range shows that aʿṭā is not lexically restricted to voluntary generosity or good conduct.

Giving and paying

Al-Tawbah says:

قَاتِلُوا الَّذِينَ لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ بِاللَّهِ وَلَا بِالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ وَلَا يُحَرِّمُونَ مَا حَرَّمَ اللَّهُ وَرَسُولُهُ وَلَا يَدِينُونَ دِينَ الْحَقِّ مِنَ الَّذِينَ أُوتُوا الْكِتَابَ حَتَّىٰ يُعْطُوا الْجِزْيَةَ عَنْ يَدٍ وَهُمْ صَاغِرُونَ

qātilū alladhīna lā yuʾminūna bi-Allāhi wa-lā bi-al-yawmi al-ākhiri wa-lā yuḥarrimūna mā ḥarrama Allāhu wa-rasūluhu wa-lā yadīnūna dīna al-ḥaqqi mina alladhīna ūtū al-kitāba ḥattā yuʿṭū al-jizyata ʿan yadin wa-hum ṣāghirūn

Al-Tawbah 9:29

Al-Tawbah 9:29

“Fight those among the people given the Book who do not believe in The God or the Last Day, do not hold forbidden what The God and His Messenger have forbidden, and do not follow the religion of truth, until they give the jizyah by their own hand while in a subordinate position.”

Yuʿṭū comes from aʿṭā.

What is given is al-jizyah.

“Pay” or “give” therefore suits the context.

This shows that iʿṭāʾ does not inherently mean giving a gift out of affection or generosity.

Giving here occurs within a specified payment.

The payment's nature, conditions, the parties' situation, and ʿan yadin wa-hum ṣāghirūn come from the verse's other components, not from ʿ ṭ w itself.

Yuʿṭū alone neither establishes voluntary payment nor specifies the exact financial or legal system involved.

Giving and receiving charitable funds

The same surah says:

وَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ يَلْمِزُكَ فِي الصَّدَقَاتِ فَإِنْ أُعْطُوا مِنْهَا رَضُوا وَإِنْ لَمْ يُعْطَوْا مِنْهَا إِذَا هُمْ يَسْخَطُونَ

wa-minhum man yalmizuka fī al-ṣadaqāti fa-in uʿṭū minhā raḍū wa-in lam yuʿṭaw minhā idhā hum yaskhaṭūn

Al-Tawbah 9:58

Al-Tawbah 9:58

“Among them is one who finds fault with you concerning charitable funds. If they are given some, they are pleased; if they are not given any, they suddenly become displeased.”

The verb appears twice in the passive:

Uʿṭū and yuʿṭaw.

The giver is not explicitly named in these constructions; attention falls on those receiving or not receiving something.

Minhā identifies the gift as a portion of the charitable funds under discussion.

Uʿṭū means “they were given,” while their pleasure and displeasure are conveyed separately by raḍū and yaskhaṭūn.

Receiving something does not itself prove the recipient right, deserving, or grateful.

Someone may be pleased to receive money yet behave wrongly when denied it.

The verse concerns people whose response to charitable funds depends on their personal share.

Aʿṭā itself must not be redefined as bribery, buying approval, or unfair distribution. Such judgments require separate contextual evidence.

5. Divine giving: creation and human blessings

The Quran uses aʿṭā several times for giving by The God.

What is given need not be money or a portable object.

Bestowing creation and guidance

Moses answers Pharaoh:

قَالَ رَبُّنَا الَّذِي أَعْطَىٰ كُلَّ شَيْءٍ خَلْقَهُ ثُمَّ هَدَىٰ

qāla rabbunā alladhī aʿṭā kulla shayʾin khalqahu thumma hadā

Ṭā Hā 20:50

Ta Ha 20:50

“He said: Our Lord is the One who gave each thing its creation, then guided.”

Aʿṭā here concerns The God.

Kulla shayʾin identifies the scope of giving, while khalqahu names what each thing receives in this reading.

Aʿṭā alone does not mean creating.

Here giving is connected to khalqahu: The God gave each thing its creation.

Hadā follows, belonging to h d y and meaning guiding.

The sentence therefore distinguishes bestowing creation and guiding.

Divine giving is not confined to external objects. Creation itself and the characteristics with which beings come into existence are presented as bestowed.

Living beings have different structures and capacities: birds, fish, plants, and humans differ in form and way of life.

These differences can help illustrate the range of kulla shayʾin khalqahu.

Aʿṭā itself does not yield a theory of genetic details, biological stages, or scientific mechanisms of creation.

Those are separate matters whose details the verse does not address.

Future giving and satisfaction

Al-Duha says:

وَلَسَوْفَ يُعْطِيكَ رَبُّكَ فَتَرْضَىٰ

wa-la-sawfa yuʿṭīka rabbuka fa-tarḍā

Al-Duha 93:5

Al-Duha 93:5

“And surely your Lord will give to you, and you will be satisfied.”

Yuʿṭīka is an imperfect verb with an attached second-person object pronoun: “He gives to you.”

La-sawfa emphasizes future giving without itself specifying how soon it will occur.

The verse does not explicitly name what will be given.

Fa-tarḍā states the outcome: the addressee's satisfaction.

A direct translation of yuʿṭīka should therefore not insert a particular gift absent from the wording.

The surah's context allows exploration of the promise alongside recalled divine support and blessings, while aʿṭā itself expresses giving.

Satisfaction is not the lexical meaning of ʿ ṭ w; a separate verb from r ḍ w expresses it.

The gift of al-Kawthar

Al-Kawthar says:

إِنَّا أَعْطَيْنَاكَ الْكَوْثَرَ

innā aʿṭaynāka al-kawthar

Al-Kawthar 108:1

Al-Kawthar 108:1

“Indeed, We have given you al-Kawthar.”

Aʿṭaynāka is a Form IV perfect verb.

Nā identifies the subject and ka the recipient.

Al-Kawthar identifies what is given.

Aʿṭaynāka means “We gave you.”

Al-Kawthar must be examined through its own root and usage; its full meaning cannot be derived from the verb of giving.

Nor does the verb establish that the gift must be material, bodily, or measurable by ordinary human methods.

The object of giving can be specified by an independent word whose meaning requires separate study.

6. Human giving: amount and subsequent conduct

Aʿṭā also describes human behavior in the Quran.

Its value depends on amount, intention, direction, and accompanying conduct, beyond the verb itself.

Giving little and stopping

وَأَعْطَىٰ قَلِيلًا وَأَكْدَىٰ

wa-aʿṭā qalīlan wa-akdā

Al-Najm 53:34

Al-Najm 53:34

“And gave a little, then stopped.”

Aʿṭā means giving.

Qalīlan specifies how much was given.

Akdā is a separate verb expressing stopping and withholding further action in this context.

Neither “little” nor “stopping” belongs inside the meaning of aʿṭā itself.

The following Quranic example further clarifies this distinction.

Giving alongside mindfulness

فَأَمَّا مَنْ أَعْطَىٰ وَاتَّقَىٰ

fa-ammā man aʿṭā wa-ittaqā

Al-Layl 92:5

Al-Layl 92:5

“As for the one who gave and was mindful.”

Aʿṭā appears alongside ittaqā.

The former expresses giving, the latter mindfulness and guarding oneself.

Al-Layl 92:6 then mentions affirming al-ḥusnā, and Al-Layl 92:7 describes facilitation toward ease.

The contrasting verse, Al-Layl 92:8, mentions stinginess and considering oneself self-sufficient.

Giving here is positive and stands opposite stinginess.

That value does not come from the root alone: the same verb describes paying jizyah in Al-Tawbah 9:29 and giving little in Al-Najm 53:34.

Giving is an action; its moral value depends on its object, intention, amount, and accompanying conduct.

One person may assist someone in need, another pay a debt, and a third pay someone to secure an improper benefit.

All involve giving, but their legal and moral character differs.

In Al-Layl 92:5, the context pairs giving with mindfulness and places it on a commendable path.

7. ʿAṭāʾ: giving and what is given

Arabic ʿaṭāʾ can denote both the act of giving and what is given.

In the Quran, this noun describes divine giving.

It does not inherently mean unlimited, everlasting, or unconditional giving. Accompanying descriptions specify each gift's qualities.

Giving extended to both groups

Al-Isra says:

كُلًّا نُمِدُّ هَٰؤُلَاءِ وَهَٰؤُلَاءِ مِنْ عَطَاءِ رَبِّكَ ۚ وَمَا كَانَ عَطَاءُ رَبِّكَ مَحْظُورًا

kullan numiddu hāʾulāʾi wa-hāʾulāʾi min ʿaṭāʾi rabbika wa-mā kāna ʿaṭāʾu rabbika maḥẓūrā

Al-Isrāʾ 17:20

Al-Isra 17:20

“We extend to all, these and those, from your Lord's giving; and your Lord's giving is not withheld.”

ʿAṭāʾ occurs twice.

First in min ʿaṭāʾi rabbika, then in ʿaṭāʾu rabbika.

Al-Isra 17:18 concerns those desiring immediate worldly life; Al-Isra 17:19 concerns those seeking the Hereafter and striving for it.

Al-Isra 17:20 then states that both groups receive from their Lord's giving.

Worldly blessings do not alone establish divine approval of everything a person does.

Believers and deniers may both receive food, water, abilities, opportunities, and other resources.

Shared benefits do not make their choices and outcomes identical.

Here ʿaṭāʾ concerns the gifts and resources The God places at people's disposal.

Maḥẓūran independently expresses being withheld or barred.

ʿAṭāʾ therefore does not inherently mean a gift that can never be restricted; the clause wa-mā kāna ʿaṭāʾu rabbika maḥẓūran states that it is not withheld.

The verse does not guarantee unlimited access to anything anyone wants at any time. It extends giving to both groups without promising fulfillment of every wish.

Uninterrupted giving

Hud says:

وَأَمَّا الَّذِينَ سُعِدُوا فَفِي الْجَنَّةِ خَالِدِينَ فِيهَا مَا دَامَتِ السَّمَاوَاتُ وَالْأَرْضُ إِلَّا مَا شَاءَ رَبُّكَ ۖ عَطَاءً غَيْرَ مَجْذُوذٍ

wa-ammā alladhīna suʿidū fa-fī al-jannati khālidīna fīhā mā dāmati al-samāwātu wa-al-arḍu illā mā shāʾa rabbuka ʿaṭāʾan ghayra majdhūdh

Hūd 11:108

Hud 11:108

“As for those made fortunate, they are in the Garden, remaining there as long as the heavens and earth endure, except as your Lord wills: an uninterrupted gift.”

ʿAṭāʾan is qualified by ghayra majdhūdh.

Majdhūdh belongs to j dh dh, concerning cutting and severance.

ʿAṭāʾan ghayra majdhūdh therefore describes a gift that is not cut off.

Uninterrupted continuity comes from ghayra majdhūdh, not from ʿaṭāʾ alone.

The qualification illā mā shāʾa rabbuka must also be retained. A single word cannot determine every detail of the duration and nature of the afterlife while that qualification is omitted.

ʿAṭāʾ names the gift; an independent description expresses its continuity.

Giving and the choice to bestow or retain

In Solomon's story:

هَٰذَا عَطَاؤُنَا فَامْنُنْ أَوْ أَمْسِكْ بِغَيْرِ حِسَابٍ

hādhā ʿaṭāʾunā fa-mnun aw amsik bi-ghayri ḥisāb

Ṣād 38:39

Sad 38:39

“This is Our gift; so bestow or withhold, without account.”

ʿAṭāʾunā denotes divine giving.

Two verbs follow: fa-mnun and amsik.

Mann here can mean bestowing, while amsik means retaining or withholding transfer.

The passage mentions a gift to Solomon and then the possibility of bestowing or retaining it.

Giving and withholding are distinguishable concepts.

Fa-mnun should not automatically be read as blameworthy reminders of favors. The branches of m n n require contextual study.

Bi-ghayri ḥisāb is independent. Explanations include freedom from accounting for the bestowal or withholding, or the abundance of the gift; the phrase should not be reduced to one interpretation without explanation.

ʿAṭāʾunā alone does not mean unlimited authority or giving without account.

Such qualities must be considered through the full construction and Solomon's situation.

Giving alongside recompense

The reward of the mindful is described:

جَزَاءً مِنْ رَبِّكَ عَطَاءً حِسَابًا

jazāʾan min rabbika ʿaṭāʾan ḥisābā

Al-Nabaʾ 78:36

Al-Naba 78:36

“A recompense from your Lord; a sufficient gift.”

Jazāʾan and ʿaṭāʾan occur together.

Jazāʾ concerns recompense and the outcome of conduct; ʿaṭāʾ emphasizes giving.

A divine gift can therefore occur in a context of reward.

This does not establish that ʿaṭāʾ is always unrelated to the recipient's conduct or that recompense can never also be a gift.

Ḥisāban is independent, with different lexical and grammatical explanations concerning the gift's amount or sufficiency.

Some emphasize sufficiency; others, proportion and measure. The selected translation here follows the sufficiency reading without combining two interpretations into one equivalent.

Either quality comes from ḥisāban, not the root ʿ ṭ w.

Understanding divine giving requires attention to its accompanying nouns and descriptions.

8. Fa-taʿāṭā: reaching and undertaking an action

Fa-taʿāṭā in Al-Qamar is an important use of this root.

It is formed from taʿāṭā, Form VI.

Form VI can express mutual participation in some verbs, but does not require it in every use.

Taʿāṭā can mean reaching to take something, undertaking a task, or accepting responsibility for an action.

It occurs in the story of Thamud.

Al-Qamar 54:27 first describes sending the she-camel as a trial, and Al-Qamar 54:28 describes the allocation of water among them.

Then:

فَنَادَوْا صَاحِبَهُمْ فَتَعَاطَىٰ فَعَقَرَ

fa-nādaw ṣāḥibahum fa-taʿāṭā fa-ʿaqar

Al-Qamar 54:29

Al-Qamar 54:29

“They called their companion; he undertook the act and hamstrung her.”

Three verbs appear in sequence:

Fa-nādaw, fa-taʿāṭā, and fa-ʿaqara.

First, a group called their companion.

Then he undertook the act.

Finally, he performed the action expressed by ʿaqara.

The meaning of taʿāṭā here

Taʿāṭā can concern reaching and taking, as when someone reaches for a tool.

No explicit object follows the verb here.

The verse does not name precisely what he took or which implement he held.

“He picked up a sword,” “took a spear,” or “reached toward the camel's leg” would add details absent from the wording. Such reconstructions require independent evidence and belong in explanation.

“He undertook the act” fits the sense of taking action while avoiding an unspecified implement or bodily movement.

Fa-ʿaqara independently expresses hamstringing.

Taʿāṭā alone should not mean “hamstringing the camel,” since the following verb expresses that act.

Is taʿāṭī inherently wrongful?

Some lexicons record taʿāṭā for undertaking something one has no right to do.

This does not mean that Form VI or taʿāṭā always denotes forbidden or wrongful conduct.

Arabic taʿāṭī can concern taking food or drink, or undertaking an activity.

In Al-Qamar 54:29, the wrongfulness of hamstringing is understood from the story's context, not simply from the lexical meaning of either verb.

Thamud faced a divine sign and trial, and the person mentioned took part in hamstringing the she-camel.

Taʿāṭā here is action leading to wrongdoing, but wrongdoing is not a fixed element of the root.

Lexical study must not transfer the moral value of one action to every use of its verb.

9. Comparison with related semantic fields

ʿ ṭ w and ʾ t y

These families overlap in giving and conveying.

Aʿṭā emphasizes giving or transferring something to another.

Ātā belongs to ʾ t y and concerns conveying and giving something to someone.

Quranic ātā occurs with wealth, the Book, wisdom, and zakat.

Aʿṭā directly expresses giving, while ātā belongs to a broader family that also includes coming and reaching. This relationship alone does not establish a rigid semantic boundary between the two verbs.

It does not mean that aʿṭā always denotes voluntary giving and ātā always the discharge of an obligatory right.

Al-Tawbah 9:29 uses aʿṭā for paying jizyah, while the Quran also uses ātā for giving charitable funds and wealth.

The payment's nature and moral value depend on object and context, not the root alone.

ʿ ṭ w and w h b

W h b concerns bestowing and giving.

Hibah ordinarily denotes a gift bestowed on another.

Aʿṭā has a broader range, including obligatory payments and transfers of property.

Someone may give property affectionately as a gift, or pay a debt.

Both involve giving without being legally or morally identical acts.

Aʿṭā should therefore not always be translated “gave as a gift.”

ʿ ṭ w and m n n

In Sad 38:39, ʿaṭāʾunā appears with fa-mnun.

One branch of m n n concerns bestowing and doing good; another concerns reminding someone of one's favors.

The two words' appearance together does not make every sense of the two roots identical.

Someone may give another a gift and then humiliate the recipient by repeatedly mentioning it.

The first act is giving; the second can be a blameworthy reminder of a favor.

Each Quranic derivative must be assessed through its own construction and context.

ʿ ṭ w and b dh l

Arabic badhl concerns yielding and making something available.

In generosity, badhl al-māl denotes spending wealth or placing it at another's disposal.

This overlaps with aʿṭā.

Badhl can emphasize expending or yielding something from one's possession, while aʿṭā directly states giving.

This is a tendency, not an absolute rule.

ʿ ṭ w and ʾ kh dh

ʾ Kh dh concerns taking and obtaining something.

Taʿāṭā, in the branch discussed here, concerns reaching, taking, or undertaking an action.

Picking something up can be expressed by akhadha.

Where the person's initiative or movement toward it is relevant, taʿāṭā may be appropriate.

It does not invariably establish successful acquisition of the object.

In Al-Qamar 54:29, completion of the subsequent act is separately stated by fa-ʿaqara.

ʿ ṭ w and n w l: tanāwala

The n w l family includes bestowal and handing over; tanāwala is used for reaching and taking. The relationship of nāla to n w l and n y l is discussed in lexicons and should not be settled solely by similarity in meaning.

Tanāwala is used in Arabic for reaching out and taking something.

It can thus overlap with taʿāṭā in some uses.

Overlap does not make the roots identical.

Historically, ʿ ṭ w and n w l require separate investigation.

ʿ ṭ w and m s k

Imsāk concerns taking and holding, and in some contexts withholding a transfer.

In Sad 38:39, bestowing and retaining occur together:

“Fa-mnun aw amsik.”

They can be contrasted: what one possesses may be transferred to another or retained.

Retaining is not always blameworthy, just as giving is not always commendable.

Object, rights, intention, and circumstances matter in both cases.

ʿ ṭ w and ʿ ṭ l

These roots differ only in their final consonant.

The main uses of ʿ ṭ w concern taking, giving, and gifts.

ʿ Ṭ l concerns emptiness, disuse, and abandonment.

ʿAṭāʾ, giving or a gift, and taʿṭīl, suspension or abandonment, therefore belong to independent families.

Similarity in writing does not justify transferring one family's meaning to the other.

10. Translation proposals for Forqan

One fixed equivalent cannot cover every Quranic use of ʿ ṭ w.

“Give” usually suits aʿṭā.

“Pay” can suit financial payments.

Depending on context, ʿaṭāʾ can be “giving,” “a gift,” or “what is given.”

For taʿāṭā in Al-Qamar 54:29, “undertake the act” suits the context.

Conclusion

Arabic ʿ ṭ w concerns reaching out, taking, giving, and placing something at another's disposal.

The verse attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays illustrates an early use of ʿaṭā yaʿṭū for reaching to take something.

Within the family, aʿṭā means giving, ʿaṭāʾ denotes giving or a gift, and taʿāṭā concerns reaching and undertaking an action.

Quranic aʿṭā occurs in different settings: divine bestowal of creation or a blessing, human giving, and specified financial payment.

Giving is therefore not lexically always voluntary generosity or commendable behavior. Its nature and value depend on object, intention, and context.

Quranic ʿaṭāʾ denotes divine giving, while continuity, absence of withholding, and measure or sufficiency come from accompanying words and descriptions.

Fa-taʿāṭā in Al-Qamar 54:29 concerns undertaking action and should not be conflated with fa-ʿaqara, which expresses the subsequent act.

The root's central field is reaching for something to take it, or giving and transferring something to another. Its Quranic derivatives have three main directions: giving in aʿṭā, giving or a gift in ʿaṭāʾ, and reaching or undertaking an action in taʿāṭā.

Forqan should preserve these distinctions. Generosity, unlimited giving, obligatory payment, or wrongfulness should enter the explanation only where the construction and context support them; independent interpretive details should not be inserted into the direct equivalent of the word.
