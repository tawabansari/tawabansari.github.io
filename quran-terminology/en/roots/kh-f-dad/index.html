Lowering, humility, and changes in standing

1. Introducing the root and its semantic field

The three-letter Arabic root KH F Ḍ denotes lowering something or occupying a lower position. This can be tangible, as in lowering a head, wing, or voice, or concern conduct and standing, as in showing humility toward others or a decline in someone’s rank.

Its opposite is R F ʿ, which denotes raising or rising. The contrast between khafḍ and rafʿ is one of the clearest guides to this family’s semantic field.

Lowering does not always mean humiliation or weakness. A person may soften their conduct and speech, showing humility out of affection and respect. By contrast, a decline in someone’s rank may involve losing status or position.

This distinction matters in the Qur’an. In three verses, the command wa-khfiḍ accompanies the image of a wing and expresses humility and gentleness. In another verse, khāfiḍah stands opposite rāfiʿah, describing the event of the Resurrection.

The root thus has a shared semantic field, but the moral value and particular instance of lowering must be understood from each sentence and context.

2. Forms examined and Qur’anic derivatives

KH F Ḍ occurs four times in the Qur’an, in four verses and two morphological forms.

وَاخْفِضْ: a Form I imperative from khafaḍa, meaning “lower.” It occurs three times: Al-Ḥijr 15:88, Al-Isrāʾ 17:24, and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:215.

خَافِضَةٌ: a feminine active participle from the same root, meaning “lowering” or “bringing down.” It occurs once, in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:3.

Other forms, such as خَفْض, خَفِيض, خَافِض, مَخْفُوض, خَفَّضَ, and انْخَفَضَ, are known in Arabic but do not occur in these exact forms in the Qur’an.

Khafḍ is also distinct from khiffah and takhfīf, from KH F F. Lowering something and making it lighter may have similar results in some circumstances, but they belong to different linguistic families.

3. The semantic core in early Arabic

Lowering as the opposite of raising

Early Arabic sources give the concise definition نَقِيضُ الرَّفْع: the opposite of raising.

This definition appears in Kitāb al-ʿAyn, attributed to Al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad, and in later dictionaries such as Al-Muḥkam wa-al-Muḥīṭ al-Aʿẓam and Lisān al-ʿArab.

For example, خَفَضَ الشَّيْءَ means lowering something, while انْخَفَضَ describes its descending or coming down.

The basic relationship here is spatial or one of rank: something occupies a lower position relative to its previous state or to something else.

“Lower” need not mean the ground or a physical vertical movement. Language can also speak of lowering a voice, rank, value, intensity, or manner of conduct.

Such extensions were already known in early Arabic. A literal physical movement should therefore not be imagined behind every use of the root.

Uses in everyday Arab life

Dictionaries record several physical and behavioral examples.

Lowering an animal’s head: خَفَضَ رَأْسَ البَعِيرِ concerns lowering a camel’s head. Some sources record takhfīḍ for drawing its head toward the ground, including to mount it.

Lowering the voice: خَفَضَ صَوْتَهُ means reducing the loudness of one’s voice. Lowering is transferred here to audible intensity.

Calming oneself: expressions such as خَفِّضْ عَلَيْكَ جَأْشَكَ concern settling oneself and reducing anxiety. This relates to calming the intensity of a state.

A peaceful, comfortable life: عَيْشٌ خَفْضٌ and عَيْشٌ خَفِيضٌ are recorded for a life of ease, calm, and ample provision.

This last use shows that KH F Ḍ is not invariably negative. Reduced hardship, anxiety, or movement can be associated with peace and ease.

These examples do not make “peace” the root’s original meaning. Here, ease is an extended use dependent on the subject being discussed.

Al-ʿAyn, Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, and Lisān al-ʿArab: lowering and ease

A witness from poetry attributed to Zuhayr

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā contains the following:

إِذَا شَالَ عَنْ خَفْضِ الْعَوَالِي الْأَسَافِلُ

idhā shāla ʿan khafḍi al-ʿawālī al-asāfilu

“When the lower parts rise while the upper parts are held low.”

This is the second hemistich of a verse, rather than a complete verse. The poem compares the praised man’s courage with a lion’s readiness to confront an opponent. The contrast between raised and lowered body parts depicts preparation for an attack.

Khafḍ here has a tangible meaning associated with bodily position. Neither ethical humility nor a decline in social rank is at issue.

The passage matters because it illustrates spatial and bodily khafḍ in poetry attributed to a period before the Qur’an. Attribution and precise readings should nevertheless be checked against reliable editions of the dīwān. A verse’s appearance in a modern collection alone does not establish its date conclusively.

Other names and technical uses

Arabic dictionaries also use khafḍ for lower ground. In grammar, khafḍ is another name for the genitive case. This is a technical designation for an inflectional state, not the meaning of every Qur’anic occurrence.

Some dictionaries also record the word as a term for cutting girls’ genitalia. This records a term in language and social practice; it is neither the meaning of Qur’anic khafḍ nor evidence that the practice is religiously authorized.

Qur’anic research must distinguish recording a dictionary usage from deriving a religious ruling from it.

4. The image of lowering the wing in Arabic

The root’s principal Qur’anic use involves the expression خَفْضُ الجَنَاح, lowering the wing.

Janāḥ primarily means a bird’s wing. A bird may spread its wings, raise them, or draw them toward its sides.

Dictionaries describe a bird lowering its wing as relaxing it and drawing it against its side. The expression خَفَضَ جَنَاحَهُ is applied figuratively to human humility, gentleness, and calm.

A composed and dignified person, for example, could be called خَافِضُ الجَنَاحِ: someone who avoids arrogant or harsh conduct and treats others gently.

In this image, the wing can represent a person’s stance and manner of interaction. Figuratively lowering it means refraining from displays of superiority, aggression, or defiance.

This should not be turned into a definitive natural-history narrative—for example, that birds always lower their wings to protect their young and that every Qur’anic use derives directly from maternal behavior in birds.

Such an explanation may offer an imaginable comparison, but it does not establish the expression’s historical origin or definitive meaning.

The lexical evidence supports an association between lowering the wing, softening one’s conduct, and abandoning superiority in dealing with another person.

Humility comes from the combination of khafḍ and janāḥ, rather than from KH F Ḍ alone. The same root has a different contextual use in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:3.

5. Qur’anic uses

A. Al-Ḥijr 15:88: gentleness toward believers

Al-Ḥijr 15:88

لَا تَمُدَّنَّ عَيْنَيْكَ إِلَىٰ مَا مَتَّعْنَا بِهِ أَزْوَاجًا مِنْهُمْ وَلَا تَحْزَنْ عَلَيْهِمْ وَاخْفِضْ جَنَاحَكَ لِلْمُؤْمِنِينَ

lā tamuddanna ʿaynayka ilā mā mattaʿnā bihi azwājan minhum wa-lā taḥzan ʿalayhim wa-khfiḍ janāḥaka li-al-muʾminīna

“Do not fix your eyes on what We have given groups of them to enjoy, and do not grieve over them. Lower your wing to the believers.”

The direct addressee is Muhammad. He is told not to fix his gaze on the advantages enjoyed by groups of people or grieve over them, and then to lower his wing to the believers.

Wa-khfiḍ is imperative; janāḥaka is its object; and li-al-muʾminīna identifies those toward whom this conduct is to be shown.

The literal wording retains the image of lowering a wing. Its behavioral sense is gentleness, humility, and kindness toward believers.

The context places other people’s advantages alongside the Prophet’s relationship with believers. Nevertheless, the root alone does not imply that the command means only economic assistance for the poor or reducing class divisions. Such concerns may arise in a wider network of verses, but are not the direct meaning of this expression.

The verse primarily concerns Muhammad’s manner of treating believers.

Lowering the wing does not mean abandoning guidance, remaining silent about wrongdoing, or accepting every request unconditionally. Gentleness and humility can coexist with principles and moral responsibility.

B. Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:215: humility toward believing followers

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:215

وَاخْفِضْ جَنَاحَكَ لِمَنِ اتَّبَعَكَ مِنَ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ

wa-khfiḍ janāḥaka li-mani ittabaʿaka mina al-muʾminīna

“Lower your wing to those believers who follow you.”

The construction closely resembles Al-Ḥijr 15:88. In both, Muhammad receives the command wa-khfiḍ janāḥaka.

The difference is that the recipients here are identified as لِمَنِ اتَّبَعَكَ مِنَ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ: those believers who have followed him.

Nearby, Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:214 speaks of warning close relatives. The command of humility toward believing followers comes next, followed by their possible disobedience in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:216.

This sequence distinguishes, while connecting, the Prophet’s humility and his responsibility concerning other people’s conduct.

Lowering the wing describes his manner of interaction; it does not endorse all his followers’ decisions and actions in advance.

There is no fundamental difference between this use of the root and Al-Ḥijr 15:88. Both employ lowering the wing as an image of gentleness and humility. The recipients and surrounding context differ.

Both verses instruct a person with responsibilities of guidance and leadership to show humility toward believers. Holding responsibility or a prominent position is thus distinct from behaving arrogantly or humiliating others.

This follows from the command and its addressee together, rather than making leadership or social responsibility part of the lexical meaning of khafḍ itself.

C. Al-Isrāʾ 17:24: humility arising from mercy

Al-Isrāʾ 17:24

وَاخْفِضْ لَهُمَا جَنَاحَ الذُّلِّ مِنَ الرَّحْمَةِ وَقُلْ رَبِّ ارْحَمْهُمَا كَمَا رَبَّيَانِي صَغِيرًا

wa-khfiḍ lahumā janāḥa al-dhulli mina al-raḥmati wa-qul rabbi irḥamhumā kamā rabbayānī ṣaghīran

“Out of mercy, lower the wing of humility to them both, and say: My Lord, have mercy on them both, as they raised me when I was small.”

This is a particularly clear connection between khafḍ and human conduct.

The preceding verse, Al-Isrāʾ 17:23, commands kindness toward parents and prohibits hurtful speech, rebuking, and ill treatment.

Verse 24 then concerns more than outward words and actions: it describes a relationship characterized by humility and mercy.

Unlike Al-Ḥijr 15:88 and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:215, the expression is not simply janāḥaka, “your wing,” but جَنَاحَ الذُّلِّ, “the wing of humility.”

This matters: janāḥ supplies the wing image, while al-dhull specifies the character of its lowering.

Humility and imposed humiliation

Dhull belongs to DH L L, whose semantic field includes humility, tractability, yielding, and abandoning a superior stance. In other contexts, the family can also concern humiliation and abasement.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:24, مِنَ الرَّحْمَةِ plays a decisive role.

The child’s requested humility should arise from mercy. The verse therefore specifies the quality and source of conduct, rather than merely an outwardly lowered stance.

Humility arising from affection and mercy differs from humiliation caused by degradation, fear, or subjection.

This distinction matters particularly for understanding relations between parents and children. The Qur’an asks children to treat their parents kindly and humbly. The word does not establish that parents own their children’s will and personality absolutely or that every parental command is binding regardless of whether it is right or wrong.

Luqmān 31:15 speaks of honorable companionship with parents even amid a fundamental disagreement over associating partners, while refusing obedience to their call to association.

Humility and kindness toward parents can therefore coexist with moral responsibility and fidelity to what is right.

The role of mina al-raḥmah

In مِنَ الرَّحْمَةِ, the preposition min can indicate the source and cause of this humility.

The natural sense is that humble conduct toward parents should arise from mercy and kindness.

Mercy is therefore not the lexical meaning of khafḍ. This separate expression gives the command its ethical direction.

In this sentence, khafḍ expresses lowering, while mina al-raḥmah identifies the source and motivation of the conduct. The distinction rests on their roles here. Two expressions appearing separately is not, by itself, proof that all their meanings differ.

The verse’s ending recalls the parents’ care for their child when small, providing a human and emotional setting for the command. The prayer رَبِّ ارْحَمْهُمَا asks for divine mercy for both parents.

Alongside outward conduct, the verse emphasizes mercy, recalling parental efforts, and wishing them well.

These ideas emerge from the whole verse. KH F Ḍ itself retains the meaning of lowering.

6. Khāfiḍatun rāfiʿah: lowering and raising at the Resurrection

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:3

خَافِضَةٌ رَافِعَةٌ

khāfiḍatun rāfiʿatun

“Lowering and raising.”

Khāfiḍah is a feminine active participle from KH F Ḍ, opposed to rāfiʿah from R F ʿ.

The semantic contrast is direct: bringing down versus raising up.

What is lowered or raised, however, must be understood from the context.

The surah opens with two verses:

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:1

إِذَا وَقَعَتِ الْوَاقِعَةُ

idhā waqaʿati al-wāqiʿatu

“When the Event occurs.”

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:2

لَيْسَ لِوَقْعَتِهَا كَاذِبَةٌ

laysa li-waqʿatihā kādhibatun

“There is no denying its occurrence.”

Khāfiḍatun rāfiʿah follows.

In the familiar nominative reading, khāfiḍah and rāfiʿah can be understood as predicates of an omitted subject, hiya, referring back to al-wāqiʿah: the Event lowers and raises. Their feminine form agrees with this referent.

Khafḍ here no longer concerns ethical humility. There is no wing or addressee commanded to behave gently.

What is at issue is a major change in position or state.

What is brought down and what is raised?

Neither participle has an explicitly stated object in the brief expression خَافِضَةٌ رَافِعَةٌ.

This matters: one possible application should not be inserted into the root’s meaning without evidence.

The following verses describe the earth shaking, mountains disintegrating, and people being divided into groups.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:7 speaks of people becoming three groups. Their positions and outcomes are then explained.

Reading khāfiḍatun rāfiʿah as the lowering and raising of people’s rank that Day accords with the surah’s broader context.

Those enjoying worldly power, wealth, or position do not necessarily hold a higher position in the Hereafter. Those of lower social standing need not remain in that position.

This application is not the definitive lexical meaning of khāfiḍah. The word itself means “lowering,” and its object is not stated.

The changes affecting earth and mountains show that the surah also discusses other immense transformations. A broad image of upheaval and changes in standing therefore suits the context, with the outcomes of human groups providing one interpretive indication. Al-Ṭabarī also transmits another reading, relating the two descriptions to the event being heard by those near and far. These differences show why the basic meanings of the participles should be distinguished from their particular exegetical application.

Al-Ṭabarī: readings of khāfiḍatun rāfiʿah

Does khafḍ mean punishment here?

A decline in some people’s standing may, in the context of judgment in the Hereafter, involve deprivation, punishment, or loss of rank.

Khāfiḍah does not itself mean “punishing,” just as rāfiʿah alone does not mean “rewarding.”

Punishment and reward emerge from the passages describing the different groups’ outcomes.

This distinction matters in translation. Rendering the expression directly as “casting sinners into Hell and raising the righteous to Paradise” replaces two brief descriptions with an interpretation of their context.

That explanation may appear in a reflection, but the basic translation should preserve the direct image of lowering and raising.

Is khāfiḍah a divine name?

Lexical sources and later collections of divine names also record al-khāfiḍ as a title for God.

The Qur’anic word in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:3, however, is khāfiḍah, rather than al-khāfiḍ. Its grammatical reference is to the Event itself.

This occurrence should therefore not be rewritten without evidence as an independent divine name.

God’s power to raise or lower people’s standing is a broader subject requiring examination of the relevant verses.

7. Distinctions from related words

KH F Ḍ (khafaḍa) and R F ʿ (rafaʿa): opposite directions

The family’s most important contrast is with R F ʿ.

Khafḍ means lowering, and rafʿ means raising. They occur directly together in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:3.

“Low” and “high” may be physical or concern standing. Raising an object, raising one’s voice, and raising someone’s rank are different applications.

Likewise, figuratively lowering a wing in human conduct and a decline in standing at the Resurrection are not identical applications.

The opposition establishes the root’s basic direction; context determines its subject and value.

Study of R F ʿ

KH F Ḍ (khafaḍa) and DH L L (dhalla): lowering and humility

These two families occur together in Al-Isrāʾ 17:24.

Khafḍ expresses lowering. Dhull specifies humility and yielding in جَنَاحَ الذُّلِّ.

Dhull can concern humiliation and abasement in some uses. Here, its association with mercy highlights humble and kind conduct.

The two roots are thus not complete synonyms. One expresses the action’s direction; the other specifies its quality in this combination.

Study of DH L L

KH F Ḍ (khafaḍa) and L Y N (lāna): lowering and gentleness

Līn denotes softness and gentleness.

Āl ʿImrān 3:159 relates the Prophet’s gentleness to God’s mercy. In Ṭā Hā 20:44, Moses and Aaron are instructed to address Pharaoh gently.

Gentleness therefore has its own semantic field.

Lowering the wing also concerns softening human conduct, but its image is specifically that of a lowered wing.

Līn may explain part of the behavioral effect of lowering the wing, without making the roots’ basic meanings identical.

KH F Ḍ (khafaḍa) and KH SH ʿ (khashaʿa): humility and khushūʿ

In the Qur’an, KH SH ʿ describes human states, bodily conduct, the gaze, prayer, and even the state of the earth.

Khushūʿ can concern humility and the subsiding of a superior stance, but it is not itself the same as lowering something.

Lowering the wing primarily depicts a softened manner of interaction through the wing image. With khushūʿ, an inward state or its outward manifestation matters, depending on context.

Not every khushūʿ can therefore be called lowering the wing, nor should every khafḍ be translated as religious reverence.

Study of KH SH ʿ

KH F Ḍ (khafaḍa) and GH Ḍ Ḍ (ghaḍḍa): reducing and restraining

Luqmān 31:19 states:

وَاغْضُضْ مِنْ صَوْتِكَ

wa-ghḍuḍ min ṣawtika

The command concerns reducing the loudness of one’s voice.

In this construction, GH Ḍ Ḍ concerns restraining or reducing intensity.

Arabic khafḍ al-ṣawt can also express lowering the voice. The expressions therefore overlap in this use.

Ghaḍḍ and khafḍ remain different roots, and their entire semantic ranges are not interchangeable. The subject and structure of the Qur’anic expression غَضُّ البَصَرِ, in particular, require separate study.

KH F Ḍ (khafaḍa) and KH F F (khaffa): lowering and making lighter

These roots resemble each other in writing, but their third letters differ.

KH F F concerns being light, becoming lighter, and making lighter. KH F Ḍ concerns lowering.

Reducing intensity may overlap with both fields in certain uses, but lowering need not mean becoming lighter, and becoming lighter need not mean descending.

Khāfiḍah in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:3, for example, cannot mean “making lighter.” Takhfīf in passages on easing an obligation or reducing the severity of punishment belongs to KH F F.

8. Translation suggestions for Forqan

Translating KH F Ḍ requires distinguishing literal lowering from the sense supplied by the combination and context.

In Al-Ḥijr 15:88 and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:215, wa-khfiḍ janāḥaka can be rendered “lower your wing,” retaining the Qur’anic image. A more explanatory rendering, “treat them humbly and gently,” conveys the expression’s sense but leaves the wing image aside.

For Forqan’s basic translation, retaining “lower your wing” is suitable: the verse itself uses imagery, and the expression is also intelligible in Persian.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:24, the distinct wording جَنَاحَ الذُّلِّ مِنَ الرَّحْمَةِ should be preserved.

An appropriate rendering is:

“Out of mercy, lower the wing of humility to them both.”

This retains the wing image, expresses humility, and shows its connection with mercy.

In Al-Wāqiʿah 56:3, khāfiḍah is most appropriately rendered “lowering” or “bringing down.”

The complete expression can be translated:

“Lowering and raising.”

More forceful Persian equivalents suggesting sinking and pulling upward may evoke a particular bodily movement that the verse itself does not specify.

Adding people’s ranks, sinners, the righteous, Paradise, or Hell is unnecessary in the direct translation. These matters belong in contextual explanation.

Suitable equivalents therefore vary with the use: “lowering,” “bringing down,” “showing humility” in expressions about human conduct, and “lowering” as a description of the Resurrection event.

9. Conclusion

Arabic KH F Ḍ denotes lowering or descending, opposed to raising. This direction can apply to physical matters, behavior, and standing.

Lowering the head, wing, and voice are tangible early Arabic uses. Within this field, lowering the wing becomes an image of humility, gentleness, and abandoning superiority.

The Qur’an uses this combination three times: twice instructing Muhammad about his conduct toward believers, and once commanding kindness and humility toward parents.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:24, the combination of janāḥ al-dhull with mina al-raḥmah indicates humility arising from mercy. The command should therefore not be equated with imposed humiliation or unconditional obedience.

In Al-Wāqiʿah 56:3, however, khāfiḍah stands opposite rāfiʿah as a description of the Resurrection event. Human gentleness is not the subject. Rather, lowering and raising occur in an event that transforms conditions and standing. Its precise application requires the surah’s context, rather than the root alone.

Proposed final definition: KH F Ḍ denotes lowering something or occupying a lower level or rank. In khafḍ al-janāḥ, this tangible meaning extends to humility and softening one’s conduct. In khāfiḍatun rāfiʿah, lowering contrasts with raising, without the root itself specifying the subject or moral value of the change. Brief root meaning: Lowering and bringing down; in “lowering the wing,” humility and gentleness in conduct.
