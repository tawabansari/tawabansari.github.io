The Arabic root Ḥ R J concerns narrowness, restriction, and difficulty in getting out of a situation. حَرَج may express a cramped place, inward pressure and constriction, difficulty performing an action, or liability and sin arising from wrongful conduct. أَحْرَجَهُ concerns placing someone under constraint; تَحَرَّجَ in an established use means avoiding something for fear of sin or liability.

In early Arabic, حَرَجَة denotes a place where trees intertwine so densely that passing through them is difficult. This gives a tangible picture of constraint: the way is blocked or restricted enough to impede easy movement and access.

The Qur’an uses the root in different settings: a constricted breast in relation to guidance, inward resistance to accepting judgment, difficulty in religious duties, exemption for people unable to perform an action, and the removal of restrictions in certain relationships and rulings.

Ḥaraj cannot be translated exclusively as “sin” or “physical hardship” throughout these uses. Sometimes the subject is inward pressure, sometimes practical difficulty, and sometimes liability or blame lifted from a person.

The root occurs fifteen times in eleven Qur’anic verses. All occurrences belong to the ḥaraj family; no independent verb from this root occurs in the Qur’an.

1. Forms and derivatives

Ḥ R J consists of the letters ḥāʾ, rāʾ, and jīm and is a sound, non-geminate Arabic root.

The principal forms in this family include:

  • حَرَج: Constraint, pressure, difficulty, and distress; in some uses, sin or liability that places a person under constraint.
  • حَرِج: Constrained or in difficulty; also someone hesitant or fearful about proceeding with an action.
  • حَرِجَ ـ يَحْرَجُ: Became constrained or came under pressure and difficulty.
  • حَرَجَة: A place of dense, intertwined trees with restricted passage between them.
  • حَرَجَات / أَحْرَاج: Recorded plurals of حَرَجَة.
  • حِرَاج: Dense collections of trees or thick thickets.
  • حَرِيج: Narrow and difficult; in another use, someone not easily driven back from fighting.
  • أَحْرَجَ ـ يُحْرِجُ: Constrained someone, exerted pressure, or placed another in difficulty.
  • إِحْرَاج: Placing under constraint, making circumstances difficult, or exerting pressure.
  • حَرَّجَ ـ يُحَرِّجُ: Imposed strictness or restriction; in some uses, declared an action improper or sinful for another.
  • تَحْرِيج: Strictness, constraining, and in some contexts a severe warning against an action.
  • تَحَرَّجَ: Avoided an action for fear of sin or blame; kept oneself from becoming involved in ḥaraj.
  • مُتَحَرِّج: Someone who refrains from an action to avoid sin or liability.
  • حَارِج: In lexical usage, a sinful person.
  • مُحْرِج: Someone or something placing another under constraint.
  • حَرُوج: In some early uses, a description of a camel with particular bodily features.

Lexical sources also record other forms with particular meanings: حِرْج for certain traps or implements, حَرَج for a wooden structure used to carry an ill or dead person, and مِحْرَاج as a description of a very cold night.

These forms matter for the root’s historical range, but do not occur in the Qur’an.

The Qur’anic forms are limited to ḥaraj with different case endings: حَرَجٌ, حَرَجٍ, and حَرَجًا. In Al-Anʿām 6:125, the word describes ṣadr, the breast; the reading حَرِجًا, with kasrah on the rāʾ, is also established.

Changes in nunation or grammatical case should therefore not be counted as independent derivatives. The descriptive use in Al-Anʿām must also be distinguished from the nominal uses in other verses.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: fifteen occurrences in eleven verses, fourteen nouns and one adjective

2. The semantic core and early Arabic usage

Gathering and the formation of constraint

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris explains the semantic basis of Ḥ R J as follows:

تَجَمُّعُ الشَّيْءِ وَضِيقُهُ

tajammuʿu al-shayʾi wa-ḍīquhu

Meaning: “The gathering and narrowness of a thing.”

This explanation brings two features together: density and constriction.

When many things gather within a limited space, passage or movement may become difficult.

One clear Arabic example is a thicket whose trees and branches intertwine.

Such a place may look green and extensive from outside, yet be difficult to move through.

Ḥarajah is accordingly used for a place containing dense, intertwined trees.

Lisān al-ʿArab reports from Abū Zayd that the name concerns the intertwining trees and the narrow route among them.

This image shows a clear connection between the root’s physical use and constraint.

It does not establish that every ethical, legal, or psychological sense of ḥaraj necessarily developed historically from the name of a dense thicket. Ibn Fāris proposes a shared theme; this helps explain relationships between meanings, but does not prove the historical order in which every derivative arose.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Lisān al-ʿArab: density, constriction, and thickets

Ḥarajah: a dense thicket and difficult passage

In Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Jawharī describes ḥarajah as a collection of trees standing so closely together that passage among them is difficult.

Other sources mention trees such as samar, ṭalḥ, sidr, and other kinds in descriptions of these thickets.

Some explanations also include a tree surrounded by other trees that grazing animals cannot easily reach.

This usage shows that constriction does not always mean that the entire place is small.

Land may be extensive while its internal routes are so blocked by branches that movement is difficult.

The physical image of ḥaraj can therefore concern restricted passage and access, rather than merely the small size of a space.

This distinction also matters in Qur’anic usage: someone may not face an outward lack of resources, yet experience inward constriction over a decision or responsibility.

A poetic witness concerning dense thickets

Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Lisān al-ʿArab transmit the following verse:

أَيَا حَرَجَاتِ الْحَيِّ حِينَ تَحَمَّلُوا
بِذِي سَلَمٍ لَا جَادَكُنَّ رَبِيعُ

ayā ḥarajāti al-ḥayyi ḥīna taḥammalū / bi-dhī salamin lā jādakunna rabīʿu

Meaning: “Thickets of the people’s dwelling, when they departed, at Dhū Salam—may spring rain not water you.”

حَرَجَات here denotes thickets and collections of trees.

It names an actual place, rather than a psychological state or sin.

The verse also appears in the collected poetry attributed to Qays ibn al-Mulawwaḥ, associated with the Islamic period. It should therefore not be assigned to the pre-Islamic period without independent evidence.

Its presence in lexical sources nevertheless shows that the dense-tree branch was an established Arabic usage.

Poem text in the collection attributed to Qays ibn al-Mulawwaḥ; no claim of a secure date

Ḥaraj as a wooden carrying structure

Lexical sources also record ḥaraj for a structure made by fastening pieces of wood together, used to carry an ill or dead person.

It resembled a bed or litter whose parts were joined to provide support for the body.

The collected poetry attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays includes:

فَإِمَّا تَرَيْنِي فِي رِحَالَةِ جَابِرٍ
عَلَى حَرَجٍ كَالْقَرِّ تَخْفِقُ أَكْفَانِي

fa-immā taraynī fī riḥālati jābirin / ʿalā ḥarajin ka-al-qarri takhfiqu akfānī

Meaning: “If you see me in Jābir’s riding litter, on a wooden frame like a carrier, with my garments fluttering…”

The verse is transmitted in a context of the poet’s illness and physical weakness.

Ḥaraj here denotes the structure on which the poet is carried.

This use differs from psychological constriction or sin, although its physical form involves pieces of wood gathered together.

The verse is recorded in Imruʾ al-Qays’s collected poetry and quoted by lexicographers for this meaning of ḥaraj. As with other poems attributed to pre-Islamic poets, recording and attribution must be distinguished from definitive proof of composition date.

For root research, the witness matters as an attestation of another physical use, rather than proof that every meaning of ḥaraj developed from this wooden litter.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Ḥ R J: the Imruʾ al-Qays witness and Jābir/Sābiḥ variant

Ḥaraj as difficulty and pressure

In general Arabic usage, ḥaraj also denotes constriction and difficulty.

This difficulty may arise from inability to perform an action, limited resources, or pressure of circumstances.

For example, imposing a responsibility on someone who lacks the necessary means or ability may place that person under ḥaraj.

Ḥaraj in this use does not necessarily mean severe physical suffering.

A person may face constraint in financial means, social circumstances, freedom of movement, or conditions of decision-making.

Its meaning can thus encompass practical hardship and pressure arising from restriction.

Ḥaraj as sin and liability

Lexical sources also record الْحَرَجُ: الْإِثْمُ: ḥaraj can mean sin in some uses.

This meaning’s connection with constraint can be explained.

An improper action confronts a person with restriction and accountability; performing it may involve sin and blame.

Expressions such as لَا حَرَجَ عَلَيْهِ may accordingly mean that no sin or liability rests upon someone.

This does not mean that ḥaraj is always equivalent to ithm.

Al-Anʿām 6:125 concerns constriction of the breast; sin is not the word’s direct explanation there.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:6 and Al-Ḥajj 22:78, religious hardship and constraint are central.

In Al-Tawbah 9:91 and Al-Fatḥ 48:17, the absence of ḥaraj concerns removing liability or blame from people in specified circumstances.

Sin is therefore one attested sense, to be used only where context supports it.

Taḥarraja: refraining to avoid sin

Arabic تَحَرَّجَ مِنَ الْأَمْرِ can mean avoiding an action for fear of sin or liability in performing it.

At first sight, this use may seem different from constraint.

Ḥaraj may be sin or difficulty, yet a mutaḥarrij is someone trying to avoid it.

Lexicographers compare this usage with similar expressions such as تَأَثَّمَ: refraining from sin.

Taḥarruj in this construction therefore does not necessarily mean committing sin; it may mean refraining so as not to become involved in it.

This distinction should not be overlooked.

A person’s abstention from an action about which they feel doubt also does not by itself establish that God has prohibited that action for everyone.

Personal caution or abstention and pronouncing a binding divine ruling require separate examination.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Miṣbāḥ al-Munīr, and Lisān al-ʿArab: taḥarruj as avoiding sin

Iḥrāj: placing someone under constraint

أَحْرَجَهُ in Arabic concerns placing another person under constraint.

Through pressure, threats, strictness, or restricting available choices, someone may put another person in a difficult position.

Iḥrāj in that case is not merely a feeling of embarrassment; it may involve real pressure and practical restriction.

In contemporary Arabic and Persian usage in Afghanistan, iḥrāj sometimes denotes embarrassment or being put in an uncomfortable situation.

This usage relates to inward pressure and constraint, but does not encompass the root’s entire historical meaning.

Neither أَحْرَجَ nor the verbal noun إِحْرَاج occurs in the Qur’an. The modern sense of embarrassment should therefore not be transferred to every Qur’anic ḥaraj.

3. A constricted breast and difficulty accepting in the Qur’an

Al-Anʿām 6:125: a narrow, constricted breast

Verse text:

فَمَنْ يُرِدِ اللَّهُ أَنْ يَهْدِيَهُ يَشْرَحْ صَدْرَهُ لِلْإِسْلَامِ وَمَنْ يُرِدْ أَنْ يُضِلَّهُ يَجْعَلْ صَدْرَهُ ضَيِّقًا حَرَجًا كَأَنَّمَا يَصَّعَّدُ فِي السَّمَاءِ كَذَٰلِكَ يَجْعَلُ اللَّهُ الرِّجْسَ عَلَى الَّذِينَ لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ

fa-man yuridi Allāhu an yahdiyahu yashraḥ ṣadrahu li-al-islāmi wa-man yurid an yuḍillahu yajʿal ṣadrahu ḍayyiqan ḥarajan ka-annamā yaṣṣaʿʿadu fī al-samāʾi kadhālika yajʿalu Allāhu al-rijsa ʿalā alladhīna lā yuʾminūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Whomever The God wills to guide, He opens that person’s breast to Islam; whomever He wills to lead astray, He makes that person’s breast narrow and constrained, as though laboriously ascending into the sky. Thus The God places defilement upon those who do not believe.”

حَرَجًا follows ضَيِّقًا in this verse and describes the breast’s condition.

Two images stand opposite one another.

In the first, the breast opens to accepting Islam.

In the second, the breast becomes narrow and constrained.

Ḥarajan in this context therefore concerns pressure, constriction, and difficulty accepting.

The verse does not describe the physical dimensions of the rib cage, since its subject is guidance and accepting Islam.

The distinction between ضَيِّقًا and حَرَجًا

Ḍayyiq belongs to Ḍ Y Q and concerns narrowness and restriction.

Ḥaraj here likewise concerns constriction, but its lexical background may highlight severe pressure and blocked passage.

Al-Zajjāj explains ḥaraj in this setting as the most severe constriction.

The pairing of ḍayyiq and ḥaraj can accordingly emphasize the severity of constriction.

It should not become an absolute rule that ḍayq always means mild narrowness and ḥaraj always its final, irreversible stage.

The two words reinforce one another here, creating an image of severe constriction.

The readings حَرَجًا and حَرِجًا

Al-Anʿām 6:125 has two established readings.

In حَرَجًا, the rāʾ has fatḥah.

In حَرِجًا, the rāʾ has kasrah.

Grammatically, ḥaraj with fatḥah can be a verbal noun used descriptively, as Arabic sometimes uses verbal nouns to describe an intense condition.

Ḥarij with kasrah is also a descriptive form, denoting being constrained.

Some early explanations treat the two forms as close or equivalent in meaning.

Completely opposed meanings should therefore not be deduced from the vowel difference alone.

In both readings, context concerns constriction and difficulty accepting.

كَأَنَّمَا يَصَّعَّدُ فِي السَّمَاءِ

After ضَيِّقًا حَرَجًا, the verse presents an image of laboriously ascending into the sky.

This image concerns the severity of the condition just described.

Ḥaraj itself does not mean altitude, ascent, or oxygen shortage.

Ascending is expressed by يَصَّعَّدُ; narrowness and constriction by ضَيِّقًا حَرَجًا.

Modern knowledge recognizes that high-altitude ascent may involve changes in pressure and available oxygen. That scientific knowledge should not, however, become the lexical meaning of ḥaraj.

The verse expresses difficulty in natural language through an image of ascent into the sky.

Does ḥaraj mean sin in this verse?

Lexical sources allow ḥaraj to mean sin.

Here, however, its association with ضَيِّقًا, صَدْرَهُ, and يَشْرَحْ صَدْرَهُ supports constriction and pressure.

“He makes his breast sinful” is therefore not an accurate, natural rendering of this expression.

Sin and rejection occur in the verse’s broader context, but ḥaraj’s particular meaning here is constriction and constraint of the breast.

Al-Ṭabarī: the readings ḥaraj and ḥarij and severe constriction of the breast

Al-Aʿrāf 7:2: no constraint in the Prophet’s breast

Verse text:

كِتَابٌ أُنْزِلَ إِلَيْكَ فَلَا يَكُنْ فِي صَدْرِكَ حَرَجٌ مِنْهُ لِتُنْذِرَ بِهِ وَذِكْرَىٰ لِلْمُؤْمِنِينَ

kitābun unzila ilayka fa-lā yakun fī ṣadrika ḥarajun minhu li-tundhira bihi wa-dhikrā li-al-muʾminīna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“A Book has been sent down to you; let there be no constraint or constriction in your breast concerning it, so that you may warn through it, and as a reminder for the believers.”

Ḥarajun here appears in فِي صَدْرِكَ حَرَجٌ.

Ṣadr, the breast, concerns the addressee’s inward state here.

Study of Ṣ D R

The direct addressee is Muhammad; the verse concerns the Book sent down to him.

فَلَا يَكُنْ فِي صَدْرِكَ حَرَجٌ مِنْهُ thus concerns the absence of inward constraint in relation to this Book.

Warning and reminding are subsequently stated as purposes.

The verse’s meaning therefore connects with responsibility for conveying the message.

Ḥaraj alone does not establish that the Prophet necessarily experienced doctrinal doubt at the moment of revelation.

The verse commands and guides; a prohibition or command does not necessarily establish that the prohibited state actually occurred.

Conversely, a theological presupposition should not render the Qur’an’s direct address to Muhammad ineffective.

The verse does genuinely instruct him not to become inwardly constrained concerning the Book and the mission of warning.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:65: inward constraint concerning judgment

Verse text:

فَلَا وَرَبِّكَ لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ حَتَّىٰ يُحَكِّمُوكَ فِيمَا شَجَرَ بَيْنَهُمْ ثُمَّ لَا يَجِدُوا فِي أَنْفُسِهِمْ حَرَجًا مِمَّا قَضَيْتَ وَيُسَلِّمُوا تَسْلِيمًا

fa-lā wa-rabbika lā yuʾminūna ḥattā yuḥakkimūka fī-mā shajara baynahum thumma lā yajidū fī anfusihim ḥarajan mimmā qaḍayta wa-yusallimū taslīman

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“No, by your Lord, they will not believe until they make you judge over their disputes, then find within themselves no constraint or resistance concerning your decision and submit completely.”

Ḥarajan here appears in فِي أَنْفُسِهِمْ حَرَجًا.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:2 spoke of the Prophet’s breast; here, the subject is the inner state of people who must accept the messenger’s judgment.

مِمَّا قَضَيْتَ specifies the object of that resistance: Muhammad’s judgment of their dispute.

Ḥaraj here therefore concerns inward constraint, resistance, and dissatisfaction with the judgment.

Ḥaraj itself does not mean judgment, however.

Judgment is expressed by قَضَيْتَ and يُحَكِّمُوكَ; ḥaraj concerns the people’s inward response to it.

The verse also concerns the messenger’s own judgment in the disputes under discussion.

Without independent evidence, this does not establish that every judgment attributed to any subsequent scholar, leader, or ruler has the same standing, or that questioning it indicates lack of faith.

Extending the verse’s scope requires independent Qur’anic evidence.

4. Absence of ḥaraj in religious duties and practical responsibility

Al-Māʾidah 5:6: removing constraint in purification rulings

Verse text:

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِذَا قُمْتُمْ إِلَى الصَّلَاةِ فَاغْسِلُوا وُجُوهَكُمْ وَأَيْدِيَكُمْ إِلَى الْمَرَافِقِ وَامْسَحُوا بِرُءُوسِكُمْ وَأَرْجُلَكُمْ إِلَى الْكَعْبَيْنِ وَإِنْ كُنْتُمْ جُنُبًا فَاطَّهَّرُوا وَإِنْ كُنْتُمْ مَرْضَىٰ أَوْ عَلَىٰ سَفَرٍ أَوْ جَاءَ أَحَدٌ مِنْكُمْ مِنَ الْغَائِطِ أَوْ لَامَسْتُمُ النِّسَاءَ فَلَمْ تَجِدُوا مَاءً فَتَيَمَّمُوا صَعِيدًا طَيِّبًا فَامْسَحُوا بِوُجُوهِكُمْ وَأَيْدِيكُمْ مِنْهُ مَا يُرِيدُ اللَّهُ لِيَجْعَلَ عَلَيْكُمْ مِنْ حَرَجٍ وَلَٰكِنْ يُرِيدُ لِيُطَهِّرَكُمْ وَلِيُتِمَّ نِعْمَتَهُ عَلَيْكُمْ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَشْكُرُونَ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū idhā qumtum ilā al-ṣalāti fa-ighsilū wujūhakum wa-aydiyakum ilā al-marāfiqi wa-imsaḥū bi-ruʾūsikum wa-arjulakum ilā al-kaʿbayni wa-in kuntum junuban fa-iṭṭahharū wa-in kuntum marḍā aw ʿalā safarin aw jāʾa aḥadun minkum mina al-ghāʾiṭi aw lāmastumu al-nisāʾa fa-lam tajidū māʾan fa-tayammamū ṣaʿīdan ṭayyiban fa-imsaḥū bi-wujūhikum wa-aydīkum minhu mā yurīdu Allāhu li-yajʿala ʿalaykum min ḥarajin wa-lākin yurīdu li-yuṭahhirakum wa-li-yutimma niʿmatahu ʿalaykum laʿallakum tashkurūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“You who believe, when you rise for ṣalāh, wash your faces and hands to the elbows, and wipe your heads and feet to the ankle prominences. If you are in a state of janābah, purify yourselves. If you are ill or travelling, or one of you comes from relieving oneself, or you have had contact with women and find no water, turn to a clean surface and wipe your faces and hands with it. The God does not will to place constraint or difficulty upon you; rather, He wills to purify you and complete His blessing upon you, so that you may be thankful.”

Ḥarajin here follows مِنْ.

مَا يُرِيدُ اللَّهُ لِيَجْعَلَ عَلَيْكُمْ مِنْ حَرَجٍ denies a will to place ḥaraj upon the addressees.

The context concerns purification, washing, wiping, illness, travel, and lack of water.

Ḥaraj here therefore concerns the difficulty and pressure of practical duties.

The same verse introduces another way for circumstances in which using water is unavailable.

The denial of ḥaraj is thus not a slogan detached from rulings; the verse itself pairs it with a practical means of relieving difficulty.

Removing ḥaraj does not abolish purification itself

The verse first states instructions concerning cleanliness.

It then introduces an alternative for some circumstances.

It concludes that God does not will to place ḥaraj upon you, but wills to purify you.

Ease in this context therefore does not mean completely eliminating responsibility for purification.

Rather, the method of performing it relates to a person’s circumstances.

Conversely, strictness should not render the Qur’an’s own stated alternative ineffective.

Where God has opened a path under specified conditions, unsupported restrictions that effectively close that path would conflict with the verse’s clear direction.

This conclusion arises from the ruling and its exception together, rather than ḥaraj’s lexical meaning alone.

Al-Ṭabarī: purification, alternatives, and the denial of constraint

Al-Ḥajj 22:78: no constraint in religion

Verse text:

وَجَاهِدُوا فِي اللَّهِ حَقَّ جِهَادِهِ هُوَ اجْتَبَاكُمْ وَمَا جَعَلَ عَلَيْكُمْ فِي الدِّينِ مِنْ حَرَجٍ مِلَّةَ أَبِيكُمْ إِبْرَاهِيمَ هُوَ سَمَّاكُمُ الْمُسْلِمِينَ مِنْ قَبْلُ وَفِي هَٰذَا لِيَكُونَ الرَّسُولُ شَهِيدًا عَلَيْكُمْ وَتَكُونُوا شُهَدَاءَ عَلَى النَّاسِ فَأَقِيمُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَآتُوا الزَّكَاةَ وَاعْتَصِمُوا بِاللَّهِ هُوَ مَوْلَاكُمْ فَنِعْمَ الْمَوْلَىٰ وَنِعْمَ النَّصِيرُ

wa-jāhidū fī Allāhi ḥaqqa jihādihi huwa ijtabākum wa-mā jaʿala ʿalaykum fī al-dīni min ḥarajin millata abīkum ibrāhīma huwa sammākumu al-muslimīna min qablu wa-fī hādhā li-yakūna al-rasūlu shahīdan ʿalaykum wa-takūnū shuhadāʾa ʿalā al-nāsi fa-aqīmū al-ṣalāta wa-ātū al-zakāta wa-iʿtaṣimū bi-Allāhi huwa mawlākum fa-niʿma al-mawlā wa-niʿma al-naṣīru

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Strive for The God with the striving due to Him. He has chosen you and has placed no constraint upon you in religion—the way of your father Abraham. He named you Muslims before, and in this, so that the messenger may be a witness over you and you may be witnesses over people. Establish ṣalāh, give zakāh, and hold fast to The God. He is your protector: an excellent protector and an excellent helper.”

Ḥaraj appears here in فِي الدِّينِ مِنْ حَرَجٍ.

Unlike Al-Māʾidah 5:6, which specifically concerns purification, this verse uses the broader expression فِي الدِّينِ, “in religion.”

The denial of ḥaraj thus concerns religion’s method and framework.

The same verse nevertheless commands striving for God, establishing ṣalāh, giving zakāh, and holding fast to God.

Absence of ḥaraj therefore does not mean the absence of every duty or responsibility.

The central point is that religious responsibility should not be equated with wrongful constraint or a duty incompatible with a person’s ability and circumstances.

Al-Baqarah 2:286 likewise connects duty with human capacity.

Relief from ḥaraj therefore relates to human ability and capacity.

Natural difficulty and imposed constraint

Some right actions may be difficult to perform.

People may, for example, encounter difficulty caring for someone in need, defending the wronged, fulfilling family responsibilities, or remaining steadfast in truth.

This does not establish that everything difficult is prohibited or outside religion according to the Qur’an.

Conversely, the difficulty of some responsibilities does not authorize inventing further restrictions or imposing unsupported pressure in God’s name.

Al-Ḥajj 22:78 places striving and the absence of ḥaraj in the same verse.

This pairing shows that serious effort and responsibility are compatible with denying wrongful constraint.

Ḥaraj should therefore not be equated with every degree of effort or exertion.

In a religious context, ḥaraj may concern difficulty and pressure that restrict the path of fulfilling responsibility or make it unbearable.

Its precise boundary in each issue, however, requires the wording of the ruling, the person’s circumstances, and other relevant verses.

Al-Ṭabarī: denying a constraint without a way out in religion

5. Absence of ḥaraj for people with excuses and limitations

Al-Tawbah 9:91: inability and lack of resources

Verse text:

لَيْسَ عَلَى الضُّعَفَاءِ وَلَا عَلَى الْمَرْضَىٰ وَلَا عَلَى الَّذِينَ لَا يَجِدُونَ مَا يُنْفِقُونَ حَرَجٌ إِذَا نَصَحُوا لِلَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ مَا عَلَى الْمُحْسِنِينَ مِنْ سَبِيلٍ وَاللَّهُ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ

laysa ʿalā al-ḍuʿafāʾi wa-lā ʿalā al-marḍā wa-lā ʿalā alladhīna lā yajidūna mā yunfiqūna ḥarajun idhā naṣaḥū li-Allāhi wa-rasūlihi mā ʿalā al-muḥsinīna min sabīlin wa-Allāhu ghafūrun raḥīmun

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“No liability or blame rests upon the weak, the ill, or those who find nothing to spend, provided they are sincere and act faithfully toward The God and His messenger. There is no ground for blame against those who do good; The God is forgiving and merciful.”

Ḥarajun appears here with لَيْسَ عَلَى.

This construction expresses the absence of ḥaraj upon the people named.

The immediate context concerns participation in a demanding responsibility requiring physical ability and financial means.

Three groups are identified: the weak, the ill, and those without the necessary resources to spend.

لَيْسَ عَلَيْهِمْ حَرَجٌ here therefore concerns the absence of liability and blame for their genuine inability.

The role of إِذَا نَصَحُوا

The verse includes an important qualification:

“إِذَا نَصَحُوا لِلَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ,” “provided they are sincere toward The God and His messenger.”

This expression concerns their sincerity, honesty, and faithful conduct toward God and His messenger.

Exemption in this context therefore does not license deception or misuse of a claim of inability.

Conversely, the verse does not say that a person must necessarily act beyond their ability to prove sincerity.

Genuine inability together with sincerity and faithful conduct provides the setting for removing ḥaraj.

The following verse, Al-Tawbah 9:92, also describes people who find no means of accompanying the others and weep with grief.

This continuity shows that practical inability is not identical with unwillingness or hostility.

Al-Ṭabarī: absence of sin for genuine inability accompanied by sincerity

Al-Fatḥ 48:17: blindness, impaired mobility, and illness

Verse text:

لَيْسَ عَلَى الْأَعْمَىٰ حَرَجٌ وَلَا عَلَى الْأَعْرَجِ حَرَجٌ وَلَا عَلَى الْمَرِيضِ حَرَجٌ وَمَنْ يُطِعِ اللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُ يُدْخِلْهُ جَنَّاتٍ تَجْرِي مِنْ تَحْتِهَا الْأَنْهَارُ وَمَنْ يَتَوَلَّ يُعَذِّبْهُ عَذَابًا أَلِيمًا

laysa ʿalā al-aʿmā ḥarajun wa-lā ʿalā al-aʿraji ḥarajun wa-lā ʿalā al-marīḍi ḥarajun wa-man yuṭiʿi Allāha wa-rasūlahu yudkhilhu jannātin tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhāru wa-man yatawalla yuʿadhdhibhu ʿadhāban alīman

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“No liability or blame rests upon the blind person, the person with impaired walking, or the ill person. Whoever obeys The God and His messenger, He will admit them into gardens beneath which rivers flow; whoever turns away, He will punish with a painful punishment.”

Ḥarajun occurs three times in this verse.

Each time, a particular group is identified and ḥaraj is then lifted from that group.

الْأَعْمَىٰ refers to a blind person.

الْأَعْرَج denotes someone whose walking or leg movement is impaired.

الْمَرِيض is an ill person.

In Al-Fatḥ’s context, the subject connects with the responsibility of participation in difficult circumstances and confrontation.

The denial of ḥaraj here therefore exempts people whose physical condition makes that responsibility difficult or impossible.

This should not be understood to remove every moral or human responsibility from all people with disabilities or illnesses in every circumstance.

Exemption from one particular responsibility is not identical with inability to bear any responsibility.

Nor is people’s human worth limited to their ability to participate in military activity.

Al-Nūr 24:61: three denials of ḥaraj in another setting

Al-Nūr 24:61 names the same three groups:

A blind person, a person with impaired walking, and an ill person.

Ḥaraj likewise occurs three times here.

The verse continues, however, by discussing eating from one’s own houses, the houses of parents, relatives, friends, and those whose keys are entrusted to people. It then addresses eating together or separately.

Its setting is therefore not entirely identical with Al-Fatḥ 48:17.

Early commentaries offer different explanations of the opening clauses’ relationship with the eating rulings. Some relate them to relieving difficulty for people with physical limitations in eating and social interaction; others also connect the opening with exemption from other responsibilities.

Linguistically, what is clear is the denial of ḥaraj upon the three groups, followed by the absence of blame in particular ways of eating and social interaction.

The verse’s entire setting should not be restricted to military combat without sufficient indication; nor should established differences over the clauses’ syntactic and thematic relationship be ignored.

Al-Ṭabarī: differing views on exemption, eating, and social interaction

Ḥaraj and junāḥ in the same verse

Al-Nūr 24:61 first uses ḥaraj.

It subsequently uses جُنَاح.

Junāḥ belongs to J N Ḥ and, in the Qur’an’s legal and ethical usage, may refer to sin or liability arising from an action.

Ḥaraj in the same field likewise concerns the absence of constraint and blame.

The two words are thus close in some uses.

Their mere occurrence in the same verse does not establish complete equivalence.

Ḥaraj’s lexical family also covers narrowness and difficulty, while junāḥ belongs to another semantic family.

In this context, both may concern removing an impediment and blame from a particular action.

6. Absence of ḥaraj in rulings concerning the messenger and family relationships

Al-Aḥzāb 33:37: removing constraint concerning marriage

After mentioning Zayd and the ending of his marital relationship, Al-Aḥzāb 33:37 discusses Muhammad’s marriage to his former wife.

It then states a purpose for this event:

“لِكَيْ لَا يَكُونَ عَلَى الْمُؤْمِنِينَ حَرَجٌ,” “so that no constraint should rest upon the believers.”

The subject of this relief is then specified: marriage to the former wives of adopted sons after their marital relationships have ended.

Ḥaraj here is not merely inward discomfort; it concerns a restriction or impediment in a social and family relationship.

Through removing ḥaraj, the verse clarifies the distinction between adoptive status and a lineage relationship in the ruling under discussion.

Ḥaraj alone should not be taken to mean that all family relationships or every marriage restriction has been abolished.

Relief from ḥaraj has a specific subject here and must be understood within that scope.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:38: no ḥaraj upon the Prophet

Verse text:

مَا كَانَ عَلَى النَّبِيِّ مِنْ حَرَجٍ فِيمَا فَرَضَ اللَّهُ لَهُ سُنَّةَ اللَّهِ فِي الَّذِينَ خَلَوْا مِنْ قَبْلُ وَكَانَ أَمْرُ اللَّهِ قَدَرًا مَقْدُورًا

mā kāna ʿalā al-nabiyyi min ḥarajin fī-mā faraḍa Allāhu lahu sunnata Allāhi fī alladhīna khalaw min qablu wa-kāna amru Allāhi qadaran maqdūran

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“No constraint or blame rests upon the Prophet concerning what The God has ordained for him. This is The God’s established way with those who passed before; The God’s command has been a determined decree.”

Ḥarajin here appears in مَا كَانَ عَلَى النَّبِيِّ مِنْ حَرَجٍ.

This sentence denies ḥaraj concerning what God has ordained for the Prophet.

فِيمَا فَرَضَ اللَّهُ لَهُ also defines the scope of that denial.

The verse therefore does not remove every responsibility from the Prophet throughout every act of life.

It lifts ḥaraj concerning the subject addressed by that context.

This distinction matters: a qualified sentence should not be turned into an unlimited, unqualified ruling.

Al-Ṭabarī: absence of sin in the specific matter ordained for the Prophet

Al-Aḥzāb 33:50: no ḥaraj in specified marriage rulings for the Prophet

Al-Aḥzāb 33:50 identifies groups of women and conditions concerning Muhammad’s marriage.

It then includes لِكَيْلَا يَكُونَ عَلَيْكَ حَرَجٌ, “so that no constraint should rest upon you.”

Ḥaraj here concerns a restriction or difficulty removed in the stated matter of marriage and particular rulings.

The verse directly addresses the Prophet.

Its detailed ruling therefore cannot be extended to all men or all believers without evidence.

The verse itself distinguishes the rulings stated for the Prophet from what has been ordained for other believers.

Ḥaraj’s meaning is thus clear: a constraint or impediment removed concerning a specific matter.

The ruling’s scope is established by the verse and its address, rather than the root’s general meaning.

7. Distinctions from related roots

Ḥ R J (ḥaraj) and Ḍ Y Q (ḍīq): constraint and narrowness

Ḍīq concerns narrowness and restriction of space, opportunity, or inward state.

Ḥaraj likewise denotes constraint and pressure.

Al-Anʿām 6:125 places the two together: ضَيِّقًا حَرَجًا.

This pairing shows that they share a semantic field.

In other uses, however, ḥaraj may also express liability, sin, or difficulty in fulfilling a duty.

Not all its uses can therefore be confined to spatial narrowness.

Ḥ R J (ḥaraj) and ʿ S R (ʿusr): difficulty and constraint

Study of ʿ S R

ʿUsr concerns difficulty and hardship in doing something.

Ḥaraj can likewise express difficulty, but also carries the image of constraint and a restricted path.

In Al-Baqarah 2:185, God expresses a will for ease rather than difficulty in the context of fasting rulings.

Al-Māʾidah 5:6 and Al-Ḥajj 22:78 mention the absence of ḥaraj.

The verses are close in semantic direction: divine rulings relate to a person’s ability, circumstances, and practical capacity to fulfil responsibility.

Yusr, ʿusr, and ḥaraj are nevertheless three different words whose distinctions should be preserved.

Ḥ R J (ḥaraj) and SH Q Q (mashaqqah): constraint and strenuous difficulty

Study of SH Q Q

Mashaqqah concerns the difficulty and suffering involved in performing an action.

Ḥaraj may encompass such suffering.

Not every difficult effort necessarily constitutes ḥaraj that makes fulfilling responsibility impossible or improper.

Conversely, ḥaraj may exist without intense physical exertion, as in inward resistance, social restriction, or lack of financial means.

Hardship and ḥaraj therefore overlap in some settings, but are not completely synonymous.

Ḥ R J (ḥaraj) and J N Ḥ (junāḥ): liability and sin

Study of J N Ḥ

Junāḥ frequently appears in Qur’anic expressions concerning the absence of sin or liability.

Ḥaraj likewise concerns lifting blame or liability in Al-Tawbah 9:91, Al-Nūr 24:61, and Al-Fatḥ 48:17.

Both families occur in Al-Nūr 24:61.

Their semantic relationship is therefore real.

Ḥaraj in Arabic also concerns dense thickets, a constricted breast, and practical difficulty, however.

Junāḥ should therefore not become a fixed equivalent for every use of ḥaraj.

Ḥ R J (ḥaraj) and ʾ TH M (ithm): constraint and sin

Study of ʾ TH M

Ithm concerns sin and wrongful conduct.

Ḥaraj can carry the same meaning in some lexical uses.

Its Qur’anic meaning is not always sin, however.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:2 and Al-Anʿām 6:125 concern inward constriction.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:6 and Al-Ḥajj 22:78, difficulty and the pressure of duty are central.

In verses of exemption, ḥaraj also concerns liability and blame.

Each verse therefore requires determining whether it concerns pressure, resistance, hardship, or sin and liability.

Ḥ R J (ḥaraj) and K L F (taklīf): constraint and responsibility

Kallafa concerns imposing work and responsibility.

Ḥaraj may express difficulty arising from fulfilling responsibility.

Al-Baqarah 2:286 relates duty to human capacity.

Al-Ḥajj 22:78 states the absence of ḥaraj in religion.

These two principles are related in meaning.

Bearing responsibility is not, however, itself identical with experiencing ḥaraj.

A responsibility may be prescribed that a person can ordinarily fulfil.

An issue arises when the person’s circumstances or the kind of limitation restrict the path to fulfilling it.

Ḥ R J (ḥaraj) and Ḥ J Z (ḥajz): constraint and obstruction

Study of Ḥ J Z

Ḥajaza concerns separating and preventing.

Ḥaraj may likewise refer to a situation in which movement and choice are restricted.

Ḥajz emphasizes the act of obstruction or the presence of a separating barrier, whereas ḥaraj may express the difficulty and pressure arising from restriction.

These families differ in their letters and should not be confused.

Nor does every barrier necessarily constitute wrongful ḥaraj. A barrier may be established to protect people.

Its rightness or wrongness requires separate assessment.

8. Challenges of interpretation and semantic development

Does absence of ḥaraj mean no hardship of any kind in religion?

No.

The Qur’an discusses responsibility, striving, patience, spending, justice, and steadfastness in truth. Some of these actions may be difficult.

At the same time, in Al-Māʾidah 5:6 and Al-Ḥajj 22:78, God states that He does not will or impose ḥaraj.

These two realities must be understood together.

Absence of ḥaraj does not eliminate every responsibility, just as responsibility does not authorize imposing every kind of pressure in religion’s name.

Each case requires examining the Qur’anic ruling, its addressees, its exceptions, and its relationship with actual human ability.

Is everything unpleasant to a person forbidden?

No.

Someone may dislike an action or find it difficult.

Unpleasantness alone does not establish that the Qur’an prohibits it.

Conversely, imposing unsupported conduct or a ruling may place a person under constraint.

Personal dissatisfaction, actual difficulty, individual caution, and a binding divine ruling therefore need to be distinguished.

This matters in discussing the permissible and forbidden: personal preference, habit, or strictness should not be turned into God’s command without evidence.

Is taḥarruj always a virtue?

Taḥarraja in Arabic can concern abstaining from something to avoid sin.

Such abstention may arise from responsibility and caution.

Personal caution is not identical with proving a general prohibition, however.

Someone may avoid a food, action, or transaction because of personal doubt.

So long as this remains a personal decision, it differs from claiming that God has prohibited the same action for everyone.

The term taḥarruj alone therefore does not guarantee the soundness of every invented restriction or strict practice.

Does ḥaraj always mean sin?

No.

Although some Arabic uses concern sin and liability, ḥaraj has a broader Qur’anic semantic range.

Al-Anʿām 6:125 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:2 concern inward constriction and pressure.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:6 and Al-Ḥajj 22:78, the subject is the absence of imposed difficulty and constraint.

Al-Tawbah 9:91 and Al-Fatḥ 48:17 concern the absence of liability and blame under specified conditions.

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:37, Al-Aḥzāb 33:38, and Al-Aḥzāb 33:50, ḥaraj concerns restrictions in particular rulings.

The Persian equivalent must therefore be chosen for each verse’s context.

Does ḥaraj mean social embarrassment?

In present-day Persian, “being in ḥaraj” or “being put in iḥrāj” sometimes means embarrassment or being placed in an uncomfortable situation.

This relates to inward pressure and constraint.

Qur’anic ḥaraj is not confined to social embarrassment, however.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:6, for example, the central subject is practical difficulty in purification.

Al-Fatḥ 48:17 concerns exemption for people in specified conditions.

The contemporary sense of embarrassment should therefore not be transferred to every verse.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

For حَرَجًا in Al-Nisāʾ 4:65, “inward constraint and resistance” is suitable.

For حَرَجًا in Al-Anʿām 6:125, “constrained and under pressure,” or “narrow and severely constricted” for the full expression, fits the context.

For حَرَجٌ in Al-Aʿrāf 7:2, “inward constraint and constriction” is suitable.

For مِنْ حَرَجٍ in Al-Māʾidah 5:6 and Al-Ḥajj 22:78, “any constraint or difficulty” is suitable under the negation.

For لَيْسَ عَلَى ... حَرَجٌ in Al-Tawbah 9:91, Al-Nūr 24:61, and Al-Fatḥ 48:17, contextual choices include “no ḥaraj rests upon him,” “no sin or blame rests upon him,” or “he bears no liability in this matter.”

Restriction and blame should be clarified in the contextual explanation: “he faces no hardship” may fail to convey the expression’s legal and practical meaning adequately.

For لِكَيْ لَا يَكُونَ عَلَى الْمُؤْمِنِينَ حَرَجٌ in Al-Aḥzāb 33:37, “so that the believers should face no constraint or restriction in this matter” is suitable.

For مَا كَانَ عَلَى النَّبِيِّ مِنْ حَرَجٍ in Al-Aḥzāb 33:38, “no constraint or blame rests upon the Prophet in this matter” is suitable.

For لِكَيْلَا يَكُونَ عَلَيْكَ حَرَجٌ in Al-Aḥzāb 33:50, “so that you should face no constraint or difficulty” is suitable.

Forqan’s word box should preserve two independent fields.

Brief root meaning: “Constraint, pressure, and restriction; being caught in difficulty or liability.”

Meaning in the verse: Depending on context, this will be “inward resistance,” “difficulty of duty,” “absence of sin and blame,” or “removal of a particular restriction.”

A fixed explanation for all fifteen occurrences would erase real differences between the verses.

10. Conclusion

The Arabic root Ḥ R J concerns constriction, density, and difficulty passing through or getting out of a situation.

In physical uses, this meaning appears in a dense thicket whose trees restrict passage.

In human uses, ḥaraj may concern inward pressure, practical restriction, difficulty fulfilling duty, or liability arising from wrongful conduct.

The root occurs fifteen times in the Qur’an.

In Al-Anʿām 6:125 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:2, ḥaraj concerns inward constriction and pressure.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:65 addresses the absence of resistance to accepting the messenger’s judgment.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:6 and Al-Ḥajj 22:78, God states that He neither wills nor places ḥaraj in religion.

In Al-Tawbah 9:91, Al-Nūr 24:61, and Al-Fatḥ 48:17, ḥaraj is lifted from people in specified circumstances.

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:37, Al-Aḥzāb 33:38, and Al-Aḥzāb 33:50, its absence concerns particular rulings and family relationships.

Thus, Ḥ R J’s central meaning is constraint and restriction, but each verse requires identifying the setting of that constraint: a person’s inner state, fulfilling responsibility, practical ability, or a ruling and liability.

This distinction matters for understanding the Qur’an’s approach to prescribing duties.

The Qur’an does not deny human responsibility, but neither does it disregard differences in people’s abilities and circumstances.

People are called to strive, act justly, and observe clear commands; means of relieving difficulty and exemption under specified conditions are also stated.

Absence of ḥaraj should therefore not become a slogan for discarding every responsibility, just as responsibility should not become unsupported constraints imposed in God’s name.

In Forqan’s translation, “constraint,” “resistance,” “difficulty,” and “absence of liability or blame” should be chosen to fit the context.

This approach preserves the root’s central meaning without reducing every occurrence to one narrow concept such as sin, embarrassment, or physical suffering.
