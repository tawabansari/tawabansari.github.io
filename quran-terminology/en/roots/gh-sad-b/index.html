Taking by force, wrongful appropriation, overriding choice, and encroaching on another’s rights

Arabic gh ṣ b concerns taking something by force, removing it from its owner’s control, and wrongfully appropriating another’s property or rights. Ghaṣb describes a person or power using coercion and dominance to take something contrary to its owner’s consent and rights.

Ghaṣaba means to take forcibly and unjustly; ghāṣib names the perpetrator; maghṣūb describes what has been wrongfully taken; and ightiṣāb denotes forcible appropriation.

This differs from taking in general. One may receive something through purchase, gift, or safekeeping with its owner’s consent. Ghaṣb involves force and infringement of another’s rights.

The root occurs once in the Quran, as ghaṣban in Al-Kahf 18:79. A king forcibly takes people’s ships. This shows that wrongful seizure is not confined to private individuals: political rulers can use their position and power to appropriate property unjustly.

1. Meaning in early Arabic

Arabic lexicons commonly define ghaṣb as taking something forcibly and unjustly.

Two features are important: taking or gaining control of something, and the coercive infringement involved in doing so.

If an owner freely sells land and the buyer receives it as agreed, that transfer is not ghaṣb.

If a powerful person occupies another’s land without right and prevents its use through threats or force, that conduct falls within this semantic field.

Force need not mean blows or physical fighting. Threats, armed power, or overwhelming authority may enable appropriation.

Early lexicons also record ghaṣabahu ʿalā al-shayʾ for compelling someone and overpowering their will.

The family therefore extends beyond taking property to imposing one’s will on another.

Historical evidence

Early Arabic lexicons, including Kitāb al-ʿAyn, define ghaṣb through wrongful, forcible taking. Later dictionaries repeat similar definitions.

Quranic ghaṣban directly attests the word in the Arabic of revelation. Its context is clear: a king uses power to take other people’s ships.

The evidence considered here does not establish a precisely dated first pre-Islamic occurrence or origin.

The wrongful, forcible taking sense is secure for the Quran’s period, but later dictionaries alone cannot fix its pre-Islamic origin.

Removing hair from a hide

Al-Azharī records another use.

Some Arabs used ghaṣabtu al-jilda for forcibly stripping hair or wool from an animal hide without first preparing it by the usual tanning methods to ease removal.

This is not appropriation of someone’s property. Forceful separation is the salient feature.

Hair loosened by preparation differs from hair pulled off by force without preparation.

The material use illustrates force and pulling something away.

Al-Azharī’s observation and report postdate the Quran. They do not prove that pre-Islamic appropriation vocabulary originated in removing hair from hides.

Nor do they make every pressure-based action ghaṣb. In social and property uses, infringement of another’s rights and control is decisive.

2. Semantic core and branches

A. Taking something against its owner’s rights

The familiar sense is taking property or something one has no right to appropriate.

The perpetrator uses power or force to remove it from the owner’s control.

Taking another’s work equipment without permission and using threats to prevent its recovery illustrates this sense.

The issue is more than the object’s movement: the owner’s rights are disregarded.

Ghaṣb need not destroy property. It can remain intact while its owner loses control and use.

This matters in the ship story: the king sought to take ships, not necessarily destroy them.

B. Taking through force and dominance

Force is not limited to physical strength.

Military, administrative, or political power may be used to overpower someone and take something contrary to their rights.

For example, threats of detention or harm to family may compel an owner to surrender property without a physical clash.

An outward handover then occurs without genuine, free consent.

Surrendering property and consenting to its transfer should therefore be distinguished.

Conversely, not every force-backed taking is wrongful appropriation. Just enforcement of an established legitimate right is not in itself the same as oppressive seizure.

The distinction concerns absence of entitlement and coercive infringement in the appropriation.

C. Compelling someone against their will

Ghaṣabahu ʿalā al-shayʾ is recorded for forcing someone into something.

The main object may be imposing one’s will rather than taking property.

Threatening someone into signing a document or acting against their wishes illustrates this.

Force overrides personal choice instead of directly appropriating an object.

This relates to coercion and compulsion, but those concepts are not identical with ghaṣb in all uses.

D. Ightiṣāb: forcible appropriation

Ightiṣāb is the form VIII verbal noun.

Ightaṣaba al-shayʾa means to take something forcibly and wrongfully, close in basic meaning to ghaṣb.

The verb can describe compulsory seizure of land, goods, or other property.

It is also used for sexual violation involving coercion and absence of consent.

In Persian, tajāvoz and sometimes ightiṣāb are known in the sexual sense, but Arabic ightiṣāb also has the broader scope of forcibly appropriating property and rights.

The familiar sexual sense should not be transferred to Quranic ghaṣban.

Al-Kahf 18:79 concerns forcibly taking people’s ships.

3. Important forms and derivatives

Recognized forms include:

  • Ghaṣaba / yaghṣibu: took something forcibly and wrongfully.
  • Ghaṣb: unjust, coercive taking or appropriation.
  • Ghāṣib: someone who forcibly appropriates another’s property or rights.
  • Ghuṣṣāb: plural of ghāṣib; wrongful appropriators.
  • Maghṣūb: something forcibly and wrongfully taken.
  • Maghṣūbah: feminine of maghṣūb.
  • Ightaṣaba / yaghtaṣibu: forcibly appropriated something against another’s consent and rights.
  • Ightiṣāb: forcible appropriation; in a specific use, coerced sexual violation.
  • Mughtaṣib: someone who commits ightiṣāb.
  • Mughtaṣab: something subjected to forcible appropriation.
  • Ghaṣabahu ʿalā al-shayʾ: compelled someone by force to do something.

Only one of these recognized Arabic forms occurs in the Quran.

Ghaṣban is an accusative verbal noun of the simple verb ghaṣaba.

The verb ghaṣaba, active participle ghāṣib, passive participle maghṣūb, and verb ightaṣaba do not occur there.

The Quranic study therefore concentrates on this single form.

The construction ghaṣban

In Al-Kahf 18:79, ghaṣban follows yaʾkhudhu.

The accusative verbal noun describes the manner of taking and can be analyzed as a verbal noun used circumstantially.

Yaʾkhudhu expresses taking itself.

Ghaṣban specifies how: forcibly and against the shipowners’ rights.

Yaʾkhudhu kulla safīnatin ghaṣban can therefore be rendered naturally as “was taking every ship by force.”

Ghaṣb here specifies the character of the action rather than simply naming an object.

4. Quranic ghaṣban

Al-Kahf 18:79

أَمَّا السَّفِينَةُ فَكَانَتْ لِمَسَاكِينَ يَعْمَلُونَ فِي الْبَحْرِ فَأَرَدْتُ أَنْ أَعِيبَهَا وَكَانَ وَرَاءَهُمْ مَلِكٌ يَأْخُذُ كُلَّ سَفِينَةٍ غَصْبًا

ammā al-safīnatu fa-kānat li-masākīna yaʿmalūna fī al-baḥri fa-aradtu an aʿībahā wa-kāna warāʾahum malikun yaʾkhudhu kulla safīnatin ghaṣbā

Al-Kahf 18:79

“As for the ship, it belonged to needy people working at sea. I wished to damage it, for beyond them was a king taking every ship by force.”

This occurs in the account of Moses accompanying one of God’s servants, whose actions he initially found incomprehensible.

In Al-Kahf 18:71 they board a ship and Moses’s companion makes a hole in it. Moses objects because the damage could endanger those aboard.

Al-Kahf 18:79 explains the action.

The ship belonged to needy people working at sea, and a king on their route was forcibly taking ships.

The damage is thus connected in the narrative with preventing the king from taking it.

Fa-kānat li-masākīn: ownership by needy people

The verse first states that the ship belonged to masākīn.

Its owners are described as needy despite possessing work equipment.

Neediness therefore does not necessarily mean possessing no property or means of production.

A fisher may own a boat but earn barely enough for food, repairs, and family needs.

Boat ownership alone does not establish wealth.

Here the ship is the working equipment of people active at sea.

Seizing it can therefore remove their means of livelihood as well as an object.

That economic consequence follows from yaʿmalūna fī al-baḥr and the wider context, not from the root alone.

A king who seized ships

Malikun yaʾkhudhu kulla safīnatin ghaṣban describes a king taking ships by force.

Malik names a ruler with governmental power.

Political power does not itself establish ownership of people’s property or entitlement to take it.

The verse distinguishes kingship from wrongful appropriation.

The king possesses power, but his treatment of ships is described as ghaṣban.

A ruler using agents to take people’s boats and work equipment without right or legitimate redress, while the owners cannot defend their possessions, illustrates the concept.

Political power becomes the means of taking others’ property.

Ghaṣb may therefore occur through abuse of governmental power as well as a strong individual overpowering a weaker one.

Conversely, not every government financial measure is ghaṣb merely because it uses public authority. Oppressive seizure differs from just enforcement of a legitimate right.

Was the king taking every ship?

The phrase is kulla safīnatin, “every ship.”

It emphasizes the broad scope of the king’s practice.

The sentence does not explicitly add “sound,” “new,” or “large.”

The damage episode suggests that an intact ship was at greater risk of seizure and a defect could prevent it.

This contextual inference should not alter the verse’s wording.

The basic translation should retain “every ship” rather than silently substitute “every sound, undamaged ship.”

Yaʾkhudhu and ghaṣban

If the verse said only yaʾkhudhu kulla safīnatin, the verb akhdh alone would not identify the kind of taking.

Taking can occur with consent or through force.

Adding ghaṣban identifies the second kind.

The words have different roles:

Yaʾkhudhu denotes taking.

Ghaṣban specifies its forcible, wrongful character.

It therefore adds a defining feature rather than merely repeating akhdh.

Does damaging the ship establish a general rule against injustice?

The story connects damaging the ship with preventing its seizure.

It does not establish that anyone may damage another’s property without their knowledge or consent and then claim to have intended to avert a greater harm.

In Al-Kahf 18:82, Moses’s companion explicitly says he did not act on his own command.

This identifies the narrative’s exceptional setting.

The explanation should not become a general authorization to interfere with property or cause harm on personal conjecture.

For this root study, the central point is the king’s wrongful, forcible taking.

5. Removing the owner’s control

Ghaṣb concerns more than physically moving property: it disregards the owner’s control.

Someone may occupy another’s land and prevent the owner from entering or using it.

Even if the land is undamaged, its owner is deprived of control and benefit.

The king might likewise preserve and use a seized ship while preventing its original owners from working with it.

Wrongful appropriation does not necessarily destroy the property.

Taking property, depriving its owner, and destroying it are distinct.

The root alone cannot establish every detail of compensation, restitution, or liability. Those belong to broader questions of rights, justice, and redress.

Ghaṣb and consent

Genuine consent of the rights-holder is absent in ghaṣb.

Lack of consent alone does not always establish it: rights may be disputed, or someone may oppose enforcement of a legitimate obligation.

In property usage, ghaṣb also involves the appropriation’s wrongfulness.

If an owner recovers property left in safekeeping from someone refusing to return it, that person’s objection alone does not make the owner a wrongful appropriator.

Conversely, an owner’s apparent consent to an unauthorized taking through threats may result from coercion.

The outward handover is not the only criterion: entitlement, freedom of choice, and manner of taking also matter.

6. Comparison with related roots

Gh ṣ b and ʾ kh dh: taking and wrongful seizure

Quranic akhdh concerns taking and has a broad range of uses.

Taking may be rightful or wrongful, voluntary or forced, material or nonmaterial.

Ghaṣb specifies taking involving force and infringement of rights.

Al-Kahf 18:79 places them together and makes the distinction clear.

Yaʾkhudhu names the action; ghaṣban describes its manner.

Every property seizure of this kind is taking, but not every taking is ghaṣb.

Gh ṣ b and s r q: forcible appropriation and theft

Sariqah commonly concerns taking another’s property without right, usually secretly.

Ghaṣb emphasizes force and dominance.

A thief may enter a house at night and take property without the owner knowing.

A powerful person may instead threaten an owner and seize possessions in front of them.

Concealment predominates in the first example; coercion in the second.

This is not an absolute boundary making theft never forceful or ghaṣb always publicly visible.

The precise use depends on circumstances and manner of taking.

Gh ṣ b and s l b: wrongful appropriation and taking away

S l b concerns taking something away from another.

Al-Ḥajj 22:73 describes false objects of worship unable to recover something a fly takes from them.

Salb here concerns taking away.

Property-related ghaṣb emphasizes forcible, wrongful appropriation.

Taking something from another by force may combine both ideas.

But not every salb is property-related ghaṣb, and ghaṣb need not involve a sudden snatching.

Gh ṣ b and q h r: overpowering and appropriation

Qahr concerns domination, overpowering, and bringing under control.

Ghaṣb may involve qahr, but in its property sense, wrongful appropriation is central.

Someone may overpower another without taking property.

Domination then exists without necessarily involving property seizure.

Using that domination to take property wrongfully introduces ghaṣb.

Al-Ḍuḥā 93:9 prohibits overpowering the orphan.

That instruction concerns treatment of the orphan and should not be restricted to taking property: qahr can include other coercive behaviour.

Gh ṣ b and ẓ l m: appropriation and injustice

Ẓulm has a broad scope, including infringement of rights, injustice, and putting people or things in a wrongful situation.

Ghaṣb is a particular unjust act involving forcible, wrongful taking.

False testimony can wrong someone without directly appropriating property.

Forcibly taking another’s land is both ghaṣb and injustice.

Ẓulm is therefore broader; ghaṣb is a specific form concerning wrongful taking and appropriation.

Gh ṣ b and gh l b: prevailing and wrongful seizure

Gh l b concerns prevailing and victory.

Not every victory is ghaṣb.

Winning a fair competition is not in itself wrongful appropriation of another’s property.

Superior power can, however, be used to seize others’ possessions.

Prevailing may enable appropriation without making the roots identical.

Gh ṣ b and gh ṣ ṣ: independent roots

These roots resemble each other in writing but are independent.

Gh ṣ b concerns forcible taking.

Gh ṣ ṣ occurs as ghuṣṣah in Al-Muzzammil 73:13, concerning something that sticks in the throat and is difficult to swallow.

Similar sound or an idea of pressure in both families does not establish historical kinship.

7. Ghaṣb within Quranic concepts

Although the root occurs once, other verses also address respecting financial rights.

Al-Baqarah 2:188 prohibits consuming one another’s property wrongfully and refers to misusing adjudication to knowingly take part of people’s wealth sinfully.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:29 also prohibits wrongful consumption of property and mentions trade by mutual consent.

These are not occurrences of gh ṣ b, but inform the Quranic principle of respecting property rights.

The king’s seizure of ships is a clear instance of forcible appropriation.

Taking with consent and legitimate entitlement is a different situation.

Power and responsibility

The perpetrator in Al-Kahf 18:79 is a king.

This matters because greater power can be used to deprive others of property.

Governmental power may serve justice and protect rights; when used for wrongful seizure, rank and office do not change the injustice.

A ruler taking fishers’ boats solely through military strength leaves the owners powerless before him.

Besides depriving individuals of property, such seizure may harm family livelihoods and wider economic activity.

Those consequences should not be added to the lexical definition. The root means forcible, wrongful taking; economic and social effects depend on the property, owners, and circumstances.

Ownership and neediness

The shipowners are described as masākīn.

Someone can be needy while owning equipment for work and daily life.

Possession of an asset alone is therefore insufficient to establish freedom from need.

Neediness also does not diminish someone’s rights over their property.

The ship belongs to those needy people, and the king’s practice of forcible seizure threatens their rights.

The narrative thus connects ownership, power, and possible abuse.

8. Difficulties in understanding and translation

Must ghaṣb involve physical violence?

No. Force may appear as threats, political dominance, or imposition of another’s will.

An owner may refrain from resisting to avert greater harm.

Outward submission is not genuine consent.

Absence of physical fighting does not by itself rule out ghaṣb.

Is every nonconsensual taking ghaṣb?

No. Entitlement and the conditions of taking also require examination.

Ghaṣb concerns wrongful, coercive taking.

Someone appropriating property without right differs from someone legitimately recovering an established right.

Consent, choice, entitlement, and manner of taking must be considered together.

Is ghaṣb restricted to movable property?

No. Arabic can use it of land, houses, ships, and other possessions.

Uses involving imposed will also show that the family is not confined to one type of property.

The specific object in Al-Kahf 18:79 is a ship; translation should not replace it with another referent without reason.

Does ghaṣb always mean secret theft?

No. Ghaṣb and theft may have similar results, depriving someone of property.

But ghaṣb emphasizes force and dominance, whereas theft concerns wrongful taking, often secretly.

The king’s power-backed seizure is more precisely described by ghaṣb than merely by “theft.”

Does ightiṣāb always mean sexual violation?

No. Arabic also uses it for forcible appropriation of property and rights.

Coerced sexual violation is one specific use.

Quranic ghaṣban concerns ships and does not carry that particular sense.

Does the verse condemn every coercive governmental action?

It describes a specific practice: a king forcibly taking people’s ships.

This does not establish that every compulsory government action is ghaṣb regardless of rights, justice, and circumstances.

Holding office and power clearly does not authorize wrongful appropriation.

Legal power, legitimate entitlement, and abuse of authority must be distinguished.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

For ghaṣban in Al-Kahf 18:79, “by force” is brief, clear, and natural.

The phrase:

«يَأْخُذُ كُلَّ سَفِينَةٍ غَصْبًا»

yaʾkhudhu kulla safīnatin ghaṣbā

Al-Kahf 18:79

“Was taking every ship by force.”

Analysis may expand this to “was forcibly appropriating every ship without entitlement, violating its owners’ rights.”

The basic translation can retain the short equivalent, with the wrongful character explained in the lexical and contextual discussion.

For other derivatives:

Ghaṣaba: took forcibly and wrongfully; appropriated unjustly.

Ghaṣb: forcible, wrongful appropriation.

Ghāṣib: someone who forcibly appropriates another’s property or rights.

Maghṣūb: forcibly and wrongfully taken.

Ightaṣaba: appropriated forcibly; in a specific use, committed coerced sexual violation.

Ightiṣāb: forcible appropriation; specifically, coerced sexual violation.

Mughtaṣib: someone committing forcible appropriation or another specified instance of ightiṣāb.

Ghaṣabahu ʿalā al-shayʾ: forced someone to do something.

Translation should distinguish taking, compelling, wrongful appropriation, and specialized senses of ightiṣāb.

Conclusion

Arabic gh ṣ b concerns forcibly and unjustly taking something and, in other uses, compelling someone against their will.

In property usage, a person without entitlement uses coercion and dominance to remove another’s property from their control.

Lexicons also record forcibly removing hair from hides. This illustrates forceful separation but does not establish the historical origin of all meanings.

The Quran uses only ghaṣban in Al-Kahf 18:79.

The ship belongs to needy people working at sea and is threatened by a king’s practice of forcibly seizing ships. The narrative does not say he actually seized this ship.

Yaʾkhudhu expresses taking; ghaṣban specifies force and infringement of the owners’ rights.

The story also shows political power enabling wrongful appropriation, potentially depriving people of work and livelihood as well as property.

The root’s meaning should not be merged with all its economic, social, and legal consequences.

Ghaṣb relates to theft, taking away, overpowering, and injustice, but none is its complete equivalent.

Its semantic core is forcibly taking or controlling something against its owner’s rights; more broadly, compelling someone by overriding their choice.

In the Quran, this concerns forcibly appropriating people’s ships. What distinguishes ghaṣb from ordinary taking is not merely transfer of property, but coercion and infringement in that transfer.
