The bucket and its movement in a well: lowering, reaching, presenting, and descending

1. Introducing the root and its semantic field

Arabic triliteral D L W concerns dalw, the vessel used to draw water from a well. Its field includes verbs for lowering and raising a bucket, bringing something closer, delivering speech or property, and descending from a height.

A bucket is attached to a rope, lowered into a well, reaches the water, and is raised. This involves distinct actions: lowering, reaching water, filling the vessel, and raising it.

Early Arabic uses different forms of D L W for these actions. Dictionaries record differing uses of adlā and dalā for lowering and raising a bucket.

The image also gives rise to nonphysical uses. Adlā bi-ḥujjatihi means presenting an argument, while adlā bi-mālihi ilā al-ḥākimi concerns delivering or presenting property to a ruler or judge.

Wells and water are no longer the subject; delivering something to another authority is central.

Tadallā is also recorded for hanging down, descending, and approaching from above. Its subject may be a person, a branch, or something else extending downward.

D L W occurs four times in three Qur’anic verses: Al-Baqarah 2:188, Yūsuf 12:19, and Al-Najm 53:8.

Yūsuf 12:19 gives the tangible use of a bucket and its lowering into a well.

In Al-Baqarah 2:188, tudlū concerns property and recourse to rulers or judges.

In Al-Najm 53:8, fa-tadallā follows danā, conveying approaching and descending.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:22 also has fa-dallāhumā bi-ghurūrin, which early lexicographers relate to drawing down and deceiving. Its morphological root is disputed, however, and the Qur’anic morphological index places it under D L L.

Distinguish the four indexed D L W occurrences from the related but disputed fa-dallāhumā.

2. Qur’anic forms and morphology

The Qur’anic morphological index records four occurrences in three verses through three types of formation.

Al-Baqarah 2:188 — wa-tudlū

A Form IV imperfect verb, second-person plural.

Its contextual sense is presenting or delivering property to rulers or judges.

Yūsuf 12:19 — fa-adlā

A Form IV perfect verb, third-person masculine singular.

Meaning: he lowered the bucket into the well.

Yūsuf 12:19 — dalwahu

The noun dalw with the possessive suffix -hu.

Meaning: the bucket belonging to him.

Al-Najm 53:8 — fa-tadallā

A Form V perfect verb, third-person masculine singular.

Meaning: descended, hung down, or approached from above, depending on context.

There are four word occurrences but three verses because Yūsuf 12:19 contains two family members.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: four occurrences in three verses

Other forms such as dalā, dālin, and dilāʾ do not occur from this root in the Qur’an.

Forms known in Arabic

Early dictionaries record these forms:

Dalw: a vessel for drawing water from a well.

Dilāʾ: a familiar plural of dalw.

Adlin: another plural, particularly for smaller numbers.

Duliyy: another plural recorded by lexicographers.

Dalā yadlū: commonly, raised a bucket from a well; in some reports, lowered it into a well.

Adlā yudlī: lowered a bucket; in some uses, delivered or presented something to another.

Idlāʾ: lowering or delivering something to another point or authority.

Dālin: someone raising a bucket or drawing water with it.

Mudlin: someone lowering a bucket; more broadly, someone presenting an argument, property, or another thing.

Dallā: lowered or suspended something.

Tadallā: hung down, descended, or approached from above.

Tadallin: descending, hanging down, or approaching from above.

Dāliyah: in some uses, a device for drawing well water and irrigating land.

Mudālāh: in some Arabic uses, gentle treatment, accommodating conduct, or cooperation to reach an aim.

These forms are not all Qur’anic, nor do they all have one fixed meaning.

In roots ending in wāw or yāʾ, some verbs are written with alif maqṣūrah. Adlā, for example, has a final alif, but that spelling alone does not turn its root into D L Y.

3. The semantic core in early Arabic sources

Ibn Fāris’s definition

Ibn Fāris gives the family this principle in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

أَصْلٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى مُقَارَبَةِ الشَّيْءِ وَمُدَانَاتِهِ بِسُهُولَةٍ وَرِفْقٍ

aṣlun yadullu ʿalā muqārabati al-shayʾi wa-mudānātihi bi-suhūlatin wa-rifqin

“A principle indicating approaching something and bringing it near with ease and gentleness.”

His principal example is the bucket and well.

The definition relates to the bucket’s movement: it is lowered toward the water, approaching it.

In reverse, it is raised from the water toward the person above the well.

Approaching and delivering something to another point are therefore important in this family.

Not every detail of bucket movement belongs in every derivative’s meaning, however.

Al-Baqarah 2:188 concerns bringing property or a financial claim before a judge, without necessarily moving vertically downward.

Ibn Fāris’s definition may illuminate relationships between uses, while each use’s direction and subject require separate determination.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: bringing near, the bucket, and the affection-share witness

Does D L W always mean downward movement?

Bucket movement in a well has two directions.

First the bucket is lowered to reach the water.

Then it is raised out of the well.

Dictionaries explain these actions through adlā and dalā.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah states:

أَدْلَيْتُ الدَّلْوَ إِذَا أَرْسَلْتَهَا فِي الْبِئْرِ، فَإِذَا نَزَعْتَ فَقَدْ دَلَوْتَ

adlaytu al-dalwa idhā arsaltahā fī al-biʾri fa-idhā nazaʿta fa-qad dalawta

“You say adlaytu the bucket when you lower it into the well; when you draw it out, you have dalawta.”

The family is therefore not restricted to downward movement.

Sources do not all report the division in the same way, however.

Al-Rāghib’s Mufradāt also records dalawtu al-dalwa for lowering and adlaytuhā for drawing out, then notes another account assigning the lowering sense.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise records both kinds of usage.

Their distinction should therefore not become an exceptionless rule for every Arabic dialect and period.

In Yūsuf 12:19, however, lowering the bucket clearly fits the narrative context.

Al-Rāghib’s Mufradāt: differing uses of dalā and adlā

Vessel, movement, and reaching

A bucket enables someone to reach water.

Someone beside a deep well may be unable to reach its water by hand. The bucket and rope bridge the distance.

This tangible use offers a semantic connection with delivering an argument, property, or request to another authority.

Something is sent toward a destination or person to obtain an outcome in both cases.

The image should nevertheless not become a certain theory of every use’s historical origin.

The evidence establishes bucket drawing and the recognized extension of some related verbs to delivering and presenting.

4. The bucket in early Arabic life

Drawing water from a well

Wells were an important water source in early Arabic life.

A vessel attached to a rope was lowered to draw out water.

It could be made from leather or other suitable materials.

Lisān al-ʿArab records masculine and feminine agreement for dalw, identifying the feminine as more common.

Its recorded plurals include adlin, dilāʾ, and duliyy.

These concern the vessel’s name and grammatical form, rather than several entirely different devices.

A poetic bucket witness

A verse attributed to Ruʾbah ibn al-ʿAjjāj says:

تَمْشِي بِدَلْوٍ مُكْرَبِ الْعَرَاقِي

tamshī bi-dalwin mukrabi al-ʿarāqī

“She walks with a bucket whose crosspieces are firmly bound.” This translates the supplied hemistich only.

Dalw appears with elements concerning its attachment and reinforcement.

In bucket terminology, ʿarāqī names parts used to reinforce the vessel and attach its rope.

A bucket was thus more than an abstract name to an Arabic listener: it was an object with a familiar construction.

Ruʾbah was an Umayyad poet; the witness should not be presented as pre-Islamic.

It remains useful evidence of classical Arabic bucket terminology.

Dāliyah as irrigation equipment

Lexicographers record dāliyah for a device of wood, palm fiber, and rope used to draw well water.

Some reports explain dāliyah as a manjanūn turned by cattle; they name nāʿūrah separately as equipment turned by water.

The family therefore also concerns simple irrigation technologies.

Dalw in Yūsuf 12:19 should nevertheless not be assumed to be part of such a large apparatus.

The verse mentions only the caravan water-drawer’s bucket.

It does not specify the vessel’s material, rope type, well construction, or precise method of bringing Joseph out.

The bucket and a person’s share

Early Arabic sometimes uses dalw figuratively for someone’s share or portion.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah transmits this verse:

آلَيْتُ لَا أُعْطِي غُلَامًا أَبَدًا
دَلَاتَهُ إِنِّي أُحِبُّ الْأَسْوَدَا

ālaytu lā uʿṭī ghulāman abadan / dalātahu innī uḥibbu al-aswadā

“I have sworn never to give a youth his portion [of affection], for I love al-Aswad.” Ibn Fāris explains al-Aswad as the poet’s son’s name.

Its explanation takes dalātahu as a share or portion of affection.

The bucket image may relate to the portion of water someone draws from a well.

Dalw does not therefore mean a right, share, or ownership in every sentence.

In Yūsuf 12:19, it is the water-drawing vessel itself.

A share or portion is one extended use of this family in Arabic.

Lisān al-ʿArab: the bucket, Ruʾbah’s witness, and irrigation equipment

5. The bucket in poetry: effort, opportunity, and outcome

Abū al-Aswad al-Duʾalī’s witness

Poetry attributed to Abū al-Aswad al-Duʾalī includes:

وَلَيْسَ الرِّزْقُ عَنْ طَلَبٍ حَثِيثٍ
وَلَكِنْ أَلْقِ دَلْوَكَ فِي الدِّلَاءِ

wa-laysa al-rizqu ʿan ṭalabin ḥathīthin / wa-lākin alqi dalwaka fī al-dilāʾi

“Provision does not come through hurried striving alone; nevertheless, cast your bucket among the buckets.”

The continuation says that the bucket sometimes emerges full and sometimes with little water or mud.

Lowering a bucket illustrates effort and uncertainty about its result.

Someone must put a bucket to work to reach water, but the amount drawn is not always the same.

The bucket can thus picture effort, seeking a share, and reaching an outcome in Arabic literary language.

Dalw itself does not mean provision, success, or failure.

Those ideas come from the comparison and the poem’s overall construction.

Abū al-Aswad belongs to early Islam. The poem is not certain pre-Qur’anic evidence, although it documents extended usage in Arabic near that period.

Al-Mufradāt: the bucket-among-buckets witness

Qays ibn al-Khaṭīm’s witness

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Qays ibn al-Khaṭīm says:

إِذَا مَا شَرِبْتُ أَرْبَعًا خَطَّ مِئْزَرِي
وَأَتْبَعْتُ دَلْوِي فِي السَّمَاحِ رِشَاءَهَا

idhā mā sharibtu arbaʿan khaṭṭa miʾzarī / wa-atbaʿtu dalwī fī al-samāḥi rishāʾahā

“When I drank four [cups], my garment trailed a line on the ground; in generosity I sent my bucket’s rope after it too.” The Ḥamāsah commentary explains the final image as completing earlier generosity.

The poet uses the bucket and rope image in a context of generosity.

Material implements become an image of giving freely.

The literary tradition could therefore use the bucket in connection with obtaining, sharing, and receiving.

The image does not make every Qur’anic use of dalw mean generosity or receiving property.

Qays was pre-Islamic, but each verse’s attribution and wording should be checked against the transmitted collection. This witness documents a familiar bucket-and-rope image in early Arabic.

Sharḥ Dīwān al-Ḥamāsah: four cups, trailing garment, and completing generosity

6. Fa-adlā dalwahu: Yūsuf 12:19

Yūsuf 12:19

وَجَاءَتْ سَيَّارَةٌ فَأَرْسَلُوا وَارِدَهُمْ فَأَدْلَىٰ دَلْوَهُ قَالَ يَا بُشْرَىٰ هَٰذَا غُلَامٌ وَأَسَرُّوهُ بِضَاعَةً وَاللَّهُ عَلِيمٌ بِمَا يَعْمَلُونَ

wa-jāʾat sayyāratun fa-arsalū wāridahum fa-adlā dalwahu qāla yā bushrā hādhā ghulāmun wa-asarrūhu biḍāʿatan wa-allāhu ʿalīmun bi-mā yaʿmalūna

“A caravan came and sent their water-drawer; he lowered his bucket and said: Good news! Here is a boy. They concealed him as merchandise; The God knows what they do.”

Two D L W words appear together:

Fa-adlā.

And:

Dalwahu.

The first verb expresses lowering.

The second noun names what was lowered.

This is a clear tangible example of the family’s usage.

Wāridahum and the water-drawer’s role

The verse introduces a caravan sending someone to obtain water.

Wārid belongs to W R D and here concerns someone sent to reach or bring water.

That person then lowers his bucket into the well.

The sequence clarifies adlā’s meaning.

It does not mean presenting an argument or paying money here.

The subject is obtaining water and the bucket.

Was Joseph inside the bucket?

The water-drawer lowers his bucket, then sees or finds a boy and cries out joyfully.

The verse does not explicitly say how Joseph reached the bucket or was brought out of the well.

A reader may connect the lowered bucket with Joseph being drawn out through the narrative image.

The details should nevertheless not be presented as explicit information contained in adlā itself.

Adlā dalwahu says only that he lowered his bucket.

It does not establish well depth, bucket shape, the number of companions, or the precise rescue method.

The bucket and Joseph’s fate

The verse continues with Joseph concealed as merchandise.

This belongs to the narrative of exploiting a person and turning him into a tradable possession.

Dalw does not itself mean human trafficking or slavery.

The bucket was used to obtain water; Joseph’s discovery follows that action in the story.

Ethical judgment about the caravan’s conduct comes from the following clauses, rather than D L W alone.

7. Wa-tudlū bihā ilā al-ḥukkāmi: Al-Baqarah 2:188

Al-Baqarah 2:188

وَلَا تَأْكُلُوا أَمْوَالَكُمْ بَيْنَكُمْ بِالْبَاطِلِ وَتُدْلُوا بِهَا إِلَى الْحُكَّامِ لِتَأْكُلُوا فَرِيقًا مِنْ أَمْوَالِ النَّاسِ بِالْإِثْمِ وَأَنْتُمْ تَعْلَمُونَ

wa-lā taʾkulū amwālakum baynakum bi-al-bāṭili wa-tudlū bihā ilā al-ḥukkāmi li-taʾkulū farīqan min amwāli al-nāsi bi-al-ithmi wa-antum taʿlamūna

“Do not consume one another’s property unjustly among yourselves, nor bring it before rulers to consume a portion of people’s property sinfully, while you know.”

Tudlū is Form IV here.

Unlike Yūsuf 12:19, its subject is not a bucket and well.

Property and rulers or judges are at issue.

The verb uses the extended sense of delivering and presenting something to another authority.

From lowering a bucket to presenting property

A bucket is lowered into a well to obtain water in the tangible use.

In the judicial use, someone may bring property, a request, or a claim before an authority to obtain an outcome.

Delivering something to another point for an aim connects the two uses.

This is a semantic image, rather than turning the ruler into an actual well or property into an actual bucket.

Neither “bucket” nor “well” belongs in this verse’s translation, since neither word appears in it.

“Bring before rulers” naturally renders tudlū bihā ilā al-ḥukkāmi here.

The referent of bihā

Bihā contains the pronoun -hā.

Its nearest explicit referent is amwālakum, “your property.”

A natural reading therefore brings property or a matter concerning property before rulers or judges.

Some early explanations relate the pronoun instead to an argument or proof: someone presents a claim before a judge.

Al-Zajjāj considers this sense.

Al-Farrāʾ and Al-Azharī prefer its connection with property because property is named explicitly whereas an argument is not.

The disagreement matters, but should not exclude either mode of corruption from the verse’s scope.

The verse concerns knowingly obtaining others’ property unjustly through recourse to rulers or judges.

This may involve bribery, false claims, concealing truth, or abusing a judge’s authority.

Each is an instance understood through the verse’s construction and purpose.

Does tudlū itself mean bribing?

Lisān al-ʿArab and other dictionaries explain adlā bi-al-māli ilā al-ḥākimi through giving property or a bribe to a ruler or judge.

Bribery is consequently an established use of this expression.

Adlā itself does not always mean bribing in Arabic, however.

Yūsuf 12:19 uses the same verb for lowering a bucket.

Adlā bi-ḥujjatihi may describe presenting an argument, which is not inherently wrong.

Bribery and corruption follow from the subject, purpose, and context of Al-Baqarah 2:188.

Li-taʾkulū farīqan min amwāli al-nāsi bi-al-ithmi identifies the wrongful aim.

Wa-antum taʿlamūna emphasizes the person’s awareness of the conduct.

Not every court application or submission of documents and evidence is therefore the conduct condemned here.

Law and justice

A significant distinction is between receiving a favorable ruling and actually having a right.

Someone may obtain an apparently lawful favorable ruling through bribery, lying, influence, or concealment.

The ruling alone does not make another person’s property rightfully theirs.

The verse condemns consuming people’s property bi-al-ithmi, “sinfully.”

Using adjudication to obtain property unjustly therefore contradicts justice.

This follows from the whole verse; D L W itself does not mean judicial corruption.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:29 likewise contrasts consuming property unjustly with trade based on mutual consent.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:58 commands returning trusts and judging justly.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:135 and Al-Māʾidah 5:8 also emphasize justice in testimony and conduct.

Recourse to adjudication should therefore serve restoring rights, rather than taking others’ possessions.

Does the verse prohibit every judicial fee?

Tudlū itself does not establish that conclusion.

A judicial system may charge transparent, fair fees for legitimate services.

Such a fee differs in purpose and subject from bribery to change a ruling or seize someone else’s property.

Al-Baqarah 2:188 principally concerns knowingly using property or judicial mechanisms to obtain others’ possessions unjustly.

Every payment associated with litigation should therefore not be labeled a bribe without examination.

Nor should every person seeking their right in court be included in the verse’s condemnation.

Al-Ṭabarī: wrongful claims, adjudication, and idlāʾ

8. Thumma danā fa-tadallā: Al-Najm 53:8

Al-Najm 53:8

ثُمَّ دَنَا فَتَدَلَّىٰ

thumma danā fa-tadallā

“Then he drew near and descended.”

Fa-tadallā is Form V.

It differs from adlā in Yūsuf 12:19.

With adlā, the agent lowers something.

With tadallā, the agent itself hangs down or descends.

Something can be lowered from a height; a person can instead descend or hang from a higher position.

The difference between danā and tadallā

Danā belongs to D N W and means approaching.

Tadallā belongs to D L W and concerns descending or approaching from above.

Their semantic fields are related, but not identical.

Danā concerns a reduction in distance.

Tadallā may highlight descending from a higher position or approaching.

Lisān al-ʿArab reports different explanations from Al-Farrāʾ and Al-Zajjāj of their relationship.

Al-Farrāʾ also explains the phrase as thumma tadallā fa-danā, allowing reordered expression with two closely related verbs. That analysis does not establish a certain physical sequence.

Al-Zajjāj emphasizes their semantic proximity, taking the expression as increased nearness.

Some readings therefore take tadallā as descent following approach; others understand reinforcement or completion of the image of nearness.

Does tadallī always mean physical descent?

In tangible use, tadallā may concern descending from a height.

A branch or fruit may, for example, hang from a tree.

Every Qur’anic use should not be assumed physically identical to a person descending on a rope.

Al-Najm 53:8 belongs to a description of revelation and seeing.

Preceding verses describe an agent as shadīdu al-quwā, “mighty in powers,” and at al-ufuq al-aʿlā, “the highest horizon.”

Nearness, revelation, and seeing follow.

The descent’s precise nature and the pronoun’s referent require examining the full context.

D L W itself does not establish whether the agent is God, an angel, or another being.

Tadallā alone establishes neither bodily form, measurable distance, nor the event’s mode of existence.

Relation to other passages

Al-Najm 53:9 says:

فَكَانَ قَابَ قَوْسَيْنِ أَوْ أَدْنَىٰ

fa-kāna qāba qawsayni aw adnā

“He was then at a distance of two bow-lengths or nearer.”

The verse continues the subject of nearness.

Qāba qawsayni is independent, however, and its entire meaning should not be placed inside D L W.

Al-Takwīr 81:19, Al-Takwīr 81:20, Al-Takwīr 81:21, Al-Takwīr 81:22, and Al-Takwīr 81:23 describe a powerful messenger, his standing, and seeing.

These may inform an independent study of the agent and event in Al-Najm.

This root study should preserve that tadallā is recorded for descending, hanging down, or approaching. It differs from danā in root and formation, although some lexicographers find the two close in meaning in this verse.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Al-Farrāʾ and Al-Zajjāj on danā and tadallā

9. Fa-dallāhumā bi-ghurūrin: Al-Aʿrāf 7:22 and disputed derivation

Al-Aʿrāf 7:22

فَدَلَّاهُمَا بِغُرُورٍ

fa-dallāhumā bi-ghurūrin

“So he drew the two of them down through deception.”

The expression belongs to Adam and his spouse’s narrative.

Satan whispers to them in the preceding verses and swears that he is a sincere adviser.

Fa-dallāhumā bi-ghurūrin then follows.

Deception and drawing down become associated here.

Disagreement over the root

The Qur’anic morphological index places fa-dallāhumā under D L L.

Lisān al-ʿArab and several early dictionaries and commentaries also discuss it under D L W.

One analysis connects dallāhumā with lowering something like a bucket.

Satan’s promises and deception lead them toward conduct with a wrongful outcome in that reading.

Another analysis relates the form to D L L and encouraging or emboldening.

Its presence in lexical D L W entries alone does not justify changing the morphological index’s count.

Its indexing under D L L likewise does not invalidate the semantic connection with dalw proposed by early sources.

Preserve the disagreement explicitly.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: fa-dallāhumā indexed under D L L

Study of D L L

Drawing down through a deceptive promise

Lowering a bucket can also supply an image explaining deception.

Some early explanations picture a thirsty person lowering a bucket into a well hoping for water, but finding none.

That image explains leading someone toward a deceptive hope.

Lisān al-ʿArab also cites Abū Jundub al-Hudhalī:

أَحُصُّ فَلَا أُجِيرُ وَمَنْ أُجِرْهُ
فَلَيْسَ كَمَنْ يُدَلَّى بِالْغُرُورِ

aḥuṣṣu fa-lā ujīru wa-man ujirhu / fa-laysa ka-man yudallā bi-al-ghurūri

“I withhold [protection] and give no refuge; but anyone I shelter is not like someone tempted by deception.” This follows the lexical explanation of aḥuṣṣu as withholding.

Yudallā bi-al-ghurūri concerns deceiving and encouraging someone to hope for something fruitless.

The connection between tadliyah and deception is therefore not merely a late reading of Adam’s narrative; it is also present in Arabic lexicographical explanations and literary witnesses.

The particular verse’s date and wording nevertheless require checking against the Hudhalī collection.

Lisān al-ʿArab: fruitless hope, a bucket in a dry well, and Abū Jundub’s witness

Does fa-dallāhumā mean physically expelling Adam from the garden?

Al-Aʿrāf 7:22 follows fa-dallāhumā bi-ghurūrin with tasting the tree and the exposure of their private parts.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:24 gives the command ihbiṭū.

Drawing down through deception in Al-Aʿrāf 7:22 and the descent command in Al-Aʿrāf 7:24 are separate statements.

A downward image in D L W does not establish that Satan personally transported Adam and his spouse bodily from the garden to earth.

The deception in verse 22 accompanies conduct they undertake after the whispering.

“Drew down through deception” can fit the context, while its precise manner and relationship with ihbiṭū require studying the complete narrative.

Relation to hal adulluka

In Ṭā Hā 20:120, Satan says:

هَلْ أَدُلُّكَ عَلَىٰ شَجَرَةِ الْخُلْدِ وَمُلْكٍ لَا يَبْلَىٰ

hal adulluka ʿalā shajarati al-khuldi wa-mulkin lā yablā

“Shall I guide you to the tree of lastingness and a dominion that does not wear away?”

Adulluka belongs to D L L, meaning guiding or showing.

It differs from adlā in Yūsuf and tudlū in Al-Baqarah.

Showing or introducing something can nevertheless serve deception in the narrative.

Distinguish guiding’s lexical meaning from whether the guidance is truthful or false.

In context, Satan claims goodwill and makes deceptive promises to lead people toward wrong conduct.

D L L does not always mean deception: guidance can be correct or incorrect.

10. Differences from related words

D L W (dalw) and D L L (dalla): delivering and guiding

These roots look similar but differ in their third letter.

D L W concerns the bucket, sending, delivering, and descending.

D L L concerns showing and guiding toward something.

Dalīl belongs to D L L.

Adlā bi-ḥujjatihi belongs to D L W, despite describing presenting an argument.

This distinction matters.

Dalīl names something helping knowledge or establishing a matter.

Idlāʾ is presenting or delivering it to an audience.

The resemblance does not make every idlāʾ presentation of a valid argument, nor every dalālah identical with bucket movement.

Study of D L L

D L W (dalw) and D N W (danā): descending and approaching

Al-Najm 53:8 places danā and tadallā together.

Dunūw means approaching.

Tadallī can describe descending or approaching from above.

Both may be present in one event without contributing identical meanings.

D N W also describes spatial, temporal, quantitative, and rank-related nearness in the Qur’an.

Qur’anic D L W concerns the bucket, presenting property, and descending.

Study of D N W

D L W (dalw) and N Z L (nazala): descending and coming down

Nuzūl concerns coming down and descending in Arabic.

Tadallā can likewise describe descent from a height.

In tangible uses, tadallā may also include an image of hanging down, downward extension, or approach.

Nuzūl has a broad range concerning rain, scripture, angels, and other things.

Their families overlap in some uses but are not complete synonyms.

“Descended” fits Al-Najm 53:8 without establishing an identical manner for every instance of nuzūl and tadallī.

Study of N Z L

D L W (dalw) and H B Ṭ (habaṭa): descending and moving to a lower position

Qur’anic hubūṭ concerns descending or moving from one position to another.

Tadallī can also concern descending.

Tangible tadallī foregrounds hanging down and approaching from above.

Hubūṭ can describe movement to a lower position.

This matters in Adam’s narrative: fa-dallāhumā bi-ghurūrin and ihbiṭū occur in separate parts.

They should not be equated without context.

D L W (dalw) and R SH W (rashā): presenting property and bribery

Tudlū bihā ilā al-ḥukkāmi in Al-Baqarah 2:188 is associated with bribery.

Bribery has an independent concept and linguistic family, however.

Someone may present property to a ruler or judge to obtain a favorable altered ruling: the action can then be bribery and corruption.

General idlāʾ is not restricted to bribery.

Adlā can also describe presenting a valid argument before an adjudicator.

Whether an action is bribery depends on its purpose and circumstances, rather than adlā alone.

D L W (dalw) and D F ʿ (dafaʿa): delivering and handing over

Adlā ilayhi bi-mālihi can concern delivering or handing property to someone.

Dafaʿa ilayhi al-māla has a similar use.

Their tangible semantic cores differ, however.

Dafʿ concerns moving aside, propelling, and transferring.

Dalw concerns the water-drawing vessel and its lowering or raising.

Tudlū in Al-Baqarah 2:188 concerns bringing property before rulers, whereas fa-idfaʿū ilayhim amwālahum in Al-Nisāʾ 4:6 concerns handing the orphans’ property over to them.

Their ethical contexts also differ: the former condemns obtaining others’ property unjustly; the latter commands delivering possessions to their owners.

Similar senses of transfer and handing over should not erase differences of context and aim.

Study of D F ʿ

D L W (dalw) and D L K (dalaka): sending and rubbing

Only the third letter differs.

D L W’s principal use concerns the bucket and sending or drawing it.

D L K concerns rubbing and friction, with solar dulūk as its Qur’anic use.

Dulūki al-shamsi in Al-Isrāʾ 17:78 belongs to D L K, not D L W.

Similar spelling does not justify explaining solar dulūk through a bucket and well.

Nor does tadallā in Al-Najm 53:8 mean solar dulūk.

Study of D L K

D L W (dalw) and D W L (dāla): the bucket and circulating turns

Qur’anic D W L concerns turns or property circulating, including Āl ʿImrān 3:140 and Al-Ḥashr 59:7.

D L W also concerns transfer and movement.

That resemblance is insufficient to unite the roots.

Dawlah and tadāwul belong to D W L.

Dalw and idlāʾ belong to D L W.

The bucket’s figurative use for a share should not make every meaning of dawlah and circulating property derive from this root.

11. Translation proposals for Forqan

Yūsuf 12:19

فَأَدْلَىٰ دَلْوَهُ

Suitable translation:

“So he lowered his bucket.”

This preserves both the lowering verb and the bucket noun.

“He lowered his bucket into the well” is also clear, but “well” is not explicit in this particular phrase and comes from context.

Forqan’s present translation is suitable and needs no change here.

Al-Baqarah 2:188

وَتُدْلُوا بِهَا إِلَى الْحُكَّامِ

Suitable translation:

“Nor bring it before rulers.”

This naturally follows the verse’s prohibitive construction and conveys delivering or presenting.

Contextual explanation can specify using property or recourse to rulers to obtain others’ possessions unjustly.

“Do not bribe” alone may excessively narrow the range of judicial abuse and wrongful claims.

“Do not present your evidence” is also unsuitable because it could imply a blanket prohibition on valid arguments.

Forqan’s present Persian “nor bring it before rulers” suits the following clauses.

Al-Najm 53:8

فَتَدَلَّىٰ

Suitable translation:

“Then he descended.”

This preserves the recognized sense of descending and approaching from above.

“Then he came nearer” deserves consideration under some lexical explanations but may obscure its distinction from danā.

Forqan’s present Persian reads:

“Then he drew near and descended.”

The two verbs remain distinguishable, which is suitable.

The descent’s manner and its agent belong in the surah’s reflection, rather than the root’s definition.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:22

فَدَلَّاهُمَا بِغُرُورٍ

Forqan’s present translation:

“So he drew the two of them down through deception.”

This fits an established lexical analysis and the narrative context.

The root listing should nevertheless note that the Qur’anic morphological index places this form under D L L, while early dictionaries also discuss it under D L W.

The verse can be examined in both root articles through cross-reference without changing D L W’s indexed count.

Non-Qur’anic equivalents

Dalw: a bucket for drawing water.

Adlā al-dalwa: lowered the bucket.

Dalā al-dalwa: commonly, raised the bucket from the well.

Adlā bi-ḥujjatihi: presented an argument.

Adlā bi-mālihi ilā al-ḥākimi: presented or delivered property to the ruler or judge.

Tadallā: hung down, descended, or approached from above.

Dālin: a water-drawer or someone raising a bucket.

Dāliyah: water-drawing or irrigation equipment, depending on usage.

Assess each equivalent through the sentence’s subject and construction.

12. Conclusion

Arabic D L W concerns the bucket and lowering it into or raising it from a well.

Someone sends a bucket down to obtain water and raises it once filled.

The field extends to delivering something to another authority, presenting arguments or property, and hanging down or descending.

Ibn Fāris explains the family through gently approaching or bringing something near. That analysis may illuminate relationships without restricting all uses to vertical bucket movement.

The root occurs four times in three Qur’anic verses.

Fa-adlā dalwahu in Yūsuf 12:19 clearly gives the tangible use of lowering a bucket.

Tudlū bihā ilā al-ḥukkāmi in Al-Baqarah 2:188 concerns delivering or presenting property to rulers. The verse condemns knowingly exploiting adjudication to obtain others’ possessions unjustly. Bribery is one clear instance, but adlā does not always mean bribing.

Fa-tadallā in Al-Najm 53:8 concerns descending or approaching from above. It follows danā, while the agent and event’s manner require the surah’s independent context.

Fa-dallāhumā bi-ghurūrin in Al-Aʿrāf 7:22 is related by early lexicographers to drawing down and deceiving. Its morphological derivation is disputed, and the Qur’anic index places it under D L L.

Preserve that disagreement in root research.

Distinguish lexical meaning from contextual ethical judgment throughout. Lowering a bucket for water is ordinary; presenting valid evidence can be legitimate; delivering property or a false claim to a judge to seize others’ possessions is condemned.

Proposed final definition: D L W’s established core concerns the bucket and lowering or raising it. Its field extends to delivering and presenting something to another point or authority, approaching, and descending. Qur’anic adlā in Yūsuf means lowering a bucket, in Al-Baqarah presenting or delivering property to rulers, and tadallā in Al-Najm descending or approaching from above. Precise meaning and value follow from morphology, prepositions, and context, rather than imposing one fixed image on every derivative. Brief root meaning: The bucket and its lowering or raising; in extended uses, delivering and presenting, hanging down, approaching, or descending.
