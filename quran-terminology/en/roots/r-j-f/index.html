The Qur’anic Root R J F (rajf)

Qur’anic and lexical forms

تَرْجُفُ
(tarjufu)
Trembles; third person feminine singular imperfect; Qur’anic form

الرَّجْفَةُ
(al-rajfah)
The tremor; Qur’anic noun, four occurrences

(al-rājifah)

Trembling, agitation, and loss of calm

Arabic R J F concerns trembling, unsettled movement, and agitation. It may affect earth, sea, trees, the body, or the human heart. Across these uses, something moves out of calm stability into trembling or unrest.

The root extends beyond physical movement. Arabic irjāf also concerns spreading reports and statements that cause anxiety, fear, and disturbance among people. Here, the subject is no longer trembling earth or bodies, but speech and information disrupting people’s calm.

R J F occurs eight times in seven Qur’anic verses in four morphological forms. These range from the trembling and destruction of earlier peoples to Resurrection and social disturbance.

Qur’anic forms: tarjufu in Al-Muzzammil 73:14 and Al-Nāziʿāt 79:6; al-rajfah in Al-Aʿrāf 7:78, Al-Aʿrāf 7:91, Al-Aʿrāf 7:155, and Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:37; al-rājifah in Al-Nāziʿāt 79:6; and al-murjifūn in Al-Aḥzāb 33:60.

Other forms, such as rajafa, rajf, rajafān, rajīf, rajjāf, irjāf, and irtijāf, are known in Arabic but do not occur in these exact forms in the Qur’an.

1. Core Meaning and Early Arabic Uses

Trembling and agitation in material things

Arabic rajafa al-shayʾu describes something beginning to tremble or move in agitation. It differs from simple displacement or orderly motion, involving a quality of unrest and trembling.

Early Arabic records rajafati al-arḍu for the earth trembling and rajafa al-baḥru for the sea’s agitation.

Related words also describe trees moving in wind, camels moving beneath loads and saddles, and a tooth wobbling when its root becomes loose.

These examples show that rajf was not confined to one movement such as an earthquake. Earth may tremble, the sea become turbulent, branches shake, and living bodies quiver.

The cause varies: sometimes wind, sometimes bodily weakness, and sometimes no cause is expressed. An external agent, direction, or specific cause is therefore not a fixed component of the root’s meaning.

Rajjāf as a description of the sea

An early witness applies rajjāf to the sea, describing the movement and turbulence of its waves.

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Maṭrūd ibn Kaʿb al-Khuzāʿī states:

وَالْمُطْعِمِينَ إِذَا الرِّيَاحُ تَنَاوَحَتْ
حَتَّى تَغِيبَ الشَّمْسُ فِي الرَّجَّافِ

wa-al-muṭʿimīna idhā al-riyāḥu tanāwaḥat / ḥattā taghība al-shamsu fī al-rajjāfi

“And those who provide food when opposing winds blow, until the sun disappears into the turbulent sea.”

Al-rajjāf denotes the sea here, with the description relating to wave movement. The sun disappearing into the sea is a poetic image of the horizon, rather than a report that it literally enters the water.

The witness illustrates early usage, although attribution of every line in this poem is not uniform. Poetic attribution should not be treated as certain without caution.

The trembling body and anxious heart

R J F extends beyond earth and sea. Rajafa al-qalbu is recorded for a heart’s agitation through fear and distress.

The subject is human anxiety: fear may bring pounding and unrest to the heart, a condition Arabic also describes within the field of trembling and agitation.

The root can thus convey both perceptible movement and inward unrest. This does not establish fear as a necessary accompaniment to every R J F: earth and sea also tremble without fear being part of their movement’s meaning.

From perceptible agitation to disturbing reports

Arabic arjafa al-qawmu is used for people circulating talk about alarming reports, war, turmoil, or frightening events.

The related arājīf is recorded for disturbing reports and rumors.

The semantic connection is clear: as movement unsettles a physical thing, circulating some reports can unsettle people’s calm.

This does not prove a particular historical development from physical trembling to rumor spreading. The evidence establishes both uses and an intelligible semantic connection.

Nor should irjāf be generalized to every unpleasant statement or alarming report. Its social use depends on the information, how it is spread, and its role in causing disturbance.

2. Al-Rajfah in Accounts of Earlier Peoples

The noun rajfah occurs four times in the Qur’an, across three stories, all describing a severe, shaking event.

Ṣāliḥ’s people and the devastating tremor

Al-Aʿrāf 7:78, concerning Ṣāliḥ and his people, states:

فَأَخَذَتْهُمُ الرَّجْفَةُ فَأَصْبَحُوا فِي دَارِهِمْ جَاثِمِينَ

fa-akhadhathumu al-rajfatu fa-aṣbaḥū fī dārihim jāthimīn

“The severe tremor seized them, and they lay fallen in their land.”

Al-rajfah here is an event that seizes Ṣāliḥ’s people and leads to their destruction.

The word’s meaning must nevertheless be distinguished from the event’s outcome. Rajfah itself denotes trembling and a severe shake; the people’s destruction is understood from the verse and story together.

Hūd 11:67 uses another expression for the same people:

وَأَخَذَ الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا الصَّيْحَةُ فَأَصْبَحُوا فِي دِيَارِهِمْ جَاثِمِينَ

wa-akhadha alladhīna ẓalamū al-ṣayḥatu fa-aṣbaḥū fī diyārihim jāthimīn

“The mighty cry seized those who had done wrong, and they lay fallen in their dwellings.”

One account uses al-rajfah and the other al-ṣayḥah.

The words are lexically distinct. Rajfah concerns trembling and agitation, while ṣayḥah concerns a cry or shout.

The Qur’an can thus describe different aspects of an event while recounting one outcome. Juxtaposing these verses does not, without further evidence, determine the event’s physical details, the order of sound and trembling, or the precise mechanism of destruction.

Shuʿayb’s people and the same expression

Al-Aʿrāf 7:91, concerning Shuʿayb, states:

فَأَخَذَتْهُمُ الرَّجْفَةُ فَأَصْبَحُوا فِي دَارِهِمْ جَاثِمِينَ

fa-akhadhathumu al-rajfatu fa-aṣbaḥū fī dārihim jāthimīn

“The severe tremor seized them, and they lay fallen in their land.”

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:37 describes the event with explicit reference to their rejection of Shuʿayb:

فَكَذَّبُوهُ فَأَخَذَتْهُمُ الرَّجْفَةُ فَأَصْبَحُوا فِي دَارِهِمْ جَاثِمِينَ

fa-kadhdhabūhu fa-akhadhathumu al-rajfatu fa-aṣbaḥū fī dārihim jāthimīn

“They denied him, so the severe tremor seized them, and they lay fallen in their land.”

Hūd also uses al-ṣayḥah concerning Shuʿayb’s people, in Hūd 11:94.

The repeated pattern in Ṣāliḥ’s and Shuʿayb’s stories shows al-rajfah describing a devastating, destructive event in these verses.

This does not establish divine punishment or human destruction as rajfah’s basic Arabic meaning. Those features arise from context. Elsewhere the root concerns the trembling of earth and mountains and, in another use, social disturbance.

The tremor that seized Moses’ seventy companions

Another al-rajfah occurs in Al-Aʿrāf 7:155, concerning Moses. Part of the verse states:

وَاخْتَارَ مُوسَىٰ قَوْمَهُ سَبْعِينَ رَجُلًا لِمِيقَاتِنَا فَلَمَّا أَخَذَتْهُمُ الرَّجْفَةُ قَالَ رَبِّ لَوْ شِئْتَ أَهْلَكْتَهُمْ مِنْ قَبْلُ وَإِيَّايَ

wa-ikhtāra mūsā qawmahu sabʿīna rajulan li-mīqātinā fa-lammā akhadhathumu al-rajfatu qāla rabbi law shiʾta ahlaktahum min qablu wa-iyyāy

“Moses chose seventy men from his people for Our appointment. When the tremor seized them, he said: My Lord, had You wished, You could have destroyed them and me before this.”

This differs significantly from the three preceding instances.

Ṣāliḥ’s and Shuʿayb’s stories follow al-rajfah with the people’s outcome and fallen condition. Here, the event affects Moses’ companions, and the text then quotes his prayer.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:155 continues with Moses asking:

أَتُهْلِكُنَا بِمَا فَعَلَ السُّفَهَاءُ مِنَّا

a-tuhlikunā bi-mā faʿala al-sufahāʾu minnā

“Will You destroy us because of what the foolish among us have done?”

The phrases together show a grave event and destruction being raised in Moses’ prayer. Al-rajfah alone, however, does not state that all his companions died then were revived, or specify the trembling’s physical nature.

Such details require independent examination of related verses and should not be imported from outside narratives into rajfah’s lexical meaning.

All four uses share a severe, agitating event, but its relationship to death, rescue, punishment, or testing emerges from each story’s context.

3. Tarjufu and Al-Rājifah in Resurrection Verses

The trembling earth and mountains

Al-Muzzammil 73:14 states:

يَوْمَ تَرْجُفُ الْأَرْضُ وَالْجِبَالُ وَكَانَتِ الْجِبَالُ كَثِيبًا مَهِيلًا

yawma tarjufu al-arḍu wa-al-jibālu wa-kānati al-jibālu kathīban mahīlā

“The day the earth and mountains tremble, and the mountains become a collapsing heap of sand.”

Tarjufu here is an imperfect verb from rajafa; the earth and mountains undergo the trembling.

The verb expresses movement and agitation. The mountains becoming a collapsing heap of sand comes from the following clause, rather than R J F itself.

This matters: translating rajf as crushing or collapse would incorporate the result described afterward into the verb’s own meaning.

The earth’s and mountains’ trembling has a direct, material sense here. Tarjufu nevertheless specifies neither movement direction nor geological mechanism.

“The day the trembling one trembles”

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:6, Al-Nāziʿāt 79:7, and Al-Nāziʿāt 79:8 state:

يَوْمَ تَرْجُفُ الرَّاجِفَةُ ۝ تَتْبَعُهَا الرَّادِفَةُ ۝ قُلُوبٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ وَاجِفَةٌ

yawma tarjufu al-rājifatu / tatbaʿuhā al-rādifatu / qulūbun yawmaʾidhin wājifah

“The day the trembling one trembles; the following one comes after it; hearts that day are fearful and trembling.”

Two R J F forms stand together: tarjufu and al-rājifah.

Tarjufu is an imperfect verb, and al-rājifah a feminine active participle serving as its grammatical subject.

Repeating the root highlights trembling, but alone determines neither the number of tremors, their duration, nor how the event unfolds.

The important question is what al-rājifah refers to.

Lexically, the form may identify a thing or event characterized by trembling. Interpretations sometimes take it as the earth or a mighty shaking event, and sometimes as the first blast of Resurrection.

These possibilities must be assessed through other verses.

Al-Muzzammil 73:14 explicitly describes earth and mountains trembling. Understanding al-rājifah in connection with the earth’s mighty shaking therefore has support among the verses.

Al-Zumar 39:68, meanwhile, describes two blowings of the trumpet involving death and rising. It can be considered when examining a connection between al-rājifah, al-rādifah, and two blasts.

The expression yawma tarjufu al-rājifah itself does not name a trumpet or blast. Identifying al-rājifah conclusively with the first blast therefore goes beyond the word’s direct meaning.

The basic translation should preserve the trembling image, leaving identification of the referent to separate explanation.

Al-rādifah in the next verse belongs to R D F rather than R J F, concerning following and coming after something. One event thus follows another, but the sequence alone does not determine either event’s physical nature.

Wājifah in the eighth verse belongs to W J F despite its similarity in sound and meaning. The hearts’ fear and agitation there should not be counted as another occurrence of R J F.

4. Al-Murjifūn: Causing Social Disturbance

The root’s most distinct Qur’anic use occurs in Al-Aḥzāb 33:60:

لَّئِن لَّمْ يَنْتَهِ الْمُنَافِقُونَ وَالَّذِينَ فِي قُلُوبِهِمْ مَرَضٌ وَالْمُرْجِفُونَ فِي الْمَدِينَةِ لَنُغْرِيَنَّكَ بِهِمْ ثُمَّ لَا يُجَاوِرُونَكَ فِيهَا إِلَّا قَلِيلًا

laʾin lam yantahi al-munāfiqūna wa-alladhīna fī qulūbihim maraḍun wa-al-murjifūna fī al-madīnati la-nughriyannaka bihim thumma lā yujāwirūnaka fīhā illā qalīlā

“If the hypocrites, those with sickness in their hearts, and those spreading disturbing reports in Medina do not stop, We will surely stir you against them; then they will remain your neighbors there only a little.”

Al-murjifūn is a sound masculine plural active participle of Form IV, arjafa.

The people here are not bodily trembling; their speech and reports cause anxiety in society.

Trembling and causing others’ agitation

In tarjufu al-arḍu, the earth itself trembles.

Al-murjifūn, however, concerns people unsettling others by circulating disturbing reports and statements.

This fits Form IV’s use and the attested Arabic meaning of irjāf.

Form IV alone does not prove that every irjāf is deliberate, organized, or motivated by a particular political intention. Those features require circumstances and evidence for each use.

In Al-Aḥzāb, the conduct’s negative character follows both from the established meaning of disturbing rumor spreading and the verse’s warning context.

Is every worrying report irjāf?

No.

Reporting a real danger, war, illness, crisis, or documented threat does not by itself make someone a murjif merely because people become anxious.

Responsible warning must be distinguished from spreading destabilizing reports.

Qur’anic al-murjifūn concerns a group whose conduct is criticized and who are told to stop. It cannot be applied indiscriminately to every critic, person giving a warning, or reporter of unpleasant news without examining truth, manner of dissemination, and the speaker’s situation.

Established social uses of irjāf emphasize baseless, false, fear-inducing reports; this aspect should not be removed from the definition. Identifying rumor, exaggeration, or disruptive reporting nevertheless requires examining reliability and dissemination. Unpleasantness alone is insufficient.

Al-Murjifūn and the verse’s other groups

Al-Aḥzāb 33:60 places three descriptions together: the hypocrites, those with sickness in their hearts, and al-murjifūn in Medina.

These need not be three entirely separate, nonoverlapping groups. The Qur’an lists characteristics and conduct receiving warning in this context.

Hypocrisy concerns a discrepancy between outward appearance and inward condition; sickness of heart is a broader description; irjāf is conduct manifest in disruptive reports and speech.

Murjif here therefore describes conduct and should not automatically be equated with every hypocrite or opponent.

5. Comparison with Related Roots

R J F (rajf) and R J J (rajj)

Study of R J J

These roots are close in describing movement and trembling.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:4 states:

إِذَا رُجَّتِ الْأَرْضُ رَجًّا

idhā rujjati al-arḍu rajjā

“When the earth is shaken violently.”

Al-Muzzammil 73:14 states:

يَوْمَ تَرْجُفُ الْأَرْضُ وَالْجِبَالُ

yawma tarjufu al-arḍu wa-al-jibāl

“The day the earth and mountains tremble.”

Rajj clearly concerns shaking and setting in motion; rajf emphasizes trembling and agitation arising in something.

The two verses also differ syntactically. Al-Wāqiʿah uses a passive verb, with the earth as its grammatical subject; Al-Muzzammil makes earth and mountains the active verb’s subject.

This should not become an absolute rule that R J J always describes only an external agent’s action, while R J F always describes spontaneous motion. Arabic evidence does not support such a rigid boundary.

Both roots describe shaking and trembling, while R J F also has a clear Arabic and Qur’anic use involving disruptive reports and social anxiety.

R J F (rajf) and Z L Z L (zalzalah)

Study of Z L Z L

Al-Zalzalah 99:1 states:

إِذَا زُلْزِلَتِ الْأَرْضُ زِلْزَالَهَا

idhā zulzilati al-arḍu zilzālahā

“When the earth is shaken with its shaking.”

Zalzalah here concerns the earth’s trembling and is close to rajf applied to the earth.

However, Arabic R J F also applies to trembling bodies, hearts, the sea, and disturbing reports. It cannot therefore always be translated as earthquake.

“Earthquake” is suitable for some al-rajfah uses in the earlier peoples’ stories, but conveys the wrong meaning for al-murjifūn.

R J F (rajf) and Ṣ Y Ḥ (ṣayḥah)

Study of Ṣ Y Ḥ

As seen in Ṣāliḥ’s and Shuʿayb’s stories, the Qur’an sometimes describes one outcome through al-rajfah and sometimes through al-ṣayḥah.

The former concerns trembling and agitation; the latter a cry or shout.

Their proximity in narrating an event does not make their lexical meanings identical. Nor does sound accompanying many violent tremors prove every rajfah inherently a ṣayḥah.

The distinction between a word’s meaning and an event’s full set of features must be maintained here too.

6. Translation Proposals for Forqan

One fixed equivalent should not be imposed on every derivative.

Rajafa: trembled; began shaking; became agitated.

Rajf: trembling, agitation, unsettled movement.

Rajfah: a tremor or violent trembling; earthquake is suitable in some contexts.

Tarjufu: trembles; begins shaking.

Al-rājifah: that which trembles; the trembling one. Identifying its referent in Al-Nāziʿāt 79:6 requires context.

Arjafa: caused disturbance; circulated alarming reports.

Al-murjifūn: spreaders of destabilizing rumors; people causing anxiety through disruptive reporting.

For Al-Aḥzāb 33:60, “those spreading destabilizing reports” or “spreaders of alarming rumors” better reflects the meaning than simply “rumor makers,” preserving both their speech activity and its unsettling effect.

For Al-Aʿrāf 7:78, Al-Aʿrāf 7:91, and Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:37, “the severe tremor seized them” is suitable.

In Al-Muzzammil 73:14, “the earth and mountains tremble” is direct and natural.

In Al-Nāziʿāt 79:6, “the day the trembling one trembles” preserves the form, though the Persian equivalent needs explanation. The interpretive rendering “the day the mighty trembling occurs” is smoother but no longer reproduces the whole wording. Basic translation and explanation of the referent should therefore remain distinct.

7. Conclusion

R J F centers on trembling, agitation, and unsettled movement. Material uses range from moving earth and sea to trembling trees and bodies.

Qur’anic al-rajfah describes a severe event in the stories of Ṣāliḥ, Shuʿayb, and Moses’ companions. Destruction, punishment, or testing are not the word’s own meaning; they arise from each story’s context.

In Resurrection verses, tarjufu describes earth and mountains trembling, while al-rājifah identifies something or an event through trembling; its precise referent requires examination of related verses.

Al-murjifūn in Al-Aḥzāb illustrates the family’s social branch: people unsettling others’ calm and confidence through destabilizing reports. This does not extend to every worrying report, disagreement, or evidence-based warning.

Final definition: R J F concerns trembling, unrest, and agitation. These may affect bodies and the environment; irjāf can concern unsettling people through disruptive reports. Each use’s intensity, cause, result, and ethical character must be determined from its form and context, rather than one broad definition imposed on every derivative.
