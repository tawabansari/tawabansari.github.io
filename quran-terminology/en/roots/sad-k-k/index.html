Collision and striking: from forceful contact between bodies to striking the face

1. Introducing the root and its forms

The Arabic root Ṣ K K concerns things colliding, striking, and contact involving pressure or force. Its principal use appears in ṣakka: he struck something or collided with it.

Sometimes this is an explicit blow, such as striking with a hand. Elsewhere two bodies meet, like knees touching during walking or running. The family also describes bringing parts together and closing them firmly, as with shutting a door.

Ṣ K K occurs only once in the Qur’an:

فَصَكَّتْ (faṣakkat) — then she struck; Al-Dhāriyāt 51:29.

This is a Form I perfect verb, third-person feminine singular. The initial fa- connects the event to the preceding clause. The doubled kāf reflects the geminate root: its final two radicals are identical.

Established Arabic forms include:

  • صَكَّ (ṣakka): struck, delivered a blow, or collided with something.
  • يَصُكُّ (yaṣukku): strikes.
  • صَكّ (ṣakk): a blow or collision.
  • اِصْطَكَّ (iṣṭakka): collided; reciprocally, two things met.
  • صَكَك (ṣakak): a condition in which knees or parts of the legs come close or meet during movement.
  • أَصَكّ (aṣakk): describing a person or animal with that leg or knee condition.
  • مِصَكّ (miṣakk): in some early Arabic uses, a strong, stout person or animal.
  • مِصَكّ (miṣakk): also, in another use, a device for securing a door.

These forms do not all occur in the Qur’an. Its sole Qur’anic form is faṣakkat.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: one Form I verb in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:29

2. The semantic core and early Arabic usage

Two things meeting forcefully

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris describes Ṣ K K’s core as two things meeting with force and intensity, as though one strikes the other.

This explains the relationship among several principal uses.

A hand striking something creates contact. Knees meeting during movement likewise involve contact. With a door closing firmly, parts can also meet forcefully.

The core must nevertheless be distinguished from each use’s particular features.

Not every collision is an injurious blow, nor is every blow an accidental collision. Ṣakka in a sentence can have a specified agent, direction, and object.

Lisān al-ʿArab records two explanations of ṣakk: a forceful blow with something broad, and, more generally, striking with anything.

This variation shows that Arabic usage does not invariably restrict the word to one instrument or manner of striking.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: forceful, intense contact

Striking with the hand

One clear use of ṣakka concerns striking with the hand.

Lexicons record ṣakka wajhahu for striking someone’s face, sometimes explaining it through laṭamahu: he struck his face with his hand.

Here the hand can be the instrument. Ṣakka alone nevertheless does not establish whether an open palm, closed fist, or fingertips were used.

Such details require context or an independent explanation.

The blow’s intensity can also vary. Sometimes it may be forceful and injurious; elsewhere a brief movement expresses surprise, distress, or a sudden reaction.

Forceful contact belongs within the root’s semantic field, but the exact force and consequences cannot be established from the verb without evidence.

An early poetic witness

Lexicons transmit lines attributed to Mudrik ibn Ḥiṣn al-Asadī:

يَا كَرَوَانًا صُكَّ فَاكْبَأَنَّا
فَشَنَّ بِالسَّلْحِ فَلَمَّا شَنَّا

yā karawānan ṣukka fa-akbaʾannā / fa-shanna bi-al-salḥi fa-lammā shannā

A rendering close to the sense:

“O bird struck and drawn in upon itself, scattering droppings in fright and agitation.”

The accompanying explanation describes a bird struck by a bird of prey and reacting to defend itself.

Ṣukka here denotes an actual blow delivered to the bird from outside.

The witness matters because ṣakka describes more than a hand striking a face: it can concern a blow between living creatures.

Early lexicons attribute these lines to Mudrik ibn Ḥiṣn, but their date is not independently established. They can therefore be cited as a transmitted early Arabic witness without declaring them certainly pre-Islamic.

Lisān al-ʿArab, K R A: Mudrik ibn Ḥiṣn’s witness and the bird of prey’s blow

Bodies colliding: iṣṭikāk

Iṣṭakka belongs to this family.

In iṣṭakka al-shayʾāni, two things collide.

This construction describes knees, feet, and teeth. For instance, Arabic can use iṣṭakkat asnānuhu when a person’s teeth chatter from cold or trembling.

There need not be a conscious agent deciding to strike something.

Contact can result from bodily movement or a natural bodily condition.

Morphologically, iṣṭakka is Form VIII. The form’s tāʾ becomes ṭāʾ alongside ṣād.

The verb shows that Ṣ K K describes mutual contact as well as an agent delivering a blow.

Ṣakak: knees meeting

Lexicons use ṣakak for a condition in which knees or parts of the legs meet during movement.

A person or animal with this condition may be called aṣakk.

The word is recorded for humans, camels, horses, and certain birds.

Some birds, for example, are described as having legs positioned so that their knees come close and touch during movement.

This differs from deliberate striking but remains related to the core of two things meeting.

Ṣakak should not, however, be directly converted into a modern medical diagnosis. Some early descriptions may resemble recognized limb conditions, but the Arabic word alone does not name one particular disease.

Shutting a door firmly

Arabic records ṣakka al-bāba for shutting a door.

Some explanations emphasize firm, forceful closure.

Its relation to contact is clear: the moving door meets its frame and settles into place.

This does not mean every Arabic use of ṣakka necessarily involves loud noise or violence.

The established meaning here is shutting the door; the precise manner follows the sentence and context.

Ṣakkah: intense midday heat

Early Arabic expressions such as ṣakkata ʿUmayy use ṣakkah in connection with intense midday heat.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah explains the expression through an account of two blind people colliding.

Other sources transmit stories concerning a person named ʿUmayy and an incident in the midday heat.

These explanations are lexicographers’ attempts to clarify the expression rather than conclusive histories of its emergence.

What can confidently be said is that the expression concerned intense midday heat. An origin story should not be accepted as established history without independent historical support.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: contact, knees, shutting doors, and midday heat

3. The Qur’anic use: faṣakkat wajhahā

The sole Qur’anic occurrence appears in the account of Abraham and his wife:

فَأَقْبَلَتِ امْرَأَتُهُ فِي صَرَّةٍ فَصَكَّتْ وَجْهَهَا وَقَالَتْ عَجُوزٌ عَقِيمٌ

fa-aqbalati imraʾatuhu fī ṣarratin fa-ṣakkat wajhahā wa-qālat ʿajūzun ʿaqīmun

“Then his wife came forward with a cry, struck her face, and said: An old woman, barren!”

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:29

In the preceding verses, divine messengers announce to Abraham the birth of a knowledgeable son. His wife responds to the news with a cry, strikes her own face, and speaks of her old age and barrenness.

The meaning of faṣakkat

Faṣakkat derives from ṣakka.

Its subject is Abraham’s wife, and its direct object wajhahā: her own face.

The initial fa- connects the verb to the preceding event and indicates the sequence of reactions.

The sentence’s construction is therefore clear: Abraham’s wife struck her face.

That rendering is lexically precise and natural. It does not require adding the hand used, the blow’s force, or a particular part of the face.

Some early explanations describe her striking her forehead with her hand; others mention striking the face with the palm.

These explain how the movement occurred, whereas the verse itself says only wajhahā.

Rendering it she struck her forehead or she slapped her cheek with her palm therefore introduces details beyond the original wording, although these are imaginable forms of the action.

Was the movement violent?

Ṣakka can denote a forceful blow, but this verse does not describe one person striking another.

Abraham’s wife responds to surprising news and strikes her own face.

In context, the movement is therefore chiefly related to expressing surprise and reacting suddenly.

The general meaning of striking does not establish an intention to injure herself or a severe, dangerous blow.

Conversely, the verb’s physical meaning must not disappear through rendering faṣakkat simply as she was surprised or she became distressed.

The act stated in the verse is striking the face; surprise and the emotional condition follow from the surrounding context.

The verse also reports an event. Describing such a reaction does not itself yield a general ruling approving or disapproving every similar movement.

Al-Ṭabarī: striking the face or forehead and responding to the announcement

Relating fī ṣarratin to faṣakkat

Fī ṣarratin precedes faṣakkat in the verse.

Ṣarrah derives from Ṣ R R, rather than Ṣ K K.

Here it concerns a cry or sound accompanying Abraham’s wife’s reaction.

The verse thus uses two distinct expressions: first the cry, then striking the face.

These behaviors belong to one scene, but crying out must not be assigned to Ṣ K K’s meaning.

Translating faṣakkat only as she cried out would remove the blow and erase the distinction between the expressions.

Study of Ṣ R R

Relating the action to ʿajūzun ʿaqīmun

At the verse’s end, Abraham’s wife says:

عَجُوزٌ عَقِيمٌ

ʿajūzun ʿaqīmun

“An old woman, barren!”

The phrase clarifies the circumstances of her surprise.

She describes herself as old and barren after hearing the announcement of a child’s birth.

Her movement therefore occurs in response to unexpected news.

ʿAjūz derives from ʿ J Z and ʿaqīm from ʿ Q M. They describe Abraham’s wife’s condition rather than Ṣ K K’s meaning.

Surprise, old age, and barrenness consequently help explain the action without being lexical meanings of ṣakka.

4. The same event elsewhere in the Qur’an

The scene can be compared with other Qur’anic accounts of the announcement of a child to Abraham to clarify the circumstances of faṣakkat wajhahā.

Hūd says:

وَامْرَأَتُهُ قَائِمَةٌ فَضَحِكَتْ فَبَشَّرْنَاهَا بِإِسْحَاقَ وَمِنْ وَرَاءِ إِسْحَاقَ يَعْقُوبَ

wa-imraʾatuhu qāʾimatun fa-ḍaḥikat fa-bashsharnāhā bi-isḥāqa wa-min warāʾi isḥāqa yaʿqūba

“His wife was standing, and she laughed; then We gave her good news of Isaac, and after Isaac, Jacob.”

Hūd 11:71

The next verse quotes her own words:

قَالَتْ يَا وَيْلَتَىٰ أَأَلِدُ وَأَنَا عَجُوزٌ وَهَٰذَا بَعْلِي شَيْخًا إِنَّ هَٰذَا لَشَيْءٌ عَجِيبٌ

qālat yā waylatā a-alidu wa-anā ʿajūzun wa-hādhā baʿlī shaykhan inna hādhā la-shayʾun ʿajībun

“She said: Alas for me! Shall I give birth while I am an old woman and this husband of mine is an old man? This is indeed a surprising thing.”

Hūd 11:72

This account expresses her surprise more explicitly.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:29 describes the situation through a cry, striking the face, and saying ʿajūzun ʿaqīmun.

Hūd 11:72 gives her question about bearing a child in old age, and she herself calls the news a surprising thing.

The accounts help clarify her emotional state. Striking her face can therefore be understood as a response to highly unexpected news.

Later in Hūd, the messengers also say:

أَتَعْجَبِينَ مِنْ أَمْرِ اللَّهِ

a-taʿjabīna min amri allāhi

“Are you surprised at The God’s command?”

Hūd 11:73

Her surprise is therefore directly confirmed within the network of verses.

None of these verses, however, specifies which hand struck, which part of the face was struck, or how much force was used.

The direct rendering she struck her own face is consequently more precise than reconstructing details of the movement.

5. Non-Qur’anic uses and their distinctions

Ṣakk as a document or financial instrument

Arabic also uses ṣakk for a deed, financial document, or written instrument recording a right.

Its plurals include ṣukūk and ṣikāk.

In later periods it was also used for documents concerning claims, payment, property, and transactions.

Its presence in Arabic lexicons nevertheless does not establish that financial documents derive from the meaning of striking.

Lisān al-ʿArab, citing Abū Manṣūr al-Azharī, identifies ṣakk meaning a document as an Arabicized loanword, tracing it to Persian jakk.

The established Arabic usage must therefore be distinguished from the word’s historical origin.

The identical form of documentary ṣakk and the verbal noun of ṣakka, to strike, does not by itself establish a shared derivation.

Documents, contracts, recording rights, and financial instruments should therefore not enter the definition of Qur’anic Ṣ K K.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: documentary ṣakk as an Arabicized loanword

Ṣakk and contact between the legs

Ṣakak is also recorded for knees coming close and meeting.

Unlike the financial document, this use has a clear semantic connection with two things coming into contact.

Knees may meet during movement without any deliberate intention to strike.

The family’s contact meaning can therefore accompany both voluntary action and involuntary movement.

Ṣakk and bodily strength

Some early uses apply miṣakk or related forms to a strong, stout person or animal.

These uses have been associated with bodily solidity and strength.

They do not establish that every Ṣ K K derivative means strength, violence, or physical power.

Faṣakkat wajhahā in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:29, for example, describes an action rather than Abraham’s wife’s strength or bodily characteristics.

6. Comparing related roots

Ṣ K K (ṣakka) and Ḍ R B (ḍaraba)

Ḍaraba has a wide Arabic semantic field, including striking, delivering a blow, and nonphysical or idiomatic uses such as setting forth an example.

Ṣakka in its principal use is more closely associated with striking and things colliding.

Ḍ R B occurs in various Qur’anic constructions, including:

يَضْرِبُونَ وُجُوهَهُمْ وَأَدْبَارَهُمْ

yaḍribūna wujūhahum wa-adbārahum

“They strike their faces and their backs.”

Al-Anfāl 8:50

The subject here is striking faces and backs.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:29 instead uses faṣakkat wajhahā.

Both verbs can express striking, but this overlap does not make all their uses identical.

Ḍaraba can appear in constructions without any physical contact being intended, whereas the Qur’anic use of ṣakka concerns an actual blow and contact.

Study of Ḍ R B

Ṣ K K (ṣakka) and L Ṭ M (laṭama)

Laṭama generally concerns striking with the hand, particularly on the face.

Lexicographers sometimes explain faṣakkat wajhahā through laṭamat wajhahā.

This closeness shows that she struck her own face can convey Abraham’s wife’s movement in the verse’s context.

Ṣakka nevertheless extends beyond a palm striking a face: knees colliding and a door closing also belong to its family.

Laṭama can therefore explain one use of ṣakka without replacing the entire family’s definition.

Ṣ K K (ṣakka) and Ṣ D M (ṣadama)

Ṣadama concerns something colliding with or striking something else.

In physical uses it approaches Ṣ K K, since both can describe bodies colliding.

That proximity does not make the roots or all their derivatives identical.

Ṣakka clearly covers hand blows, mutual contact, and knees meeting; ṣadama prominently expresses collision and impact.

This is a tendency rather than an absolute rule for every Arabic form.

Ṣ K K (ṣakka) and S K K (sakka)

Ṣ K K and S K K resemble one another in writing and sound but are distinct roots.

A doubled kāf in both does not justify transferring one family’s meaning to the other.

Root studies must distinguish ṣād from sīn and examine each derivative through its own family’s evidence.

Likewise, documentary ṣakk’s spelling does not prove a historical connection between these roots or between all their uses.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

In its Qur’anic use, ṣakka concerns striking.

Ṣakka: struck, delivered a blow; according to object and context, collided with something.

Faṣakkat: then she struck; in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:29, then she struck her own face.

Iṣṭakka: collided; in reciprocal usage, two things met.

Ṣakak: the condition of knees or parts of the legs coming close or meeting during movement.

Ṣakka al-bāba: shut the door; where intensity is intended, shut the door firmly.

Ṣakk: a blow or collision; in the separate Arabicized loanword sense, a deed or financial document.

For Al-Dhāriyāt 51:29, the current Forqan rendering is:

“Then his wife came forward with a cry, struck her face, and said: An old woman, barren!”

This is suitable because it preserves faṣakkat’s action without guessing the instrument or force of the blow.

The lexical explanation can place the movement within her surprise at the announcement of a child. She was surprised should nevertheless not replace the act of striking.

8. Conclusion

Ṣ K K concerns things colliding and striking. Its established uses cover forceful contact, bodies meeting, firmly shutting a door, and parts of the legs meeting during movement.

The Qur’an uses only faṣakkat. In the account of Abraham’s wife, it describes her striking her own face.

The context places this movement in her response to the announcement of a child despite old age and barrenness. Hūd 11:71, Hūd 11:72, and Hūd 11:73 further clarify her surprise.

Ṣ K K itself nevertheless means neither surprise nor grief: it states the action performed in those circumstances.

Arabic also has ṣakk meaning a financial document, but lexicons describe it as an Arabicized loanword. It should therefore not be presented as a branch of striking without historical evidence.

Brief root meaning for Forqan:

Two things colliding, striking, or coming into contact with force; in other uses, parts of a body meeting or things coming firmly together.

Meaning in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:29:

فَصَكَّتْ (faṣakkat): then she struck her own face; in context, Abraham’s wife’s reaction to the unexpected announcement of a child.
