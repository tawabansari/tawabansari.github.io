1. The root in early Arabic

The Arabic root ك ر ه concerns disliking, finding something objectionable, hardship, reluctance, and compelling another to act against their will.

Ibn Faris places its basic meaning opposite satisfaction and love. When people dislike something, they lack inclination, acceptance, or satisfaction toward it.

Early Arabic also used كَرِيهَة for distressing events, particularly the hardship and intensity of war.

A poem attributed to al-Samaw’al, traditionally classified among pre-Islamic poets, describes a warrior:

فِي كَفِّهِ مُرْهَفُ الْغِرَارِ إِذَا أَهْوَى بِهِ مِنْ كَرِيهَةٍ رَسَبَا

In his hand is a keen-edged sword: when he brings it down in battle, it sinks deep.

كَرِيهَة here denotes a harsh, unwelcome encounter in battle, a situation difficult to endure.

This witness from poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period illustrates the family’s use for unwelcome, difficult circumstances, although its precise date of composition has not been independently established.

Arabic lexicons also record:

  • كَرِهَ الشَّيْءَ: he disliked the thing.
  • فَعَلَهُ عَلَى كُرْهٍ: he did it reluctantly or with hardship.
  • أَكْرَهَهُ عَلَى الْأَمْرِ: he compelled him to do it against his will.
  • كَرَّهَ إِلَيْهِ الشَّيْءَ: he made the thing displeasing to him.
  • أَمْرٌ كَرِيهٌ: an unwelcome or objectionable matter.

These uses connect disliking something, enduring its difficulty, and being compelled to do it, but these three conditions are not identical.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important Arabic forms include:

  • كَرِهَ ـ يَكْرَهُ: to dislike; find objectionable.
  • كَرْه: reluctance; unwillingness; compulsion.
  • كُرْه: unpleasantness; difficulty; hardship.
  • كَرَاهَة and كَرَاهِيَة: displeasure and dislike.
  • كَارِه: someone who dislikes something or is unwilling.
  • كَارِهُون and كَارِهِين: plural forms.
  • كَرِيه: unpleasant or objectionable.
  • كَرِيهَة: a distressing event; the hardship of battle.
  • مَكْرُوه: something disliked.
  • مَكَارِه: unwelcome, difficult things.
  • كَرَّهَ: to make displeasing.
  • تَكْرِيه: making something displeasing.
  • أَكْرَهَ: to compel another against their will.
  • إِكْرَاه: coercion; compulsion against another’s will.
  • مُكْرِه: one who compels.
  • مُكْرَه: someone compelled.
  • اِسْتَكْرَهَ: to dislike, or compel another.

Qur’anic forms examined:

كَرِهَ ـ كَرِهُوا ـ كَرِهْتُمُوهُنَّ ـ كَرِهْتُمُوهُ ـ تَكْرَهُوا ـ يَكْرَهُونَ ـ كَرَّهَ ـ تُكْرِهُ ـ أُكْرِهَ ـ أَكْرَهْتَنَا ـ تُكْرِهُوا ـ يُكْرِهْهُنَّ ـ كُرْهٌ ـ كُرْهًا ـ كَرْهًا ـ كَارِهُونَ ـ كَارِهِينَ ـ مَكْرُوهًا ـ إِكْرَاهَ ـ إِكْرَاهِهِنَّ

كَرَاهَة, كَرَاهِيَة, and كَرِيهَة do not occur in the Qur’an in these nominal forms, though related meanings appear through other derivatives.

3. Core meaning

The core concerns lack of inclination or consent, dislike, or encountering circumstances contrary to one’s will.

People may dislike something because of its difficulty, harm, ugliness, or conflict with their desires.

Disliking an action does not necessarily make it wrong. A patient may dislike bitter medicine even when it benefits their health.

A person may also dislike an action yet freely choose it, recognizing its necessity and benefit.

This differs from coercion, where another applies pressure or threats to make a person act against their will and choice.

Three concepts must therefore be distinguished:

  • Disliking something.
  • Enduring difficulty or acting despite reluctance.
  • Being compelled to act against one’s will.

This distinction is especially important in Qur’anic usage.

The difference between كَرْه and كُرْه

Arabic lexicographers do not wholly agree about these forms. Some regard them as variants of one word; others distinguish their meanings.

Al-Farra associates كُرْه, with damma, more with hardship a person endures, and كَرْه, with fatha, with an action another compels them to perform.

The Qur’anic usages in Hafs’s transmission from ʿAsim largely accord with this distinction.

كُرْه appears in al-Baqarah 2:216 concerning the unpleasantness of fighting and in al-Ahqaf 46:15 concerning pregnancy and childbirth.

كَرْه, by contrast, appears in al-Nisa 4:19 concerning inheriting women against their will, and in several other verses opposite طَوْع.

Nevertheless, recognized reading variants and recorded Arabic usages prevent treating this as an absolute rule. Context determines the precise meaning.

4. Qur’anic usages

كُرْه: unpleasantness and hardship

Here كُرْه is a noun, and تَكْرَهُوا an imperfect verb from the same family.

War involves danger, injury, loss of life and property, and considerable hardship. Disliking war is therefore a natural human response.

The verse distinguishes a person’s feeling about something from its actual value.

Someone may recognize the necessity of defending themselves and others while being unhappy about war. Disliking war does not contradict accepting the need for defense in particular circumstances.

Conversely, people may love something for apparent pleasure, profit, or comfort while its consequences harm them.

Love and dislike alone are therefore not conclusive measures of good and evil.

Al-Anfal 8:5 describes some believers as unhappy about going out under the circumstances. Disliking a difficult situation is not, by itself, evidence of disbelief or hypocrisy.

كُرْه: the hardship of pregnancy and childbirth

كُرْهًا occurs twice here for the difficulties a mother endures during pregnancy and childbirth.

This is important lexical evidence: a mother may love her child while pregnancy and delivery involve pain and hardship.

Love for a child and the hardship of bringing that child into the world are distinct and can coexist.

“With hatred” or “under compulsion” is therefore unsuitable here. The verse concerns the hardship the mother endured.

كَرْه: depriving women of choice

Three forms from this family occur in the verse:

  • كَرْهًا: against the women’s will and consent.
  • كَرِهْتُمُوهُنَّ: if you dislike them.
  • تَكْرَهُوا: you dislike something.

The first concerns depriving women of agency. A woman is not property whose life and future others may determine against her will after her husband’s death.

The other two concern a man’s feeling of dislike toward his wife.

The Qur’an distinguishes dissatisfaction from wrongful conduct. Disliking someone does not authorize disregarding their dignity, rights, or choice.

طَوْعًا وَكَرْهًا: willingly or unwillingly

طَوْعًا and كَرْهًا describe two different conditions of submission.

People can accept divine command knowingly and voluntarily; whether believing or denying, however, they remain within divine sovereignty and the laws governing creation.

No one escapes death merely by disliking it. Likewise, denying The God does not make a person independent of the laws governing the body, earth, and creation.

Submission to the created order is not identical with faith and conscious acceptance of religion.

These verses concern divine sovereignty over creation, not human authority to impose beliefs upon one another.

Willing and reluctant giving

The same contrast between willingness and reluctance concerns spending here.

The next verse explains why their spending is not accepted:

كَارِهُونَ describes the givers’ inner state while spending.

The value of an action is not determined solely by its appearance. Even willingness alone does not guarantee acceptance.

Nor can these verses be generalized to everyone who finds a financial duty difficult. The Qur’an explains this group’s rejection through a combination of their conduct and positions.

إِكْرَاه: religious coercion

إِكْرَاه is the verbal noun of form IV, meaning to compel another against their will and choice.

The opening لَا is the particle of categorical negation, and إِكْرَاهَ its noun. The construction negates coercion within the sphere of religion.

The Qur’an immediately speaks of sound direction becoming distinct from error. Religious acceptance proceeds through understanding, awareness, and choice, rather than pressure and compulsion.

If threats to life, property, or social standing cause someone to utter words, the outward statement does not establish inward belief.

Yunus expresses the principle as a direct question:

تُكْرِهُ is an imperfect form IV verb. Addressed to Muhammad, the question clarifies the boundary between conveying a message and imposing belief.

If The God’s Messenger himself is not authorized to compel belief, no one can claim such authority in The God’s name.

Freedom of acceptance in Noah’s call

كَارِهُونَ describes people lacking inclination and acceptance toward what Noah has brought.

أَنُلْزِمُكُمُوهَا, from ل ز م, concerns obliging and imposing.

Despite possessing clear evidence, Noah does not treat the truth of his message as permission to impose it on others.

By contrast, in al-Aʿraf 7:88, the arrogant leaders of Shuʿayb’s people threaten him and his fellow believers with expulsion unless they return to the leaders’ creed. Shuʿayb replies by raising their unwillingness.

The two contexts distinguish inviting others to truth from imposing belief through power.

أُكْرِهَ: coerced behavior and inner conviction

أُكْرِهَ is a passive perfect form IV verb.

The verse distinguishes someone acting under pressure from someone inwardly embracing disbelief.

A coerced person’s outward behavior does not necessarily reflect inward conviction.

This is a central witness to the connection between choice and responsibility. The Qur’an exempts the coerced person whose heart remains firm in faith from the judgment on those who open themselves to disbelief.

Evaluating conduct therefore requires attention to its circumstances, the person’s freedom, and their intention.

It also shows that faith and disbelief cannot simply be produced by forcing verbal affirmation or denial.

أَكْرَهْتَنَا: coercion under Pharaoh’s domination

These are the words of the sorcerers who believed after seeing Moses’ signs and stood against Pharaoh’s threats.

أَكْرَهْتَنَا refers to an act Pharaoh compelled them to perform.

The context connects coercion with power over behavior. Pharaoh could compel an act, but could not control their inward acceptance of truth.

Their belief against his wishes exposes the boundary between control of outward conduct and inner human choice.

Prohibiting the coercion of women into prostitution

Three forms of the family occur here:

  • تُكْرِهُوا: do not compel.
  • يُكْرِهْهُنَّ: compels them.
  • إِكْرَاهِهِنَّ: their being compelled.

In this setting, people under another’s authority could be exploited for financial gain.

The Qur’an prohibits those with power from forcing these women into prostitution.

The conclusion mentions divine forgiveness and mercy after their coercion. In context, this concerns the women compelled, not a license for those exploiting them.

The condition that women desire chastity does not authorize exploiting women with different backgrounds or circumstances. Coercion fundamentally means making someone act against their will.

Like al-Nahl 16:106, this distinguishes the responsibility of the coerced person from that of the coercer.

كَرَّهَ: making something displeasing

كَرَّهَ is a form II perfect verb, distinct from form IV أَكْرَهَ.

The former means making something displeasing to another; the latter means compelling another against their will.

حَبَّبَ and كَرَّهَ stand opposite each other: faith is made beloved, and disbelief, defiance, and disobedience displeasing.

This concerns inward moral orientation, rather than imposing behavior against someone’s will.

A person of sound judgment does not avoid wrong solely through fear of punishment, but recognizes and dislikes its ugliness.

Disliking backbiting

The Qur’an brings the ugliness of backbiting home to the conscience through an image people find repellent.

فَكَرِهْتُمُوهُ denotes natural dislike and revulsion toward that image.

The verse calls for the same aversion toward backbiting another person.

Disliking truth and guidance

Several verses describe people who dislike truth, revelation, or divine approval.

People may dislike a truth because it conflicts with their interests, desires, previous beliefs, or social standing.

Disliking truth does not change its nature.

Al-Anfal 8:8 and Yunus 10:82 describe truth being established despite the criminals’ dislike.

Al-Tawbah 9:32 and al-Saff 61:8 speak of divine light being completed despite the disbelievers’ dislike. Al-Tawbah 9:33 and al-Saff 61:9 describe the associators’ dislike of the religion of truth prevailing.

Al-Tawbah 9:48 describes divine command becoming manifest despite opposition; Ghafir 40:14 calls believers to invoke The God sincerely despite the disbelievers’ dislike.

In al-Tawbah 9:81, a group dislikes striving with wealth and life in The God’s way.

Muhammad 47:9 and Muhammad 47:26 concern disliking what The God revealed. Muhammad 47:28 places dislike of divine approval alongside following what provokes divine anger.

Dislike can therefore extend beyond a natural response to hardship and become a stance toward truth and moral responsibility.

Al-Nahl 16:62 also condemns those who attribute to The God what they dislike for themselves.

Their personal aversion exposes a contradiction in conduct and belief.

كَرِهَ اللَّهُ: divine disapproval of an action

Here كَرِهَ is attributed to The God.

Al-Tawbah 9:47 explains that had they gone out with the believers, they would only have added disorder and sought discord among them.

Disapproval of their departure thus concerns the harmful consequences of their presence.

Divine كراهة here expresses that an action is disapproved of by The God; it should not be equated with human bodily and emotional reactions.

مَكْرُوه: objectionable before the Lord

مَكْرُوهًا is the passive participle of كَرِهَ, denoting something disapproved of.

The preceding verses address conduct and duties including not killing children, avoiding zina, not killing unjustly, respecting orphans’ rights, keeping covenants, and avoiding arrogance.

In Hafs’s transmission from ʿAsim, سَيِّئُهُ is nominative and refers to the evils discussed in the preceding verses.

مَكْرُوه describes those evils as objectionable before the Lord.

5. Qur’anic مَكْرُوه and the later juristic term

In common juristic terminology, makruh usually denotes an action better avoided, though performing it does not necessarily entail sin or punishment. Some legal schools distinguish degrees and kinds of disapproval.

This technical classification should not be imposed on the Qur’an’s lexical usage.

The verses before al-Isra 17:38 include acts explicitly prohibited.

Al-Isra 17:33 also prohibits killing a life made inviolable by The God except with right.

Al-Isra 17:38 calls the evils in this context مَكْرُوه before the Lord.

Qur’anic usage therefore does not mean an action that must be permissible but is merely preferable to avoid.

مَكْرُوه basically means disliked or objectionable. An act’s ruling and degree of prohibition must be understood from the relevant commands and context.

The later juristic category cannot therefore define the Qur’anic word.

6. Morphology and syntax

كَرِهَ, كَرَّهَ, and أَكْرَهَ

Despite belonging to one family, these verbs have different meanings.

The basic triliteral كَرِهَ expresses disliking something.

Form II كَرَّهَ expresses making something displeasing to another.

Form IV أَكْرَهَ expresses compelling someone to act against their will.

A person may dislike wrongful behavior: this is expressed by كَرِهَ.

Learning its consequences may make a behavior displeasing: this relates to تَكْرِيه.

When another uses pressure or threats to compel action against someone’s will, the concept is إِكْرَاه.

مَكْرُوه and مُكْرَه

مَكْرُوه is the passive participle of the basic verb, meaning something disliked.

مُكْرَه is the form IV passive participle, meaning someone compelled.

One denotes the object of dislike; the other, a person under compulsion.

كَارِهُونَ: describing a person’s state

كَارِه is an active participle on the pattern فَاعِل.

In usages such as Hud 11:28, al-Tawbah 9:54, and al-Mu’minun 23:70, it describes people’s state or attitude toward the matter discussed.

Being unwilling is not the same as being compelled. People may dislike something without anyone forcing them to accept or do it.

لَا إِكْرَاهَ: categorical negation of coercion

In al-Baqarah 2:256, لَا إِكْرَاهَ فِي الدِّينِ consists of categorical لَا, its noun, and a prepositional phrase.

The construction itself does not restrict coercion to a particular individual, people, historical period, or kind.

Negating religious coercion does not contradict commands, responsibilities, and moral consequences. Stating a responsibility differs from imposing a belief upon the conscience.

7. Comparison with related roots

ك ر ه compared with ح ب ب

حُبّ concerns love, attachment, and inclination; كراهة concerns dislike and lack of inclination.

They contrast in al-Baqarah 2:216. Likewise, حَبَّبَ stands opposite كَرَّهَ in al-Hujurat 49:7.

Yet liking something is not conclusive evidence of its goodness, nor disliking it of its evil.

ك ر ه compared with ط و ع

طَوْع concerns willing acceptance, agreement, and compliance.

كَرْه contrasts with it through action without willingness or consent, or against one’s wishes.

The contrast appears in Al ʿImran 3:83, al-Raʿd 13:15, al-Tawbah 9:53, and Fussilat 41:11.

A person can nevertheless act voluntarily while finding the difficulty involved unpleasant.

ك ر ه compared with ب غ ض

بُغْض concerns aversion and hatred and also contrasts with love.

In al-Mumtahanah 60:4, بَغْضَاء occurs alongside enmity.

كراهة has a broader range of unpleasantness, including a difficult task, an unwanted situation, or an unpleasant experience.

Not every dislike amounts to hatred or intense hostility.

ك ر ه compared with س خ ط

سَخَط concerns displeasure and dissatisfaction and can include anger and strong disapproval.

Here إِسْخَاط concerns what provokes divine anger and displeasure; كراهة concerns the human stance toward divine approval.

Their juxtaposition shows that semantic proximity does not make them exact synonyms in every context.

إِكْرَاه compared with ق ه ر

قَهْر concerns overcoming, dominance, and subjugation.

إِكْرَاه concerns compelling another to act against their will.

Power and dominance can enable coercion, but not every instance of dominance means forcing someone to perform a specific act.

إِكْرَاه compared with ض ر ر

اضْطِرَار, from ض ر ر, concerns necessity and hardship restricting a person’s choices.

Al-Baqarah 2:173 addresses someone compelled by necessity to consume prohibited food.

In إِكْرَاه, another person may be the coercer; in اضْطِرَار, circumstances and necessities of life may compel action.

Both involve limited choice, but its cause and manner are not always identical.

إِكْرَاه compared with ل ز م

إِلْزَام concerns obliging and binding someone to something.

Not every obligation is coercion. A person freely accepting a contract may be bound to fulfill its commitments.

When obligation becomes a means of imposing belief or conduct against someone’s will, it can involve coercion.

In Hud 11:28, Noah presents imposing an unwelcome mercy upon his people as a rhetorical question.

Conclusion

ك ر ه concerns dislike, lack of inclination or consent, hardship, and compulsion.

The Qur’an uses it for dislike of difficult things, enduring hardship, disapproval of evil conduct, opposition to truth, and compulsion against a person’s will.

Several principles emerge from these usages:

Disliking something does not necessarily make it wrong, just as liking it does not establish its goodness.

Coerced conduct does not necessarily reflect inward will, belief, or consent. Choice and circumstances therefore matter in evaluating responsibility.

Pressure cannot create faith in the heart. The God’s Messengers clarify truth and convey the message, rather than imposing belief on the conscience.

Qur’anic مَكْرُوه means objectionable before The God and should not be confined to its later technical juristic definition.

Definition for Forqan:

ك ر ه concerns dislike, lack of willingness and consent, enduring hardship, and acting against one’s will. إِكْرَاه denotes compelling another against their choice and will.
