The Qur’anic Root F H M (فَهِمَ)

Qur’anic and lexical forms

Understanding, grasping meaning and intent, and enabling another person to understand: from recognizing an issue to informed judgment

1. The root’s meaning in early Arabic

The three-letter root ف ه م concerns understanding, grasping meaning, and recognizing a subject through awareness and perception. فَهِمَ الشَّيْءَ means that someone understood something and grasped its meaning. فَهِمَ الْكَلَامَ likewise means understanding speech and recognizing the speaker’s intent.

Classical lexical sources explain فَهْم as مَعْرِفَتُكَ الشَّيْءَ بِالْقَلْبِ: knowing something within the sphere of human perception and awareness.

Here “heart” concerns inward apprehension and cognition. The expression should not be taken to make the root’s meaning depend on the bodily organ or to present a particular theory of the biological location of thought.

Other explanations gloss فَهِمَ الشَّيْءَ with عَقَلَهُ وَعَرَفَهُ: apprehending and knowing it.

These explanations place understanding within the field of cognition, without making it identical to every kind of knowing.

Someone may hear the words of a statement and even remember them without understanding its intent. They may also possess information about a problem without correctly identifying the relationships within it.

Understanding in such cases means grasping the meaning of what is known or expressed.

Nevertheless, this distinction does not establish that understanding necessarily ranks above knowledge or recognition in every Arabic usage. Lexical sources sometimes explain these words through one another. Their precise differences must be determined from usage and context.

The historical evidence

Classical Arabic clearly records understanding and apprehension as meanings of this family. The Qur’an itself directly attests the root in the Arabic of the period of revelation.

Precisely reconstructing its pre-Islamic meaning, however, requires independently datable evidence, such as pre-Islamic poetry with an assessable attribution or a text whose date can be established.

This study did not obtain an independent poetic example whose wording, attribution, and pre-Qur’anic date were sufficiently established. An example must therefore not be invented, nor should meanings recorded in later dictionaries be attributed without qualification to the entire pre-Islamic period.

Despite this limitation, the general meaning is clear: grasping and recognizing the meaning of something. The Qur’anic use fits this semantic field.

The recognized meaning of ف ه م directly concerns cognitive activity. No original physical image has been established as the historical source of all its forms.

Expressions such as “unlocking a hidden meaning” may help explain understanding, but should not be presented as a historically proven root meaning.

2. Forms and semantic core

Important Arabic forms in this family include:

فَهِمَ, “understood” or “grasped”; يَفْهَمُ, “understands”; فَهْم, “understanding”; فَاهِم, “one who understands”; فَهِم and فَهِيم, describing someone perceptive or quick to understand; فَهَّمَ and أَفْهَمَ, “made someone understand”; تَفَهَّمَ, “applied oneself to understanding” or “gradually understood”; اِسْتَفْهَمَ, “requested understanding or explanation”; and تَفَاهَمَ, in its recognized usage of mutual understanding.

Nouns such as تَفْهِيم, إِفْهَام, اِسْتِفْهَام, and مَفْهُوم also belong to this family.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an.

The root ف ه م occurs only once in the Qur’an, as فَفَهَّمْنَاهَا in al-Anbiyāʾ 21:79.

This is a Form II verb. Its base form is فَهَّمَ and its verbal noun is تَفْهِيم.

The simple verb فَهِمَ primarily describes someone’s understanding: the person grasps something.

فَهَّمَ expresses imparting understanding or bringing another person to understand.

For example, فَهِمَ الرَّجُلُ الْأَمْرَ means “the man understood the matter,” whereas فَهَّمْتُهُ الْأَمْرَ means “I made him understand the matter.”

The person receiving understanding and the matter understood have different roles in this construction.

Form II here expresses imparting understanding, but its pattern alone does not imply extensive repetition, prolonged teaching, or a sudden transfer of information.

The morphology does not specify the method of imparting understanding.

The semantic core may be stated as:

Grasping a subject’s meaning, intent, or reality; and, in the causative construction, enabling someone else to grasp it.

This definition preserves the family’s general meaning while fitting its particular Qur’anic use.

3. Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:79: giving Solomon understanding of a case

The root’s sole Qur’anic occurrence appears in the account of David and Solomon.

To identify the subject, we must first read the preceding verse.

وَدَاوُودَ وَسُلَيْمَانَ إِذْ يَحْكُمَانِ فِي الْحَرْثِ إِذْ نَفَشَتْ فِيهِ غَنَمُ الْقَوْمِ وَكُنَّا لِحُكْمِهِمْ شَاهِدِينَ

Wa-Dāwūda wa-Sulaymāna idh yaḥkumāni fī l-ḥarthi idh nafashat fīhi ghanamu l-qawmi wa-kunnā li-ḥukmihim shāhidīn.

“And [remember] David and Solomon, when they were judging concerning the cultivated field into which the people’s sheep had strayed; and We were witnesses to their judgment.”

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:78

The verse concerns a dispute involving a cultivated field and sheep.

الْحَرْث concerns cultivation and cultivated land. In recognized Arabic usage, نَفَشَتْ describes livestock spreading out and grazing, particularly at night.

The context indicates a case involving sheep entering a cultivated field and dealing with the consequences.

David and Solomon were judging the matter, and God says He witnessed their judgment.

The next verse follows:

فَفَهَّمْنَاهَا سُلَيْمَانَ وَكُلًّا آتَيْنَا حُكْمًا وَعِلْمًا وَسَخَّرْنَا مَعَ دَاوُودَ الْجِبَالَ يُسَبِّحْنَ وَالطَّيْرَ وَكُنَّا فَاعِلِينَ

Fa-fahhamnāhā Sulaymāna wa-kullan ātaynā ḥukman wa-ʿilman wa-sakhkharnā maʿa Dāwūda l-jibāla yusabbiḥna wa-l-ṭayra wa-kunnā fāʿilīn.

“So We gave Solomon understanding of that matter; and to each We granted judgment and knowledge. And with David We subjected the mountains, glorifying, and the birds; and We were the doers.”

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:79

The construction فَفَهَّمْنَاهَا سُلَيْمَانَ is linguistically significant.

God does not say He had given knowledge only to Solomon. He says He gave Solomon understanding of a particular matter, then immediately affirms that both received judgment and knowledge.

Understanding a specific case and possessing knowledge and the general capacity to judge are therefore distinguishable.

The structure of فَفَهَّمْنَاهَا

This form consists of several components.

فَـ is a connective linking the sentence to the judgment discussed in the preceding verse.

فَهَّمْ is the Form II past-tense verb stem, expressing imparting understanding.

نَا is the subject pronoun, referring here to God.

هَا is an attached feminine singular pronoun referring to the matter made understood.

سُلَيْمَانَ names the person given understanding of that matter.

The construction thus distinguishes the matter made understood from the person who understands it.

Translation should preserve this distinction.

“We understood that Solomon…” is incorrect: God is the agent, and the action is imparting understanding to Solomon.

“Solomon understood” conveys the result but removes the explicitly stated agent who imparted understanding.

“We made Solomon understand it” or “We gave Solomon understanding of that matter” is therefore more faithful to the Arabic.

What does the pronoun هَا refer to?

In فَفَهَّمْنَاهَا, هَا is feminine singular.

In the preceding verse, الْحَرْث denotes cultivated land and is grammatically masculine. The feminine pronoun cannot simply be assigned to that noun without explanation.

غَنَم can be grammatically feminine, but the thing made understood here is not the sheep themselves.

The principal contextual clue is David and Solomon’s judgment concerning the dispute.

Thus هَا refers to the matter, case, or judgment under discussion: a meaning supplied by the preceding sentence, although an independent noun meaning “case” or “matter” does not appear in the Arabic.

An Arabic pronoun may refer to something understood from a verb and its context without an explicitly named antecedent in the preceding sentence.

Accordingly, “that matter” is more natural in translation than “that field” or “those sheep.”

What Solomon was given to understand was the case involving the field and the sheep.

4. How does understanding differ from knowing and judging?

An important feature of al-Anbiyāʾ 21:79 is its affirmation that both received judgment and knowledge:

God explicitly states in this verse that He gave David and Solomon judgment and knowledge alike.

After describing the understanding imparted to Solomon, God affirms that both possessed judgment and knowledge.

كُلًّا here refers to each of the two: David and Solomon.

حُكْمًا concerns judgment and adjudication.

عِلْمًا denotes knowledge and awareness.

This prevents Solomon’s particular understanding from being taken as evidence of David’s general ignorance or incompetence.

David too possessed knowledge and judgment.

At the same time, the Qur’an attributes understanding of this particular matter to Solomon.

A distinction emerges: someone may have knowledge and a capacity to judge, while understanding a particular case still requires correctly grasping its details and relationships.

For example, two people may know the principles for resolving a dispute. Yet when faced with a particular case, one may notice an aspect the other has overlooked.

Better understanding of one issue does not necessarily imply superiority in every field of knowledge.

This example illustrates the distinction between knowledge and understanding; it does not reconstruct David and Solomon’s historical judgment.

Had David issued an incorrect ruling?

These two verses do not give the details of David’s and Solomon’s rulings.

Some exegetical reports say David proposed transferring the sheep to the field’s owner, while Solomon offered a means of compensation followed by the return of the properties.

These details do not appear in the Qur’anic text, and the reports differ over the manner of adjudication.

A Qur’anic study should not insert one such report into the verse without justification and then base the root’s meaning on that narrative.

The Qur’an explicitly states that David and Solomon judged concerning the field, God gave Solomon understanding of the matter, and both possessed judgment and knowledge.

This identifies Solomon’s particular understanding, but does not independently explain the final ruling’s details, the form of compensation, or the precise difference between the two judgments.

Nor can this single event establish that Solomon’s judgment was superior to David’s in every matter.

Understanding one subject is not the same as absolute knowledge or permanent superiority in every subject.

How did God impart understanding?

In فَفَهَّمْنَاهَا, God identifies Himself as the agent imparting understanding.

The verse does not explain how Solomon received it.

Was it direct revelation? Did God guide his discernment while he examined the case? Did the truth become clear to him during adjudication?

The verb’s construction alone does not determine the method.

What is stated is that God imparted understanding. The manner cannot be derived from فَهَّمَ itself.

It should therefore not be restricted to a sudden transfer of information without thought, or to a particular mental process.

Conversely, the verse does not allow God’s role to be removed from the sentence and the entire matter attributed solely to Solomon’s personal ability.

The Qur’an says God gave Solomon understanding of the matter. This statement must be preserved in the direct meaning.

5. Understanding, knowledge, and teaching in one Qur’anic passage

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:79 contains three related concepts: imparting understanding, judgment, and knowledge.

The next verse, al-Anbiyāʾ 21:80, speaks of teaching David how to make armor.

This passage therefore presents both understanding a judicial case and instruction in a practical skill.

Teaching, understanding, and knowledge can be connected while each retains its particular role in a sentence.

عَلَّمَ concerns teaching and conveying knowledge.

فَهَّمَ concerns bringing someone to understand a subject.

Teaching may produce understanding, but not every lesson produces complete understanding. Someone may also understand through experience, observation, or thought, without receiving direct instruction at that moment.

The objects of the two verbs also help clarify their Qur’anic uses.

In al-Anbiyāʾ 21:79, understanding concerns a judicial case.

In al-Anbiyāʾ 21:80, instruction concerns making a practical means of protection against harm in war.

Understanding should therefore not be defined as purely theoretical cognition, nor knowledge as purely practical skill. Both can operate in different fields of cognition.

The main distinction is that understanding emphasizes grasping a subject’s meaning; knowledge expresses knowing or awareness of it; teaching is the act of instruction.

This distinction is explored further in Forqan’s study of the Qur’anic root ع ل م.

6. Distinctions from related roots

ف ه م and ف ق ه: understanding and fiqh

ف ه م and ف ق ه are among the closest semantic families in Arabic.

Both can denote understanding and grasping meaning.

The Qur’an uses ف ق ه in different settings: understanding speech, grasping signs, recognizing truth, and understanding religion.

In al-Anbiyāʾ 21:79, ف ه م concerns giving Solomon understanding of a specific case.

Differences in frequency and subject matter do not establish that fiqh always means profound understanding and fahm superficial apprehension.

The two overlap in classical Arabic and have been used to explain one another.

In al-Tawbah 9:122, لِيَتَفَقَّهُوا, a Form V construction, concerns seeking understanding of religion.

By contrast, فَفَهَّمْنَاهَا سُلَيْمَانَ in al-Anbiyāʾ 21:79 concerns God imparting understanding of a subject.

The difference between these constructions depends not only on their roots, but also on the verbal forms, agents, objects, and subjects understood.

For a fuller study, see Forqan’s article on the Qur’anic root ف ق ه.

ف ه م and ف ك ر: understanding and thinking

ف ك ر concerns thinking and using the mind to examine a subject.

ف ه م concerns grasping that subject’s meaning or intent.

The two may accompany one another in a cognitive process.

Someone may think about a problem, examine its different aspects, and eventually understand it.

But thought does not always lead to correct understanding. A person may think about something for a long time and still fail to understand it or reach a false conclusion.

Al-Muddaththir 74:18 uses فَكَّرَ of someone who thinks and then reaches a conclusion the Qur’an condemns.

Thinking alone therefore does not guarantee a correct result.

Nor should understanding be confined to a fixed stage invariably following thought. A simple statement may be understood immediately, while a difficult issue may require prolonged reflection.

This distinction is discussed in the study of the Qur’anic root ف ك ر.

ف ه م and ع ق ل: understanding and reasoning

In Arabic, ع ق ل is associated with cognition, assessment, and exercising reason.

Its Qur’anic use involves examining signs, statements, and realities.

Understanding may result from such examination: a person grasps the subject.

Reason and understanding are not wholly unrelated concepts in Arabic. Lexical sources also explain فَهِمْتُ الشَّيْءَ through عَقَلْتُهُ.

This overlap cautions against imposing an artificial hierarchy upon them.

In the Qur’anic occurrences, ع ق ل covers a broader field of assessing and apprehending signs, whereas the sole use of ف ه م focuses on imparting understanding of a particular matter.

This distinction describes the attested occurrences; it is not an absolute rule for all Arabic.

ف ه م and ح ك م: understanding and judgment

One principal branch of ح ك م concerns adjudication, settling a case, and resolving a dispute through a ruling.

ف ه م concerns grasping meaning and recognizing the subject.

The two families occur together in al-Anbiyāʾ 21:78 and al-Anbiyāʾ 21:79.

David and Solomon judged concerning the field; God then says He gave Solomon understanding of the matter.

This connection shows the role understanding can play in judgment.

Someone who has misunderstood a dispute may misapply general rules despite knowing them.

But understanding a matter and issuing a ruling are not identical.

Someone may understand a case without the authority or responsibility to judge it. Another person may hold judicial office yet need greater knowledge and understanding of a particular case.

Forqan’s study of the Qur’anic root ح ك م separately examines the family of judgment, adjudication, and wisdom.

ف ه م and د ب ر: understanding and considered reflection

تَدَبُّر belongs to د ب ر. In Qur’anic use, it concerns considered examination of discourse and attention to its connections and implications.

Understanding can be one result of such reflection.

Someone may read a sentence and grasp its apparent meaning, yet need further examination to understand its relationship to what precedes and follows it.

This examination may deepen understanding or correct an initial interpretation.

Nevertheless, reflection and understanding are not interchangeable names for one action, nor is either invariably a necessary condition for the other.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:79 uses فَهَّمَ. That lexical choice alone does not establish that Solomon necessarily undertook a particular process of reflection or had no need of it.

ف ه م and ع ر ف: grasping meaning and recognition

ع ر ف belongs to the field of knowing, familiarity, and recognition.

In Arabic, someone may know a person or recognize a thing by its signs.

Understanding more commonly concerns grasping meaning, intent, or a subject requiring apprehension.

Knowing a speaker, for example, is not the same as understanding their words.

Someone may know a person for years yet misunderstand their intention in a particular conversation.

Conversely, a reader may understand the meaning of words written by a stranger.

This is a useful distinction in usage, although knowing and understanding remain connected in many situations.

7. Understanding and later terminology

Arabic has developed a wide range of terms from this root.

In its general sense, اِسْتِفْهَام means seeking understanding or asking for explanation. In grammar and rhetoric, it later became a technical term for interrogative constructions and ways of asking questions.

The word اِسْتِفْهَام itself does not occur in the Qur’an.

The presence of questions in the Qur’an does not make all of them etymological members of ف ه م. Interrogation names a linguistic function, not the root of interrogative words.

In general usage, مَفْهُوم means what is understood. In logic, philosophy, and other disciplines, it acquired technical meanings, including a mental concept or meaning examined in relation to its instances or other subjects.

These terms illuminate the family’s development, but their technical senses should not be imposed on al-Anbiyāʾ 21:79.

In contemporary Persian, the related word for mutual understanding also commonly denotes agreement and reaching a shared understanding.

Mutual understanding can facilitate agreement, but understanding another person’s words does not necessarily mean agreeing with them.

Two people may understand one another completely while still disagreeing about the conclusion.

Thus understanding does not fundamentally mean agreement, nor does imparting understanding mean forcing someone to accept an opinion.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

For the simple verb فَهِمَ, principal equivalents are “understood,” “grasped,” and, where appropriate, “recognized the intent.”

For فَهْم, “understanding,” “grasping the meaning,” and “comprehension of the subject” are suitable.

For فَاهِم, “one who understands” or “someone who grasps the subject” is natural.

Depending on context, فَهِيم may be rendered “quick to understand” or “perceptive.”

For فَهَّمَ and أَفْهَمَ, clear equivalents are “made someone understand” and “brought someone to understanding.”

For تَفَهَّمَ, “understood,” “sought to understand,” or “gradually understood” may fit, provided the particular construction and context support the chosen meaning.

For اِسْتَفْهَمَ, “asked for explanation” or “asked in order to understand” is suitable.

Except for the Form II verb in al-Anbiyāʾ 21:79, these forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

Translating the Qur’anic form فَفَهَّمْنَاهَا

The principal proposal for Forqan is:

“So We gave Solomon understanding of that matter.”

This rendering has several features:

First, it preserves the agent: God imparts understanding.

Second, it takes هَا as referring contextually to the judicial case.

Third, it identifies Solomon as the recipient of understanding.

Fourth, it adds no method of imparting understanding where the verse specifies none.

Another option is:

“So We made Solomon understand that matter.”

Both are defensible. The Persian wording favors a direct causative equivalent; the English may naturally express giving understanding of the matter.

“So Solomon understood it” conveys the result but removes the agent who imparted understanding.

“So We revealed to Solomon how to judge” is not a direct translation either: فَهَّمْنَاهَا itself does not identify the means or method of imparting understanding.

“So We made Solomon more knowledgeable than David” expands the verse’s meaning and does not reflect its subsequent emphasis on both receiving judgment and knowledge.

Forqan’s brief, precise rendering is therefore:

“So We gave Solomon understanding of that matter.”

Conclusion

The Arabic root ف ه م concerns understanding, grasping meaning, and recognizing the intent or subject before a person.

The simple verb فَهِمَ emphasizes apprehending meaning; Form II فَهَّمَ expresses imparting understanding of a subject to another person.

Arabic has various forms in this family, including understanding, imparting understanding, seeking understanding, requesting explanation, and mutual understanding; only فَفَهَّمْنَاهَا occurs in the Qur’an.

In al-Anbiyāʾ 21:79, God says He gave Solomon understanding of the case concerning the field and sheep, then affirms that He granted David and Solomon judgment and knowledge alike.

This juxtaposition shows that understanding a particular case is not wholly identical to general knowledge and capacity to judge. Solomon’s particular understanding does not by itself establish David’s ignorance or Solomon’s superiority in every matter.

Nor does the Qur’an state the details of the two rulings or the method of imparting understanding. Historical reports and later hypotheses must not be added to the root’s meaning as though those details were stated.

Understanding is related to knowledge, fiqh, thinking, reasoning, reflection, and recognition, but these families cannot be placed in a fixed hierarchy without exceptions.

Thinking may lead to understanding, knowledge may prepare the way for it, reflection may deepen it, and correct understanding may assist judgment. None of these relationships makes the words’ lexical meanings identical.

Final root definition for Forqan:

ف ه م: apprehending and recognizing the meaning, intent, or reality of a subject, statement, or problem; and, in the causative construction, enabling someone else to understand it.

In its sole Qur’anic use, فَفَهَّمْنَاهَا سُلَيْمَانَ, the root denotes God giving Solomon understanding of a judicial case, without specifying the method, the ruling’s detailed outcome, or the overall levels of David’s and Solomon’s knowledge.
