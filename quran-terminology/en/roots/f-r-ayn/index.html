The root ف ر ع — The upper part, branches, and extension from a foundation

Qur’anic and lexical forms

فَرْعُهَا
(farʿuhā)
Qur’anic form: its crown

فَرْع / فُرُوع
(farʿ / furūʿ)
Upper part; branch and branches

فَرْعَاء
(farʿāʾ)
Lexical form: long-haired

The triliteral root ف ر ع in Arabic concerns height, prominence, the upper part, and something extending from its foundation. In physical use, فَرْع denotes the upper part of something: the top of a tree, a mountain summit, or long hair growing and extending from the head.

For a tree, فَرْع refers to a branch or crown extending upward or outward from its trunk and foundation. By contrast, أَصْل is the tree’s root and foundational part.

This relationship also underlies broader Arabic uses, such as branches of a family, subdivisions of a subject, and matters originating from a foundation.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as فَرْعُهَا in Ibrāhīm 14:24. A good word is compared to a good tree whose root is firm and whose upper part extends toward the sky.

فَرْع does not itself mean growth, fruit, success, or moral superiority. These concepts may be associated with branches and a tree’s crown, but the word primarily denotes an upper part or a part extending from a foundation.

1. The root in early Arabic and evidence attributed to the pre-Qur’anic period

Height and the upper part

In recognized early Arabic usage, فَرْعُ الشَّيْءِ denoted the upper part of something.

For example, فَرْعُ الْجَبَلِ refers to a mountain’s top, and فَرْعُ الشَّجَرَةِ to a tree’s upper part or branches.

In some constructions, the verb فَرَعَ also means ascending or reaching the top of something.

فَرَعَ الْجَبَلَ means that someone climbed the mountain or reached its top.

These uses show that height and occupying an upper position are among this family’s principal meanings.

Being higher does not always mean being superior in value. A mountain summit stands above its slopes, but that spatial distinction is not itself a moral judgment.

First poetic example: فَرْع in Imruʾ al-Qays

The pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays says in his Muʿallaqah, describing a woman’s hair:

وَفَرْعٍ يُغَشِّي الْمَتْنَ أَسْوَدَ فَاحِمٍ
أَثِيثٍ كَقِنْوِ النَّخْلَةِ الْمُتَعَثْكِلِ

Wa-farʿin yughashshī al-matna aswada fāḥimin / athīthin ka-qinwi al-nakhlati al-mutaʿathkili.

Translation: “And long, coal-black hair covering her back, abundant and thick, like the laden cluster of a date palm.”

فَرْع here refers to the hair of the head.

The poet describes hair that has grown long from the head and fallen over the back.

This use relates to the upper part and something extending from it.

Here فَرْع means hair, not a tree branch.

The example shows that the root’s semantic field in pre-Islamic language was not confined to trees: it also covered hair and its extension.

The verse is preserved in Imruʾ al-Qays’s celebrated Muʿallaqah and is a familiar example of pre-Islamic Arabic. Like other pre-Islamic poetry, however, its text reaches us through later transmission and manuscripts.

Second poetic example: فَرْعَاء in al-Aʿshā

The renowned Arab poet al-Aʿshā says in his celebrated ode:

غَرَّاءُ فَرْعَاءُ مَصْقُولٌ عَوَارِضُهَا
تَمْشِي الْهُوَيْنَى كَمَا يَمْشِي الْوَجِي الْوَحِلُ

Gharrāʾu farʿāʾu maṣqūlun ʿawāriḍuhā / tamshī al-huwaynā kamā yamshī al-wajī al-waḥilu.

Translation: “Radiant-faced, with long, abundant hair and polished teeth, she walks slowly and gracefully, like someone with sore feet walking through mud.”

فَرْعَاء describes a woman with long, abundant hair.

The word belongs to the same family, ف ر ع.

Here the root concerns the length and abundance of hair, not tree branches.

The example is preserved in al-Aʿshā’s well-known ode and its early commentaries.

These examples from Imruʾ al-Qays and al-Aʿshā show that height and extension were recognized meanings of this family in early poetry.

They do not establish that فَرْع was first coined for hair and then transferred to a tree branch, or necessarily developed in the reverse direction.

Establishing the historical sequence requires earlier, independent evidence.

Other physical and social uses

Early lexicons also record فَرْع and its derivatives in connection with the height of people, mountains, trees, and land.

In one recognized use, رَجُلٌ أَفْرَعُ is a man with hair on his head, rather than a bald man.

امْرَأَةٌ فَرْعَاءُ is a woman with long or abundant hair.

فَرَعَ رَأْسَهُ is also recorded in some constructions for striking the upper part of the head.

In expressions of social standing, فَرْع and its derivatives sometimes describe a group’s leading or noble members. Physical height is extended to social position.

This extension is understandable: in many languages, being high can suggest greater standing and influence.

It should not become the definition of every derivative. An upper part is not necessarily nobler or more valuable than a lower one.

With different vowels, فَرَع also designated the first offspring of livestock, especially camels or sheep, in some early uses. Historical accounts of Arab animal husbandry record it.

The precise connection between this designation and branching or height is not clearly established. The firstborn of livestock should therefore not be asserted without evidence as the root’s earliest meaning or used to explain the Qur’anic verse.

2. Linguistic forms and the semantic core

The main recognized forms include:

  • فَرْع: the upper part of something, a tree branch, or a part extending from a foundation.
  • فُرُوع: the plural of فَرْع; branches and subdivisions.
  • فَرَعَ: ascended, reached the upper part, or stood above something.
  • فَرَعَ رَأْسَهُ: struck his head; a use associated with the upper part of the body.
  • تَفَرَّعَ: branched out or extended from a foundation into different parts.
  • تَفَرُّع: branching and the emergence of multiple branches.
  • أَفْرَع: having hair on the head; in some uses, tall or prominent.
  • فَرْعَاء: a woman with long, abundant hair.
  • فَارِع: tall, elevated, or prominent in standing.
  • فَرِيع: in some Arabic uses, eminent or of high standing.
  • فَرَع: in another branch, the first offspring of livestock.

In later Arabic, فَرْع became widely used in scholarship, law, genealogy, and the organization of subjects.

For example, a scholarly question can be discussed in terms of foundation and branch. The foundation is the basic subject or rule, while the branch is a part derived from it.

In genealogy, children and later generations can likewise be called the branches of a family.

These uses can be explained through the image of tree branches, but their precise historical emergence requires independent evidence.

Of all these forms, the Qur’an uses only فَرْعُهَا.

The semantic core relevant to the Qur’an may be defined as:

The upper, extended part of something which, in a structure such as a tree, rises from its foundation and extends upward or outward.

This definition contains two features: height and a relationship to a foundation.

They are not always equally prominent or expressed in the same way. In فَرْعُ الْجَبَلِ, the upper part is prominent; in فُرُوعُ الشَّجَرَةِ, the branches’ relationship to the trunk and roots also matters.

A narrow, exceptionless meaning should therefore not be imposed on every derivative.

3. Qur’anic usage: a firm root and a lofty crown

Surah Ibrāhīm 14:24

أَلَمْ تَرَ كَيْفَ ضَرَبَ اللَّهُ مَثَلًا كَلِمَةً طَيِّبَةً كَشَجَرَةٍ طَيِّبَةٍ أَصْلُهَا ثَابِتٌ وَفَرْعُهَا فِي السَّمَاءِ

A-lam tara kayfa ḍaraba Allāhu mathalan kalimatan ṭayyibatan ka-shajaratin ṭayyibatin aṣluhā thābitun wa-farʿuhā fī al-samāʾi.

Translation: “Have you not seen how The God sets forth a parable? A good word is like a good tree: its root is firm and its crown is in the sky.”

This is the only Qur’anic occurrence of ف ر ع.

A good word is compared to a good tree.

The tree has two prominent features: a firm root and an upper part extending toward the sky.

أَصْلُهَا refers to the tree’s root and foundation.

ثَابِتٌ expresses its firmness.

فَرْعُهَا concerns the tree’s upper part and crown.

فِي السَّمَاءِ indicates the elevated position of that part in the verse’s image.

The Qur’an thus describes both a stable foundation and a high, spreading upper part.

Does فَرْع mean just one branch?

فَرْعُهَا is singular in the verse.

This matters because some translations render it “its branches.”

In Arabic, فَرْع can denote the upper part of a tree, including its branches and crown.

The singular therefore does not imply that the tree has only one branch.

When translating it as “its branches,” the plural should be understood as a way of expressing the crown, not a claim that the Arabic word itself is plural.

Rather than emphasizing the number of branches, فَرْعُهَا contrasts the tree’s upper part with its root and foundation.

“Its crown” or “its upper part” can therefore convey the meaning better than “one of its branches.”

What does فِي السَّمَاءِ express?

In Arabic, سَمَاء relates to height and what is overhead.

The tree’s crown is described as so high that it is in the sky.

This image should not imply that a branch has left Earth’s atmosphere or reached a particular region of the cosmic heavens.

A tall tree can be rooted in the ground while its branches extend into the space overhead.

This is the verse’s natural and readily understandable image.

Yet فِي السَّمَاءِ should not be omitted from the translation: the height of the crown is one of the parable’s two principal features.

فَرْع names the upper part, while فِي السَّمَاءِ makes its height and position more explicit.

The relationship between root and branch

A rooted tree’s upper parts are connected to that root and trunk.

Roots are in the ground and contribute to anchoring and nourishing the plant. The trunk and branches extend upward, with leaves and fruit appearing on various parts.

Ibrāhīm 14:24 emphasizes the firm root and lofty crown.

The next verse, Ibrāhīm 14:25, describes the same tree bearing fruit.

The sequence produces a fuller image: a firm foundation, an extended upper part, and fruit yielded by the tree.

All these concepts should not be packed into the meaning of فَرْع.

فَرْع does not itself mean fruit. Fruiting is expressed by another word and clause in the next verse.

Nor does فَرْع alone guarantee firmness. A tree may have tall branches yet weak roots, and fall in the wind.

In the Qur’anic parable, firmness comes from ثَابِت and goodness from طَيِّبَة.

Each component of the sentence thus contributes its own meaning.

4. The crown in the parable of a good word

Ibrāhīm 14:24 speaks of a natural tree, but its purpose is more than teaching botany.

The tree illustrates كَلِمَةً طَيِّبَةً, a good word.

A good word is likened to a tree with a firm foundation and lofty crown.

At the level of the parable, a connection can be seen between a word’s sound foundation and its far-reaching effects.

For example, a statement grounded in truth, justice, and goodness may begin with one person, reach others, and influence their conduct.

Sound teaching may likewise benefit other people many years after it is expressed.

In such reflection, the word’s foundation resembles the root, and its spreading influence resembles the branches.

These illustrations should not become the lexical definition of فَرْع.

In the verse, فَرْع names the tree’s upper part. The social effect of speech is an inference from the parable and its connection with other verses.

Is every widely spreading statement good?

No. A statement’s spread does not by itself establish its truth.

A false statement may circulate quickly and gain many followers.

The height and spread of the crown are therefore not the parable’s only features.

The verse first calls both the word and the tree طَيِّبَة, good.

It then describes the root as firm.

Ibrāhīm 14:26 compares a bad word to a tree uprooted from the ground, without stability.

The contrast shows that a word’s spread and appearance are not detached from its foundation and quality in the Qur’anic parable.

A statement may be famous yet rest on an unsupported claim. Conversely, a truth may initially be known to only a few, yet retain its rational and ethical foundation despite opposition.

The criterion is therefore not merely a branch’s height: foundation, quality, and fruit also matter.

Does فَرْع mean righteous action here?

In Qur’anic reflection, the worthy effects and conduct arising from sound belief and speech may be compared to a tree’s crown and fruit.

Fāṭir 35:10 also discusses good speech and righteous action in relation to ascent and elevation.

This connection can help broaden understanding of the parable in Ibrāhīm.

In a direct translation of Ibrāhīm 14:24, however, فَرْعُهَا does not mean “its righteous action.”

The verse presents the tree’s image and invites the reader to understand its relationship to a good word.

Replacing the noun with an interpretation erases the natural image.

It is therefore preferable to translate فَرْع as “crown” or “upper part of the tree,” discussing its connection with the effects of speech in the explanation of the parable.

5. فَرْع and Pharaoh: two different matters

The name فِرْعَوْن appears many times in the Qur’an.

Its initial letters resemble ف ر ع, which may lead readers to treat Pharaoh as another derivative of this root.

Such classification is not justified by historical etymology.

Pharaoh is a historical name or title of non-Arabic origin associated with the rulers of ancient Egypt.

Its recognized origin is an ancient Egyptian expression meaning “great house” or “great palace,” which came to refer to Egypt’s ruler.

The Arabic form فِرْعَوْن reached Arabic through linguistic change and transmission.

The letters ف ر ع therefore do not establish that Pharaoh originally meant “an eminent person,” “a high branch,” or “someone who has risen above others.”

Later Arabic also formed تَفَرْعَنَ and فَرْعَنَة from Pharaoh’s name, describing someone acting like Pharaoh or becoming autocratic and rebellious.

This verb was formed from the historical name; it does not establish that the name itself originally derived from the triliteral Arabic root ف ر ع.

Nor does تَفَرْعَنَ occur in the Qur’an with this later meaning.

Pharaoh’s conduct should be studied through the Qur’an’s actual verbs and descriptions, such as transgression, arrogance, oppression, and corruption.

These behaviors are known from the verses about him, not from a supposed derivation of his name from فَرْع.

In the Qur’anic index, therefore, فَرْعُهَا is the only occurrence of the Arabic root ف ر ع; Pharaoh’s name should not be counted among its derivatives.

6. Comparison with related roots

ف ر ع and أ ص ل

These two words are juxtaposed in Ibrāhīm 14:24.

أَصْل denotes a root or foundation.

فَرْع concerns the upper part or a part extending from that foundation.

In a tree, root and crown are connected parts of one structure.

This distinction does not imply that the branch is worthless or that the foundation is absolutely superior in every context.

Roots and branches perform different, complementary roles in a tree’s life.

Being a branch also does not inherently imply falsity or imitation. In scholarly and social uses, a branch may be a valid part developed from a foundation.

ف ر ع and غ ص ن

غُصْن in Arabic denotes a tree branch.

فَرْع can also denote a branch, but its semantic field is broader, including the upper part or crown.

For example, غُصْن may be a particular branch of a tree.

فَرْعُ الشَّجَرَةِ may instead encompass its entire upper part and branches.

This distinction helps explain فَرْعُهَا in Ibrāhīm 14:24.

The root غ ص ن does not occur in the Qur’an. Its comparison with ف ر ع therefore rests on Arabic linguistic evidence, not direct juxtaposition in verses.

ف ر ع and ع ل و

ع ل و concerns being high, ascending, and gaining superiority.

One semantic branch of ف ر ع also concerns the upper part and height.

Yet عُلُوّ can express the condition of being high or the act of rising.

By contrast, Qur’anic فَرْع names a part of the tree occupying an elevated position.

Not everything high is therefore called فَرْع.

The sky, for example, is high, but that alone does not make it the branch of something else.

In the verse from Ibrāhīm, being a branch concerns the relationship between the tree’s upper part and its foundation.

ف ر ع and ر ف ع

In Qur’anic language, رَفْع concerns raising or lifting.

A person can lift something off the ground without its being a branch or the upper part of a structure.

فَرْع, by contrast, is the name of a part or branch.

In Ibrāhīm 14:24, فَرْع is not a verb meaning “to raise”; its height is understood from the complete expression.

Translating فَرْعُهَا directly as “raising it” would therefore turn a noun into a verb.

ف ر ع and ش ع ب

ش ع ب concerns the separation and branching of parts, and also the joining of separated parts.

In Al-Ḥujurāt 49:13, شُعُوب refers to human groups and communities.

In its Qur’anic use, however, فَرْع is part of a natural structure extending from the tree’s foundation.

The two families may share branching imagery, but are not identical in root or use.

Nor does فَرْع establish that all human groups are branches of one particular social or religious system. That claim would require independent evidence.

ف ر ع and ث م ر

ثَمَر concerns a tree’s fruit and yield.

فَرْع is the upper part and branches of a tree.

Fruit may appear on a branch, but fruit and branch are not the same thing.

Ibrāhīm 14:24 describes the tree’s root and crown.

Ibrāhīm 14:25 describes its bearing fruit.

The sequence clarifies the relationship between foundation, extension, and yield; fruiting should nevertheless not become part of the lexical meaning of فَرْع.

7. Challenges in understanding and translation

The first challenge is restricting فَرْع to a single thin branch.

Its Qur’anic use can instead denote the tree’s upper part or crown.

The second challenge is converting height into moral superiority.

Something can be tall without being superior in value or quality. A tree may have very tall branches but poor fruit or weak roots.

The height of the crown does not itself establish goodness or soundness.

The third challenge is substituting a figurative interpretation for the lexical meaning.

In the verse from Ibrāhīm, فَرْع does not lexically mean faith, righteous action, religious law, knowledge, or the community of believers.

These may be considered in reflection on a good word and its effects, but the word itself names the tree’s upper part.

The fourth challenge is assuming that every branch is inferior and less valuable than its foundation.

In scholarly and social uses, foundation and branch occupy different positions within a relationship. This is not necessarily a ranking of value.

A branch may develop from a foundation and perform a function that the foundation alone does not.

The fifth challenge is equating فَرْع with Pharaoh.

Similar letters do not prove a common root. Pharaoh is a name of non-Arabic origin and should be examined separately from ف ر ع.

The sixth challenge is making فِي السَّمَاءِ a physical description of branches entering the cosmic heavens.

The verse offers a natural, understandable image of a lofty crown. Physical or cosmological details cannot be derived from فَرْع itself.

Finally, the relationships among the parable’s elements must be preserved: root, firmness, branch, and fruit each have a distinct meaning.

Replacing all these elements with the general phrase “growth and success” erases the precision of the Qur’anic image.

8. Translation recommendations for Forqan

For general uses of فَرْع, suitable equivalents include “upper part,” “branch,” and, for a tree, “crown.”

Depending on the subject, فُرُوع can be rendered “branches,” “subdivisions,” or “branches of a larger whole.”

In height-related uses, فَرْعُ الْجَبَلِ can mean “summit” or “upper part of the mountain.”

In uses concerning hair, فَرْع means long hair on the head.

In the sole Qur’anic occurrence, Ibrāhīm 14:24, the expression is:

«وَفَرْعُهَا فِي السَّمَاءِ»

Wa-farʿuhā fī al-samāʾi.

Two natural renderings are possible:

“And its crown is in the sky.”

Or:

“And its upper part rises toward the sky.”

The first is closer to the Arabic’s direct construction.

The second clarifies the image of height and upward extension for the Persian reader.

“Its branch is in the sky” is also lexically defensible, but may suggest a single particular branch.

For Forqan’s base translation, “its crown is in the sky” is suitable: it preserves the tree’s upper part and conveys the image without adding new details.

A brief lexical explanation can preserve these distinctions:

Word form: وَفَرْعُهَا

Root: ف ر ع

Brief root meaning: the upper, projecting part of something; a branch or part extending from a foundation.

Meaning in this verse: the crown and upper part of the good tree, extending toward the sky.

A brief lexical explanation should remain distinct from a detailed account of the good-word parable. The root study examines the word’s relationship to the verse’s other components.

9. Synthesis

In early Arabic, ف ر ع concerns height, prominence, and the upper parts of things.

Pre-Islamic poetic evidence, particularly فَرْع in Imruʾ al-Qays and فَرْعَاء in al-Aʿshā, shows that this family also described long hair extending from the head.

Arabic also recognizes فَرْع for a tree’s upper part, a mountain summit, and parts branching from a foundation.

The root occurs in the Qur’an only in Ibrāhīm 14:24, as فَرْعُهَا.

Ibrāhīm 14:24 compares a good word to a good tree with a firm root and lofty crown. Ibrāhīm 14:25 describes its fruiting, while Ibrāhīm 14:26 contrasts an unstable tree with the good tree.

The image shows that a firm foundation, an extended upper part, and fruitfulness are related but distinguishable aspects.

The semantic core of ف ر ع is the upper, projecting part of something; in structures such as trees, it denotes a branch or part extending from a foundation.

فَرْع itself does not mean success, moral superiority, or fruit. Such meanings arise only where appropriate indications support them in the expression and context.

Despite similar initial letters, Pharaoh’s name is historically non-Arabic and should not be counted as a Qur’anic derivative of ف ر ع.

In Ibrāhīm 14:24, “crown” or “upper part of the tree” conveys the word well, while its relationship to the firm root and fruit is clarified through explanation of the parable.
