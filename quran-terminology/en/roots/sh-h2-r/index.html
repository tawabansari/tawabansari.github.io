1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

The three-letter Arabic root ش ه ر is associated with becoming visible, being apparent, and becoming known. Within this family, شَهْر names a month as a recognized period of time. The connection between these meanings appears in recognizing a month through the crescent’s emergence: a sign that makes the beginning of a new period visible to people.

In the Qur’an, this root occurs only in the temporal sense “month.” Its use is not confined to a particular occasion. A month may count time, determine a waiting period or the duration of discharging a responsibility, regulate pilgrimage and fasting, or be connected with inviolability, security, and respect for people’s rights.

The principal forms examined are:

  • شَهْر: a month; singular. Used in the Qur’an: yes.
  • شَهْرَيْنِ: two months; dual. Used in the Qur’an: yes.
  • أَشْهُر: months; a broken plural. Used in the Qur’an: yes.
  • شُهُور: months; another broken plural. Used in the Qur’an: yes.
  • شَهَرَ / يَشْهَرُ: to reveal, make known, or put on view. Used in the Qur’an: no.
  • شُهْرَة: visibility, becoming known, renown. Used in the Qur’an: no.
  • شَهِير / مَشْهُور: known or famous. Used in the Qur’an: no.
  • شَهَّرَ / تَشْهِير: to publicize or make widely known; in some uses, to expose to disgrace. Used in the Qur’an: no.
  • اِشْتَهَرَ: to become apparent or well known. Used in the Qur’an: no.
  • أَشْهَرَ: in some Arabic uses, to enter a month, or for a month to pass over a person or place. Used in the Qur’an: no.
  • مُشَاهَرَة: an agreement or transaction arranged by the month. Used in the Qur’an: no.

The root ش ه ر occurs 21 times in 17 verses across nine Qur’anic surahs. Every occurrence belongs to the noun شَهْر in its singular, dual, or plural forms. No verb from this root occurs in the Qur’anic text.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: 21 occurrences of the noun shahr

The central Qur’anic meaning must therefore begin with the noun شهر. Renown, fame, and making visible help us understand the lexical family, but should not be inserted without evidence into the direct meaning of every verse.

2. The linguistic core: from visibility to recognizing the month

Becoming visible and being apparent

In Arabic, شَهَرَ الشَّيْءَ means to reveal something or make it known. شَهَرَ سَيْفَهُ describes someone who draws a sword from its sheath and displays it.

The meaning here is more than removing an implement. A sword previously concealed in its sheath is now visible and on display. Similarly, when news or someone’s characteristic becomes famous, it passes from being unknown to being known among people.

Arabic شُهْرَة can describe either good renown or disgrace. Infamy and moral excellence are therefore not fixed components of the root itself; the subject and context determine the kind of fame involved.

Classical lexical explanations describe a core close to clarity and visibility, accompanied by becoming evident. This account connects displaying a sword, news becoming known, and naming the month.

This semantic connection must not become a claim beyond the evidence. Visibility helps explain the lexical family, whereas Qur’anic “month” names an established unit of time and should not be translated as “becoming visible” on every occasion.

Why is a month called شَهْر?

In Arabic lexical accounts, an older meaning of شَهْر is the celestial moon itself or its crescent. In such usage, an Arab speaker might speak of seeing the shahr while meaning the crescent.

The usage is intelligible: a sign in the sky makes the start of a monthly period recognizable. A new crescent appears, and people recognize entry into another period through it. The name of the sign that reveals the time is thus also used for the period itself.

Nevertheless, three things must be distinguished: the celestial moon, the crescent as one of its visible forms, and a month as a period of time.

These are related, but are not identical.

When the Qur’an speaks of شَهْرُ رَمَضَانَ, it means Ramadan as a period of time, rather than merely a celestial body or the crescent seen at its beginning.

Although the crescent’s appearance is relevant to explaining the naming of shahr, its direct and predominant Qur’anic meaning remains “month.”

Evidence from older Arabic

A verse attributed to Al-Aʿshā, the well-known Arab poet who lived during the pre-Islamic period, reads:

بَنُو الشَّهْرِ الْحَرَامِ فَلَسْتَ مِنْهُمْ
وَلَسْتَ مِنَ الْكِرَامِ بَنِي الْعُبَيْدِ

banū al-shahri al-ḥarāmi fa-lasta minhum / wa-lasta mina al-kirāmi banī al-ʿubaydi

That is: “You are not of Banū al-Shahr al-Ḥarām, nor of the noble Banū al-ʿUbayd.”

In this verse, بنو الشهر الحرام occurs while naming groups and lineages. It attests the expression الشهر الحرام in older poetic language, but does not by itself establish which particular calendar month the poet meant. The date of this particular verse is also not independently certain.

Lisān al-ʿArab, entry ʿabd: the Al-Aʿshā witness

In a clearer example, Dhū al-Rummah, a poet of the Umayyad period, says:

فَأُصْبِحُ أُجْلِي الطَّرْفَ مَا يَسْتَزِيدُهُ
يَرَى الشَّهْرَ قَبْلَ النَّاسِ وَهُوَ نَحِيلُ

fa-uṣbiḥu ujlī al-ṭarfa mā yastazīduhu / yarā al-shahra qabla al-nāsi wa-huwa naḥīlu

The sense is: “I keep straining my sight so that it may see the slender crescent before other people do.”

Here الشهر denotes the crescent itself, and نحيل, “slender,” suits its thin shape.

This poem was composed after the period of Qur’anic revelation and should not be presented as conclusive pre-Qur’anic evidence. Its value lies in recording an older Arabic usage in which shahr denotes the celestial body or sign that makes the month recognizable.

Arabic evidence thus shows that the connection between shahr and the crescent’s visibility is not simply a new comparison. These examples alone, however, do not conclusively establish that the crescent sense historically preceded the temporal sense.

3. The month as a unit of time: ordering the year and counting months

Al-Tawbah 9:36: twelve months and four inviolable months

Verse text

إِنَّ عِدَّةَ الشُّهُورِ عِندَ اللَّهِ اثْنَا عَشَرَ شَهْرًا فِي كِتَابِ اللَّهِ يَوْمَ خَلَقَ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ مِنْهَا أَرْبَعَةٌ حُرُمٌ ذَٰلِكَ الدِّينُ الْقَيِّمُ فَلَا تَظْلِمُوا فِيهِنَّ أَنفُسَكُمْ وَقَاتِلُوا الْمُشْرِكِينَ كَافَّةً كَمَا يُقَاتِلُونَكُمْ كَافَّةً وَاعْلَمُوا أَنَّ اللَّهَ مَعَ الْمُتَّقِينَ

inna ʿiddata al-shuhūri ʿinda Allāhi ithnā ʿashara shahran fī kitābi Allāhi yawma khalaqa al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍa minhā arbaʿatun ḥurumun dhālika al-dīnu al-qayyimu fa-lā taẓlimū fīhinna anfusakum wa-qātilū al-mushrikīna kāffatan kamā yuqātilūnakum kāffatan wa-iʿlamū anna Allāha maʿa al-muttaqīna

English translation

The number of months with The God, in the record of The God from the day He created the heavens and the earth, is indeed twelve months; four of them are inviolable. That is the upright order. So do not wrong yourselves in them, and fight the associators collectively as they fight you collectively; and know that The God is with those who are mindful.

Usage analysis

This is one of the clearest Qur’anic uses of shahr, because it places the word within the counting of the year.

عِدَّةَ الشُّهُورِ concerns the number of months, while اثْنَا عَشَرَ شَهْرًا specifies it. Here shuhūr and shahran are two forms of one noun, not two different concepts.

Twelve months make up the year; four of these twelve are described as حُرُم.

The word shahr itself does not mean “sacred month.” Inviolability is supplied by another word. A month is a unit of time; inviolability is a status and prescription assigned to certain months.

This verse specifies four inviolable months but does not name them individually. Nor can particular months be identified from the word shahr alone.

The next verse, Al-Tawbah 9:37, discusses nasīʾ: shifting or manipulating the months’ inviolability so that what is prohibited in one year is declared permitted in another. The concern therefore extends beyond mathematical counting; the ordering of time is connected with observing or violating inviolability.

Nevertheless, the root ش ه ر itself does not mean inviolability, war, peace, or rites. These concepts arise from other words and the verses’ context.

Months and celestial signs

In Al-Baqarah 2:189, the Qur’an presents crescents as time markers for people and pilgrimage. In Yūnus 10:5, it describes stations for the celestial moon so that people may know the number of years and the reckoning of time.

Although neither verse uses shahr, both are relevant to understanding the monthly system of time.

The Qur’an itself supports a connection between monthly periods and the moon’s visible changes. Yet shahr and hilāl are different words. The crescent marks time; shahr is the period recognized and counted through it.

Another caution follows: shahr alone does not mean an invariable thirty days. A dictionary’s description of a month as thirty days should not replace examination of the actual lunar period. The familiar monthly system based on the moon’s cycle does not give every month the same fixed length.

In Qur’anic usage, shahr is therefore a real, countable unit of time. The details of establishing a month’s beginning or calendar rules cannot be derived from this noun’s lexical meaning alone.

4. Months in religious rites: Ramadan, pilgrimage, and inviolable months

Al-Baqarah 2:185: the month of Ramadan

Verse text

شَهْرُ رَمَضَانَ الَّذِي أُنْزِلَ فِيهِ الْقُرْآنُ هُدًى لِلنَّاسِ وَبَيِّنَاتٍ مِنَ الْهُدَىٰ وَالْفُرْقَانِ فَمَنْ شَهِدَ مِنْكُمُ الشَّهْرَ فَلْيَصُمْهُ وَمَنْ كَانَ مَرِيضًا أَوْ عَلَىٰ سَفَرٍ فَعِدَّةٌ مِنْ أَيَّامٍ أُخَرَ يُرِيدُ اللَّهُ بِكُمُ الْيُسْرَ وَلَا يُرِيدُ بِكُمُ الْعُسْرَ وَلِتُكْمِلُوا الْعِدَّةَ وَلِتُكَبِّرُوا اللَّهَ عَلَىٰ مَا هَدَاكُمْ وَلَعَلَّكُمْ تَشْكُرُونَ

shahru ramaḍāna alladhī unzila fīhi al-qurʾānu hudan li-al-nāsi wa-bayyinātin mina al-hudā wa-al-furqāni fa-man shahida minkumu al-shahra fa-l-yaṣumhu wa-man kāna marīḍan aw ʿalā safarin fa-ʿiddatun min ayyāmin ukhara yurīdu Allāhu bikumu al-yusra wa-lā yurīdu bikumu al-ʿusra wa-li-tukmilū al-ʿiddata wa-li-tukabbirū Allāha ʿalā mā hadākum wa-laʿallakum tashkurūna

English translation

The month of Ramadan is that in which the Qur’an was sent down, as guidance for people and clear signs of guidance and discernment. So whoever among you is present for the month shall fast it; whoever is ill or on a journey, then a number of other days. The God intends ease for you and does not intend hardship for you, so that you may complete the number and magnify The God for having guided you, and so that you may give thanks.

Usage analysis

Shahr occurs twice in this verse.

First, شَهْرُ رَمَضَانَ identifies a particular month by name. Ramadan is the month’s name, while shahr identifies it as a temporal unit. Its significance here is made known through the event described: the Qur’an’s descent within it.

Second, الشَّهْرَ occurs in فَمَنْ شَهِدَ مِنْكُمُ الشَّهْرَ.

This is linguistically important because شَهِدَ and شَهْر belong to different roots. شَهِدَ comes from ش ه د and concerns presence, testimony, and observation; الشهر comes from ش ه ر and means “month.”

Their resemblance should not lead us to derive either word from the other.

In this context, شَهِدَ الشَّهْرَ can indicate being present for or experiencing the month. How that presence relates to residence, travel, or sighting the crescent is a grammatical and contextual question, not something settled by ش ه ر alone.

Shared letters therefore do not establish that the fasting command applies only to someone who personally sees the crescent. Such a claim requires separate examination of شهد, the context of illness and travel, and the verses about marking time.

Nor does shahr establish that people in every geographical or bodily condition must fast in an identical way. Conditions and exceptions must be understood from the fasting verses themselves.

The stable meaning here is Ramadan as a recognized period. Fasting, ease, travel, and illness belong to examination of the prescription’s structure, not the lexical definition of a month.

Al-Baqarah 2:197: the known months of pilgrimage

Verse text

الْحَجُّ أَشْهُرٌ مَعْلُومَاتٌ فَمَنْ فَرَضَ فِيهِنَّ الْحَجَّ فَلَا رَفَثَ وَلَا فُسُوقَ وَلَا جِدَالَ فِي الْحَجِّ وَمَا تَفْعَلُوا مِنْ خَيْرٍ يَعْلَمْهُ اللَّهُ وَتَزَوَّدُوا فَإِنَّ خَيْرَ الزَّادِ التَّقْوَىٰ وَاتَّقُونِ يَا أُولِي الْأَلْبَابِ

al-ḥajju ashhurun maʿlūmātun fa-man faraḍa fīhinna al-ḥajja fa-lā rafatha wa-lā fusūqa wa-lā jidāla fī al-ḥajji wa-mā tafʿalū min khayrin yaʿlamhu Allāhu wa-tazawwadū fa-inna khayra al-zādi al-taqwā wa-ittaqūni yā ulī al-albābi

English translation

Pilgrimage is in known months. Whoever undertakes pilgrimage in them shall allow no sexual impropriety, transgression, or disputing during pilgrimage. Whatever good you do, The God knows it. Take provision, for the best provision is mindfulness; and be mindful of Me, people of understanding.

Usage analysis

أَشْهُرٌ مَعْلُومَاتٌ refers to several known months. Ashhur is plural, and maʿlūmāt describes it.

The first clear implication is that pilgrimage is situated within a monthly period presented as known to its audience.

The expression itself does not list the months’ names or exact number. Identifying them is therefore separate from defining ش ه ر.

On the other hand, the plural ashhur should not be emptied of significance. The verse does not say pilgrimage is only one day or one month. Yet the plural alone does not establish that every pilgrimage act may be performed identically on every day of those months.

The distinction between “known months,” “known days” in Al-Ḥajj 22:28, and “numbered days” in Al-Baqarah 2:203 requires separate examination of the rites’ structure.

The feminine plural pronoun فِيهِنَّ is grammatically significant: it refers to ashhur, confirming that the temporal setting is the months just mentioned.

Maʿlūmāt belongs to ع ل م. The months’ being known is supplied by this adjective, rather than shahr meaning “well known” in every use.

“Known months” or “recognized months” therefore renders the expression accurately. Establishing calendar and ritual details is a further stage of inquiry.

Inviolable months and their social place

Al-Baqarah 2:194

الشَّهْرُ الْحَرَامُ بِالشَّهْرِ الْحَرَامِ وَالْحُرُمَاتُ قِصَاصٌ فَمَنِ اعْتَدَىٰ عَلَيْكُمْ فَاعْتَدُوا عَلَيْهِ بِمِثْلِ مَا اعْتَدَىٰ عَلَيْكُمْ وَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ وَاعْلَمُوا أَنَّ اللَّهَ مَعَ الْمُتَّقِينَ

al-shahru al-ḥarāmu bi-al-shahri al-ḥarāmi wa-al-ḥurumātu qiṣāṣun fa-mani iʿtadā ʿalaykum fa-iʿtadū ʿalayhi bi-mithli mā iʿtadā ʿalaykum wa-ittaqū Allāha wa-iʿlamū anna Allāha maʿa al-muttaqīna

English translation

The inviolable month is for the inviolable month, and violations of inviolability are subject to proportionate requital. Whoever attacks you, respond against him to the same extent as his attack upon you. Be mindful of The God, and know that The God is with those who are mindful.

Usage analysis

Shahr occurs twice with the adjective ḥarām. The repeated expression sets two inviolable months in correspondence within the observance of inviolability and proportionate response.

The verse concerns time and inviolability, but inviolability is not inherent in shahr. The same word describes Ramadan in Al-Baqarah 2:185 and a waiting period in Al-Baqarah 2:226 without containing a prescription of inviolability in its own meaning.

Al-Baqarah 2:217 raises fighting in the inviolable month. Its answer sets the gravity of fighting there alongside barring people from the way of The God and the inviolable mosque, and expelling its inhabitants from their home and land.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:2, the month’s inviolability appears alongside divine signs, sacrificial offerings, garlanded offerings, and the inviolability of those heading for the inviolable House. This association connects protected time with safe passage and respect for people.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:97, the inviolable month likewise appears alongside the Kaʿbah, the inviolable House, and sacrificial offerings:

جَعَلَ اللَّهُ الْكَعْبَةَ الْبَيْتَ الْحَرَامَ قِيَامًا لِلنَّاسِ وَالشَّهْرَ الْحَرَامَ وَالْهَدْيَ وَالْقَلَائِدَ ذَٰلِكَ لِتَعْلَمُوا أَنَّ اللَّهَ يَعْلَمُ مَا فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَمَا فِي الْأَرْضِ وَأَنَّ اللَّهَ بِكُلِّ شَيْءٍ عَلِيمٌ

jaʿala Allāhu al-kaʿbata al-bayta al-ḥarāma qiyāman li-al-nāsi wa-al-shahra al-ḥarāma wa-al-hadya wa-al-qalāʾida dhālika li-taʿlamū anna Allāha yaʿlamu mā fī al-samāwāti wa-mā fī al-arḍi wa-anna Allāha bi-kulli shayʾin ʿalīmun

English translation

The God has made the Kaʿbah, the inviolable House, the inviolable month, the offerings, and the garlanded offerings a support for people. This is so that you may know that The God knows what is in the heavens and on the earth, and that The God knows all things.

Protected time is thus more than a calendar division. Together with place, rites, and signs of inviolability, it belongs to an order connected with sustaining people’s life.

Yet ش ه ر itself does not mean security or support. That connection comes from the combination of words and the verse’s structure.

Al-Tawbah 9:2 and Al-Tawbah 9:5: four months of respite and the inviolable months

Al-Tawbah 9:2

فَسِيحُوا فِي الْأَرْضِ أَرْبَعَةَ أَشْهُرٍ وَاعْلَمُوا أَنَّكُمْ غَيْرُ مُعْجِزِي اللَّهِ وَأَنَّ اللَّهَ مُخْزِي الْكَافِرِينَ

fa-sīḥū fī al-arḍi arbaʿata ashhurin wa-iʿlamū annakum ghayru muʿjizī Allāhi wa-anna Allāha mukhzī al-kāfirīna

English translation

Travel about the land for four months, and know that you cannot frustrate The God, and that The God brings disgrace upon the disbelievers.

Al-Tawbah 9:5

فَإِذَا انْسَلَخَ الْأَشْهُرُ الْحُرُمُ فَاقْتُلُوا الْمُشْرِكِينَ حَيْثُ وَجَدْتُمُوهُمْ وَخُذُوهُمْ وَاحْصُرُوهُمْ وَاقْعُدُوا لَهُمْ كُلَّ مَرْصَدٍ فَإِنْ تَابُوا وَأَقَامُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَآتَوُا الزَّكَاةَ فَخَلُّوا سَبِيلَهُمْ إِنَّ اللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ

fa-idhā insalakha al-ashhuru al-ḥurumu fa-uqtulū al-mushrikīna ḥaythu wajadtumūhum wa-khudhūhum wa-uḥṣurūhum wa-uqʿudū lahum kulla marṣadin fa-in tābū wa-aqāmū al-ṣalāta wa-ātawū al-zakāta fa-khallū sabīlahum inna Allāha ghafūrun raḥīmun

English translation

When the inviolable months have passed, kill the associators wherever you find them, capture and besiege them, and lie in wait for them at every lookout. If they repent, establish prayer, and give the purifying due, let them go on their way. The God is indeed forgiving and merciful.

Usage analysis

In Al-Tawbah 9:2, أَرْبَعَةَ أَشْهُرٍ sets a four-month period. The expression occurs in the context of declaring release from certain associators and discussing treaty relations.

In Al-Tawbah 9:5, الْأَشْهُرُ الْحُرُمُ stands at the end of a period; insalakha indicates that period’s passing or ending.

The shared number four in Al-Tawbah 9:2 and Al-Tawbah 9:36 is not sufficient to identify the two sets conclusively. Al-Tawbah 9:36 describes four inviolable months within the twelve-month year; Al-Tawbah 9:2 describes a four-month respite in a context of treaties and declared release.

Their precise relationship and the referent of الْأَشْهُرُ الْحُرُمُ in Al-Tawbah 9:5 require examination of the verses’ continuity and treaty setting.

The context itself also prevents unrestricted generalization. Al-Tawbah 9:4 requires honoring the treaties of those who have not broken them; Al-Tawbah 9:6 provides for an associator seeking protection to hear the word of The God and be conveyed to safety.

Even here, “month” is a unit of time. The command’s audience, treaty type, scope of confrontation, and people’s rights come from other expressions and verses.

Lexical study must maintain this boundary: ashhur means months; determining the scope of accompanying commands is a separate question of interpreting the verses.

5. Months as periods of waiting, redress, and responsibility

Another group of Qur’anic uses measures specified durations. The month here is neither the name of a special occasion nor necessarily inviolable; it sets temporal boundaries for rights and responsibilities.

Al-Baqarah 2:226: four months of waiting

Verse text

لِلَّذِينَ يُؤْلُونَ مِنْ نِسَائِهِمْ تَرَبُّصُ أَرْبَعَةِ أَشْهُرٍ فَإِنْ فَاءُوا فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ

li-alladhīna yuʾlūna min nisāʾihim tarabbuṣu arbaʿati ashhurin fa-in fāʾū fa-inna Allāha ghafūrun raḥīmun

English translation

For those who swear abstinence from their wives there is a waiting period of four months. If they return, The God is indeed forgiving and merciful.

Usage analysis

Here أربعة أشهر establishes a time limit for a family situation. Choosing four months belongs to the verse’s prescription, not the meaning of shahr.

The month is the unit of counting. The responsibility and the consequence of the period’s ending are understood through yuʾlūna, tarabbuṣ, and the continuing context.

Al-Baqarah 2:234: four months and ten days

Verse text

وَالَّذِينَ يُتَوَفَّوْنَ مِنْكُمْ وَيَذَرُونَ أَزْوَاجًا يَتَرَبَّصْنَ بِأَنْفُسِهِنَّ أَرْبَعَةَ أَشْهُرٍ وَعَشْرًا فَإِذَا بَلَغْنَ أَجَلَهُنَّ فَلَا جُنَاحَ عَلَيْكُمْ فِيمَا فَعَلْنَ فِي أَنْفُسِهِنَّ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ وَاللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ خَبِيرٌ

wa-alladhīna yutawaffawna minkum wa-yadharūna azwājan yatarabbaṣna bi-anfusihinna arbaʿata ashhurin wa-ʿashran fa-idhā balaghna ajalahunna fa-lā junāḥa ʿalaykum fī-mā faʿalna fī anfusihinna bi-al-maʿrūfi wa-Allāhu bi-mā taʿmalūna khabīrun

English translation

Those among you who die and leave wives: those women shall wait concerning themselves for four months and ten days. When they reach the end of their term, there is no blame upon you for what they do concerning themselves in an accepted manner. The God is aware of what you do.

Usage analysis

The waiting period is specified through months with an additional number of days.

أَرْبَعَةَ أَشْهُرٍ وَعَشْرًا expresses a specific duration. The counted noun after ʿashran is not stated. Grammatical explanations commonly supply nights together with their accompanying days; another account supplies complete day-and-night periods. The duration conventionally understood is four months and ten days, not fourteen months.

Al-Qurṭubī on Al-Baqarah 2:234: the unexpressed counted noun after ʿashran

This clearly illustrates the month as a unit in legal and family reckoning.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:92 and Al-Mujādilah 58:4: two consecutive months

Al-Nisāʾ 4:92 specifies fasting شَهْرَيْنِ مُتَتَابِعَيْنِ as one way of making redress for an accidental killing.

Al-Mujādilah 58:4 uses the same expression in making redress for ẓihār.

Shahrayn is the dual of shahr, and mutatābiʿayn specifies that the two months are consecutive. “Two months” and “two consecutive months” therefore differ in wording; continuity comes from the accompanying adjective, not from shahr itself.

In both verses, the month measures the duration of fasting. Who falls under the prescription in different circumstances, and what alternatives exist for someone unable to fulfill it, require examination of the complete verses.

Al-Ṭalāq 65:4: three months and differing circumstances

In Al-Ṭalāq 65:4, ثَلَاثَةُ أَشْهُرٍ concerns the waiting period of a group of women. The same verse separately addresses pregnant women and connects the end of their term with giving birth.

“Three months” thus measures a legal duration. Identifying those addressed depends on the entire verse and its expressions concerning menstruation, doubt, and pregnancy.

Ashhur alone establishes no independent rule about marriage age, puberty, or the conditions of a valid marriage. Those questions require consideration of the verses concerning marriage, maturity, and human responsibility.

Together, these five uses show the month setting temporal limits for responsibilities. Differences between prescriptions come from each verse’s subject, not a different meaning of ش ه ر.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:15: thirty months of pregnancy and weaning

Verse text

وَوَصَّيْنَا الْإِنْسَانَ بِوَالِدَيْهِ إِحْسَانًا حَمَلَتْهُ أُمُّهُ كُرْهًا وَوَضَعَتْهُ كُرْهًا وَحَمْلُهُ وَفِصَالُهُ ثَلَاثُونَ شَهْرًا حَتَّىٰ إِذَا بَلَغَ أَشُدَّهُ وَبَلَغَ أَرْبَعِينَ سَنَةً قَالَ رَبِّ أَوْزِعْنِي أَنْ أَشْكُرَ نِعْمَتَكَ الَّتِي أَنْعَمْتَ عَلَيَّ وَعَلَىٰ وَالِدَيَّ وَأَنْ أَعْمَلَ صَالِحًا تَرْضَاهُ وَأَصْلِحْ لِي فِي ذُرِّيَّتِي إِنِّي تُبْتُ إِلَيْكَ وَإِنِّي مِنَ الْمُسْلِمِينَ

wa-waṣṣaynā al-insāna bi-wālidayhi iḥsānan ḥamalathu ummuhu kurhan wa-waḍaʿathu kurhan wa-ḥamluhu wa-fiṣāluhu thalāthūna shahran ḥattā idhā balagha ashuddahu wa-balagha arbaʿīna sanatan qāla rabbi awziʿnī an ashkura niʿmataka allatī anʿamta ʿalayya wa-ʿalā wālidayya wa-an aʿmala ṣāliḥan tarḍāhu wa-aṣliḥ lī fī dhurriyyatī innī tubtu ilayka wa-innī mina al-muslimīna

English translation

We enjoined the human being to show kindness to his parents. His mother carried him with hardship and gave birth to him with hardship; his bearing and weaning are thirty months. When he reaches full strength and reaches forty years, he says: “My Lord, enable me to give thanks for Your favor bestowed upon me and my parents, to do righteous work that pleases You, and make my offspring righteous for me. I have indeed turned back to You, and I am among those who submit.”

Usage analysis

In ثَلَاثُونَ شَهْرًا, the month counts the duration of bearing and weaning.

The subject differs from the inviolable months and Ramadan, but the difference lies in the verse’s topic rather than a changed meaning of shahr.

The same verse also mentions أَرْبَعِينَ سَنَةً. Sanah supplies the larger unit of years, while shahr supplies a smaller unit for specifying a period.

Their occurrence together shows the Qur’an using different temporal measures to express stages of human life.

The thirty-month expression should not yield medical details or further legal conclusions without separately examining bearing and weaning. Such reasoning requires comparison of verses and thematic investigation, not merely the etymology of shahr.

Sabaʾ 34:12: a month’s distance or duration in describing the wind’s movement

Verse text

وَلِسُلَيْمَانَ الرِّيحَ غُدُوُّهَا شَهْرٌ وَرَوَاحُهَا شَهْرٌ وَأَسَلْنَا لَهُ عَيْنَ الْقِطْرِ وَمِنَ الْجِنِّ مَنْ يَعْمَلُ بَيْنَ يَدَيْهِ بِإِذْنِ رَبِّهِ وَمَنْ يَزِغْ مِنْهُمْ عَنْ أَمْرِنَا نُذِقْهُ مِنْ عَذَابِ السَّعِيرِ

wa-li-sulaymāna al-rīḥa ghuduwwuhā shahrun wa-rawāḥuhā shahrun wa-asalnā lahu ʿayna al-qiṭri wa-mina al-jinni man yaʿmalu bayna yadayhi bi-idhni rabbihi wa-man yazigh minhum ʿan amrinā nudhiqhu min ʿadhābi al-saʿīri

English translation

And for Solomon, the wind: its morning course was a month and its afternoon course a month. We made a spring of copper flow for him. Among the jinn were those who worked before him by his Lord’s permission; whoever among them deviated from Our command, We made him taste the punishment of the blazing fire.

Usage analysis

This is a distinctive use of shahr. It describes neither a waiting period nor a named month, but the movement of the wind for Solomon.

غُدُوُّهَا concerns its morning movement; رَوَاحُهَا concerns movement in the later part of the day. Each is assigned a measure of shahr.

A common linguistic reading understands a comparison between the wind’s movement during part of a day and a distance ordinarily traveled in a month.

The verse, however, supplies no separate word for “distance.” Direct translation and contextual explanation should therefore remain distinct.

A direct translation can preserve the verse’s compact construction, then explain that a month here measures the extent or amount of movement.

This should not be used without evidence to extract modern physical theories of speed, space, or time. What is clear lexically is the use of shahr as a familiar scale for describing extraordinary movement in the verse’s context.

Al-Qadr 97:3: a thousand months in an expression of superiority

Verse text

لَيْلَةُ الْقَدْرِ خَيْرٌ مِنْ أَلْفِ شَهْرٍ

laylatu al-qadri khayrun min alfi shahrin

English translation

The Night of Determination is better than a thousand months.

Usage analysis

This brief verse illustrates another important use of ش ه ر.

أَلْفِ شَهْرٍ combines a number with a temporal unit. Its direct meaning is “a thousand months.”

The month supplies a standard of comparison. The verse expresses the superiority of the Night of Determination over a very long period.

A thousand months can mathematically be converted into years, but that conversion is not the verse’s lexical meaning. Nor should a thousand become a merely unlimited symbol without independent evidence, or the verse’s entire purpose be reduced to calculating years and days.

At the linguistic level, the Qur’an compares one night with a thousand months and declares that night’s value superior.

Shahr therefore remains the recognized temporal unit; comparison and evaluation come from the sentence’s structure.

7. Distinguishing morphological forms and related roots

شَهْر, أَشْهُر, and شُهُور

شَهْر is singular, شَهْرَيْنِ dual, and أَشْهُر and شُهُور are two broken plurals.

Arabic grammar commonly classifies أَشْهُر as a plural of paucity and شُهُور as a plural of abundance. This should not become an absolute rule that every ashhur specifies only a particular small number or that every shuhūr necessarily implies a very large number.

The Qur’an uses ashhur for specified groups of months and shuhūr in the general count of the year’s months. This accords with the grammatical tendency, but context and the accompanying number determine each use’s meaning.

In أَرْبَعَةَ أَشْهُرٍ, ثَلَاثَةُ أَشْهُرٍ, and أَرْبَعَةَ أَشْهُرٍ وَعَشْرًا, the accompanying numeral specifies the number of months, not the plural pattern itself.

SH H R compared with H L L (هِلَال)

Hilāl and ahillah belong to ه ل ل, not ش ه ر.

A crescent is a visible celestial sign; shahr is a temporal period recognized and counted through the moon’s changes.

Although Arabic sometimes uses shahr for a crescent, these two root families should not be merged.

Al-Baqarah 2:189 concerns crescents and timekeeping; Al-Tawbah 9:36 concerns the number of months in the year. The verses are related within one temporal system, but approach it from different angles.

Study of H L L

SH H R compared with Q M R (قَمَر)

Qamar names the celestial body, the moon. In predominant Qur’anic use, shahr names the monthly period.

The celestial moon can mark passing time, but its name is not identical to the temporal unit.

Translation must therefore attend to context. Persian māh can denote either the celestial moon or a month, whereas Qur’anic Arabic distinguishes them through qamar and shahr.

Study of Q M R

SH H R compared with SH H D (شَهِدَ)

These roots share only their first two letters.

Shahida concerns presence, testimony, and observation, while Qur’anic shahr is a temporal unit.

They occur together in فَمَنْ شَهِدَ مِنْكُمُ الشَّهْرَ in Al-Baqarah 2:185. Their proximity may mislead a reader who overlooks the different roots.

The verb shahida requires separate examination. Being present during the month, sighting the crescent, and testifying are not equivalent without grammatical and contextual investigation.

Study of SH H D

SH H R compared with S N W and ʿ W M

Sanah and ʿām concern years, while shahr is the monthly unit.

Although counting years and months is connected in the Qur’an, different roots do not establish an absolute evaluative or philosophical opposition. For example, their difference alone cannot establish that sanah always means a year of hardship and ʿām always a year of ease.

Their relationship must be examined in each verse’s context.

Study of S N W; Study of ʿ W M

Arabic شَهْر compared with Persian شهر

Persian-speaking readers should note that Arabic شَهْر, “month,” does not share a root with Persian شهر, “city” or “large settlement.”

Persian shahr belongs to an older Iranian word family concerning rule, land, and dominion; the meaning “city” later became established in Persian.

Their identical written shape should not introduce urban life, political territory, or settlement into the Qur’anic root ش ه ر.

Although written identically in modern Persian, the two words differ in linguistic origin and meaning.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

In Persian Qur’an translation, شَهْر should generally be rendered māh, “month.” This is natural Persian and preserves its established direct meaning.

شَهْرُ رَمَضَانَ is appropriately “the month of Ramadan”; الشَّهْرَ الْحَرَامَ, “the inviolable month”; and أَشْهُرٌ مَعْلُومَاتٌ, “known months” or “recognized months.”

شَهْرَيْنِ مُتَتَابِعَيْنِ is accurately “two consecutive months.” ثَلَاثُونَ شَهْرًا should remain “thirty months,” and أَلْفِ شَهْرٍ, “a thousand months.”

In Sabaʾ 34:12, the initial translation should preserve the compact construction as far as possible, leaving the comparison with a distance ordinarily traveled in a month to a separate reflection.

Shahr should not be translated as “fasting time” in Ramadan passages, “the ritual season” in pilgrimage passages, or “a period of security” in inviolable-month passages, even though contextual reflection may relate these concepts to those periods.

Direct translation should preserve “month,” leaving responsibilities, rites, inviolability, and security to the verse’s other words and a separate reflection.

9. Final synthesis

In Arabic, ش ه ر concerns becoming visible, becoming known, and making apparent. Within this family, shahr has also denoted the celestial moon or its crescent and the temporal period recognized through lunar changes.

However, every Qur’anic occurrence belongs to the noun shahr in singular, dual, or plural form and carries the temporal meaning “month.”

A month is a real unit for ordering and measuring time in the Qur’an. A year consists of twelve months; Ramadan is a particular month; pilgrimage relates to known months; four months of the year are declared inviolable; and durations such as two months of fasting, three months of waiting, or four months and ten days use this unit.

The root also supplies a comparative measure for Solomon’s wind, while a thousand months expresses the superiority of one night in Al-Qadr.

These uses show that shahr itself means neither worship, inviolability, security, social fame, nor hardship or ease. Where those concepts occur, they come from accompanying words and the verse’s structure.

The connection between a month, the crescent, and timekeeping does not authorize translating shahr as “crescent” in every verse. The crescent marks time; the month, as a temporal period, has its own established meaning.

Final definition: In Qur’anic usage, شَهْر is a countable monthly period of time within the recognized system of months. It can provide a basis for determining dates, durations, rites, inviolabilities, and human responsibilities.

Here, visibility helps explain the word’s background, while “month” is its direct Qur’anic meaning. Maintaining this distinction preserves accurate translation and prevents interpretive conclusions and prescriptions concerning a month from being imposed upon the word’s lexical definition.
