The root f-z-ʿ — Sudden alarm, agitation, and seeking refuge in danger

Qur’anic and lexical forms

فَفَزِعَ / فَزِعُوا
(fa-faziʿa / faziʿū)
Becoming alarmed

فَزَع / الْفَزَعُ الْأَكْبَرُ
(fazaʿ / al-fazaʿu l-akbar)
Alarm; the greatest terror

فُزِّعَ عَنْ قُلُوبِهِمْ
(fuzziʿa ʿan qulūbihim)
Fear removed from their hearts

The three-consonant Arabic root f-z-ʿ concerns alarm, intense fear, and agitation before an actual or possible danger. فَزِعَ describes someone losing composure and becoming frightened or anxious because of an alarming event, sudden news, or a sense of danger.

Other uses belong to the same family: فَزِعَ إِلَيْهِ means seeking refuge and help from someone, while فَزِعَ لَهُ means hastening to another’s aid. فَزَّعَ عَنْهُ can also mean removing someone’s fear.

This family therefore expresses more than fear itself: human responses to danger, seeking refuge, giving aid, and the removal of fear also fall within its range.

F-z-ʿ occurs six times in six Qur’anic verses. Its uses range from David’s alarm at the litigants’ sudden entrance to widespread terror in resurrection scenes, the safety of those who do good, and fear being removed from hearts.

Crucially, fazaʿ does not itself mean sin, faithlessness, or cowardice. Alarm can be a natural response to danger, although some verses use the word for people’s condition when facing the consequences of their actions and their ultimate fate.

1. The root in pre-Qur’anic Arabic

Alarm and agitation

In early Arabic, فَزِعَ يَفْزَعُ فَزَعًا means becoming afraid or alarmed.

Lexical explanations connect fazaʿ with ذُعْر, فَرَق, and رَوْع, each concerning aspects of fear and agitation.

Fazaʿ commonly emphasizes a sudden disturbance of ordinary composure in response to something alarming.

For example, someone hearing a breaking sound at home during the night may become alarmed. They do not yet know what has happened, but possible danger has disturbed their composure.

They might get up, approach the door, flee, or ask someone for help.

All these reactions may result from fazaʿ, but none alone is the word’s meaning.

Someone may become alarmed without moving. Another may respond to fear by helping others rather than fleeing.

Thus alarm, flight, and seeking help are three related but distinct concepts.

Evidence from Salāmah ibn Jandal’s poetry

The pre-Islamic poet Salāmah ibn Jandal describes his people’s readiness to help:

كُنَّا إِذَا مَا أَتَانَا صَارِخٌ فَزِعٌ
كَانَ الصُّرَاخُ لَهُ قَرْعَ الظَّنَابِيبِ

Kunna idha ma atana sarikhun fazi'un / kana s-surakhu lahu qar'a z-zanabibi.

Translation: “When an alarmed caller for help came to us, our response to his cry was to move swiftly and rise to his aid.”

صَارِخٌ فَزِعٌ here is someone calling out anxiously for help.

قرع الظنابيب evokes swift movement and readiness to face danger. Older explanations relate it to horses moving rapidly or warriors preparing to give aid.

The verse juxtaposes two situations: the fear of someone seeking help and the action of those hastening to assist him.

Some lexical explanations interpret faziʿ here as مستغيث, someone seeking help. Another explanation takes it as a frightened person who consequently asks for assistance.

These readings are close but not identical.

The first emphasizes the request for help; the second sees fear as its cause.

The verse clearly connects fazaʿ with danger, anxiety, and seeking help.

It is preserved in the collected poetry attributed to Salāmah ibn Jandal and in older literary sources. Different transmissions begin with كُنَّا, إِنَّا, or, in some versions, إِذَا مَا أَتَانَا.

These variants do not change the basic occurrence of fazaʿ. As with other pre-Islamic poetry, however, the text reaches us through later transmission.

Fazaʿ as giving aid

Another Arabic branch of this root concerns helping someone in distress.

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Kalḥabah al-Yarbūʿī reads:

فَقُلْتُ لِكَأْسٍ أَلْجِمِيهَا فَإِنَّمَا
حَلَلْتُ الْكَثِيبَ مِنْ زَرُودَ لِأَفْزَعَا

Fa-qultu li-Ka'sin aljimiha fa-innama / halaltu l-kathiba min Zaruda li-afza'a.

Translation: “I said to Kaʾs: Put the bridle on her, for I have camped on the dune at Zarūd to hasten to help.”

لِأَفْزَعَا here means hastening to assist and give aid.

Kaʾs is the name of the person addressed. The verse concerns readiness to help in a difficult situation.

Lexical accounts explain this use with أُغِيثَ: giving aid to someone who calls for help.

This example shows that early Arabic verbs of f-z-ʿ could express both fear and a supportive response to another person’s fear and danger.

An important distinction emerges here.

Someone who is frightened may seek refuge with another person.

The person offering refuge and assistance may act to help them.

Arabic accordingly recognizes both فَزِعَ إِلَيْهِ for seeking refuge and فَزِعَ لَهُ for giving aid.

Ibn Fāris likewise distinguishes two branches: ذعر, alarm, and إغاثة, assistance.

Their relationship can be explained through danger and human responses to it, but without sufficient historical evidence we should not claim that assistance necessarily developed from fear at a particular stage.

2. Linguistic forms and semantic core

The main recorded forms include:

  • فَزِعَ: became alarmed, afraid, or agitated.
  • فَزَع: alarm and fear.
  • فَزِع: an alarmed person; in some uses, someone seeking aid.
  • فَازِع: afraid or alarmed.
  • أَفْزَعَ: frightened or alarmed; in other constructions, gave aid.
  • فَزَّعَ: frightened; in فَزَّعَ عَنْهُ, removed fear.
  • تَفَزَّعَ: became alarmed or entered a state of fear.
  • فَزَّاعَة: someone who frequently frightens others; in another use, someone frequently alarmed.
  • مَفْزَع: a refuge, shelter, or person approached in danger.
  • مَفْزَعَة: something frightening; in another use, a place of refuge.
  • إِفْزَاع: frightening; in some lexical uses, giving aid.
  • تَفْزِيع: frightening or removing fear, depending on the construction.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an.

Only three main structures appear there:

فَزِعَ in different verbal forms, describing becoming alarmed.

فَزَع as a noun, describing alarm itself.

فُزِّعَ in Form II, describing fear being removed from hearts.

This family should not be defined solely as “fear” while ignoring its other branches. Nor should an artificial single meaning such as “movement from danger to safety” be devised for every derivative.

Such a definition might explain some uses, but does not adequately identify the direct meaning of fazaʿ in resurrection scenes or David’s alarm.

A more precise definition is:

Fazaʿ is alarm and agitation when facing danger or an alarming event. Other constructions in the family also express seeking refuge, giving aid, and removing this fear.

3. David’s alarm at the litigants’ sudden entrance

Ṣād 38:22

إِذْ دَخَلُوا عَلَىٰ دَاوُودَ فَفَزِعَ مِنْهُمْ ۖ قَالُوا لَا تَخَفْ ۖ خَصْمَانِ بَغَىٰ بَعْضُنَا عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ فَاحْكُمْ بَيْنَنَا بِالْحَقِّ وَلَا تُشْطِطْ وَاهْدِنَا إِلَىٰ سَوَاءِ الصِّرَاطِ

Idh dakhalu 'ala Dawuda fa-fazi'a minhum; qalu la takhaf; khasmani bagha ba'duna 'ala ba'din fa-hkum baynana bi-l-haqqi wa-la tushtit wa-hdina ila sawa'i s-sirat.

Translation: “When they entered upon David, he became alarmed at them. They said: Do not fear. We are two disputing parties, one of whom has wronged the other. Judge between us in truth, do not depart from justice, and guide us to the right way.”

فَفَزِعَ describes David’s reaction when a group suddenly enters the place where he is.

The preceding verse, Ṣād 38:21, says that they climbed over the wall of the sanctuary.

This is not an ordinary way of entering.

If people suddenly enter someone’s private space by an unfamiliar route, alarm is a natural response: the person does not yet know who they are or what they intend.

Fazaʿ and khawf

Two different roots occur in the verse:

«فَفَزِعَ مِنْهُمْ»

Fa-fazi'a minhum.

and

«لَا تَخَفْ»

La takhaf.

Fazaʿ here indicates David’s agitation and sudden alarm.

Khawf expresses fear of danger or an unwelcome consequence.

The concepts are close but not precisely identical.

Someone may be alarmed by a sudden event before having time to assess its actual danger.

Their fear may then diminish as they understand the situation.

In David’s story, unexpected entry causes alarm, and the litigants’ words seek to reassure him.

Did David’s alarm indicate weak faith?

The verse itself makes no such judgment.

Ṣād 38:17 describes David as a strong servant who repeatedly turns to God.

Ṣād 38:20 also mentions his wisdom and capacity for judgment.

These qualities do not prevent the human reaction of alarm when strangers enter suddenly.

Fazaʿ here should therefore not be taken as proof of sin, cowardice, or deficient faith.

The story subsequently addresses David’s judgment and trial. That subject should be examined through his words and conduct in the following verses, rather than inferred from his alarm when the litigants arrived.

4. Alarm when the Trumpet is blown

Al-Naml 27:87

وَيَوْمَ يُنْفَخُ فِي الصُّورِ فَفَزِعَ مَنْ فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَمَنْ فِي الْأَرْضِ إِلَّا مَنْ شَاءَ اللَّهُ ۚ وَكُلٌّ أَتَوْهُ دَاخِرِينَ

Wa-yawma yunfakhu fi s-suri fa-fazi'a man fi s-samawati wa-man fi l-ardi illa man sha'a llahu; wa-kullun atawhu dakhirin.

Translation: “And on the day the Trumpet is blown, whoever is in the heavens and whoever is on earth will be alarmed, except whom The God wills; and all will come to Him humbled.”

Fazaʿ here concerns a great and encompassing event.

فَفَزِعَ is a perfect verb, but because the sentence describes a future event, its meaning is rendered with future reference.

مَنْ فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَمَنْ فِي الْأَرْضِ expresses the extensive reach of the alarm.

The subject is more than one individual’s fear before a limited danger: the event encompasses the inhabitants of the heavens and earth.

إِلَّا مَنْ شَاءَ اللَّهُ

The verse immediately states an exception:

«إِلَّا مَنْ شَاءَ اللَّهُ»

Illa man sha'a llah.

That is, except whom God wills.

This qualification must not be omitted from translation or analysis.

The Qur’an does not say every being experiences this alarm without exception.

The phrase itself, however, does not identify the names or number of those exempted.

Without evidence, the exception cannot be restricted to one particular group.

Al-Naml 27:89 describes people who come with goodness and are safe from alarm that day. This matters for understanding the promise of safety, but juxtaposing the verses alone does not determine every detail of the exception in verse 87.

Does fazaʿ mean death or unconsciousness?

No. Its lexical meaning is alarm and agitation.

Intense fear may cause fainting or another physical reaction, but these consequences are not the root’s direct meaning.

Al-Zumar 39:68 uses صَعِقَ when describing the blowing of the Trumpet. This belongs to a different root and should not be equated with fazaʿ without examination.

Nor does the verse in al-Naml alone establish the number of trumpet blasts or the complete sequence of resurrection events.

It clearly describes widespread alarm at the event, an exception by divine will, and a humbled coming before God.

5. Safety from alarm on that day

Al-Naml 27:89

مَنْ جَاءَ بِالْحَسَنَةِ فَلَهُ خَيْرٌ مِنْهَا وَهُمْ مِنْ فَزَعٍ يَوْمَئِذٍ آمِنُونَ

Man ja'a bi-l-hasanati fa-lahu khayrun minha wa-hum min faza'in yawma'idhin aminun.

Translation: “Whoever comes with goodness will have better than it, and they will be safe from alarm that day.”

This verse follows descriptions of great resurrection events.

Al-Naml 27:87 described widespread alarm.

Verse 89 identifies a group enjoying safety.

فَزَعٍ here is a noun referring to alarm itself.

آمِنُونَ belongs to ʾ-m-n and expresses their safety.

Two distinct concepts are thus placed together: alarm and safety from it.

Goodness and safety

The verse begins with a condition:

«مَنْ جَاءَ بِالْحَسَنَةِ»

Man ja'a bi-l-hasanah.

That is, whoever comes with goodness.

It then describes a better reward and safety on that day.

The promise of safety is therefore connected with bringing goodness.

Fazaʿ itself does not mean punishment of sinners. It names alarm, while safety from it is the condition promised to the group described.

Nor should الْحَسَنَة be restricted to one particular act or religious designation without independent examination.

The verse speaks of goodness; understanding its scope requires examining other Qur’anic uses concerning faith, righteous action, and good conduct.

Does safety mean the absence of every difficult experience?

آمِنُونَ concerns safety from the alarm in question.

The expression alone does not explain every detail of human experience during the stages of resurrection.

It should not become the basis of a theory about consciousness, feeling, or the physical character of existence in the hereafter.

The promise itself is clear, however: those described will be protected from that day’s alarm.

The following verse, al-Naml 27:90, describes those who come with evil and face the consequences of their conduct.

This contrast shows the relationship between conduct and outcome; it does not make fazaʿ itself mean reckoning or punishment.

6. الْفَزَعُ الْأَكْبَرُ: the great terror

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:103

لَا يَحْزُنُهُمُ الْفَزَعُ الْأَكْبَرُ وَتَتَلَقَّاهُمُ الْمَلَائِكَةُ هَٰذَا يَوْمُكُمُ الَّذِي كُنْتُمْ تُوعَدُونَ

La yahzunuhumu l-faza'u l-akbaru wa-tatalaqqahumu l-mala'ikatu hadha yawmukumu lladhi kuntum tu'adun.

Translation: “The greatest terror will not grieve them, and the angels will welcome them: This is your day, which you were promised.”

Here الْفَزَع is qualified by الْأَكْبَر.

Fazaʿ means alarm itself.

Al-akbar expresses its exceptional magnitude and intensity.

Fazaʿ alone therefore does not mean “the greatest terror”; that feature comes from its modifier.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:101 describes those for whom goodness has already been decreed and who are kept far from the Fire.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:102 describes their peace and distance from the sound of the Fire.

Verse 103 then mentions the great terror and the angels’ welcome.

This connection shows that the verse concerns a group’s safety and peace before a terrifying event.

Alarm and grief

The sentence reads:

«لَا يَحْزُنُهُمُ الْفَزَعُ الْأَكْبَرُ»

La yahzunuhumu l-faza'u l-akbar.

It employs two different roots.

Fazaʿ is alarm.

Ḥuzn is grief.

Someone may become alarmed by danger and remain sorrowful afterward.

Yet alarm and grief do not always occur together.

Someone may fear a sudden danger but regain composure once it passes.

The verse states that the great terror will not grieve the group described.

Without attention to its construction, this statement should not be turned into a theory about the absolute absence of every feeling throughout the hereafter.

The promise should not be weakened either: those identified in the context enjoy security and the angels’ welcome in the face of that great terror.

Is the greatest terror the alarm at the blowing of the Trumpet?

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:103 and al-Naml 27:87 have a clear thematic relationship.

Both concern great resurrection events and speak of fazaʿ.

Al-Naml 27:87 describes alarm when the Trumpet is blown.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:103 describes the great terror and a group’s protection from it.

The images may refer to one event or to aspects of related events.

The expression الْفَزَعُ الْأَكْبَرُ itself, however, does not establish its exact timing or mechanism.

It should therefore not be confined to one specific stage of resurrection without evidence.

7. فُزِّعَ عَنْ قُلُوبِهِمْ: fear removed from hearts

Sabaʾ 34:23

وَلَا تَنْفَعُ الشَّفَاعَةُ عِنْدَهُ إِلَّا لِمَنْ أَذِنَ لَهُ ۚ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا فُزِّعَ عَنْ قُلُوبِهِمْ قَالُوا مَاذَا قَالَ رَبُّكُمْ ۖ قَالُوا الْحَقَّ ۖ وَهُوَ الْعَلِيُّ الْكَبِيرُ

Wa-la tanfa'u sh-shafa'atu 'indahu illa li-man adhina lahu; hatta idha fuzzi'a 'an qulubihim qalu madha qala rabbukum; qalu l-haqqa; wa-huwa l-'aliyyu l-kabir.

Translation: “Intercession does not benefit before Him except for one to whom He has given permission; until, when fear is removed from their hearts, they say: What did your Lord say? They say: The truth. And He is the Most High, the Great.”

This verse is important evidence for the root’s semantic range.

Elsewhere in the Qur’an, فَزِعَ means becoming alarmed.

Here, however, فُزِّعَ عَنْ قُلُوبِهِمْ refers to fear being removed from hearts.

Why has the verb’s meaning changed?

فُزِّعَ is a passive perfect verb in Form II.

An Arabic transitive verb from this family can mean frightening someone.

But فَزَّعَ عَنْهُ is also an established construction for removing someone’s fear.

The preposition عَنْ matters here:

«عَنْ قُلُوبِهِمْ»

'An qulubihim.

That is, from their hearts.

The complete construction therefore describes fear departing or being removed from hearts.

Translating فُزِّعَ عَنْ قُلُوبِهِمْ as “their hearts were frightened” reverses the sentence’s meaning.

An appropriate rendering is:

“When fear was removed from their hearts.”

This shows how morphology and a preposition can direct a root’s meaning differently.

Who is being described?

Sabaʾ 34:22 discusses those invoked apart from God, who possess no independent ownership, partnership, or supporting role in relation to Him.

Verse 23 also makes intercession conditional on divine permission.

It then describes fear being removed from hearts and a question about the Lord’s words.

Older explanations commonly associate the pronoun in قلوبهم with angels, although other readings of its antecedent and the conversation’s setting also exist.

The linguistic construction establishes that fear is removed from a group’s hearts and that they ask about their Lord’s words.

Details about how revelation is heard, the kind of sound, or angels’ physical condition do not follow from f-z-ʿ itself.

قَالُوا الْحَقَّ also identifies the answer to the question as truth.

This connection with permission and intercession matters, but a root study should not become a full discussion of intercession.

Is removing fear the same as removing grief?

No.

Fazaʿ is alarm; ḥuzn is grief.

Removing one may bring broader peace, but the words do not mean the same thing.

This verse concerns removing alarm from hearts.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:103 states that the great terror does not grieve a particular group.

The constructions show a close but distinct relationship between alarm and grief.

8. Alarm among those who can no longer escape

Sabaʾ 34:51

وَلَوْ تَرَىٰ إِذْ فَزِعُوا فَلَا فَوْتَ وَأُخِذُوا مِنْ مَكَانٍ قَرِيبٍ

Wa-law tara idh fazi'u fa-la fawta wa-ukhidhu min makanin qarib.

Translation: “If only you could see when they became alarmed: there was no escape, and they were seized from a nearby place.”

فَزِعُوا is a plural perfect verb.

The alarm is attributed to people facing an inescapable situation.

The verse continues:

«فَلَا فَوْتَ»

Fa-la fawt.

That is, there is no release or escape beyond reach.

Then:

«وَأُخِذُوا مِنْ مَكَانٍ قَرِيبٍ»

Wa-ukhidhu min makanin qarib.

That is, they are seized from a nearby place.

These three parts are clearly related.

First, alarm arises.

Then it becomes clear that escape is impossible.

Finally, their capture is stated.

Fazaʿ itself does not mean being captured or seized. Those events are expressed by the verse’s other verbs and phrases.

Alarm and awareness of one’s fate

In Sabaʾ 34:52, the same group say they have believed in the truth, but the passage describes how remote access to it is in that situation.

Sabaʾ 34:53 mentions their earlier rejection.

The context connects their alarm in verse 51 with suddenly confronting an outcome they had previously refused to accept.

For example, someone might repeatedly receive warnings about wrongful conduct’s consequences yet dismiss them. When the actual consequence arrives and can no longer be prevented, they become alarmed.

This example does not make fazaʿ lexically mean regret or admitting error.

Someone may become alarmed without genuine repentance or reform.

Regret, admission, repentance, and alarm are different concepts and should be examined in context.

9. Distinguishing alarm, fear, terror, and distress

F-z-ʿ (فَزَع) and kh-w-f (خَوْف)

Khawf concerns fear of danger or an unwelcome outcome.

Such fear may precede the danger and continue for some time.

Fazaʿ often emphasizes sudden reaction and agitation before something alarming.

In Ṣād 38:22, David is alarmed by people entering unexpectedly, and they say لا تخف, “Do not fear.”

Their occurrence together shows that the roots share a semantic field but may differ in emphasis.

There should be no absolute rule that khawf is always calm and gradual while fazaʿ is always sudden. These are tendencies of usage; each occurrence requires contextual examination.

F-z-ʿ (فَزَع) and r-w-ʿ (رَوْع)

Rawʿ in Arabic concerns alarm and fear affecting the heart.

Hūd 11:74 says:

«فَلَمَّا ذَهَبَ عَنْ إِبْرَاهِيمَ الرَّوْعُ»

Fa-lamma dhahaba 'an Ibrahima r-raw'.

That is, when alarm had departed from Abraham.

This is semantically close to فُزِّعَ عَنْ قُلُوبِهِمْ in Sabaʾ 34:23.

In both, fear is removed from someone.

Rawʿ and fazaʿ nevertheless belong to independent roots.

Their closeness also does not establish identical intensity and quality in every use.

F-z-ʿ (فَزَع) and r-ʿ-b (رُعْب)

Ruʿb concerns intense terror.

Al-Kahf 18:18 describes a sight that could fill someone with terror.

Fazaʿ can also be intense, but the word does not always specify the same degree of intensity.

In Ṣād 38:22, David is alarmed by the litigants’ sudden entrance.

In al-Naml 27:87, alarm accompanies an encompassing event.

Its intensity and extent are therefore determined by the situation and the sentence’s descriptions.

F-z-ʿ (فَزَع) and j-z-ʿ (جَزَع)

Jazaʿ concerns distress and loss of steadfastness under suffering or fear.

Fazaʿ is alarm itself.

Someone may be alarmed by danger yet remain steadfast in conduct.

They may also respond to the same danger with distress and disordered behavior.

Fazaʿ does not, in itself, mean jazaʿ.

This distinction matters for understanding the responses of Prophets and righteous people. Natural fear alone does not prove an inability to remain steadfast or an absence of faith.

F-z-ʿ (فَزَع) and f-z-z (اِسْتَفَزَّ)

Similar consonants and related meanings can lead these roots to be confused.

Qur’anic f-z-ʿ refers to alarm and its removal.

F-z-z in al-Isrāʾ concerns inciting, unsettling, and dislodging.

Istifzāz may cause fazaʿ, but not every provocation or displacement is alarm.

Nor does every experience of alarm result from another person’s deliberate provocation.

Someone may be alarmed by a natural event without anyone intending to frighten them.

F-z-ʿ (فَزَع) and f-r-r (فَرَّ)

Firār is the act of fleeing and moving away from danger or an unwanted situation.

Fazaʿ is alarm that may cause flight.

Someone can become alarmed and remain still, just as they may move away from danger to protect life without intense fear.

In the Qur’an, Moses fled a threat to his life, whereas David was alarmed at the litigants’ sudden entrance, but the story does not describe him fleeing.

This illustrates fear and flight as separate actions or conditions.

10. Morphology and syntax

F-z-ʿ is a sound three-consonant root.

Its basic verb is فَزِعَ يَفْزَعُ.

The perfect verb

فَفَزِعَ appears in al-Naml 27:87 and Ṣād 38:22.

The first fāʾ is a connective particle; فَزِعَ means “became alarmed.”

Sabaʾ 34:51 uses the plural فَزِعُوا.

These three uses are active, attributing alarm to their subjects.

The noun فَزَع

In al-Anbiyāʾ 21:103:

«الْفَزَعُ الْأَكْبَرُ»

Al-faza'u l-akbar.

In al-Naml 27:89:

«مِنْ فَزَعٍ يَوْمَئِذٍ»

Min faza'in yawma'idhin.

In both, fazaʿ is a noun expressing the condition of alarm itself.

Its definiteness or indefiniteness relates to the sentence’s construction.

In al-Anbiyāʾ, الْفَزَع appears with الْأَكْبَر.

In al-Naml, فَزَع follows مِنْ and relates to safety on that day.

Without further evidence, this grammatical distinction should not become two entirely separate kinds of alarm in the hereafter.

فُزِّعَ: the Form II passive verb

In Sabaʾ 34:23, فُزِّعَ is a Form II passive perfect verb.

The passive construction alone does not name the agent removing the fear.

عَنْ قُلُوبِهِمْ indicates that alarm has been removed from their hearts.

This illustrates the importance of prepositions.

Translating solely from the root’s form, without attention to عَنْ, might incorrectly produce “they were frightened.”

The complete construction means “fear was removed from their hearts.”

Nor does this verse establish that every Form II verb from f-z-ʿ always means removing fear. That sense belongs to the particular construction and use.

مِنْ and إِلَى

Arabic recognizes the following constructions:

فَزِعَ مِنْهُ — He became alarmed by him or it.

فَزِعَ إِلَيْهِ — In danger, he sought refuge and help with him.

The first highlights what causes alarm.

The second identifies the person or source approached for help.

This shows why all constructions of a root cannot be interpreted without examining their prepositions.

11. Challenges in understanding and translation

The first error is equating fazaʿ with cowardice.

David experiences alarm in Ṣād 38:22, responding to the litigants’ sudden entrance.

The Qur’an does not present this reaction itself as a sin or character flaw.

The second error is restricting fazaʿ to ordinary, insignificant fear.

In al-Naml 27:87 and al-Anbiyāʾ 21:103, the root occurs in great resurrection scenes.

Fazaʿ can therefore be intense and widespread. Its degree is established by context and modifiers such as الْأَكْبَر.

The third error is equating alarm with unconsciousness, death, or flight.

These may sometimes result from fear, but are not the root’s direct meaning.

The fourth error is overlooking the removal of fear in Sabaʾ 34:23.

فُزِّعَ عَنْ قُلُوبِهِمْ describes fear being lifted from hearts, not increased.

The fifth error is omitting إِلَّا مَنْ شَاءَ اللَّهُ in al-Naml 27:87.

The Qur’an describes extensive alarm while explicitly stating an exception.

The sixth error is treating all stages of resurrection as identical.

Different verses about alarm, safety, reckoning, and coming before God may describe related events. Without evidence, they should not all be confined to one moment and one specific condition.

Finally, natural human alarm should not be equated completely with moral fear of wrongdoing or informed reverence before God.

These concepts may be related, but differ linguistically and contextually.

12. Translation proposals for Forqan

For general uses of فَزِعَ, suitable equivalents include “became alarmed,” “was afraid,” and “became agitated with fear.”

فَزَع may be rendered “alarm,” “terror,” or “intense fear.”

فَزَّعَ عَنْهُ in the construction for removing fear means “removed fear from him.”

The lexical expression فَزِعَ إِلَيْهِ means “sought refuge with him” or “asked him for help.”

Qur’anic forms

In Ṣād 38:22:

“When they entered upon David, he became alarmed at them.”

In al-Naml 27:87:

“And on the day the Trumpet is blown, whoever is in the heavens and whoever is on earth will be alarmed, except whom The God wills.”

In al-Naml 27:89:

“And they will be safe from alarm that day.”

In al-Anbiyāʾ 21:103:

“The greatest terror will not grieve them.”

In Sabaʾ 34:23:

“Until, when fear is removed from their hearts.”

In Sabaʾ 34:51:

“If only you could see when they became alarmed: there was no escape.”

A brief lexical explanation may retain the following distinction:

Brief root meaning: alarm and agitation before danger; in other constructions, seeking refuge, giving aid, and removing fear.

Meaning in Ṣād 38:22: David’s alarm at the litigants’ unexpected entrance.

Meaning in al-Naml 27:87: widespread alarm when the Trumpet is blown, with the exception stated in the verse.

Meaning in al-Naml 27:89: the alarm from which those who do good are safe that day.

Meaning in al-Anbiyāʾ 21:103: the great terror of the hereafter that does not grieve the group described.

Meaning in Sabaʾ 34:23: fear being removed from hearts; a passive perfect verb.

Meaning in Sabaʾ 34:51: alarm on confronting an outcome from which escape is no longer possible.

Each occurrence’s explanation should emphasize its particular form and context; historical branches and comparisons with related roots should remain separate.

13. Conclusion

In early Arabic, f-z-ʿ concerns alarm, fear, and agitation before danger. Pre-Islamic poetic evidence shows this condition accompanying requests for help and others hastening to assist someone in distress.

Arabic also recognizes uses in this family for seeking refuge, giving aid, and removing alarm.

These branches relate to a shared human situation: people become frightened before danger, may seek refuge, and others may act to relieve their fear or rescue them.

This illustrative relationship does not prove the historical sequence in which all the root’s uses developed.

The root occurs six times in the Qur’an.

In David’s story, alarm is a human response to people entering suddenly.

In al-Naml 27:87, fazaʿ concerns the great event of the Trumpet’s blowing.

Al-Naml 27:89 and al-Anbiyāʾ 21:103 describe groups’ safety and peace before that day’s alarm.

In Sabaʾ 34:23, فُزِّعَ عَنْ قُلُوبِهِمْ expresses fear being removed from hearts.

In Sabaʾ 34:51, alarm accompanies facing an inescapable outcome.

The root’s main semantic core is alarm and agitation before danger or a frightening event; related branches also express seeking refuge, giving aid, and the removal of this fear.

Semantically and morally, experiencing alarm is not identical with cowardice, sin, faithlessness, or loss of steadfastness. David was alarmed at the litigants’ sudden entrance, but that reaction itself is not condemned.

By contrast, alarm in scenes of reckoning and the disclosure of people’s ultimate fate relates to their condition and conduct.

The Qur’an also shows that alarm is not everyone’s uniform fate. In the same scenes that describe great terror, some enjoy safety through goodness and divine grace.

Translation and study should therefore distinguish alarm itself, its cause, the human response to danger, seeking refuge, and the removal of fear.

This preserves the word’s historical meaning and allows its different uses in David’s story, resurrection scenes, and فُزِّعَ عَنْ قُلُوبِهِمْ to be understood without imposing one fixed, narrow sense.
