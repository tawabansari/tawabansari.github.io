Qur’anic Root Q R Ḥ

Wounds, injury, and suffering from harm

The Arabic word family Q R Ḥ has three prominent semantic branches: wounds and their pain; purity or being unmixed; and drawing out or originating something previously unavailable.

The branch concerning wounds and bodily injury is clearly recorded in classical Arabic lexicons, and Qur’anic usage confirms it in the language of revelation. This study does not rely on an independently dated pre-Islamic witness for that branch; this does not establish that no such witness exists.

The purity branch also appears in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period. In a verse attributed to ʿUrwa ibn al-Ward, he describes his generosity:

أُقَسِّمُ جِسْمِي فِي جُسُومٍ كَثِيرَةٍ / وَأَحْسُو قَرَاحَ الْمَاءِ وَالْمَاءُ بَارِدُ

I divide my body’s sustenance among many bodies, while I sip plain water, though the water is cold.

The poet shares his food with others and contents himself with water. قَرَاح (qarāḥ) here describes water unmixed with milk or anything else.

Of these branches, only wounds and injury occur in the Qur’an.

Forms examined: قَرَحَ (qaraḥa), قَرِحَ (qariḥa), قَرْح (qarḥ), قُرْح (qurḥ), قُرُوح (qurūḥ), قَرْحَة (qarḥa), قَرِيح (qarīḥ), مَقْرُوح (maqrūḥ), قَرَاح (qarāḥ), قَرِيحَة (qarīḥa), اِقْتَرَحَ (iqtaraḥa), and اِقْتِرَاح (iqtirāḥ).

Qur’anic form: قَرْح (qarḥ), read as قُرْح (qurḥ) in some recitations. It occurs three times in two verses: twice in Āl ʿImrān 3:140 and once in Āl ʿImrān 3:172.

1. The principal meanings and branches

Wounds and bodily injury

In Arabic, قَرَحَهُ (qaraḥahu) means to wound someone, while قَرِحَ جِلْدُهُ (qariḥa jilduhu) describes sores appearing on someone’s skin.

قَرْحَة (qarḥa) denotes a wound or sore; قُرُوح (qurūḥ) denotes wounds or sores. قَرِيح (qarīḥ) and مَقْرُوح (maqrūḥ) describe someone wounded.

This branch is not limited to injuries caused by blows or weapons. Derivatives also describe sores arising from illness or other bodily damage.

Expressions such as قُرْحَةُ الْمَعِدَةِ (qurḥat al-maʿida), “stomach ulcer,” and قُرُوحُ الْجِلْدِ (qurūḥ al-jild), “skin sores,” illustrate medical usage; they are not Qur’anic forms or independent evidence of pre-Islamic usage.

The family can also express inner suffering. The expression قَرِحَ قَلْبُهُ مِنَ الْحُزْنِ (qariḥa qalbuhu mina l-ḥuzn) depicts a heart wounded by grief. Persian likewise uses a wounded heart to convey emotional pain.

Purity and being unmixed

مَاءٌ قَرَاحٌ (māʾun qarāḥun) is plain, unmixed water. For land, قَرَاح (qarāḥ) is recorded for an open or cleared plot for cultivation or planting, including land without buildings or trees. It should not be reduced to chemically unmixed soil.

As in the verse attributed to ʿUrwa ibn al-Ward, this branch is distinct from wounds and injury.

Drawing out, natural aptitude, and proposing

Arabic lexicons use اِقْتَرَحَ الْبِئْرَ (iqtaraḥa l-biʾra) for digging a well and drawing out its water. قَرِيحَة (qarīḥa) also denotes the first water drawn from a well.

A person’s qarīḥa is their natural aptitude for thinking, understanding, and producing speech or ideas.

اِقْتِرَاح (iqtirāḥ) can mean originating an idea, making a proposal, or putting forward a request.

The well-water image helps explain the association: just as water is drawn from a previously hidden place, an idea can emerge from a person’s mental capacity.

However, the historical relationship of all these branches is not established. Wounding, purity, and drawing out should not be forced into a single original meaning.

2. Qarḥ and qurḥ

The word is recorded with both a and u on the initial qāf.

Some lexicographers treat قَرْح (qarḥ) and قُرْح (qurḥ) as two linguistic forms with the same meaning: a wound or injury.

Others distinguish them:

  • قَرْح (qarḥ) refers to the wound itself.
  • قُرْح (qurḥ) refers to the pain and suffering it causes.

Another recorded distinction associates the first form with injury from an external cause and the second with a sore arising in the body.

Among the ten canonical recitations, Shuʿba from ʿĀṣim, Ḥamza, al-Kisāʾī, and Khalaf the tenth read all three occurrences with u on the qāf; the others read a.

Since Arabic sources also record “wound” for both forms, no absolute boundary should be imposed. The distinction between an injury and its pain nevertheless helps clarify the word’s semantic range.

3. Qur’anic qarḥ: wounds and battle injuries

Injury is not the exclusive fate of one group

إِنْ يَمْسَسْكُمْ قَرْحٌ فَقَدْ مَسَّ الْقَوْمَ قَرْحٌ مِثْلُهُ وَتِلْكَ الْأَيَّامُ نُدَاوِلُهَا بَيْنَ النَّاسِ وَلِيَعْلَمَ اللَّهُ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَيَتَّخِذَ مِنْكُمْ شُهَدَاءَ ۗ وَاللَّهُ لَا يُحِبُّ الظَّالِمِينَ

In yamsaskum qarḥun fa-qad massa l-qawma qarḥun mithluhu, wa-tilka l-ayyāmu nudāwiluhā bayna l-nāsi wa-li-yaʿlama llāhu lladhīna āmanū wa-yattakhidha minkum shuhadāʾa, wa-llāhu lā yuḥibbu l-ẓālimīn.

If a wound has touched you, a similar wound has touched those people. And those days We alternate among people, and so that The God may know those who have believed and take witnesses from among you; and The God does not love the wrongdoers.

Al-Imran 3:140

Qarḥ occurs twice in this verse: once for the injury suffered by the believers and once for that suffered by the opposing group.

The Qur’an uses the same word for both and expresses the similarity of their injuries with مِثْلُهُ (mithluhu), “like it.”

Here qarḥ can encompass the injuries a community has suffered in battle. Its singular form does not mean that only one individual received a single wound.

The preceding verse states:

وَلَا تَهِنُوا وَلَا تَحْزَنُوا وَأَنْتُمُ الْأَعْلَوْنَ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ مُؤْمِنِينَ

Wa-lā tahinū wa-lā taḥzanū wa-antumu l-aʿlawna in kuntum muʾminīn.

And do not weaken or grieve, for you are uppermost if you are believers.

Al-Imran 3:139

These two verses contain three distinguishable concepts: qarḥ, wahn, and ḥuzn.

Qarḥ is the injury suffered; wahn is weakness or diminished strength; ḥuzn is the grief experienced after an unwelcome event.

Someone may be wounded without losing resolve. Conversely, someone without a bodily wound may experience weakness and grief.

The Qur’an does not deny the believers’ injuries; it calls on them not to weaken in response to them.

The alternation of victory and defeat also shows that a group’s temporary victory does not by itself prove its cause true, just as another group’s injury does not prove its path false.

The verse ends by denying The God’s love for wrongdoers. Their temporary power or victory does not imply His approval of their wrongdoing.

Responding to The God’s call after injury

الَّذِينَ اسْتَجَابُوا لِلَّهِ وَالرَّسُولِ مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا أَصَابَهُمُ الْقَرْحُ ۚ لِلَّذِينَ أَحْسَنُوا مِنْهُمْ وَاتَّقَوْا أَجْرٌ عَظِيمٌ

Alladhīna stajābū li-llāhi wa-l-rasūli min baʿdi mā aṣābahumu l-qarḥu, li-lladhīna aḥsanū minhum wa-ttaqaw ajrun ʿaẓīm.

Those who responded to The God and the Messenger after injury had struck them: for those among them who did good and practiced taqwa there is a great reward.

Al-Imran 3:172

Here الْقَرْحُ (al-qarḥ) refers to the known injury suffered by this group.

Āl ʿImrān 3:172 makes the sequence clear: they suffered injury and subsequently responded to The God and the Messenger.

Their conduct is valued because earlier injuries did not cause them to turn away from their responsibility.

The verse assigns the great reward to those among them who did good and practiced taqwa. Being wounded, enduring pain, or participating in a battle cannot replace goodness and taqwa.

A wound is not itself a virtue. What a person does in facing injury, and the path they take afterward, are matters of ethical evaluation.

4. Grammatical and structural observations

In Āl ʿImrān 3:140, both instances of qarḥ are grammatical subjects: first of يَمْسَسْ (yamsas), then of مَسَّ (massa).

The first clause describes injury reaching the addressees; the next describes a similar injury reaching the other people.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:172, الْقَرْحُ (al-qarḥ) is likewise the subject of أَصَابَ (aṣāba), while the attached hum pronoun refers to those whom the injury struck.

In all these occurrences, qarḥ is an injury people have suffered, rather than an act by which they wound others.

This observation concerns the Qur’anic constructions. In Arabic, a verb of the same root can also express wounding someone else.

5. Comparison with neighboring roots

Q R Ḥ (qarḥ) and J R Ḥ (jurḥ)

Both roots concern wounds and injuries in Arabic, with considerable overlap.

In al-Māʾida 5:45, الْجُرُوحَ (al-jurūḥ) occurs in the context of retribution for bodily wounds.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:140 and Āl ʿImrān 3:172, qarḥ concerns injuries suffered in battle.

Lexically, qarḥ can cover sores arising from illness and pain accompanying injury, as well as externally inflicted wounds.

This does not mean that jurḥ is always superficial and qarḥ always deep, or that qarḥ necessarily denotes a more painful wound.

J R Ḥ also has Qur’anic branches concerning hunting and acquiring deeds, whereas all Qur’anic uses of Q R Ḥ concern wounds and injury.

Q R Ḥ (qarḥ) and A L M (alam)

Alam denotes experienced pain or suffering; qarḥ more often denotes injury and, in some uses, its pain.

The Qur’an says of pain in battle:

وَلَا تَهِنُوا فِي ابْتِغَاءِ الْقَوْمِ ۖ إِنْ تَكُونُوا تَأْلَمُونَ فَإِنَّهُمْ يَأْلَمُونَ كَمَا تَأْلَمُونَ ۖ وَتَرْجُونَ مِنَ اللَّهِ مَا لَا يَرْجُونَ ۗ وَكَانَ اللَّهُ عَلِيمًا حَكِيمًا

Wa-lā tahinū fī btighāʾi l-qawmi, in takūnū taʾlamūna fa-innahum yaʾlamūna ka-mā taʾlamūna, wa-tarjūna mina llāhi mā lā yarjūna, wa-kāna llāhu ʿalīman ḥakīmā.

And do not weaken in pursuing those people. If you suffer pain, they suffer pain as you do, while you hope from The God for what they do not hope for. And The God is Knowing, Wise.

An-Nisa 4:104

This is close in meaning to Āl ʿImrān 3:140: one verse speaks of injuries shared by two groups, the other of their shared pain.

A wound can cause pain, but not every pain comes from a wound.

Q R Ḥ (qarḥ) and W H N (wahn)

Wahn denotes weakness and diminished strength.

Qarḥ can cause wahn, but the two concepts are not identical.

Āl ʿImrān 3:139 calls on believers not to weaken; Āl ʿImrān 3:140 speaks of the wound they have suffered.

A person may have suffered bodily injury while retaining resolve, perseverance, and the capacity to face hardship.

Q R Ḥ (qarḥ) and Ḍ R R (ḍarar)

Ḍarar has a broader semantic range, covering bodily, financial, and social harm, or damage to a person’s resources and interests.

Qarḥ relates more particularly to wounds, injuries, and the suffering they cause.

A bodily wound can be an instance of ḍarar, but losing property or an opportunity in life, though harmful, is not necessarily called qarḥ.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Render قَرْح (qarḥ) in Āl ʿImrān 3:140 as “wound” or “injury,” and in Āl ʿImrān 3:172 as “injury.”

When explaining the reading قُرْح (qurḥ), “pain and suffering from injury” may also be mentioned, without presenting it as the only possible meaning of that reading.

Brief root meaning: Wounding, bodily injury, and pain from harm; in non-Qur’anic branches, purity and drawing out or originating something.

Conclusion

Arabic Q R Ḥ has branches concerning wounds, purity, and drawing out. The Qur’an uses only the branch of wounds and injury.

Qur’anic qarḥ concerns injury suffered in battle. It is used for believers and the opposing group alike, and does not in itself express the ethical standing of either.

Qarḥ, alam, wahn, and ḥuzn are related but distinguishable: injury, pain, weakness, and grief. Their meanings overlap in experience; pain in particular need not arise from a wound.

The Qur’an does not deny injury or make its endurance a virtue in itself. The conduct praised in Āl ʿImrān is perseverance in faith, goodness, and taqwa despite injury.
