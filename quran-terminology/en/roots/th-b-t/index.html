The Qur’anic root TH B T

Firmness, remaining established, and making firm: from steadfast feet and tree roots to hearts, speech, and conduct

Arabic TH B T concerns firmness, remaining established, and not slipping from a position or condition. A firm thing withstands factors that could displace, remove, or change it. This is evident in tree roots or human footing, and extends to decisions, knowledge, speech, and conduct.

Someone retaining a sound decision under difficulty can be steadfast. Speech whose validity remains established can be firm. Something securely situated can also be physically firm.

Firmness nevertheless need not mean absolute immobility. A person may move while remaining firm in decision and direction, or remain physically motionless without intellectual or moral steadfastness.

Tathbīt concerns making something firm or maintaining it; ithbāt can concern establishing something, recording it, or substantiating a claim’s validity.

Another use concerns holding someone in place and preventing movement. In Al-Anfāl 8:30, opponents seek to yuthbitūka the Prophet: restrain his movement and freedom of action.

The root occurs eighteen times in seventeen Qur’anic verses. Subjects include firm feet in battle, strengthening believers, a tree’s established root, firm speech, strengthening the Prophet’s heart, erasure and establishment, and slipping after firmness.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: eighteen occurrences in seventeen verses

Firmness should remain distinct from what is firm. A foot, heart, statement, and action differ, although each can possess a kind of stability or endurance.

1. The semantic core in early Arabic

Ibn Fāris writes in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

«الثَّاءُ وَالْبَاءُ وَالتَّاءُ كَلِمَةٌ وَاحِدَةٌ، وَهِيَ دَوَامُ الشَّيْءِ»

al-thāʾu wa-al-bāʾu wa-al-tāʾu kalimatun wāḥidatun wa-hiya dawāmu al-shayʾi

The family has one principle: something’s continuing and remaining established.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: continuing establishment

He records verbal nouns thabāt and thubūt and descriptions thabt and thabīt.

Al-Rāghib says in Al-Mufradāt:

«الثَّبَاتُ ضِدُّ الزَّوَالِ»

al-thabātu ḍiddu al-zawāli

Firmness stands opposed to passing away or displacement.

The definitions emphasise related aspects.

Dawām foregrounds continuation.

Opposition to zawāl indicates that what is firm has not disappeared or been displaced in the relevant condition.

Al-Miṣbāḥ al-Munīr likewise explains the root through dāma and istaqarra: continuing and becoming established.

Al-Mufradāt and Al-Miṣbāḥ: firmness,continuation,and establishment

Both physical and nonphysical uses exhibit this meaning.

A nail firmly driven into a wall stays in place.

An established tree root resists displacement.

Someone refusing to abandon a position under fear or pressure remains firm in decision and direction.

These examples should nevertheless not be conflated.

A nail’s stability and a decision’s firmness differ in how they arise: physical attachment and resistance in one, human will, knowledge, and conduct in the other.

The shared core is remaining established and retaining stability against change or removal.

Firmness and immobility

Equating firmness with complete immobility is a possible misunderstanding.

Thabata bi-al-makāni can mean remaining in a place.

Thabata ʿalā al-amri concerns persistence in a matter or decision.

Someone may travel between cities to defend a right while remaining firm upon it.

Bodily movement and firm purpose are compatible.

The relevant question is what remains firm.

If place is the subject, remaining in a particular location matters.

If a decision is involved, retaining direction matters.

For speech or a judgment, established validity becomes relevant.

The Qur’an likewise distinguishes firm feet in battle, firm speech, and strengthening the heart; one physical image cannot explain all three.

Firmness and correctness

Thabata al-amru can mean a matter became clear, established, or confirmed.

Qawlun thābitun can likewise describe speech with established validity.

The root consequently extends into evidence and proof.

Persisting in a claim nevertheless differs from its being true.

Someone can insist upon a false claim for years. Behavioural persistence does not make it true.

Conversely, a statement can be established through sound evidence and validity.

Epistemic thubūt and ithbāt therefore require evidence and assessment of a claim, rather than merely the duration of belief.

2. Witnesses from early Arabic poetry

Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd: firm understanding and decision

The pre-Islamic poet Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd says:

فَالْهَبِيتُ لَا فُؤَادَ لَهُ
وَالثَّبِيتُ ثَبْتُهُ فَهَمُهُ

fa-al-habītu lā fuʾāda lahu / wa-al-thabītu thabtuhu fahamuhu

Translation: “The fainthearted man has no firm heart; the steadfast man finds his support in understanding and judgment.”

Al-habīt contrasts with al-thabīt.

The Dīwān commentary explains habīt as weak-hearted and thabīt as brave and firm. The second hemistich also has variants such as qalbuhu qiyamuhu; the supplied thabtuhu fahamuhu wording is retained here.

Al-Aʿlam al-Shantamarī: Ṭarafah’s witness and variant

The poet is not merely describing physical strength.

Fahamuhu connects the person’s firmness with understanding and judgment.

Thabīt thus describes someone firm in intellect, decision, and confronting difficulty in early poetry.

Human firmness was not confined to feet standing securely.

That does not make TH B T itself mean knowledge or understanding.

Understanding supports the person’s firmness; thabīt continues to describe that firmness.

Al-Aʿshā: a firmly fastened saddle and retaining strap

Al-Aʿshā describes a mount:

زَيَّافَةٌ بِالرَّحْلِ خَطَّارَةٌ
تَلْوِي بِشَرْخَيْ مُثْبَتٍ قَاتِرِ

zayyāfatun bi-al-raḥli khaṭṭāratun / talwī bi-sharkhay muthbatin qātiri

Translation: “A she-camel moving nimbly and swaying with her saddle, twisting its front and rear bows, securely fastened and fitting well.”

Muthbat describes the fastened saddle, while lexicons name its securing strap thibāt.

Lisān al-ʿArab: muthbat,the securely fastened saddle

Thibāt denotes a strap used to secure a saddle or load on a mount.

The usage illustrates a directly physical branch.

A moving mount can displace its load; a securing strap prevents unwanted movement.

The mount moves while saddle and load remain stable relative to their positions.

Firmness therefore need not stop all motion; it can maintain a connection and position during movement.

Lisān al-ʿArab and other lexicons attribute this witness to Al-Aʿshā.

The Ṭarafah and Al-Aʿshā witnesses illustrate firm understanding and decision, and physical securement in place.

3. Morphology and the family’s extensions

Thabata and thabuta

Thabata yathbutu concerns remaining established and being firm.

Thabuta is also recorded for firm intellect, resolve, or personal strength.

Recognised verbal nouns include thubūt and thabāt.

They share a semantic field while differing in sentence construction and usage.

Thubūt can concern establishment, stability, or a matter’s being confirmed.

Thabāt frequently concerns persistence against change.

The form difference does not establish an absolute physical–spiritual division.

Thābit, thabt, and thabīt

Thābit is an active participle describing an established or firm person or thing.

Thabt and thabīt can describe a firm, courageous person with sound judgment.

Thabat, with fatḥah on both thāʾ and bāʾ, can also mean evidence or proof.

Lisān and Tāj: thabt versus thabat

Lā aḥkumu illā bi-thabatin means not judging without evidence.

Firmness here extends into the validity of knowledge and judgment.

Evidence is nevertheless not the word’s meaning in every occurrence.

Thabbata and tathbīt

Form II thabbata means making firm or giving stability.

Thabbata aqdāmahu can mean making someone’s feet secure and steadfast.

Thabbata qalbahu concerns strengthening the heart and resolve.

The verbal noun is tathbīt.

An agent generally causes stability or maintains something in the relevant condition.

This is the family’s most frequent Qur’anic verbal form.

Athbata and ithbāt

Athbata is Form IV.

It can likewise mean making something established.

In an evidential setting, athbata al-ḥujjata means establishing and clarifying a proof.

In writing, athbata al-isma can mean recording a name.

Physical uses can concern securing someone or preventing movement.

Form IV is not wholly separate from Form II in meaning; their uses overlap.

Qur’anic li-yuthbitūka in Al-Anfāl 8:30 and wa-yuthbitu in Al-Raʿd 13:39 are Form IV with differing contextual meanings.

The first concerns holding someone in place and preventing movement.

The second concerns retaining or establishing something against erasing it.

Tathabbata and istathbata

Tathabbata fī al-amri concerns deliberation, avoiding haste, and examining a matter before deciding.

Istathbata likewise concerns checking and ascertaining correctness.

The connection to firmness is intelligible: a decision should not rest upon unstable, uncertain grounds.

Tathabbut and tathbīt nevertheless differ.

Tathabbut is the Form V verbal noun and can concern scrutiny before decision.

Tathbīt is the Form II verbal noun, making something firm.

The distinction especially matters in Al-Baqarah 2:265.

Qur’anic forms

The family has six derived forms in the Qur’anic corpus:

  • Form I verb: once, Al-Anfāl 8:45.
  • Form II verb: ten times.
  • Form IV verb: twice.
  • Thubūt, a verbal noun: once.
  • Thābit, an active participle: twice.
  • Tathbīt, the Form II verbal noun: twice.

These total eighteen occurrences in seventeen verses, because Ibrāhīm 14:27 contains both yuthabbitu and al-thābiti.

4. Firm feet: standing against pressure and danger

Firm feet form a prominent Qur’anic expression in this family.

It can concern physical footing and, in confrontation, personal steadfastness and not abandoning responsibility.

Each verse determines which aspect is foregrounded.

Al-Baqarah 2:250

Verse text:

وَلَمَّا بَرَزُوا لِجَالُوتَ وَجُنُودِهِ قَالُوا رَبَّنَا أَفْرِغْ عَلَيْنَا صَبْرًا وَثَبِّتْ أَقْدَامَنَا وَانْصُرْنَا عَلَى الْقَوْمِ الْكَافِرِينَ

wa-lammā barazū li-Jālūta wa-junūdihi qālū rabbanā afrigh ʿalaynā ṣabran wa-thabbit aqdāmanā wa-unṣurnā ʿalā al-qawmi al-kāfirīna

Translation:

“When they came forth before Goliath and his forces, they said: Our Lord, pour patience upon us, make our feet firm, and help us against the disbelieving people.”

The setting is military confrontation.

The prayer asks for patience, firm feet, and help against the enemy.

Together they relate firm feet to persistence amid battle’s difficulty.

Thabbit aqdāmanā itself means neither patience nor victory.

Ṣ B R expresses patience; N Ṣ R expresses help.

Firm feet express maintaining stability in confrontation.

Someone may be patient while lacking the physical capacity or means to stand sufficiently firm.

Someone may also hold a position for a time without ultimately winning.

Each request therefore has its own meaning.

Āl ʿImrān 3:147

Verse text:

وَمَا كَانَ قَوْلَهُمْ إِلَّا أَنْ قَالُوا رَبَّنَا اغْفِرْ لَنَا ذُنُوبَنَا وَإِسْرَافَنَا فِي أَمْرِنَا وَثَبِّتْ أَقْدَامَنَا وَانْصُرْنَا عَلَى الْقَوْمِ الْكَافِرِينَ

wa-mā kāna qawlahum illā an qālū rabbanā ighfir lanā dhunūbanā wa-isrāfanā fī amrinā wa-thabbit aqdāmanā wa-unṣurnā ʿalā al-qawmi al-kāfirīna

Translation:

“Their words were only: Our Lord, forgive our sins and excess in our affairs, make our feet firm, and help us against the disbelieving people.”

This too concerns believers enduring a difficult confrontation.

The request for firmness accompanies forgiveness and correction of their own conduct.

Desired firmness is therefore not blind insistence upon every past action.

They ask pardon for sin and excess before asking firmness and help.

One can stand firm against danger while acknowledging and correcting mistakes.

Qur’anic firmness should not become obstinacy or refusal to reconsider any decision.

Al-Anfāl 8:45

Verse text:

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِذَا لَقِيتُمْ فِئَةً فَاثْبُتُوا وَاذْكُرُوا اللَّهَ كَثِيرًا لَعَلَّكُمْ تُفْلِحُونَ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū idhā laqītum fiʾatan fa-uthbutū wa-udhkurū Allāha kathīran laʿallakum tufliḥūna

Translation:

“You who believe, when you encounter a group, remain firm and remember The God abundantly, so that you may prosper.”

Fa-uthbutū is a Form I imperative.

Here the addressees are commanded to endure, rather than praying for The God to make their feet firm as in Al-Baqarah and Āl ʿImrān.

The command concerns confronting the opposing group.

It should not become a universal ban on changing place, tactical withdrawal, or reconsidering conduct in every circumstance.

Its subject is steadfastness in the particular encounter.

Abundant remembrance of The God accompanies that steadfastness.

Thabāt and dhikr nevertheless differ: one expresses persistence, the other remembrance.

Muḥammad 47:7

Verse text:

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِنْ تَنْصُرُوا اللَّهَ يَنْصُرْكُمْ وَيُثَبِّتْ أَقْدَامَكُمْ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū in tanṣurū Allāha yanṣurkum wa-yuthabbit aqdāmakum

Translation:

“You who believe, if you help The God, He will help you and make your feet firm.”

The God is the agent making feet firm.

This relates to the prayers in Al-Baqarah 2:250 and Āl ʿImrān 3:147.

Making feet firm does not establish believers’ immunity from every harm or apparent defeat in all encounters.

The Qur’an also describes trials, some believers being killed, and real difficulties.

Firm footing belongs within steadfastness and divine help, rather than a promise removing every danger.

5. Rain and physical footing: Al-Anfāl 8:11

Verse text:

إِذْ يُغَشِّيكُمُ النُّعَاسَ أَمَنَةً مِنْهُ وَيُنَزِّلُ عَلَيْكُمْ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً لِيُطَهِّرَكُمْ بِهِ وَيُذْهِبَ عَنْكُمْ رِجْزَ الشَّيْطَانِ وَلِيَرْبِطَ عَلَىٰ قُلُوبِكُمْ وَيُثَبِّتَ بِهِ الْأَقْدَامَ

idh yughashshīkumu al-nuʿāsa amanatan minhu wa-yunazzilu ʿalaykum mina al-samāʾi māʾan li-yuṭahhirakum bihi wa-yudhhiba ʿankum rijza al-shayṭāni wa-li-yarbiṭa ʿalā qulūbikum wa-yuthabbita bihi al-aqdāma

Translation:

“When He caused drowsiness to cover you as security from Him, and sent water from the sky to purify you with it, remove Satan’s defilement, strengthen your hearts, and make your feet firm with it.”

The verse concerns the Battle of Badr.

It names distinct effects: secure drowsiness, water descending, purification, removing Satan’s defilement, strengthening hearts, and firm footing.

The final bihi relates to the water sent down.

Water’s relation to firm feet is therefore present in the sentence.

Firm ground underfoot

Early accounts describe the believers’ ground as sandy or soft and difficult to traverse.

Rain could bind soil and sand, improving the surface for movement and standing.

Al-Ṭabarī foregrounds this explanation of wa-yuthabbita bihi al-aqdāma.

Al-Ṭabarī: water and firm ground underfoot

It accords clearly with bihi referring to water.

The expression should therefore not be translated solely as heightened morale or courage.

Wa-li-yarbiṭa ʿalā qulūbikum has already introduced strengthening hearts.

Then comes firm footing through water.

The sequence distinguishes related inward steadiness and physically secure feet.

Is a nonphysical aspect also possible?

Firm feet in military confrontation can also involve endurance and continuing capability.

That possibility should not erase the physical aspect and water’s explicit relation to the ground.

Nor does the verse establish that every rain improves movement on every terrain.

Rain may firm one surface while creating mud, slipping, or flooding elsewhere.

The verse concerns a particular situation.

Its water–firmness connection should be understood within that event rather than generalised to every ground condition.

6. Strengthening believers and inward steadfastness

Al-Anfāl 8:12

Verse text:

إِذْ يُوحِي رَبُّكَ إِلَى الْمَلَائِكَةِ أَنِّي مَعَكُمْ فَثَبِّتُوا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا ۚ سَأُلْقِي فِي قُلُوبِ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا الرُّعْبَ فَاضْرِبُوا فَوْقَ الْأَعْنَاقِ وَاضْرِبُوا مِنْهُمْ كُلَّ بَنَانٍ

idh yūḥī rabbuka ilā al-malāʾikati annī maʿakum fa-thabbitū alladhīna āmanū sa-ulqī fī qulūbi alladhīna kafarū al-ruʿba fa-iḍribū fawqa al-aʿnāqi wa-iḍribū minhum kulla banānin

Translation:

“When your Lord revealed to the angels: I am with you; strengthen those who believe. I shall cast terror into disbelievers’ hearts; strike above the necks and strike every finger of theirs.”

Fa-thabbitū addresses the angels.

Those who believe are the verb’s object.

Unlike Al-Anfāl 8:11, the object is the believers themselves rather than feet.

Its meaning cannot therefore be restricted to firmer ground underfoot.

In battle, strengthening believers can concern morale, resolve, and capacity to endure.

The verb does not specify exactly how angels do this.

The verse alone does not establish a definitive theory of their bodily presence or psychological and physical influence.

Nor does this strengthening make every military action by these believers necessarily correct or immune from error.

Fa-thabbitū means strengthen; particular conduct requires its own evidence for assessment.

7. Strengthening from within and firmness through action

Al-Baqarah 2:265

Verse text:

وَمَثَلُ الَّذِينَ يُنْفِقُونَ أَمْوَالَهُمُ ابْتِغَاءَ مَرْضَاتِ اللَّهِ وَتَثْبِيتًا مِنْ أَنْفُسِهِمْ كَمَثَلِ جَنَّةٍ بِرَبْوَةٍ أَصَابَهَا وَابِلٌ فَآتَتْ أُكُلَهَا ضِعْفَيْنِ فَإِنْ لَمْ يُصِبْهَا وَابِلٌ فَطَلٌّ ۗ وَاللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ بَصِيرٌ

wa-mathalu alladhīna yunfiqūna amwālahumu ibtighāʾa marḍāti Allāhi wa-tathbītan min anfusihim ka-mathali jannatin bi-rabwatin aṣābahā wābilun fa-ātat ukulahā ḍiʿfayni fa-in lam yuṣibhā wābilun fa-ṭallun wa-Allāhu bi-mā taʿmalūna baṣīrun

Translation:

“Those spending their wealth seeking The God’s pleasure and strengthening from within themselves are like a garden on elevated ground: abundant rain reaches it and it gives double its produce; if abundant rain does not reach it, light rain does. The God sees what you do.”

This is one of the family’s most difficult Qur’anic uses.

It concerns people spending their wealth for The God’s pleasure.

Wa-tathbītan min anfusihim follows.

Tathbīt is the Form II verbal noun, making firm.

What is strengthened, and the role of min anfusihim, require examination.

First reading: firmness arising from inward conviction

Early explanations connect the expression with certainty and belief within those giving.

They give from firm resolve and belief in their action’s value.

Al-Ṭabarī prefers this and explains their selves as strengthening their owners in giving for The God’s pleasure.

The reading accords with the verbal noun tathbīt.

It presents a strengthening that originates within the person.

This should not make tathbīt itself mean certainty in Arabic.

Certainty may support or cause firmness; tathbīt expresses making firm.

Second reading: examining where charity goes

Other accounts relate tathbītan to checking a donation’s destination.

The person examines an appropriate use before giving.

This is morally intelligible and consistent with responsible giving.

It nevertheless raises an important morphological objection.

Tathabbata expresses deliberation and examination; its verbal noun is tathabbut.

The verse instead uses Form II tathbītan.

Al-Ṭabarī and other early grammarians and commentators observe this distinction.

Al-Ṭabarī: inward strengthening and distinction from tathabbut

Tathbītan should not become tathabbutan without independent grammatical evidence.

Proposed understanding

Firm inward decision and direction have clearer morphological support.

The basic translation should nevertheless not replace tathbītan directly with certainty.

“Strengthening from within themselves” or “strengthening themselves” comes closer to the verbal noun.

Explanation can then connect giving for The God’s pleasure with informed decision, belief, and avoidance of ostentation.

This preserves the distinction between translation and interpretation.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:66

Verse text:

وَلَوْ أَنَّا كَتَبْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ أَنِ اقْتُلُوا أَنْفُسَكُمْ أَوِ اخْرُجُوا مِنْ دِيَارِكُمْ مَا فَعَلُوهُ إِلَّا قَلِيلٌ مِنْهُمْ ۖ وَلَوْ أَنَّهُمْ فَعَلُوا مَا يُوعَظُونَ بِهِ لَكَانَ خَيْرًا لَهُمْ وَأَشَدَّ تَثْبِيتًا

wa-law annā katabnā ʿalayhim ani uqtulū anfusakum awi ukhrujū min diyārikum mā faʿalūhu illā qalīlun minhum wa-law annahum faʿalū mā yūʿaẓūna bihi la-kāna khayran lahum wa-ashadda tathbītan

Translation:

“Had We prescribed for them: Kill yourselves or leave your homes, only a few would have done it. Had they acted upon the counsel given them, it would have been better and more strengthening for them.”

Tathbītan is again a verbal noun.

Its subject differs from Al-Baqarah 2:265.

Acting upon counsel is connected with good and greater firmness.

Firmness is not confined to claims and intentions; conduct also helps establish a person.

Tathbīt itself nevertheless does not mean blind obedience or merely carrying out an order without understanding.

The particular setting concerns conduct, sincerity, and responsibility.

The opening conditional should not be detached and made a general instruction to kill oneself or others.

It presents a hypothesis and the group’s response, then the benefit of acting upon counsel.

8. Firm roots and established speech

Ibrāhīm 14:24

Verse text:

أَلَمْ تَرَ كَيْفَ ضَرَبَ اللَّهُ مَثَلًا كَلِمَةً طَيِّبَةً كَشَجَرَةٍ طَيِّبَةٍ أَصْلُهَا ثَابِتٌ وَفَرْعُهَا فِي السَّمَاءِ

a-lam tara kayfa ḍaraba Allāhu mathalan kalimatan ṭayyibatan ka-shajaratin ṭayyibatin aṣluhā thābitun wa-farʿuhā fī al-samāʾi

Translation:

“Have you not seen how The God sets forth an example? A good word is like a good tree whose root is firm and whose branch is in the sky.”

This is a clear physical example of firmness.

A tree with a firm root is described.

Aṣluhā names its root or foundation; thābitun qualifies it.

Its branch, by comparison, is in the sky.

The image combines a firm foundation with extending branches.

Thābit itself does not mean root.

Aṣl names the root; firmness describes it.

The firm tree’s role in the analogy

Secure roots help a tree resist some displacing forces.

A firm root does not stop every movement or branches swaying in wind.

It also does not guarantee immunity to damage in every circumstance.

The image presents a well-founded, established tree.

It illustrates kalimatan ṭayyibatan, a good word.

Thābit itself should not directly become divine unity, belief, or truthful speech.

Those concepts concern the analogy’s subject; thābit describes the tree’s rooted stability within the image.

Ibrāhīm 14:25 next describes the tree giving fruit at its times by its Lord’s permission.

The complete image connects a firm foundation with continuing benefit.

Firmness and bearing fruit nevertheless differ: one supports the tree’s endurance, the other expresses its yield and function.

Ibrāhīm 14:27

Verse text:

يُثَبِّتُ اللَّهُ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا بِالْقَوْلِ الثَّابِتِ فِي الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا وَفِي الْآخِرَةِ ۖ وَيُضِلُّ اللَّهُ الظَّالِمِينَ ۚ وَيَفْعَلُ اللَّهُ مَا يَشَاءُ

yuthabbitu Allāhu alladhīna āmanū bi-al-qawli al-thābiti fī al-ḥayāti al-dunyā wa-fī al-ākhirati wa-yuḍillu Allāhu al-ẓālimīna wa-yafʿalu Allāhu mā yashāʾu

Translation:

“The God strengthens those who believe through firm speech in worldly life and the Hereafter. The God lets wrongdoers go astray, and does whatever He wills.”

TH B T occurs twice in this verse.

Yuthabbitu is Form II, making firm.

Al-thābiti qualifies al-qawli.

Firm speech is presented as a means or ground of believers’ strengthening.

What is firm speech?

Early explanations commonly connect it with affirming divine unity and belief in The God.

This relates to preceding verses about the good word.

Lexically, al-qawlu al-thābitu means established, firm speech.

The root alone does not restrict this to one particular formula with an exact word count.

Nor should its firmness be reduced to verbal repetition alone.

Speech can rest upon truth and influence the speaker’s conduct.

Its full referent requires the surrounding verses and further Qur’anic evidence.

Firmness in this life and the Hereafter

The verse explicitly names worldly life and the Hereafter.

Its entire meaning should not be confined to one earthly situation.

Conversely, its afterlife firmness should not become a particular conversation or detailed transmitted account without evidence.

Exegetical reports connect the passage with questioning after death.

Al-Ṭabarī: firm speech in this life and the Hereafter

Such reports can be examined as historical material; the verse does not fully specify that situation’s manner.

The clear sentence concerns The God strengthening believers through firm speech in this life and the Hereafter.

9. Strengthening the Prophet’s heart through revelation and reminders

Four passages directly connect strengthening with revelation, truth, and the Prophet’s steadfastness.

Hūd 11:120

Verse text:

وَكُلًّا نَقُصُّ عَلَيْكَ مِنْ أَنْبَاءِ الرُّسُلِ مَا نُثَبِّتُ بِهِ فُؤَادَكَ ۚ وَجَاءَكَ فِي هَٰذِهِ الْحَقُّ وَمَوْعِظَةٌ وَذِكْرَىٰ لِلْمُؤْمِنِينَ

wa-kullan naquṣṣu ʿalayka min anbāʾi al-rusuli mā nuthabbitu bihi fuʾādaka wa-jāʾaka fī hādhihi al-ḥaqqu wa-mawʿiẓatun wa-dhikrā li-al-muʾminīna

Translation:

“We recount to you from the messengers’ reports all that strengthens your heart. In this, truth, counsel, and a reminder for believers have come to you.”

Nuthabbitu concerns strengthening the Prophet’s heart.

Recounting earlier messengers’ histories provides the means.

Their stories therefore do more than inform about the past: here they strengthen the Prophet’s heart and direction.

Tathbīt itself nevertheless does not mean narrating a story.

Naquṣṣu expresses recounting; strengthening the heart is its attributed effect or purpose.

Al-Furqān 25:32

Verse text:

وَقَالَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا لَوْلَا نُزِّلَ عَلَيْهِ الْقُرْآنُ جُمْلَةً وَاحِدَةً ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ لِنُثَبِّتَ بِهِ فُؤَادَكَ وَرَتَّلْنَاهُ تَرْتِيلًا

wa-qāla alladhīna kafarū lawlā nuzzila ʿalayhi al-qurʾānu jumlatan wāḥidatan kadhālika li-nuthabbita bihi fuʾādaka wa-rattalnāhu tartīlan

Translation:

“Those disbelieving said: Why was the Qur’an not sent down to him all at once? Thus, so that We strengthen your heart through it; We recited it in measured order.”

The question concerns how the Qur’an descends.

The answer connects that manner with strengthening the Prophet’s heart.

Hūd 11:120 uses messengers’ histories as a means of strengthening.

Here the Qur’an and its presentation are associated with a firm heart.

The connection does not make tathbīt lexically mean gradual revelation.

Descent and strengthening remain distinct concepts.

Wa-rattalnāhu tartīlan belongs to independent R T L; ordered, measured presentation should not become TH B T’s direct meaning.

Al-Naḥl 16:102

Verse text:

قُلْ نَزَّلَهُ رُوحُ الْقُدُسِ مِنْ رَبِّكَ بِالْحَقِّ لِيُثَبِّتَ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَهُدًى وَبُشْرَىٰ لِلْمُسْلِمِينَ

qul nazzalahu rūḥu al-qudusi min rabbika bi-al-ḥaqqi li-yuthabbita alladhīna āmanū wa-hudan wa-bushrā li-al-muslimīna

Translation:

“Say: The Holy Spirit brought it down from your Lord in truth, to strengthen those who believe, as guidance and good news for those who submit.”

Believers too are the subject of strengthening; it is not restricted to the Prophet.

Its accompaniment by guidance and good news matters.

The three should not be treated as synonyms.

Strengthening concerns steadfastness.

Guidance concerns showing and recognising the way.

Good news conveys a favourable, hopeful report.

Guidance and good news may support steadfastness without becoming lexically identical to it.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:74

Verse text:

وَلَوْلَا أَنْ ثَبَّتْنَاكَ لَقَدْ كِدْتَ تَرْكَنُ إِلَيْهِمْ شَيْئًا قَلِيلًا

wa-lawlā an thabbatnāka la-qad kidta tarkanu ilayhim shayʾan qalīlan

Translation:

“Had We not strengthened you, you would nearly have inclined toward them a little.”

Strengthening is directly attributed to The God.

Thabbatnāka makes the Prophet the object of divine strengthening.

The sentence then presents coming close to a kind of inclination toward the opposing group.

The construction matters.

Lawlā introduces the conditional; kidta expresses approaching an action’s occurrence.

It should not become an assertion that the Prophet actually yielded and then returned.

The verse concerns divine strengthening preventing inclination or slipping.

Firmness again does not mean bodily immobility.

It concerns maintaining direction and truth under opponents’ pressure and proposals.

10. Li-yuthbitūka: holding someone firm as restraint

Al-Anfāl 8:30

Verse text:

وَإِذْ يَمْكُرُ بِكَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا لِيُثْبِتُوكَ أَوْ يَقْتُلُوكَ أَوْ يُخْرِجُوكَ ۚ وَيَمْكُرُونَ وَيَمْكُرُ اللَّهُ ۖ وَاللَّهُ خَيْرُ الْمَاكِرِينَ

wa-idh yamkuru bika alladhīna kafarū li-yuthbitūka aw yaqtulūka aw yukhrijūka wa-yamkurūna wa-yamkuru Allāhu wa-Allāhu khayru al-mākirīna

Translation:

“When those disbelieving plotted against you to hold you captive, kill you, or expel you: they planned, and The God planned; The God is the best of planners.”

Li-yuthbitūka is Form IV.

Here strengthening the Prophet’s heart or helping him is not intended.

His opponents’ plot is the subject.

The alternatives are confinement, killing, and expulsion.

Restraint and confinement

Early explanations give binding, imprisonment, and preventing movement.

Al-Ṭabarī: confinement and restraint in li-yuthbitūka

This accords with the root’s core.

Holding someone captive restricts free movement and relocation.

Ithbāt here concerns forcibly fixing the body in place.

Keeping something fixed is therefore not inherently positive or negative.

Strengthening a believer against danger may help.

Holding someone in bonds or prison may constitute oppression.

Subject, purpose, and circumstances determine moral value.

Other explanations

Some lexical explanations understand severe wounding that prevents movement.

Athbatat-hu al-jirāḥatu illustrates that use.

The verse separately names killing and expulsion.

Restraint and imprisonment form a clear independent alternative alongside them.

“Hold captive” or “restrain” therefore suits the basic translation.

Severe wounding can be discussed as a possible lexical use without making it the verse’s exclusive meaning without a contextual indication.

11. Yamḥū Allāhu mā yashāʾu wa-yuthbitu: erasing and retaining

Al-Raʿd 13:39

Verse text:

يَمْحُو اللَّهُ مَا يَشَاءُ وَيُثْبِتُ ۖ وَعِنْدَهُ أُمُّ الْكِتَابِ

yamḥū Allāhu mā yashāʾu wa-yuthbitu wa-ʿindahu ummu al-kitābi

Translation:

“The God erases whatever He wills and retains; with Him is the source of the Book.”

Yuthbitu contrasts with yamḥū.

The contrast is significant.

Maḥw concerns effacing a trace or record.

Ithbāt here concerns retaining or establishing it.

Athbata al-isma can mean recording a name.

Erasure and establishment alongside ummu al-kitābi strengthen a writing and recording context.

Does the verse concern changing a divine command?

Early commentaries record several readings.

Some concern changing or preserving matters of people’s lives.

Others concern erasure and establishment in divine records.

Another relates it to changing or retaining certain rulings and verses.

Al-Ṭabarī: interpretations of erasure and establishment

These can be examined separately through erasure, the Book, and divine determination.

Yuthbitu itself does not mean changing destiny, abrogating a ruling, or recording a person among a particular group.

It directly concerns retaining or establishing against erasure.

Ummu al-kitābi

The verse concludes that ummu al-kitābi is with The God.

Its independent words concern the Book’s source or reference.

The phrase does not by itself establish the number of unseen books, how every event is recorded, or the timing of changes.

Translating the root need only retain the clear erasure–establishment contrast.

Yuthbitu here should not become making someone steadfast; the subject differs.

This illustrates the family’s flexibility across contexts.

12. Baʿda thubūtihā: slipping after firmness

Al-Naḥl 16:94

Verse text:

وَلَا تَتَّخِذُوا أَيْمَانَكُمْ دَخَلًا بَيْنَكُمْ فَتَزِلَّ قَدَمٌ بَعْدَ ثُبُوتِهَا وَتَذُوقُوا السُّوءَ بِمَا صَدَدْتُمْ عَنْ سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ وَلَكُمْ عَذَابٌ عَظِيمٌ

wa-lā tattakhidhū aymānakum dakhalan baynakum fa-tazilla qadamun baʿda thubūtihā wa-tadhūqū al-sūʾa bi-mā ṣadadtum ʿan sabīli Allāhi wa-lakum ʿadhābun ʿaẓīmun

Translation:

“Do not make your oaths a means of deception among yourselves, lest a foot slip after being firm and you taste harm for obstructing The God’s way; a great punishment awaits you.”

Thubūt is the Form I verbal noun.

It contrasts with slipping, tazilla.

The image shows previously secure footing giving way.

Ordinary walking is not the context.

The subject is deceptive use of oaths and covenants.

Slipping therefore concerns lost behavioural and moral firmness.

Firmness and keeping a covenant

Al-Naḥl 16:91 speaks of fulfilling covenants.

Al-Naḥl 16:92 depicts breaking what effort has made firm through the image of spun thread.

Al-Naḥl 16:94 then presents slipping after firmness.

The sequence connects moral firmness with trustworthiness and covenant fidelity.

Thubūt itself nevertheless does not mean covenant.

Other words express covenants and oaths.

Thubūt names the condition lost through deceit and breach.

Is every changed decision a slip?

The verse does not make every reconsidered decision or commitment necessarily sinful slipping.

It concerns using an oath or covenant to deceive.

Contractual circumstances may change or parties may agree to a fair revision.

That differs from exploiting trust through deceit.

Desirable firmness here is faithful conduct without deception, rather than clinging to any undertaking even if founded on injustice.

13. Related roots

TH B T and Ṣ B R

Study of Ṣ B R

Ṣabr concerns persistence, bearing pressure, and resisting collapse or haste along a course.

Thabāt emphasises firmness and remaining established.

Al-Baqarah 2:250 uses both in one prayer.

They are related without being entirely synonymous.

Patience can support firmness, but an established tree root or record need not involve suffering.

TH B T and R B Ṭ

Study of R B Ṭ

Physical rabṭ concerns binding and connecting.

Al-Anfāl 8:11 combines strengthening hearts with making feet firm.

Binding can produce firmness while foregrounding connection and secure fastening.

Thabāt describes the resulting or continuing firm condition.

The roots can serve a common purpose without sharing an identical semantic core.

TH B T and Q R R

Study of Q R R

Qarār and istiqrār concern settling and coming to rest.

Thabāt too can describe remaining in a position.

It can more strongly foreground resisting change or removal.

Qarār may instead foreground the place, stillness, or settling itself.

This is a tendency, rather than an absolute boundary across every use.

TH B T and M K N

Study of M K N

Tamkīn concerns gaining position, capacity, and the means of establishment.

Thabāt emphasises remaining firm.

Someone may gain a position and resources without firm resolve.

Someone with few resources can remain steadfast in hardship.

Capacity and firmness can support one another without being lexically identical.

TH B T and Y Q N

Study of Y Q N

Yaqīn concerns knowledge and conviction.

Thabāt concerns firmness and remaining established.

In Al-Baqarah 2:265, conviction is one explanation of inward strengthening’s basis.

Conviction is not itself the act of strengthening.

It can support firm decision, but context must establish the relationship.

TH B T and B Q Y

Study of B Q Y

Baqāʾ concerns remaining and continuing in existence or condition.

Thabāt likewise relates to endurance and establishment.

It frequently foregrounds stability against change or displacement.

Goods may survive for a long time without being secured in place.

Something may also remain firm for a period and eventually perish.

Endurance and firmness are related without one fully defining the other.

TH B T and TH B Ṭ

Study of TH B Ṭ

These families resemble each other in sound and writing but differ in their final radical.

TH B T concerns firmness and strengthening.

Qur’anic TH B Ṭ concerns restraining and slowing people’s movement.

Fa-thabbaṭahum in Al-Tawbah 9:46 concerns preventing a group from going forth.

Some outcomes may resemble one another, including restricted movement, without giving the verbs one root.

Li-yuthbitūka in Al-Anfāl 8:30 belongs to TH B T: movement is restricted by holding someone fixed.

Al-Tawbah 9:46 retains its independent TH B Ṭ root.

TH B T and TH B Y

Study of TH B Y

Another morphological distinction matters.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:71 contains thubātin.

Despite resembling thabāt, firmness, the Qur’anic morphological index assigns this to TH B Y for separate groups.

Thubātin in Al-Nisāʾ 4:71 should not become another TH B T occurrence merely through similar unvocalised spelling.

The distinction matters for Qur’anic root assignment and counts.

14. Translation proposals for Forqan

For thabata, use “remained firm,” “remained established,” or “stayed in place,” according to context.

For thābit, use “firm,” “secure,” or “established.”

For thubūt, use “firmness” or “being established.”

For thabbata, use “made firm,” “strengthened,” or “maintained.”

For tathbīt, use “making firm” or “strengthening.”

For evidential athbata, use “proved” or “established.”

In writing, “recorded”; for restraining a person, “held captive” or “prevented movement” may fit.

For tathabbata, use “examined carefully,” “deliberated,” or “avoided haste.”

This form is absent from the Qur’anic corpus’s base-text count followed here. Other recognised readings nevertheless have fa-tathabbatū in Al-Nisāʾ 4:94 and Al-Ḥujurāt 49:6 where the base text reads fa-tabayyanū. Its absence should not be generalised to every reading.

15. Conclusion

Arabic TH B T concerns firmness, remaining established, and endurance.

Ibn Fāris gives dawāmu al-shayʾi as its principle; al-Rāghib opposes thabāt to zawāl.

Early Arabic poetic witnesses describe firm understanding and decision and securely maintaining physical objects.

The Qur’anic base text has eighteen occurrences in seventeen verses.

Al-Baqarah 2:250, Āl ʿImrān 3:147, Al-Anfāl 8:45, and Muḥammad 47:7 concern endurance in difficult confrontation.

Al-Anfāl 8:11 connects firm footing with rainwater, including physical stability on the ground.

Al-Anfāl 8:12 makes believers themselves the object of strengthening.

Al-Baqarah 2:265 relates strengthening to giving and inward resolve.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:66 connects acting upon counsel with greater firmness.

Ibrāhīm 14:24 depicts a firm tree root to illustrate the good word.

In Ibrāhīm 14:27, The God strengthens believers through firm speech in this life and the Hereafter.

Hūd 11:120, Al-Naḥl 16:102, and Al-Furqān 25:32 relate revelation and reminders to hearts and believers’ direction being strengthened.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:74 presents divine strengthening as preventing the Prophet’s inclination in the situation described.

In Al-Anfāl 8:30, ithbāt concerns confinement and preventing movement.

In Al-Raʿd 13:39, establishment contrasts with erasure and concerns retaining something.

Al-Naḥl 16:94 depicts slipping after firmness in covenants and conduct.

Different subjects nevertheless share an establishment or remaining-firm field.

Firmness is not always virtuous: standing for truth and justice may be good, while insisting upon falsehood or oppressively holding someone captive is not.

The root alone does not determine value; what is maintained, why, and toward what end require examination.

Firmness also does not exclude every movement or change. A tree’s branches can move above firm roots; a person can move along a sound path while retaining direction.

Final root definition: TH B T concerns something being firm and remaining established in its position, condition, or validity. Causative forms express making firm, maintaining, or establishing something.

Physical uses concern stability against displacement; behavioural uses, steadfastness; epistemic uses, established evidence or validity; and certain uses, restricting a person’s movement.

The family distinctively concerns maintaining establishment against removal, slipping, or displacement from the relevant condition, rather than mere bodily stillness, blind insistence, or the certain truth of anything long retained.

“Remaining firm,” “making firm,” “being established,” and “retaining” are the basic equivalents. In Al-Anfāl 8:30, “holding captive” conveys the particular contextual sense.

Translation and interpretation should retain the shared meaning while establishing its manner, subject, and moral value from the complete verse.
