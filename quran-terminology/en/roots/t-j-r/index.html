Brief Introduction to the Root

The root T J R in Arabic relates to trade, buying and selling, and exchange undertaken to obtain profit. Within this word family, property or merchandise enters a field of exchange, and a person seeks gain through buying, selling, and commercial use of capital.

Only the noun tijārah occurs from this root in the Qur’an. Sometimes it denotes actual economic exchange: trade by mutual consent, present trade, stagnating trade, or people becoming occupied with trade. Elsewhere, the Qur’an uses commercial language figuratively for human choices: people exchange guidance for error and their trade yields no profit, or faith is compared to a trade that brings rescue.

The root’s central field may be stated briefly as:

Entering exchange and putting property or capital into circulation in pursuit of profit and benefit.

This definition should not be expanded until every effort, choice, or use of time is called “trade.” The Qur’anic metaphor works where the context contains the structure of exchange, investment, surrendering something, and expecting a result.

Important Forms and Derivatives of the Root

The root T J R occurs nine times in the Qur’an, always in the nominal form tijārah. No verb or active participle from this root occurs in the Qur’anic text.

تِجَارَة — tijārah

Tijārah is a noun or nominal infinitive denoting trade, commerce, a transaction, or activity undertaken in pursuit of profit.

It occurs in several Qur’anic constructions:

فَمَا رَبِحَتْ تِجَارَتُهُمْ

“So their trade yielded no profit.”

Al-Baqarah 2:16

إِلَّا أَنْ تَكُونَ تِجَارَةً حَاضِرَةً تُدِيرُونَهَا بَيْنَكُمْ

“Unless it is present trade that you circulate among yourselves.”

Al-Baqarah 2:282

إِلَّا أَنْ تَكُونَ تِجَارَةً عَنْ تَرَاضٍ مِنْكُمْ

“Unless it is trade arising from mutual consent among you.”

Al-Nisa 4:29

وَتِجَارَةٌ تَخْشَوْنَ كَسَادَهَا

“And trade whose stagnation you fear.”

Al-Tawbah 9:24

لَا تُلْهِيهِمْ تِجَارَةٌ وَلَا بَيْعٌ

“Neither trade nor sale distracts them.”

Al-Nur 24:37

يَرْجُونَ تِجَارَةً لَنْ تَبُورَ

“They hope for a trade that will never perish.”

Fatir 35:29

هَلْ أَدُلُّكُمْ عَلَىٰ تِجَارَةٍ تُنْجِيكُمْ مِنْ عَذَابٍ أَلِيمٍ

“Shall I guide you to a trade that will rescue you from a painful punishment?”

Al-Saff 61:10

وَإِذَا رَأَوْا تِجَارَةً أَوْ لَهْوًا انْفَضُّوا إِلَيْهَا

“When they saw trade or amusement, they dispersed toward it.”

Al-Jumu’ah 62:11

قُلْ مَا عِنْدَ اللَّهِ خَيْرٌ مِنَ اللَّهْوِ وَمِنَ التِّجَارَةِ

“Say: what is with The God is better than amusement and trade.”

Al-Jumu’ah 62:11

Non-Qur’anic Lexical Forms

The following forms are established in Arabic but do not occur in the Qur’an:

tajara yatjuru: he traded or engaged in buying and selling.

ittajara yattajiru: he engaged in commerce.

tājir: a trader or merchant.

tajr, tijār, and tujjār: plural forms of tājir.

matjar or matjarah: a place, land, or field in which, or toward which, trade is conducted.

These forms help identify the lexical family, but they must not be listed as Qur’anic derivatives.

The Root's Semantic Core

The core of this word family is buying and selling and the commercial employment of property in pursuit of profit.

In trade, a person places property into exchange, buys or sells something, and hopes that the transaction will produce increase or benefit. Trade and profit therefore have a natural relationship, but they are not identical:

Trade is the activity and field of exchange.

Profit is the increase or gain produced by it.

Trade may take place without producing profit. The expression:

فَمَا رَبِحَتْ تِجَارَتُهُمْ

rests precisely on this distinction: their trade took place, but it did not profit.

In some contexts, tijārah can denote not only commercial activity but also the merchandise, shipment, or object of trade. This extension occurs when an action noun names what the action concerns. In Al-Jumu’ah 62:11, seeing “trade” may refer to seeing a caravan, commercial goods, or an opportunity for exchange, while the word still preserves the field of commerce.

Effort, knowledge of the market, evaluation, risk, time, and the movement of capital often accompany real trade, but they are not all fixed components of the lexical definition of T J R. Not every trade entails transporting goods between cities, and not every commercial profit results from risk shared by all parties.

The definition should therefore remain centered on the linguistic evidence:

Exchange and commercial use of property in pursuit of profit.

The Root's Material Image

The tangible image is a merchant who brings property or merchandise into the market, buys or sells it, and waits for the transaction’s outcome.

Capital does not remain untouched in the merchant’s possession; it enters an exchange relationship. In return for property given, the merchant receives property, goods, or benefit and hopes that the value obtained will exceed the cost.

The image has three clear elements:

First, something enters a transaction.

Second, exchange occurs between the transaction’s parties.

Third, an outcome may be profit, loss, or stagnation.

“Movement of capital” should nevertheless be understood as an explanation of the mechanism of trade, not as an independent lexical meaning of the root. Risk and timing may also be present, but not every Qur’anic use of tijārah emphasizes them.

In Al-Baqarah 2:282, the image is present trade circulating among people. In Al-Tawbah 9:24, it is a market activity that may stagnate. In Al-Baqarah 2:16, the economic image is transferred to human choice: something was given, something was received, yet the transaction produced no profit.

Use of the Root in Early Arabic

Arabic lexicons use tajara yatjuru tajran wa-tijāratan for buying, selling, and trading.

A tājir is one who commercially employs property and engages in buying and selling. Plurals such as tajr, tijār, and tujjār are recorded.

In a definition attributed to Al-Raghib, trade is:

al-taṣarrufu fī ra’si al-māli ṭalaban lil-ribḥ

That is, employing or disposing of capital in pursuit of profit. The definition makes two elements clear: capital subjected to commercial use and profit sought as the objective.

In Arabic usage, describing someone as a trader in a particular matter could mean that the person was skilled in it and knew how to gain benefit through it. This shows that expertise can accompany trade, but “expertise” is not the root’s primary meaning.

Some sources also use nāqatun tājirah for a she-camel that attracts buyers and has a brisk market when offered for sale. Its opposite is stagnant merchandise. This illustrates the relation of trade to marketability, briskness, and stagnation.

Some early evidence uses tājir specifically for a wine seller. That specialized or conventional usage should not be treated as the primary meaning of tājir or T J R. The general meaning remains commerce and buying and selling.

History of Interpretation and Semantic Limitation

One excessive extension is to define trade as every effort made to obtain benefit. A person may seek knowledge, prestige, power, or another person’s approval, but not every pursuit of benefit is linguistically trade.

Trade extends into such fields only when a discourse contains the structure of exchange and investment. The Qur’an itself makes that transfer in Al-Baqarah 2:16 and Al-Saff 61:10. Figurative use is therefore possible, but it must be supported by context.

Another narrowing reduces trade to a single moment of buying or selling. Trade can be a broader activity or course containing many transactions. Yet this does not mean that bayʿ is always merely one moment while tijārah is always a long economic system. Their fields overlap, and their difference must be assessed in each context.

The claim that trade means “profit accompanied by shared risk” also requires caution. The possibility of gain and loss is a feature of much commercial activity, but T J R alone does not provide a complete theory of risk distribution or economic justice.

Nor is trade in the Qur’an inherently pure, just, or approved. The Qur’an speaks of consensual trade, but elsewhere trade may occupy a person, occasion departure from a gathering, or become the object of fear because it may stagnate.

The ethical value of trade therefore does not arise from the word itself. Its conditions, object, method, and effects must be examined separately.

Methodological Note

Three levels should be distinguished when studying this root.

The first is lexical meaning: buying and selling and the commercial employment of property in pursuit of profit.

The second is the ethical and legal conditions of trade in the verses: mutual consent, avoiding consumption of property through falsehood, recording debt, and not being distracted from remembrance of The God.

The third is figurative commercial usage: exchanging guidance for error or a trade that rescues from punishment.

The rulings and conditions of the second level must not be made part of the root’s dictionary meaning. For example, ʿan tarāḍin is a condition for legitimate trade in the context of Al-Nisa 4:29; “consent” is not part of the etymology of T J R.

Nor should the figurative third level be expanded until every righteous act or human decision is simply called trade. In these verses, the Qur’an deliberately uses an economic structure to explain choosing, giving, receiving, and awaiting an outcome.

A detailed discussion of usury, gambling, exploitation, risk, loans, guaranteed profit, and economic justice exceeds the capacity of a root article. Here, the linguistic and contextual relationship between trade and those concepts should be clarified, not a complete economic theory extracted from T J R.

Qur'anic Uses of the Root

Trade That Yielded No Profit

أُولَٰئِكَ الَّذِينَ اشْتَرَوُا الضَّلَالَةَ بِالْهُدَىٰ فَمَا رَبِحَتْ تِجَارَتُهُمْ وَمَا كَانُوا مُهْتَدِينَ

“Those are the ones who purchased error in exchange for guidance; so their trade yielded no profit, and they were not guided.”

Al-Baqarah 2:16

The verse constructs a complete commercial image. Ishtaraw establishes purchase and exchange. Error is what they took, and guidance is what they surrendered. The transaction’s outcome is then measured with the root R B H:

فَمَا رَبِحَتْ تِجَارَتُهُمْ

Trade is attributed to them, but the verb rabiḥat has “their trade” as its subject. English can similarly say that their trade did not profit or yielded no profit.

The verse does not mean that they entered an actual financial transaction. Economic language explains an existential choice: they surrendered superior capital and took something inferior in its place.

The main point is not merely failure to obtain profit. They gave guidance and took error, so the very structure of the exchange was inverted.

Present Trade in Circulation

إِلَّا أَنْ تَكُونَ تِجَارَةً حَاضِرَةً تُدِيرُونَهَا بَيْنَكُمْ فَلَيْسَ عَلَيْكُمْ جُنَاحٌ أَلَّا تَكْتُبُوهَا

“Unless it is present trade that you circulate among yourselves; then there is no blame upon you if you do not write it down.”

Al-Baqarah 2:282

The phrase occurs in the verse about recording debts. It distinguishes a debt remaining until a specified term from tijāratan ḥāḍiratan.

Ḥāḍirah may denote a present, immediate, non-deferred transaction. Tudīrūnahā baynakum depicts the transaction being circulated or passed among its parties.

Trade here is real and economic. The verse is not describing trade’s spiritual value; it concerns recording and witnessing transactions.

“Cash trade” may convey part of the intent, but ḥāḍirah does not literally mean cash. “Present trade” or “an immediate transaction that you circulate among yourselves” remains closer to the construction.

Trade by Mutual Consent

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَا تَأْكُلُوا أَمْوَالَكُمْ بَيْنَكُمْ بِالْبَاطِلِ إِلَّا أَنْ تَكُونَ تِجَارَةً عَنْ تَرَاضٍ مِنْكُمْ

“O you who have believed, do not consume one another’s property among yourselves through falsehood, unless it is trade arising from mutual consent among you.”

Al-Nisa 4:29

Trade is set against consuming property through falsehood, but merely giving something the name “trade” does not automatically remove it from falsehood. ʿAn tarāḍin minkum specifies the quality of the relationship.

Tarāḍ expresses mutual consent between two or more parties. That consent must be real, not merely the appearance of acceptance under pressure, deception, or absence of choice, although the detailed law of this question is not derived from T J R itself.

The verse shows that legitimate circulation of property among people may occur through consensual trade. It does not say that every consensual trade is ethically just in every respect. Deception, concealment of defects, public harm, or breach of covenant may still need evaluation through other verses.

Trade Whose Stagnation Is Feared

وَتِجَارَةٌ تَخْشَوْنَ كَسَادَهَا

“And trade whose stagnation you fear.”

Al-Tawbah 9:24

The verse names trade among major attachments of life: family, kin, acquired property, trade, and pleasing dwellings.

Kasād is lack of briskness, merchandise failing to sell, or market activity coming to a standstill. Trade may exist while its circulation and demand decline.

The verse does not call trade or fear of stagnation sinful in itself. The problem arises when these attachments become dearer to a person than The God, His messenger, and striving in His way.

This usage shows that trade is not only an isolated transaction; it can be a livelihood and an economic structure whose decline a person fears.

Trade and Sale in Contrast with Distraction

رِجَالٌ لَا تُلْهِيهِمْ تِجَارَةٌ وَلَا بَيْعٌ عَنْ ذِكْرِ اللَّهِ وَإِقَامِ الصَّلَاةِ وَإِيتَاءِ الزَّكَاةِ

“Men whom neither trade nor sale distracts from remembrance of The God, establishing salat, and giving zakat.”

Al-Nur 24:37

Tijārah and bayʿ occur together. Their coordination shows that they are not wholly identical, but the verse does not give a technical definition of the difference.

Bayʿ may emphasize a specific sale or contract, while tijārah may encompass broader commercial activity. This distinction, however, should not be made absolute.

Tulhīhim shows that the problem is not trade or sale itself. The problem is economic activity occupying people until remembrance of The God, salat, and zakat are set aside.

The verse does not remove economics from spiritual life. The praised men may be engaged in trade and sale, but those activities do not seize the center of their attention and responsibility.

Trade That Will Not Perish

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ يَتْلُونَ كِتَابَ اللَّهِ وَأَقَامُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَأَنْفَقُوا مِمَّا رَزَقْنَاهُمْ سِرًّا وَعَلَانِيَةً يَرْجُونَ تِجَارَةً لَنْ تَبُورَ

“Those who recite the Book of The God, establish salat, and spend secretly and openly from what We have provided them hope for a trade that will never perish.”

Fatir 35:29

Trade is figurative here. Recitation of the Book, establishing salat, and spending are not commercial goods, but the Qur’an places them in a structure in which a person gives something and hopes for an outcome.

Lan tabūr means that this trade will not reach ruin, perishing, or fruitlessness. “Will never be fruitless” conveys part of the sense but weakens the connection with the market field of stagnation.

“They hope for a trade that will never stagnate or perish” preserves more of the image, although “stagnate” alone does not exhaust bawār, which can extend to destruction and failure.

The verse does not make The God a commercial debtor equal to a human partner. Commercial language assures that the deed will not be lost; it does not turn the relation between a human being and The God into an equal financial contract.

Trade That Brings Rescue

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا هَلْ أَدُلُّكُمْ عَلَىٰ تِجَارَةٍ تُنْجِيكُمْ مِنْ عَذَابٍ أَلِيمٍ

“O you who have believed, shall I guide you to a trade that will rescue you from a painful punishment?”

Al-Saff 61:10

The next verse explains this trade:

تُؤْمِنُونَ بِاللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ وَتُجَاهِدُونَ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ بِأَمْوَالِكُمْ وَأَنْفُسِكُمْ

“You believe in The God and His messenger and strive in the way of The God with your property and your lives.”

Al-Saff 61:11

The Qur’an itself identifies what the trade concerns. The capital named is property and life; the outcome is rescue and forgiveness.

This usage shows that the Qur’anic commercial metaphor is not merely an inward feeling. Faith and striving with property and life are present.

Yet the metaphor should not be taken to mean that human action is an equal price for forgiveness or that the relation with The God precisely follows human commercial accounting. The familiar language of trade presents a beneficial, rescuing choice.

Trade and Leaving the Gathering

وَإِذَا رَأَوْا تِجَارَةً أَوْ لَهْوًا انْفَضُّوا إِلَيْهَا وَتَرَكُوكَ قَائِمًا

“When they saw trade or amusement, they dispersed toward it and left you standing.”

Al-Jumu’ah 62:11

Trade is something visible and attractive here. The reference may be to the arrival of a caravan, a commercial shipment, or a market opportunity.

The feminine singular pronoun in ilayhā agrees with tijārah, even though amusement also occurs in the phrase.

The verse continues:

قُلْ مَا عِنْدَ اللَّهِ خَيْرٌ مِنَ اللَّهْوِ وَمِنَ التِّجَارَةِ

“Say: what is with The God is better than amusement and trade.”

The criticism is not directed at trade itself. It addresses the hasty preference for a commercial opportunity or amusement over hearing the message and remaining in the gathering.

Together with Al-Nur 24:37, this usage shows two responses: some are not distracted by trade, while others see trade and leave the gathering.

Examination of the Qur'anic Derivative

تِجَارَة as Commercial Activity

In verses such as Al-Nisa 4:29, Al-Tawbah 9:24, and Al-Nur 24:37, tijārah denotes actual commercial activity and exchange.

“Trade,” “commerce,” and, in some contexts, “commercial exchange” are suitable renderings.

“Business” is natural in modern English but has a wider range than trade and may encompass services, production, or non-exchange employment. It should not therefore become the fixed equivalent of tijārah.

تِجَارَة as a Present Transaction

In Al-Baqarah 2:282, tijāratan ḥāḍiratan focuses on a present transaction circulating among the parties. Here “transaction” may be more natural than “trade” because the verse concerns recording a particular exchange, not commerce as a profession.

تِجَارَة as Merchandise or a Commercial Opportunity

In Al-Jumu’ah 62:11, people see tijārah and go toward it. An action noun may be naming the object of trade, caravan, or shipment.

The literal “when they saw trade” preserves the Arabic construction but is somewhat unusual in English. “When they saw a caravan or commercial goods” is more explanatory and adds information. “When they saw a commercial opportunity” is a middle option, but “opportunity” is also not explicit in the verse. Translation must balance the construction of the text with natural English.

تِجَارَة in the Metaphor of Choice

In Al-Baqarah 2:16, tijārah continues the commercial frame established by ishtaraw. It is a coherent commercial metaphor, not a random transferred usage.

In Al-Saff 61:10, the word moves from the economic field into faithful choice, while the following verses identify the elements of that trade. In Fatir 35:29, it concerns deeds hoped not to perish.

These contexts should not be treated as identical: in Al-Baqarah the trade is inverted and unprofitable; in Fatir it is hoped to be free of ruin; and in Al-Saff it rescues from punishment.

Grammatical and Structural Notes

Trade as the Grammatical Subject

In fa-mā rabiḥat tijāratuhum, tijāratuhum is the subject of rabiḥat. The Qur’an does not say merely that they did not profit from trade; it presents the trade itself as yielding no profit.

This construction places their transaction before the account of gain and loss as an economic activity.

The Adjective حَاضِرَة

In tijāratan ḥāḍiratan, ḥāḍirah is a feminine adjective agreeing with the grammatically feminine tijārah.

Its presence is understood in contrast with the deferred debt earlier in the verse. “Immediate transaction” or “present trade” is therefore more precise than “existing trade.”

تُدِيرُونَهَا بَيْنَكُمْ

The pronoun hā refers to tijārah. Tudīrūnahā speaks of turning or circulating it among the parties.

The phrase indicates exchange and circulation, but it alone should not become a theory about circulation of all capital in an economy.

عَنْ تَرَاضٍ

In tijāratan ʿan tarāḍin minkum, ʿan can express the source from which the trade arises: trade proceeding from your mutual consent.

“By mutual consent” is natural; “arising from mutual consent among you” makes the construction of ʿan more visible.

Fear of Stagnation

In takhshawna kasādahā, hā refers to trade. What is feared is not trade itself but its stagnation.

Kasād approaches lack of market activity, merchandise not selling, and the circulation of the market stopping.

لَا تُلْهِيهِمْ

In Al-Nur 24:37, tijārah and bayʿ are delayed subjects of tulhīhim. The verb is feminine singular, agreeing with the first subject, tijārah.

The verse does not mean that these people do not trade. It means trade does not divert or occupy them away from the named responsibilities.

لَنْ تَبُورَ

In Fatir 35:29, lan tabūr describes or qualifies trade. Lan negates a future occurrence and denies that this trade will reach ruin.

“A trade that will never perish” is more precise than “a trade that presently has no loss.”

تِجَارَةٍ تُنْجِيكُمْ

In Al-Saff 61:10, tunjīkum is a clause qualifying trade: “a trade that rescues you.”

Rescue is grammatically attributed to trade, while the next verse clarifies that this commercial title refers to faith and striving with property and life.

Comparison with Related Roots

T J R and B Y ʿ: Trade and Sale

B Y ʿ denotes buying and selling and transferring something through a transaction. Bayʿ can focus on the contract or specific act of sale.

Tijārah often describes the broader field of commercial activity and seeking profit through buying and selling.

The distinction is not absolute. Trade consists of purchases and sales, and bayʿ can also occur in broad economic usage. Their coordination in Al-Nur 24:37 shows that they are not fully identical without establishing a complete technical boundary.

T J R and SH R Y: Trade and Purchasing

In the Qur’an, SH R Y can denote buying and, in some constructions, selling. It focuses on one side of exchange or on the act of exchanging.

Tijārah names the activity or process within which such exchanges occur. In Al-Baqarah 2:16, ishtaraw expresses the act of exchange and tijāratuhum names the whole transaction and its result.

T J R and R B H: Trade and Profit

Tijārah is the activity of exchange; ribḥ is the increase or gain resulting from it.

Every trade seeks profit, but not every trade reaches it: fa-mā rabiḥat tijāratuhum.

Translating trade as “profitability” is therefore wrong because it inserts the desired result into the meaning of the activity.

T J R and KH S R: Trade and Loss

Loss denotes diminution, reduction of capital, or forfeiture of value.

Trade is the field of action, while loss is one possible outcome. In the metaphor of Al-Baqarah 2:16, the wording says “did not profit,” though the transaction is conceptually ruinous.

Trade must not be treated as inherently profitable. The possibility of loss belongs to the external reality of trade, although tijārah itself does not mean loss or risk.

T J R and K S D: Trade and Stagnation

Kasād approaches lack of market activity, merchandise failing to sell, and reduced circulation.

Trade may continue yet suffer stagnation: wa-tijāratun takhshawna kasādahā. Stagnation is a condition of a market or trade, not an equivalent of trade itself.

T J R and B W R: Trade and Ruin

Bawār denotes perishing, ruin, fruitlessness, or an outcome without vitality or result.

In tijāratan lan tabūr, trade is protected from ruin. This implies that trade can ordinarily be exposed to ruin, while that particular trade will not be.

Bawār is wider than kasād: stagnation can be recession and lack of briskness, whereas ruin may reach complete destruction and failure.

T J R and R B W: Trade and Ribā

R B W denotes increase, rising, and growth. Ribā is a kind of increase in financial relations that the Qur’an contrasts with sale: wa-aḥalla Allāhu al-bayʿa wa-ḥarrama al-ribā.

Trade and ribā are related insofar as both may involve increase in property, but Qur’anically they are not the same.

T J R alone does not provide a complete definition of ribā or a criterion for every usurious transaction. It is not sound to say merely that every profit without risk is ribā and every profit with risk is trade. Such judgments require independent study of R B W, the contexts of its verses, and the actual contract.

T J R and D W R: Trade and Circulation

In Al-Baqarah 2:282, trade is described with tudīrūnahā baynakum.

D W R denotes turning and circulation. Trade can be something circulated among people, but circulation is not the meaning of T J R itself.

Their collocation shows that present exchange involves transfer and circulation among the parties.

T J R and K S B: Trade and Acquisition

Kasb denotes obtaining, earning, and acquiring, and its range is wider than trade. A person can acquire property, sin, reward, or the result of an act.

Trade is one means of acquisition, but not every acquisition is trade. Trade depends on exchange and pursuit of profit; acquisition focuses on what is obtained or the act of obtaining it.

T J R and ʾ K L Concerning Property

In Al-Nisa 4:29, consuming property through falsehood is contrasted with trade arising from consent.

“Consuming property” images wrongful appropriation and use of another’s wealth. Trade is one way property circulates among parties, but legitimacy is tied to conditions including absence of falsehood and presence of consent.

The contrast does not mean that every transfer of property outside trade is false; inheritance, gifts, charity, and other legitimate avenues also exist.

Translation Suggestions for Forqan

تِجَارَة

Depending on context: trade, commerce, commercial exchange, transaction, commercial activity, or, in an explanatory translation, commercial goods.

“Trade” is the closest general equivalent. In Al-Baqarah 2:282, “transaction” may be more natural.

فَمَا رَبِحَتْ تِجَارَتُهُمْ

Primary suggestion: So their trade yielded no profit.

Other possibilities: Their exchange brought no gain or Their transaction produced no profit.

“Their trade lost” conveys the implication, but the verse negates profit rather than directly stating loss.

تِجَارَةً حَاضِرَةً تُدِيرُونَهَا بَيْنَكُمْ

Primary suggestion: Unless it is a present transaction that you circulate among yourselves.

More natural: Unless it is an immediate exchange conducted among yourselves.

“Cash trade” may specify the medium of payment more than the verse itself does.

تِجَارَةً عَنْ تَرَاضٍ مِنْكُمْ

Primary suggestion: Unless it is trade arising from mutual consent among you.

More natural: Unless it is trade conducted by your mutual consent.

“Mutual consent” also accommodates more than two parties.

تِجَارَةٌ تَخْشَوْنَ كَسَادَهَا

Trade whose stagnation you fear, or commerce that you fear may lose its market.

لَا تُلْهِيهِمْ تِجَارَةٌ وَلَا بَيْعٌ

Neither trade nor sale distracts them, or, more explicitly, No trade or buying and selling occupies them and distracts them from remembrance of The God.

تِجَارَةً لَنْ تَبُورَ

Primary suggestion: A trade that will never perish.

More pictorial: A trade that will never fall into stagnation and ruin.

“A trade that will not be fruitless” is intelligible but weakens the economic image of bawār.

تِجَارَةٍ تُنْجِيكُمْ

A trade that rescues you, or A transaction that delivers you from painful punishment.

“Transaction” strengthens the metaphor, while “trade” preserves the Qur’anic term more directly.

وَإِذَا رَأَوْا تِجَارَةً

Literal: When they saw trade.

More natural but explanatory: When they saw a commercial opportunity or commercial goods.

“Caravan” should be added only when a translation deliberately includes historical background; tijārah does not explicitly mean caravan.

Summary

The Arabic root T J R concerns buying and selling and the commercial employment of property in pursuit of profit. It occurs nine times in the Qur’an and only as the noun tijārah. Forms such as tajara, ittajara, tājir, tujjār, and matjar belong to the valid Arabic lexical family but do not occur in the Qur’an.

The core of trade is bringing property or goods into an exchange relationship in pursuit of gain. Market knowledge, effort, time, possible loss, and movement of capital may accompany trade, but they should not all be treated as certain dictionary meanings of the root.

The Qur’an uses tijārah for present circulating transactions, trade arising from consent, trade exposed to stagnation, trade and sale that must not distract, and a commercial attraction that caused people to leave a gathering.

In three contexts commercial language is figurative. In Al-Baqarah 2:16, people exchange guidance for error and their trade yields no profit. In Fatir 35:29, deeds are presented as a trade hoped never to perish. In Al-Saff 61:10, faith and striving with property and life are compared to a rescuing trade.

Trade and profit are not identical: trade is the field of exchange, and profit is its beneficial outcome. Trade may encounter stagnation, loss, or ruin. Trade and sale overlap, but their coordination in Al-Nur 24:37 prevents treating them as wholly synonymous.

The relation between trade and ribā also requires care. The Qur’an distinguishes sale from ribā, but T J R alone does not offer a complete theory of risk, profit, loans, and economic justice. Those matters require an independent study of the ribā verses and the actual structure of transactions.

T J R relates to roots such as B Y ʿ, SH R Y, R B H, KH S R, K S D, B W R, R B W, D W R, and K S B, but its distinctive field remains entering buying, selling, and exchange in pursuit of profit.
