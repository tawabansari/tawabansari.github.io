Taswīl: making an action attractive in a person's eyes and turning desire into justification

1. The root in early Arabic

Arabic records two semantic branches of S W L that should be distinguished: looseness and drooping, and taswīl, making an action attractive, pleasing, or acceptable to someone.

In the first branch, سَوِلَ يَسْوَلُ سَوَلًا (sawila yaswalu sawalan) means becoming loose or drooping. Al-sawal describes laxity and sagging of the abdomen below the navel, and aswal describes someone with this condition. The same adjective is recorded for a cloud with a hanging, lowered underside.

In the second branch, سَوَّلَ لَهُ الشَّيْءَ (sawwala lahu al-shayʾa) means making something attractive to someone, pleasing in their eyes, or apparently easy and acceptable to do. Saying that someone's self has adorned an action for them means that it has presented the action in a way that inclines them to undertake it.

Taswīl in this sense is more than the emergence of a desire or thought: it is the embellishment of that desire, preparing the way for acceptance and action. A person may come to see something they would ordinarily disapprove of as justified, useful, easy, or even admirable.

A witness attributed to a pre-Islamic poet

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Mutanakhkhil al-Hudhalī reads:

كَالسُّحُلِ الْبِيضِ جَلَا لَوْنَهَا
سَحُّ نِجَاءِ الْحَمَلِ الْأَسْوَلِ

ka-al-suḥuli al-bīḍi jalā lawnahā / saḥḥu nijāʾi al-ḥamali al-aswali

“Like white garments whose color has been brightened by pouring rain from a dark, drooping cloud.” The poem describes wild cattle, likening them to white cloth washed bright by rain; it does not liken the garments to the cattle.

Al-aswal in this verse describes the cloud and refers to its hanging underside. The witness shows that the sawal branch, concerning looseness and drooping, was known in early Arabic.

Literary sources attribute the verse to a pre-Islamic poet, but its wording and attribution reach us through collections compiled later. It is evidence of an early usage, not definitive proof of when the word first arose.

For taswīl in the sense of making something attractive and pleasing, the Qur'an itself provides some of the clearest evidence for the language of its period. Independent historical evidence establishing precisely when, and how widely, the verb was used before the Qur'an is more limited.

Early lexicographers also differed about the connection between these branches. Ibn Fāris records looseness and drooping under S W L, but suggests that taswīl may be related to suʾl, a person's wish or desired object: presenting something as corresponding to that wish. This explanation involves easing the hamzah.

The suggestion is semantically understandable: someone desires something, and that desire becomes attractive enough to incline them to act. Yet suʾl, with its hamzah, belongs to S ʾ L. This hypothesis alone does not justify merging all the meanings of the two roots.

For Qur'anic study, the main basis is therefore the clear meaning and actual use of sawwala in the verses, rather than a supposedly certain history of its development from bodily laxity to psychological deception.

2. Forms of the root in the Qur'an

The root occurs four times in the Qur'an, always as a Form II verb, sawwala.

The Qur'anic forms are سَوَّلَتْ (sawwalat) in Yūsuf 12:18, Yūsuf 12:83, and Ṭā Hā 20:96, and سَوَّلَ (sawwala) in Muḥammad 47:25.

Sawwalat is a third-person feminine singular perfect verb; sawwala is third-person masculine singular perfect. Their grammatical gender corresponds to the subjects: anfusukum and nafsī, on the one hand, and al-shayṭān, on the other.

These uses reveal an important feature: taswīl is attributed both to the human self and to Satan. The verb does not name a particular being or power; it describes an action whose agent and object must be identified in each context.

The verbal noun taswīl and other forms in the family, although known in Arabic, do not occur in the Qur'anic text.

3. The self's taswīl in the story of Joseph

Making a wrongful action attractive

Yūsuf 12:18 states:

وَجَاءُوا عَلَىٰ قَمِيصِهِ بِدَمٍ كَذِبٍ ۚ قَالَ بَلْ سَوَّلَتْ لَكُمْ أَنْفُسُكُمْ أَمْرًا ۖ فَصَبْرٌ جَمِيلٌ ۖ وَاللَّهُ الْمُسْتَعَانُ عَلَىٰ مَا تَصِفُونَ

wa-jāʾū ʿalā qamīṣihi bi-damin kadhibin qāla bal sawwalat lakum anfusukum amran fa-ṣabrun jamīlun wa-allāhu al-mustaʿānu ʿalā mā taṣifūn

Yūsuf 12:18

They brought false blood upon his shirt. He said, “Rather, your selves have made an affair attractive to you. So, beautiful patience; and The God is the one whose help is sought against what you describe.”

Confronting the account his sons had fabricated about Joseph, Jacob does more than point to its falsity: he describes a process that had already taken place within them.

They had wanted something, and that desire had become so attractive to them that they decided to act upon it.

Earlier verses clarify this process. In Yūsuf 12:8 and Yūsuf 12:9, Joseph's brothers resent their father's greater love for Joseph and his brother, and say:

اقْتُلُوا يُوسُفَ أَوِ اطْرَحُوهُ أَرْضًا يَخْلُ لَكُمْ وَجْهُ أَبِيكُمْ وَتَكُونُوا مِنْ بَعْدِهِ قَوْمًا صَالِحِينَ

uqtulū yūsufa awi iṭraḥūhu arḍan yakhlu lakum wajhu abīkum wa-takūnū min baʿdihi qawman ṣāliḥīn

Yūsuf 12:9

“Kill Joseph or cast him into a land, so that your father's attention may be yours alone; afterward, you can become righteous people.”

This statement clearly illustrates how taswīl works.

They have a desire: winning their father's attention and affection. They see removing their brother as a solution. They even construct an attractive picture of what will follow: after doing this, they will become righteous people.

Here, personal desire becomes justification for wrongdoing, as though one could first commit injustice and then make it acceptable by becoming righteous later.

“Your selves have made an affair attractive to you” takes its meaning in this setting. The self presents its desire as something feasible and acceptable.

Yet the verb's meaning should be distinguished from the story's details. Sawwalat does not itself mean envy, lying, killing, or abduction. These come from other verses in the narrative. The verb describes making the action attractive in their eyes.

The same expression in Yūsuf 12:83

After the incident in which Joseph's other brother remains in Egypt, Jacob says again:

قَالَ بَلْ سَوَّلَتْ لَكُمْ أَنْفُسُكُمْ أَمْرًا ۖ فَصَبْرٌ جَمِيلٌ ۖ عَسَى اللَّهُ أَنْ يَأْتِيَنِي بِهِمْ جَمِيعًا ۚ إِنَّهُ هُوَ الْعَلِيمُ الْحَكِيمُ

qāla bal sawwalat lakum anfusukum amran fa-ṣabrun jamīlun ʿasā allāhu an yaʾtiyanī bihim jamīʿan innahu huwa al-ʿalīmu al-ḥakīm

Yūsuf 12:83

He said, “Rather, your selves have made an affair attractive to you. So, beautiful patience. Perhaps The God will bring them all to me. He is indeed the Knowing, the Wise.”

Repeating the earlier expression shows that Jacob again relates the event to his sons' inward decisions and judgments.

The difference between the two situations must nevertheless be preserved.

In the first incident, the Qur'an explicitly describes the brothers' decision to remove Joseph and invent a false report. In the second, the detention of Joseph's brother in Egypt is narrated with different details, and the brothers themselves say they testified only to what they knew.

Repeating “your selves have made an affair attractive to you” does not by itself prove that they fabricated exactly the same lie and deception again. What is clear is Jacob's judgment about the role of their selves in what had happened.

This distinction matters for understanding the root: taswīl means making an action attractive to someone, but identifying that action, the person's knowledge, and the details of responsibility requires the context of each event.

4. The self's taswīl in the account of the Samaritan

Ṭā Hā 20:96 states:

قَالَ بَصُرْتُ بِمَا لَمْ يَبْصُرُوا بِهِ فَقَبَضْتُ قَبْضَةً مِنْ أَثَرِ الرَّسُولِ فَنَبَذْتُهَا وَكَذَٰلِكَ سَوَّلَتْ لِي نَفْسِي

qāla baṣurtu bi-mā lam yabṣurū bihi fa-qabaḍtu qabḍatan min athari al-rasūli fa-nabadhtuhā wa-kadhālika sawwalat lī nafsī

Ṭā Hā 20:96

He said, “I perceived what they did not perceive, so I took a handful from the messenger's trace and cast it away; thus my self made it attractive to me.”

Here the Samaritan describes his own action and finally attributes it to his self's taswīl.

His wording differs clearly from Jacob's. Jacob tells his sons, “Your selves have made it attractive to you”; the Samaritan says, “My self made it attractive to me.”

In both cases the self is the agent of taswīl, but in the Samaritan's account the person himself offers this description of his action.

The context shows his role in producing the calf toward which the people turned. Aaron had already warned them against this error, but they insisted on remaining devoted to it; see Ṭā Hā 20:88, Ṭā Hā 20:90, and Ṭā Hā 20:91.

Attributing an action to the self's taswīl does not erase personal responsibility. The Samaritan does not say he played no part in his decision. He acted, then explained that his self had made the action attractive to him.

Nor does the verse permit unwritten details about the nature of the messenger's trace, the calf's construction, or its unexplained mechanism to be inserted into the root's meaning.

The clear sense remains: the action became attractive and acceptable to the Samaritan, and he undertook it.

5. Satan's taswīl: making retreat from truth attractive

Muḥammad 47:25 states:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ ارْتَدُّوا عَلَىٰ أَدْبَارِهِمْ مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا تَبَيَّنَ لَهُمُ الْهُدَى الشَّيْطَانُ سَوَّلَ لَهُمْ وَأَمْلَىٰ لَهُمْ

inna alladhīna irtaddū ʿalā adbārihim min baʿdi mā tabayyana lahumu al-hudā al-shayṭānu sawwala lahum wa-amlā lahum

Muḥammad 47:25

Those who turned back after guidance had become clear to them—Satan made it attractive to them and prolonged their hopes.

Unlike the previous three occurrences, Satan is the subject of sawwala here.

The verb still means making something attractive and pleasing; its object is identified through the sentence's context: turning back from guidance that had become clear to them.

“After guidance had become clear to them” is a decisive qualification. The verse concerns people to whom guidance had become clear, and who then turned away. This differs from someone who has not yet properly recognized the truth, is mistaken, or is confused by conflicting claims.

The next verse, Muḥammad 47:26, further clarifies their behavior:

ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمْ قَالُوا لِلَّذِينَ كَرِهُوا مَا نَزَّلَ اللَّهُ سَنُطِيعُكُمْ فِي بَعْضِ الْأَمْرِ

dhālika bi-annahum qālū li-alladhīna karihū mā nazzala allāhu sa-nuṭīʿukum fī baʿḍi al-amr

Muḥammad 47:26

That is because they said to those who disliked what The God sent down, “We will obey you in some matters.”

Here taswīl is associated with retreat from guidance and alignment with those who disliked what The God had revealed.

Satan here performs taswīl: he makes the action attractive to them. The Qur'an does not say that he decided in their place or that they lacked choice and responsibility.

Wa-amlā lahum should also be considered separately from sawwala lahum. The active reading أَمْلَىٰ in the quoted text should not be translated as the passive “they were granted respite.” “Prolonged their hopes” here follows the interpretation that takes Satan as the subject; another explanation concerns granting respite. Other readings and interpretations of the subject are also recorded. These differences do not belong in the lexical definition of taswīl.

The verse thus describes Satan's role in making retreat from truth attractive, while also addressing the conscious action of people who chose another path after guidance had become clear.

6. Taswīl and related concepts

S W L (sawwala) and Z Y N (zayyana)

Tazyīn concerns adornment and making something beautiful, and has a broader range than taswīl. The Qur'an speaks of the adornment of creation, the adornment of life, and wrongful deeds being made attractive.

For example, Al-Anfāl 8:48 states:

وَإِذْ زَيَّنَ لَهُمُ الشَّيْطَانُ أَعْمَالَهُمْ

wa-idh zayyana lahumu al-shayṭānu aʿmālahum

Al-Anfāl 8:48

And when Satan made their deeds attractive to them.

This use is close to taswīl: in both, an action can appear desirable in a person's eyes.

The Qur'anic occurrences of taswīl all appear in contexts condemning conduct or explaining an inclination toward wrongdoing. Taswīl can therefore describe misleading embellishment of an action. In Yūsuf 12:83 this is Jacob's assessment; the repeated expression alone does not independently establish the same earlier deception.

This does not mean that every kind of adornment is linguistically taswīl, or that all uses of Z Y N are negative.

S W L (sawwala) and W S W S (waswasa)

Waswasah is more closely associated with hidden, recurring suggestions or speech within a person. The Qur'an speaks of both Satan's whispering and the self's whispering.

Qāf 50:16 states:

وَنَعْلَمُ مَا تُوَسْوِسُ بِهِ نَفْسُهُ

wa-naʿlamu mā tuwaswisu bihi nafsuhu

Qāf 50:16

And We know what his self whispers to him.

Taswīl emphasizes making an action attractive and pleasing; waswasah concerns the hidden suggestion of a thought.

They can occur together in a process: a thought arises in someone's mind, then becomes so attractive that acting on it seems easy and acceptable.

This connection should not be turned into an invariable sequence. The Qur'an does not say that every taswīl necessarily follows whispering or that every whisper leads to taswīl.

S W L (sawwala) and S ʾ L (saʾala)

This distinction is especially relevant to suʾl.

Ṭā Hā 20:36 states:

قَالَ قَدْ أُوتِيتَ سُؤْلَكَ يَا مُوسَىٰ

qāla qad ūtīta suʾlaka yā mūsā

Ṭā Hā 20:36

He said, “You have been granted your request, Moses.”

Suʾl here refers to a wish or request and belongs to the hamzah-containing root S ʾ L.

Some lexicographers suggest a possible connection between this word and taswīl: what a person wants is made attractive in their eyes.

But wanting something is not itself taswīl. A person may have a rightful, legitimate wish, as Moses' request is accepted in this verse.

By contrast, the Qur'anic uses of taswīl describe making a wrongful action acceptable and pleasing to a person.

Suʾl should therefore not be counted uncritically among the Qur'anic derivatives of S W L, nor should taswīl be reduced to wishing or requesting in general.

7. Suggested renderings for Forqan

The principal rendering of sawwala and sawwalat is “make attractive in someone's eyes” or “make pleasing to someone.”

In Yūsuf 12:18 and Yūsuf 12:83, the expression:

بَلْ سَوَّلَتْ لَكُمْ أَنْفُسُكُمْ أَمْرًا

bal sawwalat lakum anfusukum amran

Yūsuf 12:18; Yūsuf 12:83

can be rendered:

“Rather, your selves have made an affair attractive to you.”

This preserves the Arabic construction without inserting a specific action in place of amran, “an affair.”

In Ṭā Hā 20:96:

وَكَذَٰلِكَ سَوَّلَتْ لِي نَفْسِي

wa-kadhālika sawwalat lī nafsī

Ṭā Hā 20:96

a suitable rendering is:

“And thus my self made it attractive to me.”

In Muḥammad 47:25:

الشَّيْطَانُ سَوَّلَ لَهُمْ

al-shayṭānu sawwala lahum

Muḥammad 47:25

one can say:

“Satan made it attractive to them.”

An explanation may add that this means making retreat from guidance attractive to them, since the beginning of the verse identifies that context.

“Deceived,” “seduced,” and “led astray” may convey part of the result of taswīl in some verses, but these meanings are not all contained in the verb itself. Deception is broader and may involve direct falsehood, concealing truth, or other means. Taswīl emphasizes making an action attractive and acceptable in someone's eyes.

Likewise, translating sawwala as “whispered” erases the distinction between these roots.

Explanatory expressions such as “making wrongdoing appear justified,” “presenting what is wrong as desirable,” and “embellishing the self's desire” may clarify taswīl, but should not be inserted without reason into every direct verse translation.

Conclusion

In early Arabic, S W L has a branch concerning looseness and drooping, used of the body and clouds. Alongside it, taswīl is known as making an action attractive and pleasing to a person. A definite historical connection between the branches has not been established; the proposed connection with suʾl, “a wish,” should likewise not be presented as proven derivation.

All Qur'anic occurrences use the verb sawwala in contexts condemning or explaining the embellishment of wrongful conduct, while preserving the distinction between a speaker's assessment and an independently established event.

In the story of Joseph's brothers, taswīl relates to a personal desire and justification of an unjust act. The Samaritan speaks of his own self's taswīl. In Muḥammad, Satan is identified as the agent, and the subject is the retreat of people to whom guidance had become clear.

In none of these cases does taswīl mean depriving people of choice. The self or Satan may make an action pleasing and apparently justified, but attributing this process to them does not by itself remove responsibility for understanding, choosing, and acting.

Final definition: In Qur'anic usage, taswīl is making an action attractive and apparently acceptable to someone, inclining them to undertake or accept it. Its shared contextual feature is presenting wrongdoing as pleasing and justified—not merely the emergence of a wish, every kind of whispering, or compulsion to act.

This distinction shows that misguidance does not always begin with overt rejection of truth. Sometimes people first construct a justification for their desire and then embrace it so thoroughly that wrongdoing appears right and defensible to them.
