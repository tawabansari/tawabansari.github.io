The Arabic root و ج س concerns perceiving or sensing something, especially a sign that is not readily apparent. This perception sometimes arises through hearing a faint sound and sometimes appears as a feeling of concern or apprehension within a person.

In the Quran, this root occurs only in the verb أَوْجَسَ, accompanied by خِيفَةً in all three instances. Understanding it, however, requires more than starting with fear and restricting the entire word family to “being afraid.” Early Arabic evidence also places alertness, perception of a sign, and inward feeling within its semantic range.

1. The Root in Early Arabic

In poetry attributed to ʿAlqamah ibn ʿAbadah, a pre-Islamic poet, تَوَجَّسَ describes an alert and cautious animal:

تُلاحِظُ السَّوْطَ شَزْرًا وَهِيَ ضَامِزَةٌ

tulāḥiẓu al-sawṭa shazran wa-hiya ḍāmizatun

كَمَا تَوَجَّسَ طَاوِي الْكَشْحِ مَوْشُومُ

kamā tawajjasa ṭāwī al-kashḥi mawshūmu

“She watches the whip sidelong, silent and close-mouthed, like a lean-flanked, marked wild bull listening warily.”

The poet describes a she-camel watching the whip cautiously and compares her behavior to an alert wild bull sensitive to signs around it.

Here, tawajjus is more than being afraid: it is cautious attention to something that might signal danger. The animal need not yet face a definite threat, but is alert to possible indications of one.

This witness shows that, at least in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period, تَوَجَّسَ was associated with alertness and the cautious perception of signs.

A variant reported for a verse attributed to al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī has مُسْتَوْجِس: someone who has sensed something and become apprehensive or watchful. The better-known reading is مُسْتَأْنِس. The variant is therefore only supporting evidence and should not be treated as conclusive without examining its transmission.

Arabic dictionaries record two notable uses of وَجْس: a faint sound, and an alarm or disturbance arising in the heart. These uses can be semantically connected: a person perceives a sound or sign and consequently feels concern.

The available evidence does not establish that “faint sound” was necessarily the root’s earliest historical meaning, from which apprehension subsequently developed. A more secure conclusion is an old association between the cautious perception of a sign and a feeling of apprehension.

2. Forms and Semantic Core

Important forms in this Arabic word family include:

وَجَسَ: To sense something or become apprehensive. Lexical usage also records something being faint or unobtrusive.

وَجْس: A faint sound, or alarm and concern arising within the heart.

تَوَجَّسَ: To listen for a faint sound, become alert to a sign, or feel apprehension and concern.

أَوْجَسَ: To sense something or find it within oneself, especially when the object is apprehension or concern.

وَاجِس: A thought or feeling passing through a person’s inner awareness, close to an inward prompting or passing thought.

Dictionaries also record limited uses of الأَوْجَس in expressions concerning time or a small quantity of food. These do not occur in the Quran, and their historical connection with perception and apprehension has not been clearly established. A single artificial image should not be devised to unite them all.

Semantic Core

A defensible account of this family’s central meaning is:

Perceiving or sensing something not readily apparent, which may arouse cautious attention or an inward response.

When this feeling concerns danger or the possibility of something unwelcome, its meaning approaches “feeling apprehension.”

Hiddenness in this family does not necessarily mean deliberate concealment. The sign itself may be faint, or the feeling may arise inwardly without the person deciding to hide it from others.

The root should not be restricted to hearing. Although hearing a faint sound is a recognized use, the Quran shows that the same verb can express a feeling prompted by observing a situation.

3. Quranic Occurrences

The root و ج س occurs three times in the Quran, each as the Form IV past-tense verb أَوْجَسَ. The differences between وَأَوْجَسَ and فَأَوْجَسَ arise from the initial conjunctions; these are not separate derivatives.

In each instance, خِيفَةً names what is felt. It comes from خ و ف and explicitly expresses fear.

A. Hūd 11:70 — Abraham’s Apprehension Before Unfamiliar Guests

فَلَمَّا رَأَىٰ أَيْدِيَهُمْ لَا تَصِلُ إِلَيْهِ نَكِرَهُمْ وَأَوْجَسَ مِنْهُمْ خِيفَةً ۚ قَالُوا لَا تَخَفْ إِنَّا أُرْسِلْنَا إِلَىٰ قَوْمِ لُوطٍ

fa-lammā raʾā aydiyahum lā taṣilu ilayhi nakirahum wa-awjasa minhum khīfatan qālū lā takhaf innā ursilnā ilā qawmi Lūṭ

Hūd 11:70

“When he saw that their hands did not reach toward it, he found them strange and felt fear of them within himself. They said: do not fear; we have been sent to the people of Lot.”

Abraham has brought food for his guests, but when he sees them refrain from reaching for it, their behavior becomes unexpected to him.

نَكِرَهُمْ indicates that Abraham finds them unfamiliar or strange. وَأَوْجَسَ مِنْهُمْ خِيفَةً then expresses his apprehension.

Three elements can be distinguished here:

First, observing unexpected behavior; second, the emergence of concern; and third, that concern’s connection with these guests.

The preposition مِنْ in مِنْهُمْ identifies the source or object of apprehension: Abraham felt fear of them.

The verse does not say that the guests actually intended harm. Abraham’s feeling arose from observing the situation, and the envoys’ subsequent words reassured him.

Thus, the construction أَوْجَسَ خِيفَةً here means “felt fear within himself.” The perception of an unexpected sign is understood from context, while خِيفَةً identifies the feeling.

B. Ṭā Hā 20:67 — Moses’ Inward Apprehension

فَأَوْجَسَ فِي نَفْسِهِ خِيفَةً مُّوسَىٰ

fa-awjasa fī nafsihi khīfatan Mūsā

Ṭā Hā 20:67

“Then Moses felt fear within himself.”

This verse follows the scene of Moses’ confrontation with the magicians.

In the preceding verse, Ṭā Hā 20:66, the magicians’ ropes and staffs appear to Moses as though they are moving. After this observation, the Quran speaks of his apprehension.

Unlike Abraham’s story, مِنْهُمْ does not occur here. Instead, we read:

فِي نَفْسِهِ

fī nafsih

Ṭā Hā 20:67

That is, “within himself” or “in his heart.”

This phrase identifies where the feeling occurs: Moses felt apprehension inwardly.

مُوسَىٰ is the subject of أَوْجَسَ, placed at the end of the sentence; خِيفَةً is its object. The sentence therefore means that Moses himself felt apprehension, not that someone else feared Moses.

In context, the feeling follows the sight of ropes and staffs that appear to move. The Quranic use of أَوْجَسَ is therefore not limited to hearing a sound.

In the next verse, Ṭā Hā 20:68, Moses is told not to fear and that he has the upper hand. This response presents the apprehension as a passing state during the confrontation.

Semantically, the Quran states that the feeling occurred; the verb أَوْجَسَ itself gives no moral judgment about Moses’ faith or courage.

C. Al-Dhāriyāt 51:28 — Abraham’s Apprehension in Another Account

فَأَوْجَسَ مِنْهُمْ خِيفَةً ۖ قَالُوا لَا تَخَفْ ۖ وَبَشَّرُوهُ بِغُلَامٍ عَلِيمٍ

fa-awjasa minhum khīfatan qālū lā takhaf wa-bashsharūhu bi-ghulāmin ʿalīm

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:28

“Then he felt fear of them. They said: do not fear; and they gave him good news of a knowledgeable boy.”

This verse presents another perspective on the envoys’ visit to Abraham described in Hūd.

In Al-Dhāriyāt 51:25, Abraham finds the guests unfamiliar. In Al-Dhāriyāt 51:27, he sets food before them and asks why they do not eat. Al-Dhāriyāt 51:28 then describes his apprehension.

فَأَوْجَسَ مِنْهُمْ خِيفَةً has the same meaning as the expression in Hūd, with فَـ emphasizing the event’s connection to what preceded it.

The envoys immediately say لَا تَخَفْ: “do not fear.”

Their response confirms the nature of Abraham’s feeling: he had become apprehensive of them, although they had not come to harm him.

Reading the accounts in Hūd and Al-Dhāriyāt together shows that أَوْجَسَ fits apprehension in response to an unexpected situation. The verb alone determines neither the kind of danger nor whether the person’s assessment is correct.

4. Semantic and Structural Points

مِنْهُمْ and فِي نَفْسِهِ

In Hūd 11:70 and Al-Dhāriyāt 51:28, مِنْهُمْ draws attention to those who occasioned Abraham’s concern.

In Ṭā Hā 20:67, فِي نَفْسِهِ emphasizes the inward location of Moses’ feeling.

This difference shows the importance of prepositions in understanding sentence structure. مِنْ and فِي should not be assigned identical functions merely because they accompany the same verb.

The structural difference does not establish two wholly separate kinds of fear. All three verses concern apprehension: one construction emphasizes its source, the other where it is felt.

Does أَوْجَسَ Mean “To Conceal”?

Arabic explanations of this verb also use أَضْمَرَ: to hold something inwardly or keep it unexpressed.

An inward feeling and deliberate concealment remain distinct.

Saying that someone felt fear inwardly does not necessarily mean that they consciously hid it from others or showed no sign of it in their behavior or face.

Such a conclusion requires further contextual evidence.

Consequently, “felt fear within himself” is more precise for these Quranic uses than “concealed his fear.” The former states the feeling itself; the latter attributes an additional decision or behavior to the person.

The Verb’s Relationship to خِيفَةً

All three Quranic occurrences of the root accompany خِيفَةً.

This matters because أَوْجَسَ expresses inward perception, while خِيفَةً identifies what was felt as fear.

The entire meaning of أَوْجَسَ خِيفَةً should therefore not be attributed indiscriminately to the root و ج س itself.

This distinction explains why lexical uses of the family include hearing a faint sound or perceiving a sign, whereas its Quranic occurrences specifically concern apprehension.

5. Comparison with Related Roots

W J S Compared with Kh W F

خ و ف covers a broad range of fear and apprehension, arising from evident danger, possible harm, an uncertain future, or a threatening situation.

In the verses examined here, أَوْجَسَ emphasizes the emergence and inward perception of apprehension.

This should not become an absolute boundary, since خَوْف can also be entirely inward.

A defensible distinction is that خَوْف names the state of fear itself, whereas أَوْجَسَ خِيفَةً describes that state being perceived and felt.

Thus, in Abraham’s story, أَوْجَسَ is followed by لَا تَخَفْ. The two expressions concern one shared experience, not wholly unrelated states.

W J S Compared with W J L

و ج ل is also associated with inward fear and apprehension.

In Al-Ḥijr 15:52, Abraham uses وَجِلُونَ concerning his guests; in Al-Ḥijr 15:53, they tell him not to fear.

This is the same story described with أَوْجَسَ in Hūd and Al-Dhāriyāt.

Considering these expressions together shows their semantic overlap.

وَجَل primarily names the state of apprehension, while أَوْجَسَ in these passages highlights experiencing that feeling inwardly.

This difference does not establish wajs and wajal as fixed, successive stages of fear. The Quran uses both to describe Abraham’s experience without prescribing a necessary psychological sequence.

6. Translation Proposals for Forqan

In these three Quranic occurrences, a suitable translation should sound natural while preserving inward perception. These are editorial proposals, not revisions of existing verse word boxes.

For وَأَوْجَسَ مِنْهُمْ خِيفَةً in Hūd 11:70, a suitable rendering is:

“And he felt fear of them within himself.”

For فَأَوْجَسَ فِي نَفْسِهِ خِيفَةً مُوسَىٰ in Ṭā Hā 20:67:

“Then Moses felt fear within himself.”

For فَأَوْجَسَ مِنْهُمْ خِيفَةً in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:28:

“Then he felt fear of them.”

The Persian equivalent “became apprehensive” is also natural, but loses some emphasis on experiencing the feeling. “Feared inwardly” may work in some sentences, while “felt fear” more clearly preserves the distinction between the verb أَوْجَسَ and the noun خِيفَةً.

“Concealed his fear” is not recommended unless deliberate concealment is independently established by the context.

For non-Quranic uses of this family, equivalents vary with sentence structure: تَوَجَّسَ may mean becoming alert, listening warily, or feeling concern. وَجْس means “faint sound” in its acoustic use and “inward apprehension or alarm” in its emotional use.

A single Persian equivalent should therefore not be imposed on every form in the family regardless of context.

7. Conclusion

Arabic evidence associates و ج س with perceiving signs, alertness, and inward feeling. The poetry attributed to ʿAlqamah ibn ʿAbadah illustrates cautious awareness in تَوَجَّسَ; dictionaries also record faint sound and inward alarm within the family’s semantic range.

In the Quran, the root occurs only in أَوْجَسَ, accompanied by خِيفَةً in all three instances. Two describe Abraham’s apprehension before unfamiliar guests; the third describes Moses’ apprehension on observing the magicians’ display.

These uses show that أَوْجَسَ does not merely mean hearing a faint sound: inward perception is also involved. Conversely, the whole meaning of fear cannot be assigned to the root alone, because خِيفَةً contributes independently to the construction.

A practical definition is: perceiving or sensing something, especially an unobtrusive sign, which may arouse cautious awareness or an inward response. In the Quranic construction أَوْجَسَ خِيفَةً, that response is expressed as a feeling of apprehension.

Thus, translating the Quranic أَوْجَسَ as “felt within himself,” together with its object, is more precise than “feared” alone, without imposing deliberate concealment, a particular intensity of fear, or a judgment about the feeling’s validity on the word.
