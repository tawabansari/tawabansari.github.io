Seeking, moving toward an objective, military expeditions, and confronting an enemy

Arabic gh z w concerns intending, seeking, and moving toward an objective. In its familiar military use, ghazw means setting out against an enemy for confrontation and combat. A ghāzin participates in such an expedition, a ghazwah is a military expedition or operation, and maghzā in general usage means an objective or intended meaning.

In early Arabic, ghazw did not name only fighting on the battlefield; movement toward an enemy was also present in its meaning. People setting out for war could be called ghuzāh even before fighting began.

The family also has a general sense of seeking: ghazā al-shayʾa means to intend or pursue something. This broader use should not obscure the familiar military sense of the Quranic occurrence.

The root occurs once in the Quran, as ghuzzan in Āl ʿImrān 3:156. This is a plural of ghāzin, referring to people who have gone out for military confrontation.

The verse principally criticizes those who attributed the deaths of travellers and combatants to their departure, confidently claiming that had they remained with their families and companions, they would not have died or been killed.

1. Meaning in early Arabic

Ghazw: setting out against an enemy

In early military usage, ghazā al-ʿaduwwa meant going out against an enemy to fight.

It could involve gathering combatants, providing mounts and equipment, travelling, and confronting the other party.

Ghazw should therefore not be defined solely as the moment two armies clash or an enemy is killed. Military movement and the intention to confront are important aspects of its use.

For example, combatants may leave home and head toward another group’s territory. Even if the enemy withdraws or agreement is reached before battle, their movement may be called ghazw by virtue of its purpose and character.

The word alone does not establish who initiated the conflict, which side was justified, or whether the purpose was defence, revenge, plunder, or conquest.

Those questions depend on historical circumstances, the cause of conflict, and the parties’ conduct, rather than the root alone.

An early witness: ghazwah in al-Aʿshā’s poetry

A poem attributed to al-Aʿshā, a poet of the late pre-Islamic period, says:

وَلَا بُدَّ مِنْ غَزْوَةٍ فِي الرَّبِيعِ

wa-lā budda min ghazwatin fī al-rabīʿi

حَجُونٍ تُكِلُّ الْوَقَاحَ الشَّكُورَا

ḥajūnin tukillu al-waqāḥa al-shakūrā

Translation:

“A far-ranging expedition in spring is unavoidable, one that exhausts even the hard-hoofed mount sustained by little fodder.”

Ghazwah here denotes a military expedition. Lexicographers explain ḥajūn as far-ranging or as an expedition whose actual destination differs from the apparent one.

The poet speaks of travel and the mount’s exhaustion. The image includes the journey and hardships of campaigning, rather than merely the moment of combat.

The witness shows ghazwah used for an expedition in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic tradition.

The verse has variant transmissions. Some read fī al-maṣīf, “in summer,” instead of fī al-rabīʿ, “in spring,” and vary the expedition’s epithet. The word ghazwah and its military use remain.

The witness supports the expedition sense, but cannot establish the word’s exact date of origin or every feature of campaigning in pre-Islamic society.

Another witness: ghuzzāʾ in Taʾabbaṭa Sharran’s poetry

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Taʾabbaṭa Sharran says:

فَيَوْمًا بِغُزَّاءٍ وَيَوْمًا بِسُرْبَةٍ

fa-yawman bi-ghuzzāʾin wa-yawman bi-surbatin

وَيَوْمًا بِخَشْخَاشٍ مِنَ الرَّجْلِ هَيْضَلِ

wa-yawman bi-khashkhāshin mina al-rajli hayḍali

Interpretive translation:

“One day I travel with campaigners, another with a troop, and another with a great company of men.”

Ghuzzāʾ is a plural of ghāzin and denotes combatants or participants in a military expedition.

Surbah here also concerns a group or band of companions.

The poet describes travelling with different groups. Ghuzzāʾ names the participants, rather than a particular battle.

The verse varies in transmission, especially its second hemistich. Ghuzzāʾ is nevertheless a recognized lexical plural of ghāzin.

Together with al-Aʿshā’s verse, it shows both the expedition’s name and a designation for its participants in early Arabic poetic tradition.

Intending and seeking something

Classical sources also record ghazā al-shayʾa as intending or seeking something.

The expression maghzā al-kalām means the purpose or intended meaning of speech.

Someone may tell a story and a listener ask what its point is.

No military expedition is meant; the question concerns the speaker’s intended purpose.

Lexical sources likewise record ghazā al-amra and ightazāhu for intending or seeking something.

These uses show that the family extends beyond warfare.

Recording a use in a later Arabic dictionary does not itself prove that it had the same scope in pre-Islamic times.

The cited early poetic witnesses more directly support the military sense. General seeking is also recorded in reliable classical sources, but establishing its historical priority requires further independent evidence.

2. Semantic core and branches

A. Intending and moving toward an objective

In general usage, ghazw concerns turning toward something and pursuing it.

This seeking may concern a person, activity, or objective.

It should not be equated with every kind of wish or thought.

Someone may wish to visit a city without having decided or acted to reach it.

Choosing a destination and moving toward it makes intention and pursuit more evident in conduct.

This illustrates the relation between general seeking and military campaigning: a group selects an objective and moves toward an enemy to confront it.

It does not prove that every historical use derives from one demonstrable original sense.

B. Campaigning and setting out for war

In the military branch, ghazw involves more than intention: it concerns movement to confront an enemy.

Ghazā al-ʿaduwwa can denote going against an enemy and engaging it.

Some early campaigns involved taking enemy property and booty, since tribal expeditions could seek wealth, livestock, or other resources.

Plunder and booty should not be treated as necessary components of every use.

Military movement may seek defence, a response to aggression, recovery of a right, or other objectives.

The designation ghazw alone does not determine the war’s moral purpose or legitimacy.

C. Ghazwah: a particular expedition

Ghazwah denotes one instance of campaigning or military movement.

The form can distinguish a particular event from broader military activity.

Several expeditions over several years may each be called a ghazwah.

The form alone does not determine troop numbers, duration, whether fighting occurred, or its outcome.

In later historical literature, ghazawāt became a familiar designation for certain military events during Muḥammad’s lifetime.

That historical usage should not restrict the root to one person’s campaigns or one period, since the word existed earlier.

D. Maghzā: purpose and intended meaning

Maghzā denotes an objective or intended meaning.

Maghzā al-kalām concerns the purpose and meaning the speaker seeks to convey.

It relates to intention without directly describing physical movement toward an enemy.

Persian also uses maghzā or maghzā-ye sokhan for a statement’s point or intended meaning.

Maghzā should not be confused merely through spelling with Persian maghz, “brain” or “kernel”; their resemblance does not establish a shared derivation.

E. The branch concerning animal reproduction

Some lexical sources use aghzati al-nāqah of a she-camel that has difficulty conceiving.

Mughziyah is also recorded for certain female animals whose delivery occurs later than usual.

This differs from military movement or seeking an enemy.

Some Arabic dictionaries identify it as a separate branch of the entry.

A connection with delay may be imagined, but such an explanation does not establish a historical relationship to the military meaning.

None of these animal-reproduction forms occurs in the Quran.

3. Important forms and derivatives

Recognized forms include:

  • Ghazā / yaghzū: went against an enemy to fight; more generally, intended or sought something.
  • Ghazw: intending and seeking; militarily, an expedition or movement against an enemy.
  • Ghazwah: a particular military expedition or operation.
  • Ghazawāt: plural of ghazwah.
  • Ghāzin: someone who sets out for war and confrontation.
  • Ghuzāh: plural of ghāzin; combatants or expedition participants.
  • Ghuzzan: another plural of ghāzin, the Quranic form.
  • Ghuzzāʾ: another plural of ghāzin.
  • Ghāziyah: feminine of ghāzin; sometimes a group setting out for war.
  • Maghzūw: someone or something targeted by an expedition.
  • Maghzā: objective, intended meaning, or place of military movement, according to context.
  • Maghāzī: expeditions, their locations, or accounts of them.
  • Aghzā: sent someone on campaign or supplied the means for it.
  • Ghazzā: prepared or dispatched someone for an expedition.
  • Ightazā: intended or pursued something.
  • Mughziyah: a woman whose husband has gone on campaign; in another branch, a female animal whose delivery is delayed.

Only ghuzzan occurs in the Quran.

The verb ghazā, verbal noun ghazw, noun ghazwah, and forms ghuzāh and maghāzī do not occur in the Quranic text.

They matter for the lexical family but should not be described as attested Quranic derivatives.

The morphology of ghuzzan

Ghuzzan is a plural of ghāzin.

Ghāzin is the active participle of the simple verb ghazā, yaghzū.

The root is final-weak with wāw as its third radical, which changes form in some inflections.

Ghuzzan follows the plural pattern fuʿʿal, with alteration of the final weak radical. Comparable plurals include rukkaʿ from rākiʿ and sujjad from sājid.

It is thus a broken plural naming campaign participants, not a past-tense verb, a verbal noun, or the name of war itself.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:156 it follows kānū as its predicate and is accusative.

Ghuzzan and ghuzāh are alternative plurals of ghāzin; they do not inherently distinguish aggressors from defenders.

Neither form alone judges the legitimacy of warfare or participants’ intentions.

4. Quranic ghuzzan

Āl ʿImrān 3:156

يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا لَا تَكُونُوا كَٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا وَقَالُوا لِإِخْوَٰنِهِمْ إِذَا ضَرَبُوا فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ أَوْ كَانُوا غُزًّى لَّوْ كَانُوا عِندَنَا مَا مَاتُوا وَمَا قُتِلُوا لِيَجْعَلَ ٱللَّهُ ذَٰلِكَ حَسْرَةً فِى قُلُوبِهِمْ وَٱللَّهُ يُحْىِۦ وَيُمِيتُ وَٱللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ بَصِيرٌ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū lā takūnū ka-alladhīna kafarū wa-qālū li-ikhwānihim idhā ḍarabū fī al-arḍi aw kānū ghuzzan law kānū ʿindanā mā mātū wa-mā qutilū li-yajʿala allāhu dhālika ḥasratan fī qulūbihim wa-allāhu yuḥyī wa-yumītu wa-allāhu bi-mā taʿmalūna baṣīr

Āl ʿImrān 3:156

“You who have believed, do not be like those who disbelieved and said about their brothers, when they travelled through the land or went out for war, ‘Had they been with us, they would not have died or been killed,’ so that The God makes that a regret in their hearts. The God gives life and causes death, and The God sees what you do.”

Two situations appear together:

First, those who ḍarabū fī al-arḍ: travelled through the land.

Second, those who kānū ghuzzan: were participating in a military expedition.

The expressions are not identical.

Ḍarabū fī al-arḍ can concern travel, seeking a livelihood, and movement through the land.

Ghuzzan here denotes people engaged in military activity.

Not every journey or departure is ghazw, and ghuzzan should not be translated simply as travellers or migrants.

Campaigning, death, and being killed

Two verbs follow:

Mātū: they died.

Qutilū: they were killed.

Neither verb belongs to gh z w.

In this context, people who leave home may die while travelling or be killed in warfare.

Ghuzzan itself does not mean those killed; it identifies participants in an expedition.

A participant may return unharmed, be wounded, be killed, or return without direct combat.

Campaigning is an activity and situation; being killed is one possible outcome, rather than a necessary lexical component.

Rendering ghuzzan only as “the war dead” would replace the noun’s meaning with a possible consequence.

The statement of those who disbelieved

Law kānū ʿindanā mā mātū wa-mā qutilū is the statement of those who disbelieved.

They said their brothers would not have died or been killed if they had remained with them.

This is their claim, not a truth affirmed by the Quran.

They turned a possibility into a certainty.

A traveller may suffer an accident and relatives say, “Had he not travelled, he would certainly still be alive.”

Avoiding that journey does not itself guarantee protection from every other danger.

Similarly, someone may be killed in battle and others confidently claim that staying home would have prevented death altogether.

That claim fails to distinguish a conceivable alternative from certain knowledge of a person’s fate.

The verse then recalls that life and death fall within divine power.

This should not be presented as making human decisions, precautions, or commanders’ and decision-makers’ responsibilities irrelevant.

People remain responsible for conduct, choices, others’ rights, and avoiding unnecessary danger. The verse criticizes making an uncertain alternative to the past into a categorical claim about what never occurred.

Connections with nearby verses

Āl ʿImrān 3:154 also mentions people saying that, had they possessed some control over the matter, they would not have been killed there.

Āl ʿImrān 3:168 records a similar claim: those who stayed behind said their brothers would not have been killed had they obeyed them.

The three verses approach a shared concern from different angles: human judgments about death, choice, military decisions, and what might have happened.

The specific meaning of ghuzzan in Āl ʿImrān 3:156 remains participation in a military expedition.

Detailed study of divine will, human choice, command responsibility, and the manner of death is distinct from defining gh z w.

5. Other Quranic words for war and movement

Gh z w and q t l: campaigning and killing

Q t l concerns killing and, in form III, fighting and military confrontation.

Military ghazw denotes moving against an enemy and participating in an expedition.

The two can occur in one event but are not identical.

An army might set out for confrontation and peace be made before battle.

Military movement has then occurred without anyone necessarily being killed.

Conversely, not every killing is ghazw, since killing can occur outside an expedition.

Āl ʿImrān 3:156 preserves the distinction: ghuzzan describes participation in military movement, while qutilū denotes being killed.

Gh z w and j h d: campaigning and exertion

J h d concerns effort, using one’s capacity, and enduring difficulty in a task.

Quranic jihād has a broader scope than military movement alone; its kind depends on its object, means, and context.

Quranic gh z w, by contrast, concerns a specific military activity.

Campaigning may involve exertion and hardship, but not every effort or jihād is ghazw.

Conveying truth, defending human rights, or enduring difficulty in social reform can involve serious effort without a military expedition.

Ghazw and jihād should not be treated as fixed synonyms in Quran translation.

Gh z w and n f r: setting out and mobilizing

Quranic n f r concerns going out, moving, or group mobilization.

Such movement may be military, but the root is not restricted to one activity.

Al-Tawbah 9:122 relates going out, gaining understanding of religion, and warning one’s people. Interpretations differ over which group gains understanding and how its departure is understood.

Going out and moving are not in themselves military expeditions.

Military ghazw specifies movement directed toward confronting an enemy.

Gh z w and q ṣ d: general intention and a military objective

Qaṣd concerns direction, moderation, turning toward, and intending something.

Lexical sources also record ghazā al-shayʾa for intending and seeking something.

The families therefore overlap in some uses.

Qaṣd has a wider scope and need not describe movement toward battle.

The military branch of ghazw concerns seeking an enemy and moving toward confrontation.

Rendering ghuzzan in Āl ʿImrān 3:156 merely as “those intending” would erase its military sense.

Gh z w and gh n m: campaigning and booty

Ghanīmah concerns a gain or property obtained, in relevant contexts, through warfare.

Booty was one motive for some early Arab expeditions.

Campaigning and booty nevertheless differ.

A group can go to war and obtain no booty; its purpose may be defending life and land rather than taking others’ property.

Plunder and appropriation should not be treated as necessary meanings of ghazw.

The morality of a military objective and the legitimacy of taking property must be examined through relevant verses on justice, fighting, people’s rights, and prohibiting transgression.

6. Difficulties in understanding and translation

Does ghazw originally mean only intending?

Arabic sources record a general sense of intending.

The military sense is also known in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period and in the Arabic of the Quran’s first audience.

The broad intention sense should not be used to remove warfare from Quranic ghuzzan.

Nor should a specific historical derivation of the military sense from general intention be asserted without adequate evidence.

Does every ghazw mean aggression?

No. The designation alone does not specify every ethical and legal circumstance of a military action.

One expedition may involve aggression and appropriation; another may respond to an attack or threat.

Distinguishing these requires examining the conflict’s cause, the parties’ conduct, the scope of action, and its consequences.

Al-Baqarah 2:190 speaks of fighting those who fight and prohibits transgression.

Al-Anfāl 8:61 speaks of inclining to peace if the other party does so.

The word ghazw alone cannot establish authorization for war or a moral judgment about participants.

Does ghuzzan mean aggressors?

In some historical settings, “attackers” can fit a group attacking another territory.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:156, however, “aggressors” could attribute an initiating role in wrongful aggression beyond what the verse states.

The verse does not independently explain the nature of their fighting or which side attacked first.

“Combatants,” “participants in an expedition,” or, in the sentence, “had gone out for war” are more precise equivalents.

Does ghuzzan mean the war dead?

No. It is a plural of ghāzin identifying participants in military movement.

Being killed is expressed by qutilū later in the verse.

A combatant may die or return alive. The active participle’s meaning should not be equated with a possible outcome of the activity.

Does Quranic ghazw mean holy war?

Ghuzzan in Āl ʿImrān 3:156 carries no such description itself.

Later historical and religious literature sometimes associates ghāzī with religious praise and fighting in God’s cause.

That evaluative, technical use is not an obligatory meaning of the root in every instance.

In the Quran, the legitimacy of war and the worth of combatants’ conduct depend on the purposes, conditions, deeds, and limits set out in relevant verses.

Ghuzzan should therefore not be rendered as “fighters in God’s cause” without supporting context.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Ghuzzan in Āl ʿImrān 3:156 denotes people who participated in an expedition.

Suitable equivalents include:

“Combatants” as a brief designation for the people.

“Participants in a military expedition” as an explanatory rendering emphasizing the movement.

“Had gone out for war” as a natural rendering of kānū ghuzzan within the full sentence.

Suggested rendering:

«إِذَا ضَرَبُوا فِي الْأَرْضِ أَوْ كَانُوا غُزًّى»

idhā ḍarabū fī al-arḍi aw kānū ghuzzan

Āl ʿImrān 3:156

“When they travelled through the land or went out for war.”

This preserves the distinction between general travel and military movement without adding aggressive intent or religious status.

For other lexical derivatives:

Ghazā: went against an enemy to fight; more generally, intended something.

Ghazw: campaigning or military movement; alternatively, intending and seeking, according to context.

Ghazwah: a military expedition or operation.

Ghāzin: a combatant or expedition participant.

Ghuzāh, ghuzzan, ghuzzāʾ: combatants or people who have gone out for war.

Maghzā: objective, intended meaning, or a statement’s purpose; militarily, an expedition’s destination.

Maghāzī: expeditions or accounts of them, according to usage.

Aghzā: sent someone to war or provided the means for departure.

Ightazā: intended or pursued something.

Mughziyah: of a woman, one whose husband has gone on campaign; of an animal, a female whose conception or delivery is delayed.

A single equivalent should not be imposed on every derivative. General seeking, military campaigning, a combatant’s designation, and speech’s intended meaning are distinct uses.

Conclusion

Arabic gh z w concerns intending and pursuing something; in its familiar military use, it denotes movement and campaigning against an enemy for confrontation and war.

Poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic tradition, including al-Aʿshā and Taʾabbaṭa Sharran, attests ghazwah for an expedition and ghuzzāʾ for its participants.

Classical lexicons also record general intention, with expressions such as maghzā al-kalām denoting speech’s purpose. The evidence does not establish either branch’s definite historical priority.

A separate recorded branch concerns difficulty conceiving or delayed delivery in certain animals; it should not be linked to warfare without evidence.

The Quran uses only ghuzzan, the plural of ghāzin in Āl ʿImrān 3:156, referring to participants in military movement.

The verse distinguishes travelling through the land from going out for war, then quotes those who confidently claimed their brothers would not have died or been killed had they stayed with them.

Ghuzzan here means combatants, not the dead, ordinary migrants, or necessarily aggressors or fighters in God’s cause.

The final definition should preserve two levels:

In general Arabic usage, gh z w concerns intending and seeking; in military usage, it denotes purposeful movement against an enemy for confrontation and war.

The Quran uses the second sense in ghuzzan. The war’s moral objective, legitimacy, and participants’ fate are not determined by the root alone; they require attention to context, conduct, and other Quranic verses.
