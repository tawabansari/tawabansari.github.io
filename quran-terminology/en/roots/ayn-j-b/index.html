1. Root Meaning and Linguistic Forms

Arabic ʿ J B concerns being astonished, finding something great or unusual, and, in some uses, liking something or being impressed by it. A person experiences ʿajab when something appears remarkable, unexpected, astonishing, or worthy of attention.

The response does not always have the same meaning or result. Someone may marvel at an unexpected event, appreciate something beautiful, be impressed by another person’s ability, or overestimate themselves and become self-admiring.

Arabic distinguishes ʿajab, ʿujb, and iʿjāb in ways important for understanding Qur’anic usage.

ʿAjab principally denotes wonder or something considered astonishing. Ethical ʿujb concerns self-admiration and an inflated view of oneself. Iʿjāb can express liking, finding something pleasing, or being impressed by it.

These meanings are connected without being exact synonyms.

A person may marvel at a garden’s beauty without becoming self-admiring. Another may be so pleased with their own ability that they overlook their limitations and mistakes. Something appears great and remarkable in both cases, but the response’s object and consequence differ.

Lisān al-ʿArab explains ʿajab as a human response to something appearing unusual because it is unfamiliar or rarely experienced.

Ibn Fāris identifies two principles for this family in Maqāyīs al-Lughah. He connects the first with regarding something as great and self-magnification, and the second with a part of animal anatomy.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Lisān al-ʿArab: two branches and vowel differences

The division shows that meanings recorded under ʿ J B need not all originate in one shared image.

Every Qur’anic occurrence concerns wonder, finding something remarkable, or admiration. The bodily sense concerning an animal’s hindmost part does not occur in the Qur’an.

Qur’anic Forms

The root occurs 27 times in 24 verses across 17 surahs.

Its Qur’anic forms fall into five categories.

ʿAjiba / yaʿjabu (عَجِبَ / يَعْجَبُ): the basic triliteral verb; eight occurrences. It concerns being astonished or finding something unexpected.

Aʿjaba / yuʿjibu (أَعْجَبَ / يُعْجِبُ): the form IV verb; eleven occurrences. Something appears pleasing or impressive to someone.

ʿAjab (عَجَب): a noun; five occurrences. Wonder or something causing wonder.

ʿAjīb (عَجِيب): an adjective; two occurrences. Astonishing or unusual.

ʿUjāb (عُجَاب): an intensive adjective; one occurrence. Very astonishing, or exceeding the ordinary degree of wonder.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: 27 occurrences in five categories

Arabic also has taʿajjaba, taʿajjub, istaʿjaba, istiʿjāb, muʿjib, muʿjab, uʿjūbah, and ʿajāʾib.

None occurs in precisely that structure in the Qur’an.

In particular, ʿujb, self-admiration, does not appear as an independent noun in the Qur’an, although some uses of aʿjaba can contextually involve self-admiration.

2. Early Arabic Evidence and Connections between Semantic Branches

Wonder at Something Unusual

Arabic ʿajiba mina al-shayʾi describes finding something astonishing or beyond one’s expectations.

The response can arise from a new experience, such as first encountering an unfamiliar phenomenon.

Not knowing the cause is nevertheless not a necessary condition of every experience of wonder.

A person may understand a phenomenon’s cause and operation while still marvelling at its scale, beauty, or grandeur.

Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī connects ʿajab with a state arising when something’s cause is unknown.

That explains some experiences of wonder, but does not explain every Qur’anic occurrence well if made a universal definition.

A farmer, for example, may be pleased and amazed by the growth of their own crop. They need not be ignorant of why plants grow; they find the growth and outcome remarkable.

A broader definition should therefore include finding something great or unusual without making ignorance an essential component.

Al-Rāghib’s Mufradāt: wonder and an unknown cause

A Poetic Witness from Aws ibn Ḥajar

Aws ibn Ḥajar, a pre-Islamic poet, is quoted as saying:

وَمُسْتَعْجِبٍ مِمَّا يَرَى مِنْ أَنَاتِنَا
وَلَوْ زَبَنَتْهُ الْحَرْبُ لَمْ يَتَرَمْرَمِ

wa-mustaʿjibin mimmā yarā min anātinā / wa-law zabanat-hu al-ḥarbu lam yataramrami

Explanatory rendering: “And many a person wonders at our forbearance; if war drove him back, he would not utter a word.” Another group’s conduct occasions the wonder, while the astonished person’s own response under war’s pressure is challenged.

Asās al-Balāghah and Maqāyīs al-Lughah: Aws ibn Ḥajar’s witness

Mustaʿjib here describes someone finding other people’s behavior unusual or astonishing.

The object of wonder is human conduct, rather than a supernatural event.

Wonder can therefore arise in response to social or ethical behavior.

Being astonished does not itself prove that the person understands that behavior correctly. Their grasp of its cause or value may be incomplete.

The witness thus distinguishes experiencing wonder from judging its object correctly.

ʿAjb: The Bodily Branch

Ibn Fāris also identifies another branch for this root.

In early bodily usage, ʿajb denotes the end of the spine or the root of an animal’s tail.

The related ʿujūb is recorded for sand dunes’ tapering ends.

Labīd ibn Rabīʿah’s poetry likewise mentions ʿujūbi anqāʾ, the narrow ends of sand heaps.

Lisān al-ʿArab: ʿajb, ʿujūb, and Labīd’s witness

This branch concerns physical shape and structure.

There is no clear evidence deriving wonder and admiration from the shape of an animal’s tailbone.

Nor should this anatomical use be conflated with the independent root ʿ J Z.

Bodily ʿajb differs from ʿajab, wonder. The vowels help distinguish the uses.

The bodily branch does not occur in the Qur’an and should not be imposed on its verses.

Did Every Meaning Originate in “Regarding as Great”?

The principal branch connects wonder, admiration, and self-admiration.

When someone marvels at something, it appears remarkable to them.

When they like it, some feature attracts their attention and pleasure.

In self-admiration, they regard their personality, abilities, or possessions as excessively great.

The semantic connection is understandable.

It does not establish that all wonder arises from self-importance or every instance of liking leads to arrogance.

The Qur’an uses this family both in censured contexts and for positive responses to beauty and plant growth.

Its ethical value consequently cannot be determined from the word alone.

3. ʿAjiba and ʿAjīb: Wonder at Truth and Unexpected Events

Wonder at a Messenger’s Coming

Awa-ʿajibtum occurs in Al-Aʿrāf 7:63 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:69.

Both concern astonishment that divine reminder and warning have reached people through a man from among them.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:63 belongs to Noah’s story; Al-Aʿrāf 7:69 to Hūd’s.

Their shared feature is finding the arrival of a divine message to a fellow human unexpected.

Yūnus 10:2 expresses the same issue with the noun ʿajaban.

Wonder at a messenger’s humanity does not itself mean calling him a liar.

Someone may be astonished by an event and accept it after examination. Alternatively, their astonishment may become a pretext for rejecting something incompatible with previous expectations.

Ṣād 38:4 and Qāf 50:2 likewise describe opponents’ astonishment that a warner has come from among them.

In those verses, wonder accompanies denial and accusation.

The astonishment itself should nevertheless remain distinct from the audience’s subsequent conduct.

ʿAjibū means “they were astonished.” Calling the Prophet a liar or magician is conveyed by other clauses.

Abraham’s Wife’s Astonishment

Hūd 11:72

قَالَتْ يَا وَيْلَتَىٰ أَأَلِدُ وَأَنَا عَجُوزٌ وَهَٰذَا بَعْلِي شَيْخًا إِنَّ هَٰذَا لَشَيْءٌ عَجِيبٌ

qālat yā waylatā a-alidu wa-anā ʿajūzun wa-hādhā baʿlī shaykhan inna hādhā la-shayʾun ʿajībun

Proposed translation in this study:

“She said: How astonishing! Shall I bear a child when I am an old woman and this husband of mine is an old man? This is indeed an astonishing thing.”

ʿAjīb here describes the announcement of childbirth.

The sentence itself explains the astonishment: both spouses are elderly, and childbirth in those circumstances exceeds ordinary expectations.

ʿAjīb nevertheless does not mean impossible.

Something may be highly unusual in human experience while remaining possible.

In Hūd 11:73, the divine messengers ask whether she is astonished at The God’s command.

The continuation places human astonishment alongside divine power and command.

Her initial response alone does not establish that she denied The God’s power.

ʿAjīb itself simply expresses how astonishing the matter appears to the speaker.

ʿUjāb: Intense Astonishment

Ṣād 38:5

أَجَعَلَ الْآلِهَةَ إِلَٰهًا وَاحِدًا إِنَّ هَٰذَا لَشَيْءٌ عُجَابٌ

a-jaʿala al-ālihata ilāhan wāḥidan inna hādhā la-shayʾun ʿujābun

Proposed translation in this study:

“Has he made the deities one deity? This is indeed a very astonishing thing.”

ʿUjāb occurs in the speech of people finding the call to divine oneness unexpected and astonishing.

Early explanations distinguish ʿajīb and ʿujāb in intensity.

ʿAjīb describes something causing wonder; ʿujāb can emphasize greater wonder beyond its ordinary degree.

Al-Muḥīṭ and Lisān al-ʿArab: ʿajīb and ʿujāb

Intensity of astonishment nevertheless differs from correctness of judgment.

The speakers marvel at monotheism, while the Qur’an presents that same divine oneness as truth.

A true matter can consequently appear very strange to someone attached to previous beliefs.

ʿUjāb itself does not mean false or impossible. Such an implication comes from the speakers’ stance and context.

Wonder at Resurrection

Al-Raʿd 13:5 places two root forms together: the verb taʿjab and the noun ʿajabun.

The verse presents the deniers’ statement about renewed creation after becoming dust as astonishing.

In Qāf 50:2 and Qāf 50:3, opponents likewise marvel at a warner coming from among them, then consider resurrection far-fetched.

An important distinction appears here.

People may marvel because something exceeds ordinary experience. Being beyond ordinary human experience does not itself establish impossibility.

Here the audience’s astonishment is connected with its beliefs about death and renewed creation.

The Qur’an, in turn, makes their statement the object of wonder.

ʿAjab can therefore occur on either side of an intellectual disagreement: one person marvels at a claim, another at its denial.

Wonder retains its lexical meaning in both, while judging truth depends on argument and context.

Wonder at Signs and Particular Events

Al-Kahf 18:9 presents the story of the People of the Cave and al-Raqīm through a question containing ʿajaban.

The verse draws attention to that story’s place among the divine signs.

ʿAjaban itself does not make the event insignificant or devoid of unusual features.

Al-Kahf 18:63 also uses ʿajaban about the fish making its way into the sea.

It expresses the event’s astonishing nature, rather than the exact movement of the fish or structure of the seawater.

In Al-Jinn 72:1, a group of jinn calls the Qur’an it has heard ʿajaban.

In Al-Jinn 72:2, the same group speaks of the Qur’an’s guidance and its own faith.

Wonder here accompanies acceptance and recognition of the message’s value.

ʿAjab is consequently not always a response of denial.

In Al-Najm 53:59, the audience marvels at this discourse; Al-Najm 53:60 contrasts its laughter with its failure to weep.

Wonder there accompanies a censured response, but the word itself means neither laughing nor mocking.

Bal ʿAjibta and Variant Readings

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:12 — Ḥafṣ’s reading

بَلْ عَجِبْتَ وَيَسْخَرُونَ

bal ʿajibta wa-yaskharūna

Proposed translation in this study:

“Rather, you were astonished, while they mock.”

Ḥafṣ reads ʿajibta with a on the t, addressing the Prophet.

Ḥamzah and Al-Kisāʾī instead read ʿajibtu, with u on the t.

That is first-person singular; within the divine speech, astonishment is attributed to The God.

The variant changes the verb’s grammatical subject and should not be ignored.

It also has an important semantic consequence.

In human experience, wonder may arise from an unknown cause or unexpected event.

The Qur’an, however, presents The God as all-knowing. In the reading attributing the verb to Him, it cannot mean ignorance or suddenly discovering a previously unknown truth.

The semantic field of regarding something as great or remarkable can help examine the attribution.

Determining its precise character nevertheless belongs to a discussion separate from the root’s lexical meaning.

Al-Ṭabarī: two recognized readings and regarding something as great

Both readings retain wa-yaskharūna, describing the other party’s mockery.

Wonder and mockery consequently remain distinct responses even when placed together in one sentence.

4. Aʿjaba: Liking, Attraction, and the Limits of Judging by Appearances

Form IV aʿjaba can express something appearing pleasing or impressive to someone.

An external object or one of its features attracts a person’s attention and liking.

Liking nevertheless differs from complete knowledge and sound judgment.

Someone may enjoy another person’s speech although it conflicts with the speaker’s actual conduct.

Likewise, large numbers, wealth, or outward beauty may impress someone without guaranteeing the value or result they expect.

Al-Baqarah 2:204: Pleasing Speech and Actual Conduct

Al-Baqarah 2:204

وَمِنَ النَّاسِ مَنْ يُعْجِبُكَ قَوْلُهُ فِي الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا وَيُشْهِدُ اللَّهَ عَلَىٰ مَا فِي قَلْبِهِ وَهُوَ أَلَدُّ الْخِصَامِ

wa-mina al-nāsi man yuʿjibuka qawluhu fī al-ḥayāti al-dunyā wa-yushhidu allāha ʿalā mā fī qalbihi wa-huwa aladdu al-khiṣāmi

Proposed translation in this study:

“Among people is one whose speech about worldly life pleases you, and he calls The God to witness what is in his heart, while he is the fiercest disputant.”

Yuʿjibuka is form IV, with qawluhu, his speech, as its subject.

What impresses the audience is therefore the person’s speech.

The verse goes beyond speech’s effect: the person calls The God to witness his inner state while being presented as fiercely contentious in reality.

Al-Baqarah 2:205 describes his corrupt conduct and damage to al-ḥartha wa-l-nasla. Al-Baqarah 2:206 describes his response when called to mindfulness.

Together, the verses portray a gap between outward speech, conduct’s consequences, and the person’s response to correction.

Iʿjāb is the speech’s initial effect on the audience in this context.

Pleasing speech does not establish a speaker’s truthfulness or trustworthiness.

This does not make every beautiful, effective speech deceptive: words can be both truthful and pleasing.

The point is that attractive expression does not replace examining conduct and actual consequences.

Al-Baqarah 2:221: Personal Liking and Other Criteria

Al-Baqarah 2:221 has both aʿjabatkum and aʿjabakum.

The first concerns a woman, the second a man.

Both concern a person being pleasing to the addressees.

The verb itself does not specify why.

Beauty, wealth, social standing, personality, or another feature might account for the liking.

Translating them as “although their beauty attracts you” without independent evidence therefore restricts their meaning.

The context distinguishes personal liking from the religious criterion presented by the verse.

That criterion and ruling emerge from the full verse, rather than from aʿjaba itself.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:52: Beauty and Liking

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:52, ḥusnuhunna, their beauty, is identified as the object occasioning admiration.

This differs importantly from Al-Baqarah 2:221.

Al-Baqarah does not specifically name the reason for liking; Al-Aḥzāb explicitly identifies beauty.

Form IV of ʿ J B can express liking in both, while the object and reason come from accompanying words.

The distinction is essential for accurate translation.

Al-Munāfiqūn 63:4: Bodies and Speech

Al-Munāfiqūn 63:4 describes certain hypocrites’ bodily appearance impressing the addressee, with their speech also attracting a listener’s attention.

The continuation nevertheless portrays their actual condition differently.

This clearly connects with Al-Baqarah 2:204.

In both, appearances can attract without ensuring complete understanding of the person’s reality.

The verses do not establish that good-looking, strong, or eloquent people are inherently dishonest.

They concern particular groups whose conduct the contexts describe.

Abundance and Value

In Al-Māʾidah 5:100, the abundance of al-khabīth may impress someone.

The verse nevertheless distinguishes khabīth and ṭayyib, showing that abundance alone does not determine purity or value.

Al-Tawbah 9:55 and Al-Tawbah 9:85 likewise warn against judging certain people wrongly from their apparent abundance of wealth and children.

Admiration arises from the appearance of abundance and resources in these uses.

The root itself does not mean abundance or intrinsically censure material possessions.

The shared issue is the limitation of judging through outward attraction.

5. Self-Admiration, Conceit, and Positive Appreciation

Al-Tawbah 9:25: Pleasure in Large Numbers

Al-Tawbah 9:25

لَقَدْ نَصَرَكُمُ اللَّهُ فِي مَوَاطِنَ كَثِيرَةٍ وَيَوْمَ حُنَيْنٍ إِذْ أَعْجَبَتْكُمْ كَثْرَتُكُمْ فَلَمْ تُغْنِ عَنْكُمْ شَيْئًا وَضَاقَتْ عَلَيْكُمُ الْأَرْضُ بِمَا رَحُبَتْ ثُمَّ وَلَّيْتُمْ مُدْبِرِينَ

la-qad naṣarakumu allāhu fī mawāṭina kathīratin wa-yawma ḥunaynin idh aʿjabatkum kathratukum fa-lam tughni ʿankum shayʾan wa-ḍāqat ʿalaykumu al-arḍu bi-mā raḥubat thumma wallaytum mudbirīna

Proposed translation in this study:

“The God has indeed helped you in many places, and on the day of Ḥunayn, when your great numbers pleased you but availed you nothing; the earth, despite its breadth, became narrow for you, then you turned back retreating.”

Kathratukum, your abundance, is what occasions admiration.

The group’s large numbers appeared pleasing and reassuring to its members.

The continuation shows that those numbers failed to deliver the expected result in that situation.

Admiration is connected here with excessive confidence in collective strength.

Aʿjabatkum itself nevertheless means neither being defeated nor fleeing.

Pleasure in large numbers is one response; their ineffectiveness and the retreat are separate events subsequently described.

Nor does the verse establish that large numbers are harmful in every circumstance.

The issue is their insufficiency as a guarantee of success.

Distinguishing ʿUjb from Kibr

Ethical ʿujb concerns self-admiration: being so pleased with one’s ability, possessions, knowledge, or conduct that one overestimates oneself.

Kibr likewise concerns self-importance, but often emphasizes superiority, rejecting truth, or magnifying oneself in relation to others.

The states may coexist without being identical.

Someone may inwardly admire themselves excessively before showing it openly toward others.

Arrogance can instead become evident in treatment of others and rejection of truth.

This should not become an absolute definition making every instance of ʿujb purely inward and every instance of kibr purely outward.

Recognizing their linguistic difference nevertheless prevents automatically calling every instance of admiration or liking arrogance.

Positive Appreciation of Plant Growth

Al-Fatḥ 48:29 describes a crop grown strong and upright that pleases the farmers.

Yuʿjibu concerns the observers’ positive response here.

The farmer takes pleasure in the growth and strength of a crop they have worked for.

That pleasure need not be self-admiration or conceit.

Al-Ḥadīd 57:20 likewise describes plant growth after rain occasioning admiration.

Al-kuffār occurs within an agricultural image there; one early meaning concerns farmers covering seed in the soil.

“Farmers” consequently has lexical support in this context.

Al-Ṭabarī: farmers and the transience of growth

The verse continues with the crop drying, yellowing, and becoming debris.

The initial beauty and pleasure thus accompany the condition’s impermanence.

Aʿjaba here nevertheless does not mean deceiving or perishing.

Appreciating and marvelling at growth is one matter; its drying and ending is another.

The two verses show admiration as a possible natural, positive response to beauty, growth, and effort’s results.

The root therefore cannot be restricted to arrogance, self-deception, or denial.

6. Distinctions from Related Words

ʿ J B overlaps with several other linguistic families without being interchangeable with them.

D H SH: dahshah primarily concerns intense bewilderment and agitation at an unexpected event. Wonder can produce this, but not every experience of wonder entails distress or loss of composure.

Ḥ Y R: ḥayrah concerns being perplexed or unable to find a route or answer. Someone can marvel without being perplexed at all.

GH R R: some Qur’anic uses of ghurūr concern deception and attachment to an incorrect impression. Admiration may facilitate that deception, but liking itself does not mean being deceived.

K B R: kibr concerns greatness and, ethically, self-importance. The self-admiring branch of ʿ J B approaches it, but marvelling at a phenomenon is not itself arrogance.

Study of K B R

Ḥ S N: ḥusn concerns beauty and goodness; admiration can be a response to it. Al-Aḥzāb 33:52 clearly shows the relationship. Beauty characterizes the object; liking is the observer’s response.

Study of Ḥ S N

R Ḍ W: riḍā concerns satisfaction and acceptance. Admiration may accompany it, but someone can be astonished at something without liking it.

Lexical sources: riḍā and R Ḍ W

Study of R Ḍ W

ʿ J Z: this root concerns inability and, in other uses, the rear part of a body. Muʿjiz belongs to it, rather than to ʿ J B. Iʿjāb and iʿjāz are therefore etymologically distinct, although modern Persian sometimes discusses them in related subject areas.

Wonder, beauty, liking, satisfaction, conceit, and inability are consequently distinct concepts.

Several may coexist in one event, but their coexistence does not justify equating word meanings.

7. Conclusions and Translation Proposals

Arabic ʿ J B expresses astonishment, finding something great or unusual, and, in some uses, finding it pleasing or impressive.

Another branch concerns self-admiration and regarding oneself as great.

Their ethical values nevertheless differ.

The Qur’an describes astonishment at childbirth announced in old age, a messenger coming from among his people, farmers enjoying plant growth, and a group’s large numbers producing admiration and misplaced confidence.

Wonder can consequently accompany knowledge, acceptance, denial, liking, or conceit, without any of those outcomes becoming a constant component of every derivative’s meaning.

The Qur’an contains five morphological categories; each meaning requires its own structure and context.

Brief root meaning: Being astonished or finding something great or unusual; in other uses, appearing pleasing or impressive, and, in the self-admiring branch, regarding oneself as great.

Meanings of the Qur’anic Forms

ʿAjiba / yaʿjabu: being astonished or finding something unexpected.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:63 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:69 concern wonder at divine reminder reaching a human from among the people.

Hūd 11:73 presents Abraham’s wife’s astonishment at The God’s command.

Al-Raʿd 13:5 concerns astonishment at those denying renewed creation.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:12 requires considering the variant readings and their subjects when examining the verb.

Ṣād 38:4 and Qāf 50:2 describe opponents’ wonder at a warner’s coming.

In Al-Najm 53:59, the audience likewise marvels at this discourse.

General translation proposals: “to be astonished” or “to marvel.”

Aʿjaba / yuʿjibu: something appearing pleasing, attractive, or impressive to someone.

In Al-Baqarah 2:204, a person’s speech pleases the addressee.

In Al-Baqarah 2:221, a man or woman may please the addressees.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:100, the abundance of khabīth may attract attention.

In Al-Tawbah 9:25, the group’s large numbers occasion admiration.

Al-Tawbah 9:55 and Al-Tawbah 9:85 concern wealth and children.

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:52, women’s beauty occasions admiration.

In Al-Fatḥ 48:29 and Al-Ḥadīd 57:20, plant growth pleases and amazes observers.

In Al-Munāfiqūn 63:4, people’s bodily appearance attracts the addressee’s attention and liking.

General translation proposals: “to please,” “to appear pleasing,” or “to impress.”

ʿAjab: wonder or something considered astonishing.

It occurs in Yūnus 10:2, Al-Raʿd 13:5, Al-Kahf 18:9, Al-Kahf 18:63, and Al-Jinn 72:1.

Proposed translations: “wonder,” “an astonishing thing,” or, where the construction suits, “in an astonishing way.”

ʿAjīb: something astonishing or unusual.

It occurs in Hūd 11:72 and Qāf 50:2.

Proposed translation: “astonishing.”

ʿUjāb: very astonishing, emphasizing greater intensity of wonder.

It occurs only in Ṣād 38:5.

Proposed translation: “very astonishing.”

Final Finding

The defensible core of Qur’anic ʿ J B is finding something remarkable, impressive, or unusual; uses concerning human beings also involve their response to that object.

The response can appear as wonder, liking, or regarding something as great.

What occasions admiration need not necessarily be true, false, good, bad, lasting, or deceptive.

A truth may astonish deniers, attractive speech may obscure its speaker’s actual conduct, and plant growth may rightly please a farmer.

The word ʿajab itself therefore cannot settle its object’s value.

Some verses warn against being impressed by appearance and abundance; others present admiration as a natural response to beauty and growth.

The necessary distinctions are something’s astonishing nature, liking it, its actual reality, and the value a person consequently assigns to it.

These distinctions preserve the family’s differing meanings while preventing natural human wonder from being equated with self-admiration and conceit.
