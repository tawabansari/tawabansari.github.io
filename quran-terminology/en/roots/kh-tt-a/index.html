1. Short Introduction to the Root

The root خ ط أ moves in the field of slipping from the right path, missing the mark, going wrong, and at times knowingly deviating from truth. This root is especially important in Qur'anic usage because the Qur'an distinguishes between an unintended mistake and a morally aware fault, and much of that distinction is carried through morphology and context.

In the oldest material image of the root, the scene is a step that slips from the path, or a traveler who places a foot wrongly on uneven ground. The same image then extends into archery and hitting the mark: an arrow goes out from the line of aim and does not land on the target.

In the Qur'an, this root is used both for faults that can be forgiven and are not deliberate, and for sins that arise from awareness, insistence, and moral deviation. Therefore, the simple English words “mistake” or “sin” are not enough for every occurrence. One must see which form appears, in what context it appears, and whether the verse speaks of an unintended slip or a conscious deviation.

Short meaning: slipping from the right path, missing the mark, making a mistake; and in some forms, conscious sin and deliberate deviation from truth.

2. Important Forms and Derivatives of the Root

Important forms of this root in the Qur'an include خَطِئَ, خَاطِئ, خَاطِئُون, خَاطِئِينَ, خَاطِئَة, خَطِيئَة, خَطَايَا, خَطَأً, أَخْطَأَ, and أَخْطَأْنَا.

خَطِئَ / خاطئ

This branch is usually connected with a fault, sin, or moral deviation for which the agent is responsible before truth.

خَطِيئَة / خَطَايَا

These forms point to faults and sins that carry moral weight and require forgiveness, covering, or release from their effect.

خَطَأً

This form appears in the verse of unintended killing and indicates an event that occurred without intent. The error is legally important because it is not the same as deliberate killing, yet it is not without consequence.

أَخْطَأَ / أَخْطَأْنَا

The form IV verb usually points to going wrong, making an error, or failing to reach the intended point without direct intent. In the final supplication of Al-Baqarah, the human being asks the Lord not to take him to account if he forgot or erred.

4. The Semantic Core of the Root

The semantic core of خ ط أ may be stated as: leaving the right path or failing to reach the mark that should have been reached. This leaving may be unintended and accidental, or it may be conscious, moral, and sinful.

At the material level, a person is moving on a path, but his foot slips or turns aside. Or an archer shoots, but the arrow does not land on the mark. In both images, there is a right path or target, but the movement does not reach it.

The Qur'an transfers this image into human moral and legal life. The human being may go wrong in understanding, will, action, judgment, or social responsibility. Sometimes this happens through forgetfulness, mistake, heedlessness, or lack of intent; at other times it happens through insistence, lying, denial, tyranny, or conscious choice.

5. The Material Image of the Root

The material image of the root in Semitic usage is connected with walking and slipping from the road. A person moves along a path, but the foot slips on uneven ground, or a step carries him away from the way. The image then extends in Arabic to the scene of archery: an arrow deviates from its intended line and misses the target.

For this reason, khata' is not merely “not knowing.” It means that a gap has opened between movement and target, or between claim and truth. If that gap is unintended, the Qur'an marks it with forms such as خَطَأً and أَخْطَأْنَا. If the gap becomes moral and conscious deviation, forms such as خاطئ and خطيئة enter the field.

6. Early Arabic Usage and Qur'anic Precision

In early Arabic, an important distinction is reported between خَطِئَ and أَخْطَأَ. In eloquent usage, خَطِئَ is closer to sin and morally charged deviation, while أَخْطَأَ is closer to going wrong or making an unintended mistake.

Yet this distinction should not be made absolute for the whole Arabic language in a mechanical way. Early Arabic contains some overlapping usages. The more important point is that the Qur'an arranges this field with striking precision: simple-stem derivatives such as خاطئ and خطيئة occur in contexts of moral fault and sin, while form IV and خَطَأً are prominent in contexts of unintended error.

7. Translation Challenges and Semantic Reduction

One challenge in translating this root is that English can use “mistake,” “fault,” “error,” “sin,” or “wrongdoing,” but none of these should be forced across all occurrences. Persian often uses “خطا” for both fields, which can blur the distinction between unintended error and conscious sin.

In Forqan translation, this distinction should be preserved as much as possible. خَطَأً in unintended killing should not be translated as if it were deliberate sin. At the same time, خاطئين or خاطئة should not be reduced to a simple harmless mistake.

8. Methodological Note

Three layers must be separated when studying this root: the material image, the morphological structure, and the Qur'anic context. The material image is slipping from the path or missing the mark. The morphology shows the difference between the simple stem and form IV. The Qur'anic context clarifies whether the verse speaks of unintended error or conscious moral deviation.

If one settles for the general meaning “mistake,” much of the Qur'an's precision is lost. If one makes the morphological distinction too absolute and ignores context, the reading again becomes simplified. The proper method is to read form and context together.

9. Qur'anic Uses of the Root

The Qur'an uses this root in several important fields: forgiveness of faults, conscious sin, unintended killing, supplication for not being taken to account for error, and the description of the center of lying and deviation in a human being.

وَقُولُوا حِطَّةٌ نَّغْفِرْ لَكُمْ خَطَايَاكُمْ

And say: a lowering of the burden of fault; We will forgive your faults.

Al-Baqarah 2:58

وَمَا كَانَ لِمُؤْمِنٍ أَن يَقْتُلَ مُؤْمِنًا إِلَّا خَطَأً

It is not for a believer to kill a believer, except by error.

An-Nisa 4:92

رَبَّنَا لَا تُؤَاخِذْنَا إِن نَّسِينَا أَوْ أَخْطَأْنَا

Our Lord, do not take us to account if we forget or err.

Al-Baqarah 2:286

This verse shows that unintended error may still stand in the field of responsibility, especially if its causes involved heedlessness or negligence; yet the human being takes refuge in the mercy of The God from being taken to account for such error.

10. Examination of Important Qur'anic Derivatives

خَطَايَا

In Al-Baqarah 2:58, faults appear in the context of forgiveness. They have weight and effect, yet the door of return and forgiveness remains open.

خَطَأً

In An-Nisa 4:92, خَطَأً functions as a circumstantial qualifier or in an exceptive structure that restricts the legal character of the killing. It is not a cognate accusative of “killing,” because it does not share the root of قتل. The verse speaks of unintended killing, not deliberate killing.

أَخْطَأْنَا

In Al-Baqarah 2:286, أخطأنا appears in supplication. The human being admits that he may err even without intending sin, and asks the Lord not to take him to account for that error.

خَاطِئَة

نَاصِيَةٍ كَاذِبَةٍ خَاطِئَةٍ

A lying, faulting forelock.

Al-'Alaq 96:16

Attributing خاطئة to the forelock is not merely a report of a bodily act. In the Qur'anic image, the forelock is connected with the forehead and the center of command and decision. Describing it as lying and faulting indicates that the very center of decision and calculation has deviated from truth.

خَاطِئِينَ

تَاللَّهِ لَقَدْ آثَرَكَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْنَا وَإِن كُنَّا لَخَاطِئِينَ

By The God, The God has preferred you over us, and we were surely faulting ones.

Yusuf 12:91

Here the fault is not a simple mental mistake. It is a confession of the wrong path and injustice they had taken against Yusuf.

11. KH TT A Compared with S W B

One of the most important opposite roots to خ ط أ is ص و ب. In Arabic, the one who reaches the target and hits the mark stands in the field of isabah and sawab. Khata' means missing the mark; sawab means reaching the right mark.

This contrast helps show that khata' in the Qur'an is not merely a mental mistake. There is a truth, path, or target. If the human being does not reach it or deviates from it, he enters the field of error. If his speech, judgment, or action lands upon truth, it belongs to the field of sawab and isabah.

12. Error, Killing, and Legal Responsibility

An-Nisa 4:92 shows that unintended error is not without effect in the Qur'an. Killing by error is not the same as deliberate killing, but because a human life has been lost, it carries legal and social responsibility. The Qur'an regulates this responsibility through compensation and the freeing of a believing slave.

Therefore, خَطَأً in this verse should not be confused with deliberate sin, but it should not be understood as complete absence of responsibility either. An error may occur without direct intent, while its consequences still remain in the real world and must be repaired and regulated.

13. Grammatical and Structural Notes

In إِلَّا خَطَأً, the word خَطَأً is accusative. It may be understood as a circumstantial qualifier or within an exceptive structure that restricts the event. The key point is that it is not a cognate accusative of the verb “to kill,” since it is not built from the root ق ت ل.

In نَاصِيَةٍ كَاذِبَةٍ خَاطِئَةٍ, two attributes describe the forelock: lying and faulting. The combination shows that the error here is joined to lying and conscious deviation, not to a simple harmless mistake.

In أَوْ أَخْطَأْنَا, form IV appears beside forgetting. This pairing shows that the verse speaks of states in which the human being is not claiming direct intent to sin, but seeks pardon from weakness, slipping, heedlessness, or mistake.

14. Differences from Nearby Roots

خ ط أ is not the same as ذ ن ب. Dhanb points to the trailing consequence and burden of sin; khata' points to slipping from path or target.

خ ط أ is also different from إ ث م. Ithm is closer to the heavy burden of sin and its restraining effect, while khata' begins from the image of slipping and missing the mark.

خ ط أ is not identical with س و أ. Su' marks badness and ugliness of act or result, while khata' highlights the relation of movement to the right path or target.

خ ط أ stands in direct contrast with ص و ب. Sawab and isabah mean reaching the right target; khata' means missing it or slipping from the path.

15. Translation Suggestions for Forqan

For خَطَايَاكُمْ: your faults; or your heavy faults, if the context emphasizes moral weight.

For خَطَأً in killing: by error; unintentionally; without deliberate intent.

For أَخْطَأْنَا: we erred; we went wrong; we slipped from the right path.

For خَاطِئِينَ: faulting ones; those who have fallen into a path of fault and sin; not merely people who made a harmless mental mistake.

For خَاطِئَة in نَاصِيَةٍ كَاذِبَةٍ خَاطِئَةٍ: faulting, deviant; or in explanation: a center of decision deviated from truth.

16. Conclusion

The root خ ط أ in the Qur'an begins from the image of slipping from a path and missing the mark, then expands into moral, legal, and supplicatory responsibility. The Qur'an distinguishes unintended error from conscious deviation and shows this through both context and morphology.

Form IV and expressions such as أخطأنا and خَطَأً are closer to going wrong, unintended error, and lack of deliberate intent. Forms such as خاطئ, خاطئين, and خطيئة carry moral and sinful weight.

This root stands opposite to sawab and isabah. Sawab means landing on the right mark; khata' means failing to reach that mark or leaving the road that should have been taken. In the Qur'anic logic, the human being is responsible for conscious sin, and in unintended errors he still needs mercy, pardon, and repair.
