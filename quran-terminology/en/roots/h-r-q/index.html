The Arabic root ح ر ق concerns burning, setting things alight, and the effect of fire. أَحْرَقَ الشَّيْءَ means burning something, اِحْتَرَقَ means that something burned, and حَرِيق concerns fire, flame, and its burning force.

The root extends beyond fire. Early Arabic also records حَرَقَ الشَّيْءَ for abrading or filing something. حَرَقَ نَابَهُ denotes grinding the teeth, particularly in anger. حُرْقَة can express physical burning or the searing feeling of grief, attachment, and pain.

The uses share an image: fire burns and transforms something; friction can produce heat, wear, and harm; and intense pain may feel like burning.

Yet these similarities do not establish a single, certain historical development for every meaning. Lexicons record more than one branch, and some explanations of origins remain analyses or possibilities.

The root occurs nine times in nine Qur’anic verses: three Form II verbs, one Form VIII verb, and five occurrences of الْحَرِيق.

In Abraham’s story, his opponents command his burning. In Moses’ story, the calf is to be destroyed and its remains scattered. Al-Baqarah 2:266 presents a garden burning in a parable. Five other verses use عذاب الحريق for burning punishment.

The uses should be distinguished. Burning something, something burning, and naming a punishment through ḥarīq are three different constructions, though all relate to burning and fire.

1. Forms and derivatives

The root ح ر ق consists of ḥāʾ, rāʾ, and qāf. It is a sound, regular triliteral Arabic root.

Important forms in the family include:

  • حَرَقَ ـ يَحْرُقُ / يَحْرِقُ: In one early use, abraded or filed something; in fire-related uses, burned it.
  • حَرَق: Fire, burning force, and its effect; in another use, damage or a hole in cloth from rubbing and beating.
  • حَرْق: Abrading and filing; also the verbal noun of some burning uses.
  • أَحْرَقَ ـ يُحْرِقُ: Burned something and exposed it to fire’s effect.
  • إِحْرَاق: Burning or setting something alight.
  • حَرَّقَ ـ يُحَرِّقُ: Burned; sometimes emphasizing intensity, repetition, or extent.
  • تَحْرِيق: Burning and setting alight.
  • اِحْتَرَقَ ـ يَحْتَرِقُ: Burned; suffered burning or destruction through fire.
  • اِحْتِرَاق: Burning; in modern scientific terminology, combustion.
  • تَحَرَّقَ ـ يَتَحَرَّقُ: Flamed up or burned; in other uses, felt searing grief, longing, or pain.
  • حَرِيق: Fire, burning, and a searing flame; in some uses, describing something that burns.
  • حُرْقَة: A burning sensation and the effect of heat; also searing physical or inner pain.
  • حَرِق: Something damaged, shortened, or worn by fire or another cause.
  • حَارِق: Burning or causing burning; in some uses, fire or something with a searing effect.
  • مَحْرُوق: Burned, or something that has been burned.
  • حُرَاق، حُرَّاق و حَرُوق: In early uses, tinder or something readily affected by fire.
  • حُرَاقَة: Material that catches a spark during firemaking.
  • حَرَّاقَة: Historically, a kind of ship or engine used to project incendiary substances.
  • حِرَاق / حُرَاق: Of fire, consuming and sparing nothing; figuratively, a destructive person or intense effect. These vocalizations should not be confused with حَرَّاقَة or tinder forms.
  • حُرَاق: Also used for very salty water, whose salinity produces a burning sensation.
  • حَارِقَة: In another lexical branch, a sinew in the thigh and hip region.
  • حَرِيقُ النَّاب: The sound of teeth grinding, particularly in anger.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an.

The actual Qur’anic forms are:

Qur’anic formCountVerses
حَرِّقُوهُ2Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:68; Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:24
لَنُحَرِّقَنَّهُ1Ṭā Hā 20:97
فَاحْتَرَقَتْ1Al-Baqarah 2:266

حَرِّقُوهُ and لَنُحَرِّقَنَّهُ are Form II verbs, but differ in person, tense, and mode of address.

حَرِّقُوهُ is a plural imperative: “Burn him.”

لَنُحَرِّقَنَّهُ is a first-person plural imperfect with an emphatic lām and nūn: “We will certainly burn it.”

فَاحْتَرَقَتْ is a Form VIII perfect, third-person feminine singular: “Then it burned.”

The five occurrences of الْحَرِيق are the same noun, all in عذاب الحريق. The inflection of عذاب follows its syntactic position: accusative in four instances and nominative in Al-Burūj 85:10.

Different verse numbers, or an attached pronoun or preposition, do not themselves create independent derivatives.

2. Semantic centers and early Arabic witnesses

Two branches in Maqāyīs al-Lughah

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies two bases for ح ر ق.

The first is rubbing one thing against another with heat and burning intensity.

The second concerns a part of the body.

He explains the first with:

حَكُّ الشَّيْءِ بِالشَّيْءِ مَعَ حَرَارَةٍ وَالْتِهَابٍ

ḥakku al-shayʾi bi-al-shayʾi maʿa ḥarāratin wa-iltihābin

That is: “Rubbing one thing against another, with heat and burning intensity.”

This differs interestingly from the common modern definition: many Persian and Arabic speakers today first associate ḥarq with fire and burning.

Ibn Fāris, however, begins with abrasion and heat in explaining the relationship between meanings.

In physical terms, intense friction can generate heat and, under suitable conditions, ignition.

But this natural relationship should not immediately be treated as the word’s certain history.

Burning and abrasion may have coexisted in Arabic from very early times without the historical priority of either being established.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: rubbing with heat and the separate bodily branch

Burning and the effect of fire

In Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Jawharī relates الحَرَق to fire and الحَرِيق to flame and burning.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise records أَحْرَقَهُ بِالنَّارِ and حَرَّقَهُ for burning something.

This is the root’s most familiar branch.

When something burns, fire or searing heat can change its structure and condition.

Wood may become ash, vegetation may perish, or cloth may be damaged.

But burning does not always mean complete conversion to ash.

Something may lose part of itself to fire or be damaged enough to lose its former function.

Ordinary iḥtirāq can therefore range from partial burning to extensive destruction.

The sentence and context establish the extent of destruction.

أَحْرَقَ, حَرَّقَ, and اِحْتَرَقَ

Arabic أَحْرَقَ is transitive: someone or something causes another thing to burn.

حَرَّقَ can convey the same meaning, though Form II sometimes emphasizes intensity, repetition, or extent.

Al-Jawharī says that doubling in حَرَّقَهُ can express multiplicity.

This should not become an absolute rule that حَرَّقَ always means burning repeatedly while أَحْرَقَ means burning only once.

In many uses the verbs have close basic meanings.

اِحْتَرَقَ differs from both.

Here the thing that burns is the grammatical subject.

For example, احْتَرَقَ الْبُسْتَانُ means “The garden burned.”

This alone does not identify who started the fire.

Causing another to burn should therefore be distinguished from something burning.

The Qur’an makes this clear: حَرِّقُوهُ commands burning another, while فَاحْتَرَقَتْ reports the garden burning.

Abrading, filing, and wearing away

Early Arabic also records حَرَقَ الشَّيْءَ for abrading something with a file or similar implement.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ explains it through بَرَدَهُ وَحَكَّهُ: filing and rubbing it down.

No flame is necessary in this use.

Metal may be scraped or reduced with an abrasive implement.

This matters in Ṭā Hā 20:97, where an early reading variant and lexical explanation of لَنُحَرِّقَنَّهُ concern abrading and filing.

But this branch should not lead the familiar, clear meaning of burning to be denied elsewhere.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:68 and Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:24 clearly contain evidence of fire and burning.

Grinding the teeth in anger

Early Arabic حَرَقَ نَابَهُ means grinding one’s teeth.

يَحْرُقُ عَلَيْكَ الْأُرَّمَ likewise describes someone grinding their teeth in anger against another.

الأُرَّم is explained lexically as teeth.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Lisān al-ʿArab transmit:

نُبِّئْتُ أَحْمَاءَ سُلَيْمَى إِنَّمَا
بَاتُوا غِضَابًا يَحْرُقُونَ الْأُرَّمَا

nubbiʾtu aḥmāʾa sulaymā innamā / bātū ghiḍāban yaḥruqūna al-urramā

That is: “I was told that Sulaymā’s relatives by marriage spent the night angry, grinding their teeth in rage.”

يَحْرُقُونَ here does not mean setting alight.

Nobody burns their mouth or teeth; they press and grind the teeth together in anger.

This attests abrasion in early Arabic.

Sources vary in the opening half and in بَاتُوا, and the poet is not securely identified.

It is therefore better described as an early rajaz witness of unknown authorship than as poetry securely attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Lisān al-ʿArab: the tooth-grinding witness and physical uses

الحَرِيق: flame and burning

Arabic حَرِيق concerns flame, fire, and burning.

Lisān al-ʿArab records الحَرَق and الحَرِيق for fire blazing up.

In some uses, ḥarīq concerns the effect or process of burning; in others, burning fire itself is prominent.

The word occurs five times in the Qur’an in عذاب الحريق, with the inflection appropriate to each sentence.

Lexically, it can express burning and fire’s searing force in that construction.

But the word alone does not specify combustible material, temperature, or the precise nature of punishment.

Such details cannot be extracted from the root alone.

A poetic witness concerning burning fire

Lisān al-ʿArab attributes this rajaz to Ghaylān al-Ribʿī:

يُثِرْنَ مِنْ أَكْدَرِهَا بِالدَّقْعَاءِ
مُنْتَصِبًا مِثْلَ حَرِيقِ الْقَصْبَاءِ

yuthirna min akdarihā bi-al-daqʿāʾi / muntaṣiban mithla ḥarīqi al-qaṣbāʾi

That is: “They raise from its dark earth a column of dust, rising like reeds ablaze.”

The poet compares rising dust with tall flames from burning reeds.

حَرِيقِ الْقَصْبَاءِ concerns reeds burning and flaming up.

حريق here therefore conveys more than heat: it creates an image of rising flame.

The witness helps establish the word’s physical use in Arabic.

The attribution to Ghaylān al-Ribʿī is recorded in lexicons, but the precise period of his life and the date of this rajaz are not securely established here. The transmission alone does not justify presenting it as certainly early Islamic or pre-Islamic.

Majāz al-Qurʾān, editorial note on Ghaylān al-Ribʿī: uncertain biography

Burning sensations in the body and human feeling

Arabic حُرْقَة is also used for a burning sensation.

An eye may burn through illness, skin may be harmed by heat, or a taste may feel searing.

Lisān al-ʿArab records حرقة for eye pain and burning, grief, affection, and an acrid taste.

Actual fire is not always present.

For example, saying grief burns the heart ordinarily describes intense inner pain and pressure through burning imagery, rather than the physical heart catching fire.

Persian has a comparable semantic extension.

The resemblance between Persian and Arabic does not itself establish historical origin; it helps explain the image connecting physical burning and intense pain.

حُرَاق and tinder

Early Arabic records حُرَاق and related forms for materials used to kindle fire.

For example, dry cloth or vegetation could catch a spark and help establish a fire.

This use belongs to everyday life when firemaking required suitable implements and readily ignitable materials.

The family also includes حَرَّاقَة, used in later periods for certain engines or warships equipped with incendiary devices.

These meanings do not occur in the Qur’an.

حَرِّقُوهُ and الْحَرِيق should therefore not be restricted to firemaking implements or later military technology.

حَارِقَة: a bodily sinew

Ibn Fāris also identifies an independent second branch for ح ر ق.

Here الحَارِقَة names a sinew in the hip and thigh region.

Some early anatomical explanations relate it to the connection between the head of the thighbone and the hip joint.

Injury to it could impair movement.

Accordingly, مَحْرُوق in one early use describes someone whose corresponding body part has been injured.

This should not be equated with injury from fire.

Ibn Fāris likewise treats it as a separate branch.

Early anatomical explanations do not necessarily match today’s precise ligament and tendon terminology. It is preferable to report the lexical description without substituting a modern anatomical name lacking firm evidence.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: tinder, salty water, and ḥāriqah

3. Burning in Abraham’s story

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:68: the command to burn

Arabic verse:

قَالُوا حَرِّقُوهُ وَانْصُرُوا آلِهَتَكُمْ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ فَاعِلِينَ

qālū ḥarriqūhu wa-unṣurū ālihatakum in kuntum fāʿilīna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“They said: Burn him and support your deities, if you intend to act.”

حَرِّقُوهُ is a plural Form II imperative.

The pronoun هُ refers to Abraham.

The speakers are opponents who, after confronting Abraham’s action and reasoning concerning their deities, command his burning.

Lexically, حَرِّقُوهُ means burn him.

Fire and burning are clear here.

The following verse, Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:69, describes God commanding the fire to be coolness and safety for Abraham.

حَرِّقُوهُ therefore cannot mean merely reprimand him or oppose him in this context.

Fire and the threat of actual burning are involved.

حَرِّقُوهُ and وَانْصُرُوا آلِهَتَكُمْ

Two commands appear in one sentence.

First, burning Abraham.

Second, supporting their deities.

This matters: the opponents present violence against Abraham as a means of defending their deities.

But حَرِّقُوهُ itself does not mean defending deities.

It means burning; the speakers’ motive is known from another part of the sentence.

The verse also reports Abraham’s opponents’ speech, rather than a divine command to burn a person.

An imperative’s presence in the Qur’an therefore does not itself authorize acting on it.

The speaker and the Qur’an’s stance toward the command must be examined.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:24: killing or burning

Arabic verse:

فَمَا كَانَ جَوَابَ قَوْمِهِ إِلَّا أَنْ قَالُوا اقْتُلُوهُ أَوْ حَرِّقُوهُ فَأَنْجَاهُ اللَّهُ مِنَ النَّارِ إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَآيَاتٍ لِقَوْمٍ يُؤْمِنُونَ

fa-mā kāna jawāba qawmihi illā an qālū uqtulūhu aw ḥarriqūhu fa-anjāhu Allāhu mina al-nāri inna fī dhālika la-āyātin li-qawmin yuʾminūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“His people’s only answer was to say: Kill him or burn him. Then The God saved him from the fire. In that are signs for people who believe.”

حَرِّقُوهُ appears here too.

This time it stands alongside اقْتُلُوهُ.

اقْتُلُوهُ comes from ق ت ل and concerns killing.

حَرِّقُوهُ comes from ح ر ق and concerns burning.

أَوْ separates the two commands.

The verse thus reports two methods of violence in the opponents’ speech.

But burning is not necessarily wholly separate from killing in its consequences: burning a person can cause their death.

The lexical difference concerns the kind of action: killing in general, and burning.

Rescue from the fire

The verse continues:

فَأَنْجَاهُ اللَّهُ مِنَ النَّارِ

fa-anjāhu Allāhu mina al-nāri

النَّار is explicit in this sentence.

حَرِّقُوهُ thus relates directly to fire.

Conversely, the verse reports Abraham’s rescue, rather than his death occurring.

The opponents’ intention should therefore be distinguished from the outcome.

They intend to burn him, but the Qur’an reports his rescue.

ح ر ق alone cannot establish the precise manner of rescue or all its natural details.

That requires examination of the relevant verses in Abraham’s story.

4. لَنُحَرِّقَنَّهُ in the calf narrative

Ṭā Hā 20:97: destroying a fabricated object of worship

Arabic verse:

قَالَ فَاذْهَبْ فَإِنَّ لَكَ فِي الْحَيَاةِ أَنْ تَقُولَ لَا مِسَاسَ وَإِنَّ لَكَ مَوْعِدًا لَنْ تُخْلَفَهُ وَانْظُرْ إِلَىٰ إِلَٰهِكَ الَّذِي ظَلْتَ عَلَيْهِ عَاكِفًا لَنُحَرِّقَنَّهُ ثُمَّ لَنَنْسِفَنَّهُ فِي الْيَمِّ نَسْفًا

qāla fa-idhhab fa-inna laka fī al-ḥayāti an taqūla lā misāsa wa-inna laka mawʿidan lan tukhlafahu wa-unẓur ilā ilāhika alladhī ẓalta ʿalayhi ʿākifan la-nuḥarriqannahu thumma la-nansifannahu fī al-yammi nasfan

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“He said: Then go; in life it is for you to say: No contact or approach; and you have an appointment that will certainly be kept. Look at the deity to which you remained devoted: we will certainly burn it, then scatter it completely in the sea.”

لَنُحَرِّقَنَّهُ is a Form II imperfect.

The lām and emphatic nūn express certainty and strong emphasis.

هُ refers to the fabricated object of worship that the Samaritan had made the center of devotion.

On the familiar reading, the verb means we will certainly burn it.

But early sources record an important lexical and reading variation.

Reading variation: burning or filing down?

Al-Ṭabarī reports several readings in explaining Ṭā Hā 20:97.

The familiar reading has لَنُحَرِّقَنَّهُ with ḍammah on nūn and doubled rāʾ.

This is Form II and means burning with fire.

Another transmission has لَنُحْرِقَنَّهُ with undoubled rāʾ, Form IV, also meaning burning.

A further reading is لَنَحْرُقَنَّهُ, explained lexically as abrading and filing with files.

This relates to an early use of حَرَقَ for scraping and abrading something.

Early reading and lexical traditions thus present two images:

Burning the fabricated object of worship with fire.

Or abrading and filing it into small particles.

Al-Ṭabarī prefers the familiar reading of burning.

This must be stated carefully: filing down is genuinely recorded in early Arabic, but should not silently replace the familiar reading of the Qur’anic text.

For Forqan’s base translation, “we will burn it” is suitable.

The reading variation and possibility of filing down belong in lexical research and reflection on the verse.

Al-Ṭabarī: two burning readings distinguished from filing down

Burning and scattering

After لَنُحَرِّقَنَّهُ comes:

ثُمَّ لَنَنْسِفَنَّهُ فِي الْيَمِّ نَسْفًا

thumma la-nansifannahu fī al-yammi nasfan

نَسَفَ belongs to the independent ن س ف family and concerns scattering, dispersing, or breaking apart.

The Qur’an thus places two actions together.

First, destroying or burning the calf.

Then scattering its remains in the sea.

Their meanings should remain distinct.

حرق itself does not mean scattering in water.

نسف alone does not mean burning.

The familiar reading presents burning followed by scattering the remains.

The filing-down reading suggests dispersing the particles produced by abrasion.

Neither establishes the precise industrial or physical method of destroying the calf without further evidence.

Was the calf actually alive?

Ṭā Hā 20:88 describes a calf with جَسَد and خُوَار.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:148 describes a calf with the same features.

These expressions belong to the discussion of the calf’s nature.

But لَنُحَرِّقَنَّهُ alone does not establish that the calf must have been alive or merely a metal statue.

Burning can concern different materials, and early Arabic also envisages filing down a body capable of being scraped.

The calf’s nature must therefore be examined through the verses about its making, جَسَد, خُوَار, and other indications.

ح ر ق alone does not resolve the question.

The aim of destroying the calf

In the narrative, the calf had become an object of worship.

Moses speaks of destroying it.

Burning here relates to removing a fabricated object of worship.

But حرق does not itself mean monotheism or rejecting polytheism.

Those concepts come from the narrative and the people’s conduct.

The lexical meaning of لَنُحَرِّقَنَّهُ should therefore not be replaced with invalidating a false deity.

Invalidating the fabricated deity is the action’s direction and result within the narrative; the direct verb expresses burning it.

5. فَاحْتَرَقَتْ: the garden burning in the Qur’anic image

Al-Baqarah 2:266: a garden lost when needed

Arabic verse:

أَيَوَدُّ أَحَدُكُمْ أَنْ تَكُونَ لَهُ جَنَّةٌ مِنْ نَخِيلٍ وَأَعْنَابٍ تَجْرِي مِنْ تَحْتِهَا الْأَنْهَارُ لَهُ فِيهَا مِنْ كُلِّ الثَّمَرَاتِ وَأَصَابَهُ الْكِبَرُ وَلَهُ ذُرِّيَّةٌ ضُعَفَاءُ فَأَصَابَهَا إِعْصَارٌ فِيهِ نَارٌ فَاحْتَرَقَتْ كَذَٰلِكَ يُبَيِّنُ اللَّهُ لَكُمُ الْآيَاتِ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَتَفَكَّرُونَ

a-yawaddu aḥadukum an takūna lahu jannatun min nakhīlin wa-aʿnābin tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhāru lahu fīhā min kulli al-thamarāti wa-aṣābahu al-kibaru wa-lahu dhurriyyatun ḍuʿafāʾu fa-aṣābahā iʿṣārun fīhi nārun fa-iḥtaraqat kadhālika yubayyinu Allāhu lakumu al-āyāti laʿallakum tatafakkarūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Would any of you wish to have a garden of palms and vines with rivers flowing beneath it and every kind of fruit, while old age has reached him and he has weak offspring, then a whirlwind containing fire strikes it and the garden burns? Thus The God makes the signs clear to you, so that you may reflect.”

فَاحْتَرَقَتْ is a Form VIII perfect.

Its grammatical subject is a feminine singular pronoun referring to جَنَّة, the garden.

Its direct meaning is therefore that the garden burned.

But the verb itself does not say who set it alight.

The event’s cause appears in the preceding clause:

فَأَصَابَهَا إِعْصَارٌ فِيهِ نَارٌ

fa-aṣābahā iʿṣārun fīhi nārun

A whirlwind containing fire reaches the garden, and the garden burns.

The construction matters.

حَرِّقُوهُ commands people to burn another.

In فَاحْتَرَقَتْ, the garden is itself the grammatical subject of burning.

إِعْصَارٌ فِيهِ نَارٌ

إِعْصَار comes from ع ص ر and means a whirlwind here.

نَار names fire.

احترقت expresses the result of fire’s effect on the garden.

The three words should not be merged into one meaning.

A garden may burn through fire, but iḥtirāq itself does not mean whirlwind.

Nor does every use of whirlwind necessarily involve fire.

Here the Qur’an explicitly describes a whirlwind containing fire.

Some early commentaries explain the image through intensely hot, scorching wind.

But direct translation should preferably retain fire for نار, leaving explanations of the wind and heat to reflection.

The burnt garden as a parable

Al-Baqarah 2:266 follows verses concerning spending, the value of action, and the danger of losing an action’s result.

Nearby verses speak of spoiling charity through reminders and harm, and spending to be seen by others.

The garden lost at the most critical moment thus forms part of a moral argument.

Its owner has become old and has weak offspring.

In those circumstances, the garden may support their life.

At that very moment, however, fire destroys it.

The image can show the loss of the fruit of work from which someone hopes to benefit when in need.

But فَاحْتَرَقَتْ itself does not mean his deeds were invalidated.

The garden burning is the verse’s physical image; its connection with action and spending follows from the broader context.

Direct translation should therefore preserve the garden burned, with the moral interpretation explained separately.

Is this a particular historical garden?

The verse begins with أَيَوَدُّ أَحَدُكُمْ.

This construction invites the addressee to imagine a particular situation.

The text therefore need not report a historical fire with a known owner, location, and date.

The burning image serves reflection on the result of action.

Conversely, the garden’s use as a parable does not empty its concrete image of meaning.

Garden, fruit, old age, weak offspring, and fire are tangible elements showing the loss’s severity.

Does احترقت mean complete destruction of all matter?

The verb itself supplies no such detail.

Ordinary language can say a garden burned even if some tree trunks or soil remain.

What matters here is losing the garden as a source of benefit and livelihood.

The verse does not specify the amount of ash, type of fire, or details of combustion chemistry.

The verb should therefore not yield a technical report on how every part of the garden burned.

6. عذاب الحريق in the Qur’an

الْحَرِيق occurs five times, each in combination with عَذَاب.

These uses concern burning punishment and the effect of fire.

But each has its own context.

Āl ʿImrān 3:181: burning punishment and responsibility for speech and action

Arabic verse:

لَقَدْ سَمِعَ اللَّهُ قَوْلَ الَّذِينَ قَالُوا إِنَّ اللَّهَ فَقِيرٌ وَنَحْنُ أَغْنِيَاءُ سَنَكْتُبُ مَا قَالُوا وَقَتْلَهُمُ الْأَنْبِيَاءَ بِغَيْرِ حَقٍّ وَنَقُولُ ذُوقُوا عَذَابَ الْحَرِيقِ

la-qad samiʿa Allāhu qawla alladhīna qālū inna Allāha faqīrun wa-naḥnu aghniyāʾu sa-naktubu mā qālū wa-qatlahumu al-anbiyāʾa bi-ghayri ḥaqqin wa-naqūlu dhūqū ʿadhāba al-ḥarīqi

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“The God has certainly heard those who said: The God is poor and we are rich. We will record what they said and their killing of the prophets without right, and say: Taste the burning punishment.”

الْحَرِيقِ is the genitive complement of عذاب in this construction.

Its meaning concerns burning force and fire.

But the verse also states the matters at issue: a group’s particular words and the wrongful killing of prophets.

حريق does not name those acts; it describes the punishment mentioned later in the verse.

Nor should the verse yield an unchanging judgment about every person of a people or generation regardless of its stated descriptions.

Its judgment concerns the speech and actions described.

Al-Anfāl 8:50: burning punishment and the scene of death

Arabic verse:

وَلَوْ تَرَىٰ إِذْ يَتَوَفَّى الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا الْمَلَائِكَةُ يَضْرِبُونَ وُجُوهَهُمْ وَأَدْبَارَهُمْ وَذُوقُوا عَذَابَ الْحَرِيقِ

wa-law tarā idh yatawaffā alladhīna kafarū al-malāʾikatu yaḍribūna wujūhahum wa-adbārahum wa-dhūqū ʿadhāba al-ḥarīqi

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“If you could see when the angels take those who disbelieve in death, striking their faces and backs, and [they are told:] Taste the burning punishment.”

عَذَابَ الْحَرِيقِ follows a scene of death and the angels’ encounter with them.

حريق alone does not mean striking faces and backs.

Striking comes from يَضْرِبُونَ.

Burning punishment comes from عَذَابَ الْحَرِيقِ.

Early grammatical explanations understand an implied saying before ذُوقُوا: they are told to taste the punishment.

This helps explain the sentence without changing ح ر ق.

Al-Ṭabarī: taking souls and the scene involving angels

Al-Ḥajj 22:9: burning punishment in response to misleading

Arabic verse:

ثَانِيَ عِطْفِهِ لِيُضِلَّ عَنْ سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ لَهُ فِي الدُّنْيَا خِزْيٌ وَنُذِيقُهُ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ عَذَابَ الْحَرِيقِ

thāniya ʿiṭfihi li-yuḍilla ʿan sabīli Allāhi lahu fī al-dunyā khizyun wa-nudhīquhu yawma al-qiyāmati ʿadhāba al-ḥarīqi

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Turning aside in pride to lead away from the way of The God; for him is disgrace in this world, and on the Day of Resurrection We make him taste the burning punishment.”

الْحَرِيقِ again appears in the construction for burning punishment.

But the context concerns someone who turns away in pride and misleads from God’s way.

حريق therefore does not mean pride or misleading.

Those behaviors are the object of judgment; ḥarīq describes the punishment.

Al-Ḥajj 22:22: burning punishment and trying to leave

Arabic verse:

كُلَّمَا أَرَادُوا أَنْ يَخْرُجُوا مِنْهَا مِنْ غَمٍّ أُعِيدُوا فِيهَا وَذُوقُوا عَذَابَ الْحَرِيقِ

kullamā arādū an yakhrujū minhā min ghammin uʿīdū fīhā wa-dhūqū ʿadhāba al-ḥarīqi

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Whenever they wish to leave it from distress, they are returned to it; and taste the burning punishment.”

Here the preceding verses explicitly mention fire and instruments of punishment.

Al-Ḥajj 22:19 describes garments of fire.

Al-Ḥajj 22:20 describes searing heat affecting the body’s interior and skins.

Al-Ḥajj 22:21 introduces implements of iron.

عَذَابَ الْحَرِيقِ in Al-Ḥajj 22:22 therefore relates directly to fire and burning.

But ḥarīq itself cannot calculate the fire’s temperature or the punishment’s precise physical mechanism.

The verse emphasizes the severity of punishment and difficulty of escaping it.

Al-Ṭabarī: distress in punishment and being returned to it

Al-Burūj 85:10: those who persecuted believers

Arabic verse:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ فَتَنُوا الْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَالْمُؤْمِنَاتِ ثُمَّ لَمْ يَتُوبُوا فَلَهُمْ عَذَابُ جَهَنَّمَ وَلَهُمْ عَذَابُ الْحَرِيقِ

inna alladhīna fatanū al-muʾminīna wa-al-muʾmināti thumma lam yatūbū fa-lahum ʿadhābu jahannama wa-lahum ʿadhābu al-ḥarīqi

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Those who subjected believing men and women to severe trial and persecution, then did not repent, have the punishment of Hell, and have the burning punishment.”

عَذَابُ الْحَرِيقِ stands alongside عَذَابُ جَهَنَّمَ.

The nearby context recounts the people of the trench and the fuel-fed fire.

Fire and burning are therefore clearly present.

But the repeated word punishment should not immediately imply two wholly separate places or systems of punishment.

One expression may emphasize the place and another its burning character.

Conversely, their difference should not be dismissed without contextual examination.

The importance of ثُمَّ لَمْ يَتُوبُوا

Al-Burūj 85:10 includes ثُمَّ لَمْ يَتُوبُوا.

This qualification should not be omitted.

The verse mentions not only wrongful conduct but also the subsequent absence of repentance.

The complete construction therefore matters in examining responsibility and outcomes.

حريق alone does not establish that repentance is closed from the outset to everyone in every situation.

Repentance requires examination through its own words and verses.

ذُوقُوا عَذَابَ الْحَرِيقِ

Four of the five occurrences of الْحَرِيق include a verb or command for tasting punishment.

ذَاقَ comes from ذ و ق and directly means tasting.

But the Qur’an also uses tasting to describe the experience of punishment.

Here the point is not merely tasting something with the tongue, but experiencing punishment’s painful effect.

That extension does not make ḥarīq merely a psychological sensation unrelated to fire.

The afterlife passages also speak of fire and burning.

It is therefore preferable to distinguish the means and image of punishment, its experience, and its ultimately unknown character.

7. Differences from related roots

Ḥ R Q and Ḥ R R: burning and heat

حَرّ belongs to ح ر ر and concerns heat.

حَرْق concerns burning and fire’s searing effect.

Heat can exist without burning.

Hot weather, for example, does not always set something alight.

Conversely, burning involves heat and fiery intensity.

The families are therefore close but not identical.

Study of Ḥ R R

Ḥ R Q and N W R: burning and fire

نَار belongs to ن و ر and commonly names fire.

حَرِيق can emphasize flaming up, burning, or fire’s searing effect.

Both fields occur in Al-Baqarah 2:266:

فِيهِ نَارٌ فَاحْتَرَقَتْ

fīhi nārun fa-iḥtaraqat

Fire is present in the whirlwind and burns the garden.

نار and احترقت thus differ in role and meaning.

Study of N W R

Ḥ R Q and L H B: burning and flame

لَهَب denotes a flame or tongue of fire.

حريق can express a fire or burning.

Flame may form part of burning, but ḥarīq is not necessarily only the name of a visible flame in every use.

Al-Masad 111:3 uses لَهَب, but that word does not come from ح ر ق.

Ḥ R Q and Ṣ L Y: burning and exposure to fire

Arabic صَلِيَ النَّارَ concerns entering fire or undergoing its effect.

حَرَقَ and اِحْتَرَقَ concern burning and setting alight.

The families overlap in descriptions of fiery punishment.

But they differ in morphology and semantic direction.

Not every صَلِيَ should become turned to ash, nor every حريق entered the fire.

Study of Ṣ L Y

Ḥ R Q and S ʿ R: burning and blazing

سَعِير belongs to س ع ر and concerns blazing, kindled fire.

حَرِيق likewise relates to burning and searing force.

The fields approach one another in punishment passages.

But they should not be treated as derivatives of one root or assigned an identical meaning in every use.

Ḥ R Q and Ḥ Ṭ M: burning and crushing

حَطَمَ concerns breaking, crushing, and smashing.

In one early Arabic branch, حَرَقَ can mean abrading or filing down.

In this particular use the results may be similar: something becomes small pieces.

But crushing does not necessarily involve fire, and burning does not always involve mechanical crushing.

The possibility of filing down in Ṭā Hā 20:97 therefore does not equate every use of حرق and حطم.

Study of Ḥ Ṭ M

Ḥ R Q and N S F: burning and scattering

نَسَفَ concerns scattering and dispersing.

Ṭā Hā 20:97 places لَنَنْسِفَنَّهُ after لَنُحَرِّقَنَّهُ.

These are two different actions.

First, destroying the calf by burning on the familiar reading.

Then scattering its remains in the sea.

نسف should therefore not be merged into حرق.

Ḥ R Q and Ḥ K K: abrasion and burning effects

حَكَّ concerns rubbing one thing against another.

Ibn Fāris brings out this field in explaining ح ر ق.

Early حرق includes grinding the teeth and filing something down.

But not every rubbing action produces fire.

The families are thus close in some uses, rather than completely synonymous.

Ḥ R Q and Ḥ R F: different roots and forms

حَرْف, with fāʾ, comes from ح ر ف and concerns an edge, side, change of direction, and other uses.

حَرْق, with qāf, comes from ح ر ق.

The written forms are similar, but the families differ.

Likewise, حَرِيق should not be confused with حَرِير from ح ر ر, nor حَرْق with خَرْق from خ ر ق.

Apparent resemblance does not replace examination of root consonants and semantic evidence.

Study of Ḥ R F

8. Challenges in understanding and semantic development

Does حَرَّقَ always mean burning repeatedly?

No.

Form II can emphasize intensity or multiplicity in some uses.

But the doubled rāʾ alone does not establish that a Qur’anic object was burned several times.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:68 concerns the command to burn Abraham.

Ṭā Hā 20:97 concerns destroying the calf.

Context establishes the kind and extent of the action.

Does لَنُحَرِّقَنَّهُ certainly mean filing down?

No.

Filing down is recorded in early Arabic and proposed for a transmitted reading of Ṭā Hā 20:97.

But the familiar reading is Form II, meaning burning.

Direct translation should therefore follow the Qur’anic form being used.

The variant can be included in the research article, but should not silently replace the principal reading.

Is the garden fire in Al-Baqarah 2:266 a historical report?

The question presents an image for reflection.

It relates to spending and the result of action.

Without evidence, the garden should not be assigned a historical owner, geographic location, or date.

But its use as a parable does not remove actual burning from the image.

Is الْحَرِيق always the name of a separate part of Hell?

The word itself does not establish this.

Arabic حريق concerns burning and searing fire.

All five Qur’anic occurrences accompany punishment.

Treating it as the name of a distinct part of the afterlife requires further evidence.

The root does not specify such a structure or division.

Does a burning sensation always mean actual fire?

No.

Arabic حرقة can describe burning eyes, pain, grief, or an acrid taste.

But the passages about burning Abraham, the calf, and the garden contain direct evidence of fire or burning, with the stated reading distinction in Ṭā Hā 20:97.

Figurative uses should therefore not erase fire from every verse.

Conversely, not every burning sensation in Arabic means the body has actually caught fire.

Is modern scientific combustion identical with early Arabic usage?

In chemistry, combustion concerns a process generally involving a substance reacting with an oxidant and releasing energy.

That scientific definition helps investigate natural phenomena.

But early Arabic used حرق and احتراق for burning and the effect of fire before modern chemistry developed.

فَاحْتَرَقَتْ should therefore not be confined to the technical definition of reaction with oxygen.

The verse describes a garden burning, rather than a chemical equation for combustion.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

For حَرِّقُوهُ in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:68 and Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:24, the basic equivalent is “burn him.”

This preserves the imperative.

For لَنُحَرِّقَنَّهُ in Ṭā Hā 20:97, “we will certainly burn it” is suitable.

Research commentary should note the alternative reading explained as filing down in reading and lexical sources.

For فَاحْتَرَقَتْ in Al-Baqarah 2:266, “then it burned” is natural and precise.

Since the referent is the garden, the complete clause can read “then the garden burned.”

For عذاب الحريق, “burning punishment” is fluent.

“Punishment of burning fire” makes fire more explicit, but its Persian equivalent can be heavier in some sentences.

“The punishment of burning” is closer to the Arabic genitive construction, though it may be less natural in ordinary Persian.

Forqan’s word box should retain two distinct fields.

Brief root meaning: “Burning and setting alight; fire and intense heat; and, in an early use, abrading and wearing away.”

Meaning in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:68 and Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:24: “The command to burn Abraham with fire.”

Meaning in Ṭā Hā 20:97: “Burning and destroying the calf; with a transmitted reading variation concerning filing down.”

Meaning in Al-Baqarah 2:266: “The garden burning through a whirlwind containing fire.”

Meaning in the five verses with الْحَرِيق: “Burning and fire’s searing effect in describing punishment.”

Translation and word boxes should preserve these differences.

Not every form should become fire: حَرِّقُوهُ is the act of burning another, فَاحْتَرَقَتْ describes something burning, and الْحَرِيق is a noun concerning burning and searing force.

10. Conclusion

Arabic ح ر ق concerns burning, setting alight, and the effect of fire.

Early lexicons also record abrading and filing down. Ibn Fāris explains the family through abrasion accompanied by heat and burning intensity.

The field includes burning sensations, intense pain, an acrid taste, and grinding the teeth in anger.

In another branch, حارقة names a sinew in the hip region, which Ibn Fāris distinguishes from the general sense of burning.

The root occurs nine times in nine Qur’anic verses.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:68 and Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:24, Abraham’s opponents command his burning.

Ṭā Hā 20:97 expresses destroying the calf with لَنُحَرِّقَنَّهُ, for which early reading and lexical traditions record burning and filing down.

In Al-Baqarah 2:266, فَاحْتَرَقَتْ describes the garden burning within an image of losing one’s livelihood and the fruit of action.

The other five verses use الْحَرِيق with punishment, emphasizing its burning force.

Thus, the central Qur’anic meaning of ح ر ق is burning and setting alight; but abrasion and wearing away must also be recognized lexically, particularly for the reading variation in Ṭā Hā 20:97.

The root itself does not specify material, the form of fire, the extent of destruction, or details of the afterlife.

The verb’s meaning should also be distinguished from its object and context. The command to burn Abraham is reported from his opponents; the garden burning is a parable; and burning punishment elsewhere belongs to judgment concerning human conduct.

For Forqan, “burn him,” “we will burn it,” “it burned,” and “burning punishment” are therefore the most suitable equivalents.

These preserve Arabic meaning while preventing interpretations, scientific details, or reading variants from being silently added to the direct Qur’anic translation.
