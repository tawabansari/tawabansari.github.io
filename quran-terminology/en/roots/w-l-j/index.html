Qur’anic Root w-l-j (wuluj)

Entering, gaining access, bringing inside, and becoming a confidant

The Arabic root w-l-j denotes entering something or gaining access to its interior. Walaja means to enter; awlaja means to bring something into something else; wuluj names the act of entering or gaining access inside.

The same family includes wilaj, a doorway or entrance; mawlij, a route or place of entry; and walijah, someone admitted to a person’s or group’s inner circle and private affairs, becoming a close associate or confidant.

The Qur’an uses this root for things entering the earth, a camel entering a needle’s eye, bringing night into day and day into night, and admitting someone to a circle of close associates and confidants.

These usages share one feature: something enters from outside, or someone gains access to a position previously outside their reach.

Physical entry, the changing of night and day, and access to private human relationships are nevertheless distinct usages. Each must be considered through its own construction and context.

1. The root in early Arabic and its historical evidence

In early Arabic, walaja al-bayta means entering a house, and walaja al-shayʾu fi ghayrihi means one thing entering another.

This usage concerns passage from outside to inside. A person may enter through a house’s doorway, an animal may enter its shelter, or something may sink into the earth.

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays reads:

وَبَيْتِ عَذَارَى يَوْمَ دَجْنٍ وَلَجْتُهُ
يَطُفْنَ بِجَبَّاءِ الْمَرَافِقِ مِكْسَالِ

wa-bayti ʿadhārā yawma dajnin walajtuhu / yaṭufna bi-jabbāʾi al-marāfiqi miksāli

Translation: “And a house of maidens I entered on a cloudy day; they moved about a leisurely woman whose elbows were concealed by the fullness of her flesh.”

Walajtuhu refers to the poet entering the house. The pronoun hu refers to bayt, the house.

The point is that the poet describes entering the house’s interior. The verb itself does not specify permission to enter, the type of doorway, or the person’s motive.

The line is transmitted in a well-known ode by Imruʾ al-Qays. Some lexical versions read dakhaltuhu instead of walajtuhu. Both express entering, but the variant wording means that walajtuhu should not be presented as an unquestionably fixed reading without acknowledging the different transmissions.

The ode’s attribution to the pre-Islamic poet has a recognized literary tradition. Its text, however, reaches us through later transmission, and the composition date of each individual line has not been independently established.

Another witness is transmitted from Umayyah ibn Abi ʿAʾidh al-Hudhali:

قَدْ كُنْتُ خَرَّاجًا وَلُوجًا صَيْرَفًا
لَمْ تَلْتَحِصْنِي حَيْصَ بَيْصَ لَحَاصِ

qad kuntu kharrājan walūjan ṣayrafan / lam taltaḥiṣnī ḥayṣa bayṣa laḥāṣi

Translation: “I was one who went out and entered often, experienced in affairs; no severe predicament had trapped me.”

Waluj here describes someone who frequently enters places. Paired with kharraj, it portrays experience in coming and going, entering and leaving, and handling affairs.

The underlying meaning remains entry; the descriptive form waluj expresses the frequency of the action.

Umayyah ibn Abi ʿAʾidh is reported to have lived before Islam and continued into the Islamic period, as late as the Umayyad era. This line’s date of composition is unknown. It attests early Arabic usage but cannot securely be assigned to the pre-Qur’anic period.

Lexicons also record wilaj for a gate or entrance. Wilaj al-wadi refers to side routes and winding passages leading into a valley.

Walajah is also used for a place such as a cleft, cave, or shelter that a person or animal can enter for protection from rain or danger.

These usages clarify the relationship between entry and interior space. A doorway provides access; a valley or cave has an interior into which someone can pass; a shelter can separate a person or animal from the outside environment.

These examples do not establish that wuluj always means entry through a very narrow passage, covert entry, or difficult penetration. Such features may occur in particular usages, but are not fixed components of the root’s meaning.

Taken together, the evidence supports entering something and gaining access to its interior as the family’s early Arabic meaning. Some branches extend this to bringing something inside or gaining access to close, private relationships.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important Arabic forms include:

  • Walaja: he entered or gained access inside.
  • Yaliju: he enters or gains access inside.
  • Wuluj: entry or entering.
  • Waluj: someone who enters places frequently.
  • Wallaj: one who enters frequently; sometimes paired with kharraj.
  • Awlaja: he brought someone or something inside.
  • Ilaj: bringing something into something else.
  • Mawlij: a place or route of entry.
  • Wilaj: a gate, entrance, or route leading inside.
  • Walajah: a cleft, shelter, or place that can be entered.
  • Walijah: someone admitted to an inner circle; a confidant or trusted close associate.
  • Walaʾij: the plural of walijah.
  • Ittalaja: he entered or gained access inside.
  • Tawallaja: he entered; in some usages, penetrated into a place.
  • Tawlaj: an animal’s shelter or hiding place, such as a den.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an.

Qur’anic derivatives fall into three groups:

1. The basic triliteral verb yaliju and yalija.

2. Form IV verbs tuliju and yuliju, including forms prefixed by a conjunction.

3. The noun walijah.

The family occurs fourteen times in the Qur’an: three basic triliteral verbs, ten Form IV verbs, and one occurrence of walijah. These fourteen occurrences are distributed across nine verses.

Form IV awlaja is causative and transitive. While walaja principally describes something entering, awlaja denotes causing entry or bringing something into something else.

For example, “the person entered the house” differs from “another person brought him into the house.”

Entry occurs in both cases, but the agent’s role in the sentence differs.

This distinction matters in the verses about night and day, since the Qur’an identifies God as the subject of yuliju.

3. Qur’anic usages

1. Yaliju: entering the earth

Sabaʾ 34:2

يَعْلَمُ مَا يَلِجُ فِي الْأَرْضِ وَمَا يَخْرُجُ مِنْهَا وَمَا يَنْزِلُ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ وَمَا يَعْرُجُ فِيهَا ۚ وَهُوَ الرَّحِيمُ الْغَفُورُ

yaʿlamu mā yaliju fī al-arḍi wa-mā yakhruju minhā wa-mā yanzilu mina al-samāʾi wa-mā yaʿruju fīhā wa-huwa al-raḥīmu al-ghafūr

Sabaʾ 34:2

Translation: “He knows what enters the earth and what emerges from it, what descends from the sky and what ascends into it. He is the Merciful, the Forgiving.”

Yaliju fi al-ard refers here to something entering the earth.

Yaliju is the basic triliteral verb, while fi al-ard specifies the space into which entry occurs.

The verse does not identify a single object. Ma encompasses a broad range of things entering the earth.

Rainwater can penetrate soil. A seed may be placed in the ground. Animals can enter holes and underground shelters. Other things may also move into the earth’s layers.

These illustrate the phrase; the Qur’an does not restrict it to these examples.

Conversely, wa-ma yakhruju minha concerns what emerges from the earth.

Plants grow out of the ground, water emerges from springs, and animals leave underground shelters.

But w-l-j must not be equated with growing, sprouting, or being buried. These may illustrate entry or emergence in context; yaliju itself does not mean growing or burying.

Yaliju and yakhruju are set opposite each other here.

One describes entry into the earth, the other emergence from it.

Two further movements follow: descending from the sky and ascending into it.

Together they describe movements that are partly visible and partly hidden from people. God knows them all.

The same construction recurs in al-Hadid 57:4.

There, this knowledge follows the account of creating the heavens and earth, and is followed by God’s being with people and knowing their actions.

The point is therefore not merely the movement of objects. The verse highlights God’s comprehensive knowledge, extending beyond what is visible on the earth’s surface.

Yaliju fi al-ard alone does not reveal the precise structure of the earth’s interior, every underground creature’s form, or the scientific mechanisms of all these movements.

The verb expresses entering, and its object has a broad scope in the verse.

2. Tuliju and yuliju: bringing night into day and day into night

Al ʿImran 3:27

تُولِجُ اللَّيْلَ فِي النَّهَارِ وَتُولِجُ النَّهَارَ فِي اللَّيْلِ ۖ وَتُخْرِجُ الْحَيَّ مِنَ الْمَيِّتِ وَتُخْرِجُ الْمَيِّتَ مِنَ الْحَيِّ ۖ وَتَرْزُقُ مَنْ تَشَاءُ بِغَيْرِ حِسَابٍ

tūliju al-layla fī al-nahāri wa-tūliju al-nahāra fī al-layli wa-tukhriju al-ḥayya mina al-mayyiti wa-tukhriju al-mayyita mina al-ḥayyi wa-tarzuqu man tashāʾu bi-ghayri ḥisāb

Āl ʿImrān 3:27

Translation: “You bring night into day and bring day into night. You bring the living out of the dead and bring the dead out of the living. You provide for whomever You will without reckoning.”

Tuliju is a Form IV imperfect verb addressed to God.

In the first clause, al-layla is the object, and fi al-nahar specifies what it is brought into.

In the second clause, night and day exchange places: day is brought into night.

This reciprocal construction matters. The Qur’an does not describe a movement in only one direction: night enters day, and day enters night.

People observe this change daily in the arrival of light after darkness and the return of darkness after light.

The duration of night and day also changes through the year in many regions. As days lengthen, some time previously dark becomes light; as nights lengthen, some time previously light becomes dark.

These are clear illustrations of ilaj al-layl fi al-nahar and ilaj al-nahar fi al-layl.

The root w-l-j itself does not establish that night and day are physical objects that enter one another like containers.

The verse uses natural language to describe their change and reciprocal relationship. Knowledge of the earth’s movement relative to the sun can clarify the mechanism today, while the lexical meaning of tuliju remains bringing inside.

Al ʿImran 3:27 places this image alongside bringing the living from the dead and the dead from the living.

Tukhriju derives from another root, kh-r-j.

The pairing of ilaj and ikhraj places two related movements together: bringing in and bringing out.

It does not follow that night necessarily means death or day necessarily means life. Such a symbolic reflection would require independent evidence.

In the verse’s direct meaning, night and day are familiar phenomena, while life and death are mentioned separately.

Other occurrences concerning night and day

The same expression appears in four other verses:

Al-Hajj 22:61: bringing night and day into one another appears alongside God’s hearing and seeing.

Luqman 31:29: the change accompanies the subjection of the sun and moon, each moving until an appointed term.

Fatir 35:13: the same image appears alongside God’s sovereignty and the limited power of other objects of worship.

Al-Hadid 57:6: bringing night and day into one another accompanies God’s knowledge of what is within hearts.

Each pairs yuliju with wa-yuliju, preserving the reciprocal relationship.

This repetition shows that bringing night into day and day into night is an established Qur’anic image of God’s governance of the world.

Each context nevertheless emphasizes a different aspect: power, knowledge, governance, sovereignty, or the relationship of celestial phenomena to human life.

These verses should not be reduced to explaining the length of night and day. Nor should unsupported physical theories about darkness and light be extracted from them.

3. Yalija: the camel and the needle’s eye

Al-Aʿraf 7:40

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ كَذَّبُوا بِآيَاتِنَا وَاسْتَكْبَرُوا عَنْهَا لَا تُفَتَّحُ لَهُمْ أَبْوَابُ السَّمَاءِ وَلَا يَدْخُلُونَ الْجَنَّةَ حَتَّىٰ يَلِجَ الْجَمَلُ فِي سَمِّ الْخِيَاطِ ۚ وَكَذَٰلِكَ نَجْزِي الْمُجْرِمِينَ

inna alladhīna kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā wa-stakbarū ʿanhā lā tufattaḥu lahum abwābu al-samāʾi wa-lā yadkhulūna al-jannata ḥattā yalija al-jamalu fī sammi al-khiyāṭi wa-kadhālika najzī al-mujrimīn

Al-Aʿrāf 7:40

Translation: “Those who denied Our signs and were arrogant toward them will not have the gates of heaven opened for them, nor will they enter the Garden until a camel passes through a needle’s eye. Thus We recompense the guilty.”

Yalija is an imperfect verb in the subjunctive after hatta.

Al-jamal is the subject, and fi sammi al-khiyat specifies the point of entry.

Samm here means the needle’s tiny opening, and al-khiyat denotes the needle.

The image is clear: a camel, a large animal, cannot pass through a needle’s small eye.

The image expresses the impossibility of the group in question entering the Garden.

The passage first uses yadkhuluna from d-kh-l, then yalija from w-l-j.

Both concern entry, but yalija appears in the image of the camel passing through the needle’s eye, drawing attention to entry into a particular passage.

The verse does not establish a rule that wuluj always means entering a very narrow opening. The poetic witness from Imruʾ al-Qays uses it for entering a house, while Sabaʾ describes things entering the earth.

The narrowness in al-Aʿraf 7:40 comes from sammi al-khiyat, not from w-l-j itself.

Nor is the camel’s passage merely ordinarily difficult. The image is chosen to convey impossibility.

Hatta therefore does not promise that a camel will one day pass through a needle’s eye and those people will then enter the Garden. It expresses the impossibility of that entry through a concrete image.

The verse’s qualifications must also be preserved when identifying whom it addresses.

It concerns those who denied God’s signs and were arrogant toward them. This description must not be extended to everyone with a different understanding without examining knowledge, awareness, and conduct.

4. Walijah: admission to an inner circle and becoming a confidant

Al-Tawbah 9:16

أَمْ حَسِبْتُمْ أَنْ تُتْرَكُوا وَلَمَّا يَعْلَمِ اللَّهُ الَّذِينَ جَاهَدُوا مِنْكُمْ وَلَمْ يَتَّخِذُوا مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ وَلَا رَسُولِهِ وَلَا الْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَلِيجَةً ۚ وَاللَّهُ خَبِيرٌ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ

am ḥasibtum an tutrakū wa-lammā yaʿlami Allāhu alladhīna jāhadū minkum wa-lam yattakhidhū min dūni Allāhi wa-lā rasūlihi wa-lā al-muʾminīna walījatan wa-Allāhu khabīrun bi-mā taʿmalūn

Al-Tawbah 9:16

Translation: “Did you suppose you would be left alone, while The God has not yet known those among you who strive and take no confidant apart from The God, His Messenger, and the believers? The God is fully aware of what you do.”

Walijah belongs to w-l-j but, unlike yaliju and yuliju, does not describe physical or natural entry.

In Arabic, walijah denotes someone admitted to personal or group relationships as a close associate or confidant.

Lexicons explain walijat al-rajul through bitanatuhu, khassatuhu, and dikhlatuhu: those with access to a person’s private affairs, counted among his close associates.

Another explanation describes someone who has entered a group without originally belonging to it.

This relates to the root’s basic image: someone passes from outside into an inner circle.

The entry is not physical. A person might have lived in the same city or community for years, becoming a walijah only upon gaining access to a group’s close, private affairs.

For example, many people within a community may know each other, while only a few know its decisions, internal disagreements, private information, and plans. Those few belong to the inner circle.

Walijah in this semantic field concerns such closeness and access to internal affairs.

The word itself does not mean spy or traitor. A confidant can be trustworthy. Betrayal, concealed intentions, and abuse of trust are separate matters.

Walijah in the context of al-Tawbah 9:16

The preceding verses discuss broken covenants, hostility, and confrontation with groups acting against the Messenger and the believers.

Al-Tawbah 9:13 mentions those who broke their pledges and sought to expel the Messenger.

Al-Tawbah 9:16 then concerns testing and identifying those who truly strive under these circumstances and take no inner confidant or support outside God, the Messenger, and the believers. Speaking of their becoming manifest explains the context of testing; it is not a separate lexical equivalent of yaʿlam.

The issue is therefore not ordinary friendship or social acquaintance. The verse addresses circumstances in which loyalty, trust, and internal relationships can affect collective security and prospects.

If someone outwardly accompanies a group while giving its confidential decisions and sensitive information to people acting against it, that conduct conflicts with genuine trustworthiness and loyalty.

Walijah in this context can encompass such an internal, concealed relationship.

But it should not be extended to every friendship with someone outside the community of believers.

Al-Mumtahanah 60:8 does not prohibit kindness and just treatment toward those who have not fought believers on account of religion or expelled them from their homes.

Al-Mumtahanah 60:9 concerns those who have fought, expelled people, or assisted in their expulsion.

This distinction shows that friendship and kindness differ from entrusting sensitive, confidential affairs to a party acting against the community.

Walijah should also not be determined solely by religious, ethnic, or social labels. Its meaning concerns the relationship and the position someone gains within the inner circle.

At the end of al-Tawbah 9:16, God describes Himself as fully aware of human actions.

This does not authorize individuals to accuse others of betrayal or secret relationships without evidence. Complete knowledge of intentions and hidden actions is attributed to God.

The Qur’anic usage thus emphasizes trustworthiness and loyalty in internal relationships, while identifying intentions and establishing betrayal require actual evidence.

4. Comparison with related words

W-l-j and d-kh-l: entering and gaining access inside

Dukhul and wuluj are close in meaning in many usages.

Both can describe entering a house or another space.

Al-Aʿraf 7:40 uses both families: yadkhuluna al-jannah and yalija al-jamal.

Dukhul has a broad Qur’anic range, from entering a house, city, or the Garden to entering relationships or other conditions.

Wuluj likewise denotes entry, but its Qur’anic usages emphasize movement into something: entering the earth, night entering day, or a camel entering a needle’s eye.

This is a tendency, not an absolute rule that every wuluj is necessarily more difficult, deeper, or more concealed than dukhul.

In some Arabic constructions, the two verbs can substitute for each other.

Study of d-kh-l

W-l-j and kh-r-j: entering and emerging

Khuruj concerns coming out.

Sabaʾ 34:2 and al-Hadid 57:4 contrast yaliju fi al-ard with yakhruju minha.

Fi identifies the space entered; min here identifies the point of departure.

The contrast helps clarify wuluj’s basic meaning. Entering and emerging are different movements that can concern the same place or being.

Entry alone does not imply remaining forever. Something entering the earth may later emerge.

Permanent residence or confinement therefore cannot be inferred from walaja without contextual evidence.

Study of kh-r-j

W-l-j and n-z-l: entering and descending

Nuzul concerns descending or coming down.

Wuluj emphasizes entering something.

Sabaʾ 34:2 places them in distinct expressions: something enters the earth, and something descends from the sky.

Rain may descend from the sky and then penetrate the soil. Descent and entry into the ground are two different aspects of that process.

This does not mean that every descent necessarily leads to entry, or that everything entering the earth previously descended from the sky.

Each verb has its own object and context in the Qur’an.

Study of n-z-l

W-l-j and n-f-dh: entering and passing through a barrier

In linguistic usage, nufudh can mean passing through something or reaching beyond it.

Wuluj concerns entry into something.

Something may enter a space and remain there without emerging on the other side. Some usages of nufudh, by contrast, emphasize passing through a barrier and reaching beyond it.

Al-Rahman 55:33 uses tanfudhu and fa-nfudhu concerning passage beyond boundaries.

In al-Aʿraf 7:40, yalija al-jamal fi sammi al-khiyat depicts a camel entering a needle’s eye.

These families overlap in passage and gaining access, but are not identical or interchangeable in every usage.

Study of n-f-dh

W-l-j and b-t-n: entering and an inner circle

Batn concerns the interior or inner part of something.

Bitanah is used for a garment’s lining and, socially, for a person’s close associates and confidants.

In Al ʿImran 3:118, bitanah describes people admitted to the circle of close associates and internal affairs.

This is closely related to walijah in al-Tawbah 9:16.

The underlying images differ: walijah relates to entering and gaining access, while bitanah draws on the inner portion closest to the body.

Both emphasize closeness and access to private affairs.

Closeness to another is not itself a sign of betrayal, and not everyone outside a group is necessarily its enemy. The relationship’s moral character depends on circumstances and conduct.

Study of b-ṭ-n

W-l-j and w-l-y: admission and closeness with responsibility

W-l-j and w-l-y are independent root families.

W-l-y concerns closeness, connection, guardianship, and support.

W-l-j concerns entering and gaining access inside.

Walijah may overlap with wali in human relationships, since both can describe someone close and trusted.

This overlap does not make their roots identical.

Walijah derives from w-l-j, not w-l-y.

All the rulings and usages concerning wali and awliyaʾ therefore cannot be transferred to walijah without independent examination.

A person with guardianship or a supportive relationship is not necessarily privy to all another person’s secrets.

Study of w-l-y

W-l-j and l-j-j: entering and persisting

These roots resemble one another in sound and writing, but are independent.

Some Qur’anic usages of l-j-j concern persistence in conduct; another branch concerns deep water and the sea.

W-l-j concerns entering.

Lajju in al-Muʾminun 23:75 therefore derives from l-j-j, not w-l-j.

Lujjah in al-Naml 27:44 and lujji in al-Nur 24:40 likewise belong to l-j-j.

Sound similarities do not establish identical roots or meanings.

5. Translation proposals for Forqan

Suitable renderings of walaja include “he entered,” “he went inside,” and “he gained entry.”

Wuluj is naturally rendered “entry,” “entering,” or “gaining access inside.”

For awlaja, “he brought inside,” “he introduced,” or “he caused to enter” are proposed.

For ilaj, “bringing inside” or “bringing one thing into another” is suitable.

Mawlij can be rendered “entrance,” “route of entry,” or “place of entry.”

In physical usage, wilaj can mean “gate” or “way in.”

Depending on context, walajah may be rendered “cleft,” “shelter,” or “a place entered.”

For social walijah, suitable equivalents include “confidant,” “someone admitted to the inner circle,” or “one trusted with private affairs.”

The following renderings are proposed for the main Qur’anic usages:

Sabaʾ 34:2 and al-Hadid 57:4 — yaliju fi al-ard

“Enters the earth” or “goes into the earth.”

“Sinks into the earth” is natural in many physical examples, but should not restrict entry to downward vertical movement.

Al ʿImran 3:27 — tuliju al-layla fi al-nahar

“You bring night into day.”

Al-Hajj 22:61, Luqman 31:29, Fatir 35:13, and al-Hadid 57:6 — yuliju al-layla fi al-nahar

“He brings night into day.”

Preserving the image of bringing inside is preferable to replacing it directly with a scientific explanation of the earth’s movement.

Al-Aʿraf 7:40 — hatta yalija al-jamalu fi sammi al-khiyat

“Until a camel passes through a needle’s eye.”

“Enters a needle’s eye” is also literally accurate, but the equivalent of “passes through a needle’s eye” is more natural in Persian and clearer for the verse’s image.

Al-Tawbah 9:16 — wa-lam yattakhidhu … walijatan

“And have taken no one as a confidant.”

The explanation should clarify that this is not merely ordinary friendship. It concerns admitting someone to close relationships and internal affairs in a context of loyalty and testing.

“Spy” or “traitor” is unsuitable as a direct equivalent of walijah, since neither feature follows from the root itself.

6. Conclusion

Arabic w-l-j denotes entering something or gaining access to its interior.

The meaning is clear in physical usages such as entering a house, cave, or the earth. Form IV expresses bringing something into another thing. In social walijah, the image extends to someone admitted to a person’s or group’s inner circle and private affairs.

The Qur’an uses this family in four main settings.

In Sabaʾ 34:2 and al-Hadid 57:4, yaliju concerns what enters the earth.

In Al ʿImran 3:27 and four other verses, yuliju and tuliju concern bringing night into day and day into night.

In al-Aʿraf 7:40, yalija depicts the impossible entry of a camel into a needle’s eye.

In al-Tawbah 9:16, walijah denotes someone admitted to an inner circle and close relationships as a confidant.

The last usage shows that entry is not always physical. A person can enter a house, but can also gain access to a group’s private affairs and trust.

Wuluj should nevertheless not always be restricted to covert penetration, difficult entry, or passage through a narrow route. The instance and context determine these features.

Walijah itself likewise does not mean spy or traitor. It describes a position within an internal relationship; that relationship’s rightness or wrongness depends on circumstances, loyalties, and conduct.

The distinction between w-l-j and d-kh-l is also a tendency, not an absolute rule. Both can express entry, but Qur’anic wuluj emphasizes gaining access inside or bringing something into another thing.

The final definition of w-l-j is entering or gaining access inside something. In Form IV, it means bringing inside or causing entry; in walijah, it extends to someone admitted to close relationships and internal affairs. The place, manner, purpose, and moral value of entry cannot be determined from the root alone. They are understood through sentence structure and each verse’s context.
