The Qur’anic Root F W R (فَارَ)

Qur’anic and lexical forms

(wa-fāra)
Surged; two occurrences

تَفُورُ
(tafūru)
Seethes; one occurrence

فَوْرِهِمْ
(fawrihim)
The immediacy of their movement; one occurrence

Boiling, surging, and gushing forth: from water and the oven to vigorous movement, immediacy, and the seething Fire

1. The root’s meaning in early Arabic

The Arabic triliteral root F W R concerns boiling, vigorous movement, and surging upward. Fāra l-māʾu means that water boiled or gushed out. Fārati l-qidru describes a pot whose contents boil and churn under heat.

Classical lexicons explain fāra l-shayʾu through jāsha: something churned, seethed, surged, or emerged from a state of calm.

Fawr and fawarān are verbal nouns in this family. Fawwār describes something that boils repeatedly or gushes forcefully; fawwārah can name an outlet or jet of water.

The meaning is not confined to boiling water. Fāra l-māʾu mina l-ʿayni describes water gushing forcefully from a spring. Such water need not be hot: its conspicuous outward movement is the salient feature.

Fāra l-ghaḍabu similarly pictures anger as something boiling up and intensifying within a person.

Thus, boiling and surging in this family can describe actual material movement, the intensification of a condition, or the swift onset of movement.

These uses are not identical. Water boiling in a pot, gushing from a spring, and anger intensifying are distinct situations described through related imagery.

An example from early Arabic poetry

A verse attributed to al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī reads:

تَفُورُ عَلَيْنَا قِدْرُهُمْ فَنُدِيمُهَا
وَنَفْثَؤُهَا عَنَّا إِذَا حَمْيُهَا غَلَا

Tafūru ʿalaynā qidruhum fa-nudīmuhā / Wa-nafthaʾuhā ʿannā idhā ḥamyuhā ghalā.

“Their pot boils against us; we still its boiling and cool it down when its heat rises.”

The boiling pot pictures intensifying hostility and confrontation. The speaker likens the other group’s conduct to a pot whose contents boil under heat.

Tafūru supplies the image of seething, while ghalā expresses boiling and rising heat. The poet uses material imagery to describe an escalating conflict between two groups.

The verse is recorded in early lexical works and attributed to al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī in some sources, although other attributions also occur. He lived both before and after the advent of Islam, and the poem’s date is not independently established. It cannot therefore be treated without qualification as securely pre-Qur’anic evidence.

Within that limitation, the verse illustrates an early connection between a boiling pot and the intensification of a situation.

From surging matter to surging emotion

Classical Arabic uses fāra fāʾiruhu of someone whose anger has been intensely aroused.

Fawratu l-ḥarri likewise denotes an intensification of heat, and fawratu l-ghaḍabi a surge of anger.

Here surging does not mean water pouring out. The shared feature is something intensifying beyond its calm or usual condition.

In other uses, fawru l-shayʾi denotes the beginning of a matter, and min fawrihi describes acting at that beginning, immediately, before the initial movement or state subsides.

For example, jiʾtu min fawrī means “I came immediately” or “I came directly, continuing from what I was doing.”

This temporal use matters for understanding min fawrihim in Āl ʿImrān 3:125.

These related senses do not mean that every Arabic fawr necessarily involves heat, fire, or anger. The subject and sentence structure establish its precise meaning.

Lexicons also record fūr, with a short u on the first consonant, as a collective name for gazelles. Shared letters alone do not establish its derivation from boiling pots or vigorous movement. The Qur’anic branch concerns seething and immediate movement; explaining it does not require merging every similar-looking form.

2. Qur’anic forms and the semantic core

F W R occurs four times in the Qur’an: three times as a Form I verb and once as a noun.

The forms are:

  • وَفَارَ in Hūd 11:40.
  • وَفَارَ in Al-Muʾminūn 23:27.
  • تَفُورُ in Al-Mulk 67:7.
  • فَوْرِهِمْ in Āl ʿImrān 3:125.

Fāra is a Form I perfect verb expressing boiling or surging upward.

Tafūru is the corresponding imperfect form; in Al-Mulk it describes the Fire’s seething state.

Fawr is a noun or verbal noun. In min fawrihim, it concerns the onset and immediacy of movement.

Fawarān, fāʾir, fawwār, fawwārah, and fawrah are known Arabic forms, but do not appear in these forms in the Qur’an.

A defensible semantic core for this branch is:

Churning and seething, surging upward, or emerging forcefully; in temporal usage, the immediate onset or continuation of movement or a state before it subsides.

This definition relates boiling water, the seething Fire, and immediate movement, while leaving each verse’s actual subject to be identified from its context.

3. Hūd 11:40: the oven’s surging and the Flood’s onset

حَتَّىٰ إِذَا جَاءَ أَمْرُنَا وَفَارَ التَّنُّورُ قُلْنَا احْمِلْ فِيهَا مِنْ كُلٍّ زَوْجَيْنِ اثْنَيْنِ وَأَهْلَكَ إِلَّا مَنْ سَبَقَ عَلَيْهِ الْقَوْلُ وَمَنْ آمَنَ وَمَا آمَنَ مَعَهُ إِلَّا قَلِيلٌ

Ḥattā idhā jāʾa amrunā wa-fāra t-tannūru qulnā iḥmil fīhā min kullin zawjayni thnayni wa-ahlaka illā man sabaqa ʿalayhi l-qawlu wa-man āmana wa-mā āmana maʿahu illā qalīl.

“Until, when Our command came and the oven surged, We said: ‘Load into it two of each kind, a pair, and your family, except those against whom the word has already gone forth, and those who have believed.’ And only a few had believed with him.”

Hūd 11:40

The oven’s surging is mentioned alongside the arrival of the divine command.

Fāra is the perfect verb, and al-tannūru its subject.

The direct sense is therefore that the oven seethed or surged.

In Arabic, tannūr names an oven for baking bread, a place operated with heat. Here fāra is connected with the extraordinary circumstances of the Flood’s beginning.

An oven ordinarily suggests fire and heat, whereas the Noah narrative concerns water, drowning, and a vessel.

Many early explanations accordingly understand the expression as water welling up and gushing from the oven.

This reading fits an unusual sign marking the Flood’s onset: a place normally used for fire and cooking becomes an outlet for water.

The expression itself does not independently mention “water.” Water’s surging is inferred from the Flood narrative and the verb’s established meaning.

Is the tannūr a bread oven?

The established, direct Arabic meaning of tannūr is a bread oven.

Early sources nevertheless record different readings of its referent in Noah’s story.

One takes it as an actual oven, with water gushing from it as the sign that the divine command had arrived.

Another understands tannūr as the earth’s surface or a place in the earth from which water emerges.

Accounts also identify particular locations for the oven or interpret it as the onset of daylight. These details are not equally supported by the verse’s language.

The direct meaning of a bread oven is clearer and better established. The earth’s surface is an exegetical possibility, but preferring it over the direct sense requires further linguistic and contextual evidence.

The basic translation should therefore retain “oven,” with the manner of its surging explained only as far as the context supports.

There is no need to assign a particular geographical location or supply its shape, material, or construction from accounts absent from the Qur’an.

Surging and the Flood waters

Al-Qamar 54:11 and Al-Qamar 54:12 present two further images of the Flood: water pouring from the sky and springs opening in the earth.

They connect Noah’s Flood with abundant water emerging from different sources.

Hūd 11:42 also describes the vessel moving amid waves like mountains.

The oven’s surging thus belongs to a network of verses about the Flood’s onset, rising water, and the rescue of Noah’s companions by the vessel.

All these phenomena should not be compressed into the meaning of fāra itself.

Fāra concerns seething and surging upward. Rainfall, the opening of springs, and the movement of waves are expressed through other words and constructions.

Nor does the oven’s surging by itself establish a volcanic eruption, an explosion of the earth, boiling temperatures throughout the Flood waters, or a particular geological mechanism.

The event marks the start of a decisive stage in Noah’s story. Its physical details beyond what the Qur’an states are not specified.

4. Al-Muʾminūn 23:27: surging as the signal to act

Al-Muʾminūn 23:27 repeats the same description of the oven surging.

Its place in the discourse matters.

The God commands Noah to build the vessel under His watch and through His revelation. He then instructs him to bring aboard those to be rescued when the command arrives and the oven surges.

In Al-Muʾminūn 23:27, the arrival of the command and the oven’s surging identify when action is to begin.

Noah must prepare the vessel beforehand. When the appointed sign appears, boarding begins.

The sequence of commands distinguishes prior preparation from action at the appropriate time.

Hūd 11:40 reports the command’s arrival and the companions’ boarding; Al-Muʾminūn 23:27 presents an instruction given to Noah in advance.

Fāra retains the same meaning in both. The difference lies in the sentence’s place in the narrative.

The repetition also shows that the oven’s surging is not ordinary cooking: both passages connect it with the divine command and the beginning of rescue.

This does not make “a sign that punishment is beginning” the lexical definition of fawr.

Surging is the event described; its function as a sign comes from the command’s context.

5. Al-Mulk 67:7: the Fire’s seething and roaring

إِذَا أُلْقُوا فِيهَا سَمِعُوا لَهَا شَهِيقًا وَهِيَ تَفُورُ

Idhā ulqū fīhā samiʿū lahā shahīqan wa-hiya tafūr.

“When they are cast into it, they hear it roaring as it seethes.”

Al-Mulk 67:7

Tafūru here describes Hell’s condition.

Hiya refers back to Hell in the preceding verse; tafūru is a feminine singular imperfect verb.

Grammatically, wa-hiya tafūru is a circumstantial clause: Hell is described while seething and surging.

This has a clear imagistic connection with a boiling pot. Heat causes the contents of a pot to churn and rise; Al-Mulk uses the image to describe Hell’s tumultuous condition.

Tafūru does not here mean that water boils. Hell is the subject.

The image of seething alone therefore does not establish the presence of hot water or a particular liquid in this verse.

Surging can describe intense movement, intensification, and rising without necessarily involving water.

Distinguishing shahīq from tafūru

The verse contains two independent descriptions:

First, in Al-Mulk 67:7, they hear a roar from Hell.

Second, Hell is seething at that moment.

Shahīq belongs to Sh H Q and concerns forceful breathing or a roaring sound.

Tafūru belongs to F W R and expresses seething and intense movement.

The images can coexist in one scene without having the same meaning.

The roaring sound comes from shahīq; the seething comes from tafūru.

The next verse reads:

تَكَادُ تَمَيَّزُ مِنَ الْغَيْظِ كُلَّمَا أُلْقِيَ فِيهَا فَوْجٌ سَأَلَهُمْ خَزَنَتُهَا أَلَمْ يَأْتِكُمْ نَذِيرٌ

Takādu tamayyazu mina l-ghayẓi kullamā ulqiya fīhā fawjun saʾalahum khazanatuhā a-lam yaʾtikum nadhīr.

“It almost bursts apart with rage. Whenever a group is cast into it, its keepers ask them: ‘Did no warner come to you?’”

Al-Mulk 67:8

This verse conveys Hell’s rage and intensity through another expression.

Tafūru in the preceding verse and al-ghayẓ here also reveal the connection between seething imagery and intense anger.

Arabic likewise uses fāra ghaḍabuhu for intense anger.

Yet tafūru in Al-Mulk 67:7 should not be reduced to being angry with no image of seething. The first verse expresses seething; the next adds the intensity of rage.

The imagery likewise does not establish a definitive theory of Hell’s physical structure, fuel, temperature, or the nature of what seethes.

The Qur’an describes roaring, seething, and intensity; these words alone do not disclose the full nature of the Hereafter.

6. Āl ʿImrān 3:125: min fawrihim and immediate movement

بَلَىٰ إِنْ تَصْبِرُوا وَتَتَّقُوا وَيَأْتُوكُمْ مِنْ فَوْرِهِمْ هَٰذَا يُمْدِدْكُمْ رَبُّكُمْ بِخَمْسَةِ آلَافٍ مِنَ الْمَلَائِكَةِ مُسَوِّمِينَ

Balā in taṣbirū wa-tattaqū wa-yaʾtūkum min fawrihim hādhā yumdidkum rabbukum bi-khamsati ālāfin mina l-malāʾikati musawwimīn.

“Yes, if you remain steadfast and mindful, and they attack you this very moment, your Lord will reinforce you with five thousand marked angels.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:125

Fawr here does not describe boiling water or fire. The subject is the movement and arrival of a group of opponents.

Fawrihim is genitive after min, and the pronoun hum refers to the group in question.

Hādhā identifies their current movement or condition.

Arabic min fawrihi describes acting at the outset, without an interval, before the initial movement subsides.

Someone proceeding directly from one task to another can say min fawrī: “at that very moment” or “immediately.”

In Āl ʿImrān 3:125, min fawrihim hādhā concerns the opponents’ coming. Its immediate sense is their arriving directly from their current movement or advance.

Does fawrihim mean their anger?

Early explanations offer several readings.

One is “from this very movement or course of theirs”: the enemy comes toward the addressees directly, without stopping or an intervening interval.

Another is “from this very anger of theirs”: their aroused anger sets them in motion.

The first emphasizes the onset and immediacy of movement; the second draws on the established link between surging and anger.

Both relate to the root’s semantic field, but yaʾtūkum principally describes the opposing group coming and arriving.

Thus, “directly from this advance of theirs” or “this very moment” fits the immediate construction more clearly. Anger may motivate their movement, but the expression alone does not establish every member’s psychological motivation.

The distinction matters: narrowing fawr to anger would wrongly imply that anyone moving swiftly must be angry.

Fawrihim also does not mean an attack. Movement toward the addressees comes from yaʾtūkum; military confrontation comes from the surrounding verses.

Its relationship to steadfastness and mindfulness

The verse joins three conditions: steadfastness, taqwa, and the opposing group’s immediate arrival.

The promise of divine reinforcement follows.

Fawrihim describes the immediacy or state of the enemy’s movement, not steadfastness, taqwa, or victory themselves.

The expression concerns a particular battle setting and promise of aid. Without independent evidence, it cannot establish that every group in every war claiming steadfastness and taqwa must receive the same number of angels.

The basic translation should preserve immediacy rather than add a historical explanation or name commanders and groups not identified in the verse.

7. Distinctions from related roots

F W R (fawr) and Gh L Y (ghalayān)

Gh L Y concerns boiling and rising heat.

Fārati l-qidru and ghalati l-qidru can describe closely related situations: a pot’s contents boil under heat.

Other uses of fawr include water gushing out, anger intensifying, and immediate action.

Thermal ghalayān therefore overlaps with fawarān without covering its entire semantic field.

Al-Dukhān 44:45 uses yaghlī for boiling; Al-Dukhān 44:46 compares it to the boiling of scalding water.

Al-Mulk 67:7 uses tafūru for Hell’s seething condition.

These images are close, but different roots do not establish a definitive system distinguishing substances, temperatures, or degrees of punishment.

F W R (fawr) and F Y Ḍ (fayḍ)

In one main branch, F Y Ḍ concerns overflowing and flowing forth in abundance.

With water, overflowing can occur when its volume exceeds a container’s capacity and spills out.

Fawarān places greater emphasis on seething, upward surging, and vigorous movement.

Both can occur together: water may gush forcefully from a spring and then overflow onto the ground.

Yet not every overflow is a surge. Water can spill gently from a container.

Nor does every surge involve overflowing a container: spring water may gush from underground.

Fawr and fayḍ thus overlap in some instances without being identical.

F W R (fawr) and N B ʿ (nabʿ)

Nabaʿa l-māʾu describes water emerging from its spring or source.

Fāra l-māʾu can describe water gushing with turbulence and force.

Water emerging from the earth may therefore both spring forth and surge, but this combination is not necessary.

A spring can well up gently, its water flowing gradually.

By contrast, boiling in a pot can be described with F W R, but is not nabʿ in the sense of water emerging from a spring.

The outlet and the manner of movement should therefore be distinguished.

F W R (fawr) and Th W R (thawr)

In its established branches, Th W R concerns rising, being stirred up, and coming into motion.

Both thāra ghaḍabuhu and fāra ghaḍabuhu can describe intensifying anger.

Thawr also concerns arousal and rising, while fawr highlights the image of boiling and surging upward.

This is a tendency, not an absolute boundary between every use of the two families.

In particular, modern thawrah, “political revolution,” does not establish that fawr originally meant social rebellion.

F W R (fawr) and F J R (fajr)

One branch of F J R concerns splitting open and opening an outlet.

Al-Qamar 54:12 describes the earth being opened into springs.

The construction concerns splitting or opening a way for water to emerge.

Fawr, however, concerns seething and gushing forth.

Opening the earth may cause water to emerge swiftly, but splitting open and seething are not the same meaning.

In Noah’s story, the oven’s surging should therefore not be translated directly as “the earth was split open”: that image comes from another verb.

F W R (fawr) and S R ʿ (surʿah)

S R ʿ concerns speed and swiftness.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:125, min fawrihim can be rendered “immediately” or “with haste.”

Fawr is not simply a word for speed. It also involves the immediate onset or uninterrupted continuation of movement or a state.

Someone can act quickly after a long wait before beginning.

Conversely, acting min fawrihi means acting directly and without an interval, although the rate of movement may remain unspecified.

Speed and immediacy are related but not always identical.

F W R (fawr) and Gh Ḍ B (ghaḍab)

Ghaḍab denotes anger.

Surging can describe anger intensifying, but F W R does not itself mean anger in every use.

Al-Mulk 67:7 describes Hell seething, and the following verse mentions ghayẓ.

Together they strengthen the image of intense anger without making “becomes angry” the meaning of tafūru everywhere.

In Noah’s story, the oven’s surging does not concern human anger; in Āl ʿImrān, possible anger among the opponents is a contextual reading, not an indispensable meaning of fawr.

8. Translation recommendations for Forqan

Principal equivalents for fāra include “boiled,” “began to seethe,” “surged,” and “gushed forth.”

For water, “gushed forth” may be more accurate than “boiled,” especially when water emerges from the earth or a spring.

For a pot, “boiled” is more natural.

Fawarān can be rendered “boiling,” “surging,” or, where appropriate, “welling up.”

Fawwār can be rendered “seething,” “gushing,” or “surging forcefully.”

Depending on the subject, fawrah can mean “a surge,” “an intensification,” or “a sudden onset.”

Apart from the forms identified as Qur’anic, these forms do not occur in the verses themselves.

Equivalents for the four Qur’anic occurrences

Hūd 11:40 — فَارَ

“And the oven surged.”

This is concise and faithful to the construction. Water’s emergence and its connection to the Flood’s onset belong in contextual discussion.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:27 — فَارَ

“And the oven surged.”

The phrase is identical in both surahs. There is no justification for rendering one “the oven surged” and the other “the earth exploded.”

Al-Mulk 67:7 — تَفُورُ

“As it seethes.”

Hell is the subject; “seethes” preserves the direct, natural image.

Āl ʿImrān 3:125 — فَوْرِهِمْ

“This very moment,” “directly from this advance of theirs,” or “with this same haste.”

Forqan’s current Persian wording, represented here as “and they attack you this very moment,” suits the military confrontation and conveys immediacy.

The root study should clarify that attacking comes from the battle setting and the verb of coming; fawr describes the immediacy or state of that movement.

Conclusion

Arabic F W R concerns seething, surging upward, and vigorous movement.

Material uses include a pot’s contents boiling and water gushing from a spring. Extended uses describe anger intensifying and movement beginning immediately.

The root occurs four times in the Qur’an.

In Hūd 11:40 and Al-Muʾminūn 23:27, the oven’s surging in Noah’s story accompanies the divine command and the Flood’s onset.

In Al-Mulk 67:7, tafūru expresses Hell’s seething condition.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:125, min fawrihim concerns the opponents coming directly from their current movement or advance.

These uses extend from material seething to movement and the intensity of a situation. They do not imply that every instance involves heat, anger, or water emerging.

In Noah’s story, the root does not specify the oven’s exact location or the physical mechanism of its surging. In describing Hell, tafūru alone likewise does not define its material structure or full nature.

Final root definition for Forqan:

F W R: something boiling, surging upward, or churning intensely, making its movement or intensity apparent. In uses concerning states and time, the swift onset and continuation of a condition, or action taken immediately before the initial movement subsides.

Qur’anic usage presents three related but distinct images: the oven surging, Hell seething, and the opponents’ immediate movement.

Each occurrence’s precise meaning is determined by its subject, sentence structure, and verse context.
