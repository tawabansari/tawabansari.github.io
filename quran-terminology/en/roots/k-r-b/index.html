The Qur’anic Root K R B (ك ر ب)

Severe distress, predicament, and overwhelming pressure

Qur’anic forms

كَرْبٍ
الْكَرْبِ

1. Meaning in early Arabic

ك ر ب concerns intensity, pressure, binding firmly, severe distress, and approaching.

Lexicons define كَرْب, with a silent ra’, as overwhelming grief or distress. كَرَب, with fatḥah, also names a strong fastening used to attach a rope to a bucket.

Ibn Faris identifies intensity and strength as the family’s foundation, connecting a tightly fastened rope with the pressure of severe trouble.

A line attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAbd Qays ibn Khuffaf al-Burjumi reads:

أَجُبَيْلُ إِنَّ أَبَاكَ كَارِبُ يَوْمِهِ فَإِذَا دُعِيتَ إِلَى الْعَظَائِمِ فَاعْجَلِ

Jubayl, your father approaches his final day; when summoned to great tasks, make haste.

This verse, recorded in al-Mufaddaliyyat, illustrates karib in the sense of approaching.

A line attributed to Al-Hutay’ah, who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, reads:

قَوْمٌ إِذَا عَقَدُوا عَقْدًا لِجَارِهِمْ شَدُّوا الْعِنَاجَ وَشَدُّوا فَوْقَهُ الْكَرَبَا

People who, when pledging protection to their neighbor, tighten the supporting strap and fasten another binding above it.

The poet compares a firm pledge and protection of a neighbor to tightly securing a bucket’s bindings so it does not detach and fall into the well.

كَرَب here denotes the bucket’s strong fastening. The line’s exact date is not established.

These examples illustrate approaching and firm binding in early poetry. They do not independently date the severe-distress sense to before Islam. The Qur’an itself and the lexicons attest that meaning in the language of revelation and later periods.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important Arabic forms include:

  • كَرْب: severe distress and overwhelming pressure.
  • كُرُوب: severe troubles and predicaments.
  • كُرْبَة: severe trouble or grief.
  • كَرَبَهُ الْأَمْرُ: the matter distressed him.
  • مَكْرُوب: troubled, grieving, or under severe pressure.
  • كَرِيب: troubled or grieving.
  • كَارِب: something causing distress; in another branch, approaching.
  • اِكْتَرَبَ: became distressed.
  • كَرَب: a firm bucket fastening; also thick remnants of palm fronds.
  • تَكْرِيب: binding firmly.
  • كُرُوب: approaching, as a verbal noun in the proximity branch.

Classical Arabic also records كَرَبَ الْأَرْضَ for turning soil for cultivation and أَكْرَبَ for hastening.

Forms examined in the Qur’an:

كَرْبٍ ـ الْكَرْبِ

Both belong to the noun كَرْب, differing only in definiteness. Neither its verbs nor plural forms occur in the Qur’an.

3. Semantic core and physical image

In the distress branch, karb denotes severe pressure and difficulty that places someone in a predicament.

A recorded physical image is tightly fastening a rope or constricting a binding. قَيْدٌ مَكْرُوبٌ denotes a tightly secured restraint.

Someone in extreme difficulty may feel that escape routes are closed and that pressure has impaired strength and peace of mind.

This helps explain the semantic connection between physical pressure and human distress, without establishing that distress historically developed from the bucket fastening.

Karb can denote both severe inward grief and external circumstances producing it.

A person caught in a turbulent sea, a family facing grave danger, or a population living under oppression may experience karb.

Qur’anic uses likewise show that it is not merely an inward feeling, but can be a real predicament requiring rescue.

4. Qur’anic uses

كُلِّ كَرْبٍ: every severe predicament

The first verse depicts people encountering danger in the darkness of land or sea and turning to The God for rescue.

The next expands beyond that particular trouble through مِنْ كُلِّ كَرْبٍ, embracing every severe predicament.

Karb is therefore not limited to maritime danger, darkness, or becoming lost on land. It can express many forms of extreme difficulty.

The focus is turning to The God in distress and forgetting afterward. Faced with limited capacities, people plead and promise gratitude, yet may return to associating partners after deliverance.

Rescue here does not merely remove fear or sadness: it releases people from dangerous, oppressive circumstances.

الْكَرْبِ الْعَظِيمِ: rescuing Noah and his family

The next verse states:

Noah’s karb is connected with his opponents’ rejection of the divine signs and their drowning.

He faced opposition and rejection for a long period. Surah Nuh describes his repeated invitations and the people’s refusal.

His distress was not merely private sadness, but difficult circumstances affecting him and his companions.

Al-Saffat also describes this rescue:

Together these verses allow “the great distress” to include both hostile opposition and the danger of drowning.

Karb itself does not mean flood or drowning. Those are contextual instances of the predicament.

The rescue of Noah’s family must also be read with Hud 11:42, Hud 11:43, and Hud 11:46, which show that kinship alone did not guarantee deliverance.

الْكَرْبِ الْعَظِيمِ: rescuing Moses, Aaron, and their people

The expression used for Noah and his family also describes Moses, Aaron, and their people.

The accounts of Pharaoh’s domination of the Children of Israel clarify the predicament:

Its nature is explicit: domination, oppression, killing, and systematic mistreatment under Pharaoh’s power.

Rescue from karb meant release from the conditions causing grief and anxiety, not simply removal of the feelings.

Al-Baqarah 2:50 also describes their deliverance at the sea and the drowning of Pharaoh’s people.

“The great distress” in Al-Saffat 37:115 can therefore encompass oppression and the dangers involved in escaping it.

Using one expression for Noah and Moses shows that karb is not limited to one kind of event. Severe trouble and the need for deliverance unite these cases.

5. Syntax and structure

كَرْب is a noun in the pattern فَعْل and is genitive in every Qur’anic occurrence.

مِنْ كُلِّ كَرْبٍ in Al-Anʿam 6:64 combines مِنْ, كُلّ, and the indefinite genitive complement كَرْبٍ, extending the scope to every severe predicament.

The other three occurrences are definite and modified by الْعَظِيمِ:

مِنَ الْكَرْبِ الْعَظِيمِ

الْعَظِيمِ modifies الْكَرْبِ, emphasizing its magnitude and severity.

Although karb itself implies severe trouble, ʿaẓīm further highlights this particular predicament’s magnitude.

Every Qur’anic occurrence accompanies a derivative of ن ج و:

- يُنَجِّيكُمْ مِنْ كُلِّ كَرْبٍ - فَنَجَّيْنَاهُ وَأَهْلَهُ مِنَ الْكَرْبِ الْعَظِيمِ - وَنَجَّيْنَاهُ وَأَهْلَهُ مِنَ الْكَرْبِ الْعَظِيمِ - وَنَجَّيْنَاهُمَا وَقَوْمَهُمَا مِنَ الْكَرْبِ الْعَظِيمِ

In these constructions, karb identifies the predicament and deliverance the action of releasing people from it.

This association is central to understanding its Qur’anic meaning.

6. Comparison with related roots

ك ر ب (كَرْب) and غ م م (غَمّ)

Ghamm concerns enveloping grief and pressure weighing on a person.

Of Jonah, the Qur’an states:

This resembles Al-Anbiya’ 21:76: one describes Noah’s rescue from karb, the other Jonah’s rescue from ghamm.

Ghamm can describe distress arising from real trouble, and karb can include inward grief.

Karb’s Qur’anic uses foreground a severe predicament and deliverance; ghamm also embraces a broad range of human grief and pressure.

This does not create an absolute separation: their meanings overlap.

ك ر ب (كَرْب) and ح ز ن (حُزْن)

Ḥuzn denotes sadness and heaviness of heart.

Yusuf 12:84 describes Jacob’s intense grief at separation from Joseph.

A person may live safely yet grieve a loved one’s absence. Sadness need not involve external danger.

Karb can likewise include grief, but its Qur’anic uses foreground a predicament from which a person or group needs release.

ك ر ب (كَرْب) and ض ي ق (ضِيق)

ض ي ق concerns narrowness, limitation, and reduced capacity.

Here ḍīq expresses inward pressure caused by opponents’ words.

The family can describe spatial narrowness, limited resources, or psychological pressure. Karb emphasizes the severity of distress and its pressure.

A constricted chest can be an effect of karb, but the words are not interchangeable in every use.

ك ر ب (كَرْب) and ض ر ر (ضُرّ)

ض ر ر concerns harm, injury, and adverse conditions affecting a person.

Al-Anbiya’ 21:83 presents Job speaking of the harm that has touched him.

Ḍurr can be illness, injury, financial loss, or another hurt. Karb can describe the severe predicament and pressure arising from it.

Harm may cause karb, but not every injury places someone in severe distress.

ك ر ب (كَرْب) and ب أ س (بَأْس)

ب أ س concerns intensity, hardship, strength, and the pressure of confrontation.

Al-Baqarah 2:177 mentions patience amid hardship, adversity, and at the time of baʾs.

Baʾs can convey intense confrontation, harmful force, or harsh conditions; karb emphasizes the severe predicament in which people find themselves.

ك ر ب (كَرْب) and ن ج و (نَجَاة)

Najāh denotes escape from danger, trouble, and an adverse outcome.

The Qur’anic relationship is clear: one identifies the predicament, the other deliverance from it.

For Noah, Moses, and Aaron, rescue from karb changes actual circumstances, rather than merely providing calm within the same oppressive conditions.

This association shows that removing external causes of trouble can be part of release from pressure and grief.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Arabic word or expressionSuggested rendering
كَرْبsevere distress; grave predicament
كُرْبَةsevere trouble; overwhelming grief
مَكْرُوبafflicted; deeply distressed
كُلِّ كَرْبٍevery severe predicament
الْكَرْبِ الْعَظِيمِthe great distress
مِنَ الْكَرْبِ الْعَظِيمِfrom the great distress

“Severe distress” is suitable because it can include both difficult external circumstances and their resulting pressure.

“The great distress” naturally preserves the construction الْكَرْبِ الْعَظِيمِ.

“Sadness” alone risks obscuring the objective predicament, particularly in the accounts of Noah, Moses, and Aaron.

Conclusion

ك ر ب includes intensity, firm binding, approaching, grief, and severe distress.

The Qur’an uses the severe-distress branch, always alongside rescue and deliverance.

Noah’s predicament concerns hostile opposition and drowning; that of Moses, Aaron, and their people concerns Pharaoh’s oppression and the dangers of escape.

كُلِّ كَرْبٍ in Al-Anʿam 6:64 shows that the concept is not restricted to a particular event.

Qur’anic karb is the severe predicament and pressure of difficult circumstances affecting a person or group and requiring deliverance.
