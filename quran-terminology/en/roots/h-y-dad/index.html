Menstrual bleeding: from the lexical sense of flowing to menstruation and the rulings concerning it

The Arabic root Ḥ Y Ḍ is associated with the flowing and discharge of liquid. In its familiar human use, it denotes women’s periodic menstrual bleeding. The words ḥayḍ, maḥīḍ, ḥāʾiḍ, and ḥayḍah belong to this family.

In early Arabic, this root was not used only of women. Related forms are recorded for the discharge of a reddish substance from certain trees and the flow of torrents. Nevertheless, women’s menstruation had a recognized, specific meaning in Arabic and should not be reduced to any kind of liquid flow.

The Qur’an uses this root in two different settings: Al-Baqarah 2:222 discusses menstruation and marital relations during it; Al-Ṭalāq 65:4 discusses women whose menstruation has ceased or who have not menstruated, and establishes a waiting period after divorce.

Understanding these uses requires distinguishing the bleeding itself, the period in which it occurs, the place from which blood issues, and the rulings the verse states concerning it. These concepts are related, but they are not identical in Arabic or in the verses’ constructions.

1. Forms of the root and its semantic core

The root Ḥ Y Ḍ occurs four times in the Qur’an, in two lexical forms: al-maḥīḍi three times across Al-Baqarah 2:222 and Al-Ṭalāq 65:4, and yaḥiḍna once in Al-Ṭalāq 65:4.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: four occurrences and their word positions

Important Arabic forms of this family include:

  • حَاضَتْ ـ تَحِيضُ (ḥāḍat–taḥīḍu): of a woman, to menstruate and discharge menstrual blood.
  • حَيْض (ḥayḍ): menstruation, women’s recognized periodic bleeding; sometimes the blood itself.
  • مَحِيض (maḥīḍ): menstruation; according to construction and context, its time or place.
  • مَحَاض (maḥāḍ): another verbal noun for menstruation.
  • حَائِض (ḥāʾiḍ): a woman menstruating or described with reference to this characteristic.
  • حَائِضَة (ḥāʾiḍah): another recorded adjectival form for a menstruating woman.
  • حَيْضَة (ḥayḍah): one occurrence or episode of menstruation.
  • حِيضَة (ḥīḍah): in some uses, the state or manner of menstruation; also recorded for a cloth associated with it.
  • حُيَّض and حَوَائِض (ḥuyyaḍ and ḥawāʾiḍ): recognized plurals for menstruating women.
  • تَحَيَّضَتْ (taḥayyaḍat): in relevant uses, to regard oneself as menstruating or act according to the menstrual state.
  • اِسْتُحِيضَتْ and مُسْتَحَاضَة (ustuḥīḍat and mustaḥāḍah): in established lexical and juristic use, concerning bleeding distinguished from ordinary menstruation.

Not all these forms appear in the Qur’an. It uses only al-maḥīḍ and yaḥiḍna from this family.

The semantic core

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris mentions a reddish liquid issuing from the samurah tree. He explains calling a woman who has recently given birth ḥāʾiḍ by comparing her blood with that liquid. This lexical account concerning a nufasāʾ, a woman after childbirth, should be distinguished from the specific sense of menstruation in the Qur’anic uses.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: samurah and the reference to a woman after childbirth

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise identifies blood flowing at recognized times as a basic sense of women’s menstruation.

This evidence shows that the flow and discharge of liquid belong to the root’s semantic field. Yet caution is needed about which use arose first historically.

The explanation involving a tree’s reddish discharge may clarify a semantic connection between uses. Its appearance in a lexicon does not establish that Arabs first used the root of trees and subsequently transferred it to women’s menstruation.

What can be established more confidently is that ḥayḍ, in its human use, concerns women’s recognized periodic menstrual bleeding.

Thus, the root’s Qur’anic meaning cannot be reduced to “flowing.” It concerns a specific kind of blood flow in a woman’s body.

Flowing clarifies the lexical background; menstrual bleeding is the word’s specific, established use.

2. Early Arabic usage and the image of flowing

Ḥāḍati al-samuratu: a tree’s reddish discharge

The lexicons record:

حَاضَتِ السَّمُرَةُ

ḥāḍati al-samuratu

That is: “A substance flowed from the samurah tree.” (Ḥāḍati al-samuratu.)

The samurah is a thorny tree of the acacia group. The lexicons describe its reddish discharge as resembling blood and name it duwadim. This lexical description alone does not establish the substance’s scientific composition.

Qatar Qur’anic Botanic Garden: identification of the samr tree

Its color resembles blood, but the substance is not human blood.

This use shows that the family Ḥ Y Ḍ could describe a reddish substance issuing from something nonhuman.

This resemblance does not make women’s menstruation a kind of tree disease or an unnatural phenomenon. These are different instances described through the same lexical family.

Ḥāḍa al-saylu: the flow of a torrent

Lisān al-ʿArab transmits from Al-Azharī that ḥāḍa al-saylu and fāḍa can both describe a torrent flowing.

This concerns the flow of water, rather than blood.

Al-Mubarrad likewise proposes a lexical connection between the naming of menstruation and a torrent’s flow.

This witness helps explain the root’s semantic background, but recognized usage must be distinguished from a theory of derivation.

The recording of ḥāḍa al-saylu is a lexical fact. The claim that the word for women’s menstruation must have arisen from this use of a torrent is a historical claim requiring independent support.

Lisān al-ʿArab: torrent flow and historical derivation

A witness from Arabic poetry

A verse by ʿUmārah ibn ʿAqīl reads:

أَجَالَتْ حَصَاهُنَّ الذَّوَارِي، وَحَيَّضَتْ
عَلَيْهِنَّ حَيْضَاتُ السُّيُولِ الطَّوَاحِمِ

ajālat ḥaṣāhunna al-dhawārī wa-ḥayyaḍat / ʿalayhinna ḥayḍātu al-suyūli al-ṭawāḥimi

A translation close to the sense:

“The scattering winds stirred their pebbles, and mighty torrents sent their surging flows over them.”

Here, ḥayyaḍat and ḥayḍātu al-suyūli concern the flow of torrents.

The poet describes wind and water: wind moving pebbles and a torrent flowing over them.

Here, the words related to ḥayḍ do not mean women’s bleeding. Movement and the flow of liquid are prominent.

The example must also be situated correctly in history. ʿUmārah ibn ʿAqīl was an Abbasid-era poet and a descendant of Jarīr. This is therefore evidence of eloquent Abbasid Arabic, rather than pre-Islamic poetry or a witness demonstrably predating the Qur’an.

Umm al-Qurā University poetry collection: ʿUmārah ibn ʿAqīl, Dīwān p. 91

Nevertheless, this use in poetry and the lexicons shows that the association of Ḥ Y Ḍ with liquid flow was recognized in Arabic.

Menstruation as a recurring event

In reference to women, ḥayḍah can denote a single episode of menstruation.

This form shows that menstruation concerns not only blood itself but an event with a beginning and an end that may recur.

Arabic distinguishes between referring to the blood flow itself and to a single occurrence.

For example, ḥayḍ can name the phenomenon generally, while ḥayḍah foregrounds one particular episode.

Menstruation should therefore not be confined to material discharged from the body. Its period and occurrence are also recognized in linguistic usage.

3. Maḥīḍ: menstruation, its time, or its place?

The range of maḥīḍ is an important question in studying this root.

The lexicons record maḥīḍ both as a verbal noun and for the time or place of menstruation.

In Al-Mufradāt, Al-Rāghib al-Isfahānī identifies three uses: menstruation itself, its time, and its place.

Al-Rāghib’s Al-Mufradāt: maḥīḍ as an event, time, and place

Arabic morphology allows this distinction, since the pattern mafʿil in some verbal families can express a verbal noun, time, or place.

Yet the availability of three possibilities does not mean that all three are intended equally in every verse.

Menstruation as a phenomenon

In ʿani al-maḥīḍi, the inquiry may concern menstruation itself.

On this reading, maḥīḍ names the phenomenon associated with periodic blood flow.

Menstruation as a time

In fī al-maḥīḍi, the intended sense may be the period in which menstruation occurs.

On this reading, fī concerns a temporal situation: during menstruation.

Menstruation as a place

The lexicons also record maḥīḍ for the place where menstruation occurs or blood issues.

On this reading, fī al-maḥīḍi may refer to the place of menstruation.

Yet this morphological possibility does not make a body part the intended meaning in every use. Choosing between time and place requires attention to the accompanying verb and sentence.

These three dimensions matter in Al-Baqarah 2:222, which contains al-maḥīḍ twice.

4. The Qur’anic use in Al-Baqarah 2:222

وَيَسْأَلُونَكَ عَنِ الْمَحِيضِ قُلْ هُوَ أَذًى فَاعْتَزِلُوا النِّسَاءَ فِي الْمَحِيضِ وَلَا تَقْرَبُوهُنَّ حَتَّىٰ يَطْهُرْنَ فَإِذَا تَطَهَّرْنَ فَأْتُوهُنَّ مِنْ حَيْثُ أَمَرَكُمُ اللَّهُ إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُحِبُّ التَّوَّابِينَ وَيُحِبُّ الْمُتَطَهِّرِينَ

wa-yasʾalūnaka ʿani al-maḥīḍi qul huwa adhan fa-ʿtazilū al-nisāʾa fī al-maḥīḍi wa-lā taqrabūhunna ḥattā yaṭhurna fa-idhā taṭahharna faʾtūhunna min ḥaythu amarakumu allāhu inna allāha yuḥibbu al-tawwābīna wa-yuḥibbu al-mutaṭahhirīna

Proposed translation for this study:

“And they ask you about menstruation. Say: It is discomfort. So keep apart from women during menstruation, and do not approach them until they become clean. Then, when they have cleansed themselves, go to them from where The God has commanded you. Indeed, The God loves those who repent and those who seek cleanliness.”

Al-Baqarah 2:222

The verse begins with a question about al-maḥīḍ.

Its first response is:

«قُلْ هُوَ أَذًى» (Qul huwa adhan.)

That is: “Say: It is discomfort.”

An instruction then follows concerning keeping apart during menstruation and not approaching until cleanliness.

Qul huwa adhan: menstruation and discomfort

Here, the pronoun huwa refers to al-maḥīḍ.

Adhā, however, belongs to a different root and must be examined independently.

Adhā can concern discomfort, distress, or harm. Here, that description applies to menstruation, rather than a woman’s character, worth, or moral purity.

Menstruation can involve pain, physical discomfort, bleeding, and temporary limitations. These examples help explain a natural understanding of adhā, but the verse does not provide a list of medical symptoms.

Nor does it state that every woman experiences the same amount of pain or discomfort in every period.

NHS: menstruation and variation between periods

More importantly, adhā does not mean sin, moral baseness, or a person’s lack of worth.

The sentence therefore does not make a menstruating woman morally contaminated or her ordinary presence among others inherently harmful.

The construction itself makes the distinction clear: al-maḥīḍ is what receives the description, rather than the woman’s essential nature.

Fa-ʿtazilū al-nisāʾa fī al-maḥīḍi: the scope of keeping apart

Iʿtazilū belongs to ʿ Z L and concerns withdrawing or keeping a distance.

Its scope, however, must be understood from the complete sentence.

After the instruction to keep apart, the verse says:

«وَلَا تَقْرَبُوهُنَّ حَتَّىٰ يَطْهُرْنَ» (Wa-lā taqrabūhunna ḥattā yaṭhurna.) — “And do not approach them until they become clean.”

After cleanliness, it also says:

«فَأْتُوهُنَّ مِنْ حَيْثُ أَمَرَكُمُ اللَّهُ» (Faʾtūhunna min ḥaythu amarakumu allāhu.) — “Then go to them from where The God has commanded you.”

These phrases show that keeping apart is discussed in the context of marital relations.

A reading grounded in the verse itself therefore relates the restriction to sexual intercourse during menstruation, rather than ending all human and social relations with a woman.

Isolating iʿtazilū al-nisāʾa from its context could suggest excluding women entirely from the home, family, or social interaction. Yet the surrounding phrases connect the issue with marital relations.

The root Ḥ Y Ḍ itself therefore cannot yield a prohibition on conversation, companionship, eating together, or ordinary interaction with a woman during this period.

This distinction does not negate the verse’s command. It identifies the command’s scope through the verse’s own construction.

Al-Ṭabarī: the scope of withdrawal and marital relations

Does fī al-maḥīḍi refer to time or place?

There are two lexical readings.

On the temporal reading, the meaning is during the menstrual period.

On the spatial reading, the phrase concerns the place of menstruation.

Both possibilities are recorded in the lexicons, but the temporal meaning has a clear place in a natural translation, since the following sentence concerns the end of menstruation and becoming clean.

The spatial dimension can also be examined alongside min ḥaythu amarakumu allāhu.

Nevertheless, not every anatomical detail or ruling concerning parts of the body can be obtained from the form al-maḥīḍ alone.

For the basic translation, “during menstruation” is clear and suitable. The possible spatial meaning should remain in the linguistic explanation.

Ḥattā yaṭhurna and fa-idhā taṭahharna

The verse then uses two verbs from Ṭ H R:

«يَطْهُرْنَ» and «تَطَهَّرْنَ» (yaṭhurna and taṭahharna).

These forms are not identical.

The first concerns becoming clean or the end of a state of impurity. The second is Form V and can denote taking action to cleanse oneself.

In the menstrual context, this distinction connects with bleeding ceasing and cleansing afterward.

This concerns Ṭ H R, however, rather than the basic meaning of Ḥ Y Ḍ.

For a study of menstruation’s root, it is sufficient to observe that the verse distinguishes the menstrual state from the state afterward.

Details about how cleansing is performed, variant readings, and juristic interpretations of these verbs require independent examination. They should not be added to the definition of al-maḥīḍ.

5. The Qur’anic use in Al-Ṭalāq 65:4

وَاللَّائِي يَئِسْنَ مِنَ الْمَحِيضِ مِنْ نِسَائِكُمْ إِنِ ارْتَبْتُمْ فَعِدَّتُهُنَّ ثَلَاثَةُ أَشْهُرٍ وَاللَّائِي لَمْ يَحِضْنَ وَأُولَاتُ الْأَحْمَالِ أَجَلُهُنَّ أَنْ يَضَعْنَ حَمْلَهُنَّ وَمَنْ يَتَّقِ اللَّهَ يَجْعَلْ لَهُ مِنْ أَمْرِهِ يُسْرًا

wa-allāʾī yaʾisna mina al-maḥīḍi min nisāʾikum ini irtabtum fa-ʿiddatuhunna thalāthatu ashhurin wa-allāʾī lam yaḥiḍna wa-ulātu al-aḥmāli ajaluhunna an yaḍaʿna ḥamlahunna wa-man yattaqi allāha yajʿal lahu min amrihi yusran

Proposed translation for this study:

“And those of your women who have lost hope of menstruation, if you are in doubt, their waiting period is three months; and likewise those who have not menstruated. As for pregnant women, their term ends when they deliver their burden. And whoever is mindful of The God, He will make their affairs easy for them.”

Al-Ṭalāq 65:4

This verse contains two forms from Ḥ Y Ḍ: al-maḥīḍi and yaḥiḍna.

Unlike Al-Baqarah 2:222, which concerns menstruation and marital relations, the topic here is determining the waiting period after divorce.

From its opening, Al-Ṭalāq discusses divorce, counting the waiting period, and the responsibilities connected with it.

Here, menstruation thus relates to women’s menstrual status and the determination of a waiting period.

Yaʾisna mina al-maḥīḍi: the end of expecting menstruation

In wa-allāʾī yaʾisna mina al-maḥīḍi, yaʾisna belongs to Y ʾ S and concerns despair or ceasing to expect something.

Its object here is al-maḥīḍ.

The phrase therefore concerns women who no longer expect menstruation to occur.

One clear instance is menstruation ceasing later in life: menopause.

This instance should not become the lexical meaning of al-maḥīḍ itself. Here, maḥīḍ still means menstruation; the cessation of expectation comes from yaʾisna.

For this group, in the situation under discussion, the verse states a period of three months.

Wa-allāʾī lam yaḥiḍna: those who have not menstruated

Yaḥiḍna is an imperfect verb from ḥāḍa–yaḥīḍu, attached to nūn al-niswah, the feminine plural subject suffix. The form is indeclinable with sukūn and, after lam, occupies the jussive position.

Lam places it within a construction negating the past.

Lam yaḥiḍna thus means “have not menstruated.”

This differs from yaʾisna mina al-maḥīḍi.

The first group concerns the end of expecting menstruation; the second describes no previous occurrence of menstruation up to the relevant time.

Lexically, lam yaḥiḍna alone does not specify why menstruation has not occurred.

Absence of menstruation can occur before the first period or in other circumstances in which a woman has not yet menstruated. Early exegetical works apply the phrase to girls before their first menstruation; some explanations also include adult women who have not menstruated.

Al-Ṭabarī: the historical interpretation of allāʾī lam yaḥiḍna

Three questions must nevertheless be distinguished: the lexical meaning of lam yaḥiḍna, its possible or historical referents, and the moral and legal assessment of marriage.

Lam yaḥiḍna itself does not state a particular age, the cause of absent menstruation, or permission for marriage or sexual relations with a child.

The verse determines waiting periods for the situations discussed in the context of divorce. Which marriages are legitimate, and who has the necessary capacity and qualifications, requires examining the wider verses concerning marriage, maturity, responsibility, and human rights.

Conversely, difficulty with the historical question does not justify categorically excluding girls before their first menstruation from the phrase’s lexical range. The wording does not itself specify age, and honest inquiry requires making that limitation clear.

An exegetical account of women of menstrual age who have not menstruated

The distinction from pregnant women

The verse then says:

«وَأُولَاتُ الْأَحْمَالِ أَجَلُهُنَّ أَنْ يَضَعْنَ حَمْلَهُنَّ» (Wa-ulātu al-aḥmāli ajaluhunna an yaḍaʿna ḥamlahunna.) — “As for pregnant women, their term ends when they deliver their burden.”

Here, pregnant women’s waiting period is determined by pregnancy ending and delivery.

This is related in subject matter to menstruation, but its principal words do not belong to Ḥ Y Ḍ.

Maḥīḍ should therefore not be taken to mean pregnancy or childbirth.

The Qur’an uses a separate expression for pregnancy.

Al-Baqarah 2:228 also discusses divorced women’s waiting period, but uses qurūʾ rather than maḥīḍ.

This lexical difference matters and requires independent study of Q R ʾ and qurūʾ. The two words cannot be declared synonymous without examination merely because both verses concern menstruation and the waiting period.

6. Distinctions from related roots and terms

Ḥ Y Ḍ (ḥayḍ) and F Y Ḍ (fayḍ): menstruation and abundant flow

Arabic fayḍ concerns flowing, abundance, and overflowing.

In some lexical expressions, ḥāḍa al-saylu and fāḍa al-saylu have closely related meanings.

F Y Ḍ and Ḥ Y Ḍ, however, are independent roots.

In its human and Qur’anic use, ḥayḍ names menstruation. Fayḍ can concern flowing water or the spread and abundance of other things.

The verbs’ resemblance in describing a torrent does not make every bodily liquid flow menstruation.

Study of F Y Ḍ

Ḥ Y Ḍ (ḥayḍ) and Ḥ W Ḍ (ḥawḍ): flowing and a place of accumulation

Ḥawḍ denotes a place where water collects.

Some lexical explanations propose a connection between liquid flowing toward a place and the name ḥawḍ.

Yet Ḥ W Ḍ and Ḥ Y Ḍ do not have identical radicals.

The proximity of wāw and yāʾ, and their alternation in some Arabic forms, does not by itself establish that all forms of these families share one historical root.

In familiar use, ḥawḍ also concerns a place where liquid collects, whereas Qur’anic ḥayḍ concerns menstruation.

Ḥ Y Ḍ (ḥayḍ) and N F S (nifās): menstruation and bleeding after childbirth

In Arabic, nifās denotes a woman’s state after childbirth and its associated bleeding.

Early sources sometimes record overlapping uses between forms associated with menstruation and postpartum bleeding. For example, Ibn Fāris mentions a woman who has recently given birth in his explanation of Ḥ Y Ḍ.

Yet in the established uses of these terms, periodic menstruation and postpartum bleeding are distinct.

Postpartum bleeding also differs medically from ordinary menstruation.

NHS: postpartum bleeding and the separate return of periods

Bleeding alone therefore does not justify identifying either their meanings or their rulings.

The Qur’an does not use nifās itself as a term for postpartum bleeding. By contrast, al-maḥīḍ and yaḥiḍna explicitly concern menstruation.

Study of N F S

Ḥ Y Ḍ (ḥayḍ) and Ṭ H R (ṭuhr): menstruation and becoming clean

Qur’anic Ṭ H R concerns purity, cleanliness, and becoming clean.

It appears alongside menstruation in Al-Baqarah 2:222, but their meanings should not be merged.

Ḥayḍ concerns menstruation, while ṭahārah concerns a state of cleanliness or the process of cleansing.

Their association does not make a menstruating woman impure in character or human worth.

The context concerns marital relations and a restriction during menstruation, rather than a judgment about women’s moral value.

Study of Ṭ H R

Ḥ Y Ḍ (ḥayḍ) and ʾ Dh Y (adhā): a bodily phenomenon and discomfort

Adhā describes al-maḥīḍ in Al-Baqarah 2:222.

Menstruation and discomfort, however, are not synonyms.

Menstruation names a particular phenomenon. Discomfort, distress, or harm is a description applied to it in this verse.

Al-maḥīḍ therefore cannot be translated as discomfort in every use.

In Al-Ṭalāq 65:4, for example, yaʾisna mina al-maḥīḍi means the end of expecting menstruation, rather than despairing of discomfort in general.

Study of ʾ Dh Y

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

In a direct translation, menstruation is suitable for al-maḥīḍ. In Persian, qāʿedagī is clearer to a general reader, while ḥayḍ is closer to the Arabic word and an established term in religious texts.

At the beginning of Al-Baqarah 2:222, wa-yasʾalūnaka ʿani al-maḥīḍi can be rendered “And they ask you about menstruation.”

For fa-ʿtazilū al-nisāʾa fī al-maḥīḍi, “So keep apart from women during menstruation” is suitable, since it naturally conveys the word’s temporal possibility.

Explaining the scope of keeping apart and its relation to sexual intercourse requires the verse’s connected phrases. It should not be presented as an independent meaning of maḥīḍ itself.

In Al-Ṭalāq 65:4, “have lost hope of menstruation” stays close to yaʾisna mina al-maḥīḍi. More idiomatically, one can say “no longer expect menstruation.”

For wa-allāʾī lam yaḥiḍna, however, “and those who have not menstruated” is the more suitable direct rendering.

The phrase should not be replaced without warrant by “prepubescent girls,” “ill women,” “infertile women,” or “women who have reached a particular age.” Such referents may be examined, but the verb itself does not independently specify any one of them.

For Forqan’s word boxes, the general and contextual meanings can also be distinguished as follows:

الْمَحِيضِ (al-maḥīḍi) — Al-Baqarah 2:222, first occurrence

  • Brief root meaning: menstruation; periodic menstrual blood flow.
  • Meaning in this verse: the phenomenon of menstruation about which the question is asked.

الْمَحِيضِ (al-maḥīḍi) — Al-Baqarah 2:222, second occurrence

  • Brief root meaning: menstruation; periodic menstrual blood flow.
  • Meaning in this verse: the menstrual period or state in which the instruction to keep apart applies.

الْمَحِيضِ (al-maḥīḍi) — Al-Ṭalāq 65:4

  • Brief root meaning: menstruation; periodic menstrual blood flow.
  • Meaning in this verse: menstruation that a group of women no longer expects to occur.

يَحِضْنَ (yaḥiḍna) — Al-Ṭalāq 65:4

  • Brief root meaning: to menstruate.
  • Meaning in this verse: to have menstruated; with lam, “have not menstruated.”

This distinction shows that, despite being the same word, al-maḥīḍ does not play an identical role in its three positions.

8. Conclusion

In Arabic, Ḥ Y Ḍ is associated with liquid flowing and issuing forth. In its human and Qur’anic use, it denotes women’s periodic menstrual bleeding.

The witnesses concerning torrents and a tree’s reddish discharge illuminate the family’s lexical background, but the historical order of these meanings’ development is uncertain.

Qur’anic al-maḥīḍ refers to menstruation itself and also occurs in a construction that may denote its period or place. Yaḥiḍna expresses menstruation occurring.

Al-Baqarah 2:222 relates menstruation to discomfort and a restriction concerning marital intercourse. Al-Ṭalāq 65:4 uses the same phenomenon to identify women who no longer menstruate or have not menstruated, and to determine a waiting period after divorce.

These differences show that Qur’anic ḥayḍ names a specific bodily phenomenon, rather than women’s baseness, sin, or lack of worth.

Rulings concerning marital relations, cleanliness, and the waiting period are understood from the verses’ constructions and contexts. They should not become part of the root’s lexical definition.

Final definition: In its recognized Qur’anic use, Ḥ Y Ḍ denotes women’s periodic menstrual blood flow. Maḥīḍ may express menstruation itself, its period, or its place; yaḥiḍna is a verb expressing menstruation occurring. The precise sense and accompanying rulings depend on the sentence’s construction and the verse’s context. Brief root meaning: Menstruation and menstrual blood flow; maḥīḍ may denote menstruation itself, its time, or its place, according to context.
