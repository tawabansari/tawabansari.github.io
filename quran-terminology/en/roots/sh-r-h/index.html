Opening, spreading, and revealing: from material uses in early Arabic to opening the breast to faith, disbelief, and responsibility in the Quran

In early Arabic, SH R Ḥ denotes opening, spreading, and making something manifest. Material uses include cutting and spreading meat, exposing its parts. Nonmaterial uses concern inward openness, clarifying something obscure, or readiness to accept something.

شَرَحَ الشَّيْءَ means opening and revealing something; شَرَحَ اللَّحْمَ means cutting and spreading meat; شَرَحَ الصَّدْرَ means opening and expanding the breast.

Opening an object, clarifying a matter, and inward openness are semantically connected, but they are not all physical operations. Explaining a difficult matter makes its hidden meaning intelligible rather than cutting its body. Likewise, opening the breast concerns inward capacity, not necessarily a physical opening of the chest.

The root occurs five times in the Quran, always as a Form I verb: Al-Anam 6:125, Al-Nahl 16:106, Ta Ha 20:25, Al-Zumar 39:22, and Al-Sharh 94:1.

All five concern ṣadr, the breast. Their direction and outcome differ: four concern guidance, prophetic mission, or divine assistance; Al-Nahl 16:106 concerns someone opening their breast to disbelief.

The latter is decisive for understanding the root: sharḥ itself means neither faith nor disbelief. It expresses opening and receptivity; their value and direction are established by what is received and by the context.

1. The root in early Arabic

Material opening and spreading

Older lexicons record opening, revealing, and spreading. A familiar material use is cutting and opening up meat.

شَرَحَ اللَّحْمَ describes cutting meat, particularly lengthwise into broad or thin pieces. شَرِيحَة denotes a piece of meat, while تَشْرِيح describes cutting and separating its parts.

An anonymous rajaz passage preserved in older lexicons says:

كَمْ قَدْ أَكَلْتُ كَبِدًا وَإِنْفَحَهْ
ثُمَّ ادَّخَرْتُ أَلْيَةً مُشَرَّحَهْ

kam qad akaltu kabidan wa-infaḥah / thumma iddakhartu alyatan musharraḥah

“How much liver and the stomach of a suckling young animal I have eaten; then I stored a sliced fat tail.”

مُشَرَّحَة describes a fat tail cut into portions.

This illustrates material cutting and opening. Its poet and precise date have not been independently established. Preservation in older dictionaries is valuable lexical evidence, but not conclusive evidence of a pre-Islamic date.

A line attributed to Durayd ibn al-Ṣimmah, a pre-Islamic poet who lived into the early Islamic period, uses مَشْرَح for part of a woman's body:

فَإِنَّكَ وَاعْتِذَارَكَ مِنْ سُوَيْدٍ
كَحَائِضَةٍ وَمَشْرَحُهَا يَسِيلُ

fa-innaka wa-iʿtidhāraka min Suwaydin / ka-ḥāʾiḍatin wa-mashraḥuhā yasīl

The poet compares a man's denial of involvement in Suwayd's death to a menstruating woman's denial contradicted by visible bodily evidence.

Mashraḥ here names part of the female genital anatomy. Versions differ in their wording. The line illustrates a form attributed to a pre-Islamic poet, but establishes neither the complete meaning of opening the breast nor the historical priority of cutting meat.

This study has not established an independent, securely dated pre-Quranic witness for inward sharḥ al-ṣadr. The Quran itself provides clear evidence for the expression, without settling how widely it circulated earlier.

Opening and cutting are recorded in older lexicons. Which usage was historically first in the entire family remains uncertain.

From opening things to clarifying meaning

Sharḥ also describes revealing and clarifying an obscure matter.

شَرَحَ أَمْرَهُ can mean making one's matter clear; شَرَحَ الْغَامِضَ means explaining something difficult or obscure.

Here, sharḥ is more than adding words: it unfolds a subject and reveals what was previously unclear to the listener.

For example, explaining a complex issue compressed into a few sentences can clarify the relation between its parts, reasons, and conclusion. The issue becomes open to the listener's understanding.

This connection between opening and clarification makes the educational and scholarly use of sharḥ intelligible.

Today's widespread expressions for commentaries on books, reports, or problems do not prove that every specialized usage was equally widespread before Islam. Each term's attestation and history require separate examination.

None of the five Quranic occurrences concerns explaining a text or lesson. All concern opening the breast.

Inward openness and receptivity

With ṣadr, the image of opening applies to the inward human condition rather than a material space.

A person may experience constriction, burden, resistance, or incapacity. Conversely, understanding, assurance, and strength may bring greater readiness to accept or undertake something.

Ordinary language likewise speaks of an opened breast, an open heart, or no longer feeling constrained about a matter.

Such expressions describe a real change in disposition without asserting a physically enlarged rib cage.

Quranic sharḥ al-ṣadr concerns this openness, with its precise sense determined by each verse's object and context.

Opening the breast for Islam directs receptivity toward submission to The God. Opening it to disbelief gives that receptivity a different direction.

The verb alone therefore establishes neither virtue nor the truth or correctness of what is accepted.

2. Important forms and derivatives

The following forms belong to the Arabic SH R Ḥ family.

شَرَحَ / يَشْرَحُ: opened, spread, revealed, or expanded; with ṣadr, it expresses inward opening.

شَرْح: opening, spreading, revealing, or clarifying; with speech, explanation and exposition of meaning.

شَرَّحَ: cut something and separated its parts; with meat, sliced or divided it.

تَشْرِيح: cutting and opening parts of a body. Its modern specialized anatomical and medical use belongs to this family.

شَرْحَة: a cut piece of meat.

شَرِيحَة: a thin or broad slice of meat or another material. Its modern use for a social segment should not be projected onto pre-Islamic Arabic.

شَرَائِح: the plural of sharīḥah.

شَرِيح: in lexical usage, a piece of meat, especially a broad or elongated piece.

شَارِح: someone who opens or explains. Older regional usage, including Yemen, also records a guardian of crops. Its historical relationship to opening is unclear and should not be invented.

مَشْرُوح: opened, spread out, or explained.

اِنْشَرَحَ: became open or spacious; with ṣadr, experienced inward openness or relief.

اِنْشِرَاح: inward expansion and openness, also associated in contemporary usage with ease of mind or a pleasant condition.

اِسْتَشْرَحَ: requested explanation or exposition.

Only Form I verbs of sharaḥa occur in the Quran. The nouns sharḥ, tashrīḥ, sharīḥah, shāriḥ, mashrūḥ, and inshirāḥ do not occur there in those forms.

No derivative appears as a divine name or epithet. The God performs the opening in certain verses, which differs from being named by a derivative.

3. Quranic uses: different directions of inward opening

Al-Anam 6:125: opening the breast for Islam

فَمَنْ يُرِدِ اللَّهُ أَنْ يَهْدِيَهُ يَشْرَحْ صَدْرَهُ لِلْإِسْلَامِ ۖ وَمَنْ يُرِدْ أَنْ يُضِلَّهُ يَجْعَلْ صَدْرَهُ ضَيِّقًا حَرَجًا كَأَنَّمَا يَصَّعَّدُ فِي السَّمَاءِ ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ يَجْعَلُ اللَّهُ الرِّجْسَ عَلَى الَّذِينَ لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ

fa-man yuridi allāhu an yahdiyahu yashraḥ ṣadrahu li-l-islāmi wa-man yurid an yuḍillahu yajʿal ṣadrahu ḍayyiqan ḥarajan ka-annamā yaṣṣaʿʿadu fī al-samāʾi kadhālika yajʿalu allāhu al-rijsa ʿalā alladhīna lā yuʾminūn

Al-Anʿām 6:125

“Whomever The God wills to guide, He opens his breast for submission; and whomever He wills to lead astray, He makes his breast tight and constricted, as though laboriously ascending into the sky. Thus The God places defilement upon those who do not believe.”

The verse explains opening the breast through a clear contrast.

The first part presents openness; the second has ضَيِّقًا حَرَجًا, tight and constricted.

Here, opening and spaciousness stand against constriction and pressure.

The purpose of this spaciousness is specified by the following phrase.

The verse says لِلْإِسْلَامِ, for Islam.

Islam belongs to S L M and here concerns submission to The God. Opening the breast describes inward readiness for that submission.

This should not be reduced to adopting a religious label or outward membership in a religious community. Someone may bear a Muslim identity while resisting truth, justice, or divine command.

The verse connects openness for Islam with divine guidance: inward receptivity to the path toward which The God guides.

The contrasting constriction is a lack of openness toward that path.

كَأَنَّمَا يَصَّعَّدُ فِي السَّمَاءِ depicts intense difficulty and constriction. Sharḥ itself does not mean breathing or opening airways, and the comparison alone does not establish a specialized account of atmospheric pressure or oxygen at altitude.

The principal subject is openness or constriction toward guidance.

Al-Anam 6:124 describes people imposing their own conditions for belief when a sign comes to them. Al-Anam 6:126 then speaks of the Lord's straight path.

Sharḥ al-ṣadr here belongs to guidance, human responses to divine signs, and receptivity to truth.

Attributing this openness to divine will should not be taken to make human choice or responsibility meaningless. The verse itself mentions people who do not believe, and neighboring verses describe their conduct.

Yashraḥ alone proves neither compelled belief nor the negation of human agency. It expresses opening the breast; divine will and human responsibility require consideration of the wider guidance passages.

Al-Nahl 16:106: opening the breast to disbelief

مَنْ كَفَرَ بِاللَّهِ مِنْ بَعْدِ إِيمَانِهِ إِلَّا مَنْ أُكْرِهَ وَقَلْبُهُ مُطْمَئِنٌّ بِالْإِيمَانِ وَلَٰكِنْ مَنْ شَرَحَ بِالْكُفْرِ صَدْرًا فَعَلَيْهِمْ غَضَبٌ مِنَ اللَّهِ وَلَهُمْ عَذَابٌ عَظِيمٌ

man kafara bi-llāhi min baʿdi īmānihi illā man ukriha wa-qalbuhu muṭmaʾinnun bi-l-īmāni wa-lākin man sharaḥa bi-l-kufri ṣadran fa-ʿalayhim ghaḍabun mina allāhi wa-lahum ʿadhābun ʿaẓīm

Al-Naḥl 16:106

“Whoever disbelieves in The God after believing, except one compelled while their heart remains secure in faith; but those who open a breast to disbelief incur wrath from The God and have a tremendous punishment.”

This verse is crucial for understanding SH R Ḥ.

In Al-Anam 6:125, The God opens the breast for Islam. Here, the human being performs the opening and its direction is disbelief.

Sharḥ al-ṣadr therefore does not inherently mean accepting truth or receiving guidance.

A person may become receptive to truth or to disbelief.

The moral value depends on what is accepted and the circumstances of acceptance.

The verse distinguishes two situations.

First, someone compelled while their heart remains assured in faith.

Second, someone opening their breast to disbelief.

In the first, external pressure may produce speech or behavior inconsistent with inward belief. The Quran exempts such a person from the condemned group.

In the second, opening the breast to disbelief concerns inward acceptance.

The distinction thus concerns coercion, inward condition, and the direction of acceptance, beyond outward speech alone.

Al-Nahl 16:107 explains the second group's condemnation:

ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمُ اسْتَحَبُّوا الْحَيَاةَ الدُّنْيَا عَلَى الْآخِرَةِ

dhālika bi-annahumu istaḥabbū al-ḥayāta al-dunyā ʿalā al-ākhirah

Al-Naḥl 16:107

“That is because they preferred worldly life to the Hereafter.”

This identifies an orientation and preference that influences conduct.

A person may abandon something previously recognized and accepted as true to preserve advantage, standing, social credit, or comfort.

Attributing such motives to a particular person requires evidence. Opposition, questioning, doubt, or changed belief alone does not establish knowing concealment of truth.

The passage concerns disbelief after faith and the distinction between coercion and inward acceptance. It should not be extended to condemn every questioner or seeker of truth.

In شَرَحَ بِالْكُفْرِ صَدْرًا, sharaḥa is a past verb with an implicit subject referring to man. Ṣadran is indefinite and accusative: one analysis makes it a direct object, another a specification with sharaḥa understood in the sense of ṭāba. Bi-al-kufr is connected to the verb by the preposition bāʾ.

This differs from شرح صدره للإسلام in Al-Anam 6:125. There, lām specifies the direction of opening; here, bāʾ connects it to disbelief.

The prepositional difference alone does not explain the complete moral contrast. Agent, direction, coercion, and context also matter.

The verse establishes openness as the root's meaning, rather than guidance, faith, or acceptance of truth in themselves.

Ta Ha 20:25: Moses asks for an open breast

When The God commands Moses to go to Pharaoh, Moses prays:

قَالَ رَبِّ اشْرَحْ لِي صَدْرِي

qāla rabbi ishraḥ lī ṣadrī

Ṭā Hā 20:25

“He said: My Lord, open my breast for me.”

His requests continue:

Ta Ha 20:26

وَيَسِّرْ لِي أَمْرِي

wa-yassir lī amrī

Ṭā Hā 20:26

“And make my task easy for me.”

Ta Ha 20:27

وَاحْلُلْ عُقْدَةً مِنْ لِسَانِي

wa-uḥlul ʿuqdatan min lisānī

Ṭā Hā 20:27

“And loosen a knot from my tongue.”

Ta Ha 20:28

يَفْقَهُوا قَوْلِي

yafqahū qawlī

Ṭā Hā 20:28

“So that they may understand my speech.”

In Ta Ha 20:29 and Ta Ha 20:30, Moses also asks that Aaron be appointed his helper.

These requests help explain sharḥ al-ṣadr.

Moses faces a demanding mission: going to Pharaoh and conveying the divine message.

Here, opening the breast can express the inward capacity needed to accept and perform that responsibility: readiness for difficulties, perseverance, and fulfilling the mission.

It should not absorb every request that follows.

وَيَسِّرْ لِي أَمْرِي asks for the task to be made easy.

وَاحْلُلْ عُقْدَةً مِنْ لِسَانِي concerns loosening a knot from the tongue.

يَفْقَهُوا قَوْلِي states the aim of making the speech understood.

Inward opening, an easier task, a loosened tongue, and understood speech are related but not interchangeable.

Someone may speak well yet feel constrained, afraid, or incapable before a heavy responsibility. Conversely, someone inwardly ready may still need help expressing their message clearly.

Moses' prayer places these different aspects together.

Ishraḥ does not itself establish a request for cutting open his physical chest or altering its anatomy.

The mission context concerns his inward capacity and readiness.

In Ta Ha 20:36, The God answers:

قَالَ قَدْ أُوتِيتَ سُؤْلَكَ يَا مُوسَىٰ

qāla qad ūtīta suʾlaka yā mūsā

Ṭā Hā 20:36

“He said: You have been granted your request, Moses.”

The requests are thus accepted as divine assistance for the mission.

Opening the breast here concerns capacity and readiness for responsibility, beyond mere happiness, carefree calm, or personal comfort.

Al-Zumar 39:22: openness, light, and hardened hearts

أَفَمَنْ شَرَحَ اللَّهُ صَدْرَهُ لِلْإِسْلَامِ فَهُوَ عَلَىٰ نُورٍ مِنْ رَبِّهِ ۚ فَوَيْلٌ لِلْقَاسِيَةِ قُلُوبُهُمْ مِنْ ذِكْرِ اللَّهِ ۚ أُولَٰئِكَ فِي ضَلَالٍ مُبِينٍ

a-fa-man sharaḥa allāhu ṣadrahu li-l-islāmi fa-huwa ʿalā nūrin min rabbihi fa-waylun li-l-qāsiyati qulūbuhum min dhikri allāhi ulāʾika fī ḍalālin mubīn

Al-Zumar 39:22

“Is someone whose breast The God has opened for submission, and who stands upon a light from his Lord…? Woe, then, to those whose hearts are hardened toward the remembrance of The God; they are in manifest error.”

As in Al-Anam 6:125, opening the breast for Islam is attributed to The God.

Here, openness is linked to light and contrasted with hardened hearts.

فَهُوَ عَلَىٰ نُورٍ مِنْ رَبِّهِ describes the person whose breast has been opened for Islam.

Sharaḥa itself does not mean light. Light appears in a separate phrase concerning guidance and illumination of the way.

قَاسِيَةِ قُلُوبُهُمْ belongs to Q S W and describes hardened hearts.

Opening the breast and hardness of heart are contrasting conditions expressed by different word families.

Al-Zumar 39:23 describes the skins of those who fear their Lord shivering at the finest discourse, followed by their skins and hearts softening toward His remembrance:

ثُمَّ تَلِينُ جُلُودُهُمْ وَقُلُوبُهُمْ إِلَىٰ ذِكْرِ اللَّهِ

thumma talīnu julūduhum wa-qulūbuhum ilā dhikri allāh

Al-Zumar 39:23

“Then their skins and hearts soften toward the remembrance of The God.”

This develops the connection between inward openness, remembrance, and softening hearts.

Līn, softness, is nevertheless not identical with sharḥ. Openness, softness, and light may accompany guidance without becoming exact lexical synonyms.

Sharḥ al-ṣadr in Al-Zumar 39:22 concerns openness to submission to The God. Light, remembrance, and softened hearts are related aspects explained by the surrounding verses.

This openness should not be reduced to claiming faith or belonging to a religious group. An outwardly professed believer may resist a truth conflicting with personal interests or inherited convictions.

The criterion is openness to Islam, submission to The God, rather than a social or religious title alone.

Al-Sharh 94:1: the Prophet's open breast and lifted burden

Al-Sharh begins with a question:

أَلَمْ نَشْرَحْ لَكَ صَدْرَكَ

a-lam nashraḥ laka ṣadrak

Al-Sharḥ 94:1

“Did We not open your breast for you?”

It continues:

Al-Sharh 94:2

وَوَضَعْنَا عَنْكَ وِزْرَكَ

wa-waḍaʿnā ʿanka wizrak

Al-Sharḥ 94:2

“And did We not lift your burden from you?”

Al-Sharh 94:3

الَّذِي أَنْقَضَ ظَهْرَكَ

alladhī anqaḍa ẓahrak

Al-Sharḥ 94:3

“Which had weighed heavily on your back.”

Al-Sharh 94:4

وَرَفَعْنَا لَكَ ذِكْرَكَ

wa-rafaʿnā laka dhikrak

Al-Sharḥ 94:4

“And did We not raise your remembrance for you?”

The surah places opening the breast beside lifting a burden and raising the Prophet's remembrance.

Together, these verses concern the assistance and relief The God has provided.

Sharaḥa itself does not mean lifting a burden: Al-Sharh 94:2 expresses that through وَوَضَعْنَا.

Wizr should not automatically be assigned the meaning of the Prophet's personal sin without contextual examination. It denotes a heavy burden, whose nature depends on the construction and context.

The surah recalls several divine gifts and forms of relief.

Al-Sharh 94:5 then says:

فَإِنَّ مَعَ الْعُسْرِ يُسْرًا

fa-inna maʿa al-ʿusri yusrā

Al-Sharḥ 94:5

“For indeed, with hardship comes ease.”

This connection between hardship and ease supports understanding the inward opening.

It can describe the capacity needed to bear responsibility, face difficulties, and continue onward.

It does not establish that every difficulty disappeared from the Prophet's life.

The surah itself mentions hardship and concludes in Al-Sharh 94:7 and Al-Sharh 94:8 by directing continued effort and turning to the Lord.

Opening the breast therefore need not mean a difficulty-free life; it can express inward capacity in facing responsibility and hardship.

Does opening the breast mean cutting the physical chest?

Some historical and religious reports connect the Prophet's opening of the breast with a physical event in which his chest was opened.

Al-Sharh itself does not directly describe cutting the chest, removing an organ, or performing surgery.

Nashraḥ is the same verb used elsewhere for inward openness toward guidance, Islam, and even disbelief.

Al-Nahl 16:106 speaks of opening the breast to disbelief; it cannot be turned into an anatomical operation without supporting evidence.

Inward opening and spaciousness therefore have direct Quranic support for understanding أَلَمْ نَشْرَحْ لَكَ صَدْرَكَ.

The historical assessment of a report describing a physical event is a separate issue. Its existence alone does not require replacing inward openness with surgery as this verse's lexical meaning.

4. Core meaning and neighboring words

SH R Ḥ compared with B S Ṭ

Basṭ concerns spreading something or extending its reach.

It may be material, such as extending a hand, or nonmaterial, such as expanding resources or provision.

Sharḥ also belongs to opening and spreading, but Quranic usage concerns the breast and inward spaciousness.

The images overlap, without making every use of basṭ equivalent to sharḥ.

In Al-Ankabut 29:62, The God expands or restricts provision for whom He wills. That concerns livelihood and resources rather than opening the breast.

Expansion is shared, but each object's nature and the sentence determine the precise meaning.

SH R Ḥ compared with F T Ḥ

Fatḥ concerns opening something closed, obstructed, or inaccessible.

It may apply to a gate, a way, a judgment, or other matters.

Sharḥ also expresses opening, emphasizing inward spaciousness and capacity in Quranic usage.

Fatḥ naturally describes opening a closed door, while sharḥ supplies the image of opening the breast to accept truth.

This does not restrict one root to material and the other to nonmaterial uses. Both have wide ranges.

These five passages use sharḥ al-ṣadr; it should not be equated without evidence with a particular sense of fatḥ elsewhere.

SH R Ḥ compared with W S ʿ

Wusʿ concerns breadth, capacity, and ability.

Al-Baqarah 2:286 says:

لَا يُكَلِّفُ اللَّهُ نَفْسًا إِلَّا وُسْعَهَا

lā yukallifu allāhu nafsan illā wusʿahā

Al-Baqarah 2:286

“The God does not charge any soul beyond its capacity.”

Wusʿ here concerns a person's capacity and ability.

Opening the breast may accompany increased readiness or inward capacity, but sharḥ does not mean unlimited ability.

Moses requests inward opening for his mission while separately asking for an easier task and a helper.

Sharḥ al-ṣadr can enlarge receptivity and capacity for action, without being synonymous with every ability and resource.

SH R Ḥ compared with Ḍ Y Q and Ḥ R J

These two roots directly contrast with opening the breast in Al-Anam 6:125.

Ḍīq denotes narrowness and restricted space or possibility.

Ḥaraj here adds constriction and difficulty.

ضَيِّقًا حَرَجًا emphasizes intense inward restriction and pressure.

By contrast, يشرح صدره للإسلام expresses openness for accepting submission.

This Quranic opposition to constriction is stronger lexical evidence than a hypothetical image assigned to the root.

SH R Ḥ compared with Q S W

Qaswah concerns hardness and inflexibility.

Al-Zumar 39:22 contrasts openness for Islam with hearts hardened toward divine remembrance.

Openness can thus imply receptivity, while hardness can imply resistance to reminder.

Lexically, however, hardness is not the same as narrowness, nor is opening identical with softening.

One image concerns openness and capacity; the other concerns hardness and inflexibility.

The Quran brings these distinct images together to explain inward human conditions.

SH R Ḥ compared with F S R and B Y N

Sharḥ, tafsīr, and tabyīn overlap in scholarly and educational language.

Sharḥ can unfold a subject and reveal its components.

Tafsīr clarifies meaning and explains what requires understanding.

Tabyīn likewise makes something manifest and clear.

Their frequent overlap today does not establish complete historical synonymy.

The Quran does not use SH R Ḥ for explaining a text, whereas bayān, tabyīn, and tafṣīl belong to discussions of clarifying the message and signs.

The modern sense of commentary on a book must therefore not be imposed on these five occurrences.

SH R Ḥ compared with S R Ḥ and SH R ʿ

These roots resemble one another in letters but remain distinct.

S R Ḥ concerns releasing and sending forth, including sending livestock to pasture.

SH R ʿ concerns an evident or established way and meanings related to prescribed law.

SH R Ḥ concerns opening, spreading, and opening the breast.

Two shared letters or visual resemblance do not establish a common historical origin or identical meaning.

5. Morphology and syntax

Only Form I occurs, but the five verses differ in construction and agent.

In Al-Anam 6:125, يَشْرَحْ is an imperfect jussive in the result clause of man. Its implicit subject refers to The God, and صَدْرَهُ is its object.

In Al-Nahl 16:106, شَرَحَ is a past verb whose subject refers to man, the person opening their breast to disbelief. صَدْرًا is indefinite and accusative, analyzed either as the object or as a specification under the alternative interpretation noted above.

In Ta Ha 20:25, اشْرَحْ is imperative in form but functions as Moses' prayerful request to The God. لِي means for Moses, and صَدْرِي is the object.

In Al-Zumar 39:22, شَرَحَ is past tense, اللَّهُ its explicit subject, صَدْرَهُ its object, and لِلْإِسْلَامِ the direction of opening.

In Al-Sharh 94:1, نَشْرَحْ is imperfect, made jussive by lam, within the question أَلَمْ نَشْرَحْ. Despite the imperfect form, this construction recalls an accomplished act.

لَكَ refers to the Prophet and صَدْرَكَ to his breast.

The shared feature is that opening concerns ṣadr in all five passages.

Agent and direction are not identical throughout.

In Al-Anam and Al-Zumar, The God opens the breast for Islam.

In Ta Ha, Moses asks The God for that opening.

In Al-Sharh, The God reminds the Prophet of the opening already granted.

In Al-Nahl, a human opens their own breast to disbelief.

Verb form alone cannot yield a fixed theory of human freedom or compulsion. Agent, direction, and the situation described must all be considered.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Translations should distinguish material opening, clarification of meaning, and inward expansion.

شَرَحَ: “opened,” “spread,” or “clarified,” according to its object.

شَرْح: “opening,” “spreading,” or, with discourse, “explanation” and “clear exposition.”

شَرَّحَ: “cut into portions” or “dissected,” depending on usage.

تَشْرِيح: cutting and opening bodily parts; in specialized usage, dissection.

شَرِيحَة: “slice,” “thin piece,” or “separated portion.”

شَارِح: “explainer” or “commentator”; other specialized senses depend on context.

مَشْرُوح: “opened,” “spread,” or “explained.”

اِنْشَرَحَ: “became open or spacious”; inwardly, “felt openness or relief.”

اِنْشِرَاح: “inward opening,” “spaciousness of mind,” or “ease of mind.”

The following renderings suit the five Quranic uses. They do not alter the installed verse translations.

Al-Anam 6:125

يَشْرَحْ صَدْرَهُ لِلْإِسْلَامِ

yashraḥ ṣadrahu li-l-islām

Al-Anʿām 6:125

“He opens his breast for submission.”

“Opens” retains the verb's image; “for submission” retains its direction.

Al-Nahl 16:106

وَلَٰكِنْ مَنْ شَرَحَ بِالْكُفْرِ صَدْرًا

wa-lākin man sharaḥa bi-l-kufri ṣadrā

Al-Naḥl 16:106

“But whoever opens a breast to disbelief.”

Opening to disbelief naturally conveys inward receptivity and acceptance.

Ta Ha 20:25

رَبِّ اشْرَحْ لِي صَدْرِي

rabbi ishraḥ lī ṣadrī

Ṭā Hā 20:25

“My Lord, open my breast for me.”

“Open” is more precise here than “explain,” since no explanation of a subject is being requested.

Al-Zumar 39:22

أَفَمَنْ شَرَحَ اللَّهُ صَدْرَهُ لِلْإِسْلَامِ

a-fa-man sharaḥa allāhu ṣadrahu li-l-islām

Al-Zumar 39:22

“Is someone whose breast The God has opened for submission…”

Al-Sharh 94:1

أَلَمْ نَشْرَحْ لَكَ صَدْرَكَ

a-lam nashraḥ laka ṣadrak

Al-Sharḥ 94:1

“Did We not open your breast for you?”

Opening and expanding preserve the root's central image throughout.

Each verse's explanation must still identify its relation to guidance, disbelief, prophetic mission, or divine assistance.

Calming, cheering, believing, accepting truth, and empowering may describe contextual results, but should not replace the verb's meaning without evidence.

“Cutting open the chest” is unsuitable as the basic rendering of these passages: it strongly suggests a physical operation and can replace inward expansion with bodily surgery.

7. Conclusion

SH R Ḥ concerns opening, spreading, and revealing. Material uses include slicing and spreading meat; nonmaterial uses concern clarification and inward openness.

The evidence establishes this semantic range without proving that every nonmaterial sense developed after the material one. The pre-Quranic circulation of sharḥ al-ṣadr likewise requires independent evidence.

Only Form I verbs occur in the Quran, all five concerning ṣadr.

Al-Anam 6:125 and Al-Zumar 39:22 attribute opening for Islam, submission to The God, to Him. In Ta Ha 20:25, Moses requests it for his demanding mission. Al-Sharh 94:1 recalls it as a divine gift to the Prophet.

Al-Nahl 16:106 instead describes a human opening the breast to disbelief. The expression is therefore neither a name for faith nor automatic evidence of guidance: its direction depends on what is received.

A defensible central meaning is opening and expanding. Release from constriction or obscurity, and readiness for acceptance, manifestation, or use, arise according to the particular usage rather than being necessary components of every derivative.

Because the Quranic verb concerns ṣadr, it expresses inward spaciousness and receptivity: a capacity for attention, acceptance, or readiness for a direction and responsibility.

“Opening the breast” is consequently the basic shared rendering. Guidance, disbelief, calm, or readiness for a mission should come from the individual verse's context rather than being built into the root in advance.
