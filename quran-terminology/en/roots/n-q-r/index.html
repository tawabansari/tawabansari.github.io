A study of naqr in early Arabic and the Qur’an: from birds pecking and carving stone to the date-stone naqīr and the nāqūr in the scene of resurrection

The three-consonant root N Q R is associated in Arabic with striking something, pecking, digging, and leaving a mark or hollow in its surface. A bird pecks at a seed or the ground with its beak; a person strikes stone with an implement and makes a hollow in it, or produces a sound through short taps. These are established uses of the N Q R family.

The same root gives such words as minqār for a bird’s beak or a carving implement, nuqrah for a depression, naqīr for a small hollow in a date stone, and nāqūr for an instrument that is sounded.

The family’s uses extend beyond physical striking. In Arabic, naqr and tanqīr have also been used for searching and close examination, finding hidden things, selecting people or objects, and even finding fault. In these branches, searching, investigation, or affecting another person becomes prominent.

The root N Q R occurs four times in three Qur’anic verses: naqīran twice in Al-Nisāʾ, and nuqira together with al-nāqūr once in Al-Muddaththir.

These uses belong to two different settings. In Al-Nisāʾ, naqīr expresses an exceedingly small amount: once concerning people who give others nothing, however little, and once concerning divine justice, which deprives people of no right, however small. In Al-Muddaththir, the nāqūr is sounded at the opening of a difficult scene of resurrection.

Understanding this root requires distinguishing three things: the act of striking or pecking, the effect that striking produces, and the instrument or object through which sound is made. These concepts are related, but the root’s derivatives do not all mean the same thing.

1. Forms and semantic core

Important forms in Arabic include:

  • نَقَرَ، يَنْقُرُ (naqara, yanquru): to strike, peck, dig, or make a depression in something.
  • نَقْر (naqr): striking, pecking, or tapping; in some uses, the resulting sound.
  • نَقْرَة and نُقْرَة (naqrah and nuqrah): a mark left by striking, a depression, or a hollow, with differences in usage.
  • نَقِير (naqīr): the small hollow in a date stone; elsewhere, wood or a tree trunk hollowed out inside.
  • مِنْقَار (minqār): a bird’s beak or an iron implement for carving and piercing.
  • مُنْقُر and مِنْقَر (munqur and minqar): in some uses, a small well, or one with a narrow opening, dug in hard ground.
  • نَقَّار (naqqār): someone who works stone, wood, or metal by striking and carving; in some uses, a habitual faultfinder.
  • نَقَّرَ and تَنْقِير (naqqara and tanqīr): carving and making marks by striking; by extension, careful searching and examining details.
  • اِنْتَقَرَ and تَنَقَّرَ (intaqara and tanaqqara): to search, select something, or investigate carefully.
  • نَاقِر (nāqir): something that strikes; in some uses, an arrow that hits its target.
  • نَاقُور (nāqūr): an instrument that is sounded; in Qur’anic usage, a trumpet or horn.
  • مُنَاقَرَة (munāqarah): conversation, dispute, verbal confrontation, and sometimes mutual faultfinding.

Other lexical uses are also recorded, including nuqrah for a melted piece or ingot of gold or silver, nuqrah for certain bodily hollows, and al-nuqarah as the name of a disease in some livestock.

These uses require separate treatment; they should not all be deduced from a single image without historical investigation.

Only three morphological forms from this family occur in the Qur’an:

نُقِرَ (nuqira): a passive perfect verb of the basic stem, in Al-Muddaththir 74:8.

النَّاقُورِ (al-nāqūr): a noun denoting the instrument that is sounded, in Al-Muddaththir 74:8.

نَقِيرًا (naqīran): a noun used for the small hollow in a date stone, in Al-Nisāʾ 4:53 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:124.

Semantic core: a strike that leaves an effect

A significant semantic core identified for this family is striking something so as to produce a depression in it.

This explanation has a clear connection with the root’s principal uses.

When a bird pecks the ground, it may leave a small mark on the surface. When someone strikes stone with an implement, part of the stone may be removed or a hollow made in it. When something is sounded through short taps, the act of tapping and the resulting sound are connected.

Three related aspects can thus be identified in the family’s physical branch: striking, making a mark or depression, and, in some uses, producing sound.

However, not every instance of naqr necessarily pierces an object. A bird may peck and pick up a seed without piercing either the seed or the ground. A person may also make a short sound with the tongue or a finger without making a lasting hollow in anything.

The image of striking and leaving an effect is therefore useful for explaining the family, provided its limits are respected.

Nor can this semantic analysis alone establish that every N Q R derivative historically developed from precisely one initial physical act. It is a valid lexical explanation, rather than conclusive proof of the historical order of every branch.

2. N Q R in early Arabic: pecking, carving, and searching

Birds pecking

One of the clearest uses of this root concerns birds:

نَقَرَ الطَّائِرُ الْحَبَّةَ

naqara al-ṭāʾiru al-ḥabbata

Translation: “The bird pecked at the seed.”

Here, the bird strikes the seed with its beak and may pick it up and eat it.

Minqār is also the name of the familiar part of a bird’s body with which it pecks, takes food, and sometimes strikes the ground or other objects.

In Arabic, the same word has also been used for certain metal implements. The connection between a beak and a carving tool is understandable in both shape and function: either can strike something with its pointed end.

This resemblance does not, however, prove that the tool was necessarily named after the bird’s beak, or the reverse. Establishing which use came first requires independent evidence.

A poetic witness attributed to a pre-Islamic poet: a pecking bird

A well-known rajaz poem attributed to Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd, the pre-Islamic poet, says:

يَا لَكِ مِنْ قُبَّرَةٍ بِمَعْمَرِ
خَلَا لَكِ الْجَوُّ فَبِيضِي وَاصْفِرِي

yā laki min qubbaratin bi-maʿmari / khalā laki al-jawwu fa-bīḍī wa-iṣfirī

وَنَقِّرِي مَا شِئْتِ أَنْ تُنَقِّرِي

wa-naqqirī mā shiʾti an tunaqqirī

Translation:

“O lark in this dwelling place! The open air is yours now: lay your eggs, sing, and peck as much as you wish.”

In this passage, naqqirī is a Form II imperative referring to the bird’s pecking.

The poet depicts a bird free to move about its surroundings and peck at seeds or the ground. Maʿmar may mean a dwelling place; another explanation treats it as a particular place name.

The witness shows naqr used in early poetry for an ordinary activity of birds.

The attribution of this rajaz is nevertheless disputed. Alongside its attribution to Ṭarafah, Kulayb ibn Rabīʿah has also been named as its author, with a different account of the circumstances in which it was composed.

Some lines also differ in wording and order.

The rajaz therefore provides evidence from transmitted early Arabic, but its attribution to Ṭarafah should not be presented as certain without acknowledging the disagreement.

Carving and making a hollow in stone

Arabic also uses naqr for carving stone and other hard materials.

نَقَرَ الْحَجَرَ بِالْمِنْقَارِ

naqara al-ḥajara bi-al-minqāri

Translation: “He struck and carved the stone with a carving implement.”

Minqār here is an iron implement for carving or marking stone and wood.

Dhū al-Rummah, a poet of the Umayyad period, says:

كَأَرْحَاءِ رَقْدٍ زَلَّمَتْهَا الْمَنَاقِرُ

ka-arḥāʾi raqdin zallamathā al-manāqiru

Translation: “Like the millstones of Raqd, shaped by carving and cutting implements.”

Al-manāqir here refers to tools used for cutting and shaping stone.

The witness shows that naqr can refer to blows that remove part of a hard material and give it shape.

The line belongs to the Umayyad period, rather than the pre-Islamic period; it remains useful evidence for the continuity of the root’s use in classical Arabic.

The expression naqr al-kitāb fī al-ḥajar is also recorded for writing or engraving on stone.

Here, writing is carried out by making marks on a hard surface.

This does not mean that N Q R itself means writing in every setting. Writing is one activity that can be performed through naqr.

Small and large hollows

Nuqrah in Arabic denotes a hollow or depression.

It may occur in the ground, in stone, or in part of the body.

For example, nuqrah has been used for a small hollow in the ground. Certain natural bodily hollows, such as the hollow at the back of the head or an eye socket, are also described by the same word in lexical usage.

Some accounts also use nuqrah for melted, collected gold or silver. The meaning and naming history of this use require independent examination; without evidence, it should not necessarily be derived from the date-stone hollow.

In each case, the meaning of a lexical form must be distinguished from the actual size of what it denotes.

For example, naqīr in the Qur’an refers to something very small, whereas a well or hollowed tree trunk called naqīr in Arabic need not be small.

Smallness is therefore not a permanent feature of the entire N Q R family.

Naqīr: a piece of wood or tree trunk hollowed out inside

Early Arabic also records naqīr for a tree trunk or piece of wood whose interior has been dug out and hollowed.

Such a vessel could be used to store or prepare certain liquids.

Historical accounts of Arab life describe a date-palm trunk hollowed out in the middle and used to hold dates and water.

This use shows that naqīr names more than the small date-stone hollow: Arabic also uses it for a larger object made by digging and hollowing.

The principal feature here is the removal and hollowing of part of the wood.

In the Qur’an, however, the context of the two Al-Nisāʾ verses concerns an exceedingly small amount, rather than a wooden vessel or tree trunk.

All the lexical meanings of naqīr should therefore not be transferred into the Qur’anic verses at once.

Searching, examination, and faultfinding

In extended Arabic usage, naqqara ʿani al-amr means to search and investigate a matter.

For example:

نَقَّرَ عَنِ الْخَبَرِ

naqqara ʿani al-khabari

Translation: “He investigated the report.”

This use can be semantically connected with digging and probing, as though a person removes layers of a subject to discover something that is not apparent.

The image only explains how the meanings may be related; it does not establish a definite history for the development of the sense of investigation.

Arabic also records naqqār and munaqqir for people who investigate matters closely.

A faultfinding use has developed in this same field.

نَقَرَ الرَّجُلَ

naqara al-rajula

In some constructions, it means “he found fault with him” or “he spoke ill of him.”

This use has a figurative connection with striking or affecting another person, but no longer means a physical blow.

Intiqār in one use also refers to selecting people or inviting certain individuals from a group.

In such usage, a person chooses particular individuals from a larger body.

Searching, selection, and faultfinding are therefore recognized branches of the family, but they should not be made part of the meanings of naqīr or nāqūr in the Qur’an.

3. Naqīran in the Qur’an: the small hollow in a date stone

Naqīr is an important use of this root.

Lexical descriptions identify it as a small depression in a date stone.

Some describe it as a hollow on the back of the stone; other early explanations refer to a depression in its middle.

Although the precise location is described differently, the difference has no major effect on the meaning needed in the Qur’anic verses: a very small date-stone depression used to express an insignificant amount.

In Arabic, such objects can serve as examples of the smallest amount in a comparison.

For example, saying that someone gives nothing even the size of a naqīr does not mean that the person was asked to detach and give away the date-stone hollow. An exceedingly small amount is intended.

This manner of expression is connected with everyday life. The date stone was familiar, and its small depression could provide a clear image of an insignificant amount for the hearer.

An early poetic witness: naqīr as something insignificant

Labīd ibn Rabīʿah says in an elegy for his brother Arbad:

وَلَيْسَ النَّاسُ بَعْدَكَ فِي نَقِيرٍ
وَلَا هُمْ غَيْرُ أَصْدَاءٍ وَهَامِ

wa-laysa al-nāsu baʿdaka fī naqīrin / wa-lā hum ghayru aṣdāʾin wa-hāmi

Translation: “After you, people are worth to me not even the slightest thing; they are only ṣadā and hāmah birds, the birds imagined in connection with the dead.”

Naqīr here means something exceedingly insignificant. Aṣdāʾ and hām refer to an old image of birds associated with the dead, rather than echoes. This poetic image reflects an old belief; quoting it does not endorse that belief.

The poet is not discussing the actual hollow of a date stone; he makes it a measure of something’s lack of worth.

The witness shows how naqīr could extend from the name of a small depression to a figurative expression for an insignificant amount or value in Arabic.

Labīd was a mukhaḍram poet who lived through the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. Since this line belongs to an elegy for Arbad, it should not be presented as conclusively pre-Qur’anic without establishing its date of composition.

Its importance lies in showing an established use of naqīr in early Arabic.

4. Al-Nisāʾ 4:53: people who will not give even a naqīr

أَمْ لَهُمْ نَصِيبٌ مِّنَ الْمُلْكِ فَإِذًا لَّا يُؤْتُونَ النَّاسَ نَقِيرًا

am lahum naṣībun mina al-mulki fa-idhan lā yuʾtūna al-nāsa naqīran

Forqan translation:

“Or do they have a share of sovereignty? Then they would not give people even the hollow of a date stone.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:53

This is the first Qur’anic occurrence of naqīr.

It asks whether a group has a share of sovereignty, then says that, if they had such authority, they would not give people even a naqīr.

What does naqīr mean here?

Naqīr expresses an exceedingly small amount in this verse.

The word’s underlying meaning is the same small hollow in a date stone, but the sentence makes that hollow a measure of an insignificant amount.

The point is therefore not that people need a date-stone hollow and those in power refuse to give it.

The point is that, supposing they had power and authority, they would withhold even the slightest benefit from others.

Here, the sense of an exceedingly small amount comes from the established use of naqīr in such a construction.

Stinginess, selfishness, and withholding gifts are understood from the whole sentence and its context, rather than from N Q R itself.

The relation to naṣībun mina al-mulk

Naṣīb and mulk occur at the beginning of the verse.

Naṣīb concerns a share or portion, while mulk here concerns sovereignty and authority.

The Qur’an then speaks of withholding even an insignificant amount.

This contrast highlights the relation between power and the way people are treated.

An individual or group may desire authority and sovereignty, but possessing power does not in itself guarantee justice or generosity.

In this context, the feature is stated in criticism of a particular group.

It should not become a lexical rule that naqīr always concerns the stinginess of those in power. Another verse uses the same word to deny wrongdoing and affirm justice.

Connection with surrounding verses

Al-Nisāʾ 4:51 speaks of people given a share of the Book who make a mistaken judgment about others’ faith and guidance.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:54 also says:

أَمْ يَحْسُدُونَ النَّاسَ عَلَىٰ مَا آتَاهُمُ اللَّهُ مِن فَضْلِهِ

am yaḥsudūna al-nāsa ʿalā mā ātāhumu Allāhu min faḍlihi

Translation: “Or do they envy people for what The God has given them from His bounty?”

This connection shows that the question in Al-Nisāʾ 4:53 concerns more than an amount of property: it also relates to envy, monopolizing privilege, and the response to blessings and power.

These concepts, however, come from the verses’ context.

Naqīr remains the name of something very small, used here to express withholding the slightest benefit.

5. Al-Nisāʾ 4:124: justice even in the smallest amount

وَمَن يَعْمَلْ مِنَ الصَّالِحَاتِ مِن ذَكَرٍ أَوْ أُنثَىٰ وَهُوَ مُؤْمِنٌ فَأُولَٰئِكَ يَدْخُلُونَ الْجَنَّةَ وَلَا يُظْلَمُونَ نَقِيرًا

wa-man yaʿmal mina al-ṣāliḥāti min dhakarin aw unthā wa-huwa muʾminun fa-ulāʾika yadkhulūna al-jannata wa-lā yuẓlamūna naqīran

Forqan translation:

“Whoever does righteous deeds, male or female, while believing: those will enter the Garden and will not be wronged even the hollow of a date stone.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:124

This is the second and final occurrence of naqīr in the Qur’an.

The word retains the underlying meaning of its earlier use: an exceedingly small amount.

Its function in the sentence, however, differs.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:53, naqīr occurs in lā yuʾtūna al-nāsa naqīran and concerns giving nothing, however small.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:124, it occurs in wa-lā yuẓlamūna naqīran and concerns the negation of even the smallest injustice.

Wa-lā yuẓlamūna naqīran

Yuẓlamūna comes from Ẓ L M and is passive.

In this construction, the verse says that those who do righteous deeds with faith will not be wronged even in the slightest amount.

Naqīr indicates the tiny measure in which wrongdoing is also denied.

This does not mean that divine justice is measured through the actual hollow of a date stone.

Nor is naqīr a specified unit of weight, currency, or measurement.

The Qur’an uses a familiar image to emphasize that no right is lost, however insignificant its amount.

Connection with righteous action and faith

The opening names two features: doing righteous deeds and faith.

The Qur’an also explicitly includes both men and women in this promise.

The verse therefore concerns more than expressing a small amount: it concerns justice in treating those with the stated qualities.

The preceding verse, Al-Nisāʾ 4:123, says:

لَّيْسَ بِأَمَانِيِّكُمْ وَلَا أَمَانِيِّ أَهْلِ الْكِتَابِ مَن يَعْمَلْ سُوءًا يُجْزَ بِهِ

laysa bi-amāniyyikum wa-lā amāniyyi ahli al-kitābi man yaʿmal sūʾan yujza bihi

Translation: “It is neither according to your wishes nor the wishes of the People of the Book: whoever does evil will be recompensed for it.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:123

Al-Nisāʾ 4:124 then discusses those who do righteous deeds and believe.

This connection shows that divine judgment is not confined to the wishes and claims of groups; a person’s actual conduct also matters.

Righteous action, faith, and justice nevertheless retain independent meanings supplied by the accompanying expressions.

N Q R itself does not mean reward, faith, or justice.

The two uses of naqīr in Al-Nisāʾ

The same word serves two different situations in these verses.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:53 concerns people who, if they had authority, would give others nothing, however little.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:124 concerns a justice that deprives people of no right, however small.

In one use, naqīr expresses the extent of withholding; in the other, it expresses the comprehensiveness of justice.

This difference shows that naqīr has no fixed moral value in itself.

An exceedingly small amount retains the same meaning in both verses, while the verb and context determine the relationship in which that amount is mentioned.

6. Fa-idhā nuqira fī al-nāqūr: the sound opening a difficult day

فَإِذَا نُقِرَ فِي النَّاقُورِ

fa-idhā nuqira fī al-nāqūri

Forqan translation:

“Then, when the trumpet is blown,”

Al-Muddaththir 74:8

Two forms of N Q R occur together here: nuqira and al-nāqūr.

This combination has particular linguistic interest because the verb and the instrument’s name belong to the same family.

The meaning of nuqira

Nuqira is a passive perfect verb from N Q R.

In ordinary uses, naqr concerns striking or sounding something.

In the construction nuqira fī al-nāqūr, however, the established meaning is sounding an instrument, here associated with blowing.

Early lexical and interpretive explanations accordingly describe the expression through nufikha fī al-ṣūr, “the horn was blown.”

The rendering “the trumpet is blown” thus fits the established Arabic usage and lexical explanations of this construction.

This use does not mean that naqr means blowing in every setting.

When a bird pecks the ground or someone makes a hollow in stone, naqr still means striking and leaving an effect.

The root’s general meaning must therefore be distinguished from the particular meaning of the construction nuqira fī al-nāqūr.

What is al-nāqūr?

Nāqūr in this verse names an instrument with which a sound is produced.

Established interpretations associate it with the ṣūr: an instrument such as a horn or trumpet sounded in the events of resurrection.

The expression nufikha fī al-ṣūr also occurs in several Qur’anic verses.

For example:

وَنُفِخَ فِي الصُّورِ فَصَعِقَ مَن فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَمَن فِي الْأَرْضِ إِلَّا مَن شَاءَ اللَّهُ

wa-nufikha fī al-ṣūri fa-ṣaʿiqa man fī al-samāwāti wa-man fī al-arḍi illā man shāʾa Allāhu

Translation: “And the horn was blown, and everyone in the heavens and everyone on earth was struck down, except whomever The God willed.”

Al-Zumar 39:68

This verse uses nafkh and ṣūr.

Al-Muddaththir 74:8 uses naqr and nāqūr.

In their use within scenes of resurrection, the expressions are closely related.

Nevertheless, all the properties of one should not be transferred to the other without independently examining every use of ṣūr and nāqūr.

For example, nāqūr alone cannot establish the instrument’s material, shape, size, location, or physical mode of operation.

Nor does this verse name the one who blows into it.

Is the nāqūr struck or blown?

Since N Q R often involves striking, one might ask whether the nāqūr must be an instrument that is struck rather than one that is blown.

The question requires attention to actual linguistic usage.

Alongside striking the surface of an object, Arabic records naqr in a construction involving a trumpet or similar instrument to mean sounding it.

Lexical explanations identify the nāqūr with the ṣūr, which is blown.

The physical semantic core “striking” alone is therefore not conclusive evidence that the nāqūr is struck with a hammer or another implement.

Conversely, one should not conclude that N Q R fundamentally means blowing and that its connection with striking and sound is unimportant.

The passage involves a particular established linguistic use: sounding the nāqūr in the scene of resurrection.

The natural Persian rendering “the trumpet is blown” conveys this usage.

Is the nāqūr the same as the ṣūr?

Early explanations commonly associate nāqūr and ṣūr with the same instrument.

This interpretive equivalence fits the similarity of the settings in which the Qur’an uses the expressions.

Lexically, however, nāqūr and ṣūr are two words from different roots.

Nāqūr belongs to N Q R, whereas ṣūr requires separate examination.

The familiar equivalent “trumpet” can therefore be used in translating Al-Muddaththir 74:8, while the lexical study should still recognize the distinction between the Qur’anic names.

Nor does this equivalence by itself identify which blowing of the horn Al-Muddaththir 74:8 concerns.

The interpretive tradition offers different explanations of its relationship to the blasts of resurrection.

The verse itself, however, specifies neither a number nor an order for the blast.

Fa-dhālika yawmaʾidhin yawmun ʿasīr

The next verse, Al-Muddaththir 74:9, says:

فَذَٰلِكَ يَوْمَئِذٍ يَوْمٌ عَسِيرٌ

fa-dhālika yawmaʾidhin yawmun ʿasīrun

Forqan translation:

“That day will be a difficult day,”

Al-Muddaththir 74:9

Then Al-Muddaththir 74:10 says:

عَلَى الْكَافِرِينَ غَيْرُ يَسِيرٍ

ʿalā al-kāfirīna ghayru yasīrin

Forqan translation:

“Not easy for the disbelievers.”

Al-Muddaththir 74:10

These verses clarify the consequence and setting of the sounding of the nāqūr.

In this context, sounding the nāqūr is connected with the opening of a difficult day.

The day’s difficulty, however, comes from yawmun ʿasīr and ghayru yasīr, rather than from the independent meaning of N Q R.

Nāqūr itself does not mean punishment or reckoning. The instrument occurs in a scene associated with warning and the difficult events of resurrection.

The act of sounding, its instrument, and the consequences of the event must therefore be distinguished.

Can the nāqūr yield a theory of sound and resurrection?

In ordinary human experience, blowing a trumpet or wind instrument produces sound.

The verse, however, does not explain its volume, propagation distance, type of sound wave, or physical effect on the world.

A theory of sound waves, cosmic frequencies, or the physical manner of resurrection cannot therefore be extracted from N Q R or the name nāqūr.

Conversely, avoiding physical questions should not lead to removing the Qur’an’s clear expression about sounding the nāqūr and translating it merely as “the beginning of a transformation.”

The sounding of the nāqūr is the Qur’an’s own expression and should remain in translation. How it occurs in the unseen is not established by lexical meaning alone.

7. Comparison with related roots

N Q R (naqr) and Q R ʿ (qarʿ)

The roots overlap in uses involving striking and knocking.

Qarʿ in Arabic concerns knocking and the impact of one thing against another, such as knocking on a door or striking a hard surface.

Naqr also denotes striking, but its established uses may be more closely associated with pecking, making a small mark, and localized carving.

For example, a bird pecks a seed with its beak, and a person uses an implement to make a hollow in stone.

This tendency does not mean that naqr always denotes a weak strike and qarʿ always a powerful one.

In Arabic, the force of a blow depends on the object, implement, and context.

Al-qāriʿah in Al-Qāriʿah 101:1 also comes from Q R ʿ, rather than N Q R. Related ideas of striking and loud sound do not make the roots identical.

N Q R (naqr) and N Q B (naqb)

In physical usage, naqb concerns piercing and opening a breach or passage through something.

Naqr can also refer to carving and making a depression.

The roots overlap in this respect.

In naqr, however, striking or pecking is prominent, and the result may be only a small surface mark.

Naqb in some uses, by contrast, refers to an opening through which one can pass.

For example, striking a stone can leave a small mark without opening a passage through it.

A breach may also be made in a wall to allow passage.

This is a tendency rather than an absolute rule. The carving implement and the way an opening is made may be shared in both uses.

Study of N Q B

N Q R (naqr) and TH Q B (thaqb)

Thaqb also concerns piercing and making an opening.

Naqr may approach thaqb in some uses, as when a pointed implement strikes an object and makes a hole.

Not every naqr, however, produces a complete hole.

A bird pecking a seed or a finger briefly tapping a hard surface may be called naqr without an opening being made in the object.

Piercing is therefore one possible outcome or use of naqr, rather than its only meaning.

Study of TH Q B

N Q R (naqr) and N F KH (nafkh)

This comparison is particularly important for understanding Al-Muddaththir 74:8.

In ordinary usage, nafkh concerns blowing and sending out a stream of air.

The physical uses of naqr are more commonly associated with striking, pecking, and producing a mark or sound.

Nevertheless, the established use of nuqira fī al-nāqūr concerns sounding an instrument such as a trumpet by blowing.

This proximity in usage does not make the roots identical.

Al-Zumar 39:68 uses nufikha, whereas Al-Muddaththir 74:8 uses nuqira.

Each requires attention both to its own root’s meaning and to the established use of the whole construction.

Study of N F KH

N Q R (naqr) and N Q Ṣ (naqṣ)

Naqṣ in Arabic concerns diminishing or reducing something.

Naqīr, however, names a small date-stone hollow that becomes a measure of an exceedingly small amount in certain verses.

This relationship does not mean that N Q R itself means diminishing or making something deficient.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:124, wa-lā yuẓlamūna naqīran concerns not losing even the smallest right.

The diminution or loss of a right is understood from the construction and the verb of wrongdoing, rather than from naqīr sharing the root of naqṣ.

The roots are independent and should not be confused because their letters resemble one another or because they occur in a related semantic field.

Study of N Q Ṣ

8. Naqīr, fatīl, and qiṭmīr: three images of an insignificant amount

Alongside naqīr, the Qur’an uses two other words in expressions involving very small amounts: fatīl and qiṭmīr.

Established lexical explanations connect all three with the date stone, but they do not denote the same thing.

Naqīr is the small depression in the date stone.

In one established explanation, fatīl is the fine strand visible in the stone’s groove.

Qiṭmīr concerns the thin covering around the date stone.

Fatīl in the Qur’an

Al-Nisāʾ 4:49 says:

وَلَا يُظْلَمُونَ فَتِيلًا

wa-lā yuẓlamūna fatīlan

Translation: “They will not be wronged even the measure of a tiny strand.”

Its point is close to wa-lā yuẓlamūna naqīran in Al-Nisāʾ 4:124.

Both deny wrongdoing even in the smallest amount.

Naqīr and fatīl are nevertheless different words and should not be reduced to a single lexical meaning.

Qiṭmīr in the Qur’an

Fāṭir 35:13 says:

وَالَّذِينَ تَدْعُونَ مِن دُونِهِ مَا يَمْلِكُونَ مِن قِطْمِيرٍ

wa-alladhīna tadʿūna min dūnihi mā yamlikūna min qiṭmīrin

Translation: “Those you call upon apart from Him own not even the thin covering of a date stone.”

Qiṭmīr here expresses how little the invented objects of worship possess.

The manner of expression is related to naqīr in Al-Nisāʾ 4:53, since both use something very small to emphasize an insignificant amount.

The three roots must nevertheless be distinguished: N Q R, F T L, and Q Ṭ M R.

Their shared reference to small parts of a date stone does not make their roots or all their meanings identical.

Study of Q Ṭ M R

Nor should these images yield a definite hierarchy of the smallest parts of the stone or precise measurements of each. The verses use familiar examples of insignificance rather than scientific units of measurement.

The comparison helps explain Qur’anic language by showing how a familiar physical image can be used in different subjects: stinginess, ownership, and justice.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

Persian equivalents for this root should be selected according to the derivative and construction of each expression.

For ordinary uses of naqara, equivalents meaning “pecked,” “struck,” or “tapped” are appropriate.

In uses involving tools and stone, “carved,” “pierced,” or “made a depression” may be more precise.

For naqqara ʿani al-amr, “searched” or “investigated carefully” is appropriate.

For minqār referring to a bird, “beak” is natural; for an implement, “carving tool” or its particular name is more suitable.

For nuqrah, “hollow” and “depression” are clear equivalents.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:53

فَإِذًا لَّا يُؤْتُونَ النَّاسَ نَقِيرًا

fa-idhan lā yuʾtūna al-nāsa naqīran

Forqan translation:

“Then they would not give people even the hollow of a date stone.”

This conveys the sense of naqīr as an exceedingly small amount effectively.

The lexical explanation can clarify that a small hollow in a date stone is meant.

A lengthy description of the stone’s shape need not be added to the translation itself.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:124

وَلَا يُظْلَمُونَ نَقِيرًا

wa-lā yuẓlamūna naqīran

Forqan translation:

“They will not be wronged even the hollow of a date stone.”

The same equivalent should be retained here.

Naqīr should not be rendered once as the date-stone hollow and elsewhere as a “particle,” “smallest weight,” or “small reward,” unless there is a clear necessity in the target language.

The underlying meaning remains the same in both verses; the conceptual difference comes from the verb and context.

Al-Muddaththir 74:8

فَإِذَا نُقِرَ فِي النَّاقُورِ

fa-idhā nuqira fī al-nāqūri

Forqan translation:

“Then, when the trumpet is blown,”

This rendering fits the established Arabic use of nāqūr and its association with blowing the horn.

The root explanation should clarify that naqr elsewhere concerns striking and sounding, whereas blowing the trumpet is the established meaning of this construction.

The form and nature of the nāqūr should not be specified with certainty beyond what the text states.

Brief root meaning and meanings in the verses

Brief root meaning: striking, pecking, and making a mark or depression; in some uses, sounding, carving, and careful searching.

Naqīran in Al-Nisāʾ 4:53: the very small hollow in a date stone; in context, an image of an insignificant amount that people refuse to give.

Naqīran in Al-Nisāʾ 4:124: the same small date-stone hollow; in context, a measure emphasizing that no right is lost, however small.

Nuqira in Al-Muddaththir 74:8: was sounded or blown, in a passive construction concerning the nāqūr.

Al-nāqūr in Al-Muddaththir 74:8: a trumpet or instrument sounded in the scene of resurrection; in established usage, associated with the ṣūr.

10. Conclusion

N Q R in Arabic is associated with striking, pecking, and making a mark or depression.

This meaning appears in birds pecking, carving stone, making hollows, engraving hard surfaces, and certain uses involving sound.

In other branches, naqr is used for close investigation, selection, and faultfinding. These uses can be explained through the image of probing and leaving an effect, but the resemblance does not establish a definite history for the development of every derivative.

The Qur’an uses the root in three verses.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:53 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:124, naqīr denotes the very small hollow in a date stone, made a measure of an insignificant amount in both sentences.

The first criticizes withholding the slightest benefit from people; the second emphasizes justice and denies the loss of even the smallest right.

The two uses show that naqīr does not itself mean stinginess, wrongdoing, or justice. What remains constant is the image of an exceedingly small amount; the sentence’s moral direction comes from the verbs and context.

In Al-Muddaththir 74:8, nuqira fī al-nāqūr concerns sounding the trumpet in the scene of resurrection.

Naqr here concerns sound and the instrument that is sounded; established Arabic explanations connect it with blowing the horn.

N Q R itself, however, does not mean resurrection, punishment, raising the dead, or the beginning of reckoning. These concepts come from the accompanying verses and other Qur’anic descriptions.

Nor should nāqūr restrict every form of the root to blowing, just as the general sense of striking should not be imposed on the particular construction nuqira fī al-nāqūr.

Final definition: in its principal branch, N Q R denotes striking, pecking, and making a mark or depression in something. The same family has established uses for carving, producing sound, and close investigation. In the Qur’an, naqīr names the small hollow in a date stone used to express an exceedingly small amount, while nāqūr names an instrument sounded in the scene of resurrection. Each derivative requires attention to its own structure and usage, rather than imposing one branch’s image on the entire family.
