Independence, sufficiency, and provision: from human wealth to God's freedom from need and wealth's inability to replace truth

In early Arabic, غ ن ي concerns independence, sufficiency, and possessing what meets a person's needs. غِنًى describes a condition in which someone does not depend on another person or resources beyond their own to meet a particular need. غَنِيّ describes someone who possesses such means or independence.

In economic usage, ghaniyy describes a wealthy person with sufficient money and resources. Yet the root is not limited to financial wealth. Someone may be independent in one respect while needing help in another. Something may meet one need yet offer no protection against another need or danger.

From this family, أَغْنَى means to make independent, provide enough, or be of use in a particular situation. اسْتَغْنَى concerns becoming independent or considering oneself free of need.

Another recorded use of غَنِيَ means to reside or live in a place. The Quran also employs this sense. غِنَاء, with its particular vocalization and formation in the sense of singing, belongs to another lexical branch that requires separate examination.

The root غ ن ي occurs 73 times in the Quran. Its uses cover human wealth and poverty, God's freedom from need, providing for others, imagined independence, the inadequacy of wealth and power, and residence in a land.

Four distinctions are essential: having wealth is not complete independence; thinking oneself independent differs from actually being so; usefulness in one situation does not ensure protection against every harm; and God's freedom from need is not on the same level as limited human wealth.

1. The Root in Early Arabic and Historical Witnesses

Wealth and poverty were familiar social and economic concepts in pre-Islamic Arab life. Livestock, property, resources, and family or tribal support could reduce a person's dependence on others.

In this usage, غِنًى indicated sufficient provision, and غَنِيّ someone well provided for or wealthy.

Independence in Arabic did not always mean owning abundant wealth. Skill, ability, or a suitable alternative might make someone independent of another person's help.

Early sources record غَنِيَ عَنْهُ for becoming independent of another, and اسْتَغْنَى بِهِ for finding sufficiency through someone or something.

A familiar use of أَغْنَى عَنْهُ likewise means to suffice, be useful, or meet a need.

These uses concern more than the amount of property: they address the relationship between need, resources, and sufficiency.

A Witness from Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā

The pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā says in his celebrated Muʿallaqah:

وَمَنْ يَكُ ذَا فَضْلٍ فَيَبْخَلْ بِفَضْلِهِ
عَلَى قَوْمِهِ يُسْتَغْنَ عَنْهُ وَيُذْمَمِ

wa-man yaku dhā faḍlin fa-yabkhal bi-faḍlihi / ʿalā qawmihi yustaghna ʿanhu wa-yudhmmami

Translation: “Whoever possesses surplus means but withholds them from his people will be dispensed with and censured.”

Here ذَا فَضْلٍ describes someone with resources beyond his own needs.

Despite this wealth, he refuses to give or help.

The consequence is يُسْتَغْنَ عَنْهُ: others dispense with him or cease relying on his help.

This is a significant lexical witness: someone may himself be wealthy while others become independent of him.

The first half concerns his resources; the second concerns others' independence of him.

Istighnāʾ therefore cannot always mean becoming rich.

Because a wealthy person is miserly, others may find another way to meet their needs and cease depending on him, even without becoming wealthy themselves.

The verse is recorded in Zuhayr's well-known Muʿallaqah and is an important witness attributed to pre-Islamic language. Its surviving form, however, reaches us through later transmission and commentaries. Its established literary attribution does not amount to a contemporary document establishing its original date and wording with certainty.

A Witness Attributed to al-Aʿshā

A poem attributed to al-Aʿshā contains the following:

كِلَانَا غَنِيٌّ عَنْ أَخِيهِ حَيَاتَهُ
وَنَحْنُ إِذَا مُتْنَا أَشَدُّ تَغَانِيًا

kilānā ghaniyyun ʿan akhīhi ḥayātahu / wa-naḥnu idhā mitnā ashaddu taghāniyā

Translation: “Each of us is independent of his brother throughout his life; when we die, our independence of one another will be greater still.”

Here غَنِيٌّ عَنْ أَخِيهِ describes a relationship between two people.

The point is independence of another person, rather than abundant property.

Two people might be financially needy while considering themselves independent of each other's help or company.

In this usage, independence is relative: a person is independent of someone or something particular.

Ibn Fāris and other lexicographers attribute this verse to al-Aʿshā. Other literary sources also transmit it in verses attributed to ʿAbd Allāh ibn Muʿāwiyah. Its attribution is therefore not uniform, and the date of this particular verse has not been independently established.

With that limitation, these witnesses show that early Arabic used this family both for provision and for independence of others.

Sufficiency and Usefulness

Lexicons also record أَغْنَى عَنْهُ for sufficing and meeting another person's need.

For example, completing one task may remove the need to perform another; the first task then serves in its place.

Similarly, money that meets a particular need is useful in that respect.

Yet the same money may be unable to help against illness, death, or another danger.

In such uses, أَغْنَى does not express unlimited ability. It concerns sufficiency or usefulness in relation to a particular need and situation.

This sense is frequent in the Quran, especially in expressions such as لَا يُغْنِي عَنْهُ and مَا أَغْنَىٰ عَنْهُمْ.

2. Semantic Branches and Forms

The Arabic family غ ن ي is extensive. Its different forms must be examined individually according to construction and context.

Provision and Independence

غِنًى: independence, provision, or sufficient resources to meet a need.

غَنِيَ: he became independent or well provided for.

غَنِيّ: well provided for, wealthy, or independent, according to the kind and scope of need.

أَغْنِيَاء: the plural of ghaniyy; wealthy people or those regarded as independent.

غُنْيَة: what suffices to meet a need; a resource or means of independence.

غَنَاء: with initial fatḥah and a long final vowel, sufficiency or usefulness in one branch of this family.

غَانٍ: someone independent in a particular respect; other constructions have other uses.

The central issue here is sufficiency and the absence of need in a specified respect.

Providing Sufficiency and Sufficing

أَغْنَى: he made independent, provided sufficient resources, or proved useful against a need.

يُغْنِي: he or it makes independent, suffices, or benefits.

إِغْنَاء: providing enough or meeting a need.

مُغْنٍ: someone or something able to meet a particular need or serve in another's place.

مُغْنُونَ: the sound masculine plural of the active participle in Quranic usage; those able to relieve another's need or harm.

The verb's sense in these forms depends on its complement.

For example, أَغْنَاهُ اللَّهُ may describe God providing for someone, whereas لَا يُغْنِي عَنْهُ مَالُهُ concerns wealth failing to help in a particular situation.

One concerns the provision of sufficiency; the other a resource's inadequacy against a need or danger.

Independence and the Sense of Being Independent

اسْتَغْنَى: he became independent, made himself independent, or considered himself free of need.

اسْتِغْنَاء: attaining independence or considering oneself independent of others.

مُسْتَغْنٍ: someone independent or who considers himself so.

Form X in this family may indicate attaining independence or acting from a position of independence.

The morphological pattern alone does not establish that the subject has actually become independent.

Some Quranic uses concern actual independence; others concern a person's imagined independence.

Residence and Flourishing Habitation

Arabic records غَنِيَ الْقَوْمُ فِي الْمَكَانِ for people residing in a place.

مَغْنًى likewise denotes a dwelling place, and مَغَانِي places of residence or homes.

Some lexicographers explain this branch by saying that people found stability and sufficiency where they settled.

This explanation suggests a relationship between residence and independence without establishing the historical origin of the residential sense.

What matters is that residing in a place is an attested usage of this family, also found in the Quran.

Singing

Arabic also records غِنَاء and تَغَنِّي in connection with sound and singing.

In this branch, غَنَّى means to sing, and أُغْنِيَة a song.

This usage should not automatically be derived from wealth or independence.

Early sources, including Ibn Fāris's lexical account, distinguish sufficiency and sound as two semantic origins within the family.

Arabic also records some uses of تَغَنَّى concerning independence. Similar written forms alone therefore do not determine a word's meaning: vocalization, construction, and context must be examined.

None of the Quranic occurrences of غ ن ي concerns singing or songs. Verses containing غَنِيّ, أَغْنَى, and اسْتَغْنَى should not be interpreted as references to singing or music.

3. Human Wealth: Actual Resources or Another Person's Impression?

Applied to people in the Quran, ghaniyy sometimes denotes financial wealth and sometimes independence of something.

Human independence, however, is relative.

Someone may possess great wealth while still depending on other things for health, food, security, human relationships, and continued life.

Someone with little property may nevertheless need no help from others in a particular matter.

Wealth in Contrast to Poverty

Al-Nisāʾ 4:6 addresses guardianship and the protection of orphans' property.

It says that a wealthy guardian should abstain from taking the orphan's property for himself, whereas a poor guardian may use it according to what is right.

Here غَنِيًّا contrasts with فَقِيرًا.

This contrast identifies the wealthy person as someone whose own financial needs do not depend on the orphan's property.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:135 uses the same two descriptions in connection with justice and testimony.

Believers are commanded to uphold justice even when testimony goes against themselves or their relatives; the verse then mentions the wealthy and the poor.

In this context, a person's wealth or poverty must not alter truth or justice.

The Quran recognizes wealth and poverty as real social conditions without identifying a person's ultimate worth with their possessions.

Needy People Mistaken for the Wealthy

Al-Baqarah 2:273

لِلْفُقَرَاءِ الَّذِينَ أُحْصِرُوا فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ لَا يَسْتَطِيعُونَ ضَرْبًا فِي الْأَرْضِ يَحْسَبُهُمُ الْجَاهِلُ أَغْنِيَاءَ مِنَ التَّعَفُّفِ تَعْرِفُهُمْ بِسِيمَاهُمْ لَا يَسْأَلُونَ النَّاسَ إِلْحَافًا وَمَا تُنْفِقُوا مِنْ خَيْرٍ فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ بِهِ عَلِيمٌ

li-l-fuqarāʾi alladhīna uḥṣirū fī sabīli allāhi lā yastaṭīʿūna ḍarban fī al-arḍi yaḥsabuhumu al-jāhilu aghniyāʾa mina al-taʿaffufi taʿrifuhum bi-sīmāhum lā yasʾalūna al-nāsa ilḥāfan wa-mā tunfiqū min khayrin fa-inna allāha bihi ʿalīm

Al-Baqarah 2:273

Translation: “For the needy who are constrained in the way of The God and cannot travel through the land. Someone unaware of their condition supposes them wealthy because of their restraint. You recognize them by their signs; they do not ask people insistently. Whatever good you spend, The God knows it.”

This verse clearly distinguishes actual wealth from being thought wealthy.

The people are introduced as الْفُقَرَاءِ.

They are needy.

Yet the verse continues:

“يَحْسَبُهُمُ الْجَاهِلُ أَغْنِيَاءَ مِنَ التَّعَفُّفِ”

Someone unaware of their condition considers them wealthy because of their restraint.

أَغْنِيَاء here describes an uninformed observer's judgment rather than their actual circumstances.

This distinction matters greatly.

Self-respect, modesty, or restraint may lead someone to conceal their need and refrain from insisting on help.

Not asking does not mean having no need.

Nor do a neat appearance or dignified conduct alone establish someone's economic position.

The verse shows that recognizing need should not depend exclusively on explicit requests.

Those with more resources should attend to needy people's circumstances within their ability and knowledge, especially where real restrictions prevent people from freely travelling or working to earn a living.

Here taʿaffuf, from ع ف ف, is distinct from wealth.

Taʿaffuf describes restrained conduct; ghinā describes provision or independence.

A person may be restrained and remain needy.

Wealth and Social Responsibility

Al-Tawbah 9:93 discusses people who, despite possessing resources, ask to be excused from participation in a particular responsibility.

أَغْنِيَاء describes people who possess the necessary means.

Their censure arises from how they behave despite their ability in that situation, rather than from wealth alone.

Wealth is consequently neither a sin in itself nor proof of virtue.

Resources may enable someone to fulfil responsibilities; their ethical worth depends on how those resources are used.

The Circulation of Property and Its Concentration among the Wealthy

Al-Ḥashr 59:7 discusses property placed at the Messenger's disposal and identifies groups for its allocation.

One stated purpose is that it should not circulate exclusively among the wealthy.

الْأَغْنِيَاءِ denotes the wealthy members of society.

This usage treats wealth as a real economic and social condition and addresses the effects of concentrating property among that group.

The designation aghniyāʾ does not itself establish that every wealthy person is unjust or corrupt.

The verse concerns the circulation and distribution of the property under discussion, rather than condemning everyone with resources.

Conversely, abundant property does not itself prove truth, virtue, or independence of others.

The Quran distinguishes resources from moral responsibility.

4. Al-Ghaniyy: God's Freedom from Need and Creation's Dependence

An important Quranic use of the root is describing God as غَنِيّ and الْغَنِيّ.

Several verses combine this description with حَمِيد, حَلِيم, كَرِيم, and expressions of God's mercy and ownership.

God's freedom from need should not be understood on the same level as human wealth.

Possessions may make someone independent in a particular respect, but their existence and abilities remain dependent on many factors.

God's freedom from need in the Quran means that He does not need His creatures, their possessions, worship, or assistance.

God's Independence and Human Need

Fāṭir 35:15

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّاسُ أَنْتُمُ الْفُقَرَاءُ إِلَى اللَّهِ ۖ وَاللَّهُ هُوَ الْغَنِيُّ الْحَمِيدُ

yā ayyuhā al-nāsu antumu al-fuqarāʾu ilā allāhi wa-allāhu huwa al-ghaniyyu al-ḥamīd

Fāṭir 35:15

Translation: “People, you are those in need of The God; and The God is the One free of need, the Praiseworthy.”

The address here is to النَّاس: people.

الْفُقَرَاءُ إِلَى اللَّهِ expresses human dependence on God.

In contrast, الْغَنِيُّ الْحَمِيدُ identifies God's independence and praiseworthiness.

Poverty here is not limited to lacking money.

Even a wealthy person's existence, continued life, and access to the world's resources depend on conditions they did not independently create.

Someone may seek no financial help from others yet remain dependent on water, air, food, health, and the conditions of life.

Economic wealth therefore does not contradict a person's existential dependence on God.

The verse concerns two different levels: limited human provision and God's independence of all creation.

God Does Not Need Human Wealth or Charity

Al-Baqarah 2:263

قَوْلٌ مَعْرُوفٌ وَمَغْفِرَةٌ خَيْرٌ مِنْ صَدَقَةٍ يَتْبَعُهَا أَذًى ۗ وَاللَّهُ غَنِيٌّ حَلِيمٌ

qawlun maʿrūfun wa-maghfiratun khayrun min ṣadaqatin yatbaʿuhā adhan wa-allāhu ghaniyyun ḥalīm

Al-Baqarah 2:263

Translation: “A kind word and forgiveness are better than charity followed by hurt; and The God is free of need, Forbearing.”

Here غَنِيّ appears beside حَلِيم.

The context concerns charity, how it is given, and avoiding injury to its recipient.

God needs neither human wealth nor charity. Giving does not meet God's need; it helps others and fulfils human moral responsibility.

Giving money to someone in need therefore does not authorize humiliating or hurting them.

A large gift may be accompanied by treatment so hurtful that its moral worth is damaged.

The verse places a kind word and forgiveness above such charity.

God's independence is thus connected with human responsibility towards others' needs, rather than their insignificance.

Al-Baqarah 2:267 also describes God as غَنِيٌّ حَمِيدٌ while calling believers to give from good and wholesome things.

Giving concerns goodness, wholesomeness, and responsibility for one's resources, rather than God's need for human property.

God's Independence of Human Belief and Worship

Al-Zumar 39:7

إِنْ تَكْفُرُوا فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ غَنِيٌّ عَنْكُمْ ۖ وَلَا يَرْضَىٰ لِعِبَادِهِ الْكُفْرَ ۖ وَإِنْ تَشْكُرُوا يَرْضَهُ لَكُمْ ۗ وَلَا تَزِرُ وَازِرَةٌ وِزْرَ أُخْرَىٰ ۗ ثُمَّ إِلَىٰ رَبِّكُمْ مَرْجِعُكُمْ فَيُنَبِّئُكُمْ بِمَا كُنْتُمْ تَعْمَلُونَ ۚ إِنَّهُ عَلِيمٌ بِذَاتِ الصُّدُورِ

in takfurū fa-inna allāha ghaniyyun ʿankum wa-lā yarḍā li-ʿibādihi al-kufra wa-in tashkurū yarḍahu lakum wa-lā taziru wāziratun wizra ukhrā thumma ilā rabbikum marjiʿukum fa-yunabbiʾukum bi-mā kuntum taʿmalūna innahu ʿalīmun bi-dhāti al-ṣudūr

Al-Zumar 39:7

Translation: “If you disbelieve, The God is independent of you; He does not approve disbelief for His servants. If you give thanks, He approves it for you. No bearer bears another's burden. Then to your Lord is your return, and He will inform you of what you used to do. He knows what is within the breasts.”

غَنِيٌّ عَنْكُمْ explicitly expresses God's independence of those addressed.

The following clause is significant:

“وَلَا يَرْضَىٰ لِعِبَادِهِ الْكُفْرَ”

God's freedom from need does not mean approval of every human choice.

God does not need His servants' belief or worship, yet the Quran evaluates their conduct as pleasing or displeasing.

These two issues must remain distinct.

An act may supply nothing that God needs as creatures do, while still holding value for a person and their relationship with truth and justice.

Ibrāhīm 14:8 likewise says that even if everyone on earth disbelieves, God remains independent and praiseworthy.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:6 describes human striving as being for one's own benefit and God as independent of all worlds.

Muḥammad 47:38 also calls God rich and people needy in the context of giving.

Belief, good deeds, striving, and charity do not remedy a deficiency in God.

Independence Together with Praise, Forbearance, and Generosity

The Quran combines ghaniyy with other descriptions.

غَنِيٌّ حَلِيمٌ in Al-Baqarah 2:263 places independence alongside forbearance.

غَنِيٌّ حَمِيدٌ in several verses joins independence with worthiness of praise.

غَنِيٌّ كَرِيمٌ in Al-Naml 27:40 joins independence with nobility and generosity.

Ghaniyy means free of need in these uses; ḥalīm, ḥamīd, and karīm each carry a separate meaning.

They should not all be absorbed into the lexical definition of ghaniyy.

God's independence does not prevent mercy, giving, forbearance, or judgment.

Nor does it imply that human deeds have no moral significance to Him. The Quran distinguishes His independence from His evaluation of His servants' deeds.

5. Aghnā and Istaghnā: Providing Sufficiency and Imagined Independence

Aghnā and its derivatives have two principal Quranic fields: providing sufficient resources, and being useful against need or danger.

Istaghnā may refer to actual or imagined independence.

God Providing Sufficiency

Al-Najm 53:48

وَأَنَّهُ هُوَ أَغْنَىٰ وَأَقْنَىٰ

wa-annahu huwa aghnā wa-aqnā

Al-Najm 53:48

Translation: “And that it is He who made independent and granted possessions.”

أَغْنَى is a Form IV past verb whose subject refers to God.

God provides the means of provision and sufficiency.

أَقْنَى comes from another root; lexicons record senses concerning ownership, giving, and possessions.

The verbs stand together but should not be treated as entirely synonymous without examination.

Aghnā concerns making independent and providing enough; aqnā has its own semantic branch.

Al-Ḍuḥā 93:8 reminds Muḥammad that God found him needy and then provided sufficiency.

It describes a change from need to provision or sufficiency supplied by God.

It does not establish that Muḥammad acquired unlimited wealth or thereafter had no human needs.

Aghnā means providing sufficiency, rather than eliminating every possible human need.

Sufficiency in Marriage and Family Life

Al-Nisāʾ 4:130

وَإِنْ يَتَفَرَّقَا يُغْنِ اللَّهُ كُلًّا مِنْ سَعَتِهِ ۚ وَكَانَ اللَّهُ وَاسِعًا حَكِيمًا

wa-in yatafarraqā yughni allāhu kullan min saʿatihi wa-kāna allāhu wāsiʿan ḥakīmā

Al-Nisāʾ 4:130

Translation: “If the two separate, The God will provide each with sufficiency from His abundance; and The God is All-Encompassing, Wise.”

يُغْنِ here concerns providing sufficiency for each party.

The context is a marital relationship and the possibility of separation.

The verse recalls that the end of a relationship does not end every possibility for life and provision.

It does not imply that every person necessarily becomes rich after separation or that all difficulties immediately disappear.

Its central concern is sufficiency and abundance from God.

Al-Nūr 24:32 likewise says, in the context of marriage, that God will enrich poor people from His bounty.

Al-Nūr 24:33 calls those without the means to marry to exercise restraint until God provides for them from His bounty.

These verses should be read together.

The first cannot become a rule that every marriage necessarily produces immediate wealth. The second acknowledges lack of means as a reality relevant to decisions and conduct.

Al-Tawbah 9:28 addresses fear of poverty following the restriction on polytheists approaching the Sacred Mosque, and qualifies enrichment from God's bounty by His will.

The promise of provision must therefore remain connected to the verse's conditions and qualifications.

Actual and Imagined Independence

Al-ʿAlaq 96:6

كَلَّا إِنَّ الْإِنْسَانَ لَيَطْغَىٰ

kallā inna al-insāna la-yaṭghā

Al-ʿAlaq 96:6

Translation: “No indeed! The human being surely transgresses.”

Al-ʿAlaq 96:7

أَنْ رَآهُ اسْتَغْنَىٰ

an raʾāhu istaghnā

Al-ʿAlaq 96:7

Translation: “Because he sees himself as independent.”

These verses connect human transgression with seeing oneself as independent.

اسْتَغْنَى is a Form X verb.

Here, however, رَآهُ is decisive.

The verse says that the person sees himself as independent, rather than merely stating that he has become so.

It therefore also concerns a person's perception and judgment of their own circumstances.

Wealth, status, knowledge, or power may lead someone to imagine no further need for guidance, correction, or accountability.

Resources do not remove actual human dependence.

Here the central problem is limited resources becoming an imagined complete independence and then transgression, rather than wealth itself.

This distinction also matters in Al-Layl 92:8, where miserliness accompanies istaghnā.

ʿAbasa 80:5 describes someone who considers himself independent or behaves from that position.

None of these uses establishes that every wealthy person is rebellious or arrogant.

Yet treating resources as proof of independence from truth, justice, and responsibility can make those resources a basis for transgression.

وَاسْتَغْنَى اللَّهُ: God's Actual Independence

Al-Taghābun 64:6

ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُ كَانَتْ تَأْتِيهِمْ رُسُلُهُمْ بِالْبَيِّنَاتِ فَقَالُوا أَبَشَرٌ يَهْدُونَنَا فَكَفَرُوا وَتَوَلَّوْا وَاسْتَغْنَى اللَّهُ ۚ وَاللَّهُ غَنِيٌّ حَمِيدٌ

dhālika bi-annahu kānat taʾtīhim rusuluhum bi-l-bayyināti fa-qālū a-basharun yahdūnanā fa-kafarū wa-tawallaw wa-staghnā allāhu wa-allāhu ghaniyyun ḥamīd

Al-Taghābun 64:6

Translation: “That was because their Messengers kept coming to them with clear signs, but they said, ‘Shall human beings guide us?’ They disbelieved and turned away; and The God is independent. The God is free of need, Praiseworthy.”

The same verb istaghnā here describes God.

Its meaning differs from imagined human independence in Al-ʿAlaq 96:7.

In Al-ʿAlaq, a person sees himself as independent, and that perception may lead to transgression.

In Al-Taghābun, God actually needs neither human acceptance nor rejection.

Form X alone does not mean that every subject is pretending to be independent.

The subject and context determine whether the issue is actual independence or someone's perception and behaviour concerning it.

6. Lā Yughnī: When Wealth, Power, and Conjecture Do Not Suffice

Denial of sufficiency or usefulness is among the root's most extensive Quranic uses.

Something useful in ordinary life may be unable to help against a particular need or danger.

This distinction is essential: failure to suffice in one situation does not make something absolutely worthless.

Wealth and Power Unable to Avert Punishment

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:28

مَا أَغْنَىٰ عَنِّي مَالِيَهْ

mā aghnā ʿannī māliyah

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:28

Translation: “My wealth has not availed me.”

The speaker faces the consequences of his deeds on the Day of Reckoning.

He speaks of wealth he possessed in worldly life which cannot now change his situation.

This does not mean that wealth has no worldly benefit.

Wealth can provide food, housing, education, treatment, and help for others.

In this situation, however, it cannot replace deeds and responsibility.

Al-Masad 111:2 makes a similar point about a person's wealth and acquisitions.

Āl ʿImrān 3:10 and Āl ʿImrān 3:116 describe possessions and children as unable to avert anything of God's judgment.

Al-Mujādilah 58:17 presents the same theme.

These verses do not condemn possessing wealth or children in itself. Property and family ties cannot release someone from responsibility for their deeds.

Strength and Planning That Do Not Always Suffice

Al-Tawbah 9:25 recalls Ḥunayn, when large numbers impressed those addressed but proved insufficient.

فَلَمْ تُغْنِ عَنْكُمْ شَيْئًا concerns numerical strength failing in a particular situation.

It does not mean that troop numbers never matter in battle.

Numbers alone do not guarantee the desired outcome.

In Yūsuf 12:67, Jacob advises his sons how to enter but explicitly says he cannot avert anything of God's judgment from them.

Yūsuf 12:68 likewise shows that this measure could not itself suffice against God's judgment.

People may plan to reduce danger, but no human plan absolutely guarantees the result.

Lā yughnī here expresses the limits of effort rather than the futility of every effort.

Conjecture in Relation to Truth

Yūnus 10:36

وَمَا يَتَّبِعُ أَكْثَرُهُمْ إِلَّا ظَنًّا ۚ إِنَّ الظَّنَّ لَا يُغْنِي مِنَ الْحَقِّ شَيْئًا ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ عَلِيمٌ بِمَا يَفْعَلُونَ

wa-mā yattabiʿu aktharuhum illā ẓannan inna al-ẓanna lā yughnī mina al-ḥaqqi shayʾan inna allāha ʿalīmun bi-mā yafʿalūn

Yūnus 10:36

Translation: “Most of them follow nothing but conjecture. Conjecture cannot replace truth in any respect. The God knows what they do.”

Here يُغْنِي concerns sufficing.

Its subject is conjecture in relation to truth, rather than money or wealth.

The sentence says that conjecture cannot replace truth or suffice in its place.

The same expression appears in Al-Najm 53:28.

This usage extends the root غ ن ي into knowledge and judgment.

Someone may hold a strong impression about something, but strength of feeling alone does not turn it into established truth.

Ghinā here concerns the adequacy of a kind of knowledge in relation to truth, rather than wealth.

When Intercession or Another Person’s Support Does Not Suffice

In Ibrāhīm 14:21, followers ask those who acted arrogantly whether they can avert anything of God's punishment from them.

The form مُغْنُونَ appears in this verse.

Ghāfir 40:47 likewise presents a similar conversation between followers and those who considered themselves great.

مُغْنُونَ here describes those expected to help against punishment or avert part of it.

The context, however, shows that they possess no such power.

Al-Najm 53:26 discusses the intercession of angels, which does not avail without God’s permission and approval.

These uses show that aghnā can concern one person sufficing or benefiting another, rather than possessions alone.

Yet inability to avert punishment does not establish that someone had no ability to help or support others in worldly life.

The issue is a particular need in the setting of divine reckoning and judgment.

Food That Does Not Relieve Hunger

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:7

لَا يُسْمِنُ وَلَا يُغْنِي مِنْ جُوعٍ

lā yusminu wa-lā yughnī min jūʿ

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:7

Translation: “It neither fattens nor relieves hunger.”

يُغْنِي مِنْ جُوعٍ concerns relieving hunger.

This clearly illustrates sufficiency in relation to a particular need.

Food is ordinarily consumed for nourishment and relief from hunger.

The food described here neither fattens the body nor relieves hunger.

Lā yughnī therefore means failing to meet that need.

The expression has no direct connection with financial wealth.

A Concern That Occupies All of a Person's Attention

ʿAbasa 80:37

لِكُلِّ امْرِئٍ مِنْهُمْ يَوْمَئِذٍ شَأْنٌ يُغْنِيهِ

li-kulli imriʾin minhum yawmaʾidhin shaʾnun yughnīh

ʿAbasa 80:37

Translation: “Each of them that day will have a concern that occupies him.”

This is another important use of the root.

يُغْنِيهِ does not mean making someone wealthy here.

The concern or situation occupies a person's attention so fully that no time or capacity remains for other matters.

شَأْنٌ يُغْنِيهِ can therefore be understood as a concern sufficient to occupy and engage someone.

This relates to sufficiency, while its contextual result is complete absorption in one's own situation.

It shows why translating every derivative of غ ن ي as wealth or financial independence is mistaken.

Residence and Flourishing Habitation

Al-Aʿrāf 7:92, Hūd 11:68, and Hūd 11:95 use كَأَنْ لَمْ يَغْنَوْا فِيهَا.

The expression describes people destroyed or uprooted from their land, as though they had never lived there.

يَغْنَوْا فِيهَا concerns residing and living in a place.

Their wealth is not the issue.

Yūnus 10:24 similarly uses كَأَنْ لَمْ تَغْنَ بِالْأَمْسِ for land and crops destroyed after flourishing and becoming adorned.

The image is of flourishing habitation or growth whose traces then disappear.

This branch must not be reduced to financial independence.

Residence and flourishing habitation are attested senses that must be preserved when translating these verses.

7. Distinctions from Related Roots

غ ن ي (Ghinā) and ف ق ر (Faqr)

Faqr in Arabic and the Quran concerns need and insufficient means in a particular respect.

Ghinā often stands in contrast to it.

Fāṭir 35:15 describes people as needy in relation to God and God as independent.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:6 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:135, however, address people's economic circumstances.

The contrast therefore does not operate on the same level in every verse.

Someone may be financially wealthy yet dependent on God.

A needy person may also be morally honourable and restrained.

Poverty must not be equated with baseness or wealth with inherent virtue.

غ ن ي (Ghinā) and ك ف ي (Kafā)

Kafā concerns something sufficing to meet a need or perform a particular task.

This closely approaches a major branch of غ ن ي.

Ghinā also emphasizes a person's state of provision or independence, whereas kafā may directly describe a person, argument, or resource being enough in a given situation.

Al-Zumar 39:36 speaks of God's sufficiency for His servant.

This relates to God's independence and ability to help, but the roots are not identical.

Ghaniyy may describe someone without need; kafā may express sufficing for someone else.

غ ن ي (Ghinā) and ر ز ق (Rizq)

Rizq in the Quran concerns gifts, sustenance, and what creatures receive.

God provides sustenance and can meet human needs through His gifts.

Yet not every provision removes every need.

Someone with daily food may still need housing or treatment.

Aghnā may also concern meeting a need that is not necessarily financial.

Rizq and ghinā overlap in provision but are not identical.

غ ن ي (Ghinā) and ع ف ف (ʿAfāf)

ʿAfāf concerns restraint and holding oneself back from inappropriate conduct.

In Al-Baqarah 2:273, needy people's restraint leads uninformed observers to think them wealthy.

Al-Nūr 24:33 also commands restraint from those without the means to marry until God provides for them from His bounty.

The roots occur together but have different meanings.

ʿAfāf describes conduct and self-restraint; ghinā describes provision or sufficiency.

Someone may exercise restraint and remain needy.

A wealthy person may lack moral restraint.

ʿAfāf should therefore not be translated as wealth, nor ghinā as chastity.

غ ن ي (Ghinā) and ن ف ع (Nafʿ)

Nafʿ concerns the benefit obtained from something.

Many uses of aghnā also include usefulness.

Yet benefiting someone does not always make them independent.

A medicine may reduce pain slightly and be useful without curing the illness or removing the need for treatment.

When something meets a particular need adequately, however, it can be described as sufficient.

Verses containing lā yughnī sometimes concern precisely this inability to suffice or avert danger.

“Does not benefit” is natural in many such verses, but analysis should identify the need or danger against which that benefit is denied.

غ ن ي (Ghinā) and غ ن م (Ghunm)

غ ن م is a separate root concerning sheep and goats, and, in another branch, gains and acquisitions.

Ghunm concerns what someone acquires; ghinā describes their state of provision or independence.

Someone may acquire spoils and remain needy.

Someone may also be wealthy without acquiring new spoils on that occasion.

Obtaining a gain and possessing sufficient resources are related but distinct matters.

The roots' similar spelling must not obscure their different meanings.

8. Translation Proposals for Forqan

No single equivalent fits this family in every verse.

Translation must account for the derivative, subject, preposition, and topic of the sentence.

غَنِيّ and الْغَنِيّ

When applied to God, “free of need” is a clear, natural equivalent.

In Fāṭir 35:15:

الْغَنِيُّ الْحَمِيدُ — “the One free of need, the Praiseworthy.”

In Al-Baqarah 2:263:

غَنِيٌّ حَلِيمٌ — “free of need, Forbearing.”

When ghaniyy describes a person in contrast to financial poverty, “wealthy” or “well provided for” may be preferable.

For example, غَنِيًّا in Al-Nisāʾ 4:6 describes a guardian with sufficient resources.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:135, غَنِيًّا أَوْ فَقِيرًا can be translated “wealthy or poor.”

أَغْنَى and يُغْنِي

In verses concerning God's gifts, “provide sufficiency” or “enrich” is appropriate.

فَأَغْنَىٰ in Al-Ḍuḥā 93:8 means providing sufficiency after need.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:130, يُغْنِ اللَّهُ كُلًّا مِنْ سَعَتِهِ may be rendered “The God will provide each with sufficiency from His abundance.”

When sufficiency is denied, “does not avail,” “does not suffice,” or “cannot avert” may be more natural.

In Al-Ḥāqqah 69:28:

مَا أَغْنَىٰ عَنِّي مَالِيَهْ — “My wealth has not availed me.”

In Yūnus 10:36:

لَا يُغْنِي مِنَ الْحَقِّ شَيْئًا — “cannot replace truth in any respect.”

In Al-Ghāshiyah 88:7:

وَلَا يُغْنِي مِنْ جُوعٍ — “nor relieves hunger.”

In ʿAbasa 80:37, the construction calls for rendering يُغْنِيهِ as “occupies him.”

اسْتَغْنَى

“Became independent” suits some constructions.

In Al-ʿAlaq 96:7, however, رَآهُ makes “sees himself as independent” more precise.

ʿAbasa 80:5 likewise concerns someone behaving from a position of independence.

اسْتَغْنَى اللَّهُ in Al-Taghābun 64:6 expresses God's actual independence.

Istighnāʾ should therefore not always be restricted to illusion or arrogance.

غَنُوا and تَغْنَ

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:92, Hūd 11:68, and Hūd 11:95, يَغْنَوْا فِيهَا means to reside or live there.

“As though they had never lived there” is appropriate for this construction.

In Yūnus 10:24, تَغْنَ concerns thriving or flourishing in the context of green, cultivated land.

“Became wealthy” would mistranslate these uses.

Brief Root Meanings for Forqan Word Boxes

For forms concerning independence:

Brief root meaning: “Sufficiency and independence; possessing what meets a particular need.”

For forms concerning usefulness and sufficiency:

Brief root meaning: “Sufficing, benefiting, or providing independence in relation to a particular need.”

For forms concerning residence:

Brief root meaning: “Residing and living in a place; an attested usage of this family.”

The meaning in each verse must be written separately.

For example, أَغْنِيَاءَ in Al-Baqarah 2:273 concerns needy people whom an uninformed observer supposes wealthy.

اسْتَغْنَىٰ in Al-ʿAlaq 96:7 concerns seeing oneself as independent.

الْغَنِيُّ in Fāṭir 35:15 describes God's independence of creation.

يُغْنِي in Yūnus 10:36 concerns conjecture's inadequacy in relation to truth.

يَغْنَوْا in Al-Aʿrāf 7:92 concerns living and residing in a place.

These distinctions should also be preserved in word-by-word boxes, keeping the root's meaning, morphological form, and contextual function distinct.

9. Conclusion

غ ن ي is an extensive family in early Arabic whose principal field is sufficiency, independence, and provision.

غِنًى expresses provision or absence of need in a particular respect. غَنِيّ describes someone with that independence. أَغْنَى concerns providing enough or being useful, and اسْتَغْنَى may express becoming independent or considering oneself so.

Residence and living in a place are also attested in Arabic and the Quran. The branch concerning singing and sound exists in Arabic but is not represented among this root's Quranic uses.

Human wealth and God's freedom from need belong to different levels in the Quran.

A financially wealthy person still depends on many factors for continued life and other needs. God, by contrast, is independent of creatures, their possessions, and their worship.

This independence does not mean indifference to human conduct. The Quran treats actions, intentions, giving, disbelief, and gratitude within moral responsibility.

It also distinguishes actual independence from imagined independence.

Al-Baqarah 2:273 presents needy people whom others suppose wealthy because of their restraint.

In Al-ʿAlaq 96:7, seeing oneself as independent can lead to transgression.

Elsewhere, property, power, children, others' support, and even conjecture fail to suffice in particular situations.

These uses show that possessing a useful resource does not make it capable of meeting every need.

Wealth may be useful but cannot replace truth, justice, and responsibility. Power may help in one situation without guaranteeing every outcome. Conjecture may prompt investigation but does not itself replace established truth.

A final definition must preserve the family's attested branches:

غ ن ي:

1. Independence and provision: having sufficient means to meet a particular need; relative and limited in humans, and independence of creation when applied to God.

2. Sufficing and providing independence: supplying what meets a need, or benefiting and averting need or harm in a particular situation.

3. Residence and flourishing habitation: living or being established in a place; a distinct attested use in Arabic and some Quranic verses.

4. Singing and vocal performance: an attested Arabic branch requiring separate semantic examination, absent from the root's Quranic uses.

Ghinā should therefore not be equated exclusively with wealth, nor istighnāʾ invariably treated as arrogance.

Someone may achieve sufficiency in one respect while remaining needy in others. Someone may have abundant wealth yet behave in ways that make others dispense with them. An economically needy person may also be mistaken for someone wealthy.

The central distinction is between possessing resources, the sufficiency of those resources, and a person's perception of their own independence.

The Quran shows that human wealth does not cancel responsibility and that limited resources do not authorize superiority or disregard for truth. People may attain prosperity and sufficiency in life, but absolute independence in the Quran's divine usage belongs to God.
