Smiling: the face opening into a smile and its distinction from laughter

1. Introducing the root and its semantic field

The Arabic triliteral root B S M denotes smiling: the lips open slightly and the face takes on a smiling expression, without necessarily producing loud, audible laughter.

بَسَمَ, ابْتَسَمَ, and تَبَسَّمَ all belong to this semantic field. The family is closely related to Ḍ Ḥ K, which concerns laughing, although their uses are not all identical.

In many Arabic uses, tabassum is a gentler form of laughter. A smile may accompany joy, satisfaction, kindness, surprise, or a response to pleasant speech. Its cause and the person’s inner feelings, however, are not always specified by the word itself and must be understood from the situation and context.

This root occurs only once in the Qur’an, in the story of Solomon and the ant. After hearing the ant’s words, Solomon smiles and prays for his Lord to enable him to give thanks for blessings and perform righteous deeds.

This use illustrates both the physical meaning of smiling and the importance of context for understanding a human response. The root conveys facial movement and expression; the ethical significance follows from the ant’s words and the ensuing prayer.

2. Forms and meaning in early Arabic

Forms examined

B S M occurs only once in the Qur’an, as فَتَبَسَّمَ in Al-Naml 27:19.

تَبَسَّمَ is a past-tense Form V verb. Solomon is its subject, and the prefixed fa- connects his smile to the ant’s preceding speech.

Other forms are also established in Arabic:

بَسَمَ يَبْسِمُ: to smile; the basic Form I verb.

ابْتَسَمَ: he smiled; Form VIII.

تَبَسَّمَ: he smiled; Form V.

بَاسِم: someone smiling or having a smiling expression.

بَسَّام and مِبْسَام: someone who smiles frequently.

بَسْم and تَبَسُّم: verbal nouns referring to smiling.

مَبْسِم: the place of a smile, lexically referring to the mouth or front teeth.

Only the verb tabassama occurs in the Qur’an. The other forms illuminate the Arabic family but should not be presented as Qur’anic derivatives.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: one occurrence in Al-Naml 27:19

The semantic core

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris explains the root through exposing the front of the mouth in pleasure, describing it as less than laughter.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: the root definition

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise treats basama, ibtasama, and tabassama as close in meaning, presenting smiling as a gentler, more pleasing degree of laughing.

Al-Layth explains basama through opening the lips.

The family’s semantic core can therefore be understood as a smiling expression appearing on the face, particularly through the lips opening gently.

This should not become a rigid rule that every smile exposes all teeth, is entirely inaudible, or always expresses profound happiness.

Lexical sources mostly describe the familiar, typical expression. The extent of mouth opening, visible teeth, and accompanying sound can vary.

Lisān al-ʿArab, Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, and Al-ʿAyn: smiling and lexical forms

Does Form V give tabassama a special meaning?

Tabassama belongs to Form V, which in some verbs can express gradual development, effort, assuming a state, or other nuances.

Those morphological possibilities do not apply identically to every word.

For B S M, lexical sources record basama, ibtasama, and tabassama with very close meanings.

Form V in Al-Naml 27:19 therefore does not itself establish that Solomon deliberately fabricated a smile, struggled to smile, or gradually moved from an unfeeling state to intense laughter.

Such features would require independent evidence.

The clear and sufficient meaning here is “he smiled.”

3. Smiling in pre-Qur’anic Arabic witnesses

Smiling in Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd’s poetry

The Muʿallaqah attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd includes:

وَتَبْسِمُ عَنْ أَلْمَى كَأَنَّ مُنَوِّرًا
تَخَلَّلَ حُرَّ الرَّمْلِ دِعْصٌ لَهُ نَدِي

wa-tabsimu ʿan almā ka-anna munawwiran / takhallala ḥurra al-ramli diʿṣun lahu nadī

“She smiles, revealing teeth beside dusky gums, like white chamomile blossoms growing on a damp mound among pure, soft sands.”

The poet describes the beauty of a smile through visible teeth.

In his commentary, Ibn al-Anbārī explains almā here through the gums’ delicate dark color, which accentuates the teeth’s whiteness.

This witness shows tabassum describing a physical state of mouth and face in pre-Islamic poetry.

The image associates smiling with beauty and pleasure, but does not make beauty part of the root’s lexical definition.

The verb expresses smiling; the verse’s other images describe its beauty.

Ibn al-Anbārī’s commentary: Ṭarafah and dusky gums

Distinguishing smiling from teeth exposed in fear

Another witness occurs in the Muʿallaqah attributed to ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād:

لَمَّا رَآنِي قَدْ نَزَلْتُ أُرِيدُهُ
أَبْدَى نَوَاجِذَهُ لِغَيْرِ تَبَسُّمِ

lammā raʾānī qad nazaltu urīduhu / abdā nawājidhahu li-ghayri tabassumi

“When he saw that I had dismounted and was coming for him, he exposed his back teeth, but not in a smile.”

The poet describes an opponent whose teeth become visible in battle.

ʿAntarah explicitly says this exposure was لِغَيْرِ تَبَسُّمِ: it should not be understood as smiling.

Al-Zawzanī’s commentary connects the expression to lips drawing back and teeth appearing through fear and distress at the situation.

This witness matters because early Arabic did not treat every exposure of teeth as smiling.

A mouth may open and teeth become visible in anger, threat, or fear, but that differs from a smile in its established sense.

Limited textual variation is also transmitted, including لِغَيْرِ تَكَلُّمِ instead of لِغَيْرِ تَبَسُّمِ. The tabassum reading is an established poetic witness, but the variant should not be concealed.

Al-Zawzanī’s commentary: teeth exposed in fear

What the two witnesses show

In Ṭarafah, smiling accompanies the teeth’s visible beauty; in ʿAntarah, exposed teeth are explicitly distinguished from smiling.

B S M should therefore not be reduced to mechanical lip movement or displaying teeth.

The smiling expression and the manner in which it appears also matter in linguistic usage.

Nor do these witnesses establish that smiling invariably signifies affection, honesty, or genuine satisfaction. A person’s feelings and intent require context.

Figurative uses in Arabic

Lexical sources also record ابْتَسَمَ السَّحَابُ عَنِ الْبَرْقِ: a cloud opened and lightning appeared through it.

تَبَسَّمَ الطَّلْعُ likewise describes the opening of parts of a palm’s emerging flower cluster and the appearance of what its covering enclosed.

These uses draw an image-based similarity between opening lips and something emerging from behind a covering.

They are recorded in dictionaries, but not all can be dated precisely. They do not establish that the root originally meant every kind of covering splitting before the Qur’an, with smiling developing later.

The family’s direct, documented meaning is smiling; opening clouds and plant coverings are related figurative uses.

Some dictionaries also record مَا بَسَمْتُ فِي الشَّيْءِ for not tasting something. This restricted use should not be imposed on the Qur’an’s clear meaning of smiling.

4. The sole Qur’anic occurrence: Al-Naml 27:19

Al-Naml 27:19

فَتَبَسَّمَ ضَاحِكًا مِّن قَوْلِهَا وَقَالَ رَبِّ أَوْزِعْنِي أَنْ أَشْكُرَ نِعْمَتَكَ الَّتِي أَنْعَمْتَ عَلَيَّ وَعَلَىٰ وَالِدَيَّ وَأَنْ أَعْمَلَ صَالِحًا تَرْضَاهُ وَأَدْخِلْنِي بِرَحْمَتِكَ فِي عِبَادِكَ الصَّالِحِينَ

fa-tabassama ḍāḥikan min qawlihā wa-qāla rabbi awziʿnī an ashkura niʿmataka allatī anʿamta ʿalayya wa-ʿalā wālidayya wa-an aʿmala ṣāliḥan tarḍāhu wa-adkhilnī bi-raḥmatika fī ʿibādika al-ṣāliḥīna

“He smiled, laughing in response to her words, and said: My Lord, prompt me to give thanks for Your blessing bestowed upon me and my parents, and to do righteous work that pleases You; admit me by Your mercy among Your righteous servants.”

Connection with the ant’s words

Al-Naml 27:17 describes Solomon’s assembled forces of jinn, humans, and birds.

In Al-Naml 27:18, when they reach the ants’ valley, an ant warns the others to enter their dwellings lest Solomon and his forces crush them unknowingly.

Verse 19 then opens with fa-tabassama.

Fa- connects Solomon’s response with the preceding event. He receives the ant’s words and smiles.

مِنْ قَوْلِهَا also identifies the cause or source: his smile arose from that ant’s words.

This is therefore no unrelated smile; his response directly concerns the ant’s speech.

The verse does not expressly specify which part of that speech prompted the smile.

The ant’s awareness of danger, warning to others, and recognition that possible harm would be unintentional may have interested or surprised Solomon. These are contextual possibilities, not separate meanings established by tabassama itself.

What is clear is that Solomon smiles in response to the ant’s words.

Fa-tabassama ḍāḥikan: smiling and laughter together

The verse does not stop at fa-tabassama, but adds ḍāḥikan.

This combination matters linguistically.

Tabassama is the main verb, conveying smiling. Ḍāḥikan is an accusative active participle of Ḍ Ḥ K functioning as a circumstantial expression, describing Solomon’s state as he smiles.

Two words from closely related semantic fields thus occur in one construction.

Syntax does not require dividing it into two separate actions: an initial smile without laughter followed by an independent laugh.

Nor does ḍāḥikan itself establish that Solomon laughed loudly, guffawed, or laughed with unusual intensity.

Dictionaries generally treat smiling as a gentler form of laughter, so ḍāḥikan may further express the smile’s laughing, pleasant quality. Grammatical accounts also report both a confirming circumstance and an anticipated circumstance; the latter takes smiling as the beginning of laughter. Their semantic connection is clear, but more than one account of their precise relationship has been transmitted.

Al-Lubāb: confirming and anticipated circumstantial readings

In Persian, expressions equivalent to “he smiled while laughing” or “he smiled with laughter” can explain their relationship.

A base translation should preserve smiling and acknowledge ḍāḥikan without asserting unspecified sound or intensity.

Was Solomon’s smile mockery?

Tabassum itself does not mean mockery.

People may smile from affection, joy, surprise, or sometimes ridicule. Attributing a particular motive to a specific person requires evidence.

Here Solomon’s subsequent response matters.

After smiling, he turns to his Lord, asking to be enabled to thank Him for blessings, perform righteous work, and join the righteous servants.

This continuation places the smile within attention to divine favor and gratitude.

Understanding it as a pleasant response associated with awareness of blessing therefore fits the context better than attributing ridicule of the ant to him.

This does not require reconstructing his precise psychological state or all his inner motives with certainty.

Smiling, power, and gratitude

Solomon possesses a large army. The ant fears that its advance will harm other ants.

Yet when he receives its words, his response leads to a prayer of thanksgiving.

The verse juxtaposes two realities: his remarkable powers and resources, and his awareness that these blessings bring responsibilities of gratitude and righteous action.

This follows from the connected story and prayer, rather than B S M itself.

A smile alone does not establish humility, justice, or gratitude. In this context Solomon’s smile accompanies conduct that clarifies its ethical direction.

Nor should the manner in which Solomon receives the ant’s speech be extracted from tabassum. The Qur’an describes his receiving the words and responding, but the verb “smile” explains no scientific mechanism of human–ant communication.

5. Distinguishing B S M from related roots

B S M and Ḍ Ḥ K: smiling and laughing

The closest root to B S M is Ḍ Ḥ K.

Both concern a laughing expression, but ḍaḥik has a broader field, including overt or audible laughter and laughter from joy, surprise, or ridicule.

Their conjunction in Al-Naml 27:19 itself shows that smiling and laughing are connected in Qur’anic language.

Hūd 11:71 uses ضَحِكَتْ of Abraham’s wife.

Al-Najm 53:43 mentions laughing and weeping together.

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:29 places laughter in the context of wrongdoers’ treatment of believers, with ridicule established by context.

These uses show that laughing does not inherently signify commendable joy or ethical conduct.

Tabassum generally conveys a gentler form of laughter. Yet no absolute boundary should make every ḍaḥik audible and every tabassum entirely silent in all circumstances.

The main distinction is the linguistic tendency toward gentleness and more limited expression of laughter in tabassum.

B S M and B SH R: smiling and a bright expression

B SH R has a broader field involving human appearance, skin, and face, and in some derivatives glad tidings and visible signs of joy.

ʿAbasa 80:38 describes faces as مُسْفِرَةٌ, radiant; ʿAbasa 80:39 describes those faces as ضَاحِكَةٌ مُسْتَبْشِرَةٌ, laughing and rejoicing. These descriptions should not all be attributed to one word or one verse.

Here ḍāḥikah conveys the face’s laughing expression, while mustabshirah relates to joy and glad tidings.

This image resembles smiling, but tabassum does not itself mean receiving good news.

Someone may smile on hearing good news, but the smile is not itself news or a glad tiding.

Smiling and receiving glad tidings should therefore remain distinct in translation.

Study of B SH R

B S M and S R R: facial expression and inner state

Some Arabic and Qur’anic uses of S R R concern joy and happiness.

Joy is a state or experience, whereas tabassum has a specific facial expression.

Someone may be happy without smiling, or smile out of social courtesy without deep happiness.

Their real-life connection therefore does not make one the lexical meaning of the other.

In Al-Naml 27:19, tabassama identifies Solomon’s smile; the ant’s words and subsequent prayer clarify its emotional and ethical direction.

Study of S R R

B S M and K SH R: smiling and exposing teeth

Kashr can refer to exposing teeth, particularly in threat, anger, or similar states.

It can resemble smiling outwardly because teeth may become visible in either case.

ʿAntarah’s witness, however, shows that early Arabic distinguished exposed teeth from an actual smile.

Tabassum should not be reduced to the entirely physical definition “showing teeth.”

This comparison concerns Arabic usage; K SH R is an independent root and does not occur in the Qur’an.

B S M and ʿ B S: smiling and frowning

ʿAbasa 80:1 uses عَبَسَ, denoting drawing the face into a frown.

Outwardly, frowning and smiling can point in opposite directions.

This contrast should not become an absolute ethical rule.

Every smile does not establish goodness or honesty, nor every troubled face arrogance or wrongdoing.

A behavior’s ethical significance follows from its cause, situation, intent, and effects.

Solomon’s smile in Al-Naml 27:19 should similarly be understood through the story and prayer, rather than deriving all his ethical qualities solely from facial appearance.

6. Distinguishing B S M from bi-smi llāh

A possible error is to equate this root with بِسْمِ اللَّهِ.

Their unvowelled spellings resemble each other, but their linguistic structures differ.

بَسَمَ is an Arabic verb of B S M, concerning smiling.

بِسْمِ consists of the preposition bi- and the noun ism, “name.”

Arab linguists have disagreed about ism’s historical derivation and proposed different origins. That disagreement does not make bi-smi a derivative of B S M.

Bi-smi llāh therefore means neither The God smiling nor smiling in His name; its structure concerns “name” and the preposition bi-.

Both expressions notably occur within Solomon’s story.

Al-Naml 27:19 uses fa-tabassama of Solomon’s smile.

In Al-Naml 27:30, Solomon’s letter is introduced with بِسْمِ اللَّهِ الرَّحْمَٰنِ الرَّحِيمِ. The full expression is translated: In the name of The God, the Entirely Merciful, the Especially Merciful.

Despite occurring in one surah and story, their derivations differ. Similar spelling and subject matter do not establish a shared root.

7. Suggested translations for Forqan

The natural basic Persian equivalent of fa-tabassama is “then he smiled.”

It conveys the root’s clear meaning without inventing a new equivalent.

In Al-Naml 27:19, the following ḍāḥikan must also be acknowledged in translation or explanation.

Tabassum and ḍaḥik are not two completely independent, successive acts here; ḍāḥikan describes Solomon’s state while smiling.

Suggested renderings can therefore include “he smiled with laughter” or “he smiled while laughing.”

None should add guffawing, loud laughter, mockery, or a particular intensity without evidence.

A base translation should distinguish what the verb conveys from contextual explanation.

“He smiled” is tabassama’s direct meaning.

“At the ant’s words” identifies the response’s source.

“Laughing” or “while laughing” conveys ḍāḥikan.

Joy, surprise, attention to a small creature, gratitude, and humility before blessing are explored through the whole story and prayer, rather than tabassum’s lexical meaning.

Where needed, natural equivalents for non-Qur’anic derivatives include bāsim, “smiling”; bassām, “frequently smiling”; tabassum, “smiling”; and mabsim in its facial use, “the place of a smile” or “mouth.”

These lexical forms should not be presented as derivatives occurring in the Qur’an.

8. Conclusion

Arabic B S M denotes smiling, an expression generally accompanying the lips opening gently and distinguished from intense, audible laughter.

Pre-Islamic poetic witnesses confirm this field. Ṭarafah describes smiling through the teeth’s visible beauty. ʿAntarah explicitly distinguishes teeth exposed through fear and distress from smiling.

In the Qur’an, the root occurs once, as fa-tabassama in Al-Naml 27:19. Solomon smiles after hearing the ant’s words, then asks The God to enable gratitude, righteous work, and inclusion among the righteous servants.

Tabassama together with ḍāḥikan shows the closeness of smiling and laughing in this context. The construction alone does not establish the intensity, audibility, or precise psychological motive.

Nor can smiling always establish joy, sincerity, affection, or humility. A person’s state and intent require context and relevant evidence.

B S M is also distinct from ism in bi-smi llāh and should not be confused with it through similar spelling.

Proposed final definition: B S M denotes smiling: a smiling expression appearing on the face, generally with gentle opening of the lips and without intense laughter. Its Qur’anic use describes Solomon’s facial response to the ant’s words; its direct equivalent is “he smiled.” Brief root meaning: Smiling; a smiling expression appearing on the face with the lips opening gently.
