Root w-s-l — Seeking nearness and the means of reaching a goal

Qur’anic and lexical forms

الْوَسِيلَةَ
(al-wasīlah)
Means of approach; Qur’anic noun

وَسَلَ
(wasala)
Desire and approach; non-Qur’anic verb

(wāsil)

The Arabic root w-s-l concerns desire, seeking access, and drawing near to a person or goal. وَسِيلَة is something through which a person seeks to reach an aim, gain closeness to someone, or attain a desired position.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an, both times as الْوَسِيلَةَ. In both verses, God is the destination, and seeking a means concerns drawing near to Him.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:35, believers are commanded to seek a means toward God. In Al-Isrāʾ 17:57, even those whom others invoke are themselves described as seeking a means toward their Lord.

These uses connect wasīlah with seeking nearness to God, without naming a particular person or thing. Its lexical meaning should neither be restricted to one devotional act nor extended automatically to every instrument and intermediary in human life.

The general linguistic meaning, the two Qur’anic uses, and the technical concept of tawassul in later religious debates must remain distinct.

1. The root in early Arabic

In early Arabic, the family concerns seeking, desiring, and finding a way to approach another.

Someone wishing to reach a person or attain an aim could seek a wasīlah toward it.

It need not be a physical tool. An action, relationship, request, or suitable approach could serve the effort to reach a goal.

A witness attributed to the pre-Islamic period

A poem attributed to ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād, and in other transmissions to Khuzaz ibn Lawdhān, says:

إِنَّ الرِّجَالَ لَهُمْ إِلَيْكِ وَسِيلَةٌ

inna al-rijāla lahum ilayki wasīlatun

إِنْ يَأْخُذُوكِ تَكَحَّلِي وَتَخَضَّبِي

in yaʾkhudhūki takaḥḥalī wa-takhaḍḍabī

“Those men have a way of reaching you; if they seize you, apply kohl and henna.”

The line belongs to a poem discussing the importance of the poet’s horse and the danger of capture by enemies.

لَهُمْ إِلَيْكِ وَسِيلَةٌ concerns access or a way of reaching another person.

The context is human vulnerability and capture, rather than spiritual closeness or religious rank.

Here wasīlah denotes a way of attaining a goal, without the word itself judging whether that goal is right or wrong.

The attribution is disputed. Some early transmitters assign it to Khuzaz ibn Lawdhān, others to ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād. الْعَدُوَّ is also transmitted instead of الرِّجَالَ in the first hemistich.

Both poets are associated with the pre-Islamic period, but the disagreement prevents certain attribution to either one.

With that reservation, the witness shows wasīlah used for access to another in the tradition of poetry attributed to pre-Islamic times.

A witness from Labīd ibn Rabīʿah

A verse attributed to Labīd ibn Rabīʿah says:

أَرَى النَّاسَ لَا يَدْرُونَ مَا قَدْرُ أَمْرِهِمْ

arā al-nāsa lā yadrūna mā qadru amrihim

بَلَى كُلُّ ذِي لُبٍّ إِلَى اللَّهِ وَاسِلُ

balā kullu dhī lubbin ilā Allāhi wāsilu

“I see people unaware of the true measure of their affairs; indeed, every person of understanding turns toward God, seeking nearness.”

وَاسِل belongs to w-s-l.

It describes someone inclined toward God and seeking closeness to Him.

Unlike the previous verse, God is the destination here.

The two uses thus concern access to a person in human life and an inclination toward God respectively.

Labīd was a mukhaḍram poet, living through both the pre-Islamic period and the emergence of Islam. This verse’s precise date is unclear; without independent evidence it cannot be declared certainly pre-Qur’anic.

It nevertheless helps illuminate the family in early Arabic close to the period of revelation.

ذِي رَأْيٍ and ذِي دِينٍ are also transmitted instead of ذِي لُبٍّ. These variants do not alter the central sense of وَاسِل.

Meanings recorded in classical Arabic

Arabic dictionaries record several related meanings:

وَسَلَ إِلَيْهِ means he desired or sought to draw near to him.

وَسِيلَة means something through which a person seeks closeness to another.

الْوَسِيلَة can also denote proximity, rank, or standing with someone possessing authority or status.

Closeness to a king, for example, could mean enjoying standing or favor with him.

Two senses approach one another here: the means of access to an authority and the standing obtained with that authority.

They are not exactly the same, however.

Wasīlah may be the means of attaining closeness, while closeness or standing is the desired outcome.

In some lexical uses, wasīlah itself denotes rank or standing. That breadth must be preserved.

What the historical witnesses establish

Together, the evidence connects early Arabic w-s-l with desire, seeking access, and drawing near.

The closeness may be social or personal, or religiously directed toward God.

The evidence does not establish that all branches originally concerned religious closeness alone.

Nor does general usage establish that every modern sense, including physical tools and administrative intermediaries, is precisely what the Qur’an intends.

2. Forms and semantic center

Important Arabic forms include:

وَسَلَ: he desired, sought, or pursued closeness to a person or goal.

يَسِلُ: the imperfect of وَسَلَ; he seeks closeness or access.

وَسْل: seeking and desire in this family’s usage.

وَاسِل: someone seeking nearness or access to a goal.

وَسِيلَة: a means through which closeness or access is sought; in some uses, closeness or standing itself.

وَسَائِل: the plural of wasīlah; means employed to reach an aim.

وُسُل: another plural of wasīlah recorded in Arabic sources.

وَسَّلَ: used for seeking closeness or providing a means of approach.

تَوَسَّلَ: he sought closeness or access by means of something.

تَوَسُّل: seeking a means to reach a goal or approach another.

These forms are known in Arabic, but not all occur in the Qur’an.

Some dictionaries also record the rare sense of taking something by theft for tawassul. It differs from the usual sense of approach, and its historical relationship to Qur’anic usage is insufficiently established. It should not enter the definition of al-wasīlah here.

Actual Qur’anic forms

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an:

  • الْوَسِيلَةَ in Al-Māʾidah 5:35.
  • الْوَسِيلَةَ in Al-Isrāʾ 17:57.

Both are definite, singular nouns in the accusative.

In both, the noun is the object of ibtaghā: a plural imperative in Al-Māʾidah and a plural imperfect in Al-Isrāʾ.

Tawassala, wassala, wāsil, and the plural wasāʾil do not occur in the Qur’an.

This matters because later technical uses of tawassul should not immediately replace the word actually used in the Qur’an.

Semantic center

The principal semantic center is:

Desiring and striving for closeness or access to a goal, and seeking the means that makes such approach possible.

Wāsil foregrounds the person seeking closeness or access.

Wasīlah foregrounds the means sought, although some lexical uses also denote closeness and standing themselves.

The word alone names neither a particular human being nor one specific devotional act, and it does not inherently ascribe independent power to bring someone to their destination.

The object of the seeking and the conditions of access must be understood from the sentence and context.

3. Al-Māʾidah 5:35 — Seeking a means toward God

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا اتَّقُوا اللَّهَ وَابْتَغُوا إِلَيْهِ الْوَسِيلَةَ وَجَاهِدُوا فِي سَبِيلِهِ لَعَلَّكُمْ تُفْلِحُونَ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū ittaqū Allāha wa-ibtaghū ilayhi al-wasīlata wa-jāhidū fī sabīlihi laʿallakum tufliḥūn

Al-Māʾidah 5:35

“O you who have believed, be mindful of The God, seek the means of nearness to Him, and strive in His way, so that you may prosper.”

Three commands occur together:

اتَّقُوا اللَّهَ

ittaqū Allāha

Al-Māʾidah 5:35

وَابْتَغُوا إِلَيْهِ الْوَسِيلَةَ

wa-ibtaghū ilayhi al-wasīlah

Al-Māʾidah 5:35

وَجَاهِدُوا فِي سَبِيلِهِ

wa-jāhidū fī sabīlihi

Al-Māʾidah 5:35

First comes taqwa, then seeking a means toward God, then striving in His way.

Their association matters for understanding wasīlah.

Ibtaghū: seeking with desire and effort

وَابْتَغُوا comes from b-gh-y and here means “seek” or “pursue.”

The verse commands seeking a means, rather than merely possessing one.

Seeking involves desire and effort.

Someone wishing to reach a destination must seek the way there. Claiming attachment to the goal is not the same as following the path.

Here the destination is God.

A person is thus commanded to seek the appropriate means of drawing near to their Lord.

Ilayhi: the direction of the seeking

In وَابْتَغُوا إِلَيْهِ الْوَسِيلَةَ, the pronoun -hi refers to God.

إِلَيْهِ means “toward Him.”

It specifies the direction of seeking a means.

The ultimate aim is nearness to God, rather than making another person or thing the final object of worship.

The preposition ilā alone does not determine every permissible instance of a means.

Its association with taqwa and striving in God’s way nevertheless places the seeking within service to Him and movement toward Him.

How do taqwa and striving relate to the means?

اتَّقُوا concerns mindfulness and guarding oneself.

جَاهِدُوا concerns striving and exerting one’s ability in the way specified by the verse.

Seeking the means stands between these commands.

The sequence connects the seeking with human conduct and responsibility.

Someone might claim to seek closeness to God while wronging others financially, withholding their rights, or knowingly concealing truth.

Merely naming a practice or attaching oneself to a person of standing cannot replace taqwa and reforming one’s conduct.

The Qur’an places mindfulness and striving alongside seeking the means.

This does not make wasīlah lexically identical with taqwa or jihād alone.

They are distinct commands sharing a context. Mindfulness and striving provide clear directions and examples of action toward God, without defining al-wasīlah as a closed list of practices.

Does the means here designate a human intermediary?

Al-Māʾidah 5:35 names no human, angel, Prophet, or other being as the means.

The command addresses believers directly, with God as its destination.

This verse alone therefore does not establish that a believer must invoke another person or make that person an intermediary in supplication.

Conversely, the general meaning of wasīlah alone cannot establish a prohibition on every form of human assistance, learning from others, or using the means of ordinary life.

The question is whether this verse commands placing a person between oneself and God in worship and supplication.

The text names no such person. Further claims about particular forms of mediation require separate evidence.

It commands seeking a means toward God alongside mindfulness and striving in His way.

The neighboring verses

Al-Māʾidah 5:34 speaks of repentance and divine forgiveness.

Al-Māʾidah 5:35 calls believers to mindfulness, seeking a means, and striving.

Al-Māʾidah 5:36 states that even if those who disbelieve possessed everything on Earth and as much again to ransom themselves from the punishment of the Day of Resurrection, it would not be accepted.

This sequence shows that a relationship with God cannot be replaced by wealth or a transaction designed to evade divine reckoning.

Seeking the means in Al-Māʾidah 5:35 is therefore not finding a way to nullify God’s justice and command.

It concerns seeking His nearness through mindfulness and action.

4. Al-Isrāʾ 17:57 — Even the nearest servants seek a means

أُولَٰئِكَ الَّذِينَ يَدْعُونَ يَبْتَغُونَ إِلَىٰ رَبِّهِمُ الْوَسِيلَةَ أَيُّهُمْ أَقْرَبُ وَيَرْجُونَ رَحْمَتَهُ وَيَخَافُونَ عَذَابَهُ ۚ إِنَّ عَذَابَ رَبِّكَ كَانَ مَحْذُورًا

ulāʾika alladhīna yadʿūna yabtaghūna ilā rabbihimu al-wasīlata ayyuhum aqrabu wa-yarjūna raḥmatahu wa-yakhāfūna ʿadhābahu inna ʿadhāba rabbika kāna maḥdhūrā

Al-Isrāʾ 17:57

“Those whom they invoke themselves seek the means of nearness to their Lord, even the nearest among them; they hope for His mercy and fear His punishment. Your Lord’s punishment is indeed to be guarded against.”

This verse is especially important: unlike Al-Māʾidah 5:35, it describes beings who themselves seek the means, rather than only commanding that search.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:56 supplies the immediate context.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:56 — Those invoked do not possess power to remove harm

قُلِ ادْعُوا الَّذِينَ زَعَمْتُمْ مِنْ دُونِهِ فَلَا يَمْلِكُونَ كَشْفَ الضُّرِّ عَنْكُمْ وَلَا تَحْوِيلًا

quli udʿū alladhīna zaʿamtum min dūnihi fa-lā yamlikūna kashfa al-ḍurri ʿankum wa-lā taḥwīlā

Al-Isrāʾ 17:56

“Say: Call upon those whom you have claimed apart from Him; they possess no power to remove harm from you or to transfer it.”

The audience is confronted with those they have invoked apart from God.

The verse denies those invoked possession of the power to remove or transfer harm. The word “independent” is not in the verse and should not be added to the direct translation to narrow that denial.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:57 then states another fact about those beings: they themselves seek a means toward their Lord.

The verses distinguish God from those invoked apart from Him.

Alladhīna yadʿūna: who seeks the means?

In أُولَٰئِكَ الَّذِينَ يَدْعُونَ, the object pronoun after يَدْعُونَ is omitted. The preceding verse clarifies the sense: those whom others invoke.

يَبْتَغُونَ then refers to those invoked.

The very beings whom others call upon themselves seek a means toward their Lord.

The distinction between invokers and those invoked must not disappear in translation.

A rendering suggesting that only the worshippers seek a means obscures the verse’s central argument.

Those receiving others’ invocations themselves need closeness, mercy, and guidance from God.

Ayyuhum aqrab: even the nearest is a seeker

The phrase has more than one grammatical interpretation.

One takes أَيُّهُمْ as a partial apposition to the subject pronoun of يَبْتَغُونَ: even the nearest among those invoked seeks a means of approach to God.

Another understands seeking as eager aspiration: they strive to be nearer to God, as in “which of them will be nearest?” This concerns aspiration toward closeness, rather than simply identifying another person as an intermediary.

The analyses differ in how the phrase connects to the preceding clause, but both depict beings who are not independent of God or free of need for Him.

They too seek closeness to their Lord.

If even the nearest servants seek a means of approach, their closeness cannot be equated with independent power free of God’s will.

Hope for mercy and fear of punishment

The verse continues:

وَيَرْجُونَ رَحْمَتَهُ وَيَخَافُونَ عَذَابَهُ

wa-yarjūna raḥmatahu wa-yakhāfūna ʿadhābahu

Al-Isrāʾ 17:57

They hope for God’s mercy and fear His punishment.

The seekers themselves stand in a relationship of service, hope, and responsibility toward their Lord.

Closeness is not presented as release from servanthood or freedom from need for mercy.

Wasīlah itself does not mean mercy or fear. Those states are expressed by the separate verbs yarjūna and yakhāfūna.

The meaning concerns a means of approach; the context associates that approach with hope for mercy and fear of punishment.

Does the verse name a particular group of beings?

Historical explanations propose different referents, including angels, jinn, and revered human beings.

The verse itself does not name one particular group.

Its construction makes clear that the beings discussed serve their Lord: they seek a means toward Him, hope for His mercy, and fear His punishment.

A particular historical person or group should not be inserted into the translation without independent evidence.

Nor should every ordinary request for education, help, or treatment from another person be classified as invoking beings مِنْ دُونِهِ on this verse’s basis.

The preceding verse concerns invoking beings placed in a position apart from God and imagined to possess power to remove harm.

Those contextual qualifications must be preserved when considering present-day practices.

5. Means, tawassul, and intermediaries: distinct concepts

In modern Persian, wasīlah commonly denotes something used to accomplish a task.

A pen enables writing, a road leads to a destination, and a tool can make work easier.

An intermediary is usually a person or thing standing between two parties and establishing a connection.

The concepts are not always identical.

A means of reaching a goal need not be a third person at all.

Conversely, someone may mediate communication without becoming its destination or object.

Early Arabic wasīlah likewise concerned access and approach. It should be distinguished from “intermediary” and later technical religious uses of tawassul, without assuming a definite historical order for every term’s emergence.

Must a wasīlah be a person?

No.

Neither Qur’anic occurrence names a particular person as the means.

Al-Māʾidah 5:35 places seeking the means alongside mindfulness and striving in God’s way.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:57 describes those invoked by others as themselves seeking a means toward God.

The central concern is therefore nearness to God, rather than specifying someone to communicate with God in a person’s place.

This should not become the separate lexical claim that wasīlah can never denote a human intermediary anywhere in Arabic.

General language imposes no such restriction.

Linguistic possibility must be distinguished from establishing a meaning in a particular verse.

The possibility of calling someone or something a means in general language does not establish that the Qur’an prescribed that person or thing as a means in worship and supplication.

Does wasīlah mean righteous action alone?

Righteous action, taqwa, salat, steadfastness, and striving in God’s way can contribute to drawing near to the Lord.

For example, Al-Baqarah 2:45 and Al-Baqarah 2:153 command seeking help through steadfastness and salat.

Al-Māʾidah 5:35 likewise places mindfulness and striving alongside seeking the means.

These connections link one’s relationship with God to conduct and responsibility.

Yet the noun wasīlah is not defined by a restricted list of acts in either occurrence.

It is more accurate to say that in Qur’anic context, wasīlah concerns seeking nearness to God and the ways of serving Him, rather than reducing the entire lexical meaning to one practice.

Technical tawassul and Qur’anic wasīlah

In later periods, tawassul became a technical term in religious discussion.

Some understand it as approaching God through righteous deeds.

Others discuss supplication mentioning the standing of Prophets or righteous people.

In some practices, revered people themselves are addressed with requests and invocations.

These practices differ in structure and object.

Asking God, mentioning someone’s standing with Him, asking a living and present person to pray, and invoking a being credited with independent power or divine authority are four distinguishable situations.

They should not all receive one judgment merely because they share the name tawassul.

Conversely, none is automatically endorsed simply by citing wasīlah in Al-Māʾidah 5:35.

Assessing a devotional practice requires examining whom it addresses, what it requests, what authority it attributes to another, and the Qur’an’s clear statements on invocation and worship.

A direct relationship with God

In Al-Baqarah 2:186, responding to the servants’ question about Him, God declares His nearness and response to the caller’s supplication.

Ghāfir 40:60 directly commands ادْعُونِي: “Call upon Me.”

These verses establish the relationship of supplication and response between human beings and God.

Seeking a means in Al-Māʾidah 5:35 should not imply that God is distant and inaccessible, with another person required to convey a caller’s voice to Him.

Other verses also command cooperation, teaching, assistance, and proper human relationships.

Direct supplication does not mean people have no need for one another’s help and social cooperation.

Using human assistance and resources differs from assigning another being a specifically divine position.

6. Comparison with related roots

w-s-l (wasīlah) compared with w-ṣ-l (waṣl)

The roots are close in sound and meaning but have different consonants.

W-ṣ-l concerns joining, connection, and one thing reaching another.

Waṣl can express the connection itself.

Wasīlah principally concerns a means of approach or access.

One may explain wasīlah as that through which a person approaches a goal, whereas waṣl emphasizes joining or reaching itself.

This is not absolute, since wasīlah is also recorded for closeness and connection.

Sound resemblance alone nevertheless does not make the roots identical.

وَصِيلَة belongs to w-ṣ-l and must not be confused with وَسِيلَة from w-s-l.

w-s-l (wasīlah) compared with q-r-b (qurb)

Q-r-b concerns nearness and approaching.

Both Qur’anic occurrences of wasīlah likewise concern nearness to God.

Qurb describes closeness itself or the state of being near.

Wasīlah can be something sought in order to attain it.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:57 contains both:

الْوَسِيلَةَ

al-wasīlah

Al-Isrāʾ 17:57

and

أَيُّهُمْ أَقْرَبُ

ayyuhum aqrab

Al-Isrāʾ 17:57

Their association connects seeking a means with being nearer or seeking closeness.

It does not make wasīlah and qurb exact synonyms in every Arabic use.

Closeness to God here should also not be converted into measurable physical distance; it concerns the relationship of service to the Lord.

w-s-l (wasīlah) compared with s-b-l (sabīl)

Sabīl means a way or path, often concerning the direction of life and conduct.

Wasīlah can be a means sought to approach a destination.

Al-Māʾidah 5:35 contains both expressions:

وَابْتَغُوا إِلَيْهِ الْوَسِيلَةَ

wa-ibtaghū ilayhi al-wasīlah

Al-Māʾidah 5:35

and

وَجَاهِدُوا فِي سَبِيلِهِ

wa-jāhidū fī sabīlihi

Al-Māʾidah 5:35

They connect seeking a means with striving in God’s way.

Wasīlah and sabīl do not, however, mean the same thing in every use.

Sabīl emphasizes a path and direction; wasīlah here concerns seeking nearness to the destination.

Reducing al-wasīlah in Al-Māʾidah 5:35 to “road” alone therefore loses its emphasis on approach and closeness.

w-s-l (wasīlah) compared with b-gh-y (ibtighāʾ)

Both verses containing al-wasīlah use ibtaghā.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:35:

وَابْتَغُوا

wa-ibtaghū

Al-Māʾidah 5:35

And in Al-Isrāʾ 17:57:

يَبْتَغُونَ

yabtaghūn

Al-Isrāʾ 17:57

Ibtighāʾ concerns seeking something.

Wasīlah is what is sought in these sentences.

The verb and noun therefore have different roles.

The act of seeking comes from ibtighāʾ; the means or desired closeness comes from wasīlah.

Desire and seeking are also recorded within w-s-l itself. Their combination naturally emphasizes seeking closeness without requiring lengthy, repetitive translation.

w-s-l (wasīlah) compared with sh-f-ʿ (shafāʿah)

Sh-f-ʿ concerns pairing and joining one thing to another; intercession concerns the intercessor’s role and effect under particular conditions.

The two Qur’anic uses of wasīlah concern seeking nearness to God.

Later religious discussion may associate the concepts, but their lexical meanings and direct Qur’anic uses are distinct.

Al-wasīlah in Al-Māʾidah 5:35 is not identified as “a particular person’s intercession.”

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:57, those invoked themselves seek closeness to their Lord.

Replacing “means” with “intercessor” without independent evidence thus restricts and changes the verse’s meaning.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

In Persian, retaining wasīlah in both occurrences is useful: the word is familiar and its precise meaning can be explained in the root study.

To avoid implying any modern tool or intermediary, the direction toward closeness should remain clear.

Al-Māʾidah 5:35

“O you who have believed, be mindful of The God, seek the means of nearness to Him, and strive in His way, so that you may prosper.”

The rendering has three features:

It preserves ibtaghū with “seek.”

It makes the destination ilayhi clear.

It does not restrict al-wasīlah to one person, action, or instrument.

“Seek a means toward Him” is also close to the Arabic construction, though a general reader may need explanation.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:57

“Those whom they invoke themselves seek the means of nearness to their Lord, even the nearest among them; they hope for His mercy and fear His punishment. Your Lord’s punishment is indeed to be guarded against.”

This chooses the grammatical reading concerning the nearest among those seeking the means.

The explanation should also preserve the other reading: their eager striving toward a nearer position, rather than treating the phrase as merely identifying a particular intermediary.

Both retain the central point: those invoked by others themselves need closeness to their Lord.

Practical equivalents in the family

وَسَلَ إِلَيْهِ may be rendered “he sought closeness to him” or “sought his favor.”

وَاسِل can mean “a seeker of closeness” or “someone seeking to approach.”

وَسِيلَة may mean a means, a way of access, or a means of approach, according to context.

تَوَسَّلَ may mean “he sought a means” or “used something to approach another.”

These equivalents should not be transferred unexamined to every later religious technical use.

In both Qur’anic occurrences, God is the destination. “A means of nearness to God” therefore explains their context more accurately than “any intermediary.” These proposals do not replace existing verse word boxes.

8. Conclusion

Arabic w-s-l concerns desire, seeking access, and approaching.

Poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period shows wasīlah used for reaching another person without an inherently religious sense.

In the verse attributed to Labīd, wāsil describes someone inclined toward God and seeking nearness, though its precise date is unknown.

Classical Arabic records wasīlah for the means of approach, closeness itself, and sometimes standing with someone of authority.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an.

Al-Māʾidah 5:35 commands believers to seek the means of nearness alongside mindfulness and striving in God’s way.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:57 portrays even the beings whom others invoke as seeking a means toward their Lord, hoping for mercy and fearing punishment.

God is the destination of seeking, and even the nearest servants are neither independent nor without need before Him.

Wasīlah itself names no particular person, and neither verse identifies someone as a necessary intermediary between human beings and God.

Nor can lexical meaning alone decide the permissibility of every form of human assistance or use of ordinary means. The nature of the act and other Qur’anic verses must be considered.

The root concerns desiring and striving for closeness or access; wasīlah is the means through which it is sought. In the two Qur’anic uses, the approach is directed toward God and associated with service, mindfulness, striving, and hope for His mercy.

Wasīlah, qurb, sabīl, ibtighāʾ, and shafāʿah must remain distinct.

Wasīlah is sought to attain qurb, the desired closeness; sabīl names the path and direction; ibtighāʾ is the act of seeking; shafāʿah is a separate concept that should not replace wasīlah without evidence.

“The means of nearness to God” is therefore a clear, defensible explanation of al-wasīlah for Forqan.

Seeking a means toward God must not become seeking to evade His justice, responsibility, or command. The Qur’an places it alongside mindfulness and striving, and describes even the nearest servants as needing their Lord’s closeness and mercy.
