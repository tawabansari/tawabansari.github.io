1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

The Arabic root ح س ب (ḥ s b) has a broad semantic family. Its principal branches include counting and measuring, calculating and examining conduct, supposing or regarding something in a particular way, and being sufficient. Earlier Arabic also uses حَسَب (ḥasab) for social standing and honour.

These meanings approach one another without being identical. Someone can count something without judging its rightness, suppose something without counting, or find something sufficient without any numerical calculation.

The root occurs 109 times in eight morphological and lexical groups in the Qur’an:

  • حَسِبَ (ḥasiba) and its verbal forms: 44 occurrences; supposing, thinking, or regarding something as being a certain way.
  • حِسَاب (ḥisāb): 39 occurrences; reckoning, counting, calculation, examination, or assessment, according to context.
  • حَسْب (ḥasb): 11 occurrences; sufficiency or being enough.
  • حَسِيب (ḥasīb): four occurrences; usages concerning reckoning, oversight, and sufficiency.
  • حُسْبَان (ḥusbān): three occurrences; twice concerning the reckoning and order of the sun and moon, and once a destructive calamity descending.
  • حَاسَبَ (ḥāsaba) and its verbal forms: three occurrences; calling to account or subjecting to reckoning.
  • اِحْتَسَبَ (iḥtasaba) and its verbal forms: three occurrences; taking into account, expecting, or anticipating.
  • حَاسِبِين (ḥāsibīn): two occurrences; the plural of ḥāsib, reckoners or those who take account.

Arabic also records حَسَب (ḥasab), social honour or standing; حِسْبَة (ḥisbah), reckoning or expectation of reward; and مُحَاسَبَة (muḥāsabah). These particular forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

This range prevents choosing one equivalent for every derivative. The verb ḥasiba can mean “supposed,” ḥasb “sufficient,” and ḥisāb “examination of deeds” in different verses.

2. Semantic centres in earlier Arabic

Counting and taking into account

One clear meaning is counting things and determining their quantity.

حَسَبَ الشَّيْءَ (ḥasaba al-shayʾa) means counting or calculating something. In some usages, ḥisāb and ḥusbān likewise denote counting, calculation, and determining quantity.

This meaning relates to daily life, trade, measurement, dividing property, determining time, and assessing quantities. Trading, dividing produce, allocating shares, paying debts, and tracking days and years all require reckoning.

Reckoning is not merely adding several numbers. It can determine proportions, measures, shares, or quantities. The Arabic expression بِحَسَبِ (bi-ḥasabi) can mean “according to the measure of” or “in proportion to.”

For example, saying that someone’s reward corresponds to their work establishes a relationship between the work and the reward’s amount. This usage concerns determining proportion and measure.

Not every use should nevertheless be restricted to mathematical operations. Arabic also extends reckoning to assessing conduct and examining it.

A poetic witness attributed to Al-Nābighah

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī describes a woman assessing a flock so that the birds, half their number, and her own dove total one hundred. In this account, the flock numbers 66; adding half, 33, makes 99, and her dove completes one hundred. Part of the poem reads:

فَحَسَبُوهُ فَأَلْفَوْهُ كَمَا حَسَبَتْ
تِسْعًا وَتِسْعِينَ لَمْ تَنْقُصْ وَلَمْ تَزِدِ

“They reckoned it and found it as she had calculated: ninety-nine, neither less nor more.”

It continues:

فَكَمُلَتْ مِائَةٌ فِيهَا حَمَامَتُهَا
وَأَسْرَعَتْ حِسْبَةً فِي ذَٰلِكَ الْعَدَدِ

“The number reached one hundred with her dove included; she was swift in reckoning that total.”

Here ḥasabū, ḥasabat, and ḥisbah concern actual counting and calculation. Ninety-nine is the flock’s number plus half of it, rather than the number of birds in the flock alone. This is not baseless guessing: the woman’s assessment is checked through calculation and a specified number.

Literary tradition attributes the poem to Al-Nābighah, while the wording and explanation of some expressions vary in transmission. It is therefore an attributed witness from earlier poetry, rather than conclusive evidence for when the root originated or precisely when each verse was composed.

From calculation to supposition

حَسِبَ (ḥasiba), with kasrah on sīn, commonly means supposing or thinking. Lexicons also record حِسْبَان (ḥisbān), with kasrah on ḥāʾ, for supposition; its pronunciation should not be equated with Qur’anic حُسْبَان (ḥusbān).

Someone who supposes a person to be wealthy has judged their circumstances, but that judgment may be right or wrong.

A cognitive relationship is recognizable: calculation determines a quantity, while supposition involves mentally assessing something in a particular way.

This semantic relationship does not prove that supposition historically arose from numerical counting. Both meanings are recognized in Arabic and have independent usages.

The vocalized forms matter. حَسَبَ (ḥasaba), counting, differs from حَسِبَ (ḥasiba), supposing, although their unvocalized spellings resemble one another.

The root’s basic verbal occurrences in the Qur’an concern supposition, while ḥisāb and ḥusbān concern counting and calculation in some verses.

Sufficiency and reaching what is needed

Another important branch is حَسْب (ḥasb), sufficiency.

حَسْبُكَ (ḥasbuka) means “enough for you,” and حَسْبِي (ḥasbī), “enough for me.”

Some lexical usages also record أَحْسَبَ (aḥsaba) for giving someone enough to satisfy their need.

This constitutes an independent application of measure and quantity: something’s amount or capacity is considered adequate for the need in question.

Sufficiency need not result from numerical calculation. Someone can say “The God is sufficient for me” without discussing a numerical amount or calculated share.

Sufficiency in such a statement concerns trust, capacity, and adequacy.

Ḥasab: social honour and standing

Earlier Arabic uses حَسَب (ḥasab) for standing, honour, and social distinction.

Lexical explanations sometimes relate it to a person’s own good deeds and sometimes to the achievements of their forebears.

Ḥasab has a recognized distinction from نَسَب (nasab). Nasab concerns kinship and lineage, whereas ḥasab can concern worthy deeds and distinctions counted to the credit of a person or family.

A verse attributed to Al-Mutalammis reads:

وَمَنْ كَانَ ذَا نَسْبٍ كَرِيمٍ وَلَمْ يَكُنْ
لَهُ حَسَبٌ كَانَ اللَّئِيمَ الْمُذَمَّمَا

“Whoever has noble lineage but no honour of his own will be base and blameworthy.”

The verse distinguishes family descent from worth and honour obtained through conduct. In the quoted poetic wording, نَسْب (nasb), with no vowel after sīn, is a shortened pronunciation of nasab accommodating the metre.

Some lexical explanations connect ḥasab with reckoning because people enumerated their own virtues and achievements, or those of their forebears, when boasting.

That connection is intelligible, but does not make ḥasb in حَسْبُنَا اللَّهُ (ḥasbunā Allāh) a Qur’anic reference to family standing or social honour.

Ḥasb, sufficiency, and ḥasab, social honour, are distinct usages. The latter does not occur in the Qur’an.

More distant lexical branches

Arabic sources record further meanings for ḥusbān, including small arrows, calamity, and harmful phenomena. A small cushion is also recorded as حُسْبَانَة (ḥusbānah), with حِسْبَانَة (ḥisbānah), beginning with kasrah, in some sources. This variation should remain visible in lexical reporting.

أَحْسَب (aḥsab) also describes a person or animal with a particular colouring of skin or hair in some usages.

These meanings cannot be traced to counting, supposition, or sufficiency without evidence. Older explanations sometimes divide the family into several distinct semantic origins.

For this root, preserving different branches is more accurate than inventing an apparently comprehensive but vague definition.

3. Reckoning: from measuring time to examining deeds

Reckoning the sun and moon

Al-Anʿām 6:96

فَالِقُ الْإِصْبَاحِ وَجَعَلَ اللَّيْلَ سَكَنًا وَالشَّمْسَ وَالْقَمَرَ حُسْبَانًا ۚ ذَٰلِكَ تَقْدِيرُ الْعَزِيزِ الْعَلِيمِ

“He is the cleaver of dawn, and He made the night a time of rest, and the sun and moon for reckoning and measurement. That is the determination of the Invincible, the Knowing.”

Here حُسْبَانًا (ḥusbānan) accompanies the sun and moon.

The context describes dawn emerging, the night’s rest, and the order of the sun and moon. Ḥusbān therefore concerns reckoning, measurement, and calculable order.

The verse points to the order of celestial movements and their relationship with determining time.

Another verse expresses that relationship more explicitly.

Yūnus 10:5

هُوَ الَّذِي جَعَلَ الشَّمْسَ ضِيَاءً وَالْقَمَرَ نُورًا وَقَدَّرَهُ مَنَازِلَ لِتَعْلَمُوا عَدَدَ السِّنِينَ وَالْحِسَابَ ۚ مَا خَلَقَ اللَّهُ ذَٰلِكَ إِلَّا بِالْحَقِّ ۚ يُفَصِّلُ الْآيَاتِ لِقَوْمٍ يَعْلَمُونَ

“He is the One who made the sun radiance and the moon light, and determined stations for it so that you might know the number of years and the reckoning [of time]. The God created these only in truth. He sets out the signs in detail for people who know.”

The relationship between the moon’s movement, its stations, and knowledge of years and reckoning is explicit here.

عَدَد (ʿadad) and حِسَاب (ḥisāb) stand together. ʿAdad concerns number, while ḥisāb can concern calculating and determining temporal relationships.

Their combination does not establish that ʿadad always means simple counting and ḥisāb always complex calculation. The precise difference depends on each construction.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:12 likewise connects knowing the number of years and reckoning with night and day.

Measuring time and recognizing the order of celestial phenomena are therefore clear Qur’anic applications of ḥisāb.

Al-shamsu wa-l-qamaru bi-ḥusbān

Al-Raḥmān 55:5

الشَّمْسُ وَالْقَمَرُ بِحُسْبَانٍ

“The sun and moon follow a determined reckoning.”

بِحُسْبَانٍ (bi-ḥusbānin) expresses the calculated order of the sun and moon.

Its construction differs from Al-Anʿām 6:96. There the sun and moon follow the verb جَعَلَ (jaʿala), “made,” in connection with ḥusbānan. Here a nominal sentence uses bi-ḥusbān to describe their state.

The difference allows the role of the sun and moon in reckoning time to stand out in Al-Anʿām, and their ordered movement in Al-Raḥmān.

It does not create two unrelated meanings of ḥusbān. Both belong to reckoning, order, and measure.

Nor does the verse supply a particular physical theory of orbits or modern calculation. Scientific inquiry can examine this calculable order without making modern terminology or models part of the word’s lexical definition.

Reckoning and determining measure

Ḥisāb approaches تَقْدِير (taqdīr) in some usages because both can involve determining measure and quantity.

Taqdīr appears separately in Al-Anʿām 6:96, however. Its entire semantic contribution should not be transferred in advance into ḥusbān.

Reckoning can express the method or result of determining a quantity, while taqdīr in its measuring usage concerns establishing or assigning measure.

Ḥusbān can therefore be understood as reckoning and measurable order without equating it with every meaning of taqdīr.

4. Reckoning deeds: examination, judgment, and accountability

The day of reckoning

Ibrāhīm 14:41

رَبَّنَا اغْفِرْ لِي وَلِوَالِدَيَّ وَلِلْمُؤْمِنِينَ يَوْمَ يَقُومُ الْحِسَابُ

“Our Lord, forgive me, my parents, and those who have faith on the day when the reckoning is established.”

Reckoning here no longer concerns counting years. It concerns the day when people will answer before The God.

يَقُومُ الْحِسَابُ (yaqūmu al-ḥisāb) expresses the establishment of reckoning: examination and assessment of deeds and their outcome.

The day of reckoning also appears elsewhere, including Ṣād 38:16, Ṣād 38:26, Ṣād 38:53, and Ghāfir 40:27.

It should not be reduced to a day on which deeds are merely added into numerical totals. Qur’anic verses connect reckoning with truth, justice, recompense, and responsibility.

Scales, justice, and reckoning

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:47

وَنَضَعُ الْمَوَازِينَ الْقِسْطَ لِيَوْمِ الْقِيَامَةِ فَلَا تُظْلَمُ نَفْسٌ شَيْئًا ۖ وَإِنْ كَانَ مِثْقَالَ حَبَّةٍ مِنْ خَرْدَلٍ أَتَيْنَا بِهَا ۗ وَكَفَىٰ بِنَا حَاسِبِينَ

“We shall set up the scales of justice for the day of resurrection, so no soul will suffer any injustice. Even if [a deed] weighs a mustard seed, We shall bring it forth. We are sufficient as reckoners.”

The form حَاسِبِينَ (ḥāsibīn) occurs here.

Reckoning accompanies the scales of justice and the denial of injustice. In this context, it is therefore more than recording numbers: it relates to a just examination.

The verse leaves out nothing, however small. That precision emerges from the mustard seed image, the scales of justice, and the denial of injustice together, rather than the root alone.

وَكَفَىٰ بِنَا حَاسِبِينَ (wa-kafā binā ḥāsibīn) combines sufficiency with the description of reckoners.

Ḥāsibīn identifies those taking account, while كَفَىٰ (kafā) expresses The God’s sufficiency. The concepts should not be fused into one lexical meaning.

Easy and severe reckoning

Al-Inshiqāq 84:8

فَسَوْفَ يُحَاسَبُ حِسَابًا يَسِيرًا

“He will then be subjected to an easy reckoning.”

يُحَاسَبُ (yuḥāsabu) and حِسَابًا (ḥisāban) stand together. The first is a passive Form III verb, and the second names the reckoning.

يَسِيرًا (yasīran) describes this reckoning as easy.

The Qur’an also describes severe reckoning.

Al-Ṭalāq 65:8

وَكَأَيِّنْ مِنْ قَرْيَةٍ عَتَتْ عَنْ أَمْرِ رَبِّهَا وَرُسُلِهِ فَحَاسَبْنَاهَا حِسَابًا شَدِيدًا وَعَذَّبْنَاهَا عَذَابًا نُكْرًا

“How many a settlement rebelled against the command of its Lord and His Messengers! We subjected it to a severe reckoning and punished it with a harsh and terrible punishment.”

فَحَاسَبْنَاهَا (fa-ḥāsabnāhā) accompanies حِسَابًا شَدِيدًا (ḥisāban shadīdan), severe reckoning.

The comparison shows that reckoning can be easy or severe. Severity and punishment are therefore not permanent components of the word ḥisāb.

Reckoning should also be distinguished from punishment. Al-Ṭalāq 65:8 mentions severe reckoning and punishment separately.

Ḥisāb concerns examination, while punishment concerns a penalty that may follow or accompany it. They are connected without being identical.

Who is responsible for a person’s reckoning?

Al-Isrāʾ 17:14

اقْرَأْ كِتَابَكَ كَفَىٰ بِنَفْسِكَ الْيَوْمَ عَلَيْكَ حَسِيبًا

“Read your record. Today you yourself are sufficient to take account against yourself.”

حَسِيبًا (ḥasīban) is attributed to the human being here.

This matters because ḥasīb is not confined to describing The God in the Qur’an. A person confronting their record can also be called ḥasīb.

The verse presents the person’s record before them and calls them to read it. In that setting, the person is sufficient to recognize and examine what their record contains.

It does not establish that people become the final judges of all their deeds or have the authority to alter the outcome. The text does not say this.

Nor does ḥasīb here mean someone of noble lineage or social standing. It concerns reckoning and facing one’s own record.

The limits of responsibility for others’ accounts

Al-Raʿd 13:40

وَإِنْ مَا نُرِيَنَّكَ بَعْضَ الَّذِي نَعِدُهُمْ أَوْ نَتَوَفَّيَنَّكَ فَإِنَّمَا عَلَيْكَ الْبَلَاغُ وَعَلَيْنَا الْحِسَابُ

“Whether We show you some of what We promise them or cause you to die [before that], your responsibility is only to convey, and Ours is the reckoning.”

This draws a clear distinction between the Messenger’s duty and divine reckoning.

بَلَاغ (balāgh) concerns conveying the message; ḥisāb here concerns examination and accountability before The God.

The distinction also matters in Al-Anʿām 6:52, Al-Anʿām 6:69, and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:113. These verses show that responsibility for others’ accounts cannot be imposed on the Messenger or community members without grounds.

Reckoning in this context therefore concerns the authority responsible for examination and the limits of responsibility, as well as knowledge of deeds.

This does not deny every human judgment concerning observable behaviour. The Qur’an assigns to The God the reckoning belonging to Him in this relationship. The limits of human social and legal duties require examining the relevant verses on justice, testimony, and judgment.

5. Ḥasiba: supposition and the difference between conception and reality

The verb حَسِبَ (ḥasiba), supposing or thinking, is a particularly frequent Qur’anic branch.

Various verses show that people can regard something as being a certain way when it is not.

A mistaken supposition based on appearances

Al-Baqarah 2:273

لِلْفُقَرَاءِ الَّذِينَ أُحْصِرُوا فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ لَا يَسْتَطِيعُونَ ضَرْبًا فِي الْأَرْضِ يَحْسَبُهُمُ الْجَاهِلُ أَغْنِيَاءَ مِنَ التَّعَفُّفِ تَعْرِفُهُمْ بِسِيمَاهُمْ لَا يَسْأَلُونَ النَّاسَ إِلْحَافًا ۗ وَمَا تُنْفِقُوا مِنْ خَيْرٍ فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ بِهِ عَلِيمٌ

“For the needy who have been restricted in the way of The God and cannot travel through the land [to earn a living]. Someone unaware of their circumstances supposes them wealthy because of their restraint. You recognize them by their signs; they do not ask people insistently. Whatever good you spend, The God knows it.”

يَحْسَبُهُمُ (yaḥsabuhumu) concerns a supposition arising from people’s outward conduct.

The needy refrain from insistent asking. Their restraint can lead others to suppose that they have no need.

Ḥasiba here is not numerical counting. Someone forms an impression of others’ circumstances that can be mistaken.

الْجَاهِل (al-jāhil) identifies someone unaware of their actual circumstances. A mistaken supposition can thus arise from insufficient knowledge, rather than necessarily from deceit or hostility.

A mirage and supposition

Al-Nūr 24:39

وَالَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا أَعْمَالُهُمْ كَسَرَابٍ بِقِيعَةٍ يَحْسَبُهُ الظَّمْآنُ مَاءً حَتَّىٰ إِذَا جَاءَهُ لَمْ يَجِدْهُ شَيْئًا وَوَجَدَ اللَّهَ عِنْدَهُ فَوَفَّاهُ حِسَابَهُ ۗ وَاللَّهُ سَرِيعُ الْحِسَابِ

“As for those who disbelieve, their deeds are like a mirage on a level plain that a thirsty person supposes to be water. When he reaches it, he finds nothing, but finds The God there, and He settles his account in full. The God is swift in reckoning.”

This verse brings together three occurrences of the family and is particularly important for studying the root.

يَحْسَبُهُ (yaḥsabuhu) concerns supposition: the thirsty person thinks the mirage is water.

حِسَابَهُ (ḥisābahu) and الْحِسَاب (al-ḥisāb) subsequently concern examination and the reckoning of deeds.

Their difference shows why every derivative cannot be translated with one equivalent.

The first part concerns a mistaken perception of reality; the second, divine examination of deeds.

A supposition can seem convincing and real to someone. The thirsty person sees what appears to be water, but reaching it exposes the mistake.

The verb does not therefore always mean being deceived. It denotes supposing; its error here emerges from comparison with reality.

A correct or mistaken supposition?

Al-Kahf 18:104

الَّذِينَ ضَلَّ سَعْيُهُمْ فِي الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا وَهُمْ يَحْسَبُونَ أَنَّهُمْ يُحْسِنُونَ صُنْعًا

“Those whose effort has gone astray in the worldly life while they suppose that they are doing good work.”

The verse separates people’s conception of their work from its actual worth.

يَحْسَبُونَ (yaḥsabūna) expresses their mental assessment: they imagine themselves to be doing good.

The verse also describes their effort as having gone astray. A person’s belief in their work’s rightness does not itself establish that rightness.

The verb does not inherently mean deliberate self-deception. Someone may sincerely suppose something to be right and be mistaken.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:30 and Al-Zukhruf 43:37 likewise describe people supposing themselves guided. Personal conviction alone is therefore not a sufficient test of an assessment’s correctness.

Ḥasiba does not always accompany an erroneous supposition in Arabic. It can describe an assessment that later proves correct.

Error is consequently not a fixed part of the verb’s meaning. Each verse establishes how the supposition relates to reality.

A prohibition against mistaken supposition

Ibrāhīm 14:42

وَلَا تَحْسَبَنَّ اللَّهَ غَافِلًا عَمَّا يَعْمَلُ الظَّالِمُونَ ۚ إِنَّمَا يُؤَخِّرُهُمْ لِيَوْمٍ تَشْخَصُ فِيهِ الْأَبْصَارُ

“Never suppose The God unaware of what the wrongdoers do. He only delays them until a day when eyes will stare fixedly.”

تَحْسَبَنَّ (taḥsabanna) appears in a prohibition. The addressee is restrained from a supposition incompatible with the truth the Qur’an presents.

Ḥasiba can therefore concern human judgment about divine action.

Muhammad is the direct addressee. The prohibition is real in its construction; without linguistic evidence, it should not be reduced to a performance intended only for other audiences.

Here again the issue is perception and judgment about The God’s awareness, rather than numerical calculation.

6. Ḥasb: sufficiency and adequacy

Ḥasb occurs eleven times in the Qur’an, consistently within the field of sufficiency.

Sometimes it expresses trust in The God, and sometimes it identifies something considered enough for someone, even when that thing is undesirable or wrong.

Ḥasbunā Allāh

Āl ʿImrān 3:173

الَّذِينَ قَالَ لَهُمُ النَّاسُ إِنَّ النَّاسَ قَدْ جَمَعُوا لَكُمْ فَاخْشَوْهُمْ فَزَادَهُمْ إِيمَانًا وَقَالُوا حَسْبُنَا اللَّهُ وَنِعْمَ الْوَكِيلُ

“Those to whom people said, ‘People have gathered against you, so fear them.’ But this increased their faith, and they said, ‘The God is sufficient for us, and what an excellent disposer of affairs!’”

حَسْبُنَا (ḥasbunā) means being sufficient here.

The issue is trust in The God’s sufficiency against a threat, rather than counting property or calculating deeds.

وَنِعْمَ الْوَكِيلُ (wa-niʿma al-wakīl) adds another meaning. Wakīl here concerns one entrusted with affairs and relied upon.

Ḥasb and wakīl are connected without being identical. The first expresses sufficiency; the second concerns entrusting affairs and relying on their disposer.

Ḥasbunā does not always express a sound position

Al-Māʾidah 5:104

وَإِذَا قِيلَ لَهُمْ تَعَالَوْا إِلَىٰ مَا أَنْزَلَ اللَّهُ وَإِلَى الرَّسُولِ قَالُوا حَسْبُنَا مَا وَجَدْنَا عَلَيْهِ آبَاءَنَا ۚ أَوَلَوْ كَانَ آبَاؤُهُمْ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ شَيْئًا وَلَا يَهْتَدُونَ

“When they are told, ‘Come to what The God has sent down and to the Messenger,’ they say, ‘What we found our fathers following is sufficient for us.’ Even if their fathers knew nothing and were not guided?”

Ḥasbunā again expresses sufficiency.

This time, however, the speakers consider what they inherited from their forebears sufficient, rather than The God.

The word does not inherently establish something’s truth or worthiness. It expresses that someone considers it enough.

Āl ʿImrān 3:173 presents The God’s sufficiency against threats; Al-Māʾidah 5:104 condemns accepting ancestral tradition as sufficient against the invitation to revelation.

Positive ethical value should therefore not be extracted from ḥasb alone.

Sufficiency and an unwelcome outcome

Al-Baqarah 2:206

وَإِذَا قِيلَ لَهُ اتَّقِ اللَّهَ أَخَذَتْهُ الْعِزَّةُ بِالْإِثْمِ ۚ فَحَسْبُهُ جَهَنَّمُ ۚ وَلَبِئْسَ الْمِهَادُ

“When he is told, ‘Be mindful of The God,’ arrogance carries him into sin. Hell is therefore sufficient for him, and what an evil resting place!”

حَسْبُهُ (ḥasbuhu) retains its meaning of sufficiency, but its referent is Hell.

Being sufficient does not always mean goodness or blessing. Something may be deemed enough as a person’s recompense or outcome.

The expression also appears in Al-Tawbah 9:68 and Al-Mujādilah 58:8.

Ḥasb is therefore lexically neutral; its positive or negative value depends on what is considered sufficient.

The God’s sufficiency and reliance upon Him

Al-Ṭalāq 65:3

وَيَرْزُقْهُ مِنْ حَيْثُ لَا يَحْتَسِبُ ۚ وَمَنْ يَتَوَكَّلْ عَلَى اللَّهِ فَهُوَ حَسْبُهُ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ بَالِغُ أَمْرِهِ ۚ قَدْ جَعَلَ اللَّهُ لِكُلِّ شَيْءٍ قَدْرًا

“He provides for him from where he does not expect. Whoever relies upon The God, He is sufficient for him. Indeed, The God accomplishes His purpose. The God has established a measure for everything.”

Two different forms of the root stand together here.

يَحْتَسِبُ (yaḥtasibu) concerns expectation and anticipation, while حَسْبُهُ (ḥasbuhu) concerns sufficiency.

Both meanings appear within one verse without becoming synonyms.

The first part describes provision arriving from an unexpected source. The second states The God’s sufficiency for someone who relies upon Him.

The verse does not say that people should abandon earning a livelihood or making plans. Nor does lā yaḥtasibu prohibit calculation and prudent preparation.

The point is that the channels of provision are not limited to human expectation and anticipation.

7. Iḥtisāb: expectation, taking into account, and seeking reward

The root’s Form VIII verb occurs three times in the Qur’an.

All three concern expecting, anticipating, or taking something into account.

From where they do not expect

لَا يَحْتَسِبُ (lā yaḥtasibu) in Al-Ṭalāq 65:3 concerns what someone does not expect.

Al-Ḥashr 59:2 likewise describes an event reaching people from where they did not expect.

Al-Zumar 39:47 describes something becoming apparent that they had not previously taken into account.

Iḥtisāb in these usages concerns human assessment and expectation of future events.

Anticipating reward in religious language

Arabic also uses اِحْتِسَاب (iḥtisāb) for hoping for reward and taking account of recompense with The God.

The semantic connection is intelligible: someone performs an action and counts its reward toward their account with The God.

General Islamic usage should nevertheless be distinguished from the Qur’an’s three verbal occurrences.

Those verses directly concern expectation or anticipation. The verb should therefore not automatically be translated “acting with the intention of earning spiritual reward.”

Later social and religious uses of iḥtisāb, including supervision of markets and public conduct, should likewise not be imported into these verses’ direct meaning.

8. Ḥasīb: reckoner, overseer, or one who suffices?

Ḥasīb occurs four times: Al-Nisāʾ 4:6, Al-Nisāʾ 4:86, Al-Isrāʾ 17:14, and Al-Aḥzāb 33:39.

Three concern The God, and one the human being.

The God as ḥasīb

Al-Nisāʾ 4:86

وَإِذَا حُيِّيتُمْ بِتَحِيَّةٍ فَحَيُّوا بِأَحْسَنَ مِنْهَا أَوْ رُدُّوهَا ۗ إِنَّ اللَّهَ كَانَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ حَسِيبًا

“When you are greeted with a greeting, respond with one better than it or return the same. Indeed, The God is a reckoner over everything.”

Ḥasīb accompanies عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ (ʿalā kulli shayʾin), “over everything.”

This widens the scope of The God’s reckoning and knowledge. Even seemingly minor actions, such as returning a greeting, belong within responsibility.

The root alone does not establish that ḥasīb means only watching over something. Oversight is understandable through the relationship of reckoning with divine knowledge and encompassing awareness.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:6 also contains وَكَفَىٰ بِاللَّهِ حَسِيبًا (wa-kafā bi-Allāhi ḥasīban) in the context of handing over orphans’ property and honouring a trust.

There The God’s being ḥasīb connects clearly with financial responsibility and accountability for other people’s property.

Ḥasīb in the setting of sufficiency

Al-Aḥzāb 33:39

الَّذِينَ يُبَلِّغُونَ رِسَالَاتِ اللَّهِ وَيَخْشَوْنَهُ وَلَا يَخْشَوْنَ أَحَدًا إِلَّا اللَّهَ ۗ وَكَفَىٰ بِاللَّهِ حَسِيبًا

“Those who convey The God’s messages and stand in awe of Him, fearing no one except The God. The God is sufficient as a reckoner.”

Ḥasīb follows the description of conveying divine messages and standing in awe of The God.

Reckoning and sufficiency approach one another in this context. The God is the final authority for examination, and His sufficiency in the face of fear of others also fits the setting.

The basic translation should not combine two independent meanings into an unexplained, ambiguous equivalent. “The God is sufficient as a reckoner” can be selected, while the connection with divine sufficiency remains in the explanation.

Is ḥasīb confined to describing The God?

Al-Isrāʾ 17:14 shows that it is not.

The person is called their own ḥasīb there. The Qur’anic form therefore operates at two different levels.

When it describes The God, reckoning and sufficiency have a scope inseparable from divine knowledge and power.

When it describes a human being, the person’s limited role in confronting their record is in view.

This does not make the usages unrelated. Reckoning is common to them; its scope, capacity, and authority depend on context.

The actual Qur’anic form حَسِيبًا (ḥasīban) should also not be rewritten to match later lists of divine names. Discussing الْحَسِيب (al-ḥasīb) as a divine name is distinct from reporting the form actually present in a verse.

9. Bi-ghayri ḥisāb: without reckoning or without a restricted measure?

بِغَيْرِ حِسَابٍ (bi-ghayri ḥisābin) occurs in several Qur’anic verses.

Some concern divine provision and giving; others concern reward.

Āl ʿImrān 3:27

تُولِجُ اللَّيْلَ فِي النَّهَارِ وَتُولِجُ النَّهَارَ فِي اللَّيْلِ وَتُخْرِجُ الْحَيَّ مِنَ الْمَيِّتِ وَتُخْرِجُ الْمَيِّتَ مِنَ الْحَيِّ وَتَرْزُقُ مَنْ تَشَاءُ بِغَيْرِ حِسَابٍ

“You cause the night to enter the day and the day to enter the night. You bring the living out of the dead and the dead out of the living, and You provide for whomever You will without reckoning.”

Bi-ghayri ḥisāb accompanies providing sustenance here.

“Without reckoning” can express abundant giving not confined within a narrow measure. It can also suggest that the giver is not called to account.

These explanations are recorded in lexical discussion, but should not replace one another without examining context.

For example, providing without reckoning does not necessarily make divine giving devoid of knowledge, measure, or wisdom. Other verses speak of divine knowledge and determination of measure.

Nor does the expression necessarily remove the recipient’s responsibility for using what they receive.

The reward of the steadfast

Al-Zumar 39:10 says the steadfast receive their reward bi-ghayri ḥisāb.

This too should not be turned into a numerical formula.

The expression can highlight the reward’s greatness and abundance beyond ordinary limited measures.

Its exact nature and quantity do not follow from the expression alone.

ʿAṭāʾan ḥisābā

Al-Nabaʾ 78:36

جَزَاءً مِنْ رَبِّكَ عَطَاءً حِسَابًا

“A recompense from your Lord, a sufficient gift.”

This is among the more difficult usages of ḥisāb.

Ḥisāban accompanies عَطَاءً (ʿaṭāʾan), a gift. Lexical usage allows ḥisāb to describe what suffices and satisfies a need.

The expression can therefore mean a sufficient gift.

Another interpretation understands it as a proportionate, measured gift.

The context concerns the reward and blessings of those mindful of The God. A sufficient and abundant gift therefore fits the setting.

The connection with reckoning and measure should not be excluded. The form belongs to a family encompassing counting, sufficiency, and quantity.

“A sufficient gift” is preferred for the basic translation, while “a proportionate and measured gift” should remain a possibility in the research explanation.

10. Ḥusbān in Al-Kahf: a calamity from the sky

Al-Kahf 18:40

فَعَسَىٰ رَبِّي أَنْ يُؤْتِيَنِ خَيْرًا مِنْ جَنَّتِكَ وَيُرْسِلَ عَلَيْهَا حُسْبَانًا مِنَ السَّمَاءِ فَتُصْبِحَ صَعِيدًا زَلَقًا

“It may be that my Lord will give me something better than your garden and send upon it a destructive calamity from the sky, so that it becomes bare, slippery ground.”

Ḥusbānan accompanies something descending from the sky that destroys the garden.

The usage clearly differs from Al-Anʿām 6:96 and Al-Raḥmān 55:5.

Those concern reckoning and the order of the sun and moon; this verse concerns something destructive.

The meaning of ḥusbān here

Arabic lexicons record several meanings: calamity and punishment, a thunderbolt, hail, and small arrows.

Some explanations treat ḥusbān as a plural of حُسْبَانَة (ḥusbānah), referring to small projectiles.

A connection can thus be imagined between arrows and a calamity descending from the sky.

The verse alone does not identify it precisely as lightning, hail, fire, or another event.

The context establishes its destructive result: the garden becomes bare, slippery ground.

Does ḥusbān mean a calculated punishment?

Some explanations understand ḥusbān here as a reckoning or recompense resulting from the person’s deeds.

That interpretation deserves consideration through its connection with the family, but is not the only possibility.

Arabic also records ḥusbān as a calamity or projectiles. The form in Al-Kahf 18:40 therefore need not be translated directly as numerical reckoning.

Nor does a shared root establish that the verse describes a mathematical formula for destroying the garden.

“A destructive calamity from the sky” conveys the contextual sense well. The lexical explanation should make clear that its precise kind is uncertain.

This shows that even an identical form, ḥusbān, can belong to different semantic branches in different contexts.

11. Distinctions from related roots

Ḥ S B (ḥisāb) and ʿ D D (ʿadad)

Both families concern counting.

عَدَد (ʿadad) concerns the number of things. Ḥisāb can encompass counting as well as calculating proportions and measures and examining conduct.

They stand together in Yūnus 10:5 and Al-Isrāʾ 17:12.

Their combination does not establish that reckoning is always more complex than counting. Their semantic fields overlap in some usages.

Ḥ S B (ḥisāb) and Ḥ Ṣ Y (iḥṣāʾ)

ح ص ي (ḥ ṣ y) concerns enumerating, recording, and encompassing the number of things.

Qur’anic usages of this root can emphasize precise enumeration that leaves nothing out.

Ḥisāb can also be precise, but encompasses assessment and accountability beyond counting.

Not every ḥisāb therefore means exhaustive enumeration, nor does every iḥṣāʾ necessarily involve judgment and recompense.

Ḥ S B (ḥasiba) and Ẓ N N (ẓanna)

The roots approach one another in supposition and belief.

Ḥasiba often concerns someone’s understanding of something. ظَنّ (ẓann) likewise concerns supposition and belief.

Neither should be restricted to mistaken supposition in every verse.

Ḥasiba can concern something a person thinks correct before the error becomes apparent. Depending on context, ẓann can range from weak supposition to expectation and strong conviction.

Their precise difference requires examining each family’s usages, rather than imposing an absolute definition.

Ḥ S B (ḥasiba) and Z ʿ M (zaʿama)

زَعَمَ (zaʿama) commonly concerns a claim needing substantiation in Qur’anic usage.

Ḥasiba concerns a person’s supposition and assessment more directly.

Someone can suppose something and subsequently assert it, but supposing and asserting are not the same action.

This matters particularly when examining mistaken beliefs. The Qur’an sometimes reports a person’s thought and sometimes their expressed claim.

Ḥ S B (ḥasb) and K F Y (kifāyah)

The families have a clear proximity in sufficiency.

Ḥasbunā Allāh expresses The God’s sufficiency for us, while كَفَىٰ بِاللَّهِ (kafā bi-Allāh) likewise expresses sufficiency.

They sometimes appear together, as in wa-kafā bi-Allāhi ḥasīban.

Their combination does not make every derivative of ḥ s b mean sufficiency. Ḥasiba often denotes supposing, and ḥisāb examination.

Ḥ S B (ḥisāb) and W Z N (wazn)

Reckoning and weighing can both assess quantity or worth, but their basic images and usages differ.

Physical وَزْن (wazn) concerns assessing heaviness and determining quantity through scales. Ḥisāb concerns counting, calculation, and examination.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:47 combines scales of justice and reckoning.

Both can therefore participate in the image of just assessment of deeds without being absolute synonyms.

The Qur’anic image of scales should not be restricted without evidence to a physical apparatus of a specified shape and size; nor should reckoning be reduced to an earthly accounting ledger.

12. Translation proposals for Forqan

For ḥasiba and its verbal forms concerning human perception, “suppose” is a suitable basic equivalent. “Think” or “regard as” can be more natural in some contexts.

For ḥisāb concerning time, “reckoning,” “calculation,” or “reckoning time” is proposed.

For deeds and resurrection, “reckoning,” “examination of deeds,” or “taking account” is appropriate. These usages should not all become “punishment.”

For ḥāsaba, “call to account” or “take account of” is appropriate. The passive yuḥāsabu can be rendered “he is subjected to reckoning” or “his account is examined.”

For ḥasb, “sufficient” or “enough” is proposed. “The God is sufficient for us” renders ḥasbunā Allāh clearly and naturally.

For iḥtasaba in its three Qur’anic occurrences, “expect,” “anticipate,” or “take into account” is appropriate. “Seek spiritual reward” should not replace their direct meaning.

Context should determine ḥasīb. Where conduct is examined, “reckoner” or “one who takes account” is appropriate. Where sufficiency stands out, that meaning should be explained separately.

For ḥāsibīn, “reckoners” or “those who take account” is proposed.

For ḥusbān in Al-Anʿām 6:96 and Al-Raḥmān 55:5, “determined reckoning” or “calculated order” is suitable. In Al-Kahf 18:40, however, “a destructive calamity” fits the context better.

ʿAṭāʾan ḥisābā in Al-Nabaʾ 78:36 can be rendered “a sufficient gift,” retaining the interpretation involving measure and reckoning in the research explanation.

A derivative’s meaning should not be transferred to the entire family. Unvocalized forms can also mislead: ḥasb, sufficiency; ḥasab, social standing; and ḥasiba, supposing, do not have identical pronunciations.

13. Conclusion

Ḥ S B is an important Qur’anic family for assessment, supposition, accountability, and sufficiency.

In numerical usage, reckoning determines quantity, measure, and order. The sun and moon’s movement and knowledge of time are clear examples.

For deeds, reckoning concerns examination and accountability. The Qur’an connects it with justice, truth, and recompense without making punishment its inherent lexical meaning. Reckoning can be easy or severe.

In mental usage, ḥasiba denotes supposing or regarding something in a particular way. The supposition can be correct or mistaken. The Qur’an repeatedly shows that human perceptions of appearances or the worth of one’s deeds can differ from reality.

Ḥasb expresses something being enough for someone. It does not itself establish that thing’s truth or goodness. The God can be sufficient for someone, while elsewhere Hell is deemed enough as a person’s outcome.

Ḥasīb concerns reckoning and sufficiency without being confined to describing The God. A person confronting their record can also be called ḥasīb.

Ḥusbān in Al-Kahf 18:40 finally shows why every branch should not be traced to numerical counting. The verse concerns a destructive calamity from the sky, distinct from the ḥusbān of the sun and moon.

The definition should therefore retain several branches: Ḥisāb is counting and assessing quantity or conduct; ḥasiba is supposing and mentally assessing; ḥasb is sufficiency and adequacy; and other derivatives, including ḥasīb and ḥusbān, take their meanings from their morphology and context.

The root’s Qur’anic importance extends beyond the day of reckoning. The family used for recognizing temporal order and measurement also highlights the gap between human supposition and reality, and confronts people with responsibility for their deeds.

These connections can be explored through broader conceptual study. In defining the root, however, each meaning should remain grounded in its own linguistic evidence and actual usage.
