Ants, fingertips, and the root’s semantic range in Qur’anic Arabic

N M L in the Qur’an is not limited to the story of the ant and Solomon. Alongside al-naml and namlah in Al-Naml, al-anāmil in Āl ʿImrān is also assigned to this root. Both branches therefore require examination without forcing them into a shared physical image.

The Qur’anic forms are al-naml twice, namlah once, and al-anāmil once. All four occurrences appear in two verses: Al-Naml 27:18 and Āl ʿImrān 3:119.

1. Basic meaning and lexical family

Naml is the familiar Arabic name for ants, small insects commonly living and moving in groups. Namlah names one ant; nimāl is also attested as a plural.

Naml and namlah are not merely two arbitrary forms. Naml is a collective generic noun, while the added tāʾ in namlah marks an individual of that kind. Namlah therefore does not necessarily mean a female ant; the tāʾ has an individualizing function.

One lexical analysis groups these three features in the N M L family:

تَجَمُّعٍ فِي شَيْءٍ وَصِغَرٍ وَخِفَّةٍ

tajammuʿin fī shayʾin wa-ṣigharin wa-khiffatin

That is, gathering, smallness, and lightness.

This description fits ants and some related figurative expressions. The features should nevertheless not be made a certain, single historical meaning without evidence. Anāmil in particular names a specific part of the hand; sharing a root does not establish its exact historical connection with the name for ants.

2. Extended meanings in early Arabic

Alongside ants, Arabic dictionaries record uses emphasizing small movements, activity, crawling, or dispersal.

Arḍun namilah describes land with many ants, and ṭaʿāmun manmūl describes food reached by ants. Both relate directly to the insect.

Farasun namilu al-qawāʾimi describes a horse with light, active, restless legs. Rajulun namilu al-aṣābiʿi describes someone whose fingers are constantly moving or doing much work. Movement and restlessness are prominent in these expressions.

Namilat yaduhu is also attested for numbness, tingling, or a crawling sensation in the hand. Namal here does not name an ant literally moving on the hand, but describes a sensation resembling tiny creatures crawling.

Namlah also names a kind of sore or skin eruption. Lexical explanation compares its gradual spread over the skin to the movement and dispersal of ants.

Words concerning tale-bearing are also attested, including numlah for malicious gossip and namila for carrying tales. This shows how some branches extend beyond the insect’s name. It does not justify translating Qur’anic al-naml as “tale-bearers” without contextual evidence.

Some lexical accounts also use namlah for a split in an animal’s hoof and kitābun munammal for writing with closely spaced strokes. These show historical usage, not meanings necessarily present in the Qur’an.

Poetic witnesses and their historical setting

A verse attributed to al-Akhṭal reads:

تَدِبُّ دَبِيبًا فِي الْعِظَامِ كَأَنَّهُ
دَبِيبُ نِمَالٍ فِي نَقًا يَتَهَيَّلُ

tadibbu dabīban fī al-ʿiẓāmi ka-annahu / dabību nimālin fī naqan yatahayyalu

Meaning: “It creeps slowly through the bones, like ants crawling through a loose, crumbling sandhill.”

The ants’ slow, creeping movement forms the comparison. Another witness is attributed to al-Kumayt:

وَلَا أُزْعِجُ الْكَلِمَ الْمُحْفِظَاتِ
لِلْأَقْرَبِينَ وَلَا أُنْمِلُ

wa-lā uzʿiju al-kalima al-muḥfiẓāti / li-al-aqrabīna wa-lā unmilu

Meaning: “I do not stir up anger-provoking words among relatives, nor do I carry tales.”

These witnesses belong to the Arabic of the first and second Islamic centuries, not definitively to the period before the Qur’an. No pre-Islamic poetic witness with sufficiently reliable attribution and wording for the forms examined was established in this study.

3. Naml and namlah in the Qur’an

All three occurrences concerning ants appear in one verse:

Al-Naml 27:18

حَتَّىٰٓ إِذَآ أَتَوْا۟ عَلَىٰ وَادِ ٱلنَّمْلِ قَالَتْ نَمْلَةٌ يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلنَّمْلُ ٱدْخُلُوا۟ مَسَـٰكِنَكُمْ لَا يَحْطِمَنَّكُمْ سُلَيْمَـٰنُ وَجُنُودُهُۥ وَهُمْ لَا يَشْعُرُونَ

ḥattā idhā ataw ʿalā wādi al-namli qālat namlatun yā ayyuhā al-namlu udkhulū masākinakum lā yaḥṭimannakum sulaymānu wa-junūduhu wa-hum lā yashʿurūna

“Until, when they reached the valley of the ants, an ant said: Ants, enter your dwellings, lest Solomon and his soldiers crush you without realizing.”

Wādi al-namli first names the valley of the ants. Namlah then introduces one individual, and yā ayyuhā al-namlu addresses the collective with a warning.

The three occurrences clarify the meaning: one ant among the ants warns the others of danger from the approaching army.

Udkhulū masākinakum uses a masculine plural command. Arabic commonly uses this form for rational addressees; here, the narrative places ants in the position of receiving speech and instructions. That construction does not itself turn the name for ants into a name for people.

Qālat namlatun also agrees grammatically with the feminine singular noun namlah. The phrase alone does not establish that the ant was biologically female, a queen, or ruler of the group.

The ant’s speech and the narrative setting

Al-Naml 27:17 describes Solomon’s forces as jinn, humans, and birds. Al-Naml 27:19 then says:

فَتَبَسَّمَ ضَاحِكًا مِّن قَوْلِهَا

fa-tabassama ḍāḥikan min qawlihā

“He smiled, laughing at the ant’s words.” (Al-Naml 27:19)

The sequence presents the ant’s speech as understood within the narrative, not merely a sound from which no meaning was received.

The manner of that understanding, the ants’ mode of communication, and how Solomon heard and understood it are not contained in the word namlah. They require independent study of the narrative and its reflection.

Wa-hum lā yashʿurūna also describes a danger of crushing the ants without Solomon and his soldiers realizing it. The verse does not attribute a deliberate intention to destroy them to the army.

Wādi al-naml lexically means the valley of the ants. The expression does not establish its exact geographical location. Naming a country, region, or its characteristics without independent evidence is not part of the root’s meaning.

The natural, supported meaning of al-naml here is therefore ants. Making it the name of a human people, a small army, a group of tale-bearers, or a social metaphor requires independent evidence and a credible textual indication.

4. Anāmil: the other Qur’anic branch

Āl ʿImrān 3:119

وَإِذَا خَلَوْا عَضُّوا عَلَيْكُمُ الْأَنَامِلَ مِنَ الْغَيْظِ

wa-idhā khalaw ʿaḍḍū ʿalaykumu al-anāmila mina al-ghayẓi

“When alone, they bite their fingertips in rage at you.”

Al-anāmil is the plural of anmulah. Lexicons describe anmulah as the final segment of a finger, particularly the part bearing the nail. “Fingertips” is therefore natural here.

The verse depicts bodily behavior expressing intense anger: people profess belief in the listeners’ presence but bite their fingertips in rage when alone. Al-anāmil names a bodily part here and does not mean ants.

Some lexical analyses treat the initial hamzah of anmulah as additional and assign the word to N M L. This agrees with its classification in root-based lexicons and Qur’anic indexes.

Two questions must remain distinct. Assigning anmulah to N M L is a morphological and lexical analysis. Why the fingertip historically received this name, and how it relates to the name for ants, is a separate question. Similar smallness alone cannot prove that connection.

5. Distinction from related roots

Two distinctions are particularly important.

N M L and N M M

Namm and namīmah concern passing on speech and carrying tales. N M L also has attested uses in that field. The overlap does not make the roots morphologically identical. Al-naml in Solomon’s story names ants on the verse’s clear evidence, not an automatic equivalent of tale-bearing.

N M L and N Ḥ L

Al-naḥl is the Qur’anic name for honey bees, discussed in Al-Naḥl 16:68 and Al-Naḥl 16:69. Bees and ants are insects capable of social living, but have independent names and roots. Behavioral or biological similarities do not transfer one word’s meaning to the other.

In both comparisons, actual usage in the sentence and context takes precedence over possible analogies.

Study of N Ḥ L

6. Proposed translations for Forqan

In Al-Naml, render al-naml as “ants,” namlah as “an ant,” and wādi al-naml as “the valley of the ants.” These preserve the distinction between an individual and the collective.

Render al-anāmil in Āl ʿImrān 3:119 as “fingertips.” This is more precise than “fingers,” identifying their ends and fitting the image of biting them in anger.

Do not add “queen ant,” “female ant,” or “commander of the ants” to namlah, since the word does not contain those specifications. Masākinakum can likewise be “your dwellings,” without adding a city or governmental system to the ants’ description.

7. Conclusion

N M L comprises a family whose most familiar member is naml, ants. Figurative and descriptive uses involve small movements, activity, lightness, dispersal, and spreading. They cannot all certainly be traced to one original historical image.

The Qur’an uses the family in two entirely different settings: al-naml and namlah for ants in Solomon’s story, and al-anāmil for fingertips in describing intense human anger.

The word for ants retains its clear meaning in Solomon’s story. Awareness, warning, protection of the community, and Solomon’s response can be examined contextually, but such themes do not replace the lexical definition of naml.

A practical definition should therefore account for ants, the distinct fingertip branch, and attested figurative Arabic uses, without forcing one into the meaning of another.
