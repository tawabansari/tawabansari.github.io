The Arabic root S Q Ṭ concerns falling, dropping, and leaving a previous position. Saqaṭa means “fell”; asqaṭa means “dropped something” or “caused it to fall”; and sāqiṭ describes something falling or descending from its position.

The family's primary sense is concrete and spatial: a leaf falling from a tree, fruit dropping, something descending from above, or an object falling to the ground. Arabic also extends it to slips in speech and conduct, loss of standing, becoming caught in an undesirable situation, and regret after recognizing an error.

The root occurs eight times in the Quran. Some occurrences concern physical falling, including leaves, dates, and fragments of the sky. By contrast, fī al-fitnati saqaṭū concerns becoming caught in trial, while suqiṭa fī aydīhim is an idiom for regret and helplessness after recognizing a mistake.

This range shows why not every form of S Q Ṭ should be restricted to bodily falling. Nor does every fall mean sin, moral defeat, or loss of dignity. Falling is the root's direct meaning; its kind and consequences emerge from each sentence's construction and context.

1. The root in early Arabic

Falling from one position to another

In Arabic lexical usage, saqaṭa al-shayʾu means that something fell.

Saqaṭa yasquṭu suqūṭan describes something falling from its position or landing elsewhere.

One lexical definition identifies falling and occurrence as this family's basic sense:

«السِّينُ وَالْقَافُ وَالطَّاءُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى الْوُقُوعِ»

al-sīnu wa-al-qāfu wa-al-ṭāʾu aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā al-wuqūʿ

That is: “Sīn, qāf, and ṭāʾ have one basic sense indicating falling or occurrence.”

This definition identifies the central association with falling and coming to rest.

In concrete usage, something may fall from a tree, building, or other height. An object may also slip from someone's hand and land on the ground.

Here, falling involves a change of position: something leaves its previous location and reaches another.

Yet suqūṭ does not itself identify the cause of a fall.

A leaf may fall because of wind, drying, or natural detachment from its branch. An object may fall after a blow or release from a hand. A person may slip and fall.

Each event can be called falling, while its cause must be established separately.

Falling also does not necessarily destroy what falls. A fallen leaf remains a leaf, and fruit detached from its tree may remain intact and usable.

The central concrete meaning can therefore be stated as follows:

Something falling or descending from its previous position, without the word necessarily specifying the cause, intensity, or outcome.

Siqṭ: the lower edge or end of a sandy tract

In early Arabic, siqṭ al-raml denotes the end, edge, or lower termination of a sandy tract.

This use appears at the opening of the famous Muʿallaqah attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays:

قِفَا نَبْكِ مِنْ ذِكْرَى حَبِيبٍ وَمَنْزِلِ
بِسِقْطِ اللِّوَى بَيْنَ الدَّخُولِ فَحَوْمَلِ

qifā nabki min dhikrā ḥabībin wa-manzili / bi-siqṭi al-liwā bayna al-dakhūli fa-ḥawmali

“Stop, you two, and let us weep at the memory of a beloved and her dwelling, at the edge of the winding sands between al-Dakhūl and Ḥawmal.”

Siqṭ al-liwā names or describes a part of the landscape. Al-liwā concerns winding sands, while siqṭ denotes their edge or termination.

Here the word refers to an actual geographical location, not a moral fall or the destruction of something.

Its semantic connection with a lower edge and the end of a sandy tract can be considered. The verse alone, however, cannot establish the entire history of the name siqṭ.

Imruʾ al-Qays is a recognized pre-Islamic poet, and this verse opens the Muʿallaqah attributed to him. That attribution provides important evidence within the pre-Islamic poetic tradition, although the poet's age alone does not establish precisely when every wording was recorded.

This witness shows that, besides the verb “fall,” the early family included a noun for a lower position or edge of land.

Siqāṭ: slips and errors in speech

Arabic also uses al-siqāṭ for errors and slips of speech.

Saqṭah can denote a single error or lapse, much as one may speak of having slipped in one's conduct.

A verse attributed to Suwayd ibn Abī Kāhil reads:

كَيْفَ يَرْجُونَ سِقَاطِي بَعْدَمَا
جَلَّلَ الرَّأْسَ بَيَاضٌ وَصَلَعُ

kayfa yarjūna siqāṭī baʿdamā / jallala al-raʾsa bayāḍun wa-ṣalaʿu

“How can they hope for my slips, now that whiteness and baldness have covered my head?”

Siqāṭī here refers to possible errors or lapses by the poet.

He speaks of people waiting for a mistake they can use against him.

Nothing is physically falling from a height. Falling has been extended figuratively to human speech or behavior.

Just as an object can fall from its place, a person may depart from correctness in speech or conduct and slip.

This explanatory image does not mean that pre-Islamic speakers necessarily understood every mistake through an image of bodily falling.

Suwayd ibn Abī Kāhil lived across the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods. The precise date of this verse is uncertain, so it should not be presented as definitively pre-Islamic without independent evidence.

Nevertheless, it illustrates siqāṭ as error or lapse independently of Quranic interpretation.

Other attested uses

Saqṭ al-matāʿ denotes low-value objects, odds and ends, and remnants separated or set aside from a larger collection.

Saqṭ al-nār is also attested for particles or sparks that fall when fire is kindled or iron is struck.

Saqīṭ can refer to snow, ice, or dew settled on the ground.

Siqṭ also denotes a fetus expelled from the womb before its development and gestation are complete.

Here the word describes premature expulsion. It does not itself determine the cause, whether the event was intentional, or its moral or legal assessment.

In social usage, sāqiṭ can describe someone who has lost standing or credibility.

These senses show extensions from physical falling to loss of position, behavioral lapses, and being discarded.

In Quranic study, however, a branch should inform translation only when the verse's construction and context support it.

2. Quranic forms and derivatives

S Q Ṭ occurs eight times in eight Quranic verses.

Its occurrences fall into four morphological groups in the reading of Ḥafṣ.

Saqaṭa yasquṭu: the basic triliteral verb, meaning “fall.” The Quran contains tasquṭu, suqiṭa, and saqaṭū.

Sāqaṭa yusāqiṭu: Form III, here meaning “drop” or “cause to drop.” Tusāqiṭ occurs in Maryam 19:25 in the reading of Ḥafṣ.

Asqaṭa yusqiṭu: Form IV, meaning “cause something to fall” or “drop it.” The Quran contains tusqiṭa, fa-asqiṭ, and nusqiṭ.

Sāqiṭ: an active participle meaning “falling.” Sāqiṭan occurs in Al-Ṭūr 52:44.

In the reading of Ḥafṣ, there are three basic-verb occurrences, one Form III occurrence, three Form IV occurrences, and one active participle.

Other Arabic forms include suqūṭ, saqṭah, siqāṭ, saqīṭ, masqiṭ, musqiṭ, and tasāquṭ, but these exact nominal formations do not occur in the Quran.

The distinction matters: dictionary derivatives should not be presented as forms actually found in the Quran.

Nor does falling function identically in the grammar of every form. Saqaṭa can express the event, while asqaṭa highlights causing something to fall.

Morphological form alone, however, does not establish the fall's intensity, speed, intentionality, or result.

3. Physical falling: leaves, dates, and fragments of the sky

A falling leaf and divine knowledge

Al-Anʿām 6:59 states:

وَعِنْدَهُ مَفَاتِحُ الْغَيْبِ لَا يَعْلَمُهَا إِلَّا هُوَ وَيَعْلَمُ مَا فِي الْبَرِّ وَالْبَحْرِ وَمَا تَسْقُطُ مِنْ وَرَقَةٍ إِلَّا يَعْلَمُهَا وَلَا حَبَّةٍ فِي ظُلُمَاتِ الْأَرْضِ وَلَا رَطْبٍ وَلَا يَابِسٍ إِلَّا فِي كِتَابٍ مُبِينٍ

wa-ʿindahu mafātiḥu al-ghaybi lā yaʿlamuhā illā huwa wa-yaʿlamu mā fī al-barri wa-al-baḥri wa-mā tasquṭu min waraqatin illā yaʿlamuhā wa-lā ḥabbatin fī ẓulumāti al-arḍi wa-lā raṭbin wa-lā yābisin illā fī kitābin mubīn

Al-Anʿām 6:59

“With Him are the keys of the unseen; none knows them but Him. He knows what is on land and in the sea. No leaf falls without His knowing it, nor is there a grain in the darknesses of the earth, or anything moist or dry, except in a clear record.”

Tasquṭu is an imperfect verb here, while waraqatin means a leaf.

The phrase directly describes a leaf falling.

A leaf may detach from a branch and fall to the ground. However small and ordinary this seems to humans, it is not outside divine knowledge.

Tasquṭu here does not mean the leaf is destroyed. Nor does it mean sinning, defeat, or a decline in moral rank.

It concerns a concrete event in nature.

Yet the verse does more than describe a falling leaf. Through a very small and familiar example, it expresses the comprehensiveness of divine knowledge.

It therefore mentions alongside the leaf a grain in the earth's darknesses and everything moist or dry.

Does the verse explain the cause of every fall?

The verse says that no leaf falls without The God knowing it.

But illā yaʿlamuhā does not mean “unless He directly drops it.”

Knowledge of an event and identification of its immediate physical cause are distinct matters.

Wind, seasonal change, drying, or another factor may detach a leaf from its branch.

The verse speaks of divine knowledge encompassing such events, without assigning a particular natural cause to each fall.

The lexical meaning of tasquṭu should therefore not become a theory of how every natural event is governed.

“Falls” or “falls down” is appropriate in translation; knowledge is expressed by the following clause.

Fresh dates falling to Maryam

Maryam 19:25 states:

وَهُزِّي إِلَيْكِ بِجِذْعِ النَّخْلَةِ تُسَاقِطْ عَلَيْكِ رُطَبًا جَنِيًّا

wa-huzzī ilayki bi-jidhʿi al-nakhlati tusāqiṭ ʿalayki ruṭaban janiyyā

Maryam 19:25

“Shake the trunk of the palm toward yourself; it will drop fresh, ripe dates upon you.”

Tusāqiṭ concerns dates falling from the palm.

Its formation differs from tasquṭu in Al-Anʿām 6:59.

In the reading of Ḥafṣ, tusāqiṭ is Form III; the palm causes the dates to drop in this construction.

Ruṭaban janiyyan specifies what falls.

Thus the verb does not merely report an unnamed object falling: it presents the palm in relation to dropping its fruit.

Other recognized readings have tassāqaṭ or tasāqaṭ, related to Form VI tatasāqaṭ. These present the dropping of fruit or falling from the palm through a different formation.

The morphological differences matter for syntax, but the overall sense remains clear: fresh dates fall to Maryam when the palm is shaken.

The role of shaking the palm

The verse begins with wa-huzzī, “shake.”

Tusāqiṭ then states the expected result of that movement.

Shaking and dropping are therefore distinct actions.

Hazz concerns moving and shaking; suqūṭ describes something falling or dropping.

Tusāqiṭ alone does not mean Maryam must pluck the fruit by hand. Conversely, huzzī should not be read as though the fruit's fall bears no relation to the palm's movement.

The verse connects Maryam's action with the provision of food.

The following verse, Maryam 19:26, speaks of eating and drinking. The falling dates thus occur within a context of food and reassurance after childbirth.

This message emerges from the passage; S Q Ṭ itself still refers here to the fruit dropping.

Causing fragments of the sky to fall

Al-Isrāʾ 17:92 states:

أَوْ تُسْقِطَ السَّمَاءَ كَمَا زَعَمْتَ عَلَيْنَا كِسَفًا أَوْ تَأْتِيَ بِاللَّهِ وَالْمَلَائِكَةِ قَبِيلًا

aw tusqiṭa al-samāʾa kamā zaʿamta ʿalaynā kisafan aw taʾtiya bi-Allāhi wa-al-malāʾikati qabīlā

Al-Isrāʾ 17:92

“Or cause the sky to fall upon us in pieces, as you claimed, or bring The God and the angels face to face.”

Tusqiṭa here is Form IV.

Unlike tasquṭu, which can express falling, tusqiṭa expresses causing something to fall.

The opponents ask the Prophet to make the sky fall on them in fragments.

Kisafan belongs to K S F and denotes pieces or fragments.

Fragmentation therefore comes from kisafan, not from tusqiṭa itself.

This distinction matters in translation.

Isqāṭ means causing to fall; other expressions identify what is dropped and in what manner.

The sentence is also an opponents' demand and challenge. Their words do not establish that the requested event actually happened.

A similar demand addressed to Shuʿayb

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:187 states:

فَأَسْقِطْ عَلَيْنَا كِسَفًا مِنَ السَّمَاءِ إِنْ كُنْتَ مِنَ الصَّادِقِينَ

fa-asqiṭ ʿalaynā kisafan mina al-samāʾi in kunta mina al-ṣādiqīn

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:187

“Then make fragments of the sky fall upon us, if you are truthful.”

These are the words of Shuʿayb's opponents.

Fa-asqiṭ is a Form IV imperative expressing causation of falling.

Here too, they demand that pieces of the sky fall upon them.

This imperative is not a divine command to the prophet; his opponents utter it as a challenge.

Reading the verse requires distinguishing the verb's meaning from the speaker's position.

Asqiṭ still means “cause to fall,” while the speakers' purpose and the assessment of their behavior emerge from the story.

The possibility of fragments falling from the sky

Sabaʾ 34:9 states:

أَفَلَمْ يَرَوْا إِلَىٰ مَا بَيْنَ أَيْدِيهِمْ وَمَا خَلْفَهُمْ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ وَالْأَرْضِ إِنْ نَشَأْ نَخْسِفْ بِهِمُ الْأَرْضَ أَوْ نُسْقِطْ عَلَيْهِمْ كِسَفًا مِنَ السَّمَاءِ إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَآيَةً لِكُلِّ عَبْدٍ مُنِيبٍ

a-fa-lam yaraw ilā mā bayna aydīhim wa-mā khalfahum mina al-samāʾi wa-al-arḍi in nashaʾ nakhsif bihimu al-arḍa aw nusqiṭ ʿalayhim kisafan mina al-samāʾi inna fī dhālika la-āyatan li-kulli ʿabdin munīb

Sabaʾ 34:9

“Have they not considered what lies before them and behind them of the sky and earth? If We will, We cause the earth to swallow them or make fragments of the sky fall upon them. In that is surely a sign for every servant who turns back.”

Nusqiṭ is a first-person plural imperfect Form IV verb.

ʿAlayhim indicates upon whom the fragments fall; kisafan mina al-samāʾ identifies fragments of the sky.

This occurrence relates to the previous two, but with an important difference.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:92 and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:187, opponents demand that prophets bring down pieces of the sky.

Sabaʾ 34:9 instead speaks of the possibility of such an event by divine will.

Sharing a verb does not mean sharing a speaker or purpose.

Lexically, nusqiṭ means “cause to fall.” Power, will, and warning arise from the full verse.

In nashaʾ is conditional: the verse describes a possibility rather than reporting its occurrence at that moment.

Sāqiṭan: something falling

Al-Ṭūr 52:44 states:

وَإِنْ يَرَوْا كِسْفًا مِنَ السَّمَاءِ سَاقِطًا يَقُولُوا سَحَابٌ مَرْكُومٌ

wa-in yaraw kisfan mina al-samāʾi sāqiṭan yaqūlū saḥābun markūm

Al-Ṭūr 52:44

“If they saw a fragment of the sky falling, they would say: ‘A heaped-up cloud.’”

Sāqiṭan is an active participle describing something in the act of falling.

Its formation differs from asqaṭa.

Asqaṭa can express causing a fall, whereas sāqiṭ describes what falls.

Here that object is kisfan mina al-samāʾ, a fragment of the sky.

The verse describes people who would call even a falling piece of the sky a massed cloud.

This description has a thematic connection with the opponents' earlier demands for falling sky fragments.

But sāqiṭ does not mean cloud. Saḥābun markūm is what those speakers say about the observed phenomenon.

Their statement therefore does not make kisf and saḥāb lexical synonyms.

Falling remains concrete here; assessment of the speakers' behavior comes from the context.

4. Falling into fitnah: becoming caught in an undesirable situation

Al-Tawbah 9:49 states:

وَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ يَقُولُ ائْذَنْ لِي وَلَا تَفْتِنِّي أَلَا فِي الْفِتْنَةِ سَقَطُوا وَإِنَّ جَهَنَّمَ لَمُحِيطَةٌ بِالْكَافِرِينَ

wa-minhum man yaqūlu iʾdhan lī wa-lā taftinnī a-lā fī al-fitnati saqaṭū wa-inna jahannama la-muḥīṭatun bi-al-kāfirīn

Al-Tawbah 9:49

“Among them is one who says: ‘Give me leave and do not put me to trial.’ Indeed, into trial they have fallen; and Hell surely encompasses the disbelievers.”

Saqaṭū is a masculine plural past-tense verb.

Falling here is not bodily.

Fī al-fitnah identifies the situation in which they have become caught.

Just as someone can fall into a pit, language can describe falling into trouble, error, or trial.

Here suqūṭ depicts being caught in an undesirable condition.

The speaker's claim and actual situation

The verse begins with someone saying:

«ائْذَنْ لِي وَلَا تَفْتِنِّي».

iʾdhan lī wa-lā taftinnī

Al-Tawbah 9:49

“Give me leave and do not put me to trial.”

The verse responds:

«أَلَا فِي الْفِتْنَةِ سَقَطُوا».

a-lā fī al-fitnati saqaṭū

Al-Tawbah 9:49

“Indeed, into trial they have fallen.”

This contrast matters.

The speaker requests leave to avoid what he calls trial, but the verse declares that they are already caught in it.

Saqaṭū thus describes becoming caught in precisely what the speaker claims to avoid.

Yet the root's meaning does not become “hypocrisy,” “excuse-making,” or “evasion of responsibility.”

Those features emerge from context and conduct; saqaṭū retains the sense of falling into or becoming caught.

Is falling into trial always unknowing?

In ordinary language, falling into something can suggest an unwanted event.

Saqaṭū alone, however, does not prove that people entered the situation without knowledge, choice, or responsibility.

The verse presents their conduct and speech in a particular setting, and its judgment concerns that conduct.

Falling into trial can therefore result from one's own decisions and actions.

Conversely, not everyone facing difficulty or asking about a decision's risks should be equated with this group.

Assessing responsibility and intent requires knowledge of people's actions and circumstances.

This occurrence shows that falling can concern moral and social conditions, while its value depends on what one falls into.

5. Suqiṭa fī aydīhim: regret after recognizing error

Al-Aʿrāf 7:149 states:

وَلَمَّا سُقِطَ فِي أَيْدِيهِمْ وَرَأَوْا أَنَّهُمْ قَدْ ضَلُّوا قَالُوا لَئِنْ لَمْ يَرْحَمْنَا رَبُّنَا وَيَغْفِرْ لَنَا لَنَكُونَنَّ مِنَ الْخَاسِرِينَ

wa-lammā suqiṭa fī aydīhim wa-raʾaw annahum qad ḍallū qālū la-in lam yarḥamnā rabbunā wa-yaghfir lanā la-nakūnanna mina al-khāsirīn

Al-Aʿrāf 7:149

“When they regretted it and saw that they had gone astray, they said: ‘Unless our Lord has mercy on us and forgives us, we will surely be among the losers.’”

This verse occurs in the account of the Children of Israel and calf worship.

After recognizing their error, they speak of divine mercy and forgiveness.

Suqiṭa fī aydīhim is an Arabic expression for regret, remorse, and helplessness after an action.

It is lexically connected with falling, but its meaning cannot be obtained by translating each word separately.

Saqaṭa here does not describe a physical object falling into hands, nor does aydīhim imply that they were actually holding an object.

The phrase is idiomatic.

Why do hands appear in an expression of regret?

Early lexical explanations offer several accounts of the expression.

One links it to a person striking one hand against the other or moving the hands in remorse. This visible behavior connects hands and falling with regret.

Another understands it as regret and remorse descending upon them, as though something distressing had come into their possession.

Others connect it with the helplessness of someone who has lost the desired path and faces the consequences of an action without recourse.

These explanations help illuminate the image, but none should be declared its sole, certain historical origin without independent evidence.

No dated pre-Quranic poetic witness for this expression is presented here.

The absence of such a witness in this discussion does not prove that the expression was absent from Arabic before the Quran.

What is clear is its established Quranic sense: regret and remorse after error becomes apparent.

Recognizing misguidance and seeking forgiveness

The verse continues:

«وَرَأَوْا أَنَّهُمْ قَدْ ضَلُّوا».

wa-raʾaw annahum qad ḍallū

Al-Aʿrāf 7:149

“And they saw that they had gone astray.”

This supplies the setting for their regret.

They do not merely feel unpleasant emotions; they recognize that their course was wrong.

They then say:

«لَئِنْ لَمْ يَرْحَمْنَا رَبُّنَا وَيَغْفِرْ لَنَا لَنَكُونَنَّ مِنَ الْخَاسِرِينَ».

la-in lam yarḥamnā rabbunā wa-yaghfir lanā la-nakūnanna mina al-khāsirīn

Al-Aʿrāf 7:149

These words express a plea for mercy and forgiveness.

The verse connects recognition of error, remorse, and turning toward divine mercy.

Lexically, however, these are distinct matters.

Suqiṭa fī aydīhim expresses regret and remorse.

Raʾaw annahum qad ḍallū expresses recognition of going astray.

The plea for mercy and forgiveness is the response that follows.

This matters because regret alone is not another name for repentance, reform, or restitution. Someone may regret an outcome without yet taking corrective action.

Here regret accompanies recognition of error and seeking mercy.

The idiom's meaning should therefore be preserved, while its moral and religious implications are drawn from the sentences together.

6. Comparison with related roots

S Q Ṭ and W Q ʿ: falling and occurring

Waqaʿa concerns occurring, happening, or coming to rest in a position.

It can describe a body falling, but has a wider semantic field.

An event occurring, for example, does not necessarily involve something descending from a height.

By comparison, the concrete use of saqaṭa highlights falling from a previous position.

Thus every fall can be an occurrence, but not every occurrence is a fall.

In figurative usage, both may refer to an event or being caught in a situation, but features of one should not be transferred to the other without evidence.

S Q Ṭ and KH R R: falling and forceful falling

The Arabic root KH R R concerns falling down; some uses highlight intensity or suddenness.

The Quran uses kharrū sujjadan for falling in prostration. Al-Ḥajj 22:31 also depicts falling from the sky in a comparison.

Saqaṭa and kharra overlap in the field of falling.

Not every khurūr is fatal, nor is every suqūṭ gentle and harmless.

The expression and context determine the fall's intensity, direction, and outcome for translation.

S Q Ṭ and N Z L: falling and descending

Nazala concerns coming down or descending.

The Quran uses the family for rain, revelation, angels, and other phenomena.

Suqūṭ can also involve movement from above to below, but highlights falling.

Nuzūl does not itself mean an involuntary fall or loss of support.

Conversely, isqāṭ is not necessarily accidental, since it can describe an identifiable agent dropping something.

Nazala should therefore not always be read as gentle descent, or saqaṭa as a violent fall.

In Maryam 19:25, falling dates result from shaking the palm; Quranic uses of nuzūl can have quite different settings.

S Q Ṭ and H B Ṭ: falling and moving to a lower position

Habaṭa concerns descending or moving to a lower position.

Al-Baqarah 2:36 and Al-Baqarah 2:38 contain the command to descend in the account of Adam.

These should not simply be translated as bodily or involuntary falling.

Hubūṭ can denote movement and descent, while concrete suqūṭ highlights falling from a previous position.

They can refer to the same event in some circumstances without being identical in meaning.

S Q Ṭ and Z L L: falling and slipping

Z L L concerns slipping and loss of stability.

In Al-Baqarah 2:36, fa-azallahumā al-shayṭān concerns the lapse of Adam and his wife.

Nonphysical S Q Ṭ can likewise concern error and falling into an undesirable situation.

But slipping and falling are distinct.

Someone may slip without falling to the ground. Something may also fall without slipping, such as fruit detaching from a branch.

Morally, a slip may denote an error, whereas falling can emphasize its result or becoming caught in an undesirable state.

This is a tendency, not an absolute rule making every slip the initial stage and every fall the final stage of error.

S Q Ṭ and L Q Y (ilqāʾ): falling and casting

Alqā means to cast, throw, or place something.

In Al-Aʿrāf, this family describes Moses casting his staff.

Saqaṭa can describe the event of falling, while alqā more directly describes an agent's act of casting.

Form IV of S Q Ṭ can also express this relationship: asqaṭa means causing something to fall.

Alqā and asqaṭa therefore overlap in some contexts without being interchangeable everywhere.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

The basic equivalent for S Q Ṭ is “fall.”

Depending on its object and formation, “fall down,” “drop,” “cause to fall,” and “become caught” may also fit.

In Al-Anʿām 6:59, tasquṭu is appropriately translated “falls.”

In Maryam 19:25, tusāqiṭ can be expressed as “drops” or “causes to drop” in the context of shaking the palm.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:92, tusqiṭa means “you cause to fall.”

Fa-asqiṭ in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:187 can be translated “then cause to fall.”

In Sabaʾ 34:9, nusqiṭ means “We cause to fall” or “We bring down.”

Sāqiṭan in Al-Ṭūr 52:44 means “falling.”

Fī al-fitnati saqaṭū in Al-Tawbah 9:49 can be rendered:

“Into trial they have fallen.”

A natural rendering of suqiṭa fī aydīhim in Al-Aʿrāf 7:149 is:

“They regretted it.”

The word-for-word rendering “it was fallen into their hands” is neither natural nor faithful to the idiom's intended meaning.

The following equivalents cover other lexical forms, with Quranic forms also included for comparison:

  • Saqaṭa: fell or fell down.
  • Asqaṭa: dropped or caused something to fall.
  • Tasāqaṭa: dropped or fell successively, depending on context.
  • Sāqiṭ: falling; in a use concerning standing, lowly or discredited.
  • Suqūṭ: falling or descent.
  • Saqṭah: a fall or lapse.
  • Siqāṭ: errors and slips in speech or conduct.
  • Masqiṭ: a place where something falls or lands.
  • Saqīṭ: something fallen or settled; in some uses, snow, ice, or dew.
  • Siqṭ: the end or lower edge of a tract; in another use, a fetus expelled before completing development.

Quranic translation should not invariably turn falling into destruction, death, sin, or defeat.

These may accompany a fall in some situations, but are not invariant parts of the root's meaning.

The distinction between falling and causing a fall should also remain clear.

Tasquṭu in Al-Anʿām 6:59 describes a leaf falling; nusqiṭ in Sabaʾ 34:9 describes causing something to fall.

This distinction comes from verbal morphology and should not be lost in translation.

Conclusion

S Q Ṭ concerns something falling or descending from its previous position.

This is clear in concrete uses such as falling leaves, dropping fruit, and objects descending from above. Early Arabic also attests siqṭ for a sandy tract's edge or end, and siqāṭ for lapses and errors.

The root occurs eight times in eight Quranic verses.

In Al-Anʿām 6:59, a falling leaf appears within the scope of divine knowledge.

In Maryam 19:25, fresh dates drop as a result of shaking the palm.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:92 and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:187, opponents demand that fragments of the sky be dropped upon them.

In Sabaʾ 34:9, the possibility of dropping sky fragments is related to divine will.

In Al-Ṭūr 52:44, sāqiṭan describes something falling.

In Al-Tawbah 9:49, however, falling describes becoming caught in trial rather than bodily descent.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:149, suqiṭa fī aydīhim idiomatically expresses regret and remorse after recognizing error.

Together these uses show that falling is the central meaning, while its subject and kind emerge from context.

A concluding definition is therefore:

S Q Ṭ primarily denotes something falling or descending from its previous position. Extended uses can express slips in speech and conduct, becoming caught in an undesirable condition, or remorse after error. These secondary senses arise from each expression's construction and context, not from bodily falling alone.

For Forqan, “fall” is the basic equivalent. “Drop” or “cause to fall” fits the relevant formations, while “regret” conveys the idiom suqiṭa fī aydīhim more accurately.

Falling does not itself mean the end of everything. Something may fall and remain intact; a person may slip and still have scope for reform; someone recognizing an error can choose to return rather than persist in it.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:149 combines regret with recognition of misguidance and seeking mercy and forgiveness. It thus presents not only departure from the right course but also the human response to recognizing that condition.

This message comes from the verse's context and does not alter the root's lexical meaning. Falling is an event or condition; human responsibility, the response to it, and the possibility of return must be examined separately through Quranic evidence.
