A coin and monetary unit: historical origins and adaptation in Arabic

1. Historical origins and entry into Arabic

Dinar originally named a monetary unit. It was known in the Roman and Iranian worlds before Islam and entered Arabic through trade, minting, and the circulation of money between regions.

The name ultimately goes back to Latin denarius, originally naming a Roman coin associated with counting in tens. Greek dēnárion and Middle Persian dēnār are important forms in its regional history. They attest its presence in several languages, but do not by themselves establish a single, certain route of transmission into every Arabic variety.

Linguistic sources record forms in Pahlavi, Parthian, Bactrian, and Sogdian, including Bactrian d(d)inaro and Sogdian δēnār. The Parthian inscriptional evidence and its contemporary Greek form attest the name before Islam, while each language’s individual witnesses require separate dating.

In Arabic, dinar chiefly denoted a gold coin. Although the Latin word was initially associated with a silver coin, the name also became established for gold coins during its historical transmission. Metal and value were consequently not identical across periods and regions.

Why do Arabic dictionaries call it arabicized Persian?

Ibn Manẓūr writes in Lisān al-ʿArab:

الدِّينَارُ: فَارِسِيٌّ مُعَرَّبٌ

al-dīnāru fārisiyyun muʿarrabun

“Dinar is Persian, adapted into Arabic.”

Abū Manṣūr al-Azharī likewise explains that dinar and certain other names for coins and goods were originally non-Arabic, but Arabs had long used them until they became part of their language.

These reports need not conflict with the word’s historical background. Its ultimate origin is Latin, while transmission into Arabic could have involved Iranian languages.

Ultimate origin must therefore be distinguished from the language through which Arabic received it. A Latin historical origin does not necessarily contradict its classification as arabicized Persian in Arabic dictionaries.

The precise route into each Arabic variety nevertheless cannot be determined conclusively from resemblance among surviving forms alone.

2. Arabic morphology and derivatives

Ibn Fāris writes in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

الدَّالُ وَالنُّونُ وَالرَّاءُ كَلِمَةٌ وَاحِدَةٌ، وَهِيَ الدِّينَارُ

al-dālu wa-al-nūnu wa-al-rāʾu kalimatun wāḥidatun wa-hiya al-dīnāru

“Dāl, nūn, and rāʾ constitute one word: dīnār.”

He then records dannara wajhu fulānin: someone’s face shone and became radiant.

Dinar thus stands at the center of the familiar Arabic D N R family, with certain other expressions developing from the coin’s name.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: dinar and a shining face

Dīnār and danānīr

The singular is dīnār and its plural danānīr. Dictionaries also record the diminutive dunaynīr.

Early lexicographers explain an underlying dinnār with doubled nūn, one nūn being replaced by yāʾ. They cite its reappearance in danānīr and dunaynīr as evidence for that analysis.

This helps explain the word’s analysis and adaptation within Arabic morphology, but should not be equated with its Latin or Iranian historical origin.

The plural danānīr alone cannot prove that the word originally arose from a native Arabic triliteral root.

Dannara and mudannar

Arabic also formed verbs and descriptions from the name dinar.

Dannara al-dhahaba means minting gold into dinars; the verb is recorded in Arabic dictionaries. The early sources examined here also record dhahabun mudannarun and dīnārun mudannarun for coined gold and a minted dinar. The verbal form should therefore be distinguished from the adjectival witness.

Rajulun mudannarun describes someone possessing many dinars.

Dannara wajhuhu describes a face shining like a dinar.

Thawbun mudannarun describes cloth whose patterns resemble dinars.

A horse’s mudannar coloring also describes rounded dark markings among a lighter color.

After dinar entered Arabic, its familiar brightness, roundness, coin patterns, and monetary value thus provided imagery for new expressions.

None of these qualities should become the primary meaning of Qur’anic dinar. It names a monetary unit, rather than brightness, roundness, or decoration.

3. Dinar in early Arabic and the pre-Islamic economy

Dinar was known regionally before Islam. Gold coins and forms of the name in Iranian and Roman territories attest that history.

Shapur I’s trilingual Sasanian inscription mentions a large sum in dinars. This third-century CE witness establishes the name’s presence in the region’s languages before Islam.

Byzantine gold coins also circulated near the emergence of Islam. Dinar was familiar in Arabia’s economic environment, although the coins’ mint and weight could vary.

A witness from early Arabic poetry

A verse attributed to Muḥriz ibn Mukaʿbir al-Ḍabbī, a poet of the late pre-Islamic period, states:

كَأَنَّ دَنَانِيرًا عَلَى قَسَمَاتِهِمْ
وَإِنْ كَانَ قَدْ شَفَّ الْوُجُوهَ لِقَاءُ

ka-anna danānīran ʿalā qasamātihim / wa-in kāna qad shaffa al-wujūha liqāʾu

“As though dinars shone upon their faces, although encounter and battle had worn those faces thin.”

Danānīr here depicts the faces’ appearance. The coin could thus provide the basis of a visual comparison in Arabic.

Literary sources attribute the line to Muḥriz, but its precise composition date remains uncertain. Attribution to a pre-Islamic poet must therefore be distinguished from conclusively dating this particular line before the Qur’an.

Lisān al-ʿArab: the witness attributed to Muḥriz under Q S M

Did dinars always have one fixed weight?

Across different periods, dinar named both an actual coin and a unit for reckoning monetary value.

Islamic coinage history recognizes gold dinars with the familiar standard of about 4.25 grams, particularly from ʿAbd al-Malik ibn Marwān’s monetary reforms in the late first Islamic century.

This weight cannot be extended to every pre-Islamic dinar without historical investigation. Earlier Byzantine gold coins and certain Iranian issues had different weights.

The Qur’an specifies no weight, purity, diameter, or design for the dinar.

Translation should therefore preserve the monetary name without adding a fixed gold quantity or present-day monetary value.

4. The sole Qur’anic occurrence: Āl ʿImrān 3:75

وَمِنْ أَهْلِ الْكِتَابِ مَنْ إِنْ تَأْمَنْهُ بِقِنْطَارٍ يُؤَدِّهِ إِلَيْكَ وَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ إِنْ تَأْمَنْهُ بِدِينَارٍ لَا يُؤَدِّهِ إِلَيْكَ إِلَّا مَا دُمْتَ عَلَيْهِ قَائِمًا ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمْ قَالُوا لَيْسَ عَلَيْنَا فِي الْأُمِّيِّينَ سَبِيلٌ وَيَقُولُونَ عَلَى اللَّهِ الْكَذِبَ وَهُمْ يَعْلَمُونَ

wa-min ahli al-kitābi man in taʾmanhu bi-qinṭārin yuʾaddihi ilayka wa-minhum man in taʾmanhu bi-dīnārin lā yuʾaddihi ilayka illā mā dumta ʿalayhi qāʾiman dhālika bi-annahum qālū laysa ʿalaynā fī al-ummiyyīna sabīlun wa-yaqūlūna ʿalā allāhi al-kadhiba wa-hum yaʿlamūna

This study’s proposed rendering: Among the People of the Book is someone who, if you entrust him with a qinṭār, returns it to you; and among them is someone who, if you entrust him with a dinar, does not return it unless you keep standing over him. That is because they say: “We bear no liability toward the unlettered.” They knowingly speak lies about God.

Dīnār is an indefinite singular noun here, introduced by bi-: bi-dīnārin.

In in taʾmanhu bi-dīnārin it denotes property entrusted to someone and expected to be returned to its owner.

Its direct meaning is the familiar coin or monetary unit. Entrusting comes from taʾmanhu and returning from yuʾaddihi, rather than from D N R itself.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: sole occurrence in Āl ʿImrān 3:75

The contrast between qinṭār and dinar

The verse contrasts two amounts of property:

Bi-qinṭārin against bi-dīnārin.

Qinṭār represents a large amount of wealth here, while dinar names a particular monetary unit, much smaller by comparison.

This does not make a dinar worthless. A gold dinar could itself have considerable value; it nevertheless exemplifies a more limited amount than a qinṭār.

The principal point is that some people faithfully return even large trusts, while others withhold even a smaller trust without persistent demands from its owner.

The amount alone therefore does not conclusively establish trustworthiness or betrayal.

Lā yuʾaddihi ilayka: failure to return property

The second part says lā yuʾaddihi: he does not deliver or return it to you.

The pronoun -hu refers to the dinar.

The owner has a right to demand and recover the property, but the person holding it refuses to discharge that obligation without persistent demands.

Dinar is the entrusted object, not the name of indebtedness, betrayal, or refusal to pay. Those meanings emerge from the verbs and context.

Does the verse portray all the People of the Book alike?

It begins wa-min ahli al-kitābi man and then says wa-minhum man.

These constructions distinguish people within a group. The first returns a trust even when substantial; the second fails to return entrusted property.

The verse therefore portrays neither all the People of the Book as trustworthy nor all as betrayers.

It subsequently concerns people justifying their conduct by claiming no liability toward the unlettered. The Qur’an identifies this attribution to God as a knowing falsehood.

The following verse, Āl ʿImrān 3:76, emphasizes fulfilling a pledge and being mindful of God. The context therefore concerns responsibility toward trusts and pledges, rather than merely a coin’s name or two amounts’ relative value.

This ethical message comes from the complete construction and context, not the lexical definition of dinar.

Al-Ṭabarī: trusts, demanding repayment and differing conduct

5. Distinguishing related words

D N R (dinar) and D Y N (dīn)

The resemblance between dinar and dīn can invite a mistaken inference.

Arabic D Y N has a broad family concerning religion, judgment, recompense, and indebtedness.

Dinar, however, is a monetary name with an independent history, indexed under D N R in Arabic dictionaries.

Similar letters alone do not make it a derivative of dīn.

Some lexical and exegetical writings connect its name with dīn and nār. Such explanations may be admonitory or wordplay, but do not constitute sound evidence for its historical origin.

Study of D Y N

D N R (dinar) and D R H M (dirham)

Dirham also occurs in the Qur’an:

وَشَرَوْهُ بِثَمَنٍ بَخْسٍ دَرَاهِمَ مَعْدُودَةٍ

wa-sharawhu bi-thamanin bakhsin darāhima maʿdūdatin

Yūsuf 12:20

The phrase means: “They sold him for a meager price, counted dirhams.” Darāhim is the plural of dirham and denotes another monetary unit.

Dirham likewise has a non-Arabic history, related to Greek drachmē, entering regional languages through exchange between territories.

Historically, dinar was chiefly associated with gold money and dirham with silver money.

The Qur’anic occurrences nevertheless establish no fixed value or conversion ratio between them. The relevant verses specify neither weights nor an exchange rate.

Study of D R H M

D N R (dinar) and Q N Ṭ R (qinṭār)

Qinṭār contrasts with dinar in Āl ʿImrān 3:75 itself.

Qinṭār denotes a substantial amount of wealth, whereas dinar names a particular monetary unit.

Their semantic functions thus differ: one denotes a large amount in this context, the other a familiar monetary unit.

The contrast illustrates different conduct toward larger and smaller trusts, rather than establishing a numerical equation between qinṭār and dinar.

6. Translation proposal for Forqan

The most appropriate Qur’anic rendering is “dinar.” The name is also familiar in Persian; retaining it gives greater historical and linguistic precision.

An explanation could identify it as “one dinar, a gold coin familiar at the time.” Such information belongs in the lexical study rather than the direct verse translation.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:75, bi-dīnārin can be rendered “a dinar,” corresponding to the Persian dīnārī.

“A negligible sum,” “one low-value coin,” “twenty-four carats of gold,” or a fixed amount of modern money goes beyond the verse’s wording.

Nor does dinar by itself prescribe a zakat amount, financial threshold, legally defined gold value, or monetary system. Those require separate legal and economic study.

7. Conclusion

Dinar is the historical name of a coin and monetary unit, ultimately derived from Latin and present in regional languages and economies before Islam. Arabic adopted it and formed a plural, diminutive, and certain verbs and descriptions from it.

The Qur’an contains only bi-dīnārin, in Āl ʿImrān 3:75, meaning one familiar monetary unit. Its contrast with qinṭār illustrates differences in people’s trustworthiness concerning different amounts of property.

Brief definition of the family: D N R is the Arabic lexical family centered on dīnār, a historical coin or monetary unit. Other recorded forms chiefly concern dinars themselves, minting, possessing many dinars, or resemblance to their appearance and brightness. Brief root meaning: Dinar: a borrowed name for a gold coin or historical monetary unit, indexed lexically under D N R.

Dinar should not be derived historically from dīn or assigned ethical and religious meaning from its name alone. In its Qur’anic use, it names particular property; trustworthiness and fulfilling pledges emerge from the verse’s construction and context.
