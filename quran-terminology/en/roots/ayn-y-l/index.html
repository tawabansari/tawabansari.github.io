Poverty, need, hardship, and insufficient means of livelihood

1. The root in early Arabic

The Arabic root ʿ–y–l denotes poverty, hardship, and need: a condition in which a person lacks sufficient means to meet life's needs.

The verb ʿāla yaʿīlu means to become poor or needy. ʿAylah names the condition of poverty and hardship, while ʿāʾil describes someone in that condition.

This meaning is not confined to having no money. Someone may possess some property that is nevertheless insufficient for essential needs. Conversely, someone without great wealth may have the means required to live.

Consider a farmer whose land feeds the family. The farmer may have little cash yet be able to meet everyday needs. If drought destroys the crop and the family can no longer be supported, the farmer approaches a condition of hardship and need.

Thus, this principal branch concerns more than the amount of property: it concerns the absence or insufficiency of the resources a person needs to live.

Evidence from pre-Islamic poetry

A verse attributed to Aḥīḥah ibn al-Julāḥ, a pre-Islamic poet of the Aws in Yathrib, reads:

وَمَا يَدْرِي الْفَقِيرُ مَتَى غِنَاهُ
وَمَا يَدْرِي الْغَنِيُّ مَتَى يَعِيلُ

wa-mā yadrī al-faqīru matā ghināhu / wa-mā yadrī al-ghaniyyu matā yaʿīlu

Translation: The poor person does not know when sufficiency will come, nor does the prosperous person know when poverty will come.

This verse clearly illustrates the meaning of yaʿīlu.

The poet juxtaposes two contrasting conditions: poverty and sufficiency.

In the first half, the poor person does not know when hardship will end. In the second, the prosperous person does not know when need may arise.

Yaʿīlu therefore means becoming poor and losing a condition of sufficiency.

This usage directly clarifies the root without requiring figurative images or later interpretations to define it.

The verse is transmitted in lexical sources under the name of Aḥīḥah ibn al-Julāḥ, a pre-Islamic poet. Its precise date of composition is not independently established here; its attribution and transmission must be distinguished from a contemporary written record.

Further evidence concerning need

A verse attributed to ʿAbd al-Aswad ibn ʿĀmir al-Ṭāʾī uses the expression ʿuyyalan abnāʾuhum.

The phrase refers to children who have become poor and needy as a result of the situation described.

Here, ʿuyyal is a plural of ʿāʾil in the sense of poor.

The attribution and full wording vary in lexical transmissions, including the name of the tribe mentioned. The verse therefore cannot establish a definitive date for the usage.

Semantically, however, ʿuyyal for needy people agrees with the preceding example and shows that this word family could describe both poverty and those experiencing it.

The semantic core

On the evidence of early usage, the principal meaning of ʿ–y–l may be defined as follows:

Being or becoming poor and needy, with insufficient resources to meet life's needs.

This definition distinguishes three matters:

First, the condition of hardship itself, expressed by ʿaylah.

Second, the person experiencing it, called ʿāʾil.

Third, entering that condition, which the verb ʿāla yaʿīlu can express.

These uses are directly related but grammatically distinct.

Poverty and need are not themselves moral descriptions. An honest, hardworking person may become needy through drought, illness, war, or other circumstances. Financial circumstances do not determine moral worth.

2. Forms and derivatives

The principal Arabic forms associated with this family include:

ʿĀla yaʿīlu: became poor, needy, or impoverished.

ʿAyl: poverty and need.

ʿAylah: hardship and insufficient means of livelihood.

ʿĀʾil: poor, impoverished, or needy.

ʿAyyil: in some lexical uses, poor or dependent on another for one's needs.

ʿĀlah: a plural of ʿāʾil meaning poor or needy people.

ʿUyyal: in some uses, a plural of ʿāʾil meaning poor and needy people.

ʿIyāl: people whose living needs are another person's responsibility, such as children or other dependants.

Muʿīl: in some Arabic uses, someone with dependants or responsible for supporting others.

Forms such as ʿiyāl, ʿayyil, and muʿīl are lexically associated with need and support. They should not all be treated as unquestionably independent derivatives of one branch without morphological and historical examination, since ʿ–y–l and ʿ–w–l overlap.

Early Arabic also records uses such as ʿāla fī al-arḍ, moving or roaming about, and ʿayyāl for someone who sways or struts while walking.

These uses do not occur in the Quran, and the available evidence does not justify deriving all of them from poverty. Need should not be imposed on every lexical branch merely to produce apparent unity.

Forms actually found in the Quran

The root ʿ–y–l occurs in the Quran in only two forms:

  • ʿAylatan in Al-Tawbah 9:28: a noun meaning poverty and hardship.
  • ʿĀʾilan in Al-Ḍuḥā 93:8: an active participle meaning poor or needy.

Both uses are directly connected with sufficiency and the removal of need.

The simple verb ʿāla yaʿīlu does not occur in the Quran, but its meaning is known from early Arabic evidence and these two Quranic derivatives.

3. ʿAylah: fear of poverty and losing one's means of living

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِنَّمَا الْمُشْرِكُونَ نَجَسٌ فَلَا يَقْرَبُوا الْمَسْجِدَ الْحَرَامَ بَعْدَ عَامِهِمْ هَٰذَا وَإِنْ خِفْتُمْ عَيْلَةً فَسَوْفَ يُغْنِيكُمُ اللَّهُ مِنْ فَضْلِهِ إِنْ شَاءَ إِنَّ اللَّهَ عَلِيمٌ حَكِيمٌ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū innamā al-mushrikūna najasun fa-lā yaqrabū al-masjida al-ḥarāma baʿda ʿāmihim hādhā wa-in khiftum ʿaylatan fa-sawfa yughnīkumu Allāhu min faḍlihi in shāʾa inna Allāha ʿalīmun ḥakīm

Al-Tawbah 9:28

Translation: You who have believed, the polytheists are indeed unclean, so let them not approach the Sacred Mosque after this year of theirs. If you fear poverty, The God will enrich you from His bounty, if He wills. Surely The God is Knowing, Wise.

Al-Tawbah 9:28

Here, ʿaylah means poverty and hardship.

The verse does not state that the addressees were necessarily poor at that moment; it speaks of their fear of becoming impoverished.

Wa-in khiftum ʿaylatan is a conditional clause.

Khiftum is a past-tense verb with a plural subject, and ʿaylatan is its object.

Thus, ʿaylah is the condition whose occurrence the addressees fear.

Connection with economic circumstances

The verse first directs that the polytheists not approach the Sacred Mosque after the year in question.

Concern about poverty follows.

This connection suggests that changes in the movement of groups of people could affect the addressees' economic life.

For example, in a town where much trade depends on travellers and caravans, fewer visitors can reduce the income of merchants, food sellers, lodging providers, and others serving them.

Even people who do not trade directly with travellers may suffer from reduced economic activity.

This example explains why restrictions on the movement of a group might raise fears of poverty.

The word ʿaylah itself does not reveal the details of the economy at that time or each group's dependence on those movements. These require separate historical research.

The verse explicitly raises the possibility of fearing poverty in connection with the preceding command; the conditional wording alone does not establish that every addressee felt such fear.

Does ʿaylah mean the loss of trade?

Reduced trade or income may cause poverty, but ʿaylah does not lexically mean reduced trade itself.

Someone may become poor through job loss, crop destruction, illness, war, or rising essential expenses.

What these situations share is need and insufficient means of living.

Although ʿaylah in Al-Tawbah 9:28 concerns economic fear in particular circumstances, the root is not restricted to trade, caravans, or income in one particular place.

Fa-sawfa yughnīkumu Allāhu min faḍlihi

Against the fear of ʿaylah, the verse speaks of The God making the addressees sufficient.

Yughnīkumu belongs to gh–n–y, not ʿ–y–l.

This contrast further clarifies ʿaylah.

If ʿaylah is a condition of need, ighnāʾ concerns removing that need or attaining sufficiency.

If a farmer has lost the crop and cannot feed the family, a new opportunity to produce or obtain necessities can bring an end to that condition.

Such a change need not mean accumulating great wealth. A person may escape hardship and attain an adequate, dignified livelihood without becoming very wealthy.

Min faḍlihi indicates that sufficiency comes from The God's bounty.

The qualification in shāʾa, “if He wills,” is also present and must not be omitted from translation or analysis.

The verse specifies no particular economic means of achieving this sufficiency. Identifying it definitively with a source of income, taxation, expanded trade, or a particular historical event requires separate evidence.

For ʿ–y–l, the central point is that ʿaylah denotes need, contrasted with ighnāʾ.

4. ʿĀʾil: a needy person whose need is met

وَوَجَدَكَ عَائِلًا فَأَغْنَىٰ

wa-wajadaka ʿāʾilan fa-aghnā

Al-Ḍuḥā 93:8

Translation: And He found you in need, then made you sufficient.

Al-Ḍuḥā 93:8

ʿĀʾilan is the active participle of ʿāla yaʿīlu in the sense of becoming needy.

Here it is accusative and describes Muhammad's condition in wa-wajadaka.

The pronoun -ka in wa-wajadaka refers to Muhammad, as does the description ʿāʾilan.

In a common syntactic analysis, ʿāʾilan is the second object of wajada. The statement is clear: The God found him in a condition of need.

Fa-aghnā follows: He made him sufficient.

This contrast is among the clearest indications of the Quranic meaning of ʿāʾil.

The relationship between ʿāʾil and sufficiency

Just as ʿaylah contrasts with ighnāʾ in Al-Tawbah 9:28, ʿāʾil contrasts here with fa-aghnā.

There is, however, a difference between the two verses.

Al-Tawbah 9:28 addresses the fear of becoming impoverished.

Al-Ḍuḥā 93:8 speaks of Muhammad's actual need and its removal.

The first concerns a feared possibility; the second concerns a condition the person experienced.

The root's meaning is consistent, while its grammatical role and the kind of statement differ.

Connection with the surrounding verses

Al-Ḍuḥā 93:6 recalls Muhammad's orphanhood and The God's sheltering him.

Al-Ḍuḥā 93:7 speaks of finding him lost and guiding him.

Al-Ḍuḥā 93:8 concerns his need and being made sufficient.

These verses describe conditions in which Muhammad needed help, shelter, guidance, and the meeting of his needs.

Al-Ḍuḥā 93:9 then directs that the orphan not be oppressed.

Al-Ḍuḥā 93:10 commands that the person asking not be rebuffed.

Al-Ḍuḥā 93:11 concerns speaking of the Lord's blessing.

This sequence connects remembrance of past need with one's treatment of others in need.

Someone who has experienced need may better understand a person who needs help today.

This ethical implication comes from the relationship between the verses, not from the isolated meaning of ʿāʾil.

Does ʿāʾil mean a family provider?

In other Arabic constructions and uses, ʿāʾil can describe someone responsible for supporting dependants.

For example, someone paying the living expenses of children or other dependants may be called ʿāʾil.

This use differs from ʿāʾil meaning a poor or needy person.

Morphologically, ʿāʾil can be associated both with ʿāla yaʿīlu, becoming poor, and with ʿāla yaʿūlu, supporting dependants. Their identical participial form does not make their contextual meanings identical.

In Al-Ḍuḥā 93:8, the direct contrast between ʿāʾilan and fa-aghnā supports the meaning of need.

“He found you with a family” or “responsible for supporting a family” therefore has less contextual support than “He found you in need.”

Dependants may increase a person's financial needs, but responsibility for supporting them is not equivalent to poverty.

Does ighnāʾ here concern financial wealth alone?

Arabic ghinā can denote sufficiency and freedom from need, taking different forms in different contexts.

In Al-Ḍuḥā 93:8, its contrast with ʿāʾil indicates the removal of need.

The verse does not explain exactly how that sufficiency came about.

Some historical accounts emphasize Khadijah's wealth in Muhammad's life. Other readings also discuss contentment and independence from others.

The verse itself neither names Khadijah nor specifies an amount of property or a particular means of livelihood.

For this root study, it is enough to say that ʿāʾil denotes need and fa-aghnā concerns its removal.

The root does not establish every historical detail of Muhammad's life.

5. Comparison with related roots

ʿ–Y–L (ʿayl) and ʿ–W–L (ʿawl)

These families resemble one another in their letters and some morphological forms.

In Quranic morphological indexing, ʿaylah and ʿāʾil are placed under ʿ–y–l, while taʿūlū in Al-Nisāʾ 4:3 is placed under ʿ–w–l.

Poverty and need are prominent in the Quranic uses of ʿ–y–l.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:3, the preferred meaning of taʿūlū is departing from justice and acting unjustly.

Different uses of these derivatives do not establish that the families are historically wholly independent.

Lexicographers including Ibn Fāris discuss the connection of ʿ–y–l with forms containing wāw and relate it historically to the wāw root.

Early sources also record poverty for certain uses of ʿāla yaʿūlu. Forms concerning livelihood and dependants likewise overlap between the families.

Two issues must therefore be distinguished: possible historical relationships and the actual meaning of each Quranic derivative.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:3, the context of justice is decisive.

In Al-Tawbah 9:28 and Al-Ḍuḥā 93:8, need and sufficiency are decisive.

Formal resemblance does not justify translating ʿaylah as injustice or taʿūlū as poverty without examining context.

ʿ–Y–L (ʿayl) and F–Q–R (faqr)

Arabic faqr concerns need and insufficient means.

ʿAylah belongs to the same semantic field.

In Al-Tawbah 9:28, ʿaylah naturally translates as poverty or hardship.

In Al-Ḍuḥā 93:8, ʿāʾil similarly means poor or needy.

There is thus clear semantic overlap.

This does not make every Arabic use of f–q–r and ʿ–y–l identical.

For example, al-fuqarāʾ in Al-Tawbah 9:60 names one specified category of recipients of charitable contributions, within a classification of recipients.

ʿAylah in Al-Tawbah 9:28, however, names a condition the addressees fear entering.

This difference concerns grammar and context; it does not necessarily establish two fixed, distinct degrees of need.

ʿ–Y–L (ʿayl) and S–K–N (maskanah)

Miskīn is a Quranic designation for a needy person.

S–k–n also concerns stillness and rest, but that lexical image does not mean every miskīn is physically immobilized or wholly inactive socially.

ʿĀʾil also describes a needy person, understood through ʿāla yaʿīlu, becoming poor.

One person may be described as both ʿāʾil and miskīn.

The roots alone establish no absolute distinction whereby one is always temporarily needy and the other permanently so.

The kind of need and the people's circumstances must be examined in each verse.

ʿ–Y–L (ʿayl) and M–L–Q (imlāq)

Quranic imlāq concerns poverty and hardship.

Al-Anʿām 6:151 uses min imlāqin.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:31 uses khashyata imlāqin.

The grammatical difference matters: one presents poverty as motivating the condemned conduct; the other expresses fear of becoming poor.

This distinction also matters when studying ʿ–y–l.

In Al-Tawbah 9:28, khiftum ʿaylatan concerns fear of need, not necessarily poverty already present.

ʿAylah and imlāq are therefore semantically close, but actual poverty must be distinguished from fear of poverty in each verse.

ʿ–Y–L (ʿayl) and Gh–N–Y (ghinā)

One of the clearest Quranic semantic relationships of ʿ–y–l is its contrast with gh–n–y.

In Al-Tawbah 9:28, ʿaylah contrasts with yughnīkumu.

In Al-Ḍuḥā 93:8, ʿāʾil contrasts with fa-aghnā.

Ghinā here concerns sufficiency or the removal of need.

Sufficiency does not always mean abundant wealth.

For example, an adequate income may cover food, clothing, and shelter without hardship, while someone with apparently greater assets still experiences shortages because of more extensive obligations and needs.

This illustrates that human need and sufficiency concern the ability to meet needs, although the amount of property may also matter.

The God's sufficiency must not be equated with relative human sufficiency. The Quran calls Him al-Ghaniyy: His independence means not depending on created beings, not possessing more financial resources than others.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

For ʿ–y–l, straightforward equivalents such as poverty, need, and hardship are preferable to cumbersome or artificial expressions.

In its principal branch, ʿāla yaʿīlu means became poor, became needy, or fell into hardship.

ʿAylah means poverty, hardship, or a condition of need.

In its poverty-related use, ʿāʾil means needy or impoverished.

ʿĀlah and ʿuyyal in this sense may be translated as poor or needy people.

In contexts of support, ʿiyāl means dependants or people whose livelihood is another person's responsibility.

These latter equivalents must not be imposed on the Quranic ʿaylah and ʿāʾil without examining morphology and context.

Translating ʿaylah in Al-Tawbah 9:28

Quranic expression:

Wa-in khiftum ʿaylatan.

Suggested translation:

“And if you fear poverty.”

This rendering is concise, natural, and accurate.

ʿAylah is the object of khiftum, so the translation should show that poverty is what the addressees fear.

“If you are poor” is inaccurate because it removes fear and possibility from the construction.

“If your trade decreases” is likewise not the direct meaning of ʿaylah, although the economic concern may relate to reduced trade.

Translating ʿāʾil in Al-Ḍuḥā 93:8

Quranic expression:

Wa-wajadaka ʿāʾilan fa-aghnā.

Suggested translation:

“And He found you in need, then made you sufficient.”

This preserves both the meaning of ʿāʾil and its contrast with ighnāʾ.

“In need” naturally expresses poverty and need without imposing a specific amount of property or type of deprivation.

“Impoverished” is also defensible, while “in need” provides a clear and appropriate proposal for Forqan.

ʿĀʾil should not become “family provider” here: its contrast with fa-aghnā and the root's other Quranic use support the meaning of need.

7. Conclusion

The principal branch of ʿ–y–l concerns poverty, need, and hardship.

The verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Aḥīḥah ibn al-Julāḥ clarifies yaʿīlu through its contrast with ghinā: a prosperous person may become poor, and a poor person may escape need.

The root occurs in only two Quranic forms.

ʿAylah in Al-Tawbah 9:28 denotes the hardship whose occurrence is feared.

ʿĀʾil in Al-Ḍuḥā 93:8 describes a needy person whom The God made sufficient.

Both directly contrast with verbs from gh–n–y, reinforcing the meaning of poverty and need.

This need should not be restricted to a lack of cash, absolute hunger, or dependence on a particular income source. It concerns inadequate means of meeting life's needs.

Poverty itself indicates neither moral weakness, unbelief, nor worthlessness. Al-Ḍuḥā 93:8 recalls Muhammad's need, and the same surah proceeds to require care for the orphan's dignity and forbids rebuffing the person asking.

The Quranic uses of ʿ–y–l and ʿ–w–l must be distinguished, while lexical sources discuss their historical relationship and overlapping forms. Separate indexing does not establish complete historical independence.

Final definition of ʿ–y–l:

In its principal branch, ʿ–y–l denotes poverty and need: being or becoming unable to meet life's needs with the resources available. ʿAylah names the condition, and ʿāʾil describes the person experiencing it. Both Quranic words contrast with sufficiency and primarily concern hardship and need, rather than family provision, injustice, or moral character.
