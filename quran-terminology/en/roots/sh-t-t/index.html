Dispersion, separation, and diversity: from spaces between parts to different paths, efforts, and orientations in the Quran

In early Arabic, SH T T concerns being dispersed, separated, or spaced apart. Separation may be spatial, as with people in different places or objects that are not touching. It may also concern kind, character, orientation, or course: people may gather in one place while their purposes diverge, or things may belong to one collection while differing in type and qualities.

Within this family, shatt describes something separate or scattered, shatīt something whose parts are spaced apart, shatāt dispersion, and shattā diverse or separate things and conditions.

The semantic core is the separateness or lack of uniformity and unity among parts of a collection in position, character, or direction. Not every use implies that the parts were previously joined and then separated. Sometimes the root expresses separation taking place; sometimes it describes existing dispersion or diversity.

This distinction matters for Quranic reading: shattā describes diverse plants, differing human efforts, and divided hearts, whereas ashtātan concerns eating separately and people coming forth separately at the resurrection. Neither form inherently carries a positive or negative value.

1. The root in early Arabic

Spaces between parts: a witness from Tarafa ibn al-Abd

The pre-Islamic poet Tarafa ibn al-Abd describes a woman’s beautiful teeth:

بَادِنٌ تَجْلُو إِذَا مَا ابْتَسَمَتْ
عَنْ شَتِيتٍ كَأَقَاحِ الرَّمْلِ غُرّ

bādinun tajlū idhā mā ibtasamat / ʿan shatītin ka-aqāḥi al-ramli ghurr

The verse describes a full-bodied woman whose smile reveals white, spaced teeth, like white flowers growing in the sand.

Shatīt here refers to teeth with natural, attractive gaps. The poet compares them to white flowers appearing separately.

This witness, attributed to a pre-Islamic poet, clearly illustrates parts belonging to one collection without being pressed together. The attribution does not independently establish an exact date of composition.

The teeth remain parts of one mouth. Shatīt does not mean that the collection has been destroyed, lost its connection, or fallen into harmful disorder. Their spacing is part of their beauty in this verse.

Dispersion in this family therefore need not mean collapse or corruption. It may simply describe spaces or distinctions between parts.

Two different circumstances: a witness from al-Asha

In a famous verse, the pre-Islamic poet al-Asha says:

شَتَّانَ مَا يَوْمِي عَلَى كُورِهَا
وَيَوْمُ حَيَّانَ أَخِي جَابِرِ

shattāna mā yawmī ʿalā kūrihā / wa-yawmu ḥayyāna akhī jābir

“How different is my day upon her saddle from my day with Hayyan, the brother of Jabir!”

The poet contrasts the hardship of travel with a day of ease and companionship.

Shattāna is classified in Arabic grammar as an expression functioning like a past-tense verb, conveying how far apart or different two things are.

This is not the spatial dispersion of several people. Two circumstances are compared, their distance lying in different conditions and experiences of life.

This witness indicates that the root’s early semantic field was not confined to physical distance. Two things can differ markedly in quality, condition, or result.

The verse does not make hardship or ease meanings of the root itself. They arise from what is compared; shattāna highlights the difference between them.

What the early witnesses show

Tarafa’s verse illustrates spaces between parts of one collection, and al-Asha’s illustrates a marked contrast between two circumstances.

These verses are attributed to recognized pre-Islamic poets and are valuable witnesses to early usage, with due attention to transmission and dating. They do not establish that every form in later dictionaries occurred with the same use and frequency in pre-Islamic Arabic.

The witnesses support this definition:

SH T T concerns separation, distance, or difference among parts, people, or matters, whether in location and arrangement or in condition and character.

2. Important forms and derivatives

The following forms are known in Arabic:

  • شَتَّ / يَشِتُّ — shatta / yashittu: became scattered or separated, or ceased to be gathered together.
  • شَتّ — shatt: separate, dispersed, or not in one shared condition with other things.
  • شَتَات — shatāt: dispersion, separation, or disunity.
  • شَتِيت — shatīt: dispersed, separate, or composed of spaced parts.
  • شَتَّى — shattā: diverse, different, or scattered; dictionaries identify it as a plural of shatīt.
  • أَشْتَات — ashtāt: a plural of shatt; separate people or things.
  • شَتَّتَ — shattata: scattered something or separated its parts.
  • أَشَتَّ — ashatta: dispersed or caused separation.
  • تَشَتَّتَ — tashattata: became dispersed or separated into parts.
  • مُشَتَّت — mushattat: scattered or disunited.
  • شَتَّانَ — shattāna: an expression functioning as a verb, conveying how far apart or different two things are.

Only two forms occur in the Quran: shattā in Ta Ha 20:53, Al-Hashr 59:14, and Al-Layl 92:4; and ashtātan in An-Nur 24:61 and Az-Zalzalah 99:6.

The verbs shatta, shattata, and tashattata, and the forms shatāt, shatīt, and shattāna, do not occur in those forms in the Quran.

Shattā can describe diversity, while ashtāt more often emphasizes separate individuals or parts. The distinction is not absolute: both belong to the shared field of separation and difference.

3. The semantic core: dispersion and diversity

Three situations should be distinguished when studying SH T T.

First is spatial separation. People may be in different places, or sit and eat separately within one place. The difference concerns their position or arrangement.

Second is diversity of kind and character. Plants growing in one area may differ in kind, shape, color, size, or other qualities. They need not previously have been one kind and subsequently separated. The wording is compatible with diversity already present; it does not settle the history of how that diversity arose.

Third is inward or behavioral divergence. People may belong to one group while their thoughts, purposes, or efforts do not point in the same direction. Outward gathering then coexists with inward division.

These situations show why the root cannot be defined only as the breaking apart of something formerly unified. That fits some uses but does not adequately explain diverse plants or differing human efforts.

Nor should its meaning expand indiscriminately to every possible difference. The family characteristically concerns separate or differing parts of a collection, or comparable matters, in distance, kind, or direction.

Dispersion and diversity are not unrelated here: both involve the absence of complete uniformity or togetherness among parts. Yet difference in kind should not be converted into spatial separation, nor spatial separation into intellectual disagreement or hostility.

4. Quranic uses

Ta Ha 20:53: the diversity of plants

وَأَنْزَلَ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً فَأَخْرَجْنَا بِهِ أَزْوَاجًا مِنْ نَبَاتٍ شَتَّىٰ

wa-anzala mina al-samāʾi māʾan fa-akhrajnā bihi azwājan min nabātin shattā

Ṭā Hā 20:53

“He sent down water from the sky, and with it We brought forth kinds of diverse plants.”

Shattā expresses the diversity of the plants. Grammarians have taken it as modifying either nabāt or azwājan; under either analysis, the passage concerns different kinds of vegetation.

The Quran describes rain and the growth of plants sharing something in common: all are plants and connected with water, yet they are not alike in kind and character.

Shattā here does not mean harmful dispersion or disintegration. Nor does the word alone imply that the plants were initially one kind and subsequently became different kinds.

The verse speaks of the diversity of plants brought forth through water.

Ta Ha 20:54 continues with eating and grazing livestock, presenting these phenomena as signs for people of understanding. Plant diversity here belongs to the blessings and order of creation.

The word describes diversity without an inherently adverse judgment. Its positive value in this passage comes from the setting of blessing and creation, rather than the root alone.

This use prevents SH T T from always being understood as disorder, division, or collapse.

An-Nur 24:61: eating together or separately

لَيْسَ عَلَيْكُمْ جُنَاحٌ أَنْ تَأْكُلُوا جَمِيعًا أَوْ أَشْتَاتًا

laysa ʿalaykum junāḥun an taʾkulū jamīʿan aw ashtātā

Al-Nūr 24:61

“There is no blame upon you for eating together or separately.”

Ashtātan appears in contrast to jamīʿan.

Jamīʿan denotes eating gathered together, while ashtātan denotes eating separately.

This may mean individually or in separate groups. The word does not require every person to eat entirely alone.

The verse first mentions people who are blind, have a walking disability, or are ill, then addresses eating from one’s own home, relatives’ and friends’ homes, and places whose keys one holds.

It then declares both arrangements free of blame: together or separately.

Ashtātan here does not mean disagreement, hostility, or abandonment of family ties. It describes how people are arranged when eating.

The significant point is that being separate is not in itself presented as objectionable or as evidence of moral division.

If people eat separately because their needs, schedules, or circumstances differ, that behavior alone does not establish broken relationships or a lack of affection.

The verse permits both arrangements rather than requiring a single pattern.

Al-Hashr 59:14: outward gathering and divided hearts

بَأْسُهُمْ بَيْنَهُمْ شَدِيدٌ تَحْسَبُهُمْ جَمِيعًا وَقُلُوبُهُمْ شَتَّىٰ

baʾsuhum baynahum shadīdun taḥsabuhum jamīʿan wa-qulūbuhum shattā

Al-Ḥashr 59:14

“Their conflict among themselves is severe; you think them united, yet their hearts are divided.”

This is a particularly clear inward use of shattā in the Quran.

It describes the hearts of the people identified in the context of Al-Hashr.

The verse distinguishes two levels: their collective appearance and their inward condition.

People may belong to the same front, defend shared interests, or cooperate against another party. Such gathering does not necessarily establish harmony of heart, mutual trust, or unity of purpose.

Taḥsabuhum jamīʿan emphasizes the observer’s impression; wa-qulūbuhum shattā describes inward division.

Shattā does not mean geographical dispersion here: the very people who appear gathered together are described as divided in their hearts.

The passage concerns more than differing tastes. One interpretation takes baʾsuhum baynahum shadīd as severe hostility among them; others emphasize their displays of strength among themselves and their weakness in confrontation. Either way, the explicit statement that their hearts are divided supports the distinction between outward gathering and inward unity.

Shattā here is close to “divergent and divided,” with the negative significance coming from the situation described.

This description should not be detached from the group under discussion and extended to every member of an ethnicity, religion, or society. The verse addresses people characterized within its particular context.

The use shows that people gathering together and their orientations being unified are different matters.

Al-Layl 92:4: the diversity of human efforts

إِنَّ سَعْيَكُمْ لَشَتَّىٰ

inna saʿyakum la-shattā

Al-Layl 92:4

“Indeed, your striving is diverse.”

Shattā here describes human striving.

Saʿy concerns effort and purposeful movement. Calling these efforts shattā means that human endeavors do not all follow the same course or direction.

The following verses explain the difference.

Al-Layl 92:5 and Al-Layl 92:6 describe someone who gives, is mindful, and affirms the best. By contrast, Al-Layl 92:8 and Al-Layl 92:9 describe someone who is miserly, considers himself self-sufficient, and denies the best.

Diversity here is therefore more than different occupations, abilities, talents, or amounts of activity. The moral direction and consequences of human choices also matter.

Two people may both work hard, while one directs that effort toward giving, mindfulness, and righteous action, and the other toward miserliness and disregard for truth.

Shattā itself does not mean good and bad efforts; the following verses supply that distinction.

It should therefore not be translated directly as “morally opposed efforts,” although the contrast is clear in the surah’s context.

In la-shattā, the lām marks emphasis and is not part of the root. The inna … la- construction emphasizes the assertion that efforts differ.

“Indeed, your striving is diverse” is consequently natural and preserves the word’s primary meaning.

Az-Zalzalah 99:6: people coming forth separately

يَوْمَئِذٍ يَصْدُرُ النَّاسُ أَشْتَاتًا لِيُرَوْا أَعْمَالَهُمْ

yawmaʾidhin yaṣduru al-nāsu ashtātan li-yuraw aʿmālahum

Az-Zalzalah 99:6

“That day people come forth separately, to be shown their deeds.”

Ashtātan describes the people’s condition when they yaṣduru, come forth or depart.

Unlike Al-Hashr 59:14, which describes divided hearts, this verse describes the people themselves as dispersed or separate.

Their separation may relate to differences between groups and deeds: the verse continues with the showing of deeds, and Az-Zalzalah 99:7 and Az-Zalzalah 99:8 speak of seeing good and evil, even to the weight of a particle.

Ashtātan itself does not specify the number of groups, their order of movement, or the exact basis of their division.

Nor does the word alone establish that people necessarily divide into two, three, or any other specified number of groups.

The verse explicitly says that people come forth separately that day to be shown their deeds.

Ashtātan does not mean aimless disorder or randomness in divine judgment. Separation when departing is compatible with order and the accounting of deeds.

Where An-Nur 24:61 concerned a normal activity of life, the same word family here describes people in the resurrection scene.

5. Distinguishing SH T T from related roots

SH T T (shatāt) and F R Q (farq)

Both roots belong to the field of separation but present it differently.

F R Q can denote separating, distinguishing, or establishing a boundary between things. The act of separation or the emergence of a distinction can be central.

SH T T more often highlights the condition of dispersion, separation, or diversity among parts.

When a group is divided into separate groups, farraqa can describe the act of division and shatāt the resulting dispersed condition.

This must not become an obligatory sequence: shattā describes diverse plants without the verse mentioning their separation from an original unified collection.

SH T T is therefore not necessarily a stage following F R Q. They overlap, but each construction highlights a particular aspect of separation or difference.

SH T T (shatāt) and J M AYN (jamʿ)

J M AYN concerns bringing parts, people, or matters together. SH T T can express their dispersion or separation.

Two Quranic verses show this contrast clearly.

An-Nur 24:61 says:

جَمِيعًا أَوْ أَشْتَاتًا

jamīʿan aw ashtātā

Al-Nūr 24:61

“Together or separately.”

Two arrangements of people are placed in contrast.

Al-Hashr 59:14, however, says:

تَحْسَبُهُمْ جَمِيعًا وَقُلُوبُهُمْ شَتَّىٰ

taḥsabuhum jamīʿan wa-qulūbuhum shattā

Al-Ḥashr 59:14

“You think them united, yet their hearts are divided.”

Here gathering and division coexist at different levels.

Jamʿ and shatāt are therefore not always absolutely exclusive conditions. A group can be spatially together while inwardly divided.

This matters for understanding collective behavior: physical proximity alone does not establish shared orientation or purpose.

SH T T (shattā) and KH L F (ikhtilāf)

Ikhtilāf can denote differences among people, matters, qualities, or directions. The Quran speaks of differences in languages, colors, and other things.

Shattā overlaps with ikhtilāf in some uses, particularly where different kinds or characteristics are involved.

Plants described as shattā may differ in color, shape, or kind. The word does not name every specific difference; it describes their diversity collectively.

Hearts described as shattā in Al-Hashr 59:14 likewise do more than possess different qualities: the setting concerns division and the absence of genuine unity.

Ikhtilāf and shatāt are not complete synonyms. Ikhtilāf may concern a difference in one quality, whereas shatāt often emphasizes separate or divergent parts of a collection.

SH T T (shatāt) and N SH R (nashr)

In uses concerning spreading and dispersion, nashr denotes unfolding, extending, or spreading something.

Shatāt emphasizes the condition of separate parts, not necessarily their movement or expansion.

Something may spread over a broad area while its parts retain an identifiable order and connection. Conversely, something can occupy a small area while its parts remain separate.

Spread and dispersion may accompany each other without being identical concepts.

6. Morphology, syntax, and translation

SH T T is a doubled root: its last two consonants are identical. In forms such as shatta, the two t sounds are represented by gemination.

Dictionaries identify Quranic shattā as a plural of shatīt, while its syntactic function can be adjectival or predicative.

In Ta Ha 20:53, shattā may modify nabāt or azwājan. Both analyses convey the diversity of the vegetation.

In Al-Hashr 59:14, shattā is the predicate of qulūbuhum, describing the hearts’ condition.

In Al-Layl 92:4, shattā is the predicate of inna; the prefixed lām emphasizes the diversity of efforts.

In An-Nur 24:61 and Az-Zalzalah 99:6, ashtātan is accusative and functions as a circumstantial expression. It describes the arrangement when eating in the former and people’s condition when coming forth in the latter.

Ashtāt is morphologically a plural of shatt. Its initial hamza belongs to the plural pattern, not the root or a Form IV verb.

Shattā belongs to SH T T and is distinct from shitāʾ, “winter,” studied under SH T W. Their resemblance does not establish a shared root or meaning.

Translation suggestions for Forqan

The equivalent should fit the kind of separation or diversity involved.

Shatt: “separate,” “scattered,” or “different,” depending on the subject.

Shatāt: “dispersion,” “separation,” or “disunity,” where the context supports rupture.

Shatīt: “scattered,” “separate,” or “having spaced parts.”

Shattā: “diverse,” “different,” “scattered,” or “divergent,” depending on what it describes.

Ashtātan: “separately,” “dispersed,” or “in separate groups.”

Shattāna: “How different they are!” or “How far apart they are!”

The following equivalents suit the five Quranic uses. These suggestions do not change the installed verse translations.

  • Ta Ha 20:53: “diverse plants,” because the subject is botanical diversity.
  • An-Nur 24:61: “separately,” contrasted with eating together.
  • Al-Hashr 59:14: “their hearts are divided,” contrasting inward divergence with outward gathering.
  • Al-Layl 92:4: “your striving is diverse,” expressing different endeavors and directions.
  • Az-Zalzalah 99:6: “people come forth separately,” describing their separation.

One fixed equivalent should not be imposed throughout. “Scattered” for plants may suggest spatial distance, where “diverse” is clearer. Conversely, “diverse” does not adequately convey the contrast involved in eating separately.

“Distraught,” “disordered,” “hostile,” and “collapsed” should not replace this root without contextual evidence. Such conditions may sometimes result from dispersion, but are not constant components of its meaning.

7. Conclusion

Early Arabic SH T T concerns separateness, spacing, dispersion, and difference among parts or matters. Poetry attributed to pre-Islamic poets illustrates both the physical spacing of teeth and the marked contrast between two circumstances of life.

The Quran uses the family in two forms, shattā and ashtātan. Their range includes natural plant diversity, eating separately, divided hearts, different human efforts, and people coming forth separately at the resurrection.

These uses show that dispersion does not always mean collapse, nor diversity harmful disagreement. In Ta Ha 20:53, plant diversity belongs to creation; An-Nur 24:61 permits eating separately; Al-Hashr 59:14 places divided hearts in a setting of conflict and divergence; Al-Layl 92:4 is explained through differing moral orientations; and Az-Zalzalah 99:6 describes people coming forth separately to see their deeds.

The positive or negative value of each use therefore depends on its subject, syntax, and context rather than the root alone.

A defensible semantic core for SH T T is “parts of a collection being separate or different in position, kind, condition, or direction.” Where distance and separation matter, “scattered” and “separate” fit; where different kinds or courses matter, “diverse” and “different” convey the sense more naturally.

This distinction allows diversity in creation, flexibility in ordinary conduct, inward human disagreement, and differing moral paths to be understood in their respective settings, without reducing all of them to a single negative meaning of division and collapse.
