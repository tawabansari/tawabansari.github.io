1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

The three-letter Arabic root N S L concerns something coming forth and separating from its source. This appears in several established fields: offspring issuing from earlier generations, hair and feathers falling from an animal’s body, and the swift movement of a creature leaving a place or advancing toward a destination.

The root occurs four times in four Qur’anic verses: twice as the noun نَسْل and twice as the present-tense verb يَنْسِلُونَ.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: two nouns and two verbs

These forms share a root but not the same direct meaning in the verses. نَسْل in Al-Baqarah 2:205 and Al-Sajdah 32:8 concerns offspring and continuity of descent. يَنْسِلُونَ in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:96 and Yā Sīn 36:51 expresses swift movement and coming forth from a place.

Important forms in this family include:

  • نَسْل: Offspring and descendants issuing from an earlier generation. Qur’anic: yes; Al-Baqarah 2:205 and Al-Sajdah 32:8.
  • نَسَلَ / يَنْسِلُ: To move quickly, emerge swiftly, or advance from a place toward a destination. Qur’anic: yes; يَنْسِلُونَ in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:96 and Yā Sīn 36:51.
  • نَسَلَ / يَنْسُلُ: To reproduce, bear offspring, or bring forth progeny; in some uses, to shed hair or feathers. Qur’anic: no, in this verbal use.
  • أَنْسَلَ / يُنْسِلُ: To bring forth offspring, cause reproduction, or in another use lose or shed hair and feathers. Qur’anic: no.
  • تَنَاسَلَ / تَنَاسُل: Successive reproduction, with later generations issuing from earlier ones. Qur’anic: no.
  • نَسِيلَة: An offspring or descendant; alternatively something separated from its source. Qur’anic: no.
  • أَنْسَال: Plural of nasl: offspring and generations. Qur’anic: no.
  • نَسُولَة: A female animal kept for reproduction and benefit from its offspring. Qur’anic: no.
  • نَسَلَان: Swift movement, especially the rapid continuous gait also described of wolves. Qur’anic: no, as an independent verbal noun.
  • نَسِيل / نُسَال / نُسَالَة: Hair, wool, or feathers shed from an animal’s body; some forms also have other uses. Qur’anic: no.
  • اِنْتَسَلَ: In some lexical uses, hair or wool separating from a body. Qur’anic: no.
  • اِسْتِنْسَال: Seeking or procuring offspring and reproduction, a form known in later usage. Qur’anic: no.

The two Qur’anic uses must remain distinct from the outset. Nasl names offspring, while يَنْسِلُونَ in its two verses does not name giving birth or reproduction.

Different medial vowels in some verbal forms also matter. Both يَنْسِلُ and يَنْسُلُ are recorded, but their semantic distinctions cannot all be reduced to an invariably fixed vowel difference. Usage and context also determine meaning.

2. The lexical core and physical image

Coming forth and separating from a source

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies one principle for N S L: drawing something out and its slipping out or separating from its original place.

Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs al-Lughah: drawing out and separation

This analysis provides an understandable connection between apparently different uses.

A child issues from an earlier generation. Hair or feathers separate from an animal’s body. A creature may also emerge from a place and move quickly toward a destination.

Each involves coming forth, separation, or movement from a prior condition.

The connection does not make every use identical.

A child’s birth, shedding feathers, and swift movement are different events. They may belong to this broad lexical field without one definition replacing another.

The Qur’an makes the two branches especially clear: Al-Sajdah 32:8 discusses offspring and reproduction, while Yā Sīn 36:51 describes swift movement after the trumpet is blown.

Nasl: the continuity of offspring

Arabic nasl denotes a child and descendants.

It can refer to the offspring of people or animals.

It is therefore not restricted to sons, males, or a particular branch of descendants.

It may denote immediate children or descendants who issue from their predecessors through successive generations.

A person’s child is their nasl, but later children and grandchildren may also fall within their progeny.

The important feature is reproduction and the continuity of living beings.

The word alone does not reveal every biological detail of inheritance, embryonic structure, or the transmission of physical traits.

Those are independent subjects requiring separate evidence.

Shedding hair and feathers

Early Arabic uses the same family for shedding bodily hair, wool, or birds’ feathers.

Forms of N S L describe old feathers falling or wool and hair separating from an animal’s body.

Coming forth and separating from a source are clear here.

This use nevertheless differs from offspring.

Hair falling from an animal does not constitute a new living being produced by reproduction.

The shared root therefore does not make hair or feathers the body’s children in a biological or Qur’anic sense.

It is one established extension within the lexical family.

Nasalān: swift movement

Arabic نَسَلَ الْمَاشِي describes someone moving quickly.

النَّسَلَان likewise denotes a manner of swift movement.

Some lexical accounts connect it with a wolf’s continuous, rapid gait as it moves out and advances.

The same verb was then also used of human movement.

The image connects this branch with emerging and moving from a previous position.

It does not mean that every ينسلون necessarily involves walking like a wolf.

The wolf’s gait illustrates the language’s possibilities rather than establishing precisely how people move in the two Qur’anic verses.

Nor can nasalān be generalized to every kind of running, jumping, or flying. The sentence and other contextual indications determine the movement.

Are reproduction and swift movement the same meaning?

No.

They can be connected within a field of emergence and separation from a source without becoming synonyms.

Someone running cannot thereby be said to be reproducing.

An animal giving birth likewise does not establish from the word alone that the birth happens rapidly.

A possible shared core must therefore be distinguished from each derivative’s established meaning.

This is essential when translating يَنْسِلُونَ: “they reproduce” or “they increase their offspring” does not fit the direct contextual evidence in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:96 and Yā Sīn 36:51.

3. Early Arabic witnesses: offspring, shedding bodily covering, and swift movement

Nasl in pastoral life

Early Arabic uses النَّسْل for human and animal offspring.

تَنَاسَلَ بَنُو فُلَانٍ describes a family or lineage whose children and later generations have come into being.

In pastoral usage, النَّسُولَة denotes a female animal kept for its offspring.

This use relates to herders’ economic and social life.

Someone keeping sheep or camels may value not only meat or milk but also the herd’s continuity through reproduction.

Nasl then denotes the offspring that make the herd’s continuation possible.

This background helps illuminate Al-Baqarah 2:205: destroying nasl can concern human and animal offspring and their continued life.

The word’s precise scope in that verse must nevertheless follow its context.

A wolf’s movement in an early verse

A verse transmitted in early Arabic linguistic and poetic sources reads:

عَسَلَانَ الذِّئْبِ أَمْسَى قَارِبًا
بَرَدَ اللَّيْلُ عَلَيْهِ فَنَسَلْ

ʿasalāna al-dhiʾbi amsā qāriban / barada al-laylu ʿalayhi fa-nasal

English translation

A movement like the wolf’s swift gait as it goes toward water in the evening; night grew cold upon it, and it hastened onward.

فَنَسَلْ concerns the wolf’s rapid movement.

Arabic عَسَلَان likewise denotes a swift, smooth movement, especially a wolf’s gait. The lexical explanation of this witness also connects قارِبًا with going toward water.

The two words together depict an animal continuing quickly along its way.

The witness establishes swift movement as a verbal sense of N S L, directly relevant to Qur’anic ينسلون.

Early sources differ on attribution: some assign the verse to Labīd, others to al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī, while some collections leave it unnamed.

A single poet therefore cannot be identified with certainty, although the verse is recorded as evidence of rapid movement.

Ibn Qutaybah, Gharīb al-Ḥadīth: qāriban and going toward water

Shedding hair in a verse attributed to Abū Dhuʾayb

Some sources attribute the following verse to Abū Dhuʾayb al-Hudhalī:

أَعَاشَنِي بَعْدَكَ وَادٍ مُبْقِلٌ
آكُلُ مِنْ حَوْذَانِهِ وَأَنْسِلُ

aʿāshanī baʿdaka wādin mubqilun / ākulu min ḥawdhānihi wa-ansilu

English translation

A green valley sustained me after you; I eat its ḥawdhān plants and, according to the explanation of this reading, grow so stout that my bodily hair falls.

The verse is cited in explanations of shedding hair and feathers.

Several points matter.

First, the verb’s vocalization varies.

For وَأَنْسِلُ, Al-Muḥkam and Lisān al-ʿArab explain: I grew stout until my hair fell. With ḍammah on the hamzah, وَأُنْسِلُ is explained through the reproduction of the speaker’s camels and sheep. The sources do not transmit these readings and explanations uniformly.

Al-Muḥkam and Lisān al-ʿArab: ansilu and unsilu readings

Second, attribution varies. Lisān al-ʿArab under N S L names Abū Dhuʾayb, but Al-Muḥkam, the critical note at that same passage, and the B Q L entry attribute it to Ibn Abī Duʾād addressing his father. In the B Q L account, the father asks what sustained him; أعاشني is consequently a statement in reply, rather than a question.

Lisān al-ʿArab, B Q L: Ibn Abī Duʾād’s reply to his father

This witness is valuable for the range of recorded uses, but cannot establish one certain meaning or a pre-Qur’anic date.

The differences in reading and attribution themselves show that poetic evidence must remain within the limits permitted by reliable text.

What the linguistic witnesses establish

Offspring, shedding hair, and swift movement are all recorded in early Arabic sources.

The wolf verse offers the closest linguistic parallel to Qur’anic يَنْسِلُونَ.

Offspring and reproduction likewise support the nominal sense of نَسْل.

None of these should become another meaning without contextual examination.

4. نَسْل: offspring and the continuity of life in the Qur’an

Al-Baqarah 2:205: destroying cultivation and offspring

Qur’anic text

وَإِذَا تَوَلَّىٰ سَعَىٰ فِي الْأَرْضِ لِيُفْسِدَ فِيهَا وَيُهْلِكَ الْحَرْثَ وَالنَّسْلَ وَاللَّهُ لَا يُحِبُّ الْفَسَادَ

wa-idhā tawallā saʿā fī al-arḍi li-yufsida fīhā wa-yuhlika al-ḥartha wa-al-nasla wa-Allāhu lā yuḥibbu al-fasāda

English translation

When he turns away, he strives through the earth to spread corruption and destroy cultivation and offspring; The God does not love corruption.

Examining the usage

النَّسْلَ is a definite accusative noun.

It occurs alongside الْحَرْثَ, both objects of يُهْلِكَ.

The verse thus places cultivation and offspring together.

Ḥarth belongs to cultivation and agriculture.

Nasl concerns offspring and living beings’ continuing descent.

They are linguistically distinct. Cultivation may be destroyed without every living creature perishing; offspring may be harmed without every field being ruined.

In actual life, however, they depend on one another.

Destroyed crops can remove human and animal food. Harm to reproduction and continuing descent can likewise threaten future human and animal life.

Their juxtaposition therefore portrays damage to life’s foundations.

Every possible consequence should nevertheless not be added to translation as a word’s direct meaning.

النسل denotes offspring and progeny; the social and environmental consequences of destroying them belong to reflection.

Does al-nasl mean only human children?

Arabic uses nasl of both people and animals.

Restricting its lexical meaning to human children therefore requires a specific indication.

Al-Baqarah 2:205 concerns cultivation, offspring, and corruption on earth.

This context can encompass a broad field of life and continuing descent.

Destroying livestock and their reproductive capacity, for example, may matter to agricultural and social life as much as harm to human families and children.

A broad reading of human and animal offspring is therefore defensible within the word’s lexical capacity.

The verse itself nevertheless does not individually name every kind of plant, animal, human, or reproductive process.

Nasl is a general designation whose referential scope must be assessed through context.

Does nasl refer only to offspring already present?

It can refer to existing offspring while also relating to continued reproduction.

Destroying an animal’s progeny may involve eliminating those already born or disrupting the possibility of later generations.

These consequences are connected but are different events.

يُهْلِكَ expresses destruction without specifying its precise method here.

The verse should therefore neither be restricted to directly killing children nor converted without evidence solely into preventing reproduction.

The principal field is harming and destroying progeny.

Offspring and corruption on earth

The verse places destroyed cultivation and offspring within لِيُفْسِدَ فِيهَا.

Corruption on earth thus connects with destroying life’s foundations.

This may matter when examining responsibility for natural resources, food production, social health, and future generations’ continued life.

Every contemporary application must nevertheless be carefully distinguished from the direct meaning.

Severe water pollution or destroyed animal habitats may, for example, harm continuing descent. Such examples help explain real consequences, but the verse itself names no particular industrial or environmental phenomenon.

A contemporary scientific or political issue should therefore not become the verse’s only application without evidence.

Lexical conclusion: النسل in Al-Baqarah 2:205 concerns offspring and continued descent. Its scope can include people and animals, while يهلك and the context of corruption establish the kind and extent of harm.

Al-Sajdah 32:8: human offspring and the source of reproduction

Qur’anic text

ثُمَّ جَعَلَ نَسْلَهُ مِنْ سُلَالَةٍ مِنْ مَاءٍ مَهِينٍ

thumma jaʿala naslahu min sulālatin min māʾin mahīnin

English translation

Then He made his progeny from an extract of a lowly fluid.

Examining the usage

نَسْلَهُ combines the noun nasl with a third-person singular pronoun.

Here, progeny relates to human creation.

The preceding verse identifies the pronoun’s referent.

Al-Sajdah 32:7 reads:

الَّذِي أَحْسَنَ كُلَّ شَيْءٍ خَلَقَهُ وَبَدَأَ خَلْقَ الْإِنْسَانِ مِنْ طِينٍ

alladhī aḥsana kulla shayʾin khalaqahu wa-badaʾa khalqa al-insāni min ṭīnin

English translation

The One who made well everything He created and began human creation from clay.

نَسْلَهُ in the next verse consequently relates to the human being just mentioned.

It concerns human offspring and the continued coming into being of people.

This differs importantly from Al-Baqarah 2:205.

There, offspring appears alongside cultivation and corruption on earth.

In Al-Sajdah, progeny belongs to the context of human creation and the source of reproduction.

The subject in Al-Sajdah is therefore specifically human.

The relationship between نَسْلَهُ and سُلَالَةٍ

Two related words appear together:

نَسْلَهُ and سُلَالَةٍ.

They do not, however, share a root.

Nasl belongs to N S L.

Sulālah belongs to S L L and concerns drawing out, extraction, or a portion taken from a substance.

The semantic and phonetic resemblance matters.

It nevertheless does not justify indexing sulālah under N S L.

Linguistically, the verse discusses human progeny and describes its source with سُلَالَةٍ مِنْ مَاءٍ مَهِينٍ.

Nasl concerns resulting offspring and their continuity; sulālah concerns what has been drawn out or separated.

Translation and analysis must preserve this distinction.

Does nasl here mean the fluid itself?

No.

The construction says:

جَعَلَ نَسْلَهُ مِنْ سُلَالَةٍ مِنْ مَاءٍ مَهِينٍ.

Progeny is something whose origin is described in relation to fluid.

نسله therefore cannot be translated as ماء or سلالة itself.

Nasl denotes offspring and continued descent.

The fluid and extract describe the source of that continuity.

The verse alone also does not explain every biological process of embryonic formation, the role of reproductive cells, or hereditary stages using contemporary scientific terminology.

Its consistency with biological knowledge may be researched, but mechanisms absent from the wording should not be added to direct translation.

Does the passage distinguish initial creation from continued descent?

Yes.

Al-Sajdah 32:7 describes the beginning of human creation.

Al-Sajdah 32:8 describes human progeny.

The distinction is present in the verses’ construction.

The first identifies initial creation with بَدَأَ خَلْقَ الْإِنْسَانِ, while the second concerns continued descent through ثُمَّ جَعَلَ نَسْلَهُ.

The Qur’an therefore expresses the beginning of human creation and the coming of later generations in separate statements.

The root nasl itself, however, cannot settle every detail of how initial creation occurred.

That requires an independent study of creation verses.

Lexical conclusion: نَسْلَهُ in Al-Sajdah 32:8 denotes human offspring and continuing descent. سلالة and ماء مهين identify its stated source, rather than supplying other meanings for nasl.

5. يَنْسِلُونَ: coming forth and moving swiftly

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:96: movement from every rise in the ground

Qur’anic text

حَتَّىٰ إِذَا فُتِحَتْ يَأْجُوجُ وَمَأْجُوجُ وَهُمْ مِنْ كُلِّ حَدَبٍ يَنْسِلُونَ

ḥattā idhā futiḥat yaʾjūju wa-maʾjūju wa-hum min kulli ḥadabin yansilūn

English translation

Until Gog and Magog are opened, and they come forth swiftly from every rise in the ground.

Examining the usage

يَنْسِلُونَ is a third-person masculine plural present-tense Form I verb.

It expresses creatures moving from كُلِّ حَدَبٍ.

Arabic ḥadab concerns elevated or raised ground.

The wording therefore portrays swift movement from different elevations and rises.

ينسلون does not mean reproducing here.

If translated “they reproduce,” its connection with مِنْ كُلِّ حَدَبٍ would be unclear.

Lexical evidence for rapid movement also supports coming forth or streaming swiftly.

Does the verb mean descending from a mountain?

Some translations explain ينسلون through descending from high ground.

This may follow from the verb combined with مِنْ كُلِّ حَدَبٍ, since movement from raised ground may involve descending.

The verb alone, however, does not mean going downward.

Its lexical contribution is swift movement.

The source of movement comes from مِنْ and حَدَب.

“They come forth swiftly from every rise in the ground” is therefore a suitable direct translation.

It retains the starting point without narrowing the direction or terrain beyond the wording.

Gog and Magog and the opening

The verse contains فُتِحَتْ يَأْجُوجُ وَمَأْجُوجُ.

This connects with Gog and Magog’s account in Al-Kahf 18:94, Al-Kahf 18:95, Al-Kahf 18:96, Al-Kahf 18:97, Al-Kahf 18:98, and Al-Kahf 18:99, which discusses the barrier and the Lord’s promise.

ينسلون itself does not mean breaking a barrier or opening an obstacle.

Opening comes from F T Ḥ.

ينسلون describes the swift movement following that condition.

The verb alone also does not determine Gog and Magog’s historical identity, population, geographical location, or precise time of emergence.

Those are separate questions requiring the related verses as a whole.

Does ينسلون describe rapid reproduction among Gog and Magog?

This reading lacks sufficient lexical support for the verse.

The noun nasl does concern offspring, but the verb here occurs with movement from every rise.

Arabic linguistic evidence likewise supports moving swiftly.

Sharing a root therefore cannot turn swift movement into rapid reproduction.

A claim that the verse concerns Gog and Magog’s population increasing requires independent linguistic and contextual evidence.

مِنْ كُلِّ حَدَبٍ يَنْسِلُونَ primarily depicts movement from different places.

The approaching promise

The next verse, Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:97, mentions the true promise drawing near.

The movement therefore belongs to a great event and the divine promise’s approach.

The verb alone does not provide the full sequence of resurrection or preceding events.

It expresses swift movement; the accompanying verses establish its timing and place within the events.

Yā Sīn 36:51: hastening from the graves toward their Lord

Qur’anic text

وَنُفِخَ فِي الصُّورِ فَإِذَا هُمْ مِنَ الْأَجْدَاثِ إِلَىٰ رَبِّهِمْ يَنْسِلُونَ

wa-nufikha fī al-ṣūri fa-idhā hum mina al-ajdāthi ilā rabbihim yansilūna

English translation

The trumpet is blown; then suddenly they come forth swiftly from the graves toward their Lord.

Examining the usage

يَنْسِلُونَ appears in exactly the same form as in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:96.

The context, however, differs.

Yā Sīn 36:51 first mentions the trumpet being blown.

People then emerge from الْأَجْدَاثِ.

Ajdāth is the plural of jadath, meaning graves.

إِلَىٰ رَبِّهِمْ gives the ultimate direction of movement.

The construction therefore has three important elements: starting point, direction, and speed.

The starting point is the graves.

The direction is toward their Lord.

ينسلون expresses swift movement.

Keeping these elements distinct is essential in translation.

Does ينسلون itself mean the dead returning to life?

No.

The context presents resurrection and coming forth from graves.

The verb itself does not mean bringing to life.

It emphasizes moving or emerging swiftly.

“They come to life” would therefore remove the image of swift movement and should not replace it.

Nor does ينسلون mean the trumpet being blown; نُفِخَ expresses that separately.

The verse places related actions and conditions together, each retaining its linguistic meaning.

Does إِلَىٰ رَبِّهِمْ describe movement toward God’s bodily location?

The phrase itself does not establish that conclusion.

Qur’anic return and gathering toward God repeatedly relate to reckoning, judgment, and human outcomes.

The movement’s ultimate direction should therefore be examined within the wider resurrection verses.

The preposition إلى does not provide a bodily map of God’s location in space.

Conversely, that does not justify reducing the people’s movement and emergence to a purely inward metaphor.

The verse itself describes graves and swift emergence.

The relationship with gathering

Other verses discuss people being gathered after resurrection.

Ḥashr belongs to Ḥ SH R and concerns gathering.

ينسلون belongs to N S L and highlights swift movement here.

The concepts may relate within one great event without being lexically identical.

People may emerge swiftly from their graves and then be gathered together.

The precise order of every stage of the Resurrection, however, should not be established solely from the roots’ difference.

Comparing the two occurrences of ينسلون

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:96 describes movement from every rise in the ground.

Yā Sīn 36:51 describes movement from graves toward the Lord.

Both use a masculine plural verb expressing swift movement.

Their subjects, starting points, and contexts differ.

Gog and Magog appear in the sentence in Al-Anbiyāʾ.

Yā Sīn concerns people emerging from graves after the trumpet is blown.

The shared verb therefore does not establish that the events are identical.

Lexical conclusion: يَنْسِلُونَ in both verses denotes moving or coming forth swiftly. Reproduction and continuing descent are not the verb’s direct meanings in these constructions.

6. Morphology, related roots, and translation proposals

The difference between نَسْل and يَنْسِلُونَ

نَسْل is a noun denoting offspring in its Qur’anic uses.

يَنْسِلُونَ is a present-tense verb expressing swift movement in two verses.

Morphology and context establish this distinction.

The noun can denote human or animal offspring, whereas the movement verb in the two verses does not necessarily concern children or reproduction.

Words sharing a root should therefore not all receive a single fixed translation.

N S L compared with S L L

Study of S L L

S L L concerns drawing out, separating, and extracting something from among or within other things.

Early lexical analysis likewise connects N S L with emergence and separation from a source.

The semantic proximity matters, but the roots remain independent.

نسله and سلالة occur together in Al-Sajdah 32:8.

The former denotes offspring and the latter what is drawn out or extracted.

Neither can therefore be treated as a derivative of the other.

اِنْسَلَّ, meaning to slip out or separate, also belongs to S L L; phonetic resemblance must not place it under N S L.

N S L compared with W L D

Study of W L D

W L D concerns being born, giving birth, and a child.

Nasl likewise belongs to the field of offspring.

W L D may emphasize a child or the event of birth, while nasl may denote the body of descendants and their continuity across generations.

This distinction is not absolute: some Arabic uses of nasl can denote a single child.

It should therefore not be said to refer invariably to a very large body of descendants.

N S L compared with DH R R

Study of DH R R

The Qur’an uses dhurriyyah for children and descendants.

Nasl may have a close meaning.

Dhurriyyah covers a wide range of descendant and lineage relationships and appears in many verses.

Nasl, by contrast, occurs only twice as the noun for offspring.

Frequency alone does not establish a categorical semantic distinction.

Nor should dhurriyyah always be restricted to sons or immediate children.

N S L compared with N S B

Study of N S B

Nasab concerns lineage and kinship relationships.

Nasl denotes offspring and continuing descent.

They are close but different concepts.

People may belong to a lineage through reproduction, while nasab emphasizes the relationship connecting them.

Al-Furqān 25:54 includes نَسَبًا وَصِهْرًا.

That passage concerns N S B, rather than N S L.

It must therefore not be counted as a fifth occurrence of N S L.

N S L compared with KH L F

Study of KH L F

Some Arabic and Qur’anic uses of KH L F concern succession and one group following another.

Nasl can likewise relate to children coming after parents.

Not every succession, however, constitutes biological descent.

Someone may succeed another in office or responsibility without being that person’s child or offspring.

Succession and reproduction therefore require separate examination.

N S L compared with S R ʿ

Study of S R ʿ

S R ʿ concerns speed in movement or action.

N S L in ينسلون likewise expresses swift movement.

Speed has a wider field, including reckoning, hastening to good deeds, and performing actions.

ينسلون in its two verses, by contrast, depicts swift movement and emergence.

The roots are close in some uses without becoming complete synonyms.

N S L compared with KH R J

Study of KH R J

Khurūj concerns emerging from a place or condition.

ينسلون likewise concerns emergence and movement in its two verses.

Emergence alone, however, does not specify speed.

Someone may leave a place slowly or quickly.

Both Qur’anic occurrences of ينسلون highlight rapid movement.

“They come forth” therefore conveys part of the meaning, while “they come forth swiftly” is fuller.

N S L compared with Ḥ SH R

Study of Ḥ SH R

Ḥashr concerns gathering and, in many uses, driving toward a shared place.

ينسلون expresses swift movement.

Swift emergence and people being gathered may belong to the same great resurrection event.

Gathering itself, however, is not identical with swift movement, nor does ينسلون mean assembling everyone in one place.

Translation proposals for Forqan

In Persian, النَّسْلَ in Al-Baqarah 2:205 is best retained as nasl; “offspring” is appropriate in English.

The Persian equivalent is natural and does not restrict the word to human children or a particular animal.

نَسْلَهُ in Al-Sajdah 32:8 is close to “his progeny.”

“His children” is understandable, but “his progeny” more clearly preserves the connection and continuity of descendants.

For يَنْسِلُونَ in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:96, the principal proposal is:

“And they come forth swiftly from every rise in the ground.”

For Yā Sīn 36:51:

“Then suddenly they come forth swiftly from the graves toward their Lord.”

Both preserve rapid movement without introducing reproduction or population growth.

Reflection should also distinguish the movement’s starting points in the two verses.

The governing translation principle is to preserve nasl in the field of offspring and ينسلون in the field of swift movement, giving each its appropriate meaning.

7. Final synthesis

Arabic N S L concerns emergence, separation, and something issuing from its source.

Early uses extend this field to human and animal offspring, shedding hair and feathers, and swift movement.

The uses have an understandable connection but concern different events.

The Qur’an uses the noun nasl twice.

Al-Baqarah 2:205 places offspring alongside cultivation and corruption on earth, condemning its destruction.

Al-Sajdah 32:8 concerns human progeny and their continued creation.

Both denote offspring themselves rather than speed of movement.

The verb ينسلون also occurs twice.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:96 depicts swift movement from every rise in the ground in the context of Gog and Magog.

In Yā Sīn 36:51, people come swiftly from graves toward their Lord after the trumpet is blown.

Established early Arabic evidence supports swift movement in both.

The common root must therefore not cause ينسلون to be translated as reproducing or multiplying offspring.

The relationship between nasl and sulālah in Al-Sajdah 32:8 also matters, but the roots are independent.

Final definition: The broad lexical field of N S L concerns something emerging and separating from its source. In Qur’anic nominal use, نَسْل denotes offspring and continuing descent; the verb يَنْسِلُونَ expresses swift movement and emergence from a specified place.

For each use, establish whether the subject is offspring coming into being or already existing creatures moving.

Also ask where the emergence begins, whether a destination is stated, and which feature of movement or coming into being the sentence highlights.

Al-Baqarah and Al-Sajdah concern continued descent; Al-Anbiyāʾ and Yā Sīn concern swift movement.

The family’s shared core may be sought in emergence and separation, while the word’s form and Qur’anic context determine each precise meaning.
