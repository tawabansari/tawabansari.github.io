Wool and woolly coverings: from sheep’s wool and its everyday uses to early expressions and the term Sufi

1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

Arabic Ṣ W F principally concerns wool: a fibrous covering growing on certain animals, particularly sheep, which people use for clothing, furnishings, covers, and other articles.

Ṣūf names this covering, with aṣwāf as its plural. Other recorded forms concern abundant wool, a tuft of wool, wool-like coverings, and certain animal characteristics.

Ṣ W F occurs only once in the Qur’an, in Al-Naḥl 16:80:

أَصْوَافِهَا (aṣwāfihā): their wool; the wool of the livestock discussed in the verse.

Important Arabic forms include:

  • صُوف (ṣūf): wool, particularly sheep’s wool.
  • أَصْوَاف (aṣwāf): wool in the plural; the root’s only Qur’anic form.
  • صُوفَة (ṣūfah): a tuft or mass of wool.
  • صُوَيْفَة (ṣuwayfah): the diminutive of ṣūfah.
  • أَصْوَف (aṣwaf): having abundant wool.
  • صَوِف، صَاف، صَائِف (ṣawif, ṣāf, ṣāʾif): in certain early uses, an animal with abundant wool.
  • صُوفَان، صُوفَانَة (ṣūfān, ṣūfānah): forms for a heavily woolled animal; ṣūfānah also named a plant with a downy covering.
  • صَافَ / يَصُوفُ (ṣāfa / yaṣūfu): concerning a sheep, became woolly or heavily woolled.
  • صَوَّفَ (ṣawwafa): in botanical use, developed a downy covering; later usage also acquired other meanings.
  • صُوفِيّ (ṣūfī): related to ṣūf; as a technical term, someone belonging to a Sufi path or tradition.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an. The Qur’anic focus is aṣwāfihā; the other forms help explain the family’s wider Arabic usage.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: one nominal occurrence in Al-Naḥl 16:80

2. The semantic core in early Arabic

Wool: the root’s principal meaning

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies a definite semantic principle:

الصَّادُ وَالْوَاوُ وَالْفَاءُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ صَحِيحٌ، وَهُوَ الصُّوفُ الْمَعْرُوفُ

al-ṣādu wa-al-wāwu wa-al-fāʾu aṣlun wāḥidun ṣaḥīḥun wa-huwa al-ṣūfu al-maʿrūfu

Ṣ W F has one established principle: familiar wool.

This fits ordinary Arabic usage.

Wool is a natural covering composed of numerous fibers. After shearing, cleaning, carding, spinning, or felting, these fibers can be used together to make various articles.

Historically, wool was an important material for clothing and everyday equipment: people could make yarn, fabric, covers, and floor coverings from it.

Spinnability or weavability is nevertheless not part of every ṣūf occurrence’s lexical definition. Wool may still be on an animal, recently shorn, or incorporated into a manufactured article.

The raw material must therefore be distinguished from what is made from it when translating the root.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: wool as the semantic principle

Distinguishing wool, hair, and camel hair

Ṣūf, wabar, and shaʿr all concern animals’ fibrous body coverings, but their established uses differ.

Ibn Sīdah explains ṣūf for sheep, shaʿr for goats, and wabar for camels.

This distinction was established particularly in Arabic pastoral vocabulary.

Sheep’s wool commonly has crimped, interlocking fibers suitable for spinning, weaving, and felting.

Camel hair is also a fibrous material used in covers and textiles.

Goat hair, particularly its longer fibers, has been used for durable coverings, ropes, and other woven articles.

This classification should not become an absolute claim about every animal. Arabic also uses ṣūf for similar animals’ coverings, while different animals’ fibers vary in softness, length, and structure.

The three words thus have a real distinction in established usage, without every animal fiber invariably belonging exclusively to one label.

Lisān al-ʿArab: ṣūf, wabar, and shaʿr in pastoral usage

Ṣūfah: a tuft or mass of wool

صُوفَة (ṣūfah) denotes a tuft or mass of wool.

It may designate a particular quantity separated from a larger amount or gathered into a clump.

Lexical sources also record صُوَيْفَة (ṣuwayfah) as its diminutive.

Ṣūf can thus name the material generically, while ṣūfah denotes a portion of it.

Someone might, for example, take a tuft from the wool they possess.

Qur’anic aṣwāfihā does not, however, necessarily mean separated tufts ready for spinning. The verse concerns livestock’s wool; the word itself does not specify its processing stage.

A heavily woolled sheep

كَبْشٌ أَصْوَفُ (kabshun aṣwafu) describes a ram with abundant wool.

Ṣawif, ṣāf, and ṣāʾif are also recorded in this field.

Lexical sources use ṣāfa or ṣawifa for a sheep becoming more woolly.

The word emphasizes the animal’s covering: its wool’s amount or condition.

Abundant wool alone does not signify a particular breed, greater health, or a definite economic value.

Animals’ wool may differ in kind, quality, and use. Aṣwaf lexically concerns abundant wool rather than every characteristic of the animal.

Lisān al-ʿArab: ṣūfah, ṣuwayfah, and abundant-wool descriptions

3. Early Arabic witnesses and broader uses

Wool and camel hair in a poetic witness

Lisān al-ʿArab quotes a poetic fragment:

حَلْبَانَةٍ رَكْبَانَةٍ صَفُوفِ
تَخْلِطُ بَيْنَ وَبَرٍ وَصُوفِ

ḥalbānatin rakbānatin ṣafūfi / takhliṭu bayna wabarin wa-ṣūfi

A rendering close to the wording:

“[An animal] yielding milk and bearing riders … mixing camel hair and wool.”

Ibn al-Aʿrābī and Al-Aṣmaʿī offer different explanations of the witness.

Ibn al-Aʿrābī understands the animal as being sold, with sheep and camels purchased from its price. Mixing wabar and ṣūf then concerns trade and converting assets.

Al-Aṣmaʿī instead relates it to the animal’s rapid movement, comparing its forelegs’ motion with a carder’s bow that sets fibers moving during carding.

The expression may thus evoke working with wool and camel hair or their economic relationship, although the whole image’s precise meaning was disputed.

Wabar and ṣūf themselves remain clear, showing early Arabic’s different names for the materials.

The poet and the fragment’s precise composition date are unclear in the transmission examined. It is a witness preserved in early lexical sources, but should not be declared certainly pre-Islamic without independent evidence.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Ibn al-Aʿrābī’s and Al-Aṣmaʿī’s interpretations of the witness

Wool in pastoral life

Wool was one benefit people could obtain from sheep without killing the animal to acquire it.

Shearing and preparing the fibers enabled clothing and other articles to be made.

Wool could also contribute to household livelihoods. A family might use it for its own needs or exchange woollen goods for other products.

Livestock’s usefulness was therefore not restricted to meat and milk.

Ṣūf itself nevertheless does not mean selling, trade, or ownership: these are activities that may involve wool.

The material must be distinguished from its economic and social uses when studying the root.

Ṣūfat al-qafā: wool-like hair at the nape

Early Arabic also records صُوفَةُ القَفَا (ṣūfat al-qafā) for soft hair or down at the nape of the neck.

أَخَذَ بِصُوفَةِ قَفَاهُ (akhadha bi-ṣūfati qafāhu) means taking someone by the hair or back of the neck.

Lexical explanations sometimes extend the expression to catching and restraining someone.

Ṣūf could thus be used for a wool-like covering of hair.

That does not make sheep’s wool and human hair identical in every use.

Resemblance in texture and appearance provides the expression’s extension here.

Ṣūfān and a plant’s downy covering

صُوفَانَة (ṣūfānah) names a short plant with fine hairs in Arabic botanical sources.

Its covering’s resemblance to animal wool matters here.

The plant does not produce sheep’s wool: its surface has a covering visually resembling it.

Modern Arabic sources also use ṣūf al-baḥr for certain materials or organisms with a wool-like appearance in the sea.

A word can thus extend from a familiar material to things resembling it in shape or texture.

Visual resemblance does not establish identical biological or chemical natures.

Arabic lexicons: the nape expression, ṣūfānah, and wool-like uses

4. Ṣūfah in the history of Arabian pilgrimage rites

Historical and lexical sources identify Ṣūfah as a group connected with particular pilgrimage duties and pilgrims’ movement before Islam.

Some reports associate the name with an Arabian family or tribal group whose permission governed pilgrims’ departure from certain ritual stations.

A verse attributed to Aws ibn Maghrā al-Saʿdī states:

وَلَا يَرِيمُونَ فِي التَّعْرِيفِ مَوْقِفَهُمْ
حَتَّى يُقَالَ أَجِيزُوا آلَ صُوفَانَا

wa-lā yarīmūna fī al-taʿrīfi mawqifahum / ḥattā yuqāla ajīzū āla ṣūfānā

Translation:

“They do not leave their station at ʿArafāt until it is said: Family of Ṣūfān, permit the departure.”

The witness concerns a group called Ṣūfah or Ṣūfān and its place in historical pilgrimage rites.

Lexical sources report differing explanations of the name’s origin and tribal affiliation.

Abū ʿUbaydah transmits an explanation involving people gathered from different tribes, likening their gathering to wool’s interlocking fibers.

That explanation alone does not establish the name’s definite historical origin.

A family or group’s name may be indexed under a lexical entry without the original reason for its naming being clearly known.

Ṣūfah’s presence in Arabian ritual history matters, but should not be treated as aṣwāfihā’s principal meaning in Al-Naḥl 16:80.

Nor does a historical group’s name establish that the Qur’an grants it religious authority or a permanent right to administer rites. That requires a separate examination of history and the pilgrimage verses.

5. The Qur’anic use: wa-min aṣwāfihā

Ṣ W F’s sole Qur’anic occurrence is in Al-Naḥl:

وَاللَّهُ جَعَلَ لَكُمْ مِنْ بُيُوتِكُمْ سَكَنًا وَجَعَلَ لَكُمْ مِنْ جُلُودِ الْأَنْعَامِ بُيُوتًا تَسْتَخِفُّونَهَا يَوْمَ ظَعْنِكُمْ وَيَوْمَ إِقَامَتِكُمْ ۙ وَمِنْ أَصْوَافِهَا وَأَوْبَارِهَا وَأَشْعَارِهَا أَثَاثًا وَمَتَاعًا إِلَىٰ حِينٍ

wa-allāhu jaʿala lakum min buyūtikum sakanan wa-jaʿala lakum min julūdi al-anʿāmi buyūtan tastakhiffūnahā yawma ẓaʿnikum wa-yawma iqāmatikum wa-min aṣwāfihā wa-awbārihā wa-ashʿārihā athāthan wa-matāʿan ilā ḥīnin

“And The God has made your houses a place of rest for you, and made houses from livestock skins that you find light on the day of departure and the day of settling; and from their wool, camel hair, and hair, furnishings and enjoyment for a time.”

Al-Naḥl 16:80

The meaning of aṣwāfihā

Aṣwāf is the plural of ṣūf, and hā refers to al-anʿām.

Aṣwāfihā therefore denotes the wool of the livestock discussed in the verse.

Established Arabic usage particularly associates wool with sheep.

The pronoun nevertheless refers to livestock collectively; sheep are not separately named in this sentence.

This matters for translation.

“Their wool” directly renders the word; “sheep’s wool” explains it through ṣūf’s established pastoral usage.

The explanation is appropriate in lexical research, but the basic translation should preferably preserve the verse’s pronoun structure.

Wool, camel hair, and hair in one expression

The verse gives three separate names:

أَصْوَافِهَا، أَوْبَارِهَا، أَشْعَارِهَا (aṣwāfihā, awbārihā, ashʿārihā).

Established Arabic usage connects aṣwāf with sheep’s wool, awbār with camel hair, and ashʿār with goats’ hair.

The distinction relates to pastoral life and knowledge of different textile materials.

Each fiber could have different characteristics and uses.

The words’ distinction should not become a claim of completely nonoverlapping natural categories. Biologically, all concern animals’ fibrous body coverings, while Arabic names reflect the animal species and its covering’s established features.

Preserving “wool, camel hair, and hair” therefore better respects the three names than calling all three simply “wool.”

Athāthan wa-matāʿan

The expression continues:

أَثَاثًا وَمَتَاعًا إِلَىٰ حِينٍ

athāthan wa-matāʿan ilā ḥīnin

“Furnishings and enjoyment for a time.”

Arabic athāth concerns everyday equipment and possessions. Early sources include floor coverings, covers, clothing, and household goods among its instances.

Matāʿ concerns what people benefit from or enjoy.

Wool and similar materials are therefore not presented only as animals’ body coverings: people can use them to make things needed for life.

Wool can, for example, become yarn, fabric, clothing, floor coverings, blankets, and other covers.

Those products are not ṣūf’s own meaning.

Ṣūf names the material; athāth concerns resulting equipment and possessions; matāʿ concerns benefit and enjoyment.

Aṣwāfihā should therefore not be translated directly as clothes or carpets instead of wool.

Study of ʾ TH TH

Is wool only for clothing?

Al-Naḥl presents wool within a broader range of everyday provisions.

The verse describes fixed houses, portable houses, livestock skins, and then wool, camel hair, and hair.

People use varied materials to make dwellings and everyday equipment.

Restricting aṣwāfihā to clothing is therefore insufficient.

Wool can warm the body, provide bedding, cover possessions, and make various textiles.

Some environments also use wool and similar fibers for tent coverings and shelters.

Not all the verse’s portable houses should, however, be assumed to be made exclusively of wool: the preceding phrase expressly mentions julūd al-anʿām, while other materials could also serve shelter construction.

The verse concerns the variety of provisions people obtain from livestock.

Ilā ḥīnin: enjoyment for a time

The verse ends:

إِلَىٰ حِينٍ

ilā ḥīnin

“For a time.”

The expression indicates a limited period of enjoyment.

Early explanations relate it sometimes to the end of human life and sometimes to the articles’ duration of use and durability.

Ḥīn itself does not specify a numerical duration.

Without evidence, it should therefore not be restricted exclusively to human lifespan or exclusively to the time before fabric and wool wear out.

The clear meaning is that these provisions serve human life, with their enjoyment bounded in time.

Ṣ W F itself contributes nothing to that time limit: wool names the material, while another phrase states the duration of benefit.

6. Wool among the verses on livestock’s benefits

Al-Naḥl 16:5 states:

وَالْأَنْعَامَ خَلَقَهَا لَكُمْ فِيهَا دِفْءٌ وَمَنَافِعُ وَمِنْهَا تَأْكُلُونَ

wa-al-anʿāma khalaqahā lakum fīhā difʾun wa-manāfiʿu wa-minhā taʾkulūna

“He created livestock; in them you have warmth and benefits, and from them you eat.”

The verse describes livestock’s warmth, benefits, and food.

Wool can provide warm clothing and coverings. There is thus a clear relationship between difʾ in Al-Naḥl 16:5 and aṣwāfihā in Al-Naḥl 16:80.

Difʾ in verse five does not alone name wool and should not be restricted to one pastoral product.

Study of D F ʾ

Al-Naḥl 16:81 also describes coverings and garments protecting people from heat and harm.

Together, these verses place livestock and natural materials within people’s actual needs.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:21 likewise identifies a lesson in livestock and their numerous benefits.

Wool is therefore one livestock benefit, without being the whole of their usefulness.

Nor does listing wool, camel hair, and hair in Al-Naḥl 16:80 mean that every human clothing or manufacturing material is restricted to these three.

The verse gives familiar examples of provisions and benefits. Cotton, linen, silk, and other materials are separate subjects; their absence from this list alone neither denies nor forbids their use.

7. Sufi and Sufism: post-Qur’anic terminology

Arabic uses “Sufi” as a designation for groups associated with asceticism and spiritual practice.

Historical and lexical sources offer differing accounts of the name’s origin.

One prominent explanation derives ṣūfī from ṣūf, because some groups of ascetics commonly wore woollen garments.

On this account, ṣūfī designated a wool-wearer, gradually becoming a religious and social title.

Other theories connect it with ṣafāʾ, ṣaff, the People of the Ṣuffah, or the historical group Ṣūfah.

Ordinary Arabic relational morphology more directly supports ṣūfī from ṣūf. Different forms would be expected for a derivation from ṣafāʾ or ṣuffah.

A plausible morphological analysis nevertheless does not by itself establish every historical stage in the development of taṣawwuf as a term.

Ṣūfī and taṣawwuf do not occur as religious or mystical titles in the Qur’anic text.

Aṣwāfihā in Al-Naḥl 16:80 concerns only livestock’s wool.

Mystical ideas, Sufi orders, asceticism, austerities, or stages of spiritual progress should therefore not be inserted into this word’s meaning in the verse.

A historical movement’s name possibly deriving from woollen clothing also does not by itself establish either the truth or falsehood of all its beliefs.

Assessing beliefs and practices is separate from word history.

8. Distinguishing related roots

Ṣ W F (ṣūf) and W B R (wabar)

Established Arabic usage connects ṣūf with sheep’s wool.

Wabar is mainly used for camel hair.

The materials are close in being fibrous and used for covers and textiles.

Al-Naḥl 16:80 names them separately.

The translation should therefore not remove the established Arabic distinction without reason.

Their different names nevertheless do not establish wholly separate natural characteristics and uses.

Ṣ W F (ṣūf) and SH ʿ R (shaʿr)

Arabic shaʿr concerns human and animal hair.

Ashʿārihā accompanies aṣwāfihā in Al-Naḥl 16:80 and, in pastoral usage, especially concerns goats’ hair.

“Hair” for shaʿr and “wool” for ṣūf are consequently natural equivalents.

SH ʿ R also has other Qur’anic uses, including awareness and perception.

Those should not be imposed on ashʿārihā in Al-Naḥl 16:80, just as wool does not mean awareness.

Study of SH ʿ R

Ṣ W F (ṣūf) and J L D (jild)

Jild concerns skin, while ṣūf names the woolly covering growing on it.

Al-Naḥl 16:80 first mentions livestock skins, then their wool, camel hair, and hair.

The order presents two ways of benefiting from livestock: using skin and using the fibers growing on their bodies.

The two are not entirely unrelated in actual life: wool grows on an animal’s skin, and both can supply everyday articles.

Skin and wool are nevertheless different materials and should not replace one another in translation.

Study of J L D

Ṣ W F (ṣūf) and Ṣ F W (ṣafw)

The roots contain similar letters, but in different orders.

Ṣ W F concerns wool.

Established Ṣ F W usage concerns clarity, purity, and choosing.

Graphic and phonetic resemblance does not establish a historical relationship.

Some theories connecting Sufi terminology with ṣafāʾ likewise do not change ṣūf’s lexical meaning.

Ṣūf in Al-Naḥl 16:80 means wool, rather than purity or inward spiritual clarity.

9. Translation suggestions for Forqan

صُوف (ṣūf): wool, particularly sheep’s woolly covering.

أَصْوَاف (aṣwāf): wool, in the plural.

أَصْوَافِهَا (aṣwāfihā): their wool; livestock’s wool in Al-Naḥl 16:80.

صُوفَة (ṣūfah): a tuft or mass of wool.

أَصْوَف (aṣwaf): having abundant wool.

صَوِف / صَاف (ṣawif / ṣāf): having abundant wool, according to form and usage.

صُوفَانَة (ṣūfānah): in botanical use, a plant with a downy covering.

صُوفِيّ (ṣūfī): in post-Qur’anic terminology, belonging to the Sufi tradition; in a prominent historical analysis, related to ṣūf.

A suitable rendering for Al-Naḥl 16:80 is:

وَمِنْ أَصْوَافِهَا وَأَوْبَارِهَا وَأَشْعَارِهَا

wa-min aṣwāfihā wa-awbārihā wa-ashʿārihā

“And from their wool, camel hair, and hair.”

This preserves the three names’ distinction and relates hā to the livestock collectively, following the Arabic structure.

A lexical note may explain that wool especially concerns sheep, camel hair concerns camels, and hair in this context concerns goats.

The animal names should preferably not replace the original pronoun in the basic translation.

Aṣwāfihā should also not be rendered as woollen garments or woollen carpets: the verse names the raw material, with manufactured products introduced afterward.

10. Conclusion

Arabic Ṣ W F concerns wool and woolly coverings. Ibn Fāris identifies one established semantic principle, explaining most forms in relation to wool.

Early Arabic uses ṣūf for wool, ṣūfah for a tuft, and aṣwaf for a heavily woolled animal.

Figurative uses also concern soft hair at the nape and certain plants’ downy coverings.

In Arabian ritual history, Ṣūfah names a group associated with pilgrims’ departure, though its exact naming origin is uncertain.

Later terminology uses Sufi for groups of ascetics and spiritual practitioners. The name’s relationship to woollen clothing is linguistically established and defensible, but Sufi terminology should not be inserted into Qur’anic wool’s meaning.

Only aṣwāfihā occurs in the Qur’an. In Al-Naḥl 16:80, it accompanies awbārihā and ashʿārihā, naming three fibrous materials used by people.

Wool here is a pastoral provision and material for furnishings and everyday equipment.

The root in this verse means neither asceticism, religious clothing, nor a particular kind of worship: it denotes actual livestock wool.

Brief root meaning for Forqan:

Wool, particularly sheep’s woolly covering; in other uses, a tuft of wool, abundant wool, or wool-like coverings.

Meaning in Al-Naḥl 16:80:

أَصْوَافِهَا (aṣwāfihā): livestock’s wool; a material used for clothing, floor coverings, covers, and other everyday equipment.
