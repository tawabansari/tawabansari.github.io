Core Qur’anic meaning: the shore or bank, where land meets a sea or river. Arabic also records other branches of S Ḥ L, including scraping and removing a surface, white cloth, single-strand thread, a donkey's braying and pouring something out. Lexicographers connect sāḥil with water wearing away the land, but its direct Qur’anic meaning is the water's edge.

1. Introduction and meaning in early Arabic

The Arabic root S Ḥ L has several semantic branches.

In one established branch, saḥala al-shayʾa means scraping, abrading or removing a surface.

Saḥala al-khashaba means “he scraped or shaved the wood.”

Saḥala al-ḥadīda denotes abrading or filing iron.

Saḥalati al-riyāḥu al-arḍa means that winds scoured the ground and removed part of its surface.

Al-sāḥil also denotes the shore: land adjoining water.

Lexical explanations connect this name with water's effect on land. Water reaching the bank can abrade, shift or wash away soil and rock.

That explanation illuminates only part of the family.

Arabic also records saḥīl for thread not plied with another strand and saḥl for white cloth.

Another usage of saḥīl denotes a donkey's voice.

Understanding the family requires examining early Arabic evidence and distinguishing these usages.

First pre-Islamic example: saḥīl and mubram in Zuhayr's Muʿallaqah

Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā, a pre-Islamic poet, says in his famous Muʿallaqah:

يَمِينًا لَنِعْمَ السَّيِّدَانِ وُجِدْتُمَا
عَلَى كُلِّ حَالٍ مِنْ سَحِيلٍ وَمُبْرَمِ

yamīnan la-niʿma al-sayyidāni wujidtumā / ʿalā kulli ḥālin min saḥīlin wa-mubrami

Translation:

“I swear: excellent lords you two have proved, in every circumstance, easy or difficult.”

Saḥīl and mubram stand in contrast here.

Applied to thread or cord, saḥīl denotes a single strand or one not firmly plied together with another.

Mubram, by contrast, describes thread or cord strengthened by twisting strands together.

The poem uses these images for easy and difficult circumstances.

Zuhayr praises two leaders who demonstrated merit and nobility in differing situations.

Saḥīl here does not mean a shore.

Nor does it mean scraping or filing an object.

It concerns the thread's construction and twisting.

The verse is transmitted in Zuhayr's Muʿallaqah and established commentaries, providing evidence for saḥīl in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

Second early example: white cloth in al-Mutanakhkhil al-Hudhalī's poetry

The early Arab poet al-Mutanakhkhil al-Hudhalī says:

كَالسُّحُلِ الْبِيضِ جَلَا لَوْنَهَا
سَحُّ نِجَاءِ الْحَمَلِ الْأَسْوَلِ

ka-al-suḥuli al-bīḍi jalā lawnahā / saḥḥu nijāʾi al-ḥamali al-aswali

Translation:

“Like white cloths whose color has been brightened by the abundant downpour of dark, trailing clouds.”

Al-suḥul is the plural of saḥl here.

In clothing terminology, saḥl denotes white cloth.

Some sources restrict it to white cotton cloth; others describe thin white cloth.

Saḥḥu in the second hemistich belongs to a different family, S Ḥ Ḥ, and refers to abundant rainfall.

It should therefore be distinguished from suḥul.

The poetic image draws on the whiteness of cloth and the cleansing, brightening effect of rain. The lexical point is suḥul as a name for white cloths.

The verse shows that saḥl also named a kind of fabric in early Arabic.

It does not make Qur’anic sāḥil mean white cloth or a place for washing fabric.

Belonging to one word family does not make the usages identical.

The historical meaning of sāḥil

Arabic sources record al-sāḥil for the shore and land adjoining water.

Lexicographers connect the name with saḥala, scraping and removing.

Rising and receding water can wash the bank and shift soil and rock.

Some therefore explain sāḥil as semantically close to masḥūl: something scoured or washed by water.

This is an etymological analysis.

This study presents no independent pre-Islamic poetic example of sāḥil itself. That does not establish the absence of such evidence elsewhere.

The Qur’an itself clearly attests the word in Arabic of the revelation period.

Its established meaning should therefore be distinguished from a proposed explanation of its naming.

Historical description of the family

Early poetry and attested classical usages distinguish several branches:

In the scraping branch, saḥl concerns abrading and removing a surface. In the textile and thread branch, saḥl and saḥīl concern white cloth or single-strand thread. In geography, sāḥil names the water's edge. Sound and pouring usages are also recorded.

These meanings should not be reduced to a single image without adequate historical evidence.

Only the geographical sāḥil occurs in the Qur’an.

2. Important forms and derivatives

Saḥala yasḥalu

The family's Form I verb.

Applied to material objects, it means scraping, abrading or removing a surface.

سَحَلَ الْخَشَبَ

saḥala al-khashaba

Meaning: “He shaved the wood.”

سَحَلَ الْحَدِيدَ

saḥala al-ḥadīda

Meaning: “He filed the iron.”

In these usages, abrasion or scraping removes part of an object's surface.

Saḥl

The verbal noun of scraping and removal in the material branch.

The same form denotes white cloth in another branch.

Lexicons describe this cloth as white, thin or made of cotton.

Those features should not be assigned to every usage of saḥl.

Saḥīl

For thread and cord, it denotes a single strand or one not firmly plied together with another.

Its contrast, mubram, denotes a firm cord made of strands twisted together.

Other usages apply saḥīl to fabric woven from such thread, although lexical authorities differ over applying the term to the cloth itself.

The form appears in Zuhayr's verse.

Suḥul, suḥūl and asḥāl

Recorded plurals of saḥl in the textile usage.

Suḥul occurs in al-Mutanakhkhil al-Hudhalī's poem.

These forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

Misḥal

In the tool usage, it denotes an implement for scraping or filing.

Al-misḥal can be a file or another tool for abrading wood, metal or other material.

Other lexical usages apply it to parts of a bridle, an orator's tongue and other things.

This diversity does not establish that all these meanings historically derive from one particular tool.

Suḥālah

Particles and filings that fall away during scraping or abrasion.

Filing metal, for example, removes small particles.

They can be called suḥālah.

In grain processing, the word also denotes husks and particles left after stripping or pounding grain.

The connection with scraping and separated particles is clear in this branch.

Insaḥala

In the scraping branch, it denotes becoming abraded or stripped.

اِنْسَحَلَ الشَّيْءُ

insaḥala al-shayʾu

Meaning: “The thing became abraded or scraped.”

This construction attributes the change to the object affected by the action.

Sāḥil

The family's noun in geographical usage.

It denotes the shore or bank adjoining water.

Its plural is sawāḥil.

This noun occurs in Ṭā Hā 20:39.

Although its shape follows fāʿil, sāḥil in established usage names a geographical location. It is not a regularly derived place noun on the mafʿal or mafʿil pattern.

It should therefore not be translated in the Qur’an as a person or thing actively engaged in scraping.

Sāḥala yusāḥilu

In geographical usage, going to the coast or following a coastal route.

سَاحَلَ الْقَوْمُ

sāḥala al-qawmu

Meaning: “The group went to the coast or took the coastal route.”

Movement to the coast is expressed by the verb and its construction.

The noun sāḥil itself does not mean travel or movement.

Saḥīl and suḥāl as sound words

In another branch, saḥīl and suḥāl denote a donkey's braying.

They describe the animal's characteristic sound.

Misḥal is also recorded as a designation for a wild ass.

This field differs from the shore and scraping usages.

Shared consonants alone do not establish that the name of the animal's sound historically developed from scraping or abrasion.

Other recorded usages

Arabic records saḥalati al-ʿaynu for shedding tears and saḥalati al-samāʾu for pouring rain.

Some financial usages connect saḥl with immediate cash payment.

These concern flowing, pouring or promptly carrying something out.

Their diversity does not justify inventing an all-encompassing meaning such as “everything moving.”

The form actually occurring in the Qur’an

The root S Ḥ L occurs only once in the Qur’an:

Bi-al-sāḥili in Ṭā Hā 20:39.

It combines the preposition bi with the definite noun al-sāḥil.

The verbs saḥala and sāḥala, and nouns such as saḥīl, misḥal and suḥālah, do not occur in the Qur’an.

The Qur’anic study of this family should therefore focus on sāḥil in Moses' story.

3. Sāḥil, scraping and erosion

One important lexical explanation connects sāḥil with water's effect on the land.

Saḥala can denote scraping and removing a surface.

Water moving against a bank can also shift soil and rock.

In some places, currents or waves gradually wear away land and alter the shoreline.

This offers an intelligible connection between saḥl and a shore environment.

Why do some lexicographers explain sāḥil as masḥūl?

Sāḥil has the shape of an active participle.

However, some historical explanations of the name relate its meaning to masḥūl.

That is, land affected by water scouring or washing its surface.

Water is the agent of erosion and the shore is its object in this analysis.

The semantic explanation is understandable.

But morphological shape and an etymological explanation must be distinguished.

In established usage, sāḥil names a geographical location; this does not make it a regularly derived place noun in technical morphology.

An Arabic speaker need not have scraping or erosion in mind whenever referring to a shore.

Were all shores formed by erosion?

The word sāḥil does not establish that conclusion.

Water margins have different forms.

Some change through erosion.

Others form through deposition and accumulated material.

A bank can also be rocky, sandy, muddy or vegetated.

The lexical explanation involving water scouring land should not become a universal theory of how all shores originated.

What matters in the established meaning is land adjoining water.

Erosion is one process that may occur there, rather than a requirement for every place called a shore.

4. Fa-l-yulqihi al-yammu bi-al-sāḥil: Moses reaches the bank

Ṭā Hā 20:39 says:

أَنِ اقْذِفِيهِ فِي التَّابُوتِ فَاقْذِفِيهِ فِي الْيَمِّ فَلْيُلْقِهِ الْيَمُّ بِالسَّاحِلِ يَأْخُذْهُ عَدُوٌّ لِي وَعَدُوٌّ لَهُ وَأَلْقَيْتُ عَلَيْكَ مَحَبَّةً مِنِّي وَلِتُصْنَعَ عَلَىٰ عَيْنِي

ani iqdhifīhi fī al-tābūti fa-iqdhifīhi fī al-yammi fa-l-yulqihi al-yammu bi-al-sāḥili yaʾkhudhhu ʿaduwwun lī wa-ʿaduwwun lahu wa-alqaytu ʿalayka maḥabbatan minnī wa-li-tuṣnaʿa ʿalā ʿaynī

Ṭā Hā 20:39

Translation:

“Put him in the chest and cast it into the river; let the river cast him onto the bank, where an enemy of Mine and his will take him. I cast love from Me upon you, so that you would be brought up under My eye.”

The verse recalls for Moses the revelation previously given to his mother.

That revelation includes instructions to place the child in a chest and entrust it to the water.

It then describes his reaching the bank and being taken by an enemy.

The direct meaning of al-sāḥil

Al-sāḥil here denotes land at the water's edge.

Bi-al-sāḥili specifies the location where he arrives or is cast ashore.

It neither means scraping nor names a device for transporting the child.

It identifies the place to which the water brings the chest or child.

Natural renderings include “onto the bank” or “at the water's edge.”

The noun supplies the location; the verb fa-l-yulqihi supplies the movement and casting.

How do al-yamm and al-sāḥil differ?

The verse contains two different words:

الْيَمِّ

al-yammi

Ṭā Hā 20:39

The body of water; the river in this story.

السَّاحِلِ

al-sāḥili

Ṭā Hā 20:39

The shore or bank.

Al-yamm denotes the body of water.

Al-sāḥil is the adjoining land at its edge.

They are semantically related but do not refer to the same thing.

The water carries the chest in Moses' story.

The bank is where movement through water meets arrival at land.

This distinction explains the use of two separate words for water and its margin.

Must a sāḥil border a saltwater sea?

Arabic lexicons often explain al-sāḥil as shāṭiʾ al-baḥr, the shore of the sea.

In Ṭā Hā 20:39, however, the story is understood as involving a river; the word sāḥil itself does not specify its geographical identity.

Forqan's current translation also renders al-yamm here as “river.”

The sāḥil in this verse should therefore not be restricted to the coast of a saltwater sea or ocean.

The bank of a great river can fall within the same spatial usage.

The relevant elements are adjoining water and land, and the chest reaching the bank.

Sāḥil alone does not determine salinity, the bank's precise form or the size of the body of water.

Does the verse identify the exact shore?

Ṭā Hā 20:39 gives no geographical name for it.

The verse names no town and gives no distance traveled by the chest.

Sāḥil does not establish that it landed at a particular river point, near a palace or beside a specific building.

Any such details in a historical reconstruction require independent evidence.

The Qur’anic account here focuses on the child reaching the bank and passing into an enemy's hands.

The pronoun in fa-l-yulqihi

The suffix -hi is masculine singular.

Both Moses and the chest are grammatically compatible with it in context.

The chest carries Moses, so its arrival and the child's arrival inside it are not entirely separate events.

The pronoun alone does not describe every detail of how the chest moved or how the child emerged from it.

Yaʾkhudhhu likewise concerns taking Moses, whose fate is the narrative's subject.

Pronoun reference must therefore be assessed through syntax and narrative continuity.

Does the shore save him?

Lexically, sāḥil names a place; it does not itself mean saving.

Reaching the bank is nevertheless part of the child's preservation in Moses' story.

His mother places him in the chest, which is entrusted to the water.

He then reaches the bank and is taken by people who play an important role later in his life.

The shore is thus one stage in the course of events.

Rescue and divine protection follow from the whole story, rather than the root S Ḥ L.

5. Connections with other verses about Moses

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:7 presents the revelation to Moses' mother in different wording.

It instructs her to nurse the child and cast him into the water when she fears for him.

It also assures her not to fear or grieve and promises his return.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:8 describes Pharaoh's household taking him up.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:9 gives Pharaoh's wife's words concerning the child.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:11, his mother asks his sister to follow him.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:13 describes his return to his mother.

These verses present the story from another perspective.

Different emphases in the two surahs

Ṭā Hā 20:39 explicitly mentions reaching the bank.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:7 and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:8 focus on entrusting the child to the water and his being taken up by Pharaoh's household.

The passages are related in subject but do not repeat identical details.

This matters lexically: a word found in one account should not be presented as the actual wording of the other.

Sāḥil occurs in Ṭā Hā 20:39.

The cited verses in Al-Qaṣaṣ describe this stage of the story with other words.

Can Moses' landing place be identified?

Together, these verses show that Moses passed into Pharaoh's household after being entrusted to the water.

They do not specify the exact landing point or distance traveled.

Ṭā Hā 20:39 describes the taker as “عَدُوٌّ لِي وَعَدُوٌّ لَهُ”: an enemy of Mine and his.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:8 mentions Pharaoh's household.

Reading them together establishes their narrative relationship, but not every detail of the location or the identity of the first finder without more specific evidence.

Those questions concern historical reconstruction.

Al-sāḥil still means the water's edge.

6. Distinctions from nearby roots

S Ḥ L (sāḥil) and SH Ṭ ʾ (shāṭiʾ)

Shāṭiʾ also denotes an edge or side, including the bank of water.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:30 describes Moses being called from the right side of the valley.

It uses the word shāṭiʾ.

Shāṭiʾ and sāḥil are close in their spatial field.

Both can refer to the water's edge in an appropriate context.

Their roots, however, differ.

Sāḥil belongs to S Ḥ L.

Shāṭiʾ belongs to SH Ṭ ʾ.

Shāṭiʾ al-wādī in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:30 refers to the side of the valley.

Al-sāḥil in Ṭā Hā 20:39 is the bank of the water carrying Moses' chest.

The verses concern different stages of Moses' life.

Semantic closeness does not establish that the two locations are identical.

Nor should the words be separated by an absolute rule that one always denotes a riverbank and the other only a saltwater coast.

S Ḥ L (sāḥil) and Y M M (yamm)

Yamm denotes the body of water.

Sāḥil denotes its edge.

Ṭā Hā 20:39 clearly illustrates the distinction.

The chest is cast into the yamm and then reaches the sāḥil.

Water and its bank are distinct referents connected within one event.

S Ḥ L (sāḥil) and B R R (barr)

In geography, barr denotes land as opposed to sea.

Sāḥil identifies land adjoining water.

A shore is therefore part of the land, but not all land is shore.

Ground far from a river or sea is land but is not called a shore.

Sāḥil has a narrower spatial scope than the general designation for land.

S Ḥ L (saḥl) and K SH Ṭ (kashṭ)

Kashṭ concerns removing a covering or surface.

The scraping and removal branch of saḥl is close to this field.

Lexicons explain saḥl through kashṭ.

Their relationship in this respect is clear.

But S Ḥ L also includes white cloth, single-strand thread, shores and sounds.

These cannot all be reduced to kashṭ without examination.

S Ḥ L (saḥl) and N Ḥ T (naḥt)

Naḥt concerns carving and shaping something by removing some of its material.

Saḥl can likewise denote shaving wood or abrading metal.

The roots overlap in this respect.

Shaping an object may be prominent in naḥt.

Saḥl also has established usages for abrading and removing a surface.

This distinction is not absolute and must be tested against actual usage.

Neither verb occurs in Ṭā Hā 20:39.

The verse names al-sāḥil as the chest's destination, rather than describing an object being carved.

S Ḥ L (sāḥil) and S H L (sahl)

The roots resemble each other in writing and sound, but their letters differ.

S Ḥ L contains ḥāʾ.

S H L contains hāʾ.

Sahl concerns smoothness, ease and the absence of difficulty.

Qur’anic sāḥil denotes the water's edge.

A shore may be level and easy to traverse, but the word itself does not entail those features.

It may also be rocky, high, uneven or difficult to cross.

Al-sāḥil should therefore not be translated as level, easily traversed ground in the Qur’an.

7. Forqan rendering and verse-specific meaning

For bi-al-sāḥili in Ṭā Hā 20:39, “onto the bank” is natural and sufficient.

The translation need not incorporate an etymological explanation of water scouring the margin.

Nor should the word be restricted to a named shore or particular kind of bank.

A brief root box could read:

Brief root meaning: “Scraping and removing a surface in one lexical branch; the shore or water's edge in geography.”

Meaning in this verse: “The riverbank reached by the chest carrying Moses.”

Preserve the installed Arabic spelling and attached bi. These proposals do not replace existing authored explanations in verse boxes.

The word is a definite genitive noun specifying where he arrives or is cast ashore.

8. Conclusion

The Arabic root S Ḥ L has several semantic branches.

In the material branch, saḥl concerns scraping, abrading and removing a surface.

Lexicons record it for wood, metal, skin and other materials.

Misḥal can denote a scraping tool, and suḥālah the particles and filings removed.

In textiles and thread, saḥl and saḥīl denote white cloth and thread not plied with another strand.

Zuhayr's Muʿallaqah contrasts saḥīl with mubram, drawing on the distinction between a single strand and a firmly plied cord.

Al-Mutanakhkhil al-Hudhalī's poetry also uses saḥl for white cloth.

These examples show that the early Arabic family was not limited to one meaning.

Geographically, al-sāḥil names land at the water's edge.

Lexicographers connect it with water scouring and removing a surface.

That explanation is understandable, but does not establish that all shores originated through erosion or that every speaker has scraping in mind when using sāḥil.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an.

Bi-al-sāḥili in Ṭā Hā 20:39 denotes the bank of the water carrying Moses' chest.

The verse describes placing Moses in a chest, entrusting it to the water, reaching the bank and the child's being taken by an enemy.

Al-yamm denotes the water; al-sāḥil denotes the adjoining land.

Reaching the bank is part of Moses' preservation, but the lexical meaning of sāḥil is not rescue or protection itself.

Nor does the word specify the exact place, terrain or distance traveled through the water.

The Qur’anic meaning of sāḥil is the shore or bank adjoining water. The broader family includes other branches such as scraping, removal, white cloth, single-strand thread, and certain sound and movement usages.

For Forqan, “onto the bank” is an accurate and natural rendering of bi-al-sāḥili.

Studying the root does not mean adding its entire history and all its branches to the translation. It helps distinguish the place name, a proposed explanation of its naming, and the meanings of related verbs.
