The Qur’anic Root N S K

Ritual worship, sacrifice, and the performance of rites

The Arabic root ن س ك concerns worship, the performance of religious rites, and sacrifice within a religious act. نُسُك can denote worship or ritual observance and, in a more specific use, an offering presented in worship or devotion. مَنْسَك can refer to a rite, its manner of performance, or the place where it is performed. A نَاسِك is someone who performs such an act or engages in worship.

In the Qur’an, this family occurs chiefly in connection with the rites of pilgrimage, sacrifice, and the worship of communities. Its meaning should nevertheless not be confined to a fixed list of pilgrimage practices or reduced to animal slaughter in every occurrence. The Qur’an places nusuk alongside Salat and human life in one passage, while elsewhere it connects mansak directly with mentioning the name of TheGod over livestock. Each use needs to be read in its own context.

This family occurs seven times in six Qur’anic verses: twice as نُسُك, four times as مَنْسَك / مَنَاسِك, and once as نَاسِكُوهُ. Attached pronouns, as in نُسُكِي, مَنَاسِكَنَا, and مَنَاسِكَكُمْ, do not create additional derived forms. Manāsik is the plural of mansak. No finite verb from this root occurs in the Qur’an: nāsikūhu is an active participle, although English can render it with the verb “perform.”

نُسُك

مَنْسَك / مَنَاسِك

Core meaning and early Arabic usage

Worship and sacrifice: related uses

In Qur’anic usage, this family concerns the performance of rites and worship. نَاسِكُوهُ in Al-Ḥajj 22:67 describes people who perform a mansak. The connection with ritual activity is clear in this construction, but the word does not itself specify every detail of the act or the performer’s inner motivation.

The specific use of nusuk for sacrifice appears both in Al-Baqarah 2:196 and in the poetic example from Zuhayr. Here it concerns killing an animal as a ritual act: a meaning that can overlap with slaughter for food but is not identical to it.

Ordinary slaughter and ritual sacrifice are meaningfully different. An animal may be slaughtered for food without the act forming part of a religious ceremony. When slaughter takes place within a rite and as an offering to a deity, it approaches the sense expressed by nusuk and nasīkah.

This distinction does not mean that nusuk was used only for sacrifices to TheGod. Pre-Islamic Arabic evidence places this word family in the setting of sacrifices offered to other objects of worship as well.

Evidence from pre-Islamic poetry

Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā describes a bird of prey that has pursued a sandgrouse without catching it:

فَزَلَّ عَنْهَا وَوَافَى رَأْسَ مَرْقَبَةٍ
كَمَنْصَبِ الْعِتْرِ دَمَّى رَأْسَهُ النُّسُكُ

Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā, Dīwān, p. 33 in the digital edition consulted. Maqāyīs al-lughah also transmits the variant «وأوفى»; the quotation above follows «ووافى» in the Dīwān.

“It failed to catch her and reached the top of a lookout, like an upright sacrificial stone whose head the sacrifices had stained with blood.”

In this verse, النُّسُك concerns ritual sacrifice or sacrificial offerings. Together with دَمَّى رَأْسَهُ, it creates the image of an upright stone with a bloodied head. العِتْر likewise belongs to the practice of sacrifice. The poet uses a ritual image to describe the bird on high ground; nusuk here does not mean washing or inward purification.

This example attests the sacrificial sense in pre-Qur’anic poetry. The image of the upright stone and bloodied offerings belongs to the sacrificial practices of that Arabic setting. The verse itself does not name a particular deity, so neither the identity of a deity nor the details of a particular ceremony should be inferred from it.

The root does not by itself establish that the object of worship is true, the rite correct, or the sacrifice accepted. Those qualities depend on the act’s orientation and conditions. When the Qur’an speaks of mentioning the name of TheGod over sacrifices, it specifies that orientation; it does not redefine the word’s entire lexical range as “sacrifice to TheGod.”

Historical evidence and a proposed physical origin

This review did not find a reliably attributable, datable pre-Qur’anic example establishing “washing clothes” or “refining metal” as the root’s original meaning. No initial physical image is therefore assigned to this family here. Ritual observance and sacrifice can be examined directly in Zuhayr’s poetry and in the Qur’an; this evidence does not establish the historical sequence in which other branches arose.

Mansak and manāsik: rites and their places of performance

In Arabic, مَنْسَك can denote an act of worship, its recognized manner of performance, or its place of performance. مَنَاسِك is its plural.

In the transmission of Ḥafṣ, both Al-Ḥajj 22:34 and 22:67 have مَنْسَكًا, with fatḥah on the sīn. The readings of Ḥamzah, Al-Kisāʾī, and Khalaf al-ʿĀshir have مَنْسِكًا, with kasrah. Relating these forms to a verbal noun or a noun of place is a morphological question. The vowel alone does not establish an absolute distinction between an act and its location in every occurrence; sentence structure and context also matter.

In pilgrimage language, manāsik concerns the practices and places associated with that worship. The root’s meaning, however, does not originate in the word ḥajj. Nusuk belongs to a separate word family with uses in sacrifice and in the broader field of ritual worship.

“Show us our rites”: asking how to worship

In the prayer of Abraham and Ishmael, we read:

رَبَّنَا وَاجْعَلْنَا مُسْلِمَيْنِ لَكَ وَمِن ذُرِّيَّتِنَا أُمَّةً مُّسْلِمَةً لَّكَ وَأَرِنَا مَنَاسِكَنَا وَتُبْ عَلَيْنَا إِنَّكَ أَنتَ التَّوَّابُ الرَّحِيمُ

“Our Lord, make us both submissive to You, and from our descendants a community submissive to You. Show us our ways of worship and turn toward us; You are indeed the One who repeatedly accepts repentance, the Merciful.”

Al-Baqarah 2:128

Here مَنَاسِكَنَا has a first-person plural pronoun and refers to the rites of Abraham and Ishmael. أَرِنَا asks that those rites be shown or made known to them.

The verse follows the account of Abraham and Ishmael raising the foundations of the House. The context therefore supports a connection between their request and the worship and rites associated with that House.

The word manāsik does not itself supply a complete list of those practices. How each act was performed, their sequence, and their details at that time cannot be recovered from this word alone.

أَرِنَا means “show us,” and in this construction it can also involve making known or teaching. Without further evidence, the verb does not establish a particular physical manner of demonstrating the rites.

Their request to know the rites also accompanies their request to be submissive. These are related but distinct ideas. مُسْلِمَيْنِ لَكَ concerns their submission to TheGod, while مَنَاسِكَنَا concerns ritual acts and ways of performing them.

Completing the rites

Al-Baqarah says:

فَإِذَا قَضَيْتُم مَّنَاسِكَكُمْ فَاذْكُرُوا اللَّهَ كَذِكْرِكُمْ آبَاءَكُمْ أَوْ أَشَدَّ ذِكْرًا فَمِنَ النَّاسِ مَن يَقُولُ رَبَّنَا آتِنَا فِي الدُّنْيَا وَمَا لَهُ فِي الْآخِرَةِ مِنْ خَلَاقٍ

“When you have completed your rites, remember TheGod as you remember your fathers, or with still greater remembrance. Among people is one who says, ‘Our Lord, give us in this world,’ while having no share in the Hereafter.”

Al-Baqarah 2:200

Here مَنَاسِكَكُمْ refers to ritual practices that can be performed and brought to completion. قَضَيْتُمْ expresses carrying out and completing those acts.

The preceding verses concern pilgrimage, departing from ʿArafāt, remembering TheGod, and seeking forgiveness. The rites in this verse therefore belong to that worship.

After the rites have been completed, the Qur’an calls for remembrance of TheGod. Completing a set of ritual acts does not bring a person’s relationship with TheGod, or remembrance of Him, to an end.

That conclusion follows from the sequence of instructions. Manāsik itself does not mean remembrance of TheGod, just as dhikr is not simply another name for rites. The two are connected in this verse while retaining their own meanings.

This occurrence also does not establish that manāsik refers exclusively to pilgrimage rites throughout Arabic or in every Qur’anic context.

Nusuk: sacrifice as an act of worship

Nusuk alongside fasting and charity

Part of Al-Baqarah 2:196 reads:

فَمَن كَانَ مِنكُم مَّرِيضًا أَوْ بِهِ أَذًى مِّن رَّأْسِهِ فَفِدْيَةٌ مِّن صِيَامٍ أَوْ صَدَقَةٍ أَوْ نُسُكٍ

“Whoever among you is ill or suffers an affliction of the head must make compensation through fasting, charity, or sacrifice.”

Al-Baqarah 2:196

The verse concerns pilgrimage and ʿumrah. Before this phrase it discusses shaving the head, and then introduces compensation for illness or an affliction affecting the head.

Here نُسُك appears alongside صِيَام and صَدَقَة. The three are presented as distinct alternatives.

Their presentation as alternative forms of compensation, together with the early attestation of sacrifice, supports rendering nusuk as “sacrifice” here. “Any kind of worship” would not convey the practical distinction between the alternatives well. Their coordination does not, however, prove that their semantic fields can never overlap.

Later legal details, such as the precise species or number of animals or the conditions for performing the sacrifice, cannot be extracted from nusuk itself. The verse presents it as a form of ritual compensation; further details require separate examination.

Nusuk and fidyah are not identical. Fidyah names the compensation offered for a specified circumstance, and nusuk is one of its forms in this verse. Not every nusuk is therefore necessarily a fidyah.

“My Salat and my nusuk”: the orientation of worship and sacrifice

Al-Anʿām says:

قُلْ إِنَّ صَلَاتِي وَنُسُكِي وَمَحْيَايَ وَمَمَاتِي لِلَّهِ رَبِّ الْعَالَمِينَ

“Say: My Salat, my sacrificial rite, my living, and my dying are for TheGod, Lord of the worlds.”

Al-Anʿām 6:162

نُسُكِي consists of nusuk with a first-person singular pronoun: “my nusuk.”

Here nusuk can refer to acts of worship, particularly ritual sacrifice. The sacrificial sense is supported by the word’s early Arabic use and by other Qur’anic occurrences. The broader sense of ritual worship also remains a lexical possibility.

Mentioning صلاتي and نسكي separately encourages a translation that preserves the distinction between them. Coordination alone, however, does not exclude a broader sense of worship: Arabic can coordinate a specific item with a general category. “My sacrificial rite” is supported here by early sacrificial usage and Qur’anic evidence. “My ritual worship” remains a broader possibility, to be distinguished from the translation selected here.

The sentence’s structure also matters. صلاتي, نسكي, محياي, and مماتي are four terms that all relate to لِلَّهِ رَبِّ الْعَالَمِينَ. The following phrase, لَا شَرِيكَ لَهُ, further clarifies that relationship.

The verse therefore does more than identify a ritual act: it specifies its orientation. Sacrifice and worship, alongside life and death, are dedicated to TheGod, with any partner in that dedication denied.

Zuhayr’s verse shows that ritual sacrifice was an established sense before the Qur’an. In Al-Anʿām 6:162–163, dedication to TheGod and the rejection of a partner are expressed by لِلَّهِ and لَا شَرِيكَ لَهُ. The monotheistic orientation comes from those verses’ wording, rather than from nusuk alone.

Mansak and nāsikūhu: the rites of communities

A mansak for every community

Al-Ḥajj says:

وَلِكُلِّ أُمَّةٍ جَعَلْنَا مَنسَكًا لِّيَذْكُرُوا اسْمَ اللَّهِ عَلَىٰ مَا رَزَقَهُم مِّن بَهِيمَةِ الْأَنْعَامِ فَإِلَٰهُكُمْ إِلَٰهٌ وَاحِدٌ فَلَهُ أَسْلِمُوا وَبَشِّرِ الْمُخْبِتِينَ

“For every community We have appointed a sacrificial rite, so that they may mention the name of TheGod over the livestock He has provided them. Your deity is one deity, so submit to Him, and give good news to the humble.”

Al-Ḥajj 22:34

Here مَنْسَكًا accompanies a stated purpose: لِّيَذْكُرُوا اسْمَ اللَّهِ عَلَىٰ مَا رَزَقَهُم مِّن بَهِيمَةِ الْأَنْعَامِ. The passage concerns mentioning the name of TheGod over livestock provided to people.

This context supports the sense of a sacrificial rite. Although mansak has a wider range in Arabic, its direct connection with livestock and sacrifice in this verse must be retained.

Mansakan does not itself name a particular ceremony in all its details. The verse does not say that every community had exactly the same kind of sacrifice, place of performance, or sequence of procedures. It speaks of appointing a mansak for communities and gives its purpose as mentioning the name of TheGod over their provision.

فَإِلَٰهُكُمْ إِلَٰهٌ وَاحِدٌ also needs to be distinguished from the lexical meaning of mansak. That clause declares the unity of the deity. The fact that communities have rites does not by itself establish that all rites are identical in form or validity.

“They perform it”: those who carry out a rite

Another verse in Al-Ḥajj says:

لِّكُلِّ أُمَّةٍ جَعَلْنَا مَنسَكًا هُمْ نَاسِكُوهُ فَلَا يُنَازِعُنَّكَ فِي الْأَمْرِ وَادْعُ إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ إِنَّكَ لَعَلَىٰ هُدًى مُّسْتَقِيمٍ

“For every community We have appointed a rite which they perform. So let them not dispute with you in the matter, and call to your Lord; you are indeed upon straight guidance.”

Al-Ḥajj 22:67

مَنْسَكًا and نَاسِكُوهُ occur together here. Nāsikūhu is the plural active participle of nāsik with the pronoun هُ, which refers back to mansak: they are performers of that rite. Morphologically, the final nūn of ناسكون is dropped in the construct with the attached pronoun. The pronoun is semantically the object of the activity, with mansak as its antecedent.

Nāsik here is therefore not merely a general label for a worshipper or ascetic. The sentence describes people who carry out a specified ritual act.

In the direct reading of this phrase, mansak is a rite that is performed, making “rite” or “act of worship” a clear translation. A locative reading can also be proposed with the sense “they worship in it,” but that explanation should be distinguished from the direct rendering “they perform it.”

The difference from Al-Ḥajj 22:34 should also be retained. In that earlier verse, the connection between mansak, livestock, and mentioning the name of TheGod is explicit. Here that explanation is not repeated, and the wording speaks more generally of a rite appointed for communities.

The surah’s context still makes worship and sacrifice relevant. Yet the word alone does not justify equating mansak with the entirety of religious law, all social legislation, or a complete religion. Such an extension goes beyond what the term directly expresses.

A purely locative translation such as “sacrificial site” needs an explanation of its relationship with nāsikūhu. “Rite,” the rendering selected here, conveys that relationship more directly. This preference does not deny the word’s locative uses elsewhere.

Sacrifice, remembrance of TheGod, and taqwā

Continuing the discussion of sacrifices, the Qur’an says:

لَن يَنَالَ اللَّهَ لُحُومُهَا وَلَا دِمَاؤُهَا وَلَٰكِن يَنَالُهُ التَّقْوَىٰ مِنكُمْ

“Neither their flesh nor their blood will reach TheGod; rather, it is taqwā from you that reaches Him.”

Al-Ḥajj 22:37

This passage follows verses about sacrifice and clarifies the place of ritual action. Slaughter and sacrifice are present in the text, but the flesh and blood are not presented as things that TheGod needs or from which He receives physical benefit.

تَقْوَىٰ belongs to a different root. Its contrast with the flesh and blood of the sacrifices does not mean that nusuk itself means taqwā or that sacrifice was originally an entirely nonmaterial act.

Sacrifice and human taqwā are connected subjects in the verse. Their relationship allows a distinction between the outward form of a rite and the person’s orientation in performing it; it does not justify substituting one for the other in translation.

The passage does not establish either that all outward ritual action is unimportant or that its outward performance alone ensures acceptance by TheGod. The same context discusses sacrifice and emphasizes taqwā.

Comparison with related roots

N S K and ʿ B D: nusuk and worship

Worship concerns adoration and the relationship of servitude, with a broad semantic range. Nusuk can also denote worship, but ritual performance and sacrifice are prominent in its clear occurrences.

A nusuk may therefore belong within worship, while not every use of “worship” can automatically be called nusuk. The Qur’anic family occurs in particular settings, and conceptual proximity does not make the terms interchangeable everywhere.

N S K and Dh B Ḥ: ritual sacrifice and slaughter

Dhabḥ concerns cutting the throat and is not restricted to animals: the Qur’an uses this family for the killing of the sons of the Israelites in Al-Baqarah 2:49. In a comparison concerning sacrifice, dhabḥ denotes the killing itself, while nusuk emphasizes its ritual character.

An animal may be slaughtered for food without the act being a religious offering. Conversely, ritual sacrifice may be carried out by slaughtering an animal. Dhabḥ thus foregrounds the killing, while nusuk in this use concerns sacrifice within a rite.

This distinction does not separate the semantic fields absolutely. A sacrificial act can be both dhabḥ and nusuk; its ritual character does not remove the physical act of slaughter from the meaning.

N S K and Q R B: nusuk and an offering

قُرْبَان denotes something presented as an offering to draw near. Nusuk has a broader connection with ritual acts and, in its sacrificial use, can refer to the offered sacrifice itself.

The terms overlap but approach the subject differently. Qurbān foregrounds presenting an offering and drawing near, whereas nusuk encompasses ritual activity and sacrifice.

The idea of “drawing near” should nevertheless not be inserted automatically into every form of N S K. Manāsik in Al-Baqarah 2:200, for example, concerns acts that are performed; the word does not necessarily mean “means of drawing near.”

N S K and H D Y: nusuk and hady

In pilgrimage contexts, هَدْي concerns a sacrificial offering intended for that worship and brought to its appointed place. Both hady and nusuk appear in Al-Baqarah 2:196, but in different parts of the verse.

Hady is connected in the sentence with reaching its designated place, while nusuk appears among the alternatives for compensation.

Their roles and uses in the sentence are therefore not necessarily identical. Both can concern sacrifice, however, and their different names do not imply that their meanings never overlap.

N S K and Sh ʿ R: mansak and shaʿāʾir

شَعَائِر concerns recognized signs and manifestations important within worship and ritual practice. Mansak denotes an act or manner of ritual observance and, in some uses, its place of performance.

In Al-Ḥajj 22:36, الْبُدْنَ are described as among شَعَائِرِ اللَّهِ. Sacrificial animals therefore connect with both semantic fields: an animal can be a ritual sign and also be involved in a mansak. Shaʿāʾir and manāsik are related in this respect without being complete synonyms.

Translation proposals

For nusuk, broader ritual worship must be distinguished from a specific sacrifice. In Al-Baqarah 2:196, “sacrifice” is natural and appropriate because it appears among the forms of compensation. In Al-Anʿām 6:162, “my sacrificial rite” conveys a specific, supported sense, while the broader “my ritual worship” remains a possibility to discuss.

For manāsikanā in Al-Baqarah 2:128, “our ways of worship” or “our rites” is appropriate. In 2:200, where their performance and completion are in view, “when you have completed your rites” is natural and preserves the connection with pilgrimage practices.

In Al-Ḥajj 22:34, mansakan can be rendered “a sacrificial rite,” given the explicit mention of livestock. In 22:67, “a rite” or “an appointed act of worship” is suitable because nāsikūhu expresses performing that mansak.

Nāsikūhu can be rendered “they perform it” or “its performers.” “His worshippers” is inaccurate here: the pronoun refers to the mansak, not to TheGod.

In translating this family, manāsik should not be confined to a fixed, presupposed list of later legal practices. The rites of Abraham, the completion of pilgrimage rites, and the mansak of communities each need to be understood within their own context.

Conclusion

In the Qur’an, N S K concerns worship and ritual activity, with sacrifice as a clear and ancient use. Zuhayr’s verse attests the history of that sense. The orientation of worship, the correctness of the act, and its dedication to a particular deity must be understood from the wording and context; the root alone does not establish all these qualities.

This Qur’anic family occurs in connection with learning and performing rites, sacrifice, worship, and the rites of communities. Nusuk denotes sacrifice specifically in some passages; mansak can name a rite or act of worship; manāsik denotes such acts in the plural; and nāsikūhu describes those who perform a mansak.

The defensible semantic core is worship and ritual performance, with an early and prominent use in sacrifice. Not every occurrence is reducible to slaughter, and the sacrificial meaning cannot be removed from the family to turn it exclusively into inward cleansing or personal spirituality.

In passages concerning this root, the Qur’an recognizes specific practices and rites while also expressing their orientation through remembrance of TheGod, submission to Him, and taqwā. These concepts relate to nusuk but are not the word’s own meaning. Distinguishing ritual action, the sacrificial offering, the manner of performance, and the orientation of worship is essential to understanding this family in the Qur’an.
