The Qur’anic Root Ḥ S D

Ḥasad: resenting another’s advantage and wishing for its loss

The root Ḥ S D appears in the Qur’an in the forms ḥasadan, yaḥsudūna, taḥsudūnanā, ḥāsid, and ḥasada. These five occurrences are found in four verses: Al-Baqarah 2:109, Al-Nisāʾ 4:54, Al-Fatḥ 48:15, and Al-Falaq 113:5. The last verse contains two forms of the root.

Unlike roots with a very broad semantic range, the Qur’anic uses of Ḥ S D are closely related. At their centre is another person who has received a blessing, favour, or desirable position, and an envious person who resents that advantage remaining with its recipient.

In Arabic, ḥasad concerns resentment of another’s blessing and a wish for its loss, whether the envious person wants the blessing transferred to themselves or merely wants the other person deprived of it.

Ḥasad is therefore not the same as simply saying, “I would like something like that too.” In envy, the other person’s possession of the advantage itself becomes the problem.

An initial definition is:

Ḥ S D: resentment of a favour, blessing, or advantage enjoyed by another, such that its loss, removal, or denial to that person is desired. The envious person’s acquiring the same blessing is not an essential part of the meaning.

Forms and designations examined

Ḥasadan: accusative verbal noun expressing motive

يَحْسُدُونَ
Yaḥsudūna: imperfect verb, they envy

تَحْسُدُونَنَا
Taḥsudūnanā: imperfect with object pronoun, you envy us

حَاسِد / حَسَدَ
Ḥāsid / ḥasada: active participle and perfect verb

A clear meaning without a securely established physical image

For Ḥ S D, no original physical image has been securely established from which the root’s core can confidently be reconstructed.

Some lexical explanations have attempted to connect envy with stripping or peeling, even associating creatures that scrape the skin or suck blood with this word family. Such images may be evocative as illustrations of envy, but they do not suffice to establish a definitive history of the root.

For understanding the Qur’anic meaning, clear examples of usage offer a firmer basis than reconstructing a hypothetical physical image.

In the familiar construction:

حَسَدَهُ عَلَى الشيء

Ḥasadahu ʿalā sh-shayʾ: he envied him for something.

Three elements can be identified:

The person who envies;
the person envied;
and something belonging to that person which is the object of envy.

The same construction is clearly visible in the Qur’an itself.

Yaḥsudūna al-nāsa ʿalā mā ātāhumu llāhu min faḍlihi: divine favour can be the object of envy

Al-Nisāʾ says:

أَمْ يَحْسُدُونَ النَّاسَ عَلَىٰ مَا آتَاهُمُ اللَّهُ مِنْ فَضْلِهِ ۖ فَقَدْ آتَيْنَا آلَ إِبْرَاهِيمَ الْكِتَابَ وَالْحِكْمَةَ وَآتَيْنَاهُمْ مُلْكًا عَظِيمًا

Am yaḥsudūna n-nāsa ʿalā mā ātāhumu llāhu min faḍlihi, fa-qad ātaynā āla Ibrāhīma l-kitāba wa-l-ḥikmata wa-ātaynāhum mulkan ʿaẓīman.

“Or do they envy people for what The God has given them of His favour? We indeed gave the family of Abraham the Book and wisdom, and We gave them great dominion.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:54

The verse’s construction makes the relationship of envy clear:

يَحْسُدُونَ النَّاسَ

Yaḥsudūna n-nāsa.

People are being envied;

And:

عَلَىٰ مَا آتَاهُمُ اللَّهُ مِنْ فَضْلِهِ

ʿAlā mā ātāhumu llāhu min faḍlihi.

The object of envy is what The God has given them of His favour.

Envy is therefore not confined to money, possessions, or beauty. The continuation mentions the Book, wisdom, and dominion. Religious, intellectual, or social favour can also be an object of envy.

This is one of the clearest passages showing that another person’s enjoyment of an advantage is central to envy.

Ḥasadan min ʿindi anfusihim: when another’s loss is desired

Al-Baqarah says:

وَدَّ كَثِيرٌ مِنْ أَهْلِ الْكِتَابِ لَوْ يَرُدُّونَكُمْ مِنْ بَعْدِ إِيمَانِكُمْ كُفَّارًا حَسَدًا مِنْ عِنْدِ أَنْفُسِهِمْ مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا تَبَيَّنَ لَهُمُ الْحَقُّ

Wadda kathīrun min ahli l-kitābi law yaruddūnakum min baʿdi īmānikum kuffāran ḥasadan min ʿindi anfusihim min baʿdi mā tabayyana lahumu l-ḥaqqu.

“Many of the People of the Book wished they could turn you back into disbelievers after your faith, out of envy arising from themselves, after the truth had become clear to them.”

Al-Baqarah 2:109

This verse places wadda and ḥasad together in one sentence.

وَدَّ

Wadda.

Expresses their wish:

لَوْ يَرُدُّونَكُمْ مِنْ بَعْدِ إِيمَانِكُمْ كُفَّارًا

Law yaruddūnakum min baʿdi īmānikum kuffāran.

They want the believers to lose their faith.

Then:

حَسَدًا

Ḥasadan.

Makes the reason for that wish clear. Here ḥasadan is an accusative verbal noun expressing motive.

The distinction lies in their roles and meanings: wadda expresses wishing, while ḥasadan identifies the motive for the wish. The mere presence of two words in one sentence would not, by itself, prove a difference in meaning.

Wadda states the outcome they desire;
ḥasad shows that another person’s enjoyment of a desirable condition has become unwelcome to them.

Here the object of envy is not even wealth or rank, but faith.

The expression:

مِنْ عِنْدِ أَنْفُسِهِمْ

Min ʿindi anfusihim.

Locates the source of this envy within themselves.

This is among the clearest Qur’anic examples connecting envy with the wish for another person’s loss of an advantage.

Envy is not identical to wishing for a similar blessing

Al-Nisāʾ says:

وَلَا تَتَمَنَّوْا مَا فَضَّلَ اللَّهُ بِهِ بَعْضَكُمْ عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ ... وَاسْأَلُوا اللَّهَ مِنْ فَضْلِهِ

Wa-lā tatamannaw mā faḍḍala llāhu bihi baʿḍakum ʿalā baʿḍin … wa-sʾalū llāha min faḍlihi.

“Do not wish for that by which The God has favoured some of you over others … and ask The God for His favour.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:32

This verse does not use the root Ḥ S D, although it again concerns favour and the different advantages people receive.

In comparing these expressions, we must distinguish simply wishing for something from wishing for another person’s deprivation. Al-Nisāʾ 4:32 itself prohibits a kind of wishing; it does not, on its own, establish that every wish for a similar blessing is permissible.

Someone may wish to have a blessing like another person’s. That wish alone does not establish that they want the other person to lose it.

Envy involves a further element: the blessing’s remaining with the other person has become unwelcome.

Lexical explanations use ghibṭah for wishing for a blessing like another’s without wishing for the other person’s loss. This distinction is conceptually useful, but ghibṭah does not occur in the Qur’an as a term opposed to ḥasad. Its absence also does not establish when the word originated.

In studying Qur’anic usage, we can therefore draw the following distinction by considering both the lexical meaning and the verses:

Wishing and desiring are broader categories; ḥasad specifically concerns another’s blessing and resentment of its remaining with that person.

Ḥ S D (ḥasad) and B GH Y (baghy): related meanings, not an inward–outward division

One of the Qur’anic semantic fields closest to envy is that of B GH Y.

Al-Baqarah 2:90 says:

بِئْسَمَا اشْتَرَوْا بِهِ أَنْفُسَهُمْ أَنْ يَكْفُرُوا بِمَا أَنْزَلَ اللَّهُ بَغْيًا أَنْ يُنَزِّلَ اللَّهُ مِنْ فَضْلِهِ عَلَىٰ مَنْ يَشَاءُ مِنْ عِبَادِهِ

Biʾsamā shtaraw bihi anfusahum an yakfurū bi-mā anzala llāhu baghyan an yunazzila llāhu min faḍlihi ʿalā man yashāʾu min ʿibādihi.

“How wretched is that for which they sold themselves: rejecting what The God has sent down, out of baghy at The God sending down His favour upon whomever He wills among His servants.”

Al-Baqarah 2:90

This construction closely resembles Al-Nisāʾ 4:54:

يَحْسُدُونَ النَّاسَ عَلَىٰ مَا آتَاهُمُ اللَّهُ مِنْ فَضْلِهِ

Yaḥsudūna n-nāsa ʿalā mā ātāhumu llāhu min faḍlihi.

In both cases, the issue is The God’s favour bestowed on someone else.

The Qur’an also speaks of disagreement after knowledge has come:

وَمَا اخْتَلَفَ فِيهِ إِلَّا الَّذِينَ أُوتُوهُ مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا جَاءَتْهُمُ الْبَيِّنَاتُ بَغْيًا بَيْنَهُمْ

Wa-mā khtalafa fīhi illā lladhīna ūtūhu min baʿdi mā jāʾathumu l-bayyinātu baghyan baynahum.

“None differed over it except those who had been given it, after clear proofs had come to them, out of baghy among themselves.”

Al-Baqarah 2:213

Nevertheless, ḥasad and baghy are not complete synonyms.

Ḥasad specifically revolves around a blessing another person possesses, whose remaining with that person is resented.

Baghy has a broader range. Its root can concern seeking, exceeding bounds, aggression, striving for superiority, and wrongful conduct. In some contexts, such as Al-Baqarah 2:90, baghy comes very close to envy because its object is precisely the favour bestowed on another.

But we cannot establish a rule that:

Ḥasad = an inward condition
And
Baghy = the outward enactment of envy

Such a fixed division is not supported by these uses.

In Al-Baqarah 2:90, baghyan itself expresses the motive and orientation of rejection. Rather than necessarily naming a separate outward act, it can describe a transgressive disposition and desire.

Conversely, envy can also appear in conduct. Al-Baqarah 2:109 clearly connects envy with the wish to turn others away from faith.

A more precise relationship is:

Ḥasad focuses on resenting another’s advantage and wishing for its loss;
baghy, in contexts of censure, is broader, focusing on pursuit that exceeds bounds, aggression, or wrongful action against another.

Envy can be one motive for baghy, but not every instance of baghy is envy, and envy does not necessarily develop into a particular act of baghy.

Bal taḥsudūnanā: envy as an accusation

Al-Fatḥ says:

سَيَقُولُ الْمُخَلَّفُونَ إِذَا انْطَلَقْتُمْ إِلَىٰ مَغَانِمَ لِتَأْخُذُوهَا ذَرُونَا نَتَّبِعْكُمْ ... فَسَيَقُولُونَ بَلْ تَحْسُدُونَنَا ۚ بَلْ كَانُوا لَا يَفْقَهُونَ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا

Sa-yaqūlu l-mukhallafūna idhā nṭalaqtum ilā maghānima li-taʾkhudhūhā dharūnā nattabiʿkum … fa-sa-yaqūlūna bal taḥsudūnanā, bal kānū lā yafqahūna illā qalīlan.

“Those left behind will say, when you set out to take spoils, ‘Let us follow you’ … Then they will say, ‘Rather, you envy us.’ Rather, they understood only a little.”

Al-Fatḥ 48:15

Here taḥsudūnanā, “you envy us,” is spoken by those left behind.

They interpret their exclusion from the expedition and its spoils as meaning that others do not want them to share in this benefit.

This use shows that in social speech, envy could also be an accusation that someone does not want another person to receive an advantage.

But the Qur’an does not endorse their diagnosis, immediately saying:

بَلْ كَانُوا لَا يَفْقَهُونَ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا

Bal kānū lā yafqahūna illā qalīlan.

The verse is therefore not evidence that the believers were envious; it is evidence of how the word is used in the opponents’ speech.

This distinction matters in studying a root: reporting a word spoken by figures in the Qur’an does not always mean endorsing their judgment.

Wa-min sharri ḥāsidin idhā ḥasada: envy and the possibility of harm

Al-Falaq says:

وَمِنْ شَرِّ حَاسِدٍ إِذَا حَسَدَ

Wa-min sharri ḥāsidin idhā ḥasada.

“And from the evil of an envier when he envies.”

Al-Falaq 113:5

Two forms of the same root stand together:

حَاسِد

Ḥāsid.

An active participle, meaning a person who envies;

And:

حَسَدَ

Ḥasada.

A verb, “he envied,” here used after idhā, “when.”

The verse focuses on:

شَرِّ حَاسِدٍ إِذَا حَسَدَ

Sharri ḥāsidin idhā ḥasada.

The evil of an envier when he envies.

Envy can thus be associated with the envier’s evil.

Al-Baqarah 2:109 connects this condition with a harmful wish: envy accompanies the wish to turn others away from faith. This wording alone does not report that the wish necessarily became an action.

But Al-Falaq 113:5 does not define how the evil operates. The expression alone cannot establish that the envier’s evil must take the form of lies, conspiracy, harassment, exclusion, or any other particular mechanism.

Nor does ḥāsid, as a word, mean “someone who casts the evil eye.” The verse uses the root Ḥ S D and does not define a separate mechanism for the evil eye.

Conversely, the envier’s evil need not be restricted to one specific outward act. The verse leaves the kind of evil unspecified.

A more secure boundary is:

Ḥasad is a disposition toward another’s blessing that can give rise to evil and harmful conduct; the root itself does not determine the kind or mechanism of that harm.

Translation proposals for Forqan

In most cases, envy is a suitable English rendering, provided that the meaning has been made clear in the article.

For:

أَمْ يَحْسُدُونَ النَّاسَ عَلَىٰ مَا آتَاهُمُ اللَّهُ مِنْ فَضْلِهِ

Am yaḥsudūna n-nāsa ʿalā mā ātāhumu llāhu min faḍlihi.

We can say:

“Or do they envy people for what The God has given them of His favour?”

For:

حَسَدًا مِنْ عِنْدِ أَنْفُسِهِمْ

Ḥasadan min ʿindi anfusihim.

“Out of envy arising from themselves”

Offers a natural English rendering.

For:

بَلْ تَحْسُدُونَنَا

Bal taḥsudūnanā.

“Rather, you envy us”

Is suitable, with the explanation that these are the words of those left behind.

And:

وَمِنْ شَرِّ حَاسِدٍ إِذَا حَسَدَ

Wa-min sharri ḥāsidin idhā ḥasada.

Can be translated directly as:

“And from the evil of an envier when he envies.”

This preserves the relationship between the person and the act of envying.

“Envier” should not become “someone who casts the evil eye,” since that translation imports another concept into the root itself.

English words such as “jealousy” can cover feelings beyond resentment of another’s blessing, while “envy” itself can sometimes be used loosely for simply wishing for a similar advantage. For a lexical study, “envy,” clarified when needed as “resenting another’s blessing and wishing for its loss,” makes the intended distinction clearer.

Conclusion

The Qur’anic root Ḥ S D centres on resentment of a favour or advantage another person enjoys, such that its remaining with that person is unwelcome and its loss or removal is desired.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:54 shows that the object of envy can be divine favour, the Book, wisdom, or standing, and is not restricted to material things. Al-Baqarah 2:109 connects envy with the explicit wish for another person to lose faith, distinguishing it from merely wishing or desiring.

Comparison with baghy reveals an important overlap, especially in Al-Baqarah 2:90, where baghyan also describes a reaction to The God’s favour being sent down upon another. Yet ḥasad and baghy are not identical. Envy specifically concerns another’s blessing and the desire for its loss; baghy covers a broader field of wrongful pursuit, aggression, and exceeding bounds. Envy can give rise to baghy, but the relationship should not become a fixed “inward–outward” formula.

Al-Falaq 113:5 also links envy to the possibility of evil without specifying its kind or mechanism.

A concise definition of the root is:

Ḥ S D: resentment of a favour, blessing, or advantage enjoyed by another, such that its loss, removal, or denial to that person is desired. Envy differs from merely wishing for a similar blessing because the other person’s enjoyment of it becomes unwelcome. This disposition can give rise to evil, baghy, and harmful conduct, but the root itself does not determine the kind or mechanism of that conduct.
