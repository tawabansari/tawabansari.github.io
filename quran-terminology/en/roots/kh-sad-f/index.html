Qur’anic Root KH Ṣ F

Qur’anic and lexical forms

Joining one thing to another: from mending sandals and weaving leaves to covering the body in Adam’s story

The Arabic root خ ص ف concerns joining, overlaying, and attaching things to one another. Its derivatives are used when a piece of leather is laid on a sandal and stitched to another part, or leaves and fibers are fitted together to form a covering.

Separate components are joined into a whole in these uses. Attachment may involve stitching, tying, weaving, or placing and securing pieces upon one another.

Khaṣf does not necessarily mean sewing with a needle and thread. Stitching sandals is one familiar instance, while joining leaves or weaving palm fibers may use other methods.

Early Arabic used this family for mending sandals, weaving palm-leaf coverings, and making containers for dates. Khaṣīf and akhṣaf also describe things combining two colors, such as a rope made of black and white strands.

The Qur’an uses the root twice, in Al-Aʿrāf 7:22 and Ṭā Hā 20:121. Both occur in Adam and his spouse’s story, in the form yakhṣifāni.

After their sawʾāt become visible, the verses state that they began placing and joining leaves of the garden over themselves.

Here the root concerns attaching and overlaying leaves. Covering the body is the purpose of the action, understood from the situation described.

We should therefore distinguish joining the leaves, placing them over the body, and the purpose of covering what had become exposed. These belong to one event but are not lexically or syntactically identical.

1. Forms of the root and its fundamental meaning

The root خ ص ف occurs in the Qur’an only as yakhṣifāni, once in Al-Aʿrāf 7:22 and once in Ṭā Hā 20:121.

This is an active imperfect verb, third-person masculine dual, from Form I khaṣafa–yakhṣifu. In this construction, it is indicative, marked by the retained nūn; its verbal clause serves as the predicate of ṭafiqā, occupying the accusative syntactic position.

The Arabic masculine dual can refer to one man and one woman together. In these verses, the subjects are Adam and his spouse.

Important derivatives recorded in lexicons include:

  • خَصَفَ ـ يَخْصِفُ, khaṣafa–yakhṣifu: joined one thing to another, patched it, or overlaid and attached components.
  • خَصْف, khaṣf: joining and patching, especially of sandals and coverings made from several layers.
  • خَصَفَ النَّعْلَ, khaṣafa al-naʿla: patched or repaired a sandal.
  • خَصْفَة, khaṣfah: a piece added to patch a sandal.
  • خَصَفَة, khaṣafah: a container or woven article made from palm leaves, with fatḥah on ṣād.
  • خِصَاف, khiṣāf: the plural of khaṣafah for woven containers; also a name for some palm-fiber coverings.
  • مِخْصَف, mikhṣaf: a leather-piercing tool for stitching and patching, like an awl.
  • خَصَّاف, khaṣṣāf: someone who patches sandals or other things.
  • اِخْتَصَفَ, ikhtaṣafa: joined something together for oneself; of the body, placed and attached leaves or a covering over oneself.
  • خَصِيف, khaṣīf: something combining two colors or kinds; in food usage, a mixture of fresh and sour milk.
  • أَخْصَف and خَصْفَاء, akhṣaf and khaṣfāʾ: describing an animal or object with two colors, especially black and white, in parts of it.
  • خَصُوف, khaṣūf: in a particular branch, describing a she-camel in relation to the timing of giving birth.
  • خَصَّفَ, khaṣṣafa: in certain uses, joined things in layers or repeatedly.

Lexical sources record these forms, but none except yakhṣifāni appears in the Qur’an.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: two yakhṣifāni occurrences, Al-Aʿrāf 7:22 and Ṭā Hā 20:121

The semantic core according to Ibn Fāris

Ibn Fāris writes in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

الْخَاءُ وَالصَّادُ وَالْفَاءُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى اجْتِمَاعِ شَيْءٍ إِلَى شَيْءٍ.

al-khāʾu wa-al-ṣādu wa-al-fāʾu aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā ijtimāʿi shayʾin ilā shayʾin

That is, خ ص ف indicates one thing joining another.

He illustrates this with mending sandals:

فَالْخَصْفُ خَصْفُ النَّعْلِ، وَهُوَ أَنْ يُطَبَّقَ عَلَيْهَا مِثْلُهَا.

fa-al-khaṣfu khaṣfu al-naʿli wa-huwa an yuṭabbaqa ʿalayhā mithluhā

One use of khaṣf is therefore patching a sandal by laying another piece upon it.

Lisān al-ʿArab also states:

وَكُلُّ مَا طُورِقَ بَعْضُهُ عَلَى بَعْضٍ فَقَدْ خُصِفَ.

wa-kullu mā ṭūriqa baʿḍuhu ʿalā baʿḍin fa-qad khuṣifa

Anything whose parts are laid upon one another in layers has undergone khaṣf.

These definitions show that the core is not simply covering, but joining several components together.

If a sandal tears, for example, someone may place leather over the damaged part and stitch it. Covering the tear is one purpose, while khaṣf concerns attaching and overlaying the pieces.

The same distinction matters in Adam’s story.

Leaves can cover the body, but yakhṣifāni also describes a way of fitting and joining them together.

The defensible fundamental meaning is joining and attaching one thing to another, especially when pieces are overlaid, patched, or woven together.

Some derivatives extend to the combination of different colors, but every branch should not be reduced to one material image without examination.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Lisān al-ʿArab: joining, patching, and overlaying

2. Early uses: sandals, palm weaving, and mixed colors

Mending sandals

Repairing sandals is a familiar use of khaṣf.

Arabic khaṣafa al-naʿla describes adding a piece to a sandal and attaching it to another part.

Leather sandals may contain several layers. If one is damaged, another piece can be placed over it and secured with an appropriate tool.

The leather-piercing tool is called a mikhṣaf.

Khaṣf here involves stitching and piercing leather, but that association does not require every instance of khaṣf to use the same tool.

Joining leaves may involve a different method.

The sandal use therefore does not establish that Adam and his spouse necessarily stitched the leaves with an awl, needle, or thread.

The Qur’an does not specify those details.

An early poetic witness: mikhṣaf describing an eagle’s beak

A poem attributed to Abū Kabīr al-Hudhalī states:

حَتَّى انْتَهَيْتُ إِلَى فِرَاشِ عَزِيزَةٍ
سَوْدَاءَ رَوْثَةُ أَنْفِهَا كَالْمِخْصَفِ

ḥattā intahaytu ilā firāshi ʿazīzatin / sawdāʾa rawthatu anfihā ka-al-mikhṣafi

Approximate translation:

“Until I reached the nest of a mighty black eagle, the tip of whose beak was like a sandal-maker’s awl.”

ʿAzīzah describes the eagle, and mikhṣaf is the pointed tool used to pierce leather. In the reading sawdāʾa, black describes the eagle; rawthatu anfihā begins the clause comparing the beak’s tip with an awl.

The poet compares part of the eagle’s beak with this implement.

This shows that mikhṣaf was an established name for a particular tool in early Arabic patching and stitching. This explanation concerns the eagle image; some literary explanations also give the image an underlying reference to a woman, so it should not necessarily be treated as a literal account of visiting a nest.

The verse itself, however, does not concern making coverings from leaves.

Its relevance to the Qur’anic passages is lexical and concerns the craft of joining pieces, rather than establishing Adam and his spouse’s precise method.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Lisān al-ʿArab, and collections of Hudhalī poetry transmit this verse.

Reports vary between sawdāʾa rawthatu anfihā and fatkhāʾa rawthatu anfihā. The variation concerns the eagle’s description, while al-mikhṣaf appears in both.

The verse is attributed to Abū Kabīr al-Hudhalī. Attribution to a poet should nevertheless be distinguished from the certain date of an individual verse, although early literary sources place this witness within Hudhalī poetry.

Maqāyīs and Lisān al-ʿArab: Abū Kabīr’s witness and the sawdāʾ and fatkhāʾ variants

Khaṣafah: an article woven from palm leaves

Early Arabic communities used palm leaves and fibers for containers, coverings, and mats.

They fitted narrow leaves or fibers beside one another and wove them into a connected whole.

One name for such an article was khaṣafah.

Lexicons use khaṣafah for a basket or container woven from palm leaves and used to store dates.

Khiṣāf is one recorded plural in this use.

This example matters for understanding the root’s fundamental meaning.

Weaving palm leaves joins separate components into a more integrated surface or container.

Khaṣafah itself does not mean every kind of container or leaf.

It names a particular product made by weaving and joining fibers.

A lexical disagreement over thick coverings

Some sources explain khaṣaf as a very thick covering or garment.

Al-Layth ibn al-Muẓaffar transmits such an expression, but Al-Azharī disputes the explanation.

Al-Azharī says that khaṣaf here is not thick cloth woven in the usual way, but coverings woven from palm leaves and fibers, usable for covering parts of tents or making date-storage containers.

The disagreement matters for identifying the material used.

A covering may look thick and coarse without necessarily being made of ordinary cloth or thread.

Analysis of khaṣf should therefore not restrict every covering to stitched fabric.

The Qur’anic use likewise concerns garden leaves, rather than cloth or ready-made clothing.

Tahdhīb and Lisān al-ʿArab: khaṣfah versus khaṣafah and Al-Azharī’s correction concerning thick coverings

Khaṣīf: two colors together

Lexicons use ḥablun khaṣīfun for a rope showing two colors, especially black and white.

Farasun akhṣafu also describes a horse whose light coloring extends from its underside to its flanks, combining with another color.

The issue here is not stitching, but different colors appearing together.

Ibn Fāris connects this with the core of one thing joining another, while distinguishing it from physical patching.

In patching, two pieces are physically overlaid and attached.

In an animal’s natural coloring, two colors appear within one whole without separate pieces being sewn together by a person.

Khaṣīf therefore does not establish that the animal has a patched body.

This use illustrates the family’s descriptive extension.

An Umayyad poetic witness concerning two colors

The Umayyad poet Al-ʿAjjāj says in a rajaz line:

أَبْدَى الصَّبَاحُ عَنْ بَرِيمٍ أَخْصَفَا

abdā al-ṣabāḥu ʿan barīmin akhṣafā

Approximate translation:

“Morning disclosed a two-colored appearance, like a woven cord.”

Barīm concerns a twisted or woven cord, and akhṣaf describes combined colors. Here the two-colored cord images the mingling of dawn’s light and night’s darkness, rather than necessarily a real cord seen in the morning.

Some reports begin the line with mina al-ṣabāḥi. In his supplement to Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Ṣaghānī prefers this wording. Abū ʿAlī al-Fārisī likewise quotes it after ḥattā idhā mā layluhu takashshafā and explains the emergence of dawn’s two-colored appearance from the night’s darkness. The quotation above preserves the wording transmitted in Al-Ṣiḥāḥ.

Abū ʿAlī al-Fārisī’s Kitāb al-Shiʿr: mina al-ṣabāḥ and the dawn image

This witness suits the branch concerning mixed colors.

Al-ʿAjjāj belonged to the Umayyad period, however, so this is not a pre-Islamic witness. Nor should its color imagery be transferred to yakhṣifāni in Adam’s story.

The Qur’anic verb concerns joining leaves, rather than producing a two-colored pattern.

Khaṣīfah: mixing two kinds of milk

Arabic also records khaṣīfah for a mixture of fresh and sour milk.

Two substances are mixed here.

This connects with joining things together but differs from patching a sandal.

In stitching, the pieces generally retain their individual identities.

Mixing two kinds of milk combines the substances into a mixture.

The core of combination can therefore appear differently in different branches.

Khaṣf itself does not mean mixing food or drink in the Qur’an.

A particular use concerning a she-camel giving birth

Lexicons also record khaṣafati al-nāqatu of a she-camel giving birth at a particular stage of pregnancy.

Ibn Fāris regards this as a branch not readily explained through joining and patching.

Reports differ over the precise month of birth and the distinction between this description and related forms.

It is therefore better to record the use without inventing an unsupported derivational image for it.

This branch does not occur in the Qur’an and contributes nothing to the meaning of yakhṣifāni.

Maqāyīs and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: two colors, milk mixtures, and differing accounts of birth timing

3. Al-Aʿrāf 7:22: joining leaves after the private parts become visible

فَدَلَّاهُمَا بِغُرُورٍ فَلَمَّا ذَاقَا الشَّجَرَةَ بَدَتْ لَهُمَا سَوْآتُهُمَا وَطَفِقَا يَخْصِفَانِ عَلَيْهِمَا مِنْ وَرَقِ الْجَنَّةِ وَنَادَاهُمَا رَبُّهُمَا أَلَمْ أَنْهَكُمَا عَنْ تِلْكُمَا الشَّجَرَةِ وَأَقُلْ لَكُمَا إِنَّ الشَّيْطَانَ لَكُمَا عَدُوٌّ مُبِينٌ

fa-dallāhumā bi-ghurūrin fa-lammā dhāqā al-shajarata badat lahumā sawʾātuhumā wa-ṭafiqā yakhṣifāni ʿalayhimā min waraqi al-jannati wa-nādāhumā rabbuhumā a-lam anhakumā ʿan tilkumā al-shajarati wa-aqul lakumā inna al-shayṭāna lakumā ʿaduwwun mubīnun

Proposed translation for this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“So he drew the two down through deception. When they tasted the tree, their private parts became visible to them, and they began fastening leaves of the garden over themselves. Their Lord called to them: Did I not forbid you that tree and tell you that Satan is a clear enemy to you?”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:22

This verse is the first Qur’anic occurrence of خ ص ف.

The story begins with Satan’s whispering and deception.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:20, Satan whispers to expose what had been concealed of their sawʾāt.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:21, he swears to them that he is a sincere adviser.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:22, after tasting the tree, what had been concealed becomes visible, and both begin placing and joining garden leaves over themselves.

This sequence matters: joining the leaves is a response to a particular condition.

Wa-ṭafiqā: beginning an action

Ṭafiqā comes from another root and here indicates beginning or setting about an action.

The following verb, yakhṣifāni, expresses the action they began.

“They began” therefore comes from ṭafiqā, rather than from خ ص ف itself.

Yakhṣifāni alone would state the action; ṭafiqā highlights beginning and entering into it.

The construction shows that, after their private parts became visible, they turned to joining leaves.

Ṭafiqā itself specifies neither their exact speed nor their level of distress nor the number of leaves.

Yakhṣifāni: how are the leaves joined?

Yakhṣifāni is a Form I verb.

Here it occurs with ʿalayhimā and min waraqi al-jannati.

Early lexicons and commentaries explain it with yulṣiqāni, yaḍummāni, and yuraqqiʿāni: attaching the leaves, overlaying them, or joining them like patches.

Al-Ṭabarī reports Mujāhid’s explanation that the leaves are joined as in patching a garment.

This accords with the established meaning of khaṣafa al-naʿla.

It nevertheless does not require the conclusion that Adam and his spouse stitched the leaves with tools specifically used for sandal-making.

The verse names no tool for attachment.

The verb and its accompanying prepositions indicate that they used garden leaves to make or place a covering over themselves, joining the leaves in some way.

Imagining leaves laid upon one another to cover the body may best convey the central image.

Specifying the covering’s exact form or how the leaves were secured nevertheless requires information absent from the Qur’anic sentence.

Al-Ṭabarī: fastening leaves and Mujāhid’s patching comparison

ʿAlayhimā: over both of them

The pronoun humā in ʿalayhimā refers to Adam and his spouse.

ʿAlā expresses the leaves’ placement over their bodies.

The action is therefore not merely joining leaves somewhere apart from the body.

In context, the leaves are placed over the two themselves.

The dual construction also shows that both participate in the action.

It should therefore not be attributed exclusively to Adam or exclusively to his spouse.

The expression speaks of both.

Min waraqi al-jannati: what kind of leaves?

Waraq here denotes leaves.

Al-jannah identifies the place from which they are taken.

Some reports in early commentaries identify them as fig leaves.

The Qur’anic text, however, does not name figs.

Waraqu al-jannah should therefore not become fig leaves in direct translation.

Nor does the verse give the leaves’ number or size, the tree species, or how they were picked.

None of these details belongs to the root’s underlying meaning either.

For the root study, it suffices to explain that the two joined the leaves and placed them over their bodies.

Joining leaves and covering the private parts

The verse first states:

بَدَتْ لَهُمَا سَوْآتُهُمَا

Meaning: their private parts became visible to them.

The joining of leaves follows immediately.

This sequence shows that the purpose was covering the parts that had become exposed.

Khaṣf itself nevertheless does not mean private parts or covering nakedness.

The same root describes joining leather pieces to repair a sandal, an action with no relation to the human body.

Joining the leaves should therefore be distinguished from the purpose of covering the body.

The verse clearly connects the two, but they are not lexically identical.

4. Ṭā Hā 20:121: the same action in another narrative context

فَأَكَلَا مِنْهَا فَبَدَتْ لَهُمَا سَوْآتُهُمَا وَطَفِقَا يَخْصِفَانِ عَلَيْهِمَا مِنْ وَرَقِ الْجَنَّةِ وَعَصَىٰ آدَمُ رَبَّهُ فَغَوَىٰ

fa-akalā minhā fa-badat lahumā sawʾātuhumā wa-ṭafiqā yakhṣifāni ʿalayhimā min waraqi al-jannati wa-ʿaṣā ādamu rabbahu fa-ghawā

Proposed translation for this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“So the two ate from it, and their nakedness became visible to them; they began covering themselves with leaves of the garden. Adam disobeyed his Lord and went astray.”

Ṭā Hā 20:121

This is the root’s second and final Qur’anic occurrence.

Wa-ṭafiqā yakhṣifāni ʿalayhimā min waraqi al-jannati is identical in both verses.

The lexical and syntactic meaning of yakhṣifāni therefore does not change in Ṭā Hā 20:121.

It still concerns joining leaves and placing them over the two bodies.

Ṭā Hā’s context nevertheless highlights other aspects of the story.

The earlier warning concerning nakedness

Ṭā Hā 20:118 states:

إِنَّ لَكَ أَلَّا تَجُوعَ فِيهَا وَلَا تَعْرَىٰ

inna laka a-llā tajūʿa fīhā wa-lā taʿrā

Proposed translation for this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“It is yours there neither to hunger nor to be naked.”

Hunger and nakedness are among the conditions from which Adam was protected in the garden.

In Ṭā Hā 20:120, Satan then whispers to him, speaking of the tree of immortality and a kingdom that does not decay.

In Ṭā Hā 20:121, after eating from the tree, nakedness becomes visible, and the two turn to covering their bodies with leaves.

This sequence shows that exposed nakedness and the effort to cover it are important to the story.

Yakhṣifāni itself does not mean becoming naked.

Nakedness is stated in the preceding clause; khaṣf is the subsequent action.

Does the Qur’an identify the earlier covering’s material?

Commentaries report different accounts of Adam and his spouse’s covering before eating from the tree.

Some mention a luminous covering, others a covering resembling skin or nails.

Ṭā Hā 20:118, however, states only that Adam would not be naked.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:27 likewise speaks of removing their clothing to expose their private parts.

These verses nevertheless do not specify the earlier covering’s precise material.

Reports about light, nails, or a particular kind of garment should therefore not enter the meaning of khaṣf itself.

What the two verses clearly establish is the use of garden leaves after something previously concealed became visible.

Al-Ṭabarī: exegetical reports of fig leaves and the earlier covering, distinct from the verse’s wording

Is khaṣf here only an outward response?

In its direct meaning, khaṣf is a material action: joining leaves and placing them over the body.

It nevertheless occurs in a context of Satan’s deception, crossing a divine prohibition, and a newly exposed condition of Adam and his spouse.

One may therefore reflect on the relationship between a lapse, recognizing its consequences, and trying to cover the body.

Such reflection should nevertheless not replace the word’s direct meaning.

Yakhṣifāni cannot, for example, be translated as “they tried to conceal their sin.”

That rendering removes the physical joining of leaves and substitutes a psychological interpretation for the Arabic verb.

Nor does this verb alone establish that they intended to deceive The God or conceal the truth from Him.

The verses speak of covering the body, rather than lying or denying what happened.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:23, both also say:

رَبَّنَا ظَلَمْنَا أَنْفُسَنَا

Meaning: “Our Lord, we have wronged ourselves.”

This expresses their acknowledgment of wrongdoing and should be distinguished from the preceding act of covering the body.

Do these verses determine every detail of rules about clothing?

These verses report an action in Adam and his spouse’s story.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:26 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:27 broaden the discussion of clothing, covering private parts, and mindfulness through an address to Adam’s children.

Studying the rules and purposes of clothing requires examining those verses and other related passages separately.

Yakhṣifāni alone, however, does not determine the required garment, extent of coverage, fabric, or conditions governing human clothing in every age.

The root itself concerns only joining things.

In this story, the action serves to cover the body.

5. Differences from neighboring roots

KH Ṣ F (khaṣf) compared with S T R (satr): joining and covering

Arabic satr concerns covering or keeping something concealed.

Khaṣf concerns joining and attaching components.

Someone can cover the body with a single piece of cloth without patching anything.

Conversely, someone may join leather pieces to make a sandal without intending to cover the body.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:22 and Ṭā Hā 20:121, both meanings meet in one action: leaves are joined to cover the body.

Lexically, however, one is the action and the other its purpose.

KH Ṣ F (khaṣf) compared with W R Q (waraq): joining and leaves

Waraq names the material Adam and his spouse use in both verses.

Khaṣf is the action performed with that material.

Khaṣf therefore does not mean leaves, nor does waraq mean patching.

Palm, fig, and other leaves may differ in form and properties, but khaṣf itself does not identify the plant species.

The verses say only waraqu al-jannah and name no particular species.

KH Ṣ F (khaṣf) compared with KH R Z (kharz): joining and stitching

Arabic kharz concerns stitching, particularly leather.

Patching sandals can also be accomplished through kharz.

Khaṣf nevertheless has a broader field and is not necessarily restricted to piercing with a tool and passing thread through.

Leaves, for example, may be overlaid and joined without requiring the same method as sandal stitching.

In these verses, “patching” or “joining together” is therefore more precise than “sewing with a needle.”

KH Ṣ F (khaṣf) compared with N S J (nasj): joining and weaving

Nasj concerns weaving and interlacing strands.

Khaṣf can also mean overlaying layers or connecting pieces.

The two fields may approach one another in weaving palm-leaf coverings.

Not every khaṣf is nevertheless nasj: repairing a sandal may involve placing and stitching one piece upon another, rather than weaving strands.

In Adam’s story, the Qur’an uses yakhṣifāni; a particular form of weaving should not therefore be declared the only possible method without evidence.

KH Ṣ F (khaṣf) compared with R Q ʿ (raqʿ): patching and joining

Arabic raqʿ concerns patching fabric or covering a tear.

Early commentaries explain yakhṣifāni with yuraqqiʿāni.

Their proximity is especially clear in patching a sandal or garment.

Khaṣf highlights attachment and overlaying components; raqʿ in its familiar use can emphasize repairing a tear.

The Qur’anic verses describe placing leaves over the body without saying that Adam and his spouse repaired a torn garment.

“Joining together like patches” is therefore preferable to turning the action into clothing repair.

KH Ṣ F (khaṣf) compared with KH W Ṣ (khawṣ): joining and palm leaves

Arabic khawṣ concerns palm leaflets or narrow fibers used for weaving and making objects.

Different containers and mats were made from this material.

Khaṣf can describe joining or weaving these fibers.

Khawṣ and khaṣf thus approach one another in craft and use, while remaining distinct in root and independent meaning.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:22 and Ṭā Hā 20:121 say waraqu al-jannah, rather than khawṣu al-nakhli.

The story’s leaves should therefore not be restricted to palm leaves.

KH Ṣ F (khaṣf) compared with KH S F (khasf): joining and sinking

These roots are very similar in writing but independent.

خ ص ف concerns joining, patching, and overlaying things.

خ س ف concerns sinking, being swallowed by the earth, and the moon growing dark.

Yakhṣifāni has ṣād as its second root consonant; the verse concerns joining leaves.

Yakhsifu instead has sīn as its second root consonant and may concern being swallowed by the earth.

Their similar sounds and spelling should therefore not mix their meanings.

Study of KH S F

KH Ṣ F (khaṣf) compared with KH L Ṭ (khalṭ): joining and mixing

Khalṭ concerns mixing things together.

Some uses of khaṣīf, such as a mixture of two kinds of milk, involve combination and mixing.

Qur’anic khaṣf itself, however, does not mean mixing leaves until their individual identities disappear.

The leaves are joined together and used for covering.

These roots approach one another in some branches, but their particular meaning should be preserved in each context.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Several renderings of yakhṣifāni in Al-Aʿrāf 7:22 and Ṭā Hā 20:121 merit consideration.

“They joined together” retains the root’s fundamental meaning, but may not convey the whole sentence’s image without mentioning the leaves and their placement over the body.

“They patched” approaches a familiar use of khaṣf but may suggest repairing a torn garment.

“They laid upon one another” clarifies the leaves’ overlap but somewhat weakens the element of attachment.

The Persian “they stuck on” is natural and accords with yulṣiqāni in lexical and exegetical explanations. It may nevertheless suggest adhesive, while the verse specifies no fastening method.

For precise lexical explanation, “they joined the leaves together and placed them over themselves” is therefore suitable.

A fluent sentence such as “they began fastening garden leaves over themselves” is also defensible, provided the explanation clarifies joining and placing leaves over the body, without asserting adhesive or a particular tool.

The currently installed Persian text of Forqan at Al-Aʿrāf 7:22 uses wording equivalent to “they began fastening garden leaves over themselves.” Ṭā Hā 20:121 instead uses wording equivalent to “they began covering themselves with garden leaves.”

Both convey part of the event. Lexically, the first is closer to attachment, while the second highlights the purpose of covering.

The root study should distinguish these directions and leave installed translations unchanged without a separate request.

Brief root meaning and meaning in each occurrence

يَخْصِفَانِ — Al-Aʿrāf 7:22

Root: خ ص ف

Grammar: Active imperfect verb, third-person masculine dual, Form I; its subjects are Adam and his spouse. The retained nūn marks the indicative, while the verbal clause serves as ṭafiqā’s predicate in the accusative syntactic position.

Brief root meaning: Joining and attaching things to one another, especially overlaying and patching pieces.

Meaning in this verse: After their private parts became visible, Adam and his spouse joined garden leaves together and placed them over their bodies.

يَخْصِفَانِ — Ṭā Hā 20:121

Root: خ ص ف

Grammar: Active imperfect verb, third-person masculine dual, Form I, in wa-ṭafiqā yakhṣifāni. The retained nūn marks the indicative, while the verbal clause serves as ṭafiqā’s predicate in the accusative syntactic position.

Brief root meaning: Joining and attaching things, especially overlaying and patching pieces.

Meaning in this verse: After eating from the tree and their nakedness becoming visible, the two began joining garden leaves together and placing them over themselves to cover their bodies.

The verb has the same form and meaning in both verses.

The surrounding narrative differs, rather than the independent meaning of yakhṣifāni.

7. Conclusion

The Arabic root خ ص ف concerns joining, attaching, and overlaying things.

Mending sandals and attaching leather pieces are among its clearest early uses. The family also supplies the name of the leather-piercing tool and of containers and coverings woven from palm leaves.

In another branch, khaṣīf and akhṣaf describe things combining two colors. Ibn Fāris connects this too with one thing joining another, while distinguishing colors occurring together from physical pieces being stitched.

A particular use concerning when a she-camel gives birth is also recorded, without a clear or certain connection to the main meaning.

The Qur’an uses the root only twice, both as yakhṣifāni.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:22 and Ṭā Hā 20:121, after their private parts or nakedness become visible, Adam and his spouse begin joining garden leaves together and placing them over their bodies.

Ṭafiqā expresses the action’s beginning, yakhṣifāni the joining of components, ʿalayhimā their placement over both bodies, and min waraqi al-jannati the material used.

The purpose is covering what had become exposed; khaṣf itself nevertheless does not mean covering private parts or concealing sin.

The Qur’an also specifies neither the tree, the number of leaves, nor the fastening tool. Reports concerning fig leaves or the covering’s shape should not enter direct translation without evidence.

Final definition: The root خ ص ف denotes joining and attaching one thing to another, especially by overlaying, patching, or weaving components. In Al-Aʿrāf 7:22 and Ṭā Hā 20:121, yakhṣifāni describes Adam and his spouse joining garden leaves and placing them over their bodies. Covering the body is the contextual purpose of the action, rather than the root’s independent meaning. Brief root meaning: Joining and attaching things to one another, especially overlaying, patching, or weaving pieces.
