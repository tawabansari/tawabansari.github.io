Naqʿ in early Arabic and the Qur’an: from the dust raised by horses and rising voices to collected water, soaking, and relief from thirst

The three-consonant root N Q ʿ has several semantic branches in Arabic. One familiar use is naqʿ, meaning rising dust: dust stirred by horses, groups of animals moving, or vigorous movement over the ground. Another branch concerns water collecting and remaining in one place, soaking something in liquid, and quenching thirst. A further use concerns a raised voice or cry.

These meanings are visibly different. Dust rises from the ground and disperses through the air, whereas water collects and remains in a hollow or vessel. A loud sound is different again from the movement of soil and water. Without historical evidence, all these uses cannot be derived from a single initial physical image.

N Q ʿ occurs only once in the Qur’an: naqʿan in Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:4.

فَأَثَرْنَ بِهِ نَقْعًا

fa-atharna bihi naqʿan

“Then they stirred up dust there.”

The verse belongs to a sequence depicting rapid movement, sparks, a morning raid, and arrival in the midst of a gathering. In this context, naqʿ refers to dust rising into the air through movement over the ground.

To understand the root precisely, its physical and linguistic uses must first be examined. The reasons for preferring dust over the other recorded meanings in Al-ʿĀdiyāt can then be established.

1. Forms and semantic branches

Important Arabic forms include:

  • نَقَعَ (naqaʿa): in water usage, to collect and remain in a place; in other uses, to quench thirst or, of a voice, to rise.
  • نَقْع (naqʿ): rising dust; also collected water and loud sound in certain uses.
  • نُقُوع (nuqūʿ): water collecting or remaining; elsewhere, becoming satisfied with water or having thirst quenched.
  • نَقِيع (naqīʿ): collected water, a well full of water, or a drink prepared by soaking materials such as raisins.
  • نَقُوع (naqūʿ): something soaked in water to prepare a drink or medicine; in some uses, the drink itself.
  • مَنْقَع (manqaʿ): a place or vessel where water collects or something is soaked.
  • مِنْقَع and مِنْقَعَة (minqaʿ and minqaʿah): a vessel for soaking materials in water.
  • اِسْتَنْقَعَ (istanqaʿa): to collect and remain in a place; of a person, also to stay in water to cool down.
  • أَنْقَعَ (anqaʿa): to soak something in water and leave it there for a time.
  • نُقَاعَة (nuqāʿah): water in which something has been soaked.
  • نَاقِع (nāqiʿ): in some uses, water that quenches thirst; also a description of potent, deadly poison.
  • نَقِيعَة (naqīʿah): in certain early uses, food prepared for a guest or someone returning from a journey; elsewhere, an animal slaughtered from booty before its division.
  • نَقَّاع (naqqāʿ): in a social use, someone who boasts about himself and his abilities beyond what he possesses.

Other restricted lexical uses are also recorded, including names for water-collecting ground, poison accumulating in a snake’s fangs, and a change in facial color.

Of all these forms, only naqʿan occurs in the Qur’an. The variety of Arabic uses should not cause non-Qur’anic derivatives to be counted as separate Qur’anic forms.

Does N Q ʿ have a single semantic core?

One lexical analysis of this family distinguishes two principal branches.

The first concerns a liquid settling in its place, as when water collects and remains in a hollow.

The second concerns sound, as when a cry rises.

Alongside these branches, naqʿ meaning rising dust is also recorded in Arabic lexicons and early poetry.

Figurative connections can be proposed between some of these meanings. Dust and a loud sound, for example, may both rise from their usual location and become apparent. Dust can also fill a space, just as water collects in a hollow.

These resemblances, however, do not establish the history of derivation.

Defining the root solely as “collecting” does not adequately explain rising dust and raised voices. Defining it solely as “rising” overlooks water collecting and remaining.

A more precise account therefore recognizes the recorded branches separately, without forcing them under one vague definition.

2. Naqʿ in early Arabic: dust and loud sound

Rising dust

One of the best-known meanings of naqʿ is dust rising into the air through movement over the ground.

Arabic says:

أَثَارَتِ الْخَيْلُ النَّقْعَ

athārati al-khaylu al-naqʿa

Translation: “The horses stirred up dust.”

Naqʿ here is the dust itself; movement and stirring it up are expressed by the accompanying verb.

Lexical descriptions particularly record naqʿ for dust that has risen and become visible. Thus, although “dust” is its direct equivalent, many uses evoke soil lifted from the ground and spread through the air.

Naqʿ itself does not necessarily identify the cause. Moving horses, wind, or another factor may raise the dust.

Early poetic witnesses: horses and dust

ʿĀmir ibn al-Ṭufayl, the Arab poet and warrior who lived through the pre-Islamic period and the beginning of Islam, says in describing horses and armed confrontation:

يَضْرِبُونَ الْكُمَاةَ فِي ثَوْرَةِ النَّقْعِ
إِذَا حَرْبُهُمْ بَدَتْ وَاسْجَهَرَّتْ

yaḍribūna al-kumāta fī thawrat al-naqʿi / idhā ḥarbuhum badat wa-isjaharrat

Translation: “When their war begins and grows fierce, they strike the armored warriors amid the rising dust.”

Thawrat al-naqʿ here refers to the rising and stirring of dust on the battlefield.

The following line also mentions swirling dust and the sound of horses neighing. This association shows naqʿ describing a concrete scene of movement, dust, and confrontation.

Another poem by the same poet says:

لِلْمُقَرَّبَاتِ غُدُوٌّ حِينَ نُحْضِرُهَا
وَغَارَةٌ تَسْتَثِيرُ النَّقْعَ فِي رَهَجِ

li-al-muqarrabāti ghuduwwun ḥīna nuḥḍiruhā / wa-ghāratun tastathīru al-naqʿa fī rahaji

Translation: “The prized, well-kept horses have a morning outing when we set them galloping, and a raid that stirs up dust in a haze.”

Tastathīru al-naqʿ here means stirring up dust.

The witness matters because it shows the use of naqʿ for dust in Arabic poetry, directly connected with horses moving.

These poems are attributed to ʿĀmir ibn al-Ṭufayl. Since he also lived through the beginning of Islam, however, not all his poetry can be called conclusively pre-Qur’anic without dating each line.

Naqʿ and ʿajāj

Arabic poetry sometimes places naqʿ and ʿajāj together in a description.

Both concern dust, but ʿajāj often describes a dense, turbulent cloud swirling in the air and potentially impairing visibility.

Naqʿ, in its clear uses, refers to the dust that has risen.

Their association should not establish a definite rule for stages of dust formation, such as naqʿ always being the first stage and ʿajāj the next.

A poet may juxtapose two related words to create a battle scene without intending to define scientific stages in the movement of soil particles.

A raised voice or cry

Another branch of N Q ʿ concerns a voice rising.

Arabic records:

نَقَعَ الصَّوْتُ

naqaʿa al-ṣawtu

Translation: “The voice rose.”

Naqīʿ and naqʿ are also recorded in some uses for a cry or loud sound.

Labīd ibn Rabīʿah says:

فَمَتَى يَنْقَعْ صُرَاخٌ صَادِقٌ
يُحْلِبُوهَا ذَاتَ جَرْسٍ وَزَجَلْ

fa-matā yanqaʿ ṣurākhun ṣādiqun / yuḥlibūhā dhāta jarsin wa-zajal

Translation: “Whenever a genuine cry is raised, they rouse war with ringing and clamor.”

Yanqaʿ here refers to the cry rising.

This use shows that N Q ʿ in Arabic is not confined to dust or water.

The precise date of the line’s composition must nevertheless be distinguished from its attribution to Labīd, who lived through both the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods.

Variants are also reported in part of the second half-line.

These witnesses establish the root’s sound-related branch, but do not prove that naqʿ means a cry in every text, particularly in Al-ʿĀdiyāt.

3. Collected water, soaking, and quenching thirst

Water collecting and remaining

Arabic uses naqaʿa al-māʾ for water that collects in its location and remains for a time.

For example:

نَقَعَ الْمَاءُ فِي الْمَسِيلِ

naqaʿa al-māʾu fī al-masīli

Translation: “The water collected and remained in the flood channel.”

Also:

اِسْتَنْقَعَ الْمَاءُ فِي الْغَدِيرِ

istanqaʿa al-māʾu fī al-ghadīri

Translation: “The water collected and remained in the pool.”

The main concern here is not rapid water movement, but its collection and settlement in a location where it can remain.

In this same field, manqaʿ denotes a place where water collects.

Lexical accounts also record naqʿ and naqīʿ for collected water and, in some cases, a well full of water.

Naqʿ itself does not mean contaminated or putrid water. Water may remain for a time and change, but changes in color, smell, or quality are possible consequences of standing, rather than necessary meanings of the root.

Soaking materials in water

Another use of anqaʿa and naqaʿa concerns putting something in water and leaving it there.

For example:

أَنْقَعَ الزَّبِيبَ فِي الْمَاءِ

anqaʿa al-zabība fī al-māʾi

Translation: “He soaked the raisins in water.”

The raisins remain in the water for a time, and some of their constituents pass into it.

Naqīʿ and naqūʿ are also recorded for a drink resulting from such soaking.

Soaking a medicine or plant in water to prepare a drink or medicinal substance was also known in Arabic usage.

This use makes the ordinary modern idea of soaking readily understandable.

It does not mean that every naqaʿa in Arabic denotes fermentation, intoxication, or the production of a particular drink.

Soaking is one action; chemical change, fermentation, and whether the product intoxicates are separate questions depending on the material and conditions.

Quenching thirst

Another branch of this semantic field concerns becoming satisfied with water.

Arabic says:

نَقَعَ الْمَاءُ الْعَطَشَ

naqaʿa al-māʾu al-ʿaṭasha

Translation: “The water quenched the thirst.”

Also:

شَرِبَ حَتَّى نَقَعَ

shariba ḥattā naqaʿa

Translation: “He drank until his thirst was satisfied.”

Naqaʿa here concerns relief from thirst.

This use can be explained through water remaining and taking effect in the body and relieving the need to drink. Such an explanation should not, however, be presented as a conclusive historical etymology.

The following line is attributed to Jarīr, the Umayyad poet. It has also been attributed to Labīd, with variants in certain words and vowel markings; neither a particular attribution nor one transmitted reading should therefore be treated as undisputed:

لَوْ شِئْتِ قَدْ نَقَعَ الْفُؤَادُ بِشَرْبَةٍ
تَدَعُ الصَّوَادِيَ لَا يَجِدْنَ غَلِيلَا

law shiʾti qad naqaʿa al-fuʾādu bi-sharbatin / tadaʿu al-ṣawādiya lā yajidna ghalīlā

Translation: “Had you wished, the heart would have been refreshed by a draught that left the thirsty feeling no more burning thirst.”

Naqaʿa here is associated with the subsiding of thirst and the calming of a need.

The use has no direct connection in this sentence with dust or a rising voice.

The root study should therefore recognize it as an actual branch of the family, rather than remove it to preserve an apparently unified definition.

Nāqiʿ poison

Summun nāqiʿun is also an established expression in early Arabic.

It denotes poison with a powerful, deadly effect. Some lexical explanations connect nāqiʿ poison with its collecting and settling in a snake’s fangs.

Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī, the pre-Islamic poet, says:

فَبِتُّ كَأَنِّي سَاوَرَتْنِي ضَئِيلَةٌ
مِنَ الرُّقْشِ فِي أَنْيَابِهَا السُّمُّ نَاقِعُ

fa-bittu ka-annī sāwaratnī ḍaʾīlatun / mina al-ruqshi fī anyābihā al-summu nāqiʿu

Translation: “I spent the night as though a slender, mottled snake had sprung at me, deadly poison lodged in its fangs.”

Nāqiʿ here describes the poison.

The use shows that the family’s meaning is not confined to drinking water and relief from thirst.

Poison described this way does not establish that naqʿ inherently means killing. Deadliness comes from the substance and this particular adjectival use.

Naqīʿah: hospitality food and returning from a journey

Early Arabic also records naqīʿah for food prepared on the occasion of someone’s return from a journey.

In certain uses, it denotes an animal slaughtered from booty before its division.

Lexical explanations differ on how these uses relate to the root’s semantic core.

Naqīʿah as hospitality food should therefore not be interpreted without evidence as “something soaked in water.”

Nor should these social uses be imported into the interpretation of Al-ʿĀdiyāt, whose context involves neither hospitality nor preparing food.

4. Qur’anic usage: Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:4

فَأَثَرْنَ بِهِ نَقْعًا

fa-atharna bihi naqʿan

Translation: “Then they stirred up dust there.”

Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:4

This is the only Qur’anic occurrence of N Q ʿ.

Understanding it requires reading the surrounding verses together:

وَالْعَادِيَاتِ ضَبْحًا

wa-al-ʿādiyāti ḍabḥan

“By those racing, panting.”

Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:1

فَالْمُورِيَاتِ قَدْحًا

fa-al-mūriyāti qadḥan

“Then those striking sparks through impact.”

Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:2

فَالْمُغِيرَاتِ صُبْحًا

fa-al-mughīrāti ṣubḥan

“Then those raiding at dawn.”

Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:3

فَأَثَرْنَ بِهِ نَقْعًا

fa-atharna bihi naqʿan

“Then they stirred up dust there.”

Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:4

فَوَسَطْنَ بِهِ جَمْعًا

fa-wasaṭna bihi jamʿan

“Then they entered the midst of a gathering there.”

Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:5

These five verses provide a continuous picture of rapid movement, impact, a raid, and arrival in the midst of a gathering.

In the familiar reading, the scene concerns horses moving rapidly and raising dust beneath their feet during a raid.

Some early interpretations also discuss camels moving, particularly in connection with pilgrims traveling between the stations of the rites.

The disagreement concerns the identity of those moving. In either reading, naqʿ retains the meaning of dust raised through movement over the ground.

The meaning of naqʿan

Naqʿ here denotes dust.

This meaning is recorded in Arabic lexicons and fits the sentence’s construction and the image of rapid movement.

In the verse, dust is what is stirred up. The use fits the familiar experience of horses and other animals moving over dry ground.

The dryness of the ground, the soil’s color, and the exact amount of dust, however, are not stated by naqʿ itself.

Movement over dry, loose ground may produce much dust, but that results from environmental conditions rather than the root’s lexical meaning.

The role of fa-atharna

Atharna comes from TH W R and Form IV. Here it concerns stirring something up and setting it in motion.

Two elements of meaning must therefore be distinguished in fa-atharna bihi naqʿan:

Atharna expresses the act of stirring up.

Naqʿan specifies what is stirred up: dust.

This matters because naqʿ itself does not mean “stirring up” here.

Describing naqʿ as rising dust fits its established use, but the sentence also explicitly expresses the act of raising and stirring it through atharna.

Grammatically, naqʿan is an accusative noun and the object of atharna.

Naqʿan here is the direct object because it names the dust that is stirred up, rather than the act of stirring itself. Different roots alone do not rule out an absolute object: a verbal noun synonymous with the verb can also occupy that position or stand in for it.

What does the pronoun in bihi refer to?

In fa-atharna bihi naqʿan, the pronoun -hi in bihi is masculine singular.

Early grammatical and interpretive explanations offer two principal possibilities.

In one reading, the pronoun refers to the place of movement or the raid: those moving stir up dust there.

In another, it refers to the preceding rapid movement or raid: dust arises through that movement.

The difference affects the pronoun’s referent, but does not change the meaning of naqʿ itself.

In either case, dust has risen through movement in the scene the verses describe.

“Then they stirred up dust there” therefore fits the locative reading of bihi without adding a particular place name. It is not neutral between every reading; a causal reading may be conveyed by “through it” or “as a result of it.”

Could naqʿ here mean a cry?

Since Arabic also records naqʿ for a loud sound or cry, another possibility in the interpretive tradition takes the verse to concern cries and clamor being raised.

As a matter of recorded lexical meaning, the reading is not entirely unsupported.

A meaning’s presence in a dictionary, however, does not suffice to prefer it in a verse.

Several indications here strengthen the dust reading.

First, the preceding verses describe rapid movement and a raid.

Second, atharna can clearly refer to stirring up dust.

Third, early Arabic poetry records ithārat al-naqʿ precisely for the dust raised by horses moving.

Fourth, the next verse concerns reaching the midst of a gathering, continuing the image of movement.

Dust is therefore the preferable meaning in this verse.

“Cries and clamor” can be recognized as a lexical and interpretive possibility, but the available evidence does not place it on equal footing with dust.

Nor does a figurative connection between dust, raised voices, or collected water by itself justify changing the verse’s meaning.

Must naqʿ mean battle dust?

Arabic poetry often uses naqʿ in descriptions of war, raids, and horses moving.

Naqʿ itself, however, does not mean war.

Dust may also rise as a group of animals moves in an ordinary way.

A martial sense in Al-ʿĀdiyāt thus comes from words such as al-mughīrāt, the scene’s construction, and the reading involving a raid, rather than from N Q ʿ itself.

Nor does its use in battle poetry establish that this Qur’anic verse necessarily names or endorses a particular historical battle.

The verses present a picture of movement, then turn to the human being, ingratitude, and reckoning.

Identifying the movement with a precise historical event requires independent evidence.

5. Naqʿ in the structure of Al-ʿĀdiyāt

After the first five verses, the Qur’an says:

إِنَّ الْإِنسَانَ لِرَبِّهِ لَكَنُودٌ

inna al-insāna li-rabbihi la-kanūdun

“Indeed, the human being is deeply ungrateful to his Lord.”

Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:6

The continuation discusses the human being’s witness to this condition, intense attachment to good or wealth, and the disclosure of what is in graves and breasts.

This shift from outward movement to the human condition and responsibility is a significant feature of the surah.

Does the dust have a symbolic meaning?

The first five verses present rapid movement, impact, a raid, and dust. The text then turns to the human being, attachment to wealth, and reckoning.

A reader may reflect on this sequence in connection with human haste through life, efforts to attain desires, or the consequences of conduct.

Such reflection, however, should not change the lexical meaning of naqʿ.

Naqʿ in the fourth verse means dust, rather than heedlessness, inner turmoil, greed, or ingratitude.

If such concepts can be defensibly connected with the surah’s opening image, that belongs in a study of context and reflection.

For the root study, it is enough to establish naqʿ as part of the image of movement, without identifying its meaning with the ethical subject of the following verses.

Is dust a sign of destruction?

Vigorous movement over the ground can create dust. In certain circumstances, such movement may also involve damage or destruction.

Naqʿ itself, however, does not mean destruction.

Nor does the verse contain a separate verb meaning to destroy the ground or ruin a cultivated field.

Rising dust therefore does not establish that those moving necessarily devastated the land or caused a particular harm.

This is another example of why literal meaning must be distinguished from an event’s possible consequences.

6. Comparison with related roots

N Q ʿ (naqʿ) and GH B R (ghubār)

Ghubār is a general name for fine particles that may settle on surfaces or disperse through the air.

In its soil-related use, naqʿ more particularly concerns dust that has risen.

Every naqʿ in this sense is thus dust, but not all dust has necessarily just risen from the ground through movement.

For example, dust long settled on an object is described as ghubār, whereas naqʿ is more natural in a scene of horses moving.

This distinction should nevertheless not become an absolute rule for every Arabic use. The words overlap in some contexts.

N Q ʿ (naqʿ) and ʿ J J (ʿajāj)

ʿAjāj concerns dense, rising, swirling dust.

This is close to naqʿ, especially in descriptions of horses moving and battle scenes.

In established uses, however, ʿajāj may place greater emphasis on density and turbulence.

Naqʿ refers to the rising dust itself.

This is a tendency. The presence of ʿajāj in a poem alone does not establish that the dust must be more intense than naqʿ or have arisen after it.

N Q ʿ (naqʿ) and TH W R (thawr)

In Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:4, atharna comes from TH W R.

Some uses of TH W R concern rising, being stirred up, and being set in motion.

N Q ʿ in the same verse refers to dust.

The roots thus have different roles in the sentence: one expresses the action, the other what is stirred up through that movement.

Defining naqʿ as “stirring up dust” without distinguishing these elements is therefore lexically imprecise.

Study of TH W R

N Q ʿ (naqʿ) and R W Y (rawiya)

In its water-related branch, N Q ʿ sometimes refers to quenching thirst and becoming satisfied with water.

Rawiya also means becoming satisfied with water in Arabic.

The words are very close in certain uses.

N Q ʿ, however, has other uses, such as water collecting in a hollow and soaking materials in liquid.

R W Y, in turn, has its own semantic family and independent constructions.

Their proximity in the sense of thirst being satisfied does not make every use of the roots identical.

N Q ʿ (naqʿ) and N F ʿ (nafʿ)

The roots sound similar, but differ in meaning.

In its water-related branch, N Q ʿ can express relief from thirst, whereas N F ʿ concerns benefit and usefulness.

Water that quenches thirst benefits the drinker, but benefit is not an independent meaning of naqʿ.

Rising dust may likewise be useful, harmful, or unrelated to benefit and harm.

Shared instances therefore establish neither an etymological connection nor a unified meaning between the families.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

The Persian equivalent should be chosen according to the semantic branch and context.

In the dust-related use, “dust” is the basic equivalent.

For naqʿ in a scene of horses moving, “rising dust” or “dust stirred up” may be suitable in the explanation.

In water-related uses, equivalents meaning “collected,” “remained in a place,” and “collected water” are natural.

For anqaʿa al-zabība fī al-māʾ, “he soaked the raisins in water” is suitable.

For naqaʿa al-māʾu al-ʿaṭasha, “the water quenched the thirst” is appropriate.

In the sound-related use, “the voice rose” or “a cry went up” is clearer.

These equivalents should not all be merged into a single definition or translation.

Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:4

فَأَثَرْنَ بِهِ نَقْعًا

fa-atharna bihi naqʿan

Proposed translation:

“Then they stirred up dust there.”

This rendering is brief and faithful to the wording.

“Dust” conveys naqʿ.

“Stirred up” comes from atharna.

“There” reflects the locative reading of bihi without adding a particular place name; a causal reading is conveyed more clearly by an expression such as “as a result of it.”

To clarify the movement in the explanation, one can state that the context involves dust rising from the ground through rapid movement.

Expressions such as “with the horses’ hooves, they raised much dust on the battlefield” are unsuitable as the basic translation: they add several explanatory elements not explicitly named in the fourth verse itself.

Brief root meaning and meaning in the verse

Brief root meaning: in Qur’anic use, rising dust; in other Arabic branches, water collecting and remaining, soaking, quenching thirst, and a rising voice.

Meaning in Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:4: dust stirred up through movement; in context, dust rising from the ground during rapid movement.

Collected water or a raised voice should not be imposed on the verse, since the contextual evidence strengthens the dust meaning.

8. Conclusion

N Q ʿ in Arabic has several branches whose meanings should not all be reduced to a single physical image.

In one branch, naqʿ names rising dust. This use is familiar in Arabic poetry, particularly concerning horses moving and scenes of confrontation.

Another branch concerns water collecting and remaining, soaking materials in liquid, and quenching thirst. Naqīʿ, naqūʿ, manqaʿ, and istanqaʿa belong to this field.

A further branch concerns a rising voice or cry, recorded in early poetry and lexicons.

Certain derivatives, such as nāqiʿ describing poison and naqīʿah naming a hospitality meal, have developed their own particular uses. Without historical evidence, these meanings should not be definitively derived from another branch.

Only naqʿan in Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:4 occurs in the Qur’an.

Dust here fits established Arabic usage and the context of rapid movement. Atharna expresses stirring up, while naqʿ is the dust itself that rises.

Although Arabic also records naqʿ for loud sound, the lexical, poetic, and contextual evidence favors “dust” in this verse.

The meaning of dust must also be distinguished from interpreting the whole scene in Al-ʿĀdiyāt. Whether those moving are horses or camels, the circumstances of their movement, and the connection between the surah’s opening image and human ingratitude are separate questions from the lexical definition of naqʿ.

Final definition: N Q ʿ has several recognized semantic branches in Arabic: rising dust; liquid collecting and remaining, with uses involving soaking and satisfaction with water; and a rising voice. Without historical evidence, these branches cannot be reduced to a single definite origin. In Qur’anic usage, naqʿ denotes rising dust and, in Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:4, names what is stirred up from the ground during rapid movement. “Dust” is therefore the appropriate basic equivalent for Forqan.
