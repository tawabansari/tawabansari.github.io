The Qur’anic Root GH Ḍ B

Intense displeasure and a response to what is unacceptable

The root غ ض ب (GH Ḍ B) belongs to the Qur’anic field of anger and intense displeasure: a state or stance arising in response to a person, action, or situation regarded as unacceptable. The Qur’an uses this word of both human beings and The God. This dual application prevents us, from the outset, from defining ghaḍab through the bodily signs of human anger.

In early Arabic, ghaḍab stood in opposition to riḍā, “approval” or “being pleased,” and was also associated with intensity and strength. Within this field, ghaḍbah (غَضْبة) is recorded as a hard rock, while ghaḍab was explained in terms of intense displeasure, or sakhaṭ. This early account makes a semantic connection between physical solidity and the intensity of a response understandable: just as a hard object is neither soft nor pliable, ghaḍab is an intense, aroused stance toward something that is not accepted. Nevertheless, this is an early semantic explanation; it does not establish that ghaḍab historically began as a name for a rock and was subsequently transferred to a psychological state.

The root GH Ḍ B occurs 24 times in 21 Qur’anic verses: six instances of the basic verb ghaḍiba (غَضِبَ), fourteen of ghaḍab (غَضَب), two of ghaḍbān (غَضْبَان), one of maghḍūb (مَغْضُوب), and one of mughāḍib (مُغَاضِب). Most occurrences concern the ghaḍab of The God, but the human uses are especially important for determining the root’s basic meaning.

Human ghaḍab: an intense state that does not require violence

Ghaḍab was a familiar word before the Qur’an. In pre-Islamic poetry, too, someone becomes angry on account of another person, and the language distinguishes ghaḍiba ʿalayhi (غضب عليه), being angry with someone, from ghaḍiba lahu (غضب له), becoming angry on someone’s behalf. A person can be angry with someone or become angry in another’s defense. These uses show that ghaḍab is not simply an aimless outburst: it normally has an object and a cause.

The Qur’an clearly depicts human ghaḍab in Moses. After returning to his people, he is described as “غَضْبَانَ أَسِفًا” (ghaḍbāna asifan). The juxtaposition of ghaḍbān and asifan matters: the Qur’an has chosen two words, and one should not be made a translation of the other. Ghaḍbān describes the intensity of anger; asaf belongs to a separate linguistic family that requires its own examination.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:154, this state subsides: “وَلَمَّا سَكَتَ عَنْ مُوسَى الْغَضَبُ أَخَذَ الْأَلْوَاحَ” (wa-lammā sakata ʿan Mūsā l-ghaḍabu akhadha l-alwāḥ): when Moses’ anger subsided, he took up the tablets. The verb sakata, “fell silent,” depicts anger as an aroused force that intensifies and then becomes quiet. This image shows that ghaḍab is not a fixed personality trait.

Ash-Shūrā 42:37 reveals another aspect: “وَإِذَا مَا غَضِبُوا هُمْ يَغْفِرُونَ” (wa-idhā mā ghaḍibū hum yaghfirūn): when they become angry, they forgive. The Qur’an does not say that a good person never becomes angry. Anger itself is a response; what follows it remains a matter of choice.

This verse also prevents us from making “revenge” a necessary part of the meaning of ghaḍab. A person can become angry and forgive. Violence, revenge, and aggression may result from anger, but they are not included in the definition of the root itself.

Descriptions of human anger in terms of boiling blood, bodily changes, or physical arousal may describe human experience, but these elements cannot be made part of the meaning of GH Ḍ B itself. The Qur’an also uses this root of The God; the word does not itself transfer human physiological characteristics to its divine subject.

The ghaḍab of The God: a stance of intense disapproval, distinct from punishment itself

The divine uses of the root show that Qur’anic ghaḍab is not merely a passing feeling. It is a severe stance toward conduct that can have real consequences.

An-Nisāʾ 4:93 says of intentional killing: “وَغَضِبَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِ وَلَعَنَهُ وَأَعَدَّ لَهُ عَذَابًا عَظِيمًا” (wa-ghaḍiba llāhu ʿalayhi wa-laʿanahu wa-aʿadda lahu ʿadhāban ʿaẓīmā): The God is angry with him, excludes him, and prepares a tremendous punishment for him. Ghaḍab, laʿn, and punishment are three separate components of the sentence. Al-Fatḥ 48:6 repeats this construction: “وَغَضِبَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِمْ وَلَعَنَهُمْ وَأَعَدَّ لَهُمْ جَهَنَّمَ” (wa-ghaḍiba llāhu ʿalayhim wa-laʿanahum wa-aʿadda lahum jahannam): The God is angry with them, excludes them, and prepares Jahannam for them. Ash-Shūrā 42:16 makes the distinction even more explicit: “وَعَلَيْهِمْ غَضَبٌ وَلَهُمْ عَذَابٌ شَدِيدٌ” (wa-ʿalayhim ghaḍabun wa-lahum ʿadhābun shadīd): upon them is wrath, and for them is severe punishment.

These constructions prevent us from translating ghaḍab simply as “to punish.” Punishment can be a consequence of ghaḍab, but the Qur’an distinguishes the two. The same applies to laʿn: as discussed under the root L AYN N (L ʿ N), laʿn means exclusion and distancing, not anger itself.

Ṭā Hā 20:81 depicts cause and consequence through another image: “وَلَا تَطْغَوْا فِيهِ فَيَحِلَّ عَلَيْكُمْ غَضَبِي وَمَنْ يَحْلِلْ عَلَيْهِ غَضَبِي فَقَدْ هَوَىٰ” (wa-lā taṭghaw fīhi fa-yaḥilla ʿalaykum ghaḍabī wa-man yaḥlil ʿalayhi ghaḍabī fa-qad hawā). First comes transgression; then wrath descends “upon” the person; after that comes fa-qad hawā, “he has fallen.” Wrath and falling are not identical: falling is the consequence of coming into that condition.

The next verse immediately adds: “وَإِنِّي لَغَفَّارٌ لِمَنْ تَابَ وَآمَنَ وَعَمِلَ صَالِحًا ثُمَّ اهْتَدَىٰ” (wa-innī la-ghaffārun li-man tāba wa-āmana wa-ʿamila ṣāliḥan thumma htadā): I am indeed abundantly forgiving toward whoever turns back, believes, acts rightly, and then follows guidance. This juxtaposition also prevents us from defining ghaḍab, for every living person, as a condition from which return is impossible.

The connection with the root B W ʾ is also significant. The Qur’an repeatedly speaks of people who “بَاءُوا بِغَضَبٍ مِنَ اللَّهِ” (bāʾū bi-ghaḍabin mina llāh), or someone who “بَاءَ بِغَضَبٍ مِنَ اللَّهِ” (bāʾa bi-ghaḍabin mina llāh): they or he returned with wrath from The God. As discussed under B W ʾ, a person returns at the end of a course with the consequence of their conduct. In this construction, ghaḍab is not only the state of the one who is angry; it is a condition that comes upon a person within the relationship between human conduct and divine judgment.

Accordingly, the shared core can still be retained in divine usage: intense displeasure and an opposing stance toward what is unacceptable. The difference is that human bodily characteristics and emotional instability must not be added to this usage.

Ghaḍab alongside sakhaṭ, ghayẓ, laʿn, and ʿadhāb

The Qur’an has several words within the field of displeasure and negative response. Translating all of them as “anger” reduces the distinctions within the text.

Sakhaṭ (سخط) is among the closest. Āl ʿImrān 3:162 sets “رِضْوَانَ اللَّهِ” (riḍwāna llāh), the approval of The God, against “سَخَطٍ مِنَ اللَّهِ” (sakhaṭin mina llāh), displeasure from The God. Muḥammad 47:28 speaks of people who followed what provoked displeasure and disliked His approval. Sakhaṭ is particularly close to disapproval and finding something displeasing; ghaḍab, in its uses, displays greater intensity and arousal. Arabic sources sometimes explain one through the other, so this distinction is best retained as a semantic tendency rather than an absolute boundary.

Ghayẓ (غيظ) is also close, but its Qur’anic imagery differs. Āl ʿImrān 3:134 says “الْكَاظِمِينَ الْغَيْظَ” (al-kāẓimīna l-ghayẓ): those who contain their anger. Ghayẓ is something a person holds in and restrains. At-Tawbah 9:15 speaks of “وَيُذْهِبْ غَيْظَ قُلُوبِهِمْ” (wa-yudhhib ghayẓa qulūbihim): removing the anger of their hearts. Ghayẓ is in the heart and can be removed. In this respect, ghayẓ emphasizes the internal pressure and agitation of anger, whereas ghaḍab is a broader designation for the intense state and stance itself. This, too, is a tendency, not a rule separating every occurrence without exception.

Laʿn (لعن) is exclusion and distancing, and it can follow ghaḍab. ʿAdhāb (عذاب) is suffering and punishment inflicted upon a person, and it too can be a consequence of ghaḍab. By placing these words together, the Qur’an prevents us from reducing them to a single meaning.

These distinctions matter for translation. If “غضب الله عليهم ولعنهم وأعد لهم جهنم” (ghaḍiba llāhu ʿalayhim wa-laʿanahum wa-aʿadda lahum jahannam) is rendered using separate expressions for “anger,” “cursing,” and “punishment,” but all three mean only “punishment” in the reader’s mind, part of the verse’s lexical relationships disappears: first the stance of intense disapproval, then exclusion, then the punitive outcome.

Ghaḍbān, maghḍūb, and mughāḍib

Three Qur’anic forms within this family also present three different perspectives through their grammatical structure.

Ghaḍbān (غضبان) occurs twice, both times describing Moses. In Arabic, this pattern often expresses an intense state and a sense of fullness. In Moses’ context, it depicts someone in whom anger has become intense.

Maghḍūb (مغضوب) is a passive participle. Al-Fātiḥah says: “غَيْرِ الْمَغْضُوبِ عَلَيْهِمْ” (ghayri l-maghḍūbi ʿalayhim). Morphologically, the expression refers to those upon whom anger has come. The word itself is not the name of any people, religion, or historical group. Its referents must be examined through Qur’anic context; the name of a particular group should not be inserted into the lexical translation of maghḍūb.

Mughāḍiban (مغاضبا) occurs only in the story of Dhū n-Nūn: “وَذَا النُّونِ إِذْ ذَهَبَ مُغَاضِبًا” (wa-dhā n-nūni idh dhahaba mughāḍiban), “And Dhū n-Nūn, when he went away in anger” (Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:87). This is an active participle of form III, the mufāʿalah pattern. In Arabic, ghāḍaba (غاضب) can express a dispute and anger between two parties, or leaving and distancing oneself in a state of angry opposition.

The significant point is that the Qur’an supplies no object for mughāḍiban. The verse says neither mughāḍiban rabbahu (مغاضبا ربه), “angry with his Lord,” nor mughāḍiban qawmahu (مغاضبا قومه), “angry with his people.” Adding either to a translation is an interpretive choice, not a translation of the word itself.

From the expression alone, we can say only that Dhū n-Nūn went away in a state of angry opposition and displeasure. Exactly who the other party to this dispute or anger was must be assessed through separate evidence.

This is a clear example of how an object added in translation can lead the reader toward a particular interpretation before the evidence has been examined.

Translation challenges and conclusion

Familiar English equivalents such as “anger” and “wrath” may seem straightforward, but their familiarity can make the Qur’anic meaning appear simpler than it is.

For human beings, “anger” or “being angry” is usually natural. Ghaḍbān can be “intensely angry.” When used of The God, “anger” may suggest a human emotional outburst to some readers, while “displeasure” may weaken the word too much. Retaining ghaḍab with an explanation such as a stance of intense disapproval toward conduct offers a more precise balance.

Maghḍūb ʿalayhim (مغضوب عليهم) should retain its passive structure: those upon whom anger has come. Mughāḍiban should likewise be translated without adding an object that the Qur’an has not supplied.

In studying the root, the image of a “hard rock” can help illuminate the element of intensity, but it should not become a definitive account of the word’s origin. What early language establishes with greater confidence is that ghaḍab was associated with intensity and strength and stood in opposition to approval.

Human Qur’anic usage presents this intensity as a state that can arise and subside, and during which a person remains capable of forgiveness. Anger is therefore neither inherently sinful nor inherently revenge.

In divine usage, the same core is separated from human bodily characteristics. Ghaḍab is a stance of intense disapproval toward conduct; the Qur’an expresses its consequences through other words, such as exclusion, punishment, and falling. This distinction shows that none of these consequences defines ghaḍab itself.

Sakhaṭ and ghayẓ also belong to this neighboring field, but the word choices carry distinctions: sakhaṭ is more closely associated with disapproval, and ghayẓ emphasizes the internal pressure of anger, while ghaḍab has a broader range for an intense stance and response.

A cautious synthesis of the root is as follows:

GH Ḍ B: a family centered on intense displeasure and response to what is unacceptable. In human beings, ghaḍab is an intense state that can be aroused, subside, and be restrained through forgiveness. In reference to The God, ghaḍab is a stance of intense disapproval toward conduct and can be accompanied by consequences such as exclusion, punishment, or falling, without being identical to any of them.
