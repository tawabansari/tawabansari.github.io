The Root TH W Y in the Qur’an

Staying, residing, and a place of residence: from hospitality to the abode of the Hereafter

Qur’anic and lexical forms

The Arabic root ث و ي (TH W Y) denotes staying or residing in a place. When someone moves from one place to another and takes up lodging there, the verb ثَوَى (thawā) may describe that stay. ثَوَاء (thawāʾ) names the residence, while مَثْوَى (mathwā) may name the place where the person resides.

In early Arabic, this root was not restricted to permanent settlement. Its forms describe a guest’s stay with a host, a person’s residence in a town or house, and even the remaining of someone slain at the place of death or in a grave.

The root ث و ي (TH W Y) occurs fourteen times in the Qur’an: thirteen as مَثْوَى (mathwā), including attached pronouns or particles, and once as ثَاوِيًا (thāwiyan). Its uses fall within two main settings: human residence and living conditions in this world, and the abode of those who face the consequences of their deeds in the Hereafter.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: fourteen occurrences in two derived forms

Crucially, the Qur’an uses مَثْوَى (mathwā) both for Joseph’s lodging in Egypt and for the abode of groups in the Fire. The word itself therefore means neither Hell nor necessarily an eternal or blameworthy dwelling. Its basic meaning concerns staying and residing; context and accompanying words establish the nature and duration of the stay.

1. The semantic core and root forms

Ibn Fāris writes in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

«الثَّاءُ وَالْوَاوُ وَالْيَاءُ كَلِمَةٌ وَاحِدَةٌ صَحِيحَةٌ تَدُلُّ عَلَى الْإِقَامَةِ»

al-thāʾu wa-al-wāwu wa-al-yāʾu kalimatun wāḥidatun ṣaḥīḥatun tadullu ʿalā al-iqāmah

That is, this lexical family has a sound basic meaning denoting residence.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: residence and root forms

In Al-Mufradāt, Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī explains the meaning as الثَّوَاءُ: الْإِقَامَةُ مَعَ الِاسْتِقْرَارِ (al-thawāʾ: al-iqāmah maʿa al-istiqrār): residence accompanied by settled staying.

Al-Rāghib’s Al-Mufradāt: residence with settled staying

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise explains ثَوَاء (thawāʾ) as a prolonged stay in a place and مَثْوَى (mathwā) as the place where someone resides.

These definitions share one point: the root concerns staying and taking up lodging, rather than mere passing presence or transit.

Nevertheless, “a long stay” must not be converted into eternal residence. The length of a stay is relative and does not by itself establish endlessness.

ثَوَى and ثَوَاء (thawā and thawāʾ)

ثَوَى يَثْوِي (thawā yathwī) is a Form I verb denoting staying or residing.

Arabic uses ثَوَى بِالْمَكَانِ (thawā bi-al-makān), meaning that someone resided in a place. ثَوَى فِي الْمَكَانِ (thawā fī al-makān) also has this meaning.

ثَوَاء (thawāʾ) is the verbal noun, naming the stay or residence itself.

The dictionaries also record ثُوِيّ (thuwiyy) as a verbal noun. This difference of form does not change the basic meaning of residence.

Al-Muḥkam and Tāj al-ʿArūs: thuwiyy and morphology

Some Arabic constructions also use the verb with a direct object. The presence or absence of a preposition is therefore not uniform; the actual construction of each use requires separate attention.

ثَاوٍ (thāwin): a person residing

ثَاوٍ (thāwin) is the active participle of ثَوَى (thawā). It denotes someone who has stayed or taken up residence in a place.

A person who lodges in a land and lives among its people, for example, may be called ثَاوٍ (thāwin).

The Qur’anic ثَاوِيًا (thāwiyan) is this active participle in an accusative syntactic position.

This participle does not require ownership of a house or land. Someone may reside in another person’s house and still be described as ثَاوٍ (thāwin).

Nor does residence alone establish political citizenship, ethnic affiliation, or birth in that land.

مَثْوَى (mathwā): an abode or residence itself

مَثْوَى (mathwā) is formed on the pattern مَفْعَل (mafʿal) from the final-weak verb ثَوَى (thawā). The last root consonant is yāʾ, which appears as alif maqṣūrah in this final-alif noun.

The word has two closely related uses in Arabic.

First, it may be a place noun: the place where someone resides. مَثْوَى الرَّجُلِ (mathwā al-rajul) in this sense is his lodging or abode.

Second, it may be a mīm-prefixed verbal noun, expressing residence itself or its condition.

These uses are related. Someone residing in a place has both a place of residence and a state of staying there.

In some sentences, distinguishing the place noun from the verbal noun matters syntactically. At Al-Anʿām 6:128, for example, Abū ʿAlī al-Fārisī analyzes مَثْوَاكُمْ (mathwākum) as a verbal noun. His reasoning concerns its syntactic relationship with خَالِدِينَ (khālidīna).

Many lexical and exegetical explanations, by contrast, clarify the same expression through the sense of a dwelling or place of residence.

The morphological pattern of مَثْوَى (mathwā) therefore does not establish that it is exclusively a place noun in every verse, or exclusively a verbal noun. The form permits both uses; context and sentence structure help identify its role.

أَثْوَى and ثَوَّى (athwā and thawwā): accommodating and hosting

In early Arabic, أَثْوَى (athwā) sometimes means residing oneself, close to the meaning of ثَوَى (thawā).

In a transitive construction, however, it may mean providing residence for someone else.

The recorded expression أَثْوَانِي الرَّجُلُ (athwānī al-rajul) means that a man hosted me and provided lodging for my stay.

ثَوَّى (thawwā) is likewise recorded for lodging or accommodating another person.

The family thus covers both a person’s residence and the provision of lodging for someone else.

The relationship between host and guest is consequently an actual field of use for this root in Arabic.

2. Residence, hospitality, and lodging in early Arabic

In Arab life, residence did not always mean owning a permanent home. A traveler might lodge with someone, remain for a time, and then continue the journey.

Words from the ث و ي (TH W Y) family describe this situation.

الثَّوِيّ (al-thawiyy) is recorded for a guest. In some uses it also names a house or lodging prepared for a guest.

أَبُو الْمَثْوَى (abū al-mathwā) denotes the host or master of the house, and أُمُّ الْمَثْوَى (umm al-mathwā) the woman in charge of the house.

These expressions show that مَثْوَى (mathwā) in everyday life was not simply the name of a building. It could also relate to residence, hospitality, and living in another person’s household.

A person may have suitable accommodation without owning the house. A host may likewise make a guest’s stay good by providing an appropriate place.

This background matters for the uses in Yūsuf, where مَثْوَى (mathwā) concerns Joseph’s living situation in someone else’s house.

A pre-Islamic witness from Al-Ḥārith ibn Ḥillizah

The pre-Islamic poet Al-Ḥārith ibn Ḥillizah al-Yashkurī opens his celebrated Muʿallaqah:

آذَنَتْنَا بِبَيْنِهَا أَسْمَاءُ
رُبَّ ثَاوٍ يُمَلُّ مِنْهُ الثَّوَاءُ

ādhanatnā bi-baynihā Asmāʾu / rubba thāwin yumallu minhu al-thawāʾu

Translation:

“Asmāʾ has informed us of her departure; many a resident’s staying becomes wearisome.”

Ibn al-Anbārī’s commentary: thāwin and thawāʾ in the Muʿallaqah

The line brings together two forms of the root: ثَاوٍ (thāwin) for the resident and ثَوَاء (thawāʾ) for the stay.

The poet speaks of departure and staying. A person’s prolonged presence can become tiresome; the poem’s setting connects this reflection with separation from the beloved.

This witness is especially useful because it shows the active participle and verbal noun being used for human residence in pre-Islamic poetry, independently of terminology about the Hereafter.

Here too, ثَوَاء (thawāʾ) does not mean endless staying. The residence may continue or end in departure.

A witness from Al-Aʿshā

A poem attributed to Al-Aʿshā contains the following line:

أَثْوَى وَقَصَّرَ لَيْلَةً لِيُزَوَّدَا
وَمَضَى وَأَخْلَفَ مِنْ قُتَيْلَةَ مَوْعِدَا

athwā wa-qaṣṣara laylatan li-yuzawwadā / wa-maḍā wa-akhlafa min Qutaylata mawʿidā

Translation:

“He stayed and held back from departing that night to receive a farewell provision; then he left, finding Qutaylah’s promise unfulfilled.”

Here أَثْوَى (athwā) denotes the stay of someone intending to travel. In Al-Fāʾiq, Al-Zamakhsharī explains the expression as residing and refraining from travel to receive provisions. In Al-Iqtiḍāb, Ibn al-Sīd al-Baṭalyawsī understands the provision as the beloved’s farewell and greeting, and explicitly states that Qutaylah failed to keep her promise, rather than the traveler breaking his own.

Al-Iqtiḍāb: Qutaylah’s unfulfilled promise and a farewell provision

The witness shows that a stay may form part of a journey and an expectation of farewell. The transmitted wording includes both لَيْلَةً (laylatan) and لَيْلَهُ (laylahu), as well as an interrogative reading أَثَوَى (a-thawā). The text printed here is one transmission; these variants should be acknowledged.

Al-Fāʾiq: residing and holding back from travel

Al-Aʿshā belongs to the pre-Islamic poetic world, while the end of his life overlapped with the beginning of Islam. Although the line appears in the poetry attributed to him, the precise date of this poem is unclear; it should not be presented without qualification as a securely pre-Qur’anic witness.

ثَوَى (thawā) in relation to death and burial

In early usage, ثَوَى (thawā) is sometimes applied to someone slain or buried.

Abū Kabīr al-Hudhalī says:

نَغْدُو فَنَتْرُكُ فِي الْمَزَاحِفِ مَنْ ثَوَى
وَنُمِرُّ فِي الْعَرَقَاتِ مَنْ لَمْ نَقْتُلِ

naghdū fa-natruku fī al-mazāḥifi man thawā / wa-numirru fī al-ʿaraqāti man lam naqtuli

Translation:

“We go out in the morning, leaving the slain in the battlefields, and bind in straps those whom we have not killed.”

Here ثَوَى (thawā) describes someone killed and left on the battlefield. Under عرق (ʿ R Q), Al-Muḥkam records نُمِرُّ (numirru) and explains the second half as taking the survivors captive and binding them in straps. It does not mean “we pass by them.” The final verb is transmitted both as نَقْتُلِ (naqtuli) and يُقْتَلِ (yuqtali).

Al-Muḥkam: numirru, captive-binding straps, and two final-verb readings

This sense is not an unrelated branch. The dead person remains at the place of death or in the grave; the verb of staying is applied on that basis.

But this use must not replace the root’s basic meaning. ثَوَى (thawā) does not fundamentally mean killing or dying and is frequently applied to living people.

The sense relating to death is therefore dependent on context.

Animal shelters and returning to a dwelling place

The dictionaries record الثَّوِيَّة (al-thawiyyah) and الثَّايَة (al-thāyah) for animal shelters, particularly places where sheep and camels gather or return.

In another use, الثَّايَة (al-thāyah), and in some sources الثَّوِيَّة (al-thawiyyah), denotes raised stones serving at night as a marker for a shepherd’s return. This stone-marker sense should be distinguished from that of an animal shelter.

Al-Muḥkam and Maqāyīs: animal shelters and stone markers

The usage relates to residence and returning to a definite place. It does not establish that the root was originally restricted to pens or pastoral life.

The semantic core remains staying and settling in a place.

3. The first Qur’anic setting: Joseph’s lodging in Egypt

Two Qur’anic uses of مَثْوَى (mathwā) concern Joseph’s life in Egypt.

These verses matter especially for lexical research: before considering the word in passages about the Hereafter, one can see its clear meaning in an ordinary human setting in this world.

Yūsuf 12:21: honoring the place of residence

Yūsuf 12:21

وَقَالَ الَّذِي اشْتَرَاهُ مِن مِّصْرَ لِامْرَأَتِهِ أَكْرِمِي مَثْوَاهُ عَسَىٰ أَن يَنفَعَنَا أَوْ نَتَّخِذَهُ وَلَدًا ۚ وَكَذَٰلِكَ مَكَّنَّا لِيُوسُفَ فِي الْأَرْضِ وَلِنُعَلِّمَهُ مِن تَأْوِيلِ الْأَحَادِيثِ ۚ وَاللَّهُ غَالِبٌ عَلَىٰ أَمْرِهِ وَلَٰكِنَّ أَكْثَرَ النَّاسِ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ

wa-qāla alladhī ishtarāhu min Miṣra li-imraʾatihi akrimī mathwāhu ʿasā an yanfaʿanā aw nattakhidhahu waladan wa-ka-dhālika makkannā li-Yūsufa fī al-arḍi wa-li-nuʿallimahu min taʾwīli al-aḥādīthi wa-Allāhu ghālibun ʿalā amrihi wa-lākinna akthara al-nāsi lā yaʿlamūna

English rendering of Forqan’s current Persian translation:

“The man from Egypt who bought him said to his wife: Honor his lodging; perhaps he may benefit us or we may adopt him as a son. Thus We established Joseph in the land, and so that We might teach him the interpretation of accounts. The God prevails over His affair, but most people do not know.”

The man who bought Joseph instructs his wife: أَكْرِمِي مَثْوَاهُ (akrimī mathwāhu).

مَثْوَاهُ (mathwāhu) consists of مَثْوَى (mathwā) and the pronoun هُ (hu): his place or condition of residence.

The command أَكْرِمِي (akrimī) shows that the matter is more than assigning a sleeping place. Joseph’s lodging is to be honored, and he is to be treated appropriately where he resides.

Lexical and exegetical sources sometimes explain the phrase as providing good lodging, food, clothing, and hospitality.

Al-Ṭabarī: honoring Joseph’s stay

These particulars, however, are not all contained in مَثْوَى (mathwā) itself. Its basic sense is a place of residence; the command to honor it clarifies how the resident should be treated.

“Honor his lodging” therefore preserves the Arabic construction, while explanations about food, clothing, and comfort belong to contextual analysis.

Yūsuf 12:23: a good stay

Yūsuf 12:23

وَرَاوَدَتْهُ الَّتِي هُوَ فِي بَيْتِهَا عَن نَّفْسِهِ وَغَلَّقَتِ الْأَبْوَابَ وَقَالَتْ هَيْتَ لَكَ ۚ قَالَ مَعَاذَ اللَّهِ ۖ إِنَّهُ رَبِّي أَحْسَنَ مَثْوَايَ ۖ إِنَّهُ لَا يُفْلِحُ الظَّالِمُونَ

wa-rāwadat-hu allatī huwa fī baytihā ʿan nafsihi wa-ghallaqati al-abwāba wa-qālat hayta laka qāla maʿādha Allāhi innahu rabbī aḥsana mathwāya innahu lā yufliḥu al-ẓālimūna

English rendering of Forqan’s current Persian translation:

“The woman in whose house he was sought to draw him to herself, closed the doors, and said: Come. He said: Refuge in The God; He is my Lord and has made my lodging good. Wrongdoers do not prosper.”

مَثْوَايَ (mathwāya) means my place or condition of residence.

It belongs to the same family and basic construction as مَثْوَاهُ (mathwāhu) in Yūsuf 12:21, but its final pronoun refers to the speaker, Joseph.

The first verse commands good treatment of Joseph’s lodging; the second has Joseph speak of his lodging having been made good.

The relationship shows that مَثْوَى (mathwā) may name a residence and also be used in connection with the quality of someone’s stay there.

The referent in إِنَّهُ رَبِّي (innahu rabbī) has been read differently: some refer it to God and others to the master of the house.

Deciding between these readings requires sentence structure, pronoun reference, and the narrative context, rather than the meaning of مَثْوَى (mathwā).

In either reading, this word continues to refer clearly to Joseph’s place or condition of residence.

Root research therefore neither authorizes changing Forqan’s current translation nor warrants imposing a particular pronoun referent on this word itself.

What the two uses in Yūsuf establish

Both verses clearly use مَثْوَى (mathwā) for human life in this world.

Joseph lives in Egypt, and the discussion concerns his situation in the house he has entered. Neither verse concerns residence after death.

The lexical definition of مَثْوَى (mathwā) therefore cannot be restricted to an abode of the Hereafter or an everlasting dwelling.

The two passages also show that a place of residence may be good and honored. The root itself has no inherent negative or condemnatory force.

4. ثَاوِيًا (thāwiyan) in Al-Qaṣaṣ: residing among people

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:45

وَلَٰكِنَّا أَنشَأْنَا قُرُونًا فَتَطَاوَلَ عَلَيْهِمُ الْعُمُرُ ۚ وَمَا كُنتَ ثَاوِيًا فِي أَهْلِ مَدْيَنَ تَتْلُو عَلَيْهِمْ آيَاتِنَا وَلَٰكِنَّا كُنَّا مُرْسِلِينَ

wa-lākinnā anshaʾnā qurūnan fa-taṭāwala ʿalayhimu al-ʿumuru wa-mā kunta thāwiyan fī ahli Madyana tatlū ʿalayhim āyātinā wa-lākinnā kunnā mursilīna

English rendering of Forqan’s current Persian translation:

“But We brought forth generations, and their lifetimes grew long. You were not residing among the people of Midian, reciting Our signs to them; but We were sending messengers.”

This is the Qur’an’s only occurrence of the active participle ثَاوٍ (thāwin).

In مَا كُنتَ ثَاوِيًا (mā kunta thāwiyan), ثَاوِيًا (thāwiyan) is the accusative predicate of كَانَ (kāna). The expression negates residence.

Al-Ṭabarī: thāwiyan means residing

فِي أَهْلِ مَدْيَنَ (fī ahli Madyan) matters: it concerns residing among the people of Midian, rather than simply a brief presence at a geographical point.

The preceding verses concern events associated with Moses. The Qur’an reminds Muhammad that he was not there when those events occurred.

The negation of residence thus forms part of an argument about the source of his knowledge of those events.

The verse relates to Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:44 and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:46, which describe his absence from earlier events from other perspectives.

Lexically, ثَاوِيًا (thāwiyan) means residing. Not residing among the people of Midian is the participle’s meaning in this context.

Receiving revelation or knowing the past, however, comes from the argument of the verses as a whole, rather than from the lexical meaning of ثَاوِيًا (thāwiyan).

Nor does this expression establish the impossibility of a brief visit to that region at any time. The verse concerns residence among its people and presence at the events under discussion.

5. مَثْوَى (mathwā) in verses about the Hereafter

Of the thirteen occurrences of مَثْوَى (mathwā), two concern Joseph’s worldly lodging and one concerns divine knowledge of human movement and residence. The other ten appear in contexts of the Fire and the consequences of human deeds.

This extensive use for the Hereafter shows the word’s importance in describing human outcomes, but does not change its basic meaning.

مَثْوَى (mathwā) and an undesirable abode

Āl ʿImrān 3:151

سَنُلْقِي فِي قُلُوبِ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا الرُّعْبَ بِمَا أَشْرَكُوا بِاللَّهِ مَا لَمْ يُنَزِّلْ بِهِ سُلْطَانًا ۖ وَمَأْوَاهُمُ النَّارُ ۚ وَبِئْسَ مَثْوَى الظَّالِمِينَ

sa-nulqī fī qulūbi alladhīna kafarū al-ruʿba bi-mā ashrakū bi-Allāhi mā lam yunazzil bihi sulṭānan wa-maʾwāhumu al-nāru wa-biʾsa mathwā al-ẓālimīna

English rendering of Forqan’s current Persian translation:

“We shall cast fear into the hearts of those who disbelieved, because they associated with The God something for which He sent down no authority. Their abode is the Fire; how evil is the abode of the wrongdoers.”

This verse contains both مَأْوَى (maʾwā) and مَثْوَى (mathwā).

Their abode is first identified by مَأْوَاهُمُ النَّارُ (maʾwāhumu al-nār). بِئْسَ مَثْوَى الظَّالِمِينَ (biʾsa mathwā al-ẓālimīn) then condemns that abode.

بِئْسَ (biʾsa) expresses blame and here marks the dwelling as bad.

Its badness therefore comes from the complete construction. مَثْوَى (mathwā) alone does not mean an evil abode.

This distinction matters: building negativity into the root would needlessly obscure its good and ordinary uses in Yūsuf.

Although مَأْوَى (maʾwā) and مَثْوَى (mathwā) refer here to the same destination, they come from different roots and must not be treated as cognates within one root.

مَثْوَى (mathwā) and the description خَالِدِينَ (khālidīna)

Al-Anʿām 6:128

وَيَوْمَ يَحْشُرُهُمْ جَمِيعًا يَا مَعْشَرَ الْجِنِّ قَدِ اسْتَكْثَرْتُمْ مِنَ الْإِنْسِ ۖ وَقَالَ أَوْلِيَاؤُهُمْ مِنَ الْإِنْسِ رَبَّنَا اسْتَمْتَعَ بَعْضُنَا بِبَعْضٍ وَبَلَغْنَا أَجَلَنَا الَّذِي أَجَّلْتَ لَنَا ۚ قَالَ النَّارُ مَثْوَاكُمْ خَالِدِينَ فِيهَا إِلَّا مَا شَاءَ اللَّهُ ۗ إِنَّ رَبَّكَ حَكِيمٌ عَلِيمٌ

wa-yawma yaḥshuruhum jamīʿan yā maʿshara al-jinni qadi istakthartum mina al-insi wa-qāla awliyāʾuhum mina al-insi rabbanā istamtaʿa baʿḍunā bi-baʿḍin wa-balaghnā ajalanā alladhī ajjalta lanā qāla al-nāru mathwākum khālidīna fīhā illā mā shāʾa Allāhu inna rabbaka ḥakīmun ʿalīmun

English rendering of Forqan’s current Persian translation:

“On the day He gathers them all: O company of jinn, you have taken many among humankind. Their allies among humankind say: Our Lord, some of us benefited from others, and we have reached the term You appointed for us. He says: The Fire is your abode, abiding in it, except as The God wills. Your Lord is wise and knowing.”

This verse is particularly useful for distinguishing مَثْوَى (mathwā) from خُلُود (khulūd).

مَثْوَاكُمْ (mathwākum) concerns their abode or residence. The following خَالِدِينَ فِيهَا (khālidīna fīhā) describes abiding there.

The uses in Yūsuf show that eternity cannot be built into مَثْوَى (mathwā) itself and then transferred without examination to every other use of the root.

خَالِدِينَ (khālidīna) shows that, beyond naming an abode, the verse also speaks about the continuance of residence.

إِلَّا مَا شَاءَ اللَّهُ (illā mā shāʾa Allāh) follows. The role and force of this exception require separate examination of the construction and related verses.

Lexically, مَثْوَى (mathwā) alone cannot establish a final judgment about the duration of residence in the Fire. Conversely, the absence of eternity from this word itself does not justify ignoring خَالِدِينَ (khālidīna) or the subsequent exception.

Each word and qualification must be examined in its own place.

The syntactic disagreement about مَثْوَاكُمْ (mathwākum)

As reported by Ibn Sīdah in Al-Muḥkam, Abū ʿAlī al-Fārisī prefers a mīm-prefixed verbal noun over a place noun in his analysis of النَّارُ مَثْوَاكُمْ (al-nāru mathwākum).

In his analysis, خَالِدِينَ (khālidīna) is a circumstantial expression relating to the addressees’ pronoun, and the verbal sense of residence in مَثْوَاكُمْ (mathwākum) can govern it. A place noun does not by itself govern a circumstantial expression as a verb does. This concerns the governing element in this particular construction, rather than a ban on any place noun occurring with a circumstantial expression.

The meaning is accordingly explained through an understood النَّارُ ذَاتُ إِقَامَتِكُمْ (al-nāru dhātu iqāmatikum): the Fire is where your residence takes place, abiding there. This analysis distinguishes the verbal noun of residence from the understood preceding possessive element.

Abū ʿAlī al-Fārisī as quoted in Al-Muḥkam: the verbal noun and circumstantial government

Exegetical and grammatical sources also offer a place-noun analysis, explaining that the element governing the circumstantial expression is understood from the construction or supplied implicitly.

Himyān al-Zād: the place-noun reading and an implicit governing element

The disagreement thus concerns sentence analysis, rather than the root’s basic meaning of residence.

Under either analysis, مَثْوَى (mathwā) remains connected with staying and settling in an abode.

Other descriptions of the Fire

Al-Naḥl 16:29, Al-Zumar 39:72, and Ghāfir 40:76 use مَثْوَى الْمُتَكَبِّرِينَ (mathwā al-mutakabbirīn). All three connect the expression with entry into Hell and condemnation of the arrogant people’s abode.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:68 and Al-Zumar 39:32 use مَثْوًى لِلْكَافِرِينَ (mathwan li-al-kāfirīn) in a question. Al-Zumar 39:60 similarly speaks of مَثْوًى لِلْمُتَكَبِّرِينَ (mathwan li-al-mutakabbirīn).

Fuṣṣilat 41:24 and Muḥammad 47:12 describe the Fire as مَثْوًى لَهُمْ (mathwan lahum).

In every case, the word’s basic meaning is an abode. The verses differ in who is assigned that abode, and in the descriptions and consequences expressed by their contexts.

The mere presence of مَثْوَى (mathwā) does not make all these verses identical in construction or message.

Some emphasize arrogance and false claims about God, others the consequence of denial, and others the contrast between human conduct and its outcome.

ث و ي (TH W Y) identifies the residence. The reasons people reach it and judgments on their conduct come from the verses’ other words and constructions.

6. Mutaqallab and mathwā: movement and residence

Muḥammad 47:19

فَاعْلَمْ أَنَّهُ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا اللَّهُ وَاسْتَغْفِرْ لِذَنْبِكَ وَلِلْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَالْمُؤْمِنَاتِ ۗ وَاللَّهُ يَعْلَمُ مُتَقَلَّبَكُمْ وَمَثْوَاكُمْ

fa-iʿlam annahu lā ilāha illā Allāhu wa-istaghfir li-dhanbika wa-li-al-muʾminīna wa-al-muʾmināti wa-Allāhu yaʿlamu mutaqallabakum wa-mathwākum

Translation:

“Know, then, that there is no deity except The God, and seek forgiveness for your sin and for the believing men and women. The God knows your place of movement and your place of residence.”

Here مَثْوَاكُمْ (mathwākum) stands alongside مُتَقَلَّبَكُمْ (mutaqallabakum).

مُتَقَلَّب (mutaqallab) comes from ق ل ب (Q L B). In this construction it concerns turning, change, movement, or the place of movement.

Alongside it, مَثْوَى (mathwā) points to the place or condition of residence and settled staying.

This pairing can express the scope of divine knowledge of different human conditions: movement and change as well as remaining at one’s residence.

The scope of these two expressions has nevertheless received different explanations.

Some relate them to human movement and residence in this world. Others connect mutaqallab with worldly life and mathwā with the place after death.

Al-Ṭabarī: interpretations of mutaqallab and mathwā

Both readings require contextual examination. مَثْوَى (mathwā) is not an exclusive name for the Hereafter, as its worldly uses in Yūsuf demonstrate.

At the same time, the Qur’anic setting and the reference to divine knowledge do not exclude an understanding extending beyond physical location alone.

“Your place of movement and your place of residence” preserves the basic difference between the two roots, leaving their ultimate scope to contextual study.

7. Comparison with related roots

ث و ي and س ك ن (TH W Y and S K N): residing and becoming still

Study of S K N

ثَوَى (thawā) denotes staying or residing in a place.

سَكَنَ (sakana) can also describe residence in a place. Besides residence, however, the س ك ن (S K N) family relates to tranquility, becoming still, and the subsiding of movement.

The roots thus overlap in living in a place, but are not identical.

ثَوَى (thawā) foregrounds staying and residence; سَكَن (sakan) also has stillness and dwelling within its semantic field.

This does not mean that a resident is always at peace or that every dwelling necessarily involves a long stay.

Context determines the precise character of each use.

مَثْوَى and مَأْوَى (mathwā and maʾwā): abode and refuge

Study of A W Y

أ و ي (A W Y) relates to taking refuge, turning toward safety, or joining a place in which to settle.

مَأْوَى (maʾwā) may name the place to which someone takes refuge or in which they lodge.

مَثْوَى (mathwā), by comparison, emphasizes residing and remaining in an abode.

The two words may refer to the same place in many sentences, but their semantic paths differ.

Both refer to the Fire in Āl ʿImrān 3:151. A single destination can thus be described as both maʾwā and mathwā.

Nevertheless, maʾwā is not always a safe or desirable refuge. The Qur’an also uses it for the Fire. Context establishes whether the destination is desirable or undesirable.

ث و ي and ق و م (TH W Y and Q W M): residence and standing

Study of Q W M

أَقَامَ (aqāma) and إِقَامَة (iqāmah) belong to ق و م (Q W M) and may concern residing or remaining in a place.

This use is close to ثَوَى (thawā), but ق و م (Q W M) has a wider field that includes rising, standing, setting up, and establishing.

By comparison, the uses of ثَوَى (thawā) examined here concern residence and taking up lodging.

In some contexts either family may express residence, but not all meanings of قَوْم (qawm), قِيَام (qiyām), and إِقَامَة (iqāmah) may be transferred to ث و ي (TH W Y).

ث و ي and خ ل د (TH W Y and KH L D): residence and continuance

Study of KH L D

This comparison is particularly relevant to verses about the Hereafter.

ثَوَى (thawā) concerns residing; مَثْوَى (mathwā) is the place or condition of residence.

خُلُود (khulūd) concerns continuing and abiding. In verses about the Hereafter it may express the continuation of residence.

Al-Anʿām 6:128 places مَثْوَاكُمْ (mathwākum) and خَالِدِينَ (khālidīna) together.

The name of the abode and the description of continuing residence are thus distinct elements of the sentence.

Consequently, مَثْوَى (mathwā) should not be translated as “eternal abode” in every verse: that qualification is not contained in the noun itself. Where a verse speaks of continuance or abiding, that qualification needs its own translation and analysis.

This lexical distinction alone does not settle the duration of residence in the Hereafter. That question requires all the relevant verses.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

For ثَوَى (thawā), “resided,” “stayed,” and “took up lodging” are suitable. Where continued residence is prominent, “resided for a time” or “remained there” may fit the context.

ثَوَاء (thawāʾ) means residence or staying. ثَاوٍ (thāwin) is a resident or someone staying in a place.

For transitive أَثْوَى (athwā), “accommodated” or “lodged” is suitable; in a setting of hospitality, “hosted” also fits.

Qur’anic مَثْوَى (mathwā) may be rendered “abode,” “place of residence,” or “dwelling place.” Where the expression concerns the quality of the stay, “condition of residence” may serve as an explanation.

9. Conclusion

The Arabic root ث و ي (TH W Y) denotes staying and residing. ثَوَى (thawā) expresses the act, ثَاوٍ (thāwin) the resident, and مَثْوَى (mathwā) the place or condition of residence.

Early Arabic witnesses show this family in everyday use for hospitality, living in another person’s house, a traveler’s stay, and residence in a land. Its use for the dead or a grave is also intelligible through remaining in place, but the basic root meaning must not be restricted to death.

The two uses of مَثْوَى (mathwā) in Yūsuf concern worldly lodging and Joseph’s living situation. ثَاوِيًا (thāwiyan) in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:45 describes residing among the people of Midian. Muḥammad 47:19 pairs مَثْوَى (mathwā) with مُتَقَلَّب (mutaqallab), relating it to human residence.

The other uses place the word in settings of the Fire and the consequences of human deeds. Yet مَثْوَى (mathwā) itself neither means punishment nor determines the duration of a stay. The abode’s badness, continuance there, and the groups assigned to it are identified by other words in the verses.

مَثْوَى (mathwā) should therefore not automatically become “eternal abode” in passages about the Hereafter, with that meaning then generalized across the root. Conversely, the absence of eternity from the word itself does not authorize omitting or neutralizing descriptions such as خَالِدِينَ (khālidīna).

This family directly expresses residing, staying, and a place of residence. The time, quality, value, and outcome of that residence require separate examination in each verse.
