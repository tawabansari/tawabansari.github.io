Lowliness, submission, and humiliation before a greater power

1. Introducing the root and its semantic field

The Arabic triliteral root D KH R denotes lowliness, becoming abased, and being in a position where a person or creature no longer occupies a superior position before a greater power.

Dākhir describes someone or something in that condition of lowliness and submission. Some uses involve humiliation, degradation, and loss of standing; others concern submission before a power that creatures cannot surpass.

This distinction matters particularly in the Qur’an.

Al-Naḥl 16:48 uses dākhirūn in describing creation and prostration to The God. Al-Ṣāffāt 37:18 and Ghāfir 40:60, by contrast, use the word concerning the outcome of those who confront truth with arrogance and denial.

A common semantic core therefore remains, while context determines the moral character and quality of the lowliness.

Creation’s submission to The God does not necessarily mean humiliation for sin; nor is the humiliation of the arrogant at judgment simply their commendable, voluntary humility.

Study should distinguish lowliness before divine power, conscious humility, and humiliation following the collapse of arrogance, without turning these into independent roots or entirely unrelated meanings.

2. Qur’anic forms and early Arabic meaning

Qur’anic forms and derivatives

D KH R occurs four times, in four Qur’anic verses, exclusively as forms of the active participle dākhir.

Its two plural forms are:

دَاخِرُونَ: the nominative sound masculine plural, in Al-Naḥl 16:48 and Al-Ṣāffāt 37:18.

دَاخِرِينَ: the same participle in the accusative, in Al-Naml 27:87 and Ghāfir 40:60.

These are not independent derivatives with different meanings. Their endings reflect their grammatical roles.

Arabic also records these members of the family:

دَخَرَ يَدْخَرُ: he became abased, lowly, or subject to submission and humiliation.

دَخِرَ: another Form I verb, with the related meaning of becoming abased or lowly.

دُخُور: a verbal noun denoting abasement and lowliness.

دَخَر: another recorded verbal noun for abasement.

دَاخِر: abased, lowly, or submissive before a greater power.

أَدْخَرَ: Form IV, to abase someone or place another in a lowly position.

These verbs and verbal nouns do not occur in the Qur’an; only the description dākhir appears.

This limitation matters: none of the four occurrences directly uses dakhara or adkhara. Rather, the participle describes the condition of a subject or group.

The semantic core in early dictionaries

Ibn Fāris defines the root’s principle in Maqāyīs al-Lughah as:

أَصْلٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى الذُّلِّ

aṣlun yadullu ʿalā al-dhulli

“A principle denoting abasement.”

He explains that a person who dakhara is in a condition of abasement, while adkharahu means someone else abased him.

Al-Jawharī’s Al-Ṣiḥāḥ also states:

الدُّخُورُ: الصَّغَارُ وَالذُّلُّ

al-dukhūru al-ṣaghāru wa-al-dhullu

“Dukhūr is lowliness and abasement.”

Lisān al-ʿArab similarly uses ṣaghār alongside dhull to explain the root.

Here ṣaghār does not merely mean bodily smallness; it conveys low rank and the absence of a superior position.

Dākhir should therefore not be translated as short, small-bodied, or physically small.

A physically strong and socially powerful person may reach a situation in which no superior power or standing remains available to them. Dākhir can describe that condition.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: the principle of abasement

Does dukhūr always mean degradation?

Dictionaries explain dākhir through words such as dhalīl, ṣāghir, and muhān.

These often concern humiliation and degradation.

Semantic analysis should nevertheless distinguish the condition of lowliness from its cause and moral value.

A person may consciously abandon arrogance before truth and justice; that conduct can be commendable in Qur’anic terms.

Alternatively, someone who considers themselves superior in this life may eventually face the collapse of their claimed power and encounter humiliation.

Both involve superiority and lowliness, but their moral qualities differ.

Dākhir should not, however, always mean a virtuous, humble person. In judgment and punishment contexts, the word can clearly carry abasement and humiliation.

Equally, humility in Al-Naḥl 16:48 should not turn all other uses into descriptions of commendable conduct.

Linguistic usage and historical evidence

Lisān al-ʿArab explains dākhir through a person who obeys an instruction, willingly or unwillingly, in a lowly condition.

This emphasizes lacking superiority or decisive control before the prevailing power.

Willingly or unwillingly should not become a necessary component of every use. Dākhir can accompany submission, but the presence or absence of independent choice requires the subject and context of each occurrence.

Al-Zamakhsharī records ṣāghiran dākhiran in Asās al-Balāghah: in a lowly, abased condition.

He also contrasts fākhir and dākhir in a literary expression: one boasts, while the other is abased and lowly.

This contrast illuminates the semantic field, but Al-Zamakhsharī’s expression is neither a pre-Islamic poetic witness nor proof of the root’s historical origin.

This study did not obtain a pre-Qur’anic poetic witness with sufficiently reliable wording, attribution, and dating. A line should therefore not be assigned to a pre-Islamic poet merely to complete an article template.

Dictionaries also record bewilderment for one form in this family. Qur’anic evidence and the principal lexical definitions, however, support lowliness and abasement for dākhir. That limited record does not justify imposing confusion upon all four Qur’anic uses.

The defensible core in these verses is submission and lowliness before a greater power; humiliation, degradation, and moral consequences emerge within particular contexts.

3. Wa-hum dākhirūn: creation’s submission to The God

Al-Naḥl 16:48

أَوَلَمْ يَرَوْا إِلَىٰ مَا خَلَقَ اللَّهُ مِنْ شَيْءٍ يَتَفَيَّأُ ظِلَالُهُ عَنِ الْيَمِينِ وَالشَّمَائِلِ سُجَّدًا لِلَّهِ وَهُمْ دَاخِرُونَ

a-wa-lam yaraw ilā mā khalaqa Allāhu min shayʾin yatafayyaʾu ẓilāluhu ʿani al-yamīni wa-al-shamāʾili sujjadan li-llāhi wa-hum dākhirūna

“Have they not looked at what The God has created, whose shadows move to the right and left, prostrating to The God, while they themselves are submissive?”

This is the root’s first occurrence in Qur’anic textual order.

The verse concerns created things and their shadows shifting to the right and left.

Their movement is connected with prostration to The God, and the verse ends with wa-hum dākhirūn.

Here dākhirūn foregrounds submission and lowliness before The God.

How are prostration and dukhūr related?

Sujjadan belongs to S J D, while dākhirūn belongs to D KH R.

Their semantic fields are related but distinct.

Qur’anic prostration can concern bodily conduct or describe creation’s submission.

Dukhūr, by contrast, conveys lowliness and the absence of a superior position.

Sujjadan li-llāh and wa-hum dākhirūn are thus related expressions joined in one image.

Dākhir does not itself mean performing prostration.

Nor should the description of moving shadows imply that every object or shadow consciously bows its head, kneels, or performs human religious rites.

This verse presents creation and its order as a sign of submission before The God.

Such submission can express creatures’ dependence on the created order and command, but dākhir itself does not specify each creature’s perception, will, or awareness.

Are shadows disgraced and degraded?

Translating dākhirūn here solely as degraded beings could suggest that The God humiliates shadows or natural creatures for wrongdoing.

The verse itself mentions no such wrongdoing or punishment.

Its subject is creation and submission before The God.

Lowly before The God or submissive therefore suits this context better than morally punitive humiliation.

This shows that the negative value associated with abasement is not equally present in every use.

Lowliness before the Creator can express creatures’ dependence rather than worthlessness or sinfulness.

What does wa-hum refer to?

The construction mentions what The God has created and its shadows, followed by the plural pronoun hum.

The pronoun can be understood in relation to created things and their submission. Exegetical discussions consider whether prostration and submission chiefly describe the things themselves or their shadows.

This affects the image but not dākhirūn’s basic meaning.

Either way, the verse conveys creation’s submission before The God.

Al-Naḥl 16:49 also describes the prostration of creatures in heaven and earth and of angels, explicitly saying that they do not act arrogantly.

This nearby connection further clarifies the contrast between submission and arrogance.

Nevertheless, natural creatures’ and shadows’ submission should not be made identical to conscious human moral choice. The verse does not specify those details.

4. Dākhirīn: lowliness at judgment

Al-Naml 27:87

وَيَوْمَ يُنْفَخُ فِي الصُّورِ فَفَزِعَ مَنْ فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَمَنْ فِي الْأَرْضِ إِلَّا مَنْ شَاءَ اللَّهُ وَكُلٌّ أَتَوْهُ دَاخِرِينَ

wa-yawma yunfakhu fī al-ṣūri fa-faziʿa man fī al-samāwāti wa-man fī al-arḍi illā man shāʾa Allāhu wa-kullun atawhu dākhirīna

“And on the day the trumpet is blown, those in the heavens and earth will be terrified, except whom The God wills; and all will come to Him in submission.”

Dākhirīn describes the condition of those appearing before The God.

Grammatically, it is a circumstantial accusative, expressing their state as they come.

Wa-kullun atawhu announces everyone’s appearance, while dākhirīn explains the manner of that appearance.

Humility or humiliation?

The verse describes a comprehensive event involving the trumpet and creatures’ terror.

It then describes everyone’s presence before The God.

In that condition, no one can display independent power and standing before their Lord.

Dākhirīn thus conveys universal lowliness and submission before divine power.

This does not make everyone so described necessarily sinful or deserving of degradation.

Wa-kullun has an inclusive scope.

Al-Naml 27:89 speaks of those who come with good and are safe from that day’s terror.

Submission before The God is therefore not necessarily incompatible with good standing before Him.

The root distinction matters: lowliness before The God can be universal, whereas punitive humiliation depends on people’s conduct and condition.

Does everyone submit out of fear?

The beginning mentions terror but also includes illā man shāʾa Allāh.

That exception prevents assigning the same fear to everyone without qualification.

Moreover, dākhirīn does not itself mean afraid. The separate verb fa-faziʿa expresses fear.

Fear and submission may coexist in a situation, but remain distinct concepts.

Rendering dākhirīn as terrified or frightened therefore replaces lowliness with another word’s meaning.

Does coming before The God imply movement to a physical place?

Atawhu describes coming before The God.

Dākhirīn does not specify the spatial nature of that coming.

It describes only the attendees’ submission and lowliness.

The root should therefore not yield a conclusive theory about The God’s location, people’s route in the hereafter, or the physical form of appearing at judgment.

Those questions require independent evidence if they are to be examined.

5. Wa-antum dākhirūn: the collapse of claimed superiority before resurrection

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:18

قُلْ نَعَمْ وَأَنْتُمْ دَاخِرُونَ

qul naʿam wa-antum dākhirūna

“Say: Yes, while you are abased.”

This answers people denying or mocking resurrection.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:16 records their question about being raised after death and becoming dust and bones.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:17 extends that question to their earlier forefathers.

Verse 18 answers briefly and directly: naʿam, yes.

It then adds wa-antum dākhirūn.

Why is abased more suitable here?

Al-Naḥl 16:48 concerns creation’s submission, while Al-Naml 27:87 concerns universal appearance before The God.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:18, however, the addressees deny and mock the announcement of resurrection.

Earlier verses describe mocking signs and calling the truth magic.

Dākhirūn here foregrounds humiliation and lowliness following the collapse of arrogance and denial.

Those confidently denying divine power to recreate will face a reality they can no longer dismiss with mockery.

Abased or in humiliation is consequently more natural here than humble.

Does dākhirūn itself mean punishment?

Dākhirūn in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:18 does not name a particular punishment.

It describes their lowly condition.

Subsequent verses describe resurrection, recognition of the day of recompense, gathering wrongdoers, and questioning them.

Those events relate to the addressees’ humiliation, but D KH R itself does not mean gathering, reckoning, or fiery punishment.

Translation should therefore not insert details of punishment or people’s ultimate fate into dākhirūn.

Conscious humility and unavoidable lowliness

There is a substantial semantic distinction between someone knowingly and freely humbling themselves before truth during life and someone unable to maintain denial after reality becomes manifest.

Both may involve lowliness before The God.

The first can accompany faith and conscious choice; the second involves the collapse of pride and loss of superiority.

Context supports the second situation in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:18.

This should not divide the root into unrelated meanings. The common core remains lowliness and the absence of superiority.

6. Dākhirīn contrasted with yastakbirūn

Ghāfir 40:60

وَقَالَ رَبُّكُمُ ادْعُونِي أَسْتَجِبْ لَكُمْ إِنَّ الَّذِينَ يَسْتَكْبِرُونَ عَنْ عِبَادَتِي سَيَدْخُلُونَ جَهَنَّمَ دَاخِرِينَ

wa-qāla rabbukumu udʿūnī astajib lakum inna alladhīna yastakbirūna ʿan ʿibādatī sa-yadkhulūna jahannama dākhirīna

“And your Lord said: Call upon Me; I will answer you. Those too arrogant to worship Me will enter Hell abased.”

This is a major witness for dukhūr’s moral direction in human usage.

The God first invites people to call upon Him.

He then describes those who act arrogantly toward worshipping Him.

Finally, dākhirīn describes their condition on entering Hell.

The contrast between arrogance and dukhūr

Yastakbirūn belongs to K B R and Form X. Here arrogance means considering oneself superior and refusing servitude to The God.

Dākhirīn, by contrast, conveys humiliation and lowliness.

The contrast is clear: those who assert superiority before The God in worldly life eventually lose that claimed standing.

Dākhirīn here is therefore more than simple humility; it carries abasement contrary to their earlier claim.

This conclusion comes from the two expressions’ contrast in this verse.

Dākhir does not always mean a defeated arrogant person in Arabic. That definition would not fit shadows and created things in Al-Naḥl 16:48.

The relationship between supplication and worship

The verse begins with udʿūnī: call upon Me.

It continues with ʿan ʿibādatī: from worshipping Me.

These related expressions belong to different roots.

Supplication concerns calling and requesting; worship has a broader field of servitude and obedience.

Here arrogant rejection of worship is set against the invitation to call upon The God.

The verse thus presents the human relationship with The God as incompatible with self-importance and claims of independence from Him.

Worship should not be reduced entirely to asking for needs, nor should supplication, worship, or arrogance be extracted from dākhirīn itself.

Each expression contributes independently to the construction.

Does humiliation here follow human choice?

The verse identifies a group acting arrogantly toward worshipping The God.

Their eventual abasement is therefore contextually connected with their conduct and orientation.

This differs fundamentally from Al-Naḥl 16:48.

There, creation submits before The God; natural creatures’ sin or arrogance is not the subject.

Ghāfir 40:60, however, explicitly names human arrogance.

Abased therefore suits this verse and should not become humble merely for superficial consistency.

7. Distinguishing D KH R from nearby roots

D KH R and D Ḥ R: lowliness and being driven away

These roots look very similar, but their second letters differ.

D KH R conveys abasement and lowliness.

D Ḥ R concerns driving away, repelling, and removing from a place or position.

Someone driven away may consequently be humiliated, but removal and being abased remain independent meanings.

Madḥūran in Al-Aʿrāf 7:18, for example, concerns driving Iblīs away.

Dākhirīn in Ghāfir 40:60 instead describes the humiliation of those entering Hell.

Madḥūr means driven away, whereas dākhir means abased or lowly.

Dākhirīn therefore does not mean they are driven out of Hell in Ghāfir 40:60. The verse describes their entering it in humiliation.

Study of D Ḥ R

D KH R and Ṣ GH R: abasement and low standing

Arabic Ṣ GH R concerns smallness, whether bodily size or reduced rank and standing.

Al-ṣāghirīn occurs in the Iblīs account in Al-Aʿrāf 7:13.

Dictionaries also explain dākhir through ṣāghir.

That proximity does not make the roots identical in every use.

Ṣ GH R has a broader field and can describe smallness in nonhuman subjects too.

Qur’anic dukhūr foregrounds lowliness and abasement before greater power.

This is a tendency rather than an absolute boundary across Arabic usage.

D KH R and DH L L: lowliness and tractability

DH L L is among D KH R’s closest semantic families.

Arabic dhull can concern abasement and humiliation, while other forms concern softness, tractability, ease, or usability.

Al-Naḥl 16:69, for example, uses dhululan concerning paths available to bees. Exegetical analysis relates it both to easy, accessible paths and to the bee’s obedience; Al-Ṭabarī considers both readings possible while preferring its connection with the paths.

Neither analysis itself means moral degradation or punishment of the bee.

Al-Ṭabarī: two analyses of dhululan

Al-Mulk 67:15 also describes the earth as dhalūlan.

Dākhir, however, can convey clear humiliation in human judgment and punishment contexts.

DH L L and D KH R therefore cannot be complete synonyms in every verse.

Their common element is lowliness or the absence of resistance and superiority before another power or condition; each derivative’s meaning and value depend on context.

Study of DH L L

D KH R and KH Ḍ ʿ: lowliness and submission

Khuḍūʿ concerns humility and lowering one’s bearing or position before another.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:4 contains fa-ẓallat aʿnāquhum lahā khāḍiʿīn.

Submission is connected there with necks and a sign bringing them into lowliness.

D KH R can likewise concern submission, but lexical explanations foreground abasement and ṣaghār.

Khāḍiʿūn can therefore explain dākhirūn in Al-Naḥl 16:48, while the same equivalent in Ghāfir 40:60 may not fully convey the humiliation following arrogance.

Submissive suits some contexts but should not replace every use of dākhir uniformly.

Study of KH Ḍ ʿ

D KH R and KH SH ʿ: humility and khushūʿ

Qur’anic KH SH ʿ describes humans, sight, sound, and even land.

Some uses concern humility and the subsiding of a superior bearing.

Khushūʿ can particularly foreground a quality of conduct, condition, or outward appearance.

Dukhūr in the uses examined here chiefly concerns lowliness before greater power.

Both may occur together in one condition, but their meanings differ.

Submission, khushūʿ, and abasement may coexist at judgment without making every humble person disgraced or degraded.

Study of KH SH ʿ

D KH R and K B R: lowliness and self-importance

Ghāfir 40:60 clearly contrasts yastakbirūn with dākhirīn.

Arrogance here concerns claiming superiority and refusing servitude to The God.

Dukhūr conveys lowliness and abasement.

Someone considering themselves superior to truth in this life may encounter the collapse of that claim at judgment.

Dukhūr should not, however, mean repentance or abandoning arrogance.

A person may turn from arrogance and consciously choose humility; unavoidable lowliness at judgment is not itself that voluntary moral reform.

This is among the central distinctions in the root’s human Qur’anic usage.

Study of K B R

D KH R and DH KH R: abasement and storing

Only their first letters differ.

D KH R concerns abasement and lowliness.

DH KH R concerns keeping and storing something for the future.

Taddakhirūn in Āl ʿImrān 3:49 belongs to DH KH R.

Unvowelled forms such as ادخر may cause confusion, but iddikhār meaning storage derives from DH KH R rather than D KH R.

The two families should remain separate in root indexes and lexical articles.

Al-Rāghib: iddikhār belongs to a different family

8. Suggested translations for Forqan

Across its four Qur’anic uses, dākhir has a common core but should not receive one invariant Persian equivalent.

Al-Naḥl 16:48

وَهُمْ دَاخِرُونَ

wa-hum dākhirūna

Suitable rendering:

“While they themselves are submissive.”

This accords with creation’s submission and prostration to The God.

“Lowly before The God” more closely conveys the lexical core, but can make a Persian verse translation heavier.

“Disgraced and degraded” does not suit this context, since natural creatures’ sin or punishment is not mentioned.

Al-Naml 27:87

وَكُلٌّ أَتَوْهُ دَاخِرِينَ

wa-kullun atawhu dākhirīna

Suitable rendering:

“And all will come to Him in submission.”

This conveys everyone’s submission and lowliness without making every attendee sinful or deserving of humiliation.

“They come before Him in a lowly condition” is defensible too, but the equivalent using humble submission is more natural in Persian.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:18

قُلْ نَعَمْ وَأَنْتُمْ دَاخِرُونَ

qul naʿam wa-antum dākhirūna

Suitable rendering:

“Say: Yes, while you are abased.”

The addressees deny and mock resurrection; abased therefore conveys the force better than humble.

Ghāfir 40:60

سَيَدْخُلُونَ جَهَنَّمَ دَاخِرِينَ

sa-yadkhulūna jahannama dākhirīna

Suitable rendering:

“They will enter Hell abased.”

This preserves their condition upon entering and its contrast with their previous arrogance.

A Persian equivalent of lowly on entering Hell is lexically possible, but conveys less of the humiliation and abasement in this context.

“They enter Hell driven away” is unsuitable: dākhir belongs to D KH R, not D Ḥ R.

Translation conclusion

Forqan’s base translation should choose between humble, lowly, and abased according to each verse’s subject.

Creation’s submission, universal appearance before The God, and arrogant people’s humiliation are three different contexts.

None requires inserting sinfulness, repentance, expulsion from a particular place, or complete destruction merely because dākhir appears.

9. Conclusion

Arabic D KH R denotes lowliness, abasement, and a condition in which a person or creature has no superior or self-important standing before a greater power.

Early dictionaries explain it through dhull and ṣaghār. These can involve human degradation and humiliation, but Qur’anic uses do not make punitive humiliation necessary in every occurrence.

The root occurs four times, exclusively as forms of the active participle dākhir.

Al-Naḥl 16:48 concerns creation’s submission before The God. Humility and lowliness fit better here than degradation for sin.

Dākhirīn in Al-Naml 27:87 describes everyone’s appearance before The God at judgment. This universal submission does not itself make every attendee sinful.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:18 addresses resurrection’s deniers and foregrounds humiliation and the collapse of their position of denial.

Dākhirīn in Ghāfir 40:60 stands directly against yastakbirūn. Those asserting superiority against servitude to The God in worldly life eventually encounter abasement.

Dukhūr itself therefore means neither sin, punishment, prostration, nor repentance. Its primary meaning is lowliness and abasement; context specifies the cause, moral value, and quality of that condition.

It should not merge with D Ḥ R, driving or removing, or DH KH R, storing.

Proposed final definition: D KH R denotes lowliness and becoming abased before a superior power or position. In creation’s submission it can convey obedience and the absence of independent superiority; in contexts of arrogance and judgment, it can emerge as humiliation and abasement. The subject and context, rather than the root alone, determine the quality and value of this lowliness. Brief root meaning: Lowliness, submission, and abasement; context determines the quality and value of this condition.
