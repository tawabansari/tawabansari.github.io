Core meaning: biting and pressing something with the teeth; extended uses include gripping firmly, refusing to let go, causing injury, and describing pressure and hardship. In the Quran, biting fingers and hands depicts intense anger and regret.

1. The root and its meaning in early Arabic

The triliteral ع ض ض is a doubled root: its second and third consonants are identical. Its established primary meaning is biting something and pressing it between the teeth.

عَضَّ الشَّيْءَ means to grip something with the teeth and exert pressure on it. This may serve eating, grasping, holding, or injuring. Biting itself has no fixed moral value; the object and context determine the action's character and consequences.

Someone may bite fruit to eat part of it. An animal may bite a person or another animal in defense or attack. Both involve gripping and pressing with the teeth, but their purposes and consequences differ.

Early dictionaries record عَضَّ, عَضّ, عَضِيض, and عِضَاض in this semantic field.

عَاضّ names a person or animal that bites; مَعْضُوض describes what has been bitten.

عَضُوض describes an animal that bites or habitually does so. كَلْبٌ عَضُوضٌ, for example, is a biting dog.

Early Arabic also uses this verb for a snake's bite, performed with its teeth. Precise lexical usage distinguishes a scorpion's sting, whose mechanism differs.

Several extended uses are associated with this bodily meaning.

First, gripping firmly and not letting go: a firm grip between the teeth can hold something and prevent its separation.

Second, injury and pressure: a bite can involve pain, wounds, and intense pressure.

Third, describing harsh and biting conditions, such as war, difficult times, or affliction that puts someone under pressure.

Biting one's fingers or hands can also outwardly express anger, helplessness, or remorse.

These feelings are not fixed components of the root. Someone can bite without anger or regret, or experience intense anger and regret without biting their fingers.

The basic meaning of ع ض ض can therefore be defined as:

Gripping and pressing something with the teeth, with extensions to holding firmly, injuring, and depicting pressure or intense feelings.

This definition must remain distinct from the sense of any particular use.

2. Early poetic and lexical evidence

An early example occurs in poetry attributed to al-Mutanakkib, Amr ibn Jabir al-Khuza'i, identified in biographical sources as an early poet:

تَنَكَّبْتُ لِلْحَرْبِ الْعَضُوضِ الَّتِي أَرَى
أَلَا مَنْ يُحَارِبْ قَوْمَهُ يَتَنَكَّبِ

tanakkabtu li-al-ḥarbi al-ʿaḍūḍi allatī arā / alā man yuḥārib qawmahu yatanakkabi

“I have turned aside from the biting war I see; whoever fights his own people turns aside.”

الْحَرْبِ الْعَضُوضِ describes a biting, harsh, and injurious war.

War has no teeth with which to bite physically. The poet draws on the painful and injurious character of biting.

The image illustrates the literary extension of عَضُوض from a biting animal to a harsh, injurious situation.

عَضُوض does not simply mean a long war or war between relatives. Harshness and biting severity come from the adjective; fighting one's own people is expressed elsewhere in the verse.

The verse is transmitted under this poet's name, but its precise date has not been independently established in this study. It illustrates early usage and semantic extension, without conclusively dating the origin of the figurative meaning.

Arabic dictionaries also record زَمَنٌ عَضُوضٌ and حَرْبٌ عَضُوضٌ.

زَمَنٌ عَضُوضٌ denotes harsh, oppressive times whose difficulties place people in distress.

Biting again figuratively describes pressure and injury.

Another recorded expression is عَضَّ الثِّقَافُ بِأَنَابِيبِ الرُّمْحِ: the spear-straightening implement firmly gripped the sections of the shaft.

No bodily teeth are involved. The retained image is firm gripping and pressure.

The extension from teeth to implements therefore involves more than injury: holding firmly and not letting go is also significant.

Their inclusion in later dictionaries does not by itself prove that all such expressions were current before Islam. Each precise dating requires independent, datable evidence.

The bodily sense of biting is clear in classical Arabic, and Quranic usage attests it in the language of revelation. Early poetry also illustrates extensions to hardship and injury, though the chronology of all these branches cannot be established with certainty.

3. Lexical and Quranic forms

Important forms include:

عَضَّ: To bite and press something with the teeth.

يَعَضُّ: The imperfect verb: he bites.

عَضّ: The verbal noun of biting.

عَضِيض: Biting; in some constructions, close adherence or firm gripping.

عِضَاض: Biting, especially in descriptions of biting animals.

عَاضّ: One who bites; active participle.

مَعْضُوض: Bitten; passive participle.

عَضُوض: A biting animal; figuratively, a harsh and injurious situation.

تَعَاضَّ: Mutual biting, when two parties bite one another.

Dictionaries also record عِضّ for a harsh or ill-tempered person and عُضّ for certain animal feeds and plants. These forms should not be reduced to biting on the basis of shared letters without examining their particular meanings and histories.

تَعْضُوض also names a variety of dates. The name alone does not demonstrate a direct semantic connection with biting.

Quranic forms

The Quran contains two verbal forms of ع ض ض:

  • عَضُّوا in Al Imran 3:119.
  • يَعَضُّ in Al-Furqan 25:27.

Both are Form I verbs.

عَضُّوا is perfect, third-person masculine plural. The final و is the plural subject pronoun.

يَعَضُّ is imperfect, third-person masculine singular.

In both verbs, the root's two ض consonants merge into one geminated ض. The shaddah reflects the doubled root, not Form II derivation.

Nouns such as عَاضّ, مَعْضُوض, and عَضُوض do not occur in the Quran.

Both Quranic uses describe biting one's own body parts, rather than an enemy or another person.

Their motives differ: intense anger in one verse, remorse and regret in the other.

4. عَضُّوا عَلَيْكُمُ الْأَنَامِلَ: biting fingertips in anger

The first occurrence in Quranic order is in Surah Al Imran:

هَا أَنْتُمْ أُولَاءِ تُحِبُّونَهُمْ وَلَا يُحِبُّونَكُمْ وَتُؤْمِنُونَ بِالْكِتَابِ كُلِّهِ وَإِذَا لَقُوكُمْ قَالُوا آمَنَّا وَإِذَا خَلَوْا عَضُّوا عَلَيْكُمُ الْأَنَامِلَ مِنَ الْغَيْظِ قُلْ مُوتُوا بِغَيْظِكُمْ إِنَّ اللَّهَ عَلِيمٌ بِذَاتِ الصُّدُورِ

hā antum ulāʾi tuḥibbūnahum wa-lā yuḥibbūnakum wa-tuʾminūna bi-al-kitābi kullihi wa-idhā laqūkum qālū āmannā wa-idhā khalaw ʿaḍḍū ʿalaykumu al-anāmila mina al-ghayẓi qul mūtū bi-ghayẓikum inna Allāha ʿalīmun bi-dhāti al-ṣudūr

Āl ʿImrān 3:119

Al Imran 3:119

“Here you are, loving them while they do not love you, and believing in the entire Scripture. When they meet you, they say: We believe; but when alone, they bite their fingertips in rage against you. Say: Die in your rage. Indeed, The God knows what lies within the breasts.”

عَضُّوا denotes the act of biting.

The object bitten is identified by a separate word: الْأَنَامِلَ.

أَنَامِل is the plural of أَنْمُلَة or أُنْمُلَة, denoting fingertips or terminal finger segments.

عَضُّوا الْأَنَامِلَ therefore means biting the fingertips.

The role of عَلَيْكُمُ

In عَضُّوا عَلَيْكُمُ الْأَنَامِلَ, عَلَيْكُمُ precedes الْأَنَامِلَ.

This does not mean that they bite the people addressed by the verse.

The direct object of عَضُّوا is الْأَنَامِلَ, the fingertips.

Here عَلَيْكُمُ indicates the direction of anger and hostility toward those addressed.

Someone thus bites their own fingertips out of intense anger against another.

This matters in translation: being angry against someone is not the same as biting them.

The role of مِنَ الْغَيْظِ

The phrase continues:

مِنَ الْغَيْظِ

mina al-ghayẓ

Āl ʿImrān 3:119

“Out of intense anger” or “because of rage.”

غَيْظ belongs to غ ي ظ and concerns intense anger, which may build up within someone.

A separate word therefore explains why they bite their fingertips.

عَضُّوا itself means biting, not becoming angry.

In anger, someone may clench a fist, grind their teeth, or bite their fingertips.

Such behavior can outwardly express anger, while each action's lexical meaning remains distinct from the feeling itself.

This verse likewise uses fingertip-biting to express the intensity of anger.

Bodily action or literary expression?

In Arabic, biting the fingers can be an actual act or a literary image of intense anger and helplessness.

It is therefore unnecessary to insist that every person described literally bit their fingertips whenever alone.

Conversely, the bodily image should not be discarded by directly replacing عَضُّوا with “they hated” or “they were hostile.”

The expression's force lies in anger so intense that it is depicted as driving someone to bite their fingers.

Translation should retain that image and derive the anger from مِنَ الْغَيْظِ.

Context and contrasting behavior

The preceding verse, Al Imran 3:118, describes people who spare no effort to harm believers and desire their suffering.

Al Imran 3:119 then describes two contrasting behaviors.

When meeting believers, they profess belief; when alone, they reveal anger and hostility.

The issue is more than an unpleasant feeling: it concerns the difference between outward speech and concealed attitudes.

Biting their fingertips thus depicts anger concealed in the addressees' presence but displayed in private.

This description should not be generalized to everyone who differs from believers in conviction.

The verses concern people with specific conduct: hostility, wishing others distress, double speech, and anger at their good fortune.

Al Imran 3:120 adds that good reaching believers distresses them, while harm reaching believers makes them rejoice.

The context further identifies the people concerned.

عَضُّوا عَلَيْكُمُ الْأَنَامِلَ therefore depicts intense anger and concealed hostility in a group with specified conduct, not every angry person or ideological opponent.

5. يَعَضُّ الظَّالِمُ عَلَىٰ يَدَيْهِ: biting the hands in regret

The second Quranic occurrence is in Surah Al-Furqan:

وَيَوْمَ يَعَضُّ الظَّالِمُ عَلَىٰ يَدَيْهِ يَقُولُ يَا لَيْتَنِي اتَّخَذْتُ مَعَ الرَّسُولِ سَبِيلًا

wa-yawma yaʿaḍḍu al-ẓālimu ʿalā yadayhi yaqūlu yā laytanī ittakhadhtu maʿa al-rasūli sabīlā

Al-Furqān 25:27

Al-Furqan 25:27

“And on the day when the wrongdoer bites both his hands, saying: If only I had taken a path with the Messenger!”

يَعَضُّ retains the primary meaning of biting.

Here يَدَيْهِ occurs instead of الْأَنَامِلَ.

يَدَيْهِ is the dual of يَد with a masculine singular suffix: his two hands.

عَلَىٰ يَدَيْهِ therefore refers to both hands, not merely the fingertips.

Translation should preserve this distinction.

The role of عَلَىٰ

Here يَعَضُّ occurs with the preposition عَلَىٰ.

عَضَّ عَلَىٰ يَدَيْهِ is an established Arabic expression for biting one's hands.

Unlike Al Imran 3:119, عَلَىٰ here introduces the hands being bitten.

In Al Imran, عَلَيْكُمُ identifies those toward whom anger is directed, while الْأَنَامِلَ is the direct object of biting.

In Al-Furqan, عَلَىٰ يَدَيْهِ specifies the body part bitten.

The same preposition should not mechanically receive one meaning in two different constructions.

Does يَعَضُّ mean becoming remorseful?

Read apart from the continuation, يَعَضُّ الظَّالِمُ عَلَىٰ يَدَيْهِ depicts the biting of both hands.

The continuation explains the reason:

يَا لَيْتَنِي اتَّخَذْتُ مَعَ الرَّسُولِ سَبِيلًا

yā laytanī ittakhadhtu maʿa al-rasūli sabīlā

Al-Furqān 25:27

“If only I had taken a path with the Messenger!”

These words express regret about a past choice.

Biting the hands thus depicts intense remorse and sorrow.

Regret is nevertheless not the direct meaning of يَعَضُّ; it emerges from the wrongdoer's words and context.

After a harmful decision, someone may put a hand to their head or bite their fingers and say: “If only I had not made that decision.”

The bodily movement signals an inner feeling, but movement and feeling remain distinguishable.

The reason for regret in the following verses

The wrongdoer continues:

يَا وَيْلَتَىٰ لَيْتَنِي لَمْ أَتَّخِذْ فُلَانًا خَلِيلًا

yā waylatā laytanī lam attakhidh fulānan khalīlā

Al-Furqān 25:28

Al-Furqan 25:28

“Woe to me! If only I had not taken so-and-so as a close friend!”

The next verse states:

لَقَدْ أَضَلَّنِي عَنِ الذِّكْرِ بَعْدَ إِذْ جَاءَنِي وَكَانَ الشَّيْطَانُ لِلْإِنْسَانِ خَذُولًا

la-qad aḍallanī ʿani al-dhikri baʿda idh jāʾanī wa-kāna al-shayṭānu li-al-insāni khadhūlā

Al-Furqān 25:29

Al-Furqan 25:29

“He led me astray from the Reminder after it had reached me; and Satan is ever one who abandons the human being.”

These verses concern more than failure to attain a worldly desire. The regret concerns a companion and a path that led away from divine remembrance.

The wrongdoer mentions two choices: the path not taken with the Messenger, and the close friendship he did choose.

This contrast is important to understanding his regret.

A person may initially consider someone's companionship beneficial or pleasant, only to discover that the attachment has drawn them away from truth and responsibility.

The regret then concerns not merely the companion but one's own decision to follow a mistaken path.

This conclusion should not be inserted into the lexical meaning of يَعَضُّ. The verb still depicts biting; the subject of regret emerges from Al-Furqan 25:27, Al-Furqan 25:28, and Al-Furqan 25:29.

The passage also says الظَّالِمُ, without naming someone to whom the image can conclusively be restricted.

The verse should therefore not be confined without evidence to one historical person's story.

6. Biting in anger and biting in regret

The two uses place the same bodily action in different emotional situations.

In Al Imran 3:119, people bite their fingertips in intense anger against others.

In Al-Furqan 25:27, the wrongdoer bites both hands when confronted with the outcome of his own choices.

In the first, anger is directed at others.

In the second, regret concerns one's own past choices and behavior.

ع ض ض is thus not inherently restricted to anger or remorse.

Biting is constant in both; context determines the feeling and motive.

This distinction is particularly important in translation.

Rendering عَضُّوا merely as “they became angry” removes the fingertip-biting image.

Rendering يَعَضُّ merely as “he regrets” removes the image of biting both hands.

Nor should the image be explained as a compulsory bodily behavior shared by every angry or remorseful person.

Biting may be understood as an actual act or a literary depiction of intense feeling. Either way, translation should retain the bodily image and let context clarify the emotion.

7. Semantic extension: from teeth to pressure and adherence

The bodily features of biting support uses of this family in other settings.

Holding firmly and not letting go

When teeth firmly grip something, it becomes difficult to pull away.

عَضَّ عَلَى الشَّيْءِ can accordingly express clinging firmly to something and not releasing it.

Actual biting is not necessary in such uses; the point is persistence in holding on.

For example, someone may insist firmly on a decision. A grip with the teeth can figuratively depict such persistence.

This does not make ع ض ض inherently mean praiseworthy steadfastness or adherence to truth.

A person may persist in a right or wrong decision. Its object and outcome determine the value of that persistence.

Biting and injurious speech

Dictionaries record عَضَّهُ بِلِسَانِهِ for harming someone with speech.

The tongue does not literally bite like teeth; rather, speech can be biting, hurtful, or injurious.

For example, insults and disparagement may harm another person's reputation or feelings.

This use draws on biting, but should not become the lexical meaning of every form of ع ض ض.

Biting times and warfare

زَمَنٌ عَضُوضٌ and حَرْبٌ عَضُوضٌ are established figurative expressions.

Harsh conditions are depicted as if biting and pressuring people.

عَضُوض here concerns pressure and injury, not teeth belonging to time or war.

Such uses show Arabic drawing on tangible bodily images to describe difficult situations and human emotions.

The Quran uses this expressive capacity to depict anger and regret.

8. Comparison with neighboring roots

ع ض ض and م ض غ

م ض غ concerns chewing and breaking down food with the teeth.

عَضَّ emphasizes gripping and pressing with the teeth.

A person may first bite food and then chew it. These acts are related but different.

An animal may also bite something without eating or chewing it.

Al Imran 3:119 and Al-Furqan 25:27 concern biting body parts, not chewing food.

عَضُّوا and يَعَضُّ should therefore not be translated as “they chewed” and “he chews.”

ع ض ض and ن ه ش

نَهْش concerns biting, tearing, or seizing part of something with the teeth.

عَضّ also means biting, emphasizing pressure and gripping with the teeth in its primary sense.

Some uses of نهش more clearly involve biting and tearing off flesh, whereas عض does not necessarily entail separating a piece.

Someone can bite a finger without detaching any part of it.

The verb عض alone therefore does not establish that the fingers or hands in these verses are severed or eaten.

This is a tendency, not an absolute rule for every use of the two roots.

ع ض ض and ق ض م

قَضْم concerns biting and eating small portions of something.

In Arabic usage, it is particularly associated with taking food using the front teeth.

عَضّ is the more general term for biting and pressing with the teeth.

The verses do not specify which teeth are used. No precise biting technique should be imposed on عَضُّوا or يَعَضُّ beyond the text.

ع ض ض and غ ي ظ

غ ي ظ concerns intense anger.

عَضُّوا and الْغَيْظِ appear in the same sentence in Al Imran 3:119.

Their combination distinguishes the bodily act from the emotion causing it.

عضّ is biting; غيظ is intense anger.

A single translation equivalent should not erase their distinction.

ع ض ض and ن د م

ندم concerns remorse over an action or regret for a lost opportunity.

In Al-Furqan 25:27, biting both hands accompanies the wrongdoer's remorseful words.

عَضَّ itself does not mean remorse.

A root may occur in different emotional contexts without naming those emotions.

ع ض ض and ع ض د

These roots share their first two consonants but differ in meaning.

ع ض ض concerns biting.

ع ض د primarily names the upper arm and extends to a helper or support.

عَضُد in Al-Qasas 28:35 and Al-Kahf 18:51 belongs to ع ض د, not ع ض ض.

Their spelling resemblance does not justify identifying the two families.

ع ض ض and ع ض ل

ع ض ل concerns restraining, constraining, and obstructing.

تَعْضُلُوهُنَّ in Al-Baqarah 2:232 belongs to this root and concerns preventing women from marrying.

عَضَّ, however, means biting.

The imagery of biting pressure and constraint may be close, but that does not make the roots identical.

ع ض ض and ع ض و

عِضِينَ in Al-Hijr 15:91 is commonly analyzed in connection with ع ض و, though lexicographers have differed over its origin.

Shared initial consonants do not make it a form of ع ض ض.

Each form requires analysis based on its own morphology and actual usage.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

The primary equivalent in both Quranic uses is “to bite.”

It is concise, natural, and faithful to the Arabic.

عَضُّوا — Al Imran 3:119

Word: عَضُّوا

Root: ع ض ض

Word type: Perfect verb, third-person masculine plural, Form I.

Brief root meaning: Biting and pressing something with the teeth.

Meaning here: They bit; in context, biting their fingertips in intense anger.

Suggested translation:

وَإِذَا خَلَوْا عَضُّوا عَلَيْكُمُ الْأَنَامِلَ مِنَ الْغَيْظِ

wa-idhā khalaw ʿaḍḍū ʿalaykumu al-anāmila mina al-ghayẓ

Āl ʿImrān 3:119

“When alone, they bite their fingertips in rage against you.”

Here “they bite” renders عَضُّوا in a conditional construction describing repeated behavior.

“Fingertips” comes from الْأَنَامِلَ, and “in rage” from مِنَ الْغَيْظِ.

عَضُّوا itself should not be changed to “they became angry.”

يَعَضُّ — Al-Furqan 25:27

Word: يَعَضُّ

Root: ع ض ض

Word type: Imperfect verb, third-person masculine singular, Form I.

Brief root meaning: Biting and pressing something with the teeth.

Meaning here: He bites; biting both hands in a situation of regret.

Suggested translation:

وَيَوْمَ يَعَضُّ الظَّالِمُ عَلَىٰ يَدَيْهِ

wa-yawma yaʿaḍḍu al-ẓālimu ʿalā yadayh

Al-Furqān 25:27

“And on the day when the wrongdoer bites both his hands.”

This retains the Arabic image.

“Both hands” comes from يَدَيْهِ and should not become “fingers.”

Adding “in remorse” to this excerpt is unnecessary, since the wrongdoer's subsequent words and the following two verses clearly express his regret.

A reflection can explain the feeling as a contextual conclusion without replacing the verb's direct meaning.

Conclusion

ع ض ض denotes biting and pressing something with the teeth. The act may serve eating, grasping, holding, or injuring.

Extended uses depict firm adherence, refusal to release, pressure, injury, and hardship. حَرْبٌ عَضُوضٌ and زَمَنٌ عَضُوضٌ illustrate these extensions.

The root occurs in only two Quranic verses, both using Form I verbs.

In Al Imran 3:119, عَضُّوا describes people biting their fingertips in intense anger against believers. الْغَيْظِ identifies the anger and عَلَيْكُمُ its direction.

In Al-Furqan 25:27, يَعَضُّ describes a wrongdoer biting both hands and regretting the path he did not take and the companionship that drew him away from divine remembrance.

Biting thus depicts two different emotions: intense anger against others and regret over one's own choices.

The semantic core of ع ض ض is biting and pressing with the teeth. Anger, regret, injury, and firm adherence must be understood from each form and context, rather than all being merged into the root's direct definition.

Forqan should preserve the bodily image of biting, which conveys the intensity of the feelings and situations described.
