Naqm and intiqām in early Arabic and the Qur’an: from faultfinding and hostility toward others’ faith to divine punishment, justice, and responsibility for actions

The three-consonant root N Q M in Arabic concerns displeasure with something, disapproving of it, finding fault, and reacting to conduct considered wrong or objectionable. In other uses of the same family, this displeasure can lead to reproach, retaliation, and punishment. Niqmah, intiqām, and muntaqim belong to this semantic field.

In ordinary human usage, revenge is often associated with anger, hostility, and a desire to respond to harm received from another. Yet not every Arabic derivative of this root means taking revenge in practice. Someone may disapprove of another’s conduct or find fault without taking steps to punish that person.

Nor is displeasure always justified. A person may resent another’s good conduct and even make it a pretext for hostility. Several Qur’anic verses present precisely this situation: people treating others’ faith or receipt of blessings as grounds for faultfinding and hostility.

When the Qur’an attributes intiqām to The God, however, it concerns punishment and a response to actions such as injustice, crime, denying the signs, and deliberate disobedience. These uses connect intiqām with divine power and the consequences of human actions.

A study of N Q M must therefore distinguish displeasure and faultfinding, taking punitive action, and whether the response is just or unjust. The first two belong to the root’s semantic field; the third requires attention to conduct, the agent responding, and each verse’s context.

1. Forms and semantic core

Important Arabic forms include:

  • نَقَمَ and نَقِمَ (naqama and naqima): to be displeased with something, disapprove of it, or find fault with someone.
  • نَقْم and نُقُوم (naqm and nuqūm): displeasure, faultfinding, and disapproval.
  • نَاقِم (nāqim): someone displeased or finding fault with another.
  • نَقْمَة and نِقْمَة (naqmah and niqmah): punishment, retribution, and, in some uses, displeasure and rejection.
  • نَقِمَات and نِقَم (naqimāt and niqam): recorded plurals of niqmah.
  • اِنْتَقَمَ (intaqama): to retaliate against or punish another in response to conduct.
  • اِنْتِقَام (intiqām): retaliation and punishment in response to an objectionable act or behavior.
  • مُنْتَقِم (muntaqim): someone who retaliates or takes punitive action.
  • مُنْتَقِمُون (muntaqimūn): the plural of muntaqim, used in the Qur’an to describe divine action.

Arabic lexicons also record naqīmah in expressions such as maymūn al-naqīmah. Some explanations regard it as a substitution of mīm for bāʾ in naqībah. Similar letters alone should therefore not make it independent evidence for the principal meaning of N Q M.

Nāqim is also recorded as a variety of dates in Oman and, in some accounts, as the name of a place or group of people. Such names in lexicons do not by themselves establish a descriptive meaning or a historical reason for the naming.

Qur’anic forms

N Q M occurs seventeen times in sixteen Qur’anic verses.

Four occurrences are basic-stem verbs, six are Form VIII verbs, four are the verbal noun intiqām, and three are active participles of that same form.

The Qur’anic forms are:

تَنْقِمُونَ، تَنْقِمُ، نَقَمُوا، فَيَنْتَقِمُ، فَانْتَقَمْنَا، انْتَقَمْنَا، انْتِقَام، مُنْتَقِمُونَ.

tanqimūna, tanqimu, naqamū, fa-yantaqimu, fa-intaqamnā, intaqamnā, intiqām, muntaqimūna

Differences in the attachment of fa-, pronouns, and grammatical case do not necessarily create a new derivative.

Naqmah, niqmah, and nāqim are known in Arabic, but do not occur in the Qur’anic text. Nor does the definite singular al-muntaqim occur, although other derivatives of the family describe The God.

Semantic core: disapproval and response

One important lexical core identified for this family is “rejecting something and finding fault with it.”

In basic usage, naqama ʿalayhi means being displeased with another’s conduct and objecting to or finding fault with it.

In intaqama minhu, the response goes beyond displeasure or faultfinding to punishment.

The connection is clear: someone considers an act objectionable and may take action against its doer in response.

This does not mean that every displeasure eventually results in retaliation.

Nor does the root itself prove that the conduct criticized was actually wrong. Someone may disapprove of injustice and corruption or, conversely, resent another’s faith and uprightness.

This is particularly important in the Qur’anic uses, since several verses attribute faultfinding to people whose own position is wrong.

N Q M must therefore be defined to preserve both displeasure and faultfinding and the punitive branch, without placing the objection’s validity or the punishment’s justice within the word in advance.

2. N Q M in early Arabic: faultfinding, displeasure, and responses to conduct

Displeasure and objection

Arabic recognizes the following expression:

نَقَمْتُ عَلَيْهِ فِعْلَهُ

naqamtu ʿalayhi fiʿlahu

Translation: “I disapproved of his action and found fault with him for it.”

The object of displeasure here is the person’s conduct.

Arabic also says:

نَقَمْتُ الْأَمْرَ

naqamtu al-amra

Translation: “I disliked that action.”

Naqama here concerns dislike and displeasure without necessarily describing action against anyone.

The basic verb is recorded in two forms, naqama and naqima. The difference in vowels does not fundamentally alter the root’s semantic field.

Some accounts describe naqm as strong displeasure and faultfinding accompanied by reproach.

Its intensity should nevertheless not be assumed identical in every use. Someone can object to conduct, but the verb alone does not specify the degree of anger or reaction.

A poetic witness: what fault can be found with me?

A rajaz passage attributed to Abū Jahl in accounts of the Battle of Badr says:

مَا تَنْقِمُ الْحَرْبُ الْعَوَانُ مِنِّي
بَازِلُ عَامَيْنِ حَدِيثٌ سِنِّي

mā tanqimu al-ḥarbu al-ʿawānu minnī / bāzilu ʿāmayni ḥadīthun sinnī

لِمِثْلِ هَذَا وَلَدَتْنِي أُمِّي

li-mithli hādhā waladatnī ummī

Translation: “What fault can seasoned, fierce war find with me? Though young, I am like a powerful, mature camel; my mother bore me for a day like this.”

The speaker praises his strength and courage and asks what fault war could find with him.

Tanqimu here means faultfinding, rather than war literally being a willing person intending revenge.

This shows naqm used figuratively for objection or finding fault.

The attribution is disputed. The passage is attributed to Abū Jahl in an account of Badr and to ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib in other transmissions. Variants also occur in ḥadīthun sinnī and other parts of the rajaz.

The witness is useful for early usage, but neither its attribution nor a pre-Islamic date should be presented as certain.

Finding fault with something actually considered good

ʿUbayd Allāh ibn Qays al-Ruqayyāt, the Umayyad-period poet, says in praise of the Umayyads:

مَا نَقَمُوا مِنْ بَنِي أُمَيَّةَ إِلَّا
أَنَّهُمْ يَحْلُمُونَ إِنْ غَضِبُوا

mā naqamū min banī umayyata illā / annahum yaḥlumūna in ghaḍibū

Translation: “They found no fault with the Umayyads except that they show forbearance when angry.”

The construction outwardly expresses faultfinding, yet what is mentioned as a fault is a praiseworthy quality in the speaker’s view.

His point is that the opponents have no real, defensible fault to criticize.

This manner of expression clearly resembles certain Qur’anic uses of N Q M.

The Qur’an, for example, also questions people who make others’ faith a cause of displeasure.

This witness belongs to Umayyad-period poetry and should therefore not be presented as pre-Qur’anic.

It nevertheless shows that faultfinding and the construction mā naqamū … illā were established in classical Arabic.

From objection to punishment

In Arabic, intaqama minhu concerns taking action against someone in response to something that person has done.

Lexical accounts explain intiqām through punishment and responding to prior conduct.

Naqama and intaqama are thus connected, but do not have identical functions.

Someone may disapprove of and complain about another’s conduct; this involves displeasure and faultfinding.

When the response directs punishment or harm at the other person, intiqām becomes more appropriate.

Form VIII alone, however, cannot establish that retaliation always involves prior planning, intense anger, a long delay, or a personal desire to satisfy resentment.

Such features require evidence from conduct and context.

In early Arabic, intiqām was also associated with blood vengeance and responding to harm. A proverb involving the arqam snake says that, if it is killed, vengeance may be sought for it. Lexical accounts transmit the expression alongside an explanation of old beliefs about snakes and revenge.

Recording the proverb does not mean that intiqām always means tribal blood vengeance.

The word has a broader field of response and punishment.

Niqmah and other family names

Naqmah and niqmah are recorded in Arabic for punishment and retribution.

Persian also knows niqmat, often contrasted with niʿmat, “blessing.”

The familiar contrast between blessing and punishment should not cause their families to be treated as historically one root.

Niʿmah comes from N ʿ M, whereas niqmah comes from N Q M.

Study of N ʿ M

Similar sound and a possible semantic contrast do not prove an etymological relationship.

Nor should niqmah always be interpreted as poverty, illness, or a natural disaster. Its established uses involve punishment; whether a particular event is actually punishment requires independent evidence.

Illness or an earthquake, for example, does not by itself justify declaring the event divine punishment for a particular group.

3. The basic verb in the Qur’an: resentment of faith and blessings

The four basic-stem occurrences are particularly important because they show displeasure and faultfinding directed at conduct the Qur’an itself presents as good and right.

Al-Māʾidah 5:59: faultfinding over faith

قُلْ يَا أَهْلَ الْكِتَابِ هَلْ تَنْقِمُونَ مِنَّا إِلَّا أَنْ آمَنَّا بِاللَّهِ وَمَا أُنْزِلَ إِلَيْنَا وَمَا أُنْزِلَ مِنْ قَبْلُ وَأَنَّ أَكْثَرَكُمْ فَاسِقُونَ

qul yā ahla al-kitābi hal tanqimūna minnā illā an āmannā bi-Allāhi wa-mā unzila ilaynā wa-mā unzila min qablu wa-anna aktharakum fāsiqūna

Translation: “Say: People of the Book, do you find fault with us for anything except that we believe in The God, what was sent down to us, and what was sent down before, and that most of you are defiantly disobedient?”

Tanqimūna minnā concerns displeasure with and criticism of the Muslims’ position.

The question rebukes and challenges: what about believing in The God and revelation merits such objection and displeasure?

Naqm here does not mean punishment.

The displeasure may lead to hostile behavior, but the verb in this expression concerns objecting and disapproving.

The ending about most of the addressees’ disobedience also belongs to the sentence’s argument. It does not come from N Q M.

The important point is that others’ displeasure concerns faith here, rather than a fault the Qur’an affirms as such.

Naqama therefore does not itself prove that the criticized person actually did something wrong.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:126: Pharaoh’s objection to the magicians’ faith

وَمَا تَنْقِمُ مِنَّا إِلَّا أَنْ آمَنَّا بِآيَاتِ رَبِّنَا لَمَّا جَاءَتْنَا

wa-mā tanqimu minnā illā an āmannā bi-āyāti rabbinā lammā jāʾatnā

Translation: “You find fault with us only because we believed in our Lord’s signs when they came to us.”

These are the words of the magicians who believed after recognizing Moses’ sign.

Pharaoh has threatened them in the preceding verses and spoken of severe punishment.

They reply that his sole cause for displeasure and hostility is their belief in their Lord’s signs.

Naqm here occurs amid a real threat, but tanqimu minnā still directly means hostile displeasure and faultfinding.

The threatened punishment is known from Pharaoh’s other words, rather than every detail of the threats being contained in naqama itself.

The verse also shows that a powerful person may resent others’ pursuit of truth and take action against them.

That displeasure provides no legitimacy for punishment.

Al-Tawbah 9:74: resentment despite receiving blessings

وَمَا نَقَمُوا إِلَّا أَنْ أَغْنَاهُمُ اللَّهُ وَرَسُولُهُ مِنْ فَضْلِهِ

wa-mā naqamū illā an aghnāhumu Allāhu wa-rasūluhu min faḍlihi

Translation: “They resented nothing except that The God and His messenger had enriched them from His bounty.”

The context concerns people who uttered words of disbelief, disbelieved after their submission, and attempted something they failed to accomplish.

The Qur’an then says that their cause for resentment was precisely their receipt of bounty and generosity.

The expression exposes the contradiction between their conduct and the blessing they received.

Naqm here does not mean ingratitude, although the conduct described can exemplify it.

N Q M itself concerns displeasure and objection; ingratitude is understood from the relationship between that conduct and the blessing received.

Nor does the verse imply that becoming wealthy is inherently objectionable. The issue is the hostile and ungrateful response of people who had received bounty.

Al-Burūj 85:8: faith as the sole pretext for hostility

وَمَا نَقَمُوا مِنْهُمْ إِلَّا أَنْ يُؤْمِنُوا بِاللَّهِ الْعَزِيزِ الْحَمِيدِ

wa-mā naqamū minhum illā an yuʾminū bi-Allāhi al-ʿazīzi al-ḥamīdi

Translation: “They found fault with them only for believing in The God, the Mighty, the Praiseworthy.”

The preceding verses concern the people of the trench and a fire prepared to torment and burn believers.

Naqamū in this context concerns the oppressors’ displeasure with and faultfinding against believers.

What they criticize, however, is faith in The God.

Their oppressive conduct is therefore no evidence that the believers’ faith was truly a fault or crime.

This is among the clearest Qur’anic examples showing that naqm can express unjust resentment and hostility toward right conduct.

Defining the root as “objecting to wrongdoing” is thus imprecise: the word itself does not establish that the object of criticism is wrong.

What the four basic-stem occurrences show

In Al-Māʾidah 5:59, Al-Aʿrāf 7:126, and Al-Burūj 85:8, the object of displeasure is faith.

In Al-Tawbah 9:74, receiving bounty and blessings occurs in a setting of resentment and ungrateful conduct.

Naqm concerns faultfinding, disapproval, and displeasure in all these instances.

The root itself does not determine whether the reaction is right or wrong.

Together, the verses show that prejudice, interests, or refusal to accept truth can lead people to make even another’s right conduct a pretext for objection and hostility.

This conclusion comes from the uses considered together; prejudice or hostility to faith is not the permanent meaning of N Q M.

4. Intiqām in the Qur’an: punishment in response to conduct

The other branch appears through intaqama, the verbal noun intiqām, and the active participle muntaqim.

All these Qur’anic uses attribute intiqām to The God.

The difference from the basic verb matters. In the earlier instances, people criticized others’ conduct, sometimes unjustly. In divine intiqām, however, the Qur’an identifies behavior connected with injustice, crime, denial, and disobedience.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:136: punishment and drowning

فَانْتَقَمْنَا مِنْهُمْ فَأَغْرَقْنَاهُمْ فِي الْيَمِّ بِأَنَّهُمْ كَذَّبُوا بِآيَاتِنَا وَكَانُوا عَنْهَا غَافِلِينَ

fa-intaqamnā minhum fa-aghraqnāhum fī al-yammi bi-annahum kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā wa-kānū ʿanhā ghāfilīna

Translation: “So We punished them and drowned them in the sea, because they denied Our signs and were heedless of them.”

The verse concerns Pharaoh and his people.

The sentence first says fa-intaqamnā minhum, then fa-aghraqnāhum.

The expressions are connected, but do not have identical lexical meanings.

Intiqām expresses divine punishment and response; drowning is the manner in which that punishment occurs in this event.

Intiqām should therefore not be defined as drowning in every verse.

The verse also identifies the cause: denying the signs and being heedless of them.

That cause comes from bi-annahum kadhdhabū and the continuation, rather than the independent meaning of N Q M.

Al-Zukhruf 43:55: responding to defiance

فَلَمَّا آسَفُونَا انْتَقَمْنَا مِنْهُمْ فَأَغْرَقْنَاهُمْ أَجْمَعِينَ

fa-lammā āsafūnā intaqamnā minhum fa-aghraqnāhum ajmaʿīna

Translation: “When they angered Us, We punished them and drowned them all.”

This verse also belongs to the account of Pharaoh and his people.

Āsafūnā precedes intaqamnā here.

The first comes from A S F and, in this context, concerns provoking anger or strong displeasure.

Study of A S F

Intiqām, however, comes from N Q M and denotes punishment.

The wording thus distinguishes provoking anger from taking punitive action.

Divine action should nevertheless not be equated without evidence with bodily anger or an impulsive human reaction.

The account describes repeated acts and defiance by Pharaoh and his people. The punishment should therefore be understood in the context of their actions and responsibility.

Al-Ḥijr 15:79 and Al-Zukhruf 43:25: the end of wrongdoers and deniers

Al-Ḥijr 15:79 says:

فَانْتَقَمْنَا مِنْهُمْ وَإِنَّهُمَا لَبِإِمَامٍ مُبِينٍ

fa-intaqamnā minhum wa-innahumā la-bi-imāmin mubīnin

Translation: “So We punished them; both are indeed on a clear road.”

The verse follows mention of the People of the Thicket and concerns their punishment.

Al-Zukhruf 43:25 also says:

فَانْتَقَمْنَا مِنْهُمْ فَانْظُرْ كَيْفَ كَانَ عَاقِبَةُ الْمُكَذِّبِينَ

fa-intaqamnā minhum fa-unẓur kayfa kāna ʿāqibatu al-mukadhdhibīna

Translation: “So We punished them. Look, then, at the outcome of those who denied.”

Intiqām in these verses follows the conduct of groups the Qur’an describes through injustice and denial.

The details of punishment, however, are not contained in intaqamnā itself.

Sometimes the Qur’an specifies the kind of punishment in the same verse; elsewhere, the surrounding verses must be consulted.

Intiqām in these uses denotes the punitive response itself, rather than one physical method repeated in every case.

Al-Rūm 30:47: punishing offenders and helping believers

فَانْتَقَمْنَا مِنَ الَّذِينَ أَجْرَمُوا وَكَانَ حَقًّا عَلَيْنَا نَصْرُ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ

fa-intaqamnā mina alladhīna ajramū wa-kāna ḥaqqan ʿalaynā naṣru al-muʾminīna

Translation: “So We punished those who committed crimes, and helping the believers was a duty upon Us.”

This verse identifies the group through alladhīna ajramū, “those who committed crimes.”

That wording connects the object of punishment with committing an offense.

The continuation also speaks of helping believers.

Divine intiqām here is not an isolated response detached from people’s fate: it appears alongside support for believers and the outcome of offenders.

Intiqām itself should not, however, be defined as helping believers. The verse states the two subjects through independent expressions.

Nor does it justify declaring a group’s victory in every present-day conflict divine retribution without sufficient evidence and knowledge of right and wrong.

Attributing a particular punishment to The God requires Qur’anic grounds or reliable independent evidence.

5. Intiqām, pardon, and warning in Qur’anic rulings

Al-Māʾidah 5:95 is a particularly important use: it concerns individual conduct and a ruling, rather than only the fate of past communities.

Al-Māʾidah 5:95: repeating a violation after pardon

عَفَا اللَّهُ عَمَّا سَلَفَ وَمَنْ عَادَ فَيَنْتَقِمُ اللَّهُ مِنْهُ وَاللَّهُ عَزِيزٌ ذُو انْتِقَامٍ

ʿafā Allāhu ʿammā salafa wa-man ʿāda fa-yantaqimu Allāhu minhu wa-Allāhu ʿazīzun dhū intiqāmin

Translation: “The God has pardoned what is past; whoever returns, The God punishes him. The God is Mighty, Possessor of retribution.”

The passage follows a command concerning hunting while in consecration for pilgrimage.

The Qur’an first prohibits killing game in this state and sets out compensation and expiation for deliberate violation.

It then states that The God has pardoned what is past, but someone returning to the conduct faces divine intiqām.

Intiqām here is connected with repeating prohibited conduct after the ruling has been stated.

Does intiqām here mean vindictiveness?

The verse itself shows a response to violating a ruling.

Before intiqām, it discusses compensation, adjudication by two just people, feeding the needy, and fasting.

The issue therefore belongs to a legal and ethical structure, rather than a personal dispute in which someone harms another to satisfy anger.

“Punishes” is a more natural and precise equivalent for divine intiqām in this context.

Avoiding the image of personal revenge should not, however, remove the word’s punitive force altogether.

The Qur’an explicitly says fa-yantaqimu Allāhu minhu. This warns of a divine response to repeating the violation; it does not merely state that the person may regret the conduct or experience a natural consequence.

The verse does not explain the details of divine intiqām in this instance. The root itself therefore cannot establish that the punishment must occur in this world, in the hereafter, or through a particular method.

Pardon and retribution: contrasting meanings in one context

ʿAfā and intaqama occur alongside one another here.

ʿAfw concerns pardon and passing over.

Intiqām concerns punishment and responding to conduct.

Their association distinguishes pardon for past conduct from warning against its repetition in this context.

The sequence should not become an absolute rule that The God always forgives once and necessarily punishes a second offense in a particular way.

The verse concerns a particular ruling and situation; extending it requires independent grounds.

Nor does intiqām’s presence rule out every possibility of repentance, return, or forgiveness. Those matters require examination alongside other Qur’anic verses.

6. Intiqām as a description of divine action

The Qur’an also uses this family to describe divine power and punishment.

Two principal constructions appear: dhū intiqām and muntaqimūn.

ʿAzīzun dhū intiqām

Āl ʿImrān 3:4 says:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا بِآيَاتِ اللَّهِ لَهُمْ عَذَابٌ شَدِيدٌ وَاللَّهُ عَزِيزٌ ذُو انْتِقَامٍ

inna alladhīna kafarū bi-āyāti Allāhi lahum ʿadhābun shadīdun wa-Allāhu ʿazīzun dhū intiqāmin

Translation: “Those who disbelieve in The God’s signs have a severe punishment. The God is Mighty, Possessor of retribution.”

ʿAzīz and dhū intiqām occur together here.

ʿAzīz concerns power, invincibility, and might.

Dhū intiqām describes possession of the power and capacity to punish.

Their association places divine punishment in the context of a power that arises neither from inability nor from needing others’ support.

The same combination occurs in Al-Māʾidah 5:95 and Ibrāhīm 14:47.

Al-Zumar 39:37 has another form:

أَلَيْسَ اللَّهُ بِعَزِيزٍ ذِي انْتِقَامٍ

a-laysa Allāhu bi-ʿazīzin dhī intiqāmin

Translation: “Is The God not Mighty, Possessor of retribution?”

Dhī is the genitive form of dhū; the case difference does not create a new concept.

Is al-Muntaqim an independent divine name in the Qur’an?

Al-Muntaqim appears in familiar lists of the Most Beautiful Names.

The precise definite form al-muntaqim, however, does not occur in the Qur’an.

The Qur’an uses dhū intiqām and muntaqimūn in divine speech.

This distinction must be preserved.

Dhū intiqām allows punishment to be recognized as a divine attribute, but it should not be claimed without explanation that the Qur’an itself gives exactly the name al-Muntaqim in that form.

Conversely, the absence of the definite form alone does not invalidate intiqām as a divine description: the Qur’an expressly uses the same word family for divine power and action.

Actual Qur’anic forms must be distinguished from later terminological lists and naming conventions.

Innā mina al-mujrimīna muntaqimūn

Al-Sajdah 32:22 says:

وَمَنْ أَظْلَمُ مِمَّنْ ذُكِّرَ بِآيَاتِ رَبِّهِ ثُمَّ أَعْرَضَ عَنْهَا إِنَّا مِنَ الْمُجْرِمِينَ مُنْتَقِمُونَ

wa-man aẓlamu mimman dhukkira bi-āyāti rabbihi thumma aʿraḍa ʿanhā innā mina al-mujrimīna muntaqimūna

Translation: “Who is more unjust than someone reminded of his Lord’s signs who then turns away from them? We indeed punish offenders.”

Muntaqimūn is associated here with offenders.

The preceding wording concerns someone reminded of the signs who nevertheless turns away.

The context connects punishment with human conduct and responsibility.

Muntaqimūn itself should not establish that every turning away or every ignorant mistake incurs one specified punishment regardless of intention and circumstances.

The designation of an offense, the person’s conduct, and the conditions of judgment require examination through the relevant verses and principles.

Al-Zukhruf 43:41: punishment does not depend on the messenger’s presence

فَإِمَّا نَذْهَبَنَّ بِكَ فَإِنَّا مِنْهُمْ مُنْتَقِمُونَ

fa-immā nadhhabanna bika fa-innā minhum muntaqimūna

Translation: “If We take you away, We will indeed punish them.”

The verse belongs to a warning addressed to deniers.

Its point is that their punishment does not depend on the messenger being personally present to witness it.

Muntaqimūn alone, however, specifies neither the timing nor the manner of punishment.

The next verse, Al-Zukhruf 43:42, also mentions the possibility of showing him what they have been promised.

A definite conclusion about punishment’s timing should therefore not be extracted from this description alone.

Al-Dukhān 44:16: al-baṭshah al-kubrā

يَوْمَ نَبْطِشُ الْبَطْشَةَ الْكُبْرَىٰ إِنَّا مُنْتَقِمُونَ

yawma nabṭishu al-baṭshata al-kubrā innā muntaqimūna

Translation: “On the day We seize them with the great, severe seizure: We will indeed punish.”

Muntaqimūn occurs alongside al-baṭshah al-kubrā here.

Baṭsh, from B Ṭ SH, concerns forceful seizing and assault, whereas intiqām denotes punishment.

Study of B Ṭ SH

The expressions are connected in one scene, but do not mean the same thing.

The interpretive tradition differs over the referent of al-baṭshah al-kubrā: some connect it with Badr, others with punishment on the Day of Resurrection.

Understanding that disagreement requires examining the surah’s context and other verses.

For N Q M, however, the point is clear: muntaqimūn denotes punishment, while al-baṭshah al-kubrā separately describes the event’s severity and character.

Intiqām should not be restricted in every use to forceful seizing or to one particular historical event.

Is divine intiqām the same as personal human revenge?

Human revenge may arise from resentment, humiliation, uncontrolled anger, or a desire to answer personal injury.

These features are not necessary elements of every lexical use of intiqām.

The Qur’an places divine intiqām in contexts of power, punishing offenders, denying the signs, and unjust conduct.

The God should therefore not be likened to a person who needs revenge because of weakness, jealousy, or wounded pride.

At the same time, divine intiqām should not be redefined so as to remove punishment and responding to conduct.

Its established meaning is a punitive response. The distinction between divine action and unjust human revenge comes from divine attributes, justice, and the verses’ contexts, rather than deleting the root’s meaning.

7. Comparison with related roots and translation proposals

N Q M (naqm) and K R H (karh)

Karh concerns finding something unpleasant and disliking it.

Naqm can also express displeasure, but its established uses give greater prominence to faultfinding, reproach, and an objecting response.

Someone may dislike a food without treating it as a moral fault or another person’s wrongdoing.

When someone expresses naqm concerning another’s conduct, however, objection or finding fault is usually involved.

This is a tendency, rather than an absolute rule for every Arabic use.

N Q M (naqm) and S KH Ṭ (sakhaṭ)

Sakhaṭ concerns strong displeasure and dissatisfaction.

Naqm may also arise from such a state, but the intiqām branch involves taking punitive action as well.

Displeasure and punishment are therefore not identical.

Someone may be angry or displeased without acting against another.

A just legal punishment may also be carried out without requiring every feature of personal human anger.

N Q M (naqm) and GH Ḍ B (ghaḍab)

Ghaḍab in human usage concerns anger.

Intiqām may accompany anger, but itself denotes response and punishment.

Āsafūnā followed by intaqamnā in Al-Zukhruf 43:55 also shows different wording for strong displeasure and punitive action.

This difference should not become a definite theory of The God’s inner states. Divine action should be understood through the verses themselves and the principle that The God is unlike created beings.

Study of GH Ḍ B

N Q M (naqm) and ʿ Q B (ʿuqūbah)

ʿUqūbah in Arabic is associated with a consequence and response to an act.

Intiqām in many uses is a form of punishment.

Not every unpleasant consequence, however, is necessarily intiqām. Someone may be harmed by an ordinary mistake without the injury being intentional punishment inflicted by another.

Qur’anic uses of divine intiqām concern a punitive response to the conduct of particular groups or individuals.

Every natural consequence should therefore not be called divine intiqām without grounds.

Study of ʿ Q B

N Q M (naqm) and TH A R (thaʾr)

Thaʾr is closely associated with blood vengeance and responding to injury or killing in Arabic.

Intiqām can also occur in this setting.

Its field is broader, however, and need not be limited to blood vengeance or someone’s death.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:95, for example, divine intiqām concerns repeating a violation of a hunting ruling, rather than a tribal dispute over blood vengeance.

“Blood vengeance” is therefore unsuitable as a general equivalent for every use of intiqām.

Translation proposals for Forqan

For Qur’anic uses of the basic verb naqama, Persian equivalents meaning “found fault,” “was displeased,” and “disapproved” are appropriate.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:59:

هَلْ تَنْقِمُونَ مِنَّا

hal tanqimūna minnā

“Do you find fault with us?”

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:126:

وَمَا تَنْقِمُ مِنَّا

wa-mā tanqimu minnā

“You find fault with us only because…”

In Al-Tawbah 9:74, “disapproved” or “resented it” may better convey the connection with receiving bounty.

In Al-Burūj 85:8, “found fault” fits the construction.

For divine uses of intaqama, “punished,” “exacted retribution,” and, in some contexts, “took vengeance” are suitable.

The Persian expression for “took revenge” is familiar, but may evoke personal revenge arising from resentment for some readers. “Punished” is therefore a natural rendering in many verses that retains the punitive response.

Using “punished” should nevertheless not erase the distinction between intiqām, ʿuqūbah, and other terms for punishment. The root explanation should clarify it.

For dhū intiqām, “Possessor of retribution” is close to the wording; “having the power to punish” can also be suitable in explanation.

For muntaqimūn in divine speech, “We will punish” or “We are those who punish” is suitable according to the construction.

The description itself should again be distinguished from the timing of its realization. An active participle alone does not establish exactly when punishment occurs.

Brief root meaning and meanings in Qur’anic usage

Brief root meaning: displeasure, finding fault, and disapproval; in the intiqām branch, responding with punishment to conduct considered wrong or objectionable.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:59, Al-Aʿrāf 7:126, and Al-Burūj 85:8: displeasure and faultfinding concerning others’ faith, presented as unjust in context.

In Al-Tawbah 9:74: displeasure and objection in a setting of receiving bounty and blessings.

In the intaqamnā verses: punishment in response to injustice, crime, denial, or defiance, with the method and outcome understood from each verse’s context.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:95: divine punishment for returning to a violation after the ruling is stated and past conduct pardoned.

Dhū intiqām and muntaqimūn: descriptions of divine power and punitive action, rather than proof of one particular method of punishment or a psychological state resembling human vindictiveness.

8. Conclusion

N Q M in Arabic concerns displeasure, disapproval, and faultfinding. In another branch, the response extends to retaliation and punishment.

The branches have a clear semantic connection: someone disapproves of conduct and may act in response.

Not every displeasure leads to retaliation, however, nor is every retaliation morally right.

The four Qur’anic occurrences of the basic verb make this clear: people resent others’ faith or the blessings they have received. Faultfinding can therefore target right conduct, and N Q M itself guarantees nothing about the criticism’s validity.

The Form VIII uses, by contrast, concern divine punishment. The Qur’an presents it following injustice, crime, denial of the signs, and defiance. Certain verses, as in Pharaoh’s story, identify its method; others state only the warning or punitive response.

Intiqām should therefore not be restricted everywhere to one kind of punishment, death, drowning, or destruction.

The difference between divine intiqām and unjust human vindictiveness must also be preserved. The Qur’an describes The God as Mighty and Possessor of retribution while also discussing justice, pardon, mercy, and human responsibility. These attributes and principles require consideration together, rather than one removing another’s meaning.

Morphologically, the definite singular al-muntaqim does not occur in the Qur’an. Its actual forms are dhū intiqām, dhī intiqām, muntaqimūn, and the associated verbs. The distinction matters both in studying divine names and attributes and in translation.

Final definition: N Q M in its principal branch denotes displeasure with something, disapproval, and faultfinding. The intiqām branch concerns punitive action in response to conduct considered wrong or objectionable. The root itself determines neither the objection’s validity nor the punishment’s legitimacy or severity. In the Qur’an, the basic verb chiefly describes unjust human faultfinding, whereas Form VIII derivatives concern divine punishment for particular actions. Displeasure and faultfinding in the basic verb, and punishment in response to conduct in intiqām, are thus the two principal, distinct axes of this Qur’anic family.
