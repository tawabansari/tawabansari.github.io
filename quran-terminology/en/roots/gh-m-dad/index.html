Sinking from view, concealment, closing the eyes, overlooking defects, and making concessions in transactions

The Arabic root غ م ض concerns recession, concealment, and lack of visibility. Geographical غَمْض denotes a depression in which something may remain unseen. غَامِض describes something not readily seen or understood. أَغْمَضَ means closing the eyes and, in another use, overlooking a defect.

غُمُوض denotes obscurity or difficulty of understanding; تَغْمِيض closing the eyes; and إِغْمَاض closing the eyes or overlooking something recognized.

Closing the eyes and overlooking a defect are intelligibly connected. Closed eyes no longer see what is before them; figuratively, someone may see a flaw yet decide to overlook it.

These are distinct situations. In the first the eyes actually close; in the second a person may knowingly behave as though the defect had not been seen.

The root occurs once in the Quran, as تُغْمِضُوا in Baqarah 2:267.

The verse concerns giving and the quality of what is given. Believers are told to give from their good earnings and not deliberately select inferior goods that they themselves would accept only by overlooking their shortcomings.

The contextual meaning is therefore overlooking a defect when accepting something, not physically closing the eyes or being unaware of its quality.

1. Meaning in early Arabic

غَمْض: a depression in the ground

One early use concerns land.

غَمْض and مَغْمَض are recorded for low, recessed ground.

In uneven terrain, a hollow between elevations may be hard to see from afar.

Someone in it may be hidden from a person surveying the landscape.

Nothing has ceased to exist; its position conceals it.

In some lexical explanations, the emphasis is not merely low elevation but a depression concealing its contents.

مَغَامِض also denotes hollows or recessed terrain.

This matters because غمض is not confined to the human eye: it also describes the obscured object itself.

غَامِض: something not apparent

غَامِض can describe something hidden from sight or understanding.

A house in a side street away from the main road may be invisible from the usual route.

Early Arabic could call it a دار غامضة.

A subject may likewise be so complex that a listener does not immediately understand it.

It is then غامض: its meaning is not apparent.

The two uses are close.

The house's position is hidden from sight.

The statement's meaning eludes immediate comprehension.

This does not make every obscure subject false or meaningless.

A true statement may require explanation because of complexity or the listener's unfamiliarity.

Conversely, difficult language is not always profound; it may simply be disorganized or unclear.

أَغْمَضَ عَيْنَهُ: he closed his eye

Closing the eyes is another familiar use.

أَغْمَضَ عَيْنَهُ means that someone closed his eye.

غَمَّضَ عَيْنَهُ is also recorded for this act.

The eyelids cover the eye, stopping or limiting direct vision.

Someone might close their eyes to rest, sleep, or avoid bright light.

Arabic calls this إغماض or تغميض.

اغتمضت عيناه can likewise refer to the eyes closing or a person sleeping.

But closing the eyes and sleeping are distinct.

A person may close their eyes while awake; sleep, conversely, is usually accompanied by closed eyes.

Early Arabic also records غُمْض, غِمَاض, and related forms for sleep.

These experiences are close, but context determines each precise sense.

أَغْمَضَ عَنْهُ: he overlooked it

In a broader use, أَغْمَضَ عَنْهُ means overlooking something.

Someone may know of another's minor mistake and decide to let it pass in view of their circumstances.

They recognize the error but do not insist on it.

Arabic can describe this as أغمض عنه.

The image relates to closing the eyes, while the meaning differs.

Physical closure restricts direct sight.

Overlooking may involve full knowledge followed by a decision not to be exacting.

This distinction is important for Quranic تغمضوا.

2. Early linguistic witnesses

A verse attributed to al-Mutanakhkhil al-Hudhali: concessions in valuation

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Mutanakhkhil al-Hudhali reads:

يَسُومُونَهُ أَنْ يُغْمِضَ النَّقْدَ عِنْدَهَا

yasūmūnahu an yughmiḍa al-naqda ʿindahā

وَقَدْ حَاوَلُوا شِكْسًا عَلَيْهَا يُمَارِسُ

wa-qad ḥāwalū shiksan ʿalayhā yumārisu

An approximate translation:

“They ask him to make a concession over payment or appraisal, though they have encountered a difficult man who contests it.”

يَسُومُونَهُ concerns asking or pressing someone to accept something.

يُغْمِضَ here means yielding, relaxing strictness, or accepting a concession.

النقد concerns payment or appraisal in this use.

شِكْسًا describes someone difficult and unyielding.

Ighmad here does not mean sleeping or physically closing the eyes; it concerns a concession in exchange or valuation.

Because the verse is cited individually in lexicons and its full setting is not clear in every transmission, details of the transaction or participants should not be invented.

Al-Mutanakhkhil is recognized as a pre-Islamic poet, and the lexical tradition attributes the verse to him. Its exact date has not been independently established.

It attests a financial-concession sense in linguistic material attributed to pre-Islamic times.

That use is directly relevant to تُغْمِضُوا فِيهِ.

Another witness: closing the eyes and sleep

A poem attributed to Ruʾba ibn al-ʿAjjaj reads:

أَرَّقَ عَيْنَيْكَ عَنِ الْغِمَاضِ

arraqa ʿaynayka ʿani al-ghimāḍi

بَرْقٌ سَرَى فِي عَارِضٍ نَهَّاضِ

barqun sarā fī ʿāriḍin nahhāḍi

Translation:

“Lightning moving through a towering cloud kept your eyes from sleep.”

الغِمَاض concerns sleep and closed eyes.

The lightning prevents repose and sleep.

The connection between eye closure and sleep is clear.

Ruʾba belongs to the Islamic period, after the Quran's revelation.

His verse supports an attested early Arabic use, but is not independent pre-Quranic evidence.

Lexical examples concerning land and speech

Sources attest غَمْض in describing terrain and غَامِض in describing speech.

مَكَانٌ غَمْضٌ is low or recessed ground.

كَلَامٌ غَامِضٌ is speech whose meaning is not readily apparent.

Concealment in this family therefore does not arise only from closing the eyes.

Sometimes an object's position conceals it.

Sometimes complexity or expression obstructs understanding.

Ibn Faris connects these uses through sinking down and inward recession.

This can illuminate the relation between hollows, obscurity, and eye closure without proving their historical order of development.

3. Important forms and derivatives

Established forms include:

  • غَمَضَ / يَغْمُضُ: became concealed, escaped sight, or became difficult to understand.
  • غَمُضَ / يَغْمُضُ: became obscure or difficult to understand.
  • غَمْض: a depression in the ground; related vowel patterns denote eye closure or sleep.
  • غُمُوض: concealment or obscurity.
  • غَامِض: hidden, unknown, or difficult to understand.
  • غَامِضَة: feminine of غامض, for example a house hidden from view.
  • أَغْمَضَ / يُغْمِضُ: closed / closes the eyes; alternatively, overlooked or made a concession.
  • إِغْمَاض: closing the eyes or overlooking.
  • غَمَّضَ / يُغَمِّضُ: closed the eyes; alternatively, made something obscure.
  • تَغْمِيض: closing the eyes; in suitable contexts, overlooking.
  • اِغْتَمَضَ: closed the eyes or slept.
  • اِغْتِمَاض: closing the eyes; in another use, something happening effortlessly.
  • غِمَاض: sleep or eye closure.
  • غَمْضَة: a single closing of the eyes.
  • مَغْمَض: a hollow; in other uses, a point of obscurity or difficulty.
  • مَغَامِض: recessed places; more broadly, difficult or intricate points.
  • أَغْمَاض and غُمُوض: plurals for recessed terrain in early geographical usage.
  • مُغْمِض: one who closes the eyes or overlooks.
  • مُغَمَّض: made obscure; of an eye, closed.
  • مُغْمِضَات: in some lexical uses, hidden matters or acts performed while overlooking their true nature; مُغَمِّضَات is also transmitted with different explanations.

Only تُغْمِضُوا occurs in the Quran.

غامض, غموض, تغميض, اغتماض, and other derivatives are established Arabic words but do not independently occur in its text.

Distinguishing غَمَضَ, غَمُضَ, أَغْمَضَ, and غَمَّضَ

Vowels and verbal patterns matter in this family.

غَمُضَ الشيءُ can mean that something became obscure or difficult to understand.

أَغْمَضَ عَيْنَهُ means that a person closed his eye.

غَمَّضَ عَيْنَهُ also describes closing the eye.

أَغْمَضَ فِي الشَّيْءِ can mean overlooking a fault, yielding, or accepting something with a concession.

These patterns should not all receive one meaning without regard to their objects.

Quranic تغمضوا belongs to Form IV أَغْمَضَ and is accompanied by فِيهِ.

That construction, together with inferior goods, clarifies the sense of overlooking a shortcoming.

4. تُغْمِضُوا in the Quran

Baqarah 2:267

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا أَنْفِقُوا مِنْ طَيِّبَاتِ مَا كَسَبْتُمْ وَمِمَّا أَخْرَجْنَا لَكُمْ مِنَ الْأَرْضِ ۖ وَلَا تَيَمَّمُوا الْخَبِيثَ مِنْهُ تُنْفِقُونَ وَلَسْتُمْ بِآخِذِيهِ إِلَّا أَنْ تُغْمِضُوا فِيهِ ۚ وَاعْلَمُوا أَنَّ اللَّهَ غَنِيٌّ حَمِيدٌ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū anfiqū min ṭayyibāti mā kasabtum wa-mimmā akhrajnā lakum mina al-arḍi wa-lā tayammamū al-khabītha minhu tunfiqūna wa-lastum bi-ākhidhīhi illā an tughmiḍū fīhi wa-iʿlamū anna Allāha ghaniyyun ḥamīd

Al-Baqarah 2:267

“You who have believed, give from the good things you have earned and from what We have brought forth for you from the earth. Do not deliberately select the inferior part to give away, when you yourselves would accept it only by overlooking its defects. Know that The God is self-sufficient, praiseworthy.”

تُغْمِضُوا is a Form IV imperfect verb.

The second-person masculine plural normally has تُغْمِضُونَ; after أَنْ it is subjunctive and loses its final nūn.

أَنْ تُغْمِضُوا thus means “that you overlook” or “by overlooking.”

فِيهِ refers to what is being considered for acceptance.

The object is inferior property or produce that someone would not ordinarily accept as it stands.

وَلَسْتُمْ بِآخِذِيهِ: you yourselves would not take it

The participle in بِآخِذِيهِ comes from أَخَذَ, taking or accepting. Its nūn is omitted in the possessive construction with the pronoun; the verse does not contain a separate finite verb أخذ.

The Quran confronts the audience with their own standards.

They select inferior goods to give away while being reluctant to accept the same goods themselves.

Someone with plentiful good fruit might separate spoiled or unsuitable fruit and send only that to people in need.

If offered it by a seller, they might reject it or accept only with a lower price and allowance for defects.

They apply one standard to themselves and a lower one to the recipient.

The Quran draws attention to this difference.

إِلَّا أَنْ تُغْمِضُوا فِيهِ: accepting with allowance

إِلَّا marks an exception.

They would not accept the item except by making allowance for it.

This can involve overlooking defects or lowering expectations of quality and value.

Someone who pays full price expects the agreed quality.

On discovering a defect at delivery, they may return the item.

Alternatively, they may accept it if part of the price is deducted.

They then make a concession regarding expected quality.

Early Arabic can call such a concession إغماض.

It need not mean that the defect goes unseen.

On the contrary, someone may recognize it clearly yet accept the item.

The distinction is important: overlooking here may be knowing, rather than the result of ignorance.

Does ighmad necessarily mean obtaining a discount?

Lexicons use أَغْمَضَ فِي السِّلْعَةِ for a concession in trade or accepting goods at a reduced price.

That explanation suits the verse's image.

But the verse does not describe a particular sale.

Its main subject is giving and the quality of the gift.

A discount is one clear transactional example, not a mandatory meaning of every تغمضوا.

Someone may accept inferior goods without paying, but only reluctantly and with allowance.

Both cases involve quality below what they would normally choose for themselves.

وَلَا تَيَمَّمُوا الْخَبِيثَ: deliberately selecting the inferior

The same verse uses تَيَمَّمُوا.

It belongs to ي م م, concerning intention and directing oneself toward something.

The verse does not merely acknowledge inferior goods; it prohibits deliberately choosing them for giving.

Someone might have two kinds of dates.

Some are sound and good.

Others are so inferior or damaged that the owner would not wish to eat or buy them.

Knowingly reserving the latter for those in need while keeping the good ones illustrates unequal standards.

This differs from giving something still sound and usable.

Someone may give clean, serviceable clothing they no longer need.

It is not new, but remains useful and appropriate.

The verse does not classify everything second-hand or inexpensive as unsuitable for giving.

Quality, fitness for use, and fair treatment matter, not price or newness alone.

خَبِيث and طَيِّب: distinct concepts

طَيِّبَات belongs to ط ي ب and الْخَبِيثَ to خ ب ث.

They contrast in the verse.

Tayyib concerns goodness, purity, pleasantness, and suitability.

Khabith here concerns inferior or unsuitable goods; it should not automatically be restricted to ritual impurity or unlawful origin.

Neither word belongs to غ م ض.

تغمضوا describes the person's response to something defective.

Three matters should therefore remain distinct:

The quality of what is given.

Its deliberate selection for giving.

The allowance the giver would require to accept it personally.

Different words express these matters; no one derivative contains them all.

5. Allowance, fairness, and the dignity of those in need

Why appeal to one's own willingness to accept?

Baqarah 2:267 asks the audience to assess giving through their own experience.

If you would not want something yourself, why consider it suitable for another without regard to quality?

This distinguishes benevolent giving from disposing of unsuitable possessions.

A shop, for example, may have food approaching the end of its usable life.

If it remains safe, has been properly stored, and recipients know its condition, using or giving it can be beneficial.

If it is spoiled and donated merely to avoid disposal costs, giving alone does not make the conduct good.

The shop has transferred part of its loss or responsibility to a person in need.

This is a modern illustration, not a direct Quranic ruling on every contemporary food-distribution method.

Does need reduce human dignity?

No.

Need does not make every unsuitable item appropriate for someone.

A person may struggle to obtain food, clothing, or shelter.

That does not erase their claim to sound, suitable, usable assistance.

Someone unable to afford shoes may benefit greatly from serviceable used shoes.

Torn, unusable shoes are not necessarily valuable help merely because they are free.

The giver's intention matters, but so do the quality and effect of what reaches the recipient.

This relates to the principle of giving from one's resources and using blessings: a blessing has a real effect when put to appropriate use in meeting needs and supporting human growth.

Is giving something inexpensive prohibited?

Low price should not be equated with unsuitability or spoilage.

A used book may cost little yet greatly help a student.

A simple, inexpensive object may meet a family's essential need.

An expensive object, conversely, may be unsuitable because it is broken or unsafe.

Price alone does not determine quality or usefulness.

Baqarah 2:267 concerns deliberately choosing khabith over tayyibat.

It does not imply that only wealthy people can give well or that small, simple gifts are worthless.

Is overlooking always praiseworthy?

No. Its value depends on what is overlooked.

Someone may let a friend's minor mistake pass to preserve a valuable relationship.

That can be commendable.

An official, however, may knowingly ignore corruption to protect personal interests.

Such overlooking can perpetuate injustice.

إغماض is therefore not itself a moral virtue.

Sometimes tolerance is kindness.

Sometimes it harms another person.

In a transaction it may simply mean agreeing to accept a defect or reduced value.

Baqarah 2:267 uses the required allowance to expose inferior quality, not to condemn overlooking in every circumstance.

6. Distinctions from related roots

غ م ض and غ ض ض: closing the eyes and lowering the gaze

غ ض ض appears in يَغُضُّوا مِنْ أَبْصَارِهِمْ in Nur 24:30.

It concerns reducing and restraining the gaze.

The eye-related sense of غمض concerns closing the eyes.

The actions may be close but are distinct.

Someone can lower their gaze without fully closing their eyes.

Another can close the eyes and cease looking.

Figurative أغمض عنه can mean overlooking a matter.

تغمضوا in Baqarah 2:267 should not be equated with يغضوا in Nur 24:30.

غ م ض and غ م ز: overlooking and eye signals

غ م ز occurs as يَتَغَامَزُونَ in Mutaffifin 83:30.

There it describes people signalling with their eyes and similar movements.

غ م ض concerns eye closure and overlooking.

A communicative wink differs from closing the eyes or disregarding a defect.

The gestures in Mutaffifin 83:30 occur in mockery and disparagement.

Baqarah 2:267 concerns allowance when accepting inferior goods.

Similar letters should not merge their meanings or morphology.

غ م ض and غ ف ل: overlooking and heedlessness

غفلة concerns inattention or something escaping notice.

Transactional إغماض can be knowingly overlooking a defect.

A buyer may fail to notice a flaw.

They are then unaware of it.

Another buyer may see it and accept a lower price.

The latter more clearly illustrates ighmad.

Heedlessness and overlooking are therefore not always identical.

Some Arabic uses of ighmad also mean pretending not to see, connecting it with apparent heedlessness.

غ م ض and ع ف و: overlooking and pardon

عفو concerns pardoning or letting something pass.

إغماض too can mean overlooking an error or defect.

The concepts approach one another in some situations.

Someone may recognize another's mistake and decide to forgive it.

But transactional ighmad can simply accept defective goods at a discount, without sin or moral forgiveness being involved.

Quranic ʿafw can concern pardoning human wrongdoing and responsibility.

تغمضوا in Baqarah 2:267 should therefore not mean forgiving sins.

غ م ض and س ت ر: concealment and covering

ستر concerns covering something.

غمض can concern what remains hidden from sight or understanding.

In eye closure, however, the observer restricts their own vision.

Cloth may cover an object.

Alternatively, the object remains exposed while someone closes their eyes.

Both images can describe concealed truth figuratively, but their roots are distinct.

غ م ض and خ ف ي: obscurity and hiddenness

خفاء concerns being hidden or unapparent.

غامض can describe something unknown or difficult to understand.

A fact may be hidden because information is unavailable.

Alternatively, information exists but its complex relationships resist comprehension.

Something is unclear in both cases, for different reasons.

غامض applied to speech can imply complexity; خفي need not.

غ م ض and ب خ س: allowance for defects and diminishing rights

بخس in the Quran concerns diminishing someone's due or something's value.

Transactional إغماض may involve accepting a defect with a lower price.

These are not identical.

A knowingly agreed lower price for genuinely defective goods is not necessarily unjust.

Deliberately understating another's property's value to deprive them raises a different issue of unfair diminution.

Not every discount is bakhs, nor every concession an unjust reduction of another's wealth.

غ م ض and خ ب ث: the recipient's action and the goods' quality

الخبيث in Baqarah 2:267 names what is inferior or unsuitable.

تغمضوا describes someone accepting it with allowance.

These are distinct concepts.

A person may reject defective goods.

Another may knowingly accept those same goods at a discount.

The goods' quality is unchanged; the recipient's response differs.

This distinction is essential to precise understanding.

7. Questions of interpretation and translation

Does تغمضوا mean physically closing your eyes?

No.

Baqarah 2:267 concerns accepting inferior property.

Eye closure is an image for overlooking a flaw or making a concession.

The verse does not say that people physically closed their eyes when transacting or receiving goods.

Does تغمضوا mean being deceived?

Not necessarily.

Deception may involve ignorance of a flaw.

Ighmad may be deliberate.

Someone can know the defect and nevertheless relax their expectations.

The verse uses that allowance to assess what is selected for giving.

Does ighmad always involve a discount?

No.

A discount is one clear transactional example.

Someone can overlook a defect without any sale.

The wider meaning is making a concession or overlooking a shortcoming.

Does the verse prohibit all second-hand gifts?

No.

It prohibits deliberately selecting inferior, unsuitable goods.

A second-hand object may be clean, sound, and fully usable.

A new one may be defective or dangerous.

Newness alone does not establish goodness.

Must someone give only their most expensive possessions?

تغمضوا establishes no such rule.

The verse concerns the goodness and suitability of what is given.

A simple, inexpensive, sound object may greatly help someone in need.

An expensive broken one may be useless.

Price is one property and cannot replace quality or usefulness.

Is overlooking defects always commendable?

No.

Letting a small fault pass can show kindness and patience.

Ignoring a defect that endangers others' lives or rights can be wrong.

Its value depends on subject, intention, and consequence.

غ م ض gives no fixed moral judgment on every act of overlooking.

Is obscure speech necessarily profound and true?

No.

غامض applied to speech describes obscure or difficult meaning.

A genuinely complex subject may require research.

But speech may simply be vague and disorganized.

Difficulty alone proves neither truth nor intellectual superiority.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

In Baqarah 2:267, “overlook” or “make allowance” naturally conveys تُغْمِضُوا.

The phrase:

وَلَسْتُمْ بِآخِذِيهِ إِلَّا أَنْ تُغْمِضُوا فِيهِ

wa-lastum bi-ākhidhīhi illā an tughmiḍū fīh

Al-Baqarah 2:267

“When you yourselves would accept it only by making allowance.”

This preserves the concession without importing details such as obtaining a discount or physically closing the eyes.

A lexical note can read:

تُغْمِضُوا

Brief root meaning: “Recession and concealment; closing the eyes.”

Contextual meaning: “Overlook the defects and inferior quality of something.”

Other Arabic uses:

غَمَضَ: became concealed or difficult to understand.

غَمُضَ: became obscure or difficult to understand.

غَمْض: a hollow; in some uses, eye closure.

غُمُوض: hiddenness or obscurity.

غَامِض: hidden, unknown, or difficult to understand.

أَغْمَضَ عَيْنَهُ: closed his eye.

أَغْمَضَ عَنْهُ: overlooked it.

أَغْمَضَ فِي الْبَيْعِ: made a concession in a transaction or accepted defects by agreement.

إِغْمَاض: eye closure or overlooking.

غَمَّضَ: closed the eyes; alternatively, made something obscure.

تَغْمِيض: closing the eyes.

اِغْتَمَضَ: closed the eyes or slept.

غِمَاض: sleep or eye closure.

مَغْمَض: a hollow or hidden place.

مَغَامِض: recessed places or difficult points.

مُغْمِض: one who closes the eyes or overlooks.

Equivalents should distinguish literal eye closure, concealment, difficult language, and knowingly overlooking a flaw.

One lexical family does not require one equivalent in every sentence.

Conclusion

غ م ض concerns recession, concealment, and lack of visibility.

Geographical غمض describes a hollow whose contents may remain unseen.

Cognitive غامض describes what is not easily known or understood.

أغمض and غمّض describe closing the eyes; related forms also denote sleep.

Broader ighmad concerns overlooking defects, relaxing strictness, and accepting something despite shortcomings.

Early evidence, including the verse attributed to al-Mutanakhkhil, illustrates valuation and concessions. Islamic-period poetry clearly attests eye closure and sleep.

These witnesses alone do not establish the historical sequence of all branches.

The Quran uses only تُغْمِضُوا, in Baqarah 2:267.

It appears in giving from good property and produce.

Believers should not deliberately select inferior goods they themselves would accept only by overlooking their shortcomings.

Ighmad here is therefore not being deceived or unable to see a flaw; it can be knowing acceptance with allowance.

The semantic core of غ م ض concerns recession and concealment from sight or understanding; its eye-related use is closure, and its figurative use overlooking what can be seen or known.

Its specific Quranic sense is overlooking the defects of something one would not ordinarily accept.

Baqarah 2:267 thus supplies a tangible criterion of fairness in giving: another's need should not justify deliberately offering inferior goods one would reject oneself.
