Drawing out, separation from an origin, an extract, and slipping away secretly

S L L in Arabic concerns drawing something out from within or among other things and separating it from its former location. Examples include drawing a sword from its sheath, extracting a strand, and someone leaving a group.

The Quran uses this root three times: twice in the noun sulālah and once in the verb yatasallalūna.

Sulālah denotes something drawn or taken from another material or source. Yatasallalūna describes people slipping out of a gathering without attracting attention.

Their shared field is coming or being drawn out from within, among, or out of a collection in which something previously lay. Separating material, a person's departure, and the emergence of offspring differ in manner and result. Each derivative requires its own construction and context.

1. Early Arabic usage and evidence attributed to the pre-Islamic period

Drawing out and separating

Early Arabic uses salla for extracting something from its location or the collection containing it.

A familiar example is salla al-sayfa min ghimdihi: drawing a sword from its sheath.

The sword is initially in its sheath and afterward outside it. The verb emphasizes drawing it out, rather than making it, its sharpness, or the purpose of drawing it.

A sword might be drawn for defense, war, or display. None of these purposes belongs to the verb salla itself.

A pre-Islamic poetic witness

The Muʿallaqah attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays says:

وَإِنْ تَكُ قَدْ سَاءَتْكِ مِنِّي خَلِيقَةٌ
فَسُلِّي ثِيَابِي مِنْ ثِيَابِكِ تَنْسُلِ

wa-in taku qad sāʾatki minnī khalīqatun / fa-sullī thiyābī min thiyābiki tansuli

“If some trait of mine has displeased you, draw my garments out from yours, and they will come apart.”

The separation of garments images the ending of the bond between the poet and his beloved.

Fa-sullī is an imperative from S L L, expressing drawing out or separating.

Tansuli at the line's end belongs to another root, N S L. The words are close in meaning within this image, but their sound and semantic proximity do not make them one root.

Early commentaries differ over whether garments stand for the bond between two hearts or their separation images two people's parting. Across these readings, fa-sullī retains drawing out and separation.

The line is an important witness in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period and preserved in the established Muʿallaqāt tradition. As with other transmitted poetry, the date of this individual line cannot be independently established.

It supports early extraction and separation senses, without proving that every later derivative arose from this poetic image.

Drawing out gently or gradually

Arabic lexical accounts include pulling a hair out of dough as an example of sall.

Something is separated from another substance without necessarily disrupting the entire mass.

Insalla describes emerging from a confined place or crowd; tasallala describes slipping away secretly or gradually from a gathering.

These uses share separation from a former location, but an important distinction remains.

Gentle extraction is well attested, yet salla need not always mean the utmost gentleness or complete absence of force. The object, agent, and context also determine the action's manner.

Secrecy is prominent in tasallul but should not be transferred to sulālah. Something can be extracted without the process being secret.

Other lexical derivatives

Salīl is also used for a child or offspring, understood as emerging from an earlier lineage or source.

Salīlah can denote a daughter or offspring. In another use, it denotes a portion of wool or hair drawn from a larger amount and prepared for spinning.

Sulālah denotes something taken from a material or collection. Some uses concern an extract or selected portion.

Islāl is recorded for secret theft. Removing something from its location or another's possession without revealing the act is central here.

Al-sull was also a name for a wasting illness. An imagery of bodily depletion can explain the association, but does not establish illness or wasting as the source of all the root's senses.

These forms belong to the Arabic lexical family. Their recording in later dictionaries does not by itself establish that all existed with identical forms and meanings before Islam.

The two clearly relevant branches for Quranic understanding are drawing or taking from a source and leaving a group.

2. Quranic forms and the semantic field

The three Quranic occurrences have two principal forms:

Sulālah: a noun on the pattern fuʿālah, denoting something drawn or taken from another material or source. It occurs in Al-Muʾminūn 23:12 and Al-Sajdah 32:8.

Yatasallalūna: a third-person masculine plural imperfect verb in Form V. In Al-Nūr 24:63 it describes slipping out of a gathering.

Sulālah focuses on what has been drawn from an origin; yatasallalūna emphasizes people's act of withdrawing from a group.

The basic verb salla, the noun salīl, the verbal noun istilāl, and insalla and salīlah are known in Arabic but do not occur in these forms in the Quran.

Sulālah belongs to S L L, whereas nasl belongs to N S L. They overlap semantically in offspring and reproduction but differ morphologically and derivationally.

Three things should be distinguished: the act of drawing out, what is drawn out, and the result produced from it.

Salla can express extraction, sulālah the extracted thing, and tasallala a person's slipping away.

These uses are related without every detail of one belonging to the others.

3. Sulālah: something taken from a source

The lexical meaning

Sulālah denotes something extracted and separated from a material or collection.

Some lexical explanations use khulāṣah: an extract or product taken from something.

That explanation needs care.

An extracted portion may be purer, more useful, selected, or simply separated. These properties are not identical.

Sulālah therefore cannot always be translated “the best and purest part.”

The central sense is being taken or separated from an origin. Purity, quality, or superiority, where present, require additional evidence and context.

This matters particularly in the Quran's two noun occurrences: one names clay as the source, the other a lowly fluid.

Human creation from clay

Al-Muʾminūn 23:12 says:

وَلَقَدْ خَلَقْنَا الْإِنْسَانَ مِنْ سُلَالَةٍ مِنْ طِينٍ

wa-la-qad khalaqnā al-insāna min sulālatin min ṭīn

Al-Muʾminūn 23:12

“We certainly created the human being from an extract of clay.”

Sulālatin is an indefinite noun in the genitive after min.

Min ṭīnin identifies the material or origin from which it is taken.

The verse does more than say humanity was created from clay: it speaks of a sulālah from clay.

This linguistic distinction should be retained.

Ṭīn names the material; sulālah what is taken from it. They are not synonyms, and substitution would lose part of the expression.

“An extract of clay” or “a portion drawn from clay” can convey the phrase. “Extract” does not require a specific process of pressing, chemical purification, or laboratory extraction.

Is sulālah simply pure clay?

Some lexical and exegetical explanations understand it here as a clear, pure, or selected portion of clay.

That is possible within the recorded semantic range, but the direct meaning should be distinguished from specifying the material's quality.

Sulālah indicates being taken from a source. The phrase does not independently specify how the portion is different or selected.

Saying someone was created from material drawn from the earth does not by itself identify how it was selected, its composition, or the natural and biological processes involved.

The phrase should not alone become a definitive theory of chemical elements, a particular soil, or the precise stages of life's formation.

Such questions go beyond the root's lexical meaning and require independent evidence.

The following verse

Al-Muʾminūn 23:13 says:

ثُمَّ جَعَلْنَاهُ نُطْفَةً فِي قَرَارٍ مَكِينٍ

thumma jaʿalnāhu nuṭfatan fī qarārin makīn

Al-Muʾminūn 23:13

“Then We made him a drop in a secure resting place.”

Verse 12 concerns creation from an extract of clay; verse 13 describes placement as a nuṭfah in a secure resting place.

These connect two levels of creation language: humanity's material origin and the nuṭfah stage in its coming into being.

Some readings relate the first verse to humanity's initial creation and the next to continuing offspring. Another takes al-insān as humanity collectively and connects earthly origin with later reproduction in a continuous account.

Judging these readings requires the other creation passages, especially Al-Sajdah 32:7 and Al-Sajdah 32:8, which distinguish the beginning of human creation from the production of offspring.

For the root study, sulālah in Al-Muʾminūn 23:12 concerns material drawn from clay. The noun itself means neither Adam nor nuṭfah and does not give a complete account of embryonic stages.

4. An extract of lowly fluid: material and continuing offspring

Al-Sajdah 32:8 says:

ثُمَّ جَعَلَ نَسْلَهُ مِنْ سُلَالَةٍ مِنْ مَاءٍ مَهِينٍ

thumma jaʿala naslahu min sulālatin min māʾin mahīn

Al-Sajdah 32:8

“Then He made his offspring from an extract of lowly fluid.”

Al-Sajdah 32:7 speaks of beginning human creation from clay; verse 8 then speaks of offspring.

That distinction matters.

Naslahu, from N S L, means his offspring. Sulālah, from S L L, denotes something taken from another material.

The sentence relates offspring to the material involved in their coming into being; it does not use the two words identically.

How does sulālah relate to māʾ?

Sulālah accompanies min māʾin mahīnin.

Māʾ names the material from which the sulālah is taken; mahīn qualifies that fluid.

The construction concerns material drawn from the fluid involved in the origin of offspring.

In reproductive contexts, sulālah can refer to nuṭfah or reproductive material. That does not make it an exact synonym of nuṭfah in every use.

Nuṭfah in reproduction names a small amount or form of the originating fluid; sulālah emphasizes being drawn from a material.

The separate study of N Ṭ F, concerning a drop, a small amount of water, and nuṭfah in human creation, develops this distinction.

Study of N Ṭ F

Does sulālah mean sperm?

Modern biological methods can examine reproductive fluids and cells involved in fertilization precisely.

In Quranic Arabic, sulālah is not a technical name for a specific cell or biological structure identified by modern science.

Its root alone therefore cannot establish that the verse specifically identifies sperm, an egg, a fertilized cell, or a particular chemical component of reproductive fluid.

Those are different scientific referents. Relating the verse to any of them requires separate linguistic, contextual, and scientific evidence.

The direct meaning remains something taken or separated from a source.

This does not deny the passage's clear connection with reproduction: naslahu and māʾin mahīnin establish the subject. It prevents turning a general Arabic word into a precise biological term without independent support.

Does mahīn qualify the human being?

In min māʾin mahīnin, mahīnin qualifies māʾin.

The construction does not establish that humans or their offspring are inherently worthless or contemptible.

The verse describes the material involved in the origin of offspring and qualifies that material.

Mahīn is not part of S L L's meaning. The same sulālah accompanies clay in Al-Muʾminūn 23:12 without that adjective.

The material's lowliness thus comes from an independent description, not from sulālah itself.

Does sulālah always mean lineage?

In modern Persian, salālah often means a family, dynasty, or descendants of a lineage.

This relates to offspring and derivation from an origin, but should not automatically be read into every Quranic occurrence.

Al-Sajdah 32:8 uses naslahu for offspring and sulālah alongside it for the material taken from a source.

Translating both as “offspring” would erase the distinction.

Here “extract” or “portion drawn from” better renders sulālah, leaving “offspring” for naslahu.

5. Yatasallalūna: slipping out of a gathering

The root's third occurrence is Al-Nūr 24:63:

لَا تَجْعَلُوا دُعَاءَ الرَّسُولِ بَيْنَكُمْ كَدُعَاءِ بَعْضِكُمْ بَعْضًا ۚ قَدْ يَعْلَمُ اللَّهُ الَّذِينَ يَتَسَلَّلُونَ مِنْكُمْ لِوَاذًا ۚ فَلْيَحْذَرِ الَّذِينَ يُخَالِفُونَ عَنْ أَمْرِهِ أَنْ تُصِيبَهُمْ فِتْنَةٌ أَوْ يُصِيبَهُمْ عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ

lā tajʿalū duʿāʾa al-rasūli baynakum ka-duʿāʾi baʿḍikum baʿḍan qad yaʿlamu Allāhu alladhīna yatasallalūna minkum liwādhan fa-l-yaḥdhari alladhīna yukhālifūna ʿan amrihi an tuṣībahum fitnatun aw yuṣībahum ʿadhābun alīm

Al-Nūr 24:63

“Do not treat the messenger's call among you like your calls to one another. The God knows those among you who slip away, taking cover behind others. Let those who oppose his command beware lest a trial strike them or a painful punishment befall them.”

The verb's form

Yatasallalūna is an imperfect verb in Form V.

Its singular is yatasallalu: he slips out from among something, or withdraws gradually or secretly.

The subject performs the departure himself. Salla can describe someone extracting something else, whereas tasallala here describes the person's own withdrawal.

This follows from the verb's construction and use. Form V alone does not make every verb in that form imply secrecy or gradual exit.

The role of minkum

In yatasallalūna minkum, minkum identifies those concerned as withdrawing from among the addressed community.

They were present in the gathering or belonged to the addressed group, and are now withdrawing.

This connects clearly with sall: separation from a collection in which something was situated.

Here, however, the subjects are people acting deliberately, rather than material or objects.

The role of liwādhan

Liwādhan belongs to L W DH and concerns seeking shelter or concealment behind another.

Lexical explanations describe people hiding behind one another to leave the group without their departure becoming apparent.

It clarifies how they withdraw.

Tasallul can itself denote secret departure, but the entire image of hiding behind someone else is not contained in S L L. Liwādhan and the context supply those details.

Gradual or separate departures are intelligible here, but this does not establish that every tasallul occurs in a fixed number or a one-by-one sequence.

The preceding context

Al-Nūr 24:62 helps explain the condemned behavior.

It describes believers who, when with the messenger on a collective matter, do not leave without seeking his permission.

It also states that when they seek leave for some of their own affairs, the messenger may permit whom he wishes.

The issue is not an absolute ban on leaving gatherings or attending to personal affairs.

Al-Nūr 24:63 warns against secretly leaving and evading responsibility in a specified collective undertaking.

The distinction is essential.

Someone who openly requests and receives permission does not perform the conduct condemned in yatasallalūna minkum liwādhan.

Collective responsibility

Tasallul here relates to evading responsibility and concealing departure from the group.

Where cooperation and attendance are needed for a shared task, secretly leaving can affect others' work.

The instance should not become a universal claim that every covert departure is inherently sinful or every officeholder is entitled to unlimited obedience.

The verse's judgment relates to the messenger's presence, the shared undertaking, seeking permission, and opposing the command.

S L L itself does not mean hypocrisy, sin, or disobedience. Those meanings may relate to the conduct contextually without becoming the root's fixed definition.

Modern tasallul also denotes covert penetration or passage. That relates to its lexical field, but Al-Nūr 24:63 should not be restricted to the modern military concept of enemy infiltration.

6. Distinctions from related roots

S L L and N S L: sulālah and nasl

These roots resemble each other in form and some senses involving emergence and offspring, but are distinct morphological families.

S L L concerns drawing out and separation from a source.

Quranic N S L concerns offspring and continuing generations, and in another verbal use emerging or moving swiftly.

Al-Sajdah 32:8 directly illustrates the difference by placing naslahu and sulālah together.

Naslahu refers to offspring; sulālah to the material drawn from a source in their formation.

The separate study of N S L examines offspring, reproduction, and emergence.

Study of N S L

S L L and N Z ʿ: drawing out and pulling away

Both can express extraction and separation.

Quranic nazʿ describes drawing out or removing, sometimes forcefully, as when wind tears people away or a hand is drawn out.

Sall is close to drawing something from within or among other things, such as a sword from its sheath.

Nazʿ is not invariably violent nor sall invariably gentle. Context determines force and manner.

Sall can emphasize extraction from a containing location, whereas nazʿ has a wider field of pulling, separating, and wrenching away. This is a tendency rather than an absolute boundary.

S L L and KH L Ṣ: extraction and purity

Khulūṣ concerns separation from admixture, purity, or exclusive belonging.

Sulālah can denote an extract or selected portion without making every instance the purest and best part of its source.

Something extracted from a collection may remain mixed with other material.

Sulālah emphasizes derivation or extraction; khulūṣ emphasizes purity or freedom from admixture.

That distinction matters in translating the two human-creation verses.

S L L and S L KH: extraction and removing a covering

Physical salkh concerns separating skin from a body or emerging from a covering.

Sall concerns drawing something from its location or the collection containing it.

The images may overlap in some situations but are not identical.

Drawing a sword from its sheath is not itself skinning, just as secretly leaving a group need not involve removing a covering.

S L L and S L S L: distinct roots

S L S L is a quadriliteral root, known in the Quran through silsilah and salāsil.

Its resemblance to S L L does not make them one family.

Strands, connected links, movement, and flow may invite comparisons, but imagery alone does not establish historical kinship or direct derivation.

Sulālah concerns a portion drawn from an origin; silsilah in its familiar use denotes connected links forming a chain.

7. Translation suggestions for Forqan

Sulālah in Al-Muʾminūn 23:12

Arabic:

مِنْ سُلَالَةٍ مِنْ طِينٍ

min sulālatin min ṭīn

Al-Muʾminūn 23:12

Suggested translation:

“From an extract of clay.”

Or, emphasizing derivation from the source:

“From a portion drawn from clay.”

The Persian equivalent meaning “extract” is natural and can retain sulālah's meaning. Its lexical explanation should clarify that something is taken from another material.

“Extract” may need explanation if it suggests pressing liquid out of clay or a specific extraction process.

Sulālah in Al-Sajdah 32:8

Arabic:

مِنْ سُلَالَةٍ مِنْ مَاءٍ مَهِينٍ

min sulālatin min māʾin mahīn

Al-Sajdah 32:8

Suggested translation:

“From an extract of lowly fluid.”

This preserves the distinctions between sulālah, māʾ, and mahīn.

It should not automatically become “from sperm,” “from an egg,” or “from a fertilized cell.” These technical terms are not established substitutes for sulālah.

Nor should sulālah here become “offspring,” since the sentence already uses naslahu.

Yatasallalūna in Al-Nūr 24:63

Arabic:

يَتَسَلَّلُونَ مِنْكُمْ لِوَاذًا

yatasallalūna minkum liwādhā

Al-Nūr 24:63

Suggested translation:

“They slip away from among you, hiding behind others.”

Or, more briefly:

“They withdraw secretly from among you.”

The first preserves liwādhan's image more clearly. The second is more compact but conveys fewer details.

A colloquial Persian expression for sneaking away conveys part of the sense. For Forqan's formal prose, “leave secretly” or “withdraw secretly” is more suitable.

Retaining the Arabic tasallul without explanation may suggest covert entry to some Persian readers. Here the people leave the group, so unexplained borrowing could obscure the direction of movement.

Conclusion

S L L concerns extracting or separating something from the place or collection containing it. Early extraction uses, including the verse attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays, attest this field.

The Quran uses the root in two distinct ways.

Sulālah denotes something taken from a source or material: clay in Al-Muʾminūn 23:12, and fluid connected with reproduction in Al-Sajdah 32:8.

Yatasallalūna in Al-Nūr 24:63 describes people secretly leaving a group. Liwādhan clarifies their manner, while the shared undertaking, permission, and responsibility establish the conduct's significance.

Extraction and separation form the family's main field, but their manner and result differ with each derivative.

Sulālah does not inherently mean the best portion, absolutely pure material, a particular biological cell, or a superior lineage. Tasallul does not invariably mean evading responsibility or sinful action.

The root can be defined as follows:

S L L denotes drawing or coming out from within, among, or out of a collection in which something previously lay. Sulālah emphasizes what is drawn from a source; tasallala emphasizes gradual or secret withdrawal from a group.

This explains all three Quranic occurrences while keeping creation language distinct from modern scientific detail, and the moral evaluation of secret departure distinct from the root's lexical meaning.
