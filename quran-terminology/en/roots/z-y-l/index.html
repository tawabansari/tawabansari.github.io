Core meaning: separating things joined, adjacent, or intermingled, and their becoming separate. The established constructions mā zāla and lā yazālu express continuation or remaining in a condition. Describing this as “not ceasing” explains the construction’s meaning without proving a single historical derivation for every form.

1. The root and its meaning in early Arabic

The Arabic root Z Y L concerns separation, moving apart, and becoming distinct.

“زِلْتُ الشَّيْءَ مِنَ الشَّيْءِ” means separating one thing from another.

“زَيَّلْتُ بَيْنَهُمْ” means “I separated them.”

“تَزَيَّلَ الْقَوْمُ” describes members of a group separating and dispersing.

“زَايَلَ فُلَانٌ فُلَانًا” likewise describes someone parting from another person.

These usages concern more than motion: they describe a connection or proximity between things coming to an end.

For example, this family can describe someone separating sheep and goats that have been mingled in a flock.

Neither group is destroyed; their intermingling ends and they become distinct.

This is an important feature of the root.

Its separation sense does not necessarily imply destruction. Two things can part while both continue to exist.

One early lexical analysis connects Z Y L with Z W L and treats its yāʾ as replacing wāw. It explains tazāyul as becoming separate or distinct and zayyaltu baynahum as separating them. This is a lexical analysis, not a definitive reconstruction of every form’s history.

The relationship does not erase morphological distinctions: zāla–yazīlu for separating, zāla–yazūlu for departing, and zāla–yazālu in continuative constructions differ in form and usage.

A pre-Islamic poetic witness: separating even after mixing

The pre-Islamic poet al-Mutalammis al-Ḍubaʿī addresses al-Ḥārith:

أَحَارِثُ إِنَّا لَوْ تُسَاطُ دِمَاؤُنَا
تَزَايَلْنَ حَتَّى لَا يَمَسَّ دَمٌ دَمَا

a-Ḥārithu innā law tusāṭu dimāʾunā / tazāyalna ḥattā lā yamassa damun damā

Translation:

“Al-Ḥārith, even if our blood were mixed together, it would separate until no blood touched other blood.”

Tusāṭu refers to mixing.

Tazāyalna refers to separating and becoming distinct.

The poet exaggerates: even if the two groups’ blood were mixed, their estrangement is so great that their blood would seemingly separate again.

The image expresses distance and incompatibility between human groups.

Early transmissions vary between tusāṭu and tushāṭu in the first hemistich, and tazāyalna and tazayyalna in the second. The ending also appears with mā in place of lā.

These variants retain the core image: components imagined as mingled become separate.

This witness matters because it supplies evidence of separation and distinction in poetry attributed to a pre-Islamic poet, rather than deriving the sense solely from the Qur’an.

The exaggerated image expresses group antagonism and claims of superiority. It neither establishes that mingled blood can literally separate nor endorses the poet’s genealogical or tribal judgments.

The lexical point is the meaning of tazāyul.

A historical definition

Early Arabic evidence associates the separation branch with things parting or being separated and with intermingling or association ending.

Separation may be spatial, as when two people move apart.

It may distinguish groups, as when two sets are sorted out of a mixed gathering.

It may also concern affiliation, as when people disavow one another and reject their previous association.

The early witness supports this definition: zayl concerns separating and distinguishing things previously adjacent, intermingled, or connected; in intransitive usage, their parting or moving apart.

Continuative usage requires separate grammatical analysis: lā yazālu does not mean “becomes separate.”

2. Important forms and derivatives

زَالَ يَزِيلُ — zāla, yazīlu

In transitive usage, it means to separate, remove, or distinguish something from another thing.

زِلْ ضَأْنَكَ مِنْ مَعْزِكَ

zil ḍaʾnaka min maʿzika

Meaning: “Separate your sheep from your goats.”

The purpose is not to destroy or discard either group, but to distinguish them.

This differs from zāla–yazūlu, which describes departure or cessation. The transitive zāla–yazīlu in this example emphasizes separating something.

زَيْل، زِيَال — zayl, ziyāl

Zayl is a verbal noun denoting separation and sorting apart.

Ziyāl likewise denotes parting and separation.

In human relationships, these words may describe moving apart or ending companionship.

زَيَّلَ يُزَيِّلُ — zayyala, yuzayyilu

A Form II verb meaning to separate or bring about separation among people.

زَيَّلَ بَيْنَ الْقَوْمِ

zayyala bayna al-qawm

Meaning: “He separated the people.”

The subject causes the groups to separate.

This is the form used in Yūnus 10:28.

تَزْيِيل — tazyīl

The verbal noun of zayyala, describing separation or sorting apart.

With groups, it describes ending their intermingling and making them distinct.

تَزَيَّلَ يَتَزَيَّلُ — tazayyala, yatazayyalu

A Form V verb.

تَزَيَّلَ الْقَوْمُ

tazayyala al-qawm

Meaning: “The people separated from one another.”

Separation is attributed to the group itself.

Tazayyalū in Al-Fatḥ 48:25 has this structure.

تَزَايَلَ يَتَزَايَلُ — tazāyala, yatazāyalu

A Form VI verb describing two or more parties separating from one another.

تَزَايَلَ الرَّجُلَانِ

tazāyala al-rajulān

Meaning: “The two men parted.”

This form also appears in al-Mutalammis’s verse.

Some Arabic dialects use tazāyala and tazayyala for the same sense of separation.

زَايَلَ يُزَايِلُ — zāyala, yuzāyilu

A Form III verb describing parting from someone or something.

زَايَلَ فُلَانٌ صَاحِبَهُ

zāyala fulānun ṣāḥibahu

Meaning: “Someone parted from his companion.”

The ending of companionship is prominent here.

مُزَايَلَة — muzāyalah

The verbal noun of zāyala, meaning parting or separation.

In relationships it concerns leaving companionship or moving away from another.

اِنْزَالَ — inzāla

Some lexical usages record it as becoming separate or moving apart.

It differs from anzala, “sent down,” from N Z L.

Their similar unvocalized spelling does not establish a common meaning.

زَيَل، أَزْيَل — zayal, azyal

In bodily description, zayal denotes a space between the thighs.

رَجُلٌ أَزْيَلُ

rajulun azyal

Meaning: “A man whose thighs stand apart.”

The feminine form is zaylāʾ.

This concerns bodily separation, rather than doctrinal or social division.

مِزْيَل — mizyal

Applied to a person, it describes someone capable of distinguishing things and recognizing their differences.

Mikhlaṭ mizyal describes someone skilled in dealing with and distinguishing affairs. It brings together the abilities to engage and connect and to separate and discern; it does not itself make a claim about all kinds of things.

This usage relates to distinction and discrimination but does not occur in the Qur’an.

3. Mā zāla and lā yazālu: continuation through an established construction

An important feature of this lexical family is the incomplete predicative verb zāla–yazālu in continuative constructions.

Here zāla belongs to the grammatical class of kāna and aṣbaḥ: it takes a subject and a predicate, rather than expressing a complete event on its own.

For example:

مَا زَالَ يَعْمَلُ

mā zāla yaʿmalu

Meaning: “He continues to work.”

لَا يَزَالُ قَائِمًا

lā yazālu qāʾiman

Meaning: “He is still standing.”

مَا زِلْتُ أَنْتَظِرُ

mā ziltu antaẓiru

Meaning: “I am still waiting.”

In these constructions, the sentence expresses continuation, not separation.

How does this construction express continuation?

The general notion of cessation describes something leaving a position or condition, so that condition ends.

Negating such an ending provides a way to explain the resulting sense of continuation. It does not independently establish a historical derivation from zāla–yazūlu.

For example:

Mā zāla qāʾiman means that his standing has not ceased.

Lā yazālu mukhtalifan means that his disagreement continues.

This explanation must not collapse every morphological construction of Z W L and Z Y L into one.

Lexical accounts distinguish zāla–yazūlu, departing, from incomplete predicative zāla–yazālu.

They also record a separate construction and usage for transitive zāla–yazīlu, separating.

In the Qur’anic morphological classification adopted here, mā zāla, lā yazālu, and mā ziltum are grouped under Z Y L. Their classification is not uniform across every lexical account, and it should not be confused with their clear continuative meaning.

Their meaning also differs from zayyalnā and tazayyalū.

Both branches must therefore retain their place: separation in the lexical usages and continuation in the incomplete predicative construction with negation.

Does lā yazālu mean eternal continuation?

Lā yazālu expresses continuing in a condition, but does not alone prove that it will never end.

Saying that someone still does something means that the activity continues through the time under discussion.

It may later stop.

Several Qur’anic occurrences also accompany lā yazālu with ḥattā, “until.”

Ḥattā can identify a limit or stage up to which continuation lasts.

Lā yazālu should therefore not always be rendered as unchangeable eternity.

4. Separating worshippers and alleged partners in Yūnus 10:28

وَيَوْمَ نَحْشُرُهُمْ جَمِيعًا ثُمَّ نَقُولُ لِلَّذِينَ أَشْرَكُوا مَكَانَكُمْ أَنْتُمْ وَشُرَكَاؤُكُمْ فَزَيَّلْنَا بَيْنَهُمْ وَقَالَ شُرَكَاؤُهُمْ مَا كُنْتُمْ إِيَّانَا تَعْبُدُونَ

wa-yawma naḥshuruhum jamīʿan thumma naqūlu li-lladhīna ashrakū makānakum antum wa-shurakāʾukum fa-zayyalnā baynahum wa-qāla shurakāʾuhum mā kuntum iyyānā taʿbudūn

Yūnus 10:28

Translation:

“On the day We gather them all, then say to those who associated partners: Stay in your places, you and your partners. Then We separate them, and their partners say: You were not worshipping us.”

Fa-zayyalnā is a past Form II verb.

Its first-person plural subject refers to The God.

Baynahum identifies the separation as occurring between the parties.

They are first gathered, and those who associated partners are addressed alongside their alleged partners.

Separation then takes place.

The sequence matters:

First, gathering.

Then, bringing the parties face to face.

Then, separating those linked in worldly life through worship and following.

Does fa-zayyalnā mean destroying?

Zayyalnā directly means separating.

The verse does not say that either party is destroyed at that moment.

It speaks of separation between them.

The alleged partners then distance themselves from the worshippers and deny the relationship those worshippers had imagined.

In Yūnus 10:29, they also invoke The God as witness to their statement.

The separation thus relates to disclosure of the relationship’s truth as well as to spatial distance.

That contextual significance must not replace the verb’s lexical meaning.

Fa-zayyalnā directly means “We separated”; disavowal and denial of the relationship emerge from the subsequent statements.

Separation and the collapse of invented dependencies

In worldly life, people may assign to persons, powers, or other beings a standing belonging to The God.

They may imagine those beings independently controlling rescue, intercession, or their destiny.

Yūnus 10:28 challenges those alleged relationships on the day of reckoning.

Al-Baqarah 2:166 likewise describes leaders disavowing their followers and their ties being severed.

In Al-Baqarah 2:167, followers wish to return and disavow those leaders in turn.

These passages are related in their depiction of separation and the ending of wrongful dependencies.

In Yūnus 10:28, zayyalnā explicitly names the act of separation.

In context, this connects with disclosure of individual responsibility and invalidation of a relationship founded on associating partners.

That conclusion comes from context, not because zayl lexically means polytheism or disavowal.

5. Believers and opponents becoming separate in Al-Fatḥ 48:25

هُمُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا وَصَدُّوكُمْ عَنِ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ وَالْهَدْيَ مَعْكُوفًا أَنْ يَبْلُغَ مَحِلَّهُ وَلَوْلَا رِجَالٌ مُؤْمِنُونَ وَنِسَاءٌ مُؤْمِنَاتٌ لَمْ تَعْلَمُوهُمْ أَنْ تَطَئُوهُمْ فَتُصِيبَكُمْ مِنْهُمْ مَعَرَّةٌ بِغَيْرِ عِلْمٍ لِيُدْخِلَ اللَّهُ فِي رَحْمَتِهِ مَنْ يَشَاءُ لَوْ تَزَيَّلُوا لَعَذَّبْنَا الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا مِنْهُمْ عَذَابًا أَلِيمًا

humu alladhīna kafarū wa-ṣaddūkum ʿani al-masjidi al-ḥarāmi wa-l-hadya maʿkūfan an yablugha maḥillahu wa-lawlā rijālun muʾminūna wa-nisāʾun muʾminātun lam taʿlamūhum an taṭaʾūhum fa-tuṣībakum minhum maʿarratun bi-ghayri ʿilmin li-yudkhila Allāhu fī raḥmatihi man yashāʾu law tazayyalū la-ʿadhdhabnā alladhīna kafarū minhum ʿadhāban alīman

Al-Fatḥ 48:25

Translation:

“They are those who disbelieved and barred you from the Sacred Mosque, with the sacrificial animals detained from reaching their place. Had it not been for believing men and believing women whom you did not know, whom you might trample and thereby incur harm on their account unknowingly, [restraint would not have occurred in this way]; so that The God may admit whom He wills into His mercy. Had they been separate, We would have punished those among them who disbelieved with a painful punishment.” The bracketed clause supplies an explanatory completion of the unexpressed response to lawlā; it is not wording present in the Arabic.

Tazayyalū is a past Form V verb following law, “if.”

It means becoming separate and distinct from one another.

Separation is attributed to the parties themselves, unlike Yūnus 10:28, where The God is the agent separating them.

Who would have needed to become separate?

The verse concerns people who blocked believers’ access to the Sacred Mosque.

Among that population were believing men and women unknown to the Muslims approaching them.

The Qur’an raises the possibility of harming those believers.

Law tazayyalū therefore concerns those unknown believers becoming separate from the opposing group.

Had the two groups been distinct and separate, the confrontation would have differed.

The root has a concrete meaning here: ending the intermingling of human groups so that they can be distinguished.

Recognizing people and preventing harm

The verse distinguishes hostility toward a particular group from harming everyone living among them.

Those blocking access to the Sacred Mosque are not equated with the believing men and women present among them.

Because not everyone’s identity is known, action without recognition could harm people who should not be targeted.

Recognizing individuals and avoiding unwitting harm therefore matter in decisions concerning conflict.

Al-Baqarah 2:190 prohibits transgression in fighting.

Al-Māʾidah 5:2 warns that hostility toward people who blocked access to the Sacred Mosque must not lead to transgression.

Al-Fatḥ 48:25 presents that responsibility where believers and opponents inhabit the same place.

The condition law tazayyalū does not itself constitute a general command to harm another group after separating it.

Punishment is attributed to The God within a particular context involving restraint from harm and the divine purpose.

The conditional clause should not be detached from that setting and made into an independent authorization for violence.

The difference from Yūnus 10:28

In Yūnus 10:28, worshippers and alleged partners are separated on the day of reckoning.

Al-Fatḥ 48:25 concerns human groups not being separate in worldly life.

The first uses transitive fa-zayyalnā.

The second attributes tazayyalū to the groups themselves.

Despite their different settings, both concern ending intermingling and distinguishing the parties.

6. Eight Qur’anic occurrences expressing continuation

In eight other occurrences, the family appears through incomplete predicative zāla or yazālu with negation.

These describe continued conduct or a continuing condition, rather than separating groups.

Al-Baqarah 2:217: continued fighting to turn people from their religion

After discussing obstruction of The God’s way, disbelief, blocking the Sacred Mosque, and expelling its people, Al-Baqarah 2:217 describes opponents continuing to fight.

“وَلَا يَزَالُونَ يُقَاتِلُونَكُمْ” says that they continue fighting the people addressed.

The following words identify the aim as turning them from their religion, with “if they can” qualifying the opponents’ ability.

Lā yazālūna thus expresses continued hostile conduct here.

The incomplete predicative verb does not itself mean fighting or hostility. The conduct is specified by yuqātilūnakum.

The plural subject refers to opponents within this context and should not be extended without evidence to everyone differing from Muslims in belief.

Al-Māʾidah 5:13: continued discovery of betrayal

After discussing covenant-breaking by a group among the Children of Israel and its consequences, Al-Māʾidah 5:13 tells the Prophet that he continues to discover betrayal among them.

Wa-lā tazālu taṭṭaliʿu expresses this continuing discovery.

The verse nevertheless excludes a small number through “إِلَّا قَلِيلًا مِنْهُمْ.”

This exception matters: the community’s members are not all presented identically.

Lā tazālu expresses continuation; the group’s scope and the exception come from other words.

Al-Tawbah 9:110: continuing unease and doubt

Al-Tawbah 9:110 concerns a building erected to cause harm, strengthen disbelief, and divide believers, as described in Al-Tawbah 9:107.

The verse says their building remains a source of doubt in their hearts unless their hearts are torn apart.

Lā yazālu expresses continuation of that consequence.

It does not mean the physical building can never be demolished.

What continues is “رِيبَةً فِي قُلُوبِهِمْ,” doubt in their hearts.

Something may physically disappear while the consequences of its builders’ conduct and intentions remain within them.

Continuation need not describe an object’s bodily endurance.

Hūd 11:118: continued human disagreement

وَلَوْ شَاءَ رَبُّكَ لَجَعَلَ النَّاسَ أُمَّةً وَاحِدَةً وَلَا يَزَالُونَ مُخْتَلِفِينَ

wa-law shāʾa rabbuka la-jaʿala al-nāsa ummatan wāḥidatan wa-lā yazālūna mukhtalifīn

Hūd 11:118

Translation:

“Had your Lord willed, He would have made people one community; and they continue to differ.”

Lā yazālūna expresses continued disagreement.

Mukhtalifīn is its accusative predicate and specifies what continues; it is not another verb.

Lā yazālūna does not alone mean disagreeing.

Continued disagreement does not itself establish that all differences are alike or every disagreement is sinful.

Hūd 11:119 adds an exception for those toward whom the Lord has mercy.

The following verse must therefore inform the scope and evaluation of the disagreement.

The construction in Hūd 11:118 nevertheless clearly means that their differing continues.

Al-Raʿd 13:31: continuing consequences of conduct

Al-Raʿd 13:31 describes those who disbelieved being struck by a calamity because of their deeds. In one reading, the calamity also settles near their homes; another takes “you settle near their homes” as addressing the Prophet. Both readings leave the continuative construction intact, up to the coming of The God’s promise.

Wa-lā yazālu expresses continuation of the situation explained in its predicate clause.

“Until The God’s promise comes” introduces a stage or limit.

Lā yazālu does not independently establish that the situation is eternal.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:15: a confession continuing to the end

فَمَا زَالَتْ تِلْكَ دَعْوَاهُمْ حَتَّىٰ جَعَلْنَاهُمْ حَصِيدًا خَامِدِينَ

fa-mā zālat tilka daʿwāhum ḥattā jaʿalnāhum ḥaṣīdan khāmidīn

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:15

Translation:

“That remained their cry until We made them mown down and lifeless.”

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:14, people confronted with the consequences of their conduct acknowledge their wrongdoing.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:15 says their cry continued until their end came.

Fa-mā zālat expresses continued utterance.

It has a stated limit: “until We made them.”

This clearly shows continuation to a particular stage rather than necessarily without end.

Ḥaṣīdan khāmidīn comes from two other roots and describes their end. That meaning should not be inserted into the lexical definition of zālat.

Al-Ḥajj 22:55: continued doubt until the Hour

Al-Ḥajj 22:55 describes disbelievers remaining in doubt concerning it until the Hour comes suddenly or the punishment of a barren day arrives. Taking the pronoun to refer to the revealed message is an interpretive reading; its reference is a separate question from the meaning of lā yazālu.

Lā yazālu expresses continued doubt.

Fī miryatin identifies the state that continues.

Ḥattā marks its limit within the sentence.

Lā yazālu itself means neither doubt nor punishment: it describes the continuation of the state placed in relation to them.

Ghāfir 40:34: remaining in doubt

وَلَقَدْ جَاءَكُمْ يُوسُفُ مِنْ قَبْلُ بِالْبَيِّنَاتِ فَمَا زِلْتُمْ فِي شَكٍّ مِمَّا جَاءَكُمْ بِهِ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا هَلَكَ قُلْتُمْ لَنْ يَبْعَثَ اللَّهُ مِنْ بَعْدِهِ رَسُولًا كَذَٰلِكَ يُضِلُّ اللَّهُ مَنْ هُوَ مُسْرِفٌ مُرْتَابٌ

wa-laqad jāʾakum Yūsufu min qablu bi-l-bayyināti fa-mā ziltum fī shakkin mimmā jāʾakum bihi ḥattā idhā halaka qultum lan yabʿatha Allāhu min baʿdihi rasūlan kadhālika yuḍillu Allāhu man huwa musrifun murtāb

Ghāfir 40:34

Translation:

“Joseph had previously come to you with clear signs, yet you remained in doubt about what he brought. Then, when he died, you said: The God will never send a messenger after him. Thus The God leads astray whoever is excessive and persistently doubtful.”

Mā ziltum is a past incomplete predicative verb accompanied by negation.

It means “you continued” or “you remained.”

Fī shakkin identifies the continuing doubt about Joseph’s message.

The context places that doubt alongside clear signs and then reports their statement after Joseph’s death.

The verse thus describes persistence in a position toward the divine message.

Mā ziltum itself does not mean doubt or denial; other expressions specify these.

The continuing doubt is tied to these addressees’ response to the signs. Every honest human question or uncertainty should not be assigned to this same situation without examination.

7. Separation and continuation are distinct

The two Qur’anic branches appear different in meaning.

In Yūnus 10:28:

فَزَيَّلْنَا بَيْنَهُمْ

fa-zayyalnā baynahum

Yūnus 10:28

Meaning: “Then We separated them.”

In Al-Fatḥ 48:25:

لَوْ تَزَيَّلُوا

law tazayyalū

Al-Fatḥ 48:25

Meaning: “Had they become separate.”

But in Hūd 11:118:

وَلَا يَزَالُونَ مُخْتَلِفِينَ

wa-lā yazālūna mukhtalifīn

Hūd 11:118

Meaning: “They continue to differ.”

And in Ghāfir 40:34:

فَمَا زِلْتُمْ فِي شَكٍّ

fa-mā ziltum fī shakk

Ghāfir 40:34

Meaning: “You remained in doubt.”

Their distinction should not be erased by changing the words’ meanings.

The first branch directly describes separation or sorting apart.

The second uses an incomplete predicative verb with negation to express a continuing state.

This continuation can be explained as a condition not ending, but mā zāla and lā yazālu are established grammatical constructions whose actual usage determines their meaning.

Rendering lā yazālūna mukhtalifīn as “they do not separate from disagreement” might suggest an explanatory connection to cessation, but is not a natural direct translation.

“They continue to differ” or “they will keep differing” is appropriate.

Continuative usage does not make every form of Z Y L mean remaining or steadfastness.

Zayyalnā and tazayyalū still mean separation.

8. Comparison with neighboring roots

Z Y L (zayl) and Z W L (zawāl)

These roots are close in form and meaning, and some lexical analyses connect their yāʾ and wāw historically.

Z W L commonly denotes departure from a position or a condition ending.

The separation branch of Z Y L emphasizes distinguishing things and separating them.

Zawāl in Ibrāhīm 14:44 belongs to Z W L.

Fa-zayyalnā in Yūnus 10:28 belongs to Z Y L.

Semantic proximity does not erase morphological distinctions.

Z Y L (tazyīl) and F R Q (farq)

Farq denotes separation and distinction.

Tazyīl likewise concerns separating or sorting apart.

The two are close in many usages.

Lexical accounts directly explain zayyala through farraqa.

Farq has a broad Qur’anic range, including separation, distinguishing truth from falsehood, and grouping.

Zayyalnā in Yūnus 10:28 separates the parties to a relationship.

Not every use of farq should be restricted to that image.

Z Y L (zayl) and M Y Z (mayz)

Mayz concerns separating and distinguishing things.

Āl ʿImrān 3:179 speaks of distinguishing the impure from the pure.

Lexical accounts explain ziltuhu, “I separated it,” through miztuhu.

The roots clearly overlap in separation and distinction.

Some usages of mayz emphasize recognizing and exposing difference, while zayl can describe the parties actually separating or moving apart.

This is a tendency rather than an absolute boundary.

Z Y L (tazayyul) and F Ṣ L (faṣl)

Faṣl concerns separating things and establishing a boundary between them.

Tazayyul likewise denotes becoming separate.

Faṣl also occurs in Qur’anic contexts of judgment, clear exposition, and distinguishing matters.

Tazayyalū in Al-Fatḥ 48:25 describes human groups separating; it does not itself name a judicial verdict.

Z Y L (ziyāl) and B R ʾ (barāʾah)

Barāʾah concerns disavowal or release from an affiliation or liability.

Ziyāl denotes parting and separation.

In Yūnus 10:28, separation accompanies the alleged partners’ denial of the worship relationship.

Separation and disavowal are close in that context.

Fa-zayyalnā does not itself mean declaring disavowal.

The verb expresses separation; the subsequent speech expresses denial of the relationship.

Z Y L (tazayyul) and KH L Ṭ (khalṭ)

Khalṭ denotes mixing things together.

Tazayyul can stand opposite that condition as intermingled things separate.

Al-Mutalammis’s verse presents the same contrast: blood imagined as mixed becomes distinct again.

Tazayyul does not require complete prior physical mixing.

Two groups may merely inhabit one place before parting.

Al-Fatḥ 48:25 concerns that intermingled human presence, rather than a physical blending of people.

Brief root meaning and contextual meaning

For the brief definition, the following is suitable. These article proposals do not replace installed verse translations or existing authored word-box explanations:

“Separating and becoming separate; in the incomplete predicative construction with negation, continuation or remaining.”

For Yūnus 10:28:

“Separating the worshippers from their alleged partners.”

For Al-Fatḥ 48:25:

“Human groups becoming separate and distinct.”

For mā zāla and lā yazālu:

“The stated condition or conduct not ceasing, but continuing.”

A contextual explanation should identify what continues, such as fighting, disagreement, or remaining in doubt.

10. Conclusion

The Arabic root Z Y L concerns separating, becoming separate, and ending intermingling or association.

In the verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Mutalammis, tazāyul describes things separating after they are imagined as mixed. This supplies early poetic evidence for separation and distinction.

Lexical accounts also record sorting groups of animals, people parting, separation, and discerning differences.

Under the classification adopted here, the root occurs ten times in ten Qur’anic verses, two directly describing separation.

In Yūnus 10:28, The God separates worshippers and alleged partners.

In Al-Fatḥ 48:25, unknown believers not being separate from opponents is discussed in the context of preventing harm to them.

In eight other occurrences, forms of mā zāla and lā yazālu express continued conduct or conditions.

These may concern fighting, discovering betrayal, unease, disagreement, doubt, or a continuing cry.

Those are not meanings of the root itself: they are the states or activities whose continuation is expressed.

Continuation is not necessarily eternal; ḥattā may introduce a limit or stage.

Z Y L and Z W L must also be distinguished. Although some lexical analyses connect them historically, zayyalnā, zawāl, and the incomplete predicative lā yazālu do not have identical usages.

The separation branch concerns things parting or being separated. The established constructions mā zāla and lā yazālu express continuation, which can be explained as the stated condition not ceasing. That explanation should not be made into an unquestionable historical derivation of all the forms.

Recognizing both usages prevents “separation” from replacing “continuation” in translation, or the reverse.
