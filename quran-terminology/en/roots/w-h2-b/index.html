Giving, granting, and bestowing without requiring compensation

The Arabic root w-h-b denotes giving or granting something to another: a gift whose basic meaning is not a transaction or exchange for compensation. وَهَبَ means “he gave”; هِبَة means a gift or act of giving; وَاهِب is a giver; and وَهَّاب describes one who gives abundantly.

This family highlights the relationship between giver, recipient, and gift. A person may give property to another, but broader Arabic usage does not restrict giving to material possessions. Children, opportunities, knowledge, and other benefits may also be objects of hibah.

The Qur’an uses this root for granting children, mercy, judgment, dominion, family blessings, and requests for good from the Lord. It also occurs once concerning a woman who offers herself for marriage to Muhammad.

The God is described three times in the Qur’an as al-Wahhāb. This description denotes His abundant giving, which should not be confined to money or the fulfillment of material wishes.

1. The root in early Arabic and transmitted evidence

In early Arabic, وَهَبَ لَهُ الشَّيْءَ means that someone gave something to another without making compensation a condition of the gift itself.

This differs from exchange or sale. In a transaction, something is transferred for specified compensation; in hibah, giving is central rather than receiving a price.

A poem attributed to the early Arab poet Rabīʿah ibn ʿAbd Allāh al-Bajalī includes:

وَسَلْهَبَةٍ وَهَبْتُ لِغَيْرِ صِهْرٍ
فَلَمْ أَبْكُرْ أُمَيْمُ عَلَى الثَّوَابِ

wa-salhabatin wahabtu li-ghayri ṣihrin / fa-lam abkur Umaymu ʿalā al-thawābi

Translation: “And a tall mare I gave to one who was no relative by marriage; I did not set out early, O Umaymah, to seek recompense.”

سَلْهَبَة here describes a tall mare.

The poet says that he gave a valuable animal to someone unrelated to him by marriage. He then explains that he did not hasten to obtain a reward or compensation.

وَهَبْتُ refers to giving valuable property, while الثَّوَاب is what might have been expected in return.

The line distinguishes giving from seeking compensation. The verb wahabtu expresses the act of giving; the following clause concerns expecting or demanding recompense.

Literary tradition identifies Rabīʿah ibn ʿAbd Allāh al-Bajalī as a pre-Islamic poet. His surviving lines concern old age and the passing of youth. They have nevertheless been preserved through later collections, and the precise date of this line has not been independently established. It is evidence of early usage, but its exact pre-Qur’anic date should not be asserted as certain.

Lexicons define هِبَة as a gift given without compensation. They also record تَوَاهَبَ النَّاسُ for people giving to one another.

The latter usage shows that two people may exchange gifts without those two acts necessarily becoming a transaction with a fixed price and compensation.

Someone may give a friend a book, and the friend may later give something in return. Both gifts may arise from affection and friendship, without an initial contract to exchange two goods.

A giver may still hope for affection, gratitude, or a kind response. The lexical meaning of hibah does not imply that every giver necessarily lacks all purpose, interest, or motivation.

The key distinction is that giving is not conditional on receiving specified compensation.

Arabic sources also record other uses under w-h-b.

In an early usage, مَوْهَبَة, with a fatḥah on the hāʾ, denotes a hollow or natural basin where water collects. In certain expressions, أَوْهَبَ concerns something becoming ready, available, or elevated.

These uses cannot be derived from giving without evidence. Nor does mawhabah, a water-collecting hollow, prove that the sense of talent or divine gift originated in the image of collecting water.

The recorded branches should remain distinct. The established core of the Qur’anic w-h-b family is giving and granting—not a physical transfer from above, the collection of water, or every change of ownership.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important forms of this family include:

  • وَهَبَ: He gave or granted.
  • يَهَبُ: He gives or grants.
  • هَبْ: Give or grant; imperative.
  • وَهْب and وَهَب: Verbal nouns denoting giving.
  • هِبَة: Giving, or something given as a gift.
  • وَاهِب: A giver.
  • وَهَّاب: One who gives abundantly.
  • وَهُوب: Another description of an abundant giver.
  • مَوْهُوب: Something or someone bestowed.
  • مَوْهِبَة: A gift; in other usage, talent or a God-given ability.
  • مَوَاهِب: Plural of mawhibah.
  • تَوَاهَبَ: They gave to one another.
  • اتَّهَبَ: He accepted a gift.
  • اسْتَوْهَبَ: He requested a gift from another.
  • أَوْهَبَ: In certain lexical uses, he made something ready or available.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an.

The Qur’an contains 25 occurrences of w-h-b: 22 forms of the basic triliteral verb وَهَبَ and three of the intensive description وَهَّاب. These occur across 22 verses.

The Qur’anic verbal forms include perfect, imperfect, and imperative forms, such as وَهَبْنَا, وَهَبَ, وَهَبَتْ, يَهَبُ, لِأَهَبَ, and هَبْ.

The initial root wāw is omitted in the imperfect and imperative. Thus, يَهَبُ and هَبْ belong to w-h-b despite lacking a visible wāw.

In Qur’anic supplications, هَبْ is not a command by which a servant obliges the Lord. Grammatically it is imperative, but its communicative function is petition and supplication.

Arabic also uses هَبْ for “suppose” or “consider.” That use should not be confused with “grant” in Qur’anic supplications. In expressions such as رَبِّ هَبْ لِي, the requested object makes the sense of granting clear.

Although widespread in Arabic, هِبَة, مَوْهِبَة, مَوْهُوب, and وَاهِب do not occur in the Qur’an in these forms.

By contrast, the definite description al-Wahhāb occurs three times, each describing The God; its case ending varies with its grammatical position.

3. Qur’anic uses: from granting children to judgment and mercy

1. Granting children: وَهَبْنَا and يَهَبُ

A large portion of Qur’anic w-h-b usage concerns children and offspring.

Al-Anʿām 6:84

وَوَهَبْنَا لَهُ إِسْحَاقَ وَيَعْقُوبَ ۚ كُلًّا هَدَيْنَا ۚ وَنُوحًا هَدَيْنَا مِنْ قَبْلُ ۖ وَمِنْ ذُرِّيَّتِهِ دَاوُودَ وَسُلَيْمَانَ وَأَيُّوبَ وَيُوسُفَ وَمُوسَىٰ وَهَارُونَ ۚ وَكَذَٰلِكَ نَجْزِي الْمُحْسِنِينَ

wa-wahabnā lahu Isḥāqa wa-Yaʿqūba kullan hadaynā wa-Nūḥan hadaynā min qablu wa-min dhurriyyatihi Dāwūda wa-Sulaymāna wa-Ayyūba wa-Yūsufa wa-Mūsā wa-Hārūna wa-kadhālika najzī al-muḥsinīn

Al-Anʿām 6:84

Translation: “And We granted him Isaac and Jacob; each We guided. And Noah We guided before; and among his descendants, David, Solomon, Job, Joseph, Moses, and Aaron. Thus do We recompense those who do good.”

وَهَبْنَا is a first-person plural perfect expressing The God’s granting.

Isaac and Jacob are granted to Abraham in this verse. The Qur’an then speaks of their guidance.

وَهَبْنَا and هَدَيْنَا are distinct verbs.

Granting a child is one matter, and that child being guided is another. The root w-h-b itself does not imply that every child bestowed is necessarily guided or righteous.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:72 also speaks of granting Isaac and Jacob to Abraham, then states their righteousness.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:27 mentions the same gift and then the placement of prophethood and the Book among Abraham’s descendants.

These recurring uses show that a child may be called a divine gift in Qur’anic language, while the child’s moral qualities and later standing are conveyed by other words and statements.

Al-Shūrā 42:49

لِلَّهِ مُلْكُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ ۚ يَخْلُقُ مَا يَشَاءُ ۚ يَهَبُ لِمَنْ يَشَاءُ إِنَاثًا وَيَهَبُ لِمَنْ يَشَاءُ الذُّكُورَ

li-llāhi mulku al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍi yakhluqu mā yashāʾu yahabu li-man yashāʾu ināthan wa-yahabu li-man yashāʾu al-dhukūr

Al-Shūrā 42:49

Translation: “To The God belongs the dominion of the heavens and the earth. He creates what He wills; He grants daughters to whom He wills, and He grants sons to whom He wills.”

يَهَبُ occurs twice in this verse.

Daughters are the object of the first granting, and sons of the second.

The clauses have the same construction. The Qur’an uses the same verb يَهَبُ for both.

This matters because some cultures regard a son’s birth as a blessing and a daughter’s as less valuable or shameful. Here, both belong within divine giving.

Al-Shūrā 42:50 speaks of granting daughters and sons together and of leaving some people without children.

This continuity shows that granting children is one expression of The God’s will and creation.

Hibah in these verses should not mean that birth requires no biological causes. Ordinarily, a child is born through pregnancy and childbirth, yet the Qur’an also calls that child The God’s gift.

Thus, divine giving and natural causes are not contradictory concepts.

People may play a biological role in a child’s conception, but that role does not confer independent ownership of the child’s creation or destiny.

Nor do these verses justify treating every person’s childlessness as divine punishment or a sign of worthlessness.

2. Zechariah’s supplication: requesting wholesome offspring

Āl ʿImrān 3:38

هُنَالِكَ دَعَا زَكَرِيَّا رَبَّهُ ۖ قَالَ رَبِّ هَبْ لِي مِنْ لَدُنْكَ ذُرِّيَّةً طَيِّبَةً ۖ إِنَّكَ سَمِيعُ الدُّعَاءِ

hunālika daʿā Zakariyyā rabbahu qāla rabbi hab lī min ladunka dhurriyyatan ṭayyibatan innaka samīʿu al-duʿāʾ

Āl ʿImrān 3:38

Translation: “There Zechariah called upon his Lord, saying: My Lord, grant me from Yourself wholesome offspring; You surely hear supplication.”

هَبْ لِي petitions the Lord for a gift.

مِنْ لَدُنْكَ identifies its source, while ذُرِّيَّةً طَيِّبَةً specifies what Zechariah requests.

طَيِّبَةً describes the requested offspring. Wholesomeness comes from this adjective, not from w-h-b itself.

Zechariah does not merely request a child; he asks for offspring marked by goodness and purity.

In Maryam 19:5 he also says:

Maryam 19:5

وَإِنِّي خِفْتُ الْمَوَالِيَ مِنْ وَرَائِي وَكَانَتِ امْرَأَتِي عَاقِرًا فَهَبْ لِي مِنْ لَدُنْكَ وَلِيًّا

wa-innī khiftu al-mawāliya min warāʾī wa-kānati imraʾatī ʿāqiran fa-hab lī min ladunka waliyyā

Maryam 19:5

Translation: “I fear for the relatives and successors after me, and my wife has been barren; so grant me from Yourself a successor.”

The requested object here is وَلِيًّا.

Walī belongs to the separate root w-l-y, concerning closeness, connection, and responsibility. The nature of the succession and the qualities Zechariah seeks become clearer in the following verses.

Study of w-l-y

Comparing these supplications shows how a request may be expressed in two ways: wholesome offspring and a suitable successor.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:90 speaks of Zechariah’s supplication being answered and John being granted to him.

Yet هَبْ itself does not mean curing barrenness or changing his wife’s physical condition. Those matters are conveyed by other words.

W-h-b expresses granting; how it is accomplished emerges from context.

3. Giving through an intermediary: لِأَهَبَ in Mary’s story

Maryam 19:19

قَالَ إِنَّمَا أَنَا رَسُولُ رَبِّكِ لِأَهَبَ لَكِ غُلَامًا زَكِيًّا

qāla innamā anā rasūlu rabbiki li-ahaba laki ghulāman zakiyyā

Maryam 19:19

Translation: “He said: I am only your Lord’s Messenger, to grant you a pure boy.”

This verse presents a distinctive use of w-h-b.

A Messenger who has come to Mary is speaking. He first identifies himself as her Lord’s Messenger, then says that he has come to grant a pure boy.

لِأَهَبَ is first-person singular imperfect, and in this construction the granting is attributed to the speaker.

That same speaker, however, identifies himself as the Lord’s Messenger.

Attributing the act to the Messenger therefore does not make him an independent source of creation or the gift of a child.

The verse distinguishes the source of the gift from the intermediary conveying or carrying it out.

Ordinarily, someone may deliver a gift on another’s behalf and say, “I have come to give you this gift.” That statement alone does not establish that the gift came from the courier’s own property.

In Mary’s story, the Messenger’s identity is clear from the sentence itself.

Some recognized readings have لِيَهَبَ, “so that He may grant you,” relating the giving more directly to The God. In لِأَهَبَ, the grammatical subject is first person and is understood here as the Messenger. This difference in how the subject is expressed matters without changing the gift’s dependence on the Lord.

Hibah therefore need not mean giving without an intermediary. The source and intermediary’s role must be identified through construction and context.

4. Granting a brother, family, and mercy

Maryam 19:53

وَوَهَبْنَا لَهُ مِنْ رَحْمَتِنَا أَخَاهُ هَارُونَ نَبِيًّا

wa-wahabnā lahu min raḥmatinā akhāhu Hārūna nabiyyā

Maryam 19:53

Translation: “And out of Our mercy We granted him his brother Aaron as a Prophet.”

Aaron is the object of the granting here.

Aaron was already Moses’ brother. The gift therefore does not mean creating a brother at that instant or transferring ownership of a person.

The Qur’an speaks of granting Aaron as a Prophet in connection with divine mercy.

This shows that someone or something already existing may be presented as a divine gift within a new relationship or role.

In Ṭā Hā 20:29, Ṭā Hā 20:30, Ṭā Hā 20:31, Ṭā Hā 20:32, Ṭā Hā 20:33, Ṭā Hā 20:34, Ṭā Hā 20:35, and Ṭā Hā 20:36, Moses asks the Lord to make Aaron his helper and partner in responsibility, and The God accepts his request.

The granting in Maryam 19:53 thus relates to this partnership in assistance and mission.

It should not be turned into ownership of another person. Aaron being granted to Moses does not imply that he became Moses’ personal property.

In Maryam 19:50, The God also speaks of granting mercy to Abraham and his family.

Ṣād 38:43, following Job’s trial, describes granting him his family and their like with them.

These uses show that hibah can concern family blessings, human relationships, and divine mercy.

In Ṣād 38:43, the exact manner of the gift and which family members were restored or added are not specified by وَهَبْنَا itself. The verb expresses the granting; its details require other evidence.

5. Granting judgment: beyond wealth and children

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:21

فَفَرَرْتُ مِنْكُمْ لَمَّا خِفْتُكُمْ فَوَهَبَ لِي رَبِّي حُكْمًا وَجَعَلَنِي مِنَ الْمُرْسَلِينَ

fa-farartu minkum lammā khiftukum fa-wahaba lī rabbī ḥukman wa-jaʿalanī mina al-mursalīn

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:21

Translation: “So I fled from you when I feared you; then my Lord granted me judgment and made me one of the Messengers.”

Moses is speaking.

فَوَهَبَ لِي رَبِّي حُكْمًا refers to the Lord granting judgment.

Ḥukm belongs to the separate root ḥ-k-m and relates in Qur’anic usage to judgment, authority to judge, and wisdom.

Giving here is therefore not confined to property or a child. The gift is an ability or position connected with judgment.

The following clause also says:

“وَجَعَلَنِي مِنَ الْمُرْسَلِينَ”

That is, The God made Moses one of the Messengers.

These expressions should remain distinct. Granting judgment relates to making Moses one of the Messengers, but the word ḥukm alone should not be made to contain every feature of messengership.

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:83, Abraham also prays:

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:83

رَبِّ هَبْ لِي حُكْمًا وَأَلْحِقْنِي بِالصَّالِحِينَ

rabbi hab lī ḥukman wa-alḥiqnī bi-al-ṣāliḥīn

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:83

Translation: “My Lord, grant me judgment and join me with the righteous.”

Receiving judgment and joining the righteous are two requests in this supplication.

This use also shows that giving may concern something spiritual, intellectual, or related to competence in judgment.

But w-h-b itself does not mean knowledge, wisdom, or judicial ability. Those meanings come from the object, حُكْمًا.

Study of ḥ-k-m

6. Granting dominion: Solomon’s supplication

Ṣād 38:35

قَالَ رَبِّ اغْفِرْ لِي وَهَبْ لِي مُلْكًا لَا يَنْبَغِي لِأَحَدٍ مِنْ بَعْدِي ۖ إِنَّكَ أَنْتَ الْوَهَّابُ

qāla rabbi ighfir lī wa-hab lī mulkan lā yanbaghī li-aḥadin min baʿdī innaka anta al-wahhāb

Ṣād 38:35

Translation: “He said: My Lord, forgive me and grant me a dominion that will not befit anyone after me; surely You are the Abundant Giver.”

Solomon makes two requests: forgiveness and dominion.

وَهَبْ لِي مُلْكًا asks for dominion to be bestowed.

Mulk belongs to m-l-k and concerns dominion and authority. Kingship therefore comes from the verb’s object, not from w-h-b.

This verse shows that divine giving may include power and dominion.

It does not follow that every political power or human government enjoys The God’s moral approval simply because this verse uses the language of giving.

Solomon’s request concerns him in its specific context.

لَا يَنْبَغِي لِأَحَدٍ مِنْ بَعْدِي is an important qualification distinguishing his request from a general desire for power and wealth.

The verse ends with al-Wahhāb, the same description used in the plea for mercy in Āl ʿImrān 3:8.

Together, these uses show that divine giving may extend from mercy and guidance to ability and dominion.

Study of m-l-k

4. Human giving: وَهَبَتْ نَفْسَهَا

Al-Aḥzāb 33:50

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّبِيُّ إِنَّا أَحْلَلْنَا لَكَ أَزْوَاجَكَ اللَّاتِي آتَيْتَ أُجُورَهُنَّ وَمَا مَلَكَتْ يَمِينُكَ مِمَّا أَفَاءَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْكَ وَبَنَاتِ عَمِّكَ وَبَنَاتِ عَمَّاتِكَ وَبَنَاتِ خَالِكَ وَبَنَاتِ خَالَاتِكَ اللَّاتِي هَاجَرْنَ مَعَكَ وَامْرَأَةً مُؤْمِنَةً إِنْ وَهَبَتْ نَفْسَهَا لِلنَّبِيِّ إِنْ أَرَادَ النَّبِيُّ أَنْ يَسْتَنْكِحَهَا خَالِصَةً لَكَ مِنْ دُونِ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ ۗ قَدْ عَلِمْنَا مَا فَرَضْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ فِي أَزْوَاجِهِمْ وَمَا مَلَكَتْ أَيْمَانُهُمْ لِكَيْلَا يَكُونَ عَلَيْكَ حَرَجٌ ۗ وَكَانَ اللَّهُ غَفُورًا رَحِيمًا

yā ayyuhā al-nabiyyu innā aḥlalnā laka azwājaka allātī ātayta ujūrahunna wa-mā malakat yamīnuka mimmā afāʾa Allāhu ʿalayka wa-banāti ʿammika wa-banāti ʿammātika wa-banāti khālika wa-banāti khālātika allātī hājarna maʿaka wa-imraʾatan muʾminatan in wahabat nafsahā li-al-nabiyyi in arāda al-nabiyyu an yastankiḥahā khāliṣatan laka min dūni al-muʾminīna qad ʿalimnā mā faraḍnā ʿalayhim fī azwājihim wa-mā malakat aymānuhum li-kaylā yakūna ʿalayka ḥarajun wa-kāna Allāhu ghafūran raḥīmā

Al-Aḥzāb 33:50

Translation: “O Prophet, We have made lawful for you your wives whose dues you have given, and those in your possession from what The God has restored to you, and the daughters of your paternal uncles, paternal aunts, maternal uncles, and maternal aunts who emigrated with you, and a believing woman if she offers herself to the Prophet, if the Prophet wishes to marry her—a provision exclusive to you, apart from the believers. We know what We have prescribed for them concerning their wives and those in their possession, so that there may be no constraint upon you. The God is Forgiving, Merciful.”

وَهَبَتْ is the third-person feminine singular perfect of وَهَبَ.

The subject is a believing woman and the object نَفْسَهَا, herself.

The context immediately clarifies the purpose: marriage to the Prophet.

إِنْ وَهَبَتْ نَفْسَهَا لِلنَّبِيِّ refers to a woman offering herself for marriage to Muhammad.

A further condition follows:

“إِنْ أَرَادَ النَّبِيُّ أَنْ يَسْتَنْكِحَهَا”

That is, if the Prophet wishes to marry her.

Both conditions matter. Her initiative alone does not constitute a marriage; the Prophet’s willingness is also specified.

“Giving herself” should not be read as transferring ownership of her body and existence to a man. The verse concerns marriage, not turning a free person into a commodity.

Nor does hibah imply that every rule of marriage, women’s rights, or the responsibilities of either party disappear.

خَالِصَةً لَكَ مِنْ دُونِ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ makes this provision particular to Muhammad.

This specific provision cannot therefore be generalized to all men or marriages without independent evidence.

Later juristic discussions often examine this verse in relation to marriage without a bridal due. Lexically, hibah relates to giving without compensation, which may provide a basis for that discussion; but the root does not itself supply all the legal details of marriage.

For root study, the central point is that Qur’anic w-h-b is not limited to property or divine giving. It also has a human use in which someone offers herself for marriage.

Here hibah must be understood within the conditions and subject matter of this verse.

Study of n-k-ḥ

5. Al-Wahhāb: The God’s abundant giving

وَهَّاب follows the pattern فَعَّال, which in many Arabic uses expresses intensity, repetition, or abundance of an action.

Derived from وَهَبَ, وَهَّاب describes one who gives abundantly.

The pattern alone does not imply that a giver grants every petitioner everything requested at every moment. It can convey abundance and continuity; the actual scope of giving depends on usage and context.

Al-Wahhāb occurs three times in the Qur’an, always describing The God.

1. Granting mercy and steadfastness in guidance

Āl ʿImrān 3:8

رَبَّنَا لَا تُزِغْ قُلُوبَنَا بَعْدَ إِذْ هَدَيْتَنَا وَهَبْ لَنَا مِنْ لَدُنْكَ رَحْمَةً ۚ إِنَّكَ أَنْتَ الْوَهَّابُ

rabbanā lā tuzigh qulūbanā baʿda idh hadaytanā wa-hab lanā min ladunka raḥmatan innaka anta al-wahhāb

Āl ʿImrān 3:8

Translation: “Our Lord, do not cause our hearts to deviate after You have guided us, and grant us mercy from Yourself; surely You are the Abundant Giver.”

This verse continues the speech of those firmly grounded in knowledge in Āl ʿImrān 3:7.

Despite their faith and knowledge, they ask the Lord not to let their hearts deviate after guidance and to grant them mercy from Himself.

وَهَبْ لَنَا requests giving, and رَحْمَةً is its object.

Mercy too can therefore be an object of hibah.

Giving here does not mean receiving money or acquiring an object. The petitioners seek mercy, which may manifest in guidance, steadfastness, and participation in divine good.

مِنْ لَدُنْكَ identifies the gift’s source.

They do not treat mercy as something they can oblige The God to pay, like a party to a transaction, merely by claiming knowledge or faith.

Ending with إِنَّكَ أَنْتَ الْوَهَّابُ accords with this understanding. They petition a giving Lord whose gifts are not bounded by human needs and wishes.

Al-Wahhāb here is directly connected with mercy and guidance.

2. Treasuries of mercy

Ṣād 38:9

أَمْ عِنْدَهُمْ خَزَائِنُ رَحْمَةِ رَبِّكَ الْعَزِيزِ الْوَهَّابِ

am ʿindahum khazāʾinu raḥmati rabbika al-ʿazīzi al-wahhāb

Ṣād 38:9

Translation: “Or do they possess the treasuries of your Lord’s mercy, the Invincible, the Abundant Giver?”

الْوَهَّابِ accompanies الْعَزِيزِ in this verse.

ʿAzīz belongs to ʿ-z-z and relates to might and invincibility.

Wahhāb belongs to w-h-b and concerns abundant giving.

Their combination places The God’s power and generosity together in this context.

They are not synonyms. ʿAzīz does not itself mean giver, nor does Wahhāb alone mean invincible.

The question challenges people who act as if they control the distribution of divine mercy and the selection of its recipients.

خَزَائِنُ رَحْمَةِ رَبِّكَ expresses the source and extent of divine mercy. No person independently owns those treasuries.

In this Qur’anic perspective, prophethood, guidance, and mercy cannot be made dependent on the decision or monopoly of a family, group, or religious class.

That conclusion comes from the broader context, not from w-h-b alone.

3. Requesting dominion

In Ṣād 38:35, after asking forgiveness, Solomon requests a particular dominion and concludes with إِنَّكَ أَنْتَ الْوَهَّابُ.

The same description accompanied a request for mercy in Āl ʿImrān 3:8.

The comparison shows that Wahhāb is not limited to a particular kind of gift.

People may seek mercy and steadfastness in guidance or, in particular circumstances, authority and dominion.

What is granted to whom depends on the request, the person’s circumstances, and divine will.

Al-Wahhāb describes The God who gives, whose generosity is not limited to one kind of blessing. It does not guarantee that every human wish will be fulfilled in its desired form and time.

6. Hibah, giving, provision, and related roots

w-h-b (wahb) and ʿ-ṭ-w (ʿaṭāʾ): gifting and giving

ʿAṭāʾ in the Qur’an concerns giving and conveying something to another.

Hibah is also a form of giving, but giving without requiring compensation is prominent in its semantic field.

Someone may give a worker their wage. This is giving in a general sense, but fundamentally it pays an entitlement arising from work.

If that person gives the worker something beyond the agreed wage without requiring repayment, the act is closer to hibah.

ʿAṭāʾ may have a broader Qur’anic range, including contexts in which the word itself does not specify the payment’s legal or ethical relationship.

Conversely, not all divine giving should be imagined as a transaction in exchange for human action. The lexical distinction creates no absolute boundary between divine gifts and human payments.

Study of ʿ-ṭ-w

w-h-b (wahb) and r-z-q (rizq): gifts and provision

Qur’anic rizq concerns provision and benefits reaching people or other beings.

Hibah may likewise provide a benefit, but Qur’anic uses often foreground the act of giving and its relationship to the giver.

For example, in Ibrāhīm 14:39 Abraham thanks The God for granting him Ishmael and Isaac.

Elsewhere, rizq can include food, resources for living, and other benefits.

A child may be both a divine blessing and, viewed through the particular relationship, a gift from Him.

Rizq and hibah may thus describe the same benefit from different angles.

Rizq need not arise without natural causes, and hibah has no such restriction either.

Study of r-z-q

w-h-b (wahb) and m-n-n (mann): giving and holding a favor over someone

M-n-n has diverse Qur’anic uses, from blessing and beneficence to reminding others of favors and, in some contexts, cutting off or diminishing.

Hibah concerns giving.

Someone may give a gift but subsequently humiliate its recipient through repeated reminders or demand obedience and compensation.

This shows that giving and holding a favor over someone are distinct acts.

Al-Baqarah 2:262 praises those who do not follow their spending with reminders of favors and injury.

That verse concerns spending and does not use hibah itself, but its ethical principle helps distinguish generosity from humiliating a recipient.

Hibah alone does not guarantee that the giver’s intentions and conduct are wholly virtuous.

Study of m-n-n

w-h-b (wahb) and f-ḍ-l (faḍl): giving and abundance

Faḍl concerns abundance, superiority, and benefits beyond an ordinary measure.

Hibah emphasizes giving.

A benefit may reach someone from The God’s bounty and also be His gift, but the roots are not identical in meaning.

Faḍl draws attention to abundance and distinction, whereas hibah foregrounds the giver-recipient relationship.

Both may connect with mercy and divine blessings, but neither should replace the other without examination.

Study of f-ḍ-l

w-h-b (wahb) and ʾ-j-r (ajr): gifts and recompense

Ajr in the Qur’an concerns wages or reward.

In its basic sense, hibah is a gift that does not put its recipient under a debt to provide compensation.

The distinction is clear in human relationships: paying wages is not identical to giving a gift.

In humanity’s relationship with The God, however, this distinction should not be turned into a transaction exactly resembling human financial dealings.

The God promises reward for righteous action. His fulfillment of that promise does not conflict with humanity’s dependence on divine giving for creation, abilities, opportunities, and many benefits.

Reward and divine giving can therefore appear together within the Qur’an’s conceptual relationships without their roots becoming synonyms.

Study of ʾ-j-r

w-h-b (wahb) and h-d-y (hady and hadiyyah): giving and a presented gift

هَدِيَّة is something sent or presented to another.

In Al-Naml 27:35, the queen of Sheba speaks of sending a gift.

Hibah and hadiyyah often overlap, but their motivations and circumstances are not always identical.

A gift may express affection, respect, a wish for friendship, or even a political objective.

The queen of Sheba’s gift also occurs within relations between two powers.

Merely calling something a hadiyyah or hibah therefore does not definitively establish the giver’s intention.

A gift’s ethical value must be considered alongside people’s intentions, circumstances, and conduct.

7. Challenges of interpretation and translation

One common difficulty is restricting w-h-b to material gifts.

The Persian verb for giving is often used of money, goods, and property; the Qur’an also speaks of granting mercy, judgment, dominion, and children.

Translation should therefore suit the object of the verb.

“Granted” suits a child; “granted judgment” also fits; and “bestowed mercy” or “granted mercy” may render a gift of mercy.

Another difficulty is confusing giving without compensation with giving without causes.

Hibah’s lexical sense emphasizes the absence of transactional compensation. It does not imply that giving always occurs without an intermediary, circumstances, or means.

Zechariah’s story includes his supplication and family circumstances.

Mary’s story includes a divine Messenger.

In ordinary life, a child is born through biological processes.

None of these removes the sense of hibah.

Nor does Wahhāb describe a giver who fulfills every request regardless of His wisdom and will.

Al-Shūrā 42:49 and Al-Shūrā 42:50 place granting children alongside childlessness. Abundant divine giving therefore does not mean identical gifts to everyone.

In Qur’anic supplication, asking The God likewise does not mean gaining control of His will.

People state their needs and ask the Lord to grant them, but what is ultimately bestowed remains within divine knowledge, wisdom, and will.

Nor should every benefit be presented, without evidence, as The God’s moral approval of its recipient. Wealth, children, and power do not by themselves constitute a final assessment of faith and righteous conduct.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

“Gave” and “granted” suit وَهَبَ.

“Gives” or “grants” is natural for يَهَبُ.

For هَبْ in supplication, “grant” is a suitable concise equivalent.

For هِبَة, “giving” and, especially for material things, “gift” are suitable.

For وَاهِب, “giver” or “bestower” is proposed.

For وَهَّاب, “abundant giver” or “one who gives abundantly” fits. As a description of The God, “the Abundant Giver” conveys the core sense.

For مَوْهُوب, “bestowed” or “something granted to another” is natural.

For تَوَاهَبَ, “they gave to one another” fits.

For اسْتَوْهَبَ, “requested a gift” is proposed; for اتَّهَبَ, “accepted a gift.”

The following renderings suit important Qur’anic uses:

Āl ʿImrān 3:8

“And grant us mercy from Yourself; surely You are the Abundant Giver.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:38

“My Lord, grant me from Yourself wholesome offspring.”

Maryam 19:19

“I am only your Lord’s Messenger, to grant you a pure boy.”

Maryam 19:53

“And out of Our mercy We granted him his brother Aaron as a Prophet.”

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:21

“Then my Lord granted me judgment.”

Al-Shūrā 42:49

“He grants daughters to whom He wills, and He grants sons to whom He wills.”

Ṣād 38:35

“And grant me a dominion that will not befit anyone after me; surely You are the Abundant Giver.”

Al-Aḥzāb 33:50

“And a believing woman if she offers herself to the Prophet, if the Prophet wishes to marry her.”

For the last use, explaining the marriage context is essential: “giving oneself” should not be taken as a transfer of ownership of a person.

9. Conclusion

In Arabic, w-h-b concerns giving and granting: a gift whose basic sense is not a transaction for compensation.

Early linguistic evidence, including the poem attributed to Rabīʿah ibn ʿAbd Allāh al-Bajalī, attests to giving property and distinguishing it from seeking recompense. Yet the precise dating of some witnesses and the historical connections among secondary branches are insufficiently clear to derive every use from one original image.

The root occurs 25 times in the Qur’an. The gift may be a child, mercy, judgment, dominion, or another benefit.

Granting children is prominent in the stories of Abraham and Zechariah; Moses uses w-h-b for judgment, while Solomon requests dominion.

In Maryam 19:19, a divine Messenger speaks of giving. Attributing a gift to an intermediary need not make that intermediary its independent source.

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:50, وَهَبَتْ نَفْسَهَا is a human use concerning marriage and must be read within that verse’s conditions and subject matter.

Al-Wahhāb describes The God three times, denoting abundant giving. It accompanies requests for mercy and dominion and a reference to the treasuries of divine mercy.

The God’s being Wahhāb is therefore not confined to material gifts or bodily needs. Mercy, guidance, opportunities, knowledge, and other benefits can also belong within divine giving.

Nevertheless, hibah does not abolish natural causes, guarantee the fulfillment of every wish, or prove divine moral approval of every recipient.

W-h-b ultimately denotes giving or granting something to another without making compensation a condition of the gift itself. Qur’anic usage includes both divine and human giving; its source, object, intermediary, and ethical value emerge from each verse’s construction and context. Al-Wahhāb describes The God whose giving is abundant and extensive, unrestricted to any particular kind of blessing.
