Core Meaning

The root ت ل ل comes through the word tallahu. This root occurs in the Qur'an only once:

فَلَمَّا أَسْلَمَا وَتَلَّهُ لِلْجَبِينِ

So when they both submitted, and he cast him down upon the side of the forehead...

Al-Saffat 37:103

T L L, in its primary use, indicates taking someone and casting them down to the ground, so that the body leaves a standing and free state and is placed on the face, the side of the forehead, the neck, or the side.

The root T L L indicates overthrowing, casting to the ground, and placing the body of a person or animal in a fallen and restrained position.

Tallahu is not merely “laying down.” In this verb, the doer takes another body, disrupts its standing state, and places it in a specific position on the ground.

Its semantic core may be summarized as taking and casting someone down, and placing him in a fallen position, especially on the side of the face or forehead.

Important Forms

تَلَّ is a form I past verb: he cast down; overthrew; threw face-down; placed in a fallen state. This verb occurs in the Qur'an only in the form tallahu.

تَلَّهُ is the past verb with an object pronoun: he cast him to the ground; he overthrew him; he placed him on the side of the face or forehead.

وَتَلَّهُ لِلْجَبِينِ

And he cast him down upon the side of the forehead.

Al-Saffat 37:103

The pronoun -hu returns to the son, and the subject of the verb is Ibrahim.

يَتُلُّ is a lexical imperfect verb: he casts down; overthrows; places face-down. This form does not occur in the Qur'an.

تَلّ in Arabic can be the verbal noun of the verb, and it can also be a noun for a mound or raised part of land. But mere formal similarity between these two uses should not be taken as proof that tallahu originally means “to make someone like a mound.” The clear verbal core is casting to the ground and placing the body in a fallen state.

مَتْلُول is a lexical passive participle: cast down, overthrown, placed in a fallen state. This form does not occur in the Qur'an.

تَلِيل appears in some lexical uses for someone fallen or overthrown. This form is also not Qur'anic.

Linguistic Note

T L L is a transitive verb. The person does not fall by himself; a doer takes him and casts him to the ground.

This distinguishes it from several nearby roots. In suqut, a person may fall downward by himself. In 'ithar, the foot catches on an obstacle. In ta's, the person slips and becomes ruinously fallen. In tallahu, a doer deliberately places another person on the ground.

Three main elements exist in T L L: control over another body; removal of the standing or free state; and placing the body in a specific position on the ground.

In the verse from Al-Saffat, tallahu comes after aslama:

فَلَمَّا أَسْلَمَا وَتَلَّهُ لِلْجَبِينِ

First, both submit; then Ibrahim performs the bodily action.

Therefore aslama is a voluntary, two-sided acceptance, while tallahu is a bodily and one-sided motion. The two verbs should not be mixed in translation or analysis.

Material Image

The material image of T L L is taking a human being or animal and bringing it down to the ground, not for rest, but to restrain movement or prepare the body for a specific act.

In this movement, the body is first standing or free; the doer takes it; changes its balance; brings it toward the ground; and places the face, the side of the forehead, the neck, or the side upon the surface.

In the verse, lil-jabin clarifies the direction of placement.

Jabin refers to the side of the forehead. Therefore, the verse does not picture laying someone on the back. The body has been turned so that the side of the forehead or side of the face is placed on the ground.

Such a position may make the neck accessible for the next act. It may also reduce direct face-to-face contact between father and son. But the Qur'an itself does not explain the reason for choosing this position; therefore, these two points should be understood as practical and psychological possibilities, not as the certain meaning of lil-jabin.

T L L itself does not mean dhabḥ. This root only states the stage of placing the body.

Classical Usage

In classical Arabic, tallahu was used for casting to the ground, overthrowing, and placing a person on the face, neck, or side of the body.

This verb could appear in different contexts: throwing someone down in a fight; restraining a person or animal; preparing an animal for slaughter; and placing a body in a position where its motion is restricted.

The common core in all these uses is removing the body from a standing state and placing it on the ground.

Tall in the sense of a mound or raised ground is also known in Arabic, but the precise historical connection between this noun and the verb tallahu is not clear. It is better not to say that through tallahu someone “becomes like a mound,” unless independent linguistic evidence for such imagery is found.

Mutual Submission Before Bodily Motion

فَلَمَّا أَسْلَمَا

So when they both submitted.

Al-Saffat 37:103

Aslama is a dual verb and returns to Ibrahim and his son.

This structure shows that before tallahu, both had participated in the decision: Ibrahim becomes ready to carry out what he understood from the vision; the son also declares his acceptance; then the bodily action takes place.

Therefore, in this verse, tallahu does not picture overpowering a resistant person. Lexically, the verb can occur in coercive contexts, but aslama shows that this scene occurs after the acceptance of both.

Tallahu: One-Sided Motion After a Two-Sided Decision

وَتَلَّهُ

And he cast him down.

In this verb, Ibrahim is the subject, the son is the object, and jabin shows the direction of the body's placement.

The son is one of the two subjects of the decision in aslama, but in tallahu he becomes the object of the bodily motion.

This grammatical difference does not erase the son's voluntary identity. Before his body is placed on the ground, he has already stated his position:

يَا أَبَتِ افْعَلْ مَا تُؤْمَرُ

O my father, do what you are commanded.

Al-Saffat 37:102

Meaning of Lil-Jabin

لِلْجَبِينِ

Jabin most often refers to one of the two sides of the forehead.

The lām in this structure shows direction or state of placement: he cast him upon the jabin; he placed him on the ground so that his jabin was on the ground; he laid him on the side of the forehead and face.

“Upon the side of the forehead” is more precise than “upon the forehead,” because jabin is not necessarily the middle of the forehead.

Practical Geometry of the Scene

Being placed upon the jabin may have several bodily results: the body leaves free movement; the head and neck are placed in a restrained position; the neck becomes accessible for the next act; and the son's face does not remain directly facing the father.

But this analysis is a practical reconstruction of the scene. The verse only states the condition lil-jabin and does not explicitly state Ibrahim's motive or the psychological effect of cutting direct eye contact.

So this possibility may be mentioned, but it should not be treated as part of the lexical meaning of T L L.

Omitted Answer of Lamma and Suspense

The verse begins with this structure:

فَلَمَّا أَسْلَمَا وَتَلَّهُ لِلْجَبِينِ

So when they both submitted, and he cast him down upon the side of the forehead...

One expects the result or answer of the clause after lamma. But the verse stops at this point and immediately says:

وَنَادَيْنَاهُ أَنْ يَا إِبْرَاهِيمُ

And We called him: O Ibrahim.

Al-Saffat 37:104

The answer of lamma is not explicitly mentioned. This omission holds the scene at its peak: both have submitted; the son has been placed on the ground; the act has reached the edge of execution; but before the next motion is stated, the call arrives.

Tallahu is the last bodily motion that the Qur'an states before the intervention of the call. This structure preserves the distance between full readiness and the stopping of the act.

T L L and the Halt Before Dhabh

After tallahu comes:

قَدْ صَدَّقْتَ الرُّؤْيَا

You have confirmed the vision.

Al-Saffat 37:105

The Qur'an does not say dhabahahu, nor that he cut his throat, spilled his blood, or that he died.

No explicit verb for cutting the throat, blood flowing, or the son's death occurs.

For this reason, tallahu should not be translated as “he slaughtered him.” The verb states that the scene reached the stage in which the body had been placed for the act, but the slaughter itself had not occurred.

Confirming the Vision and Reaching the Edge of Action

Tallahu shows that Ibrahim's acceptance was not only mental or verbal. He brought the decision into concrete motion.

Nevertheless, the Qur'an declares the vision confirmed before the son is killed.

This order shows that in this narrative, truthfulness of intention, mutual acceptance, and serious arrival at the edge of action are fulfilled without a human death taking place.

But this conclusion is understood from the set of verses, not from the meaning of T L L alone.

T L L, Dhabh, and Human Dignity

The word tallahu states a material motion; by itself, it does not judge the value or dignity of the person.

That the Qur'an stops the narrative before dhabḥ is structurally important: the son is not killed and a ransom replaces him.

At the same time, without historical research, one cannot say that the Qur'an deliberately used tallahu to avoid “violent vocabulary of ancient religions.” The Qur'an, in this same story, uses the word dhabḥ in the son's speech and then uses dhibh azim:

إِنِّي أَرَىٰ فِي الْمَنَامِ أَنِّي أَذْبَحُكَ

I see in the dream that I slaughter you.

Al-Saffat 37:102

وَفَدَيْنَاهُ بِذِبْحٍ عَظِيمٍ

And We ransomed him with a great sacrifice.

Al-Saffat 37:107

So the Qur'an did not avoid the word dhabḥ. Its precision lies in this: regarding the son, the act actually performed is named tallahu, not dhabahahu.

The difference is this: dhabḥ appears in the vision and in the ransom; tallahu is the last bodily action performed upon the son; the son's death does not occur in the text.

This distinction is more precise than the general claim of “avoiding violent language.”

Similar Uses

Tallahu and laying down: Laying down can be calm and for rest. Tallahu indicates casting down and placing the body in a restrained position. Laying down: placing in a lying position. Tallahu: overthrowing and placing on the ground.

Tallahu and falling: Falling may happen by itself or through loss of balance. Tallahu is transitive; someone else casts the person down. Saqata: he fell. Tallahu: he cast him down.

Tallahu and 'ithar: 'Ithar is the foot catching on an obstacle and stumbling. Tallahu is not stumbling; it is the deliberate casting down of another.

Tallahu and ta's: Ta's is the person's own slipping and ruinous falling with failure. Tallahu is another person casting someone down.

Tallahu and kabb: Kabb indicates throwing face-down. Tallahu may have a similar result, but it preserves more strongly the act of taking and placing in a fallen position.

Tallahu and sar': Sar' means throwing someone to the ground and removing him from standing. Tallahu is close to this, but in the verse lil-jabin also specifies the direction of the body's placement.

Tallahu and adja'a: Idja' means laying on the side. Tallahu preserves more strongly the force of casting down and restraining the body.

Tallahu and dhabḥ: Dhabḥ is cutting the passages of the neck. Tallahu is placing the body before that. Tallahu: he cast down. Dhabḥ: he cut the throat. Regarding the son, the Qur'an states the first, not the second.

Tallahu and qatl: Qatl is ending life. Tallahu does not necessarily lead to death.

Tallahu and sujud: Sujud is a person's voluntary descent. Tallahu is casting a person down by another.

Tallahu and submission: Submission in the verse is expressed by aslama and concerns the acceptance of both. Tallahu is the bodily motion after it. Aslama: they both submitted. Tallahu: one cast the other down.

Grammar and Structure

فَلَمَّا أَسْلَمَا: lamma begins a temporal, condition-like clause: when they both submitted... Aslama is a dual verb, and its subjects are Ibrahim and his son.

وَتَلَّهُ: the structure of the verb is this: talla is a past verb; its hidden subject is Ibrahim; -hu is the object pronoun, referring to the son. The wāw connects this motion to the previous submission.

لِلْجَبِينِ: the lām expresses state and direction of placement. Suitable translations: upon the side of the forehead; upon the jabin; while his jabin was on the ground.

After falamma aslama wa-tallahu lil-jabin, the explicit answer of lamma is not mentioned. This omission keeps the sentence suspended and brings the call directly into the scene.

One should not reconstruct the omitted answer with certainty. One may suppose that something like “We called” or “the matter reached the final stage” is implicit in the structure, but the text does not state it explicitly.

The verse contains two different structures: aslama, with two subjects; and tallahu, with one subject and one object. This shift shows that acceptance is shared, while the bodily execution is carried out by Ibrahim.

Nearby Roots

T L L and S R AYN (sar'): Sar' indicates throwing to the ground. Tallahu, beyond overthrowing, highlights the body's placement upon the jabin or side of the face.

T L L and K B B (kabb): Kabb is throwing face-down. Tallahu more clearly preserves the action of taking and placing in a fallen position.

T L L and S Q T (suqut): Suqut is falling downward. Tallahu is transitive casting down by another.

T L L and AYN TH R ('athar): 'Ithar is stumbling of the foot. Tallahu is not stumbling; it is a deliberate motion of one doer upon another body.

T L L and T AYN S (ta's): Ta's is a person's own downfall and failure. Tallahu is casting another down.

T L L and D J AYN (idja'): Idja' is laying on the side. Tallahu carries more of the force of bringing down and restraining the body.

T L L and DH B H (dhabḥ): Dhabḥ is cutting the throat. Tallahu is preparation and placing of the body.

T L L and Q T L (qatl): Qatl is ending life. Tallahu is only changing the body's position.

T L L and S J D (sujud): Sujud is voluntary descent. Tallahu is a transitive act upon another body.

T L L and S L M (silm): S L M in aslama indicates willing acceptance and surrender. T L L indicates the bodily motion after that.

Translation Suggestions for Forqan

For تَلَّ: cast down; overthrew; threw face-down; took and placed on the ground.

For تَلَّهُ: he cast him to the ground; he overthrew him; he placed him face-down on the ground; he took him and placed him in a fallen position.

For تَلَّهُ لِلْجَبِينِ: he cast him down upon the side of the forehead; he placed him upon the jabin on the ground; he cast him face-down, upon the side of the face, to the ground; he placed him on the ground so that his jabin was on the ground.

For فَلَمَّا أَسْلَمَا وَتَلَّهُ لِلْجَبِينِ, suggested standard translation:

فَلَمَّا أَسْلَمَا وَتَلَّهُ لِلْجَبِينِ

So when they both submitted, and he cast him down upon the side of the forehead...

A clearer translation:

So when they both submitted, and Ibrahim cast the son down upon the side of the forehead...

The ellipsis can preserve the suspended structure of the original, because the sentence shifts at this point into the following call.

Summary

The root T L L indicates taking someone, casting him to the ground, and placing his body in a fallen and restrained position.

In the Qur'an, this root appears only in the story of Ibrahim and his son. First, both aslama; that is, both shared in acceptance. Then Ibrahim tallahu lil-jabin; that is, he cast the son down upon the side of the forehead.

The verse reaches its peak at this point. The explicit answer of lamma is omitted, and before any other verb about cutting or death is stated, the call arrives. Therefore, tallahu is the last bodily motion actually carried out upon the son and the boundary between full readiness and the stopping of the act.

Tallahu does not mean dhabḥ or qatl. The Qur'an uses the word dhabḥ in the statement of the vision and in the substitute sacrifice, but regarding the son it does not say dhabahahu. This difference shows that the body was placed on the ground for the act, but the human slaughter did not occur.

T L L is close to sar', kabb, falling, stumbling, ta's, laying on the side, dhabḥ, and sujud, but it is not identical with any of them.

The special core of T L L is taking and casting a person down, and placing him in a fallen position, especially upon the side of the face or forehead.

Suggested standard translation for this entry: “So when they both submitted, and he cast him down upon the side of the forehead...”
