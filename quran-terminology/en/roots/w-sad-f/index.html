Root w-ṣ-f — Description, characteristics, and claims about people and things

Qur’anic and lexical forms

(yaṣifūn)
They describe; imperfect

تَصِفُونَ
(taṣifūn)
You describe; imperfect

تَصِفُ
(taṣifu)
It describes; imperfect

(waṣfahum)
Their description; Qur’anic noun

(ṣifah)

The Arabic root w-ṣ-f concerns expressing the characteristics, qualities, and condition of a person, thing, or situation. To describe something is to speak about it so that a listener can recognize its features, condition, or standing.

A description may be accurate or inaccurate. Someone may describe something as it is, or attribute to it a quality it does not possess.

In the Qur’an, this root occurs chiefly where people make false claims about others or God. Joseph’s brothers make allegations about him; some people invent rulings on what is lawful and forbidden; others attribute qualities and relationships to God that contradict His oneness.

Nevertheless, the falsity of these descriptions does not come from the root w-ṣ-f itself. Description is the act of expressing a characteristic or making an attribution; its truth or falsity is a separate question.

This distinction matters when reading the Qur’an, particularly where rulings are attributed to God or His reality is described.

1. The root in early Arabic

In early Arabic, وَصَفَ الشَّيْءَ meant to state something’s characteristics. A person describes what they know, have seen, or have heard about, making it known to another.

Lexicons explain waṣf through naʿt and ḥilyah. Here ḥilyah does not mean merely ornament or adornment, but the features by which a person or thing is recognized.

For example, a description of a horse might mention its color, build, speed, and movement.

A description of a person might mention appearance, conduct, character, or position.

In either case, description introduces the subject to the listener.

A poetic witness attributed to Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd reads:

إِنِّي كَفَانِي مِنْ أَمْرٍ هَمَمْتُ بِهِ

innī kafānī min amrin hamamtu bihī

جَارٌ كَجَارِ الْحُذَاقِيِّ الَّذِي اتَّصَفَا

jārun ka-jāri al-Ḥudhāqiyyi alladhī ittaṣafā

“A neighbor relieved me of a matter I had intended to undertake—a neighbor like the neighbor of al-Ḥudhāqī, renowned for being a good neighbor.”

Here اتَّصَفَا concerns someone characterized by a recognized quality.

The lexical explanation understands this as a reputation for good neighborliness.

Description here therefore identifies a person through one of his qualities.

Description need not concern physical appearance. Good neighborliness, courage, and generosity can also be described.

The verse is attributed to Ṭarafah in lexicographical sources. That attribution alone does not establish an undisputed date or textual transmission; those questions require separate examination.

Description through conduct and movement

A poem attributed to al-Shammākh ibn Ḍirār, who lived across the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods, reads:

إِذَا مَا أَدْلَجَتْ وَصَفَتْ يَدَاهَا

idhā mā adlajat waṣafat yadāhā

لَهَا الْإِدْلَاجَ لَيْلَةَ لَا هُجُوعِ

lahā al-idlāja laylata lā hujūʿi

“When she traveled by night, her forelegs displayed her night journey through a sleepless night.”

The verb وَصَفَتْ is attributed to the mount’s forelegs.

An animal’s forelegs do not speak, yet their movement can reveal the character of its gait.

The poet uses this image, applying “describe” to the manifestation of a characteristic through movement.

The usage shows that description in Arabic was not confined to verbal reporting. Something’s character could also become evident through its conduct or visible signs.

This verse belongs to a poetic tradition close to early Islam; without dating its composition, it cannot be presented as conclusively pre-Qur’anic.

What the historical evidence supports

Early witnesses and recorded Arabic usage support understanding waṣf as making a person or thing known through its characteristics.

These may concern appearance, conduct, ethics, condition, or standing.

Waṣf can also express a claim about someone or something, even when that claim does not match reality.

A defensible account of the root’s historical meaning is therefore:

Expressing or revealing a characteristic, condition, or attribution concerning a person, thing, or subject, so that an audience forms a conception of it.

This definition alone does not determine whether the description is true, false, commendable, or objectionable.

2. Forms and semantic core

Important Arabic forms in this family include:

وَصَفَ: he expressed something’s character or condition; he characterized it by a quality.

يَصِفُ: he describes or attributes a characteristic.

وَصْف: the act of description, or a statement describing something.

صِفَة: a quality or characteristic attributed to someone or something.

وَاصِف: someone who describes something.

مَوْصُوف: the person or thing whose characteristics are described.

وَصَّاف: someone who describes frequently or is skilled in description.

تَوَاصَفُوا: they described something to one another.

اسْتَوْصَفَ: he asked another to describe something to him.

اتَّصَفَ: he was known for, or characterized by, a quality.

تَوْصِيف: stating and specifying characteristics; in later usage, also systematic or specialized description.

These forms are known in Arabic, but not all occur in the Qur’an.

Lexicons also record وَصِيف and وَصِيفَة for young servants, including enslaved boys and girls in historical usage. وَصَفَ الْمُهْرُ is also recorded of a foal whose gait has become good.

These specialized uses should neither be imposed on the basic meaning of description nor declared the certain origin of every branch without historical investigation.

Qur’anic forms

The Qur’an uses two principal structures from this root:

The basic verb in يَصِفُونَ, تَصِفُونَ, and تَصِفُ.

The noun وَصْف, appearing as وَصْفَهُمْ.

All Qur’anic verbal occurrences are imperfect forms.

يَصِفُونَ refers to a third-person plural subject: they describe.

تَصِفُونَ addresses a plural audience: you describe.

تَصِفُ appears with أَلْسِنَة in two verses: tongues describe.

وَصْفَهُمْ combines the noun with the pronoun hum: their description, or the describing they have done.

The forms صِفَة, وَاصِف, مَوْصُوف, and تَوْصِيف do not occur in the Qur’an.

Semantic core

The family’s core meaning is:

Expressing a characteristic, condition, or attribution concerning someone or something, making it known through speech or a sign that reveals that characteristic.

In Qur’anic usage, the verb chiefly concerns statements and the attribution of claims.

Such a claim may concern a person, a ruling, or God.

The object of description therefore matters.

Describing conduct is not identical to describing a person.

Describing a fact is also different from inventing a ruling in God’s name.

The root remains the same, while the content of the description and the speaker’s responsibility emerge from context.

3. Description and false claims in Joseph’s story

Yūsuf 12:18 — A description Jacob rejects

وَجَاءُوا عَلَىٰ قَمِيصِهِ بِدَمٍ كَذِبٍ قَالَ بَلْ سَوَّلَتْ لَكُمْ أَنْفُسُكُمْ أَمْرًا فَصَبْرٌ جَمِيلٌ وَاللَّهُ الْمُسْتَعَانُ عَلَىٰ مَا تَصِفُونَ

wa-jāʾū ʿalā qamīṣihi bi-damin kadhibin qāla bal sawwalat lakum anfusukum amran fa-ṣabrun jamīlun wa-Allāhu al-mustaʿānu ʿalā mā taṣifūn

Yūsuf 12:18

“They brought false blood upon his shirt. He said: Rather, your souls have made a matter seem attractive to you. So, beautiful patience; and The God is the One whose help is sought against what you describe.”

Jacob addresses his sons with تَصِفُونَ in this verse.

They had claimed in Yūsuf 12:17 that a wolf had eaten Joseph.

To support their account, they brought his shirt with blood to their father.

Jacob rejects their narrative, saying that their souls have made a matter seem attractive to them.

He then speaks of beautiful patience and God’s help.

Why does taṣifūn not simply mean “you lie”?

Joseph’s brothers are speaking falsely in this account.

But the root w-ṣ-f itself does not mean lying.

They have described an event to their father. Their claim about Joseph’s fate does not correspond to the truth.

The account and its evidence establish the description’s falsity; the verb alone does not.

Rendering taṣifūn only as “you lie” may convey the situation, but loses the distinction between describing and the description being false.

“What you describe” stays closer to the Arabic wording.

An explanation can clarify that the reference is to the brothers’ false account of Joseph’s fate.

The verse also shows how a visible sign may be used to make a false description seem credible.

The existence of a sign alone does not prove every part of a narrative constructed around it.

Yūsuf 12:77 — An allegation of theft

In Yūsuf 12:77, Joseph’s brothers say of their younger brother that if he has stolen, a brother of his had stolen before him.

In a recognized reading, Joseph keeps his response within himself rather than revealing it to them; other explanations of the pronoun hā are also recorded.

On this reading, the statement that God knows best what they describe belongs to that inward response; it should not be presented unequivocally as something spoken aloud to them.

The form تَصِفُونَ occurs here too.

The description concerns the brothers’ allegation of theft.

They attribute conduct to Joseph and his brother that the narrative presents as an unjust allegation.

This usage shows that waṣf may attribute an action to a person.

For example, “so-and-so stole” does more than describe an outward feature: it attributes an act to someone.

Such an attribution may be true or false.

The reader of Joseph’s story already knows something of the brothers’ previous behavior.

Description can therefore carry serious moral responsibility, especially where another’s reputation and rights are involved.

These passages do not imply that every critical description of conduct is wrong. The criteria are the claim’s truth and supporting evidence.

4. False description and inventing rulings in God’s name

Al-Anʿām 6:139 — Waṣfahum and responsibility for their claims

وَقَالُوا مَا فِي بُطُونِ هَٰذِهِ الْأَنْعَامِ خَالِصَةٌ لِذُكُورِنَا وَمُحَرَّمٌ عَلَىٰ أَزْوَاجِنَا وَإِنْ يَكُنْ مَيْتَةً فَهُمْ فِيهِ شُرَكَاءُ سَيَجْزِيهِمْ وَصْفَهُمْ إِنَّهُ حَكِيمٌ عَلِيمٌ

wa-qālū mā fī buṭūni hādhihi al-anʿāmi khāliṣatun li-dhukūrinā wa-muḥarramun ʿalā azwājinā wa-in yakun maytatan fa-hum fīhi shurakāʾu sa-yajzīhim waṣfahum innahu ḥakīmun ʿalīm

Al-Anʿām 6:139

“They said: What is in the bellies of these livestock is exclusively for our males and forbidden to our women; but if it is dead, they share in it. He will repay them for their description. Indeed, He is Wise, Knowing.”

وَصْفَهُمْ is the root’s only nominal occurrence in the Qur’an.

Why are they held accountable for their description?

In this very verse, they declare things lawful or forbidden for men and women.

Al-Anʿām 6:138 likewise mentions restrictions they have invented concerning livestock and crops.

Al-Anʿām 6:140 condemns such behavior as fabricating lies about God and forbidding the provision He has given.

Their “description” in Al-Anʿām 6:139 is therefore not neutral reporting: through it they have instituted rules and restrictions in the name of religion.

How does description become a claim to legislate?

Consider the difference between these statements:

“This food is unsuitable for some people.”

“God has forbidden this food to that group.”

The first statement may rest on experience, knowledge, or assessment of people’s circumstances.

The second attributes a ruling to God.

Personal taste, habit, imagination, or family custom is not sufficient grounds for that attribution.

In Al-Anʿām 6:139, people have differentiated between men’s and women’s entitlement to particular food.

The Qur’an’s criticism concerns more than stating the food’s characteristics: it addresses invented restrictions lacking divine authorization.

Waṣfahum here refers to their speech and claim, one with legal and social consequences.

Al-Naḥl 16:62 — Tongues describing falsehood

وَيَجْعَلُونَ لِلَّهِ مَا يَكْرَهُونَ وَتَصِفُ أَلْسِنَتُهُمُ الْكَذِبَ أَنَّ لَهُمُ الْحُسْنَىٰ لَا جَرَمَ أَنَّ لَهُمُ النَّارَ وَأَنَّهُمْ مُفْرَطُونَ

wa-yajʿalūna li-Allāhi mā yakrahūna wa-taṣifu alsinatuhumu al-kadhiba anna lahumu al-ḥusnā lā jarama anna lahumu al-nāra wa-annahum mufraṭūn

Al-Naḥl 16:62

“They assign to The God what they themselves dislike, and their tongues falsely claim that the best outcome is theirs. Certainly, the Fire is theirs, and they are sent ahead to it.”

تَصِفُ is a third-person feminine singular imperfect verb here.

Its subject is أَلْسِنَتُهُمُ: their tongues.

Grammatically, alsinah is a nonhuman plural, which can take feminine singular verbal agreement.

In the familiar grammatical analysis, الْكَذِبَ is the object of taṣifu.

The clause أَنَّ لَهُمُ الْحُسْنَىٰ states the content of their claim. The translation of مُفْرَطُونَ above follows the explanation “sent ahead”; “abandoned” is another recorded explanation and is not settled by the meaning of w-ṣ-f.

Why are tongues the subjects of description?

People speak, but language sometimes attributes speech to their tongues.

This expression directs attention to the utterance or claim passing their lips.

The tongues are not being assigned beliefs or moral responsibility independently of their owners.

The speakers are the same people whose behavior is mentioned at the verse’s beginning.

They attribute to God what they dislike for themselves, yet claim the best outcome for themselves.

This inconsistency exposes their mistaken judgment.

Description here is a spoken claim that does not correspond to reality.

Al-Naḥl 16:116 — Attributing lawful and forbidden rulings to God

وَلَا تَقُولُوا لِمَا تَصِفُ أَلْسِنَتُكُمُ الْكَذِبَ هَٰذَا حَلَالٌ وَهَٰذَا حَرَامٌ لِتَفْتَرُوا عَلَى اللَّهِ الْكَذِبَ إِنَّ الَّذِينَ يَفْتَرُونَ عَلَى اللَّهِ الْكَذِبَ لَا يُفْلِحُونَ

wa-lā taqūlū li-mā taṣifu alsinatukumu al-kadhiba hādhā ḥalālun wa-hādhā ḥarāmun li-taftarū ʿalā Allāhi al-kadhiba inna alladhīna yaftarūna ʿalā Allāhi al-kadhiba lā yufliḥūn

Al-Naḥl 16:116

“Do not say, on the basis of the falsehood your tongues describe, ‘This is lawful and this is forbidden,’ so as to fabricate lies against The God. Those who fabricate lies against The God will not prosper.”

This is one of the clearest uses of w-ṣ-f concerning speech’s religious and social responsibility.

تَصِفُ is imperfect, and أَلْسِنَتُكُمُ is its subject.

More than one grammatical analysis has been offered for لِمَا تَصِفُ أَلْسِنَتُكُمُ الْكَذِبَ.

In one analysis, mā introduces a verbal-noun construction, concerning the tongues’ false description.

In another, mā is relative, referring to things the tongues falsely describe.

Different analyses also concern al-kadhiba: it may be the object of taṣifu or be connected with taqūlū.

Despite these differences, the main point is clear: one must not declare something lawful or forbidden by God without warrant.

Description and fabrication are distinct

Two important word families occur together in this verse.

تَصِفُ belongs to w-ṣ-f.

تَفْتَرُوا belongs to f-r-y.

Description expresses a characteristic or makes an attribution.

Fabrication in this context invents a false statement and attributes it to God.

The warning thus concerns a description that becomes fabrication against God, not description in the abstract.

Translation should preserve this distinction.

A person may hold an opinion about the usefulness or harm of a food, behavior, or way of life.

When that opinion is proclaimed without warrant as God’s definitive ruling, it goes beyond personal judgment.

Someone might say, for example, that using something harms their own health.

That is different from saying, “God has forbidden it to everyone.”

The latter requires divine authority and evidence.

The responsibility of religious influence

This issue extends beyond food.

Anyone imposing duties, prohibitions, or penalties on people in God’s name must be able to support the claim with valid evidence.

Within Forqan’s Qur’an-based method, religious position, a widely known fatwa, inherited custom, or repetition of a report cannot by itself replace a clear divine command when a ruling cannot be established from the Qur’an. This is the study’s methodological application, not the lexical definition of waṣf.

A personal interpretation of a verse is nevertheless different from knowingly fabricating a lie about God.

A person may misunderstand a subject.

Someone who knows there is no evidence yet presents a statement as God’s definitive judgment bears greater responsibility.

This particularly matters for people whose words affect others’ lives, property, freedom, and rights.

In Al-Naḥl 16:116, the central danger is presenting a humanly invented claim to people in God’s name.

5. False descriptions of God

Many Qur’anic occurrences concern claims people attribute to God.

These passages include expressions such as عَمَّا يَصِفُونَ.

Does this mean people must never speak about or describe God?

The passages themselves must guide the answer.

Al-Anʿām 6:100 — Attributing partners and children

وَجَعَلُوا لِلَّهِ شُرَكَاءَ الْجِنَّ وَخَلَقَهُمْ وَخَرَقُوا لَهُ بَنِينَ وَبَنَاتٍ بِغَيْرِ عِلْمٍ سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَىٰ عَمَّا يَصِفُونَ

wa-jaʿalū li-Allāhi shurakāʾa al-jinna wa-khalaqahum wa-kharaqū lahu banīna wa-banātin bi-ghayri ʿilmin subḥānahu wa-taʿālā ʿammā yaṣifūn

Al-Anʿām 6:100

“They assigned the jinn as partners to The God, although He created them, and invented sons and daughters for Him without knowledge. Glorified is He, and exalted above what they describe.”

The content of the false description is explicit.

People have assigned partners and children to God.

The Qur’an rejects these claims.

Here ʿammā yaṣifūn refers to descriptions incompatible with God’s reality.

The issue is not a prohibition of every description of God, but attributing characteristics that the Qur’an identifies as false.

Al-Anʿām 6:101 also challenges the claim that God has children by recalling His creative power.

The rejection rests on the knowledge of God presented by the Qur’an.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:22 — A description incompatible with God’s oneness

لَوْ كَانَ فِيهِمَا آلِهَةٌ إِلَّا اللَّهُ لَفَسَدَتَا فَسُبْحَانَ اللَّهِ رَبِّ الْعَرْشِ عَمَّا يَصِفُونَ

law kāna fīhimā ālihatun illā Allāhu la-fasadatā fa-subḥāna Allāhi rabbi al-ʿarshi ʿammā yaṣifūn

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:22

“Had there been gods in the two besides The God, both would have fallen into ruin. Glorified is The God, Lord of the Throne, above what they describe.”

The verse presents an argument about God’s oneness.

It then declares Him beyond descriptions incompatible with that oneness.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:91 likewise rejects attributing a child to God or placing another god alongside Him.

It explains that, were this so, each god would take away what it had created, and some would seek ascendancy over others.

God is then declared beyond what they describe.

In both passages, the claim’s content and the Qur’anic argument establish the description’s falsity.

The verb yaṣifūn itself does not mean shirk or kufr.

Attributing a partner or child to God, however, makes the description false in that context.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:159 — The exception concerning purified servants

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:159 states:

سُبْحَانَ اللَّهِ عَمَّا يَصِفُونَ

subḥāna Allāhi ʿammā yaṣifūn

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:159

“Glorified is The God above what they describe.”

Immediately afterward, Al-Ṣāffāt 37:160 states:

إِلَّا عِبَادَ اللَّهِ الْمُخْلَصِينَ

illā ʿibāda Allāhi al-mukhlaṣīn

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:160

“Except the purified servants of The God.”

The sequence matters.

If the exception is connected with yaṣifūn, it distinguishes the purified servants’ description from false descriptions. Since the exception has other grammatical analyses, this connection alone cannot establish a definitive conclusion.

The exception has also been connected with those brought forth for punishment in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:158. Its interpretation should therefore be distinguished from the wider argument about describing God.

These passages do not justify treating every statement about God’s characteristics as forbidden or false.

The Qur’an itself describes God as Knowing, Wise, Merciful, and Powerful.

A description grounded in divine guidance must therefore be distinguished from one invented without knowledge.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:180 — The Lord of might is beyond their descriptions

سُبْحَانَ رَبِّكَ رَبِّ الْعِزَّةِ عَمَّا يَصِفُونَ

subḥāna rabbika rabbi al-ʿizzati ʿammā yaṣifūn

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:180

“Glorified is your Lord, the Lord of might, above what they describe.”

Here too, God is declared beyond false descriptions.

The surah then speaks of peace upon the Messengers and praise of the Lord of all worlds.

This sequence shows that speaking about God is not itself being rejected.

The Qur’an itself calls Him رَبِّ الْعِزَّةِ and رَبِّ الْعَالَمِينَ.

What matters is the description’s truth, not merely the use of an attribute.

Al-Zukhruf 43:82 likewise identifies God as Lord of the heavens, earth, and Throne, beyond false descriptions.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:18 and Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:112 — Human claims and divine judgment

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:18 describes truth overcoming falsehood and warns the audience of woe for what they describe.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:112 asks the Lord to judge in truth and identifies God as the One whose help is sought against the opponents’ descriptions.

Both concern truth, falsehood, and judgment of human speech.

But the descriptions in different verses should not all be reduced to one historical claim.

Each passage addresses a statement or allegation in its own context.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:96 — Responding well to false description

In Al-Muʾminūn 23:96, Muhammad is commanded to repel evil with what is better.

God is then said to know best what the opponents describe.

This shows that responding to others’ false descriptions and claims is also a subject of Qur’anic guidance.

Someone may make an unjust statement about another.

The response need not imitate that wrongdoing.

This context commands a better response and recalls God’s knowledge of their words.

The sentence as a whole conveys this; the root waṣf itself does not mean insult or abuse.

6. Description, lying, fabrication, and naming

W-ṣ-f (waṣf) compared with n-ʿ-t (naʿt)

Naʿt concerns stating something’s characteristics.

In this respect it is very close to waṣf.

Lexicons sometimes use them to explain each other.

Their proximity does not make every morphological or technical form in the two families identical.

Arabic grammar uses ṣifah and naʿt to explain the role of certain words in a sentence.

This technical use relates to description’s broader Qur’anic meaning, but should not replace it as the primary definition in the verses.

W-ṣ-f (waṣf) compared with q-w-l (qawl)

Qawl concerns saying and speech.

Waṣf concerns stating a characteristic or attribution.

Every verbal description can take the form of speech, but not every utterance is a description.

“Come,” for example, is a command.

“This person is knowledgeable” describes someone.

Al-Naḥl 16:116 uses both verbs: saying and describing.

Their combination distinguishes speaking from its descriptive content.

W-ṣ-f (waṣf) compared with k-dh-b (kadhib)

Kadhib concerns false speech or a statement contrary to truth.

Waṣf is the act of describing or attributing a characteristic.

They can occur together, as in Al-Naḥl 16:62 and Al-Naḥl 16:116.

Their combination does not make them identical.

A person may offer a true description or construct a false one.

Waṣf should therefore not always be translated as “lying.”

W-ṣ-f (waṣf) compared with f-r-y (iftirāʾ)

In speech, iftirāʾ concerns inventing a false claim and attributing it to another.

Fabrication against God is a serious Qur’anic concern.

Al-Naḥl 16:116 places false description alongside fabrication against God.

Description can convey a fabrication, but not every description is fabricated.

An inaccurate description alone is also insufficient to accuse someone of knowingly inventing a lie. Their knowledge, awareness, and conduct require examination.

This distinction matters when judging people.

W-ṣ-f (waṣf) compared with s-m-y (tasmiyah)

Naming assigns a name to someone or something.

Description may express its characteristics.

A being can be known by a name while also being described through certain qualities.

Al-Najm 53:23 discusses names people and their ancestors assigned to invented deities without God sending down any authority for them.

This relates in one respect to false descriptions of God.

Naming and describing nevertheless differ lexically.

Someone may invent an unsupported name or attribute an unsupported quality.

Both call for scrutiny of truth and evidence, but their linguistic forms differ.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

“Describe,” “characterize,” and “attribute” suit most Qur’anic occurrences. These proposals do not replace existing verse word boxes.

The final choice must fit the verb’s object and context.

Yūsuf 12:18 and Yūsuf 12:77

“What you describe.”

Retaining description suits both passages, since they concern claims about Joseph.

Al-Anʿām 6:139

“He will repay them for their description.”

An explanation can identify the false claims and rules they invented about food and people’s rights.

Al-Naḥl 16:62

“Their tongues falsely claim that the best outcome is theirs.”

“Claim” suits the context because they attribute a favorable outcome to themselves.

Al-Naḥl 16:116

“Do not say, on the basis of the falsehood your tongues describe, ‘This is lawful and this is forbidden.’”

The distinction between description, falsehood, and attributing a ruling to God must be preserved.

Al-Anʿām 6:100, Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:22, Al-Muʾminūn 23:91, Al-Ṣāffāt 37:159, Al-Ṣāffāt 37:180, and Al-Zukhruf 43:82

“He is glorified above what they describe.”

This conveys the shared sense, but each verse’s translation must retain its actual subject and titles, such as “your Lord” or “Lord of the Throne.”

Each verse’s explanation should clarify which quality or relationship is being attributed to God.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:18, Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:112, and Al-Muʾminūn 23:96

“What you describe” or “what they describe.”

Here too, the statement’s content must be identified from its context.

Non-Qur’anic forms likewise require equivalents appropriate to their use:

وَصَفَ: he described, characterized, or expressed a feature.

صِفَة: a feature or characteristic.

وَاصِف: one who describes.

مَوْصُوف: someone or something described.

تَوَاصَفُوا: they described to one another.

اسْتَوْصَفَ: he requested a description.

اتَّصَفَ: he was known for, or possessed, a characteristic.

A shared root does not make every derivative’s grammatical role and function identical.

8. Conclusion

The Arabic root w-ṣ-f concerns expressing characteristics, conditions, and attributions relating to people and things.

Poetry attributed to pre-Islamic times and the period near revelation illustrates physical, behavioral, and ethical description. Even an animal’s movement can reveal its character in poetic imagery.

The root occurs fourteen times in the Qur’an: thirteen as the basic verb and once as the noun waṣf.

Its Qur’anic uses chiefly concern false human claims.

Joseph’s brothers present false narratives and allegations about him.

In Al-Anʿām 6:139, people use description and claims to impose restrictions on food and people’s rights.

Al-Naḥl 16:116 warns against attributing lawful and forbidden rulings to God without warrant.

Other verses address claims about God incompatible with His oneness and reality.

None of this makes waṣf inherently mean lying or fabrication.

The resulting definition is: expressing or revealing a characteristic, condition, or attribution concerning a person, thing, or subject, so that an audience acquires knowledge or a conception of it. Its truth or falsity depends on its content and relevant evidence, not on the root itself.

In Qur’anic usage, description can also make a claim about human conduct, a religious ruling, or God’s reality.

The more consequential the subject and its effects, the greater the speaker’s responsibility.

A false statement about someone can damage their reputation and rights.

An unsupported claim about what God permits or forbids can restrict many people’s freedom and lives.

Attributing characteristics to God without knowledge can convey a false conception of Him to others.

Forqan’s translations should therefore preserve the distinction between description, its truth or falsity, and responsibility for attributing it to others or God.

Description conveys knowledge; when an unsupported claim is presented as truth, however, it can become an instrument of deception, fabrication, and invented rulings.
