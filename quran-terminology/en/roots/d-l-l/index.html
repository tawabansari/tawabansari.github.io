Qur’anic Root ḍ l l

Losing the way, leaving the path of truth, wandering, moving away from guidance, and misleading others

Forms covered from this root

This page studies the Qur’anic root ḍ l l and then explains the most important Qur’anic forms connected to this field of meaning:

  • ḍalla / yaḍillu / taḍillu / ḍallū — Form I verb; to lose the way, go astray, fall out of the right path
  • aḍalla / yuḍillu / yuḍillūna / aḍallū — Form IV verb; to mislead, lead away, cause others to leave the path
  • aḍall — comparative form; more astray, farther from the right path
  • ḍalāl — noun/verbal noun; error, wandering, being away from guidance
  • ḍalālah — noun/verbal noun; misguidance, lostness, a state of being away from guidance
  • ḍāll / ḍāllūn / ḍāllīn — active participle; astray, those who are off the path
  • taḍlīl — Form II verbal noun; misleading, making the path corrupt or lost
  • muḍill / muḍillūn — Form IV active participle; one who misleads, misleaders

Note: forms such as passive Form I or passive Form IV may be morphologically possible in Arabic, but they are not treated here as the central direct Qur’anic forms of this root. The focus is on the Qur’anic forms that shape the vocabulary field of ḍ l l.

General meaning of the root ḍ l l

The Qur’anic root ḍ l l points to losing the way, falling out of the path of truth, wandering without right direction, moving away from guidance, and leading others into misguidance.

This root should not be reduced to simple lack of information. A person may be astray because he does not know, but he may also go astray after signs, reminders, and evidence have already reached him.

In the Qur’an, misguidance can be intellectual, moral, spiritual, or social. It is not only a missing fact; it is a lost direction.

Ḍalāl: more than not knowing

One common mistake is to read ḍalāl as mere ignorance. Ignorance can be part of misguidance, but it does not exhaust the meaning.

Some people do not know and need teaching, patience, and guidance. Others receive signs and arguments, yet remain attached to pride, inherited assumptions, group loyalty, or desire.

Misguidance therefore can begin as not knowing, but it becomes heavier when the person refuses to return after the way becomes clearer.

Ḍalla / yaḍillu: losing the way

Ḍalla and yaḍillu refer to losing the way or falling out of the right path. This can occur in belief, ethics, action, or communal direction.

A person may be confident and still be walking in the wrong direction. Confidence does not prove guidance. The real question is whether the path stands with truth, justice, humility, and accountability before God.

This root warns that a human being can lose not only information, but orientation.

Aḍalla / yuḍillu: misleading others

The Form IV branch, such as aḍalla and yuḍillu, points to misleading others. This is heavier than being personally astray, because the person becomes a cause of misguidance for others.

Misleading can happen through lies, distortion, fear, power, religious abuse, false promises, or turning conjecture into certainty.

The most dangerous form of misguidance is often the one that comes dressed as guidance.

Ḍāll / ḍāllūn / ḍāllīn: those who are astray

Ḍāll and its plural forms refer to those who are in a state of misguidance: those who have lost the path, moved away from it, or remained in wandering.

These terms should not become tools of arrogance or contempt. The Qur’an gives moral criteria, not a license for self-righteous labeling.

Before calling others astray, a person must ask whether he himself remains open to evidence, correction, and truth.

Al-ḍāllīn: not merely the uninformed

The expression al-ḍāllīn in Sūrat al-Fātiḥah is one of the most important uses of this root. The seeker asks not to be placed on the path of those who have gone astray.

This does not mean only those who lack religious information. Misguidance can arise from conjecture, desire, inherited bias, heedlessness, or abandoning guidance after the path has become clear.

The phrase is therefore first a mirror for self-watchfulness: am I seeking the path, or only defending what I already believe?

Ḍalāl and ḍalālah: the state of misguidance

Ḍalāl and ḍalālah describe misguidance, wandering, and being away from guidance. This state can be individual or collective.

A society can be lost as a society when it normalizes injustice, replaces knowledge with conjecture, and turns religion into an instrument of power.

Misguidance is not always the absence of a map. Sometimes it is the possession of a false map.

Aḍall: farther astray

Aḍall means more astray or farther from the right path. This form shows that misguidance has degrees.

The one who makes a mistake unknowingly is not the same as the one who knowingly distorts truth. The one who is confused is not the same as the one who builds a system to mislead others.

The word invites careful moral measurement rather than shallow labeling.

Taḍlīl: active misguidance

Taḍlīl refers to misleading, making the way lost, or corrupting the path. It goes beyond personal confusion because it affects the path of others.

Taḍlīl can be done through attractive speech, distorted interpretation, fabricated authority, social pressure, or false religious certainty.

This is a grave responsibility: whoever misleads others carries more than a private error.

Muḍill and muḍillūn: misleaders

Muḍill refers to one who misleads. The misleader can be a person, a devilish influence, a system of power, blind tradition, or any force that takes people away from truth.

A misleader is not always visibly hostile. Sometimes he appears as a protector, scholar, ruler, preacher, or loyal member of the group.

The standard is the direction of the path: does it bring people nearer to truth, justice, tawḥīd, and humility, or farther away?

Misguidance, conjecture, and blind following

One root of misguidance is following conjecture instead of knowledge. When a person accepts something without evidence and then becomes loyal to it, he becomes vulnerable to misguidance.

Blind following of ancestors, leaders, sects, media, rulers, or inherited systems can move a person away from the path of truth.

Learning from others is not the problem. The problem is refusing to test inherited claims against truth, evidence, and Qur’anic justice.

Misguidance and desire

Misguidance is not only a mistake in thought. It can come from desire. A person may understand the truth but reject it because it conflicts with power, status, habit, or pleasure.

Desire can enslave the intellect and turn reasoning into justification. The person no longer searches for truth; he searches for arguments to defend what he wants.

For this reason, guidance requires purity of intention, not information alone.

Misguidance after guidance

One of the gravest forms of misguidance is going astray after guidance has become clear. A person may receive signs, knowledge, and opportunity, yet turn away through pride or ingratitude.

This form is heavier because the person was not merely in darkness; he saw light and then moved away from it.

No community is safe merely because it carries a religious name. Guidance must be preserved through humility, action, and continued openness to truth.

The responsibility of guides

Those who teach, interpret, lead, preach, or hold religious authority carry a serious burden. If they guide rightly, they may become a means of good. If they distort the way, they become part of misguidance.

The danger of a misguided guide is greater than that of a private wanderer, because he makes the wrong path appear legitimate to others.

Every claim made in the name of God, the Qur’an, religion, or truth must therefore be carried with caution, humility, and fear of distortion.

Misguidance and the possibility of return

Despite the danger of misguidance, the Qur’an keeps the door of return open. A person who realizes he has lost the way still has hope.

The real danger is not merely being lost; it is insisting that the wrong path is the right path and leaving no room for correction.

Recognizing misguidance can become the beginning of guidance, if the person turns back sincerely.

Summary

The root ḍ l l calls attention to losing the way, wandering, leaving the path of truth, and misleading others. It cannot be reduced to simple ignorance.

Ḍalla and yaḍillu speak of going astray. Aḍalla and yuḍillu speak of misleading others. Ḍalāl and ḍalālah describe the state of misguidance. Ḍāll and ḍāllīn describe those who are astray. Taḍlīl refers to active misguidance, and muḍill to one who misleads.

If this root is understood carefully, the reader does not rush to label others. He first watches himself: am I open to truth? Am I willing to leave conjecture, bias, and desire? Could my words or actions lead others away from the path?
