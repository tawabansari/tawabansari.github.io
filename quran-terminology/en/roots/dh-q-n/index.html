Chin: the front of the lower jaw, with descriptive uses concerning falling, resting and the position of the head

The Arabic root DH Q N is chiefly associated with a specific body part: dhaqan, the chin, the lower facial region where the two sides of the lower jaw meet. Other Arabic uses extend this name to the position of the head and neck, resting on the chin and even the configuration of certain objects.

The root occurs three times in the Qur’an, only as the plural adhqān. Al-Isrāʾ 17:107 and Al-Isrāʾ 17:109 concern people falling in prostration upon hearing the Qur’an; Yā Sīn 36:8 depicts shackles reaching the chins and holding the heads raised.

The word’s meaning is shared across these occurrences, but the situations differ: falling and humility in one, raised heads and restricted movement in the other. Neither humility nor defiance is part of dhaqan’s lexical meaning. Those ideas arise from other words and the verses’ contexts.

1. Central meaning and usage in early Arabic

Dhaqan: the lower part of the face

Lexical analysis treats the chin as the central meaning to which other derivatives return: the lower, front part of the face where the two sides of the lower jaw meet.

One early definition states:

الذَّقَنُ مُجْتَمَعُ اللَّحْيَيْنِ

al-dhaqanu mujtamaʿu al-laḥyayn

That is: the chin is where the two sides of the lower jaw meet.

Laḥyayn here denotes the two sides of the lower jaw, rather than the upper and lower jaws. Dhaqan should therefore not be equated with the entire beard. A beard may cover the chin, but the chin names the bodily part underneath.

Both dhaqan and dhiqn are recorded; their recognized plural is adhqān.

The word describes humans and animals. It generally concerns the lower, front part of the face, whose position matters when lowering, raising or supporting the head.

This bodily meaning remains in Qur’anic usage. The verses differ in people’s actions and positions, rather than the chin acquiring a new lexical meaning in each verse.

A pre-Islamic poetic witness: chins describing trees

In his famous Muʿallaqah, the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays describes powerful rain and flooding:

فَأَضْحَى يَسُحُّ الْمَاءَ عَنْ كُلِّ فِيقَةٍ
يَكُبُّ عَلَى الْأَذْقَانِ دَوْحَ الْكَنَهْبَلِ

fa-aḍḥā yasuḥḥu al-māʾa ʿan kulli fīqatin / yakubbu ʿalā al-adhqāni dawḥa al-kanahbal

Translation: The rain then poured down after each interval, overturning the great kanahbal trees as though they fell upon their chins.

Dawḥ denotes large, spreading trees; kanahbal names a kind of tree recognized in Arabian lands. Fīqah here concerns an interval or turn between rainfall bursts.

The poet depicts rain and flooding that uproot even large trees and cast them down.

Al-adhqān is used figuratively for trees. Trees have no human chins, but when their upper parts fall to the ground the poet likens their position to falling upon the chin.

In explaining the image, al-adhqān is understood as a metaphor for the trees: a body part’s name pictures their overturning.

The first hemistich has variants such as ʿan kulli fīqatin and min kulli fīqatin. Yakubbu ʿalā al-adhqāni dawḥa al-kanahbali remains in recognized transmissions.

This matters for Qur’anic usage because it shows that, in pre-Qur’anic Arabic, falling “upon the chin” could picture overturning and the front or upper part of something falling down.

It does not establish that the chin itself must be the first point to touch the ground in every use of adhqān. The poem’s broad image of overturning matters.

The chin as a support

An Arabic proverb is recorded as follows:

مُثْقَلٌ اسْتَعَانَ بِذَقَنِهِ

muthqalun istaʿāna bi-dhaqanihi

That is: one weighed down by a heavy burden seeks help from his chin.

The explanation describes a camel under a heavy load, unable to rise, bracing its chin against the ground.

The image is applied to someone who, in helplessness, seeks assistance from a person or means equally unable to help.

The proverb is recorded in lexical material, but its first appearance is unknown. It should not be presented as certainly pre-Islamic without evidence.

Its lexical importance lies in using the chin as a support. It retains the bodily meaning of dhaqan while its figurative application concerns helplessness and reliance.

The proverb does not make dhaqan itself mean incapacity. That comes from the camel’s condition and the proverb’s use.

Other derivatives

Recorded Arabic forms also include dhāqinah, dhaqūn, dhaqanā and the verb dhaqana.

Al-dhāqinah in some uses denotes the part beneath the chin or the prominent extremity of the windpipe.

Nāqatun dhaqūnun describes a she-camel lowering or moving its chin or head in a particular way while walking. Some explanations also associate it with brisk, vigorous movement.

Dalwun dhaqanā denotes a bucket or leather water container with a lip inclining to one side rather than sitting evenly.

Dhaqana al-rajulu can concern placing the hand beneath the chin and resting upon it; transitive dhaqana-hu is recorded for striking another person’s chin.

These uses relate the body part’s name to movement, support, contact or a similar configuration in objects.

None of these derivatives occurs in the Qur’an. Its study therefore centers on the noun adhqān.

2. Al-Isrāʾ 17:107: falling upon the chins in prostration

Al-Isrāʾ 17:107 states:

قُلْ آمِنُوا بِهِ أَوْ لَا تُؤْمِنُوا إِنَّ الَّذِينَ أُوتُوا الْعِلْمَ مِنْ قَبْلِهِ إِذَا يُتْلَىٰ عَلَيْهِمْ يَخِرُّونَ لِلْأَذْقَانِ سُجَّدًا

qul āminū bihi aw lā tuʾminū inna alladhīna ūtū al-ʿilma min qablihi idhā yutlā ʿalayhim yakhirrūna li-al-adhqāni sujjadā

Translation: Say: Believe in it or do not believe. Those given knowledge before it, when it is recited to them, fall upon their chins in prostration.

Al-adhqāni is the plural of dhaqan and appears with the preposition li-.

Yakhirrūna belongs to KH R R and denotes falling. Sujjadan describes their condition while falling.

Study of KH R R

Three elements must therefore remain distinct: falling, the chins and prostration.

Adhqān names a facial part; yakhirrūna expresses falling; and sujjadan specifies their prostrating condition.

The meaning of li-al-adhqāni

Kharra li-al-adhqāni concerns falling upon the front of the face.

Early grammatical explanations understand lām here as close to ʿalā: falling upon the chins.

Lexical and contextual explanations also sometimes take adhqān in this construction as representing the face or its front.

Two explanations are transmitted: falling upon the faces, and attention to the chins themselves or the beard-covered region. These explain the sentence’s image; the basic meaning of dhaqan is the front of the lower jaw.

The body part’s meaning should therefore be distinguished from the movement’s whole image.

Lexically, adhqān remains “chins.” The complete expression describes falling face downward and is not necessarily an independent instruction that only the chin should touch the ground.

“Fall upon their chins” preserves the Arabic structure. The explanation should clarify that the image is falling in prostration, rather than defining every detail of a ritual act.

Knowledge and humility

The verse concerns people given knowledge before the Qur’an is recited to them.

Their response to its recitation is expressed by yakhirrūna li-al-adhqāni sujjadan.

Falling here is not an empty motion: it responds to what they hear and recognize.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:108 also records their words: they declare their Lord free of imperfection and affirm His promise’s fulfillment.

Their bodily movement, recognition and speech form one connected scene.

This does not mean every knowledgeable person must show the same physical response or that knowledge is measured solely by outward prostration.

The verse describes a particular group responding this way to Qur’anic recitation.

Dhaqan itself does not mean knowledge, faith or humility. Those concepts arise from other elements and the context.

3. Al-Isrāʾ 17:109: falling with tears and increasing humility

Al-Isrāʾ 17:109 states:

وَيَخِرُّونَ لِلْأَذْقَانِ يَبْكُونَ وَيَزِيدُهُمْ خُشُوعًا

wa-yakhirrūna li-al-adhqāni yabkūna wa-yazīduhum khushūʿā

Translation: They fall upon their chins, weeping, and it increases their humility.

The verse repeats yakhirrūna li-al-adhqāni, adding yabkūna and yazīduhum khushūʿan.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:107 described their prostrating condition with sujjadan. Al-Isrāʾ 17:109 mentions weeping and increased humility.

The verses describe different aspects of a connected scene. They need not be treated as entirely unrelated events.

The chin and the image of humility

When someone falls face downward, the positions of the head and chin matter in describing the movement.

Here this falling accompanies tears and humility.

Khushūʿ belongs to another root and concerns humility and submissiveness. Bukāʾ denotes weeping.

Dhaqan itself therefore does not mean humility or tears. The chin belongs to the bodily image; other words describe the people’s inward and behavioral condition.

The verse also describes increasing humility. The response is more than an outward movement: the context concerns the effect of hearing the Qur’an upon them.

It does not establish that every prostration without tears is invalid or that anyone not weeping upon hearing the Qur’an lacks humility.

Such conclusions exceed the word’s sense and the description of this group.

Connection with Maryam 19:58

Maryam 19:58 likewise describes people falling in prostration and weeping when the verses of the Entirely Merciful are recited to them.

Its combination of falling, prostration and weeping relates to Al-Isrāʾ 17:107 and Al-Isrāʾ 17:109.

Adhqān does not occur in Maryam 19:58.

The chin need not therefore be mentioned to express falling in prostration. The Qur’an can describe the same kind of response using other wording.

Adhqān belongs to these two Al-Isrāʾ verses’ particular image. It names neither prostration itself nor a lexical condition for its occurrence.

4. Yā Sīn 36:8: shackles reaching the chins

Yā Sīn 36:8 states:

إِنَّا جَعَلْنَا فِي أَعْنَاقِهِمْ أَغْلَالًا فَهِيَ إِلَى الْأَذْقَانِ فَهُمْ مُقْمَحُونَ

innā jaʿalnā fī aʿnāqihim aghlālan fa-hiya ilā al-adhqāni fa-hum muqmaḥūn

Translation: We have placed shackles around their necks, reaching their chins, so their heads are held raised.

Al-adhqān again means chins, now in ilā al-adhqāni.

Ilā expresses an extent or endpoint: the shackles reach the chins.

This differs from li-al-adhqāni in Al-Isrāʾ. There the chins describe the direction and manner of falling; here they mark the shackles’ extent.

Shackles and the head’s position

Aʿnāqihim at the sentence’s opening denotes their necks; aghlālan denotes restraining shackles or bonds.

Then comes fa-hiya ilā al-adhqāni.

Grammatically, the feminine singular pronoun hiya can naturally refer to aghlālan: a nonhuman plural can take a feminine singular pronoun in Arabic.

Some early explanations instead take it to refer to the bound hands, although hands are not named in the verse and are inferred from the familiar arrangement of shackles.

For a direct translation, reference to the shackles is clearer and closer to the stated wording.

The verse concludes fa-hum muqmaḥūn.

Muqmaḥūn belongs to Q M Ḥ and here concerns heads held raised, with the ability to lower them restricted.

The raised position comes from muqmaḥūn and the whole image, rather than adhqān itself.

The chin remains the body part reached by the shackles, affecting the head’s position.

Bodily image and contextual meaning

Yā Sīn 36:7 mentions the word taking effect against most of the group and their not believing.

Yā Sīn 36:8 then depicts shackles reaching the chins.

Yā Sīn 36:9 describes barriers before and behind them and their being covered so that they do not see.

The shackles-and-chin image thus belongs to a sequence describing restriction, blocked access and inability to see.

Early contextual explanations sometimes understand this as a likeness of people closed and unreceptive to guidance.

That reading relates to the context, but the image and its interpretive conclusion must remain distinct.

The Qur’an explicitly mentions shackles, necks, chins and heads held raised. The extent to which the image is understood bodily or figuratively belongs to a broader contextual study.

The name “chin” alone does not establish the bodily character of punishment, the construction of instruments in the hereafter or every person’s psychological condition.

Comparing the two Qur’anic images

A noteworthy contrast can now be seen between these uses.

In Al-Isrāʾ, people fall upon hearing the Qur’an, with prostration, weeping and humility.

In Yā Sīn, a group has heads held raised in the neck-shackles image, while surrounding verses concern restricted sight and failure to accept warning.

The first image combines downward movement with humility; the second holds the head in a raised, restricted position.

The contrast can matter in reflection on the scenes, but should not become the root’s lexical definition.

Dhaqan means neither humility nor arrogance. The chin is the same body part in both images. Ethical and contextual differences arise from the people’s conduct and the verses’ construction.

5. Comparison with related words

DH Q N (dhaqan) and W J H (wajh): chin and face

Wajh denotes the face or its front and also has broader semantic uses.

Dhaqan names a specific part of the lower face.

Every chin is part of a face, but the entire face cannot be reduced to the chin.

In some Arabic uses, a body part’s name represents a larger whole. This possibility is raised in explaining yakhirrūna li-al-adhqāni.

The figurative use does not change dhaqan’s principal meaning.

DH Q N (dhaqan) and L Ḥ Y (liḥyah): chin and beard

Liḥyah concerns the beard and occurs in Aaron’s words to Moses in Ṭā Hā 20:94.

Dhaqan names the bodily region of the lower face that a beard may cover.

Early definitions’ mujtamaʿu al-laḥyayni concerns where the two sides of the lower jaw meet. It does not make dhaqan and liḥyah the same word in their direct meanings.

Qur’anic adhqān is therefore better translated “chins” than “beards.”

DH Q N (dhaqan) and J B H (jabhah): chin and forehead

Jabhah denotes the upper front of the face: the forehead.

Al-Tawbah 9:35 uses the plural jibāh alongside other body parts.

Chin and forehead are both facial parts, but occupy different positions.

This matters for prostration verses: mentioning the chin in Al-Isrāʾ 17:107 and Al-Isrāʾ 17:109 does not mean the Qur’an uses the word “forehead” in those expressions.

Conversely, mentioning the chin alone does not determine every detail and body part involved in prostration.

DH Q N (dhaqan) and ʿ N Q (ʿunuq): chin and neck

ʿUnuq denotes the neck, joining the head to the torso.

Yā Sīn 36:8 contains both words: shackles are placed around necks and reach the chins.

Their co-occurrence shows that ʿunuq and dhaqan denote different parts, each with its own place in the image.

Taking chin to mean neck would blur the sentence’s distinction. Aʿnāq should remain “necks,” and adhqān “chins.”

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

“Chins” is an accurate, natural equivalent of adhqān in all three Qur’anic occurrences.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:107, yakhirrūna li-al-adhqāni sujjadan can be rendered:

“They fall upon their chins in prostration.”

This preserves the Arabic words and the falling movement. Contextual explanation can clarify that it also pictures falling face downward.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:109, wa-yakhirrūna li-al-adhqāni yabkūna can be rendered:

“They fall upon their chins, weeping.”

Weeping comes from yabkūna and should not be incorporated into adhqān’s meaning.

In Yā Sīn 36:8, fa-hiya ilā al-adhqāni means “so they reach the chins.”

Given the pronoun’s referent and the preceding sentence, “shackles reaching their chins” is natural and defensible.

Forqan’s current Persian translations retain the word for chins in all three verses. The root’s principal meaning gives no reason to change that equivalent.

7. Conclusion

DH Q N centers on a particular body part: the chin, low in the face where the two sides of the lower jaw meet.

Arabic also uses the name for resting upon the chin, lowering or moving the head, and figuratively for the position of certain objects.

Imruʾ al-Qays’s witness shows that falling “upon the chins” could picture overturning in pre-Islamic poetry, even when applied to trees without actual chins.

The root occurs three times in the Qur’an, exclusively as the plural adhqān.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:107 and Al-Isrāʾ 17:109, the chins belong to an image of falling in prostration, associated with recognition, tears and humility.

In Yā Sīn 36:8, the chins mark the extent of shackles restricting the heads’ position. The context concerns failure to accept warning and obstructed sight.

Final definition: Dhaqan names the chin, the lower, front part of the face where the two sides of the lower jaw meet. Adhqān is its plural. In imagery, the part can describe the direction of falling, support or the head’s position; the word itself does not become humility, defiance, falling or restricted movement.

The bodily meaning “chin” remains throughout the three Qur’anic verses. What differs is the associated movement, situation and contextual message.
