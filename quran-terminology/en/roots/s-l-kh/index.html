The Arabic root S L KH concerns stripping and separating a covering from something. In its familiar use, salakha means to skin an animal, salkh denotes skinning, and insalakha means to emerge or separate from a covering previously associated with something.

Arabic extends this meaning to other subjects. A snake emerging from its old skin, taking off clothing, a person leaving a former position or attachment, and the passing of a period can all be expressed through this family.

An important feature is separation from something that previously covered, accompanied, or surrounded the subject. Physically, skin separates from a body; in extended uses, a person leaves something with which they were associated, or a period ends.

The root occurs three times in the Quran: of a person separating from divine signs, of the sacred months passing, and of day being stripped from night.

Although their subjects differ, all three concern emergence, withdrawal, or separation. The physical details of skinning an animal must not be transferred literally to signs, time, or day and night.

1. The root in early Arabic

Salkh: stripping skin from a body

An explicit lexical definition of salkh is:

«كَشْطُ الْإِهَابِ عَنْ ذِيهِ»

kashṭu al-ihābi ʿan dhīhi

“Stripping the hide from that to which it belongs.”

Salakha al-shāta describes someone removing a sheep's skin from its body.

Here the skin is attached to the body before the action. Salkh breaks that connection and separates the skin from what it covers.

The point is more than moving an object: it is removing a covering previously connected to a surface or body.

Another early definition describes this family as bringing something out of its skin.

This clarifies the root's physical image.

Yet Arabic does not restrict the word to animal skins. The same relation of separation extends to other coverings and even periods of time.

The basic account must therefore distinguish physical skinning from the broader sense of separating from a covering or former attachment.

Skinning and shedding skin

Two related but distinct uses are known in Arabic.

First, a person skins an animal. An external agent performs the separation.

Second, an animal such as a snake leaves and emerges from its old skin.

The transitive verb salakha naturally expresses the first use.

In the second, insalakha can express the animal's emergence from its former skin or covering.

Mislākh and salkhah are also recorded for a snake's shed skin.

This distinction helps explain the Quranic forms.

With salakha, an agent can separate something from its covering.

With insalakha, the subject is presented as emerging from its previous attachment or covering.

Form VII alone does not prove that this separation is conscious, voluntary, or gradual. Those features depend on the subject and context.

Salkh and silkh: the action and the skin

Arabic records a distinction between these similar forms.

Salkh, with a short a, primarily denotes skinning.

Silkh, with a short i, can denote the skin itself.

Maslūkh describes an animal or object that has been skinned.

Salīkh can describe a skinned animal. The related salīkhah is also recorded for wood or a bow stave stripped of its outer covering; these forms should be distinguished.

These forms show that the family can express both the action of stripping and its result.

Each derivative must nevertheless be understood through its own form and usage. Not every occurrence of this family can be translated as an animal's skin.

Taking off clothing

The Arabic expression salakhat al-marʾatu ʿanhā dirʿahā means that a woman took off her shirt or garment.

Clothing is not the body's natural skin. It is a covering that a person puts on and later removes.

The relation is clear: what previously covered the body is separated from it.

This extension offers a useful basis for understanding nonphysical applications.

A person can emerge from clothing, leave a situation, or set aside something previously connected with their life.

This similarity does not mean that every separation must be painful or harmful.

Skinning an animal may involve violence or injury, whereas removing clothing or natural shedding need not.

Pain, violence, and destruction are therefore not constant components of the root.

An early witness: passing through time

An important witness occurs in the Muʿallaqah of Labīd ibn Rabīʿah:

حَتَّىٰ إِذَا سَلَخَا جُمَادَىٰ سِتَّةً
جَزَآ فَطَالَ صِيَامُهُ وَصِيَامُهَا

ḥattā idhā salakhā Jumādā sittatan / jazaʾā fa-ṭāla ṣiyāmuhu wa-ṣiyāmuhā

“When the two had passed through the six winter months, they subsisted on fresh herbage; his and her abstention from drinking grew long.” This follows one interpretation of the time expression.

In this part of the poem, Labīd describes a wild ass and its mate.

Salakhā means that the two passed through or completed a period.

Early explanations differ over Jumādā sittatan: one understands six winter months, while another identifies Jumādā al-Ākhirah, the sixth month of the year. Neither should be presented as the sole certain reading.

Another transmitted wording has Jumādā kullahā, “all of Jumādā,” in place of Jumādā sittatan.

Despite this variation, salakhā in both readings concerns passing through and completing a period.

No animal is being skinned in this verse. Its subject is the passage of time.

One can understand the period as a covering around the creatures' lives and movement, from which they emerge when it ends.

This explains the semantic image; it does not establish how every pre-Islamic Arabic speaker imagined time.

Labīd lived across the transition from the pre-Islamic period to Islam. The verse belongs to his famous Muʿallaqah and its early commentarial tradition.

The verse's exact date is not independently established by the attribution of the poem. It is evidence for early Arabic, without alone proving a precise pre-Quranic date for this wording.

Its significance is that the temporal use of salkh also belongs to the early poetic tradition and is not confined to explanations of Quranic verses.

2. Quranic forms and derivatives

S L KH occurs three times in three Quranic verses.

The occurrences represent two morphological formations.

Naslakhu: stripping away

Yā Sīn 36:37 has:

Naslakhu.

This is the first-person plural imperfect of the basic verb salakha.

The subject is “We,” and al-nahāra, “day,” is the object.

The verbal construction thus expresses an act of separation.

Fa-insalakha: separating

Al-Aʿrāf 7:175 has:

Fa-insalakha.

This is a third-person masculine singular perfect verb in Form VII.

Its subject is the person to whom the divine signs had been given.

The prefixed fa- connects it to what precedes.

Insalakha: passing

Al-Tawbah 9:5 has:

Insalakha.

This too is a Form VII perfect verb.

Its subject, however, is al-ashhuru al-ḥurumu, “the sacred months.”

Although the formation is the same in Al-Aʿrāf and Al-Tawbah, the subjects differ: a person separates from the signs in the former, while a period passes in the latter.

Other Arabic forms in the family include:

  • Salakha: skinned or removed a covering.
  • Salkh: skinning; also passing through and completing a period.
  • Silkh: skin or a separated covering.
  • Insalakha: emerged from a covering or separated from something.
  • Sulūkh: separation or passing through a period.
  • Sālikh: one who skins; also used of a snake that sheds its skin.
  • Maslūkh: skinned or stripped of a covering.
  • Mislākh: skin, particularly a snake's shed skin; distinguish it from maslakh, a place of skinning.
  • Salīkh: a skinned animal.

These forms illuminate the semantic field, but the Quran has only naslakhu and the stated forms of insalakha.

3. Fa-insalakha minhā: separating from divine signs

Al-Aʿrāf 7:175 states:

وَاتْلُ عَلَيْهِمْ نَبَأَ الَّذِي آتَيْنَاهُ آيَاتِنَا فَانْسَلَخَ مِنْهَا فَأَتْبَعَهُ الشَّيْطَانُ فَكَانَ مِنَ الْغَاوِينَ

wa-utlu ʿalayhim nabaʾa alladhī ātaynāhu āyātinā fa-insalakha minhā fa-atbaʿahu al-shayṭānu fa-kāna mina al-ghāwīn

Al-Aʿrāf 7:175

“Recite to them the account of the one to whom We gave Our signs, but he separated from them; then Satan pursued him, and he became one of those astray.”

Here fa-insalakha is used of a human being.

He had previously received the divine signs but subsequently separated from them.

Separating from what was previously connected with a person

In the physical sense, an animal may emerge from its old skin.

Here the person separates from the divine signs.

The signs are not a person's physical skin, but the image of separation operates at another level.

The expression presupposes an earlier relationship. This person was not encountering something wholly foreign and unknown: the signs had been given to him.

Insalakha minhā therefore differs from a situation in which someone never had any knowledge of the signs.

The issue here is separation after receiving the signs.

This matters for understanding the person's responsibility.

The referent of minhā

The pronoun -hā in minhā refers to āyātinā, “Our signs.”

The verse therefore says that the person separated from the signs.

The pronoun does not refer to God or another person.

A direct translation is:

“Then he separated from them.”

The image can be explained as emerging from a covering previously associated with him.

For the basic translation, however, “separated from them” or “came out of them” is clearer.

Were the signs taken away from him?

The verse says:

«فَانْسَلَخَ مِنْهَا».

fa-insalakha minhā

Al-Aʿrāf 7:175

The person himself is the grammatical subject of the intransitive verb.

The sentence does not expressly say that God took the signs back from him or that someone forcibly separated him from them.

This follows from the expressed sentence structure. Grammatical subjecthood alone does not prove free choice; the next verse's account of following desire clarifies his conduct.

Nor does insalakha by itself mean forgetting.

A person may recognize a truth while distancing their conduct from it.

In that case, abandoning guidance and practical commitment is not identical with losing every piece of mental knowledge.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:176 further clarifies the direction of this separation.

Clinging to the earth and following desire

Al-Aʿrāf 7:176 states:

وَلَوْ شِئْنَا لَرَفَعْنَاهُ بِهَا وَلَٰكِنَّهُ أَخْلَدَ إِلَى الْأَرْضِ وَاتَّبَعَ هَوَاهُ

wa-law shiʾnā la-rafaʿnāhu bihā wa-lākinnahu akhlada ilā al-arḍi wa-ittabaʿa hawāhu

Al-Aʿrāf 7:176

“Had We willed, We would have raised him by them; but he clung to the earth and followed his own desire.”

This shows that the signs could have been the means of his elevation.

Instead, the person chose another course and followed his desire.

In this sequence, insalakha is more than a change of situation: it is separation from guidance previously available to him.

His error is not attributed simply to a failure to receive information.

The issue is his response and choice after receiving the signs.

Knowing the truth and remaining faithful to it are therefore distinct matters.

A person may recognize a truth yet distance themselves from it through self-interest, attachment, fear, or desire.

These possible motives must not all be attributed to the particular person described. The verse specifies clinging to the earth and following desire.

Fa-atbaʿahu al-shayṭānu: the sequence of events

After fa-insalakha minhā comes:

«فَأَتْبَعَهُ الشَّيْطَانُ».

fa-atbaʿahu al-shayṭān

Al-Aʿrāf 7:175

Al-shayṭānu is the subject, and the object pronoun -hu refers to that person.

In the straightforward reading, Satan pursued and caught up with him.

Subject and object should not be reversed by silently changing the construction into “he followed Satan.”

The continuation does describe the person's following his own desire and makes his error clear; the sequence and construction of the two sentences should still be preserved.

First, the person separates from the signs.

Then Satan pursues him.

Finally, he is described as one of those astray.

This sequence gives separation from the signs an important narrative role.

The verb insalakha does not establish that Satan had never tempted him before. The verse reports events in a particular order.

The person's identity

The Quran does not name the person here.

Historical explanations propose identities, but those names are absent from the verse itself.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:176 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:177, his story becomes a comparison for those who deny the divine signs and wrong themselves.

The text thus focuses on character and behavior, rather than ancestry, ethnicity, or personal fame.

This prevents the lesson from being confined to one historical individual.

Anyone who has access to a truth and then knowingly distances themselves from it may be assessed in terms of that behavior.

Yet without knowing a particular person's intention and awareness, one should not pronounce them someone who has “sloughed off” the signs like the person in this verse.

4. Fa-idhā insalakha al-ashhuru al-ḥurumu: the passing of time

Al-Tawbah 9:5 states:

فَإِذَا انْسَلَخَ الْأَشْهُرُ الْحُرُمُ فَاقْتُلُوا الْمُشْرِكِينَ حَيْثُ وَجَدْتُمُوهُمْ وَخُذُوهُمْ وَاحْصُرُوهُمْ وَاقْعُدُوا لَهُمْ كُلَّ مَرْصَدٍ

fa-idhā insalakha al-ashhuru al-ḥurumu fa-uqtulū al-mushrikīna ḥaythu wajadtumūhum wa-khudhūhum wa-uḥṣurūhum wa-uqʿudū lahum kulla marṣad

Al-Tawbah 9:5

“Then, when the sacred months have passed, kill the polytheists wherever you find them, seize them, besiege them, and lie in wait for them at every place of ambush.”

Here insalakha is applied to months.

Months have no physical skin and do not emerge from a body.

The verb therefore has a temporal meaning: a period ends, passes, or elapses.

Salkh and the end of a month

In early Arabic, salakhtu al-shahra meant passing through a month or reaching its end.

Insalakha al-shahru likewise means that the month passed and ended.

The same temporal use appears in Labīd's verse about passing through the period described as Jumādā.

Al-Tawbah 9:5 thus uses an established Arabic temporal expression.

There is no need to translate it as “the months were skinned.”

The root's physical image may explain the relation, but the natural contextual equivalent is “pass” or “elapse.”

The sacred months and the appointed respite

The opening of Al-Tawbah addresses treaties and the declaration of dissociation.

Al-Tawbah 9:2 states:

فَسِيحُوا فِي الْأَرْضِ أَرْبَعَةَ أَشْهُرٍ

fa-sīḥū fī al-arḍi arbaʿata ashhur

Al-Tawbah 9:2

“Travel freely through the land for four months.”

Al-Tawbah 9:4 requires fulfillment, until their term, of treaties with those who have not failed in their obligations or supported anyone against the Muslims.

Al-Tawbah 9:5 then speaks of the sacred months passing.

Time in this sequence is thus related to respite, treaties, and the conditions of confrontation.

Two major early readings concern the exact identification of al-ashhuru al-ḥurumu in Al-Tawbah 9:5.

One identifies them with the four months of respite stated in Al-Tawbah 9:2.

The other relates the designation to the recognized sacred months of the Arabic calendar.

The close sequence of Al-Tawbah 9:2, Al-Tawbah 9:3, Al-Tawbah 9:4, and Al-Tawbah 9:5 provides important contextual support for a connection with the respite announced at the surah's opening.

The root S L KH itself does not decide between these readings.

Insalakha only expresses the period's ending. Identifying that period requires examination of “the sacred months” and the surrounding verses.

A period ending and a treaty ending

Al-Tawbah 9:4 expressly requires respect for the treaties of those who have remained faithful to their obligations.

Al-Tawbah 9:6 requires protection for a polytheist who seeks refuge, so that he may hear God's word, followed by safe conveyance to his place of security.

Al-Tawbah 9:7 also calls for upright conduct toward those who remain upright in their treaty commitments.

The end of a respite in Al-Tawbah 9:5 should not be isolated from these qualifications and made to mean that everyone's security or every treaty becomes void.

At the same time, changing the meaning of insalakha must not conceal the explicit commands in the verse.

Insalakha establishes a temporal condition. The action prescribed afterward is understood from the other verbs, qualifications, and full context.

The end of a period and a change of status

A period's ending can mark a change in the relevant situation.

For example, a treaty term, respite, or designated period ends, and a new situation follows.

Salkh itself does not mean annulling a treaty, declaring war, or removing every restriction.

These may be connected with a period's ending, but their meaning and validity depend on independent provisions and conditions.

In Al-Tawbah 9:5 too, the root directly marks the ending of a period.

An appropriate rendering is:

“When the sacred months have passed.”

This expresses the temporal condition.

It preserves the construction and conveys the established temporal sense naturally.

5. Naslakhu minhu al-nahāra: stripping day from night

Yā Sīn 36:37 states:

وَآيَةٌ لَهُمُ اللَّيْلُ نَسْلَخُ مِنْهُ النَّهَارَ فَإِذَا هُمْ مُظْلِمُونَ

wa-āyatun lahumu al-laylu naslakhu minhu al-nahāra fa-idhā hum muẓlimūn

Yā Sīn 36:37

“And night is a sign for them: We strip day from it, and suddenly they are in darkness.”

This is one of the root's clearest figurative uses in the Quran.

Physically, a skin is removed from what it covers.

In this verse, day is removed from night.

Day and night are not actual skin and body. The Quran uses the familiar action of stripping a covering to express the transition between light and darkness.

What is removed from what?

The construction is crucial:

«نَسْلَخُ مِنْهُ النَّهَارَ».

naslakhu minhu al-nahār

Yā Sīn 36:37

Naslakhu is the verb.

Minhu refers to night.

Al-nahāra, “day,” is the object.

The verse therefore says:

We strip day from night.

It does not say that night is stripped from day.

This distinction must be maintained in translation and explanation.

In the image, daylight is like a covering that is removed, revealing darkness.

The continuation supports this reading:

«فَإِذَا هُمْ مُظْلِمُونَ».

fa-idhā hum muẓlimūn

Yā Sīn 36:37

That is, suddenly they find themselves in darkness.

The contextual result of stripping day away is the arrival of darkness.

Why stripping rather than merely the passing of day?

A simple expression could describe day passing and night arriving.

Naslakhu, however, creates a more tangible image.

It presents the withdrawal of light as removing a covering previously laid over something.

When the covering is removed, what lay beneath becomes visible.

In this image, the darkness of night appears as daylight withdraws.

The expression matters because it does more than name two periods: it depicts the transition itself.

It does not imply that daylight is a substance like skin, or that night is a body wearing it.

These are elements of figurative imagery, not a physical description of the universe's construction.

Is night primary and day its covering?

The image may suggest that night is the underlying state and day its covering within this comparison.

That is understandable as an account of the rhetorical image.

Without further evidence, it should not become a definitive claim about darkness having been created first, the universe's fundamental nature, or its physical structure.

The verse itself presents night as a sign.

Yā Sīn 36:38 and Yā Sīn 36:39 address the sun's movement and the moon's stations.

Yā Sīn 36:40 also states:

وَلَا اللَّيْلُ سَابِقُ النَّهَارِ وَكُلٌّ فِي فَلَكٍ يَسْبَحُونَ

wa-lā al-laylu sābiqu al-nahāri wa-kullun fī falakin yasbaḥūn

Yā Sīn 36:40

“Nor does night overtake day; each swims in an orbit.”

Together these passages emphasize order and the relationships among temporal and celestial phenomena.

Yā Sīn 36:37 thus directs attention to the ordered transition between day and night.

For a broader study of night and its relationship with day, see Forqan's root study L Y L.

Study of L Y L

Salkh and takwīr

Al-Zumar 39:5 states:

يُكَوِّرُ اللَّيْلَ عَلَى النَّهَارِ وَيُكَوِّرُ النَّهَارَ عَلَى اللَّيْلِ

yukawwiru al-layla ʿalā al-nahāri wa-yukawwiru al-nahāra ʿalā al-layl

Al-Zumar 39:5

“He wraps night over day and wraps day over night.”

The verb yukawwiru here belongs to K W R.

Takwīr in its familiar sense concerns wrapping one thing over another.

Salkh, by contrast, concerns removing a covering.

The expressions provide different images of a natural phenomenon.

One emphasizes wrapping night and day over each other.

The other emphasizes day being stripped from night.

The difference shows the range of Quranic imagery; it need not imply two entirely separate physical processes.

The verses can address different aspects of the transition between night and day.

A shared subject does not justify translating takwīr and salkh identically.

Salkh and darkness

Muẓlimūna in Yā Sīn 36:37 belongs to Ẓ L M and here concerns entering darkness.

Salkh by itself does not mean becoming dark.

Removing a covering may reveal something previously hidden.

Here what appears when day is removed is the darkness of night.

Darkening is thus the result understood by relating naslakhu minhu al-nahāra to fa-idhā hum muẓlimūna.

That distinction prevents translating S L KH as “darken” in every use.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:175 uses the same family for a person's separation from the signs, and Al-Tawbah 9:5 for the months passing.

Darkness is therefore not the root's general meaning, but the particular result in Yā Sīn 36:37.

6. Distinctions from related roots

S L KH and K SH Ṭ: stripping a covering

Kashṭ concerns removing or setting aside a covering.

Al-Takwīr 81:11 states:

وَإِذَا السَّمَاءُ كُشِطَتْ

wa-idhā al-samāʾu kushiṭat

Al-Takwīr 81:11

“And when the sky is stripped away.”

Kashṭ and salkh overlap in removing a covering.

The lexical definition of salkh even uses kashṭ al-ihāb, “stripping the hide.”

Salkh has a specific physical connection with skinning and extends to leaving a former attachment or completing a period.

Kashṭ emphasizes removing or setting a covering aside.

This is a tendency, not an absolute, immutable boundary.

S L KH and N Z ʿ: separating and pulling out

Nazʿ concerns pulling something from its place or attachment.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:43 uses nazaʿnā for removing rancor from people's breasts.

Salkh likewise expresses a form of separation or removal.

Its physical sense, however, has a clearer connection with skin and covering.

Nazʿ can concern removing things that are not coverings at all.

Salkh may thus be a kind of separation, but not every nazʿ is skinning or emerging from a covering.

S L KH and KH L ʿ: removing a covering or garment

Khalʿ concerns taking off or setting aside something worn or held.

In Ṭā Hā 20:12, Moses is commanded:

فَاخْلَعْ نَعْلَيْكَ

fa-ikhlaʿ naʿlayk

Ṭā Hā 20:12

“Take off your sandals.”

This expresses removing something worn on the body.

Salkh too is recorded for taking off clothing.

Its familiar direct meaning is skinning, whereas khalʿ in clothing usage concerns removing a covering that can be put on and taken off.

Overlap in one application does not make two roots fully synonymous.

S L KH and S L B: shedding a covering and taking from another

Salb concerns taking or snatching something from another.

Al-Ḥajj 22:73 describes a fly taking something from the invoked deities, who cannot recover it.

The relation between taker, former possessor, and taken object is prominent.

Salkh instead emphasizes stripping a covering or separating from a former attachment.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:175 the person separates from the signs; the verse does not say someone snatched them away from him.

Fa-insalakha minhā should therefore not become “the signs were taken from him” without supporting evidence.

S L KH and M Ḍ Y: a period passing

Maḍā concerns passing, going, and elapsing.

Temporally, maḍā al-shahru means “the month passed.”

Insalakha al-shahru can express the same result.

Salkh, however, has the semantic background of emerging from a period or leaving a temporal covering.

“The sacred months have passed” remains an accurate, natural translation of insalakha al-ashhuru al-ḥurumu.

The skinning image need not be reconstructed literally in every translation.

S L KH and ʿ R Ḍ: separating and turning away

Iʿrāḍ concerns turning away and distancing oneself from something.

Many Quranic verses describe people turning away from signs or reminders.

Insalakha in Al-Aʿrāf 7:175 likewise concerns distancing oneself from the signs, but creates a stronger image of separation after an earlier connection.

Iʿrāḍ may describe someone unwilling to listen from the outset.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:175 instead describes someone who received the signs and then separated from them.

Previous receipt of the signs is therefore important in this context.

That follows from the verb together with the preceding clause; the root itself does not always presuppose receiving revelation.

7. Translation suggestions for Forqan

The basic physical equivalents are “skin” and “strip a covering.”

All three Quranic applications concern subjects other than animal skin.

Translation should therefore preserve the image without imposing a physical meaning on the text.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:175

«فَانْسَلَخَ مِنْهَا»

fa-insalakha minhā

Al-Aʿrāf 7:175

Suggested translation:

“Then he separated from them.”

“Then he emerged from them” also fits the root and preserves more of the image of emerging from a covering.

An explanation can state that the person distanced himself from the divine signs he had received and abandoned his attachment to them.

“The signs were taken from him” changes the expressed construction: the person is the subject of “separated,” and no external agent taking them away is stated.

Al-Tawbah 9:5

«فَإِذَا انْسَلَخَ الْأَشْهُرُ الْحُرُمُ»

fa-idhā insalakha al-ashhuru al-ḥurum

Al-Tawbah 9:5

Suggested translation:

“Then, when the sacred months have passed.”

“When the sacred months have ended” is also appropriate.

The skinning image has extended into a temporal meaning, for which “passing” is a natural equivalent.

Yā Sīn 36:37

«نَسْلَخُ مِنْهُ النَّهَارَ»

naslakhu minhu al-nahār

Yā Sīn 36:37

Suggested translation:

“We strip day from it.”

This preserves the image of stripping and separation.

“We separate day from it” is also clear and accurate, though less forceful as an image.

The explanation should clarify that the pronoun in minhu refers to night and that day is what is removed from it.

Suitable equivalents for other Arabic expressions include:

  • Salakha al-shāta: skinned the sheep.
  • Salakha al-jilda: removed the skin.
  • Insalakhat al-ḥayyatu: the snake emerged from its old skin.
  • Salkh: skinning or removing a covering.
  • Silkh: skin or a separated covering.
  • Maslūkh: skinned.
  • Salīkh: a skinned animal.
  • Salakha ʿanhu thawbahu: took his garment off him; the pronoun's referent is determined by context.
  • Salakha al-shahra: passed through the month.
  • Insalakha al-shahru: the month passed and ended.
  • Mislākh: skin, especially a snake's shed skin, according to usage.

Separation and emergence from a former connection remain important across these renderings.

The connection's subject differs in each sentence: skin, clothing, signs, a period, or daylight.

Conclusion

S L KH is known through the physical sense of skinning and removing a covering.

Early uses concern animal skins, a snake shedding its skin, and removing clothing. The temporal use for a month or period ending also appears in Labīd's poetry and Arabic lexical usage.

The root occurs three times in the Quran, each in a different setting.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:175 a person given divine signs separates from them, creating an image of distancing oneself after receiving guidance.

In Al-Tawbah 9:5 insalakha concerns the sacred months passing. The period ends; the provisions for what follows must be understood through the treaty context and the surah's other commands.

In Yā Sīn 36:37 day is stripped from night, and people enter darkness as light withdraws. The transition is expressed through the tangible image of removing a covering.

The family's central account is therefore:

S L KH primarily denotes skinning or removing a covering previously connected with something. Extended uses express leaving a situation or attachment, a period passing, and daylight withdrawing.

Separation matters throughout, but its agent, subject, and result differ.

In Al-Aʿrāf a person separates from signs; in Al-Tawbah a period ends; in Yā Sīn day is stripped from night.

The root must therefore be read with each sentence's construction and context.

For Forqan, “separate” in Al-Aʿrāf 7:175, “pass” in Al-Tawbah 9:5, and “strip” in Yā Sīn 36:37 suit the respective applications.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:175 carries a significant human lesson: receiving the signs does not by itself guarantee lifelong faithfulness to them.

Knowing truth, receiving guidance, and remaining committed to it are related but distinct matters.

The Quran describes a person who received the signs but separated from them, then explains that he clung to the earth and followed his desire.

As a covering can separate from something, a person may abandon a connection with guidance previously recognized.

This lesson comes from the context, while the lexical image remains separation from a covering or former attachment.
