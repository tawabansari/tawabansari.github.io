Bringing near, joining, and gathering: from movement of the hand to bringing components together

In early Arabic, ض م م denotes bringing one thing near another, bringing two or more components together, and joining them. ضَمَّ is used when something is brought towards something else, the distance between them decreases, or components are placed together.

Examples include drawing a hand towards the chest, embracing someone, and placing several things together. Related forms also describe scattered people gathering into a group.

ضم does not necessarily mean complete intermixture, fusion of the components’ identities, or permanent attachment. Two things may come together while retaining separate identities.

The principal lexical definitions emphasize bringing near, gathering, and joining components. This semantic field fits the clear Quranic usages.

The root occurs twice in the Quran, both times as the imperative وَاضْمُمْ: Ṭā Hā 20:22 and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:32. Both address Moses, but the constructions and objects of the commands differ.

1. Early meaning and Arabic evidence

In tangible Arabic usages, ضَمَّ الشَّيْءَ إِلَى الشَّيْءِ means bringing one thing near another and joining them.

The construction has two main components: what is moved and what it is brought towards. إِلَى specifies that direction.

For example, ضَمَمْتُهُ إِلَى صَدْرِي means “I drew him to my chest” or “I embraced him.”

ضم expresses the movement of bringing close. Affection, kindness, and the purpose of the movement are not supplied by the root alone. An embrace may express affection, but the lexical meaning is not confined to that feeling.

A poetic witness attributed to the pre-Islamic period

The poem known as Lāmiyyat al-ʿArab, attributed to Al-Shanfarā, contains:

تَوَافَيْنَ مِنْ شَتَّى إِلَيْهِ فَضَمَّهَا
كَمَا ضَمَّ أَذْوَادَ الْأَصَارِيمِ مَنْهَلُ

tawāfayna min shattā ilayhi fa-ḍammahā / ka-mā ḍamma adhwāda al-aṣārīmi manhalu

Translation: “They came to it from scattered places, and it gathered them, as a watering place gathers separate herds of camels.”

The verse describes birds travelling to water.

The birds arrive from different directions. Their gathering is compared with herds coming from scattered places to a common watering point.

The two occurrences of ضَمَّ highlight scattered components being brought together into a collection.

The watering place does not physically fasten the animals to one another; it brings them together at one location. Here ضم concerns gathering and proximity, not fusion.

The attribution of Lāmiyyat al-ʿArab to Al-Shanfarā is disputed, and some have assigned it a later date. The verse remains evidence for a recorded literary usage but does not alone provide certain pre-Quranic attestation.

Gathering different groups

A poem attributed to Muzarrid ibn Ḍirār, who lived across the advent of Islam, reads:

خُرُوجُ أَضَامِيمٍ وَأَحْصَنُ مَعْقِلٍ
إِذَا لَمْ تَكُنْ إِلَّا الْجِيَادُ مَعَاقِلُ

khurūju aḍāmīmin wa-aḥṣanu maʿqilin / idhā lam takun illā al-jiyādu maʿāqilu

Translation: “Outstripping groups of horses, and the surest refuge when swift horses are the only refuges.”

أَضَامِيم here denotes gathered groups of horses.

إِضْمَامَة is recorded for a group of people or things brought together, even without a common origin, family, or descent. Its plural is أَضَامِيم.

Thus ضم can concern components without a prior connection.

For example, people from several tribes may gather for a common purpose without sharing kinship or a single identity.

Muzarrid lived before and after the emergence of Islam, but this verse’s precise date is unclear. It illustrates early usage without establishing the word’s exact origin.

Classical lexical evidence and Quranic usage strongly support bringing near and gathering. They do not prove that every known derivative was already current before the Quran.

2. Semantic core and derivatives

ض م م is a doubled root: its second and third consonants are identical. In ضَمَّ, the two mīms are ordinarily represented by gemination, marked with a shaddah.

The basic verbal pattern is:

ضَمَّ يَضُمُّ ضَمًّا

It means bringing one thing near another, gathering it, or joining it to something.

Several semantic and morphological groups are recognized.

Bringing near and joining

ضَمَّ: brought near, gathered, or joined.

يَضُمُّ: brings near, joins, or gathers.

ضَمّ: bringing near, joining, or gathering.

ضَامّ: someone or something that gathers components or brings them close.

مَضْمُوم: something joined to another or gathered into a collection.

ضَمِيمَة: something added or attached to another.

The action commonly proceeds from one thing towards another.

A document added to a file, for instance, can be an attachment. The principal document and attachment become associated but need not share the same content.

This usage is familiar in modern Persian as well.

Gathering and joining others

انْضَمَّ: joined another or became part of a group.

انْضِمَام: joining or becoming attached.

تَضَامَّ: several people or things drew together or gathered.

ضَامَّ: associated with or drew close to another.

إِضْمَامَة: a gathered group of people or things.

أَضَامِيم: the plural of إضمامة, gathered groups or collections.

Gathering may be brought about by an agent or result from components drawing together.

“Someone gathered several people” differs from “several people gathered.” The verb form and sentence identify the agent and those brought together.

Embracing and holding close

Some usages involve holding something close, such as drawing the arms around something or embracing a person.

Complete enclosure, confinement, or possession is not essential to every derivative.

Someone may draw something near without enclosing it completely. Several people may gather without one surrounding another.

The meaning of bringing close must therefore be distinguished from the image supplied by a particular situation.

ضم in later terminology

In Arabic grammar and phonetics, ضَمَّة names a short vowel and its written mark. Its name is connected with the position of the lips during pronunciation.

ضمير, ضميمة, and انضمام are familiar in modern Persian and Arabic, but they must be distinguished.

ضميمة and انضمام belong to ض م م; ضمير belongs to the separate root ض م ر.

Similar beginnings do not make their roots identical.

Of these forms, the Quran uses only the imperative اضْمُمْ. Quranic study of the family therefore centres on that verb and its two constructions.

3. First Quranic usage: drawing the hand to the side

Ṭā Hā 20:22

وَاضْمُمْ يَدَكَ إِلَىٰ جَنَاحِكَ تَخْرُجْ بَيْضَاءَ مِنْ غَيْرِ سُوءٍ آيَةً أُخْرَىٰ

wa-ḍmum yadaka ilā janāḥika takhruj bayḍāʾa min ghayri sūʾin āyatan ukhrā

Ṭā Hā 20:22

Translation: “Draw your hand to your side; it will come out white, without harm, as another sign.”

The God directly addresses Moses here.

In the preceding verses, Moses is commanded to cast down his staff. It becomes a moving serpent. He is then told to take it and not fear.

A further command follows: to draw his hand to his side so that it comes out white and unharmed.

The construction وَاضْمُمْ

وَاضْمُمْ consists of two elements:

وَ is a conjunction connecting the command with the preceding discourse.

اضْمُمْ is the masculine singular imperative of ض م م.

It commands Moses to act. Its object is يَدَكَ, “your hand.”

إِلَىٰ جَنَاحِكَ specifies the direction: towards his wing or side.

The basic construction is thus: bring your hand towards your side and draw it close.

ضم has a tangible bodily meaning here, describing movement of the hand towards part of the body.

جَنَاح in this construction

جَنَاح commonly means a bird’s wing. In reference to a human body it can denote the arm, side, or area beneath the arm.

This extension relates the position of human arms to that of a bird’s wings.

Because the hand is to move towards the جناح, the word identifies the part of Moses’ body to which it is brought.

The side or area beneath the arm is a fitting contextual image.

This comes from يَدَكَ إِلَىٰ جَنَاحِكَ, not from ضم itself.

The role of تَخْرُجْ بَيْضَاءَ

The command is followed by its result: the hand comes out white.

تَخْرُجْ expresses coming out; بَيْضَاءَ describes the hand’s condition as it emerges.

مِنْ غَيْرِ سُوءٍ specifies that the whiteness involves no harm or defect.

آيَةً أُخْرَىٰ identifies another sign alongside the preceding event involving the staff.

The hand’s becoming a divine sign is understood from the complete verse, not from ض م م.

ضم means neither making white nor producing a miracle. It describes drawing the hand close.

Here that movement is commanded by The God and accompanies an extraordinary event.

Its lexical meaning should therefore not be expanded into transforming, illuminating, or healing.

4. Second Quranic usage: drawing in the wing in fear

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:32

اسْلُكْ يَدَكَ فِي جَيْبِكَ تَخْرُجْ بَيْضَاءَ مِنْ غَيْرِ سُوءٍ وَاضْمُمْ إِلَيْكَ جَنَاحَكَ مِنَ الرَّهْبِ ۖ فَذَانِكَ بُرْهَانَانِ مِنْ رَبِّكَ إِلَىٰ فِرْعَوْنَ وَمَلَئِهِ ۚ إِنَّهُمْ كَانُوا قَوْمًا فَاسِقِينَ

usluk yadaka fī jaybika takhruj bayḍāʾa min ghayri sūʾin wa-ḍmum ilayka janāḥaka mina al-rahbi fa-dhānika burhānāni min rabbika ilā firʿawna wa-malaʾihi innahum kānū qawman fāsiqīn

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:32

Translation: “Put your hand into the opening of your garment; it will come out white, without harm. Draw your arm towards yourself against fear. These are two proofs from your Lord to Pharaoh and his chiefs; they have been a defiantly disobedient people.”

This verse also concerns Moses, the staff, the hand, and his mission to Pharaoh.

Its command is nevertheless constructed differently from Ṭā Hā 20:22.

In Ṭā Hā, the command is:

وَاضْمُمْ يَدَكَ إِلَىٰ جَنَاحِكَ

wa-ḍmum yadaka ilā janāḥika

Ṭā Hā 20:22

Draw your hand towards your side.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ, it is:

وَاضْمُمْ إِلَيْكَ جَنَاحَكَ

wa-ḍmum ilayka janāḥaka

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:32

Draw your wing towards yourself.

Both contain ضم and إِلَى, but their stated objects and destinations differ.

Comparing the constructions

In Ṭā Hā 20:22, يَدَكَ is the object and جَنَاحِكَ the destination towards which the hand moves.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:32, جَنَاحَكَ is itself the object, while إِلَيْكَ specifies the direction.

The root meaning remains the same: drawing close or bringing together.

The bodily image differs: the first draws the hand towards the side; the second draws the wing or arm towards the person himself.

Translation should preserve this distinction, since the object and prepositional phrase contribute to the sentence’s meaning.

The role of مِنَ الرَّهْبِ

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:31, Moses turns away without looking back when he sees the staff move. He is then told to approach and not fear.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:32, مِنَ الرَّهْبِ accompanies the command to draw close.

رَهْب concerns fear here. Drawing the wing in is thus connected with Moses’ fearful state.

One interpretation understands a bodily movement of drawing in the arm to contain the agitation associated with fear.

Another reads drawing in the wing figuratively as composure, self-command, and steadfastness. Calming oneself in this interpretation belongs to the whole expression, not the independent meaning of ضم.

A person frightened by something may move restlessly, raise the hands defensively, or draw the body back. Drawing the arm in can provide an image of returning to a calmer, more prepared posture.

Such an illustration should not be made into a universal theory of bodily responses to fear.

The underlying linguistic image is drawing the wing towards oneself; understanding the entire expression as bodily or figurative requires context.

The connection with containing fear comes from the context and مِنَ الرَّهْبِ.

Is this the same command as the sign of the white hand?

The beginning of Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:32 tells Moses to put his hand into his garment opening so that it emerges white and unharmed.

وَاضْمُمْ إِلَيْكَ جَنَاحَكَ مِنَ الرَّهْبِ follows.

The commands may concern related or overlapping movements. One interpretation relates both to the hand’s movement, but their stated purposes differ; another reads drawing in the wing figuratively as self-command.

The first sentence explicitly mentions the hand emerging white; the second mentions fear.

Different purposes alone do not prove two separate bodily movements. Nor should their details be identified without examining the wording and interpretive possibilities.

The closing فَذَانِكَ بُرْهَانَانِ refers, in the story’s context, to the staff and hand as two proofs. Drawing in the wing against fear need not be a third proof.

For this root study, the essential point is that both commands draw on bringing close, while each command’s purpose and situation come from its own sentence and context.

5. Comparison with related roots

ض م م (ḍamma) compared with ج م ع (jamaʿa)

جمع concerns gathering people or things into a collection.

Ṭā Hā 20:64 uses أَجْمِعُوا كَيْدَكُمْ for settling upon or coordinating a plan. This is أَجْمَعَ, distinct in form from جَمَعَ, though in the same family. It illustrates the family’s application to nonphysical matters.

ضم foregrounds bringing something near another and placing them together.

Assembling a town’s inhabitants in one location may be جمع; bringing two groups together and joining them may be ضم.

The roots overlap in many usages but are not interchangeable in every context.

Ṭā Hā 20:22 emphasizes drawing a hand to the side rather than assembling scattered objects. ضم supplies a more specific image for that movement.

ض م م (ḍamma) compared with ق ب ض (qabaḍa)

قبض concerns grasping, taking possession, and in some usages, drawing in or closing something.

Al-Mulk 67:19 describes birds spreading and drawing in their wings.

This approaches the bodily image of ضم but has a different emphasis.

قبض foregrounds grasping or folding inward; ضم highlights bringing one thing close to another and joining components.

Someone may grasp something in a fist without drawing it to another object, or join two things without holding them in a fist.

In the commands to Moses, إِلَىٰ جَنَاحِكَ and إِلَيْكَ make direction an important part of the construction.

ض م م (ḍamma) compared with و ص ل (waṣala)

وصل concerns connection, continuity, and overcoming separation.

The meanings can approach one another, but the roots should not be identified.

Two things may be brought near without a lasting connection. Conversely, people may share kinship or a relationship while physically far apart.

ضم supplies an image of drawing close, whereas وصل may emphasize the relationship itself or its continuation.

ضم alone does not establish that a connection is permanent or inseparable.

ض م م (ḍamma) compared with ض م ن (ḍamina)

ض م ن is an independent root associated with containing, being within, and in some usages, guaranteeing or bearing responsibility.

Its shared first two consonants do not transfer guarantee or responsibility into ضم.

Attaching something to a collection does not by itself establish acceptance of legal responsibility for it.

Guaranteeing an obligation need not involve bringing two physical objects together.

These roots must remain distinct in their respective studies.

ض م م (ḍamma) compared with ض م ر (ḍamara)

ض م ر has a branch concerning loss of flesh and slenderness, and another concerning concealment.

A reduced object might be described as having its components closer together, but that shared image does not identify the roots.

ضم denotes bringing close or joining; bodily ضمور denotes leanness or becoming slender.

ضم should not be translated as becoming lean, nor ضامر as something joined to another.

6. Social and extended usages

Beyond bodily action, ضم can describe people, groups, or matters joining together.

When someone joins a group, انضمام occurs: a person formerly outside it enters a relationship with its members.

Joining does not inherently mean complete doctrinal agreement, unconditional obedience, or loss of individual identity.

Two groups gathering for a common purpose can likewise illustrate ضم. The root itself does not establish the alliance’s justice, legitimacy, equality, or moral worth.

One group may unite to help others and another to commit injustice. Both gather; their purpose and conduct determine the moral assessment.

These examples clarify the root’s semantic capacity but are not direct meanings of the two verses concerning Moses.

The Quranic commands use a bodily image; Al-Qaṣaṣ also admits an established figurative interpretation concerning composure. Wider social or philosophical conclusions require their own evidence.

7. Suggested translations for Forqan

Possible equivalents include bringing near, joining, drawing together, gathering, and attaching.

The construction determines the choice.

In Ṭā Hā 20:22, the phrase:

وَاضْمُمْ يَدَكَ إِلَىٰ جَنَاحِكَ

wa-ḍmum yadaka ilā janāḥika

Ṭā Hā 20:22

can be rendered:

“Draw your hand to your side.”

Or:

“Bring your hand in against your side.”

The first clearly conveys the direction and is natural in Persian. The second is defensible but may suggest folding the hand itself rather than moving it towards the side.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:32:

وَاضْمُمْ إِلَيْكَ جَنَاحَكَ مِنَ الرَّهْبِ

wa-ḍmum ilayka janāḥaka mina al-rahb

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:32

may be rendered:

“Draw your arm towards yourself against fear.”

Or, preserving the literal image of جناح:

“Draw your wing close to yourself against fear.”

If “wing” is retained, the reader needs an explanation of its bodily application to the arm or side here.

“Arm” is clearer in a concise, natural Persian translation. To preserve the original image, “wing” can be retained with an explanation.

“Bringing near” and “drawing together” express the root’s contribution in both verses.

“Embracing” is an attested contextual usage of ضم, but it is not appropriate to every occurrence. Firm pressure, calming, or making white should not be treated as inherent meanings in these commands: they require separate contextual support.

Other derivatives can be rendered according to their form: انضمام, joining; ضميمة, an attachment; مضموم, joined or gathered; and تضام, drawing or gathering together.

Word-by-word explanations should likewise distinguish each derivative’s construction and context.

8. Conclusion

ض م م concerns bringing one thing near another, placing components together, and joining them.

Early literary witnesses describe birds and herds gathering, people joining, and groups forming. They illustrate the range of usage, although the dates of all witnesses attributed to the pre-Islamic period are not independently established.

The Quran uses only وَاضْمُمْ, both times directly addressing Moses.

Ṭā Hā 20:22 commands him to draw his hand to his side so that it emerges white and unharmed. Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:32 commands him to draw his wing or arm towards himself against fear.

The underlying root meaning remains constant, while object, preposition, and context give the action a different image.

These usages do not make ضم mean producing a miracle, eliminating fear, affection, obedience, or permanent unity. Where relevant, such concepts come from other wording or the wider situation.

The root can be defined as:

ض م م: bringing one thing near another, gathering components, and placing them together so that distance decreases or a connection or association forms; without necessarily implying complete intermixture, fusion of identities, or permanent attachment.

This explains the bodily image of hand and arm in the Quranic commands, alongside wider Arabic usages of gathering and joining, without turning the result of one particular usage into the meaning of every derivative.
