Making firm, strengthening, and confirming a commitment

In Arabic, w-k-d denotes making something firm and strengthening it against weakening or coming apart. Wakkada means “made firm,” tawkīd means strengthening or confirmation, and muwakkad describes something firmly established or confirmed.

The root is used of physical objects as well as commitments and statements. Someone may secure an animal’s saddle, strengthen a covenant, or emphasize a statement to reduce uncertainty about their intention.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as tawkīdihā in Al-Naḥl 16:91. The passage concerns confirming oaths and a person’s responsibility to uphold an accepted commitment.

1. The root in early Arabic

Arabic lexicons record wakkada al-ʿahda for confirming a covenant and wakkada al-sarja for securely fastening a saddle.

Despite their different objects, these uses share a feature: something liable to loosen or come apart is made more secure.

In physical usage, wikād denotes a rope used to restrain cattle during milking. Wakāʾid denotes straps holding the parts of a saddle securely together.

For example, an improperly fastened camel saddle may shift or slip during movement. Tightening its straps makes it more stable.

The point here is securing the connections between existing parts, rather than adding something new to the saddle.

A poetic line attributed to Ḥumayd ibn Thawr al-Hilālī, who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, reads:

تَرَى الْعُلَيْفِيَّ عَلَيْهِ مُوكَدَا

tarā al-ʿulayfiyya ʿalayhi mūkadā

Translation: “You see the saddle securely fastened upon its back.”

Al-ʿulayfī refers here to a type of saddle or pack saddle; mūkadan describes it as securely fastened.

This witness illustrates the root’s physical use. Its transmitted wording varies, however: variants include muʾkadan with hamzah and another reading, mūfadan.

Ḥumayd ibn Thawr lived both before and after the advent of Islam. Reports place this verse in an account of his Islamic period; without independent evidence establishing its date, it cannot securely demonstrate pre-Qur’anic usage.

The independently datable evidence examined here does not establish the root’s earliest meaning before the Qur’an. Qur’anic tawkīd directly attests the confirmation of a commitment during the period of revelation. Lexicons also record physical uses, but these do not establish that fastening a saddle historically preceded confirming a covenant.

The semantic relationship between securely fastening something and confirming a covenant is clear, but it should not become an unsupported account of the root’s historical origin.

Classical Arabic records other uses as well.

Wakada al-amra refers to pursuing or working at something. Wakd may denote intention or purpose, and wukd activity or persistent effort. Mā zāla dhālika wukdī describes something a person continually pursues.

Wakada bi-al-makān is also recorded for staying or residing in a place.

These uses may be related through persistence and determined pursuit, but the evidence does not establish one historical image from which all of them developed.

The relevant Qur’anic sense is more specific: making an oath or commitment firm.

2. Forms and derivatives

Principal recorded forms include:

  • Wakada: in its recorded basic-form uses, pursued or intended something, or stayed in a place.
  • Wakd: intention, purpose, or pursuit of a task; meanings depend on context.
  • Wukd: activity and persistent effort.
  • Wakkada: made firm or strengthened.
  • Tawkīd: strengthening, confirming, or reinforcing.
  • Muwakkad: made firm or emphasized.
  • Awkada: strengthened or made firm.
  • Īkād: the verbal noun of awkada, meaning strengthening.
  • Tawakkada: became firm or strengthened.
  • Mutawakkid: in some uses, someone ready and determined to undertake a task.
  • Wikād: a rope or fastening cord.
  • Wakāʾid: fastening straps.
  • Muwākidah: of a she-camel, one that persists in moving onward.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an. The family’s sole Qur’anic form is tawkīdihā.

Tawkīd is the Form II verbal noun of wakkada. Here it denotes the act of making something firm.

Arabic also has taʾkīd, with hamzah, in the same sense. Some lexicographers preferred tawkīd in certain uses; others distinguished wāw for oaths from hamzah for contracts. Older accounts also associate the two forms with different regional varieties of Arabic.

Their uses nevertheless overlap considerably, so no absolute boundary applies to every occurrence.

The Qur’an’s actual form is tawkīd, with wāw. Taʾkīd, with hamzah, does not occur in the Qur’anic text and should not replace the attested form in a study of the root.

3. Qur’anic use: confirming an oath

Al-Naḥl 16:91

وَأَوْفُوا بِعَهْدِ اللَّهِ إِذَا عَاهَدْتُمْ وَلَا تَنْقُضُوا الْأَيْمَانَ بَعْدَ تَوْكِيدِهَا وَقَدْ جَعَلْتُمُ اللَّهَ عَلَيْكُمْ كَفِيلًا ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ يَعْلَمُ مَا تَفْعَلُونَ

wa-awfū bi-ʿahdi Allāhi idhā ʿāhadtum wa-lā tanquḍū al-aymāna baʿda tawkīdihā wa-qad jaʿaltumu Allāha ʿalaykum kafīlan inna Allāha yaʿlamu mā tafʿalūn

Al-Naḥl 16:91

Translation: “And fulfill the covenant of The God when you make a covenant, and do not break oaths after confirming them, when you have made The God a guarantor over you. Indeed, The God knows what you do.”

Several words concerning commitment occur together here, but each has a distinct semantic role.

ʿAhd concerns a covenant or commitment.

ʿĀhadtum expresses entering into a covenant.

Awfū commands fulfillment and complete discharge of the commitment.

Al-aymān is the plural of yamīn and means oaths in this context.

Tanquḍū concerns breaking or undoing something previously established and made firm.

Tawkīdihā expresses confirming those same oaths.

The combination concerns more than uttering a few words. A person accepts a commitment, confirms it, and becomes responsible for upholding it.

The structure of tawkīdihā

Tawkīd is a Form II verbal noun; the attached pronoun hā refers to al-aymān.

Arabic may refer to a nonhuman plural with a feminine singular pronoun. Here hā therefore refers to the oaths.

Baʿda tawkīdihā means after the oaths have been confirmed and made firm.

Confirmation applies to the oath itself. The expression does not require the person to have repeated it several times.

Emphasis in Arabic may involve repetition, but repetition is not a necessary component of w-k-d.

Someone may swear a serious oath only once, consciously and with a clear intention. Conversely, someone may repeat a sentence many times without intending a real commitment.

Baʿda tawkīdihā alone specifies neither a number of repetitions nor a fixed ceremony for confirming an oath.

Its principal meaning is making the commitment firm.

Confirming and fulfilling are distinct

The verse first commands fulfillment of the covenant, then prohibits breaking confirmed oaths.

The actions are related but distinct.

Suppose two people agree to undertake a task. One confirms their commitment with a serious oath.

The oath makes the commitment more firmly declared; the agreement has not yet been carried out.

When the time for performance comes, the person must fulfill it.

Tawkīd therefore concerns confirming the commitment, while wafāʾ concerns carrying it out and discharging it.

Someone may swear a very solemn oath and later break it. Firm wording cannot substitute for practical faithfulness.

The Qur’an addresses this gap between commitment and conduct: a confirmed oath must not gain another person’s trust only to be abandoned when action is required.

“When you have made The God a guarantor over you”

The next clause underscores the responsibility attached to the oath.

Those swearing in this context have made The God a guarantor over them. Their commitment therefore involves more than their human counterpart: The God’s name has also been invoked.

Kafīl belongs to k-f-l, not w-k-d. Its meaning should not be included in the lexical definition of tawkīd.

Nor does an oath compel The God to do something contrary to His will. Responsibility rests on those who accepted the commitment and invoked Him as witness and guarantor.

The verse’s ending recalls that responsibility: The God knows what people do.

The significance of an oath is thus not confined to strong wording or invoking The God’s name. Conduct after accepting the commitment is also subject to accountability.

4. Tawkīd and naqḍ: making firm and undoing

The relationship between tawkīd and naqḍ is particularly important here.

Tawkīd concerns making firm; naqḍ concerns undoing or breaking something previously bound or established.

Al-Naḥl 16:92 illustrates this contrast through a woman who unravels her spun thread after it has become strong.

Thread painstakingly spun has acquired cohesion and strength. Unravelling it destroys the result of the earlier effort.

The image fits confirming a covenant and then breaking it.

People create trust through covenants and oaths. When one party deliberately breaks a confirmed covenant, they do more than retract an earlier sentence: they damage the trust built upon it.

For example, two groups may agree to cooperate, maintain security, or respect one another’s rights. If one becomes more powerful and uses the covenant deceptively before breaking it, the damage extends beyond the agreement’s wording to the security and trust it created.

Al-Naḥl 16:92 identifies this danger and prohibits using oaths deceptively because one group is more numerous or advantaged than another.

Al-Naḥl 16:94 continues the connection. It again prohibits deceptive use of oaths and describes slipping after firmness as a consequence.

Al-Naḥl 16:95 also prohibits selling The God’s covenant for a small price.

These verses show that the firmness of a commitment is not merely verbal. Gaining another person’s trust through an oath entails ethical and social responsibility.

Deception, betrayal, and covenant-breaking are not all contained in w-k-d itself. They arise from human conduct and the verses’ context.

Tawkīd is the act of confirming; subsequent faithfulness or betrayal calls for a separate ethical judgment.

5. Must every oath be kept in every circumstance?

Al-Naḥl 16:91 prohibits breaking confirmed oaths, but other oath passages must also be considered to understand the command’s scope.

Al-Baqarah 2:224 warns against making The God’s name and one’s oaths an obstacle to doing good, God-consciousness, and reconciliation between people.

An oath should therefore not become an excuse for abandoning what is right.

Al-Baqarah 2:225 distinguishes unintentional oaths from what the heart has earned through intention and action.

Al-Māʾidah 5:89 makes the distinction more explicit: it contrasts idle or unintentional oaths with oaths deliberately contracted as commitments, and sets out expiation in connection with accountable oaths.

Tawkīdihā therefore does not make every oath-shaped utterance an unchangeable obligation in every circumstance.

Nor does expiation authorize using commitments to deceive. Someone cannot enter a covenant in The God’s name, endanger another party’s rights through it, and assume that paying expiation removes every consequence of the breach without addressing responsibility to that person.

A personal oath, a commitment creating rights for another person, and an agreement contrary to justice or goodness must be distinguished. Their rulings require consideration of the relevant passages together, not deduction from a single word.

The lexical meaning of tawkīd remains confirmation throughout. Whether the oath’s object is right or wrong, and how one should act, are separate questions.

6. Lexical confirmation and grammatical emphasis

In contemporary Persian and Arabic, “emphasis” often means highlighting a statement or repeating it to remove uncertainty.

Al-tawkīd is also an established topic in Arabic grammar.

Grammarians distinguish verbal emphasis, which may repeat a word or phrase, from semantic emphasis using words such as nafs, ʿayn, kull, and jamīʿ.

These constructions clarify a statement’s scope or remove possible ambiguity.

The grammatical term relates to strengthening but is not the direct meaning of tawkīdihā in Al-Naḥl 16:91.

The verse concerns oaths and commitments, rather than the grammatical emphasis of a sentence.

Someone may repeat words for emphasis without making any covenant or oath. Conversely, a person may accept a clear, firm commitment without repeating the wording several times.

Emphasizing a statement and confirming a commitment are related but distinct uses.

Modern “emphasis” may also mean insisting or giving a firm instruction. Such usage should not replace the Qur’anic sense without regard to its object.

7. Comparison with related roots

W-k-d and w-th-q: confirmation and a firm bond

W-th-q concerns firmness, confidence, and a secure bond. Mīthāq belongs to this family and denotes a firm covenant.

Qur’anic w-k-d emphasizes the act of confirming an oath; w-th-q can express firmness itself, a trustworthy bond, or a firm covenant.

The families overlap in matters of commitment, but their derivatives are not all synonymous.

Al-Baqarah 2:27 describes breaking a covenant after its confirmation, while Al-Raʿd 13:20 describes those who fulfill the covenant and do not break the bond. These passages relate conceptually to Al-Naḥl 16:91, but mīthāq belongs to w-th-q, not w-k-d.

Study of w-th-q

W-k-d and ʿ-q-d: tying and confirming

Physically, ʿaqd concerns tying a knot; socially, it concerns establishing a covenant or commitment.

Tawkīd concerns strengthening something, which may be a covenant, contract, or oath.

Al-Māʾidah 5:89 discusses deliberately contracted oaths. This relates to Al-Naḥl 16:91, without making ʿaqd and tawkīd identical.

ʿAqd foregrounds establishing a bond or commitment; tawkīd foregrounds making it firm.

In practice, the actions may coincide. Someone can make a covenant and confirm its seriousness at the same time. Their coincidence does not erase the difference between the roots.

Study of ʿ-q-d

W-k-d and n-q-ḍ: strengthening and undoing

Physically, naqḍ concerns undoing something tied or spun; socially, it concerns breaking a covenant.

Tawkīd and naqḍ are contrasted in Al-Naḥl 16:91.

One makes the bond firm; the other undoes it.

This does not make naqḍ exclusively the opposite of tawkīd in every use. Something established without special confirmation may still be broken or undone.

Study of n-q-ḍ

W-k-d and w-f-y: confirming and fully discharging

Wafāʾ concerns fulfilling and completely discharging a commitment.

Tawkīd concerns making it firm.

Both concepts appear in Al-Naḥl 16:91: people must fulfill the covenant and refrain from breaking confirmed oaths.

Someone may strengthen a commitment by oath yet fail to fulfill it. Confirmation alone therefore does not establish practical faithfulness.

Study of w-f-y

For a fuller examination of ʿahd, mīthāq, ʿaqd, and wafāʾ, see Forqan’s study “Covenant in the Qur’an: The Forgotten Moral Pillar.”

W-k-d and w-k-l: confirmation and entrusting responsibility

These roots resemble one another in writing but differ in meaning.

W-k-l concerns entrusting an affair, relying on someone, and delegating responsibility. Tawakkul belongs to that family.

W-k-d concerns making firm and strengthening.

Someone may confirm a commitment and then entrust a task to another, but these are distinct actions.

Tawkīd and tawakkul should not be assigned to the same root.

W-k-d and sh-d-d: strengthening and intensity

Shadd covers a broad field of fastening tightly, becoming hard, and increasing in intensity.

The principal use of w-k-d also concerns strengthening.

The families may approach one another in physical settings such as securing saddle straps. Yet shiddah is not equivalent to tawkīd in every use.

The intensity of pain, severity of an event, or strength of a person is not necessarily confirmation.

Al-Naḥl 16:91 does not concern increased intensity in general; it concerns oaths and confirming a specific commitment.

8. Suggested translations for Forqan

For physical wakkada, “secured” or “made firm” is suitable.

For tawkīd in contexts of covenants and oaths, “confirmation,” “making firm,” and sometimes “reinforcement” are natural equivalents.

For muwakkad, “confirmed,” “strengthened,” or “emphasized” is suitable.

For tawakkada, “became firm” or “gained strength” is suggested.

For wikād, “fastening rope” or “securing cord” is appropriate.

For wakd in the field of intention and effort, “purpose,” “effort,” or “persistent pursuit” may fit the context. This branch should not be transferred to tawkīdihā in the verse.

Taʾkīd, the form with hamzah familiar in classical Arabic and modern Persian, may naturally mean “emphasis.” When quoting and analyzing the Qur’anic word, however, its actual form tawkīd must be preserved.

In Al-Naḥl 16:91, the suggested rendering of baʿda tawkīdihā is:

“After confirming them.”

This conveys the act of making firm and allows readers to identify the oaths as the pronoun’s referent.

“After making them firm” is also accurate; the Persian wording for establishing firmly is particularly natural with covenants and oaths.

“After repeating them several times” is unsuitable because the verse specifies no number of repetitions.

“After giving them verbal emphasis” likewise imports grammatical terminology without justification.

The suggested complete translation is:

“And fulfill the covenant of The God when you make a covenant, and do not break oaths after confirming them, when you have made The God a guarantor over you. Indeed, The God knows what you do.”

9. Conclusion

Arabic w-k-d concerns strengthening and making firm. It applies to fastening saddles and equipment, confirming covenants, and reinforcing statements.

Lexicons also record intention, persistent effort, and residence within this family. Their semantic connections deserve examination, but insufficient evidence prevents asserting that all arose from one historically established original meaning.

The Qur’an’s sole form is tawkīdihā in Al-Naḥl 16:91. It concerns confirming oaths alongside the command to fulfill covenants and the prohibition on breaking oaths.

Confirmation here requires neither mandatory repetition nor specified additional ceremonies. It means making an accepted commitment firm.

Its distinction from fulfillment and breach is clear: tawkīd confirms a commitment, wafāʾ fulfills it, and naqḍ breaks the established bond.

In Al-Naḥl, this has broad social significance. Oaths and covenants must not gain people’s trust only to be abandoned for greater advantage, superiority, or power.

W-k-d itself does not mean faithfulness, justice, or goodness. A person may confirm a commitment and later betray it. Conduct is evaluated through the commitment’s content, intention, and actual performance.

The principal definition of w-k-d is strengthening something so its bond or standing becomes firmer and less liable to weaken. Qur’anic tawkīdihā denotes confirming oaths, highlighting responsibility for an accepted covenant without containing every ruling concerning fulfillment, breach, or expiation.
