Encompassing, gathering, and the left side: from early Arabic to right and left in the Quran

Early Arabic SH M L has two important branches: encompassing, covering, and gathering; and shimāl, the left side, opposite yamīn, the right.

In the first branch, shamila describes something including people or things: a garment covering the body, a condition affecting a group, or a womb containing a fetus. Shaml also concerns the togetherness of a group’s members or parts.

In the second, shimāl means the left hand or side. It identifies bodily position, spatial direction, or someone’s place. Shamāʾil is one of its plurals.

Other uses include the north wind, coverings and garments, and shimāl as disposition or character. Some senses are related, but should not all be forced into a single original image.

The Quran uses ishtamalat, shamāʾil, and shimāl. Ishtamalat describes what animal wombs contain; shamāʾil refers to left sides; shimāl describes position, the left hand, and a group in the Hereafter.

Lexical meaning must be distinguished from contextual value. Being left does not itself mean sin, misfortune, or error; encompassing does not always mean protection, ownership, or kindness.

1. The root in early Arabic

Encompassing and extending over a group

Shamilahumu al-amru describes a condition encompassing an entire group.

Khayrun shāmilun likewise describes a good whose reach includes a range of people or things.

The sense is not merely collecting things at one point, but including them within the reach of a condition or matter.

If an event affects several families, its effects may be said to include them. They need not be physically gathered in one place; all fall within its effects.

Likewise, when a good reaches a group, shumūl concerns their inclusion within its scope.

Early dictionaries record shamala and shamila with differences in vocalization and usage, sometimes noting rarity or disagreement. A shared sense does not justify imposing one unexamined form or morphological rule on every verb.

Covering, garments, and ishtimāl

One physical application concerns covering the body with a garment.

Shamlah denotes a covering of wool or animal hair wrapped around the body. Mishmal and mishmalah likewise refer to bodily coverings in established Arabic uses.

Ishtamala bi-thawbihi means that someone wrapped himself in his garment.

Encompassing is tangible here: the garment rests on and surrounds parts of the body.

Shimāl also names a covering fastened over an animal’s udder, resembling a bag enclosing it.

Shamala al-shāta can accordingly describe fastening such a covering over a sheep’s udder.

Encompassing is thus not only abstract; it appears in clothing, coverings, and everyday equipment.

These similarities do not prove that every branch, including the word for the left side, arose from dressing in a garment.

Shaml: togetherness and gathered parts

Shaml concerns the togetherness of people or affairs.

Jamaʿa Allāhu shamlahum means that The God reunited them or restored their scattered affairs to order.

Tafarraqa shamluhum and farraqa shamlahum instead concern the dispersal or disruption of their group or affairs.

This need not be physical gathering. A family can live together with disordered relationships, while people living apart can retain shared bonds and organization.

Jamʿ shaml may therefore involve reunion, restoring broken ties, or ordering scattered affairs.

It relates to encompassing because dispersed parts rejoin a common whole. But shaml does not invariably mean moral unity, agreement in belief, or complete sameness.

The Quran does not use the noun shaml in this social sense, although ishtamalat belongs to the field of encompassing.

Shimāl: the left hand and side

The other branch concerns shimāl, left, opposite yamīn, right.

Shimāl can name a person’s left hand or the left side of a setting. Spatial orientation depends on the observer or reference point.

Something may stand to the left of someone facing a gate; when that person turns, the same object may lie on another side relative to them.

Here shimāl is a relative direction. It should not automatically be translated as geographic north.

Recorded plurals include ashmul, shumul, and shamāʾil. The Quran uses shamāʾil for left sides.

The north wind in pre-Islamic poetry

Al-shamāl names a wind blowing from the north; variants include al-shamʾal and al-shaʾmal.

A prominent witness occurs in the Muʿallaqah attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays:

فَتُوضِحَ فَالْمِقْرَاةِ لَمْ يَعْفُ رَسْمُهَا

fa-tūḍiḥa fa-l-miqrāti lam yaʿfu rasmuhā

لِمَا نَسَجَتْهَا مِنْ جَنُوبٍ وَشَمْأَلِ

li-mā nasajathā min janūbin wa-shamʾali

Translation:

“Then at Tūḍiḥ and al-Miqrāh, its traces have not vanished, for south and north winds have woven across them.”

Janūb and shamʾal are winds from different directions. The poet describes their passage over a former dwelling place.

One explanation in the early commentaries is that winds from opposite directions alternately cover traces with sand and uncover them, preventing their complete disappearance. This is an interpretation of the image, not the only grammatical reading of the line.

Shamʾal here names the north wind, not a person’s left hand.

The line belongs to the familiar transmission of the Muʿallaqah and importantly attests the north-wind name in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period. It does not independently date the line or establish the historical sequence of the wind’s different names.

Some lexical explanations connect north with left in a particular orientation. That depends on where one faces and does not make geographic north the left side of every observer.

The Quran does not use shimāl to mean the north wind.

Shimāl and shamāʾil as disposition

Another use of shimāl concerns a person’s temperament or character; its plural is also shamāʾil.

A verse attributed to Labīd ibn Rabīʿah, who lived before and after the rise of Islam, reads:

هُمُ قَوْمِي وَقَدْ أَنْكَرْتُ مِنْهُمْ

humu qawmī wa-qad ankartu minhum

شَمَائِلَ بَدَّلُوهَا مِنْ شِمَالِي

shamāʾila baddalūhā min shimālī

Translation:

“They are my people, yet I disapprove of traits in them that have replaced the disposition I know as my own.”

Shamāʾil here concerns people’s qualities and behavior, not the left side.

The poet is displeased with their changed disposition and distinguishes their conduct from his own.

This attests shimāl and shamāʾil as words for temperament and character.

Labīd lived through both periods, but this study has not established this line’s precise date. It is evidence of early usage without proving this particular line predates the Quran.

The same sense is known in expressions such as ḥusn al-shamāʾil and karīm al-shamāʾil, referring to good or noble traits.

However, shamāʾil does not mean moral traits in either of its two Quranic occurrences. Both contexts clearly concern left sides.

The character-related meaning should not be imported into those verses.

2. Core meanings and Quranic forms

The root occurs 12 times in 11 Quranic verses, in three principal forms:

Ishtamalat, from form VIII ishtamala, means encompassed; shamāʾil means left sides; shimāl means left or the left hand.

Ishtamalat is feminine singular perfect. In both occurrences its subject is arḥāmu, “wombs”; feminine singular agreement with this nonhuman plural is natural Arabic grammar.

Shamāʾil is a broken plural, appearing as shamāʾilihim and al-shamāʾili.

Shimāl can identify a left side or hand, or occur within a title for a group associated with that side.

The two Quranic semantic branches should remain distinct:

In ishtimāl, the core is containing or encompassing something within a boundary or covering.

In shimāl, the core is the left side or hand, opposite the right.

Connections may be proposed for some historical uses, but a demonstrated common derivation or image need not be imposed on all of them.

Shimāl should especially not be defined solely through encompassing. In the passages contrasting right and left, direction is clear in its own right.

3. Ishtimāl: containment in the womb

Al-Anʿām 6:143 states:

أَمَّا اشْتَمَلَتْ عَلَيْهِ أَرْحَامُ الْأُنْثَيَيْنِ

am-mā ishtamalat ʿalayhi arḥāmu al-unthayayni

Al-Anʿām 6:143

Translation:

“Or what the wombs of the two females contain?”

The expression is repeated in Al-Anʿām 6:144.

The first verse concerns sheep and goats; the second concerns camels and cattle. The Quran questions claims that certain animals are forbidden.

Each asks which category is supposedly forbidden: males, females, or what female wombs contain.

The meaning of ishtamalat

Here ishtamalat is followed by ʿalayhi.

Arḥāmu, the female animals’ wombs, is the subject. Mā refers to what they contain. In ammā, am, “or,” and relative mā are joined; it is not the conditional expression ammā.

In the reproductive setting, this refers to the fetus or contents of the womb.

Ishtimāl expresses physical containment: the womb holds the fetus within it.

It is semantically related to a covering surrounding the body, but the verse refers to actual animal wombs without explicitly comparing them to garments.

Ishtimāl and ḥaml

The two expressions can overlap in discussing pregnancy, but their perspectives differ.

Ḥaml can mean lifting, carrying, or being pregnant.

Ishtimāl here emphasizes containment within a boundary.

“What the wombs contain” closely preserves the construction. “What the wombs carry” conveys the general meaning but less clearly distinguishes the verbs.

Its place in the verses’ argument

Al-Anʿām 6:143 and Al-Anʿām 6:144 do more than describe wombs biologically. Ishtimāl participates in an argument about claims of prohibition.

Those making such claims are asked to identify the exact category affected and the knowledge supporting their attribution to The God.

Al-Anʿām 6:143 ends:

نَبِّئُونِي بِعِلْمٍ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ صَادِقِينَ

nabbiʾūnī bi-ʿilmin in kuntum ṣādiqīn

Al-Anʿām 6:143

Translation:

“Inform me with knowledge, if you are truthful.”

Al-Anʿām 6:144 also questions whether they witnessed the divine command and raises fabrication against The God.

The womb expression thus contributes to an argument requiring evidence for a claimed prohibition.

Ishtamalat does not itself mean forbidden, permitted, or a forbidden fetus. It expresses containment; prohibition is questioned by the full sentence.

4. Shimāl and shamāʾil: left sides in the perceptible world

Right and left in Iblīs’s speech

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:17, Iblīs says:

ثُمَّ لَآتِيَنَّهُمْ مِنْ بَيْنِ أَيْدِيهِمْ وَمِنْ خَلْفِهِمْ وَعَنْ أَيْمَانِهِمْ وَعَنْ شَمَائِلِهِمْ

thumma la-ātiyannahum min bayni aydīhim wa-min khalfihim wa-ʿan aymānihim wa-ʿan shamāʾilihim

Al-Aʿrāf 7:17

Translation:

“Then I will surely approach them from before them, from behind them, from their right sides and their left sides.”

Four directions or aspects are named: front, back, right, and left.

Shamāʾilihim is the plural of shimāl, contrasted with aymānihim.

The combination depicts Iblīs’s broad efforts to approach and mislead people.

Shamāʾil itself does not make the left inherently a place of evil, sin, or temptation.

He also approaches from the right. The directions themselves therefore do not determine the moral character of his conduct.

Without evidence, each direction should not be assigned a specific sin, stage of life, or separate domain of the unseen.

Shamāʾil means left sides; the breadth of the threat emerges from all four directions together.

Shadows turning right and left

Al-Naḥl 16:48 states:

أَوَلَمْ يَرَوْا إِلَىٰ مَا خَلَقَ اللَّهُ مِنْ شَيْءٍ يَتَفَيَّأُ ظِلَالُهُ عَنِ الْيَمِينِ وَالشَّمَائِلِ سُجَّدًا لِلَّهِ وَهُمْ دَاخِرُونَ

a-wa-lam yaraw ilā mā khalaqa allāhu min shayʾin yatafayyaʾu ẓilāluhu ʿani al-yamīni wa-l-shamāʾili sujjadan li-llāhi wa-hum dākhirūn

Al-Naḥl 16:48

Translation:

“Have they not considered what The God created, whose shadows turn to the right and left, prostrating to The God in humility?”

Al-shamāʾili concerns left sides, contrasted with al-yamīn.

The subject is shadows’ movement and changing position.

As the sun’s apparent position relative to objects changes, shadows change direction and size, visibly throughout the day.

Yatafayyaʾu expresses their turning or returning; shamāʾil still means left sides.

Singular al-yamīn beside plural al-shamāʾil does not prove that right is one fixed direction while left comprises separate directions. The construction requires linguistic and contextual analysis.

The verse relates this movement to prostration and humility before The God. Those meanings come from separate words and constructions, not SH M L itself.

Right and left in the cave narrative

Al-Kahf 18:17 describes the sun’s position relative to the cave:

وَإِذَا غَرَبَتْ تَقْرِضُهُمْ ذَاتَ الشِّمَالِ

wa-idhā gharabat taqriḍuhum dhāta al-shimāl

Al-Kahf 18:17

Translation:

“And when it set, it passed them on the left.”

The same verse uses dhāta al-yamīn for sunrise.

Shimāl therefore identifies left relative to the cave and its occupants.

It should not automatically become geographic north. Precise orientation requires knowing the positions of observer, cave, and entrance.

The verse does not give the cave’s geographical coordinates.

Al-Kahf 18:18 also states:

وَنُقَلِّبُهُمْ ذَاتَ الْيَمِينِ وَذَاتَ الشِّمَالِ

wa-nuqallibuhum dhāta al-yamīni wa-dhāta al-shimāl

Al-Kahf 18:18

Translation:

“And We turn them to the right and to the left.”

Right and left here concern the directions in which their bodies are turned.

The previous verse described the sun relative to the cave; this expression describes turning the occupants themselves.

Even adjacent verses thus use shimāl in different relationships: the sun’s observed passage and the turning of bodies.

The basic meaning remains the same, while the frame of reference comes from context.

Two gardens, right and left

Sabaʾ 34:15 states:

لَقَدْ كَانَ لِسَبَإٍ فِي مَسْكَنِهِمْ آيَةٌ جَنَّتَانِ عَنْ يَمِينٍ وَشِمَالٍ

la-qad kāna li-sabaʾin fī maskanihim āyatun jannatāni ʿan yamīnin wa-shimāl

Sabaʾ 34:15

Translation:

“For Sheba there was a sign in their dwelling place: two gardens, on the right and left.”

Shimālin names the left side opposite yamīnin.

Unlike the judgment setting, left carries no blame here: one garden lies on that side.

This clearly shows that shimāl is not inherently negative in Quranic language.

The gardens on either side belong to the people’s blessings. Their positive value comes from their being provisions and from the context, not their direction.

Right and left in receiving and recording deeds

Qāf 50:17 states:

إِذْ يَتَلَقَّى الْمُتَلَقِّيَانِ عَنِ الْيَمِينِ وَعَنِ الشِّمَالِ قَعِيدٌ

idh yatalaqqā al-mutalaqqiyāni ʿani al-yamīni wa-ʿani al-shimāli qaʿīd

Qāf 50:17

Translation:

“When the two receivers receive, seated on the right and on the left.”

Two receivers are described on two sides.

Al-shimāl identifies one side. Qāf 50:18 then mentions a ready watcher in relation to human speech.

Shimāl does not itself mean recording sin, evil, or punishment. Nor does it determine each receiver’s precise task or the manner of recording.

Those questions require the wider passages about recording and judgment.

Al-Maʿārij 70:37 also states:

عَنِ الْيَمِينِ وَعَنِ الشِّمَالِ عِزِينَ

ʿani al-yamīni wa-ʿani al-shimāli ʿizīn

Al-Maʿārij 70:37

Translation:

“On the right and on the left, in groups.”

It concerns people gathered in separate groups around the addressee.

ʿIzīn expresses their grouping; yamīn and shimāl express their positions.

Shimāl again means the left side, not a name for people of the fire or an intrinsically blameworthy direction.

5. The companions of the left: a title in the Hereafter

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:41 states:

وَأَصْحَابُ الشِّمَالِ مَا أَصْحَابُ الشِّمَالِ

wa-aṣḥābu al-shimāli mā aṣḥābu al-shimāl

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:41

Translation:

“And the companions of the left—what of the companions of the left!”

Aṣḥābu al-shimāl identifies a group in the Hereafter.

The surrounding surah helps explain that title.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:27 introduces the companions of the right; the following verses describe their provisions and outcome.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:41 introduces the companions of the left; Al-Wāqiʿah 56:42, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:43, and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:44 describe their painful condition.

Right and left thus distinguish groups with different outcomes in this setting.

But this use does not turn the lexical meaning of shimāl into wickedness or sin. It still concerns left; the group’s title and evaluation arise from the scene and the descriptions of conduct and outcome.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:45 and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:46 describe their previous life of indulgent luxury and persistence in great sin. Mutrafīn describes luxury or indulgence; the judgment must be read with the accompanying conduct, rather than treating any comfort as sin.

Their blame therefore concerns conduct and outcome, not a spatial position itself.

Receiving the record in the left hand

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:25 states:

وَأَمَّا مَنْ أُوتِيَ كِتَابَهُ بِشِمَالِهِ فَيَقُولُ يَا لَيْتَنِي لَمْ أُوتَ كِتَابِيَهْ

wa-ammā man ūtiya kitābahu bi-shimālihi fa-yaqūlu yā laytanī lam ūta kitābiyah

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:25

Translation:

“As for the one given his record in his left hand, he says: Would that I had not been given my record!”

Bi-shimālihi identifies the person’s left hand.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:19 introduces the one given his record in his right hand.

This makes the contrast between right and left explicit in judgment.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:41 gives a group title; Al-Ḥāqqah 69:25 describes receiving the record in the left hand as an event.

The uses are related but should not automatically be made complete definitions of each other. One classifies a group in the Hereafter; the other identifies a person’s left hand in receiving the record.

Does being left imply sin?

Different cultures sometimes assign different values to right and left, associating right with good fortune and left with ill fortune.

Such associations should not replace the lexical definition of shimāl in the Quran.

The same word appearing in the blameworthy group’s title in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:41 names the side of a blessed garden in Sabaʾ 34:15.

Turning the cave occupants left in Al-Kahf 18:18 likewise carries no blame.

The negative value of the companions of the left comes from judgment and their outcome, not the direction itself.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:9 also uses aṣḥābu al-mashʾamah, from SH ʾ M. It should not be conflated with SH M L, although the two titles concern an unhappy outcome within the surah’s classification.

Shared reference does not make their roots or lexical meanings identical.

6. Comparison with related roots

SH M L and ʿ M M

In its encompassing branch, SH M L concerns including things or people within something’s scope.

ʿ M M likewise concerns generality and extensive coverage.

The roots overlap: an event may include a group or affect it generally.

This does not make every derivative in both families identical.

Wrapping in clothing and containment in the womb give SH M L a particular image of surrounding or covering. ʿ M M retains its own range of generality and extension.

SH M L and J M ʿ

Jamʿ concerns bringing together things that may be separate or scattered.

Shaml as encompassing concerns the scope within which people or matters fall.

Jamʿ shaml brings both together: gathering what has scattered and restoring a whole whose ties have broken.

It would be too rigid to confine jamʿ always to movement from dispersal to assembly, and shaml always to surrounding. They overlap; exact distinctions depend on construction.

SH M L and Ḥ W Y

Ḥ W Y also concerns containing, holding, and gathering within a space.

In Al-Anʿām 6:143 and Al-Anʿām 6:144, ishtamalat ʿalayhi arḥāmu concerns fetal containment in wombs.

“Holding within” can explain the meaning. The similarity does not make all uses of ishtimāl and Ḥ W Y interchangeable.

Forqan’s study should preserve the image of encompassing in ishtimāl while choosing a contextually natural equivalent.

SH M L and Y M N

Quranic spatial uses contrast shimāl with yamīn.

Yamīn can mean right hand or side; shimāl means left hand or side.

Many passages use them without independent moral values, including the cave narrative and Sheba’s gardens.

In the Hereafter, they acquire a particular significance in judgment and group classification. That evaluation comes from reward and punishment in context.

Just as yamīn does not always mean goodness or felicity, shimāl does not always mean misfortune or sin.

SH M L and SH ʾ M

Shuʾm concerns ill fortune and an unhappy outcome.

Shimāl directly means the left side.

Linguistic and cultural traditions sometimes bring the fields close, but cultural association or orientation does not establish identical root meanings.

The Quranic titles aṣḥābu al-shimāl and aṣḥābu al-mashʾamah should be studied with their distinct roots preserved.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

For ishtimāl, natural equivalents include “encompass,” “contain,” “hold within,” and, where appropriate, “include.”

For Al-Anʿām 6:143 and Al-Anʿām 6:144, a suitable rendering is:

“Or what the wombs of the two females contain?”

It preserves containment and the Arabic construction without adding an interpretive identification.

For spatial shimāl, “left,” “left side,” or “to the left” is suitable. Where a bodily member is meant, “left hand” is more natural.

Shamāʾil in Al-Aʿrāf 7:17 can be rendered “their left sides”; in Al-Naḥl 16:48, simply “left” may read more smoothly.

Aṣḥābu al-shimāl in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:41 can be “companions of the left” or “the group of the left.” Keeping “left” preserves the title more directly than substituting “people of the fire,” whose outcome becomes clear in subsequent verses.

Bi-shimālihi in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:25 naturally means “in his left hand.”

The north wind and human disposition remain valid Arabic senses in their own settings, but should not be imported into the directional verses.

One fixed equivalent is unnecessary across the family. Ishtimāl and shimāl represent distinct Quranic branches requiring contextual translations. These proposals do not alter installed verse translations.

Conclusion

SH M L is a branching Arabic family whose two important fields are encompassing and the left side.

Shaml and ishtimāl concern surrounding, coverage, and containment: a garment around the body, a condition including a group, or a womb containing a fetus. Shaml also concerns a group’s togetherness and ties.

Shimāl means the left hand or side; Quranic shamāʾil is the plural referring to that direction.

Other Arabic uses include the north wind and human character. The witnesses attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays and Labīd help document them without establishing the precise historical origins and relationships of every branch.

Ishtamalat in Al-Anʿām 6:143 and Al-Anʿām 6:144 concerns womb containment. Shamāʾil in Al-Aʿrāf 7:17 and Al-Naḥl 16:48 means left sides. Shimāl also appears in the cave narrative, Sheba’s gardens, the receivers’ positions, surrounding groups, and scenes of judgment.

The direction is not inherently positive or negative. Sabaʾ 34:15 places a blessed garden on the left, while Al-Wāqiʿah 56:41 names a group with an unhappy outcome. Evaluation arises from context.

The Quranic definition therefore has two branches:

SH M L in ishtimāl expresses encompassing or containing within a boundary or covering; in shimāl it names the left hand or side opposite the right.

Both meanings should remain distinct in Forqan’s translation and study. Proposed historical connections must not replace the clear usage in each verse.
