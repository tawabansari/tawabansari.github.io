Qur’anic and lexical forms

From languor and neglected fidelity to covenant-breaking and betrayal of trust

The Arabic root خ ت ر concerns covenant-breaking, betrayal, and deceit. In ethical usage, khatr describes abandoning fidelity or an obligation one should uphold and betraying another person. Khattār describes someone characterized by such betrayal and covenant-breaking.

Early lexicons also record another meaning: languor, diminished capacity, and bodily strength subsiding. Ibn Fāris treats this as the root’s basic principle and connects covenant-breaking to it: a covenant-breaker fails to carry out an obligation and abandons fidelity.

This semantic connection is understandable, but bodily weakness and moral betrayal are different. Someone may become weak through illness or fatigue without betraying anyone. Conversely, covenant-breaking can be deliberate and carried out with strength and planning.

Early Arabic recognized khatr as a word for perfidy and betrayal. Some sources describe it as an especially grave or ugly form of perfidy. Poetic witnesses likewise apply khattār to a treacherous, unfaithful person.

The Qur’an uses this root only once, in Luqmān 31:32. People facing overwhelming waves call upon The God with sincere devotion, but after rescue and arrival on land their responses differ. The verse ends by associating denial of divine signs with خَتَّارٍ كَفُورٍ.

This places covenant-breaking within the human relationship with The God and the contrast between conduct in danger and after deliverance. Nevertheless, the verse records no particular spoken oath or covenant from those rescued. An unstated commitment should therefore not be attributed to them as an explicitly reported event.

1. Forms and semantic core

The Qur’an uses خ ت ر only as خَتَّارٍ, in Luqmān 31:32.

Khattār follows the intensive pattern فَعَّال. This construction can express a trait’s intensity, prominence, or recurrence.

Important Arabic forms include:

  • خَتَرَ ـ يَخْتِرُ / يَخْتُرُ: He betrayed someone, broke a covenant, or acted perfidiously.
  • خَتْر: Covenant-breaking, perfidy, and betrayal; خَتَر: in the bodily branch, languor and weakness resembling khadar.
  • خُتُور: Another recorded verbal noun of khatara.
  • خَاتِر: Someone who betrays or breaks a covenant.
  • خَتَّار: A highly treacherous person, or someone prominently characterized by covenant-breaking.
  • خَتِير and خَتُور: Other descriptions of a treacherous or covenant-breaking person.
  • خِتِّير: Another intensive description of someone given to perfidy.
  • خَتِرَ: In one usage, he became languid or weak through illness, drink, or medicine.
  • خَتَّرَ: It caused corruption, languor, or impairment in someone’s condition.
  • تَخَتَّرَ: He became languid or listless, or walked slowly and sluggishly.

Lexicons recognize these forms, but only خَتَّارٍ occurs in the Qur’an.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the sole occurrence, Luqmān 31:32, word twenty-one

The root’s fundamental meaning

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris writes:

الْخَاءُ وَالتَّاءُ وَالرَّاءُ أَصْلٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى تَوَانٍ وَفُتُورٍ

al-khāʾu wa-al-tāʾu wa-al-rāʾu aṣlun yadullu ʿalā tawānin wa-futūrin

That is, the root signifies languor, slowness, and diminished force.

He then describes sluggish walking and connects khatr, perfidy, with the same principle.

His explanation is that a person betraying another falls short of fidelity to an obligation.

Lisān al-ʿArab, by contrast, directly explains ethical khatr through perfidy and deception, recording views that identify it with deception itself or the ugliest perfidy.

These accounts can be read together without making them identical.

Ibn Fāris highlights the relationship between languor and abandoning fidelity.

Explanations concerning perfidy emphasize someone’s conduct toward obligations and other people’s trust.

Someone entrusted with property who knowingly abuses its owner’s confidence may be committing betrayal. Someone unable to perform a duty because of an uncontrollable accident, however, is not proved treacherous by failure alone.

Ethical usage thus highlights covenant-breaking and betrayal of obligations or trust, rather than mere inability to accomplish something.

Ibn Fāris’s connection between languor and perfidy is an early lexical analysis. It does not alone prove that betrayal historically developed from bodily weakness.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Lisān al-ʿArab, and Tāj al-ʿArūs: perfidy and languor

2. Early Arabic usage

Khatr: perfidy and betrayal of trust

Arabic records خَتَرَ بِهِ and خَتَرَهُ for acting perfidiously toward or betraying someone.

This concerns relationships involving trust or an expectation of fidelity.

Someone may, for example, promise another person protection but knowingly abandon the commitment when circumstances become personally advantageous.

This is more than changing a decision: fidelity and trust have been broken.

Some lexical explanations also give deception as khatr’s meaning. Deception relates to perfidy because covenant-breaking may involve concealing one’s real intentions or cultivating another person’s confidence.

Not every kind of deception should, however, be called khatr. Someone may lie about a matter without breaking a particular covenant or relationship of trust.

Conversely, a covenant-breaker may violate an earlier commitment without telling a new lie.

Betrayal and deception can therefore coexist without encompassing each other completely.

A poetic witness attributed to Al-Aʿshā

Abū ʿUbaydah’s Majāz al-Qurʾān quotes a verse attributed to Al-Aʿshā to explain khattār:

بِالْأَبْلَقِ الْفَرْدِ مِنْ تَيْمَاءَ مَنْزِلُهُ
حِصْنٌ حَصِينٌ وَجَارٌ غَيْرُ خَتَّارِ

bi-al-ablaqi al-fardi min taymāʾa manziluhu / ḥiṣnun ḥaṣīnun wa-jārun ghayru khattāri

Translation:

“His dwelling is at Al-Ablaq al-Fard in Taymāʾ: a mighty fortress and a neighbor who is no betrayer.”

غَيْرُ خَتَّارِ describes someone recognized for fidelity and avoidance of perfidy.

The poet combines the fortress’s strength and the neighbor’s trustworthiness. They stand together without meaning the same thing.

A strong fortress offers physical protection; a faithful neighbor provides security and trust within human relationships.

Here khattār does not mean weak, slow-moving, or ill. It concerns betrayal and unfaithfulness.

Al-Aʿshā was a renowned poet of the pre-Islamic period close to Islam’s beginnings. This particular verse nevertheless has wording variants.

Abū ʿUbaydah and some lexicons transmit غَيْرُ خَتَّارِ; other versions give غَيْرُ غَدَّارِ.

This variation shows the semantic proximity of khattār and ghaddār, while preventing an unqualified claim that khattār is the sole certain reading.

The verse is therefore an early witness attributed to Al-Aʿshā for perfidy, with its textual variation acknowledged.

Yāqūt’s Muʿjam al-Buldān: ghayru ghaddār in Al-Aʿshā’s verse

A witness attributed to ʿAmr ibn Maʿdīkarib

Abū ʿUbaydah and Al-Qurṭubī also attribute this verse to ʿAmr ibn Maʿdīkarib:

فَإِنَّكَ لَوْ رَأَيْتَ أَبَا عُمَيْرٍ
مَلَأْتَ يَدَيْكَ مِنْ غَدْرٍ وَخَتْرِ

fa-innaka law raʾayta abā ʿumayrin / malaʾta yadayka min ghadrin wa-khatri

Translation:

“Had you seen Abū ʿUmayr, you would have filled your hands with his perfidy and betrayal.”

Ghadr and khatr appear together.

Their combination characterizes the person as treacherous and unfaithful.

Combining close terms can emphasize or intensify a description. This single verse does not establish ghadr and khatr as invariably separate stages or entirely independent kinds of betrayal.

Lexicons sometimes explain khatr as the ugliest perfidy. This reflects its strongly negative force in some uses, without creating a definitive legal classification of every form of betrayal.

ʿAmr ibn Maʿdīkarib was a poet and warrior who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. This verse’s composition date is not independently established; attribution to such a poet does not alone prove composition before the Qur’an’s revelation.

Al-Qurṭubī: the poetic witnesses of Al-Aʿshā and ʿAmr ibn Maʿdīkarib

Bodily languor and slow movement

Lexicons record تَخَتَّرَ الرَّجُلُ فِي مِشْيَتِهِ for someone walking like a languid, listless person.

Takhattara also describes diminished bodily strength through illness or drink.

The primary issue here is diminished force and movement quieting or slowing.

Someone recovering from illness may, for example, lose the ability to walk quickly and proceed with slow steps.

This linguistically relates to khatr but is not itself evidence of betrayal or moral corruption.

Some sources also record خَتَرَتْ نَفْسُهُ for someone becoming unwell or their condition deteriorating.

خَتَّرَهُ الشَّرَابُ describes drink producing weakness, languor, or a disturbed condition.

These meanings show that خ ت ر has more than an ethical branch.

Qur’anic khattār, however, carries the recognized sense of perfidy and betrayal, rather than illness, fatigue, or slow movement.

3. Qur’anic usage: changed conduct after deliverance

وَإِذَا غَشِيَهُمْ مَوْجٌ كَالظُّلَلِ دَعَوُا اللَّهَ مُخْلِصِينَ لَهُ الدِّينَ فَلَمَّا نَجَّاهُمْ إِلَى الْبَرِّ فَمِنْهُمْ مُقْتَصِدٌ وَمَا يَجْحَدُ بِآيَاتِنَا إِلَّا كُلُّ خَتَّارٍ كَفُورٍ

wa-idhā ghashiyahum mawjun ka-al-ẓulali daʿawū allāha mukhliṣīna lahu al-dīna fa-lammā najjāhum ilā al-barri fa-minhum muqtaṣidun wa-mā yajḥadu bi-āyātinā illā kullu khattārin kafūrin

Proposed translation for this root study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“When a wave like canopies engulfs them, they call upon The God, making their devotion sincere to Him. When He delivers them to land, some are moderate; none denies Our signs except a treacherous, deeply ungrateful person.”

Luqmān 31:32

This is the sole Qur’anic occurrence of خ ت ر.

The verse describes people confronted by immense waves at sea. In that condition they call upon The God sincerely.

Their responses after deliverance and arrival on land, however, are not identical.

The ending attributes denial of divine signs to someone described by خَتَّارٍ and كَفُورٍ.

This usage should be read with its surrounding passage.

The blessing of sailing ships and divine signs

Luqmān 31:31 says:

أَلَمْ تَرَ أَنَّ الْفُلْكَ تَجْرِي فِي الْبَحْرِ بِنِعْمَتِ اللَّهِ لِيُرِيَكُمْ مِنْ آيَاتِهِ إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَآيَاتٍ لِكُلِّ صَبَّارٍ شَكُورٍ

a-lam tara anna al-fulka tajrī fī al-baḥri bi-niʿmati allāhi li-yuriyakum min āyātihi inna fī dhālika la-āyātin li-kulli ṣabbārin shakūrin

Proposed translation for this root study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Have you not seen that ships sail through the sea by The God’s blessing, so that He may show you some of His signs? In this are signs for everyone deeply steadfast and grateful.”

Luqmān 31:31

The verse connects ships moving at sea with The God’s blessing and signs.

It ends with صَبَّارٍ شَكُورٍ.

The next verse describes another situation: the same marine environment brings immense waves and engulfing danger.

In that condition, the people described call upon The God sincerely.

The context thus connects enjoying blessings, facing danger, and responding to rescue.

Khattār should not directly mean fearing the sea or seeking help in danger. Those actions occur at the opening; khattār appears at the ending in relation to denying signs.

Sincerity in danger

The verse says:

“دَعَوُا اللَّهَ مُخْلِصِينَ لَهُ الدِّينَ”

Mukhliṣīna comes from خ ل ص and concerns making pure and free from admixture.

Facing danger, they call upon The God and make their devotion sincere to Him.

The verse does not say that they swear a particular oath or make a specific spoken promise then.

This distinction matters for khattār.

Lexicons commonly explain khatr through perfidy and covenant-breaking. In Luqmān 31:32, sincerity in danger contrasted with subsequent ingratitude and denial can suggest unfaithfulness to the same orientation.

This does not mean the verse quotes a particular spoken covenant from those rescued.

It is therefore better to explain khattār through unfaithfulness to the commitment expressed by behavior in need, rather than adding an unspoken oath to the text.

فَمِنْهُمْ مُقْتَصِدٌ: those rescued are not all alike

After their deliverance to land, the Qur’an says:

“فَمِنْهُمْ مُقْتَصِدٌ”

That is, some of them are muqtaṣid.

Muqtaṣid comes from ق ص د and here relates to moderation or following a moderate course.

Early explanations differ over whom this describes. Some identify someone remaining faithful and committed to divine oneness after rescue. Al-Ṭabarī also reports Mujāhid’s reading as moderation in speech and outward acknowledgment while concealing disbelief. This second reading is more than a lesser degree of fidelity and should be recognized as a distinct interpretation.

The verse itself does not explain muqtaṣid’s characteristics in detail here.

It clearly does not describe everyone rescued as identical.

One should therefore not claim that everyone calling upon The God sincerely in danger necessarily becomes khattār after rescue.

The verse describes differing human responses.

Its ending attributes denial to a particular description, rather than everyone who was at sea.

Al-Ṭabarī: Mujāhid’s reading of muqtaṣid, distinct from fidelity

وَمَا يَجْحَدُ بِآيَاتِنَا: denying the signs

The verb يَجْحَدُ occurs at the verse’s ending.

It comes from ج ح د and concerns denying or refusing acknowledgment.

Clear Qur’anic uses can combine juḥūd with recognizing truth yet refusing it: Al-Naml 27:14 describes denying signs despite inward certainty.

In Luqmān 31:32, too, the word itself must remain distinct from contextual evidence.

These people have experienced danger, called upon The God sincerely, and been rescued; the verse then speaks of denying divine signs.

This context reveals a distance between conduct in need and the response after deliverance.

Experiencing danger alone does not establish complete knowledge of every divine truth in every rescued person.

The verse attributes denial to a specifically described group. Beyond this context, someone’s intentions and degree of understanding cannot be judged definitively without evidence.

For a fuller examination of this distinction, see Forqan’s study of the root J Ḥ D.

كُلُّ خَتَّارٍ كَفُورٍ: betrayal and ingratitude

Two descriptions stand together:

خَتَّارٍ comes from خ ت ر; كَفُورٍ comes from ك ف ر.

Khattār emphasizes perfidy, betrayal, and unfaithfulness.

Kafūr here relates to ingratitude, covering a blessing, and refusing gratitude for it.

The descriptions occupy neighboring ethical fields without being complete synonyms.

Someone may betray a covenant: breaking fidelity belongs to khatr’s field.

Someone may also enjoy a blessing yet disregard it or be ungrateful: this belongs to kufrān’s field.

Luqmān 31:32 brings both together: unfaithfulness to an orientation and commitment expressed in danger, and ingratitude for rescue and divine signs.

Khattār alone does not therefore mean ingratitude, nor does kafūr always mean breaking a covenant.

The intensive forms also establish no fixed number of betrayals or acts of ingratitude. They can express a trait’s intensity or prominence without counting events.

Al-Qurṭubī: differing readings of muqtaṣid and the explanation of khattār

Does khattār concern only humanity’s relationship with The God?

The sole Qur’anic occurrence concerns humanity’s response to divine signs and blessings.

Early Arabic evidence also places khatr within relationships between people.

Someone betraying another’s trust or deliberately breaking a covenant can be called khattār.

The single Qur’anic occurrence does not confine the root itself to covenant-breaking before The God.

Conversely, not every error or inability to fulfill an obligation is khatr.

Perfidy and betrayal matter in its ethical use.

4. Distinctions from related roots

خ ت ر (khatr) and غ د ر (ghadr): betrayal and covenant-breaking

Arabic ghadr concerns breaking a covenant and betraying someone.

Many lexicons explain khatr through this word.

Some accounts describe khatr as the ugliest perfidy; poetic witnesses also combine the words.

This does not establish a fixed classification in which every ghadr is mild and every khatr severe.

The clear shared meaning is betraying a covenant or trust. The severity of condemnation may reflect the form, accompanying descriptions, and context.

In Luqmān 31:32, intensive khattār alongside kafūr heightens the negative description.

خ ت ر (khatr) and خ و ن (khiyānah): covenant-breaking and betrayed trusts

Qur’anic khiyānah concerns violating a trust and acting contrary to confidence placed in someone.

Al-Anfāl 8:27 forbids believers to betray The God, the Messenger, and their trusts.

This is close to khatr’s field.

Khiyānah may concern something entrusted to someone without an elaborate spoken covenant between the parties.

Khatr, in recognized witnesses, has a prominent connection with perfidy and unfaithfulness.

This is a tendency, not an absolute boundary: khiyānah can itself encompass violated covenants and fidelity.

Persian “betrayer” can therefore render khattār, although “covenant-breaker” makes fidelity and its violation clearer in Luqmān’s context.

خ ت ر (khatr) and ن ق ض (naqḍ): breaking and betraying a commitment

Arabic naqḍ concerns undoing, unravelling, or taking something apart.

Applied to a covenant, naqḍ al-ʿahd means breaking an established commitment.

Al-Naḥl 16:92 illustrates covenant-breaking through a woman undoing her firmly spun thread.

Khatr can also concern covenant-breaking, but perfidy and unfaithfulness stand out in its ethical use.

Naqḍ may thus express the act of breaking a covenant, while khatr makes its treacherous character more evident.

Both can describe one action; they should not become two obligatory stages.

Someone knowingly breaking a covenant for personal gain has violated it and may also have committed khatr.

خ ت ر (khatr) and خ د ع (khidāʿ): deceit and betrayal

Khidāʿ concerns deceiving someone and creating a false impression.

Deceit can accompany khatr, especially when someone first wins another person’s confidence and then acts against it.

Not every deception necessarily breaks a covenant.

Someone giving false information about a price may be deceptive without violating a separate covenant.

Conversely, someone may knowingly break a commitment without telling a new lie while doing so.

Khatr and khidāʿ can therefore coexist without being identical.

Study of KH D ʿ

خ ت ر (khatr) and ج ح د (juḥūd): unfaithfulness and denial

These roots occur in the same sentence in Luqmān 31:32.

Yajḥadu expresses denying.

Khattār describes someone characterized by perfidy and unfaithfulness.

The verb states the act of denial; the description identifies a blameworthy trait.

Their combination does not make every denial covenant-breaking.

Someone may reject a statement through ignorance, lack of evidence, or misunderstanding. Without examining the evidence, this is not called khatr.

Conversely, someone may betray a covenant without explicitly denying that it exists.

Luqmān 31:32 places denial within danger experienced, sincerity in prayer, and deliverance to land, alongside khattār and kafūr.

خ ت ر (khatr) and ك ف ر (kufr): betrayal and ingratitude

Qur’anic kufr has a broad field, including covering or rejecting truth and being ungrateful for blessings.

Rescue from danger is a clear contextual basis for understanding kafūr in Luqmān 31:32.

Someone calling upon The God in danger but disregarding the blessing and divine sign after deliverance may move toward ingratitude.

Khatr highlights another aspect: unfaithfulness and betrayal of commitment and trust.

Both concepts combine in one behavior without becoming lexically identical.

5. Khattārin: morphology and careful translation

خَتَّارٍ follows فَعَّال, an intensive pattern.

In Luqmān 31:32, it is the genitive complement of كُلُّ and is therefore genitive.

كَفُورٍ modifies خَتَّارٍ and agrees with its case, expressing another trait of the same person.

كُلُّ خَتَّارٍ كَفُورٍ therefore concerns someone with two blameworthy traits.

A trait’s intensity or recurrence should not be confused with a fixed number of actions.

Khattār need not mean someone who has broken a covenant a particular number of times. It can describe someone prominently characterized by betrayal and covenant-breaking.

Restriction at the verse’s ending

In the sentence:

“وَمَا يَجْحَدُ بِآيَاتِنَا إِلَّا كُلُّ خَتَّارٍ كَفُورٍ”

Mā … illā expresses restriction.

Denying divine signs is attributed here to someone characterized as khattār kafūr.

The sentence should not be reversed without justification to conclude that anyone once unfaithful to another person necessarily denies every divine sign.

The verse connects denial with blameworthy traits in its particular context.

Grammatically, khattār alone is not the subject of every earlier verb. The opening concerns people calling upon The God in danger; it then distinguishes responses after rescue and finally attributes denial to a specifically described group.

This structural distinction prevents everyone mentioned in the verse from being treated as identical without evidence.

Translation proposal for Forqan

Two principal Persian equivalents suit Qur’anic khattār: “covenant-breaker” and “betrayer.”

“Covenant-breaker” makes fidelity and violated commitment clearer.

“Betrayer” is broader, encompassing perfidy and conduct contrary to trust.

Given early Arabic khatr and Luqmān 31:32, “covenant-breaker” is suitable, without implying that the verse quotes a particular spoken covenant.

Its intensive force can be explained through “habitually covenant-breaking” or “deeply treacherous.”

The natural Persian combination “a covenant-breaker, deeply ungrateful” suits خَتَّارٍ كَفُورٍ. It preserves betrayal and ingratitude, while khattār’s intensive force can be made clearer in the lexical explanation.

“An ungrateful deceiver” is also defensible within the semantic field, but “deceiver” alone does not make fidelity and betrayal as apparent as “covenant-breaker.”

“Languid and lazy” is unsuitable: that belongs to another branch, whereas the Qur’anic context concerns denial and ingratitude.

Nor should khattār become “disbeliever,” with kafūr treated as a repetition. The Qur’an gives different descriptions, and translation should preserve their distinction as far as possible.

Brief root meaning and meaning in the verse

خَتَّارٍ — Luqmān 31:32

Root: خ ت ر

Grammar: Intensive فَعَّال form; masculine singular, indefinite, genitive, within كُلُّ خَتَّارٍ كَفُورٍ.

Brief root meaning: Covenant-breaking, perfidy, and betrayal of commitment or trust; a separate lexical branch also records languor and diminished strength.

Meaning in this verse: Someone prominently characterized by unfaithfulness and covenant-breaking, described alongside ingratitude and denial of divine signs in the context of rescue from danger.

This keeps the root’s lexical meaning distinct from other actions and descriptions in the verse.

6. Conclusion

Arabic خ ت ر has two recognized fields: languor and diminished force, and perfidy and betrayed fidelity.

Ibn Fāris connects them through abandoning fidelity. The connection is understandable but does not establish the certain historical origin of every usage.

Early poetic witnesses apply khattār to someone treacherous and unfaithful. The Al-Aʿshā verse’s variation between khattār and ghaddār likewise shows their semantic proximity.

The Qur’an uses this root only in Luqmān 31:32. People call upon The God sincerely in danger but respond differently after rescue. The ending associates denial of divine signs with khattār kafūr.

This connects unfaithfulness, ingratitude, and denial without making them identical. Khatr concerns perfidy and unfaithfulness; kufr here concerns ingratitude and covering blessings; juḥūd concerns denying signs.

Nor does the verse describe everyone rescued as covenant-breaking or quote a particular spoken oath from them.

Final definition: Ethical خ ت ر signifies perfidy, covenant-breaking, and betrayal of commitment or trust. Khattār intensively describes someone prominently characterized by this trait. In Luqmān 31:32 it occurs alongside kafūr, in the context of denying divine signs after danger and rescue. The root itself means neither ingratitude nor denial, but unfaithfulness and betrayal. Brief root meaning: Covenant-breaking, perfidy, and betrayal of commitment or trust; in the bodily branch, languor and diminished strength.
