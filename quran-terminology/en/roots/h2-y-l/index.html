The root ه ي ل — Pouring and the cascading of granular materials

Qur’anic and lexical forms

مَهِيلًا
(mahīlan)
Loose and cascading; describing a sand heap

From falling sand and soil and flowing grains to mountains that lose their solidity

The three-consonant Arabic root ه ي ل concerns pouring and cascading granular materials: sand, soil, flour, and grain whose particles can slide over one another and flow from place to place.

With sand and soil, it particularly describes a mass lacking sufficient cohesion and solidity, parts of which fall away under movement or pressure.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as mahīlan. In a scene of resurrection, mountains become a heap of loose, cascading sand, losing their former firmness and coherence.

Mahīl does not simply mean sand, soil, or fragmentation. It describes material that readily collapses as its particles move over one another.

In the verse, kathīb identifies the sand heap, while mahīl describes its loose, cascading condition.

1. Meaning in early Arabic

Classical Arabic hāla yahīlu haylan means to pour or tip something out, particularly a substance composed of separate grains or particles.

Hāla al-turāba means “He poured out the soil.”

Hāla al-ramla means “He set the sand moving or poured it down.”

Hiltu al-daqīqa fī al-jirābi means “I poured the flour into the sack.”

The action is more than moving a single solid object: material is poured so that its components can move separately and settle upon one another.

Lifting a large stone and setting it elsewhere moves a coherent object. Tilting a vessel of sand, however, makes grains stream down successively and accumulate in a new place.

The familiar use of hayl is closer to the second picture.

Hayl and ḥathy

Arabic dictionaries record a distinction between hayl and ḥathy.

Ḥathy describes taking a quantity of soil or granular material in the hand and then scattering or pouring it.

Hayl can describe pouring and moving material without first lifting it in the hand.

A person may scoop up soil and scatter it on the ground: ḥathy can describe this action.

Alternatively, someone may shift a sand heap from the side, causing sand to cascade without first gathering it in the hand. This is closer to hayl.

This traditional distinction should not become an absolute rule that hayl can never be done by hand. Its broader uses also denote pouring and tipping materials.

For sand, the significant feature is the successive movement and falling of particles.

Hayl and kayl

In Maqāyīs al-lugha, Ibn Fāris explains this family through pouring a measurable substance without measuring it out.

This definition suits grain, flour, and other granular materials.

Kayl, from ك ي ل, concerns measuring by capacity; hayl in this usage describes pouring the material.

Someone may measure wheat and then pour it into a container, or empty it directly from one sack into another without measuring it.

The pouring in the second case can be expressed by hayl.

This does not mean the root always signifies “pouring without measuring.” The Qur’anic passage concerns loose, collapsing sandlike material, rather than measurement.

The absence of measuring in some everyday and commercial uses therefore does not exhaust the root’s meaning.

2. Evidence from early Arabic

A verse attributed to Sāʿida ibn Juʾayya

A poem attributed to Sāʿida ibn Juʾayya al-Hudhalī describes a hyena digging at a grave:

«فَذَاحَتْ بِالْوَتَائِرِ ثُمَّ بَدَّتْ
يَدَيْهَا عِنْدَ جَانِبِهِ تَهِيلُ»

Fa-dhāḥat bi-al-watāʾiri thumma baddat / yadayhā ʿinda jānibihi tahīlu.

“She hurried along the narrow stretches of ground, then worked her two forepaws beside the grave, casting the soil aside.”

Tahīlu here describes moving and pouring soil.

The hyena pushes soil aside with its paws, shifting its particles from their original position.

The usage directly illustrates the material sense. It concerns casting soil aside, rather than fear, an earthquake, or the destruction of a mountain.

Sāʿida ibn Juʾayya is associated with the transition between pre-Islamic and early Islamic poetry; literary sources differ over classifying him as pre-Islamic or as a poet who lived through both periods.

His poetry survives in the Hudhalī collection, but the exact date of this verse is unknown. It cannot confidently be presented as predating the Qur’an.

It nevertheless supplies significant evidence from an Arabic poetic tradition close to the period of revelation.

A verse by Muzāḥim al-ʿUqaylī

The Umayyad poet Muzāḥim al-ʿUqaylī says of sand dunes:

«بِكُلِّ نَقًا وَعْثٍ إِذَا مَا عَلَوْتَهُ
جَرَى نَصَفًا هَيْلَانُهُ الْمُتَسَاوِقُ»

Bi-kulli naqan waʿthin idhā mā ʿalawtahu / jarā naṣafan haylānuhu al-mutasāwiqu.

“On every soft, difficult sand dune, when you climb it, its streaming sand runs successively down either side.”

Haylān here concerns the movement and falling of the dune’s sand.

The poet describes sand shifting and sliding downward under the pressure of someone climbing the dune.

The picture closely resembles the Qur’anic meaning of mahīl.

A dune may look fixed from a distance, but its grains are not joined like solid rock. Pressure on its slope can make some of the sand fall away.

Muzāḥim lived after the Qur’an’s revelation, so this is not pre-Islamic evidence. It illustrates the continued classical use of the root for flowing sand.

Limits of the historical evidence

The Qur’an, poetry associated with the transition to Islam, and Umayyad poetry provide clear evidence for pouring and cascading granular materials.

This study has not securely established an independent poetic example of this particular branch whose wording, attribution, and pre-Qur’anic date are all reliable.

The recorded classical meaning can be explained confidently, and the Qur’an attests its use during revelation. Its precise origin should not be dated without decisive evidence.

What is clear in the sand and soil examples is the falling and movement of particles not united into a hard, coherent body.

3. Forms and derivatives

The root occurs once in the Qur’an, as mahīlan.

Important Arabic forms include:

  • Hāla yahīlu: poured or tipped out, especially sand, soil, or granular material.
  • Hayl: pouring and cascading such material.
  • Ahāla: poured or tipped out; sometimes synonymous with the basic verb.
  • Hayyala: poured or made material cascade by moving it.
  • Inhāla: collapsed or cascaded successively.
  • Tahayyala: began falling or flowing.
  • Mahīl: poured material, or something in a loose, cascading condition.
  • Muhāl: a passive participle in certain uses of ahāla.
  • Ahyal and hāʾil: descriptions of loose, cascading sand.
  • Haylān and hayāl: in certain lexical uses, sand or material that has fallen and streamed down.

These forms are known in Arabic, but only mahīlan occurs in the Qur’an.

Dictionaries also record inhāla ʿalayhi al-qawmu for people descending upon someone successively with blows, insults, or pressure.

Their successive movement is likened to a continuous shower of material.

Jāʾa bi-al-hayli wa-al-haylamāni is also recorded for bringing great wealth, associated with abundant sand and things accumulating or pouring forth.

Abundant wealth and an onslaught of people are other uses of the family, not the direct meanings of Qur’anic mahīl.

The morphology of mahīl

Mahīl is the passive participle of hāla yahīlu.

The verb is hollow: its second root consonant is yāʾ.

Sound and morphological changes produce the usual participle mahīl.

Traditional morphology posits mahyūl as the underlying form, becoming mahīl after vowel transfer and the loss of one of the adjacent vowelless letters.

Some sources also mention mahyūl as its underlying or explanatory form.

Its appearance alone should not lead us to treat mahīl as a simple faʿīl adjective unrelated to a passive participle.

In Qur’anic usage, mahīlan describes something loose and ready to collapse, like poured sand.

Its participial form alone does not identify the agent, time, or precise manner of falling.

4. Qur’anic usage: mountains like flowing sand

Al-Muzzammil 73:14

«يَوْمَ تَرْجُفُ الْأَرْضُ وَالْجِبَالُ وَكَانَتِ الْجِبَالُ كَثِيبًا مَهِيلًا»

Yawma tarjufu al-arḍu wa-al-jibālu wa-kānati al-jibālu kathīban mahīlā.

“On the day when the earth and mountains tremble, and the mountains become a heap of loose, cascading sand.”

The verse describes the transformation of earth and mountains at resurrection.

Earth and mountains tremble, and the mountains are pictured in a condition quite unlike their familiar one.

Mountains, ordinarily associated with greatness, height, and solidity, become loose, cascading sand.

Three principal words should be distinguished to understand this picture.

Tarjufu: trembling and agitation

Tarjufu comes from ر ج ف.

This root concerns trembling, shaking, and strong agitation.

Both earth and mountains undergo this trembling at the verse’s beginning.

The first picture is thus of widespread movement and instability.

Tarjufu itself does not mean that mountains become sand; the following clause conveys that transformation.

Nor does the verb specify a numerical magnitude or geological mechanism.

Kathīban: a sand heap

Kathīb, from ك ث ب, denotes a heap or mound of sand in descriptions of terrain.

Sand may gather and assume the shape of a hill.

From a distance it looks like a single structure, although it consists of separate grains.

Kathīban is singular in the verse, whereas al-jibāl, “the mountains,” is plural.

This presents the mountains collectively as a sand heap. The singular alone does not establish that all earthly mountains must gather into one geographical dune.

The word conveys the mountains’ new condition in the verse’s picture.

Kathīb alone does not establish that the sand is falling: under some conditions a dune retains its shape.

The following description supplies the flowing, unstable quality.

Mahīlan: loose, cascading sand

Mahīlan qualifies kathīban.

The phrase describes a heap whose grains readily move and fall away.

Imagine a sloping mound of dry sand. Pressure from a foot makes grains slide downward, changing its former shape.

Removing sand from its base may cause the upper portions to follow and cascade.

This property approximates mahīl.

An ordinary mountain has rocks and layers joined on a large scale. Mahīl sand lacks that solidity: movement or pressure readily dislodges its grains.

The Qur’anic image concerns more than a smaller or lower mountain: it also depicts lost solidity and transformation into loose, flowing material.

Fragmentation and flowing

The picture of a mountain becoming sand entails the loss of its hard, coherent structure.

Fragmentation and flowing are nevertheless distinct concepts.

A stone may break into smaller pieces that remain piled in a relatively stable place.

Mahīl sand, by contrast, readily slides and cascades as grains move over one another.

Mahīl therefore conveys more than small particle size: the heap’s instability and readiness to fall matter too.

“Crushed sand” or “soft soil” alone does not fully convey the description.

“Loose, cascading sand” is more precise.

Does the verse describe the complete annihilation of mountain matter?

Mahīlan does not mean that.

It describes material becoming loose and liable to cascade.

A mountain becoming sand depicts profound structural change. The root itself does not establish that all its matter is annihilated into absolute nonexistence.

Conversely, the explicit picture should not be reduced to a purely psychological or symbolic change. The verse directly mentions earth, mountains, and their transformation.

The physical mechanism and its relationship to the world after resurrection go beyond the lexical meaning of mahīl.

What the verse directly states is the trembling of earth and mountains and the mountains becoming loose, cascading sand.

5. Other Qur’anic pictures of mountains

Other verses also describe mountains changing at resurrection.

These pictures concern a broad shared subject but use different words and emphasize different properties.

Nasf: uprooting and scattering

Ṭā Hā 20:105 uses nasfan of mountains.

From ن س ف, it concerns uprooting and dispersing mountains in that context.

Ṭā Hā 20:106 describes the ground afterward as level and smooth.

Nasf and hayl overlap insofar as both can concern the loss of mountains’ stable condition.

Nasf emphasizes uprooting and dispersal; mahīl describes loose, cascading material.

Their similarity does not establish that they name exactly the same action or stage.

Bass: breaking mountains apart

Al-Wāqiʿa 56:5 says:

«وَبُسَّتِ الْجِبَالُ بَسًّا»

Wa-bussati al-jibālu bassā.

The root ب س س depicts mountains being broken and shattered.

Al-Wāqiʿa 56:6 then describes them as scattered dust.

Bass particularly concerns the breaking action and loss of structure.

Mahīl describes a condition resembling flowing sand.

The pictures may be compatible, but these verses use different words.

Dakk: crushing and leveling

Al-Ḥāqqa 69:14 describes earth and mountains being crushed together.

The expression belongs to د ك ك.

Its picture emphasizes impact and crushing.

Hayl concerns granular material cascading.

Similar outcomes do not justify inserting “crushing” into the lexical definition of mahīl.

ʿIhn manfūsh: dispersal like wool

Al-Qāriʿa 101:5 likens the mountains to al-ʿihni al-manfūsh.

ʿIhn concerns colored wool, while manfūsh describes wool opened out and dispersed.

It resembles the mahīl sand image in the loss of former cohesion and solidity.

Wool and sand remain different materials with different properties.

The wool image emphasizes loosened, scattered fibers; kathīb mahīl emphasizes loose, cascading sand grains.

Both can describe mountain transformation without being physically identical or specifying the same chronological stage.

6. Distinctions from neighboring roots

ه ي ل and ص ب ب: pouring and cascading

Ṣabb concerns pouring and causing something, especially liquid, to flow.

ʿAbasa 80:25 uses ṣababnā of pouring down water.

Hayl also concerns pouring, but its familiar applications involve sand, soil, flour, and grain whose particles move over one another.

This is a tendency rather than an absolute distinction, since ṣabb can also apply to nonliquids.

Māʾ maṣbūb highlights a liquid flow; raml mahīl highlights moving and falling sand particles.

ه ي ل and ح ث و: pouring and scattering by hand

Ḥathw describes scooping and scattering granular material, especially by hand.

Hayl likewise applies to soil and sand without necessarily requiring a handful to be lifted.

Someone may scatter a handful of soil or shift a sand heap so that it cascades.

Material moves in both cases, but the manner differs.

This early distinction should not become an absolute rule excluding any overlap.

ه ي ل and ن ث ر: falling and scattering

Nathr concerns scattering and strewing objects.

Nathr and hayl may overlap when applied to grains and particles.

Hayl in the flowing-sand picture emphasizes successive downward movement.

Nathr can indicate dispersal even without a continuous stream.

Falling and scattering may accompany one another without being lexically identical.

ه ي ل and ه و ر: cascading sand and structural collapse

The meanings approach one another because both can concern falling apart.

Al-Tawba 9:109 uses ه و ر to describe an unstable, collapsing bank.

Hārin describes structural instability; fa-inhāra describes the collapse itself.

ه ي ل in al-Muzzammil 73:14 instead describes sand whose grains readily cascade.

Hawr particularly concerns the collapse of structures such as banks and buildings; hayl in its principal branch describes pouring and flowing granular material.

An earthen bank may collapse and its soil cascade as particles. Both concepts can apply to the event, but one event does not make the roots identical.

ه ي ل and ه و ل: cascading and terror

These families resemble one another in their letters and some forms but differ significantly in meaning.

ه و ل concerns fear, terrifying greatness, and dreadful conditions.

Qur’anic ه ي ل concerns cascading sand.

Mountains collapsing may be terrifying, but terror is not the lexical meaning of mahīl.

Depending on its root and formation, hāla can also concern frightening someone. Surface resemblance alone is insufficient: the imperfect verb and lexical family must be examined.

Hāla yahīlu concerns pouring and tipping; the fear-related uses belong to ه و ل.

Mahīl in al-Muzzammil 73:14 should therefore not be translated “terrifying.”

7. Interpretation and translation

A challenge in Persian translation is that several words approximate loose, flowing sand, yet no single equivalent clearly conveys all its properties.

“Flowing” is useful because it conveys particle movement and falling.

“Loose” conveys insufficient solidity, although readers might understand only general weakness.

“Scattered” conveys a possible result, but mahīl need not mean that all particles have already dispersed.

“Soft” captures something of sand’s quality, but a soft material may retain a relatively stable structure.

For mahīlan in al-Muzzammil 73:14, “loose and cascading” is therefore clear.

Suggested verse translation

«يَوْمَ تَرْجُفُ الْأَرْضُ وَالْجِبَالُ وَكَانَتِ الْجِبَالُ كَثِيبًا مَهِيلًا»

Yawma tarjufu al-arḍu wa-al-jibālu wa-kānati al-jibālu kathīban mahīlā.

“On the day when the earth and mountains tremble, and the mountains become a heap of loose, cascading sand.”

This preserves three distinct concepts:

Tarjufu conveys trembling, kathīban the sand heap, and mahīlan its loose, cascading condition.

The shorter “the mountains become a heap of flowing sand” is also defensible.

“Loose, cascading sand” makes the condition clearer to readers unfamiliar with the root.

A lexical note may read:

“Mahīlan: loose and cascading; describing a sand heap whose grains readily move over one another and fall away.”

Non-Qur’anic uses likewise require context-sensitive equivalents.

Hāla al-turāba: “He poured out the soil.”

Hāla al-ramla: “He poured the sand down.”

Inhāla al-ramlu: “The sand collapsed or cascaded.”

Hāla al-daqīqa fī al-jirābi: “He poured the flour into the sack.”

Inhālū ʿalayhi, of people, can mean “They descended upon him successively.”

Pouring or successive movement connects these uses, while the material or object determines the natural translation.

8. Synthesis

ه ي ل concerns pouring and cascading granular materials, especially sand, soil, flour, and grain.

With sand, it denotes moving and falling particles that lack the cohesion of a solid body.

The poem attributed to Sāʿida illustrates casting soil aside; Muzāḥim’s poem describes sand cascading as someone climbs a dune. These witnesses belong near or after the revelation period and should not be assigned pre-Islamic dates without evidence.

The root occurs once in the Qur’an, as mahīlan.

A passive participle of hāla yahīlu, it describes loose, cascading sand in al-Muzzammil 73:14.

Forqan definition: ه ي ل principally concerns pouring and cascading granular material. Qur’anic mahīl describes sandlike material whose particles lack sufficient cohesion and solidity, readily moving over one another and falling away.

It is distinct from roots denoting crushing, scattering, shaking, or frightening. These events may coexist in a scene, while each root expresses its own feature.

Al-Muzzammil 73:14 portrays apparently firm mountains becoming loose, cascading sand. It conveys lost solidity and structural transformation without specifying the physical mechanism of every stage.

The contrast is central: a mountain is known as hard and coherent, whereas kathīb mahīl is a mass whose components readily shift and fall. Placing these conditions together makes the mountains’ tremendous transformation at resurrection clear and imaginable.
