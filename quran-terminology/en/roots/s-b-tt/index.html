Qur’anic Root S B Ṭ

Sibṭ and asbāṭ: generations, family branches, and the twelve tribes of the Children of Israel

The three-consonant root S B Ṭ occurs in the Qur’an only in the plural form أَسْبَاط (asbāṭ). The word belongs to the sphere of descent and the family branches of the Children of Israel. In Al-Aʿrāf 7:160 it clearly designates tribes; identifying the individuals intended in the revelation passages requires separate examination. One verse describes the division of the Children of Israel into twelve tribes. Other verses place al-asbāṭ alongside Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, and Jacob. Some of these passages concern belief in what was sent down to them or revelation attributed to them.

The Arabic word sibṭ has a wider range than this Qur’anic usage. Lexicons record it both for a grandchild and for a group of children and descendants belonging to a common ancestor. The same root entry also includes uses concerning elongated limbs, straight and flowing hair, and certain plants. Examining these branches helps establish the root’s semantic range, but their similarities do not demonstrate that all its meanings developed from one historically established image.

The root occurs five times in the Qur’an, always in the plural asbāṭ: Al-Baqarah 2:136 and 2:140, Āl ʿImrān 3:84, Al-Nisāʾ 4:163, and Al-Aʿrāf 7:160. Four verses have the definite form الْأَسْبَاط (al-asbāṭ); Al-Aʿrāf 7:160 has the indefinite أَسْبَاطًا (asbāṭan). The singular سِبْط (sibṭ), verbs from this root, and its other lexical derivatives do not occur in the Qur’an.

1. Semantic range and lexical evidence

Extension, length, and smoothness

Arabic lexicons record extension and length without twisting or knotting among the meanings of S B Ṭ. The expressions شَعْرٌ سَبْطٌ (shaʿrun sabṭun) and شَعْرٌ سَبِطٌ (shaʿrun sabiṭun) describe straight, flowing hair without tight curls. By contrast, جَعْد (jaʿd) describes curly hair.

The description is also recorded for bodily form. رَجُلٌ سَبْطُ الْجِسْمِ (rajulun sabṭu al-jismi) describes a man with an elongated, well-proportioned physique. Here, length does not mean unlimited height; it concerns the extended, even form of the body.

A line transmitted in works of lexicography and grammar reads:

«فَجَاءَتْ بِهِ سَبْطَ الْعِظَامِ كَأَنَّمَا
عِمَامَتُهُ بَيْنَ الرِّجَالِ لِوَاءُ»

“She bore him with a long, well-proportioned frame, as though his turban among the men were a raised banner.”

In this line, سَبْطَ الْعِظَامِ describes the person’s physique, conveying length and proportion. In the poem’s context, the speaker describes his own son. Lexical works transmit the line without naming its poet, while some sources give a tribal attribution. Neither the poet’s name nor the date of composition was securely established in this investigation. The line is useful evidence for lexical usage, but cannot be treated as a securely dated pre-Qur’anic witness.

In other Arabic uses, سَبْطُ الْكَفَّيْنِ (sabṭu al-kaffayni) can indicate openhandedness and generosity. In such a context, the open hand provides an image of generosity; that semantic explanation does not by itself establish the historical order in which the two uses developed. The adjective sabṭ is also recorded for abundant, continuous rain.

A poem attributed to Ḥassān ibn Thābit, who lived both before Islam and during the period of revelation, uses this expression to describe the poet’s maternal uncles:

«رب خال لي لو أبصرته
سبط الكفين في اليوم الخصر»

“Many a maternal uncle of mine, had you seen him, was openhanded even on a cold day.”

Here, sabṭ al-kaffayn appears in praise of generosity; the cold day gives the description its setting. The attribution to Ḥassān is transmitted in his collected poetry and in lexical works, but this investigation did not establish the piece’s precise date. It should therefore not be presented without qualification as definitely preceding revelation. Lexical accounts of hair, physique, and plants likewise do not by themselves date the emergence of those meanings without a datable witness.

These examples show that S B Ṭ is not restricted to family and descent in Arabic. Nevertheless, generosity, abundant rain, and straight hair should not be imported directly into the Qur’anic definition of al-asbāṭ.

Sabaṭ as a plant name

Arabic lexicons also record السَّبَط (al-sabaṭ) as the name of a plant growing in sandy ground and known as fodder. Accounts of its form and characteristics differ. Some describe a tall, slender plant; others refer to its numerous branches and stems.

The plural of this plant name can also be أَسْبَاط. The written form asbāṭ alone therefore does not determine the meaning. In the Qur’an, the context of the Children of Israel and the word’s association with Prophets and human groups make clear that a plant is not intended.

Some lexicographers explain asbāṭ in the sense of descendants through the image of a plant with one base and many branches. In this account, a father or common ancestor is compared to a tree’s trunk and the children to its branches.

This image helps illustrate the growth of a family, but should not be treated as the word’s established historical origin. Recording a plant name and the designation of lineage groups under the same Arabic root does not, by itself, prove that the groups were originally named after that plant.

Sibṭ: a grandchild and a branch of descent

Lexicons also record سِبْط for a child’s child: a grandchild, two generations after the person concerned. This definition should be distinguished from the broader sense of descendants and branches of descent.

The scope of the word is disputed. Some accounts restrict it to a daughter’s children; others explicitly include the children of both sons and daughters. In some uses, sibṭ can also designate a family branch or a group descended from one ancestor.

Restricting the basic meaning of sibṭ to “a daughter’s child” is therefore inaccurate. That use is recognized in Arabic, but does not cover the word’s entire semantic range.

In collective usage, sibṭ designates a group tracing its descent to one father or ancestor. In explanations concerning the Children of Israel, lexicographers compare sibṭ with the Arabic qabīlah, “tribe.” Just as several families may be branches of a tribe, one sibṭ may comprise families and generations connected to a common ancestor.

A central distinction is that sibṭ can designate an individual descended from another person, whereas sibṭ in the sense of a tribe designates a lineage community, not just one individual. Context determines which meaning is intended in a particular use.

A limited comparison with Hebrew

Hebrew also records שֵׁבֶט, transliterated šēḇeṭ, for a tribe or clan. In other uses, the same word means a rod, staff, or royal sceptre.

This evidence is relevant to the history of asbāṭ because the linguistic tradition of the Children of Israel also contains a designation for tribes that is close to the Arabic word in form and meaning.

Similarity alone, however, does not establish the direction or mechanism of historical transmission. The Hebrew word’s use for both a staff and a tribe does not mean that sibṭ in Qur’anic Arabic meant “staff,” or that the tribes of Israel received this name because they possessed a staff of leadership.

Nor does the tree-branch image in Arabic lexical explanations independently establish the Hebrew word’s historical derivation. Evidence from the two languages must be examined separately. The defensible conclusion here is a linguistic and semantic proximity in the sphere of tribe and descent, rather than a definitive origin or route of borrowing.

2. Asbāṭan umaman: twelve tribes and communities of the Children of Israel

The clearest Qur’anic use of this root occurs in Al-Aʿrāf 7:160:

«وَقَطَّعْنَاهُمُ اثْنَتَيْ عَشْرَةَ أَسْبَاطًا أُمَمًا وَأَوْحَيْنَا إِلَىٰ مُوسَىٰ إِذِ اسْتَسْقَاهُ قَوْمُهُ أَنِ اضْرِبْ بِعَصَاكَ الْحَجَرَ فَانْبَجَسَتْ مِنْهُ اثْنَتَا عَشْرَةَ عَيْنًا قَدْ عَلِمَ كُلُّ أُنَاسٍ مَشْرَبَهُمْ وَظَلَّلْنَا عَلَيْهِمُ الْغَمَامَ وَأَنْزَلْنَا عَلَيْهِمُ الْمَنَّ وَالسَّلْوَىٰ كُلُوا مِنْ طَيِّبَاتِ مَا رَزَقْنَاكُمْ وَمَا ظَلَمُونَا وَلَٰكِنْ كَانُوا أَنْفُسَهُمْ يَظْلِمُونَ»

“We divided them into twelve tribes, as distinct communities. We revealed to Moses, when his people asked him for water: Strike the rock with your staff. Then twelve springs gushed from it, and each group knew its drinking place. We shaded them with clouds and sent down manna and quails upon them. Eat of the good things We have provided for you. They did not wrong Us; rather, they were wronging themselves.”

(Al-Aʿrāf 7:160)

Here, أَسْبَاطًا refers to the twelve groups of the Children of Israel. أُمَمًا (umaman) further indicates human communities. “Twelve grandchildren” or “twelve children” therefore fails to convey this occurrence accurately.

The verb قَطَّعْنَاهُمُ (qaṭṭaʿnāhum) belongs to another root and expresses their division into sections. اثْنَتَيْ عَشْرَةَ specifies the number of these sections, while أَسْبَاطًا أُمَمًا identifies the kinds of groups that resulted.

The verse continues with twelve springs of water. قَدْ عَلِمَ كُلُّ أُنَاسٍ مَشْرَبَهُمْ states that each group knew its drinking place. In the Qur’anic account, division into asbāṭ is therefore more than an abstract designation: identifiable groups are also distinguished in their use of an essential resource.

The verse does not specify the population, form of government, degree of political independence, or territorial boundaries of each tribe. Twelve groups with recognized drinking places do not provide enough information to reconstruct their entire social and administrative organization.

Why is the feminine form of “twelve” used?

The verse uses the feminine اثْنَتَيْ عَشْرَةَ, although سِبْط, the singular of أَسْبَاط, is ordinarily masculine. Grammarians have drawn attention to this difference.

One established analysis understands an implicit word such as فِرْقَة (firqah, “group”) or قِطْعَة (qiṭʿah, “section”): they were divided into twelve groups, and those groups were asbāṭ. On this analysis, asbāṭan explains or stands in apposition to the twelve groups; it is not the regular numerical specification, or tamyīz, which would be singular in this construction.

Other analyses discuss the influence of the collective sense of أُمَمًا. Differences over the precise grammatical construction do not alter the main point: the Children of Israel had been divided into twelve communities or tribes.

The feminine numeral alone does not establish that sibṭ itself is necessarily feminine in this verse. Nor does the plural asbāṭ prevent us from understanding the number twelve: the verse concerns twelve collective units rather than twelve separate individuals.

Twelve tribes, twelve springs, and twelve representatives

Al-Baqarah 2:60 states, without using the word asbāṭ:

«فَانْفَجَرَتْ مِنْهُ اثْنَتَا عَشْرَةَ عَيْنًا قَدْ عَلِمَ كُلُّ أُنَاسٍ مَشْرَبَهُمْ»

“Then twelve springs gushed from it, and each group knew its drinking place.”

(Al-Baqarah 2:60; part of the verse)

This statement corresponds directly to Al-Aʿrāf 7:160. Both passages describe twelve springs and a recognized drinking place for each group. Al-Aʿrāf 7:160 additionally states explicitly that the Children of Israel were divided into twelve tribes.

Al-Māʾidah 5:12 states:

«وَلَقَدْ أَخَذَ اللَّهُ مِيثَاقَ بَنِي إِسْرَائِيلَ وَبَعَثْنَا مِنْهُمُ اثْنَيْ عَشَرَ نَقِيبًا»

“The God indeed took a covenant from the Children of Israel, and We raised up twelve representatives from among them.”

(Al-Māʾidah 5:12; part of the verse)

The number twelve appears here too, but نَقِيب (naqīb) is not identical to سِبْط. Naqīb designates a person with a representative or supervisory role, whereas sibṭ in Al-Aʿrāf 7:160 denotes a collective unit defined by descent.

Reading these passages together allows examination of the relationship between the twelve groups of the Children of Israel, their social organization, and their responsibilities. Equal numbers do not mean that sibṭ itself means naqīb, or that the verse alone identifies each of the twelve representatives and their duties.

Twelve denotes a specific quantity in these passages. Without supporting evidence, it should not be turned into an unlimited symbol of multiplicity or an esoteric numerical system.

3. Al-asbāṭ alongside Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, and Jacob

In the other four occurrences, al-asbāṭ accompanies the names of individuals associated with prophethood and the family of Abraham. Their contexts differ from Al-Aʿrāf 7:160, but the word’s collective and genealogical dimensions should not be overlooked.

Belief in what was sent down to the asbāṭ

Al-Baqarah 2:136 states:

«قُولُوا آمَنَّا بِاللَّهِ وَمَا أُنْزِلَ إِلَيْنَا وَمَا أُنْزِلَ إِلَىٰ إِبْرَاهِيمَ وَإِسْمَاعِيلَ وَإِسْحَاقَ وَيَعْقُوبَ وَالْأَسْبَاطِ وَمَا أُوتِيَ مُوسَىٰ وَعِيسَىٰ وَمَا أُوتِيَ النَّبِيُّونَ مِنْ رَبِّهِمْ لَا نُفَرِّقُ بَيْنَ أَحَدٍ مِنْهُمْ وَنَحْنُ لَهُ مُسْلِمُونَ»

“Say: We believe in The God and what was sent down to us, and what was sent down to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and the asbāṭ; and what Moses and Jesus were given, and what the Prophets were given from their Lord. We make no distinction between any of them, and to Him we submit.”

(Al-Baqarah 2:136)

Al-asbāṭ follows Jacob in this verse. That ordering is compatible with understanding the asbāṭ as branches of descent arising from Jacob’s family.

The verse, however, concerns belief in revelation. وَمَا أُنْزِلَ إِلَىٰ ... وَالْأَسْبَاطِ places the asbāṭ among the persons or groups to whom a divine sending-down is attributed.

This attribution should not disappear from translation or be reduced to a purely familial relationship. The passage goes beyond introducing a genealogy: it concerns belief in the divine message and refusing to discriminate among its recipients.

A closely related construction occurs in Āl ʿImrān 3:84:

«قُلْ آمَنَّا بِاللَّهِ وَمَا أُنْزِلَ عَلَيْنَا وَمَا أُنْزِلَ عَلَىٰ إِبْرَاهِيمَ وَإِسْمَاعِيلَ وَإِسْحَاقَ وَيَعْقُوبَ وَالْأَسْبَاطِ وَمَا أُوتِيَ مُوسَىٰ وَعِيسَىٰ وَالنَّبِيُّونَ مِنْ رَبِّهِمْ لَا نُفَرِّقُ بَيْنَ أَحَدٍ مِنْهُمْ وَنَحْنُ لَهُ مُسْلِمُونَ»

“Say: We believe in The God and what was sent down upon us, and what was sent down upon Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and the asbāṭ; and what Moses, Jesus, and the Prophets were given from their Lord. We make no distinction between any of them, and to Him we submit.”

(Āl ʿImrān 3:84)

Al-Baqarah 2:136 has أُنْزِلَ إِلَىٰ, “sent down to,” while Āl ʿImrān 3:84 uses أُنْزِلَ عَلَىٰ, “sent down upon.” The difference in preposition should be preserved as it appears in the text. By itself, however, it neither changes the meaning of al-asbāṭ nor establishes two entirely different kinds of revelation.

In both verses, the central subject is belief in divine revelation and avoidance of selective discrimination among those to whom it is attributed.

Does al-asbāṭ here mean only Jacob’s twelve sons?

Historically and genealogically, the names of the twelve tribes of Israel are connected with Jacob’s children. Nevertheless, the founding children of a family should be distinguished from the tribes that emerge from them over subsequent generations.

Al-asbāṭ is plural and clearly names communities in Al-Aʿrāf 7:160. Without independent evidence, we should not assume that every Qur’anic use refers exclusively to the original twelve individuals.

In some contexts, a designation for a lineage group may refer to its founders, just as a family or tribal name may be used for ancestors as well as subsequent generations. That linguistic possibility does not prove that Al-Baqarah 2:136 or Āl ʿImrān 3:84 refers to all twelve sons of Jacob, and only those individuals.

Neither verse names each of the asbāṭ or explains which individuals from each tribe received the revelation in question, or in which period. The attribution of a divine sending-down to the asbāṭ is clear, but the word alone does not identify every person or supply the historical details.

4. Al-asbāṭ, revelation, and prophethood

Al-Nisāʾ 4:163 states:

«إِنَّا أَوْحَيْنَا إِلَيْكَ كَمَا أَوْحَيْنَا إِلَىٰ نُوحٍ وَالنَّبِيِّينَ مِنْ بَعْدِهِ وَأَوْحَيْنَا إِلَىٰ إِبْرَاهِيمَ وَإِسْمَاعِيلَ وَإِسْحَاقَ وَيَعْقُوبَ وَالْأَسْبَاطِ وَعِيسَىٰ وَأَيُّوبَ وَيُونُسَ وَهَارُونَ وَسُلَيْمَانَ وَآتَيْنَا دَاوُودَ زَبُورًا»

“We have revealed to you as We revealed to Noah and the Prophets after him. We revealed to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, the asbāṭ, Jesus, Job, Jonah, Aaron, and Solomon; and We gave David a zabūr.”

(Al-Nisāʾ 4:163)

This verse is more explicit than Al-Baqarah 2:136 and Āl ʿImrān 3:84: it uses أَوْحَيْنَا, “We revealed,” and places al-asbāṭ directly among the recipients of revelation.

Without further examination, this does not establish that every member of the twelve tribes was a Prophet. Nor can familiarity with the story of Jacob’s children alone justify claiming that none of them could have received revelation.

Several claims must be distinguished. First, the Qur’an attributes revelation to al-asbāṭ: the verse explicitly states this. Second, the word always refers to twelve particular individuals: that restriction is not established by the term itself. Third, each of those twelve individuals, or every member of the twelve tribes, was a Prophet: the verse does not make such an individual-by-individual declaration.

Sūrat Yūsuf separately recounts Joseph’s brothers’ actions, their plan, and what they said about him. These accounts matter for understanding the individuals and their conduct, but should not replace analysis of the word al-asbāṭ itself.

Coordination of al-asbāṭ with the names of Prophets also does not, by itself, establish an identical kind or degree of standing for every person included under the designation. The coordination brings them together in an account of revelation, without specifying the identity and exact number of the individuals in each group.

Conversely, the uncertainty should not be resolved by arbitrarily changing the passage’s sense and claiming that al-asbāṭ must refer exclusively to Prophets arising from among the tribes. This may be an interpretive possibility, but the verse does not explicitly state that restriction.

The more precise conclusion is that al-asbāṭ in Al-Nisāʾ 4:163 is a collective designation among the recipients of revelation, but the designation itself does not provide a list of individuals or establish each person’s individual standing.

This distinction preserves the Qur’an’s explicit attribution of revelation while avoiding conclusions beyond the text.

5. Al-asbāṭ and religious identity

Another occurrence of the root is in Al-Baqarah 2:140:

«أَمْ تَقُولُونَ إِنَّ إِبْرَاهِيمَ وَإِسْمَاعِيلَ وَإِسْحَاقَ وَيَعْقُوبَ وَالْأَسْبَاطَ كَانُوا هُودًا أَوْ نَصَارَىٰ قُلْ أَأَنْتُمْ أَعْلَمُ أَمِ اللَّهُ وَمَنْ أَظْلَمُ مِمَّنْ كَتَمَ شَهَادَةً عِنْدَهُ مِنَ اللَّهِ وَمَا اللَّهُ بِغَافِلٍ عَمَّا تَعْمَلُونَ»

“Or do you say that Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and the asbāṭ were Jews or Christians? Say: Do you know better, or does The God? Who is more unjust than someone who conceals testimony entrusted to him from The God? The God is not unaware of what you do.”

(Al-Baqarah 2:140)

Al-asbāṭ again appears alongside Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, and Jacob. The subject here is the attribution of religious identities to earlier people rather than the sending down of revelation.

The verse challenges the claim that these individuals and groups were Jews or Christians. This usage shows that the asbāṭ belong to the genealogical and religious past of the Children of Israel within Qur’anic discourse; their designation can feature in arguments about identity and religious affiliation.

Nevertheless, sibṭ does not itself mean a Jew, a Christian, a believer, or a Prophet. The designation primarily concerns family relationships and branches of descent. The beliefs and conduct of a tribe’s members are a separate matter, to be established through evidence concerning those people and their period.

A common ancestor does not establish that all subsequent generations held identical beliefs or behaved alike. Various Qur’anic passages describe belief, fulfilment of covenants, opposition, and disobedience among the Children of Israel. Al-asbāṭ thus identifies a genealogical and collective unity rather than an identical moral or religious condition for every member of those communities.

Two levels should be distinguished: the asbāṭ as branches of descent within the Children of Israel, and the claims later groups make about those ancestors’ religious identity. The Qur’an challenges the latter without removing the genealogical sense of the former.

6. Related words and translation challenges

S B Ṭ (sibṭ) and Q B L (qabīlah)

One of the closest words to sibṭ in its collective sense is qabīlah, “tribe.” Both can refer to a community whose members trace themselves to a common ancestor.

In Arabic, qabīlah is a broad designation for groups defined by descent, whereas the Qur’anic uses of asbāṭ concern the Children of Israel. This restriction should not be turned into an essential difference between two kinds of people or two wholly separate social structures.

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:13 uses شُعُوبًا وَقَبَائِلَ, “peoples and tribes,” for human groupings, without using asbāṭ. Al-Aʿrāf 7:160, by contrast, uses أَسْبَاطًا for the twelve groups of the Children of Israel.

Qabīlah can therefore help translate and explain sibṭ, but does not convey all the historical associations and specific Qur’anic usage of al-asbāṭ.

S B Ṭ (sibṭ) and ʾ M M (umam)

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:160, أَسْبَاطًا and أُمَمًا occur together. Sibṭ here is a tribe or branch of descent; ummah in its collective use can be a human community identified by various ties and characteristics.

Their combination shows that the asbāṭ in this verse were distinct communities. Grammatical analyses treat أُمَمًا as a descriptive modifier or an appositional substitute; no coordinating conjunction connects the two words. This grammatical relationship does not mean that sibṭ and ummah are synonymous in every use.

Ummah has a broader range in the Qur’an and is not always based on blood relationship or descent. Sibṭ in this passage, by contrast, concerns family and a branch of descent.

S B Ṭ (sibṭ) and N S L (nasl)

Nasl concerns the continuation of children and offspring across generations. Sibṭ in one of its uses means a child’s child; in collective usage, it designates a particular branch of a family.

Every sibṭ in the genealogical sense thus relates to descent, but not every generation or child is necessarily called a sibṭ in Qur’anic usage. Al-asbāṭ is a particular designation for recognized groups of the Children of Israel.

Sibṭ should also not be restricted to a son or male child. Lexical definitions concerning a child’s child include the children of both sons and daughters. This, however, does not justify translating every occurrence of al-asbāṭ simply as “grandchildren.”

S B Ṭ (sabṭ) and B S Ṭ (basaṭa)

S B Ṭ and B S Ṭ resemble one another in their consonants and some meanings. Basṭ concerns opening and spreading, while extension and length are also recorded under sabṭ.

This proximity can help explain certain physical descriptions, but the roots are distinct. A change in consonant order and a similarity of meaning do not, on their own, prove a historical derivation of one from the other.

In Qur’anic al-asbāṭ, the sense of tribe and branch of descent is established. Understanding the word does not require first imposing a general meaning such as spreading or expansion and then deriving all the characteristics of the Israelite communities from that image.

S B Ṭ (sibṭ) and S B T (sabt)

These roots resemble each other in writing, but their third consonants differ. س ب ط ends in ṭāʾ and is associated in the Qur’an with al-asbāṭ. س ب ت ends in tāʾ and has Qur’anic uses including سَبْت (sabt) and سُبَات (subāt).

The distinction matters particularly because the Qur’anic accounts of the Children of Israel include both the asbāṭ and the Sabbath. Related historical subject matter does not make the two words share a root.

Stopping work, a day of rest, or a rule concerning the Sabbath cannot be derived from sibṭ itself.

Translation recommendations for Forqan

“Tribes” is an appropriate rendering of أَسْبَاطًا in Al-Aʿrāf 7:160. “Twelve tribes” translates the phrase together with its numeral; asbāṭan itself does not mean twelve. أَسْبَاطًا أُمَمًا can be rendered “tribes, as distinct communities.” In this context, “twelve children” or “twelve grandchildren” narrows the collective meaning of the verse.

In Al-Baqarah 2:136 and Āl ʿImrān 3:84, where al-asbāṭ accompanies names of individuals and recipients of revelation, retaining asbāṭ with a brief explanation may be more precise. “Jacob’s descendants” alone can suggest that the passage concerns every descendant without distinction. “The tribes of the Children of Israel,” or “the asbāṭ, the branches of descent within the Children of Israel,” makes the word’s collective range clearer.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:163, too, it is preferable to retain asbāṭ alongside the names of revelation’s recipients. Substituting either “Jacob’s twelve sons” or “all the Prophets of the Children of Israel” identifies the referents more narrowly or extensively than the term itself does. The details belong in a separate explanation rather than the base translation.

In Al-Baqarah 2:140, “the asbāṭ” or “the tribes of the Children of Israel” is appropriate, since the passage concerns applying the labels Jewish and Christian to earlier individuals and groups.

Overall, asbāṭ is best introduced as a Qur’anic and historical term, with its sense of the tribes and branches of descent of the Children of Israel explained at first use. Retaining the term thereafter in translation and analysis can avoid repeated lengthy explanations.

7. Conclusion

Arabic records uses of S B Ṭ concerning extension, length, and smoothness. The same root entry includes words for straight hair, an elongated physique, certain plants, and the relationship of children and descendants. Their inclusion under one lexical root does not, by itself, establish a common historical origin for every use.

Only the form asbāṭ occurs in the Qur’an. Its clearest occurrence, Al-Aʿrāf 7:160, refers to the twelve tribes of the Children of Israel: collective units rather than merely twelve individuals. Other verses use the designation in connection with Abraham’s and Jacob’s family, the sending down of revelation, and the religious identity of earlier people.

The central caution is against automatically equating al-asbāṭ with Jacob’s twelve sons in every verse. The word can be a collective designation for branches of descent. The explicit attribution of revelation to the asbāṭ in Al-Baqarah, Āl ʿImrān, and Al-Nisāʾ must be preserved, but that attribution alone does not identify every member of those tribes or establish each person’s individual standing.

Nor does sibṭ itself designate faith, prophethood, purity of descent, or moral superiority. Family relationships and individuals’ religious standing must be understood separately.

The Qur’anic semantic focus of asbāṭ is family relationship and the branches of descent of the Children of Israel. In Al-Aʿrāf 7:160 it clearly denotes tribes and communities; in the revelation passages, the precise referents cannot be determined from the word alone. This semantic field is compatible with the lexical use of sibṭ for a grandchild or family branch. It does not establish a definitive theory about the word’s botanical origin, the standing of every individual, or uniform beliefs and conduct across generations.
