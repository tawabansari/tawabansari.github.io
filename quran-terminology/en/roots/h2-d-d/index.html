Hadd: violent breaking, collapse, and the disintegration of a solid structure

1. Meaning and background in early Arabic

Arabic hā–dāl–dāl concerns violent breaking, collapse, and the disintegration of something previously solid. Hadda commonly describes demolishing a building, breaking a wall, or bringing down a structure whose parts were connected.

A wall collapsing under a severe blow or pressure can illustrate hadda. The event is more than a small crack or a body’s displacement: the damage destroys its stability and integrity.

Early Arabic sources define hadd as severe breaking and demolition. Hadda al-bināʾ describes breaking and dismantling a building, while inhadda describes something collapsing or breaking.

The family describes breaking objects, collapsing buildings and mountains, and people weakened by calamity.

To assess its pre-Quranic history, however, poetry attributed to that period must be distinguished from uses recorded in later dictionaries. A dictionary’s record of severe destruction does not alone establish pre-Islamic usage.

A poetic witness from ʿAdī ibn Zayd

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic Arab poet ʿAdī ibn Zayd includes:

وَأَطْلُبُ الْخُطَّةَ النَّبِيلَةَ بِالْقُوَّةِ

wa-aṭlubu al-khuṭṭata al-nabīlata bi-al-quwwati

إِذْ يُسْتَهَدُّ طَالِبُهَا

idh yustahaddu ṭālibuhā

“I pursue the noble undertaking with strength when its seeker is deemed weak.”

Yustahaddu here concerns considering someone weak or incapable.

The poet describes someone pursuing something worthy whose ability or standing may be disregarded.

The usage attests the family’s branch of weakness and diminished personal strength in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

The text varies between sources: some lexical transmissions begin with lam aṭlub and end with in yustahadd. The witness is useful for istahadda meaning to deem weak, but its variant readings must be acknowledged; it does not establish the root’s first historical meaning.

A poetic witness from Kuthayyir ʿAzzah

The Umayyad-era poet Kuthayyir ʿAzzah says:

فَلَوْ كَانَ مَا بِي بِالْجِبَالِ لَهَدَّهَا

fa-law kāna mā bī bi-al-jibāli la-haddahā

وَإِنْ كَانَ فِي الدُّنْيَا شَدِيدًا هُدُودُهَا

wa-in kāna fī al-dunyā shadīdan hudūduhā

“Were what afflicts me to fall upon the mountains, it would shatter them, however difficult their destruction may be in this world.”

The poet portrays his grief as so overwhelming that even mountains could not bear it.

La-haddahā means breaking and bringing down the mountains; hudūduhā refers to their collapse and destruction.

The meaning is especially clear because it employs the image of mountains collapsing also found in Maryam 19:90.

Kuthayyir lived after the Quran’s revelation, however. His poetry attests the continuation of this usage in Islamic-era Arabic, rather than independently proving pre-Islamic usage.

Historical conclusion: early witnesses show weakness and deeming someone weak in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period. Violent breaking and collapse are clearly attested in the Quran and in lexical and poetic sources from nearby and later periods. Establishing the first historical meaning of every branch would require further datable evidence.

2. Forms and semantic branches

Important recorded forms include:

  • هَدَّ: broke, demolished, or brought down.
  • يَهُدُّ: breaks or demolishes.
  • هَدّ: severe breaking and demolition; also collapse.
  • هُدُود: collapse and destruction.
  • اِنْهَدَّ: collapsed or broke.
  • هَدَّة: a tremendous noise, especially from something large falling or collapsing.
  • هَدِيد: a loud sound, roar, or heavy rumbling.
  • هَدّ and هِدّ: descriptions of people’s weakness or strength, with differences in vocalization and meaning.
  • اِسْتَهَدَّ: deemed someone weak.
  • تَهَدَّدَ: threatened or intimidated.
  • تَهْدِيد: intimidation or warning of harm or an unwelcome consequence.
  • مُتَهَدِّد: threatening.

These forms are recorded in Arabic, but only هَدًّا occurs in the Quran.

First branch: breaking and collapse

The clearest branch concerns severe breaking and the loss of structural stability.

In هَدَّ الحَائِطَ, a wall is damaged so that it falls apart.

In اِنْهَدَّ الجَبَلُ, the mountain collapses and loses its structure.

Such uses distinguish minor breakage from structural collapse. A rock may crack and remain in place; when a body breaks apart and collapses, hadd is more apt.

Nevertheless, not every occurrence should be equated with total, irreversible destruction. Breaking and collapse do not themselves express the absolute annihilation of matter.

Second branch: tremendous sound and roaring

Haddah describes a heavy, tremendous noise from a falling wall, part of a mountain collapsing, or a similar event.

Hadīd likewise denotes loud sound, roaring, or heavy rumbling in certain uses.

When a large wall collapses, its parts break and a tremendous noise accompanies them. Arabic describes that noise through forms of this family.

Lexical sources also record uses involving thunder, animal roaring, and a heavy sound heard from the sea.

Thus the family is not limited to the visual image of breaking; some forms concern intense sound.

The historical relationship between the branches should not be assumed to run in only one direction. It is not certain that breaking came first and the name of the sound followed, or the reverse.

Third branch: weakness and the breaking of strength

Human applications such as هَدَّهُ الأَمْرُ can describe a severe event exhausting someone’s strength or laying them low.

An English parallel is: “The calamity broke him.”

This means neither literal broken bones nor bodily fracture, but loss of emotional or practical strength and stability.

The example clarifies the semantic relationship without proving the historical origin of the figurative Arabic use.

Lexicons also disagree about hadd as a description of a person. Some apply it to a weak or cowardly person; others distinguish vowel patterns, reporting strength, nobility, or generosity for certain forms.

A single definition such as “a weak person” therefore cannot cover all these forms.

Fourth branch: threatening and intimidation

Tahaddud and tahdīd are used for intimidation and warning of unwelcome consequences.

Someone frightens another with possible harm or announces an intention or possibility of harming them.

The loanword tahdīd is also familiar in contemporary Persian with this meaning.

A semantic connection can be seen between threatening and the prospect of breaking someone’s strength or security: a threat may evoke injury, defeat, or loss.

This does not prove the word’s etymological history. Threatening must be understood from its own attested use, rather than imposed without evidence as Quranic hadd’s primary meaning.

Haddan in Maryam 19:90 does not mean “threatening.”

3. Quranic usage: Maryam 19:90

The root occurs only once in the Quran.

Maryam 19:90

تَكَادُ السَّمَاوَاتُ يَتَفَطَّرْنَ مِنْهُ وَتَنْشَقُّ الْأَرْضُ وَتَخِرُّ الْجِبَالُ هَدًّا

takādu al-samāwātu yatafaṭṭarna minhu wa-tanshaqqu al-arḍu wa-takhirru al-jibālu haddā

Maryam 19:90

“The heavens almost rupture from it, the earth split apart, and the mountains fall in shattered collapse.”

The verse combines three immense images: the heavens rupturing, the earth splitting, and the mountains collapsing.

Haddan specifies the character of the mountains’ fall. This is not simply an intact object falling from a height; the image involves the breaking and disintegration of their solid structure.

Context: attributing a child to the Entirely Merciful

The surrounding verses clarify the image.

Maryam 19:88

وَقَالُوا اتَّخَذَ الرَّحْمَٰنُ وَلَدًا

wa-qālū ittakhadha al-raḥmānu waladā

Maryam 19:88

“They said: ‘The Entirely Merciful has taken a child.’”

Maryam 19:89

لَقَدْ جِئْتُمْ شَيْئًا إِدًّا

la-qad jiʾtum shayʾan iddā

Maryam 19:89

“You have indeed brought forth a monstrous thing.”

Maryam 19:90 follows with the heavens and earth splitting and the mountains collapsing.

Maryam 19:91

أَنْ دَعَوْا لِلرَّحْمَٰنِ وَلَدًا

an daʿaw li-al-raḥmāni waladā

Maryam 19:91

“Because they have attributed a child to the Entirely Merciful.”

Maryam 19:92

وَمَا يَنْبَغِي لِلرَّحْمَٰنِ أَنْ يَتَّخِذَ وَلَدًا

wa-mā yanbaghī li-al-raḥmāni an yattakhidha waladā

Maryam 19:92

“It does not befit the Entirely Merciful to take a child.”

The image’s subject is therefore clear: attributing a child to The God and the incompatibility of that claim with His oneness and incomparability.

To convey the enormity of the claim, the Quran portrays the world’s immense, stable bodies: heavens almost rupturing, earth splitting, and mountains seemingly losing their ability to stand.

Haddan does not mean the claim’s ugliness; it describes the mountains breaking and collapsing. The claim’s gravity and ugliness emerge from the preceding and following verses.

Why mountains?

In ordinary human experience, mountains exemplify stability, immensity, and endurance.

People may see buildings, trees, or small hills damaged by wind, floods, or other natural forces. A great mountain appears vastly more stable.

Describing mountains collapsing creates an image of something ordinarily associated with immense stability falling apart.

The combination of al-jibāl with haddan consequently heightens the image’s force.

This does not imply that mountains scientifically never change. They too undergo erosion, fracture, and transformation. The point concerns their familiar image of grandeur and stability in human language.

Does the verse report mountains actually collapsing?

The sentence begins with takādu, expressing that an event is close to occurring.

The context conveys the enormity and ugliness of a claim through a vast transformation of heavens, earth, and mountains.

Without supporting indications, it should not be converted into a report of an earthquake or mountains actually falling at the moment the claim was uttered.

Conversely, the forceful imagery does not make its physical words unimportant. Yatafaṭṭarna, tanshaqqu, takhirru, and haddan together portray rupture and collapse.

The language can be understood as depicting the gravity and horror of attributing a child to The God without deriving a physical mechanism for the response of heavens and mountains.

Nor does the verse alone establish that mountains biologically possess human emotions or that every false human statement causes a particular geological reaction.

Why does haddan follow takhirru?

Takhirru derives from khāʾ–rāʾ–rāʾ and here expresses the mountains falling.

Haddan derives from hā–dāl–dāl and specifies the intensity and character of that fall.

A simple example clarifies the difference.

A stone falling intact from a wall has fallen; a large wall collapsing into broken parts has undergone both falling and structural destruction.

وَتَخِرُّ الْجِبَالُ هَدًّا combines the two aspects: falling and breaking apart.

Grammatically, haddan is an accusative verbal noun. One analysis treats it as a cognate-accusative substitute, specifying the kind or intensity of the fall through its semantic relation to takhirru.

Another takes the verbal noun as a circumstantial expression, approximately “in a shattered state.”

The grammatical disagreement does not fundamentally alter the image: both analyses concern the mountains’ severe breaking and collapse.

Haddan is not a verbal noun sharing takhirru’s root; the roots differ. If a cognate-accusative function is accepted, it follows from their semantic proximity in the sentence, rather than a shared root.

Does haddan also convey a tremendous noise here?

The Arabic family is also associated with tremendous noise from falling or collapsing.

The image of mountains collapsing can therefore evoke the terrifying sound of their breaking.

In وَتَخِرُّ الْجِبَالُ هَدًّا, however, severe collapse and breaking apart remain the primary, most fitting meaning.

Translating haddan merely as “a loud sound” would lose an important part of the structural destruction.

The tremendous noise is better treated as an imaginable feature of the scene than as the word’s principal equivalent.

4. Comparison with related roots

Hā–dāl–dāl and hā–dāl–mīm: severe collapse and demolition

Hadm concerns demolishing and dismantling a building or structure.

Al-Ḥajj 22:40 uses huddimat for places of worship being demolished.

This shows hā–dāl–mīm directly describing the destruction of buildings in context.

Hadd too can describe demolishing buildings and collapsing structures.

Its principal uses especially emphasize breakage and the severity of collapse. This is not an absolute boundary: the roots overlap in destruction and loss of structure.

Some lexical explanations accordingly define hadd as severe hadm.

Hā–dāl–dāl and khāʾ–rāʾ–rāʾ: breaking and falling

Kharra denotes falling down.

The root occurs in different Quranic settings. Maryam 19:58, for instance, describes Prophets falling in prostration and tears upon hearing the signs of the Entirely Merciful.

Maryam 19:90 uses the same verbal family for mountains falling.

These occurrences show that khurūr does not inherently mean destruction or annihilation. Someone can fall in prostration without breaking or being destroyed.

Haddan in Maryam 19:90, however, adds severe breakage to the mountains’ fall.

Khurūr thus expresses falling here, while hadd emphasizes its destructive character.

Hā–dāl–dāl and shīn–qāf–qāf: splitting and collapse

Shaqqa concerns splitting and the separation of parts.

In Maryam 19:90, وَتَنْشَقُّ الْأَرْضُ describes the earth splitting apart.

The same verse uses haddan for the mountains.

A surface splitting and a body collapsing are different images.

A deep fissure may form in the ground without the entire ground becoming a collapsed heap. A building’s collapse, meanwhile, may involve its parts breaking and falling.

The roots are related in some consequences but are not complete synonyms.

Hā–dāl–dāl and dāl–kāf–kāf: shattering and leveling

Quranic dāl–kāf–kāf concerns crushing and the loss of elevation or structural stability.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:143 uses جَعَلَهُ دَكًّا concerning a mountain.

Al-Fajr 89:21 describes the earth’s transformation with the repeated دَكًّا دَكًّا.

These occurrences emphasize crushing and loss of the former shape.

Hadd likewise concerns collapse and severe breakage, but should not be equated exactly with dakk in every usage.

Maryam 19:90 principally depicts mountains falling and shattering, rather than necessarily explaining how the ground becomes level afterward.

Hā–dāl–dāl and hamzah–dāl–dāl: neighboring roots in successive verses

Maryam 19:89 contains iddan.

Maryam 19:90 contains haddan.

The words resemble one another in sound and writing, but belong to different roots.

Iddan characterizes something enormous, ugly, and horrifying in context.

Haddan denotes severe breaking and collapse.

The two verses first state a claim’s enormity and ugliness, then depict the terrifying rupture of heavens and earth and collapse of mountains.

The words’ sonic proximity reinforces the rhetorical connection, but resemblance in sound does not establish identical roots or lexical meanings.

Hā–dāl–dāl and hā–dāl–hā–dāl: hudhud

Hudhud names the hoopoe, mentioned in Al-Naml 27:20.

The word is indexed under quadriliteral hā–dāl–hā–dāl and should not be assigned to triliteral hā–dāl–dāl simply because hāʾ and dāl recur.

Arabic can use repeated formations in animal names, sounds, and movements, but that pattern alone does not prove historical derivation from a shorter root.

5. Challenges in understanding and translation

Does hadd only mean sound?

No. Its breaking and destruction branch has a clear, independent meaning.

Haddah and hadīd can concern sound, but this does not reduce Quranic haddan to roaring alone.

Its combination with takhirru al-jibāl in Maryam 19:90 supports severe collapse and breaking apart.

Does hadd mean absolute annihilation?

No. Breaking and collapse need not mean all matter disappears or something ceases to exist altogether.

A collapsed building loses its previous form and stability, but its material parts can remain.

A translation should not introduce complete erasure from existence or absolute annihilation without grounds.

Should contemporary tahdīd determine the meaning of haddan?

No. Tahdīd is a familiar Persian loanword from this Arabic family, but its contemporary Persian meaning should not determine every Quranic derivative’s translation.

Maryam 19:90 does not speak of threatening mountains; it depicts them collapsing and breaking apart.

A later or familiar derivative in the target language cannot replace the Quranic form’s own meaning.

Is the verse about a particular natural event?

Maryam 19:90 principally conveys the gravity of attributing a child to The God.

Without evidence, its imagery should not become a geological theory, a collision between celestial bodies, or a specific destructive explosion.

The verse uses intelligible natural imagery to express a theological truth. Its words’ physical meanings matter, but alone do not supply scientific details or a physical mechanism.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Proposed equivalents vary by form and context:

هَدَّ: shattered, brought down, or demolished.

هَدّ: violent breaking, shattering, or destructive collapse.

هُدُود: collapse or destruction.

اِنْهَدَّ: collapsed or broke apart.

هَدَّة: the tremendous crash of something large falling or collapsing.

هَدِيد: heavy rumbling, roaring, or a loud sound.

اِسْتَهَدَّ: deemed weak.

تَهَدَّدَ: threatened.

تَهْدِيد: intimidation or threatening.

The best equivalent for the sole Quranic form, haddan, must fit both the root’s meaning and a natural sentence with takhirru.

A suitable rendering of Maryam 19:90 is:

“The heavens almost rupture from it, the earth split apart, and the mountains fall in shattered collapse.”

This works because “fall” conveys takhirru and “shattered collapse” preserves haddan’s image.

“The mountains fall with a loud noise” might convey an audible aspect, but does not sufficiently express breakage and structural collapse.

“The mountains are annihilated” is also imprecise, substituting absolute destruction for breaking and falling apart.

“Shattered” or “in violent collapse” are therefore suitable equivalents for haddan here.

7. Conclusion

The principal branch of hā–dāl–dāl concerns violent breaking, collapse, and the disintegration of solid structures. Other Arabic forms concern tremendous sound, weakness or deeming someone weak, and threatening.

Poetic and lexical witnesses establish several semantic fields, but these should not all be traced to one certain original image without historical evidence.

Only haddan occurs in the Quran. With takhirru al-jibāl in Maryam 19:90, it depicts the mountains collapsing and breaking apart violently.

The context uses that image to express the enormity and ugliness of attributing a child to the Entirely Merciful. The lexical meaning of collapse remains distinguishable from the verse’s theological message.

Final definition for Forqan:

The principal branch of hā–dāl–dāl concerns violently breaking or collapsing something formerly stable and integrated. Other branches concerning tremendous sound, weakness, and threats are also recorded, but each derivative must be understood in its own use.

In its sole Quranic occurrence, haddan means severe collapse and breaking apart. Alongside the mountains falling, it expresses the loss of their stability and integrity.
