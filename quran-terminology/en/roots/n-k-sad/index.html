A study of nukūṣ in early Arabic and the Qur’an: from retreat in confrontation and refraining from action to withdrawing from a commitment and turning away from divine verses

The three-letter root N K Ṣ in Arabic concerns retreating, drawing back, and refraining from continuing something toward which someone has advanced or intended to act. When someone retreats before danger, abandons a confrontation, or withdraws from an undertaking, the verb nakaṣa can describe that behavior.

A recognized expression is «نَكَصَ عَلَىٰ عَقِبَيْهِ»: he drew back on his heels or withdrew from his previous course or action.

The expression relates to backward movement, but its use is not restricted to physical retreat. Someone may abandon a decision, refrain from continuing an activity, or retreat from a previous position when facing difficulty, without necessarily taking actual steps backward.

N K Ṣ occurs only twice in the Qur’an. Al-Anfāl 8:48 describes Satan, who first encourages a group to expect victory, then retreats when the two groups confront one another and disowns his companions. Al-Muʾminūn 23:66 describes people to whom divine verses were recited but who turned away from them.

Despite their different settings, both occurrences share the idea of retreating from an encounter ahead. In the first, retreat accompanies abandoning people previously promised support. In the second, it concerns turning away from divine verses.

Nukūṣ should nevertheless not be equated in every use with fear, breaking a covenant, or denying truth. These features may accompany retreat, but do not follow from the root alone.

1. Forms and semantic core

Important forms of this family in Arabic include:

  • نَكَصَ: retreated, drew back, or refrained from continuing an action.
  • يَنْكِصُ and يَنْكُصُ: retreats or withdraws from a previous undertaking; both imperfect forms are attested.
  • نَكْص: retreat or withdrawal from an undertaking.
  • نُكُوص: drawing back, turning away from a task, or refraining from continuing it.
  • نَاكِص: someone who retreats or withdraws from an undertaking.
  • نَكِيصَة: falling behind or refraining from action.
  • مَنْكَص: a place or position of retreat; in some uses, a place aside or somewhere to withdraw.
  • أَنْكَصَ: made another retreat; attested in early lexical accounts.
  • نَكَّصَ and تَنْكِيص: in attested Arabic uses, made another refrain from an undertaking or retreat.

Only the basic verb nakaṣa occurs in the Qur’an:

نَكَصَ in Al-Anfāl 8:48 is a third-person masculine singular perfect verb.

تَنْكِصُونَ in Al-Muʾminūn 23:66 is a second-person masculine plural imperfect verb.

Thus N K Ṣ occurs twice, in two verses, and only in one verbal stem.

Nouns such as nukūṣ, nākiṣ, nakīṣah, and mankaṣ are known in Arabic, but these forms themselves do not occur in the Qur’an.

Semantic core: drawing back and refraining from continuing

Nukūṣ relates to iḥjām: refraining and drawing back from something a person intended or was able to undertake.

The expression nakaṣa ʿalā ʿaqibayhi is used in some contexts of someone drawing back from an undertaking out of fear or cowardice.

Alongside refraining from action, returning backward is also an established meaning of this family.

These uses reveal two closely related aspects.

The first is actual retreat, as when someone moves backward before an enemy or danger.

The second is refraining from continuing an undertaking, as when someone decides to act but abandons it on facing difficulty.

In the second use, bodily movement is unnecessary. Someone may remain in the same place but withdraw from their undertaking or previous position.

The semantic core therefore cannot be defined solely as physical flight.

Conversely, extending nukūṣ to every change of decision or return would erase its distinctive feature. Drawing back and refraining from advancing or continuing an action are prominent in this family.

The central meaning of N K Ṣ is therefore retreating and drawing back from a course or undertaking toward which someone has turned.

Fear, weakness, caution, a change of mind, or abandoning others may motivate or result from this retreat. None alone defines every use of the root.

2. N K Ṣ in early Arabic

Retreating before danger

A recognized use of this root concerns confrontation and battle.

Arabic uses the expression:

نَكَصَ عَلَىٰ عَقِبَيْهِ

nakaṣa ʿalā ʿaqibayhi

Translation: “He drew back on his heels.”

Also:

نَكَصَ عَنِ الْأَمْرِ

nakaṣa ʿani al-amri

Translation: “He withdrew from that undertaking and refrained from continuing it.”

The first expression emphasizes the image of backward movement.

The second concerns an undertaking from which someone withdraws.

Early explanations sometimes associate nukūṣ with moving backward without first turning completely around.

In this use, backward movement accompanies drawing away and fleeing.

Other explanations recognize a broader range, using nukūṣ for return and retreat generally, even when someone does not necessarily walk backward.

The distinction matters. The basic image involves backward movement, while extended uses can describe refraining and withdrawing from an undertaking without a particular bodily movement.

Pre-Islamic poetry: warriors who do not retreat

Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā, a pre-Islamic poet, describes warriors standing firm in battle in a poem praising Harim ibn Sinān:

هُمْ يَضْرِبُونَ حَبِيكَ الْبِيضِ إِذْ لَحِقُوا
لَا يَنْكُصُونَ إِذَا مَا اسْتُلْحِمُوا وَحَمُوا

hum yaḍribūna ḥabīka al-bīḍi idh laḥiqū / lā yankuṣūna idhā mā ustulḥimū wa-ḥamū

Translation: “When they reach the enemy, they strike the patterned helmets; when caught in fierce battle and stirred by proud resolve, they do not retreat.”

Lā yankuṣūna in this verse describes refusing to retreat in difficult conditions of war.

The poet speaks of warriors who do not abandon their position when confrontation intensifies.

Ḥabīk concerns patterns and lines woven or formed on the surface of helmets; al-bīḍ here is the plural of bayḍah, a helmet.

In the second line, ustulḥimū concerns becoming caught in fierce battle, while ḥamū relates to aroused proud resolve and refusal of humiliation.

The important point is that nukūṣ stands against continuing the battle.

By denying retreat, the poet praises the warriors’ steadfastness.

The verse has been transmitted with different wordings. Some versions end the second line with laḥimū; the version used here has wa-ḥamū.

The verse clearly supports the use of N K Ṣ in pre-Qur’anic Arabic.

The praise of steadfast warriors does not show that every retreat was linguistically blameworthy. The poet’s judgment relates to the battle setting and the purpose of the poem.

The root itself describes the behavior of retreating.

Retreat contrasted with advancing

Another verse transmitted in early linguistic and interpretive discussions reads:

لَيْسَ النُّكُوصُ عَلَى الْأَعْقَابِ مَكْرُمَةً
إِنَّ الْمَكَارِمَ إِقْدَامٌ عَلَى الْأَسَلِ

laysa al-nukūṣu ʿalā al-aʿqābi makrumatan / inna al-makārima iqdāmun ʿalā al-asali

Translation: “Retreating on one’s heels is no honor; noble deeds lie in advancing toward the spears.”

Nukūṣ here contrasts with iqdām.

Iqdām in this context concerns advancing and entering confrontation.

Nukūṣ, conversely, concerns drawing back and withdrawing from it.

This opposition is a particularly clear way of understanding nukūṣ in a battle setting.

The speaker and exact date of this verse have not been established in the material examined. It therefore cannot confidently be attributed to a particular pre-Islamic poet.

It shows the recognized expression al-nukūṣ ʿalā al-aʿqāb in early usage; Zuhayr’s verse has firmer attribution and support in the transmitted poetry.

Does nukūṣ only mean withdrawing from something good?

Some lexical explanations restrict nakaṣa ʿalā ʿaqibayhi to withdrawing from something good, treating withdrawal from something bad as unattested.

This describes a tendency in usage: in many settings, nukūṣ concerns someone refraining from a desirable, courageous, or expected action.

In Zuhayr’s verse, for example, the warriors’ refusal to retreat signifies steadfastness.

This explanation should not become an absolute semantic rule.

Al-Anfāl 8:48 itself attributes nukūṣ to Satan when he withdraws support from people whose wrongful deeds he had previously made attractive to them.

His retreat here does not mean an ethical return to the right path. It accompanies abandoning those whom he had promised to support.

One therefore cannot claim that every nukūṣ necessarily abandons a good action, or that everyone who retreats had previously followed the right path.

The root means retreating and withdrawing from an undertaking. Whether the previous course was right or wrong must be established separately.

Nukūṣ and withdrawing from a decision

The root is not used exclusively for military confrontation.

The following expression is also recognized:

أَرَادَ أَمْرًا ثُمَّ نَكَصَ عَنْهُ

arāda amran thumma nakaṣa ʿanhu

Translation: “He intended an undertaking, then drew back from it.”

The person may not yet have begun the activity. He intended to act but changed his mind before doing or continuing it.

Nukūṣ therefore need not mean abandoning something already fully carried out.

Nakīṣah is likewise explained in Arabic as falling behind and refraining from action.

Alongside returning from a path already traveled, this family can thus express refraining from advancing along a course toward which someone has turned.

This matters for the Qur’an: Al-Muʾminūn 23:66 does not necessarily concern people who had accepted the verses and later abandoned faith. It describes people to whom the verses were recited, but who drew back and turned away from them.

3. Al-Anfāl 8:48: Satan’s retreat after promising support

وَإِذْ زَيَّنَ لَهُمُ الشَّيْطَانُ أَعْمَالَهُمْ وَقَالَ لَا غَالِبَ لَكُمُ الْيَوْمَ مِنَ النَّاسِ وَإِنِّي جَارٌ لَّكُمْ فَلَمَّا تَرَاءَتِ الْفِئَتَانِ نَكَصَ عَلَىٰ عَقِبَيْهِ وَقَالَ إِنِّي بَرِيءٌ مِّنكُمْ إِنِّي أَرَىٰ مَا لَا تَرَوْنَ إِنِّي أَخَافُ اللَّهَ وَاللَّهُ شَدِيدُ الْعِقَابِ

wa-idh zayyana lahumu al-shayṭānu aʿmālahum wa-qāla lā ghāliba lakumu al-yawma mina al-nāsi wa-innī jārun lakum fa-lammā tarāʾati al-fiʾatāni nakaṣa ʿalā ʿaqibayhi wa-qāla innī barīʾun minkum innī arā mā lā tarawna innī akhāfu allāha wa-allāhu shadīdu al-ʿiqābi

Translation:

“When Satan made their deeds attractive to them and said: No one among the people will overcome you today, and I am your protector. But when the two groups came into view of one another, he drew back on his heels and said: I am free of you; I see what you do not see; I fear The God. And The God is severe in punishment.”

Al-Anfāl 8:48

This is the first Qur’anic occurrence of N K Ṣ.

Satan first makes a group’s deeds appear attractive to them and encourages them to expect victory.

He says no one will overcome them that day and that he will protect and support them.

When the two groups confront one another, however, he retreats and disowns the very people whom he had promised to support.

The meaning of «نَكَصَ عَلَىٰ عَقِبَيْهِ»

Nakaṣa here is a perfect verb of the basic stem.

Its subject, given the beginning of the verse, is Satan.

ʿAqibayhi is the dual of ʿaqib with a third-person masculine singular pronoun: his two heels.

The construction presents the image of someone moving backward on his heels.

Arabic recognizes this expression for retreat and withdrawal from a previous position.

The literal meaning must nevertheless be distinguished from the precise manner of the event.

The linguistic image concerns moving backward, but the expression alone does not explain Satan’s physical form or manner of presence in that situation.

Some early narrative interpretations describe Satan appearing among the opponents in the form of a particular person, then fleeing on seeing the angels. Other interpretations explain the verse through deception, temptation, and Satan’s subsequent abandonment of his followers.

The verse itself does not name that person or describe Satan’s bodily form.

Understanding nukūṣ therefore does not require treating details of external narratives as certain.

In either case, the Qur’an describes Satan withdrawing his claimed support.

«فَلَمَّا تَرَاءَتِ الْفِئَتَانِ»: when the retreat occurred

The phrase fa-lammā tarāʾati al-fiʾatāni indicates that the retreat occurred after the two groups confronted one another.

The preceding verses discuss the believers’ confrontation with their opponents, preparation, steadfastness, and battle conditions.

The retreat thus occurs in a real situation of encounter and danger.

Grammatically, nakaṣa is the response to lammā.

That is, when the two groups saw one another, Satan retreated.

The verb itself does not identify the reason for that retreat.

Its reason or explanation comes from Satan’s following words and the verse’s context.

Does nukūṣ inherently mean being afraid?

Fear and cowardice are recognized motivations for nukūṣ in lexical descriptions.

Satan also says in this verse:

إِنِّي أَرَىٰ مَا لَا تَرَوْنَ إِنِّي أَخَافُ اللَّهَ

innī arā mā lā tarawna innī akhāfu allāha

“I see what you do not see; I fear The God.”

The verse thus mentions fear in Satan’s speech.

These are, however, words attributed to Satan. Reporting them does not alone prove that his claim about his motive is ethically truthful and trustworthy.

The Qur’an has already described him making others’ deeds attractive to them and promising support.

His nukūṣ should therefore not be interpreted as repentance or an ethical return to truth.

Conversely, being afraid is not the independent meaning of nukūṣ.

Someone may retreat out of fear, but may also withdraw because of changing conditions, inability, caution, or another motive.

Here, fear appears in the speaker’s words and the situation of confrontation, not in the verb’s lexical structure.

Nukūṣ and abandoning companions

Before the confrontation, Satan had said:

وَإِنِّي جَارٌ لَّكُمْ

wa-innī jārun lakum

“I am your protector.”

When retreating, however, he says:

إِنِّي بَرِيءٌ مِّنكُمْ

innī barīʾun minkum

“I am free of you.”

This contrast matters.

Support and protection are offered at first, but withdrawal and disavowal replace them at the moment of danger.

Nukūṣ in this context thus accompanies abandoning people who had trusted the earlier promise.

“Betrayal” or “breaking a covenant” should nevertheless not become its lexical meaning.

Any betrayal or disloyalty understood from Satan’s behavior comes from comparing his earlier promise with his later withdrawal.

Nukūṣ itself expresses retreat.

This distinction also matters when comparing N K Ṣ with N K TH.

Does this retreat mean abandoning deception?

The verse shows Satan withdrawing his claimed support in that situation.

It does not establish that he has generally stopped misleading humanity or reformed his position toward truth and falsehood.

Al-Ḥashr 59:16 offers another example of this pattern of deception and withdrawal:

كَمَثَلِ الشَّيْطَانِ إِذْ قَالَ لِلْإِنسَانِ اكْفُرْ فَلَمَّا كَفَرَ قَالَ إِنِّي بَرِيءٌ مِّنكَ إِنِّي أَخَافُ اللَّهَ رَبَّ الْعَالَمِينَ

ka-mathali al-shayṭāni idh qāla li-al-insāni ukfur fa-lammā kafara qāla innī barīʾun minka innī akhāfu allāha rabba al-ʿālamīna

Translation: “Like Satan when he said to the human being: Disbelieve. When he disbelieved, he said: I am free of you; I fear The God, Lord of all worlds.”

The same pattern of calling someone toward wrongful behavior and subsequently disowning them appears here.

Although N K Ṣ does not occur in Al-Ḥashr 59:16, the verse helps explain the behavior described in Al-Anfāl 8:48.

The central point is that someone encouraging wrongdoing may abandon the person who accepted that encouragement when its consequences arrive.

This contextual connection clarifies retreat in Al-Anfāl without making every feature of the behavior a fixed component of N K Ṣ.

4. Al-Muʾminūn 23:66: drawing back before divine verses

قَدْ كَانَتْ آيَاتِي تُتْلَىٰ عَلَيْكُمْ فَكُنْتُمْ عَلَىٰ أَعْقَابِكُمْ تَنْكِصُونَ

qad kānat āyātī tutlā ʿalaykum fa-kuntum ʿalā aʿqābikum tankiṣūna

Forqan’s rendering:

“My signs were recited to you, but you used to turn back on your heels,”

Al-Muʾminūn 23:66

This is the second and final Qur’anic occurrence of N K Ṣ.

Unlike Al-Anfāl 8:48, it does not concern Satan’s retreat when two groups confront one another.

It concerns people’s response to divine verses recited to them, from which they turned away.

The meaning of «تَنْكِصُونَ»

Tankiṣūna is a second-person masculine plural imperfect verb of the basic nakaṣa.

It means “you retreat” or “you draw back.”

The verb here follows fa-kuntum.

This construction describes the listeners’ past behavior as repeated or continuing: the verses were recited to them, and they drew back before them.

The verb’s own meaning should not be changed to “declaring false,” “disbelieving,” or “insulting.”

Such behaviors may be present in context, but nukūṣ concerns the retreat and turning away itself.

«عَلَىٰ أَعْقَابِكُمْ»: retreating on your heels

Aʿqāb is the plural of ʿaqib, meaning heel.

In Al-Anfāl 8:48, ʿaqibayhi is dual and refers to the two heels of the singular subject.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:66 uses the plural aʿqābikum in addressing a group of people.

Both expressions present an image of drawing back and withdrawing from a position ahead.

In Al-Muʾminūn, however, the movement need not be restricted to physically stepping backward when hearing the verses.

The listeners may remain seated or standing in the same place while turning away from hearing, accepting, or following the verses’ message.

In this setting, drawing back on one’s heels can depict withdrawing from an encounter with the verses.

This extension is recognized in Arabic, where nukūṣ describes refraining from continuing an activity as well as backward movement.

Had they previously believed?

This question helps establish the limits of nukūṣ.

One might assume that anyone described as retreating on their heels must have previously accepted truth and subsequently abandoned faith.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:66 does not explicitly state that.

It says the verses were recited to them, but they drew back.

Hearing a message is not the same as accepting it.

Nukūṣ here may therefore describe turning away when encountering the verses, without requiring the listeners to have previously believed and then left the religion.

Comparison with other verses, such as Muḥammad 47:25, clarifies the distinction:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ ارْتَدُّوا عَلَىٰ أَدْبَارِهِم مِّن بَعْدِ مَا تَبَيَّنَ لَهُمُ الْهُدَى

inna alladhīna irtaddū ʿalā adbārihim min baʿdi mā tabayyana lahumu al-hudā

Translation: “Those who turned back after guidance had become clear to them…”

Here, irtaddū and the phrase “after guidance had become clear to them” describe a more specific situation.

In Al-Muʾminūn 23:66, the use of nukūṣ alone is insufficient to establish that same situation.

Tankiṣūna should therefore not be translated without evidence as “you were abandoning faith.”

Its connection with arrogance and speech

The following verse says:

مُسْتَكْبِرِينَ بِهِ سَامِرًا تَهْجُرُونَ

mustakbirīna bihi sāmiran tahjurūna

Forqan’s rendering:

“Arrogant concerning it, speaking idly in your night gatherings.”

Al-Muʾminūn 23:67

This links the listeners’ behavior with arrogance and the speech of their nighttime gatherings.

Grammatically, mustakbirīna describes the condition of the people drawing back in the preceding verse.

Nukūṣ in this context therefore accompanies arrogant behavior.

Arrogance, however, belongs to K B R, not N K Ṣ.

Tahjurūna likewise belongs to another family and explains their speech or turning away.

The antecedent of bihi and the precise meaning of tahjurūna have been disputed: for example, whether the pronoun refers to the Qur’an or to a place in which the listeners took pride.

These differences matter for a full examination of the verse, but do not change the independent meaning of tankiṣūna.

What is clear across the connected verses is the listeners’ turning away from the verses and continuing arrogant behavior.

From turning away to failing to reflect

Al-Muʾminūn 23:68 says:

أَفَلَمْ يَدَّبَّرُوا الْقَوْلَ أَمْ جَاءَهُم مَّا لَمْ يَأْتِ آبَاءَهُمُ الْأَوَّلِينَ

a-fa-lam yaddabbarū al-qawla am jāʾahum mā lam yaʾti ābāʾahumu al-awwalīna

Translation: “Have they not reflected on the word, or has something come to them that did not come to their earliest forefathers?”

This question follows the description of nukūṣ.

Subsequent verses discuss recognizing the messenger, alleging madness, and most of them disliking truth.

In Al-Muʾminūn 23:70, the Qur’an says:

بَلْ جَاءَهُم بِالْحَقِّ وَأَكْثَرُهُمْ لِلْحَقِّ كَارِهُونَ

bal jāʾahum bi-al-ḥaqqi wa-aktharuhum li-al-ḥaqqi kārihūna

Translation: “Rather, he has brought them the truth, but most of them dislike the truth.”

This sequence connects their retreat before the verses with rejecting truth and failing to reflect.

The root’s meaning must still be distinguished from the contextual conclusion.

Nukūṣ concerns retreating and turning away. Failure to reflect, arrogance, and dislike of truth are features stated by the accompanying verses.

Is every withdrawal before a statement blameworthy?

No.

Someone hearing a statement may refrain from accepting it hastily in order to investigate its truth.

Such caution is not necessarily blameworthy.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:66 does not merely concern pausing to examine a claim. The Qur’an describes the listeners’ behavior in a setting of arrogance, failure to reflect, and dislike of truth.

The moral judgment therefore comes from their overall behavior.

The verb nukūṣ alone cannot establish that every reversal of a decision or refusal to continue along a path is sinful.

Sometimes, returning from a wrong path is itself right and necessary.

5. Limits to the moral evaluation of nukūṣ

In both Qur’anic occurrences, retreat appears in a blameworthy situation.

In Al-Anfāl, Satan withdraws his claimed support.

In Al-Muʾminūn, a group turns away from encountering divine verses.

These occurrences do not establish that N K Ṣ inherently means a forbidden act, betrayal, or disbelief.

Whether retreat is right or wrong depends on its subject and circumstances.

Al-Anfāl 8:15, for example, prohibits turning one’s back in military confrontation. Al-Anfāl 8:16 makes exceptions for repositioning in battle or joining another group.

Even in a military setting, moving backward or changing position cannot always be judged identically without considering the reason and purpose.

In ordinary life, someone may also withdraw from a wrong decision after finding new evidence or recognizing its harm.

That return may show a willingness to reform.

If someone withdraws after recognizing truth and accepting a clear responsibility because they fear lost advantages or pressure from others, however, the behavior may be morally blameworthy.

This judgment comes from the circumstances of the act, not from N K Ṣ itself.

6. N K Ṣ and related roots

N K Ṣ and N K S

These roots are very close in their letters and some uses, but do not have identical meanings.

N K Ṣ concerns retreating, drawing back, and refraining from continuing an undertaking.

N K S concerns turning something upside down and reversing its direction or condition.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:65 says:

ثُمَّ نُكِسُوا عَلَىٰ رُءُوسِهِمْ

thumma nukisū ʿalā ruʾūsihim

“Then they were turned upside down upon their heads.”

The verb here belongs to N K S, not N K Ṣ.

In Al-Sajdah 32:12, nākisū ruʾūsihim likewise concerns lowered heads.

In Al-Anfāl 8:48, however, nakaṣa ʿalā ʿaqibayhi concerns Satan’s retreat.

N K S highlights inversion of direction or condition; N K Ṣ highlights drawing back and withdrawing from an undertaking.

The ideas may approach one another in some settings, but should not be conflated because of similar sounds.

Study of N K S

N K Ṣ and R J ʿ

R J ʿ has the broad Arabic meaning of returning.

People may return from a journey, abandon an undertaking, or regain a previous condition.

In its recognized uses, N K Ṣ places greater emphasis on retreating and refraining from continuing a path or action.

Someone returning home after a journey is not necessarily called nākiṣ.

Someone abandoning a path before danger or drawing back in confrontation may be described with nukūṣ.

This is a semantic tendency, not an absolute rule.

Lexical explanations also describe nukūṣ through returning, but not every use of R J ʿ falls within its field.

Study of R J ʿ

N K Ṣ and F R R

Farra in its familiar use concerns fleeing.

Nakaṣa may likewise express retreat and flight in a dangerous situation.

The ideas approach one another in Al-Anfāl 8:48, where Satan retreats as the two groups confront one another.

In Al-Muʾminūn 23:66, however, nukūṣ concerns turning away from verses and need not mean the listeners actually fled a place.

Flight is therefore one situation in which nukūṣ can occur, but not all its uses can be restricted to physical flight.

Fear may also motivate flight, but the verb nukūṣ does not always determine its degree or kind.

N K Ṣ and N F R

N F R in some uses concerns shying away or avoiding something; in others, movement and setting out to undertake a task.

N K Ṣ emphasizes retreating from a course or action toward which someone has turned.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:46 says:

وَلَّوْا عَلَىٰ أَدْبَارِهِمْ نُفُورًا

wallaw ʿalā adbārihim nufūran

“They turn their backs and flee.”

The scene resembles drawing back on your heels in Al-Muʾminūn 23:66.

Both involve turning away and withdrawing.

N F R and N K Ṣ are nevertheless independent families, with different ranges of derivatives.

For example, infirū in some verses concerns setting out to fulfill a responsibility, not retreating.

Study of N F R

N K Ṣ and N K TH

Nakth in Arabic concerns unravelling twisted strands and breaking a covenant, oath, or pledge of allegiance.

Nukūṣ concerns retreating and refraining from continuing an undertaking.

Someone may withdraw from a commitment and consequently break a covenant. Both concepts may then describe different aspects of the behavior.

That overlap does not make the roots identical.

Someone may retreat before an enemy without breaking any covenant.

Conversely, someone may secretly violate a covenant without retreating in a physical confrontation.

In Al-Anfāl 8:48, Satan withdraws from his earlier promise of support. Its consequences may involve disloyalty, but the verse uses nukūṣ to describe the retreat itself.

The verb’s meaning should therefore remain distinct from the behavior’s moral consequence.

Study of N K TH

N K Ṣ and W L Y

Some constructions of W L Y concern turning one’s back or turning away.

Al-Anfāl 8:15, for example, says:

فَلَا تُوَلُّوهُمُ الْأَدْبَارَ

fa-lā tuwallūhumu al-adbāra

“Do not turn your backs to them.”

This expresses turning away and presenting one’s back to the other side.

Nukūṣ may accompany the same behavior, but does not necessarily have the same construction and image.

Nukūṣ emphasizes retreating and drawing back; tawliyat al-adbār emphasizes turning one’s back to the other side.

Someone may move backward while still facing the other side. Someone may also turn around without moving backward at that moment.

In actual situations, these behaviors can of course occur together.

The distinction should therefore be understood as a tendency, not an absolute boundary without overlap.

Study of W L Y

7. Proposed translations for Forqan

For general uses of nakaṣa, “retreated,” “drew back,” “went backward,” and “withdrew from continuing the undertaking” are suitable.

“Retreated” is more natural in settings of danger and confrontation.

For positions and decisions, “withdrew from his position” or “refrained from continuing the undertaking” may be more precise.

For the verbal noun nukūṣ, “retreat” and “withdrawal” are suitable.

For nākiṣ, “retreating” or, in some contexts, “someone withdrawing from an undertaking” can be used.

These words should not always be translated as “coward,” “traitor,” or “disbeliever,” since those meanings depend on the person’s behavior and circumstances.

Al-Anfāl 8:48

نَكَصَ عَلَىٰ عَقِبَيْهِ

nakaṣa ʿalā ʿaqibayhi

Forqan’s current rendering:

“He turned back on his heels.”

This is accurate and natural.

“Turned back” conveys the verb nukūṣ, while “on his heels” preserves the Qur’anic expression’s image.

Contextual explanation can add that, after promising support, Satan withdrew his claimed backing when the two groups confronted one another and disowned his companions.

All that explanation should not be placed inside the verb’s independent meaning.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:66

فَكُنْتُمْ عَلَىٰ أَعْقَابِكُمْ تَنْكِصُونَ

fa-kuntum ʿalā aʿqābikum tankiṣūna

Forqan’s current rendering:

“You used to turn back on your heels.”

This likewise preserves the image of the root and the verse’s construction.

Explanation may clarify that drawing back in this context means turning away from divine verses recited to the listeners.

It should not be changed without evidence to “you were abandoning faith,” since the verse alone does not establish those listeners’ prior acceptance of faith.

Brief root meaning and meanings in the verses

Brief root meaning: retreating, drawing back, and refraining from continuing a course or undertaking toward which someone has turned.

In Al-Anfāl 8:48: he retreated; in context, Satan withdrew his claimed support as the two groups confronted one another and disowned those whom he had promised to help.

In Al-Muʾminūn 23:66: you were drawing back; in context, the listeners’ withdrawal and turning away from divine verses as they were recited.

In both cases, the root’s meaning must remain distinct from the motive and moral judgment of the behavior.

8. Conclusion

The Arabic root N K Ṣ concerns retreating, drawing back, and refraining from continuing something toward which someone has turned.

In physical uses, nukūṣ can mean actual backward movement, particularly before danger or confrontation.

In extended uses, the family describes withdrawing from a decision, refraining from action, and turning away from a situation encountered.

Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā’s pre-Islamic verse contrasts nukūṣ with standing firm and advancing in battle. Early lexical descriptions also connect it with iḥjām, drawing back and refraining from action.

Fear and cowardice should nevertheless not be considered permanent components of nukūṣ. The claim that it only concerns withdrawing from something good also fails to fit its broader use, especially Al-Anfāl 8:48.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an.

In Al-Anfāl 8:48, Satan makes a group’s deeds attractive to them and promises support, then retreats and disowns them as the two groups confront one another. Nukūṣ here involves abandoning companions and withdrawing from a previous position.

In Al-Muʾminūn 23:66, nukūṣ takes place before divine verses. The verses were recited to the listeners, but they drew back from encountering them. Subsequent verses relate this behavior to arrogance, failure to reflect, and dislike of truth.

The occurrences show that nukūṣ does not necessarily mean abandoning a faith already accepted. Someone may turn away from an invitation or message from the outset.

Retreat also has no fixed moral value in itself. Whether it is right or wrong depends on the course abandoned, the circumstances of the decision, and the responsibility undertaken.

Nukūṣ should therefore not be treated as fully synonymous with breaking a covenant, apostasy, flight, or fear, although it overlaps with those concepts in some settings.

Final definition: N K Ṣ primarily denotes retreating and drawing back from a course, position, or undertaking toward which someone has turned. This may be actual backward movement or refraining and withdrawing from continuing an undertaking. In the Qur’an, nakaṣa describes Satan’s retreat from his claimed support, while tankiṣūna describes listeners turning away from divine verses. Fear, disloyalty, arrogance, or denial of truth are features understood from the context of each occurrence, not from the root’s independent meaning.
