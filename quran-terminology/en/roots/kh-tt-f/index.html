Qur’anic Root

The Qur’anic Root KH Ṭ F

To snatch, seize suddenly, carry off swiftly, and separate something from its place before it has time to resist or stabilize

Important Forms from This Root

خَطِفَ / خَطَفَ (khaṭifa / khaṭafa)

khaṭifa / khaṭafa; snatched suddenly, seized quickly, carried off from its place.

يَخْطَفُ (yakhṭafu)

yakhṭafu; snatches, seizes quickly and without delay.

خَطْف / خَطْفَة (khaṭf / khaṭfah)

khaṭf; swift snatching. Khaṭfah; a brief, passing, unstable snatch.

تَخَطَّفَ / يَتَخَطَّفُ (takhaṭṭafa / yatakhaṭṭafu)

takhaṭṭafa / yatakhaṭṭafu; was exposed to snatching, was seized from the sides, was in a condition of sudden insecurity.

Method Note

The root KH Ṭ F should not be reduced to general words like “take” or “obtain.” In Arabic, taking can be calm, legal, gradual, or open. In khaṭf, speed, surprise, and sudden separation are central. What is snatched has no time to defend itself, prepare, or hold its place. It is suddenly pulled away from its support.

In the Qur’an, this root appears in different scenes: the image of lightning, the action of birds, social and political insecurity, and the stealthy snatching of something from the field of news or hearing. The shared feature is the loss of safety in a very short moment. Khaṭf is taking something at the instant before it can hold itself.

Central Meaning

The central meaning of KH Ṭ F is sudden swift taking: taking in such a way that the relation between the thing and its previous place is cut at once. The meaning is tied to speed and surprise. The snatching agent may be a predatory bird, lightning, an enemy, or a hidden force that seizes a fragment of information.

So khaṭf is not merely taking. It is striking, unprepared taking. What is snatched usually does not remain in its former condition. It is torn from its place, removed from the field of safety, or deprived of its former function.

Material Image

The material image is a bird of prey suddenly diving from above or from the side and carrying off prey before it has time to escape. The time is short, and the distance between sensing danger and being taken is extremely small.

Another image is a sudden flash that overwhelms the eye. The eye wants to see, but the speed and intensity of light are so strong that it almost takes away the function of sight. Here the snatching is not directed at a physical object, but at the function of an organ: the power to see.

Classical Arabic Note

In classical Arabic, KH Ṭ F is used for grabbing something quickly: carrying off prey, suddenly taking something from a hand or from the ground, or seizing a piece of speech or news quickly. The word carries speed, surprise, and separation from the previous place.

The word خَطْفَة (khaṭfah) points to a short, unstable taking: not full reception, not encompassing knowledge, and not stable access. A person may catch only a khaṭfah of something: a brief glance, a sudden hearing, or a fragment of news. This meaning is especially important in the Qur’anic use concerning hidden snatching in the field of information.

Qur’anic Usage

The Visual Faculty Startled in the Parable of Ṣayyib

In يَكَادُ الْبَرْقُ يَخْطَفُ أَبْصَارَهُمْ (yakādu al-barqu yakhṭafu abṣārahum), the lightning is so intense and sudden that it is close to snatching away their sight 2:20. Here khaṭf does not mean the physical eyes are carried away. It means the power of seeing is almost taken, and the visual faculty is overwhelmed.

The pairing of yakādu and يَخْطَفُ (yakhṭafu) is precise. Yakādu shows that the event has reached the threshold, and yakhṭafu identifies the event: swift sudden snatching. Lightning in this parable is not steady, peaceful illumination. It leaps suddenly and nearly disables the power of sight.

Falling from Tawḥīd and Being Snatched from the Sides

In وَمَن يُشْرِكْ بِاللَّهِ فَكَأَنَّمَا خَرَّ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ فَتَخْطَفُهُ الطَّيْرُ أَوْ تَهْوِي بِهِ الرِّيحُ فِي مَكَانٍ سَحِيقٍ (waman yushrik billāhi faka-annamā kharra mina al-samāʾi fatakhaṭṭafuhu al-ṭayru aw tahwī bihi al-rīḥu fī makānin saḥīq), the one who associates others with The God is like someone who has fallen from the sky, then birds snatch him or the wind casts him to a far place 22:31.

Here, takhaṭṭaf shows loss of support and safety. One who falls away from tawḥīd is like a body without center, refuge, or stable gravity. The birds snatch him in the very condition of falling. Khaṭf here is not only an outside attack; it reveals helplessness after falling from the place of tawḥīd.

Social Insecurity and Being Snatched from Around

In أَوَلَمْ يَرَوْا أَنَّا جَعَلْنَا حَرَمًا آمِنًا وَيُتَخَطَّفُ النَّاسُ مِنْ حَوْلِهِمْ (awalam yaraw annā jaʿalnā ḥaraman āminan wayutakhaṭṭafu al-nāsu min ḥawlihim), people around the sanctuary are being snatched away, while the sanctuary is made secure 29:67. Here yatakhaṭṭafu points to social and geographic insecurity: outside the secure boundary, people are exposed to sudden attack, plunder, capture, or being carried off.

A similar verse says: وَقَالُوا إِن نَّتَّبِعِ الْهُدَىٰ مَعَكَ نُتَخَطَّفْ مِنْ أَرْضِنَا (waqālū in nattabiʿi al-hudā maʿaka nutakhaṭṭaf min arḍinā) 28:57. They say that if they follow guidance with the Messenger, they will be snatched from their land. The statement expresses fear of political and social exposure, using the language of takhaṭṭuf.

A Brief Hidden Snatch from the Field of News

In إِلَّا مَنْ خَطِفَ الْخَطْفَةَ فَأَتْبَعَهُ شِهَابٌ ثَاقِبٌ (illā man khaṭifa al-khaṭfata fa-atbaʿahu shihābun thāqib), someone snatches a khaṭfah, and then a piercing flame follows him 37:10. Here khaṭf concerns a quick passing seizure from the field of news or hearing. The receiver does not obtain full, lawful, stable access. He snatches only a fragment.

The word الْخَطْفَة (al-khaṭfah) is precise. It is a short, unstable, snatched reception: not encompassing knowledge, not full understanding, not settled access. Against this hidden snatching comes the piercing flame. The verse shows that khaṭf can occur in the field of information and perception, not only in physical movement.

Khaṭf and the Faculties of Perception

In the Qur’an, khaṭf is connected both with sight, as in يَخْطَفُ أَبْصَارَهُمْ (yakhṭafu abṣārahum), and with the hidden taking of news, as in خَطِفَ الْخَطْفَةَ (khaṭifa al-khaṭfah). These uses show that khaṭf is not only the snatching of a body. Sometimes a sense, a datum, a capacity, or an opportunity to receive is suddenly taken.

In this way, KH Ṭ F enters the field of perception. The eye may almost be snatched by the intensity of lightning; hearing or access to news may occur only as a khaṭfah; and a human being in social insecurity may himself be snatched from his place.

Difference from Nearby Words

Akhḏ is taking in general. It may be calm, legal, complete, or gradual. Khaṭf is a kind of taking, but sudden and swift. Every khaṭf is a kind of akhḏ, but not every akhḏ is khaṭf.

Qabḍ is closer to grasping in the hand, gathering, or taking into possession. Salb is taking away or stripping something from its owner, but it does not necessarily carry the speed and surprise of khaṭf. Nahb is open plunder, often in the field of property and war. Ikhtilās is stealthy theft or snatching, but khaṭf is not necessarily secret; it may be open, fast, and striking.

Lamḥ is a quick glance or flash. Khaṭf is quick taking. Lightning may be seen in a moment, but when it nearly يَخْطَفُ الْأَبْصَار (yakhṭafu al-abṣār), the issue is no longer only visual speed; it is the snatching of the function of sight.

Translation Method

When translating KH Ṭ F, words like “snatch,” “seize suddenly,” “carry off,” “grab away,” or “pull out of reach” are better than simple “take.” In formal English, “snatch” usually carries the speed and surprise well. In very physical images, “grab” or “carry off” may help, but the context should decide.

For يَخْطَفُ أَبْصَارَهُمْ (yakhṭafu abṣārahum), one should not simply say “takes their eyes.” Better: “snatches away their sight,” or “suddenly disables their power of seeing.” In al-Ṣāffāt, خَطِفَ الْخَطْفَةَ (khaṭifa al-khaṭfah) must preserve the brevity and instability of the reception: “snatched a brief snatch,” “seized a passing fragment,” or “stole away a fragment swiftly.”

Forqan Translation Suggestions

يَخْطَفُ أَبْصَارَهُمْ (yakhṭafu abṣārahum): snatches away their sight; suddenly disables their power of seeing.

فَتَخْطَفُهُ الطَّيْرُ (fatakhaṭṭafuhu al-ṭayr): then the birds snatch him; then the birds suddenly carry him off.

وَيُتَخَطَّفُ النَّاسُ مِنْ حَوْلِهِمْ (wayutakhaṭṭafu al-nāsu min ḥawlihim): people are snatched from around them; people around them are exposed to snatching and insecurity.

نُتَخَطَّفْ مِنْ أَرْضِنَا (nutakhaṭṭaf min arḍinā): we will be snatched from our land; we will be torn away from our place and land.

إِلَّا مَنْ خَطِفَ الْخَطْفَةَ (illā man khaṭifa al-khaṭfah): except one who snatches a brief snatch; except one who swiftly and secretly seizes a fragment.

Summary

The root KH Ṭ F points to sudden, swift, unprepared taking. Speed and surprise are essential. What is snatched is pulled from its place or loses its function before it has time to resist or stabilize.

In the Qur’an, khaṭf can describe lightning that nearly snatches sight, birds that snatch someone after a fall, people who are seized from around in social insecurity, or hidden reception where someone obtains only a khaṭfah. In Forqan’s reading, khaṭf is swift snatching from a field of safety: taking something before it can hold itself.
