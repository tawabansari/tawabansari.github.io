Success, attaining what is sought, and escaping harm: from worldly victories to salvation in divine judgment

1. The root’s meaning in early Arabic

The Arabic triliteral root F W Z concerns succeeding, attaining something desired, and escaping an unwelcome condition.

Fāza bi-l-shayʾi means to attain something, obtain it, or succeed in reaching it.

Fāza mina l-sharri means to escape evil or danger.

Fawz names this success or deliverance; fāʾiz describes someone who has achieved the desired outcome or escaped a harmful condition.

Classical lexicons explain the family through two prominent meanings:

الظَّفَرُ بِالْخَيْرِ: attaining good or what is sought.

النَّجَاةُ مِنَ الشَّرِّ: escaping evil and harm.

These meanings are related, but not identical.

Someone may gain something valuable without having faced a particular danger, or escape a great danger without receiving new wealth or an advantage.

Both situations can be called fawz.

A winner in a contest attains the desired outcome. A traveler surviving a dangerous journey may also be considered successful, having passed through danger to safety.

Yet every use of fawz need not combine acquiring a benefit with escaping danger. One aspect may be more prominent than the other.

Another important point is that the lexical meaning of fawz does not, by itself, establish the moral rightness of the goal.

Someone may attain what they regard as a great victory without that outcome being valuable by the standards of truth and justice.

The Qur’an also illustrates this distinction, particularly in Al-Nisāʾ 4:73, which quotes a person speaking of worldly success.

Fawz in Arabic and everyday life

Classical usage includes attaining a wish, gaining a benefit, and succeeding in competition.

For example, fāza l-qidḥu is recorded for a lot-arrow that brought its owner the desired result or came out ahead in a draw involving gambling arrows.

This attests a use involving winning and receiving a share. Its inclusion in later dictionaries alone, however, does not establish exactly when it originated before Islam.

Fāʾizah is also recorded for something welcome whose acquisition brings a person joy.

Fawz thus belongs to the field of attaining a desired outcome, but personal desirability is not always divine approval.

2. Mafāzah: a dangerous desert or a place of deliverance?

Mafāzah is one of this family’s notable historical uses.

Alongside success and deliverance, classical Arabic uses mafāzah for a vast, dry desert that is difficult and dangerous to cross, especially water-scarce country requiring a long journey to the destination.

An example from early Arabic poetry

A poem attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays reads:

وَكَمْ دُونَهَا مِنْ مَهْمَهٍ وَمَفَازَةٍ
وَكَمْ أَرْضِ جَدْبٍ دُونَهَا وَلُصُوصِ

Wa-kam dūnahā min mahmahin wa-mafāzatin / Wa-kam arḍi jadbin dūnahā wa-luṣūṣ.

“How many vast deserts and difficult wildernesses lie between me and her; how many barren lands and robbers along that distance!”

Here mafāzah names land whose crossing entails difficulty and danger.

Its association with mahmah, a broad wilderness, jadb, dry and barren land, and luṣūṣ, robbers, establishes the danger of the journey.

The poem appears in the collection attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays, but its wording varies; another version gives the second hemistich in a different order. Its attribution and vocabulary can be examined, but preservation in later compilations alone does not securely date this particular verse.

With that limitation, the poem attests mafāzah as a desert or difficult crossing in the tradition of early Arabic poetry.

Why call a dangerous desert mafāzah?

Lexicons record several explanations.

One treats the name as an optimistic expression: land whose dangers one hopes to survive and cross safely.

Another connects the place with the desired result of crossing it: whoever successfully traverses the dangerous desert has attained fawz.

A third invokes the less familiar use of fāza for perishing or dying. On this account, mafāzah can name land where death threatens.

These explanations are recorded, but the evidence does not adequately establish which meaning came first historically.

Neither optimistic naming nor an original meaning of destruction should therefore be asserted as certain.

Was fawz also used for perishing?

Some Arabic sources record fāza and fawwaza for dying or perishing.

A poem attributed to Kaʿb ibn Zuhayr uses fawwaza of someone’s death.

That use must be distinguished from the established principal Qur’anic meaning.

No Qur’anic occurrence of fāza means dying or perishing. Even in scenes of the Hereafter, fawz concerns success and deliverance, not death itself.

Apparently opposed meanings in lexicons also do not make fawz mean both salvation and destruction simultaneously in every verse.

Understanding its Qur’anic meaning requires beginning with Qur’anic usage.

3. Qur’anic forms and the semantic core

F W Z occurs 29 times in 27 Qur’anic verses, in five principal forms.

Fāza / afūza: the Form I verb, meaning to succeed or attain what is sought, occurs in Āl ʿImrān 3:185, Al-Nisāʾ 4:73, and Al-Aḥzāb 33:71.

Fawz: a noun or verbal noun for success and attaining a desired outcome. It is sometimes qualified by ʿaẓīm, mubīn, or kabīr.

Fāʾizūn: the plural active participle, “the successful,” occurs in Al-Tawbah 9:20, Al-Muʾminūn 23:111, Al-Nūr 24:52, and Al-Ḥashr 59:20.

Mafāz: in Al-Nabaʾ 78:31, it can express success, deliverance, or the place where success is attained.

Mafāzah: in Āl ʿImrān 3:188 and Al-Zumar 39:61, it concerns deliverance and success.

Fawwaza, tafawwaza, afāza, fāʾizah, and the plural mafāwiz are known Arabic forms but do not occur in these forms in the Qur’an.

A defensible semantic core is:

Attaining a desired outcome or emerging from a harmful situation, thereby achieving success or deliverance.

This defines the family lexically. Whether the outcome is truly good in The God’s judgment or only counted as success by a person depends on context.

4. Qur’anic fawz: true and perceived success

Āl ʿImrān 3:185: success in the final reckoning

كُلُّ نَفْسٍ ذَائِقَةُ الْمَوْتِ وَإِنَّمَا تُوَفَّوْنَ أُجُورَكُمْ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ فَمَنْ زُحْزِحَ عَنِ النَّارِ وَأُدْخِلَ الْجَنَّةَ فَقَدْ فَازَ وَمَا الْحَيَاةُ الدُّنْيَا إِلَّا مَتَاعُ الْغُرُورِ

Kullu nafsin dhāʾiqatu l-mawti wa-innamā tuwaffawna ujūrakum yawma l-qiyāmati fa-man zuḥziḥa ʿani n-nāri wa-udkhila l-jannata fa-qad fāza wa-mā l-ḥayātu d-dunyā illā matāʿu l-ghurūr.

“Every soul will taste death, and only on the Day of Resurrection will you be paid your rewards in full. Whoever is kept away from the Fire and admitted to the Garden has indeed succeeded. The life of this world is only a deceptive enjoyment.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:185

This is one of the clearest occurrences of F W Z.

Two events are joined: being kept away from the Fire and admitted to the Garden.

The outcome follows: that person has attained fawz.

Here success has two aspects: escaping a harmful end and attaining a desired condition.

This one verse does not make both elements necessary in every Arabic use of fawz. Here the complete sentence supplies the two conditions.

Zuḥziḥa expresses being moved away from the Fire.

Udkhila expresses being admitted to the Garden.

Fāza names their outcome as success.

The verse then describes worldly life as deceptive enjoyment. Temporary worldly prosperity should not be the final measure of success.

Someone wealthy, influential, or powerful may find their life’s outcome in divine judgment different from their own expectations.

Conversely, someone who faced hardship in this world may attain fawz in the final reckoning.

The verse thus connects ultimate success with a person’s end, rather than worldly possessions, fame, or power.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:73: success in the eyes of a self-interested person

وَلَئِنْ أَصَابَكُمْ فَضْلٌ مِنَ اللَّهِ لَيَقُولَنَّ كَأَنْ لَمْ تَكُنْ بَيْنَكُمْ وَبَيْنَهُ مَوَدَّةٌ يَا لَيْتَنِي كُنْتُ مَعَهُمْ فَأَفُوزَ فَوْزًا عَظِيمًا

Wa-la-in aṣābakum faḍlun mina llāhi la-yaqūlanna ka-an lam takun baynakum wa-baynahu mawaddatun yā laytanī kuntu maʿahum fa-afūza fawzan ʿaẓīmā.

“But if a bounty from The God reaches you, he will surely say, as though there had never been affection between you and him: ‘If only I had been with them, so that I might have attained a great success!’”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:73

This verse is particularly important for the root’s meaning.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:72 describes someone who hangs back from joining others and is pleased not to have participated when misfortune strikes them.

When they instead receive bounty and a favorable outcome, the same person wishes he had joined them and shared in it.

He calls this outcome fawzan ʿaẓīman, “a great success.”

Crucially, the speaker is the person criticized by the Qur’an; The God is not thereby endorsing that person’s conduct as a great success.

The presence of “great success” in a sentence does not by itself establish divine approval of the speaker’s assessment or conduct.

The speaker focuses on a benefit he missed, assessing success from the perspective of his own wishes and advantage.

This shows that Arabic F W Z can describe attaining a worldly objective.

Human and divine assessments of success are not always the same.

Fa-afūza is a first-person singular imperfect verb in the subjunctive following the wish laytanī. Fawzan is its cognate accusative, qualified by ʿaẓīman to express the greatness of the success desired by the speaker.

Fawzan ʿaẓīman here belongs to that person’s speech and wish. His assessment should not be treated as an independent Qur’anic judgment of that benefit.

The contrast with Al-Aḥzāb 33:71

Al-Aḥzāb 33:71 also combines a verb with its cognate noun: fāza fawzan ʿaẓīman.

There, success is connected with obeying The God and His Messenger.

The nearby verses command believers to exercise taqwa and speak sound, truthful words, then speak of setting deeds right, forgiving sins, and success.

“Great success” in Al-Nisāʾ 4:73 and Al-Aḥzāb 33:71 is linguistically similar, but its subject and value differ.

One reports a person’s appraisal of worldly gain; the other announces success through obedience and the rectification of conduct.

The word’s meaning remains, while the rightness or wrongness of the goal is understood from the speaker and context.

5. Mafāzah and mafāz: success, deliverance, and its place

Āl ʿImrān 3:188: imagining oneself safe

لَا تَحْسَبَنَّ الَّذِينَ يَفْرَحُونَ بِمَا أَتَوْا وَيُحِبُّونَ أَنْ يُحْمَدُوا بِمَا لَمْ يَفْعَلُوا فَلَا تَحْسَبَنَّهُمْ بِمَفَازَةٍ مِنَ الْعَذَابِ وَلَهُمْ عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ

Lā taḥsabanna lladhīna yafraḥūna bi-mā ataw wa-yuḥibbūna an yuḥmadū bi-mā lam yafʿalū fa-lā taḥsabannahum bi-mafāzatin mina l-ʿadhābi wa-lahum ʿadhābun alīm.

“Do not think that those who rejoice in what they have done and love to be praised for what they have not done are safe from punishment. A painful punishment awaits them.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:188

Bi-mafāzatin mina l-ʿadhāb means being safe from punishment or escaping it.

Mina l-ʿadhāb identifies what is escaped.

Mafāzah here does not name a desert or a geographical location.

The verse concerns people pleased with what they have done who want praise for what they have not done.

In this context, mafāzah describes escape from punishment, which the Qur’an denies them.

Lexically, it concerns deliverance and safety; the conduct occasioning punishment comes from the descriptions of the people in the verse.

This does not make every joy or desire for praise incompatible with mafāzah. The sentence supplies two specific descriptions whose qualifications must be retained.

Al-Zumar 39:61: success accompanied by freedom from harm

وَيُنَجِّي اللَّهُ الَّذِينَ اتَّقَوْا بِمَفَازَتِهِمْ لَا يَمَسُّهُمُ السُّوءُ وَلَا هُمْ يَحْزَنُونَ

Wa-yunajjī llāhu lladhīna ttaqaw bi-mafāzatihim lā yamassuhumu s-sūʾu wa-lā hum yaḥzanūn.

“And The God saves those who were mindful, with their success and deliverance: no evil touches them, nor do they grieve.”

Al-Zumar 39:61

Two related families appear together: yunajjī from N J W and mafāzatihim from F W Z.

Yunajjī expresses saving; mafāzatihim denotes their success or deliverance.

The preposition bāʾ connects saving with their mafāzah. Grammatically, a cause or means of deliverance, or accompaniment by a state of success, can be considered; these possibilities should not be narrowed to one exclusive meaning without grounds.

The common Ḥafṣ reading has singular bi-mafāzatihim. Some other readings have plural bi-mafāzātihim. This difference in number does not alter the basic field of deliverance and success.

The rest of the verse identifies two features: evil does not touch them, and they do not grieve.

Success here is thus associated with security and freedom from harm.

That does not make “absence of grief” the meaning of mafāzah throughout Arabic. This feature comes from the continuation of the sentence.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:31: mafāz and attaining success

إِنَّ لِلْمُتَّقِينَ مَفَازًا

Inna li-l-muttaqīna mafāzā.

“Indeed, for the mindful there is success.”

Al-Nabaʾ 78:31

Mafāz can be a mīm-prefixed verbal noun from fāza, denoting success and deliverance themselves.

Its structure also allows consideration as a noun of place: a place where success is realized.

The next verse, Al-Nabaʾ 78:32, mentions gardens and grapes; subsequent verses describe further blessings.

The success here therefore concerns the blessings and enjoyment of the mindful.

If mafāz is a verbal noun, it means attaining success, with the following blessings exemplifying its outcome.

If taken as a noun of place, it denotes the place of success where those blessings are provided.

Both analyses merit grammatical consideration, but “success” is clear and sufficient in the basic translation.

Mafāz should not be rendered “dry desert” merely because of its resemblance to mafāzah. The context clearly concerns blessings for the mindful, not their crossing an earthly desert.

6. Fāʾizūn: those who have succeeded

Fāʾiz is the active participle of fāza.

Al-fāʾizūn is its sound masculine plural, referring to those who have attained success.

This form occurs four times in the Qur’an.

Al-Tawbah 9:20 mentions faith, migration, and striving with possessions and lives in The God’s way, describing those who do so as greater in rank with Him and among the successful.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:111 describes The God rewarding people for their steadfastness and calls them fāʾizūn. The preceding context concerns enduring ridicule and abuse.

Al-Nūr 24:52 reads:

وَمَنْ يُطِعِ اللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُ وَيَخْشَ اللَّهَ وَيَتَّقْهِ فَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْفَائِزُونَ

Wa-man yuṭiʿi llāha wa-rasūlahu wa-yakhsha llāha wa-yattaqhi fa-ulāʾika humu l-fāʾizūn.

“And whoever obeys The God and His Messenger, stands in awe of The God, and is mindful of Him—those are the successful.”

Al-Nūr 24:52

Here success is linked to obedience, awe, and taqwa.

Fāʾizūn itself does not mean “the obedient” or “the mindful.” Those qualities come from the preceding conditions.

Al-Ḥashr 59:20 also states:

لَا يَسْتَوِي أَصْحَابُ النَّارِ وَأَصْحَابُ الْجَنَّةِ أَصْحَابُ الْجَنَّةِ هُمُ الْفَائِزُونَ

Lā yastawī aṣḥābu n-nāri wa-aṣḥābu l-jannati aṣḥābu l-jannati humu l-fāʾizūn.

“The people of the Fire and the people of the Garden are not equal. The people of the Garden are the successful.”

Al-Ḥashr 59:20

The two groups’ final outcomes are contrasted.

Calling the Garden’s inhabitants fāʾizūn does not limit the lexical definition of fāʾiz to “a resident of the Garden.”

Fāʾiz names someone who has succeeded; the Garden’s inhabitants are its referent here.

The distinction between a word’s meaning and its Qur’anic referent should be preserved.

7. Great, manifest, and mighty success

The Qur’an qualifies fawz with three prominent descriptions.

Al-fawzu l-ʿaẓīm: great success

Many verses use al-fawzu l-ʿaẓīm for the Hereafter’s blessings, The God’s approval, and the outcome of faith and good deeds.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:13 connects observing divine limits with entry into the Garden and great success.

Al-Tawbah 9:89, Al-Tawbah 9:100, Al-Tawbah 9:111, Al-Fatḥ 48:5, Al-Ḥadīd 57:12, Al-Ṣaff 61:12, and Al-Taghābun 64:9 likewise connect great success with divine reward and the final outcome.

One verse highlights another aspect:

وَعَدَ اللَّهُ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَالْمُؤْمِنَاتِ جَنَّاتٍ تَجْرِي مِنْ تَحْتِهَا الْأَنْهَارُ خَالِدِينَ فِيهَا وَمَسَاكِنَ طَيِّبَةً فِي جَنَّاتِ عَدْنٍ وَرِضْوَانٌ مِنَ اللَّهِ أَكْبَرُ ذَٰلِكَ هُوَ الْفَوْزُ الْعَظِيمُ

Waʿada llāhu l-muʾminīna wa-l-muʾmināti jannātin tajrī min taḥtihā l-anhāru khālidīna fīhā wa-masākina ṭayyibatan fī jannāti ʿadnin wa-riḍwānun mina llāhi akbaru dhālika huwa l-fawzu l-ʿaẓīm.

“The God has promised believing men and believing women gardens beneath which rivers flow, to remain in them, and good dwellings in gardens of lasting residence. And approval from The God is greater. That is the great success.”

Al-Tawbah 9:72

Success here is not expressed solely through gardens and comfortable dwellings. The God’s approval is also mentioned and declared greater than the blessings described.

True success in this context concerns both enjoyment and the human relationship with the Lord.

Al-Māʾidah 5:119 likewise associates the mutual approval of The God and His truthful servants with great success.

These verses do not make “The God’s approval” the root’s lexical meaning. Divine approval is a central element and instance of success in these passages.

Al-fawzu l-mubīn: manifest success

Al-Anʿām 6:16 reads:

مَنْ يُصْرَفْ عَنْهُ يَوْمَئِذٍ فَقَدْ رَحِمَهُ وَذَٰلِكَ الْفَوْزُ الْمُبِينُ

Man yuṣraf ʿanhu yawmaʾidhin fa-qad raḥimahu wa-dhālika l-fawzu l-mubīn.

“Whoever has punishment turned away from him that Day, He has indeed shown him mercy. That is the manifest success.”

Al-Anʿām 6:16

Here success concerns punishment being turned away and divine mercy.

Mubīn, from B Y N, describes success as manifest or clear.

Al-Jāthiyah 45:30 uses the same description for those with faith and good deeds being admitted into their Lord’s mercy.

Mubīn does not identify a separate kind of success distinct from great success; it highlights its evident character.

Al-fawzu l-kabīr: great success

Al-Burūj 85:11 follows the promise of gardens beneath which rivers flow with al-fawzu l-kabīr.

Kabīr describes the greatness of success.

Without independent evidence, the difference between kabīr and ʿaẓīm does not establish a fixed hierarchy in which one must rank above the other.

Nor does mubīn mean a third degree of success.

The descriptions emphasize greatness and clarity in their contexts; they do not define three formal classes with fixed names.

Is fawz restricted to the Hereafter?

Most positive, direct Qur’anic uses concern otherworldly reward and outcome.

Yūnus 10:64, however, speaks of good tidings for those in a close bond with The God in worldly life and the Hereafter, associating the whole with great success.

Promised success can therefore include worldly good and glad tidings, although its final and complete assessment becomes clear in the Hereafter.

Conversely, Al-Nisāʾ 4:73 shows the word in human speech about a worldly desire.

Restricting its lexical meaning to “entering the Garden after death” is therefore mistaken, as is reducing every Qur’anic use to financial or social success.

8. Distinctions from related roots

F W Z (fawz) and F L Ḥ (falāḥ): success and flourishing

These roots have closely related Qur’anic meanings; both can concern human success and salvation.

Their uses nevertheless differ.

Fawz often emphasizes attaining a desired outcome and realizing success.

Qur’anic falāḥ is broadly associated with a sound life bearing fruit, purification, taqwa, and conduct.

For example, the Qur’an uses falāḥ for those who purify their souls, and fawz for those kept away from the Fire and admitted to the Garden.

This does not establish an absolute rule that falāḥ always names a process and fawz always an outcome. Both can express success realized.

A clearer distinction is that Al-Nisāʾ 4:73 uses fāza for a worldly desire in a self-interested speaker’s words, whereas direct Qur’anic uses of falāḥ strongly associate it with a sound course and a good end.

For further study, see Forqan’s article on the Qur’anic root F L Ḥ.

F W Z (fawz) and N J W (najāh): success and rescue

Najāh concerns escape from danger, injury, or a harmful condition.

Fawz can include rescue, but also encompasses attaining good and what is sought.

Al-Zumar 39:61 places saving and mafāzah together, showing their close relationship without requiring complete synonymy.

Someone rescued from drowning has escaped danger. If they also reach their desired destination, one can speak of their success in the journey.

This example should not become a rule that every fawz follows physical rescue.

Forqan’s study of the Qur’anic root N J W examines rescue and its distinction from fawz in detail.

F W Z (fawz) and Ẓ F R (ẓafar): attainment and overcoming

Arabic ẓafar also concerns attainment, prevailing, and reaching what is sought.

Lexicons sometimes explain fawz through ẓafar, especially in al-ẓafaru bi-l-khayr.

Qur’anic Ẓ F R has a more limited range, however, and not every success means overcoming an enemy.

Someone can attain fawz without defeating another person in competition.

Conversely, overcoming an enemy leaves the moral and otherworldly outcome of one’s conduct open to assessment.

Fawz and ẓafar thus overlap, but Qur’anic fawz is not restricted to military victory.

See Forqan’s separate study of the root Ẓ F R.

F W Z (fawz) and R B Ḥ (ribḥ): success and profit

Ribḥ concerns profit and gain, particularly in trade and exchange.

Worldly fawz may involve obtaining a benefit, but it is not limited to financial profit.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:73, a person expresses missing a benefit as missing great success.

Verses about the Hereafter instead associate fawz with divine approval, mercy, rescue, and the Garden.

Fawz should therefore not be translated as “economic profit.”

Al-Tawbah 9:111 also employs the image of sale and exchange for divine reward, but fawz itself does not mean commerce.

For details, see the study of the Qur’anic root R B Ḥ.

F W Z (fawz) and Kh S R (khusrān): success and loss

Qur’anic khusrān concerns loss and the forfeiture of true capital or achievement.

Fawz can stand against khusrān, since one concerns attaining what is sought and the other loss.

This does not make them exact lexical opposites in every use.

Someone may profit financially in a transaction while suffering moral loss through oppression or deception.

They may have achieved what they wanted without being successful in divine judgment.

This distinction also fits Al-Nisāʾ 4:73.

Forqan’s study of the Qur’anic root Kh S R examines loss and khusrān further.

F W Z (fawz) and N Ṣ R (naṣr)

Naṣr concerns giving assistance to prevail or escape difficulty.

Fawz expresses attaining a desired outcome.

Assistance may produce success, but the words are not synonymous.

Aid in battle can lead to victory; elsewhere, success may arise without confrontation or overcoming an enemy.

Rendering every fawz as “military victory” or “defeating an enemy” therefore removes part of its semantic range.

9. Translation recommendations for Forqan

Suitable principal equivalents for fāza are “succeeded,” “attained what was sought,” and, in some contexts, “escaped.”

For fawz, “success,” “salvation,” and where appropriate “attaining good and what is sought” are natural.

“Victory” can suit some uses, but must not be restricted to military conquest.

For fāʾiz and al-fāʾizūn, use “successful,” “the successful,” or “those who attain salvation.”

Depending on context, mafāzah can be “deliverance,” “safety,” or “success.”

“Success” is a suitable basic rendering of mafāz in Al-Nabaʾ 78:31. A place of success can be discussed in grammatical and contextual explanation.

For the non-Qur’anic geographical mafāzah, “a dry, dangerous desert” is appropriate, but must not be transferred into Qur’anic verses.

Conclusion

Arabic F W Z concerns attaining what is sought, obtaining good, and escaping a harmful condition.

Classical usage covers both success in competition or gaining a benefit and rescue from danger.

Mafāzah also names a dry, dangerous desert. Several historical and lexical explanations address the name, but none should be made the certain origin of every root meaning without adequate evidence.

The Qur’an uses the verb fāza, the noun fawz, the participle fāʾizūn, and the forms mafāz and mafāzah.

Its lexical meaning should not be restricted from the outset to otherworldly salvation. Al-Nisāʾ 4:73 shows someone calling worldly gain great success from his own perspective.

Most direct Qur’anic uses, however, concern genuine human success in divine judgment.

Āl ʿImrān 3:185 calls being kept from the Fire and admitted to the Garden fawz. Al-Tawbah 9:72 joins the Garden’s blessings with divine approval and declares that approval greater.

Other verses connect success with faith, good deeds, obedience, mindfulness, awe, perseverance, and sound conduct.

True fawz therefore goes beyond getting what one wants: it is attaining an outcome that constitutes real good and deliverance by the measure of truth.

This distinction does not come from the lexical meaning alone. The Qur’an clarifies it by establishing the criteria of success.

Fawz relates to falāḥ, najāh, ẓafar, and ribḥ, but none is wholly synonymous with another. Fawz can express deliverance or attaining good, while the other roots emphasize particular aspects of rescue, prevailing, profit, or flourishing.

Final root definition for Forqan:

F W Z: attaining a desired outcome, obtaining good, or escaping a harmful condition, thereby achieving success or deliverance.

Qur’anic usage can express both success as imagined by people and genuine success affirmed by The God. Speaker, goal, and context distinguish them.

Fāza expresses success attained, fawz success itself, fāʾiz the successful person, and mafāz and mafāzah success, deliverance, or, in an appropriate construction, its place.

In the Qur’an’s final reckoning, the greatest success is escaping a harmful end and attaining divine mercy, approval, and reward.
