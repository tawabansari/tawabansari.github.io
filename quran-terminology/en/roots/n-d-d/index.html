Qur’anic Root

The Qur’anic Root N D D

A rival, equal, counterpart, or something placed beside another in a comparable or competing position

Important Forms from This Root

نِدّ (nidd)

counterpart, rival, equal, or opponent placed in a comparable position.

أَندَاد (andād)

claimed equals, imagined rivals, or constructed counterparts to The God.

نَدَّ (nadda)

in one layer of classical Arabic: to bolt, flee, or break away from proper place and control.

Method Note

The root N D D appears in the Qur’an mainly through the plural أَندَاد (andād). At first, the word may be translated as equals, counterparts, or rivals. But in Qur’anic context the issue is not simple resemblance. A nidd is something given a competing or parallel position, as if it stands beside the central truth and draws toward itself some share of love, obedience, fear, hope, or servanthood.

For this reason, andād usually appears in Qur’anic criticism of shirk. The problem is not only that a person thinks something resembles The God. The deeper problem is that he places something in a position that belongs only to The God. N D D is therefore an important root for understanding how rivals are constructed against The God: authorities, loves, fears, hopes, or systems placed beside Him.

Central Meaning

The central meaning of N D D is the placing of something opposite, beside, or on a parallel level with another, in a way that creates rivalry, equalizing, or departure from proper order. In Qur’anic theology, a nidd is not something that is truly equal to The God. It is something the human being places in that kind of position in mind, behavior, love, or social order.

For this reason, nidd is not the same as mithl. Mithl may simply mark resemblance. Nidd usually carries a rival-like, position-making force. When a human being sets up andād for The God, he gives a creature, a limited and dependent thing, some share of divine place. This is where tawḥīd is harmed: not because The God has gained a real rival, but because the human being has constructed rivalry in his own understanding and servanthood.

Material Image

One material image from classical Arabic is an animal that bolts, breaks loose, and runs away from its herd or proper course. This helps illuminate the theological layer of the root. A nidd is something displaced from its proper place and imagined as independent or rival-like.

In the context of shirk, the human being takes created and dependent beings, powers, or authorities out of their real place and sets them beside The God. They do not actually become independent or equal to The God. But in the human mind and behavior, they become centers of love, obedience, fear, or hope. That displacement is the danger of andād.

Classical Arabic Note

In classical Arabic, nidd could mean a match, equal, peer, or rival: someone or something that stands against another and claims some kind of equality or opposition. In conflict, two opponents could be nidd to one another. But in relation to The God, the Qur’an corrects the language sharply: nothing is truly a nidd to The God. The issue is that human beings make or assign andād for Him.

This act of “making” or “assigning” matters. The Qur’an does not normally say that The God has andād. It says people set up andād for Him. So andād are not independent realities standing against The God. They are constructs of distorted understanding, love, obedience, and human systems.

Qur’anic Usage

The Prohibition of Setting Up Andād for The God

Surat al-Baqarah says: فَلَا تَجْعَلُوا لِلَّهِ أَندَادًا وَأَنتُمْ تَعْلَمُونَ (falā tajʿalū lillāhi andādan wa-antum taʿlamūn) 2:22. This comes after the reminder of earth, sky, water, and provision.

The God who spread the living ground, built the sky, sent down water, and brought provision out from the earth should not have rivals placed beside Him. Andād here are beings or powers to which people assign some share of The God’s place, even though the blessings and causes themselves depend on The God.

Andād in Love: Displacing the Center of Devotion

Surat al-Baqarah says: وَمِنَ النَّاسِ مَن يَتَّخِذُ مِن دُونِ اللَّهِ أَندَادًا يُحِبُّونَهُمْ كَحُبِّ اللَّهِ (wamina al-nāsi man yattakhidhu min dūni allāhi andādan yuḥibbūnahum kaḥubbi allāh) 2:165. This verse shows that andād are not only stone or wooden idols.

A human being can create rivals to The God in love. When something is loved in a way that occupies the place of love owed to The God, that thing becomes a nidd. Shirk does not happen only in official creed or speech. It can happen in loyalty, attachment, love, and the direction of the heart.

Andād and Misleading from The God’s Way

Surat Ibrāhīm says: وَجَعَلُوا لِلَّهِ أَندَادًا لِّيُضِلُّوا عَن سَبِيلِهِ (wajaʿalū lillāhi andādan liyuḍillū ʿan sabīlih) 14:30. Andād are connected here with misleading people from The God’s way.

When a person or society sets up rivals for The God, servanthood and guidance become distorted. A nidd is not only a mental belief. It makes paths, shapes behavior, and draws people away from The God’s way. The conversations between the weak and the arrogant in this surah also show that misguidance does not only come from physical idols. Intellectual, political, and social authorities may lead people into rival-making.

Making Andād for the One Who Created the Earth

Surat Fuṣṣilat says: وَتَجْعَلُونَ لَهُ أَندَادًا (watajʿalūna lahu andādā) 41:9. The phrase appears beside the reminder that The God created the earth.

The question is: how can you assign rivals to the One who created the earth? The context exposes the absurdity of making andād. Something located inside the created world cannot be placed beside the Creator in Lordship.

Andād Inside a Misleading System of Command

In Surat Sabaʾ, followers say to misleading leaders: إِذْ تَأْمُرُونَنَا أَن نَّكْفُرَ بِاللَّهِ وَنَجْعَلَ لَهُ أَندَادًا (idh taʾmurūnanā an nakfura billāhi wanajʿala lahu andādā) 34:33.

This verse shows that rival-making can result from command, propaganda, and pressure from human structures. Andād do not always arise from simple individual ignorance. Intellectual, political, or religious systems may push people to assign rivals to The God. In that case, shirk is not only an isolated personal choice; it forms inside networks of obedience and misleading leadership.

Andād and Forgetting the Source of Blessing

Surat al-Zumar says: وَجَعَلَ لِلَّهِ أَندَادًا لِّيُضِلَّ عَن سَبِيلِهِ (wajaʿala lillāhi andādan liyuḍilla ʿan sabīlih) 39:8. The verse speaks of a person who calls upon The God during hardship, but when blessing is given to him, he forgets and assigns andād to The God.

In ease, the human being may forget the relation between blessing and its source. He may place causes and intermediaries on a level parallel to The God. In that way, blessing, instead of leading to gratitude, may become a field of rival-making.

Difference from Nearby Words

Mithl points more to likeness or resemblance. Two things may be similar from one angle, but nidd usually carries rivalry, equalizing, and position-making. Sharīk is a partner or associate: something given a share in ownership, action, ruling, or power. A nidd may be imagined as a partner, but the emphasis is more on rival-making and placing something opposite The God.

Kufuwan is close to equivalence or matching rank. Surat al-Ikhlāṣ says: وَلَمْ يَكُن لَّهُ كُفُوًا أَحَدٌ (walam yakun lahu kufuwan aḥad). Kufuwan denies any real existential equal to The God. Andād points more to constructed equals in the practice and imagination of mushrikūn.

Walī means protector, near ally, or supporter. It is not always negative; the Qur’an uses it for The God and for believers. But nidd in relation to The God is negative because it gives something a rival-like and parallel place. Ilāh is an object of worship or ultimate devotion. Nidd is not identical to ilāh, but when something is placed beside The God in love, obedience, fear, hope, or law-giving, it approaches the field of a constructed ilāh.

Translation Method

For andād, “equals” or “counterparts” may work, but the negative and rival-like force must be clear from context. In some places, “rivals” is more direct for the modern reader. The translation should not become so long that the Qur’anic force is lost, but where misunderstanding is likely, a short explanatory phrase may help.

It is also important to remember that the Qur’an does not confirm the real existence of rivals to The God by using andād. It speaks about what human beings “make,” “take,” or “assign,” not about something that truly has the power to stand against The God.

Forqan Translation Suggestions

فَلَا تَجْعَلُوا لِلَّهِ أَندَادًا (falā tajʿalū lillāhi andādā): do not set up equals for The God; do not make rivals against The God.

يَتَّخِذُ مِن دُونِ اللَّهِ أَندَادًا (yattakhidhu min dūni allāhi andādā): he takes counterparts apart from The God; he takes rivals beside The God.

يُحِبُّونَهُمْ كَحُبِّ اللَّهِ (yuḥibbūnahum kaḥubbi allāh): they love them as one should love The God; they love them with a love that belongs to The God alone.

وَجَعَلُوا لِلَّهِ أَندَادًا لِّيُضِلُّوا عَن سَبِيلِهِ (wajaʿalū lillāhi andādan liyuḍillū ʿan sabīlih): they set up equals for The God in order to mislead from His way.

وَتَجْعَلُونَ لَهُ أَندَادًا (watajʿalūna lahu andādā): you set up equals for Him; you make rivals against Him.

نَجْعَلَ لَهُ أَندَادًا (najʿala lahu andādā): that we should set up equals for Him; that we should make rivals for Him in authority.

Summary

The root N D D in the Qur’an is a warning against making rivals for The God. A nidd is not something truly equal to The God; no such thing exists in tawḥīd. A nidd is something the human being places, in mind, love, obedience, fear, hope, or social structure, in a position it does not deserve.

Andād are therefore not only obvious idols. Any power, person, system, desire, wealth, tradition, or authority that receives a share of absolute place beside The God and turns the heart or behavior away from tawḥīd can take on the color of nidd in Qur’anic logic. Forqan translation should show that the issue is not real similarity to The God. The issue is imagined and practical rival-making against The God, who has no equal, no kufu, and no partner.
