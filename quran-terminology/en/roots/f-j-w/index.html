The Root ف ج و — Openness, Separation, and Spacious Areas between Things

Qur’anic and lexical forms

فَجْوَةٍ
(fajwatin)
A spacious area; the sole Qur’anic form

فَجَا
(fajā)
To open; lexical use

The three-letter Arabic root ف ج و is associated with openness, separation between two parts, and the formation of an open, spacious area. فَجْوَة denotes a broad space between two things or within a place, such as an opening between heights, a house’s open courtyard, or a spacious part of a cave.

This root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as فَجْوَةٍ in al-Kahf 18:17. The Qur’an uses the word to describe where the young Companions of the Cave were situated inside the cave.

An important distinction from the related root ف ج ج is that فَجّ in Qur’anic usage denotes a passage or passable route, whereas فَجْوَة is an open, spacious area that need not provide a route to another destination.

1. The Root’s Meaning in Early Arabic

In established Arabic usage, فَجْوَة denotes an opening or broad space between two things or within a place.

Early lexical explanations emphasize two features: separation between parts and the spaciousness of the area between them.

For example, فَجْوَةُ الدَّار denotes a house’s open courtyard. A house may have rooms, walls, and roofed sections, while the open area between them is called a fajwah.

Similarly, a broad space between heights or sections of terrain may be called a fajwah.

Some lexical accounts use the word for land that is both broad and depressed or lower than its surroundings. Low elevation, however, is not essential to every use of fajwah. Its broader meaning is an open, spacious area.

Evidence from Early Arabic

A verse preserved in early lexical sources reads:

وَنَحْنُ مَلَأْنَا كُلَّ وَادٍ وَفَجْوَةٍ
رِجَالًا وَخَيْلًا غَيْرَ مِيلٍ وَلَا عُزْلِ

Wa-naḥnu malaʾnā kulla wādin wa-fajwatin / rijālan wa-khaylan ghayra mīlin wa-lā ʿuzli.

Translation: “We filled every valley and open space with men and horses—men neither cowardly nor unarmed.”

In this example, fajwah appears alongside wād, “valley.”

Wād denotes a valley or natural course through the terrain, whereas fajwah emphasizes an open, broad area where people and horses may gather or move.

The verse shows that the word was not restricted to the inside of a cave; it could also describe open environments.

The verse is transmitted in early lexicons without a clear historical attribution. It cannot therefore be confidently assigned to a particular pre-Islamic poet, although it remains valuable evidence for the word’s recorded Arabic meaning.

Another poetic example reads:

أَلْبَسْتَ قَوْمَكَ مَخْزَاةً وَمَنْقَصَةً
حَتَّى أُبِيحُوا وَحَلُّوا فَجْوَةَ الدَّارِ

Albasta qawmaka makhzātan wa-manqaṣatan / ḥattā ubīḥū wa-ḥallū fajwata al-dāri.

Translation, following the reading حَلُّوا: “You brought shame and dishonor upon your people, until they were exposed to violation and came to occupy the open courtyard.” The subject of حَلُّوا is the people themselves; “others entered” is not a direct equivalent of this wording. The variant خَلُّوا, “they vacated,” is also transmitted.

Here, فَجْوَةَ الدَّارِ denotes the courtyard or open area of a dwelling, not a narrow crack in a wall.

The attribution and date of this verse are also insufficiently clear for it to be treated as certain evidence predating the Qur’an.

The Verb in Arabic Usage

Lexical sources also record فَجَا in the sense of opening something.

For example, فَجَا بَابَهُ is reported in the dialect of Ṭayyiʾ with the meaning “he opened his door.”

A verse attributed to al-Ṭirimmāḥ, a poet of the Umayyad period, reads:

كَحَبَّةِ السَّاجِ فَجَا بَابَهَا
صُبْحٌ جَلَا خُضْرَةَ أَهْدَامِهَا

Ka-ḥabbati al-sāji fajā bābahā / ṣubḥun jalā khuḍrata ahdāmihā.

In this poetic image, dawn is described as opening a door, an opening accompanied by the emergence of a scene and its illumination.

This example supports the meaning “to open.” Since the poet lived after the Qur’an’s revelation, however, it cannot serve as independent pre-Islamic evidence.

Taken together, the lexical and poetic evidence clearly illustrates openness and spaciousness. The Qur’anic occurrence itself also firmly attests this meaning in Arabic at the time of revelation. More securely dated evidence would nevertheless be needed to establish that every branch of the root was already current before the Qur’an.

2. Linguistic Forms and the Semantic Core

Arabic records several forms belonging to this word family:

  • فَجَا: he opened.
  • فَجْو: opening or creating separation.
  • فَجْوَة: an open space, broad interval, or spacious part of a place.
  • فَجَوَات and فِجَاء: plural forms of fajwah.
  • فَجْوَاء: describing something whose parts are spaced apart.
  • فَجِيَ: in some lexical uses, to have widely spaced legs or joints.
  • أَفْجَى: a person or animal with unusually wide spacing between the legs or certain joints.
  • تَفَاجَى: to open out or develop a gap or opening.
  • اِنْفَجَى: to open or move apart.

Of these forms, only فَجْوَةٍ occurs in the Qur’an.

The expression قَوْسٌ فَجْوَاءُ describes a bow whose string stands away from the middle of its body.

Here too, the relevant feature is the space between two parts.

In descriptions of human or animal anatomy, أَفْجَى is also recorded for noticeable spacing between the thighs, knees, or legs.

These uses show that the root is not merely a name for a place. Separation and openness may describe bodily form, tools, and geographical space.

Nevertheless, all these uses need not derive from one demonstrable original image. A shared meaning does not by itself establish the derivational history of every form.

A defensible semantic core is separation and openness between parts—an opening that creates an open or spacious area.

This space may lie inside a place or in an open environment. It need not be a passage, dwelling, or safe location; such features depend on the particular usage and context.

3. Qur’anic Usage: A Spacious Area within the Cave

Al-Kahf 18:17

وَتَرَى ٱلشَّمْسَ إِذَا طَلَعَت تَّزَٰوَرُ عَن كَهْفِهِمْ ذَاتَ ٱلْيَمِينِ وَإِذَا غَرَبَت تَّقْرِضُهُمْ ذَاتَ ٱلشِّمَالِ وَهُمْ فِى فَجْوَةٍ مِّنْهُ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ مِنْ ءَايَٰتِ ٱللَّهِ ۗ مَن يَهْدِ ٱللَّهُ فَهُوَ ٱلْمُهْتَدِ ۖ وَمَن يُضْلِلْ فَلَن تَجِدَ لَهُۥ وَلِيًّا مُّرْشِدًا

Wa-tarā al-shamsa idhā ṭalaʿat tazāwaru ʿan kahfihim dhāta al-yamīni wa-idhā gharabat taqriḍuhum dhāta al-shimāli wa-hum fī fajwatin minhu. Dhālika min āyāti Allāhi. Man yahdi Allāhu fa-huwa al-muhtadi wa-man yuḍlil fa-lan tajida lahu waliyyan murshidan.

Translation: “And you see the sun, when it rises, incline away from their cave to the right, and when it sets, pass them on the left, while they are in a spacious part of that cave. That is among the signs of The God. Whoever The God guides is rightly guided; and whoever He leads astray, you will never find for him a guardian who guides.”

This verse belongs to the account of young people who withdrew from their society and took refuge in a cave to preserve their faith and freedom.

Al-Kahf 18:10 describes their taking refuge in the cave and their prayer for mercy and guidance.

In al-Kahf 18:16, taking shelter in the cave is presented as a means of withdrawing from a dangerous environment.

Al-Kahf 18:17 describes their position within the cave and the sunlight’s relation to it.

What Does فَجْوَةٍ مِنْهُ Mean?

Two elements matter in this expression:

فَجْوَةٍ — an open, spacious area.

مِنْهُ — “of it,” with the pronoun referring to the cave.

The expression therefore means “in a spacious part of the cave.”

The important point is that the Qur’an does not say they were in a narrow fissure, at the cave’s mouth, or outside it.

Fajwah denotes a part of the cave with sufficient open space.

A natural cave may have a narrow entrance but a spacious chamber farther inside. Alternatively, its mouth may be broad while its other sections have varying amounts of space.

These examples illustrate natural possibilities. Fajwah alone does not establish the cave’s exact geometry or the precise location where the young people slept.

The preposition مِنْ in مِنْهُ connects the spacious area to the cave itself. This construction supports “in a spacious area of it,” rather than “in the open area outside the cave.”

The Spacious Area and the Sun’s Movement

The verse first describes the sun at sunrise and sunset, then states that the young people were in a spacious part of the cave.

This juxtaposition matters.

Their position is described in relation to the directions of sunlight. At sunrise the sun inclines to one side, and at sunset it passes on the other.

Thus, the young people occupy a spacious area, while the sun’s relation to their position is described as avoiding direct exposure or passing alongside them.

This scene may reflect the cave’s particular situation and orientation; the verse also calls it one of The God’s signs. But fajwah itself does not specify the entrance’s direction, geographical location, angle of sunlight, or mechanism involved.

Some early explanations propose a particular orientation for the cave. Such proposals cannot be established from the lexical meaning of fajwah alone.

What is clear is the spaciousness of the area occupied by the young people within the cave.

Does Fajwah Mean Fresh Air or Ventilation?

A spacious area within a cave may allow more air movement and comfort than a narrow fissure.

An explanation of the young people’s physical circumstances may therefore discuss the suitability of their location.

But fajwah does not lexically mean fresh air, airflow, or ventilation.

These may be consequences of a place’s conditions, but they should not replace the word’s definition in translation.

Nor does this verse specify the cave’s dimensions, number of openings, amount of light, temperature, or quality of air circulation.

Thus, “a well-ventilated area,” though potentially an explanation of the setting, is not a direct equivalent of fajwah.

4. Morphological and Syntactic Notes

فَجْوَةٍ derives from ف ج و and follows the pattern فَعْلَة.

This is a final-weak root with wāw as its third radical.

In فَجْوَة, the wāw is visibly retained before the final feminine ending.

The Qur’anic form is a singular, feminine, indefinite noun in the genitive case.

In the expression:

«وَهُمْ فِي فَجْوَةٍ مِّنْهُ»

Wa-hum fī fajwatin minhu.

هُمْ is a plural pronoun functioning as the subject of the nominal clause.

فِي فَجْوَةٍ is a prepositional phrase indicating their location.

مِنْهُ further identifies the spacious area as belonging to the cave.

In context, the complete clause وَهُمْ فِي فَجْوَةٍ مِّنْهُ is circumstantial: it describes the young people’s situation while the sun’s movement is being portrayed.

The indefiniteness of فَجْوَةٍ also suits a part of the cave not previously identified in the sentence.

This construction expresses the spaciousness of one part of the cave. Its indefinite form, however, does not establish immense size, limitless space, or several separate chambers.

Nor does the pattern فَعْلَة by itself prove that the word here names a single act of opening or a passing event. In this verse, fajwah names a space, not the act of opening.

5. Comparison with Related Roots

ف ج و (فَجْوَة) and ف ج ج (فَجّ)

These roots resemble one another in their letters and semantic fields, but their Qur’anic uses differ.

Fajj in al-Anbiyāʾ 21:31, al-Ḥajj 22:27, and Nūḥ 71:20 concerns passages and passable routes.

By contrast, fajwah in al-Kahf 18:17 denotes an open, spacious part of the cave.

A fajj may be an open mountain pass through which people travel.

A fajwah, however, may be an area within a cave or house where someone stays without necessarily passing through it to another destination.

Thus, both roots involve openness, but a passage is prominent in the Qur’anic use of ف ج ج, and the spaciousness of an area in ف ج و.

Their semantic resemblance alone does not establish a historical relationship or a shared derivational origin.

ف ج و (فَجْوَة) and ف ر ج (فَرْج)

ف ر ج is also associated with a gap, separation, and an opening.

In some lexical uses, furjah denotes an interval or gap between two things.

Fajwah too may denote the space between two parts, but its Qur’anic use emphasizes a location’s openness and spaciousness.

Not every gap is spacious. Two surfaces may be only slightly separated.

Fajwah, by contrast, generally suggests a noticeable open area.

This is nevertheless a tendency, not an absolute boundary that can be imposed on every Arabic use of the two families.

ف ج و (فَجْوَة) and ج و ف (جَوْف)

Jawf in Arabic concerns the inside or interior space of something.

Something may have an interior that is narrow. Conversely, an open area may be very broad without lying inside a body or object.

In al-Kahf 18:17, the fajwah is within the cave. It may therefore be called an interior space, but its spaciousness is conveyed by fajwah itself.

This distinction shows that being inside and being spacious are different properties.

ف ج و (فَجْوَة) and ف س ح (فَسَحَ)

ف س ح concerns spaciousness, expanding space, and making room.

In al-Mujādilah 58:11, believers are instructed to make room for one another in gatherings.

Here, fasḥ emphasizes opening up space and providing room for others.

Fajwah, however, names an open space.

Thus, fasḥ may concern creating or increasing spaciousness, while fajwah describes the spacious area itself.

The concepts may be close in their results, but their forms and uses are not identical.

6. Challenges in Understanding and Translation

The first error is to equate fajwah with a narrow fissure.

Although the root involves separation and openness, its Qur’anic occurrence denotes a spacious area. The Companions of the Cave are not described as occupying a narrow crack between rocks, but a broad part of the cave.

A second error is to identify the fajwah with the cave’s mouth.

The mouth may be open and broad, but مِنْهُ in the verse refers to an area belonging to the cave itself. Whether it was near the entrance or deep inside requires separate evidence.

A third error is to turn fajwah into a passage. Resemblance to ف ج ج may suggest this, but al-Kahf 18:17 concerns a place of rest rather than a route being traversed.

A fourth error is to add light, ventilation, safety, or a suitable temperature to the word’s lexical meaning.

These features may be discussed when analyzing the cave’s conditions, but they do not follow from ف ج و itself.

Modern senses of “gap” or “distance,” as in a knowledge gap, social differences, or class divisions, should not be transferred to Qur’anic usage without historical evidence. They may represent later extensions or figurative applications, rather than the direct meaning of fajwah in the Companions of the Cave account.

7. Suggested Renderings for Forqan

Several natural equivalents are available for فَجْوَة:

“An open space,” “a spacious area,” “a broad, open part,” and, in some other contexts, “an open interval between two things.”

In al-Kahf 18:17, an appropriate rendering of وَهُمْ فِي فَجْوَةٍ مِّنْهُ is:

“And they were in a spacious part of that cave.”

Or:

“And they were situated in a broad area of that cave.”

Both preserve the word’s central meaning and its connection to the cave.

“A spacious part of that cave” is preferable as the base rendering, since it identifies the location as part of the cave while conveying its breadth.

By contrast, “at the cave’s mouth,” “in a gap between mountains,” and “in a place with suitable airflow” introduce information the word itself does not express.

The following wording may also be proposed for Forqan’s brief word-box information:

Brief root meaning: Separation and openness between parts; an open, spacious area.

Meaning in this verse: The broad, open part of the cave where the young people were situated.

8. Conclusion

The Arabic root ف ج و concerns openness, separation, and spaciousness. Fajwah names an open space between two things or within a place.

Recorded uses concerning courtyards, spaces between heights, spacing between limbs, and the shape of a bow clarify this root’s semantic field, although the precise history of its branches is not equally established.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as فَجْوَةٍ in al-Kahf 18:17. The verse describes the Companions of the Cave in a spacious part of the cave, alongside the sun’s position at sunrise and sunset.

The root’s semantic core is openness and separation that create an open or spacious area between parts.

The Qur’anic emphasis is on the spaciousness of the young people’s resting place, not on a passage, cave entrance, ventilation, or a specific geometry.

“A spacious part of the cave” is therefore a precise, natural rendering of this word in the context of al-Kahf 18:17.
