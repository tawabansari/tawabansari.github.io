Doubt in understanding, lack of certainty, and its relationship to the root’s physical uses in early Arabic

The root SH K K has two important fields of use in early Arabic: inserting something into something else, piercing and joining parts; and uncertainty in understanding, without reaching certainty about a particular matter.

In its cognitive use, shakk describes a person’s lack of assurance about whether a report is true, an event occurred, a claim is valid or a judgment is sound. Several possibilities may remain open, or the available evidence may not suffice to accept one of them.

Doubt is therefore related to not knowing, but it is not identical with every kind of ignorance. Someone may know nothing about a subject without yet having a question or doubt about it. Doubt arises when a matter has come before the mind but judgment about it has not reached certainty.

This cognitive meaning is central to every Qur’anic occurrence of SH K K.

1. The root in early Arabic

Inserting, piercing and joining

One established early Arabic use is to drive an object such as a spear, arrow or pointed tool into another body.

Shakkahu bi-al-rumḥ was used for striking someone with a spear and driving it into the body. This use can include piercing something and passing a tool through it.

The Muʿallaqah attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād contains:

فَشَكَكْتُ بِالرُّمْحِ الْأَصَمِّ ثِيَابَهُ

fa-shakaktu bi-al-rumḥi al-aṣammi thiyābahu

لَيْسَ الْكَرِيمُ عَلَى الْقَنَا بِمُحَرَّمِ

laysa al-karīmu ʿalā al-qanā bi-muḥarram

Translation:

“I pierced his garments with the solid spear; even a noble man is not immune to spear shafts.”

Here fa-shakaktu denotes the spear thrust and penetration. The point is not merely tearing the surface of clothing: the poet describes a blow that passes through clothing into the warrior’s body.

Another use in this field involves inserting and joining parts, as when an implement passes through several pieces to connect them.

The Muʿallaqah attributed to Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd also contains:

كَأَنَّ جَنَاحَيْ مُضْرَحِيٍّ تَكَنَّفَا

ka-anna janāḥay muḍraḥiyyin takannafā

حِفَافَيْهِ شُكَّا فِي الْعَسِيبِ بِمِسْرَدِ

ḥifāfayhi shukkā fī al-ʿasībi bi-misrad

Translation:

“As though a white vulture’s two wings flanked both sides of its tail, pinned into the tailbone with an awl.”

In describing a camel’s tail, the poet uses the image of two wings apparently fastened to it with a pointed implement. Shukkā refers here to insertion and attachment. Muḍraḥī is explained as a white or large vulture; it does not necessarily mean an old bird.

These two witnesses, attributed to pre-Islamic poets, illustrate physical uses in two settings: a spear penetrating a body and parts fastened with a tool. The precise dates of these individual lines have not been independently established in this study.

Military equipment and implements

In classical Arabic, shikkah denotes weapons, especially the equipment worn by a warrior. Shākkun fī al-silāḥ describes someone fully equipped with arms and protective gear.

This use can be understood within the field of wearing, enclosing and joining the parts of military equipment. Such a relationship does not establish that every sense of the root originated in the name of a weapon or protective covering.

Forms such as shikkah, shākk and the verb shakka are established in Arabic, but they do not occur among the Qur’anic uses of SH K K.

The relationship between physical use and mental doubt

Lexicographers have proposed an image connecting these semantic fields: just as one thing enters another or several things are joined, different possibilities gather in a doubting person’s mind without one yet being distinguished from the others with certainty.

This image helps explain a relationship between meanings, but does not by itself establish the history of the cognitive sense.

The poetic witnesses illustrate physical uses in language attributed to the pre-Islamic period. Cognitive shakk clearly occurs in the Qur’an and is recorded in Arabic dictionaries. Independent historical evidence is nevertheless needed to establish exactly when it developed from physical usage, or whether that development necessarily occurred.

In studying the Qur’an, shakk should therefore first be understood in its established sense of doubt and lack of certainty. Images of spears, piercing or physical attachment should not be imported into the verses.

2. Qur’anic forms and central meaning

In the Qur’an, SH K K occurs fifteen times, all as the noun shakk, in the inflected forms shakkun and shakkin.

Verbs such as shakka, “to doubt,” and shakkaka, “to cause doubt,” and forms such as shākk and the plural shukūk are known in Arabic but do not occur in the Qur’anic text.

The central Qur’anic meaning of shakk may be defined as:

A state of doubt and absence of certainty about a particular matter, in which a person has not reached an assured judgment concerning its truth, validity or occurrence.

This definition does not specify the object of doubt. It may be God, revelation, scripture, a messenger’s invitation, a historical event or the Hereafter. The verse’s context establishes the object and the person’s stance toward it.

Later technical definitions that sometimes require two possibilities to be exactly equal should not be imposed on every Qur’anic occurrence. Lack of certainty does not necessarily mean equal probabilities in the person’s judgment.

Most Qur’anic occurrences combine shakk with fī, as in fī shakkin. This expresses the condition of being in doubt. The preposition alone does not establish that the doubt is permanent, intentional or impossible to resolve; such features require accompanying expressions.

3. Doubt concerning reports, truth and disagreement

A particularly clear occurrence is Al-Nisāʾ 4:157, where the Qur’an speaks of disagreement concerning Jesus’ fate:

وَإِنَّ الَّذِينَ اخْتَلَفُوا فِيهِ لَفِي شَكٍّ مِنْهُ

wa-inna alladhīna ikhtalafū fīhi la-fī shakkin minhu

Al-Nisāʾ 4:157

Translation:

“Those who differed concerning him are indeed in doubt about him.”

It then says:

مَا لَهُمْ بِهِ مِنْ عِلْمٍ إِلَّا اتِّبَاعَ الظَّنِّ

mā lahum bihi min ʿilmin illā ittibāʿa al-ẓann

Al-Nisāʾ 4:157

Translation:

“They have no knowledge of it, only the following of conjecture.”

Three concepts appear together here: disagreement, doubt and following conjecture.

Ikhtilāf concerns differing opinions. Shakk expresses failure to reach certainty. In this context, ẓann concerns conjecture that has taken the place of reliable knowledge.

These words do not have one identical meaning. Disagreement alone does not prove that everyone involved is doubtful, just as every supposition is not necessarily doubt. Here, however, the Qur’an expressly describes those who disagree as being in doubt and lacking reliable knowledge of the matter.

Shakk here concerns knowledge and the validity of a historical claim. The word alone cannot establish details of the event or how the matter became unclear beyond what the verse states.

Shubbiha lahum in the same verse belongs to another root, SH B H. An event being made unclear and someone doubting it are related but distinct concepts.

4. Doubt toward messengers’ invitations and divine scripture

Doubt concerning the invitation

In Hūd 11:62, Ṣāliḥ’s people say:

وَإِنَّنَا لَفِي شَكٍّ مِمَّا تَدْعُونَا إِلَيْهِ مُرِيبٍ

wa-innanā la-fī shakkin mimmā tadʿūnā ilayhi murīb

Hūd 11:62

Translation:

“We are indeed in unsettling doubt about that to which you call us.”

A closely similar construction is reported in Ibrāhīm 14:9 from communities confronting their messengers:

وَإِنَّا لَفِي شَكٍّ مِمَّا تَدْعُونَنَا إِلَيْهِ مُرِيبٍ

wa-innā la-fī shakkin mimmā tadʿūnanā ilayhi murīb

Ibrāhīm 14:9

Translation:

“We are indeed in unsettling doubt about that to which you all call us.”

In both verses, the object of doubt is the messengers’ invitation. Mimmā tadʿūnā ilayhi locates the uncertainty in the content of that invitation.

Shakk is not alone here: it is qualified by murīb, from R Y B. This adjective emphasizes an unsettling, disturbing or distrust-arousing aspect of the doubt.

Shakk and rayb overlap in meaning, but their differing ranges must be assessed from multiple usages. Their combination here is not sufficient proof of a difference, since closely related expressions can also reinforce one another.

In Ibrāhīm 14:10, the messengers reply:

أَفِي اللَّهِ شَكٌّ فَاطِرِ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ

a-fī allāhi shakkun fāṭiri al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍ

Ibrāhīm 14:10

Translation:

“Is there doubt about God, Originator of the heavens and the earth?”

Shakkun occurs in a rhetorical question challenging the doubt. The messengers appeal to God’s creative activity in disputing their opponents’ uncertainty.

The value and direction of doubt in this exchange emerge from its object, the argument and the parties’ responses. The root SH K K does not inherently mean hostility or disbelief.

Doubt about religion and the possibility of questioning

In Yūnus 10:104, Muḥammad is instructed to tell the people:

إِنْ كُنْتُمْ فِي شَكٍّ مِنْ دِينِي

in kuntum fī shakkin min dīnī

Yūnus 10:104

Translation:

“If you are in doubt about my religion.”

This is a conditional construction. It raises the possibility of doubt among the addressees without establishing that every one of them actually had it.

In Yūnus 10:94, Muḥammad is addressed:

فَإِنْ كُنْتَ فِي شَكٍّ مِمَّا أَنْزَلْنَا إِلَيْكَ فَاسْأَلِ الَّذِينَ يَقْرَءُونَ الْكِتَابَ مِنْ قَبْلِكَ

fa-in kunta fī shakkin mimmā anzalnā ilayka fa-isʾali alladhīna yaqraʾūna al-kitāba min qablika

Yūnus 10:94

Translation:

“If you are in doubt about what We have sent down to you, ask those who read the Book before you.”

This verse is especially significant for understanding the nature of doubt.

First, the sentence begins with “if.” A conditional does not itself report that the addressee actually doubted. Conversely, its conditional form alone is not sufficient to establish that such a state is impossible.

Second, the response to the condition is fa-isʾal: “ask.” This construction connects questioning and consulting available knowledge with resolving uncertainty.

The lexical meaning of shakk therefore cannot justify a rule that every question about a religious subject automatically indicates disbelief, hostility or corrupt intent. A person’s state of knowledge and response to evidence require separate examination.

Doubt concerning scripture and the divine message

Hūd 11:110 reports that Moses received the Book and disagreement arose concerning it, then describes the group under discussion as la-fī shakkin minhu murīb. The same theme recurs in Fuṣṣilat 41:45.

In Al-Shūrā 42:14, la-fī shakkin minhu murīb describes those who inherited the Book after earlier generations.

These verses show that doubt can arise amid disagreement over scripture and the transmission of religious teachings. The word shakk alone does not attribute identical intentions and responsibility to every generation. Al-Shūrā 42:14, for example, distinguishes in its wording between those who divided through transgression after knowledge came and those who subsequently inherited the Book.

Ṣād 38:8 describes a group questioning why the Reminder was sent down to Muḥammad:

بَلْ هُمْ فِي شَكٍّ مِنْ ذِكْرِي

bal hum fī shakkin min dhikrī

Ṣād 38:8

Translation:

“Rather, they are in doubt about My Reminder.”

Here the object of doubt is the Reminder. Their question about why it descended upon Muḥammad belongs to the context; shakk itself still means lack of certainty concerning the matter at issue.

5. Doubt concerning the Hereafter and its consequences

The Hereafter is another clear sphere of Qur’anic usage.

Al-Naml 27:66 states:

بَلْ هُمْ فِي شَكٍّ مِنْهَا بَلْ هُمْ مِنْهَا عَمُونَ

bal hum fī shakkin minhā bal hum minhā ʿamūn

Al-Naml 27:66

Translation:

“Rather, they are in doubt about it; rather, they are blind to it.”

The object of doubt is the Hereafter. Bal hum minhā ʿamūn then describes their condition more severely.

Shakk and ʿamūn do not mean the same thing. The former conveys cognitive uncertainty; the latter expresses blindness in perception and discernment here. The intensified description comes from the verse’s construction, not from blindness being part of shakk’s own meaning.

Sabaʾ 34:21 also distinguishes between someone who believes in the Hereafter and someone in doubt about it.

This contrast places doubt concerning the Hereafter against belief in it. It should not become a universal definition equating every doubt, on every subject, with knowing disbelief.

Sabaʾ 34:54 describes people separated from what they desire in the scene of their final outcome:

إِنَّهُمْ كَانُوا فِي شَكٍّ مُرِيبٍ

innahum kānū fī shakkin murīb

Sabaʾ 34:54

Translation:

“They had been in unsettling doubt.”

The expression refers to their earlier condition. Their doubt is situated within a context where the consequences of their choices and beliefs become apparent.

Remaining in doubt despite evidence

Ghāfir 40:34 mentions Joseph’s arrival with clear proofs and then addresses the people:

فَمَا زِلْتُمْ فِي شَكٍّ مِمَّا جَاءَكُمْ بِهِ

fa-mā ziltum fī shakkin mimmā jāʾakum bihi

Ghāfir 40:34

Translation:

“Yet you remained in doubt about what he brought you.”

Mā ziltum expresses continuation. The issue is not merely a passing uncertainty, but doubt continuing despite the arrival of clear proofs.

Persistence thus comes from mā ziltum, not from the independent meaning of shakk.

Al-Dukhān 44:9 also states:

بَلْ هُمْ فِي شَكٍّ يَلْعَبُونَ

bal hum fī shakkin yalʿabūn

Al-Dukhān 44:9

Translation:

“Rather, they are in doubt, playing.”

Yalʿabūn describes the conduct of the particular group. It cannot be generalized to everyone uncertain about a subject.

The Qur’an can therefore speak of doubt alongside treating truth playfully, persistence despite evidence or distrust. These associations do not make every such behavior part of shakk’s lexical definition.

6. SH K K and related roots

SH K K (shakk) and R Y B (rayb)

Shakk focuses on lack of certainty and remaining doubt about a particular matter.

Rayb overlaps with doubt, but distrust, suspicion or unease is also prominent in many of its uses.

Shakkin murīb is an important example of their proximity and distinction. Murīb is an added qualification of doubt; its entire content cannot simply be derived from shakk itself.

The study of R Y B examines this distinction more fully.

Study of R Y B

SH K K (shakk) and SH B H (shabah)

Shabah concerns likeness in appearance or signs. Such similarity sometimes makes identification difficult.

Shakk is the uncertainty of someone who has not reached certainty about a subject.

Similarity between two things may cause doubt, but not every similarity does so, nor does every doubt arise from similarity.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:157 places shubbiha lahum and la-fī shakkin minhu in one context without making them synonymous.

For further uses of resemblance and ambiguity, see the study of SH B H.

Study of SH B H

SH K K (shakk) and Ẓ N N (ẓann)

Ẓann concerns supposition or judgment whose confidence and validity must be understood from context. The Qur’anic root is not always limited to weak conjecture; sometimes it approaches firm belief and assurance.

Shakk, by contrast, expresses lack of certainty about the matter.

Someone may doubt whether an event occurred while considering one possibility more likely. Doubt does not necessarily exclude every supposition or mental preference.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:157 places following ẓann alongside lack of knowledge and the presence of doubt. This contextual relationship does not make the roots completely synonymous.

SH K K (shakk) and M R Y (miryah and mirāʾ)

Qur’anic miryah is close to doubt and lack of assurance, as in fa-lā taku fī miryatin minhu in Hūd 11:17.

Mirāʾ and its verbal forms in the same family also concern discussion, disputation and contention over a subject.

This root’s semantic range can encompass both doubt and dispute. It does not follow that every miryah necessarily involves overt argument, or that every doubt leads to conflict.

Doubt, ignorance and denial

Three conditions should also remain distinct:

Someone may not know a subject and have no judgment about it. Another may encounter a claim but remain uncertain of its truth. A third may recognize a truth yet knowingly deny or conceal it.

These conditions are not identical.

Doubt alone does not specify a person’s knowledge, access to evidence, intention or response after recognizing the truth. Moral judgment also requires evidence concerning these matters.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

The primary Persian equivalent of shakk is shakk, the familiar Persian loanword meaning doubt. It is natural and ordinarily needs no artificial substitute in Qur’anic passages.

Tardīd is also suitable, especially when clarifying the person’s cognitive condition. Dodelī may work in some contexts, but it can suggest indecision in practical choices and should not replace every Qur’anic instance. In English, “doubt” or “uncertainty” usually conveys the central sense.

Important combinations can be rendered as follows:

  • Fī shakkin: in doubt; in uncertainty.
  • Fī shakkin minhu: in doubt about it.
  • Fī shakkin mimmā tadʿūnā ilayhi: in doubt about that to which you call us.
  • Shakkin murīb: unsettling doubt; doubt involving distrust or unease, as context supports.
  • A-fī allāhi shakkun: Is there doubt about God?

A translation of shakkin murīb should preserve the adjective’s role. Replacing the entire expression with plain “doubt” loses a distinction explicitly made in Arabic.

Where only shakk occurs, words such as “obstinacy,” “excuse-making,” “hostility to truth” or “deliberate doubt” should not be inserted without evidence. If the context supports such features, they belong in the verse’s analysis. These proposals do not change the site’s installed verse translations.

Conclusion

SH K K has physical uses in early Arabic for insertion, piercing and joining parts, with examples in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period. In cognitive usage, shakk denotes doubt and lack of certainty concerning a particular matter.

Every Qur’anic occurrence is the noun shakk and belongs to this cognitive field.

Doubt may concern a historical report, messengers’ invitations, divine scripture, God or the Hereafter. It sometimes appears alongside disagreement and conjecture, is qualified by murīb, or has its persistence expressed by other wording. People’s response to truth may also be criticized in the same context.

These features must be understood in their proper place. The word doubt neither proves sincere inquiry nor by itself establishes knowing denial of truth. It describes a cognitive condition; moral responsibility also depends on what the person knows, the evidence available and the response adopted toward truth.

The central Qur’anic meaning of shakk is therefore uncertainty about the truth or validity of a particular matter, without reaching certainty about it. Translation should preserve that meaning, adding ethical, psychological or behavioral characteristics only insofar as the verse and its context support them.
