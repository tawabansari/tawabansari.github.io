The Qur’anic Root Ḥ L M

Qur’anic and lexical forms

From dreams and puberty to reason, forbearance, and considered self-restraint

The Arabic root ح ل م has several semantic branches. حُلْم denotes what someone sees in sleep; حِلْم concerns forbearance, sound judgement, and restraining impulsive conduct; and حَلِيم describes someone who responds to anger, provocation, or disagreeable behaviour with judgement and self-restraint.

The same family expresses a child’s reaching puberty. بُلُوغُ الْحُلُمِ denotes the transition from childhood to sexual maturity. The plural أَحْلَام is also recorded in Arabic both for dreams and for human reason and discernment.

These meanings must be kept distinct. Dreaming is not the same as wisdom; sexual maturity does not necessarily mean complete intellectual development; and forbearance does not mean overlooking every wrong or being unable to act.

The root occurs twenty-one times in twenty Qur’anic verses: fifteen occurrences of حَلِيم and six from the حُلْم and أَحْلَام family. حَلِيم describes both The God and human beings. أَحْلَام refers to dreams or human reason in different verses.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: twenty-one occurrences in twenty verses

Qur’anic forms: حَلِيم, الْحَلِيم, أَحْلَام, أَحْلَامُهُمْ, and الْحُلُم. Differences in case, definiteness, or an attached pronoun do not necessarily create a separate derivative.

Forms such as حَلَمَ, حَلُمَ, حِلْم, حُلْم, حَلِيمَة, حُلَمَاء, احْتَلَمَ, تَحَلَّمَ, تَحْلِيم, and حَالِم are also recorded in Arabic but do not appear in these forms in the Qur’an.

1. Semantic Principles and Early Arabic Usage

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies three separate principles for ح ل م: avoiding haste, the perforation and deterioration of something, and seeing something in sleep.

He expressly notes their considerable semantic divergence: not every word can be reduced to a single meaning through an analogical derivation.

This matters here. Although reason, maturity, and self-restraint have intelligible connections, dreaming or damage to a hide cannot be derived from forbearance without historical evidence.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: three distinct principles of Ḥ L M

حُلْم: what is seen in sleep

One Arabic use of حَلَمَ يَحْلُمُ means seeing something in sleep.

حُلْم and حُلُم denote what someone sees while asleep; their plural is أَحْلَام.

Al-ʿAyn and Lisān al-ʿArab connect this use with dreams and seeing things in sleep.

احْتَلَمَ is also recorded for dreaming, while another established use concerns a sexual experience during sleep.

تَحَلَّمَ can concern dreaming and, in some uses, falsely claiming to have seen a dream. The meaning of a false claim must not be extended to every form of this root.

حُلْم and رُؤْيَا frequently occupy the same semantic field in Arabic: both can denote what is seen in sleep.

Some hadiths and lexical explanations distinguish a good vision from an unpleasant dream. This is not an absolute lexical rule governing every use: early dictionaries also record overlap between حُلْم and رُؤْيَا. Such a terminological distinction should therefore not be transferred indiscriminately to all Qur’anic occurrences.

The word حُلْم itself does not establish that every dream so named is necessarily confused, false, or meaningless.

Lisān al-ʿArab and lexical sources: overlap and terminological distinctions between ḥulm and ruʾyā

حِلْم: self-restraint against haste and anger

حِلْم, with kasrah on the first consonant, concerns forbearance, considered conduct, and avoiding impulsive behaviour.

Ibn Fāris contrasts it with طَيْش: rashness and thoughtless conduct.

Lisān al-ʿArab also explains الحِلْم through الأَنَاة and العَقْل.

أَنَاة here means deliberation and considered conduct, rather than merely moving slowly or postponing every action.

A forbearing person does not immediately surrender control of his conduct to emotion when provoked, angry, or confronted with disagreeable behaviour.

This is compatible with the power to respond. Someone may be able to answer, punish, or resist another person while exercising judgement about the manner and timing of the response.

Forbearance therefore means neither incapacity nor silence before every injustice.

Nor should it be reduced to enduring suffering. Difficult circumstances may be endured without considered choice, even through weakness or fear; praiseworthy حِلْم concerns restraining impulsive conduct and maintaining judgement.

حَلِيم: a forbearing, judicious person

حَلِيم is an adjectival form, ṣifah mushabbahah, on فَعِيل; in ethical usage it describes someone possessing حِلْم.

It can indicate a relatively enduring characteristic, rather than one instance of calm conduct.

The pattern فَعِيل alone does not establish that someone behaves identically in every circumstance without exception. The extent and quality of the characteristic require actual evidence and context.

حَلِيم is also sometimes applied to a wise person possessing considered judgement.

This use connects with self-restraint: someone considering the consequences of his conduct is less likely to decide under the sway of anger and haste.

Forbearance and knowledge are nevertheless distinct. Someone may possess extensive knowledge yet behave rashly and harshly; someone with limited knowledge can act patiently and judiciously in relationships.

Translating حَلِيم therefore requires attention to both forbearance and judgement, without turning it into unrestricted knowledge or intelligence.

Al-Rāghib’s Mufradāt: restraint of anger and the relationship with reason

أَحْلَام: the plural of dreams and of reason

An important feature of this family is that أَحْلَام is recorded with two different meanings.

It can be the plural of حُلْم, a dream or vision in sleep.

It can also pluralize حِلْم in the sense of reason, judgement, and forbearance.

The consonants are identical, while the vowel on the first consonant and the semantic fields differ.

أَحْلَام in the Qur’an cannot therefore automatically be translated as “dreams” without considering the context.

Yūsuf 12:44 concerns dreams; in Al-Ṭūr 52:32, the same plural concerns human reason and discernment.

Recognizing the root is thus insufficient: the nominal form, its established usage, and the sentence containing it must also be examined.

حُلُم: reaching puberty

حُلُم is connected with dreaming in Arabic, but بُلُوغُ الْحُلُمِ denotes reaching sexual maturity.

Lexical sources also record احْتِلَام for a sexual experience during sleep. Through this connection, a child’s reaching puberty is expressed as بُلُوغُ الْحُلُمِ.

The expression should not be taken to mean that every child is considered mature only after personally experiencing a particular sexual dream.

Puberty is a stage of physical development that can become apparent through different signs. The Arabic expression uses one familiar experience associated with that stage, but the stage’s designation must not be equated with a particular dream occurring in every individual.

Sexual maturity and fully developed intellectual judgement are also distinct. An adolescent may be biologically mature while still needing education and experience for certain complex responsibilities.

This distinction matters when examining the verses in Al-Nūr and comparing them with Al-Nisāʾ 4:6.

2. Early Poetry and Other Lexical Branches

Dreams in pre-Islamic poetry

The pre-Islamic poet Bishr ibn Abī Khāzim opens an ode with:

أَحَقٌّ مَا رَأَيْتُ أَمِ احْتِلَامُ
أَمِ الْأَهْوَالُ إِذْ صَحْبِي نِيَامُ

a-ḥaqqun mā raʾaytu ami iḥtilāmu / ami al-ahwālu idh ṣaḥbī niyāmu

Meaning: Was what I saw real, or a dream? Or terrifying images while my companions slept?

احْتِلَام here concerns something seen in sleep.

The poem distinguishes waking experience from what is seen while asleep.

It attests the use of احْتِلَام for dreaming in pre-Islamic poetry.

The word does not mean reaching puberty in this verse. The same lexical form can elsewhere concern sexual experience during sleep and, through that association, puberty.

The meaning of puberty must therefore not be transferred into every occurrence of احْتِلَام.

Bishr ibn Abī Khāzim’s poem: a-ḥaqqun mā raʾaytu am iḥtilām

Forbearance contrasted with foolishness

A verse attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā reads:

وَإِنَّ سَفَاهَ الشَّيْخِ لَا حِلْمَ بَعْدَهُ
وَإِنَّ الْفَتَى بَعْدَ السَّفَاهَةِ يَحْلُمُ

wa-inna safāha al-shaykhi lā ḥilma baʿdahu / wa-inna al-fatā baʿda al-safāhati yaḥlumu

Meaning: “An old man’s foolishness has no forbearance after it; a young man becomes forbearing after foolishness.” The first statement is emphatic poetic language. Its point may be understood as the difficulty of correcting foolishness in old age, rather than an absolute rule excluding moral growth in all older people.

حِلْم is contrasted with سَفَاه in this verse.

The contrast shows that forbearance exceeds outward calm: it concerns considered conduct and avoiding foolishness.

يَحْلُمُ here means becoming forbearing and judicious, rather than dreaming.

The verse appears in transmissions of Zuhayr’s poetry, although the presence and order of verses differ among collections. Some literary accounts also show Muʿāwiyah quoting it in conversation with ʿAqīl; someone’s quoting a verse does not by itself make him its author. Poetic attribution, transmitted variants, and later citation must therefore be distinguished.

Ansāb al-Ashrāf: Muʿāwiyah quoting the verse and its attribution to Zuhayr

Nevertheless, the opposition between حِلْم and سَفَاه accords with reliable lexical accounts.

Ashʿār al-Shuʿarāʾ al-Sittah: a note on transmitted verses of Zuhayr

Forbearance, anger, and reason

Arabic poetry also contrasts حَلِيم with جَاهِل and سَفِيه.

جَهْل in such contrasts does not always mean merely lacking information; it can concern rashness, violence, and conduct departing from considered judgement.

Forbearance can accordingly accompany reason, dignity, and restraint of anger.

This does not mean that every forbearing person knows everything or that experiencing anger once makes someone devoid of forbearance.

The ethical quality must be identified in conduct and responses. Someone may become angry at injustice or corruption while remaining judicious in responding.

Forbearance should therefore not become the absence of every feeling of anger.

حَلِمَ الْأَدِيمُ: damage to a hide

Another branch is recorded in حَلِمَ الْأَدِيمُ.

أَدِيم denotes prepared hide, or a hide intended for tanning and use.

حَلِمَ الْأَدِيمُ describes a hide perforated and spoiled through the activity of small creatures.

Ibn Fāris presents this as an independent principle, separate from ethical forbearance and dreaming.

A verse attributed to Al-Walīd ibn ʿUqbah reads:

فَإِنَّكَ وَالْكِتَابَ إِلَى عَلِيٍّ
كَدَابِغَةٍ وَقَدْ حَلِمَ الْأَدِيمُ

fa-innaka wa-l-kitāba ilā ʿaliyyin / ka-dābighatin wa-qad ḥalima al-adīmu

Meaning: You and your writing to ʿAlī are like someone trying to tan a hide that has already been damaged and spoiled.

This witness belongs to the Islamic period and should not be called pre-Islamic poetry.

حَلِمَ here means neither dreaming nor becoming forbearing; it concerns deterioration and perforation of the hide.

Al-ʿIqd al-Farīd: Al-Walīd ibn ʿUqbah’s damaged-hide witness

حَلَم and حَلَمَة: small creatures and other uses

حَلَم is recorded for small creatures, including a kind of parasite or tick.

حَلَمَة denotes a small creature and, in another usage, a nipple.

Ibn Fāris also records تَحَلَّمَ for becoming fat or full, relating it to the image of an engorged tick; بَعِيرٌ حَلِيمٌ is likewise recorded for a fat camel. This lexical account of fullness and fatness must be distinguished from ethical forbearance.

These meanings do not occur in the Qur’an.

They show that the lexical family extends beyond dreams, reason, and forbearance. A definite historical connection must not, however, be invented for all these uses.

In particular, a small creature or perforated hide provides no basis for interpreting حَلِيم as a description of The God or a human being.

Ibn Fāris: hide, ticks, and the image of fullness

3. حُلْم and أَحْلَام: Dreams, Reason, and Puberty in the Qur’an

Yūsuf 12:44: confused dreams

قَالُوا أَضْغَاثُ أَحْلَامٍ وَمَا نَحْنُ بِتَأْوِيلِ الْأَحْلَامِ بِعَالِمِينَ

qālū aḍghāthu aḥlāmin wa-mā naḥnu bi-taʾwīli al-aḥlāmi bi-ʿālimīna

Translation: They said, “Confused dreams; and we are not knowledgeable about interpreting these dreams.”

أَحْلَام occurs twice in this verse.

The first occurrence is أَضْغَاثُ أَحْلَامٍ. أَضْغَاث comes from a different root and concerns things intertwined and mixed together.

أَحْلَام denotes dreams that the group addressed considers confused and jumbled.

The second occurrence, بِتَأْوِيلِ الْأَحْلَامِ, concerns interpreting or making clear the dreams’ meaning.

أَحْلَام does not therefore itself mean confused dreams: confusion is supplied by أَضْغَاث.

This distinction matters in Joseph’s story.

The king had seen a dream and asked others about it. They considered it jumbled dreams and professed no knowledge of interpreting such dreams.

Later in the story, Joseph offers an orderly explanation of the same dream relating to years of abundance and difficulty.

The first group’s judgement that the dream was confused must therefore not be accepted as its established reality.

The verse reports their words; the word أَحْلَام does not itself pronounce the dream meaningless.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:5: the accusation of confused dreams

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:5, opponents say of what the Prophet has brought:

بَلْ قَالُوا أَضْغَاثُ أَحْلَامٍ بَلِ افْتَرَاهُ بَلْ هُوَ شَاعِرٌ

bal qālū aḍghāthu aḥlāmin bali iftarāhu bal huwa shāʿirun

Translation of the quoted passage: “Rather, they said, confused dreams; rather, he fabricated it; rather, he is a poet.”

أَضْغَاثُ أَحْلَامٍ appears again, but this time the subject is not interpreting a king’s dream.

These are accusations concerning the Prophet and his message. His opponents first call it confused dreams, then claim fabrication, and then call him a poet.

Their shifting accusations show that their discourse itself is criticized in the context.

أَحْلَام denotes dreams here, but the word’s presence does not mean that the Qur’an actually identifies the Prophet’s message as a confused dream.

The verse reports the opponents’ claim rather than delivering a final judgement affirming it.

Al-Ṭūr 52:32: أَحْلَام as reason

أَمْ تَأْمُرُهُمْ أَحْلَامُهُمْ بِهَٰذَا أَمْ هُمْ قَوْمٌ طَاغُونَ

am taʾmuruhum aḥlāmuhum bi-hādhā am hum qawmun ṭāghūna

Translation: Do their minds command them to do this, or are they a transgressing people?

This is an important witness to the root’s semantic range.

أَحْلَامُهُمْ does not refer to their nightly dreams.

The preceding verses discuss accusations against the Prophet: they call him a soothsayer and a madman, and also a poet.

The verse then asks whether their minds command such statements or whether the issue is their transgression.

أَحْلَام can pluralize حِلْم in the sense of reason and considered judgement.

“Their minds” or “their powers of reason” is therefore appropriate here.

This does not mean that every Qur’anic أَحْلَام denotes reason: in Yūsuf 12:44 and Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:5, the same form means dreams.

The context determines the distinction.

The verse also connects reason with the ability to make considered judgements. Its question treats the opponents’ statements as inconsistent with sound judgement.

This does not imply that the opponents biologically or psychologically lacked every intellectual capacity.

The verse criticizes their conduct and judgement. Their actual mental capacity cannot be diagnosed from أَحْلَام itself.

Al-Nūr 24:58 and Al-Nūr 24:59: reaching puberty

Al-Nūr 24:58 mentions الَّذِينَ لَمْ يَبْلُغُوا الْحُلُمَ: those who have not yet reached puberty.

The verse discusses asking permission to enter at three specified times: before the dawn prayer, when putting aside clothing at midday, and after the night prayer.

Al-Nūr 24:59 states:

وَإِذَا بَلَغَ الْأَطْفَالُ مِنْكُمُ الْحُلُمَ فَلْيَسْتَأْذِنُوا كَمَا اسْتَأْذَنَ الَّذِينَ مِنْ قَبْلِهِمْ كَذَٰلِكَ يُبَيِّنُ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ آيَاتِهِ وَاللَّهُ عَلِيمٌ حَكِيمٌ

wa-idhā balagha al-aṭfālu minkumu al-ḥuluma fa-l-yastaʾdhinū ka-mā istaʾdhana alladhīna min qablihim ka-dhālika yubayyinu allāhu lakum āyātihi wa-allāhu ʿalīmun ḥakīmun

Translation: When your children reach puberty, let them ask permission as those before them asked permission. Thus The God makes His signs clear to you; and The God is Knowing, Wise.

الْحُلُمَ denotes puberty in both verses.

This is not unrelated to dreaming, since احْتِلَام is also recorded for a sexual experience during sleep.

بُلُوغُ الْحُلُمِ here nevertheless names a stage of human development; the ruling is not confined to children who have personally experienced a particular dream.

Puberty here concerns privacy and seeking permission. The direct subject is the change in responsibility for respecting others’ privacy after reaching puberty.

The verse alone does not determine every ruling about financial competence, ability to manage property, readiness for marriage, or social responsibilities.

The ruling in Al-Nisāʾ 4:6 distinguishes reaching marriageable age from establishing sound judgement before handing over property.

The verses differ in subject and vocabulary, but comparing them reminds us not to equate biological maturity with the ability to manage complex affairs responsibly without examination.

Al-Nūr 24:58 and Al-Nūr 24:59 distinguish the two groups’ permission requirements according to whether puberty has been reached, rather than establishing complete ethical and intellectual maturity.

Al-Ṭabarī, Al-Nūr 24:59: puberty and seeking permission

4. حَلِيم Applied to Humans: Forbearance with Judgement

The Qur’an also applies حَلِيم to several people. These uses matter for understanding its ethical meaning: forbearance is not exclusively a divine attribute.

Abraham: Hūd 11:75

إِنَّ إِبْرَاهِيمَ لَحَلِيمٌ أَوَّاهٌ مُنِيبٌ

inna ibrāhīma la-ḥalīmun awwāhun munībun

Translation: Indeed, Abraham was forbearing, tender-hearted, and constantly turning back to The God.

The verse places three descriptions together: حَلِيم, أَوَّاه, and مُنِيب.

حَلِيم concerns his forbearance and considered conduct.

أَوَّاه belongs to another family and concerns tenderness of heart, responsiveness, and deep concern.

مُنِيب concerns returning and turning toward The God.

These descriptions must not be treated as complete synonyms.

In the preceding verses, Abraham discusses the people of Lot after receiving news concerning them.

His forbearance does not mean indifference to others’ fate. He can be concerned about a people’s conduct and destiny without acting rashly or in anger.

Forbearance accompanies compassion and turning toward The God here, but all three must not be folded into the lexical meaning of حَلِيم.

Abraham: Al-Tawbah 9:114

After explaining Abraham’s seeking forgiveness for his father and the clarification of his father’s situation, Al-Tawbah 9:114 states:

إِنَّ إِبْرَاهِيمَ لَأَوَّاهٌ حَلِيمٌ

inna ibrāhīma la-awwāhun ḥalīmun

Translation of the quoted passage: “Indeed, Abraham is tender-hearted and forbearing.”

Abraham is again described through أَوَّاه and حَلِيم.

The context shows that his compassion and forbearance are compatible with recognizing the truth and responding appropriately once it becomes clear.

The verse explains that he dissociated himself from his father when his father’s situation became clear to him.

Abraham’s forbearance does not therefore imply that a forbearing person must retain an earlier position under all circumstances, even after the truth becomes clear.

Forbearance is compatible with revising one’s position and deciding on the basis of new knowledge.

This distinguishes it from blind insistence: forbearance can enable someone to assess reality and act accordingly without captivity to anger or attachment.

غُلَامٍ حَلِيمٍ: Al-Ṣāffāt 37:101

فَبَشَّرْنَاهُ بِغُلَامٍ حَلِيمٍ

fa-bashsharnāhu bi-ghulāmin ḥalīmin

Translation: So We gave him glad tidings of a forbearing boy.

حَلِيم describes the boy whose birth is announced to Abraham.

غُلَام denotes a boy or youth, but حَلِيم need not mean that he possessed an adult’s behavioural maturity at the moment of birth.

The announcement can be understood as also identifying a future characteristic.

The subsequent story presents the boy’s response to his father’s words, a context consistent with his description as forbearing.

The meaning of حَلِيم should not, however, be confined to one particular act in that story. It expresses a broader ethical characteristic.

Nor does غُلَامٍ حَلِيمٍ itself name the boy. Identifying him is a separate question from studying this root’s vocabulary.

Shuʿayb: Hūd 11:87

In Hūd 11:87, Shuʿayb’s people object to his call and then say:

إِنَّكَ لَأَنْتَ الْحَلِيمُ الرَّشِيدُ

innaka la-anta al-ḥalīmu al-rashīdu

Translation: You are indeed the forbearing, rightly guided one!

This is spoken by Shuʿayb’s opponents rather than presented as a direct divine account of his character.

They first ask whether his prayer commands him that they abandon their ancestors’ objects of worship or refrain from doing whatever they wish with their property.

In this setting, “You are the forbearing, rightly guided one” may have a mocking tone.

Al-Ṭabarī and several early commentators explain it as mockery. Another reading also exists: they may be invoking Shuʿayb’s recognized forbearance and judgement to portray his opposition to their customs as unexpected.

The readings differ in tone, without changing the lexical meaning of حَلِيم.

Even if spoken mockingly, the word still means forbearing and judicious; the speaker uses it ironically.

الْحَلِيم should consequently not be translated directly as “ignorant” or “foolish.” Irony belongs in the contextual explanation, rather than replacing the word’s meaning with its opposite.

Even a positive description can thus be used mockingly in human speech. Identifying tone is separate from establishing lexical meaning.

Al-Ṭabarī, Hūd 11:87: mockery and the meaning of ḥalīm

5. حَلِيم Applied to The God: Power with Deliberation and Judgement

Eleven of the fifteen Qur’anic occurrences of حَلِيم concern The God.

The description appears alongside names and attributes such as غَفُور, عَلِيم, غَنِيّ, and شَكُور.

In human usage, forbearance concerns restraining impulsive conduct and anger. Applied to The God, forbearance and not hastening to hold accountable or punish become prominent.

Human psychological characteristics should not all be attributed to The God. The Qur’an does not describe Him as ignorant, losing control, or unable to act.

Divine forbearance must therefore not mean inability to punish, approval of injustice, or definite forgiveness of every wrong.

Al-Baqarah 2:225: the heart’s responsibility and forbearance

لَا يُؤَاخِذُكُمُ اللَّهُ بِاللَّغْوِ فِي أَيْمَانِكُمْ وَلَٰكِنْ يُؤَاخِذُكُمْ بِمَا كَسَبَتْ قُلُوبُكُمْ وَاللَّهُ غَفُورٌ حَلِيمٌ

lā yuʾākhidhukumu allāhu bi-l-laghwi fī aymānikum wa-lākin yuʾākhidhukum bi-mā kasabat qulūbukum wa-allāhu ghafūrun ḥalīmun

Translation: The God does not hold you accountable for idle speech in your oaths, but holds you accountable for what your hearts have earned; and The God is Forgiving, Forbearing.

حَلِيم appears beside غَفُور in this verse.

The verse distinguishes idle speech in oaths from what human hearts have earned.

It neither denies human responsibility nor subjects every oath-like statement to identical accountability.

غَفُور concerns forgiveness, while حَلِيم concerns forbearance and not hastening in response.

These can be related in divine action while retaining distinct meanings.

Divine forbearance does not mean absence of accountability: the verse expressly affirms accountability for what hearts have earned.

“Forbearance means never holding accountable” is therefore inconsistent with this occurrence itself.

Al-Baqarah 2:235: knowledge of inward matters and forbearance

After discussing marriage and what is held within people’s hearts, Al-Baqarah 2:235 points to The God’s knowledge of their inward affairs and ends with غَفُورٌ حَلِيمٌ.

This juxtaposition shows that His awareness of inward human affairs is compatible with His forbearance.

Forbearance does not arise from ignorance of the truth.

A human may fail to respond to another’s wrongdoing through ignorance, but that situation cannot without evidence be attributed to divine forbearance.

Knowledge, warning, and forgiveness occur within the same context here.

Al-Baqarah 2:263: good speech and injury following charity

قَوْلٌ مَعْرُوفٌ وَمَغْفِرَةٌ خَيْرٌ مِنْ صَدَقَةٍ يَتْبَعُهَا أَذًى وَاللَّهُ غَنِيٌّ حَلِيمٌ

qawlun maʿrūfun wa-maghfiratun khayrun min ṣadaqatin yatbaʿuhā adhan wa-allāhu ghaniyyun ḥalīmun

Translation: Fitting speech and forgiveness are better than charity followed by hurt; and The God is Self-Sufficient, Forbearing.

حَلِيم appears alongside غَنِيّ.

The verse concerns financial assistance and human dignity. Charity accompanied by hurt is not considered better than fitting speech and forgiveness.

Divine forbearance accompanies His self-sufficiency here: He does not depend on human conduct to gain a benefit or fulfil a personal need.

حَلِيم does not imply that The God approves of hurting the needy or considers it insignificant.

The verse itself criticizes hurtful conduct.

Forbearance is thus compatible with ethical judgement and criticism of wrongdoing.

Āl ʿImrān 3:155: wrongdoing, slipping, and pardon

After mentioning a group’s conduct on a day of confrontation, Āl ʿImrān 3:155 states that The God pardoned them and then describes Him as غَفُورٌ حَلِيمٌ.

The subject is an actual event and the consequences of human conduct.

His pardon and forbearance occur together without being identical.

Pardon concerns overlooking a wrong or its consequence; forbearance concerns restraint and not hastening in response.

Forbearance can accompany pardon, but their conjunction does not establish that every instance of forbearance necessarily ends in final forgiveness.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:12 and Al-Aḥzāb 33:51: knowledge and forbearance

After stating certain inheritance provisions and the qualification concerning bequests and debts, Al-Nisāʾ 4:12 describes The God as عَلِيمٌ حَلِيمٌ.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:51, after provisions concerning the Prophet’s family relationships, mentions His knowledge of what is in hearts and ends with عَلِيمًا حَلِيمًا.

Knowledge and forbearance appear together in both passages.

Divine forbearance accompanies awareness, rather than inability to recognize circumstances or ignorance of human conduct.

حَلِيم must not, however, be identified with all the inheritance or family provisions. Those provisions emerge from independent clauses in the verses.

Al-Māʾidah 5:101: questions and forbearance

Al-Māʾidah 5:101 discusses questions whose disclosed answers could cause the addressees difficulty.

It then mentions divine pardon and describes Him as غَفُورٌ حَلِيمٌ.

Forbearance here concerns His response to human conduct and questions.

It does not establish that asking questions to discover the truth is inherently objectionable.

The kind of question, its circumstances, and the consequence warned about require separate examination. حَلِيم does not itself mean a prohibition on questioning.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:44 and Fāṭir 35:41: forbearance and the order of creation

Al-Isrāʾ 17:44 describes the glorification of the heavens, earth, and their inhabitants, states that humans do not understand their glorification, and describes The God as حَلِيمًا غَفُورًا.

Fāṭir 35:41 likewise describes Him as حَلِيمًا غَفُورًا after speaking of His sustaining the heavens and earth against passing away.

Divine forbearance thus appears beyond individual rulings or particular human faults: it also occurs in broader accounts of creation and limited human awareness.

The word حَلِيم itself must not yield a physical theory of celestial motion or the mechanism sustaining the earth.

Its meaning remains forbearance and not hastening to hold accountable. Details of creation and other divine actions are expressed through independent words.

Al-Ḥajj 22:59 and Al-Taghābun 64:17: forbearance with knowledge and appreciation

Al-Ḥajj 22:59 describes The God as لَعَلِيمٌ حَلِيمٌ. The preceding verse, Al-Ḥajj 22:58, concerns those who migrated in His cause and were then killed or died; the following verse, Al-Ḥajj 22:60, discusses responding to aggression.

Forbearance in this context must not become divine approval of injustice or a denial of the wrongdoers’ responsibility.

Al-Taghābun 64:17 discusses lending The God a good loan, multiplication of reward, and forgiveness, followed by شَكُورٌ حَلِيمٌ.

شَكُور and حَلِيم are independent descriptions. The former concerns appreciating and responding to good action here; the latter concerns forbearance.

Divine forbearance is thus compatible with justice, awareness, reward, and human responsibility.

6. Distinctions from Related Roots and Translation Proposals

ح ل م (ḥilm) and ص ب ر (ṣabr)

صَبْر concerns perseverance and steadfastness in the Qur’an, whether amid hardship and pressure or in carrying out a worthy action.

Ethical حِلْم particularly emphasizes judgement and restraint of hasty reactions, especially under anger or provocation.

Someone can be both steadfast and forbearing in one situation, maintaining a right decision while avoiding an impulsive response.

The words are nevertheless not complete synonyms.

صَبْر can concern continuing an effort and holding to a course; حِلْم particularly concerns the quality of someone’s response and judgement under provocation.

Neither inherently means silence before injustice.

Study of Ṣ B R

ح ل م (ḥilm) and ع ق ل (ʿaql)

عَقْل concerns understanding, discernment, and assessment.

Ethical حِلْم connects with reason; in Al-Ṭūr 52:32, أَحْلَام means minds or powers of reason.

Possessing the ability to understand differs from using it properly.

Someone may understand something yet act against his considered judgement through anger, prejudice, or personal interest.

Forbearance can assist restraint and sound judgement here, without itself meaning unrestricted knowledge or reasoning.

Study of ʿ Q L

ح ل م (ḥilm) and أ ن ي (anāh)

أَنَاة concerns deliberation and avoiding haste in conduct.

حِلْم likewise connects with avoiding rashness, while its human usage also involves reason, forbearance, and restraint of anger.

Deliberation can indicate forbearance, but not every delay does so.

Someone may refrain from action through fear, incapacity, or indecision. Such inaction cannot be called forbearance without examination.

The quality of the decision and response matters, rather than merely the time elapsed before acting.

Study of ʾ N Y

ح ل م (ḥilm) and ك ظ م (kaẓm)

كَظْم concerns containing and restraining something, including anger.

Āl ʿImrān 3:134 mentions الْكَاظِمِينَ الْغَيْظَ.

This concept is close to forbearance while remaining distinct.

كَظْم can emphasize containing anger at a particular moment; established ethical حِلْم expresses a broader judgement and restraint in conduct.

Someone may contain anger while still contemplating rash revenge; forbearance can involve assessing the situation judiciously and choosing an appropriate response.

This is a tendency in usage, rather than an absolute rule for every occurrence of the roots.

ح ل م (ḥilm) and غ ف ر (ghafara)

غ ف ر concerns forgiveness and covering or overlooking wrongdoing.

Applied to The God, حِلْم concerns forbearance and not hastening to hold accountable.

These descriptions repeatedly occur together in the Qur’an.

Their frequent conjunction does not make them synonymous.

Accountability may be deferred in a situation without that deferral establishing final forgiveness of the wrong.

“Forgiving, Forbearing” therefore preserves the independent meanings of غَفُورٌ حَلِيمٌ.

Study of GH F R

ح ل م (ḥulm) and ر أ ي (ruʾyā)

An established use of رُؤْيَا denotes what someone sees in sleep.

حُلْم also occurs in this semantic field.

In Yūsuf 12:44, however, أَضْغَاث is added to dreams, expressing the speakers’ judgement of their confusion.

A dream in Joseph’s story and other verses can also be interpreted or fulfilled.

This difference does not establish that رُؤْيَا is always true and حُلْم always false.

The Arabic words overlap, and a dream’s validity must be judged from the evidence in its particular context.

Study of R ʾ Y

ح ل م (ḥulum) and ب ل غ (bulūgh)

ب ل غ denotes reaching a limit or stage.

حُلُم identifies puberty in بُلُوغُ الْحُلُمِ.

Al-Nūr 24:58 and Al-Nūr 24:59 place the verb بَلَغَ and noun حُلُم together.

بُلُوغ does not itself mean dreaming or sexual maturity: الْحُلُم specifies the stage being reached.

بَلَغَ الْحُلُمَ must therefore be understood as a combination, rather than translated as “he dreamed.”

Study of B L GH

ح ل م (ḥilm) and س ف ه (safah)

سَفَه can concern foolishness, unsound judgement, and lack of sense.

Arabic contrasts حِلْم with rash and foolish behaviour.

Early poetry also attests this opposition.

It does not follow that everyone who errs on one question is wholly foolish, or that acting forbearingly once makes someone judicious in everything.

Ethical descriptions require consideration of a person’s knowledge, intention, conduct, and actual circumstances.

Translation proposals for Forqan

For أَضْغَاثُ أَحْلَامٍ in Yūsuf 12:44 and Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:5, “confused dreams” or “jumbled dreams” is appropriate. Confusion comes from أَضْغَاث; أَحْلَام itself denotes dreams.

For بِتَأْوِيلِ الْأَحْلَامِ in Yūsuf 12:44, “about interpreting dreams” is precise and natural.

For أَحْلَامُهُمْ in Al-Ṭūr 52:32, “their minds” or “their powers of reason” is appropriate. “Their dreams” would be incorrect here.

In Al-Nūr 24:58 and Al-Nūr 24:59, الْحُلُمَ is “puberty.” Commentary may explain its connection with احْتِلَام, but the basic translation should not confine puberty to experiencing a particular sexual dream.

For حَلِيم applied to a person, “forbearing” is the basic equivalent. “Judicious and forbearing” can be used in explanation where appropriate.

حَلِيم describes Abraham as forbearing in Hūd 11:75 and Al-Tawbah 9:114.

غُلَامٍ حَلِيمٍ in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:101 is likewise “a forbearing boy.”

الْحَلِيمُ الرَّشِيدُ in the speech of Shuʿayb’s people in Hūd 11:87 is best rendered “forbearing, rightly guided,” explaining the possible mocking tone through context.

“Forbearing” also suits حَلِيم applied to The God. Commentary can explain His not hastening to hold accountable or punish, without implying incapacity, ignorance, or approval of wrongdoing.

In غَفُورٌ حَلِيمٌ, عَلِيمٌ حَلِيمٌ, غَنِيٌّ حَلِيمٌ, and شَكُورٌ حَلِيمٌ, each description should retain its independent meaning.

Conclusion

The Arabic root ح ل م has several semantic branches that must not all be reduced to one artificial definition.

حُلْم denotes what is seen in sleep. Associated with this field are احْتِلَام for a sexual experience during sleep and بُلُوغُ الْحُلُمِ for reaching puberty.

Another branch, حِلْم, concerns forbearance, reason, and judgement. حَلِيم describes someone who retains control of his response under anger, provocation, or disagreeable treatment and avoids rash action.

Arabic also records damage to a hide through small creatures and certain names concerning bodies and animals. These branches do not occur in the Qur’an, and their historical relationship with dreams and forbearance is not definitively established.

Three fields stand out in the Qur’an: dreaming, puberty, and reason and forbearance.

أَحْلَام means dreams in Yūsuf 12:44 and Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:5, but reason in Al-Ṭūr 52:32; الْحُلُم denotes puberty in Al-Nūr 24:58 and Al-Nūr 24:59.

Forbearance and considered conduct are prominent in حَلِيم. Abraham is praised with this description, a boy possessing it is announced to him, and Shuʿayb’s people also use it in addressing him, albeit possibly mockingly.

Applied to The God, حَلِيم concerns forbearance with power and knowledge. It accompanies forgiveness, knowledge, self-sufficiency, and appreciation of good action without being synonymous with them.

Brief root meaning: Dreams and visions in sleep; puberty in بُلُوغُ الْحُلُمِ; reason, forbearance, and self-restraint against rashness. The ethical branch centres on avoiding rashness and preserving considered judgement, especially under anger and provocation. This can accompany reason, deliberation, and forbearance without implying inability to act or overlooking every wrong.

The dream branch retains its independent meaning. Qur’anic أَحْلَام sometimes denotes dreams and sometimes reason: identical form must not produce identical translation in every verse.

For Forqan, “dream,” “puberty,” “reason,” and “forbearing” are the principal equivalents for this root’s Qur’anic uses. Vowels, syntax, and context should determine the choice.

One lexical family can thus contain several fields without their all developing from a single image. Careful root study identifies those fields and explains each derivative in its own setting.
