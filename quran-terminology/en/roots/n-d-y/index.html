1. Introducing the root and its semantic field

The Arabic root N D W concerns people gathering, an assembly or gathering place, calling, and directing speech to an addressee. Recognized forms include nādī, nadiyy, nadwah, nidāʾ, nādā, and tanādā.

Social nādī denotes an assembly or place where people gather. Nidāʾ is calling or addressing someone, usually by voice. Tanādī can concern people calling one another or mutual invitation.

These meanings are related but distinct. Gathering, summoning someone to attend, addressing someone far away, and privately calling upon one’s Lord are different activities, all relevant to this family’s Qur’anic uses.

The morphological index places N D W at fifty-three Qur’anic word positions. Nādā and its inflected forms are most frequent, occurring forty-four times. Nominal and participial forms include nidāʾ, munādī, nādī, and nadiyy. Tanādaw and al-tanād concern mutual calling.

Qur’anic counts and forms: Quranic Arabic Corpus

The family has three prominent Qur’anic fields: calling and addressing, assemblies and gathering places, and mutual calling.

One further lexical point matters from the outset. Arabic sources often treat N D W and N D Y together because their final radicals are weak and their forms close. The wider family also includes nadā, moisture or dew, and, in another use, generosity. These meanings must not be imposed on Qur’anic nidāʾ and nādī without evidence.

2. The semantic core and important forms

Gathering and calling

Ibn Fāris’s Maqāyīs al-Lughah identifies gathering and moisture as two fields within this family.

He describes the first as follows:

النَّادِي وَالنَّدِيُّ: الْمَجْلِسُ، يَنْدُو الْقَوْمُ حَوَالَيْهِ

al-nādī wa-al-nadiyyu: al-majlisu, yandū al-qawmu ḥawālayhi

“Al-nādī and al-nadiyy mean an assembly; the people gather around it.”

He then mentions gathering in Dār al-Nadwah, connecting its name with people assembling for discussion and consultation.

The second field is al-nadā, moisture or wetness. Arabic also associates this field with other meanings, including generosity and giving.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: gathering and moisture

This division prevents reducing the whole family to a single invented image.

People gather in an assembly, while calling summons or addresses someone. The linguistic connection is clear, but nādā does not itself mean sitting in an assembly except in a specifically attested usage.

Nor is dew nadā the direct meaning of nidāʾ. Moisture, generosity, and qualities of the voice appearing together in lexical sources do not alone establish that they originally shared one physical image.

Important Arabic forms

Nadā yandū, in the gathering field, concerns people assembling or being brought together in an assembly. Early usage also records other senses involving livestock movement.

Nādā yunādī is Form III, prominently meaning calling, summoning, or addressing someone in the Qur’an. Some early lexical uses also concern sitting together, consultation, or competing in boasts.

Nidāʾ is a verbal noun of nādā: a call, summons, or calling out.

Munādin and munādī are active-participial forms describing a caller or summoner. Their Qur’anic surface forms also vary with grammatical role and inflection.

Form VI tanādā yatanādā means calling one another or mutual invitation. Some lexical uses also record gathering in an assembly.

Tanādin is its verbal noun and can denote mutual calling.

Nādin or nādī denotes an assembly or gathering place. Context can also make it refer to the assembly’s people.

Nadiyy denotes an assembly, meeting place, or assembled group. Nadiyyan occurs in the Qur’an.

Nadwah means a gathering or assembly, especially for discussion and consultation.

Muntadā likewise names a place for gathering and discussion. It is recognized Arabic, but this form does not occur in the Qur’an.

Nadā in the moisture field means dew, wetness, and sometimes rain. Applied to people, it can express generosity.

Adjectival nadiyy can also mean moist or damp. Its spelling matching nadiyy, “assembly,” does not give it the same use in every sentence.

Andā can mean more generous, and, of a voice, more far-reaching or pleasing.

The Qur’an most often uses nādā. Nādī and nadiyy as assemblies, and tanādī as mutual calling, also occur.

The physical and social image

Imagine an assembly where a family or group gathers. Arabic can call the meeting place and those attending al-nādī or al-nadiyy.

Nādī then need not name only a building; it may also name the assembled people.

Another image is someone attracting an addressee’s attention, calling them and directing speech or a message toward them.

The call might seek attendance or help, give a warning, or convey news. Nādā alone does not determine its purpose.

Nor must every nidāʾ become a loud shout. The Qur’an describes Zechariah’s private call. Volume and distance can matter contextually without being fixed features of every instance.

3. Early Arabic usage

An assembly and a numerous people: Bishr ibn Abī Khāzim

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Bishr ibn Abī Khāzim reads:

وَمَا يَنْدُوهُمُ النَّادِي وَلَكِنْ
بِكُلِّ مَحَلَّةٍ مِنْهُمْ فِئَامُ

wa-mā yandūhumu al-nādī wa-lākin / bi-kulli maḥallatin minhum fiʾāmu

“One assembly cannot contain them all; in every locality there are great groups of them.”

The poet describes a people’s great numbers.

Al-nādī is the gathering place; yandūhum here concerns containing or accommodating them.

The assembly is not vocally summoning them: one meeting place is insufficient for everyone.

Fiʾām likewise denotes large groups of people.

Transmitted in Bishr’s collection and lexical commentaries, the verse shows nādī as a real gathering place before its modern terminological uses.

Bishr’s transmitted poem with al-Ḍabbī’s and al-Ṭūsī’s commentary

Gathering and calling one another

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Muraqqish al-Akbar reads:

وَالْعَدْوَ بَيْنَ الْمَجْلِسَيْنِ إِذَا
وَلَّى الْعَشِيُّ وَقَدْ تَنَادَى الْعَمُّ

wa-al-ʿadwa bayna al-majlisayni idhā / wallā al-ʿashiyyu wa-qad tanādā al-ʿammu

“And hurrying between the two assemblies, as evening drew to its close and the people had gathered.” Lexical explanations take al-ʿamm here as a group of people; it need not be restricted to relatives.

Tanādā appears in a setting of gathering and interaction.

Lexical sources explain tanādā in this witness as gathering and sitting together. Calling can be associated with such an assembly, but this use’s gathering sense need not be restricted to an exchange of vocal calls.

Lexical transmissions also give the second half as āda al-ʿashiyyu wa-tanādā al-ʿammu. Wallā al-ʿashiyyu wa-qad tanādā al-ʿammu appears in other transmissions. These variants must remain visible rather than making one reading establish a conclusive sequence or details of the gathering.

Sharḥ Abyāt Mughnī al-Labīb: al-Muraqqish and the assemblies

Calling in boastful rivalry and conversation

Early Arabic nādā al-rajula sometimes means sitting with someone in an assembly, and can also concern competing in boasts.

For example, a verse by al-Aʿshā reads:

فَتًى لَوْ يُنَادِي الشَّمْسَ أَلْقَتْ قِنَاعَهَا
أَوِ الْقَمَرَ السَّارِي لَأَلْقَى الْقَلَائِدَا

fatan law yunādī al-shamsa alqat qināʿahā / awi al-qamara al-sārī la-alqā al-qalāʾidā

“A youth who, if he vied with the sun, would make it lower its veil; and if with the night-traveling moon, would make it cast off its necklaces.”

The poet is not literally summoning the sun and moon to hear a message. Exaggerated praise depicts a young man’s superiority through rivalry with them.

Nādā can thus extend beyond a vocal summons in early usage. Boastful rivalry should nevertheless not become the default Qur’anic translation without contextual support. Lisān al-ʿArab transmits al-qalāʾidā and notes the variant al-maqālidā; the present witness retains the first reading.

Lisān al-ʿArab: boastful rivalry and al-Aʿshā’s readings

Calling, volume, and disclosure

Arabic andā ṣawtan describes a more far-reaching or pleasing voice.

Some lexical uses give nādā bi-sirrihi for revealing a secret.

Calling here relates to bringing speech out of concealment and conveying it to an addressee.

Disclosure differs from shouting: someone may reveal a secret to one person without raising their voice.

Nādā can also describe something appearing, such as vegetation or a marker on a route becoming visible.

These uses show the language’s range without establishing an unsupported common historical stage.

Moisture, dew, and generosity

Arabic nadā denotes moisture settling on earth, plants, or other things.

Nadiyy al-kaff describes generosity. The image of a moist hand connects with giving and benefits reaching others.

Ibn Fāris nevertheless separates moisture from gathering. Generosity alone therefore cannot make Qur’anic nidāʾ mean giving benefit or spreading mercy.

These senses belong in the wider lexical family, while the Qur’anic occurrences mainly concern calling, addressing, and assemblies.

4. Qur’anic uses of the root

A. Calling and prayer: addressing the Lord

An important Qur’anic use of nādā is a person addressing their Lord.

Zechariah’s story states:

إِذْ نَادَىٰ رَبَّهُ نِدَاءً خَفِيًّا

idh nādā rabbahu nidāʾan khafiyyan

“When he called upon his Lord with a private call.”

Maryam 19:3

Nādā and nidāʾan share a family. The accusative verbal noun emphasizes the act of calling; khafiyyan describes its manner.

This verse is fundamental evidence that a call need not be a loud shout.

If nidāʾ invariably meant an overt, loud shout, nidāʾan khafiyyan would require further explanation. Qur’anic calling can be private and quiet.

Zechariah next describes bodily weakness, old age, and his request. Calling here is prayerful address to The God.

N D W and D ʿ W must nevertheless remain distinct. Prayer can be made through a call, while the call’s independent meaning is summoning or addressing.

Jonah’s story likewise states:

وَذَا النُّونِ إِذْ ذَهَبَ مُغَاضِبًا فَظَنَّ أَنْ لَنْ نَقْدِرَ عَلَيْهِ فَنَادَىٰ فِي الظُّلُمَاتِ أَنْ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا أَنْتَ سُبْحَانَكَ إِنِّي كُنْتُ مِنَ الظَّالِمِينَ

wa-dhā al-nūni idh dhahaba mughāḍiban fa-ẓanna an lan naqdira ʿalayhi fa-nādā fī al-ẓulumāti an lā ilāha illā anta subḥānaka innī kuntu mina al-ẓālimīna

“And the man of the fish, when he went away in anger and supposed We would not constrain him; then he called in the darkness: There is no deity but You. Glory be to You; I have been among the wrongdoers.”

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:87

Fa-nādā accompanies fī al-ẓulumāti, “in the darkness.”

The darkness alone does not establish volume. The following statement identifies the call’s content.

Job calls upon his Lord and describes his affliction in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:83; Zechariah calls upon Him again in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:89. Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:76 and Al-Ṣāffāt 37:75 describe Noah’s call and the divine response.

Calling can thus convey a request, appeal for help, or direct address to The God.

Nādā itself does not mean asking for help: the ensuing speech identifies the request or acknowledgment.

B. Calls between people: address, warning, and invitation

Noah’s story states:

وَهِيَ تَجْرِي بِهِمْ فِي مَوْجٍ كَالْجِبَالِ وَنَادَىٰ نُوحٌ ابْنَهُ وَكَانَ فِي مَعْزِلٍ يَا بُنَيَّ ارْكَبْ مَعَنَا وَلَا تَكُنْ مَعَ الْكَافِرِينَ

wa-hiya tajrī bihim fī mawjin ka-al-jibāli wa-nādā nūḥun ibnahu wa-kāna fī maʿzilin yā bunayya irkab maʿanā wa-lā takun maʿa al-kāfirīna

“It carried them through waves like mountains, and Noah called his son, who was apart: My son, board with us, and do not be with the disbelievers.”

Hūd 11:42

Nādā is transitive here, with ibnahu as its object.

Wa-kāna fī maʿzilin places Noah’s son apart from him. The call fits addressing someone not beside the speaker.

Its content is explicit: Noah calls his son to board the ship.

This invitation does not come from N D W alone. The verb expresses calling, and irkab maʿanā identifies the invitation’s subject.

Later, in Hūd 11:45, Noah calls upon his Lord. One verb thus appears in a father’s address to his son and a servant’s address to his Lord.

Calling remains common to both, while addressee and content differ.

C. Calling and social conduct: respecting the addressee

Al-Ḥujurāt states:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ يُنَادُونَكَ مِنْ وَرَاءِ الْحُجُرَاتِ أَكْثَرُهُمْ لَا يَعْقِلُونَ

inna alladhīna yunādūnaka min warāʾi al-ḥujurāti aktharuhum lā yaʿqilūna

“Those who call you from behind the chambers: most of them do not reason.”

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:4

Yunādūnaka is a Form III imperfect verb, with -ka as its object.

Min warāʾi al-ḥujurāti identifies the callers’ location.

The verse also criticizes their conduct in calling the Messenger from behind the chambers.

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:5 adds that waiting until he came out to them would have been better.

The connection concerns impatience and failing to respect the addressee’s circumstances.

Nādā itself therefore does not mean discourtesy: the Qur’an also uses it for prophets, angels, and divine address.

Calling is a communicative act whose ethical value depends on manner, addressee, purpose, and circumstances.

The criticism concerns calling the Messenger without appropriate respect and timing, rather than calling as a linguistic act.

D. The public call to ṣalāh

Al-Māʾidah states:

وَإِذَا نَادَيْتُمْ إِلَى الصَّلَاةِ اتَّخَذُوهَا هُزُوًا وَلَعِبًا ۚ ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمْ قَوْمٌ لَا يَعْقِلُونَ

wa-idhā nādaytum ilā al-ṣalāti ittakhadhūhā huzuwan wa-laʿiban; dhālika bi-annahum qawmun lā yaʿqilūna

“When you call to ṣalāh, they take it as mockery and play; that is because they are people who do not reason.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:58

Nādaytum is a Form III perfect verb; ilā al-ṣalāti gives the call’s destination or subject.

Calling here invites participation in a shared act, beyond addressing one identified person.

Al-Jumuʿah also states:

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِذَا نُودِيَ لِلصَّلَاةِ مِنْ يَوْمِ الْجُمُعَةِ فَاسْعَوْا إِلَىٰ ذِكْرِ اللَّهِ وَذَرُوا الْبَيْعَ ۚ ذَٰلِكُمْ خَيْرٌ لَكُمْ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ تَعْلَمُونَ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū idhā nūdiya li-l-ṣalāti min yawmi al-jumuʿati fa-sʿaw ilā dhikri Allāhi wa-dharū al-bayʿa; dhālikum khayrun lakum in kuntum taʿlamūna

“O you who believe, when the call to ṣalāh is given on Friday, proceed to remembrance of The God and leave trade; that is better for you, if you know.”

Al-Jumuʿah 62:9

Nūdiya is a passive perfect of nādā.

Li-l-ṣalāti identifies its subject; min yawmi al-jumuʿati supplies its temporal setting.

Lexically, this is an announcement and public summons.

Nūdiya itself specifies neither the number of calls nor their exact wording, the caller’s name, or the manner of performance.

Those details must not be added to the root’s meaning.

Nidāʾ and ṣalāh are also distinct concepts: the call invites people to ṣalāh without itself meaning ṣalāh.

N D W has a communicative and social role here: conveying a summons directing its audience to a shared act.

E. A caller and the message of faith

Āl ʿImrān states:

رَبَّنَا إِنَّنَا سَمِعْنَا مُنَادِيًا يُنَادِي لِلْإِيمَانِ أَنْ آمِنُوا بِرَبِّكُمْ فَآمَنَّا ۚ رَبَّنَا فَاغْفِرْ لَنَا ذُنُوبَنَا وَكَفِّرْ عَنَّا سَيِّئَاتِنَا وَتَوَفَّنَا مَعَ الْأَبْرَارِ

rabbanā innanā samiʿnā munādiyan yunādī li-l-īmāni an āminū bi-rabbikum fa-āmannā; rabbanā fa-ghfir lanā dhunūbanā wa-kaffir ʿannā sayyiʾātinā wa-tawaffanā maʿa al-abrāri

“Our Lord, we heard a caller calling to faith: Believe in your Lord; so we believed. Our Lord, forgive our sins, remove our misdeeds, and take us in death with the righteous.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:193

Munādiyan is an active participle; yunādī an imperfect verb from the same root.

First comes hearing a caller, then the act and content of calling.

Li-l-īmāni gives its direction, and an āminū bi-rabbikum makes its content explicit.

Faith is therefore not part of munādī’s lexical meaning. A caller summons; the summons may concern faith or something else.

The call’s value is identified through its content here.

Munādī alone does not conclusively identify the historical individual or means of conveying the message. The verse emphasizes hearing the call to faith and the believers’ positive response.

F. Nidāʾ and duʿāʾ: hearing sound without understanding the message

Al-Baqarah states:

وَمَثَلُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا كَمَثَلِ الَّذِي يَنْعِقُ بِمَا لَا يَسْمَعُ إِلَّا دُعَاءً وَنِدَاءً ۚ صُمٌّ بُكْمٌ عُمْيٌ فَهُمْ لَا يَعْقِلُونَ

wa-mathalu alladhīna kafarū ka-mathali alladhī yanʿiqu bi-mā lā yasmaʿu illā duʿāʾan wa-nidāʾan; ṣummun bukmun ʿumyun fa-hum lā yaʿqilūna

“The example of those who disbelieve is like someone calling to what hears only a summons and a call: deaf, mute, blind, so they do not reason.”

Al-Baqarah 2:171

Duʿāʾan and nidāʾan appear together.

Both concern calling and sound, while remaining distinct words.

Yanʿiqu belongs to N ʿ Q and concerns raising a call, especially when addressing livestock.

Study of N ʿ Q

Hearing sound stands against understanding and reasoning in the example. Its recipient receives sound without comprehension leading to recognition of the intended meaning.

Sound reaching an ear thus differs from understanding a message.

Nidāʾ itself is not meaningless sound. Here, failure to understand belongs to the recipient rather than all calls lacking clear meaning.

Nor does juxtaposing duʿāʾ and nidāʾ establish an absolute near-versus-far distinction. Their difference requires examination across usages.

G. Calling in conversations of the hereafter

Nādā appears repeatedly in resurrection scenes.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:44, the people of the Garden call to the people of the Fire and ask about their Lord’s promise being fulfilled.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:50, the people of the Fire call to the people of the Garden, asking for water or some provision they received.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:46 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:48 also involve calls connected with the people of the Heights.

Al-Zukhruf 43:77 describes those punished calling to Mālik; Al-Ḥadīd 57:14 depicts one group calling to another in the hereafter.

Qur’anic calling thus extends beyond worldly life.

The verb’s use in the hereafter does not establish physical distances, spatial structures, or mechanisms of sound transmission in the unseen.

Nādā expresses an address or summons between participants; its manner of realization in the hereafter exceeds the root’s lexical meaning.

Qāf states:

وَاسْتَمِعْ يَوْمَ يُنَادِ الْمُنَادِ مِنْ مَكَانٍ قَرِيبٍ

wa-istamiʿ yawma yunādi al-munādi min makānin qarībin

“Listen for the day when the caller calls from a nearby place.”

Qāf 50:41

Al-munādi is an active participle; yunādi is an imperfect verb.

Min makānin qarībin identifies the call’s location in the verse.

Distance can thus be specified in a call’s description, but proximity comes from makānin qarībin rather than N D W itself.

Fuṣṣilat 41:44, conversely, describes people as though called from a distant place, in the context of failing to receive the message properly.

Calling may thus accompany distance or proximity without being restricted to either.

H. Divine calling and addressing prophets

The Qur’an repeatedly describes divine calls to prophets.

In Ṭā Hā 20:11, Moses is called when he approaches the fire.

Al-Naml 27:8 and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:30 describe the same event differently.

In Maryam 19:52, The God describes calling Moses from the side of the Mount and bringing him near for private conversation.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:104 likewise addresses Abraham through a call.

Calling can therefore also describe divine address.

This does not make divine nidāʾ necessarily identical to human sound production through bodily organs, nor justify reducing divine address to an ordinary psychological state.

The verses state calling, address, and reception of a message; specifying its manner exceeds nādā’s lexical meaning.

Nādaynāhu and najiyyan occur together in Maryam 19:52 but belong to separate roots. Najiyyan belongs to N J W and concerns close, private conversation. Proximity in the verse does not make them share a root.

5. Nādī and nadiyy: assemblies, social standing, and those gathered

A. Wa-aḥsanu nadiyyan: an assembly and social standing

Maryam states:

وَإِذَا تُتْلَىٰ عَلَيْهِمْ آيَاتُنَا بَيِّنَاتٍ قَالَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا لِلَّذِينَ آمَنُوا أَيُّ الْفَرِيقَيْنِ خَيْرٌ مَقَامًا وَأَحْسَنُ نَدِيًّا

wa-idhā tutlā ʿalayhim āyātunā bayyinātin qāla alladhīna kafarū li-alladhīna āmanū ayyu al-farīqayni khayrun maqāman wa-aḥsanu nadiyyan

“When Our clear signs are recited to them, those who disbelieve say to those who believe: Which of the two groups has the better position and finer assembly?”

Maryam 19:73

Nadiyyan concerns an assembly or gathering.

Maqāman concerns position or standing. Together in the question, they compare the groups’ social status and circumstances.

Aḥsanu nadiyyan means having a finer assembly, rather than a louder call or more beautiful voice.

Nadiyy here names an assembly rather than describing a human voice.

A grander assembly or higher social position can support a claim of superiority, without the claim itself establishing truth.

Maryam 19:74 mentions earlier generations with better furnishings and outward appearance who were destroyed. The continuation thus challenges the speakers’ valuation by visible standing.

B. Fī nādīkumu al-munkara: wrongdoing in the assembly

In Al-ʿAnkabūt, Lot addresses his people:

أَئِنَّكُمْ لَتَأْتُونَ الرِّجَالَ وَتَقْطَعُونَ السَّبِيلَ وَتَأْتُونَ فِي نَادِيكُمُ الْمُنْكَرَ ۖ فَمَا كَانَ جَوَابَ قَوْمِهِ إِلَّا أَنْ قَالُوا ائْتِنَا بِعَذَابِ اللَّهِ إِنْ كُنْتَ مِنَ الصَّادِقِينَ

a-innakum la-taʾtūna al-rijāla wa-taqṭaʿūna al-sabīla wa-taʾtūna fī nādīkumu al-munkara; fa-mā kāna jawāba qawmihi illā an qālū iʾtinā bi-ʿadhābi Allāhi in kunta mina al-ṣādiqīna

“Do you approach men, cut off the road, and commit wrongdoing in your assembly? His people’s only response was: Bring us The God’s punishment, if you are truthful.”

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:29

Nādīkumu refers to the people’s assembly or gathering place.

Al-munkara is the object of taʾtūna and names the objectionable act.

Its objectionable character comes from al-munkar, not nādī itself.

Nādī is not inherently an assembly of corruption or a place of sin; it can also describe an ordinary gathering.

The verse criticizes those present and their conduct.

Early commentaries identify various instances of this wrongdoing, while the expression alone names no single specific act.

An independently transmitted story should therefore not replace the expression’s direct meaning without supporting evidence.

C. Fa-l-yadʿu nādiyahu: assembly members and social support

Al-ʿAlaq states:

فَلْيَدْعُ نَادِيَهُ

fa-l-yadʿu nādiyahu

“Then let him summon his assembly’s people.”

Al-ʿAlaq 96:17

سَنَدْعُ الزَّبَانِيَةَ

sa-nadʿu al-zabāniyata

“We shall summon the zabāniyah.”

Al-ʿAlaq 96:18

Nādiyahu is the object of fa-l-yadʿu in the first verse.

Fa-l-yadʿu belongs to D ʿ W and means summoning; nādiyahu belongs to N D W and concerns the assembly’s members or the person’s associated group.

This morphological and semantic distinction must remain.

The person is not merely being told to summon a building. The assembly is named through its members.

Arabic recognizes using a gathering place’s name for its people.

The sūrah concerns someone preventing a servant from ṣalāh, then tells him to summon his assembly.

Nādī thus concerns his social backing: those he might summon for help and support.

It does not inherently name a military force or fighting group; such a role would come from the situation.

In the three Qur’anic assembly uses, nadiyy and nādī concern an assembly, its members, and social position rather than calling itself.

6. Tanādī: calling one another

Fa-tanādaw muṣbiḥīn: mutual calling at daybreak

The garden owners’ story states:

فَتَنَادَوْا مُصْبِحِينَ

fa-tanādaw muṣbiḥīna

“Then they called one another at daybreak.”

Al-Qalam 68:21

Tanādaw is a Form VI perfect verb.

Nādā can describe one person calling another, while tanādaw here emphasizes mutual calling within the group.

Al-Qalam 68:22 supplies the content: they tell each other to go early to their field to harvest.

Tanādaw names the calling; the following speech identifies traveling and harvesting.

The continuation describes their intention to exclude the needy.

Tanādī must nevertheless not be restricted to conspiracy or wrongdoing. This story uses gathering and mutual calling for a wrongful purpose without fixing the verb’s ethical value.

Yawma al-tanādi: the day of mutual calls

Ghāfir states:

وَيَا قَوْمِ إِنِّي أَخَافُ عَلَيْكُمْ يَوْمَ التَّنَادِ

wa-yā qawmi innī akhāfu ʿalaykum yawma al-tanādi

“My people, I fear for you the day of mutual calling.”

Ghāfir 40:32

Al-tanādi characterizes a day involving reciprocal calls.

Afterlife scenes repeatedly include calls, such as between the people of the Garden and Fire in Al-Aʿrāf 7:44 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:50.

Yawmu al-tanādi thus has clear Qur’anic connections with resurrection and its calls.

The whole day must not be reduced to one specific call: the Qur’an describes divine address, people’s calls, and different groups’ summonses.

Al-tanād in the familiar reading relates to the Form VI verbal noun; deletion of its final yāʾ is recognized in the verse’s spelling and transmitted form.

Some reading reports also give a doubled dāl, connected with N D D and fleeing. That reading requires separate etymological examination; similarity of spelling does not merge calling tanād with fleeing nadda.

Al-Qurṭubī: al-tanād and the reported doubled-dāl reading

7. Distinctions from related roots

N D W (nidāʾ) and D ʿ W (duʿāʾ)

Study of D ʿ W

Nidāʾ and duʿāʾ approach each other in calling.

Nidāʾ usually emphasizes directing address or a summons to someone. Duʿāʾ can also concern requesting, seeking, and summoning.

They appear together in Al-Baqarah 2:171. Zechariah calls his Lord in Maryam 19:3 and then expresses his request.

Prayer and calling can thus coincide in one act.

Their difference must not become an absolute rule that duʿāʾ is always quiet and nidāʾ always loud: nidāʾan khafiyyan limits such a division.

Fa-l-yadʿu in Al-ʿAlaq 96:17 belongs to D ʿ W, while its object nādiyahu belongs to this study’s family. Their proximity does not merge the roots.

N D W (nādā) and Ṣ Y Ḥ (ṣāḥa)

Study of Ṣ Y Ḥ

Arabic ṣiyāḥ concerns shouting and loud cries.

A nidāʾ can also be loud, especially with a distant addressee or public summons.

Calling itself is nevertheless not always shouting.

Maryam 19:3 describes a private call. Ṣāḥa and nādā therefore cannot always share a translation.

“Shouted” can fit particular contexts, while “called” or “summoned” is the broader, more cautious equivalent.

N D W (nādā) and D ʿ W (daʿā), compared with A DH N (adhdhana)

Study of A DH N

Adhdhana belongs to A DH N and in some uses means announcing or making known.

Calling can be a means of announcement, and announcing may use a vocal summons.

The call to ṣalāh in Al-Jumuʿah 62:9, for example, informs people of the devotional summons.

Calling and announcing nevertheless differ.

Calling emphasizes address or summons; announcing emphasizes conveying awareness and making news known.

The present religious use of adhān must therefore not be substituted for every Qur’anic form of nādā without examination.

N D W (nādā) and J H R (jahara)

Study of J H R

Jahr concerns being overt and expressing something openly or loudly.

Nādā concerns calling or addressing someone.

A call may involve jahr, without always doing so.

Nidāʾan khafiyyan shows that a call can be private.

Calling alone therefore does not lexically specify how overt or loud it is.

N D W (nādī) and J M ʿ (jamʿ)

Study of J M ʿ

Jamʿ concerns bringing people or things together.

Social nādī concerns an assembly or meeting place.

The concepts are close, but nādī does not name every arbitrary collection of objects.

Qur’anic nādī concerns human gatherings and their social environment.

Jamʿ can concern people, property, objects, and other matters.

Nādī is thus a particular form of human assembly rather than an equivalent of every use of gathering.

N D W (nādī) and J L S (jalasa)

Study of J L S

Jalasa concerns sitting.

Nādī names an assembly or meeting place.

People may sit and talk there, while nādī itself does not mean sitting.

Fa-l-yadʿu nādiyahu refers to assembly members, not their act of sitting.

The social place, attending it, and those attending must therefore be distinguished.

N D W (nādā) and N J W (najwā)

Study of N J W

Najwā belongs to N J W and concerns private or whispered conversation.

Nidāʾ commonly concerns calling or address.

They can approach each other in some situations without being lexically identical.

The God calls Moses and brings him near for private conversation in Maryam 19:52.

Their combination helps distinguish calling from the private character of conversation.

It does not make all calls public or all private conversations soundless.

N D W (nādī) and N D D (nidd)

Study of N D D

N D W concerns calling and assemblies; one Qur’anic field of N D D concerns a counterpart or rival.

Andād belongs to N D D and must not be assigned to N D W because their opening letters resemble each other.

Arabic nadda can also mean fleeing or bolting.

This matters for reports of the reading yawmu al-tanād: the exact form and root must be identified before interpreting it.

8. Morphological and structural notes

Nādā: a Form III verb

Nādā is Form III.

It sometimes takes a direct object, as in nādā Nūḥun ibnahu in Hūd 11:42.

Sometimes a preposition accompanies it, as in nādaytum ilā al-ṣalāti in Al-Māʾidah 5:58.

Sometimes it addresses The God, as in nādā rabbahu in Maryam 19:3.

Form III alone does not imply both parties necessarily call each other; one person can call while the other is simply addressed.

Form VI tanādā expresses mutual calling.

Fa-tanādaw: the Form VI plural

Fa-tanādaw in Al-Qalam 68:21 is the plural perfect of tanādā.

Its reciprocal construction expresses calling one another.

Tanādaw must not be equated on spelling alone with nādaw, the plural form of Form III nādā.

Nādaw can describe a group calling someone else, whereas tanādaw in the garden owners’ story emphasizes members calling one another.

Nūdiya: the passive verb

Nūdiya in Al-Jumuʿah 62:9 is a passive perfect verb.

Its agent is unnamed and attention falls on the calling event.

Neither the caller’s identity nor exact words can therefore be extracted from the passive alone.

The form also occurs in calls to Moses, with a setting different from public calls to ṣalāh.

Munādiyan and al-munād

Munādiyan in Āl ʿImrān 3:193 is an accusative active participle.

Al-munādi in Qāf 50:41 is a definite active participle serving as a nominative subject; its ḍammah is understood on the deleted yāʾ. The deletion relates to this verse’s spelling and reading, rather than a general rule for every definite defective noun.

Al-Jadwal fī Iʿrāb al-Qurʾān transcription: the cited verse

The forms share a derivational family; differences in spelling and inflection do not require separate lexical meanings.

Nādī and nadiyy

These nouns concern an assembly and its people.

Nadiyyan in Maryam 19:73 compares two groups’ social standing.

Nādīkumu in Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:29 follows fī and concerns the assembly’s setting.

Nādiyahu in Al-ʿAlaq 96:17 is the object of fa-l-yadʿu, with the assembly’s people intended.

Grammar and context identify these differences, without restricting nādī always to a building or always to people.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

Nādā primarily means “called” or “summoned.” “Called out to” can also suit an identified addressee.

Nādā rabbahu nidāʾan khafiyyan in Maryam 19:3 can be “He called upon his Lord with a private call.”

Wa-nādā Nūḥun ibnahu in Hūd 11:42 is naturally “And Noah called to his son.”

Nādaytum ilā al-ṣalāti in Al-Māʾidah 5:58 means “you called to ṣalāh.”

Idhā nūdiya li-l-ṣalāti in Al-Jumuʿah 62:9 can be “when the call to ṣalāh is given.”

Nidāʾ primarily means “call,” “summons,” or “calling.” “Shout” requires evidence of loudness in context.

Munādin means “caller” or “summoner.”

Tanādā naturally means “they called one another” or “called to each other.”

Fa-tanādaw muṣbiḥīna in Al-Qalam 68:21 can be “Then they called one another at daybreak.”

Yawma al-tanādi in Ghāfir 40:32 can be “the day of mutual calls” or “the day of calling one another.”

Nādī can mean “assembly,” “meeting place,” or “assembly members,” depending on context.

Fa-l-yadʿu nādiyahu in Al-ʿAlaq 96:17 can be “Then let him summon his assembly’s people.”

Nadiyy in Maryam 19:73 means “assembly”; aḥsanu nadiyyan can be “having a finer, better-appointed assembly.”

Nadwah can mean “gathering” or “assembly for discussion,” but does not itself occur in the Qur’an.

Non-Qur’anic nadā in the moisture field means “dew” or “moisture”; in the generosity field, “generosity” or “giving” is suitable.

These senses must not enter translations of Qur’anic calling and assembly words without evidence.

10. Summary

N D W concerns people gathering, assemblies, and calling. Lexical sources also record moisture and generosity within the broader N D W / N D Y family, without justifying their collapse into a single meaning.

Calling and addressing are prominent Qur’anic uses. Prophets call upon their Lord, The God addresses prophets, people call one another, and afterlife scenes describe various calls.

Calling does not always mean shouting. Zechariah’s call is private, while other calls are described as coming from near or far. Distance and volume must therefore come from the sentence.

Nādī and nadiyy show another field: assemblies, gathering places, and their members. Nadiyy concerns an assembly and social standing in Maryam 19:73; nādī supplies conduct’s social setting in Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:29 and a person’s assembly and social backing in Al-ʿAlaq 96:17.

Tanādī emphasizes mutual calling: the garden owners call one another at daybreak, and yawmu al-tanādi in Ghāfir 40:32 concerns the calls of the final day.

Calling and assembly can be related because people are summoned to gather and speak together. Yet not every call invites an assembly, nor is every nādī a place of shouting.

The brief meaning of N D W in its main Qur’anic uses is calling and addressing; in its nominal branch, an assembly or gathering of people.

Translation must keep these fields distinct and clear. The call’s subject, addressee, volume, purpose, and ethical value do not all come from the root; the sentence and context specify them.
