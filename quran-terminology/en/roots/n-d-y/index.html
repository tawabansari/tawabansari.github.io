The Root N D Y: Calling, Assemblies, Moisture and Generosity

Semantic range: calling and summoning, assemblies and gatherings, moisture and dew, generosity

N D Y is a branching Arabic word family. One branch concerns calling, conveying a voice and addressing someone; another connects with people gathering in an assembly and a shared space of conversation. A further branch concerns moisture, dew and generosity.

A morphological point matters when identifying this family: dictionaries do not always register its final consonant alike. The material appears under N D Y in some treatments and N D W in others. This relates to weak verbs and their differing inflected forms; it does not by itself establish two wholly independent semantic families.

This study retains the heading N D Y and examines the related N D W forms within the same lexical field, while preserving real distinctions between derivatives.

1. Forms and Derivatives

The Qur’anic morphological index registers this family with final w and records fifty-three occurrences in fifty verses. Its principal Qur’anic forms include:

  • نَادَى، نَادَيْنَا، نُودِيَ، يُنَادِي و يُنَادَوْنَ: Calling, summoning and being addressed.
  • نِدَاء: A call or summons.
  • الْمُنَادِ و مُنَادِيًا: A caller or summoner.
  • تَنَادَوْا و التَّنَادِ: Calling one another and reciprocal calls.
  • نَادِي و نَدِيّ: An assembly, gathering or, in some contexts, its members.

Other early Arabic forms examined within the family include nadā, nadāwah, nadwah, muntadā, intadā and andā. Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an.

Nidāʾ concerns the act of calling; nadiyy and nādī concern people gathering, their gathering place or the assembled body. Sharing a root does not give these words identical meanings.

2. Semantic Branches and Early Arabic Uses

A. Calling, Voice and Address

In familiar Arabic usage, nādā means calling to someone, summoning them or conveying speech to an addressee.

Ordinary calling often involves raising the voice to attract someone’s attention, perhaps at a distance. Loudness, however, is not an invariable feature of every use.

Nidāʾ can describe a public summons, addressing someone, seeking a response or conveying a message. The addressee, distance, subject and circumstances determine the precise meaning.

A verse attributed to al-Aʿshā, a celebrated pre-Islamic poet, says:

فَتًى لَوْ يُنَادِي الشَّمْسَ أَلْقَتْ قِنَاعَهَا
أَوِ الْقَمَرَ السَّارِي لَأَلْقَى الْمَقَالِدَا

fatan law yunādī al-shamsa alqat qināʿahā / awi al-qamara al-sāriya la-alqā al-maqālidā

Meaning: A young man who, if he called the sun, would make it unveil its face; and if he called the travelling moon, it would submit and obey him.

This poetic image praises the person. Its lexical relevance is that yunādī connects caller and addressee even when the addressee is imaginatively the sun or moon.

Early explanations also propose a reading involving companionship and competitive boasting. The witness must therefore not be treated as exclusive proof of one meaning.

Lexical expressions also use نَدَى الصَّوْت for a voice’s extension and reach. Describing someone as أَنْدَى صَوْتًا can indicate a more carrying or far-reaching voice.

Vocal range is not the specific meaning of every derivative. Calling retains the basic act of address or summons, while context determines its loudness.

B. Nadiyy and Nādī: Gathering and Assembly

In the social branch, nadiyy and nādī denote where people gather and converse, or sometimes the assembled company itself.

Early lexical accounts describe a nadiyy as an assembly where people have gathered. They even specify that once its members disperse, that ongoing gathering no longer exists.

A nādī in early usage was therefore not necessarily an independent building or formal institution. Sometimes the assembled people themselves are central to the word’s use.

Bishr ibn Abī Khāzim, a pre-Islamic poet, describes the multitude of a community:

وَمَا يَنْدُوهُمُ النَّادِي وَلَكِنْ
بِكُلِّ مَحَلَّةٍ مِنْهُمْ فِئَامُ

wa-mā yandūhumu al-nādī wa-lākin / bi-kulli maḥallatin minhum fiʾāmu

Meaning: The assembly cannot contain them all; rather, in every quarter there are numerous groups of them.

Yandūhum here concerns accommodating or containing them. The poet describes a community too numerous for one assembly to hold.

This witness illustrates the word’s connection with social life, people gathering and their shared space.

Nadwah and muntadā also belong to this branch, denoting an assembly or gathering place.

The historical name Dār al-Nadwah in Mecca comes from this social use: a place for consultation and collective affairs. It witnesses the word’s social function without making every nādī a political decision-making council.

Lexical records also give nādaytuhu the particular senses of sitting together, consulting or engaging another in competitive boasting. These must not replace the familiar meaning “called” in every verse.

C. Nadā: Moisture and Dew

Another Arabic branch concerns moisture.

Nadā denotes dampness, dew and sometimes rain or moisture settling on ground and plants. نَدِيَ الشيء means something became damp, and أرض نَدِيَة describes moist ground.

This sense occurs in everyday life, animal husbandry, plants and descriptions of the natural environment.

Figurative expressions also arise in this field. A hand becoming stained or wet can provide an image of contact and being affected. Early Arabic records مَا نَدِيَتْ كَفِّي لَهُ بِشَرٍّ: my hand has not been stained by doing him harm.

Some dictionaries transmit a phrase attributed to al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī: مَا إِنْ نَدِيتُ بِشَيْءٍ أَنْتَ تَكْرَهُهُ. Other versions occur in the poetry collection and literary records. It is useful as a recorded lexical use, but cannot confidently be identified as the original wording of pre-Qur’anic poetry.

D. Nadā: Generosity and Giving

Arabic also uses nadā for generosity, giving and beneficence.

نَدِيُّ الكَفّ describes someone generous, and andā can compare people’s generosity.

Literary language likens a giving hand to a moist hand, against an image of dryness and deprivation. This resemblance alone does not establish the historical development of generosity from moisture.

Moisture and generosity are both recorded lexical uses. The Qur’anic forms studied here do not use nadā in those senses. Neither giving nor dew should therefore enter translations of nidāʾ or nādī without evidence.

3. Qur’anic Uses: Calling and the Addressee

A. A Call Can Be Hidden

One especially clear verse for the range of nidāʾ describes Zechariah:

إِذْ نَادَىٰ رَبَّهُ نِدَاءً خَفِيًّا

idh nādā rabbahu nidāʾan khafiyyan

Maryam 19:3

Translation: When he called his Lord with a hidden call.

Nādā is the verb, nidāʾan its verbal noun, and khafiyyan describes the call’s manner.

This qualifies an absolute definition of nidāʾ as a loud, public cry. Calling can take place privately, without public disclosure.

In Maryam 19:4, Zechariah begins with رَبِّ إِنِّي وَهَنَ الْعَظْمُ مِنِّي, “My Lord, my bones have grown weak,” and also speaks of his supplication: وَلَمْ أَكُنْ بِدُعَائِكَ رَبِّ شَقِيًّا, “I have not been unfortunate in calling upon You, my Lord.”

Calling and supplication concern the same event here. Zechariah addresses his Lord, with a request and speech directed to Him.

Their shared referent here does not make N D Y and D ʿ W exact synonyms throughout Arabic.

The verse highlights address and turning to an addressee, while a separate description can specify whether the call is public or hidden.

B. Calling across Distance and Separation

Noah’s story says:

وَنَادَىٰ نُوحٌ ابْنَهُ وَكَانَ فِي مَعْزِلٍ يَا بُنَيَّ ارْكَبْ مَعَنَا

wa-nādā nūḥun ibnahu wa-kāna fī maʿzilin yā bunayya irkab maʿanā

Hūd 11:42

Translation: Noah called to his son, who was apart: My son, embark with us.

The son’s separation is stated through وَكَانَ فِي مَعْزِلٍ. Calling describes how Noah addresses him.

Distance and separation matter to the scene, but nādā alone specifies neither a distance nor a volume.

Another example says:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ يُنَادُونَكَ مِنْ وَرَاءِ الْحُجُرَاتِ أَكْثَرُهُمْ لَا يَعْقِلُونَ

inna alladhīna yunādūnaka min warāʾi al-ḥujurāti aktharuhum lā yaʿqilūna

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:4

Translation: Most of those calling you from behind the chambers do not reason.

Calling is not inherently wrong here. The reproach concerns people whose way of addressing the Messenger was inappropriate in the stated circumstances.

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:5 describes waiting patiently for the Messenger to emerge as better for them. The moral assessment follows from context rather than an inherently blameworthy verb.

Other stories show the same distinction: a call may convey mercy and good news, or reproach, warning or a request.

C. Calling in Request and Response

Al-Anbiyāʾ says of Job:

وَأَيُّوبَ إِذْ نَادَىٰ رَبَّهُ أَنِّي مَسَّنِيَ الضُّرُّ وَأَنْتَ أَرْحَمُ الرَّاحِمِينَ

wa-ayyūba idh nādā rabbahu annī massaniya al-ḍurru wa-anta arḥamu al-rāḥimīna

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:83

Translation: And Job, when he called his Lord: Harm and suffering have touched me, and You are the most merciful of the merciful.

Calling here expresses his condition and his appeal to his Lord.

Of Jonah, it says:

فَنَادَىٰ فِي الظُّلُمَاتِ أَنْ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا أَنْتَ سُبْحَانَكَ

fa-nādā fī al-ẓulumāti an lā ilāha illā anta subḥānaka

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:87

Translation: He called in the darknesses: There is no deity but You; You are free of imperfection.

Both calls contain meaningful speech: one conveys suffering and seeks mercy, while the other conveys acknowledgment and glorification.

Calling alone does not necessarily contain a particular request. Request, acknowledgment, good news and warning follow from the speech’s content and context.

D. Calling by The God and the Angels

Qur’anic nādā is not confined to humans calling.

Of Zechariah, it says:

فَنَادَتْهُ الْمَلَائِكَةُ وَهُوَ قَائِمٌ يُصَلِّي فِي الْمِحْرَابِ

fa-nādathu al-malāʾikatu wa-huwa qāʾimun yuṣallī fī al-miḥrābi

Āl ʿImrān 3:39

Translation: The angels called him while he stood performing ṣalāh in the sanctuary.

In Moses’s story:

فَلَمَّا أَتَاهَا نُودِيَ يَا مُوسَىٰ

fa-lammā atāhā nūdiya yā mūsā

Ṭā Hā 20:11

Translation: When he reached it, a call was given: O Moses.

Nūdiya is passive: Moses was addressed. This construction emphasises his receiving the call.

It also says:

وَنَادَيْنَاهُ مِنْ جَانِبِ الطُّورِ الْأَيْمَنِ وَقَرَّبْنَاهُ نَجِيًّا

wa-nādaynāhu min jānibi al-ṭūri al-aymani wa-qarrabnāhu najiyyan

Maryam 19:52

Translation: We called him from the right side of the Mount and brought him near for confidential speech.

Nādaynāhu and najiyyan belong to different roots. One concerns calling, the other confidential conversation. Their combination does not make calling and private speech identical concepts.

Attributing a call to The God does not itself establish a bodily voice, speech organs or a method of transmitting the divine message. The Qur’an reports the address; an unstated manner must not be added as a certain part of the word’s meaning.

E. Calling at Resurrection

The resurrection passages describe groups calling to one another.

For example:

وَنَادَىٰ أَصْحَابُ الْجَنَّةِ أَصْحَابَ النَّارِ

wa-nādā aṣḥābu al-jannati aṣḥāba al-nāri

Al-Aʿrāf 7:44

Translation: The people of the Garden called to the people of the Fire.

And:

وَنَادَىٰ أَصْحَابُ النَّارِ أَصْحَابَ الْجَنَّةِ

wa-nādā aṣḥābu al-nāri aṣḥāba al-jannati

Al-Aʿrāf 7:50

Translation: The people of the Fire called to the people of the Garden.

The first concerns fulfilment of the promise of The God; the second, a request for water and provision.

The verb is shared, but the content differs. One verb can serve reporting, questioning, requesting and reciprocal address.

A call at resurrection can also express desperation:

وَنَادَوْا يَا مَالِكُ لِيَقْضِ عَلَيْنَا رَبُّكَ

wa-nādaw yā māliku li-yaqḍi ʿalaynā rabbuka

Al-Zukhruf 43:77

Translation: They call: O Mālik, let your Lord bring us to an end.

By contrast, Al-Aʿrāf 7:43 connects a call with the announcement of entering the Garden.

Calling is neither good news nor punishment in itself. The address’s content and the addressee’s condition determine the event’s character.

4. Qur’anic Derivatives and Their Distinctions

Nidāʾ: Calling and the Voice of Address

Al-Baqarah 2:171 says:

وَمَثَلُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا كَمَثَلِ الَّذِي يَنْعِقُ بِمَا لَا يَسْمَعُ إِلَّا دُعَاءً وَنِدَاءً

wa-mathalu alladhīna kafarū ka-mathali alladhī yanʿiqu bi-mā lā yasmaʿu illā duʿāʾan wa-nidāʾan

Translation: The likeness of those who disbelieve is that of someone calling to a creature that hears only a call and a summons.

Duʿāʾan and nidāʾan appear together. The first belongs to D ʿ W, the second to N D Y.

The concepts can share one communicative situation. Without independently examining all uses of duʿāʾ, however, one cannot impose an absolute boundary whereby it always means requesting, while nidāʾ always means mere loud sound.

Al-Baqarah 2:171 particularly concerns receiving sound without understanding its meaning. The creature hears the cry without grasping the human message intended by the caller.

Nidāʾ highlights speech’s audible, summoning aspect here. This differs from Maryam 19:3, where khafiyyan specifies the call’s manner.

Munādī: The Caller

Āl ʿImrān 3:193 says:

رَبَّنَا إِنَّنَا سَمِعْنَا مُنَادِيًا يُنَادِي لِلْإِيمَانِ أَنْ آمِنُوا بِرَبِّكُمْ فَآمَنَّا

rabbanā innanā samiʿnā munādiyan yunādī li-al-īmāni an āminū bi-rabbikum fa-āmannā

Translation: Our Lord, we heard a caller calling to faith: Believe in your Lord; and we believed.

Munādiyan is an active participle, while yunādī is an imperfect verb from the same family.

The participle identifies a person or agent serving as caller. The content is specified by لِلْإِيمَانِ and أَنْ آمِنُوا.

The noun alone does not determine the caller’s personal or historical identity. That requires evidence from the particular context.

Qāf 50:41 also says:

وَاسْتَمِعْ يَوْمَ يُنَادِ الْمُنَادِ مِنْ مَكَانٍ قَرِيبٍ

wa-istamiʿ yawma yunādi al-munādi min makānin qarībin

Translation: Listen for the day the caller calls from a nearby place.

Al-munādi denotes the caller; مِنْ مَكَانٍ قَرِيبٍ describes the call’s location or origin. Nearness is not inherent in the noun.

Nādī and Nadiyy: Assembly and Gathering

The Qur’an has three specific uses of this social branch.

First, Maryam 19:73:

أَيُّ الْفَرِيقَيْنِ خَيْرٌ مَقَامًا وَأَحْسَنُ نَدِيًّا

ayyu al-farīqayni khayrun maqāman wa-aḥsanu nadiyyan

Translation: Which of the two groups has a better position and a finer assembly?

Those who disbelieve claim superior position and assembly in comparison with the believers.

Nadiyyan concerns the social gathering where participants, standing and group prestige become visible.

Political power, wealth and class superiority are not part of its lexical definition. They may accompany social boasting in context, but must not enter the word’s basic meaning.

The second use is Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:29:

وَتَأْتُونَ فِي نَادِيكُمُ الْمُنْكَرَ

wa-taʾtūna fī nādīkumu al-munkara

Translation: You commit wrongdoing in your assembly.

Nādī identifies their gathering place; al-munkara denotes the wrongdoing they commit and is grammatically the object of taʾtūna.

An assembly can therefore host wrongdoing, but nādī is not inherently negative. The wrongdoing is conveyed by al-munkar and the context.

The third use is Al-ʿAlaq 96:17:

فَلْيَدْعُ نَادِيَهُ

fa-l-yadʿu nādiyahu

Translation: Let him call his assembly and its members.

Nādī is more than a building or seating area here: it can include the gathering’s members, whom the person calls for support.

Fa-l-yadʿu belongs to D ʿ W, while nādiyahu is its object. Their semantic closeness does not make them derivatives of one root.

Al-ʿAlaq 96:18 says:

سَنَدْعُ الزَّبَانِيَةَ

sa-nadʿu al-zabāniyata

Al-ʿAlaq 96:18

Translation: We shall call the zabāniyah.

The contrast between calling one’s assembly and the divine summons highlights the collective, supportive aspect of nādī. Its lexical meaning does not necessarily imply a fighting force, political apparatus or coercive power.

These uses link nādī and nadiyy with gathering and social position, while their precise meanings vary by context.

Tanādaw and Al-Tanād: Calling One Another

Al-Qalam 68:21 says:

فَتَنَادَوْا مُصْبِحِينَ

fa-tanādaw muṣbiḥīna

Translation: They called one another in the morning.

The verb is Form VI, tanādā, expressing reciprocal calling here. Al-Qalam 68:22 supplies the content: they urge one another to set out early to harvest their crop.

The call is meaningful speech serving coordination and collective action.

Ghāfir 40:32 says:

وَيَا قَوْمِ إِنِّي أَخَافُ عَلَيْكُمْ يَوْمَ التَّنَادِ

wa-yā qawmi innī akhāfu ʿalaykum yawma al-tanādi

Translation: My people, I fear for you the day of reciprocal calls.

Al-tanādi in this reading concerns calling one another. Al-Aʿrāf 7:44 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:50 likewise show different groups addressing one another at resurrection.

Recitation variants are recorded. Al-tanādī, retaining the final yāʾ, belongs to the same reciprocal form. Another transmitted reading doubles the dāl, al-tanādd, and is analysed through the separate root N D D, concerning flight and dispersal.

The doubled form is outside the ten recognised canonical readings and must not be equated with al-tanādi without explanation. This is more than a spelling difference: it changes both root and meaning.

5. Comparison with Related Words

N D Y and D ʿ W: Calling and Supplication

Nādā and daʿā overlap in many Qur’anic situations.

Both can summon someone. Duʿāʾ also ranges across invitation, requesting, calling and seeking, whereas nidāʾ particularly focuses on addressing and summoning an addressee.

Maryam 19:3 and Maryam 19:4 use calling and supplication of one event; Al-Baqarah 2:171 places them together.

Their relationship is overlap rather than absolute separation. A call can also be a supplication, and a supplication can take the form of a call, according to context.

Study of D ʿ W

N D Y and N J W: Calling and Confidential Speech

Najwā concerns private, confidential conversation; nidāʾ concerns addressing and summoning.

Maryam 19:52 combines the fields through nādaynāhu and najiyyan.

This does not make every call acoustically public and every confidential conversation acoustically hidden. A call can also be khafiyyan, as in Maryam 19:3.

The distinction chiefly concerns the type of communication: nidāʾ emphasises calling and address; najwā, private and exclusive conversation.

Study of N J W

N D Y and Ṣ W T: Calling and Sound

Ṣawt denotes sound or an audible voice, while nidāʾ usually concerns sound functioning as address or summons.

Every vocal call may involve sound, but not every sound is a call. Wind, something breaking or moving water does not become nidāʾ merely through being audible.

Divine and otherworldly calling must nevertheless not be defined by physical assumptions drawn from human voices.

Study of Ṣ W T

N D Y Compared with H T F and Ṣ Y Ḥ

Hatafa and ṣāḥa can also express calling and raising the voice. The Qur’an uses ṣayḥah in various settings, including overwhelming events.

Nidāʾ does not necessarily imply intensity or suddenness, though these may be prominent in some uses of ṣayḥah.

Each verse’s construction and context must determine acoustic intensity, rather than an absolute, mechanical comparison between roots.

Study of Ṣ Y Ḥ

6. Translation Proposals for Forqan

Translations should follow each derivative’s role and context.

Nādā and its verbal forms can be rendered “called,” “called out” or “summoned.” Persian nidā kard may preserve the Arabic construction especially well where the addressee is The God or a divine address is described. Ṣadā zad, “called out,” is natural in human dialogue such as Noah addressing his son.

Nidāʾ can be “a call” or “a summons.” Al-Baqarah 2:171 highlights its audible aspect; Maryam 19:3 requires preserving the description “hidden.”

Munādī and munādin mean a caller or summoner. The active participle itself does not identify a particular person.

Nādī and nadiyy can be “assembly,” “gathering” or, where appropriate, “members of the assembly.” “Assembly” suits Maryam 19:73 and is clear in Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:29; in Al-ʿAlaq 96:17, “his assembly and its members” conveys the contextual meaning better.

Tanādaw can be “they called one another”; al-tanādi in Ghāfir 40:32, “reciprocal calls” or “the day of calling one another.”

The word’s meaning must remain distinct from information conveyed by the sentence. Loudness, distance, requests, warnings, social support and conduct can all be present in context, but none should become a fixed meaning for every derivative without evidence.

7. Conclusion

N D Y has several established Arabic semantic fields: calling and summoning, gathering and assembly, moisture and dew, and generosity.

The first two are prominent in the Qur’anic uses.

Qur’anic calling addresses and summons another. It can be public or hidden, human or divine, accompanied by a request or report, and convey good news or warning. Context completes the meaning. Maryam 19:3 shows that loudness is not an invariable condition of every call.

Nādī and nadiyy concern gatherings and sometimes their members. Such a gathering can host conversation, boasting, wrongdoing or a request for support, but none of these alone defines the word.

Moisture and generosity are recorded in early Arabic, but must not be imported without evidence into the Qur’anic derivatives.

Understanding this family requires distinguishing its two main Qur’anic branches and its other Arabic uses, rather than forcing every use into one highly general definition. Their possible historical connections can be studied linguistically; each derivative’s meaning must first follow actual linguistic evidence and its own Qur’anic use.
