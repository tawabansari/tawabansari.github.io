حُلْقُوم names the throat or throat passage of a human or animal, especially in relation to breathing. Early lexicons describe the passage of breath and coughing, distinguishing it from مَرِيء, the passage of food and drink.

حَلْقَمَهُ concerns severing an animal’s throat in slaughter. حَلَاقِم and حَلَاقِيم are recorded plurals. The extended expression حَلَاقِيمُ الْبِلَادِ concerns outlying or remote parts of a country.

The noun’s derivation is disputed. Some lexicographers treat mīm as a root letter and discuss the quadriliteral family ح ل ق م. Others consider it additional and derive the word from حَلْق, under triliteral ح ل ق.

The dispute affects classification but does not change the noun’s direct Qur’anic meaning.

الْحُلْقُومَ occurs only once: Al-Wāqiʿah 56:83.

The verse depicts a dying person’s life reaching the throat while others are present. The following verses discuss divine nearness, limited human sight, and inability to bring that life back.

The noun names a familiar bodily part, but the passage is not simply teaching respiratory anatomy. It places the throat in a scene of dying to show limits to human control over death.

The anatomical meaning, the image of life reaching the throat, and the challenge to bring it back should remain distinct.

1. Word structure and disputed derivation

حُلْقُوم: four root letters, or three with an addition?

Its written form contains ḥāʾ, lām, qāf, wāw, and mīm.

The wāw marks a long vowel here.

The principal disagreement concerns final mīm: is it a radical, or an addition to حَلْق?

In the section on words exceeding three letters in Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris writes:

وَمِنْهُ الْحُلْقُومُ، وَلَيْسَ ذَلِكَ مَنْحُوتًا، وَلَكِنَّهُ مِمَّا زِيدَتْ فِيهِ الْمِيمُ، وَالْأَصْلُ الْحَلْقُ

wa-minhu al-ḥulqūmu wa-laysa dhālika manḥūtan wa-lākinnahu mimmā zīdat fīhi al-mīmu wa-al-aṣlu al-ḥalqu

“Among these is ḥulqūm. It is not a blend, but a word to which mīm has been added; its basis is ḥalq.”

Ibn Fāris therefore accepts a triliteral basis.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: Ibn Fāris’s additional-mīm analysis

Lisān al-ʿArab records both the view that mīm is radical and the view deriving the noun from حَلْق.

Tāj al-ʿArūs records the disagreement with the names of grammarians.

Ibn ʿUṣfūr is identified with the radical-mīm analysis.

Abū Ḥayyān al-Andalusī prefers treating it as additional.

This concerns the word’s morphology, rather than merely where a dictionary files it.

Tāj al-ʿArūs and Lisān al-ʿArab: radical or additional mīm

Two analyses of the pattern

With radical mīm, حُلْقُوم is analyzed as فُعْلُول, reflecting four radicals.

With additional mīm, it is analyzed as فُعْلُوم.

The pattern difference follows the disagreement over mīm.

The presence of wāw before mīm alone does not settle it.

The clear resemblance to حَلْق supports a semantic connection without resolving every historical or morphological issue.

Forqan can therefore retain an independent ح ل ق م entry while explaining the alternative classification under ح ل ق from the outset.

Principal forms

Recorded forms include:

  • حُلْقُوم: The throat; in early anatomical descriptions, the passage of breath and coughing.
  • حَلَاقِم: A plural of حُلْقُوم: throats.
  • حَلَاقِيم: Another plural of حُلْقُوم.
  • حَلْقَمَ ـ يُحَلْقِمُ: Severed an animal’s throat; also used for a stage of date ripening.
  • حَلْقَمَة: Cutting the throat.
  • مُحَلْقِم: A date beginning to ripen and soften near its point of attachment.
  • حُلْقَامَة / حِلْقَامَة: Recorded vocalizations for a date at a stage of ripening. An independent حُلْقَام was not confirmed in the inspected sources.
  • حَلَاقِيمُ الْبِلَادِ: Extended usage for outlying, remote, or confined regions of a country.

The date-ripening forms are also associated with nūn forms such as حَلْقَنَ and مُحَلْقِن.

Their sound variation and derivation are disputed.

حلقم and حلقن should not simply be treated as one certain root without examination.

Only الْحُلْقُومَ occurs in the Qur’an.

The verb, its plurals, and date-ripening forms are absent from the Qur’anic text.

Its classification under ح ل ق in Qur’anic indexing

Some morphological indexes, including the Quranic Arabic Corpus, classify الْحُلْقُومَ under triliteral ح ل ق.

That classification gives the root three Qur’anic occurrences:

Al-Baqarah 2:196 concerns shaving the head, Al-Fatḥ 48:27 those with shaved heads, and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:83 the throat.

Their direct senses differ.

Al-Baqarah 2:196 and Al-Fatḥ 48:27 concern removing hair.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:83 names a bodily part.

Even if mīm is considered additional, حُلْقُوم cannot be translated as shaving the head.

With radical mīm, the noun belongs to an independent quadriliteral classification.

The lexical data should record the disagreement without sacrificing the verse’s clear meaning to either classification.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: ḥulqūm classified under Ḥ L Q

2. The meaning in early Arabic

The throat and airway

Kitāb al-ʿAyn defines حَلْقَمَة as cutting the throat.

Al-Jawharī explains حُلْقُوم through حَلْق in Al-Ṣiḥāḥ.

Ibn Sīdah gives a more specific anatomical explanation in Al-Muḥkam wa-al-Muḥīṭ al-Aʿẓam, also quoted in Lisān al-ʿArab:

الْحُلْقُومُ مَجْرَى النَّفَسِ وَالسُّعَالِ مِنَ الْجَوْفِ

al-ḥulqūmu majrā al-nafasi wa-al-suʿāli mina al-jawfi

“Ḥulqūm is the passage of breath and coughing from the body’s interior.”

The description continues with a structure of cartilage extending from the tongue’s base towards the respiratory passage.

In this more precise lexical account, it is not merely the neck or the skin outside the throat; it concerns breathing.

Early anatomical descriptions should not be assumed identical to modern terminology.

Modern anatomy distinguishes the pharynx, larynx, trachea, and oesophagus.

Some newer definitions are broader and approach the pharyngeal region, whereas Ibn Sīdah emphasizes the airway.

“Throat” is therefore preferable for Qur’anic translation to exclusively substituting a narrowly defined modern anatomical organ.

Air passage and food passage

Tahdhīb al-Lughah describes حُلْقُوم and حَنْجُور as passages of breath.

It distinguishes the food-and-drink passage as مَرِيء.

The anatomical distinction matters.

The paths of air and food are related in the throat but are not identical along their whole lengths.

Everyday language may call this whole region the throat.

More precise lexical descriptions can emphasize the airway.

الْحُلْقُومَ should not be translated as the oesophagus or food pipe in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:83.

Nor does the noun itself mean breath or soul. It names a bodily location within the image of approaching death.

Al-Muḥkam and Tahdhīb al-Lughah: airway distinguished from food passage

حَلْقَمَهُ: cutting the throat

حَلْقَمَ الْحَيَوَانَ concerns cutting an animal’s throat in slaughter.

حَلْقَمَة names that action.

The use shows that the throat was a familiar bodily part in early Arabic experience.

People knew the animal’s breathing passage and where it was cut in slaughter.

The verb does not mean every kind of killing.

Severing the throat is its specific lexical feature.

The verb’s existence does not itself determine religious slaughter requirements, permitted foods, or the list of lawful animals.

Those require separate study of the relevant verses.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:83 does not describe cutting the throat.

The bodily noun should remain distinct from the non-Qur’anic slaughter verb.

A poetic witness

Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Muḥkam transmit this poetic line:

حَتَّىٰ إِذَا ابْتَلَّتْ حَلَاقِيمُ الْحُلُقْ

ḥattā idhā ibtallat ḥalāqīmu al-ḥuluq

“Until the throat-passages of the throats became wet.”

Some transmissions have بُلَّتْ instead of ابْتَلَّتْ.

The important form is حَلَاقِيم, a plural of حُلْقُوم.

الْحُلُق is itself a plural of حَلْق.

The two related words thus occur together in one image.

The witness attests حَلَاقِيم for the throat region in poetry.

It requires an important qualification.

The line is usually transmitted without its poet’s name, and a definite attribution has not been established here.

Its opening wording also varies.

It should not be called a securely dated pre-Islamic witness.

Its presence in early lexicons nevertheless attests the plural’s poetic use.

Lisān al-ʿArab, Ḥ L Q: the poetic witness ḥalāqīmu al-ḥuluq

حَلَاقِيمُ الْبِلَادِ: outlying regions

Lisān al-ʿArab explains the expression through outlying or remote parts of a country.

Another explanation emphasizes narrowness and restricted access.

This extends the bodily sense.

A passage or outlying region can be described through an image comparable to the throat’s passage in the body.

The comparison does not establish the geographical usage’s precise historical origin.

The noun should not always be translated as a border or geographical strait.

The dying scene in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:83 clearly suits the bodily throat.

حُلْقُومُ النَّعَامَةِ: an image of narrowness

Lexicons also record:

نَزَلْنَا فِي مِثْلِ حُلْقُومِ النَّعَامَةِ

nazalnā fī mithli ḥulqūmi al-naʿāmati

“We alighted in a place like an ostrich’s throat.”

The expression concerns a confined place.

A narrow passage provides the comparison.

The throat can thus appear in spatial similes.

The noun itself does not always mean narrowness.

It primarily names a bodily part; confinement emerges through its features and the simile.

It should not become merely “difficulty” or “predicament” in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:83.

It is a bodily location in an image of dying.

مُحَلْقِم and ripening dates

Early sources use مُحَلْقِم for a date beginning to ripen near its attachment point.

حُلْقَامَة is also recorded; the inspected Tāj al-ʿArūs has حِلْقَامَة. These vocalizations should be identified by source rather than replaced with an unverified independent حُلْقَام form.

Related nūn forms such as مُحَلْقِن and حُلْقَان can describe dates substantially ripe and softened.

Their transmission and derivational analysis vary.

They help explain the lexical family but do not occur in the Qur’an.

They do not establish ripening or softening fruit as the noun’s original meaning.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: date-ripening forms

3. الْحُلْقُومَ in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:83

Reaching the throat at the approach of death

Verse text:

فَلَوْلَا إِذَا بَلَغَتِ الْحُلْقُومَ

fa-lawlā idhā balaghati al-ḥulqūma

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Then why, when it reaches the throat…”

الْحُلْقُومَ is definite and accusative.

It is the object of بَلَغَتْ.

That verb, from ب ل غ, means reaching.

The noun identifies the place something reaches.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:83 does not explicitly name the subject.

The third-person feminine singular verb has an implicit subject.

What is it that has reached the throat?

The subject of بَلَغَتْ

Early commentaries usually supply النَّفْس or الرُّوح.

Al-Ṭabarī explains it as the person’s life departing the body.

This fits Al-Wāqiʿah 56:87, which concerns bringing back what reached the throat.

Contextually, the scene concerns a person’s life or soul at the approach of death.

Direct wording and contextual explanation should remain distinct.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:83 does not explicitly contain النَّفْس or الرُّوح.

A closely worded translation can retain the short, incomplete construction and explain the referent in reflection.

An explanatory translation can supply “life” or “soul,” provided the distinction is recognized.

Does حُلْقُوم name the soul itself?

No.

It names the throat.

Nafs and rūḥ have independent Qur’anic uses.

The throat is the location something reaches in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:83.

It should not itself be translated as soul, spirit, or life.

Nor does بَلَغَتِ الْحُلْقُومَ define the throat as the soul’s permanent dwelling.

The verse offers no such definition.

Why does the throat appear in the image of death?

The throat relates to breathing and voice.

Changes or difficulty in breathing and loss of speech near death may be perceptible to those present.

The throat can therefore be a familiar bodily feature in depicting the end of life.

This does not mean that everyone shows precisely the same respiratory signs when dying.

The verse does not explain the timing of cardiac arrest, brain activity, or medical processes of death.

It uses a tangible image of life’s end approaching.

The image should not become a technical account of the soul’s physical route through the body.

Al-Ṭabarī: the implicit subject and scene of approaching death

4. Al-Wāqiʿah 56:83, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:84, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:85, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:86, and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:87 together

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:84: those present watching

Verse text:

وَأَنْتُمْ حِينَئِذٍ تَنْظُرُونَ

wa-antum ḥīnaʾidhin tanẓurūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“While you are then watching.”

The verse introduces people witnessing the approach of death.

The scene addresses those around the dying person as well as depicting that person.

تَنْظُرُونَ does not alone say they perceive the event’s whole reality.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:85 contrasts nearness with their inability to see.

Human observation is not unlimited knowledge or power.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:85: nearness and limited sight

Verse text:

وَنَحْنُ أَقْرَبُ إِلَيْهِ مِنْكُمْ وَلَٰكِنْ لَا تُبْصِرُونَ

wa-naḥnu aqrabu ilayhi minkum wa-lākin lā tubṣirūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“We are nearer to him than you, but you do not see.”

Divine nearness is set against the presence of those around the dying person.

Some early interpretations relate this to God’s knowledge and power.

Others explain the presence of the messengers taking the person’s soul.

The verse does not explicitly name angels here.

A direct translation should retain “We are nearer to him than you.”

The nature of that nearness belongs to reflection and comparison with other verses.

حُلْقُوم names a bodily location in the preceding verse without defining the nature of divine nearness.

لَا تُبْصِرُونَ does not establish that the onlookers see nothing at all.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:84 explicitly says they are watching.

Watching the visible scene is therefore different from perceiving its whole reality. This contextual contrast should not become an absolute lexical opposition between ن ظ ر and ب ص ر in every usage.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:86: a challenge concerning authority and accountability

Verse text:

فَلَوْلَا إِنْ كُنْتُمْ غَيْرَ مَدِينِينَ

fa-lawlā in kuntum ghayra madīnīna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Then why, if you are not subject to judgement and accounting…”

مَدِينِينَ belongs to د ي ن.

Here it concerns being subject to judgement, accounting, or recompense.

Its exact scope needs independent study of that root.

The challenge addresses those who would regard themselves as outside divine accountability and rule.

حُلْقُوم itself does not mean judgement or recompense.

That theme comes from Al-Wāqiʿah 56:86.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:87: bringing it back

Verse text:

تَرْجِعُونَهَا إِنْ كُنْتُمْ صَادِقِينَ

tarjiʿūnahā in kuntum ṣādiqīna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“…do you not bring it back, if you are truthful?”

تَرْجِعُونَهَا concerns returning what reached the throat at the passage’s beginning. It is an imperfect verb, not an imperative; the question continues the preceding فَلَوْلَا.

The pronoun هَا is feminine singular.

This fits supplying النَّفْس or الرُّوح as the implicit subject of بَلَغَتْ.

The challenge now becomes complete.

When life approaches death and others are present, why do they not bring it back if they claim exemption from judgement and accounting?

The bodily throat thus participates in an argument about limits to human control.

The noun alone does not establish every detail of life, body, and death.

فَلَوْلَا at the passage’s beginning and middle

The expression recurs in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:83 and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:86.

لَوْلَا here urges a response or challenges action.

The opening construction is completed by what follows; تَرْجِعُونَهَا supplies the challenged action.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:83 should not be translated as an entirely independent sentence unrelated to the following verses.

Its short wording belongs to a larger argument.

The point is not merely where the throat is, but confronting a death that people cannot reverse at will.

Al-Ṭabarī: the two challenges completed by tarjiʿūnahā and the sense of madīnīn

5. Comparison with related roots and words

Ḥ L Q M and Ḥ L Q: throat and the ḥalq family

حَلْق concerns the throat region.

The same lexical family contains حَلَقَ الرَّأْسَ for shaving hair.

Their precise relationship and the structure of حُلْقُوم require care.

Following Ibn Fāris, the noun derives from حَلْق with added mīm.

With radical mīm, it constitutes an independent quadriliteral family.

Either way, its sense in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:83 is the throat, rather than shaving.

Study of Ḥ L Q

Ḥ L Q M and Ḥ N J R: throat and laryngeal region

حَنْجَرَة concerns a part of the throat associated with voice and breathing.

Early sources also connect حُلْقُوم with the airway.

The referents are close, but the words should not be made complete synonyms in every usage without examination.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:10 and Ghāfir 40:18 use الْحَنَاجِر.

Their settings involve severe fear and pressure.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:83 places الْحُلْقُوم in the approach of death.

Similar bodily regions do not make all the Qur’anic images identical.

Ḥ L Q M and M R ʾ: throat and oesophagus

مَرِيء in anatomical Arabic concerns the passage of food and drink.

More specific early descriptions connect حُلْقُوم with the airway.

The distinction should remain.

“Throat” suits the verse without requiring the technical name of a particular bodily tube.

Ḥ L Q M and W R D: throat and neck vein

Qāf 50:16 contains حَبْلِ الْوَرِيدِ.

The expression concerns a vein in the neck.

حُلْقُوم is also in the neck region but concerns the throat and airway.

They do not name one identical bodily structure.

Qāf 50:16 discusses divine nearness; Al-Wāqiʿah 56:83, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:84, and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:85 also introduce nearness in the scene of death.

The thematic connection merits comparison without equating the expressions lexically.

Ḥ L Q M and T R Q W: throat and collarbones

Al-Qiyāmah 75:26 contains بَلَغَتِ التَّرَاقِيَ.

التَّرَاقِي is the plural of تَرْقُوَة, concerning the bones at the upper chest near the neck.

That verse also uses بَلَغَتْ without explicitly naming its subject.

It therefore has a clear imagistic connection with Al-Wāqiʿah 56:83.

The throat and collarbone are nevertheless different structures.

One concerns the throat passage, the other bones near the neck and shoulders.

A shared theme of dying does not make the words synonyms.

Ḥ L Q M and B L GH: the throat and reaching

بَلَغَتْ in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:83 comes from ب ل غ.

الْحُلْقُومَ names what is reached.

Reaching is the verb’s meaning, rather than the noun’s.

Confusing them could incorrectly turn حُلْقُوم into “the final stage of life.”

It participates in that image while retaining its lexical meaning of throat.

Study of B L GH

6. Interpretive questions and semantic development

Does the soul physically exit through the throat?

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:83 depicts reaching the throat.

The wording and context concern dying.

The verse does not describe an anatomically measurable route for the soul’s departure.

It should not be made to assert that the soul is a material object travelling through the airway along a medically observable route.

The bodily image should not be erased either.

The verse uses a familiar bodily location to depict approaching death.

A linguistic image should be distinguished from a precise physical claim.

Is the throat the dwelling of life or the soul?

The noun has no such meaning itself.

It names the throat or airway.

The dying scene depicts life reaching it.

This does not establish that the soul dwells there throughout life or that it is its permanent storage place.

Those claims require separate evidence.

Does the verse explain every medical mechanism of death?

No.

Medical evaluation of death involves specialized assessment of bodily functions; the verse does not supply those clinical criteria.

حُلْقُوم is not the name of a modern diagnostic criterion.

The verse presents a familiar, tangible experience.

It should not become a report of every biological stage of death.

Is the Qur’anic noun only figurative?

It lexically names a real bodily part.

It therefore has a physical referent.

That part is used in an image and argument concerning life’s end.

The usage has both a bodily basis and a rhetorical role.

Natural language often uses a real organ in describing a wider experience.

Does inability to restore life make treatment useless?

No.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:83, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:84, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:85, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:86, and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:87 concern dying and inability to return life in the situation described.

This does not establish that treating illness, caring for patients, or trying to save life is useless or forbidden.

Treatment and care can help sustain or restore vital functions in different circumstances.

That differs from claiming unlimited control of life and death.

The root itself supplies no medical or ethical judgement about treatment.

Does divine nearness establish physical proximity?

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:85 speaks of being nearer than those present.

That sentence alone does not determine a spatial or bodily mode of nearness.

Early interpretations discuss divine knowledge and power or the presence of messengers taking the soul.

The question requires independent study of the verse.

The noun cannot establish God’s physical location or the unseen world’s structure.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

For الْحُلْقُومَ in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:83, “throat” is the best basic equivalent.

Persian gulu is natural, while gulūgāh gives a clearer image of the passage of breath.

The Arabic loanword ḥulqūm is also familiar in Persian and can be retained.

Some readers may associate it with the whole throat or food passage; gulūgāh is therefore a suitable general Persian equivalent.

For the verse itself:

فَلَوْلَا إِذَا بَلَغَتِ الْحُلْقُومَ

fa-lawlā idhā balaghati al-ḥulqūma

A rendering close to the wording is:

“Then why, when it reaches the throat…”

This does not add an unnamed subject and preserves the construction completed in the following verses.

A contextual explanation can add:

“When the dying person’s life reaches the throat.”

This should remain distinct from the direct rendering: “life” is not explicit in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:83 itself.

For non-Qur’anic forms:

  • حَلَاقِم and حَلَاقِيم mean “throats.”
  • حَلْقَمَهُ in the slaughter use means “cut his throat.”
  • حَلَاقِيمُ الْبِلَادِ concerns outlying or remote regions.
  • مُحَلْقِم in the date-ripening use requires explanation of that stage,rather than the human throat.

Forqan’s word box should retain two distinct parts.

Brief root meaning: “The throat; the region or passage associated with breathing.”

Meaning in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:83: “The human throat, reached by life in the Qur’anic image of approaching death.”

A root note should record the disputed status of mīm.

Choosing the quadriliteral heading should not suppress the established triliteral analysis.

8. Conclusion

حُلْقُوم concerns the throat or passage of breath.

Early lexicons explain it through حَلْق and, more specifically, distinguish it from the food-and-drink passage.

The plurals حَلَاقِم and حَلَاقِيم and the verb حَلْقَمَ are recorded in Arabic.

Other uses concern outlying regions, confined space, and ripening dates; none occurs in the Qur’an.

The root’s structure is disputed.

Ibn Fāris treats mīm as additional and derives it from ح ل ق.

Some other lexicographers and grammarians take mīm as radical under ح ل ق م.

Research should record both analyses.

الْحُلْقُومَ occurs only once, in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:83.

It appears in the approach of death.

The following verses introduce onlookers, divine nearness, and inability to bring life back.

Lexically, it names a specific bodily region.

Contextually, it participates in an image of dying and limits to human power.

The noun’s direct meaning is the throat. Life reaching it is the Qur’anic image of approaching death, rather than the noun’s anatomical definition.

This avoids two errors.

First, making the noun mean the soul or life itself.

Second, imposing a precise medical or physical theory of the soul’s exit route on wording that gives no such detail.

“Throat” is a suitable basic equivalent for Forqan.

Retaining “Then why, when it reaches the throat…” allows the referent and argument to become clear through Al-Wāqiʿah 56:83, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:84, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:85, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:86, and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:87, without replacing the Arabic wording with interpretive explanation.
