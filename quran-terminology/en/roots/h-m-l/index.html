From carrying loads and pregnancy to bearing responsibility and the consequences of deeds

The Arabic root ح م ل concerns lifting, carrying, and bearing something. حَمَلَ is used when a person, animal, or means of transport carries something, whether on the back or shoulders, inside a vehicle, or otherwise borne by the carrier.

Within this family, حَمْل can denote the unborn child in the mother’s womb or, in another use, the fruit borne by a tree. حِمْل denotes a load carried on the back or head. حَمَّلَ can mean loading something upon another or entrusting someone with a responsibility; احْتَمَلَ likewise concerns carrying, undertaking, or bearing consequences in different usages.

The Qur’an uses the root for transporting loads and passengers, pregnancy, bearing the Throne, accepting the trust, messengers’ responsibilities, and the consequences of human deeds. Carrying thus extends beyond bodily movement, but nonmaterial uses must not turn its tangible uses into metaphors.

The family distinguishes an actual load, a responsibility assigned to someone, and a responsibility properly fulfilled. A task can be assigned yet refused; a person can also bear the consequences of conduct without those consequences being a transportable object.

Qur’anic forms include active and passive forms of the basic verb حَمَلَ, Form II حَمَّلَ, Form VIII احْتَمَلَ, the nouns حَمْل, حِمْل, أَحْمَال, and حَمُولَة, and the descriptive forms حَامِلَات, حَامِلِين, and حَمَّالَة.

The root occurs 64 times in 50 verses across 29 surahs: 41 Form I verbs, five Form II verbs, three Form VIII verbs, and 15 nominal or descriptive forms. Of the five Form II occurrences, four are passive and one active.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: 64 occurrences and ten morphological groups

1. Semantic Core and Early Arabic Uses

حَمَلَ: lifting and carrying

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies one principle for ح م ل: إِقْلَالُ الشَّيْءِ, lifting or raising something.

Al-ʿAyn also records حَمَلَ for lifting and carrying. It can concern people, animals, vehicles, or things that support or convey something else.

In tangible usage, there is a relationship between carrier and carried: what carries and what is carried.

A person can place a load on a shoulder, a camel can carry goods on its back, and a ship can convey people and objects over water.

Carrying can accompany movement, but movement is not required in every use. Someone may support a load while standing still.

Nor does حَمْل necessarily mean difficulty. A load may be light or heavy, depending on its size, the carrier’s strength, and accompanying words.

This matters in Qur’anic usage. Al-Aʿrāf 7:189 mentions حَمْلًا خَفِيفًا, a light pregnancy or burden; heaviness is therefore not an invariant component of the root.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: lifting and carrying as the root principle

حَمْل and حِمْل: pregnancy and an external load

The vowel on ḥāʾ matters in distinguishing حَمْل and حِمْل.

حَمْل, with fatḥah, commonly denotes what is in a mother’s womb. A tree can also bear fruit, and this form can name its fruit.

حِمْل, with kasrah, more commonly denotes a load borne on the back or head.

Ibn al-Sikkīt and Al-Aṣmaʿī record the distinction. Ibn Fāris also distinguishes what is in the womb or on a tree from a load on the back or head.

Lexicographers nevertheless differ over tree fruit: some use حَمْل and others حِمْل. Some explanations compare attached fruit with what is borne in the womb, favouring fatḥah on that account.

The usual distinction is therefore clear, but it should not become an exceptionless rule for every dialect and historical period.

The Qur’an illustrates it: حَمْل occurs in pregnancy passages, whereas حِمْلُ بَعِيرٍ in Yūsuf 12:72 denotes a camel-load.

حَامِل and حَامِلَة

حَامِل is the active participle of حَمَلَ.

Arabic uses امْرَأَةٌ حَامِلٌ for a pregnant woman; حَامِلَة is also recorded.

Some lexicographers explain that pregnancy’s association with females allows حَامِل without a feminine ending, whereas a woman carrying something on her head or back is called حَامِلَة.

Other grammarians note that the rules for feminine descriptive endings are not always so simple or uniform.

The distinction can help explain some uses, but the presence or absence of tāʾ cannot identify the kind of carrying without context.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Miṣbāḥ: ḥaml, ḥiml, and ḥāmil

حَمُولَة: a load-bearing animal or means of transport

حَمُولَة denotes an animal used to carry loads.

Dictionaries record it for camels and other burden-bearing animals.

حُمُول can denote camels bearing howdahs or loads, and in some usages the loads themselves.

حُمْلَان is recorded for providing someone with a mount and sometimes for the expense or means of transport.

The family thus names more than lifting a load: derivatives can name the means, animal, or expense associated with carrying.

Forms such as حَمُولَة in these uses name burden-bearing animals, rather than every vehicle such as a ship. Whether a camel, horse, mule, donkey, or another animal is intended requires lexical evidence and context.

حَمَّلَ, تَحَمَّلَ, and احْتَمَلَ

Form II حَمَّلَ can mean placing a load upon another or assigning someone a task.

تَحَمَّلَ is used for accepting and undertaking a load or responsibility, sometimes with effort or hardship.

Form VIII احْتَمَلَ is likewise recorded for carrying and undertaking.

The three cannot be separated mechanically. Form II does not always express unjust imposition, nor does Form VIII always mean enduring psychological or ethical hardship.

فَاحْتَمَلَ السَّيْلُ زَبَدًا رَابِيًا in Al-Raʿd 13:17 describes a torrent carrying swelling foam, showing that احْتَمَلَ can concern physically carrying material.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:112 and Al-Aḥzāb 33:58 use the same form for bearing the consequences of sin and false accusation.

The appropriate meaning therefore follows the object, construction, and context.

حَمَالَة and financial guarantee

Early Arabic also uses حَمَالَة for a financial undertaking, particularly assuming payment of blood money, compensation, or another person’s debt.

حَمِيل can likewise denote a guarantor or surety.

Ibn Fāris treats these as extensions of carrying: someone undertakes a responsibility previously borne by another.

What is carried here is a financial commitment, rather than a physical object.

The extension from material loads to legal responsibility was thus familiar in Arabic.

This does not establish that any responsibility can be transferred without consent or necessary conditions. The validity of such a transfer is a legal question, not determined by the root alone.

حَمَل: the lamb

حَمَل, with fatḥah on both ḥāʾ and mīm, also names a lamb, particularly a male lamb. Some dictionaries record حُمْلَان and أَحْمَال as its plurals.

This must not be confused with حَمْل, pregnancy, or حِمْل, a load.

الحَمَل also names the zodiacal sign Aries.

These uses do not occur in the Qur’an. Shared consonants alone cannot connect the lamb or zodiacal name with every use of carrying without historical evidence.

Lisān al-ʿArab: burden-bearing, guarantees, and lamb

2. Early Poetry: Material Loads and Nonmaterial Responsibility

Pre-Islamic poetry uses the root for both actual carrying and undertaking conduct or responsibility.

Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā says:

وَمَنْ لَا يَزَلْ يَسْتَحْمِلُ النَّاسَ نَفْسَهُ
وَلَا يُغْنِهَا يَوْمًا مِنَ الدَّهْرِ يُسْأَمِ

wa-man lā yazal yastaḥmilu al-nāsa nafsahu / wa-lā yughnihā yawman mina al-dahri yusʾami

Meaning: One who continually places himself upon others and never meets his own needs will eventually weary them.

يَسْتَحْمِلُ النَّاسَ نَفْسَهُ does not mean physically carrying the person’s body on others’ backs.

The poet describes someone who continually places his needs and affairs upon others.

اِسْتِحْمَال can thus express asking others to bear someone’s load or responsibility.

The verse is transmitted in material attributed to Zuhayr and in Sībawayh’s Al-Kitāb. Sībawayh’s transmission has nominative يَسْتَحْمِلُ as the predicate of يَزَلْ. It witnesses a nonmaterial early use, while variants and attribution require separate examination.

Sībawayh’s Al-Kitāb: nominative yastaḥmilu in Zuhayr’s witness

A verse attributed to Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī also reads:

إِنَّا اقْتَسَمْنَا خُطَّتَيْنَا بَيْنَنَا
فَحَمَلْتُ بَرَّةَ وَاحْتَمَلْتَ فَجَارِ

innā iqtasamnā khuṭṭataynā baynanā / fa-ḥamaltu barrata wa-ḥtamalta fajāri

Meaning: We divided our two courses between us: I undertook goodness, and you undertook wickedness.

حَمَلْتُ and احْتَمَلْتَ occur in one construction.

Their object is ethical conduct or a person’s course, rather than a bodily load.

Carrying can therefore describe human responsibility or conduct.

The two verbs do not inherently assign goodness to حَمَلَ and evil to احْتَمَلَ. Goodness and wickedness come from بَرَّة and فَجَار, rather than the verbs themselves.

The verse occurs in early grammatical works and Al-Nābighah’s dīwān. Some transmissions vary in pronouns and verb vocalization, but both forms clearly concern ethical conduct.

Khizānat al-Adab: Al-Nābighah’s witness and the names for goodness and wickedness

Together, the witnesses concern social dependence and ethical conduct, showing that extending carrying to responsibility was not confined to religious terminology developed after the Qur’an.

3. Material Carrying: Loads, Mounts, Ships, and Transport

Al-Naḥl 16:7: carrying human loads

وَتَحْمِلُ أَثْقَالَكُمْ إِلَىٰ بَلَدٍ لَمْ تَكُونُوا بَالِغِيهِ إِلَّا بِشِقِّ الْأَنْفُسِ إِنَّ رَبَّكُمْ لَرَءُوفٌ رَحِيمٌ

wa-taḥmilu athqālakum ilā baladin lam takūnū bālighīhi illā bi-shiqqi al-anfusi inna rabbakum la-raʾūfun raḥīmun

Translation: They carry your heavy loads to a land you could reach only with great hardship; your Lord is indeed Kind and Merciful.

تَحْمِلُ concerns livestock physically carrying loads.

أَثْقَالَكُمْ comes from ث ق ل and denotes heavy loads.

Carrying and the load’s heaviness are therefore separate concepts.

The animals transport human loads to a distant destination. The work may involve bodily exertion, but حَمْل itself does not mean suffering.

It expresses moving the load and conveying it to its destination.

Al-Anʿām 6:142: حَمُولَة and فَرْش

وَمِنَ الْأَنْعَامِ حَمُولَةً وَفَرْشًا كُلُوا مِمَّا رَزَقَكُمُ اللَّهُ وَلَا تَتَّبِعُوا خُطُوَاتِ الشَّيْطَانِ إِنَّهُ لَكُمْ عَدُوٌّ مُبِينٌ

wa-mina al-anʿāmi ḥamūlatan wa-farshan kulū mimmā razaqakumu allāhu wa-lā tattabiʿū khuṭuwāti al-shayṭāni innahu lakum ʿaduwwun mubīnun

Translation: Among livestock, [He created] burden-bearing animals and smaller animals. Eat what The God has provided you, and do not follow Satan’s footsteps; he is an evident enemy to you.

حَمُولَة concerns animals used for carrying loads.

It is contrasted with فَرْش, from ف ر ش, describing another livestock group in this context.

Interpretations of فَرْش include smaller animals or those closer to the ground. The translation above selects smaller animals without making all recorded interpretations identical.

حَمُولَة clearly concerns animals suitable for, or used in, carrying loads.

The verse also commands كُلُوا, eat. Calling animals حَمُولَة does not itself make eating their meat obligatory or forbidden.

Their transport function and dietary status are separate matters.

Al-Ṭabarī: burden-bearing animals and interpretations of farsh

Yūsuf 12:72: حِمْلُ بَعِيرٍ

قَالُوا نَفْقِدُ صُوَاعَ الْمَلِكِ وَلِمَنْ جَاءَ بِهِ حِمْلُ بَعِيرٍ وَأَنَا بِهِ زَعِيمٌ

qālū nafqidu ṣuwāʿa al-maliki wa-li-man jāʾa bihi ḥimlu baʿīrin wa-anā bihi zaʿīmun

Translation: They said, “We are missing the king’s measuring cup; whoever brings it shall receive a camel-load, and I guarantee it.”

حِمْلُ بَعِيرٍ denotes an amount of goods carried by a camel.

It illustrates the difference between حِمْل and حَمْل in the pregnancy sense.

The expression does not fix a weight in kilograms or modern units. The load’s size can depend on the goods, animal, and transport conditions.

زَعِيمٌ concerns guarantee and belongs to another root; guarantee must not be inserted into حِمْل itself.

People carried in ships and on mounts

Hūd 11:40 instructs Noah to carry the specified creatures and people in the ship.

Al-Qamar 54:13 describes Noah carried on something made of planks and fastenings.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:11 mentions people carried in the ship when the water overwhelmed its bounds.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:70 describes Adam’s children carried on land and sea.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:22 and Ghāfir 40:80 also mention people carried on livestock and ships.

These uses concern people transported by something else.

Carrying need not mean placing a load on a back. A person may be inside a ship and carried by its movement.

Yā Sīn 36:41 mentions offspring carried in a laden ship. Its actual sense of carrying and transport should be examined without removing a real ship from the direct meaning merely because broader readings are possible.

Objects and natural materials

Al-Raʿd 13:17 reads:

فَاحْتَمَلَ السَّيْلُ زَبَدًا رَابِيًا

fa-ḥtamala al-saylu zabadan rābiyan

Meaning: The torrent carried swelling foam.

احْتَمَلَ describes foam conveyed by flowing water.

Form VIII in this family therefore does not inherently express an ethical judgement or enduring hardship.

حَمَلَتْ ظُهُورُهُمَا in Al-Anʿām 6:146 concerns what is borne on the backs of cattle and sheep.

لَا تَحْمِلُ رِزْقَهَا in Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:60 describes creatures that do not carry their provision. Early explanations relate this to not carrying or storing food for the future.

It does not mean that no animal in nature stores food. The verse describes many creatures that do not, rather than every animal without exception.

Nor does the verb mean inability to eat; the subject is carrying or keeping provision.

Al-Ṭabarī: carrying or storing provision for the next day

فَالْحَامِلَاتِ وِقْرًا: Al-Dhāriyāt 51:2

فَالْحَامِلَاتِ وِقْرًا

fa-l-ḥāmilāti wiqran

Translation: And by those bearers that carry a heavy load.

الْحَامِلَاتِ is a feminine plural active participle; وِقْرًا denotes a heavy load.

Early explanations commonly identify the bearers as clouds carrying water.

Other readings include winds carrying clouds.

Lexically, حَامِلَات only describes bearing. It does not independently name clouds, winds, or another being.

Clouds bearing water can be examined through context and early reports, but clouds must not become an invariant part of the root’s meaning.

Al-Samʿānī: clouds and winds carrying clouds as alternative readings

4. حَمْل: Pregnancy and Bearing the Unborn Child

Pregnancy is one of this family’s clearest Qur’anic branches.

The mother bears the unborn child within her body. This differs from a load on a shoulder, although both involve carrier and carried.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:189: a light pregnancy becoming heavy

Al-Aʿrāf 7:189 reads:

فَلَمَّا تَغَشَّاهَا حَمَلَتْ حَمْلًا خَفِيفًا فَمَرَّتْ بِهِ فَلَمَّا أَثْقَلَتْ دَعَوَا اللَّهَ رَبَّهُمَا

fa-lammā taghashshāhā ḥamalat ḥamlan khafīfan fa-marrat bihi fa-lammā athqalat daʿawā allāha rabbahumā

Translation: When he joined with her, she bore a light pregnancy and continued with it; when she became heavy, both called upon The God, their Lord.

The verb حَمَلَتْ and noun حَمْلًا occur together.

The noun concerns pregnancy, whose condition is described as خَفِيفًا, light.

أَثْقَلَتْ, from ث ق ل, subsequently describes the mother becoming heavy.

Pregnancy is therefore not described as equally heavy throughout.

Lexically, حَمْل does not itself mean extreme heaviness; its degree may change through different stages.

Maryam 19:22 and Maryam 19:27: two kinds of carrying

Maryam 19:22 reads:

فَحَمَلَتْهُ فَانْتَبَذَتْ بِهِ مَكَانًا قَصِيًّا

fa-ḥamalathu fa-ntabadhat bihi makānan qaṣiyyan

Meaning: She conceived him and withdrew with him to a distant place.

Maryam 19:27 also reads:

فَأَتَتْ بِهِ قَوْمَهَا تَحْمِلُهُ

fa-atat bihi qawmahā taḥmiluhu

Meaning: She brought him to her people, carrying him.

The first concerns Mary’s pregnancy.

In the second, the child has been born and Mary carries him with her.

The same account thus uses the verb both for bearing an unborn child in the womb and carrying a child after birth.

The verb alone does not identify the prenatal or postnatal stage; context and the carried person’s condition distinguish them.

Pregnancy, birth, and nurture

Luqmān 31:14 describes the mother’s bearing of the human as وَهْنًا عَلَىٰ وَهْنٍ, weakness upon weakness.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:15 describes pregnancy and childbirth as difficult, then gives 30 months for حَمْل and فِصَال together.

حَمْل concerns pregnancy; فِصَال concerns weaning.

وَحَمْلُهُ وَفِصَالُهُ ثَلَاثُونَ شَهْرًا therefore does not mean 30 months of pregnancy. The number concerns the two periods together.

Al-Raʿd 13:8, Fāṭir 35:11, and Fuṣṣilat 41:47 also describe The God’s knowledge of what women bear or deliver.

حَمْل in these passages concerns pregnancy and the unborn child, rather than any load a woman might carry.

حَمْل and pregnancy-related provisions

أُولَاتُ الْأَحْمَالِ in Al-Ṭalāq 65:4 concerns pregnant women.

The same verse marks the end of their waiting period with أَنْ يَضَعْنَ حَمْلَهُنَّ, laying down their pregnancy or giving birth.

Al-Ṭalāq 65:6 also discusses pregnant women and providing for them until delivery.

أَحْمَال and حَمْل belong to pregnancy here.

The word does not determine all legal details of waiting periods, maintenance, or family responsibility. Those provisions arise from the verses’ other clauses.

Al-Ḥajj 22:2 depicts وَتَضَعُ كُلُّ ذَاتِ حَمْلٍ حَمْلَهَا: every pregnant one lays down what she bears.

The expression depicts the intensity of a terrifying event.

It alone does not establish every biological or medical detail of the scene.

5. Bearing Responsibility: Trust, Scripture, and Consequences

Al-Baqarah 2:286: a burden laid upon people

Al-Baqarah 2:286 contains three occurrences in two successive requests:

رَبَّنَا وَلَا تَحْمِلْ عَلَيْنَا إِصْرًا كَمَا حَمَلْتَهُ عَلَى الَّذِينَ مِنْ قَبْلِنَا

rabbanā wa-lā taḥmil ʿalaynā iṣran ka-mā ḥamaltahu ʿalā alladhīna min qablinā

Translation: Our Lord, do not lay upon us a heavy burden as You laid upon those before us.

Then:

رَبَّنَا وَلَا تُحَمِّلْنَا مَا لَا طَاقَةَ لَنَا بِهِ

rabbanā wa-lā tuḥammilnā mā lā ṭāqata lanā bihi

Translation: Our Lord, do not burden us with what we have no capacity to bear.

In the first, تَحْمِلْ عَلَيْنَا is Form I; إِصْرًا is what the speakers ask not to have laid upon them.

In the second, تُحَمِّلْنَا is Form II, assigning a load or responsibility beyond someone’s capacity.

The two constructions occupy one semantic field, but their morphological difference should be preserved.

إِصْر belongs to another root and concerns a heavy burden or restrictive obligation.

طَاقَة belongs to ط و ق and concerns ability and capacity.

Heaviness and exceeding endurance therefore do not come from ح م ل alone. The object and accompanying words are decisive.

The verse begins with لَا يُكَلِّفُ اللَّهُ نَفْسًا إِلَّا وُسْعَهَا.

The connection requires responsibility and actual human capacity to be examined together.

The root alone cannot make every responsibility necessarily difficult, unjust, or beyond a person’s capacity.

Study of ʾ Ṣ R

Al-Aḥzāb 33:72: bearing the trust

إِنَّا عَرَضْنَا الْأَمَانَةَ عَلَى السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ وَالْجِبَالِ فَأَبَيْنَ أَنْ يَحْمِلْنَهَا وَأَشْفَقْنَ مِنْهَا وَحَمَلَهَا الْإِنْسَانُ إِنَّهُ كَانَ ظَلُومًا جَهُولًا

innā ʿaraḍnā al-amānata ʿalā al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍi wa-l-jibāli fa-abayna an yaḥmilnahā wa-ashfaqna minhā wa-ḥamalahā al-insānu innahu kāna ẓalūman jahūlan

Translation: We offered the trust to the heavens, earth, and mountains; they refused to bear it and feared it, but the human undertook it. He was indeed greatly unjust and ignorant.

The root occurs twice: يَحْمِلْنَهَا and حَمَلَهَا.

Its object is الْأَمَانَةَ, the trust.

Bearing the trust does not mean moving an object on the shoulders or back. It concerns a responsibility undertaken.

The verb itself does not identify the trust’s precise meaning.

Exegetical explanations include obligation, freedom of choice, responsibility, and trusts concerning the relationship between humanity and The God.

That disagreement belongs in an independent study of the trust.

Study of ʾ M N

The verse distinguishes offering the trust, declining to bear it, and undertaking it.

ظَلُومًا جَهُولًا at the end must not become ح م ل’s lexical meaning.

The description contributes to the verse’s judgement concerning humanity; carrying itself does not mean injustice or ignorance.

Al-Jumuʿah 62:5: responsibility assigned but unfulfilled

مَثَلُ الَّذِينَ حُمِّلُوا التَّوْرَاةَ ثُمَّ لَمْ يَحْمِلُوهَا كَمَثَلِ الْحِمَارِ يَحْمِلُ أَسْفَارًا بِئْسَ مَثَلُ الْقَوْمِ الَّذِينَ كَذَّبُوا بِآيَاتِ اللَّهِ وَاللَّهُ لَا يَهْدِي الْقَوْمَ الظَّالِمِينَ

mathalu alladhīna ḥummilū al-tawrāta thumma lam yaḥmilūhā ka-mathali al-ḥimāri yaḥmilu asfāran biʾsa mathalu al-qawmi alladhīna kadhdhabū bi-āyāti allāhi wa-allāhu lā yahdī al-qawma al-ẓālimīna

Translation: Those entrusted with the Torah who then failed to bear it are like a donkey carrying books. Wretched is the likeness of people who denied The God’s signs; The God does not guide wrongdoing people.

The root occurs three times:

First, passive Form II حُمِّلُوا expresses a responsibility assigned to a group.

Second, Form I لَمْ يَحْمِلُوهَا concerns failure to fulfil it.

Third, يَحْمِلُ أَسْفَارًا concerns a donkey physically carrying books.

The verse clearly illustrates carrying’s extension from a material load to nonmaterial responsibility.

Possessing a book or even carrying it on one’s back does not mean accepting and fulfilling responsibility for its contents.

حُمِّلُوا and لَمْ يَحْمِلُوهَا should therefore not receive identical renderings.

The first assigns responsibility; the second describes its nonfulfilment.

The donkey, conversely, physically carries books. Its carrying must not be equated with human ethical responsibility.

The verse also identifies the criticized group as الَّذِينَ كَذَّبُوا بِآيَاتِ اللَّهِ. Its judgement should not be extended indiscriminately to everyone who reads a book or has difficulty understanding it.

Al-Nūr 24:54: the messenger’s and audience’s responsibilities

Al-Nūr 24:54 reads:

فَإِنْ تَوَلَّوْا فَإِنَّمَا عَلَيْهِ مَا حُمِّلَ وَعَلَيْكُمْ مَا حُمِّلْتُمْ

fa-in tawallaw fa-innamā ʿalayhi mā ḥummila wa-ʿalaykum mā ḥummiltum

Meaning: If you turn away, he is responsible only for what he has been charged with, and you for what you have been charged with. تَوَلَّوْا here addresses the audience and is shortened from تَتَوَلَّوْا; it is not a past-tense report that “they turned away.”

The passage contains two passive Form II verbs.

Responsibilities are assigned to both parties without being identified as the same.

The messenger has one responsibility; the audience has a separate one.

حُمِّلَ itself does not establish equal responsibility for everything. Its scope follows its object and context.

Being assigned responsibility also differs from fulfilling it. Someone can face a duty and then choose to fulfil it or turn away.

Al-Ṭabarī: tawallaw addressing the audience and distinct responsibilities

Sin and consequences: bearing burdens and faults

Al-Anʿām 6:31 describes people carrying أَوْزَارَهُمْ on their backs.

Al-Naḥl 16:25 also describes carrying their own burdens fully and some burdens of those they misled without knowledge.

In Ṭā Hā 20:100, one who turns away from the reminder bears a burden on the Day of Resurrection. Ṭā Hā 20:101 describes that burden as حِمْلًا.

Ṭā Hā 20:111 has مَنْ حَمَلَ ظُلْمًا, one who bears wrongdoing.

The burden here is conduct or consequences for which a person is responsible, rather than a physical object.

The root does not itself mean sin or punishment. وِزْر, ظُلْم, and other objects specify the kind of burden.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:112 and Al-Aḥzāb 33:58 use احْتَمَلَ for bearing false accusation and manifest sin.

Their context concerns falsely blaming or harming innocent people.

احْتَمَلَ here therefore concerns responsibility arising from one’s own misconduct, rather than innocently suffering others’ pain.

Can one person bear another’s sin?

In Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:12, a group invites believers to follow its way and promises to bear their sins.

The same verse rejects that claim: they bear none of their followers’ sins.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:13 then says that they will bear their own burdens and further burdens alongside them.

The verses require distinguishing transferred responsibility from additional responsibility incurred by misleading others.

Someone encouraging harmful conduct can be responsible for their role in it. That additional responsibility does not absolve the person who actually performs the conduct.

Fāṭir 35:18 also states that no bearer bears another’s burden; even when a heavily burdened person calls someone to help carry it, nothing is carried, even by a close relative.

حِمْل concerns the person’s burden of responsibility here.

One must distinguish one’s own sin, responsibility for misleading others, and the claim to accept someone else’s sin in their place.

The root alone does not settle these questions, but the image of bearing a load provides a common way to express responsibility.

Ṭā Hā 20:87: the people’s ornaments as loads

Ṭā Hā 20:87 has حُمِّلْنَا أَوْزَارًا مِنْ زِينَةِ الْقَوْمِ.

أَوْزَارًا is associated with the people’s ornaments.

This matters because وِزْر can concern an actual material load in context.

It should not automatically be translated sin or punishment without examining the sentence.

Passive حُمِّلْنَا alone does not establish whether the load was imposed by force, accepted willingly, or assumed otherwise; the account’s context must be examined.

6. Particular Uses and Nearby Roots

Al-Aʿrāf 7:176: تَحْمِلْ عَلَيْهِ as driving away or pressing upon

Al-Aʿrāf 7:176 says of the dog:

إِنْ تَحْمِلْ عَلَيْهِ يَلْهَثْ أَوْ تَتْرُكْهُ يَلْهَثْ

in taḥmil ʿalayhi yalhath aw tatrukhu yalhath

Meaning: If you drive at it, it hangs out its tongue and pants; if you leave it alone, it still pants.

تَحْمِلْ عَلَيْهِ does not mean placing a load on the dog’s back here.

حَمَلَ عَلَى فُلَانٍ can express attacking, driving away, or moving forcefully toward someone.

It demonstrates how the preposition and construction affect the verb’s meaning.

Rendering it “if you place a load on the dog” changes the image.

The root is not inherently violent or aggressive; this is one established usage with عَلَى.

Al-Ṭabarī: ḥamala ʿalayhi in the dog simile

Bearing the Throne

Ghāfir 40:7 mentions those who bear the Throne.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:17 also depicts bearing the Throne in the hereafter.

The root retains carrying and bearing in these passages.

The verb itself cannot determine the Throne’s substance, dimensions, material weight, or manner of existence.

Nor should bearing the Throne become political responsibility, human government, or a particular physical theory without evidence.

The Throne and the unseen scene require an independent study alongside the relevant verses.

The earth and mountains being lifted

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:14 has وَحُمِلَتِ الْأَرْضُ وَالْجِبَالُ.

The passive Form I verb concerns the earth and mountains.

The verse subsequently describes their being crushed.

Lifting or setting something in motion fits the semantic field, but the verb does not explain the event’s physical mechanism.

It therefore cannot establish a definitive scientific model of the earth’s and mountains’ movement in the hereafter.

ح م ل (ḥamala) and و ز ر (wizr)

و ز ر concerns heavy burdens in Arabic and the responsibility and consequences of sin in Qur’anic usage.

ح م ل expresses lifting or bearing a load.

يَحْمِلُونَ أَوْزَارَهُمْ combines the carrying verb with an object from another root.

وِزْر does not name the same kind of burden everywhere, as Ṭā Hā 20:87 demonstrates.

Carrying does not itself mean sin, nor does وِزْر mean the act of carrying.

ح م ل (ḥamala) and ث ق ل (thiql)

ث ق ل concerns heaviness.

ح م ل concerns lifting or bearing something.

In Al-Naḥl 16:7, أَثْقَالَكُمْ names heavy loads and تَحْمِلُ the carrying of them.

مُثْقَلَة in Fāṭir 35:18 likewise describes someone heavily burdened.

Both families can occur in one scene, but not everything carried is heavy.

Study of TH Q L

ح م ل (ḥamala) and ك ل ف (kallafa)

تَكْلِيف concerns assigning someone responsibility or a task.

Nonmaterial carrying can also concern undertaking responsibility.

The two fields come close in Al-Baqarah 2:286.

The distinction nevertheless remains: تَكْلِيف emphasizes the assigned obligation, whereas carrying emphasizes what someone bears or has placed upon them.

They can coincide without being complete synonyms.

ح م ل (ḥamala) and ط و ق (ṭāqah)

طَاقَة concerns ability and capacity to perform something.

ح م ل concerns the actual lifting or bearing of a load.

وَلَا تُحَمِّلْنَا مَا لَا طَاقَةَ لَنَا بِهِ distinguishes them: something can be a load or responsibility, while the capacity to bear it is a separate question.

Occurrence of a carrying verb therefore does not establish that the load is necessarily within, or beyond, someone’s ability.

Study of Ṭ W Q

ح م ل (ḥamala) and ك ف ل (kafala)

كَفَالَة concerns guarantee, guardianship, and accepting responsibility.

Some Arabic uses extend carrying to financial guarantee.

But not every instance of carrying is a guarantee.

Someone can transport a load without becoming its financial or legal guarantor.

Conversely, someone can guarantee another’s obligation without carrying any physical load.

ح م ل (ḥamala) and و ض ع (waḍaʿa)

وَضَعَ tangibly concerns setting down or placing something.

Pregnancy passages place carrying and laying down together.

Fuṣṣilat 41:47 mentions women bearing and delivering.

Al-Ṭalāq 65:4 and Al-Ṭalāq 65:6 likewise describe pregnancy ending through delivery.

They identify two different conditions: bearing the unborn child within the body and that stage ending in birth.

وَضَعَ does not always mean childbirth, just as حَمَلَ does not always mean pregnancy.

ح م ل (ḥamala) and ر ك ب (rakiba)

رُكُوب concerns riding something.

ح م ل can describe someone being transported by a mount or ship.

In Al-Tawbah 9:92, people approach the Prophet to be provided with mounts for a journey. تَحْمِلَهُمْ and أَحْمِلُكُمْ describe providing transport.

Carrying can thus extend to providing the means of travel.

It does not itself mean riding: the carrier and the person carried have different grammatical roles.

7. Translation Proposals for Forqan

For physically carrying objects, “carry,” “lift,” and “transport” are suitable.

تَحْمِلُ أَثْقَالَكُمْ in Al-Naḥl 16:7 is “they carry your heavy loads.”

حِمْلُ بَعِيرٍ in Yūsuf 12:72 is “a camel-load.”

فَاحْتَمَلَ السَّيْلُ زَبَدًا رَابِيًا in Al-Raʿd 13:17 can be “the torrent carried swelling foam.”

Al-Tawbah 9:92 and similar transport passages require distinguishing carrying, riding, and providing a mount.

حَمْل in pregnancy passages can be “pregnancy” or “unborn child,” according to context.

حَمَلَتْ حَمْلًا خَفِيفًا in Al-Aʿrāf 7:189 can be “she bore a light pregnancy,” or, in fluent prose, “she began an easy pregnancy.”

فَحَمَلَتْهُ in Maryam 19:22 suits “she conceived him,” whereas تَحْمِلُهُ in Maryam 19:27 is “she was carrying him.”

أُولَاتُ الْأَحْمَالِ in Al-Ṭalāq 65:4 is “pregnant women”; يَضَعْنَ حَمْلَهُنَّ is “give birth.”

For responsibility, “undertake,” “assign,” and “bear the burden of responsibility” are suitable.

مَا حُمِّلَ in Al-Nūr 24:54 is “what he has been charged with”; مَا حُمِّلْتُمْ is “what you have been charged with.”

Al-Jumuʿah 62:5 should distinguish حُمِّلُوا التَّوْرَاةَ, “were entrusted with the Torah,” from لَمْ يَحْمِلُوهَا, “did not bear it” or “failed to fulfil their responsibility toward it.”

حَمَلَهَا الْإِنْسَانُ in Al-Aḥzāb 33:72 is “the human undertook it”; the trust’s precise meaning requires separate explanation.

احْتَمَلَ in Al-Nisāʾ 4:112 and Al-Aḥzāb 33:58 can be “has borne the burden of false accusation and manifest sin.”

إِنْ تَحْمِلْ عَلَيْهِ in Al-Aʿrāf 7:176 suits “if you drive at it,” referring to attacking or driving away rather than loading the animal’s back.

الْحَامِلَاتِ وِقْرًا in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:2 suits the basic “bearers of a heavy load”; clouds or winds should be identified separately in commentary.

حَمَّالَةَ الْحَطَبِ in Al-Masad 111:4 directly suits “firewood carrier” or “one who carries much firewood.” The figurative tale-bearing reading and relationship to punishment belong in a separate study of ح ط ب.

Study of Ḥ Ṭ B

Conclusion

Brief root meaning: Lifting, carrying, and bearing something; from physical loads and pregnancy to responsibility, commitment, and the consequences of deeds.

Physical carriers can be people, animals, ships, or flowing water; their loads may be goods, people, torrent foam, or something else.

Pregnancy extends the family to bearing the unborn child within the mother’s body. Trees can also bear fruit. حَمْل and حِمْل help distinguish these uses, but no exceptionless rule explains every form and dialect.

Nonmaterial carrying extends to responsibility, commitment, trust, and consequences.

The trust, scripture, and consequences passages show that being assigned responsibility, accepting it, and properly fulfilling it are different concepts.

Al-Jumuʿah 62:5 describes people entrusted with scripture who failed to bear it properly; Al-Aḥzāb 33:72 concerns bearing the trust; Al-Nūr 24:54 distinguishes messenger and audience responsibilities.

In sin passages, carrying concerns consequences borne by someone. The image does not allow anyone to remove another’s responsibility merely by promising or claiming to do so.

Conversely, احْتَمَلَ in Al-Raʿd 13:17 shows that Form VIII does not invariably imply ethical judgement or enduring suffering: a torrent physically carries foam.

تَحْمِلْ عَلَيْهِ in Al-Aʿrāf 7:176 also shows how combining a verb with a preposition can create a particular sense such as attacking or driving away.

The semantic core is lifting and bearing something, whether a physical load, an unborn child, responsibility, commitment, or consequences. Each construction and context determines the load, carrier, direction of the relationship, and result.

This does not make all carrying difficult or all loads ethical responsibilities. Carrying may be useful and easy, concern a demanding responsibility, or provide an image for sin and its consequences.

For Forqan, the principal equivalents are “carry,” “lift,” “pregnancy,” “undertake,” and “assign.” Morphology, the verb’s object, and context determine the choice.

The central distinction is that having a load, being assigned it, and fulfilling its responsibility are not the same. Preserving it clarifies tangible carrying and prevents confusing ethical responsibility with merely moving an object.
