Root S K B: pouring, flowing, and the movement of liquids

The Arabic root S K B concerns pouring liquids and making them flow. Sakaba al-māʾa means “poured the water”; insakaba al-māʾu means “the water poured out and flowed”; and maskūb means poured out or made to flow.

The family is chiefly used for water, rain, tears, and blood. Its forms describe sustained rainfall, water flowing over the ground, and tears running from the eyes.

Pouring also extends figuratively: a swift horse may move as fluidly as running water, or speech may pour continuously from the tongue.

The root occurs only once in the Quran, as maskūbin in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:31. The verse describes flowing water among the Garden's blessings.

The water is described by its state and movement. Maskūb is not a special kind of water; it describes water poured out and flowing.

Understanding it requires distinguishing water, pouring, flow, and features such as abundance, continuity, and accessibility. These can be related without all belonging to the root's direct meaning.

1. The root in early Arabic

Sakb: pouring liquid and making it flow

Classical Arabic defines al-sakb as ṣabb al-māʾ, pouring water.

Sakaba al-māʾa describes someone pouring water from its container or location and making it flow.

The verb can also concern liquids such as blood, tears, and oil.

The liquid leaves its previous state and flows onto a surface or along another course.

Water may be poured from a vessel onto the ground, run down from somewhere, or move in a continuous stream.

These share an image of pouring and flowing, though volume, intensity, and duration need not be the same.

One lexical definition states the root's basic sense as follows:

«السِّينُ وَالْكَافُ وَالْبَاءُ أَصْلٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى صَبِّ الشَّيْءِ»

al-sīnu wa-al-kāfu wa-al-bāʾu aṣlun yadullu ʿalā ṣabbi al-shayʾ

“Sīn, kāf, and bāʾ have a basic sense indicating the pouring of something.”

This clarifies the central meaning: pouring something and setting it flowing, especially a liquid.

Arabic attests both transitive and intransitive uses of the verb.

Sakaba al-māʾa means “he poured the water.”

Sakaba al-māʾu can also mean “the water poured out and flowed.”

Insakaba likewise describes liquid pouring out or flowing.

In the first construction, an agent pours; in the second, the liquid itself is described as flowing.

The root therefore does not imply that every flow of water results from direct pouring by a human.

A poetic witness attributed to the pre-Islamic period

A poem attributed to Janūb, sister of ʿAmr Dhū al-Kalb al-Hudhalī, reads:

وَالطَّاعِنُ الطَّعْنَةَ النَّجْلَاءَ يَتْبَعُهَا
مُثْعَنْجِرٌ مِنْ دَمِ الْأَجْوَافِ أُسْكُوبُ

wa-al-ṭāʿinu al-ṭaʿnata al-najlāʾa yatbaʿuhā / muthʿanjirun min dami al-ajwāfi uskūbu

“The spearman delivers the gaping thrust, followed by a surging stream of blood pouring from within the body.”

Uskūb describes flowing, pouring blood here.

Al-ṭaʿnah al-najlāʾ denotes a broad, gaping wound from a thrust; muthʿanjir describes blood surging from it, one portion following another.

Uskūb thus concerns liquid pouring out from a place and flowing.

The family was not restricted to pouring water from vessels: blood could also be described with it.

Janūb is presented as a pre-Islamic woman poet, and her lament for her brother is transmitted in poetic and lexical traditions.

Another reading replaces uskūb with uthʿūb. This variation affects the witness: uskūb should not be presented as the poem's sole, certain original wording.

Despite the variant, the witness matters for uskūb in the early lexical tradition; the date of the original wording is not independently established.

Uskūb in an image of lightning and rain

A hemistich attributed to Zuhayr ibn ʿUrwah al-Māzinī, described as a pre-Islamic poet nicknamed al-Sakb, reads:

بَرْقٌ يُضِيءُ أَمَامَ الْبَيْتِ أُسْكُوبُ

barqun yuḍīʾu amāma al-bayti uskūbu

“Lightning shines before the house, as though pouring down rain.”

Some versions have khilāla al-bayt instead of amāma al-bayt.

Uskūb grammatically describes the lightning in this quotation. One lexical explanation understands it as though the lightning pours rain; the association with water comes through the poetic image, not literal liquid lightning.

This witness illustrates an extension of pouring imagery to lightning and rain. It is not a direct report of water being poured from a vessel.

Al-sakb and al-uskūb are also attested for sustained rainfall.

Continuity belongs to these uses, but not every derivative of S K B necessarily means uninterrupted rain.

Nor does uskūb in this poem confine the whole root to rain or lightning.

Zuhayr ibn ʿUrwah is described in literary accounts as pre-Islamic, and the nickname al-Sakb is associated with this line. These reports survive in later sources; the exact dates of composition and recording are not independently established.

Together the witnesses show pouring and flowing imagery applied to blood and to a poetic description of lightning associated with rain.

The defensible basic meaning remains pouring and liquid flow, without requiring a flood, extreme intensity, or permanent flow in every use.

2. Forms and semantic branches

Pouring, flowing, and descriptions of flow

Arabic has several familiar forms in the S K B family.

Sakaba: poured or made flow, as with water and tears.

Sakb: a verbal noun meaning pouring liquid or making it flow.

Sukūb: pouring and flowing, especially where the liquid itself flows.

Taskāb: an attested verbal noun for pouring or flowing.

Insakaba: poured out or flowed.

Sākib: an active participle; pouring, or something from which liquid flows.

Sakūb: an adjective for flowing water or pouring rain.

Uskūb: an adjective for pouring rain, liquid flow, and some related phenomena.

Maskūb: a passive participle; poured out or made to flow.

These share a semantic field but differ grammatically.

Sākib concerns the agent or subject of pouring; maskūb concerns what has been poured.

This matters because the Quran says māʾin maskūbin, not māʾin sākibin.

Water-related descriptions can nevertheless overlap through the sense of flow. Their morphological differences do not warrant physical or philosophical conclusions beyond the evidence.

Sakb describing a swift horse

Early Arabic uses farasun sakb for a swift, agile horse.

A lexical explanation relates this to flowing water, as though the horse moves forward with the fluidity of a stream.

The comparison evokes swift, continuous movement.

The horse does not literally become liquid; the resemblance concerns fluidity and speed.

Farasun sakb therefore means “a swift horse,” not “a poured-out horse.”

A root describing liquid pouring can thus characterize another creature's movement.

Sharing an image does not make sakb a term for every rapid movement.

Other lexical uses

Al-sakb is also recorded for a fine fabric, whose thinness and delicacy are compared to flowing water.

Al-sakab, with a vowel on the kāf, names a fragrant plant. Al-sakb is also recorded for copper, and al-sakab for lead; these vocalizations should be distinguished.

Iskābah denotes a small wooden piece used to close or repair a tear in a waterskin. It is also recorded for a disc placed in a funnel used for oil or similar liquids.

Lexicons therefore record varied uses under this family.

Shared form does not prove that the plant, metal, and every implement were all named from flowing water.

Some relationships are explicable, such as the comparison of delicate cloth to water; other historical connections are unclear.

These should be recognized as attested branches rather than forced into an unproven single origin.

3. Māʾin maskūbin: the sole Quranic occurrence

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:31 states:

وَمَاءٍ مَسْكُوبٍ

wa-māʾin maskūb

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:31

“And flowing water.”

This is the only Quranic occurrence of S K B.

Maskūbin is the passive participle of the basic triliteral verb sakaba.

It is masculine singular, indefinite, and genitive, modifying māʾin.

Māʾ names water; maskūb describes its condition.

Maskūb does not denote a different substance: it is water described as poured out and flowing.

The direct meaning of maskūb

Morphologically, maskūb means poured out.

With water, it can depict water set flowing and in motion.

A merely literal Persian equivalent of “poured-out water” may suggest water emptied once from a container and left on the ground.

Here water belongs among the Garden's blessings, where its flow is prominent.

“Flowing water” is therefore natural and appropriate. A lexical explanation preserves its connection with pouring and liquid movement.

Two matters should nevertheless be distinguished:

First, pouring and flowing themselves.

Second, the water's quantity and the flow's duration.

Maskūb alone specifies neither volume, source, nor how long the flow continues.

Those features require context and other evidence.

The verse within Al-Wāqiʿah

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:27 introduces the companions of the right.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:28 then states:

فِي سِدْرٍ مَخْضُودٍ

fī sidrin makhḍūd

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:28

“Among thornless lote trees.”

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:29 states:

وَطَلْحٍ مَنْضُودٍ

wa-ṭalḥin manḍūd

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:29

“And ṭalḥ trees with closely arranged fruit.”

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:30 states:

وَظِلٍّ مَمْدُودٍ

wa-ẓillin mamdūd

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:30

“And extended shade.”

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:31 then describes flowing water, followed in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:32 by:

وَفَاكِهَةٍ كَثِيرَةٍ

wa-fākihatin kathīrah

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:32

“And abundant fruit.”

Together these depict an environment supplied with trees, shade, water, and food.

Each blessing receives an appropriate description.

The lote trees are makhḍūd.

The ṭalḥ is manḍūd.

The shade is mamdūd.

The water is maskūb.

The fruit is kathīrah.

The differing adjectives show that the blessings are not all described by one generic quality such as abundance; each has a particular feature highlighted.

Maskūb here describes the water's flow.

Flowing water and access to it

Water is a basic need of life.

Access may be difficult: water can be in deep wells, enclosed reservoirs, or places requiring effort and tools to extract and deliver it.

Flowing water instead evokes liquid in motion rather than stagnant or confined in place.

Among shade and abundant fruit, māʾin maskūbin therefore contributes to an image of ease and provision.

Easy access is not its direct lexical meaning, however; it is an implication of water's place among the Garden's blessings.

The word itself concerns pouring and flowing.

Earthly water may flow powerfully while being difficult or dangerous to drink from. Flow alone does not establish safe access.

Lexical meaning and the image of a blessing should therefore be distinguished.

Does maskūb mean uninterrupted flow?

Some early explanations understand māʾin maskūbin as water that flows continually without interruption.

This suits the Garden's image of abundance and ease.

Lexically, however, maskūb does not itself include “always” or “without any interruption.”

Water poured from a vessel can also be maskūb even if the pouring is brief.

Sukūb and uskūb can describe sustained rainfall in some Arabic uses, but that feature does not impose one meaning on every form.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:33 says of the fruit:

لَا مَقْطُوعَةٍ وَلَا مَمْنُوعَةٍ

lā maqṭūʿatin wa-lā mamnūʿah

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:33

“Neither cut off nor withheld.”

This highlights the fruit's continuity and availability; its qualifications should not automatically become part of maskūb's lexical meaning.

“Flowing water” is therefore suitable for the basic translation. The continuity of the Garden's blessings is studied through the verses together, not through this passive participle alone.

Must it flow without a channel?

Some lexical and interpretive explanations describe māʾin maskūbin as water flowing over the ground without a dug channel or fixed watercourse.

That depicts freely flowing, accessible water.

Maskūb alone, however, does not state the absence of a channel, river, or particular course.

Water can be poured from a vessel, flow over the ground, or follow a defined route.

Pouring and flowing are conceivable in each case.

The expression therefore does not establish that the Garden's water necessarily moves without a bed or route, or contrary to every known feature of liquids.

The verse does not supply those details.

Conversely, actual water flow should not be erased in favor of purely abundant blessing or spiritual satisfaction.

The verse names water and gives it a flow-related description.

Does maskūb mean a waterfall?

A waterfall clearly exemplifies pouring water descending from a height.

But not all maskūb water is a waterfall.

Water may be poured from a vessel or flow down a gentle slope without falling from a substantial height.

“Waterfall” selects only one possible image and narrows the expression's range.

The verse does not specify the source's elevation or the shape of its course.

“Flowing water” avoids this restriction and better serves the basic translation.

4. Comparison with related roots

S K B and Ṣ B B: pouring and pouring down

The closest root is Ṣ B B.

Ṣabba concerns pouring and causing something to run down.

ʿAbasa 80:25 states:

أَنَّا صَبَبْنَا الْمَاءَ صَبًّا

annā ṣababnā al-māʾa ṣabbā

ʿAbasa 80:25

“That We poured down water in a pouring.”

Ṣababnā expresses pouring water, while the verbal noun ṣabban emphasizes the action.

Sakaba also concerns pouring water and is lexically explained through ṣabb.

The families therefore have clear semantic overlap.

Yet māʾin maskūbin in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:31 describes water's poured-out, flowing state, while ṣababnā al-māʾa in ʿAbasa 80:25 attributes pouring to an agent.

This difference also comes from verb versus participle, not an absolute boundary between the roots themselves.

No rule should therefore make ṣabb invariably forceful and sakb invariably gentle; lexical evidence does not establish such an absolute contrast.

S K B and D F Q: pouring and gushing

D F Q concerns liquid emerging and pouring, particularly with force.

Al-Ṭāriq 86:6 states:

خُلِقَ مِنْ مَاءٍ دَافِقٍ

khuliqa min māʾin dāfiq

Al-Ṭāriq 86:6

“He was created from a gushing fluid.”

Dāfiq describes the fluid and highlights its movement and emergence.

S K B also concerns liquid flow, but does not itself require forceful gushing.

Water gently poured from a container can be maskūb, just as pressurized water can be poured.

Dafq more specifically evokes gushing or spurting.

Maskūb in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:31 should not become “gushing under pressure” without evidence.

S K B and TH J J: flow and abundant pouring

TH J J concerns abundant, forceful pouring of liquid.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:14 states:

وَأَنْزَلْنَا مِنَ الْمُعْصِرَاتِ مَاءً ثَجَّاجًا

wa-anzalnā mina al-muʿṣirāti māʾan thajjājā

Al-Nabaʾ 78:14

“We sent down abundant pouring water from rain-bearing clouds.”

Thajjājan highlights abundant, intense rainfall.

Maskūb can describe flowing, pouring water without necessarily specifying a very large volume.

“Abundant pouring water” can fit some contexts, but abundance should not be extracted from S K B alone.

S K B and J R Y: pouring and flowing along a course

Jarā concerns flowing and moving along a course.

The Quran applies the root to rivers, ships, the sun, and other phenomena.

Water-related S K B can likewise concern flow.

There is nevertheless a recognizable distinction.

Sakb primarily concerns pouring liquid and setting it flowing; jary has a wider range of movement along a course.

The sun can tajrī in Quranic usage without being poured out like water.

Conversely, poured water can subsequently flow.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:31 presents māʾin maskūbin, while Muḥammad 47:15 describes rivers of water. The images overlap in water and flow, but the words retain their distinctions.

Maskūb concerns pouring and flow; nahr concerns a watercourse or established stream.

Maskūb alone does not imply a particular river, just as nahr does not itself explain how water pours from its source.

S K B and F Y Ḍ: pouring and overflowing

F Y Ḍ concerns increase, overflowing, and flow beyond a usual capacity or measure.

Al-Māʾidah 5:83 uses it for tears flowing from the eyes.

Sakb can also describe flowing tears.

The roots therefore overlap in this setting.

Fayḍ can highlight overflowing abundance, whereas sakb more directly concerns liquid pouring and flowing.

Again, the difference is not absolute: both may describe one event from different aspects.

S K B and S Q Y: flowing water and giving water

S Q Y concerns giving water, providing a drink, or supplying water to another.

Sakb concerns pouring liquid and making it flow.

Someone may give another water to drink without pouring it on the ground.

Someone may also empty a vessel without anyone drinking from it.

Giving water and pouring it are related but distinct acts.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:31 describes water as maskūb; the phrase itself contains no verb of giving a drink to someone.

“Water they are given to drink” would therefore add an explanation beyond the direct wording.

5. Translation proposals for Forqan

Translation should preserve the two ideas of pouring liquid and its flowing.

For sakaba, “pour” or “make flow” is basic.

Insakaba can be “pour out,” “run down,” or “flow.”

For Quranic maskūb, both the passive formation and the image of flowing water matter.

In Al-Wāqiʿah 56:31:

وَمَاءٍ مَسْكُوبٍ

wa-māʾin maskūb

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:31

Suggested translation:

“And flowing water.”

This is brief, natural, and consistent with the Garden's blessings.

“And cascading water” is also defensible, but can suggest falling from a height more strongly.

“And poured-out water” follows the passive formation more closely, yet may suggest water poured once and left on the ground.

“Flowing water” is therefore preferable for the basic translation, with pouring explained in the lexical discussion.

The following alternatives provide more information than the word directly conveys:

“Water that never ceases”: endless continuity does not come from the root alone.

“Water flowing without channels or rivers”: the watercourse is unspecified.

“A tall, cascading waterfall”: height and the shape of the fall are unstated.

“Water that reaches people without any effort”: ease of access is contextual, not the direct meaning of maskūb.

Other Arabic forms can be translated as follows:

Sakaba al-māʾa: poured the water.

Sakaba al-damʿa: shed tears.

Insakaba al-māʾu: the water poured out or flowed.

Sakb: pouring liquid or making it flow.

Sukūb: pouring and flow.

Sākib: pouring or making flow, according to the subject.

Māʾun sakūbun: flowing, pouring water.

Maṭarun uskūbun: sustained pouring rain.

Farasun sakbun: a swift, agile horse.

Maskūb: poured out or made to flow.

Figurative uses should likewise convey the intended sense naturally. Farasun sakb is not “a poured-out horse”; speed and fluidity of movement are intended.

Plant names and other objects recorded under sakb should not become flowing water without evidence.

Conclusion

S K B concerns pouring liquids and making them flow.

This is clear in uses involving water, rain, blood, and tears. In the poem attributed to Janūb, uskūb describes pouring blood; the quotation attributed to Zuhayr ibn ʿUrwah uses the family in imagery associated with lightning and rain.

The semantic field therefore extends beyond pouring water from a container.

Arabic attests transitive and intransitive uses: sakaba al-māʾa means pouring water, and insakaba al-māʾu means water pouring out and flowing.

Extended usage can explain a horse's speed or fluent speech through liquid flow. Such uses should not become necessary meanings of every derivative.

The root occurs only once in the Quran, as maskūbin in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:31.

The verse belongs among the blessings of the companions of the right: trees, extended shade, flowing water, and abundant fruit.

Maskūb describes the water's poured-out, flowing state.

It contributes to ease and provision in the Garden, but not every feature of that blessing comes from this one word.

Maskūb alone specifies neither volume, height, absence of channels, source, nor exact duration.

The central meaning can therefore be stated as follows:

S K B denotes pouring liquid and making it flow, or, in intransitive forms, its pouring out and flowing. Maskūb describes something poured out and flowing; in its Quranic occurrence, it describes flowing water among the Garden's blessings.

For Forqan, “flowing water” is the most suitable basic equivalent of māʾin maskūbin.

Water is presented not merely as an existing substance but through its flowing condition.

In Al-Wāqiʿah, together with shade and abundant fruit, this forms part of a well-provided, restful environment. The root's own meaning remains clear and limited: pouring and liquid flow.
