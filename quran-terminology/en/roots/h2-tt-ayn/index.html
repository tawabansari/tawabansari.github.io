Root H Ṭ ʿ — Hastening toward something and fixed attention

Qur’anic and lexical forms

مُهْطِعِينَ
(muhṭiʿīna)
Hastening toward; plural active participle

From rapid movement and a fixed gaze to human responses to a summons or confrontation

The Arabic triliteral root ه ط ع concerns turning toward something, moving swiftly in a particular direction, and sustained attention to a person or destination. Some usages emphasize bodily movement; others the eyes, head, and neck.

The Qur’an uses the root three times, always as مُهْطِعِينَ. Two occurrences concern resurrection scenes; the third describes some of Muhammad’s opponents approaching him.

Muhṭiʿ does not invariably mean frightened, humiliated, or compelled. A person may hasten toward something, gaze intently, or approach with neck extended and eyes fixed. Fear, humiliation, or arrogance can arise from accompanying descriptions rather than the root alone.

1. The root in early Arabic

Classical Arabic records هَطَعَ and أَهْطَعَ, with partly overlapping usages.

هَطَعَ بِبَصَرِهِ means directing one’s gaze toward something without looking away, emphasizing sustained visual attention.

أَهْطَعَ also denotes approaching a destination swiftly, of a person or animal moving quickly toward something.

Some lexicons associate haṭʿ and ihṭāʿ with fear, submission, or obedience; others describe an extended neck, a lowered head, or fixed eyes.

These explanations differ and should not all become mandatory components of every occurrence.

Hastening can arise from fear, eagerness, curiosity, or response to a summons. Fixed attention can accompany fear, surprise, or concentration.

Two principal branches are thus recognizable: approaching or moving quickly toward something, and directing sustained visual attention toward it.

A witness attributed to pre-Qur’anic poetry

A line sometimes attributed in literary and lexical collections to a Ḥimyarite Tubbaʿ king reads:

«تَعَبَّدَنِي نِمْرُ بْنُ سَعْدٍ وَقَدْ أَرَى
وَنِمْرُ بْنُ سَعْدٍ لِي مُطِيعٌ وَمُهْطِعُ»

Taʿabbadanī Nimru bnu Saʿdin wa-qad arā / wa-Nimru bnu Saʿdin lī muṭīʿun wa-muhṭiʿu.

“Nimr ibn Saʿd has made me his servant, though once I saw Nimr ibn Saʿd obedient and submissive to me.”

مُهْطِع alongside مُطِيع describes obedience, attention, and submission to another.

It illustrates the branch in which a person turns attentively toward another in submission.

The attribution is uncertain. Some citations name no poet; variants include وَقَدْ أَرَى versus وَقَدْ دَرَى, and مُطِيعٌ versus مَدِينٌ.

It witnesses a recorded lexical usage, but neither its exact date nor its pre-Islamic origin is securely established.

An early Islamic poetic witness

The early Islamic poet Yazīd ibn Mufarrigh al-Ḥimyarī is credited with:

«بِدِجْلَةَ أَهْلُهَا وَلَقَدْ أَرَاهُمْ
بِدِجْلَةَ مُهْطِعِينَ إِلَى السَّمَاعِ»

Bi-Dijlata ahluhā wa-laqad arāhum / bi-Dijlata muhṭiʿīna ilā s-samāʿi.

“Its people are by the Tigris; indeed I have seen them by the Tigris hastening attentively to listen.”

Some versions have دَارُهُمْ instead of أَهْلُهَا.

مُهْطِعِينَ إِلَى السَّمَاعِ describes eager, attentive movement toward listening.

It contains no explicit punishment, terror, or humiliation. Muhṭiʿīn describes their movement or attention toward listening.

The poet lived after Islam’s emergence, so this is not direct pre-Islamic evidence. It illustrates usage in a period close to the Qur’an’s revelation.

These witnesses and lexical reports prevent defining the root solely as being afraid or humiliated.

Describing camels

بَعِيرٌ مُهْطِعٌ can describe a camel extending its neck forward or downward.

أَهْطَعَ الْبَعِيرُ فِي سَيْرِهِ is also recorded for a camel accelerating its pace.

These usages concern animal posture and direction of movement.

They do not establish that every human muhṭiʿ must lower the neck. Head and neck position depend on the accompanying description.

This matters in Ibrāhīm 14:43, where muhṭiʿīn accompanies a separate description of raised heads.

2. Qur’anic forms and derivatives

The root occurs three times, in one morphological form:

  • مُهْطِعِينَ — Ibrāhīm 14:43: people’s condition on the Day of Account.
  • مُهْطِعِينَ — al-Qamar 54:8: movement toward the caller.
  • مُهْطِعِينَ — al-Maʿārij 70:36: opponents approaching Muhammad.

مُهْطِع is the Form IV active participle of أَهْطَعَ. مُهْطِعِينَ is its sound masculine plural in the accusative or genitive form.

In all three verses, it is accusative and describes the people’s circumstances or state.

Arabic also records هَطَعَ, يَهْطَعُ, أَهْطَعَ, يُهْطِعُ, إِهْطَاع, هُطُوع, and اِسْتَهْطَعَ; these exact forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

The Qur’an uses only the plural active participle to describe how a group approaches or turns toward something.

The participial form alone establishes neither permanent movement nor a fixed intention or emotion in every instance.

3. Muhṭiʿīn — Haste and fixed eyes on the Day of Account

Ibrāhīm 14:43

«مُهْطِعِينَ مُقْنِعِي رُءُوسِهِمْ لَا يَرْتَدُّ إِلَيْهِمْ طَرْفُهُمْ وَأَفْئِدَتُهُمْ هَوَاءٌ»

Muhṭiʿīna muqniʿī ruʾūsihim lā yartaddu ilayhim ṭarfuhum wa-afʾidatuhum hawāʾ.

“Hastening, with their heads raised; their gaze does not return to them, and their hearts are empty.”

Ibrāhīm 14:42 warns against imagining The God unaware of wrongdoers’ deeds. They are deferred until a day when eyes stare fixedly.

Ibrāhīm 14:43 describes their condition through successive expressions.

First comes مُهْطِعِينَ, then مُقْنِعِي رُءُوسِهِمْ, followed by لَا يَرْتَدُّ إِلَيْهِمْ طَرْفُهُمْ and وَأَفْئِدَتُهُمْ هَوَاءٌ.

Each contributes separately to the scene.

مُهْطِعِينَ — Movement or staring?

Two principal lexical readings are offered.

The first is rapid movement toward a destination or impending scene.

The second is an unbroken gaze directed toward something.

Both have support in Arabic usage.

Since fixed eyes have already been mentioned and the gaze is immediately described again, “hastening” can preserve muhṭiʿīn’s distinct contribution more clearly.

This does not exclude the gaze reading. Rapid movement and fixed attention can coexist; a basic translation need not compress every possible feature into one word.

مُقْنِعِي رُءُوسِهِمْ — The position of the heads

مُقْنِعِي belongs to ق ن ع, not ه ط ع.

Here the phrase denotes raising the heads and directing them toward something.

A camel description involving a downward neck therefore does not require lowered human heads here.

The verse specifies head position separately through مُقْنِعِي.

This illustrates why root meaning must be distinguished from accompanying descriptions.

لَا يَرْتَدُّ إِلَيْهِمْ طَرْفُهُمْ — Their gaze does not return

Ṭarf here concerns looking and eye movement.

Their eyes are so fixed on the scene that their gaze does not return to its ordinary condition.

This connects with the staring eyes of Ibrāhīm 14:42.

The scene combines haste or intense attention, raised heads, fixed eyes, and empty hearts.

Together these convey terror and disturbance; the whole image is not the lexical definition of muhṭiʿīn.

وَأَفْئِدَتُهُمْ هَوَاءٌ — Empty hearts

أَفْئِدَة belongs to another root, and هَوَاء is also a separate word.

Empty hearts can portray terror, turmoil, and loss of inner stability in this context.

This intensifies the description, but haṭʿ itself does not mean an emptied heart.

The imagery is not evidence for an otherwise unsupported physical theory about brains, eyes, or hearts at resurrection. It presents their condition in intelligible descriptive language.

4. Muhṭiʿīn — Approaching the caller

Al-Qamar 54:8

«مُّهْطِعِينَ إِلَى الدَّاعِ يَقُولُ الْكَافِرُونَ هَٰذَا يَوْمٌ عَسِرٌ»

Muhṭiʿīna ilā d-dāʿi yaqūlu l-kāfirūna hādhā yawmun ʿasir.

“Hastening toward the caller; the disbelievers say: This is a difficult day.”

مُهْطِعِينَ accompanies إِلَى الدَّاعِ.

إِلَى specifies direction; الدَّاعِ identifies the one calling.

“Hastening toward the caller” is therefore a clear, natural rendering here.

Connection with the preceding verses

Al-Qamar 54:6 describes a day when the caller summons people to something unfamiliar and alarming.

In al-Qamar 54:7, people emerge from graves with lowered eyes, compared to scattered locusts.

Al-Qamar 54:8 then says مُهْطِعِينَ إِلَى الدَّاعِ.

Those emerging turn toward the caller and move rapidly in that direction.

The locust comparison depicts collective movement and the scene’s extent; إِلَى الدَّاعِ specifies its direction.

Here the construction thus conveys movement toward a destination, not merely standing and staring.

Does haṭʿ mean being afraid?

The verse ends with the disbelievers saying: “This is a difficult day.”

Their words show that they face a hard and frightening situation.

Fear and difficulty arise from their speech and the resurrection setting; مُهْطِعِينَ does not necessarily mean “the frightened.”

Translating it only as “terrified” weakens the movement toward the caller.

The final statement is expressly attributed to الْكَافِرُونَ. This designation and their assessment should not be extended to everyone present without further evidence.

The final sentence names the speakers as disbelievers; identifying all participants in the scene requires examining the wider verses.

5. Muhṭiʿīn — Opponents approaching Muhammad

Al-Maʿārij 70:36

«فَمَالِ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا قِبَلَكَ مُهْطِعِينَ»

Fa-māli lladhīna kafarū qibalaka muhṭiʿīn.

“What is the matter with those who disbelieve, hastening toward you?”

Unlike the other two instances, this verse does not directly describe resurrection.

It addresses Muhammad concerning people present before him.

قِبَلَكَ here means “toward you” or “before you.” It is distinct from قَبْلَكَ, “before you” in time.

مُهْطِعِينَ describes their manner of approach.

The group’s behavior in context

Al-Maʿārij 70:37 describes them in groups on the right and left.

Al-Maʿārij 70:38 asks whether each expects admission to a garden of delight.

This sequence places al-Maʿārij 70:36 among opponents approaching or gathering around Muhammad.

They may listen, watch, show curiosity, or attend to his words; muhṭiʿīn itself does not establish acceptance of his call.

Since they are described as الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا, their approach is not automatically evidence of faith or sincere eagerness for guidance.

Haste or concentrated attention belongs directly to the root; their stance toward the message follows from the disbelief description and context.

Why this use matters for the definition

An invariably frightened and humiliated muhṭiʿ would fit al-Maʿārij 70:36 poorly.

The opponents approach Muhammad and gather in groups; the verse alone does not say they fear him or submit to him.

Their presence may involve opposition or mockery, but that requires independent contextual evidence.

The verse supports approach and directed attention within the root’s range, with emotion determined by context.

6. Common meaning and comparison with nearby roots

Across the three Qur’anic usages, a person turns through movement or gaze toward a particular destination, directing attention that way.

Ibrāhīm 14:43 presents haste and staring in the accounting scene.

Al-Qamar 54:8 emphasizes movement toward the caller.

Al-Maʿārij 70:36 describes opponents approaching Muhammad with haste and attention.

This shared tendency does not mean speed, staring, submission, fear, and an extended neck must always coexist.

ه ط ع and س ر ع — Directed haste and speed

س ر ع denotes speed and haste.

Muhṭiʿ is often translated “hastening,” but directed approach and attention also matter in its recorded uses.

Al-Qamar 54:8 involves speed, while إِلَى الدَّاعِ supplies the destination.

This is a usage tendency, not proof that س ر ع never has direction or that ه ط ع always fully expresses both speed and staring.

ه ط ع and ه ر ع — Haste and impelled movement

ه ر ع also concerns swift movement.

Hūd 11:78 uses يُهْرَعُونَ إِلَيْهِ of people rushing toward Lot.

Lexical explanations associate that verb with rapid movement, sometimes being driven or impelled toward something.

Haṭʿ can likewise denote haste, while its Qur’anic uses also raise questions of visual attention and direction.

Their similarity does not transfer every feature of one root to the other. Each must be read with its participants, destination, and context.

ه ط ع and و ف ض — Rapid movement toward a destination

Al-Maʿārij 70:43 depicts people emerging rapidly from graves, as though racing toward an erected marker.

It uses يُوفِضُونَ from و ف ض.

Its rapid, directed movement resembles مُهْطِعِينَ إِلَى الدَّاعِ in al-Qamar 54:8.

The roots remain independent. Wafḍ here denotes haste; the haṭʿ family additionally includes sustained visual attention.

Similar scenes do not make the words synonymous in every usage.

ه ط ع and خ ش ع — Directed attention and humility

Al-Qamar 54:7 has خُشَّعًا أَبْصَارُهُمْ; al-Qamar 54:8 then uses مُهْطِعِينَ.

خ ش ع concerns humility, lowering, and submission.

Haṭʿ concerns approach, haste, or fixed attention in Qur’anic usage.

Someone may both lower their gaze and hasten toward the caller without making the two roots identical.

Al-Maʿārij 70:36 calls Muhammad’s opponents muhṭiʿīn without itself describing them as humble.

Humility is therefore not a necessary component of every muhṭiʿ.

ه ط ع and ه ل ع — Hasty approach and restlessness

ه ل ع resembles ه ط ع in writing but is an independent root.

Al-Maʿārij 70:19 uses هَلُوعًا; al-Maʿārij 70:20 and al-Maʿārij 70:21 explain distress at adversity and withholding when good comes.

Halaʿ concerns inner disposition and response there; haṭʿ in al-Maʿārij 70:36 describes opponents approaching.

Shared chapter placement or similar letters do not justify merging meanings.

7. Interpretive challenges and suggested translations

مُهْطِعِينَ has several nearby Arabic usages, which cannot all be expressed simultaneously by one short equivalent.

“Hastening,” “gazing intently,” “approaching,” and “with necks extended” each reflect part of its recorded range.

The verse’s construction and context determine the choice.

مُهْطِعِينَ — Ibrāhīm 14:43

“Hastening”

The complete translation can read:

“Hastening, with their heads raised; their gaze does not return to them, and their hearts are empty.”

Raised heads come from مُقْنِعِي رُءُوسِهِمْ and fixed eyes from لَا يَرْتَدُّ إِلَيْهِمْ طَرْفُهُمْ; neither needs repeating inside muhṭiʿīn.

مُهْطِعِينَ — al-Qamar 54:8

“Hastening toward the caller”

This fits إِلَى الدَّاعِ and preserves direction.

“Terrified” may explain the scene but is insufficient as the participle’s translation.

مُهْطِعِينَ — al-Maʿārij 70:36

“Hastening toward you”

This preserves both directed approach and the haste attested in the root’s usage.

A lexical note can mention its other sense of fixing one’s attention or gaze on a person or destination.

Adding “out of fear” requires independent contextual evidence.

“Obeying” or “submitting” does not suit all three occurrences; submission is one recorded branch, not equally present in every verse.

8. Conclusion

ه ط ع concerns directed approach, rapid movement, and fixed attention to a person or destination.

Some usages include an extended neck, lowered head, or continuous gaze; others involve fear or submission. These features are not uniform across all occurrences.

Poetic witnesses illustrate obedience and hastening to listen. The Tubbaʿ attribution and date remain uncertain; Ibn Mufarrigh belongs to early Islam. Not every detail can therefore be securely assigned to pre-Islamic Arabic.

The Qur’an uses the root three times, always as مُهْطِعِينَ.

In Ibrāhīm 14:43, it contributes to a scene combining haste, raised heads, staring eyes, and empty hearts.

In al-Qamar 54:8, hastening toward the caller is prominent.

In al-Maʿārij 70:36, opponents approach Muhammad, showing that fear and humiliation are not invariable meanings.

Forqan definition: Qur’anic ه ط ع denotes hasty approach or intense attention toward a person or destination. Swift movement, sustained gaze, and head or neck posture form related lexical branches, emphasized differently by construction and context. Fear or submission may accompany them but are not necessary in every usage.

“Hastening” and “hastening toward a destination” suit the Qur’anic occurrences. Where gaze or posture matters, the contributions of separate words and contextual clues must remain distinct.
