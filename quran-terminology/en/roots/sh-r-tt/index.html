Signs, indications, precursors, and conditions: from early Arabic usage to the Qur’anic meaning of ashrāṭ al-sāʿah

The root SH R Ṭ has a broad semantic range in early Arabic. One central strand concerns marking, specifying, and identifying something by a sign. Sharaṭ, with a vowel on the middle consonant, denotes a sign that reveals something or distinguishes it from other things. Its plural is ashrāṭ.

The same family includes sharṭ, a condition or obligation stipulated in an agreement; ashraṭa, to mark or designate and prepare something for a particular task; and shurṭah, a group distinguished by a special mark or duty.

Ashrāṭ also denotes the beginnings or precursors of something: developments that appear before its full manifestation and indicate its approach or inception.

A physical branch concerns scratching or incising the skin. Sharaṭa al-ḥajjāmu al-jilda means making a shallow incision in the skin. Such an action leaves a visible mark, suggesting a possible relationship between cutting and marking. The available evidence does not establish which sense historically preceded the other.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as أَشْرَاطُهَا in Muḥammad 47:18. It refers to the signs or precursors of the Hour.

The central meaning of the Qur’anic branch is a sign indicating the emergence, beginning, or approach of something. Muḥammad 47:18 adds a crucial point: the sentence states that its signs have come, rather than announcing their future arrival.

1. The root in early Arabic

Marking and designation: a witness from Aws ibn Ḥajar

Aws ibn Ḥajar, a pre-Islamic poet, writes in a poem describing a man climbing a mountain to obtain suitable wood for a bow:

فَأَشْرَطَ فِيهَا نَفْسَهُ وَهُوَ مُعْصِمٌ
وَأَلْقَى بِأَسْبَابٍ لَهُ وَتَوَكَّلَا

fa-ashraṭa fīhā nafsahu wa-huwa muʿṣimun / wa-alqā bi-asbābin lahu wa-tawakkalā

The sense of the verse is:

“He committed himself to obtaining it, holding fast to a rope; he put his cords to use and placed his trust.”

The preceding verses describe a man in a difficult position, seeking a tree branch. He must negotiate a height and expose himself to danger.

Ashraṭa fīhā nafsahu means that he designated and prepared himself for this task and accepted its danger.

Early lexical explanations also describe this as making oneself identifiable for the undertaking: singling oneself out for it.

The verse does not imply that the man literally marked his body. Designating and preparing oneself for a task is the relevant sense.

Attribution to a pre-Islamic poet supports the early use of this family for designation or commitment to a particular task. The precise date of this verse has not been independently established here.

The verse does not directly establish ashrāṭ in the sense of signs of an event. Other evidence must also be considered.

Ashrāṭ as initial signs

A verse attributed to Abū al-Aswad al-Duʾalī, a poet of the first Islamic century, reads:

فَإِنْ كُنْتَ قَدْ أَزْمَعْتَ بِالصَّرْمِ بَيْنَنَا
فَقَدْ جَعَلَتْ أَشْرَاطُ أَوَّلِهِ تَبْدُو

fa-in kunta qad azmaʿta bi-l-ṣarmi baynanā / fa-qad jaʿalat ashrāṭu awwalihi tabdū

“If you have resolved to sever the bond between us, its first signs have begun to appear.”

Here ashrāṭ denotes signs emerging before an event is fully realized.

The poet speaks of ending a relationship. The central event is still presented as a decision or developing process, while conduct indicating its onset has appeared.

Ashrāṭ awwalihi therefore refers to its first signs or initial manifestations.

This witness helps explain the Qur’anic ashrāṭuhā: the word can denote indications connected with the beginning or approach of an event, rather than only a fixed, timeless marker.

This witness belongs to the early Islamic literary tradition. It should not be treated as securely dated pre-Islamic evidence; Abū al-Aswad’s poetry is associated with the first Islamic century.

With this chronological distinction, the use of ashrāṭ for initial signs is attested in Arabic close to the Qur’anic period.

Marking in everyday life

Ashraṭa min iblihi wa-ghanamihi is recorded for someone who sets aside and identifies some of his camels or sheep for sale.

The owner distinguishes the selected animals, whether by separating them or by a mark making the intention clear.

Marking thus has a practical function. A sign can communicate information about an object’s status or its owner’s purpose.

Setting aside or marking a particular group within a herd, for example, tells others that those animals have a different status.

Recording the expression in later dictionaries does not independently date all its details to the pre-Islamic period.

Conditions in agreements and transactions

Sharṭ also denotes a condition or obligation accepted by the parties to an agreement.

A seller and buyer may agree on payment time, delivery, or the responsibilities each will assume.

Each stipulation may constitute a condition of the transaction.

Here a condition is something whose acceptance or fulfillment matters within the agreement, beyond merely indicating an event.

Its semantic relationship with marking can be understood through the parties’ specification of the transaction’s boundaries and obligations.

This explanation does not establish that all contractual uses arose from marking livestock or goods at one identifiable historical moment.

The contractual meaning is established in Arabic, but the precise history of its development from “sign” requires independent evidence.

Technical jurisprudential and legal definitions may be narrower than ordinary usage. They should remain distinct from general lexical meaning and Qur’anic usage.

Shurṭah: a marked or selected group

Shurṭah also denotes a group designated and prepared for a particular mission.

Early explanations give two aspects of the naming: a distinguishing mark, and selection or preparation for a special duty.

In military usage, it could refer to troops entering battle ahead of others or prepared for a dangerous assignment.

In governmental usage, the word also denotes special forces charged with maintaining order and carrying out the authorities’ commands.

The Arabic designation for the police belongs to this family.

Modern police structures, legal duties, and administrative meanings should not be projected onto pre-Islamic Arabic.

For each period, the group and its responsibilities must be identified in context.

Other branches: skin incisions and special usages

Sharaṭa al-jilda refers to making a shallow cut or scratch in the skin.

Mishraṭ names an instrument used for such a cut.

An incision leaves a distinct mark, suggesting a semantic connection with marking.

Cutting is nevertheless different from an event serving as a sign. The cutting instrument does not imply that the Qur’anic ashrāṭ al-sāʿah means cutting or splitting the world.

Some lexical records use sharaṭ for inferior livestock and shurṭah for a select group. Contrasting uses of ashrāṭ for low-ranking people and selected people are also reported.

These differences caution against reducing every branch to one exceptionless definition. Some concern classification, selection, or valuation, but their precise historical relationship with “sign” is not always clear.

2. Important forms and derivatives

Major forms in this family include:

  • شَرَط: sharaṭ; a sign or indication, sometimes a beginning or precursor.
  • أَشْرَاط: ashrāṭ; plural of sharaṭ: signs, indications, or precursors.
  • شَرْط: sharṭ; a condition or obligation stipulated in an agreement.
  • شُرُوط: shurūṭ; conditions or obligations, plural of sharṭ.
  • شَرِيطَة: sharīṭah; a stipulated condition; in another recorded usage, a cord or strand.
  • شَرَائِط: sharāʾiṭ; plural of sharīṭah, contractual stipulations or conditions.
  • شَرَطَ: sharaṭa; stipulated a condition; physically, incised the skin.
  • أَشْرَطَ: ashraṭa; marked, specified, prepared, or designated for a task.
  • اشْتَرَطَ: ishtaraṭa; stipulated a condition or qualified an undertaking.
  • شَارَطَ: shāraṭa; agreed with another on conditions.
  • تَشَارَطَ: tashāraṭa; stipulated mutually or accepted reciprocal conditions.
  • تَشَرَّطَ: tasharraṭa; imposed conditions on accepting an undertaking.
  • مَشْرُوط: mashrūṭ; conditional or dependent on fulfillment of a condition.
  • شُرْطَة: shurṭah; a special or marked group prepared for a specific duty.
  • شُرْطِيّ: shurṭī; a member of the shurṭah.
  • مِشْرَط and مِشْرَاط: mishraṭ and mishrāṭ; instruments for making shallow incisions.
  • شَرِيط: sharīṭ; a cord or strand, sometimes made of fibers in early usage.
  • شَرَطَان: sharaṭān; two stars in Aries known in traditions concerning lunar mansions and seasons.

In astronomical usage, al-Sharaṭān names two particular stars. It should not be confused with the dual of sharṭ meaning two contractual conditions.

In one astronomical account, the name ashrāṭ is used when a nearby third star is counted with this pair. This special usage is not the meaning of ashrāṭuhā in Muḥammad 47:18, where the pronoun refers to the Hour.

Only أَشْرَاطُهَا occurs in the Qur’an.

Sharṭ, shurūṭ, sharīṭah, ishtirāṭ, shurṭah, mashrūṭ, and mishraṭ do not occur there in these forms.

Familiarity with the modern sense “condition” must not lead us to translate ashrāṭuhā as “the necessary conditions for the Resurrection.”

3. Qur’anic usage: Muḥammad 47:18

The Qur’an states:

فَهَلْ يَنْظُرُونَ إِلَّا السَّاعَةَ أَنْ تَأْتِيَهُمْ بَغْتَةً فَقَدْ جَاءَ أَشْرَاطُهَا فَأَنَّىٰ لَهُمْ إِذَا جَاءَتْهُمْ ذِكْرَاهُمْ

fa-hal yanẓurūna illā al-sāʿata an taʾtiyahum baghtatan fa-qad jāʾa ashrāṭuhā fa-annā lahum idhā jāʾathum dhikrāhum

Muḥammad 47:18

“Are they waiting for anything but the Hour to come upon them suddenly? Its precursors have already come. How will their taking heed avail them when it comes upon them?”

This is the root’s only Qur’anic occurrence.

Three parts should be read together: awaiting the Hour, the arrival of its preceding signs, and the opportunity to take heed before it arrives.

Fa-hal yanẓurūn: waiting until when?

The verse opens with this question:

فَهَلْ يَنْظُرُونَ إِلَّا السَّاعَةَ

fa-hal yanẓurūna illā al-sāʿah

Muḥammad 47:18

“Are they waiting for anything but the Hour?”

Yanẓurūn here means waiting, rather than merely looking with the eyes.

The preceding verses describe people who hear the divine message but fail to take it seriously or follow their own desires.

Muḥammad 47:16 states:

وَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ يَسْتَمِعُ إِلَيْكَ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا خَرَجُوا مِنْ عِنْدِكَ قَالُوا لِلَّذِينَ أُوتُوا الْعِلْمَ مَاذَا قَالَ آنِفًا

wa-minhum man yastamiʿu ilayka ḥattā idhā kharajū min ʿindika qālū li-lladhīna ūtū al-ʿilma mādhā qāla ānifā

Muḥammad 47:16

“Among them are those who listen to you, until, when they leave your presence, they ask those given knowledge: What did he say just now?”

Muḥammad 47:17 then speaks of people who have accepted guidance and whose guidance is increased.

In this context, the question in Muḥammad 47:18 warns those who can hear and receive the message but fail to benefit from it.

Are they waiting for the Hour itself before taking the truth seriously?

The verse says that the Hour comes suddenly and its precursors have already appeared.

An taʾtiyahum baghtatan: sudden arrival

The next phrase reads:

أَنْ تَأْتِيَهُمْ بَغْتَةً

an taʾtiyahum baghtah

Muḥammad 47:18

“To come upon them suddenly.”

Baghtah, from B GH T, means suddenly and unexpectedly.

This adverb describes how the Hour arrives.

An event’s unexpected arrival means its moment cannot be calculated by those surprised by it from a certain, previously known timetable.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:187 likewise states:

لَا تَأْتِيكُمْ إِلَّا بَغْتَةً

lā taʾtīkum illā baghtah

Al-Aʿrāf 7:187

“It will come upon you only suddenly.”

Al-Zukhruf 43:66 states:

هَلْ يَنْظُرُونَ إِلَّا السَّاعَةَ أَنْ تَأْتِيَهُمْ بَغْتَةً وَهُمْ لَا يَشْعُرُونَ

hal yanẓurūna illā al-sāʿata an taʾtiyahum baghtatan wa-hum lā yashʿurūn

Al-Zukhruf 43:66

“Are they waiting for anything but the Hour to come upon them suddenly, while they are unaware?”

These connections show that the Hour’s suddenness is not incidental to the discussion.

The Qur’an states it repeatedly, and Al-Aʿrāf 7:187 reserves knowledge of its timing to The God.

Ashrāṭ in Muḥammad 47:18 must therefore be understood consistently with that suddenness.

Signs and precursors do not amount to a definite timetable predicting the exact moment.

Fa-qad jāʾa ashrāṭuhā: signs that have come

The central phrase reads:

فَقَدْ جَاءَ أَشْرَاطُهَا

fa-qad jāʾa ashrāṭuhā

Muḥammad 47:18

“Its precursors have already come.”

Ashrāṭ is the plural of sharaṭ, a sign or indication. Beginnings and precursors also fit this context.

The verb’s construction is crucial.

Jāʾa is past tense, and qad here emphasizes the event’s realization.

This sentence reports that the Hour’s precursors have come; it does not announce their future arrival.

The distinction must be preserved.

Translating it as “many signs will come in the future, and the Resurrection will follow their completion” changes its tense and structure.

The verse reports preceding signs whose arrival was a reality at the time of the address.

It does not itself list those signs.

The plural does not specify an exact count: three, seven, ten, or another stated number.

The past tense establishes the arrival of the signs referred to, but does not by itself prove that every other possible sign before the Hour had also occurred by that time.

What is affirmed is the arrival of the signs referred to in this verse, rather than an unlimited list of future events that must occur in sequence.

What might the Hour’s precursors have been?

Possible referents require examination of related Qur’anic passages.

The Qur’an presents resurrection as judgment and accountability. Divine warning reaches people before judgment, allowing recognition, choice, and reform.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:40 calls Muḥammad khātam al-nabiyyīn, the Seal of the Prophets.

Al-Naḥl 16:89 describes the revealed Book in terms of clarification, guidance, mercy, and good news.

Read together, these passages can support an interpretation connecting Muḥammad’s mission and the Qur’an’s proclamation with the final prophetic message; neither directly identifies the referents of ashrāṭ in Muḥammad 47:18.

The beginning of the final prophet’s mission and the arrival of warning and guidance may thus be understood as an important precursor of the Hour.

This reading fits the past-tense phrase, since the mission and proclamation had already begun when the verse addressed its audience.

Al-Māʾidah 5:3 also speaks of the religion’s completion. On the commonly accepted chronology, that announcement belongs to a later stage of the mission; its date should not simply be equated with Muḥammad 47:18.

The proposed argument is therefore not that every stage of the mission and the final announcement of completion had already ended. It concerns the beginning of the final prophetic proclamation and its subsequent fulfillment.

This connection is interpretive; ashrāṭ does not lexically mean the Prophet’s commissioning or the Qur’an’s revelation.

The word denotes signs and precursors. Identifying them requires contextual and Qur’anic evidence.

Does ashrāṭ mean the familiar lists of end-time signs?

Later narrative and religious literature developed extensive lists of “minor and major signs of the Resurrection.”

They discuss individuals, governments, wars, natural phenomena, and other events. Some arrange them in sequences that can lead readers to think they can identify the remaining stages before the world’s end.

Muḥammad 47:18 provides no such list.

It does not name the Dajjāl, Mahdī, Sufyānī, black banners, particular future wars, or a sequence of end-time governments.

Those names and accounts cannot be derived from ashrāṭ itself.

The verse speaks of signs that have arrived. It does not itself prescribe a sequence to await in later centuries; assessing any additional proposed sign requires independent evidence.

The presence of a Qur’anic word does not authorize attaching every later story or prediction to it.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:187 reserves the Hour’s timing to The God, while Al-Zukhruf 43:66 describes its unexpected arrival. These passages do not support a definite end-time timetable.

Recognizing a warning sign differs from possessing a certain schedule for an event.

The beginning of a process may be evident without allowing calculation of its exact endpoint.

The signs function as warnings, rather than necessarily as units in a countdown measuring the time left.

Fa-annā lahum idhā jāʾathum dhikrāhum: the opportunity to take heed

The verse ends with this question:

فَأَنَّىٰ لَهُمْ إِذَا جَاءَتْهُمْ ذِكْرَاهُمْ

fa-annā lahum idhā jāʾathum dhikrāhum

Muḥammad 47:18

“How will their taking heed avail them when it comes upon them?”

The question makes the warning’s practical consequence clear.

Before the Hour arrives, a person can hear the message, reflect on it, and change their conduct.

Someone who neglects that opportunity and takes the truth seriously only when the Hour arrives cannot expect belated recognition to have the same effect as recognition before judgment.

The verse addresses people now, calling them to awareness rather than supplying a timetable whose precise dates and sequence they do not know.

This fits the preceding context.

Muḥammad 47:16 describes people who hear the message but fall short in receiving it seriously.

Muḥammad 47:17 speaks of increased guidance for those who have accepted it.

Muḥammad 47:18 then warns that waiting until the Hour arrives suddenly cannot replace using the present opportunity.

Ashrāṭ is thus part of a warning about human opportunity and responsibility.

4. Distinctions from related roots and concepts

SH R Ṭ compared with ʿ L M: ʿalāmah

ʿAlāmah, from ʿ L M, denotes a sign by which something is recognized.

Al-Naḥl 16:16 states:

وَعَلَامَاتٍ وَبِالنَّجْمِ هُمْ يَهْتَدُونَ

wa-ʿalāmātin wa-bi-l-najmi hum yahtadūn

Al-Naḥl 16:16

“And landmarks; and by the star they find their way.”

These signs help people identify routes and directions.

Sharaṭ overlaps with ʿalāmah in meaning “sign,” but in ashrāṭ al-sāʿah the signs relate to an event’s beginning or approach.

Both concern recognition through signs, while context determines the relationship between sign and referent.

An ʿalāmah may be a landmark, identifier, or means of recognition; ashrāṭ in Muḥammad 47:18 concerns an event’s precursors.

This does not make every sharaṭ temporal and every ʿalāmah spatial. Arabic usage supports no such absolute boundary.

SH R Ṭ compared with ʾ Y Y: āyah

Āyah can denote a sign, a verse of revelation, or a phenomenon disclosing a truth.

Passages on creation, for example, identify divine signs in the heavens, earth, night, day, and human life.

Ashrāṭ al-sāʿah refers specifically to signs or precursors associated with the Hour.

A divine sign should not therefore be called a sign of the Hour without a relevant indication.

Being a sign in the world is different from indicating the approach of a particular event.

The alternation of night and day, for example, signifies order in creation. That description alone does not make every sunrise a special sign of the world’s end.

In Muḥammad 47:18, the suffix hā specifies what the signs concern: that very Hour.

SH R Ṭ compared with B D ʾ and ʾ Z F

Badʾ concerns beginning or starting.

Ashrāṭ can also denote beginnings and precursors.

A precursor and an event’s actual beginning are not always identical. A preceding sign may indicate its approach before the main occurrence.

The root ʾ Z F concerns the approach of an event’s time.

Al-Najm 53:57 states:

أَزِفَتِ الْآزِفَةُ

azifati al-āzifah

Al-Najm 53:57

“The approaching event has drawn near.”

This emphasizes the event’s approach.

Ashrāṭ denotes signs and manifestations that may announce its beginning or approach.

An event’s approach and the appearance of its signs are related, but lexically distinct.

Nor should “drawing near” in warnings about resurrection be converted without evidence into a fixed number of years or generations.

Sharaṭ as a sign compared with contractual sharṭ

The middle consonant’s vocalization distinguishes two important usages.

Sharaṭ commonly means a sign or indication, with the plural ashrāṭ.

Sharṭ, with no vowel after the middle consonant, denotes an agreed condition whose acceptance or fulfillment is required. Its usual plural is shurūṭ.

Some lexicographers also report both vocalizations for the singular of ashrāṭ, so the vocalic distinction is not absolute across all reports and dialects. The distinction between a sign and a contractual obligation remains clear.

If payment is stipulated as a condition of delivery, this is an obligation accepted by the parties.

A change in someone’s conduct indicating a decision to end a relationship, by contrast, is a sign of a developing situation.

The two meanings must be distinguished.

Muḥammad 47:18 concerns the Hour’s signs and precursors, not contractual stipulations or obligations whose nonperformance would prevent it.

The Persian rendering “conditions of the Resurrection” can mislead because “condition” commonly suggests a prerequisite.

“Signs” or “precursors” is more accurate here.

Distinguishing SH R Ṭ from SH R ʿ, SH R Y, and Ṣ R Ṭ

Several roots share some of its consonants.

SH R ʿ has its own semantic field concerning paths, ways, and religious law.

SH R Y concerns buying, selling, and exchange.

Ṣ R Ṭ concerns a road or path and appears in the Qur’anic word ṣirāṭ.

SH R Ṭ belongs to the family of signs, stipulations, designation, and related uses.

In particular, ishtaraṭa, “stipulated,” must not be identified with ishtarā, “bought,” merely because they look similar.

Buying and stipulating may occur together in a transaction, but these are different verbs from different roots.

Likewise, sharṭ and sharʿ may appear together in legal or religious discussion without sharing a root or meaning.

5. Morphology, syntax, and translation proposals for Forqan

The Qur’anic form in Muḥammad 47:18 is:

أَشْرَاطُهَا

ashrāṭuhā

Muḥammad 47:18

It comprises ashrāṭ, the broken plural of sharaṭ, “sign,” and hā, the attached feminine singular pronoun referring to al-sāʿah.

In fa-qad jāʾa ashrāṭuhā, ashrāṭ is the nominative subject of jāʾa. The suffix occupies the possessive position in the construct phrase.

Jāʾa is past tense, and qad emphasizes that the coming has occurred.

The verb is masculine singular although ashrāṭ is plural. Singular number fits a verb preceding its explicit subject; masculine agreement is also permissible with this broken plural. Neither feature reduces the signs to one.

Three words deserve particular semantic attention.

Ashrāṭ names the signs or precursors.

Jāʾa reports their coming or realization.

Baghtatan describes the Hour’s sudden arrival.

These meanings should remain distinct.

Ashrāṭ itself does not mean “have come”: that information comes from jāʾa. It does not mean “suddenly” either; that belongs to baghtatan.

Translation proposals

Sharaṭ, as a sign: “sign,” “indication,” or “preceding sign,” depending on context.

Ashrāṭ: “signs,” “indications,” or “precursors.”

Sharṭ, in agreements: “condition,” “stipulation,” or “specified obligation.”

Shurūṭ: “conditions” or “agreed stipulations.”

Sharīṭah: “condition” or “stipulation”; in another usage, “cord” or “strand.”

Ashraṭa: “marked,” “specified,” or “designated and prepared for a task.”

Ishtaraṭa: “stipulated” or “made an undertaking conditional.”

Shāraṭa: “agreed on conditions with another.”

Shurṭah: a “special or marked group” in early usage; “police” in modern administration.

Mishraṭ: an “incision tool” or “scalpel,” according to usage.

Mashrūṭ: “conditional” or “qualified.”

For the Qur’anic phrase:

فَقَدْ جَاءَ أَشْرَاطُهَا

fa-qad jāʾa ashrāṭuhā

Muḥammad 47:18

The proposed rendering is:

“Its precursors have already come.”

“Its signs have already come” is also lexically clear and sound.

“Precursors” is another suitable choice here because it conveys signs connected with an event’s emerging beginnings. This proposal does not alter the installed verse translation.

“Its conditions have been fulfilled” is unsuitable because it suggests prerequisites, a meaning not conveyed by ashrāṭ here.

Nor should the phrase become “the signs of the Resurrection will come in the future,” which changes its tense and explicit statement.

6. Conclusion

Early Arabic uses SH R Ṭ for signs, marking, designation, contractual conditions, and shallow incisions.

The verse attributed to Aws ibn Ḥajar illustrates committing oneself to a dangerous undertaking; its precise date is not established here. Abū al-Aswad’s first-century Islamic verse illustrates ashrāṭ as an event’s initial signs.

Ashrāṭ al-shayʾ also denotes its beginnings and precursors: indications emerging before its full realization and signaling its inception or approach.

Only ashrāṭuhā occurs in the Qur’an, in Muḥammad 47:18.

It denotes the Hour’s signs or precursors. The full statement also depends on fa-qad jāʾa, which reports that those signs have come.

The verse calls the Hour’s arrival sudden and ends by asking what taking heed will avail its audience once it arrives.

Its focus is warning people who can still listen and change their conduct, rather than providing a fixed schedule of future events.

The final prophet’s mission and the beginning of the final divine proclamation may be understood as important referents through an interpretive reading of related verses. Ashrāṭ itself identifies no particular person or event.

A defensible central meaning of the Qur’anic branch is a sign or initial manifestation indicating something’s emergence, beginning, or approach.

“Precursors” and “signs” therefore suit Muḥammad 47:18. “Its precursors have already come” preserves the noun’s meaning and the statement that their arrival has occurred.

Distinguishing signs from contractual conditions, precursors from events themselves, and already realized signs from future predictions helps prevent later meanings from being imposed on the verse.
