1. Introducing the root

The three-consonant Arabic root J L B has two important semantic branches. The first concerns bringing, driving, or drawing something from one place to another. It includes bringing goods and livestock for sale, gathering people, mobilizing forces, and rousing a group with shouting and sound. The second concerns covering and enclosing, as in jilbāb, leather coverings for equipment, and the crust that forms over a wound.

The familiar Persian word jalb belongs to this family. Bringing an item from elsewhere expresses its meaning in that setting. In Arabic, this can concern goods, livestock, people, and other things.

By contrast, jilbāb in clothing usage names a garment or covering. Early sources define it as a shirt, an enveloping covering, a loose garment, or something worn over other clothes. These differing definitions caution against assuming one precisely fixed shape and size for every historical jilbāb.

Ibn Fāris identifies two basic principles for this root in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

الْجِيمُ وَاللَّامُ وَالْبَاءُ أَصْلَانِ: أَحَدُهُمَا الْإِتْيَانُ بِالشَّيْءِ مِنْ مَوْضِعٍ إِلَى مَوْضِعٍ، وَالْآخَرُ شَيْءٌ يُغَشِّي شَيْئًا.

al-jīmu wa-al-lāmu wa-al-bāʾu aṣlāni aḥaduhumā al-ityānu bi-al-shayʾi min mawḍiʿin ilā mawḍiʿin wa-al-ākharu shayʾun yughashshī shayʾan

Translation: “Jīm, lām, and bāʾ have two principles: one is bringing something from one place to another; the other is something that covers something else.”

Ibn Fāris: bringing and covering as two principles

This division matters. Connections between bringing and covering may be imagined in particular uses, but the entire family cannot be reduced without evidence to one meaning such as “drawing a covering toward something.” Brief root meaning: Bringing and gathering, mobilizing; and, in another branch, covering and enclosing.

The Qur’an uses this root twice, in two different forms:

  • وَأَجْلِبْ in Al-Isrāʾ 17:64: a Form IV imperative addressed to Satan, meaning to mobilize forces, gather them for an attack, or rouse them against others.
  • جَلَابِيبِهِنَّ in Al-Aḥzāb 33:59: the plural of jilbāb with a feminine plural possessive pronoun, meaning their outer garments.

The first form appears in a context of rousing and mobilizing forces. The second is a noun concerning garments and covering.

No other form of this root occurs in the Qur’an.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: two J L B occurrences

Both branches therefore require study. The importance of women’s clothing should not obscure the usage in Al-Isrāʾ, nor should bringing and mobilizing be imposed on the noun jilbāb.

2. Jalb: bringing something from one place to another

Moving and transferring

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

الْجَلْبُ سَوْقُ الشَّيْءِ مِنْ مَوْضِعٍ إِلَى آخَرَ.

al-jalbu sawqu al-shayʾi min mawḍiʿin ilā ākhara

Translation: “Jalb is driving or bringing something from one place to another.”

This definition relates two locations: something is in one place and is then brought or driven elsewhere.

The transfer may involve moving an animal, carrying goods, or bringing something to someone.

Arabic jalaba can be transitive: someone brings something. Ijtalaba is also used for bringing something to oneself or obtaining it from elsewhere.

For example, a merchant bringing camels or sheep from grazing grounds to a market may be described as performing jalb.

Transfer is central here. Whether the goods are valuable, harmful, permissible, or forbidden does not follow from the verb itself.

Bringing goods and livestock

In early Arabic, al-jalab also names goods or animals brought from elsewhere for sale.

Jalūbah and jalībah in certain uses refer to livestock or goods brought for sale.

Jallāb also describes someone who brings livestock or goods from another location.

These usages connect jalb with Arab economic life.

Herders might keep camels, sheep, or other animals where they were raised, then bring them to markets or gathering places for sale.

Jalb in such circumstances is both the physical transfer of animals and part of commercial activity.

The root itself, however, does not mean trade. Trade can be one setting for jalb, just as bringing something for personal use or helping another person can be expressed by the same verb.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān: bringing goods and livestock

Jalīb: brought from elsewhere

Lexical sources use jalīb for something or someone brought from another land or location.

This can concern animals and goods, and in some historical settings people brought from elsewhere.

The word concerns transfer from a location, rather than a person’s intrinsic worth or standing.

Its use for a human being therefore does not establish any inferior moral or human status.

People’s legal or social circumstances are a separate matter, requiring contextual and historical evidence.

Ijtalaba: bringing and obtaining

Ijtalaba is Form VIII.

It can concern bringing something to oneself or using something obtained from elsewhere.

In literary usage, a poet may be accused of taking another person’s verse or speech, with that act described as ijtilāb.

The word here extends from transferring material goods to bringing in speech and poetry.

This extension is understandable: poetry can pass from one speaker to another or be presented under another person’s name.

Ijtalaba does not mean plagiarism in every use. Wrongdoing or deception follows from the setting and manner in which the acquired material is used.

3. Gathering forces and rousing through sound

Ajlaba ʿalayhi: mobilizing against another

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

وَأَجْلَبُوا عَلَيْهِ إِذَا تَجَمَّعُوا وَتَأَلَّبُوا.

wa-ajlabū ʿalayhi idhā tajammaʿū wa-taʾallabū

Translation: “They are said to have ajlabū against him when they gathered and joined forces against him.”

This usage no longer concerns bringing goods for sale.

It concerns people assembling and preparing forces to confront someone else.

Taʾallabū refers to a group gathering and joining forces against a person or another group.

This directly relates to the Qur’anic wa-ajlib ʿalayhim.

The preposition ʿalā matters because it identifies the direction of action: the forces are assembled and deployed against a party.

Shouting and urging on

Al-Jawharī records another use in Al-Ṣiḥāḥ:

جَلَبَ عَلَى فَرَسِهِ ... صَاحَ بِهِ مِنْ خَلْفِهِ وَاسْتَحَثَّهُ لِلسَّبْقِ.

jalaba ʿalā farasihi ... ṣāḥa bihi min khalfihi wa-istaḥaththahu li-al-sabqi

Translation: “He performed jalb upon his horse: he shouted at it from behind and urged it to get ahead.”

This is intelligible in a horse-racing setting.

Someone may try to make a horse move faster through shouting, sound, or clamor.

Jalb here connects with urging through sound.

Ajlaba ʿalayhi also concerns shouting and mobilizing forces in some confrontational uses.

Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī explains this branch through shouting and exerting pressure in Al-Mufradāt.

There is thus a connection within this semantic field between assembling forces and rousing through sound.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Lisān, and Al-Mufradāt: clamor and mobilization

This does not make shouting part of every use. Bringing goods from a market or covering something in leather does not entail it.

Jalabah: clamor and collective noise

Jalabah in Arabic also means loud, mingled sounds.

When a group assembles with shouting, movement, and noise, the resulting sound can be called jalabah.

Some uses also concern the sound of moving crowds or animals.

Sound here results from a group’s movement or activity.

Not every sound is jalabah. A person’s quiet voice, an animal’s call, and a crowd’s clamor may be expressed by different words.

Jalabah particularly concerns the audible commotion resulting from activity or assembly.

Ajlaba and assistance

Some lexical accounts also gloss ajlabahu as helping someone.

This connects with mobilizing forces and joining in an undertaking.

Gathering others to support someone can increase that person’s strength and resources.

Assistance does not in itself determine an action’s moral direction.

A group may assemble to defend a right or to wrong someone else.

In the Qur’an, wa-ajlib ʿalayhim addresses Satan in a context of misleading human beings. Its negative direction follows from that context, rather than every act of jalb or gathering being blameworthy in Arabic.

4. The other branch: covering and enclosing

The covering over a wound

Ibn Fāris discusses julbah under the root’s second branch in Maqāyīs al-Lughah. This form, with an initial ḍammah, differs from jalabah, meaning clamor.

Al-Qāmūs: julbah with initial ḍammah for crust and covering

One meaning is the crust that forms over a wound as it heals.

The crust lies over the wound and covers part of its surface.

Arabic uses jalaba al-jurḥu for a wound over which such a crust has formed.

The concern is not bringing something from another location: something lies over and covers another surface.

This fits Ibn Fāris’s second principle, shayʾun yughashshī shayʾan: something covering something else.

It does not establish that every Arabic covering developed from the image of a wound. A wound crust is one attested use, rather than a proven historical cause of every derivative in this branch.

Leather coverings for equipment

Julbah in another use names a piece of leather placed over part of a saddle or an animal’s equipment.

Lexical sources explain that skin or leather was sometimes stretched over a particular part of the equipment and became firmly fixed when it dried.

This belongs to nomadic life and the use of camels and other pack animals.

The leather covering might protect, secure, or repair part of the equipment.

The precise purpose of each covering requires knowledge of the particular implement.

Julbah here names a piece covering a surface or part of an implement.

Cases, coverings, and sheaths

Lisān al-ʿArab also records julbah for leather coverings on other implements, including a covering on a knife’s handle or joining section.

Some uses also concern enclosing an amulet or small object in leather.

In all these examples, something lies over or encloses something else.

They show that the covering branch of J L B extends beyond human clothing.

Julbah should nevertheless not be treated as a complete synonym of jilbāb in every usage.

The former can name a crust, covering, or piece of leather; the latter, in its familiar use, names a human garment or covering.

A cloud as a covering of the sky

Arabic sources also record jilb and julb for a kind of cloud.

Some definitions describe a thin, rainless cloud; another concerns a cloud spreading across the sky with a mountainlike appearance.

These accounts differ in the cloud’s shape and qualities.

Both nevertheless connect the name with a phenomenon in the sky.

Ibn Fāris considers this usage under covering.

A cloud can visibly cover part of the sky, but this image alone does not establish that the earliest meaning of this family throughout Arabic was a cloud or the sky’s covering.

Nor should a rainless cloud in some definitions be taken to mean that every cloud called by this name is necessarily rainless in all circumstances.

Al-Qāmūs: jilb and julb for cloud

5. Jilbāb: bodily covering in early Arabic

Defining jilbāb

Jilbāb is one of this root’s most important forms.

Lisān al-ʿArab records several definitions:

الْجِلْبَابُ: الْقَمِيصُ.

al-jilbābu al-qamīṣu

Translation: “Jilbāb: a shirt.”

Another definition states:

وَالْجِلْبَابُ ثَوْبٌ أَوْسَعُ مِنَ الْخِمَارِ، دُونَ الرِّدَاءِ، تُغَطِّي بِهِ الْمَرْأَةُ رَأْسَهَا وَصَدْرَهَا.

wa-al-jilbābu thawbun awsaʿu mina al-khimāri dūna al-ridāʾi tughaṭṭī bihi al-marʾatu raʾsahā wa-ṣadrahā

Translation: “A jilbāb is a garment wider than the khimār and smaller than the ridāʾ, with which a woman covers her head and chest.”

Other accounts define jilbāb as milḥafah, mulāʾah, or a garment a woman wears over her other clothes.

These definitions identify a familiar bodily covering, while differing over its exact size and placement.

Some approach a shirt, others a loose covering over other clothes, and others a garment enclosing larger parts of the body.

No single definition therefore establishes one exceptionless form for every jilbāb at the time of revelation.

Lisān: differing definitions of jilbāb

Is jilbāb the same as khimār?

Lisān al-ʿArab transmits Ibn al-Sikkīt’s report of an Arab woman defining jilbāb as khimār.

This indicates that the two words could overlap in certain Arabic uses.

Another definition, however, describes jilbāb as wider than khimār.

Two differing lexical accounts thus exist: one brings the terms together, while the other distinguishes their size and extent.

This difference should remain visible.

One definition should not invalidate all the others merely by existing.

Garment names can differ with region, fabric, manner of wearing, or social use.

It is therefore more accurate to call jilbāb a known covering, loose and enveloping in many definitions, whose shape and extent vary between accounts.

Jilbāb as an outer garment

Some Lisān definitions identify jilbāb as a garment worn over a woman’s other clothes.

This supports “outer garment” as a Qur’anic translation.

An outer garment need not have one fixed cut, color, size, or design.

It may be a loose garment draped over the body or a covering enclosing some of the other clothes.

Without evidence, the Qur’anic jilbāb should therefore not be equated with a particular modern chador, cloak, niqāb, burqa, or tailored coat.

Such garments may resemble certain uses of jilbāb in coverage, while differing in historical name and construction.

Covering the head, chest, and body

Some early definitions mention covering the head and chest. Others describe a garment enclosing much or all of the body.

The lexical accounts therefore do not establish one fixed extent of coverage.

Their clear common point is that jilbāb names a garment or covering, rather than a bodily organ.

Where a verse mentions jilbāb, it should first be understood as a covering. The verb, prepositions, and context then determine the action requested with it.

Every detail of wearing it should not be extracted from the noun alone.

Figurative uses of jilbāb

Arabic sometimes uses jilbāb to express the encompassing character of a condition.

Something is pictured as a garment surrounding a person or the surrounding space.

For example, literary language may liken night’s darkness to a covering spread over the surroundings.

This draws on a garment’s covering quality.

Such figurative usage should not replace the direct meaning in Al-Aḥzāb 33:59.

That verse concerns women and coverings they draw over themselves. Its basic reading should begin with the familiar garment sense.

6. Poetic witnesses from the pre-Islamic period and near the time of revelation

Bringing horses in ʿAlqamah ibn ʿAbadah’s poetry

ʿAlqamah ibn ʿAbadah, known as ʿAlqamah al-Faḥl, is a well-known pre-Islamic poet.

A poem attributed to him states:

وَنَحْنُ جَلَبْنَا مِنْ ضَرِيَّةَ خَيْلَنَا

wa-naḥnu jalabnā min Ḍariyyata khaylanā

Meaning: “And we brought our horses from Ḍariyyah.”

Ḍariyyah names a location in Arabian geography.

Jalabnā here refers to moving and bringing horses from one location to another.

The witness matters because the actual object, horses, makes the meaning of transfer and movement clear.

In Arab life, bringing animals could serve travel, trade, or confrontation with another group.

The verb itself does not determine the movement’s ultimate purpose.

ʿAlqamah’s poem concerns moving horses and confrontational events. Jalb thus also occurs in a military or collective-movement setting.

This supports the bringing-and-driving branch in language transmitted as pre-Islamic verse.

Umm Al-Qura University: ʿAlqamah’s horse witness

Jilbāb in Janūb’s lament for her brother ʿAmr Dhū al-Kalb

Janūb, sister of ʿAmr Dhū al-Kalb al-Hudhalī, is a female poet of the pre-Islamic period.

A lament attributed to her for her brother states:

تَمْشِي النُّسُورُ إِلَيْهِ وَهِيَ لَاهِيَةٌ
مَشْيَ الْعَذَارَى عَلَيْهِنَّ الْجَلَابِيبُ

tamshī al-nusūru ilayhi wa-hiya lāhiyatun / mashya al-ʿadhārā ʿalayhinna al-jalābību

Translation: “Vultures walk toward him at ease, like maidens walking with jalābīb upon them.”

Lisān al-ʿArab and other literary sources record this verse.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān: jalābīb in Janūb’s lament

The poet compares the vultures’ movement to girls walking calmly while wearing coverings.

Al-jalābīb here names garments on the maidens.

The poet uses a familiar garment name without explaining its shape or size.

This matters historically because it indicates familiarity with the name jilbāb in pre-Islamic Arabic.

The verse itself does not specify how far down the body the garment reached, how it was sewn, or how much of the face it covered.

The comparison concerns walking and a leisurely manner of movement.

It therefore cannot establish one definitive garment shape or a particular face-covering rule.

Variants of the witness

Some sources begin the first hemistich with tamshī, while others transmit tashuqqu.

Literary manuscripts also show spelling differences in certain forms of al-jalābīb.

These variants should be recognized when using the witness.

In the main wording quoted in Lisān, however, al-jalābīb clearly names the maidens’ coverings.

A minor variant in the first hemistich’s verb does not change that meaning.

The mujlib incantation performer in ʿAlqamah’s poetry

Another poem by ʿAlqamah ibn ʿAbadah uses mujlib while describing a horse.

The verse is transmitted as follows:

بِغَوْجٍ لَبَانُهُ يَتِمُّ بَرِيمُهُ
عَلَى نَفْثِ رَاقٍ خَشْيَةَ الْعَيْنِ مُجْلِبِ

bi-ghawjin labānuhu yatimmu barīmuhu / ʿalā nafthi rāqin khashyata al-ʿayni mujlibi

The poet describes a horse with a broad chest, furnished with an amulet to protect it from the evil eye.

Mujlib here is an active participle of ajlaba qualifying rāqin, the incantation performer. An early commentary explains it as someone who blows abundantly while reciting incantations. It should therefore not be read as mujlab describing an amulet’s covering.

Al-Akhfash, Al-Ikhtiyārayn: mujlib qualifies the incantation performer and abundant blowing

The setting concerns an amulet and incantation, but mujlib describes the performer’s act. The word by itself is not evidence for the covering sense.

Enclosing an amulet in leather is an independently recorded use of julbah. Its definition appearing beside this verse should not lead to confusion between the two forms.

Al-Qāmūs: julbah independently names an amulet’s leather case

This witness nevertheless does not establish belief in the evil eye as the root’s original meaning.

The evil eye belongs to the beliefs and social practices of that period. Mujlib describes the performer and his incantation here; a leather covering is understood from the separate definition of julbah.

The witnesses’ historical significance

The witnesses attributed to ʿAlqamah and Janūb illustrate two important branches in language associated with the pre-Islamic period.

ʿAlqamah’s jalb concerns bringing and moving horses.

Janūb’s jilbāb concerns women’s clothing.

The amulet witness also illustrates an incantation setting. Covering and enclosing, however, should be understood from the independent use of julbah, rather than the adjective mujlib in this verse.

These witnesses illuminate actual usages, but should not support conclusions beyond what they say.

For example, a pre-Islamic mention of jilbāb does not establish one identical garment design throughout every Arabian tribe and region.

Nor does jalb in military horse poetry make every Arabic use concern warfare.

Each witness illuminates the usage actually present in it.

7. First Qur’anic usage: mobilizing forces in Al-Isrāʾ 17:64

وَاسْتَفْزِزْ مَنِ اسْتَطَعْتَ مِنْهُم بِصَوْتِكَ وَأَجْلِبْ عَلَيْهِم بِخَيْلِكَ وَرَجِلِكَ وَشَارِكْهُمْ فِي الْأَمْوَالِ وَالْأَوْلَادِ وَعِدْهُمْ ۚ وَمَا يَعِدُهُمُ الشَّيْطَانُ إِلَّا غُرُورًا

wa-istafziz mani istaṭaʿta minhum bi-ṣawtika wa-ajlib ʿalayhim bi-khaylika wa-rajilika wa-shārikhum fī al-amwāli wa-al-awlādi wa-ʿidhum wa-mā yaʿiduhumu al-shayṭānu illā ghurūran

Existing Persian Forqan translation, rendered here in English: “Stir up whomever you can among them with your voice, charge against them with your cavalry and infantry, share in their wealth and children, and promise them; Satan promises them nothing but delusion.”

The verse follows the account of Iblīs’s disobedience and request for respite.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:63 discusses the fate of those who follow him.

Verse 64 then addresses him with several actions: rousing through sound, mobilizing cavalry and infantry, sharing in wealth and children, and making promises.

The ending identifies Satan’s promises as delusion.

The meaning of wa-ajlib ʿalayhim

Ajlib is a Form IV imperative from J L B.

Here it concerns mobilizing, gathering, and setting forces in motion against another group.

The preposition ʿalā clarifies the direction of action.

The forces are not simply brought to a place: they are deployed in confrontation with an opposing party.

This makes the Persian rendering “charge against them” natural in context.

That rendering should not make the root invariably mean military raiding.

Charging and attacking follow here from the verb’s combination with ʿalayhim and bi-khaylika wa-rajilika.

Bi-khaylika wa-rajilika: cavalry and infantry

Khayl names horses and can evoke mounted forces in a military setting.

Rajil concerns infantry in this context.

Cavalry and infantry together depict the mobilization of different forces.

The division into mounted and foot soldiers was familiar in Arabic military language.

The expression can therefore concern gathering every available force for attack and pressure.

The verse does not, however, explain that Satan possesses a particular physical army of actual horses and infantry.

Conversely, the military image should not be removed without evidence merely because the subject is Satan.

A clear reading recognizes the language of mobilization and attack, presenting Satan’s forces as his cavalry and infantry.

Identifying precisely who or what those forces represent requires independent evidence.

The difference between istafziz and ajlib

The verse begins:

“وَاسْتَفْزِزْ مَنِ اسْتَطَعْتَ مِنْهُم بِصَوْتِكَ”.

This verb comes from F Z Z and here concerns stirring someone, rousing them, or dislodging them from their previous state.

Ajlib ʿalayhim follows, concerning mobilizing and gathering forces.

The actions can be connected without being identical in meaning.

The first expression emphasizes rousing people through sound.

The second concerns mobilizing forces against them.

Rendering both simply as “shout” would hide the distinction in the sentence.

Nor should a fixed chronology be imposed, as though Satan always first agitates through sound and only then mobilizes his forces.

The verse places several methods together without prescribing that necessary sequence.

Ṣawt and jalabah

Arabic jalb and jalabah can concern shouting and clamor.

There is consequently an imagistic connection between bi-ṣawtika and ajlib.

The verse nevertheless employs two different words.

Ṣawt concerns sound, while ajlib in this construction concerns mobilizing and attacking.

Satan’s voice should therefore not be presented as ajlib’s sole, basic meaning.

The verse itself also does not identify one specific kind of his sound.

The expression alone cannot establish every loud sound, musical performance, song, or communication device as a definite instance of “Satan’s voice.”

Such identifications require separate evidence.

Al-Ṭabarī: voice, mobilization, cavalry, and infantry

Does the imperative morally authorize misleading others?

The verse addresses Satan, but that grammatical form does not in itself express divine moral approval of misleading people.

The same verse calls Satan’s promises delusion.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:65 also states that Satan has no independent authority over God’s servants.

An action’s occurrence within the sphere of possibility and divine permission should therefore be distinguished from its moral approval or legitimacy.

This distinction follows from context and should not be added to ajlib’s lexical meaning.

Findings from the first usage

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:64, J L B belongs to the branch of gathering, mobilizing, and rousing forces.

Ajlib, combined with ʿalayhim and bi-khaylika wa-rajilika, depicts organized attack and pressure.

Neither bodily covering nor jilbāb is the subject here.

The verb’s meaning therefore requires examination independently of the clothing branch.

8. Second Qur’anic usage: jilbāb in Al-Aḥzāb 33:59

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّبِيُّ قُل لِّأَزْوَاجِكَ وَبَنَاتِكَ وَنِسَاءِ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ يُدْنِينَ عَلَيْهِنَّ مِن جَلَابِيبِهِنَّ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ أَدْنَىٰ أَن يُعْرَفْنَ فَلَا يُؤْذَيْنَ ۗ وَكَانَ اللَّهُ غَفُورًا رَّحِيمًا

yā ayyuhā al-nabiyyu qul li-azwājika wa-banātika wa-nisāʾi al-muʾminīna yudnīna ʿalayhinna min jalābībihinna dhālika adnā an yuʿrafna fa-lā yuʾdhayna wa-kāna Allāhu ghafūran raḥīman

Existing Persian Forqan translation, rendered here in English: “O Prophet, tell your wives, your daughters, and the women of the believers to draw from their outer garments over themselves. That is more likely that they will be recognized and not harmed; and The God is Forgiving, Merciful.”

This verse contains the only Qur’anic occurrence of jilbāb.

It directly addresses the Prophet, commanding him to communicate the matter to his wives, daughters, and the women of the believers.

Jalābībihinna is the plural of jilbāb with a feminine plural possessive pronoun.

It refers to coverings belonging to the women addressed.

These coverings are the means used in the action expressed by yudnīna.

Jalābībihinna: their coverings

Lexical sources define jilbāb as a kind of garment or covering.

Some emphasize its outer, enveloping character.

Others compare its size with a khimār or ridāʾ.

“Their outer garments” therefore accords with a clear, attested use.

This translation should not imply that the noun itself specifies each garment’s precise shape.

The verse gives no color, fabric, sewing method, or numerical dimensions.

The plural jalābīb also does not by itself establish that each woman must possess several jilbābs.

Since a group of women is addressed, a plural for their garments is natural.

Yudnīna ʿalayhinna: drawing the covering over themselves

Yudnīna belongs to D N W and concerns bringing near or drawing something down.

Here it expresses what the women do with their coverings.

ʿAlayhinna specifies the covering’s placement: over the women themselves.

The sentence therefore describes an action with a garment as well as naming it.

This distinction matters.

Jilbāb names the covering, while drawing it down or near follows from yudnīna.

That action should not be incorporated into the noun’s lexical meaning.

Conversely, simply translating “wear a jilbāb” may not adequately convey the verb chosen in the Qur’an.

The verse emphasizes placing and drawing the covering over oneself.

Min in min jalābībihinna

The preposition min has been subject to grammatical discussion.

A familiar analysis is partitive: the women draw part of their covering over themselves.

Another interpretation found in some commentaries concerns selecting and using a covering from among those available.

These analyses are not identical.

The first considers part of a garment; the second can concern one of the garments belonging to the woman.

Min’s presence in the sentence needs to be preserved and analyzed. The preposition alone, however, does not determine which body part must be covered.

Nor does min alone establish that women necessarily owned several jilbābs or always had to use only part of a garment.

Al-Kashshāf: two partitive analyses of min

A basic translation should preserve the relationship between the covering and drawing it over oneself as far as possible, leaving the grammatical disagreement to explanation.

Does jilbāb necessarily cover the face?

Some commentaries describe jilbāb as placed on the head, with part drawn over the face.

Other accounts emphasize covering the head, chest, and body.

Some lexicographers likewise bring jilbāb close to khimār, while others describe a larger and more enveloping covering.

The linguistic and exegetical sources therefore do not present one entirely uniform image.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:59 itself does not name the face, hair, or another specific body part as the command’s direct object.

It explicitly states drawing the coverings over the women themselves.

The noun jilbāb alone therefore cannot establish a definitive rule of covering the entire face or exposing a specified portion.

Conversely, the verse’s not directly naming the face does not prove that no historical way of wearing jilbāb covered it.

Lexical meaning, historical wearing practices, and the verse’s explicit command should be kept distinct.

Al-Ṭabarī: reported wearing practices and the distinction between interpretation and the noun’s meaning

Dhālika adnā an yuʿrafna fa-lā yuʾdhayna

The verse then gives the command’s purpose:

“ذَٰلِكَ أَدْنَىٰ أَن يُعْرَفْنَ فَلَا يُؤْذَيْنَ”.

Women’s being recognized and unharmed is connected here with how the covering is used.

This sentence itself does not explain the exact social sign or outward feature by which they are recognized.

Some exegetical accounts explain this through a distinction between free and enslaved women.

Those accounts matter for understanding historical interpretations, but the verse’s own words do not explicitly state that legal classification.

Al-Kashshāf: the historical free–enslaved distinction in interpretation

The verse names the Prophet’s wives, his daughters, and the women of the believers.

Jalābībihinna itself therefore cannot establish covering as the exclusive right of one group or permission to harass women of another social status.

Nor should the purpose of avoiding harm be recast as placing responsibility for harassment on women.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:58 explicitly condemns those who harm believing men and women without their having committed an offense.

The clothing command and the moral responsibility of those who harm others should therefore be distinguished.

The verse’s relationship to rules and social conduct

Al-Aḥzāb 33:59 stands in a context discussing harm to the Prophet and believers, social conduct, and people causing unrest and harm in the community.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:60 also mentions hypocrites, those with sickness in their hearts, and rumor spreaders in the city.

The jilbāb command thus arises in a setting involving harassment and social security.

This context matters for understanding its stated purpose.

It should not, however, put every social and historical detail into jilbāb’s lexical meaning.

Jilbāb names a covering. Harm, social recognition, and people’s treatment of one another follow from other parts of the verse and its immediate context.

Jilbāb and other Qur’anic coverings

Al-Nūr 24:31 uses khumur and speaks of drawing coverings over garment openings at the chest.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:59 uses jalābīb and concerns drawing them over the women themselves.

This is a real distinction.

It does not establish that khimār and jilbāb were always two wholly separate garments of fixed shape and size in every historical usage.

Lexical sources record overlaps between the terms.

Conversely, that overlap does not make every rule and construction in the two verses identical.

Each verse requires examination through its own words, verbs, prepositions, and context.

9. Distinguishing jilbāb from later clothing and terminology

Jilbāb and modern garments

Women’s garments have different names and shapes across societies.

Some are tailored; others are loose cloth draped over the body; others cover only part of it.

Some languages also use words resembling jilbāb for modern garments.

A name’s contemporary use does not by itself establish its historical form at the time of revelation.

Similarity of names therefore cannot equate the Qur’anic jilbāb with one particular modern garment.

Jilbāb and jallābiyyah

In contemporary Arabic, jallābiyyah names a long, loose garment used in certain regions by men and women.

The name relates to the clothing vocabulary around jilbāb, but its present use may denote a garment with a familiar cut and shape.

Early accounts of jilbāb, by contrast, encompass a range of coverings.

Similar names do not suffice to establish identical garment construction.

Dozy’s Supplement: jallābiyyah in later evidence

Jilbāb and ḥijāb

Arabic ḥijāb concerns a barrier, curtain, or partition between two sides.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:53 uses min warāʾi ḥijābin: from behind a barrier.

Jilbāb, however, names a covering worn upon a person.

It too can function as a covering, but this overlap does not make the roots identical.

Contemporary ḥijāb has become a broad term for religious covering. That terminological usage should be distinguished from its direct meaning in Qur’anic verses.

A J L B root study should therefore recognize jilbāb as a covering without replacing it with later terminology.

10. Comparison with related roots

J L B (jalb) and S W Q (sawq)

Study of S W Q

Arabic sawq concerns driving and moving something.

Jalb too can describe bringing animals or goods from one place to another.

In some usages they are very close.

Sawq more particularly emphasizes driving or moving itself, while familiar uses of jalb can emphasize bringing something to a destination or assembling it elsewhere.

This distinction is not absolute or universal.

Someone bringing livestock to market may both drive it and perform jalb.

Translation should consider whether the sentence emphasizes movement or arrival at a destination.

J L B (ajlaba) and J M ʿ (jamaʿa)

Study of J M ʿ

Jamaʿa concerns gathering and collecting.

Ajlaba ʿalayhi in one use concerns gathering forces against another.

Assembly here combines with directed action and pressure against an opposing party.

Not every gathering is therefore ajlaba.

People may gather for consultation, worship, or an ordinary task, whereas Al-Isrāʾ 17:64 depicts assembling forces to attack others.

That direction follows from the preposition and the sentence’s other elements.

J L B (jalabah) and Ṣ W T (ṣawt)

Study of Ṣ W T

Ṣawt concerns sound.

Jalabah in some uses denotes clamor and loud, mingled noise.

Jalabah can thus be a kind of sound, without every sound being jalabah.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:64 places bi-ṣawtika beside ajlib.

Their proximity does not make them complete synonyms.

Sound can be a means of rousing, while ajlib concerns mobilization and attack.

J L B (jilbāb) and KH M R (khimār)

Study of KH M R

Arabic khimār concerns covering and, in its familiar use for women, a covering placed on the head.

Many definitions describe jilbāb as wider or more enveloping.

Some lexical accounts nevertheless consider jilbāb and khimār close or synonymous.

Their distinction therefore requires attention to actual usage and context.

Al-Nūr 24:31 relates khumur to garment openings at the chest; Al-Aḥzāb 33:59 relates jalābīb to drawing coverings over women.

These different Qur’anic constructions should not be merged without evidence.

J L B (jilbāb) and J Y B (jayb)

Study of J Y B

Arabic jayb names the neckline or opening at the upper part of a garment.

Jilbāb names a covering placed over the body.

One thus concerns a garment’s part or opening; the other names the covering itself.

Al-Nūr 24:31 commands covering garment openings at the chest.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:59 concerns drawing outer garments over the women themselves.

The terms are not synonymous merely because both concern clothing and women.

J L B (jilbāb) and S T R (satr)

Study of S T R

Arabic satr concerns covering and concealing something.

Jilbāb is one object that can cover the body.

Not everything that covers is a jilbāb.

A wall, curtain, roof, and other coverings can also cover something.

Jilbāb therefore names a particular kind of covering in clothing vocabulary, while satr is a broader term for the act of covering.

J L B (jilbāb) and D N W (idnāʾ)

Study of D N W

Arabic D N W concerns being near and bringing near.

Yudnīna in Al-Aḥzāb 33:59 belongs to this family.

Jilbāb names the covering, while idnāʾ expresses bringing it near or drawing it down.

This distinction matters for understanding the sentence.

Defining jilbāb itself as “drawing a covering down” would obscure the verse’s separate verb.

The covering’s name and the action performed with it should therefore remain distinct.

J L B (jilbāb) and J L D (jild)

Study of J L D

Jild concerns skin.

Within J L B, forms such as julbah are also recorded for leather coverings.

The two families can overlap in the semantic field of covering and skin.

Their roots nevertheless differ.

Jilbāb belongs to J L B and should not be derived from J L D merely through resemblance in sound.

A leather covering for equipment also differs from jilbāb as a human garment.

11. Questions of interpretation and translation

Did every meaning develop from bringing and drawing?

Ibn Fāris identifies two principles in Maqāyīs al-Lughah.

Without historical investigation, covering usages cannot all be derived from transfer and bringing.

A connection may sometimes be imagined between drawing something over a surface and covering it.

That mental image does not establish the history of every word.

Both branches should be recognized, with any proposed relationship limited to what the evidence supports.

Does ajlib always mean “shout”?

No.

Some Arabic uses connect ajlaba with shouting and urging on.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:64, however, bi-khaylika wa-rajilika also brings mobilization and attack into the image.

Rendering the verb solely as “shout” may therefore lose part of its contextual meaning.

Is jilbāb always a full-length garment?

Some lexical definitions describe an enveloping covering.

Other accounts relate it to a shirt, khimār, or differently sized garment.

One fixed dimension cannot therefore be imposed upon every historical usage.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:59 names a familiar kind of covering without specifying its precise size or cut.

Does jilbāb itself determine a face-covering rule?

No.

The noun itself identifies a kind of covering.

Some lexical and historical descriptions place it over the head and face as well.

The garment’s name alone does not determine which body part a Qur’anic command requires covering, to what extent, or by what method.

Such a judgment also requires examination of the verb, prepositions, context, and other relevant verses.

Was jilbāb used only for free women?

Some historical interpretations of the Al-Aḥzāb verse distinguish free women’s garments from those of enslaved women.

This does not follow from jilbāb’s lexical meaning alone.

A garment’s name does not in itself determine its owner’s legal status.

The verse itself also addresses the women of the believers without using “free women” as a direct restriction upon the command.

Restricting the word to one social class’s exclusive clothing therefore requires independent historical and Qur’anic evidence.

Are clothing and the harasser’s responsibility the same issue?

Al-Aḥzāb 33:59 connects covering with women being recognized and unharmed.

Observing a clothing command and bearing responsibility for harming someone else are nevertheless separate issues.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:58 explicitly condemns unjustly harming believing men and women.

The jilbāb verse therefore does not permit harming someone dressed differently or transfer responsibility from the perpetrator to the person harmed.

This distinction follows from the network of verses and should remain separate from jilbāb’s lexical meaning.

12. Translation proposals for Forqan

Equivalents in this family should follow the particular derivative and context.

For jalaba in the transfer sense, “brought,” “brought elsewhere,” or “drove from one place to another” are appropriate.

For ijtalaba, “obtained,” “brought from elsewhere,” or “drew toward oneself” may fit the context.

Jalb in economic usage concerns bringing goods or livestock.

Jalīb means brought from elsewhere.

In sound-related usage, jalabah can be rendered “clamor,” “noise,” or “the tumult of a crowd.”

In the covering branch, julbah can concern a wound crust or leather covering, according to context.

For ajlaba ʿalayhi, suitable equivalents include “gathered forces against him,” “mobilized against him,” or, in a military setting, “charged against him.”

The existing Persian Forqan translation at Al-Isrāʾ 17:64 reads, rendered here in English:

“And charge against them with your cavalry and infantry.”

This fits wa-ajlib ʿalayhim bi-khaylika wa-rajilika, conveying mobilization and attack.

The root explanation should nevertheless clarify that the verb extends beyond military charging and also connects with gathering forces and rousing through shouting.

“Outer garment” is a suitable equivalent for jilbāb.

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:59, jalābībihinna is rendered “their outer garments.”

The complete expression is:

“يُدْنِينَ عَلَيْهِنَّ مِن جَلَابِيبِهِنَّ”

The current Persian Forqan translation reads, rendered here in English:

“To draw from their outer garments over themselves.”

This keeps the garment’s name distinct from the action of drawing it over oneself.

The lexical explanation can add that early sources define jilbāb as a loose garment, an enveloping covering, a shirt, or something worn over other clothes.

Without evidence, the covering’s name should not be converted directly into one modern garment design or a complete rule about covering a particular body part.

Jalābīb is the plural of jilbāb. Its Qur’anic usage concerns women’s garments, rather than mobilizing forces or clamor.

13. Conclusion

Arabic J L B has two important semantic branches: bringing and gathering something from one place to another, and covering or enclosing something.

In the first branch, jalaba concerns moving and bringing. This appears in herding, trade, transporting goods, and moving forces.

In mobilization contexts, ajlaba ʿalayhi can concern gathering forces against another or rousing through shouting and clamor.

In the second branch, julbah concerns coverings such as wound crusts and leather sheaths, while jilbāb names a human garment or covering.

Pre-Islamic poetic witnesses illuminate both fields. ʿAlqamah ibn ʿAbadah uses jalb for moving horses; in Janūb’s lament for her brother ʿAmr Dhū al-Kalb, jalābīb names maidens’ garments.

These witnesses indicate both branches were familiar in early Arabic. They do not establish that every historical use developed from one image.

The Qur’an uses this root twice.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:64, ajlib addresses Satan in a setting of mobilizing forces against human beings. Combined with cavalry and infantry, it depicts attack and pressure.

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:59, jalābībihinna concerns women’s coverings. The command concerns drawing them over the women themselves, with a stated purpose involving recognition and avoiding harm.

These usages should not be confused.

Ajlib in Al-Isrāʾ does not mean covering, while jalābīb in Al-Aḥzāb does not mean gathering forces or rousing through sound.

The noun jilbāb also does not determine a garment’s exact design, color, size, or every detail of bodily coverage. Where necessary, these require the verse’s construction, linguistic evidence, and other relevant verses.

For Forqan, “charge against them” conveys the contextual verb in Al-Isrāʾ 17:64, while “their outer garments” is a natural, defensible equivalent in Al-Aḥzāb 33:59.

This study’s principal finding is that J L B encompasses both transfer and mobilization, and covering. Neither branch should be imposed upon every other derivative without evidence.

Each form’s meaning follows from its morphology, accompanying particles, and actual context. These distinctions allow both Qur’anic usages to be understood without invented derivations or the insertion of unstated historical or exegetical details.
