1. Root Meaning and Its Lexical Branches

In Arabic, sabab denotes something through which a person reaches something else or by which two things are connected. It may be a rope for climbing, a route to a destination, a means of doing something, or a bond between people.

Lisān al-ʿArab gives a broad definition: kullu shayʾin yutawaṣṣalu bihi ilā ghayrihi, “anything by which something else can be reached.”

Lisān al-ʿArab: means of access and connection

This definition illustrates the word’s semantic range. Sabab need not be something that brings an event into existence; it can be a route, means, or relationship making access to a destination possible.

Qur’anic sabab therefore differs from its common use in modern Persian. Expressions such as “the cause of illness” or “the cause of rain” usually refer to a factor contributing to an event’s occurrence. Arabic also recognizes that meaning, but not every Qur’anic use of sabab can be translated as a productive cause.

In the Qur’an, sabab may denote a route to a destination, a means of acting, a rope, or bonds connecting people.

The S B B family also has another branch: sabba, to insult, revile, or speak with contemptuous abuse.

This branch appears in the Qur’an as tasubbū and fa-yasubbū. Although their consonants place them under the same triliteral entry, their meaning differs from sabab as a route or means.

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies cutting and severing in part of the S B B family and connects insulting with it. In his explanation, insult can represent a severing of respect and relations between people.

For sabab as a rope, he then considers another possible branch concerning length and extension.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: cutting, insult, and possible extension branch

This distinction matters. One lexical entry may contain several semantic branches whose historical relationship is not entirely clear. All uses of sabb and sabab need not be derived from a single original image.

The root occurs eleven times in ten Qur’anic verses: nine nouns, sabab and its plural asbāb, and two instances of the verb sabba in one verse.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: nine nouns and two verbs

Both branches must be preserved in studying the root. Omitting insult would ignore part of its Qur’anic use, while reducing the whole family to cutting would fail to account for the broad field of routes and means.

2. The Tangible Sabab: Rope, Extension, and Access

An early use of sabab is a rope through which a person gains access to a place or climbs to it.

In Al-Mufradāt, al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī writes: al-sababu al-ḥablu alladhī yuṣʿadu bihi al-nakhlu, “sabab is the rope used for climbing a date palm.”

Al-Rāghib’s Al-Mufradāt: a rope for climbing date palms

In this image, the rope connects a person with the place they wish to reach. Without that means, the destination may be inaccessible.

This reveals an important feature of sabab: it is a means of connection or access. Its function and value lie in opening a way to something else.

Some sources apply sabab specifically to a rope with one end suspended or attached somewhere. That qualification should not define every use, however, since Arabic also records routes, bonds, and other means of access.

Early Poetic Evidence

In Arabic poetry, asbāb al-samāʾ can denote routes, directions, or means of reaching the heights of the sky.

The following verse is attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā:

وَمَنْ هَابَ أَسْبَابَ الْمَنَايَا يَنَلْنَهُ
وَلَوْ رَامَ أَسْبَابَ السَّمَاءِ بِسُلَّمِ

wa-man hāba asbāba al-manāyā yanalnahu / wa-law rāma asbāba al-samāʾi bi-sullami

It means that death reaches someone who fears its avenues and circumstances, even if he seeks the ways of heaven by a ladder.

Khizānat al-Adab: Zuhayr’s verse with this wording

Asbāb al-manāyā concerns routes or circumstances leading to death, while asbāb al-samāʾ appears in an image of ascending by a ladder.

These uses show that the word can concern both access to a place and a way of reaching an outcome.

Al-Aʿshā’s poetry also contains asbāb al-samāʾ:

لَئِنْ كُنْتَ فِي جُبٍّ ثَمَانِينَ قَامَةً
وَرُقِّيتَ أَسْبَابَ السَّمَاءِ بِسُلَّمِ

la-in kunta fī jubbin thamānīna qāmatan / wa-ruqqīta asbāba al-samāʾi bi-sullami

Verse rendering: “Even if you were in a well eighty times a person’s height in depth and were raised to the ways of heaven by a ladder…” The following verse completes the warning.

The poet describes someone who cannot escape the consequence intended, even if he is in a very deep well or raised by a ladder to the heights of the sky.

Here too, asbāb al-samāʾ concerns ascent and access to heights.

Lisān al-ʿArab: al-Aʿshā’s witness and continuation

These witnesses show that the use of sabab for a route or means of access is not derived solely from Qur’anic exegesis. It is also present in early Arabic poetry.

They do not establish that asbāb al-samāʾ must always refer to actual ropes or specific material routes in the sky. In poetry such expressions may form part of an exaggerated image of reaching a distant, difficult place.

Semantic Extension from Rope to Route and Bond

Arabic also uses sabab for anything that makes access to something else possible.

People may reach a goal through knowledge, tools, cooperation, power, travel, or a social relationship. Any of these can fall within the broad field of sabab when it provides a way to a desired end.

Human bonds can be understood in the same light.

Just as a rope connects two points, kinship, friendship, or dependence can establish a bond between people.

This imagery should not become a definite claim about the historical order of meanings. What the evidence establishes is the presence of rope, route, means, and bond in Arabic usage.

A brief definition of sabab should therefore encompass both a tangible rope and intangible means and connections, without reducing it to an excessively general word such as thing or factor.

3. Asbāb in Dhū al-Qarnayn’s Story: Resources and Routes of Access

The story of Dhū al-Qarnayn in Al-Kahf provides one of the clearest Qur’anic sequences for understanding sabab.

Al-Kahf 18:84

إِنَّا مَكَّنَّا لَهُ فِي الْأَرْضِ وَآتَيْنَاهُ مِنْ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ سَبَبًا

innā makkannā lahu fī al-arḍi wa-ātaynāhu min kulli shayʾin sababan

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“We empowered him in the land and gave him a means to everything.”

Sababan here follows makkannā lahu fī al-arḍi, the statement of his empowerment in the land.

Tamkīn concerns possessing the power and ability to establish oneself and act in the land. The verse then says that Dhū al-Qarnayn was given a sabab to everything.

In context, sabab is a means or resource enabling him to act and reach his objective.

It could include geographical routes, practical resources, necessary knowledge, organizational power, or suitable tools. The verse does not list these possibilities individually.

Min kulli shayʾin sababan should therefore not be turned into a claim that Dhū al-Qarnayn possessed every possible kind of knowledge or owned everything in the world. The expression concerns his capacity and action, describing broad provision of the means needed for his undertakings.

The next verse states:

Al-Kahf 18:85

فَأَتْبَعَ سَبَبًا

fa-atbaʿa sababan

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“So he followed a route.”

This verse shows that sabab in practical use can be a route someone follows.

Dhū al-Qarnayn does more than possess resources: he uses them and follows his course.

The same expression appears twice more: thumma atbaʿa sababan in Al-Kahf 18:89 and Al-Kahf 18:92.

In all three cases the expression connects with movement and arrival at a destination in the subsequent narrative.

Sabab here therefore does not have only the abstract meaning of cause. It concerns a route or means employed in movement and action.

The contrast between ātaynāhu … sababan and atbaʿa sababan also deserves attention.

The former concerns possession of a means; the following verses concern pursuing and employing it.

Having a resource is thus different from using it. In the Qur’anic narrative, Dhū al-Qarnayn turns his resources into action and movement.

Fa-atbaʿa sababan alone does not establish that all his journeys were planned in advance according to a specific map or technical system. Such details cannot be extracted from the word itself.

The preferable meaning here is a route or means of access, followed toward the next destination.

Al-Rāghib’s Al-Mufradāt: sabab in Dhū al-Qarnayn’s story

4. The Ways of the Heavens and Severed Bonds

A Rope in Al-Ḥajj

Al-Ḥajj 22:15

مَن كَانَ يَظُنُّ أَن لَّن يَنصُرَهُ اللَّهُ فِي الدُّنْيَا وَالْآخِرَةِ فَلْيَمْدُدْ بِسَبَبٍ إِلَى السَّمَاءِ ثُمَّ لْيَقْطَعْ فَلْيَنظُرْ هَلْ يُذْهِبَنَّ كَيْدُهُ مَا يَغِيظُ

man kāna yaẓunnu an lan yanṣurahu Allāhu fī al-dunyā wa-l-ākhirati fa-l-yamdud bi-sababin ilā al-samāʾi thumma l-yaqṭaʿ fa-l-yanẓur hal yudhhibanna kayduhu mā yaghīẓu

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Whoever supposes that The God will not help him in this world and the Hereafter, let him extend a rope toward the sky, then cut; then let him see whether his scheme removes what enrages him.”

Bi-sababin accompanies fa-l-yamdud, “let him extend,” and the direction ilā al-samāʾi, “toward the sky.”

This combination clearly suits a tangible rope: something is extended, and cutting is then mentioned.

Accordingly, most lexicographers and commentators explain sabab here as a rope.

There are nevertheless differing interpretations of al-samāʾ, the pronoun in yanṣurahu, and the object of l-yaqṭaʿ.

One reading understands samāʾ as the roof of a house or the space overhead, explaining cutting through an image of an enraged person’s futile action.

Another takes samāʾ as the sky, picturing the person’s inability to cut off help coming from God.

These differences require a full interpretation of the verse and its context. For the word sabab itself, the central point remains clear: rope or an extended means of access has direct linguistic support in this expression.

Disagreement over the whole verse should therefore not become unlimited uncertainty about the meaning of sabab.

Al-Qurṭubī: the rope and interpretations of Al-Ḥajj 22:15

Asbāb in Ṣād

Ṣād 38:10

أَمْ لَهُم مُّلْكُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ وَمَا بَيْنَهُمَا فَلْيَرْتَقُوا فِي الْأَسْبَابِ

am lahum mulku al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍi wa-mā baynahumā fa-l-yartaqū fī al-asbābi

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Or does sovereignty over the heavens, the earth, and what is between them belong to them? Then let them ascend by the ways.”

Al-asbāb accompanies fa-l-yartaqū, “let them ascend.”

Irtiqāʾ denotes going upward, supporting routes or means of ascent and access as the meaning of asbāb.

Ṣād 38:9 asks whether the treasures of the Lord’s mercy are in the opponents’ possession. The question of sovereignty over the heavens and earth follows.

Fa-l-yartaqū fī al-asbābi thus appears in a context challenging their claims to power and control.

Asbāb can here be understood as routes or means of access to higher levels. The expression does not necessarily report actual, specific ladders or ropes in the sky.

For translation, routes or means of ascent fit better than causes.

The Ways of the Heavens in Pharaoh’s Speech

Ghāfir 40:36

وَقَالَ فِرْعَوْنُ يَا هَامَانُ ابْنِ لِي صَرْحًا لَّعَلِّي أَبْلُغُ الْأَسْبَابَ

wa-qāla firʿawnu yā hāmānu ibni lī ṣarḥan laʿallī ablughu al-asbāba

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Pharaoh said: Hāmān, build me a lofty structure, so that I may reach the ways.”

Ghāfir 40:37

أَسْبَابَ السَّمَاوَاتِ فَأَطَّلِعَ إِلَىٰ إِلَٰهِ مُوسَىٰ وَإِنِّي لَأَظُنُّهُ كَاذِبًا وَكَذَٰلِكَ زُيِّنَ لِفِرْعَوْنَ سُوءُ عَمَلِهِ وَصُدَّ عَنِ السَّبِيلِ وَمَا كَيْدُ فِرْعَوْنَ إِلَّا فِي تَبَابٍ

asbāba al-samāwāti fa-aṭṭaliʿa ilā ilāhi mūsā wa-innī la-aẓunnuhu kādhiban wa-kadhālika zuyyina li-firʿawna sūʾu ʿamalihi wa-ṣudda ʿani al-sabīli wa-mā kaydu firʿawna illā fī tabābin

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“The ways of the heavens, so that I may look upon the deity of Moses; indeed, I consider him a liar. Thus Pharaoh’s evil conduct was made attractive to him, and he was barred from the way. Pharaoh’s scheming led only to ruin.”

Pharaoh orders a lofty structure built so he can reach al-asbāb. The next verse specifies them as asbāb al-samāwāt.

Routes and means of access again fit the context.

Asbāba al-samāwāti in the second verse further explains al-asbāba in the first.

These are Pharaoh’s quoted words. The Qur’an does not necessarily endorse his claim that such ways can be reached.

Pharaoh continues that he wishes to look upon Moses’ deity while considering Moses a liar. The Qur’an then describes the beautification of Pharaoh’s evil conduct and the ruin of his scheme.

The verse therefore cannot establish specific material corridors between earth and the heavens.

In Pharaoh’s speech the word expresses routes or means he imagines will lead to his claimed destination.

Sabab names a way of access, while the reality of the claimed route is a separate question from the word’s lexical meaning.

Lisān al-ʿArab: heavenly ways and levels in asbāb

Severed Asbāb in Al-Baqarah

Al-Baqarah 2:166

إِذْ تَبَرَّأَ الَّذِينَ اتُّبِعُوا مِنَ الَّذِينَ اتَّبَعُوا وَرَأَوُا الْعَذَابَ وَتَقَطَّعَتْ بِهِمُ الْأَسْبَابُ

idh tabarraʾa alladhīna uttubiʿū mina alladhīna ittabaʿū wa-raʾaw al-ʿadhāba wa-taqaṭṭaʿat bihimu al-asbābu

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“When those who were followed disown those who followed, and they see the punishment, and the bonds between them are severed.”

Al-asbāb here no longer concerns routes of ascent or means of travel, but human relationships.

The verse describes people who were followed in worldly life and those who followed them. On seeing the punishment, the leaders disown their followers, and the asbāb between them break.

Asbāb here denotes the ties, dependencies, and relationships connecting them in worldly life.

Those relationships might have rested on affection, influence, power, interests, or allegiance. The verse does not enumerate all such cases, but describes the severing of what connected the parties.

Taqaṭṭaʿat, “were severed,” is also significant. Severing suits the image of rope and connection associated with sabab.

Just as a rope can connect two things and then be cut, human relationships can be established and later disappear.

Al-asbāb should nevertheless not be translated as actual ropes here. The intended bonds are those between leaders and followers.

Al-Baqarah 2:167 then describes the followers’ regret: they wish to return and disown their leaders just as the leaders disowned them.

This continuation identifies severed asbāb as the collapse of relationships and dependencies that had appeared firm in worldly life.

Sabab can therefore be understood here as a bond establishing a relationship. It fits the general field of connection and access, while its referent is social and human.

Al-Ṭabarī: severed bonds and dependencies

5. Sabba: Insult and the Violation of Respect

The other branch of this word family occurs in the Qur’an through the verb sabba.

Al-Anʿām 6:108

وَلَا تَسُبُّوا الَّذِينَ يَدْعُونَ مِن دُونِ اللَّهِ فَيَسُبُّوا اللَّهَ عَدْوًا بِغَيْرِ عِلْمٍ كَذَٰلِكَ زَيَّنَّا لِكُلِّ أُمَّةٍ عَمَلَهُمْ ثُمَّ إِلَىٰ رَبِّهِم مَّرْجِعُهُمْ فَيُنَبِّئُهُم بِمَا كَانُوا يَعْمَلُونَ

wa-lā tasubbū alladhīna yadʿūna min dūni Allāhi fa-yasubbū Allāha ʿadwan bi-ghayri ʿilmin kadhālika zayyannā li-kulli ummatin ʿamalahum thumma ilā rabbihim marjiʿuhum fa-yunabbiʾuhum bi-mā kānū yaʿmalūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Do not insult those whom they invoke apart from The God, lest they insult The God in transgression without knowledge. Thus We have made every community’s conduct attractive to it. Then to their Lord is their return, and He will inform them of what they used to do.”

This verse contains tasubbū and fa-yasubbū.

Both come from the triliteral sabba yasubbu. Tasubbū is jussive after prohibitive lā; fa-yasubbū is subjunctive after the causal fāʾ, with an implied an, expressing the consequence of the prohibited conduct. In both forms, omission of nūn marks the inflection.

Sabba here means to insult, revile, or speak contemptuous abuse.

This meaning should be distinguished from criticism, disagreement, and explaining a belief’s falsity.

Other Qur’anic verses explicitly criticize idolatry and associating partners with God. Prohibiting sabb therefore does not prohibit examining or criticizing false beliefs.

The difference concerns the kind of speech. Criticism can proceed through evidence and clear explanation; insult involves contempt and abuse.

Al-Anʿām 6:108 connects this distinction with the social consequences of conduct. It warns that insulting what others invoke can lead them to insult God in transgression and ignorance.

The issue is not merely an offensive word or sentence, but reciprocal conduct beginning with insult and leading to further insult.

Al-Anʿām 6:106 and Al-Anʿām 6:107 provide relevant context: they command following revelation and turning away from the polytheists, and state that the Prophet was not appointed their keeper or agent.

Calling people to truth is therefore different from claiming a right to humiliate them. The Qur’an can criticize associationism while prohibiting insults directed at what others worship.

Al-Anʿām 6:108 continues with everyone’s return to the Lord and their being informed of their deeds. Final judgment retains its place, while human speech also carries responsibility.

Al-Ṭabarī: the prohibition of insult and its reciprocal consequence

Did Insult Develop from Cutting?

Lexical sources connect sabb with cutting and severing as well as insult.

Ibn Fāris accepts this connection, explaining insult as a form of severing relations and respect.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: insult and severance

This semantic account is understandable. Insult can break mutual respect, turn friendship into enmity, and destroy a social relationship.

It explains a connection between meanings but does not by itself prove their historical order of development.

Nor does it establish that every severed relationship is called sabb in Arabic or that every insult necessarily ends a relationship completely.

Tasubbū and fa-yasubbū directly mean insulting in Al-Anʿām 6:108. Severing relations should not replace that translation.

Arabic also records al-sibāb, al-musābbah, subbah, and sabbāb for insult, reciprocal abuse, a disgrace or fault, and a person who habitually insults.

These derivatives do not occur in the Qur’an, but show that the insulting branch of S B B has an independent, established lexical use.

Al-Miṣbāḥ al-Munīr and Mukhtār al-Ṣiḥāḥ: forms of insult and disgrace

6. Sabab Compared with Related Words, and Conclusion

Understanding sabab precisely requires distinguishing it from several related words.

Sabīl: A way or path, whether a route someone travels or a course they follow. Sabab also sometimes denotes a route, but has a wider range encompassing rope, tools, resources, and bonds. The words can overlap in a particular use without being complete synonyms.

Study of S B L

Wasīlah: Something by which a person approaches an objective or gains proximity and access. This is close to sabab, but wasīlah can highlight approaching and drawing near, while sabab emphasizes the route or factor establishing access. This is a semantic tendency, not an absolute boundary.

Ḥabl: A rope or cord. Sabab can name a rope, but not every sabab is a rope. In Al-Baqarah 2:166, asbāb are human relationships and bonds, rather than material cords.

Study of H B L

ʿIllah: In philosophical and scientific usage, a cause contributing to something’s occurrence or realization. Sabab can be synonymous or closely related in that field, but its Qur’anic uses are broader. In Dhū al-Qarnayn’s story, it primarily denotes a means of action or access, rather than necessarily a productive cause.

Ṣilah and Rābiṭah: Connection and a bond between things or people. This approaches al-asbāb in Al-Baqarah 2:166. Beyond relationship, sabab can also be a route through which a destination is reached.

Shatm: Insult and abuse, clearly overlapping with sabb in Al-Anʿām 6:108. This branch does not imply that sabab means insult in the other verses.

These comparisons show why one Persian equivalent cannot translate S B B in every verse.

Connection and access are central in the sabab family. A rope brings someone to a higher place; a route enables travel; a means makes action possible; and a social bond connects people.

In the sabba family, insult and abuse are clear. A possible historical connection with cutting or severing should not remove that direct meaning from its definition.

Semantic Conclusion

S B B has two principal Qur’anic branches.

First, sabab and asbāb, denoting a route, means, rope, or bond. Their shared feature is enabling connection or access from one thing to another.

Second, sabba, meaning to insult or speak contemptuous abuse in Qur’anic use.

Both branches should remain in the root definition without presenting their uncertain historical connection as an established fact.

In the Qur’an, sabab may be a resource available to Dhū al-Qarnayn, a route he follows, a rope extended for access, the ways of the heavens in Pharaoh’s speech, or bonds severed between leaders and followers.

Restricting sabab to the familiar meaning of a productive cause cannot account for all these uses.

Brief Root Meaning

Sabab: A route, means, or bond enabling access and connection between two things.

Sabba: To insult and offend through contemptuous speech. Lexical sources also connect this branch with cutting.

Meanings of the Qur’anic Forms and Translation Proposals

Al-asbābu — Al-Baqarah 2:166: Bonds and dependencies established between leaders and followers. Proposed translation: “bonds.”

Tasubbū — Al-Anʿām 6:108: You insult. In the prohibition: “do not insult.”

Fa-yasubbū — Al-Anʿām 6:108: Then they insult; the consequence of reciprocal abuse.

Sababan — Al-Kahf 18:84: A means or resource for reaching an objective. Proposed translation: “a means.”

Sababan — Al-Kahf 18:85, Al-Kahf 18:89, and Al-Kahf 18:92: A route or course Dhū al-Qarnayn follows. Proposed translation: “a route.”

Bi-sababin — Al-Ḥajj 22:15: By means of a rope. Proposed translation: “with a rope.”

Al-asbābi — Ṣād 38:10: Routes or means of ascent and access. Proposed translation: “ways.”

Al-asbāba — Ghāfir 40:36: Routes or means of access in Pharaoh’s speech. Proposed translation: “ways.”

Asbāba al-samāwāti — Ghāfir 40:37: The ways of the heavens. Proposed translation: “the ways of the heavens.”

The brief root meaning should distinguish these branches while showing how each derivative varies in context.

Sabab in the Qur’an does not name only the cause of an event. It can be something through which a person travels, reaches a destination, connects with someone else, or finds a way to act.

Sabb, meaning insult, recalls another branch of the same lexical entry, where speech turns toward contempt and abuse rather than dialogue and understanding.

The two semantic branches differ. Each must be understood from its own evidence, rather than through a unity constructed solely from shared consonants.
