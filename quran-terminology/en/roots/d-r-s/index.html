Study and repeated engagement with a text, learning, and the wearing away of traces

1. Introducing the root and its semantic field

The Arabic root D R S has two major branches: wearing away, fading, and the disappearance of traces; and reading, rereading, and sustained study of a text to become familiar with it.

In early Arabic, darasa al-rasmu described a trace left on the ground that time, wind, or rain had faded or effaced. Thawbun darīsun was a garment worn through use.

By contrast, darasa al-kitāba means to read and study a book. Attention and returning to the text matter; repeated reading can lead to familiarity, memorization, and mastery.

A relationship between the branches can be explained. Repeated passage along a path may alter its marks; continued use wears a garment; returning to writing makes it more familiar to its reader. The outcomes differ: a physical trace may disappear while a text’s meaning remains in the reader’s mind.

Dars should therefore not mean effacing in every use, just as not every form means teaching or study.

The root occurs six times in the Qur’an, in two principal forms: inflections of the verb darasa and the verbal noun dirāsah.

All six occurrences in the reading of Ḥafṣ from ʿĀṣim, which underlies this article, concern reading, studying, or learning a book or discourse. None directly describes worn clothing or the fading traces of a settlement.

The tangible branch nevertheless matters to the word’s linguistic background, especially because a variant reading of Al-Anʿām 6:105 introduces becoming old or effaced.

2. Qur’anic forms and the root’s meaning in early Arabic

D R S occurs six times in six verses, with two principal morphological categories.

The Form I verb darasa: five occurrences

  • Tadrusūna in Āl ʿImrān 3:79
  • Darasta in Al-Anʿām 6:105
  • Wa-darasū in Al-Aʿrāf 7:169
  • Yadrusūnahā in Sabaʾ 34:44
  • Tadrusūna in Al-Qalam 68:37

The verbal noun dirāsah: one occurrence

  • Dirāsatihim in Al-Anʿām 6:156

Differences between tadrusūna, darasū, and darasta concern tense, person, number, and sentence structure. They are not different roots.

Other forms in this Arabic family include:

Darasa–yadrusu: he read or studied; or, in another use, a trace wore away and disappeared.

Dars: study and rereading; elsewhere, effacement or wearing away. Dictionaries also record dirs for a faded trace or worn garment.

Dirāsah: study and engagement with a text through reading, learning, or memorization.

Dārasa: he studied and reviewed with another person.

Tadārasa: they studied and reviewed together.

Darrasa: he taught or repeatedly read and learned a text, depending on the construction.

Mudarris: someone who teaches.

Madrūs: something studied; in the other branch, something worn away.

Darīs: a worn, old garment or other object.

Dāris: a reader or student; elsewhere, something whose traces are fading.

Madras and midrās: a place for study and reading in recorded lexical uses.

Madrasah: a place of teaching and study.

In the reading of Ḥafṣ from ʿĀṣim, only the Form I verb and dirāsah occur among these forms. Dārasta in another reading of Al-Anʿām 6:105 is discussed separately.

Later institutional senses, such as school in its modern sense, should not be presented as Qur’anic derivatives.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: five verbs and one verbal noun in six verses

The semantic core in Maqāyīs al-Lughah

Ibn Fāris explains the root’s principle through three words:

خَفَاءٍ وَخَفْضٍ وَعَفَاءٍ

khafāʾin wa-khafḍin wa-ʿafāʾin

“Hiddenness, lowering, and the effacement of traces.”

He then discusses a faint path, effaced traces of a settlement, and a worn garment.

For studying a book, he explains that the reader follows what was read before, like someone following a path.

This analysis seeks a relationship between traces effaced through repeated passage and a text followed through rereading.

It should not become a definitive history of derivation. A semantic relationship alone cannot establish when or how the study sense developed from wearing away.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: fading traces and following a text

Lisān al-ʿArab also records both principal branches.

Darasa al-shayʾu concerns something fading or wearing away.

Darasa al-kitāba concerns reading and studying a book.

Explanations of the latter emphasize returning repeatedly to the text and becoming more familiar with it.

Sustained study and rereading are therefore important features of this use. They do not mean that every occurrence necessarily entails complete memorization, correct understanding, or acting on the text.

3. Tangible uses in early dictionaries and a pre-Islamic poetic witness

The fading traces of a dwelling or settlement

Darasa al-manzilu described a dwelling or site whose traces time had faded or erased.

In the Arabian desert, tent sites, travelers’ stopping places, and traces of habitation could disappear beneath sand and dust.

Wind, rain, and repeated passage also changed and wore away these marks.

A rasmun dārisun is thus a trace that may still partly remain but has lost its original clarity and condition.

This sense was well known in early Arabic poetry.

A witness from Imruʾ al-Qays’s Muʿallaqah

The pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays says in his Muʿallaqah:

وَإِنَّ شِفَائِي عَبْرَةٌ مُهْرَاقَةٌ
فَهَلْ عِنْدَ رَسْمٍ دَارِسٍ مِنْ مُعَوَّلِ

wa-inna shifāʾī ʿabratun muhrāqatun / fa-hal ʿinda rasmin dārisin min muʿawwali

“My remedy is a tear poured out; yet beside a worn and fading trace, is there any refuge—or any useful place for weeping?”

The poet stands at his beloved’s former dwelling, looking at marks worn by time.

Rasmin dārisin describes a remaining trace no longer as visible as before.

Reading, teaching, and study are not at issue here.

Dāris describes the condition of a physical trace.

This witness illuminates the branch of wearing away and effaced traces in Arabic before the Qur’an.

It also shows that dars need not mean total destruction. Something may have changed until only a faint mark remains.

Imruʾ al-Qays’s Muʿallaqah with Al-Zawzanī’s commentary: rasmin dārisin

A garment wearing out

Dictionaries record thawbun darīsun for an old, worn garment.

Wearing, washing, contact, and repeated use gradually reduce a garment’s original quality.

Here wear results from use and the passage of time.

The image resembles fading settlement traces, though its object differs.

In one case, marks of a place fade; in the other, a garment’s fabric or appearance wears away.

Neither use concerns learning a book.

Threshing grain and repeated passage

Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Lisān al-ʿArab record another use concerning wheat and grain:

دَرَسْتُ الْحِنْطَةَ

darastu al-ḥinṭata

“I threshed the wheat,” placing it beneath feet or threshing implements to separate grain from the ears.

Some Arabic dialects use dirās for this action.

This use concerns pressure and repeated action.

Repeated beating or treading changes the ears’ original structure and separates their grain.

Ibn Fāris compares this with passing along a path and wearing away its traces.

This does not make studying a book literally mean “beating the writing.”

These are related uses within a lexical family. An analogy may explain their relationship but should not enter the direct translation of darasa al-kitāba.

Training and becoming accustomed

Early Arabic also records darasa al-nāqata for training and taming a camel.

Through repeated training, the animal becomes accustomed to a condition or command.

Dictionaries invoke a similar relationship when explaining book study: repeated reading can make a text familiar and easier to memorize.

This does not mean that every act of study tames a person or requires unquestioning obedience to a text.

Studying a book concerns reading, knowing, and learning; its results vary with the text and the manner of study.

Study and repeated reading

Lisān al-ʿArab connects studying a book with repeatedly returning to it until it becomes familiar.

Darastu al-sūrata is also recorded for reading and reviewing a surah until it becomes familiar or memorized.

Study is not unrelated to reading; reading is an important part of it.

Study can nevertheless involve more than reading once.

Someone may review a text repeatedly, examine its words, compare its parts, and work to memorize or understand it.

Not all these activities are necessary conditions for using dars.

The verb can simply concern reading and study; the extent of understanding, memorization, or critical analysis requires other evidence.

4. Study and teaching in Qur’anic usage

A. Āl ʿImrān 3:79: teaching and studying

Āl ʿImrān 3:79

مَا كَانَ لِبَشَرٍ أَنْ يُؤْتِيَهُ اللَّهُ الْكِتَابَ وَالْحُكْمَ وَالنُّبُوَّةَ ثُمَّ يَقُولَ لِلنَّاسِ كُونُوا عِبَادًا لِي مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ وَلَٰكِنْ كُونُوا رَبَّانِيِّينَ بِمَا كُنْتُمْ تُعَلِّمُونَ الْكِتَابَ وَبِمَا كُنْتُمْ تَدْرُسُونَ

mā kāna li-basharin an yuʾtiyahu Allāhu al-kitāba wa-al-ḥukma wa-al-nubuwwata thumma yaqūla li-al-nāsi kūnū ʿibādan lī min dūni Allāhi wa-lākin kūnū rabbāniyyīna bi-mā kuntum tuʿallimūna al-kitāba wa-bi-mā kuntum tadrusūna

“It is not for a human being to whom The God gives the Book, judgment, and prophethood then to tell people, ‘Be servants of mine instead of The God.’ Rather, be devoted to the Lord through teaching the Book and through studying it.”

Two verbs occur alongside each other:

“تُعَلِّمُونَ الْكِتَابَ”

and:

“تَدْرُسُونَ”

The first is from ʿ L M and concerns teaching the Book.

The second is from D R S and describes studying and engaging with the text.

Their combination shows that study and teaching are related but distinct.

Someone may study a book without teaching others. A teacher should also know it, but teaching is an action that conveys knowledge to someone else.

Does dars mean teaching?

In contemporary Persian, dars dādan and tadrīs kardan are close expressions for teaching.

Yet Āl ʿImrān 3:79 uses two different verbs.

Tuʿallimūna in the reading of Ḥafṣ is Form II and means teaching. Another reading has taʿlamūna, “you know”; this discussion distinguishes the teaching form present in the text used here.

Tadrusūna is Form I and here concerns studying the Book.

Translating both as “you were teaching” would erase the distinction.

“You were teaching and studying” preserves it better.

Al-Qurṭubī: teaching and study, and readings of taʿlamūna/tuʿallimūna

Does studying the Book alone make someone rabbānī?

The verse connects the call to be devoted to the Lord with teaching and studying the Book.

The root D R S itself does not mean being rabbānī.

Rabbānī is an independent word whose meaning requires its own root and context.

Nor does the verse establish that everyone who repeatedly reads scripture necessarily becomes devoted to the Lord in conduct and thought.

The Qur’an also describes people who knew or studied the Book but acted against its contents.

Study and teaching can provide knowledge and responsibility; truthfulness, trustworthiness, and right action must be assessed separately.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:79, receiving the Book, judgment, and prophethood does not authorize calling people to serve a human being instead of God.

This follows from the whole verse, rather than the lexical meaning of dars alone.

B. Al-Anʿām 6:156: study among two earlier communities

Al-Anʿām 6:156

أَنْ تَقُولُوا إِنَّمَا أُنْزِلَ الْكِتَابُ عَلَىٰ طَائِفَتَيْنِ مِنْ قَبْلِنَا وَإِنْ كُنَّا عَنْ دِرَاسَتِهِمْ لَغَافِلِينَ

an taqūlū innamā unzila al-kitābu ʿalā ṭāʾifatayni min qablinā wa-in kunnā ʿan dirāsatihim la-ghāfilīna

“Lest you say, ‘The Book was sent down only to two communities before us, and we were unaware of their study.’”

Dirāsatihim combines the verbal noun dirāsah with a plural pronoun.

Dirāsah concerns study and sustained reading.

The pronoun -him refers to the two communities mentioned earlier.

The construction concerns their study; the word itself does not mean their book or language.

Study and access to a book

The verse concerns people who might claim that the Book had been sent down only to earlier communities and that they were unaware of those communities’ study.

Dirāsah thus appears within communities’ relationship to revealed books.

The context concerns a lack of access to another community’s tradition of study or scriptural content.

Dirāsah should not be restricted to a particular language such as Hebrew or Syriac. The verse does not name their language.

Nor does the word establish that everyone in those communities studied with the same understanding.

Studying a book, understanding it correctly, and acting on it are three related but different matters.

C. Al-Aʿrāf 7:169: they studied the Book, yet...

Al-Aʿrāf 7:169

فَخَلَفَ مِنْ بَعْدِهِمْ خَلْفٌ وَرِثُوا الْكِتَابَ يَأْخُذُونَ عَرَضَ هَٰذَا الْأَدْنَىٰ وَيَقُولُونَ سَيُغْفَرُ لَنَا وَإِنْ يَأْتِهِمْ عَرَضٌ مِثْلُهُ يَأْخُذُوهُ أَلَمْ يُؤْخَذْ عَلَيْهِمْ مِيثَاقُ الْكِتَابِ أَنْ لَا يَقُولُوا عَلَى اللَّهِ إِلَّا الْحَقَّ وَدَرَسُوا مَا فِيهِ وَالدَّارُ الْآخِرَةُ خَيْرٌ لِلَّذِينَ يَتَّقُونَ أَفَلَا تَعْقِلُونَ

fa-khalafa min baʿdihim khalfun warithū al-kitāba yaʾkhudhūna ʿaraḍa hādhā al-adnā wa-yaqūlūna sa-yughfaru lanā wa-in yaʾtihim ʿaraḍun mithluhu yaʾkhudhūhu a-lam yuʾkhadh ʿalayhim mīthāqu al-kitābi an lā yaqūlū ʿalā Allāhi illā al-ḥaqqa wa-darasū mā fīhi wa-al-dāru al-ākhiratu khayrun li-alladhīna yattaqūna a-fa-lā taʿqilūna

“Then successors came after them who inherited the Book, taking the goods of this nearer life and saying, ‘We will be forgiven.’ If similar goods come to them, they take them again. Was the covenant of the Book not taken from them that they should say only the truth about The God? And they had studied its contents. The abode of the Hereafter is better for those who remain mindful. Will you not reason?”

This is among the root’s most significant Qur’anic uses.

A group has inherited the Book but pursues passing worldly gains, repeatedly behaving this way while claiming forgiveness.

The verse then recalls their covenant to speak only truth about God.

It continues:

“وَدَرَسُوا مَا فِيهِ”

They had studied what was in the Book.

Having a book and studying it

The verse begins with warithū al-kitāba.

This describes inheriting the Book.

It later adds wa-darasū mā fīhi.

These statements are distinct.

A person or community might receive a book from earlier generations without ever studying its contents.

Here both inheritance and study are stated.

The issue is therefore more than possessing scripture or belonging to a scriptural community. They had also engaged with its contents.

Study and intellectual responsibility

Here studying the Book is connected with the responsibility to speak truth about God.

When someone has studied a text and knows its contents, a claim of ignorance about matters clearly stated there deserves closer examination.

Darasū alone cannot establish that every member understood all the Book’s rulings correctly or knowingly violated them all.

Each person’s knowledge, intention, and responsibility require separate consideration.

The verse clearly states that the criticized group had inherited the Book and studied its contents, yet committed blameworthy acts.

Does darasū mean they wore out the Book?

Because the root also concerns wearing away and effacement, some lexical explanations connect darasū with wearing out the Book or abandoning action on it.

In the reading examined here, however, wa-darasū mā fīhi has the natural construction of studying the Book’s contents.

The verb has a specified object: mā fīhi, what is in it.

The context also concerns the Book’s covenant and speaking truth about God.

“They studied” therefore fits the construction and context better.

They are not criticized because study itself was wrong, but because their behavior conflicted with the Book’s responsibilities.

Studying a text can coexist with failing to act on it. Failure to act is not the verb’s lexical meaning.

5. Study, claims to knowledge, and absent evidence

A. Al-Anʿām 6:105: darasta in the opponents’ speech

Al-Anʿām 6:105

وَكَذَٰلِكَ نُصَرِّفُ الْآيَاتِ وَلِيَقُولُوا دَرَسْتَ وَلِنُبَيِّنَهُ لِقَوْمٍ يَعْلَمُونَ

wa-kadhālika nuṣarrifu al-āyāti wa-li-yaqūlū darasta wa-li-nubayyinahu li-qawmin yaʿlamūna

“Thus We present the signs in varied ways; and so that they may say, ‘You have studied’; and so that We may make it clear to people who know.” This translation retains the lām construction’s surface form. The lām in wa-li-yaqūlū is also interpreted as expressing outcome: opponents consequently say this, rather than their statement being endorsed.

Darasta is a past verb addressing one male.

Its direct meaning is “you studied” or “you learned.”

It occurs in the opponents’ speech, which the Qur’an reports here.

It should not become a direct divine statement about the source of Muḥammad’s knowledge.

Study as an allegation by opponents

In context, a group judges the signs and the source of Muḥammad’s discourse.

Al-Naḥl 16:103 reports a similar claim that a human teaches him.

Al-Furqān 25:5 reports opponents alleging earlier writings were copied for him or read to him.

Together these verses show that learning from human sources was one explanation some opponents offered for his message.

Darasta itself names no teacher, book, or place of study.

Such details should not enter the word’s translation without evidence.

The readings darasta and dārasta

Al-Anʿām 6:105 has an important reading variation.

In the reading examined here, darasta has no alif after dāl: “you studied.”

Another reading has dārasta.

This is Form III and can concern studying and reviewing with someone else.

This reading makes the opponents’ claim more explicitly concern Muḥammad’s educational interaction or shared study with others.

Form III alone does not establish that both participants had equal knowledge or identical roles in teaching and learning.

Commentaries explain this reading through learning or studying with scriptural communities. Identifying those supposedly involved requires separate historical investigation.

Readings involving darasat

Alongside darasta and dārasta, reading sources record darasat in Ibn ʿĀmir’s reading. The passive durisat is also reported from Qatādah. That latter transmission should not be presented without qualification as equivalent in standing to the established readings.

In one analysis, darasat concerns something becoming old and its traces fading.

On that reading, the opponents can be understood as claiming these sayings are old matters remaining from the past.

The passive durisat instead concerns something having been read or studied.

The variation shows that both semantic branches were recognized in the linguistic and reading traditions.

They should not all be merged into one translation.

In darasta, the primary meaning remains “you studied.”

Other forms require separate treatment as reading variants and linguistic analyses.

B. Sabaʾ 34:44: books not given to them for study

Sabaʾ 34:44

وَمَا آتَيْنَاهُمْ مِنْ كُتُبٍ يَدْرُسُونَهَا وَمَا أَرْسَلْنَا إِلَيْهِمْ قَبْلَكَ مِنْ نَذِيرٍ

wa-mā ātaynāhum min kutubin yadrusūnahā wa-mā arsalnā ilayhim qablaka min nadhīrin

“We had given them no books to study, nor had We sent them any warner before you.”

Yadrusūnahā is a present verb with an object pronoun.

The pronoun -hā refers to kutub, books.

It therefore means “they study those books.”

The sentence, however, is negative: wa-mā ātaynāhum min kutubin.

These are books that had not been given to the group for study.

Studying a book as support for a claim

In Sabaʾ 34:43, opponents make judgments about the signs and Muḥammad’s message.

Verse Sabaʾ 34:44 then recalls that they had received no books whose study could support such claims.

In Sabaʾ 34:46 they are invited to reflect and examine the messenger’s situation.

The sequence raises the issue of evidence and grounds for knowledge against their claims.

Yadrusūnahā itself does not mean proving or disproving a claim. It describes studying books.

Study’s potential to support knowledge follows from the sentence’s context.

The verse also concerns its particular audience. It does not establish that no Arab community or other person before Muḥammad ever studied a book.

C. Al-Qalam 68:37: “Do you have a book in which you study?”

Al-Qalam 68:37

أَمْ لَكُمْ كِتَابٌ فِيهِ تَدْرُسُونَ

am lakum kitābun fīhi tadrusūna

“Or do you have a book in which you study?”

Tadrusūna has the same form here as in Āl ʿImrān 3:79.

Its context differs, however.

Al-Qalam 68:35 asks whether those who submit and the offenders are to be treated alike.

Al-Qalam 68:36 then asks the audience how they judge.

Verse Al-Qalam 68:37 asks whether they have a book in which they have studied such a judgment.

Al-Qalam 68:38 states the content supposedly present in that book.

The question therefore concerns an authoritative written basis for their judgment.

Does possessing a book validate every claim?

The verse asks about a book containing the audience’s claim.

Dars does not mean that everything written in any book must be true.

A book can be studied, but its contents’ truth requires evidence.

Here the question challenges a group judging the relationship between offenders and those who submit.

Study’s relationship to valid argument follows from the surrounding verses. D R S does not itself mean decisive proof or established truth.

6. Dars and neighboring words

D R S and Q R ʾ: study and reading

Q R ʾ concerns reading in the Qur’an.

Qirāʾah can concern reading a text and expressing it in speech.

Qur’anic dars also includes reading, but its Arabic uses are more closely associated with returning to a text, rereading, and sustained familiarity.

No absolute boundary makes every qirāʾah a single reading and every dars several readings.

Reading can be repeated, and study can in a particular situation refer to one reading session.

The distinction concerns semantic emphasis and usage, rather than a fixed number of repetitions.

Study of Q R ʾ

D R S and T L W: study and recitation

Tilāwah in the T L W family concerns following and continuity; with books and signs it concerns reading and presenting a continuous text.

Dars in relation to a book focuses on study and engagement with the text.

Someone may recite a text for others while they listen.

Someone else may study alone, returning to passages to understand and learn them.

These activities can occur together but are distinct.

Āl ʿImrān 3:79 speaks of studying the Book, while many other verses speak of reciting it.

Preserving the distinction helps translation and research.

Study of T L W

D R S and R T L: study and measured reading

Tartīl concerns clear ordering and continuity in presenting speech.

Al-Muzzammil 73:4 speaks of tartīl of the Qur’an.

Dars instead concerns study and rereading.

Tartīl can describe the quality of reading; dars concerns engaging with a text to become familiar with and learn it.

Tadrusūna should therefore not always be translated as “you read with tartīl.”

D R S and D B R: study and reflection

Qur’anic tadabbur concerns considered reflection on speech and signs.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:82 connects it with examining the Qur’an’s consistency.

The direct use of dars concerns studying and engaging with a text.

Study can prepare for reflection, but studying alone does not establish sound reflection.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:169 provides a clear example: a group had studied the Book’s contents yet behaved against its responsibilities.

Study is an activity; reflection can describe a manner of examining and thinking within it.

This does not make study invariably superficial or reflection invariably deep. Study too can be careful and considered.

Study of D B R

D R S and ʿ L M: study and knowledge

ʿIlm concerns knowing and knowledge.

Dars is one way to learn and increase knowledge.

Not every act of study leads to correct knowledge.

Someone may study a text but misunderstand it or approach it with prejudice.

People can also gain knowledge through observation, experience, and other methods.

Study and knowledge are therefore related but not complete synonyms.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:79, tuʿallimūna and tadrusūna also reveal this distinction.

Study of ʿ L M

D R S and Ḥ F Ẓ: study and memorization

Repeated study could lead to memorization in early Arabic usage.

Some lexicographers therefore explain darasa al-kitāba as repeated reading until memorization and mastery.

Memorization is not a necessary meaning of every use of dars.

Someone may study a text without knowing it by heart.

Conversely, someone may memorize a book’s words without understanding them correctly.

Memorization can be an outcome of study, rather than its entire definition.

Study of Ḥ F Ẓ

7. Idrīs: resemblance and the question of derivation

The name Idrīs occurs in Maryam 19:56 and Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:85.

Its spelling resembles the D R S family.

Some lexical and historical sources say he was named for extensively studying books or writings.

Other analyses of the name’s historical origin consider it non-Arabic.

The Qur’an does not explain why he was given this name.

Letter resemblance alone therefore cannot make Idrīs a definite D R S derivative without independent examination.

This matters in root research: a personal name resembling an Arabic derivative does not necessarily come historically from that root.

Idrīs is consequently excluded from the morphological index’s six secure D R S occurrences.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

The most suitable general equivalent in Qur’anic usage is “to study,” sometimes rendered in Persian as dars khwāndan.

Āl ʿImrān 3:79

تَدْرُسُونَ

“You were studying” or “you were learning.”

Because tuʿallimūna occurs independently, the distinction between teaching and studying should be retained.

“You were teaching the Book and studying it” is a clear rendering.

Al-Anʿām 6:105

دَرَسْتَ

“You have studied” or “you have learned.”

This is the opponents’ statement, not an established Qur’anic declaration about revelation’s source.

Dārasta concerns studying with another person in a variant reading; that reading should be explained separately from the base translation.

Al-Anʿām 6:156

دِرَاسَتِهِمْ

“Their study.”

The Persian “their lesson” is understandable but can blur the distinction between their activity of studying and what they teach.

If the sentence requires it, “we were unaware of their study” is natural.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:169

وَدَرَسُوا مَا فِيهِ

“And they had studied what was in it.”

This preserves the verb’s meaning without putting the verse’s moral conclusion into the word itself.

“They had distorted the Book” or “they had not acted on it” cannot directly replace darasū, even though context establishes their blameworthy behavior.

Sabaʾ 34:44

يَدْرُسُونَهَا

“They study them.”

The pronoun -hā refers to kutub, books.

“Books for them to study” is natural within the complete sentence.

Al-Qalam 68:37

فِيهِ تَدْرُسُونَ

“In which you study.”

The question concerns a book that could support the audience’s claim.

Tadrusūna itself does not mean proving that claim true.

Uses outside the Qur’an

In the physical branch, darasa al-rasmu means a trace faded and became effaced.

Thawbun darīsun is a worn garment.

Darasa al-ḥinṭata concerns threshing wheat to separate its grain.

With a text, dirāsah means study and rereading.

These meanings belong in their respective settings. Effacement should not replace study in these Qur’anic passages, and study should not be extended to every tangible Arabic use.

9. Conclusion

Arabic D R S has two major established branches: traces wearing away and fading, and studying and rereading texts.

Physically, traces of dwellings and paths, or garments, can wear through time or repeated use.

With books, sustained attention and reading can lead to familiarity, learning, and memorization.

Repeated action and following traces can explain a semantic relationship between these branches without proving their historical order of development.

The Qur’an has six occurrences, all concerning study, reading, and learning a text in the reading examined here.

Āl ʿImrān 3:79 places studying the Book alongside teaching it.

Al-Anʿām 6:156 uses dirāsah for earlier communities’ study.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:169, a group inherited the Book and studied its contents but acted against its stated responsibilities.

In Al-Anʿām 6:105, darasta occurs in opponents’ speech about Muḥammad; its reading variants also illuminate different semantic branches.

Sabaʾ 34:44 and Al-Qalam 68:37 place studying a book within questions about the grounds for claims and judgments.

These uses show that study does not itself guarantee correct understanding, trustworthiness, or acting on a book’s contents.

Study can be a means to knowledge, while the result’s truth, the quality of understanding, and responsibility for conduct require independent evidence.

Nor should Idrīs be made a definite derivative merely because of spelling resemblance.

Proposed final root definition: Arabic D R S has two documented branches: traces wearing away and fading through time or repeated action; and sustained reading and study for familiarity, learning, or memorization. The second branch occurs in the Qur’anic uses examined here: engagement with a book and its contents, without the word guaranteeing correct understanding or acting on what is learned. Brief root meaning: Studying and rereading a text; in another branch, wearing away and fading traces.
