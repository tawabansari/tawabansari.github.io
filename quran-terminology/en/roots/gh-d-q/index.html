Abundance, plentiful water, copiousness, and ample provision

Arabic gh–d–q denotes abundance, especially plentiful water, rainfall, moisture, and the benefits they bring. Ghadaq denotes plentiful water or heavy rain, extending to prosperous living, ample livelihood, and generosity.

The root highlights water's abundance, not simply its presence. Well-watered land, a copious spring, and plentiful rain may be described through this family.

It occurs once in the Quran, as ghadaqan in Al-Jinn 72:16:

«لَأَسْقَيْنَاهُم مَّاءً غَدَقًا»

la-asqaynāhum māʾan ghadaqā

Al-Jinn 72:16

“We would have given them plentiful water to drink.”

Ghadaqan describes māʾan, water, and expresses abundance. The next verse makes that provision a setting for testing humanity.

1. Meaning in early Arabic

Arabic lexical accounts connect gh–d–q with copiousness, plentiful water, and ample provision.

Al-ghadaq describes abundant rain, beyond a few drops or a slight shower.

It also describes plentiful water from sources other than rain. Abundance, not a particular source, defines this use.

A spring yielding a little water differs from one producing a copious flow; the latter can be described through this family.

Ghadiqati al-arḍu describes land receiving abundant water and moisture. Fertility may follow, but plentiful water alone does not establish fertile conditions.

ʿAyshun ghaydāq describes plentiful living, and rajulun ghaydāq a generous person who gives abundantly.

These uses need not concern water: abundance and ample provision connect them.

Their recording in classical dictionaries does not independently establish that every sense circulated before the Quran. Datable evidence must be examined separately.

An early witness: ghaydāq in Taʾabbaṭa Sharran

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Taʾabbaṭa Sharran reads:

حَتَّى نَجَوْتُ وَلَمَّا يَنْزِعُوا سَلَبِي

ḥattā najawtu wa-lammā yanziʿū salabī

بِوَالِهٍ مِنْ قَبِيضِ الشَّدِّ غَيْدَاقِ

bi-wālihin min qabīḍi al-shaddi ghaydāq

“Until I escaped, before they stripped me of my possessions, with frantic, swift, unstinting running.”

The poet describes escape from pursuers.

Ghaydāq describes expansive, vigorous running rather than water or rain.

The witness illustrates abundance beyond water in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

It is transmitted in collections of early poetry, including the poem attributed to Taʾabbaṭa Sharran in the Mufaḍḍaliyyāt. Attribution and transmitted wording remain distinct from independently dating each usage.

The running sense does not establish the historical source of plentiful water. It shows only that the family was not restricted to water.

An early Islamic witness: describing clouds

The Umayyad poet al-Akhṭal writes:

فِي مُظْلِمٍ غَدِقِ الرَّبَابِ كَأَنَّمَا

fī muẓlimin ghadiqi al-rabābi ka-annamā

يَسْقِي الْأَشَقَّ وَعَالِجًا بِدَوَالِي

yasqī al-ashaqqa wa-ʿālijan bi-dawālī

“Within a dark, rain-laden cloud, as though it waters al-Ashaqq and ʿĀlij with water-drawing devices.”

Ghadiqi al-rabāb describes clouds bearing abundant moisture and rain.

The image of repeated water-drawing makes the rainfall's abundance tangible.

This post-Quranic witness should not be called pre-Islamic. It illustrates continued use for copious clouds and rainfall in early Islamic Arabic.

Alongside the Quran and lexical accounts, it confirms an established branch concerning plentiful water and rain.

2. Semantic core and branches

A. Plentiful water and rain

Plentiful water is the clearest use.

A slight shower may merely dampen the ground; abundant rainfall can be described as ghadaq.

The word does not itself mean a destructive flood, palatable water, or clean water. Those features require other words or circumstances.

Māʾun ghadaq should therefore not become “sweet,” “clean,” or “beneficial water” merely because it is plentiful, although abundance often supports life and cultivation.

B. Well-watered land

Arḍun ghadiqah describes land with abundant water and moisture.

ʿUshbun ghadiq describes moist, well-watered vegetation.

The field extends from the liquid itself to land and plants.

Land and vegetation are distinct objects, both described through their moisture.

The family thus describes a well-watered condition as well as quantity.

C. Ample livelihood

ʿAyshun ghaydāq and fī ghadaqin mina al-ʿaysh describe plentiful living.

The usage extends from water and moisture into material provision.

The connection is understandable: water can sustain farming, livestock, food, and livelihood in communities dependent on it.

That relationship should not become a restrictive definition. In livelihood contexts, ghadaq expresses abundance, not necessarily rainfall each time.

D. Generosity and capacity

Ghaydāq also describes a generous person.

Abundance here concerns giving rather than water or possessions alone.

It can also describe expansive, vigorous movement, as in the verse attributed to Taʾabbaṭa Sharran.

These branches express abundance in different settings, but each derivative must be read in its particular construction.

3. Important forms and derivatives

Recorded forms include:

  • Ghadaq: abundance, especially copious water or rainfall.
  • Ghadiqa / yaghdaqu: became well-watered or abundantly moist.
  • Ghadiq: well-watered, moist, or plentiful in the relevant sense.
  • Ghadiqah: feminine of ghadiq, describing land or a spring.
  • Aghdaqa / yughdiqu: became plentiful; in giving, bestowed abundantly.
  • Ighdāq: making plentiful or giving abundantly.
  • Ighdawdaqa: became very well-watered.
  • Mughdawdiq: having abundant water or rain.
  • Ghaydāq: plentiful, expansive, prosperous, or generous.
  • Ghaydaq: lexically, ample, prosperous, soft, or comfortable.
  • Ghaydaqa: became plentiful; recorded for copious rainfall or discharge.

Only ghadaqan occurs in the Quran.

Dictionary forms such as aghdaqa, ghaydāq, and ighdawdaqa should not be listed as Quranic occurrences.

The construction ghadaqan

In Al-Jinn 72:16, ghadaqan follows and describes māʾan.

Ghadaq is also recorded as a verbal noun and can function descriptively to emphasize abundance.

Māʾan ghadaqan means plentiful water.

Some lexical and reading accounts record ghadiqan, a more explicit adjectival form. The text studied here reads ghadaqan.

Both concern abundance; quotations must preserve the actual form in the selected Quranic text.

4. Quranic usage

Al-Jinn 72:16

وَأَلَّوِ اسْتَقَٰمُوا عَلَى الطَّرِيقَةِ لَأَسْقَيْنَٰهُم مَّآءً غَدَقًا

wa-allawi istaqāmū ʿalā al-ṭarīqati la-asqaynāhum māʾan ghadaqā

Al-Jinn 72:16

“Had they remained steadfast on the way, We would have given them plentiful water to drink.”

The verse connects steadfastness on the way with abundant water.

Istaqāmū concerns remaining upright on the way; la-asqaynāhum expresses giving or providing water; ghadaqan describes its abundance.

Gh–d–q itself means neither steadfastness, guidance, nor testing. Those ideas come from other words and clauses.

Why mention plentiful water?

Water supports drinking, plant growth, livestock, and daily activities.

Where rain and water are scarce, abundance can transform living conditions.

A farmer may have suitable land but insufficient water. Adequate rainfall and water supplies can improve food production, livestock keeping, and livelihood.

Abundant water can thus represent ample provision.

The verse still explicitly says water. A direct translation should not replace it with wealth, money, or provision alone.

Connecting water with prosperity is contextual reflection, supported by water's role in life and related verses. The phrase's direct meaning remains plentiful water.

Connections with other verses

Al-Aʿrāf 7:96 connects blessings from heaven and earth with communities' belief and mindfulness.

Al-Māʾidah 5:66 links upholding what the Lord has revealed with receiving provision from above and beneath.

These passages relate conduct and responsibility to communal provision.

They do not establish that every rich community is righteous or every poor or drought-stricken community is punished for wrongdoing.

Other Quranic discussions address testing, human conduct, and its social consequences among the settings of hardship and abundance.

Abundance as a test

Al-Jinn 72:17

لِّنَفْتِنَهُمْ فِيهِ ۚ وَمَن يُعْرِضْ عَن ذِكْرِ رَبِّهِ يَسْلُكْهُ عَذَابًا صَعَدًا

li-naftinahum fīhi wa-man yuʿriḍ ʿan dhikri rabbihi yaslukhu ʿadhāban ṣaʿadā

Al-Jinn 72:17

“To test them through it. Whoever turns away from remembrance of his Lord, He will lead him into arduous punishment.”

This immediately follows the promise of plentiful water.

Li-naftinahum fīhi makes that provision itself a setting for testing.

Someone might otherwise treat abundant wealth, water, or harvests as conclusive evidence of divine approval.

The Quran does not draw that conclusion from abundance alone.

Provision can support gratitude, proper use, cultivation, and others' rights. It can also be used for corruption, waste, injustice, or neglect.

A community may use abundant water to meet people's needs and sustain agriculture. Alternatively, some may monopolize or pollute it, or consume so much that others are deprived. The same resource then exposes different conduct.

This social example illustrates testing through provision; it is not the lexical meaning of ghadaq.

The root conveys abundance. Moral assessment comes from conduct and the wider verses.

5. Comparison with nearby roots

Gh–D–Q and Gh–Z–R: abundance

Gh–z–r concerns abundance, especially of milk, water, and other liquids.

The two families are close in some uses.

A copious spring may be described through either.

Shared abundance does not make every derivative or historical use identical.

Gh–d–q also extends to ample livelihood. Each phrase should determine the distinction, without rigid boundaries between the families.

Gh–D–Q and R–Gh–D: abundance and ease

R–gh–d concerns plentiful, comfortable living in the Quran.

Al-Baqarah 2:35 uses raghadan for eating with ample provision.

Ghadaq highlights abundance, especially water; raghad emphasizes ease and ample enjoyment.

Resources can support comfort, but not invariably.

Mismanagement or unequal distribution can leave people without comfortable lives despite abundant water.

Abundance and the comfort derived from it are related but distinct.

Gh–D–Q and F–Y–Ḍ: abundance and overflowing

F–y–ḍ concerns filling, flowing, and overflowing.

A large quantity of liquid can produce overflow.

Ghadaq does not itself mean overflowing: a spring can be plentiful without overflowing a container or boundary.

Fayḍ may highlight movement beyond a limit.

The water imagery overlaps, while the focus differs.

Gh–D–Q and Gh–Y–Th: plentiful water and rain

Ghayth concerns rain and, in relevant contexts, relief through water.

Ghadaq describes water or rain as abundant.

Ghayth can name the rain, while ghadaq describes its quantity.

Ghadaq is also recorded for plentiful rain, but is not restricted to naming rain: it describes springs, land, plants, and livelihood too.

Gh–D–Q and Gh–R–Q: abundance and drowning

Despite similar spelling, these are independent roots.

Gharaq concerns sinking in water and drowning.

Ghadaq concerns plentiful water, which may cause drowning under particular conditions. That consequence is not its lexical meaning.

Māʾan ghadaqan alone does not imply a destructive flood or drowned people.

6. Challenges in understanding and translation

Does ghadaq mean sweet, palatable water?

Its central meaning is abundance, not sweetness or cleanliness.

Some related forms record pleasant qualities of copious water, but these should not be generalized without evidence.

Al-Jinn 72:16 describes plentiful water. Additional evidence is needed for sweetness, purity, or palatability.

Is ghadaq always positive?

Abundant water is often beneficial, but the root does not morally evaluate every consequence.

Heavy rain may water dry land or cause damaging floods.

Quantity, circumstances, and consequences determine the difference.

Al-Jinn itself pairs abundant water with testing. Abundance alone does not establish final success.

Is plentiful water only a metaphor for wealth?

The verse directly mentions water.

Its fundamental role in life permits connections with provision and prosperity elsewhere in the Quran.

Those connections do not erase māʾ's direct meaning.

Keep water in the translation and explain broader livelihood implications separately.

Does steadfastness always produce material wealth?

Al-Jinn 72:16 conditionally connects steadfastness with plentiful water.

It should not become a rule that every righteous individual is always wealthy or every deprived person has departed from truth.

Al-Baqarah 2:155 describes testing through fear, hunger, and losses of property and lives.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:35 presents both good and adversity as tests.

Material abundance or deprivation alone cannot determine someone's worth.

This accords with Al-Jinn 72:17, where provision itself tests people.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

“Plentiful” is a concise equivalent for ghadaqan in māʾan ghadaqan.

The clause may be rendered:

“We would have given them plentiful water to drink.”

This preserves the direct meaning without adding purity, sweetness, or wealth.

Non-Quranic equivalents vary with context:

Ghadaq: abundance, copiousness, plentiful water or rain.

Ghadiq: well-watered, moist, or plentiful.

Aghdaqa: made plentiful, gave abundantly, or became abundant in water and rainfall.

Ighdāq: making plentiful or giving copiously.

Ghaydāq: plentiful, expansive, prosperous, or generous.

ʿAyshun ghaydāq: ample, prosperous living.

Arḍun ghadiqah: well-watered, abundantly moist land.

Identify what is abundant. Water, livelihood, and giving are related uses, but one equivalent will not suit every case.

Conclusion

Gh–d–q concerns abundance and ample provision, especially water, rainfall, and moisture. It also describes watered land, moist plants, prosperous living, and generous giving.

Poetic and lexical evidence illustrates abundance across settings, without establishing the history of every derivative from shared meaning alone.

Only ghadaqan occurs in the Quran, describing water in Al-Jinn 72:16.

The next verse makes that provision a test. Abundance in this context concerns blessing, but does not alone establish final divine approval or guarantee success.

The central sense is abundance, especially of water and rain; more broadly, ample provision.

“Plentiful water” clearly renders māʾan ghadaqan. Cultivation, livelihood, and testing belong in contextual reflection, rather than replacing its direct meaning.
