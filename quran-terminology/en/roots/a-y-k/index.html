Thickets, dense trees, and the naming of a Qur’anic community

1. Introducing the root and its semantic field

The three-letter Arabic root A Y K concerns trees growing together and intertwining. Its principal word, aykah, denotes a thicket or dense cluster of trees, whose branches and foliage grow close together to form thick vegetation.

Density and interwoven tree growth characterize this family. Aykah usually denotes a thicket or group of trees, although some sources also explain it as al-shajarah al-multaffah, a densely branching tree. Categorically excluding a single luxuriant tree is therefore imprecise. The sense appropriate to the Qur’anic title must be chosen through context and lexical evidence.

The family occurs four times in the Qur’an, exclusively in the expression aṣḥāb al-aykah, naming a community associated with Aykah.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ introduces Shuʿayb as a messenger who addressed them, calling for mindfulness, justice in measuring and weighing, respect for people’s rights, and avoidance of corruption.

Root research must distinguish the lexical sense of aykah as a thicket or dense trees from its use in the title of a historical community.

The lexical sense helps explain the title, but does not by itself establish the community’s precise location, social structure, livelihood, or entire history.

2. Qur’anic forms and the root in early Arabic

Forms and derivatives

A Y K occurs four times in four Qur’anic verses:

  • Al-Ḥijr 15:78
  • Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:176
  • Ṣād 38:13
  • Qāf 50:14

In all four passages, the principal use is the noun aykah in the genitive construction aṣḥāb al-aykah.

Orthographic and recitational differences occur in Al-Shuʿarāʾ and Ṣād, as examined below. These differences should not be assumed to establish a wholly independent word or root without investigation.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the genitive noun and its form

Arabic records other forms in this family:

أَيْك: dense trees; classified as a collective noun with aykah as its unit, and sometimes as its plural.

أَيْكَة: a thicket or collection of intertwined trees.

أَيِكَ: a verb describing tree branches becoming dense and intertwined.

اسْتَأْيَكَ: trees growing dense and forming a thicket.

These verbs and other family uses appear in dictionaries but not in the Qur’an. They should not be presented as Qur’anic derivatives.

Semantic core: trees gathered together

Ibn Fāris identifies the root’s principle in Maqāyīs al-Lughah as:

اجْتِمَاعُ شَجَرٍ

ijtimāʿu shajarin

Meaning: trees gathered together.

This concise definition highlights the family’s principal feature: a collection of trees, rather than merely green ground, shade, or a particular plant species.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Muḥkam wa-al-Muḥīṭ al-Aʿẓam also explain aykah through:

الشَّجَرُ الْكَثِيرُ الْمُلْتَفُّ

al-shajaru al-kathīru al-multaffu

Meaning: numerous, intertwined trees.

Two features are present: numerous trees, and their branches growing close or intertwining.

This does not require every branch literally to be knotted to another. Iltifāf describes dense and interwoven tree growth, rather than a fixed geometric arrangement.

Persian bīshah, “thicket,” is therefore appropriate. It evokes trees growing close and densely together without necessarily suggesting the extensive area that “forest” may bring to mind.

Maqāyīs, Lisān al-ʿArab, and Al-Miṣbāḥ: dense trees and the unit of ayk

Is aykah the name of a particular tree?

Dictionaries offer several accounts of the trees forming an aykah.

Some describe a thicket of sidr and arāk. Others restrict it to a place where athl grows, or speak of a collection of arāk trees.

By contrast, Abū Ḥanīfah al-Dīnawarī, a scholar of plants in the Arabic tradition, records a broader use encompassing different trees, even palms.

These differences show that Arabic aykah was not restricted to one plant species.

A locality may have used the word especially for certain trees. Their shared feature remains growing together densely.

Translating aykah everywhere as an arāk thicket, palm grove, or sidr thicket therefore introduces a restriction the word need not carry.

Some exegetical reports identify the community’s trees as dūm. Such reports may belong in historical study, but the Qur’an does not identify the local tree species.

No particular species should therefore enter the Qur’anic translation as the definitive meaning of aykah.

3. Arabic witnesses and the natural image of a thicket

Intertwining branches

Dictionaries record أَيِكَ الأَرَاكُ for arāk trees becoming dense.

It means that the arāk trees grew dense and intertwined.

Istaʾyaka is also used for this state.

These expressions describe a natural feature of vegetation: trees growing sufficiently close to form a dense cluster.

The family therefore means more than simply a tree. It draws attention to trees gathering and their density.

This matters for aṣḥāb al-aykah. “Owners of trees” loses the name’s distinctive feature. “People of the thicket” better conveys the community’s association with dense woodland.

An early Arabic witness

Dictionaries quote a rajaz passage without a clearly identified poet:

وَنَحْنُ مِنْ فَلْجٍ بِأَعْلَى شِعْبِ
أَيْكِ الْأَرَاكِ مُتَدَانِي الْقَضْبِ

wa-naḥnu min faljin bi-aʿlā shiʿbi / ayki al-arāki mutadānī al-qaḍbi

“We are from Falj, in the upper part of a ravine, amid dense arāk whose branches grow close together.”

The witness describes thick vegetation. Mutadānī al-qaḍb also indicates branches growing close together, agreeing with the sense of intertwined trees.

Ibn Sīdah takes ayki al-arāk here as a contraction of ayiki al-arāk: an adjective describing the density of arāk. The contracted adjective should not silently be equated with the collective noun ayk. The differing reading and morphological analysis must remain visible when using this witness.

Without independent identification of the poet and composition date, this cannot be called securely pre-Qur’anic evidence. Its value is as an attested use in the Arabic lexical tradition.

Ibn Sīdah: contraction of the adjective ayik in the rajaz

A witness with disputed attribution

In Al-Nukat wa-al-ʿUyūn, Al-Māwardī quotes the following verse under the name “Abū Dāwūd al-Iyādī.” Al-Zamakhsharī, however, attributes it to Khālid ibn Ṣaqʿab under N B Ḥ in Asās al-Balāghah. Neither the poet’s identity nor the witness’s precise date is therefore treated as settled:

كَأَنَّ عَرِينَ أَيْكَتِهِ تَلَاقَى
بِهَا جَمْعَانِ مِنْ نِبْطٍ وَرُومِ

ka-anna ʿarīna aykatihi talāqā / bihā jamʿāni min nibṭin wa-rūmi

“As though, in the lair amid its dense thicket, two groups of Nabataeans and Romans had met.”

Aykah describes a dense wooded setting, whose spatial character supports the poetic image.

Al-Māwardī’s Al-Nukat wa-al-ʿUyūn: the bihā reading

Attribution is not the only difference: Asās al-Balāghah has bihi in place of bihā. This study retains the supplied Al-Māwardī reading bihā, but that quotation alone does not establish a pre-Islamic composition date.

Asās al-Balāghah: attribution to Khālid ibn Ṣaqʿab and the bihi reading

A clear use in Umayyad poetry

Al-Akhṭal, an Umayyad poet, describes a palm grove:

يَكَادُ يَحَارُ الْمُجْتَنِي وَسْطَ أَيْكِهَا
إِذَا مَا تَنَادَى بِالْعَشِيِّ هَدِيلُهَا

yakādu yaḥāru al-mujtanī wasṭa aykihā / idhā mā tanādā bi-al-ʿashiyyi hadīluhā

“The fruit gatherer almost loses his way amid its dense trees when their birds’ cooing answers from place to place at evening.”

The sense is clear: numerous trees form such a dense setting that finding one’s way among them seems difficult.

Al-Akhṭal lived after Islam’s emergence, so this is not pre-Islamic evidence. As a classical Arabic example, it nevertheless shows ayk being applied to palm growth and collections of trees rather than a single species.

Together, these witnesses support aykah as a thicket or dense trees. None alone identifies the community’s geographical location or the precise vegetation of its region.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: Al-Akhṭal’s palm-tree witness

4. Qur’anic uses of aṣḥāb al-aykah

A. Al-Ḥijr 15:78: a community described as wrongdoing

Al-Ḥijr 15:78

وَإِنْ كَانَ أَصْحَابُ الْأَيْكَةِ لَظَالِمِينَ

wa-in kāna aṣḥābu al-aykati la-ẓālimīna

“The people of the thicket were indeed wrongdoers.”

Aṣḥāb al-aykah names a community described here as ẓālimīn.

Aṣḥāb expresses an association or affiliation. Al-aykah names or describes the locality through which the community is identified.

The direct sense can therefore be “people of the thicket” or “people associated with that thicket.”

Ẓālimīn belongs to the separate root Ẓ L M. Wrongdoing is not part of aykah’s lexical meaning.

Nor does aykah identify how they committed wrongdoing. Their behavior requires other passages that narrate the story more fully.

In Al-Ḥijr, the verse follows the account of Lot’s people. Al-Ḥijr 15:79 then mentions retribution and the consequences of their conduct.

Their proximity in the passage connects the accounts as warnings. It does not by itself establish a shared location or specified geographical boundaries.

Lexically, the chief point is that aykah forms part of a historical community’s title. Its ethical description comes from the separate statement la-ẓālimīn.

B. Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:176: a community denying the messengers

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:176

كَذَّبَ أَصْحَابُ لْـَٔيْكَةِ الْمُرْسَلِينَ

kadhdhaba aṣḥābu al-aykati al-mursalīna

“The people of the thicket denied the messengers.”

This opens a fuller account of the community.

In the following verse, Shuʿayb addresses them, asking whether they will be mindful.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:181, Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:182, and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:183 command full measure, correct weighing, not diminishing people’s rights, and avoiding corruption on earth.

Shuʿayb’s warning therefore concerns more than a mental belief or religious claim: their economic and social conduct is also criticized.

These matters should nevertheless not enter aykah’s lexical meaning.

Aykah itself means neither giving short measure, economic corruption, associating partners, nor denying messengers. Other sentences in the account identify those actions.

Does aykah mean a forested land?

Lexically, aykah is associated with thickets and dense trees.

The word alone does not specify the region’s area, climate, rainfall, rivers, or human settlement patterns.

A dry region might contain a limited thicket near a water source. A larger area might also have dense trees. Aykah itself does not conclusively choose between these possibilities.

The title therefore does not establish that the community necessarily inhabited an extensive humid forest resembling forests familiar today.

“People of the thicket” preserves dense tree growth while avoiding unestablished geographical detail.

Did the community worship trees?

Some exegetical and historical reports attribute particular religious beliefs to them.

The expression aṣḥāb al-aykah itself establishes only their association with Aykah.

Combining aṣḥāb with a place or natural feature does not itself mean worshipping it.

“People of the thicket” therefore does not establish that trees were their objects of worship. That claim requires independent evidence.

Nor should aykah become an idol, temple, sacred tree, or worship center in translation: none is part of the word’s meaning.

C. Ṣād 38:13: the community among earlier peoples

Ṣād 38:13

وَثَمُودُ وَقَوْمُ لُوطٍ وَأَصْحَابُ لْـَٔيْكَةِ أُولَٰئِكَ الْأَحْزَابُ

wa-thamūdu wa-qawmu lūṭin wa-aṣḥābu al-aykati ulāʾika al-aḥzābu

“And Thamūd, Lot’s people, and the people of the thicket: those were the groups.”

The passage mentions Noah’s people, ʿĀd, Pharaoh, Thamūd, Lot’s people, and the people of the thicket.

It does not repeat the community’s detailed history, but places its name alongside other historical groups.

Ulāʾika al-aḥzāb refers to the groups mentioned collectively.

Ṣād 38:14 then speaks of denying messengers and punishment taking effect.

No new lexical information about tree species or Aykah’s location emerges. The title identifies a community known in the Qur’anic account.

This verse also supplies no independent basis for conclusively identifying the people of Aykah with, or separating them from, Madyan.

D. Qāf 50:14: the people of Aykah and Tubbaʿ

Qāf 50:14

وَأَصْحَابُ الْأَيْكَةِ وَقَوْمُ تُبَّعٍ كُلٌّ كَذَّبَ الرُّسُلَ فَحَقَّ وَعِيدِ

wa-aṣḥābu al-aykati wa-qawmu tubbaʿin kullun kadhdhaba al-rusula fa-ḥaqqa waʿīdi

“And the people of the thicket and Tubbaʿ’s people: all denied the messengers, so My warning came to pass.”

Aykah’s people appear alongside Tubbaʿ’s people, continuing the sequence of peoples named in preceding verses.

Their common subject is denial of messengers and its consequences.

Aykah again belongs to an identifying title, rather than naming something the community did.

The verse supplies no new explanation of their economic structure, tree species, or precise location.

Gaps in historical information should therefore not be filled with stories or details the verse does not state.

Findings from the four verses

In each occurrence, a word from the natural field of dense trees forms part of a community’s title.

Aykah means neither wrongdoing, denial, short measure, nor punishment in any of these verses.

Those concepts appear in the account’s context, but do not belong to the root’s lexical meaning.

Every Qur’anic occurrence is confined to this title. A broad Qur’anic field of ethical or philosophical meanings should not be invented where the occurrences do not support it.

5. Aykah, Madyan, and differing readings

Are the people of Aykah the people of Madyan?

Al-Aʿrāf 7:85, Hūd 11:84, and Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:36 associate Shuʿayb with Madyan and introduce him through expressions such as akhāhum Shuʿayban, “their brother Shuʿayb.”

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:177, however, says of Aykah’s people:

إِذْ قَالَ لَهُمْ شُعَيْبٌ أَلَا تَتَّقُونَ

idh qāla lahum shuʿaybun a-lā tattaqūna

“When Shuʿayb said to them: Will you not be mindful?”

Unlike some earlier passages in Al-Shuʿarāʾ, akhūhum, “their brother,” is absent here.

This difference has contributed to differing accounts of the relationship between Madyan and Aykah.

Some understand one community with two geographical or social titles. Others hold that Shuʿayb was sent to two communities: Madyan and Aykah.

Several points matter in assessing this.

First, the Qur’an names Shuʿayb in both sets of passages. Instructions concerning measure, weight, justice, and avoiding corruption also closely resemble one another.

Second, the titles Madyan and Aykah differ, but two names do not themselves prove two wholly independent peoples. A community may be identified through a town, territory, tribe, or natural feature of its locality.

Third, the absence of akhūhum in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:177 does not alone establish that Shuʿayb had no genealogical or social connection with them. A description’s absence from a verse is not its conclusive negation.

Conversely, the shared invitation and Shuʿayb’s name alone do not establish complete identity between the communities and every detail of their histories.

The passages are closely related, but the root A Y K itself does not settle whether Aykah and Madyan were one community with two titles or two related communities.

A root study should preserve this boundary rather than determine separate geographical locations or histories without sufficient evidence.

Al-Ṭabarī: Aykah’s people and their relationship with Madyan

Al-aykah and Laykah in the readings

Differing recitation and orthography are among this root’s principal linguistic questions.

Al-Ḥijr 15:78 and Qāf 50:14 involve the lexical form al-aykati, with the definite article and genitive inflection. Some modes of recitation, including Warsh’s transfer of the hamzah’s vowel, alter its pronunciation. This must be distinguished from the proper-name reading Laykata in the other two passages.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:176 and Ṣād 38:13 have differing readings.

One reading is al-aykati, with the definite article and genitive case.

Another, transmitted from Nāfiʿ, Ibn Kathīr, Ibn ʿĀmir, and Abū Jaʿfar, has Laykata.

This latter form is read without an initial hamzah or independent definite article, and with final fatḥah.

Some early lexicographers and reciters understood it as the proper name of a town or region.

On that analysis, Laykah is a feminine diptote proper noun. Although genitive as the second term of the aṣḥāb construction, its case marker is fatḥah. This does not make it syntactically accusative.

Laykati, with final kasrah and explained through hamzah reduction or vowel transfer by some grammarians, must be distinguished from Laykata with final fatḥah. The first explanation, found for example in Al-Zajjāj, relates it to al-aykah; it does not by itself explain the inflection of the second reading.

These linguistic analyses hold that a difference in spelling or pronunciation need not establish an independent word or place.

Does Laykah have another root?

The attested Laykata reading must be recorded as a linguistic fact. Its outward difference alone does not establish a separate root with an independent meaning.

Under the proper-name reading, the name could historically relate to aykah, but establishing its origin requires additional evidence.

Under the other reading, the word is al-aykah, belonging to A Y K.

The shared subject matter, association with Aykah’s people, and familiar al-aykah form elsewhere do not justify inventing two different cities or peoples solely from the reading difference.

Equally, the Laykata reading should not be ignored or omitted simply because it differs from a preferred lexical analysis.

The two verses have differing readings and grammatical analyses. That alone neither proves two independent locations nor conclusively establishes their historical identity.

Tāj al-ʿArūs and Lisān al-ʿArab: readings and inflection

Aykah and ẓullah

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:189 describes their punishment as ʿadhāb yawm al-ẓullah.

Ẓullah belongs to Ẓ L L, associated with shade or a canopy.

Dense trees can create shade, so an association between a thicket and shade may initially seem natural.

This does not establish that ẓullah here means the trees of Aykah themselves or that the punishment occurred beneath that thicket’s shade.

Aykah and ẓullah belong to separate roots. The event’s nature and the manner of punishment require independent examination of the verses and evidence.

Reports concerning a cloud, intense heat, or people gathering beneath shade should not be presented as aykah’s lexical meaning or information explicitly stated by the Qur’an.

6. Distinctions from related words

A Y K (aykah) and SH J R (shajar): thicket and tree

Qur’anic SH J R occurs in discussions of trees and woody plants.

Shajar can denote a tree or trees collectively without dense, intertwined branches always being necessary to its meaning.

Attested aykah highlights trees densely gathered and growing close together.

A thicket consists of trees, but not every tree or scattered collection necessarily constitutes one.

Al-Baḥr al-Muḥīṭ: aykah glossed as al-shajarah al-multaffah

Study of SH J R

A Y K (aykah) and J N N (janna): thicket and garden

J N N concerns concealment and covering. Jannah, when referring to a garden, denotes a place with plant and tree cover.

In the Qur’an, jannah can describe worldly gardens or gardens of the Hereafter.

Aykah is also wooded, but emphasizes trees growing densely together. Jannah can denote a place where plants and trees are cultivated without necessarily conveying the same dense thicket.

Both may apply to one location, yet their semantic fields differ.

Aykah is also not a Qur’anic designation for a garden of Paradise or a place of reward in the Hereafter.

Study of J N N

A Y K (aykah) and R W Ḍ (rawḍah): thicket and meadow

Arabic rawḍah denotes land with green vegetation. Qur’anic expressions include rawḍāt al-jannāt.

Rawḍah concerns green ground and growing plants.

Aykah, however, emphasizes trees gathering densely.

A meadow may be green without dense trees; a thicket may have tree cover without resembling an open meadow.

Rendering aykah as grassland or meadow without a suitable indication is therefore inappropriate.

A Y K (aykah) and A R K (arāk): thicket and tree species

Arāk names a tree known in Arabic.

Because dictionaries sometimes explain aykah as arāk trees gathered together, the words can be confused.

Arāk names the tree, while aykah concerns a collection’s character.

A thicket can consist of arāk without aykah being restricted to that species.

Arāʾik, used in the Qur’an for adorned couches or reclining seats, is studied under A R K in lexical sources, rather than A Y K. Its outward resemblance to ayk does not establish a shared root with that word.

7. Translation suggestions for Forqan

In all four Qur’anic occurrences, aykah forms part of a community’s title. Translation should preserve both its natural sense and its identifying role.

The most suitable Persian equivalent for aykah is bīshah, “thicket.”

Aṣḥāb al-aykah can therefore be rendered:

“People of the thicket.”

This is concise, natural, and clear, without introducing unestablished tree species, precise locations, or historical details.

A fuller Persian expression for “the people of the thicket” is also suitable, although ahl-e bīshah is more compact and fluent as a community title.

“Owners of the thicket” follows the Arabic construction closely, but may suggest legal ownership. Aṣḥāb in such titles need not denote ownership; it can express association with a place or condition.

“Forest dwellers” is close in meaning, but may imply permanent habitation in an extensive forest. The Qur’anic title does not specify these details.

“Palm-grove dwellers,” “people of the arāk trees,” “tree worshippers,” and “people of the town Laykah” should not become a general translation of all four verses. Each introduces information or a reading beyond aykah’s direct meaning.

The difference between al-aykah and Laykah in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:176 and Ṣād 38:13 belongs in lexical commentary. For a basic translation following the al-aykah reading, “people of the thicket” remains appropriate.

If a separate edition following Laykata is prepared and treats the word as a proper name, it may retain the name and explain its possible sense or relationship to aykah in commentary. That requires a conscious choice of reading, rather than a silent change to the current translation.

8. Conclusion

Arabic A Y K concerns trees growing together and intertwining. Aykah denotes a thicket or dense trees, while dictionaries record ayk for tree growth collectively and sometimes as its plural.

The word is not confined to one plant species, although early sources give examples involving arāk, sidr, athl, and palms.

In the Qur’an, the family occurs only four times, always in the title aṣḥāb al-aykah. Al-Shuʿarāʾ depicts Shuʿayb calling this community to mindfulness, justice in measuring and weighing, respect for people’s rights, and avoidance of corruption.

Those actions are not part of the root’s meaning. Aykah means neither short measure, association, wrongdoing, nor denial. Such concepts emerge from the account’s context.

Differing readings of al-aykah and Laykah in Al-Shuʿarāʾ and Ṣād must be preserved. They alone establish neither an independent geographical identity, two different peoples, nor the name’s definitive origin.

The relationship between Aykah and Madyan is likewise a historical and Qur’anic question not settled by A Y K itself. Shuʿayb’s presence and the similar invitation closely connect the passages, but the communities’ precise identities require restraint beyond the available evidence.

Proposed final definition: A Y K denotes trees growing together and intertwining. Aykah is a thicket or dense cluster of trees. The Qur’an uses it in aṣḥāb al-aykah to identify a historical community, naturally rendered in Persian as “people of the thicket.” Brief root meaning: Trees growing together and intertwining; aykah: a thicket or dense tree growth; in the Qur’an, part of the title “people of Aykah.”
