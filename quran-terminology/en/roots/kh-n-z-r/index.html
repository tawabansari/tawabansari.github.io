1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

In Arabic, khinzīr names the familiar animal, the pig. It occurs both in the singular and as the plural khanāzīr. The Qur’an uses it in two different settings: dietary rules and the account of a group subjected to divine anger and punishment.

The family KH N Z R is recorded five times in five verses across four surahs. Four occurrences are the singular khinzīr and one is the plural khanāzīr. No independent verb from this root occurs in the Qur’an.

The Qur’anic forms are الْخِنْزِيرِ (al-khinzīri) in Al-Baqarah 2:173, Al-Māʾidah 5:3 and Al-Naḥl 16:115; خِنْزِيرٍ (khinzīrin) in Al-Anʿām 6:145; and الْخَنَازِيرَ (al-khanāzīra) in Al-Māʾidah 5:60.

In the four dietary passages, the word occurs in laḥmu al-khinzīr or laḥma khinzīr. The animal’s name thus combines with laḥm, “flesh,” to identify the subject of a food ruling. In the fifth passage, al-khanāzīr stands independently beside al-qiradah; eating or avoiding its flesh is not the subject.

The distinction matters: in all five verses the word names the same animal, but its grammatical role and the question addressed by the passage differ.

2. Word formation and the disputed root

Does khinzīr have a four-consonant root?

The Qur’anic morphological index places khinzīr under the quadriliteral KH N Z R. Arabic lexicographers and grammarians nevertheless disagreed about the noun’s historical formation.

Tāj al-ʿArūs reports two principal analyses.

The first treats khinzīr as having four root consonants, with yāʾ added in the word’s formation. Khāʾ, nūn, zāy and rāʾ are therefore radicals.

Under the second, nūn may also be additional, allowing the word to be related to the triliteral KH Z R. Those proposing this possibility considered the animal’s eyes and manner of looking.

The early lexicographer Kurāʿ, among others, proposed a connection with khazar in the eye. Arabic khazar denotes a condition of the eyes or looking from the corner of the eye. He considered this a characteristic of the animal that might explain its name.

This is a derivational analysis, rather than conclusive evidence for when or why the animal was first named.

Observing a physical feature does not establish that an animal’s historical name must derive from it. Independent linguistic and historical evidence would be needed.

Nor does classification under a quadriliteral root in the Qur’anic index independently settle the historical disagreement.

KH N Z R is therefore an appropriate heading for this Qur’anic family in Forqan, without being conclusive proof of one derivational theory.

A possible relationship to KH N Z: decay and unpleasant smell

Arabic also has a separate triliteral root KH N Z. Lisān al-ʿArab records khaniza for meat, dates and walnuts becoming spoiled or foul-smelling.

For example, خَنِزَ اللَّحْمُ (khaniza al-laḥmu) means that meat spoiled or developed an unpleasant smell.

Some lexical reports also record forms such as al-khanzuwān for the male pig. Such forms have led lexicographers to consider a possible relationship between these words and the animal’s name.

The existence of KH N Z meaning decay does not, however, establish that Qur’anic khinzīr means “spoiled meat.”

The principal distinction is clear: khaniza is a verb describing something becoming spoiled, whereas khinzīr is the established name of an animal.

Even if the name were eventually shown to derive from an unpleasant feature of smell or behaviour, its established meaning could not be reduced to that proposed origin. Words may arise from a description but acquire an independent meaning as the name of a creature.

An animal named long ago after a colour, behaviour or bodily feature need not evoke that original description every time its name is used.

Translating laḥma khinzīr as “spoiled meat” or “foul-smelling meat” therefore conflicts with established usage and the Qur’anic context.

Al-Māʾidah 5:60 makes this especially clear: al-khanāzīr stands beside al-qiradah, both familiar animal names.

3. Other uses in earlier Arabic

Coarseness and a heavy tool

The lexicons also record al-khanzarah for coarseness or thickness. The same form denotes a large, heavy axe used to break stones.

Ibn Durayd is among those who mentioned thickness when discussing the derivation of khinzīr. Tāj al-ʿArūs reports this alongside other analyses.

The family therefore includes recorded Arabic meanings beyond the animal’s name.

Their historical relationship is not conclusively established. One cannot assert that khinzīr originally named any coarse or heavy creature and only later became specific to the pig.

That account remains a possible derivational explanation.

Khanāzīr as the name of an ailment

Besides the plural of khinzīr, the lexicons record al-khanāzīr as an ailment’s name. Lisān al-ʿArab describes it as hard sores appearing on the neck. This historical description should not be equated with a precise modern medical diagnosis without independent investigation.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs mention this usage, and the ailment’s name is also known in later medical and lexical sources.

Its recorded medical sense does not itself establish that the meaning was current at the time of revelation or intended in Al-Māʾidah 5:60.

Preferring the ailment to the animal would require a clear contextual indication. The pairing of al-khanāzīr with al-qiradah supplies none; reading both as animal names is the more natural reading.

The medical sense is therefore a recorded historical usage, rather than an appropriate translation in that verse.

A place name in earlier poetry

Some literary reports also record Khinzīr as a place name.

A line attributed to al-Aʿshā says:

فَالسَّفْحُ يَجْرِي فَخِنْزِيرٌ فَبُرْقَتُهُ

fa-l-safḥu yajrī fa-khinzīrun fa-burqatuhu

حَتَّى تَدَافَعَ مِنْهُ السَّهْلُ وَالْجَبَلُ

ḥattā tadāfaʿa minhu al-sahlu wa-l-jabalu

Explanatory translation: “Then [the rainwater on] the slope flowed; then Khinzīr and its barqah, until [the runoff from] plain and mountain drove together from it.”

Khinzīr here is a geographical location, rather than an animal appearing in a rain scene. Lisān al-ʿArab quotes the line in this lexical entry. Barqah denotes a tract of mixed stones and soil. The ending pictures flows from low and high ground converging, rather than the mountain and plain themselves moving. Lexical and geographical sources transmit variants of the line; the supplied Arabic wording is retained here.

This witness shows why not every occurrence of khinzīr in earlier poetry can be evidence for a description of pigs. First establish whether it names an animal, place or person.

The poetic witness quoted here does not unambiguously describe the animal, and cannot serve as such a witness in pre-Qur’anic poetry. This limitation does not mean the animal was unknown in Arabic before the Qur’an; it prevents treating this line, or an unsourced description, as evidence for the animal’s name or attributing it to pre-Islamic poets without support.

What is clear is the animal name’s definite use in the Qur’an and its independent definition in authoritative lexicons.

4. Four Qur’anic dietary passages

Four Qur’anic verses use laḥma al-khinzīr or laḥma khinzīr in prohibiting this meat.

They share a subject but not an identical construction or setting. Three give the rule more compactly, while Al-Māʾidah 5:3 has a longer list of forbidden foods.

A. Al-Baqarah 2:173: the animal named beside carrion and blood

إِنَّمَا حَرَّمَ عَلَيْكُمُ الْمَيْتَةَ وَالدَّمَ وَلَحْمَ الْخِنْزِيرِ وَمَا أُهِلَّ بِهِ لِغَيْرِ اللَّهِ ۖ فَمَنِ اضْطُرَّ غَيْرَ بَاغٍ وَلَا عَادٍ فَلَا إِثْمَ عَلَيْهِ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ

innamā ḥarrama ʿalaykumu al-maytata wa-l-dama wa-laḥma al-khinzīri wa-mā uhilla bihi li-ghayri llāhi fa-mani uḍṭurra ghayra bāghin wa-lā ʿādin fa-lā ithma ʿalayhi inna llāha ghafūrun raḥīmun

Translation: “He has forbidden you only carrion, blood, the flesh of swine, and that over which a name other than The God’s has been invoked. But whoever is compelled, neither seeking transgression nor exceeding the limit, incurs no sin. Surely The God is forgiving, merciful.”

Here al-khinzīri is the genitive complement of laḥma. The expression means “the flesh of swine,” rather than the act of raising the animal or every human relationship with it.

The lexical distinction is clear: this sentence names the animal together with its flesh in a rule about forbidden foods.

After the prohibition, the verse addresses necessity. The rule thus stands alongside consideration of human circumstances.

Necessity does not change the lexical meaning of khinzīr. The meat remains the same, while the verse removes sin for someone compelled, under specified conditions.

Distinguishing the rule’s subject from its conditions of application is essential to understanding the passage.

B. Al-Māʾidah 5:3: pork within a longer list

حُرِّمَتْ عَلَيْكُمُ الْمَيْتَةُ وَالدَّمُ وَلَحْمُ الْخِنْزِيرِ وَمَا أُهِلَّ لِغَيْرِ اللَّهِ بِهِ وَالْمُنْخَنِقَةُ وَالْمَوْقُوذَةُ وَالْمُتَرَدِّيَةُ وَالنَّطِيحَةُ وَمَا أَكَلَ السَّبُعُ إِلَّا مَا ذَكَّيْتُمْ وَمَا ذُبِحَ عَلَى النُّصُبِ وَأَنْ تَسْتَقْسِمُوا بِالْأَزْلَامِ ۚ ذَٰلِكُمْ فِسْقٌ ۗ الْيَوْمَ يَئِسَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا مِنْ دِينِكُمْ فَلَا تَخْشَوْهُمْ وَاخْشَوْنِ ۚ الْيَوْمَ أَكْمَلْتُ لَكُمْ دِينَكُمْ وَأَتْمَمْتُ عَلَيْكُمْ نِعْمَتِي وَرَضِيتُ لَكُمُ الْإِسْلَامَ دِينًا ۚ فَمَنِ اضْطُرَّ فِي مَخْمَصَةٍ غَيْرَ مُتَجَانِفٍ لِإِثْمٍ فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ

ḥurrimat ʿalaykumu al-maytatu wa-l-damu wa-laḥmu al-khinzīri wa-mā uhilla li-ghayri llāhi bihi wa-l-munkhaniqatu wa-l-mawqūdhatu wa-l-mutaraddiyatu wa-l-naṭīḥatu wa-mā akala al-sabuʿu illā mā dhakkaytum wa-mā dhubiḥa ʿalā al-nuṣubi wa-an tastaqsimū bi-l-azlāmi dhālikum fisqun al-yawma yaʾisa alladhīna kafarū min dīnikum fa-lā takhshawhum wa-khshawnī al-yawma akmaltu lakum dīnakum wa-atmamtu ʿalaykum niʿmatī wa-raḍītu lakumu al-islāma dīnan fa-mani uḍṭurra fī makhmaṣatin ghayra mutajānifin li-ithmin fa-inna llāha ghafūrun raḥīmun

Translation: “Forbidden to you are carrion, blood, the flesh of swine, that over which a name other than The God’s has been invoked, the strangled animal, one struck down, one killed by a fall, one gored, and that of which a beast of prey has eaten—except what you slaughter in time—and that sacrificed upon erected stones; and that you seek allotment by divining arrows. That is disobedience. Today those who rejected faith have despaired of your religion; do not fear them, but fear Me. Today I have completed your religion for you, fulfilled My favour upon you, and approved Islam—submission to The God—as your religion. But whoever is compelled by severe hunger, without inclining toward sin—surely The God is forgiving, merciful.”

Laḥmu al-khinzīr is the same construction as in Al-Baqarah 2:173, now alongside other animals dead or slaughtered under prohibited conditions.

The treatment of pork differs in one respect from that of strangled, struck or gored animals. The latter descriptions concern the condition of death or slaughter, whereas the former specifies the animal itself.

One therefore cannot use this context to claim that the prohibition of pork concerns only how the animal was killed. The passage names the animal separately from those forms of death.

The ending again addresses necessity, with fī makhmaṣatin referring to severe hunger.

The principal rule and the exception of necessity should not be confused. The passage first states the prohibition and then opens a way to relief from responsibility in compulsion, qualified by avoidance of an inclination to sin.

C. Al-Anʿām 6:145: the blood qualifier and rijs

قُلْ لَا أَجِدُ فِي مَا أُوحِيَ إِلَيَّ مُحَرَّمًا عَلَىٰ طَاعِمٍ يَطْعَمُهُ إِلَّا أَنْ يَكُونَ مَيْتَةً أَوْ دَمًا مَسْفُوحًا أَوْ لَحْمَ خِنْزِيرٍ فَإِنَّهُ رِجْسٌ أَوْ فِسْقًا أُهِلَّ لِغَيْرِ اللَّهِ بِهِ ۚ فَمَنِ اضْطُرَّ غَيْرَ بَاغٍ وَلَا عَادٍ فَإِنَّ رَبَّكَ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ

qul lā ajidu fī mā ūḥiya ilayya muḥarraman ʿalā ṭāʿimin yaṭʿamuhu illā an yakūna maytatan aw daman masfūḥan aw laḥma khinzīrin fa-innahu rijsun aw fisqan uhilla li-ghayri llāhi bihi fa-mani uḍṭurra ghayra bāghin wa-lā ʿādin fa-inna rabbaka ghafūrun raḥīmun

Translation: “Say: ‘In what has been revealed to me, I find nothing forbidden for an eater to eat unless it is carrion, blood poured out or the flesh of swine—for it is unclean—or something over which a name other than The God’s has been invoked in transgression. But whoever is compelled, neither seeking transgression nor exceeding the limit—your Lord is forgiving, merciful.’”

This passage matters to the root study in several ways.

First, laḥma khinzīrin is indefinite here. Unlike the other three passages, khinzīr has no al-. This reflects the construction and does not change the animal’s identity.

Second, the same verse has daman masfūḥan. Masfūḥ describes blood, rather than pork. It must not become part of khinzīr’s meaning or a condition attached to the prohibition of its flesh.

Third, fa-innahu rijsun immediately follows laḥma khinzīrin.

Grammatically, the nearest referent for the pronoun is the flesh of swine, although the scope of its reference and its relation to the preceding foods can also be examined separately in commentary.

One principle remains clear: rijs is not the lexical meaning of khinzīr. It occurs in a separate clause as a description attributed to the rule’s subject.

The animal’s basic translation therefore cannot become “an unclean animal” or “unclean meat” on the assumption that the Arabic noun itself means this.

Laḥma khinzīr directly means “the flesh of swine” or “pork.” Rijs should be examined where it occurs, in light of the pronoun and dietary setting.

D. Al-Naḥl 16:115: the rule and necessity repeated

إِنَّمَا حَرَّمَ عَلَيْكُمُ الْمَيْتَةَ وَالدَّمَ وَلَحْمَ الْخِنْزِيرِ وَمَا أُهِلَّ لِغَيْرِ اللَّهِ بِهِ ۖ فَمَنِ اضْطُرَّ غَيْرَ بَاغٍ وَلَا عَادٍ فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ

innamā ḥarrama ʿalaykumu al-maytata wa-l-dama wa-laḥma al-khinzīri wa-mā uhilla li-ghayri llāhi bihi fa-mani uḍṭurra ghayra bāghin wa-lā ʿādin fa-inna llāha ghafūrun raḥīmun

Translation: “He has forbidden you only carrion, blood, the flesh of swine, and that over which a name other than The God’s has been invoked. But whoever is compelled, neither seeking transgression nor exceeding the limit—surely The God is forgiving, merciful.”

The construction closely resembles Al-Baqarah 2:173: both give the principal list and the exception of necessity.

The setting in Al-Naḥl adds another point. Immediately afterward, Al-Naḥl 16:116 forbids falsely attributing statements of lawful and forbidden to The God.

Read together, the passages connect stating actual prohibitions with refraining from inventing groundless ones. The same text that forbids a food also restrains people from fabricating divine rulings.

Studying the name khinzīr should therefore neither excuse denying an explicit Qur’anic prohibition nor authorize attributing new rules about other animals to The God without Qur’anic evidence.

5. Does laḥma al-khinzīr mean every part of a pig?

This question sits at the boundary of language and legal interpretation and requires care.

Directly, laḥm means flesh and khinzīr specifies the animal. At this level, the expression means “the flesh of swine” or “pork.”

The lexical meaning of a phrase and the practical scope of a rule expressed through it are not always identical.

A prominent part can sometimes stand for food obtained from an animal. This possibility, however, must not become an absolute rule without evidence.

Conversely, Al-Anʿām 6:146 uses a separate word, shuḥūm, for animal fat. Qur’anic language can therefore distinguish flesh and fat lexically.

Three matters should remain separate: the direct meaning of laḥm, the ordinary scope of meat terminology in food usage, and the limits of the divine ruling concerning different animal parts.

Laḥm alone cannot yield a definitive ruling about every part of a pig without further study, either to include all parts in the prohibition or to declare everything outside a narrow sense of flesh permissible.

Such an inquiry must compare the relevant food passages, the distinction between laḥm and shaḥm, the prohibition’s context and the expression of exceptions.

A root study can clarify this boundary, but should not impose a legal conclusion upon the word’s meaning without that independent inquiry.

6. Al-khanāzīra in Al-Māʾidah 5:60

The preceding four occurrences concern food. The fifth has a very different subject.

Al-Māʾidah 5:60

قُلْ هَلْ أُنَبِّئُكُمْ بِشَرٍّ مِنْ ذَٰلِكَ مَثُوبَةً عِنْدَ اللَّهِ ۚ مَنْ لَعَنَهُ اللَّهُ وَغَضِبَ عَلَيْهِ وَجَعَلَ مِنْهُمُ الْقِرَدَةَ وَالْخَنَازِيرَ وَعَبَدَ الطَّاغُوتَ ۚ أُولَٰئِكَ شَرٌّ مَكَانًا وَأَضَلُّ عَنْ سَوَاءِ السَّبِيلِ

qul hal unabbiʾukum bi-sharrin min dhālika mathūbatan ʿinda llāhi man laʿanahu llāhu wa-ghaḍiba ʿalayhi wa-jaʿala minhumu al-qiradata wa-l-khanāzīra wa-ʿabada al-ṭāghūta ulāʾika sharrun makānan wa-aḍallu ʿan sawāʾi al-sabīli

Translation: “Say: ‘Shall I tell you of those worse than that in recompense with The God? Those whom The God has cursed, upon whom He has become angry, and from among whom He made apes and pigs, and who worshipped al-ṭāghūt: those are worse in position and further astray from the even path.’”

The meaning of khanāzīr here

Al-khanāzīra is the plural of khinzīr, beside al-qiradah, the plural of qird.

Both fall under jaʿala, “made,” while minhum indicates people from among the group being discussed.

This matters: the passage does not say that every member of an ethnic community or every adherent of a religion became a pig or an ape. Minhum specifies some among the group in question.

Al-Māʾidah 5:57, Al-Māʾidah 5:58, Al-Māʾidah 5:59 and Al-Māʾidah 5:60 discuss people mocking religion and worship and taking a hostile position toward others’ faith. The passage thus concerns conduct and the history of a particular group, rather than defining the moral nature of every individual within an ethnic community.

Bodily transformation or figurative description?

On an initial Arabic reading, jaʿala minhum al-qiradata wa-l-khanāzīra can indicate making or transforming some of them into apes and pigs. This can be studied alongside other Qur’anic passages, particularly the command kūnū qiradatan khāsiʾīn in Al-Baqarah 2:65 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:166.

Khanāzīr alone nevertheless does not determine how the event described occurred.

Whether the passage concerns an actual bodily transformation or a figurative, humiliating description is an exegetical question requiring attention to jaʿala, the narrative setting, parallel passages and independent indications.

Difficulty imagining a bodily transformation does not justify erasing the animal’s name and translating khanāzīr as “base people,” “corrupt people” or “the sick.” Such substitutions decide the interpretation in advance.

The basic translation should retain the animal’s name; the kind of transformation or punishment belongs to a separate explanation.

Khinzīr here is not a general insult

Nor should the passage become permission to demean people.

First, its subject concerns particular people and conduct, rather than every member of a community or religion.

Second, even if it speaks of some humans taking an animal form, pigs and apes as natural creatures do not thereby become responsible for moral wrongdoing.

Animals act according to their natures and biological needs. This passage concerns human responsibility and punishment, rather than putting an animal species on moral trial.

It therefore does not establish that every natural pig symbolizes a sinful human, or that the animal’s existence originated through human sin.

The distinction preserves the passage’s meaning and prevents extending a particular judgment to humans or animals without evidence.

7. Comparison with neighbouring roots

KH N Z R (khinzīr) and KH N Z (khanaz)

Their derivational relationship is disputed.

The lexicons use KH N Z for decay and unpleasant smell, whereas khinzīr names the animal.

The meanings must not be merged in translating the Qur’an. Even a historical connection would not erase the animal name’s established sense in the passage.

KH N Z R (khinzīr) and KH Z R (khazar)

Khazar concerns a condition of the eyes or manner of looking. Some lexicographers related the pig’s name to it.

That connection is a theory about the name’s origin, rather than an obligatory meaning of the word in every use.

Qur’anic al-khinzīr does not mean a creature with particular eyes or someone looking from the corner of the eye.

KH N Z R (khinzīr) and L Ḥ M (laḥm)

Study of L Ḥ M

The roots appear together in four dietary passages.

Laḥm names flesh; khinzīr names the animal. Their combination identifies a particular kind of meat.

The prohibition concerns a particular food expression. Laḥm itself does not mean forbidden, nor does khinzīr lexically mean prohibition.

KH N Z R (khinzīr) and R J S (rijs)

Study of R J S

In Al-Anʿām 6:145, rijs occurs as a separate description, rather than a synonym for the animal’s name.

Distinguish the animal’s name, the subject of the food ruling and the description used in that setting.

Their occurrence together does not make khinzīr a lexical synonym of rijs.

KH N Z R (khinzīr) and Q R D (qird)

Study of Q R D

Al-Māʾidah 5:60 pairs two animal names: qird, “ape,” and khinzīr, “pig.”

Their juxtaposition matters to the punishment setting but does not establish shared roots or one lexical meaning.

They are independent nouns occurring together in a Qur’anic expression.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

The principal English equivalent of khinzīr is “pig,” or “swine” in a more formal register. Both suit its direct meaning as the animal’s name.

In the four dietary verses, laḥmu al-khinzīr and laḥma khinzīr can be “the flesh of swine” or “pork.” The basic translation need not add “unclean,” “spoiled,” “foul-smelling” or “impure” to the animal’s name. Where the passage supplies rijs or a prohibition, translate and explain it in its own place.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:60, al-khanāzīra is “pigs” or “swine.” Pigs makes the plural explicit; swine is an established formal equivalent with an unchanged plural form.

“Pig-like people,” “unclean creatures” and “people behaving like pigs” are not direct translations. They belong only in commentary if a figurative reading has been established.

No distinction between domestic and wild pigs need be added to the translation: none of the five occurrences qualifies the animal that way. Any inquiry into the inclusion of wild and domestic animals requires independent linguistic and subject-specific evidence.

9. Conclusion

KH N Z R is the Qur’anic index’s classification for khinzīr, the pig, whose plural is khanāzīr.

Several lexical analyses address its historical formation: some treat it as quadriliteral, while others explore a relationship with KH Z R or KH N Z. The evidence presented does not conclusively establish one explanation. Guesses about the earliest root must not change the established animal name’s meaning.

Four Qur’anic occurrences place pork among forbidden foods. The direct meaning is clear: the flesh of a familiar animal. The rule’s scope, animal parts and the exception of necessity are examined through construction and context rather than the animal name’s etymology alone.

The fifth occurrence, Al-Māʾidah 5:60, places al-khanāzīr beside al-qiradah in the history of a group subjected to divine anger. Retain the animal’s name; the nature of transformation or a possible figurative reading requires a separate exegetical inquiry.

Khinzīr must therefore not be reduced to decay, impurity, disease or an ethical attribute. Those are other usages, separate descriptions or proposals about the name’s origin.

An appropriate final definition for Forqan:

Qur’anic KH N Z R denotes the familiar animal khinzīr, the pig, and its plural khanāzīr. Its historical derivation is disputed, but its direct Qur’anic meaning is clear. It occurs four times in “the flesh of swine” and once in an account of punishment. Dietary rules, rijs and the nature of that punishment follow from construction and context rather than the animal name’s lexical meaning.
