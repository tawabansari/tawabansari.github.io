Qur’anic root ك س د

Stagnant trade, poor sales, and diminished demand

Qur’anic forms

1. The root in early Arabic

ك س د concerns lack of commercial activity, unsold goods, diminished demand, and low esteem.

In commerce, goods lacking sufficient buyers and difficult to sell are called كَاسِد. The word also describes a market with little trading activity.

Ibn Faris locates the basic meaning in something held in little esteem and attracting little desire.

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Mu‘awiyah ibn Malik al-‘Amiri states:

إِذْ كُلُّ حَيٍّ نَابِتٌ بِأَرُومَةٍ نَبْتَ الْعِضَاهِ فَمَاجِدٌ وَكَسِيدُ

Every clan grows from a root and lineage, like thorn trees: one noble, another held in little esteem.

كَسِيد contrasts with مَاجِد. The poet depicts differing standing among people or clans through plants growing from different roots.

This witness attributed to the pre-Islamic period shows that the family was not confined to commerce, but also expressed low esteem.

It is not direct evidence of commercial usage, however, and its precise composition date has not been independently established.

The Qur’an itself and Arabic dictionaries show that commercial stagnation and goods remaining unsold were recognized meanings in the language of revelation.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important Arabic forms include:

  • كَسَدَ ـ يَكْسُدُ: became unsaleable; lost commercial activity.
  • كَسَاد: stagnant trade; poor sales.
  • كُسُود: another verbal noun.
  • كَاسِد: lacking demand; difficult to sell.
  • كَاسِدَة: feminine form.
  • كَسِيد: little sought; lacking activity or esteem.
  • أَكْسَدَ: caused stagnation; also used of people whose market became stagnant.
  • إِكْسَاد: causing stagnation.

Classical expressions include:

  • كَسَدَتِ السُّوقُ: the market became stagnant.
  • كَسَدَ الْمَتَاعُ: the goods failed to sell.
  • سِلْعَةٌ كَاسِدَةٌ: goods without sufficient demand or buyers.

The Qur’anic form examined:

كَسَادَهَا

The only Qur’anic form is كَسَاد with the attached pronoun هَا in al-Tawbah 9:24.

3. Core meaning and practical image

In commerce, the core is inactivity arising from absent or reduced demand for purchases and exchange.

Imagine merchants offering goods in a market visited by few buyers. Goods remain in shops, and trading falls short of expectations.

Such a market is stagnant, and the unsold goods are كَاسِد.

Stagnation does not necessarily mean damaged goods, lost capital, or bankruptcy. Sound, valuable goods may sell poorly because purchasing power declines, needs change, supply rises, or market conditions worsen.

Merchants may still hold their capital but cannot convert their goods into money and keep that capital circulating.

Continued stagnation may reduce income, produce losses, or destroy part of the capital. These consequences, however, are not necessary components of the word’s meaning.

Stagnation, loss, and bankruptcy must therefore be distinguished.

Lexicons also contrast كَسَاد with نَفَاق. In this commercial sense, the latter means circulation and sale of goods, and must not be confused with religious or moral hypocrisy.

4. Qur’anic usage

كَسَادَهَا: fear of stagnant trade

The verse identifies important human attachments: family, relatives, property, commerce, and homes.

Commerce receives a particular description: trade whose stagnation people fear.

تَخْشَوْنَ كَسَادَهَا expresses more than attachment to commerce; it reveals anxiety about its economic future.

A merchant may have spent years gathering capital, acquiring goods, finding customers, and expanding business. Concern about weak markets and unsold goods is natural.

This concern can influence moral and social decisions. Someone may avoid a responsibility they believe in simply to protect business, income, or economic standing.

The verse addresses priorities under these circumstances.

It does not condemn trade itself, love of family, wealth, or satisfaction with one’s home. It condemns placing these attachments above The God, His Messenger, and striving in His way.

Al-Nisa 4:29 recognizes trade by mutual consent, while al-Baqarah 2:282 discusses immediate transactions circulating among people.

Fear of stagnant trade is therefore not inherently defiance. The problem arises when fear of economic harm prevents commitment to truth and fulfillment of responsibility.

Worldly commerce and trade that never fails

Fatir describes another kind of trade:

In al-Tawbah 9:24, people fear their worldly business becoming stagnant. Fatir 35:29 speaks of hope for a trade that never comes to ruin.

The second verse uses commercial language to explain the value and outcome of righteous deeds.

Worldly markets can lose activity, goods remain unsold, and capital decline; righteous conduct does not perish before The God.

This comparison neither devalues worldly commerce nor guarantees believers’ financial success. It distinguishes transient worldly resources from the lasting value of righteous actions before The God.

5. Morphology and syntax

كَسَاد is the verbal noun of the basic triliteral كَسَدَ, on the pattern فَعَال.

In تَخْشَوْنَ كَسَادَهَا, كَسَادَ is the accusative object of تَخْشَوْنَ. The feminine pronoun هَا refers to تِجَارَة.

The object of fear is therefore not trade itself, but the possibility of its stagnation.

The clause تَخْشَوْنَ كَسَادَهَا qualifies تِجَارَةٌ, describing that trade through its owners’ concern.

The verse does not say the business has already stagnated. It describes a business whose possible stagnation is feared.

This matters because fear of financial loss can shape behavior even before any loss occurs.

6. Comparison with related roots

ك س د compared with ت ج ر

تِجَارَة denotes trading and exchange undertaken for gain.

كَسَاد is one condition trade may encounter.

A merchant can continue trading while reduced buyer numbers and difficulty selling make the business stagnant.

Trade names the activity; stagnation describes its condition.

ك س د compared with ب و ر

بَوَار concerns ruin, fruitlessness, stagnation, and destruction.

It overlaps with كَسَاد commercially: Arabic also calls an inactive market سُوقٌ بَائِرَةٌ.

Its range is broader, however, extending to wasted deeds, failed schemes, and destruction.

Fatir 35:10 describes evil plotting coming to ruin, whereas Fatir 35:29 denies ruin for the trade described there.

Qur’anic كَسَاد denotes poor commercial activity without necessarily implying total destruction.

ك س د compared with خ س ر

خُسْرَان concerns loss, diminished capital, and losing something valuable.

Stagnation can cause loss but is not identical with it.

A merchant’s goods may lack buyers, making business stagnant. Without a decline in capital value or additional costs and losses, however, one cannot infer that the capital has been destroyed merely because the goods have not sold.

Conversely, a merchant may sell many goods yet lose money through high costs or prices below acquisition costs.

Loss can then occur without stagnation.

ك س د compared with ر ب ح

رِبْح denotes profit or increase from trading.

The verse describes trade yielding no profit.

Unprofitable trade need not be stagnant. Transactions may occur without a beneficial result.

Al-Tawbah 9:24 concerns fear of stagnant commerce; al-Baqarah 2:16 concerns an unfavorable transaction outcome.

Profit and stagnation therefore describe different aspects of commercial activity.

7. Suggested renderings for Forqan

Arabic expressionSuggested rendering
كَسَدَbecame stagnant; failed to sell
كَسَادstagnant trade; poor sales
كَاسِدlacking demand
كَسِيدlittle sought; held in low esteem
سِلْعَةٌ كَاسِدَةٌgoods that do not sell
كَسَادَهَاits stagnation
تَخْشَوْنَ كَسَادَهَاyou fear its stagnation
تِجَارَةٌ تَخْشَوْنَ كَسَادَهَاtrade whose stagnation you fear

Because کساد is familiar in Persian and Dari and retains its commercial meaning, it is a suitable translation equivalent there.

بی‌رونقی is also a clear alternative for readers less familiar with کساد.

“Bankruptcy” or “destruction of capital” is unsuitable for كَسَادَهَا because these add consequences the word does not necessarily imply.

The modern technical term “economic recession” also has a wider scope whose full characteristics should not be imposed on the Qur’anic word.

Conclusion

ك س د concerns reduced demand, inactive trade, unsold goods, and low esteem.

Al-Tawbah 9:24 uses it for fear that one’s business may lose activity.

It relates to loss, reduced profit, and destroyed capital without being identical with any of them.

The Qur’an recognizes natural concern about economic prospects while refusing to make it the ultimate criterion of moral decision-making.

Commerce serves human needs; truth and responsibility should not be sacrificed to it.
