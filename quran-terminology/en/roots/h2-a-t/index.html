Hāt and hātū: bringing and making available what is requested of the addressee

1. Meaning and use in early Arabic

هَاتِ in Arabic commands someone to bring, give, or make something available. The speaker asks the addressee to bring within reach something they possess or are able to produce.

In ordinary usage, someone saying هَاتِ may mean “give,” “bring,” or “bring it to me.” This sense existed in early Arabic and was not restricted to Quranic usage or religious terminology.

A witness from pre-Islamic Arabic poetry

In a poem attributed to al-Aʿshā, a poet of the late pre-Islamic period, a group continually drinking wine is described as follows:

لَا يَسْتَفِيقُونَ مِنْهَا وَهِيَ رَاهِنَةٌ

lā yastafīqūna minhā wa-hiya rāhinatun

إِلَّا بِهَاتِ وَإِنْ عَلُّوا وَإِنْ نَهِلُوا

illā bi-hāti wa-in ʿallū wa-in nahilū

“They do not recover from it, while it remains with them, except to say ‘bring more,’ whether they have drunk again or taken their first drink.”

In this image, after drinking, the people ask the cupbearer for more wine. هَاتِ is a practical request: they want something and expect someone else to bring it.

The witness illustrates the use of hāt in early Arabic to request something in ordinary life. The poem is transmitted among al-Aʿshā’s works, although its precise date of composition is not independently established. It can therefore be treated as evidence from the late pre-Islamic poetic tradition, not as a precisely dated record of the word’s first appearance.

One version of a verse attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays likewise contains هَاتِي نَاوِلِينِي, “give; pass it to me.” Another version of the line does not contain hātī, however. This reading is consequently less secure evidence for the word’s precise antiquity than an undisputed witness would be.

In both examples, the intended sense is requesting something from the addressee or asking for it to be passed to the speaker.

2. Linguistic forms and word structure

The principal form under discussion is هَاتِ. Its form varies with the number and gender of the addressees.

Recognized forms include:

  • هَاتِ: addressing one male: bring; give.
  • هَاتِي: addressing one female: bring; give.
  • هَاتِيَا: addressing two people: bring.
  • هَاتُوا: addressing a male or mixed group: bring.
  • هَاتِينَ: addressing a female group: bring.

Of this set, only هَاتُوا occurs in the Quran.

Disagreement over the root and structure of hāt

Arabic grammarians disagree about the structure of hāt. Their disagreement does not decisively affect its clear Quranic meaning, but matters for etymology.

Many grammarians classify هَاتِ as a defective imperative verb: a verb used principally in the imperative, without the usual range of commonly used tense forms.

An important argument for this analysis is its acceptance of markers identifying the addressee. Arabic uses hātī for a female, hātiyā for two people, and hātū for a group. This behavior is consistent with an imperative verb.

Others classify it as an imperative verb-equivalent noun: a word conveying a command but not analyzed as an ordinary verb.

Different accounts of its derivation are also recorded. Some relate its structure to هَاتَى، يُهَاتِي، مُهَاتَاةً; others propose a connection with آتَى through replacement of hamzah by hāʾ.

Classical Arabic also records forms such as يُهَاتِي and مُهَاتَاة, but these do not occur in the Quran. Their existence alone does not prove that every hypothetical part of a verbal paradigm was current in pre-Islamic Arabic.

Quranic indexes may consequently list hātū under hā–hamzah–tāʾ, while some classical dictionaries discuss it under هتا. Such variation shows why an indexing label should not be accepted without examination as the word’s certain historical root.

What linguistic and Quranic usage securely establishes is that هَاتِ and هَاتُوا request the bringing or presentation of something.

3. Quranic occurrences

هَاتُوا occurs four times in the Quran: Al-Baqarah 2:111, Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:24, Al-Naml 27:64, and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:75.

In every occurrence the command accompanies بُرْهَانَكُمْ: “Bring your proof.”

This combination shows the Quran using an ordinary command to bring or present something in an argumentative setting. What is requested is now proof supporting a claim, rather than a material object.

Al-Baqarah 2:111: proof against an exclusive claim to the Garden

وَقَالُوا لَنْ يَدْخُلَ الْجَنَّةَ إِلَّا مَنْ كَانَ هُودًا أَوْ نَصَارَىٰ ۗ تِلْكَ أَمَانِيُّهُمْ ۗ قُلْ هَاتُوا بُرْهَانَكُمْ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ صَادِقِينَ

wa-qālū lan yadkhula al-jannata illā man kāna hūdan aw naṣārā tilka amāniyyuhum qul hātū burhānakum in kuntum ṣādiqīn

Al-Baqarah 2:111

“They said: ‘None will enter the Garden unless they are Jews or Christians.’ Those are their wishes. Say: ‘Bring your proof, if you are truthful.’”

The verse presents a claim restricting entry to the Garden. The Quran calls it wishful thinking and then asks its proponents to produce proof for their assertion.

هَاتُوا here means presenting something capable of supporting the claim. Merely expressing an opinion, repeating an inherited belief, or relying on religious identity does not replace producing proof.

هَاتُوا does not in itself mean “prove.” When its object is بُرْهَانَكُمْ, however, the command amounts to a demand for substantiating evidence.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:24: requesting proof for other deities

أَمِ اتَّخَذُوا مِنْ دُونِهِ آلِهَةً ۖ قُلْ هَاتُوا بُرْهَانَكُمْ ۖ هَٰذَا ذِكْرُ مَنْ مَعِيَ وَذِكْرُ مَنْ قَبْلِي ۗ بَلْ أَكْثَرُهُمْ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ الْحَقَّ ۖ فَهُمْ مُعْرِضُونَ

ami ittakhadhū min dūnihi ālihatan qul hātū burhānakum hādhā dhikru man maʿiya wa-dhikru man qablī bal aktharuhum lā yaʿlamūna al-ḥaqqa fa-hum muʿriḍūn

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:24

“Or have they taken deities besides Him? Say: ‘Bring your proof. This is the reminder of those with me and the reminder of those before me.’ Yet most of them do not know the truth, so they turn away.”

The subject here is the acceptance of deities other than The God.

هَاتُوا asks the claimants to provide proof for such a belief. The question is not merely the names of these deities or how many followers they have, but the support for a claim assigning them divine status.

The continuation speaks of truth and turning away. The demand for proof is therefore tied to recognizing truth, rather than merely winning an argument.

Al-Naml 27:64: proof in response to questions about creation and provision

أَمَّنْ يَبْدَأُ الْخَلْقَ ثُمَّ يُعِيدُهُ وَمَنْ يَرْزُقُكُمْ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ وَالْأَرْضِ ۗ أَإِلَٰهٌ مَعَ اللَّهِ ۚ قُلْ هَاتُوا بُرْهَانَكُمْ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ صَادِقِينَ

amman yabdaʾu al-khalqa thumma yuʿīduhu wa-man yarzuqukum mina al-samāʾi wa-al-arḍi a-ilāhun maʿa Allāhi qul hātū burhānakum in kuntum ṣādiqīn

Al-Naml 27:64

“Or who originates creation and then restores it, and who provides for you from heaven and earth? Is there a deity alongside The God? Say: ‘Bring your proof, if you are truthful.’”

Here the demand for proof follows questions about originating creation, restoring it, and providing sustenance.

The Quran directs the addressee’s attention to the phenomena and acts under discussion, then asks whether there is a deity alongside The God.

Anyone making such a claim must produce their proof.

هَاتُوا still means bringing and presenting. Its object, however, concerns a claim to divinity and its relation to creation. The breadth of the subject must not change the verb’s lexical meaning into “proving creatorship” or “knowing the secrets of creation.”

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:75: requesting proof in the scene of judgment

وَنَزَعْنَا مِنْ كُلِّ أُمَّةٍ شَهِيدًا فَقُلْنَا هَاتُوا بُرْهَانَكُمْ فَعَلِمُوا أَنَّ الْحَقَّ لِلَّهِ وَضَلَّ عَنْهُمْ مَا كَانُوا يَفْتَرُونَ

wa-nazaʿnā min kulli ummatin shahīdan fa-qulnā hātū burhānakum fa-ʿalimū anna al-ḥaqqa li-llāhi wa-ḍalla ʿanhum mā kānū yaftarūn

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:75

“We drew out a witness from every community and said: ‘Bring your proof.’ Then they knew that the truth belongs to The God, and what they had fabricated deserted them.”

This verse presents the same command in the scene of judgment.

At its beginning, a witness is drawn from each community. The addressees are then asked to present their proof.

Its ending describes the recognition of truth and the disappearance of what they had fabricated.

Here too هَاتُوا commands the bringing of proof, but the context places the request within an accounting and assessment of claims.

The four Quranic occurrences thus share a structure while addressing different subjects: exclusive entitlement to the Garden, acceptance of other deities, a claim to divinity in relation to creation and provision, and accountability in judgment.

4. Core meaning and comparison with related words

Hātū and hāʾum

The expressions are closely related in use, but the direction of the request differs in the occurrences considered here.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:19 reads:

فَأَمَّا مَنْ أُوتِيَ كِتَابَهُ بِيَمِينِهِ فَيَقُولُ هَاؤُمُ اقْرَءُوا كِتَابِيَهْ

fa-ammā man ūtiya kitābahu bi-yamīnihi fa-yaqūlu hāʾumu iqraʾū kitābiyah

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:19

“As for the one given his record in his right hand, he says: ‘Take it; read my record!’”

The speaker offers something to the addressees and invites them to take it.

In هَاتُوا بُرْهَانَكُمْ, by contrast, the speaker asks the addressees to bring what they are expected to present.

Two simple situations clarify the difference:

Someone holding a book tells friends, “Take it and read.” This situation resembles hāʾum.

When someone instead asks another person, “Bring your book,” the situation is closer to hāt.

In these uses, hāʾum accompanies an offer from the speaker for the addressee to receive, whereas hāt requests that the addressee bring something toward the speaker.

This functional difference does not by itself prove that the forms are historically unrelated. Their possible linguistic relationship is a separate question from their clear meanings in a sentence.

Study of hā–alif–hamzah

Hātū and iʾtū: comparison with hamzah–tāʾ–yāʾ

أَتَىٰ has a broad Quranic semantic field, including coming, arriving, and bringing in different constructions.

For example, Al-Baqarah 2:23 contains فَأْتُوا بِسُورَةٍ, “then bring a surah.”

فَأْتُوا and هَاتُوا can both be translated “bring” in certain contexts, but their structures and uses are not identical.

أَتَىٰ occurs in different tenses and constructions in the Quran. Hāt, however, appears in Quranic usage as a command.

Moreover, فَأْتُوا بِسُورَةٍ uses the preposition bi-, whereas in هَاتُوا بُرْهَانَكُمْ, “your proof” is the command’s direct object.

This syntactic distinction matters, but does not by itself establish an absolute rule about the kind of bringing or the degree of proof in every use of the expressions.

Hāt and aḥḍara: bringing and making present

أَحْضَرَ belongs to ḥāʾ–ḍād–rāʾ and concerns making something present or bringing it into someone’s presence.

Hātū likewise asks an addressee to bring something and make it available. For this reason, Arabic grammarians sometimes explain hāt through aḥḍir, “make present.”

Nevertheless, the sense of presence and the entire semantic range of ḥāʾ–ḍād–rāʾ must not be transferred to hāt. Its four Quranic occurrences directly command the bringing of proof; they do not report something becoming present.

5. Why does every occurrence say “bring your proof”?

The consistent pairing of هَاتُوا with بُرْهَانَكُمْ is a clear feature of the word’s Quranic usage.

In ordinary speech, someone might ask another person to bring water, food, a book, or an implement. The Quran uses this familiar command to request proof.

The difference lies in what is requested, not in a transformation of hāt into a specialized doctrinal term.

In these four verses, claimants must provide something that takes their assertion beyond mere words.

The study of bāʾ–rāʾ–hāʾ–nūn discusses proof and its distinction from claims, suppositions, and other forms of evidence. The principle “Seeking Evidence and Proof” also explains this Quranic method as a principle of knowledge and judgment.

Study of bāʾ–rāʾ–hāʾ–nūn

Repeating the phrase in four settings shows that requesting proof is not restricted to one particular religious claim. Someone making claims about salvation, divinity, or truth must be able to support what they say.

This broader principle arises from the verses together and the meaning of “proof.” Hātū alone simply commands bringing or presenting something.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

In general Arabic, hāt can be rendered “bring,” “give,” or “give it to me,” depending on the situation.

In all four Quranic occurrences, however, its object is “your proof,” making “bring” a natural equivalent.

Thus:

هَاتُوا بُرْهَانَكُمْ

hātū burhānakum

Al-Baqarah 2:111

“Bring your proof.”

This translation preserves the word’s meaning and reads naturally.

“Present your proof” also conveys the sentence’s meaning, while “bring” follows the verb’s direct wording more closely.

“Prove your claim” explains the intended outcome of producing proof, but is not a direct translation of hātū; it is better used in analysis of the sentence.

7. Conclusion

The Quranic entry hā–alif–tāʾ concerns the command hāt and its plural form hātū. Its recognized early Arabic meaning requests the bringing, giving, or making available of something.

Early Arabic poetic witnesses illustrate its use in everyday speech to request something from another person. Its historical root and grammatical classification are disputed, but this does not obscure its practical Quranic meaning.

Only hātū occurs in the Quran, and each of its four occurrences accompanies “your proof.”

In this construction, hātū commands bringing, while “your proof” specifies what must be brought. Demands for substantiation and accountability follow from the combination and the surrounding verses.

The practical definition of this entry for Forqan is therefore:

Hā–alif–tāʾ: requesting that an addressee bring or present something; the Quranic plural command hātū means “bring.”

This meaning remains constant across the four Quranic occurrences, while the claim at issue and the circumstances of the demand for proof differ in each verse.
