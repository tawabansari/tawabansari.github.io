Slenderness, delicacy, and close joining: from small ears and fine limbs to monastic places of worship

1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

The Arabic root Ṣ M ʿ concerns slenderness, delicacy, compactness, and parts coming close together. Early uses describe small ears close to the head, fine leg joints, unopened plants, feathers sticking together, and buildings with a narrow, prominent upper part.

Another branch records expressions such as aṣmaʿ al-qalb for someone sharp-minded and firm in judgment.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as ṣawāmiʿu in Al-Ḥajj 22:40. This is the plural of ṣawmaʿah and, in context, denotes monks’ places of worship.

Important Arabic forms include:

  • صَمِعَ (ṣamiʿa): became small, narrow, or compact, according to subject.
  • صَمَع (ṣamaʿ): smallness, slenderness, and delicacy, especially of ears or limb joints.
  • أَصْمَع (aṣmaʿ): small and fine with a narrow or drawn-together extremity; of a person, also sharp-minded and firm in judgment.
  • صَمْعَاء (ṣamʿāʾ): feminine of aṣmaʿ, used of ears, limbs, plants, and certain narrow, compact objects.
  • مُصَمَّع (muṣammaʿ): with closely joined or compact parts; in some uses, an animal with narrow, distinctive ears or horns.
  • تَصَمَّعَ (taṣammaʿa): had its parts come close or compact together, like arrow feathers sticking together.
  • صَوْمَعَة (ṣawmaʿah): a tall building, often narrowing at the top; in established usage, a monk’s worship place or retreat.
  • صَوَامِع (ṣawāmiʿ): plural of ṣawmaʿah; the family’s sole Qur’anic form.
  • صَوْمَعَ (ṣawmaʿa): raised a building or elevated its upper part.

These derivatives have different Arabic uses, but the Qur’an uses only ṣawāmiʿ. That word should therefore be central to the Qur’anic study, with the other forms examined for its linguistic background.

2. The semantic core in early Arabic

Delicacy and parts coming close together

Ibn Fāris proposes a semantic principle for Ṣ M ʿ in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

لَطَافَةٍ فِي الشَّيْءِ وَتَضَامٍّ (laṭāfatin fī al-shayʾi wa-taḍāmmin)

Delicacy in something, and its parts drawing together or being compact.

This fits numerous uses within the family.

An animal’s ear may be small and close to its head, its leg joint fine and scarcely projecting, and a plant bud still unopened with its parts packed together.

Each shows delicacy or compactness, but its form and subject differ.

Ṣamaʿ should therefore not invariably be translated only as becoming small or coming together. Each use follows what the word describes.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah and early lexicons: delicacy and closely joined parts

A small ear close to the head

One of the best-known uses concerns human and animal ears.

Arabic says:

أُذُنٌ صَمْعَاءُ (udhunun ṣamʿāʾu)

A small, delicate ear close to the head.

Aṣmaʿ also describes a person or animal with small ears that scarcely project.

Early sources apply the adjective to goats, gazelles, and ostriches. Explanations mention short ears close to the head and sometimes a narrow or pointed shape.

Ṣamʿāʾ here concerns the ear’s shape and size rather than hearing ability.

An animal’s being aṣmaʿ therefore establishes neither weak nor strong hearing.

Fine leg joints

Another use concerns the joints of limbs.

Arabic kaʿbun aṣmaʿu describes a fine, scarcely projecting joint or ankle.

Ṣumʿu al-kuʿūbi also describes animals’ legs with small, fine joints lacking large protrusions.

Like small ears, this emphasizes fine shape and reduced projection.

The word itself nevertheless means neither a thin body overall nor animal weakness. A powerful animal may have fine, well-shaped leg joints.

A pre-Islamic poetic witness

Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī describes a wild bull pursued by hunting dogs:

فَبَثَّهُنَّ عَلَيْهِ وَاسْتَمَرَّ بِهِ
صُمْعُ الْكُعُوبِ بَرِيئَاتٌ مِنَ الْحَرَدِ

fa-baththahunna ʿalayhi wa-istamarra bihi / ṣumʿu al-kuʿūbi barīʾātun mina al-ḥaradi

A rendering close to the sense:

“He unleashed the dogs upon him; legs with fine joints, free of defects, bore him onward in flight.”

Ṣumʿu al-kuʿūbi here describes the wild bull’s legs; baththahunna refers to releasing the hunting dogs against him.

Fine joints accompany sound limbs. The poet therefore describes delicacy and proportion rather than weak legs.

This witness from the pre-Islamic Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī shows the family’s use for animal anatomy before the Qur’an.

Khizānat al-Adab citing Al-Aghānī: the wild bull’s legs in Al-Nābighah’s witness

Compact plants and unopened buds

Early Arabic buhmā ṣamʿāʾ describes a growth stage in which the plant has risen but its parts have not fully opened.

Burʿūmatun ṣamʿāʾ likewise describes a bud whose parts remain compact and closed.

Smallness alone is not decisive: a bud may have grown while remaining unopened with its parts close together.

This fits compactness and closely joined parts.

It does not make every derivative mean immaturity, closure, or readiness to blossom. Those features belong specifically to the plant context.

Feathers sticking together

In a poem about an arrow striking game, Abū Dhuʾayb al-Hudhalī says:

فَرَمَى فَأَنْفَذَ مِنْ نَحُوصٍ عَائِطٍ
سَهْمًا فَخَرَّ وَرِيشُهُ مُتَصَمِّعُ

fa-ramā fa-anfadha min naḥūṣin ʿāʾiṭin / sahman fa-kharra wa-rīshuhu mutaṣammiʿu

A rendering close to the sense:

“He shot an arrow through a nonpregnant female animal; the arrow fell with its feathers clinging together.”

Mutaṣammiʿ describes the arrow’s feathers drawn together and sticking because of blood or moisture.

The use clearly shows parts joining closely.

Abū Dhuʾayb was a mukhaḍram, living through both the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. This particular line cannot be declared certainly pre-Qur’anic without dating its composition, but remains useful evidence for early Arabic.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab: Abū Dhuʾayb’s witness and arrow feathers

3. From physical delicacy to sharpness of thought

Lexicons record qalbun aṣmaʿu for an alert, quick-minded, receptive heart or mind.

Raʾyun aṣmaʿu likewise denotes firm, considered judgment.

Some explanations relate this to gathering and concentrating strength or thought, as though the person’s thinking were collected rather than scattered and their decisions firm.

This is a semantic and figurative connection rather than proof of a physical mechanism in the brain or heart.

Qalbun aṣmaʿu does not mean a physically small heart. Nor does intelligence necessarily imply small ears or delicate limbs.

An Arabic word family can extend from a physical feature to mental or behavioral ability without retaining every physical characteristic in its figurative use.

The same field records ʿazmatun ṣamʿāʾu for firm resolve or determination.

Firmness does not make every decision morally right. Its value follows its purpose and consequences.

4. Ṣawmaʿah: from architectural form to a place of worship

The architectural meaning of ṣawmaʿah

Arabic ṣawmaʿah describes a tall building with a generally narrower, prominent upper part.

Lexical explanations emphasize its shape and the upper portion’s gradual narrowing.

Sībawayh also relates its formation to aṣmaʿ: something with a narrow, drawn-together extremity.

This fits delicacy and close joining.

No single geometry should nevertheless become necessary for every use. Buildings called ṣawmaʿah could differ in size, material, and architectural form across regions and periods.

A narrow upper part therefore explains the name’s lexical background, while established usage also makes ṣawmaʿah the name of a building and place of worship.

A monk’s retreat and place of worship

Arabic sources use ṣawmaʿatu al-rāhibi for a monk’s place of worship and seclusion.

It could stand apart from ordinary settlements, providing a place for worship and ascetic life.

Some uses describe a tall tower or elevated chamber occupied by a monk; broader usage extends to monks’ places of worship or residence.

Tower alone therefore does not adequately explain ṣawmaʿah. A tower is one architectural feature of some examples, while religious use also belongs to the established meaning.

Ṣawmaʿah and dayr are not entirely identical in every use. Dayr can denote a complex housing a community of monks, whereas traditional ṣawmaʿah often denotes a monk’s retreat or place of worship.

Later languages and regional usage have not always maintained that distinction.

Other objects called ṣawmaʿah

Early Arabic also records ṣawmaʿatu al-tharīdi.

Tharīd is a dish of bread with food or broth over it. A tall mound with a narrow, prominent top could be compared with a ṣawmaʿah.

Some sources also record ṣawāmiʿ for a kind of head covering or tall hood.

Ṣawmaʿah therefore was not restricted to worship buildings; appearance also contributed to naming.

These nonreligious uses should not enter the translation of ṣawāmiʿ in Al-Ḥajj 22:40, whose context concerns places of worship.

Early lexicons: sharp thought, monastic buildings, tharīd, and hoods

The word’s historical origin

Arabic lexicographers classify ṣawmaʿah with Ṣ M ʿ and explain its name through a narrow, prominent upper part.

Some historical studies of religious vocabulary also propose influence from neighboring languages, particularly Ethiopic and South Arabian, on the name’s use.

Two questions should remain distinct: how Arabic understood and classified the word, and the language of its earliest historical origin.

An Arabic derivational explanation alone does not prove the word never received outside influence. Conversely, resemblance across languages without sufficient historical evidence does not establish the direction of borrowing.

For the Qur’anic use, ṣawāmiʿ’s established meaning as monks’ places of worship remains clear.

5. The Qur’anic use: ṣawāmiʿ in Al-Ḥajj 22:40

The sole Qur’anic occurrence appears in Al-Ḥajj:

وَلَوْلَا دَفْعُ اللَّهِ النَّاسَ بَعْضَهُمْ بِبَعْضٍ لَهُدِّمَتْ صَوَامِعُ وَبِيَعٌ وَصَلَوَاتٌ وَمَسَاجِدُ يُذْكَرُ فِيهَا اسْمُ اللَّهِ كَثِيرًا

wa-lawlā dafʿu allāhi al-nāsa baʿḍahum bi-baʿḍin la-huddimat ṣawāmiʿu wa-biyaʿun wa-ṣalawātun wa-masājidu yudhkaru fīhā ismu allāhi kathīran

“Had The God not repelled some people through others, monasteries, churches, synagogues, and mosques in which The God’s name is often remembered would have been demolished.”

Al-Ḥajj 22:40

The meaning of ṣawāmiʿ

Ṣawāmiʿ is the broken plural of ṣawmaʿah and here denotes monks’ places of worship.

Most early exegetical explanations identify Christian monks’ places of worship; some mention Sābian places of worship.

The variation suggests historical application to worship buildings of different communities, but every exegetical disagreement does not create a separate lexical meaning.

The context concerns religious buildings exposed to destruction.

Monasteries is therefore suitable: it preserves the recognized building name without reducing it to one architectural form or a narrower group than the verse specifies.

Distinguishing ṣawāmiʿ from other worship buildings

The verse places four expressions together:

صَوَامِعُ، بِيَعٌ، صَلَوَاتٌ، مَسَاجِدُ (ṣawāmiʿu, biyaʿun, ṣalawātun, masājidu)

Ṣawāmiʿ in established usage denotes monks’ places of worship.

Biyaʿ is the plural of bīʿah, used here for Christian churches and places of worship.

The familiar early explanation relates ṣalawāt to Jewish places of worship, although sources record disagreement over its exact identification.

Masājid are places of prostration and worship, especially suggesting Muslim mosques in this list.

The distinct names do not establish every form of worship as identical or erase communities’ differences of belief.

The verse concerns buildings recognized among different religious communities. Their separate names acknowledge these worship places alongside one another.

Monastery and church: close but different names

Both can historically belong to Christian tradition while remaining different names.

Ṣawmaʿah is more closely associated with monks’ seclusion and individual worship or worship by a small community.

A church may provide a more public place for believers’ collective worship.

This helps explain ṣawāmiʿ and biyaʿ appearing together.

It does not establish every ṣawmaʿah as necessarily built for one person or every church as never used for individual retreat or worship.

The terms have changed historically, and religious buildings differ in scale and function.

6. Monastic places within the defense of worship buildings

Unjust expulsion from homes

Al-Ḥajj 22:40 begins:

الَّذِينَ أُخْرِجُوا مِنْ دِيَارِهِمْ بِغَيْرِ حَقٍّ إِلَّا أَنْ يَقُولُوا رَبُّنَا اللَّهُ

alladhīna ukhrijū min diyārihim bi-ghayri ḥaqqin illā an yaqūlū rabbunā allāhu

“Those expelled from their homes without right, only because they say: Our Lord is The God.”

This places the verse within oppression and expulsion because of belief.

The preceding verse also concerns people subjected to fighting and wronged.

Al-Ḥajj 22:40 then explains that without God’s repelling some people through others, the named places of worship would be destroyed.

Monasteries therefore appear for more than incidental architectural description. They belong among places exposed to aggression and destruction.

Repelling people through one another

Dafʿu allāhi al-nāsa baʿḍahum bi-baʿḍin concerns God’s repelling some people through others.

This can be understood within preventing aggression, resisting oppression, and restraining power that violates others’ rights.

Ṣawāmiʿ itself, however, specifies neither a defensive method, a kind of war, nor a form of government. Those questions require the broader verses on defense, oppression, and social responsibility.

The verse’s image clearly connects absence of restraint against aggression with the destruction of places of worship.

Within that context, it highlights preserving the possibility of worship and preventing attacks on religious places.

Study of D F ʿ

Does the passage concern mosques alone?

The list ends with:

يُذْكَرُ فِيهَا اسْمُ اللَّهِ كَثِيرًا (yudhkaru fīhā ismu allāhi kathīran)

The God’s name is often remembered in them.

Early exegesis records two tendencies for fīhā’s referent: all the listed places, or the nearest name, mosques.

Either way, ṣawāmiʿ explicitly belongs to the list of places that would be demolished without such repelling.

Monasteries cannot therefore be deleted from the verse or the passage narrowed as though only demolished mosques were at issue.

Conversely, naming these places alone does not approve every belief, teaching, or practice they may contain. Protecting people’s lives and right to worship differs from judging every belief correct.

Other verses concerning obstruction of worship

Al-Baqarah 2:114 says:

وَمَنْ أَظْلَمُ مِمَّنْ مَنَعَ مَسَاجِدَ اللَّهِ أَنْ يُذْكَرَ فِيهَا اسْمُهُ وَسَعَىٰ فِي خَرَابِهَا

wa-man aẓlamu mimman manaʿa masājida allāhi an yudhkara fīhā ismuhu wa-saʿā fī kharābihā

“Who is more unjust than one who prevents The God’s name being remembered in His places of worship and strives to destroy them?”

The verse places preventing God’s remembrance and seeking worship places’ destruction within injustice.

Al-Ḥajj 22:40 likewise addresses monasteries and other worship places exposed to destruction.

The verses connect through respect for worship and the danger of attacking its places, while their names and contexts differ.

Al-Baqarah 2:256 also states the absence of compulsion in religion. Alongside verses on oppression and defense, it provides a broader setting for studying freedom of belief and worship.

Ṣawāmiʿ alone nevertheless does not yield every legal detail concerning worship places, property, social regulation, or conditions of defense. Those require an independent study across the relevant verses.

Al-Ṭabarī: repelling people, the worship-place list, and both readings of fīhā

7. Comparing related roots and words

Ṣ M ʿ (ṣamiʿa) and S M ʿ (samiʿa)

The roots resemble one another in spelling and sound but differ in meaning.

S M ʿ concerns hearing and receiving sound.

Ṣ M ʿ principally concerns delicacy, slenderness, compactness, and, in its Qur’anic branch, monastic worship places.

The small ear in aṣmaʿ does not mean hearing. Ṣawāmiʿ likewise has no direct derivational connection with samiʿa.

The resemblance of letters therefore does not justify deriving ṣawmaʿah from hearing.

Study of S M ʿ

Ṣ M ʿ (ṣamiʿa) and Ṣ W M (ṣāma)

Ṣawmaʿah contains wāw in its spelling, but Arabic morphological classification assigns it to Ṣ M ʿ.

Ṣ W M instead concerns ṣawm and abstention.

The wāw in ṣawmaʿah does not make it a derivative of ṣawm or fasting.

Monks may abstain from behaviors or fast as part of religious life, but that behavioral connection is not etymological evidence.

Ṣawmaʿah therefore should not be defined as a house of fasting.

Study of Ṣ W M

Ṣ M ʿ (ṣamiʿa) and Ṣ L W (ṣalāh)

Ṣalāh has its own Qur’anic semantic field and uses.

Al-Ḥajj 22:40 places ṣalawāt beside ṣawāmiʿ, with the familiar historical explanation relating it to Jewish places of worship.

Ṣawmaʿah and ṣalāh nevertheless are different words.

Ṣawmaʿah names a religious building, while ṣalāh in its wider Qur’anic uses concerns devotional action and relationship, and in certain contexts also names a worship place.

Worship within a ṣawmaʿah does not alone make ṣalāh its lexical meaning.

Study of Ṣ L W

Ṣ M ʿ (ṣawmaʿah) and S J D (masjid)

Masjid derives from S J D and concerns a place of prostration.

Arabic ṣawmaʿah names a devotional building historically associated with monks’ life and worship.

Both may be places of worship, while belonging to separate word families.

Al-Ḥajj 22:40 puts them in one list because both are places whose destruction the verse envisages.

Co-occurrence establishes neither one root nor identical forms of worship within them.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

Persian should retain the familiar ṣawmaʿah name and its plural ṣawmaʿah-hā for Qur’anic ṣawāmiʿ; English can use monasteries.

Ṣawmaʿah: a monastery or a monk’s place of worship or retreat.

Ṣawāmiʿ: monasteries; in Al-Ḥajj 22:40, recognized religious buildings, especially monks’ places of worship.

Ṣamiʿa: became small, delicate, or compact, according to subject.

Aṣmaʿ: small and delicate; of an ear, small and close to the head; of thought, sharp-minded and firm in judgment.

Ṣamʿāʾ: delicate and drawn together; of plants, a bud or plant whose parts have not opened.

Taṣammaʿa: came close together or had its parts stick together.

Ṣawmaʿa al-bināʾa: raised the building or elevated its upper part.

In Al-Ḥajj 22:40, monasteries better conveys the term than towers, pointed buildings, or places of seclusion.

Those alternatives may explain aspects of some buildings’ architectural history or function but do not adequately convey the recognized worship-place name.

9. Conclusion

Ṣ M ʿ concerns delicacy, slenderness, and closely joined parts.

This appears in small ears, fine leg joints, unopened buds, feathers sticking together, and narrow architectural parts.

Figurative uses describe sharp thought and firm judgment, without making bodily features of ears or limbs definitions of intelligence and will.

Established Arabic ṣawmaʿah denotes a building for monks’ worship and seclusion. A narrow, prominent upper part provides an intelligible lexical connection with Ṣ M ʿ without exhausting the building name’s meaning.

The Qur’an uses only ṣawāmiʿ. Al-Ḥajj 22:40 lists it with churches, synagogues, and mosques as worship places that would be demolished without restraint against aggression.

The verse supports studying protection against oppression and destruction of worship places across Qur’anic passages. Ṣawāmiʿ itself neither independently judges every belief nor prescribes defensive methods or management rules for worship places.

Brief root meaning for Forqan:

Delicacy, slenderness, and parts being close or compact; in architecture, a tall building or one with a narrow upper part, and in religious use, a monastery or monks’ place of worship.

Meaning in Al-Ḥajj 22:40:

صَوَامِعُ (ṣawāmiʿu): monasteries; monks’ places of worship, named alongside other worship places.
