Brief Introduction to the Root

The root T Y H belongs to the field of wandering, bewilderment, loss of direction, and movement that does not find a way out. Its central image is a person moving through a broad space without finding a clear sign, a fixed direction, or a path of escape.

Wandering here does not mean stillness. A person in tayh may move extensively, try different routes, and cover a great distance, yet the movement does not reach its destination. Tayh should therefore not be translated only as “getting lost”; it also contains continued movement together with inability to find the right course.

In the Qur’an, the root occurs concerning a people who, after refusing to enter the appointed land, wander in the earth for forty years:

قَالَ فَإِنَّهَا مُحَرَّمَةٌ عَلَيْهِمْ أَرْبَعِينَ سَنَةً يَتِيهُونَ فِي الْأَرْضِ فَلَا تَأْسَ عَلَى الْقَوْمِ الْفَاسِقِينَ

“He said: then that land is forbidden to them for forty years; they will wander in the earth. So do not grieve over the defiantly disobedient people.”

Al-Ma’idah 5:26

The root’s core can be stated briefly as:

Wandering movement through a space in which a person cannot find the right way, a fixed direction, or a path of exit.

Important Forms and Derivatives of the Root

The root T Y H occurs only once in the Qur’an, and its only Qur’anic form is yatīhūna.

يَتِيهُونَ — yatīhūna

Yatīhūna is a third-person masculine plural imperfect active verb from the Form I verb tāha yatīhu.

يَتِيهُونَ فِي الْأَرْضِ

“They wander in the earth.”

Al-Ma’idah 5:26

The imperfect verb conveys the continuing and recurring character of the wandering. The verse does not describe one brief moment of becoming lost, but a condition continuing over a long period.

Non-Qur’anic Lexical Forms

The following forms are known in Arabic but do not occur in the Qur’an:

tāha: he wandered, lost the way, or remained bewildered; and in some usages, he acted arrogantly.

yatīhu: he wanders, or behaves arrogantly, according to context.

tayh, tīh, and tayahān: wandering, being lost, and movement accompanied by bewilderment.

tā’ih: a wandering or lost person; and in some linguistic usages, an arrogant person.

tayyāh and tayhān: intensely wandering, reckless, or one who enters matters without due evaluation, depending on usage.

tayyaha: he caused someone or something to wander, become lost, or be wasted.

These forms help map the lexical field but must not be introduced as Qur’anic derivatives.

The Root's Semantic Core

The semantic core of T Y H combines movement, bewilderment, and absence of direction.

A wandering person does not merely lack knowledge of location; the person does not know how to leave the present condition. Movement may continue, but it is not situated on a clear and effective route.

The meaning has three components:

First, movement: tayh is not identical with remaining completely still.

Second, bewilderment and inability to determine direction: the person cannot distinguish the right way from the wrong one or lacks sufficient signs for choosing a course.

Third, failure to reach an exit or destination: movement does not bring passage beyond the present condition.

Tayh is therefore not wholly separate from ḍalāl, but it is not exactly identical with it. Ḍalāl covers the wider field of losing a path, departing from a way, and intellectual or ethical error. Tayh highlights the more specific image of bewildered movement through a disorienting space.

In Al-Ma’idah 5:26, this wandering is not merely an inward state. It is connected with land, time, and prohibition from entering a specified territory. Before any ethical or symbolic extension, the verse’s direct meaning is therefore actual wandering in the earth.

The Root's Material Image

The material image is a person in an open, extensive land without clear signs. The person walks, but the horizons resemble one another; the routes cannot be distinguished and no reliable indication of an exit appears.

In such a space, movement does not necessarily become progress. A person may think that the starting point has been left far behind, yet return to the same area or continue along a route that does not reach the destination.

Arabic uses al-tīhu mina al-arḍ for broad land or desert in which a person becomes bewildered and loses the way. This usage shows how the name of a state of wandering can be transferred to the place that causes or hosts that wandering.

The image of tayh should not be restricted simply to “desert.” A desert is one of its clearest manifestations, but the root’s core is not a particular place. It is the relation of a person to a space in which direction and a way out cannot be found.

Use of the Root in Early Arabic

Arabic lexical sources use tāha fī al-arḍ for one who moved in bewilderment through the land, lost the way, and failed to find a path out.

Infinitives such as tayh, tīh, tawh, and tayahān are reported, with tīh among the better-known forms. A person in this condition is called tā’ih or tayyāh.

Within the same family, tayyaha al-shay’a means to waste, lose, or cause something to wander. This causative form shows that tayh may be a person’s condition or a condition into which someone or something is placed.

Lexicons also record another sense for tāha yatīhu tayhan: arrogance, self-magnification, and haughty conduct. An arrogant person may likewise be called tā’ih or tayyāh.

The exact historical relation between “wandering” and “arrogance” is uncertain. The arrogant person may have been compared to one who has left the balanced way, become lost in self-regard, or moves without regard for route and measure. This remains a possible explanation and must not be presented as certain etymology.

In Al-Ma’idah 5:26, wandering in the land is clearer and more direct than the sense of arrogance.

History of Interpretation and Semantic Limitation

One common narrowing translates yatīhūna merely as “they go around” or “they walk.” This removes the central element of bewilderment and loss of direction. Not every movement through land is tayh.

At the other extreme, “they become misguided” may transfer the word too fully into doctrinal or ethical misguidance. The context describes actual movement in the earth. Their defiance may be related to the consequence of wandering, but translation should not erase the spatial meaning.

The traditional phrase “they remained in the Desert of Tīh” may make readers think that tīh was originally the proper name of a particular desert. Lexically, however, the word first denotes wandering and also land in which someone wanders. Naming a region Tīh may have developed from that description.

Some interpretive reports add details about their daily route, returning every night to the starting point, the precise geography, or the state of different generations. These details are not stated in the verse and should not enter the root’s meaning without independent evidence.

The verse states with certainty only that the land becomes forbidden to them for forty years and that they will wander in the earth.

Methodological Note

Three levels should be distinguished in studying T Y H.

The first is lexical meaning: moving bewildered and without direction through the land.

The second is the verse’s historical context: a people refuse to enter a land they have been called to enter; consequently, it is forbidden to them for a period and they remain wandering in the earth.

The third is conceptual reflection: a person or society may be intensely active, yet because it lacks clear direction and destination, its movement does not become progress.

The third level can be inspired by the verse’s image, but it must not be presented as the direct meaning of yatīhūna. T Y H does not directly mean “identity crisis,” “political confusion,” “spiritual alienation,” or “absence of a social program.” These ideas can be employed figuratively only where the structure of directionless movement and failure to reach a destination is truly present.

The lexical branch of “arrogance” must not be placed in the translation of yatīhūna merely because the people refused the preceding command. Arrogance or disobedience may provide the background of the consequence, but the verb in this verse denotes wandering in the earth.

Qur'anic Use of the Root

The Context Before the Wandering

Before the verse of tayh, Moses calls his people to enter the holy land:

يَا قَوْمِ ادْخُلُوا الْأَرْضَ الْمُقَدَّسَةَ الَّتِي كَتَبَ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ وَلَا تَرْتَدُّوا عَلَىٰ أَدْبَارِكُمْ فَتَنْقَلِبُوا خَاسِرِينَ

“O my people, enter the holy land that The God has appointed for you, and do not turn back on your heels, lest you return as losers.”

Al-Ma’idah 5:21

The command is clear: enter the land and do not turn backward.

The people answer that powerful inhabitants prevent their entry:

قَالُوا يَا مُوسَىٰ إِنَّ فِيهَا قَوْمًا جَبَّارِينَ وَإِنَّا لَنْ نَدْخُلَهَا حَتَّىٰ يَخْرُجُوا مِنْهَا

“They said: O Moses, in it are a tyrannical people, and we will never enter it until they leave it.”

Al-Ma’idah 5:22

Despite encouragement from two God-fearing men, they persist in refusal:

قَالُوا يَا مُوسَىٰ إِنَّا لَنْ نَدْخُلَهَا أَبَدًا مَا دَامُوا فِيهَا فَاذْهَبْ أَنْتَ وَرَبُّكَ فَقَاتِلَا إِنَّا هَاهُنَا قَاعِدُونَ

“They said: O Moses, we will never enter it while they remain there. So go, you and your Lord, and fight; we are sitting right here.”

Al-Ma’idah 5:24

This statement creates an important contrast with yatīhūna. They say, innā hāhunā qāʿidūn—“we are sitting right here.” Yet their consequence is not comfortable stillness; it is wandering movement through the land.

Refusal to move along the appointed path becomes movement without direction and destination.

Wandering in the Earth

قَالَ فَإِنَّهَا مُحَرَّمَةٌ عَلَيْهِمْ أَرْبَعِينَ سَنَةً يَتِيهُونَ فِي الْأَرْضِ فَلَا تَأْسَ عَلَى الْقَوْمِ الْفَاسِقِينَ

“He said: then that land is forbidden to them for forty years; they will wander in the earth. So do not grieve over the defiantly disobedient people.”

Al-Ma’idah 5:26

Yatīhūna fī al-arḍ describes their condition: they move through the land without reaching entry and settlement in the intended territory.

The preposition fī places them within the land or the space of wandering. The verse does not say merely that they do not know the destination; their entire movement occurs in an environment where no effective direction or way out is found.

Arbaʿīna sanatan specifies the duration of this condition or the prohibition. Grammarians have discussed whether the phrase attaches precisely to muḥarramah or yatīhūna, but either way the verse joins prohibition and wandering within a forty-year period.

The verse ends by calling them al-qawmi al-fāsiqīn.

Fisq expresses departure from the boundary and path of a command. The description shows that tayh is not an event unrelated to their prior conduct, but this does not make T Y H mean defiance or disobedience. Fisq describes the people; tayh describes their condition in the earth.

The Contrast Between Sitting and Wandering

In verse 24 the people describe themselves as sitting: innā hāhunā qāʿidūn.

In verse 26 their consequence is tayh: yatīhūna fī al-arḍ.

The structural contrast is significant. They refuse purposeful movement but are not released from movement. Instead of moving toward a destination, they enter movement without destination.

The relation can be stated as a reflection:

Not taking the right path does not necessarily mean remaining motionless; it may become extensive movement without arrival.

This is a conceptual reflection arising from the context, not a direct lexical translation of T Y H.

Prohibition and Wandering

The verse first says fa-innahā muḥarramatun ʿalayhim, and then yatīhūna fī al-arḍ.

Tayh occurs where the way into the destination has been closed. They remain in the earth, but the intended land is forbidden to them.

This construction shows that wandering does not always arise from lack of motion. It may arise from the closure of a destination toward which the person had earlier refused to move.

The verse does not, however, say that every person who fails to reach a destination must be wandering as punishment for disobedience. That relation belongs to the particular context of these people.

Examination of the Qur'anic Derivative

The Form يَتِيهُونَ

Yatīhūna is an imperfect verb and one of the five inflected imperfect forms. The wāw is the masculine plural subject, and the nūn marks the indicative mood.

Its past plural is tāhū, and its singular imperfect is yatīhu.

The verb is hollow with a medial yā’. In inflection, the middle radical appears as the long vowel in tāha and yatīhu.

Continuity in the Imperfect Verb

The imperfect can indicate continuing or repeated action throughout the specified period. “They will wander” is temporally sound, while “they will remain wandering” conveys the duration more explicitly.

“They will get lost for forty years” is less suitable because getting lost may sound like a momentary event. The verse describes their continuing condition.

The Verb’s Intransitivity

Tāha is intransitive in this usage. The people do not cause a person or object to wander; they themselves are in the state of tayh.

Arabic may use a form such as tayyahahū to express causation, but that form is not in the verse.

“He causes them to wander in the earth” therefore inserts an agent into the direct construction of yatīhūna, even though the overall consequence is expressed within the divine answer.

Grammatical and Structural Notes

The Position of أَرْبَعِينَ سَنَةً

In fa-innahā muḥarramatun ʿalayhim arbaʿīna sanatan yatīhūna fī al-arḍ, two principal syntactic attachments have been proposed.

Arbaʿīna sanatan may state the duration for which the land is forbidden: “that land is forbidden to them for forty years.”

Alternatively, it may state the duration of their wandering: “for forty years they wander in the earth.”

In the overall meaning, both readings join prohibition from entry and forty years of wandering within one period. Translation can preserve the construction without claiming more certainty than the syntax permits:

Then that land is forbidden to them; for forty years they will wander in the earth.

This attaches the forty years more openly to wandering. Another rendering is:

Then that land is forbidden to them for forty years, and they will wander in the earth.

This relates the duration more directly to the prohibition.

The Preposition فِي

In fī al-arḍ, fī identifies the place and space in which tayh occurs: they wander in the earth.

“On the earth” may convey physical location, but “in the land” better preserves the sense of being within the space of wandering.

The فَاء in فَلَا تَأْسَ

The fā’ in fa-lā ta’sa ʿalā al-qawmi al-fāsiqīn connects this command with the preceding judgment. After the prohibition and wandering are announced, Moses is told not to grieve for the defiantly disobedient people.

This clause does not define T Y H, but it places tayh within the consequence of the people’s conduct.

The Specified Land and الْأَرْض

The preceding verses speak of al-arḍa al-muqaddasata, “the holy land,” but the tayh verse uses the more general fī al-arḍ, “in the earth/land.”

They are prevented from entering one specified land and wander in land more generally. This change in expression strengthens the contrast between a defined destination and a directionless space.

Comparison with Related Roots

T Y H and Ḍ L L: Tayh and Going Astray

Ḍ L L covers losing the way, departing from a path, disappearance of a trace, and intellectual or ethical error.

T Y H presents the more specific image of bewildered movement through land or space without clear direction.

Every tayh includes a kind of losing the way, but not every ḍalāl is tayh. A person may be intellectually or ethically astray without wandering spatially. In Al-Ma’idah 5:26, “they will wander” is more precise than “they will be misguided.”

T Y H and Ḥ Y R: Tayh and Bewilderment

Ḥ Y R denotes bewilderment, inability to decide, and the mind or gaze moving back and forth among alternatives.

Bewilderment can be inward and mental even when the person does not move. Tayh in its primary usage connects bewilderment to movement and place: the person is confused and moves in that state.

Bewilderment is therefore one element of tayh, while tayh has a clearer spatial and kinetic image.

T Y H and ʿ M Y: Tayh and Blindness

ʿ M Y denotes physical blindness and, in other usages, failure to see a sign or truth.

A blind person may lose the way, but tayh does not mean blindness. A wandering person may see the surroundings yet fail to identify direction and a way out.

Blindness concerns inability to see; tayh concerns inability to orient oneself and leave the disorienting space.

T Y H and ʿ M H: Tayh and ʿAmah

The Qur’an uses ʿ M H for blind wandering in transgression:

فِي طُغْيَانِهِمْ يَعْمَهُونَ

“They remain wandering blindly in their transgression.”

ʿAmah occurs more in the field of blindness of insight, bewilderment, and aimless movement in transgression or ethical error.

Tayh in Al-Ma’idah 5:26 is spatial wandering in the earth. The roots approach one another in the image of directionless movement, but their Qur’anic contexts differ.

T Y H and T R D D: Tayh and Going Back and Forth

Taraddud denotes repeated movement back and forth and failure to settle upon a decision or route.

A wandering person may go back and forth, but tayh is not restricted to movement between two points. One may continue forward and still fail to find the right direction.

Taraddud highlights the shape of movement; tayh highlights absence of a way and destination.

T Y H and T B ʿ: Tayh and Following

T B ʿ denotes moving behind a person, trace, or preceding route. In following, something ahead determines the direction of movement.

In tayh, such a reliable leader or sign is absent or inaccessible. Movement continues, but nothing orders it into an effective course.

Following guidance therefore offers a useful contrast to tayh: one is movement along a guide, the other movement without finding a way. This is explanatory, not a complete dictionary opposition between the roots.

T Y H and H D Y: Tayh and Guidance

H D Y denotes showing a way, bringing to a path, and giving direction.

Tayh is the condition of one who does not find the way and whose movement does not reach the destination. Guidance clarifies the path and direction of movement.

Tayh and guidance do not occur as explicit opposites in one Qur’anic verse. Their relation is inferred from their semantic fields, not from direct lexical coordination.

T Y H and S B L: Tayh and a Way

S B L concerns a road or traversable way.

Tayh occurs when a person moves through the land without recognizing a clear path to the destination.

The existence of a way is not enough; the person must recognize and travel it. Conversely, tayh does not necessarily mean that no path exists in reality. A path may exist while the person fails to find it.

Translation Suggestions for Forqan

تَاهَ — tāha

According to lexical context: wandered, lost the way, moved bewildered through the land, or, in another usage, acted arrogantly.

“Acted arrogantly” is suitable only where context confirms that semantic branch.

يَتِيهُ — yatīhu

He wanders, moves bewildered, or cannot find his way.

تَيْه or تِيه — tayh or tīh

Wandering, being lost, bewildered movement, or land in which a person loses the way.

“Desert” is suitable only in a spatial context and should not be the fixed equivalent.

تَائِه — tā’ih

Wandering, lost, bewildered on the way, or, in a different context, arrogant.

يَتِيهُونَ فِي الْأَرْضِ

Primary suggestion: They will wander in the earth.

Closer to the element of movement: They will move wandering through the land.

Explanatory: They will roam through the land without finding their way or destination.

The third is suitable for explanation, not direct translation, because “their destination” is not explicit in the wording.

A Complete Translation of Al-Ma’idah 5:26

First suggestion:

He said: then that land is forbidden to them for forty years; they will wander in the earth. So do not grieve over the defiantly disobedient people.

Second suggestion:

He said: then that land is forbidden to them; for forty years they will wander in the earth. So do not grieve over the defiantly disobedient people.

The second places the forty years closer to the verb of wandering. The first relates the duration more closely to the prohibition. The final choice should follow Forqan’s syntactic decision for the verse.

The following are unsuitable:

“They will become misguided in the earth,” because it lets doctrinal meaning dominate the spatial meaning.

“They will remain in the desert,” because it reduces movement and wandering and adds “desert” to the text.

“They will be in the Desert of Tīh,” because it turns the word into an unexplained geographical proper name.

Summary

The root T Y H occurs only once in the Qur’an, in the form yatīhūna. Its core is wandering movement accompanied by bewilderment, loss of direction, and inability to find a way out or reach a destination.

A person in tayh is not necessarily motionless. The person may move extensively, but without clear direction and an effective path, movement does not become arrival. This feature distinguishes tayh both from stillness and from purely mental error.

In Al-Ma’idah 5:26, tayh is the consequence for a people who refuse to enter the appointed land. They say, “we are sitting right here,” but their sitting does not result in ease and stability; they wander in the earth. They refuse purposeful movement and become caught in movement without destination.

Lexicons record two main branches for this root: wandering and losing the way, and arrogance or self-magnification. In the Qur’anic use, the first meaning is clear and direct. Arrogance may historically have been related to departure from balance or figurative wandering, but it must not enter the translation of yatīhūna fī al-arḍ.

T Y H relates semantically to roots such as Ḍ L L, Ḥ Y R, ʿ M Y, ʿ M H, H D Y, T B ʿ, and S B L, but its distinctive feature is the image of a person moving through the land without finding an effective way.
