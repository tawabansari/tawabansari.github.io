Qur’anic Root B ʿ L

Husband and life companion, owner and lord, and the name of a deity invoked instead of The God

The Arabic root ب ع ل has several semantic branches. The most important concern marriage, ownership and lordship, bewilderment and confusion, and land or plants that grow without ordinary irrigation. Their relationship is not immediately clear, and shared consonants alone should not force them into one image.

The Qur’an uses بَعْل in two entirely different settings. In most occurrences, it means a husband: a man in a marital relationship with a woman. In Al-Ṣāffāt, however, the same linguistic form denotes a deity invoked by Elijah’s people.

This difference matters. Baʿl may be a human title or the name or title of a deity, but that overlap does not make their positions or natures identical. The husband sense should not be transferred to the deity passage, nor should ownership or divine authority be imposed on every marital use.

The root occurs seven times in five Qur’anic verses: six uses concern husbands and one belongs to Elijah’s account. The forms are وَبُعُولَتُهُنَّ, بَعْلِهَا, بَعْلِي, لِبُعُولَتِهِنَّ, two instances of بُعُولَتِهِنَّ, and بَعْلًا.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: six husband uses and one proper name

بَعْل is singular and بُعُولَة its plural. Most differences among their verse forms arise from attached pronouns, prepositions, and grammatical position, rather than multiple derivatives with independent semantic cores.

1. The root’s semantic branches in early Arabic

Ibn Fāris identifies three separate principles for ب ع ل in Maqāyīs al-Lughah.

The first is الصَّاحِب, companion or possessor. He places both husband and the naming of certain false deities within this branch.

The second concerns bewilderment and astonishment: a person becomes confused and does not know how to act.

The third concerns elevated ground and land or plants whose water supply differs from ordinarily irrigated cultivation.

This division shows that early lexical analysis itself did not reduce every use to one established principle.

Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs al-Lughah: the three principles of baʿl

In Al-Mufradāt, al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī attempts a broader semantic connection. Beginning with the husband’s position in the social structure he describes, he uses superiority and lordship to explain other applications.

Under such an analysis, baʿl can be someone superior to, or exercising authority over, another. This image is then connected with a deity’s title, elevated land, and an established palm tree.

Two matters must nevertheless be distinguished: the actual existence of these linguistic uses and a proposed explanation of how they developed historically.

A dictionary’s ability to construct a shared image does not itself establish that all the meanings actually arose from that image.

Al-Rāghib, Al-Mufradāt: analysis connecting the meanings

Each branch should therefore first be studied through its own evidence. Connections can then be discussed where their semantic relationship is clear and defensible.

2. Baʿl in marriage

بَعْل is an established early Arabic name for a husband, and بُعُولَة is one of its plurals.

Al-Jawharī, Ibn Manẓūr, and other lexicographers record this meaning. Al-Rāghib likewise identifies baʿl as the male partner in a marriage.

In some verbal constructions, بَعَلَ means becoming a husband or marrying. Recorded uses of اسْتَبْعَلَ likewise concern taking a wife or entering the position of husband.

بَاعَلَ and مُبَاعَلَة concern marital companionship and intimacy. Early sources also use تَبَعَّلَتِ الْمَرْأَةُ for a woman’s conduct in marriage, including affectionate companionship and seeking her husband’s approval.

The family thus extends beyond a nominal title to derivatives concerning marriage itself and conduct within it.

Some dictionaries also record baʿl for a woman and بَعْلَة for a female spouse. In all six Qur’anic occurrences relating to marriage, however, the word refers to a husband.

This distinction should remain: a word’s linguistic range can be broader than its attested Qur’anic uses.

A witness from Arabic poetry

Ibn Fāris quotes a line by al-Ḥuṭayʾah when explaining baʿl:

وَكَمْ مِنْ حَصَانٍ ذَاتِ بَعْلٍ تَرَكْتَهَا
إِذَا اللَّيْلُ أَدْجَى لَمْ تَجِدْ مَنْ تُبَاعِلُهْ

wa-kam min ḥaṣānin dhāti baʿlin taraktahā / idhā al-laylu adjā lam tajid man tubāʿiluh

Translation: “How many a chaste, married woman you left, who, when night grew dark, found no one with whom to share marital intimacy.”

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: the al-Ḥuṭayʾah witness

ذَاتِ بَعْلٍ denotes a woman with a husband. تُبَاعِلُهْ belongs to the same family and concerns marital intimacy.

The witness thus places two related uses together: baʿl as husband and mubāʿalah as conduct within marriage.

Al-Ḥuṭayʾah was a mukhaḍram poet, living across the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods. The line itself does not establish that it was composed before the Qur’an’s revelation. It is an early Arabic witness without a proven pre-Qur’anic date.

3. Baʿl as possessor, lord, and owner

Dictionaries do not restrict baʿl to husband. Expressions such as بَعْلُ الشَّيْءِ denote something’s possessor, owner, or overseer.

Some Arabic accounts, for example, have a person call himself an animal’s baʿl, meaning its owner.

The word can thus apply outside marriage to a person’s relationship with something in their possession or control.

This does not mean that baʿl necessarily means owner in every sentence.

One word can describe different relationships and, over time, acquire a particular meaning within each.

Baʿl meaning husband is an established family title. Even if historically associated in some settings with male authority and lordship, its direct meaning in هَٰذَا بَعْلِي remains “This is my husband.”

Rendering it “This is my owner” introduces a relationship not established by the sentence itself.

Where baʿl concerns an animal’s or object’s owner, however, “owner” or “possessor” is appropriate, since its referent and context establish that relationship.

This distinction is fundamental to root study: historical overlap between uses does not authorize replacing one with another in every sentence.

4. Baʿl in land and agriculture

Another important branch concerns land and plants that grow without direct, ordinary irrigation.

Some sources use baʿl for high ground that flowing water does not reach and that receives only limited rainfall.

Agricultural uses also apply it to palms or crops growing through moisture in the ground without direct irrigation.

The two uses are close but not identical.

High ground with little water

Al-ʿAyn and Lisān al-ʿArab describe baʿl as elevated land receiving little rain.

A line attributed to Salāmah ibn Jandal is quoted to illustrate this meaning:

إِذَا مَا عَلَوْنَا ظَهْرَ بَعْلٍ عَرِيضَةٍ
تَخَالُ عَلَيْهَا قَيْضَ بَيْضٍ مُفَلَّقِ

idhā mā ʿalawnā ẓahra baʿlin ʿarīḍatin / takhālu ʿalayhā qayḍa bayḍin mufallaqi

Translation: “Whenever we climbed onto a broad stretch of high ground, you would imagine broken eggshells upon it.”

Here, baʿl describes land by elevation and the condition of its surface.

The witness’s wording is disputed, however. Accounts of the poet’s dīwān report another form in place of baʿl. The line should therefore not be treated without qualification as definitive evidence of pre-Qur’anic baʿl.

Al-ʿAyn: the high-ground witness and the nashz variant

The agricultural meaning is clearly recorded in dictionaries, but establishing this particular form in this poem requires closer examination of textual witnesses.

Palms and crops without direct irrigation

In another use, baʿl denotes a palm or plant that does not require direct irrigation to grow.

Two conditions should be distinguished here.

One is a crop or plant supplied by natural rainfall, growing without artificial irrigation.

The other is a tree whose roots reach moisture or groundwater and obtain its necessary water through them.

In a lexical explanation, Abū ʿAmr treats baʿl and عِذْي as close and relates both to natural rainfall.

Al-Aṣmaʿī distinguishes them: ʿidhy is watered by rain, whereas baʿl reaches water in the ground through its roots and does not need ordinary irrigation.

Al-Azharī further explains this distinction through palm roots in the soil.

The agricultural uses therefore cannot all be restricted either to rainfed land or to trees supplied by groundwater.

Their shared characteristic is independence from direct, ordinary irrigation, while water source and soil conditions differ among lexical accounts.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab: differing accounts of baʿl and ʿidhy

اسْتَبْعَلَ also describes land or palms reaching such a condition.

Modern Arabic uses زراعة بعلية for rainfed cultivation. This modern term should not erase the distinctions recorded in early Arabic.

This branch does not occur in the Qur’an.

5. Baʿl as bewilderment and confusion

Another branch concerns someone facing a situation with bewilderment, fear, or confusion, not knowing what to do.

بَعِلَ, with kasrah on the middle radical, is recorded in this sense.

Al-ʿAyn illustrates it through someone confronting warfare or danger who becomes so bewildered that they lose the ability to decide and act deliberately.

A poem attributed to Aʿshā Hamdān says:

فَجَاهَدَ فِي فُرْسَانِهِ وَرِجَالِهِ
وَنَاهَضَ لَمْ يَبْعَلْ وَلَمْ يَتَهَيَّبِ

fa-jāhada fī fursānihi wa-rijālihi / wa-nāhaḍa lam yabʿal wa-lam yatahayyabi

The line describes someone who fought among his horsemen and men and rose to the encounter, without becoming bewildered or held back by fear.

Al-ʿAyn: the Aʿshā Hamdān witness with past-tense verbs

لَمْ يَبْعَلْ here concerns bewilderment and disorientation, rather than becoming a husband or owner.

This witness likewise should not be introduced uncritically as pre-Islamic, since Aʿshā Hamdān belongs to the Islamic period.

Lexical sources use بَعِلَ بِأَمْرِهِ for someone confused about an affair and uncertain how to act.

Some early uses also apply امرأة بَعِلَة to a woman who does not know how to dress or arrange her clothing skillfully.

These uses relate semantically to confusion and inability to put affairs in order. They should not, however, lead to defining Qur’anic baʿl as bewilderment or disorientation.

None of the seven Qur’anic occurrences concerns this branch.

6. Husband in Abraham’s account: Hūd 11:72

Verse text:

قَالَتْ يَا وَيْلَتَىٰ أَأَلِدُ وَأَنَا عَجُوزٌ وَهَٰذَا بَعْلِي شَيْخًا ۖ إِنَّ هَٰذَا لَشَيْءٌ عَجِيبٌ

qālat yā waylatā a-alidu wa-anā ʿajūzun wa-hādhā baʿlī shaykhan inna hādhā la-shayʾun ʿajībun

Translation:

“She said, ‘Alas for me! Shall I bear a child when I am an old woman and this husband of mine is an old man? This is indeed an astonishing thing.’”

Hūd 11:72

This is one of the clearest instances of baʿl meaning husband.

Faced with news of a child, Abraham’s wife speaks of her own and her husband’s advanced age.

بَعْلِي consists of the noun بَعْل and attached pronoun ي, meaning “my husband.”

Ownership, lordship, and a deity’s name are not the subject here. The sentence concerns the familiar marital relationship between an elderly woman and man.

شَيْخًا clarifies the husband’s condition. In the standard grammatical analysis, it is an accusative circumstantial expression.

The sentence thus places two facts together: the woman sees herself as old, and her husband is also old. Her astonishment concerns news of childbearing under these conditions.

The use also matters for distinguishing baʿl from zawj. It does not establish that baʿl is merely a legal or social title for a man without a real marital relationship with the woman.

The woman discusses shared childbearing with her husband. Baʿl here therefore denotes a husband in an actual marital relationship.

There is no contextual indication for translating it as owner, legal guardian, or a man whose conjugal relationship has ended.

7. Baʿl in divorce and return to shared life

Al-Baqarah 2:228

Verse text:

وَالْمُطَلَّقَاتُ يَتَرَبَّصْنَ بِأَنْفُسِهِنَّ ثَلَاثَةَ قُرُوءٍ ۚ وَلَا يَحِلُّ لَهُنَّ أَنْ يَكْتُمْنَ مَا خَلَقَ اللَّهُ فِي أَرْحَامِهِنَّ إِنْ كُنَّ يُؤْمِنَّ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ ۚ وَبُعُولَتُهُنَّ أَحَقُّ بِرَدِّهِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ إِنْ أَرَادُوا إِصْلَاحًا ۚ وَلَهُنَّ مِثْلُ الَّذِي عَلَيْهِنَّ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ ۚ وَلِلرِّجَالِ عَلَيْهِنَّ دَرَجَةٌ ۗ وَاللَّهُ عَزِيزٌ حَكِيمٌ

wa-al-muṭallaqātu yatarabbaṣna bi-anfusihinna thalāthata qurūʾin wa-lā yaḥillu lahunna an yaktumna mā khalaqa Allāhu fī arḥāmihinna in kunna yuʾminna bi-Allāhi wa-al-yawmi al-ākhiri wa-buʿūlatuhunna aḥaqqu bi-raddihinna fī dhālika in arādū iṣlāḥan wa-lahunna mithlu alladhī ʿalayhinna bi-al-maʿrūfi wa-li-al-rijāli ʿalayhinna darajatun wa-Allāhu ʿazīzun ḥakīmun

Translation:

“Divorced women shall wait for three periods of qurūʾ, and it is not lawful for them to conceal what The God has created in their wombs, if they believe in The God and the Last Day. Their husbands have greater entitlement to take them back during that time, if they seek reconciliation. Women have rights corresponding to their responsibilities, according to what is recognized as right; and men have a degree over them. The God is Mighty and Wise.”

Al-Baqarah 2:228

بُعُولَتُهُنَّ combines the plural of baʿl with هُنَّ and refers to the husbands of the women under discussion.

The important point is its setting: divorce and the possibility of returning to marriage.

This shows that baʿl may also describe a man whose relationship with the woman is at a stage of separation and resolution.

It does not make “separated man” or “former husband” the word’s primary meaning. The divorce context supplies that particular situation.

The verse describes husbands as having greater entitlement to take the women back under specified conditions, accompanied by فِي ذَٰلِكَ and the qualification إِنْ أَرَادُوا إِصْلَاحًا.

The title buʿūlatuhunna alone therefore does not determine the verse’s entire legal ruling.

The word itself does not establish male ownership of women, unlimited discretion in taking them back, or disregard for women’s wishes and rights.

The same verse mentions reciprocal rights and responsibilities after discussing return:

«وَلَهُنَّ مِثْلُ الَّذِي عَلَيْهِنَّ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ»

wa-lahunna mithlu alladhī ʿalayhinna bi-al-maʿrūfi

Translation: “Women have rights corresponding to their responsibilities, according to what is recognized as right.”

This expression matters for the verse’s legal analysis, but it does not turn baʿl’s lexical meaning into a complete ruling on the structure of marriage.

For root study, the conclusion is clear: baʿl means husband, and the verse’s other expressions specify his particular legal situation.

8. Baʿl and conflict within marriage

Al-Nisāʾ 4:128

Verse text:

وَإِنِ امْرَأَةٌ خَافَتْ مِنْ بَعْلِهَا نُشُوزًا أَوْ إِعْرَاضًا فَلَا جُنَاحَ عَلَيْهِمَا أَنْ يُصْلِحَا بَيْنَهُمَا صُلْحًا ۚ وَالصُّلْحُ خَيْرٌ ۗ وَأُحْضِرَتِ الْأَنْفُسُ الشُّحَّ ۚ وَإِنْ تُحْسِنُوا وَتَتَّقُوا فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ كَانَ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ خَبِيرًا

wa-ini imraʾatun khāfat min baʿlihā nushūzan aw iʿrāḍan fa-lā junāḥa ʿalayhimā an yuṣliḥā baynahumā ṣulḥan wa-al-ṣulḥu khayrun wa-uḥḍirati al-anfusu al-shuḥḥa wa-in tuḥsinū wa-tattaqū fa-inna Allāha kāna bi-mā taʿmalūna khabīran

Translation:

“If a woman fears estrangement or avoidance on her husband’s part, there is no blame on the two if they reach a reconciliation between themselves; reconciliation is better. Souls are attended by avarice. If you do good and remain mindful, The God is fully aware of what you do.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:128

بَعْلِهَا means “her husband.”

The subject is a woman who fears her husband’s conduct. نُشُوزًا and إِعْرَاضًا specify what concerns her.

Neither behavior forms part of baʿl’s lexical definition.

Baʿl remains the marital title, while nushūz and iʿrāḍ are behaviors that may occur within the relationship.

This matters because some proposed distinctions from zawj restrict baʿl to a relationship that no longer includes full marriage or actual companionship.

Hūd 11:72 does not support that restriction: baʿlī occurs in a childbearing context. Here, baʿlihā describes a husband amid conflict and apprehension.

The word is present in both conditions, so neither can become its permanent definition.

Al-Nisāʾ also speaks of both parties repairing their relationship. عَلَيْهِمَا and بَيْنَهُمَا refer to woman and husband, making reconciliation an affair of both within the sentence itself.

Calling the husband baʿl therefore does not mean the woman has no standing or rights in the relationship.

The word names the husband. The full sentence determines conduct, responsibility, and the means of reconciliation.

9. Buʿūlah in family relationships: Al-Nūr 24:31

Verse text:

وَقُلْ لِلْمُؤْمِنَاتِ يَغْضُضْنَ مِنْ أَبْصَارِهِنَّ وَيَحْفَظْنَ فُرُوجَهُنَّ وَلَا يُبْدِينَ زِينَتَهُنَّ إِلَّا مَا ظَهَرَ مِنْهَا ۖ وَلْيَضْرِبْنَ بِخُمُرِهِنَّ عَلَىٰ جُيُوبِهِنَّ ۖ وَلَا يُبْدِينَ زِينَتَهُنَّ إِلَّا لِبُعُولَتِهِنَّ أَوْ آبَائِهِنَّ أَوْ آبَاءِ بُعُولَتِهِنَّ أَوْ أَبْنَائِهِنَّ أَوْ أَبْنَاءِ بُعُولَتِهِنَّ أَوْ إِخْوَانِهِنَّ أَوْ بَنِي إِخْوَانِهِنَّ أَوْ بَنِي أَخَوَاتِهِنَّ أَوْ نِسَائِهِنَّ أَوْ مَا مَلَكَتْ أَيْمَانُهُنَّ أَوِ التَّابِعِينَ غَيْرِ أُولِي الْإِرْبَةِ مِنَ الرِّجَالِ أَوِ الطِّفْلِ الَّذِينَ لَمْ يَظْهَرُوا عَلَىٰ عَوْرَاتِ النِّسَاءِ ۖ وَلَا يَضْرِبْنَ بِأَرْجُلِهِنَّ لِيُعْلَمَ مَا يُخْفِينَ مِنْ زِينَتِهِنَّ ۚ وَتُوبُوا إِلَى اللَّهِ جَمِيعًا أَيُّهَ الْمُؤْمِنُونَ لَعَلَّكُمْ تُفْلِحُونَ

wa-qul li-al-muʾmināti yaghḍuḍna min abṣārihinna wa-yaḥfaẓna furūjahunna wa-lā yubdīna zīnatahunna illā mā ẓahara minhā wa-l-yaḍribna bi-khumurihinna ʿalā juyūbihinna wa-lā yubdīna zīnatahunna illā li-buʿūlatihinna aw ābāʾihinna aw ābāʾi buʿūlatihinna aw abnāʾihinna aw abnāʾi buʿūlatihinna aw ikhwānihinna aw banī ikhwānihinna aw banī akhawātihinna aw nisāʾihinna aw mā malakat aymānuhunna awi al-tābiʿīna ghayri ulī al-irbati mina al-rijāli awi al-ṭifli alladhīna lam yaẓharū ʿalā ʿawrāti al-nisāʾi wa-lā yaḍribna bi-arjulihinna li-yuʿlama mā yukhfīna min zīnatihinna wa-tūbū ilā Allāhi jamīʿan ayyuha al-muʾminūna laʿallakum tufliḥūna

Translation:

“Tell believing women to lower their gaze and guard their private parts, and not display their adornment except what is apparent of it; let them draw their coverings over their neck openings. Let them not display their adornment except to their husbands, their fathers, their husbands’ fathers, their sons, their husbands’ sons, their brothers, their brothers’ sons, their sisters’ sons, their women, those their right hands possess, male attendants without desire, or children not yet aware of women’s intimate parts. Let them not strike their feet so that their hidden adornment becomes known. Turn together to The God, believers, so that you may prosper.”

Al-Nūr 24:31

Buʿūlah occurs three times here.

First, لِبُعُولَتِهِنَّ refers to the women’s own husbands.

Second, آبَاءِ بُعُولَتِهِنَّ identifies their husbands’ fathers.

Third, أَبْنَاءِ بُعُولَتِهِنَّ identifies their husbands’ sons.

All three use the same noun and basic meaning, but their sentence roles differ.

In the first expression, the husband himself is one of the persons named.

In the second, the family relationship passes through the husband to his father.

In the third, it passes through the husband to his son.

The three groups should therefore be distinguished: husband, husband’s father, and husband’s son.

This distinction is essential to the verse’s network of family relationships, while buʿūlah itself means husbands in all three.

Morphologically, بُعُولَة is the plural of بَعْل. هِنَّ relates it to the women addressed. The لِـ in لِبُعُولَتِهِنَّ is a preposition specifying the word’s grammatical relationship, rather than part of its root.

Baʿl as husband is thus not restricted to divorce or family conflict. It also names ordinary, established family relationships.

The broader questions of adornment, covering, and the persons listed belong to an independent study of the verse. The root study focuses on correctly identifying the husband title and the family relationships arising through it.

10. Baʿl in Elijah’s account

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:125

Verse text:

أَتَدْعُونَ بَعْلًا وَتَذَرُونَ أَحْسَنَ الْخَالِقِينَ

a-tadʿūna baʿlan wa-tadharūna aḥsana al-khāliqīna

Translation:

“Do you invoke Baal and leave the best of creators?”

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:125

Elijah addresses these words to his people.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:123 identifies Elijah among the messengers. In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:124, he asks his people why they are not mindful. Al-Ṣāffāt 37:125 then challenges them about invoking Baal instead of The God.

بَعْلًا no longer means husband here. It names a deity people invoked and placed in a position that should belong to The God alone.

Two lexical and historical possibilities have been proposed for this use.

First, Baal is the proper name of a deity familiar among the people.

Second, baʿl means lord or possessor and was applied religiously to a deity other than The God.

The possibilities need not conflict. A word meaning lord or possessor can become established as a deity’s title or name within a religious tradition.

Baal in the region’s ancient languages

In Northwest Semitic languages, forms cognate with baʿl are known with meanings such as lord, possessor, or ruler.

In Ugaritic texts, the title Baal concerns a deity associated with rain, storms, and the fertility of the land. In many such texts, Baal is connected with Hadad, one of the region’s established deities.

Musée du Louvre: Baal, Ugarit, and rain

These witnesses matter for the name’s historical background. They show that Baal was more than a word in later Arabic dictionaries: it also had a history in ancient regional religious traditions.

Metropolitan Museum research: Baal and Hadad in regional traditions

This does not establish that every detail of Ugaritic rites belongs to precisely the same deity named in the Qur’anic account of Elijah.

Ancient traditions could use Baal for differing local deities or manifestations of a deity. Shared naming alone does not establish complete historical identity.

Was Baal the deity of Baalbek?

Some historical and interpretive reports associate Baal with a deity worshipped around Baalbek.

The report matters for the history of interpretation, but the Qur’an itself names no city, temple form, statue material, or exact location of worship.

Similarity between Baal and Baalbek therefore cannot alone turn the city’s entire history into part of the verse’s meaning.

Interpretive sources also differ on whether Baal was a particular idol’s name, a deity’s title, or another name.

Al-Ṭabarī: accounts of the name, title, and Baalbek

The clear common point is the verse’s subject: a people invoked something or someone other than The God as a deity.

Baal, lordship, and treating something as a deity

The next verse, Al-Ṣāffāt 37:126, is particularly important:

«اللَّهَ رَبَّكُمْ وَرَبَّ آبَائِكُمُ الْأَوَّلِينَ»

Allāha rabbakum wa-rabba ābāʾikumu al-awwalīna

Translation: “The God, your Lord and the Lord of your earlier forefathers.”

It identifies The God as the Lord of the audience and their earlier forefathers.

The opposition between Baal in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:125 and رَبَّكُمْ in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:126 shows that more than a statue’s name is at issue: it concerns a position people attributed to it.

If Baal was their deity’s name, Elijah criticizes invoking it instead of The God.

If the word began as a title of lordship and possession before becoming a divine name, the same contrast remains: something is invoked in a position that truly belongs to The God.

Neither case permits translating baʿl here as husband. Nor can a concept of family ownership be transferred from the husband use to this religious use without examination.

The direct contextual meaning is Baal as the name or title of the deity invoked.

11. Distinguishing related words

ب ع ل (baʿl) and ز و ج (zawj)

Study of Z W J

Zawj denotes one member of a pair or related set of two. The Qur’an applies it to wives and husbands and to other pairs and kinds.

By comparison, baʿl names the husband in the six Qur’anic marital occurrences.

The difference is clear: zawj has a broader semantic field, extending beyond husband, whereas baʿl in these passages identifies the male marital partner.

This does not mean zawj necessarily describes a complete, equal, sexually intimate relationship while baʿl names only a man with no actual marriage relationship.

Such a boundary does not fit Qur’anic usage.

In Hūd 11:72, Abraham’s wife uses baʿlī in connection with childbearing.

Al-Baqarah 2:228 uses buʿūlatuhunna in divorce and the possibility of return.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:128 uses baʿlihā for a husband amid conflict and fear of turning away.

Al-Nūr 24:31 uses buʿūlatihinna as the ordinary husband title in family relationships.

Baʿl thus occurs in differing marital conditions, none of which should become its fixed meaning.

The principal Qur’anic distinction is that baʿl directly names a husband, whereas zawj emphasizes the pair relationship and has a scope wider than marriage.

ب ع ل (baʿl) and ر ب ب (rabb)

Study of R B B

Rabb concerns nurturing, directing, overseeing, and possessing authority.

Some lexical uses of baʿl likewise denote a possessor or lord, creating an overlap between the families.

Rabb and baʿl are nevertheless not fully synonymous.

Baʿl frequently means husband in the Qur’an, where rabb is not its ordinary replacement.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:125 and Al-Ṣāffāt 37:126, however, the fields enter a religious opposition: the Baal invoked stands against The God, the true Lord.

The contrast matters for Baal’s religious position without making the roots’ entire semantic ranges identical.

ب ع ل (baʿl) and م ل ك (milk)

Study of M L K

Mulk and related forms concern ownership, power, or rule, with meanings varying by formation.

Some lexical uses call a possessor or owner baʿl. In the husband sense, however, it is a family title.

Calling a husband baʿl does not itself establish that his wife is linguistically his property.

Marriage should be studied independently through the full range of verses concerning rights, responsibility, consent, reconciliation, and separation.

Nor does calling a being Baal as a deity mean it truly owns the world. Al-Ṣāffāt critiques that very position.

ب ع ل (baʿl) and س و د (sayyid)

Quranic Arabic Corpus: S W D as the root of sayyid

Study of S W D

Sayyid denotes a lord or someone of superior social standing and appears in differing Qur’anic settings.

Yūsuf 12:25 uses سَيِّدَهَا for the person who is the woman’s husband in the story’s context.

The same person can therefore be identified through a title other than baʿl or zawj.

This overlap does not make sayyid, baʿl, and zawj identical in every sentence.

Each emphasizes an aspect of a relationship or social position, with its precise meaning established by context.

12. Translation proposals for Forqan

The chief principle is to avoid imposing one equivalent on every branch.

For marital بَعْل, “husband” is clear and natural.

For بُعُولَة, “husbands” is appropriate. Attached pronouns should retain their referents.

In Al-Baqarah 2:228, وَبُعُولَتُهُنَّ means “and their husbands.”

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:128, بَعْلِهَا means “her husband.”

In Hūd 11:72, بَعْلِي means “my husband.”

In Al-Nūr 24:31, لِبُعُولَتِهِنَّ means “to their husbands,” آبَاءِ بُعُولَتِهِنَّ “their husbands’ fathers,” and أَبْنَاءِ بُعُولَتِهِنَّ “their husbands’ sons.”

For Al-Ṣāffāt 37:125, the best basic rendering preserves the name Baal:

“Do you invoke Baal and leave the best of creators?”

An explanation may add that Baal was the name or title of the deity invoked by Elijah’s people.

Replacing Baal simply with “idol” removes a name the Qur’an explicitly includes. Translating it as “lord” without explanation can also obscure the distinction between a false deity and the true Lord.

For non-Qur’anic uses, possessor, owner, or lord may fit the sentence.

In agriculture, rainfed land, cultivation without direct irrigation, or a palm supplied by ground moisture should be selected according to the particular use.

For the bewilderment branch, suitable equivalents include “became bewildered,” “was disoriented,” or “remained confused about an affair.”

The range shows a branched family that cannot be reduced in one short phrase to ownership, authority, or marriage alone.

13. Conclusion

The Arabic root ب ع ل has several semantic branches. Ibn Fāris distinguishes companionship and possession, bewilderment and confusion, and land or plants with a particular water supply.

Within human relationships, baʿl names a husband. Early poetry and dictionaries record this use, which occurs six times in the Qur’an.

In the ownership and lordship branch, the word may denote someone with a particular relationship of possession or authority to something. This must not turn every husband use into ownership.

Agricultural baʿl concerns land or plants supplied with necessary water without ordinary direct irrigation. Lexical reports differ on whether the water comes from rainfall, ground moisture, or groundwater.

In another branch, بَعِلَ denotes bewilderment, confusion, or disorientation. This meaning does not occur in the Qur’an.

The Qur’anic uses fall into two principal settings.

First, marriage: Hūd places the husband in childbearing and old age; Al-Baqarah in divorce and possible return; Al-Nisāʾ in conflict and reconciliation; and Al-Nūr in family relationships.

Second, the name or title of a deity invoked in Elijah’s account. In Al-Ṣāffāt, Baal stands against The God, the true Lord.

Linguistic and historical overlap therefore does not necessarily mean identity in the positions of the persons or subjects involved.

Final definition in Qur’anic usage: بَعْل means husband within the family: the man in a marital relationship with a woman. In Elijah’s account, Baal is the name or title of a deity invoked instead of The God.

The wider Arabic family also records possessor and lord, land and cultivation without ordinary irrigation, and bewilderment and confusion. These branches should be understood through their own evidence, rather than imposing one on the rest to construct an apparently unified core.

The key translation point is that being a husband, being an owner, and being invoked as a deity are different relationships. Baʿl’s occurrence in all three fields does not permit directly transferring the meaning or rulings of one to another.
