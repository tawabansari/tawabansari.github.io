Arabic ح د ب is associated with something rising or protruding above its surroundings. This image appears in elevated ground, a protruding back, and raised forms of sand or water. A shared general image does not make all referents and usages identical.

Another branch uses حَدِبَ عَلَيْهِ and تَحَدَّبَ عَلَيْهِ for compassion and caring attention. Lexicographers connect this with bending toward another person; that imagery alone does not establish the meaning’s actual historical origin.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an: حَدَبٍ in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:96. Here it concerns elevated ground. Swiftness comes from يَنْسِلُونَ, rather than from the noun حَدَب itself.

1. Forms and derivatives of the root

ح د ب consists of ḥāʾ, dāl, and bāʾ and is a sound root. Arabic lexicons record the following forms; only حَدَبٍ occurs in the Qur’an.

  • حَدَب (ḥadab): elevated or rising ground; in another use, a protrusion of the back.
  • حِدَاب / أَحْدَاب (ḥidāb / aḥdāb): recorded plurals of ḥadab for elevations and rises.
  • حَدَبَة (ḥadabah): a particular protrusion of the back; also an elevation or rise in the ground.
  • حَدِبَ ـ يَحْدَبُ (ḥadiba–yaḥdabu): the back became curved or protruding; with ʿalā, showed compassion or caring concern for someone.
  • حَدِب (ḥadib): describing someone or something with a protrusion; in the appropriate context, compassionate and caring.
  • أَحْدَب (aḥdab): having a curved or protruding back, with the precise referent determined by context.
  • حَدْبَاء (ḥadbāʾ): feminine of aḥdab, describing a person, animal, or certain raised objects.
  • أَحْدَبَ (aḥdaba): in transitive use, caused a protrusion or curvature of someone’s back.
  • اِحْدَوْدَبَ (iḥdawdaba): a back or surface became curved and raised.
  • تَحَدَّبَ عَلَيْهِ (taḥaddaba ʿalayhi): showed compassion and caring attention toward someone.

حَدَبٍ in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:96 is an indefinite genitive singular noun within كُلِّ حَدَبٍ. The case ending and nunation do not create a separate derivative. The verbs, descriptions of the back, and compassionate expressions listed above do not occur in the Qur’an.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the sole occurrence of ḥadab, Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:96

2. Core meaning and usage in early Arabic

Elevation and protrusion in lexical explanation

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies ارْتِفَاعُ الشَّيْءِ, something’s rising, as the root’s foundation. He explains elevated ground and a protruding back through this image. This is a lexicographer’s analysis of the family, rather than independent evidence identifying its earliest historical use.

Al-Jawharī’s Al-Ṣiḥāḥ explains حَدَب through مَا ارْتَفَعَ مِنَ الْأَرْضِ: what rises from the ground, giving ḥidāb as its plural. This indicates relative elevation, without restricting the word to a mountain of a particular size or height.

Ibn Fāris and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: rising and elevated ground

A curved back and bodily protrusion

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab record حَدِبَ ظَهْرُهُ and اِحْدَوْدَبَ for a curved or protruding back, with أَحْدَب and حَدْبَاء as related descriptions. Here the words describe bodily shape without independently identifying a medical cause or manner of development.

Some uses of nāqah ḥadbāʾ describe a she-camel whose hip and back bones have become prominent, including through thinness. A description should therefore not be assigned one identical medical condition across people and animals, or transferred to elevated ground.

Raised sand and water

Al-Zamakhsharī’s Asās al-Balāghah uses حَدَبُ الرَّمْلِ for sand raised by the wind. The subject is not a body, but the raised form of the sand’s surface.

He also mentions حَدَبُ الْغَدِيرِ and rising floodwater, quoting this expression from Al-ʿAjjāj:

نَسَجَ الشَّمَالُ حَدَبَ الْغَدِيرِ

nasaja al-shamālu ḥadaba al-ghadīri

Meaning: “The north wind wove the rises of the pool’s water.” The image presents the water’s waves and raised forms as a texture produced by the wind. The dictionary quotation witnesses literary use, without establishing its precise date or the word’s first naming.

Al-Zamakhsharī, Asās al-Balāghah: raised sand and the expression attributed to Al-ʿAjjāj

Compassion and bending toward another

Lexicons explain حَدِبَ عَلَيْهِ and تَحَدَّبَ عَلَيْهِ through عَطَفَ عَلَيْهِ: showed compassion and turned toward someone with care. This use does not simply mean a back’s shape or elevated ground.

Ibn Fāris draws an image-based connection between this care and a compassionate person bending toward another. Al-Rāghib likewise proposes a possible derivational relationship between a protruding back and elevated ground. These accounts help explain semantic connections without conclusively establishing their historical direction.

Three settings should therefore remain distinct: rising ground, the raised shape of a body or surface, and compassion in the construction with ʿalayhi. Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:96 supports the first setting.

Lisān al-ʿArab, Maqāyīs, and Al-Rāghib: back, compassion, and caution in derivation

3. Qur’anic use of the root

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:96: coming forth from every rise in the ground

Arabic verse:

حَتَّىٰ إِذَا فُتِحَتْ يَأْجُوجُ وَمَأْجُوجُ وَهُمْ مِنْ كُلِّ حَدَبٍ يَنْسِلُونَ

ḥattā idhā futiḥat yaʾjūju wa-maʾjūju wa-hum min kulli ḥadabin yansilūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Until, when Gog and Magog are released, they come forth swiftly from every rise in the ground.”

حَدَبٍ here is an indefinite genitive singular noun.

كُلِّ حَدَبٍ is a genitive construction preceded by the preposition مِنْ.

Syntactically, مِنْ كُلِّ حَدَبٍ relates to يَنْسِلُونَ, indicating the starting point or direction of coming forth and advancing.

The expression thus concerns elevated or rising ground from which movement begins or over which it proceeds.

Is حَدَب the same as a mountain?

Lexicons record حَدَب for an elevated or raised part of the ground.

Explaining this verse, Al-Farrāʾ relates it to أَكَمَة and any elevated place.

أَكَمَة denotes a hill or rise in the ground.

Al-Ṭabarī likewise records sharaf, nashaz, and akamah in explaining مِنْ كُلِّ حَدَبٍ. These belong to the field of elevated and prominent ground.

They do not all identify precisely the same geographical shape or size.

جَبَل normally identifies a mountain, while حَدَب can include a smaller rise or hill.

“From every high mountain” therefore restricts the verse’s meaning too much.

“From every rise in the ground,” or “from every hill and elevation,” is closer to the word’s semantic range.

Conversely, حَدَب should not be restricted to a geometrical point or small curve. With مِنْ كُلِّ, its Qur’anic use creates a broad image of elevated and uneven ground.

Al-Ṭabarī and the citation of Al-Farrāʾ: sharaf, nashaz, and akamah

كُلِّ: comprehensiveness within the image

كُلِّ in مِنْ كُلِّ حَدَبٍ extends the statement’s scope.

The verse does not identify only one particular hill or elevation, but pictures movement from every rise.

The expression can convey movement’s breadth and abundance.

كُلِّ and حَدَبٍ nevertheless do not map the exact location of every group or route of every individual.

Nor does “from every rise” lexically mean “from every direction”: حَدَب denotes elevated ground, rather than geographical directions.

The resulting image can nevertheless evoke an advance from many places and directions.

The levels should remain distinct: the word directly means elevated ground, while the movement’s breadth comes from مِنْ كُلِّ and the sentence’s context.

The difference between حَدَبٍ and يَنْسِلُونَ

The Qur’anic expression places two distinct meanings together.

حَدَب denotes elevated or rising ground.

يَنْسِلُونَ belongs to ن س ل and here concerns coming forth and moving swiftly.

حَدَب itself therefore does not mean hastening or descending in a rush.

مِنْ كُلِّ حَدَبٍ alone would identify a place or starting point, without specifying the manner of movement.

يَنْسِلُونَ completes the image of swift movement.

Rendering حَدَب as “assault” or “a flood of people” is consequently imprecise.

The context may evoke a large advancing multitude, but that image comes from the whole sentence, rather than from ح د ب itself.

Does حَدَب here mean a protrusion of the human back?

As discussed above, Arabic also records حَدَب for a protrusion of the back.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:96, however, مِنْ كُلِّ حَدَبٍ accompanies a verb of movement.

Elevated ground is the clearer and more fitting contextual meaning.

Nothing in the verse indicates a human or animal back as the referent.

It should therefore not be translated as “from behind every person” or “from bodily humps.”

Shared protruding imagery does not make the referents identical.

حَدَب and Gog and Magog

The verse begins:

“حَتَّىٰ إِذَا فُتِحَتْ يَأْجُوجُ وَمَأْجُوجُ.”

It then says:

“وَهُمْ مِنْ كُلِّ حَدَبٍ يَنْسِلُونَ.”

In the more direct reading of the immediate context, هُمْ refers to Gog and Magog.

The swift movement from elevated ground is thus attributed to the groups named at the verse’s beginning.

Al-Kahf 18:94, Al-Kahf 18:95, Al-Kahf 18:96, Al-Kahf 18:97, Al-Kahf 18:98, and Al-Kahf 18:99 also discuss Gog and Magog in connection with corruption on earth, building a barrier, and its removal at the appointed time.

These verses provide important context for Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:96.

حَدَب itself, however, is not a people’s name and specifies neither Gog and Magog’s identity, number, language, home, nor history.

The word does not establish that they necessarily inhabit a particular mountainous region or will emerge through a particular passage in today’s world.

Such claims require independent examination of the Qur’anic text and historical and geographical evidence.

Does the verse concern people moving at the Resurrection?

Some early exegetical explanations also connect وَهُمْ مِنْ كُلِّ حَدَبٍ يَنْسِلُونَ with the general movement of humanity at the Resurrection.

This reading relates to the next verse, Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:97, which mentions the true promise drawing near.

Yā Sīn 36:51 also uses مِنَ الْأَجْدَاثِ إِلَىٰ رَبِّهِمْ يَنْسِلُونَ for people coming forth from graves.

The verses must nevertheless be distinguished.

In Yā Sīn 36:51, الْأَجْدَاث denotes graves and belongs to ج د ث.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:96, حَدَب concerns elevated ground and belongs to ح د ب.

The shared verb يَنْسِلُونَ does not make حَدَب and جَدَث identical.

The immediate context of Al-Anbiyāʾ more clearly supports the pronoun referring to Gog and Magog. The scene’s relationship with the true promise and Resurrection nevertheless belongs in a full study of the verses.

For root research, the firmer conclusion is that حَدَب means elevated or rising ground. Identifying every detail of the event and the moving groups goes beyond this word’s lexical meaning.

Al-Ṭabarī: alternative identifications of those moving, and yansilūn

Does مِنْ كُلِّ حَدَبٍ mean domination of the whole earth?

The expression presents a broad scene of emergence or advance.

It does not itself specify political domination, occupation of every territory, or the groups’ form of government.

حَدَب describes terrain and its elevations, rather than ownership or rule over it.

يَنْسِلُونَ likewise expresses movement, rather than its political or military outcome.

Discussion of Gog and Magog’s reach or the consequences of their movement must therefore use related verses and separate evidence.

Adding the name of a country, region, contemporary people, or historical theory to the translation of حَدَب has no lexical basis.

4. Differences from related roots

ح د ب (ḥadab) and ن ش ز (nashz): rising and elevated ground

Arabic نَشْز concerns elevation and a raised part of the ground.

حَدَب likewise denotes elevation and prominence.

Lexical and exegetical explanations bring the words close together.

The physical uses of حَدَب can also highlight something’s protruding, curved shape, as seen in the back, sand, and water.

نَشْز has its own semantic family, requiring separate examination in other Qur’anic uses.

Their proximity in describing ground does not make all derivatives identical.

ح د ب (ḥadab) and ج ب ل (jabal): a rise and a mountain

Study of J B L

جَبَل names a mountain in the Qur’an.

حَدَب can denote a hill, elevation, or rise in the ground.

Their referents may overlap: raised ground can, for example, form part of a mountain.

حَدَب nevertheless need not be a large mountain.

“Rises in the ground” therefore preserves the breadth of Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:96 better than “high mountains.”

ح د ب (ḥadab) and ر ب و (rabwah): a rise and an elevated place

Study of R B W

Arabic رَبْوَة denotes an elevated place.

The Qur’an also uses it for elevated ground.

حَدَب is related through elevation and prominence.

The ḥadab family also connects with a curved back and the form of waves, while rabwah belongs to another family and must be examined through that root’s uses.

Their proximity does not make every rabwah necessarily curved or hump-shaped.

ح د ب (ḥadab) and ح د ر (ḥadūr): a rise and a downward slope

Some dictionaries explain حَدَب through expressions concerning downward slopes and sloping ground.

This may initially seem inconsistent with elevation.

A natural rise can nevertheless have both an elevated part and a slope descending from it.

Mentioning a slope when describing a rise therefore need not negate elevation as a central meaning.

حَدَب should nevertheless not automatically be translated as “downward slope” wherever it appears.

Elevated or rising ground agrees better with the lexical evidence and context of Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:96.

ح د ب (ḥadab) and ح ن و (ḥanā): curvature and compassion

Arabic حَنَا concerns bending and can also carry a sense of compassion and kindness.

حَدِبَ عَلَيْهِ likewise concerns compassion and protective attention.

Bending toward someone and compassionate care can share an image-based relationship.

They nevertheless belong to distinct roots.

The compassionate sense of حَدِبَ عَلَيْهِ should not make حَدَبٍ in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:96 mean support or sympathy.

ح د ب (ḥadab) and ن س ل (nasala): elevated ground and swift movement

Study of N S L

The roots occur together in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:96.

حَدَب expresses the terrain’s position and raised form.

يَنْسِلُونَ expresses swift movement.

One thus concerns a characteristic of the place, the other the act of movement.

The distinction matters in translation: combining both meanings within حَدَب would disregard يَنْسِلُونَ.

ح د ب (ḥadab) and ج د ث (jadath): elevated ground and a grave

Study of J D TH

حَدَب, with ḥāʾ and bāʾ, concerns rising ground.

جَدَث, with jīm and thāʾ, denotes a grave.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:96 has حَدَبٍ.

Yā Sīn 36:51 has الْأَجْدَاث.

Although both verses contain يَنْسِلُونَ, the nouns differ in root and meaning.

مِنْ كُلِّ حَدَبٍ should therefore not be translated as “from every grave.”

5. Interpretive challenges and semantic development

Does حَدَب always mean a hump of the back?

No.

A protrusion of the back is one established use of this family.

Arabic also records حَدَب for elevated ground, raised sand, and water’s waves.

The broader meaning concerns prominence and protrusion, rather than a particular medical condition.

The Qur’anic use concerns terrain; a picture of illness or spinal abnormality should not be transferred to it.

Is every elevated area حَدَب?

Not necessarily.

Lexicons often describe حَدَب as ground rising prominently above its surroundings.

A high, entirely level plain may stand above sea level without necessarily being described by this Arabic word.

Relative elevation and raised shape matter for understanding حَدَب.

“A rise in the ground” is therefore useful in the root explanation, while a natural equivalent for elevated ground suits the verse’s translation.

Is حَدَب a scientific geological term?

Contemporary Persian and Arabic use terms for protrusion, convexity, and convex surfaces in geometry, engineering, and natural science.

Qur’anic حَدَبٍ does not, however, name a particular tectonic structure, crustal fold, or geometrical shape with specified measurements.

The verse gives no numerical elevation, rock type, gradient, or geological origin for the rises.

A contemporary scientific term should therefore not be imposed as the word’s exclusive meaning.

Did compassionate حَدَب develop from bodily protrusion?

Lexicographers including Ibn Fāris offer an image-based explanation for this relationship.

Someone showing compassion may bend toward another to provide protection, while the bending body takes a raised or curved form.

The explanation is understandable, but does not establish the actual history of derivation.

As with other roots, resemblance in imagery must be distinguished from historical evidence.

The root study can therefore explain both branches without constructing a certain story about the first naming.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

For حَدَبٍ in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:96, the most suitable brief Persian equivalent is “bolandi-ye zamin”: elevated ground.

“Hill” is also suitable in many uses, but may restrict the word to a landform of a particular shape and size.

“A rise in the ground” is closer to the root’s imagery and useful in lexical explanation; the simpler Persian “bolandi,” an elevation, flows better in the verse.

“Mountain” is too restrictive, since not every ḥadab is a mountain.

“Every direction” likewise does not directly translate حَدَب, although مِنْ كُلِّ حَدَبٍ can suggest movement from many points.

The proposed rendering of مِنْ كُلِّ حَدَبٍ يَنْسِلُونَ is:

“They come forth swiftly from every rise in the ground.”

It preserves three components: مِنْ for the starting point, كُلِّ حَدَبٍ for the elevations, and يَنْسِلُونَ for swift emergence.

Forqan’s lexical box can retain two distinct fields:

Brief root meaning: “Protrusion and prominence; a surface or back rising and curving.”

Meaning in this verse: “An elevation or rise in the ground from which they come forth and advance.”

The reflection can explain the whole sentence’s image of a swift advance from many rises, without placing every detail in the direct translation of the word.

7. Conclusion

Arabic ح د ب concerns protrusion, prominence, and something rising above its surroundings.

Its physical uses include elevated ground, a protruding back, raised sand, and waves of water.

أَحْدَب and حَدْبَاء likewise concern visible curvature and prominence.

Another branch uses حَدِبَ عَلَيْهِ for compassion and caring concern. Lexicographers relate this to bending toward someone, without establishing a certain historical origin.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an: حَدَبٍ in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:96.

Here حَدَب denotes rising ground, while يَنْسِلُونَ expresses swift movement.

مِنْ كُلِّ حَدَبٍ يَنْسِلُونَ thus pictures a swift emergence from many elevations in the ground.

The expression alone specifies neither Gog and Magog’s historical identity, home, nor exact geographical route. The word also does not establish graves, very large mountains, or a particular tectonic landform as the intended meaning.

The finding is that ح د ب centers on protrusion and prominence; its clear meaning in Al-Anbiyāʾ is elevated ground.

For Forqan, “they come forth swiftly from every rise in the ground” is therefore a natural rendering that preserves the noun’s meaning while separately conveying the verb of movement.
