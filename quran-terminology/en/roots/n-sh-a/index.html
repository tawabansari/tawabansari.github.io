Coming into being, arising, and growing: from clouds and human upbringing to creation and another origination

1. Introducing the root and its semantic center

The Arabic root ن ش أ concerns coming into being, beginning, arising, and growing. نَشَأَ is used when something begins to appear, develops into another condition, or gradually takes shape.

Established uses concern clouds appearing and rising on the horizon, children growing, plants emerging, and matters newly begun.

أَنْشَأَ directs attention to bringing something into being or shaping it. أَنْشَأَ بَيْتًا can mean beginning to build a house, while أَنْشَأَ حَدِيثًا can mean beginning to speak or producing an utterance.

The Qur’an uses أَنْشَأَ for bringing humans, generations, gardens, clouds, and other creatures into being.

This breadth shows that the root is not confined to first creation. Something may originate for the first time, emerge after something else, develop into a new condition, or be brought forth in another origination.

Several related ideas must therefore be distinguished: the beginning of existence, subsequent growth, bringing something into being, and emergence or transition into a new condition.

They are connected, but their uses are not identical.

نَشَأَ الْغُلَامُ, for example, describes a boy growing, rather than being created from nothing at every moment. أَنْشَأَ اللَّهُ الْخَلْقَ, however, can concern bringing creatures into existence.

Coming into being and taking shape, potentially involving arising and growth, form a defensible semantic center. The root alone does not determine whether something originates from nothing, is made from existing material, or develops into a new condition.

That distinction comes from the verb’s construction, its object, and context.

Qur’anic forms

The root occurs 28 times in 25 verses across 14 sūrahs, in seven morphological categories.

Forms examined:

  • أَنْشَأَ and its inflected forms: 20 occurrences, meaning to bring into being, produce, shape, or establish.
  • النَّشْأَة: three occurrences, meaning origination, creation, or a stage of coming into being.
  • يُنَشَّأُ: once, meaning being brought up or nurtured.
  • إِنْشَاء: once, the Form IV verbal noun, meaning bringing into being.
  • مُنْشِئُون: once, the Form IV active participle, meaning originators.
  • مُنْشَآت: once, the Form IV passive participle, describing ships.
  • نَاشِئَة: once, in نَاشِئَةَ اللَّيْلِ.

The Form I verb نَشَأَ does not directly occur in the Qur’an, although nāshiʾah belongs to this family.

Other familiar Arabic forms include نُشُوء, نَشْء, نَاشِئ, نَاشِئُون, تَنْشِئَة, تَنَشَّأَ, and مَنْشَأ, but they do not occur independently in the Qur’anic text.

Verbal form matters: نَشَأَ can describe something itself arising or growing, whereas أَنْشَأَ generally describes bringing something into being.

Form IV does not therefore always mean creating from nothing. Clouds, gardens, and ships show that origination can involve existing material, conditions, and processes.

2. Early Arabic: appearance, growth, and beginning

Nashaʾa: arising and appearing

One lexical explanation introduces the root as follows:

أَصْلٌ صَحِيحٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى ارْتِفَاعٍ فِي شَيْءٍ وَسُمُوٍّ

aṣlun ṣaḥīḥun yadullu ʿalā irtifāʿin fī shayʾin wa-sumuwwin

Translation:

“An established root indicating rising and elevation in something.”

This highlights a familiar image: something emerges from its previous condition and becomes more apparent or elevated.

Arabic says, for example:

نَشَأَ السَّحَابُ

nashaʾa al-saḥābu

Meaning:

“The cloud appeared and rose.”

The cloud forms in the sky and appears from the horizon or another direction.

Arising does not always mean physical movement upward. Applied to a person, nashaʾa can concern growing and maturing, rather than merely becoming taller.

An explanation based on elevation and arising is therefore useful, but should not become a compulsory physical image for every derivative.

Clouds beginning to form

One clear early use concerns clouds coming into being.

Lexical traditions distinguish a cloud newly appearing on the horizon from one that has spread and developed more fully.

نَشْءُ السَّحَابِ can denote a cloud’s initial appearance.

نَشْءٌ حَسَنٌ is attested in lexical usage for a cloud at the beginning of its appearance.

The following verse is transmitted in the Arabic lexical tradition:

إِذَا هَمَّ بِالْإِقْلَاعِ هَمَّتْ بِهِ الصَّبَا
فَعَاقَبَ نَشْءٌ بَعْدَهَا وَخُرُوجُ

idhā hamma bi-al-iqlāʿi hammat bihi al-ṣabā / fa-ʿāqaba nashʾun baʿdahā wa-khurūju

A translation conveying the sense:

“When the cloud intends to withdraw, the east wind moves with it; then fresh cloud and another emergence follow in succession.”

نَشْء concerns fresh cloud formation.

The image depicts clouds appearing successively in the sky.

The verse is preserved in lexical traditions, but its poet and date are unclear in the transmission examined. It cannot be called certainly pre-Islamic without qualification.

The sense of a cloud’s initial appearance is nevertheless independently established in lexical accounts.

This matters because nashaʾa can describe a natural process with an initial stage followed by growth and expansion.

It does not make every Qur’anic occurrence a slow, gradual process. The root alone establishes neither speed nor duration.

A human growing up

نَشَأَ الصَّبِيُّ means that a child grew up.

Likewise:

نَشَأَ فِي بَنِي فُلَانٍ

nashaʾa fī banī fulānin

Meaning:

“He grew up among a particular family or group.”

The topic is more than life’s beginning: a person develops within a particular environment.

نَاشِئ denotes a youth who has passed beyond childhood and is growing toward maturity.

نَشْء can also name the younger generation or a group of youths.

The Umayyad poet Nuṣayb ibn Rabāḥ says:

وَلَوْلَا أَنْ يُقَالَ صَبَا نُصَيْبٌ
لَقُلْتُ بِنَفْسِيَ النَّشَأُ الصِّغَارُ

wa-lawlā an yuqāla ṣabā nuṣaybun / la-qultu bi-nafsiya al-nashaʾu al-ṣighāru

Translation:

“Were it not that people would say Nuṣayb had become lovestruck, I would say: My life for those young women.”

النَّشَأ here refers to young women. Arabic also uses the word for young people of either sex.

Nashaʾa and its derivatives were thus established for growth and youth.

Nuṣayb was an Islamic-period poet. His verse illustrates classical Arabic rather than directly witnessing language before the Qur’an.

Nor does the word here mean creation from nothing: these people had already been born and were at a stage of growth.

Inshāʾ: beginning and bringing into being

أَنْشَأَ also expresses starting an activity or producing something.

For example:

أَنْشَأَ دَارًا

anshaʾa dāran

Meaning:

“He began building a house, or established a house.”

And:

أَنْشَأَ حَدِيثًا

anshaʾa ḥadīthan

Meaning:

“He began speaking.”

Inshāʾ can thus denote the start of an activity without requiring every stage to be completed at that moment.

أَنْشَأَ يَقُولُ likewise means that someone began to speak.

This helps explain the word’s modern use as well.

Persian sometimes uses inshāʾ for composition or writing a text. Classical Arabic also uses it for initiating or producing speech and writing, with broader technical senses in literary and administrative disciplines.

That technical sense should not be imposed on إِنْشَاءً in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:35.

The verse concerns bringing into being, rather than writing a text.

Uses concerning plants and earth

Early Arabic attests نَشِيئَة for fresh growth whose stalks or other parts have not fully developed.

The connection with emergence and growth is clear.

Some lexical accounts also record نَشِيئَةُ الْبِئْرِ for soil removed while digging a well.

This use concerns material becoming apparent or being brought out.

It does not establish soil extraction or plant growth as the original meaning of every form in the family.

Available evidence cannot support that historical sequence.

Appearance, growth, and bringing into being are better treated as established semantic centers, without making one physical image the certain origin of all meanings.

3. Qur’anic uses concerning human creation and growth

A substantial portion of Qur’anic ن ش أ concerns humans, generations, and stages of creation.

A. Al-Anʿām 6:98: humans from a single self

وَهُوَ الَّذِي أَنْشَأَكُمْ مِنْ نَفْسٍ وَاحِدَةٍ فَمُسْتَقَرٌّ وَمُسْتَوْدَعٌ قَدْ فَصَّلْنَا الْآيَاتِ لِقَوْمٍ يَفْقَهُونَ

wa-huwa alladhī anshaʾakum min nafsin wāḥidatin fa-mustaqarrun wa-mustawdaʿun qad faṣṣalnā al-āyāti li-qawmin yafqahūna

Translation:

“He is the One who brought you into being from a single self; then a place of settlement and a place of deposit. We have made the signs clear for people who understand.”

Al-Anʿām 6:98

أَنْشَأَكُمْ refers to bringing humans into being.

مِنْ نَفْسٍ وَاحِدَةٍ expresses their shared origin.

The verb itself does not identify that self’s sex or explain the entire manner of humanity’s emergence.

Those questions require accompanying expressions and other verses about human creation.

“Brought into being” fits better here than “raised up,” since the objects are humans themselves.

B. Hūd 11:61: bringing forth from the earth

Ṣāliḥ says to Thamūd:

هُوَ أَنْشَأَكُمْ مِنَ الْأَرْضِ وَاسْتَعْمَرَكُمْ فِيهَا

huwa anshaʾakum mina al-arḍi wa-istaʿmarakum fīhā

Translation:

“He brought you into being from the earth and appointed you to inhabit and cultivate it.”

Hūd 11:61, part of the verse

أَنْشَأَكُمْ is accompanied by مِنَ الْأَرْضِ.

The earthly origin comes from the second expression, rather than the root ن ش أ itself.

اسْتَعْمَرَكُمْ is a separate verb from ع م ر. Cultivation or habitation should not become part of anshaʾa’s definition.

Al-Najm 53:32 also says:

هُوَ أَعْلَمُ بِكُمْ إِذْ أَنْشَأَكُمْ مِنَ الْأَرْضِ وَإِذْ أَنْتُمْ أَجِنَّةٌ فِي بُطُونِ أُمَّهَاتِكُمْ

huwa aʿlamu bikum idh anshaʾakum mina al-arḍi wa-idh antum ajinnatun fī buṭūni ummahātikum

Translation:

“He knows you best, when He brought you into being from the earth and when you were embryos in your mothers’ wombs.”

The same verse continues:

فَلَا تُزَكُّوا أَنْفُسَكُمْ

fa-lā tuzakkū anfusakum

“So do not declare yourselves pure or superior.”

Recalling human creation is connected here with divine knowledge and avoiding self-praise.

Humility and avoiding self-praise are contextual messages, rather than the lexical meaning of ن ش أ.

C. Al-Muʾminūn 23:14: another creation

The account of human creation says:

ثُمَّ أَنْشَأْنَاهُ خَلْقًا آخَرَ فَتَبَارَكَ اللَّهُ أَحْسَنُ الْخَالِقِينَ

thumma anshaʾnāhu khalqan ākhara fa-tabāraka allāhu aḥsanu al-khāliqīna

Translation:

“Then We brought him forth as another creation. Blessed is The God, the best of creators.”

Al-Muʾminūn 23:14, the verse’s closing part

This is one of the root’s most important occurrences.

The same verse and the preceding one mention a drop, a clinging substance, a morsel, bones, and clothing bones with flesh.

The expression ثُمَّ أَنْشَأْنَاهُ خَلْقًا آخَرَ then follows.

ثُمَّ indicates a sequence of stages.

أَنْشَأْنَاهُ concerns bringing the being forth into a new condition.

خَلْقًا آخَرَ expresses another creation or mode of existence.

The sentence thus presents a different stage or condition following those already mentioned.

Anshaʾa itself does not identify precisely which biological or nonbiological event constitutes that difference.

Some interpretations connect it with the spirit being breathed in; others discuss the completion of human form, new capacities, or a developmental transformation.

These readings require examination through verses and independent evidence.

أَنْشَأْنَاهُ should not be translated directly as “We breathed a spirit into him”: neither the verb for breathing nor the word for spirit appears in that expression.

Conversely, the entire meaning cannot be confined to one familiar medical stage.

The clear lexical sense is that the being is brought forth in another stage.

D. Bringing forth hearing, sight, and hearts

Al-Muʾminūn 23:78 says:

وَهُوَ الَّذِي أَنْشَأَ لَكُمُ السَّمْعَ وَالْأَبْصَارَ وَالْأَفْئِدَةَ قَلِيلًا مَا تَشْكُرُونَ

wa-huwa alladhī anshaʾa lakumu al-samʿa wa-al-abṣāra wa-al-afʾidata qalīlan mā tashkurūna

Translation:

“He is the One who brought forth hearing, sight, and hearts for you. How little you give thanks.”

Al-Mulk 67:23 also says:

قُلْ هُوَ الَّذِي أَنْشَأَكُمْ وَجَعَلَ لَكُمُ السَّمْعَ وَالْأَبْصَارَ وَالْأَفْئِدَةَ قَلِيلًا مَا تَشْكُرُونَ

qul huwa alladhī anshaʾakum wa-jaʿala lakumu al-samʿa wa-al-abṣāra wa-al-afʾidata qalīlan mā tashkurūna

Translation:

“Say: He is the One who brought you into being and provided hearing, sight, and hearts for you. How little you give thanks.”

Both verses mention human faculties alongside human creation.

The verbs’ difference nevertheless matters.

Al-Muʾminūn applies أَنْشَأَ directly to hearing, sight, and hearts.

Al-Mulk uses أَنْشَأَكُمْ for humans themselves, followed by جَعَلَ for their faculties.

Anshaʾa is thus not confined to one kind of object.

It may concern a human being or faculties originating within that being.

The root itself does not mean hearing, seeing, or thinking.

Those faculties are expressed by separate nouns serving as objects.

4. Generations, gardens, and clouds

A. New generations

Al-Anʿām 6:6 says:

فَأَهْلَكْنَاهُمْ بِذُنُوبِهِمْ وَأَنْشَأْنَا مِنْ بَعْدِهِمْ قَرْنًا آخَرِينَ

fa-ahlaknāhum bi-dhunūbihim wa-anshaʾnā min baʿdihim qarnan ākharīna

Translation:

“We destroyed them for their sins and brought forth another generation after them.”

The pattern also appears elsewhere:

وَكَمْ قَصَمْنَا مِنْ قَرْيَةٍ كَانَتْ ظَالِمَةً وَأَنْشَأْنَا بَعْدَهَا قَوْمًا آخَرِينَ

wa-kam qaṣamnā min qaryatin kānat ẓālimatan wa-anshaʾnā baʿdahā qawman ākharīna

“How many wrongdoing settlements We shattered, bringing forth another people after them.”

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:11

And:

ثُمَّ أَنْشَأْنَا مِنْ بَعْدِهِمْ قَرْنًا آخَرِينَ

thumma anshaʾnā min baʿdihim qarnan ākharīna

“Then We brought forth another generation after them.”

Al-Muʾminūn 23:31

Al-Muʾminūn 23:42 and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:45 likewise speak of bringing forth other generations.

Anshaʾa in these cases concerns groups of humans emerging and becoming established.

The root itself does not require every new generation to follow an earlier generation’s complete destruction. In some verses, sequence comes from مِنْ بَعْدِهِمْ or other contextual elements.

Al-Anʿām 6:133 also says:

كَمَا أَنْشَأَكُمْ مِنْ ذُرِّيَّةِ قَوْمٍ آخَرِينَ

ka-mā anshaʾakum min dhurriyyati qawmin ākharīna

“Just as He brought you forth from the descendants of another people.”

This makes the connection between a new generation’s emergence and a preceding generation explicit.

Bringing generations into being does not necessarily mean producing humans without descent or any preceding process.

B. Gardens and vegetation

Al-Anʿām 6:141 says:

وَهُوَ الَّذِي أَنْشَأَ جَنَّاتٍ مَعْرُوشَاتٍ وَغَيْرَ مَعْرُوشَاتٍ

wa-huwa alladhī anshaʾa jannātin maʿrūshātin wa-ghayra maʿrūshātin

Translation:

“He is the One who brought forth gardens, trellised and untrellised.”

Al-Muʾminūn 23:19 also says:

فَأَنْشَأْنَا لَكُمْ بِهِ جَنَّاتٍ مِنْ نَخِيلٍ وَأَعْنَابٍ

fa-anshaʾnā lakum bihi jannātin min nakhīlin wa-aʿnābin

Translation:

“Through it We brought forth gardens of date palms and grapevines for you.”

The gardens are presented in connection with water and plant growth.

Anshaʾa can thus concern a living natural arrangement whose growth depends on conditions such as water and earth.

Watering, producing fruit, and a plant’s physical ascent are not all independent meanings of anshaʾa.

They come from context and other words in the verses.

مَعْرُوشَات and غَيْرَ مَعْرُوشَات describe the gardens and should not enter the definition of ن ش أ.

C. Al-Raʿd 13:12: producing heavy clouds

هُوَ الَّذِي يُرِيكُمُ الْبَرْقَ خَوْفًا وَطَمَعًا وَيُنْشِئُ السَّحَابَ الثِّقَالَ

huwa alladhī yurīkumu al-barqa khawfan wa-ṭamaʿan wa-yunshiʾu al-saḥāba al-thiqāla

Translation:

“He shows you lightning, with fear and hope, and brings forth heavy clouds.”

Al-Raʿd 13:12

This clearly connects with the early expression نَشَأَ السَّحَابُ.

That expression denotes a cloud rising and appearing.

The verse uses Form IV يُنْشِئُ for producing heavy clouds.

الثِّقَال describes the clouds’ heaviness or massiveness.

The rainy context makes a connection with the water in clouds understandable. The verb alone, however, establishes no theory of condensation, air pressure, or the precise stages of cloud formation.

The root is clearly not restricted to human creation.

Nor does it always mean physical ascent. The clouds’ formation and emergence naturally fit the subject here.

5. First and subsequent origination: re-creation in the Qur’an

A. Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:20: observing creation’s beginning

قُلْ سِيرُوا فِي الْأَرْضِ فَانْظُرُوا كَيْفَ بَدَأَ الْخَلْقَ ثُمَّ اللَّهُ يُنْشِئُ النَّشْأَةَ الْآخِرَةَ إِنَّ اللَّهَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ قَدِيرٌ

qul sīrū fī al-arḍi fa-unẓurū kayfa badaʾa al-khalqa thumma allāhu yunshiʾu al-nashʾata al-ākhirata inna allāha ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīrun

Translation:

“Say: Travel through the earth and observe how He began creation. Then The God brings forth the later origination. He has power over everything.”

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:20

Two different expressions occur together.

بَدَأَ الْخَلْقَ concerns beginning creation.

يُنْشِئُ النَّشْأَةَ الْآخِرَةَ concerns bringing forth the later origination.

ب د أ concerns beginning, while ن ش أ can encompass emergence and taking shape.

Together they connect the later origination with creation after the initial condition.

Nashʾah alone does not specify every detail of that condition.

Lexically, it denotes coming into being or the resulting condition. الْآخِرَة identifies the subsequent origination here.

“Later” is not a fixed component of nashʾah itself: the Qur’an also uses the word with الْأُولَى, “first.”

B. Al-Najm 53:47: the other origination

وَأَنَّ عَلَيْهِ النَّشْأَةَ الْأُخْرَىٰ

wa-anna ʿalayhi al-nashʾata al-ukhrā

Translation:

“And bringing forth the other origination rests with Him.”

Al-Najm 53:47

النَّشْأَةَ appears with الْأُخْرَى.

Its meaning approaches Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:20.

The difference between الْآخِرَة and الْأُخْرَى should not be turned without evidence into two entirely different worlds or stages.

Both descriptions can concern an origination after the first condition.

The verse concerns divine power and the task of bringing forth that condition, without describing its bodily form or precise timing.

C. Al-Wāqiʿah 56:62: the first origination

وَلَقَدْ عَلِمْتُمُ النَّشْأَةَ الْأُولَىٰ فَلَوْلَا تَذَكَّرُونَ

wa-la-qad ʿalimtumu al-nashʾata al-ūlā fa-lawlā tadhakkarūna

Translation:

“You have known the first origination. Why, then, do you not take heed?”

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:62

Nashʾah here is qualified by الْأُولَى.

The word itself is consequently not confined to the world after death.

The first origination concerns familiar human emergence and life.

The preceding verses discuss human creation, death, replacement, and the possibility of emergence in another condition.

The reference forms part of an argument concerning divine power to re-create.

Nashʾah does not mean memory, awareness, or thought here. Those meanings come from عَلِمْتُم and تَذَكَّرُونَ.

D. Yā Sīn 36:79: the first originator and re-creation

قُلْ يُحْيِيهَا الَّذِي أَنْشَأَهَا أَوَّلَ مَرَّةٍ وَهُوَ بِكُلِّ خَلْقٍ عَلِيمٌ

qul yuḥyīhā alladhī anshaʾahā awwala marratin wa-huwa bi-kulli khalqin ʿalīmun

Translation:

“Say: He who first brought them into being will give them life, and He knows every creation.”

Yā Sīn 36:79

The preceding verse asks about restoring life to decayed bones.

The response points to their original maker.

أَنْشَأَهَا concerns the bones, to which its pronoun refers.

أَوَّلَ مَرَّةٍ specifies the first occasion.

“First” comes from this adverbial phrase, rather than ن ش أ itself.

The verse invokes first creation to establish the possibility of re-creation.

Anshaʾa alone does not explain how bones are restored, the sequence of bodily stages, or physical mechanisms.

E. Al-Wāqiʿah 56:61: an unknown condition

عَلَىٰ أَنْ نُبَدِّلَ أَمْثَالَكُمْ وَنُنْشِئَكُمْ فِي مَا لَا تَعْلَمُونَ

ʿalā an nubaddila amthālakum wa-nunshiʾakum fī mā lā taʿlamūna

Translation:

“To replace you with your counterparts and bring you forth in a condition you do not know.”

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:61

Two independent verbs occur:

نُبَدِّلَ, from ب د ل.

نُنْشِئَكُمْ, from ن ش أ.

The first concerns replacement, the second bringing into being.

فِي مَا لَا تَعْلَمُونَ describes a condition unknown to the addressees.

That unfamiliarity should not become part of anshaʾa’s meaning.

Nor does the verse specify every detail of future creation.

Some interpretations connect the phrase with humans changing form or condition in the Hereafter.

This fits the sūrah’s context, but its exact nature cannot be extracted from the root.

6. Special uses: upbringing, ships, fire, and night

A. Al-Zukhruf 43:18: being brought up in adornment

أَوَمَنْ يُنَشَّأُ فِي الْحِلْيَةِ وَهُوَ فِي الْخِصَامِ غَيْرُ مُبِينٍ

a-wa-man yunashshaʾu fī al-ḥilyati wa-huwa fī al-khiṣāmi ghayru mubīnin

A close rendering:

“One who is brought up amid ornaments and is not clear in disputation?”

Al-Zukhruf 43:18

This is the root’s sole Form II Qur’anic occurrence in the Ḥafṣ reading.

يُنَشَّأُ is a passive Form II imperfect verb, meaning being brought up or nurtured in a particular environment.

فِي الْحِلْيَةِ specifies that environment.

The verb itself means neither ornamentation nor weakness in disputation. Those descriptions come from other parts of the sentence.

The context and the claim concerning daughters

The preceding verses criticize people who attributed daughters to The God while disliking the birth of daughters themselves.

Al-Zukhruf 43:17 says:

وَإِذَا بُشِّرَ أَحَدُهُمْ بِمَا ضَرَبَ لِلرَّحْمَٰنِ مَثَلًا ظَلَّ وَجْهُهُ مُسْوَدًّا وَهُوَ كَظِيمٌ

wa-idhā bushshira aḥaduhum bi-mā ḍaraba li-al-raḥmāni mathalan ẓalla wajhuhu muswaddan wa-huwa kaẓīmun

Translation:

“When one of them receives news of what he assigns as a likeness to the Merciful, his face darkens as he suppresses his anger.”

Verse 18 belongs to a criticism of this group’s behavior and claims.

Being raised amid ornaments and lacking clear expression in disputation should not immediately become a categorical judgment about every woman’s intellectual capacity.

Linguistically, the verse describes someone in a particular setting. Contextually, the description forms part of an argument against people who disliked daughters for themselves but attributed them to The God.

Interpretations differ over how the sentence reflects the audience’s social assumptions and the argument’s purpose.

For this root study, the lexical point is clearer: يُنَشَّأُ concerns upbringing and growth, rather than a person’s sex, reasoning ability, or moral worth.

Variant readings

Ḥafṣ reads يُنَشَّأُ as a passive Form II verb.

Other established readings use the Form I يَنْشَأُ.

The former emphasizes being brought up.

The latter expresses growing or maturing itself.

The morphological distinction matters, while both retain the broad sense of growth within a particular environment.

B. Al-Raḥmān 55:24: raised ships

وَلَهُ الْجَوَارِ الْمُنْشَآتُ فِي الْبَحْرِ كَالْأَعْلَامِ

wa-lahu al-jawāri al-munshaʾātu fī al-baḥri ka-al-aʿlāmi

Translation:

“His are the raised ships in the sea, like mountains.”

Al-Raḥmān 55:24

This is a distinctive and important use of ن ش أ.

الْجَوَارِ denotes ships traveling on water.

الْمُنْشَآتُ in the Ḥafṣ reading is a Form IV passive participle.

Lexical explanations connect it with ships whose sails have been raised.

Another interpretation understands ships constructed and prepared to sail.

Another established reading has الْمُنْشِئَاتُ, with a kasrah on shīn, an active participle. The ships then initiate movement or raise their sails.

The active and passive formations must therefore be distinguished.

The active participle does not establish that ships necessarily move by independent will.

Arabic can attribute an action to a vehicle or instrument even when people set it in motion.

Does الْمُنْشَآت mean created?

As a passive participle of anshaʾa, “brought into being” or “constructed” is morphologically possible.

The specific interpretation involving ships and raised sails nevertheless has established lexical support.

“Ships with raised sails” can therefore be chosen in translation.

This does not make raising sails the root’s only general meaning.

It is a particular derivative’s use in a maritime setting.

كَالْأَعْلَامِ is a separate comparison, referring here to high mountains.

The ships’ size comes from that comparison, rather than munshaʾāt itself.

C. Al-Wāqiʿah 56:72: producing the fire’s tree

أَأَنْتُمْ أَنْشَأْتُمْ شَجَرَتَهَا أَمْ نَحْنُ الْمُنْشِئُونَ

a-antum anshaʾtum shajaratahā am naḥnu al-munshiʾūna

Translation:

“Did you bring its tree into being, or are We the originators?”

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:72

The verse follows a question about the fire that people kindle.

أَنْشَأْتُمْ is a second-person plural verb, while الْمُنْشِئُونَ is a plural active participle.

Both belong to Form IV.

The object is شَجَرَتَهَا, the tree of that fire.

Lexical and historical explanations discuss trees whose wood was used to kindle fire.

The root ن ش أ itself does not mean kindling fire.

It describes bringing the tree into being; fire and kindling are expressed by other words and verses.

الْمُنْشِئُونَ denotes those who bring into being.

The participial form does not place humanity and The God on the same level as two equal powers.

The sentence asks about the tree’s origin and belongs to an argument about creation.

D. Al-Wāqiʿah 56:35: أَنْشَأْنَاهُنَّ إِنْشَاءً

إِنَّا أَنْشَأْنَاهُنَّ إِنْشَاءً

innā anshaʾnāhunna inshāʾan

Translation:

“We have indeed brought them into being in a special manner.”

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:35

The verb أَنْشَأْنَاهُنَّ and verbal noun إِنْشَاءً occur together.

The verbal noun is a cognate accusative emphasizing bringing into being.

The construction alone does not establish whether this necessarily means creation from nothing or an existing being’s transformation.

The surrounding verses must be examined to identify the feminine plural pronoun’s referent.

The preceding verses describe the Garden’s blessings and raised couches; the following verses include أَبْكَارًا, عُرُبًا, and أَتْرَابًا.

Two main interpretations appear in exegetical tradition.

One understands the pronoun as referring to women or companions of the Garden brought forth for that condition.

The other concerns women who lived in this world and are brought into a new condition in the Hereafter.

Anshaʾa alone does not conclusively select either reading.

Stories about their ages, previous condition, or bodily details should not enter the root’s meaning without Qur’anic specification.

Translation should retain bringing into being, leaving the pronoun’s precise referent and the nature of that origination to contextual discussion.

E. Al-Muzzammil 73:6: نَاشِئَةَ اللَّيْلِ

إِنَّ نَاشِئَةَ اللَّيْلِ هِيَ أَشَدُّ وَطْئًا وَأَقْوَمُ قِيلًا

inna nāshiʾata al-layli hiya ashaddu waṭʾan wa-aqwamu qīlan

Translation:

“Rising at night is indeed firmer in steadfastness and more upright and fitting for speech.”

Al-Muzzammil 73:6

This is the Qur’an’s only occurrence of نَاشِئَة.

Several established interpretations exist.

First reading: the night’s hours

This reading understands successive parts or hours of the night that come into being one after another.

Each new portion of the night can be called nāshiʾah.

This accords with the root’s broad sense of emergence and beginning.

Second reading: rising for worship at night

Another reading concerns a person rising at night to worship and recite the Qur’an.

It clearly fits the sūrah’s opening verses:

قُمِ اللَّيْلَ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا

qumi al-layla illā qalīlan

“Rise through the night, except a little.”

Al-Muzzammil 73:2

Nāshiʾat al-layl can thus be connected with rising at night.

Third reading: rising after sleep

Some lexical accounts connect it with someone who sleeps for part of the night and then rises for worship.

This interpretation merits linguistic examination, but the phrase alone does not make prior sleep a necessary condition.

Early sources explain nāshiʾah both as the beginning of the night and as all its hours.

Its meaning cannot therefore be definitively restricted to worship following a particular sleep.

Relation to أَشَدُّ وَطْئًا وَأَقْوَمُ قِيلًا

Two descriptions follow in the verse.

أَشَدُّ وَطْئًا in the Ḥafṣ reading has been connected with firmness, difficulty, or a deeper effect.

Other readings include وِطَاءً, connected with accord and agreement.

أَقْوَمُ قِيلًا concerns greater uprightness or correctness of speech.

The sūrah presents night as having different conditions from daytime for rising, reciting, and attending to speech.

The root ن ش أ itself does not mean difficulty, harmony between heart and tongue, or speaking correctly.

Those meanings come from the verse’s separate descriptions and context.

“Rising at night” is a natural basic rendering that fits the sūrah’s opening.

The night-hours interpretation nevertheless has lexical support and should not be discarded without evidence.

7. Comparison with related roots

ن ش أ and ب د أ: emergence and beginning

ب د أ concerns beginning or starting.

ن ش أ can likewise concern something’s emergence, but extends beyond the initial moment to growth and formation.

Both roots occur in Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:20:

كَيْفَ بَدَأَ الْخَلْقَ

kayfa badaʾa al-khalqa

and:

يُنْشِئُ النَّشْأَةَ الْآخِرَةَ

yunshiʾu al-nashʾata al-ākhirata

Their association has a significant semantic distinction.

It does not establish that badaʾa always concerns only an initial instant and nashaʾa always subsequent gradual growth.

Anshaʾa itself can mean beginning in some uses.

The distinction is a tendency requiring examination in each context.

Study of B D A

ن ش أ and خ ل ق: bringing into being and creating

خ ل ق concerns creation, determining proportions, and shaping in the Qur’an.

ن ش أ likewise frequently denotes bringing humans and other creatures into being.

Both families occur in Al-Muʾminūn 23:14’s account of human creation.

This does not establish a theory that khalq concerns only bodily matter and nashʾ concerns only spirit or immaterial life.

Such a distinction would require independent evidence.

Both Qur’anic roots can be applied to bodily beings.

Study of KH L Q

ن ش أ and ن ب ت: growth and sprouting

ن ب ت concerns plants sprouting.

ن ش أ also concerns plant growth and things emerging.

Nabt more particularly concerns vegetation, whereas nashʾ also applies to humans, clouds, speech, generations, and new conditions.

ن ش أ therefore has a broader range of objects.

Not every instance of plant growth should become inshāʾ, nor every instance of inshāʾ be confined to vegetation.

Study of N B T

ن ش أ and ر ب و: growth and increase

ر ب و concerns increase, abundance, and rising.

ن ش أ can also concern growth and maturation.

A youth may grow both in bodily size and in capacities and life stage.

Increase in quantity, however, is not the entirety of nashʾ.

Something may emerge without its increasing size being the sentence’s main topic.

Growth and increase are thus related without being identical.

Study of R B W

ن ش أ and ق و م: arising and standing up

ق و م concerns standing, rising, and establishing.

ن ش أ can also express arising or rising in some uses.

This proximity has led some lexical and exegetical explanations to connect nāshiʾat al-layl with nighttime rising.

ق و م nevertheless has a broad Qur’anic field involving standing, establishing, and uprightness.

ن ش أ more particularly concerns beginning, emergence, and growth.

The nighttime expression does not make the roots synonymous everywhere.

Study of Q W M

ن ش أ and ش ي أ: bringing into being and willing

Some forms may look similar to Persian readers, but the roots are entirely different.

ن ش أ concerns emergence, growth, and bringing into being.

ش ي أ in شَاءَ concerns willing and volition.

إِنْشَاء is the verbal noun from ن ش أ and denotes bringing into being.

إِنْ شَاءَ instead comprises the conditional particle إِنْ and the verb شَاءَ.

إِنْ شَاءَ اللَّهُ therefore means “if The God wills,” rather than “The God’s creation.”

Their surface resemblance should not lead to mixing the roots.

Study of SH Y A

8. Interpretive difficulties and translation proposals for Forqan

Does inshāʾ always mean creation from nothing?

No.

The Qur’an applies anshaʾa to gardens, clouds, generations, human faculties, and creation in another origination.

Some cases concern creation’s beginning.

Others concern emergence following an earlier process or condition.

“Bringing into being” is therefore a more suitable general equivalent than “creating from nothing.”

First creation or the absence of previous material, if relevant, must be established from the verse or independent evidence.

Does nashʾah mean a separate, independent world?

Nashʾah concerns origination and the condition resulting from it.

The Qur’an mentions the first origination and the later or other origination.

It thus distinguishes the initial condition of creation from a subsequent one.

The word alone does not specify that condition’s spatial, bodily, or temporal nature.

“Another origination” or “another creation” is suitable, with details examined contextually.

Is growth essential to every derivative?

Upbringing and growth clearly suit يُنَشَّأُ فِي الْحِلْيَةِ.

They also plainly suit the word’s use for a youth.

أَنْشَأَكُمْ or أَنْشَأَ جَنَّاتٍ, however, principally concern bringing into being.

Not every form should simply be translated as “causing to grow.”

الْمُنْشَآت in Al-Raḥmān also has a specific maritime use connected with construction or raising.

“Brought into being” is a useful baseline for أَنْشَأَ, with “nurtured,” “established,” or another natural equivalent chosen where context requires.

Persian can retain the familiar term nashʾah, since first and subsequent creation encompass more than the instant of birth.

“Ships with raised sails” in Al-Raḥmān 55:24 accords with established lexical explanation; the variant reading and construction interpretation should remain in the discussion.

“Rising at night” in Al-Muzzammil 73:6 is defensible and contextually fitting, without adding prior sleep as a condition.

“Is brought up” directly renders Al-Zukhruf 43:18’s verb; a social or gender judgment should not replace the root’s meaning.

“Brought into being” suits Al-Wāqiʿah 56:35, leaving the pronoun’s referent and the mode of creation to separate examination.

9. Conclusion

The Arabic root ن ش أ concerns coming into being, arising, beginning, and growth.

Early uses concern clouds, people, plants, speech, buildings, and other subjects.

نَشَأَ can describe something itself emerging or developing, while أَنْشَأَ more often emphasizes bringing something into being or beginning it.

This is not an absolute rule making nashaʾa only natural growth and anshaʾa only creation from nothing.

The root occurs 28 times in seven morphological categories in the Qur’an.

Most uses concern bringing humans, generations, gardens, clouds, and human faculties into being.

Others mention first and subsequent origination and divine power to re-create.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:14 presents another creation after preceding stages, without the root specifying the precise transformation.

Al-Zukhruf 43:18 uses Form II for being brought up.

الْمُنْشَآت in Al-Raḥmān 55:24 describes ships and concerns construction or raised sails.

Inshāʾ in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:35 emphasizes bringing into being, without identifying the pronoun’s referent or its previous condition by itself.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:72 uses الْمُنْشِئُونَ for originators, within a question about the tree used for kindling fire.

Nāshiʾat al-layl in Al-Muzzammil 73:6 has been understood as successive night hours or rising for nighttime worship. The sūrah’s opening favors the latter as a fitting interpretation, without justifying dismissal of established alternatives.

Early Arabic confirms cloud formation and human growth as established uses. Construction, initiating speech, and upbringing also show that the family is broader than a simple upward-motion image.

Coming into being and taking shape are its central shared senses. First creation, gradual growth, renewed origination, and raising something are different contextual expressions of that field.

A proposed final definition for Forqan

ن ش أ concerns coming into being, beginning, arising, and growing. أَنْشَأَ extends this to bringing something into being, shaping, or establishing it.

This can concern humans, generations, plants, clouds, faculties, and new conditions. The root does not necessarily mean creation from nothing, a fixed speed, or a particular physical process.

Qur’anic أَنْشَأَ generally means brought into being; يُنَشَّأُ concerns upbringing; النَّشْأَة concerns origination or creation at a particular stage; الْمُنْشَآت has a special maritime sense involving construction or raising; and نَاشِئَةَ اللَّيْلِ concerns nighttime rising or successive night hours.

Coming into being and bringing into being, potentially involving growth and formation, provide a defensible central definition. Each derivative’s precise meaning comes from morphology, subject matter, and context, rather than a predetermined physical image or interpretive theory.
