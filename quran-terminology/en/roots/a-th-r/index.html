The Qur’anic Root A TH R

Athar: a trace or remnant, following predecessors, transmitting what survives of a report, and preferring one thing over another

1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

The Arabic root A TH R has several related branches of meaning. Its best-known noun, أَثَر (athar), denotes a trace or remnant: a footprint left by a person or animal, a surviving mark of a building, or a consequence of an action.

The same field includes following someone. A person may follow another’s footprints, but following need not involve physical movement. Continuing the ways of one’s predecessors can also be expressed as following their traces.

Another branch concerns transmitting and preserving reports. أَثَرْتُ الْحَدِيثَ (athartu al-ḥadītha) means “I transmitted a report from someone else,” while مَأْثُور (maʾthūr) describes something handed down from predecessors.

آثَرَ (āthara) means choosing or preferring one thing over another. The preference may benefit someone else, as when placing a person in need before oneself, or harm the chooser, as when preferring worldly life to the Hereafter.

A TH R therefore has no inherently positive or negative value. Nor can all its derivatives be translated simply as “footprint,” “influence,” or “altruism.”

Qur’anic forms

A TH R occurs 21 times in 21 verses:

The noun forms occur in Al-Māʾidah 5:46, Al-Kahf 18:6 and Al-Kahf 18:64, Ṭā Hā 20:84 and Ṭā Hā 20:96, Al-Rūm 30:50, Yā Sīn 36:12, Al-Ṣāffāt 37:70, Ghāfir 40:21 and Ghāfir 40:82, Al-Zukhruf 43:22 and Al-Zukhruf 43:23, Al-Fatḥ 48:29, and Al-Ḥadīd 57:27.

The five forms concerning preference occur in Yūsuf 12:91, Ṭā Hā 20:72, Al-Ḥashr 59:9, Al-Nāziʿāt 79:38, and Al-Aʿlā 87:16.

Yuʾtharu occurs in Al-Muddaththir 74:24, and athāratin in Al-Aḥqāf 46:4.

Other familiar Arabic forms include إِثْر (ithr), “following” or “after”; إِيثَار (īthār), “preference”; تَأْثِير (taʾthīr), “leaving an effect”; مَأْثُور (maʾthūr), “transmitted”; مَأْثَرَة (maʾtharah), a remembered good deed or distinction; and اسْتِئْثَار (istiʾthār), appropriating something for oneself. These exact forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

The morphological distinction matters: Form IV āthara differs from the noun athar and from athara, “to transmit a report.”

2. Athar in early Arabic: traces, following, and reports

A trace remaining after something

Arabic athar denotes a mark indicating that something exists or has passed by.

A person crossing soft ground may leave footprints. A wheel may mark the soil, and a ruined building may retain walls and foundations that speak of its past.

In each case, a trace relates to something that existed or acted and left a sign behind.

The trace need not be a separate object. It may remain on something itself, like a scar on skin or a change of color caused by one substance contacting another.

Lexical sources explain أَثَرُ الشَّيْءِ (atharu al-shayʾ) through بَقِيَّتُهُ (baqiyyatuhu): what remains of it.

Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī likewise describes athar as what indicates something’s existence.

This clarifies the central field: a trace indicates an existence, movement, or event, even when its cause is no longer before one’s eyes.

It need not belong to a distant past. A footprint made moments ago can be athar just as the remains of an ancient building can.

Athar and ithr: following someone

Arabic uses فِي إِثْرِهِ (fī ithrihi) and عَلَى أَثَرِهِ (ʿalā atharihi) for coming after or following someone.

The connection with footprints is clear: following another’s signs of movement can reveal their route and allow one to continue along it.

Many uses no longer require actual footprints on the ground.

Someone entering a place after another may be said to have followed them even when no visible footprints remain.

In these constructions, athar can therefore concern another person’s trail, route, or temporal precedence.

English “following in someone’s footsteps” has comparable breadth: it may concern physical movement or continuing another person’s way of life.

A poetic witness from ʿUrwah ibn al-Ward

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris quotes a verse attributed to ʿUrwah ibn al-Ward:

وَقَالُوا مَا تَشَاءُ فَقُلْتُ أَلْهُو
إِلَى الْإِصْبَاحِ آثَرَ ذِي أَثِيرِ

wa-qālū mā tashāʾu fa-qultu alhū / ilā al-iṣbāḥi āthara dhī athīri

“They asked what I wanted. I said: first of all, to enjoy myself until morning.”

Here āthara dhī athīr is a fixed expression explained by lexicographers as “before anything else” or giving an action precedence. Its construction should not simply be equated with the inflected Form IV verb āthara.

The witness matters because choosing and giving precedence were also known in Arabic poetry.

ʿUrwah ibn al-Ward was a pre-Islamic poet. A dictionary’s quotation should nevertheless be distinguished from independent confirmation of the exact wording and date of the particular verse.

The witness helps establish the field of precedence, but does not restrict every Qur’anic āthara to pleasure or amusement.

Athara al-ḥadītha: transmitting another person’s words

Early Arabic uses أَثَرَ الْحَدِيثَ (athara al-ḥadītha) for transmitting a report from someone else.

A statement passes from person to person and remains in circulation.

Maʾthūr likewise describes a report that has been narrated and transmitted.

A verse attributed to Al-Aʿshā says:

إِنَّ الَّذِي فِيهِ تَمَارَيْتُمَا
بَيِّنٌ لِلسَّامِعِ وَالْآثِرِ

inna alladhī fīhi tamāraytumā / bayyinun li-al-sāmiʿi wa-al-āthiri

“What you two disputed is clear to the hearer and the transmitter.”

Al-āthir here is someone who transmits a report from another.

Athar’s field therefore extends beyond marks on the ground or material remains to reports and knowledge passing between people.

Transmission alone does not establish truth: a person may narrate a true or a false statement.

Calling something maʾthūr describes its transmission. Its reliability and accuracy require separate examination.

Did all meanings develop from one origin?

Ibn Fāris identifies three fields: giving precedence, mentioning and transmitting, and a surviving trace.

Relationships can be recognized: a sign survives from the past, someone follows it, a report passes between generations, and one thing is placed before another in a choice.

These semantic connections should not become claims of a certain historical derivation.

In particular, similar imagery alone does not establish that preferring originally meant stepping in another person’s footprints.

The firmer conclusion is that Arabic usage contains several related branches. Each derivative must be understood through its structure and context.

3. Footprints, following, and continuing a path

In many verses, athar and āthār concern moving after someone or following their route.

Moses and his companion retrace their steps

Al-Kahf, 18:64

قَالَ ذَٰلِكَ مَا كُنَّا نَبْغِ ۚ فَارْتَدَّا عَلَىٰ آثَارِهِمَا قَصَصًا

qāla dhālika mā kunnā nabghi fa-irtaddā ʿalā āthārihimā qaṣaṣā

“He said, ‘That is what we were seeking.’ So the two turned back, tracing their own footsteps.”

This is a clear example of the spatial sense of athar.

After learning of something that happened along their journey, Moses and his companion return along the route they traveled.

Āthārihimā refers to the traces of their route.

“Their own footsteps” is natural and clear here.

The word alone does not establish that every step made a visible impression in soft ground. The expression can also concern retracing the route already taken.

قَصَصًا (qaṣaṣan) likewise concerns tracing the route here, but belongs to Q Ṣ Ṣ, not A TH R.

Its presence reinforces the sense of following a route.

ʿAlā atharī: following Moses

Ṭā Hā, 20:84

قَالَ هُمْ أُولَاءِ عَلَىٰ أَثَرِي وَعَجِلْتُ إِلَيْكَ رَبِّ لِتَرْضَىٰ

qāla hum ulāʾi ʿalā atharī wa-ʿajiltu ilayka rabbi li-tarḍā

“He said, ‘They are following me, and I have hastened to You, my Lord, so that You may be pleased.’”

Moses speaks of people who are behind him and following him.

Atharī carries the first-person singular suffix: my trace or route.

“They are following me” is more natural than the strictly literal “they are on my footprint.”

Athar can therefore concern the order and spacing of people’s movement.

The word alone does not determine their exact distance from Moses or how far in time he had gone ahead.

Following in the path of earlier messengers

Al-Māʾidah 5:46 and Al-Ḥadīd 57:27 describe sending messengers after earlier messengers.

Āthārihim accompanies قَفَّيْنَا (qaffaynā).

Qaffaynā belongs to Q F W and here expresses bringing or sending someone after another.

ʿAlā āthārihim thus concerns messengers following their predecessors’ path and the continuity of the message.

Al-Māʾidah 5:46 presents Jesus, son of Mary, after earlier messengers and also mentions confirmation of the Torah.

This differs from unquestioning imitation of ancestral customs.

The concern is continuity of the message and confirmation of what preceded Jesus.

Coming after someone “in their traces” is therefore not necessarily blameworthy in the Qur’an.

Following the ways of the fathers

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:70 speaks of people hastening along their fathers’ traces.

In Al-Zukhruf 43:22 and Al-Zukhruf 43:23, groups say they found their fathers following a way or community and that they themselves are guided by, or imitate, their footsteps.

Al-Zukhruf, 43:22

بَلْ قَالُوا إِنَّا وَجَدْنَا آبَاءَنَا عَلَىٰ أُمَّةٍ وَإِنَّا عَلَىٰ آثَارِهِمْ مُهْتَدُونَ

bal qālū innā wajadnā ābāʾanā ʿalā ummatin wa-innā ʿalā āthārihim muhtadūn

“Rather, they said, ‘We found our fathers following a way, and we are guided by following them.’”

Āthārihim here is not confined to the fathers’ physical footprints.

It concerns continuing a way inherited from predecessors.

Athar itself should not be defined as a religion or creed. The way or tradition comes from عَلَىٰ أُمَّةٍ (ʿalā ummatin) and the context; the traces express following their path.

This distinction matters.

Al-Māʾidah 5:46 concerns following earlier messengers in confirmation and continuation of the message.

Al-Zukhruf 43:22 and Al-Zukhruf 43:23 present reliance on the fathers’ way as a response to the messengers’ invitation and challenge.

Thus following predecessors’ traces does not itself establish that a position is right or wrong. One must ask what path is followed and what supports its validity.

Following their traces in Al-Kahf

Al-Kahf 18:6 describes Muḥammad’s profound grief over those who do not believe the message.

It also contains ʿalā āthārihim.

This need not mean following their physical footprints.

The context concerns people turning away from the message and the Prophet’s grief over their outcome.

“In their wake” or “after their turning away” can clarify the sense.

In expressions of following someone, athar can thus extend beyond a physical route to an ongoing response or situation after another’s departure.

4. Signs and remnants: the human past and the consequences of action

Earlier peoples’ traces on earth

Ghāfir 40:21 and Ghāfir 40:82 recall earlier peoples greater in strength and in the traces of their activity on earth.

Āthāran fī al-arḍ can here concern buildings, structures, signs of development, and other remains of their activity.

It should not be translated only as “ancient ruins.”

Their buildings and works may have been intact and in use while they lived. After their destruction, the same works could become historical remains.

Āthār therefore concerns what human power, development, and activity produced and left on earth.

The verses do more than identify ancient monuments: they recall peoples whose greater resources and strength did not shield them from the consequences of their deeds.

That conclusion comes from the whole context rather than athar’s lexical meaning alone.

Traces of the mercy of The God in nature

Al-Rūm, 30:50

فَانْظُرْ إِلَىٰ آثَارِ رَحْمَتِ اللَّهِ كَيْفَ يُحْيِي الْأَرْضَ بَعْدَ مَوْتِهَا ۚ إِنَّ ذَٰلِكَ لَمُحْيِي الْمَوْتَىٰ ۖ وَهُوَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ قَدِيرٌ

fa-unẓur ilā āthāri raḥmati allāhi kayfa yuḥyī al-arḍa baʿda mawtihā; inna dhālika la-muḥyī al-mawtā wa-huwa ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīr

“Look, then, at the traces of the mercy of The God: how He revives the earth after its death. Surely The God Himself revives the dead, and He has power over all things.”

Here the traces are attributed to the mercy of The God.

The preceding verses concern winds, clouds, and rain, so the traces relate to the results of rainfall and the earth’s revival.

After water reaches dry land, plants grow and signs of life appear.

This growth and transformation are traces of divine mercy in this context.

Āthār itself does not mean rain. Rain may cause or bring about the change, while greening land and growing plants are its observable results.

Among the seven readings, Nāfiʿ, Ibn Kathīr, Abū ʿAmr, and Shuʿbah from ʿĀṣim read the singular أَثَرِ رَحْمَتِ اللَّهِ (athari raḥmati allāh). Ibn ʿĀmir, Ḥafṣ from ʿĀṣim, Ḥamzah, and Al-Kisāʾī read the plural. Singular and plural do not change the basic semantic field here.

This shows that athar need not concern people or the historical past: a natural event’s sign or result can also be a trace.

Recording what people send ahead and leave behind

Yā Sīn, 36:12

إِنَّا نَحْنُ نُحْيِي الْمَوْتَىٰ وَنَكْتُبُ مَا قَدَّمُوا وَآثَارَهُمْ ۚ وَكُلَّ شَيْءٍ أَحْصَيْنَاهُ فِي إِمَامٍ مُبِينٍ

innā naḥnu nuḥyī al-mawtā wa-naktubu mā qaddamū wa-āthārahum; wa-kulla shayʾin aḥṣaynāhu fī imāmin mubīn

“It is We who revive the dead and record what they have sent ahead and their traces. We have counted everything in a clear record and register.”

This is one of the root’s most important Qur’anic uses.

It places مَا قَدَّمُوا (mā qaddamū) beside آثَارَهُمْ (āthārahum).

Mā qaddamū concerns what people have done or sent ahead.

Āthārahum concerns what remains from them or follows their deeds.

Early explanations include both actual footsteps and the consequences of actions.

Both fall within the lexical field of athar.

In the context of recording deeds and reviving the dead, the enduring consequences of human action have particular importance.

An act may end when performed while its effect continues in others’ lives.

Someone may establish a useful route, transmit knowledge, or cause an injustice whose consequences continue afterward.

These examples show that a trace need not be confined to the first action itself.

The root should not, however, be defined as necessarily lasting forever, nor should it establish that every remote, indirect consequence is reckoned exactly like the initial act.

The limits of responsibility, the knowledge of The God, and the recording of deeds must be understood through the relevant verses on account and recompense as a whole.

The trace of prostration on faces

Al-Fatḥ 48:29 describes a sign in believers’ faces associated with the trace of prostration.

Athar here is something made apparent in a person through prostration or the condition accompanying it.

Two kinds of explanation are offered: a visible mark on the face, and an expression or bearing through which worship, humility, and conduct become apparent.

Athar alone does not establish that this must be a darkened or calloused forehead.

Nor should it be confined without contextual examination to something entirely invisible.

Lexically, athar is a remaining mark or sign indicating something’s presence. Identifying this mark requires examining sīmā, “sign”; wujūh, “faces”; sujūd, “prostration”; and the verse’s context.

A mark on the forehead should therefore not automatically be treated as certain proof of faith or spiritual superiority. The root alone supports no such conclusion.

The messenger’s trace in the Sāmirī’s words

Ṭā Hā, 20:96

قَالَ بَصُرْتُ بِمَا لَمْ يَبْصُرُوا بِهِ فَقَبَضْتُ قَبْضَةً مِنْ أَثَرِ الرَّسُولِ فَنَبَذْتُهَا وَكَذَٰلِكَ سَوَّلَتْ لِي نَفْسِي

qāla baṣurtu bi-mā lam yabṣurū bihi fa-qabaḍtu qabḍatan min athari al-rasūli fa-nabadhtuhā wa-ka-dhālika sawwalat lī nafsī

“He said, ‘I perceived what they did not perceive, so I took a handful from the messenger’s trace and cast it away. Thus my soul made it seem attractive to me.’”

This is one of the most difficult Qur’anic uses of athar.

The Sāmirī says he took a handful from athari al-rasūl and cast it away.

Athar can lexically mean a footprint or remaining mark, and its association with qabḍah, “a handful,” favors taking something material.

Early narrative explanations commonly identify the messenger as Gabriel and the trace as soil from his horse’s hoofprint.

The verse itself, however, neither names Gabriel nor introduces a horse or a miraculous property of soil beneath its hoof.

Arabic athar can also denote someone’s path or surviving sign. An interpretation concerning something taken from a messenger’s teaching or way is therefore possible within the word’s semantic range.

That interpretation must still be weighed against قَبَضْتُ قَبْضَةً (qabaḍtu qabḍatan) and فَنَبَذْتُهَا (fa-nabadhtuhā). It should not be preferred merely to fit a prior interpretation.

Crucially, this is the Sāmirī’s own statement. The Qur’an reports his words, and he ends by saying his soul made the act seem attractive to him.

His claims about what he saw or took should therefore not automatically become direct declarations by The God about the event’s external reality.

A precise understanding requires separate study of the messenger’s identity, qabḍ, the calf episode, and the Sāmirī’s words.

The root study should retain athar’s range: a sign, remnant, or something left by another’s presence or path. Gabriel, the horse, and the soil are not elements of the word’s lexical definition.

5. Transmitted reports and remnants of knowledge

Siḥrun yuʾtharu: something transmitted from others

Al-Muddaththir, 74:24

فَقَالَ إِنْ هَٰذَا إِلَّا سِحْرٌ يُؤْثَرُ

fa-qāla in hādhā illā siḥrun yuʾthar

“Then he said, ‘This is nothing but magic transmitted from others.’”

Yuʾtharu is a passive imperfect of Form I.

It differs from āthara, “to prefer.”

Athara al-ḥadītha means transmitting another’s words; yuʾtharu describes something narrated or handed on from others.

The speaker attempts to present the Qur’anic message as something taken from other people.

The Qur’an reports this as the speaker’s claim, rather than affirming its truth.

Yuʾtharu’s occurrence therefore does not mean that the Qur’an describes itself as transmitted magic.

Siḥr belongs to another root. Yuʾtharu expresses only the claimed transmission.

“Magic handed down from predecessors” stays close to the sentence, but the verb itself does not identify a particular person or group as its source.

Athāratin min ʿilm: a remnant of knowledge

Al-Aḥqāf, 46:4

قُلْ أَرَأَيْتُمْ مَا تَدْعُونَ مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ أَرُونِي مَاذَا خَلَقُوا مِنَ الْأَرْضِ أَمْ لَهُمْ شِرْكٌ فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ ۖ ائْتُونِي بِكِتَابٍ مِنْ قَبْلِ هَٰذَا أَوْ أَثَارَةٍ مِنْ عِلْمٍ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ صَادِقِينَ

qul a-raʾaytum mā tadʿūna min dūni allāhi arūnī mādhā khalaqū mina al-arḍi am lahum shirkun fī al-samāwāt; iʾtūnī bi-kitābin min qabli hādhā aw athāratin min ʿilmin in kuntum ṣādiqīn

“Say: Have you considered what you invoke apart from The God? Show me what they have created of the earth, or what share they have in the heavens. Bring me a book preceding this or a remnant of knowledge, if you are truthful.”

The audience is asked to provide evidence for its claim.

The verse first asks what the deities they invoke have created of the earth, or what share they have in the heavens.

It then asks for a preceding book or athāratin min ʿilm.

Lexical sources explain athārah as a remnant or surviving trace of knowledge.

Some accounts understand knowledge transmitted from predecessors or a sign from which a truth can be discerned.

Some exegetical reports also connect athārah with lines or writing. That proposed instance should not become the word’s sole Qur’anic meaning.

Al-Qurṭubī reports أَثَرَة (atharah), with vowels on hamzah and thāʾ and no alif after thāʾ, from Al-Sulamī, Al-Ḥasan, and Abū Rajāʾ. This transmitted reading should be distinguished from athārah in the familiar reading; further reports give other vocalizations. These forms have been explained through remnants, transmission, or a share of knowledge.

Is every inherited report authoritative?

The verse requests athāratin min ʿilm: something actually connected with knowledge.

It does not make every old statement or transmitted report automatically valid evidence.

Something may survive from predecessors while its truth remains open to examination.

Min ʿilm, “of knowledge,” is a crucial qualification: the request is for a remnant of knowledge, rather than any unsupported trace or report.

Athārah should therefore not be translated merely as ancestral tradition.

Nor does the verse establish that historical precedent alone validates every religious claim.

The audience is specifically challenged to substantiate its deities’ alleged participation in creation.

Athāratin min ʿilm is therefore something that could be offered as evidence grounded in knowledge for that claim.

This usage is linguistically connected with yuʾtharu in Al-Muddaththir 74:24, but their roles differ: one reports a claim of transmission; the other requests evidence from knowledge.

6. Āthara: preferring, giving precedence, and altruism

Another branch concerns choosing or placing one thing before another.

The Qur’an uses this branch five times in Form IV.

Āthara does not mean ethical altruism in every occurrence.

Preferring Joseph over his brothers

Yūsuf, 12:91

قَالُوا تَاللَّهِ لَقَدْ آثَرَكَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْنَا وَإِنْ كُنَّا لَخَاطِئِينَ

qālū ta-allāhi la-qad ātharaka allāhu ʿalaynā wa-in kunnā la-khāṭiʾīn

“They said, ‘By The God, The God has preferred you over us, and we have indeed been at fault.’”

Joseph’s brothers acknowledge an advantage bestowed on him by The God.

Ātharaka concerns preferring or giving precedence here.

It does not mean giving away property or relinquishing a personal right.

The God is the subject, and Joseph the object.

Sacrifice for another person should therefore not be made an invariable element of āthara.

Refusing to prefer Pharaoh over the clear signs

Ṭā Hā, 20:72

قَالُوا لَنْ نُؤْثِرَكَ عَلَىٰ مَا جَاءَنَا مِنَ الْبَيِّنَاتِ وَالَّذِي فَطَرَنَا فَاقْضِ مَا أَنْتَ قَاضٍ ۖ إِنَّمَا تَقْضِي هَٰذِهِ الْحَيَاةَ الدُّنْيَا

qālū lan nuʾthiraka ʿalā mā jāʾanā mina al-bayyināti wa-alladhī faṭaranā fa-iqḍi mā anta qāḍin; innamā taqḍī hādhihi al-ḥayāta al-dunyā

“They said, ‘We will never prefer you over the clear signs that have reached us, or over the One who created us. So decree whatever you will: you decree only in this worldly life.’”

The speakers stand against Pharaoh.

They will not place him before the clear signs or their Creator.

Nuʾthiraka belongs to the same field of preference.

The verb is negated: they refuse to make that preference.

The meaning is more than “we do not love you.” They reject placing Pharaoh above the truth they have recognized.

Preference can thus concern judgment and choosing between two different directions.

Placing others before oneself

Al-Ḥashr, 59:9

وَالَّذِينَ تَبَوَّءُوا الدَّارَ وَالْإِيمَانَ مِنْ قَبْلِهِمْ يُحِبُّونَ مَنْ هَاجَرَ إِلَيْهِمْ وَلَا يَجِدُونَ فِي صُدُورِهِمْ حَاجَةً مِمَّا أُوتُوا وَيُؤْثِرُونَ عَلَىٰ أَنْفُسِهِمْ وَلَوْ كَانَ بِهِمْ خَصَاصَةٌ ۚ وَمَنْ يُوقَ شُحَّ نَفْسِهِ فَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْمُفْلِحُونَ

wa-alladhīna tabawwaʾū al-dāra wa-al-īmāna min qablihim yuḥibbūna man hājara ilayhim wa-lā yajidūna fī ṣudūrihim ḥājatan mimmā ūtū wa-yuʾthirūna ʿalā anfusihim wa-law kāna bihim khaṣāṣah; wa-man yūqa shuḥḥa nafsihi fa-ulāʾika humu al-mufliḥūn

“Those who had settled in the homeland and embraced faith before them love those who migrated to them, and find no desire in their hearts for what the migrants were given. They prefer others over themselves, even when they themselves are in need. Those protected from their own souls’ avarice are the successful.”

This is one of the root’s most important ethical uses.

Yuʾthirūna ʿalā anfusihim means placing others before themselves.

The qualification وَلَوْ كَانَ بِهِمْ خَصَاصَةٌ (wa-law kāna bihim khaṣāṣah) shows that they do so even when in need themselves.

Here preference involves relinquishing personal advantage and attending to others’ needs.

This makes the familiar contemporary ethical meaning of īthār, selflessness or altruism, readily understandable.

It does not make every Qur’anic āthara mean altruism or self-sacrifice.

ʿAlā anfusihim, “over themselves,” and their own need give preference its particular ethical character here.

The same verb can appear in a context of condemnation when someone prefers worldly life to the Hereafter.

Preferring worldly life

Al-Nāziʿāt, 79:38

وَآثَرَ الْحَيَاةَ الدُّنْيَا

wa-āthara al-ḥayāta al-dunyā

“And preferred worldly life.”

Al-Aʿlā, 87:16

بَلْ تُؤْثِرُونَ الْحَيَاةَ الدُّنْيَا

bal tuʾthirūna al-ḥayāta al-dunyā

“Rather, you prefer worldly life.”

These verses use the same verbal family as Al-Ḥashr 59:9.

This time the object of preference is worldly life.

In Al-Nāziʿāt, that preference accompanies transgression, and the following verses state the outcome.

In Al-Aʿlā, it is followed by a reminder that the Hereafter is better and more enduring.

A preference’s moral value therefore comes from its object and context.

Āthara itself does not mean “did a good deed.”

This is fundamental to a root study. The familiar ethical sense of īthār is positive; imposing that value on all Qur’anic occurrences would distort āthara al-ḥayāta al-dunyā, preferring worldly life.

Īthār and istiʾthār

Īthār can concern placing another person first.

Istiʾthār, by contrast, can mean reserving something for oneself and excluding others.

Both belong to one family, but the direction of preference differs.

In the īthār of Al-Ḥashr 59:9, a person gives another precedence over themselves.

In istiʾthār, someone may place themselves before others and seek an exclusive share or privilege.

Istiʾthār does not occur independently in the Qur’an and should not be counted among its attested derivatives.

7. Semantic distinctions and translation choices for Forqan

A TH R is close to several other Arabic families, but should remain distinct from them.

Athar is close to baqiyyah, “remnant,” since both can concern something remaining after its source has gone or ended. A trace need not, however, be a material piece of the original thing: a consequence or sign of presence can also be athar.

Study of B Q Y

Athar overlaps with ʿalāmah and āyah, “sign,” because a trace can indicate an existence or event. Not every sign is necessarily the remnant of an earlier event, nor is every āyah merely a footprint or residue.

Study of ʿ L M; Study of A Y Y

Athar, ithr, and qafw meet in the field of following. Qafā and iqtafā emphasize the act of following, whereas athar can be the mark or route followed.

Study of Q F W

Āthara is close to faḍḍala and ikhtāra in choosing or preferring. In āthara shayʾan ʿalā shayʾin, however, the relationship of placing one thing before another is particularly explicit.

Study of F Ḍ L; Study of KH Y R

A TH R must also remain distinct from TH W R, stirring up or overturning. Some discussions of athāratin min ʿilm speak of drawing out or stirring up knowledge, but that interpretive connection does not change the word’s root.

Study of TH W R

Translating the Qur’anic forms

Basic equivalents for athar and āthār are “trace,” “mark,” or “remnant”; in movement constructions, “footsteps” or “following” may be more natural.

In Al-Kahf 18:64, “they turned back, retracing their footsteps” is suitable.

In Ṭā Hā 20:84, “they are following me” is more natural than “they are on my trace.”

In Al-Māʾidah 5:46 and Al-Ḥadīd 57:27, “We sent after them” clarifies the succession of messengers.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:70, Al-Zukhruf 43:22, and Al-Zukhruf 43:23, “following their path” or “following the ways of predecessors” is suitable. Athar should not invariably be made to mean religion or religious law.

In Ghāfir 40:21 and Ghāfir 40:82, “their surviving traces on earth” or “their works and signs of development” is appropriate.

In Al-Rūm 30:50, “traces of the mercy of The God” preserves the meaning. Growing plants and revived land are instances in context.

In Yā Sīn 36:12, “what they have sent ahead and their traces” is suitable. Explanation can include consequences and signs remaining after human deeds.

In Al-Fatḥ 48:29, “the trace of prostration” is more precise than restricting the translation to a calloused forehead.

In Ṭā Hā 20:96, “a trace of the messenger” is preferable pending separate investigation of the messenger’s identity and the trace’s nature.

For yuʾtharu in Al-Muddaththir 74:24, “is transmitted” or “is handed down from predecessors” is suitable, rather than “is preferred.”

For athāratin min ʿilm in Al-Aḥqāf 46:4, “a remnant of knowledge” or “a trace of knowledge” is appropriate. “Evidence from earlier knowledge” can clarify its contextual role, without treating every old report as reliable knowledge or valid proof.

For āthara, nuʾthira, yuʾthirūna, and tuʾthirūna, the basic equivalents are “prefer” and “give precedence.”

In Yūsuf 12:91, “The God has preferred you over us” is suitable.

In Ṭā Hā 20:72, “We will never prefer you over the clear signs or our Creator” is suitable.

In Al-Ḥashr 59:9, “they prefer others over themselves” preserves the selfless sense.

In Al-Nāziʿāt 79:38 and Al-Aʿlā 87:16, “preferred worldly life” and “you prefer worldly life” are suitable respectively.

Brief meanings for word-by-word boxes

  • أَثَر (athar): a mark, footprint, or remnant, depending on context.
  • آثَار (āthār): traces, footprints, remnants, or consequences.
  • أَثَارَة (athārah): a remnant or trace; in Al-Aḥqāf 46:4, of knowledge.
  • يُؤْثَرُ (yuʾtharu): is transmitted or retold from another.
  • آثَرَ (āthara): preferred or gave precedence.
  • يُؤْثِرُونَ (yuʾthirūna): they prefer or give precedence; in Al-Ḥashr 59:9, to others over themselves.

These equivalents must remain distinct. Translation cannot be determined from the root letters alone: morphology and sentence structure help establish the final meaning.

8. Conclusion

A TH R has a broad semantic range, but its usages fall into several clear branches.

The noun athar denotes a mark surviving an existence, movement, or event. It may be a footprint, a building’s remains, a mark on the face, a result in nature, or a consequence of human action.

The same field includes following someone and continuing predecessors’ ways, whether physically retracing a route or continuing a practice.

Following earlier traces is not inherently good or bad in the Qur’an. Messengers can follow earlier messengers, while others may rely on ancestral ways without sufficient reason. Its value depends on what is followed and what supports its validity.

Another branch concerns transmitting and preserving reports and knowledge. Yuʾtharu in Al-Muddaththir 74:24 concerns a speaker’s claim that something is taken from others. Al-Aḥqāf 46:4 asks for a surviving trace of knowledge supporting the audience’s claim.

Transmission alone does not establish a statement’s truth.

Form IV āthara means preferring or giving precedence. In Al-Ḥashr 59:9, others are preferred over oneself despite personal need. In Al-Nāziʿāt 79:38 and Al-Aʿlā 87:16, the same verb describes preferring worldly life.

The positive ethical sense of contemporary īthār should therefore not be imposed on every form in the family.

The family connects traces, following, transmission, and precedence, but those connections should not be forced into an invented image or certain historical derivation.

For Forqan, the basic definition is: Athar is a surviving mark or something indicating an existence or passage. In movement constructions, it can denote someone’s path or trail. In transmitting knowledge and reports, it concerns what has come down and remained from predecessors. Āthara means choosing or placing one thing or person before another.

This definition accommodates all 21 Qur’anic occurrences without equating physical footprints with doctrinal imitation, transmitted reports with valid proof, or preference with ethical selflessness.
