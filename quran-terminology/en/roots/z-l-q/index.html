Core meaning: slipping and losing a stable footing or position; transitively, making something slip, displacing it, or driving it from its place.

1. The root and its meaning in early Arabic

Z L Q concerns slipping, instability, and loss of secure position. It appears in a slipping foot, slippery ground, a smooth surface, and something being displaced.

زَلِقَتِ الْقَدَمُ means that the foot slipped and could not remain secure. أَرْضٌ زَلَقٌ describes ground so slippery or smooth that a foot cannot hold firm.

Transitively, زَلَقَهُ and أَزْلَقَهُ can mean making someone or something slip, displacing it, or moving it away.

An early lexical explanation describes the root as follows:

أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى تَزَلُّجِ الشَّيْءِ عَنْ مَقَامِهِ

aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā tazalluji al-shayʾi ʿan maqāmihi

Meaning: “One root indicating something slipping away from its position.”

Smoothness alone is not the point: loss of stability and displacement are central.

The displacement may be involuntary, as with a slipping foot, or caused by another, as when someone is made to slip or displaced.

The root before Islam

A dictionary definition must be distinguished from independent historical evidence when establishing pre-Quranic meaning.

Early lexicons record slipping and instability, and both Quranic uses fit that field. The evidence examined here does not establish an independently dated pre-Islamic poetic witness for the root.

Later dictionary definitions alone cannot establish that every recorded form and sense already existed before the Quran.

A post-Islamic poetic witness clearly illustrates the physical meaning.

A verse attributed to Muhammad ibn Yasir: slippery ground

This verse is attributed to Muhammad ibn Yasir al-Riyashi, a poet of the Abbasid period; he should not be confused with the Umayyad poet Muhammad ibn Bashir:

أَبْصِرْ لِرِجْلِكَ قَبْلَ الْخَطْوِ مَوْضِعَهَا
فَمَنْ عَلَا زَلَقًا عَنْ غِرَّةٍ زَلِجَا

abṣir li-rijlika qabla al-khaṭwi mawḍiʿahā / fa-man ʿalā zalaqan ʿan gharratin zalajā

Translation:

“Look where your foot will land before taking a step; whoever unsuspectingly steps onto slippery ground will slip.”

زَلَقًا denotes a slippery place requiring care when stepping onto it.

The poet uses a physical experience to express the need to consider one’s course and its consequences.

The ground’s defining feature here is the absence of secure footing.

On this attribution, the witness is Abbasid rather than Umayyad or pre-Islamic.

The evidence supports the following definition:

In early Arabic, zalaq denotes slipping and loss of secure position. Applied to ground, it describes a surface on which the foot cannot remain firm; transitively, the root denotes making someone or something slip or displacing it.

This defines the attested semantic field without asserting when each derivative first arose.

2. Important forms and derivatives

زَلِقَ يَزْلَقُ

This Form I verb means to slip or lose stability.

زَلِقَتْ رِجْلُهُ

zaliqat rijluhu

Meaning: “His foot slipped.”

The foot loses its secure position.

The verb does not necessarily mean that the whole person falls: someone can slip and regain balance.

زَلَق and زَلْق

These recorded verbal nouns concern slipping and instability.

Zalaq also describes a place or ground:

مَكَانٌ زَلَقٌ

makānun zalaqun

Meaning: “A slippery place.”

A verbal noun here serves descriptively to characterize the place.

This is the branch used in Al-Kahf 18:40.

زَلِق

It describes something or somewhere slippery.

أَرْضٌ زَلِقَةٌ

arḍun zaliqatun

Meaning: “Slippery ground.”

The surface’s quality is at issue, not necessarily movement of the ground itself.

أَزْلَقَ يُزْلِقُ

This Form IV verb means to make slip, displace, or cause another to lose footing.

أَزْلَقَ قَدَمَهُ

azlaqa qadamahu

Meaning: “He made his foot slip.”

أَزْلَقَهُ عَنْ مَكَانِهِ

azlaqahu ʿan makānihi

Meaning: “He displaced him or drove him away from his place.”

The construction distinguishes the cause of slipping from what undergoes it.

This verb occurs in Al-Qalam 68:51.

زَلَقَهُ

Arabic also records transitive Form I زَلَقَهُ for making someone slip or displacing them.

Causing slipping is therefore not confined to Form IV.

This matters for the readings of Al-Qalam 68:51: one uses transitive Form I, another Form IV.

زَلَّقَ يُزَلِّقُ

This Form II verb can mean making a surface smooth and slippery.

زَلَّقَ الْمَكَانَ

zallaqa al-makāna

Meaning: “He smoothed the place so that it became slippery.”

In another use:

زَلَّقَ رَأْسَهُ

zallaqa raʾsahu

Meaning: “He shaved his head smooth.”

The skin’s smoothness after hair removal is prominent.

Several forms are recorded for this shaving expression; none should be assumed to be the certain historical origin of the root’s other senses.

تَزَلَّقَ

One lexical use describes grooming and making the body smooth and glossy with oil or similar substances.

تَزَلَّقَ الرَّجُلُ

tazallaqa al-rajulu

It can describe a man oiling and grooming himself.

This concerns surface smoothness and slipperiness, but does not occur in the Quran.

مَزْلَق and مَزْلَقَة

These denote a place where the foot cannot remain firm.

مَزْلَقَةُ الْقَدَمِ

mazlaqatu al-qadami

Meaning: “A place where the foot slips.”

More broadly, the word can describe a dangerous situation in which someone risks error or losing their position.

That figurative use should not replace the root’s physical meaning in the relevant Quranic context.

زَلَّاقَة

It denotes slippery ground or a slippery surface.

Again, lack of secure footing is prominent.

زَلُوق

It describes a swiftly moving animal, especially a camel.

Speed is involved here but is not the meaning of every derivative.

أَزْلَقَتِ النَّاقَةُ

The expression is recorded for a camel losing its fetus before gestation is complete.

Here something is expelled before its usual time.

A semantic connection with leaving or being separated from a position is conceivable, but does not establish the term’s historical origin.

مِزْلَاق

As an implement, it denotes a piece used to fasten or open a door by moving or sliding it.

Some sources record it as a form related to مِزْلَاج.

It does not occur in the Quran and has no direct role in defining the two Quranic forms.

The root occurs twice, in two verses. In the Hafs transmission examined here, its forms are زَلَقًا and لَيُزْلِقُونَكَ.

3. First Quranic use: slippery ground and lost cultivation

Al-Kahf 18:40

فَعَسَىٰ رَبِّي أَن يُؤْتِيَنِ خَيْرًا مِّن جَنَّتِكَ وَيُرْسِلَ عَلَيْهَا حُسْبَانًا مِّنَ السَّمَاءِ فَتُصْبِحَ صَعِيدًا زَلَقًا

fa-ʿasā rabbī an yuʾtiyani khayran min jannatika wa-yursila ʿalayhā ḥusbānan mina al-samāʾi fa-tuṣbiḥa ṣaʿīdan zalaqā

Al-Kahf 18:40

Translation:

“Perhaps my Lord will give me something better than your garden and send a calamity upon it from the sky, so that it becomes smooth, slippery ground.”

زَلَقًا describes صَعِيدًا.

Ṣaʿīd denotes the ground’s surface; zalaq characterizes it.

The phrase pictures ground made smooth and slippery, no longer retaining its former cultivated, fertile condition.

Lexical explanations also describe it as smooth ground stripped of vegetation.

The word’s meaning must nevertheless be distinguished from the contextual outcome.

Zalaq does not independently mean a garden’s destruction or drought. Bare smooth ground is recorded in explanations of this expression, while the loss of the garden’s cultivation emerges from the whole story; it is not a necessary feature of every derivative.

The story of the two gardens

Al-Kahf 18:32 introduces two men, one possessing two vineyards and agricultural resources.

In Al-Kahf 18:34, the owner boasts of greater wealth and supporters.

In Al-Kahf 18:35, he enters his garden and imagines it will never perish.

In Al-Kahf 18:36, he doubts the Hour and assumes that even if returned to his Lord, he will find something better.

His companion speaks of human creation, the oneness of The God, and the insecurity of absolute reliance on wealth.

Al-Kahf 18:40 then raises the possibility of the garden’s loss and transformation into smooth, slippery ground.

In context, ṣaʿīdan zalaqan pictures the loss of cultivation and material blessing.

Land formerly containing trees, crops, and irrigation can become a surface stripped of that prosperity.

This follows from the story, not because zalaq lexically means poverty, destruction, or punishment.

Does zalaq mean wet ground?

Many everyday surfaces become slippery through water, mud, or a soft material covering them.

Slipperiness need not depend on moisture: smooth stone, loose soil, or stripped ground can also provide insecure footing.

Al-Kahf 18:40 does not specify that the resulting ground must be wet or muddy.

Al-Kahf 18:41 offers another possibility, without settling whether the surface described previously is wet or dry:

أَوْ يُصْبِحَ مَاؤُهَا غَوْرًا فَلَن تَسْتَطِيعَ لَهُ طَلَبًا

aw yuṣbiḥa māʾuhā ghawran fa-lan tastaṭīʿa lahu ṭalabā

Al-Kahf 18:41

Translation:

“Or its water may sink away, so that you cannot reach it.”

This describes water sinking beyond reach.

Translating zalaq as wet, muddy ground therefore adds details not established by the word or passage.

“Smooth and slippery,” or “ground offering no secure footing,” is more suitable.

Slipperiness and absence of vegetation

Al-Kahf 18:8 describes what is on the earth becoming صَعِيدًا جُرُزًا.

Juruz concerns ground devoid of vegetation.

Al-Kahf 18:40 instead uses صَعِيدًا زَلَقًا.

The resulting images are close: both can depict ground stripped of cultivation and cover.

The roots and central meanings nevertheless differ.

Juruz emphasizes the absence or removal of vegetation.

Zalaq emphasizes smoothness, slipperiness, and insecure footing.

These features can coexist in the garden story without making the roots fully synonymous.

4. Second Quranic use: looks that almost bring someone down

Al-Qalam 68:51

وَإِن يَكَادُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا لَيُزْلِقُونَكَ بِأَبْصَارِهِمْ لَمَّا سَمِعُوا الذِّكْرَ وَيَقُولُونَ إِنَّهُ لَمَجْنُونٌ

wa-in yakādu alladhīna kafarū la-yuzliqūnaka bi-abṣārihim lammā samiʿū al-dhikra wa-yaqūlūna innahu la-majnūn

Al-Qalam 68:51

Translation:

“Those who disbelieve almost bring you down with their looks when they hear the Reminder, and they say: He is surely mad.”

In the Hafs transmission, لَيُزْلِقُونَكَ is a present-tense Form IV verb.

Its subject is الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا.

The pronoun كَ refers to the person addressed.

بِأَبْصَارِهِمْ identifies the means or cause: their looks.

The root extends here from physical slipping to displacing or bringing someone down.

The word itself does not mean killing, casting a spell, or causing harm through an invisible force.

Its core is making someone slip or displacing them; the construction and context inform how the image is understood.

Why are looks presented as causing slipping?

Arabic can use an intense gaze to express anger, hostility, and psychological pressure.

Persian likewise has expressions such as “His look almost brought me down” and “He wanted to destroy me with his gaze.”

Such expressions do not necessarily attribute an independent physical force to the eye capable of throwing a person.

In Al-Qalam 68:51, the deniers look at the addressee upon hearing the Reminder as though they would dislodge him.

The same verse then records their calling him mad.

The connection places their gaze within hostility to the Quranic message.

Al-Qalam 68:52 responds:

وَمَا هُوَ إِلَّا ذِكْرٌ لِّلْعَالَمِينَ

wa-mā huwa illā dhikrun li-al-ʿālamīn

Al-Qalam 68:52

Translation:

“It is nothing but a reminder for all peoples.”

The ending contrasts the opponents’ statement with the Quran’s role.

In the two verses, the hostile gaze and accusation of madness are two forms of opposition to the message.

Is this about the evil eye?

One interpretation in early commentary holds that the opponents sought to harm the messenger with their eyes.

This became associated with belief in the evil eye.

The verse itself neither uses ʿayn as a technical term for the evil eye nor explains a mechanism by which eyes cause harm.

It says they almost make the addressee slip or bring him down with their looks.

Linguistically, this accommodates a deeply hostile, threatening gaze.

The evil-eye interpretation is also historically attested, but the expression alone does not establish that the verse necessarily describes an independent invisible physical effect.

Nor does it establish that every envious or angry gaze certainly causes illness or death.

The verse provides no such universal rule or specified mechanism.

Conversely, a rhetorical reading does not justify omitting بِأَبْصَارِهِمْ from translation: the looks are explicitly part of the image and must remain.

The role of يَكَادُ

The expression begins:

وَإِن يَكَادُ

wa-in yakādu

Al-Qalam 68:51

“It is almost the case that…”

This construction expresses an action approaching occurrence.

The verse therefore does not say that the opponents actually succeeded in bringing the messenger down with their looks.

It describes an intensity approaching that outcome.

This matters for translation.

Rendering the verb as an accomplished event would erase the difference between almost happening and actually happening.

Differences of reading

In the Hafs transmission of Asim used here, لَيُزْلِقُونَكَ has ḍammah on the yāʾ.

This is Form IV, from أَزْلَقَ.

Another reading has لَيَزْلِقُونَكَ, with fatḥah on yāʾ, from transitive Form I زَلَقَهُ.

Both forms are recorded for making another slip or displacing them.

The difference concerns morphology, while the central meanings remain close.

Both describe people who almost dislodge the addressee with their looks.

5. The connection between the Quranic uses

In Al-Kahf 18:40, زَلَقًا characterizes ground.

In Al-Qalam 68:51, لَيُزْلِقُونَكَ expresses an action people almost inflict on someone.

The subjects differ: land and a garden in one, opponents confronting the messenger in the other.

A clear lexical connection nevertheless exists.

The first use describes ground without secure footing.

The second describes opponents seeking to dislodge the addressee from stability or position.

Both involve loss of secure footing or placement.

Zalaqan describes a surface, whereas yuzliqūnaka is transitive and concerns acting on another.

Their moral settings also differ.

Slippery ground has no intention or moral conduct of its own.

In Al-Qalam 68:51, human agents act in hostility and denial, making their conduct morally assessable.

Zalaq does not inherently mean wickedness, envy, or destruction. Where applicable, those notions come from context and agency.

6. Related roots

Z L Q (زَلَق) and Z L L (زَلَل)

Zalal also concerns slipping and loss of stability.

زَلَّتِ الْقَدَمُ and زَلِقَتِ الْقَدَمُ can describe the same physical event.

The Quran also uses Z L L for departing from the right course and falling into error.

Al-Baqarah 2:209 mentions زَلَلْتُمْ after clear signs have come.

The Quranic uses of Z L Q concern slippery ground and making a person slip or displacing them.

This does not exclude nonphysical use: Al-Qalam 68:51 itself extends the root into human conduct.

Z L Q (زَلَق) and D Ḥ Ḍ (دَحْض)

Daḥḍ also concerns slipping and instability.

Slippery ground can be described as دَحْض.

The Quran also uses this root for seeking to invalidate truth or undermine an argument.

Al-Kahf 18:56 describes disputing with falsehood to nullify truth.

In that use, the root expresses displacing and discrediting.

Z L Q in Al-Qalam 68:51 also concerns displacing someone, but the objects and constructions differ.

Features of disputation and reasoning should not be transferred wholesale into zalaq’s definition.

Z L Q (زَلَق) and Z L J (زَلَج)

Zalaj concerns slipping and swift, smooth movement.

In the quoted poem, zalaqan denotes slippery ground and zalajā is the verb expressing slipping.

Their pairing illustrates semantic affinity.

Their consonants differ, however, and one should not be called a transformed version of the other without historical evidence.

Z L Q (زَلَق) and J R Z (جُرُز)

Juruz concerns land without vegetation or the loss of plant cover.

Zalaq concerns slipperiness and a smooth surface.

Al-Kahf 18:8 and Al-Kahf 18:40 describe land stripped of its former condition.

The semantic distinction should remain.

Absence of vegetation is prominent in juruz; slipperiness and lack of firm footing in zalaq.

The properties can coexist without being identical.

Z L Q (زَلَق) and Ṣ L D (صَلْد)

Ṣald concerns a hard, smooth stone surface.

Al-Baqarah 2:264 describes a smooth rock covered by soil; rain removes the soil and leaves the rock bare.

The exposed smooth surface relates to the image in Al-Kahf 18:40.

Ṣald emphasizes the hard, bare surface, whereas zalaq emphasizes slipperiness and insecure footing.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

The following equivalents suit important forms. These research proposals do not replace installed verse translations or existing authored word-box explanations.

زَلِقَ: slipped or lost secure footing.

زَلَق: slipping; a slippery surface when describing a place.

زَلِق: slippery or unstable.

أَزْلَقَ: made slip, displaced, or drove from a position.

زَلَقَهُ: made him slip or displaced him.

زَلَّقَ: made smooth and slippery; shaved the head smooth in the relevant use.

تَزَلَّقَ: oiled and polished oneself in the bodily use.

مَزْلَق and مَزْلَقَة: a place of slipping or insecure footing.

زَلَّاقَة: slippery ground or a slippery place.

زَلُوق: swift-moving, when describing an animal.

مِزْلَاق: a sliding door-fastening implement in the relevant use.

Proposed rendering of Al-Kahf 18:40

فَتُصْبِحَ صَعِيدًا زَلَقًا

fa-tuṣbiḥa ṣaʿīdan zalaqā

Al-Kahf 18:40

“So that it becomes smooth, slippery ground.”

A brief root meaning:

“Slipping and loss of stability.”

A verse-specific explanation:

“Smooth, slippery ground on which the foot cannot remain firm.”

Explain the loss of the garden’s trees and crops contextually, rather than making it zalaq’s general definition.

Proposed rendering of Al-Qalam 68:51

وَإِن يَكَادُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا لَيُزْلِقُونَكَ بِأَبْصَارِهِمْ

wa-in yakādu alladhīna kafarū la-yuzliqūnaka bi-abṣārihim

Al-Qalam 68:51

“Those who disbelieve almost bring you down with their looks.”

A brief root meaning:

“Making someone slip or displacing them.”

A verse-specific explanation:

“With hostile looks, they almost dislodge you from your position.”

“Bring down” is a natural rendering of the extended verbal meaning here.

Hostility follows from the verse and context; envy or the evil eye should not be presented as the root’s direct meaning.

8. Conclusion

Z L Q concerns slipping, loss of stability, and displacement.

Physically, it appears in a slipping foot and smooth, slippery ground.

Transitively, it extends to making someone or something slip or displacing them.

The evidence examined here does not establish an independently dated pre-Islamic poetic witness. Slipping and insecure placement are nevertheless well attested in early lexicons and both Quranic uses.

Al-Kahf 18:40 describes smooth, slippery ground as a possible outcome of the garden’s loss of cultivation.

Al-Qalam 68:51 describes opponents looking so hostilely at the addressee upon hearing the Reminder that they almost bring him down.

Despite differing subjects, both share the notion of lost stability.

The first passage does not make destruction or loss of vegetation the root’s independent general meaning; nor does the second make envy or a physical evil-eye effect part of its lexical definition.

The root concerns slipping and loss of secure position; transitively, it denotes making another slip or displacing them.

This includes the physical description of ground and the extended description of human conduct without replacing lexical meaning with contextual outcomes or interpretations.
