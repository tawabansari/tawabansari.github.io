Caravan, movement, reproach, and other semantic branches

1. The root in early Arabic

Arabic ʿ–y–r has several semantic branches, chiefly concerning movement and coming and going, caravans and beasts of burden, reproach and fault-finding, and raised features on objects.

In early Arabic, ʿīr designated a caravan traveling with pack animals, usually transporting goods or provisions. It could describe the animals collectively or a caravan including the people traveling with them.

ʿAyr, with an a-vowel, named the donkey, particularly the male, whether wild or domesticated. By contrast, caravan-related ʿīr, with an i-vowel, referred to a group of pack animals and their companions.

Lexicons differ over the word's range. Some restrict it to camels carrying provisions; others also record donkeys, mules, and other pack animals.

These differing reports show that some recorded uses extend beyond a single animal species. The relevant caravan sense involves a traveling group with means of carrying loads, although some lexicographers give a narrower definition.

A pre-Islamic poetic witness concerning a caravan

A verse attributed both to al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī and to Aws ibn Ḥajar, two pre-Islamic poets, reads:

وَدِّعْ أُمَامَةَ وَالتَّوْدِيعُ تَعْذِيرُ
وَمَا وَدَاعُكَ مَنْ فَضَّتْ بِهِ الْعِيرُ

waddiʿ umāmata wa-al-tawdīʿu taʿdhīru / wa-mā wadāʿuka man faḍḍat bihi al-ʿīru

Translation: Bid Umāmah farewell, though farewell is only an excuse; what use is your farewell to someone whom the caravan has carried away from you?

Al-ʿīr here refers to the caravan that has taken the person away.

The use is clear: ʿīr is a traveling group. The poet's attention is directed toward the people who have left with it, rather than the pack animals themselves.

The verse has variant wordings and a disputed attribution between al-Nābighah and Aws ibn Ḥajar. It can therefore be treated as a witness attributed to pre-Islamic poetry, without asserting a certain attribution to either poet.

A witness concerning reproach

Poetry attributed to al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī contains:

وَعَيَّرَتْنِي بَنُو ذُبْيَانَ خَشْيَتَهُ
وَهَلْ عَلَيَّ بِأَنْ أَخْشَاكَ مِنْ عَارِ؟

wa-ʿayyaratnī banū dhubyāna khashyatahu / wa-hal ʿalayya bi-an akhshāka min ʿāri

Translation: Banū Dhubyān reproached me for fearing him; is there any disgrace upon me for fearing you?

ʿAyyaratnī here means reproaching someone and attributing a fault to them.

The poet reports that a group condemned his fear, while he himself does not regard that fear as disgraceful.

This shows that finding fault does not necessarily establish a real fault. Others may condemn conduct that the person reproached considers right or reasonable.

ʿAyyara, the act of reproaching, should therefore be distinguished from ʿār, disgrace or a social fault. The former names the action; the latter names what is regarded as disgraceful.

Do all the meanings have one origin?

Some lexical analyses propose two main axes for this family:

First, movement and coming and going.

Second, something projecting or rising above its surroundings.

In the first branch, forms such as ʿāra yaʿīru concern movement. In the second, ʿayr is also recorded for a projecting bone or the raised part of certain objects.

It is not clear, however, that these branches historically developed from one another.

Nor can reproach or measurement be derived with certainty from animal movement or projecting bones without independent evidence.

The documented branches should therefore be distinguished rather than merged into a fabricated image.

2. Principal forms and derivatives

Arabic recognizes various forms in this family.

ʿĪr: a caravan of pack animals and their companions; “caravan” in Quranic use.

ʿAyr: a donkey, particularly the male, whether domesticated or wild.

Aʿyār: a plural of ʿayr in the animal sense.

ʿĪrāt: a plural of ʿīr meaning caravan.

ʿĀra yaʿīru: to move, come and go, or wander, according to context.

ʿĀʾir: someone or something moving about; in some constructions, an animal that has broken loose and wanders.

ʿAyyara: to reproach someone or attribute a fault to them.

Taʿyīr: reproach, condemnation, and fault-finding.

Taʿāyara: to reproach one another or attribute faults to each other.

ʿĀr: disgrace, a social fault, or something for which a person is reproached.

ʿIyār: a standard, measuring instrument, or quantity used for measurement.

Miʿyār: something used for measurement and comparison.

ʿĀyara: to measure or compare two things to establish equality or difference, such as comparing two measures or weights.

Other forms and uses are recorded. For example, ʿayr can name a projecting bone or the central ridge of a blade.

These should not be equated with Quranic “caravan.”

Forms actually occurring in the Quran

The Quran uses this root only in the noun ʿīr.

It occurs in three verses of Yūsuf:

  • Yūsuf 12:70: al-ʿīru.
  • Yūsuf 12:82: wa-al-ʿīra.
  • Yūsuf 12:94: al-ʿīru.

The differences concern the conjunction and grammatical case, not three separate derivatives.

Forms such as ʿayyara, taʿyīr, ʿār, ʿiyār, and miʿyār do not occur in the Quran.

The focus of Quranic study is therefore ʿīr meaning caravan.

3. Quranic uses of “ʿīr”

First occurrence: addressing the caravan

فَلَمَّا جَهَّزَهُمْ بِجَهَازِهِمْ جَعَلَ السِّقَايَةَ فِي رَحْلِ أَخِيهِ ثُمَّ أَذَّنَ مُؤَذِّنٌ أَيَّتُهَا الْعِيرُ إِنَّكُمْ لَسَارِقُونَ

fa-lammā jahhazahum bi-jahāzihim jaʿala al-siqāyata fī raḥli akhīhi thumma adhdhana muʾadhdhinun ayyatuhā al-ʿīru innakum la-sāriqūn

Yūsuf 12:70

Translation: When he had equipped them with their supplies, he placed the drinking cup in his brother's baggage. Then a caller announced, “O caravan, you are indeed thieves.”

Yūsuf 12:70

After Joseph's brothers have received their baggage and supplies, a caller addresses them. The accusation is part of the reported call; the word ʿīr itself neither means “thieves” nor establishes the accusation's truth.

Ayyatuhā al-ʿīru means “O caravan.”

The sentence outwardly addresses the caravan, but clearly intends the people traveling with it.

Several linguistic features are significant.

First, al-ʿīr is addressed with ayyatuhā, consistent with the noun's feminine grammatical usage in Arabic.

Second, the masculine plural pronoun in innakum immediately follows.

This shift shows that, although ʿīr can be grammatically feminine, the actual addressees are the caravan's people.

Thus ʿīr here does not name only pack animals; it encompasses the caravan and its companions.

Must “ʿīr” be a commercial caravan?

Arabic ʿīr has often been associated with carrying goods and provisions.

Joseph's brothers also travel to obtain provisions in this story. A pack caravan therefore fits the context.

The word alone does not establish that every member was a professional merchant or that trade was the journey's sole purpose.

A group might travel to obtain provisions or accomplish a particular task using pack animals.

“Caravan” is therefore more precise than “trading caravan” for the base translation unless the context emphasizes commerce.

Second occurrence: the caravan as a witness

وَاسْأَلِ الْقَرْيَةَ الَّتِي كُنَّا فِيهَا وَالْعِيرَ الَّتِي أَقْبَلْنَا فِيهَا وَإِنَّا لَصَادِقُونَ

wa-isʾali al-qaryata allatī kunnā fīhā wa-al-ʿīra allatī aqbalnā fīhā wa-innā la-ṣādiqūn

Yūsuf 12:82

Translation: Ask the settlement where we were and the caravan with which we came; we are indeed telling the truth.

Yūsuf 12:82

Here Joseph's brothers want their father to be able to verify their account.

They point to two sources: the settlement where they stayed and the caravan with which they returned.

Wa-al-ʿīra allatī aqbalnā fīhā is especially significant.

The caravan is not merely a collection of pack animals here, since it is presented as a source to question and consult about the report.

Asking the caravan therefore means asking the people present in it who know about the incident.

This also relates to the preceding wa-isʾali al-qaryah.

“Ask the settlement” means asking its people, not its buildings or land.

The same manner of expression is used for the caravan.

The word's relationship to the caravan's people

In Yūsuf 12:70, the caller addresses the caravan because its people are the intended audience.

In Yūsuf 12:82, it is a source for verifying the report because its people can testify about the incident.

In both verses, ʿīr designates a human group together with its means of travel.

The Quranic word is therefore not limited to cargo or animals. Its collective use includes the people traveling with the caravan.

That does not mean its primary sense is simply “people.” Its distinguishing feature remains the caravan and group travel.

Third occurrence: the caravan's departure

وَلَمَّا فَصَلَتِ الْعِيرُ قَالَ أَبُوهُمْ إِنِّي لَأَجِدُ رِيحَ يُوسُفَ لَوْلَا أَنْ تُفَنِّدُونِ

wa-lammā faṣalati al-ʿīru qāla abūhum innī la-ajidu rīḥa yūsufa law-lā an tufannidūn

Yūsuf 12:94

Translation: When the caravan departed, their father said, “I perceive Joseph's scent, if you do not consider me foolish.”

Yūsuf 12:94

Al-ʿīr is the subject of faṣalat here.

Faṣalati al-ʿīru describes the caravan setting off and leaving its previous location.

This again shows that ʿīr concerns a mobile group.

Faṣalat is also feminine singular, consistent with the grammatical use of al-ʿīr.

The immediate subject is the caravan's departure, rather than cargo, trade, or animal species.

Jacob then speaks of perceiving Joseph's scent. That is a separate part of the verse and should not be added to the lexical definition of ʿīr.

The word alone does not disclose how the scent was perceived, the distance between Jacob and the caravan, or the mechanism of the event.

The three Quranic occurrences thus show three aspects of one concept: a caravan addressed, a caravan whose companions can testify, and a caravan departing.

4. Other semantic branches

ʿAyr: the male donkey

Arabic ʿayr, with an a-vowel, designates a male donkey, whether domesticated or wild.

This use is recognized in early poetry and expressions.

For example, in in dhahaba ʿayrun fa-ʿayrun fī al-ribāṭ, one animal has gone while another remains tethered in its place.

The proverb expresses the availability of a replacement for something lost.

Some lexical analyses connect this animal's name with its movement and coming and going.

That explanation alone does not establish a certain history of naming.

Nor should caravan-related ʿīr be assumed to derive in every use from the donkey's name. Some lexicographers propose a connection, but the historical relationship requires independent evidence.

ʿĀr and taʿyīr: disgrace and reproach

In another branch, ʿār denotes something regarded as a source of disgrace, blame, or dishonor.

Taʿyīr is the act of reproaching someone for conduct, a characteristic, or a condition.

The concepts are close but not identical.

Someone may feel shame about an act without being reproached; another may be reproached unjustly for conduct that is not actually objectionable.

The witness attributed to al-Nābighah illustrates the distinction: he reports being reproached but rejects its justification.

Taʿyīr is therefore the act of attributing a fault, not proof of a fault's existence.

This distinction should be preserved when translating reproach-related words.

ʿIyār and muʿāyarah: measurement and comparison

ʿIyār denotes a standard or something used to assess another thing's quantity, weight, or quality.

Muʿāyarah concerns comparing two instruments or quantities to establish equality or difference.

Two measuring vessels might, for example, be compared to determine whether both measure the same amount.

The subject here is measurement and evaluation.

A recognized use of ʿiyār also concerns determining the proportion of pure material in metals and coins; precisely dating that use requires separate historical evidence.

These measurement-related uses do not occur in the Quran.

Nor does the shared root justify deriving “standard” necessarily from caravan movement or reproaching people.

Projection and raised features

Arabic also records ʿayr for certain projecting parts of bones or blades.

A distinct bony prominence or raised ridge in the middle of a blade may, for example, be described with this word.

This differs in subject matter from caravan movement and human reproach.

Some lexicographers present it separately from the coming-and-going branch.

A certain historical relationship should therefore not be invented without evidence.

5. Comparison with related roots

ʿ–Y–R (ʿīr) compared with S–Y–R (sayr)

Quranic s–y–r concerns movement, going, and traveling along a route.

In Yūsuf 12:19, sayyārah describes a group of travelers.

This is close to ʿīr in terms of group travel.

There is nevertheless a distinction.

Sayyārah denotes a traveling group, while the established Arabic use of ʿīr has a closer connection with caravans and pack animals.

An ʿīr may also be a sayyārah because it is a traveling caravan, but overlap does not make the words exact synonyms.

ʿ–Y–R compared with R–K–B (rakb)

R–k–b concerns riding, being positioned upon something, and using a mount or means of transport.

Quranic rakb refers to a group of riders.

In Al-Anfāl 8:42, al-rakb describes the position of a mounted group.

An ʿīr may include riders, but its distinguishing Quranic feature is the caravan as a whole.

Rakb thus emphasizes riding, while ʿīr emphasizes the caravan group, though one actual group may fit both descriptions.

ʿ–Y–R compared with M–Y–R (mīrah)

M–y–r concerns obtaining provisions and supplying necessary food.

In Yūsuf 12:65, namīru refers to obtaining provisions for the family.

This relates to the brothers' journey, since they travel to Egypt to obtain necessities.

Mīrah and ʿīr do not, however, share a root.

Mīrah concerns provisions and obtaining them, whereas ʿīr denotes the caravan.

A caravan may carry provisions, but transporting food and the caravan itself are distinct concepts.

The words' similarity of sound must not merge m–y–r with ʿ–y–r.

ʿ–Y–R compared with B–ʿ–R (baʿīr)

Quranic baʿīr denotes a camel.

It also occurs in Yūsuf 12:72, in the same part of the story concerning the missing measuring vessel.

Baʿīr names the animal, while Quranic ʿīr names the caravan.

Some recorded Arabic uses of ʿīr extend beyond camels to other pack animals; that breadth should be distinguished from the narrower definitions of some lexicographers.

Al-ʿīr should therefore not be translated simply as “camels” in all three verses of Yūsuf.

ʿ–Y–R (taʿyīr) compared with ʿ–Y–B (ʿayb)

ʿ–y–b concerns defect, deficiency, making something defective, and finding fault with others.

The taʿyīr branch of ʿ–y–r concerns reproach and attributing a fault.

Someone may criticize another's conduct and then use that conduct as a pretext to humiliate and reproach them.

Fault-finding and taʿyīr may accompany one another in such a case.

Taʿyīr emphasizes the act of reproaching, whereas ʿayb may also denote an actual defect in something.

Aʿībahā in Al-Kahf 18:79 should therefore not be equated with uʿayyiruhā. The former concerns introducing a defect into the ship, not reproaching it.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Translation should distinguish the root's different branches.

The principal equivalent for Quranic ʿīr is “caravan.”

The Persian equivalent is clear and natural and can include both the means of travel and the accompanying people.

For animal-related ʿayr, “male donkey,” or contextually “wild ass” or “domestic donkey,” are precise equivalents.

In the movement branch, ʿāra yaʿīru may mean “came and went,” “moved continually,” or “wandered.”

In the reproach branch, ʿayyara means “reproached,” “found fault,” or “condemned someone for something.”

Natural equivalents for taʿyīr are “reproach” or “fault-finding and condemnation.”

ʿĀr may be rendered “disgrace,” “dishonor,” or “cause for reproach.”

Measurement-related ʿiyār and miʿyār mean “standard,” “measure,” or “criterion of comparison.”

Proposed renderings for the three Quranic uses are:

Yūsuf 12:70 — ayyatuhā al-ʿīru

“O caravan.”

There is no need to add “people of the caravan” in Persian, since addressing the caravan is natural and its human audience is clear.

Yūsuf 12:82 — wa-al-ʿīra allatī aqbalnā fīhā

“And the caravan with which we came.”

This retains the collective sense and companionship in travel.

Yūsuf 12:94 — wa-lammā faṣalati al-ʿīru

“When the caravan departed.”

Departure and movement come from faṣalat here; they should not be added as the independent definition of the noun ʿīr.

“Caravan” is precise and sufficient in all three verses. Adding “commercial,” “camels,” “merchants,” or a destination requires contextual evidence beyond the presence of ʿīr itself.

7. Conclusion

Arabic ʿ–y–r has several semantic branches.

In the movement-related branch, ʿīr denotes a caravan traveling with pack animals, with its people included in the collective use.

In another branch, ʿayr names the donkey, particularly the male.

Reproach and disgrace, measurement and standards, and raised features on certain objects are also recorded within the family. Shared consonants alone do not establish a common historical origin for every branch.

Poetic witnesses attributed to pre-Islamic poets illustrate caravan-related ʿīr and reproach-related taʿyīr. Disputed attribution and wording must nevertheless be acknowledged when assessing their precise age.

Only ʿīr occurs in the Quran, and all three occurrences are in Yūsuf.

In Yūsuf 12:70, the caravan is addressed by a caller.

In Yūsuf 12:82, it is presented as traveling companions who can be asked about an incident.

In Yūsuf 12:94, its departure is described.

These uses show that ʿīr names more than pack animals: it includes the caravan and its accompanying people.

Final definition of ʿ–y–r:

In its Quranic branch, the form ʿīr denotes a caravan of pack animals and their companions, traveling to transport people and loads. Other Arabic branches record the male donkey, movement, reproach, measurement, and raised features. Those meanings should not be imposed on Quranic ʿīr without contextual evidence.
