Turning aside and departing from a path; shoulders, sides, and regions of the earth

In Arabic, N K B concerns inclining, turning aside from a path, and being on one side. This field includes نَكَبَ, نَاكِب, مَنْكِب, مَنَاكِب, and نَكْبَاء. Each has its own use: نَكَبَ عَنِ الطَّرِيقِ means turning aside from a road; مَنْكِب names the shoulder, where the upper arm meets the shoulder blade; and نَكْبَاء describes a wind diverging from the recognized principal wind directions.

The root occurs in only two Qur’anic forms: لَنَاكِبُونَ in Al-Muʾminūn 23:74 and مَنَاكِبِهَا in Al-Mulk 67:15. The first concerns people turning away from a path; the second describes the earth’s areas or regions.

This difference matters. Turning aside and being at a side or flank can be connected semantically, but moral deviation should not be imported into مَنَاكِب, nor should the bodily مَنْكِب be made to establish a definite picture of Earth’s physical structure.

1. Core meaning and forms

Inclination—a tilt or departure in something—is one explanation of N K B’s central meaning. It suits the root’s attested uses.

Arabic uses the following expressions:

نَكَبَ عَنِ الطَّرِيقِ: he turned aside or departed from the road.

نَكَبَ عَنِ الصَّوَابِ: he turned away from what was right.

تَنَكَّبَ عَنْهُ: he avoided or turned aside from him or it.

The preposition عَنْ matters in these constructions: it specifies what is being left or departed from. The movement may be physical, such as leaving a road, or behavioral, such as departing from a correct position.

مَنْكِب, meanwhile, names a body part: the shoulder, where the upper arm and shoulder blade meet. Its plural is مَنَاكِب. Its application to the sides or elevated parts of land is also known in Arabic.

Morphologically, مَنْكِب names a body part and should not automatically be treated as a place noun derived from a verb merely because of its pattern. Its sense must be learned from actual usage rather than an imposed morphological rule.

Other forms include تَنَكَّبَ, اِنْتَكَبَ, نَكْبَاء, أَنْكَب, نَكْبَة, and مَنْكُوب. They help establish the root’s semantic field, although these forms do not themselves occur in the Qur’an.

2. Early Arabic uses: from a camel’s shoulders to wind and deviation

The shoulder and the side of the body

A clear witness to مَنْكِب in pre-Islamic Arabic occurs in a poem attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays, describing a strong she-camel:

بَعِيدَةٌ بَيْنَ الْمَنْكِبَيْنِ كَأَنَّمَا
تَرَى عِنْدَ مَجْرَى الضَّفْرِ هِرًّا مُشَجَّرَا

baʿīdatun bayna al-mankibayni ka-annamā / tarā ʿinda majrā al-ḍafri hirran mushajjarā

“A she-camel broad between the shoulders, as though you see a tethered cat beside the braided girth-strap.”

الْمَنْكِبَيْنِ refers to the camel’s two shoulders. Their wide spacing describes its bodily structure, broad chest, and strength.

Versions differ in part of the second half-line, but بَيْنَ الْمَنْكِبَيْنِ remains stable in the examined accounts and clearly illustrates the relevant meaning.

The witness matters historically because مَنْكِب names a real body part in poetry transmitted as pre-Islamic. Understanding Qur’anic مَنَاكِب therefore does not require inventing a new meaning first; the question is how the body-part name also describes land.

Turning aside and tilting something

Ordinary Arabic uses نَكَبَ and نَكَّبَ for turning aside or causing something to incline.

For example, نَكَبَ الْإِنَاءَ describes tilting or overturning a vessel to empty it. Some lexical explanations specify nonliquid material, such as soil, within the vessel.

Here the deviation is a visible physical movement: the vessel tilts or overturns from its previous position.

تَنَكَّبَ قَوْسَهُ is also recorded for slinging a bow over one’s shoulder. It directly involves the shoulder’s bodily position and shows that the family extends beyond departing from a path.

In social usage, مَنْكِبُ الْقَوْمِ can describe someone with responsibility within a group or someone assisting its supervisor. One derivational explanation compares this with the shoulder’s supporting role in the body: a person who supports others in communal affairs.

The social use is recorded, but should not automatically be dated to pre-Islamic times without independent historical evidence.

نَكْبَاء: a wind departing from the usual direction

In traditional Arabic classifications of winds, نَكْبَاء describes a wind blowing between the recognized principal directions.

A poetic witness concerning wind reads:

لَا تَعْدِلَنَّ أَتَاوِيِّينَ تَضْرِبُهُمْ
نَكْبَاءُ صِرٌّ بِأَصْحَابِ الْمُحِلَّاتِ

lā taʿdilanna atāwiyyīna taḍribuhum / nakbāʾu ṣirrun bi-aṣḥābi al-muḥillāt

“Do not equate strangers struck by a bitterly cold nakbāʾ wind with those who possess the equipment and means to settle and live.”

أَتَاوِيِّينَ describes strangers coming from elsewhere, in contrast with people possessing what they need to establish a dwelling.

نَكْبَاء describes the wind, while صِرّ concerns its bitter cold. The poet depicts the hardship of people exposed to it.

The lexical accounts transmit this witness without a securely identified poet, so it cannot confidently be assigned to a pre-Islamic author. It nevertheless illustrates the attested use of نَكْبَاء for wind.

Different directions are reported for this wind. The shared feature is departure from the principal directions in the relevant classification. Coldness, dryness, or force may characterize particular instances, but are not invariable components of the word’s meaning.

From bodily harm to نَكْبَة

Arabic also uses نَكَب for an ailment in a camel’s shoulder that impairs its gait. An affected camel may lean to one side as it walks.

نَكْبَة also denotes a calamity or misfortune, and مَنْكُوب someone afflicted by such harm.

In نَكَبَتْهُ حَوَادِثُ الدَّهْرِ, the events of time have harmed someone.

The association of inclination, injury, and misfortune can help explain connections between these branches. It does not establish a certain derivational history in which calamity necessarily arose directly from “life leaving its course.”

More importantly, the senses of نَكْبَة and مَنْكُوب should not be imposed on the two Qur’anic uses. نَاكِبُونَ in Al-Muʾminūn concerns departure from the path, while مَنَاكِب in Al-Mulk concerns land regions.

3. لَنَاكِبُونَ: turning aside from the upright path

Al-Muʾminūn 23:74 reads:

وَإِنَّ الَّذِينَ لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ بِالْآخِرَةِ عَنِ الصِّرَاطِ لَنَاكِبُونَ

wa-inna alladhīna lā yuʾminūna bi-al-ākhirati ʿani al-ṣirāṭi la-nākibūn

“Those who do not believe in the hereafter are indeed deviating from that path.”

نَاكِبُونَ is the sound masculine plural of the active participle نَاكِب. The prefixed l in لَنَاكِبُونَ adds emphasis and is not part of the root.

The preposition عَنْ also makes the direction explicit: the verse describes departing from the path.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:73 is particularly important for understanding the expression:

وَإِنَّكَ لَتَدْعُوهُمْ إِلَىٰ صِرَاطٍ مُسْتَقِيمٍ

wa-innaka la-tadʿūhum ilā ṣirāṭin mustaqīm

“And indeed you call them to an upright path.”

In Al-Muʾminūn 23:73, the call is إِلَىٰ صِرَاطٍ مُسْتَقِيمٍ, toward an upright path. In Al-Muʾminūn 23:74, departure is عَنِ الصِّرَاطِ, away from that path.

The contrast clarifies نَاكِبُونَ in context: they are called to a path but turn aside from it. This is more than bodily movement or a geographical change of route; the path concerns guidance and people’s orientation toward truth.

Earlier verses provide the context of this aversion. Al-Muʾminūn 23:68 asks about failing to reflect on the discourse. Al-Muʾminūn 23:70 says truth has come to them, but most dislike it. Al-Muʾminūn 23:71 states conditionally that if truth followed their desires, the heavens, earth, and their inhabitants would be corrupted; it then describes their aversion to the reminder.

The deviation here therefore concerns a group’s stance toward truth and its reminder.

Nevertheless, نَاكِب alone does not establish someone’s degree of knowledge, inner motive, or reason for leaving a path. Those questions require evidence from the particular situation.

The verse also identifies the group through الَّذِينَ لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ بِالْآخِرَةِ. This qualification should not be ignored by extending the judgment to anyone merely for a different religious interpretation, without examining their knowledge and conduct.

Is nākib identical with being lost?

The Persian word for being astray may describe a consequence of departure, but نَاكِب specifically retains the image of turning aside from a path.

Someone can leave a route without losing their destination. Conversely, someone can lose the way without deliberately choosing to leave it.

The results can overlap, but their linguistic implications are not identical.

Thus, “are deviating from that path” is a precise, natural equivalent for عَنِ الصِّرَاطِ لَنَاكِبُونَ in Al-Muʾminūn 23:74.

4. مَنَاكِبِهَا: the earth’s sides, elevations, and regions

Al-Mulk 67:15 reads:

هُوَ الَّذِي جَعَلَ لَكُمُ الْأَرْضَ ذَلُولًا فَامْشُوا فِي مَنَاكِبِهَا وَكُلُوا مِنْ رِزْقِهِ وَإِلَيْهِ النُّشُورُ

huwa alladhī jaʿala lakumu al-arḍa dhalūlan fa-imshū fī manākibihā wa-kulū min rizqihi wa-ilayhi al-nushūr

“He is the One who made the earth manageable for you; walk through its expanses and eat of His provision; to Him is the resurrection.”

مَنَاكِبِهَا is the plural of مَنْكِب with a pronoun referring to the earth.

Besides the shoulder, مَنْكِب can describe a side or elevated area. This has led to several lexical readings of the earth’s مَنَاكِب.

Sides and regions of the earth

One lexical explanation glosses مَنَاكِب with جَوَانِب: sides and regions.

One interpretive preference likewise selects regions and sides, comparing the earth’s regions with human shoulders at the body’s sides.

This reading takes مَنَاكِب as describing land areas through which people can travel and whose resources they can use.

The human shoulder helps explain the spatial application; it does not make the earth literally possess human-like organs.

Mountains and elevated land

Another early lexical and interpretive explanation understands the earth’s مَنَاكِب as its mountains and elevations.

A competing preference favors elevations, reasoning from ذَلُولًا at the verse’s beginning: if The God has made the earth so manageable that people can traverse even its mountains, this illustrates the breadth of access to its resources.

The contextual argument deserves consideration. A shoulder is also a raised body part, providing a possible image for naming elevated land.

The preference does not prove that مَنَاكِب always means only mountains in Arabic. Sides and routes are also recorded meanings.

Routes and passages across the earth

Another explanation takes مَنَاكِب as routes or passages. This suits فَامْشُوا, since walking through the earth brings traversable areas to mind.

A route or passage, however, is only one spatial instance. مَنْكِب does not necessarily name a constructed road.

Regions, mountains, and routes are therefore related explanations within one word’s field, each emphasizing a different aspect of its spatial use.

Which sense best fits the context?

Al-Mulk 67:15 first describes the earth as ذَلُولًا, made manageable and usable for people. It then directs movement through its مَنَاكِب and eating of divine provision.

The topic is therefore the possibility of travel and benefit from the earth.

Reading مَنَاكِب as sides and regions emphasizes the breadth of traversable land. Limiting it to mountains and elevations puts greater emphasis on crossing more difficult terrain.

Both can fit the context, but regions and sides has wider scope and can include routes and elevations.

Forqan’s Persian “walk through its expanses” is therefore defensible. “Expanses” is broader than bodily shoulders and does not explicitly express sidedness, but it avoids restricting the verse to one type of land, as mountains or roads would.

To stay closer to the explanation of regions and sides, “walk through its regions” is also a precise, natural rendering.

In either case, مَنَاكِب does not establish that the earth possesses physically specified shoulders, or independently provide a scientific account of its geometry, internal layers, or motion.

The pairing of فَامْشُوا فِي مَنَاكِبِهَا and وَكُلُوا مِنْ رِزْقِهِ also joins travel with benefiting from The God’s provision. Such benefit can take different forms, but no particular occupation or economic activity is contained in مَنَاكِب itself.

The ending, وَإِلَيْهِ النُّشُورُ, places resurrection toward The God beside earthly enjoyment. This completes the verse’s message without changing مَنَاكِب’s lexical meaning.

5. Distinctions from related roots

N K B (nakaba) compared with M Y L (māla): departure and inclination

Inclination in Arabic has broad scope, applying to a body, gaze, desire, or direction of movement.

The familiar use نَكَبَ عَنْ focuses on turning aside or departing from something.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:74 makes this explicit through عَنِ الصِّرَاطِ. Nevertheless, not every inclination is slight or every nukūb severe; context determines the degree.

N K B (nakaba) compared with Z Y GH (zāgha) and Ḍ L L (ḍalla)

Zaygh and ḍalāl also concern departure from the right way, but each has particular uses.

For example, Al-Ṣaff 61:5 associates zaygh with people and their hearts turning aside. Ḍalāl has broad Qur’anic uses involving losing the way and departing from guidance.

By contrast, نَاكِبُونَ in Al-Muʾminūn 23:74 occurs with عَنْ and صِرَاط, clearly preserving the image of leaving a route.

This is not an absolute separation of the three roots; in some situations they can describe related conditions.

Study of Ḍ L L

N K B (nakaba) compared with J N B (janaba): shoulder and side

جَانِب concerns a side or direction and has broad spatial application. مَنْكِب can also denote a side, but its bodily sense names the shoulder, a particular body part.

جَوَانِب can therefore explain the earth’s مَنَاكِب without the two words being complete synonyms in every Arabic use.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

The two Qur’anic forms require different equivalents.

In Al-Muʾminūn 23:74, لَنَاكِبُونَ means “are deviating.” A suitable rendering is:

“Those who do not believe in the hereafter are indeed deviating from that path.”

This retains the relationship expressed by عَنِ الصِّرَاطِ without adding a judgment about people’s inner intentions.

In Al-Mulk 67:15, مَنَاكِبِهَا can be rendered “its regions,” “its sides,” or “its expanses.”

Forqan’s existing Persian translation, translated here for comparison:

Original Persian translation: Al-Mulk 67:15

“He is the One who made the earth manageable for you; walk through its expanses and eat of His provision; to Him is the resurrection.”

The rendering is defensible and needs no change. “Expanses” refers to different land regions, rather than literal bodily shoulders.

“Through its mountains” also has lexical support, but selects only one transmitted interpretation. “Through its routes” emphasizes suitability to the verb of walking.

For a general Qur’an translation, preserving an equivalent that conveys the word’s spatial breadth is therefore preferable.

7. Concluding findings

N K B in Arabic concerns inclining, turning aside, and being on one side. Its field includes departure from a path, the shoulder and body’s side, nakbāʾ winds, and land regions.

In its Qur’anic descriptive use, نَاكِبُونَ denotes people turning aside from the path in question. In Al-Muʾminūn 23:74, this is the upright path to which Al-Muʾminūn 23:73 describes the call.

The noun مَنَاكِب is the plural of مَنْكِب, used for the shoulder and for sides or elevated areas. In Al-Mulk 67:15 it describes land regions, capable of encompassing sides, routes, and elevations.

نَكْبَاء for a wind departing from the usual direction and نَكْبَة for calamity are also attested, but their meanings should not be generalized to every derivative.

Final definition: In its branch concerning departure from a path, N K B describes turning aside from a specified route or direction. The Qur’anic form in this branch is نَاكِبُونَ, a plural active participle; no finite verb from the root occurs in the Qur’an. The nominal branch مَنْكِب and مَنَاكِب concerns shoulders and the sides or elevated parts of things, including land regions.

Departure is not itself a moral judgment, and مَنَاكِب does not mean misguidance or calamity. Each derivative’s precise sense follows from attested usage and the sentence’s structure and context.
