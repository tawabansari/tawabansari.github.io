The Qur’anic Root F T Ḥ

From Opening What Is Closed to Access, Judgment, and a Decisive Opening

F T Ḥ is a root whose concrete meaning is very clear, yet whose Qur’anic uses extend beyond that initial image. The starting point is opening something that was closed, blocked, concealed, or difficult to access. From this core arise such branches as opening a door, opening the way for mercy and blessing, disclosing what was previously inaccessible, deciding between two parties, and, in a conflict, a decisive opening that may lead to prevailing and victory.

The available evidence indicates that even before Islam, F T Ḥ was not restricted to military victory. The simple meaning of opening existed in everyday life. More importantly, the branch concerning judgment, adjudication, and deciding between parties in conflict also has a pre-Qur’anic history. This has direct significance for understanding Qur’anic expressions such as افْتَحْ بَيْنَنَا (iftaḥ baynanā, decide between us) and الْفَتَّاح (al-Fattāḥ).

This breadth means that consistently translating fatḥ as “victory,” or conversely rendering all its uses merely as “opening,” loses part of the root’s semantic network. The Qur’an itself is the best guide to the relationship between these branches.

Forms Examined

The root F T Ḥ occurs 38 times in the Qur’anic text in eight principal morphological forms: the form I verb فَتَحَ (fataḥa) and its inflections; one occurrence of form II, تُفَتَّحُ (tufattaḥu); three occurrences of form X, such as يَسْتَفْتِحُونَ (yastaftiḥūna); the noun فَتْح (fatḥ); the description الْفَتَّاح (al-Fattāḥ); the plural active participle الْفَاتِحِينَ (al-fātiḥīn); مَفَاتِح (mafātiḥ); and the form II passive participle مُفَتَّحَةً (mufattaḥatan).

Forms such as فُتُوحات (futūḥāt), افتتاح (iftitāḥ), فاتحة (fātiḥah), and مفتاح (miftāḥ) are familiar in Arabic. Their familiarity in Arabic or Islamic literature, however, must not lead us to count them among the derivatives actually present in the Qur’anic text. For example, “الفاتحة” (al-Fātiḥah) is the established name of the first surah, but this form itself does not occur within the Qur’anic verses as a derivative of F T Ḥ.

1. The Semantic Core: Removing Closedness

The clear, concrete meaning of فَتَحَ (fataḥa) in Arabic is “to open,” as opposed to closing: opening a door, opening a way, or removing something that prevented access. Classical dictionaries likewise place this opposition to closedness at the beginning of the entry. Ibn Faris identifies the root’s core as the opposite of ighlāq, closing, and relates the other uses back to that principle.

The root’s concrete image is therefore uncomplicated. Something is closed; through fatḥ, its closed condition is removed, allowing what lies behind it to pass, appear, flow, or become accessible.

This concrete meaning must not be confused with the later meaning of “territorial conquest.” Research into the word’s history likewise indicates that military conquest is not its intrinsic meaning, and that the development of fatḥ into a general term for political conquests became prominent in later Islamic literature.

The Qur’an clearly preserves the simple image of opening:

وَلَمَّا فَتَحُوا مَتَاعَهُمْ وَجَدُوا بِضَاعَتَهُمْ رُدَّتْ إِلَيْهِمْ

Wa-lammā fataḥū matāʿahum wajadū biḍāʿatahum ruddat ilayhim

And when they opened their baggage and goods, they found their trading capital had been returned to them.

Yusuf 12:65

No technical meaning is needed here. فَتَحُوا مَتَاعَهُمْ (fataḥū matāʿahum) means that they opened their bundles and baggage.

The same concrete use appears in relation to doors:

حَتَّىٰ إِذَا جَاءُوهَا فُتِحَتْ أَبْوَابُهَا

Ḥattā idhā jāʾūhā futiḥat abwābuhā

Until, when they reached it, its gates were opened.

Az-Zumar 39:71

And:

جَنَّاتِ عَدْنٍ مُفَتَّحَةً لَهُمُ الْأَبْوَابُ

Jannāti ʿadnin mufattaḥatan lahumu al-abwāb

Gardens of Eden, with their gates opened for them.

Sad 38:50

In these uses, the root retains the simple image of removing closedness.

2. Use in Arabic Before Islam and Among Pre-Islamic Arabs

To reconstruct the semantic field known to the Qur’an’s first audience, we must distinguish several kinds of evidence: poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period, lexical reports about Arab dialects, literary accounts recorded later about that period, and genuinely pre-Islamic inscriptions. These witnesses do not all have the same evidentiary value.

Opening in Everyday Usage

The simple meaning of opening is so fundamental that Arabic lexicography places it opposite ighlāq, closing. Literary accounts concerning Imruʾ al-Qays also report this verb in a description of opening two containers to eat their contents. That story, however, reaches us through sources compiled after the pre-Islamic period. It can therefore illustrate the continuity of a natural Arabic usage, but should not be treated as direct, conclusive evidence of everyday pre-Islamic speech on a par with a contemporary inscription or securely attributed poetry.

This caution matters. The aim is not to find a pre-Islamic anecdote for every Qur’anic meaning. It is to show, wherever reliable evidence exists, how the Qur’an’s first audience might have understood a word before later Islamic terminology developed.

Fatḥ as Judgment and Adjudication

There is more significant evidence for the judicial branch. Early Arabic dictionaries record الفتاح (al-fattāḥ) for a ruler or judge and الفتاحة (al-fatāḥah) for judgment and adjudication, attributing this usage to the language of Yemen and Himyar. Recorded expressions also include افتح بيننا (iftaḥ baynanā), meaning “judge between us.”

More importantly, a verse is attributed to al-Asʿar al-Juʿfi, whom literary sources count among the pre-Islamic poets. He says:

أَلَا مَنْ مُبْلِغٌ عَمْرًا رَسُولًا

Alā man mublighun ʿamran rasūlan

فَإِنِّي عَنْ فُتَاحَتِكُمْ غَنِيُّ

Fa-innī ʿan futāḥatikum ghaniyyu

That is: Is there someone who will convey this message to Amr—that I have no need of your judgment?

The word فُتَاحَة (futāḥah) in this witness belongs to the field of judgment and adjudication. Literary sources place the poet in the pre-Islamic period, and the verse itself is transmitted in the lexical tradition to explain this meaning of fatāḥah. Nevertheless, like much pre-Islamic poetry that reaches us through later compilations, it is better described as a “transmitted pre-Islamic witness” than as a contemporary inscriptional document.

This witness is particularly important because it shows that when the Qur’an says:

رَبَّنَا افْتَحْ بَيْنَنَا وَبَيْنَ قَوْمِنَا

Rabbanā iftaḥ baynanā wa-bayna qawminā

we need not imagine that the Qur’an suddenly created an entirely new metaphor from “opening a door.” The use of fatḥ for judgment and settlement already had a history in the Arabic linguistic environment.

Evidence from Ancient South Arabia

Evidence from the languages of Ancient South Arabia is also relevant here, although these languages must not be equated with Qur’anic Arabic. In Sabaic, attested in Yemen from many centuries before Islam until close to its emergence, the fth family is reported in fields such as legal matters, disputes, and judicial proceedings. This evidence accords with the Arabic dictionaries’ reports of the judicial use of fatḥ in Yemen.

This does not establish that every Arab in Mecca or Medina used the word exactly as in Sabaic. Sabaic was a different language within the Semitic language family of the Arabian Peninsula. The evidence is valuable because it shows that the connection of the F T Ḥ family with adjudication and disputes also had a pre-Islamic history in southern Arabia; it was not merely a product of juristic terminology developed centuries later.

By contrast, pre-Islamic North Arabian inscriptional evidence for the root F T Ḥ itself is very sparse. Existing research reports that the root has not yet been clearly attested as a verb or common noun in North Arabian inscriptions. Claims here must therefore remain within the evidence.

Taken together, these data yield an important picture: the Qur’an’s first audience was not encountering a root whose primary meaning was “conquering countries.” The older field included opening and, at least in parts of the Arabic and South Arabian linguistic environment, judgment and settlement. This background must be considered before the political meanings of later periods.

3. Opening a Way, a Flow, and Access

Sometimes something is “opened” in the Qur’an even though no physical door is being discussed. What opens is access to something, its flow, or its reaching human beings.

One of the clearest examples concerns mercy:

مَا يَفْتَحِ اللَّهُ لِلنَّاسِ مِن رَّحْمَةٍ فَلَا مُمْسِكَ لَهَا وَمَا يُمْسِكْ فَلَا مُرْسِلَ لَهُ مِن بَعْدِهِ

Mā yaftaḥi Allāhu li-l-nāsi min raḥmatin fa-lā mumsika lahā wa-mā yumsik fa-lā mursila lahu min baʿdihi

Whatever mercy The God opens for people, none can withhold it; and whatever He withholds, none can release it after Him.

Fatir 35:2

The contrast يَفْتَح / يُمْسِك (yaftaḥ / yumsik, opens / withholds) is especially important here. Fatḥ does not simply mean “giving.” The verse pictures the way for mercy to arrive being opened, while withholding prevents its arrival.

The same structure appears in relation to blessings:

وَلَوْ أَنَّ أَهْلَ الْقُرَىٰ آمَنُوا وَاتَّقَوْا لَفَتَحْنَا عَلَيْهِم بَرَكَاتٍ مِّنَ السَّمَاءِ وَالْأَرْضِ

Wa-law anna ahla al-qurā āmanū wa-ttaqaw la-fataḥnā ʿalayhim barakātin mina al-samāʾi wa-l-arḍ

And if the people of the settlements had believed and practiced taqwa, We would have opened blessings from heaven and earth upon them.

Al-Araf 7:96

Here too, fatḥ means establishing access and flow. A blessing is not a door that must physically open. The linguistic structure expresses the arrival of something previously unavailable to them through the image of opening.

But the root itself carries no positive moral value. The Qur’an uses precisely the same construction in a very different setting:

فَلَمَّا نَسُوا مَا ذُكِّرُوا بِهِ فَتَحْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ أَبْوَابَ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا فَرِحُوا بِمَا أُوتُوا أَخَذْنَاهُم بَغْتَةً

Fa-lammā nasū mā dhukkirū bihi fataḥnā ʿalayhim abwāba kulli shayʾin ḥattā idhā fariḥū bi-mā ūtū akhadhnāhum baghtatan

Then, when they forgot what they had been reminded of, We opened the doors of everything upon them, until, when they rejoiced in what they had been given, We seized them suddenly.

Al-Anam 6:44

Opening is therefore not in itself a sign of approval, mercy, or reward. That direction comes from context. Even a door may lead toward punishment:

حَتَّىٰ إِذَا فَتَحْنَا عَلَيْهِم بَابًا ذَا عَذَابٍ شَدِيدٍ إِذَا هُمْ فِيهِ مُبْلِسُونَ

Ḥattā idhā fataḥnā ʿalayhim bāban dhā ʿadhābin shadīdin idhā hum fīhi mublisūn

Until, when We open upon them a door of severe punishment, they suddenly despair within it.

Al-Muminun 23:77

These examples prevent us from deriving goodness, mercy, or victory from the root F T Ḥ alone. Its core is opening; the nature of what opens is determined by the sentence and context.

4. From Opening to Disclosure and Availability

One of the root’s most subtle uses occurs in Al-Baqarah 2:76:

أَتُحَدِّثُونَهُم بِمَا فَتَحَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْكُمْ لِيُحَاجُّوكُم بِهِ عِندَ رَبِّكُمْ أَفَلَا تَعْقِلُونَ

A-tuḥaddithūnahum bi-mā fataḥa Allāhu ʿalaykum li-yuḥājjūkum bihi ʿinda rabbikum a-fa-lā taʿqilūn

Do you inform them of what The God has opened to you, so that they may argue against you with it before your Lord? Do you not use reason?

Al-Baqarah 2:76

Early sources transmit several explanations of بِمَا فَتَحَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْكُمْ (bi-mā fataḥa Allāhu ʿalaykum): what The God disclosed or explained, what He taught or made known, what He made available to them in the Book, or what He judged concerning them. This disagreement itself shows that a very restrictive translation may decide on a semantic branch before the verse does.

Yet the verse’s own structure makes one thing clear: what The God has opened to them can now be told to others, who can in turn use that same thing in argument. Thus the sense “what has been opened and made known to you” fits the construction in this passage.

The translation “what The God has opened to you” preserves the root’s basic meaning without turning one interpretive identification into the text itself. An explanation can then clarify that, in context, this opening concerns some knowledge, scripture, or divine judgment made available to them that can be communicated and used in argument.

Fatḥ can therefore extend from “opening a door” to “opening access to knowledge.” But this use must not lead us to conclude that the root independently means revelation or knowledge. Those meanings arise from structure and context.

5. Fatḥ Between Two Parties: How Does It Come to Mean Settlement?

One of the root’s most important branches is the construction فَتَحَ بَيْنَ (fataḥa bayna), deciding between.

Shuayb says:

رَبَّنَا افْتَحْ بَيْنَنَا وَبَيْنَ قَوْمِنَا بِالْحَقِّ وَأَنتَ خَيْرُ الْفَاتِحِينَ

Rabbanā iftaḥ baynanā wa-bayna qawminā bi-l-ḥaqqi wa-anta khayru al-fātiḥīn

Our Lord, decide between us and our people in truth, and You are the best of those who decide.

Al-Araf 7:89

And Noah says:

فَافْتَحْ بَيْنِي وَبَيْنَهُمْ فَتْحًا وَنَجِّنِي وَمَن مَّعِيَ مِنَ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ

Fa-ftaḥ baynī wa-baynahum fatḥan wa-najjinī wa-man maʿiya mina al-muʾminīn

Then decide between me and them, and save me and the believers who are with me.

Ash-Shuara 26:118

This use should therefore not be regarded merely as a late metaphor of “untying the knot of a problem.” As the pre-Qur’anic witnesses showed, fatḥ already had a history in the field of judgment and settlement within the Arabic linguistic environment.

Its connection with the root’s core can also be explained: a dispute is unresolved; judgment brings it to a conclusion and clarifies an uncertain situation. This, however, is only an explanation of the relationship between meanings. It must not become a definitive account of how the judicial meaning arose historically.

The Qur’an itself makes this use very clear:

قُلْ يَجْمَعُ بَيْنَنَا رَبُّنَا ثُمَّ يَفْتَحُ بَيْنَنَا بِالْحَقِّ وَهُوَ الْفَتَّاحُ الْعَلِيمُ

Qul yajmaʿu baynanā rabbunā thumma yaftaḥu baynanā bi-l-ḥaqqi wa-huwa al-Fattāḥu al-ʿAlīm

Say: Our Lord will bring us together; then He will decide between us in truth, and He is al-Fattāḥ, the Knowing.

Saba 34:26

In this verse, يَفْتَحُ بَيْنَنَا بِالْحَقِّ (yaftaḥu baynanā bi-l-ḥaqq), He decides between us in truth, does not allow الْفَتَّاح (al-Fattāḥ) to be detached from context and understood only as “the opener of the doors of provision.” The divine description follows immediately after a verb concerning a decision between two parties.

Yawm al-Fatḥ Is Not Always the Day of Military Victory

Surah As-Sajdah provides another important example. A few verses earlier, it says:

إِنَّ رَبَّكَ هُوَ يَفْصِلُ بَيْنَهُمْ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ فِيمَا كَانُوا فِيهِ يَخْتَلِفُونَ

Inna rabbaka huwa yafṣilu baynahum yawma al-qiyāmati fīmā kānū fīhi yakhtalifūn

Indeed, your Lord will decide between them on the Day of Resurrection concerning that over which they differed.

As-Sajdah 32:25

Then comes:

وَيَقُولُونَ مَتَىٰ هَٰذَا الْفَتْحُ إِن كُنتُمْ صَادِقِينَ ۝ قُلْ يَوْمَ الْفَتْحِ لَا يَنفَعُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا إِيمَانُهُمْ وَلَا هُمْ يُنظَرُونَ

Wa-yaqūlūna matā hādhā al-fatḥu in kuntum ṣādiqīn. Qul yawma al-fatḥi lā yanfaʿu alladhīna kafarū īmānuhum wa-lā hum yunẓarūn

And they say: If you are truthful, when will this fatḥ be? Say: On the day of fatḥ, the faith of those who practiced kufr will not benefit them, nor will they be granted a reprieve.

As-Sajdah 32:28–29

The presence of يَفْصِلُ بَيْنَهُمْ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ (yafṣilu baynahum yawma al-qiyāmah), He decides between them on the Day of Resurrection, in this same context, followed by يَوْمَ الْفَتْحِ (yawma al-fatḥ), a day when faith no longer benefits them, shows that fatḥ here is at least connected with final judgment and the disclosure of the outcome. Simply translating it as “victory” may conceal this internal relationship between the verses.

6. Fatḥ, Naṣr, and a Decisive Opening

One important Qur’anic use of fatḥ concerns the decisive outcome of a confrontation—the field in which “victory” sometimes becomes a natural translation.

But the Qur’an gives an important indication that fatḥ and naṣr must not be made completely synonymous:

نَصْرٌ مِّنَ اللَّهِ وَفَتْحٌ قَرِيبٌ

Naṣrun mina Allāhi wa-fatḥun qarībun

Help from The God and a near opening.

As-Saff 61:13

And:

إِذَا جَاءَ نَصْرُ اللَّهِ وَالْفَتْحُ

Idhā jāʾa naṣru Allāhi wa-l-fatḥ

When the help of The God and the opening arrive.

An-Nasr 110:1

If naṣr and fatḥ meant exactly the same thing in both cases, the verses’ distinction in vocabulary would disappear. Naṣr has a core of help and assistance, whereas fatḥ focuses on opening and on a closed situation reaching an outcome.

A semantic tendency can therefore be discerned: naṣr focuses more on help and support, while fatḥ focuses on the situation opening and an outcome being reached. In conflict, that outcome may be prevailing and victory.

This distinction must not become a mechanical rule such as “naṣr always occurs before fatḥ.” The two words occurring together shows only that they are not fully interchangeable.

This point has particular importance at the opening of Surah Al-Fath:

إِنَّا فَتَحْنَا لَكَ فَتْحًا مُّبِينًا

Innā fataḥnā laka fatḥan mubīnan

Indeed, We have opened for you a clear opening.

Al-Fath 48:1

The construction فَتَحْنَا ... فَتْحًا (fataḥnā ... fatḥan) places the verb and verbal noun of the same root together. But the expression itself names no city, nor does the word fatḥ alone contain the meaning “military capture of a city.” Identifying the historical event being referred to is a question separate from the root’s lexical meaning.

The same surah also speaks of فَتْحًا قَرِيبًا (fatḥan qarīban), a near opening. In such a context, “a near victory” may be appropriate, but this usage must not lead to the conclusion that the root’s basic meaning is “military victory.”

Indeed, even research into the history of the term futūḥāt indicates that the development of fatḥ into a general designation for territorial expansion and conquests of the Islamic state became established in later literature. That later meaning must not be projected back onto every Qur’anic use.

7. Istiftāḥ: Asking for What?

Form X of this root occurs three times in the Qur’an. The construction اسْتَفْتَحَ (istaftaḥa) can convey seeking fatḥ, but the kind of fatḥ sought in each verse must be understood from context.

In Al-Anfal we read:

إِن تَسْتَفْتِحُوا فَقَدْ جَاءَكُمُ الْفَتْحُ

In tastaftiḥū fa-qad jāʾakumu al-fatḥ

If you were seeking fatḥ, then fatḥ has now come to you.

Al-Anfal 8:19

Both the branch of judgment and that of prevailing are connected here with the context of conflict. If two groups ask The God to settle the matter between them, the arrival of judgment may mean help for one and defeat for the other.

This is precisely where “judgment,” “help,” and “victory” must not be treated as three entirely unrelated meanings. In a dispute, seeking settlement can simultaneously mean seeking the clarification of truth and seeking an outcome favorable to one side.

Surah Ibrahim also states:

وَاسْتَفْتَحُوا وَخَابَ كُلُّ جَبَّارٍ عَنِيدٍ

Wa-staftaḥū wa-khāba kullu jabbārin ʿanīd

And they sought fatḥ, and every obstinate tyrant was disappointed.

Ibrahim 14:15

The referent of the pronoun in this verse has been debated. A translation that unnecessarily inserts an explicit subject therefore goes beyond the expression itself. At the level of the root, what is clear is the seeking of fatḥ and the struggle reaching an outcome.

Al-Baqarah 2:89 also states:

وَكَانُوا مِن قَبْلُ يَسْتَفْتِحُونَ عَلَى الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا

Wa-kānū min qablu yastaftiḥūna ʿalā alladhīna kafarū

The construction عَلَى الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا (ʿalā alladhīna kafarū), against those who practiced kufr, strengthens the oppositional direction of the request. In this context, seeking fatḥ therefore approaches seeking an outcome or prevailing against the opposing party.

One fixed equivalent must therefore not be imposed on every occurrence of اسْتَفْتَحَ (istaftaḥa). “Seeking an opening,” “seeking judgment,” or, in an appropriate context, “seeking victory” can be different renderings of it.

8. مَفَاتِح (mafātiḥ): Keys, Access, and What Opens the Closed

مَفَاتِح (mafātiḥ) occurs three times in the Qur’an:

وَعِندَهُ مَفَاتِحُ الْغَيْبِ لَا يَعْلَمُهَا إِلَّا هُوَ

Wa-ʿindahu mafātiḥu al-ghaybi lā yaʿlamuhā illā huwa

And with Him are the mafātiḥ of the unseen; none knows them except Him.

Al-Anam 6:59

In An-Nur 24:61:

أَوْ مَا مَلَكْتُم مَّفَاتِحَهُ

Aw mā malaktum mafātiḥahu

Or that whose mafātiḥ are in your possession.

And concerning Qarun:

وَآتَيْنَاهُ مِنَ الْكُنُوزِ مَا إِنَّ مَفَاتِحَهُ لَتَنُوءُ بِالْعُصْبَةِ أُولِي الْقُوَّةِ

Wa-ātaynāhu mina al-kunūzi mā inna mafātiḥahu la-tanūʾu bi-l-ʿuṣbati ulī al-quwwah

And We had given him such treasures that their mafātiḥ weighed heavily upon a band of strong men.

Al-Qasas 28:76

The lexical tradition records more than one explanation of mafātiḥ, especially in Al-Qasas 28:76: keys or instruments for opening, as well as treasuries or that which provides access to stores.

It is therefore better to take from the root what is clearer: mafātiḥ is connected with opening and access to something closed. Whether a particular verse means keys, a means of access, or treasuries must be examined in that verse’s own context.

In مَفَاتِحُ الْغَيْبِ (mafātiḥu al-ghayb), “the keys of the unseen” preserves the root’s image, provided that no physical theory is built from it. The verse does not say that the unseen is a material treasury with bodily locks and keys. The linguistic image is access to something closed and unknown to others.

In An-Nur 24:61, the connection with control and access is more tangible: possessing something’s mafātiḥ makes it possible to open and access it.

9. الْفَتَّاح (al-Fattāḥ) as a Divine Description

The root F T Ḥ occurs once in the Qur’an in the form الْفَتَّاح (al-Fattāḥ) as a description of The God:

قُلْ يَجْمَعُ بَيْنَنَا رَبُّنَا ثُمَّ يَفْتَحُ بَيْنَنَا بِالْحَقِّ وَهُوَ الْفَتَّاحُ الْعَلِيمُ

Qul yajmaʿu baynanā rabbunā thumma yaftaḥu baynanā bi-l-ḥaqqi wa-huwa al-Fattāḥu al-ʿAlīm

Say: Our Lord will bring us together, then He will decide between us in truth; and He is al-Fattāḥ, the Knowing.

Saba 34:26

The pattern فَعَّال (faʿʿāl) can express intensity or frequency in a description, but a theological system must not be built from a morphological pattern alone. More important than the pattern is the sentence itself: ثُمَّ يَفْتَحُ بَيْنَنَا بِالْحَقِّ (thumma yaftaḥu baynanā bi-l-ḥaqq), then He decides between us in truth, occurs immediately before الْفَتَّاحُ الْعَلِيمُ (al-Fattāḥu al-ʿAlīm).

In this verse, al-Fattāḥ is therefore clearly connected with deciding and opening up the matter in dispute. This meaning also accords with the judicial usage known before Islam.

The pairing of al-Fattāḥ with al-ʿAlīm, the Knowing, is also significant in context: the One who decides between two parties in truth knows the reality of the matter.

Nevertheless, this one verse must not lead us to conclude that al-Fattāḥ is entirely detached from the broader core of “opening.” The same semantic family remains present, but this verse’s context foregrounds the branch of adjudication and settlement.

For the Persian translation, “گشاینده” (goshāyandeh, opener) preserves the root’s image but weakens the verse’s judicial aspect. “داور” (dāvar, judge) or “فیصله کننده” (feyṣaleh-konandeh, one who decides) conveys the context more clearly but makes the connection with the root’s core less apparent. The basic translation should therefore be natural and appropriate to the verse, leaving the root study to provide the fuller explanation.

10. Challenges of Understanding and Restricting Meaning

Fatḥ Does Not Equal Victory

The most familiar restriction turns the entire root into the concept of victory or military conquest. Such a translation can be appropriate in some Qur’anic uses, but the root is also used of doors, heaven, mercy, blessings, baggage, decisions between parties, and the mafātiḥ of the unseen.

“Victory” is thus one outcome that fatḥ can take on in a particular context, not the root’s general definition.

The Judicial Use Is Not a Late Meaning

Transmitted pre-Islamic witnesses and reports concerning the language of Yemen indicate that the connection of fatḥ with judgment and adjudication was known before Islamic juristic and judicial institutions took shape. In verses containing افْتَحْ بَيْنَنَا (iftaḥ baynanā), therefore, we need not regard “judge between us” as a meaning invented by later commentators.

Not Every Opening Is Positive

Al-Anam 6:44 and Al-Muminun 23:77 show that something can be opened upon human beings with punishment as its outcome. The root F T Ḥ therefore carries no inherently positive moral connotation.

Fatḥ and Naṣr Are Not Complete Synonyms

Their occurrence together in As-Saff 61:13 and An-Nasr 110:1 shows that their difference in semantic tendency should be preserved. Naṣr focuses more on help, while fatḥ focuses on an opening or outcome being achieved. This is a tendency, not an absolute boundary.

Futūḥāt Is a Later Historical Usage

The broad use of futūḥāt for the territorial expansion of Islamic states in later periods is of interest for the word’s history, but is secondary for determining its Qur’anic meaning. The Qur’an’s own uses and the language known before it must be examined first.

The Image of a Door Must Not Be Physicalized Without Grounds

When the Qur’an speaks of opening heaven, mercy, blessings, or the unseen, the verbal image of opening should be preserved. But it cannot yield a physical architecture of the unseen world, an energy gateway, or a particular scientific mechanism.

11. Comparison with Nearby Roots

F T Ḥ (فَتَح, fataḥ) Compared with N Ṣ R (نَصَر, naṣar): Fatḥ and Naṣr

Naṣr has a core of help and support, especially against an enemy. Fatḥ has a core of opening a closed situation and, in a conflict, can extend to an outcome, a decision, and prevailing.

This is why the Qur’an can say:

نَصْرٌ مِّنَ اللَّهِ وَفَتْحٌ قَرِيبٌ

Naṣrun mina Allāhi wa-fatḥun qarībun

The two words occupy a shared field but view the event from two angles: help that arrives and an opening that is achieved.

This distinction must not become a fixed temporal formula such as “first naṣr, then fatḥ.”

F T Ḥ (فَتَح, fataḥ) Compared with F T Q (فَتَق, fataq): Opening and Splitting

فَتَقَ (fataqa) focuses on splitting or separating something that had been joined together.

Fatḥ does not necessarily involve splitting or tearing. A door can open without being split; mercy can be opened; a dispute can be settled. Both roots may therefore concern the removal of a kind of closedness, but their images are not identical.

F T Ḥ (فَتَح, fataḥ) Compared with F R J (فَرَج, faraj): Opening and Creating a Gap

In the F R J family, the image of a gap, interval, or open space between two things is prominent.

Fatḥ focuses more on the act of opening or the availability of access, rather than on the resulting gap itself.

F T Ḥ (فَتَح, fataḥ) Compared with F Ṣ L (فَصَل, faṣal): Settlement and Separation

Faṣl fundamentally denotes separating and drawing boundaries between things, and from there also extends to deciding and settling a dispute.

Fatḥ also reaches the meaning of settlement in its judicial branch, but its basic image is not separating two things. The closeness of these concepts in Surah As-Sajdah is noteworthy: first يَفْصِلُ بَيْنَهُمْ (yafṣilu baynahum), He decides between them, and then يَوْمَ الْفَتْحِ (yawma al-fatḥ), the day of opening or judgment.

This occurrence in the same context shows that two roots can approach one another within a semantic field without having identical lexical definitions.

12. Translation Suggestions for Forqan

For F T Ḥ, no single equivalent should be imposed on every verse. The core of “opening” should remain the principal reference, while natural Persian and the context of each verse determine the most suitable wording.

فَتَحَ الباب / فُتِحَت الأبواب (fataḥa al-bāb / futiḥat al-abwāb):

“He opened / opened up”; “they were opened.”

فَتَحُوا مَتَاعَهُمْ (fataḥū matāʿahum):

“They opened their baggage” or “they opened their bundles.”

مَا يَفْتَحِ اللَّهُ لِلنَّاسِ مِن رَّحْمَةٍ (mā yaftaḥi Allāhu li-l-nāsi min raḥmatin):

“Whatever mercy The God opens for people...”

لَفَتَحْنَا عَلَيْهِم بَرَكَاتٍ (la-fataḥnā ʿalayhim barakātin):

“We would have opened blessings upon them.”

بِمَا فَتَحَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْكُمْ (bi-mā fataḥa Allāhu ʿalaykum):

“What The God has opened to you,” with a contextual explanation of what has been disclosed or made known to them.

افْتَحْ بَيْنَنَا (iftaḥ baynanā):

In natural translation: “decide between us” or “judge between us.” This use also accords with the root’s known judicial meaning in early Arabic.

فَتْحًا (fatḥan) in Ash-Shuara 26:118:

“A settlement” or “a decisive judgment,” rather than automatically “victory.”

يَوْمَ الْفَتْحِ (yawma al-fatḥ) in As-Sajdah 32:29:

“The day of fatḥ,” “the day of judgment,” or a rendering that conveys the final opening of the matter; “the day of victory” may be too restrictive in this context.

فَتْحٌ قَرِيبٌ (fatḥun qarībun) in a Context of Conflict:

“A near fatḥ,” “a near opening,” or, if context warrants it, “a near victory.”

إِنَّا فَتَحْنَا لَكَ فَتْحًا مُّبِينًا (innā fataḥnā laka fatḥan mubīnan):

“Indeed, We have opened for you a clear opening.”

An explanation can clarify that fatḥ is a decisive opening and that the word itself does not name a military operation or the capture of a particular city.

اسْتَفْتَحَ (istaftaḥa):

“He sought an opening,” “he sought judgment,” or “he sought victory,” according to context.

مَفَاتِح (mafātiḥ):

“Keys,” where the image of access is appropriate; disagreement about its precise meaning in each verse must be examined through that verse’s context.

الْفَتَّاح (al-Fattāḥ) in Saba 34:26:

In the verse’s context, “the One who decides” or “the Judge” conveys the direct meaning more clearly. The root study should explain that this use belongs to the broader family of fatḥ and that pre-Qur’anic linguistic evidence also exists for its judicial meaning.

Conclusion

The root F T Ḥ begins from a clear core: opening something closed, or removing a barrier that has restricted access.

The study of pre-Islamic language, however, shows that the Qur’an’s first audiences knew more than the concrete use of “opening a door.” In transmitted pre-Islamic poetry and reports concerning the language of Yemen, this same root family also appears in judgment, adjudication, and settlement. Ancient South Arabian evidence further supports this judicial connection in the pre-Islamic period, although it must be distinguished from Qur’anic Arabic and not pressed beyond its evidentiary capacity.

This is an important methodological point: understanding Qur’anic fatḥ must not begin with the historical term futūḥāt, which became common in later periods for Muslim political and military expansion. The linguistic field before the Qur’an should be examined first, followed by the Qur’an’s own uses.

The Qur’an uses this root along several paths. Sometimes a physical door or bundle is opened; sometimes the way for mercy and blessings to arrive; sometimes knowledge becomes accessible; sometimes a dispute reaches a conclusion through judgment; and sometimes this same decisive opening in a confrontation leads to prevailing and victory.

“Victory” is therefore only one branch of F T Ḥ. Even in confrontation, the Qur’an’s placement of naṣr and fatḥ together shows that they do not have exactly the same semantic perspective. Naṣr focuses on help; fatḥ on the situation opening and an outcome being achieved.

The branch of judgment and settlement is not peripheral or a product of later interpretations. Pre-Qur’anic linguistic evidence and clear Qur’anic uses show that fatḥ between two parties can mean adjudication and deciding the matter. The divine description الْفَتَّاح (al-Fattāḥ) occurs in precisely this construction in the Qur’an.

A concise definition capable of encompassing the Qur’anic uses without imposing one particular instance is therefore:

F T Ḥ: removing a closed condition or barrier and opening the way to access, emergence, or an outcome; and, in conflict, opening an unresolved matter through a decision that may lead to prevailing and victory.

This definition allows a door truly to open in the most concrete use; the way for something to arrive to open in the context of mercy; a disputed matter to reach an outcome in adjudication; and fatḥ to appear as a decisive opening in conflict, without any one of these branches being substituted for the entire root.
