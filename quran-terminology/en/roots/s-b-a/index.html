Core Qur’anic meaning: Sabaʾ is the proper name of a historical community and territory in southern Arabia. It appears in two verses, once in the hoopoe’s report to Solomon and once in the description of Sabaʾ’s land and people. Arabic lexicons also record buying wine, heat affecting skin, and a distant journey under S B ʾ, but their derivational connection with the historical name is not established.

1. The name and its pre-Islamic background

Sabaʾ in the Qur’an is a proper name, not a verb or description whose meaning can be obtained by translating its root letters.

Two matters must be distinguished: the name attested before the Qur’an, and other lexical uses collected under S B ʾ in Arabic dictionaries.

A historical name may share letters with a verbal family without its origin being recoverable from that verb’s meaning.

A pre-Qur’anic witness: Sabaʾ in South Arabian inscriptions

Sabaʾ is not known solely from the Qur’an or Islamic-period histories.

Ancient South Arabian inscriptions record it as the name of a political and social entity.

An important example is a bronze altar inscription from Nashshān, dated to the last quarter of the eighth century BCE. It identifies Yathaʿʾamar Watar as “mukarrib of Sabaʾ.”

Mukarrib was a title of rulership in ancient South Arabia.

The inscription also mentions Sabaʾ alongside another political entity. The name therefore denotes an identifiable political and social community.

This clarifies the name’s historical reference:

Pre-Islamic evidence identifies Sabaʾ as a real community and political power, rather than merely a symbolic term for dispersal, travel, or migration.

Other inscriptions also use the name in connection with rulers, people, and territory.

Maʾrib, in present-day Yemen, was an important center. Its archaeological and epigraphic remains indicate organized political life, economic activity, and public works.

The inscriptions are in ancient South Arabian languages; their vocabulary and structures cannot simply be equated with Qur’anic Arabic.

They establish the name’s antiquity, but do not by themselves derive it from the Arabic verb sabaʾa.

Was Sabaʾ originally a personal name?

Islamic-period lexical and genealogical accounts sometimes identify Sabaʾ as a man to whom Yemeni tribal groups traced their descent.

Other accounts directly describe Sabaʾ as a land, town, or community.

These are historically different explanations.

A person’s name can pass to a people, or a territorial name to its inhabitants. Possibility alone does not establish that this happened.

Pre-Qur’anic inscriptions show Sabaʾ to be a longstanding political and social identity.

They do not themselves prove that the name first belonged to a man and then passed to his people or land.

A defensible historical description is therefore:

Sabaʾ is the established name of a community and political territory in southern Arabia, attested in pre-Islamic inscriptions and used in the Qur’an for that historical identity or its associated community.

The name’s derivation from any particular Arabic verbal meaning is not established with certainty.

2. Lexical branches recorded under S B ʾ

Alongside the proper name, Arabic dictionaries record several other uses under these letters.

They matter lexically, but need not all be forced into a single image or meaning.

Their inclusion in later dictionaries does not itself establish that every use was current in precisely that form before Islam.

Sabaʾa yasbaʾu: buying wine

Sabaʾa al-khamra is recorded for buying wine.

This is a specialized use.

سَبَأَ الْخَمْرَ

sabaʾa al-khamra

“He bought wine.”

يَسْبَأُ الْخَمْرَ

yasbaʾu al-khamra

“He buys wine.”

Lexical explanations expressly restrict this usage to wine purchases.

It does not establish a general verb meaning to buy any commodity.

Sibāʾ and masbaʾ

Sibāʾ is a verbal noun associated with buying wine.

Masbaʾ likewise denotes its purchase, or in context the cost of purchase.

An Islamic-period poetic witness illustrates this use.

Ibrāhīm ibn Harmah, a poet of the second Islamic century, describes wine:

كَأْسًا بِفِيهَا صَهْبَاءَ مُعْرَقَةً
يَغْلُو بِأَيْدِي التُّجَّارِ مَسْبَؤُهَا

kaʾsan bi-fīhā ṣahbāʾa muʿraqatan / yaghlū bi-aydī al-tujjāri masbaʾuhā

Translation:

“A cup containing reddish wine, little diluted, whose purchase becomes costly in merchants’ hands.” In the preceding line, a young woman offers the cup; the expression does not mean that she holds it in her mouth.

Masbaʾuhā refers to purchasing the wine.

Its high cost follows from yaghlū, not from S B ʾ itself.

The witness documents the wine-purchasing use in early Arabic.

Ibn Harmah lived under Umayyad and Abbasid rule. His poetry is not direct evidence of pre-Islamic Arabic.

Istabaʾa

Istabaʾa al-khamra also means buying wine.

It is a Form X verb.

In this recorded use it overlaps with sabaʾa.

Form X alone does not make it mean a prolonged search for wine, requesting a purchase, or repeated attempts to buy.

Its established usage here is simply buying wine.

Sabbāʾ and sabīʾah

Sabbāʾ is recorded as a wine seller.

Sabīʾah is a recorded name for wine.

These uses belong to the field of wine and its trade.

Shared letters alone do not establish their precise historical relationship with Sabaʾ.

Sabaʾat-hu al-shams: heat affecting the body

Another branch concerns skin and heat.

سَبَأَتْهُ الشَّمْسُ

sabaʾat-hu al-shams

“The sun affected him and altered his skin.”

The expression concerns heat and sunlight affecting bodily appearance.

Lexical explanations describe changes of color, scorching, or altered skin.

سَبَأَتْهُ النَّارُ

sabaʾat-hu al-nār

The fire scorched him or affected his skin.

This likewise concerns fire scorching the body.

Its meaning follows from the condition of the skin and the effect of heat upon it.

It should not be equated with buying wine, nor should a historical transition between them be invented without evidence.

Sabaʾa al-jilda: burning or removing skin

Sabaʾa al-jilda is also recorded for burning or stripping skin.

The skin itself is the object here.

Some uses concern heat, others detachment or removal.

They can be studied within a shared field involving skin without becoming one identical action.

Insabaʾa al-jildu

Insabaʾa al-jildu means that the skin came off or peeled.

The skin is the subject of the verb.

This differs from sabaʾa al-jilda in construction and subject role.

Subʾah: a distant journey

Subʾah is recorded as a long journey.

For example:

إِنَّكَ تُرِيدُ سُبْأَةً

innaka turīdu subʾatan

“You intend a distant journey.”

The lexical explanation connects long travel with changes in appearance caused by sunlight and hardship.

This offers an intelligible connection between travel and altered appearance.

It does not prove that travel was the original historical meaning of the entire family.

Nor does subʾah justify translating Sabaʾ as “land of migrants” or “traveling people.”

Masbaʾ: a mountain path

Another use of masbaʾ denotes a mountain route or passage.

This belongs to the field of paths and movement.

Masbaʾ for wine purchasing and masbaʾ for a mountain passage do not have identical meanings.

Their identical form does not establish one as the historical source of the other.

Other limited uses

Lexicons also record sabaʾa ʿalā yamīnin kādhibah for taking a false oath heedlessly, and asbaʾa li-amri Allāh for submitting or humbling oneself.

These differ semantically from wine purchasing, skin changes, and long journeys.

Dating them and establishing historical connections requires independent evidence.

They are best treated as recorded Arabic uses, rather than branches necessarily derived from the proper name.

Lexical result

The dictionary entry S B ʾ contains several branches:

Buying wine; heat affecting and removing skin; long journeys and mountain paths; and a few limited uses.

None of these verbs or common nouns occurs in the Qur’an.

The Qur’anic use covered by this entry is exclusively the proper name Sabaʾ.

The study should therefore center the name and its two occurrences, rather than derive the verses’ meanings from unrelated lexical branches.

3. Qur’anic forms and grammar

The name appears twice in the Qur’an.

Sabaʾin in Al-Naml 27:22

مِنْ سَبَإٍ

min Sabaʾin

Al-Naml 27:22

From Sabaʾ.

Sabaʾin is a proper name in the genitive after min.

In the Ḥafṣ reading used here it bears genitive nunation. This is a feature of reading and inflection, not merely spelling.

Li-Sabaʾin in Sabaʾ 34:15

لِسَبَإٍ

li-Sabaʾin

Sabaʾ 34:15

For Sabaʾ.

The preposition lām is attached to the name.

The following noun is genitive.

The verse then has a plural suffix in maskanihim.

The pronoun helps identify the referent as a human community.

Variation in reading and inflection

Readings with and without nunation are recorded for the name.

Grammatical analyses sometimes connect the distinction to construing Sabaʾ as a person or population, or as a land or tribe.

These grammatical explanations do not themselves establish the name’s historical origin.

A proper name can refer to a person, community, or territory, with grammar reflecting that construal.

Nunation does not prove that the Qur’an is introducing a person named Sabaʾ.

Its absence likewise does not prove that the name cannot denote a community.

Each use must be understood from its sentence and context.

4. Min Sabaʾin: the hoopoe’s report in Al-Naml 27:22

فَمَكَثَ غَيْرَ بَعِيدٍ فَقَالَ أَحَطْتُ بِمَا لَمْ تُحِطْ بِهِ وَجِئْتُكَ مِنْ سَبَإٍ بِنَبَإٍ يَقِينٍ

fa-makatha ghayra baʿīdin fa-qāla aḥaṭtu bi-mā lam tuḥiṭ bihi wa-jiʾtuka min Sabaʾin bi-nabaʾin yaqīn

Al-Naml 27:22

Translation:

“It stayed away only briefly, then said: I have learned what you have not learned, and I bring you certain news from Sabaʾ.”

The hoopoe addresses Solomon.

It first claims knowledge of something Solomon does not yet know.

It then announces certain news from Sabaʾ.

What does Sabaʾ mean here?

Min identifies the source or point of origin.

The hoopoe says it has brought news from Sabaʾ.

Sabaʾ therefore identifies a territory or community about which it has learned.

The name does not mean travel, migration, purchase, or dispersal.

Although the hoopoe has returned with news, movement and arrival follow from jiʾtuka and min.

Sabaʾ itself names the report’s origin.

Sabaʾin and nabaʾin: similar sounds, different roots

The sentence contains two words:

سَبَإٍ

Sabaʾin

Al-Naml 27:22

Sabaʾ; a proper name.

نَبَإٍ

nabaʾin

Al-Naml 27:22

News.

Their sounds and endings are close.

Their roots and meanings differ.

Sabaʾ is a proper name.

Nabaʾ belongs to N B ʾ and means news.

Bi-nabaʾin yaqīn describes news that the hoopoe presents as certain.

The sound correspondence creates verbal harmony without making the meanings identical.

Min Sabaʾin bi-nabaʾin yaqīn means certain news from Sabaʾ, not that Sabaʾ itself means news or knowledge.

Sabaʾ’s community in the continuing narrative

In Al-Naml 27:23, the hoopoe reports finding a woman ruling them.

Sabaʾ is therefore more than an uninhabited geographical point in this report.

The account describes a community with a ruler, population, and power structure.

Al-Naml 27:24 reports the woman’s and her people’s religious conduct.

The subsequent narrative introduces Solomon’s letter and the ruler’s response.

In Al-Naml 27:32, she seeks the chiefs’ opinions in deciding what to do.

In Al-Naml 27:33, the chiefs state their strength but leave the decision to her.

Together these passages depict leadership, prominent advisers, and political and military capacity.

None of those features is the name’s lexical meaning.

The name identifies the community; the narrative supplies its government and conduct.

Does the Qur’an name Sabaʾ’s ruler?

The narrative describes a woman ruling the people of Sabaʾ.

It does not give her personal name.

Names supplied in later historical or narrative accounts should not be silently inserted into the verse.

Al-Naml 27:22 does not require inserting the ruler’s name, form of government, or other historical details into the translation of Sabaʾ.

The proper name remains, while contextual analysis explains the community.

The result for Al-Naml 27:22

Sabaʾin names the territory and community reported on by the hoopoe.

Min identifies the report’s origin.

The claim to certainty follows from nabaʾin yaqīn.

The woman’s rule, people’s conduct, and other characteristics appear in later verses.

These details should not become the name’s lexical definition.

5. Li-Sabaʾin: land, provision, and communal life in Sabaʾ 34:15

لَقَدْ كَانَ لِسَبَإٍ فِي مَسْكَنِهِمْ آيَةٌ جَنَّتَانِ عَنْ يَمِينٍ وَشِمَالٍ كُلُوا مِنْ رِزْقِ رَبِّكُمْ وَاشْكُرُوا لَهُ بَلْدَةٌ طَيِّبَةٌ وَرَبٌّ غَفُورٌ

laqad kāna li-Sabaʾin fī maskanihim āyatun jannatāni ʿan yamīnin wa-shimālin kulū min rizqi rabbikum wa-shkurū lahu baldatun ṭayyibatun wa-rabbun ghafūr

Sabaʾ 34:15

Translation:

“For Sabaʾ there was indeed a sign in their dwelling place: two gardens, to the right and left. Eat of your Lord’s provision and give thanks to Him—a good land and a forgiving Lord.”

Li-Sabaʾin includes lām.

Here lām relates the sign to Sabaʾ.

Fī maskanihim then identifies the people’s dwelling place.

Why does the pronoun hum matter?

Hum in maskanihim is plural.

It shows that Sabaʾ here concerns a human community.

A name singular in form need not mean one individual.

Ethnic and geographical names can take plural pronouns with reference to their people.

The verse concerns Sabaʾ’s people and their dwelling place.

Contextual explanation can therefore say “the people of Sabaʾ.”

The basic translation can retain the name without an explanatory addition.

Two gardens to the right and left

جَنَّتَانِ عَنْ يَمِينٍ وَشِمَالٍ

jannatāni ʿan yamīnin wa-shimāl

Sabaʾ 34:15

Two gardens to the right and left.

This describes two gardens on either side of their habitation.

The gardens relate to land, water, and access to natural resources.

The command to eat the Lord’s provision and thank Him follows.

Agriculture, gardens, and provision are among the community’s blessings here.

None is the meaning of Sabaʾ itself.

The name cannot be lexically translated as “gardens,” “fertile land,” or “wealthy people.”

Gardens and provision describe the community’s circumstances.

Baldatun ṭayyibah: describing the land

The verse ends with baldatun ṭayyibatun wa-rabbun ghafūr.

Baldah identifies a land or settlement.

Ṭayyibah describes its good condition.

These belong to other roots.

The land’s favorable condition is not part of Sabaʾ’s definition.

A land can prosper in one period and suffer destruction in another while retaining its name.

The name is therefore distinct from its people’s historical and economic circumstances.

Changed conditions in Sabaʾ 34:16

After the people turn away, Sabaʾ 34:16 describes the flood of al-ʿarim and the replacement of their gardens.

A destructive event transforms the former situation.

Sayla al-ʿarim consists of other words.

Sabaʾ does not mean flood, destruction, or collapse.

Nor does the name identify a precise historical date, particular dam, or engineering details of a waterworks failure.

Archaeological evidence of irrigation and construction around Maʾrib can inform historical study, but matching a specific event to the verse requires its own investigation.

Dispersal in Sabaʾ 34:19

After describing their conduct and condition, Sabaʾ 34:19 states:

وَمَزَّقْنَاهُمْ كُلَّ مُمَزَّقٍ

wa-mazzaqnāhum kulla mumazzaq

Sabaʾ 34:19

And We scattered them completely.

The phrase describes scattering them.

Mazzaqnāhum belongs to M Z Q.

Dispersal follows from that verb, not the name Sabaʾ.

This matters because an Arabic proverb also associates Sabaʾ with scattering.

6. Tafarraqū aydī Sabā: a people’s name or a word for dispersal?

Arabic records the following expressions, commonly without hamzah in the name:

تَفَرَّقُوا أَيْدِي سَبَا

tafarraqū aydī Sabā

They scattered like the groups of Sabaʾ.

تَفَرَّقُوا أَيَادِي سَبَا

tafarraqū ayādī Sabā

They scattered in every direction like the groups of Sabaʾ.

They describe groups scattering in different directions.

Their explanations refer to Sabaʾ’s dispersed population.

The thematic connection with Sabaʾ 34:19 is clear, since the verse also describes dispersal.

The name must nevertheless remain distinct from the story associated with it.

Sabā remains a proper name in the proverb.

Dispersal comes from tafarraqū and the whole expression.

A proverb about dispersal does not make Qur’anic S B ʾ mean scattering.

Recording the proverb in a lexicon does not establish its date without independent evidence.

A historical name may enter a proverb because of a famous event.

The proverb’s meaning then follows from its story and usage; a later event need not determine the name’s original meaning.

Sabaʾ names the community; dispersal is part of an event reported about it.

The two should not be conflated.

7. The relationship between the two Qur’anic occurrences

The two uses refer to a recognizable historical identity in different contexts.

Al-Naml 27:22 introduces a report about a community with a ruler, chiefs, and particular religious practices.

Sabaʾ 34:15 describes its habitation, gardens, provision, and people’s circumstances.

Different subjects in the two contexts

In Al-Naml, the report concerns Solomon’s learning about another community, followed by communication and interaction with its ruler.

In Sabaʾ, the subject is blessing, gratitude, turning away, and changed communal circumstances.

The difference does not give the name two independent lexical meanings.

A proper name can appear in different narratives without changing its basic identity.

A country’s name can occur in an account of government and another of agriculture and economy.

Different subjects do not make its name two separate words.

Do the narratives concern the same historical period?

Sharing the name does not itself establish that all events in the two chapters were simultaneous.

Nor does it prove that Al-Naml’s ruler remained in power during the events described in Sabaʾ.

A community’s name can persist across many periods.

Each event’s date requires independent evidence.

These verses supply neither calendar dates nor every ruler’s name.

Lexical study must not substitute for a complete historical reconstruction.

The name and communal identity

In Al-Naml 27:23, the plural suffix in tamlikuhum refers to the people ruled by the woman.

Al-Naml 27:24 uses qawmahā for her people.

In Sabaʾ 34:15, hum in maskanihim refers to Sabaʾ’s people.

Both contexts thus concern a human community.

Sabaʾ can identify the people’s collective identity as well as a geographical place.

This contextual conclusion should not become a categorical claim about the name’s origin.

8. Distinctions from similar roots

S B ʾ (Sabaʾ) and N B ʾ (nabaʾ)

They occur together in Al-Naml 27:22.

Sabaʾ is a proper name.

Nabaʾ means news.

Their similar sounds, especially in min Sabaʾin bi-nabaʾin, create verbal correspondence.

Their meanings remain independent.

Sabaʾ does not mean news, nor does nabaʾ name the land.

S B ʾ (Sabaʾ) and Ṣ B ʾ (ṣabaʾa)

Despite superficial resemblance, they differ fundamentally.

S B ʾ begins with sīn.

Ṣ B ʾ begins with ṣād.

Arabic ṣabaʾa is recorded for leaving one religious affiliation for another.

The Qur’an also has al-ṣābiʾīn and al-ṣābiʾūn.

These must not be identified with Sabaʾ.

Sabaʾ names a historical community in the Qur’an.

Ṣābiʾīn is a group designation in religious contexts.

Similar letters and sounds do not establish identical history, identity, or meaning.

S B ʾ (Sabaʾ) and S B Y (saby)

S B Y concerns taking captives and carrying them away.

Lexicons also record carrying or transferring in that family.

S B ʾ in its wine usage concerns buying wine.

Some lexicographers distinguish the hamzated and yāʾ forms in wine-related usage.

Similarity does not make every derivative identical in meaning and history.

Subʾah as a long journey does not make Qur’anic Sabaʾ mean captivity or forced displacement.

S B ʾ (Sabaʾ) and F R Q (farq)

Farq concerns separation and distinction.

Sabaʾ appears in a proverb about scattered groups.

Separation in the proverb comes from tafarraqū and the complete expression.

Sabā names the historical group associated with the dispersal.

The two cannot be reduced to one root or meaning.

S B ʾ (Sabaʾ) and M Z Q (mazq)

Sabaʾ 34:19 contains mazzaqnāhum.

It describes scattering or tearing apart.

Sabaʾ names the community undergoing this event.

Mazq describes the event; Sabaʾ identifies its historical referent.

This keeps a story’s outcome from becoming the lexical meaning of a character’s or community’s name.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

Principal meanings in the entry

Sabaʾ: the proper name of the historical community and territory.

Sabaʾa al-khamra: bought wine; a non-Qur’anic lexical use.

Yasbaʾu al-khamra: buys wine.

Sibāʾ: wine purchasing in recorded usage.

Masbaʾ: wine purchasing or its cost in one use; a mountain path in another.

Istabaʾa: bought wine.

Sabbāʾ: wine seller.

Sabīʾah: a recorded name for wine.

Sabaʾat-hu al-shams: the sun affected and altered his skin or appearance.

Sabaʾa al-jilda: burned or stripped skin, depending on the use.

Insabaʾa al-jildu: the skin came off or peeled.

Subʾah: a distant journey.

Asbaʾa: submitted or humbled oneself in some recorded uses.

These lexical meanings should be recognized without replacing the proper name in the two Qur’anic verses.

Suggested Qur’anic renderings

In Al-Naml 27:22:

وَجِئْتُكَ مِنْ سَبَإٍ بِنَبَإٍ يَقِينٍ

wa-jiʾtuka min Sabaʾin bi-nabaʾin yaqīn

Al-Naml 27:22

“And I bring you certain news from Sabaʾ.”

Sabaʾ remains a proper name.

Commentary may explain that it names a historical community and territory in southern Arabia.

That explanation need not be inserted into the direct translation.

In Sabaʾ 34:15:

لَقَدْ كَانَ لِسَبَإٍ فِي مَسْكَنِهِمْ آيَةٌ

laqad kāna li-Sabaʾin fī maskanihim āyah

Sabaʾ 34:15

“For Sabaʾ there was indeed a sign in their dwelling place.”

The proper name remains here too.

Hum refers to the community’s people.

“The people of Sabaʾ” can clarify context, but “people” is explanatory, not the name’s lexical meaning.

Brief entry meaning and verse-specific meaning

For a brief explanatory box about the name:

“The proper name of a historical community and territory in southern Arabia; its derivational origin is uncertain.”

For Al-Naml 27:22:

“The community and land from which the hoopoe brought its certain news.”

For Sabaʾ 34:15:

“The community whose dwelling place, gardens, and provision are described as bearing a divine sign.”

Preserve Sabaʾin exactly in Al-Naml 27:22’s box.

Preserve li-Sabaʾin with attached lām in Sabaʾ 34:15.

Do not replace the proper name with non-Qur’anic meanings such as buying wine or a long journey.

Assigning “dispersal,” “migration,” “buying,” or “movement” as the name’s core meaning lacks sufficient grounds. Preserve its existing proper-name classification and do not invent a derivational root; these proposals do not replace installed translations or authored explanations.

10. Conclusion

Arabic dictionaries collect several semantic branches under S B ʾ.

They include buying wine, heat affecting skin, peeling skin, long travel, and some limited usages.

Their meanings can be studied, but their dates are not equally clear.

Ibn Harmah’s witness attests wine purchasing in Islamic-period Arabic, not the pre-Islamic period.

The proper name Sabaʾ, by contrast, has independent ancient historical evidence.

Pre-Islamic inscriptions identify a community and political territory in southern Arabia.

They establish the name’s antiquity, not derivation from a particular Arabic verb.

Its two Qur’anic occurrences are both proper names.

Al-Naml 27:22 reports certain news from Sabaʾ; the narrative describes a ruler, population, and political structure.

Sabaʾ 34:15 describes a sign in their dwelling place, two gardens, provision, and a good land.

Later verses describe changed circumstances and dispersal.

None of these events is the name’s lexical meaning.

The proverb tafarraqū aydī Sabā does not turn the name into a word meaning to scatter.

Its resemblance to nabaʾ does not establish a shared root, just as S B ʾ must remain distinct from Ṣ B ʾ and S B Y.

Sabaʾ in the Qur’an names a historical community and territory; it is not a common noun meaning travel, dispersal, purchase, or migration.

The earliest origin of the name requires distinguishing attested inscriptional and Qur’anic usage from later explanatory hypotheses.

Forqan’s translation should preserve the name Sabaʾ.

Al-Naml 27:22 identifies the source of news; Sabaʾ 34:15 relates the name to the people and their habitation.

Historical knowledge can illuminate the setting, but history, genealogical narratives, and similar-looking verbs must not replace the Qur’an’s wording.
