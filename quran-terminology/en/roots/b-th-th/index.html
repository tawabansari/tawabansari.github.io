Qur’anic root ب ث ث — B TH TH

Dispersing, spreading and disclosing: from creatures distributed across the earth to the distress Jacob voices to The God

The three-letter Arabic root ب ث ث (bāʾ thāʾ thāʾ) concerns dispersing, spreading and disclosing something. Its family can describe people or animals spreading through an area, objects distributed around a place, or news passing from one person to others.

In these uses, something situated in one place or held by one person spreads or becomes known more widely. The outcome varies. People’s dispersal may involve disorder; carpets spread around a gathering may indicate an orderly, prepared setting; disclosing distress means voicing suffering held within.

The Qur’an uses this root for dispersing people and animals, mountains becoming scattered particles, people’s dispersal on the Day of Resurrection, and carpets spread out in the Garden. In a different use, Jacob says إِنَّمَا أَشْكُو بَثِّي وَحُزْنِي إِلَى اللَّهِ (innamā ashkū baththī wa-ḥuznī ilā Allāhi), “I complain of my distress and sorrow only to The God.” Bathth here concerns suffering and sorrow a person voices in complaint.

The root occurs nine times in nine verses, in five morphological and lexical groupings. The verb بَثَّ (baththa) and imperfect يَبُثُّ (yabuththu) together occur five times: Al-Baqarah 2:164, Al-Nisāʾ 4:1, Luqmān 31:10, Al-Shūrā 42:29 and Al-Jāthiyah 45:4. The noun بَثّ (bathth) occurs once in Yūsuf 12:86; مُنْبَثّ (munbathth) once in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:6; and مَبْثُوث (mabthūth), in masculine and feminine forms, in Al-Qāriʿah 101:4 and Al-Ghāshiyah 88:16.

Other Arabic forms include أَبَثَّ (abaththa), بَثَّثَ (baththatha), بَاثَّ (bāththa), تَبَاثَّ (tabāththa) and اِسْتَبَثَّ (istabaththa), none occurring in these forms in the Qur’an. Arabic also records the reduplicated four-consonant form بَثْبَثَ (bathbatha), whose structure should be distinguished from ordinary derivatives of the three-consonant root.

1. The root’s meaning and early Arabic uses

Dispersing something in several directions

بَثَّ الشَّيْءَ (baththa al-shayʾa) means dispersing or spreading something. A person may scatter seeds, dates, equipment or a group of animals around a place.

In the language of herding and hunting, بَثَّ الصَّيَّادُ كِلَابَهُ (baththa al-ṣayyādu kilābahu) means that a hunter sent his dogs out in several directions to find or pursue prey. بَثُّ الْخَيْلِ (baththu al-khayli) is also recorded for dispersing horses in a raid and in movement in different directions.

Bathth in these uses is more than movement: it involves spreading out or leaving one place and becoming dispersed across an area.

Lexical sources record تَمْرٌ بَثٌّ (tamrun baththun) for scattered dates, rather than dates collected and packed together in a container or store. بَثَثْتُ الطَّعَامَ (bathathtu al-ṭaʿāma) can also mean turning food over and spreading it about.

Dispersal does not always mean disorder. Carpets spread around a gathering are distributed through the space, yet each may occupy an appropriate place.

Bathth should therefore not be restricted to disorder, throwing or destruction. Its core concerns spreading and dispersal; the subject and context identify their form and outcome. Lexical accounts explain these forms’ meanings but do not independently establish when each was current before revelation.

Evidence from poetry before Islam

The well-known pre-Islamic poet al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī describes the hunting of a wild bull:

«فَبَثَّهُنَّ عَلَيْهِ وَاسْتَمَرَّ بِهِ
صُمْعُ الْكُعُوبِ بَرِيئَاتٌ مِنَ الْحَرَدِ»

“Then the hunter dispersed his dogs against him, while the wild bull’s sound legs, slender at the joints, bore him firmly.”

In this scene, the hunter releases his dogs to pursue a wild bull. فَبَثَّهُنَّ عَلَيْهِ (fa-baththahunna ʿalayhi) concerns sending and dispersing the dogs against the quarry.

The line matters because it shows the root’s physical and dynamic meaning in pre-Islamic poetic language. Bathth here concerns neither distress nor transmitting news: a hunter sends a group of animals after prey.

صُمْعُ (ṣumʿu) in the second hemistich concerns slenderness and well-formed limbs; بَرِيئَاتٌ مِنَ الْحَرَدِ (barīʾātun mina al-ḥaradi) describes legs free of a defect affecting movement. Commentators differ over this hemistich: some understand the wild bull’s legs, others the dogs’ legs. The translation above follows the former explanation. In either case, fa-baththahunna still concerns sending out the dogs.

The verb baththa therefore concerns the hunter’s dogs, rather than an animal’s legs. Differences over the second hemistich should not change the subject matter of the verb itself.

This use also shows that dispersal can have a purpose. The dogs have not been released aimlessly; they are sent to pursue prey. A destination or plan is consequently compatible with bathth.

From scattering objects to spreading speech

بَثَّ الْخَبَرَ (baththa al-khabara) means spreading news and conveying it to others. بَثَّ الْحَدِيثَ (baththa al-ḥadītha) likewise concerns relating and disclosing words that may previously have been kept by one person.

Here what spreads is speech or news, rather than a material object.

When someone passes news to several people, it moves beyond one person’s knowledge and becomes known to others. This gives its use for news a clear semantic connection with scattering objects and creatures.

أَبَثَّ فُلَانًا سِرَّهُ (abaththa fulānan sirrahu) is also recorded for revealing one’s secret to another person. The secret need not be made known to many people: sometimes only one is addressed, and something previously hidden becomes known to that person.

This clarifies two aspects of the family: sometimes dispersal across space is prominent; sometimes the emphasis is on bringing something out of concealment and making it known to an addressee.

Modern broadcasting also uses bathth for transmitting radio and visual programmes. This newer use relates to spreading information, but should not be treated as the root’s earliest historical meaning or the intended meaning of Qur’anic verses.

Does bathth have only one underlying meaning?

Ibn Fāris places “dispersing” and “disclosing” together in explaining this root.

This analysis can explain most established uses. Objects and creatures spread through space; news spreads among recipients; distress within a person is voiced.

That explanatory connection should not make every derivative mean simply “dispersing” in its sentence.

بَثِّي (baththī) in Jacob’s words is an established noun for suffering and distress. Translating it as “my dispersal” would not convey the expression’s actual meaning.

The meanings’ relationship can be understood while preserving each use’s linguistic form and contextual place.

2. Baththa: dispersing creatures and spreading life

Wa-baththa minhumā rijālan kathīran wa-nisāʾan

Al-Nisāʾ 4:1 says:

«يَا أَيُّهَا النَّاسُ اتَّقُوا رَبَّكُمُ الَّذِي خَلَقَكُمْ مِنْ نَفْسٍ وَاحِدَةٍ وَخَلَقَ مِنْهَا زَوْجَهَا وَبَثَّ مِنْهُمَا رِجَالًا كَثِيرًا وَنِسَاءً وَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ الَّذِي تَسَاءَلُونَ بِهِ وَالْأَرْحَامَ إِنَّ اللَّهَ كَانَ عَلَيْكُمْ رَقِيبًا»

“People, practise taqwa towards your Lord, who created you from a single self, created its mate from it, and spread from the two many men and women. Practise taqwa towards The God, in whose name you ask one another, and respect the ties of kinship. Indeed, The God watches over you.”

(Al-Nisāʾ 4:1)

خَلَقَ (khalaqa) and بَثَّ (baththa) occur together here, but their meanings differ.

Khalaqa concerns creating. Baththa concerns men and women spreading from the two mentioned at the verse’s beginning.

The verse thus distinguishes coming into being from spreading. It speaks of a single self and its mate, then of the spread of many men and women.

مِنْهُمَا (minhumā), “from the two,” also matters. Its dual pronoun refers to the two preceding entities. Bathth here therefore relates to the emergence of generations and the spread of humankind.

This use does not itself explain the precise biological means of human reproduction, migration routes or when each community emerged. The verse concerns humankind’s common origin and spread.

The conclusion also mentions taqwa—mindful care to avoid wrongdoing—and kinship ties. This contextual connection places the reminder of human origin and spread alongside people’s responsibilities towards one another.

Bathth itself does not mean kinship or taqwa. Other expressions in the verse establish those concepts.

Wa-baththa fīhā min kulli dābbatin

Among the signs of creation, Al-Baqarah 2:164 says:

«فَأَحْيَا بِهِ الْأَرْضَ بَعْدَ مَوْتِهَا وَبَثَّ فِيهَا مِنْ كُلِّ دَابَّةٍ»

“Then through it He brought the earth to life after its death, and dispersed throughout it creatures of every kind.”

(Al-Baqarah 2:164, excerpt)

فِيهَا (fīhā), “in it,” refers to the earth. The sentence itself therefore identifies the area of dispersal: the earth.

مِنْ كُلِّ دَابَّةٍ (min kulli dābbatin) concerns creatures of different kinds. Bathth here relates to the variety and spread of living creatures on earth.

Luqmān 31:10 also discusses this:

«خَلَقَ السَّمَاوَاتِ بِغَيْرِ عَمَدٍ تَرَوْنَهَا وَأَلْقَىٰ فِي الْأَرْضِ رَوَاسِيَ أَنْ تَمِيدَ بِكُمْ وَبَثَّ فِيهَا مِنْ كُلِّ دَابَّةٍ وَأَنْزَلْنَا مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً فَأَنْبَتْنَا فِيهَا مِنْ كُلِّ زَوْجٍ كَرِيمٍ»

“He created the heavens without pillars you can see, placed firm mountains in the earth so that it would not sway with you, and dispersed throughout it creatures of every kind. And We sent down water from the sky and caused every noble pair or kind to grow in the earth through it.”

(Luqmān 31:10)

Bathth here occurs amid discussion of the heavens, earth, mountains, water and plant growth.

Dispersing creatures is thus part of a broader description of creation and the provision of conditions for life.

Bathth itself, however, does not mean creating all animal species at one particular time. The verb concerns spreading and dispersing. Questions of initial creation, reproduction and other stages of life require separate words and evidence.

Wa-mā yabuththu min dābbatin: the imperfect verb

Al-Jāthiyah 45:4 says:

«وَفِي خَلْقِكُمْ وَمَا يَبُثُّ مِنْ دَابَّةٍ آيَاتٌ لِقَوْمٍ يُوقِنُونَ»

“And in your creation and the creatures He disperses are signs for people who are certain.”

(Al-Jāthiyah 45:4)

Unlike the preceding uses, this has the imperfect verb يَبُثُّ (yabuththu), “He disperses.”

The form can present dispersing as ongoing or repeated. The verse places the spread of creatures alongside human creation among The God’s signs.

The imperfect form alone does not establish that the verse concerns the continuous emergence of new animal species or a particular scientific process. Verbal tense does not determine such details.

In Al-Baqarah, Luqmān and Al-Jāthiyah alike, the principal semantic field is the spread of creatures within creation.

The difference concerns sentence construction and contextual place, rather than a new meaning of the root in each verse.

Wa-mā baththa fīhimā min dābbatin: the heavens and earth

Al-Shūrā 42:29 says:

«وَمِنْ آيَاتِهِ خَلْقُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ وَمَا بَثَّ فِيهِمَا مِنْ دَابَّةٍ وَهُوَ عَلَىٰ جَمْعِهِمْ إِذَا يَشَاءُ قَدِيرٌ»

“And among His signs are the creation of the heavens and the earth and the creatures He has dispersed in the two; and He has power to gather them whenever He wills.”

(Al-Shūrā 42:29)

This differs importantly from the preceding uses. Al-Baqarah 2:164 and Luqmān 31:10 have fīhā, “in it,” referring to earth. Here فِيهِمَا (fīhimā) is dual and refers to the heavens and earth.

A translation should not erase that distinction without justification and restrict the expression simply to earthly creatures.

Bathth itself, however, cannot identify every heavenly being. Interpretations differ over the range of دَابَّة (dābbah), a moving creature, here and what heavenly beings it encompasses.

Some older explanations include angels among heavenly beings. Others understand the expression collectively of the heavens and earth, without necessarily establishing a particular kind of creature in each domain.

The disagreement concerns dābbah’s scope and the construction fīhimā, rather than the meaning of baththa. In each case, the verb expresses dispersing.

Another point is the verse’s conclusion: وَهُوَ عَلَىٰ جَمْعِهِمْ إِذَا يَشَاءُ قَدِيرٌ (wa-huwa ʿalā jamʿihim idhā yashāʾu qadīrun), “and He has power to gather them whenever He wills.”

It first presents creatures as dispersed, then states The God’s power to gather them.

Dispersal and gathering clearly contrast here. Bathth itself does not mean the final gathering or renewed creation; those concepts require the concluding clause and other relevant verses.

3. Munbathth and mabthūth: dispersal in different scenes of Resurrection

Mountains and habāʾan munbaththan

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:4, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:5 and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:6 say:

«إِذَا رُجَّتِ الْأَرْضُ رَجًّا ۝ وَبُسَّتِ الْجِبَالُ بَسًّا ۝ فَكَانَتْ هَبَاءً مُنْبَثًّا»

“When the earth is shaken violently and the mountains are crushed and ground down, becoming scattered particles.”

(Al-Wāqiʿah 56:4; Al-Wāqiʿah 56:5; Al-Wāqiʿah 56:6)

مُنْبَثًّا (munbaththan) is a descriptive form of اِنْبَثَّ (inbaththa), Form VII: something dispersed or spreading.

These verses first describe the earth shaking and the mountains being crushed. The mountains then become هَبَاءً مُنْبَثًّا (habāʾan munbaththan).

هَبَاء (habāʾ) concerns very fine, dust-like particles; مُنْبَثّ (munbathth) describes those particles’ dispersal.

The two words differ. Habāʾ identifies their fine, particulate character, while munbathth identifies their scattered condition.

بُسَّتِ (bussati) in the preceding verse belongs to ب س س (B S S), rather than ب ث ث. It concerns the mountains being crushed and ground down.

بَسّ (bass) and بَثّ (bathth) should not therefore be identified merely because they sound similar. The verses describe two stages or aspects: mountains being crushed and what remains becoming dispersed.

Munbathth itself means neither complete destruction nor becoming dust. The dust-like, particulate character comes from habāʾan and the context of mountains being crushed; the expression does not itself assert annihilation without remainder.

People like al-farāsh al-mabthūth

Al-Qāriʿah 101:4 and Al-Qāriʿah 101:5 say:

«يَوْمَ يَكُونُ النَّاسُ كَالْفَرَاشِ الْمَبْثُوثِ ۝ وَتَكُونُ الْجِبَالُ كَالْعِهْنِ الْمَنْفُوشِ»

“The day when people will be like scattered winged insects, and the mountains like coloured wool teased apart.”

(Al-Qāriʿah 101:4; Al-Qāriʿah 101:5)

الْمَبْثُوث (al-mabthūth) is a passive participle of بَثَّ (baththa), qualifying الْفَرَاش (al-farāsh).

Older Arabic explanations use farāsh for small winged insects, including those moving around light and fire. “Butterflies” may convey part of the image, but should not restrict it to the colourful, tranquil butterflies a modern reader might picture.

The verse uses small winged creatures and their dispersal to describe people’s condition on the day of al-Qāriʿah, the Striking Calamity.

Mabthūth expresses dispersal. The scene’s terror comes from the surrounding verses, including al-Qāriʿah and the mountains’ transformation; mabthūth does not invariably mean frightened and bewildered.

Another distinction is between مَبْثُوث (mabthūth) and مَنْفُوش (manfūsh).

The following verse, Al-Qāriʿah 101:5, compares mountains to الْعِهْنِ الْمَنْفُوشِ (al-ʿihni al-manfūshi), coloured wool teased apart. Manfūsh belongs to ن ف ش (N F SH), here concerning wool being opened out and fluffed.

Mabthūth belongs to ب ث ث and describes the insects’ dispersal.

Both images can involve parts ceasing to be compact, but the roots differ. Nafsh foregrounds fibres being opened and fluffed; bathth foregrounds spreading and dispersal.

Wa-zarābiyyu mabthūthah: spreading in the Garden’s tranquillity

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:13, Al-Ghāshiyah 88:14, Al-Ghāshiyah 88:15 and Al-Ghāshiyah 88:16 say:

«فِيهَا سُرُرٌ مَرْفُوعَةٌ ۝ وَأَكْوَابٌ مَوْضُوعَةٌ ۝ وَنَمَارِقُ مَصْفُوفَةٌ ۝ وَزَرَابِيُّ مَبْثُوثَةٌ»

“There are elevated couches, cups set in place, cushions arranged in rows and carpets spread out.”

(Al-Ghāshiyah 88:13; Al-Ghāshiyah 88:14; Al-Ghāshiyah 88:15; Al-Ghāshiyah 88:16)

These verses describe several kinds of furnishings in the Garden.

نَمَارِق (namāriq) concerns cushions and supports; مَصْفُوفَة (maṣfūfah) describes their orderly arrangement in rows.

زَرَابِيّ (zarābiyy) denotes fine carpets or floor coverings; مَبْثُوثَة (mabthūthah) describes them spread out.

This use is especially important because it shows that bathth does not inherently mean disorder, terror or aimless dispersal.

Al-Qāriʿah 101:4 uses mabthūth in a terrifying scene. Al-Ghāshiyah 88:16 uses the same family to describe a tranquil setting of abundance.

The carpets are spread in different places or present abundantly throughout the setting. Lexically, mabthūthah concerns their distribution. Their beauty, value and suitability for comfort come from zarābiyy itself and the Garden’s blessings in context.

Mabthūthah should therefore not be rendered “strewn haphazardly” or “disordered” here. Such translations distort the verse’s image.

These passages in Al-Qāriʿah and Al-Ghāshiyah offer a useful test of a definition of the root. Defining bathth as invariably disorderly dispersal would fail to explain the Garden’s carpets.

4. Innamā ashkū baththī wa-ḥuznī ilā Allāhi: the distress Jacob voices

Yūsuf 12:86 says:

«قَالَ إِنَّمَا أَشْكُو بَثِّي وَحُزْنِي إِلَى اللَّهِ وَأَعْلَمُ مِنَ اللَّهِ مَا لَا تَعْلَمُونَ»

“He said: I complain of my distress and sorrow only to The God, and I know from The God what you do not know.”

(Yūsuf 12:86)

This is the Qur’an’s only occurrence of the noun bathth. Its meaning differs from the uses concerning the dispersal of creatures and objects.

Jacob speaks of his suffering. He places بَثِّي (baththī), “my distress,” alongside حُزْنِي (ḥuznī), “my sorrow,” and says that he brings his complaint to The God.

Understanding bathth here requires first considering older usage, then examining the story’s context.

Bathth in Arabic: sorrow or the disclosure of sorrow?

Lexical sources record الْبَثّ (al-bathth) for sorrow, grief and suffering borne by a person. Some explain it as distress so intense that the sufferer cannot keep it within and eventually voices it.

Another explanation distinguishes bathth from ḥuzn: ḥuzn can remain in the heart, whereas bathth more particularly concerns suffering disclosed and related to another.

These explanations are close, but distinct.

The first foregrounds suffering’s intensity; the second, its disclosure and expression.

In explaining ب ث ث, Ibn Fāris also connects distress with disclosing: a person expresses suffering to someone else.

This does not establish that bathth is necessarily more intense than ḥuzn in every use. Yūsuf 12:86 includes both without specifying a numerical degree or fixed ranking of intensity.

“Distress and sorrow” or “inner suffering and sorrow” can therefore preserve two aspects of the expression. A lexical explanation should clarify bathth’s relationship to suffering that a person voices, or feels a need to voice.

Why does Jacob say baththī wa-ḥuznī?

Yūsuf 12:84 says:

«وَتَوَلَّىٰ عَنْهُمْ وَقَالَ يَا أَسَفَىٰ عَلَىٰ يُوسُفَ وَابْيَضَّتْ عَيْنَاهُ مِنَ الْحُزْنِ فَهُوَ كَظِيمٌ»

“And he turned away from them and said: Alas for Joseph! His eyes became white from sorrow, while he kept his grief within.”

(Yūsuf 12:84)

The verse includes الْحُزْن (al-ḥuzn), sorrow, and كَظِيم (kaẓīm), one who contains his grief. Jacob grieves, and his suffering has even affected his eyes.

In the following verse, his children address him about continuing to remember Joseph. Jacob then says that he complains of his distress and sorrow only to The God.

This sequence matters. Jacob is neither without feeling nor denying his pain. He grieves, voices it and specifies whom he addresses with his complaint.

Baththī concerns suffering within him, expressed as complaint to The God.

The verse does not say that Jacob had previously publicised all his grief among people. Bathth’s use for distress can concern disclosing it to an addressee, even when that addressee is The God alone.

Widespread circulation among people is therefore not a condition of bathth’s meaning here.

Bathth distinguished from ḥuzn

حُزْن (ḥuzn) concerns sorrow and grief in the Qur’an. A person may grieve over losing someone dear, fear of the future or an adverse event.

Bathth here is also close to sorrow, but its older explanations foreground suffering being voiced and disclosed.

One can thus consider this distinction: ḥuzn names the state of sorrow, while bathth can concern suffering weighing so heavily that the person discloses or relates it.

This boundary is an explanatory aid to understanding the two words, rather than an absolute rule covering every Arabic use without exception.

The verse makes both objects of أَشْكُو (ashkū), “I complain.” Jacob complains of both his bathth and his ḥuzn to The God.

The two are therefore not complete opposites here. Their meanings overlap, while mentioning them separately can foreground two aspects of his suffering.

Is Jacob’s complaint incompatible with his patience?

In Yūsuf 12:83, Jacob says:

«فَصَبْرٌ جَمِيلٌ عَسَى اللَّهُ أَنْ يَأْتِيَنِي بِهِمْ جَمِيعًا إِنَّهُ هُوَ الْعَلِيمُ الْحَكِيمُ»

“Then beautiful patience! May The God bring them all back to me. Indeed, He is the Knowing, the Wise.”

(Yūsuf 12:83, excerpt)

A few verses later, the same Jacob speaks of his bathth and ḥuzn.

Within the story itself, patience and sorrow are not opposites. Jacob can patiently endure while suffering separation from his children.

His hope also remains. In Yūsuf 12:87, he sends his children to search for Joseph and his brother and warns them against despairing of The God’s mercy.

In this story, voicing distress to The God thus accompanies retaining hope and continuing to act.

Innamā ashkū baththī wa-ḥuznī ilā Allāhi should not become a general rule forbidding all expression of suffering or requests for help from other people.

Jacob identifies the addressee of his complaint in that situation. The expression alone does not rule on every form of seeking justice, asking for assistance or telling someone of grief.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:148 gives an exception for someone wronged concerning public expression of unwelcome speech. Al-Mujādilah 58:1 also describes a woman discussing her husband with the Prophet and bringing her complaint to The God.

Voicing pain and seeking justice should therefore be assessed by their subject and manner, rather than treating complaint itself as incompatible with patience or faith.

Does bathth here mean illness?

Some lexical sources also record bathth for severe illness, particularly illness causing suffering too great to conceal.

That use concerns suffering’s intensity. In Yūsuf 12:86, however, ḥuznī and the context of Joseph’s absence clarify the subject.

Translating baththī as “my illness” here therefore lacks sufficient contextual support.

The verse concerns Jacob’s suffering and sorrow. Yūsuf 12:84 describes physical effects of that grief, but bathth in Yūsuf 12:86 should not be turned into the name of a particular illness.

5. Comparison with related roots

B TH TH (baththa) and N SH R (nashara)

نَشَرَ (nashara), from ن ش ر (nūn shīn rāʾ), concerns opening, spreading and publishing. It can describe opening and laying out cloth, circulating news or something spreading.

Bathth is close to it in dispersing and circulating.

Some uses of nashr emphasise unfolding something previously gathered or closed, whereas clear uses of bathth foreground the dispersal or distribution of parts and individuals.

In Al-Baqarah 2:164, وَبَثَّ فِيهَا مِنْ كُلِّ دَابَّةٍ (wa-baththa fīhā min kulli dābbatin) concerns creatures spreading through the earth. In Al-Ghāshiyah 88:16, mabthūthah describes carpets spread out.

“Spreading” or “dispersing” can express this proximity, but does not make every use of bathth and nashr identical.

B TH TH (baththa) and F R Q (farraqa)

فَرَّقَ (farraqa), from ف ر ق (fāʾ rāʾ qāf), also concerns separating and distinguishing. Bathth can involve objects or individuals dispersing and consequently becoming distant from one another.

Separating two things does not necessarily disperse them over a broad area.

Two objects may be separated yet both remain in one place. When a group of animals disperses through a region, by contrast, the area it occupies changes.

Separation may therefore result from bathth in some uses, but its meaning should not be restricted to severing connections between things.

B TH TH (baththa) and B AYN TH (baʿatha)

These roots differ only in their middle consonant, but have different meanings.

بَعَثَ (baʿatha), from ب ع ث (bāʾ ʿayn thāʾ), can concern sending, rousing or raising. The Qur’an uses this root for sending messengers and being raised after death.

بَثَّ (baththa) more particularly concerns dispersing and spreading.

Someone may send a group to perform a task, after which the group disperses across an area. Sending and dispersing are nevertheless distinct actions.

Thus وَمَا بَثَّ فِيهِمَا مِنْ دَابَّةٍ (wa-mā baththa fīhimā min dābbatin) in Al-Shūrā 42:29 should not be translated as “the creatures that were raised.”

The resemblance between بَثّ (bathth) and بَعْث (baʿth) likewise does not identify creatures’ dispersal with renewed creation after death.

B TH TH (bathth) and Ḥ Z N (ḥazina)

The two families occur together in Yūsuf 12:86.

حُزْن (ḥuzn), from ح ز ن (ḥāʾ zāy nūn), expresses sorrow. Bathth here also concerns suffering; lexical explanations foreground its intensity and tendency to be disclosed.

“Distress and sorrow” can convey the pairing. An explanation should clarify that bathth is more than mental disarray or absent-mindedness.

It concerns suffering that weighs upon someone and is voiced or related to an addressee.

B TH TH (bathth) and SH K W (shakā)

شَكَا (shakā), from ش ك و (shīn kāf wāw), concerns expressing suffering, dissatisfaction or a problem to an addressee.

In Yūsuf 12:86, the verb أَشْكُو (ashkū) and noun بَثِّي (baththī) have different roles.

Ashkū expresses the act of voicing suffering. Baththī names what Jacob complains of.

Bathth should therefore not be made identical with the verb “complain” here. Doing so would approach a sentence such as “I complain of my complaining.”

Bathth is the subject matter of the complaint—Jacob’s suffering and distress. Ashkū identifies how he expresses it.

B TH TH (munbathth) and B S S (bassa)

These families occur together in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:5 and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:6.

بُسَّتِ الْجِبَالُ بَسًّا (bussati al-jibālu bassan) concerns mountains being crushed and ground down. هَبَاءً مُنْبَثًّا (habāʾan munbaththan) describes the resulting particles dispersed.

ب س س (B S S) and ب ث ث (B TH TH) should not therefore be confused because their writing and sounds are close.

The two verses also show that dispersal can follow crushing, but bathth does not always mean being crushed. In Al-Nisāʾ 4:1, humans spread and disperse without any meaning of crushing.

6. Suggested translations for Forqan

For بَثَّ (baththa) in verses about creatures, “disperse” or “spread” is appropriate. In Al-Nisāʾ 4:1, “spread from the two many men and women” naturally reflects its connection to the spread of human generations. In Al-Baqarah 2:164 and Luqmān 31:10, “dispersed throughout the earth creatures of every kind” preserves the meaning.

In Al-Shūrā 42:29, retain the dual pronoun فِيهِمَا (fīhimā): “the creatures He has dispersed in the heavens and the earth.” Identifications of the creatures should not enter the basic translation without evidence.

For يَبُثُّ (yabuththu) in Al-Jāthiyah 45:4, “He disperses” or “He spreads” is appropriate.

For مُنْبَثًّا (munbaththan) in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:6, “dispersed” or “scattered” is precise. Habāʾan munbaththan can be rendered “scattered dust-like particles.” Crushing the mountains comes from the preceding verse.

الْفَرَاشِ الْمَبْثُوثِ (al-farāshi al-mabthūthi) in Al-Qāriʿah 101:4 can be rendered “scattered winged insects.” This avoids limiting the image to the beautiful butterflies familiar to modern readers.

For زَرَابِيُّ مَبْثُوثَةٌ (zarābiyyu mabthūthatun) in Al-Ghāshiyah 88:16, “carpets spread out” or “floor coverings laid out” is appropriate. “Scattered and disordered” does not fit the furnishings and comfort of the Garden.

بَثِّي وَحُزْنِي (baththī wa-ḥuznī) in Yūsuf 12:86 can be rendered “my distress and sorrow.” “My suffering and sorrow” is also natural, though it may show the two words’ distinction less clearly. The lexical explanation should clarify suffering being disclosed or voiced.

The following rendering is appropriate for Forqan’s basic text:

“He said: I complain of my distress and sorrow only to The God, and I know from The God what you do not know.”

“Distress” here is more than ordinary distraction: it concerns the weighty suffering Jacob voices to The God.

7. Conclusion

B TH TH concerns dispersing, spreading and disclosing. Material uses include dispersing horses or hunting dogs, spreading furnishings and creatures spreading through space. In speech, the same family can concern circulating news or disclosing a secret or inward suffering.

The Qur’an’s five occurrences of the verb concern the spread of humans and creatures. Al-Nisāʾ 4:1 describes men and women spreading from the two origins mentioned. Al-Baqarah, Luqmān, Al-Shūrā and Al-Jāthiyah concern creatures distributed within creation.

Munbathth in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:6 describes scattered particles following the mountains’ transformation. Mabthūth in Al-Qāriʿah 101:4 uses winged insects’ dispersal to picture people’s condition on the Day of Resurrection. Mabthūthah in Al-Ghāshiyah 88:16, however, concerns carpets spread throughout the Garden.

Dispersal is therefore not inherently disorder or destruction. One context associates it with terror and drastic transformation; another with the distribution and abundance of comforts.

Baththī in Yūsuf 12:86 is another important branch. Jacob complains of his suffering and sorrow to The God. Some lexical explanations foreground the grief’s intensity; others its disclosure and expression. These aspects are compatible, but neither should become the word’s exclusive definition without evidence.

B TH TH’s semantic core can be expressed as dispersing or spreading something across an area, or bringing it out of concealment and disclosing it to another; in its use for distress, it concerns weighty suffering a person voices or complains of.

This encompasses creatures and objects spreading, and explains bathth’s relationship to disclosing news and suffering without restricting every use to physical dispersal.

Each use requires identifying what spreads or becomes known and its outcome in context. People spreading across the earth, carpets laid out in the Garden and Jacob’s complaint of sorrow are distinct situations expressed through one linguistic family.
