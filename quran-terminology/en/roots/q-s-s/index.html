Arabic lexicons place two usages under Q S S that should be distinguished: an Arabic verb concerning pursuit and seeking information, and qass or qissīs, a Christian religious title with a history in neighboring Semitic languages. Their placement under one entry does not by itself establish a shared historical derivation.

A poetic witness and its dating limits

A verse attributed to Umayyah ibn Abī al-Ṣalt, who lived through the pre-Islamic period and the beginning of Qur’anic revelation, reads:

لَوْ كَانَ مُنْفَلِتٌ كَانَتْ قَسَاقِسَةٌ / يُحْيِيهِمُ اللَّهُ فِي أَيْدِيهِمُ الزُّبُرُ

If anyone could escape death, it would have been priests whom The God kept alive, with scriptures in their hands.

Some versions have qasāwisah instead of qasāqisah. Both are recorded plural forms of the priestly title.

The poem’s precise date cannot be established, so it is not a securely dated witness from before Qur’anic revelation. It nevertheless illustrates the title in the transmitted poetic tradition.

Forms examined: قَسَّ (qassa), يَقُسُّ (yaqussu), قَسّ (qass), قَسَس (qasas), قَسَّاس (qassās), تَقَسَّسَ (taqassasa), تَقَسُّس (taqassus), قِسِّيس (qissīs), قِسِّيسُون (qissīsūn), قَسَاوِسَة (qasāwisah), قَسَاقِسَة (qasāqisah), and قُسُوسَة (qusūsah).

Qur’anic form: قِسِّيسِينَ (qissīsīn), occurring once, in Al-Ma’idah 5:82. The other verses quoted here explain the context and related themes; they are not additional occurrences of this root.

1. The Arabic branch: pursuit and seeking information

The Arabic expression qassa l-shayʾ denotes pursuing, seeking out, or following something.

Taqassasa aṣwātahum bi-l-layl describes someone following and listening for people’s voices at night.

Qassās denotes someone who pursues information about others and, in some usages, a gossip or backbiter.

Ruʾbah ibn al-ʿAjjāj, an Umayyad-period poet, describes women who remain apart from gossip and malicious speech:

يَمْسِينَ مِنْ قَسِّ الْأَذَى غَوَافِلَا

They pass the evening unmindful of, and apart from, the pursuit of hurtful talk.

This post-Qur’anic witness illustrates qass in connection with pursuing information and harmful talk.

Lexicons also use qass for a person knowledgeable about tending camels and attentive to their care.

Pursuit, inquiry, and attentive care are prominent in this branch. The evidence presented here does not independently date every one of these senses to the pre-Islamic period.

2. Qissīs: linguistic history and religious office

In Arabic, qissīs and qass name Christian priests or religious leaders associated with religious learning, teaching, and worship.

Some Arabic lexicographers explain the title through pursuit and the search for knowledge. On that interpretation, a qissīs seeks to understand and learn religious matters.

The word’s history in other Semitic languages must also be considered.

Syriac has qaššīšā, meaning an older person, a community elder, or a priest. Classical Ethiopic, or Geʿez, also has qäsis for a Christian priest.

These parallels support the view that the title entered Arabic through contact with Christian communities. They do not, by themselves, establish every stage of its transmission or a particular intermediary language.

The Arabic verb qassa, to pursue, must therefore be distinguished from the religious title qissīs. A common dictionary entry does not prove that Arabic inquiry vocabulary produced the title.

In the Qur’an, qissīs functions as an established religious title. Translation should convey that title, not substitute a proposed root meaning.

3. Qissīsīn in the Qur’an: religious learning and freedom from arrogance

لَتَجِدَنَّ أَشَدَّ النّاسِ عَدٰوَةً لِلَّذينَ ءامَنُوا اليَهودَ وَالَّذينَ أَشرَكوا وَلَتَجِدَنَّ أَقرَبَهُم مَوَدَّةً لِلَّذينَ ءامَنُوا الَّذينَ قالوا إِنّا نَصٰرىٰ ذٰلِكَ بِأَنَّ مِنهُم قِسّيسينَ وَرُهبانًا وَأَنَّهُم لا يَستَكبِرونَ

La-tajidanna ashadda l-nāsi ʿadāwatan li-lladhīna āmanū l-yahūda wa-lladhīna ashrakū. Wa-la-tajidanna aqrabahum mawaddatan li-lladhīna āmanū lladhīna qālū innā naṣārā. Dhālika bi-anna minhum qissīsīna wa-ruhbānan wa-annahum lā yastakbirūn.

You will surely find the Jews and those who associate others with The God the most intense of people in hostility toward those who believe; and you will surely find those who say, “We are Christians,” nearest in affection to those who believe. That is because among them are priests and monks, and because they do not act arrogantly.

Al-Ma'idah 5:82

Qissīsīn is the sound masculine plural of qissīs. It is the delayed noun of anna, accusative with yāʾ as its case marker; minhum supplies the preceding predicate, and ruhbānan is coordinated with qissīsīn in the accusative. The preposition bi- introduces the reason; the following anna-clause concerning their lack of arrogance adds a further part of that explanation.

Minhum, “among them,” matters: the verse does not say that all Christians are priests or monks, but that such people are present among them.

The presence of priests and monks, together with freedom from arrogance, is given as an explanation of this community’s closeness and affection toward believers.

Religious learning is valuable when it prepares a person to recognize truth. Someone who uses learning to claim superiority may resist even a sound statement, regarding acceptance as a loss of status and authority.

The following verse describes how this community responds to revelation:

وَإِذا سَمِعوا ما أُنزِلَ إِلَى الرَّسولِ تَرىٰ أَعيُنَهُم تَفيضُ مِنَ الدَّمعِ مِمّا عَرَفوا مِنَ الحَقِّ يَقولونَ رَبَّنا ءامَنّا فَاكتُبنا مَعَ الشّٰهِدينَ

Wa-idhā samiʿū mā unzila ilā l-rasūli tarā aʿyunahum tafīḍu mina l-damʿi mimmā ʿarafū mina l-ḥaqq. Yaqūlūna rabbanā āmannā fa-ktubnā maʿa l-shāhidīn.

When they hear what has been sent down to the Messenger, you see their eyes overflow with tears because of the truth they recognize. They say, “Our Lord, we believe; so record us among the witnesses.”

Al-Ma'idah 5:83

The expression concerning the truth they recognize shows that they discern truth in what they hear.

Their prior knowledge need not obstruct recognition of new revelation; in this reading of the passage, it can prepare them to recognize it.

These verses describe people whose religious affiliation and prior acquaintance with scripture have not prevented them from accepting truth. Together with Aal Imran 3:113, this cautions against turning a description of a particular response into an unqualified verdict on every member of a religious community.

Their conduct differs from that of people who absolutize their inherited tradition until they refuse any evidence or truth outside it.

4. Religious standing and moral responsibility

The favorable mention of priests and monks in Al-Ma’idah 5:82 does not unconditionally endorse every religious leader or institution.

The Qur’an also discusses monks in other contexts:

اتَّخَذوا أَحبارَهُم وَرُهبٰنَهُم أَربابًا مِن دونِ اللَّهِ وَالمَسيحَ ابنَ مَريَمَ وَما أُمِروا إِلّا لِيَعبُدوا إِلٰهًا وٰحِدًا لا إِلٰهَ إِلّا هُوَ سُبحٰنَهُ عَمّا يُشرِكونَ

Ittakhadhū aḥbārahum wa-ruhbānahum arbāban min dūni llāhi wa-l-Masīḥa bna Maryama wa-mā umirū illā li-yaʿbudū ilāhan wāḥidā. Lā ilāha illā huwa. Subḥānahu ʿammā yushrikūn.

They took their scholars and monks, and the Messiah, son of Mary, as lords apart from The God, although they were commanded only to serve one deity. There is no deity except Him. Glory be to Him above what they associate with Him.

At-Tawbah 9:31

The criticism concerns elevating religious leaders to the position of lords. In the interpretation developed here, religious learning gives no one the right to assume divine authority and independently prescribe religion and religious law for others.

At-Tawbah 9:34 likewise condemns many scholars and monks for consuming people’s wealth wrongfully and barring them from the way of The God.

The qualifier “many of the scholars and monks” defines the scope of that criticism and prevents its extension to every scholar and monk.

The same Qur’an that describes corruption among some religious leaders also recognizes humility and receptiveness to truth among others.

This distinction is stated explicitly concerning the People of the Book:

لَيسوا سَواءً مِن أَهلِ الكِتٰبِ أُمَّةٌ قائِمَةٌ يَتلونَ ءايٰتِ اللَّهِ ءاناءَ الَّيلِ وَهُم يَسجُدونَ

Laysū sawāʾā. Min ahli l-kitābi ummatun qāʾimatun yatlūna āyāti llāhi ānāʾa l-layli wa-hum yasjudūn.

They are not all alike. Among the People of the Book is an upright community who recite the signs of The God during the hours of the night, while they prostrate.

Al-Imran 3:113

A religious title or affiliation alone therefore does not establish a person’s righteousness or corruption.

This principle can be applied to religious leaders in any community. A scholar may help others recognize truth or misuse their authority to turn people away from it.

5. Comparison with related roots

Q S S (qissīs) and R H B (rāhib)

Qissīs is a Christian religious title associated with teaching, religious learning, and worship.

Rāhib belongs to R H B, a family concerning wary reverence and fear, and, in religious usage, ascetic and monastic life.

Al-Ma’idah 5:82 places the two titles side by side.

This distinction does not make their roles mutually exclusive. A monk may have extensive religious learning, and a priest may also live a monastic life.

The separate R H B study examines rāhib and monasticism in greater detail.

Q S S (qissīs) and Ḥ B R (ḥabr)

Aḥbār in the Qur’an denotes scholars among the People of the Book, especially Jewish scholars.

Qissīs is likewise a religious title, but its Qur’anic use concerns Christian leaders.

Both titles may involve scriptural learning and religious teaching, while belonging to different historical and social settings.

Neither qissīs means a righteous scholar nor ḥabr a corrupt scholar. The conduct of those holding these titles determines its moral quality.

Q S S (qass) and Q Ṣ Ṣ (qaṣṣ)

The roots overlap in the branch concerning pursuing or following something.

Arabic lexicons record both qassa l-athar and qaṣṣa l-athar for following a trace.

Q Ṣ Ṣ has a broader Qur’anic range, including following traces, recounting events, and proportionate retaliation.

Q S S occurs in the Qur’an only in the religious title qissīsīn.

Their lexical proximity does not justify translating qissīs as storyteller, narrator, or follower.

6. Translation guidance for Forqan

Render qissīsīn in Al-Ma’idah 5:82 as priests. The word’s explanation may identify Christian religious scholars and leaders.

Scholars alone loses the title’s religious and historical setting; monks would erase the distinction between qissīsīn and ruhbān in the same verse.

Brief root meaning: In the Arabic verbal branch, pursuing and seeking information; in the religious title, qass and qissīs mean a Christian priest or religious leader, with a distinct historical linguistic background.

Meaning in Al-Ma’idah 5:82: Christian priests present among the community described in the verse.

Concluding synthesis

The verbal senses and the religious title must be distinguished. Lexical similarity does not establish historical derivation, nor can it replace a title’s contextual meaning.

The sole Qur’anic occurrence is qissīsīn in Al-Ma’idah 5:82; translating it as priests preserves its distinction from monks.

The passage connects the described community’s affection toward believers with the presence of priests and monks and with their lack of arrogance. The following verse shows a response of recognition and faith.

Religious learning is valuable when it brings people closer to recognizing and accepting truth. Joined to arrogance, it can itself become an obstacle to guidance.
