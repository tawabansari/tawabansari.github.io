Qur’anic Root w-n-y (wanā)

Qur’anic and lexical forms

تَنِيَا
(taniyā)
Slacken, you two; dual imperfect in a Qur’anic prohibition

وَنَى
(wanā)
He slackened; lexical form

وَانٍ
(wānin)
Weak or weary; non-Qur’anic active participle

تَوَانَى
(tawānā)
He slackened; non-Qur’anic verb

Slackening, waning strength, and faltering in continued effort

The Arabic root w-n-y denotes becoming weak, losing strength, or slackening in the performance or continuation of a task. وَنَى means “he grew weak” or “his effort lost intensity or persistence”; وَنَى and وَنْي denote weakness and slackening; and تَوَانَى means to slacken or fall short in pursuing something.

This root is used both for physical weakness and fatigue and for diminished effort and seriousness in fulfilling a responsibility. A person may be unable to continue because of exhaustion, or may slacken despite having the ability to proceed. Both involve reduced strength or activity, but their causes and the person’s responsibility are not identical.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as تَنِيَا in Ṭā Hā 20:42. The God commands Moses and Aaron to go with His signs and not slacken in remembering Him.

1. The root in early Arabic and transmitted poetic evidence

In early Arabic, وَنَى يَنِي was used for becoming weak or tired and losing strength while continuing an activity.

الوَنَى was used of both people and animals. A horse or camel exhausted by prolonged movement and no longer able to maintain its former pace could be described with this word family.

The Muʿallaqah attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays includes:

مِسَحٍّ إِذَا مَا السَّابِحَاتُ عَلَى الْوَنَى
أَثَرْنَ غُبَارًا بِالْكَدِيدِ الْمُرَكَّلِ

misaḥḥin idhā mā al-sābiḥātu ʿalā al-wanā / atharna ghubāran bi-al-kadīdi al-murakkali

Translation: “A swift-running horse that still races on when other running horses, weary and weakened, raise dust on hard, hoof-beaten ground.”

The poet compares his horse with other horses. Their running has brought them to fatigue and declining strength, while his horse remains capable of swift movement.

الْوَنَى here refers to fatigue and slackening that diminish the ability to keep moving.

This witness matters for understanding the pre-Qur’anic sense: the word describes an animal worn down by physical exertion, rather than a person deliberately shirking a responsibility.

Thus, weakness and declining strength in this root do not necessarily mean deliberate laziness or moral negligence. Sometimes the cause is real exhaustion.

This line is transmitted in the well-known Muʿallaqah of Imruʾ al-Qays and its early commentaries. The poem’s attribution to the pre-Islamic poet is established in literary tradition, but its text reaches us through later transmission, and the precise date of each line cannot be independently verified.

Another use of this word family appears in a line attributed to Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd:

كَالْجَوَابِي لَا تَنِي مُتْرَعَةً
لِقِرَى الْأَضْيَافِ أَوْ لِلْمُحْتَضِرْ

ka-al-jawābī lā tanī mutraʿatan / li-qirā al-aḍyāfi aw li-al-muḥtaḍir

Translation: “Like great reservoirs, remaining full to provide hospitality for guests or those present.”

The poet describes large serving bowls kept filled for guests and others in attendance.

لَا تَنِي here conveys that the bowls do not cease to be full and ready for hospitality.

An inanimate bowl has neither will nor moral responsibility. This use therefore shows that w-n-y can concern the continuation or cessation of a state without implying laziness or conscious choice.

The difference between the two witnesses is clear. Imruʾ al-Qays emphasizes the horses’ weariness and weakness. In the line attributed to Ṭarafah, negated slackening expresses continuing readiness to offer hospitality.

The second line is transmitted under Ṭarafah’s name in exegetical and linguistic discussions. This attribution alone does not establish the precise date of composition or eliminate differences in the transmitted wording.

Lexicons record وَنَيْتُ فِي الْأَمْرِ as “I slackened in the matter” and تَوَانَيْتُ فِي حَاجَتِي as “I fell short in pursuing my need or undertaking.”

نَاقَةٌ وَانِيَةٌ also describes a she-camel weakened and worn down by fatigue.

Expressions such as رِيحٌ وَانِيَةٌ or نَسِيمٌ وَانٍ denote a wind or breeze blowing weakly and gently.

These uses show that the root’s core field is not limited to human beings; it can also describe diminished intensity of movement or activity.

Taken together, the evidence supports this early meaning: a decline in strength, intensity, or persistence in carrying out and continuing an activity.

In human affairs, such slackening may result in neglect or failure to meet a responsibility. That consequence, however, depends on the activity and the person’s circumstances, rather than on the root alone.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important forms recorded in Arabic include:

  • وَنَى: He grew weak, lost strength, or slackened in a task.
  • يَنِي: He weakens or slackens in pursuing a task.
  • وَنْي and وَنَى: Weakness, slackening, and diminished strength.
  • وُنِيّ: Another verbal noun recorded for this root.
  • وَانٍ: Weak, weary, or slackening in a task.
  • وَانِيَة: The feminine of وَانٍ; used of a person, animal, or something whose activity has slackened.
  • تَوَانَى: He slackened, fell short in a task, or delayed it through inattention.
  • تَوَانٍ: Slackening and failure to persist in a task.
  • مُتَوَانٍ: Someone who slackens in fulfilling a responsibility or pursuing an undertaking.
  • أَوْنَى: He weakened, tired, or exhausted another.
  • وَنْيَة: Slackening or a pause arising from diminished effort.

Arabic sources also record forms such as وَنَاة and أَنَاة for a woman whose movement or manner is unhurried. Some lexicographers regard the initial hamzah of أناة in this use as a replacement for wāw.

This use should not lead us to make the entire notion of calmness, forbearance, or considered behavior the root meaning of w-n-y.

In another lexical branch, وَنِيَّة is recorded for a pearl. There is no single certain account of its naming or historical relationship to the weakness branch. This use does not determine the meaning of the Qur’anic word.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an.

The only Qur’anic derivative of w-n-y is the verb تَنِيَا.

It is formed from the basic triliteral وَنَى يَنِي. The initial root wāw disappears in the imperfect, as it does in some other triliteral verbs beginning with wāw.

Thus, تَنِيَا belongs to w-n-y even though no wāw is visible in the surface form.

3. Qur’anic usage: وَلَا تَنِيَا فِي ذِكْرِي

Ṭā Hā 20:42

اذْهَبْ أَنتَ وَأَخُوكَ بِآيَاتِي وَلَا تَنِيَا فِي ذِكْرِي

idhhab anta wa-akhūka bi-āyātī wa-lā taniyā fī dhikrī

Ṭā Hā 20:42

Translation: “Go, you and your brother, with My signs, and do not slacken, either of you, in remembering Me.”

This verse continues The God’s conversation with Moses.

Moses is commissioned to go to Pharaoh, who has transgressed. Moses asks his Lord to expand his breast, ease his task, loosen the knot in his tongue, and let his brother Aaron share his responsibility.

In Ṭā Hā 20:33 and Ṭā Hā 20:34, Moses also explains the purpose of Aaron’s companionship: that they may glorify The God often and remember Him often.

The God accepts his request and then, in Ṭā Hā 20:42, commands both to go with the divine signs and not slacken in remembering Him.

The form تَنِيَا

تَنِيَا is the second-person dual imperfect of وَنَى يَنِي.

Its indicative form is تَنِيَانِ: “you two slacken.”

In the verse, however, it follows prohibitive لَا and is jussive. The final nūn is therefore omitted, leaving تَنِيَا.

The final alif marks the dual subject, indicating that two people are addressed.

At the beginning of the verse, the singular command اذْهَبْ addresses Moses, while his brother is included in the same command. In وَلَا تَنِيَا, however, the address is explicitly dual.

This distinction matters: the prohibition against slackening applies to both Moses and Aaron.

A singular rendering addressed only to Moses would therefore fail to convey the full Arabic construction.

فِي ذِكْرِي specifies the sphere of the verb. What they must not slacken in is remembering The God.

Here فِي marks the sphere in which slackening might occur. It does not imply physically entering a place.

The phrase directly means:

“Do not slacken, either of you, in remembering Me.”

What does not slackening in remembering The God mean?

ذِكْرِي belongs to the separate root dh-k-r and here concerns remembrance of The God.

In this context, however, remembrance of The God is not detached from the mission of Moses and Aaron.

Moses has already asked The God to let Aaron share his responsibility so that together they may glorify Him often and remember Him often. Having accepted that request, The God now commands those same two people to maintain remembrance of Him.

This connection shows that وَلَا تَنِيَا فِي ذِكْرِي directly relates to Moses’ earlier request.

The next verse specifies their mission: they must go to Pharaoh because he has transgressed.

In Ṭā Hā 20:44, they are also commanded to speak gently to Pharaoh, so that he might take heed or feel reverent fear.

Moses and Aaron thus face a difficult mission before a defiant power while being required to maintain remembrance of The God.

Dhikr here should not be reduced to the uninterrupted repetition of a few verbal formulas. Remembrance of The God may be present in speech, attention, decisions, and the fulfillment of the mission He has entrusted to them.

Nevertheless, the direct sense of ذِكْرِي should not be erased by translating it solely as “conveying the message.” The word concerns remembrance of The God; its connection with conveying the message comes from the context.

These two aspects are linked in the story of Moses and Aaron: they must convey The God’s message and maintain remembrance of Him throughout that responsibility.

For a broader examination of dhikr, Forqan’s study of the root dh-k-r discusses other uses of this family.

Study of dh-k-r

Not slackening does not mean never feeling fear or fatigue

In Ṭā Hā 20:45, Moses and Aaron express their concern. They fear that Pharaoh may act precipitately against them or transgress further.

In Ṭā Hā 20:46, The God tells them not to fear, for He is with them, hearing and seeing.

These verses help clarify the limits of وَلَا تَنِيَا.

The God asks them not to slacken in remembering Him, yet the narrative itself shows that they face anxiety and the prospect of real danger.

Not slackening therefore does not mean that a person must never experience fear, pressure, or fatigue.

Someone may fear a difficult responsibility yet pursue it despite that fear. Another person may be capable of a task but decline to pursue it.

These are not identical situations.

In the story of Moses and Aaron, The God does not leave them unaware of danger or treat their concern as an inability to fulfill the mission. He reassures them by reminding them of His presence with them.

The command ولا تنيا should therefore not be turned into a demand for unlimited physical capacity or a denial of natural human feelings.

Its contextual significance is to maintain steadfastness and prevent remembrance and commitment to the responsibility from waning in the face of difficulty.

Slackening and delay: is every slow action a failure?

In Arabic, تَوَانَى فِي الْأَمْرِ can mean to slacken or fall short in carrying out a task.

But not every delay or slow action is tawānī.

Some tasks require time. A person may examine the situation before deciding, consult others, or avoid haste.

If such a pause serves the proper performance of the task, it should not be called slackening without justification.

For example, a physician who examines a patient’s condition before treatment has not necessarily neglected a responsibility. That examination may be essential to doing the job properly.

Conversely, when someone understands a responsibility and has the necessary means but postpones it without reason, tawānī may aptly describe the behavior.

This distinction also matters in the story of Moses and Aaron.

In Ṭā Hā 20:44, The God commands them to speak gently to Pharaoh. Gentle speech does not mean slackening in conveying the truth.

They must remain steadfast in their mission and in remembering The God, but that steadfastness is not to be equated with harshness, violence, or haste.

Thus, steadfastness in responsibility is not the same as severity or violence in conduct.

A person can speak gently while remaining firm in stating the truth.

4. Differences from related roots

w-n-y (wanā) and w-h-n (wahn): slackening in activity and weakness of strength

In the Qur’an, w-h-n concerns weakness and diminishing strength, including bodily conditions, hardship, or confrontation with pressure.

W-n-y can likewise refer to weakness and fatigue, but in work and responsibility it highlights waning effort and persistence.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:139, believers are told not to weaken or grieve in the face of hardship.

In Ṭā Hā 20:42, the focus is more specific: Moses and Aaron must not slacken in remembering The God.

The roots overlap, but no occurrence of wahn should be replaced with wanā without examining its use.

Wahn can express weakness itself, whereas in work-related usage wanā also draws attention to its effect on continued activity and seriousness.

w-n-y (wanā) and f-t-r (futūr): subsiding activity

F-t-r concerns diminished intensity, subsiding, and slackening after activity.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:20 describes beings who glorify night and day without slackening.

This use is semantically close to Ṭā Hā 20:42.

Yet f-t-r may describe subsiding heat, intensity, or activity, while w-n-y in the evidence considered here is close to weakening strength or persistence in an undertaking.

The distinction is a tendency, not an absolute boundary.

w-n-y (wanā) and k-s-l (kasal): diminished effort and reluctance to act

Kasal in Arabic concerns laziness and sluggishness in doing something.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:142 describes people who rise for ṣalāh sluggishly.

In some uses, w-n-y may likewise lead to neglect, but it is not inherently limited to deliberate laziness.

The tired horse in Imruʾ al-Qays’ poem provides a clear example. Its weakness comes from physical exhaustion, not a morally blameworthy reluctance to fulfill a duty.

Rendering every use of w-n-y as “laziness” is therefore inaccurate. In Ṭā Hā 20:42, “do not slacken” fits better than “do not be lazy.”

w-n-y (wanā) and b-ṭ-ʾ (buṭʾ): slackening and slowness

Buṭʾ concerns slowness, late arrival, or delay.

W-n-y refers to weakening strength and waning activity, which may lead to slowness.

Someone may perform a task slowly but carefully and persistently. Slowness does not necessarily mean slackening.

Conversely, someone may begin quickly but later fail to continue.

Slowness of movement and slackening in continued activity are thus related but distinct concepts.

In Ṭā Hā 20:42, ولا تنيا should not be reduced to “do not delay,” since the issue is not slackening in remembering The God.

Study of b-ṭ-ʾ

w-n-y (wanā) and ʿ-j-z (ʿajz): inability and failure to persist

ʿAjz concerns inability to do something.

W-n-y can describe diminished strength and slackening in pursuit, but it does not necessarily mean complete inability.

A person may be capable of a task yet fail to pursue it. Another may truly be unable to perform it without being justly accused of deliberate slackening.

This distinction matters for moral responsibility.

Genuine inability differs from abandoning a duty through indifference. The word w-n-y alone therefore cannot justify a definitive judgment about people’s intentions or degree of fault without examining their circumstances.

Study of ʿ-j-z

w-n-y (wanā) and ṣ-b-r (ṣabr): slackening and steadfastness

In Qur’anic usage, ṣabr concerns steadfastness and holding oneself to a course or within a situation.

W-n-y may describe waning strength and persistence along that same course.

In doing what is right, ṣabr and avoiding slackening can complement one another.

But ṣabr is not always the opposite of w-n-y: a person may restrain themselves to prevent wrongdoing and avoid rushing into an action.

Persistence has positive moral value only when the undertaking itself is right.

The root w-n-y therefore does not imply that every stop or decrease in activity is blameworthy. The task, the person’s ability, and the direction of the behavior must be considered.

5. Translation proposals for Forqan

For وَنَى in activity-related contexts, suitable equivalents include “grew slack,” “lost persistence,” or “fell short in the task.”

In bodily contexts, “lost strength,” “grew weary and weak,” or “his strength diminished” is more natural.

For الوَنَى, suitable equivalents include “weakness,” “slackening,” “diminished strength,” or, in a context of fatigue, “exhaustion.”

وَانٍ can be rendered “weak,” “weary,” or “someone who has slackened in pursuing a task.”

For وَانِيَة describing an animal, “weary and weak” fits. For movement or the blowing of wind, “weak” or “of low intensity” is more natural.

Suitable equivalents for تَوَانَى include “slackened,” “fell short in the task,” or “procrastinated in pursuing it.”

For transitive أَوْنَى, “weakened,” “made weary,” or “exhausted” is proposed.

In Ṭā Hā 20:42, a suitable translation of وَلَا تَنِيَا فِي ذِكْرِي is:

“And do not slacken, either of you, in remembering Me.”

This rendering preserves the root’s core sense and explicitly conveys the dual address to Moses and Aaron. The plural Persian form, together with the context, also includes both; adding “you two” makes the dual explicit.

“Do not fall short in remembering Me” also fits the context, though “do not slacken” is closer to the root’s central field.

“Do not become tired in remembering Me” is unsuitable, because it may suggest that The God demands that they never experience natural fatigue.

“Do not delay in remembering Me” restricts the meaning too narrowly to time.

“Never forget Me” approaches an implication of the command, but forgetting belongs to the root n-s-y and is not identical to w-n-y. Forgetting and slackening in remembrance may be connected, yet remain distinct concepts.

For the full verse, the following rendering is proposed:

“Go, you and your brother, with My signs, and do not slacken, either of you, in remembering Me.”

6. Conclusion

In Arabic, w-n-y denotes growing weak, losing strength, and slackening in the continued pursuit of an activity.

Early poetic evidence shows the root used both for physical fatigue and, when negated, for uninterrupted activity. A weary horse may be وَانٍ; vessels continually ready for hospitality may be described as لا تني: they do not cease to remain ready.

The family is therefore not limited to deliberate laziness or moral negligence. Slackening may result from real exhaustion, reduced capacity, or indifference to responsibility. Each case depends on circumstances and context.

The Qur’an contains only تَنِيَا in Ṭā Hā 20:42. It is a dual imperfect in a prohibitive construction, addressed to Moses and Aaron.

The God asks them to go with His signs and not slacken in remembering Him.

The command directly relates to Moses’ earlier request for Aaron’s companionship and frequent remembrance of The God. It also occurs within a difficult mission confronting Pharaoh, a mission requiring steadfastness, attentiveness, and trust in The God.

In the following verses, Moses and Aaron express their concern and The God reassures them. Not slackening should therefore not be equated with never fearing or never experiencing any natural fatigue.

Nor does the command to persist license harsh conduct. Those same two people must speak gently to Pharaoh. Firmness in truth and gentleness in expression can coexist.

The root w-n-y ultimately denotes growing weak or losing strength or seriousness in performing and continuing a task. In Qur’anic usage, وَلَا تَنِيَا commands Moses and Aaron not to slacken or fall short in remembering The God. It calls for maintaining commitment to responsibility, not denying natural fatigue or treating every considered pause as negligence.
