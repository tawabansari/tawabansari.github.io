The three-consonant Arabic root f-s-r concerns clarifying, revealing, and explaining something whose meaning or purpose is not clear to the listener. فَسَرَ الشَّيْءَ and فَسَّرَهُ mean explaining something and making its reality or intended meaning clear.

In the same family, تَفْسِير means explanation and clarification, while اِسْتِفْسَار means requesting an explanation.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as تَفْسِيرًا in al-Furqān 25:33. God assures Muhammad that the opponents bring him no example, argument, or objection without God bringing him the truth with a better explanation.

Tafsīr here does not name a discipline codified in later periods. It denotes the act of clarifying the truth and explaining a matter about which a question or objection has been raised.

The semantic core of f-s-r is making something’s meaning or reality clear, resolving its ambiguity for the listener so that the intended meaning can be understood more fully.

1. The root in early Arabic

Explaining and clarifying

In established Arabic usage, فَسَرَ الشَّيْءَ means explaining and clarifying something.

فَسَّرَ الْكَلَامَ means explaining a statement and making its intended meaning clear.

Early lexicons explain الفَسْر through البَيَان, explanation. They also use كَشْفُ الْمُغَطَّى, uncovering what is covered.

This expression helps explain the relationship between the word’s meaning and its use.

For example, someone may know a sentence’s words yet not understand their relationship or the speaker’s intention. Explaining the sentence’s structure and context makes clear what was obscure to that listener.

Here, tafsīr does not mean adding unrelated remarks. It means clarifying a meaning to be understood from the sentence itself and its contextual indications.

Explaining a statement does not always mean discovering a hidden secret, however. Sometimes the subject is clear from the outset, but the listener needs an explanation to understand its details or the relationships between its parts.

F-s-r should therefore not be restricted to uncovering secrets or hidden matters.

The standing of pre-Qur’anic evidence

A historical definition must distinguish usage established for Arabic during revelation from usage independently attested before Islam.

تَفْسِيرًا in al-Furqān 25:33 directly attests this family in the Arabic of revelation.

Early lexicons also record explanation, clarification, and disclosure as meanings of فسر and فسّر.

Among the evidence examined for this study, no independently datable pre-Islamic poetic example securely establishing this root’s meaning has been verified. This limitation does not prove that no such evidence exists.

Definitions in dictionaries compiled centuries later cannot alone establish that every form in this family circulated with the same semantic range in pre-Islamic Arabic poetry and speech.

What can be stated with clear support is that Qur’anic tafsīr means clarification and explanation, a meaning also recorded in early lexicons.

تَفْسِرَة: an indication of an unseen condition

Lexicons record another use of this family, called تَفْسِرَة.

In some early medical writings, tafsirah referred to a urine specimen examined by a physician, who inferred information about illness from its color and appearance.

The material examined served as evidence of a condition not directly visible.

For example, a physician might use an observable sign to judge a patient’s internal condition.

This usage relates to clarifying or disclosing something unknown.

An important historical qualification applies, however. The early lexicographer al-Jawharī suggested that this expression might have originated in a later period.

Without independent evidence, the medical use of tafsirah cannot therefore be treated as the root’s original, pre-Islamic meaning.

Nor does it establish that tafsīr began as medical diagnosis and subsequently came to mean explaining speech.

What is evident is a shared sense of disclosure through signs and explanation, not a securely established chronological sequence of these uses.

2. Linguistic forms and semantic core

The principal recorded forms include:

  • فَسَرَ: explained, clarified, or revealed something’s intended meaning.
  • فَسْر: explanation and clarification.
  • فَسَّرَ: explained and made meaning clear.
  • تَفْسِير: explaining and disclosing meaning or purpose.
  • مُفَسِّر: someone who explains a subject or statement.
  • مُفَسَّر: something explained and clarified.
  • اِسْتَفْسَرَ: requested an explanation.
  • اِسْتِفْسَار: a request for clarification or explanation.
  • تَفْسِرَة: in recorded medical usage, a sign or specimen examined to understand a patient’s condition.

Classical Arabic recognizes both the basic verb فَسَرَ and the Form II verb فَسَّرَ in the sense of explaining.

In some constructions, Form II can emphasize active or more extensive performance. Yet the doubled sīn in فَسَّرَ does not alone establish that every tafsīr must be longer, deeper, or more certain than any other explanation.

Such qualities must be established from actual usage and context.

Only the Form II verbal noun تَفْسِيرًا appears in the Qur’an.

The verbs فَسَرَ, فَسَّرَ, and اِسْتَفْسَرَ, and titles such as مفسر, do not occur in the Qur’anic text.

Forms familiar from later Arabic literature should therefore not be listed as Qur’anic derivatives.

3. Qur’anic usage: truth and the finest explanation in response to objections

Al-Furqān 25:33

وَلَا يَأْتُونَكَ بِمَثَلٍ إِلَّا جِئْنَاكَ بِالْحَقِّ وَأَحْسَنَ تَفْسِيرًا

Wa-la ya'tunaka bi-mathalin illa ji'naka bi-l-haqqi wa-ahsana tafsira.

Translation: “They bring you no example without Our bringing you the truth and a better explanation.”

This is the root’s sole Qur’anic occurrence.

God speaks here of the opponents’ dealings with Muhammad.

They put forward examples, arguments, or objections, and God provides the Messenger with truth and a clearer, better explanation in response.

Understanding تفسيرًا precisely requires distinguishing several parts of the sentence.

بِمَثَلٍ identifies what the opponents put forward.

بِالْحَقِّ refers to a true and sound response.

أَحْسَنَ تَفْسِيرًا emphasizes the superior quality of the explanation.

The verse thus concerns more than giving an answer: the answer must be true and make the matter clear.

What does مَثَل mean here?

مَثَل has various Qur’anic uses.

Sometimes it is an example or comparison helping explain a truth; in opponents’ speech it can concern a claim, objection, or argument.

The context of al-Furqān 25:33 concerns matters opponents raise to challenge the Messenger.

Mathal here should therefore not be restricted to a parable or literary simile.

For example, an objection asking why the whole Qur’an was not revealed at once can fall within the matters addressed by this verse.

Tafsīr itself does not mean giving an example or raising an objection.

One side presents an example or objection; the other clarifies the truth.

The role of أَحْسَنَ

In أَحْسَنَ تَفْسِيرًا, أَحْسَنَ comes from ḥ-s-n.

It expresses better or finer quality.

تفسيرًا is syntactically an accusative specification, identifying the respect in which superiority is asserted: explanation and clarification.

Aḥsan does not itself mean explanation, nor does tafsīr alone mean being the best.

Together, the two words express the quality of clarification.

The divine answer’s superiority does not imply that the opponents’ claim must itself have been true or good, with God’s answer only slightly better.

Arabic can use أَحْسَنَ to express superiority without requiring the comparison’s other side to possess the same positive quality.

بِالْحَقِّ sets the essential criterion: the response conveys truth.

The distinction between truth and explanation

Truth and tafsīr are related but distinct concepts in this sentence.

Truth concerns the correctness and reality of the matter.

Tafsīr concerns clarifying and explaining it.

Someone may state something true so obscurely that the listener cannot understand it well.

Conversely, someone may explain a false statement fluently and engagingly.

Fluency alone therefore does not establish an explanation’s truth.

Al-Furqān 25:33 places truth and good explanation together.

The response is sound in content and superior in its articulation and explanation to what the opponents present.

These two qualities should not be merged into a single concept.

4. Context: gradual revelation and the clarification of responses

In al-Furqān 25:32, opponents ask why the Qur’an was not revealed to Muhammad all at once.

The answer speaks of strengthening the Messenger’s heart and of measured recitation.

Verse 33 then adds that they bring him no example without God bringing him truth and a better explanation.

This connection situates revelation amid questions, objections, and the events of life.

For example, a question may call for an answer that also exposes a hidden error in how the question was framed.

The answer then does more than refute a statement: it clarifies which part of the claim was sound, which lacked support, and how the matter should be understood.

Strengthening the Messenger’s heart

In the preceding verse, God speaks of strengthening Muhammad’s heart.

The Messenger himself was thus a recipient of divine guidance and strengthening during revelation.

When opponents raised a new objection, divine response and clarification could strengthen his understanding and delivery of the message.

The verses’ direct meaning should not be set aside by assuming that the Messenger knew every answer and detail independently of revelation from the outset.

In al-Furqān 25:33, God says جِئْنَاكَ, “We brought to you.”

This expresses receiving a response from God.

Muhammad is commissioned to convey revelation; the clarification in this sentence comes from God.

Clarification in response to different claims

Earlier parts of al-Furqān report various claims by opponents about Muhammad and the Qur’an.

In al-Furqān 25:4, they describe the Qur’an as fabricated speech.

In al-Furqān 25:5, they call it tales of the ancients.

In al-Furqān 25:7, they object to the Messenger’s being human, eating food, and walking about.

In al-Furqān 25:32, they challenge the manner of gradual revelation.

وَلَا يَأْتُونَكَ بِمَثَلٍ in verse 33 thus stands amid varied objections and claims.

Each objection has its own subject, and the Qur’an presents truth and an appropriate explanation in response.

Tafsīr here is an explanation relevant to the issue raised, not an accumulation of information with no clear connection to the question.

5. Tafsīr and taʾwīl: related words with different applications

Later religious and scholarly literature sometimes uses tafsīr and taʾwīl interchangeably and sometimes distinguishes them.

Some early lexical accounts associate both with explaining and clarifying meaning.

Certain later definitions relate tafsīr more to clarifying speech, and taʾwīl to selecting among possible meanings or relating a matter to its underlying reality and outcome.

Understanding their distinction requires examining Qur’anic usage first.

Tafsīr in the Qur’an

In al-Furqān 25:33, tafsīr concerns clarifying truth in response to the opponents’ examples and objections.

In this usage, its Qur’anic meaning is explanation and disclosure of the matter’s truth.

Taʾwīl in the Qur’an

Taʾwīl comes from the separate root ʾ-w-l.

In Yūsuf 12:100, Joseph speaks of the taʾwīl of his earlier dream: the dream’s connection with an event now realized.

Al-Kahf 18:78 and al-Kahf 18:82 speak of the taʾwīl of actions whose reasons or reality Moses did not initially know.

In al-Aʿrāf 7:53, taʾwīl concerns the outcome and realization of what had previously been announced.

Āl ʿImrān 3:7 also concerns the taʾwīl of ambiguous verses and the limits of human knowledge of it.

These uses connect taʾwīl with reality, outcome, or that to which a statement or event ultimately refers.

By comparison, tafsīr in al-Furqān 25:33 emphasizes explaining and clarifying the matter.

Are tafsīr and taʾwīl always entirely separate?

Neither language nor the history of usage supports an exceptionless boundary between these words.

Someone explaining an event’s underlying reality may also discuss its taʾwīl in that explanation.

Later Arabic literature likewise sometimes uses the terms in closely related senses.

Semantic proximity does not justify identifying their roots or transferring every feature of taʾwīl to tafsīr.

Qur’anic تفسيرًا in al-Furqān accompanies clarification of truth. Taʾwīl elsewhere has a broader range concerning reality, realization, and outcome.

This distinction does not establish that anyone capable of explaining a verse necessarily knows the ultimate taʾwīl of every ambiguous passage.

Nor does difficulty concerning some matters’ taʾwīl make understanding and explaining all Qur’anic verses impossible.

Each question should be examined in its own setting with the relevant evidence.

6. Qur’anic tafsīr and the later discipline of exegesis

After the period of revelation, tafsīr became the name of a discipline devoted to explaining the Qur’an’s meanings and concepts.

It has examined Arabic language, morphology, syntax, readings, context, history, transmitted reports, and exegetes’ views.

This extensive body of methods and writings is not the lexical meaning of f-s-r itself.

Al-Furqān 25:33 does not discuss exegetical books compiled centuries later.

Tafsīr there concerns explaining and clarifying truth.

Does every work called tafsīr explain the Qur’an correctly?

The label tafsīr does not alone establish an interpretation’s correctness.

One writer may explain a verse with linguistic precision and contextual care, making its meaning clearer to readers.

Another may hold a religious presupposition or an accepted theory and try to align the Qur’an’s wording with it.

Both may call their work tafsīr, but the shared name does not give their arguments equal value.

An interpretation should be assessed by asking whether word meanings are correctly identified, sentence structure preserved, related verses examined, and conclusions consistent with the Qur’anic text.

It should also distinguish what follows from the verse itself from the author’s inference, possibility, or application.

For example, a verse about a natural event can be explained linguistically and contextually before considering possible connections with present scientific knowledge.

A scientific theory does not become a word’s certain meaning simply because it is attractive or superficially fits the verse.

This distinction permits new research and reflection while keeping explanation, possibility, and certainty separate.

Clarifying the text or adding material from outside it?

Tafsīr may draw on relevant evidence and information to explain speech.

External information attributed to the text without support cannot simply be called the word’s revealed meaning.

For example, where the Qur’an leaves a person unnamed, an explanation of the story may be sound and well supported. Adding a name from a historical report does not itself make that name part of the Qur’an’s statement.

We should distinguish what the text says, what independent evidence establishes, and what remains unknown.

Sound interpretation resolves actual ambiguity as far as evidence permits; it does not invent an unsupported answer for every unknown.

7. Comparison with related roots

F-s-r (فَسَّرَ) and b-y-n (بَيَّنَ)

B-y-n concerns becoming manifest, distinct, or identifiable, and making things clear.

Tabyīn can mean clarifying truth or identifying distinctions between things.

Tafsīr likewise concerns explaining and clarifying meaning.

They are close within the semantic field of clarification.

In al-Furqān 25:33, however, tafsīr responds to the opponents’ examples and objections.

Many uses of bayān concern making a ruling, truth, or distinction apparent.

Tafsīr is thus a kind of clarification, while not every use of bayān necessarily responds to an obscure statement or objection.

F-s-r (فَسَّرَ) and f-ṣ-l (فَصَّلَ)

F-ṣ-l concerns separating parts and making their boundaries clear.

Qur’anic tafṣīl also concerns explaining a subject and clarifying its components separately.

Tafsīr may involve tafṣīl, but explanation does not always mean dividing a subject into multiple parts.

A short sentence may resolve an ambiguity effectively. Elsewhere, an issue needs to be divided into parts to clarify their differences.

The direct meaning of تفسيرًا in al-Furqān 25:33 is clarification, without requiring a lengthy or subdivided explanation.

F-s-r (فَسَّرَ) and sh-r-ḥ (شَرَحَ)

Sharḥ in Arabic concerns opening and expanding.

Applied to speech, it can mean opening up a subject and explaining its components.

Tafsīr likewise concerns clarifying meaning.

The two families’ linguistic imagery nevertheless differs.

Sharḥ foregrounds opening and expansion; tafsīr foregrounds explanation and disclosure of intent.

This should not become an absolute rule that sharḥ is always long and tafsīr always short.

F-s-r (فَسَّرَ) and k-sh-f (كَشَفَ)

Kashf concerns removing a covering and exposing something.

Lexical explanations of f-s-r use كشف المغطى, uncovering what is covered.

This similarity allows tafsīr to be explained through the image of revealing something hidden.

It does not mean every interpretation necessarily discovers an unknown secret.

Sometimes the task is simply clarifying relationships between words or answering a listener’s question.

Kashf thus provides useful imagery for part of tafsīr’s semantic field, without replacing its direct definition.

F-s-r (فَسَّرَ) and s-f-r (سَفَرَ)

F-s-r and s-f-r contain similar letters in a different order.

S-f-r also has established uses concerning disclosure and removal of a covering.

For example, أَسْفَرَ sometimes means becoming light or manifest.

This semantic proximity has led some writers to discuss a historical relationship between the roots.

Reordered consonants and similar meaning do not alone establish historical kinship or a common origin.

F-s-r and s-f-r should therefore be studied as independent roots unless sufficient historical evidence supports a particular derivational relationship.

8. Morphology and syntax of تَفْسِيرًا

In al-Furqān 25:33, تَفْسِيرًا is a Form II verbal noun.

Its perfect verb is فَسَّرَ and its imperfect is يُفَسِّرُ.

تفسيرًا is indefinite and accusative.

In the phrase:

«وَأَحْسَنَ تَفْسِيرًا»

Wa-ahsana tafsira.

تفسيرًا is a specification identifying the kind of superiority expressed by أَحْسَنَ.

The construction indicates that the offered explanation and clarification are better in quality.

Another point is its connection with جِئْنَاكَ بِالْحَقِّ.

جئناك is a perfect verb with a first-person plural subject suffix and a second-person singular object pronoun.

بالحق identifies the content of the divine response.

وأحسن تفسيرا specifies its explanatory quality.

Tafsīr in the verse is thus not unrelated speech detached from truth; it is clarification accompanying a true response.

وَلَا يَأْتُونَكَ بِمَثَلٍ إِلَّا also forms a negative construction with an exception.

Whenever opponents bring an example as an objection, God provides truth and a better explanation in response.

The verse therefore does not merely describe a single question; it states a pattern of responding to objections raised in this context.

This does not put every human work called tafsīr on the same footing as the divine response.

God Himself is the agent of جئناك in the verse.

9. Challenges in understanding and translation

The first error identifies tafsīr’s lexical meaning with the entire structure of exegetical scholarship in later centuries.

The Qur’an speaks of clarifying truth. Naming a discipline and codifying its methods later is a separate historical matter.

A second error equates tafsīr with taʾwīl in every usage.

Their meanings can approach one another in explanation, but Qur’anic uses of taʾwīl concerning dreams, outcomes, and events’ underlying reality cannot be transferred to tafsīr without examination.

A third error assumes a longer explanation is always a better tafsīr.

أَحْسَنَ in al-Furqān 25:33 concerns explanatory quality, not word count, length, or the amount of added information.

A brief, precise explanation may resolve ambiguity better than a very long account.

A fourth error replaces explanation with speculation and storytelling.

Where the Qur’an does not explicitly clarify a matter, adding reports or possibilities does not establish that its certain meaning has been uncovered.

Interpretation must rest on evidence that can be examined.

A fifth error ignores the speaker and addressee in al-Furqān 25:33.

The verse describes God’s response to objections presented to Muhammad.

It should not be translated as though every human interpretation throughout history were God’s direct and infallible speech.

Conversely, human exegetes’ fallibility does not make explaining and researching the Qur’an worthless.

The question is whether the explanation is faithful to the text and evidence.

Finally, tafsīr should not be restricted to replacing an Arabic word with a Persian equivalent.

Translation can contribute to clarification, but understanding a sentence may require syntax, pronoun reference, context, and other uses of a word.

Nor should interpretation stray so far that an author’s explanation replaces the Qur’an’s own speech.

10. Suggested translations for Forqan

For فَسَرَ, suitable linguistic equivalents include “explained,” “clarified,” and “made something’s meaning clear.”

فَسَّرَ may likewise be rendered “explained,” “clarified the meaning,” or “expressed the statement’s intended sense.”

In general usage, تَفْسِير means “explanation,” “clarification,” or “elucidation of meaning.”

اِسْتِفْسَار means “requesting an explanation.”

A مُفَسِّر explains a subject or statement; the title does not itself guarantee the correctness of every explanation offered.

Qur’anic translation

In al-Furqān 25:33:

«وَلَا يَأْتُونَكَ بِمَثَلٍ إِلَّا جِئْنَاكَ بِالْحَقِّ وَأَحْسَنَ تَفْسِيرًا»

Wa-la ya'tunaka bi-mathalin illa ji'naka bi-l-haqqi wa-ahsana tafsira.

Suggested rendering:

“They bring you no example without Our bringing you the truth and a better explanation.”

This preserves tafsīr’s contextual meaning without restricting it to later exegetical scholarship.

The accompanying explanation can clarify that mathal here concerns the examples, claims, and objections raised by opponents.

A brief explanation can distinguish the root’s general meaning from its contextual use as follows:

Word form: تَفْسِيرًا

Root: f-s-r

Brief root meaning: explaining and clarifying meaning or intent; resolving ambiguity.

Meaning in this verse: a better, more precise clarification of truth in response to examples and objections presented to Muhammad; a Form II verbal noun.

This brief explanation identifies the root’s meaning and its contextual use; the history of exegetical scholarship and comparison with taʾwīl are separate discussions.

11. Conclusion

F-s-r concerns explaining and disclosing meaning in Arabic. Its Qur’anic use and early lexical definitions clearly attest this semantic field.

Independently datable pre-Qur’anic evidence verified in this study is insufficient to establish every detail of the root’s meaning. Later technical uses should therefore not be assigned to pre-Islamic Arabic without evidence.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as تَفْسِيرًا in al-Furqān 25:33.

The verse addresses opponents’ objections in the context of gradual revelation. God assures Muhammad that truth and a better explanation will be provided in response to their claims.

Qur’anic tafsīr thus means clarifying a matter, not exclusively naming a collection of reports, opinions, or works compiled later.

Tafsīr and taʾwīl also derive from separate roots. Their applications overlap, but their actual Qur’anic distinctions should not be overlooked.

The semantic core of f-s-r is explaining and clarifying the meaning or reality of something that remains obscure to the listener or needs explanation.

Clarification is valuable when consistent with truth. An explanation may be fluent, lengthy, or engaging, yet elegant expression cannot make it correct if it contradicts the text and evidence.

Al-Furqān 25:33 places truth alongside أَحْسَنَ تَفْسِيرًا. In the divine answer, sound content and explanatory quality belong together.

The same distinction matters for Qur’anic research: first establish what the text says, then explain it clearly with reasoning that can be examined.

Tafsīr does not replace truth; it helps us see and understand it more clearly.
