Covering and being covered: outer garments, accumulation, and traces fading away

1. Introducing the root and its semantic field

The Arabic triliteral root D TH R has several related uses, chiefly covering something, a covering settling over it, things accumulating, and traces disappearing over time.

Dithār denotes a covering or garment worn over clothing close to the body or wrapped around oneself. Tadaththara means covering oneself with it. Dathara and indathara, by contrast, can describe aging, fading, or the disappearance of traces.

These meanings initially seem different, but a relationship is understandable: something can lie over and cover something else; accumulated coverings can hide what lies beneath. A settlement’s remains, for example, can gradually disappear beneath earth and sand.

Such connections should not become a certain historical account of how every meaning arose. Abundant wealth and fading remains are also established uses, each requiring its particular meaning in a sentence.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as الْمُدَّثِّرُ at the opening of Al-Muddaththir. It addresses Muhammad, describing someone who has drawn a garment over himself or wrapped himself in a covering.

Understanding it requires distinguishing the direct meaning of clothing and covering from the addressee’s situation and the following commands.

2. Forms in Arabic and the Qur’an

D TH R occurs only once in the Qur’an, in one morphological form:

الْمُدَّثِّرُ: a masculine Form V active participle meaning someone wrapped in a garment or covering; Al-Muddaththir 74:1.

Other Arabic forms also matter for understanding the family’s historical meanings:

دَثَرَ يَدْثُرُ: grew old, its traces faded, or disappeared.

دَاثِر: something whose traces have aged and faded, such as a building whose remains are disappearing.

دُثُور: fading, deterioration, or the disappearance of traces.

دِثَار: a garment or covering over clothing close to the body, such as an outer covering or blanket.

تَدَثَّرَ: covered himself with a garment or blanket.

مُتَدَثِّر: someone wrapped in a covering.

ادَّثَرَ: covered himself with a dithār; a form closely related morphologically to tadaththara.

دَثْر: abundance or plentiful quantity, particularly abundant wealth.

دُثُور: also recorded as a plural concerning plentiful wealth. It should not invariably be equated with the verbal noun for traces fading away.

This family has several branches, but only al-muddaththir occurs in the Qur’an. A root study should examine the important branches without imposing non-Qur’anic uses on that particular Qur’anic word.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: one occurrence in Al-Muddaththir 74:1

The morphology of al-muddaththir

The underlying explanatory form is al-mutadaththir.

Mutadaththir is the active participle of Form V tadaththara. In the Qur’anic form, the Form V tāʾ assimilates into dāl, producing muddaththir.

The doubled dāl therefore matters morphologically. It does not mean the root has two dāls; its three letters remain D TH R.

Early grammatical and reading accounts also transmit unassimilated al-mutadaththir. This concerns pronunciation and morphology rather than a new independent meaning. Al-Qurṭubī, for example, attributes this form to Ubayy; that report should remain distinct from the article’s base text in the reading of Ḥafṣ.

Al-Qurṭubī: assimilation and the form attributed to Ubayy

Form V here expresses a person taking a covering over himself or wrapping himself in it.

The form alone does not imply repeatedly dressing, consciously avoiding responsibility, or remaining covered for a long time. Such details require other evidence.

3. The semantic core in early Arabic

Covering and accumulation

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris explains the family through تضاعف شيء وتناضده بعضه على بعض: something increasing and its parts lying upon one another.

This definition seeks to connect covering, accumulation, and abundance.

Several fabric layers form a thicker covering. Accumulated wealth highlights abundance. Earth and sand building up over a settlement’s remains can likewise hide and efface its traces.

Ibn Fāris uses this image to explain the relationship between dithār and duthūr.

The analysis is semantically understandable, but does not itself establish the historical sequence of all uses. More securely, covering, accumulation, abundance, and fading traces are recorded within D TH R.

For the Qur’anic use, clothing and covering the body are directly relevant.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: layering, dithār, and abundance

Dithār: an outer covering

Arabic lexical sources distinguish dithār from shiʿār.

Shiʿār denotes clothing next to the body, in close contact with skin or body hair.

Dithār is the covering placed over it.

Someone already wearing clothes may draw another covering over themselves while resting or sleeping. That outer covering can be called dithār.

Tahdhīb al-Lughah, Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, and Lisān al-ʿArab explain dithār through this relationship.

Tadaththara bi-al-thawb likewise means wrapping and covering oneself in a garment.

Muddaththir therefore describes more than someone ordinarily dressed; being covered by a garment or blanket is prominent.

The covering need not necessarily be thick, woolen, intended for sleep, or of a particular color or shape. Such features may belong to an individual case without being inherent in the word.

Dithār and warmth

Some lexical definitions describe dithār as a covering worn over close-fitting clothing for warmth.

This accords with everyday life and the human need to retain warmth.

A familiar use of an object should nevertheless be distinguished from its lexical meaning.

A blanket can serve warmth, rest, bodily covering, or another purpose. Dithār should therefore not be restricted to an instrument for relieving cold.

In Al-Muddaththir 74:1, the word alone does not establish that Muhammad was cold or covered himself to warm up.

Abundance and accumulated wealth

Another branch concerns abundance.

Arabic records مَالٌ دَثْرٌ for plentiful wealth, and أَهْلُ الدُّثُور for possessors of substantial wealth.

Accumulation helps explain this usage.

Dathr in this sense does not simply mean every kind of property or wealth; abundance is prominent.

Nor does this branch make Qur’anic muddaththir mean wealthy or possessing accumulated riches. They share a family but differ in construction and context.

Traces fading and deterioration

Lisān al-ʿArab uses دَثَرَ الرَّسْمُ of a trace that has faded with time.

A deserted dwelling or settlement may initially leave visible signs, gradually covered by wind, rain, and accumulating earth.

Its traces can then be described by dathara: they have aged or faded.

Indithār, also familiar in Persian, belongs to this branch.

Dāthir can also describe a sword long left unpolished and rusted.

Duthūr can therefore denote deterioration and effaced traces rather than simply wearing a garment.

Yet fading away should not imply that the Qur’anic muddaththir wishes to destroy his identity or disappear from the world. The word’s morphology and context do not directly support that interpretation.

Lisān al-ʿArab, Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, and Tahdhīb: covering and fading traces

4. Witnesses from pre-Qur’anic Arabic and the period around revelation

A witness attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays includes:

لَعَمْرِي لَقَوْمٌ قَدْ تَرَى فِي دِيَارِهِمْ
مَرَابِطَ لِلْأَمْهَارِ وَالْعَكَرِ الدَّثِرِ

la-ʿamrī la-qawmun qad tarā fī diyārihim / marābiṭa li-al-amhāri wa-al-ʿakari al-dathiri

“By my life, a people in whose lands you can see tethering places for foals and plentiful herds of camels…”

Al-ʿakar denotes a group or herd of camels; al-dathir expresses their abundance.

The word here concerns plentiful quantity rather than wearing garments or traces growing old.

Early dictionaries, including Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Lisān al-ʿArab, quote the verse and attribute it to Imruʾ al-Qays. La-qawmun is nominative and begins a sentence whose predicate, أَحَبُّ إِلَيْنَا, “dearer to us,” comes in the following verse. This witness is therefore part of a sentence, rather than an independent complete statement.

Variations in wording and the final word’s vowels are also reported. Lexicographers explain that the thāʾ vowel in some transmissions relates to meter or pronunciation. The quoted text should also have nominative لَقَوْمٌ rather than genitive لَقَوْمٍ.

The six poets’ collection: the sentence continues in the next verse

Despite limited variation, the witness clearly uses dathr in the sense of abundance.

The family was therefore not confined to garments and covering in early Arabic.

Sleep and covering

Lexical sources record دَثُور for a covered person and also for someone sleepy or lazy. These senses of the adjective dathūr should not be transferred directly to muddaththir.

An unattributed verse in Lisān al-ʿArab contrasts poor people’s little sleep with the sleep of someone at ease and wrapped in clothing.

Covering relates naturally to rest and sleep here: someone covering themselves for sleep can be described through this family.

This association should not make tadaththur mean sleeping in every occurrence.

People may be awake while wrapped in a garment, or remain briefly covered without sleeping.

Sleep is a common accompanying situation rather than the verb’s necessary meaning.

Since the cited verse’s attribution and date are not securely established, it cannot be treated as a certain pre-Islamic witness. Its lexical transmission nevertheless helps explain the association of covering and sleep.

Lisān al-ʿArab: dathūr and the sleep witness

Deterioration and disappearing signs

Arabic poetry frequently depicts deserted remains and settlements’ weathered traces.

Lisān al-ʿArab quotes a verse containing قَدِيمُهُمْ لَمْ يَدْثُرِ, comparing a group’s enduring honor and past with traces that do not disappear.

The expression means their inherited standing has not deteriorated or faded.

This extends physical deterioration to reputation and social standing.

The cited source does not establish the poem’s date sufficiently to call it pre-Qur’anic without qualification.

It illustrates semantic extension, rather than proving the figurative use’s precise date of origin.

Lisān al-ʿArab: inherited standing that has not faded

What the linguistic witnesses establish

The witnesses show three main fields: outer garments and covering, abundance and accumulation, and deterioration or effaced traces.

Layering and covering can explain connections, but a shared image should not become the definite meaning of every derivative.

For Qur’anic muddaththir, the branch directly involving covering oneself with clothing fits morphology and context.

5. Qur’anic usage: Al-Muddaththir 74:1

Al-Muddaththir 74:1

يَا أَيُّهَا الْمُدَّثِّرُ

yā ayyuhā al-muddaththiru

“O you who have drawn a garment over yourself!”

The verse begins with direct address.

Yā is a vocative particle. Ayyuhā is a construction for addressing someone, and al-muddaththir identifies the addressee by a description.

Al-muddaththir describes someone who has drawn a garment or covering over himself.

In the surah’s context, the addressee is Muhammad, since the ensuing commands concern warning and conveying the message.

Muddaththir itself, however, does not mean prophet, messenger, warner, or recipient of revelation. Those roles follow from other Qur’anic passages and subsequent commands.

In its direct meaning, the word describes a person covered in a garment.

The address and the following command

Al-Muddaththir 74:2

قُمْ فَأَنْذِرْ

qum fa-andhir

“Arise and warn.”

The command to rise and warn immediately follows the address al-muddaththir.

This connection matters semantically.

The first verse addresses someone described as covered in clothing; the second commands him to rise and warn.

The contrast between being covered and being told to rise naturally depicts a transition into activity.

It does not establish that Muhammad must previously have been asleep. Qum can denote physically rising or, in commands to act, initiating action.

The first verse also does not specify how long he had been covered, who placed the garment over him, or why he was in that situation.

The direct sense is therefore that someone who has drawn a garment over himself is addressed, then told to rise and warn.

Taken together, the verses suggest undertaking a public responsibility, but personal details or the addressee’s psychological state should not be added without independent evidence.

Does covering oneself mean withdrawing from responsibility?

A reflective interpretation may understand covering and the command to arise as an image of leaving private repose to undertake public responsibility.

That reading fits the sequence, but its limits should remain clear.

D TH R itself does not mean laziness, fear, evading duty, or deliberate withdrawal from people.

Attributing those motives to Muhammad requires separate evidence.

Being addressed as muddaththir therefore does not establish that he was refusing his mission or covering himself to hide from people.

Being covered is the described situation; rising and warning are the ensuing commands.

A verse can begin with an ordinary human situation and call someone to great responsibility without making that ordinary situation sinful or ethically weak.

Does muddaththir mean wrapped in sorrow or fear?

Historical and narrative reports offer differing accounts of revelation’s circumstances and Muhammad being wrapped in clothing.

Some mention fear or anxiety following the experience of revelation; others refer to resting or being covered with a blanket.

These reports may be examined historically, but the word’s lexical meaning does not depend on them.

Al-muddaththir alone does not establish fear, grief, illness, fever, or sleep.

It describes being covered in clothing.

Independent investigation into that situation’s cause should distinguish evidential reliability from what the Qur’an states directly.

Wa-thiyābaka fa-ṭahhir and its connection to dithār

Al-Muddaththir 74:4

وَثِيَابَكَ فَطَهِّرْ

wa-thiyābaka fa-ṭahhir

“And purify your garments.”

The first verse describes the addressee as covered in a garment; the fourth commands purifying garments.

Their proximity can matter in studying outward clothing and purity.

Thiyāb, however, belongs to another root and should not be derived semantically from D TH R.

Nor does verse 4 specify that the exact blanket covering the addressee is the object of purification.

An independent study may examine garment cleanliness and possible broader implications. That discussion should not be transferred into muddaththir’s lexical meaning.

Muddaththir denotes someone who has covered himself, while fa-ṭahhir commands purification. They occur close together in one surah, each contributing its own meaning.

6. Distinguishing D TH R from related roots

D TH R and Z M L: covering and wrapping oneself in clothing

The nearest Qur’anic expression to al-muddaththir is al-muzzammil at the opening of Al-Muzzammil.

Al-Muzzammil 73:1

يَا أَيُّهَا الْمُزَّمِّلُ

yā ayyuhā al-muzzammilu

“O you wrapped in a garment!”

Both are active participles concerning clothing or wrapping a garment around oneself.

Their lexical origins nevertheless differ.

Muzzammil belongs to Z M L and concerns wrapping and enclosing oneself in a garment.

Muddaththir belongs to D TH R and relates to dithār, an outer covering.

One may therefore say that muddaththir foregrounds the outer covering, whereas muzzammil foregrounds being wrapped in clothing.

This distinction should not be absolute.

Both verbs can describe someone who has covered and wrapped themselves in clothing. Neither necessarily means sleep rather than wakefulness, or fear rather than calm.

The two surahs’ contexts also differ.

Al-Muzzammil 73:2 follows the address with rising at night; Al-Muddaththir 74:2 commands rising and warning.

This concerns the commands’ subjects, rather than making Z M L and D TH R lexically mean nocturnal worship and public warning respectively.

D TH R and L B S: outer covering and clothing

One branch of L B S concerns wearing clothes.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:26 uses libās for human clothing; Al-Baqarah 2:187 uses it figuratively for spouses’ relationship.

Libās can denote what someone wears, without necessarily being an outer layer over another garment.

Established lexical dithār, by contrast, is a covering over clothing next to the body.

Every dithār can thus be a covering or garment, while not every garment is necessarily a dithār.

Figurative uses of libās should not transfer automatically to muddaththir. One clothing word serving as a metaphor for a human relationship does not establish that every related covering word has the same metaphorical meaning.

Study of L B S

D TH R and TH W B: a garment and covering oneself with it

Thawb denotes a garment or clothing.

Dithār primarily concerns a covering drawn over oneself.

Thawb names what is worn, whereas tadaththur expresses covering oneself in a garment.

Al-Muddaththir 74:1 describes the covered person; Al-Muddaththir 74:4 makes the garments themselves the object of purification.

Muddaththir should therefore not be translated as “garment”; it denotes the person who has covered himself with one.

Study of TH W B

D TH R and GH SH Y: being covered and being enveloped

GH SH Y has a broad Qur’anic field, including covering, enveloping, or something being surrounded.

In some verses, for example, night is described as covering day or the earth.

In result, that image resembles covering by a dithār.

GH SH Y is not confined to garments, whereas Qur’anic tadaththur has a more specific connection with clothing.

Being covered is an understandable overlap, but the roots are not completely synonymous.

If GH SH Y expresses unconsciousness, overwhelming fear, or another condition in some context, that sense should not transfer to muddaththir.

D TH R and S T R: covering and concealing

Arabic S T R concerns covering and concealing.

Covering the body with clothes, however, is not always intended as concealment.

Someone may cover themselves for warmth, modesty, rest, or another reason.

Tadaththur foregrounds clothing oneself in a garment, whereas sitr may foreground something remaining unseen.

Rendering muddaththir as “one who hides” could therefore wrongly attribute a motive of concealment.

“One who has drawn a garment over himself” is more precise here.

7. Suggested translations for Forqan

Since al-muddaththir is this root’s sole Qur’anic form, choosing its equivalent does not present extensive complexity.

The current Forqan Persian rendering is:

“O you who have drawn a garment over yourself!”

This is lexically suitable and has several strengths.

First, it preserves “garment,” making the reference to an actual covering clear.

Second, “drawn over yourself” expresses the person’s state rather than simply wearing clothes.

Third, it follows the Arabic without adding sleep, fear, sorrow, or another motive.

“O you who have wrapped yourself in a garment” is also clear but longer in Persian and may overlap more with al-muzzammil.

“O you who have drawn a blanket over yourself” is defensible for dithār, but “blanket” can restrict the use too strongly to sleep and rest.

“O covered one” is brief but obscures the direct connection with clothing.

“O sleeper,” “O frightened one,” “O one hiding from people,” and “O one avoiding responsibility” are unsuitable direct translations, adding information the word does not convey.

The base Forqan Persian rendering should therefore remain “O you who have drawn a garment over yourself!”

Lexical explanation can clarify that it denotes someone covered and wrapped in a garment or outer covering.

All historical details and other root branches need not enter the translation.

8. Conclusion

Arabic D TH R has several established uses: outer garments and covering, accumulation and abundance, and deterioration or fading traces.

Ibn Fāris connects these through things lying over one another. This can explain their semantic relationship without independently proving their historical sequence.

In its familiar lexical use, dithār is a covering over clothing next to the body. Tadaththur denotes covering oneself with such a garment.

The root occurs once in the Qur’an as al-muddaththir in Al-Muddaththir 74:1. It is a Form V active participle, underlyingly al-mutadaththir, with tāʾ assimilated into dāl.

Its direct meaning is someone who has drawn a garment over himself.

The next verse, qum fa-andhir, calls him to rise and warn. Their connection depicts a transition from being covered in a garment to undertaking a public responsibility, without itself establishing sleep, fear, or evasion of duty.

Muddaththir is close in meaning to muzzammil but differs in root. The former relates to dithār and an outer covering; the latter to wrapping oneself in clothing. Context helps establish the precise meaning of each.

Proposed final definition: D TH R is a linguistic family whose important uses concern covering, accumulation, and traces fading away. In Qur’anic muddaththir, it specifically denotes someone who has covered himself with a garment or outer covering, without specifying the cause, psychological state, or whether he is asleep or awake. Brief root meaning: Covering and wrapping in clothing; accumulation and abundance; aging and fading traces.
