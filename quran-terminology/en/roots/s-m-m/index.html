Openings and passages, poison, scorching wind, and penetrating heat

The Arabic root S M M has several branches, notably openings, poison, and intensely hot wind. Early lexical explanations connect them through penetration: an opening permits passage, poison acts within the body, and the intense heat of samūm harms living things.

This shared imagery does not prove that every usage developed from one original historical meaning. Recorded meanings, explanations of their relationship, and established historical origins must be distinguished.

The root occurs four times in the Quran in two principal forms: samm once for an opening in sammi al-khiyāṭ, and samūm three times for scorching wind or heat.

The senses differ. Samm in Al-Aʿrāf 7:40 denotes a needle's tiny eye; samūm in Al-Ḥijr 15:27, Al-Ṭūr 52:27, and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:42 concerns fire, punishment, and intense heat.

Samm does not mean poison in any Quranic occurrence, although poison is a recognized Arabic meaning of the family. Nor should Quranic samūm automatically become a toxic substance, lethal gas, or specified scientific phenomenon.

1. The root in pre-Quranic and early Arabic

Openings, poison, and scorching heat

Arabic lexicons record samm and summ for an opening and also for a poisonous substance.

The first sense denotes an opening through which something can enter or leave. It may be tiny, like a needle's eye or a bodily opening.

In the second sense, samm is a substance capable of harmful or lethal effects after entering the body.

Samūm names an intensely hot, scorching wind affecting bodies and their surroundings.

These three usages are recorded in early and classical Arabic. Their historical assessment requires attention to the age and independence of witnesses.

Scorching wind appears in a poem attributed to a pre-Islamic poet. The Quran itself directly attests “opening” in its own period. Poison is well recorded in early Arabic sources, but dating its emergence and historical relationship to “opening” requires more than dictionary explanations.

Pre-Islamic poetry: an intensely hot day

A poem attributed to ʿAlqamah ibn ʿAbadah, known as ʿAlqamah al-Faḥl, says:

وَقَدْ عَلَوْتُ قُتُودَ الرَّحْلِ يَسْفَعُنِي
يَوْمٌ تَجِيءُ بِهِ الْجَوْزَاءُ مَسْمُومُ

wa-qad ʿalawtu qutūda al-raḥli yasfaʿunī / yawmun tajīʾu bihi al-jawzāʾu masmūmu

“I had mounted the camel's saddle frame, scorched by a day brought by al-Jawzāʾ, a day of burning wind.”

The next line continues:

حَامٍ كَأَنَّ أُوَارَ النَّارِ شَامِلُهُ
دُونَ الثِّيَابِ وَرَأْسُ الْمَرْءِ مَعْمُومُ

ḥāmin ka-anna uwāra al-nāri shāmiluhu / dūna al-thiyābi wa-raʾsu al-marʾi maʿmūmu

“Hot, as though the blaze of fire enveloped the body beneath the clothes, even with the man's head wrapped in a turban.”

Masmūm describes a day marked by samūm and intense heat. Yasfaʿunī conveys scorching, and the following line compares its intensity to fire.

Here masmūm does not mean a day contaminated by poison, but one dominated by scorching wind and heat.

The witness places this usage within the tradition of poetry attributed to pre-Islamic Arabic.

As with many poems compiled later, attribution to a poet differs from dating a particular line precisely. Its transmission in early literature makes it useful evidence for usage, without settling every detail of the word's history.

Samm: an opening and passage

Samm denotes an opening. Samm al-khiyāṭ is a needle's eye, the small hole through which thread passes.

Nostrils and ear openings also belong to this field. Plurals including sumūm and simām are recorded for openings.

Masāmm likewise denotes bodily pores or openings.

One clear branch thus concerns passages for entry and exit.

An Islamic-period witness attributed to the Umayyad poet al-Farazdaq says:

فَنَفَّسْتُ عَنْ سَمَّيْهِ حَتَّى تَنَفَّسَا
وَقُلْتُ لَهُ لَا تَخْشَ شَيْئًا وَرَائِيَا

fa-naffastu ʿan sammayhi ḥattā tanaffasā / wa-qultu lahu lā takhsha shayʾan warāʾiyā

“I relieved his nostrils until he breathed, and said: Fear nothing while behind me.”

Sammayhi denotes his two nostrils.

Opening the way to breathe pictures relief from distress and pressure.

The line illustrates Islamic-period usage. Since al-Farazdaq lived after the Quran's revelation, it is not definite pre-Islamic evidence.

Poison and its bodily effects

Samm also denotes a poisonous substance.

Sammahu can mean administering poison to someone; samma al-ṭaʿāma means putting poison in food.

Masmūm can describe something containing poison or affected by it.

Lexical explanations connect poison and openings through entry into the body and action within it.

The image is intelligible but does not establish that every poison enters only through skin pores, or that the name necessarily arose from this process.

The route by which a toxic substance enters the body and the manner of its effects require independent investigation; the Arabic name alone does not determine them.

Connecting openings and poison through entry is a lexical explanation, not an established derivational history or scientific definition of poison.

2. Semantic core and Quranic forms

The root occurs four times in two principal nouns.

Samm — once in Al-Aʿrāf 7:40, meaning an opening.

Samūm — three times, in Al-Ḥijr 15:27, Al-Ṭūr 52:27, and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:42, concerning scorching wind and heat.

Samma, sammama, tasammama, masmūm, masāmm, and sāmm are recognized Arabic forms but do not occur in these forms in the Quran.

Do samm and samūm mean the same thing?

Some unvocalized spellings resemble one another, but their formations and uses differ.

Samm in Al-Aʿrāf 7:40 is a noun for an opening.

Samūm in the other three verses names scorching wind or heat.

Sumūm can also be a plural of certain uses of samm. This plural should not be confused with samūm, scorching wind: vowels, morphology, and context distinguish them.

Similar spelling alone cannot establish meaning.

Is penetration the single core of every branch?

One familiar lexical analysis explains the family through a way into something's interior.

An opening permits entry, poison penetrates the body, and scorching wind strongly affects living things.

This can illuminate the relationship between the three branches.

A root study nevertheless needs to distinguish two levels:

First, actually recorded meanings: opening, poison, and scorching wind.

Second, the proposed connection: entry or penetration into something else.

Without independent historical evidence, the second cannot replace all the first.

Quranic samūm in particular does not merely mean “something that penetrates.” It names or describes intensely scorching wind or heat.

A definition should preserve the actual branches instead of reducing them all to a general word such as penetration.

3. Sammi al-khiyāṭ: the needle's eye and impossibility

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:40:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ كَذَّبُوا بِآيَاتِنَا وَاسْتَكْبَرُوا عَنْهَا لَا تُفَتَّحُ لَهُمْ أَبْوَابُ السَّمَاءِ وَلَا يَدْخُلُونَ الْجَنَّةَ حَتَّىٰ يَلِجَ الْجَمَلُ فِي سَمِّ الْخِيَاطِ وَكَذَٰلِكَ نَجْزِي الْمُجْرِمِينَ

inna alladhīna kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā wa-stakbarū ʿanhā lā tufattaḥu lahum abwābu al-samāʾi wa-lā yadkhulūna al-jannata ḥattā yalija al-jamalu fī sammi al-khiyāṭi wa-kadhālika najzī al-mujrimīn

Al-Aʿrāf 7:40

“Those who denied Our signs and arrogantly turned away from them will not have the gates of heaven opened for them, nor enter the Garden until a camel passes through a needle's eye. Thus We recompense the offenders.”

Samm here means an opening.

Al-khiyāṭ here denotes a needle, not a tailor. Sammi al-khiyāṭ therefore means a needle's eye.

It accompanies yalija, from W L J, meaning to enter.

The construction is clear: the camel must enter through a very small opening.

The camel and the needle's eye

The verse contrasts objects of very different sizes: a camel and a needle's eye.

A camel is large, while the eye accommodates thread.

Together they form an image of impossibility.

“Until the camel enters the needle's eye” does not promise that this will someday happen. It makes the described group's entry into the Garden conditional on an impossible event.

This form of expression emphasizes the impossibility of the outcome.

Some transmitted variant readings of al-jamal refer to a thick rope. In the reading of Ḥafṣ followed here, al-jamal means a camel. Both images contrast a large object with a tiny opening, but rope should not replace the usual text without explaining the reading difference.

Does samm mean poison here?

Although Arabic samm can denote poison, that sense is inappropriate here.

Samm al-khiyāṭ is the established expression for a needle's eye; yalija likewise suits entry through an opening.

Rendering samm as poison destroys the image.

Sharing a root never licenses choosing an inappropriate sense within a recognized expression.

The scope of the verse's judgment

The verse describes a group with two specified characteristics:

Kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā: they denied God's signs.

Wa-stakbarū ʿanhā: they arrogantly turned away from them.

The severe image thus concerns denial and arrogance.

The verse alone does not establish that everyone lacking clear understanding, experiencing doubt, or declining a religious interpretation necessarily belongs to this group.

Its judgment concerns people described by those characteristics. Removing them and universalizing the judgment goes beyond the text.

This limitation does not weaken the warning: the Quran presents entry into the Garden as impossible for the denial and arrogance described.

4. Nāri al-samūm: fire and scorching wind in the creation of jinn

In Al-Ḥijr 15:27:

وَالْجَانَّ خَلَقْنَاهُ مِنْ قَبْلُ مِنْ نَارِ السَّمُومِ

wa-al-jānna khalaqnāhu min qablu min nāri al-samūm

Al-Ḥijr 15:27

“And the jinn We created earlier from the fire of scorching wind.”

Al-samūm follows nār and specifies the fire's kind or quality.

Nār means fire, while samūm concerns intensely scorching wind or heat.

Rīḥ al-samūm in Arabic is a wind of severe, harmful heat.

Nāri al-samūm therefore evokes fire of scorching, penetrating heat.

Nār and samūm

They are independent words in this expression.

Nār belongs to N W R, and samūm to S M M.

Both may concern heat and burning without being lexically identical.

Fire can burn and produce light. Samūm in familiar Arabic usage is an intensely hot, harmful wind.

Their combination emphasizes the scorching quality of what the jinn were created from.

The separate Forqan study of N W R, “Light, fire, and what illumination reveals,” discusses nār and nūr further.

Study of N W R

Were jinn created from wind or fire?

The verse says min nāri al-samūm, not merely min al-samūm.

Nār must not be omitted to present the verse as saying that jinn were created simply from wind.

Conversely, samūm shows that the expression does not stop at the general word fire.

Al-Raḥmān 55:15 describes the creation of the jānn from mārijin min nār. It is thematically related, but mārij requires study of its own root and context.

Both expressions can be read together without translating one in place of the other.

Is samūm invisible fire?

Some infer invisible fire, a special gas, unknown energy, or a material of specified physical composition.

Samūm itself supplies none of those scientific specifications.

Its recognized meaning concerns scorching wind and heat.

The exact material, properties, and conditions of the jinn's creation are not established by this word's lexical meaning alone.

Conversely, uncertainty about the manner of creation does not justify turning scorching fire into a metaphor devoid of any real meaning.

The verse reports the jinn's creation and mentions the fire of samūm. Its exact manner goes beyond defining this root.

5. ʿAdhāba al-samūm: scorching heat and deliverance

In Al-Ṭūr 52:27:

فَمَنَّ اللَّهُ عَلَيْنَا وَوَقَانَا عَذَابَ السَّمُومِ

fa-manna Allāhu ʿalaynā wa-waqānā ʿadhāba al-samūm

Al-Ṭūr 52:27

“Then The God bestowed favor upon us and protected us from the punishment of scorching wind.”

Samūm here accompanies punishment.

The subject is deliverance from a severe, scorching punishment described through samūm.

Context

In Al-Ṭūr 52:26, those enjoying blessed circumstances recall their fear and concern in their previous life.

In Al-Ṭūr 52:27, they recall God's favor and protection from the punishment of samūm.

In Al-Ṭūr 52:28, they say that they previously called upon Him and affirm that He is the Beneficent, the Merciful.

The sequence identifies the punishment of samūm as something from which they were protected.

Waqānā derives from W Q Y and concerns protecting against harm.

The verse thus distinguishes scorching punishment from protection against it.

Is this punishment poison?

Samūm does not mean poison here.

Although S M M includes poison in Arabic, this expression belongs to scorching heat.

Elsewhere samūm appears alongside fire and hot water.

“Punishment of poison” or “toxic substances” would move beyond the word's established field without evidence.

Nor does the expression specify the punishment's exact physics. Scorching heat is its direct meaning, but a particular temperature, gas, or scientific mechanism requires separate evidence.

6. Fī samūmin wa-ḥamīm: scorching wind and hot water

In Al-Wāqiʿah 56:42:

فِي سَمُومٍ وَحَمِيمٍ

fī samūmin wa-ḥamīm

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:42

“In scorching wind and boiling water.”

The verse concerns the companions of the left, introduced in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:41.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:43 mentions a shade of yaḥmūm, and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:44 says that it is neither cool nor pleasant.

The passage brings together scorching wind, boiling water, and a shade that offers no cooling comfort.

Samūm and ḥamīm

Samūm here concerns scorching wind and heat.

Ḥamīm derives from Ḥ M M and here denotes intensely hot or boiling water.

Both concern heat, but one names scorching wind and the other here refers to hot water.

Samūm should not become boiling water, nor ḥamīm scorching wind.

This distinction matters for translation and the verse's imagery.

Samūm and ḥarūr

Ḥarūr also denotes intense heat or hot wind in Arabic.

Some lexical explanations distinguish their usual times: samūm is associated more with daytime, ḥarūr with night. Those accounts also allow occurrence at other times.

The tendency should not become an absolute rule confining one to day and the other to night.

In Fāṭir 35:21, ḥarūr contrasts with shade:

«وَلَا الظِّلُّ وَلَا الْحَرُورُ»

wa-lā al-ẓillu wa-lā al-ḥarūr

Fāṭir 35:21

“Nor shade and scorching heat.”

In Al-Wāqiʿah, samūm likewise accompanies intense heat and lack of comfort.

The shared field does not make their formations and uses identical.

Does samūm always denote a physical wind?

In early Arabic poetry, it can denote a real, hot desert wind.

The Quran uses the word for the jinn's creation and punishment in the Hereafter.

Using a familiar natural term for an otherworldly subject neither proves that every detail matches earthly winds nor permits eliminating its heat and scorching force.

The language clearly conveys intense, harmful heat; the root alone does not determine its otherworldly mechanism or quality.

7. Comparison with related roots

S M M and TH Q B: an opening and piercing

Thaqb concerns piercing, splitting, and opening a passage.

Samm in Al-Aʿrāf 7:40 names the opening itself.

Sammi al-khiyāṭ concerns the resulting hole, not necessarily the act of making it.

The words overlap in the sense of an opening, without identical formations and ranges.

Thāqib elsewhere in the Quran can concern penetration or piercing brightness. Those meanings must not enter samm al-khiyāṭ without evidence.

S M M and N F DH: an opening and passage through it

Nafadha concerns passing through or finding a way through something.

Samm can name an opening permitting passage.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:40, yalija supplies entry, and sammi al-khiyāṭ identifies its location.

The opening and the act of passing are distinct.

Penetration may likewise help explain the relationship between poison, openings, and samūm, but cannot replace every derivative's specific meaning.

S M M and Ḥ R R: scorching wind and heat

Ḥarr and ḥarūr concern heat.

Samūm in its familiar usage names intensely hot, scorching wind.

Samūm involves intense heat, but not all heat is samūm.

Sunshine, fire, and hot water can be described with different words.

“Scorching heat” explains samūm, but where the wind image matters, “scorching wind” better preserves it.

S M M and N W R: samūm and fire

Nār and samūm appear together in Al-Ḥijr 15:27.

Nār is fire; samūm concerns scorching wind or heat.

Their combination joins two words in a thermal image without giving them a shared root.

Nār can denote ordinary fire or punitive fire. Quranic samūm accompanies severe, harmful heat.

Nār should not always become samūm, nor samūm a general name for all fire.

S M M and Ṣ M M: openings and closure

The roots differ only in their first consonant but remain lexically independent.

A principal branch of S M M denotes an opening or entrance.

Ṣ M M concerns deafness and blocked hearing in the Quran, and includes solidity and closure in Arabic.

The imagery can be contrasted: an opening for entry versus a blocked channel of reception.

This is not historical evidence that one derives from the other.

Phonetic and semantic resemblance must not be confused with established kinship.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

Sammi al-khiyāṭ — Al-Aʿrāf 7:40

A suitable equivalent:

“The eye of a needle”

It is concise, natural, and faithful to the Arabic expression.

In Persian, both “opening of the needle” and “hole of the needle” are accurate; the latter is more immediately familiar.

“Poison of the needle” is incorrect: samm here denotes an opening, not poison.

The complete phrase:

«حَتَّىٰ يَلِجَ الْجَمَلُ فِي سَمِّ الْخِيَاطِ»

ḥattā yalija al-jamalu fī sammi al-khiyāṭ

Al-Aʿrāf 7:40

“Until a camel passes through a needle's eye.”

Nāri al-samūm — Al-Ḥijr 15:27

Proposed equivalent:

“The fire of scorching wind”

Nār must remain fire, while samūm conveys its intensely scorching quality.

A lexical explanation may read:

“Samūm: intensely hot, scorching wind that strongly affects bodies and their surroundings.”

“Scorching fire” is fluent but omits part of samūm's particular imagery.

ʿAdhāba al-samūm — Al-Ṭūr 52:27

Proposed equivalent:

“The punishment of scorching wind”

It preserves samūm and the reference to punishment.

“The punishment of scorching heat” is intelligible but makes the wind image less explicit.

Fī samūmin wa-ḥamīm — Al-Wāqiʿah 56:42

Proposed equivalent:

“In scorching wind and boiling water”

This preserves the two distinct elements.

Samūm is scorching wind; ḥamīm here is intensely hot or boiling water.

They should not collapse into a general “intense heat,” since the Quran places two different words together.

Conclusion

S M M has three important Arabic branches: openings, poison, and scorching wind.

Lexical witnesses record samm for a needle's eye and bodily openings, as well as poison; samūm denotes intensely hot and harmful wind.

The poem attributed to ʿAlqamah al-Faḥl illustrates masmūm for a scorching day in the pre-Islamic poetic tradition. Later witnesses clarify openings and other family meanings.

The root occurs four times in the Quran.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:40, samm denotes the needle's eye in the image of a camel's impossible passage.

In Al-Ḥijr 15:27, samūm accompanies fire in the jinn's creation.

Al-Ṭūr 52:27 describes deliverance from its punishment.

In Al-Wāqiʿah 56:42, samūm joins boiling water in a description of otherworldly punishment.

Entry and penetration can make the relationship between openings, poison, and scorching wind intelligible. This connection must not become a certain historical origin or erase each branch's meaning.

The family can be defined while preserving its branches:

In Arabic, S M M concerns openings and passages, poisonous substances, and intensely scorching wind. Entry and penetration can explain connections between them, but Quranic samm means an opening and samūm scorching wind or intense heat.

The distinction prevents a needle's eye becoming poison, scorching wind becoming toxic gas, or the fire of samūm becoming an unestablished physical phenomenon.

Each word must be understood in its own construction and context. Lexical imagery can illuminate relationships, but cannot replace the Quran's direct meaning.
