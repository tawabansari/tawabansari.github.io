The Qur’anic Root KH F F

From lightness and reduced burdens to loss of steadiness

The root خ ف ف (KH F F) has a clear basic image in Arabic: lightness as opposed to heaviness. This lightness, however, does not refer only to physical weight. Something can be light to carry, a burden can be reduced, scales can be light, and a person can be drawn out of steadiness and considered judgment.

Against this breadth, some translations identify the derivative istikhfāf too narrowly with “humiliating” or “considering worthless.” The Qur’an’s own uses show that this verbal form has a much wider range.

Qur’anic forms

The Qur’anic forms examined here are خَفَّتْ (khaffat), خَفِيفًا (khafīfan), خِفَافًا (khifāfan), the various forms of خَفَّفَ / يُخَفِّفُ / يُخَفَّفُ (khaffafa / yukhaffifu / yukhaffafu), the verbal noun تَخْفِيف (takhfīf), and three uses of Form X: تَسْتَخِفُّونَهَا (tastakhiffūnahā), يَسْتَخِفَّنَّكَ (yastakhiffannaka), and فَاسْتَخَفَّ (fa-stakhaffa). This family occurs seventeen times in the Qur’an, within these five morphological groups.

1. The semantic core: lightness against heaviness and composure

Ibn Fāris identifies one basic principle for KH F F, placing it opposite ثقل (thiql) and رزانة (razānah): heaviness and composure. خَفَّ الشيء (khaffa al-shayʾu) means that something became light, and خفيف (khafīf) describes something without heaviness. The same family also includes a condition becoming lighter, a burden decreasing, movement becoming easier, and moving from a place.

This definition preserves two aspects together: first, the very tangible meaning of weight and burden; second, a branch that contrasts lightness with steadiness, weight of judgment, and composure. Nevertheless, not every use should be interpreted from the outset as “frivolity.” Lightness of a body, of a burden, and of behavior are related branches, not one identical meaning in every sentence.

The basic image can be expressed as follows:

KH F F: having little heaviness or a reduction in it; from this field, greater ease of carrying and movement, or loss of weight and steadiness in behavior and decision.

An Arabic witness before the Qur’an

In the well-known transmission of Imruʾ al-Qays’s Muʿallaqah, the description of a very swift horse includes:

يَزِلُّ الغُلامُ الخِفُّ عَنْ صَهَواتِهِ
وَيُلْوِي بِأَثْوابِ العَنيفِ المُثَقَّلِ

Yazillu al-ghulāmu al-khiffu ʿan ṣahawātihi; wa-yulwī bi-athwābi al-ʿanīfi al-muthaqqali.

The lightweight youth slips from its back, while it sets the heavy rider’s clothes in motion. In this verse, الخِفّ (al-khiff) stands directly against المثقل (al-muthqal). A textual variation between يزل (yazillu) and يطير (yaṭīru) is reported, but the contrast between al-khiff and al-muthqal remains in the familiar transmissions.

This witness shows that lightness and heaviness were a natural, living contrast in the language before the Qur’an. Explaining this root requires no invented complex historical origin or physical image: the experience of weight and carrying itself suffices for the semantic core.

2. Tangible lightness in the Qur’an

“حَمْلًا خَفِيفًا (ḥamlan khafīfan)”: actual lightness followed by heaviness

One of the clearest uses of the root occurs in Al-Aʿrāf:

«حَمَلَتْ حَمْلًا خَفِيفًا فَمَرَّتْ بِهِ ... فَلَمَّا أَثْقَلَتْ»

Ḥamalat ḥamlan khafīfan fa-marrat bihi … fa-lammā athqalat.

“She bore a light burden and went on with it … then, when she became heavy.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:189

At first, the burden is خَفِيف (khafīf); then the same condition enters a stage of heaviness through أَثْقَلَتْ (athqalat). The contrast between lightness and heaviness is present in the verse itself. Khafīf therefore requires no figurative meaning here: the burden is lighter at one stage and becomes heavy afterward. Early linguistic sources likewise understood the contrast this way.

This verse is important evidence because it shows that the root’s basic meaning remains wholly tangible and physical in the Qur’an.

“تَسْتَخِفُّونَهَا (tastakhiffūnahā)”: finding something light

Al-Naḥl speaks of dwellings made from livestock skins:

«وَجَعَلَ لَكُم مِّن جُلُودِ الْأَنْعَامِ بُيُوتًا تَسْتَخِفُّونَهَا يَوْمَ ظَعْنِكُمْ وَيَوْمَ إِقَامَتِكُمْ»

Wa-jaʿala lakum min julūdi al-anʿāmi buyūtan tastakhiffūnahā yawma ẓaʿnikum wa-yawma iqāmatikum.

“And He made for you, from the skins of livestock, dwellings that you find light on the day of your travel and the day of your staying.”

Al-Naḥl 16:80

تَسْتَخِفُّونَهَا (tastakhiffūnahā) does not mean humiliating these dwellings. People find these tents light: carrying, erecting, and moving them is not burdensome as it is with fixed dwellings. Early lexicographers and commentators also explain the phrase through يخف عليكم حملها (yakhiffu ʿalaykum ḥamluhā), “carrying them is light for you.”

This verse is especially important for understanding Form X. استَخَفَّ (istakhaffa) does not in itself mean “treated disrespectfully.” In the Qur’an itself, it can mean finding something light.

“خِفَافًا وَثِقَالًا (khifāfan wa-thiqālan)”: a contrast whose specific application remains open

Al-Tawbah states:

«انْفِرُوا خِفَافًا وَثِقَالًا»

Infirū khifāfan wa-thiqālan.

“Go forth, light and heavy.”

Al-Tawbah 9:41

The literal meaning is clear: “Go forth, light and heavy.” The verse does not, however, specify the precise respect in which lightness and heaviness are meant.

Early exegetical reports consequently offer various possibilities: young and old, prosperous and poor, energetic and sluggish, mounted and on foot, occupied and free. This variation itself shows why none of these applications should be inserted into the lexical meaning of khifāfan.

The root supplies only the contrast between light and heavy. The context keeps the command general, and the two descriptions may encompass a range of different circumstances.

A basic rendering such as:

“Go forth, light and heavy”

is therefore lexically more cautious than one that confines khifāfan to “the young,” “the wealthy,” or “mounted riders.”

3. Takhfīf: reducing heaviness or a burden

Form II of this root has a very consistent meaning in the Qur’an: something previously heavier becomes lighter, or its burden is reduced.

“ذَٰلِكَ تَخْفِيفٌ مِن رَّبِّكُمْ (dhālika takhfīfun min rabbikum)”

In Al-Baqarah, after the ruling on requital and the possibility of pardon and payment, it is said:

«ذَٰلِكَ تَخْفِيفٌ مِن رَّبِّكُمْ وَرَحْمَةٌ»

Dhālika takhfīfun min rabbikum wa-raḥmatun.

“That is a lightening from your Lord, and mercy.”

Al-Baqarah 2:178

Takhfīf here names a reduction in the weight of a ruling or obligation. The word itself does not yet convey all its legal details; those are understood from the preceding sentences.

Familiar specialized senses of “reduction,” such as a price discount or a reduced punishment, should not narrow the Qur’anic field of takhfīf. Its meaning is more basic: reducing burden and heaviness.

“يُرِيدُ اللَّهُ أَنْ يُخَفِّفَ عَنْكُمْ (yurīdu Allāhu an yukhaffifa ʿankum)”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:28 has the same construction:

«يُرِيدُ اللَّهُ أَنْ يُخَفِّفَ عَنْكُمْ وَخُلِقَ الْإِنسَانُ ضَعِيفًا»

Yurīdu Allāhu an yukhaffifa ʿankum wa-khuliqa al-insānu ḍaʿīfan.

“The God wishes to lighten your burden, and the human being was created weak.”

عنكم (ʿankum) specifies the direction of the reduction: some of the weight upon them is removed. The root alone does not identify that burden precisely; it is understood from the preceding rulings in the passage. Morphologically, this word is also Form II of the same root.

“الْآنَ خَفَّفَ اللَّهُ عَنْكُمْ (al-āna khaffafa Allāhu ʿankum)”

In Al-Anfāl, the relationship between lightening and weakness is more explicit:

«الْآنَ خَفَّفَ اللَّهُ عَنْكُمْ وَعَلِمَ أَنَّ فِيكُمْ ضَعْفًا»

Al-āna khaffafa Allāhu ʿankum wa-ʿalima anna fīkum ḍaʿfan.

“Now The God has lightened your burden, knowing that there is weakness in you.”

Al-Anfāl 8:66

The verse then reduces the previous ratio for confrontation. خَفَّفَ (khaffafa) here is therefore not a vague psychological concept: an actual reduction in obligation and pressure is meant.

Lightening punishment

The same construction also appears in the opposite direction:

«لَا يُخَفَّفُ عَنْهُمُ الْعَذَابُ»

Lā yukhaffafu ʿanhumu al-ʿadhābu.

“The punishment will not be lightened for them.”

This expression occurs with minor variations in Al-Baqarah 2:86 and 2:162, Āl ʿImrān 3:88, and Al-Naḥl 16:85. Fāṭir 35:36 also says:

«وَلَا يُخَفَّفُ عَنْهُم مِّنْ عَذَابِهَا»

Wa-lā yukhaffafu ʿanhum min ʿadhābihā.

“Nor will any of its punishment be lightened for them.”

And in Ghāfir 40:49, the people of the Fire ask:

«يُخَفِّفْ عَنَّا يَوْمًا مِّنَ الْعَذَابِ»

Yukhaffif ʿannā yawman mina al-ʿadhābi.

“Let Him lighten for us a day of the punishment.”

All these morphological forms return to the same meaning of reduction and lightening.

Thus takhfīf is neither inherently mercy nor inherently related to punishment. The root expresses only a reduction of heaviness. Whether a ruling, obligation, or punishment is reduced is specified by what the verb concerns and by the context.

4. “خَفَّتْ مَوَازِينُهُ (khaffat mawāzīnuhu)”: lightness of the scales

The Qur’an uses this construction three times:

«وَمَنْ خَفَّتْ مَوَازِينُهُ»

Wa-man khaffat mawāzīnuhu.

“And whoever’s scales are light.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:9
Al-Muʾminūn 23:103

And:

«وَأَمَّا مَنْ خَفَّتْ مَوَازِينُهُ»

Wa-ammā man khaffat mawāzīnuhu.

“But as for the one whose scales are light.”

Al-Qāriʿah 101:8

In all three cases, lightness stands directly opposite ثَقُلَتْ مَوَازِينُهُ (thaqulat mawāzīnuhu), “whose scales are heavy.”

At the level of the root, the expression says only that the scales, or what is being weighed, are light. That this lightness signals loss is understood from what follows in the verse, not from KH F F alone.

In Al-Aʿrāf, the continuation says that these people have brought loss upon themselves; in Al-Muʾminūn, the same outcome is connected with Hell; and in Al-Qāriʿah, they are contrasted with the person whose scales are heavy and who will have عيشة راضية (ʿīshatin rāḍiyatin), “a satisfying life.”

The root should therefore not be defined as:

“KH F F = worthlessness of deeds”

That conclusion belongs to the context of weighing and judgment. The word’s meaning remains “becoming light.”

This distinction matters because the same lightness in Al-Naḥl, describing the tents, is a practical blessing. The root therefore carries no inherently negative moral charge.

5. From lightness of weight to diminished steadiness

The semantic field of KH F F in Arabic is not confined to weight. Just as a light object moves more easily, a person can be drawn from settled composure into movement, haste, or instability.

Classical dictionaries record expressions such as استخفه الفرح (istakhaffahu al-faraḥu) for someone carried out of composure by joy, and استخفه عن رأيه (istakhaffahu ʿan raʾyihi) for someone turned away from, or made unsteady in, their judgment and deliberation. استخف به (istakhaffa bihi) is also recorded in the sense of treating someone as lowly or of little worth.

The branch of disrespect and humiliation is therefore real, but it is not the whole meaning of istikhfāf.

The three Qur’anic uses of Form X show this clearly.

“تَسْتَخِفُّونَهَا (tastakhiffūnahā)”: finding light

In Al-Naḥl 16:80, the objects are tents, and the sentence has a physical, practical meaning: you find them light.

“لَا يَسْتَخِفَّنَّكَ (lā yastakhiffannaka)”: let them not draw you out of steadiness

At the end of Al-Rūm, it is said:

«فَاصْبِرْ إِنَّ وَعْدَ اللَّهِ حَقٌّ وَلَا يَسْتَخِفَّنَّكَ الَّذِينَ لَا يُوقِنُونَ»

Fa-ṣbir inna waʿda Allāhi ḥaqqun wa-lā yastakhiffannaka alladhīna lā yūqinūna.

“So be patient; indeed, the promise of The God is true. And let not those who lack certainty draw you out of steadiness.”

Al-Rūm 30:60

The sentence begins with فاصبر (fa-ṣbir), “so be patient.” The context therefore centers on steadiness and patience. On the other side are people who must not be allowed to draw the addressee out of that steadiness, as expressed by يستخفنك (yastakhiffannaka).

Lexicographers explain this use through لا يستفزنك (lā yastafizzannaka) and لا يستجهلنك (lā yastajhilannaka): do not let them draw you out of sound judgment and forbearance or provoke you into a flighty, immature response. Al-Ṭabarī likewise explains it in connection with forbearance and judgment.

Renderings such as:

“Do not let those who lack certainty draw you out of steadiness”

or, depending on the translation’s style:

“Do not let those who lack certainty make you flighty and unsteady”

are therefore closer to the verse’s construction than “do not let them humiliate you.” The Prophet’s humiliation is not the context’s direct subject; the danger concerns their influence on his steadiness and the direction of his conduct.

“فَاسْتَخَفَّ قَوْمَهُ فَأَطَاعُوهُ (fa-stakhaffa qawmahu fa-aṭāʿūhu)”

In the account of Pharaoh, it is said:

«فَاسْتَخَفَّ قَوْمَهُ فَأَطَاعُوهُ ۚ إِنَّهُمْ كَانُوا قَوْمًا فَاسِقِينَ»

Fa-stakhaffa qawmahu fa-aṭāʿūhu; innahum kānū qawman fāsiqīna.

“So he drew his people into frivolity, and they obeyed him. Indeed, they were a defiantly disobedient people.”

Al-Zukhruf 43:54

This verse is one of the root’s most important uses.

Lexical sources offer several closely related explanations: Pharaoh drew them into frivolity and ignorance; he diverted them from sound judgment; or he found them light and easily led, and was able to bring them along with him. Early commentaries also contain expressions such as استفزهم (istafazzahum), “roused them,” استجهلهم (istajhalahum), “drew them into ignorance,” and حملهم على الخفة والجهل (ḥamalahum ʿalā al-khiffati wa-l-jahli), “drove them toward frivolity and ignorance.”

The continuation of the sentence is crucial:

«فَأَطَاعُوهُ»

Fa-aṭāʿūhu.

“And they obeyed him.”

Their obedience is thus the result of istikhfāf.

If استخف قومه (istakhaffa qawmahu) is translated merely as “he humiliated his people,” the natural relationship between the sentence’s two parts weakens: why did humiliating them lead directly to obedience?

If, however, the root is understood within the field of making light, drawing people out of steadiness and deliberation, or finding them light and setting them in motion, the connection becomes clearer. Through his words, Pharaoh led after himself a people susceptible to being made light and drawn along in this way; “and they obeyed him.”

For Forqan, a rendering along the following lines appears more precise than “he humiliated”:

“So he drew his people into frivolity, and they obeyed him.”

Or, to retain more of the root’s image:

“So he made his people light and unsteady, and they obeyed him.”

The final choice between these renderings is best made when examining the verse itself and the full context of Pharaoh’s speech.

6. Istikhfāf does not equal humiliation

Istikhfāf is often understood as disrespect, belittlement, or humiliation. This branch also existed in Classical Arabic; the meaning is therefore neither invented nor wholly late.

The error arises when this one branch is imposed on every use of the root.

The Qur’an itself provides three clear witnesses in Form X:

تَسْتَخِفُّونَهَا (tastakhiffūnahā) concerns tents and means finding them light.

لَا يَسْتَخِفَّنَّكَ (lā yastakhiffannaka) concerns not losing steadiness before those who lack certainty.

فَاسْتَخَفَّ قَوْمَهُ (fa-stakhaffa qawmahu) concerns Pharaoh’s relationship with a people who obeyed him as a result of this istikhfāf.

Form X of this root therefore has no single fixed translation. What the verb concerns and its context determine whether lightness relates to weight, ease of carrying, finding light, destabilizing, inducing frivolity, or considering of little worth.

7. KH F F compared with TH Q L

The principal root opposed to خ ف ف (KH F F) is ث ق ل (TH Q L). This contrast does not come from dictionaries alone: the Qur’an repeatedly places the roots face to face.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:189, the burden is initially خَفِيف (khafīf), followed by أَثْقَلَتْ (athqalat).

In Al-Tawbah 9:41, خِفَافًا (khifāfan) appears alongside ثِقَالًا (thiqālan).

In the verses of the scales, ثَقُلَتْ مَوَازِينُهُ (thaqulat mawāzīnuhu) stands against خَفَّتْ مَوَازِينُهُ (khaffat mawāzīnuhu).

A clear tendency emerges from this contrast:

KH F F: reduction of weight, burden, or steadiness.

TH Q L: increase of weight, burden, or heaviness.

Even this contrast must not, however, become a fixed moral system. A light tent is desirable; a lightened obligation is mercy; but light scales in the context of judgment are harmful. Conversely, the heaviness of pregnancy can be difficult, whereas heavy scales at the Resurrection are desirable.

Value comes from context, not from the two roots themselves.

8. The role of morphology and syntax

The simple verb خَفَّ (khaffa) describes a state in its Qur’anic use concerning the scales: “the scales became light.”

خَفَّفَ (khaffafa) in Form II is transitive and denotes a reduction of heaviness: something or some burden is made lighter.

تَخْفِيف (takhfīf) names this act of reduction.

خَفِيف (khafīf) describes something light, and خِفَاف (khifāf) is its plural form.

Form X is more complex: its morphological pattern alone does not yield one meaning for every occurrence. The Qur’an shows in practice that this form can express finding something light or drawing a person into a state of lightness.

The preposition عن (ʿan) in constructions such as:

«يُخَفِّفَ عَنْكُمْ»

Yukhaffifa ʿankum.

“To lighten your burden.”

and:

«لَا يُخَفَّفُ عَنْهُمُ الْعَذَابُ»

Lā yukhaffafu ʿanhumu al-ʿadhābu.

“The punishment will not be lightened for them.”

also indicates the direction in which a burden is lifted or reduced. ʿAn itself, however, does not identify that burden as a ruling, obligation, pressure, or punishment; the nouns and context determine this.

9. Challenges in understanding and translation

First, khaffa does not always refer to physical weight, but the physical meaning is a reliable starting point for the root.

Second, not every lightness carries a negative charge. Light tents and lightened obligations are positive examples; light scales have a negative outcome in the context of judgment.

Third, خفافا وثقالا (khifāfan wa-thiqālan) should not be confined without supporting evidence to a particular pair such as young/old or poor/wealthy.

Fourth, takhfīf in the Qur’an is not merely “reduction of punishment.” Any reduction of burden or heaviness can be expressed through this root.

Fifth, istikhfāf is not a fixed equivalent of “humiliation.” In Al-Naḥl 16:80, the Qur’an itself uses this form for finding tents light.

Sixth, in لا يستخفنك (lā yastakhiffannaka), the main contextual danger is losing steadiness and patience, not merely being insulted.

Seventh, in فاستخف قومه فأطاعوه (fa-stakhaffa qawmahu fa-aṭāʿūhu), the relationship between istikhfāf and obedience must be preserved. A translation that removes this connection loses part of the verse’s construction.

Eighth, the behavioral branch of the root must not become a theory that every person who makes a mistake is “light.” This description should enter a translation only where the construction and context support it.

10. Translation suggestions for Forqan

For خَفَّتْ (khaffat) in the verses of the scales: “became light / were light.”

For خَفِيف (khafīf): “light.”

For خِفَافًا وَثِقَالًا (khifāfan wa-thiqālan): preferably “light and heavy,” with an explanation that the expression itself does not specify the precise respect in which these descriptions apply.

For خَفَّفَ / يُخَفِّف (khaffafa / yukhaffif): “lightened,” “reduced the burden,” “reduced,” or “made lighter,” according to what the verb concerns and with tense adjusted to the sentence.

For تَخْفِيف (takhfīf): “lightening,” “alleviation,” or “reduction of burden,” depending on the sentence.

For تَسْتَخِفُّونَهَا (tastakhiffūnahā): “you find them light” or “carrying them is light for you.”

For لَا يَسْتَخِفَّنَّكَ (lā yastakhiffannaka): “do not let them draw you out of steadiness” or “do not let them make you flighty and unsteady.”

For فَاسْتَخَفَّ قَوْمَهُ (fa-stakhaffa qawmahu): “he drew his people into frivolity” appears better suited to the lexical field than the restrictive “he humiliated his people,” although the final wording should be settled when translating the whole verse.

11. Conclusion

The root KH F F rests on lightness as opposed to heaviness. This lightness can be wholly physical: a pregnancy is initially light, or a tent is considered light to move. It can mean reduced burden and pressure, as when The God lightens a ruling or reduces an obligation. It can appear in the scales, where lightness expresses only a low weight and its moral outcome comes from the context of judgment.

A behavioral branch also develops from this field: what leaves composure and steadiness becomes light and easy to move. This branch matters especially in لا يستخفنك (lā yastakhiffannaka) and فاستخف قومه (fa-stakhaffa qawmahu). In the first, those who lack certainty must not draw the addressee out of patience and steadiness; in the second, Pharaoh drew his people into a condition that resulted in their obedience to him.

Istikhfāf therefore cannot be translated throughout the Qur’an by one fixed equivalent such as “humiliation.” The Qur’an itself uses the same form for portable tents. Each occurrence’s meaning must be established from the root, morphology, object, and context of that verse.

The root’s final definition can be expressed concisely as follows:

KH F F: being or becoming light as opposed to heavy; reducing burden and pressure; and, in its behavioral branch, losing or diminishing steadiness and composure so that a person or thing can be set in motion more easily.
