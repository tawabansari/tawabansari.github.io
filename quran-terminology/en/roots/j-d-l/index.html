1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

The triliteral Arabic root J D L concerns intertwining and firmly twisting something, strength, and, in speech, an exchange of words, a confrontation of arguments, and an effort to defend a position or answer another’s.

The Qur’an uses this root both for constructive discussion and defending truth, and for contentiousness, deliberate denial, unsupported disputation, and attempts to portray truth as false.

This distinction is fundamental. Disputation can clarify truth, but can also conceal it or seek victory over another without evidence. Its ethical value therefore cannot be determined from the root alone.

Important forms in this family include:

  • جَادَلَ / يُجَادِلُ (jādala / yujādilu): to dispute, engage another in reasoned discussion, or enter verbal contention. Qur’anic use: yes, twenty-five occurrences in various Form III constructions.
  • جِدَال (jidāl): disputation, an exchange of words, or verbal confrontation. Qur’anic use: yes, twice, in Al-Baqarah 2:197 and Hūd 11:32.
  • جَدَل (jadal): argument and disputation, or the inclination and capacity for such verbal confrontation. Qur’anic use: yes, twice, in Al-Kahf 18:54 and Al-Zukhruf 43:58.
  • جَدَلَ / يَجْدِلُ (jadala / yajdilu): to twist something firmly, especially a rope; in another use, to throw someone to the ground. Qur’anic use: no, in this verbal form.
  • جَدْل (jadl): firm twisting and intertwining. Qur’anic use: no, in this verbal-noun sense.
  • جَدِلَ (jadila): to engage in argument or verbal contention. Qur’anic use: no, in this Form I pattern.
  • مُجَادَلَة (mujādalah): reasoned discussion, contention, or an exchange of words between parties. Qur’anic use: no, as an independent verbal noun, although its verb occurs.
  • مُجَادِل (mujādil): someone who disputes. Qur’anic use: no, as an independent active participle.
  • جَدِيل (jadīl): a firmly twisted rope or rein, especially a leather rein. Qur’anic use: no.
  • جَدِيلَة (jadīlah): a strand or something twisted; in some older uses, a group or lineage branch. Qur’anic use: no.
  • مَجْدُول (majdūl): firmly twisted, compactly formed, or firmly built in body. Qur’anic use: no.
  • جَدَّلَ / تَجْدِيل (jaddala / tajdīl): to throw down or cast to the ground. Qur’anic use: no.
  • تَجَدَّلَ / اِنْجَدَلَ (tajaddala / injadala): to fall or be thrown to the ground. Qur’anic use: no.
  • جَدَالَة (jadālah): the ground or surface upon which someone falls. Qur’anic use: no.
  • مِجْدَل (mijdal): a strong building, fortress, or firmly constructed palace. Qur’anic use: no.
  • أَجْدَل (ajdal): in older usage, a falcon or a powerfully built bird of prey. Qur’anic use: no.

All Qur’anic derivatives belong to discussion and argumentative confrontation. Physical senses such as twisting rope, strengthening a building, or throwing an opponent down matter for linguistic history, but are not the direct meaning of any of the twenty-nine Qur’anic occurrences. These occur in twenty-seven verses across sixteen surahs.

2. The linguistic core: from twisting rope to verbal confrontation

Jadl: firmly intertwining

One familiar use of jadala in older Arabic is firmly twisting rope.

In jadaltu al-ḥabla, strands are twisted together so that the rope becomes strong.

Jadīl also denotes a twisted rein, especially a camel’s leather rein.

Several strands become one connected structure. What matters is more than twisting: it is the firmness resulting from intertwining.

The same field also supplies words for a firm building, closely woven armor, and a compact bodily build.

These uses should nevertheless not all be reduced to one simple physical image. Majdūl al-khalq, for example, need not describe a body twisted like rope; it can denote a firm, well-proportioned build.

The physical image of intertwining is therefore part of the root’s linguistic background, not a compulsory definition for every derivative.

Jidāl: intertwining arguments

In speech, jādalahu means entering discussion or confrontation with another, involving statements, answers, reasons, objections, or defense of a position.

A figurative connection can be seen between rope-twisting and disputation: as strands engage one another, the parties’ statements and arguments answer and interweave.

This image should nevertheless not generate more meaning than it can support.

Twisting rope does not establish that every dispute necessarily involves violence, deception, or an attempt to destroy another person’s dignity.

Nor does every dispute involve equally knowledgeable parties or arguments of equal merit.

One party may defend truth while the other advances falsehood. Alternatively, both may seek understanding while disagreeing over evidence.

The general meaning of jidāl is thus verbal confrontation and exchange; its truthfulness and manner depend on other contextual indications.

The role of prevailing in disputation

Some lexical explanations connect mujādalah with contention and striving to prevail: each party seeks to overcome the other’s position in discussion.

This feature appears in many uses, but should not become the established intention of everyone who disputes.

A woman objecting in defense of her rights, for example, may seek to establish her claim. That does not mean her sole purpose is humiliating or defeating her addressee.

Nor can the Qur’anic command to dispute in the better manner have only psychological victory as its purpose.

Seeking to establish a position is therefore an important feature of disputation, while intention, method, and value require assessment in each context.

Disputation and throwing down

Arabic also uses jaddalahu and related forms for throwing someone to the ground.

Older jadālah likewise denotes the ground or surface onto which someone falls.

This supplies another figurative explanation of disputation: the parties resemble wrestlers, each trying to throw the other down.

The image is understandable, but does not establish a certain historical origin for every use of disputation.

Qur’anic disputation therefore need not be translated as wrestling or physical conflict.

Linguistic conclusion: In its physical field, J D L concerns twisting, firmness, and, in some forms, throwing down. Its Qur’anic core is verbal confrontation and exchange to present, defend, answer, or oppose a position.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Mufradāt: twisting, strength, and disputation

3. Older Arabic evidence: rope, strength, and confrontation

Jadīl in the poetry of Imruʾ al-Qays

A familiar line attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays reads:

وَكَشْحٍ لَطِيفٍ كَالْجَدِيلِ مُخَصَّرٍ
وَسَاقٍ كَأُنْبُوبِ السَّقِيِّ الْمُذَلَّلِ

wa-kashḥin laṭīfin ka-al-jadīli mukhaṣṣarin / wa-sāqin ka-unbūbi al-saqiyyi al-mudhallali

English translation

And a slender, shapely waist, like a finely twisted rein, and a leg like the smooth stem of a well-watered plant.

Al-jadīl here denotes a firmly twisted rein.

To describe a slender and proportioned body, the poet draws on a tangible object in Arab life.

The witness matters because it shows the family’s physical meaning in older poetic language.

It does not make Qur’anic jidāl mean beauty or bodily proportion.

The connection concerns linguistic background, not identical direct meaning.

Lisān al-ʿArab: jadīl in Imruʾ al-Qays’s verse

Mijdal in the poetry of al-Aʿshā

Verse attributed to al-Aʿshā states:

فِي مِجْدَلٍ شُيِّدَ بُنْيَانُهُ
يَزِلُّ عَنْهُ ظُفْرُ الطَّائِرِ

fī mijdalin shuyyida bunyānuhu / yazillu ʿanhu ẓufru al-ṭāʾiri

English translation

In a fortress firmly built, so high and inaccessible that even a bird’s claw slips upon it.

Mijdal here denotes a strong, firmly constructed building.

The use concerns structural firmness, not conversation or argument.

One root can thus supply a physical object’s name as well as descriptions of speech and conduct.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Tāj al-ʿArūs: mijdal in al-Aʿshā’s verse

Jadālah and falling to the ground

A rajaz witness transmitted by the lexicons for jadālah reads:

قَدْ أَرْكَبُ الْآلَةَ بَعْدَ الْآلَةِ
وَأَتْرُكُ الْعَاجِزَ بِالْجَدَالَةِ

qad arkabu al-ālata baʿda al-ālati / wa-atruku al-ʿājiza bi-al-jadālati

English translation

At times I enter one situation after another and leave the incapable upon the ground.

Al-jadālah here means the ground. Al-ālah in this witness means a state or situation, rather than a strategy.

This witness is connected with jaddalahu, meaning to throw someone to the ground.

Tāj al-ʿArūs attributes this rajaz to Abū Qurdūdah al-Aʿrābī; Lisān al-ʿArab under J D L transmits it simply as “the rajaz poet said.” We therefore do not treat its attribution to al-ʿAjjāj as established or present it as a securely dated pre-Qur’anic witness.

Tāj al-ʿArūs, ʾ W L: al-ālah and the rajaz attribution

Its presence in older Arabic nevertheless shows that connecting verbal disputation with physical confrontation was a familiar lexical explanation.

The limits of linguistic witnesses

These witnesses clarify three fields: firm twisting, structural strength, and throwing down.

None independently proves that Qur’anic disputation always means objectionable contentiousness.

The Qur’an provides more explicit evidence for evaluating disputation: the command to dispute better, a woman disputing about her rights, and condemnation of disputation using falsehood.

The article therefore begins with physical imagery but derives ethical conclusions from Qur’anic uses.

4. Wa-jādilhum bi-allatī hiya aḥsan: disputation as a method of invitation

Al-Naḥl 16:125

Verse text

ادْعُ إِلَىٰ سَبِيلِ رَبِّكَ بِالْحِكْمَةِ وَالْمَوْعِظَةِ الْحَسَنَةِ وَجَادِلْهُمْ بِالَّتِي هِيَ أَحْسَنُ إِنَّ رَبَّكَ هُوَ أَعْلَمُ بِمَنْ ضَلَّ عَنْ سَبِيلِهِ وَهُوَ أَعْلَمُ بِالْمُهْتَدِينَ

udʿu ilā sabīli rabbika bi-al-ḥikmati wa-al-mawʿiẓati al-ḥasanati wa-jādilhum bi-allatī hiya aḥsanu inna rabbaka huwa aʿlamu bi-man ḍalla ʿan sabīlihi wa-huwa aʿlamu bi-al-muhtadīna

English translation

Invite to your Lord’s way with wisdom and good counsel, and dispute with them in the manner that is better. Your Lord knows best who has strayed from His way, and He knows best the guided.

Examining the usage

Wa-jādilhum is a Form III imperative.

It accompanies two other methods: inviting with wisdom and good counsel.

Disputation here is therefore not condemned; it is explicitly situated within inviting to the Lord’s way.

The command, however, includes bi-allatī hiya aḥsan, rather than jādilhum alone.

This qualification determines the quality of disputation.

Aḥsan is comparative: better or more fitting.

The verse thus calls for choosing a better manner of verbal engagement, beyond merely avoiding indecency and insults.

Such a manner must fit wisdom and the purpose of invitation.

For someone raising a real question, for example, a clear, reasoned answer without humiliation better fits this principle than labeling or accusing them.

These are practical illustrations; aḥsan itself does not provide a closed list of discussion methods.

Does disputation mean compelling acceptance?

No.

The verse closes by attributing knowledge of the misguided and guided to The God.

This ending distinguishes the task of invitation and disputation from claiming complete control over people’s inner lives.

Disputation can explain truth, present evidence, and criticize an incorrect claim. The verb itself does not mean compelling someone to accept.

Nor does the command make apparent victory over the addressee the necessary goal of every discussion.

A dispute may be conducted correctly while the other party still does not accept.

The soundness of the method therefore differs from the discussion’s immediate outcome.

Wisdom, counsel, and disputation

Ḥikmah, mawʿiẓah, and mujādalah are distinct words.

Wisdom concerns sound judgment; counsel concerns reminder and admonition; disputation concerns an argumentative exchange between parties.

One discussion may involve all three without making them synonymous.

Better disputation should therefore not be restricted to gentle speech without reasons or answers to objections.

When a specific objection has been raised, a clear, supported answer forms part of disputation.

Lexical conclusion: In Al-Naḥl 16:125, disputation is permitted and commanded, subject to the better manner. This verse alone refutes the claim that Qur’anic jidāl is always negative.

5. Discussion with the people of the Scripture: the better method and the boundary of injustice

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:46

Verse text

وَلَا تُجَادِلُوا أَهْلَ الْكِتَابِ إِلَّا بِالَّتِي هِيَ أَحْسَنُ إِلَّا الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا مِنْهُمْ وَقُولُوا آمَنَّا بِالَّذِي أُنْزِلَ إِلَيْنَا وَأُنْزِلَ إِلَيْكُمْ وَإِلَٰهُنَا وَإِلَٰهُكُمْ وَاحِدٌ وَنَحْنُ لَهُ مُسْلِمُونَ

wa-lā tujādilū ahla al-kitābi illā bi-allatī hiya aḥsanu illā alladhīna ẓalamū minhum wa-qūlū āmannā bi-alladhī unzila ilaynā wa-unzila ilaykum wa-ilāhunā wa-ilāhukum wāḥidun wa-naḥnu lahu muslimūna

English translation

Do not dispute with the people of the Scripture except in the manner that is better, except those among them who have acted unjustly. Say, “We believe in what was sent down to us and to you. Our deity and your deity are one, and we submit to Him.”

Examining the usage

Tujādilū is a second-person plural present verb in a prohibitive construction.

The prohibition is not unrestricted: it includes the exception illā bi-allatī hiya aḥsan.

The verse therefore does not prohibit every reasoned discussion with the people of the Scripture. It prescribes the better manner for such engagement.

It then gives an example of what should be said: belief in revealed scripture, the deity’s oneness, and submission to Him.

Better disputation thus concerns true content, fairness, and actual shared ground as well as outward conversational etiquette.

The role of illā alladhīna ẓalamū minhum

The verse contains a second exception:

“Except those among them who have acted unjustly.”

This exception recognizes differences among the addressees’ circumstances.

It should not automatically permit lying, insults, humiliation, or every violent act against anyone considered unjust.

The exception to the better manner requires careful grammatical and contextual examination; by itself it is not a specific command for violence or abandoning justice.

In particular, mere disagreement in belief cannot be identified with this injustice without evidence.

Nor does the exception classify all the people of the Scripture as one unjust group: minhum distinguishes some among them.

Has another verse abrogated this one?

J D L itself says nothing about naskh.

A claim that another ruling has removed the command for better disputation requires independent Qur’anic evidence.

The existence of verses on war and defense alone does not invalidate better discussion in every situation.

War, disputation, and the addressees’ circumstances require their own examination.

Lexical conclusion: Disputation in Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:46 concerns reasoned discussion with the people of the Scripture. The Qur’an establishes the better manner while stating an exception for those acting unjustly. The details of conduct toward that group do not arise from mujādalah itself.

6. A woman disputing about her husband: defending rights and having her voice heard

Al-Mujādilah 58:1

Verse text

قَدْ سَمِعَ اللَّهُ قَوْلَ الَّتِي تُجَادِلُكَ فِي زَوْجِهَا وَتَشْتَكِي إِلَى اللَّهِ وَاللَّهُ يَسْمَعُ تَحَاوُرَكُمَا إِنَّ اللَّهَ سَمِيعٌ بَصِيرٌ

qad samiʿa Allāhu qawla allatī tujādiluka fī zawjihā wa-tashtakī ilā Allāhi wa-Allāhu yasmaʿu taḥāwurakumā inna Allāha samīʿun baṣīrun

English translation

The God has heard the statement of the woman who disputes with you about her husband and complains to The God. The God hears your mutual conversation. The God is indeed Hearing and Seeing.

Examining the usage

Tujādiluka is a Form III present verb.

The woman is its subject; the suffix ka refers to the verse’s addressee, the Prophet.

This use matters because the person disputing is speaking about her family situation.

The verse describes her engagement through mujādalah.

Disputing with the Prophet therefore cannot always be declared sinful or a sign of disbelief merely because it is disputation.

The verse also emphasizes that the woman’s statement is heard.

This has semantic and social significance: a woman speaks about a relationship affecting her life, objects or argues, and presents her complaint to The God.

The following verses examine ẓihār and a man’s claim about his wife.

Tujādiluka itself nevertheless means neither ẓihār nor divorce. The subject comes from fī zawjihā and the surah’s continuation.

Tujādiluka and taḥāwurakumā

Two lexical families occur in the same verse:

Tujādiluka belongs to J D L, and taḥāwurakumā to Ḥ W R.

Mujādalah emphasizes confronting and pursuing a disputed matter through speech.

Taḥāwur here describes an exchange between two parties.

The two are related but distinct.

Disputation can be a kind of dialogue, while not every dialogue is necessarily disputation.

Two people may simply exchange information without disagreement or competing claims.

Here the woman pursues a position through discussion, and The God has heard their entire exchange.

Was the woman merely asking for pity?

The verse mentions her complaint but does not limit it to seeking pity.

Tujādiluka shows her active participation in a discussion about her husband.

Tashtakī ilā Allāh also describes complaining to The God.

These expressions are connected, but neither should be deleted.

The woman both speaks and complains, and the following verses examine her situation.

Lexical conclusion: This verse clearly shows that disputation can defend a right and pursue a real issue in life. Mujādalah itself does not mean impoliteness, disobedience, or sinful contentiousness.

7. Abraham and Noah disputing: the importance of subject and intention

Hūd 11:74

Verse text

فَلَمَّا ذَهَبَ عَنْ إِبْرَاهِيمَ الرَّوْعُ وَجَاءَتْهُ الْبُشْرَىٰ يُجَادِلُنَا فِي قَوْمِ لُوطٍ

fa-lammā dhahaba ʿan Ibrāhīma al-rawʿu wa-jāʾathu al-bushrā yujādilunā fī qawmi Lūṭin

English translation

When fear had left Abraham and the good news had reached him, he was disputing with Us about Lot’s people.

Examining the usage

Yujādilunā is a Form III present verb with the attached pronoun nā.

The verse attributes the disputation to Abraham.

Its subject matter is fī qawmi Lūṭin: about Lot’s people.

The next verse, Hūd 11:75, states:

إِنَّ إِبْرَاهِيمَ لَحَلِيمٌ أَوَّاهٌ مُنِيبٌ

inna ibrāhīma la-ḥalīmun awwāhun munībun

English translation

Abraham was indeed forbearing, deeply compassionate, and turning back toward The God.

This proximity matters greatly.

If disputation were inherently objectionable in every use, attributing it to Abraham alongside these praises would require another explanation.

Recognizing the core field as an exchange about a matter of concern makes the use clearer.

Abraham engages in discussion and pursuit of Lot’s people’s fate.

This fits his compassion and forbearance.

The verse nevertheless does not report the whole conversation. Details from external stories should not therefore be introduced as the Qur’an’s certain text.

Does Abraham’s disputation mean opposing The God?

Not necessarily.

The verse mentions disputation without identifying it as denying The God or knowingly opposing truth.

Hūd 11:76 then describes the end of this pursuit and the certainty of the divine command.

A compassionate request or discussion of people’s fate must therefore be distinguished from insisting on rejecting a settled divine ruling.

The pronoun nā in yujādilunā refers to The God, but Abraham is the subject performing the disputation. This should not become a bodily image of debating with The God; the mode of communication is examined within the story’s context and the conversation with divine messengers.

Al-Ṭabarī on Hūd 11:74: Abraham and conversation with the messengers

Linguistically, the clear matter is verbal engagement over Lot’s people’s fate.

Hūd 11:32: Noah’s people weary of disputation

Verse text

قَالُوا يَا نُوحُ قَدْ جَادَلْتَنَا فَأَكْثَرْتَ جِدَالَنَا فَأْتِنَا بِمَا تَعِدُنَا إِنْ كُنْتَ مِنَ الصَّادِقِينَ

qālū yā Nūḥu qad jādaltanā fa-aktharta jidālanā faʾtinā bi-mā taʿidunā in kunta mina al-ṣādiqīna

English translation

They said, “Noah, you have disputed with us and prolonged your disputation with us. Bring us what you promise us, if you are truthful.”

Examining the usage

J D L occurs twice in this verse.

First as the verb jādaltanā, then as the noun jidālanā.

The speakers describe the frequency and repetition of Noah’s discussion with them.

This is the statement of Noah’s people, not an independent divine judgment that his invitation was objectionable.

Fa-aktharta jidālanā alone therefore does not show that Noah acted incorrectly in his invitation.

The verse does show that discussion may become prolonged and its addressee weary or dissatisfied.

An addressee’s dissatisfaction alone does not establish that discussion is wrong, just as length alone does not establish that it is right.

Conclusion: Abraham’s and Noah’s disputations show that the root can describe prophets’ discussions. The subject, manner, audience’s response, and outcome require separate examination in each case.

8. Defending others: disputation as advocacy and accountability

Al-Nisāʾ 4:107

Verse text

وَلَا تُجَادِلْ عَنِ الَّذِينَ يَخْتَانُونَ أَنْفُسَهُمْ إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يُحِبُّ مَنْ كَانَ خَوَّانًا أَثِيمًا

wa-lā tujādil ʿani alladhīna yakhtānūna anfusahum inna Allāha lā yuḥibbu man kāna khawwānan athīman

English translation

Do not argue in defense of those who betray themselves. The God does not love anyone persistently treacherous and sinful.

Examining the usage

Tujādil ʿan here uses the preposition ʿan.

The construction directs disputation toward defending people.

Disputation therefore need not occur face to face with the person being defended. Someone may argue on behalf of an individual or group against another claim.

This verse, however, prohibits defending those who betray themselves.

That does not absolutely prohibit defending every accused person.

The verse addresses those committing betrayal, whose conduct is presented in context.

Rightly defending an accused person’s rights and examining evidence fairly must be distinguished from knowingly defending betrayal.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:109

Verse text

هَا أَنْتُمْ هَٰؤُلَاءِ جَادَلْتُمْ عَنْهُمْ فِي الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا فَمَنْ يُجَادِلُ اللَّهَ عَنْهُمْ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ أَمْ مَنْ يَكُونُ عَلَيْهِمْ وَكِيلًا

hā antum hāʾulāʾi jādaltum ʿanhum fī al-ḥayāti al-dunyā fa-man yujādilu Allāha ʿanhum yawma al-qiyāmati am man yakūnu ʿalayhim wakīlan

English translation

Here you are, having argued in their defense in worldly life. Who will argue with The God on their behalf on the Day of Resurrection, or who will be their guardian and trustee?

Examining the usage

Jādaltum and yujādilu occur here in a construction concerning defending others.

The verse’s question highlights the responsibility of those who argued in support of a group in worldly life.

Verbal defense can depart from truth.

Someone may defend another through family ties, group allegiance, or personal interest despite knowing that person acted wrongly.

Mujādalah here does not itself mean betrayal; the defense’s direction and subject account for its condemnation.

Conversely, evidence-based defense of someone wronged or removal of a false accusation is not condemned merely because it involves disputation.

Al-Naḥl 16:111: each self pleading for itself

Verse text

يَوْمَ تَأْتِي كُلُّ نَفْسٍ تُجَادِلُ عَنْ نَفْسِهَا وَتُوَفَّىٰ كُلُّ نَفْسٍ مَا عَمِلَتْ وَهُمْ لَا يُظْلَمُونَ

yawma taʾtī kullu nafsin tujādilu ʿan nafsihā wa-tuwaffā kullu nafsin mā ʿamilat wa-hum lā yuẓlamūna

English translation

The day when every self comes pleading for itself, and every self is given in full what it has done, and they are not wronged.

Examining the usage

Tujādilu ʿan nafsihā here denotes a person’s defense of themself.

It is close to disputing in defense of others, but the subject of defense is the person themself.

Jidāl can therefore occur in seeking justice, defense, and accountability.

The verb nevertheless does not mean the person is certainly acquitted.

The verse continues by speaking of full recompense for deeds and the absence of injustice.

Making a defense is therefore distinct from having it accepted.

Lexical conclusion: Jādala ʿan emphasizes arguing in defense of someone or something. Its value depends on the matter’s truth and the manner of defense, rather than verbal advocacy itself.

9. Unsupported disputation: denying truth and arguing with falsehood

Al-Ḥajj 22:8

Verse text

وَمِنَ النَّاسِ مَنْ يُجَادِلُ فِي اللَّهِ بِغَيْرِ عِلْمٍ وَلَا هُدًى وَلَا كِتَابٍ مُنِيرٍ

wa-mina al-nāsi man yujādilu fī Allāhi bi-ghayri ʿilmin wa-lā hudan wa-lā kitābin munīrin

English translation

Among people is one who disputes about The God without knowledge, guidance, or an illuminating scripture.

Examining the usage

Yujādilu alone is not what this verse condemns.

Three qualifications accompany it:

“Without knowledge,” “nor guidance,” and “nor an illuminating scripture.”

These qualifications locate the problem in disputation lacking knowledge and guidance as its foundation.

Al-Ḥajj 22:3 likewise mentions disputing about The God without knowledge, alongside following every rebellious satan.

Luqmān 31:20 repeats the same threefold construction: disputation without knowledge, guidance, or an illuminating scripture.

These verses should therefore not make every question about The God or every difference of interpretation inherently objectionable disputation.

Someone asking to understand truth differs from someone arguing without evidence to deny it.

Equally, claiming knowledge does not establish actually possessing evidence.

Assessment must rest on the evidence presented.

Ghāfir 40:5: disputing with falsehood to displace truth

Verse text

كَذَّبَتْ قَبْلَهُمْ قَوْمُ نُوحٍ وَالْأَحْزَابُ مِنْ بَعْدِهِمْ وَهَمَّتْ كُلُّ أُمَّةٍ بِرَسُولِهِمْ لِيَأْخُذُوهُ وَجَادَلُوا بِالْبَاطِلِ لِيُدْحِضُوا بِهِ الْحَقَّ فَأَخَذْتُهُمْ فَكَيْفَ كَانَ عِقَابِ

kadhdhabat qablahum qawmu Nūḥin wa-al-aḥzābu min baʿdihim wa-hammat kullu ummatin bi-rasūlihim li-yaʾkhudhūhu wa-jādalū bi-al-bāṭili li-yudḥiḍū bihi al-ḥaqqa fa-akhadhtuhum fa-kayfa kāna ʿiqābi

English translation

Before them, Noah’s people and the factions after them denied. Every community intended to seize its messenger, and they argued with falsehood to displace truth through it. So I seized them. How, then, was My punishment?

Examining the usage

This is among the clearest examples of condemned disputation.

Two features are explicit:

First, the disputation is bi-al-bāṭil, with falsehood.

Second, its purpose is li-yudḥiḍū bihi al-ḥaqqa, to displace truth through it.

The issue therefore goes beyond disagreement: it is using falsehood to set truth aside.

D Ḥ Ḍ is an independent root concerning making something slip, ineffective, or appear invalid.

That verb specifies the disputation’s purpose.

Jādalū alone therefore should not be translated as “they invalidated” or “they destroyed the truth.”

Disputation is the verbal act; the false means and the purpose of displacing truth come from accompanying expressions.

Ghāfir 40:35 and Ghāfir 40:56: disputation without authority and with arrogance

In Ghāfir 40:35, disputing over the signs of The God is qualified by “without an authority that has come to them.”

Ghāfir 40:56 repeats the qualification, then mentions arrogance within the disputants’ breasts.

In these cases, the Qur’an distinguishes disputation itself from lacking valid support.

It also identifies arrogance as a feature of the specified group, rather than making everyone who disputes arrogant in every circumstance.

The woman defending her rights in Al-Mujādilah 58:1, for example, is described with the same root.

Other condemned uses

In Al-Anʿām 6:25, people dispute with the Prophet and call revelation tales of the ancients.

Al-Anʿām 6:121 describes suggestions conveyed for disputing with believers about food and divine ruling.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:71, Hūd describes his people disputing over names they and their fathers have named without any authority having been sent down for them.

Al-Anfāl 8:6 has fī al-ḥaqqi baʿdamā tabayyana: disputing over truth after it has become clear.

Al-Raʿd 13:13 mentions disputation about The God amid signs of divine power.

Ghāfir 40:4, Ghāfir 40:69, and Al-Shūrā 42:35 also describe disputation over divine signs in settings of denial and opposition.

These uses differ, but many share verbal opposition to a truth whose evidence has been presented.

Judging a person nevertheless requires knowing their state of understanding. Disputing after clearly recognizing truth differs from a question or objection by someone who has not yet understood the evidence. In Al-Ḥajj 22:68, the response to continued disputation also refers the matter to divine knowledge of the addressees’ deeds.

10. Jadal and jidāl: verbal contention and the human capacity for it

Al-Kahf 18:54

Verse text

وَلَقَدْ صَرَّفْنَا فِي هَٰذَا الْقُرْآنِ لِلنَّاسِ مِنْ كُلِّ مَثَلٍ وَكَانَ الْإِنْسَانُ أَكْثَرَ شَيْءٍ جَدَلًا

wa-la-qad ṣarrafnā fī hādhā al-qurʾāni li-al-nāsi min kulli mathalin wa-kāna al-insānu akthara shayʾin jadalan

English translation

We have indeed explained in various ways in this Qur’an for people every kind of example; and the human being is more disputatious than anything.

Examining the usage

Jadalan here is a noun.

The verse concerns the human inclination toward, or abundance of, disputation.

It does not establish that human beings are inherently enemies of truth in every condition.

Elsewhere the Qur’an also calls attention to thinking, questioning, reflection, and speaking with evidence.

In the same surah, Al-Kahf 18:56 describes disbelievers disputing with falsehood to displace truth.

In the nearby context, disputation thus concerns resistance to truth.

The human capacity for argument and objection can nevertheless also be directed rightly.

Al-Naḥl 16:125 and Al-Mujādilah 58:1 make that distinction clear.

Al-Zukhruf 43:58

Verse text

وَقَالُوا أَآلِهَتُنَا خَيْرٌ أَمْ هُوَ مَا ضَرَبُوهُ لَكَ إِلَّا جَدَلًا بَلْ هُمْ قَوْمٌ خَصِمُونَ

wa-qālū a-ālihatunā khayrun am huwa mā ḍarabūhu laka illā jadalan bal hum qawmun khaṣimūna

English translation

They said, “Are our deities better, or is he?” They presented this to you only for argument. Rather, they are a contentious people.

Examining the usage

Jadalan here occurs alongside khaṣimūn.

This shows that disputation can occur in a setting of contentiousness and striving to prevail in debate.

The verb ḍaraba and the description khaṣimūn also help determine the sentence’s meaning.

Not every illustration, comparison, or critical question therefore becomes condemned disputation.

What is criticized is a manner of presenting argument that has departed from seeking truth in this context.

Jidāl in Hūd and pilgrimage

Jidāl in Hūd 11:32 describes prolonged verbal exchange between Noah and his people.

The same noun occurs in the pilgrimage setting of Al-Baqarah 2:197.

One nominal form can thus occur in different contexts.

In Hūd, Noah’s people complain about frequent disputation; in Al-Baqarah, the Qur’an prohibits jidāl during pilgrimage.

In neither does the noun itself mean pilgrimage, denial, or a long duration.

Those features arise from the sentence and context.

11. Fa-lā jidāla fī al-ḥajj: prohibiting disputation in pilgrimage

Al-Baqarah 2:197

Verse text

الْحَجُّ أَشْهُرٌ مَعْلُومَاتٌ فَمَنْ فَرَضَ فِيهِنَّ الْحَجَّ فَلَا رَفَثَ وَلَا فُسُوقَ وَلَا جِدَالَ فِي الْحَجِّ وَمَا تَفْعَلُوا مِنْ خَيْرٍ يَعْلَمْهُ اللَّهُ وَتَزَوَّدُوا فَإِنَّ خَيْرَ الزَّادِ التَّقْوَىٰ وَاتَّقُونِ يَا أُولِي الْأَلْبَابِ

al-ḥajju ashhurun maʿlūmātun fa-man faraḍa fīhinna al-ḥajja fa-lā rafatha wa-lā fusūqa wa-lā jidāla fī al-ḥajji wa-mā tafʿalū min khayrin yaʿlamhu Allāhu wa-tazawwadū fa-inna khayra al-zādi al-taqwā wa-ittaqūni yā ulī al-albābi

English translation

Pilgrimage is in known months. Whoever undertakes pilgrimage in them, let there be no rafath, no fusūq, and no jidāl during pilgrimage. Whatever good you do, The God knows it. Take provisions, for the best provision is mindfulness. Be mindful of Me, people of understanding.

Examining the usage

Jidāla here follows lā.

Fa-lā rafatha wa-lā fusūqa wa-lā jidāla places three designations together.

Outwardly, the grammar negates these behaviors; in the ruling’s context, it functions as prohibition and restraint.

Jidāl is one of the behaviors to be set aside during pilgrimage.

Its relationship to the command for better disputation in Al-Naḥl 16:125 nevertheless requires explanation.

The verses address different situations and use different formulations.

Al-Naḥl concerns reasoned discussion inviting to the divine way and prescribes the better manner.

Al-Baqarah presents pilgrimage as a particular responsibility, prohibiting rafath, fusūq, and jidāl within it.

Neither therefore invalidates the other.

Is every conversation prohibited in pilgrimage?

No; jidāl itself does not establish this meaning.

Ordinary conversation, asking directions, learning a ruling, addressing a practical problem, and clarifying a disagreement are not necessarily disputation in the sense of contention and quarrelsomeness.

Arabic jidāl ordinarily involves verbal confrontation and insistence on a position.

The prohibition therefore should not become a ban on speaking, scholarly questions, or explaining ritual duties.

Conversely, every verbal conflict cannot be excluded from the prohibition merely by calling it scholarly discussion.

Debating during pilgrimage to defeat or humiliate another, or prolong fruitless hostility, is clearly connected with the prohibition of jidāl.

Jidāl, fusūq, and rafath

Rafath concerns sexual speech and conduct.

Fusūq concerns departing from boundaries and disobedience.

Jidāl concerns verbal contention and argumentative confrontation.

The three are distinct.

Translating them all simply as “misconduct” erases substantive lexical differences.

Their appearance together nevertheless shows that pilgrimage involves conduct, language, and relations with others as well as outward ritual actions.

Does every disagreement invalidate pilgrimage?

Fa-lā jidāla fī al-ḥajj alone does not establish every legal or ritual consequence of a violation.

The verse states the prohibition without detailing numbers of expiations or separate invalidation conditions for every kind of disputation.

Prohibition must therefore be distinguished from specifying punishment or compensation for violating it.

The absence of expiation details should equally not make the prohibition unimportant.

Disputation and mindfulness

The verse ends by emphasizing taqwā.

This connects governing one’s conduct in pilgrimage with awareness and responsibility before The God.

Mindfulness nevertheless does not mean silencing reason or abandoning the defense of truth.

A person can avoid fruitless or contentious debate in one setting while fairly examining a real question with evidence under appropriate circumstances.

Lexical conclusion: Jidāl in Al-Baqarah 2:197 denotes disputation and verbal contention. Its pilgrimage prohibition should neither cover every conversation and scholarly question nor become ineffective through calling contentiousness scholarly discussion.

12. Nearby roots and grammatical points

J D L compared with Ḥ J J (ḥujjah)

Study of Ḥ J J

Some uses of Ḥ J J concern presenting reasons, argumentation, and argumentative confrontation.

J D L likewise concerns disputation and verbal exchange.

Ḥujjah, however, is evidence or an argument used to establish a position; jidāl is the process of verbal engagement.

Someone may possess a strong argument and use it in disputation.

Someone may also dispute without valid evidence, as the Qur’anic expression bi-ghayri sulṭānin indicates.

Disputation therefore does not establish possession of evidence.

J D L compared with Ḥ W R (ḥiwār)

Study of Ḥ W R

Ḥiwār concerns an exchange of speech between parties.

Jidāl also involves exchange, but disagreement, objection, or defending a view is more prominent.

Al-Mujādilah 58:1 places tujādiluka alongside taḥāwurakumā.

One discussion can therefore be both ḥiwār and mujādalah without making their core meanings identical.

J D L compared with M R Y (mirāʾ)

Study of M R Y

Mirāʾ belongs to verbal disputation and contention, and can denote discussion involving doubt or contentiousness.

Al-Kahf 18:22 uses mirāʾan ẓāhiran for engagement over the cave companions.

M R Y nevertheless remains independent of J D L.

Their overlap does not make every disputation objectionable mirāʾ.

Al-Naḥl 16:125 and Al-Mujādilah 58:1 prevent such a generalization.

J D L compared with KH Ṣ M (khiṣām)

Study of KH Ṣ M

Khuṣūmah concerns opposition, litigation, or confrontation between parties.

Al-Zukhruf 43:58 places jadalan alongside khaṣimūn.

Disputation can thus accompany hostility.

Not every disputation, however, involves personal enmity. Two people can seriously disagree over a scholarly question without being enemies.

J D L compared with N Z ʿ (nizāʿ)

Study of N Z ʿ

Nizāʿ denotes contention or disagreement that may be verbal or practical.

Qur’anic jidāl belongs to verbal confrontation.

Contention may begin with disputation, or disputation may occur during contention, without making the concepts identical.

J D L compared with B R H N (burhān)

Study of B R H N

Qur’anic burhān denotes clear evidence and support for proof.

Jidāl is the process of discussion and verbal engagement.

A person may present burhān in disputation, but disputation itself is not proof.

The Qur’an accordingly also describes disputation without knowledge or authority.

The role of prepositions

Prepositions accompanying the verb matter in Qur’anic uses.

Jādala fī foregrounds the subject of discussion, such as disputing about The God or His signs.

Jādala ʿan more often concerns defending a person or position, as in Al-Nisāʾ 4:107 and Al-Naḥl 16:111.

Jādala bi- can identify method or means, such as disputing with falsehood or in the better manner.

These are clear structural tendencies, not absolute rules deriving every meaning from one preposition.

Form III

All twenty-five Qur’anic verbal occurrences are Form III.

In this context, the pattern fits a verbal relationship between parties.

The pattern itself does not show equal amounts of speech, equal correctness, or equal blame.

In Al-Mujādilah 58:1, the woman is the verb’s subject, while taḥāwurakumā describes her entire conversation with the Prophet.

In Al-Naḥl 16:125, the Prophet receives the imperative without the pattern itself revealing the outcome or the addressees’ conduct.

13. Translation proposals for Forqan

Jādala can be translated as “dispute,” “discuss and argue,” or, in an appropriate context, “engage in debate and verbal contention.”

It should not invariably become “quarrel,” whose excessive negative connotation misrepresents better disputation and the woman defending her rights.

Al-Naḥl 16:125 can use “and dispute with them in the manner that is better.”

In Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:46, “do not dispute with the people of the Scripture except in the better manner” preserves prohibition and exception.

In Al-Mujādilah 58:1, “the woman who disputes with you about her husband” closely follows the wording. “Discusses her husband with you” conveys the context but less clearly distinguishes disputation from ordinary conversation.

In Hūd 11:74, “he was disputing with Us about Lot’s people” is appropriate; the following verse indicates his compassionate motivation.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:107, “do not argue in defense of those who betray themselves” clarifies ʿan.

In Al-Naḥl 16:111, “each person pleads for themself” is natural. A reflection can explain that the Arabic verb belongs to J D L.

In Ghāfir 40:5, “they disputed with falsehood to displace truth through it” distinguishes the verb from its accompanying purpose.

In Al-Baqarah 2:197, “during pilgrimage let there be no rafath, no fusūq, and no jidāl” closely follows the Arabic structure.

Jadalan in Al-Kahf 18:54 may be rendered “disputation” or “being disputatious.”

“Only for argument” in Al-Zukhruf 43:58 fits the context.

Jidālanā in Hūd 11:32 can be translated as “disputation with us.”

In each case, translation should retain speech and argumentative engagement, deriving ethical value from the verse’s own qualifications.

The central translation boundary is that jidāl itself is not identical to lying, violence, arrogance, seeking truth, or defending the oppressed. These features can accompany different uses, but are not fixed components of the word.

14. Final synthesis

Arabic J D L includes the physical fields of firm twisting, strength, and, in some uses, throwing down. All its Qur’anic occurrences, however, concern speech and argumentative confrontation.

Here mujādalah denotes exchange, raising objections, defending a position, and attempting to answer or establish a claim.

The Qur’an nevertheless demonstrates different forms of disputation.

Al-Naḥl 16:125 commands the better manner; Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:46 states the same principle for engagement with the people of the Scripture.

In Al-Mujādilah 58:1, a woman disputes with the Prophet about her family situation, and The God hears their words and conversation.

In Hūd 11:74, Abraham disputes about Lot’s people; the following verse describes him as forbearing and compassionate.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:107, Al-Nisāʾ 4:108, and Al-Nisāʾ 4:109 address disputation in defense of people, condemning defense of betrayal. In Al-Naḥl 16:111, every self pleads for itself on the day of accounting.

The Qur’an also describes disputation without knowledge, guidance, or authority, and arguing with falsehood to displace truth.

These uses show that the essential criteria are the subject, evidence, manner, and direction of disputation, beyond merely engaging in it.

Fa-lā jidāla fī al-ḥajj is a real prohibition of disputation in pilgrimage, without making every ordinary conversation, necessary question, or scholarly inquiry prohibited.

During pilgrimage, people are restrained from contention and quarrelsomeness. In invitation, the better manner of disputation is commanded. The statements address different circumstances and need not conflict.

Jadal in Al-Kahf 18:54 also shows the broad human capacity for argument, without the word itself making that capacity a certain virtue or inherent sin.

Final definition: Qur’anic J D L denotes verbal confrontation and exchange between parties to present, defend, answer, or oppose a position. It may involve evidence and fairness, or falsehood, ignorance, and contentiousness.

Understanding each use requires three questions: what is being disputed, what evidence and method are used, and whether the discussion clarifies truth or conceals and displaces it.

Without these distinctions, the woman’s defense of her rights could be equated with misleading disputation, or the command for better engagement could seem to contradict the pilgrimage prohibition.

Attending to morphology, prepositions, the verb’s object, and each verse’s context clarifies the root: disputation is a mode of verbal engagement; truth, evidence, and human conduct determine its value and consequences.
