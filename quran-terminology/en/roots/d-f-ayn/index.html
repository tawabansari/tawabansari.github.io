Repelling and restraining: defending against harm, checking corruption, and handing rights to their owners

1. Introducing the root and its semantic field

Arabic triliteral D F ʿ concerns driving, moving aside, and removing something from its position or course. Dafaʿa al-shayʾa describes forcefully moving something aside, propelling it forward, or preventing its arrival at a destination.

This field includes averting harm, defending a person, opposing groups checking one another, swiftly moving water, and delivering something to someone.

These uses do not share a single direction of movement.

Averting harm from someone means preventing that harm from reaching them or continuing.

Delivering property to its owner does not mean pushing the owner away: it means transferring the property and placing it at their disposal.

When two groups repel one another, the subject may be resistance and checking the opposing force.

The root’s meaning therefore depends on agent, object, preposition, and context.

The root occurs ten times in the Qur’an. Its uses can be examined in four principal settings: defense and averting harm; handing property to its owner; responding to evil through better conduct; and restraining aggression and corruption through people checking one another.

Two further verses deny any dāfiʿ against divine punishment.

These uses show that dafʿ is not restricted to warfare or monetary payment and does not inherently determine moral goodness or badness. Repelling may prevent oppression, or appear in conduct that unjustly drives someone away.

Its moral value depends on its cause, object, manner, and result.

2. Qur’anic forms and morphology

D F ʿ occurs ten times in nine verses through four morphological formations.

Form I verb: five occurrences:

  • Idfaʿū in Āl ʿImrān 3:167
  • Fa-idfaʿū in Al-Nisāʾ 4:6
  • Dafaʿtum in Al-Nisāʾ 4:6
  • Idfaʿ in Al-Muʾminūn 23:96
  • Idfaʿ in Fuṣṣilat 41:34

Form III verb: one occurrence:

  • Yudāfiʿu in Al-Ḥajj 22:38

Verbal noun: two occurrences:

  • Dafʿu in Al-Baqarah 2:251
  • Dafʿu in Al-Ḥajj 22:40

Active participle: two occurrences:

  • Dāfiʿin in Al-Ṭūr 52:8
  • Dāfiʿun in Al-Maʿārij 70:2

Al-Nisāʾ 4:6 contains two forms, making ten occurrences in nine verses.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: ten occurrences in nine verses

Arabic also has other forms of this family:

Dafaʿa–yadfaʿu: drove, moved aside, restrained, or handed something to another.

Dafʿ: the verbal noun: moving aside, driving, restraining, or delivering, depending on construction.

Difāʿ: a verbal noun used for repelling and defense; in some constructions associated with dāfaʿa.

Dāfaʿa: defended, averted harm, or opposed another party.

Mudāfaʿah: defense, resistance, or repelling; in some uses, putting off doing something.

Tadāfaʿa: repelled one another, or something moved successively.

Indafaʿa: began moving or advanced swiftly.

Tadaffaʿa: moved forcefully or successively.

Istadfaʿa: sought the averting of harm or something else.

Dāfiʿ: one who repels, restrains, or defends another.

Madfūʿ: something driven, moved aside, or delivered to someone.

Dafʿah: one act of pushing or pouring; in another use, an amount flowing at once.

Duffāʿ: in some early uses, a great torrent or powerful wave.

These forms help explain the family’s semantic extensions, but not all occur in the Qur’an.

3. The semantic core in early dictionaries

Moving something aside

Ibn Fāris writes in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

الدَّالُ وَالْفَاءُ وَالْعَيْنُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ مَشْهُورٌ، يَدُلُّ عَلَى تَنْحِيَةِ الشَّيْءِ

al-dālu wa-al-fāʾu wa-al-ʿaynu aṣlun wāḥidun mashhūrun yadullu ʿalā tanḥiyati al-shayʾi

“Dāl, fāʾ, and ʿayn have one familiar principle, indicating moving something aside.”

He continues:

دَفَعْتُ الشَّيْءَ أَدْفَعُهُ دَفْعًا

dafaʿtu al-shayʾa adfaʿuhu dafʿan

“I performed dafʿ upon the thing: I moved it aside.”

Lisān al-ʿArab also states:

الدَّفْعُ: الْإِزَالَةُ بِقُوَّةٍ

al-dafʿu al-izālatu bi-quwwatin

“Dafʿ is removal with force.”

The definitions are close, but emphasize different aspects.

Ibn Fāris foregrounds moving aside and removing.

Ibn Manẓūr includes force in the definition as well as removal.

Quwwah does not establish that every dafʿ necessarily involves a blow, war, or bodily violence.

A person may avert danger through conversation, planning, barriers, or other appropriate action.

Force in such uses may involve actual influence on an event’s course, rather than necessarily a physical blow.

Repelling something also does not always mean destroying it.

Moving a stone off a path does not destroy the stone: only its position changes.

This distinction matters in Qur’anic uses. Harm may be averted by preventing, removing, or stopping it without complete destruction of its source being inherent in the root.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: moving aside and removing

Dafaʿa ʿan: averting harm

Arabic dafaʿa ʿanhu al-sharra means averting evil from someone.

Lisān al-ʿArab also records dāfaʿa ʿanhu in this semantic field.

The preposition ʿan matters in this construction.

Harm or threat is removed from a person, group, or place.

Defense here therefore concerns support and protection.

The verb itself nevertheless does not specify how that protection occurs.

Someone may defend another bodily, or avert harm through truthful testimony, preventing an unjust decision, or suitable assistance.

The kind of defense comes from the situation and other sentence components.

Dafaʿa ilā: handing over and transferring

Dafaʿa ilayhi al-shayʾa also means delivering or handing something to another.

Ilā identifies the transfer’s direction.

When property is delivered to its owner, dafʿ concerns handing it over and taking it out of the custodian’s control.

Its direction differs from dafaʿa ʿanhu.

With dafaʿa ʿanhu, something is removed from a person.

With dafaʿa ilayhi, something is transferred toward a person.

These should therefore not receive one identical Persian equivalent.

Dafʿ in Al-Nisāʾ 4:6 means handing property to its owners, rather than defending them or driving them away.

Dāfaʿa and its relation to dafaʿa

Form III can express participation or opposition between two parties in some Arabic uses.

For comparison, tadāfaʿa al-qawmu, from Form VI, describes people pushing one another. This form should be distinguished from Form III itself.

Not every Form III verb must therefore express reciprocal action between two agents of equal standing.

Dāfaʿa ʿanhu may simply mean defending and averting harm.

Al-Ḥajj 22:38 attributes yudāfiʿu to God.

The form does not establish that God confronts an equal power in a reciprocal struggle.

The verb must be understood through agent and context.

4. Tangible uses and early Arabic witnesses

Moving water and torrents

One tangible use of D F ʿ concerns flowing water.

Dictionaries use duffāʿ for a great torrent or powerful wave.

Ibn Fāris explains this as one part of the water propelling another.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn also records duffāʿ for the force of a torrent or wave.

These sources transmit the verse:

جَوَادٌ يَفِيضُ عَلَى الْمُعْتَفِينَ
كَمَا فَاضَ يَمٌّ بِدُفَّاعِهِ

jawādun yafīḍu ʿalā al-muʿtafīna / kamā fāḍa yammun bi-duffāʿihi

“A generous one whose bounty pours upon those seeking help, as a sea overflows with its powerful waves and current.”

Human generosity is compared with abundant flowing water.

Duffāʿ here concerns water’s force and motion, rather than military defense or handing property to someone.

The examined transmissions do not clearly identify the poet. It should therefore not be presented as certainly pre-Islamic poetry without independent evidence.

Its value lies in documenting duffāʿ for forceful water movement in Arabic lexicographical tradition.

Dafʿah: one outpouring

Arabic dafʿah may concern one occurrence or an amount of water flowing all at once.

This is also recorded for rain, blood, and other liquids.

It need not involve repelling an enemy or averting harm: a single movement or pouring is at issue.

Some lexical explanations distinguish dafʿah with fatḥah, one occurrence of the action, from dufʿah with ḍammah, an amount poured out.

Movement and transfer thus form a broad branch of the root’s uses.

A single outpouring should nevertheless not be imported into dafʿu Allāhi in Al-Baqarah 2:251, where restraining corruption through people checking one another is at issue.

Mudaffaʿ: a person turned away

Dictionaries use mudaffaʿ for a person whom others repel or whose requests they disregard.

It is particularly recorded for someone poor and unsupported.

When a needy person approaches others and is turned away successively, this description may apply.

Sources also record the word for the poor and abased. Its lexical explanation is that others repel them, rather than every form of this root independently meaning poverty.

The distinction matters. Someone may be poor yet respected and supported; another may be excluded through social standing or a different dispute.

Mudaffaʿ in this use therefore also pictures other people’s excluding behavior.

A mudaffaʿ camel

Maqāyīs al-Lughah records another use that initially seems different from the poor person’s case.

A valuable, powerful camel kept from carrying loads for its preservation was also called mudaffaʿ.

Whenever brought for loading, it would be set aside and another camel used instead.

In poetry Ibn Fāris attributes to Ḥumayd:

وَقَرَّبْنَ لِلتَّرْحَالِ كُلَّ مُدَفَّعٍ

wa-qarrabna li-al-tarḥāli kulla mudaffaʿin

“And they brought near for departure camels that were spared load-carrying.”

Setting an animal aside here does not mean it is worthless: it may protect a valuable animal.

Moving something aside therefore has no fixed moral or social value by itself.

A person may be turned away with contempt, while a camel may be spared heavy work because of its value.

Moving aside is present in both, but the reason differs.

5. Fa-idfaʿū ilayhim amwālahum: handing over orphans’ property

Al-Nisāʾ 4:6

وَابْتَلُوا الْيَتَامَىٰ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا بَلَغُوا النِّكَاحَ فَإِنْ آنَسْتُمْ مِنْهُمْ رُشْدًا فَادْفَعُوا إِلَيْهِمْ أَمْوَالَهُمْ

wa-ibtalū al-yatāmā ḥattā idhā balaghū al-nikāḥa fa-in ānastum minhum rushdan fa-idfaʿū ilayhim amwālahum

“And test the orphans until they reach the stage of marriage; if you perceive sound judgment in them, hand over their property to them.”

This is the first Qur’anic passage where dafʿ means delivering something to another.

The same verse says a second time:

فَإِذَا دَفَعْتُمْ إِلَيْهِمْ أَمْوَالَهُمْ فَأَشْهِدُوا عَلَيْهِمْ

fa-idhā dafaʿtum ilayhim amwālahum fa-ashhidū ʿalayhim

“Then when you hand their property over to them, have witnesses over them.”

Ilayhim identifies the transfer’s direction in both expressions.

The object is amwālahum: property belonging to the orphans themselves.

Transferring property rather than gifting it

This distinction matters.

The verse does not instruct guardians to give their own property to the orphans.

It speaks of amwālahum, their own property.

Dafʿ therefore concerns handing over assets already belonging to the recipient.

The guardian or custodian returns control over the property to its original owner.

“Handing over” and “delivering” are therefore preferable to “giving as a gift.”

Persian “paying” may suggest only money or a financial debt.

Amwāl can include various assets.

“Hand their property over to them” is consequently more precise.

Sound judgment and responsibility

The verse connects handing over property with testing the orphans and observing sound judgment in them.

Dafʿ itself does not mean testing, developing sound judgment, or reaching the stage of marriage.

Those conditions appear in independent words and expressions.

Lexical research should preserve each part’s contribution.

Fa-idfaʿū commands delivery.

Ilayhim identifies the recipients.

Amwālahum identifies what is delivered.

The preceding expressions clarify the conditions of that transfer.

Witnesses after delivery

The verse subsequently commands taking witnesses.

This concerns recording and establishing delivery of property.

Dafʿ itself does not mean taking witnesses.

Witnessing is an independent action that may reduce later disagreement or denial concerning delivery of assets.

Dafʿ thus expresses transfer and handing over; conditions and means of establishing it come from the rest of the verse.

6. Aw idfaʿū: defending against a threat

Āl ʿImrān 3:167

وَقِيلَ لَهُمْ تَعَالَوْا قَاتِلُوا فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ أَوِ ادْفَعُوا

wa-qīla lahum taʿālaw qātilū fī sabīli allāhi awi idfaʿū

“And they were told: Come, fight in God’s way, or defend.”

The verse concerns military confrontation and a group refusing to accompany others in that situation.

Two independent verbs appear:

“Qātilū.”

And:

“Idfaʿū.”

The first is Form III from Q T L, referring to fighting in this context.

The second belongs to D F ʿ and concerns defense and checking a threat.

Is dafʿ identical with fighting?

The verbs occur alongside one another, separated by aw.

Their distinction should therefore not be erased.

Qātilū refers to participating in fighting.

Idfaʿū may concern defense and preventing the enemy’s advance or harm.

Commentaries offer different accounts of practical defense: preventing attack, assisting defense, or strengthening the defenders’ numbers.

The root itself does not specify a particular military action.

It cannot alone be equated with attacking, killing an enemy, or close combat.

Yet the military setting should not be turned into ordinary conversation unrelated to a military threat.

Is every defense legitimate?

Dafʿ’s lexical meaning does not itself judge an action’s legitimacy.

A group may defend itself against aggression.

Another may violate others’ rights while claiming defense.

The action’s causes and circumstances require examination.

Āl ʿImrān 3:167 concerns a particular confrontation.

General principles of defense also require examining Al-Ḥajj 22:39, Al-Ḥajj 22:40, and Al-Baqarah 2:190.

Idfaʿū itself therefore provides no unrestricted authorization for violence or warfare.

7. Idfaʿ bi-allatī hiya aḥsanu: countering evil in a better way

Two verses use this root in similar commands.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:96

ادْفَعْ بِالَّتِي هِيَ أَحْسَنُ السَّيِّئَةَ نَحْنُ أَعْلَمُ بِمَا يَصِفُونَ

idfaʿ bi-allatī hiya aḥsanu al-sayyiʾata naḥnu aʿlamu bi-mā yaṣifūna

“Repel evil with what is better; We know best what they describe.”

Idfaʿ is an imperative here.

Al-sayyiʾata is its object, while bi-allatī hiya aḥsanu identifies the method or means of countering.

The verse therefore speaks of countering evil and specifies how.

It does not mean absolutely ignoring evil or accepting it, but repelling it through a better way.

Fuṣṣilat 41:34

وَلَا تَسْتَوِي الْحَسَنَةُ وَلَا السَّيِّئَةُ ادْفَعْ بِالَّتِي هِيَ أَحْسَنُ فَإِذَا الَّذِي بَيْنَكَ وَبَيْنَهُ عَدَاوَةٌ كَأَنَّهُ وَلِيٌّ حَمِيمٌ

wa-lā tastawī al-ḥasanatu wa-lā al-sayyiʾatu idfaʿ bi-allatī hiya aḥsanu fa-idhā alladhī baynaka wa-baynahu ʿadāwatun ka-annahu waliyyun ḥamīmun

“Good and evil are not equal; repel with what is better, and the one between whom and you there is enmity becomes as though a close friend.”

The command to repel follows the distinction between good and evil.

The passage then speaks of the possibility of transforming an antagonistic relationship.

Dafʿ here therefore involves more than exerting direct force to remove another person.

A thoughtful, good response may diminish hostility or prevent evil from spreading.

Does repelling evil mean submitting to oppression?

These two verses do not themselves establish that conclusion.

Idfaʿ still concerns restraining and countering.

The difference lies in means and manner.

Truthful speech, forgiveness, good conduct, or fair action may prevent hostility from continuing.

These verses alone do not say people must abandon every defensive action against bodily aggression or deprivation of rights.

Defense against oppression also has a place in the Qur’an.

Distinguish countering evil through better conduct from completely abandoning protection of rights.

Nor should allatī hiya aḥsanu always be restricted to one behavior, such as silence or smiling.

The better way must be assessed through the kind of evil, people’s rights, and the conduct’s actual outcome.

Does goodness always turn an enemy into a friend?

Fuṣṣilat 41:34 describes a hostile relationship becoming like close friendship.

This image shows the significance of good treatment.

It should not become a mechanical promise that every aggressor, under all circumstances, necessarily reforms after a single good response.

The following verse relates this conduct to patience and a great share.

The passage presents a valuable way of countering evil, rather than a formula guaranteeing identical choices and responses from everyone.

Lexically, dafʿ does not mean turning an enemy into a friend; that possible result comes from the verse’s context.

8. Inna Allāha yudāfiʿu ʿan alladhīna āmanū: divine defense

Al-Ḥajj 22:38

إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُدَافِعُ عَنِ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يُحِبُّ كُلَّ خَوَّانٍ كَفُورٍ

inna allāha yudāfiʿu ʿani alladhīna āmanū inna allāha lā yuḥibbu kulla khawwānin kafūrin

“Indeed, God defends those who have believed; indeed, God does not love any treacherous, ungrateful person.”

Yudāfiʿu is Form III here.

Its subject is God; ʿan alladhīna āmanū identifies those benefiting from the defense.

Does Form III mean opposition between equal powers?

Form III may express reciprocal action in some Arabic constructions.

Here, however, the verb applies to God.

It does not establish that God confronts an equal power.

Yudāfiʿu ʿan here concerns supporting believers and averting harm from them.

Some established readings also have the verb yadfaʿu.

This variation shows that dafaʿa and dāfaʿa are very close in meaning in this use.

Form III alone should therefore not ground a theory of equal opposition between independent powers.

Divine defense and human defense

Immediately afterward, Al-Ḥajj 22:39 grants permission to resist to those being fought and subjected to injustice.

Al-Ḥajj 22:40 speaks of people unjustly expelled from their homes.

Divine defense in this passage thus relates to the actual condition of oppressed people.

Yudāfiʿu nevertheless does not establish that God necessarily removes every worldly harm from believers in every situation without human action.

The Qur’an also speaks of believers suffering, undergoing trials, being killed, and experiencing persecution.

Divine defense’s manner and outcome require examining verses about assistance, trial, justice, and human responsibility together.

Conversely, divine defense should not be restricted to a single military event or immediate victory.

That restriction likewise does not follow from the root itself.

9. Wa-lawlā dafʿu Allāhi al-nāsa baʿḍahum bi-baʿḍin: checking corruption

This expression occurs twice in the Qur’an:

  • Al-Baqarah 2:251
  • Al-Ḥajj 22:40

The shared construction is:

وَلَوْلَا دَفْعُ اللَّهِ النَّاسَ بَعْضَهُمْ بِبَعْضٍ

wa-lawlā dafʿu allāhi al-nāsa baʿḍahum bi-baʿḍin

“And were it not for God repelling some people by means of others…”

Dafʿ is a verbal noun in this expression.

Allāh is its semantic agent.

Al-nāsa is its object; baʿḍahum bi-baʿḍin expresses the means and relationship of opposition among groups of people.

The expression attributes repelling to God through people checking one another.

Its construction does not make every opposing party morally equivalent.

One party may be aggressive while another defends rights and security.

Al-Baqarah 2:251: confrontation and preventing earthly corruption

Al-Baqarah 2:251

فَهَزَمُوهُمْ بِإِذْنِ اللَّهِ وَقَتَلَ دَاوُودُ جَالُوتَ وَآتَاهُ اللَّهُ الْمُلْكَ وَالْحِكْمَةَ وَعَلَّمَهُ مِمَّا يَشَاءُ وَلَوْلَا دَفْعُ اللَّهِ النَّاسَ بَعْضَهُمْ بِبَعْضٍ لَفَسَدَتِ الْأَرْضُ وَلَٰكِنَّ اللَّهَ ذُو فَضْلٍ عَلَى الْعَالَمِينَ

fa-hazamūhum bi-idhni allāhi wa-qatala dāwūdu jālūta wa-ātāhu allāhu al-mulka wa-al-ḥikmata wa-ʿallamahu mimmā yashāʾu wa-lawlā dafʿu allāhi al-nāsa baʿḍahum bi-baʿḍin la-fasadati al-arḍu wa-lākinna allāha dhū faḍlin ʿalā al-ʿālamīna

“So they defeated them with God’s permission, and David killed Goliath; God gave him kingship and wisdom and taught him of what He willed. And were it not for God repelling some people by means of others, the earth would have become corrupt; but God is gracious to the worlds.”

The expression concerning dafʿ follows the account of Saul, Goliath, and David.

The immediate setting therefore concerns two groups confronting one another and preventing an opposing force’s dominance.

Yet the stated outcome is more than Goliath’s defeat: the verse speaks of earthly corruption if such checking were absent.

This connection matters.

Opposing a force capable of causing corruption is presented in relation to preserving order and preventing ruin.

Is people checking people a rule of perpetual warfare?

The expression itself does not establish that conclusion.

Al-Baqarah 2:251 contains an instance of military confrontation.

Dafʿ’s lexical meaning, however, is not restricted to war.

The same root describes responding to evil through better conduct elsewhere in the Qur’an.

The broader principle may therefore encompass different ways of checking corruption and aggression, from actual defense against attack to restraining oppression through justice and suitable action.

Without adequate evidence, however, Al-Baqarah 2:251 should not be said to directly name all those nonmilitary methods.

Its immediate example is warfare and defeating an aggressive force; the root’s meaning is broader than warfare.

Nor does the expression establish that every war, however unjust or aggressive, exemplifies divinely approved dafʿ.

Preventing corruption is identified as an important result. The parties’ conduct must be assessed through justice and the Qur’an’s other directives.

Al-Ḥajj 22:40: defending people and places of worship

Part of Al-Ḥajj 22:40

الَّذِينَ أُخْرِجُوا مِنْ دِيَارِهِمْ بِغَيْرِ حَقٍّ إِلَّا أَنْ يَقُولُوا رَبُّنَا اللَّهُ وَلَوْلَا دَفْعُ اللَّهِ النَّاسَ بَعْضَهُمْ بِبَعْضٍ لَهُدِّمَتْ صَوَامِعُ وَبِيَعٌ وَصَلَوَاتٌ وَمَسَاجِدُ يُذْكَرُ فِيهَا اسْمُ اللَّهِ كَثِيرًا

alladhīna ukhrijū min diyārihim bi-ghayri ḥaqqin illā an yaqūlū rabbunā allāhu wa-lawlā dafʿu allāhi al-nāsa baʿḍahum bi-baʿḍin la-huddimat ṣawāmiʿu wa-biyaʿun wa-ṣalawātun wa-masājidu yudhkaru fīhā ismu allāhi kathīran

“Those who were unjustly expelled from their homes only for saying: Our Lord is God. And were it not for God repelling some people by means of others, monasteries, churches, synagogues, and mosques in which God’s name is frequently remembered would have been demolished.”

This follows Al-Ḥajj 22:39, which grants permission to resist to those being fought and subjected to injustice.

The subject is therefore defense against aggression and unjust expulsion.

The Qur’an then identifies destruction of places where God’s name is remembered as the consequence of absent restraint.

Protecting the right to worship

A significant feature is that the verse names more than Muslim mosques.

Monasteries, churches, Jewish places of worship, and mosques appear together.

Preventing destruction of places of worship is thus not restricted here to protecting one religious community.

This conclusion comes from the entire verse and its naming of places of worship, rather than the lexical meaning of dafʿ alone.

Dafʿ itself does not mean religious freedom or preserving a place of worship.

Those are purposes and results expressed by the verse’s construction.

Their connection with dafʿ nevertheless matters: checking aggression may enable people’s right to worship and live without coercion.

Does defense authorize imposing belief?

Al-Ḥajj 22:39 and Al-Ḥajj 22:40 concern people attacked, oppressed, and unjustly expelled from their homes.

These verses should therefore not become authorization to impose beliefs without regard to defense’s cause.

Al-Baqarah 2:256 states the absence of compulsion in religion.

Defending people’s right to worship and preventing their expulsion for belief differs from imposing that belief upon others.

Preserve this distinction when examining war, peace, justice, and religious freedom.

Does such checking occur only through warfare?

In the immediate setting of Al-Ḥajj 22:39, permission to resist concerns a military situation.

Military defense is therefore a clear instance.

Lexically, however, dafʿ is not restricted to warfare.

Early commentaries also discuss restraining injustice through justice, preventing aggression, and checking one group by means of another.

Such broader readings should nevertheless not obscure the verse’s own setting of warfare and oppression.

Distinguish a root’s general meaning from a verse’s particular example.

Al-Ṭabarī: resistance and checking oppression

10. The dafʿ and difāʿ reading variation

Al-Baqarah 2:251 and Al-Ḥajj 22:40 have a significant reading variation.

Ḥafṣ reads dafʿu Allāhi.

Nāfiʿ, Abū Jaʿfar, and Yaʿqūb read difāʿu Allāhi.

Dafʿ is the familiar Form I verbal noun.

Difāʿ may be a Form III verbal noun; another recorded analysis treats it as a verbal noun of dafaʿa itself, like kitāb for kataba.

Reading and linguistic sources offer different explanations of the relationship.

Some relate difāʿ here to opposition and two parties participating in repelling.

Others explain that dāfaʿa and dafaʿa may have similar meanings in averting harm and that Form III does not require opposing equal agents.

The variation matters morphologically.

It should not be made into two wholly incompatible theories about God’s and humanity’s roles.

Both readings retain checking corruption through people repelling one another.

Al-Naḥḥās, Iʿrāb al-Qurʾān: analysis of dafʿ and difāʿ

For a Ḥafṣ-based text, dafʿ remains the base form, while the established difāʿ variant belongs in lexical explanation.

Itḥāf Fuḍalāʾ al-Bashar: the difāʿ reading of Nāfiʿ, Abū Jaʿfar, and Yaʿqūb

11. Mā lahu min dāfiʿin: no restrainer against punishment

Al-Ṭūr 52:7 and Al-Ṭūr 52:8

إِنَّ عَذَابَ رَبِّكَ لَوَاقِعٌ
مَا لَهُ مِنْ دَافِعٍ

inna ʿadhāba rabbika la-wāqiʿun / mā lahu min dāfiʿin

“Indeed, your Lord’s punishment will occur, and it has no restrainer.”

Dāfiʿin is an active participle from dafaʿa.

The subject is someone or something capable of checking or averting punishment.

The sentence negates that possibility.

Min dāfiʿin follows negation and reinforces the absence of a restrainer.

The verse thus denies any agent capable of averting the specified punishment’s occurrence.

Al-Maʿārij 70:1, Al-Maʿārij 70:2, and Al-Maʿārij 70:3

سَأَلَ سَائِلٌ بِعَذَابٍ وَاقِعٍ
لِلْكَافِرِينَ لَيْسَ لَهُ دَافِعٌ
مِنَ اللَّهِ ذِي الْمَعَارِجِ

saʾala sāʾilun bi-ʿadhābin wāqiʿin / li-al-kāfirīna laysa lahu dāfiʿun / mina allāhi dhī al-maʿāriji

“An asker asked for an impending punishment, for the disbelievers, which has no restrainer, from God, Lord of the ways of ascent.”

Dāfiʿ is again an active participle.

The construction concerns punishment without anyone to avert it.

Is dāfiʿ identical with an intercessor?

Lexically, dāfiʿ names someone or something preventing an event or its arrival.

Intercession has a different linguistic family and requires its own semantic study.

A theological discussion may connect averting punishment with claims about intercession, but that does not make the roots identical.

Dāfiʿ alone does not define the presence or absence of every kind of intercession.

These passages clearly state that the specified punishment has no restrainer.

Nor do they establish that people never need to seek forgiveness, reform, or repent, or that such actions are ineffective.

They concern punishment whose occurrence is presented as certain in that context.

Repentance, forgiveness, and human responsibility must be examined through the relevant passages.

12. Differences between D F ʿ and related roots

D F ʿ (dafaʿa) and M N ʿ (manaʿa): repelling and preventing

Manʿ concerns restraint and prevention.

Dafʿ, too, can prevent harm from reaching someone.

In many uses, however, manʿ emphasizes withholding permission or preventing something from occurring.

Dafʿ can instead highlight moving aside something that is already moving or exerting an effect.

For example, someone might prevent entry to a place from the outset, or repel a danger as it approaches.

This distinction should not become an absolute rule that manʿ always precedes an occurrence while dafʿ always follows it.

Both families can be used in Arabic for prevention and restraint.

Study of M N ʿ

D F ʿ (dafaʿa) and R D D (radda): moving aside and returning

Radd in Arabic concerns returning or sending something back.

Dafʿ, too, can move someone or something away from a position.

Repelling does not, however, necessarily mean returning something to its original point.

A stone may be moved out of a path without being returned to its previous location.

Radd can emphasize return to a previous state or point of reference.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:6, handing property over to an orphan is close in outcome to returning an owner’s possessions, but the verb itself describes transfer and delivery.

Study of R D D

D F ʿ (dafaʿa) and Ṣ D D (ṣadda): repelling and barring a path

In the Qur’an, ṣadd concerns restraint, turning away, or preventing movement toward a goal.

This family describes, for example, barring people from God’s path or from the Sacred Mosque.

Dafʿ can also express restraint.

Ṣadd may highlight obstruction of a particular path or goal, whereas dafʿ has a broader range encompassing moving aside, defending, and transferring.

Consequently, not every act of ṣadd is legitimate defense, and not every act of dafʿ means barring someone from a particular path or belief.

D F ʿ (dafaʿa) and D Ḥ R (daḥara): driving away and expulsion

Daḥr concerns driving someone away from a position.

Dafʿ, too, can mean moving aside or driving away.

Qur’anic uses of daḥr, however, involve expulsion and being kept away.

Dafʿ can also describe defending someone, handing over property, or responding to evil with goodness.

Its positive and legal uses should therefore not be restricted to expulsion and abasement.

Study of D Ḥ R

D F ʿ (dafaʿa) and D ʿ ʿ (daʿʿa): repelling and harsh driving

Daʿʿ in Arabic concerns forceful driving accompanied by harshness and mistreatment.

Al-Māʿūn 107:2 uses this root for driving away an orphan.

Qur’anic uses of dafʿ, however, encompass resisting an enemy, handing over property, and responding to evil with goodness.

Not every instance of dafʿ is therefore daʿʿ.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:6, handing an orphan’s property over to its owner is expressed through dafʿ; harshly driving an orphan away in Al-Māʿūn 107:2 is expressed through daʿʿ.

The comparison matters: two verbs may sound similar, yet one occurs in a context of protecting an orphan’s rights and the other in a context of mistreating an orphan.

Study of D ʿ ʿ

D F ʿ (dafaʿa) and N Ṣ R (naṣara): defense and help

Naṣr concerns help and victory.

Dafʿ can be one way of helping: preventing harm to someone is a form of support.

Help does not always mean averting a danger, however.

Someone may help another person by providing food, knowledge, or necessary resources without driving away a threat.

Nor does averting one danger necessarily mean final victory in every sphere.

In Al-Ḥajj 22:39 and Al-Ḥajj 22:40, defense and divine help appear alongside one another, but each word contributes its own meaning.

Study of N Ṣ R

D F ʿ (dafaʿa) and Q T L (qatala): repelling and fighting

In Āl ʿImrān 3:167, qātilū and idfaʿū appear alongside one another.

Their association shows that dafʿ there concerns defense in a military situation.

The root dafʿ itself does not mean killing.

Resistance to aggression can also take different forms.

Every Qur’anic use of dafʿ should not be turned into armed combat, nor should every military action be deemed legitimate defense without examining its grounds and limits.

Study of Q T L

D F ʿ (dafaʿa) and D F Q (dafaqa): propelling and gushing

These roots share their first two letters, but their final letters differ.

Dafʿ denotes moving aside, propelling, or removing.

In uses concerning liquids, dafq describes forceful flowing or gushing out.

In Al-Ṭāriq 86:6, the expression māʾin dāfiqin concerns a gushing fluid.

Dāfiq in that verse belongs to D F Q, not D F ʿ.

Although both families can describe the movement of water, that overlap does not make them a single root.

Dāfiʿ in Al-Ṭūr 52:8 should therefore not be confused with dāfiq in Al-Ṭāriq 86:6.

Study of D F Q

13. Translation proposals for Forqan

A single fixed Persian equivalent cannot serve every Qur’anic form of D F ʿ.

The most suitable translation must follow the verb’s construction and preposition.

Al-Baqarah 2:251 and Al-Ḥajj 22:40

دَفْعُ اللَّهِ النَّاسَ بَعْضَهُمْ بِبَعْضٍ

“God’s repelling some people by means of others.”

More fluently:

“If God had not repelled some people by means of others…”

“Repelling” is appropriate here because it encompasses resistance and preventing domination and corruption.

“Stirring up war among people” and “destroying one group by means of another” are not direct translations; they introduce additional ideas.

Āl ʿImrān 3:167

أَوِ ادْفَعُوا

“Or defend.”

This rendering fits the context of military confrontation.

“Or fight” obscures its distinction from qātilū.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:6

فَادْفَعُوا إِلَيْهِمْ أَمْوَالَهُمْ

“Then hand their property over to them.”

فَإِذَا دَفَعْتُمْ إِلَيْهِمْ أَمْوَالَهُمْ

“Then when you hand their property over to them.”

“Hand over” or “deliver” suits both expressions.

“Pay money” is too narrow, while “give property as a gift” fails to convey the orphans’ prior ownership.

Al-Ḥajj 22:38

يُدَافِعُ عَنِ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا

“He defends those who have believed.”

This rendering follows the Form III construction and the preposition ʿan.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:96

ادْفَعْ بِالَّتِي هِيَ أَحْسَنُ السَّيِّئَةَ

“Repel evil with what is better.”

Repelling here still means responding to evil, but its method is specified by allatī hiya aḥsan, “what is better.”

Fuṣṣilat 41:34

ادْفَعْ بِالَّتِي هِيَ أَحْسَنُ

“Repel with what is better.”

If “evil” is understood as the object from the preceding context to make the sentence fluent, the base translation should preferably follow the Arabic structure, with explanation in the reflection.

Al-Ṭūr 52:8 and Al-Maʿārij 70:2

دَافِع

“Restrainer” or “one who averts.”

“No restrainer” is a natural rendering in both verses.

It should not be translated directly as “no intercessor,” since intercession belongs to a separate root.

Non-Qur’anic equivalents

Dafaʿa al-shayʾa: he moved something aside or drove it away.

Dafaʿa ʿanhu: he averted harm from him or defended him.

Dafaʿa ilayhi: he handed something over to him.

Tadāfaʿa al-qawmu: the people pushed one another back or opposed one another.

Indafaʿa: it began moving or advanced swiftly.

Duffāʿ: in uses concerning water, a torrent or powerful wave.

These meanings should be distinguished and used in their own contexts.

14. Conclusion

D F ʿ in Arabic concerns moving something aside, removing it, or propelling it forward.

Ibn Fāris identifies its semantic core as tanḥiyat al-shayʾ, “moving something aside”; Ibn Manẓūr emphasizes removal by force.

From this basic meaning come uses concerning defending someone, preventing harm, resistance between two groups, forceful movement of water, and handing something over to another person.

The root occurs ten times in nine Qur’anic verses.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:6, dafʿ means handing the orphans’ property over to them. The preposition ilā specifies the direction of transfer.

In Al-Ḥajj 22:38, yudāfiʿu ʿan describes defending believers and averting harm from them.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:167, dafʿ appears in a military confrontation alongside qātilū, “fight.”

Al-Muʾminūn 23:96 and Fuṣṣilat 41:34 speak of repelling evil by a better method. Dafʿ therefore does not necessarily mean violent resistance.

In Al-Baqarah 2:251 and Al-Ḥajj 22:40, repelling people by means of other people concerns preventing corruption and the destruction of places of worship.

In the context of Al-Ḥajj, defense relates to suffering oppression, wrongful expulsion, and preserving different places of worship. These verses should therefore not be made an unlimited authorization for war or imposing belief.

In Al-Ṭūr 52:8 and Al-Maʿārij 70:2, the active participle dāfiʿ occurs in negative constructions, expressing the absence of anyone who can avert the punishment in question.

These uses show that the root itself carries no fixed moral value. Moving something aside may prevent oppression; handing over property may restore its owner’s rights; and repelling evil may be accomplished through good conduct.

Conversely, the presence of a verb from dafʿ alone does not establish the legitimacy of a violent action.

Proposed final definition: In its established semantic core, D F ʿ denotes moving something aside, removing it, or propelling it forward. With ʿan, it can describe averting harm and defending another person; with ilā, handing something over and transferring it to a recipient; in constructions of resistance, restraining one force by means of another. In the Qur’an it describes defense against aggression, responding to evil in a better way, handing over financial rights, and the absence of a restrainer against punishment. The direction and value of each use must be understood from its construction and context, rather than from the root alone. Brief root meaning: Moving aside, removing, or propelling; with ʿan, defending and averting harm; with ilā, handing over and delivering.
