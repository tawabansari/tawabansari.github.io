Core meaning: a burning flame or tongue of fire; related lexical uses include intense heat and, in some early reports, smoke.

The Arabic root sh w ẓ concerns flames and tongues of fire. شُوَاظ describes the burning flame that rises from a fire.

Many lexicons define شُواظ as a flame without smoke. Other reports use it for smoke or the intense heat of fire and the sun.

Flame and burning heat are therefore central to the family. Smokelessness is prominent in its common definition but is not a condition shared by every recorded use.

The root occurs only once in the Quran, as شُوَاظٌ in Al-Rahman 55:35.

1. The root in early Arabic

شُوَاظ: a burning tongue of fire

Early Arabic uses شُوَاظ for a flame or tongue of fire.

A blacksmith's furnace illustrates its physical image.

As the smith blows air into the furnace, flames rise between glowing coals and the fire grows hotter.

One can distinguish the fire as a whole from its rising flames.

نار names fire broadly, while شُواظ foregrounds its burning flame.

A witness from early Arabic poetry

A poem attributed in lexicons to Umayyah ibn Khalaf says:

يَمَانِيًّا يَظَلُّ يَشُدُّ كِيرًا
وَيَنْفُخُ دَائِبًا لَهَبَ الشُّوَاظِ

yamāniyyan yaẓallu yashuddu kīran / wa-yanfukhu dāʾiban lahaba al-shuwāẓ

“A Yemeni man who keeps working the bellows and steadily blows into the flames.”

Here شُواظ accompanies لَهَب and depicts furnace flames.

كِير is a bellows for blowing air into fire, while يَنْفُخُ means blowing.

This witness thus concerns actual fire rising from a furnace.

Some sources attribute the poem to Umayyah ibn Khalaf, others to Umayyah ibn Abi al-Salt. Parts of its wording also vary. It is preserved in the Arabic lexical tradition, but its precise authorship and date should not be treated as certain.

Its semantic evidence is clear: شُواظ describes a burning flame. The verse alone does not establish that it is smokeless or that it must always be detached from the fire.

Figurative use in poetry

A poetic witness attributed to Ruʾbah ibn al-ʿAjjaj says:

إِنَّ لَهُمْ مِنْ وَقْعِنَا أَقْيَاظًا
وَنَارَ حَرْبٍ تُسْعِرُ الشَّوَاظَا

inna lahum min waqʿinā aqyāẓā / wa-nāra ḥarbin tusʿiru al-shawāẓā

“Our blows bring them scorching heat, and a fire of war that kindles the flames.”

Here شَواظ describes the fire of war.

The image need not mean actual fire: the poet evokes violent confrontation and its harm through burning flames.

Ruʾbah lived in the Islamic period and died in the second century AH. This is therefore a witness to that period's literary Arabic, not independent evidence of pre-Islamic usage.

It shows how شُواظ could depict an intense, searing situation as well as actual fire.

The figurative use draws on the flame image rather than replacing it.

Is شُواظ always smokeless?

Many lexical definitions state:

الشُّوَاظُ: اللَّهَبُ الَّذِي لَا دُخَانَ فِيهِ

al-shuwāẓu al-lahabu alladhī lā dukhāna fīh

That is, “shuwaz is a flame without smoke.”

This definition is significant and should not be overlooked.

Reports attributed to Ibn Shumayl, however, also record the word for smoke, the heat of fire, and even the sun's heat.

For example, أَصَابَنِي شُوَاظٌ مِنَ الشَّمْسِ describes being struck by the sun's burning heat.

Here the sun is not a furnace sending visible tongues of flame toward the person; its intense heat is in view.

The lexical record therefore extends beyond the narrow definition of a smokeless flame.

This does not mean smoke and flame were always considered identical. A word can refer in different uses to flame, smoke from fire, or burning heat.

Of these uses, flame is the clearest contextual rendering in the Quranic verse.

2. Forms and uses

Compared with many Quranic roots, this family has relatively few recorded morphological forms.

Its principal noun is شُوَاظ, also known with the pronunciation شِوَاظ.

Some sources also record شَوَاظ.

These differ in the initial vowel, without necessarily differing in meaning.

Later dictionaries also record شَاظَ for the intensification or stirring of pain, illness, or anger.

Such uses should not be assigned to pre-Islamic Arabic without independent historical evidence. No verb from this family occurs in the Quran.

Quranic form: شُوَاظٌ

This noun follows the pattern فُعَال and occurs as an indefinite singular in Al-Rahman 55:35.

Reported readings of the verse also include شِوَاظ, with an initial i vowel.

This reading variation does not change the basic sense of flame.

3. Quranic use: flame sent upon jinn and humankind

Al-Rahman 55:35

يُرْسَلُ عَلَيْكُمَا شُوَاظٌ مِنْ نَارٍ وَنُحَاسٌ فَلَا تَنْتَصِرَانِ

yursalu ʿalaykumā shuwāẓun min nārin wa-nuḥāsun fa-lā tantaṣirān

Al-Raḥmān 55:35

“A flame of fire and molten copper will be sent upon you two groups, and you will be unable to defend yourselves.” Here “molten copper” selects one established interpretation of نُحاس; the alternative “smoke” is discussed below.

Al-Rahman 55:35

شُوَاظٌ here describes a flame or tongue of fire.

يُرْسَلُ is passive, and شُوَاظٌ is its grammatical subject, traditionally called the substitute for the agent.

Being sent comes from يُرْسَلُ, not from شُواظ itself.

مِنْ نَارٍ identifies the flame's substance or source.

Three parts thus have different functions:

يُرْسَلُ expresses being sent; شُوَاظٌ identifies a burning flame; مِنْ نَارٍ relates it to fire.

Why does شُواظ appear alongside نار?

If شُواظ already means a flame, why add مِنْ نَارٍ?

The words differ in scope.

نار is the broader name for fire; شُواظ identifies its flame.

Similarly, “a tongue of fire” specifies a feature, while “of fire” identifies its source or substance.

شُوَاظٌ مِنْ نَارٍ has this kind of construction.

The phrase is therefore meaningful rather than a pointless repetition: it distinguishes the flame from the fire to which it belongs.

The address to jinn and humankind

Al-Rahman 55:33 addresses the two communities of jinn and humankind.

It speaks of passing beyond the regions of the heavens and earth and of doing so only with sulṭān.

Al-Rahman 55:35 then speaks of شُواظ and نحاس being sent upon both groups and their inability to defend themselves.

عَلَيْكُمَا therefore addresses those two communities, rather than two individual persons.

The verse depicts divine power and encompassing authority, and the two groups' inability to resist.

In this context, شُواظ contributes to the imagery of warning and punishment.

The word itself does not mean punishment, encirclement, or inability to escape. Those ideas arise from the verse and its context.

شُواظ and نُحاس

شُواظٌ مِنْ نَارٍ appears beside نُحَاسٌ.

نحاس belongs to a different lexical family and requires separate examination.

نُحاس names copper in Arabic, but early explanations of this verse also give the sense of smoke. “Molten” clarifies the copper interpretation in this setting; it is not a separate word in the Arabic verse.

Readings with both nominative and genitive نحاس are reported.

In وَنُحَاسٌ, the noun is coordinated with شُوَاظٌ, with both included in what is sent.

In وَنُحَاسٍ, a grammatical relationship with نَارٍ is discussed.

This matters for the whole verse's analysis without changing the main sense of شُواظ.

In either construction, شُواظ can be understood as a burning flame.

Its proximity to نحاس does not justify importing copper or molten metal into sh w ẓ, nor does it by itself establish a smoke sense for شُواظ.

Does شُواظ identify a particular physical phenomenon?

Intense flames can be compared to eruptions of fire, flows of molten material, or very hot gases.

The verse's word is not a scientific label specifying composition or physical structure.

It uses a familiar Arabic image: a burning flame or tongue of fire.

The word alone does not determine temperature, color, fuel, gas composition, or how the flame is sent.

Nor do the nearby verses about passing beyond the heavens and earth establish that شُواظ names a known natural barrier in space.

Such identifications need independent evidence and should not replace the lexical meaning.

4. Comparison with related roots

sh w ẓ and l h b

لَهَب means a flame or tongue of fire.

شُوَاظ occupies the same semantic field.

Lexicons often explain شُواظ through لَهَب; poetic witnesses also place them together.

The common definition of شُواظ emphasizes a pure, smokeless flame, whereas لهب is a broader word for flame.

This is not an absolute boundary throughout the language, since some lexical reports use شُواظ for smoke or heat.

لهب is thus a close Arabic equivalent, without requiring every detail of usage to coincide.

sh w ẓ and s ʿ r

سَعِير, from s ʿ r, concerns a blazing or kindled fire.

شُوَاظ more particularly identifies its burning flame.

A fire may blaze intensely and send up many tongues of flame.

The fire's blazing condition and its flames are related but distinguishable aspects.

Rendering شُواظ simply as “a blazing fire” can therefore weaken its specific flame image.

sh w ẓ and d kh n

دُخَان means smoke.

The familiar Quranic interpretation of شُواظ concerns flame.

Many lexicons express the distinction by defining it as a flame without smoke.

A fire may produce abundant smoke while its bright flames remain visible.

Smoke and flame can accompany one another without looking or behaving alike.

Yet recorded smoke uses of شُواظ prevent smokelessness from becoming a condition of every historical occurrence.

In Al-Rahman 55:35, مِنْ نَارٍ makes its relationship with fire explicit. The flame interpretation rests on lexical usage and explanations of the verse, not on this phrase alone.

sh w ẓ and sh ẓ y

شَظِيَّة, from sh ẓ y, names a fragment or splinter separated from something, such as wood, bone, or a broken object.

Some lexical explanations likewise describe شُواظ as a piece of fire.

That shared image of separation does not make the roots identical.

شظية concerns a fragment of an object; شواظ concerns flame and burning heat.

“Shrapnel” or “projected fragments of fire” is therefore not a direct equivalent unless a particular context independently supports that image.

“Flame” or “tongue of fire” is clearer and more direct in Al-Rahman 55:35.

5. Translation proposals for Forqan

Quranic word: شُوَاظٌ

Root: sh w ẓ

Word type: an indefinite singular noun in the nominative, following فُعَال.

Brief root meaning: a burning flame or tongue of fire; lexical uses also include intense heat and sometimes smoke.

Meaning in this verse: a flame of fire sent upon jinn and humankind.

Suggested rendering: “a flame” or “a tongue of flame.”

The phrase شُوَاظٌ مِنْ نَارٍ may be translated:

“A tongue of fire.”

With نحاس understood as molten copper, the full Al-Rahman 55:35 may be rendered:

“A tongue of fire and molten copper will be sent upon you two groups, and you will be unable to defend yourselves.”

“Tongue of fire” makes the image vivid, while “flame” is also natural and defensible.

“Smokeless” reflects a common lexical explanation and can be used in an interpretive translation. The broader “flame” preserves the main sense without settling the full range of recorded differences.

“Explosive fire,” “fireballs,” or “a flow of plasma” adds characteristics not directly expressed by شُواظ.

6. Conclusion

The Arabic root sh w ẓ concerns a burning flame or tongue of fire.

Early uses connect it with furnace fire, flames, and intense heat.

Many lexicons define شُواظ as smokeless flame, while reports of smoke and heat uses show a wider semantic field.

The root occurs once in the Quran, in Al-Rahman 55:35.

There it describes a flame of fire sent upon jinn and humankind.

Being sent is expressed by يُرْسَلُ and the relationship to fire by مِنْ نَارٍ. The entire scene should therefore not be inserted into the root's definition.

Final definition of sh w ẓ:

“A burning flame or tongue of fire, emphasizing flame and intense heat. Many lexical uses specify smokeless flame, while smoke and intense heat are also recorded. In the Quran, شُوَاظ describes a tongue of fire sent upon jinn and humankind in a scene of warning and punishment.”
