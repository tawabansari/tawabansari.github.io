Core meaning: becoming complete, ample, and extensive, so that something is full and sufficient in extent, coverage, or provision. In material usage, this concerns the length and breadth of clothing and armor; with blessings, it concerns making provision full and abundant.

1. Introducing the root and its pre-Islamic Arabic meaning

The Arabic root S B GH concerns completeness, amplitude, extent, and fullness.

Sabagha al-shayʾu means that something became complete, ample, or extensive.

Sabagha al-thawbu describes a garment that is full in length and breadth.

Dirʿun sābighatun is long, ample armor providing full coverage.

Niʿmatun sābighatun likewise denotes an extensive, abundant blessing.

These uses do not merely concern size. Something can be long and extensive yet deficient in function.

In established usage, sābigh describes something whose extension, breadth, or coverage warrants calling it complete and ample.

Armor witnesses are particularly important for understanding this family in pre-Qur’anic Arabic: sābighah explicitly denotes armor in poetry attributed to pre-Islamic poets.

First pre-Islamic witness: long, ample armor in ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm

The pre-Islamic poet ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm says in his celebrated Muʿallaqah:

عَلَيْنَا كُلُّ سَابِغَةٍ دِلَاصٍ
تَرَى فَوْقَ النِّطَاقِ لَهَا غُضُونَا

ʿalaynā kullu sābighatin dilāṣin / tarā fawqa al-niṭāqi lahā ghuḍūnā

Translation:

“We wear every ample, gleaming coat of mail, whose folds you see above the belt.”

Here sābighah denotes armor.

Dilāṣ conveys its smoothness and sheen.

Al-niṭāq is the belt.

Ghuḍūn denotes the folds visible in the armor.

The poet describes armor sufficiently long and ample for parts of it to fold above the belt.

Sābighah here does more than identify armor: it describes its breadth, length, and extent of coverage.

This gives a tangible illustration of the root: the armor extends over the body and covers more of it than a short, tight covering would.

Its sheen, however, comes from dilāṣ, not sābighah.

Smoothness, brilliance, and beauty should therefore not be included in the root’s core meaning.

Second pre-Islamic witness: full armor in ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād

The pre-Islamic poet ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād says in his Muʿallaqah:

وَمِشَكِّ سَابِغَةٍ هَتَكْتُ فُرُوجَهَا
بِالسَّيْفِ عَنْ حَامِي الْحَقِيقَةِ مُعْلِمِ

wa-mishakki sābighatin hataktu furūjahā / bi-l-sayfi ʿan ḥāmī al-ḥaqīqati muʿlimi

Translation:

“Many a full coat of mail have I torn open with my sword, exposing a marked warrior defending what he was bound to protect.”

Early explanations of mishakk differ: they include armor itself, its connecting rivets, and an armed man. The translation here follows the armor interpretation; sābighah remains associated with ample armor in these explanations.

Sābighah is armor with long, extensive coverage.

Hataktu furūjahā speaks of tearing open parts of the armor.

The poet highlights his sword’s force by describing the extensive armor it pierced.

Lexically, the verse matters because sābighah describes full, ample armor.

It also shows that full coverage does not entail absolute impenetrability: the poet speaks of piercing that very armor.

Sābighah should therefore not be expanded into “armor no weapon can penetrate.”

Full coverage and absolute strength are distinct concepts.

What the two witnesses establish

Both verses use sābighah in connection with armor.

ʿAmr’s verse clearly displays its length and breadth.

ʿAntarah presents armor as an extensive, connected covering.

Both witnesses are transmitted in Muʿallaqāt attributed to pre-Islamic poets. Although this poetry reached us through later transmission, its presence in two celebrated poems supports the ancient usage of sābighah.

These witnesses independently attest the description of full, long, ample coverage.

They do not alone date every abstract or figurative use of the root.

A historically grounded definition

On the basis of poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period and recorded Arabic meanings, the following definition is possible:

In its established ancient sense, S B GH denotes becoming complete and extensive, especially when coverage, length, or breadth makes something full and ample.

Armor and clothing are clear material examples.

Arabic lexical records also attest abundant blessings, ample livelihood, extended rainfall, and the length of certain bodily parts or objects.

These recorded uses should not all be assigned to the same pre-Islamic period without independent evidence.

Nor does their semantic connection prove that every branch originated historically in the image of armor or clothing.

2. Important forms and derivatives

Sabagha yasbughu

This is the family’s basic verb.

It means becoming complete, ample, or extensive.

سَبَغَ الثَّوْبُ

sabagha al-thawbu

“The garment became long and ample.”

سَبَغَتِ الدِّرْعُ

sabaghat al-dirʿu

“The armor became extensive and full in coverage.”

سَبَغَتِ النِّعْمَةُ

sabaghat al-niʿmatu

“The blessing became extensive and abundant.”

The subject differs across these examples.

With clothing and armor, extent can concern dimensions and coverage.

With blessings, it concerns abundance and provision.

Each specific sense must therefore be understood in relation to its subject.

Subūgh

The verbal noun of the basic verb.

It denotes completeness, amplitude, and extent.

Subūgh al-niʿmah concerns the abundance and extent of a blessing.

Applied to clothing and armor, it can express full, ample coverage.

Sābigh

The active participle of the basic verb.

It describes something complete, ample, or extensive.

For example:

ثَوْبٌ سَابِغٌ

thawbun sābighun

“A long, ample garment.”

مَطَرٌ سَابِغٌ

maṭarun sābighun

“Rain whose reach or duration extends”; this need not mean rainfall over every place.

ذَنَبٌ سَابِغٌ

dhanabun sābighun

In lexical usage, a tail described as long and full.

These examples show that sābigh is not exclusively the name of military equipment.

It can describe different things where breadth, length, or completeness is relevant.

Sābighah

The feminine form of sābigh.

دِرْعٌ سَابِغَةٌ

dirʿun sābighatun

“Ample, full armor.”

Dirʿ, meaning armor, is treated as feminine in this expression, and its adjective agrees with it.

In established Arabic usage, sābighah can denote full, ample armor without explicitly mentioning dirʿ.

The adjective then stands on its own for the object.

This usage matters for understanding Qur’anic sābighāt.

Sābighāt

The sound feminine plural of sābighah.

This form occurs in Sabaʾ 34:11.

There it denotes ample coats of mail with extensive coverage.

That meaning follows from established usage and the context of ironworking and linking armor.

The root’s general meaning is not “armor.”

Armor is one thing it can describe.

Asbagha yusbighu

A Form IV verb.

It means making something complete, ample, or extensive.

أَسْبَغَ الثَّوْبَ

asbagha al-thawba

“He made the garment ample and extensive.”

أَسْبَغَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِ النِّعْمَةَ

asbagha Allāhu ʿalayhi al-niʿmata

“The God made the blessing upon him abundant and full.”

In this construction, the subject causes something to become complete or extensive.

This is the verb occurring in Luqmān 31:20.

Isbāgh

The verbal noun of Form IV.

It denotes completing, extending fully, or performing something thoroughly and sufficiently.

Isbāgh al-wuḍūʾ is a recognized Arabic expression.

It means performing ablution fully and properly.

That precise expression does not occur in the Qur’an.

This extra-Qur’anic usage cannot itself add details about washing limbs, repetitions, or particular rulings to the ablution verses.

Lexical isbāgh means thorough completion; how ablution is performed requires examining the instructions and constructions concerning ablution itself.

Sabghah

Lexical records also give sabghah for amplitude, ease, and ample provision.

For example, sabghatun mina al-ʿaysh denotes an ample, comfortable livelihood.

This belongs to the field of completeness and breadth.

Sabghah from S B GH and ṣibghah from Ṣ B GH are nevertheless distinct words.

Tasbīgh and tasbighah

Tasbīgh concerns making something ample or complete.

In armor and helmet terminology, tasbighah denotes linked mail or a covering attached to the helmet that covers the neck and the gap between helmet and armor.

This has a clear material connection with completing coverage.

A covering restricted to the head can leave the neck exposed.

Adding neck protection can make the coverage fuller.

The image helps explain the meaning; it is not a proven historical origin for every derivative.

Musbigh

The active participle of Form IV.

Someone or something that causes completion or extension.

In armor usage, it also describes someone wearing ample armor.

Forms actually occurring in the Qur’an

The root occurs at only two locations, in two forms:

  • Wa-asbagha in Luqmān 31:20.
  • Sābighātin in Sabaʾ 34:11.

The first is a Form IV past verb.

The second is the sound feminine plural of sābighah.

Other forms, including sabagha, subūgh, sābigh, isbāgh, and tasbighah, are recorded in Arabic but do not occur in those forms in the Qur’an.

3. The semantic core: completeness, extent, and coverage

To understand the connection between the two Qur’anic uses, the root’s general meaning must be distinguished from each verse’s particular features.

Completeness of coverage

With armor, subūgh concerns length, breadth, and extent of coverage.

Short armor may protect only part of the body.

Long, ample armor covers more of it.

Sābighah in this setting emphasizes full coverage.

It does not alone establish greater thickness, weight, or impenetrability.

Those depend on the metal, construction, workmanship, and other factors.

Abundance and extent of provision

With blessings, the subject is no longer an object’s length or bodily covering.

Subūgh of a blessing concerns abundant, extensive provision.

For example, sufficient resources and ample opportunities for living can be described through this family’s meaning.

The material image of clothing or armor can help explain comprehensiveness here.

Blessings should not therefore be said to be physically draped over the body like clothing.

This is a semantic explanation, not a physical description of blessing.

Completeness and absolute perfection

An important distinction is between completeness relative to a subject or function and absolute perfection.

Armor may be complete in size and coverage without resisting every weapon.

A garment can be long and ample but poor in material or stitching.

Likewise, enjoying abundant blessings does not mean having no needs, difficulties, or shortages.

Subūgh does not itself mean the absence of every defect or limitation in every respect.

The semantic core is completeness and extent in the respect specified by the subject and context, not absolute perfection in every respect.

4. An iʿmal sābighātin: ample armor in Sabaʾ 34:11

Sabaʾ 34:11 states:

أَنِ اعْمَلْ سَابِغَاتٍ وَقَدِّرْ فِي السَّرْدِ وَاعْمَلُوا صَالِحًا إِنِّي بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ بَصِيرٌ

ani iʿmal sābighātin wa-qaddir fī al-sardi wa-iʿmalū ṣāliḥan innī bi-mā taʿmalūna baṣīr

Sabaʾ 34:11

Translation:

“Make ample coats of mail, and observe measure in linking them; and do righteous work. Indeed, I see what you do.”

Sābighātin is the feminine plural of sābighah.

Morphologically it is an adjective derived from the basic verb.

Here it functions as a designation for full, ample coats of mail.

Why does sābighāt denote armor here?

In Sabaʾ 34:10, The God mentions a favor granted to David.

The verse ends:

وَأَلَنَّا لَهُ الْحَدِيدَ

wa-alannā lahu al-ḥadīd

Sabaʾ 34:10

“And We softened iron for him.”

Sabaʾ 34:11 then commands making sābighāt and observing measure in sard.

This connection identifies the subject as making equipment or a covering from iron.

In armor terminology, sard concerns linking and joining its rings and components.

“Ample coats of mail” thus follows from the established use of sābighah and the context of iron and linking.

The adjective’s meaning must nevertheless be distinguished from its referent.

Sābigh basically means full and ample.

Armor is the object to which this description applies in established usage and this verse’s context.

Why do length and breadth matter?

Armor protects the body.

An excessively short covering leaves parts of the body unprotected.

Unsuitable construction can hinder movement.

Armor proportionate in length, breadth, and coverage can protect more of the body.

Pre-Islamic poetic witnesses likewise associate sābighah armor with length and amplitude.

The description does not specify exact dimensions, sleeve length, metal weight, or a particular construction method.

Sābighāt itself does not state these details.

Wa-qaddir fī al-sard: measure in construction

After commanding ample armor, the verse says:

وَقَدِّرْ فِي السَّرْدِ

wa-qaddir fī al-sard

Sabaʾ 34:11

And observe measure in linking.

This uses another root, Q D R.

Qaddir commands measuring or observing measure.

Al-sard here concerns linking the components of armor.

The verse thus addresses proportion in construction as well as ample coverage.

The expressions complement each other.

Sābighāt conveys full coverage.

Qaddir fī al-sard concerns measure and proportion in joining the components.

In practice, full coverage and sound construction are related qualities.

The meanings of qaddir and sard should not be incorporated into the lexical definition of S B GH.

Does sābighāt mean oversized armor?

Sābighāt emphasizes ample, full coverage, not necessarily disproportionate size.

The command wa-qaddir fī al-sard also makes proportion relevant.

The verse does not establish that longer, heavier, or wider armor is always better.

Extent of coverage must be considered alongside proportionate construction.

Connection with Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:80

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:80 states:

وَعَلَّمْنَاهُ صَنْعَةَ لَبُوسٍ لَكُمْ لِتُحْصِنَكُمْ مِنْ بَأْسِكُمْ فَهَلْ أَنْتُمْ شَاكِرُونَ

wa-ʿallamnāhu ṣanʿata labūsin lakum li-tuḥṣinakum min baʾsikum fa-hal antum shākirūn

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:80

Translation:

“And We taught him the making of protective clothing for you, to shield you against your violence in battle. Will you then be grateful?”

This verse too concerns David and the teaching of protective manufacture.

Labūs belongs to L B S and here denotes a covering used for protection in battle.

Sabaʾ 34:11 addresses ample armor and measure in construction.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:80 states its protective function.

The verses help identify the subject of armor-making while retaining their own vocabulary and emphases.

Sābighāt does not itself mean protection; protection is the armor’s function explained in the other verse.

Craft and righteous work

Sabaʾ 34:11 ends:

وَاعْمَلُوا صَالِحًا إِنِّي بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ بَصِيرٌ

wa-iʿmalū ṣāliḥan innī bi-mā taʿmalūna baṣīr

Sabaʾ 34:11

And do righteous work; indeed, I see what you do.

The opening iʿmal is singular.

The later iʿmalū is plural.

This grammatical distinction must be preserved.

The first command concerns making ample armor and addresses David in context.

The second concerns righteous work and uses a plural address.

The change alone does not identify every detail of the plural audience without further evidence.

Nevertheless, craft and righteous action are connected in the context.

Skill in making an instrument does not alone determine the moral worth of everything its maker does.

Workmanship, use, and human conduct are further questions requiring their own grounds of assessment.

This follows from the verse as a whole, not from a lexical meaning of righteousness or piety in sābighāt.

5. Wa-asbagha ʿalaykum niʿamahu: abundant blessings in Luqmān 31:20

Luqmān 31:20 states:

أَلَمْ تَرَوْا أَنَّ اللَّهَ سَخَّرَ لَكُمْ مَا فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَمَا فِي الْأَرْضِ وَأَسْبَغَ عَلَيْكُمْ نِعَمَهُ ظَاهِرَةً وَبَاطِنَةً وَمِنَ النَّاسِ مَنْ يُجَادِلُ فِي اللَّهِ بِغَيْرِ عِلْمٍ وَلَا هُدًى وَلَا كِتَابٍ مُنِيرٍ

a-lam taraw anna Allāha sakhkhara lakum mā fī al-samāwāti wa-mā fī al-arḍi wa-asbagha ʿalaykum niʿamahu ẓāhiratan wa-bāṭinatan wa-mina al-nāsi man yujādilu fī Allāhi bi-ghayri ʿilmin wa-lā hudan wa-lā kitābin munīr

Luqmān 31:20

Translation:

“Have you not seen that The God has subjected to your benefit whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on earth, and lavished upon you His outward and hidden blessings? Yet among people are those who dispute about The God without knowledge, guidance, or an illuminating book.”

Asbagha is a Form IV past verb.

Its subject is The God.

ʿAlaykum identifies the recipients of the blessings.

Niʿamahu is the verb’s object.

In the reading used here, it is the plural of niʿmah with the attached pronoun -hu.

Ẓāhiratan wa-bāṭinatan describes the blessings as outward and hidden.

The meaning of asbagha here

Asbagha is morphologically related to sabagha.

The basic verb concerns becoming full or extensive.

In Form IV, the subject makes something full or extensive.

In Luqmān 31:20, The God is the subject and blessings are the object.

Asbagha thus concerns making blessings abundant and extensive.

Translating it simply as “gave” can lose part of the meaning.

It also emphasizes the fullness and extent of provision.

Renderings such as “made His blessings upon you abundant” or “lavished His blessings upon you” can preserve this better.

Does asbagha mean covering?

In clothing and armor usage, the root concerns extensive coverage.

That image can help explain the extent of blessings.

But the object in Luqmān 31:20 is blessings, not clothing or armor.

Asbagha in this sentence need not denote physically covering a person.

Nor does ʿalā in ʿalaykum itself establish that physical image.

The natural meaning is making blessings abundant and full for the recipients.

Outward and hidden blessings

The verse uses two descriptions:

ظَاهِرَةً

ẓāhiratan

Luqmān 31:20

Outward; manifest.

بَاطِنَةً

bāṭinatan

Luqmān 31:20

Hidden; inward.

They belong to other roots.

Ẓāhir concerns what is manifest, and bāṭin what is hidden or inward.

Here divine blessings are not restricted to what people directly and visibly observe.

Some benefits may be clear and tangible.

Others may not be immediately apparent, or people may not know all their dimensions.

For example, one can see the food one eats without directly seeing every natural process and condition involved in producing and benefiting from it.

This illustrates the distinction, but does not restrict outward blessings to possessions and food or hidden blessings to spiritual matters.

The two descriptions alone do not establish such a fixed classification.

Does abundant blessing exclude suffering and shortage?

The verse speaks of the extent of divine blessings.

Asbagha alone does not say that someone enjoying blessings will never face illness, poverty, fear, loss, or difficulty.

The Qur’an speaks both of human blessings and of trials, shortages, and injuries people may experience.

Al-Baqarah 2:155, for example, mentions testing with fear, hunger, and losses of possessions, lives, and produce.

These circumstances do not themselves invalidate the meaning of abundant blessing.

Nor does Luqmān 31:20 mean everyone has equal wealth, health, ability, or resources.

It concerns abundant divine blessings, not numerical equality of every individual benefit.

Connection with the heavens and earth being subjected to human benefit

Luqmān 31:20 begins:

سَخَّرَ لَكُمْ مَا فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَمَا فِي الْأَرْضِ

sakhkhara lakum mā fī al-samāwāti wa-mā fī al-arḍ

Luqmān 31:20

He subjected to your benefit whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on earth.

It then speaks of abundant blessings.

The blessings are thus placed in a context broader than a particular possession or instrument.

Human life depends on an array of resources and conditions in the world.

Some are used directly.

Others contribute to the conditions of life.

Taskhīr belongs to S KH R, whereas isbāgh belongs to S B GH.

They should not be equated.

Taskhīr here concerns the relation of created beings and resources to human benefit.

Isbāgh conveys the extent and abundance of blessings.

Connection with disputing without knowledge

The verse ends:

وَمِنَ النَّاسِ مَنْ يُجَادِلُ فِي اللَّهِ بِغَيْرِ عِلْمٍ وَلَا هُدًى وَلَا كِتَابٍ مُنِيرٍ

wa-mina al-nāsi man yujādilu fī Allāhi bi-ghayri ʿilmin wa-lā hudan wa-lā kitābin munīr

Luqmān 31:20

Yet among people are those who dispute about The God without knowledge, guidance, or an illuminating book.

After recalling abundant blessings, it speaks of people disputing about The God without knowledge, guidance, or an illuminating book.

This contextual relationship matters.

People benefit from the world’s many resources yet may speak about their origin and reality without knowledge.

Asbagha does not itself mean disputation, ignorance, or ingratitude.

Those concepts arise from other clauses.

The specific meaning of asbagha in this verse

In Luqmān 31:20, asbagha means making blessings abundant and extensive.

It shares the field of completeness and extent with sābighāt, but has a different subject matter.

Armor involves material coverage.

Blessings involve extensive provision.

6. Connections and differences between the Qur’anic uses

The root occurs in two verses in different constructions.

In Sabaʾ 34:11:

أَنِ اعْمَلْ سَابِغَاتٍ

ani iʿmal sābighātin

Sabaʾ 34:11

“Make ample coats of mail.”

In Luqmān 31:20:

وَأَسْبَغَ عَلَيْكُمْ نِعَمَهُ

wa-asbagha ʿalaykum niʿamahu

Luqmān 31:20

“And He lavished His blessings upon you.”

Shared meaning

Both uses concern fullness and extent.

With armor, that extent concerns coverage.

With blessings, it concerns abundant, full provision.

This connection clarifies the family’s common semantic core.

Morphological difference

Sābighātin is the feminine plural of sābighah.

It describes a quality of something.

Asbagha is a Form IV past verb.

It denotes making something complete or extensive.

The forms should not be forced into the same translated construction.

Sabaʾ 34:11 concerns making armor characterized by ample coverage.

In Luqmān 31:20, The God is the agent extending blessings.

Did the meaning develop from armor to blessings?

Semantically, full coverage and abundant blessing have a clear relationship.

Both involve extent and fullness.

Comparing these verses alone does not prove that the material armor sense came first historically and generated the blessing sense.

Pre-Islamic poetic witnesses attest the antiquity of the armor usage.

The blessing usage is also recorded in Arabic.

Dating the emergence of these branches requires independent, datable evidence.

Their semantic relationship can be explained without presenting an unproven history of derivation as certain.

7. Distinctions from neighboring roots

S B GH and T M M

Tamām concerns completion and reaching the intended extent.

Subūgh too can convey completeness.

Lexical explanations gloss sabagha with tamma.

Its uses for clothing, armor, and blessings also have a clear setting of amplitude and extent.

T M M occurs in the Qur’an for completing various matters, including a duration, a blessing, or an undertaking.

Al-Naḥl 16:81 uses yutimmu niʿmatahu ʿalaykum.

Luqmān 31:20 uses asbagha ʿalaykum niʿamahu.

Both concern blessings.

Without reviewing all uses, one cannot claim that tamām only means ending and isbāgh only increasing.

Their distinction is contextual and a matter of tendency, not an absolute boundary.

S B GH and K M L

Kamāl concerns completeness in the relevant respect.

Subūgh likewise conveys fullness and extent in its established usage.

Al-Māʾidah 5:3 uses akmaltu concerning religion.

Luqmān 31:20 uses asbagha concerning blessings.

The difference of subject must be retained.

It does not establish a rule limiting kamāl to religion or isbāgh to material blessings.

Each family must be understood through its range of uses.

S B GH and W S ʿ

W S ʿ concerns breadth, capacity, and extent.

S B GH can overlap with this field.

Breadth matters in clothing and armor usage.

Blessing usage concerns ample provision.

The families thus overlap semantically.

W S ʿ also occurs in the Qur’an for ability, capacity, mercy, knowledge, and other subjects.

S B GH has only two Qur’anic forms; the entire semantic field of W S ʿ should not be transferred to it.

S B GH and W F Y

Wafāʾ occurs for rendering fully, giving in full, and honoring commitments.

S B GH likewise concerns fullness and amplitude.

Wafāʾ can concern fulfillment of a right, covenant, or specified amount.

Clear S B GH uses concern ample covering and extensive blessings.

The families may converge in completeness without being interchangeable everywhere.

Paying someone their full due and having ample armor describe different kinds of situation.

S B GH and Ṣ B GH

These roots are similar in sound and writing.

Their first letters differ.

S B GH begins with sīn.

Ṣ B GH begins with ṣād.

S B GH concerns completeness, amplitude, and extent.

Ṣ B GH in established usage concerns dyeing and immersion.

Ṣibghata Allāh in Al-Baqarah 2:138 belongs to Ṣ B GH.

Asbagha in Luqmān 31:20 belongs to S B GH.

Phonetic resemblance does not make them identical.

Dyeing may cover something with color, but a shared covering image does not prove historical kinship or identical meaning.

Asbagha in Luqmān 31:20 should not be translated as dyeing blessings or changing their color.

S B GH and L B S

In clothing usage, L B S concerns wearing something.

S B GH concerns the fullness and extent of coverage.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:80 uses labūs.

Sabaʾ 34:11 uses sābighāt.

They converge in the context of making protective clothing.

Labūs and sābighāt nevertheless differ in meaning.

Wearability belongs to the L B S family.

Full, ample coverage belongs to S B GH.

In Sabaʾ 34:11, that description applies to armor.

Proposed renderings of the Qur’anic forms

In Luqmān 31:20:

وَأَسْبَغَ عَلَيْكُمْ نِعَمَهُ ظَاهِرَةً وَبَاطِنَةً

wa-asbagha ʿalaykum niʿamahu ẓāhiratan wa-bāṭinatan

Luqmān 31:20

“And He lavished upon you His outward and hidden blessings.”

“He made His blessings upon you abundant and full” is also semantically defensible.

The final translation should choose a natural expression rather than accumulate synonymous equivalents.

In Sabaʾ 34:11:

أَنِ اعْمَلْ سَابِغَاتٍ

ani iʿmal sābighātin

Sabaʾ 34:11

“Make ample coats of mail.”

“Ample coats of mail” follows from the established nominal use of sābighāt and the context.

“Long, ample coats of mail” makes the physical characteristic more explicit.

“Large, heavy armor” is unsuitable because sābighāt does not entail weight.

“Impenetrable armor” likewise exceeds the word’s meaning.

Brief root meaning and verse-specific meaning

For the brief root box:

“Being complete, ample, and extensive; making something full and comprehensive.”

For Luqmān 31:20:

“Making divine outward and hidden blessings abundant and extensive for people.”

For Sabaʾ 34:11:

“Long, ample coats of mail providing full coverage, which David is commanded to make.”

Preserve the exact form wa-asbagha in the Luqmān 31:20 box.

Preserve the exact form sābighātin in the Sabaʾ 34:11 box.

Keep the brief general root meaning separate from the verse-specific meaning in both boxes. These proposals do not replace existing authored explanations.

Do not equate the general root meaning with armor or introduce armor into the verse about blessings.

9. Conclusion

The Arabic root S B GH concerns completeness, amplitude, and extent.

In poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period, sābighah denotes long, ample armor providing full coverage.

ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm describes armor whose length and breadth allow folds to appear above the belt.

ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād describes extensive, connected armor that he tears open.

These witnesses show the ancient use of sābigh for full, ample coverage.

Lexical records also attest full clothing, armor, extended rainfall, abundant blessings, and ample livelihood.

This variety does not prove that every derivative originated in a single historical image.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an.

Sābighāt in Sabaʾ 34:11 denotes ample coats of mail.

Full coverage is accompanied by an instruction to observe measure in construction.

Asbagha in Luqmān 31:20 concerns making divine blessings abundant and extensive.

The blessings are described as outward and hidden.

The subjects differ, but both uses belong to the field of fullness and extent.

Armor involves material dimensions and coverage.

Blessings involve abundant, extensive provision.

Completeness here does not entail absolute perfection in every respect.

Ample armor need not resist every weapon.

Extensive blessings do not alone exclude difficulties, needs, or differences in people’s provision.

S B GH ultimately denotes being full, ample, and extensive, or making something so, in coverage, dimensions, or provision.

For Forqan, “ample coats of mail” for sābighāt in Sabaʾ 34:11 and “lavished” for asbagha in Luqmān 31:20 are suitable equivalents.

Different translations retain the shared meaning while showing how one family functions in two settings: material coverage and blessings.
