Emergence and appearance: from heavenly stars and earthly plants to scheduled portions

1. Introduction and semantic core

The Arabic root N J M concerns something rising, emerging, or becoming visible. The verb najama means “it rose,” “appeared,” or “emerged.” It can describe a star appearing in the sky, a plant emerging from the earth, a tooth coming through, or a matter becoming apparent after concealment.

In established Arabic usage, najm denotes both a star and a kind of plant growing at ground level without an upright, woody stem. Although these uses initially seem different, both have an intelligible connection with emergence: the star becomes visible in the sky, and the plant emerges from the earth.

Another branch concerns specified portions and due dates. Najm can denote an instalment or portion of a payment, and tanjīm can mean dividing something into scheduled portions. Explanations connect this with setting dates by the rising and setting of stars, but that lexical connection alone does not establish every stage of the expression’s historical development.

“Emerging and appearing” is therefore an important lexical axis of this family, but cannot replace the established meaning of each noun or derivative. Celestial najm names a heavenly body, rather than anything that appears. Botanical najm names a category of vegetation, rather than everything that emerges from the earth.

Qur’anic forms

N J M occurs thirteen times in thirteen Qur’anic verses. Every occurrence is a nominal form: najm or its plural nujūm.

The Qur’anic forms can be summarized as follows:

  • Al-najm: a star, or, in one disputed occurrence, stemless vegetation; Al-Naḥl 16:16, Al-Najm 53:1, Al-Raḥmān 55:6, and Al-Ṭāriq 86:3.
  • Al-nujūm: stars; Al-Anʿām 6:97, Al-Aʿrāf 7:54, Al-Naḥl 16:12, Al-Ḥajj 22:18, Al-Ṣāffāt 37:88, Al-Ṭūr 52:49, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:75, Al-Mursalāt 77:8, and Al-Takwīr 81:2.

Differences involving an attached conjunction or preposition, or a change in case ending, do not create independent derivatives.

The Qur’an uses no direct verb from this root, such as najama, yanjumu, najjama, or tanajjama. Nor does it explicitly use nujūm for financial instalments or tanjīm for division into portions.

This distinction is essential: knowing the family’s broader Arabic uses can assist understanding, but purely lexical or technical uses should not be presented as forms actually occurring in the Qur’an.

2. Early Arabic usage and semantic branches

Najm: rising and appearing

Arabic najama al-shayʾu describes something appearing or becoming visible.

For example:

نَجَمَ النَّبَاتُ means “the plant grew and emerged from the earth.”

نَجَمَ السِّنُّ means “the tooth came through.”

نَجَمَ الْقَرْنُ means “the horn appeared or emerged.”

نَجَمَ الْكَوْكَبُ means “the star or heavenly body appeared.”

These examples show that the Arabic verb is not restricted to stars.

An important core of the root can be expressed as طُلُوعٍ وَظُهُورٍ: rising and appearing. These concepts connect the appearance of a star with a plant’s emergence.

Appearance, however, does not mean creation from nonexistence. A star may have existed before becoming visible to an observer. Likewise, a tooth’s emergence does not mean its entire substance suddenly came into existence.

Here the root concerns becoming visible; how things originate or come into existence requires separate examination.

Najm: a star in the sky

One of the most familiar meanings of najm is “star.”

Najm can denote a single star, and nujūm is its plural. Stars had an important place in Arab life, especially in night travel, navigation, and recognizing seasons.

In some early Arabic usage, al-najm also specifically designated al-Thurayyā, the stellar group known as the Pleiades, or Parvīn in Persian.

This specific use matters because the singular noun did not invariably denote one individual star; it could name a recognized group.

It does not follow that every Qur’anic al-najm with the definite article necessarily means the Pleiades. The word can denote a class, referring to a star or stars generally. Its referent depends on the verse’s construction and context.

In daily life, the rising and setting of stars were observable signs. Recognizing them could help travellers establish direction or people identify approximate times of year.

Using stars to establish direction and time must nevertheless be distinguished from claiming to know human destinies through them. These involve different kinds of claims and different evidential support.

Najm: vegetation growing at ground level

In early Arabic, najm also denoted plants emerging from the earth without an upright, woody stem like a tree’s.

This use is commonly explained in contrast with shajar. Shajar denotes vegetation with an upright stem or trunk, while najm in this classification includes plants growing close to the ground or spreading along it.

This is an ordinary lexical classification, rather than a precise definition in modern botany. “Stemless” does not mean the plant scientifically has no stem; it lacks the upright trunk or substantial stem characteristic of a tree.

Najmah is also recorded for a particular wild plant spreading along the ground, known in Arabic as ثَيِّل.

Botanical najm can therefore be a general designation for low-growing vegetation or appear in the naming of a particular plant.

A witness attributed to pre-Islamic poetry

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā describes a pool:

مُكَلَّلٌ بِأُصُولِ النَّجْمِ تَنْسُجُهُ
رِيحٌ خَرِيقٌ لِضَاحِي مَائِهِ حُبُكُ

mukallalun bi-uṣūli al-najmi tansujuhu / rīḥun kharīqun li-ḍāḥī māʾihi ḥubuku

Translation:

“A pool encircled by the roots and clumps of najm, with a fierce wind weaving rippled patterns across its exposed surface.”

Al-najm here does not denote heavenly stars. The poet describes vegetation growing beside the pool.

The phrase uṣūl al-najm supports this reading: it concerns roots and the lower parts of plants.

The verse is a witness from poetry attributed to a pre-Islamic poet for the botanical use of najm. Attribution to a poet is different from independently establishing the exact date of composition.

The plant meaning therefore cannot simply be dismissed as something created by interpreting a Qur’anic verse; it also has support in the Arabic poetic and linguistic tradition.

Nujūm: scheduled portions and instalments

Beyond stars and vegetation, Arabic najm is recorded for a portion or instalment of payment.

Najjama al-māla means dividing money or a debt into specified due dates, with payment made in stages.

This use also appears in poetry by Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā:

يُنَجِّمُهَا قَوْمٌ لِقَوْمٍ غَرَامَةً
وَلَمْ يُهَرِيقُوا بَيْنَهُمْ مِلْءَ مِحْجَمِ

yunajjimuhā qawmun li-qawmin gharāmatan / wa-lam yuharīqū baynahum milʾa miḥjami

Translation:

“One people pays the compensation to another in appointed instalments, although they themselves have not shed even a cupping vessel’s measure of blood between them.”

The verse concerns compensation and blood money paid to end hostility between groups.

Yunajjimuhā here concerns paying compensation in stages, rather than studying stars or plants growing.

The witness shows the division of payment into specified portions in poetry attributed to Zuhayr. Its exact date should not be inferred merely from its preservation in lexical transmission.

A historical explanation connects this usage with Arabs setting financial dates by the rising and setting of stars or lunar stations, leading the payment portions to be called nujūm.

The connection with timekeeping is intelligible, but the verse alone cannot establish the entire development of the expression. More securely, the instalment and payment-date uses are recorded in early poetry.

The same usage is also found in the later term tanjīm for dividing a subject into successive portions.

Discussion of Qur’anic revelation likewise uses “revelation in portions,” meaning revelation in separate sections rather than all at once.

This terminology should nevertheless not be imposed without examination on every Qur’anic najm or nujūm.

Other Arabic branches

Manjam is recorded for a place of emergence or a clear path; minjam, with initial i, for the crosspiece of a balance holding its tongue. Related forms also describe bony projections of the foot. These vocalizations and senses should not be indiscriminately treated as one form.

In some constructions, anjama means that something such as rain or fever ceased or abated.

For example, anjama al-maṭaru can mean that rainfall ceased.

This initially differs from emergence. There is no need to invent a story making the end of rain another kind of rising or appearing merely to harmonize all branches.

The Arabic family extends beyond stars and plants. Qur’anic study should nevertheless emphasize the branches that help explain the forms actually used in the verses.

3. Stars in the Qur’an: navigation, order, and subjection

A. Al-Anʿām 6:97: stars as aids to navigation

وَهُوَ الَّذِي جَعَلَ لَكُمُ النُّجُومَ لِتَهْتَدُوا بِهَا فِي ظُلُمَاتِ الْبَرِّ وَالْبَحْرِ ۗ قَدْ فَصَّلْنَا الْآيَاتِ لِقَوْمٍ يَعْلَمُونَ

wa-huwa alladhī jaʿala lakumu al-nujūma li-tahtadū bihā fī ẓulumāti al-barri wa-al-baḥri qad faṣṣalnā al-āyāti li-qawmin yaʿlamūna

Translation:

“And He is the one who appointed the stars for you so that you may find your way by them in the darknesses of land and sea. We have indeed explained the signs for people who know.”

Al-Anʿām 6:97

Al-nujūma here clearly denotes the stars in the sky.

Li-tahtadū bihā explains their role: knowing the stars’ positions can help people find their way.

This clearly relates to life among people travelling through deserts and seas. At night, when earthly landmarks are less visible, stars can help establish direction.

Navigation, however, is not the lexical meaning of najm. This role comes from li-tahtadū and the preposition bi- in bihā.

The verse also does not say that the stars independently exercise will and guide people; people can use them as signs.

Guidance here concerns finding a route through the darknesses of land and sea. Reflection on recognizing signs is possible, but the verse’s direct meaning should not be restricted to spiritual or doctrinal guidance.

B. Al-Naḥl 16:16: navigation by al-najm

وَعَلَامَاتٍ ۚ وَبِالنَّجْمِ هُمْ يَهْتَدُونَ

wa-ʿalāmātin wa-bi-al-najmi hum yahtadūna

Translation:

“And landmarks; and by the star they find their way.”

Al-Naḥl 16:16

Unlike Al-Anʿām 6:97, this verse uses singular al-najmi.

The singular need not designate a particular star. An Arabic definite singular can also denote a class.

Bi-al-najmi can therefore concern finding one’s way by a star or stars.

Al-Naḥl 16:15 mentions mountains, rivers, and paths; Al-Naḥl 16:16 then mentions landmarks and the star.

The sequence places navigation by stars alongside other signs in nature.

The singular alone does not justify restricting the verse to the Pole Star or another particular star; that identification requires independent evidence.

Al-Anʿām 6:97 and Al-Naḥl 16:16 are the clearest Qur’anic uses concerning people finding routes by stars.

C. Al-Aʿrāf 7:54 and Al-Naḥl 16:12: stars in the created order

Al-Aʿrāf says:

وَالشَّمْسَ وَالْقَمَرَ وَالنُّجُومَ مُسَخَّرَاتٍ بِأَمْرِهِ

wa-al-shamsa wa-al-qamara wa-al-nujūma musakhkharātin bi-amrihi

Translation:

“And the sun, moon, and stars, subjected to His command.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:54, excerpt

Al-Naḥl also says:

وَالنُّجُومُ مُسَخَّرَاتٌ بِأَمْرِهِ

wa-al-nujūmu musakhkharātun bi-amrihi

Translation:

“And the stars are subjected to His command.”

Al-Naḥl 16:12, excerpt

Both passages concern heavenly stars.

The distinction from Al-Anʿām 6:97 and Al-Naḥl 16:16 concerns the sentence’s subject matter. Those passages foreground people navigating by stars; Al-Aʿrāf 7:54 and Al-Naḥl 16:12 concern stars subjected to His command alongside other created phenomena.

Musakhkharāt describes the sun, moon, and stars in Al-Aʿrāf 7:54, and the stars in the quoted phrase from Al-Naḥl 16:12. Both express subjection within the created order.

Subjection should not be included in the independent meaning of najm. A star names a heavenly phenomenon; being subjected is what the sentence says about it.

Neither passage supplies specific details about every star’s orbit, natural forces, or physical structure. The statement concerns their relation to divine order and command; its scientific explanation is a separate subject.

D. Al-Ḥajj 22:18: the prostration of stars and other creatures

أَلَمْ تَرَ أَنَّ اللَّهَ يَسْجُدُ لَهُ مَنْ فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَمَنْ فِي الْأَرْضِ وَالشَّمْسُ وَالْقَمَرُ وَالنُّجُومُ وَالْجِبَالُ وَالشَّجَرُ وَالدَّوَابُّ وَكَثِيرٌ مِنَ النَّاسِ

a-lam tara anna allāha yasjudu lahu man fī al-samāwāti wa-man fī al-arḍi wa-al-shamsu wa-al-qamaru wa-al-nujūmu wa-al-jibālu wa-al-shajaru wa-al-dawābbu wa-kathīrun mina al-nāsi

Translation:

“Have you not seen that to The God prostrate whoever is in the heavens and whoever is on earth, the sun, moon, stars, mountains, trees, moving creatures, and many people?”

Al-Ḥajj 22:18, excerpt

Stars appear alongside other creatures, with prostration attributed to them.

Nujūm itself does not mean “those prostrating.” Prostration is an independent action attributed to a collection of beings.

Since stars, mountains, and trees are included, the manner of their prostration should not simply be assumed to match human bodily movement.

The creatures’ subjection before God can be one aspect of understanding this prostration. Its manner and semantic scope require separate study of S J D and other Qur’anic uses.

For N J M, the main point is that these stars are heavenly bodies; prostration is what the sentence attributes to them.

4. Stars in Qur’anic events, times, and imagery

A. Al-Ṣāffāt 37:88: Abraham looking at the stars

فَنَظَرَ نَظْرَةً فِي النُّجُومِ

fa-naẓara naẓratan fī al-nujūmi

Translation:

“Then he cast a glance at the stars.”

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:88

The phrase occurs in Abraham’s story.

The following verse says:

فَقَالَ إِنِّي سَقِيمٌ

fa-qāla innī saqīmun

“Then he said: I am ill.”

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:89

The Qur’an then describes his people turning away and leaving, followed by Abraham’s action against their objects of worship.

Several interpretations of his glance have been proposed: actually looking at the sky, reflecting on a matter, or using a behaviour familiar to his people in confronting them.

The Qur’anic phrase alone does not say that Abraham predicted his future illness through the stars.

Nor does it establish Qur’anic approval of astrology as a means of predicting human destinies.

Looking at stars, scientifically recognizing their positions, and claiming to extract unseen information from them should be distinguished.

In a direct reading, nujūm denotes stars; an idiomatic reading of “reflecting on a matter” has also been proposed for the phrase. Abraham’s precise intention is contextual and interpretive, rather than a fixed meaning to be inserted into the root.

B. Al-Ṭūr 52:49: the retreat of stars and the night’s end

وَمِنَ اللَّيْلِ فَسَبِّحْهُ وَإِدْبَارَ النُّجُومِ

wa-mina al-layli fa-sabbiḥhu wa-idbāra al-nujūmi

Translation:

“And glorify Him during part of the night, and at the retreat of the stars.”

Al-Ṭūr 52:49

Nujūm denotes stars, but the main concern is a time for glorification.

Idbār comes from D B R and concerns turning away, retreating, or a stage drawing to its end.

In idbār al-nujūm, the contextual sense concerns stars retreating or disappearing at the end of the night.

This can indicate the approach of dawn, when increasing sky brightness hides many stars from the observer.

Disappearance from human sight should not be equated with the stars’ actual destruction.

Idbār al-nujūm is not itself the name of a particular prayer whose meaning follows directly from N J M. Any connection with prayer times requires independent examination of the relevant glorification and prayer verses.

C. Al-Najm 53:1: an oath by the star as it descends

وَالنَّجْمِ إِذَا هَوَىٰ

wa-al-najmi idhā hawā

Translation:

“By the star when it descends.”

Al-Najm 53:1

This is an important and disputed occurrence of N J M.

Al-najmi can denote a star generally. Since Arabic also used al-najm for the Pleiades, the reading of a star or a recognized stellar group has lexical support.

Hawā comes from H W Y and concerns descending, falling, or moving downward.

Applied to stars, it can indicate their descent or setting from an observer’s view.

The verse does not name a particular star. The phrase alone therefore cannot establish the Pleiades or another specific heavenly body as its definitive referent.

Does najm here refer to portions of Qur’anic revelation?

Another traditional interpretation takes najm as a portion of the Qur’an descending at a stage of revelation.

This relates to Arabic tanjīm for dividing a subject into successive portions.

The following verses discuss the Messenger, his freedom from error, and revelation. This context has led some readers to connect najm with the descent of revelation.

Linguistic possibility should nevertheless be distinguished from preference between meanings.

Arabic uses najm for an instalment or scheduled portion, but this alone does not prove that the opening al-najmi necessarily denotes a portion of the Qur’an.

The star meaning, by comparison, is directly established in the Qur’an and early Arabic, and naturally fits idhā hawā.

The star as it descends is therefore the preferred basic translation here. A connection with revelation in stages can be examined separately as an interpretation.

Neither reading should be declared certain solely from the surah’s name or an individual interpretive report.

D. Al-Wāqiʿah 56:75: stellar positions or stages of revelation?

فَلَا أُقْسِمُ بِمَوَاقِعِ النُّجُومِ

fa-lā uqsimu bi-mawāqiʿi al-nujūmi

Translation:

“Then I swear by the positions of the stars.”

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:75

Mawāqiʿ is the plural of mawqiʿ, from W Q ʿ. It can denote a place of occurrence, a position, or a place where something descends.

In the direct celestial reading, al-nujūm refers to stars.

Mawāqiʿ al-nujūm can then encompass their positions or places of rising, setting, and appearance.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:76 says:

وَإِنَّهُ لَقَسَمٌ لَوْ تَعْلَمُونَ عَظِيمٌ

wa-innahu la-qasamun law taʿlamūna ʿaẓīmun

“And it is indeed a tremendous oath, if you knew.”

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:76

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:77 then speaks of قُرْآنٌ كَرِيمٌ, “a noble Qur’an.”

This sequence has supported another reading: mawāqiʿ al-nujūm refers to the places or stages in which portions of the Qur’an descended.

Two considerations support examining that possibility: Arabic najm can denote specified portions and instalments, and the following verses concern the Qur’an and its revelation.

Neither consideration alone proves that al-nujūm here necessarily means portions of the Qur’an.

An oath by a natural phenomenon can affirm a different truth without the oath’s object and its following subject belonging to the same category.

The contextual connection with the Qur’an should nevertheless not be ignored.

The two readings can thus be distinguished: positions of heavenly stars, and occasions or stages in the descent of Qur’anic portions.

“Positions of the stars” is the more direct basic rendering here, requiring less additional explanation. Revelation in stages remains an interpretation to examine. “Then I swear” also follows a reading of lā uqsimu as an oath formula; analysis of lā is a separate grammatical and contextual issue, independent of nujūm’s meaning.

Al-Furqān 25:32 also indicates that the Qur’an was not revealed all at once. It does not, however, use N J M and cannot alone determine the meaning of mawāqiʿ al-nujūm.

E. Al-Mursalāt 77:8 and Al-Takwīr 81:2: transformed stars

Al-Mursalāt says:

فَإِذَا النُّجُومُ طُمِسَتْ

fa-idhā al-nujūmu ṭumisat

“Then when the stars are effaced.”

Al-Mursalāt 77:8

Al-Takwīr also says:

وَإِذَا النُّجُومُ انْكَدَرَتْ

wa-idhā al-nujūmu inkadarat

“And when the stars become darkened and altered.”

Al-Takwīr 81:2

Both concern stars, but use different accompanying verbs.

Ṭumisat, from Ṭ M S, concerns effacement or the removal of something’s visible traces.

Inkadarat, from K D R, concerns change, darkening, or disruption of the stars’ condition here. Scattering or falling has also been proposed in explaining the verb.

The two verbs should therefore not be rendered identically without examination.

Extinguishing, darkening, scattering, or falling is also not the independent meaning of nujūm; these events come from the accompanying verbs and the passages’ overall imagery.

The stars’ transformation forms part of the description of tremendous events at the end of the present world order.

The physical mechanism is not stated. These verses alone cannot establish a specific theory about stellar death, astronomical explosions, or the universe’s ultimate transformation.

F. Al-Ṭāriq 86:3: the bright and piercing star

النَّجْمُ الثَّاقِبُ

al-najmu al-thāqibu

Translation:

“The bright and piercing star.”

Al-Ṭāriq 86:3

The surah opens:

وَالسَّمَاءِ وَالطَّارِقِ

wa-al-samāʾi wa-al-ṭāriqi

“By the sky and that which comes by night.”

Al-Ṭāriq 86:1

It then asks:

وَمَا أَدْرَاكَ مَا الطَّارِقُ

wa-mā adrāka mā al-ṭāriqu

“And what has made you know what al-ṭāriq is?”

Al-Ṭāriq 86:2

The third verse answers: النَّجْمُ الثَّاقِبُ.

Najm denotes a star, with thāqib describing it.

Th Q B concerns piercing, penetrating, or passing through. Applied to a star, thāqib can evoke penetrating light and evident brightness.

This quality should not be incorporated into the independent meaning of najm.

Al-najm al-thāqib should also not be restricted without decisive evidence to a specific category in modern astronomy, such as a neutron star or pulsar.

The verse describes a star as thāqib without specifying a scientific designation, physical structure, or measured properties.

Study of Th Q B

Al-Ṭāriq 86:4 speaks of a guardian or watcher over every soul. Its relation to the opening oath is contextual, rather than something deduced from N J M itself.

5. Al-najm wa-al-shajar yasjudān: earthly plants or heavenly stars?

Al-Raḥmān 55:6 is among the most important verses for understanding this root’s semantic range:

وَالنَّجْمُ وَالشَّجَرُ يَسْجُدَانِ

wa-al-najmu wa-al-shajaru yasjudāni

Translation following the plant reading:

“And stemless plants and trees prostrate.”

Al-Raḥmān 55:6

Two established linguistic and interpretive readings exist.

First reading: najm as stemless vegetation

On this reading, al-najm denotes low-growing plants without an upright trunk, while al-shajar denotes trees or vegetation with an upright stem or trunk.

The verse then places two groups of earthly vegetation together.

This reading has clear lexical support: botanical najm is recorded in early Arabic.

The juxtaposition of al-najm and al-shajar is also significant. Both then concern plants, distinguished by structure and manner of growth.

The verse can thus be understood as all vegetation, whether spreading along the ground or growing with trunks, prostrating before God.

Second reading: najm as a star

The alternative takes najm as a heavenly star.

This is lexically possible too: the celestial meaning is well established.

An important contextual consideration is the preceding verse:

الشَّمْسُ وَالْقَمَرُ بِحُسْبَانٍ

al-shamsu wa-al-qamaru bi-ḥusbānin

“The sun and moon follow a reckoning.”

Al-Raḥmān 55:5

After the sun and moon, mentioning a star can naturally continue the discussion of heavenly phenomena.

Verse 6 then concerns a star and a tree, joining phenomena from heaven and earth.

Al-Ḥajj 22:18 also explicitly attributes prostration to stars and trees together. Attributing prostration to stars therefore has clear Qur’anic precedent.

Which reading is preferred?

Both meanings are established in Arabic, and each fits part of the Qur’anic context.

The plant reading has stronger support within this phrase because al-najm is directly joined to al-shajar, with an established contrast between stemless vegetation and trees.

The star reading remains defensible because the previous verse mentions the sun and moon, and Al-Ḥajj 22:18 explicitly mentions stars prostrating.

Preferring the plant reading therefore does not make the celestial reading baseless.

For Forqan’s basic translation, “stemless plants and trees” can be chosen, with “star and tree” discussed in the verse’s explanation.

This distinguishes the preferred meaning from another possible one without artificially merging two different meanings into one translation.

What does the prostration of najm and shajar mean?

Both readings attribute prostration to beings that do not perform human bodily prayer and prostration in the same manner.

The plant reading concerns vegetation and trees; the celestial reading concerns a star and a tree.

In either case, prostration must be examined within its own Qur’anic semantic range.

Verses on the subjection and obedience of creatures support understanding such prostration in relation to divine command and order.

This should not be restricted to a particular physical movement, such as plants bending in wind or their shadows changing direction, without independent evidence for that identification.

For N J M, the main point is that prostration is not the meaning of najm; it comes from the verse’s independent verb.

Study of S J D

6. Related roots and translation questions

N J M (نَجَمَ) and Ṭ L ʿ (طَلَعَ): rising and appearing

Arabic Ṭ L ʿ concerns rising, emerging, and becoming visible.

In uses such as sunrise or appearance over a horizon, it approaches N J M.

Beyond the verb of appearance, however, najm is also an established name for a star and certain plants.

Not every najm can therefore be replaced by “rising.”

Al-nujūm in Al-Anʿām 6:97 means stars, rather than the action of their rising.

On the plant reading of Al-Raḥmān 55:6, najm names a group of plants, rather than the action of growing.

A shared emergence sense helps explain the family without replacing the noun’s established meaning.

Study of Ṭ L ʿ

N J M (نَجَمَ) and K W K B (كَوْكَب): stars and heavenly bodies

Kawkab denotes a visible heavenly body in Arabic and overlaps semantically with najm.

The Qur’an says:

فَلَمَّا جَنَّ عَلَيْهِ اللَّيْلُ رَأَىٰ كَوْكَبًا

fa-lammā janna ʿalayhi al-laylu raʾā kawkaban

“Then when night covered him, he saw a heavenly body.”

Al-Anʿām 6:76, excerpt

This verse uses kawkab, while passages concerning navigation and heavenly order often use nujūm.

This difference does not establish that Qur’anic najm necessarily means a star in today’s astrophysical definition and kawkab necessarily means a planet orbiting a star.

Modern distinctions between stars and planets use characteristics different from the observational classifications of earlier language.

Translation should therefore follow linguistic usage and context, rather than projecting modern scientific distinctions into Qur’anic words without evidence.

Study of K W K B

N J M (نَجَمَ) and Sh J R (شَجَرَ): low-growing plants and trees

This comparison matters for Al-Raḥmān 55:6.

One Arabic use of najm denotes low-growing or spreading vegetation, while shajar denotes plants with an upright stem or trunk.

The distinction concerns plants’ outward structure.

It should not become an absolute botanical boundary: ordinary early language did not necessarily classify vegetation by modern scientific criteria.

The plant use also does not cancel the celestial use. A word can have multiple established uses, with context identifying the appropriate one.

Study of Sh J R

N J M (نَجَمَ) and Q S Ṭ (قِسْط): scheduled portions and payments

Arabic najm can denote a due date or a specified portion of payment.

It therefore relates to qisṭ as a share or portion of payment. This noun’s vocalization should be distinguished from qasṭ meaning injustice.

The financial use is nevertheless one branch of najm; the entire family cannot be reduced to instalments.

Zuhayr’s poetry uses tanjīm for paying compensation in specified portions. This is historically and lexically significant, but no such verbal form occurs in the Qur’an.

Qisṭ also has other Qur’anic uses, especially justice and fairness. Justice and a financial instalment should not simply be equated without historical and linguistic examination.

N J M (نَجَمَ) and N B T (نَبَتَ): plant emergence and growth

N B T concerns growing and the emergence of plants.

The plant meaning of najm also includes emergence from the earth.

Nabt concerns growth itself or vegetation, whereas najm in the use under discussion also names a particular category of plants.

Not every plant that grows is therefore necessarily called najm in the Arabic classification.

The general meaning of a verb does not exhaust the meaning of a related noun.

Study of N B T

Translation proposals for Forqan

Translation of N J M should distinguish three principal branches: stars, low-growing vegetation, and scheduled portions.

For the lexical verb najama, suitable equivalents include “rose,” “emerged,” and “became visible.”

Celestial najm is naturally “star,” and nujūm “stars.”

Botanical najm can be “stemless vegetation” or “low-growing plants spreading along the ground.”

In a payment context, tanjīm is “payment by instalments” or “division into specified due dates.” Applied to revelation in stages, “revelation in portions” can be used.

Translation should not predetermine every najm as a star; equally, the plant usage should not remove the celestial meaning from other verses.

Nor should nujūm in passages about stars be turned into portions of Qur’anic revelation without justification.

Choose the basic meaning from the word and its context, keeping any defensible interpretive alternative in a separate explanation.

7. Conclusion

Arabic N J M concerns rising, emerging, and becoming visible, as in a plant emerging from the earth, a tooth coming through, or a star appearing.

The family is nevertheless broader than one general verb.

Celestial najm names a star and sometimes the recognized group called the Pleiades.

Botanical najm denotes vegetation growing close to the ground without an upright trunk like a tree’s.

Another branch concerns specified portions and due dates, including financial instalments. The verse attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā provides an early poetic witness.

The Qur’an contains thirteen occurrences, all nominal.

Most clearly concern heavenly stars, presented as navigation aids in darkness, part of the created order, and beings subjected to command. Their transformation is also described in scenes of the present world order’s end.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:88 describes Abraham looking at the stars, but does not alone endorse predicting destinies through stars.

Beyond the direct celestial readings, Al-Najm 53:1 and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:75 have also been interpreted in relation to revelation in stages. Arabic tanjīm and the revelatory context support examining this possibility without categorically replacing “star.”

In Al-Raḥmān 55:6, stemless vegetation has strong contextual support through its pairing with shajar. The star reading nevertheless remains defensible through the previous verse’s sun and moon and other Qur’anic passages attributing prostration to stars.

The family thus requires distinguishing the verb’s general core, the noun’s established meaning, and each verse’s referent.

Proposed final definition for Forqan

An important axis of N J M is rising, emerging, and appearing. Arabic najm denotes a heavenly star and low-growing vegetation without an upright trunk. Another branch uses najm for an appointed portion or due date and tanjīm for dividing work or payment into scheduled portions.

Qur’anic najm and nujūm usually mean a star and stars, while stemless vegetation is a strong and preferable reading in Al-Raḥmān 55:6. In some oath and revelation passages, revelation in portions is an interpretive possibility rather than a certain lexical meaning.

Emergence and appearance form an important connection within this family, but Qur’anic translation must select the particular word’s sense—such as star, stars, or stemless vegetation—according to its usage and immediate context.
