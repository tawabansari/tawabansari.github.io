In its principal Quranic use, S L Ḥ concerns weapons and the tools of armed confrontation. Silāḥ is a general name for implements used to fight, defend oneself, or counter a threat. Its plural asliḥah means weapons or armaments.

In early Arabic, swords, spears, bows, and other military implements could fall under silāḥ. The word was not restricted to one particular instrument.

Lexicons sometimes distinguish weapons from protective equipment such as shields. Some define silāḥ as what one fights with and junnah as what wards off blows. General usage can nevertheless encompass the equipment of armed confrontation.

The root occurs four times in the Quran, all in Al-Nisāʾ 4:102, as the plural asliḥah with an attached pronoun.

The verse concerns believers facing the risk of enemy attack while establishing ṣalāt. It directs them to keep their weapons, remain aware of the enemy, and take precautions against a sudden assault.

The same verse permits putting weapons down when rain or illness makes carrying them difficult, while emphasizing continued caution.

This sequence matters: a weapon is a means of meeting danger, but weapons are not identical with awareness, caution, or security. Someone may be armed yet surprised, or need to put a weapon aside while still safeguarding life.

1. The root in early Arabic

Silāḥ: Implements of warfare and defense

Arabic lexicons define al-silāḥ as a general name for the implements of war.

One lexical definition states:

«السِّلَاحُ اسْمٌ جَامِعٌ لِآلَةِ الْحَرْبِ»

al-silāḥu ismun jāmiʿun li-ālati al-ḥarb

“Silāḥ is a collective designation for the implements of war.”

The definition shows that the word is not inherently limited to one weapon type.

Swords, spears, and bows were familiar early examples: people could use them to defend themselves or attack an opponent in battle.

A weapon's use is not determined solely by its shape. A sword may repel an attack or be used for aggression.

Silāḥ names an implement, not the ethical purpose of its use.

Some early explanations restrict it to iron implements, but that restriction does not fit every recorded use.

A staff can also serve for defense and is called silāḥ in some Arabic witnesses.

An animal's horn can likewise be figuratively called its weapon because it uses it for defense.

Iron is therefore not an essential condition of the word's general meaning.

What groups these implements together is their role in confrontation, fighting, or warding off danger.

A witness from Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd's Muʿallaqah

The celebrated Muʿallaqah of the pre-Islamic poet Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd says:

إِذَا ابْتَدَرَ الْقَوْمُ السِّلَاحَ وَجَدْتَنِي
مَنِيعًا إِذَا بَلَّتْ بِقَائِمِهِ يَدِي

idhā ibtadara al-qawmu al-silāḥa wajadtanī / manīʿan idhā ballat bi-qāʾimihi yadī

“When the people rush for weapons, you find me impregnable, once my hand grasps the sword's hilt.”

Al-silāḥ refers to implements of war.

Ibtadara al-qawmu indicates people hastening toward weapons.

The poet says that in such a situation he can defend himself and stand firm by grasping his sword's hilt.

Silāḥ here names a real, familiar implement.

The reference to a hilt also identifies a sword as a particular weapon in this verse.

That instance does not establish that silāḥ always meant only a sword throughout Arabic.

Ṭarafah is a recognized pre-Islamic poet, and this verse appears in the Muʿallaqah attributed to him and its early commentaries.

It offers strong support for pre-Quranic usage, while the precise recording and transmission of each verse remain distinct from the poem's general attribution.

The witness establishes the meaning of a martial implement independently of the Quranic occurrence.

Silāḥ and junnah: Weapons and protective equipment

One early lexical distinction between silāḥ and junnah is expressed as follows:

«السِّلَاحُ مَا قُوتِلَ بِهِ، وَالْجُنَّةُ مَا اتُّقِيَ بِهِ»

al-silāḥu mā qūtila bihi wa-al-junnatu mā ittuqiya bihi

“Silāḥ is what one fights with; junnah is what one protects oneself with.”

This definition separates two categories by their function.

Swords and spears are ordinarily used to strike, whereas a shield prevents a blow from reaching the body.

The distinction should not become an absolute boundary.

In actual confrontation, a weapon can be used defensively, and protective equipment can form part of military gear.

General language may also refer to a warrior's combined equipment as arms or implements of war.

The distinction helps identify different functions, but does not prove that silāḥ can never include defensive equipment in any period or Arabic construction.

General and specific uses of silāḥ

Arabic sources sometimes use silāḥ specifically for a sword.

This is understandable because the sword was among the best-known weapons of the time.

Using a general name for a prominent instance does not abolish its wider meaning.

Someone may speak of “his weapon” in a story where context identifies a sword.

Elsewhere, the same word may indicate a bow or several implements.

Its sense must therefore be understood through the person, action, and circumstances.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:102 specifies no weapon type; translation should not restrict it to swords, spears, or another particular implement.

Tasalluḥ: Becoming armed

The family includes tasallaḥa: taking up weapons or equipping oneself with them.

Rajulun sāliḥ describes a man carrying weapons.

Mutasalliḥ likewise identifies someone wearing or carrying arms.

Early maslaḥah can denote a group of armed guards or their guard post at a frontier or observation point.

The family thus names not only equipment but also an armed person's state, an armed group, and a guard station.

None of these derivatives occurs in that exact form in the Quran.

A different branch: Sulāḥ as excrement

Another Arabic form with the same consonants must be distinguished from silāḥ, weapons.

Al-sulāḥ, with ḍamma on the sīn, and al-salḥ in certain uses, denote excrement discharged by humans or animals.

Salaḥa al-ḥayawānu is also recorded for an animal defecating.

This differs semantically from swords, spears, and implements of war.

Early sources place both under S L Ḥ, but shared consonants alone do not prove their historical or derivational connection.

Words with identical consonants may have independent uses and different histories.

A story linking these meanings through the origin of the word for weapon should therefore not be invented without reliable historical evidence.

The Quranic use is unambiguous: asliḥah is the plural of silāḥ, weapons, and the excrement sense is irrelevant to its meaning there.

2. Quranic forms and occurrences

S L Ḥ occurs four times, all in Al-Nisāʾ 4:102.

All four are uses of the plural asliḥah.

The forms are:

Asliḥatahum — first occurrence

“Their weapons.”

It appears in wa-l-yaʾkhudhū asliḥatahum.

Wa-asliḥatahum — second occurrence

“And their weapons.”

It appears in wa-l-yaʾkhudhū ḥidhrahum wa-asliḥatahum.

Asliḥatikum — third occurrence

“Your weapons.”

It follows the preposition ʿan and is consequently genitive.

Asliḥatakum — fourth occurrence

“Your weapons.”

It is the object of taḍaʿū: what they are permitted to put down.

Asliḥah is a broken plural on the pattern afʿilah; its singular is silāḥ.

Silāḥ can be grammatically masculine or feminine, though masculine usage is described as more prevalent.

Other plurals, including suluḥ and sulḥān, are recorded in lexicons but do not occur in the Quran.

The Quran therefore has four occurrences of one broken plural, not four independent root meanings.

They differ chiefly in attached pronoun, case, and position within the sentence.

3. Wa-l-yaʾkhudhū asliḥatahum: Weapons under threat

The complete text of Al-Nisāʾ 4:102 is:

وَإِذَا كُنْتَ فِيهِمْ فَأَقَمْتَ لَهُمُ الصَّلَاةَ فَلْتَقُمْ طَائِفَةٌ مِنْهُمْ مَعَكَ وَلْيَأْخُذُوا أَسْلِحَتَهُمْ فَإِذَا سَجَدُوا فَلْيَكُونُوا مِنْ وَرَائِكُمْ وَلْتَأْتِ طَائِفَةٌ أُخْرَىٰ لَمْ يُصَلُّوا فَلْيُصَلُّوا مَعَكَ وَلْيَأْخُذُوا حِذْرَهُمْ وَأَسْلِحَتَهُمْ وَدَّ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا لَوْ تَغْفُلُونَ عَنْ أَسْلِحَتِكُمْ وَأَمْتِعَتِكُمْ فَيَمِيلُونَ عَلَيْكُمْ مَيْلَةً وَاحِدَةً وَلَا جُنَاحَ عَلَيْكُمْ إِنْ كَانَ بِكُمْ أَذًى مِنْ مَطَرٍ أَوْ كُنْتُمْ مَرْضَىٰ أَنْ تَضَعُوا أَسْلِحَتَكُمْ وَخُذُوا حِذْرَكُمْ إِنَّ اللَّهَ أَعَدَّ لِلْكَافِرِينَ عَذَابًا مُهِينًا

wa-idhā kunta fīhim fa-aqamta lahumu al-ṣalāta fa-l-taqum ṭāʾifatun minhum maʿaka wa-l-yaʾkhudhū asliḥatahum fa-idhā sajadū fa-l-yakūnū min warāʾikum wa-l-taʾti ṭāʾifatun ukhrā lam yuṣallū fa-l-yuṣallū maʿaka wa-l-yaʾkhudhū ḥidhrahum wa-asliḥatahum wadda alladhīna kafarū law taghfulūna ʿan asliḥatikum wa-amtiʿatikum fa-yamīlūna ʿalaykum maylatan wāḥidatan wa-lā junāḥa ʿalaykum in kāna bikum adhan min maṭarin aw kuntum marḍā an taḍaʿū asliḥatakum wa-khudhū ḥidhrakum inna Allāha aʿadda li-al-kāfirīna ʿadhāban muhīnā

Al-Nisāʾ 4:102

“When you are among them and establish ṣalāt for them, let a group stand with you and take their weapons. When they have prostrated, let them be behind you; let another group who have not performed ṣalāt come and perform it with you, taking their precautions and weapons. Those who deny wish you would neglect your weapons and belongings so they could attack you all at once. There is no blame on you, if rain causes you hardship or you are ill, for putting down your weapons; but maintain your precautions. Indeed, The God has prepared a humiliating punishment for the deniers.”

The verse follows Al-Nisāʾ 4:101, which discusses travel and fear of hostile action.

This is therefore not an ordinary peaceful gathering.

A group under threat must coordinate ṣalāt with vigilance against possible attack.

Taking weapons as preparedness for defense

The first occurrence reads:

«وَلْيَأْخُذُوا أَسْلِحَتَهُمْ».

wa-l-yaʾkhudhū asliḥatahum

Al-Nisāʾ 4:102

Let them take or carry their weapons.

Akhdh belongs to ʾ KH DH and means taking.

Here taking weapons is more than picking an object up from the ground.

Context indicates carrying them for readiness against danger.

A person might hold a weapon or carry it in a way that makes it available when needed.

The verse does not prescribe a precise carrying method or position on the body.

It therefore supplies no specific technical instruction for carrying a sword, bow, or other weapon.

What is clear is the group's need to remain prepared for the threat.

Weapons during ṣalāt

The verse directs a group to stand with the Prophet for ṣalāt and take their weapons.

It then mentions prostration and directs another group, who have not yet performed ṣalāt, to come and perform it with him.

Ṣalāt here involves collective action and components including standing and prostration.

The verse addresses not only worship's details but also its performance under danger.

Worship and defensive readiness are not opposed in the text.

The Quran does not say that establishing ṣalāt makes external danger irrelevant.

Nor does it say that danger requires abandoning ṣalāt entirely.

Group organization and awareness of the situation accompany worship.

This connection may be explored further in studying ṣalāt, but a root study should keep the lexical meaning of weapons distinct from the details of worship.

The pronoun in wa-l-yaʾkhudhū

The first part reads:

«فَلْتَقُمْ طَائِفَةٌ مِنْهُمْ مَعَكَ وَلْيَأْخُذُوا أَسْلِحَتَهُمْ».

fa-l-taqum ṭāʾifatun minhum maʿaka wa-l-yaʾkhudhū asliḥatahum

Al-Nisāʾ 4:102

The nearest grammatical sequence connects taking weapons with the group standing with the Prophet.

Grammatical and exegetical accounts also consider a reference to those guarding against the enemy within the arrangement of the groups.

This difference in assigning duties does not change asliḥah's meaning.

Both readings concern actual weapons and readiness against danger.

Disagreement over the precise division of duties should not become disagreement over silāḥ's lexical meaning.

4. Four uses of weapons in one verse

The four occurrences in Al-Nisāʾ 4:102 each belong to a different part of a connected instruction.

First: Carrying weapons

«وَلْيَأْخُذُوا أَسْلِحَتَهُمْ»

wa-l-yaʾkhudhū asliḥatahum

Al-Nisāʾ 4:102

This part emphasizes readiness under danger.

Weapons are means of meeting a possible attack.

Taking weapons is not itself a command to attack.

Readiness for defense differs from initiating war.

The main setting is safeguarding the group while establishing ṣalāt.

Second: Weapons alongside caution

«وَلْيَأْخُذُوا حِذْرَهُمْ وَأَسْلِحَتَهُمْ»

wa-l-yaʾkhudhū ḥidhrahum wa-asliḥatahum

Al-Nisāʾ 4:102

Two things appear together:

Ḥidhrahum and asliḥatahum.

Ḥidhr concerns vigilance and avoiding surprise.

Weapons are material means of meeting danger.

Their conjunction provides an occasion to examine the distinction, but conjunction alone does not always prove that two words differ in meaning.

Here their meanings and the later instruction to retain caution after putting down weapons establish the distinction.

A person may be armed but inattentive to the environment and enemy behavior.

Another may recognize danger correctly but lack sufficient equipment to meet it.

The Quran therefore does not reduce preparedness to possessing weapons.

Awareness of danger and possession of the means to meet it are distinct parts of preparedness.

Third: The danger of neglecting weapons and belongings

«وَدَّ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا لَوْ تَغْفُلُونَ عَنْ أَسْلِحَتِكُمْ وَأَمْتِعَتِكُمْ فَيَمِيلُونَ عَلَيْكُمْ مَيْلَةً وَاحِدَةً»

wadda alladhīna kafarū law taghfulūna ʿan asliḥatikum wa-amtiʿatikum fa-yamīlūna ʿalaykum maylatan wāḥidah

Al-Nisāʾ 4:102

“Those who deny wish you would neglect your weapons and belongings so they could attack you all at once.”

Asliḥatikum here follows ʿan.

Taghfulūna ʿan means becoming inattentive to something.

The issue is not merely putting weapons down, but neglecting equipment and resources important to the group's safety under threat.

The verse also places asliḥatikum alongside amtiʿatikum.

Amtiʿah concerns accompanying belongings, supplies, and possessions.

It may include things needed for travel and daily life that are not necessarily weapons.

Arms and belongings are thus separate designations here.

The enemy wishes to exploit neglect of both.

Readiness therefore extends beyond safeguarding weapons to the group's necessary resources and supplies.

Fourth: Permission to put weapons down

«وَلَا جُنَاحَ عَلَيْكُمْ إِنْ كَانَ بِكُمْ أَذًى مِنْ مَطَرٍ أَوْ كُنْتُمْ مَرْضَىٰ أَنْ تَضَعُوا أَسْلِحَتَكُمْ»

wa-lā junāḥa ʿalaykum in kāna bikum adhan min maṭarin aw kuntum marḍā an taḍaʿū asliḥatakum

Al-Nisāʾ 4:102

“There is no blame on you, if rain causes you hardship or you are ill, for putting down your weapons.”

This part is particularly important.

After directing people to take weapons, the Quran identifies conditions permitting them to put them down.

Two are explicit: hardship from rain and illness.

Both may make carrying weapons difficult or harmful.

The verse gives no technical specification of the illness, rainfall intensity, or weapon weight.

The basic ruling is clear: in these circumstances, carrying weapons is not required without regard to actual hardship.

The command serves safety; carrying weapons is not an independent purpose of worship.

Wa-khudhū ḥidhrakum: Caution remains

Immediately after permission to put weapons down comes:

«وَخُذُوا حِذْرَكُمْ».

wa-khudhū ḥidhrakum

Al-Nisāʾ 4:102

“Maintain your precautions.”

This clarifies the relationship between weapons and caution.

Illness or rain may make carrying weapons difficult, while vigilance against danger remains necessary.

Putting weapons aside does not mean setting vigilance aside.

If weapons and caution were identical, retaining caution after putting down weapons would be difficult to understand.

The verse distinguishes equipment from precaution.

This is among the principal linguistic and conceptual points of Al-Nisāʾ 4:102.

5. Weapons, defense, and the limits of the verse's meaning

A weapon is a means, not independent permission for war

Mentioning weapons does not alone authorize their use in every circumstance.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:102 discusses them under threat of enemy attack.

Their use here concerns readiness and avoiding surprise.

Al-Baqarah 2:190 also says:

وَقَاتِلُوا فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ الَّذِينَ يُقَاتِلُونَكُمْ وَلَا تَعْتَدُوا إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يُحِبُّ الْمُعْتَدِينَ

wa-qātilū fī sabīli Allāhi alladhīna yuqātilūnakum wa-lā taʿtadū inna Allāha lā yuḥibbu al-muʿtadīn

Al-Baqarah 2:190

“Fight in the way of The God those who fight you, and do not transgress. The God does not love transgressors.”

This distinguishes fighting attackers from transgression.

Possessing military equipment or preparing to meet a threat therefore gives no unlimited permission to harm others.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:90 also limits hostile action against those who withdraw from fighting and offer peace.

Verses concerning weapons should be read alongside the broader passages on war, peace, security, and justice.

Defensive readiness, initiating aggression, and continuing war after a threat ends are distinct issues within that reading.

Actual circumstances and relief from difficulty

Al-Nisāʾ 4:102 considers real human difficulties as well as possible attack.

Rain and illness are two clear examples.

An ill person may be unable to bear the weight or use certain equipment.

Difficult environmental conditions may also complicate movement and maintenance of belongings.

The Quran permits putting weapons down in these circumstances.

This concession accompanies the command to retain caution.

The verse takes preparedness seriously while keeping it connected to actual human capacity.

This is also consistent with “The God does not burden a soul beyond its capacity” in Al-Baqarah 2:286.

Rain and illness should nevertheless not become an unlimited list of new rulings without examining circumstances and evidence.

The direct meaning is permission to put weapons down in the stated situations while maintaining caution.

Carrying weapons does not determine moral worth

A weapon may be held by someone defending themselves and others.

The same implement may be used by another for threats and oppression.

Its name stays the same while the conduct's ethical value differs.

Silāḥ's lexical meaning therefore cannot determine its holder's intention, legitimacy, or responsibility.

An armed person is not necessarily courageous, just, or aggressive.

Such judgments require examination of conduct, purpose, circumstances, and others' rights.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:102 places weapons within enemy threat and protection of the group.

That setting clarifies their use without turning the implement's name into a moral quality.

6. Comparison with related roots

S L Ḥ and Ṣ L Ḥ

These families look similar in writing but have different roots.

S L Ḥ begins with sīn and concerns weapons in its Quranic use.

Ṣ L Ḥ begins with ṣād and concerns soundness, fitness, reform, and reconciliation.

Asliḥah belongs to S L Ḥ.

Ṣulḥ, iṣlāḥ, ṣāliḥ, and muṣliḥ belong to Ṣ L Ḥ.

The resemblance between silāḥ and ṣalāḥ does not mean that peace means disarmament or that weapons were inherently named for producing reform.

Legitimate defensive equipment may help maintain security and peace, but that social relationship does not establish a shared root.

S L Ḥ and Ḥ DH R: Equipment and caution

Ḥadhar concerns care, vigilance, and avoidance of danger.

Silāḥ names material means of confrontation.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:102 combines them and repeats the command to retain caution after permitting weapons to be put down.

This clearly illustrates their distinction.

Weapons without vigilance may be insufficient, and vigilance does not always replace necessary equipment.

They can complement each other without being synonymous.

S L Ḥ and J N N: Weapons and protective covering

Junnah belongs to J N N; defensively, it describes what covers a person and protects them from harm.

A shield is a clear example.

Al-Munāfiqūn 63:2 describes people making their oaths a junnah.

No literal metal shield is meant: oaths serve to cover or protect their conduct.

Junnah's range concerns covering and protection and can extend figuratively.

Silāḥ in Al-Nisāʾ 4:102 names military implements.

Both may concern defense, while their roots and primary meanings differ.

S L Ḥ and ʿ D D: Weapons and prepared equipment

ʿUddah concerns what is prepared for a task.

Al-Tawbah 9:46 discusses preparing equipment for departure.

ʿUddah can encompass the supplies, resources, and preparation needed for an undertaking.

Silāḥ names a particular category of implements.

Weapons may form part of military preparations, but not every preparation is a weapon.

Food, water, travel gear, and other resources may also be necessary.

ʿUddah should therefore not always be translated as weapons.

S L Ḥ and M T ʿ: Weapons and accompanying belongings

Al-Nisāʾ 4:102 places asliḥatikum and amtiʿatikum together.

Matāʿ has a broader range encompassing belongings, property, and resources people use.

Weapons are a particular class of implements for armed confrontation.

The separate terms distinguish weapons from the group's other belongings.

Omitting either or merging both into a general “your equipment” can obscure this distinction.

S L Ḥ and Q W Y: Instruments of power and ability itself

Quwwah concerns strength and ability.

Al-Anfāl 8:60 commands preparing strength against the enemy.

Weapons may provide defensive capability, but strength is not restricted to them.

A person's or group's ability may also depend on knowledge, coordination, resources, skill, and other conditions.

Silāḥ and quwwah are therefore not synonyms.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:102 practically illustrates the distinction by combining weapons with continued caution.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

The natural Persian equivalent of silāḥ is the established word “weapon.”

“Armament” is also suitable and can clarify its historical sense.

Quranic translation need not limit it to a sword, spear, or other particular implement.

The four forms in Al-Nisāʾ 4:102 are rendered as follows:

Asliḥatahum

“Their weapons.”

In wa-l-yaʾkhudhū asliḥatahum:

“Let them take their weapons.”

Wa-asliḥatahum

“And their weapons.”

In wa-l-yaʾkhudhū ḥidhrahum wa-asliḥatahum:

“Let them take their precautions and weapons.”

Asliḥatikum

“Your weapons.”

In taghfulūna ʿan asliḥatikum wa-amtiʿatikum:

“Neglect your weapons and belongings.”

Asliḥatakum

“Your weapons.”

In an taḍaʿū asliḥatakum:

“To put down your weapons.”

“Put down” preserves the natural image of taḍaʿū.

It does not mean destroying, permanently discarding, or transferring ownership of them.

The meaning is laying them aside under specified circumstances.

Suitable equivalents for other lexical forms follow; the Quranic plural asliḥah is also included for comparison:

  • Silāḥ: A weapon or armament.
  • Asliḥah: Weapons or armaments.
  • Tasallaḥa: Equipped himself with weapons.
  • Mutasalliḥ: Armed or equipped with weapons.
  • Sāliḥ: Possessing weapons.
  • Sallaḥa shakhsan: Armed a person.
  • Taslīḥ: Supplying weapons or equipping with arms.
  • Maslaḥah: Armed guards or their station, according to usage.
  • Al-sulāḥ: Excrement, in a different lexical branch.
  • Salaḥa al-ḥayawānu: The animal defecated.

Only the weapons branch is relevant to the Quranic translation.

Modern uses such as “propaganda weapon,” “economic weapon,” and “political weapon” should not be imported without contextual support.

Such expressions usually use weapon figuratively for pressure, confrontation, or influence.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:102 concerns actual portable weapons under danger, mentioning taking, carrying, and putting them down.

Conclusion

Quranic S L Ḥ concerns weapons and implements of war.

Early Arabic silāḥ was a general designation, not limited to one implement. Ṭarafah's poetic witness also supports its pre-Islamic use for actual weapons.

Lexicons sometimes distinguish it from protective junnah, but this does not mean weapons are never used defensively.

A differently pronounced branch, including al-sulāḥ, concerns excrement. Shared consonants do not alone establish one historical origin for both senses.

The root occurs four times, all in Al-Nisāʾ 4:102.

The verse concerns ṣalāt under danger and directs the groups to carry weapons and remain vigilant.

The four occurrences address carrying weapons, combining them with caution, the danger of neglecting weapons and belongings, and permission to put weapons down during illness or hardship from rain.

The key distinction is between defensive equipment and caution.

Weapons help meet danger, but awareness and care remain necessary. Even when actual hardship permits putting weapons aside, the command to retain caution remains.

The final definition is therefore:

In its weapons branch, S L Ḥ denotes implements used for armed confrontation, fighting, or defense against threats. Silāḥ is their general name and asliḥah its plural. In Al-Nisāʾ 4:102, weapons serve readiness against possible enemy attack, remaining distinct from caution, strength, general equipment, and peace.

For Forqan, “weapon” and “weapons” are the most suitable equivalents.

The verse also distinguishes preparedness from negligence, lack of planning, or abandonment of worship. People can establish ṣalāt under threat while safeguarding themselves and others.

Actual human conditions, including illness and environmental hardship, are considered within the instruction.

Weapons are thus neither sacred objects independent of their purpose nor possessions that automatically authorize aggression. Their role is understood within circumstances, threat, and human responsibility.

This distinction preserves the root's meaning and allows the verse's message to be understood without imposing additional senses.
