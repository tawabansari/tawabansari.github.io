Abundant flow and outpouring: milk, rain, and pearl-like brilliance

1. Introducing the root and its semantic field

Arabic D R R has several semantic branches, chiefly abundant flow and outpouring, movement and current, and the name of the pearl.

Early Arabic darra al-labanu describes milk flowing abundantly from an animal’s udder. Saḥāb midrār describes a cloud pouring plentiful rain, while samāʾ midrār describes a sky bringing abundant rainfall.

The family is not limited to milk and rain. Dictionaries also record rapid animal movement, rotation, wind currents, lively trade, and pearls.

Durr names a pearl, while durriyy describing a star concerns a pearl’s brilliance and clarity.

These images can be compared without assuming one original physical meaning historically produced every use. Milk flowing and pearls shining differ substantially; shared root form or some resemblance does not establish a historical derivation.

The root occurs four times in the Qur’an: three instances of midrāran and one of durriyyun.

All three midrār passages concern abundant rain. Durriyy in Al-Nūr 24:35 instead describes a brilliant star within a Qur’anic parable.

The distinction matters: rainfall should not transfer into durriyy, nor should pearls make midrār mean brilliance.

2. Qur’anic forms and early Arabic meaning

D R R occurs four times in four verses, with two lexical forms.

Midrāran: on the mifʿāl pattern, abundantly pouring or bringing plentiful rain. It occurs three times:

  • Al-Anʿām 6:6
  • Hūd 11:52
  • Nūḥ 71:11

Durriyyun: a relational adjective associated with durr, pearl, describing a bright, brilliant star in Al-Nūr 24:35.

Its reading and derivation vary across Qur’anic recitations and receive separate treatment below.

Early Arabic also records these family members:

دَرَّ يَدِرُّ or يَدُرُّ: to flow abundantly, particularly milk, rain, or tears.

دَرّ: milk, its outpouring, or its flow, depending on construction.

دُرُور: flowing and abundant outpouring.

دِرَّة: abundance and flow of milk; elsewhere rainfall.

دُرَّار and دَرُور: durrār is a plural describing milk-giving camels; darūr describes abundant milk production or outpouring. Their morphological structures differ.

مِدْرَار: abundantly pouring or bringing plentiful rain.

أَدَرَّ: to cause flowing or greater outpouring.

اسْتَدَرَّ: to seek flowing or extraction, for example when milking.

دُرّ: pearl.

دُرَّة: one pearl, sometimes specifically a large, outstanding pearl.

دُرِّيّ: associated with pearl; brilliant and clear like a pearl.

دِرَّة: in another use, a whip or striking implement.

دَرِير: in certain early uses, swift or rapidly moving.

دُرْدُر: the place where teeth grow, recorded with two ḍammah vowels. Dardara and dardarah are reduplicated four-consonant constructions concerning chewing or movement and water sounds. They should not be equated with the noun durdur or with the Qur’anic forms.

These are not all Qur’anic; only midrār and durriyy occur in the text.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: three midrār and one durriyy

Two semantic principles in Maqāyīs al-Lughah

Ibn Fāris identifies two principles:

أَحَدُهُمَا تَوَلُّدُ شَيْءٍ عَنْ شَيْءٍ، وَالثَّانِي اضْطِرَابٌ فِي شَيْءٍ

aḥaduhumā tawalludu shayʾin ʿan shayʾin wa-al-thānī iḍṭirābun fī shayʾin

“One is something coming forth from something else; the second is movement within something.”

Milk flowing and rain falling illustrate the first.

Rapid animal movement, rotating objects, and other motion-related uses illustrate the second.

Even this early analysis does not reduce the entire family to one simple image.

Flowing liquid cannot adequately define every use, such as fast-moving animals.

Conversely, the very broad movement of something obscures the specific abundance and outpouring of darra al-laban and midrār.

Distinguishing the branches and cautiously explaining their possible connections is preferable.

Maqāyīs and early dictionaries: flow, movement, and pearl

3. Milk and rain: tangible darr

Milk flowing

Animal milk is among the family’s fundamental Arabic uses.

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

دَرَّ اللَّبَنُ وَالدَّمْعُ وَنَحْوُهُمَا

darra al-labanu wa-al-damʿu wa-naḥwuhumā

“Milk, tears, and similar things flowed.”

When milk flows abundantly from an animal’s udder, one says:

«دَرَّتِ النَّاقَةُ بِلَبَنِهَا»

darrati al-nāqatu bi-labanihā

That is, the camel’s milk flowed abundantly.

Nāqah darūr similarly describes a camel producing abundant milk.

This directly connects with pastoral life, where milk supplied food and livelihood and its abundance could mean prosperity.

Darra itself means neither economic value nor moral goodness; that setting connects it with a benefit.

Milk’s presence versus its flow

Dictionaries distinguish milk accumulating in the udder from flowing during milking.

Darr therefore does more than name the substance. In verbal usage, abundance or beginning to flow matters.

Darra al-ḍarʿu, for example, describes an udder yielding milk.

This clarifies midrār: not merely containing water, but also plentiful outpouring.

From milk to rain

Arabic also uses the verb for sky and cloud:

دَرَّتِ السَّمَاءُ بِالْمَطَرِ

darrati al-samāʾu bi-al-maṭari

“The sky rained abundantly.”

And:

سَحَابٌ مِدْرَارٌ

saḥābun midrārun

“A cloud pouring abundant rain.”

Milk and rain share a clear resemblance: liquids flowing or falling from a source.

That does not make the sky physically a milk-producing animal in Arabic thought.

A familiar milk-flow verb can describe abundant rain without transferring every animal and milking feature.

Persian translation should therefore not invent the awkward image of a milk-giving sky.

Rain-laden or bringing abundant rain is natural.

Abundance and continuity of rainfall

Dirrah concerning rain can convey falling or pouring.

Some explanations also concern successive rains.

This matters for midrār.

Rain may be abundant through intensity or repeated falls over time.

Midrār concerns abundant rainfall; context can also bring out continuity and repetition.

It does not necessarily require rain without even a moment’s interruption.

Natural language can describe continuous, abundant rains despite gaps between individual falls.

Lisān al-ʿArab: milk flow, rain, and successive rainfall

4. Early Arabic witnesses

Abundant rain in a poem attributed to Al-Nimr ibn Tawlab

A poem attributed to Al-Nimr ibn Tawlab says:

سَلَامُ الْإِلَهِ وَرَيْحَانُهُ
وَرَحْمَتُهُ وَسَمَاءٌ دِرَرْ
غَمَامٌ يُنَزِّلُ رِزْقَ الْعِبَادِ
فَأَحْيَا الْبِلَادَ وَطَابَ الشَّجَرْ

salāmu al-ilāhi wa-rayḥānuhu / wa-raḥmatuhu wa-samāʾun dirar / ghamāmun yunazzilu rizqa al-ʿibādi / fa-aḥyā al-bilāda wa-ṭāba al-shajar

“The deity’s peace, bounty, and mercy, and a rain-laden sky; a cloud sending down the servants’ sustenance, bringing land to life and trees to flourishing.”

Samāʾ dirar describes a sky with abundant outpouring.

The next line describes cloud and descending sustenance, followed by revived land and thriving trees.

This illuminates Qur’anic midrār by connecting rain with flourishing land, growth, and human resources.

Revived land and growing trees nevertheless come from the whole poetic image rather than dirar alone.

Al-Nimr was a mukhaḍram poet who lived before and during early Islam. These lines cannot be conclusively dated pre-Islamic without dating their composition.

More securely, they document abundant-rain dirar in a linguistic tradition close to the revelation’s era.

Flowing water in a poem attributed to Al-Ḥādirah

A poem attributed to Al-Ḥādirah, Quṭbah ibn Aws al-Ghaṭafānī, says:

فَكَأَنَّ فَاهَا بَعْدَ أَوَّلِ رَقْدَةٍ
ثَغْبٌ بِرَابِيَةٍ لَذِيذُ الْمَكْرَعِ
بِغَرِيضِ سَارِيَةٍ أَدَرَّتْهُ الصَّبَا
مِنْ مَاءٍ أَسْحَرَ طَيِّبِ الْمُسْتَنْقَعِ

fa-ka-anna fāhā baʿda awwali raqdatin / thaghbun bi-rābiyatin ladhīdhu al-makraʿi / bi-gharīḍi sāriyatin adarrathu al-ṣabā / min māʾin asḥara ṭayyibi al-mustanqaʿi

“Her mouth after the first sleep is like a cool pool on high ground, pleasant to drink from, filled with fresh night-rain water poured by the east wind into a pleasant basin.”

Adarrathu describes rainwater flowing or descending.

Adarra could therefore describe water’s outpouring.

Freshness and pleasantness are conveyed through rainwater and a pool.

Adarra itself does not mean the water is cool or tastes pleasant; other words supply those features.

Al-Ḥādirah is counted among early Arab poets and dictionaries record the witness, but reliable poetic transmission remains necessary for definitive dating and wording.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Al-Nimr and Al-Ḥādirah witnesses

Pearls in a poem attributed to Al-Rabīʿ ibn Ḍabʿ

A poem attributed to Al-Rabīʿ ibn Ḍabʿ al-Fazārī says:

أَقْفَرَ مِنْ مَيَّةَ الْجَرِيبُ إِلَى الزَّجْـ
جَيْنِ إِلَّا الظِّبَاءَ وَالْبَقَرَا
كَأَنَّهَا دُرَّةٌ مُنَعَّمَةٌ
مِنْ نِسْوَةٍ كُنَّ قَبْلَهَا دُرَرَا

aqfara min mayyata al-jarību ilā al-zajjayni / illā al-ẓibāʾa wa-al-baqara / ka-annahā durratun munaʿʿamatun / min niswatin kunna qablahā durara

“From Al-Jarīb to Al-Zajjayn the land is empty of Mayyah, with only gazelles and wild cattle remaining; she was like a cherished pearl among women who before her were pearls.”

Durrah and durar compare a woman’s beauty to pearls.

Beauty, clarity, and visible value make the pearl the vehicle of comparison.

Unlike milk and rain, this concerns a familiar beautiful object rather than liquid outpouring.

Al-Rabīʿ was an early, long-lived poet reported to have experienced pre-Islamic times, but this passage’s exact date is uncertain.

The witness establishes pearl as a use of durr without proving derivation from milk flowing or liquid movement.

Al-Murtaḍā’s Amālī: Al-Rabīʿ ibn Ḍabʿ witness

5. Pearls, brilliance, and another branch

Durr and durrah

Arabic durr names pearl.

Durrah names an individual pearl and sometimes particularly a large, outstanding one.

Clarity, brilliance, brightness, and beauty make pearls frequent Arabic comparisons.

Kawkab durriyy describes a bright, brilliant star through those qualities.

The pearl is a tangible object here, not a meaning directly yielded by flowing milk.

Does pearl derive from milk flowing?

Ibn Fāris identifies two main principles.

He connects pearl with the second, movement, portraying its clarity through wave-like motion or light moving within it.

This can imaginatively link the pearl’s brilliance with moving light in the observer’s perception.

It does not conclusively establish the pearl name’s historical origin.

Nor must durr derive from darra al-laban: similar sound and inclusion within one morphological family do not date their meanings’ emergence.

Qur’anic study needs the established Arabic pearl sense and the recognized relational reading of unhamzated durriyy.

6. Midrāran in three Qur’anic verses

A. Al-Anʿām 6:6: abundant rain and earlier peoples

Al-Anʿām 6:6

أَلَمْ يَرَوْا كَمْ أَهْلَكْنَا مِنْ قَبْلِهِمْ مِنْ قَرْنٍ مَكَّنَّاهُمْ فِي الْأَرْضِ مَا لَمْ نُمَكِّنْ لَكُمْ وَأَرْسَلْنَا السَّمَاءَ عَلَيْهِمْ مِدْرَارًا وَجَعَلْنَا الْأَنْهَارَ تَجْرِي مِنْ تَحْتِهِمْ فَأَهْلَكْنَاهُمْ بِذُنُوبِهِمْ وَأَنْشَأْنَا مِنْ بَعْدِهِمْ قَرْنًا آخَرِينَ

a-lam yaraw kam ahlaknā min qablihim min qarnin makkannāhum fī al-arḍi mā lam numakkin lakum wa-arsalnā al-samāʾa ʿalayhim midrāran wa-jaʿalnā al-anhāra tajrī min taḥtihim fa-ahlaknāhum bi-dhunūbihim wa-anshaʾnā min baʿdihim qarnan ākharīna

“Have they not seen how many generations We destroyed before them, established on earth as We have not established you? We sent them repeated rain and made rivers flow beneath them; We destroyed them for their sins and brought forth another generation after them.”

Midrāran accompanies wa-arsalnā al-samāʾa ʿalayhim.

Besides heaven or sky, samāʾ can in some constructions name rain descending from it.

The sentence does not describe the entire sky falling bodily onto people.

It concerns sending abundant rain from the sky.

Midrāran conveys abundant outpouring; the next clause concerns flowing rivers.

These are connected but distinct statements.

Abundant rain may form or replenish rivers; midrār itself does not mean river.

Abundant rain does not guarantee lasting approval

The verse notably describes people possessing extensive resources.

They were established on earth, received abundant rain, and had rivers flowing through their land.

It then describes their destruction for sins.

Natural and economic advantage does not itself establish a society’s ultimate moral merit.

A society may possess water, land, agriculture, and extensive means while practicing injustice and corruption.

Midrār marks abundant benefits here, not a certificate of the recipients’ moral purity.

The inverse claim—that every prosperous, rain-rich society must face destruction—also does not follow.

The verse describes particular peoples and independently names their sins as the cause through bi-dhunūbihim.

B. Hūd 11:52: abundant rain in the invitation to return

Hūd 11:52

وَيَا قَوْمِ اسْتَغْفِرُوا رَبَّكُمْ ثُمَّ تُوبُوا إِلَيْهِ يُرْسِلِ السَّمَاءَ عَلَيْكُمْ مِدْرَارًا وَيَزِدْكُمْ قُوَّةً إِلَىٰ قُوَّتِكُمْ وَلَا تَتَوَلَّوْا مُجْرِمِينَ

wa-yā qawmi istaghfirū rabbakum thumma tūbū ilayhi yursili al-samāʾa ʿalaykum midrāran wa-yazidkum quwwatan ilā quwwatikum wa-lā tatawallaw mujrimīna

“My people, seek your Lord’s forgiveness, then turn back to Him; He will send you abundant rain and add strength to your strength. Do not turn away as offenders.”

Hūd invites his people to seek forgiveness and repent.

Abundant rain and added strength follow.

Midrāran retains the same plentiful-rain meaning.

Its construction and subject differ from Al-Anʿām 6:6.

Al-Anʿām recounts earlier peoples’ material advantages.

Hūd addresses a particular people, connecting rain with their return to their Lord.

Context, rather than a changed meaning of midrār, supplies the distinction.

Seeking forgiveness, repentance, and rain

Seeking forgiveness and repentance are independent expressions.

Istighfār concerns asking forgiveness; tawbah concerns returning.

Sending rain and adding strength follow.

Hūd connects returning to the Lord with greater benefit and strength.

This does not make every rainfall a reward for religious observance or every drought necessarily punishment for people’s sins.

D R R itself does not establish that generalization.

Rainfall also depends on natural, geographic, and climatic conditions; a contemporary drought’s causes require examining those conditions.

The verse gives a promise within Hūd’s address to his people. A broader principle would require other passages on blessings, testing, divine patterns, and human responsibility.

What conveys added strength?

The verse continues with wa-yazidkum quwwatan ilā quwwatikum.

Z Y D and quwwah convey increasing strength, rather than midrār itself.

Rain can improve livelihood, agriculture, and social capacity without containing all increased-strength meaning lexically.

The root’s contribution remains abundant rainfall; independent expressions convey the other benefits.

C. Nūḥ 71:11: abundant rain alongside wealth, children, and gardens

Nūḥ 71:11

يُرْسِلِ السَّمَاءَ عَلَيْكُمْ مِدْرَارًا

yursili al-samāʾa ʿalaykum midrāran

“He will send you abundant rain.”

This follows Noah’s invitation to seek forgiveness.

Nūḥ 71:10 mentions seeking the Lord’s forgiveness.

Rain follows, while Nūḥ 71:12 names wealth, children, gardens, and rivers.

Rain is therefore one element within several benefits of life.

Where farming and livestock depend on water, plentiful rain can support vegetation, crops, and livelihood.

Midrār itself does not mean wealth, children, gardens, or rivers.

Those are independent things named in the next verse.

Does midrār mean torrential rain?

All three passages convey plentiful rainfall.

The word alone does not establish destruction, flooding, or injury.

Persian torrential rain usually evokes great intensity and possible flooding.

Midrār can instead describe plentiful, beneficial rain.

Hūd 11:52 and Nūḥ 71:11 particularly frame it as benefit and relief.

Abundant rain or rain-laden therefore suits translation better than torrential rain.

Does midrār mean uninterrupted rainfall?

Mifʿāl can emphasize abundance or intensity.

The pattern alone does not establish uninterrupted rain, day and night, for an unlimited duration.

Successive rainfall also appears within some lexical uses of darr.

Repeatedly is therefore defensible in Al-Anʿām 6:6, provided it does not imply absolutely uninterrupted continuity.

Persian renderings emphasizing abundant rainfall in Hūd 11:52 and Nūḥ 71:11 naturally convey the same field.

7. The syntax of midrāran

Midrāran is accusative in all three verses.

A well-attested analysis makes it a circumstantial accusative referring to al-samāʾ.

The sky is thus sent in a condition of abundantly raining.

In this adjectival use, mifʿāl can apply to masculine and feminine subjects alike.

Its apparently masculine form beside feminine samāʾ therefore creates no inherent grammatical problem.

“A sending accompanied by plentiful rain” can paraphrase the meaning in Persian, but that paraphrase alone does not establish a separate syntactic analysis as an adjective qualifying an omitted verbal noun.

This article adopts the documented circumstantial analysis referring to al-samāʾ, distinguishing semantic paraphrase from grammatical parsing.

Abundant outpouring remains the principal meaning in either explanation.

Al-Qurṭubī: midrār as circumstance and samāʾ standing for rain

Arsalnā al-samāʾ and yursili al-samāʾ

Arsalnā in Al-Anʿām 6:6 is past tense.

Yursili in Hūd 11:52 and Nūḥ 71:11 is imperfect.

This difference fits the contexts.

Al-Anʿām recounts earlier peoples’ benefits.

Hūd and Nūḥ promise rain within addresses to their peoples.

Al-samāʾ accompanies midrāran in each, making abundant rain clear.

Samāʾ can stand for rain coming from it, a familiar Arabic expression.

Natural translation can therefore say sent abundant rain or sent the sky with abundant rainfall.

D R R itself does not mean heaven: samāʾ belongs to S M W, while midrār describes plentiful outpouring.

8. Kawkab durriyy: brilliance in the light parable

Al-Nūr 24:35

اللَّهُ نُورُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ مَثَلُ نُورِهِ كَمِشْكَاةٍ فِيهَا مِصْبَاحٌ الْمِصْبَاحُ فِي زُجَاجَةٍ الزُّجَاجَةُ كَأَنَّهَا كَوْكَبٌ دُرِّيٌّ يُوقَدُ مِنْ شَجَرَةٍ مُبَارَكَةٍ زَيْتُونَةٍ لَا شَرْقِيَّةٍ وَلَا غَرْبِيَّةٍ يَكَادُ زَيْتُهَا يُضِيءُ وَلَوْ لَمْ تَمْسَسْهُ نَارٌ نُورٌ عَلَىٰ نُورٍ يَهْدِي اللَّهُ لِنُورِهِ مَنْ يَشَاءُ وَيَضْرِبُ اللَّهُ الْأَمْثَالَ لِلنَّاسِ وَاللَّهُ بِكُلِّ شَيْءٍ عَلِيمٌ

Allāhu nūru al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍi mathalu nūrihi ka-mishkātin fīhā miṣbāḥun al-miṣbāḥu fī zujājatin al-zujājatu ka-annahā kawkabun durriyyun yūqadu min shajaratin mubārakatin zaytūnatin lā sharqiyyatin wa-lā gharbiyyatin yakādu zaytuhā yuḍīʾu wa-law lam tamsashhu nārun nūrun ʿalā nūrin yahdī Allāhu li-nūrihi man yashāʾu wa-yaḍribu Allāhu al-amthāla li-al-nāsi wa-Allāhu bi-kulli shayʾin ʿalīmun

“The God is the light of the heavens and earth. The likeness of His light is a niche containing a lamp; the lamp is within glass, the glass as though a brilliant star, kindled from a blessed olive tree, neither eastern nor western. Its oil almost shines although no fire has touched it. Light upon light! The God guides whom He wills to His light; The God gives people parables, and The God knows everything.”

Durriyyun qualifies kawkabun.

Kawkab is a star or luminous heavenly body; durriyy describes its brilliance.

Dictionaries associate the unhamzated reading with durr, pearl.

A pearl supplies comparison through clarity, brightness, beauty, and brilliance.

Kawkab durriyy thus describes a star whose brilliance resembles pearl.

Glass and star

The direct object of comparison is al-zujājah, the glass.

The Qur’an describes it as though it were a brilliant star.

It does not say the glass literally becomes a star or consists of pearl.

The comparison foregrounds illumination and outward appearance.

The lamp sits within glass, which resembles a star through its brilliance.

Durriyy here means neither milk, rainfall, nor liquid flow.

It belongs to the pearl-brilliance branch.

Does durriyy mean a star made of pearl?

Resemblance in one quality does not require identical material.

Comparing a face to the moon does not make the face consist of lunar material.

Similarly, kawkab durriyy here does not mean a star physically made from pearl.

The comparison highlights brilliance, clarity, and beauty.

The word also identifies no particular star or planet. Such identification requires separate evidence.

Physical light or guidance?

The lamp, glass, oil, and illumination imagery serves a parable about divine light.

Durriyy’s tangible meaning therefore contributes to a larger image.

These levels should remain distinct.

Durriyy lexically describes brilliance, rather than directly meaning faith, guidance, knowledge, or moral purity.

Those concepts belong to the verse’s broader field and the expression yahdī Allāhu li-nūrihi.

Interpretation can study light and guidance without turning its conclusion into D R R’s lexical definition.

9. The readings durriyyun and dirrīʾ

Al-Nūr 24:35 has a reading difference directly affecting derivation.

Ḥafṣ and several other recitations use durriyyun without hamzah.

Other recitations record forms such as dirrīʾ or durrīʾ with hamzah.

These are more than case-ending differences, since derivational analysis changes too.

The unhamzated reading: association with pearl

Many lexicographers relate unhamzated durriyy to durr.

It consequently means brilliant and clear like a pearl.

Al-Farrāʾ, Al-Zajjāj, and others explain the verse through this analysis.

It suits the lamp and glass image, where illuminated glass is brilliant and striking. However, Al-Qurṭubī and Abū ʿAlī al-Fārisī as quoted in Lisān al-ʿArab also allow hamzah simplification from D R ʾ for the unhamzated form. Pearl association is thus a recognized analysis, rather than a historically certain conclusion from the absence of hamzah alone.

The hamzated reading: possible D R ʾ derivation

Some lexicographers derive hamzated forms from D R ʾ.

D R ʾ concerns repelling, driving, or moving aside.

Daraʾa al-kawkabu can also describe a star appearing or coming forward.

This analysis connects the description with striking emergence, movement, or radiance.

It concerns D R ʾ rather than D R R.

All recited forms should not therefore merge under one certain root while ignoring hamzah.

Why the difference matters for Forqan research

A Qur’anic index based on Ḥafṣ places durriyyun under D R R.

This accords with its familiar pearl derivation.

Valid hamzated readings should nevertheless remain visible because their origins may be analyzed differently.

That difference does not necessarily make one reading incompatible with light or brilliance.

Both describe a striking, brilliant star despite differences of derivation and imagery.

Nor does variation require combining both possible derivations into one base-translation phrase.

A brilliant star naturally suits the Ḥafṣ-based version; derivational disagreement can remain in the root explanation.

Al-Qurṭubī: readings, pearl association, and possible hamzah simplification

10. Distinguishing related roots and words

D R R and Ṣ B B: outpouring and pouring down

Arabic Ṣ B B concerns pouring or pouring down.

ʿAbasa 80:25 says:

أَنَّا صَبَبْنَا الْمَاءَ صَبًّا

annā ṣababnā al-māʾa ṣabban

“We poured down the water abundantly.”

Ṣ B B and D R R overlap concerning rain.

Ṣ B B foregrounds pouring itself; Qur’anic midrār foregrounds plentiful outpouring.

Both can describe rain without being complete synonyms in every construction.

Ṣ B B also concerns pouring other liquids or materials.

D R R and GH D Q: abundant rain and plentiful water

Al-Jinn 72:16 says:

لَأَسْقَيْنَاهُمْ مَاءً غَدَقًا

la-asqaynāhum māʾan ghadaqan

“We would certainly have given them plentiful water to drink.”

Ghadaq concerns plentiful water.

Qur’anic midrār likewise concerns plentiful rain.

Ghadaq emphasizes abundant water, while midrār emphasizes abundant falling or rainfall.

Water can be abundant in a spring, river, or other source; all three midrār passages concern sky and rain.

Plentiful water and plentiful rain overlap contextually without identical semantic roles.

D R R and W B L: abundant rain and heavy rainfall

Al-Baqarah 2:265 mentions wābil.

This concerns heavy or intense rain.

Midrār can likewise describe abundant rain.

Wābil foregrounds rainfall’s heaviness or intensity, while midrār foregrounds plentiful outpouring and flow.

Both may describe one rainfall without identical meanings.

Not every midrār must therefore have very large droplets or accompany flooding.

D R R and L ʾ L ʾ: pearls and their brilliance

Qur’anic luʾluʾ names pearls, including Al-Raḥmān 55:22 and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:23.

Arabic durr also names pearl, but the independent noun does not occur in the Qur’an.

Unhamzated durriyy in Al-Nūr 24:35 relates to pearl.

The verse describes pearl-associated brilliance rather than naming an actual pearl object.

A brilliant star therefore reads more naturally than a pearl star.

Where luʾluʾ itself appears, pearl is the direct equivalent.

Study of L ʾ L ʾ

D R R and N W R: brilliance and light

Qur’anic nūr has a wide field, including tangible illumination and guidance.

Durriyy in Al-Nūr 24:35 describes the star’s brilliance.

Durriyy is not itself identical to nūr.

Its presence in the Light Verse does not make every D R R meaning concern illumination.

Rain is the subject in the other three uses.

Related meanings within a verse therefore do not make their roots identical.

Study of N W R

D R R and D R ʾ: brilliance and repelling

The roots share two initial letters but differ in the third.

D R R is doubled, with two rāʾs.

D R ʾ ends in hamzah.

This directly matters for Al-Nūr 24:35’s readings.

The unhamzated reading supports pearl association.

Lexical and recitation sources also derive hamzated forms from D R ʾ.

Removing hamzah and then historically or semantically equating the families is unjustified.

Study of D R ʾ

D R R and Ḍ R R: abundant outpouring and harm

Careless writing may confuse them.

D R R begins with dāl; Ḍ R R begins with ḍād.

Qur’anic Ḍ R R concerns injury and harm.

Midrār instead belongs to D R R and means abundant rainfall.

It does not mean harmful or injurious.

Rain may sometimes lead to flooding or harm without that possible consequence belonging to D R R’s lexical meaning.

Study of Ḍ R R

11. Suggested translations for Forqan

The two principal Qur’anic forms differ and need separate translations.

Midrāran

Suitable equivalents for the three occurrences include:

Rain-laden, bringing plentiful rain, abundant rainfall, and in some contexts repeatedly.

Forqan’s Persian rendering in Al-Anʿām 6:6, “We sent them rain repeatedly,” is defensible and highlights sustained or repeated rainfall.

Explanation should make clear that abundance matters as well as temporal repetition.

In Hūd 11:52, an equivalent of sending the sky upon you with plentiful rain naturally conveys the sentence.

In Nūḥ 71:11, He sends you abundant rain likewise suits.

Milk, pearls, tears, and every other family meaning need not enter these translations.

Wealth, agricultural growth, and divine mercy should not directly replace midrār. Context can convey them, but the word concerns abundant rainfall.

Durriyyun

Forqan’s Persian brilliant in Al-Nūr 24:35 is suitable.

The expression:

«كَأَنَّهَا كَوْكَبٌ دُرِّيٌّ»

ka-annahā kawkabun durriyyun

Naturally becomes:

“As though it were a brilliant star.”

Explanation can note unhamzated durriyy’s familiar pearl derivation and its clarity and brightness.

The base rendering need not construct an unwieldy pearl-like star with luminous clarity.

Nor should it name a particular star or planet.

Hamzated readings need separate D R ʾ analysis; brilliant is clear and sufficient for the present Ḥafṣ-based version.

12. Conclusion

Arabic D R R has several familiar semantic branches.

Abundant flowing and outpouring is central, as in milk and rain.

Another branch concerns movement, including fast-moving animals and rotating things in early usage.

Durr names pearl, while unhamzated durriyy in Al-Nūr 24:35 connects with pearl’s brilliance and clarity.

Ibn Fāris identifies something coming forth from something else and movement as two principles. A single simple physical image therefore does not conclusively unite every use.

The root occurs four times in the Qur’an.

Three midrāran occurrences concern sky and plentiful rain.

Al-Anʿām 6:6 describes earlier peoples receiving rain among their resources before destruction for sins.

Hūd 11:52 connects plentiful rain with seeking forgiveness and returning to the Lord.

Nūḥ 71:11 places it alongside wealth, children, gardens, and rivers.

Midrār does not itself mean wealth, offspring, gardens, or moral worth. It directly conveys plentiful rainfall.

Durriyyun in Al-Nūr 24:35 describes a brilliant star within the divine-light parable. Its unhamzated reading has a familiar pearl analysis, while some hamzated readings have a D R ʾ analysis.

Both the two Qur’anic branches and valid recitation differences deserve preservation.

Proposed final definition: One main D R R branch denotes abundant flowing from a source, particularly milk and rain. Other recorded branches concern movement and pearls, without conclusive historical linkage between every use. Qur’anic midrār describes abundant rainfall; unhamzated durriyy expresses brilliance and pearl-like clarity under a familiar derivation. Morphology and each construction’s context determine their meanings. Brief root meaning: Abundant flow and outpouring; durr: pearl, and durriyy: brilliant and pearl-like.
