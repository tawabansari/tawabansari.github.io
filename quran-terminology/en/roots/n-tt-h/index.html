From horned animals in Arabic to al-naṭīḥah and its place in the Qur’an’s food provisions

The three-consonant Arabic root N TT H primarily denotes animals such as rams, goats and cattle striking with their horns. An animal’s horn strike is called naṭḥ. If two animals butt one another, verbs such as tanāṭaḥa and intaṭaḥa can describe the mutual action.

The family also extends to collision and confrontation: waves clashing, warriors fighting and forceful encounters between opponents. Nevertheless, its primary sense should not merge with every blow, fall or accidental collision. Its distinctive image is horn-butting, familiar from horned animals.

Only one derivative occurs in the Qur’an: al-naṭīḥah in Al-Māʾidah 5:3. It appears among descriptions of animals that can die from various injuries without ritual slaughter. The prohibition and subsequent exception distinguish animals already dead from those still eligible for ritual slaughter. The word draws on familiar livestock language to explain this situation.

1. Forms and semantic core

Important recorded Arabic forms include:

  • نَطَحَ: struck or butted with a horn.
  • نَطْح: butting or a horn strike.
  • نَاطِح: an animal that butts.
  • نَطَّاح: an animal that butts frequently or is known for doing so.
  • نَطِيح: butted or gored; in other uses, particular meanings such as ill-omened.
  • نَطِيحَة: a gored animal; in the Qur’anic setting, an animal that has died from goring.
  • تَنَاطَحَ وَاِنْتَطَحَ: butted one another.
  • نِطَاح و مُنَاطَحَة: mutual butting or confrontation.

These forms are recorded in Arabic, but only al-naṭīḥah occurs in the Qur’an.

The semantic core

The clear core is striking with a horn. The action can produce a clash between animals, push an opponent back, cause injury or even kill it, but none of those results is necessary to the verb’s meaning.

A ram can butt another without serious injury, wound it, or cause its death. Naṭḥ occurs in all three cases; the outcome differs.

The action of butting must therefore be distinguished from its result. This matters for al-naṭīḥah: the Qur’anic context concerns not only the collision but also the injured animal’s outcome within food provisions.

2. Early Arabic uses

Animals butting

A principal, well-attested use in lexical accounts describes rams and similar animals butting, including:

نَطَحَ الْكَبْشُ

naṭaḥa al-kabshu

Translation: “The ram butted.”

For two animals facing one another:

تَنَاطَحَ الْكَبْشَانِ

tanāṭaḥa al-kabshāni

Translation: “The two rams butted one another.”

The second expression makes the animals’ mutual participation clear. The simple verb states butting without necessarily implying retaliation or the other animal’s death.

Nāṭiḥ likewise describes an animal that butts. In the following expression:

مَا لَهُ نَاطِحٌ وَلَا خَابِطٌ

mā lahu nāṭiḥun wa-lā khābiṭun

Translation: “He has neither an animal that butts nor one that strikes with its foot.”

Nāṭiḥ denotes livestock such as sheep and goats, while khābiṭ denotes camels. The expression means that the person has no such livestock.

This connects the root directly with livestock life and familiar animal behavior. Its presence in lexical sources does not alone date the expression’s origin.

Extension to conflict and collision

Arabic also uses the image of butting for conflict, forceful collision and confrontation beyond actual animals.

For example:

تَنَاطَحَتِ الْأَمْوَاجُ

tanāṭaḥati al-amwāju

Translation: “The waves crashed against one another.”

Waves have no horns; their mutual, forceful collision is compared with butting animals.

Nawāṭiḥ al-dahr similarly describes hardships and difficult turns of fortune, as though adverse events struck a person with force.

A familiar rajaz line states:

اللَّيْلُ دَاجٍ وَالْكِبَاشُ تَنْتَطِحُ

al-laylu dājin wa-al-kibāshu tantaṭiḥu

Translation: “The night is dark, and the rams are butting one another.”

The rams here represent warriors fighting. Lexical sources and proverb collections transmit the line, with some reports placing it in the battle of Ṣiffīn. It should therefore not be presented as certainly pre-Islamic, though it is useful evidence of the root’s figurative use in early Arabic.

Such extensions do not justify broadening al-naṭīḥah in the Qur’anic verse to every collision or accident.

Other uses of naṭīḥ and nāṭiḥ

Lexical accounts also use these words for an animal or bird appearing directly in front of someone. Such encounters acquired particular significance in omen-taking beliefs. Rajul naṭīḥ is likewise recorded for a person considered ill-omened or unlucky.

Naṭīḥ also occurs in descriptions of a horse’s forehead markings, while al-naṭḥ and al-nāṭiḥ are recorded as names in traditional star lore.

These show the family’s range, without importing omens, stars or divination into Al-Māʾidah. Each use requires its own context.

3. Al-naṭīḥah: form and the distinction between butting and being gored

Al-naṭīḥah follows faʿīlah and, in its familiar Qur’anic use, denotes an animal that has been gored.

Its meaning is close to manṭūḥah: an animal struck by another animal’s horn.

The roles of agent and recipient must remain distinct:

Nāṭiḥ is the animal that butts, while naṭīḥ is the animal that has been butted.

Two animals can both butt and receive blows. The distinction concerns each animal’s role in a particular action, rather than requiring one to be solely the attacker and the other solely the injured party.

Why does naṭīḥah end in tāʾ?

Arabic descriptions on the faʿīl pattern with passive meaning often remain unchanged between masculine and feminine, as in imraʾah qatīl, a slain woman.

Both naʿjah naṭīḥ and naʿjah naṭīḥah are accordingly recorded.

When a description becomes an independent, established noun, a final tāʾ can become fixed in its form, as with al-dhabīḥah, al-farīsah and al-naṭīḥah.

A familiar explanation treats the Qur’anic word this way: it has become the name for an animal in that condition, retaining the final tāʾ.

Other analyses propose a sense closer to an active description, since both animals can participate in butting. The passive sense, the gored animal, nevertheless fits the established use and the verse’s context more directly.

The final tāʾ does not limit the provision to female animals. The Arabic noun has a feminine form, but the category concerns injury and outcome rather than excluding males.

4. The Qur’anic use: Al-Māʾidah 5:3

In listing forbidden foods, the Qur’an names several animal categories together:

وَالْمُنْخَنِقَةُ وَالْمَوْقُوذَةُ وَالْمُتَرَدِّيَةُ وَالنَّطِيحَةُ وَمَا أَكَلَ السَّبُعُ إِلَّا مَا ذَكَّيْتُمْ

wa-al-munkhaniqatu wa-al-mawqūdhatu wa-al-mutaraddiyatu wa-al-naṭīḥatu wa-mā akala al-sabuʿu illā mā dhakkaytum

Translation: “And the strangled, the beaten to death, the fallen, the gored, and what a predator has eaten, except what you slaughter.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:3

This follows “حُرِّمَتْ عَلَيْكُمُ الْمَيْتَةُ”: the prohibition of carrion, followed by particular conditions that can result in death without ritual slaughter.

Different causes of injury and death are distinguished:

Al-munkhaniqah concerns strangling; al-mawqūdhah, death from a blow; al-mutaraddiyah, falling; al-naṭīḥah, goring; and mā akala al-sabuʿ, an animal attacked and partly eaten by a predator.

The terms share a context without sharing one lexical meaning. The passage names particular events rather than using a single general term such as injured.

Study of KH N Q; Study of S B ʿ

Why name al-naṭīḥah separately?

If carrion has already been prohibited, why separately name an animal that died from goring?

Within the livestock setting of this verse, carrion is a general category for an animal that died without ritual slaughter. The cause can vary: illness, strangling, a blow, a fall, goring or a predator’s attack. This contextual account does not replace separate examination of other food categories and exceptions.

Naming these conditions makes clear that accidental death does not itself remove an animal from the carrion category. An animal killed by another animal’s horn does not become ritually slaughtered merely because its cause of death is known or because it bled.

Al-naṭīḥah here identifies an animal whose death resulted from goring. This comes from the word together with the opening prohibition and subsequent exception, rather than making the verb naṭaḥa always mean to kill.

Study of M W T

The importance of “إِلَّا مَا ذَكَّيْتُمْ”

Immediately after those descriptions, the passage states:

إِلَّا مَا ذَكَّيْتُمْ

illā mā dhakkaytum

Translation: “Except what you slaughter.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:3

The exception is decisive for understanding al-naṭīḥah.

Suppose two sheep butt one another and one is injured. On the reading that includes animals still eligible for ritual slaughter in the exception, goring alone does not make the meat permanently forbidden. If ritual slaughter meeting the necessary conditions occurs before death, the situation differs from an animal killed by the blow. The root naṭḥ alone does not establish those conditions.

Three situations therefore need distinction: butting occurring, an animal being injured, and its dying from the injury. They need not happen together.

The exception shows that accidental injury and death without ritual slaughter are not one inseparable category.

Interpreters and grammarians have debated the exception’s scope. A familiar, well-supported reading applies it to preceding categories eligible for ritual slaughter, including al-naṭīḥah. An animal still alive after injury and ritually slaughtered is then distinguished from one that died without slaughter.

Determining life, injury severity and the manner of slaughter involves separate jurisprudential questions. The root alone does not settle them.

Related verses on carrion

Al-Baqarah 2:173, Al-Anʿām 6:145 and Al-Naḥl 16:115 also discuss carrion and forbidden foods.

Those use the general carrion category, while Al-Māʾidah 5:3 also names particular causes of death.

Al-naṭīḥah is therefore not wholly separate from carrion: the issue is an animal dying without ritual slaughter, distinguished from one slaughtered.

This should not merge with every other lawful and unlawful food question. The root study explains the term and its role here; a full account of food provisions, necessity and other conditions belongs to independent study.

5. Related roots

N TT H and W Q DH: horn strikes and blows

Both can concern injury from a blow, but they differ.

Naṭḥ primarily means animal horn-butting. Waqdh concerns a forceful, injurious blow; al-mawqūdhah in Al-Māʾidah 5:3 denotes an animal killed by such a blow.

Goring can be a kind of blow, but not every blow is naṭḥ.

The separate categories identify different causes of injury, even where both result in death without ritual slaughter.

N TT H and R D Y: horn strikes and falling

Al-mutaraddiyah principally concerns falling, such as from a height or into a well.

Naṭḥ concerns a horn strike. An animal can fall after being gored, without the two connected events making the roots identical.

The relevant category depends on which event and cause-and-result relation the description foregrounds.

N TT H and SAD D M: horn strikes and collision

Arabic can also express bodies colliding through Ṣ D M, a broader concept.

Vehicles colliding, an animal striking an obstacle or waves striking a shore can be described with suitable collision terms. Naṭḥ primarily means horn-butting, retaining that confrontation image in figurative uses.

Not every collision should therefore be called naṭḥ without contextual support. “Involved in a collision” is particularly imprecise for al-naṭīḥah, because it loses the horn-strike feature.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Naṭaḥa naturally means butted or struck with a horn. Tanāṭaḥa is better rendered as butted one another.

Nāṭiḥ can be rendered as an animal that butts; naṭīḥ, in its passive use, as gored.

Forqan’s existing Persian “shākh-khordah”, gored, preserves al-naṭīḥah’s direct meaning in Al-Māʾidah 5:3.

It does not replace the word with a complete interpretation or ruling. The reader first encounters goring, then learns from the prohibition and slaughter exception which situation is meant.

A proposed word-box explanation is:

Brief root meaning: Butting or striking with a horn.

Meaning in this verse: A gored animal; in context, one that died from goring without ritual slaughter, except where slaughter occurs before death.

The full explanation of death and the slaughter exception belongs in the word’s explanation or root study, rather than crowding every detail into its brief equivalent.

7. Conclusion

N TT H primarily denotes animals butting with horns. Further uses concern mutual collision, forceful conflict and confrontation. Some derivatives also occur in omen-related expressions and traditional names, without bringing those senses into the Qur’anic al-naṭīḥah.

The root occurs once in the Qur’an, as al-naṭīḥah in Al-Māʾidah 5:3. It denotes a gored animal and, in context, an animal that died from goring without ritual slaughter.

Its distinction from related terms lies in the event: al-mawqūdhah concerns a forceful blow, al-mutaraddiyah a fall, and al-naṭīḥah goring. All may result in death without ritual slaughter while retaining distinct lexical meanings.

The exception also shows that being gored does not itself mean the animal is dead. An injured animal still alive and slaughtered before death differs from one killed by the same injury.

Final definition: N TT H primarily means butting or striking with a horn, with extended uses for forceful confrontation and collision. Al-naṭīḥah is the gored animal discussed within the Qur’an’s carrion provisions: an animal whose death resulted from goring before ritual slaughter took place.
