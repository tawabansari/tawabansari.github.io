Summer and the warm season: from seasonal cycles and summer rain to residence, seasonal journeys, and deviation from a course

1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

Arabic ص ي ف (Ṣ Y F) chiefly concerns summer and the part of the year when the weather grows warmer. The family includes words for hot days, summer residence, places for spending summer, seasonal rain, and activities undertaken during this period.

In another branch, صَافَ (ṣāfa) concerns inclining, deviating, or turning aside from a course. It can describe an arrow going off target or something departing from its path.

Ṣ Y F occurs only once in the Qur’an, as وَالصَّيْفِ (wa-al-ṣayfi) in Quraysh 106:2.

Here al-ṣayf means summer and stands alongside al-shitāʾ, winter. The verse concerns Quraysh’s seasonal journeys.

Principal Arabic forms include:

  • صَيْف (ṣayf): summer, a period associated with the warm season.
  • أَصْيَاف و صُيُوف (aṣyāf, ṣuyūf): plurals of ṣayf: summers.
  • صَائِف (ṣāʾif): relating to summer; hot when describing a day or night.
  • صَيْفِيّ (ṣayfī): summery or belonging to summer.
  • صَائِفَة (ṣāʾifah): the summer period; in other uses, a summer journey or campaign.
  • صَافَ / يَصِيفُ (ṣāfa / yaṣīfu): spent summer somewhere; in another branch, deviated from a course.
  • أَصَافَ (aṣāfa): entered the summer season.
  • تَصَيَّفَ و اِصْطَافَ (taṣayyafa, iṣṭāfa): spent summer in a place.
  • مَصِيف و مُصْطَاف (maṣīf, muṣṭāf): a place or time for spending summer.
  • مُصَايَفَة (muṣāyafah): a transaction, contract, or undertaking for the summer period.
  • صَيِّف و صَيِّفَة (ṣayyif, ṣayyifah): summer rain; in some uses, vegetation growing during this season.
  • صَيْفِيُّون (ṣayfiyyūn): in an early use, children born to a father in his old age.
  • صَيْفُوفَة (ṣayfūfah): turning aside from a course, in the branch concerning inclination and deviation.

These forms are recorded in Arabic sources, but only the noun ṣayf occurs in the Qur’an. Its seasonal meaning should therefore remain central to Qur’anic study; other uses illuminate the language’s historical field.

2. Semantic cores in early Arabic

Two branches in Ibn Fāris’s analysis

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies two semantic principles for Ṣ Y F:

أَحَدُهُمَا يَدُلُّ عَلَى زَمَانٍ، وَالْآخَرُ يَدُلُّ عَلَى مَيْلٍ وَعُدُولٍ

aḥaduhumā yadullu ʿalā zamānin wa-al-ākharu yadullu ʿalā maylin wa-ʿudūlin

“One concerns a time, and the other inclination and turning aside.”

The first branch covers summer and its associated phenomena and activities.

The second concerns departure from a course, change of direction, and inclination.

This distinction matters: not every derivative should be explained through heat and summer.

When Arabic describes an arrow going off target, for example, the warm season need not be involved.

Nor do shared consonants establish that “summer” historically developed from “the sun’s change of course.” An appealing image alone cannot prove a word’s origin without independent historical evidence.

The two branches should therefore retain their separate places.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: the time and inclination/deviation branches

Ṣayf: the name of a season

Arabic صَيْف (ṣayf) names a period of the year associated with warmth and contrasted with winter.

Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī identifies it as the season opposite shitāʾ.

Early Arabic seasonal boundaries did not always match common present-day calendars.

Al-Jawharī’s Al-Ṣiḥāḥ places ṣayf after spring and before qayẓ.

Qayẓ here denotes the period of intense heat following ṣayf.

Lisān al-ʿArab reports Ibn Kunāsah’s fourfold division: the first rabīʿ, corresponding in his account to Persian autumn, then winter, ṣayf, and qayẓ. Al-Azharī also explains that ṣayf corresponds to what people in Iraq and Khurasan call spring.

In this division, ṣayf could cover a period overlapping parts of present-day spring, while qayẓ named the warmer period that followed.

Other accounts also record a sixfold division of the seasons.

Historical Arabic season names thus relate to rainfall, vegetation, heat, and people’s lives rather than always matching fixed present-day boundaries.

These differences do not obscure al-ṣayf’s clear meaning in Quraysh 106:2: the summer or warmer part of the year, contrasted with winter.

Lisān al-ʿArab: seasonal divisions reported by Al-Azharī and Ibn Kunāsah

Ṣayf and heat

Arabic يَوْمٌ صَائِفٌ (yawmun ṣāʾifun) means a hot day, and لَيْلَةٌ صَائِفَةٌ (laylatun ṣāʾifatun) a hot night.

The adjective derives from the season’s name and expresses its familiar characteristic.

Summer and heat should nevertheless be distinguished.

Summer names a period of the year; heat is a temperature condition that can occur across different seasons and regions.

A very hot day may occur outside summer, while a highland or cold region’s summer can be temperate.

Ṣayf should therefore not always be translated simply as “heat.”

Quraysh 106:2 concerns travelling in a season rather than merely in hot weather.

3. Rain, vegetation, and summer life

Ṣayyif: seasonal rain

Arabic sources record صَيِّف (ṣayyif), with a doubled yāʾ, for rain falling in the ṣayf season.

Ṣayfī can also describe vegetation growing at that time.

Lisān al-ʿArab reports Ibn Sīdah and Al-Jawharī’s accounts of rain in this period also bearing the season’s name.

Ibn Barrī distinguishes ṣayf, the season, from ṣayyif, summer rain.

The difference in vocalization matters.

Although summer brings heat and dryness to many areas, this does not establish that every Arabic-speaking region had entirely rainless summers.

In some regions, summer rain could matter for vegetation and water supply.

The season’s name could accordingly also name its rainfall.

A witness from early poetry

Abū Kabīr al-Hudhalī says in a poem:

وَلَقَدْ وَرَدْتُ الْمَاءَ لَمْ يَشْرَبْ بِهِ
حَدَّ الرَّبِيعِ إِلَى شُهُورِ الصَّيِّفِ

wa-laqad waradtu al-māʾa lam yashrab bihi / ḥadda al-rabīʿi ilā shuhūri al-ṣayyifi

An approximate rendering, read with the following line:

“I reached a watering place where, from the spring period to the months of summer rain, none had drunk except the animals described in the following line.”

Al-ṣayyif here is explained in connection with the summer-rain period.

Ibn Barrī explains that yashrab’s subject appears in the next line: animals that return to the watering place. The exception continuing in that line shows why the excerpt does not deny the presence of all water or drinking by every creature.

The witness matters because seasonal change and the watering place’s condition enter the portrayal of animal life and movement.

Ṣayf was thus more than a calendar label detached from life: it related to rain, water resources, and pasture conditions.

Lisān al-ʿArab: ṣayyif rain and the continuation of Abū Kabīr’s witness

Ṣifnā: receiving summer rain

Arabic used the expression:

صِفْنَا

ṣifnā

“Summer rain reached us.”

Its morphological explanation treats it as a modified passive form concerning the arrival of seasonal rain.

صِيفَتِ الأرض (ṣīfati al-arḍu) likewise describes land receiving this period’s rain.

This differs from aṣāfa al-qawmu, “the people entered summer.”

One concerns entering a season, the other rain occurring within it.

The forms should therefore not all receive a single uniform translation without attention to their structure and subject.

Lisān al-ʿArab: passive ṣifnā and land receiving seasonal rain

4. Summer residence and seasonal activities

Ṣāfa and taṣayyafa: spending summer somewhere

Arabic صَافَ بِالْمَكَانِ (ṣāfa bi-al-makāni) means residing in a place during summer.

تَصَيَّفَ (taṣayyafa) and اِصْطَافَ (iṣṭāfa) occupy the same field.

A family might, for example, move in the warm season from a dry region to a place with water and a milder climate and spend summer there.

The verb here expresses residence during the season rather than merely the season’s arrival.

By contrast, أَصَافَ القوم (aṣāfa al-qawmu) means that the people entered summer.

Entering a season and spending it in a particular place are related but distinguishable uses.

A poetic witness from Labīd

A verse attributed to Labīd ibn Rabīʿah in the dictionaries says:

فَتَصَيَّفَا مَاءً بِدَحْلٍ سَاكِنًا
يَسْتَنُّ فَوْقَ سَرَاتِهِ الْعُلْجُومُ

fa-taṣayyafā māʾan bi-daḥlin sākinan / yastannu fawqa sarātihi al-ʿuljūmu

Approximate rendering:

“The two spent summer beside still water at Daḥl, with an aquatic creature moving over its surface.”

Taṣayyafā here concerns spending summer in a particular place.

Daḥl names a watering place or locality in early geographical accounts.

The witness therefore illustrates summer residence.

Labīd was a mukhaḍram poet, living both before and after Islam. The precise date of this verse is not independently established, so it should not be categorically called pre-Islamic without evidence.

Muʿjam al-Buldān: Daḥl and Labīd’s summer-residence witness

Maṣīf: a place for spending summer

Arabic مَصِيف (maṣīf) and مُصْطَاف (muṣṭāf) can name a place or time for spending summer.

In its locative use, maṣīf is a place chosen for residence during the warm season.

Water, shade, elevation, or more suitable weather may make it attractive.

Maṣīf itself nevertheless does not mean a mountain region, a cold region, or a holiday resort.

It may be a place for herders to stay and use pasture, or somewhere people remain to avoid intense heat.

Present-day Arabic often applies maṣīf to a summer resort town or area. This relates to seasonal residence, but modern tourism should not be imported into every early use.

Ṣāʾifah: seasonal provisions and journeys

Arabic صَائِفَة (ṣāʾifah) denotes the summer period.

Another use, ṣāʾifatu al-qawmi, names provisions people obtain for that period.

The lexicons also discuss summer mīrah: food or supplies prepared to get through the season.

In post-Islamic historical periods, al-ṣāʾifah also named summer military campaigns, undertaken during the warmer season and ending before severe cold.

A season’s name can thus transfer to a particular activity occurring within it.

The campaign sense of ṣāʾifah is nevertheless not a fixed part of ṣayf’s meaning and should not be imposed on Quraysh’s journey in the Qur’an.

Quraysh 106:2 concerns a riḥlah, a journey, rather than warfare or campaigning.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Muḥkam, and Asās al-Balāghah: residence, maṣīf, and ṣāʾifah

Ṣayfī: belonging to a season or stage of life

Ordinary صَيْفِيّ (ṣayfī) describes something related to summer, such as rain, seasonal food, or produce obtained during that period.

Early sources also record صَيْفِيُّون (ṣayfiyyūn) for children born to a father in old age.

A rajaz attributed to Saʿd ibn Mālik ibn Ḍubayʿah, and in other accounts to Aktham ibn Ṣayfī, says:

إِنَّ بَنِيَّ صِبْيَةٌ صَيْفِيُّونْ
أَفْلَحَ مَنْ كَانَ لَهُ رِبْعِيُّونْ

inna baniyya ṣibyatun ṣayfiyyūn / aflaḥa man kāna lahu ribʿiyyūn

Translation:

“My sons are young children born to me in old age; fortunate is the man who had his children in youth.”

Ṣayfiyyūn here is not confined to summer births: it is contrasted with children fathered in youth.

The established expression must determine the meaning, rather than the embedded season name fixing the children’s precise birth season.

Sources disagree over the rajaz’s attribution; it should not be assigned to one poet with certainty without further examination.

Lisān al-ʿArab: ṣayfiyyūn and the rajaz’s differing attribution

5. Ṣ Y F as inclination and deviation

Ṣāfa ʿani al-shayʾi: turning aside from something

In the second branch, صَافَ عَنِ الشَّيْءِ (ṣāfa ʿani al-shayʾi) means turning or deviating away from something.

صَافَ السَّهْمُ عَنِ الْهَدَفِ (ṣāfa al-sahmu ʿani al-hadafi) describes an arrow going off target.

The subject is change of direction or departure from an intended course or target, rather than summer or heat.

Ibn Fāris likewise treats this branch separately from the season’s meaning.

A poetic witness about a deviating arrow

Abū Zubayd al-Ṭāʾī says in a verse:

كُلَّ يَوْمٍ تَرْمِيهِ مِنْهَا بِرَشْقٍ
فَمُصِيبٌ أَوْ صَافَ غَيْرَ بَعِيدِ

kulla yawmin tarmīhi minhā bi-rashqin / fa-muṣībun aw ṣāfa ghayra baʿīdi

Approximate rendering:

“Every day arrows are shot towards him: some hit their mark, while others stray only a little.”

Ṣāfa here stands against muṣīb.

Muṣīb describes an arrow reaching its target.

Ṣāfa in this context concerns something deviating from the target.

Inclination and deviation are thus clear.

Abū Zubayd is known as a mukhaḍram and early Islamic poet. This verse’s composition date is unclear, so it should not be treated as a firmly dated pre-Islamic witness.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: Abū Zubayd’s witness and an arrow going off target

Was summer named from the sun’s inclination?

One may imagine a connection between seasonal changes in the sun’s apparent position and inclination or deviation.

That image alone does not establish the historical origin of ṣayf.

Ibn Fāris also identifies time and deviation as separate branches.

Root study should therefore not assert that summer originally meant “the season of the sun’s deviation” without independent linguistic and historical evidence.

Al-ṣayf in Quraysh 106:2 names a season; deviation from a course is not present in that use.

6. The Qur’anic use: winter and summer journeys

Ṣ Y F’s sole Qur’anic occurrence is in Quraysh:

لِإِيلَافِ قُرَيْشٍ
إِيلَافِهِمْ رِحْلَةَ الشِّتَاءِ وَالصَّيْفِ

li-īlāfi qurayshin / īlāfihim riḥlata al-shitāʾi wa-al-ṣayfi

“For the familiarity of Quraysh; their familiarity with winter and summer travel.”

Quraysh 106:1 and Quraysh 106:2

The meaning of wa-al-ṣayfi

وَالصَّيْفِ (wa-al-ṣayfi) consists of the conjunction wa, the definite noun al-ṣayf, and a genitive ending.

Al-ṣayf means summer and is coordinated with al-shitāʾ, winter.

Two seasons are thus discussed in connection with Quraysh’s journey.

The lexical meaning is clear: summer.

The word itself does not identify the destination, number of caravans, goods, or length of stay.

Those matters require separate examination of context or historical evidence.

Quraysh’s seasonal journeys

Early commentaries chiefly associate Quraysh’s winter journey with Yemen and their summer journey with Syria.

Al-Ṭabarī transmits Ibn Zayd and other authorities’ accounts of Quraysh travelling to Yemen in winter and Syria in summer.

Explanations consider climatic differences, trading opportunities, and conditions for caravan travel.

The verse itself names neither Yemen nor Syria.

These routes should therefore be identified as historical and exegetical reports rather than al-ṣayf’s direct meaning.

Other accounts describe spending winter in Mecca and summer in Ṭāʾif.

Details of Quraysh’s seasonal travel and residence thus differ among reports.

The verse nevertheless explicitly mentions familiar winter and summer journeys. Understanding Ṣ Y F does not require treating every historical detail as certain.

Al-Ṭabarī: the Syria/Yemen reports and the Mecca/Ṭāʾif account

Why mention winter and summer together?

Both seasons occur in one expression.

Their conjunction may suggest regular, recurring travel at different times of year.

Winter and summer journeys may differ in weather, water, routes, and trading opportunities.

Warm-season movement may require different arrangements for water and rest, while winter travel may encounter cold and changing road conditions.

The verse does not, however, say that winter travel was necessarily easier or harder than summer travel.

Riḥlah is singular and attached to two seasons. This conveys familiar seasonal travel without establishing that Quraysh made only two particular journeys all year and no others.

Their relationship to winter and summer travel is what the verse highlights.

Al-ṣayf and īlāfihim

Al-ṣayf forms part of riḥlata al-shitāʾi wa-al-ṣayfi in the verse.

This expression depends on īlāfihim.

Īlāf belongs to ʾ L F and concerns becoming accustomed, familiarity, and the establishment of a familiar, lasting bond.

Quraysh’s seasonal journeys here relate to an established, recurring situation.

Al-ṣayf alone nevertheless does not mean familiarity, security, trade, or covenant.

Those concepts emerge from other words in the surah and accounts of Quraysh’s circumstances.

Ṣ Y F’s study should therefore retain summer as a season and leave a full analysis of īlāf to its own root and surah.

Study of ʾ L F

7. Seasonal journeys, food, and security

Quraysh continues:

فَلْيَعْبُدُوا رَبَّ هَٰذَا الْبَيْتِ
الَّذِي أَطْعَمَهُمْ مِنْ جُوعٍ وَآمَنَهُمْ مِنْ خَوْفٍ

fa-l-yaʿbudū rabba hādhā al-bayti / alladhī aṭʿamahum min jūʿin wa-āmanahum min khawfin

“Let them worship the Lord of this House, who fed them against hunger and made them secure against fear.”

Quraysh 106:3 and Quraysh 106:4

These verses place seasonal journeys within a setting connected with food and security.

Food and trade

Historical accounts identify Quraysh with trade and caravan journeys.

Seasonal travel could provide access to goods, food, and resources not readily available where they lived.

Quraysh explicitly mentions feeding and relief from hunger.

The verse nevertheless does not say that all Quraysh’s food came exclusively from summer or winter journeys.

Nor does al-ṣayf itself mean trade or obtaining provision.

The connection between travel and livelihood comes from the surah as a whole and historical evidence.

Travel security and Mecca’s position

Al-ʿAnkabūt states:

أَوَلَمْ يَرَوْا أَنَّا جَعَلْنَا حَرَمًا آمِنًا وَيُتَخَطَّفُ النَّاسُ مِنْ حَوْلِهِمْ

a-wa-lam yaraw annā jaʿalnā ḥaraman āminan wa-yutakhaṭṭafu al-nāsu min ḥawlihim

“Have they not seen that We made a secure sanctuary, while people around them are snatched away?”

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:67

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:57 also discusses a secure sanctuary to which fruits and produce are brought.

Security within the sanctuary and access to necessities were thus established concerns in Mecca’s setting.

Such conditions could affect trade and caravan movement.

These verses do not, however, establish that every Quraysh trade route was always safe or that travellers faced no danger.

Security may be relative and dependent on social and historical conditions.

Quraysh 106:4 attributes security to God, but that attribution alone does not specify trade agreements, numbers of guards, or methods of managing caravans.

The surah’s message and human responsibility

Quraysh recalls travel, food, and security in connection with worshipping the Lord of this House.

This construction directs attention from the resources themselves to human responsibility for blessings.

Ṣ Y F’s interpretation need not repeat the entire study of Quraysh’s economy, worship, security, and relationships.

The root’s particular contribution is naming one travel season.

Summer here is therefore not a metaphor for misguidance, an indication that travel is forbidden or obligatory, or the name of a particular act of worship.

It directly concerns the journey’s seasonal timing.

8. Comparison with related roots

Ṣ Y F (ṣayf) and SH T W (shitāʾ)

Ṣayf concerns summer and shitāʾ winter.

Quraysh 106:2 places the words together.

Early Arabic generally associated winter with cold and different conditions of rainfall and seasonal life, while ṣayf concerned the warmer period.

This does not mean that all regions had identical weather in the same seasons.

Lexically, both name seasons.

Neither word independently names a trade route, type of goods, or mode of travel.

Ṣ Y F (ṣayf) and Q Y Ẓ (qayẓ)

Arabic qayẓ concerns intense heat.

In some early seasonal divisions, qayẓ followed ṣayf.

Broader uses also applied qayẓ to the severity of summer heat.

The words are close without always being fully synonymous.

Ṣayf names a seasonal period; familiar qayẓ emphasizes more intense heat.

Qayẓ does not occur in the Qur’an.

Quraysh 106:2 uses al-ṣayf, which should not be replaced without evidence by qayẓ or “scorching heat.”

Ṣ Y F (ṣayf) and Ḥ R R (ḥarr)

Ḥarr concerns heat.

Al-Tawbah 9:81 states:

لَا تَنْفِرُوا فِي الْحَرِّ

lā tanfirū fī al-ḥarri

“Do not go forth in the heat.”

The subject here is hot weather.

Heat and summer nevertheless differ.

Heat may occur in a season whose name is absent from the sentence.

Conversely, naming summer does not independently specify a temperature.

Al-ṣayf in Quraysh 106:2 should therefore be translated as “summer,” rather than simply “heat.”

Study of Ḥ R R

Ṣ Y F (ṣayf) and R B ʿ (rabīʿ)

Arabic rabīʿ concerns the season of growth and greenery; other uses also name rain and vegetation.

Some ṣayf usages concern a period close to spring in other regions’ calendars.

The historical boundary between these names was therefore not identical everywhere.

This seasonal proximity does not make the roots identical.

Nor should rabīʿ be assumed to occur in the Qur’an: the form does not appear there as a season name.

Study of R B ʿ

Ṣ Y F (ṣayf) and KH R F (kharīf)

Familiar kharīf concerns the harvest season and autumn.

Ṣayf likewise relates to a time of year and seasonal activities.

Historical Arabic season names sometimes followed rainfall, plant growth, or harvest; their precise sequence could vary by region and calendar system.

Ṣayf and kharīf nevertheless remain different names and roots.

The Qur’an contains al-ṣayf but not kharīf as a season name.

Ṣ Y F (ṣayf) and Ṣ W F (ṣūf)

These roots have similar but different consonants.

Ṣ Y F chiefly denotes summer.

Ṣ W F concerns wool.

Some lexicons also record forms such as ṣayyif and ṣāʾif for an animal with abundant wool. These are explained through Ṣ W F despite resembling Ṣ Y F derivatives.

Written resemblance alone is therefore insufficient to determine a root.

Al-ṣayf in Quraysh 106:2 names a season and has no connection with wool.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Majmūʿ al-Mughīth: similar-looking forms from Ṣ W F

Study of Ṣ W F

Ṣ Y F (ṣāfa) and M Y L (māla)

In Ṣ Y F’s second branch, ṣāfa ʿani al-shayʾi concerns inclining or turning aside from something.

This use approaches M Y L.

An arrow going off target may, for example, be described through both concepts.

This overlap does not establish that summer originally meant inclination.

Inclination is one recorded branch of Ṣ Y F rather than a meaning necessarily present in every derivative.

9. Translation suggestions for Forqan

صَيْف (ṣayf): summer; the warmer season.

أَصْيَاف و صُيُوف (aṣyāf, ṣuyūf): summers.

صَائِف (ṣāʾif): summery; hot when describing a day or night.

صَيْفِيّ (ṣayfī): relating to summer; in another use, a child born to a father in old age.

صَائِفَة (ṣāʾifah): the summer period; in another historical use, a summer journey or campaign.

صَافَ بِالْمَكَانِ (ṣāfa bi-al-makāni): spent summer in that place.

أَصَافَ القوم (aṣāfa al-qawmu): the people entered summer.

تَصَيَّفَ و اِصْطَافَ (taṣayyafa, iṣṭāfa): spent summer somewhere.

مَصِيف و مُصْطَاف (maṣīf, muṣṭāf): a place for spending summer.

صَيِّف (ṣayyif): summer rain; in some uses, vegetation of the season.

مُصَايَفَة (muṣāyafah): a summer-period transaction or contract.

صَافَ عَنِ الشَّيْءِ (ṣāfa ʿani al-shayʾi): deviated or turned aside from something.

صَيْفُوفَة (ṣayfūfah): deviation or departure from a course, in the inclination branch.

The existing Forqan rendering of Quraysh 106:2 is appropriate:

إِيلَافِهِمْ رِحْلَةَ الشِّتَاءِ وَالصَّيْفِ

īlāfihim riḥlata al-shitāʾi wa-al-ṣayfi

“Their familiarity with winter and summer travel.”

Wa-al-ṣayfi here means “and summer.”

The lexical explanation can clarify that it means the warmer season opposite winter, during which Quraysh undertook familiar seasonal journeys.

Syria, Yemen, types of goods, and caravan details should nevertheless not be added to al-ṣayf’s own translation: those details come from historical accounts rather than its lexical meaning.

10. Conclusion

Arabic Ṣ Y F has two established semantic branches: the summer season and inclination or deviation from a course.

The first branch names a period of the year associated with warmth and seasonal activities.

It supplies words for summer residence, places for spending summer, seasonal rain, obtaining provisions, and related activities.

In the second branch, ṣāfa may concern turning aside, as when an arrow misses and goes off target.

The branches should not be reduced to one original meaning without historical evidence.

Early Arabic divisions did not always place ṣayf, qayẓ, and spring within identical boundaries. Its Qur’anic meaning nevertheless clearly denotes summer opposite winter.

Only wa-al-ṣayfi occurs in the Qur’an.

In Quraysh 106:2 it stands beside al-shitāʾ in the discussion of seasonal journeys.

Historical accounts chiefly connect them with trade and travel between Mecca, Yemen, and Syria, but the verse itself names no destination or detailed route.

The surah continues by recalling food and security and calling Quraysh to worship the Lord of this House.

Summer therefore names an actual season here, rather than trade, intense heat, worship, or a particular geographical route.

Brief root meaning for Forqan:

Summer and the warmer part of the year; related uses concern summer residence, travel, and rain. Another branch concerns inclination and deviation from a course.

Meaning in Quraysh 106:2:

Wa-al-ṣayfi: and summer; the warmer season, named alongside winter as the time of Quraysh’s familiar seasonal journeys.
