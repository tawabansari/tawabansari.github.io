The Qur’anic Root KH B T

Qur’anic and lexical forms

(wa-akhbatū)

(al-mukhbitīna)
Al-Ḥajj 22:34; a sound masculine plural active participle, accusative

From low, settled ground to humility, reassurance, and conscious acceptance of truth

The Arabic root KH B T is associated with lowliness, settling, and humility. In early usage, khabt denoted broad, low ground lying below the surrounding elevations. In human usage, ikhbāt describes a person’s turning humbly toward what they regard as true and worthy of acceptance, distancing themselves from arrogance and defiance.

Dictionaries explain ikhbāt through such words as khushūʿ, tawāḍuʿ, khuḍūʿ, and iṭmiʾnān: reverent humility, modesty, submission, and reassurance. These meanings are related but not identical. Khushūʿ can express humility and inward responsiveness; tawāḍuʿ concerns avoiding self-importance; and iṭmiʾnān concerns the heart’s becoming calm and settled.

Qur’anic ikhbāt occurs alongside faith, righteous action, submission to God, and acceptance of truth. It is therefore more than a passing emotion or an outward bodily posture; it concerns a person’s orientation toward God.

The Qur’an uses this root three times: once of people who turn humbly toward their Lord, once in the description mukhbitīn, and once of hearts that become humble before truth after recognizing it.

These uses show that ikhbāt means neither thoughtlessness and blind acceptance nor a calmness devoid of responsiveness or responsibility. In one verse, recognizing truth precedes the heart’s ikhbāt; in another, the humble are characterized by steadfastness, establishing ṣalāh, and giving.

1. Forms and semantic core

KH B T occurs in three Qur’anic verses in two morphological categories—verb and active participle—with three word forms: «وَأَخْبَتُوا» (wa-akhbatū) in Hūd 11:23, «الْمُخْبِتِينَ» (al-mukhbitīna) in Al-Ḥajj 22:34, and «فَتُخْبِتَ» (fa-tukhbita) in Al-Ḥajj 22:54.

All three belong to Form IV. Two are verb forms of «أَخْبَتَ ـ يُخْبِتُ» (akhbata–yukhbitu); the third is the active participle «مُخْبِت» (mukhbit).

This Arabic root family also has other forms.

«خَبْت» (khabt) denotes low, broad, and sometimes desert ground. Its plurals include «خُبُوت» (khubūt) and «أَخْبَات» (akhbāt).

«خَبَتَ المكانُ» (khabata al-makānu) means that a place lay low or became low. «خَبَتَ ذِكْرُهُ» (khabata dhikruhu) is also recorded for someone’s renown or remembrance becoming faint or obscured.

In spatial usage, akhbata can mean going to low ground or alighting there; in human usage, it indicates humility and reverent submission.

«إِخْبَات» (ikhbāt), the Form IV verbal noun, denotes humility and entering a condition of reverent modesty.

A mukhbit is someone characterized by ikhbāt. Some dictionaries also record «خَبْتَة» (khabtah) as humility.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an. The actual Qur’anic forms are only akhbatū, al-mukhbitīna, and tukhbita.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: three occurrences, two morphological categories, three word forms

The root’s basic meaning

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies one basic principle for the root and explains it through khushūʿ, humility. He then describes khabt as “a desert tract without vegetation.” This is one recorded definition, rather than an essential feature of every spatial use of khabt.

Lisān al-ʿArab describes khabt through expressions such as «ما اطمأن من الأرض واتسع» (mā iṭmaʾanna min al-arḍi wa-ittasaʿa): the low, expansive part of the land.

This explanation clarifies iṭmaʾanna in spatial usage. It does not mean that the earth has psychological peace; the ground lies low and level compared with surrounding elevations.

In «أَخْبَتَ إِلَى رَبِّهِ» (akhbata ilā rabbihi), “he turned humbly toward his Lord,” however, the word belongs to human conduct and orientation. Humility, reverent submission, and the heart’s settling before the Lord are involved.

Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī also connects khabt as low ground with ikhbāt as humility, treating the latter as an extension of the former.

The connection is understandable: the land lies below the heights, and a humble person likewise relinquishes a position of arrogance and self-importance.

This image should not become a categorical historical rule asserting that every psychological meaning of ikhbāt necessarily originated in observation of the land. Linguistic evidence establishes the existence and proximity of these fields, but their precise historical sequence is not easily demonstrated.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Lisān al-ʿArab, and Al-Rāghib’s Mufradāt: low ground and ikhbāt

Together, this evidence suggests that human ikhbāt is humility and reverent submission associated with settling and turning toward an accepted authority or truth.

In the Qur’an, this authority is God and the truth proceeding from Him.

2. Khabt in early Arabic: low and expansive ground

One clear pre-Islamic Arabic use of this root names a kind of terrain.

Khabt denoted broad, low ground that might lie between surrounding hills and elevations.

Dictionaries record different descriptions: sometimes level, sandy ground; sometimes a deep, broad valley; and in some accounts land with little or no vegetation.

These differences matter. Lack of vegetation is not a feature of every tract called khabt. Some lexicographers also mention vegetated valleys.

Khabt should therefore not be translated exclusively as “dry, barren ground.” Lowness and breadth recur across several descriptions.

A pre-Islamic poetic witness: the Muʿallaqah of Imruʾ al-Qays

The pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays says in his Muʿallaqah:

فَلَمَّا أَجَزْنَا سَاحَةَ الْحَيِّ وَانْتَحَىٰ
بِنَا بَطْنُ خَبْتٍ ذِي حِقَافٍ عَقَنْقَلِ

fa-lammā ajaznā sāḥata al-ḥayyi wa-intaḥā / binā baṭnu khabtin dhī ḥiqāfin ʿaqanqali

Translation:

“Then, when we had passed beyond the tribe’s encampment and reached low ground surrounded by densely gathered sand ridges…”

In this line, baṭnu khabtin refers to the low, expansive part of the terrain.

Ḥiqāf are curved sand hills or ridges; ʿaqanqal concerns densely gathered, compacted sand.

The poet leaves the tribe’s encampment and describes terrain beside sandy elevations.

Khabt has no religious or moral meaning here. It describes the natural shape of the land.

The witness matters historically because it occurs in the Muʿallaqah attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays and is recorded in his Dīwān and established commentaries on the Muʿallaqāt.

Al-Zawzanī also explains khabt as low ground. He treats attributing intaḥā to baṭnu khabt as an extended expression, explaining the overall sense as the pair’s reaching such a place. He also records different grammatical analyses of the response to lammā, “when.” The translation therefore presents this isolated line as part of a continuing sentence.

Al-Zawzanī, Sharḥ al-Muʿallaqāt al-Sabʿ: Imruʾ al-Qays’s line and analyses of the lammā construction

Before approaching Qur’anic ikhbāt, we thus have a witness from the pre-Islamic poetic tradition illustrating the root’s spatial meaning.

Does khabt necessarily mean inferior, worthless land?

No.

Being geographically low does not make land worthless.

Low ground may collect water or support vegetation. A level, expansive area may also be suitable for travel, settlement, or passage.

The spatial description “low” should therefore not make human ikhbāt mean self-degradation, denying human worth, or submitting to other people’s oppression.

Just as a mountain’s height is not inherently arrogance, low ground is not inherently a moral virtue.

The connection between these uses is linguistic and figurative. The moral value of Qur’anic ikhbāt is known from its object and the conduct of those described by it.

Akhbata al-qawmu: reaching low ground

Dictionaries use «أَخْبَتَ القَوْمُ» (akhbata al-qawmu) when a group reaches low ground or alights there.

This construction can be compared with aṣḥara and anjada, which in some uses mean entering the desert or the land of Najd.

Here Form IV concerns being in a place or reaching it.

In the Qur’anic «أَخْبَتُوا إِلَى رَبِّهِمْ» (akhbatū ilā rabbihim), however, the word cannot mean physically travelling to a low tract of land.

The verb instead concerns the human relationship with God.

Knowing the spatial meaning illuminates the figurative background, but the verse’s meaning must be established through its own sentence and the recognized use of ikhbāt.

Renown becoming obscured

Lisān al-ʿArab also records khabata dhikruhu: someone’s remembrance or renown became faint or obscured.

Something previously apparent or prominent no longer has its former visibility in this use.

This meaning is figuratively related to lowliness, but it does not imply that every humble person must be unknown, isolated, or without social standing.

Someone may be publicly known and hold responsibilities while remaining humble before truth.

The fading of renown is therefore one lexical use of the family, rather than the definition of ikhbāt praised in the Qur’an.

3. Hūd 11:23: humble turning toward the Lord

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَعَمِلُوا الصَّالِحَاتِ وَأَخْبَتُوا إِلَىٰ رَبِّهِمْ أُولَٰئِكَ أَصْحَابُ الْجَنَّةِ هُمْ فِيهَا خَالِدُونَ

inna alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti wa-akhbatū ilā rabbihim ulāʾika aṣḥābu al-jannati hum fīhā khālidūna

Proposed translation for this study:

“Indeed, those who believe, do righteous deeds, and turn toward their Lord with humility and reassurance are the people of the Garden; they will abide therein.”

Hūd 11:23

Here ikhbāt accompanies faith and righteous action.

Three characteristics stand together: believing, doing righteous deeds, and turning humbly toward the Lord.

This matters because ikhbāt replaces neither faith nor righteous action. All three describe the group identified as the people of the Garden.

Wa-akhbatū ilā rabbihim

Here akhbatū occurs with the preposition ilā, “toward.”

Arabic explains akhbata ilayhi through finding reassurance in someone and turning humbly toward them.

Ilā rabbihim, “toward their Lord,” thus specifies the direction of this humility and reassurance.

This orientation should not become bodily movement from one place to another. The concern is the human relationship with one’s Lord.

“They turned toward their Lord with humility and reassurance” conveys the sentence’s natural meaning in this context.

Ikhbāt and inābah should nevertheless be distinguished.

Some early explanations interpret ikhbāt through inābah, turning back. The concepts may be close without being identical.

Inābah emphasizes returning and turning toward, whereas ikhbāt highlights humility and settling within that orientation.

In Hūd 11:23, ikhbāt occurs with ilā, making its orientation toward the Lord explicit.

Al-Ṭabarī: humility, modesty, turning back, reassurance, and the preposition accompanying ikhbāt

Does ikhbāt mean inactivity?

Its occurrence with ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāt, “they did righteous deeds,” limits this interpretation.

Those praised in the verse have done more than sit quietly; they have performed righteous actions.

Settling before the Lord is therefore compatible with activity and practical responsibility.

Someone may be humble before truth while acting to correct an error, defend an oppressed person, or fulfil a responsibility.

These examples are not the exclusive lexical meaning of KH B T. They illustrate why humility before God should not be equated with abandoning responsibility or lacking a will.

Nor is every kind of calm ikhbāt. Someone may show no concern or response because they are indifferent to injustice and corruption.

Simply being calm does not make that condition an instance of the ikhbāt praised in the Qur’an.

In this verse, humble turning toward the Lord accompanies faith and righteous action. This association clarifies its orientation and value.

4. Al-Ḥajj 22:34: who are the humble?

وَلِكُلِّ أُمَّةٍ جَعَلْنَا مَنْسَكًا لِيَذْكُرُوا اسْمَ اللَّهِ عَلَىٰ مَا رَزَقَهُمْ مِنْ بَهِيمَةِ الْأَنْعَامِ فَإِلَٰهُكُمْ إِلَٰهٌ وَاحِدٌ فَلَهُ أَسْلِمُوا وَبَشِّرِ الْمُخْبِتِينَ

wa-li-kulli ummatin jaʿalnā mansakan li-yadhkurū isma allāhi ʿalā mā razaqahum min bahīmati al-anʿāmi fa-ilāhukum ilāhun wāḥidun fa-lahu aslimū wa-bashshiri al-mukhbitīna

Proposed translation for this study:

“For every community We appointed a rite, so that they may mention the name of The God over the livestock He has provided them. Your deity is one deity; so submit to Him alone, and give glad tidings to the humble.”

Al-Ḥajj 22:34

Here ikhbāt occurs in the context of rites, remembrance of God, and submission to the one deity.

«فَإِلَٰهُكُمْ إِلَٰهٌ وَاحِدٌ» (fa-ilāhukum ilāhun wāḥidun), “your deity is one deity,” emphasizes the deity’s oneness.

The command «فَلَهُ أَسْلِمُوا» (fa-lahu aslimū), “so submit to Him,” follows, and the verse ends by announcing glad tidings for the humble.

This sequence shows the relationship between ikhbāt and submission to God in this context.

A mukhbit is not merely someone who performs outward rites, however. The next verse explains the group’s characteristics.

Their characteristics in the following verse

الَّذِينَ إِذَا ذُكِرَ اللَّهُ وَجِلَتْ قُلُوبُهُمْ وَالصَّابِرِينَ عَلَىٰ مَا أَصَابَهُمْ وَالْمُقِيمِي الصَّلَاةِ وَمِمَّا رَزَقْنَاهُمْ يُنْفِقُونَ

alladhīna idhā dhukira allāhu wajilat qulūbuhum wa-al-ṣābirīna ʿalā mā aṣābahum wa-al-muqīmī al-ṣalāti wa-mimmā razaqnāhum yunfiqūna

Proposed translation for this study:

“Those whose hearts tremble when The God is remembered, who remain steadfast through what befalls them, who establish ṣalāh, and who give from what We have provided them.”

Al-Ḥajj 22:35

The verse describes the humble through four characteristics.

First, their hearts experience wajal, trembling apprehension, when God is remembered.

Second, they remain steadfast through what befalls them.

Third, they establish ṣalāh.

Fourth, they give from what God has provided them.

These characteristics help identify Qur’anic ikhbāt, but should not all become the lexical meaning of mukhbit itself.

Wajal comes from W J L, ṣabr from Ṣ B R, ṣalāh from Ṣ L W, and infāq from N F Q.

The Qur’an describes the humble through these actions and conditions, but each word has its own semantic field.

Are reassurance and wajal incompatible?

At first glance, someone who feels assured might appear unable to experience apprehension and emotional responsiveness.

The two conditions, however, are not necessarily opposites.

Reassurance in God can mean trust and the heart’s settling in its relationship with Him. Wajal at His remembrance can arise from awareness of responsibility, divine greatness, and fear of opposing truth.

A person may therefore be steadfast in trusting God while remaining responsive to their conduct and responsibilities when remembering Him.

Calmness here does not mean the absence of all emotion. Ikhbāt concerns humility and reassurance before the Lord, rather than numbness or the absence of any concern.

Al-Ṭabarī: apprehensive hearts, steadfastness, ṣalāh, and giving as characteristics of the humble

Al-Ḥajj 22:35 consequently prevents translating mukhbit merely as “calm and free of anxiety.”

Ikhbāt and steadfastness

The same verse also characterizes the humble through ṣabr, steadfastness.

This association shows a relationship between humility before God and remaining steadfast through difficulty.

Ṣabr and ikhbāt, however, are different words.

Ṣabr concerns restraint and perseverance, whereas ikhbāt here expresses a person’s humble condition and orientation before God.

That orientation may sustain a person in doing what is right, but perseverance is an outcome or companion of ikhbāt, rather than its complete definition.

Nor should the verse imply that the humble must endure every suffering or injustice without objection.

Standing firm in truth differs from inactivity in the face of oppression.

This matter is examined in detail in the separate study Ṣabr in the Qur’an: Patience, Steadfastness, and What Makes Them Worthwhile.

Ikhbāt and social conduct

Al-Ḥajj 22:35 ends by describing the humble as people who give from their provision.

This shows that the Qur’an’s praised ikhbāt is more than an inward condition detached from social life.

Humility before God accompanies responsibility toward others.

Someone might, for example, speak very modestly but consider only their own interests when it comes to helping those in need or respecting others’ rights.

An outwardly humble manner alone cannot equate that conduct with the combined characteristics of the humble in this verse.

At the same time, ikhbāt itself does not mean giving. The Qur’an places the two together as a related quality and action.

Ikhbāt and rites

Al-Ḥajj 22:34 concerns rites, the provision of livestock, and remembrance of God’s name.

Its ending, however, emphasizes the one deity and announces glad tidings to the humble, showing that rites are connected with a person’s orientation toward divine oneness.

This connection becomes clearer in Al-Ḥajj 22:37, which denies that flesh and blood reach God and highlights human mindfulness of Him.

Merely performing an outward action without considering one’s orientation and responsibility therefore does not express the whole meaning of servanthood.

This conclusion nevertheless does not deny the rites themselves or reduce them to purely mental symbols.

The verse speaks of actual rites while relating them to remembrance of God and submission to Him.

5. Al-Ḥajj 22:54: the heart’s humility after recognizing truth

وَلِيَعْلَمَ الَّذِينَ أُوتُوا الْعِلْمَ أَنَّهُ الْحَقُّ مِنْ رَبِّكَ فَيُؤْمِنُوا بِهِ فَتُخْبِتَ لَهُ قُلُوبُهُمْ وَإِنَّ اللَّهَ لَهَادِ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِلَىٰ صِرَاطٍ مُسْتَقِيمٍ

wa-li-yaʿlama alladhīna ūtū al-ʿilma annahu al-ḥaqqu min rabbika fa-yuʾminū bihi fa-tukhbita lahu qulūbuhum wa-inna allāha la-hādi alladhīna āmanū ilā ṣirāṭin mustaqīmin

Proposed translation for this study:

“And so that those given knowledge may know that it is the truth from your Lord, believe in it, and their hearts become humble and settled before it. Indeed, The God guides those who believe to a straight path.”

Al-Ḥajj 22:54

This verse is especially important for understanding ikhbāt because the verb is explicitly attributed to hearts:

«فَتُخْبِتَ لَهُ قُلُوبُهُمْ» (fa-tukhbita lahu qulūbuhum), “and their hearts may humbly submit to it.”

That is, their hearts become humble and settled before it.

Ikhbāt here differs in construction from the previous two uses.

In Hūd 11:23, the believers themselves are its subject.

In Al-Ḥajj 22:34, a group is described as “the humble.”

In Al-Ḥajj 22:54, however, qulūbuhum, “their hearts,” is the subject of tukhbita.

Ikhbāt can therefore describe a person’s orientation and also be used specifically for the heart’s condition.

Knowledge, faith, and ikhbāt

Several events are connected in this verse.

First, those given knowledge recognize the truth of what comes from the Lord.

They then believe in it.

Their hearts subsequently become humble before it.

This sequence matters.

Here humility before truth is not presented without knowledge and faith. Recognizing truth and believing precede the heart’s ikhbāt.

The verse therefore does not make the virtue of ikhbāt mean disabling reason, disregarding evidence, or accepting every claim without examination.

In context, its object is something recognized as truth from the Lord.

This recognition should not, however, mean knowing every detail of the universe or having a definitive answer to every question.

The verse concerns recognizing the truth of what has come from the Lord, followed by faith and the heart’s humble response.

Ikhbāt here is therefore humble acceptance of truth after recognizing it, rather than a substitute for recognition.

The humble heart and the hardened heart

The preceding verse, Al-Ḥajj 22:53, speaks of those with disease in their hearts and those whose hearts have hardened.

Al-Ḥajj 22:54, by contrast, presents those who recognize truth, believe in it, and whose hearts become humble.

This contrast matters.

A hardened heart in this context lacks receptiveness to truth, whereas a humble heart becomes lowly before truth after recognizing it.

Ikhbāt can thus be understood as connected with abandoning defiance and unjustified resistance to truth.

This does not make everyone who still has a question or asks for evidence hard-hearted.

Questioning and investigation can be part of reaching understanding.

The issue is how someone responds once truth becomes clear: do they accept it and align themselves with it, or resist because of arrogance, interests, and attachments?

This question arises from the network of verses concerning knowledge, faith, and hardness of heart, rather than tukhbita alone containing all these concepts.

Fa-tukhbita lahu: humble before what?

Here ikhbāt occurs with the preposition li-.

Hūd 11:23 used akhbatū ilā rabbihim, “they turned humbly toward their Lord.”

Al-Ḥajj 22:54 uses fa-tukhbita lahu qulūbuhum, “and their hearts may humbly submit to it.”

The difference deserves attention.

Dictionaries explain akhbata ilayhi through reassurance and turning toward someone, and akhbata lahu through reverent humility before someone.

The different prepositions should not establish an absolute rule that one always means only reassurance and the other only humility.

These semantic fields can overlap in Arabic, and context matters.

In Al-Ḥajj 22:54, lahu, “to it,” can be related to the truth mentioned at the verse’s beginning. On this reading, believers’ hearts become humble before the truth they have recognized.

Exegetical explanations express this through accepting divine verses and being humble before the Qur’an.

Al-Ṭabarī: hearts becoming humble before the Qur’an and accepting it

Since the verse describes the truth as min rabbika, “from your Lord,” humility before divine truth remains connected with orientation toward God.

A direct translation should retain this connection without replacing the pronoun with lengthy explanatory additions.

Is ikhbāt a pleasant feeling and permanent calm?

The verse gives no such definition.

A heart may become humble before truth while still experiencing concern and apprehension when confronting responsibility, error, or the consequences of its actions.

Someone may also face fear and external pressure in fulfilling a difficult duty while remaining steadfast before God.

Ikhbāt should therefore not be reduced to a fixed, permanent psychological state without any anxiety, fear, or questions.

Al-Ḥajj 22:54 highlights the heart’s receptiveness and humility before recognized truth.

This condition may accompany deeper understanding, corrected conduct, and practical fidelity to truth, but these outcomes should not all become independent lexical meanings of tukhbita.

6. Distinctions from related words

KH B T (ikhbāt) and KH SH ʿ (khushūʿ): humility before truth and reverent submission

Qur’anic khushūʿ concerns humility and inward responsiveness that may also appear in conduct and outward posture.

Dictionaries also repeatedly explain ikhbāt through khushūʿ.

This proximity is real, but the Qur’anic uses of KH B T highlight orientation toward the Lord and the heart’s humility before truth.

Khushūʿ is therefore a valid equivalent for ikhbāt, but does not always make the full role of ilā and li- explicit in Qur’anic constructions.

In Hūd 11:23, ilā rabbihim draws attention to the direction of this humility.

Al-Ḥajj 22:54 likewise describes the heart before truth through tukhbita lahu qulūbuhum.

This does not mean that khushūʿ cannot be directed toward God or that ikhbāt never becomes outwardly apparent. The words’ semantic fields overlap.

Study of KH SH ʿ

KH B T (ikhbāt) and KH Ḍ ʿ (khuḍūʿ): humility and submission

Khuḍūʿ concerns lowering oneself and submitting before another.

This may arise from respect, acceptance of truth, or external pressure and power.

All three Qur’anic uses of ikhbāt, however, occur in contexts of praise and relate to faith, submission to God, or acceptance of truth.

This difference matters, but should not make the lexical construction of ikhbāt inherently morally positive in every historical use.

Its orientation and context are clear in the Qur’an.

Someone who bows before a powerful person out of fear may appear submissive, but that conduct alone does not establish the ikhbāt praised in the Qur’an.

Ikhbāt in the verses examined concerns the Lord, truth, and faith.

KH B T (ikhbāt) and Ṭ M A N / Ṭ M N (ṭumaʾnīnah): settling and peace of heart

Qur’anic iṭmiʾnān concerns becoming calm, settled, and free of restlessness. The family’s morphological analysis is disputed: some examine it through the triliteral Ṭ M N, including the Quranic Arabic Corpus. Sources such as Al-Miṣbāḥ al-Munīr use the entry Ṭ M Hamzah N, and Forqan’s index uses Ṭ M A N. This difference in analysis should not be confused with a categorical error in the meaning of reassurance.

Al-Miṣbāḥ al-Munīr: Ṭ M Hamzah N and differing analyses of the hamzah

Lexicographers explain khabt through al-arḍ al-muṭmaʾinnah, “low, level ground.”

The land’s settling differs from human psychological reassurance.

In human usage, ikhbāt can accompany the heart’s settling before God.

Iṭmiʾnān alone does not always express humility before truth.

Someone may feel assured of something false; trust and calm alone do not establish that the belief is correct.

Qur’anic ikhbāt has a specified object: the Lord and divine truth.

Reassurance is therefore an appropriate element in explaining ikhbāt, but using it alone without humility and orientation may restrict the meaning.

KH B T (ikhbāt) and N W B (inābah): humility and returning

Inābah concerns returning and turning toward; in religious usage, returning to God. It is the verbal noun of anāba, from N W B: the initial hamzah marks Form IV and is not part of the root.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: anāba and inābah from N W B

Some early explanations interpret akhbatū ilā rabbihim through anābū, “they turned back.”

This proximity is understandable because someone turning toward God may abandon defiance and self-centeredness.

Inābah highlights returning and turning toward, whereas ikhbāt concerns humility and settling within this relationship.

Someone might, for example, decide to return after recognizing an error. That return can be examined within the field of inābah.

Becoming humble before truth after returning, and no longer placing oneself above correction and acceptance of truth, is closer to the semantic field of ikhbāt.

This example clarifies the conceptual distinction; it does not imply that the Qur’an always divides the two into fixed successive stages.

KH B T (ikhbāt) and S L M (islām): humility and submission

In Al-Ḥajj 22:34, fa-lahu aslimū, “so submit to Him,” immediately precedes the glad tidings to the humble.

This association shows a close relationship between ikhbāt and submission to God.

Islām concerns submitting, whereas ikhbāt highlights humility and reverent receptiveness before the same authority.

The concepts may come together in a believer’s conduct, but neither should be wholly absorbed into the other.

The verse itself uses two separate words: a command to submit and a description of those receiving glad tidings.

Study of S L M

KH B T (ikhbāt) and KH B W (khubūw): humility and a fire’s subsiding

Certain written forms of KH B T and KH B W may be confused.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:97, khabat concerns a fire’s subsiding.

That verb, however, belongs to KH B W rather than KH B T.

KH B W concerns a flame’s dying down or subsiding, whereas Qur’anic KH B T concerns humility and reverent submission.

Akhbatū and tukhbita should therefore not be translated as the heart’s being extinguished, the destruction of human capacity, or the annihilation of the will.

These are independent root families; outward similarity does not justify identifying them.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

“Humility,” “reverent submission,” and, where appropriate, “settling before God” are defensible equivalents for Qur’anic ikhbāt.

None is equally fluent or complete in every sentence.

In Hūd 11:23, wa-akhbatū ilā rabbihim expresses orientation toward the Lord. “They turned toward their Lord with humility and reassurance” is therefore appropriate.

In Al-Ḥajj 22:34, al-mukhbitīna names a group of believers. “The humble” is concise and natural; an explanatory note can clarify the orientation as “those who are humble before God.”

“The humble” suffices in the direct translation, while the next verse explains their characteristics.

In Al-Ḥajj 22:54, fa-tukhbita lahu qulūbuhum concerns the heart’s condition. “Their hearts become humble and settled before it” fits the context of recognizing truth.

“They feel reassured” alone does not convey ikhbāt fully here, because it weakens the heart’s humility before truth.

Conversely, the Persian expression “their hearts break” may suggest defeat, sorrow, or humiliation, making it unsuitable for direct translation.

“They obey without question” also adds something the verb does not itself express, especially since recognizing truth precedes ikhbāt in Al-Ḥajj 22:54.

Distinguishing the root meaning from each contextual use

وَأَخْبَتُوا (wa-akhbatū) — Hūd 11:23

Root: KH B T.

Grammatical form: A third-person masculine plural perfect verb, Form IV.

Brief root meaning: Humility, reverent submission, and settling before something.

Meaning in this verse: They turned toward their Lord with humility and reassurance.

الْمُخْبِتِينَ (al-mukhbitīna) — Al-Ḥajj 22:34

Root: KH B T.

Grammatical form: A sound masculine plural active participle, Form IV, accusative.

Brief root meaning: Humble and reverently submissive.

Meaning in this verse: Those who are humble before God and receive glad tidings in this context.

فَتُخْبِتَ (fa-tukhbita) — Al-Ḥajj 22:54

Root: KH B T.

Grammatical form: A third-person feminine singular imperfect verb, Form IV, subjunctive; its subject is qulūbuhum, “their hearts.”

Brief root meaning: Becoming humble and settled before something.

Meaning in this verse: Their hearts become humble before the truth they have recognized as coming from the Lord.

These three uses share a semantic core, while the subject, preposition, and context clarify their precise orientation.

8. Conclusion

The Arabic root KH B T has two important related uses: khabt for low, expansive ground and ikhbāt for human humility and reverent submission.

The pre-Islamic witness attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays illustrates the root’s spatial meaning in the early Arabic tradition. Dictionaries also explain ikhbāt through humility, modesty, and reassurance.

The Qur’an uses the root three times, always in a context of praise.

In Hūd 11:23, ikhbāt accompanies faith and righteous action, oriented toward the Lord. Al-Ḥajj 22:34 announces glad tidings to the humble, and Al-Ḥajj 22:35 characterizes them through apprehensive hearts, steadfastness, establishing ṣalāh, and giving. In Al-Ḥajj 22:54, the heart’s humility follows recognizing truth and believing in it.

These uses make the Qur’an’s praised ikhbāt neither irresponsible calm nor blind submission to every claim or powerful person. Nor does it mean human degradation, destruction of the will, or refraining from questions and understanding.

Ikhbāt concerns humility before truth, which can accompany reassurance, responsiveness of heart, and responsible conduct.

Final definition: In spatial use, KH B T concerns low, expansive ground; in human ikhbāt, it indicates humility and reverent submission associated with settling and turning toward. In the Qur’an, ikhbāt denotes a human condition and orientation before God and divine truth, accompanying faith, acceptance of truth, and worthy conduct. Brief root meaning: Low, expansive ground; ikhbāt: humility and reverent submission, with turning toward and settling as the context indicates.
