Fruit stones, intention, departure, and distance: two early Arabic semantic branches and Qur’anic al-nawā

N W Y has two important Arabic branches that initially appear different: intending and turning toward a particular destination, and the stones of dates and other fruits.

The first concerns voluntary orientation: envisaging a destination, deciding to travel, or intending an act. It includes niyyah, nawā, intawā, and expressions of departure and distance.

The second concerns nawāh, the hard inner part of fruits such as dates, apricots, and peaches. Its plural or collective generic noun is nawā.

One lexical definition explicitly records two meanings:

أَحَدُهُمَا مَقْصِدٌ لِشَيْءٍ، وَالْآخَرُ عَجَمُ شَيْءٍ

aḥaduhumā maqṣidun li-shayʾin wa-al-ākharu ʿajamu shayʾin

Meaning: “One is a purpose directed toward something; the other is a hard inner kernel.”

This distinction matters. Without historical evidence, intention should not be derived from fruit stones, or stones named from intending and direction. Shared morphological roots alone establish neither relationship.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an: wa-al-nawā in Al-Anʿām 6:95. There it means fruit stones, not intention, purpose, migration, or distance. Niyyah, nawaytu, and intawā are known in Arabic but do not occur in the Qur’anic text.

1. Semantic cores in early dictionaries

Intention, destination, and movement

One explanation of the first branch discusses moving from one dwelling to another, then gives نَوَى الأَمْرَ يَنْوِيهِ for intending an act.

Another explains nawā as a destination someone intends, whether travel or another undertaking.

Three related but distinct concepts appear: intending, the intended destination, and moving toward it.

Someone may intend to travel without having begun. Intention exists, but movement has not occurred. Intending and performing an act should remain distinct.

In departure-related uses, nawā can name the destination or distance from a previous dwelling. Travel and nomadic life make the extension understandable: a new destination often involves leaving the old place behind.

Distance and separation are not inherent in every intention. Someone may intend an act where they already live, without spatial movement.

Date stones and fruit stones

The second branch is nawāh, a fruit stone.

Dictionaries record it for the hard inner part of dates and other fruits; nawā denotes these collectively.

An interpretation of Al-Anʿām 6:95 distinguishes ḥabb from nawā: grains such as cereals in the first case, fruit stones from which trees grow in the second.

Botanically, what is called a stone does not have identical anatomy in every fruit. In fruits such as apricots, a hard fruit layer encloses the seed, whereas a date stone is the seed itself. The Arabic lexical definition should therefore not be rewritten as one uniform, precise modern botanical category.

Understanding the verse requires recognizing familiar fruit stones, particularly dates and similar fruits.

Nawā itself does not mean sprouting, splitting, or becoming alive in this branch. Other sentence elements express those actions.

Do the branches share one meaning?

A fruit stone holds the possibility of future growth, while intention directs a person toward the future. That can be a literary comparison, but it is not evidence of the word’s historical origin.

The cited lexical definition records the branches separately, and the examined evidence does not establish their historical connection.

Forqan’s lexical study should therefore not define N W Y through “intention hidden in the stone,” “the seed’s inner goal,” or “nature’s intention to grow.”

Such comparisons may appear as acknowledged imagery in reflective writing but cannot replace etymological investigation.

2. Important forms and derivatives

Nawā yanwī: to intend

Nawā is a Form I verb with a final yāʾ radical; its imperfect is yanwī.

In the branch concerning will and decision, it means intending or envisaging an act.

نَوَى السَّفَرَ means he intended to travel; نَوَى الأَمْرَ means he intended an undertaking.

The verb concerns intention and decision, not necessarily completion of the act.

Niyyah: intention and orientation

Niyyah belongs to the same family and denotes intention or something a person envisages doing.

An intention may concern good or bad. The word does not inherently mean purity, correctness, or moral approval.

One lexical definition describes what a person inwardly intends, whether good or evil.

Intention is therefore distinct from its moral value. Two people may perform outwardly similar acts for different purposes. Their evaluation depends on content and ethical criteria.

Intawā: to intend and depart

Intawā is Form VIII.

It is recorded for intending and for departing from one territory to another.

اِنْتَوَى الْقَوْمُ may describe a group leaving its place of residence.

Form VIII alone does not make the journey necessarily long, forced, or permanent.

Nāwin and manwiyy

Nāwin is an active participle: someone intending, including someone intending departure.

Manwiyy is a passive participle: something intended.

Early uses particularly apply nāwin to someone resolved to depart or separate. That feature comes from the travel context, not an invariable requirement of the participle.

Nawiyy: a companion in intention or travel

Nawiyy is recorded for a companion, particularly a fellow traveler.

The explanation connects two people through intention and destination.

This fits the intention branch: a shared destination may make people companions.

It does not require every use to imply a formal agreement or completely identical decisions.

Anwā: becoming distant and traveling much

Early Arabic records anwā for becoming distant and traveling extensively.

This belongs to departure and movement.

Anwā is not restricted to those senses: related forms also occur in the fruit-stone branch. Objects and context determine the meaning.

Nawāh, nawā, and nawayāt: a stone and stones

Nawāh names one fruit stone.

Nawā is its plural or collective generic noun; nawayāt is also used for several stones.

Nuwiyy and niwiyy are recorded plurals as well.

Anwāʾ is also reported as a plural of the collective nawā. It must remain distinct from astronomical anwāʾ belonging to N W ʾ.

Nawat al-busrah: a stone forming

نَوَتِ البُسْرَةُ and أَنْوَتْ describe an unripe date forming its stone.

The verb here concerns the stone, not intention.

نَوَيْتُ النَّوَى is also recorded for separating, collecting, or discarding date stones after eating. Reports differ over collecting versus throwing them away.

Even one semantic branch does not reduce every verbal use to one particular movement.

3. Secondary and rare uses

People and animals becoming fat

نَوَتِ النَّاقَةُ تَنْوِي describes a she-camel becoming fat.

Al-nayy concerns fat, while al-niyy concerns fatness; niwāʾ describes fat camels.

The use is also recorded for male camels and other animals.

Shared roots alone do not establish that fattening came from eating date stones, or that intention originally meant accumulating fat.

These are recorded branches whose precise historical relationship requires independent evidence.

Nawāh as a weight unit

Nawāh also names a quantity of weight or value of precious metals.

One lexical explanation gives its weight as five dirhams.

Other transmissions record the same amount, but differing values and quantities also appear.

It should remain a historical measurement term, without assigning it a fixed modern weight or importing it into Qur’anic nawā.

An expression of protection

Some dictionaries record نَوَاهُ اللَّهُ for The God protecting someone.

One analysis connects it with intending, as though the person receives directed attention and protection.

Lexical transmissions also express doubt about the use’s validity.

It can be reported without treating it as certain evidence that nawā extends to divine protection.

Other uses

Some material records nawāh for a need or want, understandably related to intending and pursuing an object.

Nawā also names a part of female genital anatomy in a rare use. It has no direct, clear bearing on the Qur’anic fruit-stone sense.

Nawā is also a place name in some texts. Identical proper names should not automatically count as semantic derivatives.

These uses demonstrate lexical breadth, but do not contribute equally to understanding the Qur’anic occurrence.

4. Early Arabic poetry and language

Intention, departure, and settling in a verse attributed to Muʿaqqir ibn Ḥimār

A line attributed to Muʿaqqir ibn Ḥimār al-Bāriqī and other poets says:

فَأَلْقَتْ عَصَاهَا وَاسْتَقَرَّ بِهَا النَّوَى
كَمَا قَرَّ عَيْنًا بِالإِيَابِ الْمُسَافِرُ

fa-alqat ʿaṣāhā wa-istaqarra bihā al-nawā / ka-mā qarra ʿaynan bi-al-iyābi al-musāfiru

The sense: “She laid down her traveling staff, and her destination settled into residence, as a traveler’s eye finds contentment on returning.”

Al-nawā denotes a destination where travel ends in residence.

It therefore does not mean permanent distance here. A traveler eventually arrives and rests.

The witness supports destination and intended direction.

Attribution varies. Al-Ṭirimmāḥ and other poets appear alongside Muʿaqqir ibn Ḥimār in the transmissions. Istaqarra and istaqarrat are also variant readings.

The line is established in lexical and literary tradition, but attribution to a single poet is not certain.

Intention in a poem attributed to al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī

A line attributed to al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī says:

إِنَّكَ أَنْتَ الْمَحْزُونُ فِي أَثَرِ الْحَيِّ
فَإِنْ تَنْوِ نِيَّهُمْ تُقِمِ

innaka anta al-maḥzūnu fī athari al-ḥayyi / fa-in tanwi niyyahum tuqimi

In one interpretation: “You grieve in the people’s wake; if you intend the same separation they intended, you will stay where you are.”

The line matters because niyy and niyyah have been discussed in explaining it.

An early lexical debate transmits disagreement over this line.

One reading understands: if you intend to follow the group, you will set the camels moving toward them.

Another understands: if you intend the same separation and severance they intended, you will remain at home and not pursue them.

Tanwi alone is therefore insufficient: niyyahum, tuqim, and the poetic situation must also be considered.

Niyy is treated here as a rare form that may be a plural of niyyah.

Al-Nābighah lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. Without dating this line, it should not certainly be presented as pre-Islamic evidence.

Broken fruit stones in Mulayḥ al-Hudhalī’s poetry

Mulayḥ al-Hudhalī says:

مُنِيرٍ تَجُوزُ الْعِيسُ مِنْ بَطِنَاتِهِ
حَصًى مِثْلَ أَنْوَاءِ الرَّضِيخِ الْمُفَلَّقِ

munīrin tajūzu al-ʿīsu min baṭinātihi / ḥaṣan mithla anwāʾi al-raḍīkhi al-mufallaqi

“[A bright tract] where pale camels pass along its inner ways over pebbles like crushed, split fruit stones.”

The verse compares pebbles to crushed, split fruit stones.

Anwāʾ belongs to fruit stones here, not the astronomical expression anwāʾ al-maṭar.

The witness shows the concrete stone sense in a poetic image: hard, broken pieces resembling pebbles in shape.

The line occurs in collected Hudhalī poetry and lexical transmissions.

Mulayḥ is known as an early Islamic poet, so the line should not be called certainly pre-Islamic.

What the witnesses establish

The evidence supports two distinct branches.

One concerns intention, movement toward a destination, departure, and separation.

The other concerns tangible fruit stones that can be collected, broken, or discarded.

The witnesses establish both meanings, without settling their historical relationship.

5. Qur’anic al-nawā in Al-Anʿām 6:95

Al-Anʿām 6:95

إِنَّ اللَّهَ فَالِقُ الْحَبِّ وَالنَّوَىٰ ۖ يُخْرِجُ الْحَيَّ مِنَ الْمَيِّتِ وَمُخْرِجُ الْمَيِّتِ مِنَ الْحَيِّ ۚ ذَٰلِكُمُ اللَّهُ ۖ فَأَنَّىٰ تُؤْفَكُونَ

inna allāha fāliqu al-ḥabbi wa-al-nawā yukhriju al-ḥayya mina al-mayyiti wa-mukhriju al-mayyiti mina al-ḥayyi dhālikumu allāhu fa-annā tuʾfakūna

Forqan’s installed Persian translation is equivalent to:

“Indeed, The God is the splitter of grain and fruit stones. He brings the living from the dead and brings the dead from the living. That is The God; how then are you turned away?”

The Persian translation is installed on Forqan’s al-Anʿām page, and its “fruit stone” equivalent agrees with the lexical evidence.

Morphology and syntax

Wa-al-nawā consists of the conjunction wa and the definite noun al-nawā.

It is a noun, not nawā, the verb meaning “he intended.”

Al-nawā is coordinated with al-ḥabbi and is genitive; both depend on fāliqu.

In number, nawā denotes stones. It is explained as a plural of nawāh or a collective generic noun, with an individual formed by adding tāʾ.

The verse concerns neither one intention nor a destination, but the stones of which The God is called the splitter.

Why are ḥabb and nawā together?

They are nearby but distinct words.

Ḥabb denotes grains such as wheat, barley, and other plant seeds.

Nawā denotes the inner stones of fruits such as dates and apricots.

This is not a division into two completely separate modern botanical groups. They are familiar Arabic names for seed-like and stone-like structures.

Both stand beside the description fāliq in Al-Anʿām 6:95.

Fāliq is an active participle from F L Q meaning splitter. Splitting belongs to fāliq, not to ḥabb or nawā themselves.

“Being split” should therefore not be inserted into the stone’s lexical definition.

Interpretive evidence concerning fāliq

Interpretations transmit several views.

Some relate splitting grain and stones to plants and trees growing.

Others understand falq as the split already present in a grain or stone.

Another report explains fāliq as creator.

One assessment prefers splitting that brings forth a plant, while treating the direct substitution of creation for splitting cautiously because the verbs are not lexically identical.

The N W Y study needs this debate only to clarify the construction. A fuller treatment belongs to F L Q.

Study of F L Q

Stones, life, and sprouting

After fāliqu al-ḥabbi wa-al-nawā, the verse says:

يُخْرِجُ الْحَيَّ مِنَ الْمَيِّتِ

yukhriju al-ḥayya mina al-mayyiti

“He brings the living from the dead.”

The sequence makes a living plant emerging from grain or a stone an understandable instance.

A scientific and linguistic distinction is needed here.

A viable stone or seed is not necessarily biologically dead: it may contain a living embryo in a dormant or low-activity state.

The verse should not establish that grains and stones are always biologically dead before becoming alive.

“Bringing the living from the dead” has a wider Qur’anic range requiring studies of Ḥ Y Y and M W T.

The clear point here is that The God presents plant life emerging from grain and stones as a sign of His power and ordering.

The nearby context

Al-Anʿām 6:96 calls The God fāliqu al-iṣbāḥ, the splitter of dawn.

It shares F L Q with fāliqu al-ḥabbi wa-al-nawā.

Al-nawā in Al-Anʿām 6:95 nevertheless remains fruit stones; the connection with dawn does not turn it into light, awareness, or awakening.

Al-Anʿām 6:99 discusses water descending, plants, clustered grains, date palms, grapes, olives, and pomegranates.

It clarifies the natural, plant-related setting of the expression.

A direct reading of stones and grain therefore fits the surah; sufficient evidence for replacing it with intention is absent.

6. Connections with other Qur’anic verses

Plants emerging from the earth

Yā Sīn 36:33 says:

وَآيَةٌ لَهُمُ الْأَرْضُ الْمَيْتَةُ أَحْيَيْنَاهَا وَأَخْرَجْنَا مِنْهَا حَبًّا فَمِنْهُ يَأْكُلُونَ

wa-āyatun lahumu al-arḍu al-maytatu aḥyaynāhā wa-akhrajnā minhā ḥabban fa-minhu yaʾkulūna

“The dead earth is a sign for them. We gave it life and brought grain from it, from which they eat.”

The word is ḥabb, not nawā.

Plant growth and food emerging from the earth nevertheless connect with Al-Anʿām 6:95.

The following verses, Yā Sīn 36:34 and Yā Sīn 36:35, mention gardens of palms and grapes and fruit people enjoy.

Al-Anʿām 6:95 can thus be studied among verses about growth, land, and food without making every word cognate or synonymous.

What people sow

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:63 and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:64 say:

أَفَرَأَيْتُمْ مَا تَحْرُثُونَ
أَأَنْتُمْ تَزْرَعُونَهُ أَمْ نَحْنُ الزَّارِعُونَ

a-fa-raʾaytum mā taḥruthūna / a-antum tazraʿūnahu am naḥnu al-zāriʿūna

“Have you considered what you cultivate? Do you make it grow, or are We the growers?”

Human cultivation is distinguished from the plant’s emergence and growth.

This connects with splitting grain and stones, which participate in growth.

Zarʿ belongs to a different root and is not nawā’s literal meaning.

Grain in an example of giving

Al-Baqarah 2:261 uses ḥabbah in an example about spending.

A grain can thus enter an ethical comparison.

That does not make every mention of grain or stones necessarily figurative.

Al-Anʿām 6:95 directly concerns natural splitting. Turning nawā into a symbol of intention or spiritual growth requires separate evidence.

7. Is intention a Qur’anic concept?

Niyyah from N W Y does not occur in the text, but human purpose and intention appear in other verses.

Al-Kahf 18:28, for example, says:

يُرِيدُونَ وَجْهَهُ

yurīdūna wajhahu

“They seek His countenance.”

Al-Insān 76:9 also says:

إِنَّمَا نُطْعِمُكُمْ لِوَجْهِ اللَّهِ لَا نُرِيدُ مِنْكُمْ جَزَاءً وَلَا شُكُورًا

innamā nuṭʿimukum li-wajhi allāhi lā nurīdu minkum jazāʾan wa-lā shukūran

“We feed you only for The God’s countenance; we seek neither reward nor thanks from you.”

These verses connect an act with its agent’s purpose.

Feeding is outward behavior; why someone does it is a separate question.

Al-Baqarah 2:264 also describes spending to show off before people. Outwardly helpful behavior may therefore have a different purpose and motive.

The verses permit a study of intention and motive in evaluating conduct.

That study must begin with the actual words, such as يُرِيدُونَ, نُرِيدُ, and رِئَاءَ. The Arabic derivation of niyyah does not make verses about will and motive additional occurrences of N W Y.

Nor does intention’s presence establish every later legal or theological category concerning it. Each claimed obligation requires independent textual evidence.

8. Distinctions from related roots

N W Y and N W ʾ: fruit stones and nawʾ

Their plurals can particularly invite confusion.

N W Y includes fruit stones, intention, and destination.

N W ʾ concerns rising under a heavy burden and nawʾ in star lore and rainfall periods.

Some dictionaries also record anwāʾ as a plural for fruit stones.

But anwāʾ in anwāʾ al-maṭar belongs to N W ʾ.

Identical spelling should not merge the roots; evidence and context distinguish each use.

Study of N W ʾ

N W Y and N ʾ Y: intending and moving away

Naʾā belongs to N ʾ Y and denotes becoming distant.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:83 says:

أَعْرَضَ وَنَأَىٰ بِجَانِبِهِ

aʿraḍa wa-naʾā bi-jānibihi

“He turns away and draws himself aside.”

The travel branch of nawā may involve departure and distance, but naʾā independently expresses moving away.

Semantic resemblance does not make their roots identical.

Study of N ʾ Y

N W Y and Q Ṣ D: intention and purpose

Qaṣd belongs to Q Ṣ D.

Lexical explanations often use qaṣada for nawā because both can express voluntary orientation toward a goal.

Shared meaning does not identify roots.

Qur’anic qaṣd also concerns moderation and a straight course. Those meanings should not automatically transfer to N W Y.

N W Y and B DH R: fruit stones and seed

Badhr concerns scattering seed for planting in Arabic.

Nawāh names a particular part of a fruit, while botanical or agricultural “seed” covers a wider range of propagative grains.

Their referents may connect, but they are not invariable lexical equivalents.

“Fruit stones” is more precise for al-nawā in Al-Anʿām 6:95.

Study of B DH R

N W Y and nīʾ: fat and uncooked meat

Lexical uses relate al-nayy to fat and al-niyy to fatness, treating them under N W Y.

Al-nīʾ, with hamzah, instead denotes raw or uncooked meat.

The difference in sound and form matters. Similarity does not transfer raw meat into Qur’anic al-nawā.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

The principal, precise equivalent for the sole Qur’anic al-nawā is “fruit stones.”

In the phrase:

إِنَّ اللَّهَ فَالِقُ الْحَبِّ وَالنَّوَىٰ

inna allāha fāliqu al-ḥabbi wa-al-nawā

An appropriate translation is:

“Indeed, The God is the splitter of grain and fruit stones.”

It agrees with Forqan’s installed Persian rendering of Al-Anʿām 6:95.

A word explanation may identify al-nawā as the plural or collective generic of nawāh: stones of dates and similar fruits.

“Intention hidden in the stone,” “life’s goal,” “nature’s center of will,” or “inner growth force” lack sufficient lexical support as translations.

Fāliq must keep its independent meaning. A stone alone is not a split or germinated stone; splitting comes from fāliq, and growth from context.

10. Conclusion

Early Arabic N W Y has two main semantic branches.

One concerns intention, purpose, destination, and orientation toward it, including departure, distance, companionship in destination, and deciding to act.

The other concerns nawāh, a date or similar fruit stone, with plural or collective nawā and recorded verbs for forming, collecting, or discarding stones.

Fattening and the nawāh weight term are additional uses requiring separate consideration.

The cited definition distinguishes intention and stones. The examined evidence does not establish one historical origin. They are better treated as two established branches rather than images in an invented theory of intention and seeds.

Only wa-al-nawā in Al-Anʿām 6:95 occurs in the Qur’an; it denotes fruit stones.

Its position with ḥabb and fāliq and the following phrase about bringing the living from the dead support the natural grain-and-stone reading. Al-Anʿām 6:96 and Al-Anʿām 6:99 continue attention to creation and growth.

The Qur’an also discusses human motives through other words. Their concepts do not authorize changing al-nawā in Al-Anʿām 6:95 into intention.

Final definition for Forqan:

N W Y has two important Arabic branches: intending a destination or act, sometimes connected with departure, distance, and movement; and stones of dates and other fruits. A certain historical relationship between the branches is unestablished. Qur’anic al-nawā in Al-Anʿām 6:95 means fruit stones.
