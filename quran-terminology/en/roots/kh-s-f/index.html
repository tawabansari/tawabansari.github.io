Qur’anic and lexical forms

Sinking, disappearance, and diminished visibility: from being swallowed by the earth to the moon growing dark

The Arabic root خ س ف concerns sinking, descending, and disappearing from view. When the ground subsides and takes what rests on it into its depths, the verb خَسَفَ is used. The same family also describes an eye sinking into its socket, the moon’s light diminishing, and certain forms of reduction or loss.

These uses share a feature: something previously visible, prominent, or in a particular condition sinks, disappears, or loses some of its extent or visibility.

Sinking into the earth and the moon losing its light are nevertheless different events. The ground may split or subside and take something into it. By contrast, the moon may appear dim or dark to an observer without falling from its position.

Lexical sources express both with khasf. Understanding the root therefore requires distinguishing its shared semantic core from the different ways that meaning appears.

Early Arabic also records khasf for deficiency, humiliation, and the imposition of a degrading condition. Someone forced into humiliating treatment may be described as subjected to khasf. Some derivatives also express hunger and emaciation.

The Qur’an uses this root eight times. Seven occurrences concern causing people to be swallowed by the earth, and one concerns the moon.

In Qārūn’s story, khasf is reported as an event that occurred; Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:40 also recalls swallowing by the earth in its account of earlier peoples. Warning passages raise the possibility of being swallowed by the earth, while Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:82 expresses, in the people’s speech, the hypothetical consequence of the absence of divine favor. Al-Qiyāmah 75:8 speaks of خَسَفَ الْقَمَرُ: the moon loses its light or grows dark.

These differences show that khasf by itself does not mean punishment, earthquake, death, or resurrection. Its central meaning concerns sinking and diminished visibility; when this happens, to whom, and with what consequence emerge from each verse’s construction and context.

1. Forms of the root and its fundamental meaning

The root خ س ف occurs eight times in eight Qur’anic verses. All its Qur’anic forms belong to the Form I verb خَسَفَ ـ يَخْسِفُ, khasafa–yakhsifu.

The Qur’anic forms are:

  • يَخْسِفَ in Al-Naḥl 16:45, Al-Isrāʾ 17:68, and Al-Mulk 67:16;
  • فَخَسَفْنَا in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:81;
  • لَخَسَفَ in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:82;
  • خَسَفْنَا in Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:40;
  • نَخْسِفْ in Sabaʾ 34:9;
  • وَخَسَفَ in Al-Qiyāmah 75:8.

Their differences concern tense, person, number, attached particles, and syntax. The Qur’an has no derivatives from other verbal forms and no nouns such as khusūf or khāsif from this root.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: eight Form I occurrences in eight verses

Important Arabic derivatives include:

  • خَسَفَ ـ يَخْسِفُ, khasafa–yakhsifu: sank, caused to subside, or caused something to be swallowed by the earth; of the moon, its light diminished or disappeared.
  • خَسْف, khasf: the sinking of the ground; in other branches, deficiency, humiliation, or the imposition of a low and difficult condition.
  • خُسُوف, khusūf: sinking or disappearance; in celestial usage, the diminishing or disappearance of the moon’s or sun’s light.
  • خَاسِف, khāsif: something sinking or losing light; in certain early uses, a hungry or emaciated person.
  • مَخْسُوف, makhsūf: something made to sink or subside.
  • اِنْخَسَفَ, inkhasafa: sank, subsided, or collapsed so that something disappeared within it.
  • اِنْخِسَاف, inkhisāf: sinking or subsidence.
  • خَسِيف, khasīf: of an eye, sunken; of a well, one with characteristics explained differently by lexicographers.
  • خَسِيفَة, khasīfah: in some uses, something damaged or sunken; in another branch, deficiency and humiliation.
  • أَخَاسِيف, akhāsīf: soft or sinking tracts of ground.
  • خَسِيفَة, khasīfah: also recorded of a she-camel producing abundant milk.

These forms are recorded in lexical sources, but their meanings should not be collapsed into one without regard to their referents and contexts.

The semantic core in the lexicographers’ accounts

Ibn Fāris writes in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

الْخَاءُ وَالسِّينُ وَالْفَاءُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى غُمُوضٍ وَغُؤُورٍ.

al-khāʾu wa-al-sīnu wa-al-fāʾu aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā ghumūḍin wa-ghuʾūrin

That is, the root indicates obscurity and sinking into depth.

He then considers the earth’s sinking, the moon’s eclipse, a sunken eye, and certain uses concerning deficiency and hunger within this field.

Lisān al-ʿArab also states:

الْخَسْفُ: سُؤُوخُ الْأَرْضِ بِمَا عَلَيْهَا.

al-khasfu suʾūkhu al-arḍi bi-mā ʿalayhā

That is, khasf is the ground sinking together with what rests upon it.

This definition accords with the root’s predominant Qur’anic use.

Ibn Fāris’s explanation is broader, pointing in two directions: sinking and ceasing to be manifest.

An eye, for example, may sink into its socket. Something then recedes from its previous outward position.

The moon may likewise appear dim or dark to an observer. Here diminished visibility concerns light, rather than necessarily a change in the moon’s location.

The shared meaning may therefore be understood as:

Sinking, or losing some previous visibility or prominence, so that something becomes hidden, submerged, or less apparent.

This definition does not imply that every use describes one physical object actually sinking into another.

In the Qur’an, the earth’s khasf and the moon’s khasf are distinct uses.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Lisān al-ʿArab: obscurity, depth, and sinking ground

2. Early uses: earth, moon, hunger, and humiliation

Ground that subsides

Arabic uses خَسَفَتِ الْأَرْضُ when the ground sinks or swallows what rests upon it.

Another expression is:

خَسَفَ اللَّهُ بِهِ الْأَرْضَ

Meaning: The God caused him to be swallowed by the earth.

This construction matters for understanding the Qur’anic verses.

The earth is where the sinking occurs, and the person or group concerned is introduced by the preposition bāʾ.

The sinking may involve part of the surface collapsing or something disappearing into the ground.

The word itself, however, does not determine the precise geological mechanism.

Ground subsidence, for example, can occur under different natural conditions. Lexical similarity does not establish that every natural subsidence is the same kind of event as the Qur’an’s account of Qārūn.

Such a judgment requires evidence beyond a shared word.

The moon’s eclipse: diminished light

Arabic uses خَسَفَ الْقَمَرُ for the diminishing or disappearance of the moon’s light.

Some sources primarily associate khusūf with the moon and kusūf with the sun.

Thaʿlab, an early lexicographer, regarded this distinction as preferable usage.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Jamharat al-Lughah, however, also report the use of both roots concerning the sun.

One cannot therefore impose an absolute rule that, throughout Arabic’s history, khasf concerned only the moon and kasf only the sun.

In modern astronomy, a lunar eclipse occurs when the moon enters Earth’s shadow, darkening some or all of its illuminated surface as seen by an observer.

This scientific explanation identifies the mechanism of a natural event; it is not the underlying meaning of every derivative of خ س ف.

In Al-Qiyāmah 75:8, the Qur’an speaks of the moon’s khasf among the scenes of resurrection.

An ordinary periodic lunar eclipse should therefore not be identified, without evidence, with the final event described in Al-Qiyāmah.

A sunken eye and a well

Lexicons also record خَسَفَتْ عَيْنُهُ of a human eye sinking into its socket or its outward appearance being impaired.

عَيْنٌ خَاسِفَةٌ belongs to the same field.

This use clarifies the root’s connection with receding below an exposed surface.

Sources also record بِئْرٌ خَسِيفٌ, biʾrun khasīfun.

Some explanations concern a well whose wall or another part has collapsed.

Others describe a well dug through rock that reaches abundant, enduring water.

These uses are not identical.

Their shared name does not establish that every khasīf well has necessarily collapsed or necessarily contains abundant water.

Ibn Fāris explains the second branch by proposing a connection with sinking and reaching hidden water sources.

This is his lexical analysis, rather than an established historical law governing the origin of every use.

Hunger and emaciation

In certain early Arabic uses, khāsif describes a hungry or emaciated person.

Al-Aṣmaʿī is reported to have said that khasf can indicate deficiency.

Tahdhīb al-Lughah also reports Abū al-Haytham’s explanation of al-khāsif as a hungry person.

Someone deprived of sufficient food for a time may lose body weight and strength, with face or limbs appearing sunken.

As a linguistic image, this condition connects with reduction and sinking.

Khasf nevertheless does not mean hunger in every use.

None of the Qur’anic forms of this root describes a hungry person.

A pre-Islamic poetic witness: khāsif in Aws ibn Ḥajar

The pre-Islamic poet Aws ibn Ḥajar describes a hunter:

أَخُو قُتُرَاتٍ قَدْ تَيَقَّنَ أَنَّهُ
إِذَا لَمْ يُصِبْ لَحْمًا مِنَ الْوَحْشِ خَاسِفُ

akhū quturātin qad tayaqqana annahu / idhā lam yuṣib laḥman mina al-waḥshi khāsifu

Translation:

“That hunter knows well that, if he obtains no meat from wild game, he will go hungry.”

In this verse, khāsif means hungry.

The poet describes someone dependent on hunting for food.

If his hunt fails, he obtains no food and experiences hunger.

This use shows that early Arabic did not restrict خ س ف to the ground and the moon.

Hunger here belongs to a particular lexical branch; it cannot be transferred to the Qur’anic يَخْسِفَ بِكُمُ الْأَرْضَ.

The first hemistich has variant readings. Aws’s Dīwān gives قَدْ تَيَقَّنَ, whereas Tahdhīb al-Lughah quotes قَدْ تَبَيَّنَ.

This variation does not decisively affect the meaning of khāsif at the verse’s end.

The Dīwān of Aws ibn Ḥajar: the hunter witness and the wording qad tayaqqana

Deficiency and humiliation

Another recorded use of khasf is deficiency, lowliness, or humiliation.

In سَامَهُ الْخَسْفَ, someone subjects another person to a degrading and difficult condition.

This use matters in human relations, since a person or group may exercise power to impose humiliation on others.

Difficulty, poverty, and weakness should not all be equated with moral abasement.

Illness or external circumstances can place someone in difficulty without diminishing that person’s character or worth.

In sāmahu al-khasfa, the central issue is the imposition of degrading treatment, rather than an actual reduction in the injured person’s human value.

A pre-Islamic witness: refusing humiliation

Balʿāʾ ibn Qays al-Kinānī, a pre-Islamic poet, says:

أَبَيْتُ لِنَفْسِي الْخَسْفَ لَمَّا رَضُوا بِهِ
وَوَلَّيْتُهُمْ شَتْمِي وَمَا كُنْتُ مُفْحَمَا

abaytu li-nafsī al-khasfa lammā raḍū bihi / wa-wallaytuhum shatmī wa-mā kuntu mufḥamā

Translation:

“When they accepted humiliation, I refused it for myself; I let them revile me, though I was not incapable of replying.”

Al-khasf here does not mean sinking into the ground.

The poet speaks about accepting or refusing a humiliating condition.

This use shows that early Arabic could extend khasf into social and moral life.

Al-Jāḥiẓ’s Al-Bayān wa-al-Tabyīn gives وَوَلَّيْتُهُمْ شَتْمِي. Ibn ʿAbd al-Barr’s Bahjat al-Majālis and some other reports give وَأَوْلَيْتُهُمْ سَمْعِي or a similar wording.

Al-Jāḥiẓ’s Al-Bayān wa-al-Tabyīn: the wording wa-wallaytuhum shatmī

The second hemistich therefore has textual variants. With shatmī, it means leaving the reviling of the poet to them, rather than directing the poet’s insults at them. With samʿī, the meaning approaches giving them a hearing; the variant therefore affects the translation of this hemistich.

Ibn ʿAbd al-Barr’s Bahjat al-Majālis: wa-awlaytuhum samʿī and its different translation

In either report, however, al-khasf in the first hemistich concerns humiliation and accepting a low condition.

This witness has historical significance: Balʿāʾ ibn Qays is identified as a pre-Islamic poet, and early literary sources transmit his poetry.

This social meaning is nevertheless not the direct meaning of the verb in any of the eight Qur’anic occurrences of خ س ف.

In the Qur’an, the verb concerns either sinking into the earth or the moon’s light diminishing.

3. Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:81 and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:82: Qārūn’s sinking and the collapse of apparent support

فَخَسَفْنَا بِهِ وَبِدَارِهِ الْأَرْضَ فَمَا كَانَ لَهُ مِنْ فِئَةٍ يَنْصُرُونَهُ مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ وَمَا كَانَ مِنَ الْمُنْتَصِرِينَ

fa-khasafnā bihi wa-bi-dārihi al-arḍa fa-mā kāna lahu min fiʾatin yanṣurūnahu min dūni allāhi wa-mā kāna mina al-muntaṣirīna

Proposed translation for this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“So We caused him and his dwelling to be swallowed by the earth. He had no group to help him against The God, and he could not defend himself.”

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:81

This verse gives the clearest Qur’anic account of khasf as an event that occurred.

The preceding verses discuss Qārūn, his great wealth, his arrogant behavior, and the responses of those who counsel him.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:79, Qārūn appears before the people in his adornment.

Those who desire worldly life wish they had been given the like of what Qārūn received.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:81, however, the situation changes.

فَخَسَفْنَا بِهِ وَبِدَارِهِ الْأَرْضَ

The verb fa-khasafnā comes from خ س ف.

Its grammatical subject is the first-person plural pronoun, referring to The God.

Bihi refers to Qārūn.

Wa-bi-dārihi includes his house or dwelling in the same event.

Al-arḍa identifies the earth involved in the sinking.

The construction makes clear that khasf here is not merely loss of status, political defeat, or a collapse in social standing.

The Qur’an speaks of Qārūn and his house being swallowed by the earth.

The expression does not specify the opening’s size, the precise depth, or the event’s geological details.

Nor does this verse name the town or geographical location of the event.

Unstated historical or natural details should therefore not be attributed to the text.

Khasf and Qārūn’s wealth

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:76 speaks of Qārūn’s great wealth.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:78, he says he acquired his possessions on the basis of knowledge he possessed.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:81, that same person and his house are swallowed by the earth.

This sequence shows the distance between a person’s apparent power and wealth and his helplessness before a great event.

Khasf itself does not mean losing wealth or economic collapse.

Qārūn’s wealth and behavior emerge from the story’s context; khasafnā expresses the event of being swallowed by the earth.

Economic and political collapses should therefore not all be directly called khasf in its Qur’anic sense.

The story can prompt reflection on the instability of power and wealth, but such reflection does not replace the word’s lexical meaning or the verse’s direct account.

فَمَا كَانَ لَهُ مِنْ فِئَةٍ يَنْصُرُونَهُ

After describing the sinking, the verse states that no group could help Qārūn.

It then says that he could not help himself either.

This part expresses the consequence of helplessness before the event.

Khasf alone does not mean being left without help.

The absence of help is explained by the independent clauses that follow.

This distinction also concerns خ ذ ل: someone may be deprived of help, but losing assistance and being swallowed by the earth are different meanings.

Study of KH DH L

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:82: the response of those who had desired Qārūn’s position

وَأَصْبَحَ الَّذِينَ تَمَنَّوْا مَكَانَهُ بِالْأَمْسِ يَقُولُونَ وَيْكَأَنَّ اللَّهَ يَبْسُطُ الرِّزْقَ لِمَنْ يَشَاءُ مِنْ عِبَادِهِ وَيَقْدِرُ لَوْلَا أَنْ مَنَّ اللَّهُ عَلَيْنَا لَخَسَفَ بِنَا وَيْكَأَنَّهُ لَا يُفْلِحُ الْكَافِرُونَ

wa-aṣbaḥa alladhīna tamannaw makānahu bi-al-amsi yaqūlūna waykaʾanna allāha yabsuṭu al-rizqa li-man yashāʾu min ʿibādihi wa-yaqdiru lawlā an manna allāhu ʿalaynā la-khasafa binā waykaʾannahu lā yufliḥu al-kāfirūna

Proposed translation for this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Those who had desired his position the day before began to say: Ah, The God expands provision for whom He wills among His servants and restricts it. Had The God not favored us, He would have caused us to be swallowed by the earth too. Ah, the disbelievers do not prosper.”

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:82

La-khasafa binā concerns an event that did not happen to the speakers themselves.

They say that, had The God not favored them, they might have suffered the same outcome.

The sentence therefore does not report another sinking.

It expresses the concern and new understanding of those who previously desired Qārūn’s position.

This difference matters: Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:81 reports an actual event, whereas Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:82 presents a response and a hypothetical outcome for others.

Qārūn’s fate is present in their minds here.

This does not establish that everyone who admires another’s wealth or prosperity necessarily deserves to be swallowed by the earth.

The verse concerns a particular group in the context of Qārūn’s behavior and fate.

It also shows that witnessing the consequences of behavior may lead people to reconsider the true value of what they once desired.

This ethical insight emerges from the narrative’s construction, rather than from the independent meaning of khasf.

4. Khasf in warning passages

Al-Naḥl 16:45: those who devise evil schemes

أَفَأَمِنَ الَّذِينَ مَكَرُوا السَّيِّئَاتِ أَنْ يَخْسِفَ اللَّهُ بِهِمُ الْأَرْضَ أَوْ يَأْتِيَهُمُ الْعَذَابُ مِنْ حَيْثُ لَا يَشْعُرُونَ

a-fa-amina alladhīna makarū al-sayyiʾāti an yakhsifa allāhu bihimu al-arḍa aw yaʾtiyahumu al-ʿadhābu min ḥaythu lā yashʿurūna

Proposed translation for this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Do those who devise evil schemes feel secure that The God will not cause them to be swallowed by the earth, or that punishment will not reach them from where they do not perceive?”

Al-Naḥl 16:45

Yakhsifa appears within a question about feeling secure.

The subject is people pursuing evil schemes.

The verb yakhsifa itself does not mean scheming or evil.

These characteristics are stated at the verse’s beginning.

Khasf is one possible consequence against which the verse warns.

The continuation also mentions punishment arriving from an unexpected direction.

The verse therefore presents two possibilities, rather than requiring both to be one event.

Does a warning establish certain occurrence?

The verse takes the form of a question:

أَفَأَمِنَ ...؟

That is: do they consider themselves secure?

This construction challenges disregard for possible consequences.

The verse itself does not say that every person concerned will necessarily be swallowed by the earth at a specified time.

Threat, warning, and a report of an event that occurred must therefore be distinguished.

Nor does this verse establish that every natural subsidence in every region is a specific punishment for the people living there.

Such a judgment requires independent evidence.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:68: security on land after rescue from the sea

أَفَأَمِنْتُمْ أَنْ يَخْسِفَ بِكُمْ جَانِبَ الْبَرِّ أَوْ يُرْسِلَ عَلَيْكُمْ حَاصِبًا ثُمَّ لَا تَجِدُوا لَكُمْ وَكِيلًا

a-fa-amintum an yakhsifa bikum jāniba al-barri aw yursila ʿalaykum ḥāṣiban thumma lā tajidū lakum wakīlan

Proposed translation for this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Do you feel secure that He will not cause a tract of dry land to sink with you, or send a stone-bearing storm upon you, after which you will find no guardian for yourselves?”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:68

The preceding verse, Al-Isrāʾ 17:67, describes people afflicted at sea who, in that condition, lose sight of those they call upon apart from The God.

After rescue to dry land, however, their behavior changes.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:68 then asks whether reaching land has provided absolute security.

Jāniba al-barri matters here.

Barr denotes dry land in contrast with the sea.

The verse therefore speaks of being swallowed on land, rather than drowning in water.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:69 also raises the possibility of being returned to the sea and afflicted there.

This sequence shows that rescue from one danger should not be mistaken for complete immunity from all dangers.

The root khasf itself does not mean sea, dry land, or rescue.

Those concepts are understood from the surrounding verses.

Sabaʾ 34:9: earth below and heaven above

أَفَلَمْ يَرَوْا إِلَىٰ مَا بَيْنَ أَيْدِيهِمْ وَمَا خَلْفَهُمْ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ وَالْأَرْضِ إِنْ نَشَأْ نَخْسِفْ بِهِمُ الْأَرْضَ أَوْ نُسْقِطْ عَلَيْهِمْ كِسَفًا مِنَ السَّمَاءِ إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَآيَةً لِكُلِّ عَبْدٍ مُنِيبٍ

a-fa-lam yaraw ilā mā bayna aydīhim wa-mā khalfahum mina al-samāʾi wa-al-arḍi in nashaʾ nakhsif bihimu al-arḍa aw nusqiṭ ʿalayhim kisafan mina al-samāʾi inna fī dhālika la-āyatan li-kulli ʿabdin munībin

Proposed translation for this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Have they not looked at what is before them and behind them of heaven and earth? If We will, We cause them to be swallowed by the earth, or cause fragments from heaven to fall upon them. In that is surely a sign for every servant who turns back.”

Sabaʾ 34:9

Nakhsif appears within the conditional construction in nashaʾ, “if We will.”

That is: if We will, We cause them to be swallowed by the earth.

Beside this possibility, the verse mentions كِسَفًا مِنَ السَّمَاءِ falling from above.

Kisaf comes from ك س ف and here denotes pieces or fragments from heaven.

The verse thus places two directions within one image: being swallowed by the earth and something falling from above.

Khasf and kasf are nevertheless different roots.

Their similar sounds do not make their meanings identical.

In this context, khasf concerns sinking and kisaf concerns pieces or fragments.

Power and possibility, rather than a report of an actual event

The conditional construction in Sabaʾ 34:9 is clear.

The verse does not say that its addressees have already sunk into the earth.

It speaks of The God’s power and of a possible event they should not disregard.

The conclusion also identifies this as a sign for كُلِّ عَبْدٍ مُنِيبٍ, “every servant who turns back.”

The verse therefore does more than describe a natural phenomenon; it also concerns human reflection and return.

This ethical message should nevertheless not erase the word’s material meaning.

The verse’s image concerns earth and heaven; these recognizable realities convey power and human responsibility.

Al-Mulk 67:16: the ground sinking and moving

أَأَمِنْتُمْ مَنْ فِي السَّمَاءِ أَنْ يَخْسِفَ بِكُمُ الْأَرْضَ فَإِذَا هِيَ تَمُورُ

a-amintum man fī al-samāʾi an yakhsifa bikumu al-arḍa fa-idhā hiya tamūru

Proposed translation for this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Do you feel secure from the One in heaven, that He will not cause the earth to sink with you, whereupon it begins to heave and sway?”

Al-Mulk 67:16

Here too, yakhsifa appears in a warning question.

Another word follows:

تَمُورُ

This verb comes from م و ر and concerns movement, going to and fro, and agitation.

Khasf and mawr are therefore distinct concepts connected within this image.

Khasf concerns being swallowed by the earth.

Mawr describes the ground’s movement and unrest.

This distinction matters: khasf alone should not be translated as shaking.

Shaking and subsidence may accompany one another in some natural events, but they are not lexically identical.

Nor does this verse establish that every earthquake or natural subsidence is certain evidence of The God’s anger toward its victims.

It is a warning question about security and divine power, rather than a statement about the moral cause of every natural disaster.

5. Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:40: khasf beside other forms of destruction

فَكُلًّا أَخَذْنَا بِذَنْبِهِ فَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ أَرْسَلْنَا عَلَيْهِ حَاصِبًا وَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ أَخَذَتْهُ الصَّيْحَةُ وَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ خَسَفْنَا بِهِ الْأَرْضَ وَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ أَغْرَقْنَا وَمَا كَانَ اللَّهُ لِيَظْلِمَهُمْ وَلَٰكِنْ كَانُوا أَنْفُسَهُمْ يَظْلِمُونَ

fa-kullan akhadhnā bi-dhanbihi fa-minhum man arsalnā ʿalayhi ḥāṣiban wa-minhum man akhadhat-hu al-ṣayḥatu wa-minhum man khasafnā bihi al-arḍa wa-minhum man aghraqnā wa-mā kāna allāhu li-yaẓlimahum wa-lākin kānū anfusahum yaẓlimūna

Proposed translation for this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Each We seized for his sin: upon some We sent a stone-bearing storm; some were seized by the mighty cry; some We caused to be swallowed by the earth; and some We drowned. The God did not wrong them; they were wronging themselves.”

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:40

The verse brings together several different forms of affliction suffered by earlier peoples:

حَاصِبًا, الصَّيْحَةُ, خَسَفْنَا بِهِ الْأَرْضَ, and أَغْرَقْنَا.

Their juxtaposition shows that the Qur’an does not equate khasf with every form of destruction.

A stone-bearing storm, a mighty cry, being swallowed by the earth, and drowning are four different expressions.

Early exegetical explanations commonly identify the khasf here with Qārūn’s fate in Al-Qaṣaṣ.

This connection accords with the explicit account in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:81.

Al-ʿAnkabūt itself, however, does not repeat Qārūn’s name in the clause about khasf.

Direct translation should therefore retain “some We caused to be swallowed by the earth,” with the identification of Qārūn supplied in contextual explanation.

The connection with justice

The verse ends with:

وَمَا كَانَ اللَّهُ لِيَظْلِمَهُمْ وَلَٰكِنْ كَانُوا أَنْفُسَهُمْ يَظْلِمُونَ

This sentence concerns the responsibility of the groups in question and denies injustice on The God’s part.

It should not be generalized to every victim of every kind of natural disaster in every age.

The verse concerns particular groups previously mentioned and their behavior.

If ground subsides today and people are injured, the event’s outward similarity to khasf does not establish those people’s guilt.

Such a judgment does not follow from the root’s meaning, and the verse does not authorize it for every natural event.

6. Al-Qiyāmah 75:8: وَخَسَفَ الْقَمَرُ

وَخَسَفَ الْقَمَرُ

wa-khasafa al-qamaru

Proposed translation for this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“And the moon grows dark.”

Al-Qiyāmah 75:8

This is the only Qur’anic occurrence of خ س ف that does not concern the earth or people swallowed by it.

The subject of khasafa here is the moon.

The lexical branch concerning diminished or disappearing light must therefore be considered.

Al-Ṭabarī explains the expression as the departure of the moon’s light.

Al-Qurṭubī likewise discusses its light diminishing or disappearing.

These explanations accord with the established Arabic use of khusūf al-qamar.

Al-Ṭabarī: the departure of the moon’s light in the resurrection context

The context of resurrection

Al-Qiyāmah 75:7 states:

فَإِذَا بَرِقَ الْبَصَرُ

fa-idhā bariqa al-baṣaru

Proposed translation for this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Then, when the sight is dazzled and bewildered.”

The moon’s khasf follows in Al-Qiyāmah 75:8.

Al-Qiyāmah 75:9 also states:

وَجُمِعَ الشَّمْسُ وَالْقَمَرُ

wa-jumiʿa al-shamsu wa-al-qamaru

Proposed translation for this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“And the sun and the moon are brought together.”

And in Al-Qiyāmah 75:10:

يَقُولُ الْإِنْسَانُ يَوْمَئِذٍ أَيْنَ الْمَفَرُّ

yaqūlu al-insānu yawmaʾidhin ayna al-mafarru

Proposed translation for this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“The human being says that day: Where is the escape?”

This context makes clear that khasf al-qamar appears among scenes concerning resurrection.

It should therefore not be restricted, without evidence, to an ordinary lunar eclipse during worldly life.

Does khasf here mean the moon falling?

Ibn Fāris identifies sinking and disappearance as the root’s shared core.

In usage concerning the moon, however, lexicons speak of its light diminishing or departing.

Translating the verse as “the moon sank into the earth” therefore lacks sufficient direct support.

The verse itself also does not say that the moon collides with Earth.

خَسَفَ الْقَمَرُ naturally concerns the moon growing dark or its light disappearing in Arabic.

Al-Qiyāmah 75:9 states the bringing together of the sun and moon in a separate expression.

The meaning of Al-Qiyāmah 75:9 should not therefore be transferred into khasafa itself in Al-Qiyāmah 75:8.

An ordinary eclipse and the moon’s khasf at resurrection

In worldly life, a lunar eclipse is a natural, predictable event.

Al-Qiyāmah, however, concerns events involving a transformation of the world’s ordinary condition and the human being’s encounter with his final outcome.

Although the verse draws on language concerning the moon’s darkening, it does not itself establish that the resurrection scene necessarily involves the temporary mechanism of ordinary eclipses.

Some early exegetical explanations likewise distinguish the world’s temporary eclipses from the moon’s final condition at resurrection.

The verse itself gives no details such as the duration of this darkness, the exact amount of remaining light, or its physical mechanism.

A precise translation should therefore retain the word’s clear meaning: the moon growing dark or its light disappearing.

7. Differences from neighboring roots

KH S F (khasf) compared with GH W R (ghawr): sinking and disappearing into depth

Arabic ghawr concerns sinking into depth or receding from an exposed surface.

Al-Mulk 67:30 states:

إِنْ أَصْبَحَ مَاؤُكُمْ غَوْرًا

That is: if your water should sink deep away.

This field is close to khasf.

Ghawr may concern water sinking beyond reach, whereas khasf in its predominant Qur’anic use concerns a person or place being swallowed by the earth.

Both concern sinking, but their referents and images may differ.

KH S F (khasf) compared with K S F (kasf): sinking, diminished light, or pieces

Arabic kasf can concern diminished light as well as a piece of something.

In Sabaʾ 34:9, كِسَفًا مِنَ السَّمَاءِ concerns fragments from heaven.

By contrast, نَخْسِفْ بِهِمُ الْأَرْضَ concerns being swallowed by the earth.

Within that same verse, the two concern different events.

In usage concerning the sun and moon, khusūf and kusūf approach one another in the field of diminished light. This proximity does not make the roots or all their derivatives identical.

KH S F (khasf) compared with KH Ṣ F (khaṣf): sinking and joining leaves

خ ص ف is an independent root.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:22 and Ṭā Hā 20:121, يَخْصِفَانِ concerns joining and placing leaves of the garden over Adam’s and his spouse’s bodies.

خ س ف, however, concerns sinking and darkening.

The difference between sīn and ṣād matters in these roots.

Similar written forms do not permit interpreting yakhṣifāni as sinking or yakhsifu as joining leaves.

KH S F (khasf) compared with R J F (rajf) and M W R (mawr): subsidence and movement

Rajf concerns shaking and agitation.

Mawr likewise concerns the ground’s movement and swaying in Al-Mulk 67:16.

Khasf, however, concerns sinking or being made to sink.

Shaking and subsidence may accompany one another in some natural events, but they are not lexically identical.

Khasf should therefore not be translated as earthquake in every verse.

KH S F (khasf) compared with B L ʿ (balʿ): sinking and swallowing

Arabic balʿ concerns swallowing and taking something into the interior.

In Hūd 11:44, the earth is addressed:

يَا أَرْضُ ابْلَعِي مَاءَكِ

Meaning: “Earth, swallow your water.”

The image is close to khasf in its taking something inward.

Balʿ belongs primarily to the field of swallowing; khasf in the use under discussion concerns subsidence or being swallowed by the earth.

Every detail of the act of swallowing should therefore not be transferred to the ground’s khasf.

Study of B L ʿ

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

For the seven earth-related uses, the most suitable equivalents are “cause to be swallowed by the earth” and “cause the earth to sink with someone.”

“Cause the ground to subside” may also work in some sentences, but may obscure the presence of the person or house sinking into it.

“Earthquake” is not a precise equivalent: khasf is not necessarily merely shaking.

“Destroy” likewise substitutes a possible outcome for the action itself and removes the image of sinking.

In Al-Qiyāmah 75:8, “the moon grows dark” or “the moon’s light diminishes or disappears” is suitable.

“Lunar eclipse” is a familiar expression, but may suggest only the temporary astronomical event. For direct translation of Al-Qiyāmah, “the moon grows dark” is therefore clearer.

Grammar: Active imperfect verb, first-person plural, jussive as the response to the condition in nashaʾ.

يَخْسِفَ — Al-Mulk 67:16

Root: خ س ف

Grammar: Active imperfect verb, third-person masculine singular, subjunctive after an.

Meaning in this verse: A warning of the possibility of the addressees being swallowed by the earth; the earth’s movement and unrest are expressed by the independent verb tamūru.

Root: خ س ف

Grammar: Active perfect verb, third-person masculine singular, with coordinating wāw; al-qamar is its subject.

Meaning in this verse: The moon grows dark in the resurrection scene, its light departing or disappearing.

This division shows that the verbs share one root, while their referents and sentence constructions determine their meaning in each verse.

9. Conclusion

The Arabic root خ س ف concerns sinking, disappearing into depth, and diminished visibility.

Its material uses include the ground sinking, things upon it disappearing, a sunken eye, and certain conditions of wells.

Of the moon, khasf denotes diminished or disappearing light, rather than necessarily the moon falling from its position.

In early Arabic, the family also extends to deficiency, hunger, emaciation, and humiliation. Aws ibn Ḥajar’s poetry uses khāsif for hungry, while Balʿāʾ ibn Qays’s poetry uses khasf for humiliation and accepting a degrading condition.

These lexical branches should not be imposed on every Qur’anic occurrence.

The Qur’an uses the root eight times.

Seven occurrences concern being swallowed by the earth. Qārūn’s story reports an event that occurred. Several other verses raise its possibility as a warning and question human feelings of security.

In Al-Qiyāmah 75:8, the root concerns the moon growing dark or losing its light.

Khasf itself therefore means neither punishment, earthquake, arrogance, death, nor resurrection. These concepts may connect with it in particular contexts, but are not the root’s independent meaning.

Nor should its use in accounts of particular groups’ fates become a judgment about the moral cause of all subsidence, earthquakes, or other natural disasters.

Final definition: The root خ س ف denotes sinking, being made to sink, and losing previous visibility or prominence. In Qur’anic usage concerning the earth, it describes people or places sinking into it; concerning the moon, it expresses diminished or disappearing light. The cause, agent, timing, and consequences emerge from each verse’s construction and context, rather than from the root alone. Brief root meaning: Sinking or being made to sink into the earth; of the moon, diminished or disappearing light.
