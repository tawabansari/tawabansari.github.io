Nafr, nufūr and nafīr in early Arabic and the Qur’an: from animals bolting and people turning away to mobilisation, setting out to learn religion and social support

The three-consonant root ن ف ر in Arabic concerns moving away, becoming distant and leaving a place or condition. An animal frightened into flight, a person turning from unwelcome speech, a group setting out on a task and people mobilising to support one another can all be described through this family.

The movement’s direction and motive differ. Nufūr can mean fleeing or turning away from something, while nafr can mean leaving and proceeding toward a goal. Fear, displeasure, responsibility, readiness to confront a threat or an effort to learn can motivate the movement.

Arabic also uses the root for a group of individuals. Nafar can denote companions or relatives; nafīr can denote a body that sets out for support, assistance or confrontation.

The Qur’an uses the family in varied settings: setting out to confront an enemy, a group leaving to understand religion, turning from divine signs and warnings, animals bolting, someone’s companions and a group of jinn listening to the Qur’an.

This range matters: the whole family must not be reduced to flight, fighting or hatred. Each form’s meaning depends on its construction, accompanying particles and the verse’s context, as well as its root.

1. Semantic Core and Forms

Important Arabic forms include:

  • نَفَرَ، يَنْفِرُ: Moved away, set out, bolted or proceeded toward a task.
  • نَفْر و نِفَار: Moving away, bolting and setting out, with differences in usage.
  • نُفُور: Flight, turning away, avoidance and displeasure leading to withdrawal.
  • نَافِر: Bolting or turning away; in some uses, a winner in competitive adjudication.
  • نَفُور: Readily bolting or fleeing; of people, someone quick to turn away.
  • نَفَر: A group of people, especially companions or those setting out on a task.
  • نَفِير: A supporting or mobilised body gathered for assistance or confrontation.
  • نَفْرَة و نَافِرَة: In some uses, relatives and companions who hasten to one another’s aid.
  • نَفَّرَ و أَنْفَرَ: Made a person or animal flee, drove away or impelled to move.
  • اِسْتَنْفَرَ: Called someone to set out and provide aid; in some constructions, bolted or moved away.
  • مُسْتَنْفِر: Bolting or fleeing; elsewhere, someone summoning others to set out.
  • مُنَافَرَة: Competition and adjudication between people or groups over superiority and social standing.

The family also has other Arabic uses, such as نفر الجلد for skin raised by swelling above the underlying tissue.

The root occurs eighteen times in sixteen Qur’anic verses, in five principal forms: the basic verb nafara, the nouns nafar, nufūr and nafīr, and the adjective mustanfirah.

The Qur’anic verbs انفروا, تنفروا, لينفروا and نفر all belong to the basic form. Mustanfirah, however, belongs to Form X.

Semantic Core: Moving Away and Leaving a Position

One lexical analysis describes the root’s central sense as تجافي و تباعد: drawing away and becoming distant.

This fits several uses.

An animal that bolts leaves its position. Someone turning away distances themselves from something. A group departing on a mission leaves its former location. Even swollen skin is described through protruding and becoming separated from the tissue beneath.

This connection must nevertheless not become an overly general definition such as “any movement,” since movement alone is not distinctive of this root.

Many uses particularly involve leaving a prior position, moving away or setting out from somewhere.

Nor is every instance necessarily driven by fear or aversion. In the Qur’an, some set out for aid and defence, while others set out to learn or convey warnings.

A principal distinction is therefore between moving away from something unwelcome and setting out toward a particular goal.

2. Early Arabic Uses

Animals Bolting

A familiar animal-related use is:

نَفَرَتِ الدَّابَّةُ

nafarati al-dābbatu

Translation: “The animal bolted.”

Also:

نَفَرَ الظَّبْيُ

nafara al-ẓabyu

Translation: “The gazelle bolted.”

The animal is frightened, senses danger or otherwise moves away from its position.

دابة نفور describes an animal that readily bolts or avoids approaching people and other things.

Nufūr here is more than ordinary movement: withdrawal or bolting from its previous situation is prominent.

Yet nafara does not necessarily express fear in every use. An animal may turn from something unwelcome or leave its position through movement and excitement.

Human Nufūr: Displeasure and Withdrawal

A familiar human expression is:

نَفَرَ مِنَ الشَّيْءِ

nafara mina al-shayʾi

Translation: “He shied away from it” or “He turned away from it.”

Min identifies what the person moves away from.

The distancing can be physical or concern displeasure and avoidance toward speech, behaviour or another person.

Someone may turn from unwelcome words without physically fleeing the location.

The meaning thus extends from actual movement to a psychological and behavioural response.

Tanfīr, making another person averse or driving them away, belongs to this field, as with conduct discouraging people from approaching or accepting speech.

Nufūr must nevertheless be distinguished from modern Persian nifrat, which often conveys hatred or strong aversion. Arabic nufūr may express only turning away, displeasure or withdrawal.

Rendering every Qur’anic instance as “intense hatred” is therefore inaccurate.

A Group Setting Out on a Task

Arabic also uses nafara for people leaving a place to undertake something.

For example:

نَفَرَ الْقَوْمُ إِلَى الْعَدُوِّ

nafara al-qawmu ilā al-ʿaduwwi

Translation: “The group set out to confront the enemy.”

Here, movement is toward the enemy rather than away from him.

Classical Arabic also describes pilgrims’ departure from Minā through nafr, with expressions such as يوم النفر and نفر الحاج من منى.

Nafr here concerns leaving a place for another destination. The verb itself does not mean being frightened or fleeing.

Pilgrims’ departure is a recorded classical use, but the establishment of precise ritual terms must not be dated before the Qur’an without historical investigation.

This example illustrates the verb’s range: setting out can be planned rather than a sudden response to danger.

Nafar and Nafīr: Companions and Supporters

Arabic uses nafar for people linked by association or companionship.

Lexical definitions often specify three to ten, traditionally describing a group of men.

That must not become an absolute rule for every period and text. Other uses concern a group, close companions or those who hasten to one another’s aid.

Nafīr can likewise denote a body gathered for an important undertaking, especially confrontation and assistance.

Related expressions, نفرة الرجل and نافرته, describe close relatives and those supporting a person.

Having more nafar or nafīr may therefore concern more than a headcount: their support and resulting social strength can matter as well.

Support and power, however, are not constant components of nafar’s meaning. Sometimes it simply denotes a group without describing its standing or capacity.

Social Munāfarah and Adjudicating Superiority

Early Arabic munāfarah describes competition between people or groups over honour, ancestry, followers or social superiority, sometimes submitted to an arbitrator.

Nāfir could denote the winner, and manfūr the defeated party.

Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā, a pre-Islamic poet, says in a well-known verse:

فَإِنَّ الْحَقَّ مَقْطَعُهُ ثَلَاثٌ
يَمِينٌ أَوْ نِفَارٌ أَوْ جَلَاءُ

fa-inna al-ḥaqqa maqṭaʿuhu thalāthun / yamīnun aw nifārun aw jalāʾu

Translation: “A claim of right is settled in three ways: an oath, adjudication of a dispute, or clarifying evidence.”

Nifār here concerns adjudication in a dispute or competition between two parties.

This differs from nufūr as bolting: the issue is recourse to judgment over superiority or a dispute.

Some transmissions replace nifār with shuhūd, witnesses. The variant must therefore be acknowledged when using the verse, although independent lexical records also establish nifār as adjudication or munāfarah.

This witness shows the root in pre-Islamic Arabic social relations and judgments of status, alongside movement and bolting.

Insufficient historical evidence prevents a claim that adjudication arose directly from animals bolting at a particular stage.

Swelling

Lexical records use نَفَرَ الْجِلْدُ for skin swollen and raised above its normal surface.

This is explained through the image of the skin’s surface drawing away from underlying tissue.

Swelling is not the family’s general meaning, and the Qur’an does not use this branch.

3. Qur’anic Nafr: Setting Out on a Task

A. Setting Out in Readiness: Al-Nisāʾ 4:71

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا خُذُوا حِذْرَكُمْ فَانْفِرُوا ثُبَاتٍ أَوِ انْفِرُوا جَمِيعًا

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū khudhū ḥidhrakum fa-unfirū thubātin awi unfirū jamīʿan

Translation: “You who believe, maintain your caution and readiness; then set out in groups or set out all together.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:71

The imperative انفروا occurs twice here.

Thubāt concerns separate groups; jamīʿan expresses setting out together.

These phrases show that nafr can occur in small groups or in a large body.

The context involves readiness for danger and confronting an enemy, connecting the movement with defence and warfare.

This does not make nafara mean fighting throughout the Qur’an. The military setting follows from خذوا حذركم, the group construction and surrounding verses.

The verb itself expresses setting out and leaving.

B. انفروا في سبيل الله: Al-Tawbah 9:38, Al-Tawbah 9:39, Al-Tawbah 9:40 and Al-Tawbah 9:41

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا مَا لَكُمْ إِذَا قِيلَ لَكُمُ انْفِرُوا فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ اثَّاقَلْتُمْ إِلَى الْأَرْضِ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū mā lakum idhā qīla lakumu unfirū fī sabīli Allāhi iththāqaltum ilā al-arḍi

Translation: “You who believe, what is the matter with you that, when told to set out in the way of The God, you sink heavily toward the earth?”

Al-Tawbah 9:38

Nafr here is joined to في سبيل الله.

Nafr describes setting out, while في سبيل الله specifies its direction and purpose.

The continuation describes those who respond sluggishly to the summons and cling to worldly life.

The contrast is clear: a call to move and act stands alongside heaviness, staying and refusing to set out.

Al-Tawbah 9:39 says:

إِلَّا تَنْفِرُوا يُعَذِّبْكُمْ عَذَابًا أَلِيمًا وَيَسْتَبْدِلْ قَوْمًا غَيْرَكُمْ

illā tanfirū yuʿadhdhibkum ʿadhāban alīman wa-yastabdil qawman ghayrakum

Translation: “If you do not set out, He will punish you with a painful punishment and replace you with another people.”

Al-Tawbah 9:39

Tanfirū here likewise concerns setting out in response to the summons.

Al-Tawbah 9:41 then says:

انْفِرُوا خِفَافًا وَثِقَالًا وَجَاهِدُوا بِأَمْوَالِكُمْ وَأَنْفُسِكُمْ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ

unfirū khifāfan wa-thiqālan wa-jāhidū bi-amwālikum wa-anfusikum fī sabīli Allāhi

Translation: “Set out, lightly or heavily burdened, and strive with your wealth and your lives in the way of The God.”

Nafr and jihād appear together, connecting the movement with effort and participation in a particular situation.

They are nevertheless different words: nafr expresses leaving and setting out; jihād concerns exertion and applying one’s capacity and resources.

Both relate here to confrontation and collective responsibility, but their lexical meanings must not be substituted for one another.

Nor does خفافا وثقالا, without independent examination, establish a universal obligation to depart regardless of actual abilities or limitations. Other Qur’anic verses acknowledge genuine excuses and differences in capacity.

Al-Tawbah 9:81 describes some discouraging departure in the heat:

وَقَالُوا لَا تَنْفِرُوا فِي الْحَرِّ قُلْ نَارُ جَهَنَّمَ أَشَدُّ حَرًّا

wa-qālū lā tanfirū fī al-ḥarri qul nāru jahannama ashaddu ḥarran

Translation: “They said: Do not set out in the heat. Say: The fire of Hell is hotter.”

The prohibition here is spoken by those discouraging others, rather than being a Qur’an-endorsed command.

Identifying the speaker matters: quoting a statement does not mean endorsing it.

C. Setting Out to Understand Religion: Al-Tawbah 9:122

وَمَا كَانَ الْمُؤْمِنُونَ لِيَنفِرُوا كَافَّةً فَلَوْلَا نَفَرَ مِن كُلِّ فِرْقَةٍ مِّنْهُمْ طَائِفَةٌ لِّيَتَفَقَّهُوا فِي الدِّينِ وَلِيُنذِرُوا قَوْمَهُمْ إِذَا رَجَعُوا إِلَيْهِمْ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَحْذَرُونَ

wa-mā kāna al-muʾminūna li-yanfirū kāffatan fa-lawlā nafara min kulli firqatin minhum ṭāʾifatun li-yatafaqqahū fī al-dīni wa-li-yundhirū qawmahum idhā rajaʿū ilayhim laʿallahum yaḥdharūna

Translation: “The believers should not all set out together. Why, then, should a party from each group not go forth to gain understanding in religion and warn their people when they return to them, so that they may beware?”

Al-Tawbah 9:122

This verse is especially important for N F R.

First, the believers are told not to set out all together; then a party from each group is mentioned.

Understanding religion and warning one’s people follow.

Does Nafr Here Mean Going to War?

The surah devotes substantial attention to military confrontation, responsibility in difficult circumstances, and staying or departing.

Some interpreters therefore relate nafr here to setting out on a military mission.

On this reading, one group departs while another stays to learn religion and convey its learning to those who return.

Another reading identifies those setting out as the people who travel to understand religion and then warn their own people.

This reading links the clauses more directly: a group sets out to gain understanding and, after returning, warn its people.

Both readings appear in early exegetical tradition.

The grammatical issue concerns the plural pronouns in ليتفقهوا, لينذروا and رجعوا. Whether they refer to the departing or remaining group affects the reading.

The text does not explicitly name the departing group’s geographical destination. It must not be restricted without evidence to a particular school, battlefield or location.

The clear points are the rejection of everyone leaving together and a division of responsibilities, alongside understanding religion and warning others.

The verse also shows that nafr does not inherently mean fighting. Even if one reading involves a military mission, the word still describes leaving and setting out.

Conversely, the educational reading does not make nafr mean “studying.” Understanding is the purpose or intended result, rather than the verb’s meaning.

4. Qur’anic Nufūr: Turning Away from Speech and Warning

Another Qur’anic branch is expressed through nufūr. These uses chiefly involve withdrawal from something, rather than movement toward a particular mission.

Qur’anic Signs and Increased Aversion

وَلَقَدْ صَرَّفْنَا فِي هَٰذَا الْقُرْآنِ لِيَذَّكَّرُوا وَمَا يَزِيدُهُمْ إِلَّا نُفُورًا

wa-laqad ṣarrafnā fī hādhā al-qurʾāni li-yadhdhakkarū wa-mā yazīduhum illā nufūran

Translation: “We have varied the explanation in this Qur’an so that they may take heed, yet it only increases their turning away.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:41

Nufūr describes those who turn away further instead of accepting the reminder.

The word itself conveys avoidance and withdrawal; the Qur’anic signs and divine reminder as its objects follow from context.

The same surah continues:

وَإِذَا ذَكَرْتَ رَبَّكَ فِي الْقُرْآنِ وَحْدَهُ وَلَّوْا عَلَىٰ أَدْبَارِهِمْ نُفُورًا

wa-idhā dhakarta rabbaka fī al-qurʾāni waḥdahu wallaw ʿalā adbārihim nufūran

Translation: “When you mention your Lord alone in the Qur’an, they turn their backs and flee in aversion.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:46

This makes the connection with turning away clearer.

وَلَّوْا عَلَىٰ أَدْبَارِهِمْ states the act of turning their backs; nufūran highlights their state and forceful withdrawal.

The object of their response is also identified: mentioning the Lord alone in the Qur’an.

Nufūr should therefore not be reduced to general discomfort. This context involves withdrawing and distancing oneself from the speech.

The root alone nevertheless does not imply that everyone who avoids something is hostile to truth. Its moral character depends on what is avoided and the reasons and circumstances of the conduct.

Nufūr in Response to the Call to Al-Raḥmān

وَإِذَا قِيلَ لَهُمُ اسْجُدُوا لِلرَّحْمَٰنِ قَالُوا وَمَا الرَّحْمَٰنُ أَنَسْجُدُ لِمَا تَأْمُرُنَا وَزَادَهُمْ نُفُورًا

wa-idhā qīla lahumu usjudū li-al-raḥmāni qālū wa-mā al-raḥmānu a-nasjudu li-mā taʾmurunā wa-zādahum nufūran

Translation: “When told, ‘Prostrate to al-Raḥmān,’ they say, ‘What is al-Raḥmān? Shall we prostrate to whatever you command us?’ And it increased their turning away.”

Al-Furqān 25:60

Nufūr here is a response to the invitation to prostrate to al-Raḥmān.

The speakers’ question and response show resistance and withdrawal from acceptance.

Nufūr itself, however, does not mean denying the name al-Raḥmān or refusing prostration. Those features follow from the accompanying clauses.

Fāṭir 35:42 also says:

فَلَمَّا جَاءَهُمْ نَذِيرٌ مَّا زَادَهُمْ إِلَّا نُفُورًا

fa-lammā jāʾahum nadhīrun mā zādahum illā nufūran

Translation: “When a warner came to them, it only increased their turning away.”

The preceding words describe people swearing that, if a warner came, they would be better guided than other communities.

When the warner arrives, however, their response contradicts their claim.

Nufūr is thus connected with the gap between their claim and actual conduct.

Al-Mulk 67:21 also says:

بَل لَّجُّوا فِي عُتُوٍّ وَنُفُورٍ

bal lajjū fī ʿutuwwin wa-nufūrin

Translation: “Rather, they persisted in insolence and turning away.”

ʿUtuww and nufūr appear together.

ʿUtuww concerns insolence; nufūr, withdrawal and turning away. Their combination describes the addressees through both features.

The roots are not exact synonyms. Fear or displeasure may make someone turn away without that conduct always constituting moral rebellion.

Here, ʿutuww and the reproachful context give nufūr its particular moral direction.

5. Nafar, Nafīr and Mustanfirah: Groups, Support and Flight

أَعَزُّ نَفَرًا: Companions and Social Strength

وَكَانَ لَهُ ثَمَرٌ فَقَالَ لِصَاحِبِهِ وَهُوَ يُحَاوِرُهُ أَنَا أَكْثَرُ مِنكَ مَالًا وَأَعَزُّ نَفَرًا

wa-kāna lahu thamarun fa-qāla li-ṣāḥibihi wa-huwa yuḥāwiruhu anā aktharu minka mālan wa-aʿazzu nafaran

Translation: “He had fruit and yield, and said to his companion while conversing with him: I have more wealth than you and am stronger in companions.”

Al-Kahf 18:34

Nafaran concerns companions, dependants or a body of people on whom the speaker relies.

The speaker compares himself with another: first greater wealth, then greater standing or strength through his nafar.

Nafar alone does not mean power. It denotes the companions, while aʿazzu highlights the standing and strength they provide.

The verse does not itself specify soldiers, workers or children. The group could include relatives, supporters or dependants.

“Companions” or “supporters” is therefore more precise here than restricting the meaning to fighting men.

أَكْثَرَ نَفِيرًا: The Number and Strength of Supporters

ثُمَّ رَدَدْنَا لَكُمُ الْكَرَّةَ عَلَيْهِمْ وَأَمْدَدْنَاكُم بِأَمْوَالٍ وَبَنِينَ وَجَعَلْنَاكُمْ أَكْثَرَ نَفِيرًا

thumma radadnā lakumu al-karrata ʿalayhim wa-amdadnākum bi-amwālin wa-banīna wa-jaʿalnākum akthara nafīran

Translation: “Then We restored your ascendancy over them, supported you with wealth and sons, and made you more numerous in companions and supporters.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:6

Nafīr concerns the body or number of people accompanying a community or coming to its aid when needed.

The increase is connected with restored power and the possession of wealth and children.

Nafīr can denote a body mobilised against an enemy. Nevertheless, “army” or “soldiers” would restrict this translation unduly, since increased numbers and social capacity are also involved.

Akthar emphasises increase. Nafīr itself does not mean victory or military superiority.

نَفَرًا مِنَ الْجِنِّ: A Group of Jinn

وَإِذْ صَرَفْنَا إِلَيْكَ نَفَرًا مِّنَ الْجِنِّ يَسْتَمِعُونَ الْقُرْآنَ

wa-idh ṣarafnā ilayka nafaran mina al-jinni yastamiʿūna al-qurʾāna

Translation: “When We directed a group of jinn toward you, listening to the Qur’an.”

Al-Aḥqāf 46:29

Nafar here denotes a group of jinn.

They tell one another to be quiet and listen. When the recitation ends, they return to their people as warners.

Al-Jinn 72:1 also says:

قُلْ أُوحِيَ إِلَيَّ أَنَّهُ اسْتَمَعَ نَفَرٌ مِّنَ الْجِنِّ فَقَالُوا إِنَّا سَمِعْنَا قُرْآنًا عَجَبًا

qul ūḥiya ilayya annahu istamaʿa nafarun mina al-jinni fa-qālū innā samiʿnā qurʾānan ʿajaban

Translation: “Say: It has been revealed to me that a group of jinn listened and said: We have heard a wondrous Qur’an.”

These verses show that Qur’anic nafar is not confined to groups of humans.

Its clear meaning here is a group or a party of individuals.

The word alone does not determine their precise number. Although some lexical definitions specify three to ten, these verses do not explicitly state that limit.

Their behaviour also differs from nufūr as turning away. They listen to the Qur’an, then return to warn their people.

The whole family therefore cannot be reduced to aversion or flight from truth.

حُمُرٌ مُسْتَنْفِرَةٌ: Bolting Animals

كَأَنَّهُمْ حُمُرٌ مُّسْتَنفِرَةٌ ۝ فَرَّتْ مِن قَسْوَرَةٍ

ka-annahum ḥumurun mustanfiratun / farrat min qaswaratin

Translation: “As though they were bolting wild asses, fleeing from a qaswarah.”

Al-Muddaththir 74:50 and Al-Muddaththir 74:51

These follow the question:

فَمَا لَهُمْ عَنِ التَّذْكِرَةِ مُعْرِضِينَ

fa-mā lahum ʿani al-tadhkirati muʿriḍīna

Translation: “What is the matter with them that they turn away from the reminder?”

Al-Muddaththir 74:49

Turning from the reminder is likened to animals bolting and fleeing danger.

The common Ḥafṣ reading has mustanfirah, with i on the fāʾ, describing bolting animals.

Another reading has mustanfarah, with a on the fāʾ, which can describe animals driven away or frightened by something.

The active/passive distinction matters morphologically, but both readings depict animals fleeing danger.

Al-Muddaththir 74:51 then uses farrat, from F R R, for actual flight.

The two verses reinforce bolting and fleeing, but mustanfirah alone does not name the source of fear.

Lexical and exegetical accounts variously explain qaswarah as a hunter, lion or group of hunters. Establishing its precise meaning requires a separate study.

For N F R, the central point is the image of strong withdrawal, without transferring every detail of wild asses’ behaviour to human psychology.

6. Comparison with Related Roots

N F R and F R R

Farra, in its familiar use, concerns fleeing or flight.

Al-Muddaththir 74:50 and Al-Muddaththir 74:51 place both roots together: mustanfirah and farrat.

N F R can express bolting, withdrawal or setting out; F R R emphasises fleeing danger or an unwelcome situation.

In that scene, the animals both bolt and flee.

This must not become an absolute rule that nafr always marks the beginning of flight and farar its end. The roots do not necessarily establish such a sequence.

Nafr in setting out for defence or understanding religion also differs from flight.

N F R and KH R J

Khurūj concerns coming out, used in the Qur’an for leaving various places or conditions.

Nafr can likewise express departure, but often highlights setting out toward a mission, assistance or confrontation.

Someone can leave a house without any particular mission or summons. Khurūj is a natural general term for this.

By comparison, unfirū in Al-Nisāʾ 4:71 and Al-Tawbah 9:41 concerns readiness for collective action.

This is a tendency rather than an absolute boundary. Arabic nafr can also describe leaving a place without war or a military mission.

Study of KH R J

N F R and H J R

Familiar uses of H J R concern leaving, distancing oneself and breaking a relationship with something.

Hajr and nafr can both accompany departure and distancing in certain situations.

Hijrah highlights leaving an environment or condition and moving elsewhere; nafr often highlights mobilisation for a particular task.

Nufūr may also be psychological or behavioural withdrawal without leaving one’s home.

Nafr and hijrah are therefore not exact synonyms. A military use of nafr does not establish that migration must also be military movement.

N F R and J M ʿ

Jamʿ concerns gathering and bringing things together.

In its branch involving groups, nafar can denote a body that sets out or provides support when needed.

Group membership and setting out are connected, but differ in meaning.

Jamīʿan alongside unfirū explicitly states that the movement is collective. Thubāt in the same verse specifies movement in separate groups. The verb itself must therefore not be restricted to the departure of every member of a community.

The construction shows that accompanying words determine the number and manner of departure.

Study of J M ʿ

7. Translation Proposals for Forqan

One fixed equivalent cannot suit all the derivatives.

For nafara in departure and action, natural equivalents include “set out,” “left” and “departed.”

For unfirū in Al-Nisāʾ 4:71, Al-Tawbah 9:38 and Al-Tawbah 9:41, “set out” is appropriate. A contextual explanation can identify readiness, defence and confrontation as the purposes.

In Al-Tawbah 9:122, nafara should likewise be translated “set out” or “departed.” Understanding and warning are expressed separately in the following clauses.

For nufūr, “turning away,” “aversion” or “withdrawal” can fit the context. Modern Persian nifrat is not always precise, since it may add a sense of intense hostility.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:46:

وَلَّوْا عَلَىٰ أَدْبَارِهِمْ نُفُورًا

wallaw ʿalā adbārihim nufūran

“They turn their backs and flee in aversion.”

For nafar as a body of people, “group,” “party” or, in certain contexts, “companions” is appropriate.

In Al-Aḥqāf 46:29:

نَفَرًا مِّنَ الْجِنِّ

nafaran mina al-jinni

“A group of jinn.”

In Al-Kahf 18:34:

وَأَعَزُّ نَفَرًا

wa-aʿazzu nafaran

“And I am stronger in companions.”

For nafīr in Al-Isrāʾ 17:6, “companions,” “supporters” or “a supporting body” fits. Within the sentence, “We made you more numerous in companions and supporters” conveys the meaning naturally.

For mustanfirah in Al-Muddaththir 74:50, “bolting” or “fleeing” is appropriate. The passive reading can be explained as “driven away” or “frightened.”

Brief Root Meaning and Qur’anic Contexts

Brief root meaning: Moving away and becoming distant; variously bolting, turning away or setting out on a task. The nominal branch concerns companions and supporters as a group.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:71, Al-Tawbah 9:38, Al-Tawbah 9:39 and Al-Tawbah 9:41: Setting out in response to a summons, contextually for confrontation and collective responsibility.

In Al-Tawbah 9:122: A party from each community setting out, contextually connected with understanding religion and warning others.

Nufūr in Al-Isrāʾ 17:41, Al-Isrāʾ 17:46, Al-Furqān 25:60, Fāṭir 35:42 and Al-Mulk 67:21: Turning away and withdrawing from speech, reminder or warning.

Nafar in Al-Kahf 18:34: Companions associated with a person’s strength and social standing.

Nafar in Al-Aḥqāf 46:29 and Al-Jinn 72:1: A group or party of jinn.

Nafīr in Al-Isrāʾ 17:6: The number of companions and supporters.

Mustanfirah in Al-Muddaththir 74:50: Bolting or frightened animals, fleeing the danger identified in the next verse.

8. Conclusion

N F R in Arabic concerns moving away, becoming distant and setting out. This appears in animals bolting, people turning away, groups departing on a task and companions mobilising.

Other branches use nafar and nafīr for groups and companions, and munāfarah for social competition and adjudication. Swelling and raised skin are also recorded uses.

These forms cannot all receive one fixed translation.

The Qur’an uses nafara for setting out to act and fulfil responsibilities, nufūr for turning from speech and warning, nafar and nafīr for groups and supporters, and mustanfirah for bolting animals.

These uses show that the root has no fixed moral value in itself.

Setting out may serve defence, assistance or learning religion. Turning away may arise from fear, displeasure or opposition. Assessment depends on the action’s purpose, situation and context.

Al-Tawbah 9:122 especially shows why nafr should not always be translated as fighting. There, group departure connects with understanding religion and warning, although interpretations differ over who leaves and who stays.

The use of nafar for jinn also shows that it is not solely a label for human men; the word does not necessarily determine an exact number.

Final definition: In its verbal branch, N F R denotes moving away, becoming distant or setting out from a position. Context can make this bolting and turning from something, or leaving toward a particular goal. In nominal branches, nafar and nafīr denote companions and those linked by association or readiness for aid and collective action. Each derivative’s precise meaning and moral direction follow from construction and context, rather than a single fixed expression for the whole family.
