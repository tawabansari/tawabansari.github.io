Calls, cries, and intense sound: from human shouts and animal calls to devastating cries and resurrection events

1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

Arabic Ṣ Y Ḥ concerns calling out, shouting, and producing loud, intense sound. Ṣāḥa can describe someone crying loudly, summoning another with a shout, or crying out from fear, anger, or surprise.

The family is not restricted to humans. Arabic also uses it for birds’ and animals’ calls. Ṣayḥah may also describe a frightening cry, a sudden warning, or an event accompanied by intense sound.

Ṣ Y Ḥ occurs thirteen times in thirteen Qur’anic verses. Every occurrence belongs to one noun: ṣayḥah.

The attested forms are:

  • الصَّيْحَةُ (al-ṣayḥatu): the cry; Hūd 11:67, Hūd 11:94, Al-Ḥijr 15:73, Al-Ḥijr 15:83, Al-Muʾminūn 23:41, and Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:40.
  • صَيْحَةً (ṣayḥatan): a cry; Yā Sīn 36:29, Yā Sīn 36:49, Yā Sīn 36:53, Ṣād 38:15, and Al-Qamar 54:31.
  • الصَّيْحَةَ (al-ṣayḥata): the cry; Qāf 50:42.
  • صَيْحَةٍ (ṣayḥatin): a cry; Al-Munāfiqūn 63:4.

These are case and definite-or-indefinite forms of one noun, rather than derivatives with independent meanings.

Other forms are also known in early Arabic:

  • صَاحَ / يَصِيحُ (ṣāḥa / yaṣīḥu): cried or called out.
  • صِحْ (ṣiḥ): call out; summon someone.
  • صَيْح (ṣayḥ): a cry or shout.
  • صِيَاح (ṣiyāḥ): shouting or loud sound.
  • صُيَاح، صَيَحَان (ṣuyāḥ, ṣayaḥān): other verbal-noun forms for producing sound.
  • صَيْحَة (ṣayḥah): one cry, shout, or intense sound.
  • صَائِح (ṣāʾiḥ): someone or something calling out.
  • صَيَّاح (ṣayyāḥ): frequently crying out or having a loud voice.
  • صَيَّحَ (ṣayyaḥa): produced intense sound or shouted.
  • صَايَحَ (ṣāyaḥa): addressed another with a shout.
  • تَصَايَحَ (taṣāyaḥa): people shouted to one another.
  • صَائِحَة (ṣāʾiḥah): a cry, particularly loud lamentation or wailing.

These derivatives are recorded in Arabic, but none of the family’s verbs occurs in these forms in the Qur’anic text.

2. The semantic core and early Arabic usage

Loud, intense sound

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris introduces the root’s principle as:

وَهُوَ الصَّوْتُ الْعَالِي

wa-huwa al-ṣawtu al-ʿālī

“Loud sound.”

He explains ṣiyāḥ and ṣayḥah through the same principle.

Lisān al-ʿArab, quoting Tahdhīb al-Lughah, relates ṣiyāḥ to anything’s sound when it becomes intense.

Intensity and loudness thus principally distinguish ṣiyāḥ from ordinary sound.

Someone may speak in an ordinary voice, for example, but enter ṣiyāḥ’s field when shouting to carry their voice over a distance or in response to danger.

Loudness alone does not determine the cause or purpose.

Someone may shout with joy, cry in pain, call out a warning, or yell to frighten another.

All may lexically be ṣiyāḥ, while their motives and value differ.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: loud sound as the semantic core

Ṣayḥah: a single occurrence of a cry

Morphologically, ṣayḥah is a noun of a single occurrence in the ṣiyāḥ family.

It can therefore denote one cry or a particular occurrence of calling out.

That structure does not establish a fixed physical intensity or quantified amount of sound.

Some Qur’anic occurrences accompany wāḥidah, meaning a single cry. The adjective makes the event’s singularity more explicit.

A ṣayḥah may be short or prolonged, human or animal, or accompany a sudden event in another setting.

The word alone identifies neither the sound’s producer nor its listeners’ response.

Calling to summon

صَاحَ بِفُلَانٍ (ṣāḥa bi-fulānin) can mean summoning someone with a shout.

صَاحَ عَلَيْهِ (ṣāḥa ʿalayhi) may mean shouting at someone, reproaching them, or restraining them with a loud voice.

The preposition and sentence structure matter.

Calling someone may seek their attention. Shouting at someone may warn, blame, or threaten.

Each sentence’s verb and accompanying preposition should therefore be examined separately.

Ṣ Y Ḥ does not always mean either invitation or threat.

Animal calls

Early Arabic also uses ṣāḥa for birds.

صاح الغراب (ṣāḥa al-ghurābu), for example, means that the crow called.

Ṣiyāḥ is therefore not restricted to human speech with meaningful words and sentences.

A bird may call without its sound having human speech’s linguistic structure.

Ṣiyāḥ alone does not establish human articulation, uttered words, or a particular linguistic message.

People may likewise produce a ṣayḥah consisting only of a cry, without a complete sentence.

A pre-Islamic poetic witness

Imruʾ al-Qays says:

دَعْ عَنْكَ نَهْبًا صِيحَ فِي حَجَرَاتِهِ
وَلَكِنْ حَدِيثًا مَا حَدِيثُ الرَّوَاحِلِ

daʿ ʿanka nahban ṣīḥa fī ḥajarātihi / wa-lākin ḥadīthan mā ḥadīthu al-rawāḥili

An approximate rendering:

“Leave aside the plunder around which cries rose; but the tale of the riding camels—what a tale!”

Ṣīḥa is passive here, denoting the cries raised during or around the plundering.

Accounts associated with the poem concern plundered property and lost riding camels.

The witness matters because Ṣ Y Ḥ describes sound connected with a social event such as raiding and plunder.

Ṣīḥa itself does not mean stealing or plundering. The cry belongs to the scene; plunder comes from the poem’s subject.

The family was thus used before the Qur’an for loud sound in sudden, tense situations.

Lisān al-ʿArab: human and animal cries and the Imruʾ al-Qays witness

Ṣayḥah in a surprise attack

Lisān al-ʿArab also records ṣayḥah for a sudden raid on a group, especially one accompanied by cries, fear, and confusion.

The usage connects with the principal meaning of loud sound.

The cry may announce the attack’s beginning or form part of the sudden event.

This does not make every Arabic ṣayḥah a military attack.

Its underlying meaning is a cry; some constructions name an event accompanied by that cry.

Lisān al-ʿArab: ṣayḥah and a surprise raid

Ṣāḥati al-shajaratu: a different use

Early sources record صَاحَتِ الشَّجَرَةُ (ṣāḥati al-shajaratu) for a tree growing tall.

Ṣāḥa al-ʿunqūdu describes a cluster emerging from its covering and growing.

Ibn Fāris treats this as a figurative extension of ṣiyāḥ: as a cry rises, a tree or cluster rises and becomes visible.

That is an explanatory image, not proof of the naming’s definite historical origin.

The tree does not necessarily produce sound here; the word describes its growth and height.

Sources also include some uses concerning split wood or dried plants, but Ibn Fāris is cautious about certain forms’ connection with Ṣ W Ḥ.

Graphic resemblance alone should therefore not place every splitting or drying word under Ṣ Y Ḥ.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: plant growth and the distinction from Ṣ W Ḥ

3. Ṣayḥah in the accounts of earlier peoples

Many Qur’anic occurrences concern events accompanying groups’ destruction.

Ṣayḥah here is more than an ordinary loud sound: it occurs in punishment and collective downfall contexts.

The root itself nevertheless does not mean punishment; that comes from the situation and event’s outcome.

Thamūd: Hūd 11:67

Ṣāliḥ’s and Thamūd’s story states:

وَأَخَذَ الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا الصَّيْحَةُ فَأَصْبَحُوا فِي دِيَارِهِمْ جَاثِمِينَ

wa-akhadha alladhīna ẓalamū al-ṣayḥatu fa-aṣbaḥū fī diyārihim jāthimīna

“The cry seized the wrongdoers, and they lay fallen in their homes.”

Hūd 11:67

Al-ṣayḥatu is the grammatical subject of akhadha.

Semantically, the cry or accompanying event overtakes the wrongdoers.

The result is explicit: they lay fallen in their homes.

Ṣayḥah thus concerns a devastating event in this context.

The verse alone does not explain the sound’s intensity, physical source, or effect on human bodies.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:78 describes Thamūd’s end with al-rajfah, a shaking or violent tremor.

Fuṣṣilat 41:17 also mentions ṣāʿiqatu al-ʿadhābi al-hūni.

The Qur’an thus uses different images in reporting Thamūd’s downfall.

A devastating event may involve sound, shaking, and impact. Those words’ presence does not establish that all are completely synonymous names for one particular physical phenomenon.

Shuʿayb’s people: Hūd 11:94

Shuʿayb’s story states:

وَأَخَذَتِ الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا الصَّيْحَةُ فَأَصْبَحُوا فِي دِيَارِهِمْ جَاثِمِينَ

wa-akhadhati alladhīna ẓalamū al-ṣayḥatu fa-aṣbaḥū fī diyārihim jāthimīna

“The cry seized the wrongdoers, and they lay fallen in their homes.”

Hūd 11:94

Its structure closely resembles Hūd 11:67.

In both verses, ṣayḥah accompanies wrongdoers’ destruction and their falling in their homes.

Hūd 11:67 nevertheless uses أَخَذَ (akhadha), while Hūd 11:94 uses أَخَذَتِ (akhadhati).

Ṣayḥah is formally feminine, but its verbal-noun use relating to ṣiyāḥ also permits agreement with that verbal noun’s meaning.

The difference does not establish two kinds of punishment or two different genders of ṣayḥah.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:91 likewise describes Shuʿayb’s people’s end through rajfah.

As with Thamūd’s account, this enables comparison of cries and shaking without going beyond the wording to assert the event’s natural details.

Lisān al-ʿArab: verb agreement with ṣayḥah and ṣiyāḥ

Lot’s people: Al-Ḥijr 15:73

Al-Ḥijr states:

فَأَخَذَتْهُمُ الصَّيْحَةُ مُشْرِقِينَ

fa-akhadhathumu al-ṣayḥatu mushriqīna

“The cry seized them at sunrise.”

Al-Ḥijr 15:73

Mushriqīna indicates the event’s timing, as the sun has risen.

The following verse describes the settlement being overturned and stones raining down.

Ṣayḥah thus forms part of an account describing several devastating events.

The word itself does not mean overturning the earth or raining stones.

Those are other actions and events stated in the next verse.

The people of Al-Ḥijr: Al-Ḥijr 15:83

Later in the surah, the people of Al-Ḥijr are described:

فَأَخَذَتْهُمُ الصَّيْحَةُ مُصْبِحِينَ

fa-akhadhathumu al-ṣayḥatu muṣbiḥīna

“The cry seized them in the morning.”

Al-Ḥijr 15:83

The preceding verses describe people who denied the divine signs and carved homes from mountains.

Muṣbiḥīna places the event in the morning.

The earlier passage about Lot’s people used mushriqīna.

Both concern the beginning of the day, but differ in wording; the distinction should not be removed without reason.

Al-ṣayḥah in both verses means the cry and intense event, while the accompanying description supplies the different timing.

Study of Ṣ B Ḥ

Another generation after Noah: Al-Muʾminūn 23:41

Al-Muʾminūn states:

فَأَخَذَتْهُمُ الصَّيْحَةُ بِالْحَقِّ فَجَعَلْنَاهُمْ غُثَاءً

fa-akhadhathumu al-ṣayḥatu bi-al-ḥaqqi fa-jaʿalnāhum ghuthāʾan

“The cry seized them in truth, and We made them like debris.”

Al-Muʾminūn 23:41

The preceding verses describe a generation after Noah, among whom a messenger was raised.

They rejected his message and ultimately perished.

Verse 41 accompanies ṣayḥah with bi-al-ḥaqqi.

The qualification concerns the judgment’s and event’s truth or justice in context, rather than ḥaqq being part of ṣayḥah’s lexical meaning.

Ghuthāʾ likewise expresses the result and an image of their end.

The cry, the qualification of truth, and the destructive outcome should therefore be distinguished.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:40: ṣayḥah alongside other events

Al-ʿAnkabūt states:

فَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ أَرْسَلْنَا عَلَيْهِ حَاصِبًا وَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ أَخَذَتْهُ الصَّيْحَةُ وَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ خَسَفْنَا بِهِ الْأَرْضَ وَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ أَغْرَقْنَا

fa-minhum man arsalnā ʿalayhi ḥāṣiban wa-minhum man akhadhathu al-ṣayḥatu wa-minhum man khasafnā bihi al-arḍa wa-minhum man aghraqnā

“Some We struck with a stone-bearing storm; some the cry seized; some We caused the earth to swallow; and some We drowned.”

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:40

This verse particularly helps identify ṣayḥah’s semantic boundaries.

It lists several events: a stone-bearing storm, ṣayḥah, the earth swallowing people, and drowning.

Always translating ṣayḥah simply as earthquake, storm, or drowning would erase the expressions’ distinction.

Their separate naming also does not establish that natural events never overlap.

An actual event may have several effects, while the verse uses different names for different peoples’ accounts.

Rendering ṣayḥah as a cry or loud call better preserves the word’s direct meaning than substituting a particular physical event without evidence.

Al-Qamar 54:31: one cry and its outcome

Al-Qamar describes Thamūd:

إِنَّا أَرْسَلْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ صَيْحَةً وَاحِدَةً فَكَانُوا كَهَشِيمِ الْمُحْتَظِرِ

innā arsalnā ʿalayhim ṣayḥatan wāḥidatan fa-kānū ka-hashīmi al-muḥtaẓiri

“We sent upon them one cry, and they became like the dry fragments of a fence-maker.”

Al-Qamar 54:31

Wāḥidah describes the ṣayḥah here.

It emphasizes one effective occurrence.

Arsalnā ʿalayhim also presents the cry as an event sent upon them in the verse’s account.

Ṣayḥah itself names neither debris nor the transformation of bodies into it.

Ka-hashīmi al-muḥtaẓiri compares their remaining condition with something else.

The whole image should therefore not be inserted into ṣayḥah’s lexical definition.

4. Ṣayḥah in the final events

Other verses connect ṣayḥah with a situation ending, people rising, and their presence before God.

They share the lexical core of an intense cry but occur in different contexts.

Yā Sīn 36:29: one cry and becoming extinguished

The story of people rejecting the messengers states:

إِنْ كَانَتْ إِلَّا صَيْحَةً وَاحِدَةً فَإِذَا هُمْ خَامِدُونَ

in kānat illā ṣayḥatan wāḥidatan fa-idhā hum khāmidūna

“It was only one cry, and suddenly they were extinguished.”

Yā Sīn 36:29

This occurrence still concerns a community’s destruction.

Khāmidūn belongs to KH M D and concerns being extinguished or subsiding.

The cry accompanies their collective extinction here.

Their extinction is not ṣayḥah’s own lexical meaning, but the result reported by the verse.

Study of KH M D

Yā Sīn 36:49: a cry while they dispute

The same surah states:

مَا يَنْظُرُونَ إِلَّا صَيْحَةً وَاحِدَةً تَأْخُذُهُمْ وَهُمْ يَخِصِّمُونَ

mā yanẓurūna illā ṣayḥatan wāḥidatan taʾkhudhuhum wa-hum yakhiṣṣimūna

“They await only one cry that seizes them while they dispute.”

Yā Sīn 36:49

Ṣayḥah here concerns surprise and the interruption of ordinary life.

People are still conversing and disputing when the event overtakes them.

The next verse says they cannot make a bequest or return to their families.

That sequence clarifies the event’s suddenness.

The suddenness comes from taʾkhudhuhum and the context as a whole, rather than every ṣayḥah throughout Arabic necessarily meaning a sudden fatal event.

Yā Sīn 36:53: the cry and human gathering

A few verses later:

إِنْ كَانَتْ إِلَّا صَيْحَةً وَاحِدَةً فَإِذَا هُمْ جَمِيعٌ لَدَيْنَا مُحْضَرُونَ

in kānat illā ṣayḥatan wāḥidatan fa-idhā hum jamīʿun ladaynā muḥḍarūna

“It is only one cry, and suddenly they are all brought before Us.”

Yā Sīn 36:53

This time the result is not extinction but people’s collective presence before God.

Earlier, Yā Sīn 36:51 describes blowing into al-ṣūr and rising from graves.

Ṣayḥah here therefore concerns people being gathered and brought forward.

The distinction matters: ṣayḥah itself does not mean death or revival.

Its result in Yā Sīn 36:29 is extinction; in Yā Sīn 36:53, presence before God.

The stable meaning is the cry, while context determines its accompanying event.

Ṣād 38:15: a cry without respite

Ṣād states:

وَمَا يَنْظُرُ هَٰؤُلَاءِ إِلَّا صَيْحَةً وَاحِدَةً مَا لَهَا مِنْ فَوَاقٍ

wa-mā yanẓuru hāʾulāʾi illā ṣayḥatan wāḥidatan mā lahā min fawāqin

“These await only one cry for which there is no respite or interval.”

Ṣād 38:15

Ṣayḥah again accompanies wāḥidah.

Mā lahā min fawāqin emphasizes the absence of respite or an interval of return in that event.

Lexical sources also connect fawāq with the interval between milkings and milk returning.

That meaning belongs to F W Q and should not be inserted into ṣayḥah.

The verse describes a cry and its temporal character; ṣayḥah itself does not name an opportunity or respite.

Al-Ṭabarī: fawāq, respite, return, and the milking interval

Study of F W Q

Qāf 50:42: hearing the cry

Qāf states:

يَوْمَ يَسْمَعُونَ الصَّيْحَةَ بِالْحَقِّ ذَٰلِكَ يَوْمُ الْخُرُوجِ

yawma yasmaʿūna al-ṣayḥata bi-al-ḥaqqi dhālika yawmu al-khurūji

“The day they hear the cry in truth: that is the day of coming forth.”

Qāf 50:42

The verse clearly illustrates ṣayḥah’s relationship with hearing.

Yasmaʿūna explicitly expresses auditory reception.

The cry is therefore presented as an audible event here.

Its audibility does not establish the sound’s precise nature, its propagation medium, or the hereafter’s physical mechanisms.

Dhālika yawmu al-khurūji also identifies the day of coming forth.

The cry is connected with coming forth and rising, but does not itself mean coming out.

Ṣayḥah and blowing into al-ṣūr

Yā Sīn 36:51 mentions نُفِخَ فِي الصُّورِ (nufikha fī al-ṣūri), while Yā Sīn 36:53 mentions صَيْحَةً وَاحِدَةً (ṣayḥatan wāḥidatan).

Their proximity shows that both expressions can describe events connected with resurrection.

Ṣayḥah and ṣūr nevertheless belong to two different roots.

Ṣayḥah concerns the cry; established Qur’anic ṣūr usage names what is blown into.

It cannot therefore be asserted without examining context that every Qur’anic ṣayḥah is the blowing into al-ṣūr.

Nor should details of ṣayḥah be inserted without evidence into every mention of blowing into al-ṣūr.

The expressions concern related events in some cases but are not lexically identical.

Study of Ṣ W R

5. Ṣayḥah in describing hypocritical conduct

Al-Munāfiqūn states:

يَحْسَبُونَ كُلَّ صَيْحَةٍ عَلَيْهِمْ

yaḥsabūna kulla ṣayḥatin ʿalayhim

“They think every cry is against them.”

Al-Munāfiqūn 63:4

This verse particularly clarifies ṣayḥah’s semantic range.

Unlike the destruction and resurrection passages, it concerns an ordinary cry that people imagine to be a threat directed against themselves.

The meaning of kulla ṣayḥatin

Kulla extends the phrase to every cry.

Ṣayḥatin is indefinite and genitive as the second term of the construct phrase.

ʿAlayhim shows that they consider the cry directed against themselves.

They thus imagine every cry or shout to be against them.

The verse does not say every sound was actually directed against them.

Yaḥsabūna clarifies the distinction between the sound’s reality and their perception.

The cry, fear, and suspicion

The verse opens with the hypocrites’ impressive appearance and speech that draws attention.

It then compares them with propped-up timbers and says they consider every cry directed against them.

The condition concerns fear, suspicion, and feeling threatened.

Ṣayḥah itself does not establish that the cry was necessarily threatening.

A sound may concern something else while someone takes it as directed at themselves.

The verse therefore distinguishes the cry itself from the listener’s perception of it.

Is Qur’anic ṣayḥah always punishment?

This verse clearly answers that question.

If ṣayḥah inherently always meant divine punishment, “they think every cry is against them” would mean every divine punishment. The context instead concerns their fearful reaction to cries.

Ṣayḥah therefore fundamentally means a cry or loud call.

In some verses, that cry accompanies destruction and punishment and can also name a devastating event in that context.

That contextual extension should not replace the word’s basic meaning.

Al-Ṭabarī: fear and the listener’s interpretation of each cry

6. Distinguishing related roots

Ṣ Y Ḥ (ṣiyāḥ) and Ṣ W T (ṣawt)

Arabic ṣawt concerns audible sound, quiet or loud, produced by people, animals, or objects.

Ṣiyāḥ principally concerns louder, more intense sound.

Every ṣiyāḥ belongs to sound’s field, but not every ṣawt is necessarily ṣiyāḥ.

A person’s quiet conversational voice is ṣawt, while their shout may be called ṣiyāḥ.

Ṣiyāḥ does not therefore always involve anger or violence: someone may call out a warning or cry in surprise.

Study of Ṣ W T

Ṣ Y Ḥ (ṣāḥa) and Ṣ R KH (ṣarakha)

Arabic ṣurākh concerns crying and intense shouting.

Some uses of that family also concern crying for help or giving aid.

Yaṣṭarikhūna in Fāṭir 35:37 describes people crying out in punishment.

Ṣiyāḥ likewise concerns an intense call, but established usage does not restrict it to cries for rescue.

A crow’s call or shouting to summon someone may be ṣiyāḥ without necessarily asking for deliverance.

Ṣiyāḥ and ṣurākh overlap, but not all their derivatives are synonymous.

Study of Ṣ R KH

Ṣ Y Ḥ (ṣayḥah) and N D W (nidāʾ)

Nidāʾ concerns calling and summoning.

Ṣayḥah emphasizes the cry itself and its intensity.

A call may be loud and thereby approach ṣiyāḥ.

Not every nidāʾ is necessarily an intense shout, nor does every ṣayḥah have a particular addressee.

Ṣayḥah in the destruction accounts does not itself mean summoning those peoples.

In ordinary human usage, by contrast, someone may use ṣiyāḥ to call another.

Study of N D W

Ṣ Y Ḥ (ṣayḥah) and R J F (rajfah)

Rajfah concerns shaking and violent trembling.

The Qur’an describes Thamūd’s and Shuʿayb’s people’s end with ṣayḥah, and elsewhere with rajfah.

The images may be connected within one event but remain lexically distinct.

Ṣayḥah denotes a cry; rajfah denotes shaking.

Ṣayḥah should therefore not always be translated as earthquake without evidence.

Rajfah’s presence likewise does not establish definite details about sound waves, shaking intensity, or the event’s natural cause.

Ṣ Y Ḥ (ṣayḥah) and Ṣ ʿ Q (ṣāʿiqah)

Qur’anic ṣāʿiqah concerns a tremendous, overpowering blow or event.

Thamūd’s story also mentions ṣāʿiqah.

Ṣāʿiqah and ṣayḥah nevertheless belong to different roots.

An event may involve an intense cry and devastating effects, but a shared outcome does not make the words’ meanings identical.

Modern ṣāʿiqah often concerns atmospheric electrical discharge. That particular scientific meaning should not be imposed without sufficient evidence on every Qur’anic ṣāʿiqah or ṣayḥah.

Study of Ṣ ʿ Q

Ṣ Y Ḥ (ṣayḥah) and N F KH (nafkh)

Nafkh concerns blowing.

The resurrection verses describe blowing into al-ṣūr.

Some ṣayḥah verses concern the same setting of great final events.

Blowing is an act, while ṣayḥah names a cry or sound event.

Ṣ Y Ḥ alone therefore identifies neither a blowing implement, the number of blasts, nor the one blowing.

Ṣ Y Ḥ (ṣāḥa) and Ṣ Y KH (aṣākha)

These roots look similar but differ.

Ṣāḥa concerns producing a cry.

Arabic aṣākha concerns listening and auditory attention.

One may therefore describe sound production and the other reception and attentive listening.

Graphic and phonetic proximity does not establish identical roots or derivational histories.

Persian bāng and faryād are both defensible equivalents.

Faryād is often clearer to contemporary readers, while bāng can suit intense nonhuman sound or events without a specified caller.

Explosion, earthquake, lightning strike, sound wave, or an angel’s cry should not replace ṣayḥah in every verse: those equivalents or particular instances carry details beyond the word itself.

8. Conclusion

Arabic Ṣ Y Ḥ concerns loud, intense sound. It may come from a person, animal, or another event and serve calling, warning, expressing emotion, or accompanying a sudden occurrence.

Early Arabic uses ṣāḥa for human and animal cries, and ṣayḥah for a particular cry or sound event. Broader uses include cries during surprise attacks and expressions for plants growing and rising.

Only the noun ṣayḥah occurs in the Qur’an.

Some occurrences accompany earlier peoples’ destruction, including Thamūd, Shuʿayb’s people, Lot’s people, and groups identified in other passages.

Other verses connect ṣayḥah with great final events, falling stricken, rising, and human gathering.

Al-Munāfiqūn 63:4 concerns an ordinary cry that people consider directed against themselves.

That occurrence shows that punishment, destructiveness, and connection with resurrection are not fixed parts of the root’s meaning.

Even in punishment passages, ṣayḥah alone does not identify the natural event, sound intensity, physical source, or precise mechanism of human death.

Its association with rajfah, ṣāʿiqah, or blowing into al-ṣūr also does not establish that all are synonymous or denote one precisely identical phenomenon.

Brief root meaning for Forqan:

A cry, shout, or loud, intense sound; in some uses, a sudden event accompanied by a cry.

Meaning in Qur’anic uses:

صَيْحَة (ṣayḥah): one cry or shout. In the earlier-peoples and resurrection verses, a cry accompanying a tremendous event; in Al-Munāfiqūn 63:4, a cry that listeners imagine directed at themselves.

Each occurrence’s precise meaning comes from its accompanying verbs, addressee, timing, and stated consequences in that verse.
