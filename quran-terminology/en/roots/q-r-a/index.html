Reading, recitation, and presenting speech: from assembling the parts of discourse to the Qur’an and the periods called qurūʾ

1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

The Arabic root Q R ʾ has a broad semantic field. Its best-known use is reading or reciting: restating or presenting words and phrases that form a discourse or text. Early usage also records gathering, containing, and a defined period or time within this family.

The Qur’an displays three principal branches: reading and causing another to recite; the word Qur’an; and qurūʾ in the waiting period of divorced women.

The principal forms include:

  • قَرَأَ / يَقْرَأُ (qaraʾa / yaqraʾu): read or recited; reads or recites.
  • اِقْرَأْ / اِقْرَءُوا (iqraʾ / iqraʾū): read or recite, addressed to one or several people.
  • قُرِئَ (quriʾa): was read or recited.
  • سَنُقْرِئُكَ (sa-nuqriʾuka): We will cause you to read or recite.
  • قُرْآن (qurʾān): originally a verbal noun associated with reading and recitation; also the established name of the Book revealed to Muhammad.
  • قُرُوء (qurūʾ): a plural of qarʾ; periods associated with women’s menstruation and purity.

Qirāʾah, qāriʾ, and muqriʾ are familiar Arabic forms, but these particular nouns do not occur in the Qur’an in those forms. Qurʾān, however, occurs repeatedly and is the family’s principal Qur’anic nominal.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: eighty-eight occurrences in four forms

2. The semantic core and early Arabic usage

Reading and assembling the parts of discourse

Many Arabic lexicographers explain Q R ʾ with reference to gathering and joining: assembling something’s parts or placing them together.

This connection is understandable in recitation: letters and words are presented as connected, intelligible speech. One word follows another, and one phrase extends into another.

The general meaning of gathering must nevertheless be distinguished from the particular meaning of reciting.

Not every gathering is recitation in Arabic. Someone who gathers people or wealth is not called a qāriʾ merely for doing so. In ordinary usage, recitation concerns words, discourse, or a text that is read or restated.

Assembling the parts of speech can therefore explain one linguistic relationship between recitation and gathering; it must not turn qaraʾa into merely gathered in every verse.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab: reading, gathering, and joining

An early witness concerning containment

A line attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm reads:

ذِرَاعَيْ عَيْطَلٍ أَدْمَاءَ بِكْرٍ
هِجَانِ اللَّوْنِ لَمْ تَقْرَأْ جَنِينًا

dhirāʿay ʿayṭalin admāʾa bikrin / hijāni al-lawni lam taqraʾ janīnan

Translation:

“The forelegs of a tall-necked, light-colored young she-camel, pure white in color, that has never contained a fetus in its womb.”

Lam taqraʾ janīnan here does not mean failing to read something. In the familiar explanation, the she-camel’s womb has not gathered and held a fetus.

The witness shows that early Q R ʾ was not restricted to reading.

Sources nevertheless differ in the line’s wording and explanation. It can attest a use concerning containment and pregnancy, but cannot alone establish the historical development of every meaning of the root.

Lisān al-ʿArab: ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm’s witness and its alternative explanations

Qarʾ as a defined time or period

Another branch concerns qarʾ. Lexical sources record it for a defined time, an occasion, or a period in which an event occurs.

Examples include expressions associated with the time winds blow, rain arrives, or natural cycles recur.

Applied to women, qarʾ concerns menstruation and periods of purity. It will be examined separately because its Qur’anic use differs clearly from reading and recitation.

A pre-Islamic witness concerning qurūʾ

Poetry attributed to Al-Aʿshā includes:

وَفِي كُلِّ عَامٍ أَنْتَ جَاشِمُ غَزْوَةٍ
تَشُدُّ لِأَقْصَاهَا عَزِيمَ عَزَائِكَا

مُوَرِّثَةً مَالًا وَفِي الْحَيِّ رِفْعَةً
لِمَا ضَاعَ فِيهَا مِنْ قُرُوءِ نِسَائِكَا

wa-fī kulli ʿāmin anta jāshimu ghazwatin / tashuddu li-aqṣāhā ʿazīma ʿazāʾikā / muwarrithatan mālan wa-fī al-ḥayyi rifʿatan / li-mā ḍāʿa fīhā min qurūʾi nisāʾikā

Translation:

“Each year you undertake the hardship of a campaign, strengthening your resolve to reach its farthest aim; a campaign that leaves you wealth and high standing among the tribe, in return for the periods of your women lost during it.”

Qurūʾ nisāʾikā refers to women’s periods during which the man, absent on campaign, has lost the opportunity to be with them.

Some lexicographers connect this witness with women’s periods of purity. The poem alone, however, does not settle the entire disagreement over qarʾ as menstruation or purity.

Its importance is that early qurūʾ concerned women’s biological and temporal cycles, with linguistic precedent independent of reading.

3. Qaraʾa in the Qur’an: reading, reciting, and presenting speech

Iqraʾ: the command to read

One prominent use of Q R ʾ opens Al-ʿAlaq:

اقْرَأْ بِاسْمِ رَبِّكَ الَّذِي خَلَقَ

iqraʾ bi-ismi rabbika alladhī khalaqa

“Read in the name of your Lord who created.”

Al-ʿAlaq 96:1

Then:

اقْرَأْ وَرَبُّكَ الْأَكْرَمُ

iqraʾ wa-rabbuka al-akramu

“Read, and your Lord is the Most Generous.”

Al-ʿAlaq 96:3

Iqraʾ commands reading. The verb itself does not, however, require a physical written document before the reader.

Arabic reading may be from writing, from memory, or through receiving and restating speech. In the setting of revelation, iqraʾ concerns receiving and presenting divine discourse.

The word should therefore not be restricted to deciphering written letters on a page. Equally, its reading and recitation sense should not be removed by replacing iqraʾ with merely think, understand, or acquire knowledge. Those activities may relate to reading, but are not direct equivalents of the verb.

Reading a book or text

Al-Isrāʾ says:

اقْرَأْ كِتَابَكَ كَفَىٰ بِنَفْسِكَ الْيَوْمَ عَلَيْكَ حَسِيبًا

iqraʾ kitābaka kafā bi-nafsika al-yawma ʿalayka ḥasīban

“Read your book; today your own self suffices to reckon against you.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:14

Kitābaka is the direct object of iqraʾ. Reading is connected with a book and accountability: the person confronts what is presented and can examine its contents.

The same surah says:

فَمَنْ أُوتِيَ كِتَابَهُ بِيَمِينِهِ فَأُولَٰئِكَ يَقْرَءُونَ كِتَابَهُمْ

fa-man ūtiya kitābahu bi-yamīnihi fa-ulāʾika yaqraʾūna kitābahum

“Those given their book in their right hand will read their book.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:71

In Al-Ḥāqqah, someone given his book in his right hand also calls to others:

هَاؤُمُ اقْرَءُوا كِتَابِيَهْ

hāʾumu iqraʾū kitābiyah

“Here, take it and read my book.”

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:19

These uses concern encountering a book’s contents. Reading itself is clear, while judgment, reckoning, and knowledge of deeds follow from the sentence’s context rather than Q R ʾ alone.

Recitation as presenting speech to others

Al-Shuʿarāʾ says:

فَقَرَأَهُ عَلَيْهِمْ مَا كَانُوا بِهِ مُؤْمِنِينَ

fa-qaraʾahu ʿalayhim mā kānū bihi muʾminīna

“And had he recited it to them, they would not have believed in it.”

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:199

The preceding verses describe the possibility of the Qur’an being sent down upon someone who was not an Arabic speaker and his reciting it to them, yet their still not believing.

Qaraʾahu ʿalayhim concerns presenting and reciting speech to an audience. The preposition ʿalā expresses the relationship between the reciter and the listeners.

Al-Isrāʾ uses the same construction:

وَقُرْآنًا فَرَقْنَاهُ لِتَقْرَأَهُ عَلَى النَّاسِ عَلَىٰ مُكْثٍ

wa-qurʾānan faraqnāhu li-taqraʾahu ʿalā al-nāsi ʿalā mukthin

“And a Qur’an We have divided into portions, so that you may recite it to the people with deliberation.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:106

The verse connects three matters: the Qur’an as revealed text, its division into portions, and reciting it to people.

Li-taqraʾahu expresses the purpose of reading and presenting; ʿalā al-nās identifies the audience; and ʿalā mukthin concerns deliberation and gradual presentation.

Reading here is therefore more than an individual act: conveying the discourse to an audience also appears in the sentence. Gradualness, deliberation, and manner of presentation follow from other components, rather than qaraʾa alone.

Recitation and hearing

Al-Aʿrāf says:

وَإِذَا قُرِئَ الْقُرْآنُ فَاسْتَمِعُوا لَهُ وَأَنْصِتُوا لَعَلَّكُمْ تُرْحَمُونَ

wa-idhā quriʾa al-qurʾānu fa-istamiʿū lahu wa-anṣitū laʿallakum turḥamūna

“When the Qur’an is recited, listen to it and remain silent, so that you may receive mercy.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:204

Quriʾa is passive here. The sentence focuses on the act of recitation and what is recited.

The command fa-istamiʿū lahu also shows that recitation is audible in this context. The audience receives the recited speech and keeps silent to hear it.

Al-Inshiqāq likewise says:

وَإِذَا قُرِئَ عَلَيْهِمُ الْقُرْآنُ لَا يَسْجُدُونَ

wa-idhā quriʾa ʿalayhimu al-qurʾānu lā yasjudūna

“And when the Qur’an is recited to them, they do not prostrate.”

Al-Inshiqāq 84:21

Recitation again concerns presenting discourse to listeners. Both witnesses show that Qur’anic recitation commonly accompanies utterance and auditory reception, although Arabic reading also applies to encountering written text.

Causing to recite: sa-nuqriʾuka

Al-Aʿlā says:

سَنُقْرِئُكَ فَلَا تَنْسَىٰ

sa-nuqriʾuka fa-lā tansā

“We will cause you to recite, so you will not forget.”

Al-Aʿlā 87:6

Sa-nuqriʾuka is Form IV. Unlike qaraʾa, which expresses reading itself, aqraʾa here concerns causing another to read and making recitation available to that person.

God is the subject and Muhammad the addressee. Fa-lā tansā also concerns retaining what he is caused to recite.

The family can therefore express both performing recitation and causing another to recite. Morphology alone, however, must not determine every detail of receiving revelation.

4. Qurʾān: from the verbal noun to the name of the Book

Qurʾān as recitation itself

Al-Qiyāmah provides particularly clear evidence for the word Qurʾān:

إِنَّ عَلَيْنَا جَمْعَهُ وَقُرْآنَهُ

inna ʿalaynā jamʿahu wa-qurʾānahu

“Indeed, gathering it and reciting it are Our responsibility.”

Al-Qiyāmah 75:17

Then:

فَإِذَا قَرَأْنَاهُ فَاتَّبِعْ قُرْآنَهُ

fa-idhā qaraʾnāhu fa-ittabiʿ qurʾānahu

“When We have recited it, follow its recitation.”

Al-Qiyāmah 75:18

Qurʾān in these verses means the act of recitation or being recited.

Verse 17 distinguishes jamʿahu from qurʾānahu. Translating both indiscriminately as gathering would strip their conjunction of its semantic distinction.

Jamʿahu concerns gathering it; qurʾānahu concerns reciting it. They may be related, but they are not completely identical concepts.

The following verse makes recitation clearer through qaraʾnāhu.

The passage then continues:

ثُمَّ إِنَّ عَلَيْنَا بَيَانَهُ

thumma inna ʿalaynā bayānahu

“Then, indeed, explaining and clarifying it are Our responsibility.”

Al-Qiyāmah 75:19

Gathering, recitation, and explanation are three distinguishable expressions. Recitation presents discourse, while explanation makes it clear.

This does not entirely separate reading from understanding. It shows that recitation itself does not encompass every stage of explanation and understanding.

Qur’an as the established name of revelation

In many occurrences, Qur’an has become the name of the text revealed to Muhammad.

Al-Baqarah says:

شَهْرُ رَمَضَانَ الَّذِي أُنْزِلَ فِيهِ الْقُرْآنُ هُدًى لِلنَّاسِ وَبَيِّنَاتٍ مِنَ الْهُدَىٰ وَالْفُرْقَانِ

shahru ramaḍāna alladhī unzila fīhi al-qurʾānu hudan li-al-nāsi wa-bayyinātin mina al-hudā wa-al-furqāni

“The month of Ramadan, in which the Qur’an was sent down, as guidance for people and clear signs of guidance and discernment.”

Al-Baqarah 2:185

Al-qurʾān refers to the known revelation, while hudan li-al-nās states its guiding role.

Al-Isrāʾ says:

إِنَّ هَٰذَا الْقُرْآنَ يَهْدِي لِلَّتِي هِيَ أَقْوَمُ

inna hādhā al-qurʾāna yahdī li-allatī hiya aqwamu

“Indeed, this Qur’an guides to what is most upright.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:9

Qur’an is therefore more than the name of an audible act. In its established use, it identifies particular content that has been revealed and carries guidance, signs, warnings, narratives, and teachings.

This is compatible with the verbal-noun meaning. A word may first denote an action and then name something associated with that action.

Qur’an and kitāb

The Qur’an uses both kitāb and Qur’an for the revelation sent down.

For example:

كِتَابٌ فُصِّلَتْ آيَاتُهُ قُرْآنًا عَرَبِيًّا لِقَوْمٍ يَعْلَمُونَ

kitābun fuṣṣilat āyātuhu qurʾānan ʿarabiyyan li-qawmin yaʿlamūna

“A book whose verses have been set out in detail, an Arabic Qur’an for people who know.”

Fuṣṣilat 41:3

Also:

تِلْكَ آيَاتُ الْقُرْآنِ وَكِتَابٍ مُبِينٍ

tilka āyātu al-qurʾāni wa-kitābin mubīnin

“These are the verses of the Qur’an and a clear book.”

Al-Naml 27:1

Kitāb and Qur’an are two distinct expressions that can refer to one revelation.

K T B concerns writing, recording, and joining the components of writing; Q R ʾ in its familiar use concerns reading and recitation.

This difference must not imply that kitāb in the Qur’an is always only a written object and Qur’an always only audible sound. The Qur’an itself uses both expressions for the revelation sent down.

The distinction between the roots is real, while their ranges of application overlap.

An Arabic Qur’an

Yūsuf says:

إِنَّا أَنْزَلْنَاهُ قُرْآنًا عَرَبِيًّا لَعَلَّكُمْ تَعْقِلُونَ

innā anzalnāhu qurʾānan ʿarabiyyan laʿallakum taʿqilūna

“Indeed, We have sent it down as an Arabic Qur’an, so that you may reason.”

Yūsuf 12:2

Al-Zukhruf likewise says:

إِنَّا جَعَلْنَاهُ قُرْآنًا عَرَبِيًّا لَعَلَّكُمْ تَعْقِلُونَ

innā jaʿalnāhu qurʾānan ʿarabiyyan laʿallakum taʿqilūna

“Indeed, We have made it an Arabic Qur’an, so that you may reason.”

Al-Zukhruf 43:3

These verses connect the Qur’an with Arabic and the audience’s reasoning.

ʿArabiyyan identifies its language; laʿallakum taʿqilūn expresses the purpose of understanding and reasoning. These are fundamental in context, but the lexical meaning of Qur’an alone is not reasoning.

The distinction matters in translation: Qur’an should not become the book of understanding and thought, even though understanding and thinking are clear aims of engagement with it.

Qurʾān al-fajr: recitation at a defined time

Al-Isrāʾ says:

أَقِمِ الصَّلَاةَ لِدُلُوكِ الشَّمْسِ إِلَىٰ غَسَقِ اللَّيْلِ وَقُرْآنَ الْفَجْرِ إِنَّ قُرْآنَ الْفَجْرِ كَانَ مَشْهُودًا

aqimi al-ṣalāta li-dulūki al-shamsi ilā ghasaqi al-layli wa-qurʾāna al-fajri inna qurʾāna al-fajri kāna mashhūdan

“Establish prayer from the sun’s decline until the darkness of night, and the recitation of dawn; indeed, the recitation of dawn is witnessed.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:78

Qurʾān al-fajr can be understood as reading or recitation at dawn.

The word Qurʾān is therefore not always merely the proper name of the entire Book. Its verbal-noun meaning remains in some constructions.

The connection with prayer is clear from context, but its complete manner, form, and details must not be extracted from Q R ʾ alone.

Reciting the Qur’an and reflecting on it

The Qur’an connects reading, listening, reflection, and remembrance.

For example:

أَفَلَا يَتَدَبَّرُونَ الْقُرْآنَ

a-fa-lā yatadabbarūna al-qurʾāna

“Do they not reflect on the Qur’an?”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:82

And:

أَفَلَا يَتَدَبَّرُونَ الْقُرْآنَ أَمْ عَلَىٰ قُلُوبٍ أَقْفَالُهَا

a-fa-lā yatadabbarūna al-qurʾāni am ʿalā qulūbin aqfāluhā

“Do they not reflect on the Qur’an, or are there locks upon hearts?”

Muḥammad 47:24

Reflection is a separate injunction in these verses. Reading may prepare for reflection, but merely pronouncing the words does not guarantee it.

Conversely, reflection’s importance must not remove reading from qaraʾa. The Qur’an discusses both reading and hearing and thinking about the content.

Recitation is thus a real linguistic act. Understanding, reflection, and acting on guidance are responsibilities discussed across a wider network of verses.

5. Qurūʾ: menstrual periods and periods of purity

Al-Baqarah contains a different use of Q R ʾ:

وَالْمُطَلَّقَاتُ يَتَرَبَّصْنَ بِأَنْفُسِهِنَّ ثَلَاثَةَ قُرُوءٍ

wa-al-muṭallaqātu yatarabbaṣna bi-anfusihinna thalāthata qurūʾin

“Divorced women shall wait for three periods.”

Al-Baqarah 2:228

Qurūʾ is a plural of qarʾ.

Early Arabic applies qarʾ to periods associated with menstruation and purity. A familiar lexical disagreement concerns its precise referent: some understand menstruation and others the period of purity.

Some lexicographers explain both uses through a defined time, because menstruation and purity each occupy a distinct period in a woman’s biological cycle.

Others seek a connection with gathering, such as blood gathering or a period encompassing a biological condition.

Another explanation emphasizes transition between conditions: from purity into menstruation or from menstruation into purity.

Qarʾ therefore does not have one entirely uncontested lexical analysis.

Al-Rāghib’s commentary: qarʾ and transition between menstruation and purity

Must qarʾ mean menstruation or purity?

Both meanings are historically attested. Simply repeating that the root’s foundation is gathering cannot establish either as the verse’s definite meaning.

The verse’s context and other verses on the waiting period must also be examined.

Al-Baqarah 2:228 continues:

وَلَا يَحِلُّ لَهُنَّ أَنْ يَكْتُمْنَ مَا خَلَقَ اللَّهُ فِي أَرْحَامِهِنَّ

wa-lā yaḥillu lahunna an yaktumna mā khalaqa allāhu fī arḥāmihinna

“It is not lawful for them to conceal what The God has created in their wombs.”

This connects the waiting period with the womb’s condition and possible pregnancy.

Al-Ṭalāq also says:

وَاللَّائِي يَئِسْنَ مِنَ الْمَحِيضِ مِنْ نِسَائِكُمْ إِنِ ارْتَبْتُمْ فَعِدَّتُهُنَّ ثَلَاثَةُ أَشْهُرٍ وَاللَّائِي لَمْ يَحِضْنَ

wa-al-lāʾī yaʾisna mina al-maḥīḍi min nisāʾikum ini irtabtum fa-ʿiddatuhunna thalāthatu ashhurin wa-al-lāʾī lam yaḥiḍna

“Those of your women who have ceased menstruating, if you are in doubt, their waiting period is three months; and likewise those who have not menstruated.”

Al-Ṭalāq 65:4

For women with a different menstrual situation, the verse specifies three months.

Three qurūʾ should therefore not, without evidence, be equated with three months. Qurūʾ concerns women’s biological cycles, whereas ashhur counts months.

For the base translation of Al-Baqarah 2:228, three periods is suitable and cautious: it conveys periodicity while avoiding a definite choice between the familiar meanings of menstruation and purity before examining the verses fully.

Lexical caution does not make the precise beginning and end of the waiting period legally unimportant. That requires an independent study of the verses on divorce and waiting periods.

Al-Ṭabarī: disagreement over qarʾ and the waiting-period context

The relationship between qurūʾ and recitation

Qur’anic morphological indexes assign recitation and qurūʾ to a shared root. Lexical explanations also seek connections through gathering, a defined time, or transition between conditions.

This does not establish that recitation originally meant menstruation or that qurūʾ originally meant reading.

The branches differ in Qur’anic use. A root study must preserve both without turning one into the other through unsupported imagery.

6. Distinguishing related roots

Q R ʾ (qaraʾa) and T L W (talā)

Qaraʾa concerns reading and reciting.

Talā belongs to the field of succession, following, and reciting.

In many Qur’anic uses, both verbs concern reading and presenting verses, but their semantic paths differ.

Q R ʾ emphasizes reading or recitation itself. T L W also has following and succession in its linguistic background.

This must not become an absolute rule implying that recitation has no continuity or that tilāwah always goes beyond reading.

Study of T L W

Q R ʾ (qaraʾa) and R T L (rattala)

Al-Muzzammil says:

وَرَتِّلِ الْقُرْآنَ تَرْتِيلًا

wa-rattili al-qurʾāna tartīlan

“And recite the Qur’an with measured recitation.”

Al-Muzzammil 73:4

Two different roots are connected in one construction.

Qurʾān concerns recitation; rattil describes its orderly, measured quality.

Tartīl can describe the quality of recitation, but qaraʾa alone does not encompass all its features.

Al-Muzzammil 73:20 likewise commands fa-iqraʾū mā tayassara mina al-qurʾān: recite what is easy of the Qur’an. The verb itself specifies neither an amount of text nor a fixed speed.

Q R ʾ (qaraʾa) and D R S (darasa)

Qaraʾa concerns reading and reciting, whereas darasa concerns study, learning, and engagement with a text or knowledge.

Al-Aʿrāf says:

وَدَرَسُوا مَا فِيهِ

wa-darasū mā fīhi

“And they have studied what is in it.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:169

Darasa concerns familiarity with and study of the book’s contents.

Reading can form part of study, but recitation alone does not establish that deep study or correct knowledge has occurred.

Study of D R S

Q R ʾ (qaraʾa) and K T B (kataba)

Qaraʾa concerns reading; kataba concerns writing and recording.

The acts are related through text, but they are not identical.

One may read writing or recite speech without having a written text.

The Qur’an uses both kitāb and Qur’an for revelation. Their overlap establishes neither complete identity of root meanings nor an absolute separation between revelation’s spoken and written forms.

Study of K T B

Q R ʾ (qaraʾa) and Q R Y (qaryah)

Q R ʾ and Q R Y are separate roots in Qur’anic morphological indexing.

Q R ʾ concerns reading, the Qur’an, and qurūʾ. Q R Y in Qur’anic use concerns a settlement and settlements.

Some lexicographers suggest a connection through gathering. Semantic resemblance and similar letters alone, however, cannot establish one historical origin for every derivative.

Qaryah must therefore not be counted as a Qur’anic derivative of Q R ʾ, nor should Qur’an and qaryah be assigned the same morphological root.

Study of Q R Y

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Qaraʾa: read, recited; depending on the object and context.

Iqraʾ: read; recite.

Iqraʾū: read; recite, addressed to a plural audience.

Quriʾa: was read; was recited.

Yaqraʾūna: they read; they recite.

Sa-nuqriʾuka: We will cause you to read; We will enable you to recite.

Qurʾān: Qur’an as the proper name; recitation or reading as a verbal noun.

Qurūʾ: periods; in Al-Baqarah 2:228, periods associated with menstruation and purity.

Some suitable examples:

اقْرَأْ بِاسْمِ رَبِّكَ (Iqraʾ bi-ismi rabbika): read in the name of your Lord.

اقْرَأْ كِتَابَكَ (Iqraʾ kitābaka): read your book.

فَقَرَأَهُ عَلَيْهِمْ (Fa-qaraʾahu ʿalayhim): then he recited it to them.

لِتَقْرَأَهُ عَلَى النَّاسِ عَلَىٰ مُكْثٍ (Li-taqraʾahu ʿalā al-nāsi ʿalā mukthin): so that you may recite it to the people with deliberation.

سَنُقْرِئُكَ فَلَا تَنْسَىٰ (Sa-nuqriʾuka fa-lā tansā): We will cause you to recite, so you will not forget.

إِنَّ عَلَيْنَا جَمْعَهُ وَقُرْآنَهُ (Inna ʿalaynā jamʿahu wa-qurʾānahu): indeed, gathering it and reciting it are Our responsibility.

وَقُرْآنَ الْفَجْرِ (Wa-qurʾāna al-fajri): and the recitation of dawn.

ثَلَاثَةَ قُرُوءٍ (Thalāthata qurūʾin): three periods.

8. Conclusion

Q R ʾ in its familiar Qur’anic use concerns reading and reciting. This may be from a book, from memory, or by presenting speech to an audience. A physical written document is therefore not an invariable requirement, just as reading alone does not establish understanding, reflection, or action upon the content.

Qurʾān is associated with recitation and, in extensive Qur’anic use, becomes the established name of the revelation sent down to Muhammad. In constructions such as qurʾānahu in Al-Qiyāmah 75:17 and Al-Qiyāmah 75:18, and qurʾān al-fajr in Al-Isrāʾ 17:78, the verbal-noun meaning of reading or recitation remains clear.

Alongside this branch, qurūʾ in Al-Baqarah 2:228 concerns women’s biological cycles. The family therefore cannot be explained solely through one narrow definition of reading. Nor should the historical relationship of every branch be declared certain through one unproven image.

Brief root meaning for Forqan:

Reading and reciting; assembling and presenting the parts of discourse continuously. In another branch, qarʾ denotes a period or defined time, particularly within women’s cycle of menstruation and purity.

Each derivative’s precise meaning must be determined from its form and the context of the particular verse.
