1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

The three-letter Arabic root ق ر أ has two prominent semantic fields: reading and voicing ordered speech, and a specified period or state within women’s cycle of menstruation and purity. قُرْآن and the verb قَرَأَ belong to the first field; قُرُوء belongs to uses concerning time, periods, and women’s bodily states.

The root occurs 88 times in the Qur’an. Its Qur’anic forms are:

  • قَرَأَ / يَقْرَأُ: to read, recite, and voice speech. Used in the Qur’an: yes, in several Form I inflections.
  • اِقْرَأْ: read; imperative. Used in the Qur’an: yes.
  • قُرِئَ: was read; passive. Used in the Qur’an: yes.
  • أَقْرَأَ / يُقْرِئُ: to cause someone to read, teach someone to read, or convey something for recitation. Used in the Qur’an: yes, as سَنُقْرِئُكَ.
  • قُرْآن: a noun associated with reading and recitation, and in familiar Qur’anic usage the name of the revealed message. Used in the Qur’an: yes.
  • قَرْء / قُرْء: a period or state connected with menstruation and purity. Used in the Qur’an: yes, only as the plural قُرُوء.
  • قِرَاءَة: reading or recitation. Used in the Qur’an: no, in this nominal form.
  • قَارِئ / قُرَّاء: reader and readers. Used in the Qur’an: no.
  • مَقْرُوء: something that is read. Used in the Qur’an: no.
  • أَقْرَاء / أَقْرُؤ: other plurals of قَرْء. Used in the Qur’an: no.
  • مُقَارَأَة / اِقْتِرَاء / اِسْتِقْرَاء: forms concerning shared reading, reading, or systematic sustained examination. Used in the Qur’an: no.

The Qur’anic count comprises sixteen Form I verbs, one Form IV verb, one occurrence of قُرُوء, and seventy occurrences of قُرْآن.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: 88 occurrences in four morphological groups

This count reveals something important: although Qurʾān often names the revealed message, in some expressions it retains the function of a noun denoting the act of recitation. Not every occurrence should therefore be treated solely as the proper name of a book without attention to context.

2. The linguistic core and evidence from older Arabic

Gathering and connection: an explanation with limits

Arabic dictionaries record “gathering” and “joining one thing to another” among the meanings of قَرَأَ. Expressions about something accumulating in a container or an animal’s womb illustrate this use.

For example, مَا قَرَأَتْ هَذِهِ النَّاقَةُ جَنِينًا is recorded in Arabic: this she-camel has not carried a fetus in her womb.

This is not reading. The verb concerns a fetus gathering or being present in the animal’s womb.

A witness recorded in older lexical sources reads:

ذِرَاعَيْ عَيْطَلٍ أَدْمَاءَ بِكْرٍ
هِجَانِ اللَّوْنِ لَمْ تَقْرَأْ جَنِينًا

dhirāʿay ʿayṭalin admāʾa bikrin / hijāni al-lawni lam taqraʾ janīnan

The verse compares a woman’s arms with those of a young, fine-colored she-camel that has not carried a fetus; the latter description is the relevant lexical witness here.

This helps establish a non-recitative use of the root. Its presence in a dictionary does not, however, prove that every other meaning developed from it.

Al-Zajjāj’s Maʿānī al-Qurʾān: the arm comparison and lam taqraʾ janīnan

Recitation has also been explained as the reader joining letters and words in a particular order and voicing them. This is linguistically intelligible: recitation is an ordered sequence of expressions rather than a random collection of isolated sounds.

Two propositions must nevertheless be distinguished: reading involves connecting words; and قَرَأَ directly means “gather” in every verse.

The first is sound and explicable; the second does not follow from Qur’anic evidence.

In اقْرَأْ كِتَابَكَ, the addressee is commanded to read his record, not assemble its parts. Similarly, فَاسْتَمِعُوا لَهُ in Al-Aʿrāf 7:204 shows that recitation there concerns audible speech.

Gathering and connection may help explain recitation’s lexical background, but “read” and “recite” are the verb’s direct meanings in clear Qur’anic uses.

Qarʾ as an appointed time or period

Another use concerns time and periods. In older Arabic, قَرْء and some related forms are recorded for a specified time or the occasion when an event occurs.

For example, a verse whose attribution differs between poets reads:

شَنِئْتُ الْعَقْرَ عَقْرَ بَنِي شُلَيْلٍ
إِذَا هَبَّتْ لِقَارِئِهَا الرِّيَاحُ

shaniʾtu al-ʿaqra ʿaqra banī shulaylin / idhā habbat li-qāriʾihā al-riyāḥu

That is: “I disliked Al-ʿAqr, the Al-ʿAqr of Banū Shulayl, when the winds blew at their appointed time.”

لِقَارِئِهَا concerns the time or season of winds blowing, rather than reading writing or reciting speech.

Poetic collections transmit the verse under differing attributions, including Taʾabbaṭa Sharran and other poets. Its temporal usage is relevant, but it should not be presented as conclusively pre-Islamic without resolving that attribution.

Al-Basīṭ: the temporal sense of qarʾ and the Hudhalī poetic witness

This shows why the temporal meaning of qarʾ cannot be ignored. That field is central to examining قُرُوء in Al-Baqarah 2:228.

Do all the root’s meanings have one origin?

Lexical explanations have proposed several connections between recitation and qarʾ: gathering, reaching an appointed time, and transition from one state to another.

Each explains part of the recorded usage, but the available evidence does not establish any one account as the unqualified, certain origin of every meaning.

It is therefore preferable to distinguish the two fields from the outset: recitation of speech and a time-bound period. Any historical connection between them requires independent evidence.

3. Qaraʾa: reading writing and reciting speech

Al-ʿAlaq 96:1, Al-ʿAlaq 96:2, Al-ʿAlaq 96:3, Al-ʿAlaq 96:4, and Al-ʿAlaq 96:5: the command to read

Verse texts

اقْرَأْ بِاسْمِ رَبِّكَ الَّذِي خَلَقَ

iqraʾ bi-ismi rabbika alladhī khalaqa

خَلَقَ الْإِنْسَانَ مِنْ عَلَقٍ

khalaqa al-insāna min ʿalaqin

اقْرَأْ وَرَبُّكَ الْأَكْرَمُ

iqraʾ wa-rabbuka al-akramu

الَّذِي عَلَّمَ بِالْقَلَمِ

alladhī ʿallama bi-al-qalami

عَلَّمَ الْإِنْسَانَ مَا لَمْ يَعْلَمْ

ʿallama al-insāna mā lam yaʿlam

English translation

Read in the name of your Lord who created.

He created the human being from a clinging substance.

Read, and your Lord is the most generous.

Who taught by the pen.

He taught the human being what he did not know.

Usage analysis

The imperative اقْرَأْ occurs twice. The addressee is commanded to read, but the first command supplies no explicit object such as a book or sheet.

This matters. Iqraʾ alone does not establish that a physical written object must have been before the addressee. Nor does an absent explicit object establish that the verb has lost its ordinary reading sense and become “gathering” or “inward understanding.”

Recitation can be performed from writing or from speech already known or received. Its mode depends on the subject and context.

بِاسْمِ رَبِّكَ specifies the manner or orientation of fulfilling the command, but does not directly name what is to be read.

The following verses concern human creation, teaching, and the pen. Reading is thus situated within creation, teaching, and the transmission of knowledge.

The verb qaraʾa itself nevertheless does not mean “learn knowledge.” Reading can serve teaching, but the act of recitation and its cognitive outcome should be distinguished.

The direct translation of iqraʾ is therefore “read.” What the addressee reads and how it was received belong to contextual reflection.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:14: reading the record of deeds

Verse text

اقْرَأْ كِتَابَكَ كَفَىٰ بِنَفْسِكَ الْيَوْمَ عَلَيْكَ حَسِيبًا

iqraʾ kitābaka kafā bi-nafsika al-yawma ʿalayka ḥasīban

English translation

Read your record; today you yourself suffice as an accountant against yourself.

Usage analysis

Iqraʾ has a clear object here: كِتَابَكَ.

The addressee is commanded to read his own record. The preceding verses identify the subject as recording and presenting human deeds on the day of account.

Reading can thus concern writing or a record available for consultation. Yet the verb itself does not mean “calculate.” Accounting appears in the second sentence and is connected with reading the record.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:71, those given their record in their right hand read it. In Al-Isrāʾ 17:93, opponents demand that a book be sent down that they can read.

These show that Qur’anic qaraʾa can concern reading a particular text or record and should not be restricted to ritual recitation.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:204: recitation that is heard

Verse text

وَإِذَا قُرِئَ الْقُرْآنُ فَاسْتَمِعُوا لَهُ وَأَنْصِتُوا لَعَلَّكُمْ تُرْحَمُونَ

wa-idhā quriʾa al-qurʾānu fa-istamiʿū lahu wa-anṣitū laʿallakum turḥamūna

English translation

When the Qur’an is recited, listen to it and remain silent, so that you may receive mercy.

Usage analysis

قُرِئَ is a passive perfect verb. The reader is unnamed; the act of the Qur’an being read is placed at the sentence’s center.

The commands فَاسْتَمِعُوا and أَنْصِتُوا show that this recitation is audible. The audience is to listen to the speech being read and remain silent.

Recitation here is therefore more than a person’s silent mental study: it involves the audible presentation of speech.

Listening and silence do not reduce the verse’s entire purpose to sound. لَعَلَّكُمْ تُرْحَمُونَ opens a horizon beyond hearing, but understanding, reflection, and accepting the message must be examined through the verses concerning guidance and contemplation.

Nor does the verse alone establish, without independent grounds, that listening is commanded only during one particular ceremony or legal setting. Its scope requires examination of the audience and context.

Recitation and conveying speech to others

Yūnus 10:94 identifies those who read the scripture before the verse’s addressee. In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:199, فَقَرَأَهُ عَلَيْهِمْ concerns reading speech to another group.

Recitation is therefore more than someone’s relationship with writing. A reader can read a text or speech to others and convey it to them.

Reading for oneself, reading a record, and reading to others should thus be distinguished situationally, though all belong to the broad field of recitation.

4. Aqraʾa and Qurʾān: teaching recitation and naming revelation

Al-Aʿlā 87:6: سَنُقْرِئُكَ

Verse text

سَنُقْرِئُكَ فَلَا تَنْسَىٰ

sa-nuqriʾuka fa-lā tansā

English translation

We shall cause you to recite, and you will not forget.

Usage analysis

سَنُقْرِئُكَ is Form IV, with sa- indicating futurity. Here it means that The God causes the Prophet to recite or teaches him recitation.

The distinction from تَقْرَأُ is clear. In taqraʾu, the person performs the reading; in sanuqriʾuka, another agent brings about the addressee’s reading.

Form IV does not automatically establish the details of how revelation is transmitted. Morphology makes the causative role apparent; its realization requires context and other verses.

فَلَا تَنْسَىٰ also concerns retaining the recitation. Reading and remembering are associated, but are not synonymous. The following verse, Al-Aʿlā 87:7, includes إِلَّا مَا شَاءَ اللَّهُ; the promise and the interpretation of this exception must therefore be considered together, rather than deriving an unrestricted conclusion from the first verse alone.

Al-Ṭabarī on Al-Aʿlā 87:6: teaching recitation and retaining it

Al-Qiyāmah 75:16, Al-Qiyāmah 75:17, Al-Qiyāmah 75:18, and Al-Qiyāmah 75:19: gathering, recitation, and explanation

Verse texts

لَا تُحَرِّكْ بِهِ لِسَانَكَ لِتَعْجَلَ بِهِ

lā tuḥarrik bihi lisānaka li-taʿjala bihi

إِنَّ عَلَيْنَا جَمْعَهُ وَقُرْآنَهُ

inna ʿalaynā jamʿahu wa-qurʾānahu

فَإِذَا قَرَأْنَاهُ فَاتَّبِعْ قُرْآنَهُ

fa-idhā qaraʾnāhu fa-ittabiʿ qurʾānahu

ثُمَّ إِنَّ عَلَيْنَا بَيَانَهُ

thumma inna ʿalaynā bayānahu

English translation

Do not move your tongue with it to hasten it.

Its gathering and its recitation are indeed Our responsibility.

When We have recited it, follow its recitation.

Then its explanation is also Our responsibility.

Usage analysis

These verses are among the root’s most important witnesses: within a few verses, the Qur’an mentions gathering, Qurʾān, qaraʾa, and explanation.

In جَمْعَهُ وَقُرْآنَهُ, two nouns are joined by a conjunction. Although recitation can involve connected, ordered words, their association does not establish that Qurʾān here is identical with gathering or that the words have no distinction.

جَمْعَهُ belongs to ج م ع, whereas قُرْآنَهُ belongs to ق ر أ. Their occurrence together shows a semantic connection, not complete identity.

In the next verse, فَإِذَا قَرَأْنَاهُ attributes recitation to The God. Recitation here is therefore not restricted to the human ability to read writing.

فَاتَّبِعْ قُرْآنَهُ introduces following the recitation. This occurs within a prohibition of haste and should not be reduced without examination to a bodily act or vocal imitation.

بَيَانَهُ follows. Explanation and clarification form another aspect or stage of engaging revelation.

The verses thus associate four related but distinct concepts: gathering, recitation, following, and explanation.

قُرْآنَهُ can readily carry the verbal-noun sense “its recitation” here. Qurʾān therefore retains the capacity to express the act of reading and recitation, rather than functioning exclusively as a book title in every instance.

Qurʾān: a verbal noun or the name of revelation?

In Arabic structure, قُرْآن is comparable with verbal nouns on the fuʿlān pattern. Lexically, its reading and recitation sense is established.

Within the Qur’an, however, it is widely used for the revelation sent down to Muhammad.

For example, Yūsuf 12:2 states:

إِنَّا أَنْزَلْنَاهُ قُرْآنًا عَرَبِيًّا لَعَلَّكُمْ تَعْقِلُونَ

innā anzalnāhu qurʾānan ʿarabiyyan laʿallakum taʿqilūna

English translation

We have indeed sent it down as an Arabic Qur’an, so that you may reason.

Qurʾān is qualified here by عَرَبِيًّا and refers to something sent down.

It is therefore more than the act of reading in this context; it refers to revelation presented in Arabic.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:9 also presents the Qur’an as guiding, and Al-Nisāʾ 4:82 invites reflection upon it. These usages identify a message and revelatory whole with meaning and content.

Two uses must be distinguished: Qurʾān as a verbal noun denoting recitation, and Qurʾān as the recognized name of revelation. This distinction does not rest on the presence or absence of al- alone; without al-, قُرْآنًا in Yūsuf 12:2 likewise denotes revelation.

In the first, reading and recitation are prominent; in the second, the reference is to a particular message sent down by The God.

This prevents two errors: severing the Qur’an’s name from its linguistic connection with recitation, or translating every Qurʾān merely as “reading” while overlooking the revealed text’s identity.

5. The Qur’an: recitation situated in time, heard, and understood

Al-Isrāʾ 17:106: reading to people with deliberation

Verse text

وَقُرْآنًا فَرَقْنَاهُ لِتَقْرَأَهُ عَلَى النَّاسِ عَلَىٰ مُكْثٍ وَنَزَّلْنَاهُ تَنْزِيلًا

wa-qurʾānan faraqnāhu li-taqraʾahu ʿalā al-nāsi ʿalā mukthin wa-nazzalnāhu tanzīlan

English translation

And a Qur’an that We have separated, so that you may read it to people with deliberation; We have sent it down progressively.

Usage analysis

قُرْآنًا and لِتَقْرَأَهُ share a root but have different grammatical roles.

Qurʾānan is a noun; li-taqraʾahu is an imperfect verb in the subjunctive after the purposive lām, expressing the stated purpose of separating the Qur’an.

The Qur’an is presented so that the Prophet may read it to people. عَلَى النَّاسِ identifies the audience; عَلَىٰ مُكْثٍ concerns deliberation and avoiding haste.

Recitation here conveys the message to people. It is more than private reading, being connected with communicating and presenting speech to an audience.

This does not make qaraʾa an exact lexical synonym of ballagha, “convey.” Recitation is the act used here to deliver the message; communication is its contextual function.

فَرَقْنَاهُ and نَزَّلْنَاهُ تَنْزِيلًا should also be distinguished. The first concerns separation and differentiation, while the closing expression concerns descent. Their relationship requires separate examination of ف ر ق and the verse’s structure.

Al-Muzzammil 73:4: recitation and tartīl

Verse text

أَوْ زِدْ عَلَيْهِ وَرَتِّلِ الْقُرْآنَ تَرْتِيلًا

aw zid ʿalayhi wa-rattili al-qurʾāna tartīlan

English translation

Or add to it, and recite the Qur’an with measured articulation.

Usage analysis

The direct command is رَتِّلْ, from ر ت ل rather than ق ر أ.

Its object is الْقُرْآنَ. The Qur’an is thus something to be recited with a particular quality.

Recitation and tartīl are connected but not identical. Recitation names the reading act; tartīl concerns the manner and order of its articulation.

Treating them as absolute synonyms would overlook the command rattil’s role.

Al-Muzzammil 73:20 also commands فَاقْرَءُوا مَا تَيَسَّرَ مِنَ الْقُرْآنِ. The same verse mentions different human circumstances, including illness, traveling through the land seeking the bounty of The God, and fighting in His way.

The amount of recitation here is connected with what is possible or easy. No fixed, identical quantity for everyone should be extracted without examining the whole verse.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:78: the dawn recitation

Verse text

أَقِمِ الصَّلَاةَ لِدُلُوكِ الشَّمْسِ إِلَىٰ غَسَقِ اللَّيْلِ وَقُرْآنَ الْفَجْرِ إِنَّ قُرْآنَ الْفَجْرِ كَانَ مَشْهُودًا

aqimi al-ṣalāta li-dulūki al-shamsi ilā ghasaqi al-layli wa-qurʾāna al-fajri inna qurʾāna al-fajri kāna mashhūdan

English translation

Establish prayer from the sun’s decline until the darkness of night, and the dawn recitation; the dawn recitation is indeed witnessed.

Usage analysis

قُرْآنَ الْفَجْرِ occurs twice. The expression is clearly connected lexically with the verbal-noun meaning “recitation.”

The addition of al-fajr specifies its time. “Dawn recitation” therefore makes the linguistic meaning clearer than simply repeating the book’s name.

Yet this recitation occurs within the command to establish prayer, and its relationship with prayer cannot be ignored.

The accusative قُرْآنَ is also significant. It is connected with the preceding command, but its precise grammatical analysis should not be determined by a translation assumed in advance.

مَشْهُودًا belongs to ش ه د. The verse calls the dawn recitation witnessed, but Qurʾān itself does not identify the witnesses or explain how the witnessing occurs.

This is important evidence that Qurʾān in some Qur’anic expressions retains the meaning of the act of recitation.

Reading, listening, and reflection

The Qur’an distinguishes recitation from human responses to it. Al-Aʿrāf 7:204 concerns listening and silence; Al-Nisāʾ 4:82, reflection; Al-Muzzammil 73:4, measured articulation.

These acts are connected, but none alone encompasses the entire meaning of another.

Someone may read words without reflecting on their content; someone else may hear recitation without attending to its message.

The verses together make these differences apparent. Understanding and reflection should not be inserted without explanation into qaraʾa’s lexical meaning, just as recitation’s cognitive role should not be reduced to producing sound.

6. Qurūʾ: women’s bodily periods in Al-Baqarah 2:228

Verse text

وَالْمُطَلَّقَاتُ يَتَرَبَّصْنَ بِأَنْفُسِهِنَّ ثَلَاثَةَ قُرُوءٍ وَلَا يَحِلُّ لَهُنَّ أَنْ يَكْتُمْنَ مَا خَلَقَ اللَّهُ فِي أَرْحَامِهِنَّ إِنْ كُنَّ يُؤْمِنَّ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ وَبُعُولَتُهُنَّ أَحَقُّ بِرَدِّهِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ إِنْ أَرَادُوا إِصْلَاحًا وَلَهُنَّ مِثْلُ الَّذِي عَلَيْهِنَّ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ وَلِلرِّجَالِ عَلَيْهِنَّ دَرَجَةٌ وَاللَّهُ عَزِيزٌ حَكِيمٌ

wa-al-muṭallaqātu yatarabbaṣna bi-anfusihinna thalāthata qurūʾin wa-lā yaḥillu lahunna an yaktumna mā khalaqa Allāhu fī arḥāmihinna in kunna yuʾminna bi-Allāhi wa-al-yawmi al-ākhiri wa-buʿūlatuhunna aḥaqqu bi-raddihinna fī dhālika in arādū iṣlāḥan wa-lahunna mithlu alladhī ʿalayhinna bi-al-maʿrūfi wa-li-al-rijāli ʿalayhinna darajatun wa-Allāhu ʿazīzun ḥakīmun

English translation

Divorced women shall wait concerning themselves for three periods. They may not conceal what The God has created in their wombs, if they believe in The God and the Last Day. Their husbands have greater entitlement to take them back during that time if they desire reconciliation. Women have rights, in an accepted manner, corresponding to the responsibilities upon them; men have a degree over them. The God is mighty and wise.

What does qurūʾ mean?

قُرُوء is a plural of قَرْء. Arabic uses qarʾ for menstruation, the period of purity between menstruations, and sometimes the appointed time or transition between these states.

This multiplicity is real and should not be ignored.

Some lexicographers connect qarʾ with menstruation, others also use it for purity from menstruation, and some propose the broader meaning of an appointed time or transition between states.

A major disagreement follows: does ثَلَاثَةَ قُرُوءٍ mean three menstrual periods, three periods of purity, or three specified stages in the cycle linking menstruation and purity?

The plural qurūʾ alone does not settle this.

An Arabic word can be used for two related or even contrasting states. Recording those usages does not mean that both are intended simultaneously and equally in a particular sentence.

Translation and interpretation must examine which meaning the construction and context support.

Does “gathering” settle the disagreement?

One argument selects purity by claiming that, because qaraʾa concerns gathering, qarʾ must denote the period when blood gathers in the womb.

An opposing argument can identify qarʾ with the period when accumulated blood becomes apparent or leaves, thus selecting menstruation.

Both face the same problem: an etymological relationship alone does not determine the precise timing of a bodily process.

Even if gathering is accepted as part of the word’s background, it does not conclusively establish whether the period’s name denotes the time before discharge, during discharge, or the boundary between the states.

“Gathering” is therefore limited evidence for qurūʾ in Al-Baqarah 2:228, not a final deciding proof.

The recorded “appointed time” sense also matters, since it suits the periodicity of women’s bodily states. It does not alone determine precisely where counting three qurʾ begins and ends.

What does the context clarify?

The verse concerns divorced women, waiting, not concealing what is in their wombs, and the possibility of returning for reconciliation.

Qurūʾ here therefore concerns recognized women’s bodily periods, not any arbitrary temporal period such as three days, three months, or three seasons.

وَلَا يَحِلُّ لَهُنَّ أَنْ يَكْتُمْنَ مَا خَلَقَ اللَّهُ فِي أَرْحَامِهِنَّ also shows the importance of the womb’s condition. Without further examination, however, it does not establish that qarʾ lexically means only menstrual blood or only purity.

Al-Ṭalāq 65:4 uses ثَلَاثَةُ أَشْهُرٍ for certain situations and connects the term for pregnancy with childbirth. Qurūʾ should therefore not be treated without evidence as synonymous with three calendar months.

Al-Ṭalāq 65:1 also concerns the timing of divorce and counting the waiting period. It matters for its beginning and end, but its precise relationship with qurūʾ requires independent study of divorce in the Qur’an.

The lexical conclusion about qurūʾ

Linguistically, qurūʾ concerns recognized periods within women’s cycle of menstruation and purity.

This prevents rendering it simply as “three months,” while also preventing either menstruation or purity from being declared the sole possible meaning without sufficient reasoning.

For direct translation, “three periods” or “three periods of the menstrual cycle” can preserve some of the original ambiguity; the disagreement over their boundaries can then be explained separately.

A final reflection on Al-Baqarah 2:228 should nevertheless examine all relevant verses and either select a more precise meaning or clearly retain the uncertainty if the evidence does not justify a conclusive decision.

A lexical article should not conceal a valid Arabic usage to support a previously adopted legal position.

Al-Basīṭ: differing qurūʾ meanings and the argument from numeral gender

7. Morphology, grammar, and distinctions from related roots

Qaraʾa, qirāʾah, and Qurʾān

قَرَأَ is Form I. قِرَاءَة and قُرْآن are associated with its verbal nouns, although the latter becomes a recognized name for revelation in the Qur’an.

Qurʾān naming a particular text does not eliminate its verbal-noun capacity. Al-Qiyāmah 75:17, Al-Qiyāmah 75:18, and Al-Isrāʾ 17:78 illustrate this.

The passive قُرِئَ also shows how reading can be foregrounded without naming the reader.

Form I and Form IV

قَرَأَ denotes reading itself; أَقْرَأَ can express causing another to recite.

This difference matters in Al-Aʿlā 87:6: The God says سَنُقْرِئُكَ, not سَتَقْرَأُ.

Morphology alone nevertheless does not establish how revelation is taught, heard, or retained.

Qurūʾ and its plural structure

قُرُوء is a broken plural of قَرْء. Arabic also records أَقْرَاء and أَقْرُؤ as plurals.

In ثَلَاثَةَ قُرُوءٍ, the singular counted noun qarʾ is grammatically masculine. The numeral thalāthah therefore has tāʾ under the gender-polarity rule for numerals three through ten, not because the numeral and noun have identical grammatical gender.

Neither the plural form nor grammatical gender establishes that menstruation must be intended, or that purity must be intended. Counting and gender rules do not by themselves settle the semantic disagreement.

Q R A compared with T L W (تَلَا)

Tilāwah has an important Qur’anic field of reading and following. Talā can mean reading verses, while other uses concern following or coming after.

Where qaraʾa denotes reading and recitation, talā’s lexical family also retains the capacity to express following.

The distinction should not be made absolute. In some verses both verbs can appropriately be translated “read.”

Identifying all their uses, however, hides differences in lexical and contextual evidence.

In Yūnus 10:61, تَتْلُو occurs with قُرْآن. The Qur’an can thus be the object of tilāwah without the two roots becoming lexically identical.

Study of T L W

Q R A compared with K T B (كَتَبَ)

Writing and reading are connected but different acts.

كَتَبَ concerns recording and writing, while قَرَأَ concerns reading and voicing speech.

Writing can be read, and recited speech can be recorded; not every recitation requires a physical written object before the reader at that moment.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:14 and Al-Isrāʾ 17:93 show reading’s relationship with a book; Al-ʿAlaq 96:1 shows that the command can occur without an explicit object.

Neither root should therefore be reduced to the other without evidence.

Study of K T B

Q R A compared with R T L (رَتَلَ)

Tartīl concerns an orderly, measured manner of delivering speech. The Qur’an is its object in Al-Muzzammil 73:4.

It is therefore one quality of Qur’anic recitation to be examined, not the general meaning of all qaraʾa occurrences.

Study of R T L

Q R A compared with Q R Y (قَرَى)

Final-yāʾ ق ر ي differs morphologically from final-hamza ق ر أ.

Arabic sources record قَرَى for providing food to a guest or gathering things. قَرْيَة also belongs to this family.

Some lexical accounts connect gathering in ق ر ي with certain ق ر أ uses and discuss substitution or overlap between forms.

That does not authorize placing every ق ر ي word, including qaryah, under ق ر أ in the index.

Even if two lexical families have historical connections in particular forms, Qur’anic morphological study must identify each word’s root accurately.

This is especially important for Forqan’s triliteral-root index: Q R A and Q R Y must remain separate entries.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

“Read” is suitable for most Qur’anic uses of قَرَأَ. When a text is read to others or the context foregrounds hearing, “recite” can also be precise.

“Read” directly and naturally renders Al-ʿAlaq 96:1 and Al-ʿAlaq 96:3; “read your record” suits Al-Isrāʾ 17:14, and “when the Qur’an is recited” suits Al-Aʿrāf 7:204.

For سَنُقْرِئُكَ, “We shall cause you to recite” preserves causation; “We shall teach you recitation” is another semantic rendering to consider. The final choice requires Al-Aʿlā 87:6 and Al-Aʿlā 87:7 together.

Where قُرْآن names revelation, retaining “Qur’an” is preferable. In قُرْآنَهُ in Al-Qiyāmah 75:17 and Al-Qiyāmah 75:18, and قُرْآنَ الْفَجْرِ in Al-Isrāʾ 17:78, “recitation” can make the verbal-noun meaning and construction clearer.

For قُرُوء in Al-Baqarah 2:228, a preliminary proposal is “periods connected with the menstrual cycle.” If the main translation uses the shorter “three periods,” the reflection must explain that these are women’s periods of menstruation and purity, rather than three unspecified stretches of time.

Directly rendering it “three menstruations” or “three periods of purity” requires independent assessment of the context and evidence.

“Gather,” “understand,” “learn,” “convey,” and “follow” should not become fixed equivalents of qaraʾa merely because they are conceptually connected with recitation. Each has its own root and semantic field.

9. Final synthesis

Qur’anic ق ر أ has two prominent fields: reading or reciting speech, and periods within women’s cycle of menstruation and purity.

In the first, قَرَأَ denotes reading, whether from writing or received speech, for oneself or for others. Some contexts foreground hearing; others concern reading a record of deeds.

أَقْرَأَ expresses causing another to recite. سَنُقْرِئُكَ in Al-Aʿlā 87:6 clearly illustrates the distinction.

قُرْآن linguistically belongs to recitation, but becomes the recognized name of revelation. Its verbal-noun sense nevertheless remains clear in passages such as Al-Qiyāmah 75:17, Al-Qiyāmah 75:18, and Al-Isrāʾ 17:78.

In the second field, qurūʾ concerns recognized women’s bodily periods. Recorded Arabic uses for menstruation, purity, and an appointed time or transition show why a simple etymological claim cannot settle Al-Baqarah 2:228.

The operative core in the recitation branch is reading and voicing ordered speech. The qarʾ branch concerns an appointed time or period and should not be forced back into “reading” without evidence.

The definition must therefore preserve two boundaries: recitation is connected with gathering, teaching, reflection, and communication but is not fully synonymous with any of them; qurūʾ shares the consonants, but its direct meaning in the divorce verse is neither reading nor gathering words.

This distinction is necessary both for translating Qur’an-related verses and examining family rights and responsibilities in Al-Baqarah 2:228.
