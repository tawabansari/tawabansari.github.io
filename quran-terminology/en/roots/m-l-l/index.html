The Qur’anic Root M L L

Dictation and millah: two uses and their historical relationship

The Qur’anic morphological index records م ل ل 18 times: three verbal occurrences of يُمِلّ / يُمْلِل, all in Al-Baqarah 2:282, and 15 occurrences of the noun مِلَّة.

Arabic also has forms with these consonants in other fields: مَلَّ for becoming weary or tired of something, and مَمْلُول for something exposed to heat. These uses must be examined separately from the two Qur’anic uses.

Shared consonants alone do not establish that all these branches developed from a single historical image. To define the Qur’anic meanings, dictation and millah must first be read in their own sentences.

The Qur’an has two clear uses; treating them as one meaning requires independent evidence:

أَمَلَّ / يُمِلُّ: to speak words for someone else to write down; to dictate.

مِلَّة: a recognized religious way or tradition to which a person or group belongs, and which can be followed, abandoned, or returned to.

The precise historical relationship between these two branches is a separate question and should not enter their definitions before it is established.

Yumlil: the speaker and the writer have distinct roles

All three verbal occurrences appear in the verse about debt. The Qur’an distinguishes the person who writes from the person who states the contents of the document:

فَلْيَكْتُبْ وَلْيُمْلِلِ الَّذِي عَلَيْهِ الْحَقُّ

The writer is to write, and the person who owes the obligation is to dictate.

A little later comes أَوْ لَا يَسْتَطِيعُ أَنْ يُمِلَّ هُوَ—“or he himself cannot dictate.” Where the person owing the obligation is unable, the verse assigns dictation with justice to his guardian:

فَلْيُمْلِلْ وَلِيُّهُ بِالْعَدْلِ

“Then let his guardian dictate with justice.”

Al-Baqarah 2:282

The verse’s construction clarifies the verb. يُمْلِل does not mean to write: the scribe and the person dictating have distinct roles. One states the obligation, and the other records it. يُمْلِل and يُمِلّ are also the uncontracted and contracted forms of the same form IV verb, rather than two verbs with different meanings.

Arabic also uses أَمْلَى / يُمْلِي for this act. Al-Furqan 25:5 says:

فَهِيَ تُمْلَىٰ عَلَيْهِ بُكْرَةً وَأَصِيلًا

According to the opponents’ allegation, those writings are dictated to him morning and evening.

Al-Baqarah 2:282 uses the doubled form يُمْلِل / يُمِلّ, while Al-Furqan 25:5 uses تُمْلَىٰ in a context of speaking material for recording or transmission. The morphological index underlying the count lists tumlā under M L W; it is not included in the 18 occurrences of M L L. This closeness in use matters, but the index’s classification does not by itself settle the history of derivation.

Millah: something that is followed

The other 15 occurrences are all forms of مِلَّة. The verbs the Qur’an uses with this noun are more revealing than a hypothetical original image.

A millah can be followed:

حَتَّىٰ تَتَّبِعَ مِلَّتَهُمْ

“Until you follow their religious way.”

Al-Baqarah 2:120

فَاتَّبِعُوا مِلَّةَ إِبْرَاهِيمَ حَنِيفًا

“Then follow the religious way of Abraham, a ḥanīf.”

Aal ‘Imran 3:95

Someone can turn away from it:

وَمَنْ يَرْغَبُ عَنْ مِلَّةِ إِبْرَاهِيمَ إِلَّا مَنْ سَفِهَ نَفْسَهُ

“Who turns away from Abraham’s religious way except someone who has made a fool of himself?”

Al-Baqarah 2:130

It can be abandoned:

إِنِّي تَرَكْتُ مِلَّةَ قَوْمٍ لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ بِاللَّهِ

“I have abandoned the religious way of a people who do not believe in The God.”

Yusuf 12:37

Someone may be compelled to return to it:

أَوْ لَتَعُودُنَّ فِي مِلَّتِنَا

“Or you must return to our religious way.”

Al-A‘raf 7:88

And concerning the companions of the cave:

أَوْ يُعِيدُوكُمْ فِي مِلَّتِهِمْ

“Or they will return you to their religious way.”

Al-Kahf 18:20

These uses take millah beyond a single belief or personal feeling. It is a way to which someone may belong, which can be followed, abandoned, and returned to.

Accordingly, “religious way” and, in some contexts, “religious tradition” are natural English renderings.

Millah does not inherently mean the true religious way

The direct expression مِلَّة إبراهيم occurs six times: Al-Baqarah 2:130 and 2:135, Aal ‘Imran 3:95, An-Nisa 4:125, Al-An‘am 6:161, and An-Nahl 16:123. Al-Hajj 22:78 has مِلَّةَ أَبِيكُمْ إِبْرَاهِيمَ, while Joseph says:

وَاتَّبَعْتُ مِلَّةَ آبَائِي إِبْرَاهِيمَ وَإِسْحَاقَ وَيَعْقُوبَ

“I have followed the religious way of my fathers, Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.”

Yusuf 12:38

Yet one verse earlier, the same Joseph says:

إِنِّي تَرَكْتُ مِلَّةَ قَوْمٍ لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ بِاللَّهِ

The same word thus describes both Abraham’s way and the way of a people who do not believe in The God.

In Al-A‘raf 7:88 and Ibrahim 14:13, the messengers’ opponents speak of مِلَّتِنَا, “our religious way,” while Al-Kahf 18:20 speaks of مِلَّتِهِمْ, “their religious way.”

Millah therefore does not inherently mean a correct, monotheistic, or divine way. It identifies a shared way and a relationship of adherence. Whether that way is true or false depends on its content and context.

As with many other Qur’anic words, the word has its semantic field, while evaluation depends on what it refers to.

Abraham’s millah: following, beyond nominal affiliation

In passages about Abraham, the verb اتبع repeatedly accompanies millah. This is significant.

The Qur’an speaks of more than simply claiming Abraham’s name: it speaks of following his way:

قُلْ إِنَّنِي هَدَانِي رَبِّي إِلَىٰ صِرَاطٍ مُسْتَقِيمٍ دِينًا قِيَمًا مِلَّةَ إِبْرَاهِيمَ حَنِيفًا وَمَا كَانَ مِنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ

“Say: My Lord has guided me to a straight path, an upright dīn, the religious way of Abraham, a ḥanīf; and he was not one of those who associated others with The God.”

Al-An‘am 6:161

Here صِرَاطٍ مُسْتَقِيمٍ, دِينًا قِيَمًا, مِلَّةَ إِبْرَاهِيمَ, and حَنِيفًا are connected within one construction. Their co-occurrence alone establishes neither complete synonymy nor an absolute boundary between their meanings.

Ḥanīf characterizes Abraham’s orientation and must be understood through its own root. Dīn likewise has its own semantic field. Millah here contributes the religious way attributed to Abraham and open to being followed.

Nor should Abraham’s millah automatically become a fixed list of rules and rituals. The features of Abraham’s way highlighted elsewhere must be understood from those passages, rather than from the word millah alone.

Does millah derive from dictation?

This is one of the difficult questions concerning the root.

It is conceivable that a religious way taught and communicated could be connected with speech and dictation. But a conceivable relationship is different from an established history of a word. Qur’anic usage alone does not demonstrate this derivation.

The possibility of such a connection does not establish that Arabic millah originally and certainly meant “revealed and dictated teaching.”

Three questions should therefore remain distinct:

The Qur’anic meaning of millah is clear from its uses: a religious way that is followed.

Its relationship to speech and imlāl is an etymological hypothesis requiring independent historical evidence.

The word’s definitive historical origin has not been established by the evidence examined. None of the possible explanations can therefore be presented as this study’s certain conclusion.

Even if the connection with dictation were correct, it would not follow that millah in the Qur’an means a written text, a revealed book, or a prophet’s teaching. Opposing peoples also have a millah, and the Qur’an mentions the millah of a people without faith.

The meaning of the verses must therefore not depend on an etymological theory.

Early Arabic evidence and the limits of this inquiry

An independent search for pre-Qur’anic uses of مِلّة meaning a religious way and of the verb for dictation found no example with sufficiently reliable attribution and dating. Searches in poetry, expressions, proverbs, and material concerning writing and contracts yielded no secure evidence for these two uses. This describes the limits of the present search; it does not prove that no such evidence exists.

For the branch concerning heat, the following verse appears in Kaʿb ibn Zuhayr’s Dīwān, in the poem Bānat Suʿād. Kaʿb was a poet whose life spanned the advent of Islam, and this is evidence from poetry close to the period of revelation. It is not treated here as securely dated pre-Qur’anic evidence:

يَوْمًا يَظَلُّ بِهِ الحِرْبَاءُ مُصْطَخِمًا
كَأَنَّ ضَاحِيَهُ بِالنَّارِ مَمْلُولُ

“On a day when the chameleon is scorched by the heat, as though its sun-exposed side were roasted by fire.”

مَمْلُول describes the sun-exposed part of the chameleon’s body as though roasted by fire. This attests a use in the field of heat; it does not establish the meaning of millah as a religious way, or a historical bridge from heat to that meaning. The 15 Qur’anic occurrences remain this study’s direct evidence for Qur’anic millah.

Millah, dīn, and sharīʿah

Millah and dīn occur together in some verses. Their contextual closeness should be recognized without assuming complete synonymy from the outset.

Al-An‘am 6:161 says:

دِينًا قِيَمًا مِلَّةَ إِبْرَاهِيمَ

And An-Nisa 4:125 says:

وَمَنْ أَحْسَنُ دِينًا مِمَّنْ أَسْلَمَ وَجْهَهُ لِلَّهِ وَهُوَ مُحْسِنٌ وَاتَّبَعَ مِلَّةَ إِبْرَاهِيمَ حَنِيفًا

This closeness shows that dīn and millah can meet within a shared semantic field, while the Qur’an continues to use two different words.

Al-Ma’idah 5:48, in turn, speaks of شِرْعَةً وَمِنْهَاجًا. This shows that the Qur’an has other terms for prescribed ways, but their co-occurrence cannot by itself establish a fixed scheme in which millah always means worldview and sharīʿah always means detailed rules.

Such a scheme would make the words carry more than the evidence establishes.

Qur’anic usage directly supports understanding millah as a religious way that can be attributed to someone and followed: Abraham’s millah, the fathers’ millah, a people’s millah, our millah, your millah, and their millah. Sad 38:7 also has الْمِلَّةِ الْآخِرَةِ. The expression itself names no particular religious tradition; identifying its referent requires a separate examination of that passage’s context.

Precise definitions of dīn and sharīʿah require examining those words independently.

Other branches: heat and weariness

Kaʿb ibn Zuhayr’s verse showed that Arabic close to the period of revelation also used these consonants in describing heat. That use does not occur in the Qur’an.

In that example, مَمْلُول accompanies بِالنَّارِ, creating an image of roasting. The verse alone does not show that millah, a religious way, derives from ashes, hot earth, or a method of cooking.

The branch of weariness is also known through مَلِلْتُ الشيء, “I became tired of something.” This verse appears in the Dīwān of al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī, but is also attributed to al-Ḥuṭayʾah: فَإِنْ تَحْيَ لَا أَمْلَلْ حَيَاتِي وَإِنْ تَمُتْ / فَمَا فِي حَيَاتِي بَعْدَ مَوْتِكَ طَائِلُ—“If you live, I will not tire of my life; if you die, there is no good in my life after your death.” أَمْلَلْ here concerns weariness of life, not dictation. Because of the conflicting attribution, it is not treated as decisive evidence for dating this branch before the Qur’an.

No Qur’anic example, however, authorizes importing these two branches into the meaning of Abraham’s millah.

One cannot define millah as a way cooked in fire, or a tradition repeated until people tire of it, or join the branches with a newly invented metaphor.

This is where the distinction between shared consonants and historically demonstrated unity of meaning becomes important.

A religious way is not a people or a political nation

For an English reader, the key translation distinction is between a religious way and the people who follow it. “Nation” commonly suggests a people or a political community; that is not the meaning established for Qur’anic millah here.

That sense of a people or nation should not be read back into the Qur’anic word.

مِلَّةَ إِبْرَاهِيمَ means “Abraham’s religious way,” not “Abraham’s people.”

Joseph says:

تَرَكْتُ مِلَّةَ قَوْمٍ

The object of taraktu in this expression is مِلَّةَ قَوْمٍ, “the religious way of a people”; qawm itself is not the direct object. The phrase concerns abandoning a religious way and does not by itself report whether Joseph physically left those people.

Shuʿayb’s people say in Al-A‘raf 7:88:

لَتَعُودُنَّ فِي مِلَّتِنَا

The demand is to return to our religious way, not to become part of their population or political citizenship again.

Rendering the expression as “Abraham’s nation” could therefore bring a population or political entity to the reader’s mind and change the sense of the verse.

For Forqan, “religious way” makes the intended sense clearer than “nation.”

Translation proposals for Forqan

In Al-Baqarah 2:282, for يُمِلّ / يُمْلِل:

“Let him dictate”

is natural and accurate.

Where an explanation is needed:

“Let him speak the words to be written down”

distinguishes this role more clearly from the scribe’s work.

For مِلّة, the basic proposal is:

“Religious way.”

This conveys a way of adherence and practice.

In some contexts, especially where following a course is emphasized:

“Religious path”

may also be appropriate.

For example:

مِلَّةَ إِبْرَاهِيمَ

“Abraham’s religious way”

حَتَّىٰ تَتَّبِعَ مِلَّتَهُمْ

“Until you follow their religious way”

إِنِّي تَرَكْتُ مِلَّةَ قَوْمٍ

“I have abandoned the religious way of a people…”

أَوْ لَتَعُودُنَّ فِي مِلَّتِنَا

“Or you must return to our religious way”

أَوْ يُعِيدُوكُمْ فِي مِلَّتِهِمْ

“Or they will return you to their religious way”

“Sect” or “denomination” is unsuitable as a basic rendering because it may import later institutional or sectarian divisions into the text.

“Religion” can be close in some contexts, but rendering millah and dīn identically everywhere can erase a distinction that the Qur’an maintains by using two words.

“Nation,” with its population and political associations, is not a suitable rendering of Qur’anic millah in these passages.

Conclusion

The evidence examined is insufficient to explain all uses of M L L through a single image. Dictation and millah are clear in the Qur’an; a use concerning heat was also found in early poetry, but the historical relationship between all these branches has not been established.

The Qur’an has two uses here.

يُمِلّ / يُمْلِل means to speak words for someone else to write down: to dictate.

مِلّة is a religious way or tradition to which a person or group belongs, and which can be followed, abandoned, turned away from, or returned to.

Millah is not inherently true or false. Both Abraham and the peoples opposing the messengers have a millah. Its value depends on that way’s content and context.

The semantic closeness of speech and dictation does not by itself determine the origin of millah. An etymological explanation being possible is different from its being established. The meaning of the verses must rest on how the word is used in the Qur’an itself.

Millah: a recognized religious way or tradition to which a person or community adheres and which they follow. In the Qur’an, it does not mean a people or a political nation, is not exclusive to the true way, and should not be equated with dīn or sharīʿah without independent examination.

In the verbal branch:

أَمَلَّ / يُمِلُّ: to speak words for someone else to write down; to dictate.
