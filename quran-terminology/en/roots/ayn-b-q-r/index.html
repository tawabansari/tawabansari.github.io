1. Word Meaning and Structure

Arabic ʿabqarī (عَبْقَرِيّ) describes something outstanding in beauty, quality, craftsmanship, or distinctive features. Early uses particularly name fine, patterned carpets and textiles. It also describes capable, skilled, outstanding people; in modern Arabic it is especially associated with genius and exceptional talent.

The Qur’an uses ʿabqarī only once, describing beautiful carpets and furnishings in the Garden. It concerns fine weavings or carpets, rather than human intelligence or talent.

Al-Jawharī explains in Al-Ṣiḥāḥ that Arabs called something ʿabqarī when its skill, quality of workmanship, or power inspired wonder.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise records patterned carpets, fine weavings, outstanding people, and things exceptional in their kind.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab: textiles and exceptional quality

The uses share two neighboring features: quality beyond the ordinary and prominence that attracts attention.

Their specific meanings nevertheless differ. A carpet called ʿabqarī for its weave and pattern does not mean precisely the same as a skilled person so described. One concerns an object’s beauty and quality; the other, a person’s ability and distinction.

Structurally, ʿabqarī has the relational suffix -iyy. In the familiar lexical account it is related to ʿAbqar (عَبْقَر), a place name in early Arabic language and reports.

Word structure should therefore remain distinct from the meaning active in a particular use.

A word may initially relate something to a place and later become a general description of outstanding things.

Qur’anic indexing records ʿ B Q R as quadriliteral. That classification does not establish derivation from an original quadriliteral Arabic verb meaning “to become a genius” or “to make beautiful.”

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the quadriliteral entry’s sole occurrence

The Qur’anic word is an established term structurally related to ʿAbqar and used for an excellent kind of weaving.

2. ʿAbqar: A Place Name and the Extension of ʿAbqarī

ʿAbqar in Early Arabic Conceptions

Lexical sources name ʿAbqar as a place with differing accounts of its location and character.

Al-Jawharī states in Al-Ṣiḥāḥ that Arabs supposed it belonged to the lands of the jinn.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise presents it as a desert place imagined to contain many jinn.

This underlies ka-annahum jinnu ʿAbqara (كَأَنَّهُمْ جِنُّ عَبْقَرَ), “as though they were the jinn of ʿAbqar.”

The expression describes people whose abilities, agility, or unusual qualities appeared astonishing.

Reporting a belief preserved in language differs from establishing its historical reality.

That Arabs regarded ʿAbqar as a jinn dwelling does not establish that such a place was actually inhabited by jinn.

The name’s occurrence in poetry likewise does not prove a supernatural land’s existence.

Lexically, what matters is that the name was known and used in similes and descriptions of astonishing things.

Another Account: ʿAbqar and Fine Textiles

Another explanation transmitted by Ibn Sīdah identifies ʿAbqar as a Yemeni village where patterned cloths and carpets were woven.

In this account, their quality made the place’s name proverbial for excellent goods.

An exceptionally beautiful, well-made cloth or carpet could accordingly be called ʿabqarī even if it was not manufactured there.

The semantic extension is understandable. A place name can become the name of a famous product and later apply to similar goods.

The actual location of ʿAbqar nevertheless remains difficult to establish.

In the lexical report, Abū ʿUbayd says that no one was found who knew where this land was or when it existed.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Ibn Sīdah and Abū ʿUbayd on ʿAbqar’s location

The Yemeni-village account therefore should not be treated as proven history.

The accounts differ: one concerns a place associated with jinn in popular conception; the other, a place producing fine weavings.

They may have become intertwined over time, but the evidence does not establish either as the certain origin of every use.

What is clearer is that Arabic connects ʿAbqar with distinction and excellence, and uses ʿabqarī to describe outstanding things.

From Place Name to Quality Description

Several uses illustrate the word’s semantic range.

One names a fine, patterned textile.

Another describes a person with exceptional ability or skill.

Lexical expressions include mālun ʿabqariyyun for excellent property and ʿabqariyyu al-qawmi for a community’s chief or outstanding person. Prominence is not always moral praise: ẓulmun ʿabqariyyun describes extreme, great wrongdoing. That use should not be transferred to the verse’s fine-textile sense.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab: people, wealth, and extraordinary wrongdoing

The word thus extends beyond a particular commodity or place relation into a description of exceptional quality.

This does not mean that every occurrence should be translated merely as “extraordinary.”

Some sentences name a kind of carpet or weaving; others describe human skill and distinction.

The subject and sentence structure determine the appropriate meaning.

3. Early Arabic Evidence: ʿAbqar and Patterned Carpets

ʿAbqar in Labīd’s Poetry

Labīd ibn Rabīʿah writes:

وَمَنْ فَادَ مِنْ إِخْوَانِهِمْ وَبَنِيهِمْ
كُهُولٌ وَشُبَّانٌ كَجِنَّةِ عَبْقَرِ

wa-man fāda min ikhwānihim wa-banīhim / kuhūlun wa-shubbānun ka-jinnati ʿabqari

“Those of their brothers and sons who have passed away were elders and young men like the jinn of ʿAbqar.”

ʿAbqar here is a place name associated with jinn.

The comparison of older and younger men to ʿAbqar’s jinn shows the name’s presence in early Arabic literary imagery.

Labīd lived across the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods. The witness matters for language history because it records ʿAbqar in early poetry. The quotation alone does not date this particular verse; the poet’s name is insufficient to establish that it certainly preceded revelation.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: Labīd’s witness

The verse concerns neither carpets nor patterned textiles, and does not independently establish a real weaving center at that time.

It attests the name and its association with a conception of unusual beings or abilities.

ʿAbqar in Imruʾ al-Qays’s Poetry

Imruʾ al-Qays also names ʿAbqar:

كَأَنَّ صَلِيلَ الْمَرْوِ حِينَ تُشِذُّهُ
صَلِيلُ زُيُوفٍ يُنْتَقَدْنَ بِعَبْقَرَا

ka-anna ṣalīla al-marwi ḥīna tushidhdhuhu / ṣalīlu zuyūfin yuntaqadna bi-ʿabqarā

Explanatory rendering: “The ringing of flint stones as the she-camel scatters them is like the ringing of debased coins being tested by tapping at ʿAbqar.” Al-Muḥkam records tushidhdhuhu, and the poetic commentary glosses it as “sending it flying.” Transmissions also give tashudduhu or tuṭīruhu. None of these variants makes the place name proof of an actual mint.

Al-Muḥkam: the vocalization tushidhdhuhu in Imruʾ al-Qays’s witness

Zuyūf concerns poor or debased coins; yuntaqadna here concerns examining and testing them.

The witness places ʿAbqar within an image of a location and human activity.

It does not establish a mint at ʿAbqar or a securely known geographical location.

Commentary on the Six Poets: testing debased coins

The poet may employ a familiar name in a simile. Establishing the place’s reality and economic activity requires further evidence.

The verse has no direct connection with fine carpets.

Its importance lies in recording ʿAbqar in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

ʿAbqar’s Weavings in Dhū al-Rummah’s Poetry

A particularly clear witness to ʿAbqar’s connection with patterned cloth appears in Dhū al-Rummah:

حَتَّى كَأَنَّ رِيَاضَ الْقُفِّ أَلْبَسَهَا
مِنْ وَشْيِ عَبْقَرَ تَجْلِيلٌ وَتَنْجِيدُ

ḥattā ka-anna riyāḍa al-quffi albasahā / min washyi ʿabqara tajlīlun wa-tanjīdu

“Until the meadows of the stony upland seemed dressed in coverings and furnishings of ʿAbqar’s patterned weavings.”

The poet compares nature’s beautiful patterns with ʿAbqar’s ornamented textiles.

Washy concerns a cloth’s pattern and decoration; tajlīl and tanjīd highlight covering and furnishing in the image.

The witness is especially important for a weaving-related use of the place relation.

Its subject is the beauty of a natural scene compared with patterned fabric, rather than jinn or unusual abilities.

Dhū al-Rummah was an Umayyad-era poet. His work attests early Arabic close to the first Islamic period, but not Arabic certainly preceding revelation.

Lisān al-ʿArab: ʿAbqar’s weavings in Dhū al-Rummah’s poem

The three witnesses establish two points.

First, ʿAbqar was a known name in early poetry.

Second, relation to ʿAbqar also occurs with patterned weavings.

Together, they do not establish its actual location, inhabitants, or the first origin of every product called ʿabqarī.

ʿAbqarī Describing People

Early Arabic also applies ʿabqarī to an outstanding person.

Asked by Al-Aṣmaʿī, Abū ʿAmr ibn al-ʿAlāʾ explains ʿabqariyyu qawmin as a community’s chief, leader, strong man, or outstanding member.

The human use was therefore not limited to intelligence or scientific talent.

Skill, power, leadership, or other exceptional qualities could motivate the description.

In modern Arabic, ʿabqarī is especially associated with intellectual genius and exceptional talent.

That specialized modern sense should not be imposed on the Qur’anic use.

Al-Raḥmān 55:76 concerns fine weavings and carpets, rather than a person of genius.

4. Qur’anic Use in Al-Raḥmān 55:76

ʿ B Q R occurs only once in the Qur’an.

Al-Raḥmān 55:76

مُتَّكِئِينَ عَلَىٰ رَفْرَفٍ خُضْرٍ وَعَبْقَرِيٍّ حِسَانٍ

muttakiʾīna ʿalā rafrafin khuḍrin wa-ʿabqariyyin ḥisānin

Translation quoted in this study, rendered here in English:

“Reclining on green cushions and fine, beautiful carpets.”

ʿAbqariyyin in the Sentence

ʿAbqariyyin follows the conjunction wa- and is connected with rafrafin.

It is genitive by coordination with rafrafin, which follows ʿalā.

Both therefore concern things on which the reclining people rest.

The first expression is rafrafin khuḍrin.

Lexical sources record rafraf for coverings, cushions, bedding, and spread-out fabrics. Khuḍr describes them as green.

The second expression is ʿabqariyyin ḥisānin.

ʿAbqarī names fine carpets or weavings; ḥisān describes their beauty.

Together, the expressions picture ornamented, pleasant furnishings and supports.

Khuḍr directly accompanies rafraf, while ḥisān accompanies ʿabqarī.

All the ʿabqarī carpets should therefore not be declared necessarily green without evidence.

The verse specifies neither their exact color, fiber, nor pattern.

The word and context make their fineness and beauty clear.

Why Is ʿAbqariyyin Singular in Form and Ḥisānin Plural?

The construction may initially seem unusual: ʿabqarī appears singular, while ḥisān is the plural of ḥasan.

Certain Arabic nouns can refer to a kind or collection with a singular-looking form.

Lexical and grammatical sources examine ʿabqarī here as a generic or collectively meaningful noun, with ʿabqariyyah as its unit.

On that reading, it refers to a collection of carpets or weavings, with ḥisān agreeing with its collective meaning.

Other collective expressions likewise have a singular-looking form but accompanying agreement reflecting the group.

Al-Ṭabarī also gives ʿabqarī a collective meaning here, identifying ʿabqariyyah as its unit.

Al-Ṭabarī: collective meaning and the unit ʿabqariyyah

Plural ḥisān therefore does not require replacing the Qur’anic word with an invented plural form.

The text uses ʿabqariyyin; that form should be retained in the study.

Variant Reading

Reading reports also attribute the uncommon ʿabāqiriyy to certain readers. Such reports should not be identified with the text examined here. Al-Ṭabarī calls the report attributing the form with an additional alif to the Prophet unpreserved and unsound in transmission.

Grammarians debate the form’s morphological validity.

Some analyze it as a plural or a relation to ʿAbāqir; others, including Al-Ṭabarī and Al-Zajjāj, object to the structure of certain transmitted forms. A reading’s being reported should remain distinct from judgments about its structure and transmission.

Al-Ṭabarī: the reported reading with an additional alif and its critique

The text under examination is wa-ʿabqariyyin ḥisānin.

Zād al-Masīr: Al-Zajjāj’s critique of certain forms

The variation matters for reading history and word structure, but does not change the central sense of fine, beautiful weavings.

Understanding the verse does not require changing ʿabqarī into another translated form.

Does ʿAbqarī Mean Genius in This Verse?

No.

The human sense “genius” is established in language, but the sentence’s subject does not support it.

The verse speaks of reclining on rafraf and ʿabqarī.

One can recline on carpets, cushions, or bedding; “reclining on genius” is not this sentence’s natural, direct meaning.

Ḥisān likewise describes the beauty of things provided for the Garden’s inhabitants’ comfort.

The context clearly favors fine carpets or weavings.

Rendering ʿabqarī here as “genius,” “extraordinary talent,” or “mental power” is therefore incorrect.

A word’s familiar modern meaning is not necessarily its suitable meaning in an early text.

Thick Carpets, Patterned Cloth, or Brocade?

Lexical and exegetical sources give several identifications of the weaving.

Al-Farrāʾ describes al-ṭanāfisu al-thikhānu, thick, fine carpets or covers.

Qatādah and others explain it through zarābiyy, a term for carpets and spread furnishings.

Mujāhid gives dībāj, fine silk fabric.

Ibn Qutaybah likewise says Arabs could call any ornamented, patterned cloth ʿabqarī.

These referents are not entirely identical.

Thick carpets, silk fabric, and patterned textiles can differ in substance, construction, and use.

They share ornamentation and excellent quality.

The verse can accordingly refer to fine carpets or weavings without a certain identification of the precise material.

Al-Qurṭubī: thick carpets, zarābiyy, brocade, and patterned weavings

“Fine carpets” conveys the field well.

“Thick silk carpets with golden patterns” would introduce details the verse does not specify.

ʿAbqarī and Ḥisān

Ḥisān explicitly expresses beauty in wa-ʿabqariyyin ḥisānin.

The textile use of ʿabqarī itself concerns fineness and exceptional quality.

The combination need not be pointless repetition.

ʿAbqarī identifies the kind or excellent quality of weaving; ḥisān highlights its beauty.

A carpet can be valuable in substance or craftsmanship while its beauty also receives separate attention.

Both qualities describe the setting of comfort here.

Their difference should nevertheless not be overstated: fineness and beauty can overlap in describing an object.

“Fine, beautiful carpets” is therefore a natural, accurate rendering.

ʿAbqarī among the Garden’s Blessings

The verse belongs to the descriptions of two further gardens in Al-Raḥmān.

Al-Raḥmān 55:62 introduces two further gardens, followed by deep green growth, gushing springs, fruit, dates, pomegranates, and other blessings.

Verse 76 reaches beautiful bedding and supports.

ʿAbqarī here forms part of an image of ease, rest, and enjoyment.

Al-Raḥmān 55:54 also names furush and their linings of istabraq.

The verses are connected through fine furnishings.

ʿAbqarī and istabraq should not, however, become complete synonyms.

Istabraq concerns a fine, thick fabric; Qur’anic ʿabqarī concerns excellent carpets or weavings whose precise substance is not uniformly identified in the sources.

Nor does ʿabqarī alone establish one pair of gardens as higher or lower than the other.

Such comparison requires the complete context, rather than a definite ranking inferred solely from two textile names.

Are the ʿAbqarī Weavings Made by Jinn?

Lexical reports of ʿAbqar as a land of jinn do not authorize that conclusion.

Al-Raḥmān 55:76 says nothing about who made the carpets or where they were woven.

It describes ʿabqariyyin ḥisānin among the Garden’s blessings.

A historical association with a conception of jinn territory does not itself establish that jinn manufactured the Garden’s carpets.

Names and expressions can have mythical, figurative, or historical origins and later acquire ordinary meanings.

The established sense of fine carpets therefore takes precedence over accounts of supposed makers in translation and study of this verse.

5. Differences from Furnishings, Zarābiyy, Brocade, and Related Words

ʿAbqarī and Furush

Furush is the plural of firāsh, referring to bedding or spread furnishings on which people sit, rest, or sleep.

The Qur’an also uses it for the Garden’s blessings.

Qur’anic ʿabqarī denotes a kind of excellent carpet or weaving.

It can be included among furnishings, without every furnishing necessarily being called ʿabqarī.

Fineness, patterning, or exceptional quality is more prominent in ʿabqarī.

ʿAbqarī and Zarābiyy

Zarābiyy occurs in Al-Ghāshiyah 88:16.

The word concerns carpets and spread furnishings.

Lexicographers also use it to explain ʿabqarī in Al-Raḥmān 55:76.

The expressions consequently overlap in certain referents.

It does not follow that every zarābiyy is necessarily ʿabqarī, or that every use of ʿabqarī names one particular kind of zarābiyy.

Root research should distinguish the general furnishing name from a description of fineness or a particular kind.

ʿAbqarī and Ṭunfusah

Arabic ṭunfusah names a kind of carpet or thick covering.

Its plural is ṭanāfis, which sources use to explain ʿabqarī.

The explanation concerns the carpet’s substance or construction.

The Qur’an does not itself use ṭunfusah in this verse.

Thick carpet can identify one referent of ʿabqarī, but a particular thickness should not become a certain element of the Qur’anic translation.

ʿAbqarī and Dībāj

Dībāj denotes fine cloth, commonly silk.

Some sources interpret ʿabqarī as dībāj.

That can convey the weaving’s fineness and beauty.

Dībāj and ʿabqarī nevertheless are not synonymous in every use.

Dībāj chiefly concerns a kind of fabric; ʿabqarī also describes excellent things and outstanding people beyond its textile use.

ʿAbqarī and Washy

Washy concerns a cloth’s pattern and ornamentation.

Dhū al-Rummah uses washy ʿAbqar, patterned or ornamented weaving attributed to ʿAbqar.

The combination places ʿabqarī in the field of patterned textiles.

Washy concerns the decoration or pattern, while ʿabqarī names or describes the excellent textile.

The distinction should remain visible.

ʿAbqarī and Zukhruf

Arabic zukhruf concerns gold, ornament, and striking adornment.

A beautiful patterned ʿabqarī carpet can be connected in that respect with ornamentation.

The words nevertheless differ.

Zukhruf can describe the earth’s adornment or embellished speech; Qur’anic ʿabqarī names a kind of fine weaving.

Rendering ʿabqarī in Al-Raḥmān 55:76 merely as “beauty” or “adornment” would erase the carpet or weaving.

Study of Z KH R F

ʿAbqarī and Genius

In modern Arabic, ʿabqarī is especially associated with a genius or an exceptionally talented person.

That use connects with the earlier outstanding-person sense, but does not encompass the word’s entire history.

Early Arabic could apply it to a community’s chief, a strong or skilled person, or something of exceptional quality.

Extraordinary intelligence is therefore only one field on which modern usage focuses.

It is not the subject of the Qur’anic occurrence.

Al-Raḥmān 55:76 names a weaving whose beauty and fineness belong to the setting of the Garden’s blessings.

6. Other Recorded Uses and Their Limits

Arabic dictionaries record several other uses under ʿ B Q R.

ʿAbqar denotes the first tender shoots from reed roots or similar plants.

ʿAbqarah is given as its unit.

Some descriptions also use ʿabqar and ʿabqarah for a beautiful, well-formed woman.

ʿAbqara al-sarābu describes a mirage shimmering.

Some sources associate ʿabqar with hail or its coldness, although the exact form and structure are disputed.

Early lexicons: reed shoots, mirage, and differences among other forms

These meanings cannot be extracted from a shared image without evidence.

Tender plant growth and fine carpets, or a shimmering mirage and human ability, need not have one historical origin.

Some forms may have converged phonetically or structurally over time, or lexicographers may have grouped them because their letters match.

Such classification does not prove one original meaning for every use.

A definition such as “beauty, softness, brilliance, ability, and excellence” should therefore not force all the senses into one branch for Qur’anic ʿ B Q R.

ʿAbqarī’s relation to the name ʿAbqar and extension to outstanding things has specific lexical support.

Its Qur’anic use concerns fine carpets or weavings.

That sense should govern translation, while other uses retain their historical and linguistic limits.

7. Conclusion and Translation Proposals

Qur’anic indexing records quadriliteral ʿ B Q R only in the form ʿabqarī.

Arabic connects it with ʿAbqar, a place name described in early accounts either as a jinn dwelling or as a source of fine weavings.

Despite its appearance in early poetry, the place’s actual location and the history of its inhabitants or textile industry are uncertain.

In usage, ʿabqarī describes something exceptional in quality, beauty, craftsmanship, or other distinctive features.

Fine, patterned carpets and weavings are one established use.

Early Arabic also records outstanding, skilled people, while intellectual genius is prominent in modern Arabic. Coexisting uses do not alone establish the exact chronological direction of semantic transfer between textile and person.

Arabic Language Academy: modern genius and exceptional ability

The only Qur’anic occurrence is Al-Raḥmān 55:76.

ʿAbqariyyin ḥisānin accompanies rafrafin khuḍrin, describing ornamented furnishings and carpets in the Garden.

Fine carpets or weavings are the clear, preferable contextual meaning.

The verse does not specify exact fabric, fiber, weaving technique, or patterns.

Brief root meaning: In the ʿabqarī family, something outstanding or exceptional in its kind; in textile usage, fine, patterned, valuable carpets or cloth.

Meaning of Qur’anic wa-ʿabqariyyin (وَعَبْقَرِيٍّ): And fine carpets or weavings, describing the Garden’s beautiful furnishings.

Proposed translation:

مُتَّكِئِينَ عَلَىٰ رَفْرَفٍ خُضْرٍ وَعَبْقَرِيٍّ حِسَانٍ

muttakiʾīna ʿalā rafrafin khuḍrin wa-ʿabqariyyin ḥisānin

“Reclining on green cushions and fine, beautiful carpets.”

The rendering preserves the tangible meaning and prevents modern “genius” from being transferred into the Qur’an.

It retains fineness and beauty without predetermining the carpets’ substance or pattern.

The central conclusion is that Qur’anic ʿabqarī means fine, excellent weaving, rather than genius or extraordinary power.

The place name’s background can illuminate usage, but accounts of a jinn land or regional weavers should not become certain information about the Garden’s carpets’ origin.

The Qur’anic meaning follows the Arabic word and context: beautiful, valuable weavings contributing to an image of comfort and enjoyment beside the other blessings.
