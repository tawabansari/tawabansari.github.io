Qur'anic Root Q R Y

1. Short Introduction to the Root

The root ق ر ي in the Qur'an appears only in the word قَرْيَة and its plural form قُرَى. This word points to a place of human residence and gathering: a place where a group lives and forms a recognizable social unit.

In modern Persian and Arabic usage, qaryah is often understood as a village or small rural settlement. This may fit some verses, but it cannot be the fixed equivalent across the Qur'an. The Qur'an uses qaryah for habitations and settlements of different positions, sizes, and social structures.

The Qur'an itself does not give a fixed population or administrative criterion for qaryah. A qaryah may have a market, walls, gates, many houses, rulers, affluent elites, messengers, agriculture, roads, or social structure; but none of these features alone is the fixed lexical meaning of the word.

In some verses, the word indicates the place itself. In others, the residents are at the center of attention. For example:

وَاسْأَلِ الْقَرْيَةَ الَّتِي كُنَّا فِيهَا

The natural meaning is: ask the people of that qaryah, because buildings and land do not answer. This transfer from place to inhabitants is common in natural language.

Short meaning:

The place of residence and gathering of a human group; a habitation or settlement whose exact size and structure must be known from context.

2. Important Forms and Derivatives of the Root

This root occurs 57 times in the Qur'an, and all its uses return to the noun قَرْيَة and its inflected or plural forms. The Qur'an does not use a verb, verbal noun, or adjective from this root.

قَرْيَة

A singular feminine noun. It points to a place of residence and the human community established in it.

Depending on context, it may be translated as:

  • habitation;
  • settlement;
  • town or city;
  • place of residence;
  • the community settled in a place.

“Village” is suitable only when the verse itself gives evidence of a small settlement.

الْقَرْيَة

The definite form. It points to a specific and contextually known settlement:

هَٰذِهِ الْقَرْيَةَ

This habitation.

الْقَرْيَةَ الَّتِي كُنَّا فِيهَا

The habitation in which we were.

قَرْيَتِكَ

The form attached to the second-person singular pronoun:

قَرْيَتِكَ الَّتِي أَخْرَجَتْكَ

Your habitation, which drove you out.

Muhammad 47:13

Attributing the act of driving out to the qaryah returns to the behavior of its residents and social forces.

قَرْيَتَنَا

The form attached to the first-person plural pronoun. In structures such as:

أَخْرِجُوهُمْ مِنْ قَرْيَتِكُمْ

or similar constructions, qaryah is tied to the collective identity of the residents.

الْقُرَى

The plural of قَرْيَة. It points to several habitations or a set of settlements.

قُرًى

The indefinite plural form:

وَقُرًى ظَاهِرَةً

Visible and near settlements.

Saba 34:18

أُمُّ الْقُرَى

A Qur'anic expression for the central or mother settlement:

لِتُنْذِرَ أُمَّ الْقُرَىٰ وَمَنْ حَوْلَهَا

So that you may warn the mother of the habitations and those around it.

This expression points to a main center in relation to the settlements around it.

4. The Semantic Core of the Root

The defensible semantic core in Qur'anic usage is:

A place where a group of human beings has settled and formed a recognizable residential community.

Three elements recur in Qur'anic uses:

Place:

A qaryah is a location that one can enter, leave, live in, or go toward.

Residents:

A qaryah has people who believe, deny, commit injustice, enjoy security, or face the consequences of their conduct.

Collective identity:

The conduct of part of the residents, especially the dominant structure of society, can be attributed to the whole qaryah.

The word does not necessarily indicate “concentration of capital,” “accumulation of resources,” “great civilization,” or “center of government.” These features may exist in some qaryahs, but they must be proven from the context of that verse.

Some lexical explanations say that qaryah is so called because it gathers people. This explanation fits the nature of a settlement, but it should not be turned without caution into a definitive theory about the historical path by which the word emerged.

The better basic definition should remain with the clear usage of the word:

Qaryah is the place where a human group lives.

5. The Material Image of the Root

The suitable material image of this root is a group of houses and paths in which people live.

Houses alone do not make a qaryah. Stable human presence, relations among the people, and the possibility of recognizing that place as a residential unit are also required.

This settlement may have formed beside a well, spring, agricultural land, trade road, or enclosure; but the Qur'an makes none of these the lexical condition of qaryah.

In a verse from the companions of the cave story, the two travelers enter a qaryah and ask its people for food:

حَتَّىٰ إِذَا أَتَيَا أَهْلَ قَرْيَةٍ اسْتَطْعَمَا أَهْلَهَا

Until, when they came to the people of a habitation, they asked its people for food.

Al-Kahf 18:77

In the same place, there is a wall belonging to two orphan boys, and beneath it a treasure is preserved. This image shows that qaryah is a residential society with houses, a wall, ownership, children, property, and social relations.

But from this verse one cannot determine the size, population, or type of administration of that place.

6. Use of the Root in Early Arabic

In Arabic, qaryah is applied to the place where people gather and reside. Some lexical sources connect its naming to people gathering in one place, and also mention miqrah for a vessel or basin in which water gathers. This explanation makes the semantic connection with gathering possible, but it does not make the exact historical path of derivation certain.

Within the same written family, qira is food prepared for a guest, and qara ad-dayf means he hosted the guest. This field is known in Arabic.

However, one should not say without sufficient evidence that hospitality must have come from “gathering scattered resources,” or that the meaning of qaryah emerged from the guest-table. Shared letters do not always mean a clear and proven derivational path.

Lexical sources also record forms from q-r-w for a basin or water vessel. The existence of a difference between y-ending and w-ending forms, and the varied reports of lexicons, requires avoiding the construction of one certain origin for all these words.

What is sufficient for a Qur'anic article is:

qaryah in Arabic is a place where people reside;

it may be small or large;

and its meaning is not identical with modern administrative divisions.

7. History of Understanding and Restricting the Meaning

The greatest restriction in Persian translation is treating qaryah as always “village” or “hamlet.”

This rendering is influenced by later Persian and Arabic usage, where qaryah stands opposite a large city. But in the Qur'an there is no such fixed administrative contrast.

The Qur'an speaks of:

  • Umm al-Qura;
  • a qaryah with market and economic interaction;
  • settlements with walls and treasure;
  • settlements with affluent elites and power structures;
  • settlements in a network of safe roads;
  • and qaryahs to which messengers come.

None of these cases by itself proves that qaryah is always a large city. Just as the fixed translation “village” is wrong, the fixed translation “metropolis” would also be wrong.

Another restriction is to treat the word itself as negative. Because many qaryah verses concern denial, injustice, and destruction, one might imagine that qaryah is inherently a corrupt society. But the Qur'an also speaks of a secure and tranquil qaryah, and presents the possibility of a qaryah believing.

Thus the moral value of a qaryah comes from the conduct of its residents, not from the word itself.

Likewise, not every destruction of a qaryah should be reduced to “concentration of wealth” or the presence of affluent elites. In some verses, affluent elites and structural corruption play a major role; in others, denial of messengers, injustice, rebellion, or a group of causes are mentioned.

8. Methodological Note

Several levels must be distinguished in analyzing this root:

Direct meaning:

A habitation or place of residence for a community.

Possible features of a qaryah:

Market, agriculture, wall, government, wealth, road, population, or religious position.

Conduct of residents:

Faith, denial, injustice, security, tyranny, or reform.

Historical result:

Growth, blessing, fear, migration, punishment, or destruction.

Features, conduct, and results must not be placed inside the translation of the word itself.

For example:

وَكَمْ مِنْ قَرْيَةٍ أَهْلَكْنَاهَا

means “How many a habitation We destroyed,” not “How many a corrupt center of accumulated capital We destroyed.”

The corruption or reason for destruction must be understood from the continuation of the verse and related verses.

Comparison between qaryah and madinah should also not be turned into an absolute theory that one means “the material body of society” and the other “legal order and government.” The Qur'an calls one place qaryah in one context and madinah later in the same story. This use shows that the two words can apply to one place from two angles, but their precise boundary is not always size or government.

9. Qur'anic Uses of the Root

Qaryah as a Place of Residence and Its People

The Qur'an reports the words of Yusuf's brothers:

وَاسْأَلِ الْقَرْيَةَ الَّتِي كُنَّا فِيهَا وَالْعِيرَ الَّتِي أَقْبَلْنَا فِيهَا وَإِنَّا لَصَادِقُونَ

Ask the habitation in which we were, and the caravan with which we came; indeed, we are truthful.

Yusuf 12:82

Buildings and land do not answer; the intended meaning is the people of that place. Just as asking the caravan means asking the people of the caravan, asking the qaryah returns to its residents.

This use is a clear example of referring to inhabitants through the name of the place.

The verse does not define the size or administrative status of the qaryah. From the fact that the event occurred in Egypt, one cannot conclude with certainty that the word itself means “the capital of Egypt.”

The Qaryah from Which Food Was Requested

The Qur'an says:

حَتَّىٰ إِذَا أَتَيَا أَهْلَ قَرْيَةٍ اسْتَطْعَمَا أَهْلَهَا فَأَبَوْا أَنْ يُضَيِّفُوهُمَا

Until, when they came to the people of a habitation, they asked its people for food, but they refused to host the two of them.

Al-Kahf 18:77

The verse distinguishes between qaryah and its people. Qaryah is the place, and ahl are its residents.

In the continuation of the same story it says:

وَأَمَّا الْجِدَارُ فَكَانَ لِغُلَامَيْنِ يَتِيمَيْنِ فِي الْمَدِينَةِ

As for the wall, it belonged to two orphan boys in the madinah.

Al-Kahf 18:82

The story gives a strong clue that qaryah and madinah can refer to the same place, or at least to the same residential environment. Therefore, the difference between the two cannot be reduced simply to small versus large.

A Secure and Tranquil Qaryah

The Qur'an gives a parable:

وَضَرَبَ اللَّهُ مَثَلًا قَرْيَةً كَانَتْ آمِنَةً مُطْمَئِنَّةً يَأْتِيهَا رِزْقُهَا رَغَدًا مِنْ كُلِّ مَكَانٍ

The God struck a parable of a habitation that was secure and tranquil; its provision came to it abundantly from every place.

An-Nahl 16:112

This qaryah is a place of security and abundant provision. Therefore, the word itself does not carry deprivation, smallness, or backwardness.

The continuation of the verse describes the ingratitude of that society and the change from security to hunger and fear. The decline is the result of conduct, not the meaning of qaryah.

A Qaryah That Could Have Believed

The Qur'an says:

فَلَوْلَا كَانَتْ قَرْيَةٌ آمَنَتْ فَنَفَعَهَا إِيمَانُهَا إِلَّا قَوْمَ يُونُسَ

So why was there no qaryah that believed and its faith benefited it, except the people of Yunus?

Yunus 10:98

Faith is attributed to the qaryah, but faith is the act of its residents.

The continuation of the verse speaks of the people of Yunus and clarifies that qaryah in this use represents the human community settled in that place.

Entering the Qaryah

In the story of the Children of Israel, it says:

ادْخُلُوا هَٰذِهِ الْقَرْيَةَ فَكُلُوا مِنْهَا حَيْثُ شِئْتُمْ رَغَدًا

Enter this habitation and eat from it abundantly wherever you wish.

Al-Baqarah 2:58

Qaryah is a place one can enter and benefit from its resources. This expression does not determine its name, size, or exact structure.

The same meaning is repeated in Al-A'raf 7:161.

Being Driven Out of the Qaryah

The opponents of Lut said:

أَخْرِجُوهُمْ مِنْ قَرْيَتِكُمْ إِنَّهُمْ أُنَاسٌ يَتَطَهَّرُونَ

Drive them out of your habitation; they are people who seek purity.

Al-A'raf 7:82

Qaryah here is not only a location but also a sphere of ownership and social identity for the speakers:

قَرْيَتِكُمْ

They consider the place theirs and use social power to remove those who oppose them.

The Qaryah to Which Messengers Came

The Qur'an presents a parable:

وَاضْرِبْ لَهُمْ مَثَلًا أَصْحَابَ الْقَرْيَةِ إِذْ جَاءَهَا الْمُرْسَلُونَ

Strike for them a parable of the people of the qaryah, when the messengers came to it.

Ya-Sin 36:13

Then it says:

وَجَاءَ مِنْ أَقْصَى الْمَدِينَةِ رَجُلٌ يَسْعَىٰ

And a man came from the farthest part of the madinah, hurrying.

Ya-Sin 36:20

Within one story, the place is first called qaryah and then madinah. This use makes a simple boundary “qaryah equals village, madinah equals city” impossible.

Each word may highlight a different angle, but the Qur'an does not explain that this difference rests on a precise administrative or population criterion.

The Qaryah That Drove the Prophet Out

The Qur'an says:

وَكَأَيِّنْ مِنْ قَرْيَةٍ هِيَ أَشَدُّ قُوَّةً مِنْ قَرْيَتِكَ الَّتِي أَخْرَجَتْكَ أَهْلَكْنَاهُمْ فَلَا نَاصِرَ لَهُمْ

How many a qaryah, stronger than your qaryah that drove you out, We destroyed; and they had no helper.

Muhammad 47:13

The phrase qaryatika allati akhrajatka refers to the habitation whose dominant forces compelled the Prophet to leave.

The verse does not present qaryah as having a will independent from people; collective conduct or the ruling power of society is attributed to the place.

This verse also shows that qaryah can be stronger in power. Thus the word is not inherently limited to a weak and small settlement.

Settlements with Affluent Elites

The Qur'an says:

وَمَا أَرْسَلْنَا فِي قَرْيَةٍ مِنْ نَذِيرٍ إِلَّا قَالَ مُتْرَفُوهَا إِنَّا بِمَا أُرْسِلْتُمْ بِهِ كَافِرُونَ

We did not send a warner into any qaryah except that its affluent ones said: We reject what you have been sent with.

Saba 34:34

Mutrafuha shows that some qaryahs have an affluent and influential class. This group can play the main role in resisting reform.

But mutraf is not part of the meaning of qaryah. The verse describes a recurring social structure in some communities.

Qaryahs That Denied

The Qur'an says:

وَكَمْ قَصَمْنَا مِنْ قَرْيَةٍ كَانَتْ ظَالِمَةً وَأَنْشَأْنَا بَعْدَهَا قَوْمًا آخَرِينَ

How many an unjust qaryah We shattered, and after it We brought forth another people.

Al-Anbiya 21:11

The adjective zalimah is attributed to the qaryah, but injustice is the conduct of the society and its residents.

The verse creates a close relation between place and people, but then reveals the human residents through the phrase qawman akharin.

Destruction of a Qaryah and the Remaining Traces

The Qur'an says:

فَكَأَيِّنْ مِنْ قَرْيَةٍ أَهْلَكْنَاهَا وَهِيَ ظَالِمَةٌ فَهِيَ خَاوِيَةٌ عَلَىٰ عُرُوشِهَا

How many an unjust qaryah We destroyed, so it lay fallen upon its roofs.

Al-Hajj 22:45

In this verse, the qaryah is both the unjust society and the material place whose collapsed structures remain after destruction.

This duality is important: the word can gather both the residents and their built space in one image.

Visible Settlements on the Route of Saba

The Qur'an says:

وَجَعَلْنَا بَيْنَهُمْ وَبَيْنَ الْقُرَى الَّتِي بَارَكْنَا فِيهَا قُرًى ظَاهِرَةً وَقَدَّرْنَا فِيهَا السَّيْرَ

We placed between them and the settlements in which We had placed blessing visible settlements, and We measured travel through them.

Saba 34:18

Quran zahirah may point to visible, nearby, or known settlements along the route.

The continuation of the verse mentions safe travel by night and day. Thus these settlements were part of a network of traversable routes.

However, “a logistical civilizational network” is not the translation of the word itself; that image is understood from the whole verse.

Umm al-Qura and Its Surroundings

The Qur'an says:

وَهَٰذَا كِتَابٌ أَنْزَلْنَاهُ مُبَارَكٌ مُصَدِّقُ الَّذِي بَيْنَ يَدَيْهِ وَلِتُنْذِرَ أُمَّ الْقُرَىٰ وَمَنْ حَوْلَهَا

This is a blessed Book We sent down, confirming what is before it, so that you may warn the mother of the habitations and those around it.

Al-An'am 6:92

And:

لِتُنْذِرَ أُمَّ الْقُرَىٰ وَمَنْ حَوْلَهَا

Ash-Shuraa 42:7

Umm al-Qura points to a main center in relation to its surroundings. “Mother” in this expression can express origin, center, or point of reference.

The translation “mother of villages” is unsuitable when “village” carries the restricted modern meaning.

More natural suggestions:

Mother of the habitations

or:

Center of the habitations

But “capital of the world” or “center of all civilizations of the earth” imports more meaning than the expression gives.

Two Qaryahs

The opponents said:

لَوْلَا نُزِّلَ هَٰذَا الْقُرْآنُ عَلَىٰ رَجُلٍ مِنَ الْقَرْيَتَيْنِ عَظِيمٍ

Why was this Qur'an not sent down upon a great man from one of the two qaryahs?

Az-Zukhruf 43:31

Al-qaryatayn is the dual of qaryah. The Qur'anic text does not name the two settlements in the verse itself.

The verse shows that both qaryahs had men of social position and prestige. Therefore, qaryah here is also not a tiny, structureless settlement.

Still, determining the exact names of the two places depends on reports outside the verse itself and should not be placed inside the base translation.

The Coastal Qaryah

The Qur'an says:

وَاسْأَلْهُمْ عَنِ الْقَرْيَةِ الَّتِي كَانَتْ حَاضِرَةَ الْبَحْرِ

Ask them about the qaryah that was present by the sea.

Al-A'raf 7:163

A qaryah may be located on the coast, and its economic or food life may be connected with the sea.

The description hadirat al-bahr explains the geographic position. Qaryah itself does not mean “port city.”

10. Examination of Important Qur'anic Derivatives

قَرْيَة

This is the only singular form of this family in the Qur'an, and it is used in several directions:

  • the place of residence itself;
  • the people residing in it;
  • the society and its collective structure;
  • or the place and the people at the same time.

In:

أَهْلَ قَرْيَةٍ

there is an explicit distinction between place and residents.

In:

وَاسْأَلِ الْقَرْيَةَ

the name of the place stands for the people.

In:

قَرْيَةٍ خَاوِيَةٍ عَلَىٰ عُرُوشِهَا

the material fabric and buildings of the place are highlighted.

قُرَى

The plural of qaryah. It may point to separate settlements, a regional set, or a network of habitations.

Plurality itself does not mean an economic and political network. The existence of a network relation must be understood from phrases such as:

بَيْنَهُمْ وَبَيْنَ الْقُرَى

and:

قُرًى ظَاهِرَةً

أُمُّ الْقُرَى

An annexed construction:

Umm: center, origin, or mother.

Al-qura: the habitations.

The meaning of the expression is built from the relation between a center and its surroundings.

القَرْيَتَيْن

The dual form. It appears only in Az-Zukhruf 43:31. This form shows that ordinary noun inflection applies to the word, and no special geographic meaning should be extracted from the dual form itself.

11. Grammatical and Structural Notes

The Feminine Form of Qaryah

Qaryah is a feminine noun, and adjectives and pronouns related to it often come in feminine form:

قَرْيَةً كَانَتْ آمِنَةً

الْقَرْيَةَ الَّتِي كُنَّا فِيهَا

جَاءَهَا بَأْسُنَا

This feminine form is a grammatical rule and does not mean that the settlement is an independent living personality.

Attributing Human Action to Qaryah

In:

قَرْيَتِكَ الَّتِي أَخْرَجَتْكَ

driving out is the act of the people and social structure of the settlement.

In:

قَرْيَةٌ آمَنَتْ

believing is the act of the residents.

In:

قَرْيَةٍ عَتَتْ عَنْ أَمْرِ رَبِّهَا

rebellion is the conduct of the society.

This structure may be understood as mentioning the place for the people of the place. But it is not necessary to add the word “people” into every translation; English too can say “the city rebelled” or “the country decided.”

Explicit Mention of the People of a Qaryah

The Qur'an sometimes brings the word ahl itself:

أَهْلَ قَرْيَةٍ

أَهْلَ هَٰذِهِ الْقَرْيَةِ

أَهْلَ الْقُرَى

In these cases, qaryah is the place and ahl are the residents.

كَمْ and كَأَيِّنْ مِنْ قَرْيَةٍ

Structures such as:

وَكَمْ مِنْ قَرْيَةٍ

and:

وَكَأَيِّنْ مِنْ قَرْيَةٍ

express the large number of historical examples.

The word qaryah after min is singular, but the meaning includes many settlements.

Qaryah Attached to a Pronoun

In:

قَرْيَتِكُمْ

and:

قَرْيَتِكَ

the annexation may show place, residence, or social identity, not legal ownership of the entire place by one person or group.

12. Q R Y Compared with M D N

Qaryah and madinah both point to places of human residence, and their fields overlap.

Two important Qur'anic witnesses prevent a simple boundary between them:

In the story of Khidr, the place is first called qaryah and then madinah:

أَهْلَ قَرْيَةٍ

Al-Kahf 18:77

لِغُلَامَيْنِ يَتِيمَيْنِ فِي الْمَدِينَةِ

Al-Kahf 18:82

In Surah Ya-Sin, the text speaks of:

أَصْحَابَ الْقَرْيَةِ

and then:

مِنْ أَقْصَى الْمَدِينَةِ

These witnesses show that the difference between the two words is not necessarily “village versus city.”

It may be that qaryah highlights the residential community as a collective unit, while madinah highlights the urban fabric or inner space of that place. But the Qur'an does not present this distinction as a technical definition.

The claim that madinah must come from “din, law, and government,” while qaryah is only material gathering, requires independent evidence and should not be made decisive in translating the verses.

13. Q R Y Compared with B L D

Balad points more to a land, region, or geographic area, which may also be a place of human residence.

The Qur'an speaks of:

هَٰذَا الْبَلَدِ

and:

بَلَدًا آمِنًا

Qaryah more strongly highlights a settlement and the community residing in it.

As a tendency:

Balad:

Land or geographic domain.

Qaryah:

Habitation and the community settled in it.

But this distinction is not absolute. One place may be both balad and qaryah, depending on the angle of speech.

Balad should not be treated as only “land without people,” nor qaryah as only “population without land.”

14. Q R Y Compared with D A R

Dar points to a house, place of residence, abode, or land in which a person lives.

Dar may be a personal house:

دَارِكُمْ

or the domain and dwelling-place of a people:

دَارَ قَوْمٍ فَاسِقِينَ

Qaryah is usually a collective and recognizable settlement. Dar more strongly highlights place of residence or living domain from the angle of “home and staying.”

A qaryah may consist of many dars, but dar can also be broad enough to include the land of a people.

15. Q R Y Compared with A H L

Ahl are the people connected with a house, place, family, or community.

Qaryah is the place of residence; ahl al-qaryah are its residents.

The Qur'an sometimes brings the two words separately:

أَهْلَ قَرْيَةٍ

and sometimes uses the place in place of the people:

وَاسْأَلِ الْقَرْيَةَ

Recognizing these two structures matters for translation.

In verses of destruction, one must also see whether the focus is on destruction of the people, ruin of the place, or both. Mechanically translating every case as “the people of the qaryah,” or every case as “the buildings of the qaryah,” removes the precision of the text.

16. Translation Suggestions for Forqan

قَرْيَة

Main suggestions:

  • habitation
  • settlement
  • place of residence
  • town or city, where context is clear
  • the community of that habitation, in explanation

“Habitation” or “settlement” is useful because it does not impose a fixed size and can include a small or large place.

الْقَرْيَة

  • that habitation
  • the intended settlement
  • the town/city, when the context makes an urban setting clear

أَهْلُ الْقَرْيَة

  • the people of that habitation
  • the residents of that settlement
  • the people of the city, in the suitable context

قُرَى

  • habitations
  • settlements
  • towns and habitations, when variety of size is intended

أُمُّ الْقُرَى

Suggestions:

  • mother of the habitations
  • center of the habitations
  • the central habitation

“Mother of villages” is unsuitable because it imposes the restricted modern meaning of village.

قُرًى ظَاهِرَة

  • visible settlements
  • apparent habitations
  • near and visible settlements, in contextual explanation

وَاسْأَلِ الْقَرْيَة

Base suggestion:

Ask that habitation.

In explanation:

That is, ask the people of that habitation.

Or in a more explanatory translation:

Ask the people of the habitation in which we were.

قَرْيَةً كَانَتْ آمِنَةً مُطْمَئِنَّةً

A habitation that was secure and tranquil.

أَصْحَابَ الْقَرْيَة

  • the people of that habitation
  • the residents of that settlement

القَرْيَتَيْن

Two habitations.

Not “two villages,” unless independent evidence about their size exists.

Expressions to Avoid as Fixed Equivalents

  • village
  • hamlet
  • metropolis
  • center of accumulated resources
  • civilizational hub
  • population base

Some of these may be suitable in contextual explanation, but they are not fixed lexical equivalents.

17. Conclusion

The root ق ر ي appears in the Qur'an only in the word قَرْيَة and its plural قُرَى.

Final proposed definition:

Q R Y: in Qur'anic usage, a habitation or place where a human community resides and gathers; without fixed determination of smallness or greatness.

A qaryah in the Qur'an may be secure or unjust, affluent or afflicted, coastal or inland, central or peripheral. It may have affluent elites, a market, walls, treasure, routes of connection, or a structure of power. But none of these features belongs inside the fixed meaning of the word.

Qaryah sometimes shows the place itself, and sometimes uses the name of the place to refer to its residents. In:

وَاسْأَلِ الْقَرْيَةَ

the intended meaning is asking the people.

In:

خَاوِيَةٌ عَلَىٰ عُرُوشِهَا

the buildings and material space of the place are highlighted.

The word should not be restricted to “village.” The Qur'an sometimes calls one place qaryah and, in the same story, madinah. Therefore, modern city-village classification should not be imposed on the text.

At the same time, in reaction to the restrictive translation “village,” qaryah should not always be defined as “metropolis,” “center of power,” or “hub of accumulated capital.” That would be another semantic imposition.

Lexical reports connect qaryah with the gathering of people, and nearby Semitic languages also have related words for city and settlement. These witnesses show that the historical meaning of the word family is connected with human habitation, but they do not prove that all qaryahs were large, fortified, or centrally governed.

Comparison of qaryah with madinah, balad, dar, and ahl shows that the Qur'an looks at human space from different angles:

Qaryah: the habitation and the community settled in it.

Madinah: the urban place or the same habitation from another angle.

Balad: land and geographic domain.

Dar: house, abode, or domain of residence.

Ahl: the people and residents.

For Forqan translation, “habitation” or “settlement” is usually the most flexible equivalent. It imposes neither the smallness of “village” nor the greatness of “city.” Where context clarifies the size or structure of the place, one may use “city,” “settlement,” or “the people of that habitation.”
