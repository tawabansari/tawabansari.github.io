Qur’anic Root

The Qur’anic Root TH M R

Fruit, yield, visible result of growth and fertility; and more broadly, the observable output of a process, possession, or effort

Important Forms from This Root

ثَمَر / ثَمَرَة (thamar / thamarah)

fruit, yield, or visible result of growth.

ثَمَرَات (thamarāt)

fruits, produce, or provisions brought forth from the earth.

ثَمَرُه / ثَمَرِهِ (thamaruhu / thamarihi)

his fruit, yield, produce, or wealth in some contexts.

أَثْمَرَ (athmara)

it bore fruit; it reached the stage of producing yield.

يَنْعِهِ (yanʿih)

its ripening; the stage where fruit reaches maturity.

Method Note

The root TH M R begins with land and trees, but it does not remain only at the agricultural level. A thamarah is what appears after a gradual process of growth, nourishment, receiving water, flowering, and ripening. So thamar is not only “fruit” as food. It is the sign that a process has come to yield.

This matters for Forqan translation. In some verses, thamar means material fruits. In other contexts, it comes closer to wealth, yield, produce, result, or the output of a person’s life. The root shows that the Qur’an pays attention to outcomes: what the earth brings out, what the tree bears, what wealth produces, and what visible result a person finally faces after what he has planted and built.

Central Meaning

The central meaning of TH M R is the final visible yield of a gradual process. Fruit appears when water moving through the stem, nourishment of branches, flowering, and growth reach the stage of mature produce. In the Qur’an, thamarah is the point where the human being meets the concrete result of a natural or economic order.

This result may lead to gratitude, because through it the human being sees provision and divine governance. But it may also become a place of slipping, when the human being separates the yield from its source and treats it as proof of his own superiority. In the Qur’an, fruit is both blessing and trial; both the earth’s yield and the mirror of the human relation to possession and outcome.

Material Image

The material image is a branch slowly bearing fruit. First there is earth, root, trunk, leaf, and blossom. Then the fruit forms, grows, takes color, and approaches ripeness. Thamarah is the moment when the natural movement of the tree becomes visible, pickable, usable produce.

In this image, fruit is not merely a beautiful or edible object. It is witness to an entire process. Water, soil, light, season, time, and measure are gathered in it. This is why, when the Qur’an speaks of fruits, it invites the human being to see behind the fruit as well: the ripened product does not appear apart from divine governance and the precise order of nature.

Classical Arabic Note

In Arabic, thamar first refers to the fruit or yield of a tree, but naturally extends to yield and result. Just as a tree gives fruit, wealth, work, and planning may also have thamar: an observable output or result.

This link appears in the Qur’an. Sometimes thamarāt are the produce of land and gardens. Sometimes the thamar of a garden-owner comes close to his wealth, assets, and broad material yield. Therefore, thamarah is not always limited to a single fruit. It may refer to the total yield of an economic or living system.

Qur’anic Usage

Fruits as Provision Brought Out from the Earth

Surat al-Baqarah says: وَأَنزَلَ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً فَأَخْرَجَ بِهِ مِنَ الثَّمَرَاتِ رِزْقًا لَّكُمْ (wa-anzala mina al-samāʾi māʾan fa-akhraja bihi mina al-thamarāti rizqan lakum) 2:22. Fruits here are the result of the relation between sky and earth.

Water descends from above, the earth becomes active, and fruits are brought out as provision for the human being. Thamarah here is not merely fruit. It is the sign that the earth’s hidden capacity has become usable provision.

Diversity of Fruits and Difference of Colors

The Qur’an says: فَأَخْرَجْنَا بِهِ ثَمَرَاتٍ مُّخْتَلِفًا أَلْوَانُهَا (fa-akhrajnā bihi thamarātin mukhtalifan alwānuhā) 35:27. Through one water, fruits of different colors and kinds are brought out.

This draws attention to the diversity of outcomes. The material input may be shared, but the outputs differ. Differences in color, taste, form, and property are signs of measure and divine governance in the plant world.

Looking at Fruit When It Bears and Ripens

Surat al-Anʿām says: انظُرُوا إِلَىٰ ثَمَرِهِ إِذَا أَثْمَرَ وَيَنْعِهِ (unẓurū ilā thamarihi idhā athmara wayanʿih) 6:99. The verse directs the eye to two moments: when the tree bears fruit, and when that fruit reaches ripeness.

The fruit here is not only the final product. It is a sign that allows the human being to see the stages of growth and maturity. The Qur’an asks the reader to look at fruit not only for eating, but for seeing the sign within the process of bearing and ripening.

Eating from Fruit and Giving Its Right

Surat al-Anʿām also says: كُلُوا مِن ثَمَرِهِ إِذَا أَثْمَرَ وَآتُوا حَقَّهُ يَوْمَ حَصَادِهِ (kulū min thamarihi idhā athmara waātū ḥaqqahu yawma ḥaṣādih) 6:141. Fruit here is joined to benefit and responsibility.

The human being may eat from the fruit, but he must also give its right on the day of harvest. Mature produce is not only for private consumption. At the moment of harvest, right and social responsibility also become visible.

Fruits as a Sign of Reviving the Earth

Surat al-Aʿrāf says: فَأَخْرَجْنَا بِهِ مِن كُلِّ الثَّمَرَاتِ كَذَٰلِكَ نُخْرِجُ الْمَوْتَىٰ (fa-akhrajnā bihi min kulli al-thamarāti kadhālika nukhriju al-mawtā) 7:57. The coming out of fruits after descending water is used as an example for the coming out of the dead.

Dry land becomes alive through rain and gives fruit. The Qur’an uses this visible experience to bring resurrection closer, moving it from a distant idea into a scene human beings can observe.

Thamar as a Trial of Pride in the Story of the Two Gardens

Surat al-Kahf says: وَكَانَ لَهُ ثَمَرٌ فَقَالَ لِصَاحِبِهِ وَهُوَ يُحَاوِرُهُ أَنَا أَكْثَرُ مِنكَ مَالًا وَأَعَزُّ نَفَرًا (wakāna lahu thamarun faqāla liṣāḥibihi wahuwa yuḥāwiruhu anā aktharu minka mālan wa-aʿazzu nafarā) 18:34.

Here thamar refers to the owner’s yield and wealth. He sees the produce of his garden, his resources, and his economic strength, but instead of gratitude he turns them into pride. Thamar becomes a trial. The problem is not having yield. The problem is separating the yield from its true source and making it proof of personal superiority.

The Destruction of Thamar: Collapse of Unstable Reliance

Later in the same story, the Qur’an says: وَأُحِيطَ بِثَمَرِهِ فَأَصْبَحَ يُقَلِّبُ كَفَّيْهِ عَلَىٰ مَا أَنفَقَ فِيهَا (wa-uḥīṭa bithamarihi fa-aṣbaḥa yuqallibu kaffayhi ʿalā mā anfaqa fīhā) 18:42. His yield was encompassed by destruction, and he began turning his hands over what he had spent on it.

This verse shows that thamar, even though it is the result of growth and effort, is not independent or guaranteed. A person may work for years on a garden, wealth, or project, but if he sees it as independent from The God and outside gratitude, the very yield he trusted may disappear suddenly.

Fruits and the Security of a City

In Abraham’s prayer, he says: رَبِّ اجْعَلْ هَٰذَا بَلَدًا آمِنًا وَارْزُقْ أَهْلَهُ مِنَ الثَّمَرَاتِ (rabbi ijʿal hādhā baladan āminan warzuq ahlahu mina al-thamarāt) 2:126. Fruits appear beside the security of the city.

A city needs more than walls, houses, and population. It needs security and provision. Fruits here are part of the stability of collective life: produce that makes life sustainable and ordered.

Loss of Fruits as Trial

The Qur’an says: وَلَنَبْلُوَنَّكُم بِشَيْءٍ مِّنَ الْخَوْفِ وَالْجُوعِ وَنَقْصٍ مِّنَ الْأَمْوَالِ وَالْأَنفُسِ وَالثَّمَرَاتِ (walanabluwannakum bishayʾin mina al-khawfi wal-jūʿi wanaqṣin mina al-amwāli wal-anfusi wal-thamarāt) 2:155. Loss of fruits is one form of trial.

This loss may mean reduced produce, harm to gardens and crops, or decrease in the yield of livelihood. The human being is tested in abundance of fruits and in their decrease. The Qur’an treats abundance as a field of gratitude and loss as a field of patience and re-recognizing the true source of support.

Fruits of Paradise

In the description of Paradise, the Qur’an says: كُلَّمَا رُزِقُوا مِنْهَا مِن ثَمَرَةٍ رِّزْقًا قَالُوا هَٰذَا الَّذِي رُزِقْنَا مِن قَبْلُ (kullamā ruziqū minhā min thamaratin rizqan qālū hādhā alladhī ruziqnā min qabl) 2:25. Fruit in Paradise is also connected with provision, but at another level.

The believers meet fruits that are in some way familiar, yet in reality belong to a higher and purer level. This use shows that thamarah is not only a sign of worldly livelihood. In the image of the Hereafter, it also becomes a sign of hospitality, honor, and continuing provision.

Difference from Nearby Words

Fākihah focuses more on fruit as enjoyable food and delight. Thamarah is broader; it can mean any visible yield or result of a process. Rizq is whatever is given to the human being as provision, benefit, or support for life. Fruits may be rizq, but rizq is not limited to fruits.

Ghallah in Arabic usage is closer to produce or revenue from land or a garden. Thamarah is more root-like and imagistic, pointing to the actual fruit or yield that has appeared. Ḥabb points to grain and seed, food that appears mostly in the form of field crops. Thamarah is closer to tree-fruit and mature yield, though it may also carry the broader sense of result and produce.

Nabāt is vegetation, the thing that has grown. Thamarah is the fruit and yield of that growth.

Translation Method

Thamar and thamarāt must be translated according to context. In verses about gardens and natural provision, “fruits” or “produce” may be right. In the story of the owner of the two gardens, thamar may be closer to “yield,” “wealth,” “produce,” or “the fruit and assets of his garden.” In the verse about loss of fruits, “loss of fruits” alone may be too narrow, because the meaning includes reduction of produce and livelihood output.

Forqan translation should preserve both senses: fruit as the visible product of a plant, and thamarah as the broader visible yield of growth.

Forqan Translation Suggestions

مِنَ الثَّمَرَاتِ رِزْقًا لَّكُمْ (mina al-thamarāti rizqan lakum): from fruits He brought out provision for you; from fruits and produce He made provision appear for you.

ثَمَرَاتٍ مُّخْتَلِفًا أَلْوَانُهَا (thamarātin mukhtalifan alwānuhā): fruits of different colors and kinds.

انظُرُوا إِلَىٰ ثَمَرِهِ إِذَا أَثْمَرَ وَيَنْعِهِ (unẓurū ilā thamarihi idhā athmara wayanʿih): look at its fruit when it bears, and at its ripening.

كُلُوا مِن ثَمَرِهِ إِذَا أَثْمَرَ (kulū min thamarihi idhā athmara): eat from its fruit when it bears.

وَكَانَ لَهُ ثَمَرٌ (wakāna lahu thamar): he had great yield from those gardens; his garden had abundant fruit and wealth.

وَأُحِيطَ بِثَمَرِهِ (wa-uḥīṭa bithamarihi): his yield and wealth were surrounded by destruction; the fruit and output of his life collapsed.

وَنَقْصٍ مِّنَ الْأَمْوَالِ وَالْأَنفُسِ وَالثَّمَرَاتِ (wanaqṣin mina al-amwāli wal-anfusi wal-thamarāt): loss in wealth, lives, and produce; decrease in property, lives, and the yield of livelihood.

Summary

The root TH M R in the Qur’an is the language of visible yield. Fruit shows that every process of growth eventually reaches an output: seed becomes produce, earth becomes provision, a garden becomes wealth, and human action becomes result. But the Qur’an does not allow the human being to stop at the fruit and forget the source. However sweet, abundant, and beautiful the fruit may be, it is not independent. It has risen from water, soil, time, measure, and The God’s governance.

In Forqan’s reading, thamarah is not only the fruit of a tree. It is the sign that a process has reached the stage of harvest and appearance. The human being is nourished by fruits, tested by their abundance, tested by their loss, and through seeing them should remember that every yield, before becoming human property, is a sign of Lordship and governance.
