The Qur’anic Root TH M R

Fruit and produce, with extensions to yield and wealth

The Arabic root ث م ر is associated with the fruit and produce borne by trees and other plants. ثَمَر names the fruit or produce, while أَثْمَرَ means to bear fruit. This family also occurs in uses involving yield and the increase of possessions. Each Qur’anic occurrence should be understood in relation to the plants, gardens, or wealth discussed in its verse.

In the Qur’an, the root appears chiefly in descriptions of fruits, gardens, provision, abundance, and diminished crops. Its two occurrences in the story of the owner of two gardens in Al-Kahf are particularly important because of their connection with wealth and differences in recitation.

The main Qur’anic forms are ثَمَر, ثَمَرَة, ثَمَرَات, and the verb أَثْمَرَ. Forms such as ثَمَرِهِ are the noun thamar with an attached pronoun, rather than a separate derivative. Alternative recitations in the two Al-Kahf verses include ثُمُر and ثُمْر, which deserve attention when examining their meaning.

In the Qur’anic text according to Ḥafṣ, this root occurs 24 times: twice as athmara, five times as thamar, once as thamarah, and 16 times as thamarāt.

Core meaning and physical image

The clear, attested meaning of thamar is the fruit or produce of plants, especially trees. A tree grows, spreads its branches, and bears fruit under suitable conditions. What develops and grows on those branches is its thamar. This familiar relationship between a tree and its fruit makes broader uses involving yield and benefit understandable.

The formation and ripening of fruit are related stages. Fruit can be called thamar as it forms, with ripening as a further stage. Al-Anʿām 6:99 places these aspects alongside one another through إِذَا أَثْمَرَ and يَنْعِهِ.

In use, thamar can denote a garden’s combined fruit and yield and can relate to wealth in a financial context. The connection between fruit-bearing and yield makes this extension understandable. More dated evidence would nevertheless be needed to establish the precise historical development of the meaning.

Thamar in early and pre-Qur’anic Arabic

Pre-Qur’anic poetry also shows this family being used for wealth and children. The following example concerns cultivating and gathering what a person possesses. Its range at that time therefore extended beyond descriptions of tree fruit.

In his poem beginning يا دار مية بالعلياء فالسند, al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī addresses al-Nuʿmān ibn al-Mundhir:

مَهْلًا فِدَاءٌ لَكَ الْأَقْوَامُ كُلُّهُمُ
وَمَا أُثَمِّرُ مِنْ مَالٍ وَمِنْ وَلَدِ

Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī, the poem beginning “Yā dāra Mayyata bi-l-ʿalyāʾi fa-l-sanadi.”

“Be gentle; may all the peoples, and what I have gathered of wealth and children, be a ransom for you.”

Here, أُثَمِّرُ is an imperfect verb in form II, applied to wealth and children. The poet offers what he has gathered and nurtured as a ransom for his addressee. This attests a nonbotanical use of the family before the Qur’an, but does not by itself establish that every noun from the root meant all kinds of possessions in every context.

The verbal form also matters: the poem’s أُثَمِّرُ comes from ثَمَّرَ, whereas أَثْمَرَ in the two Al-Anʿām verses is form IV and means to bear fruit. The proximity of these forms does not erase differences in their construction and use.

Wealth and children in the verse are things a person gathers and nurtures. The example makes a connection between fruitfulness and the growth of what one possesses intelligible. The modern technical meaning of “investment” is unnecessary for explaining either the poem or the Qur’anic verses.

In its botanical sense, ثَمَر is a collective noun, and ثَمَرَة can identify one unit of it; ثَمَرَات is the plural of thamarah. Forms such as ثِمَار and ثُمُر are also examined as plural formations. Differences in the analysis of some plurals do not, by themselves, determine a verse’s meaning.

This evidence presents two related uses: the fruit and produce of plants, and cultivating or increasing human possessions. Within the Qur’an, the individual verse supplies the clues to which aspect is prominent.

Thamar in the Qur’an: bearing fruit, ripening, and harvest

Bearing fruit and ripening are different

One of the clearest uses appears in Al-Anʿām:

انظُرُوا إِلَىٰ ثَمَرِهِ إِذَا أَثْمَرَ وَيَنْعِهِ

“Look at its fruit when it bears fruit, and at its ripening.”

Al-Anʿām 6:99

Here, ثَمَرِهِ names the plant’s fruit or produce. أَثْمَرَ, a form IV verb, expresses bearing fruit. The following يَنْعِهِ describes the fruit’s ripening.

إِذَا أَثْمَرَ draws attention to bearing fruit, and يَنْعِهِ to that fruit’s ripening. Distinguishing the expressions helps keep fruit formation separate from maturation.

يَنْع belongs to the separate root ي ن ع. It describes the ripening stage in this verse and is not a derivative of TH M R.

Fruit, food, and the due at harvest

The same verb recurs elsewhere in Al-Anʿām:

كُلُوا مِن ثَمَرِهِ إِذَا أَثْمَرَ وَآتُوا حَقَّهُ يَوْمَ حَصَادِهِ

“Eat of its fruit when it bears fruit, and give its due on the day of its harvest.”

Al-Anʿām 6:141

Thamar here denotes produce from which people benefit. The instruction to eat the plant’s fruit accompanies the instruction to give its due at harvest.

حَصَاد, harvesting, belongs to a different root. Three concepts should therefore remain distinct: thamar is the produce; athmara expresses bearing fruit; and ḥaṣād concerns harvesting it.

وَآتُوا حَقَّهُ connects enjoying the produce with giving its due. Entitlement and responsibility belong to this command; thamar remains the name of the fruit or produce.

The emergence and variety of fruits

Fāṭir states:

فَأَخْرَجْنَا بِهِ ثَمَرَاتٍ مُّخْتَلِفًا أَلْوَانُهَا

“Then through it We brought forth fruits of varying colors.”

Fāṭir 35:27

ثَمَرَات denotes fruits collectively here. مُخْتَلِفًا أَلْوَانُهَا describes their different colors. Although fruits also differ in taste, shape, and other properties, this particular expression specifies color.

Al-Raʿd 13:3 also mentions زَوْجَيْنِ اثْنَيْنِ in connection with fruits. The phrase needs to be examined in context, rather than automatically being converted into a particular botanical theory or a modern classification of plant sexuality.

Fuṣṣilat 41:47 also says:

وَمَا تَخْرُجُ مِن ثَمَرَاتٍ مِّنْ أَكْمَامِهَا

“And no fruit emerges from its coverings…”

Fuṣṣilat 41:47

The verse relates the emergence of fruits from their coverings to the knowledge of TheGod. In this image, fruit already bears that name as it emerges from its natural covering; naming it thamar does not depend on its being picked or ready for sale.

Fruits in provision, life, and livelihood

Fruits as provision

Al-Baqarah states:

وَأَنزَلَ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً فَأَخْرَجَ بِهِ مِنَ الثَّمَرَاتِ رِزْقًا لَّكُمْ

“And He sent down water from the sky, then through it brought forth fruits as provision for you.”

Al-Baqarah 2:22

The same construction recurs in Ibrāhīm 14:32. Both verses connect descending water and the emergence of fruits with human provision.

Thamarāt are the plant products themselves, while رِزْقًا describes their role in sustaining people. Fruits can be provision, but thamar and rizq are not synonyms. Provision has a much broader Qur’anic range than fruit or crops alone.

Al-Naḥl 16:11 places crops, olives, palms, grapes, and fruits together. This combination presents a range of growing plants and produce without requiring all the terms to have identical lexical meanings.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:57, bringing forth fruits after rain also provides an analogy for bringing forth the dead. Growth and the emergence of produce supply the scene for the comparison. The analogy does not change the lexical meaning of thamarāt from fruits into “resurrection.”

Yā Sīn 36:35 speaks of eating fruit after describing the revival of the earth and the emergence of gardens. Produce belongs to human life and nourishment in these verses; it is not a general technical term for every human achievement.

Fruits and communal security

Abraham’s prayer says:

رَبِّ اجْعَلْ هَٰذَا بَلَدًا آمِنًا وَارْزُقْ أَهْلَهُ مِنَ الثَّمَرَاتِ

“My Lord, make this a secure land, and provide its people with fruits.”

Al-Baqarah 2:126

The request for provision from fruits recurs in Ibrāhīm 14:37, this time concerning the settlement of some of Abraham’s descendants in a valley without cultivation.

Both verses connect fruits with people’s lives and sustenance. Yet thamarāt does not itself mean security, a city’s economy, or settlement and flourishing. Security and provision occur together in the verses, each retaining its own meaning.

The connection becomes clearer in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:57:

أَوَلَمْ نُمَكِّن لَّهُمْ حَرَمًا آمِنًا يُجْبَىٰ إِلَيْهِ ثَمَرَاتُ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ رِّزْقًا مِّن لَّدُنَّا

“Have We not established for them a secure sanctuary to which varied produce is brought, as provision from Us?”

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:57

يُجْبَىٰ refers to gathering and bringing in. Here, produce is brought to a secure sanctuary. It need not have grown in that location: goods and produce can arrive from elsewhere.

ثَمَرَاتُ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ expresses the abundance and variety of produce arriving at the sanctuary. The context of supplying provision clarifies the scope of “everything”; it does not mean that every individual entity has fruit. “Varied produce” preserves this breadth without making thamarāt a name for every kind of merchandise.

Fruits and what people make from them

Al-Naḥl 16:67 says:

وَمِن ثَمَرَاتِ النَّخِيلِ وَالْأَعْنَابِ تَتَّخِذُونَ مِنْهُ سَكَرًا وَرِزْقًا حَسَنًا إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَآيَةً لِّقَوْمٍ يَعْقِلُونَ

“And from the fruits of date palms and grapevines you make intoxicating drink and good provision. In that is indeed a sign for people who reason.”

Al-Naḥl 16:67

This verse distinguishes fruit from what people make from it. Dates and grapes are the raw materials from which products are prepared. Thamarāt therefore identifies the material from which those products are made, rather than the manufactured products themselves.

The ruling or valuation concerning سَكَرًا and رِزْقًا حَسَنًا belongs to the wording and context of that verse. It should not be transferred into the lexical definition of thamar.

In Al-Naḥl 16:69, thamarāt also appears in the address to the bee. It should be understood within the context of the bee’s feeding and its relation to vegetation. Without clear linguistic evidence, however, a precise modern biological term such as “floral nectar” should not simply replace thamarāt in the basic translation.

Diminished fruits and lost produce

Al-Baqarah says:

وَلَنَبْلُوَنَّكُم بِشَيْءٍ مِّنَ الْخَوْفِ وَالْجُوعِ وَنَقْصٍ مِّنَ الْأَمْوَالِ وَالْأَنفُسِ وَالثَّمَرَاتِ وَبَشِّرِ الصَّابِرِينَ

“We shall certainly test you with something of fear and hunger, and loss of wealth, lives, and crops. Give good news to those who remain patient.”

Al-Baqarah 2:155

Thamarāt appears alongside wealth and lives. This highlights the importance of produce in human life without making the terms identical. Amwāl denotes possessions, while thamarāt here is closer to the fruit and produce on which livelihoods depend.

“Loss of crops” conveys the botanical sense and makes the livelihood context clear. The wider consequences of such a shortage can be explored in explaining the verse.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:130 likewise describes Pharaoh’s people experiencing difficult years and diminished fruits. Here too, نقص من الثمرات concerns reduced produce, a shortage that can affect people’s lives and food supply.

Al-Baqarah 2:266 presents a garden with palms, grapes, and varied produce that is destroyed by a fiery whirlwind. Although the parable occurs in a context of spending and human conduct, the fruits within the image remain garden produce. The garden’s figurative role does not, by itself, turn thamarāt into a word meaning human deeds.

Thamar in the story of the two gardens: produce or wealth?

Two verses in Al-Kahf are especially important for the broader meaning of this family.

The story begins with a man who owns two gardens of grapevines, surrounded by palms, with crops between them. Both gardens yield their produce, and a river runs between them.

It then says:

وَكَانَ لَهُ ثَمَرٌ فَقَالَ لِصَاحِبِهِ وَهُوَ يُحَاوِرُهُ أَنَا أَكْثَرُ مِنكَ مَالًا وَأَعَزُّ نَفَرًا

“He had produce, and said to his companion while conversing with him: I have more wealth than you, and greater strength in people.”

Al-Kahf 18:34

Thamar can be understood in connection with the gardens’ fruit and yield. The man’s immediately following statement, أَنَا أَكْثَرُ مِنكَ مَالًا, shows that this yield has monetary value in his life and belongs to a context of possessions and economic power.

The preceding verse said كِلْتَا الْجَنَّتَيْنِ آتَتْ أُكُلَهَا: both gardens produced their yield. The statement that he had thamar follows. This proximity permits understanding the term as both garden produce and the wealth associated with it. It does not, on its own, prove that the word names all his possessions, from money and livestock to every other property.

Recitation variants and meaning

In the transmission of Ḥafṣ from ʿĀṣim, Al-Kahf 18:34 has ثَمَرٌ, and 18:42 بِثَمَرِهِ. Abū ʿAmr’s recitation has ثُمْرٌ and بِثُمْرِهِ, with ḍammah on thāʾ and sukūn on mīm; Nāfiʿ’s has ثُمُرٌ and بِثُمُرِهِ, with ḍammah on both letters. These are examples of the recitation differences at these two locations.

These forms are considered in analyses of plurals and collective nouns. Their vowels alone do not establish a rule that fatḥah always means “fruit” and ḍammah always means “money.” In each recitation, the gardens, their produce, and the owner’s statement about wealth provide the main contextual evidence.

Later in the story:

وَأُحِيطَ بِثَمَرِهِ فَأَصْبَحَ يُقَلِّبُ كَفَّيْهِ عَلَىٰ مَا أَنفَقَ فِيهَا وَهِيَ خَاوِيَةٌ عَلَىٰ عُرُوشِهَا وَيَقُولُ يَا لَيْتَنِي لَمْ أُشْرِكْ بِرَبِّي أَحَدًا

“His produce was engulfed in destruction. He began turning his hands over in regret at what he had spent on the garden, while it lay fallen upon its trellises, and said: If only I had associated no one with my Lord!”

Al-Kahf 18:42

Mentioning the man’s expenditure and the garden’s ruin connects the destruction of thamar with the loss of its economic yield. ثَمَرِهِ denotes his fruit and produce; the context also brings its monetary value and associated benefit into view.

The man’s produce and possessions stand at the center of the story. His arrogance, sense of self-sufficiency, and regret are understood through his words and the events. They are not components of thamar’s lexical meaning.

In these verses, garden produce and its financial value are intertwined. “The garden’s yield,” “fruit and produce,” and, when explaining the context, “wealth associated with the gardens” make that connection clear.

Fruits in the Garden and comparison with related words

Thamarah in descriptions of the Garden

Al-Baqarah states:

كُلَّمَا رُزِقُوا مِنْهَا مِن ثَمَرَةٍ رِّزْقًا قَالُوا هَٰذَا الَّذِي رُزِقْنَا مِن قَبْلُ وَأُتُوا بِهِ مُتَشَابِهًا

“Whenever they are provided with a fruit from those gardens as provision, they say: This is what we were provided with before; and it is brought to them in resemblance.”

Al-Baqarah 2:25

The singular ثَمَرَة appears here in a description of enjoying the gardens of the Hereafter. وَأُتُوا بِهِ مُتَشَابِهًا speaks of resemblance. Without further evidence, the precise nature of that resemblance should not be restricted to a particular difference in taste, shape, or the fruits’ underlying nature.

Muḥammad 47:15 also states:

وَلَهُمْ فِيهَا مِن كُلِّ الثَّمَرَاتِ وَمَغْفِرَةٌ مِّن رَّبِّهِمْ

“In it they have every kind of fruit and forgiveness from their Lord.”

Muḥammad 47:15

Thamarāt thus also occurs in descriptions of the Hereafter. The familiar meaning of fruit and garden produce remains, but the nature and reality of blessings in the Hereafter cannot be determined solely from our experience of earthly fruits.

The association of fruits and forgiveness does not make thamarāt mean forgiveness itself or spiritual reward. Both are mentioned in the verse, each in its own place.

TH M R (thamar) compared with F K H (fākihah)

فَاكِهَة also occurs in Qur’anic contexts of food and enjoyment of fruit. In ʿAbasa 80:31–32, for example, it stands alongside other vegetation, followed by مَتَاعًا لَكُمْ وَلِأَنْعَامِكُمْ. Thamar highlights the fruit and produce of plants, and its family also has financial uses. This comparison does not establish pleasantness as an exclusive semantic component of fākihah.

The difference is not an absolute boundary. Many fruits are both thamar and fākihah. An occurrence of thamar does not exclude eating and enjoyment, nor does fākihah eliminate the idea of a plant’s produce.

TH M R (thamar) compared with ʾ K L (ukul)

In uses concerning trees and gardens, أُكُل denotes edible yield or produce. In the story of the two gardens, آتَتْ أُكُلَهَا describes the gardens giving their yield, while thamar subsequently refers to their fruit and produce.

These words overlap. Their difference in emphasis is that thamar names the produce, whereas ukul can present that produce as something eaten and enjoyed. Ukul also has a wider range in Arabic and should not be restricted to the act of eating a single fruit.

TH M R compared with Z R ʿ (zarʿ) and N B T (nabāt)

زَرْع concerns cultivation and what is sown and grown, whereas thamar refers to the plant’s produce. Fields and gardens can yield produce; sowing and growing are concepts related to the product itself.

نَبَات concerns vegetation and plant growth. Al-Anʿām 6:99 speaks of bringing forth vegetation, then grains and fruits. The sequence presents plant growth and the emergence of produce as related but distinct aspects.

TH M R compared with R Z Q (rizq)

Rizq is what reaches someone for benefit and sustenance, and its semantic range extends beyond plant products. Fruits can be rizq, as in Al-Baqarah 2:22 and Ibrāhīm 14:32. Yet not all provision is fruit, and a tree’s produce is not defined solely by calling it provision.

The Arabic تَمْر, meaning dates, does not share a root with ثَمَر. Their similar sound should not lead to confusing T M R with TH M R.

Translation proposals

In verses about plants bearing fruit, thamar can normally be rendered “fruit,” “produce,” or “yield.” Thamarah can be “a fruit” or “a piece of fruit,” while thamarāt can be “fruits,” “kinds of fruit,” or “crops,” depending on context.

For athmara, “bore fruit” or “bears fruit” is natural. In Al-Anʿām 6:99, its distinction from yanʿ must remain: the first concerns fruit-bearing, the second ripening.

In verses such as Al-Baqarah 2:155 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:130, “loss of crops” or “diminished produce” is appropriate. It preserves the botanical meaning and makes the role of crops in people’s livelihoods understandable.

In Al-Kahf 18:34 and 18:42, “yield” or “garden produce” stays close to the story’s imagery. Its monetary value can be made explicit in explanation. “Wealth” is also a broader contextual reading, but its type and extent cannot be determined from the word alone.

Conclusion

In its clear Qur’anic use, TH M R concerns the fruit and produce of plants. Al-Nābighah’s verse also attests a nonbotanical use of a verb from the same family for wealth and children. The connection helps illuminate the financial aspect of the story of the two gardens, without establishing the precise history of every semantic development.

In the Qur’an, thamar can be a tree’s fruit, combined produce, or yield and wealth associated with a garden. It occurs alongside provision, harvest, security, shortage, abundance, and blessings of the Garden. None of those concepts alone is its lexical definition.

The clear semantic core is the fruit and produce of plants. In pre-Qur’anic Arabic, the family also served to describe nurturing and increasing possessions; in the story of the two gardens, produce and its monetary value are intertwined. Each occurrence is understood through the word’s form and the context of its sentence.
