Pressing together, joining parts, and the firmness produced by their connection

The Arabic root R Ṣ Ṣ concerns bringing the parts of something close together, pressing them together, and joining them firmly. This sense appears in descriptions of buildings whose stones or components fit closely, closely set teeth, tightly arranged eggs, and people drawing together in a rank.

In this family, رَصَّ means to press parts together and join them firmly, while مَرْصُوص describes something whose parts are firmly and closely joined.

This root expresses more than merely gathering several things: closeness, connection, and the resulting firmness are prominent. Yet not every gathering is raṣṣ, nor is everything described as marṣūṣ necessarily made from the same material, in the same size, or in the same shape.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as مَرْصُوصٌ. The Qur’an compares those who fight in ranks in The God’s way to a building whose parts are firmly joined.

The comparison concerns human beings, but its image comes from construction. The root’s physical meaning, the qualities of a closely joined building, and the comparison’s purpose in context therefore require separate consideration.

Qur’anic form: مَرْصُوصٌ (Al-Ṣaff 61:4).

Arabic records forms including رَصَّ, يَرُصُّ, رَصّ, رَصَص, رَصِيص, رَصَّصَ, تَرْصِيص, تَرَاصَّ, تَرَاصّ, اِرْتَصَّ, أَرَصّ, رَصَّاء, and رَصَاص. The related reduplicated quadriliteral forms رَصْرَصَ, رَصْرَاص, and رَصْرَاصَة belong near this semantic field but should not simply be classified as triliteral derivatives. These forms do not occur as such in the Qur’an.

1. Core meaning and early Arabic usage

Pressing parts together and joining them firmly

A lexical explanation introduces the root’s core meaning as follows:

أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى انْضِمَامِ الشَّيْءِ إِلَى الشَّيْءِ بِقُوَّةٍ وَتَدَاخُلٍ

aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā inḍimāmi al-shayʾi ilā al-shayʾi bi-quwwatin wa-tadākhulin

“A single principle indicating one thing joining another with strength and interconnection.”

This definition highlights two features: the parts coming close together and the firmness of their connection.

In Arabic, رَصَصْتُ الْبُنْيَانَ means that I brought a building’s parts close together and secured them.

A lexical explanation describes this usage as follows:

رَصَّ الْبُنْيَانَ يَرُصُّهُ رَصًّا، فَهُوَ مَرْصُوصٌ وَرَصِيصٌ

raṣṣa al-bunyāna yaruṣṣuhu raṣṣan fa-huwa marṣūṣun wa-raṣīṣun

“He joined the building’s parts firmly together; the building is therefore marṣūṣ or raṣīṣ.”

Here, stones or building components are placed together so that unsuitable gaps are reduced and the whole forms a connected structure.

Bringing parts close, joining them, and the structure’s eventual durability must nevertheless be distinguished.

Raṣṣ emphasizes the arrangement and connection of parts. How much pressure, impact, or wear a building can withstand also depends on its materials and other construction features.

Marṣūṣ should therefore not be defined as “indestructible” or “a building that never collapses.”

Firmness and connection belong to its meaning; everlasting durability does not follow from the word alone.

رَصَّصَ and رَصْرَصَ: making a building firm

Arabic also records رَصَّصَ الْبُنْيَانَ and رَصْرَصَهُ for making a building firm and joining its parts.

رَصَّصَ is a Form II triliteral verb, while رَصْرَصَ is a reduplicated quadriliteral verb. Both differ structurally from رَصَّ, although their construction senses overlap.

All three verbs are recorded for bringing building components together and securing them.

The repetition of ṣād in رَصْرَصَ alone does not prove that the verb necessarily expresses several distinct stages or a numerical degree of compression.

A morphological pattern may convey intensity or repetition, but its precise degree and character must be established from actual usage.

In another usage, رَصْرَصَ also concerns remaining firmly established in one place.

This is connected with stability, but does not make absolute immobility the meaning of every use in the family.

A marṣūṣ building is chiefly characterized by its connected and firmly arranged parts; the word does not deny every possibility of change or movement.

تَرَاصَّ: drawing close to one another

In Arabic, تَرَاصَّ الْقَوْمُ means that people drew close and stood beside one another.

This usage is explained as follows:

وَتَرَاصَّ الْقَوْمُ: تَضَامُّوا وَتَلَاصَقُوا

wa-tarāṣṣa al-qawmu: taḍāmmū wa-talāṣaqū

“The people drew together: they came close and into contact with one another.”

Here the subject is a group of people rather than stones or building materials.

People can stand close enough to reduce the gaps between them and form a connected group.

This usage is also familiar in descriptions of ranks.

An early explanation of تَرَاصّ describes people drawing so close that no obvious gap remains between them.

This should not be understood as erasing differences in personality, will, or responsibility.

People can stand in a coordinated rank without becoming a single being.

Just as joined stones remain distinct components in the building image, human cohesion does not mean the destruction of individual identity.

رَصِيص: something whose parts are closely arranged

Arabic uses رَصِيص to describe something whose parts are close together and connected.

It is recorded alongside مَرْصُوص as a description of a firm building.

Its use is not confined to buildings.

بَيْضٌ رَصِيصٌ describes eggs arranged closely and compactly together.

رَصِيص is also used of closely set teeth.

These usages show that the root does not refer only to strength produced by joining stone or metal.

Softer or separate items can also be arranged so closely and orderly within a group that they are described as رَصِيص.

The definition should therefore emphasize compactness, closeness, and connection rather than a particular material.

2. Early poetic evidence: closely arranged eggs and stones

بَيْضٌ رَصِيصٌ in the poetry of Imruʾ al-Qays

An ode attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays contains this verse:

عَلَىٰ نِقْنِقٍ هَيْقٍ لَهُ وَلِعِرْسِهِ
بِمُنْعَرَجِ الْوَعْسَاءِ بَيْضٌ رَصِيصُ

ʿalā niqniqin hayqin lahu wa-li-ʿirsihi / bi-munʿaraji al-waʿsāʾi bayḍun raṣīṣu

“… As though I and my equipment were upon a tall male ostrich, for whom and his mate there are closely packed eggs at a bend in the sandy ground.” The opening of this rendering is supplied from the preceding verse to clarify the attachment of عَلَىٰ; this verse does not mention a sandy mound.

نِقْنِق refers to the male ostrich, هَيْق to its tall stature, and عِرْس to its mate.

الْوَعْسَاء concerns soft, sandy ground.

The poet describes eggs lying close together in one place.

رَصِيص here qualifies the eggs.

This matters for understanding the root: compact arrangement is not exclusive to buildings or hard materials.

Eggs may be close and orderly without being joined like the stones of a wall.

Not every use of R Ṣ Ṣ therefore means permanently bonding parts with another material.

In this witness, closeness and compact arrangement are prominent.

The verse is transmitted in the ode beginning أَمِنْ ذِكْرِ سَلْمَى أَنْ نَأْتَكَ تَنُوصُ.

Variants reported for part of the second hemistich include بِمُنْخَدَعِ الْوَعْسَاءِ, بِمُنْعَرَجِ الْوَعْسَاءِ, and بِمُنْقَطَعِ الْوَعْسَاءِ.

These readings do not fundamentally alter the use of رَصِيص for closely packed eggs.

The witness matters because this description appears in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period, outside Qur’anic commentary. The precise composition date of this verse has not been independently established here.

رَصْرَاصَة in the poetry of al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī

A verse attributed to al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī reads:

حِجَارَةُ قَلْتٍ بِرَصْرَاصَةٍ
كُسِينَ غِشَاءً مِنَ الطُّحْلُبِ

ḥijāratu qaltin bi-raṣrāṣatin / kusīna ghishāʾan mina al-ṭuḥlubi

“The stones of a water hollow, beside a mass of closely joined stones, have been clothed with a covering of algae.”

رَصْرَاصَة here concerns stones lying close together around a water place.

قَلْت means a hollow in rock or ground where water collects.

طُحْلُب concerns the vegetation or algae covering water and stones.

This usage accords with stones gathering and lying firmly together.

There is, however, an important variant in the transmission of this verse.

Some lexical transmissions read بِرَصْرَاصَةٍ; others record بِرَضْرَاضَةٍ.

رَضْرَاضَة belongs to another family and concerns small stones or gravel.

Without acknowledging this variant, the verse cannot be treated as conclusive evidence for رَصْرَاصَة.

Al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, and the precise composition date of this verse is not independently clear.

The witness is thus chiefly useful for understanding usages recorded in early lexicography; it should not bear the main burden of establishing pre-Qur’anic use of the root.

What the poetic witnesses establish

In Imruʾ al-Qays’s poetry, رَصِيص describes eggs lying close together.

In one reading of the verse attributed to al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī, رَصْرَاصَة concerns closely joined stones beside water.

Together with the lexical usages concerning buildings and teeth, these witnesses connect the core meaning with compactness and close arrangement.

The degree of connection is not identical in every example.

Closely arranged eggs, closely set teeth, and stones in a firm building are three different situations.

The final definition should preserve this distinction rather than reduce every usage to a single construction image.

3. رَصَاص: lead and its relationship to the root

One familiar word in this family is رَصَاص, the Arabic name for the metal lead.

Early usage records this name with both fatḥah and kasrah on the rāʾ.

Lexical records describe الرَّصَاص as a metal used in certain construction work and in making or repairing vessels.

The related مُرَصَّص describes something coated with lead or secured using it.

In some usages, تَرْصِيص concerns coating a vessel or object with lead.

This branch matters because some early explanations of بُنْيَانٌ مَرْصُوصٌ mention lead.

Was رَصّ derived from the name of lead?

One lexical explanation, after discussing compactness and interconnection, proposes a relationship with رَصَاص and even treats the metal’s name as the origin of this field.

That analysis explains the relationship through the cohesion of lead’s parts.

This is significant in the history of Arabic lexicography, but should not be accepted as an established historical fact.

Proving that the metal’s name preceded رَصَّ and produced the verb, or the reverse, requires independent historical and linguistic evidence.

The presence of both usages in early records does not by itself establish their chronological order.

The expression بَيْضٌ رَصِيصٌ in poetry attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays also shows that compactness can be described without lead or any other metal.

The sense of compactness should therefore not be restricted to lead throughout the family.

Does Qur’anic مَرْصُوص mean “coated with lead”?

Early explanations of Al-Ṣaff 61:4 offer two familiar readings.

One describes مَرْصُوص as a building whose parts are closely and firmly joined.

The other likens it to a building secured with lead, or whose gaps between materials are filled with lead.

One early explanation emphasizes the connection between مَرْصُوص and lead.

Another emphasizes the close, firm joining of the building’s parts into a single stable whole.

These explanations are similar as construction images but are not lexically identical.

بُنْيَانٌ مَرْصُوصٌ in the Qur’an itself names no metal.

Rendering مَرْصُوص directly as “lead-coated” or “cast with lead” therefore confines the verse to a particular construction method.

The clearer, broader meaning is a building whose parts are tightly and firmly joined.

If such a building uses lead in a particular instance, this is one possible example rather than a fixed condition of the word’s meaning.

The Persian expression meaning “a lead wall” may also suggest a wall made of lead, although the verse makes no such statement.

“A firm, closely joined building” is therefore more precise for the main translation. If needed, the lead construction image can be discussed separately in a lexical explanation.

4. Other lexical usages: teeth, coverings, and compactness

أَرَصّ: closely set teeth

Arabic uses رَصَص for teeth set close together.

رَجُلٌ أَرَصُّ and امْرَأَةٌ رَصَّاءُ describe a person whose teeth are closely and compactly set.

The subject here is not a building or the arrangement of materials.

Teeth occur naturally in the mouth; رَصَص describes their closeness and arrangement.

This shows that the family can describe an existing compact arrangement without someone artificially placing the parts together.

Deliberate construction or bonding should therefore not be made a condition of every form in this family.

The transitive رَصَّ expresses bringing parts close and securing them, while descriptive forms can characterize an existing condition.

Bringing a veil close to the face

Lexical records use رَصَّصَتِ الْمَرْأَةُ of a woman who has fitted her veil so closely that only her eyes are visible.

Here the closeness and snug fitting of the covering to the face are prominent.

This usage does not establish a religious ruling or dress requirement.

The dictionary reports a linguistic and social practice; it does not rule whether a form of covering is obligatory or forbidden.

Nor does this mean that R Ṣ Ṣ originally means concealing the face.

Fitting a covering closely is one example within the family; concealing parts of the face is the result in this particular usage.

رَصْرَاصَة: closely grouped stones

Arabic records رَصَّاصَة and رَصْرَاصَة for stones lying close together around a spring or water place.

رَصْرَاصَة is also recorded for hard, firm ground.

These usages concern closeness, compactness, and firmness.

Hard ground and closely grouped stones do not make “stone” or “hardness of ground” the root’s meaning everywhere.

R Ṣ Ṣ also describes eggs in Imruʾ al-Qays’s poetry and teeth in Arabic usage.

Stone as a material and physical hardness are therefore not necessary components of every use.

Limited secondary usages

Some records also use رَصَّصَ for persistence and insistence in asking.

In another usage, رَصَّاصَة is figuratively applied to a miser.

Lexical explanations relate this expression to hardness and the miser’s closed hand.

These are limited secondary usages and should not be imposed upon the main definition.

بُنْيَانٌ مَرْصُوصٌ, for example, does not imply that the praised group must be harsh, miserly, or inflexible toward other people’s requests.

No such meaning appears in the verse.

5. كَأَنَّهُمْ بُنْيَانٌ مَرْصُوصٌ: the sole Qur’anic occurrence

The root’s only Qur’anic occurrence is in Al-Ṣaff 61:4:

إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُحِبُّ الَّذِينَ يُقَاتِلُونَ فِي سَبِيلِهِ صَفًّا كَأَنَّهُمْ بُنْيَانٌ مَرْصُوصٌ

inna allāha yuḥibbu alladhīna yuqātilūna fī sabīlihi ṣaffan ka-annahum bunyānun marṣūṣun

Al-Ṣaff 61:4

“The God indeed loves those who fight in His way in ranks, as though they were a firm, closely joined building.”

This verse concerns a group acting in ranks in a specific situation: fighting in The God’s way.

The Qur’an compares them to a building whose parts are firmly joined.

To understand مَرْصُوص precisely, each word’s contribution to this comparison must be considered separately.

يُقَاتِلُونَ فِي سَبِيلِهِ

يُقَاتِلُونَ comes from Q T L in Form III.

Here the verb concerns fighting and military confrontation.

فِي سَبِيلِهِ identifies the direction and purpose of that fighting.

The verse therefore does not praise every orderly fighting group; it concerns those whose fighting is described as being “in The God’s way.”

This qualification is fundamental to the verse’s construction.

An army may be highly organized in formation, coordination, and equipment while pursuing aggression, corruption, or oppression.

Military order alone does not establish that fighting takes place in The God’s way.

Al-Baqarah 2:190 says of fighting:

وَقَاتِلُوا فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ الَّذِينَ يُقَاتِلُونَكُمْ وَلَا تَعْتَدُوا

wa-qātilū fī sabīli allāhi alladhīna yuqātilūnakum wa-lā taʿtadū

Al-Baqarah 2:190

“And fight in The God’s way those who fight you, and do not transgress.”

This shows that “in The God’s way” should not be detached from the Qur’an’s other limits and criteria.

Establishing all the rulings and conditions of fighting requires a separate study.

The lexical point here is clear: مَرْصُوص concerns cohesion and firmness; the legitimacy of fighting does not follow from this word.

صَفًّا: the arrangement of those fighting

صَفًّا in this verse belongs to Ṣ F F.

It concerns arranging people or objects in a row or rank.

Grammatically, صَفًّا is accusative and, in familiar analyses, may be a verbal noun functioning as a circumstantial expression: the fighters stand in ranks.

Another analysis treats it as a cognate accusative of an understood verb such as يَصُفُّونَ أَنْفُسَهُمْ, “they arrange themselves in a rank.”

Both analyses convey the fighters’ formation in ranks.

Ṣaff and raṣṣ nevertheless belong to different roots.

Ṣaff emphasizes arrangement in a row and placement beside one another.

Raṣṣ emphasizes compactness and the firm joining of parts.

A group can therefore stand in one rank while obvious gaps and breaks remain between individuals.

A rank whose members stand firmly and act in coordination more closely resembles the image of a marṣūṣ building.

The pairing of صَفًّا and بُنْيَانٌ مَرْصُوصٌ thus highlights two related aspects: collective arrangement and firm cohesion.

كَأَنَّهُمْ: comparison, rather than turning people into a building

كَأَنَّهُمْ introduces a comparison.

The Qur’an compares these people to a building; it does not say that they are literally a building.

This distinction matters.

Human beings possess will, understanding, responsibility, and the ability to decide.

Stones and building materials do not possess these qualities.

Every property of a building should therefore not be transferred to human beings.

The comparison does not imply, for example, that fighters must lack will, the right to think, or moral responsibility as stones do.

The defensible point of comparison is the group’s cohesion and firmness in the situation being discussed.

The building image also does not mean that all individuals are identical in ability, role, and responsibility.

Different components can also form a firm whole in a building.

Cohesion therefore does not necessarily imply that every part is alike.

بُنْيَانٌ: a building or constructed whole

بُنْيَان belongs to B N Y and denotes a building or constructed structure.

In كَأَنَّهُمْ بُنْيَانٌ مَرْصُوصٌ, بُنْيَانٌ is the predicate of كَأَنَّ, while مَرْصُوصٌ is its adjective.

Both are nominative.

مَرْصُوصٌ therefore describes a quality of the building to which the fighters are compared.

بُنْيَان names the building itself.

مَرْصُوص specifies how its parts are connected and arranged.

These words should not be merged into an indistinct meaning.

“A firm building,” for example, conveys part of the sense, but “a firm, closely joined building” also makes the connection of its parts clearer.

Does مَرْصُوص mean there are no gaps?

Lexical explanations describe a marṣūṣ building through the close fitting of its parts and the absence of unsuitable gaps.

This explanation clearly relates to buildings and ranks.

The image should nevertheless not be turned into an absolute geometrical condition.

A marṣūṣ building has firmly connected parts; the word alone does not establish a zero measurement for every space between its particles or components.

In the human comparison, a firm and coordinated rank is likewise important.

The verse therefore does not imply that every physical space between two fighters, under every circumstance and in every formation, violates a Qur’anic command.

The quality of formation and collective action depends on actual conditions and the intended purpose.

The verse itself highlights the rank’s cohesion and firmness in the fighting described.

6. Cohesion in action and truthful speech in the context of Al-Ṣaff

The two preceding verses help establish the comparison’s place.

Al-Ṣaff 61:2 says:

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لِمَ تَقُولُونَ مَا لَا تَفْعَلُونَ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū lima taqūlūna mā lā tafʿalūna

Al-Ṣaff 61:2

“You who have believed, why do you say what you do not do?”

Al-Ṣaff 61:3 then says:

كَبُرَ مَقْتًا عِنْدَ اللَّهِ أَنْ تَقُولُوا مَا لَا تَفْعَلُونَ

kabura maqtan ʿinda allāhi an taqūlū mā lā tafʿalūna

Al-Ṣaff 61:3

“It is deeply hateful in the sight of The God that you say what you do not do.”

The verse containing بُنْيَانٌ مَرْصُوصٌ follows these two warnings.

This sequence matters.

Claiming solidarity and actually standing together

In Al-Ṣaff 61:2 and Al-Ṣaff 61:3, the Qur’an censures speech unaccompanied by action.

In Al-Ṣaff 61:4, it describes a group fighting in The God’s way in ranks, like a closely joined building.

Read together, these verses place sincerity in action and collective firmness within a shared context.

This does not make “truthful in speech” the lexical meaning of مَرْصُوص.

Truthfulness in speech and action comes from the preceding verses; the group’s firm cohesion comes from the fourth verse’s comparison.

These concepts can complement one another in context while retaining their separate lexical meanings.

Does cohesion mean blind obedience?

That conclusion does not follow from the comparison.

A marṣūṣ building has connected parts, but people are not stones or building materials without a will.

Elsewhere the Qur’an calls people to reason, examine, and avoid misguided following.

Comparing people in ranks to a closely joined building therefore does not establish a general rule that everyone must obey any commander or group without assessing right and wrong.

The verse itself also contains the qualification فِي سَبِيلِهِ.

Practical cohesion and coordination, without regard to an action’s direction and purpose, are therefore insufficient criteria for its rightness.

A group may be highly coordinated in carrying out a wrong action; its coordination does not make that action right.

A group’s firmness in carrying out an action must therefore be distinguished from the rightness of the action itself.

Raṣṣ conveys the former quality; the rightness of direction and purpose is understood from context and the Qur’an’s other criteria.

Avoiding disputes that destroy collective strength

Al-Anfāl 8:46 says:

وَأَطِيعُوا اللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُ وَلَا تَنَازَعُوا فَتَفْشَلُوا وَتَذْهَبَ رِيحُكُمْ وَاصْبِرُوا إِنَّ اللَّهَ مَعَ الصَّابِرِينَ

wa-aṭīʿū allāha wa-rasūlahu wa-lā tanāzaʿū fa-tafshalū wa-tadhhaba rīḥukum wa-iṣbirū inna allāha maʿa al-ṣābirīna

Al-Anfāl 8:46

“And obey The God and His Messenger, and do not dispute with one another, lest you falter and your strength depart. Remain steadfast; The God is indeed with those who remain steadfast.”

In the context of confronting an enemy, this verse warns against disputes that produce weakness and the loss of collective strength.

Conceptually, this warning relates to the image of بُنْيَانٌ مَرْصُوصٌ.

A building whose components become disconnected loses its former firmness.

A group engaged in a common task can likewise lose its ability to act when destructive conflict and fragmentation take hold.

R Ṣ Ṣ should nevertheless not be made to mean the absence of every difference of opinion.

Conflict that breaks down collective action is different from questioning, reasoned criticism, and examining decisions.

Cohesion in doing something right can coexist with differing views and individual accountability.

The closely joined building comparison should therefore not be used to silence criticism, conceal errors, or enforce obedience to a wrong decision.

Is this verse about prayer ranks?

Lexical records and Arabic usage also employ تَرَاصّ for people drawing close in prayer ranks.

Al-Ṣaff 61:4, however, explicitly uses يُقَاتِلُونَ.

Its direct subject is therefore formation in ranks while fighting in The God’s way.

The verse alone does not establish the number of prayer ranks, the required space between worshippers, or their precise standing posture.

Prayer ranks can illustrate the lexical meaning of tarāṣṣ as one Arabic usage, but this usage should not replace the verse’s explicit context.

بُنْيَانٌ مَرْصُوصٌ here thus images a group’s cohesion and firmness in fighting. It is not a complete, independent instruction covering every arrangement of ranks in worship or social life.

7. Comparison with related roots

R Ṣ Ṣ (raṣṣ) and Ṣ F F (ṣaff)

These two roots occur together in Al-Ṣaff 61:4.

Ṣaff concerns arranging people or things in a row.

Raṣṣ concerns compactness, closeness, and firmly joining parts.

Forming ranks and drawing tightly together can coincide, but are not lexically identical.

People can stand in an orderly rank while considerable spaces remain between them.

Objects can also lie closely packed without forming a single straight row, as with eggs arranged close together in one place.

مَرْصُوص should therefore not be translated merely as “in ranks.”

صَفًّا already expresses formation in ranks; مَرْصُوص adds another quality to the image.

Study of Ṣ F F

R Ṣ Ṣ (raṣṣ) and B N Y (bināʾ)

Bināʾ concerns building and producing a structure.

Raṣṣ concerns how closely and firmly its parts are arranged.

In Al-Ṣaff 61:4, these appear together in بُنْيَانٌ مَرْصُوصٌ.

The phrase distinguishes being a building from being marṣūṣ.

A structure can have been built while its components remain loose or poorly arranged.

A marṣūṣ building, by contrast, is firm in the connection of its parts.

Al-Tawbah 9:109 says:

أَفَمَنْ أَسَّسَ بُنْيَانَهُ عَلَىٰ تَقْوَىٰ مِنَ اللَّهِ وَرِضْوَانٍ خَيْرٌ أَمْ مَنْ أَسَّسَ بُنْيَانَهُ عَلَىٰ شَفَا جُرُفٍ هَارٍ

a-fa-man assasa bunyānahu ʿalā taqwā min allāhi wa-riḍwānin khayrun am man assasa bunyānahu ʿalā shafā jurufin hārin

Al-Tawbah 9:109

“Is one who founded his building upon mindfulness of The God and His approval better, or one who founded his building on the edge of a collapsing bank?”

The stability of the foundation and the soundness of what the construction rests upon are at issue here.

This image is conceptually close to the marṣūṣ building comparison, but the roots differ.

Raṣṣ concerns the connection of parts; تَأْسِيس and بُنْيَان concern founding and building.

The moral soundness of the foundation in Al-Tawbah also comes from تَقْوَىٰ مِنَ اللَّهِ وَرِضْوَانٍ, rather than from the meaning of “building” itself.

Study of B N Y

R Ṣ Ṣ (raṣṣ) and R D M (radm)

In its main usage, radm concerns closing a breach and filling an opening.

The account of Dhū al-Qarnayn says in Al-Kahf 18:95:

أَجْعَلْ بَيْنَكُمْ وَبَيْنَهُمْ رَدْمًا

ajʿal baynakum wa-baynahum radman

Al-Kahf 18:95

“So that I may build a barrier between you and them.”

That account also mentions pieces of iron and molten copper in the barrier’s construction.

In practice, radm may involve placing materials beside or upon one another, producing a firm structure.

Radm and raṣṣ nevertheless do not mean the same thing.

Radm emphasizes closing an opening and creating a barrier.

Raṣṣ emphasizes compactness and a firm connection between parts.

A radm can be constructed by closely joining materials, but this relationship does not make the roots identical.

Al-Ṣaff 61:4 does not concern blocking a passage between two mountains; it uses the image of a connected building to describe those fighting.

The features of Dhū al-Qarnayn’s barrier should therefore not be transferred into the meaning of بُنْيَانٌ مَرْصُوصٌ.

Study of R D M

R Ṣ Ṣ (raṣṣ) and R S S (rass)

Early lexical explanations connect making a building firm with both word families.

One usage of R S S concerns firmness and becoming established.

R Ṣ Ṣ also concerns compactness and firmly joining parts.

Their root structures nevertheless differ.

In R S S, the second and third consonants are sīn.

In R Ṣ Ṣ, the second and third consonants are ṣād.

Similarity in sound and the shared idea of firmness do not establish that they were originally one root.

In the Qur’an, R S S appears only in the designation “the people of al-Rass,” while R Ṣ Ṣ describes the closely joined building.

The two therefore require independent study.

Study of R S S

R Ṣ Ṣ (raṣṣ) and R B Ṭ (rabṭ)

Arabic rabṭ concerns binding something and making it firm.

Āl ʿImrān 3:200 says:

وَرَابِطُوا

wa-rābiṭū

Āl ʿImrān 3:200

“And remain steadfast in readiness and vigilance.”

Al-Kahf 18:14 also says:

وَرَبَطْنَا عَلَىٰ قُلُوبِهِمْ

wa-rabaṭnā ʿalā qulūbihim

Al-Kahf 18:14

“And We made their hearts firm.”

In these usages, rabṭ can concern firmness and being made steadfast.

Raṣṣ emphasizes parts lying closely and compactly together.

Rabṭ may involve a means of binding or establishing a connection, while raṣṣ does not necessarily require rope, a knot, or a particular fastening tool.

The roots can thus converge in the result of firmness without having identical methods or semantic fields.

Study of R B Ṭ

R Ṣ Ṣ (raṣṣ) and Ḍ M M (ḍamm)

Arabic ḍamm concerns bringing things close together and alongside one another.

Lexical explanations also use ḍamm to explain raṣṣ.

In its clear construction usage, however, raṣṣ places greater emphasis on the compactness and firmness of that arrangement.

Two things can be brought together without a firm connection forming between them.

When a building’s components are described as مَرْصُوص, their closeness and firm connection are prominent.

This difference should not become an absolute hierarchy in which ḍamm is always loose and raṣṣ always exceptionally strong.

Depending on usage, both can convey differing degrees of closeness and connection.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

Translations of this family should preserve compactness and firm joining without adding a specific material such as lead or an outcome such as indestructibility.

رَصَّ: pressed together; joined firmly; placed parts closely and securely together.

يَرُصُّ: places tightly and firmly together; joins parts to one another.

رَصّ: pressing together; tightly arranging; firm joining of parts.

رَصَص: closeness and compactness; of teeth, their close and orderly setting.

مَرْصُوص: firmly joined; closely and securely fitted together.

رَصِيص: close and compact; of a building, firmly joined; of eggs, closely and orderly arranged.

رَصَّصَ: made firm and compact; in another usage, coated or secured with lead.

تَرْصِيص: making firm and compact; in the metal-related usage, coating with lead.

تَرَاصَّ: drew close to one another; stood tightly and connectedly together.

تَرَاصّ: compactness and the drawing together of people or parts.

اِرْتَصَّ: became compact and close; its parts joined together.

رَصْرَصَ: made firm and stable; in another usage, remained established in place.

رَصْرَاصَة: closely joined stones beside a spring; in another usage, hard and firm ground.

أَرَصّ: someone with closely set teeth.

رَصَّاء: the feminine adjective describing closely set teeth.

رَصَاص: lead, the familiar metal. This word does not occur in the Qur’an.

Al-Ṣaff 61:4

إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُحِبُّ الَّذِينَ يُقَاتِلُونَ فِي سَبِيلِهِ صَفًّا كَأَنَّهُمْ بُنْيَانٌ مَرْصُوصٌ

inna allāha yuḥibbu alladhīna yuqātilūna fī sabīlihi ṣaffan ka-annahum bunyānun marṣūṣun

Al-Ṣaff 61:4

The Persian Forqan rendering, translated here:

“The God indeed loves those who fight in His way in ranks, as though they were a firm, closely joined building.”

This rendering accords with the root’s lexical meaning and the verse’s construction.

بُنْيَان is rendered as “building,” and مَرْصُوص as “firm, closely joined.”

This naturally conveys the parts’ compactness and connection without restricting the building to lead as a material.

For a brief equivalent of مَرْصُوص alone, “firmly joined” is suitable.

“Compact and firm” can also convey the closeness of the parts.

“Lead-coated” or “made of lead” is unsuitable as the main translation: the association with lead is one lexical and historical explanation, not a meaning present in every use of the family.

Expressions such as “invincible unity,” “complete obedience,” “uniformity of thought,” or “unquestioning submission” should likewise not enter the translation as the direct meaning of مَرْصُوص.

These meanings do not follow from the root itself.

9. Conclusion

The Arabic root R Ṣ Ṣ concerns bringing parts close together, pressing them together, and joining them firmly.

This sense appears in buildings, closely set teeth, compactly arranged eggs, closely joined stones, and dense ranks.

Lexical records use رَصَّ for arranging a building’s parts firmly together and تَرَاصَّ for people or objects drawing close to one another.

مَرْصُوص and رَصِيص describe things whose parts lie closely and firmly together.

Imruʾ al-Qays’s poetic witness shows رَصِيص describing closely arranged eggs in language attributed to the pre-Islamic period. This matters because the family’s meaning was not confined to buildings or joining with metal.

Lexical records also connect the metal name رَصَاص with this family. Some propose a historical relationship between compactness and lead, but this alone does not prove that every form arose from the metal’s name.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as مَرْصُوصٌ.

Al-Ṣaff 61:4 compares those fighting in The God’s way in ranks to a building whose parts are firmly joined.

The comparison highlights the group’s cohesion and firmness; it should not be understood as erasing individual personality or will, or requiring uniformity of thought.

A group’s organization and coordination also do not independently establish its purpose’s legitimacy. The verse qualifies the fighting with فِي سَبِيلِهِ; the soundness of its direction and action must be assessed through the Qur’an’s other criteria.

The preceding verses of Al-Ṣaff censure those who say what they do not do. The closely joined building comparison thus occurs in a context where truthful speech and action and collective firmness matter; مَرْصُوص itself, however, does not mean truthfulness.

Comparisons with related roots must also preserve their distinctions.

Ṣaff concerns arrangement in a row, bināʾ building, radm closing a breach, rabṭ establishing a bond, and raṣṣ compactness and firmly joining parts.

These concepts can occur together in one situation, but should not be reduced to one meaning without evidence.

مَرْصُوص also does not mean indestructible, made of lead, or possessed of unlimited power. These qualities are not established by the word itself.

Final definition: R Ṣ Ṣ concerns bringing the parts of something close together and joining them firmly into a compact, stable whole. مَرْصُوص describes something whose parts fit closely and securely together. In the Qur’an, it appears in the comparison of those fighting in ranks in The God’s way to a firm, closely joined building. The material used, the structure’s durability, the purpose of collective action, and its moral value do not follow from the root alone; they must be understood from sentence structure and the verses’ context.
