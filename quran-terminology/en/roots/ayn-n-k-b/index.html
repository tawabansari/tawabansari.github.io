1. Word Meaning and Structure

Arabic ʿankabūt (عَنْكَبُوت) names the familiar animal called a spider. It is known for spinning silk, making webs, and using silk for hunting, movement, protection, and other needs.

The Qur’anic word occurs only in Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:41. There the spider’s house illustrates the unreliability of depending on protectors adopted apart from The God.

Lisān al-ʿArab defines it as duwaybbatun tansuju fī al-hawāʾi wa-ʿalā raʾsi al-biʾri nasjan raqīqan muhalhalan (دُوَيْبَّةٌ تَنْسُجُ فِي الْهَوَاءِ وَعَلَى رَأْسِ الْبِئْرِ نَسْجًا رَقِيقًا مُهَلْهَلًا): a small creature weaving a delicate, open fabric in the air or over a well’s mouth.

Lisān al-ʿArab: the animal name and woven web

The definition highlights the animal itself and its ability to spin a web.

These remain distinct: ʿankabūt names the animal, while bayt al-ʿankabūt identifies its house or constructed dwelling.

Biologically, spiders are arachnids and arthropods, rather than insects. Their bodies normally have two main regions and four pairs of legs.

Spiders vary greatly in size, color, habitat, hunting behavior, and silk use. Some build broad prey-catching webs; others inhabit burrows or crevices; others pursue prey without building capture webs.

Australian Museum: arachnids, body structure, and hunting diversity

Silk spinning is therefore important without every spider necessarily making a circular, netlike house.

The Qur’anic image concerns a spider taking a house for itself, with that house presented as supremely frail among houses.

Qur’anic morphology indexes ʿ N K B as quadriliteral. Lexicographers nevertheless disagree about whether every letter was radical in the word’s historical formation.

Three questions should remain distinct: the familiar animal name, its Arabic morphology, and possible historical origins.

The Qur’anic animal name is clear; its derivational history is less certain.

2. Forms and Disagreement about the Root

ʿAnkabūt and Plural Forms

The common, Qur’anic form is ʿankabūt.

Arabic records the following forms:

ʿAnkabūt (عَنْكَبُوت): the spider name used in the Qur’an.

ʿAnākib (عَنَاكِب): a common broken plural.

ʿAnākīb (عَنَاكِيب): another plural recorded by Al-Liḥyānī and other lexicographers.

ʿAnkabūtāt (عَنْكَبُوتَات): a plural formed with -āt.

ʿAnkabāʾ (عَنْكَبَاء): a form also transmitted by Sībawayh.

ʿAnkabāh (عَنْكَبَاة) and ʿankabūh (عَنْكَبُوه): other recorded names for the same animal.

ʿAkanbāh (عَكَنْبَاة): a form attributed to Yemeni usage, with letters differently ordered from the familiar form.

ʿUnaykib (عُنَيْكِب) and ʿunaykīb (عُنَيْكِيب): diminutives recorded in morphological sources.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: plurals and diminutives

The forms show variation in pronunciation and morphology within the Arabic animal name.

None of these alternative or plural forms occurs in the Qur’an; its form is al-ʿankabūt.

Is the N Radical?

Early sources disagree about the word’s formation.

One analysis treats ʿ N K B as radical and examines the word accordingly.

Another treats n as additional, relating the name to triliteral ʿ K B.

Al-Jawharī places ʿankabūt under ʿ K B. Some lexicographers interpret that arrangement as indicating an additional n.

Tāj al-ʿArūs instead cites Sībawayh’s rule against treating a second-position n as additional without evidence. Analysis of the plural ʿanākib also bears on the disagreement.

Tāj al-ʿArūs discusses the disagreement in detail.

One principle is that n in second position should not be assumed additional without sufficient evidence.

Tāj al-ʿArūs: second-position n and differing morphological analyses

A radical n supports quadriliteral ʿ N K B; an additional n requires a separate case for a relationship with ʿ K B.

The disagreement matters for word formation and history, but does not change the animal name’s meaning.

The final t is also debated. Forms such as ʿankabāʾ led some grammarians to regard it as additional.

Final t does not necessarily function simply as a marker of female sex in every occurrence. Arabic animal names do not follow one simple rule equating word endings and biological sex.

A Semantic Connection with ʿ K B?

ʿ K B has independent senses involving gathering, accumulation, thickness, and parts coming close together.

Ibn Fāris, for example, records ʿukūb for camels crowding together at a watering place.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: gathering and crowding in ʿ K B

Other uses concern thickness or firmness in a body part.

The spider’s interconnected web could be imagined as resembling gathering and joining parts.

The resemblance does not prove derivation of the name from ʿ K B.

Derivation from thickness or gathering requires evidence for that connection in actual language history.

A spider’s connected threads do not establish that its name arose from joining threads.

With an independent quadriliteral analysis, there is no need to invent a derivation from a hypothetical image of weaving or knotting.

Qur’anic usage retains the established animal name; disputed derivation remains a separate question.

3. Grammatical Gender and Early Arabic Evidence

Is ʿAnkabūt Feminine?

Early Arabic generally uses ʿankabūt as grammatically feminine.

Al-Farrāʾ expressly says feminine usage is more common, while some Arabs also use it as masculine.

Al-Mubarrad likewise records the difference.

The word therefore did not have wholly uniform grammatical gender, although feminine use predominated.

This matters for ittakhadhat in Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:41.

Ittakhadhat is feminine singular, agreeing with al-ʿankabūt.

Feminine agreement alone does not prove the verse necessarily singles out a biologically female spider.

Grammatical gender and biological sex do not invariably coincide in Arabic.

An animal name can be grammatically feminine while referring generically to its kind.

A specifically female referent therefore requires evidence beyond the verb’s feminine form.

ʿAnkab and ʿAnkabah

Ibn al-Aʿrābī reports ʿankab for the male spider and ʿankabah for the female.

Another account treats ʿankab as a collective generic name and ʿankabah as its unit.

Lisān al-ʿArab: grammatical gender, ʿankab, and ʿankabah

Even closely related animal-name forms are consequently not analyzed uniformly across sources.

One lexical division does not establish that every unqualified ʿankabūt must identify only one biological sex.

Definite al-ʿankabūt in the verse can refer to the familiar kind of animal.

Feminine agreement fits the word’s predominant grammatical use.

A Masculine-Usage Witness

In Lisān al-ʿArab, Al-Farrāʾ quotes this verse as evidence of masculine usage:

عَلَى هَطَّالِهِمْ مِنْهُمْ بُيُوتٌ
كَأَنَّ الْعَنْكَبُوتَ هُوَ ابْتَنَاهَا

ʿalā haṭṭālihim minhum buyūtun / ka-anna al-ʿankabūta huwa ibtanāhā

Explanatory rendering: “On their Haṭṭāl stand houses of theirs, as though the spider had built them.” The lexical commentary identifies Haṭṭāl as a mountain.

Lisān al-ʿArab: masculine agreement and Haṭṭāl as a mountain name

Masculine huwa provides Al-Farrāʾ’s evidence that some Arabic usage allows masculine agreement.

The witness thus records masculine usage too.

The examined quotation does not establish the poet or precise date. Without independent evidence it should not be assigned to a particular poet or certainly dated before Islam.

Its value is attesting a linguistic use and supporting the lexicographers’ report of possible masculine agreement.

A Witness from Abū al-Najm

Lisān al-ʿArab also quotes Abū al-Najm:

مِمَّا يُسَدِّي الْعَنْكَبُوتُ إِذْ خَلَا

mimmā yusaddī al-ʿankabūtu idh khalā

Explanatory rendering: “From what the spider weaves when the place is empty.” Yusaddī concerns spinning threads; Abū Ḥātim takes khalā as referring to the place being empty.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Abū al-Najm’s witness and Abū Ḥātim on khalā

The use connects the animal name with its familiar silk-spinning activity.

It does not make ʿankabūt itself mean “weaver.”

The noun names the animal; the accompanying verb describes its activity.

Abū al-Najm was an Islamic-era poet; his work should not be presented as securely pre-Qur’anic Arabic evidence.

Yemeni ʿAkanbāh

Lexicons record ʿakanbāh as a Yemeni form of the spider name.

A rajaz witness also mentions baytu ʿakanbātin, the spider’s house.

This records differing word structures in Arabic dialect usage.

Reordered letters in one dialect form do not securely establish it as the historical origin of every other form.

Nor does the dialect form create a different meaning for the animal.

Together, the witnesses show neighboring names and morphological forms for the spider in early Arabic, with its house or web also appearing in comparisons.

4. Qur’anic Use in Al-ʿAnkabūt

ʿ N K B occurs twice in the Qur’an, both in one verse as al-ʿankabūti.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: two occurrences in Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:41

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:41

مَثَلُ الَّذِينَ اتَّخَذُوا مِن دُونِ اللَّهِ أَوْلِيَاءَ كَمَثَلِ الْعَنْكَبُوتِ اتَّخَذَتْ بَيْتًا وَإِنَّ أَوْهَنَ الْبُيُوتِ لَبَيْتُ الْعَنْكَبُوتِ لَوْ كَانُوا يَعْلَمُونَ

mathalu alladhīna ittakhadhū min dūni allāhi awliyāʾa ka-mathali al-ʿankabūti ittakhadhat baytan wa-inna awhana al-buyūti la-baytu al-ʿankabūti law kānū yaʿlamūna

Forqan’s existing Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“The likeness of those who have taken protectors instead of The God is like the spider that took a house; and the frailest of houses is the spider’s house, if they knew.”

Two Occurrences in One Verse

The first al-ʿankabūti occurs in ka-mathali al-ʿankabūti.

Ka- establishes the comparison, introducing the spider as the object with which a human situation is compared.

The second occurs in la-baytu al-ʿankabūti.

It is genitive in an annexation linking the house to the spider.

Both uses mean the same familiar animal.

Their sentence roles nevertheless differ.

The first introduces the animal in the likeness; the second identifies its house as the subject of the judgment about frailty.

The distinction matters: the verse does not call the spider itself necessarily the weakest animal; it concerns bayt al-ʿankabūt.

Ittakhadhat Baytan: The House the Spider Took

In ittakhadhat baytan, ittakhadhat belongs to ʾ KH DH and the iftiʿāl form.

It can express taking, choosing, or providing something for oneself.

Here the spider has provided itself with a house.

The verse does not expressly use nasajat, “wove.” Familiar Arabic usage and spider behavior nevertheless make a spun web one recognizable way of providing such a dwelling.

Explanation can accordingly discuss a woven spider house without erasing taking and providing from a direct rendering of ittakhadhat.

Its connection with the verse’s opening is more important.

People first appear taking awliyāʾ instead of The God.

The spider then takes a bayt.

Repeated ittakhadhū and ittakhadhat link the comparison’s two sides.

In each, a being chooses or provides something for itself.

The chosen thing’s function and reliability matter.

On one side, people depend on protectors taken instead of The God; on the other, the spider provides a house presented as a model of frailty.

The comparison therefore concerns more than thread material: reliance on something unable to fulfill the expected role.

Awhana al-Buyūt: The House’s Frailty

Awhana belongs to W H N and expresses a superlative of weakness or frailty here.

Al-buyūt is the plural of bayt.

Awhana al-buyūt thus presents a house as supremely frail compared with other houses.

Grammatically, awhana is the noun governed by inna; la-baytu al-ʿankabūti supplies its predicate with an emphatic lām.

The structure emphasizes the judgment.

Lexical frailty should nevertheless remain distinct from all scientific measures of strength.

Arabic wahn concerns weakness or limited capacity, which can appear in an object’s structure, resistance, or function.

Awhana al-buyūt concerns a house, ordinarily associated with covering, shelter, stability, and protection.

The image compares the delicate web-house with substantial shelters: it does not provide comprehensive covering and protection like a house’s walls and roof.

That fits the comparison with reliance on powerless protectors.

Al-Ṭabarī: reliance on protectors and the house comparison

It does not establish that every silk strand is weaker than every other natural material in every test.

The verse supplies no such conclusion.

Strong Silk and a Frail House

Biological research shows that some spider silks have impressive tensile strength relative to their weight.

Silk can combine considerable strength and flexibility, catching prey, supporting egg sacs, or providing a safe route for movement.

“The spider’s house is frail” should consequently not be equated with “spider silk has no appreciable strength.”

These are distinct questions.

A mechanically effective material can form a structure with limitations for a particular kind of shelter or protection.

A network of thin threads, for example, can capture insects effectively without excluding wind, rain, or larger hazards like a substantial building’s roof and walls.

Its natural effectiveness therefore need not conflict with limited performance as a substantial shelter.

Nor should the distinction become a claim that silk provides no protection at all.

Some spiders use silk to make shelters, protect eggs, or hide from predators. These protective functions belong in a biological assessment.

Australian Museum: silk types, strength, and protective functions

The image’s frailty should not deny all the web’s actual functions.

The verse chiefly compares reliability; it does not supply a laboratory ranking of natural materials.

Does the Verse Give Every Spider the Same House?

Al-ʿankabūt can function generically, presenting a familiar kind of animal.

Generic usage does not make the verse a uniform, detailed account of every spider species’ structure.

Some spiders build orb webs, others irregular networks, funnel structures, or silken retreats; others hunt without a capture web.

Science should recognize that diversity.

Different species do not themselves invalidate the Qur’anic comparison.

The image uses a familiar spider house or web to illustrate the limits of relying on it as a substantial shelter.

It should not yield a scientific claim that every spider dwelling has one identical form.

Nor does preserving the comparison require denying species diversity.

Does Feminine Ittakhadhat Make a Biological Discovery?

Some modern accounts claim the feminine verb identifies a fact that only female spiders build a house or web.

Arabic grammar does not provide sufficient support for that inference.

As already noted, early Arabic generally treats ʿankabūt as grammatically feminine.

Feminine agreement therefore follows an ordinary grammatical pattern.

Silk-related behavior also varies by species. In some, females build capture webs while mature males no longer do so; males can nevertheless produce and use silk for movement, reproduction, and other needs.

Australian Museum: an example of silk web production by males

One cannot claim that all male spiders are incapable of spinning any silk.

The verse should not be interpreted through a generalized biological claim about spider sex.

Its direct meaning is clear: the spider took a house for itself.

Does Frailty Mean the Spider’s Family Relationships?

Some modern interpretations connect the house’s frailty with mating behavior, females consuming males, or relationships among spider family members.

Such behavior is observed in some species.

It is not uniform across species and conditions.

Burke Museum: limits on generalizing mate cannibalism

More importantly, the verse does not discuss spider family relationships.

It concerns baytan and awhana al-buyūt within a comparison about taking awliyāʾ.

No direct textual clue establishes family relationships as the principal meaning of frailty here.

Those readings may be proposed as reflections on some natural behavior, but should not become the lexical meaning of ʿankabūt or the verse’s certain meaning.

5. The Comparison in the Surah’s Context

Protectors Taken Instead of The God

Before verse 41, the Qur’an describes the fate of groups resisting signs and messengers.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:38 mentions ʿĀd and Thamūd; Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:39 names Qārūn, Pharaoh, and Hāmān; Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:40 describes the consequences of these groups’ actions.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:41 then introduces the spider comparison.

It therefore stands within a discussion of power, reliance, human behavior, and its outcome.

Awliyāʾ is the plural of walī, from W L Y.

The family has a broad Qur’anic field involving nearness, continuity, support, companionship, and undertaking responsibility.

Ittakhadhū min dūni Allāhi awliyāʾ concerns more than simply having friends or social relationships.

Min dūni Allāh places these adopted ties and supports in relation to reliance on The God.

The spider image highlights the inadequacy of that support.

People may consider someone or something powerful, only to find it unable to provide actual protection when needed.

The spider’s house evokes that situation: a dwelling has been provided without the stability expected of a reliable shelter.

The main comparison thus concerns the gap between imagining one has support and that support’s actual capacity.

Does the Verse Call Every Human Relationship Frail?

No.

Elsewhere the Qur’an also describes believers’ awliyāʾ, mutual support, and proper social ties.

The spider image alone consequently does not make all human reliance, friendship, cooperation, or social support wrong.

The verse concerns taking protectors min dūni Allāh.

That qualification must remain in the analysis.

Walī likewise should not be restricted to one kind of protection or authority throughout the Qur’an.

Here reliance is placed in opposition to The God and ultimately cannot supply the expected backing.

The meaning follows the comparison and its context, rather than the animal name alone.

Study of W L Y

Law Kānū Yaʿlamūn: Knowing the Support’s Reality

The verse closes with law kānū yaʿlamūna.

Knowing the matter’s reality is therefore important to understanding the comparison.

People may appear to have secured support while misjudging its real capacity.

The spider house pictures that distance between appearance and needed stability.

It does not establish that the spider is unaware of its house’s weakness or that the animal’s ignorance is being morally condemned.

The people introduced at the opening are the principal referents of kānū.

The closing concerns human understanding and judgment, rather than the animal’s moral responsibility.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:42 continues discussing what is called upon apart from The God.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:43 then states that these comparisons are presented for people and that understanding them calls for reasoning and knowledge.

The continuation reinforces reflection on genuine reliance and support.

Distinguishing the Comparison from a Judgment on Nature

The natural spider has a complex body and behavior.

Silk production, web building, hunting, and environmental adaptation are important aspects of its life.

A comparison can nevertheless highlight just one feature among an organism’s many characteristics.

Using the spider’s house to describe unreliable support does not necessarily judge every ability or behavior of the animal.

House frailty therefore should not imply that spiders are useless or ecologically worthless.

Their complex abilities should likewise not reverse the comparison into the opposite of what the verse states.

Two levels remain distinct: the animal’s natural functions, and its house as an illustration of reliability.

6. Differences from Related Words and Concepts

ʿAnkabūt and Naml

Arabic naml names ants.

Ants and spiders are both arthropods, with different bodies and ways of life.

Ants are insects, normally with six legs; spiders are arachnids with eight.

The Qur’an names ants in Al-Naml 27:18 and the spider in Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:41.

Their presence in a Qur’anic narrative or image does not make their features or roles identical.

ʿAnkabūt and Nasj

Nasj concerns weaving or joining threads.

Spiders are known for spinning webs, a feature present in their early lexical definitions.

ʿAnkabūt nevertheless does not mean the act of weaving.

Nasj names an activity; ʿankabūt names an animal that can perform it.

Not every woven thing is a spider web, and not everything spun becomes a spider’s house.

ʿAnkabūt and Bayt

Bayt can name a dwelling or shelter.

The Qur’an uses it for human houses, The God’s House, and other places of dwelling.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:41 assigns it to a structure the spider provides for itself.

Bayt is consequently broader; bayt al-ʿankabūt is one instance.

The verse’s frailty concerns that instance, rather than making weakness the meaning of bayt in every use.

Study of B Y T

ʿAnkabūt and Wahn

Wahn concerns weakness or frailty.

Its Qur’anic family describes weakness in different settings, including bodily weakness and difficult human conditions.

ʿAnkabūt and wahn nevertheless belong to independent roots.

One names the animal; the other weakness or limited strength.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:41 brings them together through the house’s description.

Frailty should therefore not be inserted into the animal name’s lexical meaning.

ʿAnkabūt and ʿ K B

As the structural discussion notes, some lexicographers relate the animal name to ʿ K B.

That entry has independent uses, including gathering and certain bodily features.

They should not enter the Qur’anic spider definition without evidence.

ʿAnkabūt and N K B

Arabic N K B concerns turning aside, deviation, and, in some uses, misfortune.

It shares letters with part of ʿ N K B.

Three shared letters alone do not prove derivation of the spider name from deviation or misfortune.

Nor does the verse provide a clue for deriving it from ill omen or calamity.

Such a connection therefore should not govern the root definition.

7. Conclusion and Translation Proposals

Arabic ʿankabūt names the familiar animal associated with silk spinning and web construction.

Lexicons record forms including ʿankab, ʿankabah, and ʿankabāʾ, with plurals such as ʿanākib and ʿanākīb.

Historical formation, especially the status of n, remains disputed. Some connect it with ʿ K B; others defend quadriliteral ʿ N K B.

That does not make the animal name unclear.

Early Arabic generally treats it as feminine, while also recording masculine usage.

Ittakhadhat alone therefore does not prove an exclusively female biological referent.

The root occurs twice in one verse, Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:41.

The first occurrence introduces the animal in the comparison; the second identifies its house.

The image concerns those adopting protectors instead of The God and depending on support unable to provide the protection they need.

The spider house illustrates frailty and failure to provide the stability expected of a shelter.

The verse does not make silk the weakest natural material under every mechanical criterion. Biological research shows some strands to be strong and effective.

Nor can house frailty be restricted without evidence to family relationships or mating behavior.

The verse concerns a house and its reliability within a comparison.

Brief root meaning: The spider: an arachnid capable of producing silk and spinning webs.

Meanings of the Qur’anic forms:

Al-ʿankabūti — Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:41, first occurrence: The spider that takes a house for itself in the comparison.

Al-ʿankabūti — Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:41, second occurrence: The same animal in bayt al-ʿankabūt, the spider’s house.

Proposed word rendering: “Spider.”

“Spider” fits both occurrences, without adding “female,” “weak,” “solitary,” or “deceived” to the animal name.

Forqan’s existing Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“The likeness of those who have taken protectors instead of The God is like the spider that took a house; and the frailest of houses is the spider’s house, if they knew.”

The rendering preserves the direct animal name and the comparison’s main structure.

The central conclusion is that ʿankabūt does not itself mean frailty: it names an animal whose house illustrates frail support and shelter in a Qur’anic comparison.

Animal name, web structure and natural function, and the comparison’s ethical and religious meaning should remain distinct.

The Qur’an uses a familiar natural creature to clarify human reliance: providing a house or choosing support does not itself secure a substantial, reliable refuge.

That meaning comes from the whole comparison; the direct meaning of ʿankabūt remains the animal name.
