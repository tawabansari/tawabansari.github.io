Root ه و ر — Structural instability and collapse

Qur’anic and lexical forms

هَارٍ
(hārin)
Unstable and crumbling

فَانْهَارَ
(fa-inhāra)
Then it collapsed

From a cracking bank and a collapsing building to an undertaking built on an unstable foundation

The Arabic root ه و ر concerns weakening, cracking, and structural collapse. It is particularly used of banks, well walls, precipices, and buildings, where soil or materials lose cohesion and strength and may fall apart.

The root occurs twice in one Qur’anic verse: hārin describes an unstable, crumbling bank, and fa-inhāra expresses its collapse.

The distinction matters. A structure may still stand while internally weakened and close to collapse. Its actual collapse is a further event.

The Qur’an uses this distinction vividly: a building on firm foundations does not share the outcome of one built on a crumbling edge.

1. The root in early Arabic

Classical hāra al-jurufu describes a bank losing strength through instability, erosion, or cracking.

Hāra al-bināʾu describes a building weakening or collapsing.

Inhāra means “collapsed”; material uses of tahawwara likewise concern collapse, falling, and disintegration.

The central issue in this branch is lost structural strength and cohesion, so that parts or the whole fall.

Cracking and collapsing

Early lexical accounts distinguish two conditions.

A bank cracked behind its edge but still standing could be described as hārin or hāʾir.

When that part detaches and falls, inhāra describes the event.

Riverbanks and precipices illustrate the distinction.

Water may gradually wash away the soil beneath a bank. From above it still looks continuous and capable of supporting weight, although its underside has weakened. Pressure or further erosion then causes collapse.

First the structure is unstable and liable to collapse; afterward the fall has occurred.

Ordinary Arabic sometimes also uses hār and hāʾir for something already collapsed. The distinction is useful and recorded, rather than an absolute rule without overlap.

An example from pre-Qur’anic poetry

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Bishr ibn Abī Khāzim describes a powerful horse:

«بِكُلِّ قَرَارَةٍ مِنْ حَيْثُ جَالَتْ
رُكَيَّةُ سُنْبُكٍ فِيهَا انْهِيَارُ»

Bi-kulli qarāratin min ḥaythu jālat / rukayyatu sunbukin fīhā inhiyāru.

“In every soft hollow where it moves, its hoof makes a little well whose sides crumble.”

The poet depicts the force of the horse’s steps.

Qarārah denotes a depression or softer ground.

Rukayyah is a small well or a well-like hole.

Sunbuk denotes the front of a horse’s hoof.

Inhiyār expresses the soil’s collapse.

The hoof sinks so deeply into soft ground that it appears to make a little well with crumbling sides.

Here inhiyār is not grief, moral failure, or human destruction. It is a material event: soil breaking apart and falling.

The verse appears in the collection attributed to Bishr ibn Abī Khāzim, but its attribution is disputed. One early transmission assigns it and the preceding verse to a man of Banū Tamīm. Versions also differ between ḥārat and jālat.

It is therefore evidence from the early Arabic poetic tradition, but neither its certain attribution to Bishr nor its exact date should be assumed.

Despite that limitation, inhiyār clearly concerns crumbling soil and the loss of strength at a hollow’s edge.

Human-built structures

Classical hawwara al-bināʾa is recorded for making a building collapse or demolishing it.

Tahawwara al-bināʾu describes the building itself collapsing.

The forms approach an event from different directions: one presents the destructive agent, the other the collapsing structure.

Rajulun hārin also describes someone lacking firmness in their affairs or decisions.

Physical structural strength is thus compared to human resolve and capacity: an unsteady decision-maker resembles a weak building.

This human application does not establish weakness of will as the root’s original meaning.

Material uses concerning ground, wells, and buildings more directly support the Qur’anic meaning.

2. Other branches

Tahawwur: acting without weighing danger

Tahawwur also denotes rash, unconsidered action.

A mutahawwir enters a difficult situation without properly weighing its dangers and consequences.

The connection can be illustrated through falling: an imprudent person resembles someone who rushes toward a crumbling precipice without checking the ground.

This illustration does not establish the historical date of the behavioral meaning.

Recklessness and inhāra are not identical: one concerns conduct and decision-making, the other falling and collapse.

Tahawwur as a behavioral noun does not occur in the Qur’an.

Night and winter passing

Arabic records tahawwara al-laylu when much of the night has passed and its darkness is nearing an end.

Tahawwara al-shitāʾu similarly concerns winter after its intensity and main portion have passed.

No wall or bank physically falls here; the intensity or major part of a previous condition passes away.

This can be understood as an extension of collapse or declining strength and intensity, but dating the development requires independent historical evidence.

Supposition and estimation

Another classical branch concerns supposition, estimation, and attributing something to a person.

Hurtuhu bi-kadhā is recorded in some uses for supposing or attributing something to someone.

In poetry attributed to Abū Mālik ibn Nuwayrah, ahūruhu concerns estimating the provisions or needs sufficient for a horse.

This branch has no clear connection with structural collapse.

One should not force all derivatives together by claiming that every supposition or estimate developed from a collapsing building.

Lexicons also record hawr for a broad body of standing water or marsh. Without independent evidence, this does not enlarge the Qur’anic definition of hār and inhāra.

The Qur’an uses the branch concerning instability and structural collapse; this remains the study’s focus.

3. Qur’anic forms

ه و ر occurs twice, only in al-Tawbah 9:109.

The forms are:

  • هَارٍ: describing something unstable, cracked, and liable to collapse.
  • فَانْهَارَ: a past-tense Form VII verb prefixed with fa-, “then it collapsed.”

Arabic also has هَارَ, يَهُورُ, هَوْر, هَائِر, هَوَّرَ, تَهَوَّرَ, اِنْهِيَار, and مُتَهَوِّر, but these exact forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

The formation of hārin

Hārin in al-Tawbah 9:109 modifies jurufin.

Its morphology has long been debated.

Some linguists treat hār as a modified hāʾir, explaining it through rearrangement or deletion within the word.

Grammatical accounts discuss both ه و ر and related uses of ه ي ر.

Some derive hār through changes to an active participle; others propose different morphological explanations.

This does not make its contextual meaning unknown: the analyses understand an unstable, crumbling object.

The debate concerns the history and formation of the word, not its basic meaning in the Qur’anic phrase.

The formation of fa-inhāra

Inhāra is the past tense of Form VII in the ه و ر family.

The initial fa- connects the event to the preceding condition.

The form presents the object itself collapsing.

For example, inhāra al-jurufu means that the bank collapsed.

Hawwara al-jurufa, by contrast, can present a person or agent making it collapse.

Form VII does not imply the absence of an external cause. Erosion, pressure, rainfall, or other factors may prepare the collapse.

Inhāra expresses the event; its physical cause requires separate investigation.

Inhāra is also distinct from anhār, “rivers.”

اِنْهَارَ is a verb from ه و ر.

أَنْهَار is the plural of nahr, from ن ه ر.

Their similar spelling must not cause their roots to be confused.

4. Qur’anic usage: building on a crumbling bank

Al-Tawbah 9:109

«أَفَمَنْ أَسَّسَ بُنْيَانَهُ عَلَىٰ تَقْوَىٰ مِنَ اللَّهِ وَرِضْوَانٍ خَيْرٌ أَمْ مَنْ أَسَّسَ بُنْيَانَهُ عَلَىٰ شَفَا جُرُفٍ هَارٍ فَانْهَارَ بِهِ فِي نَارِ جَهَنَّمَ وَاللَّهُ لَا يَهْدِي الْقَوْمَ الظَّالِمِينَ»

A-fa-man assasa bunyānahu ʿalā taqwā mina Allāhi wa-riḍwānin khayrun am man assasa bunyānahu ʿalā shafā jurufin hārin fa-inhāra bihi fī nāri jahannama wa-Allāhu lā yahdī al-qawma al-ẓālimīn.

“Is one who founded his building on mindfulness of The God and His approval better, or one who founded his building on the edge of an unstable, crumbling bank, which collapsed with him into the fire of Hell? The God does not guide the wrongdoing people.”

The verse contrasts two kinds of construction.

One is founded on taqwa and God’s approval.

The other rests on a crumbling edge that carries its building down as it falls.

Understanding the second image requires distinguishing several words.

Assasa bunyānahu: laying a foundation

Assasa, from أ س س, concerns founding and laying foundations.

Bunyān denotes a building or constructed structure.

The issue is not merely its appearance but the foundation supporting it.

A building may look beautiful, large, and sturdy, yet outward strength cannot ensure survival on unstable ground.

This image prepares the comparison of the two structures.

Shafā: an edge

Shafā here means an edge or brink.

It can denote the edge of a precipice, a well’s rim, or the last stretch of ground before a hollow.

Shafā itself does not mean falling or collapse; it identifies the edge.

The verse combines it with juruf.

Juruf: an eroded bank

Juruf belongs to ج ر ف.

Arabic uses it for a bank whose soil has been partly removed by water or another agent, leaving an eroded edge.

A river can wash away the soil and undercut a bank.

From above the bank may still seem firm despite inadequate support beneath it.

Building on such an edge can be dangerous.

Juruf identifies the bank; hār specifies its crumbling condition.

Hārin: unstable and close to collapse

Hārin modifies jurufin in this verse.

The bank is not just any bank: it has lost strength and become liable to collapse.

It may still appear to stand, although cracks and weakness show inadequate capacity to bear pressure.

This distinction is central to the image.

On firm rock, a building’s load passes into ground capable of supporting it.

On a crumbling earthen edge, strong building materials cannot compensate for weak ground beneath.

The danger lies in the building’s location and foundation.

Hār alone does not lexically contain all these explanations of soil, pressure, engineering, and erosion. They are natural illustrations of the image.

The word itself denotes instability and liability to collapse.

Fa-inhāra bihi: collapse occurring

The description hār is followed by fa-inhāra.

The sequence distinguishes two conditions.

First, the bank is unstable and liable to collapse.

Then it falls.

Fa-inhāra no longer presents mere possibility; it expresses actual collapse.

Bihi refers to someone or something carried down with it.

Grammatically, the building and its owner are relevant possible referents. Early explanations also describe the bank collapsing with the building.

The overall picture is clear: a structure on an unstable foundation falls when that foundation collapses.

This does not establish that the preposition bi- always means accompaniment in a fall. Its function comes from this construction and context.

Fī nāri jahannam: the image’s outcome

The verse does not merely depict an ordinary building falling into a hollow. Its collapse ends in Hell’s fire.

The material image serves a moral and eschatological outcome.

It portrays the fate of an undertaking built on a false foundation.

It does not establish that the actual building necessarily suffered an engineering collapse or that a geographical precipice led into Hell.

The Qur’an uses a recognizable natural and architectural situation to distinguish stable and unstable foundations.

The lexical sense remains instability and collapse; its application to human actions and their outcome comes from the verse’s analogy.

5. The preceding and following verses

Al-Tawbah 9:107 describes people who established a mosque to cause harm, promote kufr, divide believers, and provide a base for opposition to God and His Messenger.

Those people claimed to intend only good.

Al-Tawbah 9:108 forbids Muhammad to stand there and identifies a mosque founded on taqwa from the beginning as more worthy.

Verse 109 then compares the foundations through a vivid image.

Appearance and foundation

Both groups may possess buildings.

Both may present them with a religious appearance.

The verses do not equate similar appearances with similar purposes and foundations.

A building for worship, good, and taqwa is not equal in worth to one for harm, division, and support of hostility.

The point is not merely strong stones and walls: an undertaking’s moral foundation is decisive in evaluating it.

This follows from the harmful mosque’s context and the comparison, not because hār itself means hypocrisy or ostentation.

Does every religious building indicate taqwa?

The passage itself answers no.

Building a mosque or using a religious title does not establish harmony between its aims, conduct, and taqwa.

A building with a sacred name may be used for harm or division.

Conversely, an outwardly modest activity may rest on sound intention and proper conduct.

Al-Tawbah 9:109 explains the distinction through a firm foundation and a crumbling edge.

It does not permit categorical judgments about any person’s or institution’s hidden intentions without actual evidence. The passage itself explicitly identifies harmful conduct and false claims.

Consequences of a false foundation

Al-Tawbah 9:110 describes a building remaining a source of doubt and disturbance in its builders’ hearts.

The connection concerns more than physical construction: the builders’ actions and purposes have consequences for them.

Hār in verse 109 should nevertheless not be translated as psychological doubt.

ه و ر conveys instability and collapse; the following verse expresses doubt and the hearts’ condition through other words.

6. Distinctions from neighboring roots

ه و ر and ه د م: collapse and demolition

Hadm concerns demolishing a building and removing its structure.

Al-Ḥajj 22:40 uses la-huddimat for places of worship being demolished.

Inhāra in al-Tawbah 9:109 describes the collapse of a structure losing its strength.

The roots can overlap, since demolition may also result in collapse.

The Qur’anic image of ه و ر concerns an unstable, collapsing bank, while ه د م can express demolishing a building.

The distinction is not absolute: ه و ر also has recorded verbs for demolishing structures.

ه و ر and س ق ط: structural collapse and falling

Saqaṭa broadly describes a thing or person falling.

In building and bank contexts, inhāra depicts falling accompanied by lost structural cohesion or strength.

A stone falling from someone’s hand is a fall without necessarily being a structural collapse.

A weakened well wall shedding earth is more appropriately described by inhiyār.

In this structural sense, collapse involves falling, but not every fall is a collapse.

ه و ر and خ ر ر: falling down and collapsing

Kharra also concerns falling down.

The Qur’an uses it for people or things falling in various situations.

It does not necessarily imply prior structural weakness or erosion.

A person may fall suddenly without their body or the ground previously undergoing structural collapse.

Hār together with inhāra in al-Tawbah 9:109 forms a more specific sequence: instability followed by collapse.

Not every use of kharra contains this sequence.

ه و ر and ج ر ف: collapse and bank erosion

These roots occur together in al-Tawbah 9:109.

Juruf concerns a bank and its cutting away or erosion.

Hār describes that bank’s unstable, crumbling state.

The words are not synonymous.

An eroded bank may still retain enough strength. Once unstable and close to falling, hār becomes significant.

Their combination makes the image more precise.

Juruf identifies the place; hār describes its instability.

ه و ر and ه و ي: collapse and downward falling

ه و ي concerns falling downward in this branch.

Qur’anic forms express falling and descending.

This is close to inhāra, but not identical.

Hawā can describe a person or object falling without prior structural weakness.

Hār and inhāra in al-Tawbah 9:109 concern a structure’s weakness and collapse.

Here ه و ي emphasizes the fall and its direction, while ه و ر concerns lost strength and disintegration.

ه و ر and ه ي ر: related forms

Lexicons record both tahawwara and tahayyara for collapse.

Some linguists discuss hār through forms containing wāw, others through forms containing yāʾ.

Morphological developments, rearrangement, or changes of letters may explain this.

Two related forms do not by themselves prove two historically independent roots with separate meanings.

For Qur’anic study, the shared sense of instability and collapse is clear; the debate mainly concerns formation and origin.

7. Challenges and suggested translations

The key translation issue is preserving the distinction between hār and inhāra.

Rendering both merely “collapsed” erases the difference between instability before the fall and the event itself.

Rendering hār only “soft” or “weak” fails to convey the central danger of collapse.

Translating hārin

Suitable equivalents in al-Tawbah 9:109 include:

“Unstable and crumbling,” “on the verge of collapse,” and “cracked and unstable.”

“Unstable and crumbling” is concise and clear for a basic translation.

It conveys more than general weakness or soft soil: the structure has become unstable enough to fall.

Translating fa-inhāra

A suitable equivalent is “then it collapsed.”

When the accompanying building or owner is made explicit, one can say:

“Then it collapsed along with it.”

Or:

“Then it carried the building down with it.”

The second makes the building’s accompanying fall explicit but is a freer rendering.

Suggested translation of the Qur’anic phrase

«عَلَىٰ شَفَا جُرُفٍ هَارٍ فَانْهَارَ بِهِ فِي نَارِ جَهَنَّمَ»

ʿAlā shafā jurufin hārin fa-inhāra bihi fī nāri jahannam.

“On the edge of an unstable, crumbling bank, which collapsed with his building into the fire of Hell.”

Each word retains its contribution:

Shafā marks the edge, juruf the cut-away and eroded bank, hār its instability and danger of collapse, and inhāra the actual fall.

The full verse can also use the fluent expression “the brink of an unstable, crumbling precipice.”

Hār should not be translated directly as hypocrisy, ostentation, lying, or corruption. Those concepts concern the builders’ moral condition as inferred from context, not the root’s lexical meaning.

Modern behavioral mutahawwir means a reckless person, but it is not an equivalent for Qur’anic hār.

Hār describes an unstable structure; behavioral mutahawwir describes someone who misjudges risks.

8. Conclusion

ه و ر concerns structural weakness, cracking, and collapse.

Material uses describe banks, well walls, and buildings. Early Arabic distinguished ground cracked behind its edge but still standing from ground actually collapsing.

The poem attributed to Bishr ibn Abī Khāzim illustrates soil collapsing under a horse’s hoof, although the precise attribution is disputed.

Classical Arabic also records weakness of decision, recklessness, the waning of night and winter, and certain senses of supposition and estimation. None should be imposed on a Qur’anic translation without evidence.

The Qur’an uses this root only in al-Tawbah 9:109, as hārin and fa-inhāra.

Hār describes a bank that has lost strength and is liable to collapse. Inhāra expresses its collapse occurring.

Final definition for Forqan: In its principal structural branch and Qur’anic usage, ه و ر denotes weakening, lost strength, and collapse. Hārin describes something exposed to collapse through weakness and cracking; inhāra expresses the collapse and fall itself.

The two forms distinguish an apparently standing structure from one whose foundation can no longer support it. When that foundation fails, what rests on it is also exposed to falling.

Al-Tawbah 9:109 turns this physical reality into an image of human undertakings: one founded on taqwa and God’s approval does not share the outcome of one founded on harm and division.

The verse thus concerns more than architecture. However religious or magnificent an undertaking looks, appearance cannot replace a sound foundation. Real stability depends on what supports it, not merely its exterior.
