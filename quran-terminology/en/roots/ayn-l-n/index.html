The Qur’anic Root AYN L N (ʿ L N)

Being Open and Making Manifest in Contrast to What Is Hidden

The root AYN L N (ʿ L N) has a highly coherent semantic range in the Qur’an. All its uses revolve around a clear contrast: something has remained in the hidden, non-public sphere, or has emerged from that sphere and become manifest.

For this reason, ʿalan should not be restricted to the later sense of “an official announcement.” What becomes manifest is not necessarily news, a declaration, or speech. The Qur’an speaks of “what you conceal and what you reveal,” leaving the object open; speech, behavior, decisions, and any other matter can therefore fall within this contrast.

The root’s Qur’anic uses are so consistent in meaning that little imagery is needed to identify its core. In every use, the Qur’an itself places ʿalan alongside a form of hiddenness, making the limits of its meaning clear.

Forms Examined

The root AYN L N (ʿ L N) occurs 16 times in the Qur’an:

Twelve times in forms of the form IV verb أَعْلَنَ (aʿlana);

Four times as عَلَانِيَة (ʿalāniyah).

The verbal forms include يُعْلِنُونَ (yuʿlinūna), تُعْلِنُونَ (tuʿlinūna), نُعْلِنُ (nuʿlinu), أَعْلَنْتُمْ (aʿlantum), and أَعْلَنْتُ (aʿlantu).

The Qur’an does not use the simple verb عَلَنَ (ʿalana). This morphological observation is noteworthy, but the absence of a form does not establish that the Qur’an deliberately denies “becoming apparent spontaneously” or always makes a particular act of will a condition of becoming public. What can be said with confidence is that the Qur’anic verb from this root occurs in form IV, أَعْلَنَ (aʿlana), and expresses the act of making something manifest in the ayahs.

1. The Semantic Core and Use in Pre-Islamic Arabic

In early Arabic, عَلَنَ الأمر (ʿalana al-amru) was said of a matter that had become apparent and manifest, while أَعْلَنَهُ (aʿlanahu) expressed making it manifest. عَلَانِيَة (ʿalāniyah) likewise stood in contrast to secrecy and concealment.

This contrast appears clearly in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād:

أَلَا أَبْلِغْ بَنِي الْعُشَرَاءِ عَنِّي
عَلَانِيَةً فَقَدْ ذَهَبَ السِّرَارُ

Alā abligh banī al-ʿusharāʾi ʿannī / ʿalāniyatan fa-qad dhahaba al-sirāru.

And further on:

وَلَمْ نَقْتُلْكُمُ سِرًّا وَلَكِنْ
عَلَانِيَةً وَقَدْ سَطَعَ الْغُبَارُ

Wa-lam naqtulkumu sirran wa-lākin / ʿalāniyatan wa-qad saṭaʿa al-ghubāru.

In the second verse, سِرًّا (sirran, secretly) and عَلَانِيَةً (ʿalāniyatan, openly) stand in direct contrast: an act performed openly rather than secretly.

The contrast between sirr and ʿalāniyah was therefore known in Arabic before the revelation of the Qur’an. The Qur’an preserves this semantic field and repeatedly uses it in exceptionally clear constructions.

Poetry attributed to ʿUbayd ibn al-Abraṣ also includes the expression:

مَا الْفَاجِعَاتُ جِهَارًا فِي عَلَانِيَةٍ

Mā al-fājiʿātu jihāran fī ʿalāniyatin.

The pairing of جِهَارًا (jihāran) and عَلَانِيَةٍ (ʿalāniyatin) shows that jahr and ʿalāniyah were not so identical that one could always replace the other without a difference. One places greater emphasis on the manner of becoming openly perceptible, the other on the state of being non-hidden itself.

2. Qur’anic Patterns: Every Occurrence Contrasts with What Is Hidden

An unusual feature of this root is that all sixteen Qur’anic occurrences are paired with some form of hiddenness in the same ayah. The contrast is sometimes constructed with S R R, sometimes with KH F Y, and sometimes with what the breasts keep within.

The most frequent construction is:

أَوَلَا يَعْلَمُونَ أَنَّ اللَّهَ يَعْلَمُ مَا يُسِرُّونَ وَمَا يُعْلِنُونَ

Awa-lā yaʿlamūna anna Allāha yaʿlamu mā yusirrūna wa-mā yuʿlinūna.

Do they not know that The God knows what they conceal and what they reveal?

Al-Baqarah 2:77

The same contrast recurs in Hud 11:5, An-Nahl 16:19 and 16:23, Ya-Sin 36:76, and At-Taghabun 64:4. The repetition of مَا يُسِرُّونَ وَمَا يُعْلِنُونَ (mā yusirrūna wa-mā yuʿlinūna) matters because the Qur’an does not say merely “what they say.” ما (mā, what) keeps the field open: whatever is kept in the hidden sphere or placed in the open sphere.

This also affects translation. “What they announce” may suggest only news and speech, whereas “what they reveal” better preserves the Arabic sentence’s breadth.

In two ayahs, the opposite of ʿalan is ikhfāʾ, concealment:

رَبَّنَا إِنَّكَ تَعْلَمُ مَا نُخْفِي وَمَا نُعْلِنُ

Rabbanā innaka taʿlamu mā nukhfī wa-mā nuʿlinu.

Our Lord, You know what we conceal and what we reveal.

Ibrahim 14:38

And:

وَيَعْلَمُ مَا تُخْفُونَ وَمَا تُعْلِنُونَ

Wa-yaʿlamu mā tukhfūna wa-mā tuʿlinūna.

And He knows what you conceal and what you reveal.

An-Naml 27:25

Here too, ʿalan is not confined to speech. Anything that is hidden can, on the other side of the contrast, be made manifest.

In An-Naml 27:74 and Al-Qasas 28:69, the contrast becomes even more vivid:

وَإِنَّ رَبَّكَ لَيَعْلَمُ مَا تُكِنُّ صُدُورُهُمْ وَمَا يُعْلِنُونَ

Wa-inna rabbaka la-yaʿlamu mā tukinnu ṣudūruhum wa-mā yuʿlinūna.

Your Lord surely knows what their breasts keep within and what they reveal.

An-Naml 27:74

One side of the ayah concerns what is kept in the breast; the other concerns what has been made manifest. This construction clearly shows that يُعْلِنُونَ (yuʿlinūna) should not be restricted to sound, speech, or an official announcement.

In Al-Mumtahanah 60:1, both fields also appear together:

تُسِرُّونَ إِلَيْهِمْ بِالْمَوَدَّةِ وَأَنَا أَعْلَمُ بِمَا أَخْفَيْتُمْ وَمَا أَعْلَنْتُمْ

Tusirrūna ilayhim bi-al-mawaddati wa-anā aʿlamu bi-mā akhfaytum wa-mā aʿlantum.

You communicate with them secretly out of affection, while I know better what you have concealed and what you have revealed.

Al-Mumtahanah 60:1

Here again, أَعْلَنْتُمْ (aʿlantum) simply denotes the open side of the matter. There is no need to restrict it through the later meaning “you officially announced.”

3. أَعْلَنَ (aʿlana) and عَلَانِيَة (ʿalāniyah) in Qur’anic Usage

The verb أَعْلَنَ (aʿlana) in the Qur’an expresses making something manifest. In most ayahs, its object is introduced with ما (mā, what), and the Qur’an deliberately leaves it unrestricted to any particular kind of content.

In Nuh 71:9, however, the context specifically concerns the manner of calling people:

ثُمَّ إِنِّي أَعْلَنْتُ لَهُمْ وَأَسْرَرْتُ لَهُمْ إِسْرَارًا

Thumma innī aʿlantu lahum wa-asrartu lahum isrāran.

Then I spoke openly to them and spoke to them privately.

Nuh 71:9

In this ayah, أَعْلَنْتُ (aʿlantu) stands in contrast to أَسْرَرْتُ (asrartu). One is a call made in the open, the other non-public, private communication. A rendering such as “I spoke openly to them” or “I presented the call openly” is therefore natural in this context.

This use does not establish that the verb itself means “to speak loudly.” That feature comes from the context of speech. In other ayahs, the same verb applies to any matter made manifest.

ʿAlāniyah in the Ayahs about Spending

All four Qur’anic occurrences of عَلَانِيَة (ʿalāniyah) concern spending, and all four stand in contrast to سِرًّا (sirran).

For example:

الَّذِينَ يُنْفِقُونَ أَمْوَالَهُمْ بِاللَّيْلِ وَالنَّهَارِ سِرًّا وَعَلَانِيَةً

Alladhīna yunfiqūna amwālahum bi-al-layli wa-al-nahāri sirran wa-ʿalāniyatan.

Those who spend their wealth by night and day, privately and openly.

Al-Baqarah 2:274

The same expression recurs in Ar-Rad 13:22, Ibrahim 14:31, and Fatir 35:29.

In these ayahs, عَلَانِيَة (ʿalāniyah) is neither a verb nor a type of verbal announcement; it describes how an act is performed. Spending can take place in the hidden sphere or be apparent to others.

These ayahs also clarify another point: an act’s being public is not, by itself, a sign of showing off or an independent virtue. Within this very construction, the Qur’an mentions concealed and open spending together. The act’s value comes from the act itself and its context, rather than from its being public or hidden alone.

4. ʿAlan Compared with Related Roots

AYN L N (ʿalan) and S R R (sirr)

This is the clearest contrast, because the Qur’an itself repeatedly places the two together.

Sirr is something that remains in the hidden, private, or inward sphere. ʿAlan is the open side of the same contrast.

In سِرًّا وَعَلَانِيَةً (sirran wa-ʿalāniyatan), one likewise describes the non-public manner of an act, the other its open manner.

Sirr, however, is not always merely something a person deliberately conceals from others; it can also refer to inward content. The contrast between sirr and ʿalan is therefore broader than “telling a secret versus announcing news.”

AYN L N (ʿalan) and J H R (jahara)

Jahr and ʿalan are close, but their perspectives differ.

ʿAlan more strongly emphasizes that something has not remained in the hidden sphere and is manifest.

In many Qur’anic uses, jahr is closer to an open and perceptible manner of expression, especially when speech and sound are the ayah’s subject:

وَأَسِرُّوا قَوْلَكُمْ أَوِ اجْهَرُوا بِهِ

Wa-asirrū qawlakum awi ijharū bihi.

Keep your speech private, or speak it openly.

Al-Mulk 67:13

In this ayah, jahr concerns qawl, speech itself. ʿAlāniyah in the spending ayahs, however, concerns a financial act in which sound is not at issue at all.

Jahr can therefore be said to lean more toward the manner of manifestation and perception, while ʿalan more strongly concerns the matter itself being open and non-hidden. This is a difference in emphasis and should not become an absolute rule.

AYN L N (ʿalan) and Ẓ H R (ẓahara)

Ẓuhūr has a broader semantic field. Something may appear, lie on the outer surface, or become manifest without anyone having “announced” it out of the hidden sphere.

In AYN L N, especially the Qur’anic form أَعْلَنَ (aʿlana), the contrast with secrecy, hiddenness, and concealed matters is more prominent.

Put simply, Ẓ H R focuses more on appearing and becoming visible; ʿalan, in Qur’anic usage, focuses on a matter’s occupying the open side in contrast to the hidden sphere.

Again, this is not an absolute boundary, but a distinction drawn from tendencies in the two families’ uses.

AYN L N (ʿalan) and KH F Y (khafiya)

Khafāʾ is broader than ʿalan. Something may be hidden, unseen, or beyond perception without being considered a “secret.”

Through the construction:

مَا نُخْفِي وَمَا نُعْلِنُ

Mā nukhfī wa-mā nuʿlinu — what we conceal and what we reveal.

the Qur’an makes clear that ʿalan can also stand opposite this invisibility and concealment.

5. Challenges in Understanding and Translation

One of the most significant restrictions of this root comes from the modern sense of iʿlān. In contemporary Arabic, it can denote an official statement, an advertisement, a notice, or the public release of news. These are real uses, but they should not be projected backward to determine the meaning of أَعْلَنَ (aʿlana) in the Qur’an.

When the Qur’an says:

مَا يُسِرُّونَ وَمَا يُعْلِنُونَ

Mā yusirrūna wa-mā yuʿlinūna — what they conceal and what they reveal.

the scope is much broader than “what they say secretly and what they officially announce.”

A more natural translation is:

What they conceal and what they reveal.

Nor should form IV be made to carry more meaning than it supports. أَعْلَنَ (aʿlana) in Qur’anic usage accords with making something manifest, but the morphological pattern alone does not establish formality, broad public dissemination, promotional intent, or the necessary intentionality of every kind of manifestation.

On the other hand, عَلَانِيَة (ʿalāniyah) does not necessarily mean “before a large crowd.” In the spending ayahs, it is enough that the act is no longer concealed. The extent of the audience or the number of people who see it is not determined by the word itself.

6. Translation Suggestions for Forqan

أَعْلَنَ (aʿlana):
He revealed; made manifest; made public.

“He announced” is suitable only where the context actually concerns conveying or stating news.

يُعْلِنُونَ (yuʿlinūna):
They reveal; they make public.

مَا يُسِرُّونَ وَمَا يُعْلِنُونَ (mā yusirrūna wa-mā yuʿlinūna):
“What they conceal and what they reveal.”

مَا نُخْفِي وَمَا نُعْلِنُ (mā nukhfī wa-mā nuʿlinu):
“What we conceal and what we reveal.”

مَا تُكِنُّ صُدُورُهُمْ وَمَا يُعْلِنُونَ (mā tukinnu ṣudūruhum wa-mā yuʿlinūna):
“What their breasts keep within and what they reveal.”

أَعْلَنْتُ لَهُمْ (aʿlantu lahum):
“I spoke openly to them,” or “I made my call to them public,” according to the translation of the complete ayah.

عَلَانِيَة (ʿalāniyah):
Openly; publicly; in a non-hidden manner.

سِرًّا وَعَلَانِيَةً (sirran wa-ʿalāniyatan):
“Privately and openly.”

Conclusion

The root AYN L N (ʿ L N) is among the Qur’an’s most coherent word families. All sixteen occurrences occupy one fundamental contrast: what is hidden and what is manifest.

In pre-Islamic Arabic, ʿalāniyah was also known in contrast to sirr. Poetry attributed to ʿAntarah explicitly preserves this contrast, showing that “openly, not secretly” was already present in this linguistic family before the Qur’an.

The Qur’an uses only two principal forms from this root: the verb أَعْلَنَ (aʿlana) and the noun عَلَانِيَة (ʿalāniyah). The verb denotes making manifest, while ʿalāniyah describes something being done openly and non-secretly. The absence of other forms should not be used to derive a rhetorical purpose or a theory about the nature of becoming manifest.

Above all, the Qur’an itself clarifies this root’s meaning through repeated contrasts: sirr and ʿalan, khafāʾ and ʿalan, what the breasts keep within and what becomes manifest. Its basic meaning can therefore be identified without resorting to later definitions or additional imagery.

AYN L N: Being manifest or making something manifest in contrast to a hidden, inward, or non-public state; ʿalāniyah describes something being done openly rather than secretly.

This definition covers the Qur’an’s verbal uses and ʿalāniyah in the spending ayahs, while preserving the necessary distinctions from jahr, ẓuhūr, and sirr.
