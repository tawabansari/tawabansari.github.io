The Root ʿ N W

Qur’anic and lexical forms

Core meaning: bowing, becoming humble, and entering a state of submission; in the captivity branch, coming under another’s control and domination.

The Arabic root ʿ N W concerns humility and submission. ʿAnā yaʿnū means to bow or submit before someone or something.

Within this family, ʿānin denotes someone captive or submissive, while ʿanwah commonly expresses force and conquest.

Submission and captivity have a clear semantic connection: a person yields to another’s power or authority. Yet yielding takes different forms. Someone may recognize truth and willingly submit, or be compelled to surrender through defeat and inability to resist.

The root should therefore neither be restricted to coercion and captivity nor invariably treated as voluntary, morally commendable humility.

Arabic lexicons also record senses such as water flowing, plants appearing, and the sides of something. These require separate consideration and should not be reduced to submission without evidence.

The root occurs only once in the Quran, as wa-ʿanati in Ta Ha 20:111, describing faces humbled before the Ever-Living, the Sustainer.

1. The root in early Arabic

ʿAnā: yielding and submission

ʿAnā lahu means to bow or submit before someone.

Lexicons explain ʿanawtu laka through khaḍaʿtu laka and aṭaʿtuka: “I submitted to you” or “I obeyed you.”

ʿAnawtu li-l-ḥaqqi is also recorded for submission to truth.

A person yields before something accepted or something beyond his power to resist.

Someone may insist on an opinion during a disagreement, then admit error when presented with clear evidence.

In this case, he has yielded to truth.

Another person may be defeated and forced to surrender without believing the opponent’s demands are right.

Both situations involve submission, but differ in freedom and moral value.

The meaning of ʿanā must therefore be distinguished from the reason for submission. The verb expresses yielding; context identifies whether recognition, acceptance, fear, or overpowering causes it.

A witness attributed to pre-Islamic speech

Early lexicons transmit the following as part of a pilgrimage invocation used by a group of Yemenis before Islam:

جَاءَتْ إِلَيْكَ عَانِيَهْ
عِبَادُكَ الْيَمَانِيَهْ
كَيْمَا تَحُجَّ الثَّانِيَهْ
عَلَى قِلَاصٍ نَاجِيَهْ

jāʾat ilayka ʿāniyah / ʿibāduka al-yamāniyah / kaymā taḥujja al-thāniyah / ʿalā qilāṣin nājiyah

“Your Yemeni servants have come to you in submission, to perform the pilgrimage again upon swift-traveling young she-camels.”

ʿĀniyah here expresses submission and humility.

The speakers present themselves as obedient in ritual language; the wording need not imply that all were actual captives.

This witness shows ʿāniyah expressing humility and obedience beyond literal captivity.

Later lexicons attribute the invocation to pre-Islamic Yemenis. The sources examined here provide no independent contemporary witness establishing this precise wording and date.

It is thus evidence of a transmitted linguistic tradition concerning that period, rather than an independently dated contemporary document.

Its lexical implication is clear: Arabic lexical tradition recognizes ʿānin for a person or group expressing submission.

ʿĀnin: a captive under another’s control

Captivity is a well-attested use of the root.

ʿĀnin means a captive; ʿāniyah is its feminine form.

ʿAnā al-rajulu yaʿnū is recorded for a man becoming captive or entering a condition of submission and constraint.

ʿAnawtu ʿinda fulānin can likewise refer to remaining captive with someone.

For example, a group may be defeated in battle and some members captured.

A captive lacks a free person’s ability to come and go, being subject to domination and restriction.

ʿĀnin then describes that condition of captivity.

Captivity alone does not establish moral guilt or acceptance of the victor’s beliefs.

An innocent person may be captured and continue opposing the captor’s conduct and demands despite being unable to escape.

Here ʿānin identifies subjection to another’s power, without establishing inward consent, the victor’s rightness, or the captive’s guilt.

This distinction matters: submission can be an external condition, while faith and conscious acceptance are separate matters.

ʿAnwah: force and conquest

In familiar Arabic usage, ʿanwah concerns force, overpowering, and taking something forcibly.

Akhadhahu ʿanwatan means “he took it by force.”

Historical and legal Arabic also uses fatḥun ʿanwatan for taking territory through fighting and conquest.

Submission here results from the other party’s superior power.

Early lexicons nevertheless report some uses of ʿanwah for surrender and obedience without fighting.

Submission is therefore broader than the specific act of forcible seizure.

These less common usages do not invalidate the established meaning in expressions of forcible conquest.

Each sentence must be examined for coercive overpowering or yielding obedience.

ʿAnwah itself does not establish the legitimacy of conquest or captivity. It describes how control was obtained or a condition of submission; moral judgment requires independent criteria.

Other lexical uses: flowing and appearing

Arabic sources also record ʿanā yaʿnū for water or blood flowing.

ʿAnati al-qirbatu can describe a waterskin leaking through a tear.

A poem attributed to Al-Mutanakhkhil al-Hudhali describes a worn, leaking waterskin:

تَعْنُو بِمَخْرُوتٍ لَهُ نَاضِحٌ
ذُو رَيِّقٍ يَغْذُو وَذُو شَلْشَلِ

taʿnū bi-makhrūtin lahu nāḍiḥun / dhū rayyiqin yaghdhū wa-dhū shalshal

“Water runs from a tear in it, continuing to pour and drip.”

Taʿnū here concerns liquid flowing through an opening, rather than submission or captivity.

Al-Mutanakhkhil is a Hudhali poet traditionally assigned to the pre-Islamic period. The line is transmitted in his poetry, with some textual variants; its individual date is not independently established here.

Another usage, ʿanati al-arḍu bi-l-nabāti, concerns earth bringing forth vegetation.

Aʿnā al-ghaythu al-nabāta likewise describes rain causing plants to appear.

Emergence or appearance is prominent in this branch.

ʿInw also denotes a side or edge, and aʿnāʾ denotes sides or diverse groups of people.

The lexical family thus extends beyond submission. A possible resemblance between water emerging, plants appearing, and human submission cannot establish a single historical origin for every branch.

Only the submission branch occurs in the Quran; water, vegetation, and sides do not directly explain the verse’s usage.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important forms in Arabic include:

ʿAnā: became humble or yielded; in another use, became captive.

Yaʿnū: submits, bows, or enters a condition of surrender.

ʿUnuww: submission and yielding.

ʿAnw and ʿanwah: submission; in the conquest branch, force and forcible taking.

ʿĀnin: someone submissive or captive.

ʿĀniyah: the feminine of ʿānin; a female captive or one described as submissive.

ʿUnāh: a plural of ʿānin; captives or submissive people.

ʿAwānin: in one lexical use, a plural of ʿāniyah.

Aʿnā: in the captivity branch, subjected or captured another; elsewhere, caused something to appear.

ʿInw: a side or edge.

Aʿnāʾ: sides, regions, or diverse groups.

These are forms recorded in Arabic, rather than a list of forms all occurring in the Quran.

The only Quranic occurrence from this root is ʿanati in Ta Ha 20:111.

It derives from the basic-stem perfect ʿanā, whose third radical is wāw, visible in the imperfect yaʿnū.

The final tāʾ in ʿanati is the feminine agreement marker, rather than a root consonant.

This matters because ʿanati must not be confused with verbs belonging to the independent root ʿ N T.

3. Quranic usage: faces humbled before the Ever-Living Sustainer

Ta Ha 20:111

وَعَنَتِ الْوُجُوهُ لِلْحَيِّ الْقَيُّومِ ۖ وَقَدْ خَابَ مَنْ حَمَلَ ظُلْمًا

wa-ʿanati al-wujūhu li-l-ḥayyi al-qayyūmi wa-qad khāba man ḥamala ẓulmā

Ṭā Hā 20:111

“Faces will be humbled before the Ever-Living, the Sustainer; whoever bears wrongdoing has indeed failed.”

Ta Ha, 20:111

ʿAnati expresses yielding and submission.

Its subject is al-wujūhu, “faces.”

Li-l-ḥayyi al-qayyūmi identifies the One before whom that submission occurs.

The phrase therefore describes faces humbled before the Ever-Living, the Sustainer.

Why “faces”?

Wujūh is the plural of wajh, “face.”

The face is a conspicuous part of the body, displaying joy, fear, grief, embarrassment, or humility.

Someone may hold his head high with pride or lower his head and gaze when ashamed and acknowledging error.

In ordinary language, such changes can express an inward condition.

The verse’s humbled faces can thus depict people yielding before their Lord.

ʿAnati alone does not require a particular bodily movement such as placing the forehead on the ground.

Submission can become visible in bodily behavior without its lexical meaning being restricted to one posture.

Why submission before al-Ḥayy al-Qayyūm?

The verse connects the submission of faces with two divine descriptions:

Al-Ḥayy and al-Qayyūm.

Al-Ḥayy concerns life and being living.

Al-Qayyūm concerns enduring subsistence and sustaining others.

Together they express the Lord’s position in relation to dependent, limited creatures.

People may possess considerable worldly power, wealth, and authority, even becoming proud enough to imagine themselves independent of others.

Their power and life remain limited.

The Quran, by contrast, speaks of the Ever-Living Sustainer whose life and sustaining power are unlike those of limited creatures.

Submission here is before the Lord upon whom all creatures depend, beyond temporary human authority.

This explanation comes from the verb’s connection with the divine descriptions and context. The root itself does not mean life, sustaining existence, or creating.

Its relationship to the resurrection scene

The surrounding verses clarify the expression.

Ta Ha 20:108 describes people following the caller and voices becoming subdued before the Entirely Merciful.

Ta Ha 20:109 makes beneficial intercession dependent on divine permission and approval.

Ta Ha 20:110 speaks of God’s encompassing knowledge and the limits of others’ knowledge.

Ta Ha 20:111 then depicts faces humbled before the Ever-Living Sustainer.

This sequence places the verse within divine judgment.

Human displays of power and claims have no independent, decisive standing before God’s truth and sovereignty.

ʿAnati al-wujūhu thus depicts submission before divine rule and judgment.

Does the submission of all faces mean everyone has faith?

No.

The verse describes humbled faces, then immediately says:

وَقَدْ خَابَ مَنْ حَمَلَ ظُلْمًا

wa-qad khāba man ḥamala ẓulmā

Ṭā Hā 20:111

That is, whoever bears wrongdoing has failed.

This juxtaposition matters.

At resurrection, even those who denied truth or oppressed others cannot escape God’s judgment.

Yielding to a reality that can no longer be practically denied does not establish that someone lived with faith and righteous conduct.

A person may persist in wrongdoing, then be forced to acknowledge reality when its consequences become visible and cannot be concealed.

Such unavoidable acknowledgment differs from recognizing truth earlier and reforming one’s conduct accordingly.

The submission of faces likewise does not imply equal moral standing for everyone.

Ta Ha 20:112 describes someone who believes and does righteous deeds, fearing neither injustice nor diminution of what is due.

The passage distinguishes submission to the Lord from the consequences of people’s conduct.

Submission to divine judgment does not by itself turn past sins into righteous deeds.

Does this submission conflict with worldly freedom of choice?

In worldly life, people can choose to accept or reject the way shown to them.

Universal submission before divine power and truth on Judgment Day does not render earlier choices meaningless.

A person may disregard a rule without thereby eliminating all consequences of his conduct.

This example illustrates only the distinction between freedom to act and inability to escape consequences.

Ta Ha 20:111 similarly describes submission before the Ever-Living Sustainer while expressing conduct’s moral outcome in a separate clause.

ʿAnati therefore does not establish that God compelled everyone to believe during worldly life.

Nor should the resurrection scene be reduced to an empty formality: the context concerns divine power, knowledge, and judgment.

4. Morphology and syntax of wa-ʿanati

Wa-ʿanati has two main components:

Wa is a coordinating conjunction.

ʿAnati is the feminine singular perfect of ʿanā yaʿnū.

The final tāʾ marks feminine agreement: its subject, al-wujūhu, is a nonhuman plural in grammatical form and can take a feminine singular verb.

The underlying inflected form is ʿanat. In connected recitation before al-wujūhu, the tāʾ takes kasrah to avoid the meeting of two vowelless consonants.

The third radical is wāw, although it is absent from this inflected form.

Why use a perfect verb for resurrection?

Morphologically, ʿanati is a perfect verb.

The passage, however, depicts Judgment Day.

Arabic can use the perfect to describe a future event, especially when presenting it as certain.

“Faces will be humbled” is therefore a natural and defensible contextual rendering.

This usage does not change the root’s meaning.

The root still expresses submission; construction and context identify the time intended.

5. Distinctions from related roots

ʿ N W (ʿunuww) and KH SH ʿ (khushūʿ)

Khushūʿ concerns humility, quieting, and subsiding before greatness or awe.

ʿUnuww likewise expresses yielding and submission.

The two can be very close in context.

Ta Ha 20:108 describes voices subdued before the Entirely Merciful.

Ta Ha 20:111 describes faces humbled before the Ever-Living Sustainer.

The imagery differs: voices subside in one, faces yield in the other.

This does not restrict khushūʿ to voices or ʿunuww to faces.

Both roots have broader uses concerning humility and submission.

Their precise distinction must be established from each construction and context.

ʿ N W (ʿunuww) and KH Ḍ ʿ (khuḍūʿ)

Khuḍūʿ is a close Arabic explanation of ʿunuww.

Both concern yielding or submission; whether this involves relinquishing pride depends on context.

Lexicons explain ʿanā lahu through khaḍaʿa lahu.

Quranic khuḍūʿ can concern behavior such as softening one’s speech, as in Al-Ahzab 33:32.

ʿAnati al-wujūhu in Ta Ha 20:111 instead concerns submission to the Lord at resurrection.

Their semantic proximity does not permit transferring every usage from one to the other without examination.

ʿ N W (ʿunuww) and DH L L (dhull)

Dhull concerns lowliness, becoming subdued, and losing power or superiority.

ʿUnuww likewise relates to submission and surrender.

Captivity may involve both: someone loses earlier freedom and power and is forced to submit.

Yet submission is not always accompanied by humiliation or oppression.

Someone may yield to truth without having been humiliated.

Being brought low by oppression also differs morally from consciously submitting to truth.

ʿUnuww should therefore not invariably be restricted to “being humiliated.”

ʿ N W (ʿunuww) and S L M (islām)

In one branch of S L M, islām concerns submitting and giving oneself over.

ʿUnuww can likewise express yielding and submission.

They are not interchangeable in every usage.

Quranic islām can concern a person’s orientation and acceptance toward God.

ʿAnati al-wujūhu in Ta Ha 20:111 describes the condition of faces at resurrection.

It must not be taken to mean that everyone there has attained equal faith and righteousness.

Submission to divine power and truth differs from judgment of earlier faith and conduct.

ʿ N W (ʿunuww) and DH ʿ N (idhʿān)

Idhʿān concerns obeying and accepting another’s wish or judgment.

An-Nur 24:49 uses this family for people who come obediently when the right is in their favor.

ʿUnuww can likewise express submission and obedience.

Idhʿān emphasizes accepting and yielding to a demand or judgment; the Quranic use of ʿunuww depicts submission before the Lord.

Semantic proximity does not make every instance of obedience an expression of sincere faith and acceptance.

ʿ N W (ʿunuww) and ʿ N T (ʿanat)

These roots resemble one another in writing but remain distinct.

ʿ N W ends in wāw and expresses submission in its Quranic use.

ʿ N T ends in tāʾ and concerns hardship, constraint, and harmful distress.

For example, la-aʿnatakum in Al-Baqarah 2:220 belongs to ʿ N T and means putting people into hardship.

Wa-ʿanati al-wujūhu in Ta Ha 20:111 belongs to ʿ N W.

Here the final tāʾ marks feminine agreement, rather than supplying the third radical.

Looking only at the written form ʿanat could therefore lead to the mistaken attribution to the hardship family.

Ta Ha describes faces submitting, rather than suffering distress.

ʿ N W (ʿunuww) and ʿ N Y (ʿināyah)

ʿInāyah concerns care and attention; maʿnā concerns what is intended or signified. Classical lexicons record ʿuniya bi-l-amri for attending to a matter, while ʿanā bi-kalāmihi concerns intending something by one’s words.

Such uses are sometimes discussed alongside ʿ N W because final-weak roots with wāw and yāʾ can resemble one another in pronunciation and writing.

Quranic study must nevertheless preserve the precise form and meaning.

ʿAnā yaʿnū in Ta Ha 20:111 concerns submission, rather than attending to a task.

ʿAnāʾ, hardship or toil, is also recorded in Arabic and discussed among related final-weak forms. Historical proximity does not justify translating the submission of Ta Ha 20:111 as toil or suffering.

ʿAwān: resemblance to another Quranic word

Lexicons record ʿawānin as a plural of ʿāniyah, female captives.

ʿAwān in Al-Baqarah 2:68 describes a cow neither old nor young, but between the two.

That usage belongs to a different lexical family and must not be translated as captive or submissive merely because the letters resemble one another.

Root identification thus requires morphology and context, beyond the visible letters alone.

6. Translation proposal for Forqan

Quranic word: Wa-ʿanati.

Root: ʿ N W.

Word type: Basic-stem triliteral perfect, feminine singular, preceded by coordinating wa.

Brief root meaning: Bowing and submitting to another; in captivity, surrendering and coming under another’s control.

Meaning in this verse: Faces become humbled before the Ever-Living Sustainer.

Suggested translation: “And will be humbled.”

Translation of the phrase:

وَعَنَتِ الْوُجُوهُ لِلْحَيِّ الْقَيُّومِ

wa-ʿanati al-wujūhu li-l-ḥayyi al-qayyūm

Ṭā Hā 20:111

“And faces will be humbled before the Ever-Living, the Sustainer.”

This rendering preserves submission without specifying a particular bodily posture or adding a judgment about the person’s faith.

“Will bow” is also defensible and makes the image of yielding more explicit.

“Will become captives” is unsuitable as a direct translation here. Although ʿānin can mean captive, Ta Ha 20:111 concerns submission before God, rather than social or military captivity.

“Will suffer hardship” likewise fails to express this contextual meaning. Hardship belongs among the senses of the independent root ʿ N T, but the occurrence here concerns submission.

The explanation should preserve the distinction between general submission at judgment and people’s differing faith, conduct, and outcomes.

7. Conclusion

ʿ N W has several Arabic semantic branches; its Quranic use concerns submission.

ʿAnā yaʿnū expresses bowing and yielding.

In the captivity branch, ʿānin denotes someone held under another’s control.

ʿAnwah commonly expresses force and conquest, although some lexical reports also record yielding without fighting.

Other recorded branches include flowing water, emerging vegetation, and the sides of something; these should not all be reduced to submission without evidence.

The root occurs only in Ta Ha 20:111.

There, faces become humbled before the Ever-Living Sustainer, within a context of resurrection, God’s encompassing knowledge, and judgment of human conduct.

Submission here expresses yielding to divine sovereignty and truth, without itself meaning that sins are erased or everyone’s moral standing becomes equal.

ʿ N W must also be distinguished from the neighboring families ʿ N T and ʿ N Y. Similar written forms do not make their meanings identical.

Final definition of ʿ N W:

“In its Quranic branch: bowing, becoming humble, and entering submission before another, whether through acceptance and obedience or through overpowering and inability to resist. From this field, ʿānin describes a captive and ʿanwah expresses force and conquest. Quranic wa-ʿanati describes faces humbled before the Ever-Living Sustainer, rather than hardship or captivity in its legal sense.”
