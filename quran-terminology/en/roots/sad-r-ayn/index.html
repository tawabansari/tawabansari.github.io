Throwing to the ground and being laid low: from wrestling and bodily falling to those struck down and dead

The Arabic root Ṣ R ʿ concerns throwing to the ground, falling, and being laid low. Its most familiar use is one person throwing another down in wrestling or a struggle. The family is not limited to wrestling: wind can knock down a tree or person, and someone may fall through illness, faintness, or another event.

This root gives ṣarʿ, throwing down; ṣarīʿ, someone laid low; muṣāraʿah and ṣirāʿ, wrestling and physical struggle; and maṣraʿ, a place of falling or death.

Ṣ R ʿ occurs only once in the Qur’an, as ṣarʿā in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7. It describes the people of ʿĀd, struck down by a destructive wind, their bodies fallen on the ground.

A fundamental distinction is between falling to the ground, being defeated, and dying. These may occur together, but their meanings differ. Ṣarʿ basically expresses throwing down or being laid low; death and destruction are established through context and other indications.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the sole ṣarʿā occurrence in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7

1. The root’s semantic core

Throwing to the ground

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris explains the root’s principle as follows:

يَدُلُّ عَلَى سُقُوطِ شَيْءٍ إِلَى الْأَرْضِ

yadullu ʿalā suqūṭi shayʾin ilā al-arḍi

That is, it indicates something falling to the ground.

He goes on to illustrate this with two people wrestling and one throwing the other down.

Lisān al-ʿArab also records:

الصَّرْعُ: الطَّرْحُ بِالْأَرْضِ

al-ṣarʿu al-ṭarḥu bi-al-arḍi

That is, ṣarʿ means throwing to the ground.

This definition clarifies the family’s main feature: something previously upright or in another position falls or is thrown to the ground by a force.

A person may be thrown down by someone else or laid low by a natural cause or another event.

The basic meaning therefore cannot be restricted to defeating someone in wrestling. Wrestling is a clear use of the root, but not every instance requires two people confronting each other.

Does ṣarʿ always mean falling from a height?

No.

In wrestling, someone standing upright may fall through the opponent’s movement or force. No great height is involved.

In the case of a tree, wind may bend it and throw it to the ground.

The main feature of ṣarʿ is therefore not falling from a specified height, but losing an upright or stable position and falling to the ground.

The verb itself also does not specify the speed, direction, or force of the fall.

Ṣaraʿa and inṣaraʿa

Arabic records:

صَرَعَهُ فَانْصَرَعَ

ṣaraʿahu fa-inṣaraʿa

That is, “He threw him to the ground, and he fell.”

This construction distinguishes two aspects of the event.

Ṣaraʿahu is transitive and concerns the agent who throws someone down.

Inṣaraʿa concerns the fall of the person or thing thrown down.

This distinction matters for the lexical family: some sentences highlight an external agent, while others highlight the fallen person’s condition.

Neither form necessarily includes death in the verb’s meaning.

Someone may be thrown down and rise again, or may die from a severe blow.

The difference must be established from context.

2. Ṣarʿ in early Arabic: wrestling, wind, and falling

Wrestling and overcoming an opponent

One established use of this root concerns a physical encounter between two people.

Ṣāraʿa fulānan means that he wrestled with someone.

Ṣāraʿahu fa-ṣaraʿahu means that he wrestled with him and ultimately threw him down.

Muṣāraʿah here concerns the encounter and effort of both sides; ṣaraʿa expresses the resulting throw to the ground.

Wrestling and throwing down are thus different stages or aspects.

Two people may wrestle without either managing to throw the other down.

Someone may also fall through a blow or an accident without any wrestling taking place.

Ṣarīʿ: someone laid low

Ṣarīʿ follows the pattern faʿīl and in its established use has the sense of maṣrūʿ: someone fallen or thrown to the ground.

Its plural is ṣarʿā.

Arabic can use ṣarīʿ for someone thrown down in wrestling, laid low by illness, or killed in war with their body fallen to the ground.

Ṣarīʿ therefore does not mean someone killed in every use.

In contexts of war and destruction it may describe a fallen person who has died; in wrestling or another event, the person may still be alive.

This distinction matters when interpreting its sole Qur’anic occurrence.

Wind and fallen trees

Lexicons record ṣaraʿati al-rīḥu al-zarʿa: the wind laid the crops flat on the ground.

Here wind is the verb’s subject and vegetation its object.

Wind may bend stems or bring a tree down from an upright position.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah also records ṣarīʿ for a branch that has drooped and fallen to the ground.

This use shows that ṣarʿ is not confined to people and animals.

A plant can also be ṣarīʿ, since the relevant feature is falling or lying on the ground.

Some lexical reports call a drooping branch ṣarīʿ even when it remains attached to its tree.

Ṣarʿ concerning plants therefore does not necessarily mean complete severing or destruction.

A branch fallen to the ground may consequently remain alive.

Ṣarīʿ: a branch used to make a bow

After describing a branch that has drooped and fallen to the ground, Ibn Fāris says that a bow made from it is also called ṣarīʿ.

In this explanation, the fallen branch’s name is also applied to the bow made from it.

This particular use therefore requires distinguishing the description of the fallen branch from the name of the bow made from it.

The witness matters because being ṣarīʿ, even in material uses involving something other than a person, does not necessarily entail destruction or uselessness.

Nor does this use establish that Qur’anic ṣarīʿ names a type of bow or weapon.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: a fallen branch and a bow made from it

3. A witness from pre-Islamic Arabic poetry

A line attributed to Balʿāʾ ibn Qays al-Kinānī, a pre-Islamic poet and warrior, reads:

رَأَتْنِي صَرِيعَ الْخَمْرِ يَوْمًا فَسُؤْتُهَا
وَلِلشَّارِبِيهَا الْمُدْمِنِيهَا مَصَارِعُ

raʾatnī ṣarīʿa al-khamri yawman fa-suʾtuhā / wa-li-al-shāribīhā al-mudminīhā maṣāriʿu

Rendering: “One day she saw me laid low by wine, and my condition distressed her; those who drink it habitually face falls and harsh ends.”

Early poetry collections attribute this line to Balʿāʾ ibn Qays. His identification as a pre-Islamic poet is established, although the precise date of this particular line has not been independently determined.

The poem contains two forms from Ṣ R ʿ: ṣarīʿ and maṣāriʿ.

Ṣarīʿa al-khamri describes someone laid low by drinking wine.

Being ṣarīʿ here does not necessarily mean dying. The poet describes someone whose intoxication has impaired normal strength and balance.

Maṣāriʿ in the second hemistich has a broader range and can refer to the drinkers’ falls and harsh ends.

The distinction shows early uses for both an actual fall and an outcome that lays someone low.

The line does not, however, make wine or intoxication part of ṣarʿ’s lexical meaning.

Intoxication is merely the cause of falling in this poetic image, just as wind, illness, or an opponent’s force can be the cause in other uses.

4. Other uses of the lexical family

Ṣirāʿ and muṣāraʿah

Arabic uses ṣirāʿ and muṣāraʿah for wrestling and a physical encounter between two sides.

Each side tries to dislodge the other from an upright position and throw them down.

In contemporary Persian, ṣirāʿ can also describe broader conflict, such as political, social, or intellectual struggle.

These extended uses can be explained through the image of wrestling and overcoming an opponent.

The modern sense of political conflict should not be imposed on the Qur’anic root. Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7 concerns fallen bodies, rather than merely disagreement or rivalry between two groups.

Ṣarrāʿ and ṣuraʿah

Ṣarrāʿ describes someone highly capable at wrestling who can throw others to the ground.

Lexicons also record ṣuraʿah in this sense: someone skilled at throwing opponents down.

Some literary and ethical uses likewise compare overcoming one’s anger and desires with wrestling.

The image presents a person as confronting an internal force that must be controlled.

This figurative use is not the historical definition of every form in the root.

It also does not establish that Qur’anic ṣarʿā refers to people defeated in a psychological or moral struggle.

In Al-Ḥāqqah, the context concerns the actual destruction of ʿĀd and the falling of their bodies.

Maṣraʿ and maṣāriʿ

Arabic uses maṣraʿ for a place of falling or where someone is laid low.

In a context of war, maṣāriʿu al-qawmi can refer to the places where people were killed.

Maṣraʿ thus also occurs in some sentences for a place of death or a fatal end.

Death comes from the particular situation and use, rather than every fall being a death.

In Persian too, “falling to the earth” can describe an actual fall or, in a context of war, being killed.

This semantic proximity clarifies the relationship between ṣarʿ and death.

Miṣrāʿ: a door leaf

Arabic uses miṣrāʿ for either leaf of a double door.

Miṣrāʿā al-bābi means the door’s two leaves.

Ibn Fāris connects the two leaves with this family as corresponding parts that face each other and come together.

Lexicons also discuss the word’s connection with ṣirʿān, meaning two parts or counterparts.

Miṣrāʿ here should therefore not be understood as someone or something necessarily fallen to the ground.

Naming a door’s two leaves is a specialized architectural use within the family.

Miṣrāʿ and taṣrīʿ in poetry

In Arabic prosody, either half of a poetic line is called a miṣrāʿ.

Taṣrīʿ describes making the first hemistich rhyme with the second, especially at an ode’s opening.

The term has been compared with a door’s two leaves: as two leaves constitute one door, two hemistichs constitute one poetic line.

This technical use does not make Qur’anic ṣarʿā refer to division into two, rhyme, or poetic structure.

Ṣirʿān: two parts or counterparts

Arabic sources record ṣirʿān for two similar things or corresponding parts.

Ṣarʿā al-nahāri is also recorded for the day’s two parts, such as morning and evening.

In some husbandry uses, ṣarʿān names two groups of camels, one coming as the other goes.

Ibn Fāris explains these forms through similarity or pairing and proposes a connection with the wrestling branch.

The explanation can clarify some uses, but does not establish that all senses of pairing historically developed directly from throwing an opponent down.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Lisān al-ʿArab, and Maqāyīs: miṣrāʿ, taṣrīʿ, and ṣirʿān

5. Ṣarʿ as a name for illness

Arabic also used al-ṣarʿ for an illness or condition in which someone may have an attack, lose normal bodily control, and fall.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Miṣbāḥ al-Munīr record this use as an established illness.

Maṣrūʿ is also used for someone affected by such a condition.

The name relates to one conspicuous manifestation of illness: falling or being laid low.

Lexical meaning must nevertheless be distinguished from medical diagnosis.

In modern medicine, epilepsy is a neurological disorder associated with epileptic seizures. Not every seizure necessarily involves falling or conspicuous convulsions.

The illness’s historical name should therefore not become its complete medical definition.

World Health Organization: variation in seizure manifestations

Some early dictionaries also explain ṣarīʿ through madness or loss of consciousness.

These descriptions reflect the linguistic and medical classifications of their times; they do not establish that epilepsy, mental illness, and all forms of unconsciousness are identical.

Nor should everyone who falls through an accident be regarded as having epilepsy.

Qur’anic ṣarʿā in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7 describes ʿĀd; nothing in the context identifies it as the name of a neurological condition.

6. The Qur’anic use: Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7

سَخَّرَهَا عَلَيْهِمْ سَبْعَ لَيَالٍ وَثَمَانِيَةَ أَيَّامٍ حُسُومًا فَتَرَى الْقَوْمَ فِيهَا صَرْعَىٰ كَأَنَّهُمْ أَعْجَازُ نَخْلٍ خَاوِيَةٍ

sakhkharahā ʿalayhim sabʿa layālin wa-thamāniyata ayyāmin ḥusūman fa-tarā al-qawma fīhā ṣarʿā ka-annahum aʿjāzu nakhlin khāwiyatin

Translation: He subjected it against them for seven nights and eight consecutive days; then you see the people fallen on the ground therein, as though they were hollow palm trunks.

The verse continues the Qur’an’s account of ʿĀd.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:6 states that ʿĀd were destroyed by a fierce, overwhelming wind.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7 then describes how long the wind continued and the people’s condition after being caught in it.

Study of Ḥ S M

Ṣarʿā here refers to people laid low, their bodies fallen to the ground.

The morphology of ṣarʿā

Ṣarʿā is the plural of ṣarīʿ.

Ṣarīʿ follows the pattern faʿīl and in the use under discussion has the sense of maṣrūʿ: someone thrown down or laid low.

Its plural follows the pattern faʿlā, like qatlā and jarḥā.

Arabic uses this structure for groups of people in a particular condition.

The plural pattern itself does not identify the condition. Being killed, wounded, and thrown down are different states, each established through its own word’s meaning.

The similar structures of ṣarʿā and qatlā therefore do not make them fully equivalent.

The syntax of ṣarʿā

In the expression:

فَتَرَى الْقَوْمَ فِيهَا صَرْعَىٰ

fa-tarā al-qawma fīhā ṣarʿā

Al-qawma is the object of tarā.

Ṣarʿā functions as a circumstantial accusative, describing the people’s condition when seen.

The sentence thus means that you see the people in a state of having fallen to the ground.

This matters because ṣarʿā here is neither their name nor a social title, but a description of their bodies’ condition.

Does ṣarʿā by itself mean the dead?

Not in general Arabic usage.

A ṣarīʿ person may be alive or dead.

In Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7, however, the context establishes death and destruction.

In Al-Ḥāqqah 69:6 the Qur’an has described ʿĀd’s destruction.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:8 also asks whether anyone remains of them.

Ṣarʿā in this verse therefore describes dead people whose bodies have fallen to the ground.

Al-Ṭabarī likewise identifies them in his explanation as the destroyed people of ʿĀd.

That conclusion comes from the connected verses, rather than ṣarʿā necessarily meaning the dead in every Arabic use.

For direct translation, “fallen on the ground” better preserves the word’s main feature. A contextual explanation can clarify that these fallen people died through the punishment.

Al-Ṭabarī: ʿĀd’s destruction and fallen bodies

What does fīhā refer to?

The verse reads:

فَتَرَى الْقَوْمَ فِيهَا صَرْعَىٰ

fa-tarā al-qawma fīhā ṣarʿā

The pronoun hā can refer to the wind mentioned in the preceding verse.

Exegetical explanations also discuss its referring to the period of seven nights and eight days.

In either reading, the verse describes the people’s condition during the same destructive event.

For studying Ṣ R ʿ, this difference over the pronoun’s referent does not change ṣarʿā’s meaning.

The word still describes the people laid low.

Ibn ʿĀshūr: fīhā referring to the nights and days

Why are the people compared with palm trunks?

The continuation reads:

كَأَنَّهُمْ أَعْجَازُ نَخْلٍ خَاوِيَةٍ

ka-annahum aʿjāzu nakhlin khāwiyatin

The comparison makes the picture of the fallen people clearer.

Aʿjāz refers to the lower parts or trunks of palms.

Khāwiyah can express emptiness and hollowness, or falling and collapse.

The Qur’an thus compares the people’s fallen bodies with palm trunks that have lost their former stability.

The image fits ṣarʿā: falling and losing an upright position are prominent in both.

Ṣarʿā itself does not mean palm trunks; that image comes from the following comparison.

Nor does the comparison establish the exact size of their bodies or the physical manner in which every individual fell.

Al-Ṭabarī: the comparison with palm bases and trunks

Study of KH W Y

Comparison with Al-Qamar 54:20

Al-Qamar 54:20 likewise compares ʿĀd with uprooted palm trunks.

That verse describes a wind tearing people away.

This clearly relates to Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7: a fierce wind strikes the people and dislodges them from an upright, stable position.

The two images together do not establish unstated details, such as how many times each person was thrown into the air, which body part struck the ground, or the exact position in which they died.

What the language establishes is the people being torn away and laid low by a destructive wind.

Does ṣarʿ here mean defeat in war?

No.

Although Arabic also uses the root for wrestling and overcoming an opponent, Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7 does not concern war or wrestling between two human groups.

The verse concerns ʿĀd’s destruction through wind.

Rendering ṣarʿā as “those defeated in battle” is therefore inaccurate.

“The vanquished” alone likewise fails to convey the image of falling to the ground.

The literal, bodily meaning of being laid low and falling to the ground has clear support here.

7. Comparison with related roots

Ṣ R ʿ (ṣaraʿa) and S Q Ṭ (saqaṭa)

Arabic saqaṭa refers to falling.

Ṣaraʿa also concerns falling, but its transitive use highlights throwing a person or thing to the ground.

A stone may, for example, fall from a height without anyone throwing it down in wrestling or a struggle.

A person, however, may be thrown down by an opponent’s force or by wind.

The difference is clear in some uses, but is not an absolute boundary: ṣaraʿa is also used concerning plants and people laid low.

Ṣ R ʿ (ṣaraʿa) and KH R R (kharra)

Arabic kharra concerns falling down or falling to the ground.

In Maryam 19:58, kharrū sujjadan wa-bukiyyan describes falling down in prostration.

Ṣaraʿa in its established use more often involves laying someone low or falling under a force.

Not every khurūr is therefore ṣarʿ.

Someone may choose to fall down in prostration, whereas in wrestling an opponent’s force throws them down.

This distinction comes from representative uses and should not become a fixed rule for all derivatives of both roots.

Study of KH R R

Ṣ R ʿ (ṣaraʿa) and Q T L (qatala)

Qatala refers to killing: an action resulting in someone’s death.

Ṣaraʿa basically concerns throwing to the ground.

Someone may fall during wrestling and remain alive, or may be killed in war and fall to the ground.

In the latter case, qatīl and ṣarīʿ can describe the same person, but each highlights a different feature.

Qatīl expresses being killed, while ṣarīʿ describes being laid low or fallen on the ground.

In Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7, the people’s death is clear from ʿĀd’s destruction, but ṣarʿā itself pictures their fallen bodies.

Study of Q T L

Ṣ R ʿ (ṣaraʿa) and H L K (halaka)

Arabic halaka concerns perishing or passing out of existence.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:6 states ʿĀd’s destruction; Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7 describes their condition as ṣarʿā.

The two expressions complement each other.

Destruction expresses their fate and the ending of their lives; ṣarʿā pictures their bodies’ condition.

They should therefore not be treated as one word in two different outward forms.

Study of H L K

Ṣ R ʿ (ṣaraʿa) and GH L B (ghalaba)

Ghalaba concerns overcoming or prevailing.

In wrestling, someone who throws an opponent down has usually prevailed over them.

Not every victory is ṣarʿ.

Someone may prevail in argument, a contest, or war without throwing another person to the ground.

Prevailing can therefore be a result or accompanying concept of ṣarʿ, but is not its direct, complete meaning.

Ṣ R ʿ (ṣaraʿa) and Ṣ R R (ṣarra)

Despite their similar letters, these are separate roots.

Qur’anic Ṣ R ʿ concerns ʿĀd being laid low.

Qur’anic Ṣ R R concerns bitter cold, a loud cry, and persistence.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:6 also uses ṣarṣar concerning wind, but the Qur’anic morphological index places that form under the quadriliteral Ṣ R Ṣ R.

Study of Ṣ R Ṣ R

The proximity of ṣarṣar and ṣarʿā in consecutive verses therefore does not make their roots identical.

Ṣarṣar describes a quality of the wind, while ṣarʿā describes the people laid low.

8. Challenges of interpretation and translation

The root’s main difficulty is distinguishing a fall’s resulting condition from its cause or eventual outcome.

In Arabic someone may be ṣarīʿ because another person has thrown them down.

They may have fallen through wind or another event.

They may also have been killed in war, their body fallen on the ground.

These three situations differ in cause and result.

Ṣarīʿ should therefore not always be translated as “killed,” “defeated,” or “a person with epilepsy.”

Does ṣarʿā mean unconscious people?

Not necessarily.

Someone may lose consciousness during an attack or a fall, but unconsciousness is not an essential part of the word’s meaning.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7 likewise does not concern temporary unconsciousness: its context concerns ʿĀd’s destruction.

Translating it as “the unconscious” therefore restricts and changes the verse’s meaning.

Does ṣarʿā mean the dead?

In this particular context, yes: the related verses concern the people’s destruction.

In a general definition of the root, no.

For precision, the basic translation can express the state of having fallen to the ground, with a contextual explanation clarifying that these people died.

This keeps the root’s lexical meaning distinct from one possible outcome.

Does ṣarʿ always involve an external force?

In transitive ṣaraʿa, the subject may throw someone else down.

Uses involving illness or being laid low do not require an external human agent.

All instances of ṣarʿ therefore cannot be confined to wrestling, war, or two opponents colliding.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7 makes this particularly clear: the cause of their fall is a destructive wind.

Can ṣarʿ be figurative?

Yes.

In Arabic, death can lay someone low, intoxication can bring them down, and literary usage can describe an internal or external force as though it throws a person to the ground.

The possibility of figurative use does not make ṣarʿ nonphysical in every verse.

In Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7, comparing the people’s bodies with fallen palm trunks strengthens the literal, bodily meaning.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

In Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7, the expression:

فَتَرَى الْقَوْمَ فِيهَا صَرْعَىٰ

fa-tarā al-qawma fīhā ṣarʿā

Is best rendered:

“Then you see the people fallen on the ground therein.”

“Then you see the people laid low” is also sound and fluent.

“Fallen on the ground,” however, conveys the word’s bodily image more clearly.

“Those killed” conveys this event’s certain outcome in context, but weakens the image of falling itself.

“The unconscious” is unsuitable because the verse concerns the people’s destruction.

“The defeated” alone is also insufficient: it may suggest only losing a confrontation without expressing their bodies’ fall.

The following equivalents suit forms outside the Qur’an:

  • Ṣaraʿahu: he threw him to the ground.
  • Ṣarīʿ: fallen on the ground, laid low.
  • Ṣāraʿahu: he wrestled with him.
  • Muṣāraʿah: wrestling.
  • Maṣraʿ: a place of falling; in a context of death, a place of destruction.
  • Miṣrāʿ: a door leaf or a poetic hemistich.

In medical use, al-ṣarʿ is the established name for epilepsy; that specialized meaning must not be transferred to ṣarʿā in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7.

10. Conclusion

The Arabic root Ṣ R ʿ expresses throwing to the ground and being laid low.

In wrestling uses, two people confront each other, and one may throw the other down.

The root is not confined to wrestling. Wind can lay vegetation or a tree flat; a person can be laid low by illness or an accident; and in war or destruction, ṣarīʿ can describe a dead body fallen on the ground.

The family also includes established Arabic words such as ṣirāʿ, muṣāraʿah, ṣarīʿ, maṣraʿ, and miṣrāʿ. Some, such as a door leaf and poetic hemistich, have acquired specialized uses and should not all be translated as falling to the ground.

Only ṣarʿā in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7 occurs in the Qur’an. It describes ʿĀd’s condition after being caught in a destructive wind.

Short definition of the root: Ṣ R ʿ basically expresses throwing a person or thing to the ground or being laid low; in contexts of war, illness, and destructive events, it can accompany defeat, falling, or death. Brief root meaning: Throwing to the ground and being laid low; death or defeat is established from context.

Ṣarʿā in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7 therefore means “those fallen on the ground,” whose death and destruction are also clear in that context.

The root’s main distinction is that falling, the cause of falling, and its eventual outcome are three different matters. The root expresses throwing down or being laid low; its cause and result must be established through the verse’s other words and context.
