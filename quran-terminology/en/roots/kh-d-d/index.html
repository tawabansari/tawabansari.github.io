From the cheek and facial hollows to an elongated trench in the ground: two distinct uses within one word family

In Arabic, the root KH D D is associated with the human cheek, clefts, and depressions in a surface. خَدّ (khadd) denotes the cheek or side of the face, while أُخْدُود (ukhdūd) denotes a long hollow or trench in the ground. خُدُود (khudūd), أَخَادِيد (akhādīd), and تَخَدُّد (takhaddud) belong to the same family.

Early Arabic also uses this root for marks left by flowing water on the ground, ruts in a road, whip marks on skin, and facial wrinkles. These examples involve some form of depression, cleft, or elongated mark on a surface.

The cheek and a cleft nevertheless differ. A cheek is a natural part of the face, whereas a cleft may result from digging, flowing water, impact, or erosion. Their visual resemblance alone does not establish that either use necessarily derives from the other.

The Qur’an uses this root in two forms. In Luqmān 31:18, خَدَّكَ (khaddaka) refers to the human cheek in a prohibition against arrogantly turning one’s face away from people. In Al-Burūj 85:4, الْأُخْدُودِ (al-ukhdūd) refers to a long cleft or trench subsequently associated with fire and the persecution of believers.

The two uses concern different subjects: one addresses conduct toward other people; the other describes an event involving persecution and violence. Both nevertheless belong to a family in which the cheek, the side, and elongated depressions are established Arabic senses.

1. Root forms and semantic core

KH D D occurs twice in the Qur’an, both times as a noun: خَدَّكَ (khaddaka) in Luqmān 31:18 and الْأُخْدُودِ (al-ukhdūd) in Al-Burūj 85:4.

Important Arabic members of this family include:

  • خَدّ (khadd): the cheek, the side of the human face; in another use, a cleft or furrow in the ground.
  • خَدَّان (khaddān): the two human cheeks.
  • خُدُود (khudūd): cheeks, the plural.
  • خَدَّ ـ يَخُدُّ (khadda–yakhuddu): to cut a cleft or furrow into the ground; in other uses, to leave an elongated mark on a surface.
  • خَدّ (khadd): also a verbal noun: cleaving or making a furrow.
  • أُخْدُود (ukhdūd): a long cleft, usually sunken into the ground.
  • أَخَادِيد (akhādīd): the plural of ukhdūd: long furrows or clefts.
  • خُدَّة (khuddah): a hollow or cleft made in the ground.
  • تَخَدَّدَ (takhaddada): to become wrinkled, hollowed, or lean, especially of the face or body.
  • تَخَدُّد (takhaddud): the appearance of folds, hollows, or signs of leanness on the body’s surface.
  • مُتَخَدِّد (mutakhaddid): a person or thing that has developed hollows or folds through leanness.
  • خِدَاد (khidād): in one early use, a branding implement or the mark made on an animal’s cheek.
  • مَخْدُود (makhdūd): something bearing a furrow, cleft, or distinctive mark.
  • مِخَدَّة (mikhaddah): a pillow on which the cheek rests.

These forms are recorded in lexicons, but only خَدّ (khadd) and أُخْدُود (ukhdūd) occur in the Qur’an.

The root’s fundamental meaning

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris proposes one underlying principle for this entry:

تَأَسُّلُ الشَّيْءِ وَامْتِدَادُهُ إِلَى السُّفْلِ

taʾassulu al-shayʾi wa-imtidāduhu ilā al-sufli

That is, something becoming slender and elongated, extending downward. In this explanation, taʾassul concerns slenderness and elongation; “depression” is not a direct translation of that word.

He then places the human cheek, a cleft in the ground, and bodily hollows caused by leanness within this family.

Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs al-Lughah, volume 2, page 149: khadd

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise identifies khadd with the two sides of the face and ukhdūd with a long, sunken cleft in the ground.

In Al-Mufradāt, Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī draws a visual connection between the human cheek and a cleft in the ground, treating the terrestrial use as an extension of the facial one.

These explanations help identify the word’s semantic field, but they do not present identical accounts of how its meanings developed.

For Ibn Fāris, slenderness and elongation, together with downward extension, form the common principle. For Al-Rāghib, the human cheek supplies the comparison, with its usage extended to the ground.

For example, the cheeks lie on either side of the nose, below the eyes, forming part of the face’s natural shape. An ukhdūd, by contrast, is a cleft extending through the ground and altering its surface.

A resemblance in shape can be perceived, but it does not establish that khadd originally meant only “cheek” and necessarily came to describe the ground, or conversely that the name of the cheek originated as a name for a trench.

Lexical evidence establishes more securely two recognized uses within one family: the cheek or side of the face, and an elongated cleft or depression in a surface.

Each Qur’anic form has its own specific meaning; one must not be translated in place of the other.

2. Material uses in early Arabic

The cheek and the two sides of the face

In Arabic, خَدّ (khadd) denotes the part of the face on either side of the nose, extending toward the ear and jaw.

Lexicons record differing descriptions of the cheek’s precise boundaries. Some describe the region from below the eye to the side of the mouth; others encompass a broader part of the side of the face.

In all these descriptions, however, the cheek is part of the face, rather than the whole head or face.

When someone turns their head toward another person, for example, the eyes, nose, and cheeks change direction together. The cheek nevertheless remains only one part of the face.

This distinction matters in Luqmān 31:18, where the Qur’an uses khadd instead of the more general wajh, “face.”

A natural expression may nevertheless mention turning the cheek to describe someone turning away. It does not imply that only the cheek’s skin moves while the other facial parts remain still.

مِخَدَّة (mikhaddah): a pillow on which the cheek rests

One familiar member of this family is مِخَدَّة (mikhaddah).

Lexicons describe it as a pillow on which a person places their cheek.

Its relationship to khadd is clear: it is an object on which the cheek rests.

This use shows that KH D D is not confined to clefts or wounds. The human cheek itself and objects associated with it also belong to the family’s range of uses.

The borrowed word mikhaddah is less common in present-day Persian, but it is an established Arabic word for a pillow.

خَدَّ الْأَرْضَ (khadda al-arḍa): cleaving the ground

In Arabic, خَدَّ الْأَرْضَ (khadda al-arḍa) means to make a cleft or furrow in the ground.

For example, if someone digs a long, deep line into the ground, the action may be described with khadda.

The resulting hollow may be called an أُخْدُود (ukhdūd).

Lexicons explain al-ukhdūd as شق في الأرض مستطيل (shaqq fī al-arḍ mustaṭīl): an elongated cleft in the ground.

Other explanations also emphasize its sunken character.

Ukhdūd should therefore not be applied indiscriminately to every small hole or irregular hollow. Lengthwise extension and depression are prominent features of its established definition.

The word alone, however, specifies no exact depth or dimensions. It may refer to a relatively shallow furrow or a deep trench; its size requires context and independent evidence.

Furrows made by flowing water

Lisān al-ʿArab records:

“خَدَّ السَّيْلُ فِي الْأَرْضِ” (khadda al-saylu fī al-arḍ).

This means that the flood, as it flowed, cut into the ground or made furrows in it.

The use shows that clefts in the ground do not always result from digging with human tools.

Flowing water can also move soil through motion and erosion, leaving furrows along its course.

Such furrows may deepen over time and establish the water’s course.

Here, khadd does not denote the water itself, but the effect it leaves on the ground.

Nor does this example establish that the ukhdūd in Al-Burūj 85:4 was necessarily formed by a flood. The subject and use of the word in Al-Burūj differ.

Marks left by ropes, hooves, and whips

In early Arabic, أَخَادِيد (akhādīd) was not restricted to furrows in soil.

Lisān al-ʿArab records أَخَادِيدُ الْأَرْشِيَةِ (akhādīd al-arshiyah) for marks left by well ropes on the well’s sides or edge.

A rope repeatedly drawn along the same course may leave grooves in stone or a softer material.

Similarly, خَدَّ الْفَرَسُ الْأَرْضَ (khadda al-farasu al-arḍa) describes the effect left by a horse’s hooves on the ground.

In another use, أَخَادِيدُ السِّيَاطِ (akhādīd al-siyāṭ) denotes elongated whip marks on skin.

In all these examples, contact, pressure, impact, or friction changes a surface, leaving something resembling a line or furrow.

The expression akhādīd al-siyāṭ does not, however, mean that ukhdūd itself lexically signifies torture or a whip.

A furrow may form in earth, stone, skin, or another surface. Whether making it involves harm or violence depends on the situation.

3. تَخَدُّد (takhaddud): facial folds and hollows in pre-Islamic poetry

A clear pre-Islamic poetic witness to this family occurs in the Muʿallaqah of Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd.

Describing a bright, beautiful face, Ṭarafah says:

وَوَجْهٌ كَأَنَّ الشَّمْسَ حَلَّتْ رِدَاءَهَا
عَلَيْهِ نَقِيُّ اللَّوْنِ لَمْ يَتَخَدَّدِ

wa-wajhun ka-anna al-shamsa ḥallat ridāʾahā / ʿalayhi naqiyyu al-lawni lam yatakhaddadi

Translation:

“And a face upon which the sun seems to have spread its robe of light: clear and bright in color, without folds or hollows developing upon it.”

Here, لَمْ يَتَخَدَّدِ (lam yatakhaddadi) describes the face.

The poet depicts a smooth, fresh face without visible signs of wrinkling or hollowing.

Lexical explanations and commentaries on the Muʿallaqah associate takhaddud with folds, hollows, or changes in the condition of facial flesh.

This use is close to the sense of a cleft or elongated mark in a surface.

As furrows develop in the ground, folds and hollows may develop on the face. The resemblance does not mean that facial folds and terrestrial clefts have identical causes or physical natures.

In one case, skin and bodily tissue change; in the other, soil or rock is cleft.

Their common feature is a change in surface shape and the appearance of a sunken or elongated mark.

The verse is recorded in Ṭarafah’s collected poetry and early commentaries on his Muʿallaqah. Ṭarafah is a pre-Islamic poet, so this witness offers clearer support for pre-Qur’anic usage than quotations known only from Umayyad or Abbasid poets.

Al-Khaṭīb al-Tabrīzī’s Sharḥ al-Qaṣāʾid al-ʿAshr: Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd’s witness

تَخَدَّدَ لَحْمُهُ (takhaddada laḥmuhu): becoming lean and losing flesh

Lexicons record تَخَدَّدَ لَحْمُهُ (takhaddada laḥmuhu) for flesh diminishing through leanness, with hollows or folds becoming visible.

Jarīr’s poetry also applies this usage to horses that have become lean.

Here, takhaddud concerns the loss of flesh itself and the effects appearing on the body’s surface.

Applied to a human, mutakhaddid may therefore describe someone whose face or body has become hollowed or folded through leanness.

The description must not be restricted to one particular illness. Leanness may have different causes; the root itself does not identify the cause.

Nor is loss of flesh the direct meaning of khaddaka in Luqmān 31:18. There, the cheek is a facial part, rather than a facial wrinkle or illness.

4. Luqmān 31:18: the cheek and arrogant conduct

وَلَا تُصَعِّرْ خَدَّكَ لِلنَّاسِ وَلَا تَمْشِ فِي الْأَرْضِ مَرَحًا إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يُحِبُّ كُلَّ مُخْتَالٍ فَخُورٍ

wa-lā tuṣaʿʿir khaddaka li-l-nāsi wa-lā tamshi fī al-arḍi maraḥan inna allāha lā yuḥibbu kulla mukhtālin fakhūrin

Proposed translation in this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Do not turn your cheek away from people, and do not walk upon the earth exultantly; surely The God does not love any conceited boaster.”

Luqmān 31:18

In this verse, خَدَّكَ (khaddaka) refers to the addressee’s cheek.

To understand the sentence, however, it must be considered together with the verb تُصَعِّرْ (tuṣaʿʿir).

Tuṣaʿʿir belongs to Ṣ ʿ R, rather than KH D D.

In Arabic, صَعَر (ṣaʿar) concerns twisting of the neck or head; early uses also describe a condition in a camel’s neck that causes the animal to turn its head aside.

This image underlies the expression of turning the cheek, or turning away from another person, out of arrogance.

The blameworthy meaning in Luqmān 31:18 therefore does not come from khadd alone. Khadd names the facial part; tuṣaʿʿir describes the kind of movement.

Why does the Qur’an use خَدَّكَ (khaddaka)?

The cheek is one of the facial parts visible when people encounter one another.

Turning one’s face away during conversation may acquire a social meaning.

Someone listening to a poor or less powerful person might, for example, turn away with contempt.

In that situation, a bodily movement accompanies an arrogant attitude.

Not every turn of the head or cheek is arrogant, however.

Someone may turn their head to see something, hear a sound, or answer another person. Pain, physical disability, or a neck condition may prevent someone from keeping their face in a particular direction.

None of these circumstances constitutes arrogance merely because the face changes direction.

Luqmān 31:18 condemns contemptuous, self-important conduct, as indicated by tuṣaʿʿir, the people toward whom the conduct is directed in li-l-nās, and the remainder of the verse.

لِلنَّاسِ (li-l-nās): conduct toward people

The expression وَلَا تُصَعِّرْ خَدَّكَ لِلنَّاسِ (wa-lā tuṣaʿʿir khaddaka li-l-nās) includes the word النَّاسِ (al-nās).

It identifies the people addressed or affected by the conduct.

The verse concerns dealing with people, rather than only family members, friends, or those who share one’s beliefs.

Respectful conduct toward human beings therefore matters in an ethical explanation of this sentence.

Khadd itself does not mean respect for people. That ethical principle emerges from the command and its context.

The connection with مُخْتَالٍ فَخُورٍ (mukhtāl fakhūr)

The Qur’an continues:

“إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يُحِبُّ كُلَّ مُخْتَالٍ فَخُورٍ” (inna allāha lā yuḥibbu kulla mukhtālin fakhūr).

This sentence further clarifies the reason for condemning the preceding conduct.

Mukhtāl concerns conceit and self-display, while fakhūr concerns boasting.

Arrogantly turning the cheek is consequently one outward manifestation of conceit in this context.

The verse mentions both facial movement and the manner of walking upon the earth.

Their conjunction shows that arrogance can appear in everyday conduct: how people deal with others, their facial expression, and their way of moving.

Khadd itself must nevertheless not be defined as arrogance.

The human cheek has no inherent positive or negative moral value. What is condemned is using one’s facial posture to belittle others.

Does tuṣaʿʿir khaddaka concern outward appearance alone?

Directly, the verse describes observable conduct: turning the cheek and face.

Its context makes clear, however, that the movement is condemned because of an arrogant attitude.

The subject should therefore not be reduced to the angle at which the head is held.

Someone might keep their face toward a listener yet belittle them through speech or conduct. Such behavior may also conflict with the verse’s ethical principle.

This ethical application comes from context, rather than from khadd independently meaning contempt, pride, or disregard.

5. Al-Burūj 85:4: the trench and the event involving fire

قُتِلَ أَصْحَابُ الْأُخْدُودِ

qutila aṣḥābu al-ukhdūdi

Proposed translations of this verse and the accompanying verses in this study, without changing the installed translations:

“Death to the people of the trench.”

Al-Burūj 85:4

Here, الْأُخْدُودِ (al-ukhdūd) denotes a long cleft or trench in the ground.

To understand its setting, the following verses should be read alongside the fourth:

النَّارِ ذَاتِ الْوَقُودِ

al-nāri dhāti al-waqūdi

“The fire supplied with fuel.”

Al-Burūj 85:5

إِذْ هُمْ عَلَيْهَا قُعُودٌ

idh hum ʿalayhā quʿūdun

“When they sat beside it.”

Al-Burūj 85:6

وَهُمْ عَلَىٰ مَا يَفْعَلُونَ بِالْمُؤْمِنِينَ شُهُودٌ

wa-hum ʿalā mā yafʿalūna bi-al-muʾminīna shuhūdun

“And they witnessed what they were doing to the believers.”

Al-Burūj 85:7

وَمَا نَقَمُوا مِنْهُمْ إِلَّا أَنْ يُؤْمِنُوا بِاللَّهِ الْعَزِيزِ الْحَمِيدِ

wa-mā naqamū minhum illā an yuʾminū bi-allāhi al-ʿazīzi al-ḥamīdi

“They resented them for nothing except their believing in The God, the Mighty, the Praiseworthy.”

Al-Burūj 85:8

Together, these verses place the ukhdūd within an event involving fire and the persecution of believers.

The lexical meaning of ukhdūd must nevertheless be distinguished from the event’s features.

أُخْدُود (ukhdūd): an elongated cleft in the ground

أُخْدُود (ukhdūd) is formed from KH D D on the pattern أُفْعُول (ufʿūl).

Lexicons describe it as a long, sunken cleft in the ground.

Its plural is أَخَادِيد (akhādīd).

Depending on context, Persian equivalents include khandaq, “trench,” gowdāl-e derāz, “long pit,” and shiyār-e ʿamīq, “deep furrow.”

In Al-Burūj 85:4, “long pit” makes the word’s material sense clear.

Ukhdūd itself contains no meaning of fire, human beings, or burning.

Those ideas come from the subsequent verses.

A cleft dug in the ground to carry water, for example, can fall within the semantic field of ukhdūd without involving any fire or violence.

Translating ukhdūd as “a furnace for burning believers” therefore blends the lexical meaning with the verse’s particular referent.

“Pit” or “trench” is more appropriate for a direct translation; the fire and perpetrators of violence belong in the contextual discussion.

أَصْحَابُ الْأُخْدُودِ (aṣḥāb al-ukhdūd): who are they?

Aṣḥāb al-ukhdūd consists of two components.

Aṣḥāb is the plural of ṣāḥib. Here it denotes a group associated with the ukhdūd.

Al-ukhdūd names the cleft or trench itself.

The expression alone does not determine whether these people are its makers, guards, victims, or people known through the event.

Identifying the title’s referent requires examining the subsequent verses.

Al-Burūj 85:6 and Al-Burūj 85:7 describe a group sitting beside the fire and witnessing what they do to the believers.

Al-Burūj 85:8 then explains that they resented the believers for nothing except their faith in God.

These indications favor understanding “the people of the trench” as the perpetrators of persecution and those overseeing it.

Early exegetical sources also present this reading.

Some commentators, however, apply the title to those trapped or killed in the trench.

This disagreement shows why the referent of aṣḥāb cannot be determined from that word alone.

Here, the context of Al-Burūj 85:5, Al-Burūj 85:6, Al-Burūj 85:7, and Al-Burūj 85:8 provides clearer support for preferring the reading concerning the perpetrators of violence.

Al-Ṭabarī: differing identifications of the title and pronouns in the trench event

قُتِلَ (qutila): a report of killing or condemnation and imprecation?

The verse begins:

“قُتِلَ أَصْحَابُ الْأُخْدُودِ” (qutila aṣḥābu al-ukhdūd).

Qutila belongs to Q T L and is passive in form.

In Qur’anic Arabic, this construction may report someone’s being killed or function as an imprecation and forceful condemnation.

For example, ʿAbasa 80:17 states:

قُتِلَ الْإِنْسَانُ مَا أَكْفَرَهُ

qutila al-insānu mā akfarahu

The expression occurs in a context condemning an ungrateful human being; it need not report a historical event in which a particular person was killed.

In Al-Burūj 85:4 too, “May they perish” or “Death to” fits the condemnatory construction and the verse’s context.

“Death to the people of the trench” is therefore an appropriate translation.

This discussion concerns Q T L, however, rather than the meaning of ukhdūd.

For a study of KH D D, the point is that the title aṣḥāb al-ukhdūd alone should not lead readers to assume that all its referents were necessarily killed in that trench.

النَّارِ ذَاتِ الْوَقُودِ (al-nār dhāt al-waqūd): the relationship between trench and fire

The fifth verse mentions fire supplied with fuel.

In the common reading, al-nār is genitive and connected with al-ukhdūd. One established grammatical analysis treats it as badal ishtimāl, an appositive identifying something encompassed by the referent: the fire within the trench.

This construction makes fire a feature of that particular event.

Waqūd belongs to W Q D, whose semantic field includes fuel and kindling.

Ukhdūd and nār are therefore two distinct words.

The trench is a material location; the fire is something kindled within it or associated with it.

This distinction must be preserved in translation and lexical explanation.

Al-Qurṭubī: imprecation, the meaning of ukhdūd, and apposition

Does the Qur’an identify the event’s exact location?

Historical and exegetical reports associate the event of the people of the trench with different regions and groups.

One of the best-known identifications concerns the persecution of Christians in Najrān, in the southern Arabian Peninsula, and its association with the kingdom of Ḥimyar.

Other sources include accounts involving the Children of Israel or other groups.

Al-Burūj itself, however, names neither Najrān nor Yemen, nor a particular king or date.

An explicit Qur’anic statement must therefore be distinguished from attempts to identify its historical referent.

A historical account may fit the Qur’anic description closely, but that agreement does not itself establish that every detail of the account appears in the Qur’an.

In a root study, historical disagreement should not obscure the clear meaning of ukhdūd.

Lexically, the word remains a long cleft or trench in the ground, whether its historical referent was in Najrān or elsewhere.

Ibn ʿĀshūr: exegetical readings and historical accounts

Condemning persecution on account of belief

Al-Burūj 85:8 identifies the perpetrators’ reason for hostility:

“إِلَّا أَنْ يُؤْمِنُوا بِاللَّهِ الْعَزِيزِ الْحَمِيدِ” (illā an yuʾminū bi-allāhi al-ʿazīzi al-ḥamīd).

That is, they were hostile toward the believers only because of their faith.

This passage clarifies the event’s moral character.

It concerns persecuting people on account of their beliefs, rather than an ordinary pit or a natural event.

Al-Burūj 85:10 also states:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ فَتَنُوا الْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَالْمُؤْمِنَاتِ ثُمَّ لَمْ يَتُوبُوا فَلَهُمْ عَذَابُ جَهَنَّمَ وَلَهُمْ عَذَابُ الْحَرِيقِ

inna alladhīna fatanū al-muʾminīna wa-al-muʾmināti thumma lam yatūbū fa-lahum ʿadhābu jahannama wa-lahum ʿadhābu al-ḥarīqi

Proposed translation in this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Surely those who persecuted believing men and believing women, then did not repent, shall have the punishment of Hell and the punishment of burning.”

Al-Burūj 85:10

This verse relates the perpetrators’ responsibility to their conduct.

The qualification ثُمَّ لَمْ يَتُوبُوا (thumma lam yatūbū), “then did not repent,” also matters. The verse identifies this outcome for those who committed such acts and subsequently failed to repent.

The qualification must not be omitted, nor the verse’s judgment generalized without accounting for it.

These matters nevertheless concern the surah’s moral message and context, rather than the lexical definition of ukhdūd.

Ukhdūd itself does not mean oppression, religious persecution, or torture. Those meanings arise from the event the Qur’an describes.

6. Distinctions from related roots

KH D D (khadd) and W J H (wajh): cheek and face

In Arabic, wajh denotes the face; extended uses may also concern direction and orientation.

Khadd, however, is a specific part of the face: the cheek.

Luqmān 31:18 uses khaddaka rather than wajhaka.

The choice foregrounds the image of turning the cheek and the face’s posture.

In practical conduct, turning the cheek nevertheless accompanies a change in the face’s direction.

A contextual explanation may therefore speak of turning away, while a precise translation of the word should preferably retain “cheek.”

Study of W J H

KH D D (khadd) and Ṣ ʿ R (ṣaʿar): the facial part and its turning

These two roots appear together in Luqmān 31:18.

Khadd denotes the cheek.

Ṣaʿar concerns twisting or inclining the head and neck; in social usage it can express arrogantly turning away.

Khadd names the part, while tuṣaʿʿir is the verb describing its movement.

Arrogance and contempt emerge here from their relationship with li-l-nās and the rest of the verse.

Defining khadd alone as “arrogance” would erase the real distinction between the two words.

KH D D (ukhdūd) and Ḥ F R (ḥafr): the resulting cleft and the act of digging

Ḥafr concerns digging into the ground and making a hollow.

Ukhdūd, however, names a type of long cleft or trench.

Digging may produce an ukhdūd, but ukhdūd itself does not mean “digging.”

Put simply, ḥafr can name the action, and ukhdūd one of its results.

A cleft can also form through flowing water, so not every ukhdūd is necessarily made by humans.

In Al-Burūj 85:4, the word denotes the trench itself; the verse does not explicitly describe how it was made.

Study of Ḥ F R

KH D D (ukhdūd) and SH Q Q (shaqq): a cleft generally and an elongated cleft

Shaqq in Arabic concerns cleaving or splitting apart.

This root has a broad range of uses, including cleaving the earth, sky, a body, or other things.

Ukhdūd is also a kind of cleft, but its lexical definition foregrounds lengthwise extension and depression in the ground.

Every material ukhdūd is therefore a cleft, but not every cleft is called an ukhdūd.

A small crack in a wall, for example, may be a shaqq without resembling an ukhdūd in shape or size.

Study of SH Q Q

KH D D (ukhdūd) and KH N D Q (khandaq): an elongated hollow and a defensive trench

Arabic khandaq denotes a long hollow or trench, often dug for a purpose such as defense or creating a barrier.

This word is also familiar in Persian.

Ukhdūd can overlap with khandaq in some uses, but its meaning is not restricted to military or defensive purposes.

Water can form an ukhdūd, and an eroded road may also have akhādīd.

“Trench” is therefore an intelligible translation in Al-Burūj 85:4, but it must not imply that the trench was necessarily a defensive structure.

In that surah, the trench is associated with fire and the persecution of believers, rather than defense of a city or fortress.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

For خَدَّكَ (khaddaka) in Luqmān 31:18, the most appropriate Persian equivalent is gūneh-at, “your cheek.”

It preserves the direct meaning of khadd.

A proposed translation of the full verse is:

“Do not turn your cheek away from people, and do not walk upon the earth exultantly; surely The God does not love any conceited boaster.”

A contextual explanation should make clear that the movement is associated in the verse with arrogance and contempt for others.

“Cheek” must not, however, be directly translated as “pride,” “arrogance,” or “disregard.”

For الْأُخْدُودِ (al-ukhdūd) in Al-Burūj 85:4, gowdāl, “pit,” and khandaq, “trench,” are appropriate Persian equivalents.

To make the lexical feature clearer, “long pit” is more precise.

As a full verse translation, “Death to the people of the trench” is natural and brief.

Fire, burning, and the persecution of believers emerge from the subsequent verses and must not be inserted into ukhdūd itself.

Brief root meaning and each word’s meaning in its verse

خَدَّكَ (khaddaka): Luqmān 31:18

Root: KH D D.

Grammar: the singular noun خَدّ (khadd) with the attached pronoun كَ (ka); in the sentence, the object of تُصَعِّرْ (tuṣaʿʿir).

Brief root meaning: the cheek and side of the face; in other uses of this family, elongated furrows and depressions.

Meaning in this verse: the addressee’s cheek, which they are forbidden to turn arrogantly away from people.

الْأُخْدُودِ (al-ukhdūd): Al-Burūj 85:4

Root: KH D D.

Grammar: a definite singular noun on the pattern أُفْعُول (ufʿūl), genitive as the construct complement in أَصْحَابُ الْأُخْدُودِ (aṣḥāb al-ukhdūd).

Brief root meaning: a long, sunken cleft, furrow, or trench.

Meaning in this verse: the pit or trench associated with fire and the persecution of believers in the event described in Al-Burūj.

These two uses reveal the word family’s shared field while preserving each form’s own meaning.

8. Conclusion

In Arabic, KH D D concerns the human cheek and elongated clefts or depressions. Early uses occur in furrows in the ground, marks left by flowing water, whip marks, facial folds, and the name of a pillow.

Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd’s pre-Islamic witness shows takhaddud being used for folds and hollows developing in the face. Lexicons also define ukhdūd as a long cleft in the ground.

The root has two Qur’anic uses.

In Luqmān 31:18, khaddaka is the human cheek within a sentence condemning arrogant, contemptuous conduct. Khadd itself does not mean arrogance; that meaning comes from the verb and context.

In Al-Burūj 85:4, al-ukhdūd names a pit or elongated cleft in the ground. The subsequent verses connect it with fire and the persecution of believers, but the word itself does not mean fire, torture, or killing.

Nor does the root determine the people of the trench’s exact historical identity. Reports differ concerning the event’s location and perpetrators, while the Qur’an itself names no city, king, or date.

Final definition: In Arabic, KH D D concerns the cheek and side of the face, as well as elongated clefts and depressions in a surface. Khadd in Luqmān 31:18 denotes the human cheek, while ukhdūd in Al-Burūj 85:4 denotes a pit or long cleft in the ground. Their visual relationship is apparent, but the historical direction of derivation is uncertain. Each verse’s ethical or historical meaning must be understood from its construction and context, rather than from the root’s independent meaning. Brief root meaning: The cheek and side of the face; in other uses, a long furrow or hollow.
