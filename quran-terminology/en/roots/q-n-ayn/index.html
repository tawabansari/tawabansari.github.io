1. Basic meaning and linguistic history

In transmitted early Arabic poetry, the Q N ʿ family expresses asking for assistance and a person in need approaching others. Lexical sources and clear Qur’anic usage also attest the branch concerning raising the head. However, al-Shammākh’s verse about camels’ mouths should not be treated as evidence for that meaning without examining its lexical explanation.

In classical Arabic, قَانِع (qāniʿ) describes both someone who asks and someone content with what they have. These meanings share the same active-participle form, but the common lexical distinction derives them from differently vocalized verbs: قَنَعَ (qanaʿa) for asking and قَنِعَ (qaniʿa) for being content. The identical form qāniʿ does not, by itself, determine which meaning a sentence intends.

The common dictionary distinction is as follows. Nevertheless, a meaning of contentment is also recorded for some verbal nouns, including qunūʿ, so the division is not absolute throughout Arabic usage:

  • قَنَعَ يَقْنَعُ قُنُوعًا (qanaʿa, yaqnaʿu, qunūʿan): asking and turning to another for help with a need.
  • قَنِعَ يَقْنَعُ قَنَاعَةً (qaniʿa, yaqnaʿu, qanāʿatan): being content and satisfied with what one has.
  • أَقْنَعَ رَأْسَهُ (aqnaʿa raʾsahu): he raised his head.

Another branch concerns covering the head, a face covering, or a covering around something.

Qur’anic forms examined: The root occurs twice, in Ibrāhīm 14:43 and Al-Ḥajj 22:36. Three additional complete verses in this article provide context and comparison.

الْقَانِعَ (al-qāniʿa): someone asking for assistance, or a contented person.

مُقْنِعِي (muqniʿī): those raising their heads.

Forms including قَنَاعَة (qanāʿah), قُنُوع (qunūʿ), قِنَاع (qināʿ), إِقْنَاع (iqnāʿ), اِقْتِنَاع (iqtināʿ), and تَقَنُّع (taqannuʿ) are known in Arabic but do not occur in these forms in the Qur’an.

2. Evidence from early Arabic

Qunūʿ: asking others for assistance

A verse attributed to ʿAdī ibn Zayd al-ʿIbādī, a pre-Islamic poet of the late sixth century, reads:

وَمَا خُنْتُ ذَا عَهْدٍ وَأُبْتُ بِعَهْدِهِ وَلَمْ أَحْرِمِ الْمُضْطَرَّ إِذْ جَاءَ قَانِعًا

“I did not betray one bound by a covenant, but kept his covenant; nor did I deprive the distressed person when he came asking.”

Lexicons explain qāniʿan in this verse as someone asking for assistance. Its association with distress and approaching another person fits that meaning. Attribution to a pre-Islamic poet supplies transmitted evidence of this usage’s history, rather than independent proof of the precise date of composition.

Al-Shammākh ibn Ḍirār, who lived through both the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods, also says:

لَمَالُ الْمَرْءِ يُصْلِحُهُ فَيُغْنِي مَفَاقِرَهُ أَعَفُّ مِنَ الْقُنُوعِ

“Wealth that a person manages so that it meets his needs is more conducive to self-restraint than asking others.”

The poem emphasizes managing property properly and using it to meet life’s needs. Al-qunūʿ here means asking people for help, rather than being content with existing possessions.

The poem’s precise date is unknown, but its use of the word for asking is clear.

Qanāʿah: contentment and sufficiency

In classical Arabic, قَنِعَ بِمَا عِنْدَهُ (qaniʿa bi-mā ʿindahu) means being content with what one possesses.

Contentment is not identical with abundant wealth or having no needs. Someone with little may be content, while someone wealthy may remain dissatisfied.

To connect these branches, Ibn Fāris proposes “turning toward”: someone in need turns toward the person asked, while a contented person turns with satisfaction toward what they possess. Another lexical explanation calls the petitioner qāniʿ because they accept whatever is given, little or much. These are analyses connecting meanings, not historical proof that one necessarily developed from the other. Not every petitioner is contented, and not every contented person asks.

Raising the head and distinguishing a description of the mouth

In poetry transmitted from al-Shammākh, camels approaching thorn trees are described as follows:

يُبَاكِرْنَ الْعِضَاهَ بِمُقْنَعَاتٍ نَوَاجِذُهُنَّ كَالْحِدَإِ الْوَقِيعِ

“They go early to the thorn trees with mouths whose teeth incline inward; their molars are like sharp axes.”

Lisān al-ʿArab cites this verse for فَمٌ مُقْنَع (famun muqnaʿ): a mouth with teeth inclined inward and effective at cutting. In this transmission, muqnaʿāt therefore does not directly attest raising the head. The first hemistich is also transmitted with yubādirna. Al-ḥidaʾ here means axes; a variant with fatḥah on the ḥāʾ is also recorded.

In Arabic, أَقْنَعَ رَأْسَهُ (aqnaʿa raʾsahu) means raising the head and directing it toward something. Raising the voice and extending the hands in supplication are also expressed through this family.

Ibn Fāris relates turning toward something, asking, and contentment within one semantic analysis, but also treats height and raising as potentially a separate branch. Such lexical classification should not be equated with a definitive historical reconstruction of the root’s origin.

Qināʿ and covering

قِنَاع (qināʿ) names a covering worn on the head; in some uses it also covers the face.

تَقَنَّعَ (taqannaʿa) means putting on a qināʿ, while قَنَّعَهُ (qannaʿahu) means covering someone’s head with it.

In modern Arabic, qināʿ also names masks and coverings that conceal the face.

A common historical origin connecting covering with contentment, asking, and raising the head has not been conclusively established. Neither Qur’anic occurrence concerns covering the head or face.

3. Muqniʿī in the Qur’an: raising the heads

In Ibrāhīm, the Qur’an describes the condition of wrongdoers on the Day of Reckoning:

وَلا تَحسَبَنَّ اللَّهَ غٰفِلًا عَمّا يَعمَلُ الظّٰلِمونَ إِنَّما يُؤَخِّرُهُم لِيَومٍ تَشخَصُ فيهِ الأَبصٰرُ

Wa-lā taḥsabanna Allāha ghāfilan ʿammā yaʿmalu al-ẓālimūna innamā yuʾakhkhiruhum li-yawmin tashkhaṣu fīhi al-abṣār.

“Never suppose that The God is unaware of what the wrongdoers do. He only defers them until a day when eyes will stare fixedly.”

Ibrahim 14:42

مُهطِعينَ مُقنِعى رُءوسِهِم لا يَرتَدُّ إِلَيهِم طَرفُهُم وَأَفـِٔدَتُهُم هَواءٌ

Muhṭiʿīna muqniʿī ruʾūsihim lā yartaddu ilayhim ṭarfuhum wa-afʾidatuhum hawāʾ.

“Hastening, with their heads raised, their gaze not returning to them, and their hearts empty.”

Ibrahim 14:43

مُقْنِعِي (muqniʿī) is the sound masculine plural of مُقْنِع (muqniʿ), the active participle of Form IV aqnaʿa. It is accusative here; its final nūn is omitted because it forms a construct with ruʾūsihim, “their heads.”

Four conditions appear together: rapid movement, raised heads, an unreturning gaze, and empty hearts.

Raising the heads is expressed by Q N ʿ; hastening by H Ṭ ʿ; and the condition of the gaze by the following phrase.

Some early explanations also give lowering the head as a meaning of iqnāʿ. Raising the head, however, is more widely recognized in lexical sources and fits the preceding verse’s image of fixed staring.

In this context, raised heads do not indicate dignity, pride, or satisfaction. The verses describe people confronting the outcome of their deeds, unable to turn their gaze from what lies before them.

Fear comes from the context, not from the independent meaning of raising the head. Raising the head can occur in different situations; the setting determines its significance in this portrayal of the Day of Reckoning.

4. Al-qāniʿ in the Qur’an: sharing sacrificial meat

The root’s other occurrence is in Al-Ḥajj:

وَالبُدنَ جَعَلنٰها لَكُم مِن شَعٰئِرِ اللَّهِ لَكُم فيها خَيرٌ فَاذكُرُوا اسمَ اللَّهِ عَلَيها صَوافَّ فَإِذا وَجَبَت جُنوبُها فَكُلوا مِنها وَأَطعِمُوا القانِعَ وَالمُعتَرَّ كَذٰلِكَ سَخَّرنٰها لَكُم لَعَلَّكُم تَشكُرونَ

Wa-l-budna jaʿalnāhā lakum min shaʿāʾiri Allāhi lakum fīhā khayrun fa-udhkurū isma Allāhi ʿalayhā ṣawāffa fa-idhā wajabat junūbuhā fa-kulū minhā wa-aṭʿimū al-qāniʿa wa-l-muʿtarra kadhālika sakhkharnāhā lakum laʿallakum tashkurūn.

“We have made the camels among the sacred symbols of The God for you; in them is good for you. So mention the name of The God over them as they stand in rows. When their sides fall, eat from them and feed the one who asks and the one who approaches for a share. Thus We have subjected them to you, so that you may be grateful.”

Al-Hajj 22:36

الْقَانِعَ (al-qāniʿa) is a singular active participle and object of aṭʿimū, “feed.” Al-muʿtarr is coordinated with it.

The main interpretive question is whether qāniʿ belongs to the qunūʿ branch, asking, or the qanāʿah branch, being content and satisfied.

First reading: qāniʿ as someone asking

On this reading, qāniʿ is someone who expresses a need and asks another for assistance.

مُعْتَرّ (muʿtarr), from ʿ R R, describes someone approaching another for assistance or a share. That person may state the request or simply make the need apparent by being present.

On this reading, the verse distinguishes asking from approaching to receive a share.

One person may approach a distributor of food and request a portion. Another may appear at the same place but say nothing out of embarrassment, self-respect, or restraint.

ʿAdī ibn Zayd’s poetic example, in which qāniʿ describes a needy person coming for assistance, provides clear support for this reading.

Second reading: qāniʿ as a contented person

On this reading, qāniʿ is someone satisfied with what they have who does not take the initiative to seek help.

Muʿtarr, by contrast, is someone who approaches the person with provisions to receive something.

This reading also has lexical support, since Arabic uses qāniʿ for someone contented and satisfied.

Under this interpretation, the command includes not only those present at the distribution of sacrificial meat, but also people in need who do not themselves approach to ask.

The translation adopted in this study

This study renders al-qāniʿ in Al-Ḥajj 22:36 as “the one who asks.” The choice rests on the attested use of qanaʿa for asking, ʿAdī’s poetic example, and its pairing with al-muʿtarr: first, someone who asks; then, someone who approaches for a share, without approaching necessarily involving a spoken request. This is a clear translation preference, not a claim that the contented-person reading is invalid. Poetry confirms that a meaning is possible, but does not by itself settle the meaning of a verse.

On either reading, the Qur’anic command concerns sharing food with others; a share of the sacrifice should not be restricted to those who openly state their need.

The same passage of the surah also says:

لِيَشهَدوا مَنٰفِعَ لَهُم وَيَذكُرُوا اسمَ اللَّهِ فى أَيّامٍ مَعلومٰتٍ عَلىٰ ما رَزَقَهُم مِن بَهيمَةِ الأَنعٰمِ فَكُلوا مِنها وَأَطعِمُوا البائِسَ الفَقيرَ

Li-yashhadū manāfiʿa lahum wa-yadhkurū isma Allāhi fī ayyāmin maʿlūmātin ʿalā mā razaqahum min bahīmati al-anʿāmi fa-kulū minhā wa-aṭʿimū al-bāʾisa al-faqīr.

“So that they may witness benefits for themselves and mention the name of The God on known days over the livestock He has provided for them. Eat from them and feed the distressed poor person.”

Al-Hajj 22:28

This verse explicitly places feeding people in need within the rites of Hajj.

5. Iqnāʿ and iqtināʿ in Arabic

In addition to its use concerning raising the head, أَقْنَعَهُ (aqnaʿahu) is recorded with the meaning of satisfying another person.

In modern usage, iqnāʿ often means persuading someone through explanation, reasoning, or evidence.

اِقْتَنَعَ بِالْأَمْرِ (iqtanaʿa bi-l-amr) likewise means accepting something and becoming convinced of its correctness or sufficiency.

Intellectual conviction and contentment with material circumstances share an element of acceptance and satisfaction, but concern different objects. One involves satisfaction with possessions or provisions; the other, acceptance of a statement, argument, or conclusion.

This usage must not be imposed on muqniʿī ruʾūsihim in the Qur’an. In Ibrāhīm 14:43, muqniʿī does not mean people who have been persuaded or who persuade others; it describes the position of their heads.

6. Comparison with related roots

Q N ʿ (qanāʿah) and R Ḍ W / R Ḍ Y (riḍā): contentment and satisfaction

Riḍā has a broad semantic field encompassing acceptance and satisfaction with a person, conduct, decision, or situation.

In relation to life’s provisions, qanāʿah more particularly concerns being satisfied with what one possesses or receives and considering it sufficient.

Someone can be pleased with another person’s conduct without that pleasure concerning contentment with property or material circumstances.

Contentment does not itself mean abandoning efforts to improve one’s life. Satisfaction with present resources can coexist with efforts to obtain better ones.

Q N ʿ (qunūʿ) and S ʾ L (suʾāl): seeking assistance and asking

Suʾāl has a broad field encompassing both questions seeking knowledge and requests to receive something.

In its asking branch, qunūʿ more particularly concerns turning to others for help in meeting a need.

Someone asking about a word’s meaning is a sāʾil, but that question is not necessarily called qunūʿ.

Someone in need who approaches another for food or financial assistance, however, also fits the asking sense of qāniʿ.

Q N ʿ (qāniʿ) and ʿ R R (muʿtarr): asking and approaching

On the qunūʿ reading, qāniʿ concerns asking, while muʿtarr concerns approaching and presenting oneself to someone to receive a share.

The same person may both approach and ask. Approaching without expressing a request is also possible.

Neither word by itself determines the person’s wealth, degree of poverty, or social standing.

Q N ʿ (iqnāʿ) and Q M Ḥ (iqmāḥ): raising the head and having it held up

The Qur’an says in Yā Sīn 36:8:

إِنّا جَعَلنا فى أَعنٰقِهِم أَغلٰلًا فَهِىَ إِلَى الأَذقانِ فَهُم مُقمَحونَ

Innā jaʿalnā fī aʿnāqihim aghlālan fa-hiya ilā al-adhqāni fa-hum muqmaḥūn.

“We have placed shackles on their necks, reaching to their chins, so their heads are held up by force.”

Ya-Sin 36:8

Here مُقْمَحُونَ (muqmaḥūn), from Q M Ḥ, describes people whose heads are held up by an obstruction.

In Ibrāhīm 14:43, muqniʿī ruʾūsihim describes people who raise their heads.

The morphological distinction is clear: muqniʿ is a Form IV active participle, whereas muqmaḥ is a Form IV passive participle from a different root.

In Yā Sīn, shackles restrict the movement of the head; the verse in Ibrāhīm mentions no such implement.

Despite the outward similarity of head position, the compulsion and restriction caused by shackles in Yā Sīn should not be transferred into the lexical meaning of iqnāʿ in Ibrāhīm.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Muqniʿī ruʾūsihim in Ibrāhīm 14:43 should be translated as “with their heads raised” or “raising their heads.”

The adopted translation of al-qāniʿ in Al-Ḥajj 22:36 is “the one who asks.” “The contented person” remains an alternative reading in the explanation; the translation itself does not join the two with “or.”

The Persian equivalent gedā is unsuitable because it often carries a disparaging tone and can conflate asking for help in need with begging as a way of life.

Outside the Qur’an, qanāʿah generally means contentment and sufficiency; qunūʿ in the asking branch means seeking assistance; qināʿ means a head covering or mask; and iqnāʿ means satisfying someone or raising the head, according to construction and context. Vocalization, word formation, and the accompanying phrase must be examined; one equivalent cannot be imposed on every form.

8. Conclusion

Q N ʿ has several Arabic semantic branches: contentment and sufficiency, asking and turning to another for help, raising the head, and covering the head with a qināʿ.

The Qur’an uses this family in two different contexts. In Ibrāhīm, raising the heads forms part of the portrayal of wrongdoers on the Day of Reckoning. In Al-Ḥajj, qāniʿ names one of those who should receive sacrificial meat.

Asking is a meaning attested in transmitted poetry and Arabic dictionaries, and is this study’s choice for qāniʿ in Al-Ḥajj. The meaning of a contented, satisfied person also remains valid. The difference between the two verbs and their identical active participles helps explain the form’s ambiguity.

Proposed definition for Forqan: Being content and satisfied with what one possesses or receives; asking and turning to another for help; and, in the head-raising branch, raising and directing the head toward something. Qināʿ and covering the head are other recognized uses of this family.
