The Qur’anic Root K D Ḥ (ك د ح)

Strenuous effort and humanity’s encounter with its deeds

Qur’anic forms

كَدْحًا

1. Meaning in early Arabic

ك د ح concerns working, strenuous effort, earning, and scratching a surface.

In human life, كَدْح denotes the effort borne in carrying out a task or reaching an objective, particularly the effort of earning a living and meeting needs.

A verse attributed to Ibn Muqbil, who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, reads:

وَمَا الدَّهْرُ إِلَّا تَارَتَانِ فَمِنْهُمَا أَمُوتُ وَأُخْرَى أَبْتَغِي الْعَيْشَ أَكْدَحُ

Time has but two conditions: in one I die; in the other I seek my livelihood, striving and toiling.

أَكْدَحُ describes the effort the poet expends to sustain life. الْعَيْش identifies its purpose.

Although included in Ibn Muqbil’s collected poetry, the line’s exact date is unknown, and some sources give another attribution. It is evidence from early Arabic poetic tradition, not securely dated pre-Islamic testimony.

Arabic lexicons also record:

  • كَدَحَ لِأَهْلِهِ: he toiled to support his family.
  • كَدَحَ فِي عَمَلِهِ: he strove in his work.
  • كَدَحَ جِلْدَهُ: he scratched his skin.

These uses show two branches: strenuous effort and making a mark or scratch on a surface.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important Arabic forms include:

  • كَدَحَ ـ يَكْدَحُ: strove, toiled, or earned; transitively, scratched.
  • كَدْح: strenuous effort; also a scratch.
  • كَادِح: one who strives and toils.
  • كَدَّحَ: scratched and marked a surface.
  • مُكَدَّح: scratched or bearing marks of injury.
  • اِكْتَدَحَ: strove to earn a livelihood.

Forms examined in the Qur’an:

كَادِحٌ ـ كَدْحًا

Both occur in Al-Inshiqaq 84:6. كَادِح is an active participle and كَدْح the verbal noun of the basic triliteral verb. No other derivative occurs in the Qur’an.

3. Semantic core and physical image

Ibn Faris locates the core in making an impression on something. Scratching skin offers a physical example.

In work and striving, kadḥ is activity that expends human strength and may leave effects on the body and life.

A farmer’s hands may grow rough and calloused after years of work. Years of demanding labor can likewise leave visible effects on a person’s face and body.

This image helps explain the semantic relationship, without proving that effort historically developed from scratching.

Nor is bodily injury required for kadḥ. Intellectual, scholarly, and other activities demanding exertion and endurance can belong to its range.

Its Qur’anic focus is effort and activity involving toil.

4. Qur’anic use

كَادِحٌ إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ: human striving through life

The verse addresses humanity, not a particular group of believers, profession, or those who consciously choose the divine path.

Throughout life, people strive to meet needs, attain desires, and accomplish goals. Education, livelihood, raising children, relationships, and difficulties occupy portions of that life.

All these activities occur along a course ending in an encounter with the Lord and accountability for deeds.

One person may expend every effort serving others; another may expend as much acquiring power or wealth or harming people. Both strive, but their effort’s value and outcome differ.

Kadḥ alone therefore does not mean righteousness, worship, or striving for The God. Purpose, manner, and outcome determine the effort’s value.

إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ identifies the course’s ultimate destination, one not restricted to those who consciously choose it.

The pronoun in فَمُلَاقِيهِ

مُلَاقِي is the active participle of Form III from ل ق ي, denoting one who meets a person or thing.

Grammatically, the pronoun هِ can refer to either of two preceding expressions:

  • رَبِّكَ: you will meet your Lord.
  • كَدْحًا: you will encounter your striving.

In the second reading, encountering striving means confronting one’s deeds and their outcome.

The second agrees with verses describing deeds present before their doers, such as Al-Kahf 18:49.

The readings are not incompatible in their implications. Meeting the Lord also entails confronting one’s deeds and responsibilities.

Given إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ and the Qur’anic parallels, reference to the Lord is preferable for direct translation, although reference to kadḥ remains grammatically defensible.

5. Morphology and syntax

كَادِحٌ is an active participle in the pattern فَاعِل and the predicate of إِنَّ.

كَدْحًا is the cognate verbal noun functioning as an absolute object, emphasizing the striving itself.

The combination كَادِحٌ ... كَدْحًا directs attention to the reality of human striving.

The active participle alone does not establish uninterrupted activity. Here, however, the general address to humanity and life’s final destination present striving as part of its course.

إِلَىٰ specifies direction and destination. إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ differs from the hypothetical لِرَبِّكَ: the course leads to the Lord, but not every human effort is necessarily undertaken for divine approval.

This does not describe traversing a material distance between humanity and The God. Return and encounter must be understood within the Qur’an’s semantic framework.

6. Comparison with related roots

ك د ح (كَدْح) and ك ب د (كَبَد)

كَبَد denotes hardship and toil.

Here kabad identifies difficult conditions in which humanity is created.

In Al-Inshiqaq 84:6, kadḥ describes human activity and effort through life.

Kabad emphasizes difficult circumstances; kadḥ emphasizes strenuous effort.

ك د ح (كَدْح) and س ع ي (سَعْي)

سَعْي has a broad range including movement, going, pursuing, and striving toward an objective.

These verses closely relate to Al-Inshiqaq 84:6: human striving is not lost, and people encounter what they have done.

Saʿy does not necessarily imply hardship, whereas exertion and endurance are more prominent in kadḥ.

ك د ح (كَدْح) and ك س ب (كَسْب)

كَسْب concerns acquiring or obtaining something.

Kadḥ foregrounds the activity and toil undertaken; kasb focuses more on what is acquired through it.

A farmer preparing soil, sowing, watering, and tending crops engages in kadḥ. The crop obtained is the fruit of that earning.

Early Arabic also uses kadḥ for earning, so the families overlap.

ك د ح (كَدْح) and ع م ل (عَمَل)

عَمَل denotes doing and activity and has a more general range.

Not every action is demanding or exhausting. A simple task may require little exertion.

Kadḥ highlights an activity’s strenuous aspect.

ك د ح (كَدْح) and ج ه د (جَهْد)

ج ه د concerns employing strength, striving, and enduring difficulty.

It is close to kadḥ: both can describe activity requiring exertion and toil.

Jahd foregrounds human capacity and the extent of exertion; kadḥ can focus on toil in work and life.

This is a semantic tendency, not an absolute boundary.

ك د ح (كَدْح) and ن ص ب (نَصَب)

One branch of نَصَب denotes fatigue and suffering from activity or endurance.

Kadḥ foregrounds strenuous effort itself; naṣab can describe its resulting fatigue and pain.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Arabic word or expressionSuggested rendering
كَدَحَstrove; toiled
كَدْحstrenuous effort; toil
كَادِحone who strives and toils
كَادِحٌ إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ كَدْحًاstriving with toil toward your Lord

“Effort” alone can understate exertion and hardship. “Suffering” alone fails to convey the activity involved.

Persian and Dari «کوشش و زحمت» preserve both aspects, as does English “strenuous effort” or “toil.”

Conclusion

ك د ح concerns striving, working, earning, and, in another branch, scratching and marking a surface.

Al-Inshiqaq 84:6 uses it for human striving through life toward the Lord.

Kadḥ is neither inherently good nor inherently bad. People may toil for construction or destruction, justice or oppression, service or personal desires.

Its value depends on how people employ their capacities and what they do through life: deeds that will ultimately be present before them.
