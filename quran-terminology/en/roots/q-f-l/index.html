Qur’anic Root Q F L

Closing and blocking access; a lock, hardness, and return from a journey

Qur’anic forms

أَقْفَالُهَا

Aqfāluhā

Their locks; sole occurrence, 47:24

1. Basic meaning and linguistic background

The uses of Q F L can be examined in two branches: dryness and hardness, with which some lexicographers connect a lock and secure closure; and return from a journey. This semantic arrangement does not itself establish the historical order in which the meanings emerged.

Poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period uses derivatives of this root for the dryness and leanness of animals. In classical Arabic, qufl is a device used to secure a door or container against access until it is opened.

Although sufficient independent, datable pre-Qur’anic evidence for qufl specifically as a locking device has not been established here, its Qur’anic metaphor suggests that the material image was intelligible to the audience of revelation.

Qur’anic form examined: aqfāluhā, “their locks.”

Other Arabic forms include qufl, a lock; aqfala, to lock; iqfāl, locking; muqfal, locked; qafīl, dry and hard; qufūl, return from a journey; and qāfilah, a caravan. These forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

2. Material imagery and early Arabic evidence

Dryness and hardness

Classical Arabic dictionaries record derivatives of Q F L for the drying of skin, wood, and other materials. Qafīl describes dry wood, while qāfil can describe a creature whose body has become withered or lean.

One transmitted wording of verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays reads:

نَحْنُ جَلَبْنَا الْقُرَّحَ الْقَوَافِلَا

“We brought the mature, lean horses.”

Al-qawāfil in this wording denotes lean, slender horses, not caravans. The witness is also transmitted as al-jawāfil. Its relevance to dryness and leanness therefore depends on the reading with qāf and should not be presented as undisputed.

Some Arabic lexicographers also connect the name of the lock with this semantic branch: it secures something and prevents its opening. The connection is intelligible, although the historical order of these uses is not established with certainty.

A lock and blocked access

Closing a door differs from locking it. A merely closed door may open when pushed; locking creates an obstacle to opening it.

A physical lock can protect property, a house, or valuables. Figuratively, the same image can express an obstacle to access, reception, or giving.

Poetry attributed to Ḥassān ibn Thābit, transmitted by Ibn Hishām in a passage discussing Islam’s emergence and subsequent events, says of his people’s generosity:

يَرْبُونَ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ مَعْرُوفَ مَنْ مَضَى / وَلَيْسَ عَلَيْهِمْ دُونَ مَعْرُوفِهِمْ قُفْلُ

“Through kindness they add to the kindness of those who passed before; no lock stands in the way of their generosity.”

The lock here is figurative: the people do not withhold their goodness and generosity. Other wordings of the hemistich about the lock are transmitted; here the two hemistichs of one verse follow Ibn Hishām’s text. This post-Islamic witness is not independent evidence of pre-Qur’anic usage.

Arabic also uses muqfal al-yadayn, “locked-handed,” for a miser, as though the person’s hands were locked against giving.

Return from a journey

The other branch appears in qafala and its verbal noun qufūl, denoting return from a journey.

Lexicographers connect qāfilah with qufūl and returning from travel, but disagree on restricting it to returning travelers. Some accept that restriction; al-Azharī, by contrast, recognizes its use for travelers setting out and explains it as an expression of hope for a safe return.

Persian borrowed qāfilah, “caravan,” from this branch.

There is insufficient historical evidence to unite returning from travel with locking and hardness. These branches are therefore best kept distinct.

3. The Qur’anic use of aqfāl

This root occurs only once in the Qur’an, in Muḥammad, in connection with reflection and the condition of hearts:

أَفَلا يَتَدَبَّرونَ القُرءانَ أَم عَلىٰ قُلوبٍ أَقفالُها

A-fa-lā yatadabbarūna l-Qurʾāna am ʿalā qulūbin aqfāluhā.

“Do they not, then, reflect on the Qur’an, or do hearts have their locks upon them?”

Muhammad 47:24

Aqfāl is the broken plural of qufl. The attached pronoun hā refers to the hearts.

Syntactically, ʿalā qulūbin is a fronted predicate and aqfāluhā the delayed subject. The sentence speaks of locks upon hearts without specifying in this verse who put them there.

Am has a transitional and interrogative force here, moving the question from failure to reflect on the Qur’an to the condition of hearts closed to its reception.

The lock image highlights obstructed reception, not merely a shortage of information. How that obstruction arose requires contextual examination. Neither the noun “lock” nor this sentence alone says that every person consciously chose to lock their own heart.

As reflection, one may distinguish ignorance that learning can remedy from refusal to examine a message. The latter may become an inward obstacle to receiving truth. This is an application of the image, not the verse’s lexical translation.

The Qur’anic heart is not only a seat of emotion; it also concerns understanding, reasoning, acceptance, and human orientation. Al-Ḥajj 22:46 speaks of hearts with which people reason.

Locks upon hearts in Muḥammad 47:24 thus depict obstructed understanding and reception, rather than a physical condition of the cardiac organ.

4. A locked heart and reflection

The relationship between the verse’s two parts is essential: the first concerns reflection on the Qur’an, and the second the locks of hearts.

Reflection involves examining discourse, relating its parts, considering its implications, and revising earlier understandings when necessary.

Al-Nisāʾ poses the same question:

أَفَلا يَتَدَبَّرونَ القُرءانَ وَلَو كانَ مِن عِندِ غَيرِ اللَّهِ لَوَجَدوا فيهِ اختِلٰفًا كَثيرًا

A-fa-lā yatadabbarūna l-Qurʾān. Wa-law kāna min ʿindi ghayri llāhi la-wajadū fīhi khtilāfan kathīrā.

“Do they not reflect on the Qur’an? Had it been from anyone other than The God, they would have found much inconsistency in it.”

An-Nisa 4:82

Here reflection concerns testing the coherence of the Qur’an’s content. People are invited to consider and examine the revealed discourse.

Someone may read the Qur’an repeatedly, memorize many verses, and know Arabic well, yet refuse to examine a verse’s implications when it challenges established beliefs, interests, or habits.

In that situation, the problem is not simply a failure of communication or insufficient information; it lies in the person’s response to the message.

The same surah, Muḥammad, states:

وَمِنهُم مَن يَستَمِعُ إِلَيكَ حَتّىٰ إِذا خَرَجوا مِن عِندِكَ قالوا لِلَّذينَ أوتُوا العِلمَ ماذا قالَ ءانِفًا أُولٰئِكَ الَّذينَ طَبَعَ اللَّهُ عَلىٰ قُلوبِهِم وَاتَّبَعوا أَهواءَهُم

Wa-minhum man yastamiʿu ilayka ḥattā idhā kharajū min ʿindika qālū li-lladhīna ūtū l-ʿilma mādhā qāla ānifā. Ulāʾika lladhīna ṭabaʿa llāhu ʿalā qulūbihim wa-ttabaʿū ahwāʾahum.

“Among them are those who listen to you, until, when they leave your presence, they say to those given knowledge, ‘What did he say just now?’ Those are the ones upon whose hearts The God has set a seal, and they have followed their desires.”

Muhammad 47:16

The verse brings together outward listening, the question after leaving, sealed hearts, and following desires in describing a particular group. Simply asking “What did he say?” in another situation does not prove inability to understand or rejection. The judgment about this group comes from the description as a whole.

Prejudice, arrogance, attachment to inherited beliefs, and placing personal interests above truth can obstruct reflection. They are possible applications of the lock image, not independent lexical meanings of the word.

Someone who finds a verse difficult or disagrees with another interpretation does not necessarily have a locked heart. Questioning, examination, and reasoned disagreement can belong to the process of reflection.

5. Comparison with related roots

Q F L (qufl) and GH L Q (ighlāq): locking and closing

GH L Q denotes closing and blocking.

Yūsuf 12:23 uses ghallaqat for closing the doors.

Qufl highlights an obstacle that prevents opening. Ighlāq more directly expresses the act of closing, although Arabic usage can also include locking.

The roots overlap, but the lock image specifically draws attention to an obstacle to opening and access.

Q F L (qufl) and KH T M (khatm): a lock and a seal

Khatm concerns bringing to an end, sealing, and closing with a seal.

Al-Baqarah 2:7 speaks of God sealing hearts and hearing.

In material imagery, a lock blocks access, while a seal may also mark completion, authentication, or preservation of contents.

Both images can convey impaired reception of truth in Qur’anic descriptions of the heart, but their lexical imagery is not identical.

Q F L (qufl) and Ṭ B ʿ (ṭabʿ): blocked access and the impression of a seal

Ṭabʿ in Arabic concerns sealing, making an impression, and fixing a form upon something.

In Qur’anic usage, sealing the heart is associated with people unreceptive to truth.

The lock emphasizes blocked reception; ṭabʿ offers the image of sealing and its established impression.

These differences do not establish a fixed sequence of stages through which hearts become closed. Nor do the words alone determine the duration of closure or the impossibility of a person’s return.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Aqfāluhā in Muḥammad 47:24 literally means “their locks,” with the pronoun referring to the hearts. The proposed clause, “do hearts have their locks upon them?”, preserves that attachment. The grammatical attachment should remain clear in explanation.

“Locks” preserves the Arabic word’s physical image and its figurative application. The base translation need not replace it with ignorance, prejudice, heedlessness, or hypocrisy. Persian and Dari likewise use the familiar equivalent قفل.

Proposed translation of the verse:

“Do they not, then, reflect on the Qur’an, or do hearts have their locks upon them?” — Muḥammad 47:24

In non-Qur’anic contexts, suitable equivalents include a lock, locking, becoming dry and hard, returning from a journey, and a caravan, according to the relevant branch.

7. Conclusion

Q F L has two semantic branches in Arabic: dryness, hardness, and secure closure; and return from a journey.

The Qur’an uses aqfāl from the first branch for hearts whose reception of, and reflection on, the divine message is obstructed.

The lock image directs attention beyond merely reading and hearing the Qur’an to how one responds to its content. Reflection bears fruit when a person is willing to weigh the discourse and revise understanding and conduct.

Proposed Forqan definition: A lock and blocked access; dryness and hardness in lexical uses; and, in qufūl, return from a journey. Aqfāl in Muḥammad 47:24 means locks. Their obstruction of reception and reflection is understood from their figurative placement upon hearts, not from every independent use of this root.
