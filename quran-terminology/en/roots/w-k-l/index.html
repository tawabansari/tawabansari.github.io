Entrusting affairs, relying on another, taking charge, and trust in God

The Arabic root w-k-l concerns entrusting a task to another, assigning responsibility for it, and relying on the one who takes charge. Wakala means to leave or entrust; wakkala means to assign responsibility or appoint someone to a task; wakil denotes one who takes charge of an affair. In its sense of trust, tawakkala means relying on someone to whom one entrusts an affair or its outcome.

In this family, the relationship between a task and the person taking responsibility matters. Someone may entrust a task to another because of personal inability, or may be capable of doing it but trust another person’s knowledge, integrity, and competence.

Entrusting a task is therefore not necessarily a sign of inability, and trust does not mean abandoning every personal responsibility.

The root occurs seventy times in the Qur’an in four morphological categories: appointing someone to a task, placing trust, the designation wakil, and the title mutawakkilin. Of these, two are Form II verbs, forty are Form V verbs, twenty-four are wakil, and four are plural active participles. The seventy occurrences are distributed across sixty-one verses.

Many Qur’anic occurrences concern human trust in God. Yet the Qur’an also speaks of people and the angel of death being appointed to specific responsibilities. Several verses emphasize that Muhammad is not a wakil charged with controlling people’s faith and conduct.

This diversity shows that w-k-l cannot be reduced to inward trust alone. Assigning responsibility, taking charge, and reliance are related aspects of the family; each derivative’s meaning depends on its form and context.

1. The root in early Arabic and its historical evidence

In early Arabic, wakala fulanun amrahu ila fulan means that someone entrusted his affair to another or left its performance to him.

Wakkalahu bi-l-amr likewise means appointing someone to carry out or attend to a task.

Three elements can be identified: the task, the person entrusting it, and the person taking charge of it.

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Nabighah al-Dhubyani reads:

كِلِينِي لِهَمٍّ يَا أُمَيْمَةَ نَاصِبِ
وَلَيْلٍ أُقَاسِيهِ بَطِيءِ الْكَوَاكِبِ

kilīnī li-hammin yā Umaymata nāṣibi / wa-laylin uqāsīhi baṭīʾi al-kawākibi

Translation: “Leave me, Umaymah, to a wearying grief and a night I struggle through, whose stars move slowly.”

Kilini is a feminine singular imperative from w-k-l. The poet asks the woman addressed to leave him with his grief and let him face it himself.

This usage matters because wakl here does not mean appointing a representative or legal agent. It means leaving someone to a situation or task he faces.

One early meaning of this family is therefore leaving someone to himself or to an affair, without the person leaving him undertaking it in his place.

The line belongs to a well-known ode by al-Nabighah associated with praise of a Ghassanid ruler. Its attribution to the pre-Islamic poet is established in the poetic tradition, but its text reaches us through later transmission. The poet’s antiquity and the precise date of each line’s recorded wording must therefore be distinguished.

Lexicons also record wakaltuhu ila nafsihi: I left him to himself, without intervening or helping him manage his affair.

This usage relates to entrusting responsibility to another, but its outcome can differ. Sometimes an affair is entrusted to a capable, reliable person; sometimes someone is left alone despite needing help.

Leaving something to someone does not by itself determine whether the act is good or bad. The person’s ability, the task, and the circumstances matter.

Classical Arabic uses wakil for someone who undertakes another person’s affairs. A person might appoint another to buy, sell, safeguard property, or attend to a task.

Wakalah names this relationship of entrusting and taking charge.

Another branch uses wakilat al-dabbah for an animal that has weakened or can no longer continue moving. Wākil can also describe an animal that depends on another in movement.

A verse by al-Qutami, a poet of the Islamic period, reads:

وَكِلَتْ فَقُلْتُ لَهَا النَّجَاءَ تَنَاوَلِي
بِي حَاجَتِي وَتَجَنَّبِي هَمْدَانَا

wakilat fa-qultu lahā al-najāʾa tanāwalī / bī ḥājatī wa-tajannabī Hamdānā

Translation: “The mount weakened, so I told her: Hasten; carry me toward my purpose, and keep away from Hamdan.”

Wakilat in this line denotes the animal’s weakness and inability to continue moving.

This usage shows that the Arabic root family also has a branch concerning weakness, slackness, and dependence on another.

Because al-Qutami belongs to the Islamic period, however, this line is not conclusive pre-Qur’anic evidence. Nor does it establish weakness and inability as the original meaning of every w-k-l derivative.

A defensible semantic core for the principal usages is leaving or entrusting an affair to another, together with the resulting relationship of reliance or responsibility.

Dependence, weakness, or inability may accompany this relationship in some usages, but is not essential to every derivative.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important forms recorded in Arabic include:

  • Wakala: he entrusted an affair to another or left someone to himself.
  • Wakl and wukul: leaving, entrusting, or relying.
  • Wakkala: he appointed someone to perform a task.
  • Tawkil: appointment and assignment of responsibility.
  • Wakil: one responsible for an affair or appointed to attend to it.
  • Wakalah: the relationship of entrusting and taking responsibility.
  • Muwakkil: the person entrusting an affair to another.
  • Muwakkal: the person appointed to a task.
  • Mawkul: something entrusted or assigned to another.
  • Tawakkala: he trusted and relied on someone; in another usage, he undertook a task for someone else.
  • Tawakkul: trust and reliance on another in carrying out or managing an affair.
  • Mutawakkil: one who trusts and relies on another.
  • Ittakala: he relied on another or became dependent on him in an affair.
  • Ittikal: reliance and dependence.
  • Tawakala: in one recorded usage, each left a task to the other and withdrew from doing it.
  • Wakil and wakilah (وَكِل، وَكِلَة): descriptions of a person who frequently depends on others, distinct in vowel length from wakīl, an agent or trustee.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an.

The attested Qur’anic forms fall into four categories:

1. Form II verbs: wakkalna and wukkila.

2. Form V verbs: tawakkala and its various inflections, such as tawakkal, tawakkaltu, tawakkalna, yatawakkalu, yatawakkaluna, and fa-l-yatawakkali.

3. The noun wakil, occurring with and without the definite article and in different grammatical cases.

4. The Form V active participle in the plural: mutawakkilin and mutawakkilun.

The Form V structure matters in tawakkala. In Qur’anic usage, this verb is constructed with the preposition ʿala and expresses human trust and reliance on God.

Arabic also records tawakkala li-fulan: someone undertook an affair for another.

The preposition and the relationship therefore help determine the meaning. The morphological pattern alone cannot establish that all usages concern only trusting or only taking charge.

Ittakala also belongs to this root despite its changed appearance. In Form VIII, the initial root letter waw changes and assimilates to ta. This morphological change does not create a new root.

3. Assigning responsibility in the Qur’an: wakkalna and wukkila

Entrusting the Scripture and guidance

Al-Anʿam 6:89

أُولَٰئِكَ الَّذِينَ آتَيْنَاهُمُ الْكِتَابَ وَالْحُكْمَ وَالنُّبُوَّةَ ۚ فَإِنْ يَكْفُرْ بِهَا هَٰؤُلَاءِ فَقَدْ وَكَّلْنَا بِهَا قَوْمًا لَيْسُوا بِهَا بِكَافِرِينَ

ulāʾika alladhīna ātaynāhumu al-kitāba wa-l-ḥukma wa-l-nubuwwata fa-in yakfur bihā hāʾulāʾi fa-qad wakkalnā bihā qawman laysū bihā bi-kāfirīn

Al-Anʿām 6:89

Translation: “Those are the ones to whom We gave the Scripture, judgment, and Prophethood. If these people reject it, We have entrusted it to a people who do not reject it.”

Wakkalna is a Form II past-tense verb, with God as its subject.

Here bi-ha refers to the matters mentioned earlier: the Scripture, judgment, and Prophethood.

Qawman denotes the people entrusted with this responsibility.

This usage does not describe those people’s personal trust in God. It concerns an affair that God has entrusted to them.

The verse shows that if a group rejects the truth conveyed to it, that truth does not depend on its acceptance. God can entrust it to another group that does not deny it.

Wakkalna bi-ha concerns appointment and responsibility, rather than merely giving a group a religious title.

This usage relates to intellectual integrity and responsibility toward the Scripture. Someone entrusted with knowledge does not exclusively own its truth and cannot invalidate that truth through denial or concealment.

Nevertheless, wakkalna alone cannot establish every practical duty of that group or the details of a legislative system. Bi-ha and the context of Scripture, judgment, and Prophethood determine the scope of responsibility.

The appointment of the angel of death

Al-Sajdah 32:11

قُلْ يَتَوَفَّاكُمْ مَلَكُ الْمَوْتِ الَّذِي وُكِّلَ بِكُمْ ثُمَّ إِلَىٰ رَبِّكُمْ تُرْجَعُونَ

qul yatawaffākum malaku al-mawti alladhī wukkila bikum thumma ilā rabbikum turjaʿūn

Al-Sajdah 32:11

Translation: “Say: The angel of death appointed over you will take you fully; then you will be returned to your Lord.”

Wukkila is a passive Form II past-tense verb.

Its contrast with wakkalna in al-Anʿam is clear.

In al-Anʿam, God is the subject and says that We have appointed a people.

In al-Sajdah, the verb is passive, and the angel of death is the one assigned a responsibility.

Bi-kum identifies those to whom the appointment relates. The angel has a responsibility concerning human beings, which the verse describes through yatawaffakum.

This usage shows that wakil and muwakkal relate to appointment and commission in Arabic. The angel of death is not presented here as possessing authority independent of God.

Other verses, such as al-Zumar 39:42 and al-Anʿam 6:61, describe the taking of life from other perspectives. These usages must be considered together. Wukkila alone does not establish the physical process of death, the structure of the self, or an angel’s independence from God’s will.

The common feature of the two Form II occurrences is clear: a responsibility has been assigned to a person or group.

One concerns the trust of Scripture and guidance; the other, the angel of death’s commission.

4. Wakil: one who takes charge of an affair

Wakil follows the faʿīl pattern and, in this family’s usages, denotes someone who has undertaken an affair or been appointed to it.

The word can describe a human being, but in the Qur’an it appears mainly as a description of God or in statements limiting the Messenger’s responsibility.

Wakil as a description of God

Al-Anʿam 6:102

ذَٰلِكُمُ اللَّهُ رَبُّكُمْ ۖ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ ۖ خَالِقُ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ فَاعْبُدُوهُ ۚ وَهُوَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ وَكِيلٌ

dhālikumu Allāhu rabbukum lā ilāha illā huwa khāliqu kulli shayʾin fa-ʿbudūhu wa-huwa ʿalā kulli shayʾin wakīl

Al-Anʿām 6:102

Translation: “That is The God, your Lord; there is no deity except Him, the Creator of everything. So serve Him; He is in charge of everything.”

Here wakil describes God.

It follows the identification of God as Lord and Creator of everything. In this context, divine wakalah therefore concerns taking charge of creation and governing its affairs.

A human being may act as another’s agent, but human power, knowledge, and authority are limited. Here God is identified as the Creator of everything, so wakil has a comprehensive scope.

Divine wakalah must therefore not be equated precisely with human legal agency.

In human agency, someone entrusts a task to another because he may lack the time, knowledge, or ability to perform it. But God’s being wakil over His servants does not arise from any weakness or need of His own.

When God is described as ʿala kulli shayʾin wakil, the meaning concerns His charge and sufficiency in the domain of lordship and governance of creation.

The same expression also occurs in al-Zumar 39:62.

Niʿma al-wakil: what an excellent trustee

Al ʿImran 3:173

الَّذِينَ قَالَ لَهُمُ النَّاسُ إِنَّ النَّاسَ قَدْ جَمَعُوا لَكُمْ فَاخْشَوْهُمْ فَزَادَهُمْ إِيمَانًا وَقَالُوا حَسْبُنَا اللَّهُ وَنِعْمَ الْوَكِيلُ

alladhīna qāla lahumu al-nāsu inna al-nāsa qad jamaʿū lakum fa-khshawhum fa-zādahum īmānan wa-qālū ḥasbunā Allāhu wa-niʿma al-wakīl

Āl ʿImrān 3:173

Translation: “Those to whom people said: People have gathered against you, so fear them. But it increased their faith, and they said: The God is sufficient for us, and what an excellent trustee He is.”

Al-wakil appears here with the definite article and describes God.

The expression occurs amid threats and attempts to frighten the believers. They are told that a group has assembled against them.

Their response has two parts:

Hasbuna Allah: The God is sufficient for us.

Wa-niʿma al-wakil: and what an excellent trustee He is.

Hasb and wakil are different words. Hasb denotes sufficiency, while wakil identifies the one who takes charge of an affair.

Their combination shows that the believers trust the one they consider sufficient for them and worthy of reliance.

This does not mean there is no danger. The verse itself describes a threat; their faith becomes manifest in facing danger, not denying its existence.

The next verse describes their return with divine blessing and bounty. This is the outcome within that story. It must not become a universal rule that anyone who says hasbuna Allah will always emerge from every incident without physical or financial harm.

The value of trust here is reliance on God under fear and pressure, not a guarantee of one particular outcome for everyone in every circumstance.

Wakil in agreements between people

Al-Qasas 28:28

قَالَ ذَٰلِكَ بَيْنِي وَبَيْنَكَ ۖ أَيَّمَا الْأَجَلَيْنِ قَضَيْتُ فَلَا عُدْوَانَ عَلَيَّ ۖ وَاللَّهُ عَلَىٰ مَا نَقُولُ وَكِيلٌ

qāla dhālika baynī wa-baynaka ayyamā al-ajalaynī qaḍaytu fa-lā ʿudwāna ʿalayya wa-Allāhu ʿalā mā naqūlu wakīl

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:28

Translation: “He said: That is between me and you. Whichever of the two terms I complete, there shall be no transgression against me; The God is trustee over what we say.”

This verse concerns Moses’ agreement with the man who offered him work and marriage.

After accepting the conditions, Moses clarifies that completing either specified term will suffice, and no one has the right to demand more.

He then says that God is wakil over what they have said.

Here wakil means the one under whose care and supervision the stated undertaking remains.

This usage approaches the preservation of commitments and accountability before God. Nevertheless, wakil and witness do not share a root. Witnessing and knowing an agreement can be associated with taking charge, but do not exhaust wakil’s meaning.

In Yusuf 12:66, after taking a pledge from his sons, Jacob likewise says:

«اللَّهُ عَلَىٰ مَا نَقُولُ وَكِيلٌ»

Allāhu ʿalā mā naqūlu wakīl

Yūsuf 12:66

Both usages present God as the trusted authority regarding human commitments.

These verses show that divine wakalah is not invoked only when seeking help in danger. Covenants and moral responsibility between people can also relate to this description.

Muhammad is not the people’s wakil

Al-Anʿam 6:107

وَلَوْ شَاءَ اللَّهُ مَا أَشْرَكُوا ۗ وَمَا جَعَلْنَاكَ عَلَيْهِمْ حَفِيظًا ۖ وَمَا أَنْتَ عَلَيْهِمْ بِوَكِيلٍ

wa-law shāʾa Allāhu mā ashrakū wa-mā jaʿalnāka ʿalayhim ḥafīẓan wa-mā anta ʿalayhim bi-wakīl

Al-Anʿām 6:107

Translation: “Had The God willed, they would not have associated partners with Him. We have not appointed you their keeper, and you are not in charge of them.”

This is one of the root’s defining Qur’anic usages.

God tells Muhammad that He has not appointed him a hafiz over people and that he is not their wakil.

Hafiz, from h-f-z, concerns keeping and safeguarding.

Wakil concerns taking charge of and managing an affair.

The words overlap in responsibility and care, but their pairing is meaningful. The Qur’an denies both designations to clarify the limits of the Messenger’s commission.

Muhammad is responsible for conveying the message and explaining the truth. He is not commissioned to undertake people’s faith on their behalf or, by his own authority, prevent their association of partners or disbelief.

Al-Anʿam 6:66, Yunus 10:108, al-Israʾ 17:54, al-Zumar 39:41, and al-Shura 42:6 repeat this limitation in similar expressions.

Al-Furqan 25:43 asks about someone who makes his own desire his deity: can Muhammad be his wakil?

These repetitions show that being a Messenger does not mean having unlimited authority over others’ faith, conscience, and destiny.

The Messenger must convey what has been revealed to him, but individuals retain their choice and responsibility.

This limitation neither denies the Messenger’s status nor the need for invitation and teaching. It distinguishes conveying guidance from assuming control over others.

Wakil in these verses should therefore not be translated merely as “legal representative.” The issue is authority and responsibility concerning people’s faith and accountability.

In al-Nisaʾ 4:109, the Qur’an also describes those who defend wrongdoers in this world and asks who will defend them or be their wakil on the Day of Resurrection.

There, support and undertaking another’s defense are prominent.

Even when wakil concerns people, its object matters: attending to affairs, defending someone, safeguarding a commitment, or managing an assigned responsibility.

5. Trust in God alongside human responsibility

In Qur’anic usage, tawakkala is constructed with ʿala and expresses human trust and reliance on God.

This trust does not remove the need for knowledge, decisions, effort, caution, or fulfilling one’s duty.

The Qur’an itself gives several clear examples of trust accompanying human action.

Consultation and decision, followed by trust

Al ʿImran 3:159

فَبِمَا رَحْمَةٍ مِنَ اللَّهِ لِنْتَ لَهُمْ ۖ وَلَوْ كُنْتَ فَظًّا غَلِيظَ الْقَلْبِ لَانْفَضُّوا مِنْ حَوْلِكَ ۖ فَاعْفُ عَنْهُمْ وَاسْتَغْفِرْ لَهُمْ وَشَاوِرْهُمْ فِي الْأَمْرِ ۖ فَإِذَا عَزَمْتَ فَتَوَكَّلْ عَلَى اللَّهِ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُحِبُّ الْمُتَوَكِّلِينَ

fa-bimā raḥmatin mina Allāhi linta lahum wa-law kunta faẓẓan ghalīẓa al-qalbi la-nfaḍḍū min ḥawlika fa-ʿfu ʿanhum wa-staghfir lahum wa-shāwirhum fī al-amri fa-idhā ʿazamta fa-tawakkal ʿalā Allāhi inna Allāha yuḥibbu al-mutawakkilīn

Āl ʿImrān 3:159

Translation: “Through mercy from The God, you were gentle with them. Had you been harsh and hard-hearted, they would have dispersed from around you. So pardon them, seek forgiveness for them, and consult them in the affair. When you have resolved, put your trust in The God. Indeed, The God loves those who trust.”

This verse clarifies the relationship between trust and practical responsibility.

Muhammad is instructed to be gentle with people, pardon their errors, seek forgiveness for them, and consult them in the affair.

Then comes the command:

«فَإِذَا عَزَمْتَ فَتَوَكَّلْ عَلَى اللَّهِ»

fa-idhā ʿazamta fa-tawakkal ʿalā Allāh

Āl ʿImrān 3:159

That is, after deciding, place your trust in God.

If trust meant abandoning understanding and decisions, the accompanying commands to consult and resolve would make no sense.

The Qur’an does not say that because God knows everything, Muhammad should not seek others’ views. It expressly commands consultation.

Trust here therefore does not replace thought and decision. It is reliance on God along a path a person pursues responsibly.

For example, someone planning to begin a task gathers information, consults knowledgeable people, weighs resources and risks, and makes a decision.

After all that effort, some factors remain beyond personal control. No one can control everyone else’s behavior or every future event.

Trust in this situation means fulfilling one’s responsibility while not imagining oneself to have absolute control over the result.

This understanding follows the verse’s clear sequence: consultation, decision, and trust.

Jacob’s practical precaution and trust in God

Yusuf 12:67

وَقَالَ يَا بَنِيَّ لَا تَدْخُلُوا مِنْ بَابٍ وَاحِدٍ وَادْخُلُوا مِنْ أَبْوَابٍ مُتَفَرِّقَةٍ ۖ وَمَا أُغْنِي عَنْكُمْ مِنَ اللَّهِ مِنْ شَيْءٍ ۖ إِنِ الْحُكْمُ إِلَّا لِلَّهِ ۖ عَلَيْهِ تَوَكَّلْتُ ۖ وَعَلَيْهِ فَلْيَتَوَكَّلِ الْمُتَوَكِّلُونَ

wa-qāla yā baniyya lā tadkhulū min bābin wāḥidin wa-dkhulū min abwābin mutafarriqatin wa-mā ughnī ʿankum mina Allāhi min shayʾin ini al-ḥukmu illā li-Allāhi ʿalayhi tawakkaltu wa-ʿalayhi fa-l-yatawakkali al-mutawakkilūn

Yūsuf 12:67

Translation: “He said: My sons, do not enter through one gate; enter through separate gates. I cannot avail you anything against The God. Judgment belongs only to The God. In Him I have placed my trust, and in Him let those who trust place their trust.”

Jacob advises his sons to enter through separate gates.

This is a practical precaution. He does not merely tell them to trust in God; he proposes a specific way of entering.

He then recalls the limits of his power, saying that he cannot independently protect them against God’s judgment.

Finally, he places his trust in God.

The verse shows that trust is compatible with practical planning and caution.

The exact reason for Jacob’s advice is not stated in the verse itself. Without conclusive evidence, we should not claim that he was certainly preventing the evil eye, spying, envy, or some other specific danger.

What is clear is the combination of practical advice, acknowledgment of human limits, and trust in God.

Yusuf 12:68 returns to the matter and makes clear that this precaution could not by itself avert God’s determination.

Human planning is not rejected, but neither is it made into an independent power guaranteeing every outcome.

Trust and the decision for peace

Al-Anfal 8:61

وَإِنْ جَنَحُوا لِلسَّلْمِ فَاجْنَحْ لَهَا وَتَوَكَّلْ عَلَى اللَّهِ ۚ إِنَّهُ هُوَ السَّمِيعُ الْعَلِيمُ

wa-in janaḥū li-l-salmi fa-jnaḥ lahā wa-tawakkal ʿalā Allāhi innahu huwa al-samīʿu al-ʿalīm

Al-Anfāl 8:61

Translation: “If they incline toward peace, incline toward it and put your trust in The God. Indeed, He is the Hearing, the Knowing.”

This verse discusses trust in a situation involving a consequential political and social decision.

If the other side inclines toward peace, Muhammad is instructed to respond with an inclination toward peace and trust in God.

Trust does not replace action here; it accompanies a practical command.

The next verse also raises the possibility of deception. Trust therefore does not require denying every danger.

Nor should the possibility of deception be turned into permission to reject every peace proposal. The command is clear: when the other side inclines toward peace, the appropriate response is also to incline toward peace.

In this context, trust is reliance on God while carrying out a decision grounded in divine instruction despite possible risks.

Trust and steadfastness under harm

In Ibrahim 14:12, the Messengers speak of God’s guidance and declare that they will remain steadfast under the harm inflicted on them.

They then speak of trusting in God.

Al-Nahl 16:42 and al-ʿAnkabut 29:59 likewise place steadfastness alongside trust.

This connection shows that reliance on God does not mean abandoning the right path when difficulties arise.

Sabr concerns steadfastness and keeping oneself on the path; tawakkul concerns trust and reliance on God.

Someone pursuing a right course may face opposition, pressure, or harm. Trust helps that person avoid seeing himself as wholly dependent on his opponents’ power, while steadfastness concerns continuing to fulfill his duty.

Neither word, however, means compulsory silence before oppression or unquestioning acceptance of harm.

In Hud 11:88, after describing his aim of reform and effort to the extent of his ability, Shuʿayb says that his success comes only through God and that he has placed his trust in Him.

This connection between reform, effort, enabling success, and trust preserves human practical responsibility.

6. Trust, divine sufficiency, and the limits of human expectation

Al-Talaq 65:3

وَيَرْزُقْهُ مِنْ حَيْثُ لَا يَحْتَسِبُ ۚ وَمَنْ يَتَوَكَّلْ عَلَى اللَّهِ فَهُوَ حَسْبُهُ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ بَالِغُ أَمْرِهِ ۚ قَدْ جَعَلَ اللَّهُ لِكُلِّ شَيْءٍ قَدْرًا

wa-yarzuqhu min ḥaythu lā yaḥtasibu wa-man yatawakkal ʿalā Allāhi fa-huwa ḥasbuhu inna Allāha bālighu amrihi qad jaʿala Allāhu li-kulli shayʾin qadrā

Al-Ṭalāq 65:3

Translation: “He provides for him from where he does not expect. Whoever puts his trust in The God, He is sufficient for him. Indeed, The God accomplishes His purpose. The God has appointed a measure for everything.”

Yatawakkal is an imperfect verb within a conditional construction; fa-huwa hasbuhu states its consequence.

For whoever trusts in God, God is sufficient.

Hasb and tawakkul, however, do not share a root.

Tawakkul concerns trust and reliance; hasb concerns sufficiency.

The verse connects them: trust in God is answered by His sufficiency.

This does not establish that every trusting person will necessarily become wealthy, never become ill, or suffer no loss.

The verse itself speaks of the measure and determination of affairs. The surah’s context concerns family responsibilities, separation, respecting limits, and mindfulness of God.

The divine promise should therefore not become an economic formula or a guarantee of every desired human outcome.

For example, someone in financial hardship may work responsibly, respect others’ rights, avoid wrongful means, and trust in God. That trust does not mean necessarily obtaining the exact wealth desired at the desired time.

God’s sufficiency is broader than any particular form of wealth or comfort.

Trust must likewise not excuse abandoning financial, family, or social responsibilities. The same surah’s verses discuss respecting limits and others’ rights.

Evading commitments in the name of trust is incompatible with this context.

Trust and freedom from Satan’s authority

Al-Nahl 16:99

إِنَّهُ لَيْسَ لَهُ سُلْطَانٌ عَلَى الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَعَلَىٰ رَبِّهِمْ يَتَوَكَّلُونَ

innahu laysa lahu sulṭānun ʿalā alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿalā rabbihim yatawakkalūn

Al-Naḥl 16:99

Translation: “He has no authority over those who believe and place their trust in their Lord.”

This verse concerns Satan.

Yatawakkalun appears alongside faith and denotes believers’ reliance on their Lord.

The next verse speaks of Satan’s authority over those who take him as their patron and associate partners through him.

This contrast matters. Trust in God is incompatible with making Satan or any other power an ultimate, independent authority.

But the verse does not mean that believers are never tempted or cannot stumble. The Qur’an repeatedly speaks of human trials, errors, and repentance.

The issue here is authority and control, not the complete absence of temptation.

Trust draws people away from imagining that Satan or other powers possess absolute control over their destiny.

Those who trust

Mutawakkil is the Form V active participle, denoting someone who places trust.

The Qur’an uses its plural forms in Al ʿImran 3:159, Yusuf 12:67, Ibrahim 14:12, and al-Zumar 39:38.

In Al ʿImran, God loves those who trust.

In Yusuf, Jacob says that those who trust should trust in God.

In Ibrahim, the Messengers emphasize trust while facing harm.

In al-Zumar, those who trust are identified through their reliance on God.

These usages show that mutawakkil in the Qur’anic family names someone whose trust is directed toward God.

This designation does not imply having no need for effort or waiting without action. In the Qur’anic examples, those who trust make decisions, offer guidance, remain steadfast under harm, and fulfill their responsibilities.

7. Trust, inaction, and passing on responsibility

Arabic recognizes a distinction in usage between tawakkul and tawakul.

In its sense of trust, tawakkul means relying on another.

One classical usage of tawakul describes people leaving a task to one another, with no one taking responsibility for it.

For example, several people share a task. Each assumes someone else will do it, and it ultimately remains unfinished.

This clearly illustrates reciprocal, unproductive passing on of responsibility.

The distinction is consistent with Qur’anic usages, but the word tawakul in this particular technical sense should not be claimed to occur in the Qur’an.

Trust in God must also not mean handing over duties the Qur’an assigns to human beings themselves.

When someone becomes ill, using medical knowledge and available treatment is compatible with trust. A farmer can prepare the soil, sow seed, and work to irrigate it while also trusting in God.

Conversely, expecting a harvest without sowing does not become Qur’anic conduct merely by calling it trust.

These are contemporary illustrations, not a claim that all agricultural or medical principles derive from w-k-l.

The Qur’anic principle is clearer: people are responsible for their actions, while not possessing absolute control over every cause and outcome.

Trust does not set these two truths against each other.

8. Comparison with related roots

W-k-l and w-k-ʾ: trust and physical leaning

These roots have similar letters and some overlapping Persian equivalents, but are independent.

In Qur’anic usage, w-k-ʾ concerns physical leaning. Moses leans on his staff, and people recline on couches and beds.

In tawakkul, w-k-l concerns trust and reliance on the one to whom a person entrusts his affair.

Persian can describe both as “leaning,” but the kind of reliance differs.

Someone can lean on a staff while trusting in God. Using a physical aid does not negate trust in God.

Study of w-k-ʾ

W-k-l and f-w-d: entrusting and committing an affair

Tafwid concerns handing over or committing an affair.

Tawakkul may also involve committing an affair, but trust in the one to whom it is committed is prominent in Qur’anic usage.

Ghafir 40:44 contains wa-ufawwidu amri ila Allah.

In that verse, the speaker commits his affair to God.

This is closely related to trust, but the roots should not be treated as identical. Tafwid emphasizes committing the affair; Qur’anic tawakkul emphasizes trust and reliance on God.

W-k-l and k-f-l: taking charge and guaranteeing

Kafalah concerns undertaking, guaranteeing, and responsibility for care or protection.

Wakalah can likewise involve taking charge and attending to something.

Arabic sources sometimes explain wakil through kafil, since both may undertake responsibility for an affair.

This overlap does not make the two families completely identical.

A wakil may be appointed to perform a task, while a kafil, in usages concerning guarantee, accepts responsibility for support or provision.

Kafilan in al-Nahl 16:91 derives from k-f-l, not w-k-l.

Both can therefore be examined in relation to responsibility, while preserving their different forms and objects.

W-k-l and w-l-y: taking charge and protective authority

Qur’anic wilayah spans closeness, guardianship, support, and relationships of responsibility.

Wakalah concerns more specifically taking charge of an affair and entrusting a task.

In Al ʿImran 3:122, God is described as the wali of two groups, after which believers are called to trust in God.

Their appearance together shows that wilayah and tawakkul are related, but not wholly synonymous.

Wilayah can express the relationship of support and guardianship, while tawakkul expresses a person’s trust within that relationship.

Being someone’s agent in a specific affair does not confer unlimited authority over that person’s entire life and choices.

This distinction helps explain the verses denying that the Messenger is the people’s wakil.

Study of w-l-y

W-k-l and h-f-z: taking charge and safeguarding

Hifz concerns keeping something and preventing its loss.

Wakalah concerns responsibility for an affair and taking charge of it.

Al-Anʿam 6:107 denies both hafiz and wakil of Muhammad in relation to the people.

The pairing shows that the two words overlap in responsibility, but neither fully encompasses the other’s meaning.

Someone may be an agent for a transaction without being responsible for safeguarding all the principal’s property. Someone else may guard a place without authority to manage all its affairs.

Verses concerning the Messenger must likewise retain the scope specified by their context.

Study of ḥ-f-ẓ

W-k-l and h-s-b: trust and sufficiency

Hasb denotes sufficiency in expressions such as hasbuna Allah.

Tawakkul concerns human trust and reliance.

The concepts appear together in Al ʿImran 3:173 and al-Talaq 65:3.

People trust in God because they consider Him sufficient for them. But sufficiency is not the direct lexical meaning of tawakkul; it is a relationship the Qur’an establishes between the two concepts.

Rendering tawakkala merely as “he considered Him sufficient” therefore obscures part of the meaning of trust.

Study of ḥ-s-b

W-k-l and ʿ-z-m: resolve and trust after deciding

ʿAzm concerns firm decision and the resolve to act.

Tawakkul follows resolve in Al ʿImran 3:159.

The sequence shows that deciding and trusting are distinct actions.

A person decides upon an affair and then trusts in God while carrying it out and facing its outcome.

This sequence should not become a rule that trust is possible only at one moment after deciding. Elsewhere, the Qur’an describes trust as an enduring disposition in believers’ lives.

The distinction between decision and trust nevertheless remains.

Study of ʿ-z-m

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

For wakala in contexts of entrusting an affair, “he left,” “he entrusted,” or “he assigned to another” are appropriate.

For wakkala, “he appointed,” “he made responsible,” and “he put in charge” are proposed.

For wukkila, “he was appointed to it” or “he was made responsible for it” are suitable.

Depending on its object and context, wakil can be rendered “one in charge,” “agent,” “guardian,” “trustee,” or “representative.”

When describing God, “trustee of affairs,” “guardian,” and in some contexts “the one who suffices and governs” help explain the intended meaning. These senses should not all be combined in a short translation of one word.

For tawakkala ʿala, “he placed his trust in” or “he trusted and relied on” are suitable.

For mutawakkil, “one who trusts” or “someone who relies on God” are appropriate.

Depending on context, ittakala can mean “he relied,” “he became dependent on another,” or “he left his affair to him.”

For tawakala in the sense of reciprocal passing on of responsibility, “they left the task to one another” is clearer than “they trusted.”

The following renderings are proposed for important Qur’anic usages:

Al-Anʿam 6:89 — wakkalna bi-ha

“We have entrusted it to a people.”

Al-Sajdah 32:11 — wukkila bi-kum

“Appointed over you.”

Al-Anʿam 6:102 — wa-huwa ʿala kulli shayʾin wakil

“He is in charge of everything.”

Al ʿImran 3:173 — wa-niʿma al-wakil

“And what an excellent trustee He is.”

Al-Anʿam 6:107 — wa-ma anta ʿalayhim bi-wakil

“You are not in charge of them.”

Al-Qasas 28:28 — wa-Allahu ʿala ma naqulu wakil

“The God is trustee over what we say.”

Al ʿImran 3:159 — fa-tawakkal ʿala Allah

“So put your trust in The God.”

Al-Talaq 65:3 — wa-man yatawakkal ʿala Allahi fa-huwa hasbuhu

“Whoever puts his trust in The God, He is sufficient for him.”

In Persian, tawakkul is familiar and need not be replaced with a lengthy phrase in every verse. The explanation must nevertheless make clear that it means trust and reliance on God, not abandoning human responsibilities.

Wakil as a description of God must also not imply that God is someone else’s legal representative. Divine wakalah differs from limited human agency in the scope of power, knowledge, and charge.

10. Conclusion

Arabic w-k-l concerns leaving or entrusting an affair and undertaking it. When someone entrusts an affair to another, a relationship of responsibility and trust arises.

In early usage, wakala may mean leaving someone to himself. Wakkala means appointing another to a task, and wakil denotes the one responsible for it.

In Qur’anic usage, tawakkala concerns trust and reliance on God.

The Qur’an uses this family for entrusting a people with Scripture and guidance, the angel of death’s commission, God’s charge over affairs, the limits of the Messenger’s authority over people, and human trust in the Lord.

These usages show that wakalah and tawakkul are not a single action. One may concern assigning responsibility; the other, the trust of the person committing his affair to another.

God’s being wakil does not imply human inability or the worthlessness of human action. The Qur’an associates trust with consultation, resolve, planning, reform, steadfastness, and responsibility.

Muhammad is commissioned to convey the message, but is not in charge of people’s faith and conduct. People are responsible for their choices and cannot transfer that responsibility to the Messenger, a leader, or another person.

Despite limited knowledge, power, and choice, believers likewise do not control every outcome. They are responsible for thinking and acting rightly, while being able to trust in God regarding what they do not know and what lies beyond their control.

Trust neither abandons effort nor claims complete control over results. People can strive to preserve life, perform a task, reform society, or reach a worthy goal while recognizing that they do not control every cause and outcome.

The final definition of w-k-l is entrusting or leaving an affair to another and taking charge of it. Wakil denotes the one responsible for an affair; Qur’anic tawakkul is human trust and reliance on God. This trust does not remove personal responsibility. It draws people away from imagining themselves independent of God or absolutely dependent on other powers.
