Core meaning: closing a passage, gap or opening and remedying a breach; in speech and conduct, being correct, sound and appropriate. In material usage, sadd is a barrier that prevents passage or penetration. In abstract usage, sadīd describes speech or action that is sound, considered and free of error or deviation. The Qur’an uses this family for natural or constructed barriers, obstructions in front and behind, and sound speech.

1. The root and its meaning in early Arabic

The Arabic root S D D concerns closing, filling gaps, remedying deficiencies and making something secure.

Sadda al-bāba means “he closed the door.”

Sadda al-thulmata means “he closed the gap or breach.”

Sadda al-ṭarīqa means “he blocked the road and prevented passage.”

In extended usage, sadda al-ḥājata means meeting a need: as though something fills an existing deficiency.

In another branch, al-sadād denotes correctness, soundness and appropriateness in speech or conduct.

Qawlun sadīdun is sound, well-founded speech.

Raʾyun sadīdun is a sound, considered opinion.

These branches are semantically related, but neither should be declared the definite historical origin of the other without evidence.

Material usage concerns closing a gap or preventing passage.

Speech-related usage concerns correctness, soundness and appropriateness.

A verse attributed to pre-Islamic poetry: sound speech

Arabic lexicons attribute the following verse to Al-Aʿshā:

مَاذَا عَلَيْهَا وَمَاذَا كَانَ يَنْقُصُهَا
يَوْمَ التَّرَحُّلِ لَوْ قَالَتْ لَنَا سَدَدَا

mādhā ʿalayhā wa-mādhā kāna yanquṣuhā / yawma al-taraḥḥuli law qālat lanā sadadā

Translation:

“What harm would it have done her, and what would she have lost, had she spoken sound, appropriate words to us on the day of departure?”

Here sadadan refers to sound and fitting speech.

The poet describes a woman who could have spoken fittingly at their parting and departure.

Closing a door or building a barrier is not the subject.

In this usage, sadad concerns the correctness and appropriateness of speech.

The witness shows that this family was known in the early Arabic linguistic tradition as a description of speech quality.

Its attribution is nevertheless disputed.

Some sources attribute it to Al-Aʿshā and others to Kaʿb ibn Zuhayr. Examination of surviving collections has not conclusively established either attribution.

It therefore cannot be treated as a securely dated pre-Qur’anic witness.

Its value lies in recording sadad as sound, fitting speech in Arabic literary and lexical tradition.

Another early witness: confrontation in Aws ibn Ḥajar’s poetry

The pre-Islamic poet Aws ibn Ḥajar says in a poem:

تَكَنَّفَنَا الْأَعْدَاءُ مِنْ كُلِّ جَانِبٍ
لِيَنْتَزِعُوا عَرَقَاتِنَا ثُمَّ يَرْتَعُوا

takannafanā al-aʿdāʾu min kulli jānibin / li-yantaziʿū ʿaraqātinā thumma yartaʿū

He continues:

فَمَا جَبُنُوا أَنَّا نَسُدُّ عَلَيْهِمُ
وَلَكِنْ لَقُوا نَارًا تَحُسُّ وَتَسْفَعُ

fa-mā jabunū annā nasuddu ʿalayhimu / wa-lākin laqū nāran taḥussu wa-tasfaʿu

Translation and explanation:

“Enemies surrounded us on every side, to tear out our roots and then graze freely.”

“They did not shrink from our meeting them fairly; rather, they encountered a fire that destroys and scorches.” This rendering follows the lexical explanation connecting nasuddu with fairness in combat; the verse has other transmitted readings and explanations.

One lexical reading relates nasuddu ʿalayhimu to confronting the enemy and blocking its advance.

The poet depicts confrontation with enemies approaching his people from several sides.

On that reading, blocking the way can concern resisting an enemy’s advance.

The wording and meaning of the verse are disputed, however.

Some transmissions read nashuddu, with shīn.

Other lexicographers connect nasuddu with acting rightly or fairly in combat, rather than simply obstructing the enemy’s path.

This witness cannot establish one exclusive meaning conclusively.

The transmitted verse and its differing explanations document discussion of this family in the language of confrontation; they do not establish every proposed meaning independently.

Material uses recorded in Arabic

Closing gaps and filling deficiencies are clear uses of this family in the lexicons.

سَدَدْتُ الثُّلْمَةَ

sadadtu al-thulmata

“I closed the gap.”

سَدَدْتُ الْبَابَ

sadadtu al-bāba

“I closed the door.”

سَدَدْتُ الْخَلَلَ

sadadtu al-khalala

“I remedied the deficiency.”

Here something is open, incomplete or breached.

The act of sadd closes the gap or supplies what is lacking.

This image is only one recognized branch of the family.

Sound speech does not necessarily mean silencing someone or preventing them from speaking.

A historical definition of the root

Early Arabic witnesses and usages recorded in classical sources establish two clear semantic fields:

First, blocking a passage, opening or gap, or remedying a deficiency; second, correctness, soundness and appropriateness in speech or conduct.

Early lexicographers connect these fields.

Just as closing a breach repairs a wall, sound speech is understood as free of defects and deviation.

This explanation helps clarify their semantic relationship.

Without independent evidence, however, it should not be presented as a definitive history deriving sadīd from physically closing a gap.

2. Important forms and derivatives

Sadda, yasuddu

This is the family’s basic verb.

Transitively, it means closing, obstructing or filling a gap.

سَدَّ الطَّرِيقَ

sadda al-ṭarīqa

“He blocked the road.”

سَدَّ الْفُرْجَةَ

sadda al-furjata

“He closed the gap.”

The subject closes something or prevents passage and penetration.

Sadd and sudd

These denote a barrier or obstruction standing between two sides and blocking the way.

The barrier may be human-made.

It may also be natural, such as a mountain or mountainous obstruction.

Lexicons sometimes distinguish sadd, with fatḥah, from sudd, with ḍammah.

One explanation assigns the former to human-made barriers and the latter to natural ones.

The distinction is not uniform across usage and recitation, and some lexicographers treat the forms as synonymous.

The vowel alone cannot always determine whether a barrier is natural or artificial.

Sidād

In the branch of closing, this denotes something used to plug a gap or opening.

سِدَادُ الْقَارُورَةِ

sidādu al-qārūrati

“The stopper of a vessel.”

سِدَادُ الثَّغْرِ

sidādu al-thaghri

Something used to close and protect a breach or vulnerable passage.

In extended usage, sidād al-ʿawaz denotes something that meets a need or remedies a shortage.

This form does not occur in the Qur’an.

Sadād

In speech and conduct, this denotes correctness, soundness, moderation and appropriateness.

سَدَادُ الْقَوْلِ

sadādu al-qawli

“Correctness and soundness of speech.”

سَدَادُ الرَّأْيِ

sadādu al-raʾyi

“Soundness and good judgment in an opinion.”

Arabic distinguishes sidād, a means of closing, from sadād, correctness, although overlapping uses are also recorded.

Sadīd

An adjective meaning correct, sound and appropriate.

قَوْلٌ سَدِيدٌ

qawlun sadīdun

“Sound, well-founded speech.”

رَأْيٌ سَدِيدٌ

raʾyun sadīdun

“A sound, considered opinion.”

This form occurs twice in the Qur’an.

Morphologically, sadīd follows the faʿīl pattern.

The pattern alone does not determine how truthful, effective or sound speech is. Its content and context must be examined.

Saddada, yusaddidu

This is the Form II verb.

In the branch concerning correctness and direction, it means setting right, aiming toward a target or guiding someone rightly.

سَدَّدَ السَّهْمَ

saddada al-sahma

“He aimed the arrow properly toward the target.”

Correct direction is prominent here.

Aiming correctly and actually hitting the target are nevertheless distinct stages.

In speech, tasdīd can concern correction and setting words right.

Tasdīd

The verbal noun of Form II.

It denotes making sound, correcting direction or guiding toward what is right.

This exact verbal noun does not occur in the Qur’an.

Musaddad

The passive participle of Form II.

It describes something set right or guided in the appropriate direction.

Of a person, it can describe being guided rightly in speech or conduct.

Insadda and istadda

Insadda denotes a passage or opening becoming closed.

Istadda is recorded for something becoming closed and secure.

Some usages also concern becoming right and sound.

Its meaning therefore depends on subject matter and context.

Suddah

Lexicons use this for an area before a door or house, a threshold, the space at an entrance or a covering near a doorway.

This belongs to the field of doors, passages and barriers.

Suddah does not occur in the Qur’an.

Forms actually used in the Qur’an

S D D occurs six times in the Qur’an, in two principal groups.

Sadd occurs four times:

  • Al-saddayni in Al-Kahf 18:93.
  • Saddan in Al-Kahf 18:94.
  • Saddan twice in Yā Sīn 36:9.

Sadīd occurs twice:

  • Sadīdan in Al-Nisāʾ 4:9.
  • Sadīdan in Al-Aḥzāb 33:70.

The Qur’an thus uses the family both for barriers and for soundness of speech.

3. Bayna al-saddayni: two barriers and the construction in Al-Kahf

حَتَّىٰ إِذَا بَلَغَ بَيْنَ السَّدَّيْنِ وَجَدَ مِنْ دُونِهِمَا قَوْمًا لَا يَكَادُونَ يَفْقَهُونَ قَوْلًا

ḥattā idhā balagha bayna al-saddayni wajada min dūnihimā qawman lā yakādūna yafqahūna qawlā

Al-Kahf 18:93

Translation:

“Until, when he reached between the two barriers, he found on this side of them a people who could scarcely understand speech.”

Al-Kahf 18:93

Al-saddayni is the definite dual of sadd.

It is in the genitive and denotes two barriers.

Bayna specifies the position between them.

The verse describes Dhū al-Qarnayn reaching a place between two barriers.

Were the two barriers human-made?

Sadd does not necessarily denote an artificial structure.

Arabic can also describe natural obstacles with this term.

Al-Kahf 18:96 uses al-ṣadafayni for two facing sides, including mountainsides.

This context indicates natural obstacles with a passage between them in the story.

Al-saddayn in Al-Kahf 18:93 can accordingly refer to two natural barriers, such as mountains or mountain walls.

This reading fits the construction described in the following verses.

The word alone cannot identify the mountains, their geographical location or their exact shape.

The people’s request for a barrier

Al-Kahf 18:94 says:

قَالُوا يَا ذَا الْقَرْنَيْنِ إِنَّ يَأْجُوجَ وَمَأْجُوجَ مُفْسِدُونَ فِي الْأَرْضِ فَهَلْ نَجْعَلُ لَكَ خَرْجًا عَلَىٰ أَنْ تَجْعَلَ بَيْنَنَا وَبَيْنَهُمْ سَدًّا

qālū yā dhā al-qarnayni inna yaʾjūja wa-maʾjūja mufsidūna fī al-arḍi fa-hal najʿalu laka kharjan ʿalā an tajʿala baynanā wa-baynahum saddā

Al-Kahf 18:94

Translation:

“They said: O Dhū al-Qarnayn, Gog and Magog cause corruption in the land. Shall we assign you a payment so that you build a barrier between us and them?”

Saddan here is an indefinite noun.

The people ask Dhū al-Qarnayn to build an obstruction between them and those whose corruption they deplore.

Unlike the two barriers of the preceding verse, this explicitly concerns constructing a new barrier.

The context establishes the requested barrier as human-made.

This follows from tajʿala and the request, not merely from the vowel in sadd.

Does sadd mean a wall here?

Sadd denotes an obstruction that blocks the way or prevents passage.

It may be a wall, an embankment, a structure between mountains or another kind of barrier.

Later verses explain more about materials and construction.

Sadd itself is not the exclusive name of one kind of wall.

Its general meaning, “barrier,” should therefore be retained; its precise shape comes from the continuing story.

Radman: another expression in the reply

In Al-Kahf 18:95, Dhū al-Qarnayn replies that he will place a radm between the groups rather than repeating sadd.

Radm belongs to R D M and concerns filling gaps and making a solid barrier.

In Arabic, it can denote filling an opening with materials and piling components together.

Sadd and radm serve the same purpose here: closing the passage.

Their difference of expression nevertheless matters.

Sadd is the general name for a barrier.

Radm here emphasizes making a barrier by filling and securing the space between two sides.

A radm may therefore be a kind of sadd, but not every sadd is restricted to that meaning.

Iron and construction materials

In Al-Kahf 18:96, Dhū al-Qarnayn asks the people for pieces of iron.

The account then describes leveling the space between the sides, blowing and pouring molten copper.

These details establish an actual constructed barrier.

Al-Kahf 18:97 says that the group could neither climb over it nor pierce it.

The construction thus has a clear function of preventing passage.

This does not mean it is physically indestructible forever.

In Al-Kahf 18:98, Dhū al-Qarnayn speaks of his Lord’s promise and a change in the barrier’s condition.

Its strength under those circumstances does not imply everlasting existence.

Does sadd mean destroying the enemy?

In Al-Kahf 18:94, the people complain of Gog and Magog’s corruption and request a barrier.

Dhū al-Qarnayn proceeds to build one.

Its immediate purpose is to prevent passage and penetration, rather than to destroy the opposing group.

This distinction matters lexically.

A barrier can practically separate groups without eliminating either.

In this story, sadd denotes a real barrier blocking a passage. Later verses establish its materials, construction and strength; the word’s basic meaning remains an obstruction preventing passage.

4. Barriers in front and behind

وَجَعَلْنَا مِنْ بَيْنِ أَيْدِيهِمْ سَدًّا وَمِنْ خَلْفِهِمْ سَدًّا فَأَغْشَيْنَاهُمْ فَهُمْ لَا يُبْصِرُونَ

wa-jaʿalnā min bayni aydīhim saddan wa-min khalfihim saddan fa-aghshaynāhum fa-hum lā yubṣirūn

Yā Sīn 36:9

Translation:

“We placed a barrier before them and a barrier behind them, and covered them, so they do not see.”

Yā Sīn 36:9

Saddan occurs twice here.

First, it names the barrier placed before them.

Second, it names the barrier behind them.

The image confronts people with obstruction in two directions.

The direct meaning of sadd here

Both occurrences denote a barrier.

Min bayni aydīhim specifies the direction in front.

Wa-min khalfihim specifies the direction behind.

Front and back are conveyed by the spatial phrases, not by S D D itself.

The verse continues with fa-aghshaynāhum.

This verb belongs to GH SH Y and concerns covering or enveloping.

Then fa-hum lā yubṣirūn states that they do not see.

The verse therefore uses three related elements:

Obstruction, covering and inability to see.

Each is expressed by its own wording.

Are the barriers material?

Yā Sīn 36:8 speaks of shackles around this group’s necks.

Yā Sīn 36:9 describes barriers before and behind them and their being covered.

Yā Sīn 36:10 says warning or not warning makes no difference to them: they do not believe.

This connection places the barrier image in a context of rejecting guidance.

The barriers leave people restricted and unable to see.

A figurative reading concerning perception therefore fits the immediate context.

The lexical meaning of sadd remains unchanged.

The barrier is still an obstruction within the image; its nature and significance emerge from context.

The image does not establish that these people were biologically blind or that invisible physical walls had been built around their bodies.

Not seeing and not accepting guidance

Yā Sīn 36:7 speaks of most of those against whom the word has become due and who do not believe.

The following verses depict them through shackles, barriers and covering.

Yā Sīn 36:11 identifies those who follow the reminder and fear the Entirely Merciful unseen.

The context contrasts rejection of warning with acceptance of reminder.

The barriers can thus picture deprivation of a view of the way and recognition of truth.

This contextual conclusion should not be added to the lexical definition of sadd.

Sadd does not mean disbelief or denial in Arabic.

A material barrier may be built to protect people.

In this Qur’anic image, however, obstruction accompanies inability to see and rejection of warning.

Context determines the barrier’s function and value.

Does this describe everyone who disagrees?

Yā Sīn 36:7 uses aktharihim, “most of them.”

This qualification refers to most of the group, not necessarily every person.

The surah also describes different responses to the divine message.

The image in Yā Sīn 36:9 should not be applied to every person with different beliefs without examining their knowledge and conduct.

In Yā Sīn 36:9, sadd means a barrier; the barriers before and behind occur in a context of covering and inability to perceive the truth.

5. Qawlan sadīdan: sound speech

The two other Qur’anic uses concern human speech.

In both verses, sadīdan qualifies qawlan.

Qawl, from Q W L, means speech.

Sadīd describes its quality.

That quality concerns correctness, soundness and appropriateness.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:9: sound speech and vulnerable descendants’ rights

وَلْيَخْشَ الَّذِينَ لَوْ تَرَكُوا مِنْ خَلْفِهِمْ ذُرِّيَّةً ضِعَافًا خَافُوا عَلَيْهِمْ فَلْيَتَّقُوا اللَّهَ وَلْيَقُولُوا قَوْلًا سَدِيدًا

wa-l-yakhsha alladhīna law tarakū min khalfihim dhurriyyatan ḍiʿāfan khāfū ʿalayhim fa-l-yattaqū Allāha wa-l-yaqūlū qawlan sadīdā

Al-Nisāʾ 4:9

Translation:

“Let those fear who, if they left weak offspring behind them, would be afraid for them. Let them be mindful of The God and speak sound words.”

Sadīdan describes the speech commanded here.

The immediate context concerns inheritance, distribution of property and survivors’ rights.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:8 mentions relatives, orphans and needy people present at distribution.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:10 strongly condemns unjustly consuming orphans’ property.

Sound speech in Al-Nisāʾ 4:9 therefore belongs to a setting of responsibility toward vulnerable people and their rights.

Why does sound speech matter in inheritance?

In inheritance, wills, guardianship and distribution of assets, words can affect others’ rights.

Speech may help preserve a right.

False, misleading or deceptive speech may instead enable the loss of a vulnerable person’s rights.

For example, knowingly misrepresenting heirs’ shares can harm those unable to defend their rights adequately.

Sound speech can clarify entitlements and help prevent abuse.

By recalling people’s concern for their own vulnerable children, the verse calls them to mindfulness and sound speech.

This connection emphasizes speech’s ethical responsibility toward others’ rights.

It does not permit deriving all rules of inheritance or wills from sadīd alone.

The root conveys correctness and soundness; the legal subject comes from context.

Is sound speech only truthful speech?

Truthfulness is an important component of sound speech.

Arabic sadād also concerns right direction and appropriateness.

A statement can be factually true while its delivery disregards another’s rights or selectively presents truth to deceive.

The truth of a few sentences does not necessarily fulfill the entire requirement of sound speech.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:9, sadīd accompanies mindfulness in a context of vulnerable people’s rights.

Soundness must therefore be understood in relation to truth, justice and the speaker’s responsibility.

These contextual criteria should nevertheless remain distinct from the short lexical definition.

Relation to qawlan maʿrūfan

Al-Nisāʾ 4:8 uses qawlan maʿrūfan.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:9 uses qawlan sadīdan.

The expressions are close but derive from different roots.

Maʿrūf concerns what is recognized and commendable.

Sadīd concerns correctness and soundness.

In distributing inheritance, speech can be both respectful and commendable, and sound and correct.

Their juxtaposition does not imply that maʿrūf is never correct or that sadīd is never gentle and commendable.

Each description emphasizes an aspect of speech quality.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:70: a general command to speak soundly

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا اتَّقُوا اللَّهَ وَقُولُوا قَوْلًا سَدِيدًا

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū ittaqū Allāha wa-qūlū qawlan sadīdā

Al-Aḥzāb 33:70

Translation:

“O you who have believed, be mindful of The God and speak sound words.”

Qawlan sadīdan appears here as well.

The audience and context differ from Al-Nisāʾ 4:9.

In Al-Nisāʾ, the subject is concern for vulnerable descendants and their rights.

In Al-Aḥzāb, the command addresses all who have believed.

Its scope is therefore more general.

Speech and action

After the command to mindfulness and sound speech, Al-Aḥzāb 33:71 speaks of setting deeds right and forgiving sins.

This connection matters.

Speech is more than sounds and words: it can shape decisions, behavior and relationships.

Unsound words may lead to deception, conflict or loss of rights.

Sound speech can enable better conduct and clarify responsibility.

Sadīd itself does not lexically mean setting deeds right or forgiving sins.

The next verse expresses those meanings with separate verbs and phrases.

Relation to the preceding verse

Al-Aḥzāb 33:69 warns believers against behaving like those who hurt Moses, whom The God cleared of what they said.

The command to sound speech thus follows a reminder of harm caused by wrongful words.

Responsibility in speaking is therefore not confined to beautiful phrasing or delivery.

Content, truth and consequences matter as well.

Qawl sadīd cannot consequently be reduced to melodious, literary or pleasing speech.

Very beautiful and persuasive speech may still misrepresent reality.

Surface beauty alone does not make it sadīd.

Must sound speech always be harsh and blunt?

Correctness and firmness do not necessarily mean harshness.

Speech may be direct and correct.

The same truth may be conveyed calmly and respectfully.

Harsh words are not in themselves evidence of truth.

The criterion is correctness, soundness and appropriateness, not volume, abrasive language or emotional force.

Sound speech can be direct and fair at the same time.

Nor should truth be distorted or another’s rights disregarded to please an audience.

Qawl sadīd is speech sound in meaning and direction, rather than merely beautiful, forceful or pleasing.

6. Closing gaps and the soundness of speech

Lexicographers explain a connection between sadd, closing a gap, and sadād, correctness.

On this account, remedying a breach or defect makes something more complete and secure.

Sound speech is accordingly likened to something free of breaches and deviation.

This makes the semantic relationship tangible.

It does not mean that qawl sadīd in Arabic means shutting other people’s mouths.

Nor does it necessarily mean a statement after which no question, criticism or discussion is possible.

Being correct is different from preventing criticism.

Speech can be sound while its details remain open to questions.

Conversely, someone may close off discussion by force or threats while speaking falsely.

Such a person may obstruct others’ speech without producing qawl sadīd.

Relation to correct aiming

In archery, tasdīd denotes proper aiming.

This example illustrates correct direction.

The archer considers direction and measure to reach the target.

A speaker likewise needs to understand the subject, truth and purpose of their words.

The analogy does not guarantee that sound speech, like an arrow, achieves its speaker’s desired result.

Correctness and audience acceptance are different.

Someone may express truth correctly and still be rejected.

False speech may also influence many listeners.

Social influence alone therefore does not establish soundness.

7. Distinctions from related roots

S D D and Ṣ D D

These roots resemble each other in letters and pronunciation but are distinct.

S D D begins with sīn.

Ṣ D D begins with ṣād.

Material sadd concerns closing a passage and creating a barrier.

Qur’anic ṣadd concerns turning away or hindering someone from a path or destination.

Al-Baqarah 2:217 uses ṣaddun ʿan sabīli Allāh.

This means hindering from the way of The God.

By contrast, saddan in Al-Kahf 18:94 concerns building a real barrier between groups.

Their practical results may overlap.

A sadd may block someone’s passage.

Ṣadd can also prevent movement or reaching a destination.

Yet hindrance does not always involve building a wall, and barriers are not necessarily built to keep people from truth.

Physical obstruction and the act of hindering should therefore remain distinct.

S D D and R D M

Radm concerns filling a gap and closing the space between sides.

Sadd is a broader term for a barrier.

In Al-Kahf 18:94, the people request a sadd.

In Al-Kahf 18:95, Dhū al-Qarnayn speaks of making a radm.

The expressions address the same purpose from different angles.

Sadd emphasizes obstruction.

Radm here emphasizes filling the space and making a solid barrier.

This is a tendency, not an absolute rule for Arabic without examining all uses.

S D D and Ḥ J B

Ḥijāb denotes a screen or barrier between sides.

A sadd can also obstruct passage or sight.

Yā Sīn 36:9 places barriers in an image of inability to see.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:45 places a ḥijāban mastūran between the messenger and a group of listeners.

Both images concern obstruction and concealment.

Ḥijāb and sadd are nevertheless not exact synonyms.

A ḥijāb may conceal something without physically preventing passage.

In its clear material use, sadd emphasizes closing a passage or opening.

Context must determine the kind of barrier in each verse.

S D D and Ḥ Q Q

Qur’anic ḥaqq concerns truth, established reality and what is right or deserved.

Sadīd also describes correctness and soundness.

They overlap in the field of sound speech.

Ḥaqq can denote truth itself or an established right.

Sadīd in qawl sadīd qualifies speech as correct and well-founded.

This difference does not mean sound speech can intentionally contradict truth.

Its soundness must be compatible with truth and responsibility.

S D D and Q W M

Mustaqīm concerns straightness, steadiness and absence of deviation.

Sadīd in speech and conduct likewise concerns correctness and soundness.

Arabic lexicons explain sadād through istiqāmah.

The Qur’an, however, also applies mustaqīm to paths, religion and other subjects.

Both Qur’anic instances of sadīd qualify speech.

The full meaning of ṣirāṭ mustaqīm should not be transferred to qawl sadīd.

Nor should sound speech be confined to the image of a straight road.

S D D and ʿ R F

Al-Nisāʾ 4:8 mentions qawl maʿrūf.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:9 mentions qawl sadīd.

Maʿrūf concerns what is recognized and commendable.

Sadīd concerns correctness and soundness.

Desirable social speech may possess both qualities.

Acceptance by a society’s conventions alone does not establish the truth of all its content.

Conversely, true words can be delivered in a way that disregards the listener’s rights and dignity.

Both descriptions matter for responsible speech in Al-Nisāʾ’s legal context.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

The branches sadd and sadīd should be distinguished in translation.

Important meanings in the family

Sadda: closed, obstructed, filled a gap or opening.

Sadd: a barrier; something preventing passage or penetration.

Sidād: something used to fill a gap or meet a deficiency.

Sadād: correctness, soundness and appropriateness in speech or conduct.

Sadīd: correct, sound and considered.

Saddada: set right or made sound; aimed toward a target.

Tasdīd: setting right, correcting direction and guiding rightly.

Musaddad: set right, made sound or guided in the appropriate direction.

Insadda: became closed.

Suddah: a threshold or area before a door or house, in the relevant lexical usage.

Proposed renderings of Qur’anic forms

In Al-Kahf 18:93, al-saddayni means “the two barriers.”

In Al-Kahf 18:94, saddan means “a barrier” whose construction is requested.

In Yā Sīn 36:9, each saddan denotes “a barrier,” placed in front or behind.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:9 and Al-Aḥzāb 33:70, qawlan sadīdan means “sound speech.”

These are translation proposals for the root study; they do not replace installed verse translations or authored explanations.

9. Conclusion

Arabic S D D concerns closing passages, filling gaps and remedying deficiencies.

In another branch, sadād and sadīd concern correctness, soundness and appropriateness of speech or conduct.

The poem attributed to Aws ibn Ḥajar uses nasuddu in confrontation with enemies, although sources differ over its reading and meaning.

Another verse, attributed to both Al-Aʿshā and Kaʿb ibn Zuhayr, uses sadad for fitting, sound speech.

These witnesses document early uses, but attribution and dating are not uniformly certain.

Lexicons also connect this family with filling gaps, meeting deficiencies, aiming correctly and speaking soundly.

S D D occurs six times in the Qur’an.

Al-Kahf 18:93 uses al-saddayn for the two barriers between which Dhū al-Qarnayn arrives.

In Al-Kahf 18:94, the people ask him to build a barrier between them and Gog and Magog.

The continuing story describes construction with iron and other materials and explains its role in preventing passage.

Yā Sīn 36:9 places two barriers before and behind a group.

The image occurs in a context of covering and inability to see or accept warning.

Sadd itself does not mean disbelief or blindness; those concepts come from the complete sentence and context.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:9 presents sound speech in a setting of vulnerable descendants’ rights and verbal responsibility.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:70 gives the same expression as a general command to believers, connected with mindfulness and setting deeds right.

In these verses, sound speech is more than pleasing or beautiful words: it is correct, considered and well-founded.

It should not be restricted to harsh or aggressive speech either.

Soundness follows from truth, direction and content, not loudness or audience acceptance.

The root’s meaning emerges in two main branches: closing passages, gaps or openings and remedying deficiencies; and correctness, soundness and appropriateness in speech and conduct.

Their semantic relationship is understandable: as closing a breach repairs a barrier, sound speech is free of defects and deviation.

This explanatory image should not become a definitive historical origin for every derivative.

For Forqan, “barrier” suits the obstruction forms and “sound” suits sadīd.

The central distinction is that material sadd prevents passage and penetration, while sadīd speech is correct and well-founded in meaning and direction. Neither should be translated as the other.
