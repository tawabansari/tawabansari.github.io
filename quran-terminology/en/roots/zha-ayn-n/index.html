Core meaning: departing, leaving a dwelling place, and moving elsewhere; especially people and families moving from one campsite to another.

1. The root in early Arabic

The three-consonant root ẓ ʿ n in early Arabic denotes leaving one place and going to another. The verb ẓaʿana meant to depart, set out, or leave one's dwelling place.

The root was familiar in Arab nomadic life. Families and groups moving between camps in search of water, pasture, suitable living conditions, or with the changing seasons could be called ẓāʿinūn; their departure was ẓaʿn.

Such movement involves leaving a campsite for another destination, beyond a person's movement within their dwelling area. Family members, animals, belongings, and portable shelters may accompany it.

Accordingly, ẓaʿn contrasts with iqāmah: departure from a dwelling place versus remaining and settling in a place.

The root is not restricted to nomads. Arabic can also call a person ẓāʿin when setting out from one town to another or leaving for trade, pilgrimage, or war.

The basic meaning is therefore leaving one place and setting out elsewhere, with movement between camps as a prominent early example.

Evidence from pre-Islamic poetry

The Muʿallaqah attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā contains:

تَبَصَّرْ خَلِيلِي هَلْ تَرَى مِنْ ظَعَائِنٍ

tabaṣṣar khalīlī hal tarā min ẓaʿāʾinin

تَحَمَّلْنَ بِالْعَلْيَاءِ مِنْ فَوْقِ جُرْثُمِ

taḥammalna bi-l-ʿalyāʾi min fawqi jurthumi

Translation: Look, my friend! Do you see women departing in their camel litters, setting out from the high ground above Jurthum?

Ẓaʿāʾin is the plural of ẓaʿīnah. Here it refers to women traveling in litters mounted on camels, although Arabic also applies the word to the litter or traveling mount itself.

Taḥammalna refers to packing up and setting out. The poet describes a caravan leaving its campsite and departing from high ground.

This couplet appears in established versions of Zuhayr's Muʿallaqah and is explained in older commentaries. Similar opening half-lines occur in other poets' work, so resemblance alone does not establish common authorship. The quoted version with تَحَمَّلْنَ بِالْعَلْيَاءِ مِنْ فَوْقِ جُرْثُمِ is a recognized witness from the poem attributed to Zuhayr.

The example connects this family in pre-Islamic Arabic with caravan departure, leaving camp, and movement with traveling equipment.

Another pre-Islamic example

The Muʿallaqah attributed to ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm contains:

قِفِي قَبْلَ التَّفَرُّقِ يَا ظَعِينَا

qifī qabla al-tafarruqi yā ẓaʿīnā

نُخَبِّرْكِ الْيَقِينَ وَتُخْبِرِينَا

nukhabbirki al-yaqīna wa-tukhbirīnā

Translation: Halt before we part, O departing woman, so that we may tell you the certain truth and you may tell us.

Ẓaʿīnā is a shortened vocative form of ẓaʿīnah addressing a traveling woman: the ending is dropped through vocative truncation, and the final vowel is lengthened for the rhyme.

The poet asks a woman to stop before departure and separation. Here too the word concerns travel and leaving a place.

Besides the root's main meaning, this example shows ẓaʿīnah as an established poetic term for a traveling woman, especially in scenes of caravan departure and parting.

Together these examples attest departure and leaving camp as familiar meanings of ẓ ʿ n in pre-Islamic Arabic. Uses referring to the traveling woman, litter, and traveling camel belong to the same setting.

2. Lexical forms and the word family

Important recorded forms include ظَعَنَ, يَظْعَنُ, ظَعْن, ظَعَن, ظُعُون, ظَاعِن, ظَاعِنَة, ظَعِينَة, ظَعَائِن, أَظْعَنَ, الظَّعُون, and الظِّعَان.

Ẓaʿana: departing and setting out

Ẓaʿana is the past-tense verb; yaẓʿanu is its imperfect.

An illustrative Arabic sentence is:

ظَعَنَ الْقَوْمُ عَنْ دِيَارِهِمْ

ẓaʿana al-qawmu ʿan diyārihim

The people departed from their homes and dwelling places.

The verb denotes movement from one place to another and is ordinarily intransitive: expressing departure does not require a direct object.

When someone causes another person or group to move, aẓʿana may be used: to send someone off or cause their departure.

The person who departs must therefore be distinguished from the person who sends another off.

Ẓaʿn, ẓaʿan, and ẓuʿūn: departure

Ẓaʿn with a vowelless ʿayn, ẓaʿan with a short a on ʿayn, and ẓuʿūn are recorded verbal nouns.

They name the act of departing or setting out.

Al-ẓaʿn may refer to departure between camps, group movement, or individual travel.

Lexical sources also record ظُعُن as a plural of ظَاعِن and ظَعَن as a collective designation for those departing. These uses must be distinguished from verbal nouns naming the act of departure itself.

Ẓāʿin: a departing person or traveler

Ẓāʿin is the active participle of ẓaʿana, denoting someone departing or setting out.

Its contrast, muqīm, denotes someone staying in a place.

أَظَاعِنٌ أَنْتَ أَمْ مُقِيمٌ؟ asks whether someone is departing or staying there.

This contrast shows that ẓaʿn is more specific than bodily motion. Walking inside one's house does not constitute departure from one's dwelling.

Nor must every ẓāʿin leave permanently. They may return later or move from one campsite to another.

Ẓaʿīnah: traveling woman, litter, and mount

Ẓaʿīnah is an important early derivative with several closely related uses.

One use denotes a woman traveling in a litter on a camel.

Another denotes the litter or carriage mounted on the camel to carry a passenger.

The word is also recorded for a camel or mount used for such travel.

Lexicographers differ about which use came first. Some prioritize the traveling woman, others the litter or traveling mount.

All these uses clearly belong to the setting of departure and travel. Woman, litter, and camel are neighboring elements of the same scene, allowing the name of one to be applied to another.

Ẓaʿāʾin is the familiar plural of ẓaʿīnah.

In broader usage, ẓaʿīnah can also mean a wife, even when she is not traveling. This extension should not erase the connection with travel and litters when translating poems about departure.

Al-ẓaʿūn: a traveling mount

Al-ẓaʿūn is recorded for a camel used as a traveling or working mount, including one carrying people or loads.

Some uses refer specifically to a camel ridden by women.

The family thus extends from departure itself to the means used for traveling.

Al-ẓiʿān: a rope securing loads and litters

Al-ẓiʿān also names a rope used to fasten a litter, saddle, or load onto a camel.

Here the word names a particular implement used in travel.

Its association with departure is clear: the rope holds the load and litter in place during movement.

This implement's name should not replace the root's basic definition. Ẓaʿn denotes departure; ẓiʿān names an associated piece of equipment.

3. The Quranic occurrence

The root occurs once in the Quran, as ظَعْنِكُمْ in Al-Naḥl.

وَاللَّهُ جَعَلَ لَكُمْ مِنْ بُيُوتِكُمْ سَكَنًا وَجَعَلَ لَكُمْ مِنْ جُلُودِ الْأَنْعَامِ بُيُوتًا تَسْتَخِفُّونَهَا يَوْمَ ظَعْنِكُمْ وَيَوْمَ إِقَامَتِكُمْ وَمِنْ أَصْوَافِهَا وَأَوْبَارِهَا وَأَشْعَارِهَا أَثَاثًا وَمَتَاعًا إِلَىٰ حِينٍ

wa-llāhu jaʿala lakum min buyūtikum sakanan wa-jaʿala lakum min julūdi al-anʿāmi buyūtan tastakhiffūnahā yawma ẓaʿnikum wa-yawma iqāmatikum wa-min aṣwāfihā wa-awbārihā wa-ashʿārihā athāthan wa-matāʿan ilā ḥīn

Al-Naḥl 16:80

And The God made your homes a place of rest for you, and made for you dwellings from livestock hides, which you find light when you depart and when you stay; and from their wool, soft hair, and hair, furnishings and provision for a time.

Al-Naḥl 16:80

The verse belongs to a sequence describing The God's varied blessings in human life.

Earlier, Al-Naḥl 16:5 mentions livestock and their benefits; Al-Naḥl 16:7 describes their carrying human burdens to distant lands; Al-Naḥl 16:8 mentions animals used for riding and adornment.

Al-Naḥl 16:80 concerns homes, shelter, and materials obtained from livestock.

Yawma ẓaʿnikum: when you depart

Ẓaʿnikum combines the verbal noun ẓaʿn with the pronoun kum, meaning “your departure.”

The phrase follows yawma.

In such a construction, yawm can indicate the time or occasion of an action, without being restricted to a full day or daylight hours.

Yawma ẓaʿnikum therefore naturally means “when you depart” or “at the time of your departure.”

Here ẓaʿn refers to leaving a dwelling place for another campsite.

A significant feature of the passage is the connection between departure and portable dwellings.

The Quran describes dwellings made from livestock hides that people find light when departing and when staying.

The context thus concerns a way of life in which shelter can move with people, beyond the journey of an individual alone.

Wa-yawma iqāmatikum: when you stay

The verse sets ẓaʿn against iqāmah.

Iqāmah, from q w m, is the verbal noun of aqāma. Here it denotes staying or settling in a place.

This contrast is especially helpful for understanding ẓ ʿ n.

When departing, people pack their belongings and shelter and carry them elsewhere. On reaching a new campsite, they can erect the same shelter and stay in it.

One resource therefore serves them in two circumstances: movement and residence.

These states are complementary descriptions, neither inherently morally positive nor negative.

Departure does not necessarily imply displacement, instability, or deprivation, just as staying does not itself imply greater status or wealth. Moving between places can be a normal way of meeting life's needs in nomadic life.

Iqāmah here need not mean permanent, unchanging residence. A family may stay somewhere for a while and depart again.

The principal contrast is between leaving a dwelling place and staying in one, rather than between permanently settled and perpetually moving ways of life.

The role of tastakhiffūnahā

Tastakhiffūnahā, from kh f f, means finding or considering something light.

In context it concerns the lightness and ease of use of dwellings made from livestock hides.

These shelters can be carried during travel and used while staying in a place.

The verse emphasizes their portability and usefulness in different living conditions.

The pronoun hā in tastakhiffūnahā refers to buyūtan: the dwellings made from livestock hides.

What is found light is the portable dwellings, rather than departure itself.

Nor does ẓaʿn mean lightness. Lightness comes from tastakhiffūnahā; ẓaʿnikum helps specify the time or circumstance of using these dwellings.

Dwellings made from livestock hides

Julūd al-anʿām refers to livestock hides.

This material served for shelters that could be packed, carried, and erected again.

The verse continues with wool, soft hair, and hair used for furnishings and necessities.

The distinction matters: hides are associated with portable dwellings, then wool and kinds of hair are mentioned separately with furnishings and provision.

These materials should not be conflated without regard to the sentence's structure.

Such resources had considerable practical value for nomads, since moving heavy belongings and building a new shelter at each campsite could be difficult.

A dwelling that can be packed, carried, and erected again enables adaptation to a changing location.

Ẓaʿn here is situated in practical human life and the need for shelter, transport, and residence.

Departure and divine blessings

Al-Naḥl 16:81 mentions shade, mountain shelters, and coverings that protect against heat and harm.

This continuity presents resources that sustain or ease human life in different environments and conditions.

Verse 80 places portable dwellings alongside residential homes, meeting the need for shelter in differing circumstances.

The blessing therefore extends to people on the move, as well as those living in a settled place.

4. Morphological and structural observations

The Quranic form ẓaʿnikum

Ẓaʿn is the verbal noun of the triliteral verb ẓaʿana.

In Al-Naḥl 16:80, it is attached to kum, producing ẓaʿnikum.

Yawma functions as a time adverbial, with ẓaʿnikum as its genitive complement.

The construction attributes departure to the addressees and specifies the time or circumstance of that action.

Established Quranic readings include ظَعْنِكُمْ and ظَعَنِكُمْ, with a vowelless ʿayn or with a short a respectively.

Both are recorded Arabic verbal nouns, and the difference does not change the basic meaning of departure here.

Forqan's Quranic text uses ظَعْنِكُمْ.

Verbal noun and active participle

The Quranic ẓaʿnikum is a verbal noun naming the act of departing.

Ẓāʿin is an active participle naming the person who departs.

Ẓaʿīnah, in older uses, denotes a traveling woman, litter, or traveling mount.

Although these belong to one family, their grammatical functions and meanings differ. Ẓaʿnikum should not become “your caravans” or “your traveling women,” since those belong to other derivatives.

Movement and residence

The contrast between ẓaʿnikum and iqāmatikum clarifies the root.

If ẓaʿn meant only bodily motion, its contrast with residence would be less specific, because a person can move within their dwelling.

Understanding it as leaving one dwelling place for another makes the contrast clear.

Thus a change of dwelling location matters in this established usage.

In broader Arabic, the verb also covers other journeys and should not be limited to moving an entire family and household.

5. Distinctions from related roots

Ẓ ʿ n (ẓaʿn) and r ḥ l (raḥīl): departure and preparing to travel

R ḥ l concerns travel loads, equipment, mounts, and departure.

Raḥl can denote baggage, a saddle, or a traveler's equipment; riḥlah names a journey.

Quraysh 106:2 mentions رِحْلَةَ الشِّتَاءِ وَالصَّيْفِ, the winter and summer journey.

In Yūsuf 12:70, raḥl denotes a traveler's baggage or belongings.

Ẓaʿn and raḥīl overlap closely in departure. Familiar uses of ẓaʿn emphasize leaving camp or changing dwelling places, while the r ḥ l family ranges over baggage, preparation, and the journey itself.

This is not an absolute division. Both families can describe the same caravan or departure, but their derivatives do not all have identical meanings.

Ẓ ʿ n (ẓaʿn) and s f r (safar): departure and travel

Safar is a general Arabic term for traveling from one place to another.

Al-Baqarah 2:184 uses عَلَىٰ سَفَرٍ for someone on a journey.

Travel may serve trade, visiting, pilgrimage, learning, work, or another purpose.

Early usage of ẓaʿn particularly evokes departure from a dwelling place and movement between camps.

A merchant leaving town for several days is a traveler; a family packing its shelter and belongings for another campsite is a particularly clear example of ẓaʿn.

Nevertheless, ẓaʿn also covers individual journeys, which should not be excluded from its possible range.

The distinction concerns typical emphasis: safar is a broader designation for travel, while ẓaʿn especially emphasizes leaving and departing.

Ẓ ʿ n (ẓaʿn) and h j r (hajara): departure and leaving

H j r concerns leaving, separation, and distancing.

In migration-related uses, leaving a place may involve a particular purpose or circumstance.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:100, for example, speaks of migration in The God's way.

Ẓaʿn itself does not specify a religious purpose, protest, social pressure, or escape from an undesirable environment.

A group moving in search of water or pasture exemplifies ẓaʿn; the movement alone does not imply migration with a particular religious or moral meaning.

Migration may involve departure and relocation, but its meaning is not identical to ẓaʿn.

Rendering yawma ẓaʿnikum as “at the time of your religious migration” would consequently narrow the verse without justification.

Ẓ ʿ n (ẓaʿn) and s y r (sayr): departure and movement

S y r concerns going, moving, and traveling along a route.

In Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:20, سِيرُوا فِي الْأَرْضِ commands travel through the earth.

Sayr can describe movement along a path without a change in residence.

The principal usage of ẓaʿn involves leaving a place and setting out elsewhere.

A person may explore the surroundings of their town and return home; this can be sayr without necessarily being ẓaʿn.

When a group leaves camp for another place, both movement and departure are involved.

Ẓaʿn thus highlights a more specific kind of movement, although both roots belong to the field of motion and travel.

Ẓ ʿ n (ẓaʿn) and ṭ ʿ n (ṭaʿn): distinct roots

The letters ظ and ط are distinct in both Arabic and Persian. In usual Persian pronunciation, ظ has the sound of z and ط the sound of t; these roots are not homophones.

Ẓ ʿ n, written with ظ, concerns departure, travel, and leaving a place.

Ṭ ʿ n, written with ط, concerns thrusting with a spear or pointed implement, and by extension, verbal attack or faultfinding.

Ẓaʿn, departure, must therefore be distinguished from ṭaʿn, stabbing or disparaging.

This is more than a spelling difference: each family has its own root and semantic range.

6. Translation recommendations for Forqan

Depending on construction and context, suitable equivalents include “depart,” “set out,” “leave camp,” and “move from one dwelling place to another.”

“Departed” fits ẓaʿana in its main use. For an individual journey, “set out” or “went traveling” may also fit.

Ẓāʿin can be rendered “departing person” or “traveler setting out.”

For ẓaʿīnah in early caravan imagery, “woman traveling in a camel litter” is clear. If the word denotes the litter or mount, that contextual meaning should be selected.

Ẓaʿāʾin in pre-Islamic verse can be “women departing in camel litters” or “a caravan of traveling women,” where the poem supports that sense.

Proposed translation of Al-Naḥl 16:80

“And The God made your homes a place of rest for you, and made for you dwellings from livestock hides, which you find light when you depart and when you stay; and from their wool, soft hair, and hair, furnishings and provision for a time.”

“When you depart” preserves the sense of leaving one's dwelling without restricting the phrase to one particular day.

Brief root meaning

Departing; leaving a dwelling place and setting out elsewhere.

Context in Al-Naḥl 16:80

Ẓaʿnikum: your departure; moving from one campsite to another, contrasted with staying and residing.

7. Conclusion

In early Arabic, ẓ ʿ n denotes departure, leaving one's dwelling, and moving elsewhere. It is especially evident in nomadic movement involving families, animals, and belongings between camps.

The poems attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā and ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm use ẓaʿāʾin and ẓaʿīnah in caravan departures and scenes of parting, attesting the family's historical association with travel.

The family also supplies terms for traveling women, litters, traveling camels, and ropes securing loads. These belong to the same setting, but the meaning of each derivative must remain distinct from that of the basic verb.

The Quran uses this root once, as the verbal noun ẓaʿnikum in Al-Naḥl 16:80.

There it contrasts with residence, describing people leaving one location for another. Dwellings made of livestock hides serve them both when departing and when staying.

The passage connects the root with practical needs for movement, portable belongings, and shelter. Nevertheless, Arabic usage extends beyond moving an entire family and all its property to other journeys as well.

Its relation to safar, raḥīl, sayr, and hijrah follows the same pattern: all can concern movement, while ẓaʿn especially emphasizes leaving a dwelling place and setting out elsewhere.

Final definition: Ẓ ʿ n denotes departure from one place for another. In early usage it especially described moving between camps; broader usage includes traveling and setting out. In the Quran, ẓaʿnikum names people's departure, contrasted with their residence. The surrounding construction yawma ẓaʿnikum expresses the time of that departure.
