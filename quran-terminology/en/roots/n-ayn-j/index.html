Naʿjah and niʿāj in early Arabic and the Qur’an: animal names, David’s account and justice in judgment

The three-letter Arabic root ن ع ج has several established uses. Its principal word, naʿjah, means a ewe, a female sheep. Some Arabic usages extend the name to female wild cattle and similar animals. Another branch concerns pure whiteness, fine coloration and, in some expressions, fatness.

Arabic sometimes uses naʿjah figuratively or in comparisons for a woman. This matters when examining David’s account: some interpreters understand the ewes in Ṣād as representing women. The existence of such a usage does not by itself prove that women are intended in those verses.

The Qur’an uses this root only in the account of two litigants before David. One says that the other has ninety-nine ewes while he has just one, yet the other wants his ewe as well. David calls the demand unjust; the account then describes David’s trial and the requirement to judge with truth.

A study of N ʿ J should therefore distinguish the established lexical meaning, the possibility of figurative use, and the account’s wider concern with justice and abuse of power.

1. Forms and Semantic Core

Important Arabic forms in this family include:

  • نَعْجَة (naʿjah): a ewe or female sheep; in some uses, a female wild bovine or similar animal.
  • نِعَاج (niʿāj): the plural of naʿjah, denoting ewes or females of the other animals to which the name applies.
  • نَعَجَات (naʿajāt): another plural of naʿjah.
  • نَعَج (naʿaj): pure whiteness; also fatness in some uses.
  • نَعَجَ اللون (naʿaja al-lawnu): its white color became pure and clear.
  • نَاعِج و نَاعِجَة (nāʿij, nāʿijah): finely colored or bright white; sometimes describing camels and other animals.
  • نَعِجَ (naʿija): in some uses, became fat; also became heavy from eating mutton.
  • أَنْعَجَ القوم (anʿaja al-qawmu): their livestock, particularly camels, became fat.
  • نَوَاعِج (nawāʿij): in camel descriptions, swift or finely colored, prized camels.

All these forms are recorded in Arabic, but only naʿjah and its plural niʿāj occur in the Qur’an.

The root occurs four times in Ṣād 38:23 and Ṣād 38:24: naʿjah twice in Ṣād 38:23, and naʿjatika and niʿājihi once each in Ṣād 38:24. Attached pronouns and case endings do not create new derivatives.

Is the Core Whiteness or the Animal Name?

Two groups of uses should be distinguished.

First is the animal name. In ordinary usage, naʿjah identifies a ewe. This alone specifies neither its color, exact age, fertility nor ownership.

Second are uses concerning whiteness, fine coloration and fatness. One lexical analysis takes pure whiteness as the basis for explaining the root and treats naʿjah within that family.

That analysis deserves attention, but independent historical evidence is needed before concluding that the ewe’s name necessarily originated from whiteness. Not all ewes are white. Its application to different animals also shows that the name became established independently of an individual animal’s color.

For the Qur’anic usage, “ewe” is the direct, clear meaning. Whiteness and fine coloration belong to other branches that should be examined separately.

2. Naʿjah and Niʿāj in Early Arabic

Ewes and Female Wild Cattle

The familiar Arabic meaning of naʿjah is a female sheep. Its range is not, however, restricted to domestic sheep in every use.

Lexical usages also record the word for female wild cattle, gazelles and certain mountain animals. The extent of that application is disputed: a narrower explanation restricts niʿāj among wild animals to wild cattle.

Niʿāj al-raml is also an established expression for female wild cattle.

“Ewe” fits ordinary and Qur’anic usage, but niʿāj should not automatically be translated as domestic sheep whenever it appears in poetry.

A Pre-Islamic Poetic Witness: Female Wild Cattle

The pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā describes a landscape:

فَذِرْوَةُ فَالْجِنَابُ كَأَنَّ خُنْسَ النِّعَاجِ الطَّاوِيَاتِ بِهَا الْمُلَاءُ

fa-dhirwatu fa-al-jinābu ka-anna khunsa al-niʿāji al-ṭāwiyāti bihā al-mulāʾu

Translation: “Then Dhirwah and al-Jināb: the slender, short-snouted wild cows there seem like white cloths.”

Niʿāj describes wild animals here. Their bodily features and natural setting show that pre-Islamic poetry could use the word for wild females, particularly wild cattle.

This provides an Arabic use of niʿāj in a setting independent of David’s account.

Niʿāj in Comparisons Describing Women

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī says:

لَا أَعْرِفَنْ رَبْرَبًا حُورًا مَدَامِعُهَا
كَأَنَّ أَبْكَارَهَا نِعَاجُ دَوَّارِ

lā aʿrifan rabraban ḥūran madāmiʿuhā / ka-anna abkārahā niʿāju dawwāri

Translation: “Let me not see a company of women with beautiful, contrasting eyes, their young women like the wild cows of Dawwār.”

The verse warns a group about the risk of their women being taken captive. The comparison with female wild cattle depicts the young women’s beauty and eyes.

The verse has variant transmitted wording. Some versions give the second part as كَأَنَّهُنَّ نِعَاجٌ حَوْلَ دَوَّارِ (ka-annahunna niʿājun ḥawla dawwāri), “as though they were wild cows around Dawwār.” Despite the variant, the comparison involving women is clear.

The witness makes two points: niʿāj can name wild females, and their image can be used to describe women.

Comparison should still be distinguished from direct meaning. A poet comparing a woman to a naʿjah uses the animal’s beauty or features to create an image. That does not make “woman” the necessary meaning of every occurrence of naʿjah in Arabic.

Lexical accounts also explicitly record figurative references to women through naʿjah and shāh. The usage is linguistically possible and established; preferring it in a particular verse still requires contextual evidence.

Whiteness, Fine Coloration and Fatness

In another branch, naʿaj denotes pure whiteness. Nāʿijah and nāʿij also occur in descriptions of animals’ color and beauty.

A rajaz passage attributed to al-ʿAjjāj, a poet of the early Islamic period, says:

فِي نَعِجَاتٍ مِنْ بَيَاضٍ نَعِجَا
كَمَا رَأَيْتَ فِي الْمُلَاءِ الْبَرْدَجَا

fī naʿijātin min bayāḍin naʿijā / ka-mā raʾayta fī al-mulāʾi al-bardajā

Translation: “Among female wild cattle of pure whiteness, like captives you have seen wrapped in garments.”

The witness connects the animal name with whiteness. Bardaj means captives here, rather than patterns on cloth. The image has been explained as comparing the white animals and their dark legs with clothed captives wearing black footwear. Al-ʿAjjāj was not a pre-Islamic poet, so this witness should not be presented as poetry predating the Qur’an.

Elsewhere, naʿijati al-ibil describes camels becoming fat. Nawāʿij can also describe swift camels.

This family extends beyond the ewe’s name. Whiteness, fatness and speed nevertheless cannot be made inherent parts of naʿjah in the Qur’anic verses.

3. Qur’anic Usage: Ninety-Nine Ewes and One Ewe

After recalling David’s strength, rule and wisdom, Ṣād recounts two litigants entering his presence and asking for fair judgment.

One says:

إِنَّ هَٰذَا أَخِي لَهُ تِسْعٌ وَتِسْعُونَ نَعْجَةً وَلِيَ نَعْجَةٌ وَاحِدَةٌ فَقَالَ أَكْفِلْنِيهَا وَعَزَّنِي فِي الْخِطَابِ

inna hādhā akhī lahu tisʿun wa-tisʿūna naʿjatan wa-liya naʿjatun wāḥidatun fa-qāla akfilnīhā wa-ʿazzanī fī al-khiṭābi

Translation: “This is my brother. He has ninety-nine ewes, and I have one ewe. He said: Entrust her to me, and he overcame me in argument.”

Ṣād 38:23

Two situations are contrasted: one person has ninety-nine ewes, the other just one.

The numbers are explicit, so the basic translation should retain ninety-nine and one. “Many” and “a little property” may help explain the imbalance, but should not replace the stated numbers.

Wa-liya naʿjatun wāḥidah emphasizes the complainant’s single ewe. A demand for it from someone already owning ninety-nine highlights the marked difference in their holdings.

Akfilnīhā: A Request to Entrust It

The owner of ninety-nine ewes goes on to say:

أَكْفِلْنِيهَا

akfilnīhā

Translation: “Entrust her to me.”

The verb belongs to K F L. Here it requests that the ewe be placed in his possession or care.

The following wa-ʿazzanī fī al-khiṭāb shows that the complainant experiences himself as overcome and pressured by the other party in argument.

The dispute is therefore about more than livestock numbers. The manner of the demand and one party’s advantage in speaking matter too.

Study of KH Ṭ B

A demand for transfer should still be distinguished from an accomplished seizure. The verse explicitly says that the person asked for the other’s ewe. This wording does not itself say that he had already taken it or that ownership had changed.

This distinction prevents an outcome beyond the text from being added to the account.

David’s Judgment

David replies:

قَالَ لَقَدْ ظَلَمَكَ بِسُؤَالِ نَعْجَتِكَ إِلَىٰ نِعَاجِهِ

qāla la-qad ẓalamaka bi-suʾāli naʿjatika ilā niʿājihi

Translation: “He said: He has indeed wronged you by asking for your ewe to add to his ewes.”

Ṣād 38:24

Naʿjatika is the complainant’s ewe, while niʿājihi refers to the other party’s ewes.

In relation to the request, ilā niʿājihi conveys adding or joining this ewe to the other’s ewes. N ʿ J itself means neither addition, appropriation nor injustice. Those meanings follow from the construction and other words.

David then states a wider principle:

وَإِنَّ كَثِيرًا مِّنَ الْخُلَطَاءِ لَيَبْغِي بَعْضُهُمْ عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ إِلَّا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَعَمِلُوا الصَّالِحَاتِ وَقَلِيلٌ مَّا هُمْ

wa-inna kathīran mina al-khulaṭāʾi la-yabghī baʿḍuhum ʿalā baʿḍin illā alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti wa-qalīlun mā hum

Translation: “Many associates indeed wrong one another, except those who believe and do righteous deeds—and how few they are.”

Ṣād 38:24

David does not restrict the issue to ewe numbers. He describes injustice that can arise in close relationships, partnerships and dealings involving shared interests.

Someone with greater resources may use position or verbal power to take another’s right. Faith and righteous conduct should prevent that injustice.

This ethical implication follows from the account as a whole. Naʿjah itself means neither small possessions, a weak person nor a violated right. Those concerns emerge from the litigants’ relationship and David’s judgment.

4. Does Naʿjah Represent a Woman in David’s Account?

This is a major interpretive question connected with the root.

Arabic records figurative use of naʿjah for a woman, and poetry compares women with female wild animals. Figurative usage cannot therefore be dismissed in advance as linguistically impossible.

Three questions must nevertheless be distinguished:

First, the direct meaning of naʿjah: a ewe or a female of another animal bearing this name.

Second, the possibility of its figurative use for a woman in Arabic.

Third, establishing that the ninety-nine naʿjah and the single naʿjah in David’s account mean particular wives or women.

The first two have lexical support. The third requires evidence from the text itself or reliable independent witnesses.

In Ṣād 38:21, Ṣād 38:22, Ṣād 38:23 and Ṣād 38:24, the Qur’an names no woman and does not explicitly discuss marriage, divorce or a marriage proposal. Its words concern naʿjah, niʿāj, a demand for transfer and an exchange of speech.

Historical interpretations transmit accounts about David, his wives and a woman married to another man. Some are connected with accounts of his life outside the Qur’an.

However familiar such accounts became in interpretive history, they cannot alone turn Qur’anic naʿjah from “ewe” into “wife.”

Nor does the existence of figurative usage authorize attributing all the details of outside accounts to the Qur’an.

Ṣād 38:24 says:

وَظَنَّ دَاوُودُ أَنَّمَا فَتَنَّاهُ فَاسْتَغْفَرَ رَبَّهُ وَخَرَّ رَاكِعًا وَأَنَابَ

wa-ẓanna dāwūdu annamā fatannāhu fa-istaghfara rabbahu wa-kharra rākiʿan wa-anāba

Translation: “David realized that We had tested him, so he sought his Lord’s forgiveness, fell down bowing, and turned back.”

Ṣād 38:24

Ṣād 38:25 also describes forgiveness and David’s good standing.

These verses explicitly describe trial, seeking forgiveness and turning back. They do not detail the exact error or trial in all the particulars supplied by some accounts.

Interpretation may raise questions about David’s manner of judgment, hearing both sides, or his response. These require examination through the verses, rather than having their conclusions placed in advance inside the word naʿjah.

The direct reading concerning ewes is lexically clear and defensible. A figurative reading about women is linguistically possible, but establishing it as the intended meaning requires further evidence.

An outside account should especially not be assumed first and then treated as proven by interpreting every detail of the verse through that assumption.

5. Related Words and Roots

N ʿ J and Ḍ A N

Ḍaʾn denotes wool-bearing sheep, as distinct from goats. It names a livestock group.

In ordinary usage, naʿjah identifies a female of that group.

Ḍaʾn can therefore include both sexes, whereas naʿjah ordinarily names the female animal.

Al-Anʿām 6:143 mentions al-ḍaʾn and al-maʿz and presents the two sexes for each. This helps distinguish an animal-group name from a term specifying sex.

The broader applications of naʿjah to wild animals must still be kept in view. Its precise sense in Arabic poetry and prose depends on context.

Study of Ḍ A N

N ʿ J and GH N M

Ghanam is a general name for sheep and goats and can include the animals of that group collectively.

Naʿjah is more specific: in familiar usage it denotes a female sheep.

Rendering naʿjah simply as “sheep” can lose the specification of sex. “Ewe” or “female sheep” is more precise.

N ʿ J and N ʿ M

Although N ʿ J and N ʿ M share their first two consonants, that spelling similarity alone establishes neither a semantic nor a historical relationship.

Niʿāj is the plural of naʿjah; niʿam and niʿmah belong to N ʿ M.

Niʿāj should not be equated with anʿām either. Anʿām is a broader Qur’anic livestock term; niʿāj names the particular animals at issue in David’s account.

6. Translation Proposals for Forqan

For Qur’anic نَعْجَة (naʿjah), “ewe” is the most precise English equivalent; the corresponding Persian is mīsh.

Mīsh is familiar in Persian and Dari and means a female sheep. A short explanation can supply “female sheep” for readers unfamiliar with the term.

For نِعَاج (niʿāj), “ewes” is appropriate.

In Ṣād 38:23:

لَهُ تِسْعٌ وَتِسْعُونَ نَعْجَةً وَلِيَ نَعْجَةٌ وَاحِدَةٌ

lahu tisʿun wa-tisʿūna naʿjatan wa-liya naʿjatun wāḥidatun

“He has ninety-nine ewes, and I have one ewe.”

In Ṣād 38:24:

بِسُؤَالِ نَعْجَتِكَ إِلَىٰ نِعَاجِهِ

bi-suʾāli naʿjatika ilā niʿājihi

“By asking for your ewe to add to his ewes.”

These renderings preserve the direct meaning without imposing a conclusion about whether the account is figurative.

For Forqan’s word boxes, the following distinctions can be made:

Brief root meaning: a ewe or female sheep; in some Arabic uses, a female wild bovine. Other branches concern whiteness and fine coloration.

Meaning in Ṣād 38:23: a ewe, the female sheep mentioned when stating the two litigants’ holdings.

Meaning in Ṣād 38:24: the complainant’s ewe and the other party’s ewes, with a demand to add the former to the latter’s group.

If figurative reference to a woman is examined in the account’s reflection, it should be discussed separately from the direct translation.

7. Conclusion

N ʿ J has several Arabic uses. Its main word, naʿjah, ordinarily means a ewe, and some early usages extend it to female wild cattle and similar animals. Other branches concern pure whiteness, fine coloration and, sometimes, fatness.

Pre-Islamic poetic witnesses show niʿāj naming wild females and appearing in comparisons describing women. They establish the possibility of figurative imagery without determining the meaning of every occurrence.

The Qur’an uses the root only in the account of two litigants before David. One has ninety-nine ewes, the other just one, yet the wealthier party demands the other’s ewe. David calls the demand unjust. The account continues with David’s trial and the requirement to judge with truth.

Lexically, “ewe” is the clearest rendering. Figurative reference to a woman is established in Arabic, but assigning that meaning to David’s account together with the details of outside historical narratives cannot be established from the Qur’an alone without sufficient evidence.

Final definition: in its animal-naming branch, N ʿ J concerns naʿjah, a ewe or female sheep and, in some usages, a female wild animal. Other branches include pure whiteness, fine coloration and certain bodily conditions. Qur’anic naʿjah and niʿāj mean “ewe” and “ewes” respectively. Figurative interpretations should be assessed separately from the direct lexical meaning, using contextual evidence.
