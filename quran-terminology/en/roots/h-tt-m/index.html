Qur’anic Root Ḥ Ṭ M (ح ط م)

Ḥaṭm: breaking and crushing, from trampling to disintegration and al-Ḥuṭamah

(yaḥṭimannakum)
Form I with emphatic nūn; one occurrence in Al-Naml 27:18

حُطَامًا
(ḥuṭāman)

1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

The Arabic root ح ط م (ḥ ṭ m) concerns breaking, crushing, and disrupting an object’s integrity through pressure or impact. حَطَمَ (ḥaṭama) denotes breaking an object, حُطَام (ḥuṭām) its broken remains, and حُطَمَة (ḥuṭamah) something or someone characterized by forceful breaking and crushing.

Earlier usages include breaking bones, fragmenting dry branches, trampling, and injury caused by crowd pressure. The family also describes severe drought, a worn-out animal, and a herdsman who drives animals harshly.

The root occurs six times in three forms in the Qur’an:

  • يَحْطِمَنَّكُمْ (yaḥṭimannakum): lest they crush or trample you; Al-Naml 27:18.
  • حُطَامًا (ḥuṭāman): dry, broken plant fragments; Al-Zumar 39:21, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:65, and Al-Ḥadīd 57:20.
  • الْحُطَمَة (al-ḥuṭamah): the crusher or shatterer; Al-Humazah 104:4 and Al-Humazah 104:5, naming the fire of punishment.

Other Arabic forms include حَطَمَ (ḥaṭama), “he broke”; حَطَّمَ (ḥaṭṭama), “he crushed or shattered”; انْحَطَمَ (inḥaṭama) and تَحَطَّمَ (taḥaṭṭama), “it broke”; حَطِيم (ḥaṭīm), broken or crushed; and حَطْمَة (ḥaṭmah), destructive hardship. These particular forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

The Qur’anic occurrences are few but cover a significant range: ants potentially crushed beneath an army, plants becoming dry fragments, and al-Ḥuṭamah in the setting of punishment in the hereafter.

Breaking and disintegration connect these usages. The action, its result, and something named for its crushing force should nevertheless remain distinct.

2. The semantic centre and earlier Arabic witnesses

Breaking as the root’s main meaning

Lexicons record حَطَمَ الشَّيْءَ (ḥaṭama al-shayʾa) as breaking something. Some describe its scope generally; others emphasize breaking hard or dry objects, such as bones and wood.

Both fit the recorded family. Breaking can result from impact, pressure, or collision, but these are not separate defining components of every derivative. Breaking and the loss of an object’s integrity form the semantic centre.

حَطَمْتُهُ (ḥaṭamtuhu) means “I broke it.” Inḥaṭama and taḥaṭṭama express being broken or coming apart.

Where breaking produces multiple pieces, crushing becomes more prominent. No particular number or size of fragments should be assumed for every occurrence.

Ḥuṭām: what has been broken into pieces

Ḥuṭām denotes material broken into fragments. Earlier Arabic particularly recognizes it for dry, broken vegetation.

As grass or plant stems dry, they become more brittle and can fragment underfoot or through wind and movement. These dry fragments are called ḥuṭām.

Lexical usage also records حُطَامُ الْبَيْضِ (ḥuṭām al-bayḍ) for broken eggshells. The word is therefore not confined to plants, although all three Qur’anic occurrences concern vegetation or crops.

Ḥuṭām does not merely mean something dry. Drying can prepare the way for breaking, but the word emphasizes fragmentation and broken remains.

This matters in translating Al-Zumar 39:21 and Al-Ḥadīd 57:20, where yellowing precedes becoming ḥuṭām. Rendering both stages merely “drying” erases the distinction.

Crushing through pressure and crowding

Ḥaṭm is not confined to inanimate objects. The family can also describe people or animals injured through crowding and pressure from one another.

Lexicons record حُطَم (ḥuṭam) for someone who drives livestock so harshly that they press against one another and suffer injury.

A transmitted rajaz fragment reads:

قَدْ لَفَّهَا اللَّيْلُ بِسَوَّاقٍ حُطَمْ

“Night has enfolded them with a harsh and relentless drover.”

Ḥuṭam describes a drover who drives forcefully and without care. It is not restricted to actually breaking bones, but concerns harshness that can lead to trampling and injury.

The fragment has differing attributions. Al-Aghānī attributes it to Rashīd ibn Rumayḍ al-ʿAnazī, while other transmissions name Al-Ḥuṭam al-Qaysī or Abū Zughbah. The line alone cannot settle its poet and precise date, or establish it as certainly pre-Islamic.

Its value is to show a usage connecting breaking and crushing with pressure arising from driving and crowding.

Ḥaṭmah: drought and destructive hardship

Arabic records حَطْمَة (ḥaṭmah) and related forms for a harsh year or severe drought in which plants and pasture perish and people and livestock suffer.

A verse attributed to Dhū al-Khiraq al-Ṭuhawī reads:

مِنْ حَطْمَةٍ أَقْبَلَتْ حَتَّتْ لَنَا وَرَقًا
نُمَارِسُ الْعُودَ حَتَّى يَنْبُتَ الْوَرَقُ

Its sense is: “A severe drought came and stripped our leaves; we struggle with hardship until the leaves grow again.”

Ḥaṭmah denotes severe drought. Its relationship with breaking lies in drought’s destruction of vegetation and the means of life. In context, نُمَارِسُ الْعُودَ (numārisu al-ʿūda) evokes endurance in deprivation and struggling with life’s hardships; it should not be reduced to a report of consuming wood and branches. A variant reads نمارس العيش (numārisu al-ʿaysh), “we struggle with life.”

Sources attribute the poem to someone known as Dhū al-Khiraq al-Ṭuhawī, but the poet’s identity and the attribution of some verses are disputed. The witness is useful lexically without establishing a certain date for the meaning’s origin. Khizānat al-adab also discusses several poets bearing the name Dhū al-Khiraq and the attribution of the poetry.

حَاطُوم (ḥāṭūm) is likewise recorded for destructive hardship or drought. Breaking can thus extend beyond injury to one object to an event that destroys many things.

Drought is not, however, part of the root’s permanent meaning. Ḥaṭm can concern breaking something entirely unconnected with drought.

Wear and the loss of strength

حَطِم (ḥaṭim) describes a horse or other animal that has lost its former strength through age or weakness in some Arabic usages.

This connects with an image of strength breaking down, without necessarily implying broken bones.

The root can express weakness and wear figuratively in such a context. That does not make ageing or weakness its meaning in every Qur’anic occurrence.

Ḥaṭīm as a recognized name

Besides meaning broken or crushed, حَطِيم (ḥaṭīm) also names a location within the precinct of the Kaʿbah.

Explanations connect the name variously with crowds, a remaining part of the building, or other events.

These explanations are neither uniform nor certain. The name alone cannot establish the Kaʿbah’s architectural history or the definitive reason for the naming.

Ḥaṭīm does not occur in the Qur’an and should not be equated with al-Ḥuṭamah in Al-Humazah.

3. Lā yaḥṭimannakum: the ants’ danger of being trampled

Al-Naml 27:18

حَتَّىٰ إِذَا أَتَوْا عَلَىٰ وَادِ النَّمْلِ قَالَتْ نَمْلَةٌ يَا أَيُّهَا النَّمْلُ ادْخُلُوا مَسَاكِنَكُمْ لَا يَحْطِمَنَّكُمْ سُلَيْمَانُ وَجُنُودُهُ وَهُمْ لَا يَشْعُرُونَ

“When they reached the valley of the ants, an ant said, ‘Ants, enter your dwellings, lest Solomon and his soldiers crush and trample you without realizing it.’”

This is the root’s only verbal occurrence in the Qur’an.

Breaking and trampling

يَحْطِمَنَّكُمْ (yaḥṭimannakum) comes from the basic triliteral verb ḥaṭama and carries an emphatic nūn.

The ants face injury as Solomon and his soldiers pass. Breaking here therefore concerns crushing or trampling under the pressure of the army’s movement.

“Crush you” stays close to the basic meaning, while “trample you underfoot” conveys the scene more naturally.

Ḥaṭm itself should not be restricted to stepping on something. Trampling is this verse’s contextual instance of breaking, rather than the root’s full definition.

Elsewhere the root concerns broken vegetation or the fire of punishment, without human feet or an army being involved.

Unintended harm

The closing وَهُمْ لَا يَشْعُرُونَ (wa-hum lā yashʿurūna) matters. Its plural pronoun refers to Solomon and his soldiers: they might pass without noticing the ants or their injury.

The danger can therefore arise without the agents’ awareness or direct intention.

This comes from the closing clause, rather than ḥaṭm itself. Breaking can be deliberate or unintended; the root alone does not determine intention.

Nor does the verse say that the ants were actually crushed. The ant warns and calls for prevention. An anticipated danger should remain distinct from an actual event.

Ḥaṭm and differences in creatures’ power

A large army is passing while ants face danger. Despite their difference in size and power, the ant recognizes the risk and calls the others into their dwellings.

In Al-Naml 27:19, Solomon smiles at the ant’s words and turns to prayer. This places the ant’s conduct within the story of his understanding and responsibility.

A root study should not derive from ḥaṭm alone a theory about ant anatomy, the material of their external covering, or a special scientific property of their crushing.

Arabic already recognizes ḥaṭm for breaking and injury through pressure. Its application to ants fits that linguistic meaning without requiring a new scientific claim.

4. Ḥuṭām: the end of growth and the fragmentation of plants

Three further Qur’anic occurrences concern vegetation and crops. In each, ḥuṭāman describes plants reduced to broken fragments.

Their contexts nevertheless differ.

Al-Zumar: from growth to fragmentation

Al-Zumar 39:21

أَلَمْ تَرَ أَنَّ اللَّهَ أَنْزَلَ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً فَسَلَكَهُ يَنَابِيعَ فِي الْأَرْضِ ثُمَّ يُخْرِجُ بِهِ زَرْعًا مُخْتَلِفًا أَلْوَانُهُ ثُمَّ يَهِيجُ فَتَرَاهُ مُصْفَرًّا ثُمَّ يَجْعَلُهُ حُطَامًا ۚ إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَذِكْرَىٰ لِأُولِي الْأَلْبَابِ

“Have you not seen that The God has sent down water from the sky and channelled it as springs in the earth? Then He brings forth crops of different colours through it; then they wither and you see them yellowing; then He makes them dry, broken fragments. In this is indeed a reminder for people of understanding.”

The verse presents distinct stages: water, growth, varied colours, withering, yellowing, and finally becoming ḥuṭām.

ثُمَّ يَهِيجُ فَتَرَاهُ مُصْفَرًّا (thumma yahīju fa-tarāhu muṣfarran) describes the plant losing freshness and becoming yellow.

ثُمَّ يَجْعَلُهُ حُطَامًا (thumma yajʿaluhu ḥuṭāman) follows. Ḥuṭām is therefore distinct from yellowing alone: it is the stage of dry, broken fragments.

The sequence makes “dry” or “yellow” alone inadequate equivalents. “Dry plant fragments” is more precise.

يَجْعَلُهُ (yajʿaluhu) is attributed to The God, placing the transformation within His action.

That attribution does not replace an explanation of natural processes, and the verse does not detail how stems physically break apart.

The ending makes the whole transformation a reminder for people of understanding. This emerges from the complete image of growth and decay, rather than ḥuṭām alone.

Al-Wāqiʿah: the possibility of losing a crop

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:65

لَوْ نَشَاءُ لَجَعَلْنَاهُ حُطَامًا فَظَلْتُمْ تَفَكَّهُونَ

“If We willed, We would make it dry, broken fragments, and you would remain astonished and lamenting.”

The verse presents the possibility of a crop becoming ḥuṭām.

The referent of هُ (hu) in لَجَعَلْنَاهُ (la-jaʿalnāhu) requires the preceding verses. Al-Wāqiʿah 56:63 and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:64 concern what people sow and who makes it grow.

The pronoun therefore concerns the crop and its growth.

The construction differs from Al-Zumar 39:21. There the plant’s transformation was described in stages; here a conditional sentence presents the possibility of reducing the crop to fragments.

The crop should not therefore be assumed to have reached its natural end before breaking apart.

لَوْ نَشَاءُ (law nashāʾu) allows the crop’s destruction whenever divine will determines it. The context draws attention to the harvest’s dependence on something beyond human effort.

فَظَلْتُمْ تَفَكَّهُونَ (fa-ẓaltum tafakkahūna) follows. Explanations of this verb in context include astonishment, regret, and lament.

Regret and astonishment are not part of ḥuṭām’s meaning. They are the response of those losing their crop.

“Dry, broken fragments” suits ḥuṭām here. “A destroyed crop” explains the contextual result but conveys less of the lexical image of fragmentation.

Al-Ḥadīd: fragmented vegetation as an image of worldly life

Al-Ḥadīd 57:20

اعْلَمُوا أَنَّمَا الْحَيَاةُ الدُّنْيَا لَعِبٌ وَلَهْوٌ وَزِينَةٌ وَتَفَاخُرٌ بَيْنَكُمْ وَتَكَاثُرٌ فِي الْأَمْوَالِ وَالْأَوْلَادِ ۖ كَمَثَلِ غَيْثٍ أَعْجَبَ الْكُفَّارَ نَبَاتُهُ ثُمَّ يَهِيجُ فَتَرَاهُ مُصْفَرًّا ثُمَّ يَكُونُ حُطَامًا ۖ وَفِي الْآخِرَةِ عَذَابٌ شَدِيدٌ وَمَغْفِرَةٌ مِنَ اللَّهِ وَرِضْوَانٌ ۚ وَمَا الْحَيَاةُ الدُّنْيَا إِلَّا مَتَاعُ الْغُرُورِ

“Know that worldly life is play, distraction, adornment, boasting among yourselves, and rivalry in wealth and children. It is like rain whose vegetation delights the farmers; then it withers and you see it yellowing; then it becomes dry, broken fragments. In the hereafter are severe punishment, and forgiveness and approval from The God. Worldly life is only deceptive enjoyment.”

The same image of growth, yellowing, and fragmentation expresses the transience of worldly appearances. “Farmers” in the translation reflects one recorded interpretation of الْكُفَّار (al-kuffār), explained through covering seed with soil. “Disbelievers” is also reported. The alternatives belong in the study of ك ف ر (k f r); ḥuṭām itself does not decide between them.

Al-Zumar 39:21 presents the process as a sign for people of understanding. Al-Ḥadīd 57:20 places it within an analogy for worldly life.

One natural image can therefore carry different messages in different contexts without changing ḥuṭām’s lexical meaning.

Both verses describe dry, broken plant fragments. In Al-Ḥadīd, this stage connects with the transience of adornment, boasting, and rivalry in worldly life.

Does ḥuṭām mean absolute worthlessness?

Lexically, ḥuṭām denotes something broken into fragments. For plants, this normally accompanies loss of freshness and the end of growth.

The word does not establish that everything fragmented is entirely useless, or that every completed stage of life loses its value.

Dry plant remains, for example, can have practical uses. Ḥuṭām itself does not pronounce an economic or ethical judgment on every such use.

Al-Wāqiʿah concerns losing a desired crop. Al-Ḥadīd uses ḥuṭām to picture worldly appearances’ transience. These implications emerge from the full verses, rather than the root’s independent definition.

Nor should ḥuṭām always be translated as worldly wealth. Its Qur’anic lexical applications concern plants; only Al-Ḥadīd 57:20 places it within the analogy for worldly life.

5. Al-Ḥuṭamah: the Crusher and Shatterer

Al-Humazah 104:4

كَلَّا لَيُنْبَذَنَّ فِي الْحُطَمَةِ

“No indeed! He will certainly be cast into the Crusher.”

Al-Humazah 104:5

وَمَا أَدْرَاكَ مَا الْحُطَمَةُ

“And what has made you aware of what the Crusher is?”

Both verses use الْحُطَمَة (al-ḥuṭamah).

The morphology of ḥuṭamah

Ḥuṭamah follows فُعَلَة (fuʿalah), a pattern that can describe something or someone in whom an action is intense or prominent.

It can therefore be understood as a forceful crusher or shatterer.

The final tāʾ does not indicate a female person. It can contribute to an intensive description.

Arabic also records ḥuṭamah for someone who eats voraciously and for a harsh herdsman. Those other applications should not determine the meaning in Al-Humazah.

Here al-Ḥuṭamah names or describes a fire identified later in the chapter. Crushing establishes its lexical connection with ḥ ṭ m.

Al-Ḥuṭamah in the chapter’s context

Al-Humazah 104:1, Al-Humazah 104:2, and Al-Humazah 104:3 condemn a habitual faultfinder and taunter who accumulates wealth and imagines that it has made him immortal.

Verse four rejects that supposition and describes his outcome with لَيُنْبَذَنَّ فِي الْحُطَمَةِ (la-yunbadhanna fī al-ḥuṭamah).

لَيُنْبَذَنَّ (la-yunbadhanna) belongs to another root and denotes being cast away. Being thrown or cast should not be made part of ḥuṭamah itself.

Al-Ḥuṭamah names what he is cast into; crushing is the recognized meaning behind the name.

The question in verse five highlights the subject’s gravity and dread. Its referent must be identified from the following verse.

How does the Qur’an identify al-Ḥuṭamah?

Al-Humazah 104:6

نَارُ اللَّهِ الْمُوقَدَةُ

“The kindled fire of The God.”

This identifies al-Ḥuṭamah as the fire of punishment in this context.

Its reference to fire should not be discarded by reducing it to psychological pressure, social defeat, a collapse of personality, or loss of reputation.

The verses nevertheless do not explain the physical mechanism of crushing. How the fire affects people, what it crushes and how, and the nature of the punishment do not follow from the root alone.

Al-Humazah 104:7 describes the fire reaching the hearts. That description belongs in interpreting that verse; ḥaṭm’s lexical meaning alone does not determine the event’s nature.

Al-Ḥuṭamah, humazah, and lumazah

هُمَزَة (humazah) and لُمَزَة (lumazah) appear at the chapter’s beginning and are morphologically close to ḥuṭamah.

The structural relationship matters to the chapter’s language. Faultfinding and taunting appear first, and the person subsequently faces al-Ḥuṭamah.

A shared pattern does not make ه م ز (h m z), ل م ز (l m z), and ح ط م (ḥ ṭ m) identical in meaning.

Hamz and lamz concern faultfinding and taunting in their Qur’anic applications, while ḥaṭm concerns breaking and crushing.

A literary and ethical correspondence can be recognized between demeaning others and the shattering outcome, but that correspondence is not the root’s lexical definition.

Nor does a person figuratively crushing others with words establish that the Qur’an specifies a particular bodily crushing as a matching penalty. Those details are not stated.

What is clear is the condemnation of faultfinding, rejection of imagined immortality through wealth, and identification of al-Ḥuṭamah as the fire of punishment.

6. Comparison with related roots

Ḥ Ṭ M (ḥaṭm) and H SH M (hashm): crushing and brittle fragments

The roots are close in breaking and fragmentation.

هَشْم (hashm) denotes breaking and crushing, especially dry and brittle objects. هَشِيم (hashīm) also denotes dry, fragmented vegetation.

Al-Kahf 18:45 describes vegetation becoming hashīm and being scattered by the wind.

This resembles ḥuṭām in Al-Zumar 39:21 and Al-Ḥadīd 57:20.

The distinction should not become an absolute rule that ḥaṭm always involves harder breaking and hashm softer breaking. Both can describe dry objects fragmenting.

Their precise difference requires examining each root’s independent usages.

Ḥ Ṭ M (ḥaṭm) and K S R (kasr): breaking and coming apart

كَسْر (kasr) generally means breaking something. Ḥaṭm shares this field and can emphasize fragmentation and more forceful breaking.

Lexicons describe ḥaṭm both generally and with particular reference to hard or dry objects.

Not every partial break must therefore be kasr and every complete break ḥaṭm. The evidence does not establish an exceptionless division.

Al-Naml 27:18 concerns crushing under pressure. The ḥuṭām verses concern the result of dry plants breaking apart.

Ḥ Ṭ M (ḥaṭm) and D K K (dakk): crushing and pounding down

د ك ك (d k k) concerns pounding down and being smashed in Qur’anic usage.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:143 uses دَكًّا (dakkan) of a mountain; Al-Ḥāqqah 69:14 describes the pounding of the earth and mountains.

Destruction and the loss of structure bring these usages close to ḥaṭm, without making them absolute synonyms.

Dakk can highlight pounding down, levelling, or shattering; ḥaṭm highlights breaking and crushing.

Their difference does not establish a particular implement or physical force for every verse.

Ḥ Ṭ M (ḥuṭām) and Ḥ Ṣ D (ḥaṣīd): fragmented remains and reaping

حَصِيد (ḥaṣīd), from ح ص د (ḥ ṣ d), concerns reaping and cutting crops.

Some verses use this family for vegetation or things destroyed like reaped crops.

Qur’anic ḥuṭām, however, denotes dry, broken plant fragments.

A crop can be reaped without becoming small, brittle pieces. Dry vegetation can also break through other causes without anyone reaping it.

“Reaped” is therefore not a precise equivalent for ḥuṭām, nor can “plant fragments” replace ḥaṣīd’s basic meaning.

Ḥ Ṭ M (ḥaṭm) and Q Ṣ M (qaṣm): breaking and destruction

قَصْم (qaṣm) concerns forceful breaking and destruction. Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:11 applies the root to the destruction of wrongdoing settlements.

Ḥaṭm also concerns injury and shattering, but not every usage concerns the destruction of a community.

Al-Naml 27:18 describes the ants’ risk of bodily injury; the ḥuṭām verses describe vegetation becoming dry fragments.

A community’s ethical or historical destruction is therefore not part of ḥaṭm’s definition.

Ḥ Ṭ M (ḥaṭm) and Ḥ Ṭ Ṭ (ḥaṭṭ): distinct roots

Ḥ Ṭ M and Ḥ Ṭ Ṭ share their first two letters and resemble one another in writing, but are different roots.

ح ط ط (ḥ ṭ ṭ) concerns putting down, lowering, or removing a burden in Arabic and Qur’anic usage.

For example, حِطَّة (ḥiṭṭah) in Al-Baqarah 2:58 belongs to ḥ ṭ ṭ, rather than ḥ ṭ m.

Ḥuṭamah should therefore not be translated as putting down a burden, forgiveness, or lowering something.

Phonetic proximity alone also does not establish a historical connection.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

For ḥaṭama, “break” or “crush” is an appropriate basic equivalent. “Shatter” can be more natural where intensity and scattered fragments stand out.

In Al-Naml 27:18, lā yaḥṭimannakum can be rendered “lest they crush and trample you underfoot.” This preserves breaking while fitting the army’s passage.

“Lest they trample you” is also brief and natural. The root explanation should nevertheless retain breaking and crushing.

For حُطَامًا (ḥuṭāman) in Al-Zumar 39:21 and Al-Ḥadīd 57:20, “dry, broken fragments” is suitable. A fluent translation can say “becomes dry plant fragments.”

The same meaning should remain in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:65. “A ruined crop” conveys the contextual result but not the full lexical image of fragmentation.

“Ash” is not a basic equivalent for ḥuṭām, since burning is not inherent in the root. Something can become ḥuṭām without burning.

For الْحُطَمَة (al-ḥuṭamah) in Al-Humazah 104:4 and Al-Humazah 104:5, “the Crusher” or “the Shatterer” is appropriate. The contextual explanation should show that the following verse identifies the title with the fire of punishment.

A complete chapter translation can retain al-Ḥuṭamah at its first occurrence with a brief explanation, since it serves as a specific designation for the fire.

“The crushing fire” is clear in the wider context, provided it is recognized that fire becomes explicit in verse six, rather than being inherent in ḥuṭamah’s morphology.

8. Conclusion

The Arabic root ḥ ṭ m concerns breaking, crushing, and the loss of structural integrity. This is clear in hard and dry objects, trampling under pressure, and broken vegetation.

The sole Qur’anic verb occurs in Al-Naml 27:18, where an ant warns the others against being crushed beneath Solomon’s army. It concerns bodily injury through pressure, and the closing clause allows for the agents’ unawareness.

Ḥuṭām denotes dry, broken plant fragments in three other verses. Al-Zumar 39:21 makes it part of the sequence of growth and decay; Al-Wāqiʿah 56:65 presents the possible destruction of a crop; Al-Ḥadīd 57:20 uses it within an analogy for worldly appearances’ transience.

Al-Ḥuṭamah is another form expressing crushing force. The chapter subsequently identifies it with the fire of punishment. That reference should neither be removed nor supplemented with confident physical details beyond the Qur’anic text.

Three levels should remain distinct: Ḥaṭm is the action of breaking and crushing; ḥuṭām is its result and fragmented remains, especially of dry vegetation; and ḥuṭamah names or describes something characterized by intense crushing.

These usages form a clear semantic family without making the agent’s intention, breaking’s ethical value, the cause of a crop’s destruction, or the nature of punishment part of the lexical definition.
