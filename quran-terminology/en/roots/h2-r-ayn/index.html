Haraʿ: hastening and being impelled toward something by strong motivation, pressure, or excitement

1. Meaning in early Arabic

Hā–rāʾ–ʿayn concerns haste in movement, especially when a person or group rushes somewhere through strong motivation, fear, desire, anger, or external pressure.

Arabic dictionaries record haraʿ and ihrāʿ for hurried movement, driving along in haste, and movement accompanied by excitement or agitation.

A group hearing news may suddenly rush toward an event through concern, curiosity, or eagerness. Prisoners might also be hurried toward a destination despite having no wish to go there.

Both situations involve haste, but its causes differ: inner motivation in the first example, and external pressure in the second.

Arabic lexical accounts highlight speed, a motivating impulse or pressure, and, in some uses, accompanying trembling or agitation.

Some scholars describe ihrāʿ as haste accompanied by trembling, giving cold, anger, fear, or fever as examples. Other accounts emphasize hastening and being impelled without making trembling obligatory.

The root is therefore not confined to fear or trembling. Its movement-related core is hastening and proceeding somewhere hurriedly, with varying motives or pressures.

A poetic witness attributed to pre-Islamic Arabic

A line attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Muhalhil ibn Rabīʿah reads:

فَجَاءُوا يُهْرَعُونَ وَهُمْ أُسَارَى

fa-jāʾū yuhraʿūna wa-hum usārā

نَقُودُهُمْ عَلَى رَغْمِ الْأُنُوفِ

naqūduhum ʿalā raghmi al-unūf

“They came hastening as prisoners, while we drove them on against their will.”

The poet describes prisoners moving quickly, without freely choosing that movement. Others drive and compel them onward.

Yuhraʿūna here expresses speed within a situation involving external pressure and direction.

Early dictionaries attribute the line to Muhalhil, and it appears in collections attributed to him. Its precise date and an attribution independent of later transmission are not securely established here. It is evidence from a poetic tradition attributed to the pre-Islamic period, rather than conclusive proof of the root’s earliest use.

Its semantic importance is that ihrāʿ can describe hurried movement involving an external cause.

This does not mean everyone described by yuhraʿu must be physically driven by another. The Quran also uses the form for people hurrying through their own motivation and desire.

2. Forms and semantic branches

Important recorded forms include:

  • هَرَعَ: hastened or moved hurriedly; هَرِعَ occurs in the branch denoting flowing.
  • هَرَع and هُرَاع: intense haste, hurried movement, or rapid driving.
  • أَهْرَعَ: hastened or moved hurriedly.
  • إِهْرَاع: hastening; rapid movement with excitement or agitation.
  • هُرِعَ and أُهْرِعَ: entered hurried movement or was impelled to hasten.
  • مُهْرَع: someone moving hurriedly or impelled into such movement.
  • مَهْرُوع: in some uses, someone driven to move or overcome by severe weakness and strain.
  • هَرِع: swift-moving, quick to weep, or flowing, according to context.
  • تَهَرَّعَ: hurried toward something.
  • هَيْرَع: in some uses, a swift wind scattering dust; elsewhere, a weak or unsteady person.

These forms are recorded in Arabic, but the Quran uses only yuhraʿūna from this family.

Semantic core: hurried movement and motivation

In descriptions of human movement, the root concerns speed and haste.

Unlike a general verb of motion, it emphasizes haste that may arise through attraction, pressure, or excitement.

Someone leaving home calmly according to a plan is moving. When news so excites that person that they hurry somewhere, hurried movement becomes the more appropriate description.

Such haste may stem from eagerness, fear, anger, or desire. Other people may also pressure someone to move quickly.

The hurried movement and its cause must therefore be distinguished.

Trembling and agitation

Uhriʿa al-rajul, written أُهْرِعَ الرجل, is also recorded for someone trembling through cold, anger, fever, or fear.

The body’s movement or condition then involves agitation.

Ibn Fāris identifies movement and agitation as a common axis of the root’s uses. This can illuminate the connection between haste, trembling, and some other senses.

These similarities alone do not establish a historical reconstruction of every branch from one original meaning. Nor should bodily trembling or fear be imposed on every Quranic occurrence of yuhraʿūna.

Flowing

Hariʿa is also recorded for the flowing of liquids.

Damun hariʿun denotes flowing blood. The family also describes running tears and continuous flow.

Rapid human movement and continuous liquid flow show a semantic resemblance, but resemblance alone does not establish their historical derivational relationship.

Quranic yuhraʿūna describes human movement, not flowing blood or tears. Liquid flow should therefore not replace hurried movement in translating these verses.

Other uses

Dictionaries also record hariʿ for someone quick to weep and hayraʿ for a weak or easily frightened person.

Rīḥun hayraʿun describes a swift wind that lifts and scatters dust.

Speed, agitation, or inability to maintain a previous state may be observed across these uses. They should not all be forced into an artificial definition without independent evidence.

Weakness and fear, in particular, are not invariant meanings of Quranic yuhraʿūna. Its two contexts concern hurried movement, whose more specific significance must be examined separately.

3. The morphology of yuhraʿūna

The Quranic form has an important grammatical feature.

Yuhraʿūna is a third-person masculine plural imperfect verb. In the familiar reading, initial yāʾ bears ḍammah and rāʾ before ʿayn bears fatḥah, giving a passive form.

Arabic records both huriʿa and uhriʿa. Their passive imperfects can coincide as yuhraʿu. The written form alone therefore cannot establish one exclusive derivation independently of linguistic usage.

Some morphological indexes classify the Quranic form under the basic triliteral verb, whereas many lexical explanations analyze it through uhriʿa and ihrāʿ.

This difference does not fundamentally alter the verses’ contextual meaning, but should remain visible.

Does passive morphology mean compulsion?

An Arabic passive usually omits the agent or focuses on the recipient of the action.

With yuhraʿūna, however, the established usage of this particular family also matters.

Arabic scholars explain that some forms use passive morphology while referring to the person’s own hastening.

An external factor may cause the movement, or an inward motive such as fear, anger, or eagerness may impel it.

For example, we might say, “The news suddenly set him in motion.”

The person moves, while the news causes the haste.

In other cases, people may be made to move through pressure from others.

Passive yuhraʿūna should therefore not invariably become “they are forcibly driven.” Its distinctive construction should likewise not disappear into an undifferentiated general verb of running.

Both Quranic occurrences concern groups impelled toward a destination by some attraction or motive. The surrounding verses identify that motive.

4. Quranic occurrences

The root occurs twice: Hūd 11:78 and Al-Ṣāffāt 37:70.

Both use yuhraʿūna, but in different settings.

Hūd 11:78: the haste of Lot’s people

وَجَاءَهُ قَوْمُهُ يُهْرَعُونَ إِلَيْهِ وَمِنْ قَبْلُ كَانُوا يَعْمَلُونَ السَّيِّئَاتِ قَالَ يَا قَوْمِ هَؤُلَاءِ بَنَاتِي هُنَّ أَطْهَرُ لَكُمْ فَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ وَلَا تُخْزُونِ فِي ضَيْفِي أَلَيْسَ مِنْكُمْ رَجُلٌ رَشِيدٌ

wa-jāʾahu qawmuhu yuhraʿūna ilayhi wa-min qablu kānū yaʿmalūna al-sayyiʾāti qāla yā qawmi hāʾulāʾi banātī hunna aṭharu lakum fa-ittaqū allāha wa-lā tukhzūni fī ḍayfī a-laysa minkum rajulun rashīd

Hūd 11:78

“And his people came rushing to him; previously they had been doing evil deeds. He said: My people, these are my daughters; they are purer for you. So be mindful of The God, and do not disgrace me concerning my guests. Is there not among you a man of sound judgment?”

Yuhraʿūna describes the people’s state as they came to Lot.

The principal verb is jāʾahu: they came to him.

Yuhraʿūna functions circumstantially, explaining how they came: they were moving toward Lot hurriedly.

Ilayhi specifies the direction, toward Lot.

Yuhraʿūna itself therefore does not mean coming to Lot. Coming belongs to jāʾahu, direction to ilayhi, and haste and manner to yuhraʿūna.

What motivated their haste?

Hūd 11:77 states that the divine Messengers came to Lot and that he became distressed concerning them.

In Hūd 11:78, his people then rush toward him, and their previous evil conduct is mentioned.

In Hūd 11:79, they tell Lot that he knows what they want.

The sequence shows purposeful haste rather than mere aimless speed: they approached Lot with a particular desire in a context of wrongdoing.

Their haste can therefore be connected here with eagerness and intense desire for their objective.

Such desire is not the direct, invariant meaning of yuhraʿūna; this context supplies it.

Nor does passive morphology establish that an unknown force physically compelled them to move.

They came toward Lot through their own wishes, although their haste could be so intense as to picture desire driving them onward.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:70: hurriedly following predecessors

فَهُمْ عَلَىٰ آثَارِهِمْ يُهْرَعُونَ

fa-hum ʿalā āthārihim yuhraʿūn

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:70

“So they hurry along in their footsteps.”

The verse describes people following their predecessors’ path rather than examining the truth.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:69 says that they found their fathers astray.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:70 then depicts them hurrying in those footsteps.

Yuhraʿūna here no longer describes a group moving toward a particular person’s house; it forms an image of following a predecessor’s way.

ʿAlā āthārihim specifies that course and direction.

Yuhraʿūna expresses their haste in pursuing it.

The Quran thus does more than state that they followed their fathers: it characterizes their movement as hurried.

Were they compelled to follow?

Passive yuhraʿūna can evoke being drawn or impelled along a course.

The form alone, however, does not prove that the followers lacked all choice.

In context, they follow their fathers’ path although those predecessors were astray.

This resembles people accustomed from childhood to following a tradition or group who continue without independent examination.

Someone may grow up in a family or society treating a belief as certain truth, accept it simply because earlier generations held it, and hurry to defend it.

Habit and social attachment may motivate such conduct, while the person remains responsible for examining truth within their capacity and knowledge.

This is a contemporary application of the verse’s image, not the lexical definition of haraʿ.

Crucially, yuhraʿūna does not itself mean blind imitation. Its direct sense is hastening; following predecessors comes from ʿalā āthārihim and the context.

The two Quranic uses compared

Hūd 11:78 describes haste toward a person to attain a particular desire.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:70 depicts haste in following predecessors’ path and footsteps.

Both uses express hurried movement, with different objects and settings.

In Hūd, the context supplies Lot’s people’s wrongful desire and eagerness.

In Al-Ṣāffāt, following misguided predecessors is central.

The root’s meaning remains consistent across two human situations: haste toward a desire and haste along another’s path.

5. Comparison with related roots

Hā–rāʾ–ʿayn and sīn–rāʾ–ʿayn: haste and speed

Sīn–rāʾ–ʿayn concerns speed and haste.

The Quran uses it for hastening in good deeds, as in yusāriʿūna fī al-khayrāt, and elsewhere for hastening toward wrongdoing.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:61, for example, describes people hastening in good deeds.

Speed and taking the lead are commendable there.

Hā–rāʾ–ʿayn likewise concerns haste, while lexical explanations emphasize movement involving excitement, pressure, or a powerful attraction.

This tendency should not become an absolute distinction making ordinary speed always calm and deliberate and haraʿ always disorderly and wrong.

Both Quranic occurrences of haraʿ concern wrongful conduct, but the negative value comes from context and direction, rather than from every kind of haste being undesirable in Arabic.

Hā–rāʾ–ʿayn and ʿayn–jīm–lām: hurried movement and impatience

ʿAjila and its derivatives concern haste and wanting something early or without delay.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:37 speaks of human hastiness.

Haraʿ, in its two Quranic occurrences, pictures people’s hurried movement.

A simple example illustrates the distinction.

Someone impatient for an answer may want the result immediately without moving from their place.

If that person runs hurriedly toward a location, rapid movement is involved as well.

ʿAjalah can therefore appear in desire, decision, or action, whereas Quranic haraʿ concerns hurried movement.

This is a tendency, not an absolute rule separating every use of the roots.

Hā–rāʾ–ʿayn and sīn–ʿayn–yāʾ: hurrying and striving

Saʿā describes both movement and purposeful effort in the Quran.

In Yā Sīn 36:20, a man comes from the city’s farthest part performing saʿy.

Al-Najm 53:39 uses the family for human effort.

Saʿy cannot therefore always be confined to running or bodily speed.

Yuhraʿūna, by contrast, emphasizes the haste and manner of movement in its two Quranic uses.

Someone may work calmly and persistently: this can be saʿy without hurried movement.

Conversely, someone may rush somewhere without that movement resulting from sustained, deliberate effort.

Hā–rāʾ–ʿayn and dāl–fāʾ–ʿayn: hastening and being driven

Dafʿ concerns pushing, driving away, or restraining.

Al-Ḥajj 22:40 describes God’s restraining some people through others.

Ihrāʿ can also involve a force or pressure causing rapid movement.

Dafʿ alone, however, does not specify speed: someone might gently push another aside.

Yuhraʿūna emphasizes haste.

Its passive form does not establish that someone physically pushes the people or followers in both verses.

The impulse may be external or an inward motive such as desire, fear, or eagerness.

Hā–rāʾ–ʿayn and tāʾ–bāʾ–ʿayn: haste and following

Tabiʿa concerns following.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:70, following predecessors is conveyed by ʿalā āthārihim.

Yuhraʿūna supplies the manner of this movement.

Someone can follow another without hurrying.

Someone else can move quickly toward a destination without following anyone.

Following and haste are therefore separate concepts brought together in this verse’s image.

6. Interpretive and translation challenges

Does haraʿ always mean fear?

No. Arabic sources include fear among the possible accompaniments of ihrāʿ, but it is not the only one.

Desire, anger, eagerness, and external pressure may also produce haste.

Hūd 11:78 concerns Lot’s people’s desire, not a necessary inference that they hurried toward him in fear.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:70 concerns following predecessors.

“They ran fearfully” is therefore not an accurate equivalent for every use.

Is bodily trembling necessary?

Trembling features in some lexical descriptions, but cannot be generalized to every instance.

Neither Hūd 11:78 nor Al-Ṣāffāt 37:70 explicitly states that their bodies trembled.

The base translation should preserve haste, leaving trembling to discussion of the broader Arabic range.

Does haraʿ mean involuntary movement?

Passive morphology may suggest being set in motion from outside.

Arabic usage, however, shows that the same construction can describe someone hastening through an inward motive.

Yuhraʿūna therefore does not establish that Lot’s people or the followers of predecessors lacked moral and practical agency.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:70 places their conduct within misguided following. If wholly external compulsion had removed their agency, responsibility would require a different examination.

The verb’s form itself does not prove such absolute compulsion.

Is every kind of haste undesirable?

No. The Quran also praises hastening in good deeds.

The two occurrences of yuhraʿūna concern situations involving wrongful conduct.

That wrongfulness comes from purpose and context.

Someone hurrying to rescue an injured person may act well; someone else may move just as quickly to cause harm.

Both involve speed. Intention, purpose, and action distinguish their ethical value.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Equivalents for the family should follow form and context.

هَرَعَ: hastened; moved hurriedly. هَرِعَ: flowed, in the liquid-flow branch.

هَرَع and هُرَاع: hurried movement; intense haste.

إِهْرَاع: hastening or being impelled into hurried movement.

مُهْرَع: hurried; someone moving quickly or impelled to move.

هَرِع: swift-moving; elsewhere, quick to weep or flowing.

هَيْرَع: swift and dust-scattering of wind; weak and unsteady in some human applications.

يُهْرَعُونَ: they hurry; they move hurriedly.

The following renderings suit the two Quranic contexts.

Hūd 11:78

“And his people came rushing to him…”

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:70

“So they hurry along in their footsteps.”

These preserve haste without introducing fear, trembling, physical pressure, or compulsion without evidence.

“They go in haste” is also natural; the proposed Persian equivalent is shorter and fits Forqan’s Quranic prose.

“They are forcibly driven” fits only when the context establishes an external compelling agent.

“They run in terror” overstates the fixed meaning in both verses, since neither fear nor running is certain from yuhraʿūna in these contexts.

8. Conclusion

Hā–rāʾ–ʿayn concerns hurried movement and being set in motion through motivation or pressure.

Fear, anger, desire, eagerness, or trembling may accompany this haste, but none alone defines every derivative.

Arabic also records liquid flow, quick weeping, and descriptions of swift wind. Each branch requires its own evidence; all should not be reduced without historical grounds to one original image.

The Quran uses only yuhraʿūna.

In Hūd 11:78, it describes Lot’s people hurrying toward him.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:70, it pictures hurried following of misguided predecessors.

Hurried movement remains present in both, while context determines cause and direction.

Final definition for Forqan:

In its movement-related branch, hā–rāʾ–ʿayn denotes hastening and proceeding somewhere hurriedly, potentially through excitement, inward attraction, or external pressure. Fear, trembling, and compulsion accompany some uses but are not necessary to all.

Quranic yuhraʿūna describes hurried movement, once toward a wrongful desire and once in hurriedly following predecessors’ path.
