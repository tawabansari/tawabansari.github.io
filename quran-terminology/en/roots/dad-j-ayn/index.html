Lying down, reclining, and the place where a body rests: from sleeping and shared beds to the places where the slain fall

1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

The Arabic root Ḍ J ʿ concerns lying down and placing the side of the body on the ground or another surface. This family can describe a person moving from standing or sitting to lying with the body on its side.

From this meaning come words for a place to sleep, the position of lying down, someone who lies beside another person, and a sleeping place used for rest.

The family is not confined to sleeping. Someone may lie down while still awake. A person may also fall to the ground through injury or death, and a word from this family may describe where the body lies.

The root occurs only three times in the Qur’an, always as the plural noun مَضَاجِع:

  • مَضَاجِعِهِمْ: their places of falling or resting; Āl ʿImrān 3:154.
  • الْمَضَاجِعِ: beds; Al-Nisāʾ 4:34.
  • الْمَضَاجِعِ: beds for sleep and rest; Al-Sajdah 32:16.

Important Arabic forms of the family include:

  • ضَجَعَ / يَضْجَعُ: to lie down or place the body on its side.
  • ضَجْع and ضُجُوع: lying down or lying on one’s side.
  • اِضْطَجَعَ: lay down, lay on one’s side, or settled down to rest.
  • اِضْطِجَاع: lying down and reclining.
  • ضَاجِع: a person lying down.
  • مُضْطَجِع: someone in a reclining position.
  • أَضْجَعَ: laid someone or something down or placed it in a reclining position.
  • مَضْجَع: a place to lie down, a bed, or a sleeping place.
  • مَضَاجِع: plural of maḍjaʿ; beds and resting places.
  • ضَجِيع and ضَجِيعَة: someone who lies beside another person; a bedfellow.
  • ضَاجَعَ / مُضَاجَعَة: shared a bed with another person or lay beside them.
  • ضَجْعَة: one instance of lying down; with a different vowel, the manner or position of lying.
  • ضُجَعَة: someone who lies down frequently; pejoratively, an idle or inactive person.
  • ضَجُوع: in human uses, weak or lacking resolve; elsewhere, something remaining in a place or inclining to one side.

Arabic also records uses concerning the sun and stars approaching the horizon, animals remaining in grazing grounds, and rain falling. These are examined as semantic extensions, but none occurs in that form in the Qur’an.

2. The semantic core in early Arabic

Placing the side on the ground

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies the root’s principle as follows:

أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى لُصُوقٍ بِالْأَرْضِ عَلَى جَنْبٍ

aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā luṣūqin bi-al-arḍi ʿalā janbin

That is, “One semantic principle indicating contact with the ground on one’s side.”

The position of the body matters in this definition.

When standing, a person places most of their weight on the feet. When sitting, another part of the body provides support. In iḍṭijāʿ, the body extends along a surface, with its side or a larger area resting on the ground or bed.

This picture is sufficient to explain the root’s principal uses.

The word should nevertheless not be reduced to a rigid anatomical position. Arabic also uses iḍṭajaʿa for sleeping and resting, without always specifying the body’s precise angle or whether it lies on its right or left side.

“Lie down” and “lie on one’s side” may therefore both translate this family in appropriate contexts.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: lying on one’s side

The morphology of iḍṭajaʿa

اِضْطَجَعَ is a Form VIII verb.

In this pattern’s underlying construction, the added tāʾ follows the first root letter. In this word, tāʾ changes to ṭāʾ beside ḍād.

The familiar form is therefore اضطجع, rather than اضتجع.

Other forms are reported in some Arabic dialects, including اضّجع and الطجع.

Lexicographers explain them through sound changes and assimilation in speech.

These phonetic differences do not, by themselves, create a new meaning. In ordinary use, all concern lying down.

Nor should a particular intention or quality be inferred automatically from Form VIII. Someone may lie down to rest or because of fatigue or illness. The context identifies the reason.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Al-Miṣbāḥ al-Munīr: substitution and assimilation in iḍṭajaʿa

Lying down and sleeping

Lisān al-ʿArab sometimes explains iḍṭajaʿa through nāma, “slept.”

Sleeping and lying down, however, are not identical concepts.

Someone can lie down to rest while remaining awake. They may also fall asleep while sitting, without lying down.

Bodily posture and the state of sleep are therefore distinct, although connected in ordinary life.

Maḍjaʿ emphasizes a place to lie down. This place is often used for sleeping, but the word alone does not establish that sleep occurs.

Ḍajʿah and ḍijʿah

Early Arabic distinguishes two vocalizations of this word.

Ḍajʿah, with fatḥah on ḍād, can denote one instance of lying down.

Ḍijʿah, with kasrah on ḍād, is used more particularly for the manner or position of lying down.

This resembles jalsah and jilsah in the field of sitting: one may emphasize a single occurrence of the action, the other its manner.

The formation does not imply that every Arabic dialect and period always maintained an absolute boundary between these forms.

For this root study, the relevant distinction is between lying down itself, one instance of it, and the manner in which it is done.

3. Early Arabic witnesses

A pre-Islamic witness: a person’s resting place

In a poem addressed to his daughter as he travels, the pre-Islamic poet Al-Aʿshā says:

عَلَيْكِ مِثْلُ الَّذِي صَلَّيْتِ فَاغْتَمِضِي
يَوْمًا فَإِنَّ لِجَنْبِ الْمَرْءِ مُضْطَجَعَا

ʿalayki mithlu alladhī ṣallayti fa-ightamiḍī / yawman fa-inna li-janbi al-marʾi muḍṭajaʿā

An approximate rendering of the sense:

“May you receive the like of the prayer you offered; now rest, for the human side will ultimately have a place to lie.”

Muḍṭajaʿ in this verse concerns the place where a person’s side rests.

The poet speaks amid travel, separation, and his daughter’s anxiety, invoking rest and the end of suffering.

Lexical explanations also connect the expression with a person’s final resting place in the grave.

The word is therefore not confined here to a bed for nightly sleep; it can contribute to an image of final repose.

This poetic use does not show that maḍjaʿ originally meant “grave.” Its basic meaning is a place to lie down; the connection with death comes from the poem’s situation and imagery.

Some transmissions have nawman in place of yawman. This variant does not change the meaning of muḍṭajaʿ.

Tāj al-ʿArūs and Lisān al-ʿArab: Al-Aʿshā’s witness and the reading nawman

Ḍajīʿ: a bedfellow

Qays ibn Dharīḥ, an early Islamic poet, says:

لَعَمْرِي لَمَنْ أَمْسَى وَأَنْتِ ضَجِيعُهُ
مِنَ النَّاسِ مَا اخْتِيرَتْ عَلَيْهِ الْمَضَاجِعُ

la-ʿamrī la-man amsā wa-anti ḍajīʿuhu / mina al-nāsi mā ukhtīrat ʿalayhi al-maḍājiʿu

Translation:

“By my life, for the person among mankind who spends the evening with you as his bedfellow, no other bed would be preferred to his.”

Ḍajīʿ here denotes someone who shares another person’s bed.

Al-maḍājiʿ likewise refers to beds and sleeping places.

This witness illustrates how the basic meaning of lying down supports a use concerning two people sharing a bed.

Ḍajīʿ does not always mean sexual intercourse. Its direct sense is someone who lies beside another person or shares their bed.

In an amorous context, sharing a bed may involve intimacy and a marital relationship. The context determines the relationship.

Qays ibn Dharīḥ was a post-Islamic poet. This verse is evidence for early Arabic, rather than a pre-Islamic witness.

Tāj al-ʿArūs: Qays ibn Dharīḥ’s witness

Ḍujaʿah: rest or idleness

Lexical sources use رَجُلٌ ضُجَعَةٌ and related expressions for someone who frequently lies down and avoids activity.

Here, a bodily posture extends to a behavioral trait.

Someone who spends most of their time in bed and avoids necessary work may be criticized through this expression.

Lying down or resting is not itself blameworthy, however.

Human beings need sleep and rest to maintain health and recover strength.

The criticism arises from inappropriate excess and neglect of responsibility, rather than from the root’s basic meaning.

Work and responsibility

Arabic records ضَجَعَ فِي أَمْرِهِ for someone who is remiss in their work or fails to discharge its responsibility.

تَضَاجَعَ عَنِ الْأَمْرِ can likewise describe holding back from or disregarding a matter.

This is an extension from the bodily position of lying down to human conduct.

When someone stays in bed instead of getting up to perform necessary work, lying down can become an image of slackness or neglect.

This figurative extension should not, however, be imported into every derivative.

In the three Qur’anic verses, maḍājiʿ does not mean laziness or neglect of responsibility. It identifies actual places to lie down.

4. Other uses: inclination, remaining, and settling

The sun and stars inclining toward setting

Early Arabic uses ضَجَعَ النَّجْمُ for a star inclining toward the horizon and approaching its setting.

ضَجَّعَتِ الشَّمْسُ is likewise recorded for the sun approaching sunset.

The celestial body is not literally lying on its side on the ground.

Its apparent movement toward the horizon is likened to something inclining and sinking after being seen higher up.

In describing a person, ضَجَعَ إِلَى فُلَانٍ can similarly indicate inclining or leaning toward someone.

These uses relate to the image of something settling to one side. They should not become an astronomical or psychological theory about the root’s origin.

An animal remaining in grazing grounds

Lexical sources use إِبِلٌ ضَاجِعَةٌ for camels remaining in a particular grazing area.

Remaining and not leaving the place is emphasized here.

سَحَابَةٌ ضَجُوعٌ likewise describes a cloud that moves slowly through its weight and abundance of water, or remains over an area.

Such uses can relate to lying down through images of staying low, heaviness, or remaining in one place.

Ḍajūʿ does not itself mean rain or camels. The accompanying noun identifies what is being described.

Maḍājiʿ al-ghayth: places where rain falls

Arabic records مَضَاجِعُ الْغَيْثِ for places where rain falls.

Maḍājiʿ here is no longer a human sleeping place; it identifies where something settles or falls.

Al-ḍawājiʿ is also recorded for certain landforms, watercourses, and natural depressions.

These uses show that the image of a resting or settling place can also apply to nature.

Such subsidiary meanings should not be transferred to the Qur’anic uses concerning marital beds or sleeping places.

Asās al-Balāghah and Tāj al-ʿArūs: setting, remaining, and maḍājiʿ al-ghayth

5. Maḍājiʿ in three Qur’anic verses

Āl ʿImrān 3:154: where the slain fall

Āl ʿImrān reads:

قُلْ لَوْ كُنْتُمْ فِي بُيُوتِكُمْ لَبَرَزَ الَّذِينَ كُتِبَ عَلَيْهِمُ الْقَتْلُ إِلَىٰ مَضَاجِعِهِمْ

qul law kuntum fī buyūtikum la-baraza alladhīna kutiba ʿalayhimu al-qatlu ilā maḍājiʿihim

“Say: Even if you had been in your houses, those for whom being killed had been decreed would have gone out to their places of falling.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:154

The meaning of مَضَاجِعِهِمْ

Maḍājiʿihim consists of the plural maḍājiʿ and the pronoun -him.

The word accompanies al-qatl, being killed, in this verse.

It therefore denotes the places where the slain fall to the ground and their bodies lie.

This clearly relates to maḍjaʿ’s general meaning: a place to lie down and rest the body.

Unlike ordinary use within a home, the context concerns where a body falls and lies after being killed.

A contextual explanation may therefore use “where the slain fall” or “the places where they are killed and their bodies lie on the ground.”

Maḍjaʿ does not itself mean killing. Being killed is expressed by كُتِبَ عَلَيْهِمُ الْقَتْلُ; maḍājiʿihim identifies its place or bodily outcome.

The relationship with houses

The verse places فِي بُيُوتِكُمْ alongside إِلَىٰ مَضَاجِعِهِمْ.

Buyūt denotes houses and places of residence.

Maḍājiʿ here concerns where the slain fall.

The expressions identify two different situations: remaining at home and going out to where death occurs.

The verse presents this statement in response to people imagining that they would not have been killed had they decided differently.

The root Ḍ J ʿ itself, however, pronounces no judgment on free choice, divine decree, or responsibility in war. Those subjects belong to the whole verse and the network of verses concerning death, testing, and human responsibility.

Lexically, maḍājiʿihim contributes only the places where the bodies fall and lie.

Does maḍjaʿ mean a grave?

Arabic poetry and usage sometimes employ maḍjaʿ for a dead person’s resting place.

It can therefore be associated with a grave.

Āl ʿImrān 3:154 does not explicitly mention graves, however. It describes those for whom being killed was decreed going out to their maḍājiʿ.

“Where they fall or are killed” is more precise here than “grave”: the place of death and place of burial are not always identical.

Maḍājiʿihim should therefore not be translated exclusively as “their graves” without evidence.

Forqan’s current Persian translation uses “their beds.” This preserves the word’s basic image, but a contextual explanation identifying where the slain fall is needed to prevent a mistaken association with ordinary beds at home.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:34: the marital bed

Al-Nisāʾ reads:

وَاللَّاتِي تَخَافُونَ نُشُوزَهُنَّ فَعِظُوهُنَّ وَاهْجُرُوهُنَّ فِي الْمَضَاجِعِ وَاضْرِبُوهُنَّ

wa-allātī takhāfūna nushūzahunna fa-ʿiẓūhunna wa-uhjurūhunna fī al-maḍājiʿi wa-iḍribūhunna

“As for those women whose recalcitrance you fear, admonish them and keep apart from them in the beds…” This translates the portion concerning maḍājiʿ; the following wa-iḍribūhunna is examined separately.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:34

Al-maḍājiʿ here denotes beds and places for sleep and sharing a bed within married life.

The meaning of فِي الْمَضَاجِعِ

Fī is a preposition, and al-maḍājiʿ is the definite plural of maḍjaʿ.

The expression concerns the setting or place of the bed.

Wa-uhjurūhunna belongs to H J R and expresses keeping apart or leaving in this context.

The two contributions must therefore remain distinct:

Hajr expresses the act of keeping apart; maḍājiʿ identifies the place to which that separation relates.

Maḍājiʿ itself does not mean severing a relationship, punishment, or keeping apart.

Within marriage, a shared bed may be a place for sleep, rest, and bodily intimacy.

Keeping apart in bed may therefore concern reducing or suspending some aspect of marital intimacy.

The precise form of that separation concerns interpretation of the complete sentence and should not be derived from Ḍ J ʿ alone.

Does maḍājiʿ necessarily mean sexual intercourse?

Arabic can use ḍājaʿa and muḍājaʿah for a man and woman sharing a bed.

In a marital context, sharing a bed may involve sexual intercourse.

Maḍjaʿ primarily denotes a place to lie down, however, rather than independently naming sexual intercourse.

Early exegesis offers different explanations of wa-uhjurūhunna fī al-maḍājiʿ: ceasing to share a bed, abstaining from sexual intercourse in a shared bed, turning one’s back in bed, or withdrawing from intimate conversation.

These differences show that the noun al-maḍājiʿ alone cannot establish one definitive way of carrying out the instruction.

The lexical meaning of a bed is clear. The scope and manner of keeping apart require examination of hajr, the preposition fī, the context of marital relations, and related verses.

Al-Ṭabarī: transmitted interpretations of hajr in beds

The following wa-iḍribūhunna belongs to another root and has its own semantic debate. Its meaning should not be inserted into the definition of maḍājiʿ.

A fuller discussion appears in the separate article “Darb in the Qur’an: Beating or Decisive Separation?”.

Al-Sajdah 32:16: rising from beds for prayer

Al-Sajdah reads:

تَتَجَافَىٰ جُنُوبُهُمْ عَنِ الْمَضَاجِعِ يَدْعُونَ رَبَّهُمْ خَوْفًا وَطَمَعًا وَمِمَّا رَزَقْنَاهُمْ يُنْفِقُونَ

tatajāfā junūbuhum ʿani al-maḍājiʿi yadʿūna rabbahum khawfan wa-ṭamaʿan wa-mimmā razaqnāhum yunfiqūna

“Their sides draw away from the beds; they call upon their Lord in fear and hope, and spend from what We have provided them.”

Al-Sajdah 32:16

The meaning of الْمَضَاجِعِ

Al-maḍājiʿ here identifies beds for sleep and rest.

Junūbuhum refers to people’s sides.

Its relationship to Ḍ J ʿ is particularly clear: the root’s core concerns placing the side of the body on the ground or a bed.

The verse thus describes sides drawing away from their resting places.

The bed is a place of rest in this context, and the person moves away from it.

The role of تَتَجَافَىٰ

Tatajāfā belongs to J F W and concerns drawing away or keeping apart in this construction.

The verb expresses the act of separating from the bed.

Maḍājiʿ, in contrast, names the place from which the body draws away.

Maḍājiʿ should therefore not be understood as rising or withdrawing. Those meanings come from the verb and the preposition ʿan.

Prayer and worship

The continuation reads:

يَدْعُونَ رَبَّهُمْ خَوْفًا وَطَمَعًا

yadʿūna rabbahum khawfan wa-ṭamaʿan

“They call upon their Lord in fear and hope.”

Drawing away from the bed thus relates in this context to turning toward prayer and remembrance of the Lord.

Nearby verses speak of people who, when reminded of the signs of The God, humbly prostrate and praise Him.

The scene therefore portrays people setting aside the comfort of bed to attend to their Lord.

Maḍājiʿ itself does not mean ṣalāh, supplication, or worship.

Nor does the verse specify an amount of sleep, a particular hour of night, or a set number of prayer cycles.

A precise timetable and ritual program should therefore not be derived from tatajāfā junūbuhum ʿani al-maḍājiʿ alone.

The picture nevertheless clearly includes sacrificing some comfort and rest for prayer and attention to The God.

Al-Ṭabarī: sides drawing away from beds and prayer

Does the verse condemn sleeping?

No. It praises those who leave their beds for prayer, but that praise does not imply that sleep or rest is inherently objectionable.

Other Qur’anic verses present sleep as one of creation’s signs and life’s blessings.

For example, Al-Rūm 30:23 reads:

وَمِنْ آيَاتِهِ مَنَامُكُمْ بِاللَّيْلِ وَالنَّهَارِ

wa-min āyātihi manāmukum bi-al-layli wa-al-nahāri

“Among His signs is your sleep by night and day.”

Al-Furqān 25:47 likewise presents sleep as a source of repose.

Al-Sajdah 32:16 therefore does not deny the human need for sleep. It describes people leaving their beds during part of their rest time to pray and attend to their Lord.

6. Comparison with neighboring roots

Ḍ J ʿ (maḍjaʿ) and N W M (nawm)

Nawm concerns sleep.

Maḍjaʿ identifies a place to lie down and rest.

A person may sleep in a bed, but the bed itself does not name sleep.

Someone may also lie down while remaining awake.

In Al-Sajdah 32:16, maḍājiʿ identifies the place of rest, while tatajāfā expresses drawing away from it.

Maḍjaʿ and nawm are therefore related but distinct concepts.

Ḍ J ʿ (maḍjaʿ) and R Q D (marqad)

R Q D concerns sleeping and resting in Arabic.

Yā Sīn 36:52 reads:

مَنْ بَعَثَنَا مِنْ مَرْقَدِنَا

man baʿathanā min marqadinā

“Who has raised us from our sleeping place?”

Marqad occurs here in the context of rising from death.

Maḍjaʿ can likewise occur in the context of being killed or the resting of a dead body, as in Āl ʿImrān 3:154.

Marqad and maḍjaʿ nevertheless belong to different roots.

Marqad concerns sleep and repose; maḍjaʿ emphasizes where a body lies down and rests.

Their overlap does not make their meanings wholly identical.

Ḍ J ʿ (maḍjaʿ) and F R SH (firāsh)

Firāsh concerns something spread out on which a person may sit or lie down.

Maḍjaʿ is a place to lie down.

A firāsh may serve as a person’s maḍjaʿ, but maḍjaʿ is not necessarily confined to a mattress, carpet, or textile furnishing.

A person may lie on the ground, stone, sand, or another surface.

Firāsh is therefore closer to something spread out and used for sitting or sleeping, while maḍjaʿ identifies where lying down takes place.

Study of F R SH

Ḍ J ʿ (ḍajaʿa) and J N B (janb)

Janb concerns the side or flank of the body.

Ḍajaʿa concerns placing the body on its side.

Junūbuhum and al-maḍājiʿ occur in one sentence in Al-Sajdah 32:16.

This proximity clarifies their semantic relationship: sides move away from the places on which they ordinarily rest.

Janb, however, names a bodily part or side; Ḍ J ʿ concerns the position or place associated with lying down.

Study of J N B

Ḍ J ʿ (maḍājiʿ) and H J R (hajara)

Hajr concerns keeping apart, leaving, and, in some uses, withdrawing from another person.

Maḍājiʿ names beds and sleeping places.

The two families occur together in Al-Nisāʾ 4:34:

وَاهْجُرُوهُنَّ فِي الْمَضَاجِعِ

wa-uhjurūhunna fī al-maḍājiʿi

“And keep apart from them in the beds.”

Hajr expresses keeping apart, while maḍājiʿ identifies the place in which that separation is addressed.

The distinction should be preserved in translation and reflection.

Study of H J R

Ḍ J ʿ (maḍājiʿ) and Ṣ R ʿ (maṣāriʿ)

Ṣ R ʿ concerns throwing to the ground, falling, and being brought down.

Maṣāriʿ can identify places where people fall or are killed.

Maḍājiʿihim in Āl ʿImrān 3:154 approaches this field in the context of killing.

Maḍjaʿ, however, derives from lying down and the body coming to rest; maṣraʿ concerns falling or being thrown to the ground.

In a killing context they may refer to the same place, without being identical in root or direct meaning.

Study of Ṣ R ʿ

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

ضَجَعَ: lay down or lay on one’s side.

ضَجْع and ضُجُوع: lying down or lying on one’s side.

اِضْطَجَعَ: lay down or rested on one’s side.

اِضْطِجَاع: lying down and reclining.

ضَاجِع and مُضْطَجِع: lying down; someone in a reclining position.

أَضْجَعَ: laid down or placed in a reclining position.

مَضْجَع: a bed, sleeping place, or place to lie down.

مَضَاجِع: beds and resting places.

ضَجِيع / ضَجِيعَة: a bedfellow; someone who lies beside another person.

ضَاجَعَ: shared a bed with another person.

ضُجَعَة: someone who lies down frequently; pejoratively, idle or inactive.

ضَجَعَ النَّجْمُ: the star inclined toward setting.

مَضَاجِعُ الْغَيْثِ: places where rain falls.

For the three Qur’anic occurrences:

Āl ʿImrān 3:154

إِلَىٰ مَضَاجِعِهِمْ

ilā maḍājiʿihim

“To their beds.”

Contextually: the places where those being killed fall or their bodies lie.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:34

وَاهْجُرُوهُنَّ فِي الْمَضَاجِعِ

wa-uhjurūhunna fī al-maḍājiʿi

“And keep apart from them in the beds.”

Maḍājiʿ here concerns beds within married life.

Al-Sajdah 32:16

تَتَجَافَىٰ جُنُوبُهُمْ عَنِ الْمَضَاجِعِ

tatajāfā junūbuhum ʿani al-maḍājiʿi

“Their sides draw away from the beds.”

Maḍājiʿ here identifies beds for sleep and rest.

In all three uses, “bed” or “place to lie down” preserves the semantic core. The context identifies the particular referent.

8. Conclusion

The Arabic root Ḍ J ʿ concerns lying down and placing the body on its side.

From this meaning come words for resting places, the reclining position, someone sharing another person’s bed, and the act of sharing a bed.

Broader uses concern the sun and stars inclining toward setting, animals remaining in a place, rain falling, and slackness in fulfilling responsibilities.

These figurative uses should not, however, alter the root’s basic meaning.

Only the plural noun maḍājiʿ occurs in the Qur’an.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:154, it identifies the places where the slain fall.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:34 addresses beds within marital relations.

Al-Sajdah 32:16 describes beds for sleep and rest, from which people draw away to pray and attend to their Lord.

These three uses show how a word may retain a shared lexical meaning while referring to different places in different contexts.

Lying down, sleeping, sharing a bed, sexual intercourse, and falling after being killed may be related in some situations, but they are conceptually distinct.

Brief root meaning for Forqan:

Lying down and placing the body on its side; a place to lie down, a bed, and repose.

Meaning in Āl ʿImrān 3:154:

مَضَاجِعِهِمْ: the places where they fall and their bodies lie, in the context of those whose being killed is addressed.

Meaning in Al-Nisāʾ 4:34:

الْمَضَاجِعِ: beds; places for sleep and sharing a bed within married life.

Meaning in Al-Sajdah 32:16:

الْمَضَاجِعِ: beds for sleep and rest, on which people’s sides lie and from which the verse describes them drawing away.
