Winter, wintering, and seasonal residence: the root in early Arabic and its Quranic use

In early Arabic, SH T W concerns winter and matters associated with that season. Its principal, familiar meaning is winter, the colder period of the year contrasted with summer. The family includes words for entering winter, spending the season, residing somewhere during it, winter quarters, and things belonging to winter.

In pre-Islamic life, particularly among pastoral and mobile communities, winter was more than a time designation: it was associated with changes of residence, seeking pasture, food and clothing needs, and preparing for cold. These activities and consequences are not themselves the root’s meaning. Shitāʾ names the season; ways of life, difficulties, and opportunities depend on geographical and social circumstances.

The root occurs only once in the Quran, as al-shitāʾi in Quraysh 106:2. It stands alongside al-ṣayfi and concerns the seasonal journeys of Quraysh.

1. The root in early Arabic

Winter and seasonal life

Early Arabic shitāʾ denotes winter in contrast to ṣayf, summer. Shatā and ashtā are related verbs, but their uses are not entirely identical.

Ashtā al-qawm denotes people entering the winter season, whereas shataw may mean spending winter or residing somewhere during that season.

The family thus names both the season and people’s relationship to it.

Where herding, seasonal movement, and grazing formed important parts of life, wintering could be directly connected with choosing a suitable place of residence.

First witness: wintering in Tarafa ibn al-Abd’s poetry

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Tarafa ibn al-Abd is preserved in lexical sources. Wording and place names vary between transmissions; the following follows one lexical version:

حَيْثُمَا قَاظُوا بِنَجْدٍ وَشَتَوْا
عِنْدَ ذَاتِ الطَّلْحِ مِنْ ثِنْيَي وُقُرْ

ḥaythumā qāẓū bi-najdin wa-shataw / ʿinda dhāti al-ṭalḥi min thinyay wuqur

Its sense is:

“Wherever they spent the hot season in Najd and wintered near Dhat al-Talh, in the area of Wuqur.”

Qāẓū and shataw stand in contrast.

Qāẓū means spending the hot season, and shataw spending winter. Both are associated with places and describe seasonal life and residence in different regions.

Shataw does not simply mean that the weather became cold. It denotes spending winter in a particular place.

This poetic witness supports the association of winter with seasonal residence in early Arabic. Attribution to a pre-Islamic poet does not independently establish an exact date of composition.

It does not establish that every Arab community migrated in winter or followed a fixed annual route. The poet speaks of particular circumstances and locations; broader generalization requires independent evidence.

Second witness: al-mashtāh and winter social life

Another verse by Tarafa says:

نَحْنُ فِي الْمَشْتَاةِ نَدْعُو الْجَفَلَى
لَا تَرَى الْآدِبَ فِينَا يَنْتَقِرْ

naḥnu fī al-mashtāti nadʿū al-jafalā / lā tarā al-ādiba fīnā yantaqir

“In winter we invite everyone to the feast; among us you will not see a host selecting only particular guests.”

Al-mashtāh is naturally understood here as the winter season, as some commentators explain it. The form can also denote a wintering place in Arabic, but this verse alone does not require that spatial meaning. The poet praises hospitality and an open invitation.

Al-jafalā means an invitation extended to all, while yantaqir concerns selecting particular people to invite.

The verse principally praises generosity and hospitality, while also providing a useful witness for SH T W.

Its use of al-mashtāh illustrates a word for the winter period; other lexical uses also establish the sense of winter quarters. These uses should be distinguished in their respective contexts.

The passage is not explained by “cold weather” alone: it concerns how people live and offer hospitality in that season, without necessarily specifying a particular residence.

Third witness: winter as the cold season

A poem attributed to al-Rabi ibn Ḍabʿ al-Fazari, described in traditional sources as an early poet associated with the period around the emergence of Islam, says:

إِذَا جَاءَ الشِّتَاءُ فَأَدْفِئُونِي
فَإِنَّ الشَّيْخَ يُهْرِمُهُ الشِّتَاءُ

idhā jāʾa al-shitāʾu fa-adfiʾūnī / fa-inna al-shaykha yuhrimuhu al-shitāʾ

“When winter comes, keep me warm, for winter makes the old man more infirm.”

The verse has variants in its opening and second half, including yahdimuhu in place of yuhrimuhu. Its exact date cannot be established from these transmissions alone.

Winter is associated here with cold and the need for warmth. The poet describes an elderly person’s physical condition in the face of the season’s hardships.

Cold and infirmity are not equivalent to the season name shitāʾ: here they are characteristics and effects associated with winter.

What the historical witnesses show

Early evidence establishes at least three uses within SH T W: the season name, spending that season, and a time or place for wintering.

They share a clear center: winter and life or activity situated within that season.

There is no need to invent a physical image, such as contraction, freezing, or arrested movement, as the root’s underlying meaning. The early evidence places winter itself at the center of the family.

2. Important forms and derivatives

Several principal forms of SH T W are known in Arabic.

شِتَاء — shitāʾ: winter, the cold season contrasted with summer. This form occurs in the Quran.

شَتْو and شَتْوَة — shatw and shatwa: forms associated with winter and wintering. Shatwa can denote one instance of wintering. Lexicographers differ over the morphological relationship between shitāʾ and shatwa, so shitāʾ should not unconditionally be described as the plural of shatwa.

شَتَا يَشْتُو — shatā yashtū: spending winter or residing somewhere during it. Some uses also express winter reaching a person or group.

أَشْتَى — ashtā: entered winter, or winter arrived for someone. This is broader than residing in a specific place.

تَشَتَّى — tashattā: spent winter in a place. In pasture-related usage it may concern residing or grazing livestock there during winter.

مَشْتَى and مَشْتَاة — mashtā and mashtāh: a place or time of wintering, according to context; mashtāh may also mean the winter season itself.

شَاتٍ and شَاتِيَة — shātin and shātiya: descriptions of a day or time with wintry, cold conditions, such as a cold winter day.

شَتَوِيّ — shatawī: pertaining to winter, or wintry. It describes rain and other phenomena associated with the season.

Dictionaries also record a figurative use of shitāʾ for famine and uses of related forms for hardship and food shortage. These are attested lexical extensions, rather than merely possible consequences of winter.

Such extensions are connected with experiences of winter scarcity. They do not make hunger or famine the primary meaning of shitāʾ or its meaning in Quraysh 106:2. Not every winter brings famine, and famine can occur outside winter.

All these forms are known in Arabic, but only the noun shitāʾ, in its definite form al-shitāʾi, occurs in the Quran. The other verbs and derivatives do not occur there.

3. Quranic use: Quraysh 106:2

Surah Quraysh opens:

Quraysh 106:1

لِإِيلَافِ قُرَيْشٍ

li-īlāfi quraysh

Quraysh 106:1

“For the accustomed bond of Quraysh.”

Quraysh 106:2

إِيلَافِهِمْ رِحْلَةَ الشِّتَاءِ وَالصَّيْفِ

īlāfihim riḥlata al-shitāʾi wa-al-ṣayf

Quraysh 106:2

“Their familiarity with the journey of winter and summer.”

It then says:

Quraysh 106:3

فَلْيَعْبُدُوا رَبَّ هَٰذَا الْبَيْتِ

fa-l-yaʿbudū rabba hādhā al-bayt

Quraysh 106:3

“Let them therefore worship the Lord of this House.”

Quraysh 106:4

الَّذِي أَطْعَمَهُمْ مِنْ جُوعٍ وَآمَنَهُمْ مِنْ خَوْفٍ

alladhī aṭʿamahum min jūʿin wa-āmanahum min khawf

Quraysh 106:4

“Who fed them against hunger and made them safe from fear.”

The meaning of al-shitāʾ in the verse

Al-shitāʾi in Quraysh 106:2 names winter, contrasted with al-ṣayfi, summer.

In this verse it does not mean a journey, commerce, migration, cold, or hunger. Those ideas arise, where applicable, from another word, the context, or historical background.

Riḥla expresses the journey or departure, and al-shitāʾi specifies its season.

Riḥlata al-shitāʾ therefore means the winter journey, not fleeing the cold or seeking food, although such motives can underlie particular winter journeys.

The word winter itself specifies neither the starting point nor destination, cargo, or duration of the journey.

Those details require separate examination using evidence about the history and life of Quraysh.

Winter and summer in contrast

Al-shitāʾ and al-ṣayf are contrasting seasons, a distinction already known in early Arabic.

Some traditional Arabic accounts divide the year into two principal parts, winter and summer, then subdivide them. The boundaries of early Arabic winter should not therefore be assumed without evidence to coincide exactly with a modern four-season calendar.

Mentioning winter and summer does not deny spring or autumn. The two seasons here concern familiar journeys of Quraysh and mark distinct periods of the year.

In riḥlata al-shitāʾi wa-al-ṣayfi, riḥla is singular and both seasons are linked to it. The construction can describe seasonal journeys collectively; its singular form does not require one continuous journey beginning in winter and lasting until summer.

Nor does this morphology alone establish the exact number of all Quraysh’s annual journeys.

Where did the winter journeys go?

Some historical and exegetical reports associate the winter journey with Yemen and the summer journey with the Levant.

That account is geographically intelligible: routes can reflect climate, market access, and travel conditions.

Other reports describe seasonal residence or movement between Mecca and Taif.

What the Quran explicitly says must therefore be distinguished from historical reports supplying details.

The verse names winter and summer journeys but does not name Yemen, the Levant, or Taif. “The winter journey to Yemen” should not replace riḥlata al-shitāʾ in the base translation.

If destinations are discussed in commentary, they should be presented as separate historical information, not as part of shitāʾ’s lexical meaning.

Winter, livelihood, and security

Quraysh 106:2 should also be read alongside Quraysh 106:3 and Quraysh 106:4.

After recalling seasonal journeys, the Quran calls on Quraysh to worship the Lord of this House and identifies two blessings: food against hunger and security against fear.

The journeys are thus situated within Quraysh’s life, provision, and security, not simply as movement during certain seasons.

For a community dependent on travel and external resources, the ability to travel and secure routes matters greatly. Insecurity can hinder movement, exchange, and access to food.

This does not establish that all Quraysh’s food came exclusively from these journeys or that the verse gives a detailed account of a specific trading practice.

The passage attributes food and security to the Lord and calls Quraysh to worship Him. The winter journey belongs to that context, while shitāʾ remains the name of the season.

4. Semantic limits and related roots

SH T W (shitāʾ) and SAD Y F (ṣayf)

Shitāʾ is winter and ṣayf summer. Both designate seasons, while each family also supplies verbs and nouns concerning residence during that season.

Just as shatā can denote wintering, forms of SAD Y F describe spending summer or entering it.

Poetry attributed to pre-Islamic poets sometimes connects the seasonal contrast with different places of residence and seasonal movement.

The two words also appear together in Quraysh 106:2. They identify seasons of travel, without assigning winter an inherent moral value in contrast to summer.

Winter should therefore not automatically symbolize hardship and summer ease. Either season can offer favorable or difficult conditions in different regions.

SH T W (shitāʾ) and B R D (bard)

Bard concerns cold, whereas shitāʾ names a season.

Winter often brings lower temperatures, but the season and its temperature characteristics are not identical meanings.

One region can have a mild winter and another severe cold; both can be described as winter.

Cold can also occur outside winter at high elevations or under particular conditions.

Shitāʾ should therefore not be translated simply as “cold.” Quraysh 106:2 concerns travel during winter, not merely travel in cold weather.

SH T W (shitāʾ) and Q Y Ẓ (qayẓ)

Qayẓ concerns intense heat and the hot season. Early Arabic maqīẓ denotes a place for spending the hot season, and qāẓa residing during it.

By contrast, mashtā and shatā concern wintering.

This contrast helps explain seasonal and mobile ways of life: people could change their residence according to heat, cold, water, and pasture.

Qayẓ and ṣayf are not interchangeable in every use, just as cold and winter are not identical. The season, temperature intensity, and seasonal activity should remain distinct.

SH T W (shitāʾ) and SH T T (shattā)

These roots resemble each other in writing but differ in structure and meaning.

SH T W concerns winter and wintering; SH T T concerns separation, dispersion, and diversity.

Shattā describes diverse plants in Ta Ha 20:53, divided hearts in Al-Hashr 59:14, and different efforts in Al-Layl 92:4.

None of these occurrences concerns winter.

Conversely, al-shitāʾi in Quraysh 106:2 names winter and should not be translated as dispersed or diverse journeys.

The final hamza in shitāʾ does not make its root SH T A. The word belongs to the weak root SH T W, whereas shattā belongs to the doubled root SH T T.

Shared initial letters do not make two roots identical.

5. Morphology and translation suggestions for Forqan

Only al-shitāʾi occurs in the Quran.

It is a definite singular noun in the genitive case, as the second term in the construct riḥlata al-shitāʾi.

Al-ṣayfi is coordinated with it by wa and is likewise genitive.

Shitāʾ is neither a verb nor an adjective here. It does not itself express traveling or residing: riḥla supplies the journey and shitāʾ specifies its season.

The following equivalents suit different family members:

  • شِتَاء — shitāʾ: “winter.”
  • شَتْوَة — shatwa: “one winter” or “one instance of wintering,” according to usage.
  • شَتَا — shatā: “spent winter” or “wintered in a place.”
  • أَشْتَى — ashtā: “entered winter” or “winter arrived for someone.”
  • تَشَتَّى — tashattā: “wintered” or “spent winter in a place.”
  • مَشْتَى and مَشْتَاة — mashtā and mashtāh: “winter quarters,” “winter residence,” or “winter period,” as appropriate.
  • شَتَوِيّ — shatawī: “winter” as an adjective, or “wintry.”

These equivalents are not interchangeable without attention to syntax. Wintering is an activity, winter names a season, and winter quarters denotes a place.

For Quraysh 106:2, the suggested rendering is:

إِيلَافِهِمْ رِحْلَةَ الشِّتَاءِ وَالصَّيْفِ

īlāfihim riḥlata al-shitāʾi wa-al-ṣayf

Quraysh 106:2

“Their familiarity with the journey of winter and summer.”

Here “winter” directly represents al-shitāʾ without adding historical travel details. This suggestion does not change the installed verse translation.

“Winter and summer journeys” is natural in explanatory prose, but a direct rendering should preserve the verse’s construction and the relation of riḥla to the two seasons as far as possible.

6. Conclusion

Early Arabic SH T W concerns winter and related matters. Winter is its principal meaning, with related uses for entering the season, spending it, and winter quarters.

Poetry attributed to pre-Islamic poets attests wintering in particular places and seasonal changes of residence in early Arabic life. Cold, the need for warmth, and sometimes food scarcity were associated circumstances, but are not constant components of every use. The recorded figurative sense of famine must also be distinguished from the Quranic season name.

Only al-shitāʾi in Quraysh 106:2 occurs in the Quran. Alongside al-ṣayfi it identifies the seasons of Quraysh’s journeys.

The surah connects those journeys with food and security and calls Quraysh to worship the Lord of this House. Shitāʾ in this verse does not mean commerce, travel to Yemen, hardship, or hunger. Where supported, those matters belong to historical and contextual explanation.

The semantic center of SH T W is “winter and spending life or residing within that season.”

“Winter” is therefore the direct equivalent of Quranic al-shitāʾ. Other derivatives call for “wintering,” “entering winter,” or “winter quarters,” according to structure and use. Forqan’s translation and lexical study should preserve the distinction between the season, seasonal activity, and place of residence.
