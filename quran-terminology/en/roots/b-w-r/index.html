The Qur’anic root B W R

Ruin, fruitlessness, and failure: from uncultivated land and stagnant trade to destruction and the outcome of human action

Qur’anic and lexical forms

(bawār)
Verbal noun or noun of ruin; Ibrāhīm 14:28

بُور
(būr)
Describing people; two Qur’anic uses; plural or descriptive verbal noun

(yabūru)
Masculine indicative imperfect; Fāṭir 35:10

(tabūra)

Arabic B W R concerns ruin, perishing, or becoming fruitless in different settings. Goods without buyers stagnate; uncultivated land yields no crop; an endeavour that misses its intended result fails; and a person caught in corruption may face a destructive outcome.

These uses involve losing an intended result, benefit, or desirable condition. Not all mean complete, irreversible destruction: a stagnant market can recover, and uncultivated land can be cultivated again. Bawār meaning perishing or destruction is much stronger.

The root occurs five times in the Qur’an: once in dār al-bawār, twice as būran describing people, and twice in the verbs yabūru and tabūra. The passages concern those exchanging The God’s favour for disbelief, people forgetting the reminder, the failure of evil plotting, and a trade that will never stagnate or prove fruitless.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: five B W R occurrences

Destruction, corruption, stagnation, and fruitlessness are related but must be distinguished in context. Early Arabic also attests a testing or examining sense that should not be transferred to Qur’anic uses without evidence.

1. The semantic core and branches in early Arabic

Ibn Fāris identifies two principles for B W R in Maqāyīs al-Lughah.

The first is a thing’s destruction and related states of disuse, emptiness, and loss of function.

The second is testing or examining something to discover its condition or characteristics.

Ibn Fāris treats these as two distinct principles. Without reliable historical evidence, testing cannot be declared every derivative’s original meaning, nor destruction necessarily derived from testing.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: ruin and testing as two principles

Destruction and loss of an intended result

Kitāb al-ʿAyn explains al-bawār as perishing.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise records bāra yabūru for perishing, becoming ruined, or passing away.

The family nevertheless extends beyond physical destruction.

Bāra al-matāʿu means that merchandise became unsaleable. The goods may still exist in sound condition while nobody wants to buy them.

Bārat al-sūqu describes a market in which trade has stagnated.

This does not mean the market building or every item has been destroyed: the usual flow of transactions and profit has faltered.

Another expression, bāra ʿamaluhu, means that someone’s work proved fruitless or ineffective.

A person may genuinely strive towards a goal without achieving the intended result. The work then fails in its purpose even though it was actually performed.

Fruitless action is not action that never existed.

These witnesses establish a connection between ruin, stagnation, fruitlessness, and destruction within the first branch.

The connection must remain precise. Every temporary interruption, economic loss, or modest return is not necessarily bawār in its strongest sense.

Uncultivated land versus destroyed land

One tangible use concerns land.

Arḍun bawārun and arḍun bāʾiratun describe land that is not cultivated or has fallen out of agricultural use.

Lexicographers differ in explaining this use.

Abū Ziyād describes undeveloped land whose cultivation is difficult or no longer feasible.

Al-Yazīdī also uses bawr and bawār for land left fallow for a year before being cultivated again the next year.

This difference matters.

Land may be unusable through dryness, salinity, or another problem; a field deliberately rested for a period, however, has not necessarily been ruined.

Temporary fallowing may even form part of proper land management.

Bawār concerning land therefore does not always mean dead soil or permanent loss of growing capacity.

The shared feature is absence of cultivation in the situation described; its cause and duration follow from the sentence and the land’s circumstances.

Lexical sources: unusable land and land left fallow for a year

The afterlife sense of dār al-bawār should consequently not be imported into every agricultural use.

Stagnation in trade

In market language, bawār clearly concerns unsaleability or stagnant trade.

The family describes goods offered without buyers, or trading that has lost its momentum.

In Al-Mufradāt, al-Rāghib stresses severe stagnation and explains that it can lead to spoilage and loss of goods or their benefit.

That offers an intelligible semantic connection between stagnation and destruction.

Food remaining unsold for a long time may spoil: first its sale fails, then the goods themselves deteriorate.

This sequence is not necessary for every kind of merchandise or every bawār use. Unsaleable goods may remain sound.

Al-Rāghib’s explanation illuminates the connection without proving a universal historical law that destruction always developed from market stagnation.

Al-Rāghib: severe stagnation and its relation to corruption

Bawār in human relations

Some early expressions use bawāru al-ayyimi for an unmarried woman who finds no suitor.

This figuratively applies market stagnation to a social situation.

It reflects its period’s language and social relations; it does not establish that human worth or women’s standing depends on marriage or receiving proposals.

An economic expression can extend into social life without transferring every feature of commerce to human relationships.

This use is not Qur’anic and should not be inserted into būran without evidence.

2. The other branch: testing and examining

Ibn Fāris also records testing as a B W R meaning.

Burtu al-shayʾa means that I tested something to discover its condition.

Burtu fulānan likewise concerns testing a person or learning that person’s characteristics.

In animal husbandry, the verb has a more specific use.

Burtu al-nāqata concerns examining a she-camel, particularly by bringing her near a male to discover whether she is pregnant or not.

The action tests and identifies the animal’s condition.

Ibtāra is also attested for testing.

Early explanations cite poetic witnesses about examining camels. Their textual readings and historical attributions are not all sufficiently clear to present them unreservedly as certain pre-Qur’anic evidence.

The lexicons establish an independent testing sense in classical Arabic.

Maqāyīs: examining whether a she-camel is pregnant

It does not directly fit any of the five Qur’anic occurrences.

Tijāratan lan tabūra does not describe a trade that is never tested, but one that does not stagnate or become fruitless.

Wa-makru ulāʾika huwa yabūru likewise describes plotting becoming ruined and failing, rather than being tested.

Dār al-bawār is not a place of testing: its context connects it with destruction.

Testing therefore belongs in a complete study but should not replace ruin and stagnation when translating the Qur’anic forms.

3. Poetic witnesses and bawār applied to humans

ʿAbd Allāh ibn al-Zibaʿrā: būr describing the speaker

A poem by ʿAbd Allāh ibn al-Zibaʿrā al-Sahmī reads:

يَا رَسُولَ الْمَلِيكِ إِنَّ لِسَانِي
رَاتِقٌ مَا فَتَقْتُ إِذْ أَنَا بُورُ

yā rasūla al-malīki inna lisānī / rātiqun mā fataqtu idh anā būru

Translation: “Messenger of the Sovereign, my tongue now mends what I tore apart when I was in ruin.”

Būr here describes the speaker himself.

He recalls a period of wrongful words and conduct whose effects he now seeks to repair.

Būr can therefore describe a living person who has been in a corrupt or ruined condition.

It does not necessarily mean that the person is dead or physically destroyed.

Ibn al-Zibaʿrā lived before Islam, but this line is transmitted as speech after his acceptance of Islam. It should not be presented as poetry composed before the Qur’an.

Al-Rawḍ al-Unuf: Ibn al-Zibaʿrā’s poem after accepting Islam

It nevertheless illuminates Arabic usage close to the period of revelation.

The speaker’s ability to describe his former self matters: he recalls a corrupt condition he now seeks to repair.

Būr does not invariably denote a fate beyond any possibility of change or correction.

Bawār and the ruin of human relationships

Lisān al-ʿArab and Maqāyīs al-Lughah attribute a line to a poet known as Abū Mukʿit al-Asadī:

قُتِلَتْ فَكَانَ تَبَاغِيًا وَتَظَالُمًا
إِنَّ التَّظَالُمَ فِي الصَّدِيقِ بَوَارُ

qutilat fa-kāna tabāghiyan wa-taẓāluman / inna al-taẓāluma fī al-ṣadīqi bawāru

Translation: “She was killed, and mutual aggression and oppression followed; oppression among friends is ruin.”

Bawār describes the consequence of injustice within a human relationship.

Oppression can corrupt friendship from within and lead to conflict or destruction.

The sense therefore extends beyond a person’s death or a place’s devastation to loss of a relationship’s goodness and order.

Sources differ over the poet’s identity and the line’s reading. Abū Mukʿit is identified differently in some reports, and the order of tabāghiyan and taẓāluman varies.

The line should therefore not be presented as certainly attributed and dated pre-Islamic poetry.

It can cautiously attest ruin in social conduct and the consequences of injustice.

Lisān and Tāj: disputed poet identity and the bawār verse reading

Together with market and land uses, these witnesses show the first branch encompassing tangible destruction, failure, and corruption in human conduct.

4. Qur’anic forms and their meanings

The Qur’an uses this root in three morphological categories.

First, bawār, a verbal noun or noun expressing ruin and perishing.

Second, būr, describing people.

Third, the Form I imperfect, once masculine as yabūru and once feminine as tabūra.

Bāra is the past verb; yabūru is its imperfect.

Bawr and bawār are attested verbal nouns.

Bāʾir is the active participle, describing something ruined, fruitless, or unsaleable according to context.

Causative abāra concerns destroying or bringing about ruin.

Bawwara is also attested for leaving land uncultivated.

These latter forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

Is būr the plural of bāʾir?

Lexicons and grammatical explanations record two important analyses.

One treats būr as a plural of bāʾir, comparable to other Arabic adjectival plurals.

Qawman būran would then mean a group of ruined people facing destruction.

The other treats būr as a verbal noun or noun used descriptively, applicable to one or several people, masculine or feminine.

Al-Farrāʾ and Abū ʿUbaydah report its use for both one and many.

Neither analysis fundamentally changes the two verses’ general meaning: the word describes a group of people in both.

Qawm being plural should therefore not make būr exclusively a broken plural. The verbal-noun analysis is also attested.

Al-Ṭabarī: plural and descriptive verbal-noun analyses of būr

Persian equivalents meaning “a ruined people” or “people caught in ruin” convey the sense without putting the morphological disagreement into the translation itself.

Distinguishing yabūru and tabūra

In Fāṭir 35:10, yabūru is third-person masculine singular and refers to makr.

In Fāṭir 35:29, tabūra is third-person feminine singular and concerns tijārah.

This reflects grammatical gender, rather than two independent root meanings.

Plotting is the subject of ruin in the first verse.

Trade is the subject in the second, where its becoming ruined is negated.

Tabūra is also subjunctive after lan, a construction negating the future.

Both forms therefore concern loss of an intended result or ruin, despite their different inflections.

5. Qur’anic uses: from the abode of destruction to failed conduct

Dāra al-bawāri: Ibrāhīm 14:28

Verse text:

أَلَمْ تَرَ إِلَى الَّذِينَ بَدَّلُوا نِعْمَتَ اللَّهِ كُفْرًا وَأَحَلُّوا قَوْمَهُمْ دَارَ الْبَوَارِ

a-lam tara ilā alladhīna baddalū niʿmata Allāhi kufran wa-aḥallū qawmahum dāra al-bawāri

Translation:

“Have you not considered those who exchanged The God’s favour for disbelief and brought their people into the abode of destruction?”

Ibrāhīm 14:28

Al-bawāri is genitive after dāra, forming dār al-bawār.

Lexically, bawār here concerns destruction and perishing.

Identifying the abode requires more than the word bawār alone.

Ibrāhīm 14:29 explicitly explains it with jahannama yaṣlawnahā.

In this setting, dār al-bawār therefore refers to Hell.

Its broad lexical range should not make this occurrence merely a psychological condition, economic failure, or ruin in earthly life.

The next verse explicitly identifies its afterlife referent.

Al-Ṭabarī: dār al-bawār and the following verse

Bawār, disbelief, and social responsibility

The verse describes people exchanging The God’s favour for disbelief and bringing their people into the abode of destruction.

Two related actions appear.

First is their own relationship with favour and disbelief.

Second is their role in bringing their people to a destructive outcome.

The meaning should therefore not be restricted to individual destruction: their conduct and direction also affect others’ collective fate.

Bawār itself does not mean corrupt leadership or leading people astray.

Those ideas follow from baddalū, aḥallū, and the context.

Bawār expresses the outcome of ruin and destruction.

Ibrāhīm 14:30 also describes setting up equals to The God and leading others away from His path, further clarifying the setting of disbelief and misguidance.

These verses should not, without establishing relevant conditions, turn every religious or political disagreement into bringing people to Hell.

The verse describes particular conduct and its outcome.

Qawman būran: Al-Furqān 25:18

Verse text:

قَالُوا سُبْحَانَكَ مَا كَانَ يَنْبَغِي لَنَا أَنْ نَتَّخِذَ مِنْ دُونِكَ مِنْ أَوْلِيَاءَ وَلَٰكِنْ مَتَّعْتَهُمْ وَآبَاءَهُمْ حَتَّىٰ نَسُوا الذِّكْرَ وَكَانُوا قَوْمًا بُورًا

qālū subḥānaka mā kāna yanbaghī lanā an nattakhidha min dūnika min awliyāʾa wa-lākin mattaʿtahum wa-ābāʾahum ḥattā nasū al-dhikra wa-kānū qawman būran

Translation:

“They said: Glory be to You! It was not fitting for us to take protectors apart from You. But You let them and their fathers enjoy life until they forgot the reminder and became a ruined people.”

Al-Furqān 25:18

The verse belongs to the questioning of those who took other deities or protectors alongside The God.

Al-Furqān 25:17 asks those who were worshipped whether they led the people astray or the people themselves strayed from the path.

Al-Furqān 25:18 gives their answer.

It ends by describing the worshippers as qawman būran.

Būr describes people here, rather than land or goods.

The semantic field of ruin and loss of good nevertheless remains.

These people enjoyed means and benefits but did not remain attentive to the reminder and right worship.

Does būr mean dead people?

Lexicons sometimes explain būr as halkā, those who have perished.

Here, however, the people described enjoyed benefits during life and forgot the reminder.

The description therefore communicates more than physical death.

It concerns their ruined direction and outcome.

A Persian equivalent meaning “a ruined people” or “people caught in ruin” expresses this more clearly than “dead people.”

Būr should also not become poor, landless, or without property.

The verse itself mentions their enjoyment and that of their fathers.

Material prosperity and moral ruin can coexist.

That does not make prosperity the certain cause of ruin.

The verse concerns forgetting the reminder and wrongful direction. Possessing benefits is not itself a sin or a sign of destruction.

Qawman būran: Al-Fatḥ 48:12

Verse text:

بَلْ ظَنَنْتُمْ أَنْ لَنْ يَنْقَلِبَ الرَّسُولُ وَالْمُؤْمِنُونَ إِلَىٰ أَهْلِيهِمْ أَبَدًا وَزُيِّنَ ذَٰلِكَ فِي قُلُوبِكُمْ وَظَنَنْتُمْ ظَنَّ السَّوْءِ وَكُنْتُمْ قَوْمًا بُورًا

bal ẓanantum an lan yanqaliba al-rasūlu wa-al-muʾminūna ilā ahlīhim abadan wa-zuyyina dhālika fī qulūbikum wa-ẓanantum ẓanna al-sawʾi wa-kuntum qawman būran

Translation:

“Rather, you thought the Messenger and the believers would never return to their families; that was made attractive in your hearts, and you entertained an evil suspicion, and you were a ruined people.”

Al-Fatḥ 48:12

This concerns a group of Bedouins who stayed behind rather than accompanying the Prophet.

In Al-Fatḥ 48:11, they say their possessions and families occupied them and ask the Prophet to seek forgiveness for them.

The same verse says they speak what is not in their hearts.

Al-Fatḥ 48:12 then exposes their inward expectation: the Prophet and believers would never return from that journey.

Qawman būran describes people characterised by evil suspicion, speech inconsistent with their inner state, and wrongful conduct.

This resembles Al-Furqān in describing people as būr.

Their settings nevertheless differ.

Al-Furqān concerns worshipping others besides The God and forgetting the reminder.

Al-Fatḥ concerns evil expectations about the Prophet and believers, and excuses inconsistent with the speakers’ inward reality.

One setting’s details should not all become the word’s definition in the other.

Both involve ruin and fruitlessness of direction; the particular conduct follows from context.

Does būr state an unchangeable essence and destiny?

Būr is severe condemnation here.

The root alone does not establish that every person described this way at some stage is permanently incapable of correction or return.

Arabic can describe a person’s corrupt condition from which that person later departs.

Al-Fatḥ 48:16 also addresses a future summons for the group who stayed behind and the consequences of obedience or turning away.

Al-Fatḥ 48:12 should therefore not become a perpetual judgment about every individual and later generation without evidence.

The possibility of correction likewise should not weaken the condemnation of their actual behaviour.

The Qur’an here identifies a particular group’s expectations and conduct as corrupt and wrong.

Al-Ṭabarī: evil expectations and ruined people

6. Plotting and trade: contrasting uses in Fāṭir

Fāṭir contains the root’s two verbal occurrences.

Fāṭir 35:10 describes plotting that comes to ruin.

Fāṭir 35:29 describes a trade that does not become ruined or stagnant.

The root therefore concerns more than people or their afterlife destiny: actions and the results of human conduct can also undergo bawār.

Plotting that fails: Fāṭir 35:10

Verse text:

مَنْ كَانَ يُرِيدُ الْعِزَّةَ فَلِلَّهِ الْعِزَّةُ جَمِيعًا ۚ إِلَيْهِ يَصْعَدُ الْكَلِمُ الطَّيِّبُ وَالْعَمَلُ الصَّالِحُ يَرْفَعُهُ ۚ وَالَّذِينَ يَمْكُرُونَ السَّيِّئَاتِ لَهُمْ عَذَابٌ شَدِيدٌ ۖ وَمَكْرُ أُولَٰئِكَ هُوَ يَبُورُ

man kāna yurīdu al-ʿizzata fa-li-Allāhi al-ʿizzatu jamīʿan ilayhi yaṣʿadu al-kalimu al-ṭayyibu wa-al-ʿamalu al-ṣāliḥu yarfaʿuhu wa-alladhīna yamkurūna al-sayyiʾāti lahum ʿadhābun shadīdun wa-makru ulāʾika huwa yabūru

Translation:

“Whoever desires might: all might belongs to The God. To Him good speech ascends, and righteous action raises it. Those who plot evil deeds face severe punishment, and their plotting is what comes to ruin.”

Fāṭir 35:10

Yabūru concerns the plotting itself.

The subject is makru ulāʾika, rather than every plotter’s body or life.

The verse describes the outcome that befalls their plot.

Makr here is a plan or strategy connected with wrongdoing.

Yabūru indicates its ultimate ruin and fruitlessness.

A Persian explanation meaning “their plotting ends in failure” can clarify that outcome.

“Their plotting comes to ruin” remains closer to the wording, preserving the verb while leaving how the failure occurs to explanation.

Does failed plotting mean that no temporary harm occurs?

The verse should not be taken to deny every evil plot any effect from its outset or any capacity to harm.

An unjust plan may be carried out and cause real injury while remaining ultimately failed and ruined in its final value and outcome.

An action’s occurrence, its harm, and its ultimate success must be distinguished.

Makru ulāʾika huwa yabūru states plotting’s outcome, rather than denying every stage and temporary consequence in one sentence.

Nor does bawār necessarily mean that every detail of the plot will be publicly exposed. Exposure may occur, but is not a fixed component of yabūru.

Its core meaning remains ruin and failure of the plot.

Al-Ṭabarī: plotting’s outcome and readings of yarfaʿuhu

Trade that does not stagnate: Fāṭir 35:29

Verse text:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ يَتْلُونَ كِتَابَ اللَّهِ وَأَقَامُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَأَنْفَقُوا مِمَّا رَزَقْنَاهُمْ سِرًّا وَعَلَانِيَةً يَرْجُونَ تِجَارَةً لَنْ تَبُورَ

inna alladhīna yatlūna kitāba Allāhi wa-aqāmū al-ṣalāta wa-anfaqū mimmā razaqnāhum sirran wa-ʿalāniyatan yarjūna tijāratan lan tabūra

Translation:

“Those who recite The God’s Book, establish ṣalāh, and spend secretly and openly from what We have provided them hope for a trade that will never stagnate or prove fruitless.”

Fāṭir 35:29

Tabūra describes trade here.

It directly connects with the established early expressions bārat al-sūqu and bāra al-matāʿu.

A merchant may commit wealth and effort yet end with stagnation, loss, or no return.

The verse describes a trade without that outcome.

Lan tabūra therefore negates stagnation, ruin, and fruitlessness of the trade.

What does trade mean here?

The verse names three actions: reciting The God’s Book, establishing ṣalāh, and spending provision secretly and openly.

It then expresses hope for a trade that does not fail.

Trade is therefore more than ordinary market transactions here: it images human action and hope for its return and reward.

Fāṭir 35:30 supports this through full reward and an increase from The God’s bounty.

The intended hope concerns the lasting return of worthy action before The God.

Tijārah itself should nevertheless not be lexically defined as worship or reward.

It retains the image of exchange and expected return, applied to the relation between human action and divine reward.

Does this promise guaranteed profit in worldly business?

Tijāratan lan tabūra does not mean every believer’s economic transactions will always avoid loss.

The verse connects its trade with recitation, ṣalāh, and spending; the next explains its outcome through divine reward.

Its main concern is these actions’ value and return before The God.

Giving wealth to someone in need may visibly reduce a person’s possessions.

If the giving is worthy and accepted by The God, its value and reward do not vanish with that worldly reduction.

Profit and loss in this image should therefore not be measured only through immediate material accounts.

Trade without bawār contrasts here with activity whose effort and impressive appearance ultimately yield nothing.

Al-Ṭabarī: unfailing trade and the reward of action

The contrast between Fāṭir’s two uses

The two passages show a clear difference.

Fāṭir 35:10 states that evil plotting comes to ruin.

Fāṭir 35:29 states that trade arising from worthy actions does not.

Both apply B W R to the result of human activity.

One affirms bawār, while the other negates it.

This does not mean every human action immediately succeeds or fails materially in proportion to its moral worth.

The verses clarify differences of outcome and value in divine assessment.

7. Related roots and expressions

B W R (bawār) and H L K (halāk)

Study of H L K

Halāk concerns destruction and the end of something’s existence or condition.

Bawār can closely approach this, as in dār al-bawār.

Its range is nevertheless broader.

A stagnant market or uncultivated land need not be physically destroyed.

Perishing may therefore translate bawār appropriately in some contexts, without making every occurrence complete destruction.

B W R (bawār) and F S D (fasād)

Study of F S D

Fasād concerns corruption, loss of soundness, or disruption of a proper condition.

Bawār can accompany this, but economic and practical uses also foreground fruitlessness and stagnation.

Goods may be unsaleable while still sound in quality.

Conversely, food may spoil even when there was demand before it spoiled.

Corruption and stagnation are therefore related but different.

In Fāṭir 35:10, evil plotting is both morally wrong and ultimately ruined; those aspects should be identified separately through the words and syntax.

B W R (bawār) and B Ṭ L (buṭlān)

Study of B Ṭ L

B Ṭ L concerns invalidity, loss of effect, or being false.

Bawār applied to work and plotting can also concern fruitlessness.

Bāra ʿamaluhu describes work that fails to reach its intended result.

Buṭlān may concern the validity of a claim, ruling, or act, whereas bawār additionally covers stagnant trade and uncultivated land.

The roots overlap without being complete synonyms.

B W R (bawār) and KH S R (khusrān)

Study of KH S R

Khusrān concerns loss of something valuable.

Bawār can lead to loss, while foregrounding stagnation, ruin, or failure of an intended return.

A transaction may make a small loss without the whole business becoming stagnant or fruitless.

A market may conversely stagnate before every merchant has calculated a definite loss.

Both can concern wrongful human outcomes in the Qur’an, but their differences should remain visible in translation.

B W R (bawār) and B Q Y (baqāʾ)

Study of B Q Y

Baqāʾ concerns continuing and not passing away.

Bawār in its strong sense reaches ruin and perishing.

They oppose one another in that respect.

Fruitlessness, however, is not always nonexistence.

Goods may remain while having no buyer.

Land may exist while remaining uncultivated.

Work may have been performed and retain material effects while losing its expected result and value.

Bawār can therefore express loss of result and function rather than invariably absence of existence.

B W R (bawār) and T B R (tabār)

Study of T B R

T B R is an independent root concerning destruction and breaking apart.

Qur’anic tabāran and other related forms concern destruction and ruin.

Bawār and tabār can consequently approach each other semantically.

Their shared destructive sense does not make them one root.

B W R also clearly covers economic and agricultural stagnation and fruitlessness. T B R requires its own evidence.

One family’s characteristics should not be transferred to the other without examination.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

For destructive bāra, use “became ruined,” “passed away,” or “perished.”

In trade, “became unsaleable,” “stagnated,” or “proved fruitless” can be more precise.

For bawār concerning perishing, use “ruin” or “destruction.”

For land-describing bāʾir, use “uncultivated” or “fallow”; it need not be permanently destroyed or impossible to cultivate.

For būr describing people, use “ruined” or “caught in ruin.”

In the testing branch, bāra al-shayʾa means “tested” or “examined.” This is not Qur’anic and should not be inserted into the five passages.

Proposed translations for the Qur’anic uses

Ibrāhīm 14:28

«دَارَ الْبَوَارِ»

dāra al-bawāri

Proposed: “The abode of destruction.”

This preserves perishing; the next verse explicitly identifies Hell in this setting.

Al-Furqān 25:18

«وَكَانُوا قَوْمًا بُورًا»

wa-kānū qawman būran

Proposed: “And they became a ruined people.”

The context concerns the group’s ruin and deviation, rather than merely physical death.

Al-Fatḥ 48:12

«وَكُنْتُمْ قَوْمًا بُورًا»

wa-kuntum qawman būran

Proposed: “And you were a ruined people.”

This preserves severe condemnation without reducing the description to one particular sin such as lying or evil suspicion.

Fāṭir 35:10

«وَمَكْرُ أُولَٰئِكَ هُوَ يَبُورُ»

wa-makru ulāʾika huwa yabūru

Proposed: “And their plotting is what comes to ruin.”

Plotting is the verb’s subject, and ruin and failure must be attributed to it.

Fāṭir 35:29

«يَرْجُونَ تِجَارَةً لَنْ تَبُورَ»

yarjūna tijāratan lan tabūra

Proposed: “They hope for a trade that will never stagnate or prove fruitless.”

The economic sense fits the trading image; the next verse identifies the intended return and reward of worthy conduct.

Translation versus interpretation

A translation should not stack every sense of destruction, stagnation, corruption, and loss without contextual reason.

Where Hell is the subject, destruction is appropriate.

Where trade is the subject, stagnation and fruitlessness are clearer.

For people, ruin preserves the family’s meaning without adding a claim about each individual’s physical death or unchangeable destiny.

Būr should not directly become unbeliever, hypocrite, liar, poor, or faithless.

Some such descriptions may apply in the surrounding context, but būr’s own lexical meaning remains ruin and loss of good.

9. Conclusion

B W R has two attested semantic branches.

The first concerns ruin, destruction, stagnation, and fruitlessness.

The second concerns testing or examining a person or thing to learn its condition.

The first does not always mean complete physical destruction: markets can stagnate, fields remain uncultivated, and work fail to achieve its intended result without materially disappearing.

In a context of perishing, however, it can communicate severe destruction.

All five Qur’anic uses belong to the first branch.

Ibrāhīm 14:28 gives the abode of destruction, identified as Hell in the next verse.

Al-Furqān 25:18 describes people forgetting the reminder amid life’s benefits and becoming ruined.

Al-Fatḥ 48:12 describes people with evil expectations about the Prophet and believers whose speech did not match their inward reality.

Fāṭir 35:10 attributes ruin to an evil plot.

Fāṭir 35:29 describes trade that does not fail: worthy actions and hope for their reward before The God.

Bawār can therefore concern human condition, actions’ results, wealth and trade, or destructive outcomes.

The root’s meaning must always be distinguished from the subject to which the sentence attributes it.

Final root definition: In its principal Qur’anic branch, B W R concerns becoming ruined and losing an intended result, benefit, or desirable condition. According to context, it can denote stagnation, fruitlessness, corruption, or destruction.

An independent branch concerns testing and examining, but is absent from the five Qur’anic occurrences.

The Qur’anic branch particularly expresses ruin and fruitlessness contrasted with continuing benefit and attainment of a result, rather than necessarily physical destruction.

Accordingly, “destruction” suits dār al-bawār, “ruin” qawman būran, “coming to ruin” makru ulāʾika huwa yabūru, and “stagnating or proving fruitless” tijāratan lan tabūra.

These translation differences preserve the shared semantic field without erasing each occurrence’s particular meaning.
