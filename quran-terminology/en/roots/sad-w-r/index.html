Form, fashioning, and the shape of beings: from inclining and herds of animals to al-ṣūr in the events of resurrection

1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

Arabic Ṣ W R has several semantic branches. Its best-known branch concerns something’s form, shape, or configuration. Ṣūrah denotes the form and structure by which something is recognized and distinguished from other things. Ṣawwara means to give form or shape.

Another branch concerns inclining or causing something to incline. Fa-ṣurhunna in Abraham’s story is considered within this field, although lexicographers disagree about its precise meaning.

Al-ṣūr also names something blown into during the events of resurrection. Early sources commonly explain it as a trumpet or horn for blowing. Its relationship to ṣūrah and the possibility of other readings have also been discussed.

Ṣ W R occurs nineteen times in the Qur’an, in the following forms:

  • فَصُرْهُنَّ (fa-ṣurhunna): incline them toward yourself; Al-Baqarah 2:260, once.
  • يُصَوِّرُكُمْ، صَوَّرْنَاكُمْ، صَوَّرَكُمْ (yuṣawwirukum, ṣawwarnākum, ṣawwarakum): fashioning and giving shape; Āl ʿImrān 3:6, Al-Aʿrāf 7:11, Ghāfir 40:64, and Al-Taghābun 64:3, four occurrences in total.
  • صُورَكُمْ (ṣuwarakum): your forms or configurations; Ghāfir 40:64 and Al-Taghābun 64:3, twice.
  • صُورَةٍ (ṣūratin): form or shape; Al-Infiṭār 82:8, once.
  • الْمُصَوِّرُ (al-muṣawwiru): the Fashioner; Al-Ḥashr 59:24, once.
  • الصُّور (al-ṣūr): what is blown into; Al-Anʿām 6:73, Al-Kahf 18:99, Ṭā Hā 20:102, Al-Muʾminūn 23:101, Al-Naml 27:87, Yā Sīn 36:51, Al-Zumar 39:68, Qāf 50:20, Al-Ḥāqqah 69:13, and Al-Nabaʾ 78:18, ten occurrences in total.

Arabic also knows forms such as تَصْوِير (taṣwīr), تَصَوُّر (taṣawwur), تَصَاوِير (taṣāwīr), أَصْوَر (aṣwar), صَوَار (ṣawār), and صِيرَان (ṣīrān), but these particular forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

One distinction must be maintained from the outset: sharing root letters does not by itself establish that every word developed from one original image or historical meaning.

2. Semantic branches in early Arabic

Does Ṣ W R have a single semantic core?

Introducing this root in Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris states:

الصَّادُ وَالْوَاوُ وَالرَّاءُ كَلِمَاتٌ كَثِيرَةٌ مُتَبَايِنَةُ الْأُصُولِ

al-ṣādu wa-al-wāwu wa-al-rāʾu kalimātun kathīratun mutabāyinatu al-uṣūli

The family contains numerous words whose semantic origins differ.

He explains that, unlike some other roots, its uses cannot all be explained by one shared derivational principle.

He identifies inclining and causing inclination as one clear branch, while separately recording the forms of beings, a group of palms, a herd of wild cattle, musk’s container or scent, and other uses.

This caution matters.

Words in a linguistic family may resemble one another in form while their precise historical relationship remains unclear. Inventing a shared image and explaining every meaning through it would then be speculative etymology rather than historical investigation.

Ṣ W R should therefore be studied while preserving its actual branches.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: branches with differing origins

Ṣūrah: form and configuration

Arabic صُورَة (ṣūrah) denotes something’s shape or configuration.

A person’s, horse’s, or tree’s form, for example, concerns the features by which its shape and structure are recognized.

In Lisān al-ʿArab, Ibn Manẓūr transmits Ibn Sīdah’s explanation connecting ṣūrah with shape. He also explains its broader use for something’s reality, configuration, or description.

In this sense, ṣūrah is not restricted to a human face.

It may concern the whole body, a building, an animal, or how an action takes place.

صورة الأمر (ṣūrat al-amr), for example, can denote an affair’s manner or configuration without that affair being a body possessing a face.

Ṣūrah thus has a broader linguistic field than “face.”

Lisān al-ʿArab: form, configuration, and distinguishing beings

Perceptible form and mentally conceived form

Broader Arabic usage distinguishes something’s perceptible shape from a person’s mental image of it.

A tree’s or animal’s shape may be seen with the eyes.

When imagining something, however, a person may reconstruct its shape, features, or manner in the mind without the object being directly present.

تَصَوَّرَ (taṣawwara) is also used for such mental conception or apprehension.

This relates to perceptible form, but does not mean that Ṣ W R always denotes thought, imagination, or inward awareness.

In the verses about fashioning humans, the principal subject is the shape and configuration of creation, rather than merely producing a mental image of a person.

Taṣwīr: representing a form

تَصْوِير (taṣwīr) likewise concerns giving form or representing something’s shape.

Someone may draw an animal on a surface or make a figure resembling it.

The representation then depicts the thing’s form.

The being’s own form must nevertheless be distinguished from its representation.

A person’s form is their actual configuration; a painting, statue, or recorded image represents that configuration.

Not every ṣūrah is therefore a painted or human-made image.

Modern taṣwīr has also acquired technical meanings in photography and digital imaging. These continue the field of form and representation, but the details of modern technology should not be imposed on the verses’ historical meanings.

3. Inclining and other early Arabic uses

Ṣawar: inclination

Lexical sources relate صَوَر (ṣawar) to inclination and deviation from an upright position.

رجل أَصْوَر (rajul aṣwar), for example, describes someone whose neck or head inclines to one side.

صُرْتُ الشَّيْءَ إِلَيَّ (ṣurtu al-shayʾa ilayya) means that I inclined something toward myself or drew it in my direction.

This matters for Al-Baqarah 2:260, since fa-ṣurhunna ilayka structurally concerns directing something toward the addressee.

Lisān al-ʿArab quotes the following poetic witness:

اللَّهُ يَعْلَمُ أَنَّا فِي تَلَفُّتِنَا
يَوْمَ الْفِرَاقِ إِلَى أَحْبَابِنَا صُورُ

allāhu yaʿlamu annā fī talaffutinā / yawma al-firāqi ilā aḥbābinā ṣūru

Translation:

“God knows that, in turning back toward our loved ones on the day of parting, we incline toward them with longing.”

Ṣūr here is an adjectival plural concerning inclination.

Inclination may here involve emotional attachment and longing. These come from the scene of parting and loved ones, however, rather than Ṣ W R always meaning love.

The witness is transmitted in lexical sources, but its precise historical attribution is unclear. It should not be assigned to a particular pre-Islamic poet without independent investigation.

Lisān al-ʿArab: inclination and the witness ilā aḥbābinā ṣūr

Inclining or cutting?

Early sources also report cutting and separating for certain verbal forms of this family.

Tawbah ibn al-Ḥumayyir, a poet of the Umayyad period, describes ascending toward his beloved:

فَمَدَّتْ لِيَ الْأَسْبَابَ حَتَّى بَلَغْتُهَا
بِرِفْقِي وَقَدْ كَادَ ارْتِقَائِي يَصُورُهَا

fa-maddat liya al-asbāba ḥattā balaghtuhā / bi-rifqī wa-qad kāda irtiqāʾī yaṣūruhā

Translation:

“She lowered the ropes for me until I reached her carefully, although my ascent had nearly severed them.”

Yaṣūruhā in this witness has been understood as cutting or breaking the ropes apart.

Some early Arabic forms in this family could thus concern cutting.

One poetic use does not, however, establish that ṣurhunna in Al-Baqarah 2:260 necessarily means cutting into pieces to the exclusion of other possibilities.

The word, preposition, and verse context must each be examined.

Ṣawār: a group of animals

Early Arabic uses صَوَار (ṣawār) for a herd of wild cattle or similar animals.

Its established plural is صِيرَان (ṣīrān).

Imruʾ al-Qays says in a poem:

وَظَلَّ لِصِيرَانِ الصَّرِيمِ غَمَاغِمٌ
يُدَاعِسُهَا بِالسَّمْهَرِيِّ الْمُعَلَّبِ

wa-ẓalla li-ṣīrāni al-ṣarīmi ghamāghimun / yudāʿisuhā bi-al-samhariyyi al-muʿallabi

An approximate rendering:

“Murmuring calls rose from the herds of wild cattle in the thicket, as he engaged them with a sturdy spear.”

Ṣīrān here denotes groups of animals.

This pre-Islamic poetic witness shows the family’s established use for animal herds.

That use concerns neither human form nor the trumpet of resurrection.

Ibn Fāris likewise lists it among branches that should not be derived from fashioning or inclining without evidence.

Majmaʿ al-Bayān: attribution of the ṣīrān witness to Imruʾ al-Qays

Groups of palms and other limited uses

Lexical sources also record الصَّوْر (al-ṣawr) for a group of palms.

الصِّوَار (al-ṣiwār) has also been reported for musk’s container or scent, with lexical disagreement about it.

These uses show that historical Arabic Ṣ W R is a family with several branches.

None of these meanings is inherently present in the direct Qur’anic uses of ṣuwarakum, al-muṣawwir, or al-ṣūr as a trumpet.

A root study should acknowledge them without constructing an imaginary link between every branch.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: groups of palms, cattle, and musk

4. Fashioning humans in the Qur’an

Yuṣawwirukum fī al-arḥāmi

Āl ʿImrān states:

هُوَ الَّذِي يُصَوِّرُكُمْ فِي الْأَرْحَامِ كَيْفَ يَشَاءُ

huwa alladhī yuṣawwirukum fī al-arḥāmi kayfa yashāʾu

“He is the one who fashions you in the wombs however He wills.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:6

Yuṣawwirukum is a Form II imperfect verb concerning giving humans form.

The verse also identifies where this fashioning occurs:

فِي الْأَرْحَامِ (fī al-arḥāmi).

That is, in the wombs.

The subject is therefore humans’ actual formation during prenatal growth.

Ṣūrah here is not merely a person’s final face, but the form and configuration of the being growing in the womb.

Kayfa yashāʾu also indicates God’s will in this fashioning.

The verse places humans’ outward and structural differences within divine creation. The root alone should not, however, be made to yield details of heredity, genes, cell division, or biological mechanisms of embryonic growth.

Those are subjects of empirical study, not matters detailed by this verse.

Khalaqnākum thumma ṣawwarnākum

Al-Aʿrāf states:

وَلَقَدْ خَلَقْنَاكُمْ ثُمَّ صَوَّرْنَاكُمْ ثُمَّ قُلْنَا لِلْمَلَائِكَةِ اسْجُدُوا لِآدَمَ

wa-laqad khalaqnākum thumma ṣawwarnākum thumma qulnā li-al-malāʾikati usjudū li-ādama

“We indeed created you, then fashioned you, then told the angels: Prostrate before Adam.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:11

The verse contains two distinct verbs:

خَلَقْنَاكُمْ (khalaqnākum) and صَوَّرْنَاكُمْ (ṣawwarnākum).

The first belongs to KH L Q and concerns creating.

The second belongs to Ṣ W R and concerns giving shape and configuration.

The distinction matters: both verbs should not simply be translated as “We created.”

Thumma and the verbs’ order do not by themselves determine the temporal and biological details of human origins.

The verse continues with Adam; the relationship between the plural address “you” and Adam’s story should therefore be examined in that verse’s reflection.

For studying Ṣ W R, the clear point is that fashioning differs from creation’s general designation.

Wa-ṣawwarakum fa-aḥsana ṣuwarakum

This expression occurs in two verses with closely similar structures.

Ghāfir 40:64 states:

وَصَوَّرَكُمْ فَأَحْسَنَ صُوَرَكُمْ وَرَزَقَكُمْ مِنَ الطَّيِّبَاتِ

wa-ṣawwarakum fa-aḥsana ṣuwarakum wa-razaqakum mina al-ṭayyibāti

“He fashioned you, made your forms good, and provided you with wholesome things.”

Al-Taghābun 64:3 also states:

وَصَوَّرَكُمْ فَأَحْسَنَ صُوَرَكُمْ وَإِلَيْهِ الْمَصِيرُ

wa-ṣawwarakum fa-aḥsana ṣuwarakum wa-ilayhi al-maṣīru

“He fashioned you and made your forms good; to Him is the final return.”

Both verses place two forms from the same family together:

صَوَّرَكُمْ (ṣawwarakum) and صُوَرَكُمْ (ṣuwarakum).

The act of giving form comes first, followed by making those forms good.

Aḥsana belongs to Ḥ S N and expresses goodness.

Goodness is therefore explicit in this sentence, but should not be treated as a fixed part of ṣawwara’s meaning.

Giving form and giving good form are distinguishable expressions.

Does goodness of form mean everyone looks alike?

These verses describe making humans’ forms good.

They do not establish that everyone has exactly the same bodily appearance or lacks natural differences in body and face.

Nor should creation’s goodness be restricted to one culture’s or period’s standards of outward beauty.

People differ in body shape, color, size, facial features, and physical abilities.

Al-Rūm 30:22 itself presents differences in human languages and colors as signs of creation.

Good fashioning should therefore not be reduced to one outward shape or a limited aesthetic judgment.

Bodily differences or congenital disabilities alone are likewise no measure of moral worth, closeness to God, or human dignity.

Ṣuwar’s lexical meaning here is human forms and configurations. Human worth and dignity concern a broader network of verses.

5. Fī ayyi ṣūratin: form and human composition

Al-Infiṭār states:

الَّذِي خَلَقَكَ فَسَوَّاكَ فَعَدَلَكَ
فِي أَيِّ صُورَةٍ مَا شَاءَ رَكَّبَكَ

alladhī khalaqaka fa-sawwāka fa-ʿadalaka / fī ayyi ṣūratin mā shāʾa rakkabaka

“The one who created you, ordered you, and proportioned you; in whatever form He willed, He composed you.”

Al-Infiṭār 82:7 and Al-Infiṭār 82:8

Four verbs and concepts are connected here:

خَلَقَكَ (khalaqaka), سَوَّاكَ (sawwāka), عَدَلَكَ (ʿadalaka), and رَكَّبَكَ (rakkabaka).

Each contributes its own meaning.

Khalq concerns creation.

Taswiyah concerns ordering and establishing an appropriate structure.

ʿAdl here relates to structural proportion and balance.

Tarkīb concerns bringing parts together and forming a structure.

Among these concepts, ṣūrah denotes the configuration or shape in which the human is composed.

The verse connects fashioning and assembling parts without making the two expressions identical.

We may discuss both a being’s constituent parts and the form in which those parts are arranged.

The verse should not, however, be made to yield a detailed theory of every biological stage’s sequence or the composition of matter and consciousness.

Ṣūrah is also not necessarily restricted to a human face here, but concerns the person’s shape and configuration within creation.

6. Al-muṣawwir: fashioning as a divine description

Al-Ḥashr states:

هُوَ اللَّهُ الْخَالِقُ الْبَارِئُ الْمُصَوِّرُ لَهُ الْأَسْمَاءُ الْحُسْنَىٰ

huwa allāhu al-khāliqu al-bāriʾu al-muṣawwiru lahu al-asmāʾu al-ḥusnā

“He is The God, the Creator, the Originator, the Fashioner; to Him belong the finest names.”

Al-Ḥashr 59:24

Al-muṣawwir is a Form II active participle of Ṣ W R.

Being the Fashioner is a divine description in this verse.

It accompanies al-khāliq and al-bāriʾ.

Distinguishing khāliq, bāriʾ, and muṣawwir

The three titles are close in creation’s field but belong to three different roots.

Khāliq concerns creating and bringing into existence.

Lexical and Qur’anic discussions connect bāriʾ with originating beings and making them distinct and identifiable.

Muṣawwir emphasizes giving form and configuration.

The verse thus expresses different aspects of creation through several terms.

Their order should not, however, be made into a definite temporal and physical system in which every being necessarily passes through three completely separate, measurable stages.

Word order may highlight different aspects of creation without fully explaining its mechanism.

Does muṣawwir concern only outward form?

The root’s direct use connects fashioning with beings’ shapes and configurations.

Ibn Manẓūr explains al-muṣawwir as the one giving beings their particular forms and configurations so that they are distinguished from one another.

This fits the verses concerning human fashioning.

It does not follow that the divine action described is limited to making faces, bodily dimensions, or outward appearance.

Nor does muṣawwir itself establish every spiritual, moral, cognitive, or destinational feature of a human being.

Fashioning contributes a particular aspect of creation’s description and should be understood within that scope.

Calling God al-muṣawwir also does not mean He works like a human painter or sculptor with tools, a material hand, and ready-made substances.

Sharing a term with human work does not make the divine action’s manner identical.

Lisān al-ʿArab: al-muṣawwir and fashioning

Human fashioning and making images

Arabic taṣwīr may also concern making or representing a being’s form.

The verses about al-muṣawwir and ṣuwarakum, however, concern divine fashioning.

These verses themselves issue no independent legal ruling about painting, sculpture, photography, or imaging.

Such rulings require examining texts about human acts, their subjects, images’ status, and their uses.

Lexically, making a figure also does not itself mean worshipping it.

The Qur’an mentions tamāthīl both in Abraham’s story, where figures are objects of religious devotion, and among Solomon’s commissioned works, without stating in the latter account that they were worshipped.

Fashioning, representing a shape, and making a figure an object of worship must therefore be distinguished.

7. Al-ṣūr: blowing in the events of resurrection

A major Qur’anic branch is al-ṣūr, occurring ten times, always in connection with blowing.

Arabic lexical sources explain al-ṣūr as a horn or trumpet blown into.

Disagreements are nevertheless recorded about its precise nature and relationship to ṣūrah.

Yunfakhu fī al-ṣūri

Al-Anʿām 6:73 states:

وَلَهُ الْمُلْكُ يَوْمَ يُنْفَخُ فِي الصُّورِ

wa-lahu al-mulku yawma yunfakhu fī al-ṣūri

“To Him belongs sovereignty on the day when al-ṣūr is blown into.”

Al-Naml 27:87 also states:

وَيَوْمَ يُنْفَخُ فِي الصُّورِ فَفَزِعَ مَنْ فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَمَنْ فِي الْأَرْضِ

wa-yawma yunfakhu fī al-ṣūri fa-faziʿa man fī al-samāwāti wa-man fī al-arḍi

“On the day when al-ṣūr is blown into, those in the heavens and those on earth are terrified.”

In both verses, al-ṣūr accompanies the verb nufikha or yunfakhu.

The expression evokes blowing into something that announces or initiates a great event.

Arabic dictionaries recognize the meaning of a trumpet or horn for blowing.

The verses themselves do not describe its material, dimensions, geometry, or precise construction.

Keeping the name al-ṣūr in the basic translation is therefore appropriate; a note may explain its established sense of a trumpet or horn.

Forms or a trumpet?

Some early lexicographers suggested that al-ṣūr in the resurrection verses is a plural of ṣūrah, meaning beings’ forms.

This reading relates blowing to renewed fashioning or the restoration of life to beings.

Other lexicographers, including Al-Farrāʾ and Abū al-Haytham, rejected this analysis on morphological and reading grounds.

The Qur’an’s established plural of ṣūrah appears in ṣuwarakum with a different vowel pattern.

Al-ṣūr in the blowing verses has a different form.

Its repeated association with blowing, and the singular pronoun referring to it in Al-Zumar 39:68, also fit something blown into.

The trumpet or horn meaning consequently has stronger lexical and contextual support.

This preference does not mean every detail of the hereafter can be known from al-ṣūr.

The verse reports blowing and accompanying events, rather than a physical mechanism in a world whose nature is not fully known to us.

Lisān al-ʿArab: ṣūr, the horn, and disagreement over the plural of ṣūrah

Blowing and different events

Al-Zumar 39:68 states:

وَنُفِخَ فِي الصُّورِ فَصَعِقَ مَنْ فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَمَنْ فِي الْأَرْضِ إِلَّا مَنْ شَاءَ اللَّهُ ثُمَّ نُفِخَ فِيهِ أُخْرَىٰ فَإِذَا هُمْ قِيَامٌ يَنْظُرُونَ

wa-nufikha fī al-ṣūri fa-ṣaʿiqa man fī al-samāwāti wa-man fī al-arḍi illā man shāʾa allāhu thumma nufikha fīhi ukhrā fa-idhā hum qiyāmun yanẓurūna

“Al-ṣūr is blown into, and those in the heavens and on earth fall stricken, except whom God wills. Then it is blown into again, and suddenly they stand looking.”

The verse describes two occurrences of blowing.

The first accompanies beings falling stricken.

It then describes another blowing and their rising.

Ṣūr itself therefore does not name the event; in nafkh fī al-ṣūr, it is what the blowing concerns.

Ṣūr should consequently not be translated directly as “death,” “resurrection,” or “the Last Day.”

Those are events described in the blowing’s context.

A single blast and the day of gathering

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:13 states:

فَإِذَا نُفِخَ فِي الصُّورِ نَفْخَةٌ وَاحِدَةٌ

fa-idhā nufikha fī al-ṣūri nafkhatun wāḥidatun

“When al-ṣūr is blown into with a single blast.”

Al-Nabaʾ 78:18 states:

يَوْمَ يُنْفَخُ فِي الصُّورِ فَتَأْتُونَ أَفْوَاجًا

yawma yunfakhu fī al-ṣūri fa-taʾtūna afwājan

“The day al-ṣūr is blown into, and you come in groups.”

Yā Sīn 36:51 also states:

وَنُفِخَ فِي الصُّورِ فَإِذَا هُمْ مِنَ الْأَجْدَاثِ إِلَىٰ رَبِّهِمْ يَنْسِلُونَ

wa-nufikha fī al-ṣūri fa-idhā hum mina al-ajdāthi ilā rabbihim yansilūna

“Al-ṣūr is blown into, and suddenly they hasten from the graves toward their Lord.”

These verses describe great events connected with blowing.

Nafkhatun wāḥidah in Al-Ḥāqqah does not alone deny every other blowing across the verses, since Al-Zumar 39:68 describes blowing again.

The Qur’an here also does not name the person or angel blowing, the instrument’s material, or the exact interval between blasts.

Such details should not be presented as ṣūr’s lexical meaning merely on the basis of later reports.

Ten occurrences in a semantic network

The ten Qur’anic occurrences of al-ṣūr are found in:

Al-Anʿām 6:73, Al-Kahf 18:99, Ṭā Hā 20:102, Al-Muʾminūn 23:101, Al-Naml 27:87, Yā Sīn 36:51, Al-Zumar 39:68, Qāf 50:20, Al-Ḥāqqah 69:13, and Al-Nabaʾ 78:18.

Across these verses, blowing accompanies terror, falling stricken, gathering, rising from graves, and human groups arriving.

Ṣūr here thus belongs to announcing and initiating great events of resurrection.

The verses support this meaning, but should not be turned into a definite theory about the hereafter’s physical nature or sound transmission there.

8. Fa-ṣurhunna: inclining in Abraham’s story

In Al-Baqarah 2:260, Abraham asks his Lord to show him how He gives life to the dead.

The response states:

فَخُذْ أَرْبَعَةً مِنَ الطَّيْرِ فَصُرْهُنَّ إِلَيْكَ ثُمَّ اجْعَلْ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ جَبَلٍ مِنْهُنَّ جُزْءًا ثُمَّ ادْعُهُنَّ يَأْتِينَكَ سَعْيًا

fa-khudh arbaʿatan mina al-ṭayri fa-ṣurhunna ilayka thumma ijʿal ʿalā kulli jabalin minhunna juzʾan thumma udʿuhunna yaʾtīnaka saʿyan

“Take four birds and incline them toward yourself; then place a portion of them on each mountain. Then call them: they will come hastening to you.”

Al-Baqarah 2:260

The meaning of fa-ṣurhunna

Fa-ṣurhunna is a Form I imperative, and hunna refers to the four birds.

The common reading has ḍammah on ṣād; another recognized reading has kasrah.

Lexicographers report two important meanings:

Inclining, bringing near, or drawing toward oneself; and cutting or dividing into pieces.

The first relates to صُرْتُ الشَّيْءَ إِلَيَّ (ṣurtu al-shayʾa ilayya), inclining something toward oneself.

Ilayka directly specifies the verb’s direction in this reading.

The second has been connected with early Arabic cutting and separating uses.

Some grammarians then connect ilayka with the preceding fa-khudh.

The disagreement therefore concerns both the word’s meaning and how the preposition attaches to a verb.

The role of the readings

Some lexicographers and commentators distinguish meanings according to ṣād’s ḍammah or kasrah.

That division is not consistent across all sources.

Al-Ṭabarī reports Basran grammarians explaining that both forms can concern cutting.

Al-Farrāʾ and others, by contrast, emphasize inclining for one form.

Changing ṣād’s vowel alone therefore does not conclusively determine the meaning.

A final preference requires weighing Arabic usage, sentence structure, ilayka, minhunna juzʾan, and the whole event’s purpose.

Al-Ṭabarī: readings, inclining, cutting, and the attachment of ilayka

The relationship to thumma ijʿal ʿalā kulli jabalin minhunna juzʾan

The command continues:

ثُمَّ اجْعَلْ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ جَبَلٍ مِنْهُنَّ جُزْءًا (thumma ijʿal ʿalā kulli jabalin minhunna juzʾan).

That is, place a portion of them on each mountain.

Juzʾan belongs to J Z ʾ and denotes a portion or share of something.

Early commentaries have treated this phrase as evidence favoring cutting the birds into pieces.

If fa-ṣurhunna instead means bringing near or inclining, the relationship between that stage, placing portions of the birds on mountains, and calling them again requires explanation.

Since the verse aims to show how the dead are given life, that continuity matters and should not be overlooked.

Study of J Z ʾ

A root article should nevertheless not impose unexpressed details—such as the number of pieces, how the birds’ bodies were mixed, or where each part was placed—on the verb’s meaning before studying the verse fully.

Both inclining and cutting have historical lexical support. A basic translation should be selected by weighing all the evidence.

Forqan’s current translation chooses “incline them toward yourself.” This fits an established Arabic meaning and the structure ilayka. How the sign of reviving the dead is realized should nevertheless be examined in a separate reflection on Al-Baqarah 2:260.

9. Distinguishing related roots

Ṣ W R (ṣūrah) and SH K L (shakl)

Arabic shakl concerns configuration and resemblance.

Ṣūrah also denotes beings’ shapes and configurations.

Their uses are often close, but their entire semantic histories are not identical.

The Qur’an expresses fashioning through ṣawwara, while established shakl usage also has a field of similarity and resemblance.

Shakl may naturally explain ṣūrah in some sentences, but all Ṣ W R branches, including al-ṣūr and fa-ṣurhunna, should not be subsumed under shape.

Ṣ W R (ṣawwara) and KH L Q (khalaqa)

Qur’anic khalq concerns creating and bringing into existence.

Ṣawwara concerns giving form and configuration.

These verbs appear together in Al-Aʿrāf 7:11 and other verses.

Their distinction is real, but should not become a universal, exceptionless biological sequence.

Fashioning may be an aspect of creation without ṣūrah itself encompassing khalq’s whole meaning.

Study of KH L Q

Ṣ W R (ṣawwara) and S W Y (sawwā)

Qur’anic taswiyah concerns ordering, proportioning, and establishing an appropriate condition.

Taṣwīr concerns giving form and configuration.

The concepts accompany one another in Al-Infiṭār 82:7 and Al-Infiṭār 82:8.

A being may be structurally ordered while its particular form is also discussed.

Ṣūrah and taswiyah nevertheless differ. Good proportion or structural quality should not be derived from Ṣ W R alone without evidence.

Study of S W Y

Ṣ W R (ṣūrah) and M TH L (timthāl)

Timthāl concerns a figure or representation of something’s shape.

Ṣūrah may be a being’s actual configuration, whereas a timthāl is normally a made object or representation resembling something.

Sabaʾ 34:13 lists tamāthīl among the works made for Solomon.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:52 describes Abraham’s people’s figures as tamāthīl.

Representing a shape and making a figure an object of worship are therefore distinct questions.

A living person’s form should likewise not be completely equated with a statue made of them.

Study of M TH L

Ṣ W R (ṣawr) and M Y L (mayl)

Arabic’s ṣawar branch concerns inclining and causing inclination.

It is close to M Y L, but the roots are not identical in form or word history.

One established reading of fa-ṣurhunna ilayka in Al-Baqarah 2:260 concerns inclining the birds toward Abraham.

That meaning should not be transferred to ṣawwara’s uses for fashioning humans.

Ṣ W R (ṣawr) and Ṣ Y R (ṣāra)

Arabic Ṣ Y R concerns becoming, reaching another condition, and coming to an outcome.

Qur’anic مَصِير (maṣīr) belongs to that family.

Al-Taghābun 64:3 contains both صَوَّرَكُمْ (ṣawwarakum) and الْمَصِيرُ (al-maṣīru).

Their proximity matters: despite similar-looking letters, two distinct roots occur in one sentence.

Ṣawwarakum concerns fashioning, while al-maṣīr concerns return and outcome.

Phonetic resemblance should not cause them to be treated as one root or their meanings to be transferred between them.

Study of Ṣ Y R

11. Conclusion

Arabic Ṣ W R is a family with several branches. Ibn Fāris explicitly states that not all its uses can be explained from one semantic core.

One important branch concerns something’s form and configuration. The verb of fashioning and the description al-muṣawwir belong to this field.

The Qur’an uses this branch for fashioning humans in the womb, creation and the ordering of human shape, and fashioning as a divine description.

Another branch concerns inclining or causing inclination. Fa-ṣurhunna ilayka in Al-Baqarah 2:260 can be studied in that connection, although early sources also report cutting into pieces.

Al-ṣūr occurs in ten verses about resurrection. Its established meaning is a trumpet or horn for blowing, but the word alone does not reveal its nature or mechanism in the hereafter’s events.

Other recorded Arabic uses concern herds of wild cattle, groups of palms, and musk’s container or scent. Their historical relationships with fashioning or inclining have not all been established.

The root’s definition should therefore neither be restricted to shaping nor derive every branch from an unsupported shared image.

Brief root meaning for Forqan:

Form, shape, and configuration; giving form and fashioning. In another branch, inclining or causing inclination; and in the independent use al-ṣūr, a trumpet or horn for blowing.

Each derivative’s meaning should be determined from its form, established Arabic usage, and the particular verse’s context. Shared roots alone do not establish every branch’s semantic or historical unity.
