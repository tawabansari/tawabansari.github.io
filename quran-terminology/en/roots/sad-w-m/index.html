1. Introducing the root and its semantic field

The three-letter Arabic root Ṣ W M concerns abstaining, ceasing an activity, and the stopping of movement or conduct. In human usage, a person refrains from something they could do, such as eating, drinking, speaking, or moving. In descriptions of nature and animals, the family can likewise express stopping, stillness, and the cessation of movement.

Ṣawm and ṣiyām are both verbal nouns of ṣāma. Both concern abstention or withholding in Arabic; what is withheld must be identified from the sentence and its context.

When food and drink are at issue, ṣawm concerns refraining from eating and drinking. When speech is at issue, it concerns refraining from speaking. Applied to a stationary horse or a wind that has ceased, it emphasizes stopping and stillness.

The root occurs fourteen times in the Qur’an in five morphological groups: twice as a Form I verb, once as the verbal noun ṣawm, nine times as the verbal noun ṣiyām, and twice as the plural active participles al-ṣāʾimīn and al-ṣāʾimāt.

Qur’anic counts and forms: Quranic Arabic Corpus

Two Qur’anic uses are especially significant. In Maryam 19:26, ṣawman in Mary’s words accompanies abstention from conversation. In Al-Baqarah 2:183; Al-Baqarah 2:184; Al-Baqarah 2:185; Al-Baqarah 2:186; Al-Baqarah 2:187, ṣiyām is an act of worship whose time limits, specified abstentions, and purpose are stated in the verses.

The root’s general meaning must therefore be distinguished from the particular form of fasting in the Qur’an. Qur’anic fasting is a kind of abstention, but not every abstention in Arabic is the prescribed fast governed by Al-Baqarah.

2. The semantic core and important forms

Abstaining and coming to a stop

Ibn Fāris writes of this root in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

الصَّادُ وَالْوَاوُ وَالْمِيمُ أَصْلٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى إِمْسَاكٍ وَرُكُودٍ فِي مَكَانٍ

al-ṣādu wa-al-wāwu wa-al-mīmu aṣlun yadullu ʿalā imsākin wa-rukūdin fī makānin

“Ṣād, wāw, and mīm indicate a root meaning of withholding and remaining still in a place.”

Two terms in this definition matter: imsāk and rukūd.

Imsāk here means abstaining or withholding: an activity is not performed, or movement is stopped.

Rukūd concerns stillness and standing, like a wind that ceases blowing or an animal standing in place.

For humans, abstention can be conscious and deliberate. Human intention and decision should not, however, be attributed to natural phenomena when the word describes wind or the sun.

This distinction shows that the root’s meaning extends beyond deliberate human abstention.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: Ṣ W M

Important Arabic forms

Ṣāma yaṣūmu (صَامَ يَصُومُ) is a Form I verb. Depending on its subject, it can mean abstaining, ceasing an activity, or remaining stationary. This verb occurs in the Qur’an.

Ṣawm (صَوْم) is a Form I verbal noun meaning abstention or withholding. It occurs once in the Qur’an, in Mary’s words.

Ṣiyām (صِيَام) is another verbal noun of the same verb. Its Qur’anic usage concerns devotional fasting and its associated abstentions.

Ṣāʾim (صَائِم) is an active participle describing someone fasting or abstaining. Its masculine and feminine plural forms occur in the Qur’an.

Ṣāʾimah (صَائِمَة) is the feminine active participle. Al-ṣāʾimāt in Al-Aḥzāb 33:35 describes women who fast.

Ṣuwwām (صُوَّام), ṣuyyām (صُيَّام), and ṣuwwam (صُوَّم) are recognized Arabic plurals for people fasting or abstaining, but these forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

As a description, ṣawwām (صَوَّام) denotes someone who fasts frequently or habitually.

Maṣām (مَصَام) and maṣāmah (مَصَامَة) denote a place of standing or stopping in early Arabic, particularly for horses.

Lexical sources also record iṣṭāma (اِصْطَامَ) as a form in this family meaning to abstain.

Al-Muḥkam: lexical forms of ṣawm

These forms show ṣawm as a general Arabic designation for abstention, while religious context can make it the name of a particular act of worship.

A physical image: movement stopping

To picture the semantic field, imagine a horse standing in a field, its movement stopped. It can move, but is presently neither running nor leaving its place.

In this image, being ṣāʾim concerns stopping and standing still.

Another image is a wind that blows and then subsides. Moving air becomes still.

In human usage, the field applies to conduct: someone able to speak decides to remain silent, or someone with access to food and drink refrains for a specified period.

“Resisting desire,” “experiencing hunger,” or “moral self-cultivation” should not replace ṣawm’s fundamental lexical meaning. They may be motives, difficulties, or results of particular abstentions, but the root does not express them in every use.

3. Early Arabic usage

Stationary horses: a witness attributed to al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī reads:

خَيْلٌ صِيَامٌ وَخَيْلٌ غَيْرُ صَائِمَةٍ
تَحْتَ الْعَجَاجِ وَخَيْلٌ تَعْلُكُ اللُّجُمَا

khaylun ṣiyāmun wa-khaylun ghayru ṣāʾimatin / taḥta al-ʿajāji wa-khaylun taʿluku al-lujumā

“Some horses stand still and others do not, amid the dust; and some horses chew their bridles.”

Ṣiyām describes horses here and can be understood as the plural of ṣāʾim. This adjectival use must be distinguished from the verbal noun ṣiyām in the fasting verses.

The horses are not performing a religious fast. The line depicts their stopping and standing in contrast with other horses’ movement.

Ghayru ṣāʾimatin, “not standing still,” makes the contrast clear. The dust also fits a scene of horses in motion.

The witness shows that the Ṣ W M family in early Arabic extended beyond abstention from food to stopping and ceasing movement. Lexical sources also explain the horse’s description as standing without grazing.

Al-Mubarrid’s al-Kāmil: the poetic reading with wa-khaylun

The sun’s apparent standstill at midday

A verse attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays includes:

إِذَا صَامَ النَّهَارُ وَهَجَّرَا

idhā ṣāma al-nahāru wa-hajjarā

“When the day reaches its midpoint and the midday heat spreads.”

Ṣāma al-nahāru describes the day at its midpoint: to an observer, the sun’s movement high in the sky seems slow or stationary.

This does not assert an actual cosmic halt of the sun. It describes the situation perceived in ordinary human experience at midday.

Lexical sources explain this usage with expressions such as ṣāma al-nahāru and ṣāmati al-shamsu.

This witness likewise connects ṣawm with stopping and stillness, without necessarily concerning food or worship.

Lisān al-ʿArab: the Imruʾ al-Qays attribution and stillness usages

A wind that has subsided

Recorded Arabic usage gives ṣāmati al-rīḥu (صَامَتِ الرِّيحُ) for a wind ceasing to blow.

The wind does not eat or deliberately refrain; its movement simply subsides or stops.

Defining the root as “conscious abstention from food and drink” therefore cannot account for every lexical use.

The broader meaning of ceasing and abstaining suits this family better; the sentence’s subject determines the particular abstention.

Refraining from speech

Arabic can also call someone who refrains from speaking ṣāʾim.

Mary’s story provides a Qur’anic example, but does not establish silence as ṣawm’s sole lexical meaning.

Silence is one instance of abstention, as not eating, not drinking, or stopping movement can be others.

Refraining from speech also differs from inability to speak. Someone who has lost speech or cannot speak is in a different situation from someone able to speak who chooses not to.

This abstention from conversation matters in the usage concerning Mary’s vow.

4. Ṣawman in Mary’s story

A significant use for understanding the root’s range appears in Maryam.

After Jesus’s birth, Mary is told:

فَكُلِي وَاشْرَبِي وَقَرِّي عَيْنًا ۖ فَإِمَّا تَرَيِنَّ مِنَ الْبَشَرِ أَحَدًا فَقُولِي إِنِّي نَذَرْتُ لِلرَّحْمَٰنِ صَوْمًا فَلَنْ أُكَلِّمَ الْيَوْمَ إِنْسِيًّا

fa-kulī wa-shrabī wa-qarrī ʿaynan; fa-immā tarayinna mina al-bashari aḥadan fa-qūlī innī nadhartu li-l-raḥmāni ṣawman fa-lan ukallima al-yawma insiyyan

“So eat and drink, and let your eyes be comforted. If you see any human being, say: I have vowed a ṣawm to the Entirely Merciful; so today I will not speak to any human being.”

Maryam 19:26

Ṣawman is the object of nadhartu, “I vowed,” and denotes what Mary has vowed to the Entirely Merciful.

The final clause identifies the abstention: fa-lan ukallima al-yawma insiyyan, “so today I will not speak to any human being.”

Mary’s ṣawm in this context therefore concerns refraining from conversation with people.

Does ṣawm here mean enduring hunger?

No. The verse itself begins fa-kulī wa-shrabī: Mary is told to eat and drink.

It then mentions her ṣawm and describes refraining from conversation.

This sequence prevents ṣawm here from being restricted to abstention from food and drink.

Nor does this use make silence ṣawm’s sole meaning throughout Arabic. Silence is its particular instance in Mary’s story, just as abstention from food and drink is a particular instance in the fasting verses of Al-Baqarah.

Fa-lan ukallima al-yawma insiyyan thus explains Mary’s particular abstention, without defining Ṣ W M exclusively.

Vowing and abstaining

Ṣawm also accompanies nadhartu in this verse.

Nadhr belongs to N DH R and concerns a commitment a person undertakes. Mary speaks here of a commitment to the Entirely Merciful.

Study of N DH R

This connection shows that her silence is neither accidental nor caused by inability to speak. It is abstention within a vow.

Ṣawm itself does not mean making a vow. Nadhr identifies the commitment; ṣawm identifies the act that is its subject.

Mary’s abstention from conversation must not become a general rule for all believers without an independent command. The Qur’an here recounts a particular event in her life.

Does fa-qūlī conflict with refraining from speech?

The verse first says fa-qūlī, “say,” then quotes Mary’s statement about not speaking.

At first, this can prompt a question: how is someone commanded to say that she will not speak?

In the sentence’s structure, fa-qūlī commands an announcement of the situation or commitment, while fa-lan ukallima al-yawma insiyyan states the abstention from conversation.

Announcing that one will not enter a conversation and refraining from further conversation are distinct matters. The precise manner of that announcement, including possible nonverbal expression, belongs in a reflection devoted to this verse.

For the root study, the clear point is that ṣawm concerns refraining from conversation, rather than inability to speak or abstention from food.

5. Ṣawm and ṣiyām in the Qur’an

An important question about the root concerns the difference between its two verbal nouns, ṣawm and ṣiyām.

Ṣawm occurs once, in Mary’s story; ṣiyām occurs nine times in verses concerning devotional fasting.

This distribution is real, but does it prove that the words have wholly separate lexical meanings?

Two verbal nouns of one verb

Both ṣawm and ṣiyām are verbal nouns of ṣāma yaṣūmu in Arabic.

Lisān al-ʿArab records both for the same verb, mentioning abstention from food, drink, intercourse, and speech within their general semantic field.

Lisān al-ʿArab: ṣawm and ṣiyām

The underlying Arabic usage therefore does not establish that ṣawm means only silence and ṣiyām only not eating.

The limited Qur’anic distribution alone cannot establish such a division.

In Maryam 19:26, abstention from conversation is identified by fa-lan ukallima al-yawma insiyyan.

In Al-Baqarah 2:187, abstention from eating, drinking, and intercourse during fasting hours is identified by that verse’s commands and limits.

The different instances thus emerge from context.

Does the fiʿāl pattern make the meaning more intense?

Ṣiyām has a different verbal-noun pattern from ṣawm.

The fiʿāl pattern alone, however, does not establish that ṣiyām necessarily involves more intense, longer, or harder abstention than ṣawm.

An Arabic verb may have more than one verbal noun without a fixed, independent semantic boundary between them in every use.

Nor do two verbal nouns necessarily assign one to bodily abstention and the other to spiritual abstention.

The Qur’an shows different uses, but that distribution is insufficient to establish an exclusive lexical rule.

The root study should therefore keep both verbal nouns in one semantic family and explain each particular instance in its verse’s context.

6. Ṣiyām as Qur’anic worship

A. The purpose of fasting: taqwā

Al-Baqarah states:

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا كُتِبَ عَلَيْكُمُ الصِّيَامُ كَمَا كُتِبَ عَلَى الَّذِينَ مِنْ قَبْلِكُمْ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَتَّقُونَ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū kutiba ʿalaykumu al-ṣiyāmu kamā kutiba ʿalā alladhīna min qablikum laʿallakum tattaqūna

“O you who believe, fasting has been prescribed for you, as it was prescribed for those before you, so that you may practice taqwā.”

Al-Baqarah 2:183

Al-ṣiyāmu is the subject matter of the prescription, while kutiba ʿalaykumu states that it is prescribed for the audience.

The verse also declares that this worship was prescribed for people before the Qur’an’s audience.

Kamā kutiba ʿalā alladhīna min qablikum does not, however, establish that every timing and practical detail of earlier groups’ fasting matched Al-Baqarah’s prescriptions.

What is explicit is that ṣiyām was prescribed for them.

Laʿallakum tattaqūn: the purpose of fasting

The verse ends by identifying the purpose: laʿallakum tattaqūn.

Taqwā belongs to W Q Y and concerns heedfulness, care, and guarding oneself against harm and wrongful consequences.

This purpose must remain distinct from ṣawm’s lexical meaning.

Ṣawm fundamentally means abstention; taqwā is the direction and purpose the verse gives this worship.

Someone might abstain from food for a period while lying, wronging others, or neglecting responsibilities.

Bodily abstention alone does not establish the achievement of taqwā.

Conversely, identifying taqwā as the purpose does not make actual abstention from food and drink unimportant, or permit any ethical conduct to replace it.

The Qur’an prescribes the particular act of ṣiyām and also states its purpose.

Ṣiyām is the devotional act; taqwā is its formative and ethical purpose. They are connected without being synonyms.

Verse 183 identifies taqwā as the purpose. Feeling the hunger of the needy or experiencing others’ difficulties may be further effects, but they do not replace the verse’s explicit purpose.

B. Number of days, illness, travel, and capacity to fast

The next verse reads:

أَيَّامًا مَعْدُودَاتٍ ۚ فَمَنْ كَانَ مِنْكُمْ مَرِيضًا أَوْ عَلَىٰ سَفَرٍ فَعِدَّةٌ مِنْ أَيَّامٍ أُخَرَ ۚ وَعَلَى الَّذِينَ يُطِيقُونَهُ فِدْيَةٌ طَعَامُ مِسْكِينٍ ۖ فَمَنْ تَطَوَّعَ خَيْرًا فَهُوَ خَيْرٌ لَهُ ۚ وَأَنْ تَصُومُوا خَيْرٌ لَكُمْ ۖ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ تَعْلَمُونَ

ayyāman maʿdūdātin; fa-man kāna minkum marīḍan aw ʿalā safarin fa-ʿiddatun min ayyāmin ukhara; wa-ʿalā alladhīna yuṭīqūnahu fidyatun ṭaʿāmu miskīnin; fa-man taṭawwaʿa khayran fa-huwa khayrun lahu; wa-an taṣūmū khayrun lakum in kuntum taʿlamūna

“A counted number of days. Whoever among you is ill or traveling shall make up the number on other days. For those who can bear it only with difficulty, there is a ransom: feeding a needy person. Whoever voluntarily does more good, it is better for them; and that you fast is better for you, if you know.”

Al-Baqarah 2:184

This verse addresses several matters: the duration of fasting, illness and travel, capacity and endurance, a compensatory ransom, and the value of fasting for someone able to perform it.

Wa-an taṣūmū khayrun lakum bears directly on the study of Ṣ W M.

Taṣūmū is a Form I imperfect verb in the same family as ṣiyām and ṣawm.

Its presence shows that the Qur’an also uses the verb ṣāma itself to express fasting, rather than referring to the worship only through the noun ṣiyām.

The verse also treats fasting in relation to people’s actual circumstances.

Illness and travel accompany making up days later. Yuṭīqūnahu also accompanies a ransom of feeding a needy person; relating it to extreme difficulty is the interpretation selected in this article’s translation, rather than its only undisputed lexical meaning.

Ordinary difficulty in worship should be distinguished from an enduring inability to bear it. A sick person or traveler able to make up days later differs from someone lacking an enduring capacity to fast.

The details require independent study of yuṭīqūnahu, fidyah, and the connected verses. Ṣ W M’s root meaning alone cannot settle every detail of the prescription.

Al-Qurṭubī: differing interpretations of yuṭīqūnahu

Wa-an taṣūmū khayrun lakum belongs within this same context. The verse affirms fasting’s value without turning it into a command to impose fasting on someone genuinely incapable.

C. Ramadan and the principle of ease

The next verse states:

شَهْرُ رَمَضَانَ الَّذِي أُنْزِلَ فِيهِ الْقُرْآنُ هُدًى لِلنَّاسِ وَبَيِّنَاتٍ مِنَ الْهُدَىٰ وَالْفُرْقَانِ ۚ فَمَنْ شَهِدَ مِنْكُمُ الشَّهْرَ فَلْيَصُمْهُ ۖ وَمَنْ كَانَ مَرِيضًا أَوْ عَلَىٰ سَفَرٍ فَعِدَّةٌ مِنْ أَيَّامٍ أُخَرَ ۗ يُرِيدُ اللَّهُ بِكُمُ الْيُسْرَ وَلَا يُرِيدُ بِكُمُ الْعُسْرَ وَلِتُكْمِلُوا الْعِدَّةَ وَلِتُكَبِّرُوا اللَّهَ عَلَىٰ مَا هَدَاكُمْ وَلَعَلَّكُمْ تَشْكُرُونَ

shahru ramaḍāna alladhī unzila fīhi al-qurʾānu hudan li-l-nāsi wa-bayyinātin mina al-hudā wa-al-furqāni; fa-man shahida minkumu al-shahra fa-l-yaṣumhu; wa-man kāna marīḍan aw ʿalā safarin fa-ʿiddatun min ayyāmin ukhara; yurīdu Allāhu bikumu al-yusra wa-lā yurīdu bikumu al-ʿusra; wa-li-tukmilū al-ʿiddata wa-li-tukabbirū Allāha ʿalā mā hadākum wa-laʿallakum tashkurūna

“The month of Ramadan, in which the Qur’an was sent down as guidance for people and clear proofs of guidance and discernment. Whoever among you is present for the month shall fast it; whoever is ill or traveling shall make up the number on other days. The God intends ease for you and does not intend hardship for you, so that you complete the number and magnify The God for guiding you, and so that you may give thanks.”

Al-Baqarah 2:185

Fa-l-yaṣumhu is an imperfect verb made jussive by the lām of command.

The pronoun -hu refers to Ramadan. Fasting is here connected with a particular month.

Ṣāma’s lexical meaning has not changed: the verb expresses abstention within fasting, and Ramadan is its prescribed time.

The verse again mentions illness and travel, and expresses the principle of ease: yurīdu Allāhu bikumu al-yusra wa-lā yurīdu bikumu al-ʿusra.

This principle belongs within the discussion of fasting, since it appears in the verse prescribing it.

Ease does not abolish the worship itself. The Qur’an connects prescribed fasting, human capacity, the possibility of making up days, and avoidance of undue hardship.

Fasting is prescribed worship here, but hardship and harm are not identified as its purpose.

D. The time limits and practical limits of fasting

Al-Baqarah 2:187 is central for the practical details:

أُحِلَّ لَكُمْ لَيْلَةَ الصِّيَامِ الرَّفَثُ إِلَىٰ نِسَائِكُمْ ۚ هُنَّ لِبَاسٌ لَكُمْ وَأَنْتُمْ لِبَاسٌ لَهُنَّ ۗ عَلِمَ اللَّهُ أَنَّكُمْ كُنْتُمْ تَخْتَانُونَ أَنْفُسَكُمْ فَتَابَ عَلَيْكُمْ وَعَفَا عَنْكُمْ ۖ فَالْآنَ بَاشِرُوهُنَّ وَابْتَغُوا مَا كَتَبَ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ ۚ وَكُلُوا وَاشْرَبُوا حَتَّىٰ يَتَبَيَّنَ لَكُمُ الْخَيْطُ الْأَبْيَضُ مِنَ الْخَيْطِ الْأَسْوَدِ مِنَ الْفَجْرِ ۖ ثُمَّ أَتِمُّوا الصِّيَامَ إِلَى اللَّيْلِ ۚ وَلَا تُبَاشِرُوهُنَّ وَأَنْتُمْ عَاكِفُونَ فِي الْمَسَاجِدِ ۗ تِلْكَ حُدُودُ اللَّهِ فَلَا تَقْرَبُوهَا ۗ كَذَٰلِكَ يُبَيِّنُ اللَّهُ آيَاتِهِ لِلنَّاسِ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَتَّقُونَ

uḥilla lakum laylata al-ṣiyāmi al-rafathu ilā nisāʾikum; hunna libāsun lakum wa-antum libāsun lahunna; ʿalima Allāhu annakum kuntum takhtānūna anfusakum fa-tāba ʿalaykum wa-ʿafā ʿankum; fa-l-āna bāshirūhunna wa-btaghū mā kataba Allāhu lakum; wa-kulū wa-shrabū ḥattā yatabayyana lakumu al-khayṭu al-abyaḍu mina al-khayṭi al-aswadi mina al-fajri; thumma atimmū al-ṣiyāma ilā al-layli; wa-lā tubāshirūhunna wa-antum ʿākifūna fī al-masājidi; tilka ḥudūdu Allāhi fa-lā taqrabūhā; kadhālika yubayyinu Allāhu āyātihi li-l-nāsi laʿallahum yattaqūna

“Intercourse with your wives during the night of fasting has been made lawful for you. They are a garment for you, and you are a garment for them. The God knew that you were betraying yourselves, so He turned toward you and pardoned you. Now have relations with them and seek what The God has prescribed for you. Eat and drink until the white thread of dawn becomes distinct to you from the black thread; then complete the fast until night. Do not have relations with them while you remain in devotional retreat in the mosques. These are The God’s limits, so do not approach them. Thus The God makes His signs clear to people, so that they may practice taqwā.”

Al-Baqarah 2:187

The verbal noun al-ṣiyām occurs twice in this verse.

First, laylata al-ṣiyāmi denotes the night associated with fasting.

Second, thumma atimmū al-ṣiyāma ilā al-layli commands completing the fast until night arrives.

Eating, drinking, and intercourse

The verse permits eating and drinking until dawn becomes apparent, then prescribes completing the fast until night.

It also permits intercourse at night during the fasting period and gives a separate restriction on intercourse during devotional retreat in mosques.

The prescribed fast in Al-Baqarah thus involves abstaining from eating, drinking, and intercourse during the fasting interval.

These limits are identified from the verse rather than Ṣ W M’s root meaning alone.

Iʿtikāf and ṣiyām are separate words. The prohibition of intercourse during retreat is related to fasting’s limits, but all rules of retreat should not be built into the meaning of ṣiyām.

The beginning and end of fasting

The abstention interval begins with dawn becoming apparent and ends at ilā al-layli.

As a translation, ilā al-layli means “until night.”

Identifying the precise signs of night’s beginning and applying them across geographical conditions belongs to studies of F J R, L Y L, and the verses concerning time.

Study of F J R; Study of L Y L

These questions should not replace the meaning of the verb “to fast” within the Ṣ W M study.

The clear point is that the Qur’an presents this fast with specified temporal and practical limits. Abstaining from food at any chosen hour, without attending to the verse’s structure, does not by itself constitute the prescribed Ramadan fast.

Abstention and taqwā

The opening of the fasting passage gives the purpose as laʿallakum tattaqūn.

Al-Baqarah 2:187 also ends with a reference to taqwā, as laʿallahum yattaqūn (لَعَلَّهُمْ يَتَّقُونَ). Its pronoun and verb form differ from laʿallakum tattaqūn (لَعَلَّكُمْ تَتَّقُونَ) in Al-Baqarah 2:183.

This recurrence matters: the fasting prescriptions stand between two references to taqwā.

In this framework, taqwā extends beyond enduring hunger or thirst. Respecting divine limits, attending to actual human circumstances, honoring responsibilities, and refraining from transgression also relate to the worship’s purpose.

This conclusion comes from the verses together. Ṣawm’s lexical meaning remains abstaining or withholding.

7. Ṣiyām in expiation and compensating for obligations

Ṣiyām in the Qur’an extends beyond Ramadan fasting. Several verses also place fasting within fidyah or expiation.

These cases show that the type and duration of fasting are not always the same. Sometimes three days are prescribed, sometimes two consecutive months, and sometimes fasting appears alongside other options.

Fasting in pilgrimage: Al-Baqarah 2:196

Al-Baqarah 2:196 discusses fasting in two different settings.

First, in the situation concerning shaving the head because of illness or an affliction of the head, it gives a compensation of fasting, charity, or a sacrificial rite.

Second, for someone in the stated pilgrimage situation who cannot obtain the required offering, it prescribes three fasting days during pilgrimage and seven after returning.

Ṣiyām here appears both alongside other compensatory options and with a specified number of days.

The amount of fasting therefore does not come from the verbal noun ṣiyām itself. Other expressions in the verse determine its duration and condition.

Fasting after an accidental killing: Al-Nisāʾ 4:92

After describing obligations arising from accidental killing, Al-Nisāʾ 4:92 mentions two consecutive months of fasting for someone unable to obtain the freeing of a person in bondage.

Shahrayni mutatābiʿayni identifies the duration and continuity.

Ṣiyām itself does not mean two months. The dual shahrayni supplies the two-month duration; mutatābiʿayni supplies continuity.

The verse concerns discharging responsibility arising from accidental killing, but that subject is not part of Ṣ W M’s root meaning.

Fasting as expiation for an oath: Al-Māʾidah 5:89

After describing expiation for an oath, Al-Māʾidah 5:89 gives three days of fasting for someone unable to perform the preceding options.

This again shows fasting prescribed within the discharge of an obligation.

It does not establish that fasting is always merely punishment or a penalty: Al-Baqarah 2:183 prescribes ṣiyām as worship directed toward taqwā.

Fasting in the matter of hunting: Al-Māʾidah 5:95

Al-Māʾidah 5:95 includes fasting among compensatory options concerning hunting while in iḥrām.

Aw ʿadlu dhālika ṣiyāman connects the amount of fasting with an equivalent assessment of what was previously stated.

Fasting here has no fixed number independent of the matter prescribed; its amount relates to the full structure of the verse.

Fasting in the matter of ẓihār: Al-Mujādilah 58:4

Al-Mujādilah first describes obligations for those who pronounce ẓihār and then go back on what they said. For someone unable to free a person in bondage, it prescribes two consecutive months of fasting before sexual contact.

Ṣiyām again accompanies a duration, continuity, and a temporal condition.

These conditions do not arise from the verbal noun itself; they are separate components of the prescription.

What the compensatory uses show

These five settings show fasting within prescriptions for compensation and expiation as well as Ramadan worship.

They should not all be collapsed into a single prescription.

Sometimes ṣiyām is one of several options; sometimes it follows inability to perform a preceding option; sometimes its number of days or months is explicit.

Its basic meaning in these cases remains fasting. The practical and prescriptive differences emerge from each verse’s conditions and context.

8. Those who fast: al-ṣāʾimīn wa-al-ṣāʾimāt

Al-Aḥzāb states:

إِنَّ الْمُسْلِمِينَ وَالْمُسْلِمَاتِ وَالْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَالْمُؤْمِنَاتِ وَالْقَانِتِينَ وَالْقَانِتَاتِ وَالصَّادِقِينَ وَالصَّادِقَاتِ وَالصَّابِرِينَ وَالصَّابِرَاتِ وَالْخَاشِعِينَ وَالْخَاشِعَاتِ وَالْمُتَصَدِّقِينَ وَالْمُتَصَدِّقَاتِ وَالصَّائِمِينَ وَالصَّائِمَاتِ وَالْحَافِظِينَ فُرُوجَهُمْ وَالْحَافِظَاتِ وَالذَّاكِرِينَ اللَّهَ كَثِيرًا وَالذَّاكِرَاتِ أَعَدَّ اللَّهُ لَهُمْ مَغْفِرَةً وَأَجْرًا عَظِيمًا

inna al-muslimīna wa-al-muslimāti wa-al-muʾminīna wa-al-muʾmināti wa-al-qānitīna wa-al-qānitāti wa-al-ṣādiqīna wa-al-ṣādiqāti wa-al-ṣābirīna wa-al-ṣābirāti wa-al-khāshiʿīna wa-al-khāshiʿāti wa-al-mutaṣaddiqīna wa-al-mutaṣaddiqāti wa-al-ṣāʾimīna wa-al-ṣāʾimāti wa-al-ḥāfiẓīna furūjahum wa-al-ḥāfiẓāti wa-al-dhākirīna Allāha kathīran wa-al-dhākirāti aʿadda Allāhu lahum maghfiratan wa-ajran ʿaẓīman

“Indeed, men and women who submit, men and women who believe, men and women who are obedient, truthful men and women, patient men and women, humble men and women, charitable men and women, men and women who fast, men and women who guard their private parts, and men and women who remember The God often: The God has prepared forgiveness and a great reward for them.”

Al-Aḥzāb 33:35

Al-ṣāʾimīn and al-ṣāʾimāt are masculine and feminine plural active participles.

The Qur’an places fasting alongside other qualities: faith, obedience, truthfulness, patience, humility, charity, guarding sexual boundaries, and remembrance of The God.

Fasting is thus one commended human quality in this context, rather than the sole criterion for identifying a righteous person.

Each quality also has its independent meaning.

Al-ṣāʾimīn does not mean al-ṣābirīn, “the patient,” although fasting can involve patience.

Al-ṣāʾimāt likewise does not mean al-ḥāfiẓāt, “those who guard,” although guarding sexual boundaries and respecting limits are connected in a believer’s life.

Men and women are both described as fasting. The masculine and feminine forms make no difference to the basic meaning of the act.

Nor do these active participles imply fasting at every moment of life. They can describe people for whom fasting is a recognized practice or quality.

9. Distinctions from related roots

Ṣ W M (ṣawm) and M S K (imsāk)

Study of M S K

Imsāk belongs to M S K and concerns grasping, holding, withholding, and preventing something from being released.

Ṣawm approaches imsāk within the field of abstention.

Imsāk can also concern holding an object, restraining someone, or retaining something in one’s possession.

In conduct, ṣawm emphasizes refraining from a particular activity.

Someone fasting may exercise imsāk from eating and drinking, but this proximity does not make every Qur’anic occurrence of amsaka a reference to fasting.

Likewise, early uses of ṣawm for wind or the sun concern stopping and stillness; its whole range cannot be restricted to holding something in one’s hand.

Ṣ W M (ṣawm) and Ṣ M T (ṣamt)

Study of Ṣ M T

Ṣamt denotes silence and refraining from speech.

One use of ṣawm can express this abstention, but its field is wider.

Maryam 19:26 associates ṣawm with not speaking, while early usages for stationary horses and a subsided wind show that the root itself extends beyond verbal silence.

Silence can therefore be an instance of ṣawm, but not every instance of ṣawm is silence.

Ṣ W M (ṣawm) and S K T (sukūt)

Study of S K T

Sukūt likewise concerns falling silent or not speaking.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:154 uses sakata for Moses’s anger subsiding. The context concerns the quieting of a state previously aroused.

Sukūt can thus also extend beyond the silence of the tongue.

Nevertheless, sakata and ṣāma belong to separate roots. Sukūt approaches falling or remaining silent, while ṣawm has the broader field of abstaining and ceasing.

The fields approach each other in Mary’s story, without making all uses of the roots equivalent.

Ṣ W M (ṣawm) and K F F (kaff)

Study of K F F

In verbal usage, kaff concerns restraining, holding back, and preventing an activity.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:77 mentions people who had been told to hold back their hands.

This can express refraining from action or ceasing a particular conduct.

Ṣawm is likewise a kind of abstention, but also names devotional fasting in Qur’anic usage.

Every ethical or practical restraint should therefore not be called ṣiyām without supporting context.

Someone may refrain from violence or lying, but that act alone is not the prescribed fast with the timing and limits of Al-Baqarah 2:187.

Ṣ W M (ṣawm) and T R K (tark)

Study of T R K

Tark concerns leaving, relinquishing something, or ceasing an activity.

Ṣawm can also involve leaving off an activity, such as not eating or not speaking.

Tark need not be temporary or have a specified duration.

Someone may leave a place, abandon a habit, or relinquish a responsibility entirely. Such uses need not involve temporary abstention.

Devotional ṣiyām in the Qur’an accompanies prescribed timing and limits.

The general meaning of leaving off should therefore not replace the particular meaning of fasting in its prescribed context.

Ṣ W M (ṣawm) and Ṣ B R (ṣabr)

Study of Ṣ B R

Ṣabr’s lexical field concerns restraining oneself and remaining steadfast.

Fasting may require ṣabr, since it can involve refraining from something a person desires and is able to do.

The roots nevertheless remain distinct.

Ṣabr may concern hardship, fulfilling a responsibility, or maintaining a position. Arabic can even describe persistence in wrongdoing with the verb ṣabara.

Devotional ṣiyām in the Qur’an is a particular abstention with specified timing and limits.

Fasting can require patience, but not every patience is fasting; nor should every fast be reduced to enduring suffering.

Ṣ W M (ṣawm) and W Q Y (wiqāyah)

Study of W Q Y

This comparison matters for Al-Baqarah 2:183.

Wiqāyah concerns protection and guarding against harm; taqwā belongs to this family.

Ṣawm concerns abstaining and ceasing.

The verse connects means and purpose: fasting is prescribed so that people may attain taqwā.

Ṣiyām therefore cannot be translated as “taqwā,” just as taqwā cannot be translated as “hunger” or “abstention from food.”

Abstention may provide a setting for taqwā, but its ethical value relates to purpose, limits, and the person’s conduct.

10. Morphological and structural notes

The two verbal nouns ṣawm and ṣiyām

Ṣawm and ṣiyām are both verbal nouns of ṣāma.

Two verbal-noun forms alone do not establish two wholly different acts.

In Qur’anic use, ṣawm appears in Mary’s story and ṣiyām in devotional fasting. The distinction between abstaining from speech and abstaining from food and drink is identified through context.

Taṣūmū: an imperfect verb in the subjunctive

In Al-Baqarah 2:184, taṣūmū follows an and is an imperfect verb in the subjunctive.

Its final wāw is the second-person plural subject pronoun.

An taṣūmū means “that you fast.”

The verb concerns devotional abstention in the fasting context, rather than Mary’s silence or a horse standing still.

Fa-l-yaṣumhu: the command to fast

In Al-Baqarah 2:185, fa-l-yaṣumhu consists of fa-, the lām of command, the jussive imperfect yaṣum, and the pronoun -hu.

The pronoun refers to Ramadan.

The construction commands fasting that month.

The pronoun -hu refers to the month, the time of fasting. A recognized grammatical analysis treats it as an object through syntactic extension, meaning “fast during that month.” This construction alone therefore does not establish ṣāma as inherently transitive; a temporal complement must be distinguished from an action’s actual object.

Al-Tibyān fī Iʿrāb al-Qurʾān: the pronoun in fa-l-yaṣumhu

Al-ṣāʾimīn and al-ṣāʾimāt

Both forms in Al-Aḥzāb 33:35 are plural active participles.

Al-ṣāʾimīn is a sound masculine plural; al-ṣāʾimāt is a sound feminine plural.

Both occur among descriptions of men and women, referring to people who fast.

Grammatical gender does not change the act’s meaning.

The verbal noun and the kind of act

Every Qur’anic use requires distinguishing the word itself from what qualifies it.

Al-ṣiyām names the act of fasting.

Thalāthati ayyāmin or shahrayni mutatābiʿayni specifies its amount and duration.

Ilā al-layli gives its temporal limit.

Laʿallakum tattaqūn identifies its purpose.

All these meanings relate to fasting, but they are not all contained within Ṣ W M itself.

11. Challenges in understanding and translation

Does ṣawm mean only silence?

No. Ṣawm in Maryam 19:26 accompanies silence, but Arabic has other uses.

A stationary horse, a subsided wind, and someone abstaining from food can all lie within the root’s field.

Restricting ṣawm to silence therefore removes part of its linguistic range.

Does ṣiyām mean only enduring hunger?

No. Hunger can result from fasting, but is not the meaning of ṣiyām.

Someone may go hungry for lack of food without fasting.

Someone fasting refrains from food and drink during a specified interval to observe prescribed worship.

Al-Baqarah 2:187 also addresses intercourse when setting this worship’s limits.

Fasting is therefore more than hunger, and hunger is not the same as fasting.

Is fasting only an exercise in enduring difficulty?

The Qur’an does not explicitly state that purpose.

Al-Baqarah 2:183 identifies taqwā as the purpose, and Al-Baqarah 2:185 relates the divine intention to ease rather than hardship.

The value of fasting should therefore not be measured by the amount of suffering endured.

Someone capable of fasting differs from someone exposed to harm through illness or incapacity.

Nor should performing worship while neglecting necessary human responsibilities automatically be taken as evidence of greater taqwā.

Can any abstention replace Ramadan fasting?

Lexically, ṣawm covers a broad field of abstention.

Al-Baqarah 2:183; Al-Baqarah 2:184; Al-Baqarah 2:185; Al-Baqarah 2:186; Al-Baqarah 2:187 nevertheless presents particular worship with prescribed timing and limits.

Refraining from speech, wrongdoing, or practicing ethical vigilance may be valuable, but these alone do not replace the particular act of fasting.

This distinguishes a root’s general meaning from its devotional instance.

Can the root supply every fasting prescription?

No. The root supplies the semantic field of abstaining and ceasing.

Beginning and ending times, illness and travel, ransom, the number of days, intercourse, and other conditions must be identified from the verses addressing each matter.

Even expiatory uses differ in the kind and amount of fasting.

Root study is therefore necessary for understanding the word, but does not replace examination of the complete sentence and Qur’anic prescription.

12. Translation proposals for Forqan

For ṣāma in general usage, “abstained,” “ceased,” or “refrained from an activity” is appropriate.

For movement stopping, “stood,” “stopped,” or “ceased moving” may be used.

The general equivalent of ṣawm is “abstention.” In Maryam 19:26, retaining ṣawm and allowing the verse’s continuation to explain its kind is linguistically faithful. “Abstention from speech” also suits an interpretive translation of the context.

For ṣiyām in verses concerning worship, the primary natural equivalent is “fasting.”

Ṣāʾim means “someone fasting,” or “someone abstaining” in general, nondevotional usage.

For al-ṣāʾimīna wa-al-ṣāʾimāti in Al-Aḥzāb 33:35, “men and women who fast” is clear and natural.

Wa-an taṣūmū in Al-Baqarah 2:184 is best rendered “and that you fast.”

In Al-Baqarah 2:185, fa-l-yaṣumhu means “then he shall fast that month” or “then he shall fast during that month.” The pronoun refers to Ramadan and has a temporal meaning; the basic translation need not add unrelated explanations to the verb.

Thumma atimmū al-ṣiyāma ilā al-layli in Al-Baqarah 2:187 is rendered “Then complete the fast until night.”

In uses concerning compensation and expiation, ṣiyām likewise means “fasting”; its amount and conditions should follow the particular verse.

The translation should retain “fasting,” while the root meaning of abstention, its relationship with taqwā, and differences of usage can be explained in a reflection or lexical study.

13. Summary

The Arabic root Ṣ W M concerns abstaining, ceasing, and stopping. Its field includes horses stopping, wind subsiding, the sun’s apparent midday standstill, and people refraining from speech, food, or other activities.

Ṣawm and ṣiyām are both verbal nouns of ṣāma. Their different Qur’anic uses are real, but do not make one exclusively silence and the other exclusively abstention from food in Arabic.

Maryam 19:26 associates ṣawm with refraining from conversation. Al-Baqarah presents ṣiyām as particular worship whose temporal and practical limits are stated.

Al-Baqarah 2:183 identifies fasting’s purpose through laʿallakum tattaqūn. Fasting is therefore more than enduring hunger and thirst: it is worship intended to lead toward taqwā.

Qur’anic fasting is also not restricted to Ramadan. Several verses include ṣiyām within compensation or expiation, with amounts varying according to the prescription’s subject.

Abstention remains common to these uses; its object, duration, conditions, and purpose must be identified from each verse.

The brief meaning of Ṣ W M is abstaining and ceasing an activity or movement.

In Qur’anic worship, this abstention takes the form of fasting with specified limits and taqwā as its purpose. Knowing the root’s linguistic core prevents reducing fasting to hunger or silence; knowing the prescriptive verses prevents substituting the broad lexical meaning for the particular worship.
