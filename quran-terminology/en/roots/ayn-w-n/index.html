1. Introduction to the Root and Its Semantic Field

The root ʿ W N has two recorded semantic fields in Arabic. The first and dominant field is help and support. ʿAwn refers to a person, force, or factor that increases the ability to carry out a task. From this meaning, forms such as أَعَانَ, meaning to help, تَعَاوَنَ, meaning mutual cooperation, اِسْتَعَانَ, meaning to seek help or take something as help, and مُسْتَعَان, meaning the one from whom help is sought, are formed. The second field appears in the adjective عَوَان. This word is used for a living being that has passed the initial stage of age or first experience, but has not yet reached the final stage of old age. In the Qur’an, ʿawān describes a cow standing between بِكْر and فَارِض: neither young and uncalved nor old, but middle-aged. In early usage, ʿawān was not used only about the age of an animal. A woman who had previously been married and a war that flared up again after a first conflict were also called ʿawān. In this branch, the word indicates passing beyond the first condition and reaching a stage of maturity, experience, or completed strength. Semantically, the possible link between the two fields can be seen in the idea of strength and firmness. ʿAwn is a force that increases another’s capacity, and ʿawān is a stage in which the living being has passed initial weakness and reached strength and maturity. Nevertheless, early lexicons did not explain the historical origin of this transition in one uniform way. Therefore “strength and firmness” is a defensible link for understanding the word-family, but the exact historical derivation of ʿawān from ʿawn cannot be reconstructed with certainty. The root ʿ W N appears eleven times in the Qur’an and in five forms.

2. Important Forms and Derivatives

The Qur’anic forms of this root are:

  • أَعَانَ and its imperative form, two times: giving help and adding strength or possibility to another’s work.
  • تَعَاوَنُوا, two times in one verse: the participation of several parties in supporting a work.
  • اِسْتَعَانَ and its conjugations, four times: seeking help or taking a person, force, or means as help for doing something.
  • مُسْتَعَان, two times: the one from whom help is sought.
  • عَوَان, once: middle-aged; describing the cow.

The noun عَوْن itself does not occur in the Qur’an. The forms إِعَانَة، مَعُونَة، مُعِين، مُعَاوَنَة، أَعْوَان and مِعْوَان also do not occur in the Qur’an, although they are formed in Arabic from this field. The form الْمَاعُون in Al-Maʿun 107:7 is outwardly close to this family, but there are different views about its derivation. Some connected it with small objects and utilities people give one another, while others proposed another origin. For this reason, al-māʿūn requires independent study and has not been counted among the eleven clear uses of the root ʿ W N.

4. Semantic Core of the Help Branch

In the main branch, the root ʿ W N indicates the addition of strength or backing to a person or work. Help does not necessarily mean doing the whole task instead of another person. The main person can remain responsible and still be the one carrying out the work, while receiving an external force, practical tool, or support that increases his ability. This support may appear as bodily strength, tools, wealth, knowledge, guidance, defense, shared work, or psychological steadiness. The shared element is that a factor is added to existing capacity and makes the work firmer or more possible. Arabic lexicons explained ʿawn with ẓahīr and muʿīn: someone or something that becomes a backing for a person in a matter. They also said that anything that helps complete a work can be ʿawn for a person. Therefore ʿawn is not only a person; a means, condition, or capacity that advances the work can also function as ʿawn. From this core, the difference among the derivatives becomes clear:

  • أَعَانَ expresses the relation between helper and helped.
  • تَعَاوَنَ shows the participation of several parties in adding strength.
  • اِسْتَعَانَ is the movement of the needy one toward seeking or using help.
  • مُسْتَعَان is the one toward whom this seeking is directed.

5. Use of the Root in Early Arabic

ʿAwn in early Arabic meant a supporter or helper. This noun could be used for one person, several people, masculine or feminine. The plural aʿwān was also known for helpers and supporting forces. Aʿantuhu iʿānatan means “I helped him.” Istaʿantu bihi means “I sought help from him” or “I took him as help.” Taʿāwana al-qawm means that the people of a group helped one another and joined their strengths in a shared work. Maʿūnah and iʿānah were names for the act of helping. Miʿwān was used for someone who helps people very much. Beside this field, ʿawān was used for a being or matter that had passed its first condition. A middle-aged cow or horse, a woman who had previously been married, and a war that happened again could be called ʿawān in early Arabic. These varied uses show that ʿawān is not merely the name of a numerical age-range. The word describes a condition that has passed beyond being bikr, has gained experience or strength, but has not yet reached the end of its period.

6. Aʿāna: Giving Help

The verb أَعَانَ appears twice in the Qur’an.

Al-Kahf 18:95

قَالَ مَا مَكَّنِّي فِيهِ رَبِّي خَيْرٌ فَأَعِينُونِي بِقُوَّةٍ أَجْعَلْ بَيْنَكُمْ وَبَيْنَهُمْ رَدْمًا

He said: What my Lord has empowered me in is better; so help me with strength, and I will place between you and them a firm barrier.

Al-Kahf 18:95

Fa-aʿīnūnī is a plural imperative addressed to the people who had asked Dhu al-Qarnayn to build a barrier between them and Ya’juj and Ma’juj. They had offered wealth, but Dhu al-Qarnayn asks them for quwwah. Quwwah in this context is the strength the community provides for carrying out the work. The following verses speak of bringing pieces of iron and blowing. Therefore iʿānah here is practical participation through human strength, materials, and execution. Dhu al-Qarnayn is the designer and director of the work, and the people add the needed strength to his work. This use clearly shows the base meaning of أَعَانَ: adding ability to another so a work can be realized. The letter bā’ in bi-quwwah indicates the type or means of help: “with strength” or “through providing capacity.”

Al-Furqan 25:4

إِنْ هَٰذَا إِلَّا إِفْكٌ افْتَرَاهُ وَأَعَانَهُ عَلَيْهِ قَوْمٌ آخَرُونَ

This is nothing but a lie he invented, and another people helped him in it.

Al-Furqan 25:4

This sentence is the speech of the opponents about the Qur’an. They claim that the Prophet invented it and that another group helped him in this work. In aʿānahu ʿalayhi, the pronoun hu is the one allegedly helped, and ʿalayhi shows the matter of help. The structure means: another people helped him in that work. This verse shows that أَعَانَ itself expresses a relation of support. Judgment about the subject of help comes from context. Here the original claim is treated by the Qur’an as false, but the lexical meaning of the opponents’ sentence is group participation in a work they attribute to the Prophet.

7. Taʿāwana: Mutual Joining of Strengths

The verb تَعَاوَنُوا appears twice, both in Al-Maidah 5:2:

وَتَعَاوَنُوا عَلَى الْبِرِّ وَالتَّقْوَىٰ وَلَا تَعَاوَنُوا عَلَى الْإِثْمِ وَالْعُدْوَانِ

And cooperate with one another upon birr and taqwa, and do not cooperate with one another upon sin and aggression.

Al-Maidah 5:2

Taʿāwana is Form VI. In this verb, that form fits participation and a many-sided relation: each side brings part of its strength, means, or share into the work, and from their joining a collective strength is formed. Cooperation does not necessarily mean that everyone does the same work. One may provide knowledge, another bodily strength, another wealth, time, or tools. What turns these participations into cooperation is that they join in the direction of a shared subject. The subject of cooperation in both parts of the verse comes with ʿalā:

  • ʿalā al-birr wa at-taqwā
  • ʿalā al-ithm wa al-ʿudwān

Therefore the verb has the same meaning in both sentences; the difference is in the field that cooperation serves. Birr and taqwa form one direction, while sin and aggression form the opposite direction. Cooperation in this structure is not merely emotional sympathy or mental agreement. The root ʿ W N indicates an addition of strength; therefore cooperation occurs when people’s participation increases the ability to carry out a work.

8. Istaʿāna: Seeking Help and Taking Something as Help

The verb اِسْتَعَانَ is Form X. In this word, Form X indicates seeking ʿawn or using it. This verb appears four times in the Qur’an.

Al-Fatihah 1:5

إِيَّاكَ نَعْبُدُ وَإِيَّاكَ نَسْتَعِينُ

Only You do we serve, and only from You do we seek help.

Al-Fatihah 1:5

In nastaʿīn, the subject is “we,” and the addressee is the one indicated by iyyāka. The placement of iyyāka before the verb highlights the direction of seeking help. In this verse, seeking help is a direct relation between servants and The God. The servants recognize their need and the limits of their strength, and they seek foundational help from Him. This structure does not conflict with human uses of ʿawn. The Qur’an speaks both about people cooperating and about Dhu al-Qarnayn asking them for help. Iyyāka nastaʿīn states the final direction of reliance and seeking help, while human cooperation can be one of the means placed within the world for human beings.

Al-Baqarah 2:45

وَاسْتَعِينُوا بِالصَّبْرِ وَالصَّلَاةِ

And take patience and salat as help.

Al-Baqarah 2:45

Al-Baqarah 2:153

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا اسْتَعِينُوا بِالصَّبْرِ وَالصَّلَاةِ إِنَّ اللَّهَ مَعَ الصَّابِرِينَ

O you who have faith, take patience and salat as help; indeed, The God is with the patient.

Al-Baqarah 2:153

In these two verses, aṣ-ṣabr and aṣ-ṣalāh come after the letter bā’. They stand in the position of the instrument or capacity of seeking help. The human being takes patience and salat as help for steadiness and continuation on the path. Patience provides the capacity to bear pressure, preserve direction, and continue. Salat establishes conscious turning and connection to The God. The precise explanation of these two concepts requires independent study of their roots and uses, but the structure of these verses shows that seeking help can mean using a capacity to strengthen action. The translation “ask patience and salat for help” may suggest in English that patience and salat are two independent agents who hear a request. “Take patience and salat as help” conveys the syntactic and semantic relation more clearly.

Al-Araf 7:128

قَالَ مُوسَىٰ لِقَوْمِهِ اسْتَعِينُوا بِاللَّهِ وَاصْبِرُوا

Moses said to his people: seek help from The God and be patient.

Al-Araf 7:128

In this verse the object of seeking help is The God. Unlike patience and salat, which are taken as capacities of help, here The God is the one from whom help is sought. The pairing of istaʿīnū bi-The God with waṣbirū shows that seeking help and human action stand together. The people of Moses are commanded to seek help and, at the same time, to remain steadfast. The letter bā’ establishes the relation of seeking help in all three structures, but the nature of what comes after bā’ is understood from context. The God is the one addressed in the request for help; patience and salat are capacities taken as help.

9. Mustaʿān: The One from Whom Help Is Sought

Mustaʿān is the passive participle of Form X and indicates the one toward whom help-seeking is directed. This form appears twice in the Qur’an, and in both cases it describes The God.

Yusuf 12:18

فَصَبْرٌ جَمِيلٌ وَاللَّهُ الْمُسْتَعَانُ عَلَىٰ مَا تَصِفُونَ

So beautiful patience; and The God is the one from whom help is sought against what you describe.

Yusuf 12:18

This is the speech of Yaʿqub after hearing his sons’ account about Yusuf. He does not accept their account, and against what they describe he places beautiful patience and seeking help from The God. Al-mustaʿān is not identical with muʿīn. Muʿīn is one who gives help; mustaʿān is the one from whom another seeks help. Of course, in this context, seeking help presupposes His ability and worthiness to help, but the morphological direction of the word is from the needy one toward the one sought for help. The letter ʿalā in ʿalā mā taṣifūn shows the field in which Yaʿqub seeks help to face what they describe.

Al-Anbiya 21:112

قَالَ رَبِّ احْكُمْ بِالْحَقِّ وَرَبُّنَا الرَّحْمَٰنُ الْمُسْتَعَانُ عَلَىٰ مَا تَصِفُونَ

He said: My Lord, judge with truth; and our Lord, The Merciful, is the one from whom help is sought against what you describe.

Al-Anbiya 21:112

The structure of this verse is very close to Yusuf 12:18. In both, al-mustaʿān is joined with ʿalā mā taṣifūn. The subject is the descriptions and claims in the face of which the human being seeks judgment and help from the Lord.

10. Al-Mustaʿān as a Divine Description

Al-mustaʿān in both Qur’anic uses is attributed to The God:

وَاللَّهُ الْمُسْتَعَانُ

and:

وَرَبُّنَا الرَّحْمَٰنُ الْمُسْتَعَانُ

Its morphological meaning is “the one from whom help is sought.” This description expresses the relation of human need and reliance upon The God. Facing a matter that one cannot solve or bear alone, the human being directs the request for help toward Him. This description is directly connected to iyyāka nastaʿīn. In Al-Fatihah, the servants state their own act: “from You we seek help.” In Yusuf and Al-Anbiya, the same relation is expressed from the side of the one from whom help is sought: al-mustaʿān.

11. ʿAwān: Specialized Examination of the Form and Semantic Development

ʿAwān appears once in the Qur’an:

قَالَ إِنَّهُ يَقُولُ إِنَّهَا بَقَرَةٌ لَا فَارِضٌ وَلَا بِكْرٌ عَوَانٌ بَيْنَ ذَٰلِكَ

He said: He says that it is a cow, neither old nor young and uncalved; middle between those two.

Al-Baqarah 2:68

Direct Meaning of ʿAwān

Three descriptions in this verse form an age-spectrum:

  • bikr: a young cow still in the first stage of age and calving;
  • ʿawān: a cow that has passed that stage and is in the middle of age;
  • fāriḍ: a cow advanced in age.

The phrase bayna dhālik explains the meaning of ʿawān: a middle between the two mentioned stages. Therefore its meaning in the verse is clear from the internal contrast of the sentence. In lexical explanations, ʿawān is said about a cow that has calved once or more and has left the state of bikr, but is not yet old. Differences about the number of calvings do not change the base meaning: a mature, middle-aged cow.

Morphological Structure

ʿAwān is on the pattern faʿāl, and in the verse it describes baqarah without a feminine marker. In Arabic this form can be used for the feminine. Its plural in early usage was ʿūn. Verbal forms are also recorded for entering this state:

  • ʿānat al-baqarah taʿūn: the cow reached the stage of ʿawān.
  • ʿawwanat al-mar’ah: the woman entered the stage or condition of ʿawān.

The existence of these forms shows that ʿawān was a stable word in the family of ʿ W N, not an accidental form or a mistaken root attribution.

Semantic Expansion of ʿAwān

The field of ʿawān in early Arabic went beyond the age of animals:

  • imra’ah ʿawān: a woman who had previously had a husband or had passed beyond being bikr.
  • ḥarb ʿawān: a war that had happened once before and flared up again.
  • faras ʿawān: a horse that is neither in first youth nor old.

In all these uses, one shared movement is visible: the subject has passed the first stage and bikr condition, gained experience and maturity, but has not yet reached the end of its period. For this reason, in the linguistic image, a first war can be called bikr, while a repeated war can be called ʿawān.

The Link Between ʿAwān and ʿAwn

Two lexical paths have been proposed to explain the connection between ʿawān and ʿawn. The first path is built on strength and fullness of capacity. The bikr animal is still at the beginning of growth, and the fāriḍ animal has passed the height of strength; ʿawān stands in a middle stage where bodily force and capability have reached firmness. In some lexical explanations, ʿawān is connected with ʿawn because it is in a stage of strength and readiness for work and assistance. Based on this analysis, the wider meaning of the root is not merely “help,” but strength that becomes useful and functions as support. In ʿawn, this strength is added to another; in ʿawān, the living being itself has reached the stage of firmness and strength. The second path takes passing beyond the first stage as central. The uses “woman ʿawān” and “war ʿawān” are more connected with experience and repetition than with giving help. In this path, ʿawān is an early branch in the same phonetic family whose meaning became fixed as middle-age and experience, while its first link with the meaning of iʿānah is no longer transparent in the history of the language. The available data make the first path possible as a semantic explanation, but they do not turn it into certain historical derivation. The firm conclusion is that ʿawān indicates the stage of firmness, maturity, and middle age: after initial immaturity and before final decline.

The Place of ʿAwān in the Structure of the Verse

Syntactically, some grammarians considered the sentence complete after wa-lā bikr, and treated ʿawānun bayna dhālik as a new sentence or explanatory phrase. In this reading, the structure is:

It is neither old nor bikr; it is middle between the two.

Whether ʿawān is taken as a later adjective for the cow or as a fresh explanatory predicate, its semantic role is the same: determining the middle point between fāriḍ and bikr.

12. Grammatical Notes and Prepositional Relations

The root ʿ W N appears in the Qur’an with several important syntactic structures.

Aʿāna + Person + ʿAlā

In:

وَأَعَانَهُ عَلَيْهِ قَوْمٌ آخَرُونَ

The helped person appears as the object pronoun, and the subject of help comes with ʿalā. The meaning is: a group helped him in that work.

Aʿāna + Person + Bi-

In:

فَأَعِينُونِي بِقُوَّةٍ

The person appears as the object pronoun, and the means or type of help comes with bā’: help me with strength.

Taʿāwana + ʿAlā

In Al-Maidah 5:2, ʿalā introduces the subject and direction upon which cooperation is formed:

عَلَى الْبِرِّ وَالتَّقْوَىٰ عَلَى الْإِثْمِ وَالْعُدْوَانِ

Istaʿāna + Bi-

With the verb of seeking help, bā’ establishes the relation of seeking help or taking as help:

اسْتَعِينُوا بِاللَّهِ اسْتَعِينُوا بِالصَّبْرِ وَالصَّلَاةِ

The precise role of the attached object is understood from its nature and the context. The God is the one from whom help is sought; patience and salat are capacities taken as help.

Mustaʿān + ʿAlā

In:

الْمُسْتَعَانُ عَلَىٰ مَا تَصِفُونَ

ʿAlā shows the matter or difficulty for facing which help is sought.

13. Comparison with Nearby Roots

ʿ W N and N Ṣ R: Help and Victory-Aid

ʿAwn is any addition of strength and support for advancing a work. This can be building a barrier, bearing difficulty, worship, or social cooperation. Naṣr leans more toward help that, against an enemy, oppression, or conflict, leads to overcoming and victory. Naṣr is one form of help, but not every ʿawn is naṣr. In Al-Kahf 18:95, Dhu al-Qarnayn says fa-aʿīnūnī, because the subject is adding practical force for construction, not reporting victory in battle.

ʿ W N and Gh W Th: Help and Rescue

Ghawth is closer to help in a state of emergency, distress, and crying out. Immediate need and rescue from pressure are prominent in it. ʿAwn has a wider range and can include help in an ordinary, long, or planned work as well.

ʿ W N and Ẓ H R: Help and Backing

Ẓahīr is someone who stands behind another and supports him. Taẓāhara can also indicate mutual backing and the joining of forces. Taʿāwana emphasizes participation in adding strength; taẓāhara more strongly highlights becoming a backing and standing behind one another. These two fields are close, but their lexical images are not identical.

ʿ W N and M D D: Help and Increase of Force

The root M D D is connected with extension, increase, and supplying more force. Imdād can be the arrival of people, resources, or added strength. ʿAwn is the more general relation of support. That support may take place through added force, tools, knowledge, patience, or practical participation.

ʿAwān, Bikr, and Fāriḍ

These three words in Al-Baqarah 2:68 form an age-spectrum:

  • bikr: the first stage; a young cow that has not yet calved.
  • ʿawān: the stage of firmness and middle age.
  • fāriḍ: the advanced stage and old age.

The meaning of ʿawān becomes clear from its middle position in this spectrum.

14. Translation Suggestions for Forqan

  • إِيَّاكَ نَسْتَعِينُ: “Only from You do we seek help.”
  • اسْتَعِينُوا بِاللَّهِ: “Seek help from The God.”
  • اسْتَعِينُوا بِالصَّبْرِ وَالصَّلَاةِ: “Take patience and salat as help.”
  • فَأَعِينُونِي بِقُوَّةٍ: “So help me with strength,” or “bring your strength to my aid.”
  • وَأَعَانَهُ عَلَيْهِ قَوْمٌ آخَرُونَ: “and another people helped him in it.”
  • تَعَاوَنُوا عَلَى الْبِرِّ وَالتَّقْوَىٰ: “cooperate with one another upon birr and taqwa.”
  • لَا تَعَاوَنُوا عَلَى الْإِثْمِ وَالْعُدْوَانِ: “do not cooperate with one another upon sin and aggression.”
  • اللَّهُ الْمُسْتَعَانُ: “The God is the one from whom help is sought.”
  • عَوَانٌ بَيْنَ ذَٰلِكَ: “middle-aged between those two,” or “in the middle between youth and old age.”

In translating this family, “to help,” “to cooperate,” “to seek help,” and “to take as help” show the differences among the derivatives better than repeating one undifferentiated word such as “help” everywhere.

15. Conclusion

The root ʿ W N in its main field indicates help, support, and the addition of strength for doing a work. Aʿāna is giving help to another; taʿāwana is joining several strengths in a shared work; istaʿāna is seeking help or taking a capacity as help; and mustaʿān is the one toward whom the request for help is directed. Al-mustaʿān in the Qur’an is a description of The God. Morphologically, this form indicates the one from whom help is sought in the face of hardship and limitation. ʿAwān is another branch of the same lexical family. About the cow, this word points to a stage in which the animal has passed first youth, reached firmness and maturity, but has not yet become old. Its early uses about a previously married woman and a repeated war also indicate passing beyond the first stage and entering the stage of experience. The semantic link between ʿawān and ʿawn can be explained through the idea of strength and firmness: ʿawn is a force that becomes a backing for work, and ʿawān is a being that has reached the stage of relative fullness of strength. This link is semantically understandable, although the precise history of transfer between the two uses is not known with certainty. The concise definition of the root is:

ʿ W N: help and adding strength or backing for doing a work; and in the form ʿawān, reaching the stage of firmness and middle position, after the first condition and before old age.
