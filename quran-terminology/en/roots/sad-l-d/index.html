Hardness, dryness, and a smooth, bare surface: from hard stone and barren ground to a fire-striker that produces no spark and to miserliness

1. Introducing the root and its forms

The Arabic root Ṣ L D concerns hardness, dryness, and a smooth, uncovered surface. Its best-known use describes a hard, smooth stone without soil or a soft covering on its surface. The family also supplies words for hard, barren ground, a hairless head, a fire-striker producing no spark, and someone who gives others nothing.

These uses converge around a hard surface from which nothing emerges or on which nothing remains. That image should nevertheless not become a definitive explanation of every derivative: hardness, smoothness, dryness, and lack of yield play different roles in different uses.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as ṣaldan in Al-Baqarah 2:264.

Important Arabic forms include:

  • صَلْد (ṣald): hard and smooth; particularly stone or a surface without soil, vegetation, or a covering.
  • صَلُود / صَلِيد (ṣalūd / ṣalīd): hard, smooth, or resistant, with differences according to the object described.
  • أَصْلَد (aṣlad): hard and smooth; figuratively, also very miserly.
  • صَلَادَة (ṣalādah): hardness and solidity.
  • صُلُود (ṣulūd): becoming hard and resistant, or a condition of not readily being affected.
  • صَلَدَ (ṣalada): became hard and dry; in some uses, failed to grow or produce something.
  • أَصْلَدَ (aṣlada): became hard and unproductive, or brought something into such a condition, according to construction and context.
  • صَلْدَاء / صَلْدَاءَة (ṣaldāʾ / ṣaldāʾah): hard, stony ground.
  • مِصْلَاد / صَلُود (miṣlād / ṣalūd): of a she-camel, yielding little milk or none after giving birth.

These forms are recorded in Arabic, but only ṣaldan occurs in the Qur’anic text.

2. The semantic core and early Arabic usage

A hard, smooth stone

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris traces Ṣ L D to hardness and dryness.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise explains ḥajarun ṣaldun as a hard, smooth stone. Al-Layth ibn al-Muẓaffar describes a smooth, dry surface when explaining the family.

Two features occur together here: the body’s hardness and its surface’s smoothness.

A stone may be hard yet uneven and full of cracks. Conversely, something can be smooth without being hard, like the soft, polished surface of certain materials.

Ṣald applied to a stone often combines these features. In the Qur’anic verse, hardness and smoothness also connect with the surface becoming bare after soil washes away.

This does not mean every hard stone necessarily lacks every crack or irregularity. The lexical description concerns the surface’s predominant character and the stone’s solidity, rather than a precise measurement of its geometry.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: hardness and dryness under Ṣ L D

A witness from pre-Islamic poetry

The pre-Islamic poet Al-Muthaqqib al-ʿAbdī compares part of a powerful camel’s body to hard stone:

تَنْمِي بِنَهَّاضٍ إِلَى حَارِكٍ
تَمَّ كَرُكْنِ الْحَجَرِ الْأَصْلَدِ

tanmī bi-nahhāḍin ilā ḥārikin / tamma ka-rukni al-ḥajari al-aṣladi

A rendering close to the sense:

“Its sturdy rise extends to the withers, fully formed and firm like the corner of a hard stone.”

Al-ḥajari al-aṣladi evokes a solid, hard stone used to describe the camel’s powerful bodily structure.

The witness matters because aṣlad was already known before Qur’anic usage as a description of stone and its solidity.

Sources preserve minor textual variants, including the opening verb and the word preceding the comparison. The expression al-ḥajari al-aṣladi is nevertheless established in lexical and poetic citations.

The witness clearly connects ṣald with physical hardness in early Arabic. The poem alone, however, does not explain every other use, such as barrenness or miserliness.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Al-Muthaqqib al-ʿAbdī’s witness and its textual variants

Hard, barren ground

Arabic says:

أَرْضٌ صَلْدٌ (arḍun ṣaldun)

That is, hard ground on which vegetation does not grow.

Ṣalada al-makānu and aṣlada also describe a place becoming hard and unproductive.

Hardness is not the only significant feature here: lack of vegetation also matters.

Plant growth requires conditions allowing roots to take hold and obtain water and necessary nutrients. A hard, rocky surface may fail to provide them.

That natural explanation should not become a claim that every hard soil or stone can never support plants under any circumstances. Plants can grow in rock crevices and thin layers of soil.

Early arḍun ṣaldun describes hard, barren ground rather than stating a scientific law covering every rock and environment.

A hairless head and forehead

Another use concerns the human head and forehead.

Arabic uses raʾsun ṣaldun for a hairless head and jabīnun ṣaldun for a smooth, hairless forehead.

Lexicons transmit a line by Ruʾbah ibn al-ʿAjjāj:

بَرَّاقُ أَصْلَادِ الْجَبِينِ الْأَجْلَهِ

barrāqu aṣlādi al-jabīni al-ajlahi

Translation:

“With shining, smooth patches of a broad, hairless forehead.”

Aṣlād here concerns the forehead’s smooth, exposed areas.

Ruʾbah was an Umayyad poet. The line therefore attests early Arabic after Islam rather than pre-Islamic usage.

Comparison with ḥajarun ṣaldun shows that hardness is not equally prominent in every derivative. Smoothness and lack of hair matter more in the forehead description.

Hard alone therefore cannot explain every use of ṣald.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Kitāb al-ʿAyn, and Asās al-Balāghah: stone, ground, and a hairless forehead

3. From a fire-striker producing no spark to an unproductive creature

A fire-striker that produces no fire

Early Arabic uses Ṣ L D for flint or a fire-striking implement failing to produce the required spark despite striking or friction.

Ṣalada al-zandu means that the stone or fire-striking implement sounded but produced no fire.

Failure to produce an expected result is prominent here.

Someone lighting a fire expects a spark from the fire-striker. When the effort produces none, Arabic can describe the implement as ṣalūd or aṣlad.

This use connects with hard stone but means more than hardness: the established expression also includes failure to obtain fire.

Ṣalada al-zandu therefore cannot adequately be translated as the stone became hard. The fire-striker’s failure to produce a spark must be clear.

A camel yielding little milk and a horse that does not sweat

Lexicons use nāqatun ṣalūdun for a she-camel yielding little milk or none after giving birth.

Nāqatun miṣlādun is also recorded in this field.

Farasun ṣalūdun describes a horse that does not sweat or sweats slowly. Some sources also record other uses concerning its reproductive characteristics.

The family thus sometimes describes a creature that does not readily produce what is normally expected of it.

These meanings should not all be assigned one bodily cause: little milk, lack of sweating, and failure to spark are different events.

Hardness, dryness, or lack of an expected output can explain their lexical connection, but this does not prove a single historical origin for every use.

A miserly person

Arabic also uses rajulun ṣaldun, ṣalūd, and aṣlad for a very miserly person.

Here the person withholds what they possess rather than sharing it.

This approaches barren ground or a fire-striker without sparks in meaning: something expected to give or yield produces no result.

Arabic poetry also uses ṣaladat zināduka for generosity that no longer produces its former results, as though a person’s fire-strikers no longer spark.

That comparison should not define every branch of the root. Miserliness is a behavioral, moral quality; a stone’s hardness and ground’s barrenness are physical features.

Aṣlad for a miserly person is therefore an established semantic extension, rather than every ṣald object lexically possessing the moral quality of miserliness.

Lisān al-ʿArab: fire-strikers, milk yield, horses, and miserliness

4. The Qur’anic use: fa-tarakahu ṣaldan

The root’s sole Qur’anic occurrence appears in Al-Baqarah:

فَمَثَلُهُ كَمَثَلِ صَفْوَانٍ عَلَيْهِ تُرَابٌ فَأَصَابَهُ وَابِلٌ فَتَرَكَهُ صَلْدًا

fa-mathaluhu ka-mathali ṣafwānin ʿalayhi turābun fa-aṣābahu wābilun fa-tarakahu ṣaldan

“His likeness is that of a smooth stone with soil upon it: a downpour strikes it and leaves it hard and bare.”

Al-Baqarah 2:264

The meaning of ṣaldan here

Ṣaldan is an accusative description expressing the stone’s condition after the rain reaches it.

Syntactically, it can be understood as a circumstantial accusative referring to hu in fa-tarakahu. Alternatively, if taraka is understood as ṣayyara, to make or render, ṣaldan becomes its second object.

Both analyses lead to one principal image: the rain has left the stone hard, smooth, and bare.

The rain has not transformed soil or a soft substance into a hard stone. The stone already existed beneath a layer of soil.

Heavy rain washes away that soil and exposes the original surface.

Ṣaldan consequently emphasizes the stone’s revealed condition once its soil covering is removed.

Distinguishing ṣafwān from ṣald

The passage contains two different words:

صَفْوَانٍ (ṣafwānin) and صَلْدًا (ṣaldan).

Ṣafwān belongs to Ṣ F W and here denotes a smooth stone.

Ṣald belongs to Ṣ L D, emphasizing hardness, dryness, and the bare surface within the verse’s image.

Although both may describe a smooth stone, their sentence roles differ.

Ṣafwān introduces the stone on which soil rests.

Ṣaldan expresses that same stone’s condition after the soil washes away.

Rendering both simply as smooth stone would weaken the image’s change of condition.

Their difference should not be exaggerated as though ṣafwān were a soft, earthy stone and ṣald something entirely different. The verse concerns one stone, initially soil-covered and subsequently bare.

Soil and rain within the image

ʿAlayhi turābun states that soil lies upon the stone.

The soil covers its surface. The stone and soil are not identical, and soil resting on it does not transform it into fertile ground.

The passage continues:

فَأَصَابَهُ وَابِلٌ

fa-aṣābahu wābilun

“Then a downpour struck it.”

Wābil denotes heavy, abundant rain.

Here the rain washes away the soil layer and leaves the stone bare.

The soil-covered appearance disappears, exposing the hard surface.

The image rests on a significant distinction: something apparently covered in soil is not necessarily fertile ground.

Ṣald itself, however, means neither deception, ostentation, nor invalidation of deeds. Those concepts emerge from the full comparison and the verse’s ethical context.

Al-Ṭabarī: soil washed away to expose hard, bare stone

5. Ṣald within the comparison concerning charitable spending

Al-Baqarah 2:264 begins:

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَا تُبْطِلُوا صَدَقَاتِكُمْ بِالْمَنِّ وَالْأَذَىٰ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū lā tubṭilū ṣadaqātikum bi-al-manni wa-al-adhā

“You who have believed, do not invalidate your charitable gifts through reminders of your generosity and hurt.”

It then describes someone spending their wealth to be seen by people, without believing in God or the last day.

The image of the soil-covered stone and the downpour follows.

Ṣald thus forms part of a comparison concerning an action’s loss of fruit or value.

Money may actually have been given, yet reminders of generosity, hurtful conduct, or showing off destroy the act’s moral value.

Soil resting on a stone can resemble ground ready for growth, but rain carries away that covering and leaves a bare surface.

This distinction between appearance and reality helps explain the comparison.

The next verse, Al-Baqarah 2:265, presents a different image:

كَمَثَلِ جَنَّةٍ بِرَبْوَةٍ أَصَابَهَا وَابِلٌ فَآتَتْ أُكُلَهَا ضِعْفَيْنِ

ka-mathali jannatin bi-rabwatin aṣābahā wābilun fa-ātat ukulahā ḍiʿfayni

“Like a garden on high ground struck by a downpour, yielding twice its fruit.”

Wābil occurs again, but the result differs.

On the soil-covered stone, rain removes the covering and leaves it bare. In the garden, the same kind of rain accompanies a larger yield.

The difference therefore concerns not only rain but also the condition of what receives it.

This contrast clarifies the spending comparison: outwardly similar actions can have different moral outcomes according to intention and conduct.

That outcome should not enter Ṣ L D’s lexical definition. The root still concerns hardness and a bare surface; the moral result follows from the network of verses on spending.

6. Comparing related roots

Ṣ L D (ṣald) and Ṣ F W (ṣafw)

The roots approach one another in describing smooth stone.

Ṣ F W also concerns clarity, purity, and freedom from admixture in various uses. Ṣafwān in Al-Baqarah 2:264 denotes a smooth stone.

Ṣ L D applied to stone expresses hardness and smoothness together with dryness or an uncovered surface.

Ṣafwān and ṣald therefore overlap in the verse while belonging to separate roots. The first introduces the stone; the second describes its condition after rain reaches it.

Their co-occurrence does not establish that ṣafā always means hardness or ṣald always conveys spiritual purity.

Study of Ṣ F W

Ṣ L D (ṣald) and Ṣ L B (ṣalb)

Ṣ L B concerns hardness, solidity, and firmness in Arabic.

Ṣald likewise expresses hardness when describing stone, but smoothness, dryness, or lack of yield also features in many uses.

A ṣald stone is thus usually hard, but not every hard thing is necessarily ṣald.

Something can be very solid yet have a rough or covered surface.

The Qur’an also uses Ṣ L B for crucifixion and ṣulb for a bodily region. Those uses must not be reduced to bare stone.

Study of Ṣ L B

Ṣ L D (ṣald) and Q S W (qasā)

Q S W concerns hardness and callousness, including descriptions of hearts in the Qur’an.

Al-Baqarah 2:74 says:

ثُمَّ قَسَتْ قُلُوبُكُمْ مِنْ بَعْدِ ذَٰلِكَ فَهِيَ كَالْحِجَارَةِ أَوْ أَشَدُّ قَسْوَةً

thumma qasat qulūbukum min baʿdi dhālika fa-hiya ka-al-ḥijārati aw ashaddu qaswatan

“Then your hearts hardened after that, becoming like stones or harder still.”

Here stone’s hardness explains the hearts’ callousness.

Ṣald can also denote hard stone, but its lexical meaning is not automatically moral callousness.

Al-Baqarah 2:264 relates bare stone to the loss of spending’s value; Al-Baqarah 2:74 uses stone in a comparison concerning hard hearts.

The images are close, but their subjects and implications differ.

Study of Q S W

Ṣ L D (ṣald) and Ṣ L Ṣ L (ṣalṣala)

The Qur’an uses ṣalṣāl for dry clay with a characteristic sound.

Al-Ḥijr 15:26 says:

وَلَقَدْ خَلَقْنَا الْإِنْسَانَ مِنْ صَلْصَالٍ مِنْ حَمَإٍ مَسْنُونٍ

wa-laqad khalaqnā al-insāna min ṣalṣālin min ḥamaʾin masnūnin

“Indeed, We created the human being from dry, sounding clay, from altered mud.”

Dryness may feature in uses of both families. Ṣ L D and Ṣ L Ṣ L nevertheless remain distinct roots.

Ṣalṣāl concerns a kind of clay and its sound, whereas Qur’anic ṣald describes hard, bare stone.

Similar sounds or a shared association with dryness do not prove a historical relationship.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

The following equivalents suit established forms of the family:

Ṣald: hard and smooth; of a surface, bare and uncovered.

Ḥajarun ṣaldun: a hard, smooth stone.

Arḍun ṣaldun: hard, barren ground.

Ṣalādah: hardness and solidity.

Aṣlad: hard and smooth; of a person, very miserly.

Ṣalūd: hard, resistant, or unproductive, according to subject and use.

Ṣalada al-zandu: the fire-striker produced no spark.

Nāqatun miṣlādun: a she-camel yielding no milk or very little.

In Al-Baqarah 2:264, and leaves it hard and bare suitably renders fa-tarakahu ṣaldan, preserving the stone’s hardness and the exposure of its uncovered surface.

Made it into hard stone is inaccurate: the stone already existed.

Cleaned it is likewise insufficient, since it fails to convey hardness and a bare surface.

Made it fruitless may explain the comparison’s result, but should not replace a direct rendering of ṣaldan.

8. Conclusion

Ṣ L D concerns hardness, dryness, and a smooth surface. Its best-known use describes hard, smooth stone.

The family also supplies words for barren ground, hairless heads, fire-strikers without sparks, animals yielding little milk, and miserly people. Some uses emphasize solidity and dryness; others lack of covering or an expected result.

Their meanings can be related without reducing them all to one unproven cause or historical image.

The Qur’an uses only ṣaldan, describing a stone left hard, smooth, and bare after rain washes away its soil covering in Al-Baqarah 2:264.

Within the spending comparison, this image relates to the moral value lost by charity accompanied by reminders and hurt, or by spending to show off.

Ṣald’s lexical meaning nevertheless concerns hardness and surface characteristics. Ostentation, miserliness, and ruined deeds follow from context or other Arabic uses rather than being invariant components of the root.

Brief root meaning for Forqan:

Hardness, dryness, and a smooth surface; in other uses, lack of covering or failure to yield an expected result or output.

Meaning in Al-Baqarah 2:264:

صَلْدًا (ṣaldan): hard, smooth, and bare; describing the stone whose soil covering the rain has washed away, exposing its uncovered surface.
