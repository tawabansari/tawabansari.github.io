The Qur’anic Root Q Ṣ F

Breaking, shattering, and a powerful, destructive force

Qur’anic forms

قَاصِفًا

Qāṣifan

The sole Qur’anic occurrence; 17:69

Contents

  2. 2. Qāṣif in the Qur’an: a wind that can break things apart

In early Arabic, Q Ṣ F concerns breaking things, especially wood, branches, and spears, as well as a loud, formidable sound.

Classical Arabic lexicons record these meanings, while the Qur’anic occurrence itself attests the sense of destructive, breaking force in the language of revelation. This investigation did not establish an independently dated pre-Islamic witness for the sense of breaking.

Forms examined: قَصَفَ, قَصِفَ, قَصْف, قَصِف, قَاصِف, قَاصِفَة, قَصِيف, قَصَّفَ, تَقَصَّفَ, اِنْقَصَفَ, اِنْقِصَاف, and مَقْصُوف.

Qur’anic form: قَاصِفًا. The root occurs once, in 17:69. The other quoted verses provide context and are not additional occurrences.

1. Core meanings and the material image

In Arabic, qaṣafa l-ʿūda means to break wood, while qaṣafati l-rīḥu l-safīnata means that the wind broke the ship apart.

Qaṣif describes something that breaks readily. Qaṣīf al-shajar denotes broken branches and debris from trees.

Inqaṣafa and taqaṣṣafa likewise denote breaking or being broken apart.

Another recorded use describes plants that have grown so tall that their stems bend under their height.

Labīd ibn Rabīʿah, a poet who lived through both the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, says:

حَتَّى تَزَيَّنَتِ الْجِوَاءُ بِفَاخِرٍ / قَصِفٍ كَأَلْوَانِ الرِّحَالِ عَمِيمِ

Until the low-lying expanses were adorned with luxuriant, tall, bending growth, thick and abundant, like the colours of woven coverings.

Qaṣif here describes tall, bending vegetation. The commentary accompanying the consulted edition of Labīd’s collected poems explains al-riḥāl as woven rugs or coverings. Some lexical transmissions instead read al-rijāl. The precise date of this poem was not independently established; the poet’s lifespan alone does not prove that this line predates the Qur’an.

Qaṣf and a formidable sound

A thick branch makes a sound when it breaks. The association between breaking and a loud sound also helps illustrate uses of this word family; it does not, by itself, establish their historical order.

Raʿdun qāṣifun denotes thunder with a very loud and formidable sound.

Qaṣīf is also used for an intense sound, and qaṣf al-baʿīr for a camel’s roar or the sound of its grinding teeth.

These uses need not refer to something actually breaking. A sound may be so intense that it suggests something being shattered.

Breaking, brittleness, and a formidable sound are therefore prominent meanings of the family, although the historical sequence in which they developed is not certain.

2. Qāṣif in the Qur’an: a wind that can break things apart

أَم أَمِنتُم أَن يُعيدَكُم فيهِ تارَةً أُخرىٰ فَيُرسِلَ عَلَيكُم قاصِفًا مِنَ الرّيحِ فَيُغرِقَكُم بِما كَفَرتُم ثُمَّ لا تَجِدوا لَكُم عَلَينا بِهِ تَبيعًا

Am amintum an yuʿīdakum fīhi tāratan ukhrā fa-yursila ʿalaykum qāṣifan mina l-rīḥi fa-yughriqakum bi-mā kafartum thumma lā tajidū lakum ʿalaynā bihi tabīʿā.

Or do you feel secure that He will not return you to it once more, send against you a shattering wind, and drown you because of your disbelief, after which you will find no one to pursue a claim against Us on your behalf concerning it?

Al-Isra 17:69

Qāṣifan is an active participle from the form I verb qaṣafa and the object of yursila, “send.” Mina l-rīḥ specifies the kind of force being sent.

Here, qāṣif describes a wind powerful enough to break things in its path.

In a maritime setting, such a wind can break sails, masts, and other parts of a ship, raise large waves, and destabilize the vessel. These are illustrations of its possible effects, not details explicitly enumerated in the verse.

The verse places qāṣif alongside its threatened consequence. Qāṣif expresses the wind’s breaking force, while fa-yughriqakum speaks of the people being drowned in its wake.

Drowning is consequently not part of the lexical meaning of qāṣif; it is the consequence described in this context.

Nor does qāṣif identify a particular meteorological class of storm. The verse does not specify its speed, direction, or atmospheric mechanism.

3. Human security and forgetting its source

The preceding verses are important for understanding this occurrence.

وَإِذا مَسَّكُمُ الضُّرُّ فِى البَحرِ ضَلَّ مَن تَدعونَ إِلّا إِيّاهُ فَلَمّا نَجّىٰكُم إِلَى البَرِّ أَعرَضتُم وَكانَ الإِنسٰنُ كَفورًا

Wa-idhā massakumu l-ḍurru fī l-baḥri ḍalla man tadʿūna illā iyyāh. Fa-lammā najjākum ilā l-barri aʿraḍtum. Wa-kāna l-insānu kafūrā.

When harm touches you at sea, those you call upon vanish from your awareness except Him. Yet when He delivers you to land, you turn away. The human being is deeply ungrateful.

Al-Isra 17:67

When confronted with danger, people become more aware of the limits of their power. Once the danger passes, however, they may again behave as though their security and means of living have always been under their own control.

The next verse questions their security on land as well:

أَفَأَمِنتُم أَن يَخسِفَ بِكُم جانِبَ البَرِّ أَو يُرسِلَ عَلَيكُم حاصِبًا ثُمَّ لا تَجِدوا لَكُم وَكيلًا

A-fa-amintum an yakhsifa bikum jāniba l-barri aw yursila ʿalaykum ḥāṣiban thumma lā tajidū lakum wakīlā.

Do you then feel secure that He will not cause the side of the land to swallow you, or send against you a stone-pelting force, after which you will find no protector for yourselves?

Al-Isra 17:68

Al-Isrāʾ 17:68 uses ḥāṣib, while al-Isrāʾ 17:69 uses qāṣif. One evokes pelting with stones; the other evokes a force that breaks things apart.

The same sea presented in 17:66 as a setting for ships to travel and people to seek the bounty of The God can, in other circumstances, become dangerous to human life.

Understanding nature, building sound ships, and anticipating conditions can reduce dangers at sea. Such capabilities do not make human beings independent of the created order.

These verses rebuke the arrogance of assumed security and ingratitude after deliverance from danger.

Bi-mā kafartum in 17:69 explicitly relates the addressees’ conduct to the threatened consequence. It does not warrant declaring every storm at sea or shipwreck a punishment for the passengers’ sins without evidence.

4. Comparison with related roots

Q Ṣ F (qāṣif) and ʿ Ṣ F (ʿāṣif)

In its wind-related branch, ʿaṣf denotes strong, forceful blowing. Qaṣf emphasizes the ability to break and shatter.

Yūnus 10:22 uses rīḥun ʿāṣifun in a sea voyage, while al-Isrāʾ 17:69 uses qāṣifan mina l-rīḥ.

A wind can be both ʿāṣif and qāṣif: its forceful blowing may break things. The intensity of the blowing and its breaking force nevertheless describe distinct aspects of the phenomenon.

Q Ṣ F (qāṣif) and Ḥ Ṣ B (ḥāṣib)

Ḥāṣib describes a force that pelts with pebbles or stones.

Qāṣif expresses the wind’s breaking force without necessarily implying that it carries or throws stones.

Their use in two consecutive verses of al-Isrāʾ makes the distinction between these images clear.

Q Ṣ F (qāṣif) and Ṣ R Ṣ R (ṣarṣar)

Arabic lexicons associate ṣarṣar with severe cold and a piercing, formidable sound.

The Qur’an uses ṣarṣar for the wind sent against ʿĀd in Fuṣṣilat 41:16, al-Qamar 54:19, and al-Ḥāqqah 69:6.

Qāṣif emphasizes the power to break things apart, whereas ṣarṣar foregrounds the wind’s coldness or sound.

A wind may possess all these features, but the words describing them do not mean the same thing.

Q Ṣ F (qaṣf) and Q Ṣ M (qaṣm)

Both roots concern breaking and shattering and are semantically close in Arabic.

Qaṣf is particularly associated with breaking branches and wood and with a wind’s breaking force. Qaṣm also denotes breaking something apart and, figuratively, destroying it or overthrowing its power.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:11 uses qaṣm of wrongdoing settlements, whereas qāṣif in al-Isrāʾ 17:69 describes a formidable wind.

This contextual difference is not an absolute boundary between the two roots throughout Arabic.

Q Ṣ F (qaṣf) and K S R (kasr)

Kasr is a more general Arabic word for breaking something.

Qaṣf belongs to the same semantic field, with brittleness of wood, breaking force, and intense sound prominent in its family.

Kasr need not involve a formidable sound or great force, just as qaṣf in a description of thunder need not indicate the actual breaking of an object.

5. Translation choices for Forqan

Render qāṣifan mina l-rīḥ in 17:69 as “a shattering wind” or “a wind that breaks things apart.”

“A strong wind” conveys part of the sense but does not adequately preserve the distinction between qāṣif and ʿāṣif.

Brief root meaning: Breaking and shattering; brittleness or readiness to break; and, in sound-related uses, an intense, formidable sound such as thunder.

Conclusion

Q Ṣ F concerns breaking, brittleness, and formidable sound.

The Qur’an uses this root only as qāṣif, describing a wind with the power to break things apart.

Qāṣif differs from ʿāṣif, ḥāṣib, and ṣarṣar. ʿĀṣif foregrounds strong blowing, ḥāṣib pelting with stones, ṣarṣar severe cold or a formidable sound, and qāṣif the wind’s breaking force.

In al-Isrāʾ, this image recalls the limits of human power and the dependence of human security on the created order. Security, knowledge, and resources should not cause people to forget the source of these blessings.
