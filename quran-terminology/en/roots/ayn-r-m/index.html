Principal meanings: a dam or water-control embankment; in other branches, intensity and unruliness, accumulation, and certain visible characteristics of things

1. Introduction and meanings in early Arabic

The triliteral root ع ر م has several semantic branches in Arabic. Its Quranic occurrence is الْعَرِمِ in سَيْلَ الْعَرِمِ, referring to a destructive flood in the land of Saba.

Ancient Sabaic evidence is particularly relevant to the word's history. Pre-Islamic inscriptions from southern Arabia use a word with the corresponding consonants ع ر م for a dam, an embankment restraining water, or parts of hydraulic structures. This usage is directly connected with Saba's irrigation system, especially the great dam at Marib. Sabaic is an Ancient South Arabian language; its evidence should be distinguished from a direct attestation in Arabic.

In this use, the Sabaic word corresponding to Arabic عَرِم concerns a structure that holds water, regulates its course, or restrains its spread. “Dam” and “water-control embankment” are therefore historically attested meanings of this related word. The consonantal inscription alone does not establish the precise vowels of the Arabic form.

Classical Arabic also records عَرِم and عَرِمَة for a barrier built across a valley. Another branch concerns intensity, unruliness, harsh conduct, and unrestrained force. عَارِم describes a fierce or unruly person, while عَرَامَة denotes intensity and obstinacy.

The existence of these branches does not mean that one necessarily developed from the other. A semantic association between the force of water and the name of a structure restraining it is conceivable, but the historical evidence does not establish that as the origin of every meaning.

2. Pre-Islamic historical evidence

Unlike many Arabic roots whose pre-Quranic meanings are chiefly investigated through poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period, عَرِم has relevant datable inscriptional evidence.

Sabaic inscriptions concerning the Marib dam use the word corresponding to عرم in accounts of building, maintaining, and repairing water-control works. An inscription from approximately the fourth century CE records damage to the dam. Other inscriptions from the fifth and sixth centuries record repairs to the structure.

These witnesses establish an association between the Sabaic word and water-control structures before the Quran's revelation. In some inscriptional uses, it apparently denotes the earthen part or principal embankment rather than necessarily the entire installation.

The distinction matters because a hydraulic installation can include an embankment, walls, sluices, channels, and other components. A name used for one part may also come to denote the whole in general usage.

Arabic poetry attributed to early poets also places عرم alongside Marib and the building of a dam. In poetry attributed to al-A'sha, for example, the word occurs in a passage about a structure controlling floodwater. The dates of composition and transmission of these witnesses are not equally certain; they should not be equated with dated inscriptions without historical examination.

The evidence supports a clear conclusion: the meaning “dam” has a real pre-Quranic basis in Sabaic. Its Quranic Arabic use may reflect linguistic and cultural contact between northern and southern Arabia, although the precise route of transmission cannot be established with certainty.

3. Semantic branches and forms

The Classical Arabic lexical family ع ر م is broader than its Quranic use. Its main forms can be examined under several branches.

A. Intensity, unruliness, and harshness

In some Arabic uses, عَرِمَ and عَرُمَ refer to fierceness, unruliness, or intensity of conduct.

عَارِم describes someone whose conduct is harsh, fierce, or unruly. عَرَامَة and عُرَام can also denote intensity, obstinacy, and unrestrained behavior.

For example, لِسَانٌ عَارِمٌ is recorded for a harsh, cutting tongue. It concerns the fierceness of speech, not the tongue's physical strength.

This branch also extends to natural forces: lexical sources record عَرِم for an overwhelming flood and for heavy rain.

Those Classical Arabic meanings do not by themselves prove that الْعَرِمِ in the Quran necessarily characterizes the flood's intensity.

B. A dam or water-control structure

In the hydraulic branch, عَرِم and عَرِمَة denote a barrier or embankment built across a valley to restrain or regulate water.

The word is associated with the force of water, but in this reading it names the structure, not necessarily the water's destructive force.

Lexical accounts differ over the number of عَرِم: some treat it as a collective or plural with no singular of the same form, while others give عَرِمَة as its singular. The disagreement does not negate the attested meaning “dam.”

C. Accumulation and heaps

عَرَمَة and related forms are also recorded for a heap of grain, piled produce, or an accumulation of sand.

For example, عَرَمَة can denote grain heaped after threshing and before winnowing is complete.

Here the central image is material gathering into a heap. A conceptual link between a mound of earth and an embankment is possible, but that resemblance alone does not establish that the word for a dam derives directly from a word for a grain heap.

D. Mottling and mixed colors

عَرَم and عُرْمَة are also recorded for a mixture of black and white or contrasting spots of color.

أَعْرَم and عَرْمَاء are forms used for animals with light and dark markings.

This branch should not be forced into the meanings of intensity or a dam merely because the consonants coincide. Identifying distinct branches is as important to historical lexical analysis as investigating possible connections.

4. The Quranic form الْعَرِمِ

The Quran uses this root only in الْعَرِمِ, in Saba 34:16.

The word is in the genitive case as the second member of the construct phrase سَيْلَ الْعَرِمِ.

The phrase thus consists of two nominal elements:

سَيْلَ denotes a flood or abundant flow of water; the sense of الْعَرِمِ must be determined from language and context.

Several readings of the construction warrant consideration:

First, the flood of the dam: the attested Sabaic use supports a flood associated with a dam or water-control works, such as water released from the dam or a flood accompanying its failure.

Second, an overwhelming flood: the Arabic branch of intensity and unruliness supports an understanding of a violent, unrestrained flood. Here عرم conveys a quality of the flood; grammatically it remains the second member of a construct phrase, not an ordinary agreeing adjective modifying سَيْل.

Third, a particular place or structure: some lexical accounts identify عرم as the name of a valley or a specific structure. This is compatible with the geographical setting of Saba's story, but the Quranic phrase alone does not identify a particular named location.

Among these possibilities, this article favors “the flood of the dam”, because the water-control meaning is attested in pre-Quranic inscriptions from the same region. That is a historically supported preference, not a claim that the other recorded readings are linguistically impossible.

This preference does not deny the flood's violence or destructive effects. Its consequences are clear from the context, but they should not without justification become the exclusive meaning of the word itself.

5. سَيْلَ الْعَرِمِ in the story of Saba

Before mentioning the flood, the Quran describes Saba's cultivated and prosperous surroundings:

لَقَدْ كَانَ لِسَبَإٍ فِي مَسْكَنِهِمْ آيَةٌ ۖ جَنَّتَانِ عَنْ يَمِينٍ وَشِمَالٍ ۖ كُلُوا مِنْ رِزْقِ رَبِّكُمْ وَاشْكُرُوا لَهُ ۚ بَلْدَةٌ طَيِّبَةٌ وَرَبٌّ غَفُورٌ

la-qad kāna li-sabaʾin fī maskanihim āyatun jannatāni ʿan yamīnin wa-shimālin kulū min rizqi rabbikum wa-shkurū lahu baldatun ṭayyibatun wa-rabbun ghafūr

Sabaʾ 34:15

Saba 34:15

“There was indeed a sign for Saba in their dwelling place: two gardens, on the right and on the left. Eat from your Lord's provision and give thanks to Him: a good land and a forgiving Lord.”

Cultivation and abundant provision form the story's initial setting. The two gardens on the right and left depict a land with vegetation and agricultural produce.

The next verse describes the change in this condition:

فَأَعْرَضُوا فَأَرْسَلْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ سَيْلَ الْعَرِمِ وَبَدَّلْنَاهُمْ بِجَنَّتَيْهِمْ جَنَّتَيْنِ ذَوَاتَيْ أُكُلٍ خَمْطٍ وَأَثْلٍ وَشَيْءٍ مِنْ سِدْرٍ قَلِيلٍ

fa-aʿraḍū fa-arsalnā ʿalayhim sayla al-ʿarimi wa-baddalnāhum bi-jannatayhim jannatayni dhawātay ukulin khamṭin wa-athlin wa-shayʾin min sidrin qalīl

Sabaʾ 34:16

Saba 34:16

“But they turned away, so We sent upon them the flood of the dam, and replaced their two gardens with two gardens bearing bitter fruit, tamarisk, and a few lote trees.”

The verse connects two events: the sending of the flood and the replacement of productive gardens with gardens of different and less valuable produce and vegetation.

If الْعَرِمِ is understood as the dam, the image is clear. A community enjoying water and flourishing gardens encountered a flood that transformed that productive environment.

The Quran's wording must nevertheless be distinguished from possible historical details. The verse mentions the flood of al-ʿarim, but does not name the Marib dam or specify the event's date, the structure's dimensions, how it failed, or how long the flood lasted.

Historical and exegetical reports include stories about a rodent perforating the dam and other details of its destruction. Such accounts should not enter the lexical study as though they were the verse's own meaning.

Independent historical evidence establishes the existence of dams and irrigation networks in Saba. An association with a water-control system is therefore historically plausible. Identifying the Quranic event conclusively with a particular dated breach of the Marib dam still requires further evidence.

Another distinction is important: فَأَعْرَضُوا at the beginning of the verse belongs to ع ر ض, not ع ر م. Its superficial resemblance to الْعَرِمِ does not establish a derivational connection.

The following verse, Saba 34:17, connects the consequence with their ingratitude. That connection does not establish a specific technical error in dam maintenance. The moral explanation given in the Quran and the physical mechanism of the flood are distinguishable questions.

6. Distinctions from related roots

ع ر م (ʿarim) and س ي ل (sayl)

سَيْل, from س ي ل, concerns flowing water, especially an abundant flow.

In the historical reading preferred here, عَرِم names a structure used to restrain and regulate water.

In سَيْلَ الْعَرِمِ, one element denotes the flow, and the other associates it with the dam. There is therefore no reason to treat عرم simply as another word for “flood.”

ع ر م (ʿarim) and س د د (sadd)

The root س د د concerns closing, blocking, and forming a barrier. سَدّ may denote an obstruction that prevents passage or penetration.

In historical Sabaic usage, the word corresponding to عرم is associated with water-control structures.

This does not mean that سَدّ cannot denote a dam. Rather, سَدّ has a broader range involving barriers and closure, whereas عرم in the evidence discussed here has a specific hydraulic use.

ع ر م (ʿarim) and غ ر ق (gharaq)

The root غ ر ق concerns sinking or drowning in water or another liquid.

Drowning may result from a flood, but “flood” does not necessarily mean that everyone or everything drowned. Saba 34:16 describes flooding and the transformation of gardens, not the drowning of every inhabitant.

A possible result of an event should not be inserted into a word that does not itself express that result.

7. Translation proposal for Forqan

The principal proposal for الْعَرِمِ in Saba 34:16 is “the dam.”

Thus the phrase:

سَيْلَ الْعَرِمِ

sayla al-ʿarim

Sabaʾ 34:16

May be translated:

“The flood of the dam”

This rendering preserves the historically attested water-control sense while expressing the association between flood and dam without adding a verb or event absent from the phrase.

“An overwhelming flood” is also a possible interpretation within Arabic's semantic range. This article prefers the dam reading in light of the Sabaic inscriptional evidence.

“The flood caused by the dam's collapse” may explain one possible mechanism, but is unsuitable as a direct rendering: سَيْلَ الْعَرِمِ contains no word meaning “breaking” or “collapsing.”

Suggested wording for a Forqan word box:

Word: الْعَرِمِ

Root: ع ر م

Brief root meaning: A dam or water-control embankment; in another branch, intensity and unruliness.

Meaning in this verse: The dam with which the flood is associated, in the reading preferred here.

Conclusion

The Arabic root ع ر م has several semantic branches, including dams and water-control embankments, intensity and unruliness, accumulation, and certain visible characteristics. These should not be reduced to one original meaning without sufficient evidence.

A notable feature is the pre-Quranic inscriptional evidence for the water-control meaning. The related Sabaic word occurs in connection with hydraulic structures and the irrigation works of Saba.

The Quran uses only الْعَرِمِ, in Saba 34:16. In light of that background, سَيْلَ الْعَرِمِ plausibly refers to a flood associated with a dam, a flood that transformed the region's productive gardens.

This article therefore proposes “the flood of the dam” as Forqan's basic rendering. It preserves the word's historical basis while avoiding unestablished details about the flood's mechanism and date, and leaves the other recorded interpretations open to separate examination.
