Root H L ʿ — Impatience and grasping desire

Qur’anic and lexical forms

هَلُوعًا
(halūʿan)
Intensely restless and acquisitive; adjective

From distress under hardship and attachment to possessions to governing human responses

The Arabic triliteral root ه ل ع concerns impatience, limited endurance, grasping desire, and intense responses to favorable or adverse circumstances. A halūʿ person readily loses composure under distress, yet may cling so tightly to good fortune that others are denied their share.

These behaviors initially seem different: agitation under hardship and possessive withholding amid plenty. Both involve an unbalanced response to perceived personal gain or loss.

The root occurs once in the Qur’an as هَلُوعًا. The next two verses explain it through جَزُوعًا and مَنُوعًا; an exception then describes people whose practices counter these tendencies.

Halaʿ thus names more than fear or worry: it is impatience and grasping desire influencing behavior across differing circumstances.

1. The root in early Arabic

Classical Arabic records two principal senses: intense impatience under adversity and strong grasping desire for what one wants.

هَلِعَ يَهْلَعُ describes becoming deeply agitated, quickly losing endurance, or showing intense covetousness.

هَلُوع describes someone in whom such qualities are pronounced.

Some lexical accounts describe halaʿ as more intense than jazaʿ; others include greed, narrowness, miserliness, and impatience.

Jazaʿ primarily concerns responses to suffering; halaʿ can include both distress under adversity and one’s response to benefit or abundance.

This wider range also appears in the Qur’an.

A pre-Qur’anic poetic witness

Poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Musayyab ibn ʿAlas includes, in a description associated with a swift mount:

«صَكَّاءَ ذِعْلِبَةٍ إِذَا اسْتَدْبَرْتَهَا
حَرَجٍ إِذَا اسْتَقْبَلْتَهَا هِلْوَاعِ»

Ṣakkāʾa dhiʿlibatin idhā stadbartahā / ḥarajin idhā staqbaltahā hilwāʿi.

“Slender and agile when viewed from behind, narrow when faced from the front, swift and light in movement.”

هِلْوَاع describes agility and quick, lively movement. Lexical explanations discuss a camel and an ostrich to which the poet compares his mount.

This illustrates an animal-motion branch alongside the human senses of fear and greed.

The line survives among transmitted poems of al-Musayyab ibn ʿAlas. Its attribution has literary support, though its exact date is not independently established beyond later transmission.

It does not directly prove the moral meaning of Qur’anic halūʿ. Hilwāʿ describes an animal’s movement, not human attachment to wealth or impatience in misfortune.

It does show an early traditional association with rapid, restless movement.

Halaʿ in human speech and behavior

Lexicons record رَجُلٌ هَلِعٌ and رَجُلٌ هَلُوعٌ for a readily impatient person with grasping desires.

ذِئْبٌ هُلَعٌ بُلَعٌ describes a greedy, voracious wolf: هُلَع concerns greed, while بُلَع concerns swallowing and eating.

Halaʿ can therefore concern an intense wish to obtain something, not only fear of losing it.

Some regional Arabic usages record sorrow; related forms have also been reported for hunger and swift, light animal movement.

Their historical support is uneven. Many are recorded in post-Qur’anic lexicons and cannot all be confidently assigned to pre-Islamic speech without independent evidence.

The Qur’an itself is an early, clearly datable witness to human halūʿ as impatient and grasping; the following verses explain it specifically.

2. Forms and derivatives

The root occurs once in the Qur’an:

هَلُوعًا — al-Maʿārij 70:19

The faʿūl pattern describes someone marked by impatience and grasping desire.

Other Arabic forms include:

  • هَلِعَ يَهْلَعُ: becoming impatient, unable to endure, or greedy.
  • هَلَع: a verbal noun for intense impatience or grasping desire.
  • هُلُوع: another recorded verbal noun for this condition.
  • هَلِع: impatient, lacking endurance, or greedy.
  • هَالِع: an active participle, someone experiencing halaʿ.
  • هَلُوع: someone prominently characterized by impatience and greed.
  • هِلْوَاع and هِلْوَاعَة: in some older uses, a swift, agile, or readily stirred animal.

Only هَلُوعًا occurs in the Qur’an.

Faʿūl can emphasize intensity or prominence, but the pattern alone does not establish permanence or inability to change.

The Qur’an itself makes an exception after describing the human being as halūʿ.

3. Qur’anic usage — The human being created halūʿ

Al-Maʿārij 70:19

«إِنَّ الْإِنسَانَ خُلِقَ هَلُوعًا»

Inna l-insāna khuliqa halūʿā.

“Indeed, the human being was created impatient and grasping.”

الْإِنسَانَ is a general designation of humanity, not merely one historical people.

خُلِقَ is a passive perfect from خ ل ق, expressing being created.

هَلُوعًا is an accusative description of the human being’s condition.

“Impatient and grasping” uses two related equivalents to convey one Arabic word, not two separate Arabic adjectives.

The next two verses clarify it further.

Al-Maʿārij 70:20

«إِذَا مَسَّهُ الشَّرُّ جَزُوعًا»

Idhā massahu sh-sharru jazūʿā.

“When adversity touches him, deeply impatient.”

This is the first manifestation of halaʿ.

الشَّرُّ here is something adverse or harmful, not exclusively sin or moral wickedness.

Illness, poverty, loss of possessions, threats, failure, and other misfortunes can illustrate such adversity.

جَزُوعًا belongs to ج ز ع and denotes impatience and lost endurance under suffering.

A halūʿ person readily becomes disturbed by unwanted circumstances, potentially losing measured judgment and balance under fear or distress.

For example, financial difficulty may cause someone to make rash decisions, blame others, or neglect responsibilities instead of considering solutions.

Concern itself is natural; its ungoverned expression approaches the impatience discussed here.

Not every tear, sorrow, or fear is halaʿ. Deep grief can coexist with responsible conduct.

The verse emphasizes intense impatience and limited endurance under adversity.

Al-Maʿārij 70:21

«وَإِذَا مَسَّهُ الْخَيْرُ مَنُوعًا»

Wa-idhā massahu l-khayru manūʿā.

“And when good touches him, withholding.”

This is the second manifestation.

Previously distressed by what he wanted to avoid, the person now clings to what he possesses and withholds it.

الْخَيْرُ is broad, not confined to money. Property, opportunity, ability, knowledge, health, and other benefits can be instances in appropriate contexts.

مَنُوعًا comes from م ن ع and concerns preventing or withholding something.

Instead of properly using the good received and respecting others’ shares, the person keeps it to himself.

Someone who constantly complained of poverty may, on gaining wealth, refuse even the needy their due: impatience before possession becomes grasping refusal afterward.

These are two expressions of one problem: agitation at loss or possible loss, and attachment in prosperity that obstructs just conduct.

Not every refusal to give is blameworthy. People also owe duties to themselves, their families, and dependents. The problem is greed preventing fulfillment of rights or appropriate good action.

How the three verses relate

Al-Maʿārij 70:19 states the general description; al-Maʿārij 70:20 and al-Maʿārij 70:21 explain its two manifestations.

Halaʿ is thus more than an emotion: it affects responses in two differing situations.

Adversity brings impatience; possession brings grasping and withholding.

Both concern the person’s relationship to personal gain and loss.

The halūʿ person loses endurance before the unwanted and the ability to let go and respect others’ rights before the desired.

This account follows from all three verses; every detail is not necessarily the independent lexical meaning of halūʿ in every Arabic context.

4. Is halaʿ unchangeable?

The verse says the human being was created halūʿ. This should not be erased or replaced with “society makes people halūʿ”: the verse speaks of human creation and disposition.

Does that mean people must remain impatient, greedy, and withholding throughout life?

The Qur’an provides an answer in the following exception.

Al-Maʿārij 70:22

«إِلَّا الْمُصَلِّينَ»

Illā l-muṣallīn.

“Except those who observe ṣalāh.”

إِلَّا introduces an exception. If halūʿ were an inescapable destiny, excluding some people and describing their differing conduct would be difficult to explain.

Al-Maʿārij 70:23, al-Maʿārij 70:24, al-Maʿārij 70:25, al-Maʿārij 70:26, al-Maʿārij 70:27, al-Maʿārij 70:28, al-Maʿārij 70:29, al-Maʿārij 70:30, al-Maʿārij 70:31, al-Maʿārij 70:32, al-Maʿārij 70:33, and al-Maʿārij 70:34 describe their qualities: constancy in ṣalāh, a recognized share in wealth for the asking and deprived, belief in the Day of Recompense, fear of their Lord’s punishment, sexual responsibility, trustworthiness, fidelity to covenants, upright testimony, and guarding ṣalāh.

These qualities are meaningfully connected to the discussion of halaʿ.

Worship and attentiveness to conduct can cultivate endurance; recognizing the deprived person’s due counters keeping everything for oneself; commitment to accountability and justice prevents fear or self-interest from becoming the only standards of decision.

Halaʿ is therefore a tendency to recognize and govern through attention to one’s responses.

The verses do not deny natural inclinations toward self-preservation, avoiding pain, or retaining possessions. Such inclinations can be beneficial in their proper place.

The problem arises when they overstep their bounds and become impatience, greed, or wrongful withholding.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:28 also speaks of humans being created weak. Weakness does not cancel responsibility or make reform impossible; neither does halūʿ remove agency or self-cultivation.

Al-Shams 91:9 and al-Shams 91:10 contrast the outcomes of purifying the self and corrupting it.

A created tendency must therefore be distinguished from responsibility for cultivating or restraining it.

5. Comparison with nearby roots

ه ل ع and ج ز ع — A broader disposition and distress under suffering

Jazaʿ concerns impatience and loss of endurance in the Qur’an.

Al-Maʿārij 70:20 presents it as one manifestation of halaʿ.

They are closely related but not identical.

Jazaʿ focuses on hardship and misfortune. Halaʿ in al-Maʿārij additionally includes grasping and withholding amid good fortune.

Ibrāhīm 14:21 contrasts jazaʿ with ṣabr, highlighting endurance.

“Intense distress” alone therefore misses the withholding side of Qur’anic halaʿ.

ه ل ع and م ن ع — Grasping and withholding good

Manʿ denotes preventing or withholding something.

مَنُوعًا in al-Maʿārij 70:21 describes the halūʿ person when good comes.

Not all prevention is bad: preventing injustice or harm and protecting rights can be good. Its value depends on its object and context.

Here it concerns greed and wrongful withholding of good.

Halaʿ is the broader disposition; withholding is one possible behavior arising from it.

ه ل ع and ش ح ح — Greed and narrowness

Shuḥḥ concerns intense greed, possessiveness, and difficulty relinquishing something.

It is close to the second side of halaʿ: withholding in prosperity.

Al-Ḥashr 59:9 and al-Taghābun 64:16 link protection from the self’s shuḥḥ with success.

Halaʿ is not limited to miserliness. Al-Maʿārij 70:20 also describes distress under hardship, even where giving wealth is not at issue.

Shuḥḥ emphasizes possessive greed; Qur’anic halaʿ encompasses unbalanced responses to both adversity and prosperity.

ه ل ع and ح ر ص — Grasping desire and intense eagerness

Ḥirṣ concerns strongly wanting something and striving to attain it.

Such eagerness is not necessarily blameworthy.

Al-Tawbah 9:128 describes Muhammad as حَرِيصٌ عَلَيْكُمْ toward his addressees, expressing earnest concern for their good and guidance.

Intensity of desire alone is therefore not halaʿ.

In halaʿ, desire is coupled with limited endurance and blameworthy withholding. The issue is the response to having or lacking something.

ه ل ع and ص ب ر — Impatience and self-restraint

Ṣabr concerns restraining oneself, remaining steadfast, and preventing uncontrolled responses.

Against jazaʿ, it helps preserve direction and conduct under hardship.

It counters one side of halaʿ, but does not by itself explain the possessive withholding side.

Endurance in hardship must be accompanied by respect for others’ rights, generosity, and responsibility in prosperity.

Al-Maʿārij 70:24 and al-Maʿārij 70:25 state that financial responsibility specifically.

For a fuller account of ṣabr and its distinction from passive endurance, see Forqan’s separate study of the root ص ب ر.

ه ل ع and ه ط ع — Impatience and hasty approach

These roots resemble one another in shape and sound but are independent.

Qur’anic ه ط ع concerns hasty approach or intense attention toward a person or destination.

ه ل ع in al-Maʿārij 70:19 concerns human impatience and grasping desire.

A worried halūʿ person may act hastily, but not every fast movement arises from halaʿ. Halaʿ can also appear in withholding wealth or inner agitation without rapid bodily movement.

Phonetic similarity and possible haste do not merge the roots.

6. Interpretive challenges and suggested translations

One familiar equivalent rarely conveys both sides of هَلُوعًا.

“Impatient” emphasizes distress and limited endurance under hardship.

“Grasping” emphasizes strong attachment to desired or possessed things.

“Miserly” covers only withholding, and is insufficient for the whole meaning.

“Worried” or “anxious” is also incomplete: worry can coexist with patience, generosity, and responsibility.

For al-Maʿārij 70:19, the suggested translation is:

“Indeed, the human being was created impatient and grasping.”

It preserves two important aspects; the study and following verses explain their relationship.

A single-word rendering such as “impatient” can obscure the withholding aspect unless al-Maʿārij 70:21 remains in view.

جَزُوعًا in al-Maʿārij 70:20 suits “deeply impatient” or “unable to endure.”

مَنُوعًا in al-Maʿārij 70:21 can be “withholding,” explained as refusing to let others benefit from the good received.

The suggested translations are therefore:

Al-Maʿārij 70:19: “Indeed, the human being was created impatient and grasping.”

Al-Maʿārij 70:20: “When adversity touches him, deeply impatient.”

Al-Maʿārij 70:21: “And when good touches him, withholding.”

The explanation should show that halaʿ affects practical conduct as well as feelings.

خُلِقَ هَلُوعًا does not remove responsibility; subsequent verses describe concrete ways these tendencies can be governed.

7. Conclusion

ه ل ع concerns impatience, limited endurance, greed, and intense responses to favorable and adverse conditions.

Older witnesses also record swift animal movement, but that branch alone does not determine halūʿ’s moral meaning.

The root occurs once in the Qur’an as هَلُوعًا.

Al-Maʿārij 70:19 gives the general description; al-Maʿārij 70:20 explains distress under adversity, and al-Maʿārij 70:21 withholding amid good fortune.

Qur’anic halaʿ is more than a passing feeling: it is a tendency capable of unbalancing responses and behavior.

Forqan definition: ه ل ع describes intense impatience and grasping desire that produce distress under adversity and withholding or narrowness when good comes. These are different manifestations of one tendency; neither alone exhausts halūʿ.

The Qur’an presents this tendency within human creation but not as unchangeable destiny. Al-Maʿārij 70:22 and the subsequent qualities show the possibility of countering it through steadfastness, self-watchfulness, rights, and responsibility.

Understanding halaʿ identifies two situations especially demanding self-restraint: losing or fearing loss, and obtaining something while refusing others any share.
