The Qur’anic Root Q W S

The bow, curvature, and bending; measuring one thing against another

Qur’anic forms

1. Fundamental meaning and linguistic background

Arabic Q W S concerns the bow, curvature and bending, and measuring something against a familiar standard.

Poetry transmitted from early poets uses this family for bows and a bent human posture. Attribution and precise dating should be distinguished from the meanings that lexicographers illustrate through the verses. Attribution to a pre-Islamic poet does not itself independently date a verse.

In classical Arabic, qaws names a curved implement for shooting arrows. A meaning of “cubit” is also recorded, and the word is used in measuring distance. No independent, datable pre-Qur’anic witness for the specific cubit sense is established here.

Ibn Fāris makes measuring one thing against another the family’s core and connects its wāw and yāʾ forms. The historical order in which bow, curvature, and measurement developed is nevertheless uncertain.

Qur’anic form: قَوْسَيْنِ (qawsayni), the genitive dual of qaws: two bows; once, Al-Najm 53:9.

Arabic qaws means bow; aqwās and qisiyy are plurals; qawwasa means making curved or becoming bent; taqawwus is curvature; and aqwas means bent. The measurement branch records both wāw and yāʾ verb forms. Qiyās should not be labeled simply a wāw derivative without explaining that connection. The Qur’an uses only the dual of qaws from this family.

2. Physical imagery and early Arabic evidence

The bow in Arab life

Bows were familiar hunting and military implements. Arabic poets described their wood, curved shape, color, strength, and sound.

A verse attributed to Ṣakhr al-Ghayy al-Hudhalī describes a bow; caution is needed over the precise period of his life and composition:

وَسَمْحَةٍ مِنْ قِسِيِّ زَارَةَ حَمْرَاءَ هَتُوفٍ عِدَادُهَا غَرِدُ

“And a supple, handy bow among the bows of Zārah, red and resonant, with a ringing voice.”

Some versions describe the bow as yellow rather than red.

Qisiyy is a plural of qaws, and the bow is associated with Zārah in the verse. The name’s exact identity is not necessary for understanding “bow” here; it should not be identified as a tribe without clear evidence.

The witness supports qaws and its plural as names for the shooting implement in early poetic tradition, although the exact date of composition is unknown.

Curvature and bending

A bow’s curved shape provides an image for describing curved objects and human posture with this family.

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays says:

أَرَاهُنَّ لَا يُحْبِبْنَ مَنْ قَلَّ مَالُهُ وَلَا مَنْ رَأَيْنَ الشَّيْبَ فِيهِ وَقَوَّسَا

“I see that they do not love one whose wealth has diminished, nor one in whom they have seen gray hair and who has grown bent.”

Qawwasa here refers to a bent human posture. The poet describes an aged person with a form from this family.

Shaykhun aqwasu describes a bent old man, while taqawwasa l-shayʾu means that something became curved or bow-shaped.

Derivatives also describe the curve of a crescent moon and an eyebrow.

Modern Arabic uses qaws for curved forms in geometry, architecture, and other fields.

Measurement and comparison

Arabic records two closely related verb forms:

«قَاسَ يَقُوسُ» (qāsa yaqūsu) and «قَاسَ يَقِيسُ» (qāsa yaqīsu).

Both are used to measure or assess something against something else.

قِسْتُ الشَّيْءَ بِغَيْرِهِ (qistu l-shayʾa bi-ghayrihi) means that I measured or assessed something using another thing as the standard.

Arabic also says:

«بَيْنِي وَبَيْنَهُ قِيسُ رُمْحٍ» (baynī wa-baynahu qīsu rumḥin)

That is, a spear’s length separates us.

In everyday life, people used body parts and familiar objects—handspans, cubits, spears, and bows—to convey length and distance before standardized measuring instruments were available.

Lexical sources also record qaws as a cubit. Ibn Fāris even connects the bow’s name to the measurement branch.

That is one lexical explanation of the relationship among meanings, not proof that the word for bow necessarily developed from a unit of measurement.

3. Qur’anic usage of qawsayn

The root occurs near the beginning of Al-Najm, in the account of receiving revelation and what Muhammad saw.

ثُمَّ دَنا فَتَدَلّىٰ

Thumma danā fa-tadallā.

“Then he drew near and descended.”

An-Najm 53:8

فَكانَ قابَ قَوسَينِ أَو أَدنىٰ

Fa-kāna qāba qawsayni aw adnā.

“So he was at a distance of two bows’ measure, or nearer.”

An-Najm 53:9

The bow serves here as a standard for expressing distance.

Three words from different root entries contribute:

  • Qāb, indexed under Q W B: a measure or extent of distance, with disagreement about its historical origin.
  • Qawsayn, from Q W S: two bows.
  • Adnā, from D N W: nearer.

Qāb gives the measure, qawsayn its standard, and adnā greater proximity. “Or nearer” should not be reduced to the speaker’s uncertainty; one grammatical explanation presents the measure from an observer’s perspective.

The subject is not shooting or using a bow. A familiar bow makes the scale of the distance tangible.

Morphology and syntax

Qawsayni is the dual of the noun qaws.

The nominative dual is qawsāni; the accusative and genitive are qawsayni.

Qāba is an accusative noun and the first term of a construct phrase. It has been analyzed as a distance adverbial attached to an omitted predicate of kāna, or as kāna’s predicate with a measure of proximity understood. The direct-predicate analysis is not the only one. Qawsayni is the genitive second term, marked by yāʾ because it is dual. This account agrees with the Q W B study.

In the direct construction, qāb is singular and qawsayn dual.

Two bows or two cubits?

Early lexical and exegetical accounts offer several interpretations of the distance standard.

On the familiar meaning of qaws, the phrase describes a distance of two bows’ measure.

Others, drawing on the recorded cubit sense, interpret it as two cubits.

Another reading relies on qāb as the span from a bow’s grip to one tip, taking the expression to mean the two sections of one bow. Some early explanations also discuss the distance between the string and the middle of the bow.

These readings share the idea of a short distance and close proximity.

Given qaws’s ordinary meaning and the phrase’s direct structure, “two bows’ measure” is preferable. The cubit sense remains open to discussion because it has lexical support.

The Qur’an does not specify the bow’s dimensions or measurement method. Neither conversion into a fixed number of meters nor a geometrical theory based on its shape follows from the verse itself.

4. Understanding qāba qawsayn

After describing the approach and distance, the Qur’an says:

فَأَوحىٰ إِلىٰ عَبدِهِ ما أَوحىٰ

Fa-awḥā ilā ʿabdihi mā awḥā.

“Then he revealed to His servant what he revealed.”

An-Najm 53:10

Qāba qawsayn occurs in a context of revelation and seeing. The image conveys proximity in terms familiar to the original audience.

Naming a bow does not identify who approached or determine the nature of the proximity. These questions require the pronouns, verbs, Al-Najm’s opening context, and other revelation verses.

In some mystical writings, qāba qawsayn became a term for a degree of spiritual proximity. The two bows have also been interpreted as two worlds, two levels of being, or paths of descent and ascent.

These are interpretive or technical usages, not lexical meanings of qaws.

The dual qawsayn alone does not prove two worlds or two spiritual levels. Such readings need evidence independent of the word’s meaning.

5. Qiyās and the development of usage

Lexicographical sources connect the wāw forms of Q W S and the yāʾ forms of Q Y S in measurement and assessment.

Qiyās means measuring or comparing one thing with another; miqyās means a standard or measuring instrument.

Comparison can be physical or mental. Measuring one cloth against another is physical measurement; examining two situations for shared and differing features is mental comparison.

Qiyās later became a technical term in logic and legal theory.

In legal theory, it denotes extending a ruling from one case to another on the basis of an identified common rationale.

The Qur’an does not use the word qiyās. The sole Qur’anic occurrence of Q W S is the noun qawsayn in Al-Najm.

The absence of the technical term alone does not settle the value of every kind of comparative reasoning. The Qur’an itself often uses comparisons and examples to clarify truth.

Comparing things to understand them differs, however, from extending a religious ruling on a humanly identified rationale. The legislative validity of qiyās is a separate question, not established simply by this lexical family’s meaning.

6. Comparison with related roots

Q W S and Q W B: the standard and the measure of distance

These two root entries occur together in Al-Najm 53:9.

Qaws names the bow and supplies the distance standard; qāb expresses the measure or extent.

As in “two handspans’ measure,” where the handspan supplies the standard, each word in qāba qawsayn has a distinct semantic role.

Qāb should therefore not be translated simply as “bow,” nor qaws simply as “distance.”

Qiyās in the wāw and yāʾ forms, and Q D R: comparing and determining measure

Qadr covers measure, amount, limits, capacity, and ability.

Qiyās in the measurement branch emphasizes determining a thing’s measure or characteristics by comparison with another standard.

For example, the amount of water in a vessel is its qadr; assessing it with a vessel of known capacity is a kind of qiyās.

The families overlap in measurement, but not every meaning of qadr, such as power and authority, is present in qiyās.

Q W S and Dh R ʿ: the bow and a unit of length

Dhirāʿ names a familiar part of the arm and a unit based on its length.

Describing a chain in the hereafter, the Qur’an says:

ثُمَّ فى سِلسِلَةٍ ذَرعُها سَبعونَ ذِراعًا فَاسلُكوهُ

Thumma fī silsilatin dharʿuhā sabʿūna dhirāʿan fa-slukūh.

“Then insert him into a chain whose measure is seventy cubits.”

Al-Haqqah 69:32

The cubit is explicitly used here as a unit.

Qaws could also denote a cubit or a standard of measurement in some Arabic usages, but its familiar primary designation is a bow.

The recorded cubit sense does not require replacing qawsayn with “two cubits” in translation.

Q W S and D N W: a distance standard and nearness

Dunūw denotes approaching and a reduction in distance.

Qaws in Al-Najm supplies a standard for describing the distance resulting from the approach.

Nearness is not an independent meaning of qaws; it follows from qāba qawsayn and adnā in context.

7. Translation choices for Forqan

“Two bows” is proposed for qawsayn in Al-Najm 53:9.

The proposed verse translation is:

“So he was at a distance of two bows’ measure, or nearer.”

“At a distance of two cubits or less” is defensible on one lexical reading, but is better discussed as an explanatory interpretation.

“Very near” conveys the general proximity but omits the bow image and the measurement standard.

Outside the Qur’an, qaws can be translated as bow, taqawwus as curvature or bending, qiyās as comparison or measurement, and miqyās as a standard of measurement.

8. Conclusion

Arabic Q W S concerns bows, curvature, and bending, and, in the connected wāw and yāʾ measurement forms, measuring one thing against another.

The Qur’an uses the dual of qaws to describe proximity in a revelation context. The bow functions as a distance standard, distinct from its role as a physical implement.

Technical mystical meanings of qāba qawsayn and legal-theoretical meanings of qiyās should not replace the direct meaning of the Qur’anic word.

Definition for Forqan: a bow, curvature, and a bow-like shape; in the measurement branch, assessing one thing against another as a standard. The direct Qur’anic meaning of qawsayn is two bows.
