Core meaning: becoming thirsty and feeling a need for water; in another branch of the family, the interval between two occasions of drinking.

1. The root in early Arabic

The triliteral root ظ م أ concerns thirst and the need for water. The verb ẓamiʾa means “became thirsty,” the verbal noun ẓamaʾ means “thirst,” and ẓamʾān describes someone who is thirsty.

In human and animal life, thirst is a state associated with the body's need for water and a desire to drink. Heat, physical activity, lack of water, or a long interval since the last drink may bring it about.

Alongside the feeling of thirst, early Arabic used ẓimʾ for the interval between two occasions of drinking. This mattered particularly in pastoral and nomadic life, where bringing camels and other livestock to water was essential.

Two related concepts should therefore be distinguished:

Ẓamaʾ refers to thirst itself: the state of needing water.

Ẓimʾ refers to the interval between drinking or watering occasions. Thirst may accompany that interval, but the word here names the interval itself.

The forms of this family should consequently not all be translated simply as “thirst” without attention to their vowels and context.

Evidence from pre-Islamic poetry

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā says:

حِيَاضُ الْمَنَايَا لَيْسَ عَنْهَا مُزَحْزَحٌ

ḥiyāḍu al-manāyā laysa ʿanhā muzaḥzaḥun

فَمُنْتَظِرٌ ظِمْأً كَآخَرَ وَارِدِ

fa-muntaẓirun ẓimʾan ka-ākhara wāridi

Translation: No one can be kept away from the watering places of death; one awaiting the next watering turn is like another who has arrived.

The poet likens death to a watering place everyone eventually reaches. Some arrive sooner and others await their turn, but none can escape arrival.

Ẓimʾ here denotes the interval between watering occasions. The waiting person need not be suffering from thirst; the image concerns a turn that has not yet arrived.

Wārid belongs to و ر د and denotes someone arriving at a watering place.

The line occurs in the ode “Laqad awratha al-ʿAbsiyyu majdan muʾaththalan,” attributed to Zuhayr. That attribution supplies evidence for an early use of ẓimʾ, although the line's precise date is not independently established.

The witness shows that, besides the feeling of thirst, this family was associated with intervals between watering livestock.

An animal description with disputed root attribution

Another verse attributed to Zuhayr reads:

وَإِنْ مَالَا لِوَعْثٍ خَاذَمَتْهُ

wa-in mālā li-waʿthin khādhamat-hu

بِأَلْوَاحٍ مَفَاصِلُهَا ظِمَاءُ

bi-alwāḥin mafāṣiluhā ẓimāʾu

The poetic description concerns an animal whose joints are lean and carry little flesh.

Ẓimāʾ describes the joints here. A joint does not literally drink or become thirsty; the description concerns leanness rather than a feeling of thirst.

Its root attribution is disputed. Some lexicographers connect the expression with the hamzated family, while Ibn Barrī assigns this usage to the weak-final root ظ م ي. It is therefore not an undisputed witness for ظ م أ.

The line is transmitted in Zuhayr's poetry and lexical works; some versions read khāzamat-hu instead of khādhamat-hu. That variation does not determine the meaning of ẓimāʾ in the second hemistich.

These examples distinguish thirst, the interval between watering occasions, and a description of lean joints whose root assignment is disputed. The last should be treated as a comparative witness.

Nevertheless, the root's direct Quranic meaning is thirst. Other usages and disputed affiliations should be considered separately and not imported into verses without evidence.

2. The word family and extensions of meaning

Important forms include ẓamiʾa, yaẓmaʾu, ẓamaʾ, ẓamāʾ, ẓamāʾa, ẓamʾān, ẓamʾā, ẓimāʾ, ẓimʾ, aẓmāʾ, aẓmaʾa, and miẓmāʾ.

Ẓamiʾa: becoming thirsty

Ẓamiʾa is the past-tense verb; yaẓmaʾu is its imperfect.

Ẓamiʾa al-rajulu means “the man became thirsty.”

The verb denotes the state itself, without necessarily specifying its intensity, duration, or cause.

Someone may become thirsty after exercise, exposure to heat, or time without drinking. The verb covers these situations without making any one of them a necessary cause.

Ẓamaʾ: thirst

Ẓamaʾ is a verbal noun naming the state of thirst.

Lexical works explain it through ʿaṭash, but differ over its intensity.

Some describe it as milder thirst, while others use it for intense thirst.

That disagreement cautions against making a particular degree of intensity an invariant part of the meaning.

The clear core is needing water and being thirsty. The person's circumstances and the surrounding language establish intensity.

A desert traveler long without water may be extremely thirsty. The intensity comes from those circumstances, rather than from ẓamaʾ necessarily meaning severe thirst in every use.

Ẓamʾān: a thirsty person

Ẓamʾān is an adjective on the faʿlān pattern, describing someone in a state of thirst.

Its feminine is ẓamʾā, and ẓimāʾ is a recorded plural.

It resembles ʿaṭshān in meaning and in its pattern for describing a state.

Ẓamʾān may describe a thirsty human or animal. Figuratively, it may also describe someone longing to attain something.

Its primary meaning, however, concerns wanting water, rather than merely being interested in anything desirable.

Aẓmaʾa: causing thirst

Aẓmaʾa is a Form IV verb meaning to make someone or something thirsty.

Its difference from ẓamiʾa is clear in the subject's role.

With ẓamiʾa, the person or animal becomes thirsty.

With aẓmaʾahu, an agent causes that person's thirst.

Prolonged activity in heat, for example, may make a person thirsty.

Taẓmiʾa is also recorded for causing thirst. It is the verbal noun of the Form II verb ẓammaʾa; the verbal noun of Form IV aẓmaʾa is iẓmāʾ.

These forms do not occur in the Quran, but illustrate the distinction between entering a state and causing it within this word family.

Miẓmāʾ: frequently becoming thirsty

Miẓmāʾ is recorded for someone or an animal that often becomes thirsty.

The form here conveys a recurring characteristic or a pronounced susceptibility to thirst.

It is consequently not identical to ẓamʾān. The latter can describe someone thirsty now; miẓmāʾ may describe someone habitually quick or frequent to become thirsty.

This form also does not occur in the Quran.

Ẓimʾ: the interval between drinks

Ẓimʾ, with kasra on ẓāʾ and an unvocalized mīm, denotes the interval between drinking or watering occasions in pastoral Arabic.

If livestock drink today and are brought to water again one or more days later, the intervening period is their ẓimʾ.

Its plural is aẓmāʾ.

In pastoral life, watering schedules depended on water availability, season, pasture, and the animals' endurance. Particular intervals accordingly had their own names and expressions.

Here ẓimʾ names an interval, not necessarily the livestock's degree of thirst.

Two groups of camels might have equal watering intervals but different levels of thirst because of bodily or environmental conditions.

Time without water and the feeling of thirst are thus closely related but distinct concepts.

Lexical works also record ẓimʾ al-ḥayāh for the span of life from birth to death.

The temporal image extends from livestock watering intervals to the entire span of life.

Figurative longing

The expression ẓamiʾa ilā liqāʾihi is recorded for longing to meet someone.

The person is not literally thirsty for water; the desire for a meeting is likened to thirst.

As a thirsty person needs and seeks water, a longing person seeks something presently lacking.

This extension is readily understandable, but should not be transferred to every Quranic occurrence.

The Quranic passages concern thirst directly or in a comparison based on a real need for water.

3. Quranic usage

The root occurs three times, in three morphological forms and three verses:

Ẓamaʾun in Al-Tawbah 9:120, taẓmaʾu in Ṭā Hā 20:119, and al-ẓamʾānu in Al-Nūr 24:39.

They express three aspects of one concept: thirst itself, becoming thirsty, and the thirsty person.

A. Ẓamaʾ: thirst during effort and hardship

مَا كَانَ لِأَهْلِ الْمَدِينَةِ وَمَنْ حَوْلَهُمْ مِنَ الْأَعْرَابِ أَنْ يَتَخَلَّفُوا عَنْ رَسُولِ اللَّهِ وَلَا يَرْغَبُوا بِأَنْفُسِهِمْ عَنْ نَفْسِهِ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمْ لَا يُصِيبُهُمْ ظَمَأٌ وَلَا نَصَبٌ وَلَا مَخْمَصَةٌ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ وَلَا يَطَئُونَ مَوْطِئًا يَغِيظُ الْكُفَّارَ وَلَا يَنَالُونَ مِنْ عَدُوٍّ نَيْلًا إِلَّا كُتِبَ لَهُمْ بِهِ عَمَلٌ صَالِحٌ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يُضِيعُ أَجْرَ الْمُحْسِنِينَ

mā kāna li-ahli al-madīnati wa-man ḥawlahum mina al-aʿrābi an yatakhallafū ʿan rasūli allāhi wa-lā yarghabū bi-anfusihim ʿan nafsihi dhālika bi-annahum lā yuṣībuhum ẓamaʾun wa-lā naṣabun wa-lā makhmaṣatun fī sabīli allāhi wa-lā yaṭaʾūna mawṭiʾan yaghīẓu al-kuffāra wa-lā yanālūna min ʿaduwwin naylan illā kutiba lahum bihi ʿamalun ṣāliḥun inna allāha lā yuḍīʿu ajra al-muḥsinīn

Al-Tawbah 9:120

It was not for the people of Medina and the desert Arabs around them to remain behind the Messenger of The God or to prefer their own lives to his. That is because no thirst, fatigue, or hunger afflicts them in the way of The God, nor do they tread any ground that enrages the disbelievers, nor obtain any gain against an enemy, without a righteous deed being recorded for them on its account. Indeed, The God does not waste the reward of those who do good.

Al-Tawbah 9:120

Ẓamaʾun here denotes thirst that may arise during travel, effort, and the endurance of hardship.

It appears alongside naṣabun and makhmaṣatun.

Naṣab concerns hardship and fatigue; makhmaṣa concerns hunger and a shortage of food.

The three words identify distinct bodily conditions: needing water, fatigue from effort, and needing food.

Thirst is a real bodily state here. The verse discusses difficulties people may face while fulfilling their responsibility.

The role of yuṣībuhum

In lā yuṣībuhum ẓamaʾun, yuṣību belongs to ص و ب.

Ẓamaʾun is its grammatical subject: thirst reaches or afflicts them.

Thirst is therefore not an act they perform with the intention of becoming thirsty, but a difficulty that may befall them during their efforts.

The verse states that such hardships, endured in God's way, do not go unrewarded.

This differs from deliberately keeping oneself thirsty to obtain more reward. Deliberate abstention from water is not conveyed by ẓamaʾun or by this construction.

Nor does the verse establish that every instance of thirst or hardship is inherently a righteous deed, regardless of circumstances.

The qualification fī sabīli Allāh specifies the setting of this endurance. The passage concerns accompanying the Messenger and fulfilling the responsibility under discussion.

The lexical meaning remains thirst; the action's value follows from its purpose, context, and circumstances.

B. Taẓmaʾu: becoming thirsty and the absence of thirst

وَأَنَّكَ لَا تَظْمَأُ فِيهَا وَلَا تَضْحَىٰ

wa-annaka lā taẓmaʾu fīhā wa-lā taḍḥā

Ṭā Hā 20:119

And that you will neither thirst there nor be exposed to the sun's heat.

Ṭā Hā 20:119

Taẓmaʾu is an imperfect verb from ẓamiʾa.

It addresses Adam and is negated by lā: he does not become thirsty there.

The preceding verse also helps clarify the passage.

إِنَّ لَكَ أَلَّا تَجُوعَ فِيهَا وَلَا تَعْرَىٰ

inna laka allā tajūʿa fīhā wa-lā taʿrā

Ṭā Hā 20:118

Indeed, it is yours neither to hunger there nor to be naked.

Ṭā Hā 20:118

Together the verses name four bodily conditions: hunger, nakedness, thirst, and exposure to the sun.

Each relates to a need of human life.

Hunger concerns food, thirst concerns water, nakedness concerns covering, and taḍḥā concerns exposure to the sun and its heat.

Thirst and sunlight

Exposure to heat and sunlight can increase the body's need for water, but taẓmaʾu itself does not mean exposure to the sun's heat.

Likewise, taḍḥā by itself does not mean thirst.

The two verbs describe distinct conditions that can be related in human life.

The absence of thirst is presented as a feature of Adam's life in the Garden.

Taẓmaʾu itself, however, explains neither the source and provision of water nor Adam's bodily constitution.

The verb directly means “you become thirsty”; the absence of thirst results from its negation in the sentence.

The story of Adam

This verse follows God's warning to Adam.

In Ṭā Hā 20:117, Adam is told that Satan is an enemy to him and his wife and must not cause their departure from the Garden.

Ṭā Hā 20:118 and Ṭā Hā 20:119 then describe the absence of hunger, nakedness, thirst, and exposure to the sun's heat there.

The connection portrays a situation in which Adam's basic needs are met.

Taẓmaʾu alone does not imply that Adam had no bodily need for water. Not feeling thirsty is different from having no biological need for water. A creature may need water without becoming thirsty if that need is continually met.

The verse states the absence of thirst without explaining water provision or its biological mechanism.

C. Al-ẓamʾānu: the thirsty person and the mirage

وَالَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا أَعْمَالُهُمْ كَسَرَابٍ بِقِيعَةٍ يَحْسَبُهُ الظَّمْآنُ مَاءً حَتَّىٰ إِذَا جَاءَهُ لَمْ يَجِدْهُ شَيْئًا وَوَجَدَ اللَّهَ عِنْدَهُ فَوَفَّاهُ حِسَابَهُ ۗ وَاللَّهُ سَرِيعُ الْحِسَابِ

wa-alladhīna kafarū aʿmāluhum ka-sarābin bi-qīʿatin yaḥsabuhu al-ẓamʾānu māʾan ḥattā idhā jāʾahu lam yajidhu shayʾan wa-wajada allāha ʿindahu fa-waffāhu ḥisābahu wa-allāhu sarīʿu al-ḥisāb

Al-Nūr 24:39

And those who disbelieved—their deeds are like a mirage on a plain that a thirsty person supposes to be water, until, on reaching it, he finds it to be nothing, and finds The God there, who renders his account in full; and The God is swift in reckoning.

Al-Nūr 24:39

Al-ẓamʾānu means the thirsty person here.

It appears in an image of a mirage: optical and environmental conditions can make a surface appear like water where no water is present.

Thirst has an important role in this image.

A thirsty person needs water and may hope that the need has been answered on seeing something water-like in the distance.

The Quran thus places a thirsty person before a mirage to portray hope that ultimately proves fruitless.

Why al-ẓamʾānu?

Someone who is not thirsty may see a mirage without an urgent need or motive to reach water.

For a thirsty person, however, something resembling water may be precious because water is exactly what is needed.

Thirst is therefore not merely incidental to the comparison. It explains why the person places hope in what is seen and pursues it.

This does not mean that every thirsty person experiences an optical illusion or that everyone with a strong desire must be deceived.

The verse employs a specific image: a thirsty person taking a mirage for water.

Yaḥsabuhu: supposing it to be water

Yaḥsabuhu belongs to ح س ب and here denotes a supposition contrary to reality.

The thirsty person takes the mirage for water.

Ẓamʾān does not itself mean “deceived” or “mistaken.” The error is conveyed through yaḥsabuhu and the reality of the mirage.

Three elements should therefore be distinguished:

Al-ẓamʾānu identifies the person who needs water.

Yaḥsabuhu expresses taking the mirage for water.

Lam yajidhu shayʾan exposes the fruitlessness of that supposition.

Together they portray hope placed in something that, when reached, does not meet the person's need.

The comparison and human deeds

The verse compares the deeds of those who disbelieve to a mirage.

A thirsty person hopes in something appearing to be water from afar, but discovers on reaching it that the expected thing is absent.

Likewise, deeds in which a person places hope may fail to yield the expected result at the reckoning.

The verse continues with God's reckoning.

The comparison therefore concerns more than failure to find water: it reveals the reality of deeds before divine reckoning.

That point emerges from the whole verse, not from the lexical meaning of ẓamʾān.

Al-ẓamʾānu should not be directly translated as “a misguided person” or “someone without guidance.” Such expressions may explain the comparison, but the word itself means a thirsty person.

The following verse

Al-Nūr 24:40 presents another image: accumulated darkness in a deep sea.

Al-Nūr 24:39 concerns seeing something and mistaking it for reality; in Al-Nūr 24:40, darkness is so encompassing that the person is not even close to seeing his hand.

The two images portray human perception and understanding in different ways.

In the mirage comparison, something is visible but its nature is misunderstood. In the image of darkness, even the ability to see is severely restricted.

This clarifies the thirsty person's role: the first comparison depicts a real need and the misidentification of something incapable of meeting it.

4. Related roots and distinctions

ظ م أ and ع ط ش: thirst

ʿAṭash is the most familiar Arabic equivalent of ẓamaʾ.

Both concern thirst and the need for water.

Lexical works sometimes describe ẓamaʾ as milder and sometimes as stronger than ʿaṭash. The disagreement prevents treating intensity as a fixed, universal distinction.

Being thirsty is their shared core; any difference of intensity must be established in a particular use.

ʿAṭash in this form does not occur in the Quran, whereas ظ م أ has three Quranic occurrences.

“Thirst” and “becoming thirsty” adequately express these uses; there is no need to invent an unusual equivalent merely to distinguish ẓamaʾ from ʿaṭash.

ظ م أ and ج و ع: thirst and hunger

ج و ع concerns hunger and the need for food.

Hunger and thirst appear together in Ṭā Hā 20:118 and Ṭā Hā 20:119.

Their juxtaposition identifies two different bodily needs.

A person may be hungry without being thirsty, or thirsty without an immediate need for food.

Ẓamaʾ and jūʿ are distinct conditions, although they can occur together.

Their absence helps describe Adam's living conditions in the Garden.

ظ م أ and خ م ص: thirst and hunger from lack of food

Makhmaṣa belongs to خ م ص and concerns an empty belly and hunger.

It appears alongside ẓamaʾ in Al-Tawbah 9:120.

Ẓamaʾ expresses a need for water; makhmaṣa refers to a need for food.

Together with naṣab, they identify different difficulties people may encounter in their efforts.

“Becoming thirsty,” “becoming hungry,” and “becoming tired” should not collapse into the general “suffering,” even though each can be painful.

ظ م أ and ص د ي: thirst and an intense need for water

ص د ي also concerns thirst in Arabic; ṣadā is recorded in one use for thirst, particularly intense thirst.

The verb ṣadiya likewise means “became thirsty.”

Ṣadā and ẓamaʾ are very close in some usages. A fixed, universal distinction cannot be established without clear historical evidence.

The Quranic forms expressing thirst come from ظ م أ, not ص د ي.

Retaining the need for water and the state of thirst is enough here; comparison with ṣadā does not require adding a particular intensity.

ظ م أ and ر و ي: thirst and being quenched

One branch of ر و ي concerns drinking water and quenching thirst.

Riyy contrasts with thirst, and rayyān describes someone whose thirst is quenched.

The contrast distinguishes needing water from having that need met.

A ẓamʾān person is thirsty; a rayyān person has drunk sufficiently.

Not every use of ر و ي should be reduced to quenching thirst, since the family has other branches.

In Al-Nūr 24:39, the thirsty person approaches what seems to be water but is not quenched, because it is a mirage.

Real need and failure to meet it consequently form an essential part of the image.

ظ م أ and و ر د: thirst and reaching water

و ر د concerns reaching a watering place or arriving at water.

In pastoral life, wird could denote the watering turn or livestock's arrival at water.

In its temporal use, ẓimʾ denotes the interval between drinking occasions.

When camels reach water and drink, that occasion concerns arrival at the water. The interval until the next occasion is their ẓimʾ.

Ẓamaʾ, with fatḥa on ẓāʾ, denotes the feeling of thirst itself.

Three related concepts thus remain distinct: thirst, arrival at water, and the interval between watering occasions.

This matters for understanding early Arabic: pastoral water vocabulary described not only bodily states, but also the scheduling and organization of daily life.

5. Morphological and semantic observations

Ẓamaʾ and ẓimʾ

These forms belong to the same family, but their established meanings differ.

Ẓamaʾ names the state of thirst.

Ẓimʾ can name the interval between drinking occasions.

The distinction illustrates the importance of Arabic vowels in identifying a derivative's meaning.

Al-Tawbah 9:120 has ẓamaʾun, meaning thirst, rather than a watering interval.

Taẓmaʾu and its subject

In Ṭā Hā 20:119, taẓmaʾu is a second-person masculine singular imperfect verb.

Its implicit subject is anta, “you,” referring to Adam.

Lā negates the verb, and fīhā refers to the Garden under discussion.

The sentence therefore says that Adam does not become thirsty there.

The verb denotes becoming thirsty, rather than abstaining from water, asking for it, or deliberately remaining thirsty.

Al-ẓamʾānu and the adjective pattern

Al-ẓamʾānu is an adjective on the faʿlān pattern describing a person's state.

In Al-Nūr 24:39, it is definite with al- and functions substantivally: “the thirsty person.”

In yaḥsabuhu al-ẓamʾānu māʾan, al-ẓamʾānu is the subject of yaḥsabu.

That person supposes the mirage to be water.

Grammatically, the word identifies the person making the supposition; the reason for the mistake emerges from the combined image of thirst and mirage.

Evaluative meaning

In its primary meaning, ظ م أ carries no inherent positive or negative moral judgment.

Thirst is a bodily state that may affect a good person, an oppressor, a child, a traveler, or other humans and animals.

In Al-Tawbah 9:120, thirst occurs in the context of striving in God's way, and endurance in those circumstances is not left unrewarded.

In Ṭā Hā 20:119, the absence of thirst characterizes Adam's life in the Garden.

In Al-Nūr 24:39, a thirsty person figures in a comparison portraying hope in something that ultimately fails to produce the desired result.

The basic meaning remains constant, while its role in each verse's argument differs.

Thirst is not inherently a virtue, a sign of punishment, or proof of misguidance. Those ideas require contextual evidence when explaining a passage.

Distinguishing ظ م أ from ظ م ي

Lexical works also record forms under ظ م ي associated with dryness and leanness.

Examples concern thin lips, scant flesh in limbs, and descriptions of the gums or eyelids. These are historical descriptions of appearance, not diagnoses of clinical anemia.

Some lexicographers connect these descriptions with the hamzated family, but Ibn Barrī assigns the lean-joint expression to ظ م ي. The final radical and the disagreement must both be considered.

The Quranic root studied here is ظ م أ. Unhamzated words should not all be treated as its direct derivatives merely because their meanings appear similar.

6. Translation recommendations for Forqan

The natural primary equivalent in Quran translation is “thirst.”

Ẓamiʾa may be rendered “became thirsty.”

Yaẓmaʾu and its second-person form taẓmaʾu mean “he becomes thirsty” and “you become thirsty.”

Ẓamaʾ directly corresponds to “thirst.”

Ẓamʾān can be rendered “thirsty” or “a thirsty person.”

In pastoral usage, ẓimʾ is more precisely “the interval between drinks” or “the interval between watering livestock.” This meaning does not occur in the Quran.

In figurative longing for a meeting, “thirsting to see someone” can preserve the Arabic image, but should not be imposed on Quranic passages concerning actual thirst.

Al-Tawbah 9:120

Ẓamaʾun: thirst.

Lā yuṣībuhum ẓamaʾun may be rendered “no thirst afflicts them” or “they suffer no thirst.”

Ṭā Hā 20:119

Taẓmaʾu: you become thirsty.

Suggested translation:

“And that you will neither thirst there nor be exposed to the sun's heat.”

Al-Nūr 24:39

Al-ẓamʾānu: the thirsty person; someone needing water.

Yaḥsabuhu al-ẓamʾānu māʾan may be rendered “a thirsty person supposes it to be water.”

Brief meanings for word boxes

7. Summary

ظ م أ concerns thirst and the need for water. Ẓamiʾa denotes becoming thirsty, ẓamaʾ denotes thirst itself, and ẓamʾān describes the thirsty person.

In early Arabic, ẓimʾ denoted the interval between drinking occasions, particularly in pastoral life. A verse attributed to Zuhayr illustrates it through arrival at a watering place. Another animal description uses ẓimāʾ for lean joints, but its assignment to ظ م أ or ظ م ي is disputed.

Thirst and the watering interval should remain distinct. The appearance-related description is best treated with notice of the disagreement, rather than as conclusive evidence for a unified root meaning.

The Quran uses this root three times.

In Al-Tawbah 9:120, ẓamaʾun denotes thirst that may be encountered during effort and endurance in God's way.

In Ṭā Hā 20:119, taẓmaʾu is negated, expressing the absence of thirst in Adam's life in the Garden.

In Al-Nūr 24:39, al-ẓamʾānu is the thirsty person who supposes a mirage to be water. Thirst expresses a real need; the mirage portrays hope placed in something unable to answer it.

None of these forms independently denotes virtue, the pursuit of suffering, misguidance, or deception. Such ideas arise, where relevant, from the verses' contexts and their treatment of thirst.

Distinctions from ʿaṭash, jūʿ, makhmaṣa, ṣadā, riyy, and wurūd should follow their respective meanings. Thirst differs from hunger and fatigue, just as a watering interval differs from the feeling of thirst itself.

Final definition: The main branch of ظ م أ denotes becoming thirsty and needing water. Ẓamaʾ names the state; ẓamʾān describes someone thirsty. In early Arabic, ẓimʾ can denote the interval between drinking occasions. All three Quranic occurrences concern thirst or the thirsty person; context supplies its intensity, cause, and role.
