The Root Ṭ M Th in the Quran

Qur’anic and lexical forms

يَطْمِثْهُنَّ
(yaṭmithhunna)
Have intercourse with them; negated in the Quran

طَمْث
(ṭamth)
Contact, menstruation, or intercourse; non-Quranic noun

طَمَثَ
(ṭamatha)
Touched or had intercourse; non-Quranic form

طَمِثَتْ
(ṭamithat)
Menstruated; non-Quranic

طَامِث
(ṭāmith)
Menstruating; non-Quranic

يَطْمُثْهُنَّ
(yaṭmuthhunna)
Reading variant with ḍammah

يُطْمَثْنَ
(yuṭmathna)
Passive form in the poem; non-Quranic

لَمْ يَطْمِثْهُنَّ
(lam yaṭmithhunna)
Has not had intercourse with them; Quranic construction

Contact, intercourse, and being untouched: from menstruation and first intercourse to the description of companions in Paradise

The Arabic family ط م ث concerns contact and affecting something. Recorded usages include sexual intercourse, first intercourse with a woman, and menstrual blood. Other expressions concern a rope touching a camel, an unused pasture, and soiling or staining.

Early lexicographers differ over its original sense. Some begin with contact and treat intercourse as a specific application; others begin with menstruation or blood associated with first intercourse and regard other senses as extensions.

Without historical investigation, none can simply be declared the proven origin of every branch.

The Quran contains two occurrences of one morphological form, يَطْمِثْهُنَّ, in Al-Raḥmān 55:56 and Al-Raḥmān 55:74. Both are negated by لَمْ and concern women in the descriptions of Paradise.

Both contexts indicate absence of prior intercourse: no human or jinn has had intercourse with them before those for whom they are described.

The Quranic usage must be distinguished from the family's entire historical range. General contact, menstrual blood, first intercourse, and intercourse are related but distinct; syntax and context determine the particular sense.

1. Historical meaning in early Arabic

طَمْث: contact and prior use

One early explanation defines طَمْث as مَسّ, contact with something.

Everyday expressions illustrate this usage.

For example:

مَا طَمَثَ هَذَا الْبَعِيرَ حَبْلٌ قَطُّ

mā ṭamatha hādhā al-baʿīra ḥablun qaṭṭ

“No rope has ever touched this camel.”

This concerns a camel never previously tied or restrained with a rope, not sexual intercourse.

Another expression reads:

مَا طَمَثَ هَذَا الْمَرْتَعَ قَبْلَنَا أَحَدٌ

mā ṭamatha hādhā al-martaʿa qablanā aḥad

“No one before us has touched this pasture.”

Here the word concerns prior use of the pasture.

Both examples demonstrate recorded uses for contact or handling.

They reach us primarily through dictionaries; their precise pre-Islamic dating is uncertain.

Recording them in old sources does not establish that they are certainly pre-Islamic witnesses.

They nevertheless show that Arabic usage was not confined to bleeding or intercourse.

طَمِثَتِ الْمَرْأَةُ: menstruation

طَمِثَتِ الْمَرْأَةُ and طَمَثَتْ are recorded for menstruating.

طَامِث describes a menstruating woman.

Some accounts restrict the usage to a girl's first menstruation; others record menstruation generally.

Without a contextual indication, it cannot always be restricted to the first period.

The connection with blood is clear: bleeding accompanies menstruation, and some old definitions explain طمث as blood itself.

A bodily process and the material associated with it should still be distinguished.

Menstruation is a bodily process, blood one of its manifestations, and طامث describes the person experiencing it.

The Quran does not use طمث in this sense.

It uses ح ي ض for menstruation. Shared lexical history does not transfer that sense to لَمْ يَطْمِثْهُنَّ in Al-Raḥmān.

طَمَثَ الْمَرْأَةَ: intercourse and first intercourse

طَمَثَ الرَّجُلُ الْمَرْأَةَ describes a man having intercourse with a woman.

Some definitions restrict it to first intercourse and the ending of virginity.

This connects with افتضاض, understood in those accounts as first intercourse with a woman who has not previously had intercourse.

Some lexicographers explain the usage through blood associated with first intercourse.

Others accept intercourse generally, whether or not it is the first time.

The disagreement cautions against reducing every usage to bleeding during first intercourse.

First intercourse does not necessarily cause bleeding. The old lexical explanation cannot be treated as a universal bodily rule.

Historical definitions are evidence about language; their accounts of physiology are not, by themselves, timeless scientific definitions.

A poetic witness from al-Farazdaq

A verse attributed to al-Farazdaq reads:

وَقَعْنَ إِلَيَّ لَمْ يُطْمَثْنَ قَبْلِي
فَهُنَّ أَصَحُّ مِنْ بَيْضِ النَّعَامِ

waqaʿna ilayya lam yuṭmathna qablī / fa-hunna aṣaḥḥu min bayḍi al-naʿām

“They came to me, untouched by intercourse before me, more intact than ostrich eggs.”

The wording here follows the dictionary transmission with وَقَعْنَ and فَهُنَّ; other transmitted versions differ.

لم يطمثن قبلي concerns absence of prior intercourse.

The image of intact ostrich eggs reinforces that description.

The sexual usage is clear in this witness.

Al-Farazdaq was an Umayyad poet. His verse witnesses early Islamic Arabic, not an independent period before Quranic revelation.

The poem does not establish that every Arabic occurrence means only first intercourse.

It demonstrates that this particular usage existed.

Corruption or staining

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAdī ibn Zayd reads:

طَاهِرُ الْأَثْوَابِ يَحْمِي عِرْضَهُ
مِنْ خَنَى الذِّمَّةِ أَوْ طَمْثِ الْعَطَنِ

ṭāhiru al-athwābi yaḥmī ʿirḍahu / min khanā al-dhimmati aw ṭamthi al-ʿaṭan

“Clean of garments, he protects his honor from disgrace in his commitments or the staining of his reputation.”

طمث here concerns corruption or staining.

عطن elsewhere names a camel resting place near water, but the lexical explanation of this particular line gives it the sense of honor or reputation. A literal livestock enclosure is therefore unsuitable in this translation.

The poet uses cleanliness and staining to describe protecting one's honor and avoiding wrongdoing.

The usage concerns corruption or an undesirable stain, not intercourse.

It can be compared semantically with contact leaving an effect on something's condition.

That comparison does not mean every contact was called طمث or that every instance implies pollution.

ʿAdī ibn Zayd is known as a pre-Islamic poet, and dictionaries attribute this line to him. Establishing this particular line's date and wording conclusively requires independent textual witnesses.

The attribution supplies an early witness associated with the pre-Quranic period, not a complete chronology of the root's semantic development.

The semantic core

The evidence distinguishes contact and prior use, menstruation and intercourse, and corruption or staining.

Some lexical analyses treat contact as the broad sense linking these usages.

They do not prove that every sense historically developed from one original physical contact.

The definition should preserve both relationships and independently recorded usages.

In its contact usages, ط م ث concerns touching or reaching something, alongside established branches for menstruation, intercourse, and, in some expressions, corruption or staining.

Quranic يطمثهن concerns intercourse in its particular context, rather than every contact or menstruation.

2. The structure of يَطْمِثْهُنَّ

The Quran uses this family only in يَطْمِثْهُنَّ.

The word combines the imperfect verb يَطْمِثْ and attached pronoun هُنَّ.

It is a Form I verb from the triliteral root ط م ث.

لَمْ precedes it in both verses, putting it in the jussive.

لَمْ يَطْمِثْهُنَّ therefore negates the occurrence of an action in the past.

هُنَّ is a feminine plural object pronoun referring to the women described in Paradise.

إِنْسٌ follows as subject; وَلَا جَانٌّ includes another category within the negation.

The verb is singular, while the negation covers both humans and jinn.

The sentence says that neither a human nor a jinn has had intercourse with these women before them.

Two vocalizations

Early Quranic reading traditions transmit يَطْمِثْهُنَّ with kasrah on the mīm and يَطْمُثْهُنَّ with ḍammah.

They reflect a recorded variation in Arabic verb inflection.

The Ḥafṣ reading used here has kasrah.

Other transmissions have ḍammah, with reports about differing treatment of the two passages.

This vowel difference does not change the basic meaning in these verses.

Both describe absence of prior intercourse.

It should not be turned into a claim about two kinds of intercourse or different groups of women.

3. The first occurrence: Al-Raḥmān 55:56

The surah states:

فِيهِنَّ قَاصِرَاتُ الطَّرْفِ لَمْ يَطْمِثْهُنَّ إِنْسٌ قَبْلَهُمْ وَلَا جَانٌّ

fīhinna qāṣirātu al-ṭarfi lam yaṭmithhunna insun qablahum wa-lā jānn

Al-Raḥmān 55:56

“In them are women restraining their gaze, with whom neither human nor jinn has had intercourse before them.”

Al-Raḥmān 55:56

The verse describes the two gardens promised to those who fear standing before their Lord.

Trees, springs, fruit, and furnishings precede it.

The women are described as قَاصِرَاتُ الطَّرْفِ.

The expression concerns gaze and attention, explained as lowering the gaze or restricting attention to their spouses.

The verse then says:

لَمْ يَطْمِثْهُنَّ إِنْسٌ قَبْلَهُمْ وَلَا جَانٌّ

lam yaṭmithhunna insun qablahum wa-lā jānn

Al-Raḥmān 55:56

“Neither human nor jinn has had intercourse with them before them.”

يطمثهن belongs here to a context of companionship and intercourse.

Translating it as no one having had even any physical contact with them is too broad for the context.

Translating it as no one having caused them to bleed is too narrowly physiological and adds what the wording does not explicitly say.

Absence of prior intercourse fits the passage.

The role of قَبْلَهُمْ

قَبْلَهُمْ combines قَبْلَ and the plural pronoun هُمْ.

It locates the action relative to those for whom the women are described.

The verse negates intercourse before them.

It therefore specifies temporal priority in addition to the kind of action.

The complete construction conveys first intercourse or absence of a prior sexual partner.

Before them is not part of the root's definition: the verb identifies the action, while قبلهم supplies its temporal relation.

Does the verse require bleeding?

Some early definitions associate the word with first intercourse accompanied by bleeding.

This verse contains no separate word for blood or bleeding.

It explicitly negates prior طمث.

It cannot establish that first intercourse must cause bleeding or that bleeding conclusively reveals a woman's sexual history.

Neither the syntax nor the diversity of human bodies supports that inference.

The Quranic description concerns prior intercourse, not a physical test for proving it.

إِنْسٌ and جَانٌّ

Both humans and jinn are named.

Al-Raḥmān addresses both groups and discusses their creation.

Al-Raḥmān 55:31 addresses الثقلان, while the repeated question فَبِأَيِّ آلَاءِ رَبِّكُمَا تُكَذِّبَانِ uses the dual form of address.

Their inclusion in these descriptions relates to the surah's audience.

The phrase alone determines neither jinn physiology, human–jinn intercourse in this world, nor the mechanics of relationships in the hereafter.

It says that neither group has previously had intercourse with the women before those intended.

The statement can be understood without adding unknown details.

4. The second occurrence: Al-Raḥmān 55:74

The second passage states:

لَمْ يَطْمِثْهُنَّ إِنْسٌ قَبْلَهُمْ وَلَا جَانٌّ

lam yaṭmithhunna insun qablahum wa-lā jānn

Al-Raḥmān 55:74

“Neither human nor jinn has had intercourse with them before them.”

Al-Raḥmān 55:74

Its wording and structure match the final clause of Al-Raḥmān 55:56.

The immediate context differs.

Al-Raḥmān 55:72 states:

حُورٌ مَقْصُورَاتٌ فِي الْخِيَامِ

ḥūrun maqṣūrātun fī al-khiyām

Al-Raḥmān 55:72

“Ḥūr kept within the pavilions.”

The same description of no prior intercourse follows.

The first passage introduces women as قاصرات الطرف.

The second has حور مقصورات في الخيام.

Different descriptions appear in the two presentations of paradisal blessings.

لم يطمثهن nevertheless retains the same meaning.

Repetition does not establish one kind of contact in the first verse and another in the second.

Nor do the accompanying descriptions alone prove wholly different modes of existence for the women.

The negated verb establishes absence of prior intercourse.

Why repeat the description?

The surah describes paradisal blessings in two sections.

Two gardens are followed by two further gardens.

Both sections describe plants, springs, fruit, and companions.

Repetition emphasizes this feature in both.

It does not mean every feature of both groups of blessings is identical.

Nor is a hidden independent meaning required for each repetition when wording and structure retain the same sense.

5. Distinguishing related roots

ط م ث and م س س: general contact and intercourse

م س س concerns touching or making contact.

It can describe bodily contact, being affected by harm, encountering something, and, in some contexts, intercourse.

Maryam 19:20 states:

قَالَتْ أَنَّىٰ يَكُونُ لِي غُلَامٌ وَلَمْ يَمْسَسْنِي بَشَرٌ وَلَمْ أَكُ بَغِيًّا

qālat annā yakūnu lī ghulāmun wa-lam yamsasnī basharun wa-lam aku baghiyyā

Maryam 19:20

“She said: How can I have a boy when no human has had intercourse with me, and I have not been unchaste?”

يَمْسَسْنِي belongs to م س س.

Pregnancy and absence of intercourse determine its sexual sense here.

This illustrates contextual overlap with طمث.

مس has a wider Arabic and Quranic range; not every contact or effect is sexual.

Lexical examples likewise record general contact for طمث, although intercourse is foregrounded in its Quranic occurrences.

The roots are not interchangeable in every usage.

ط م ث and ح ي ض: intercourse and menstruation

The Quran uses ح ي ض for menstruation and its associated condition.

Al-Baqarah 2:222 discusses الْمَحِيضِ and conduct during that time.

Classical طَامِث can also describe a menstruating woman.

This shared subject does not make طمث in Al-Raḥmān refer to menstruation.

In Al-Raḥmān 55:56 and Al-Raḥmān 55:74, يطمثهن has humans and jinn as subjects and هُنَّ as object.

The construction concerns an action performed toward the women.

Menstrual طَمِثَتِ الْمَرْأَةُ instead describes a condition experienced by the woman herself.

Syntax therefore helps distinguish the usages.

These verses do not directly state that the women have never menstruated.

ط م ث and ن ك ح: intercourse and marriage

ن ك ح appears in various Quranic constructions concerning marriage.

Arabic also associates it with intercourse, but many Quranic passages concern the marital relationship and its provisions.

The two Quranic instances of طمث negate intercourse.

That negation alone cannot establish absence of every previous legal relationship or marital agreement: the verb concerns intercourse, not all contracts.

The paradisal context is clear about prior intercourse, while legal details or the nature of relationships in the hereafter cannot be derived from this word alone.

The roots overlap in some senses without being identical.

ط م ث and ب ك ر: no prior intercourse and virginity

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:36 states:

فَجَعَلْنَاهُنَّ أَبْكَارًا

fa-jaʿalnāhunna abkārā

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:36

“And We made them virgins.”

أَبْكَار is the plural of بِكْر and here describes women who have not previously had intercourse.

It clearly relates to the negation of طمث in Al-Raḥmān.

The constructions nevertheless differ.

أبكارا describes a state.

لم يطمثهن negates an action's occurrence in the past.

Both can convey absence of previous intercourse, but one names a condition and the other denies an action.

Context should also be examined before treating every woman in the various descriptions of Paradise as one identical group with the same history.

ط م ث and ط م س: contact and effacement

ط م س concerns effacing, removing traces, or altering something.

Quranic contexts include obliterated signs and transformed faces.

طمث and طمس sound similar but are distinct roots.

Their written resemblance does not transfer effacement or destruction to طمث.

Al-Raḥmān 55:56 and Al-Raḥmān 55:74 concern prior intercourse, not erasing women's personality or existence.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Translation must distinguish contact, menstruation, and intercourse.

طَمَثَ الشَّيْءَ in general Arabic usage may mean touched something or handled it.

مَا طَمَثَهُ أَحَدٌ, of a pasture or object, may mean no one has previously used or touched it.

Menstrual طَمِثَتِ الْمَرْأَةُ: the woman menstruated.

طَامِث in that branch: a menstruating woman.

Sexual طَمَثَ الْمَرْأَةَ: had intercourse with the woman; more narrowly, had first intercourse with her.

طَمْث may refer to contact, menstruation, or intercourse, according to context.

Only يَطْمِثْهُنَّ occurs in the Quran.

Al-Raḥmān 55:56

لَمْ يَطْمِثْهُنَّ إِنْسٌ قَبْلَهُمْ وَلَا جَانٌّ

lam yaṭmithhunna insun qablahum wa-lā jānn

Al-Raḥmān 55:56

“Neither human nor jinn has had intercourse with them before them.”

Al-Raḥmān 55:74

لَمْ يَطْمِثْهُنَّ إِنْسٌ قَبْلَهُمْ وَلَا جَانٌّ

lam yaṭmithhunna insun qablahum wa-lā jānn

Al-Raḥmān 55:74

“Neither human nor jinn has had intercourse with them before them.”

The same rendering preserves the meaning in both passages; the object pronoun refers to the women and the second pronoun to their intended companions.

No one has touched them preserves a contact image but can suggest any physical contact and leave the sexual sense unclear.

No one has ended their virginity through bleeding is unsuitable because it adds bleeding to the text.

Has had intercourse with them conveys the contextual meaning without that addition.

A lexical explanation can connect the absence of previous intercourse with the description of virginity.

The base translation should preserve the verb and its negation.

7. Summary

Recorded Arabic usages include contact, prior use, menstruation, intercourse, and corruption or staining.

Some old explanations begin with contact; others with menstrual blood or blood associated with first intercourse.

The available evidence does not establish any one explanation as the definitive historical origin of every usage.

The rope and pasture expressions demonstrate meanings beyond intercourse.

The verse attributed to ʿAdī ibn Zayd illustrates corruption or staining; al-Farazdaq's Umayyad-era poem clearly illustrates the sexual usage.

The Quran contains two occurrences in the same negated construction, لَمْ يَطْمِثْهُنَّ.

Both occur in Al-Raḥmān and concern women described among paradisal blessings.

In Al-Raḥmān 55:56 the phrase follows قاصرات الطرف. Al-Raḥmān 55:74 repeats it after حور مقصورات في الخيام.

Both describe absence of prior intercourse.

This should neither be expanded to absence of all physical contact nor reduced to bleeding or supposed physical signs of virginity.

Mentioning humans and jinn does not establish jinn physiology, the mechanics of relationships in the hereafter, or human–jinn intercourse in this world.

The definition should preserve distinct branches: general contact or prior use, menstruation and associated blood, and intercourse, especially first intercourse in narrower usages.

The Quran uses the sexual branch, while لم يطمثهن negates prior intercourse.

This fits the syntax and avoids reducing the family's history to one narrow sense or a bodily theory of virginity.

The verses describe no prior intercourse; they do not provide a bodily criterion for human worth or explain unknown mechanisms of relationships in the hereafter.

The study should preserve the linguistic meaning without adding details the verses do not supply.
