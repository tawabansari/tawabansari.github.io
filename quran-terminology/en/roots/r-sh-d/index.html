The right course, sound judgment, and proceeding toward what is good

Arabic R SH D concerns finding the right way, judging correctly, and following a course conducive to soundness and a worthy outcome. It applies to choosing a direction, understanding and deciding, managing life’s affairs, and religious guidance.

Rushd stands opposite ghayy: a course toward rightness and soundness, contrasted with deviation from it. Rāshid and rashīd describe someone who recognizes the right course or decision, or follows it in conduct. Murshid guides someone else to the right way.

Rushd should not be restricted to knowing a fact, reaching puberty, or possessing abundant information. Someone may have knowledge without using it well in judgment and conduct. Another person may manage one particular task competently without complete knowledge of every area of life.

The Qur’an uses the root for responding to the divine call, distinguishing the right way from error, managing an orphan’s property, following guidance, asking for the right course, Abraham’s understanding and judgment, a discerning person, claims to sound leadership, and deliberately choosing truth.

This range connects rushd with both understanding and conduct, while each verse’s subject and context determine its precise meaning.

The root occurs nineteen times in nineteen Qur’anic verses. This study groups its forms into seven sets: the verb yarshudūna; the nouns rushd, rashad, and rashād; the adjective rashīd; and the active participles rāshid and murshid.

Qur’anic forms: يَرْشُدُونَ (yarshudūna), Al-Baqarah 2:186; الرُّشْدُ (al-rushdu), Al-Baqarah 2:256; رُشْدًا (rushdan), Al-Nisāʾ 4:6 and Al-Kahf 18:66; الرُّشْدِ (al-rushdi), Al-Aʿrāf 7:146 and Al-Jinn 72:2; رَشِيدٌ (rashīdun), Hūd 11:78; الرَّشِيدُ (al-rashīdu), Hūd 11:87; بِرَشِيدٍ (bi-rashīdin), Hūd 11:97; رَشَدًا (rashadan), Al-Kahf 18:10, Al-Kahf 18:24, Al-Jinn 72:10, Al-Jinn 72:14, and Al-Jinn 72:21; مُرْشِدًا (murshidan), Al-Kahf 18:17; رُشْدَهُ (rushdahu), Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:51; الرَّشَادِ (al-rashādi), Ghāfir 40:29 and Ghāfir 40:38; الرَّاشِدُونَ (al-rāshidūna), Al-Ḥujurāt 49:7.

Arabic also records رَشَدَ (rashada), رَشِدَ (rashida), رُشْد (rushd), رَشَد (rashad), رَشَاد (rashād), رَاشِد (rāshid), رَشِيد (rashīd), أَرْشَدَ (arshada), إِرْشَاد (irshād), رَشَّدَ (rashshada), تَرْشِيد (tarshīd), اِسْتَرْشَدَ (istarshada), اِسْتِرْشَاد (istirshād), مَرَاشِد (marāshid), and رِشْدَة (rishdah). Not all occur in exactly these forms in the Qur’an; actual Qur’anic derivatives must be distinguished from other Arabic uses.

1. Core meaning and early Arabic uses

Finding the right way and direction

Lexical explanation states the semantic core as follows:

أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى اسْتِقَامَةِ الطَّرِيقِ

aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā istiqāmati al-ṭarīqi

“One core denoting the straightness and rightness of the way.”

The explanation continues:

وَالرُّشْدُ وَالرَّشَدُ خِلَافُ الْغَيِّ

wa-al-rushdu wa-al-rashadu khilāfu al-ghayyi

“Rushd and rashad stand opposite ghayy.”

This clarifies the core: the right direction and course, rather than deviation from it.

Rashada al-rajulu describes a person finding and following the right way.

Rashida amrahu is also recorded for judging or acting soundly in one’s affairs.

This goes beyond finding a geographical route. A way can be a physical path or a method, decision, or direction in life.

The right course should nevertheless be distinguished from certainly attaining every desired result.

Someone may make a sound decision yet external circumstances prevent the entire desired outcome.

Failure of the outcome alone does not establish that the original decision lacked rushd.

Conversely, accidentally achieving a desirable result does not necessarily demonstrate sound judgment.

Rushd should therefore not be equated with apparent success or final profit. The right direction, suitable understanding, and considered judgment matter in this field.

Rushd, rashad, and rashād

Early Arabic records these three forms with closely related meanings.

Early lexical explanations place them opposite ghayy and ḍalāl.

Al-rashād is likewise defined opposite al-ghayy.

Lexical accounts differ over rushd, with ḍammah on rāʾ and sukūn on shīn, and rashad, with fatḥah on both.

Some distinguish soundness in worldly and otherworldly affairs from specifically religious rectitude.

Others regard them as closely related or synonymous linguistic forms and reject an absolute distinction.

The variation also appears in Qur’anic readings.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:146, for example, has established readings of both al-rushd and al-rashad.

Al-Kahf 18:66 likewise has a rashadan reading alongside rushdan.

Early explanations propose both a semantic distinction and close or identical meanings. Both readings of Al-Aʿrāf 7:146 have also been understood as compatible in their core meaning.

There should therefore be no exceptionless rule that rushd always means only financial or worldly competence and rashad only religious guidance.

The Qur’anic uses do not support such a rigid division.

Rashadan in Al-Kahf 18:10 appears in the prayer of young people seeking refuge in difficulty; in Al-Jinn 72:10, it stands opposite evil in discussing what their Lord intends for people.

Rushd, conversely, concerns both managing an orphan’s property and religious guidance.

Possible differences should follow actual uses and readings rather than a division imposed on every verse beforehand.

Marāshid: the right destinations or directions

Arabic records marāshid for the proper destinations and directions of routes.

It is explained as مَقَاصِدُ الطُّرُقِ (maqāṣidu al-ṭuruqi), the destinations or directions of paths.

This connects rushd with choosing a way and finding the right direction.

It does not establish that every use originally concerned maps or physical travel alone.

The right way can also mean the proper method of carrying out a task.

Someone competent in managing property has not necessarily found a geographical route, but has demonstrated suitable financial judgment and conduct.

Maqṣid here means a recognized direction or destination, without making every aim rushd merely because it is an aim.

An aim can be right or wrong; rushd concerns the right direction.

Arshada: guiding another person

Arshadahu means guiding someone and showing the right way.

Irshād likewise concerns direction and guidance.

This distinguishes recognizing the right way oneself from guiding another person toward it.

Rashada can concern the person who has found the right way.

Arshada can concern giving someone else guidance.

Murshid names the person providing that guidance.

Showing a way and accepting it are different events.

Someone may show another the right way without that person accepting it.

Someone may also receive guidance yet err in carrying it out.

Irshād alone therefore does not guarantee that its recipient chooses the right way.

Istarshada: seeking the right way

Istarshada describes seeking guidance or looking for the right way.

Istarshada fulānan can mean asking someone for guidance.

Istarshada li-amrihi is also recorded for seeking a suitable course or decision in one’s affairs.

The family therefore includes seeking and striving for right guidance, as well as describing someone who has attained it.

Asking for guidance does not itself guarantee a correct answer.

The search may be sincere or superficial; its outcome also depends on the method and evidence.

Other non-Qur’anic uses

Rishdah and rashdah are recorded opposite ghiyyah in historical descriptions of birth within recognized marriage.

This historical social and legal use does not determine a child’s moral worth through the parents’ relationship.

Ḥabbu al-rashād also became the name of an edible plant. Early explanations connect the name with renaming another plant and preferring the favorable word rashād.

That naming use is not the direct meaning of al-rashād in Ghāfir.

Modern Arabic also uses expressions such as tarshīd of consumption and sound management for prudent use and administration of resources.

These relate to sound judgment, but modern technical meanings should not be transferred into Qur’anic verses without examination.

2. Poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period: rushd against ghayy and poor judgment

Durayd ibn al-Ṣimmah: sound judgment in practical decisions

A poem by Durayd ibn al-Ṣimmah, a poet and warrior associated with the pre-Islamic period, states:

أَمَرْتُهُمُ أَمْرِي بِمُنْعَرَجِ اللِّوَى
فَلَمْ يَسْتَبِينُوا الرُّشْدَ إِلَّا ضُحَى الْغَدِ

amartuhumu amrī bi-munʿaraji al-liwā / fa-lam yastabīnū al-rushda illā ḍuḥā al-ghadi

“I gave them my advice and command at Munʿaraj al-Liwā, but they did not discern the right course until the next morning.”

Al-rushd here concerns the correct decision in a practical situation.

The poet describes people who rejected his warning and recognized its soundness only after facing the consequences.

This shows rushd was not exclusively a religious or otherworldly term in early Arabic.

Understanding a situation and its dangers and choosing appropriately also belong to its semantic field.

The next verse says:

فَلَمَّا عَصَوْنِي كُنْتُ مِنْهُمْ وَقَدْ أَرَى
غِوَايَتَهُمْ وَأَنَّنِي غَيْرُ مُهْتَدِ

fa-lammā ʿaṣawnī kuntu minhum wa-qad arā / ghiwāyatahum wa-annanī ghayru muhtadi

“When they disobeyed me, I remained one of them, seeing their misguided course and knowing that I was not rightly guided in going with them.”

He then says:

وَمَا أَنَا إِلَّا مِنْ غَزِيَّةَ إِنْ غَوَتْ
غَوَيْتُ وَإِنْ تَرْشُدْ غَزِيَّةُ أَرْشُدِ

wa-mā anā illā min ghaziyyata in ghawat / ghawaytu wa-in tarshud ghaziyyatu arshudi

“I am only one of Ghaziyyah: if it strays, I stray; if Ghaziyyah takes the right course, so do I.”

Ghawat and tarshud stand directly opposite one another.

This contrast matters for identifying the root’s core.

The poet distinguishes sound from unsound decisions, yet expresses tribal loyalty so strongly that recognizing error does not stop him accompanying his people.

The poem therefore does not make accompanying one’s people itself a sign of rushd.

The poet instead distinguishes the right course from tribal loyalty.

These verses appear in early poetry and literary collections. The second hemistich of the first verse also has al-nuṣḥ instead of al-rushd; tarshud in the third nevertheless retains the contrast between rushd and ghayy. This study does not independently establish their precise composition date.

Al-Afwah al-Awdī: sound guidance and refusing advice

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Afwah al-Awdī states:

لَا يَرْشُدُونَ وَلَنْ يَرْعَوْا لِمُرْشِدِهِمْ
فَالْجَهْلُ مِنْهُمْ مَعًا وَالْغَيُّ مِيعَادُ

lā yarshudūna wa-lan yarʿaw li-murshidihim / fa-al-jahlu minhum maʿan wa-al-ghayyu mīʿādu

“They do not take the right course or heed their guide; ignorance comes from them all, and error is their appointed meeting place.”

It contains two forms from this root: yarshudūna and murshidihim.

Yarshudūna concerns taking the right course.

Murshidihim concerns the person guiding them.

They occur together without having identical meanings.

A guide may point out a way that the listeners do not follow.

The presence of a guide therefore does not by itself ensure their sound conduct.

The verse occurs in early poetry collections and the collected poetry attributed to al-Afwah. Its second hemistich has variants, including minhum and fīhim; the examined transmissions retain lā yarshudūna wa-lan yarʿaw li-murshidihim. Its exact composition date is likewise not independently established here.

The witness connects the root with sound judgment, guidance, and human conduct.

What the early witnesses show

Durayd uses rushd for practical decisions and discerning the right course.

Al-Afwah contrasts rushd with ignorance and ghayy, while the murshid shows the right way.

In poetry attributed to pre-Islamic poets, these witnesses show a meaning extending beyond religion. Attribution and the exact date of each transmission nevertheless require separate assessment.

Neither establishes that rushd invariably brings a definite outcome, victory, or social power.

The clearer meaning concerns the right way and discerning and orienting oneself toward it.

3. Rushd, ghayy, and guidance in the Qur’an

Qad tabayyana al-rushdu mina al-ghayyi

Al-Baqarah 2:256 includes:

لَا إِكْرَاهَ فِي الدِّينِ قَدْ تَبَيَّنَ الرُّشْدُ مِنَ الْغَيِّ

lā ikrāha fī al-dīni qad tabayyana al-rushdu mina al-ghayyi

Al-Baqarah 2:256

“There is no compulsion in religion; the right course has become distinct from error.”

Al-rushdu stands opposite al-ghayy in this expression.

This is among the clearest Qur’anic evidence for the root’s meaning.

Rushd concerns the right direction and way; ghayy concerns deviation from it.

Making the right way clear should be distinguished from compelling someone to choose it.

The verse first says:

لَا إِكْرَاهَ فِي الدِّينِ

lā ikrāha fī al-dīni

Al-Baqarah 2:256

“There is no compulsion in religion.”

It then speaks of the distinction between rushd and ghayy becoming clear.

Within this verse, truth being clear does not become permission for coercion.

The continuation discusses rejecting false powers and believing in The God.

The right way here therefore relates to faith and rejecting ṭāghūt.

Rushd does not itself lexically mean rejecting ṭāghūt; that feature comes from the rest of the verse.

Sabīla al-rushdi and sabīla al-ghayyi

Al-Aʿrāf 7:146 includes:

وَإِنْ يَرَوْا سَبِيلَ الرُّشْدِ لَا يَتَّخِذُوهُ سَبِيلًا وَإِنْ يَرَوْا سَبِيلَ الْغَيِّ يَتَّخِذُوهُ سَبِيلًا

wa-in yaraw sabīla al-rushdi lā yattakhidhūhu sabīlan wa-in yaraw sabīla al-ghayyi yattakhidhūhu sabīlan

Al-Aʿrāf 7:146

“If they see the right way, they do not adopt it as their way; if they see the way of error, they adopt it.”

This clarifies the distinction between recognizing a way and choosing it.

The verse describes people who see the way of rushd but do not choose it.

They choose the way of ghayy instead.

Seeing or recognizing the right way does not itself mean following it.

This does not establish that every person who fails to accept the right way necessarily rejects it knowingly and with bad intent.

The verse concerns people described through arrogance, denial of the signs, and heedlessness.

Those qualities concern that group and its conduct; they should not be extended without evidence to everyone who errs.

Sabīl is from another root and explicitly supplies the meaning of a way.

Rushd identifies that way’s rightness.

Sabīla al-rushdi therefore means the right way, without making rushd invariably a physical path.

Guidance and rushd: related concepts

Hudā and rushd repeatedly occupy related semantic territory in the Qur’an.

For example, Al-Jinn 72:2 says of the Qur’an:

يَهْدِي إِلَى الرُّشْدِ

yahdī ilā al-rushdi

Al-Jinn 72:2

“It guides toward the right course.”

Guidance and rushd are related but are distinct words in this construction.

Yahdī is the verb of guiding.

Al-rushd is the right direction or course toward which the guidance leads.

This one construction does not establish that guidance always only means showing a way and rushd always only means actually following it.

The two roots have broader ranges in Arabic and the Qur’an.

Rushd, for example, can express sound decision-making capacity, while hudā also extends beyond showing a simple route in various settings.

The distinction is therefore a tendency: guidance concerns directing, while rushd emphasizes the right course, sound discernment, and considered orientation.

They overlap without being wholly identical.

Laʿallahum yarshudūna: responding to the divine call

Al-Baqarah 2:186 states:

وَإِذَا سَأَلَكَ عِبَادِي عَنِّي فَإِنِّي قَرِيبٌ أُجِيبُ دَعْوَةَ الدَّاعِ إِذَا دَعَانِ فَلْيَسْتَجِيبُوا لِي وَلْيُؤْمِنُوا بِي لَعَلَّهُمْ يَرْشُدُونَ

wa-idhā saʾalaka ʿibādī ʿannī fa-innī qarībun ujību daʿwata al-dāʿi idhā daʿāni fa-al-yastajībū lī wa-al-yuʾminū bī laʿallahum yarshudūna

Al-Baqarah 2:186

“When My servants ask you about Me, I am near. I answer the supplicant’s call when they call upon Me; let them also respond to Me and believe in Me, so that they may find the right course.”

Yarshudūna is a plural imperfect verb in Form I.

The verse speaks of divine nearness and responding to servants’ prayers, then asks them to respond and believe.

Its ending presents rushd as an aim of that orientation.

Yarshudūna should not be made to mean that their prayer must be answered in precisely the desired form.

Praying, responding to the divine call, believing, and finding the right course are related but distinct concepts.

Rushd here concerns a person’s right choice and orientation toward The God.

4. Rushdan in Al-Nisāʾ: managing property and independent judgment

An important contrasting use occurs in Al-Nisāʾ 4:6:

وَابْتَلُوا الْيَتَامَىٰ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا بَلَغُوا النِّكَاحَ فَإِنْ آنَسْتُمْ مِنْهُمْ رُشْدًا فَادْفَعُوا إِلَيْهِمْ أَمْوَالَهُمْ

wa-ibtalū al-yatāmā ḥattā idhā balaghū al-nikāḥa fa-in ānastum minhum rushdan fa-idfaʿū ilayhim amwālahum

Al-Nisāʾ 4:6

“Test the orphans until they reach marriageable maturity; if you perceive sound judgment in them, hand over their property to them.”

Rushdan here concerns managing property.

Rushd therefore extends beyond religious belief or knowledge of the hereafter’s way.

A person can have financial rushd: the capacity to understand and manage their property properly.

Puberty and sound judgment differ

The verse raises two separate matters.

First:

حَتَّىٰ إِذَا بَلَغُوا النِّكَاحَ

ḥattā idhā balaghū al-nikāḥa

Al-Nisāʾ 4:6

“Until they reach marriageable maturity.”

Second:

فَإِنْ آنَسْتُمْ مِنْهُمْ رُشْدًا

fa-in ānastum minhum rushdan

Al-Nisāʾ 4:6

“If you perceive sound judgment in them.”

Reaching marriageable maturity and establishing rushd are distinct matters in the verse’s construction.

Maturity concerns a stage of physical development and capacity associated with marriage.

Rushd here concerns discerning and managing property properly.

Financial capacity should therefore not be assumed without examination simply from reaching a certain age or bodily stage.

Conversely, financial rushd should not be expanded to knowing every rule, possessing abundant knowledge, or having complete life experience.

The verse concerns handing property to its owner.

The required judgment must relate to that responsibility.

Wa-ibtalū al-yatāmā: testing capacity

The verse begins with wa-ibtalū, instructing that the orphans be tested.

It belongs to B L W and concerns testing.

The continuation discusses recognizing sound judgment.

The sequence matters: capacity is recognized through assessment and observation.

Testing itself is not the meaning of rushd.

Rushd is competence and sound judgment; ibtilāʾ here expresses how it is assessed.

The verse does not prescribe one technical method for everyone.

Assessment can relate to the circumstances and financial responsibility, but rushd itself gives no detailed procedure.

Fa-in ānastum minhum rushdan

Ānastum belongs to A N S and here concerns perceiving signs of sound judgment.

A guardian’s mere assumption or wish about someone’s capacity is therefore not the issue.

Some sign of actual competence must be recognized.

Conversely, the expression should not become permission to retain people’s property indefinitely.

After establishing rushd, the verse says:

فَادْفَعُوا إِلَيْهِمْ أَمْوَالَهُمْ

fa-idfaʿū ilayhim amwālahum

Al-Nisāʾ 4:6

“Hand over their property to them.”

Recognizing sound judgment thus leads to giving the owner their property.

The rest of the verse also forbids consuming an orphan’s property wastefully and hastily.

Rushd relates to financial rights and guardians’ responsibilities here without granting guardians unrestricted power to deprive owners of their rights.

Does rushd mean wealth?

No.

Someone with little property can demonstrate suitable judgment in managing it.

Someone with abundant property may instead make poor decisions about its use.

Wealth and financial rushd are therefore different.

Nor should rushd be limited to profit in every transaction.

A sound financial decision may bring no immediate gain while preventing unjustified loss or preserving another person’s right.

The core here remains sound judgment and management, with property defining the particular field.

5. Rushd in Abraham’s story and Al-Kahf

Ātaynā ibrāhīma rushdahu

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:51 states:

وَلَقَدْ آتَيْنَا إِبْرَاهِيمَ رُشْدَهُ مِنْ قَبْلُ وَكُنَّا بِهِ عَالِمِينَ

wa-la-qad ātaynā ibrāhīma rushdahu min qablu wa-kunnā bihi ʿālimīna

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:51

“We had already given Abraham his sound judgment, and We knew him.”

Rushdahu is attributed to Abraham here.

The final pronoun hu refers to him.

The following verses have Abraham question the objects of his people’s devotion and invite reconsideration through argument.

His rushd thus relates to sound discernment concerning inherited beliefs and divine oneness.

Rushdahu itself does not establish that Abraham possessed every possible field of knowledge in every aspect of life from birth.

The verse expresses the gift of sound judgment without specifying the amount or every branch of his knowledge.

Min qablu marks the gift as earlier, without itself specifying Abraham’s exact age or its date.

Rushd here concerns his understanding and right orientation rather than physical maturity alone.

Wa-hayyiʾ lanā min amrinā rashadan

In the cave companions’ story, Al-Kahf 18:10 states:

إِذْ أَوَى الْفِتْيَةُ إِلَى الْكَهْفِ فَقَالُوا رَبَّنَا آتِنَا مِنْ لَدُنْكَ رَحْمَةً وَهَيِّئْ لَنَا مِنْ أَمْرِنَا رَشَدًا

idh awā al-fityatu ilā al-kahfi fa-qālū rabbanā ātinā min ladunka raḥmatan wa-hayyiʾ lanā min amrinā rashadan

Al-Kahf 18:10

“When the young people took refuge in the cave, they said: Our Lord, grant us mercy from Yourself and prepare for us a sound course in our affair.”

They ask The God for rashadan.

Their request concerns an actual difficult situation.

They have sought shelter in the cave and ask for an appropriate course and arrangement for their affairs.

Rashad can therefore concern a suitable decision and way forward in difficulty.

It does not itself mean shelter, cave, bodily safety, or certain rescue.

Those features come from the story and other words.

Nor does asking for rashad mean these young people possessed no understanding at all.

They had already shown their faith and chosen to seek refuge to preserve it.

Seeking rashad can therefore mean asking for guidance and a sound arrangement to continue something deliberately begun.

Murshidan: one who guides another to the right way

Al-Kahf 18:17 also states:

مَنْ يَهْدِ اللَّهُ فَهُوَ الْمُهْتَدِ وَمَنْ يُضْلِلْ فَلَنْ تَجِدَ لَهُ وَلِيًّا مُرْشِدًا

man yahdi allāhu fa-huwa al-muhtadi wa-man yuḍlil fa-lan tajida lahu waliyyan murshidan

Al-Kahf 18:17

“Whoever The God guides is rightly guided; whoever He leaves astray, you will find for them no protecting, guiding friend.”

Murshidan is the Form IV active participle of arshada.

It qualifies waliyyan here.

Waliyyan murshidan therefore describes someone serving as a protector or guardian and guiding toward the right way.

Waliyy and murshid are distinct words.

Not every guardian necessarily guides rightly; murshid itself does not mean unrestricted authority over others.

The context relates guidance and error to The God, but murshid itself should not become a title for someone with absolute knowledge or independent power to guide.

Li-aqraba min hādhā rashadan

Al-Kahf 18:24 includes:

وَقُلْ عَسَىٰ أَنْ يَهْدِيَنِ رَبِّي لِأَقْرَبَ مِنْ هَٰذَا رَشَدًا

wa-qul ʿasā an yahdiyani rabbī li-aqraba min hādhā rashadan

Al-Kahf 18:24

“And say: Perhaps my Lord will guide me to a course nearer than this to what is right.”

Rashadan appears in a construction expressing greater closeness to the right course.

Aqraba is comparative and expresses being nearer.

A person can therefore seek a course more suitable in rightness and guidance than the one previously contemplated.

This does not make rushd invariably a numerical quantity or measurable distance.

Nearness here concerns direction and rightness, rather than necessarily geographical distance.

The verse asks for guidance rather than claiming certain knowledge of every future course.

Moses asks to learn rushd

Al-Kahf 18:66 states:

قَالَ لَهُ مُوسَىٰ هَلْ أَتَّبِعُكَ عَلَىٰ أَنْ تُعَلِّمَنِ مِمَّا عُلِّمْتَ رُشْدًا

qāla lahu mūsā hal attabiʿuka ʿalā an tuʿallimani mimmā ʿullimta rushdan

Al-Kahf 18:66

“Moses said to him: May I follow you so that you teach me sound guidance from what you have been taught?”

Rushd relates to learning here.

Moses asks someone to teach him part of the knowledge given to that person.

Rushd can therefore be something taught and learned.

Knowledge and rushd nevertheless differ.

Knowledge can enable recognition of the right course; rushd here also concerns sound understanding and orientation in the subject taught.

Moses subsequently encounters events whose causes and results are initially unclear to him.

Explanations are then given.

Part of learning thus concerns understanding the relationship between actions, circumstances, and consequences.

The story does not establish that every incomprehensible or objectionable action necessarily has a hidden sound wisdom and must be accepted without examination.

It concerns particular events and explanations the Qur’an itself supplies.

Nor does Moses’ request authorize unconditional obedience to anyone claiming special knowledge.

Rushd should be assessed by truth and rightness, rather than a teacher’s claim alone.

6. Rashīd and rashād: sound judgment and claims to leadership

A-laysa minkum rajulun rashīdun

In Lot’s story, Hūd 11:78 includes:

فَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ وَلَا تُخْزُونِ فِي ضَيْفِي أَلَيْسَ مِنْكُمْ رَجُلٌ رَشِيدٌ

fa-ittaqū allāha wa-lā tukhzūni fī ḍayfī a-laysa minkum rajulun rashīdun

Hūd 11:78

“Be mindful of The God and do not disgrace me concerning my guests. Is there not a discerning man among you?”

Rashīd describes a person here.

Confronting his people’s behavior, Lot asks whether anyone has sound judgment and conduct.

Rashīd should not be reduced to an adult male or a person of social rank.

The verse concerns wrongful behavior toward guests and Lot’s call to mindfulness of The God.

Rashīd here therefore describes someone able to distinguish right conduct and take a proper stance against unjustified behavior.

That conduct comes from context; rashīd itself does not lexically mean protecting guests.

Innaka la-anta al-ḥalīmu al-rashīdu

In Shuʿayb’s story, Hūd 11:87 states:

قَالُوا يَا شُعَيْبُ أَصَلَاتُكَ تَأْمُرُكَ أَنْ نَتْرُكَ مَا يَعْبُدُ آبَاؤُنَا أَوْ أَنْ نَفْعَلَ فِي أَمْوَالِنَا مَا نَشَاءُ إِنَّكَ لَأَنْتَ الْحَلِيمُ الرَّشِيدُ

qālū yā shuʿaybu a-ṣalātuka taʾmuruka an natruka mā yaʿbudu ābāʾunā aw an nafʿala fī amwālinā mā nashāʾu innaka la-anta al-ḥalīmu al-rashīdu

Hūd 11:87

“They said: Shuʿayb, does your ṣalāh command you that we abandon what our fathers worshipped, or refrain from doing as we wish with our property? You are indeed the forbearing, discerning one!”

Al-rashīdu occurs in the speech of Shuʿayb’s opponents.

Ḥalīm and rashīd are literally favorable descriptions.

The context, however, shows objection and possible ridicule.

His opponents question his call about worship and financial conduct, then call him forbearing and discerning.

Early interpretations often understand the expression as ironic or mocking.

The word’s literal meaning should nevertheless be distinguished from the speakers’ intention.

Rashīd still lexically concerns discernment and the right course.

Their mocking use emerges from the exchange and its objectional tone.

An ironic use does not change a word’s basic meaning.

Wa-mā amru firʿawna bi-rashīdin

Hūd 11:97 includes:

فَاتَّبَعُوا أَمْرَ فِرْعَوْنَ وَمَا أَمْرُ فِرْعَوْنَ بِرَشِيدٍ

fa-ittabaʿū amra firʿawna wa-mā amru firʿawna bi-rashīdin

Hūd 11:97

“They followed Pharaoh’s command, although Pharaoh’s command was not sound or rightly guiding.”

Rashīd here matters because it describes amr rather than a person.

Amru firʿawn can concern Pharaoh’s command or manner and direction of government.

The Qur’an says that this amr was not rashīd.

Rashīd therefore extends beyond a person’s character to decisions, commands, or a course of administration.

Hūd 11:98 continues:

يَقْدُمُ قَوْمَهُ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ فَأَوْرَدَهُمُ النَّارَ

yaqdumu qawmahu yawma al-qiyāmati fa-awradahumu al-nāra

Hūd 11:98

“He goes before his people on the Day of Resurrection and leads them into the Fire.”

This clarifies the outcome of following Pharaoh’s command.

Rashīd does not lexically mean rescue from the Fire; a course’s or command’s rightness here relates to its ultimate outcome.

Sabīla al-rashādi in Pharaoh’s speech

Ghāfir 40:29 includes:

قَالَ فِرْعَوْنُ مَا أُرِيكُمْ إِلَّا مَا أَرَىٰ وَمَا أَهْدِيكُمْ إِلَّا سَبِيلَ الرَّشَادِ

qāla firʿawnu mā urīkum illā mā arā wa-mā ahdīkum illā sabīla al-rashādi

Ghāfir 40:29

“Pharaoh said: I show you only what I see, and I guide you only to the right way.”

Pharaoh himself claims to guide people to rashād.

This use is especially useful for understanding the root.

Calling one’s course sabīla al-rashād does not itself prove its rightness.

Hūd 11:97 explicitly describes Pharaoh’s command as lacking rashīd judgment.

His claim and the Qur’an’s judgment therefore differ.

A speaker can claim rushd or rashād without establishing that it actually exists.

This linguistic point extends beyond Pharaoh: anyone may call their course right, but its rightness requires independent assessment.

Ahdikum sabīla al-rashādi in the believer’s speech

Ghāfir 40:38 states:

وَقَالَ الَّذِي آمَنَ يَا قَوْمِ اتَّبِعُونِ أَهْدِكُمْ سَبِيلَ الرَّشَادِ

wa-qāla alladhī āmana yā qawmi ittabiʿūni ahdikum sabīla al-rashādi

Ghāfir 40:38

“The one who believed said: My people, follow me; I will guide you to the right way.”

Sabīla al-rashādi occurs in the believing man’s speech here.

Its wording resembles Pharaoh’s claim in Ghāfir 40:29.

Their contextual orientations differ.

Pharaoh defends his power and viewpoint; the believer warns his people of a wrongful outcome and invites another course.

The believer continues in Ghāfir 40:39:

يَا قَوْمِ إِنَّمَا هَٰذِهِ الْحَيَاةُ الدُّنْيَا مَتَاعٌ وَإِنَّ الْآخِرَةَ هِيَ دَارُ الْقَرَارِ

yā qawmi innamā hādhihi al-ḥayātu al-dunyā matāʿun wa-inna al-ākhirata hiya dāru al-qarāri

Ghāfir 40:39

“My people, this worldly life is only passing enjoyment, and the hereafter is the abiding home.”

A way’s rightness should therefore be assessed through its content and outcome rather than the speaker’s chosen title alone.

Al-rashād means the right way in both expressions, but the two claims differ in truth and context.

7. Rushd, faith, and seeking the right course in Al-Jinn

This root occurs four times in Al-Jinn.

The uses connect rushd with the Qur’an’s guidance, willing good, deliberate choice, and the messenger’s limits.

Yahdī ilā al-rushdi

Al-Jinn 72:1 and Al-Jinn 72:2 include:

إِنَّا سَمِعْنَا قُرْآنًا عَجَبًا ۝ يَهْدِي إِلَى الرُّشْدِ فَآمَنَّا بِهِ وَلَنْ نُشْرِكَ بِرَبِّنَا أَحَدًا

innā samiʿnā qurʾānan ʿajaban / yahdī ilā al-rushdi fa-āmannā bihi wa-lan nushrika bi-rabbinā aḥadan

Al-Jinn 72:1; Al-Jinn 72:2

“We have heard a wondrous Qur’an guiding to the right course, so we believe in it and will associate no one with our Lord.”

Rushd concerns the direction toward which the Qur’an guides people.

The listeners express faith after receiving the message.

The Qur’an’s guidance here accompanies understanding and a choice of faith.

Al-rushd does not itself lexically mean the Qur’an; the Qur’an guides toward rushd.

Fa-āmannā bihi expresses the listeners’ response, rather than an independent part of the root meaning.

Am arāda bihim rabbuhum rashadan

Al-Jinn 72:10 also states:

وَأَنَّا لَا نَدْرِي أَشَرٌّ أُرِيدَ بِمَنْ فِي الْأَرْضِ أَمْ أَرَادَ بِهِمْ رَبُّهُمْ رَشَدًا

wa-annā lā nadrī a-sharrun urīda bi-man fī al-arḍi am arāda bihim rabbuhum rashadan

Al-Jinn 72:10

“We do not know whether evil has been intended for those on earth or their Lord has intended a sound course and outcome for them.”

Rashadan stands opposite sharrun here.

Rushd can therefore relate to good and a worthy outcome.

That does not make rushd exactly synonymous with good in every occurrence.

The verse concerns an intention and outcome for earth’s inhabitants about which the speakers admit ignorance.

Intention and outcome clarify the meaning in this setting.

Lā nadrī must also be retained in understanding the verse.

The speakers acknowledge limited knowledge rather than claiming full understanding of the event.

Fa-ulāʾika taḥarraw rashadan

Al-Jinn 72:14 states:

وَأَنَّا مِنَّا الْمُسْلِمُونَ وَمِنَّا الْقَاسِطُونَ فَمَنْ أَسْلَمَ فَأُولَٰئِكَ تَحَرَّوْا رَشَدًا

wa-annā minnā al-muslimūna wa-minnā al-qāsiṭūna fa-man aslama fa-ulāʾika taḥarraw rashadan

Al-Jinn 72:14

“Among us are those who submit and those who deviate from justice; those who submit have sought and chosen the right course.”

Rashadan appears with taḥarraw.

Taḥarrā concerns carefully seeking and intending something.

Rushd thus relates to deliberate choice here.

It is not merely an external condition imposed without human orientation.

In this setting, people have sought and proceeded toward the right way.

Rashadan itself does not mean searching; taḥarraw supplies that meaning.

Submission relates to choosing rightly here, but a verbal claim to submission alone does not establish someone’s actual rushd.

Lā amliku lakum ḍarran wa-lā rashadan

Al-Jinn 72:21 states:

قُلْ إِنِّي لَا أَمْلِكُ لَكُمْ ضَرًّا وَلَا رَشَدًا

qul innī lā amliku lakum ḍarran wa-lā rashadan

Al-Jinn 72:21

“Say: I possess for you neither harm nor sound guidance and well-being.”

The messenger is instructed to state his limits of ability and authority.

Ḍarran concerns harm.

Rashadan here can concern bringing someone to the right course and soundness.

This does not mean the messenger has no capacity to convey a message and guide people.

Al-Jinn 72:22 and Al-Jinn 72:23 continue with seeking refuge in The God and conveying His messages.

Guiding and conveying a message should therefore be distinguished from independent control over others’ guidance and fate.

The verse denies independent authority to bring harm or rushd to those addressed.

Rashadan should not be restricted to financial gain, economic success, or worldly advantage either.

Those meanings may arise in some uses concerning success and well-being, but here rushd relates to guidance and divine authority.

8. Ulāʾika humu al-rāshidūna: believers’ orientation

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:7 states:

وَاعْلَمُوا أَنَّ فِيكُمْ رَسُولَ اللَّهِ لَوْ يُطِيعُكُمْ فِي كَثِيرٍ مِنَ الْأَمْرِ لَعَنِتُّمْ وَلَٰكِنَّ اللَّهَ حَبَّبَ إِلَيْكُمُ الْإِيمَانَ وَزَيَّنَهُ فِي قُلُوبِكُمْ وَكَرَّهَ إِلَيْكُمُ الْكُفْرَ وَالْفُسُوقَ وَالْعِصْيَانَ أُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الرَّاشِدُونَ

wa-iʿlamū anna fīkum rasūla allāhi law yuṭīʿukum fī kathīrin mina al-amri la-ʿanittum wa-lākinna allāha ḥabbaba ilaykumu al-īmāna wa-zayyanahu fī qulūbikum wa-karraha ilaykumu al-kufra wa-al-fusūqa wa-al-ʿiṣyāna ulāʾika humu al-rāshidūna

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:7

“Know that the messenger of The God is among you. If he followed you in many matters, you would suffer hardship. But The God has made faith dear to you, adorned it in your hearts, and made disbelief, transgression, and disobedience hateful to you. Those are the rightly discerning.”

Al-rāshidūna is the sound masculine plural of rāshid.

It is morphologically the active participle of a Form I verb.

The rightly discerning are described through a particular orientation toward faith, disbelief, transgression, and disobedience.

Is rushd identical to faith?

They are related in this verse but lexically distinct.

Ḥabbaba ilaykumu al-īmāna expresses faith being made dear.

Karraha ilaykumu al-kufra wa-al-fusūqa wa-al-ʿiṣyāna expresses aversion to opposing orientations.

Al-rāshidūna describes those associated with the right course in this setting.

Rushd therefore relates to belief and conduct here.

That does not make rāshid mean only believer.

Other verses use the family for financial competence, sound decisions, and guidance to the right way.

Rushd and independent judgment

The verse begins by warning about the messenger following people’s wishes.

Collective wishes are therefore not necessarily always sound decisions.

People may favor an opinion that, accepted without assessment, brings difficulty and harm.

This does not make every collective opinion wrong or deny the right to ask, examine, or criticize.

The setting concerns believers’ relationship with the messenger and divine guidance.

The relevant point is that a decision’s soundness is not determined merely by its supporters’ numbers or insistence.

This agrees with other uses, especially Pharaoh calling his course sabīl al-rashād while the Qur’an denies his command’s rightness.

9. Comparison with related roots

R SH D (rushd) and H D Y (hudā)

Qur’anic hudā concerns guidance and direction.

Rushd concerns the right way, sound judgment, and appropriate orientation.

Al-Jinn 72:2 combines them:

يَهْدِي إِلَى الرُّشْدِ

yahdī ilā al-rushdi

Al-Jinn 72:2

“It guides to the right course.”

They are related but have different roles in the sentence.

They should not, however, be separated through absolute divisions.

Guidance can also concern receiving and following the right way, and rushd can be taught and guided toward.

The distinction should follow actual uses and context.

Study of H D Y

R SH D (rushd) and GH W Y (ghayy)

Ghayy is among rushd’s most important Qur’anic opposites.

Al-Baqarah 2:256 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:146 explicitly contrast them.

Rushd concerns the right course and ghayy deviation from it.

The contrast does not establish that every misguided person has knowingly and deliberately chosen error.

Some verses discuss arrogance and knowing denial; elsewhere ignorance or error may lead someone astray.

Moral responsibility and intention therefore require separate contextual evidence.

R SH D (rushd) and Ṣ L Ḥ (ṣalāḥ)

Ṣalāḥ concerns soundness, proper order, and freedom from corruption.

Rushd also concerns right judgment and course.

They are especially close in some uses.

Financial rushd, for example, can appear in managing property properly and avoiding foolish decisions.

Qur’anic ṣalāḥ concerns people, deeds, relationships, and various conditions.

Rushd more prominently emphasizes the right course and direction, discernment, and proceeding accordingly.

This is a tendency rather than an absolute boundary.

Study of Ṣ L Ḥ

R SH D (rushd) and ʿ Q L (ʿaql)

Qur’anic ʿaql concerns thinking, understanding, and using discernment.

Rushd likewise concerns sound judgment without being identical.

Someone can be capable of reasoning yet make a wrong decision.

Competence in one field, such as managing property, likewise does not mean complete knowledge of every subject.

Rushd should therefore not be restricted to having intellect or performing mental assessment.

Right direction and decisions also matter in this family.

Study of ʿ Q L

R SH D (rushd) and B L GH (bulūgh)

Bulūgh concerns reaching a particular stage or limit.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:6 separately mentions reaching marriageable maturity and establishing sound judgment.

This distinguishes puberty from financial rushd.

Rushd should therefore not mean reaching puberty in every Qur’anic use.

Physical growth, increased height and weight, or a living thing becoming larger can be expressed through other Arabic words such as numuww and kibar.

The modern Persian use of rushd for physical growth should not automatically define every Qur’anic derivative of R SH D.

10. Translation proposals for Forqan

One fixed equivalent is insufficient for every verse in this family.

رَشَدَ / رَشِدَ: Rashada/rashida: found the right way, judged soundly, followed a proper course in one’s affairs.

رُشْد: Rushd: the right course, sound judgment, capacity for proper decisions and conduct, according to context.

رَشَد: Rashad: right guidance, a worthy direction or arrangement, a sound outcome or decision, according to its subject.

رَشَاد: Rashād: rightness of course, the right way, considered orientation.

رَاشِد: Rāshid: rightly guided, discerning, someone who has chosen the right course.

رَشِيد: Rashīd: discerning, judicious, sound in direction and judgment.

أَرْشَدَ: Arshada: guided, showed the right way.

إِرْشَاد: Irshād: guiding toward the right way.

مُرْشِد: Murshid: a guide who directs another toward the right way.

اِسْتَرْشَدَ: Istarshada: asked for guidance, sought the right way.

تَرْشِيد: Tarshīd: guiding and ordering judgment or conduct; in modern use, making consumption and resource management prudent and efficient.

11. Conclusion

Arabic R SH D concerns finding and following the right way, sound judgment, and appropriate direction in affairs.

Lexical explanations contrast rushd and rashād with ghayy and record uses for physical paths as well as human decisions and conduct.

Poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period likewise shows a meaning beyond religion. Durayd’s poetry concerns practical discernment opposed to ghayy; al-Afwah’s distinguishes finding the right course from guiding another.

The Qur’an uses this root nineteen times across a broad semantic field.

Al-Baqarah 2:256 contrasts rushd with ghayy and expresses the distinction between the right way and error. The same verse combines that clarity with the absence of compulsion in religion.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:146 describes people who see the right way without choosing it, distinguishing recognition from following.

Rushd in Al-Nisāʾ 4:6 concerns an orphan’s capacity to manage property. The verse distinguishes marriageable maturity from sound judgment, so rushd cannot mean physical maturity alone.

In Abraham’s story, rushd concerns his understanding and right orientation.

In Al-Kahf, it can be requested in prayer, sought as a suitable arrangement, learned, and guided toward.

Rashīd describes a discerning person in Lot’s story, occurs ironically in Shuʿayb’s opponents’ speech, and is denied of Pharaoh’s command.

Pharaoh and the believer both speak of sabīl al-rashād in Ghāfir, but a speaker’s claim alone does not establish a way’s soundness. Its content, direction, and outcome require assessment.

In Al-Jinn, rushd concerns the Qur’an’s guidance, intending good, deliberately seeking the right course, and the messenger’s limits of authority.

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:7 describes the rightly discerning through faith and aversion to disbelief, transgression, and disobedience.

These uses go beyond information or achieving a desired result: sound judgment, course, and orientation are central.

Contextual features such as faith, mindfulness of The God, financial administration, learning, following a guide, or ultimate salvation should not all be built into the root’s independent meaning.

Rushd applies in religious and practical affairs. Variant rushd and rashad readings do not justify separating those fields absolutely and without exception.

Rushd should not be equated with attachment to previous beliefs, following the crowd, obeying power, or accepting a self-proclaimed guide either.

Final definition: R SH D concerns the right course, sound judgment, and appropriate direction in affairs. Rushd and rashad can express right guidance or competent judgment; rāshid and rashīd describe a person or matter characterized by soundness and discernment; murshid guides another to the right way. Qur’anic uses extend from religious guidance to managing property and discerning rightly in different situations. The kind of rushd, its field, and how far it is realized must follow the form, subject, and verse context rather than a single restrictive definition for every derivative.
