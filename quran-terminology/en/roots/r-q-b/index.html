The Qur’anic Root R Q B (Raqabah, Raqīb, and Irtiqāb)

Guarding, watching, waiting, and observing obligations: from the neck and an elevated lookout to honoring covenants, freeing people in bondage, and divine watchfulness

1. The root and its Qur’anic forms

In older Arabic, R Q B concerns keeping something in view, watching over it, waiting, and guarding. Someone standing on high ground to observe an enemy’s movements keeps watch; someone awaiting news looks out for it; someone observing a covenant or right refrains from disregarding or violating it.

From this family, رَقِيب (raqīb) denotes a guardian, watcher, or someone whose attention does not overlook what is watched. تَرَقُّب (taraqqub) and اِرْتِقَاب (irtiqāb) concern waiting and watching for what may happen.

The family also has another important branch: رَقَبَة (raqabah), the neck of a person or animal. Arabic subsequently also uses the word for a whole person, especially someone held in slavery.

The Qur’an reflects this range. Raqīb describes The God and also a watcher beside a person; yarqubu concerns respecting a covenant or a statement; yataraqqabu describes Moses watching his surroundings in danger; irtaqib concerns awaiting a future event; and raqabah concerns the neck, a person’s release, and emancipation.

The root occurs 24 times in 20 verses, in six morphological groups:

  • The basic form I verb يَرْقُبُ (yarqubu) and its inflections: three occurrences.
  • Form V يَتَرَقَّبُ (yataraqqabu): two occurrences.
  • Form VIII imperative اِرْتَقِبْ (irtaqib) and its inflections: four occurrences.
  • The noun رَقَبَة (raqabah) and plural رِقَاب (riqāb): nine occurrences.
  • The description رَقِيب (raqīb): five occurrences.
  • Form VIII active participle مُرْتَقِبُونَ (murtaqibūn): one occurrence.

The three occurrences of رَقَبَةٍ (raqabatin) in Al-Nisāʾ 4:92 are counted separately. This is one reason the number of word occurrences exceeds the number of verses.

The root must not be confined to a single application. Raqīb does not mean “slave,” just as raqabah in emancipation provisions does not mean “guardian.” Nevertheless, lexicographers propose a connection between an upright neck and watching from a height; both the evidence and the limits of that explanation require examination.

2. The semantic core in older Arabic

Raising the neck to observe and watch

Ibn Fāris writes in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

«أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ مُطَّرِدٌ، يَدُلُّ عَلَى انْتِصَابٍ لِمُرَاعَاةِ شَيْءٍ»

aṣlun wāḥidun muṭṭaridun yadullu ʿalā intiṣābin li-murāʿāti shayʾin

Translation: “One consistent semantic basis, indicating standing upright to keep watch over something.”

He identifies raqīb as a guardian and مَرْقَب (marqab) as an elevated position on which an observer stands. He also connects raqabah with the neck’s upright posture.

This explanation provides a clear image of keeping watch. A person wishing to see something at a distance may raise the head and neck or stand on high ground for a better view.

That image must nevertheless be distinguished from the words’ established meanings.

Keeping watch does not always require physically raising the neck. Someone can watch a child nearby, attend silently to a statement, or await an event without looking toward the horizon.

Likewise, raqabah as a body-part name in a statement about striking a neck or freeing an enslaved person does not necessarily depict someone raising the neck to look around.

Ibn Fāris’s explanation thus proposes an intelligible connection between meanings; it must not become a compulsory image governing every derivative’s translation.

Raqaba: watching, preserving, and waiting

Lisān al-ʿArab records several related senses for رَقَبَهُ (raqabahu): watching someone or something, waiting, keeping watch, and protecting.

Raqīb is also explained through حَفِيظ (ḥafīẓ) and مُنْتَظِر (muntaẓir): someone who guards or waits.

Although distinct, these two directions often meet in ordinary experience. A guardian typically watches something to notice a possible event. Someone awaiting a traveler’s arrival may likewise keep looking down the road.

Waiting and protecting are not complete synonyms. Someone can await an event without being able to prevent it; a guardian can protect something without waiting for a particular event at a particular time.

Context must therefore retain its role in the Qur’anic uses of irtaqib and raqīb.

Marqab: a lookout position

Lexicons use مَرْقَب (marqab) and مَرْقَبَة (marqabah) for elevated ground or a position from which a person surveys the surroundings and observes distant events.

This can be a hill, a natural elevation, a tower, or a point overlooking other people’s route.

Lisān al-ʿArab explains marqabah as a lookout at the top of a mountain or fortress.

This application shows the clear connection of watching and guarding with seeing and paying attention in Arabic.

Yet marqab does not always mean a constructed tower: it can be a natural elevation. The word should therefore not be confined to military buildings or wartime surveillance.

Raqabah: the neck, and then the whole person

In Arabic, raqabah names the neck; more specific explanations sometimes identify the back of the neck or its junction with the body.

Its plurals can include رِقَاب (riqāb), رَقَبَات (raqabāt), and رَقَب (raqab).

In referring to people, a body-part name sometimes stands for the whole person. Just as various languages use “heads” to count people or animals, Arabic uses raqabah for someone under another person’s control or in bondage.

Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī explains raqabah as originally a body-part name, then used for the whole person and, in a social application, for enslaved people.

This is an extension in which a part names the whole.

It does not imply that the Qur’an presents a person merely as a neck or as lifeless property. This usage of raqabah is a familiar linguistic designation; emancipation concerns the actual person and release from bondage.

Nor does Qur’anic riqāb always concern slavery. In Muḥammad 47:4, the necks in the battle scene retain their physical meaning. A distinction is therefore needed between the body part and raqabah as a figurative designation for a person in bondage.

3. Older Arabic evidence and social usages

Watching from elevated ground

Lisān al-ʿArab quotes this verse to explain marqabah:

وَمَرْقَبَةٍ كَالزُّجِّ أَشْرَفْتُ رَأْسَهَا أُقَلِّبُ طَرْفِي فِي فَضَاءٍ عَرِيضِ

wa-marqabatin ka-al-zujji ashraftu raʾsahā uqallibu ṭarfī fī faḍāʾin ʿarīḍi

Translation: “And a lookout like a spear’s lower iron fitting: I climbed to its summit, turning my gaze across a broad expanse.”

The lookout in this witness is high and commands a view of the surroundings. From there, the poet can sweep his gaze across open space.

Here zujj is the iron fitting at the lower end of a spear; it should not be confused with sinān, the spearhead at the upper end. The comparison evokes the elevation’s narrow shape and difficulty of ascent, rather than making the lookout itself necessarily an implement of war.

Lisān al-ʿArab, Z J J

Lisān al-ʿArab does not name the poet here. However, the same verse appears in Ashʿār al-Shuʿarāʾ al-Sittah al-Jāhiliyyīn, in the poems of Imruʾ al-Qays, with the variant أشرفت فوقها (ashraftu fawqahā). The attribution to his poetry therefore has textual support. The quotation above retains the Lisān reading, and a transmitted attribution should not be equated with a manuscript contemporary with the poet.

Ashʿār al-Shuʿarāʾ al-Sittah: the Imruʾ al-Qays witness

The image helps explain raqīb, but does not exhaust its meaning. A guardian can watch from high ground; observing a covenant, statement, or person’s conduct does not necessarily require an elevated position.

Watching a star while awaiting departure

The same source gives this poetic expression:

يُرَاقِبُ النَّجْمَ رِقَابَ الْحُوتِ

yurāqibu al-najma riqāba al-ḥūti

Translation: “He watches the star with eager expectation, like a fish longing for water.”

Ibn al-Aʿrābī transmits the witness about someone awaiting a star’s rising to determine when to set out on a journey.

The person is not simply looking at the star: he awaits an occurrence important to his next decision.

Observation and expectation can therefore coexist in applications of R Q B.

Keeping watch does not always entail anxiety. Waiting can be calm, hopeful, fearful, or threatening. The situation determines the emotional state.

The source examined does not securely identify the hemistich’s poet or date. It therefore supplies no independent basis for assigning this usage to the period before the Qur’an.

Raqīb in drawing lots

One specialized use of raqīb in older Arab society appears in drawing lots with arrows and distributing shares.

Some sources use raqīb for the trusted person or supervisor overseeing the distribution and drawing of lots.

In other sources, the same word names one of the arrows carrying shares, particularly the third of those participating in the draw.

A verse by Kaʿb ibn Zuhayr states:

لَهَا خَلْفَ أَذْنَابِهَا أَزْمَلٌ مَكَانَ الرَّقِيبِ مِنَ الْيَاسِرِينَا

lahā khalfa adhnābihā azmalun makāna al-raqībi mina al-yāsirīnā

Sense translation: “Behind their tails there is a sound, in a position like that of the supervisor among the lot drawers.”

The poet uses the supervisor’s position among the participants in drawing lots to form a comparison.

Ibn Sīda’s Al-Muḥkam and Lisān al-ʿArab quote this witness in explaining raqīb.

This application shows that supervision in older Arabic was not confined to military observation: it also occurred in particular social practices.

The word raqīb does not, however, inherently mean gambling, a draw, or an arrow. These are specialized applications within a particular practice.

Kaʿb ibn Zuhayr lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, and the evidence examined does not securely date this particular verse. It should not be presented without qualification as pre-Qur’anic poetry.

Recording a practice in a lexicon does not amount to religious approval of it.

Ruqbā: a gift dependent on awaiting a death

Arabic رُقْبَى (ruqbā) named a type of grant of property or a house, whose reversion or retention depended on one party outliving the other.

Lexical explanations state that each party could await the other’s death in such an arrangement; this connects the name ruqbā with watching and waiting.

Ibn Fāris and al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī report this usage.

The subject here is neither a physical neck nor watching from a mountain, but awaiting a future event.

This example shows the extension of expectation in the R Q B family to financial and social relationships.

Nevertheless, ruqbā does not occur in the Qur’an. Provisions governing this kind of grant must not be inserted into the meanings of Qur’anic yarqubūn or irtaqib.

Raqūb: waiting with sorrow or caution

Lexicons use رَقُوب (raqūb) for a woman whose children do not survive, or who, having experienced children’s deaths, continually fears losing them.

Another recorded application describes a she-camel that hangs back from a crowded watering place and waits for the other camels to drink.

Ibn Fāris connects these applications with waiting and keeping watch.

The two situations do not have the same value or emotional character. One can concern anxiety and grief, the other caution or an animal’s behavior beside water.

The waiting itself must therefore be distinguished from its cause.

These usages illuminate the language’s range, but the form raqūb does not occur in the Qur’an.

4. Watching and observing obligations: yarqubu with covenants and statements

The three Qur’anic occurrences of the basic form I verb are not confined to visual observation. They foreground respecting and taking account of a right or statement.

Disregarding bonds and covenants

Al-Tawbah 9:8

كَيْفَ وَإِنْ يَظْهَرُوا عَلَيْكُمْ لَا يَرْقُبُوا فِيكُمْ إِلًّا وَلَا ذِمَّةً ۚ يُرْضُونَكُمْ بِأَفْوَاهِهِمْ وَتَأْبَىٰ قُلُوبُهُمْ وَأَكْثَرُهُمْ فَاسِقُونَ

kayfa wa-in yaẓharū ʿalaykum lā yarqubū fīkum illan wa-lā dhimmatan yurḍūnakum bi-afwāhihim wa-taʾbā qulūbuhum wa-aktharuhum fāsiqūna

Translation: “How could it be, when, if they prevail over you, they respect neither a bond nor a covenant concerning you? They please you with their mouths, but their hearts refuse, and most of them are disobedient.”

Here يَرْقُبُوا (yarqubū) is an imperfect verb in the jussive as the conditional consequence. لَا (lā) supplies negation, while فِيكُمْ (fīkum) identifies the addressees as those toward whom the obligation is disregarded.

Two separate words follow the verb: إِلًّا (illan) and ذِمَّةً (dhimmatan).

Early sources give several explanations of ill: kinship, covenant, oath, and even a designation of The God. In this setting, dhimmah likewise concerns a covenant or undertaking.

Al-Ṭabarī reports these differences at length. Since Arabic ill can encompass several related meanings, he does not restrict the verse’s force to just one.

For the study of R Q B, the key point is that the verb concerns respecting a right or bond, rather than merely looking at it.

Someone can be fully aware of a covenant and still violate it. In this construction, R Q B therefore concerns practical conduct toward an undertaking.

“They do not look at the covenant” is consequently neither natural nor precise. The point is their failure to respect a covenant or right.

The scope of the verse’s judgment

The statement occurs amid the particular covenants and confrontations of Sūrat al-Tawbah.

Al-Tawbah 9:4 and Al-Tawbah 9:7 distinguish those who have kept their covenants from those who have broken them.

Al-Tawbah 9:8 should therefore not be turned into a general judgment about every non-Muslim.

The criticized conduct is that the people under discussion would disregard bonds and obligations if they gained the upper hand, while their outward words conflict with their inward intent.

Such conduct is blameworthy, but R Q B itself does not mean unbelief, war, or hostility.

The same expression in Al-Tawbah 9:10

Al-Tawbah 9:10

لَا يَرْقُبُونَ فِي مُؤْمِنٍ إِلًّا وَلَا ذِمَّةً ۚ وَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْمُعْتَدُونَ

lā yarqubūna fī muʾminin illan wa-lā dhimmatan wa-ulāʾika humu al-muʿtadūna

Translation: “They respect neither a bond nor a covenant concerning a believer; those are the transgressors.”

Here يَرْقُبُونَ (yarqubūna) is an indicative imperfect, unlike the jussive yarqubū in Al-Tawbah 9:8.

The verb’s principal meaning has not changed.

Again, bonds and covenants are disregarded. The Qur’an then identifies these people as الْمُعْتَدُونَ (al-muʿtadūn), the transgressors.

That word belongs to ʿ D W and concerns exceeding a limit.

The negated verb لا يرقبون (lā yarqubūn) must therefore be distinguished from the subsequent judgment of transgression.

Disregarding a covenant can be an instance of transgression, but R Q B does not inherently mean transgressing or breaking a covenant. Negation and the verb’s object together constitute the criticized conduct.

Wa-lam tarqub qawlī: observing Moses’ instruction

Ṭā Hā 20:94

قَالَ يَبْنَؤُمَّ لَا تَأْخُذْ بِلِحْيَتِي وَلَا بِرَأْسِي ۖ إِنِّي خَشِيتُ أَنْ تَقُولَ فَرَّقْتَ بَيْنَ بَنِي إِسْرَائِيلَ وَلَمْ تَرْقُبْ قَوْلِي

qāla ya-bna-umma lā taʾkhudh bi-liḥyatī wa-lā bi-raʾsī innī khashītu an taqūla farraqta bayna banī Isrāʾīla wa-lam tarqub qawlī

Translation: “He said: ‘O son of my mother, do not seize my beard or my head. I feared that you would say: “You divided the Children of Israel and did not observe my instruction.”’”

Aaron says this in reply to Moses.

After the people have turned to the calf, Moses speaks with Aaron. Aaron explains his fear of creating division among the Children of Israel and of Moses accusing him of disregarding his instruction.

تَرْقُبْ (tarqub) is an imperfect verb made jussive by لَمْ (lam); قَوْلِي (qawlī) is its object.

Lisān al-ʿArab gives not awaiting or not taking account of the statement among the explanations of lam tarqub qawlī. Commentators likewise explain it through preserving and observing Moses’ instruction.

In context, observing the instruction is stronger than a purely temporal sense of waiting.

Why?

Aaron speaks about possible blame for what he did or failed to do in Moses’ absence. The subject is a responsibility Moses had previously entrusted to him concerning the people.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:142, Moses had told Aaron to take his place among the people, put matters right, and avoid the way of those who cause corruption.

Thus, lam tarqub qawlī in Ṭā Hā 20:94 concerns taking account of and observing Moses’ prior instruction.

The expression alone does not determine exactly which alternative Aaron should have chosen among every possible course. His conduct requires examination of the whole account.

Findings on the basic form I verb

In Al-Tawbah 9:8 and Al-Tawbah 9:10, the verb concerns bonds and covenants. In Ṭā Hā 20:94, it concerns Moses’ statement.

All three involve taking account of something so that it is respected in a person’s conduct.

This application remains connected with watchfulness but goes beyond looking.

Someone observing a covenant does more than know that it exists: the person takes it into account in practice.

“Respecting” or “observing” is therefore an essential rendering of Qur’anic R Q B. Its forms cannot all be translated solely as “guarding” or “waiting.”

5. Taraqqub and irtiqāb: attentive expectation

Qur’anic forms V and VIII of this family concern waiting and watching for an event that has not yet arrived.

The waiting person’s circumstances and the anticipated event nevertheless differ among the verses.

Moses in the city: waiting amid danger

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:18

فَأَصْبَحَ فِي الْمَدِينَةِ خَائِفًا يَتَرَقَّبُ فَإِذَا الَّذِي اسْتَنْصَرَهُ بِالْأَمْسِ يَسْتَصْرِخُهُ ۚ قَالَ لَهُ مُوسَىٰ إِنَّكَ لَغَوِيٌّ مُبِينٌ

fa-aṣbaḥa fī al-madīnati khāʾifan yataraqqabu fa-idhā alladhī istanṣarahu bi-al-amsi yastaṣrikhuhu qāla lahu Mūsā innaka la-ghawiyyun mubīnun

Translation: “Morning found him in the city, afraid and keeping watch for what might happen. Suddenly, the man who had sought his help the previous day was again crying out to him for help. Moses said to him: ‘You are clearly astray.’”

Here يَتَرَقَّبُ (yataraqqabu) is a form V imperfect verb.

خَائِفًا (khāʾifan) is an accusative circumstantial expression; the clause yataraqqabu continues the description of Moses’ state.

Two characteristics stand together: fear and watchful expectation.

Their conjunction does not make fear part of the inherent meaning of taraqqub.

A person can await good news or calmly observe an event. Here fear comes from khāʾifan, and watchfulness from yataraqqabu.

Al-Ṭabarī likewise explains that Moses feared the discovery of the killing and its consequences, and awaited news concerning himself.

“News,” however, is not expressed within yataraqqabu itself. The context supplies that object.

The brief root meaning is therefore not “he awaited news of the killing,” but “he kept watch and waited”; the account identifies what he was watching for.

Moses leaving the city

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:21

فَخَرَجَ مِنْهَا خَائِفًا يَتَرَقَّبُ ۖ قَالَ رَبِّ نَجِّنِي مِنَ الْقَوْمِ الظَّالِمِينَ

fa-kharaja minhā khāʾifan yataraqqabu qāla rabbi najjinī mina al-qawmi al-ẓālimīna

Translation: “So he left it, afraid and keeping watch. He said: ‘My Lord, save me from the wrongdoing people.’”

The same two words, خَائِفًا يَتَرَقَّبُ (khāʾifan yataraqqabu), recur here.

Moses’ situation has changed, however. In the first verse he was inside the city; here he is leaving it.

In both, he watches for what might happen concerning him.

Al-Ṭabarī explains yataraqqabu here as expecting pursuit and fearing that those he was escaping might catch and seize him.

The repetition shows that watchful expectation can occur while remaining somewhere and while moving.

Taraqqub therefore does not mean bodily stillness or standing in one position.

The verses also combine Moses’ fear and caution with his prayer. The word’s use cannot establish that someone trusting The God should disregard danger.

Moses keeps watch while asking his Lord to save him.

That conclusion comes from the account as a whole; R Q B itself does not mean trust or prayer.

Shuʿayb: waiting alongside his people

Hūd 11:93

وَيَا قَوْمِ اعْمَلُوا عَلَىٰ مَكَانَتِكُمْ إِنِّي عَامِلٌ ۖ سَوْفَ تَعْلَمُونَ مَنْ يَأْتِيهِ عَذَابٌ يُخْزِيهِ وَمَنْ هُوَ كَاذِبٌ ۖ وَارْتَقِبُوا إِنِّي مَعَكُمْ رَقِيبٌ

wa-yā qawmi iʿmalū ʿalā makānatikum innī ʿāmilun sawfa taʿlamūna man yaʾtīhi ʿadhābun yukhzīhi wa-man huwa kādhibun wa-irtaqibū innī maʿakum raqībun

Translation: “And, my people, act according to your position and way; I too am acting. You will soon know who receives a humiliating punishment and who is lying. And wait; I too am waiting with you.”

Two forms of the root occur together here:

وَارْتَقِبُوا (wa-irtaqibū): a plural form VIII imperative.

رَقِيبٌ (raqībun): a description on the pattern faʿīl.

The construction matters. Shuʿayb calls his people to await the outcome, then states that he is also waiting.

Here raqīb describes a human being, Shuʿayb. The word’s form alone therefore cannot establish that it is always a name or attribute of The God.

Nor must raqīb here mean a guardian protecting the people from danger.

The main indications are wa-irtaqibū and the preceding statement about the outcome of conduct becoming clear. Waiting is therefore the stronger sense here.

Al-Ṭabarī likewise explains both forms through awaiting the outcome of the confrontation between Shuʿayb and his people.

This example shows that raqīb can approach “one who waits” in one context, while denoting a guardian or watcher elsewhere.

Fa-irtaqib in Sūrat al-Dukhān

Al-Dukhān 44:10

فَارْتَقِبْ يَوْمَ تَأْتِي السَّمَاءُ بِدُخَانٍ مُبِينٍ

fa-irtaqib yawma taʾtī al-samāʾu bi-dukhānin mubīnin

Translation: “So await a day when the sky brings a manifest smoke.”

Here فَارْتَقِبْ (fa-irtaqib) is a singular imperative, and يَوْمَ (yawma) identifies what is awaited.

The construction makes the verb’s relation to a future event clear.

Yet irtaqib does not mean smoke, punishment, or sky. Other parts of the sentence supply those elements.

Exegetical sources disagree about the smoke’s referent and timing, but those disagreements do not change the verb’s basic sense of waiting.

Fa-irtaqib must therefore not be translated as “bring down punishment” or “produce smoke.” The addressee is to await the event, rather than cause it.

Waiting on opposing sides

Al-Dukhān 44:59

فَارْتَقِبْ إِنَّهُمْ مُرْتَقِبُونَ

fa-irtaqib innahum murtaqibūna

Translation: “So wait; they too are waiting.”

Two forms of the same derived pattern occur here:

فَارْتَقِبْ (fa-irtaqib) is an imperative.

مُرْتَقِبُونَ (murtaqibūna) is a masculine plural active participle of form VIII.

Both lexically concern waiting, but the two sides need not be waiting for the same outcome.

Al-Ṭabarī connects the prophet’s expectation with divine aid and the opponents’ expectation with hoping to defeat him.

That explanation is intelligible within the conflict and opposition.

Nevertheless, murtaqibūn itself does not mean hoping for the prophet’s defeat. Context and exegetical reports identify what they await.

Repeating the root on both sides thus preserves the shared sense of waiting, while its object and direction differ.

The same point appeared in Hūd 11:93.

Fa-irtaqibhum wa-iṣṭabir: watching and patience

Al-Qamar 54:27

إِنَّا مُرْسِلُو النَّاقَةِ فِتْنَةً لَهُمْ فَارْتَقِبْهُمْ وَاصْطَبِرْ

innā mursilū al-nāqati fitnatan lahum fa-irtaqibhum wa-iṣṭabir

Translation: “We are sending the she-camel as a trial for them; so watch them and be patient.”

Here فَارْتَقِبْهُمْ (fa-irtaqibhum) is an imperative with the object pronoun هم (hum), “them.”

Unlike Al-Dukhān 44:10, where a day is awaited, a group of people is the verb’s object here.

The meaning therefore approaches watching them and attending to their conduct.

وَاصْطَبِرْ (wa-iṣṭabir) is another, independent verb expressing patience.

Together, the verbs connect watching the people’s conduct with remaining steadfast through what will happen.

However, irtaqib must not be defined as “be patient,” since the Qur’an adds the separate word iṣṭabir alongside it.

Nor does irtaqibhum establish that the prophet was instructed to physically track every movement in a particular manner. The she-camel’s trial and the people’s conduct determine the kind of watching intended.

Findings on forms V and VIII

In Moses’ account, yataraqqabu approaches attentive watching and waiting amid possible danger.

In some verses irtaqib concerns awaiting an event; in Al-Qamar 54:27 it concerns watching people’s conduct.

Murtaqibūn likewise describes people who wait.

The two derived forms therefore meet in the field of waiting and watching. Their morphological difference alone must not be used to establish a definite intensity or duration of expectation.

The event, the verb’s object, and the context indicate whether waiting is calm, fearful, or expressed as a warning in a confrontation.

6. Raqīb: human, angelic, and divine watchfulness

Raqīb follows the pattern faʿīl. Within this family, it denotes a watcher, guardian, or someone keeping something under observation.

This form occurs five times in the Qur’an.

Three occurrences describe The God: Al-Nisāʾ 4:1, Al-Māʾidah 5:117, and Al-Aḥzāb 33:52.

One describes Shuʿayb: Hūd 11:93.

One describes a watcher beside a person: Qāf 50:18.

This distribution shows that raqīb is not restricted to The God. When attributed to The God, however, the extent of His watchfulness is not equated with limited human vigilance.

The God watches over human conduct

Al-Nisāʾ 4:1 commands reverence, recalls humanity’s creation from one self, attends to family bonds, and warns against disregarding kinship. It then describes The God as raqīb over people.

Divine watchfulness is connected here with people’s conduct toward one another.

Yet raqīb does not lexically mean kinship, family justice, or reverence. Those subjects come from the verse’s other expressions.

The description recalls that human conduct does not escape The God’s watchfulness and knowledge.

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:52, following an instruction concerning the prophet’s family relationships, The God is likewise described as raqīb over everything.

Together, these uses show that divine watchfulness is not confined to one domain, while each verse connects it to its own particular subject.

Jesus: witnessing and watching

Al-Māʾidah 5:117

مَا قُلْتُ لَهُمْ إِلَّا مَا أَمَرْتَنِي بِهِ أَنِ اعْبُدُوا اللَّهَ رَبِّي وَرَبَّكُمْ ۚ وَكُنْتُ عَلَيْهِمْ شَهِيدًا مَا دُمْتُ فِيهِمْ ۖ فَلَمَّا تَوَفَّيْتَنِي كُنْتَ أَنْتَ الرَّقِيبَ عَلَيْهِمْ ۚ وَأَنْتَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ شَهِيدٌ

mā qultu lahum illā mā amartanī bihi ani uʿbudū Allāha rabbī wa-rabbakum wa-kuntu ʿalayhim shahīdan mā dumtu fīhim fa-lammā tawaffaytanī kunta anta al-raqība ʿalayhim wa-anta ʿalā kulli shayʾin shahīdun

Translation: “I told them only what You commanded me: ‘Worship The God, my Lord and your Lord.’ I was a witness over them for as long as I remained among them. Then, when You took me, You Yourself were the watcher over them; and You are a witness over everything.”

This is a particularly important witness for al-raqīb as a description of The God.

Jesus first speaks of his witnessing among the people. He then says that, after The God took him, The God Himself watched over them.

Two distinct lexical families occur here: SH H D and R Q B.

شَهِيدًا (shahīdan) concerns being a witness; الرَّقِيبَ (al-raqība) concerns being a watcher and guardian.

Both can involve awareness of others’ conduct, but they are not complete synonyms.

The construction also makes a distinction clear: مَا دُمْتُ فِيهِمْ (mā dumtu fīhim) limits Jesus’ presence among the people, whereas divine watchfulness is also stated after that presence ends.

This must not be taken to mean that The God only became aware of their conduct after Jesus’ departure.

The verse itself ends by declaring The God a witness over everything.

Divine watchfulness is therefore not confined by a physical location, time, or distance.

Does al-raqīb here mean Jesus’ successor?

No. Lexically, raqīb does not mean a political or religious successor.

Jesus speaks of his witnessing over the people while among them and of divine watchfulness afterward.

This does not mean that The God assumed Jesus’ human responsibility in an identical manner or had not watched over them previously.

The verse distinguishes two relationships: the prophet’s limited presence and witnessing, and comprehensive divine watchfulness.

Nor can R Q B alone settle the nature of تَوَفَّيْتَنِي (tawaffaytanī). The meaning of tawaffī belongs to W F Y and the relevant verses, rather than the definition of raqīb.

Raqībun ʿatīd: watching over human speech

Qāf 50:18

مَا يَلْفِظُ مِنْ قَوْلٍ إِلَّا لَدَيْهِ رَقِيبٌ عَتِيدٌ

mā yalfiẓu min qawlin illā ladayhi raqībun ʿatīdun

Translation: “He utters no word without a ready watcher beside him.”

Here رَقِيبٌ (raqībun) describes a watcher beside the person, while عَتِيدٌ (ʿatīdun) expresses readiness and presence.

Raqīb belongs to R Q B; ʿatīd belongs to ʿ T D.

Readiness and presence should therefore not be made an inherent part of raqīb itself. The Qur’an adds this feature through a separate word.

Qāf 50:17 speaks of two receivers seated on the right and left. Verse 18 continues that setting by describing watch over a person’s speech.

Early commentaries explain raqībun ʿatīd through the angel or angels recording human words and deeds.

R Q B itself, however, does not mean writing. Recording deeds or speech is a role understood from the wider set of verses about watchers and those who write deeds down.

This matters in studying words individually: raqīb is a watcher, rather than a record book or the act of writing.

Does watching mean compelling a person?

The description of a watcher or guardian does not establish that every decision of the person watched is forcibly caused by the watcher.

In ordinary life, too, a guard can observe people’s conduct without causing all their choices.

Likewise, raqīb alone must not be used to derive a complete Qur’anic theory of compulsion and free choice.

Verses about recording conduct, human responsibility, and divine judgment need to be studied together.

In Qāf 50:18, the clear point is that a person’s speech does not escape watchfulness. The speaker’s agency and responsibility are established through other, distinct discussions.

Findings on raqīb

In Shuʿayb’s speech in Hūd 11:93, raqīb approaches awaiting an outcome.

In Qāf 50:18, it refers to a watcher beside a person.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:1, Al-Māʾidah 5:117, and Al-Aḥzāb 33:52, it describes The God.

One mechanical equivalent should therefore not govern every occurrence.

“Watcher” and “guardian” are principal equivalents, but “one who waits” better suits Hūd 11:93.

The lexical idea of watching must also be distinguished from its extent. A human’s or angel’s limited capacity and awareness should not be equated without grounds with The God’s comprehensive knowledge.

7. Raqabah and riqāb: the neck, people in bondage, and release

This branch has particular social and juridical importance in the Qur’an.

A word originally naming the neck is used in certain verses for a person in bondage, whose emancipation is presented as a good deed or a compensatory responsibility.

In one verse about battle, however, the same plural riqāb refers to physical necks.

The distinction must be carefully maintained.

Fakku raqabah: releasing a person from bondage

Al-Balad 90:13

فَكُّ رَقَبَةٍ

fakku raqabatin

Translation: “Freeing a person from bondage.”

This brief verse forms part of the Qur’an’s answer about crossing العقبة (al-ʿaqabah), the difficult ascent.

Al-Balad 90:11, Al-Balad 90:12, Al-Balad 90:13, Al-Balad 90:14, Al-Balad 90:15, and Al-Balad 90:16 concern undertaking the difficult ascent, freeing a person, and feeding others on a day of hunger.

Here فَكّ (fakk) belongs to F K K and concerns opening or loosening a bond.

Raqabah identifies the person in bondage.

The combination therefore concerns a person’s release, rather than merely undoing a knot around a physical neck.

Al-Ṭabarī explains the expression as release from slavery and reports the differing recitations of the nominal فَكُّ رَقَبَةٍ (fakku raqabatin) and its verbal counterpart.

Lexically, raqabah clearly refers here to the whole person.

Yet fakk and raqabah do not mean the same thing. Release from bondage comes from combining the two words.

Does raqabah here refer only to a slave belonging to an owner?

The historical use of raqabah in emancipation expressions was directly connected with slavery.

Nevertheless, the expression fakku raqabah, as a linguistic image, concerns releasing a person from bondage.

Lexical reports also use fakka raqabah for releasing a captive. A single lexical form therefore cannot establish that every instance of emancipation at every time must belong to one particular arrangement of slave ownership.

Conversely, this possible breadth must not become a reason to disregard the clear historical referent of slavery.

Determining which forms of captivity, domination, or deprivation of freedom fall within a specific provision requires examination of its context, people’s rights, and other verses.

In Al-Balad 90:13, releasing a person from bondage is the clear, direct meaning.

Wa-fī al-riqāb: giving wealth toward release

Al-Baqarah 2:177

لَيْسَ الْبِرَّ أَنْ تُوَلُّوا وُجُوهَكُمْ قِبَلَ الْمَشْرِقِ وَالْمَغْرِبِ وَلَٰكِنَّ الْبِرَّ مَنْ آمَنَ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ وَالْمَلَائِكَةِ وَالْكِتَابِ وَالنَّبِيِّينَ وَآتَى الْمَالَ عَلَىٰ حُبِّهِ ذَوِي الْقُرْبَىٰ وَالْيَتَامَىٰ وَالْمَسَاكِينَ وَابْنَ السَّبِيلِ وَالسَّائِلِينَ وَفِي الرِّقَابِ وَأَقَامَ الصَّلَاةَ وَآتَى الزَّكَاةَ وَالْمُوفُونَ بِعَهْدِهِمْ إِذَا عَاهَدُوا ۖ وَالصَّابِرِينَ فِي الْبَأْسَاءِ وَالضَّرَّاءِ وَحِينَ الْبَأْسِ ۗ أُولَٰئِكَ الَّذِينَ صَدَقُوا ۖ وَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْمُتَّقُونَ

laysa al-birra an tuwallū wujūhakum qibala al-mashriqi wa-al-maghribi wa-lākinna al-birra man āmana bi-Allāhi wa-al-yawmi al-ākhiri wa-al-malāʾikati wa-al-kitābi wa-al-nabiyyīna wa-ātā al-māla ʿalā ḥubbihi dhawī al-qurbā wa-al-yatāmā wa-al-masākīna wa-ibna al-sabīli wa-al-sāʾilīna wa-fī al-riqābi wa-aqāma al-ṣalāta wa-ātā al-zakāta wa-al-mūfūna bi-ʿahdihim idhā ʿāhadū wa-al-ṣābirīna fī al-baʾsāʾi wa-al-ḍarrāʾi wa-ḥīna al-baʾsi ulāʾika alladhīna ṣadaqū wa-ulāʾika humu al-muttaqūna

Translation: “Goodness is not that you turn your faces toward east and west. Rather, goodness belongs to one who believes in The God, the Last Day, the angels, the Book, and the prophets; who gives wealth, despite loving it, to relatives, orphans, those in need, stranded travelers, and those who ask, and toward freeing people in bondage; who establishes ṣalāt and gives zakāt; and those who fulfill their covenants when they undertake them, and remain steadfast in hardship, distress, and battle. Those are the truthful, and those are the reverent.”

Here وَفِي الرِّقَابِ (wa-fī al-riqāb) appears among the uses of wealth.

The preposition matters. With groups such as relatives, orphans, and people in need, the construction expresses giving wealth to them; with riqāb, it uses فِي (fī), “in.”

This construction concerns spending wealth within a particular sphere: toward emancipating and releasing people in bondage.

Lexically, al-riqāb is the plural of raqabah, but spending wealth does not mean paying money to a physical neck.

“Toward freeing people in bondage” conveys the expression’s familiar force in this setting.

The Qur’an also places this act among the qualities of بِرّ (birr), goodness.

Releasing people is therefore a social good here, rather than merely a way to expiate an error or wrongdoing.

However, riqāb alone does not establish that every form of release can occur only through payment. Giving wealth is this sentence’s subject; other ways of freeing people require separate examination.

Wa-fī al-riqāb among the uses of charitable contributions

Al-Tawbah 9:60

إِنَّمَا الصَّدَقَاتُ لِلْفُقَرَاءِ وَالْمَسَاكِينِ وَالْعَامِلِينَ عَلَيْهَا وَالْمُؤَلَّفَةِ قُلُوبُهُمْ وَفِي الرِّقَابِ وَالْغَارِمِينَ وَفِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ وَابْنِ السَّبِيلِ ۖ فَرِيضَةً مِنَ اللَّهِ ۗ وَاللَّهُ عَلِيمٌ حَكِيمٌ

innamā al-ṣadaqātu li-al-fuqarāʾi wa-al-masākīni wa-al-ʿāmilīna ʿalayhā wa-al-muʾallafati qulūbuhum wa-fī al-riqābi wa-al-ghārimīna wa-fī sabīli Allāhi wa-ibni al-sabīli farīḍatan mina Allāhi wa-Allāhu ʿalīmun ḥakīmun

Translation: “Charitable contributions are only for the poor, those in need, those administering them, and those whose hearts are brought together; toward freeing people in bondage; for debtors; in the way of The God; and for stranded travelers—an obligation from The God. The God is knowing and wise.”

Here, too, fī al-riqāb occurs among designated financial uses.

Early commentaries identify assistance to enslaved people who had contracted with their owners to obtain freedom as one familiar application. Historically, such people were called mukātabūn.

Some exegetical positions also discuss purchasing and freeing enslaved people or helping release captives under this category.

These juridical differences must not become the root’s brief lexical definition.

Riqāb here concerns people in bondage. Exactly how financial resources are used for their release is a question of implementing the provision.

Nor should its use in an older system of slavery disconnect fī al-riqāb from the basic subject of releasing people.

Allocating part of charitable resources to freeing people is clear in this verse.

Nevertheless, identifying present-day forms of bondage and captivity requires independent research. The category should neither be extended without grounds nor confined to a historical instance without examination.

Is freeing people always expiation in the Qur’an?

No. Comparing Al-Balad 90:13 and Al-Baqarah 2:177 with expiation verses shows emancipation in two different roles.

Sometimes it is a social good, presented alongside feeding others and helping those in need.

Sometimes it is a compensatory responsibility following an error or breach of an undertaking.

The full meaning of taḥrīru raqabah or fakku raqabah must therefore not be confined to expiation.

Conversely, emancipation’s goodness does not make the details of every expiation provision identical across verses.

Taḥrīru raqabah: emancipation following an accidental killing

Al-Nisāʾ 4:92

وَمَا كَانَ لِمُؤْمِنٍ أَنْ يَقْتُلَ مُؤْمِنًا إِلَّا خَطَأً ۚ وَمَنْ قَتَلَ مُؤْمِنًا خَطَأً فَتَحْرِيرُ رَقَبَةٍ مُؤْمِنَةٍ وَدِيَةٌ مُسَلَّمَةٌ إِلَىٰ أَهْلِهِ إِلَّا أَنْ يَصَّدَّقُوا ۚ فَإِنْ كَانَ مِنْ قَوْمٍ عَدُوٍّ لَكُمْ وَهُوَ مُؤْمِنٌ فَتَحْرِيرُ رَقَبَةٍ مُؤْمِنَةٍ ۖ وَإِنْ كَانَ مِنْ قَوْمٍ بَيْنَكُمْ وَبَيْنَهُمْ مِيثَاقٌ فَدِيَةٌ مُسَلَّمَةٌ إِلَىٰ أَهْلِهِ وَتَحْرِيرُ رَقَبَةٍ مُؤْمِنَةٍ ۖ فَمَنْ لَمْ يَجِدْ فَصِيَامُ شَهْرَيْنِ مُتَتَابِعَيْنِ تَوْبَةً مِنَ اللَّهِ ۗ وَكَانَ اللَّهُ عَلِيمًا حَكِيمًا

wa-mā kāna li-muʾminin an yaqtula muʾminan illā khaṭaʾan wa-man qatala muʾminan khaṭaʾan fa-taḥrīru raqabatin muʾminatin wa-diyatun musallamatun ilā ahlihi illā an yaṣṣaddaqū fa-in kāna min qawmin ʿaduwwin lakum wa-huwa muʾminun fa-taḥrīru raqabatin muʾminatin wa-in kāna min qawmin baynakum wa-baynahum mīthāqun fa-diyatun musallamatun ilā ahlihi wa-taḥrīru raqabatin muʾminatin fa-man lam yajid fa-ṣiyāmu shahrayni mutatābiʿayni tawbatan mina Allāhi wa-kāna Allāhu ʿalīman ḥakīman

Translation: “A believer may not kill another believer except by mistake. Whoever kills a believer by mistake must free a believing person from bondage and deliver compensation to the victim’s family, unless they waive it as charity. If the victim belonged to a people hostile to you but was a believer, a believing person must be freed. If the victim belonged to a people with whom you have a covenant, compensation must be delivered to the family and a believing person freed. Whoever cannot find the means must fast for two consecutive months, as repentance from The God. The God is knowing and wise.”

رَقَبَةٍ مُؤْمِنَةٍ (raqabatin muʾminatin) occurs three times here.

The repetition is not accidental. The verse speaks of freeing a believing person in three different situations concerning accidental killing.

Here تَحْرِير (taḥrīr) belongs to Ḥ R R and means emancipation; raqabah identifies the person to be freed; مُؤْمِنَة (muʾminah) describes that person.

Faith is therefore not inherent in raqabah: a separate adjective expresses it.

This distinction matters when comparing other expiation verses.

Freeing a person and paying compensation are also separate acts. One cannot be treated as the meaning of raqabah while the other is disregarded.

The verse gives particular provisions about compensation and emancipation for different situations, and introduces an alternative for someone unable to carry out emancipation.

R Q B itself contains none of these juridical details. Its role is to name the person whose freedom is the provision’s subject.

Expiation of an oath: no explicit faith qualification for raqabah

Al-Māʾidah 5:89

لَا يُؤَاخِذُكُمُ اللَّهُ بِاللَّغْوِ فِي أَيْمَانِكُمْ وَلَٰكِنْ يُؤَاخِذُكُمْ بِمَا عَقَّدْتُمُ الْأَيْمَانَ ۖ فَكَفَّارَتُهُ إِطْعَامُ عَشَرَةِ مَسَاكِينَ مِنْ أَوْسَطِ مَا تُطْعِمُونَ أَهْلِيكُمْ أَوْ كِسْوَتُهُمْ أَوْ تَحْرِيرُ رَقَبَةٍ ۖ فَمَنْ لَمْ يَجِدْ فَصِيَامُ ثَلَاثَةِ أَيَّامٍ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ كَفَّارَةُ أَيْمَانِكُمْ إِذَا حَلَفْتُمْ ۚ وَاحْفَظُوا أَيْمَانَكُمْ ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ يُبَيِّنُ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ آيَاتِهِ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَشْكُرُونَ

lā yuʾākhidhukumu Allāhu bi-al-laghwi fī aymānikum wa-lākin yuʾākhidhukum bi-mā ʿaqqadtumu al-aymāna fa-kaffāratuhu iṭʿāmu ʿasharati masākīna min awsaṭi mā tuṭʿimūna ahlīkum aw kiswatuhum aw taḥrīru raqabatin fa-man lam yajid fa-ṣiyāmu thalāthati ayyāmin dhālika kaffāratu aymānikum idhā ḥalaftum wa-iḥfaẓū aymānakum kadhālika yubayyinu Allāhu lakum āyātihi laʿallakum tashkurūna

Translation: “The God does not hold you accountable for unconsidered words in your oaths, but He holds you accountable for the oaths you bind deliberately. Their expiation is feeding ten people in need from the average of what you feed your families, or clothing them, or freeing a person from bondage. Whoever cannot find the means must fast three days. That is the expiation of your oaths when you swear; and safeguard your oaths. Thus The God makes His signs clear to you, so that you may give thanks.”

Here taḥrīru raqabah is one form of expiation for an oath.

Unlike Al-Nisāʾ 4:92, مؤمنة (muʾminah) does not follow raqabah here.

This verbal difference must be preserved in translation.

The qualification should not be inserted without explanation into Al-Māʾidah 5:89 merely because another verse about accidental killing specifies raqabah muʾminah.

Traditional jurisprudence discusses applying emancipation qualifications across different expiations. Such application requires its own argument and cannot replace each verse’s direct wording.

Al-Māʾidah 5:89 itself presents freeing a person alongside feeding and clothing people in need.

Again, raqabah identifies a person in bondage, and taḥrīr expresses emancipation.

Emancipation before resuming marital intimacy

Al-Mujādilah 58:3

وَالَّذِينَ يُظَاهِرُونَ مِنْ نِسَائِهِمْ ثُمَّ يَعُودُونَ لِمَا قَالُوا فَتَحْرِيرُ رَقَبَةٍ مِنْ قَبْلِ أَنْ يَتَمَاسَّا ۚ ذَٰلِكُمْ تُوعَظُونَ بِهِ ۚ وَاللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ خَبِيرٌ

wa-alladhīna yuẓāhirūna min nisāʾihim thumma yaʿūdūna li-mā qālū fa-taḥrīru raqabatin min qabli an yatamāssā dhālikum tūʿaẓūna bihi wa-Allāhu bi-mā taʿmalūna khabīrun

Translation: “Those who pronounce ẓihār concerning their wives, then go back on what they said, must free a person from bondage before the couple touch one another. This is the admonition given to you, and The God is fully aware of what you do.”

Taḥrīru raqabah occurs here as well.

The provision concerns a particular act within marital relationships, expressed by يُظَاهِرُونَ (yuẓāhirūna).

In the lexical study, raqabah again refers to a person in bondage, rather than a body part of either spouse.

مِنْ قَبْلِ أَنْ يَتَمَاسَّا (min qabli an yatamāssā) specifies the timing of emancipation. That qualification is not contained within R Q B itself.

Here, too, unlike Al-Nisāʾ 4:92, muʾminah does not explicitly follow raqabah.

The three verses therefore concern freeing people in several compensatory settings, but their qualifications are not all identical.

These verbal and juridical differences must remain visible in translation and research.

Ḍarba al-riqāb: physical necks in battle

Muḥammad 47:4

فَإِذَا لَقِيتُمُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا فَضَرْبَ الرِّقَابِ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا أَثْخَنْتُمُوهُمْ فَشُدُّوا الْوَثَاقَ فَإِمَّا مَنًّا بَعْدُ وَإِمَّا فِدَاءً حَتَّىٰ تَضَعَ الْحَرْبُ أَوْزَارَهَا ۚ ذَٰلِكَ وَلَوْ يَشَاءُ اللَّهُ لَانْتَصَرَ مِنْهُمْ وَلَٰكِنْ لِيَبْلُوَ بَعْضَكُمْ بِبَعْضٍ ۗ وَالَّذِينَ قُتِلُوا فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ فَلَنْ يُضِلَّ أَعْمَالَهُمْ

fa-idhā laqītumu alladhīna kafarū fa-ḍarba al-riqābi ḥattā idhā athkhantumūhum fa-shuddū al-wathāqa fa-immā mannan baʿdu wa-immā fidāʾan ḥattā taḍaʿa al-ḥarbu awzārahā dhālika wa-law yashāʾu Allāhu la-intaṣara minhum wa-lākin li-yabluwa baʿḍakum bi-baʿḍin wa-alladhīna qutilū fī sabīli Allāhi fa-lan yuḍilla aʿmālahum

Translation: “When you meet those who disbelieve in battle, strike their necks until you have thoroughly subdued them, then bind the captives firmly. Thereafter, either release them graciously or for ransom, until the war lays down its burdens. Thus it is. Had The God willed, He could have prevailed over them Himself, but He tests some of you through others. Those killed in the way of The God—He will not let their deeds be lost.”

This verse is particularly important to R Q B because al-riqāb differs here from its uses in emancipation.

Ḍarba al-riqāb concerns combat, with necks retaining their physical meaning.

Arabic “striking necks” can express killing an enemy on the battlefield. The expression combines ضرب (ḍarb) and riqāb; raqabah itself does not mean killing.

Nor does ḍarb mean emancipation here. Release appears in the following passage, concerning captives and setting them free.

Why does riqāb here differ from Al-Tawbah 9:60?

In Al-Tawbah 9:60, fī al-riqāb designates spending toward people’s release.

In Muḥammad 47:4, ḍarba al-riqāb occurs in battle.

The noun alone does not produce this difference; the accompanying expression and sentence structure make it clear.

In one verse, fī and the charitable-contributions context identify emancipation; in the other, ḍarb and the war context foreground physical necks.

A fixed rendering such as “slaves” or “necks” for every Qur’anic riqāb therefore cannot accurately convey every verse.

Nor should Muḥammad 47:4 be turned into a general command to harm any non-Muslim in any situation.

The verse itself concerns war and, after combat, describes taking captives and two ways of releasing them: graciously or for ransom.

This sequence shows a wartime situation with distinct stages.

Determining every condition governing legitimate war, covenants, defense, and treatment of noncombatants requires examination of the wider set of verses on war and peace.

For lexical study, the established point is the distinction between physical necks and their figurative use for people in bondage.

Does raqabah lexically mean a man or a woman?

No. Raqabah is grammatically feminine, but that alone does not establish that its human referent must be female.

In emancipation expressions, it can identify a person in bondage; the noun’s feminine form does not by itself restrict the person’s sex.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:92, muʾminah grammatically agrees with raqabah. The adjective’s feminine form alone therefore cannot show that emancipation in that verse concerns only women.

This distinction between grammatical gender and a person’s sex matters in translation.

8. Distinctions from related roots

R Q B (murāqabah) and N Ẓ R (naẓar)

Naẓar concerns looking and, in extended applications, thinking and paying attention.

R Q B can likewise concern looking, but many of its applications foreground continued observation or awaiting a particular event.

Someone may glance momentarily at the sky; a person watching a star to determine when to depart exemplifies the familiar R Q B usage.

Nevertheless, naẓar is not invariably momentary, nor R Q B invariably prolonged. Context determines the duration and kind of attention.

Study of N Ẓ R

R Q B (murāqabah) and R Ṣ D (raṣd)

R Ṣ D is among the families closest to R Q B.

Both can concern watching, monitoring a route, and awaiting an event.

Lexical explanations often explain taraqqub through raṣd.

However, they are not interchangeable in every application. In Al-Tawbah 9:8 and Al-Tawbah 9:10, yarqubu concerns respecting bonds and covenants, which cannot be turned into surveillance or ambush without supporting evidence.

Some contexts bring raṣd close to lying in wait or watching in order to obtain something. R Q B itself does not necessarily express hostility or threat.

Study of R Ṣ D

R Q B (raqīb) and Ḥ F Ẓ (ḥafīẓ)

Ḥ F Ẓ concerns keeping and protecting.

Lexicons explain raqīb through ḥafīẓ. In verses concerning divine watchfulness, their semantic fields approach each other.

Nevertheless, watching and preserving have a meaningful distinction.

Someone can observe an event without being able to prevent it; preserving can also emphasize keeping something from loss or destruction.

Raqīb alone therefore does not establish that the watcher prevents every event.

Qāf 50:18 concerns watching over human speech, rather than preventing every wrongful utterance.

Study of Ḥ F Ẓ

R Q B (irtiqāb) and N Ẓ R (intiẓār)

Arabic intiẓār concerns awaiting something. It is the form VIII verbal noun of N Ẓ R: tāʾ is added by the derived pattern and is not a root consonant.

Forms of N Ẓ R

In Hūd 11:93 and Al-Dukhān 44:59, irtaqib is very close to انتظر (intaẓir), “wait.”

In Al-Qamar 54:27, however, a group of people is the verb’s object, and watching their conduct is also involved.

Waiting is therefore a basic application of irtiqāb, but does not always exhaust its watchful aspect.

Nor does waiting inherently mean either hope or fear. What is awaited determines those states.

Study of N Ẓ R

R Q B (raqabah) and ʿ N Q (ʿunuq)

In physical applications, ʿunuq and raqabah are very close and can both denote the neck.

However, in expressions such as taḥrīru raqabah, raqabah also stands for a whole person in bondage.

This does not mean that ʿunuq never has figurative uses. Rather, in the verses studied, raqabah is the familiar form in emancipation expressions.

Thus, riqāb in Muḥammad 47:4 can be translated as “necks,” while a literal “freeing a neck” in Al-Balad 90:13 does not clearly communicate the social meaning.

Study of ʿ N Q

R Q B (raqabah) and Ḥ R R (taḥrīr)

These two families occur together in expiation verses.

Raqabah identifies the person in bondage; taḥrīr identifies emancipation.

R Q B itself therefore does not mean emancipation.

Nor should taḥrīr be taken to mean watching or waiting. It belongs to another root and expresses the action intended in the construction.

Study of Ḥ R R

R Q B (raqabah) and F K K (fakk)

In Al-Balad 90:13, fakk concerns loosening a bond.

Raqabah identifies the subject of release.

In outcome, fakku raqabah and taḥrīru raqabah both approach a person’s emancipation, but their lexical images differ.

Fakk emphasizes undoing a bond and release from confinement; taḥrīr emphasizes making free.

The expressions must not therefore be treated as derivatives of one root, nor should either action be presented as the definition of raqabah itself.

Study of F K K

R Q B (raqabah) and R B Q (ribqah)

These roots contain similar consonants in a different order.

In its physical application, R Q B concerns the neck.

R B Q concerns a loop, bond, or rope encircling something, including an animal’s neck.

Ibn Fāris explains R B Q through a neck restraint and something placed around a body.

The thematic connection is intelligible, since a bond can encircle a neck. It does not establish that the two roots historically constitute a single root.

Fakku raqabah should therefore not be analyzed morphologically as a derivative of رِبْقَة (ribqah), even though freeing someone can evoke undoing a bond.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

No single fixed equivalent serves the R Q B family. Watching and keeping under observation form a central meaning, but Qur’anic applications extend across different settings.

In Al-Tawbah 9:8 and Al-Tawbah 9:10, yarqubū and yarqubūna are best rendered as “respect” or “observe.” The point is taking bonds and covenants into account and honoring them in conduct, rather than looking at them.

In Ṭā Hā 20:94, wa-lam tarqub qawlī can be rendered “and you did not observe my instruction.” “You did not await my statement” deserves consideration in light of some lexical explanations, but Aaron’s responsibility and Moses’ previous words favor observing and taking account of the instruction.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:18 and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:21, yataraqqabu suits “he kept watch” or “he was watching for what might happen.” Fear comes from khāʾifan and must not become an invariant part of the brief root meaning.

In Hūd 11:93, Al-Dukhān 44:10, and Al-Dukhān 44:59, irtaqib and related forms can be translated as “wait” or “await.” In Al-Qamar 54:27, the object hum, “them,” makes “watch them” more appropriate.

For raqīb, “watcher” and “guardian” are principal equivalents. In Hūd 11:93, however, “one who waits” better suits the context. In Qāf 50:18, “a ready watcher” preserves the two distinct descriptions in raqībun ʿatīd. In Al-Māʾidah 5:117, “You Yourself were the watcher over them” naturally conveys the divine relationship.

For raqabah and riqāb, two applications require distinction.

In Muḥammad 47:4, ḍarba al-riqāb concerns “striking necks” in battle. The physical meaning must remain visible.

In Al-Balad 90:13, fakku raqabah means “freeing a person from bondage.”

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:92, Al-Māʾidah 5:89, and Al-Mujādilah 58:3, taḥrīru raqabah concerns “freeing a person from bondage.” In Al-Nisāʾ 4:92, the qualification muʾminah, “believing,” must also remain, since it explicitly accompanies all three occurrences.

In Al-Baqarah 2:177 and Al-Tawbah 9:60, fī al-riqāb can be translated as “toward freeing people in bondage.” This clarifies the noun’s figurative application in the context of spending wealth.

10. Conclusion

In older Arabic, R Q B connects with watching, guarding, waiting, and observing obligations. Ibn Fāris connects these applications with raising the neck to observe. His explanation helps relate the meanings but must not become an obligatory image for every derivative.

The root occurs 24 times in 20 Qur’anic verses.

In Al-Tawbah 9:8 and Al-Tawbah 9:10, yarqubu concerns respecting bonds and covenants. In Ṭā Hā 20:94, the same verb concerns observing Moses’ instruction.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:18 and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:21, yataraqqabu expresses Moses’ state of concern and vigilance for possible danger.

In Hūd, Al-Dukhān, and Al-Qamar, irtaqib and murtaqibūn concern awaiting an event or watching people’s conduct. Waiting can accompany hope, fear, or warning, but the root itself does not invariably determine any one of these states.

Qur’anic raqīb describes a human being, a watcher beside a person, and The God. The word itself is therefore not exclusive to The God, while divine watchfulness is not equated with human limitations.

The raqabah branch includes the physical neck and its figurative use for a whole person, especially someone in bondage.

Muḥammad 47:4 retains the physical meaning. In emancipation verses, raqabah identifies someone to be freed; Al-Baqarah 2:177 and Al-Tawbah 9:60 concern spending wealth toward people’s release.

These uses show emancipation as both a social good and, in certain provisions, a compensatory responsibility. Each provision’s details nevertheless come from the accompanying words and its context, rather than R Q B itself.

A suitable final definition for Forqan:

R Q B concerns watching, keeping under observation, waiting, and respecting something. Within the family, raqīb denotes a watcher or guardian; taraqqub and irtiqāb concern attentive waiting and watching; raqabah names the neck. Figuratively, raqabah can also name a whole person, especially someone in bondage. The Qur’an uses this family for respecting covenants, vigilance amid danger, awaiting events, divine watchfulness, and releasing people from bondage. These applications are semantically connected, but the construction and context identify the kind of watching, what is awaited, and the person’s circumstances. R Q B therefore does not always mean waiting or always mean guarding, nor does raqabah mean an enslaved person in every verse. Each form must be understood in its own setting.
