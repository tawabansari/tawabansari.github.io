Qur’anic Root F K K (ف ك ك)

Opening, separating, and releasing: from undoing bonds and freeing people to fakku raqabatin and the meaning of munfakkīn in the Qur’an

Qur’anic forms

فَكُّ رَقَبَةٍ
(fakku raqabatin)
Verbal noun; freeing a person from bondage; Al-Balad 90:13 in Ḥafṣ

فَكَّ رَقَبَةً
(fakka raqabatan)
Past-tense verb in the readings of Ibn Kathīr, Abū ʿAmr, and Al-Kisāʾī; Al-Balad 90:13

مُنْفَكِّينَ
(munfakkīna)
Plural active participle of Form VII in a negative construction; Al-Bayyinah 98:1

The Arabic triliteral root ف ك ك (fāʾ–kāf–kāf) concerns opening something that is closed or attached to something else, and separating it from its previous condition. When a bond is undone, two things bound together separate. When pledged property is redeemed, the relationship preventing its recovery ends. When a person is freed from captivity or slavery, a constraint limiting that person’s freedom is removed.

These uses share a feature: something is separated from a connection, constraint, or condition that held it. The connection is not always of the same kind. It may be a physical bond, a legal relationship such as property being held in pledge, or a condition in which a person or group remains.

The Qur’an uses this root only twice. In Al-Balad, فَكُّ رَقَبَةٍ (fakku raqabatin) concerns freeing a person from bondage. In Al-Bayyinah, مُنْفَكِّينَ (munfakkīn) describes people who had not separated or desisted from a condition before the clear proof arrived. The first concerns release; the second, a separation or an ending of a condition that had not occurred.

This distinction matters for understanding the root. Fakk should not be confined to freeing slaves, just as اِنْفِكَاك (infikāk) does not mean release from captivity in every sentence.

In the transmission of Ḥafṣ, the Qur’anic فَكُّ (fakku) is the verbal noun of the basic verb فَكَّ (fakka). مُنْفَكِّينَ (munfakkīn) is the masculine plural active participle of Form VII, اِنْفَكَّ (infakka). Other Arabic forms include فَكَّ (fakka), يَفُكُّ (yafukku), فِكَاك (fikāk), فَكَاك (fakāk), اِفْتَكَّ (iftakka), تَفَكَّكَ (tafakkaka), and مَفْكُوك (mafkūk), but these particular forms do not occur in the transmission of Ḥafṣ. Fakka itself is a past-tense verb in another canonical reading of Al-Balad 90:13; this distinction is discussed in the section on readings.

1. The root in early Arabic: undoing a connection and separating

Fakka al-shayʾa: opening and separating

In Arabic, فَكَّ الشَّيْءَ يَفُكُّهُ فَكًّا (fakka al-shayʾa yafukkuhu fakkan) means opening something or separating it from its connection with something else.

One familiar early example is breaking open the seal of something closed. To open a sealed book or letter, its seal or fastening must be undone. فَكُّ الْخَاتَمِ (fakku al-khātami) denotes this action.

Another example is separating two interconnected things. When two pieces or parts are attached so that their separation requires undoing a connection, fakka can describe the act.

The account of Al-Layth preserved in later lexical sources describes separating interconnected things. Ibn Fāris likewise identifies تَفَتُّح (tafattuḥ), opening, and اِنْفِرَاج (infirāj), parting or widening, as central to the root: something closed or joined opens and separates.

This definition explains both physical uses and some legal uses of the root.

Opening, however, does not always mean breaking or destroying. A lock can be opened without being broken. Two things can also be detached while both remain intact.

Fakk therefore concerns the ending of an attachment or constraint that held something in its previous condition, rather than necessarily emphasizing its destruction.

Fakku al-rahni: redeeming pledged property

In Arabic legal and economic usage, فَكُّ الرَّهْنِ (fakku al-rahni) means releasing property from pledge.

For example, someone pledges property as security for a debt. While the relevant obligation remains in force, the property is held in pledge. Once the conditions for release are met and the property is redeemed, one can say فُكَّ الرَّهْنُ (fukka al-rahnu), “the pledge was released.”

No physical bond need be tied around the property. What is undone is a legal relationship.

Arabic also records فِكَاك (fikāk) and فَكَاك (fakāk) for that by which pledged property or a captive person is released, such as a payment made to obtain release.

These words do not invariably denote a particular sum of money. Release may be obtained by payment or by another means.

This branch shows fakk extending beyond undoing a physical attachment to ending a legal constraint.

Fakku al-asīri: freeing a captive

Arabic فَكَّ الْأَسِيرَ (fakka al-asīra) means freeing a captive.

Captivity may involve bonds, chains, or forcible detention. Freedom from captivity is not limited to removing a particular chain, however; ending the condition of captivity is the central matter.

Another established expression, فَكُّ الرَّقَبَةِ (fakku al-raqabati), concerns freeing a person from slavery.

Here too, it is the person who is released; the person’s neck is not physically severed from the body.

The connection with the basic meaning is clear: someone’s freedom is constrained, and fakku raqabah releases that person from the condition.

Arabic رَقَبَة (raqabah), “neck,” can stand for the whole person. Releasing a raqabah therefore means freeing a human being.

This legal use is central to understanding Al-Balad.

Infakka: separating and the ending of a connection

اِنْفَكَّ الشَّيْءُ (infakka al-shayʾu) means that something became detached from its connection or emerged from a condition in which it was bound or held.

The distinction from fakka concerns the verbal construction. Fakka can emphasize the act of separating something; infakka more directly describes that thing becoming separated.

If someone undoes a bond, for example, fakka describes that person’s act. Once the bond opens and the two sides separate, infakka can describe the result.

In broader uses, infikāk can concern two people separating, a relationship ending, or someone desisting from a condition.

It is not limited to “becoming free.” Someone may leave a relationship without having been a captive or slave.

This is essential for مُنْفَكِّينَ (munfakkīn) in Al-Bayyinah, where the subject is not physical captivity.

An example from early Arabic poetry

A verse attributed to Abū Qays ibn al-Aslat reads:

الْحَزْمُ وَالْقُوَّةُ خَيْرٌ مِنَ الْإِدْهَانِ وَالْفَكَّةِ وَالْهَاعِ

“Resolve and strength are better than wrongful accommodation, weakness, and intense greed.”

Here, الْفَكَّة (al-fakkah) concerns weakness and slackness.

This use can be understood through looseness and the loss of cohesion: something lacking the necessary firmness and connectedness becomes weak and unstable.

This explanation does not make weakness or incapacity the meaning of fakk in every use. Freeing a captive may require considerable strength and effort.

Collections of early poetry attribute the verse to Abū Qays ibn al-Aslat, a poet close to the emergence of Islam, but its precise date of composition is unknown. Early explanations of this very example understand الْهَاع (al-hāʿ) both as intense greed and as fright or fearfulness; the translation above is therefore not its only transmitted explanation. There is also a variant الْفَهَّة (al-fahhah) in place of الْفَكَّة (al-fakkah), explained as inability to speak fluently. When using this verse as evidence for F K K, the reading al-fakkah must therefore be kept in view.

This example helps illuminate the family’s branch involving weakness and disintegration. It cannot, by itself, prove that this was the root’s earliest historical meaning.

In established Arabic usage, the clearer central theme remains opening, separating, and releasing from a connection.

2. Fakku raqabatin: freeing a person from bondage

Al-Balad states:

فَلَا اقْتَحَمَ الْعَقَبَةَ ۝ وَمَا أَدْرَاكَ مَا الْعَقَبَةُ ۝ فَكُّ رَقَبَةٍ ۝ أَوْ إِطْعَامٌ فِي يَوْمٍ ذِي مَسْغَبَةٍ ۝ يَتِيمًا ذَا مَقْرَبَةٍ ۝ أَوْ مِسْكِينًا ذَا مَتْرَبَةٍ ۝ ثُمَّ كَانَ مِنَ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَتَوَاصَوْا بِالصَّبْرِ وَتَوَاصَوْا بِالْمَرْحَمَةِ

“Yet he did not cross the difficult pass. And what has made you know what the difficult pass is? Freeing a person from bondage, or feeding on a day of hunger and scarcity: an orphan who is a relative or someone destitute and reduced to the dust. Then being among those who believe and urge one another to patience and urge one another to mercy.”

(Al-Balad 90:11; Al-Balad 90:12; Al-Balad 90:13; Al-Balad 90:14; Al-Balad 90:15; Al-Balad 90:16; Al-Balad 90:17)

These verses speak of الْعَقَبَة (al-ʿaqabah), a mountain pass that is not easy to cross.

They then identify actions associated with crossing it: first fakku raqabatin, followed by feeding someone in need on a day of hardship.

What does fakku raqabatin express?

فَكُّ (fakku) is a verbal noun, and رَقَبَةٍ (raqabatin), in the genitive construction, is the object of that action.

Raqabah physically means “neck,” but here stands for the entire person. The intention is to release someone from a condition that has taken away their freedom.

In established Arabic legal usage, fakku raqabah concerns freeing a person from slavery.

“Opening a neck” or “undoing a neck bond” is consequently inadequate without explanation. Readers might imagine that the verse concerns only unfastening a chain or knot around someone’s neck.

The important matter is the person’s release from slavery and the restoration of freedom.

This fits the root’s basic meaning: the bond or relationship constraining the person’s freedom is undone.

Fakk itself does not specify whether freedom is achieved by payment, agreement, direct emancipation, or several people’s participation. The expression conveys the result of release; determining how it is achieved requires other evidence and circumstances.

Does fakku raqabah mean only one person completely freeing another?

In Arabic, fakku raqabah can convey the result of freeing a person without requiring its agent to be the only individual who meets all costs or conditions of release.

Someone may free another person alone, for example, or several people may help make that person’s freedom possible.

Early lexical and legal accounts sometimes distinguish between complete emancipation by one person and helping someone attain freedom.

This legal distinction should not be made the exclusive meaning of the Qur’anic phrase without evidence. Al-Balad does not specify a payment method or the number of people securing release.

The clearer intention is that a person in bondage be released.

Conversely, not every financial contribution to someone in need is automatically fakku raqabah. Where neither slavery nor captivity is involved, applying this particular expression requires linguistic and contextual grounds.

The broad meaning of release from a constraint must therefore be distinguished from its particular application in Al-Balad.

Why is raqabah used instead of “person”?

In Arabic, the name of a body part can stand for a whole person. رَأْس (raʾs), “head,” for example, can refer in some constructions to a person or a count of people.

Raqabah has this kind of use as well.

Where someone is enslaved or held captive, the neck can feature in the image of binding and holding that person. This does not establish that all slaves or captives at all times were necessarily held with an actual bond around the neck.

Raqabah here has an established legal use: the whole person who is freed from slavery.

This also prevents a mistaken interpretation: fakku raqabah means neither breaking a neck nor severing it from the body. Fakk here concerns release rather than bodily injury.

The distinction from taḥrīru raqabatin

Al-Nisāʾ 4:92 states:

وَمَنْ قَتَلَ مُؤْمِنًا خَطَأً فَتَحْرِيرُ رَقَبَةٍ مُؤْمِنَةٍ

“And whoever kills a believer by mistake is obliged to free a believing person from slavery.”

(Al-Nisāʾ 4:92; part of the verse)

تَحْرِيرُ رَقَبَةٍ (taḥrīru raqabatin) also occurs in Al-Māʾidah 5:89 and Al-Mujādilah 58:3.

تَحْرِير (taḥrīr), from ح ر ر (ḥāʾ–rāʾ–rāʾ), concerns making free. Fakk speaks of undoing a constraint and releasing something from bondage.

In the context of slavery, both can result in someone’s freedom, but their linguistic images differ.

Fakk emphasizes the removal of a bond or constraining relationship; taḥrīr emphasizes the person becoming free.

This does not mean that they establish two different rulings in every situation. In Al-Balad, fakku raqabah occurs among difficult, good actions; elsewhere, taḥrīru raqabatin occurs in particular legal and compensatory contexts.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:92 also qualifies the raqabah as مُؤْمِنَةٍ (muʾminatin), “believing.” Al-Balad 90:13 contains no such qualification.

A qualification in one verse should not be added without evidence to another verse’s wording. Fakku raqabatin itself means freeing a person from bondage; the phrase alone does not specify every religious or legal characteristic of the person being freed.

Fakku raqabah and iṭʿām: two actions on one path

Al-Balad places freeing a person from bondage alongside feeding someone in need.

أَوْ إِطْعَامٌ فِي يَوْمٍ ذِي مَسْغَبَةٍ (aw iṭʿāmun fī yawmin dhī masghabatin) concerns providing food on a day of hunger and scarcity.

Two particular groups are then identified: an orphan who is a relative and a destitute person.

This sequence connects crossing the difficult pass with actions addressing real human suffering: loss of freedom in one case, deprivation of food in the other.

Fakk itself does not mean feeding, patience, or mercy. Those concepts are expressed in separate verses.

Nor should fakku raqabah, because it occurs in this context, be treated solely as a metaphor for someone becoming free of their own sins.

Release from sin can be a separate subject of ethical reflection. Here, however, the established use of raqabah and its association with practical care for people in need support freeing an enslaved person as the more direct and firmer meaning.

Al-Balad thus speaks of actions through which people bring about real changes in others’ lives.

3. Fakku raqabatin in Qur’anic readings

In the transmission of Ḥafṣ, Al-Balad 90:13 and Al-Balad 90:14 read:

فَكُّ رَقَبَةٍ ۝ أَوْ إِطْعَامٌ فِي يَوْمٍ ذِي مَسْغَبَةٍ

“Freeing a person from bondage, or feeding on a day of hunger and scarcity.”

(Al-Balad 90:13; Al-Balad 90:14)

In this reading, فَكُّ (fakku) is a nominative verbal noun, and رَقَبَةٍ (raqabatin) its genitive complement. إِطْعَامٌ (iṭʿāmun) is also a verbal noun, coordinated with fakku.

The two actions, freeing a person and providing food, are therefore introduced through nominal forms.

In another canonical reading, transmitted from Ibn Kathīr, Abū ʿAmr, and Al-Kisāʾī, the passage is read as follows:

فَكَّ رَقَبَةً ۝ أَوْ أَطْعَمَ فِي يَوْمٍ ذِي مَسْغَبَةٍ

Here, فَكَّ (fakka) is a past-tense verb and رَقَبَةً (raqabatan) its object. أَطْعَمَ (aṭʿama) is likewise a past-tense verb.

The two verbs themselves mean “he freed a person from bondage” and “he fed on a day of hunger.” They have also been analyzed as an explanation of, or substitution for, اقْتَحَمَ (iqtaḥama). Within the negation of فَلَا اقْتَحَمَ (fa-lā iqtaḥama), the passage concerns not performing these actions, rather than definitively reporting that they were performed.

The syntactic difference between the readings is real: one presents the actions through verbal nouns, the other through past-tense verbs.

Both nevertheless concern the same semantic field: freeing a person from bondage and providing food on a day of hardship.

The distinction should neither be ignored nor taken to imply that one reading concerns actual freedom while the other concerns only spiritual freedom. The morphology itself establishes no such distinction.

4. Munfakkīn: not separating from a condition

Al-Bayyinah 98:1 states:

لَمْ يَكُنِ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا مِنْ أَهْلِ الْكِتَابِ وَالْمُشْرِكِينَ مُنْفَكِّينَ حَتَّىٰ تَأْتِيَهُمُ الْبَيِّنَةُ

“Those among the People of the Book and the polytheists who disbelieved would not separate from [the condition they were in] until the clear proof came to them.”

(Al-Bayyinah 98:1)

مُنْفَكِّينَ (munfakkīn) is the masculine plural active participle of اِنْفَكَّ (infakka).

Its basic sense is “those separating” or “those becoming detached from something.” Here, however, it occurs in the negative construction لَمْ يَكُنْ ... مُنْفَكِّينَ (lam yakun … munfakkīn).

The statement therefore concerns a separation that had not occurred, or a condition continuing until الْبَيِّنَة (al-bayyinah), the clear proof, arrived.

This differs from fakku raqabah. In Al-Balad, a person is released from bondage. In Al-Bayyinah, a group had not separated from a condition before something specified arrived.

Translating munfakkīn here as “freed from slavery” is consequently incorrect.

From what were they not separating?

This is the central question concerning the verse.

After munfakkīn, the verse itself contains no phrase such as مِنْ كُفْرِهِمْ (min kufrihim), “from their disbelief.” Translating “they would not desist from their disbelief” therefore supplies the object of separation from the context; the complete phrase is not present in the verse’s wording.

Early linguistic and exegetical explanations offer several interpretations.

On one interpretation, those who disbelieved would not desist from their disbelief and previous condition until the clear proof came to them.

Another relates infikāk to separation and the emergence of disagreement over a particular matter. The arrival of the clear proof then marks the point at which the groups’ positions toward the truth become clearer and their disagreement becomes apparent.

Another explanation concerns the Prophet’s description and expectation of him before his mission: people did not relinquish the description of the Prophet in the earlier scripture until he himself was sent, after which they disagreed about him. This interpretation likewise supplies the unexpressed complement of munfakkīn from the context. “Expectation” is not an independent meaning of the word itself.

These interpretations differ in subject matter. The general meaning of infikāk alone cannot establish one as certain.

What the word clarifies is a separation or an ending of a condition that had not occurred. Precisely what that condition was must be examined through the verse’s relationship with the rest of the surah.

Ḥattā taʾtiyahumu al-bayyinatu

In the verse, حَتَّىٰ (ḥattā), “until,” introduces the time associated with the clear proof’s arrival.

Munfakkīn is thus connected with an event that is to constitute clear proof for them.

The next two verses explain that clear proof:

رَسُولٌ مِنَ اللَّهِ يَتْلُو صُحُفًا مُطَهَّرَةً ۝ فِيهَا كُتُبٌ قَيِّمَةٌ

“A Messenger from The God, reciting purified sheets, containing upright writings.”

In this use, كُتُب (kutub) has been understood as writings or written ordinances.

(Al-Bayyinah 98:2; Al-Bayyinah 98:3)

The clear proof is therefore not left as an abstract concept without an identified referent. In the surah’s context, it is connected with the Messenger and what he recites.

Ḥattā alone does not establish that everyone addressed necessarily abandoned disbelief or came to believe after the clear proof arrived.

This must also be assessed against the remainder of the surah.

Wa-mā tafarraqa alladhīna ūtū al-kitāba

Al-Bayyinah 98:4 states:

وَمَا تَفَرَّقَ الَّذِينَ أُوتُوا الْكِتَابَ إِلَّا مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا جَاءَتْهُمُ الْبَيِّنَةُ

“And those who were given the Book did not divide until after the clear proof had come to them.”

(Al-Bayyinah 98:4)

The verb تَفَرَّقَ (tafarraqa) belongs to ف ر ق (fāʾ–rāʾ–qāf), rather than F K K.

Its meaning nevertheless connects with one interpretation of munfakkīn.

Verse 4 concerns division among the People of the Book after the clear proof arrived. Some have therefore explained munfakkīn in the first verse through separation and disagreement as well.

An important distinction remains. The first verse has الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا مِنْ أَهْلِ الْكِتَابِ وَالْمُشْرِكِينَ (alladhīna kafarū min ahli al-kitābi wa-al-mushrikīna). In the fourth, the subject is الَّذِينَ أُوتُوا الْكِتَابَ (alladhīna ūtū al-kitāba), “those who were given the Book.”

The groups covered by the sentences are therefore not identical. The first concerns those who disbelieved among the People of the Book and the polytheists; the fourth names the People of the Book in connection with division.

Without evidence, this cannot establish that all polytheists and People of the Book agreed on one belief before the clear proof arrived and separated from one another afterward.

Conversely, ignoring the fourth verse and definitively limiting munfakkīn to abandoning disbelief also lacks the necessary caution.

The verses should be read together while preserving the differences between their words and the groups they address.

Munfakkīn and the meaning of continuing

In some Arabic constructions, مَا اِنْفَكَّ (mā infakka) and لَا يَنْفَكُّ (lā yanfakku) express an action or condition continuing. For example, ما انفك يفعل (mā infakka yafʿalu) means “he continues to do.”

In this construction, infakka functions as a verb expressing continuation through the negation of separation.

In Al-Bayyinah 98:1, however, munfakkīn is an active participle serving as the predicate of يَكُنْ (yakun).

On this basis, some grammarians emphasize separation as its meaning here, distinguishing it from the particular syntactic construction mā infakka yafʿalu.

The distinction matters for translation.

An explanation may say that the group “continued in their previous condition,” but this results from the sentence’s negation of separation. Munfakkīn by itself, without negation, should not be assumed to mean “those continuing to remain.”

On this analysis, the basic meaning remains separation; the sentence’s negation states that this separation had not occurred.

Does the verse make the same judgment about all the People of the Book?

The verse says الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا مِنْ أَهْلِ الْكِتَابِ وَالْمُشْرِكِينَ (alladhīna kafarū min ahli al-kitābi wa-al-mushrikīna).

The qualification الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا (alladhīna kafarū), “those who disbelieved,” and the phrase مِنْ أَهْلِ الْكِتَابِ (min ahli al-kitābi), “among the People of the Book,” therefore matter.

A statement concerning those among the People of the Book who disbelieved should not be extended without evidence to all the People of the Book.

Nor does munfakkīn itself say that everyone under discussion necessarily behaved alike or met the same outcome after the clear proof arrived.

The remainder of the surah also distinguishes believing and doing good deeds from persisting in disbelief.

The arrival of clear proof and people’s responses to it are therefore separate matters.

5. Distinctions from related words

F K K (fakka) and Ḥ L L (ḥalla)

Arabic حَلّ (ḥall) concerns undoing a knot or fastening and, in other uses, solving a problem.

Fakk likewise approaches opening and separating.

Some uses of ḥall emphasize undoing a knot or fastening, while uses of fakk such as freeing a captive or redeeming pledged property highlight the ending of a constraint.

This is a tendency, not an absolute rule. Both verbs can sometimes suit the same action.

حِلّ (ḥill) in the sense of being lawful belongs to another semantic branch. The proximity of the roots does not justify translating fakku raqabah as “making a raqabah lawful.”

F K K (fakka) and Ṭ L Q (ṭalaqa)

In some Arabic uses, ط ل ق (ṭāʾ–lām–qāf) concerns being free or released.

أَطْلَقَ (aṭlaqa) can mean releasing a person or thing, approaching fakk in this respect.

In its clear uses, fakk emphasizes undoing the connection or constraint preventing release, while إِطْلَاق (iṭlāq) can emphasize the act of letting go or setting free itself.

Freeing a captive, for example, may be expressed with either verb. Neither encompasses every semantic branch of the other.

طَلَاق (ṭalāq) also has a particular legal use in family relationships that should not be transferred without evidence to fakku raqabah.

F K K (fakka) and Ḥ R R (ḥarrara)

Taḥrīr concerns making free, and the Qur’an uses it for freeing a person from slavery.

Fakk can convey the same result, although its linguistic image differs.

In fakku raqabah, removing a bond and ending a constraining condition stand out. In taḥrīru raqabah, the person’s becoming free is expressed more directly.

This distinction matters in studying roots, but does not by itself give the actions different legal standing or ethical value.

F K K (fakka) and F D Y (fadā)

Qur’anic فِدَاء (fidāʾ) concerns giving something in exchange for release or to avert an outcome.

After discussing war captives, Muḥammad 47:4 states:

فَإِمَّا مَنًّا بَعْدُ وَإِمَّا فِدَاءً حَتَّىٰ تَضَعَ الْحَرْبُ أَوْزَارَهَا

“Afterward, either release them graciously or in exchange for ransom, until the war lays down its burdens.”

(Muḥammad 47:4; part of the verse)

Here, a captive’s release can involve a ransom.

Fakk itself does not mean paying a ransom. Release may take place with or without payment.

Fidāʾ is therefore one possible means or relationship involved in release, rather than the exclusive definition of F K K.

F K K (infakka) and F R Q (faraqa)

فَرْق (farq) concerns separating and distinguishing.

Infikāk likewise approaches separation, but can concern leaving a connection or a condition coming to an end.

Both families occur in Al-Bayyinah 98:1 and Al-Bayyinah 98:4: مُنْفَكِّينَ (munfakkīn) and تَفَرَّقَ (tafarraqa).

This proximity deserves examination without making the words completely identical.

A person may leave a previous condition without division arising among several groups. A group may likewise become divided without each individual abandoning every previous belief.

Infikāk and تَفَرُّق (tafarruq) can therefore accompany each other in some events while remaining distinct concepts.

F K K (fakka) and N Q Ḍ (naqaḍa)

نَقْض (naqḍ) concerns breaking or undoing something made firm, as in breaking a covenant.

Fakk can also undo a connection, but does not necessarily mean breaking something or violating an obligation.

Pledged property may properly be released after an obligation has been fulfilled. In that case, the pledge relationship has ended, but this ending is not necessarily a breach of covenant.

Every separation or release from a constraint should therefore not be treated as covenant breaking.

F K K is also distinct from ف ك ر (fāʾ–kāf–rāʾ), concerning thought, and ف ك ه (fāʾ–kāf–hāʾ), the family involving fruit and certain uses concerning speech. Sharing the first two letters does not establish a common root.

6. Translation recommendations for Forqan

For physical uses of فَكّ (fakk), “open,” “undo,” and “separate” are suitable.

For expressions involving captivity and slavery, “free” or “release from bondage” is more natural.

In Al-Balad 90:13, فَكُّ رَقَبَةٍ (fakku raqabatin) is best rendered:

“Freeing a person from bondage.”

This preserves the meaning of release and avoids the unsuitable “opening a neck.”

Where explanation is needed, it can clarify that the expression’s historical and legal use concerns freeing a person from slavery.

For مُنْفَكِّينَ (munfakkīn) in Al-Bayyinah 98:1, “those separating” is the active participle’s direct lexical sense. Within the verse’s negative construction, however, a more natural rendering is needed.

The proposed basic translation is:

“Those among the People of the Book and the polytheists who disbelieved would not separate from [the condition they were in] until the clear proof came to them.”

This preserves the central direction of infikāk and cautiously states what is being left behind.

An explanation can present the two main interpretations: not desisting from disbelief and the previous condition, or the relevant separation and disagreement not emerging before the clear proof’s arrival.

The basic translation should not mix both interpretations into an ambiguous expression.

Nor should munfakkīn here be translated as “freed people,” “freed slaves,” or “those released from captivity.” Such a meaning does not fit the surah’s context.

Conclusion

The Arabic root F K K concerns undoing a connection, separating attached things, and releasing something from a constraint or condition that limits it.

This appears in physical uses such as undoing a seal or separating two pieces, legal uses such as redeeming pledged property, and human relationships such as freeing a captive or slave.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an.

In Al-Balad 90:13, fakku raqabatin means freeing a person from bondage. The act occurs among the deeds through which crossing the difficult pass is described. The same passage also mentions feeding people in need and mutually urging patience and mercy.

In Al-Bayyinah 98:1, munfakkīn concerns people who had not separated from a condition before the clear proof arrived. There is a recognized difference of interpretation concerning the precise object of this separation. One relates it to not abandoning disbelief and the previous condition; another emphasizes separation and disagreement after the clear proof arrived.

This difference shows that the meaning of infikāk itself is clear, but identifying what people separate from requires context.

The two Qur’anic forms also differ in another respect. Fakk in Al-Balad concerns an act of release, while munfakkīn in Al-Bayyinah describes people whose separation is negated by the sentence’s negative construction.

The semantic core of F K K can be stated as undoing a connection or constraint that binds or limits something, and separating or releasing that thing from its previous condition.

This encompasses opening a physical connection as well as legal release and human emancipation. It also relates infikāk to separation from a condition without imposing a meaning of slavery or captivity.

Fakku raqabatin in Al-Balad should accordingly be understood in its clear sense of freeing a person from bondage. Munfakkīn in Al-Bayyinah should be examined through the sentence’s negation and the verses’ context. They belong to one linguistic family, but one expresses an act of release and the other the absence of a separation whose precise object requires contextual investigation.
