From tending property and caring for people to granting blessings, maternal uncles, and maternal aunts: gift, possession, and responsibility in the Qur’an

The Arabic root KH W L has several semantic branches. One important branch concerns care, maintenance, supervision, and management. Within the same family, خَوَّلَ means to grant property or a blessing and place something at another person’s disposal. In another branch, خال and خالة name two specific relationships on the mother’s side.

This family also had varied uses in Arab social life. خائل could designate someone who tended livestock and property; خَوَل referred to servants, dependents, and people under another person’s management or authority; and خَوَّلَهُ مَالًا meant granting someone property and placing it at their disposal.

These uses do not all mean the same thing. Caring for property, owning it, granting it to another person, and having maternal relatives are four different situations. Although Arabic dictionaries record them within one lexical family, independent evidence is needed to derive them all from a single earliest historical meaning.

In the Qur’an, the root occurs eight times in six verses, in three lexical groups: خَوَّلَ for granting and placing at someone’s disposal, خال for a mother’s brother, and خالة for a mother’s sister.

Its Qur’anic uses matter in two respects: a person’s relationship with a blessing received and their response to it; and maternal kinship within family structures and social rulings.

1. Forms and Semantic Fields

KH W L is a triliteral hollow root with wāw as its second radical. In certain inflected forms, this wāw becomes alif.

Its principal recorded forms include:

  • خَالَ ـ يَخُولُ: To manage property or livestock, maintain it, and attend to its needs.
  • خَوْل and خِيَال: Verbal nouns for care, maintenance, and management.
  • خَائِل: A caretaker, supervisor, or someone who tends property and livestock.
  • خَوَل: Servants, dependents, and people under another’s authority or management; in another use, gifts and resources granted to someone.
  • خَوْلِيّ: A person responsible for tending property, livestock, or other people’s affairs.
  • خَوَّلَ ـ يُخَوِّلُ: To grant something to another and place it at their disposal.
  • تَخْوِيل: Granting property or a blessing and making it available.
  • مُخَوَّل: Someone granted property, a blessing, or resources.
  • تَخَوَّلَ: To attend to a person or thing, returning to it at suitable opportunities; in kinship usage, to take someone as a maternal uncle.
  • اِسْتَخْوَلَ: In some uses, to request property or an item for use; in another, to take someone or a group as maternal kin or dependents.
  • خَال: A mother’s brother.
  • خَالَة: A mother’s sister.
  • أَخْوَال: Plural of خال.
  • خَالات: Plural of خالة.
  • خُؤُولَة: Kinship through maternal uncles and the maternal family.
  • مُخْوِل: In kinship usage, someone distinguished by their maternal uncles and maternal connections.

Lexical sources also record خَوْلَة for a female gazelle and as a woman’s name, and أَخْوَلَ أَخْوَلَ for people or things scattering.

Arabic خال also has other senses, including an owner, an army’s banner, and a kind of cloth. Some identically written forms also appear under KH Y L in dictionaries. Identical spelling alone therefore cannot establish an identical root or history.

Only three main groups from this family occur in the Qur’an:

خَوَّلَ, خال, and خالة.

The verb خَوَّلَ occurs as خَوَّلْنَاكُمْ, خَوَّلَهُ, and خَوَّلْنَاهُ.

خال appears as أَخْوَالِكُمْ and خَالِكَ, and خالة as خَالَاتُكُمْ, خَالَاتِكُمْ, and خَالَاتِكَ.

Different pronouns and grammatical cases do not create new meanings for the root itself.

2. Care, Maintenance, and Bestowal

Ibn Fāris on the Root’s Core

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies one fundamental principle for KH W L:

الْخَاءُ وَالْوَاوُ وَاللَّامُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى تَعَهُّدِ الشَّيْءِ.

al-khāʾu wa-al-wāwu wa-al-lāmu aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā taʿahhudi al-shayʾi

Translation:

“Khāʾ, wāw, and lām have one fundamental principle, indicating attending to and caring for something.”

He illustrates it with uses in which someone tends property, livestock, or other people’s affairs.

For example, Arabic has the expression:

فُلَانٌ يَخُولُ عَلَى أَهْلِهِ

fulānun yakhūlu ʿalā ahlihi

Meaning: “So-and-so attends to his family’s needs.”

Likewise:

خَالَ الْمَالَ يَخُولُهُ

khāla al-māla yakhūluhu

Meaning: “He tended and managed the property.”

Here, care involves more than looking at property. It includes activities that maintain, manage, and make proper use of it.

Someone tending a flock, for example, may take the animals to pasture, provide water, milk them, and protect them from harm.

These activities can fall within the semantic field of خَالَ الْمَالَ.

However, caring for property is different from owning it. A shepherd may be responsible for animals belonging to someone else.

Describing someone as خائل therefore does not, by itself, establish ownership of the property.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: attending to property

From Tending Property to Granting It

Within the same family, خَوَّلَهُ مَالًا is recorded for granting someone property.

Ibn Fāris connects this use with tending and caring for property. In his explanation, property granted to someone is something placed under their care and at their disposal.

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

خَوَّلَهُ اللَّهُ نِعْمَةً: مَلَّكَهُ إِيَّاهَا

khawwalahu allāhu niʿmatan: mallakahu iyyāhā

Meaning: “God granted him a blessing and placed it in his possession or at his disposal.”

خَوَّلَهُ الْمَالَ is also explained as granting property, sometimes out of generosity and favor.

Two parties are distinguished in this use: the giver and the recipient.

Yet خَوَّلَ alone does not determine every condition of ownership. The verb does not specify how long the property remains available, what obligations accompany it, or whether it may be taken back under different circumstances.

Such details depend on the kind of grant, the positions of giver and recipient, and the sentence’s context.

In Qur’anic usage, when God is the agent of خَوَّلَ, property and blessings are things a person has received. Human possession, however, is different from absolute ownership independent of God.

خَوَل: Servants and Dependents

In early Arabic, خَوَلُ الرَّجُلِ designated a person’s servants, dependents, and people connected with managing their household or property.

In some uses, it also applied to enslaved men and women.

Al-Farrāʾ explains خَوَل in one use as a person’s followers, whereas other lexicographers relate it to service, care, or possession.

These differences matter: people called خول within a social group did not necessarily all have the same legal status.

A person might be an agent, servant, shepherd, or household dependent. Older social structures also included people subject to another person’s ownership or domination.

These uses reveal historical linguistic realities. Recording them does not endorse every form of dependence or domination, ethically or legislatively.

Moreover, خَوَل meaning servants or dependents does not occur in the Qur’an. It should therefore not replace other expressions such as عبد, أجير, or مَا مَلَكَتْ أَيْمَانُكُمْ.

Each expression needs study within its own lexical field and context.

تَخَوُّل: Care at Suitable Opportunities

Arabic تَخَوَّلَ فُلَانًا means attending to and caring for someone. Returning and renewing that attention may be involved, but the expression does not necessarily mean uninterrupted attention. The example of exhortation is explained with regard to choosing appropriate opportunities and avoiding tiring the audience.

Al-Jawharī in Al-Ṣiḥāḥ also records تَخَوَّلَتِ الرِّيحُ الْأَرْضَ: the wind repeatedly passed over the land and affected it.

This use highlights renewed attention or return. It should not be restricted to kindness, help, or uninterrupted continuity in every instance.

A wind’s repeated passage can have different effects on the land. What the linguistic use establishes is recurrence and follow-up, rather than a positive or negative value for every consequence.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Muḥkam, and Tāj al-ʿArūs: khawal, takhawwul, and kinship

3. Early Arabic Witnesses: Generosity and Kinship

Granting Property in Verse Attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā

Verse attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā reads:

هُنَالِكَ إِنْ يُسْتَخْوَلُوا الْمَالَ يُخْوِلُوا
وَإِنْ يُسْأَلُوا يُعْطُوا وَإِنْ يَيْسِرُوا يُغْلُوا

hunālika in yustakhwalū al-māla yukhwilū / wa-in yusʾalū yuʿṭū wa-in yaysirū yughlū

Translation:

“There, if asked for property to use, they make it available; if asked, they give; and when they engage in maysir, they procure fat camels at a high price for it.” Al-Ṭabarī’s commentary explains يَيْسِرُوا as gambling and يُغْلُوا as selecting expensive fat camels for it. This witness describes generosity within the historical practice of maysir; it does not constitute Qur’anic approval of that practice.

The poet praises a group for generosity and making property available to others.

In this transmission, يُسْتَخْوَلُوا refers to being asked for property or an item to use, and يُخْوِلُوا to making it available.

Lexical explanations relate this use to lending a she-camel for her milk and wool, or a horse for travel and warfare.

Bestowal in this witness therefore need not involve permanent transfer of ownership. Another person may simply be enabled to use the property.

This clarifies the semantic field of making something available.

There is, however, a credible textual variant in this very verse.

A commentary on Zuhayr’s collection also records:

هُنَالِكَ إِنْ يُسْتَخْبَلُوا الْمَالَ يُخْبِلُوا

hunālika in yustakhbalū al-māla yukhbilū

In that reading, the verb belongs to KH B L rather than KH W L.

Abū ʿUbaydah transmits يُسْتَخْوَلُوا from Abū ʿAmr ibn al-ʿAlāʾ, while Yūnus reports يُسْتَخْبَلُوا.

The verse can therefore witness the transmitted use of استخوال in early Arabic, but the KH W L wording must not be presented as certain and undisputed in every transmission of Zuhayr’s poetry.

Study of KH B L

Variants are also reported in the second half. Generosity is clear from the poetic context, but root analysis should rely on the particular wording recorded in a reliable lexical source.

Al-Ṭabarī: Zuhayr’s witness, KH W L / KH B L variants, and maysir

Maternal Kinship in Imruʾ al-Qays’s Poetry

The Muʿallaqah attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays includes:

فَأَدْبَرْنَ كَالْجَزْعِ الْمُفَصَّلِ بَيْنَهُ
بِجِيدِ مُعَمٍّ فِي الْعَشِيرَةِ مُخْوَلِ

fa-adbarna ka-al-jazʿi al-mufaṣṣali baynahu / bi-jīdi muʿammin fī al-ʿashīrati mukhwali

Translation:

“Then those animals turned away, like the variegated beads of a necklace set around the neck of a child from a family with honorable paternal and maternal uncles.”

مُعَمّ refers to family connections and standing through paternal uncles, while مُخْوَل concerns maternal connections and maternal uncles.

Early commentaries on the Muʿallaqah explain these two adjectives through the distinction of the paternal and maternal relatives.

This witness matters because maternal relationships also contributed to identifying family standing in Arabic poetry.

Here مُخْوَل does not mean someone granted property. It is a description of kinship.

Nor does the verse establish that خال historically received its name because of guardianship, generosity, or care for a family. The verse witnesses only the kinship use.

Al-Zawzanī’s commentary on the Muʿallaqāt: a child of distinguished kin

Did خال Develop from the Meaning of Care?

Maternal relatives may be socially associated with support and care, just as a paternal or maternal uncle may support a sibling’s child in some families.

That social reality alone does not establish that خال derives from خَالَ الْمَالَ, tending property.

For خُؤُولَة, Lisān al-ʿArab explicitly states that no independent verb expresses the kinship relationship itself, although forms such as تَخَوَّلَ خَالًا are recorded for taking or calling someone a maternal uncle.

Ibn Fāris’s core of care and maintenance thus provides clear support for explaining خَوَّلَ, خائل, and خَوَل. Extending it to the historical origin of خال and خالة requires further evidence.

Understanding their Qur’anic meaning does not require settling that historical connection. خال and خالة denote clear, established family relationships in the Qur’an.

4. خَوَّلَ in the Qur’an: Blessings Made Available to People

What People Leave Behind on the Day of Reckoning: Al-Anʿām 6:94

وَلَقَدْ جِئْتُمُونَا فُرَادَىٰ كَمَا خَلَقْنَاكُمْ أَوَّلَ مَرَّةٍ وَتَرَكْتُمْ مَا خَوَّلْنَاكُمْ وَرَاءَ ظُهُورِكُمْ وَمَا نَرَىٰ مَعَكُمْ شُفَعَاءَكُمُ الَّذِينَ زَعَمْتُمْ أَنَّهُمْ فِيكُمْ شُرَكَاءُ

wa-laqad jiʾtumūnā furādā ka-mā khalaqnākum awwala marratin wa-taraktum mā khawwalnākum warāʾa ẓuhūrikum wa-mā narā maʿakum shufaʿāʾakumu alladhīna zaʿamtum annahum fīkum shurakāʾu

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“You have indeed come to Us individually, just as We created you the first time; you have left behind your backs what We had placed at your disposal; and We do not see with you your intercessors, whom you claimed to be partners concerning you.”

Al-Anʿām 6:94

خَوَّلْنَاكُمْ is a perfect verb of Form II.

Its subject is the pronoun نا, “We,” and the recipients are expressed by كم, “you.”

مَا in مَا خَوَّلْنَاكُمْ refers to what God had placed at people’s disposal.

Here bestowal concerns possession of property and resources, but the word is not limited to money or cash assets.

In context, what a person received in this world does not accompany them when they appear on the Day of Reckoning.

The verse contrasts two situations: a person possessed resources in worldly life and has now come alone before God.

This contrast shows that worldly possession, though real, does not provide permanent, independent companionship.

However, the lexical meaning of خَوَّلْنَاكُمْ must not become “We took back” or “We destroyed.” The verb expresses granting and making available; leaving property behind at life’s end is conveyed by وَتَرَكْتُمْ.

The continuation speaks of intercessors whom people imagined to be partners concerning them.

This connection shows the Qur’an challenging both reliance on worldly possessions and claims about intercessors and partners at the reckoning.

Yet تخويل itself does not mean intercession, partnership, or a claim to divine power. These concepts come from separate parts of the verse.

A Blessing after Hardship: Al-Zumar 39:8

وَإِذَا مَسَّ الْإِنْسَانَ ضُرٌّ دَعَا رَبَّهُ مُنِيبًا إِلَيْهِ ثُمَّ إِذَا خَوَّلَهُ نِعْمَةً مِنْهُ نَسِيَ مَا كَانَ يَدْعُو إِلَيْهِ مِنْ قَبْلُ وَجَعَلَ لِلَّهِ أَنْدَادًا لِيُضِلَّ عَنْ سَبِيلِهِ

wa-idhā massa al-insāna ḍurrun daʿā rabbahu munīban ilayhi thumma idhā khawwalahu niʿmatan minhu nasiya mā kāna yadʿū ilayhi min qablu wa-jaʿala li-allāhi andādan li-yuḍilla ʿan sabīlihi

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“When harm touches a person, he calls upon his Lord, turning back to Him. Then, when He grants him a blessing from Himself, he forgets the One he previously called upon, and sets up rivals for The God to lead astray from His path.”

Al-Zumar 39:8

خَوَّلَهُ here refers to granting a person a blessing.

نِعْمَةً specifies what is granted, and مِنْهُ identifies the grant’s source.

At the beginning, the person is afflicted by ضُرّ, harm. In hardship, he turns to his Lord and calls upon Him.

The situation then changes, and a blessing is granted.

In this context, the blessing may involve removal of the earlier harm, but نِعْمَةً itself is not restricted to one instance.

The important distinction is that granting a blessing and a person’s response are different concepts.

خَوَّلَهُ expresses the act of granting.

نَسِيَ introduces the person’s forgetful response. The proposed translation takes مَا as referring to the One previously invoked; Al-Ṭabarī also discusses a verbal-noun analysis, abandoning the earlier supplication. This exegetical difference does not alter the meaning of خَوَّلَهُ.

وَجَعَلَ لِلَّهِ أَنْدَادًا describes another action in the continuation.

Thus خَوَّلَ here does not mean ingratitude, forgetting, or setting up rivals. These responses follow receipt of the blessing and are expressed by separate verbs.

Nor does receiving a blessing itself establish that someone committed associationism. The verse criticizes a person who, after a change in circumstances, forgets God and sets up rivals for Him.

The distinction matters for understanding blessings and human responsibility: receiving a blessing is one reality; how someone responds is another matter.

Al-Ṭabarī: two analyses of mā and the meaning of bestowal

A Blessing and a Claim to Personal Merit: Al-Zumar 39:49

فَإِذَا مَسَّ الْإِنْسَانَ ضُرٌّ دَعَانَا ثُمَّ إِذَا خَوَّلْنَاهُ نِعْمَةً مِنَّا قَالَ إِنَّمَا أُوتِيتُهُ عَلَىٰ عِلْمٍ بَلْ هِيَ فِتْنَةٌ وَلَٰكِنَّ أَكْثَرَهُمْ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ

fa-idhā massa al-insāna ḍurrun daʿānā thumma idhā khawwalnāhu niʿmatan minnā qāla innamā ūtītuhu ʿalā ʿilmin bal hiya fitnatun wa-lākinna aktharahum lā yaʿlamūna

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“When harm touches a person, he calls upon Us. Then, when We grant him a blessing from Us, he says: ‘I was given it only on the basis of knowledge.’ Rather, it is a trial, but most of them do not know.”

Al-Zumar 39:49

This verse resembles Al-Zumar 39:8 in structure.

In both, a person first experiences harm and invokes God. A blessing is then granted, and the person’s behavior or speech changes.

The kind of response, however, differs.

Verse eight mentions forgetting and setting up rivals.

In verse forty-nine, the person says:

إِنَّمَا أُوتِيتُهُ عَلَىٰ عِلْمٍ

innamā ūtītuhu ʿalā ʿilmin

This is the person’s own claim, rather than the Qur’an’s endorsed judgment about the true reason for receiving the blessing.

The Qur’an then says:

بَلْ هِيَ فِتْنَةٌ

bal hiya fitnatun

That is, the blessing in question is a trial.

This sequence shows that a person may claim entitlement after receiving a blessing. Attributing it to the person’s knowledge is one reading of عَلَىٰ عِلْمٍ; Al-Ṭabarī explains it as a claim that God knew the person deserved it. The verse itself does not contain عِنْدِي. That word should not be added to the Arabic, nor should one interpretation be treated as the only possible meaning.

The verse does not establish that human knowledge, effort, skill, and decision-making play no part in obtaining resources.

What is criticized is the person’s claim and manner of attributing the blessing to himself. The verse itself ends by speaking of a trial.

Lexically, خَوَّلْنَاهُ means “We granted him” or “We placed at his disposal.”

The sense of trial comes from another word in the verse, فتنة. It should not become part of the fixed definition of تخويل.

Al-Ṭabarī: ʿalā ʿilm as a claim to deserving the blessing

The Three Uses of خَوَّلَ Together

In all three verses, God is the giver and the human being the recipient.

What follows receipt of the blessing differs.

In Al-Anʿām 6:94, the person has left behind what had been placed at their disposal.

In Al-Zumar 39:8, receiving a blessing is followed by forgetting God and setting up rivals.

In Al-Zumar 39:49, the person regards the blessing as received “on the basis of knowledge.” Whose knowledge is intended is an exegetical question; the Qur’an identifies the blessing as a trial.

The meaning of خَوَّلَ in these three uses is therefore granting and making available. Ethical and existential implications emerge from each verse’s context.

5. Maternal Uncles and Aunts in the Qur’an

Maternal Aunts among Relatives Forbidden in Marriage: Al-Nisāʾ 4:23

Sūrat al-Nisāʾ states:

حُرِّمَتْ عَلَيْكُمْ أُمَّهَاتُكُمْ وَبَنَاتُكُمْ وَأَخَوَاتُكُمْ وَعَمَّاتُكُمْ وَخَالَاتُكُمْ وَبَنَاتُ الْأَخِ وَبَنَاتُ الْأُخْتِ

ḥurrimat ʿalaykum ummahātukum wa-banātukum wa-akhawātukum wa-ʿammātukum wa-khālātukum wa-banātu al-akhi wa-banātu al-ukhti

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Forbidden to you are your mothers, your daughters, your sisters, your paternal aunts, your maternal aunts, your brothers’ daughters, and your sisters’ daughters.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:23

خَالَاتُكُمْ is the plural of خالة and refers to mothers’ sisters.

Maternal aunts appear alongside paternal aunts and other specified relatives.

Lexically, خالة means a mother’s sister, rather than any older woman or a woman emotionally close to someone.

Some cultures respectfully call women without a direct family relationship “aunt.” That social usage does not change the genealogical meaning here.

A maternal aunt must also be distinguished from her daughter.

A maternal aunt is the mother’s sister. Her daughter belongs to a different generation and has a different relationship to the person.

This distinction matters in family rulings; the two relationships must not be conflated merely because their names overlap.

Houses of Maternal Uncles and Aunts: Al-Nūr 24:61

Al-Nūr 24:61 includes:

أَوْ بُيُوتِ أَخْوَالِكُمْ أَوْ بُيُوتِ خَالَاتِكُمْ

aw buyūti akhwālikum aw buyūti khālātikum

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Or the houses of your maternal uncles, or the houses of your maternal aunts.”

Both maternal relationships appear alongside other relatives.

أَخْوَالِكُمْ is the plural of خال, and خَالَاتِكُمْ the plural of خالة.

Both nouns are attached to the pronoun كم and refer to the addressees’ relatives.

The verse concerns eating in one’s own home, relatives’ homes, and those of certain other people.

The list also mentions the houses of fathers, mothers, brothers, sisters, paternal uncles, and paternal aunts.

This structure places maternal and paternal relations together within actual networks of kinship and social interaction.

Yet أَخْوَالِكُمْ and خَالَاتِكُمْ themselves do not establish a right for anyone, at any time, to consume household property without regard for its owners’ consent and rights.

The verse needs to be understood within its subject of food, close relationships, and other principles concerning people’s rights.

The lexical conclusion is clear: maternal uncle and aunt are two specific kinship titles, used with those established meanings in the Qur’an.

Daughters of Maternal Uncles and Aunts: Al-Aḥzāb 33:50

In Sūrat al-Aḥzāb, the Prophet is addressed:

وَبَنَاتِ عَمِّكَ وَبَنَاتِ عَمَّاتِكَ وَبَنَاتِ خَالِكَ وَبَنَاتِ خَالَاتِكَ اللَّاتِي هَاجَرْنَ مَعَكَ

wa-banāti ʿammika wa-banāti ʿammātika wa-banāti khālika wa-banāti khālātika allātī hājarna maʿaka

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“And the daughters of your paternal uncle, the daughters of your paternal aunts, the daughters of your maternal uncle, and the daughters of your maternal aunts who migrated with you.”

Al-Aḥzāb 33:50

Here خَالِكَ is singular and خَالَاتِكَ plural.

The difference does not establish that the Prophet had exactly one maternal uncle and several maternal aunts.

This construction can express established categories of family relationship. It does not determine the actual number of someone’s relatives.

The verse also concerns family relationships in the context of marriage.

A maternal uncle’s daughter and a maternal aunt’s daughter have different relationships to the addressee, although both belong to maternal kinship.

Another point is the qualification اللَّاتِي هَاجَرْنَ مَعَكَ.

It forms part of the verse and should not be omitted when discussing its rulings.

The verse specifically addresses the Prophet. Conclusions about general marriage rulings therefore require examining the relevant verses together, rather than relying on خال or خالة alone.

Lexically, the use clearly illustrates this family: خال is a mother’s brother, خالة a mother’s sister, and their daughters are relatives in the next generation.

The Place of Maternal Kinship

Together, these three verses recognize maternal relationships in several distinct settings: the prohibition of marriage to a maternal aunt, social interaction and eating in maternal relatives’ houses, and reference to their daughters in the context of marriage.

The title of maternal uncle or aunt alone does not imply guardianship, ownership, legal authority, or control over every aspect of someone’s life.

A maternal uncle may provide important support within a family. That social role should not be inserted without evidence into the lexical definition of the relationship.

Nor can the presence of maternal uncles and aunts in Qur’anic rulings yield a complete system of inheritance, guardianship, or financial responsibility. Each subject needs its own relevant wording and verses.

6. Comparison with Related Roots

KH W L (تَخْوِيل) and W H B (هِبَة)

W H B concerns giving and granting.

In Qur’anic usage, خَوَّلَ likewise means granting and making something available.

Both can often be translated “to grant,” but their lexical fields differ.

Giving is prominent in W H B. Alongside bestowal, KH W L has a lexical background of care and possession.

This should not become an absolute rule that هبة always transfers ownership permanently while تخويل always entrusts property temporarily. Such a universal distinction is not established by the roots themselves.

KH W L (تَخْوِيل) and M L K (مِلْك)

M L K concerns ownership, authority, and the capacity to manage or rule.

Lexical explanations sometimes gloss خَوَّلَهُ مَالًا as مَلَّكَهُ إِيَّاهُ.

Yet خَوَّلَ expresses granting or placing something at another’s disposal, whereas مِلْك can describe the relationship of ownership or control itself.

Bestowal may thus result in possession or ownership, but the verb alone does not specify its details.

In Al-Anʿām 6:94, people also leave behind what they possessed when returning to God.

The context shows that worldly possession, even where socially real ownership exists, does not amount to independent, permanent control.

Study of M L K

KH W L (تَخْوِيل) and R Z Q (رِزْق)

رزق concerns a share or provision reaching a person or creature and sustaining their enjoyment or benefit.

خَوَّلَ is used in the Qur’an for granting a blessing.

The fields may overlap in one instance: property God grants someone may also be their provision.

However, رزق itself does not mean the action of تخويل.

Blessings in the Qur’an are not limited to property and food. خَوَّلَهُ نِعْمَةً should therefore not invariably become “He gave him money.”

Study of R Z Q

KH W L (خَال) and ʿ M M (عَمّ)

خال is a mother’s brother; عم is a father’s brother.

These are different family relationships, although both belong to the parents’ generation.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:23 and Al-Aḥzāb 33:50 place paternal and maternal aunts, or daughters of paternal and maternal uncles and aunts, alongside one another.

This juxtaposition preserves the genealogical distinction.

Translation must also preserve the difference between maternal and paternal uncles and aunts, even where their social roles are similar.

KH W L (تَخْوِيل) and Ḥ W L (حَوْل)

Ḥ W L is a separate root with Qur’anic uses concerning change, surroundings, and capacity.

KH W L, however, concerns care, bestowal, and maternal kinship.

Similar sounds or a change of letters do not make the two roots identical.

Persian words such as تحويل may also cause confusion. تحويل comes from Ḥ W L, rather than KH W L.

خَوَّلْنَاهُ must therefore not be translated as changing or transforming something merely because it resembles حوّلناه in writing.

Study of Ḥ W L

KH W L (خَائِل) and R ʿ Y (رِعَايَة)

In material use, R ʿ Y concerns pasturing and tending animals; other uses concern observance and care.

Arabic خائل can likewise designate someone tending livestock and property.

The two overlap in certain instances but are not identical.

Tending a flock, for example, may be described with either رعاية or خَوْل. Yet Qur’anic خَوَّلَهُ نِعْمَةً refers to granting a blessing and cannot become “He cared for him” without supporting context.

Nor should خال, a mother’s brother, be defined as a shepherd or caretaker simply because it resembles خائل.

Study of R ʿ Y

7. Translation Proposals for Forqan

The two Qur’anic branches need separate translations.

For the three uses of خَوَّلَ, the basic equivalents are “grant” and “place at someone’s disposal.”

In Al-Anʿām 6:94, “what We had placed at your disposal” is natural and accurate: the verse emphasizes resources people possessed and have now left behind.

In Al-Zumar 39:8 and Al-Zumar 39:49, “He granted him a blessing” and “We granted him a blessing,” respectively, are suitable. نِعْمَةً explicitly identifies the object granted, and مِنْهُ or مِنَّا indicates its source.

The kinship forms have clear Persian and Dari equivalents: خال, خاله, and their plurals, corresponding to maternal uncle and maternal aunt.

Rendering خال as “maternal guardian” or “family caretaker” is inappropriate here: those roles are not necessary parts of the kinship relationship’s meaning.

Brief Root Meaning

Care and maintenance; granting and placing at someone’s disposal. In the kinship branch, maternal uncle and maternal aunt.

Meaning in Qur’anic Uses

خَوَّلْنَاكُمْ — Al-Anʿām 6:94: What We had given you and placed at your disposal.

خَوَّلَهُ — Al-Zumar 39:8: He granted him a blessing.

خَوَّلْنَاهُ — Al-Zumar 39:49: We granted him a blessing.

خَالَاتُكُمْ — Al-Nisāʾ 4:23: Your maternal aunts; your mothers’ sisters.

أَخْوَالِكُمْ — Al-Nūr 24:61: Your maternal uncles; your mothers’ brothers.

خَالَاتِكُمْ — Al-Nūr 24:61: Your maternal aunts.

خَالِكَ — Al-Aḥzāb 33:50: Your maternal uncle; your mother’s brother.

خَالَاتِكَ — Al-Aḥzāb 33:50: Your maternal aunts; your mother’s sisters.

The three uses of the verb تخويل concern blessings and possession. The five uses of خال and خالة concern family relationships.

Shared root letters should not force these two branches into one fixed Persian equivalent.

Conclusion

Arabic KH W L has several semantic branches. In the branch Ibn Fāris explains through care and maintenance, words concern managing property, tending livestock, caring for people, and granting resources to another.

In the kinship branch, خال and خالة denote two specific maternal relationships. Early poetic witnesses show these relationships were established in Arabic language and family life, but a definite historical connection with care and guardianship has not been demonstrated.

Qur’anic خَوَّلَ occurs three times with God as its agent. In all three, a person receives something granted or made available to them. Worldly possession is nevertheless impermanent, and human responses can differ.

In Al-Anʿām 6:94, what had been placed at people’s disposal is left behind at the reckoning. In Al-Zumar 39:8, a person forgets God after receiving a blessing and sets up rivals for Him. In Al-Zumar 39:49, the person claims the blessing was received “on the basis of knowledge,” while the Qur’an identifies it as a trial.

In the kinship branch, maternal uncles and aunts appear in contexts of marriage, family interaction, and relatives’ homes. These uses preserve the established meaning of maternal relationships.

Therefore, the root should be explained with its two main branches kept distinct: care and bestowal on one side, maternal kinship on the other.

For خَوَّلَ, the preferred Qur’anic meaning is granting and placing at someone’s disposal. The verb alone does not establish absolute ownership, permanent bestowal, every gift’s being a trial, or the moral value of the recipient’s response.

These distinctions emerge from the verses’ contexts. Possessing a blessing, the relationship with its giver, and responsibility in using it are connected but distinct concepts. Brief root meaning: Care and maintenance; granting and placing at someone’s disposal; in the kinship branch, maternal uncle and maternal aunt.
