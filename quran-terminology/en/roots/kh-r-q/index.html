Qur’anic Root KH R Q

Qur’anic and lexical forms

وَخَرَقُوا
(wa-kharaqū)
Active perfect, third-person masculine plural; Ḥafṣ; Al-Anʿām 6:100

تَخْرِقَ
(takhriqa)
Subjunctive imperfect after lan, second-person singular; Al-Isrāʾ 17:37

خَرَقَهَا
(kharaqahā)
Third-person singular perfect, with object hā; Al-Kahf 18:71

أَخَرَقْتَهَا
(a-kharaqtahā)
Second-person singular perfect with interrogative hamzah; not Form IV; Al-Kahf 18:71

وَخَرَّقُوا
(wa-kharraqū)
Form II in the readings of Nāfiʿ and Abū Jaʿfar; Al-Anʿām 6:100

خِرْقَة
(khirqah)
A piece of cloth; a non-Qur’anic lexical form

اِخْتَرَقَ ـ مُخْتَرَق
(ikhtaraqa–mukhtaraq)
Passing through and a place of passage; non-Qur’anic lexical forms

خُرْق ـ أَخْرَق
(khurq–akhraq)
Lack of skill and its personal description; not Qur’anic

Splitting, piercing, and passing through a barrier: from physical tears to fabricating unsupported claims

The Arabic root خ ر ق concerns splitting, piercing, tearing, and passing through or across something. Its derivatives may describe torn fabric, a hole made in a ship’s hull, or someone traversing an extensive land.

Physically, خَرْق can be an opening that disrupts a surface’s continuity: previously intact fabric acquires a hole when pierced. Another usage describes crossing extensive land with خَرَقَ الْأَرْضَ, as a person makes their way through it.

Early Arabic also developed other usages. خُرْق stands opposite رِفْق and concerns unconsidered, unskillful behavior. خَرَقَ الْكَذِبَ also means constructing a false, unsupported statement.

These usages must not be reduced to one meaning. Piercing something may be deliberate and considered, or hasty and harmful. Traversing land likewise does not necessarily mean damaging it.

The Qur’an uses this root four times in three verses.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:37 concerns someone walking arrogantly on earth, although unable to split the earth or match the mountains in height.

In Al-Kahf 18:71, Moses’ companion makes an opening in a ship, and Moses fears for its passengers. The later explanation identifies the purpose: damaging the ship to prevent its seizure.

In Al-Anʿām 6:100, the word concerns speech and assertion: people attribute sons and daughters to The God without knowledge or grounds. Here خَرْق means inventing a false claim.

The root therefore has physical usages and a usage concerning speech and belief. Each requires distinguishing basic meaning, morphology, and context.

1. Forms and Semantic Core

In this study’s base text, following the reading of Ḥafṣ, every occurrence of خ ر ق belongs to the unaugmented triliteral verb:

  • وَخَرَقُوا — Al-Anʿām 6:100
  • تَخْرِقَ — Al-Isrāʾ 17:37
  • خَرَقَهَا — Al-Kahf 18:71
  • أَخَرَقْتَهَا — Al-Kahf 18:71

These are four occurrences in three verses. Two forms appear in Al-Kahf 18:71 because the verb is repeated in the narrative and Moses’ question, not because two different roots are present.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: four occurrences in the Ḥafṣ text, in three verses

Important Arabic forms include:

  • خَرَقَ ـ يَخْرِقُ: split, pierced, tore, or passed through something.
  • خَرْق: splitting or piercing; also the resulting opening.
  • خُرُوق: plural of خَرْق, openings or holes.
  • خِرْقَة: a piece of cloth or a fragment detached from fabric.
  • خَرَّقَ ـ يُخَرِّقُ: split or tore something into parts; sometimes with intensity or repetition.
  • اِخْتَرَقَ: passed through, split, or crossed a barrier.
  • اِنْخَرَقَ: became split or pierced.
  • مُخْتَرَق: a passage or route through which something passes.
  • خَرِيق: in one usage, wind or a current passing through spaces; sources differ on its strength.
  • خَرْق: geographically, extensive land or a desert to be traversed.
  • خِرْق: in another usage, a generous, openhanded person.
  • خُرْق: opposed to رِفْق: lack of skill, inexperience, or unconsidered behavior.
  • أَخْرَق and خَرْقَاء: someone lacking skill, judgment, or finesse in a task; physically, something pierced.
  • تَخَرَّقَ: became split or torn; in another branch, invented a false statement.
  • مِخْرَاق: in some early usages, an implement for play or striking; elsewhere, a description of an exceptionally generous person.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an. Some are recorded only in dictionaries, poetry, or Arabic prose. The geminated خَرَّقُوا occurs in certain readings of Al-Anʿām 6:100, discussed separately below.

Basic Meaning

Ibn Fāris writes in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

الْخَاءُ وَالرَّاءُ وَالْقَافُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ، وَهُوَ مَزْقُ الشَّيْءِ وَجَوْبُهُ.

al-khāʾu wa-al-rāʾu wa-al-qāfu aṣlun wāḥidun wa-huwa mazqu al-shayʾi wa-jawbuhu

That is, خ ر ق has one underlying principle: tearing and splitting something or passing through it.

Two words matter in this definition.

مَزْق concerns tearing and pulling apart.

جَوْب here concerns splitting, traversing, or passing through something.

The two meanings are close, but not identical.

Torn fabric may lose its former function. Someone crossing a desert, however, does not necessarily damage it; they have passed through an expanse.

The semantic core can therefore be described as splitting or opening a way through something, so that its continuity, surface, or extent is breached or crossed.

This does not establish an actual hole or physical damage in every usage.

خَرَقُوا لَهُ بَنِينَ وَبَنَاتٍ concerns speech and unsupported attribution, rather than piercing an object.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: tearing, passage, and traversing land

Does Kharq Always Involve Harm or Lack of Judgment?

In Al-Mufradāt, Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī explains خَرْق through cutting or splitting without reflection or assessment.

On that basis he contrasts it with خَلْق, relating the latter to measured, considered action and the former to action lacking such ordering.

This helps explain one branch but should not become a definitive account of every usage.

For example, someone pierces the ship in Al-Kahf 18:71. Moses initially considers the act dangerous and objectionable, but Al-Kahf 18:79 explains its purpose as preventing seizure.

In this Qur’anic occurrence, therefore, an apparently destructive act has a specific purpose.

Lack of judgment is consequently not essential to خَرَقَ.

Someone may likewise traverse land deliberately and according to a plan in خَرَقَ الْأَرْضَ.

Splitting and crossing should therefore remain distinct from an ethical judgment of the act.

Al-Rāghib’s Al-Mufradāt, kh-r-q: judgment, splitting, and two readings of crossing the earth

2. Early Usages: Fabric, Deserts, Wind, and Human Behavior

Piercing Fabric and Skin

A clear Arabic usage is خَرَقَ الثَّوْبَ: piercing or tearing fabric.

Here خَرْق disrupts the fabric’s continuity at a particular point or along an extent.

Fabric may be pierced with a sharp tool, torn by tension, or split through wear.

The verb itself does not specify cause or intention.

Cutting cloth for clothing can be purposeful work. Tearing another person’s clothing to harm them gives the physical act a different ethical significance.

The related خِرْقَة denotes a piece of cloth.

Lexical sources also record خَرْقَاء for an animal with a pierced or split ear.

These usages concern an opening in a surface. The opening alone nevertheless does not judge the act’s legitimacy, purpose, or outcome.

Traversing Land

Early Arabic records:

خَرَقَ الْأَرْضَ

This means traversing or passing across land.

It need not involve splitting the ground with a tool.

A person may travel a long route through a desert.

As a noun, خَرْق also denotes a very extensive desert or expanse of land.

Ibn Fāris connects this usage with passage and making a way through.

Lisān al-ʿArab also records خَرْق for extensive, remote land where a traveler must cover a great distance.

This branch therefore emphasizes crossing a spatial extent.

It matters for Al-Isrāʾ 17:37, since some lexicographers explain لَنْ تَخْرِقَ الْأَرْضَ through human inability to traverse or pierce the earth.

A Hudhalī Poetic Witness

Lisān al-ʿArab attributes this verse to Maʿqil ibn Khuwaylid al-Hudhalī:

وَإِنَّهُمَا لَجَوَّابَا خُرُوقٍ
وَشَرَّابَانِ بِالنُّطَفِ الطَّوَامِي

wa-innahumā la-jawwābā khurūqin / wa-sharrābāni bi-al-nuṭafi al-ṭawāmī

Approximate translation:

“Those two traverse vast deserts and drink from abundant, gathered waters.”

خُرُوق here denotes deserts and extensive lands.

جَوَّابَا likewise concerns traversing and passing through them.

This witness clarifies the geographical sense: خَرْق is the name of an expanse crossed by a traveler, rather than an actual hole in the ground.

Maʿqil ibn Khuwaylid was an early Hudhalī poet, but the precise date of this verse is unclear in the available witnesses. It is therefore evidence of early Arabic rather than proof of the verse’s definite pre-Qur’anic date.

Lisān al-ʿArab: desert usage and Maʿqil ibn Khuwaylid’s witness

خَرِيق Wind in Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā’s Poetry

The pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā describes water among low ground and vegetation:

مُكَلَّلٍ بِأُصُولِ النَّجْمِ تَنْسُجُهُ
رِيحٌ خَرِيقٌ لِضَاحِي مَائِهِ حُبُكُ

mukallalin bi-uṣūli al-najmi tansijuhu / rīḥun kharīqun li-ḍāḥī māʾihi ḥubuku

Translation:

“Water bordered with plant roots, upon whose exposed surface a passing wind weaves rippling patterns.”

رِيحٌ خَرِيقٌ describes the wind.

The poet depicts wind passing over water, producing ripples and patterns on its surface.

Lexical sources record خَرِيق for strong wind, but some reports also give a gentle wind or one lacking a constant direction.

A particular strength should therefore not be made certain in every usage.

Here the word chiefly describes wind passing and affecting the water’s surface.

This witness appears in Zuhayr’s collected poetry and early lexical sources. The first hemistich varies between أُصُولِ النَّجْمِ and أُصُولِ النَّبْتِ; this does not alter رِيحٌ خَرِيقٌ or its role in the image.

Zuhayr was pre-Islamic, and the verse matters for understanding an early derivative of the root.

Tāj al-ʿArūs, n-j-m: Zuhayr’s witness with the reading al-najm

It does not establish that Qur’anic خَرْق must mean blowing wind or rippling water.

خُرْق Opposed to رِفْق

Arabic خُرْق can denote behavior involving ignorance, lack of skill, or lack of care and gentleness.

In this usage it stands opposite رِفْق.

رِفْق concerns gentleness, judgment, and an appropriate way of doing something.

Conversely, an أَخْرَق person may work hastily without sufficient skill and spoil the result.

Someone forcing open an implement and breaking its parts without knowing how to repair it can exemplify this field.

A mistake alone, however, does not prove a lack of reason or good intention.

People may err with good intentions because of ignorance or inexperience.

The lexical description أَخْرَق should therefore not become a fixed judgment of a person’s moral worth.

This branch does not occur as an independent Qur’anic derivative, but helps explain an extension from splitting and damage to unconsidered behavior.

خَرَقَ الْكَذِبَ: Inventing a False Statement

Lexical sources record خَرَقَ الْكَذِبَ and تَخَرَّقَ الْكَذِبَ for inventing or fabricating a false statement.

This is essential to Al-Anʿām 6:100.

The subject here is neither torn fabric nor a hole in the earth.

Someone invents an unsupported statement and presents it as news or truth.

Early lexicographers explain this usage through اِخْتِلَاق, اِفْتِرَاء, and كَذِب: invention, false attribution, and falsehood.

This does not establish that every new statement or hypothesis is خَرْق or falsehood.

Someone openly proposing a tentative hypothesis and seeking evidence differs from someone presenting an unsupported assertion as certain truth.

Al-Anʿām 6:100 concerns attributing sons and daughters to The God without knowledge. That qualification clarifies the censured usage.

Lisān al-ʿArab: fabric, wind, behavior, and fabricating speech

3. Al-Isrāʾ 17:37: Can a Human Split the Earth?

وَلَا تَمْشِ فِي الْأَرْضِ مَرَحًا إِنَّكَ لَنْ تَخْرِقَ الْأَرْضَ وَلَنْ تَبْلُغَ الْجِبَالَ طُولًا

wa-lā tamshi fī al-arḍi maraḥan innaka lan takhriqa al-arḍa wa-lan tablugha al-jibāla ṭūlan

Proposed translation in this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Do not walk upon the earth exultantly; you will never split the earth, nor reach the mountains in height.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:37

تَخْرِقَ is an imperfect verb from خ ر ق.

Its subject is an implicit second-person singular pronoun, and its object is الْأَرْضَ.

The verse begins by prohibiting walking مَرَحًا.

لَنْ تَخْرِقَ الْأَرْضَ should therefore be examined within that context of arrogant walking.

Two Lexical Approaches to خَرْقُ الْأَرْضِ

Two related meanings have been proposed.

First, splitting the earth: arrogant walking cannot split the ground or demonstrate extraordinary power over it.

Second, traversing the whole earth: arrogance and self-exaltation do not give someone unlimited ability to cross it.

Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī reports both possibilities.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah also records خَرَقْتُ الْأَرْضَ for traversing the earth.

Contextually, splitting the earth has a clear visual relationship with the following expression:

وَلَنْ تَبْلُغَ الْجِبَالَ طُولًا

You will not reach the mountains in height.

One part concerns the earth beneath a person’s feet; the other, the tall mountains before them.

The image reveals the limits of human power and stature before the physical world.

Why Mention Walking?

The verse prohibits walking مَرَحًا.

Here مَرَح concerns ostentation, exultation, and arrogance.

The issue is therefore more than walking quickly, stepping firmly, or crossing land.

Someone may walk quickly for work, exercise, or necessity without wishing to belittle others or exalt themselves.

The verse criticizes an arrogant state manifested in behavior.

In that setting, inability to split the earth or match the mountains’ height challenges self-exaltation.

تَخْرِقَ itself, however, does not mean arrogance.

That sense comes from مَرَحًا and the full construction.

Nor should the verse become an absolute scientific assertion that humans cannot dig or pierce the earth with any instrument.

It addresses someone displaying greatness and superiority through arrogant walking, rather than someone excavating through tools, knowledge, and engineering.

“You will not split the earth” must therefore be understood in its walking context.

Al-Ṭabarī: arrogance and traversing the earth in context

4. Al-Kahf 18:71: Piercing the Ship and Distinguishing Act from Intention

فَانْطَلَقَا حَتَّىٰ إِذَا رَكِبَا فِي السَّفِينَةِ خَرَقَهَا قَالَ أَخَرَقْتَهَا لِتُغْرِقَ أَهْلَهَا لَقَدْ جِئْتَ شَيْئًا إِمْرًا

fa-inṭalaqā ḥattā idhā rakibā fī al-safīnati kharaqahā qāla a-kharaqtahā li-tughriqa ahlahā la-qad jiʾta shayʾan imran

Proposed translation in this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“The two set out until, when they boarded the ship, he made a hole in it. He said: Have you made a hole in it to drown its passengers? You have certainly done something grave and objectionable.”

Al-Kahf 18:71

خ ر ق occurs twice here:

خَرَقَهَا and أَخَرَقْتَهَا.

Both belong to the unaugmented triliteral verb.

خَرَقَهَا is a declarative perfect verb describing piercing the ship.

In أَخَرَقْتَهَا, the initial hamzah is interrogative: Moses questions his companion about that act.

It does not turn the verb into Form IV.

In both forms, هَا refers to the ship.

Does Kharq Mean Destroying the Ship?

Arabic خَرْقُ السَّفِينَةِ concerns making a hole or opening in its hull.

The verb alone does not say that the ship necessarily sank or was completely destroyed.

Moses raises the risk of the passengers drowning:

لِتُغْرِقَ أَهْلَهَا

His question shows that he considers the act dangerous.

Three matters must nevertheless remain distinct: piercing the ship, the possibility or fear of drowning, and the actual purpose of the person piercing it.

Piercing is the event reported.

Drowning is the danger raised in Moses’ objection.

The true purpose is explained later in the story.

Confusing these three could wrongly suggest that Moses’ companion intended to kill the passengers.

The later explanation does not support that conclusion.

The Explanation in Al-Kahf 18:79

The narrative continues:

أَمَّا السَّفِينَةُ فَكَانَتْ لِمَسَاكِينَ يَعْمَلُونَ فِي الْبَحْرِ فَأَرَدْتُ أَنْ أَعِيبَهَا وَكَانَ وَرَاءَهُمْ مَلِكٌ يَأْخُذُ كُلَّ سَفِينَةٍ غَصْبًا

ammā al-safīnatu fa-kānat li-masākīna yaʿmalūna fī al-baḥri fa-aradtu an aʿībahā wa-kāna warāʾahum malikun yaʾkhudhu kulla safīnatin ghaṣban

Proposed translation in this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“As for the ship, it belonged to needy people working at sea. I wished to damage it; ahead of them was a king seizing every ship by force.”

Al-Kahf 18:79

This explains why the ship was pierced.

Moses’ companion identifies his purpose as أَنْ أَعِيبَهَا: to make it defective.

This distinguishes the act’s meaning from its purpose.

خَرْق is making the opening itself.

عَيْب is the defect resulting from it.

Here that defect formed part of a plan to prevent the ship’s seizure.

Early commentaries explain that the king took sound ships and left damaged ones.

That fits the narrative, but Al-Kahf 18:79 does not explicitly place “sound” beside كُلَّ سَفِينَةٍ. The qualification follows from the story’s cause and effect.

It is therefore better to leave the direct translation unqualified and explain the role of damaging the ship separately.

Al-Ṭabarī: damaging the ship and seizure of sound vessels

Is Kharq Always Bad?

This narrative shows that the verb alone cannot settle an act’s ultimate value.

A ship might be pierced without justification and with harmful intent.

Here, however, an independent explanation connects the act with preventing seizure.

This event should nevertheless not become a general license to damage others’ property under the pretext of a hidden benefit.

In Al-Kahf 18:82, Moses’ companion states:

وَمَا فَعَلْتُهُ عَنْ أَمْرِي

I did not do it on my own initiative.

This story therefore has special conditions; its figure’s behavior should not become a universal rule without regard to that qualification.

For root study, the narrower conclusion is clear: kharq means making an opening; deliberateness, purpose, risk, and ethical value are identified from context.

5. Al-Anʿām 6:100: Inventing a Claim about The God without Knowledge

وَجَعَلُوا لِلَّهِ شُرَكَاءَ الْجِنَّ وَخَلَقَهُمْ وَخَرَقُوا لَهُ بَنِينَ وَبَنَاتٍ بِغَيْرِ عِلْمٍ سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَىٰ عَمَّا يَصِفُونَ

wa-jaʿalū li-allāhi shurakāʾa al-jinna wa-khalaqahum wa-kharaqū lahu banīna wa-banātin bi-ghayri ʿilmin subḥānahu wa-taʿālā ʿammā yaṣifūna

Proposed translation in this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“They assigned the jinn as partners to The God, although He created them; and without any knowledge they fabricated sons and daughters for Him. Exalted and far above what they describe is He.”

Al-Anʿām 6:100

وَخَرَقُوا belongs to the same root used for piercing the ship and splitting the earth.

The object, however, differs.

Here the subject is inventing and attributing a claim about The God, rather than making an actual opening in an object.

Early lexicographers explain this through اِخْتَلَقُوا and اِفْتَرَوْا: inventing a statement and attributing it to The God.

بَنِينَ وَبَنَاتٍ: The Claim’s Object

In خَرَقُوا لَهُ بَنِينَ وَبَنَاتٍ, sons and daughters are the object of the claim attributed to The God.

This does not mean that the people actually created beings for Him.

They asserted the attribution of sons and daughters to Him.

This distinction matters in translation.

Rendering خَرَقُوا as “created” might suggest that people could actually create beings for The God.

“Fabricated” or “falsely attributed” clarifies the kind of speech involved.

The subject is constructing a claim, rather than physical creation.

بِغَيْرِ عِلْمٍ: A Claim without Grounds in Knowledge

The Qur’an adds:

بِغَيْرِ عِلْمٍ

Without knowledge.

This qualification is central.

The people concerned make an assertion about The God without knowledge to establish it.

The criticism therefore extends beyond a false result to their manner of asserting it.

Inventing an unsupported statement is connected here with attributing it to The God.

The claim’s falsehood must nevertheless be distinguished from each person’s awareness of that falsehood.

The verse itself says that this attribution was بِغَيْرِ عِلْمٍ.

We cannot therefore claim without evidence that all its speakers certainly knew the truth and consciously stated its opposite.

Some may have originated the assertion; others may have accepted it from their tradition and surroundings.

Knowledge and intention matter when assessing personal responsibility.

As a claim, however, the Qur’an identifies it as uninformed and rejects it.

Al-Ṭabarī: fabrication and attributing sons and daughters without knowledge

وَخَلَقَهُمْ and وَخَرَقُوا

Two similar forms appear together:

وَخَلَقَهُمْ, from خ ل ق.

وَخَرَقُوا, from خ ر ق.

The root letters خ ل ق and خ ر ق differ only in the middle radical. The complete forms وَخَلَقَهُمْ and وَخَرَقُوا, however, also differ in their pronouns and verbal endings.

Their meanings in this verse differ as well.

خَلْق concerns creation.

خَرْق here concerns fabrication and improper attribution.

The verse therefore emphasizes divine creation on one side and unsupported human attributions on the other.

This contrast helps explain its construction.

Visual similarity alone nevertheless does not establish that خ ر ق historically developed from خ ل ق.

Both have independent Arabic usages, and two shared letters do not prove their precise historical relationship.

The Readings خَرَقُوا and خَرَّقُوا

In the familiar reading of Ḥafṣ, وَخَرَقُوا has an ungeminated rāʾ.

Nāfiʿ and Abū Jaʿfar instead read وَخَرَّقُوا with a geminated rāʾ.

Both belong to the same root.

Early explanations associate the geminated form with intensification or numerous unsupported attributions.

This reading difference does not change the main contextual meaning.

Both concern attributing sons and daughters to The God without knowledge.

A base text following Ḥafṣ should therefore retain وَخَرَقُوا and discuss the other reading separately.

Itḥāf Fuḍalāʾ al-Bashar: geminated rāʾ in Nāfiʿ and Abū Jaʿfar

Relationship with the Next Verse

Al-Anʿām 6:101 states:

بَدِيعُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ أَنَّىٰ يَكُونُ لَهُ وَلَدٌ وَلَمْ تَكُنْ لَهُ صَاحِبَةٌ وَخَلَقَ كُلَّ شَيْءٍ وَهُوَ بِكُلِّ شَيْءٍ عَلِيمٌ

badīʿu al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍi annā yakūnu lahu waladun wa-lam takun lahu ṣāḥibatun wa-khalaqa kulla shayʾin wa-huwa bi-kulli shayʾin ʿalīmun

Proposed translation in this study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“The Originator of the heavens and earth. How could He have a child when He has had no consort? He created everything, and He knows everything.”

Al-Anʿām 6:101

This verse examines the earlier assertion in the light of divine creation and knowledge.

Thus خَرَقُوا in Al-Anʿām 6:100 concerns an improper attribution rather than the actual creation of beings.

Al-Anʿām 6:101 also challenges the claim of divine offspring through a critical question and a reminder of His creating.

خَرْق itself, however, does not mean having no consort, negating offspring, or creating. Those concepts come from the verses’ independent clauses.

6. Distinctions from Related Roots

خ ر ق (KH R Q, Kharq) and ش ق ق (SH Q Q, Shaqq): Piercing and Splitting

Arabic شَقّ concerns splitting and separating something.

Physical خَرْق likewise concerns a hole, tear, or opening.

They are not always identical, however.

شَقّ can describe a lengthwise split, while خَرْق can be a hole in fabric or a ship’s hull.

No absolute boundary separates them: both can describe a similar injury in some cases.

خَرَقَهَا in Al-Kahf 18:71 concerns an opening in the ship, but does not specify its exact length, width, or shape.

Study of SH Q Q

خ ر ق (KH R Q, Kharq) and ث ق ب (TH Q B, Thaqb): A Tear and a Hole

Arabic ثَقْب concerns a hole or opening.

In physical usages, خَرْق and ثَقْب can describe similar conditions.

خَرْق has a broader field, however, also including tearing, splitting, and crossing land.

ثَقْب generally focuses more on the hole itself or its creation.

“Making a hole” therefore suits خَرَقَهَا in Al-Kahf 18:71, but the full range of خ ر ق cannot be limited to ثَقْب.

Study of TH Q B

خ ر ق (KH R Q, Kharq) and ق ط ع (Q Ṭ ʿ, Qaṭʿ): Splitting and Cutting

Arabic قَطْع concerns cutting, separating, or ending a connection or extension.

خَرْق may likewise split a body or separate some parts.

Not every خَرْق, however, results in complete severance.

A ship with a hole in its hull may remain in one piece and be repairable.

Cutting through a rope, conversely, can separate its two ends.

This distinction matters in Al-Kahf 18:79: the purpose was to make the ship defective, rather than necessarily destroy it.

Study of Q Ṭ ʿ

خ ر ق (KH R Q, Kharq) and خ ل ق (KH L Q, Khalq): Splitting and Creation

These roots occur together in Al-Anʿām 6:100.

خَلْق concerns creating and bringing into being.

Physically, خَرْق concerns splitting and tearing; in speech, it can concern fabricating an unsupported statement.

Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī contrasts the two through considered action and its absence.

This does not establish that every Arabic خَلْق is ethically right and every خَرْق ethically wrong.

Their Qur’anic senses must be identified through subject, object, and context.

Study of KH L Q

خ ر ق (KH R Q, Kharq) and خ ر ص (KH R Ṣ, Kharṣ): Fabricating a Claim and Conjecture

خَرَقُوا in Al-Anʿām 6:100 concerns attributing sons and daughters to The God without knowledge.

تَخْرُصُونَ in Al-Anʿām 6:148 concerns people attributing their behavior and prohibitions to divine will without knowledge.

Both approach one critical field: speaking about The God without reliable grounds.

The roots are nevertheless different.

The main branch of خَرْص concerns estimation and conjecture.

The speech branch of خَرْق concerns inventing a statement and making an improper attribution.

A claim may be both unsupported conjecture and a fabricated attribution. That combination does not erase the lexical distinction.

Study of KH R Ṣ

خ ر ق (KH R Q, Kharq) and ف ر ي (F R Y, Iftirāʾ): Splitting and Fabrication

Qur’anic اِفْتِرَاء concerns inventing and attributing a false statement.

خَرَقُوا in Al-Anʿām 6:100 approaches this field.

اِفْتِرَاء has its own lexical branch, however, and cannot be equated with every physical meaning of خَرْق.

Piercing the ship in Al-Kahf 18:71, for example, is not اِفْتِرَاء: no invented false statement is under discussion.

The proximity concerns only the particular usage of speech and unsupported assertion.

خ ر ق (KH R Q, Khurq) and ر ف ق (R F Q, Rifq): Unconsidered and Considered Behavior

Early Arabic خُرْق concerns lack of skill and finesse, opposed to رِفْق.

This opposition helps explain certain human behaviors.

خَرْق itself nevertheless does not mean lack of judgment in every context.

In the ship narrative, a physical act occurs whose purpose is explained afterward.

The evaluative sense of unskillfulness should therefore not be imposed on خَرَقَهَا.

7. Translation Proposals for Forqan

Suitable physical equivalents include “split,” “pierce,” and, for land, “traverse” or “pass through.”

In the speech usage of Al-Anʿām 6:100, “fabricate” or “falsely attribute” better conveys the contextual meaning.

Translation should nevertheless avoid adding unstated details.

خَرَقَهَا in the ship story, for example, does not mean “drown.” Drowning is raised in Moses’ question.

Nor does خَرَقُوا لَهُ بَنِينَ وَبَنَاتٍ mean actually creating offspring for The God.

Brief Root Meaning and Meaning in Each Verse

وَخَرَقُوا — Al-Anʿām 6:100

Root: خ ر ق (KH R Q).

Grammar: Form I active perfect, third-person masculine plural, preceded by conjunction wāw.

Brief root meaning: Splitting, piercing, and making a passage; in speech, fabricating unsupported statements.

Meaning here: Without knowledge, they fabricated the attribution of sons and daughters to The God.

Proposed translation: “Without any knowledge, they fabricated sons and daughters for Him.”

تَخْرِقَ — Al-Isrāʾ 17:37

Root: خ ر ق (KH R Q).

Grammar: Active imperfect, second-person masculine singular, subjunctive after لَنْ.

Brief root meaning: Splitting, piercing, or passing through something.

Meaning here: Through arrogant walking, the addressee cannot split the earth or demonstrate power beyond human limits.

Proposed translation: “You will never split the earth.”

خَرَقَهَا — Al-Kahf 18:71

Root: خ ر ق (KH R Q).

Grammar: Active perfect, third-person masculine singular, with the object pronoun هَا referring to the ship.

Brief root meaning: Splitting and piercing something.

Meaning here: Moses’ companion made an opening or hole in the ship.

Proposed translation: “He made a hole in the ship.”

أَخَرَقْتَهَا — Al-Kahf 18:71

Root: خ ر ق (KH R Q).

Grammar: Active perfect, second-person masculine singular, with interrogative hamzah and object pronoun هَا.

Brief root meaning: Splitting and piercing something.

Meaning here: Moses asks whether his companion made a hole in the ship, with concern for the passengers drowning.

Proposed translation: “Have you made a hole in it?”

Its initial hamzah forms a question. This is therefore not Form IV, and its basic verbal meaning is unchanged from خَرَقَهَا.

8. Conclusion

Arabic خ ر ق concerns splitting, piercing, tearing, and passing through something.

Its early usages involve fabric, skin, deserts, wind, and traversing land.

Another branch places خُرْق opposite رِفْق for unskillful, unconsidered behavior. In speech, خَرْق also extends to inventing and attributing an unsupported claim.

The Qur’an uses this root four times in three verses.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:37 presents human inability to split the earth in a criticism of arrogance.

Al-Kahf 18:71 describes piercing the ship as a physical act that initially seems dangerous, with its purpose explained in Al-Kahf 18:79.

Al-Anʿām 6:100 contains the speech usage: inventing and attributing sons and daughters to The God without knowledge.

These occurrences show that خَرْق alone does not mean intentional destruction, lack of judgment, sin, or falsehood. Each may become connected with it in a particular setting, but context determines the connection.

Final definition: The root خ ر ق denotes splitting, piercing, tearing, and passing through something. Physically, something’s continuity or extent is breached or traversed; in speech, the word can also describe inventing a statement or unsupported attribution. Its physical usages appear in Al-Isrāʾ 17:37 and Al-Kahf 18:71, and its speech usage in Al-Anʿām 6:100. Intention, the act’s rightness or wrongness, and its consequences follow from each verse’s construction and context, rather than the root alone. Brief root meaning: Splitting, piercing, and passing through something; in speech, fabricating a statement or unsupported attribution.
