Driving and moving toward a destination; the leg and plant stem; markets and the family’s semantic connections in the Quran

1. Meaning in early Arabic

S W Q comprises three recognized Arabic branches: driving and setting in motion; the leg of a human or animal and the stem of a plant; and the market as a place of exchange. The Quran has 17 occurrences in 16 verses: seven verbs of driving, one sāʾiq, one masāq, six occurrences of sāq or its plural, and two of aswāq.

As a verb, sāqa yasūqu means driving a person or animal, or moving something in a particular direction. In the familiar setting of Arab life, someone driving a flock toward pasture, water, or a resting place performs sawq. This can involve moving it along, directing its route, or urging it forward. The verb does not invariably mean violence, compulsion, or pulling from behind.

In the nominal branch, sāq names a part of the human or animal leg involved in standing and movement. In humans it usually denotes the lower leg, between knee and ankle. In plants it also denotes the trunk or principal supporting stem.

Sūq, market, is also established Arabic. Early lexicographers linked the name with bringing goods and driving livestock to a place of exchange. This explanation is semantically intelligible but does not itself prove how the name historically originated.

Evidence attributed to pre-Quranic language

A poem attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays describes possessions and livestock:

لَنَا غَنَمٌ نَسُوقُهَا غِزَارٌ
كَأَنَّ قُرُونَ جِلَّتِهَا الْعِصِيُّ

lanā ghanamun nasūquhā ghizārun / ka-anna qurūna jillatihā al-ʿiṣiyyu

“We have abundant-milking sheep that we drive along; the horns of their large, mature ones are like staffs.”

Nasūquhā here denotes the specific act of driving the flock. The verbal branch is thus clearly associated in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period with moving livestock and managing their route. Some transmissions read nusawwiquhā, also expressing driving them along.

In the Muʿallaqah attributed to the same poet, a horse is described:

لَهُ أَيْطَلَا ظَبْيٍ وَسَاقَا نَعَامَةٍ
وَإِرْخَاءُ سِرْحَانٍ وَتَقْرِيبُ تَتْفُلِ

lahu ayṭalā ẓabyin wa-sāqā naʿāmatin / wa-irkhāʾu sirḥānin wa-taqrību tatfuli

The horse’s two flanks are compared with a gazelle’s, its two legs with an ostrich’s, and its gaits with those of a wolf and a young fox.

Sāqā has an anatomical meaning here. The poet describes a part of the animal’s body, not the action of driving.

Another witness is transmitted from Saʿd ibn Mālik, grandfather of Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd:

كَشَفَتْ لَهُمْ عَنْ سَاقِهَا
وَبَدَا مِنَ الشَّرِّ الصُّرَاحُ

kashafat lahum ʿan sāqihā / wa-badā mina al-sharri al-ṣurāḥu

“War bared its severity to them, and unhidden evil appeared.” Literally, the image is of war uncovering its leg.

The line concerns war, and “uncovering the leg” portrays the emergence of severe difficulty. War has no bodily leg: the expression transfers an image of bodily readiness for a demanding encounter to war itself.

These poems survive in later literary collections and commentaries and are attributed to pre-Islamic poets. They are valuable evidence for early Arabic’s semantic field, but attribution alone cannot establish each line’s exact date or the developmental history of every meaning.

The witnesses and recorded usages support driving, the anatomical leg, and the disclosure of severity in the expression “uncovering the leg” within the early linguistic tradition. The meaning “market” is also clearly attested in the Quran itself.

2. Forms and semantic centers

Important forms include:

  • Sāqa / yasūqu: drove or moved toward a destination.
  • Sawq: the act of driving.
  • Sāʾiq: one who drives another toward a destination.
  • Masāq: driving, being led onward, or the course and outcome of that movement, according to context.
  • Sāq: a human or animal leg, or a plant’s stem or trunk.
  • Sūq: a plural of sāq in some uses; also the identically written word for market.
  • Aswāq: the plural of market.

Arabic also has siyāq, insiyāq, tasāwuq, taswīq, and tasawwuq. These forms do not occur independently in the Quran and should be distinguished from the actual Quranic forms.

Do all branches have one core?

Some early lexicographers relate the entire family to urging onward and driving. On this account, a market is where goods are brought, while the leg is the limb upon which the body moves when walking.

This relationship helps explain semantic connections, but is not historical proof that all the words initially had just one meaning.

In usage, driving is an action; sāq names a bodily part or a plant’s support; and market names a social and economic place. All belong to one lexical family, but one definition should not replace another.

3. Sawq in the Quran: driving and conveying toward a destination

The Quranic verbs range from the movement of clouds and water to people’s movement in this life and the hereafter. Direction and destination matter throughout, while the manner of movement follows from context.

Driving clouds and water

Al-Aʿrāf 7:57 states:

حَتَّىٰ إِذَا أَقَلَّتْ سَحَابًا ثِقَالًا سُقْنَاهُ لِبَلَدٍ مَيِّتٍ فَأَنْزَلْنَا بِهِ الْمَاءَ

ḥattā idhā aqallat saḥāban thiqālan suqnāhu li-baladin mayyitin fa-anzalnā bihi al-māʾ

Al-Aʿrāf 7:57

“Until, when the winds have borne heavy clouds, We drive it toward a lifeless land and send down water by it.”

Suqnāhu is the past-tense form of sāqa. Its pronoun refers to the cloud, while li-baladin mayyitin states the destination.

Fāṭir 35:9 also states:

وَاللَّهُ الَّذِي أَرْسَلَ الرِّيَاحَ فَتُثِيرُ سَحَابًا فَسُقْنَاهُ إِلَىٰ بَلَدٍ مَيِّتٍ

wa-Allāhu alladhī arsala al-riyāḥa fa-tuthīru saḥāban fa-suqnāhu ilā baladin mayyit

Fāṭir 35:9

“And The God is the one who sent the winds, which stir up a cloud, and We drive it toward a lifeless land.”

In both verses, sawq expresses the cloud’s directed movement. Sending winds, stirring up clouds, driving them onward, and water descending are related parts of the event, but not all are contained in the lexical meaning of sāqa.

Al-Sajdah 32:27 provides another use:

أَوَلَمْ يَرَوْا أَنَّا نَسُوقُ الْمَاءَ إِلَى الْأَرْضِ الْجُرُزِ فَنُخْرِجُ بِهِ زَرْعًا

a-wa-lam yaraw annā nasūqu al-māʾa ilā al-arḍi al-juruzi fa-nukhriju bihi zarʿā

Al-Sajdah 32:27

“Have they not seen that We drive water toward barren land and thereby bring forth crops?”

Water itself is the object of nasūqu. The Quran describes its conveyance to barren land and identifies plant growth as the result.

The verb does not itself name a particular natural mechanism. It alone cannot specify the kind of rainfall, atmospheric circulation, or exact manner of water’s movement.

Driving people and the unwelcome prospect of death

Al-Anfāl 8:6 states:

يُجَادِلُونَكَ فِي الْحَقِّ بَعْدَ مَا تَبَيَّنَ كَأَنَّمَا يُسَاقُونَ إِلَى الْمَوْتِ وَهُمْ يَنْظُرُونَ

yujādilūnaka fī al-ḥaqqi baʿda mā tabayyana ka-annamā yusāqūna ilā al-mawti wa-hum yanẓurūn

Al-Anfāl 8:6

“They dispute with you about the truth after it has become clear, as though they were being driven toward death while looking on.”

Yusāqūna is a passive imperfect verb. The comparison portrays people experiencing movement toward what lies ahead as though they were being driven to death.

Fear, reluctance, and dread of death emerge from the whole expression, not from S W Q itself. This distinction becomes especially important in afterlife passages.

Driving the guilty and the God-conscious

Maryam 19:86 states:

وَنَسُوقُ الْمُجْرِمِينَ إِلَىٰ جَهَنَّمَ وِرْدًا

wa-nasūqu al-mujrimīna ilā jahannama wirdā

Maryam 19:86

“And We drive the guilty toward Hell, thirsty, like a group approaching a watering place.”

The preceding verse, Maryam 19:85, describes the God-conscious:

يَوْمَ نَحْشُرُ الْمُتَّقِينَ إِلَى الرَّحْمَٰنِ وَفْدًا

yawma naḥshuru al-muttaqīna ilā al-Raḥmāni wafdā

Maryam 19:85

“On the day We gather the God-conscious toward the All-Merciful as an honored delegation.”

The contrast between wafdan and wirdan is significant: the former concerns a guest-like delegation, the latter a thirsty company. This difference does not arise solely from sawq or ḥashr; the accompanying expressions and destinations also shape the portrayal.

Al-Zumar 39:71 states:

وَسِيقَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا إِلَىٰ جَهَنَّمَ زُمَرًا

wa-sīqa alladhīna kafarū ilā jahannama zumarā

Al-Zumar 39:71

“And those who disbelieved were driven toward Hell in groups.”

Yet Al-Zumar 39:73 uses the same verb:

وَسِيقَ الَّذِينَ اتَّقَوْا رَبَّهُمْ إِلَى الْجَنَّةِ زُمَرًا

wa-sīqa alladhīna ittaqaw rabbahum ilā al-jannati zumarā

Al-Zumar 39:73

“And those who were mindful of their Lord were led toward the Garden in groups.”

These verses are decisive for defining the root. Sīqa is identical in both, while the destination is Hell in one and the Garden in the other.

Sawq does not therefore inherently mean dragging into punishment, humiliating, penalizing, or treating violently. It denotes movement toward a destination; the ethical status and final outcome are supplied by other parts of the verse.

This evidence prevents generalizing negative meanings from certain occurrences to the entire family.

Sāʾiq and masāq

Qāf 50:21 states:

وَجَاءَتْ كُلُّ نَفْسٍ مَعَهَا سَائِقٌ وَشَهِيدٌ

wa-jāʾat kullu nafsin maʿahā sāʾiqun wa-shahīd

Qāf 50:21

“And every soul came, accompanied by one driving it onward and a witness.”

Sāʾiq is the active participle of sāqa: someone who drives or conveys another toward a destination. Shahīd expresses the role of witnessing.

The titles stand together but their functions should not be conflated. Their identities and manner of presence require examination through relevant Quranic evidence; sāʾiq alone does not determine the nature of the being performing that role.

Al-Qiyāmah 75:30 states:

إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ يَوْمَئِذٍ الْمَسَاقُ

ilā rabbika yawmaʾidhin al-masāq

Al-Qiyāmah 75:30

“Toward your Lord, on that day, is the driving onward.”

Al-masāq is a mīm-prefixed verbal noun of sāqa and denotes being driven or conveyed onward. Ilā rabbika specifies the ultimate direction in this context.

The phrase should not be used to infer a bodily location for God or a physical mechanism carrying people into His presence. It expresses humanity’s return and outcome, not their spatial architecture.

4. Bodily legs and plant stems in the Quran

Another branch names a limb or supporting part. Sāq in these uses should not be translated as the verb “drive.”

The legs of the queen of Sheba

Al-Naml 27:44 states:

فَلَمَّا رَأَتْهُ حَسِبَتْهُ لُجَّةً وَكَشَفَتْ عَنْ سَاقَيْهَا

fa-lammā raʾathu ḥasibathu lujjatan wa-kashafat ʿan sāqayhā

Al-Naml 27:44

“When she saw it, she thought it was deep water and uncovered her legs.”

Sāqayhā is the dual of sāq with a feminine possessive pronoun, referring to her two lower legs.

This is a clear bodily use. In the Quranic account, the woman mistakes the surface before her for water and bares her legs. The word alone provides no further details about their beauty, shape, or appearance beyond the action the narrative describes.

The legs of Solomon’s horses

Ṣād 38:33 states:

رُدُّوهَا عَلَيَّ فَطَفِقَ مَسْحًا بِالسُّوقِ وَالْأَعْنَاقِ

ruddūhā ʿalayya fa-ṭafiqa masḥan bi-al-sūqi wa-al-aʿnāq

Ṣād 38:33

“Bring them back to me; then he began passing his hands over the legs and necks.”

Al-sūq is the plural of sāq here, not markets. Al-aʿnāq and the context of horses in Ṣād 38:31 and Ṣād 38:32 make the anatomical sense clear.

Masḥan is a verbal noun, not a finite verb. Following ṭafiqa, it expresses the action he began. The juxtaposition of legs and necks does not itself prove that Solomon cut them; such an interpretation requires evidence from masḥ or another reliable contextual indication.

The body parts are clear in the text, while the precise manner of masḥ must be examined through that word and its context.

Plant stems

Al-Fatḥ 48:29 states:

كَزَرْعٍ أَخْرَجَ شَطْأَهُ فَآزَرَهُ فَاسْتَغْلَظَ فَاسْتَوَىٰ عَلَىٰ سُوقِهِ يُعْجِبُ الزُّرَّاعَ

ka-zarʿin akhraja shaṭʾahu fa-āzarahu fa-istaghlaẓa fa-istawā ʿalā sūqihi yuʿjibu al-zurrāʿ

Al-Fatḥ 48:29

“Like a crop that brings forth its shoots and strengthens them, then grows thick and stands firm upon its stems, delighting the growers.”

Sūqihi is the plural of sāq, referring to plant stems. Growth and thickening are portrayed alongside the plant standing firmly upon them.

The verse clearly attests the botanical use: the part upon which a plant stands and which supports its developed structure.

The Quran uses the image to compare the believing community’s growth and firmness. Social cooperation and growth in faith are not, however, the lexical meaning of sāq itself; they emerge from the whole comparison.

5. “Uncovering the leg”: from a bodily image to the disclosure of hardship

Al-Qalam 68:42 states:

يَوْمَ يُكْشَفُ عَنْ سَاقٍ وَيُدْعَوْنَ إِلَى السُّجُودِ فَلَا يَسْتَطِيعُونَ

yawma yukshafu ʿan sāqin wa-yudʿawna ilā al-sujūdi fa-lā yastaṭīʿūn

Al-Qalam 68:42

“On the day hardship is laid bare and they are called to prostrate, but cannot.” This rendering follows the idiomatic interpretation of “a leg is uncovered.”

Sāqin is indefinite and yukshafu is passive. The expression does not identify whose leg is uncovered, and no pronoun within it attributes the leg to God.

Early Arabic uses expressions such as war uncovering its leg or standing upon a leg to portray the emergence of severe difficulty. The earlier line attributed to Saʿd ibn Mālik exemplifies this use.

The image associates baring the leg with readiness for a serious, demanding task: lifting a garment and uncovering the lower leg could accompany preparing for movement, strenuous work, or confrontation. The image then serves to describe severe war and hardship.

Thus, reading Al-Qalam 68:42 as “the day great hardship is laid bare” has clear support in Arabic usage and the resurrection context. Establishing a bodily limb for God does not follow from the verse’s own construction.

Wa-iltaffati al-sāqu bi-al-sāq

Al-Qiyāmah 75:29 states:

وَالْتَفَّتِ السَّاقُ بِالسَّاقِ

wa-iltaffati al-sāqu bi-al-sāq

Al-Qiyāmah 75:29

“And leg entwined with leg.”

The phrase concerns a person dying. Al-Qiyāmah 75:26 mentions reaching the collarbones; Al-Qiyāmah 75:27 the search for someone to heal or help; and Al-Qiyāmah 75:28 the certainty of separation. Al-Qiyāmah 75:30 then describes being conveyed toward the Lord.

Two linguistic readings merit consideration.

In a bodily reading, the legs entwining or coming together portray the body at death. This fits the nearby bodily signs, although the verse need not prescribe an identical medical condition for everyone.

In an idiomatic reading, sāq relates to hardship, and the phrase can describe two great difficulties converging, such as life ending and confronting what follows.

The direct sense is leg entwining with leg. An idiomatic reading also has support in established Arabic usage. The death context allows both levels to be considered, without declaring additional bodily or otherworldly details certain.

The distinction from Al-Qalam 68:42 matters. There, yukshafu ʿan sāqin closely resembles a familiar idiom; in Al-Qiyāmah, two legs entwining also provides a direct, intelligible image. A shared root does not authorize translating both constructions identically.

6. Sūq: the market in Quranic language

Al-Furqān 25:7 states:

وَقَالُوا مَالِ هَٰذَا الرَّسُولِ يَأْكُلُ الطَّعَامَ وَيَمْشِي فِي الْأَسْوَاقِ

wa-qālū mā li-hādhā al-rasūli yaʾkulu al-ṭaʿāma wa-yamshī fī al-aswāq

Al-Furqān 25:7

“And they said: What sort of messenger is this, who eats food and walks in the markets?”

Al-aswāq is the plural of sūq and means markets.

In Arab society, a market was where goods were brought, exchanged, bought, and sold, and where people gathered. A market need not denote a permanent building: it can name a place of economic and social gathering.

Lexicons relate the name to bringing and driving goods there. Though intelligible, that explanation need not be historically proven to understand al-aswāq in the Quran; its usage is clear.

Al-Furqān 25:20 states:

وَمَا أَرْسَلْنَا قَبْلَكَ مِنَ الْمُرْسَلِينَ إِلَّا إِنَّهُمْ لَيَأْكُلُونَ الطَّعَامَ وَيَمْشُونَ فِي الْأَسْوَاقِ

wa-mā arsalnā qablaka mina al-mursalīna illā innahum la-yaʾkulūna al-ṭaʿāma wa-yamshūna fī al-aswāq

Al-Furqān 25:20

“We sent no messengers before you except that they ate food and walked in the markets.”

These verses use al-aswāq for the familiar setting of ordinary life and trade. Messengers’ presence in markets is part of their human lives, although opponents use it as a pretext for objection.

Sūq itself establishes neither the market’s sanctity nor condemnation of commerce. Those questions, when relevant, require examination through verses on trade, justice, and economic conduct.

7. Important distinctions and translation suggestions for Forqan

S W Q (sawq) and S Q Y (saqā)

Despite similarities of spelling and sound, these roots differ.

The verbal branch of S W Q denotes driving or moving toward a destination; S Q Y focuses on giving drink, watering, or supplying something to drink.

In Al-Sajdah 32:27, nasūqu al-māʾa means driving water toward the land. In wa-saqāhum rabbuhum sharāban ṭahūran in Al-Insān 76:21, saqāhum means giving them a drink.

The former concerns the water’s direction of movement; the latter, supplying drink to its recipients. Keeping these functions separate prevents confusion between driving water and giving drink.

S W Q (sawq) and Q W D (qawd)

Ordinary Arabic associates sawq more with driving onward and qawd with leading and guiding. Some lexical explanations distinguish driving from behind from leading in front.

This should not become an absolute rule about the driver’s or guide’s physical position. People and flocks may be moved in different ways; translation depends on the verb’s role and actual context.

S W Q (sawq) and Ḥ Sh R (ḥashr)

Sawq emphasizes moving toward a destination; ḥashr principally expresses gathering people or other beings.

The two verbs appear in related images in Maryam 19:85 and Maryam 19:86. In Al-Zumar 39:71 and Al-Zumar 39:73, sīqa describes groups moving toward their final destinations.

This is a tendency, not an absolute boundary. Gathering and driving can coexist within one event, so the verbs’ difference does not entail permanently separate referents.

Siyāq and the sequence of speech

Siyāq also belongs to this family and is used for the extension, continuity, and course of speech. “Context of discourse” means the relationships and sequence of statements that help an expression be understood.

This relates to speech following on and flowing, but the methodological term siyāq in Quranic study should not be equated with sawq as used in the verses.

Siyāq does not occur as an independent Quranic word, although examining the context of verses remains essential for understanding roots.

Translation suggestions

For suqnāhu, nasūqu, and sīqa, equivalents such as “We drove,” “We drive,” “were driven,” and, where appropriate, “were led toward their destination” are natural and close to the Arabic.

Sāʾiq can be “one who drives onward” or “a driver.” If “driver” in Qāf 50:21 suggests only a vehicle driver to modern readers, a brief explanation of the role is useful.

Al-masāq in Al-Qiyāmah 75:30 is close to “being driven,” “being conveyed toward a destination,” or, more freely, “being brought back.” Ilā rabbika completes the final direction.

Sāq in Al-Naml 27:44 means the lower leg; al-sūq in Ṣād 38:33 means legs; and sūqihi in Al-Fatḥ 48:29 means its stems.

Al-aswāq in Al-Furqān 25:7 and Al-Furqān 25:20 is simply markets.

In Al-Qalam 68:42, “hardship being laid bare” is defensible as an idiomatic reading based on the Arabic construction and context. In Al-Qiyāmah 75:29, retaining the direct image of leg entwining with leg, while explaining the possible idiom separately, is preferable.

Conclusion

S W Q has three clear Quranic branches: driving or moving toward a destination, bodily legs and plant stems, and markets.

The verbal branch centers on driving and directing movement. Its objects can be clouds, water, people, or groups. Destination and manner follow from context. The same sīqa for movement toward Hell and the Garden shows that punishment, violent compulsion, and humiliation are not invariable components of the root.

Sāq names a limb or supporting part. The Quran uses it for human lower legs, horses’ legs, and plant stems. “Uncovering the leg” is also an established Arabic image for the disclosure of severe difficulty, but this idiom should not be imposed without examination on every bodily use.

Sūq, market, is another established nominal branch, used in the Quran to describe messengers’ ordinary lives.

Final definition: In its verbal branch, S W Q denotes driving or moving toward a destination. Sāq names a particular part of a limb used in movement or a plant’s supporting structure. Sūq denotes a place where goods are gathered and trade occurs. The branches’ semantic relationship can be explained, but each form must be understood in its own construction and context; a shared root cannot replace independent examination of its uses.
