1. The root in early Arabic

The three-consonant family Q L M in Arabic is associated with cutting, shortening, and trimming something to put it in order or prepare it for use. Within this family, qalam is the name of a writing instrument.

Qalama al-ẓufra means “he trimmed the nail.” Qallama al-aẓfāra likewise denotes clipping and tidying the nails. Qalama al-ʿūda concerns cutting or trimming wood, while qulāmah names a clipping removed from a nail.

Classical lexical descriptions explain qalm through cutting or trimming to adjust an object’s shape. Unlike cutting in general, many familiar uses involve removing part of an extremity to shorten it, smooth it, or prepare it for a particular purpose.

For example, trimming a nail does not remove the entire nail; its excess is cut away. When a reed is prepared for writing, its end is trimmed into a usable nib.

This relationship helps explain the name of the writing instrument.

The pen in pre-Islamic Arabic life

Ancient writing pens were often made from reeds or suitable plant stems. The stem was cut and its end shaped into a writing point.

The prepared pen applied ink to leather or another writing surface.

In Arabic, qalam names the instrument itself, not the act of writing or the resulting written text.

This distinction matters. A pen is what a person writes with; writing itself is expressed by verbs such as kataba and saṭara.

A witness from early Arabic poetry

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Muraqqish al-Akbar includes the following:

الدَّارُ قَفْرٌ وَالرُّسُومُ كَمَا
رَقَّشَ فِي ظَهْرِ الْأَدِيمِ قَلَمْ

Al-dāru qafrun wa-l-rusūmu kamā / raqqasha fī ẓahri l-adīmi qalam.

“The dwelling is deserted, and its remaining traces are like the marks a pen has inscribed on the surface of leather.”

Here the poet compares the traces of an abandoned dwelling to lines inscribed on leather with a pen.

Al-adīm denotes leather, while raqqasha in this context concerns making marks or writing.

Qalam is the instrument that makes those lines.

This witness shows the pen as a recognized writing instrument in the tradition of early Arabic poetry. Writing on leather also provided a familiar image for describing surviving lines and traces.

Collections of early poetry attribute the verse to al-Muraqqish al-Akbar. The attribution is established in the literary tradition, but the poem reaches us through later transmissions and compiled manuscripts; the date of this particular verse cannot therefore be independently fixed with certainty.

With that qualification, it remains an important witness to qalam as a writing instrument in early Arabic.

Was the pen named after trimming?

Arabic lexicographers connected qalm, trimming, with qalam, the writing instrument.

On this explanation, the pen received its name because its tip is cut and trimmed.

The semantic connection is intelligible, but a separate question concerns the historical origin of the instrument’s name.

Ancient Greek had the word kalamos for a reed and a writing instrument. Related forms of the name also existed in other regional languages.

Linguistic research identifies Arabic qalam as a loanword related to this Greek name. Whether it entered Arabic directly or through an intermediary language is a separate question whose precise answer should not be treated as settled.

Two issues must therefore be distinguished:

First, Arabic qalm concerns cutting and trimming, and a pen was an instrument trimmed in preparation for use.

Second, exactly how the name of the instrument developed and passed between languages is a distinct historical issue.

Semantic resemblance to the verb for trimming does not by itself establish the historical derivation of the name.

Even if the instrument’s name originated outside Arabic, qalam was a recognized word in Arabic at the time of revelation and had entered Arabic morphological patterns.

2. Forms of the family and Qur’anic usage

Forms recorded in Arabic include the following:

Qalam: a writing pen and, in certain uses, a trimmed stick or shaft used for drawing lots.

Aqlām: the plural of qalam.

Qilām: another Arabic plural of qalam.

Qalama: he cut or trimmed.

Qallama: he shortened or trimmed, as in clipping nails.

Qalm: cutting or trimming.

Taqlīm: the act of shortening or trimming.

Qulāmah: a piece removed when clipping a nail or trimming.

Miqlām: a term recorded for a cutting implement, such as shears.

Miqlamah: in its familiar usage, a container or case for pens.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an.

The family Q L M occurs only four times in the Qur’an, always as a noun:

  • Aqlāmahum in Āl ʿImrān 3:44.
  • Aqlāmun in Luqmān 31:27.
  • Al-qalami in Al-Qalam 68:1.
  • Bi-l-qalami in Al-ʿAlaq 96:4.

No independent verb from this root, such as qalama or qallama, occurs in the Qur’an.

The two singular occurrences clearly concern the writing instrument. The plural in Luqmān 31:27 likewise occurs in an image of writing. In Āl ʿImrān 3:44, however, the pens are used to determine Mary’s guardianship.

This difference shows the instrument’s range of use: a trimmed stick or reed employed in drawing lots could also be called a qalam.

In analyzing this family, two functions of the instrument must remain distinct: writing and drawing lots.

Both may involve a pen or trimmed stick, but writing and drawing lots are different actions.

3. The pen, teaching, and recording knowledge

Al-ʿAlaq 96:4: teaching by the pen

الَّذى عَلَّمَ بِالقَلَمِ

Alladhī ʿallama bi-l-qalam.

“Who taught by the pen.”

Al-ʿAlaq 96:4

This is one of the clearest Qur’anic uses of qalam.

ʿAllama belongs to the root AYN L M (ʿ L M) and denotes teaching.

Bi-l-qalami identifies the instrument or means of that teaching.

“Teaching” should therefore not be treated as part of the lexical meaning of Q L M. The pen is an instrument used in the process of teaching.

The following verse states:

عَلَّمَ الإِنسٰنَ ما لَم يَعلَم

ʿAllama l-insāna mā lam yaʿlam.

“He taught the human being what he did not know.”

Al-ʿAlaq 96:5

These two verses make the relationship between the pen, knowledge, and human learning clear.

A person can hear speech and remember it, but individual memory is limited. Writing allows information to outlast one person’s memory and become available to others.

For example, someone may write down the results of years of observation, calculation, or investigation. Another person can read that record and benefit from knowledge whose production they did not witness directly.

In the same way, one generation can record its knowledge and experience for later generations.

This does not mean that knowledge is acquired only through writing. The Qur’an also speaks of seeing, hearing, thinking, and other ways of knowing.

Al-ʿAlaq 96:4 highlights the pen as an instrument of teaching.

The pen records knowledge in readable signs, enabling what has been learned to pass from one person to another.

Is the meaning of “pen” restricted to a particular material or shape?

In earlier periods, reed and wooden pens were familiar writing instruments.

The Arabic meaning of qalam is not confined to an instrument of a single fixed shape and size.

In present-day usage, a pen may likewise be made of reed, wood, metal, or other materials.

What its writing uses share is the instrument’s role in making lines and recording text.

Bi-l-qalami therefore does not imply that every kind of teaching and recording knowledge must use reeds and ink.

Conversely, the existence of newer tools does not justify removing the concrete meaning of qalam from Qur’anic Arabic and translating it simply as “knowledge” or “information.”

The pen is an instrument; knowledge is something that can be learned and recorded with its help.

Al-Qalam 68:1: the pen and what is written

ن وَالقَلَمِ وَما يَسطُرونَ

Nūn. Wa-l-qalami wa-mā yasṭurūn.

“Nūn. By the pen and what they write.”

Al-Qalam 68:1

Here the pen accompanies a verb of writing.

Wa-l-qalami introduces an oath by the pen.

Yasṭurūna belongs to S Ṭ R and means “they write” or “they record.”

Mā in this construction can refer to what is written. It can also be analyzed as a nominalizing particle, making the oath concern their act of writing.

“What they write” gives a natural English rendering and makes the relationship between pen and written text clear.

The phrase does not explicitly name those who perform yasṭurūna.

The verb is masculine plural, but that form alone does not determine whether the subjects are human writers, angels, or another group.

Al-qalami can also be understood as the familiar name of the writing instrument. Some interpretations propose a particular pen, but the verse itself identifies no specific pen by name, material, or location.

The physical identity of a particular unseen pen and the contents of its record require evidence beyond the lexical meaning of qalam.

The verse speaks of the pen and writing. Determining the instrument’s specific nature or everything written with it requires independent evidence.

Nūn is one of the isolated opening letters. The letter alone does not establish a definite lexical meaning such as “inkwell.”

In the surah’s sequence, the next verse answers an accusation made against Muhammad. His reward and character are then discussed.

The opening oath thus belongs to a setting concerned with the message’s credibility and judgments about its bearer.

This connection does not make “proof,” “truthfulness,” or “prophethood” the lexical meaning of qalam.

The pen remains a writing instrument; its place in the oath and its relation to the message emerge from syntax and context.

For yasṭurūna, see the related Forqan study of the root S Ṭ R.

4. Āl ʿImrān 3:44: pens used to determine Mary’s guardianship

ذٰلِكَ مِن أَنباءِ الغَيبِ نوحيهِ إِلَيكَ وَما كُنتَ لَدَيهِم إِذ يُلقونَ أَقلٰمَهُم أَيُّهُم يَكفُلُ مَريَمَ وَما كُنتَ لَدَيهِم إِذ يَختَصِمونَ

Dhālika min anbāʾi l-ghaybi nūḥīhi ilayka wa-mā kunta ladayhim idh yulqūna aqlāmahum ayyuhum yakfulu Maryama wa-mā kunta ladayhim idh yakhtaṣimūn.

“That is among the reports of the unseen which We reveal to you. You were not with them when they cast their pens as to which of them would care for Mary, nor were you with them when they disputed.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:44

Aqlāmahum is the plural of qalam with the plural pronoun hum: “their pens.”

The verse’s subject, however, is not the writing of a text.

A group disputed Mary’s guardianship and cast their pens to determine who would undertake her care.

Yulqūna expresses the act of casting.

Aqlāmahum names the instruments being cast.

The question of which person would care for Mary identifies the matter to be decided.

Taken together, these elements indicate that the pens were used in a procedure of drawing lots or making a selection.

Were these ordinary writing pens?

Classical Arabic records qalam not only for a writing instrument but also for a trimmed stick or shaft used in drawing lots.

One explanation understands aqlāmahum here as shafts or sticks used as lots.

Another understands them as the participants’ writing pens, employed during the dispute to determine Mary’s guardian.

Both readings can be examined in terms of language and the use of the instrument.

The verse itself does not specify the exact shape of these pens.

It does not state their wood or reed type, what marks they carried, or how one was selected.

Water, a river, and whether the pens floated or sank are not mentioned in verse 44; the verse does not establish those procedural details.

Such details should therefore not be inserted into the meaning of qalam or the basic verse translation.

In translation, “they cast their pens” preserves the wording, while the drawing of lots can be explained through contextual analysis.

Mary’s guardianship and the role of the pens

In Āl ʿImrān 3:37, The God describes Mary’s gracious acceptance and upbringing and her being entrusted to Zechariah’s care.

Āl ʿImrān 3:44 refers to a group’s dispute concerning that responsibility.

This relationship shows the pens being used to determine the guardian.

The word aqlām itself does not mean guardianship or care.

Kafala belongs to K F L and concerns assuming responsibility for someone’s care and support.

The pens are the instruments of selection in this scene; guardianship is the responsibility being assigned.

Describing a drawing of lots in Mary’s story does not by itself command that every child’s guardianship or every human dispute be settled by lot.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:141 also mentions taking part in drawing lots, but that verse uses S H M, not Q L M.

The procedure must therefore be understood from the action and context, rather than making “lot” the meaning of qalam in all its uses.

The verse also tells Muhammad that he was not present at the event and received this account through revelation.

This concerns the source of Muhammad’s knowledge of that event, not his ability or inability to write with a pen.

5. Luqmān 31:27: if all the trees became pens

وَلَو أَنَّما فِى الأَرضِ مِن شَجَرَةٍ أَقلٰمٌ وَالبَحرُ يَمُدُّهُ مِن بَعدِهِ سَبعَةُ أَبحُرٍ ما نَفِدَت كَلِمٰتُ اللَّهِ إِنَّ اللَّهَ عَزيزٌ حَكيمٌ

Wa-law anna-mā fī l-arḍi min shajaratin aqlāmun wa-l-baḥru yamudduhu min baʿdihi sabʿatu abḥurin mā nafidat kalimātu llāhi inna llāha ʿazīzun ḥakīm.

“And if all the trees on earth were pens, and the sea were replenished by seven more seas after it, the words of The God would not be exhausted. Indeed, The God is Almighty, Wise.”

Luqmān 31:27

Aqlāmun here is the plural of qalam.

This time, however, the pens belong to a hypothetical image.

All the trees on earth are imagined as pens, and the sea as supplemented by seven further seas.

The image concerns writing the words of The God.

Why are trees imagined as pens?

Ancient pens were commonly made from reeds and wood. Turning trees into pens would therefore present an intelligible image to an Arabic-speaking audience.

Writing requires both an instrument and material.

The pen is the writing instrument, and ink is the material through which marks are left on a surface.

The verse imagines an immense number of pens, with the sea supplying the image of abundant writing material.

However extensive these resources may be, they remain limited.

In contrast, the Qur’an states that the words of The God are not exhausted.

The image thus contrasts the limited resources of human writing with the breadth of divine words.

Are “the words of The God” only the wording of a book?

Kalimātu Allāh belongs to K L M, not Q L M.

The pen is the writing instrument; the words are what is being written in the image.

In the Qur’an, divine words can concern speech, command, promise, judgment, and other senses determined by their contexts.

The presence of pens alone therefore does not restrict “the words of The God” here to the number of words in a written book.

Al-Kahf 18:109 also presents the sea as writing material and the Lord’s words as inexhaustible.

Together, these verses show that the breadth of divine words is not bounded by human resources for counting and recording.

The image does not establish the physical nature of unseen pens or the existence of a limitless material ledger.

The verse uses a familiar writing instrument to convey an expansive truth.

Seven seas in the image

Luqmān 31:27 uses sabʿah: seven.

In its direct construction, the numeral gives a definite quantity and should not be turned into an entirely unspecified number without contextual grounds.

Even with seven further seas added to the first, the words of The God would not be exhausted.

The seven added seas therefore enlarge the hypothetical resources for writing.

The purpose is not to calculate the number of pens, the volume of seawater, or an exact total of divine words.

Pens and seas serve the illustration, whose conclusion is the inexhaustibility of the words of The God.

For further discussion, see Forqan’s study of the root K L M.

6. Q L M and related roots

Q L M (qalam) and K T B (kataba): instrument and act of writing

Qalam names the writing instrument.

Kataba concerns writing and recording; in broader Qur’anic usage, it can also concern prescribing and recording a ruling.

The two can participate in one activity without being lexically identical.

A person may write something with a pen: the pen is the instrument, and writing is the action.

Al-Baqarah 2:282 discusses the written recording of financial obligations and the scribe’s responsibilities.

There the kātib is responsible for writing, and recording obligations, justice, and preventing disputes are important concerns.

Those legal duties cannot all be extracted from the root of qalam itself.

Nor is every use of K T B confined to writing with a physical pen. The Qur’an also uses this family for recording and prescribing a ruling.

See the fuller discussion in Forqan’s study of the root K T B.

Q L M (qalam) and S Ṭ R (saṭara): the pen and writing in lines

S Ṭ R in Arabic concerns arranging things in rows and writing ordered lines.

In Al-Qalam 68:1, al-qalami and yasṭurūna occur together.

Their conjunction clarifies the distinction between the roots.

The pen is the writing instrument, while saṭara expresses writing and recording signs along an ordered line.

Qalam should therefore not be equated with the written text itself, nor yasṭurūna with merely holding a pen.

Writing may use a pen, but the instrument and the action remain distinct concepts.

Q L M (qalam) and Q R A (qaraʾa): writing and reading

Q R A in Qur’anic usage concerns reading, recitation, and presenting spoken words.

Qalam denotes the writing instrument.

They can be connected within the transmission of knowledge: one person writes words and another reads them.

Al-ʿAlaq 96:1 and Al-ʿAlaq 96:3 contain the command iqraʾ, while Al-ʿAlaq 96:4 mentions teaching by the pen.

Their proximity brings reading, teaching, and writing into one sequence.

The verb qaraʾa alone, however, does not establish that every recitation must be read from a written text; likewise, qalam does not mean recitation itself.

This distinction is examined separately in the study of the root Q R A.

Q L M (qalam) and AYN L M (ʿalima): instrument and knowledge

AYN L M concerns knowing, understanding, and teaching.

The pen is one instrument through which people can record and transmit knowledge.

Al-ʿAlaq 96:4 clearly illustrates this connection.

Possessing a pen does not mean possessing knowledge.

Someone may have many writing tools without properly understanding what they write.

Conversely, a person may acquire knowledge through observation, listening, and experience without using a pen at that moment.

Pen and knowledge are related, but neither should be made the lexical meaning of the other.

Q L M (qalm) and Q Ṭ AYN (qaṭʿ): trimming and cutting

Q Ṭ AYN in Arabic concerns cutting and separating.

Qalm likewise denotes a kind of cutting or trimming in its recorded uses, especially when an extremity is shortened or prepared for use.

Clipping nails and trimming the end of a reed are examples.

Qaṭʿ has a broader semantic range and can also concern cutting off a route, separating a group, or ending a relationship.

Not every act of cutting can therefore be called qalm.

In Qur’anic usage, qalam is a noun naming an instrument; none of the family’s verbs of trimming occurs.

7. Translation guidance for Forqan

The straightforward English equivalent of the noun qalam is “pen.”

This preserves its concrete sense as a writing instrument without replacing it with an abstract concept.

Where the historical material matters, “reed pen” or “a pen cut from reed” may be appropriate.

For the plural aqlām, “pens” is suitable.

In the drawing of lots, “lot-sticks” or “pens used as lots” can clarify the contextual function, preferably in the accompanying explanation.

Equivalents for the four Qur’anic occurrences

Āl ʿImrān 3:44 — Aqlāmahum

“Their pens.”

In context, these pens are cast to determine Mary’s guardian. The explanation can identify them as pens or sticks used in drawing lots.

Luqmān 31:27 — Aqlāmun

“Pens.”

Here the trees on earth hypothetically become pens for writing.

Al-Qalam 68:1 — Wa-l-qalami

“By the pen.”

This preserves both the instrument’s name and its role in the oath.

Al-ʿAlaq 96:4 — Bi-l-qalami

“By the pen.”

Here the preposition bi- expresses the instrument’s relationship to teaching, rather than an oath.

The basic translation should not replace qalam with “knowledge,” “revelation,” “book,” “lot,” or “decree,” since these are distinct concepts.

For the non-Qur’anic lexical forms:

Qalama al-ẓufra: “he clipped or trimmed the nail.”

Qallama al-shajara: “he pruned the tree’s branches.”

Qulāmah: “a nail clipping” or “a cut-off piece.”

Taqlīm: “shortening,” “trimming,” or “pruning.”

These equivalents explain the linguistic family and should not replace the Qur’anic sense of qalam in its four verses.

Conclusion

The family Q L M in Arabic is associated with two related areas: cutting and trimming, and the pen as a writing instrument.

Earlier pens were often prepared from reeds or wood, with their ends trimmed for writing. Arabic lexicographers accordingly connected the name of the pen with the verb for trimming.

The instrument’s name also has a historical connection with a Greek word for reed and pen. The semantic relationship between trimming and the instrument is clear, but a direct historical derivation from the Arabic verb should not be asserted without decisive evidence.

The family occurs only four times in the Qur’an, always in nominal forms.

In Al-ʿAlaq 96:4, the pen is an instrument of teaching.

In Al-Qalam 68:1, the pen accompanies writing and recording words.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:44, pens are cast to determine Mary’s guardian. The use concerns drawing lots, but the verse does not specify the shape of the pens or the procedure’s details.

In Luqmān 31:27, all the trees on earth hypothetically become pens, illustrating the inexhaustible breadth of the words of The God in contrast with limited human writing resources.

The pen is related to writing, inscription, recitation, and knowledge, but none is its synonym. The pen is the instrument; writing records, reading recites, and knowledge can be preserved and transmitted with the pen’s help.

Final definition for Forqan:

Q L M: in the Arabic verbal branch, cutting, shortening, or trimming part of something, especially to put it in order or prepare it for use; in the nominal branch, qalam is a writing instrument, commonly made from trimmed reed or wood. In some uses it also names a trimmed stick or shaft used for drawing lots.

The central Qur’anic sense of qalam is the writing instrument; Āl ʿImrān 3:44 shows its use in choosing a guardian by lot.

The instrument’s kind, function, and the result of its use must be understood from the sentence and each verse’s context, rather than putting writing, teaching, drawing lots, and decree into the word qalam itself.
