Qur’anic Root

The Qur’anic Root AYN DH B (ʿ DH B)

Sweetness, tasting the quality of a state, and the bitter consequence of denial and corruption

Qur’anic forms of this root

The root ع ذ ب (AYN DH B / ʿ DH B) is one of the most sensitive roots in the Qur’an. Through common translations, it is often immediately associated with suffering, pain, torture, or harsh punishment. But the Qur’an itself also uses this root for ʿadhb water: sweet, pleasant, drinkable, pure, and life-giving water.

The central meaning of the root

The root ع ذ ب (AYN DH B / ʿ DH B) is one of the most sensitive roots in the Qur’an, because in the mind of many readers, especially through common translations, it is identified with suffering, pain, torture, and severe punishment. This misunderstanding is not only Persian. In other languages as well, when ʿadhāb is always translated with words equivalent to torture, suffering, or punishment, the reader may assume that this is the root’s original meaning.

But the internal structure of the Qur’an invites careful re-reading. This same root is used in the Qur’an for ʿadhb water: water that is sweet, fresh, pleasant to drink, and life-giving. So AYN DH B is not, within the Qur’an itself, inherently equal to pain or torture. One branch points to sweetness and purity, while the dominant branch points to ʿadhāb, bitter consequence, pressure, deprivation, and the human being facing the result of his own action.

This point is very important for the Forqan method. Human languages constantly change. Words gain new burdens, become narrower or wider, or drift away from older root-senses in daily use. But Qur’anic roots should not be imprisoned by today’s linguistic habits. They must be understood from within the Qur’an itself, from the network of verses, and from the relation between derivatives.

The central meaning of this root can be understood this way: AYN DH B means experiencing the quality of a result: either the sweetness of blessing or the bitterness of consequence.

ʿAdhb: sweetness and life-giving quality

عَذْب (ʿadhb) appears in the Qur’an for pleasant water. This word shows that the root AYN DH B, in its Qur’anic foundation, is not only pain and suffering. ʿAdhb water is water whose drinking is pleasant, pure, sweet, and life-giving. The human being tastes it and feels its quality in body and soul.

In Al-Furqān 25:53, it says:

هَٰذَا عَذْبٌ فُرَاتٌ وَهَٰذَا مِلْحٌ أُجَاجٌ

This one is sweet and fresh, and that one is salty and bitter.

In Fāṭir 35:12, the same contrast appears:

هَٰذَا عَذْبٌ فُرَاتٌ سَائِغٌ شَرَابُهُ وَهَٰذَا مِلْحٌ أُجَاجٌ

This one is sweet and fresh, pleasant to drink, and that one is salty and bitter.

This branch is crucial for understanding the whole root. If we leave ʿadhb aside and see only ʿadhāb, we understand the root incompletely. By mentioning ʿadhbun furāt, the Qur’an shows that this root is tied to the quality of experience and tasting a state: sometimes a pleasant quality, and sometimes a bitter and heavy one.

ʿAdhāb: the bitter experience of consequence

عَذَاب (ʿadhāb) is the most frequent use of this root in the Qur’an. But ʿadhāb should not be immediately translated as torture. In many verses, ʿadhāb means the painful experience of consequence: a condition in which the human being faces the result of denial, injustice, corruption, arrogance, or rejection.

ʿAdhāb in the Qur’an does not come without prior proof. Before it come message, warning, sign, respite, invitation, and the possibility of return. The Qur’an states this principle clearly in Al-Isrāʾ 17:15:

وَمَا كُنَّا مُعَذِّبِينَ حَتَّىٰ نَبْعَثَ رَسُولًا

And We were not to bring punishment until We had sent a messenger.

This verse is foundational for understanding ʿadhāb. Qur’anic ʿadhāb is not an initial, crude, or reasonless reaction. It appears after communication, warning, the path becoming clear, and excuses being closed. Therefore, ʿadhāb cannot be understood as baseless violence or emotional revenge. In the Qur’anic order, it is tied to justice, responsibility, established proof, and the result of action becoming manifest.

This does not mean that ʿadhāb is merely a natural consequence unrelated to God. The Qur’an speaks of ʿadhāb in relation to God’s judgment, knowledge, power, will, and justice. So two reductions must be avoided: do not reduce ʿadhāb to blind torture, and do not reduce it to a natural process without reckoning or judgment.

ʿAdhdhaba / yuʿadhdhibu: bringing painful consequence into reality

عَذَّبَ / يُعَذِّبُ (ʿadhdhaba / yuʿadhdhibu) in Form II means to punish, bring someone into ʿadhāb, or make one face a painful consequence. This branch shows that ʿadhāb is not merely a mental state. It may become an outward, historical, social, bodily, or Hereafter reality.

Translation requires care here. If we always say “tortured,” we narrow the Qur’anic semantic structure and damage the transcendent understanding of divine justice. In many verses, renderings such as “brought into punishment,” “made them face painful consequence,” “recompensed,” or “brought the bitter result upon them” may be more precise, depending on context.

Muʿadhdhib and muʿadhdhabīn: transcendent care in translation

مُعَذِّب (muʿadhdhib) means one who brings punishment or one who causes ʿadhāb, depending on the structure of the verse. When this relation is used about God, it must be understood with transcendent care. The divine Lordship is free from human resentment, revenge-seeking, blind anger, or desire to harm. ʿAdhāb in relation to God belongs within justice, knowledge, wisdom, warning, and reckoning.

Here too, translation can mislead. If muʿadhdhib is carelessly translated as “torturer,” a human, harsh, and limited meaning is imposed upon God. In the Forqan method, divine ʿadhāb must be distinguished from human violence. Divine ʿadhāb in the Qur’an emerges from the order of truth, responsibility, judgment, and established proof, not from injustice, resentment, or desire to harm.

مُعَذَّبِين (muʿadhdhabīn) means those who have entered a state of ʿadhāb: those upon whom punishment has fallen or who are caught in painful consequence. This should not always be rendered as “the tortured.” Sometimes “those punished” is enough, but often “those caught in painful consequence” or “those upon whom ʿadhāb has fallen” gives a more precise sense.

Dhawq: fully tasting the consequence

One key to understanding ʿadhāb in the Qur’an is the language of tasting punishment. Tasting means direct and immediate experience. The human being no longer merely hears about the result; he tastes it. What he denied in the world becomes an experienced reality in the state of ʿadhāb.

This expression agrees with the axis of the root. ʿAdhb is a pleasant quality that is tasted, and ʿadhāb is a bitter quality that is tasted. In both cases, the matter is not merely information; it is experience. The difference lies in moral direction and existential result.

So ʿadhāb is not simply “inflicting pain.” ʿAdhāb means that the human being enters the quality of consequence. The truth he took lightly in the world now becomes a bitter, heavy, inescapable experience present within him. Just as fresh ʿadhb water reveals the quality of life to the thirsty mouth, ʿadhāb reveals the bitter quality of the human record without a veil.

Worldly ʿadhāb and Hereafter ʿadhāb

The Qur’an speaks of worldly ʿadhāb and Hereafter ʿadhāb. Worldly ʿadhāb may appear as collapse, fear, hunger, disgrace, defeat, anxiety, loss of security, or the social and historical consequence of injustice. This kind of ʿadhāb does not always mean fire or final punishment; sometimes corruption itself drags society and the human being into a bitter state.

But the ʿadhāb of the Hereafter is more final and deeper. In the Hereafter, the veils are removed and the human being faces the truth of his orientation. There, ʿadhāb is not only an outward consequence; it is the full manifestation of the human being’s relation to truth, action, and responsibility.

In the Forqan method, these two should be distinguished, but not separated absolutely. Worldly ʿadhāb may be warning, awakening, or a sample of consequence. Hereafter ʿadhāb is the final and fuller result.

ʿAdhāb and mercy

The Qur’an does not explain ʿadhāb apart from mercy. Warning about ʿadhāb can itself be mercy, because it awakens the human being before he reaches the final result. If God did not warn the human being, did not show consequence, and did not leave open the path of return, then the human being would continue unknowingly toward ruin.

So the mention of ʿadhāb in the Qur’an is not merely raw frightening. Warning about ʿadhāb is part of the moral training of the human being. The Qur’an brings the human being face to face with consequence so that before tasting the final bitterness, he may correct his path.

This view cleanses the image of God from baseless violence. In the Qur’an, God is both Raḥmān and Just. His mercy shows the way, warns, gives respite, and leaves the possibility of return. His justice does not allow injustice, rejection, corruption, and ruin to remain without reckoning.

Translation misunderstanding

One major problem in understanding this root is uniform and habit-driven translation. When ʿadhāb is always translated with words that create only torture, harm, or bodily pain in the reader’s mind, the Qur’anic meaning of the root becomes closed. This problem can occur in Persian, English, and many other languages.

Human languages are alive and words change. But the Qur’an should not be limited by later everyday burdens of words. We must see how the Qur’an itself uses a root, what words it places beside it, in what contexts it repeats it, and what range of meaning it keeps alive.

In the root AYN DH B, the presence of ʿadhb alongside ʿadhāb warns us not to derive the root’s meaning from only one famous and heavy use. The whole Qur’anic network must be seen.

Methodological note

In translating this root, one fixed word should not always be used. “Punishment” or “ʿadhāb” may be appropriate in many verses, but the explanation must make clear that the meaning is not simply torture.

In some verses, “recompense” is suitable. In others, “painful consequence” is clearer. In others, “the suffering of result” or “the bitter experience of consequence” may help. For ʿadhb, words such as sweet, fresh, pleasant to drink, and life-giving should be used.

Most importantly, divine ʿadhāb must not be collapsed into human violence. A human being may torture out of resentment, weakness, anger, or desire to harm. But ʿadhāb in relation to God in the Qur’an is understood within truth, proof, responsibility, justice, and judgment.

Suggested rendering in Forqan

This root should be rendered according to context.

  • ʿadhb: sweet, fresh, pleasant to drink, pure, life-giving.
  • ʿadhāb: punishment, painful consequence, bitter experience of consequence, recompense, suffering of result, bitter state made manifest.
  • ʿadhdhaba / yuʿadhdhibu: punished, brought into punishment, made one face painful consequence, recompensed, brought the bitter result upon them; according to context.
  • muʿadhdhib: one who brings punishment, one who brings consequence into reality; for God, with transcendent explanation within justice and established proof.
  • muʿadhdhabīn: those punished, those caught in painful consequence, those upon whom ʿadhāb has fallen.

Summary

ع ذ ب (AYN DH B / ʿ DH B) in the Qur’an is not only the root of suffering and torture. It shows the quality of a state being tasted. Sometimes that quality is sweet and life-giving, as with ʿadhb water; and sometimes it is bitter, heavy, and painful, as with the ʿadhāb of those who, after warning and the path becoming clear, insist on injustice, corruption, rejection, or denial.

In the Forqan method, ʿadhāb must be protected from two misunderstandings: it must not be reduced to blind torture and baseless violence, and it must not be separated from God’s justice and judgment. Qur’anic ʿadhāb is the bitter experience of consequence within the order of truth: a consequence about which the human being had been warned, had heard signs, had a path of return, yet moved toward it by his own choice.

This root reminds the human being that both blessing and consequence are tasted. Fresh water is tasted and gives life to the soul; ʿadhāb is also tasted and reveals the truth of the human path. The central question is: through his own choice, which quality is the human being preparing himself to taste?
