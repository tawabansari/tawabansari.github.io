From diminution and lost fidelity to betrayal of trusts, covenants, human relationships, and concealed conduct

The Arabic root KH W N concerns diminution and taking away. In ethical and social use, this becomes betrayal when someone fails to maintain the fidelity owed to a trust, deposit, covenant, or responsibility, thereby violating a right that should have been respected.

خيانة is not limited to taking property or revealing secrets. People can betray a trust through its safekeeping, fulfilling a covenant, carrying out responsibilities, speaking, judging, or treating someone close to them. These actions share a failure in the right or fidelity expected within a relationship and commitment.

Not every Arabic use of the root carries moral judgment. Early Arabic speaks of a sword “betraying” when it fails to perform as expected, or of time “betraying” when its passage wears something down. Such uses concern diminution and disappointed expectations, rather than a sword or time consciously deciding to deceive.

This distinction matters for understanding the Qur’an. When betrayal is attributed to people, more is involved than a reduced quantity or a defective tool: their relationship with responsibility, awareness, trust, and others’ rights is at issue.

The root occurs sixteen times in eleven Qur’anic verses. Its uses range from betrayal of God and the Messenger, people’s trusts, and social covenants to self-betrayal, concealed conduct of the eyes, and betrayal within family relationships.

These verses distinguish betrayal from unwitting error, involuntary inability, disagreement, and mere accusation. Betrayal is nevertheless not restricted to one action and can arise in very different circumstances.

1. Forms and the Semantic Core

KH W N is a triliteral hollow root with wāw as its second radical. In certain inflected forms, wāw becomes alif.

Its basic verb is خَانَ ـ يَخُونُ.

Its principal recorded Arabic forms include:

  • خَانَ ـ يَخُونُ: To fall short in a right, trust, or fidelity; in human conduct, to betray.
  • خَوْن: Diminution, taking away, and betrayal.
  • خِيانَة: Betrayal; failing to honor trust or commitment.
  • خَانَة and مَخَانَة: Verbal nouns meaning betrayal.
  • خَائِن: Someone who betrays.
  • خَائِنَة: The feminine of خائن; in some constructions, a name for betrayal or a treacherous act.
  • خَوَّان: Someone markedly or repeatedly treacherous.
  • خَؤُون: An intensive description of a treacherous person.
  • خَوَنَة: A plural for betrayers.
  • اِخْتَانَ ـ يَخْتَانُ: To betray; in some uses, to seek or attempt betrayal.
  • اِخْتِيَان: The verbal noun of اختان, meaning betrayal or seeking it.
  • تَخَوَّنَ: To diminish something gradually; in another use, to seek someone’s betrayals or lapses.
  • خَوَّنَ: To accuse someone of betrayal; in some lexical uses, to diminish something.

Some sources also record تَخَوَّنَ for attending to someone and returning to them. This disputed use will be considered with the early witnesses.

Six principal groups from the family occur in the Qur’an:

خَانَ, اِخْتَانَ, خَائِن, خَائِنَة, خَوَّان, and خِيَانَة.

Form I occurs five times, Form VIII twice, the active participle خائنين three times, خائنة twice, خوّان twice, and the noun خيانة twice.

These are morphological groupings. Different pronouns, grammatical cases, and prefixes such as فَـ and وَـ do not create separate derivatives.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: sixteen occurrences in eleven verses

The Semantic Core in Maqāyīs al-Lughah

Ibn Fāris writes:

الْخَاءُ وَالْوَاوُ وَالنُّونُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ، وَهُوَ التَّنَقُّصُ.

al-khāʾu wa-al-wāwu wa-al-nūnu aṣlun wāḥidun wa-huwa al-tanaqquṣu

Translation:

“Khāʾ, wāw, and nūn have one fundamental principle: diminution.”

He then explains:

يُقَالُ خَانَهُ يَخُونُهُ خَوْنًا. وَذَلِكَ نُقْصَانُ الْوَفَاءِ.

yuqālu khānahu yakhūnuhu khawnan wa-dhālika nuqṣānu al-wafāʾi

Meaning: “One says he betrayed him; this is a diminution of fidelity.”

The definition clarifies the connection between the root’s general field and its ethical use.

Diminution occurs when something that should remain complete or preserved is reduced.

When someone fails to honor a commitment, the deficiency concerns trust and obligation.

However, diminution as the root’s general field must be distinguished from betrayal as human moral conduct.

The loss of an animal’s flesh through travel and exertion can, linguistically, be described with this family. Neither the animal nor its owner has necessarily committed moral betrayal because of that loss.

Conversely, someone entrusted with property who knowingly conceals or appropriates part of it causes more than a material reduction: a trust and a right are violated.

Lisān al-ʿArab transmits Ibn Sīdah’s explanation:

الْخَوْنُ أَنْ يُؤْتَمَنَ الْإِنْسَانُ فَلَا يَنْصَحَ.

al-khawnu an yuʾtamana al-insānu fa-lā yanṣaḥa

Meaning: “Khawn is when a person is trusted but fails to act with the necessary good faith and fidelity.”

Trust and responsibility are thus central to the ethical meaning of betrayal.

The brief root definition cannot therefore be merely “lying” or “stealing.” Either may serve as a means of betrayal, but betrayal has a broader field.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Lisān al-ʿArab, and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: diminution and fidelity

2. Diminution, Broken Trust, and Early Arabic Uses

Falling Short in Rights and Fidelity

Arabic has the expression:

خَانَهُ فِي عَهْدِهِ

khānahu fī ʿahdihi

Meaning: “He betrayed his covenant with him.”

Likewise:

خَانَ الْأَمَانَةَ

khāna al-amānata

Meaning: “He betrayed the trust.”

Al-Muʿjam al-Wasīṭ explains betrayal of a trust as failing to discharge it, or failing to discharge part of it.

Betrayal therefore need not destroy the whole trust. Someone may honor one part while violating another.

A person responsible for safeguarding another’s property, for example, might return most of it but retain a portion.

Returning the larger portion does not undo the wrongful withholding of the rest.

Lexical sources accordingly record تَخَوَّنَنِي فُلَانٌ حَقِّي for someone gradually diminishing another person’s right.

Yet this infringement of a right differs from every reduction in quantity.

A right may be reduced with its holder’s consent, by agreement, or because legal circumstances change. Reduced quantity alone does not make that betrayal.

In ethical betrayal, the relationship of obligation and right is decisive.

Betrayal in Advice and Counsel

Arabic also records خَانَ النَّصِيحَةَ.

It concerns someone trusted as an adviser who fails in the necessary fidelity and honesty toward the interests and rights of the person seeking advice.

Someone might seek advice about a transaction, for example. The adviser knows a serious risk but conceals it for personal advantage.

The issue is not simply an incorrect statement: the trust inherent in the advisory relationship has been violated.

However, not every mistaken recommendation or failed prediction is betrayal.

An adviser may make a sincere recommendation based on available information that later produces an unfavorable result.

There may have been an error of judgment, but attributing betrayal requires examining the adviser’s awareness, obligation, and actual conduct.

Distinguishing error from betrayal matters linguistically and ethically.

A Sword’s Betrayal: Failure without Intention

Early Arabic has the expression:

خَانَهُ سَيْفُهُ

khānahu sayfuhu

Meaning: “His sword failed him,” or “His sword did not perform as expected.”

This describes a sword failing to take proper effect when striking.

The sword has concealed nothing and made no conscious decision to deceive its owner.

Yet language uses the same family because an expected function was not fulfilled.

The example clarifies the boundary between the general and ethical meanings.

In nonhuman uses, deficiency or failure can be called khawn; in ethical use, betrayal concerns trust and responsibility.

Arabic also uses خَانَتْهُ رِجْلَاهُ for someone whose legs can no longer carry them.

This means bodily incapacity, rather than the legs committing a moral wrong.

Time’s Betrayal: Changed Circumstances and Diminished Well-Being

Verse attributed to Al-Aʿshā reads:

وَخَانَ الزَّمَانُ أَبَا مَالِكٍ
وَأَيُّ امْرِئٍ لَمْ يَخُنْهُ الزَّمَنْ

wa-khāna al-zamānu abā mālikin / wa-ayyu imriʾin lam yakhunhu al-zaman

Translation:

“Time turned against Abū Mālik, bringing him down from his former condition; and what person has time not treated likewise?”

Betrayal is attributed to time.

Time, however, is not a person who made a covenant and deliberately broke it.

The image concerns changed circumstances and a loss of former well-being, power, or comfort.

In literary language, betrayal can thus describe an unfavorable change in condition.

Lexical and historical sources attribute the verse to Al-Aʿshā, but that attribution alone does not establish its precise date.

Nor should the poetic image of time’s betrayal reduce human moral betrayal to an involuntary, unaccountable action.

3. Poetic Witnesses: Gradual Attrition and Diminution

Labīd’s She-Camel Worn by Travel

Labīd ibn Rabīʿah describes a powerful she-camel:

عُذَافِرَةٌ تُقَمِّصُ بِالرُّدَافَى
تَخَوَّنَهَا نُزُولِي وَارْتِحَالِي

ʿudhāfiratun tuqammiṣu bi-al-rudāfā / takhawwanahā nuzūlī wa-irtiḥālī

Translation:

“A powerful she-camel carrying the riders behind with brisk vigor; my halts and departures have diminished her flesh and fat.” Lexical commentary identifies الرُّدَافَى as the plural of رديف, a rider seated behind another rider, rather than merely companions alongside the camel.

تَخَوَّنَهَا here concerns gradual reduction of the animal’s strength or bodily condition.

Lexical explanations specify the loss of the camel’s flesh and fat.

Here khawn means neither covenant-breaking nor deception, but attrition through activity and travel.

The witness matters for the root’s core because diminution also had uses independent of moral judgment in early Arabic.

Labīd was a mukhaḍram poet who lived before and after Islam. The verse is recorded in his collection, but its precise date is not apparent from the text itself.

Lisān al-ʿArab: the reading tuqammiṣu and the explanation of al-rudāfā

A Dwelling Worn by Rain and Wind: Dhū al-Rummah

Dhū al-Rummah says:

لَا، بَلْ هُوَ الشَّوْقُ مِنْ دَارٍ تَخَوَّنَهَا
مَرًّا سَحَابٌ وَمَرًّا بَارِحٌ تَرِبُ

lā bal huwa al-shawqu min dārin takhawwanahā / marran saḥābun wa-marran bāriḥun taribu

Translation:

“No, it is yearning for a dwelling whose traces have been diminished, now by rain, now by a dust-laden wind.”

تَخَوَّنَهَا describes the dwelling or its surviving traces.

Rain and wind have gradually worn away and effaced some of those traces.

The connection with gradual diminution is clear.

It also shows that تَخَوُّن can express attrition or repeated taking away in certain constructions.

This feature must not be generalized to every use of خان or خيانة. A human betrayal can occur in one action and need not be a lengthy or gradual process.

Dhū al-Rummah was an Umayyad poet. This is therefore early literary Arabic from the Islamic period, rather than pre-Islamic verse.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Lisān al-ʿArab: Dhū al-Rummah’s verse

تَخَوُّن as Attending and Returning: A Lexical Disagreement

Some sources record تَخَوَّنَهُ for repeatedly attending to someone and returning to them.

Another witness from Dhū al-Rummah describes a fawn whose mother grazes nearby and periodically checks it by voice or sight.

Al-Aṣmaʿī explains تَخَوُّن in that witness as attending and returning.

Ibn Fāris, however, suggests that this use may historically belong to KH W L, with nūn replacing lām.

Another explanation is that the mother’s call diminishes the fawn’s sleep or repose.

The two lexical analyses are therefore attending and returning, or diminishing the preceding state.

Their disagreement cautions against combining all recorded uses of تَخَوُّن into one definition without examining historical derivation.

Nor does this use mean Qur’anic خيانة signifies guardianship or care. Its Qur’anic occurrences concern trust, obligation, and treacherous conduct.

4. Betrayal in the Qur’an: Trust, Commitment, and Responsibility

Betraying God, the Messenger, and Trusts: Al-Anfāl 8:27

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَا تَخُونُوا اللَّهَ وَالرَّسُولَ وَتَخُونُوا أَمَانَاتِكُمْ وَأَنْتُمْ تَعْلَمُونَ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū lā takhūnū allāha wa-al-rasūla wa-takhūnū amānātikum wa-antum taʿlamūna

Proposed translation of the quoted text, without changing the installed verse translation:

“You who believe, do not betray The God and the Messenger, or betray your trusts, while you know.”

Al-Anfāl 8:27

The root occurs twice:

تَخُونُوا اللَّهَ وَالرَّسُولَ

takhūnū allāha wa-al-rasūla

And:

تَخُونُوا أَمَانَاتِكُمْ

takhūnū amānātikum

The first expression concerns a person’s relationship with God and the Messenger.

The second mentions trusts for which people have assumed responsibility.

Lexically, خان concerns deficient fidelity or broken trust. When its objects are God and the Messenger, it concerns human responsibility toward the divine message and command.

It should not be understood as inflicting deficiency or injury on God’s being.

A person’s betrayal diminishes their own fidelity and responsibility, rather than reducing God’s power, knowledge, or perfection.

The Role of وَأَنْتُمْ تَعْلَمُونَ

The verse’s final phrase is particularly significant:

وَأَنْتُمْ تَعْلَمُونَ

wa-antum taʿlamūna

Meaning: “While you know.”

This qualification brings the addressees’ awareness explicitly into the command.

The verse therefore concerns more than a mistake or unintended deficiency; conscious betrayal is central to its warning.

Someone might fail to discharge a responsibility completely through forgetfulness or genuine inability.

That differs from someone recognizing the responsibility and knowingly violating it.

The distinction does not remove every consequence or responsibility for unintentional error. It shows why conduct and degree of awareness matter when attributing moral betrayal.

Al-Anfāl 8:28 also discusses possessions and children as a trial. This connection shows how worldly attachments can compete with responsibility.

Yet KH W N itself does not make love of family or possession of wealth inherently treacherous. Betrayal arises when fidelity to a recognized right and responsibility is violated.

Betraying Oneself: Al-Baqarah 2:187

Sūrat al-Baqarah states:

عَلِمَ اللَّهُ أَنَّكُمْ كُنْتُمْ تَخْتَانُونَ أَنْفُسَكُمْ فَتَابَ عَلَيْكُمْ وَعَفَا عَنْكُمْ

ʿalima allāhu annakum kuntum takhtānūna anfusakum fa-tāba ʿalaykum wa-ʿafā ʿankum

Proposed translation of the quoted text, without changing the installed verse translation:

“The God knew that you were betraying yourselves, so He turned toward you and pardoned you.”

Al-Baqarah 2:187

تَخْتَانُونَ is an imperfect verb of Form VIII.

Its object is أَنْفُسَكُمْ, “yourselves.”

This construction reveals the root’s breadth: betrayal is not confined to another person’s rights or property. People can betray themselves.

The verse concerns fasting, marital relations during its nights, eating and drinking, and prescribed limits.

تَخْتَانُونَ أَنْفُسَكُمْ thus concerns the addressees’ conduct within those limits.

How Can Someone Betray Themselves?

Linguistically, betrayal usually involves a relationship in which trust or a right should be preserved.

With أَنْفُسَكُمْ as object, people act against a responsibility or right concerning themselves.

This should not be explained by imagining two independent people within one individual.

Someone may recognize what is right yet act against it, creating a conflict between knowledge, commitment, and action.

After mentioning that conduct, Al-Baqarah 2:187 speaks of turning toward them and pardon, then describes what is now permitted.

The verse connects earlier conduct, pardon, and a new allowance.

The verb تَخْتَانُونَ itself, however, does not specify every earlier regulation governing the addressees’ lives.

A complete historical set of regulations should therefore not be assumed without firm evidence merely to explain betrayal’s lexical meaning.

Al-Rāghib’s Mufradāt and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: differing analyses of ikhtiyān

Self-Betrayal and Defending Wrongdoing: Al-Nisāʾ 4:107

وَلَا تُجَادِلْ عَنِ الَّذِينَ يَخْتَانُونَ أَنْفُسَهُمْ إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يُحِبُّ مَنْ كَانَ خَوَّانًا أَثِيمًا

wa-lā tujādil ʿani alladhīna yakhtānūna anfusahum inna allāha lā yuḥibbu man kāna khawwānan athīman

Proposed translation of the quoted text, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Do not argue on behalf of those who betray themselves. The God does not love anyone who is treacherous and sinful.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:107

This uses the same verb, اختان, as Al-Baqarah 2:187.

Its context, however, differs.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:105 concerns judging between people according to truth and refusing to advocate for betrayers.

Verse 107 continues that command with الَّذِينَ يَخْتَانُونَ أَنْفُسَهُمْ.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:108 then describes people concealing their conduct from others but unable to conceal it from God.

Here self-betrayal is associated with wrongdoing, concealment, and defending misconduct.

The difference from Al-Baqarah 2:187 shows that betraying oneself is not confined to one sin or action.

خَوَّانًا أَثِيمًا: Treachery and Sin

خَوَّان follows the pattern فَعَّال. As a description, it highlights frequent or pronounced betrayal.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:107, it appears alongside أَثِيمًا.

The verse thus gives two descriptions: treacherous and sinful.

خَوَّان here does not merely describe someone who happened to produce an unfavorable result; it foregrounds betrayal in the person’s conduct.

Yet the pattern فَعَّال alone supplies neither an exact number of betrayals nor a specified numerical threshold for repetition.

The intensive description occurs within a context of wrongdoing and concealment.

Another distinction concerns defending someone’s rights versus defending their betrayal.

وَلَا تَكُنْ لِلْخَائِنِينَ خَصِيمًا in Al-Nisāʾ 4:105 and وَلَا تُجَادِلْ عَنِ الَّذِينَ يَخْتَانُونَ أَنْفُسَهُمْ in Al-Nisāʾ 4:107 challenge advocacy for treacherous conduct in judgment.

They do not establish that a person accused of betrayal loses every right to a fair hearing before the accusation is proved.

The verses concern supporting betrayal against truth. Judging individuals requires evidence.

5. Betrayal in Covenants and Social Relations

Fear of Betrayal and Open Termination of a Covenant: Al-Anfāl 8:58

وَإِمَّا تَخَافَنَّ مِنْ قَوْمٍ خِيَانَةً فَانْبِذْ إِلَيْهِمْ عَلَىٰ سَوَاءٍ إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يُحِبُّ الْخَائِنِينَ

wa-immā takhāfanna min qawmin khiyānatan fa-inbidh ilayhim ʿalā sawāʾin inna allāha lā yuḥibbu al-khāʾinīna

Proposed translation of the quoted text, without changing the installed verse translation:

“If you fear betrayal from a people, return the covenant to them openly and on equal terms. The God does not love betrayers.”

Al-Anfāl 8:58

خِيَانَةً is a verbal noun.

Unlike the preceding examples, betrayal is not here presented simply as an act whose occurrence has been conclusively established.

The wording says:

وَإِمَّا تَخَافَنَّ مِنْ قَوْمٍ خِيَانَةً

wa-immā takhāfanna min qawmin khiyānatan

Meaning: if you fear betrayal from a people.

An instruction then specifies how to deal with them:

فَانْبِذْ إِلَيْهِمْ عَلَىٰ سَوَاءٍ

fa-inbidh ilayhim ʿalā sawāʾin

Al-Anfāl 8:56 concerns covenants made with groups, some of whom have broken their covenant.

In that context, نبذ concerns openly returning or ending a covenant.

عَلَىٰ سَوَاءٍ matters: the covenant’s status must not secretly be altered to benefit one side while the other still believes itself secure.

Fear of Betrayal versus Its Established Occurrence

The verse distinguishes feared betrayal from betrayal itself.

The distinction matters ethically and legally.

There may be signs of a covenant’s being broken without every feared act having been established.

In such circumstances, the Qur’an emphasizes making the covenant’s status clear, rather than permitting secret betrayal in response to possible betrayal.

This connects with the conclusion:

إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يُحِبُّ الْخَائِنِينَ

inna allāha lā yuḥibbu al-khāʾinīna

Fear of another’s betrayal therefore does not itself authorize reciprocal betrayal.

The verse nevertheless does not detail every evidentiary criterion, investigative procedure, or condition for ending a covenant.

تَخَافَنَّ should therefore not become an unlimited license to act on groundless suspicion.

The clearer contextual sense is attention to signs of possible betrayal and an open, non-treacherous response.

Al-Ṭabarī: making the covenant’s status clear

Earlier Betrayal and Possible New Betrayal: Al-Anfāl 8:71

وَإِنْ يُرِيدُوا خِيَانَتَكَ فَقَدْ خَانُوا اللَّهَ مِنْ قَبْلُ فَأَمْكَنَ مِنْهُمْ وَاللَّهُ عَلِيمٌ حَكِيمٌ

wa-in yurīdū khiyānataka fa-qad khānū allāha min qablu fa-amkana minhum wa-allāhu ʿalīmun ḥakīmun

Proposed translation of the quoted text, without changing the installed verse translation:

“If they intend to betray you, they previously betrayed The God, so He gave you power over them. The God is Knowing, Wise.”

Al-Anfāl 8:71

The root occurs twice:

خِيَانَتَكَ is the noun “betrayal” with the addressee’s pronoun.

خَانُوا اللَّهَ is a plural perfect verb.

The first part concerns a possible future intention to betray; the second reports earlier betrayal.

The conditional structure makes this distinction clear.

إِنْ يُرِيدُوا expresses a conditional intention or desire.

فَقَدْ خَانُوا reports conduct in the past.

The verse thus distinguishes a possible new intention from previous conduct.

Earlier betrayal does not, by itself, prove that every future intention or action will also be betrayal.

The verse has a particular context of captives and relationships after battle.

It should not become a rule permanently denying every former wrongdoer the possibility of trust or reform.

Earlier conduct provides context for assessing possible new betrayal, but discerning each case’s intention and reality remains a separate matter.

خَائِنَة among a Group of the People of the Book: Al-Māʾidah 5:13

وَلَا تَزَالُ تَطَّلِعُ عَلَىٰ خَائِنَةٍ مِنْهُمْ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا مِنْهُمْ فَاعْفُ عَنْهُمْ وَاصْفَحْ إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُحِبُّ الْمُحْسِنِينَ

wa-lā tazālu taṭṭaliʿu ʿalā khāʾinatin minhum illā qalīlan minhum fa-uʿfu ʿanhum wa-iṣfaḥ inna allāha yuḥibbu al-muḥsinīna

Proposed translation of the quoted text, without changing the installed verse translation:

“You continue to discover betrayal from them, except a few of them. Pardon them and overlook. The God loves those who do good.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:13

This verse uses خَائِنَةٍ.

Structurally, it can be a feminine active participle. In some Arabic uses, however, خائنة also denotes betrayal itself or a treacherous action.

Al-Muʿjam al-Wasīṭ explains الخائنة with الخيانة: a verbal noun or abstract noun in a form resembling an active participle.

“A betrayal from them” is consequently natural and defensible here.

The context concerns breaking a covenant, displacing words, and forgetting part of what had been recalled to them.

خائنة thus concerns conduct incompatible with fidelity to the covenant and the trust of the message.

The continuation, however, says:

إِلَّا قَلِيلًا مِنْهُمْ

illā qalīlan minhum

This exception matters.

The verse does not place every member of a group under one undifferentiated collective judgment.

It also commands pardon and overlooking in dealing with the group from whom betrayal is discovered.

The subject therefore includes both recognizing betrayal and responding to people.

Lexically, خائنة refers to treacherous conduct. The verse does not authorize judging every present-day person of the same religious designation without examining their conduct and circumstances.

6. Betrayal in Close Relationships and Concealed Conduct

فَخَانَتَاهُمَا: The Wives of Noah and Lot, Al-Taḥrīm 66:10

ضَرَبَ اللَّهُ مَثَلًا لِلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا امْرَأَتَ نُوحٍ وَامْرَأَتَ لُوطٍ كَانَتَا تَحْتَ عَبْدَيْنِ مِنْ عِبَادِنَا صَالِحَيْنِ فَخَانَتَاهُمَا فَلَمْ يُغْنِيَا عَنْهُمَا مِنَ اللَّهِ شَيْئًا وَقِيلَ ادْخُلَا النَّارَ مَعَ الدَّاخِلِينَ

ḍaraba allāhu mathalan li-alladhīna kafarū imraʾata nūḥin wa-imraʾata lūṭin kānatā taḥta ʿabdayni min ʿibādinā ṣāliḥayni fa-khānatāhumā fa-lam yughniyā ʿanhumā mina allāhi shayʾan wa-qīla udkhulā al-nāra maʿa al-dākhilīna

Proposed translation of the quoted text, without changing the installed verse translation:

“The God sets forth the wife of Noah and the wife of Lot as an example for those who disbelieve. They were married to two righteous servants of Ours, but betrayed them. Those two men could avail them nothing against The God, and it was said: ‘Enter the Fire with those who enter.’”

Al-Taḥrīm 66:10

فَخَانَتَاهُمَا is a perfect verb with two feminine subjects.

Tāʾ marks the verb’s feminine agreement, and the following alif is the dual subject pronoun. هُمَا refers to the two husbands.

Grammatically, betrayal is attributed to two women, and its objects are identified as two righteous men.

The verb alone does not restrict that betrayal to one kind of marital conduct.

In Arabic, betrayal of a spouse can concern various acts, including broken trust, revealing secrets, or cooperation against rights and responsibilities.

The verse does not specify their precise conduct in detail.

Early exegetical sources transmit explanations concerning religious opposition and disclosure of information to opponents.

Those explanations can be examined alongside the narratives of Noah and Lot, but should not be presented as explicit details of this verse itself.

Conversely, attribution of betrayal to wives does not, without evidence, establish that sexual infidelity is necessarily intended.

Al-Ṭabarī: exegetical accounts of the two wives’ betrayal

Family Relationship and Personal Responsibility

The verse opens with an example for those who disbelieve.

It then identifies two women married to righteous servants, whose proximity did not save them from their conduct’s consequences.

Al-Taḥrīm 66:11 presents Pharaoh’s wife as an example for believers.

This connection shows that family relationship alone does not determine a person’s moral destiny.

Someone may live with a righteous person yet differ in belief and conduct; another may inhabit a corrupt environment yet choose a different path.

Lexically, betrayal here concerns failed fidelity within a relationship of responsibility.

Judgments concerning belief, disbelief, and people’s ultimate fate come from the full context and other expressions, rather than خان’s lexical definition alone.

لَمْ أَخُنْهُ بِالْغَيْبِ: Yūsuf 12:52

ذَٰلِكَ لِيَعْلَمَ أَنِّي لَمْ أَخُنْهُ بِالْغَيْبِ وَأَنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يَهْدِي كَيْدَ الْخَائِنِينَ

dhālika li-yaʿlama annī lam akhunhu bi-al-ghaybi wa-anna allāha lā yahdī kayda al-khāʾinīna

Proposed translation of the quoted text, without changing the installed verse translation:

“This is so that he may know I did not betray him in his absence, and that The God does not guide the scheming of betrayers.”

Yūsuf 12:52

أَخُنْهُ is the first person singular imperfect of خَانَ, made jussive by لَمْ.

The pronoun ـهُ refers to the person whom the speaker says they did not betray.

بِالْغَيْبِ concerns absence, beyond the person’s presence and immediate awareness.

Betrayal can thus be discussed where the person whose right or trust is involved is absent.

Someone might use the absence of direct supervision to violate a trust.

In this sentence itself, however, the speaker denies betrayal.

Who Speaks These Words?

In Yūsuf 12:51, the governor’s wife acknowledges that she sought to seduce Joseph and that he is truthful.

Verse 52 then begins with ذَٰلِكَ لِيَعْلَمَ.

Early explanations differ over whether this continues the wife’s speech or is Joseph’s speech.

In one reading, the wife explains that by confessing the truth she has not spoken falsely against Joseph in his absence. In another reading with the same speaker, such as Ibn Kathīr’s, the pronoun refers to her husband: she confesses so that he knows the ultimate alleged act did not occur. Identifying the speaker therefore does not, by itself, resolve every disagreement over the pronoun’s referent.

In the other main reading, Joseph speaks about preserving a trust and not betraying the master of the household in his absence.

This disagreement affects the speaker’s identity and the pronoun’s reference, but not the verb أَخُنْهُ’s basic meaning.

Both readings concern fidelity and not betraying someone in their absence.

Lexical study should not ignore credible disagreement over the speech’s attribution to reach a predetermined conclusion.

Al-Ṭabarī, Al-Qurṭubī, and Ibn Kathīr: speaker and pronoun reference

خَائِنَةَ الْأَعْيُنِ: Ghāfir 40:19

يَعْلَمُ خَائِنَةَ الْأَعْيُنِ وَمَا تُخْفِي الصُّدُورُ

yaʿlamu khāʾinata al-aʿyuni wa-mā tukhfī al-ṣudūru

Proposed translation of the quoted text, without changing the installed verse translation:

“He knows the betrayal of the eyes and what the breasts conceal.”

Ghāfir 40:19

خَائِنَةَ occurs in the genitive construction خَائِنَةَ الْأَعْيُنِ.

Lexical sources present two analyses.

In one, خائنة refers to a treacherous glance or movement of the eyes.

In the other, it means خيانة, an abstract noun in a form resembling an active participle.

In both, the subject concerns the eyes’ conduct.

Lisān al-ʿArab explains the expression through a concealed glance with wrongful intention.

The glance is treacherous because of the intention and conduct that the person seeks to conceal.

Is Every Concealed Glance a Betrayal?

Concealment alone is insufficient for that conclusion.

Someone might look cautiously, shyly, fearfully, or without wrongful intent.

Conversely, an apparently ordinary glance can be deliberate and treacherously intended.

The relationship between eye movement and what is concealed within the breast matters here.

After خَائِنَةَ الْأَعْيُنِ, the verse says:

وَمَا تُخْفِي الصُّدُورُ

wa-mā tukhfī al-ṣudūru

The eyes’ visible or partly concealed conduct is thus placed beside the person’s inner state.

More than physical eye movement is involved: God’s knowledge of conduct and concealed intention is also at issue.

Lexically, خَائِنَةَ الْأَعْيُنِ can mean a treacherous glance or betrayal carried out through the eyes.

Identifying moral instances requires examining intention and actual circumstances, rather than merely observing the direction in which the eyes move.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Miṣbāḥ: khāʾinat al-aʿyun

7. Treachery, Error, and Accusation

خَوَّانٍ كَفُورٍ: Al-Ḥajj 22:38

إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُدَافِعُ عَنِ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يُحِبُّ كُلَّ خَوَّانٍ كَفُورٍ

inna allāha yudāfiʿu ʿani alladhīna āmanū inna allāha lā yuḥibbu kulla khawwānin kafūrin

Proposed translation of the quoted text, without changing the installed verse translation:

“The God defends those who believe. The God does not love anyone treacherous and deeply ungrateful.”

Al-Ḥajj 22:38

خَوَّانٍ appears alongside كَفُورٍ.

Both descriptive forms express intensity or prominence of conduct.

خوّان concerns pronounced betrayal or habitual treachery. كفور belongs to K F R and must be understood through that root’s own meaning in this context.

The words should not be equated.

Someone may be ungrateful, but their particular conduct needs separate examination before it can also be called betrayal.

Conversely, betrayal can accompany ingratitude, denial of a right, or abuse of trust.

The verse places the descriptions together without defining either as the other’s lexical meaning.

A Treacherous Person versus Someone Accused of Betrayal

Al-Nisāʾ 4:105, Al-Nisāʾ 4:106, and Al-Nisāʾ 4:107 discuss advocacy for betrayers and those who betray themselves.

Al-Anfāl 8:58, however, speaks of fear of betrayal.

These contexts distinguish knowing treacherous conduct from its possible occurrence.

Accusation alone does not establish betrayal.

Nor can every error become deliberate betrayal without examining awareness and obligation.

Entrusted property may be lost through inadequate care, an unforeseeable accident, genuine inability, or deliberate appropriation.

The owner may suffer in every case, but responsibility and the applicability of betrayal are not identical.

Moral judgment thus requires more than harm occurring: conduct, awareness, obligation, and the person’s relationship to the right also matter.

Must Every Betrayal Be Concealed?

Betrayal often involves concealment.

Someone appropriating a trust or breaking a covenant may hide the truth to avoid discovery.

Yet concealment and betrayal are not synonyms.

A person can conceal a legitimate private matter without betraying anyone.

Conversely, someone may openly violate a commitment and thereby betray a right or covenant.

Concealment is therefore a possible characteristic of betrayal, rather than a universal lexical condition.

In Ghāfir 40:19, too, concealed looking matters through its association with wrongful intent; not every unnoticed eye movement is inherently treacherous.

8. Comparison with Related Roots

KH W N (خِيَانَة) and ʾ M N (أَمَانَة)

ʾ M N concerns security, trust, and entrusted responsibility.

أمانة is something entrusted to someone, or a responsibility they must preserve and discharge.

In ethical usage, betrayal violates that trust or the corresponding responsibility.

Al-Anfāl 8:27 makes the contrast explicit by mentioning betrayal and trusts together.

Trust should not, however, be limited to deposited property.

The Qur’anic field of trust can include various responsibilities. Betrayal can likewise concern covenants, confidence, and relationships without a material object being deposited.

خيانة and أمانة are thus closely related within fidelity, but neither is merely a mechanically inverted definition of the other.

Study of ʾ M N

KH W N (خِيَانَة) and W F Y (وَفَاء)

وفاء concerns completion, fulfillment, and honoring what one has undertaken.

Ibn Fāris calls betrayal نُقْصَانُ الْوَفَاءِ, diminution of fidelity.

The connection is clear.

Fulfilling a covenant means honoring and performing its terms.

Betrayal can arise when someone falls short in that commitment or knowingly acts against it.

The contrast does not make every inability to fulfill an obligation completely a moral betrayal, regardless of cause.

Circumstances may change and render performance impossible. Responsibility in that situation requires separate consideration.

Study of W F Y

KH W N (خِيَانَة) and GH D R (غَدْر)

غدر concerns covenant-breaking, faithlessness, and conduct violating another person’s trust.

خيانة and غدر are close in many uses.

غدر often concerns breaking a covenant and deceiving someone who relied on a promise or assurance of security.

Betrayal has a broader field, including financial trusts, advice, responsibility, family relationships, and even concealed conduct of the eyes.

In Al-Anfāl 8:58, betrayal’s covenant context brings it close to غدر.

Yet خَائِنَةَ الْأَعْيُنِ in Ghāfir 40:19 cannot be restricted to political or military covenant-breaking.

The roots therefore overlap in some instances without being complete synonyms.

KH W N (خِيَانَة) and GH L L (غُلُول)

In established Arabic and Qur’anic usage, غلول concerns secretly or wrongfully taking property, especially property belonging to a group.

Āl ʿImrān 3:161 uses this root in وَمَنْ يَغْلُلْ.

غلول can be an instance of betraying a financial trust, but not every betrayal is غلول.

Breaking a covenant, revealing a secret, or knowingly defending falsehood, for example, may constitute betrayal without appropriating collective property.

KH W N must therefore not be restricted to embezzlement or taking assets.

KH W N (خِيَانَة) and K DH B (كَذِب)

كذب concerns lying and stating what is contrary to fact.

Betrayal can involve lying, especially when falsehood conceals conduct.

The two are nevertheless different.

Someone may lie without violating a particular entrusted relationship or covenant.

Betrayal may also occur through action without a specific spoken lie.

Lying is thus one possible means or accompaniment of betrayal, rather than its entire meaning.

Study of K DH B

KH W N (خِيَانَة) and K T M (كِتْمَان)

كتمان concerns keeping something concealed, especially knowledge or truth that could be expressed.

Betrayal can occur through concealment: someone entrusted with information may knowingly withhold a truth they are obliged to disclose.

Yet not every concealment is betrayal.

Keeping a personal secret or confidential information may itself fulfill a trust.

Revealing the secret, rather than preserving it, may then be betrayal.

Judgment therefore begins by determining who has a right to know and who has a duty to disclose the information.

Study of K T M

KH W N (خَوْن) and N Q Ṣ (نُقْصَان)

نقص concerns reduced quantity or diminished completeness.

Ibn Fāris also explains خَوْن through تَنَقُّص.

The roots do not, however, coincide in every use.

Property may decrease through consumption, accident, or natural wear. Diminution has occurred, but moral betrayal need not have occurred.

Conversely, when someone knowingly violates another’s right despite their responsibility, diminution can become betrayal.

Diminution therefore helps identify the root’s general core, while trust and responsibility also matter in human conduct.

Study of N Q Ṣ

KH W N (تَخَوُّن) and KH W L (تَخَوُّل)

KH W L is an independent root, one branch of which concerns care and maintenance.

KH W N’s principal field, however, concerns diminution and taking away.

Some Arabic witnesses also use تَخَوُّن for attending and returning.

This led some lexicographers to suggest nūn had replaced lām in that use, making it historically part of KH W L.

The disagreement shows that similar sounds and a shared use cannot alone establish identical histories for two roots.

Qur’anic خَوَّلَ concerns granting and making a blessing available, whereas خان and اختان concern betrayal.

The roots should therefore remain distinct in translation and definition.

9. Translation Proposals for Forqan

For Qur’anic uses, Persian and Dari khiyānat kardan and khiyānat are suitable equivalents. These established loanwords readily convey breach of trust; English can use “betray” and “betrayal.”

Differences among the forms should nevertheless be preserved.

خَانَ generally means “he betrayed.” Al-Anfāl 8:71 concerns earlier betrayal; Al-Taḥrīm 66:10 concerns the two wives’ betrayal of their husbands.

تَخُونُوا in Al-Anfāl 8:27 is “do not betray,” concerning conscious violation of fidelity and trusts.

اِخْتَانَ in Al-Baqarah 2:187 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:107 concerns betraying oneself. “You were betraying yourselves” preserves the direct meaning.

خَائِنِين means “betrayers.” The title must be understood through conduct and the verses’ descriptions, rather than mere accusation.

خَوَّان can be rendered “treacherous” or “habitually treacherous.” In Al-Nisāʾ 4:107 and Al-Ḥajj 22:38, it is more emphatic than the simple active participle.

خِيَانَة denotes betrayal itself. Al-Anfāl 8:58 and Al-Anfāl 8:71 concern possible or intended betrayal in relation to another party.

خَائِنَة in Al-Māʾidah 5:13 is close to “betrayal” or “treacherous conduct.” In Ghāfir 40:19, خَائِنَةَ الْأَعْيُنِ can be “betrayal of the eyes” or “a treacherous glance.”

Brief Root Meaning

Diminution and taking away; in human conduct, violating trust, an entrusted responsibility, or fidelity, and infringing a right that should be respected.

Meaning in Qur’anic Uses

تَخْتَانُونَ — Al-Baqarah 2:187: You were betraying yourselves, in the context of failing to observe fasting’s limits.

الْخَائِنِينَ — Al-Nisāʾ 4:105: Betrayers; those whose wrongdoing must not be defended.

يَخْتَانُونَ — Al-Nisāʾ 4:107: They betray themselves, within a context of wrongdoing and concealment.

خَوَّانًا — Al-Nisāʾ 4:107: A treacherous person.

خَائِنَةٍ — Al-Māʾidah 5:13: Betrayal or treacherous conduct from the group described.

تَخُونُوا — Al-Anfāl 8:27, two occurrences: Do not betray God, the Messenger, or trusts.

خِيَانَةً — Al-Anfāl 8:58: Possible betrayal in a covenant context.

الْخَائِنِينَ — Al-Anfāl 8:58: Betrayers and covenant-breakers.

خِيَانَتَكَ — Al-Anfāl 8:71: An intention to betray you.

خَانُوا — Al-Anfāl 8:71: They betrayed previously.

أَخُنْهُ — Yūsuf 12:52: I did not betray him in his absence.

الْخَائِنِينَ — Yūsuf 12:52: Betrayers, in a context of scheming and violated trust.

خَوَّانٍ — Al-Ḥajj 22:38: A treacherous person.

خَائِنَةَ — Ghāfir 40:19: Betrayal of the eyes or a treacherous glance.

فَخَانَتَاهُمَا — Al-Taḥrīm 66:10: The two women betrayed their husbands.

These equivalents preserve the general meaning while identifying betrayal’s object through the verb’s complement and each verse’s context.

Conclusion

Arabic KH W N concerns diminution and taking away. Nonhuman uses can describe wear, incapacity, or disappointed expectations, such as a sword failing to work or time diminishing a person’s comfort and strength.

In ethical use, betrayal concerns trust, deposits, covenants, and responsibility. Someone who fails in required fidelity or knowingly violates a right they should respect commits betrayal.

Qur’anic contexts include betrayal of God and the Messenger, people’s trusts, social covenants, concealed conduct, self-betrayal, and family relationships.

Betrayal is thus not limited to taking property, lying, covenant-breaking, or marital infidelity, although each can constitute betrayal in particular circumstances.

The Qur’an also distinguishes conscious betrayal, fear of betrayal, intention to betray, and betrayal that has occurred. These differences prevent every error, failure, or accusation from becoming betrayal without examination.

The central distinction is between diminution as the root’s general meaning and violation of fidelity as its ethical and Qur’anic meaning.

The family’s overall meaning can therefore be expressed as:

“Diminishing what should remain preserved or complete; in human relationships, failing to honor trust, an entrusted responsibility, or commitment, and violating a right to which fidelity was owed.”

This definition explains early uses involving diminution and attrition while preserving the difference between moral betrayal, involuntary error, and genuine inability.

Qur’anic betrayal concerns more than outward action: responsibility, awareness, intention, and a person’s relationship to rights also matter. Their degree and the precise obligation and violation must nevertheless be established from each verse and its evidence, rather than presumed as details built into the root itself. Brief root meaning: Diminution and taking away; in human conduct, violating trust, an entrusted responsibility, or fidelity, and infringing a right that should be respected.
