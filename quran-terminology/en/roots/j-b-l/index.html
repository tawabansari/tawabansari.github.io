The Qur’anic Root J B L

Mountain, height, and firmness: from the earth’s structure to creation and human multitudes

Jabal is the Arabic name for a mountain: a large, projecting mass of earth characterized by height, hardness, and firmness. Other uses of the lexical family concern human constitution and creation, as well as large human communities.

The Qur’an clearly distinguishes these uses. Mountains provide refuge or stone for houses; appear as signs of creation and cosmic order; and, despite their firmness, are uprooted and scattered in scenes of the Resurrection. In two verses, however, derivatives concern human multitudes and earlier created generations rather than mountains.

Three concepts must therefore be distinguished: the mountain as a natural feature, creation and disposition as properties of beings, and a great multitude as a collection of people. These uses are related in Arabic, but not identical.

1. Root forms and semantic core

Arabic jabal is singular, with jibāl as its familiar plural. Dictionaries also record ajbāl and ajbul.

The family has 41 Qur’anic occurrences, falling into two nominal groups:

Jabal and jibāl: These occur 39 times, referring to a mountain or mountains. Forms such as al-jabal, jabalin, al-jibāl, wa-al-jibāl, and ka-al-jibāl differ through number, case, or attached particles rather than constituting independent derivatives. In Al-Nūr 24:43, the referent and figurative role of jibāl require interpretation; it should not silently be treated as a description of terrestrial mountains.

Jibillah and jibill: These appear in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:184 and Yā Sīn 36:62, concerning created beings and human multitudes.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: 41 occurrences, including 39 for mountain

Early Arabic sources also record jabala, majbūl, jibillah, jabīlah, ajbala, and tajabbala. Apart from the two nominal groups already mentioned, these forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies the root’s basis as تَجَمُّعُ الشَّيْءِ فِي ارْتِفَاعٍ (tajammuʿu al-shayʾi fī irtifāʿin): something gathering into an elevated mass.

Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs: gathering at a height

This fits a mountain’s image: a mass rising above its surroundings. A great multitude can likewise suggest mass and magnitude.

Not every derivative can nevertheless be reduced directly to “gathering at a height.” Jabala, creating or constituting, and jibillah, disposition or constitution, have established uses requiring their own evidence.

Height and mass are thus important to understanding the family, but must not become compulsory definitions of every form. Brief root meaning: a mountain or elevated mass; natural constitution and creation; a large human multitude.

2. Mountain, constitution, and multitude in early Arabic

The mountain as a physical image

Arabic sources use jabal for a large elevation of the earth, distinguished from minor rises or low hills, although actual usage supplies no precise universal boundary of height or size.

Ibn Manẓūr’s Lisān al-ʿArab explains jabal through size and height and also mentions akamah and qunnah in the discussion. Qunnah should not always be reduced to a small hill: it can also denote a summit or the upper part of a mountain.

Lexical sources: qunnah and a mountain’s summit

Mountains could serve as recognizable landmarks for locating places, navigating, and identifying territory. Height and hardness also afforded refuge, settlement, or use of their rocky portions.

These real properties furnished comparisons. A great, powerful person or group could be likened to a mountain for size, firmness, or resistance to defeat, rather than a mountain-like bodily shape.

A poem attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays begins with this hemistich:

«لِمَنْ طَلَلٌ بَيْنَ الْجُدَيَّةِ وَالْجَبَلْ»

li-man ṭalalun bayna al-judayyati wa-al-jabal

“Whose ruined dwelling is this, between Al-Judayyah and the mountain?”

Al-jabal occurs beside a place-name and may indicate a mountain or a geographical proper name; the hemistich alone cannot settle the distinction. Attribution of the poem to Imruʾ al-Qays is also disputed, and some editions place it among poems attributed to him. It cannot therefore serve as certain evidence of pre-Islamic usage.

Another witness quoted by Ibn Fāris calls Quraysh جَبَلُ اللَّهِ (jabalu Allāhi), comparing the community’s greatness with mountains. Jabal here praises a community’s magnitude and standing rather than naming a geographical feature.

Maqāyīs: the jabalu Allāhi witness about Quraysh

This helps illustrate comparing a great community to a mountain, without by itself proving the historical order in which the meanings developed. The witness’s precise date and attribution should remain distinct from the recorded lexical meaning.

Jabala: creating and constituting

Dictionaries record جَبَلَ اللَّهُ الْخَلْقَ (jabala Allāhu al-khalqa) for creating beings, and جَبَلَهُ عَلَى الشَّيْءِ (jabalahu ʿalā al-shayʾi) for creating or constituting someone with a particular trait or inclination.

Majbūl describes someone or something created or formed with a particular structure and disposition.

This concerns khilqah and ṭabīʿah, constitution and nature. Saying a person is constituted with a trait refers to a structure or inclination present in their nature.

This lexical use does not establish that every human action is immutably predetermined. Natural traits are not identical with having no choice in any action.

Jabala conveys creating and constituting; philosophical questions about the influence of nature, upbringing, environment, and choice require independent arguments.

Jibillah: disposition, structure, and creation

Arabic sources use jibillah for disposition, constitution, and something’s fundamental character.

Ibn Manẓūr explains jiblat al-shayʾ as its nature, origin, and what it is built upon. Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī likewise connects the word with the constitution placed in a created being.

For humans, jibillah can concern natural character and structure, but is not always limited to psychological or moral traits.

For example, a verse attributed to Qays ibn al-Khaṭīm reads:

«بَيْنَ شُكُولِ النِّسَاءِ خِلْقَتُهَا
قَصْدٌ فَلَا جَبْلَةٌ وَلَا قَضَفُ»

bayna shukūli al-nisāʾi khilqatuhā / qaṣdun fa-lā jablatun wa-lā qaḍafu

“Among the forms of women, her build is moderate and proportioned: neither bulky nor lean.”

Jablah here concerns bodily bulk, contrasted with qaḍaf, leanness or scant flesh.

The family therefore also describes bodily structure and size. Differences in vocalization and case among lexical quotations matter and should not be transferred without examination to Qur’anic jibillah. In Ibn Barrī’s account, jablah is an adjective for a stout woman; its structure should not simply be equated with the Qur’anic noun jibillah for created beings or constitution.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Ibn Barrī on jablah and its distinction from jibillah

Jibill: a great human multitude

Arabic also records jibill and related forms for a great population or numerous company.

Its linguistic image can be compared with a mountain’s mass and magnitude: a great community may be depicted through a similarly imposing mass.

In actual usage, however, jibill has the established meaning of a human company or population. Its translation need not directly recreate the mountain image.

Dictionaries record differing forms, including jibill, jubull, jubl, and jabl. Some occur in Qur’anic readings; all retain the general sense of a numerous company.

Lisān and Tāj: variant forms for human multitudes

Other uses of the family

Ajbala al-qawmu is recorded for a group reaching mountains or entering mountainous territory.

Ajbala al-ḥāfiru describes a digger encountering a hard or rocky layer that obstructs progress. Related figurative uses concern being at a loss or unable to achieve a result.

Lexical sources: a digger reaching rock and figurative extensions

Jablat al-arḍ concerns the ground’s hardness and firmness; tajabbala can denote entering or residing in mountainous terrain.

The Arabic family is thus broader than its Qur’anic nominal forms. None of these uses licenses importing helplessness, harsh character, or compulsion into every verse mentioning mountains.

3. Mountains in nature and human life

A real place for dwelling and refuge

وَأَوْحَىٰ رَبُّكَ إِلَى النَّحْلِ أَنِ اتَّخِذِي مِنَ الْجِبَالِ بُيُوتًا وَمِنَ الشَّجَرِ وَمِمَّا يَعْرِشُونَ

wa-awḥā rabbuka ilā al-naḥli ani ittakhidhī mina al-jibāli buyūtan wa-mina al-shajari wa-mimmā yaʿrishūna

“Your Lord revealed to the bee: Take dwellings in the mountains, in the trees, and in what they raise.” (Al-Naḥl 16:68)

Mountains stand beside trees and human-built structures as environments bees can use for their dwellings.

Al-jibāl has its familiar natural meaning here. Mountains provide an actual habitat and refuge, rather than necessarily symbolizing power or firmness.

Al-Naḥl 16:81 also mentions aknān in the mountains. From K N N, the word concerns concealment and shelter, showing human use of natural mountain spaces for protection.

Study of K N N

Regarding Thamūd, Al-Aʿrāf 7:74, Al-Ḥijr 15:82, and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:149 speak of carving houses in mountains.

Mountains are therefore not remote from human life in the Qur’an: they belong to the environments of dwelling, refuge, construction, and use of nature.

Mountain height and human limitation

وَلَا تَمْشِ فِي الْأَرْضِ مَرَحًا إِنَّكَ لَنْ تَخْرِقَ الْأَرْضَ وَلَنْ تَبْلُغَ الْجِبَالَ طُولًا

wa-lā tamshi fī al-arḍi maraḥan innaka lan takhriqa al-arḍa wa-lan tablugha al-jibāla ṭūlan

“Do not walk upon the earth with exultant self-display: you will never split the earth, nor attain the mountains in height.” (Al-Isrāʾ 17:37)

Mountain height serves here to remind people of their limits.

A person may act arrogantly, yet remains limited before the earth’s extent and the mountains’ height.

Al-jibāl still means natural mountains; their actual height serves an ethical argument.

This distinguishes lexical meaning from contextual role. “Mountain” does not mean arrogance: the mountain’s height becomes a means of criticizing human pride.

Mountains and imagined security

قَالَ سَآوِي إِلَىٰ جَبَلٍ يَعْصِمُنِي مِنَ الْمَاءِ قَالَ لَا عَاصِمَ الْيَوْمَ مِنْ أَمْرِ اللَّهِ إِلَّا مَنْ رَحِمَ وَحَالَ بَيْنَهُمَا الْمَوْجُ فَكَانَ مِنَ الْمُغْرَقِينَ

qāla sa-āwī ilā jabalin yaʿṣimunī mina al-māʾi qāla lā ʿāṣima al-yawma min amri Allāhi illā man raḥima wa-ḥāla baynahumā al-mawju fa-kāna mina al-mughraqīna

“He said: I shall seek refuge on a mountain that will protect me from the water. He said: Today there is no protector from The God’s command, except one whom He has mercy upon. The waves came between them, and he was among the drowned.” (Hūd 11:43)

Noah’s son regards the mountain as refuge from the flood.

That expectation draws on ordinary experience: moving to high ground when water rises can offer escape. His choice is therefore neither meaningless nor merely symbolic.

The narrative nevertheless shows that reliance on this refuge did not guarantee rescue from that event.

Hūd 11:42 compares waves to mountains. Mawjin ka-al-jibāli conveys their size and height, rather than water literally turning into rock or mountains.

Mountains serve once as natural refuge and once as the comparison for great waves. Their familiar meaning remains in both.

4. Mountains in creation, motion, and cosmic transformation

Mountains and the earth’s firmness

وَالْجِبَالَ أَوْتَادًا

wa-al-jibāla awtādan

“And the mountains as stakes.” (Al-Nabaʾ 78:7)

Following the earth described as a resting place, the verse compares mountains to awtād, stakes.

A watad is a peg driven into the ground to hold something firm; the comparison thus concerns establishment and firmness.

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:32 also uses arsāhā of mountains. From R S W, the verb concerns establishing or setting firmly.

Study of R S W

Jabal nevertheless must remain distinct from awtād and rawāsī. Jabal names the mountain; comparison to a stake or description of establishment comes from other words and constructions.

These verses alone are not technical explanations of geological mechanisms. Claims about tectonic plates, underground mountain structure, or their precise role in earthquakes require independent scientific evidence.

Mountains’ varied colours and structures

Fāṭir 35:27 speaks of white and red streaks or portions of varying colours in mountains.

This concerns observable features: mountains are not uniformly coloured or shaped, differing in colour, material, and visible structure.

Al-Nūr 24:43 also uses min jibālin fīhā min baradin in describing a phenomenon concerning the sky and hail.

The setting differs from terrestrial mountains. Mention of jibāl while hail descends from the sky requires examination of imagery and syntax.

Mountain-like masses or great accumulations are possible considerations. Determining the precise referents of jibāl and fīhā, or turning them into a technical description of clouds, requires separate investigation.

The verse should not change every occurrence of jabal into “cloud,” a celestial mass, or a nonterrestrial phenomenon.

Do mountains move?

وَتَرَى الْجِبَالَ تَحْسَبُهَا جَامِدَةً وَهِيَ تَمُرُّ مَرَّ السَّحَابِ صُنْعَ اللَّهِ الَّذِي أَتْقَنَ كُلَّ شَيْءٍ إِنَّهُ خَبِيرٌ بِمَا تَفْعَلُونَ

wa-tarā al-jibāla taḥsabuhā jāmidatan wa-hiya tamurru marra al-saḥābi ṣunʿa Allāhi alladhī atqana kulla shayʾin innahu khabīrun bi-mā tafʿalūna

“You see the mountains, thinking them motionless, while they pass like the passing of clouds—the work of The God, who has made everything precise and firm. He is fully aware of what you do.” (Al-Naml 27:88)

Al-jibāl remains “mountains”; tamurru and the comparison marra al-saḥābi attribute motion to them.

Context matters: Al-Naml 27:87 speaks of the trumpet being blown and creatures’ terror. Reading the mountains’ motion among Resurrection events therefore has contextual support.

The verse also describes seeing mountains, thinking them motionless, their cloud-like passing, and the precision of The God’s work. These expressions have prompted questions about mountain motion in the natural world too.

A decisive preference requires examining the contextual evidence, verbal constructions, and related passages on mountains’ transformation.

The lexical point is clear: jabal remains “mountain.” The dispute concerns the time and manner of motion, rather than changing the word into another concept.

Nor does the verse alone set out a particular scientific theory of rotation, tectonic plates, or a specific mountain displacement. Such applications must be assessed separately from the text’s direct meaning.

Firm mountains transformed at the Resurrection

Ordinary Qur’anic descriptions associate mountains with height, magnitude, and firmness; passages on cosmic transformation depict those same mountains losing their previous condition.

Ṭā Hā 20:105 describes their pulverizing and scattering; Al-Kahf 18:47, their being moved. Al-Wāqiʿah 56:5 and Al-Ḥāqqah 69:14 place them in scenes of immense earthly transformation.

Al-Maʿārij 70:9 and Al-Qāriʿah 101:5 compare mountains to wool. Al-Muzzammil 73:14 speaks of earth and mountains shaking and mountains becoming a heap of loose sand.

Al-Ṭūr 52:10, Al-Mursalāt 77:10, Al-Nabaʾ 78:20, and Al-Takwīr 81:3 likewise describe displacement, scattering, or loss of their familiar condition.

Thus a mountain’s familiar firmness is not absolute immutability. It may seem very hard and stable to humans yet remain changeable within creation.

Defining jabal as something inherently incapable of motion or destruction conflicts with the full range of Qur’anic uses.

5. Mountains expressing greatness, worship, and responsibility

The mountain in Moses’ story

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:143, Moses asks to see his Lord. His attention is directed to a mountain, whose remaining in place is set as a condition. Upon his Lord’s manifestation to it, the mountain is crushed and Moses falls unconscious.

The mountain’s familiar magnitude and firmness do not enable it to withstand the described event.

Its real meaning fits the passage: something experienced as hard and stable serves to convey an immense event.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:171 also describes placing a mountain over a group of Israelites.

Despite both mentioning mountains, the situations differ: one concerns Moses’ request and divine manifestation, the other covenant and an address to the Israelites.

All mountain references in Moses’ story should therefore not be reduced to one mental symbol, such as fear, greatness, or religious law.

Mountains glorifying with David

وَلَقَدْ آتَيْنَا دَاوُودَ مِنَّا فَضْلًا يَا جِبَالُ أَوِّبِي مَعَهُ وَالطَّيْرَ وَأَلَنَّا لَهُ الْحَدِيدَ

wa-la-qad ātaynā Dāwūda minnā faḍlan yā jibālu awwibī maʿahu wa-al-ṭayra wa-alannā lahu al-ḥadīda

“We granted David favour from Us: Mountains, return with him—and the birds too. And We softened iron for him.” (Sabaʾ 34:10)

The mountains are directly addressed. Yā jibālu is a vocative construction; awwibī commands them.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:79 and Ṣād 38:18 also speak of mountains glorifying with David.

The Qur’an thus does not depict mountains as objects unrelated to divine command. The word jabal itself nevertheless cannot disclose how their glorification occurs.

Was it audible to humans, or another kind of harmony and obedience within creation? That requires examining verses on creatures’ glorification, rather than changing “mountain” lexically.

Al-Ḥajj 22:18 includes mountains among beings prostrating to The God. It extends worship and obedience beyond humanity without defining a mountain’s prostration as identical with human bodily movements.

The mountain and the trust

إِنَّا عَرَضْنَا الْأَمَانَةَ عَلَى السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ وَالْجِبَالِ فَأَبَيْنَ أَنْ يَحْمِلْنَهَا وَأَشْفَقْنَ مِنْهَا وَحَمَلَهَا الْإِنْسَانُ إِنَّهُ كَانَ ظَلُومًا جَهُولًا

innā ʿaraḍnā al-amānata ʿalā al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍi wa-al-jibāli fa-abayna an yaḥmilnahā wa-ashfaqna minhā wa-ḥamalahā al-insānu innahu kāna ẓalūman jahūlan

“We offered the trust to the heavens, the earth, and the mountains. They declined to bear it and feared it, but the human being bore it. He has indeed been greatly unjust and greatly ignorant.” (Al-Aḥzāb 33:72)

Mountains stand beside heavens and earth in an account of offering the trust, declining to carry it, and fearing it.

This belongs within the question of the trust and humanity’s position. Naming mountains places immense, firm features of creation before a responsibility that humanity undertook.

Jabal alone nevertheless does not tell us how the offering occurred or what kind of event the mountains’ refusal was.

We should neither turn it without evidence into mountains conversing with human voices nor declare the entire account a literary device without any external reality unless adequately argued.

“Mountain” is clear; the harder issue is the manner of offering the trust and its relation to humans and other created beings.

The mountain in an example concerning the Qur’an

لَوْ أَنْزَلْنَا هَٰذَا الْقُرْآنَ عَلَىٰ جَبَلٍ لَرَأَيْتَهُ خَاشِعًا مُتَصَدِّعًا مِنْ خَشْيَةِ اللَّهِ وَتِلْكَ الْأَمْثَالُ نَضْرِبُهَا لِلنَّاسِ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَتَفَكَّرُونَ

law anzalnā hādhā al-Qurʾāna ʿalā jabalin la-raʾaytahu khāshiʿan mutaṣaddiʿan min khashyati Allāhi wa-tilka al-amthālu naḍribuhā li-al-nāsi laʿallahum yatafakkarūna

“Had We sent this Qur’an down upon a mountain, you would have seen it humbled and split apart in awe of The God. We set these examples before people so that they may reflect.” (Al-Ḥashr 59:21)

Law anzalnā is conditional. The verse presents a hypothetical example conveying the magnitude and weight of the Qur’an’s message.

The mountain’s hardness, size, and firmness give it a particular role. Depicting such a firm thing humbled and split before the Qur’an invites reflection on its message.

The ending explicitly describes examples set before people.

Jabal thus remains a mountain within a parabolic construction. Its lexical meaning has not become “human heart” or “powerful person”; familiar properties create a comparison inviting thought.

6. Jibillah and jibill: creation and human multitudes

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:184: earlier created generations

وَاتَّقُوا الَّذِي خَلَقَكُمْ وَالْجِبِلَّةَ الْأَوَّلِينَ

wa-ittaqū alladhī khalaqakum wa-al-jibillata al-awwalīna

“Be mindful of the one who created you and the earlier generations.” (Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:184)

This belongs to Shuʿayb’s address to his people.

Al-jibillata stands alongside the object -kum in khalaqakum, both connected to khalaqa. The creator made both the addressees and those created before them.

Lexically, jibillah can concern constitution and creation. Here al-jibillata al-awwalīna refers to earlier communities or created generations.

“The disposition of the predecessors” is therefore less clear and suitable than “earlier created generations”: the verse concerns generations and communities preceding its addressees.

Al-awwalīn also refers to those who came before, emphasizing that human creation is not confined to the present generation.

This matters in Shuʿayb’s exhortation to mindfulness: he recalls divine creative power over his audience and earlier generations.

Jibillah here is neither a mountain nor merely ethical or psychological nature; it concerns earlier created communities.

Yā Sīn 36:62: a numerous human multitude

وَلَقَدْ أَضَلَّ مِنْكُمْ جِبِلًّا كَثِيرًا أَفَلَمْ تَكُونُوا تَعْقِلُونَ

wa-la-qad aḍalla minkum jibillan kathīran a-fa-lam takūnū taʿqilūna

“He led a great multitude of you astray. Would you not use reason?” (Yā Sīn 36:62)

Jibillan appears with kathīran and denotes a numerous company of people.

The context concerns Satan misleading humans. Yā Sīn 36:60 addresses Adam’s children about not serving Satan; Yā Sīn 36:61 presents serving The God as the straight path.

Yā Sīn 36:62 then recalls Satan having misled a great multitude.

Jibillan cannot mean a mountain when human misguidance is the subject. Nor do “abundant disposition” or “much nature” fit the construction and context.

A great community or numerous population is the appropriate sense.

Readings and dictionaries transmit differing forms, such as jubullan, jublan, and jibillan. Their pronunciation and structure differ without removing the general meaning of a great company.

The two Qur’anic uses are related: one concerns earlier created generations, the other a numerous human company. Both move beyond natural mountains into human communities.

That relationship does not make every form merely a numerical expression. Arabic jibillah also concerns disposition and creation, while jibillan kathīran in Yā Sīn emphasizes a numerous population.

7. Nearby roots and translation proposals

J B L (jabal) compared with KH L Q (khalq)

Study of KH L Q

Qur’anic khalq concerns creating, bringing into being, and created structure. Arabic also records jabala for creating and constituting.

They approach one another in certain uses, but KH L Q has a far larger range of Qur’anic verbs and derivatives; J B L supplies no Qur’anic verb.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:184 combines khalaqakum and al-jibillata al-awwalīna: khalaqa expresses creating, while al-jibillah denotes earlier created communities.

Even here, their roles differ.

J B L (jabal) compared with R S W (rasw)

Study of R S W

R S W concerns settling, establishment, and standing firm, and occurs in expressions about mountains.

Jabal names the natural feature, while rasā and associated forms express establishment and firmness.

“Mountain” should therefore not always become “that which holds the earth still.” Where that meaning is present, it comes from other expressions in the context.

J B L (jabal) compared with J B R (jabr)

Study of J B R

J B R and J B L are distinct roots.

J B R concerns restoration, mending, power, and compulsion; J B L concerns mountains, constitution, and created communities.

Firmness and power may converge figuratively, but semantic proximity does not establish a common root or interchangeable derivatives.

Translation proposals for Forqan

Jabal normally has the natural, precise equivalent “mountain”; plural jibāl becomes “mountains.”

In Al-Baqarah 2:260, jabalin may denote a mountain or mountainous elevation as the setting requires; it need not be restricted without reason to a small hill.

Hūd 11:42 uses ka-al-jibāli, “like mountains.” The comparative ka- should remain in translation.

Jabalin in Hūd 11:43 is the mountain Noah’s son intends as refuge.

Jibillan kathīran in Yā Sīn 36:62 can be rendered “a great multitude” or “a large community.”

For al-jibillata al-awwalīna in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:184, “earlier created generations” preserves both creation and the expression’s collective sense.

Al-jibāl remains “mountains” in Al-Naml 27:88 and other passages on motion and transformation. Their manner belongs to contextual analysis, rather than changing the word’s meaning.

Conclusion

Arabic J B L concerns mountains, elevation, height, firmness, creation, and great multitudes. Ibn Fāris identifies gathering at a height as its basis, yet the recorded uses cannot all be confined to that image.

Qur’anic jabal and jibāl mostly concern natural mountains: places for dwelling, refuge, and human or animal use, also appearing in accounts of creation, divine power, parables, and Resurrection events. Al-Nūr 24:43 on hail requires separate investigation of referent and imagery.

Firmness and size are real properties sometimes used for reflection and comparison, but do not imply absolute immutability: other verses describe motion, crushing, and transformation.

Jibillah and jibill concern creation and human communities: earlier created generations in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:184 and a multitude misled in Yā Sīn 36:62.

Thus, jabal’s primary Qur’anic meaning is a mountain; its lexical family extends beyond natural mountains to disposition, creation, and great multitudes. These meanings are related, but each form must be translated by its actual sentence-level usage, rather than one fixed, universal etymological image.
