The Qur’anic Root D F Q (دَفَقَ)

1. Introduction and semantic core

The three-letter root D F Q in Arabic refers to pouring something out or its flowing forth, especially a liquid. Its prominent feature is the liquid’s movement and outward flow, which may involve pressure, speed, or a sudden discharge.

A simple image is water poured out of a vessel, a stream gushing from an opening, or floodwater advancing along its channel. These uses concern the liquid’s movement and pouring, rather than its substance or chemical composition.

In the Qur’an, this root occurs only once, in the form دَافِقٍ in Al-Ṭāriq 86:6:

خُلِقَ مِن مَّاءٍ دَافِقٍ

khuliqa min māʾin dāfiqin

“He was created from gushing water.”

Al-Ṭāriq 86:6

Here, dāfiq describes māʾ, “water.” It describes the water’s flow or discharge. The word dāfiq itself, however, does not name a particular substance, bodily organ, or complete reproductive process. Identifying the water under discussion depends on the context of human creation; the lexical meaning of dāfiq should be understood separately.

2. Lexical forms and the root’s central image

D F Q has several established forms in Arabic, but only dāfiq occurs in the Qur’an.

دَفَقَ is the Form I verb. In transitive use, it means “poured” or “poured out,” as when pouring water from a vessel. An intransitive use, describing the liquid itself flowing or pouring out, is also recorded in lexical sources, although lexicographers differed over the currency and acceptability of this construction.

دَفْق is the verbal noun, meaning pouring, expelling, or the flowing forth of liquid. دُفُوق is also recorded as a verbal noun in some lexical accounts.

دَافِق is an active participle on the pattern فَاعِل. Applied to liquid, it describes something gushing, flowing forth, or associated with a discharge. Lexicographers debated its precise semantic function; the discussion follows below.

مَدْفُوق is the passive participle, describing something poured or expelled.

انْدَفَقَ, Form VII, refers to flowing or pouring out when applied to liquids. تَدَفَّقَ is likewise used for flowing, pouring continuously, or streaming forth.

دَفَّقَ is Form II. In uses concerning liquids, it can refer to pouring or driving them out.

دُفْقَة denotes one outpouring or gush. The expression دُفْقَةً وَاحِدَةً is also recorded in Arabic for a single, simultaneous occurrence.

دُفَاق describes a copious flow, as in سَيْلٌ دُفَاقٌ, a plentiful, voluminous flood.

These forms belong to one semantic family, but their differences should be preserved. For example, دَفْق refers to the pouring itself; dāfiq describes something associated with this condition; and madfūq refers to something upon which the act of pouring has been performed.

The physical image: liquid moving outward

To understand this root, imagine a vessel full of water being tipped over at once. Water pours through its opening and begins to flow.

Now imagine a stream of water emerging under pressure from a narrow opening. Here, the liquid’s gushing movement is more conspicuous.

Both situations can fall within the semantic field of dafq. Nevertheless, the pressure, speed, and manner of discharge are not identical in every use. Sometimes a single outpouring is prominent; elsewhere, rapid movement and successive flow are emphasized.

The root should therefore not be restricted to one particular kind of gush. Dafq concerns pouring and outward flow; the sentence and its subject determine the movement’s precise character.

A foundational Arabic definition

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris describes the root’s semantic core as follows:

الدَّالُ وَالْفَاءُ وَالْقَافُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ مُطَّرِدٌ قِيَاسُهُ، وَهُوَ دَفْعُ الشَّيْءِ قُدُمًا

al-dālu wa-al-fāʾu wa-al-qāfu aṣlun wāḥidun muṭṭaridun qiyāsuhu, wa-huwa dafʿu al-shayʾi quduman

“Dāl, fāʾ, and qāf have one consistently applicable semantic principle: pushing something forward.”

This definition makes propulsion and forward movement the family’s semantic basis. Ibn Fāris then mentions pouring water and the expression māʾ dāfiq.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: the definition of D F Q

The definition shows that outward movement or propulsion lies behind the use concerning flowing water.

Nevertheless, Ibn Fāris’s definition should not be extended to make every forward movement dafq. The verb’s familiar use concerns pouring and the flow of liquid; other applications, including rapid movement or an animal’s bodily configuration, are particular branches of the family.

3. Older Arabic usage and extensions of meaning

Pouring water and shedding tears

Recorded Arabic uses associate this root with water and tears.

دَفَقَ الْمَاءَ means “he poured the water.” In some usages, however, دَفَقَ الْمَاءُ or انْدَفَقَ الْمَاءُ describes the water itself flowing forth.

Dafq and dufūq are also recorded for the shedding of tears. Here, the concern is tears moving outward and streaming down.

These examples show that dafq does not fundamentally name a particular kind of water. Ordinary water, tears, and other liquids can be described with this verb.

Consequently, in the Qur’anic expression māʾ dāfiq, the reproductive sense does not arise from D F Q itself; that setting is supplied by the sentence and its context.

Floodwater and voluminous flow

Classical dictionaries record سَيْلٌ دُفَاقٌ for floodwater that fills a valley.

They also record دَفَقَ النَّهْرُ or دَفَقَ الْوَادِي for a river or valley filling with so much water that it spills over its sides.

Here, pouring is associated with abundant, advancing water. Yet abundance belongs to the flood and its description as dufāq in this use. It does not follow that every dāfiq in every sentence must have a large volume or resemble a flood.

Arriving all at once and moving rapidly

The expression جَاءُوا دُفْقَةً وَاحِدَةً is recorded for a group arriving in one movement or all at once.

This use no longer concerns water. The quality of a single, simultaneous movement has been extended to the arrival of a group of people.

Lexical sources also record الدِّفَقّ for a fast-moving camel and الدِّفِقَّى for walking rapidly.

These uses involve forward movement, but speed should not be treated as a fixed component of every form of the root. A liquid may be poured from a vessel without its speed being comparable to a camel’s movement or a person’s haste.

Describing the human or animal body

A less familiar branch of this root is the adjective أَدْفَق.

Some Arabic descriptions call a camel adfaq when its elbows stand away from its flanks. Some sources also apply the adjective to a person whose back is bent by old age or sorrow.

These uses concern the projection or altered position of a limb, rather than the pouring of liquid.

A connection can be perceived between this branch and outward movement or displacement. It should not, however, replace the familiar meaning of dāfiq when describing water.

These senses are recorded in classical dictionaries. Recording an expression in such sources does not, by itself, establish exactly when it originated. This article does not quote an independent poetic witness after examining its wording, attribution, and date; the reported lexical uses should therefore not all be presented as securely established evidence from pre-Islamic poetry.

4. Qur’anic usage: مَاءٍ دَافِقٍ

The root’s only Qur’anic occurrence is Al-Ṭāriq 86:6. The verse appears within a discussion of human creation and The God’s power to bring the human being back.

From what was the human being created?

First, the Qur’an calls on the human being to consider the origin of his creation:

فَلْيَنْظُرِ الْإِنْسَانُ مِمَّ خُلِقَ

fa-l-yanẓuri al-insānu mimma khuliqa

“So let the human being consider from what he was created.”

Al-Ṭāriq 86:5

This verse asks about the origin of creation. مِمَّ combines مِنْ and مَا; here, it asks about that from which the human being was created.

The next verse answers:

خُلِقَ مِنْ مَاءٍ دَافِقٍ

khuliqa min māʾin dāfiqin

“He was created from gushing water.”

Al-Ṭāriq 86:6

خُلِقَ is a past passive verb from KH L Q. The agent who performs the creation is not named in this sentence; the verb refers to the human being who was the subject of the previous verse.

مِنْ مَاءٍ identifies the material origin being discussed, while دَافِقٍ describes that water.

The significant point is that the Qur’an does not simply mention water here; it introduces it through the quality dāfiq.

This description indicates the liquid’s flowing or pouring out. The description alone does not establish its substance, quantity, composition, place of production, or all the biological stages associated with it.

Within the context of human creation, this is water associated with the human being’s coming into existence. Nevertheless, dāfiq itself does not mean semen, sperm, or nuṭfah. It describes the liquid’s movement and discharge, rather than its biological nature.

Why dāfiq rather than madfūq?

Morphologically, دَافِق follows the pattern فاعل. The regular passive participle of this root would be مَدْفُوق.

This difference prompted classical lexicographers to offer several analyses of māʾ dāfiq.

First reading: dāfiq as flowing forth or gushing

On this reading, دَفَقَ can also be used intransitively: the water itself flows or gushes out.

Māʾ dāfiq would then mean water that gushes or pours forth. The active-participle construction retains that meaning.

Some lexical sources record this usage, but not all earlier lexicographers accepted the intransitive use of dafaqa to the same extent.

Second reading: dāfiq as madfūq

Al-Farrāʾ, an early philologist, interpreted dāfiq in this verse as madfūq: water that is poured or driven out.

Arabic has examples in which an active-participle form describes something that semantically receives the action. Expressions such as سِرٌّ كَاتِمٌ, understood as a concealed secret, are cited to explain this usage.

On this analysis, the water is called dāfiq, while the resulting sense is water that is poured out.

This analysis is established in grammatical sources, but it does not license freely converting every Arabic active participle into a passive participle.

Third reading: dāfiq as possessing dafq

In an analysis al-Zajjāj reports when explaining Sībawayh’s position, dāfiq means ذُو دَفْقٍ: something possessing the quality of dafq or outpouring.

The expression would accordingly mean “water characterized by gushing and flowing.”

This reading relates the participial form to a quality of the water. It requires neither imagining the water as an independent, conscious agent nor necessarily interpreting the form as madfūq.

Lisān al-ʿArab: intransitive usage and the analyses of al-Farrāʾ and al-Zajjāj

What follows from this lexical disagreement?

Despite their differences in grammatical analysis, all three readings converge within one semantic field: water associated with pouring, emergence, or flow.

Their principal difference is whether dāfiq describes the water itself as flowing, as something poured out, or as possessing the quality of dafq.

This distinction matters for morphological research, but it does not necessarily produce three wholly different natural Persian equivalents.

“Gushing water” can convey the perceptible quality without adding a claim about the physical cause of the gush or the Arabic verb’s precise construction.

The pattern فاعل here likewise does not mean that the water possesses intention, choice, or independent power. Water is a liquid; dāfiq describes its condition of movement.

How dāfiq relates to the next verse

After introducing the gushing water, the seventh verse says:

يَخْرُجُ مِنْ بَيْنِ الصُّلْبِ وَالتَّرَائِبِ

yakhruju min bayni al-ṣulbi wa-al-tarāʾibi

“It comes out from between the ṣulb and the tarāʾib.”

Al-Ṭāriq 86:7

يَخْرُجُ comes from KH R J and means coming out. It is semantically close to dāfiq because both involve emergence in this context.

There is nevertheless a difference. Dāfiq describes the quality of flowing and pouring out, whereas yakhruju states the emergence itself and is accompanied by مِنْ بَيْنِ الصُّلْبِ وَالتَّرَائِبِ.

In the sentence’s sequence, the gushing water is the nearest antecedent for the unexpressed subject of yakhruju. Nevertheless, a detailed study of the verse must assess the grammatical possibilities and the pronoun’s referent separately.

In any event, the liquid’s anatomical origin or place of emergence cannot be deduced from D F Q itself. That question concerns the seventh verse’s construction and the meanings of ṣulb and tarāʾib.

Nor should “from between” be treated as part of dāfiq’s meaning. It comes from the preposition مِنْ and the noun بَيْن in the following verse.

The description’s place in the surah’s argument

The eighth verse says:

إِنَّهُ عَلَىٰ رَجْعِهِ لَقَادِرٌ

innahu ʿalā rajʿihi la-qādirun

“Indeed, He has power to bring him back.”

Al-Ṭāriq 86:8

Read together, Al-Ṭāriq 86:5, Al-Ṭāriq 86:6, Al-Ṭāriq 86:7, and Al-Ṭāriq 86:8 concern the origin of human creation and The God’s power to bring the human being back.

The passage directs the human being’s attention to his beginning, then speaks of the power to bring him back. Creation and re-creation are central to this sequence.

Within this argument, māʾ dāfiq expresses the material origin of human creation. The word dāfiq itself, however, does not mean creation, life, death, or return. Those concepts arise from the verses together and from their other words.

Nor does the Qur’anic statement about the origin of creation, by itself, provide a complete biological account of reproduction. The word dāfiq should not be made to yield a theory about liquid pressure, the producing organ, where reproductive cells form, or the reproductive system’s precise characteristics.

The lexical meaning is clear: the water is introduced through the quality of gushing or pouring out. Biological and anatomical explanations require separate investigation.

5. Distinctions from related roots

D F Q (دَفَقَ) and D F ʿ (دَفَعَ): pouring out and pushing

Study of D F ʿ

Dafʿ in Arabic involves pushing, repelling, or moving something against another force.

Its scope is broader than dafq. Someone may push another person away, repel a danger, or propel something forward without any liquid being involved.

Dafq, by contrast, is more closely associated with pouring and liquid flow in its familiar use.

Ibn Fāris explains dafq’s semantic core through دفع الشيء قدما, pushing something forward. This proximity, however, does not make the two roots fully interchangeable.

Dafʿ emphasizes pushing and repelling; dafq, when applied to water, highlights pouring out and flowing forth.

D F Q (دَفَقَ) and Ṣ B B (صَبَّ): gushing and pouring

Study of Ṣ B B

Ṣabb refers to pouring something and is used especially for liquids.

The Qur’an says:

أَنَّا صَبَبْنَا الْمَاءَ صَبًّا

annā ṣababnā al-māʾa ṣabban

“That We poured down the water in a thorough outpouring.”

ʿAbasa 80:25

Here, ṣababnā means pouring water. The verb itself does not require water to gush under pressure from a narrow opening.

Dafq also concerns pouring, but uses such as māʾ dāfiq make the liquid’s rapid or gushing movement more prominent.

This should not, however, become an absolute rule that every ṣabb is gentle and every dafq forceful. The particular usage establishes the movement’s intensity and the liquid’s quantity.

D F Q (دَفَقَ) and F Y Ḍ (فَاضَ): gushing and overflowing

Study of F Y Ḍ

F Y Ḍ involves becoming abundant, overflowing, or flowing forth.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:83, the Qur’an speaks of eyes overflowing with tears.

Here, fayḍ involves abundance and something passing beyond its usual boundary.

Dafq can describe a liquid pouring or gushing outward even when no vessel fills and overflows.

A stream might therefore be both dāfiq and, in some circumstances, fāʾiḍ, but the two roots do not express precisely the same aspect of meaning.

D F Q (دَفَقَ) and N Ḍ KH (نَضَخَ): pouring out and gushing forth

Study of N Ḍ KH

Surah Al-Raḥmān says:

فِيهِمَا عَيْنَانِ نَضَّاخَتَانِ

fīhimā ʿaynāni naḍḍākhatāni

“In those two are two springs gushing forth.”

Al-Raḥmān 55:66

نَضَّاخَتَانِ comes from N Ḍ KH and describes the two springs as gushing and sending water forth.

This is close to dafq’s semantic field: both concern the conspicuous outward movement of liquid.

Nevertheless, naḍkh has a particular use for water gushing forth, whereas dafq can encompass pouring water from a vessel, flowing floodwater, or a liquid’s discharge in different circumstances.

The resemblance does not establish that the gushing water in Al-Ṭāriq must move exactly like a gushing spring.

D F Q (دَفَقَ) and KH R J (خَرَجَ): the manner of emergence and emergence itself

Study of KH R J

Khurūj in Arabic means coming out, whether the subject is a human being, animal, plant, liquid, or something else.

In the consecutive verses Al-Ṭāriq 86:6 and Al-Ṭāriq 86:7, dāfiq and yakhruju appear together.

Their proximity helps distinguish their meanings. Khurūj states emergence itself; dafq, when applied to liquid, highlights its pouring and movement.

Every liquid that gushes outward undergoes a kind of emergence, but not everything that emerges is necessarily dāfiq.

6. Challenges in understanding and translation

Must dāfiq mean being expelled under great pressure?

Not as an absolute rule.

Pressure and speed can be prominent in the root’s semantic field. Nevertheless, the word itself specifies no particular pressure or speed.

Dāfiq alone also does not establish that the liquid travels in a straight line, is projected a certain distance, or emerges from a particular organ.

If a discussion requires these details, they must be established through independent evidence.

Must dāfiq mean madfūq?

Madfūq is one established lexical explanation of dāfiq in Al-Ṭāriq, but it is not the only analysis offered.

The intransitive use of dafaqa and the analysis “possessing dafq” also appear in classical linguistic discussions.

Consequently, the difference between an active-participle form and a passive-participle form alone does not establish that the Qur’an’s wording is incorrect or that the word necessarily departs from its ordinary meaning.

Arabic descriptive constructions are varied, and dāfiq naturally describes a quality of the water in this context.

Is māʾ dāfiq a scientific name for a substance?

No.

In the sentence, māʾ names the liquid and dāfiq describes it.

Within the context of human creation, this water relates to reproduction and the human being’s origin. The expression alone, however, is not a scientific name for a particular substance or cell.

Consequently, rendering māʾ dāfiq directly as “sperm,” “a male reproductive cell,” “secretions of a particular organ,” or a laboratory term reduces the verse’s verbal precision.

This does not deny scientific knowledge of reproduction. The concern is to preserve the distinction between translating a Qur’anic word and scientifically explaining its referent.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

For transitive دَفَقَ, “poured” and “poured out” are natural equivalents; “drove out under pressure” can suit an appropriate context.

For the intransitive use or the forms انْدَفَقَ and تَدَفَّقَ, suitable equivalents include “poured out,” “flowed,” “began to flow,” and “gushed.” “Spouted forth” is also possible in an appropriate setting, but should not become the root’s invariable equivalent.

For دَفْق, “outpouring,” “gushing,” and “pouring” are suitable.

For دُفْقَة, “one outpouring” or “a single gush” is proposed; for مَدْفُوق, “poured out” or “expelled.”

For the Qur’anic مَاءٍ دَافِقٍ, the principal choices are “gushing water,” “water flowing under pressure,” and “water that pours out in a gush.”

Of these, “gushing water” is preferable for the base translation because it is concise, natural, and close to the Arabic description.

The proposed translation of Al-Ṭāriq 86:6 is therefore:

خُلِقَ مِنْ مَاءٍ دَافِقٍ

khuliqa min māʾin dāfiqin

“He was created from gushing water.”

This rendering preserves the passive خُلِقَ, the preposition مِنْ, the noun māʾ, and the description dāfiq. It does not add semen, the liquid’s anatomical origin, or a full account of reproduction to the verse’s wording.

If a reflection on the verse requires further explanation, it can state that dāfiq here describes water associated with gushing and discharge, and that this description accompanies the origin of human creation in the surah’s context.

8. Conclusion

D F Q in Arabic refers to pouring something out or its outward flow, especially a liquid. Its uses highlight outward movement, flow, gushing, and sometimes speed or pressure.

In Arabic, this family concerns pouring water, shedding tears, flowing floodwater, and movement occurring all at once. More limited branches describe rapid movement and aspects of the human or animal body; these should not be imposed on the Qur’anic meaning of dāfiq.

Only دَافِقٍ occurs in the Qur’an, describing māʾ in Al-Ṭāriq 86:6.

Morphologically, dāfiq is an active participle. Three important lexical explanations are offered: water flowing forth, water poured out, and water possessing the quality of dafq. These readings differ grammatically but all concern a liquid’s outpouring and flow.

In Al-Ṭāriq’s context, the gushing water relates to the origin of human creation. The root itself, however, does not specify the kind of liquid, the producing organ, the place of emergence, or the biological details of the process. These questions require investigation apart from the root’s meaning.

The brief meaning of D F Q is: a liquid pouring or gushing out, often with flow and pressure.

In Qur’anic use, dāfiq describes water characterized by this movement and discharge. “Gushing water” is therefore a precise, natural rendering that preserves the linguistic meaning without substituting later explanations for the Qur’an’s wording.
