Flowing, releasing, and letting loose: from moving water to sāʾibah and the Qur'anic criticism of invented restrictions

1. The root in early Arabic

In Arabic, S Y B concerns flowing, moving without obstruction, and releasing something. Two main uses are the free movement or flow of something, and leaving it free to continue moving without its previous restraint.

Sāba al-māʾu yasību means that water flowed. Sayb al-māʾ refers to the flowing of water, while al-sīb denotes its channel or watercourse. Here the subject is water moving along its course.

Sābat al-ḥayyah is also used for a snake's movement; the lexical explanations include moving swiftly. Insābat al-ḥayyah likewise describes a snake moving or gliding along. The movement may appear continuous, but uninterrupted movement and gentleness are not necessary meanings in every use.

In another branch, sayyaba al-shayʾa means leaving something or letting it loose. Sayyaba al-dābbah means releasing an animal to go where it wishes, without restraining its movement as before.

There is an important distinction between sāba and sayyaba. The former can describe something's own movement; the latter describes an agent releasing it or removing its previous restraint.

Accordingly, sāʾibah can describe something or an animal moving freely or left loose.

Historical definition and the limits of evidence

These meanings are recorded in established Arabic dictionaries. Ibn Fāris identifies continued movement and going onward as the connection between uses of this entry. This explains the flowing of water, a snake's movement, and releasing an animal.

Recording these senses in dictionaries compiled in the Islamic period does not by itself establish that every form and semantic detail had precisely the same range before the Qur'an.

An early poetic witness is transmitted:

أَتَذْهَبُ سَلْمَى فِي اللِّمَامِ فَلَا تُرَى
وَبِاللَّيْلِ أَيْمٌ حَيْثُ شَاءَ يَسِيبُ

a-tadhhabu salmā fī al-limāmi fa-lā turā / wa-bi-al-layli aymun ḥaythu shāʾa yasību

An approximate rendering following the quoted wording is: “Does Salmā go away during brief visits and remain unseen, while a snake at night moves wherever it wishes?”

Yasību here refers to the snake's movement. The verse is transmitted in early lexicographical sources, but its attribution and date are not sufficiently clear to treat it as a securely dated pre-Qur'anic witness.

Another branch appears in a verse attributed to Mafrūq ibn ʿAmr al-Shaybānī:

فَمَا أَنَا مِنْ رَيْبِ الْمَنُونِ بِجُبَّإٍ
وَمَا أَنَا مِنْ سَيْبِ الْإِلَهِ بِآيِسِ

fa-mā anā min raybi al-manūni bi-jubbaʾin / wa-mā anā min saybi al-ilāhi bi-āyisi

“I am no coward before the vicissitudes of death, nor am I despairing of the deity's bounty.”

Sayb here means a gift or bounty. Mafrūq is counted among poets close to the emergence of Islam, but the verse's date is not independently established. It is useful evidence for this early sense, not conclusive proof of usage at a particular time before the Qur'an.

Another use appears in verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Uḥayḥah ibn al-Julāḥ:

أَقْسَمْتُ لَا أُعْطِيكَ فِي
كَعْبٍ وَمَقْتَلِهِ سَيَابَهْ

aqsamtu lā uʿṭīka fī / kaʿbin wa-maqtalihi sayābah

“I swore to give you, over Kaʿb and his killing, not even an unripe date.”

Sayābah names a single unripe date. This shows that forms within the lexical entry also name objects. It does not establish that the name for an unripe date necessarily derives from flowing or release. Ibn Fāris likewise treats this branch as outside the proposed main principle.

A historical account of the family should therefore distinguish clear lexical meaning, early evidence, and theories about the historical relationship between senses.

2. Forms and semantic branches

Important forms recorded in Arabic include:

  • سَابَ / يَسِيبُ (sāba / yasību): to flow, move along, or move freely.
  • سَيْب (sayb): flowing or movement; also a gift or bounty.
  • السِّيب (al-sīb): a watercourse.
  • سَيَّبَ / يُسَيِّبُ (sayyaba / yusayyibu): to release, let loose, or leave.
  • تَسْيِيب (tasyīb): releasing.
  • سَائِب / سَائِبَة (sāʾib / sāʾibah): loose, released, or moving without a previous restraint.
  • اِنْسَابَ / يَنْسَابُ (insāba / yansābu): to flow or move along.
  • سُيُوب (suyūb): a plural recorded for watercourses and also for buried wealth or mineral deposits, explained through bounty or flowing veins.
  • سَيَاب / سَيَابَة (sayāb / sayābah): unripe dates / one unripe date; a nominal branch whose historical relationship to movement and release is uncertain.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur'an.

The root occurs only as سَائِبَةٍ (sāʾibatin) in Al-Māʾidah 5:103. Its morphological pattern is the feminine active participle of sāba. In this use, however, it names an animal that people had released and exempted from certain ordinary uses; lexicographers also explain its sense as musayyabah, “released.”

An active-participle pattern need not always indicate the conscious agent of an action: it may describe an entity in the state expressed by the verb. Distinguishing morphological form from contextual meaning explains the rendering “released” here.

3. The connection between flowing and release

Many uses of S Y B involve movement without obstruction.

Water flows along its course. A snake glides from one place to another. An animal left loose is no longer subject to the same direct restraint by its keeper.

In this respect, there is a clear semantic relationship between flowing and letting loose.

Yet the relationship has two different sides: sometimes something itself moves, and sometimes someone removes an obstacle or restriction on its movement.

For example, sāba al-māʾu concerns water flowing, whereas sayyaba al-nāqata describes someone letting a she-camel loose.

The distinction should be preserved. Sayyaba expresses release; what is released and how it moves depend on its object and context. Sāba, likewise, does not necessarily mean release from ownership or law in every sentence.

Sayb as a gift

Sayb is also recorded with the senses of giving, bounty, and kindness.

Lexicographers connect this branch with something passing from a giver to a recipient and becoming available to them. A gift can thus be something the giver lets go from their possessions and conveys to someone else.

This is a meaningful connection between giving and release, but does not establish that the sense of giving necessarily arose after that of flowing water.

For the Qur'anic sāʾibah, the gift sense may help explain some historical accounts of releasing livestock, but “gift” should not replace sāʾibah itself in translating the verse.

4. Sāʾibah in the Qur'an

Al-Māʾidah 5:103 states:

مَا جَعَلَ اللَّهُ مِنْ بَحِيرَةٍ وَلَا سَائِبَةٍ وَلَا وَصِيلَةٍ وَلَا حَامٍ وَلَٰكِنَّ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا يَفْتَرُونَ عَلَى اللَّهِ الْكَذِبَ وَأَكْثَرُهُمْ لَا يَعْقِلُونَ

mā jaʿala allāhu min baḥīratin wa-lā sāʾibatin wa-lā waṣīlatin wa-lā ḥāmin wa-lākinna alladhīna kafarū yaftarūna ʿalā allāhi al-kadhiba wa-aktharuhum lā yaʿqilūn

Al-Māʾidah 5:103

The God has instituted no bahirah, sa’ibah, wasilah, or ham. But those who disbelieve fabricate lies against The God, and most of them do not reason.

This is the only verse containing a derivative of S Y B.

Sāʾibah appears alongside three other designations: baḥīrah, waṣīlah, and ḥām. These concerned categories assigned to certain livestock, with restrictions on using the animals or their products.

Lexically, sāʾibah refers to an animal left loose. In the social setting addressed by the verse, however, this was more than ordinary livestock management: it was associated with beliefs and restrictions claimed to have religious authority.

Sāʾibah in pre-Islamic Arabian life

Historical and lexical accounts describe people declaring an animal sāʾibah because of a vow, recovery from illness, a successful return from travel, or an event they considered a reason for gratitude.

An animal receiving this designation might be exempted from certain ordinary uses. Accounts mention refraining from riding it, loading it, milking it, or barring it from pasture and water.

Some accounts link the release to dedicating the animal to deities people worshipped. Others use sāʾibah for an animal removed from ordinary use after a specified number of births or some other condition.

These reports differ in detail: the number of births, kind of animal, conditions of designation, and permitted uses vary.

Despite those differences, the shared point is clear: people assigned special designations to certain livestock and imposed restrictions on their use accordingly.

No single historical account should therefore be presented as the only possible form of sāʾibah, nor should all its details be treated as the word's lexical meaning.

Lexically, sāʾibah means loose or released; in Al-Māʾidah 5:103 it is a recognized designation within a social and religious system concerning livestock.

Why does the Qur'an reject sāʾibah?

The verse's construction matters:

مَا جَعَلَ اللَّهُ مِنْ بَحِيرَةٍ وَلَا سَائِبَةٍ وَلَا وَصِيلَةٍ وَلَا حَامٍ

mā jaʿala allāhu min baḥīratin wa-lā sāʾibatin wa-lā waṣīlatin wa-lā ḥām

Al-Māʾidah 5:103

Mā jaʿala Allāhu states that The God has not instituted these categories.

The verse does not say people never called an animal sāʾibah or that the custom did not exist. On the contrary, mentioning these names indicates that the audience knew them.

What is denied is the attribution of these regulations and classifications to The God.

The continuation makes the point explicit:

وَلَٰكِنَّ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا يَفْتَرُونَ عَلَى اللَّهِ الْكَذِبَ

wa-lākinna alladhīna kafarū yaftarūna ʿalā allāhi al-kadhib

Al-Māʾidah 5:103

But those who disbelieve fabricate lies against The God.

The issue is therefore more than keeping or releasing an animal: it is creating a religious status or rule and attributing it to The God when He has instituted no such rule.

Letting an animal loose, giving property, or personally refraining from using something is not itself a claim of divine command or prohibition. The difference arises when a human choice is made into a religious law attributed to The God.

5. Sāʾibah and other Qur'anic passages

Although S Y B occurs only once, its subject continues in other verses, particularly those concerning invented prohibitions and attributing rulings to The God.

Invented prohibitions concerning livestock

Al-Anʿām 6:138 states:

وَقَالُوا هَٰذِهِ أَنْعَامٌ وَحَرْثٌ حِجْرٌ لَا يَطْعَمُهَا إِلَّا مَنْ نَشَاءُ بِزَعْمِهِمْ وَأَنْعَامٌ حُرِّمَتْ ظُهُورُهَا وَأَنْعَامٌ لَا يَذْكُرُونَ اسْمَ اللَّهِ عَلَيْهَا افْتِرَاءً عَلَيْهِ سَيَجْزِيهِمْ بِمَا كَانُوا يَفْتَرُونَ

wa-qālū hādhihi anʿāmun wa-ḥarthun ḥijrun lā yaṭʿamuhā illā man nashāʾu bi-zaʿmihim wa-anʿāmun ḥurrimat ẓuhūruhā wa-anʿāmun lā yadhkurūna isma allāhi ʿalayhā iftirāʾan ʿalayhi sa-yajzīhim bi-mā kānū yaftarūn

Al-Anʿām 6:138

They said, “These livestock and crops are restricted; none may eat them except those we wish”—so they claim—and there are livestock whose backs are forbidden, and livestock over which they do not mention the name of The God, fabricating against Him. He will repay them for what they used to fabricate.

The verse describes classifications of livestock and agricultural produce by which use was permitted to some people and forbidden to others.

It also mentions livestock whose backs were considered forbidden to use: for riding or carrying loads.

These descriptions relate to the social setting of sāʾibah and similar designations, but this verse does not repeat the name sāʾibah. Not every case mentioned should therefore be treated as an instance of that single designation without evidence.

Al-Anʿām 6:139 gives another example: what was in the animals' bellies was assigned to men and forbidden to their wives; if born dead, both shared in it. Interpretations differ over the precise referent, but the verse clearly describes a distinction between recipients.

These verses show that more than naming animals was involved: a set of religious and social rules had been devised that affected access to life's resources.

Prohibiting unsupported attribution of permission and prohibition to The God

Al-Naḥl 16:116 states:

وَلَا تَقُولُوا لِمَا تَصِفُ أَلْسِنَتُكُمُ الْكَذِبَ هَٰذَا حَلَالٌ وَهَٰذَا حَرَامٌ لِتَفْتَرُوا عَلَى اللَّهِ الْكَذِبَ إِنَّ الَّذِينَ يَفْتَرُونَ عَلَى اللَّهِ الْكَذِبَ لَا يُفْلِحُونَ

wa-lā taqūlū li-mā taṣifu alsinatukumu al-kadhiba hādhā ḥalālun wa-hādhā ḥarāmun li-taftarū ʿalā allāhi al-kadhiba inna alladhīna yaftarūna ʿalā allāhi al-kadhiba lā yufliḥūn

Al-Naḥl 16:116

Do not say, on the basis of the falsehood your tongues describe, “This is lawful and this is forbidden,” so as to fabricate lies against The God. Those who fabricate lies against The God do not prosper.

This principle clarifies the criticism in Al-Māʾidah 5:103.

Letting an animal loose, declining to use it, or giving it to another person is not itself inventing a religious rule. But a claim that The God forbids its use requires divine evidence for that attribution.

Al-Anʿām 6:150 likewise calls on those claiming a divine prohibition to produce testimony:

قُلْ هَلُمَّ شُهَدَاءَكُمُ الَّذِينَ يَشْهَدُونَ أَنَّ اللَّهَ حَرَّمَ هَٰذَا

qul halumma shuhadāʾakumu alladhīna yashhadūna anna allāha ḥarrama hādhā

Al-Anʿām 6:150

Say, “Bring your witnesses who testify that The God has forbidden this.”

The issue therefore extends beyond an ancient custom: people cannot invent permissions and prohibitions in The God's name.

Following ancestors versus what has been revealed

Immediately after the sāʾibah verse, Al-Māʾidah 5:104 states:

وَإِذَا قِيلَ لَهُمْ تَعَالَوْا إِلَىٰ مَا أَنْزَلَ اللَّهُ وَإِلَى الرَّسُولِ قَالُوا حَسْبُنَا مَا وَجَدْنَا عَلَيْهِ آبَاءَنَا أَوَلَوْ كَانَ آبَاؤُهُمْ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ شَيْئًا وَلَا يَهْتَدُونَ

wa-idhā qīla lahum taʿālaw ilā mā anzala allāhu wa-ilā al-rasūli qālū ḥasbunā mā wajadnā ʿalayhi ābāʾanā a-wa-law kāna ābāʾuhum lā yaʿlamūna shayʾan wa-lā yahtadūn

Al-Māʾidah 5:104

When they are told, “Come to what The God has sent down and to the Messenger,” they say, “What we found our fathers following is enough for us.” Even if their fathers knew nothing and were not guided?

This connection reveals another dimension. Designations such as sāʾibah could acquire apparent religious authority because they had long been practiced and inherited from earlier generations.

A custom's long history, however, does not by itself demonstrate divine origin.

Rather than unconditional acceptance of ancestral inheritance, the Qur'an here calls for turning to what The God revealed and exercising reason.

This distinguishes knowing a practice's history from accepting its religious authority. Longstanding practice does not automatically invalidate a custom or make it a divine command. Attributing it to The God requires independent evidence.

6. S Y B and related words

S Y B (sayb) and S R Ḥ (sarḥ)

Sarḥ concerns sending livestock out, going to pasture, and moving in open space. In Al-Naḥl 16:6, tasraḥūna refers to taking livestock out to graze.

Tasrīḥ and tasyīb can thus overlap in releasing or sending out animals.

In livestock usage, sarḥ can emphasize sending animals to pasture, while tasyīb emphasizes leaving them loose and removing a previous restraint.

The two may occur together in practice, but this does not make their roots or all their uses identical.

S Y B (sayb) and S Y L (sayl)

Both families relate to movement and flow.

Sayl denotes flowing water or a flood, and the Qur'an uses the family for the flowing of water and floods.

S Y B has a different range: besides water flowing, it is recorded for a snake's movement, releasing livestock, and giving.

Sayl should not be classified under S Y B merely because the words sound similar. They are distinct roots with partially overlapping semantic fields.

S Y B (sayb) and H M L (hamal)

Ihmāl commonly denotes leaving something, inattention, or failure to care for it. Something released may in some cases also be left unattended.

Tasyīb, however, does not necessarily mean carelessness or irresponsibility. Someone may deliberately release an animal or make something available to another person.

In modern Arabic, tasayyub often describes disorder, lack of discipline, or undesirable laxity. This contemporary negative sense should not automatically be read into Qur'anic sāʾibah.

Al-Māʾidah 5:103 criticizes an invented religious designation, not every freedom of movement or act of release.

7. Distinguishing release, giving, and inventing religious rules

One central distinction is that releasing something is one action, while making a religious rule about what has been released is another.

Someone might release an animal to graze, grant someone else its use, or exempt it from difficult work. These are different acts, each requiring assessment according to its subject and circumstances.

Sayb in the sense of a gift also shows that relinquishing something can be beneficial and good.

In the sāʾibah custom criticized by the Qur'an, however, release belonged to a framework of beliefs and restrictions attributed to The God.

The objection to that custom should not be transferred into the lexical meaning of release itself.

Defining sāʾibah as “a forbidden animal” confuses the designation of a condition, the rules people imposed upon it, and the claim that those rules were divine.

Conversely, simply saying “a free animal,” without explaining the accompanying social and religious rules, does not adequately clarify the particular use in Al-Māʾidah 5:103.

A precise definition should preserve both levels: the linguistic sense of release, and its historical use as a special designation for certain livestock.

8. Suggested renderings for Forqan

For sāba yasību referring to water, “flow” or “run” is appropriate. For a snake or other creature, “move along,” “move swiftly,” or “move” may fit the context.

Sayyaba can be rendered “released,” “let loose,” or “left to itself.” The choice depends on the object and circumstances.

Insāba can often be expressed as “flowed,” “glided along,” or “flowed gently.” Gentleness or speed must be supported by context in each use.

For sayb meaning a gift, “giving,” “bounty,” or “a bestowed benefit” is appropriate.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:103, however, sāʾibah is a recognized historical designation. Retaining the name in the base translation and explaining it in a word note preserves this better than “a released animal” alone, which does not convey the full social and religious significance.

The current Forqan translation reads:

The God has instituted no bahirah, sa’ibah, wasilah, or ham. But those who disbelieve fabricate lies against The God, and most of them do not reason.

This appropriately retains the four designations without inserting interpretive details and relates the negation of jaʿala to not instituting those categories.

A word note could read:

“Sāʾibah: an animal left loose; in the custom addressed by the Qur'an, a designation for certain livestock whose use people restricted on the basis of particular beliefs.”

This preserves both the root meaning and the word's particular use in the verse.

Conclusion

S Y B concerns flowing, free movement, and release. Water flows, a snake moves along, and an animal left loose can move without a previous restraint.

Sayb also means a gift or bounty. Its semantic relationship to relinquishing and conveying something is understandable, but the historical order of these senses is uncertain. Sayābah, an unripe date, is also an early word and should not be forced into the same central image without evidence.

Only sāʾibah occurs in the Qur'an. In Al-Māʾidah 5:103 it names livestock that people released according to their customs and beliefs, imposing restrictions on their use.

The Qur'anic criticism concerns attributing those categories and restrictions to The God. Release, giving, or personal choice is not identical to inventing a divine rule.

Al-Anʿām 6:138, Al-Anʿām 6:139, Al-Anʿām 6:150, and Al-Naḥl 16:116 place such claims within the broader question of declaring things lawful or forbidden without divine evidence. Al-Māʾidah 5:104 also warns that following ancestors does not replace knowledge and evidence.

Final definition: In its main branch, S Y B concerns flowing and moving freely; its transitive form concerns release and removing restraint. Qur'anic sāʾibah refers to an animal released within a recognized custom and subjected to special rules. Its lexical meaning is being left loose, while the Qur'anic rejection concerns the invented religious authority of those rules and their attribution to The God, not release or freedom as such.
