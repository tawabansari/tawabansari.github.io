From gravel and pelting with stones to what is cast into a fire

The Arabic root ح ص ب concerns gravel and pebbles, throwing them, and phenomena that set stones and dust in motion. Within this family, حَاصِب describes a wind or phenomenon that carries and hurls gravel, stones, or other particles. The root has another use as well: حَصَب can name something cast into a fire that contributes to its burning.

The root occurs in two Qur’anic forms: حَاصِبًا in four verses and حَصَبُ in one. The first four concern a pelting punishment; the fifth occurs in حَصَبُ جَهَنَّمَ. Understanding the relationship between these branches matters for accurate translation.

Qur’anic forms: حَاصِبًا, an accusative active participle in four verses; and حَصَبُ, a nominative noun in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:98.

Other forms, such as حَصْبَاء, حَصَبَ, حَصَّبَ, مُحَصَّب, and حَصْبَة, are recorded in Arabic but do not occur in the Qur’an.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: four active participles and one noun

1. The semantic core and Arabic usage

In the root’s concrete usage, حَصْبَاء means gravel or pebbles. حَصَبَهُ بِالْحَصْبَاءِ means pelting someone with pebbles. The person is the target, while بِالْحَصْبَاءِ identifies what is thrown.

Early dictionaries also apply حَصَب to gravel and stones. حَصْب, with an unvowelled ṣād, is the verbal noun of pelting with pebbles. The vocalization matters: one form can identify the material or what is thrown, while the other names the act of throwing.

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris traces this family’s basis to a material of the earth—gravel or pebbles—and explains other uses in connection with it.

Sources record several familiar uses related to this concrete meaning.

حَاصِب: of wind, a wind that raises or carries gravel and dust. Some lexical accounts also apply it to a cloud or phenomenon throwing hail or icy particles. The hurling of particles matters; the word should not be generalized to every strong wind or storm.

حَصَّبَ الْمَوْضِعَ: strewing or covering a place with gravel. Here the pebbles are spread over the ground rather than thrown at someone.

مُحَصَّب: ground containing or covered with pebbles. The word also occurs in place names, including geographical accounts of Mecca and Minā. Its geographical meaning does not by itself establish a religious ruling or rite.

حَصْبَة: also recorded for a skin eruption. Ibn Fāris relates this use to the visual resemblance of scattered spots to pebbles. That lexical explanation does not mean every skin disease or condition is named from this root.

Lexical explanations connect these uses with the concrete image of gravel and pebbles, but not every derivative refers to one action. The reference may be to gravel itself, throwing it, spreading it on the ground, or something named through resemblance. The semantic connection alone does not establish the historical order in which every use arose.

2. Early witnesses and extensions of meaning

Early Arabic poetry also uses this family for fierce, dust-raising winds. A line attributed to Labīd ibn Rabīʿah reads:

جَرَّتْ عَلَيْهَا أَنْ خَوَتْ مِنْ أَهْلِهَا أَذْيَالَهَا كُلُّ عَصُوفٍ حَصِبَهْ

jarrat ʿalayhā an khawat min ahlihā adhyālahā kullu ʿaṣūfin ḥaṣibah

That is: when the dwellings became empty of their inhabitants, every fierce, gravel-bearing wind dragged its skirts over them.

Here حَصِبَة describes a wind raising dust and gravel. Its attribution to Labīd is disputed in literary sources, however, so this line alone cannot establish a securely pre-Islamic date. The Dīwān commentary reports that Al-Jawharī attributed it to Labīd, while Al-Ṣaghānī rejected the attribution.

Labīd’s Dīwān: Al-Jawharī and Al-Ṣaghānī on the disputed attribution

A line attributed to Al-Aʿshā also uses حَاصِب for a military force. Lexicographers explain it as archers or numerous infantry; commentary on the poem compares the force to a pelting wind advancing and stirring the ground.

This shows a literary transfer from a natural phenomenon to a human force with similar behavior or effect. It does not make حاصب mean an army, archers, or human force in every context.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn: Al-Aʿshā’s witness and the military comparison

Another semantic branch concerns fire. Lexical accounts record حَصَبَ النَّارَ for casting something into a fire and kindling it. حَصَب also names what is cast into a fire or oven and contributes to its burning.

The early lexicographer Al-Farrāʾ reports حَصَب as firewood in the language of Yemen and as something thrown into a fire in the language of Najd. This attests two lexical explanations; it does not prove that every speaker in either region used the word exclusively that way.

Two elements should therefore remain distinct in this branch: casting something into a fire and using it as fuel. They can coincide in a particular use without being identical.

Al-Farrāʾ, Maʿānī al-Qurʾān: reported Yemeni and Najdī uses

3. Ḥāṣib in Qur’anic usage

Ḥāṣib on land: Al-Isrāʾ 17:68

أَفَأَمِنْتُمْ أَنْ يَخْسِفَ بِكُمْ جَانِبَ الْبَرِّ أَوْ يُرْسِلَ عَلَيْكُمْ حَاصِبًا ثُمَّ لَا تَجِدُوا لَكُمْ وَكِيلًا

a-fa-amintum an yakhsifa bikum jāniba al-barri aw yursila ʿalaykum ḥāṣiban thumma lā tajidū lakum wakīlan

Suggested translation: “Do you feel secure that He will not cause a part of the land to swallow you, or send upon you a stone-pelting wind, and then you will find no one to take charge of your protection?”

Here ḥāṣib occurs alongside the ground swallowing people. The preceding verses describe rescue at sea: once danger passes and people reach land, they may imagine themselves safe.

The verse makes clear that land is not beyond danger or harm. The ground swallowing people and the sending of a ḥāṣib are two different dangers.

حاصب occurs with يُرْسِلَ عَلَيْكُمْ, something sent upon the people addressed. “A stone-pelting wind” suits its lexical meaning, but the word alone does not specify stone size, exact wind strength, or the event’s physical mechanism.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:69 introduces another danger at sea with قَاصِفًا مِنَ الرِّيحِ. These expressions should not be equated: قاصف and حاصب belong to distinct roots and have their own semantic features. The verse does not say رِيحًا قَاصِفًا; an Arabic quotation must preserve its actual construction.

Ḥāṣib in accounts of earlier peoples: Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:40

فَكُلًّا أَخَذْنَا بِذَنْبِهِ فَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ أَرْسَلْنَا عَلَيْهِ حَاصِبًا وَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ أَخَذَتْهُ الصَّيْحَةُ وَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ خَسَفْنَا بِهِ الْأَرْضَ وَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ أَغْرَقْنَا وَمَا كَانَ اللَّهُ لِيَظْلِمَهُمْ وَلَٰكِنْ كَانُوا أَنْفُسَهُمْ يَظْلِمُونَ

fa-kullan akhadhnā bi-dhanbihi fa-minhum man arsalnā ʿalayhi ḥāṣiban wa-minhum man akhadhat-hu al-ṣayḥatu wa-minhum man khasafnā bihi al-arḍa wa-minhum man aghraqnā wa-mā kāna allāhu li-yaẓlimahum wa-lākin kānū anfusahum yaẓlimūna

Suggested translation: “We seized each for his sin: upon some We sent a stone-pelting phenomenon; some were seized by the mighty cry; some We caused the earth to swallow, and some We drowned. God did not wrong them; they wronged themselves.”

Ḥāṣib is one among several kinds of punishment here. الصَّيْحَة, خَسْف, and غَرَق are named separately. Translating ḥāṣib merely as punishment would erase distinctions the verse itself makes.

The verse does not explain precisely how the ḥāṣib arose, how large its stones were, or its natural mechanism. The lexical family supports pelting with stones, but the word alone does not establish the physical details.

Ḥāṣib in the account of Lot’s people: Al-Qamar 54:34

إِنَّا أَرْسَلْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ حَاصِبًا إِلَّا آلَ لُوطٍ نَجَّيْنَاهُمْ بِسَحَرٍ

innā arsalnā ʿalayhim ḥāṣiban illā āla lūṭin najjaynāhum bi-saḥarin

Suggested translation: “We sent upon them a shower of stones, except Lot’s household, whom We rescued at dawn.”

This verse helps identify the Qur’anic referent of ḥāṣib. Other accounts of the same event mention stones being sent down.

Hūd 11:82 mentions stones of سِجِّيل. Al-Ḥijr 15:74 also describes stones raining upon them, while Al-Dhāriyāt 51:33 uses حِجَارَةً مِنْ طِينٍ.

In the account of Lot’s people, these verses directly connect ḥāṣib with falling stones. A shower or rain of stones is therefore more precise here than storm alone.

The name ḥāṣib does not establish that an ordinary wind necessarily carried the stones. The verse clearly describes a pelting punishment being sent; the word does not specify its precise mechanism.

Ḥāṣib as a warning: Al-Mulk 67:17

أَمْ أَمِنْتُمْ مَنْ فِي السَّمَاءِ أَنْ يُرْسِلَ عَلَيْكُمْ حَاصِبًا فَسَتَعْلَمُونَ كَيْفَ نَذِيرِ

am amintum man fī al-samāʾi an yursila ʿalaykum ḥāṣiban fa-sa-taʿlamūna kayfa nadhīri

Suggested translation: “Or do you feel secure from the One in heaven, that He will not send upon you a stone-pelting phenomenon? Then you will know what My warning is like.”

Again, ḥāṣib accompanies يُرْسِلَ and the preposition عَلَى. The audience is warned of the possibility of a fearsome phenomenon being sent upon them.

The preceding verse, Al-Mulk 67:16, mentions the earth swallowing people. This resembles Al-Isrāʾ 17:68, where that danger and ḥāṣib likewise appear as distinct possibilities.

Al-Mulk 67:17 warns about a possible event rather than reporting a particular ḥāṣib occurring then. It does not justify declaring every present-day storm or shower of stones divine punishment of particular people without independent evidence.

The four occurrences emphasize pelting and the throwing of particles. A fierce wind is a clear natural instance; in Lot’s story, other verses emphasize stones falling.

4. حَصَبُ جَهَنَّمَ: Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:98

إِنَّكُمْ وَمَا تَعْبُدُونَ مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ حَصَبُ جَهَنَّمَ أَنْتُمْ لَهَا وَارِدُونَ

innakum wa-mā taʿbudūna min dūni allāhi ḥaṣabu jahannama antum lahā wāridūna

Suggested translation: “You and what you worship apart from God are what is cast into Hell and becomes its fuel; you will enter it.”

Unlike the preceding four occurrences, حَصَبُ does not name something sent against someone. It occurs in a nominal sentence, predicating something of the audience and what they worship. Grammatically, حَصَبُ is the nominative predicate of إِنَّ and the first term of a genitive construction with جَهَنَّمَ. The latter is genitive in function, marked with fatḥah because it is a diptote.

Two principal readings are relevant to its lexical analysis.

First reading: what is cast into a fire.

On the basis of حَصَبَ as throwing or casting, حَصَبُ جَهَنَّمَ refers to things cast into Hell.

Al-Ṭabarī compares this with ḥāṣib from the same root and favors casting into the fire as an interpretation. This reading centers on throwing and what is thrown.

Al-Ṭabarī: preference for being cast into Jahannam

Second reading: fuel for the fire.

Because حَصَب is recorded for something placed in an oven or fire, the expression can refer to Hell’s fuel.

Lexicographers also distinguish wood not yet used for burning from something actually cast into a fire. In this explanation, حَصَب is not merely a piece of wood: it identifies something introduced into a fire to kindle or feed it.

The readings are related. Something cast into a fire can also fuel it. Their difference should remain visible in translation: the first emphasizes being cast in, the second being fuel.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: casting into a fire and fuel

Other verses about Hell’s fuel

Al-Baqarah 2:24 and Al-Taḥrīm 66:6 describe a fire whose وَقُودُهَا النَّاسُ وَالْحِجَارَةُ: its fuel is people and stones.

Al-Jinn 72:15 also uses حَطَبًا in connection with Hell.

Independently of حَصَب, the Qur’an thus describes people and stones as fuel. Reading Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:98 as Hell’s fuel fits that wider network of verses.

Nevertheless, حَصَب, حَطَب, and وَقُود differ in root and structure. Sharing a context does not make them complete synonyms in every usage.

Study of W Q D

The scope of وَمَا تَعْبُدُونَ

The root study primarily concerns the meaning of حَصَب, but its accompanying phrase also matters.

The verse addresses those who have taken objects of worship apart from God, mentioning them and their objects of worship. Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:99 then describes those objects’ inability to possess divinity.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:101, meanwhile, describes those for whom good has preceded from God as kept far from that punishment. Isolating وَمَا تَعْبُدُونَ from context therefore does not establish that every righteous person whom others wrongly worship shares that fate because of the worshippers’ actions.

The lexical meaning of حَصَب and identifying the persons or things covered by the statement are different questions. Lexical research clarifies the first; context and other verses address the second.

5. Distinctions from nearby roots and translation proposals

ح ص ب resembles several word families in form or meaning, but should not be equated with them.

Ḥ Ṣ B (حَصَب) and Ḥ Ṣ Y (حَصَى): حَصَى means pebbles, and حَصْبَاء concerns the same concrete material. Their root forms differ, however. ح ص ب has particular uses for pelting, a stone-pelting wind, and material thrown into a fire. Counting and enumeration should not be transferred to it through its resemblance to حصى.

Ḥ Ṣ B (حَصَب) and Ḥ S B (حَسَب): the second root has sīn, not ṣād, as its middle consonant. Reckoning, ḥasab, and many uses of supposing belong to ح س ب. Even if expressions from both families occur in adverse contexts, contextual resemblance does not make the roots identical.

Study of Ḥ S B

Ḥ Ṣ B (حَصَب) and Ḥ Ṭ B (حَطَب): حَطَب commonly means firewood or fuel. حَصَب in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:98 also concerns Hell’s fuel while retaining a connection with casting something into a fire. Firewood can convey part of that meaning, but not the root’s entire lexical range.

Forqan’s translations should distinguish the two Qur’anic forms.

For حَاصِبًا, suitable renderings include a stone-pelting wind and a shower of stones. The former suits the lexical usage in Al-Isrāʾ 17:68 and Al-Mulk 67:17. In Al-Qamar 54:34, other verses about Lot’s people favor a shower of stones. In Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:40, a pelting punishment is possible, though a shower of stones or stone-pelting storm preserves the concrete image more clearly.

For حَصَبُ جَهَنَّمَ, Hell’s fuel is brief and natural. A lexical explanation can preserve its distinction from حَطَب and وَقُود by noting its connection with what is cast into a fire.

A basic translation should not become cumbersome or unnatural merely to retain a root image. Subtle lexical distinctions can remain in the study while the verse’s translation stays clear and natural.

Conclusion

ح ص ب concerns gravel, pebbles, and throwing. حَصْبَاء names the concrete material; حَصَبَ can express pelting with pebbles; and حَاصِب in natural settings describes a phenomenon raising and moving gravel, stones, or other particles.

Pelting is prominent in the four Qur’anic occurrences of حَاصِبًا. They occur in warnings about danger on land and accounts of earlier peoples’ fate. Verses about Lot’s people further clarify its connection with falling stones.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:98, حَصَب represents another branch: something cast into a fire and serving there as fuel. Hell’s fuel fits this context, while its lexical connection with casting into the fire should remain clear.

Brief root meaning: The root’s concrete core concerns gravel, pebbles, and pelting with them; its fire-related branch concerns material cast into a fire and burned. Both branches can be distinguished in Qur’anic usage, and accurate translation requires interpreting each derivative through its own structure and context.
