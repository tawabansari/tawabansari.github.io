Root w-ṭ-r — Need, desire, and fulfillment of a purpose

Qur’anic and lexical forms

وَطَرًا
(waṭaran)
Need or desire; Qur’anic noun

أَوْطَار
(awṭār)
Desires; non-Qur’anic plural

قَضَىٰ مِنْهَا وَطَرًا
(qaḍā minhā waṭaran)
Qur’anic phrase with qaḍā

قَضَوْا مِنْهُنَّ وَطَرًا
(qaḍaw minhunna waṭaran)
Plural verb and pronoun; singular waṭar

The Arabic root w-ṭ-r concerns a need, desire, or purpose that someone is intent on attaining. Waṭar is something a person seeks; when the purpose is achieved or the undertaking completed, one says qaḍā waṭarahu: he fulfilled his desire or completed his business.

The root occurs twice in a single Qur’anic verse, Al-Aḥzāb 33:37. Both occurrences concern marriage: first Zayd and his wife, then a general provision concerning the wives of adopted sons.

Lexically, waṭar is restricted neither to marriage nor to sexual intercourse. It can concern a task, journey, meeting, relationship, or personal goal.

The essential distinction is that waṭar names a need or purpose, while qaḍāʾ al-waṭar concerns its fulfillment or completion. Context identifies the desire, the circumstances of fulfillment, and the consequences.

1. The root in early Arabic

Waṭar: a need someone seeks to fulfill

In early Arabic, waṭar denoted a need or desire in whose fulfillment someone took an interest.

Classical lexical sources explain it through ḥājah, arab, and bughyah.

These equivalents should not be treated as identical in every respect.

Ḥājah can denote a need of any kind.

Bughyah concerns something sought.

Arab likewise concerns a need or purpose someone is intent on fulfilling.

Waṭar belongs to this semantic field, particularly a desire whose fulfillment matters to the person.

Older lexical explanations state that when someone attains the desired object, one says:

قَضَى وَطَرَهُ

qaḍā waṭarahu

“He attained his purpose” or “fulfilled his desire.”

This expression is particularly relevant because the Qur’an likewise combines waṭar with qaḍā.

Poetry attributed to Rabīʿ ibn Ḍabʿ al-Fazārī

A poem attributed to the early Arab poet Rabīʿ ibn Ḍabʿ al-Fazārī says:

وَدَّعَنَا قَبْلَ أَنْ نُوَدِّعَهُ

waddaʿanā qabla an nuwaddiʿahu

لَمَّا قَضَى مِنْ جِمَاعِنَا وَطَرًا

lammā qaḍā min jimāʿinā waṭarā

“Youth bade us farewell before we bade it farewell, having fulfilled its purpose in our companionship.”

The line belongs to verses about youth passing and old age arriving.

The preceding line personifies youth as a living companion that eventually departs.

Waṭar here is a desire attributed figuratively to youth; the end of its companionship follows from the image, rather than being an independent meaning of the noun.

The poet does not say that youth was killed or committed wrongdoing. He describes its departure through fulfillment of a purpose.

The expression can thus portray a period or relationship reaching its end, rather than solely satisfaction of a bodily need.

The wording given here is attested in transmitted poetry. Other transmissions have fāraqanā qabla an nufāriqahu, “it left us before we left it,” in the first half-line. Differences in transmission should be distinguished from the lexical meaning of waṭar.

Across these formulations, youth is depicted as departing after its companionship with the person.

Literary sources place Rabīʿ ibn Ḍabʿ in the pre-Islamic period near the beginning of Islam. The precise date of this line is not independently established: it is evidence from an attributed early poetic tradition, rather than undisputed proof of a pre-Qur’anic date for this exact wording.

Another witness to the broader usage

Abū al-Najm al-ʿIjlī, an Umayyad-period poet, says:

يَوْمَ قَدَرْنَا وَالْعَزِيزُ مَنْ قَدَرْ

yawma qadarnā wa-al-ʿazīzu man qadar

وَآبَتِ الْخَيْلُ وَقَضَّيْنَا الْوَطَرْ

wa-ābati al-khaylu wa-qaḍḍaynā al-waṭar

“On the day we prevailed—and mighty is whoever prevails—the horses returned, and we accomplished our purpose.”

Qaḍḍaynā al-waṭar here concerns accomplishing the intended goal.

The subject is victory and returning horses, not marriage.

This witness is post-Islamic. It illustrates continuity of usage, but cannot substitute for pre-Islamic evidence.

Together, poetry and lexicons attest waṭar as a person’s desire or purpose, and qaḍāʾ al-waṭar as attaining it or completing the associated undertaking.

Must waṭar mean appetite or lust?

Some lexical explanations associate waṭar with desire, inclination, and affection.

This is understandable because people also have wishes within emotional and marital relationships.

Shahwah does not exhaust the meaning of waṭar.

Someone pursuing and attaining a goal can be described through qaḍāʾ al-waṭar without a sexual desire being involved.

Three levels should therefore be distinguished: the word’s general meaning, its application to emotional or marital relationships, and the particular situation of a verse.

Its Qur’anic use in marriage should not become the definition of the entire lexical family.

2. Forms and semantic core

The family’s principal Arabic forms are limited.

Waṭar: a need, desire, or purpose someone is intent on attaining.

Awṭār: the plural of waṭar; desires or purposes.

Qaḍā waṭarahu: he fulfilled his desire, attained his purpose, or completed his undertaking.

Qaḍā minhu waṭaran: he attained a desire through something or someone, or completed the associated need or undertaking.

Older lexicons note that they did not recognize a commonly used independent verb from w-ṭ-r in this sense of need or purpose.

Qaḍā waṭarahu is a combination of words belonging to two different roots.

Qaḍā derives from q-ḍ-y.

Waṭar derives from w-ṭ-r.

They should not be merged into one root.

Qur’anic forms

The root occurs twice, both times as waṭaran.

Both are in Al-Aḥzāb 33:37:

فَلَمَّا قَضَىٰ زَيْدٌ مِنْهَا وَطَرًا

fa-lammā qaḍā Zaydun minhā waṭarā

Al-Aḥzāb 33:37

إِذَا قَضَوْا مِنْهُنَّ وَطَرًا

idhā qaḍaw minhunna waṭarā

Al-Aḥzāb 33:37

Waṭaran is singular, indefinite, and accusative in both phrases, functioning as the object of qaḍā or its plural form qaḍaw.

The Qur’an contains neither the plural awṭār nor an independent verb or another derivative of this root.

This absence does not mean that Arabic lacks the plural or that its absence proves an intended rejection of it. The observable fact is simply that waṭaran is the Qur’anic form.

Semantic core

The core meaning is:

A need or desire someone is intent on fulfilling and through which they seek their purpose.

Qaḍā waṭaran adds another element:

Attaining the desire, fulfilling the need, or completing the associated undertaking.

The phrase’s meaning therefore comes from two components.

Waṭar names the desire.

Qaḍā expresses accomplishing or completing it.

Waṭar itself does not mean divorce, separation, or the ending of a marriage.

These may be contextual consequences, but are not independent lexical meanings of the noun.

3. Al-Aḥzāb 33:37 — Two occurrences in one verse

وَإِذْ تَقُولُ لِلَّذِي أَنْعَمَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِ وَأَنْعَمْتَ عَلَيْهِ أَمْسِكْ عَلَيْكَ زَوْجَكَ وَاتَّقِ اللَّهَ وَتُخْفِي فِي نَفْسِكَ مَا اللَّهُ مُبْدِيهِ وَتَخْشَى النَّاسَ وَاللَّهُ أَحَقُّ أَنْ تَخْشَاهُ ۖ فَلَمَّا قَضَىٰ زَيْدٌ مِنْهَا وَطَرًا زَوَّجْنَاكَهَا لِكَيْ لَا يَكُونَ عَلَى الْمُؤْمِنِينَ حَرَجٌ فِي أَزْوَاجِ أَدْعِيَائِهِمْ إِذَا قَضَوْا مِنْهُنَّ وَطَرًا ۚ وَكَانَ أَمْرُ اللَّهِ مَفْعُولًا

wa-idh taqūlu li-alladhī anʿama Allāhu ʿalayhi wa-anʿamta ʿalayhi amsik ʿalayka zawjaka wa-ittaqi Allāha wa-tukhfī fī nafsika mā Allāhu mubdīhi wa-takhshā al-nāsa wa-Allāhu aḥaqqu an takhshāhu fa-lammā qaḍā Zaydun minhā waṭaran zawwajnākahā li-kay lā yakūna ʿalā al-muʾminīna ḥarajun fī azwāji adʿiyāʾihim idhā qaḍaw minhunna waṭaran wa-kāna amru Allāhi mafʿūlā

Al-Aḥzāb 33:37

“And when you were saying to the one whom God had favored and whom you had favored, ‘Keep your wife with you and be mindful of God,’ while concealing within yourself what God would disclose, and fearing people, though God was more entitled to your fear. Then, when Zayd’s relationship with her had run its course, We married her to you, so that believers should face no constraint concerning the wives of their adopted sons once their relationships with them have run their course. And God’s command is to be accomplished.”

A specific event in Zayd’s life is followed by the stated purpose of a general provision concerning adopted sons.

This connection matters: the expression recurs with a plural subject and pronouns in the general provision, while waṭaran itself remains singular in both occurrences.

First occurrence: fa-lammā qaḍā Zaydun minhā waṭaran

Zaydun is the subject of qaḍā.

Minhā refers to his wife.

Waṭaran names the desire or need under discussion.

Fa-lammā supplies the temporal connection: when that situation had come about.

The construction describes Zayd reaching the completion of his desire or undertaking in relation to his wife.

Qaḍā and waṭar together form a familiar Arabic expression.

Translation should convey the phrase naturally, rather than depend on either word in isolation.

The Persian wording “his business with her came to an end” conveys this contextually; English can say “his relationship with her had run its course.”

To make the need or desire more explicit, one could say “he brought his desire and need in that relationship to completion.”

This is heavier wording and may misleadingly suggest a single, specific bodily need.

The marital relationship’s ending supplies the relevant context.

Is fulfillment of waṭar itself divorce?

Qaḍā waṭarahu means attaining a purpose or completing a desire.

Ṭalāq derives from ṭ-l-q and has a separate legal meaning.

Study of ṭ-l-q

Waṭar is therefore not lexically identical to divorce.

The two can nevertheless belong to one event.

A marital relationship may reach a point where its continuation ends and separation follows.

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:37, Muhammad had told Zayd to retain his wife; subsequently, after Zayd’s relationship with her had ended, she marries Muhammad.

This sequence indicates that the earlier marriage had ended.

Qaḍā minhā waṭaran does not itself enumerate every legal stage in ending a marriage.

Two matters should thus remain distinct:

The phrase expresses completion of the desire or undertaking associated with the relationship.

Termination of the earlier marriage is understood through context and the subsequent marriage.

Does it refer only to intercourse?

Lexical and exegetical explanations sometimes interpret the phrase here through marital enjoyment or intercourse.

That reading merits consideration because the context is a husband and wife.

The general lexical meaning of waṭar is nevertheless not restricted to intercourse.

The verse does not use other Qur’anic expressions more directly associated with sexual relations.

The reading adopted here takes the expression as the completion of Zayd’s relationship and associated desire; it should not be presented as the only recorded explanation.

An assertion that it necessarily refers exclusively to intercourse would require evidence narrowing the word’s broader range to that particular act.

Likewise, the phrase alone does not establish that Zayd subsequently felt no affection whatsoever for his wife.

A general noun cannot establish detailed conclusions about someone’s inner feelings and motives.

Zawwajnākahā: a separate event

After the expression concerning Zayd comes:

زَوَّجْنَاكَهَا

zawwajnākahā

Al-Aḥzāb 33:37

“We married her to you.”

This verb derives from z-w-j.

Two events are therefore distinguished.

First, the ending of Zayd’s earlier relationship.

Second, the woman’s marriage to Muhammad.

The noun waṭar neither creates the second marriage nor means transferring a woman from one man’s ownership to another’s.

She is described as a wife, and the second marriage is expressed through a separate verb.

The phrase should not portray a woman as an object automatically passed to someone else after another person has finished using it.

The verse describes the ending of one relationship and the formation of another marriage, to be understood alongside the relevant marital rights and rules.

4. Second occurrence: idhā qaḍaw minhunna waṭaran

The latter part of the verse reads:

لِكَيْ لَا يَكُونَ عَلَى الْمُؤْمِنِينَ حَرَجٌ فِي أَزْوَاجِ أَدْعِيَائِهِمْ إِذَا قَضَوْا مِنْهُنَّ وَطَرًا

li-kay lā yakūna ʿalā al-muʾminīna ḥarajun fī azwāji adʿiyāʾihim idhā qaḍaw minhunna waṭarā

Al-Aḥzāb 33:37

So that believers should face no constraint regarding the wives of their adopted sons once those sons’ relationships with the women have run their course.

Unlike the first phrase, the subject here is plural:

قَضَوْا

qaḍaw

Al-Aḥzāb 33:37

“They accomplished.”

Minhunna also has a feminine plural pronoun, referring to the wives of the adopted sons.

Waṭaran remains a singular indefinite noun.

The wording moves from Zayd’s particular experience to a general situation.

The particular event and the general provision

The first part names Zayd.

The second refers to adʿiyāʾihim, those recognized as adopted sons.

The stated purpose therefore extends beyond one person’s marriage.

The broader issue is the distinction between biological and adopted sons in marriage provisions.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:4 and Al-Aḥzāb 33:5 establish this distinction earlier.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:4 says that God has not made adopted sons biological sons.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:5 directs that they be attributed to their fathers; if the fathers are unknown, they are brothers in religion and associates.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:37 continues this distinction between actual lineage and adoption as a designation.

Biological and adopted sons

Al-Nisāʾ 4:23 includes the wives of biological sons among prohibited marriage relationships.

It specifies alladhīna min aṣlābikum: sons descended from you.

This qualification matters to the provision’s scope.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:37, by contrast, concerns the wives of adopted sons.

Rules concerning biological children cannot simply be transferred wholesale to adopted children without evidence.

This legal distinction does not negate affection, care, or moral responsibility toward a child raised in a family.

These verses specifically concern lineage and its legal consequences.

Why repeat qaḍaw minhunna waṭaran?

Repetition shows that the relevant condition is not exclusive to Zayd’s life.

The context concerns an adopted son’s earlier marriage having ended.

In that situation, adoption alone is not to create the same marriage impediment as biological sonship.

This does not authorize marriage to someone who remains another person’s wife.

The context involves an earlier relationship ending before a subsequent one begins.

Waṭar itself does not determine every requirement involving marriage, separation, the waiting period, or the parties’ consent.

Those matters require examination of the relevant verses on marriage and divorce.

Study of n-k-ḥ

Thus fulfillment of waṭar here expresses the completion of the undertaking or desire within the earlier relationship; it neither names a particular legal procedure nor permits other marriage provisions to be disregarded.

5. Comparison with related roots

Waṭar compared with ḥājah

Ḥājah concerns human need.

Study of ḥ-w-j

Waṭar belongs to the same field.

Lexical explanations emphasize a desire someone is intent on attaining.

“Need” is defensible, although “desire” or “purpose” may fit some sentences better.

A person may need water to survive.

They may also wish to meet someone, complete a task, or achieve a goal.

Both involve need or desire, without the same degree of bodily necessity.

Waṭar should not always be restricted to vital need or compulsion.

Waṭar compared with arab

Arab is among the closest lexical equivalents.

Lexicons sometimes explain each through the other.

Both can denote need, purpose, or something sought.

In Ṭā Hā 20:18, Moses describes his staff as having maʾāribu ukhrā for him: other needs or uses.

The ʾ-r-b family can therefore concern practical human purposes.

Its closeness to waṭar does not establish complete synonymy in every construction and historical period.

Qur’anic waṭar occurs only with qaḍā, while ʾ-r-b has other forms and applications.

Comparison should preserve those actual differences.

Waṭar compared with bughyah

Bughyah denotes something sought.

In this respect it is related to waṭar.

Qur’anic b-gh-y has several branches, including seeking and requesting, and in some contexts transgression or rebellion.

Its entire range should not be transferred to w-ṭ-r.

Waṭar foregrounds the person’s desire or purpose.

In qaḍā waṭaran, attainment is expressed through another verb.

Waṭar compared with qaḍāʾ

This distinction particularly matters because both occurrences in Al-Aḥzāb accompany qaḍā.

Q-ḍ-y occurs in constructions involving judging, deciding, accomplishing, and ending an undertaking.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:36 says:

إِذَا قَضَى اللَّهُ وَرَسُولُهُ أَمْرًا

idhā qaḍā Allāhu wa-rasūluhu amrā

Al-Aḥzāb 33:36

Here the subject is God and His Messenger deciding a matter.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:37, however, says:

قَضَىٰ زَيْدٌ مِنْهَا وَطَرًا

qaḍā Zaydun minhā waṭarā

Al-Aḥzāb 33:37

Here the object is waṭar, and the phrase concerns fulfillment of a desire or completion of an undertaking.

One fixed equivalent should not be imposed on qaḍā in both verses.

“Decide” or “settle” suits the first phrase.

“Accomplish” or “come to completion” is more natural in the second.

The verb’s object and the sentence construction establish the difference.

Waṭar compared with gharaḍ

Gharaḍ can denote an aim or destination someone seeks.

It is close to waṭar in this respect.

Other uses include an archery target or an action’s objective.

Waṭar lies more particularly within the field of needs and desires someone wishes to fulfill.

This is a semantic tendency, not an absolute boundary.

“Goal,” “purpose,” and “desire” may overlap; translation should select what best fits the context.

Waṭar compared with shahwah

Shahwah concerns inclination toward and desire for something.

It can involve food, possessions, sexual relations, or other objects of desire.

Waṭar can likewise accompany personal inclination in some contexts.

Its meaning, however, should not be confined to shahwah or sexual desire.

The marital context of Al-Aḥzāb 33:37 permits consideration of desires associated with that relationship.

The context alone does not establish sexual desire as the root’s original lexical core.

Poetry and general Arabic expressions demonstrate a broader range.

Waṭar compared with ṭalāq

Ṭalāq concerns ending a marriage within a legal framework.

Waṭar denotes a person’s need or desire.

The two matters are contextually related in Al-Aḥzāb 33:37 because Zayd’s marriage has ended.

They are not lexical equivalents.

Fulfillment of waṭar can allude here to the earlier relationship’s end, but that contextual allusion should not become the noun’s general definition.

The word alone does not determine divorce procedures or conditions.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Translation should retain the sense of need or desire while remaining natural. These proposals do not replace existing verse word boxes.

First occurrence in Al-Aḥzāb 33:37

فَلَمَّا قَضَىٰ زَيْدٌ مِنْهَا وَطَرًا

fa-lammā qaḍā Zaydun minhā waṭarā

Al-Aḥzāb 33:37

A suitable rendering is:

“Then, when Zayd’s relationship with her had run its course…”

This is brief and appropriate to the marital context.

Explanation can clarify that the Arabic phrase expresses fulfillment or completion of Zayd’s need or desire within that relationship.

A rendering closer to the separate Arabic components would be:

“Then, when Zayd had fulfilled his desire in that relationship…”

The corresponding literal Persian wording is less natural.

“When Zayd had sexual enjoyment of her” is not proposed: it restricts the broader meaning to one particular act without sufficient justification.

“When Zayd divorced her” may convey the contextual outcome but is not a direct lexical rendering of the phrase.

Divorce and the ending of the relationship should be clarified in the contextual explanation.

Second occurrence in Al-Aḥzāb 33:37

إِذَا قَضَوْا مِنْهُنَّ وَطَرًا

idhā qaḍaw minhunna waṭarā

Al-Aḥzāb 33:37

A suitable rendering is:

“Once their relationships with them have run their course.”

The plural pronouns refer to adopted sons and their wives.

Translation should preserve those plural referents; the Arabic noun waṭaran itself remains singular.

The passage removes a constraint on marriage to adopted sons’ former wives, so the marriage’s ending is understood from the subject of the verse.

Waṭar itself should not be translated as “divorce” or “waiting period.”

General equivalents

Depending on context, “need,” “desire,” and “purpose” suit waṭar.

Awṭār means “desires” or “purposes.”

Qaḍā waṭarahu can be rendered “he attained his desire,” “met his need,” or “completed his undertaking.”

Qaḍā minhu waṭaran can mean “he attained his purpose through it” or “his undertaking concerning it came to an end.”

In human relationships, Persian “his business with her was finished” may sound natural, but should not imply that the person has lost value or is merely an instrument.

The verse concerns the ending of a marriage and a subsequent marriage provision, rather than measuring a woman’s human worth by a man’s desires.

7. Conclusion

W-ṭ-r concerns a need, desire, or purpose someone is intent on attaining.

Early lexicons explain it through ḥājah, arab, and bughyah. Poetry attributed to Rabīʿ ibn Ḍabʿ uses fulfillment of waṭar to depict youth’s companionship reaching its end.

Umayyad poetry also uses the expression for completing a nonmarital purpose.

These witnesses establish that waṭar is neither inherently intercourse nor a technical term confined to marriage and divorce.

It occurs twice in the Qur’an, only in Al-Aḥzāb 33:37.

The first occurrence concerns the completion of Zayd’s earlier marital relationship and its associated desire.

The second applies the same situation within a general provision concerning adopted sons’ wives.

Repetition connects Zayd’s particular experience with the broader distinction between biological and adopted sons.

The word’s lexical meaning should nevertheless remain distinct from every detail of that provision.

Divorce, termination of marriage, a subsequent marriage, and the differing provisions concerning biological and adopted sons are understood through the relevant verses and their legal context.

Waṭar itself expresses the person’s desire or purpose.

The final definition is: a need or desire someone is intent on attaining; in qaḍā waṭaran, the phrase expresses fulfillment of that desire or completion of the intended undertaking.

This is confined neither to bodily need nor to the ending of sexual desire.

Fulfillment of waṭar does not inherently mean divorce, although it is associated with the marriage’s end in Al-Aḥzāb 33:37.

For Forqan, “when Zayd’s relationship with her had run its course” and “once their relationships with them have run their course” are contextually appropriate renderings.

The explanation should make clear that the phrase concerns completion of the associated desire or need, not the other person’s human worth or every legal stage of separation.

This preserves the distinction between lexical meaning and historical or legal interpretation while explaining the word’s actual contribution to Al-Aḥzāb 33:37.
