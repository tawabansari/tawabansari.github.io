Qur’anic Root B Ṭ SH

Seizing with force, exercising coercive power and dominance, and severity of action

Arabic B Ṭ SH concerns seizing, laying hold of, or acting against someone or something from a position of force and dominance. The action alone is not the point: the force involved and the subject’s ability to overpower also matter. Baṭsh can consequently describe a hand’s power to grasp, a person’s forceful assault, a community’s coercive capacity, or severe divine action.

The root is closely associated with violence and force, but its uses must not all be restricted to beating or killing. Nor does forcefulness itself establish justice or oppression. The agent, object, and context clarify that distinction.

The root occurs ten times in eight verses in three main forms: the verb بَطَشَ in different inflections, verbal noun بَطْش, and noun بَطْشَة. These express the capacity to exert force, communities’ might, and forceful, severe action. They show one semantic core operating in different settings.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: ten occurrences in three forms

1. The semantic core and concrete image

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris defines the root:

«أَخْذُ الشَّيْءِ بِقَهْرٍ وَغَلَبَةٍ وَقُوَّةٍ»

That is, taking something through coercion, dominance, and force.

Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs al-Lughah: the core of baṭsh

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise explains baṭsh as taking forcefully in an onslaught or exerting a strong grasp. Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī calls it تَنَاوُلُ الشَّيْءِ بِصَوْلَةٍ, laying hold of something with force and power.

These explanations share the point that baṭsh is more than ordinary touching, taking, or reaching. Force enables the agent to overpower the object or display the capacity to do so.

A concrete image is a hand gripping, restraining, or mastering something firmly. This must not restrict the root to the hand’s physical movement: Arabic also uses baṭsh for power and authority without describing one particular hand movement.

Not every firm grasp is necessarily baṭsh either. Picking something up simply to keep it need not foreground force and dominance. The root fits more closely when taking involves overpowering, force, an onslaught, or severe action.

Human uses often involve conflict and violence. Uses concerning power may instead describe the capacity to exert force, rather than one actual attack.

The core can therefore be described as seizing or acting with force and dominance. Intensity and overpowering distinguish it from ordinary taking.

2. Lexical forms and early Arabic usage

The basic verb is بَطَشَ. Arabic records both يَبْطِشُ and يَبْطُشُ as its imperfect. Both are Form I; the different middle vowel does not itself create a separate meaning.

بَطْش is the verbal noun, denoting forceful seizing or action. In context it can also express the ability to exert force and coercive power.

بَطْشَة may denote a forceful action, fierce assault, or severe intervention. Its final tā relates to a single occurrence or particular manifestation, but this does not make every baṭshah one individual bodily blow.

بَاطِش is an active participle: someone seizing forcefully or exerting force. Lexicons use يَدٌ بَاطِشَةٌ for a hand capable of grasping and exerting force.

Arabic also records بَاطَشَهُ and verbal noun مُبَاطَشَة, concerning forceful confrontation and struggle. Neither form occurs in the Qur’an.

A less closely connected use is also recorded: بَطَشَ مِنَ الْحُمَّى, recovering from fever while still weak. This limited use must not be transferred to Qur’anic passages lacking that setting.

A poetic witness and its attribution

Ibn Sīdah in Al-Muḥkam wa-l-Muḥīṭ al-Aʿẓam and Ibn Manẓūr in Lisān al-ʿArab quote this rajaz when explaining بَاطَشَ:

حُوتًا إِذَا مَا زَادُنَا جِئْنَا بِهِ
وَقَمْلَةً إِنْ نَحْنُ بَاطَشْنَا بِهِ

ḥūtan idhā mā zādunā jiʾnā bihi / wa-qamlatan in naḥnu bāṭashnā bihi

The lines criticize someone’s appetite and weakness as an ally. حُوتًا compares his swallowing and consumption of food to a large fish; قَمْلَة depicts his insignificance when relied upon in a fight. The Lisān entry on Ḥ W T also explains the first image as devouring food without being satisfied.

Lisān al-ʿArab: voracity in the fish image

Ibn Sīdah specifically explains the bā in بَاطَشْنَا بِهِ as instrumental or expressing assistance, like اسْتَعَنَّا بِهِ. The person is not the target of an assault; the phrase concerns relying on him during a struggle.

Al-Muḥkam and Lisān al-ʿArab: bā in bāṭashnā bihi

The witness illustrates mubāṭashah in confrontation and conflict. These lexical citations do not name its poet, however. Without independent evidence it cannot be presented as certainly pre-Islamic or pre-Qur’anic poetry.

Lexical accounts of baṭsh, bāṭish, and mubāṭashah help establish early meanings. Yet an expression’s early recording in a dictionary does not establish when it originated.

3. Hands and the ability to grasp: Al-Aʿrāf 7:195

Qur’anic text:

أَلَهُمْ أَرْجُلٌ يَمْشُونَ بِهَا أَمْ لَهُمْ أَيْدٍ يَبْطِشُونَ بِهَا أَمْ لَهُمْ أَعْيُنٌ يُبْصِرُونَ بِهَا أَمْ لَهُمْ آذَانٌ يَسْمَعُونَ بِهَا قُلِ ادْعُوا شُرَكَاءَكُمْ ثُمَّ كِيدُونِ فَلَا تُنْظِرُونِ

a-lahum arjulun yamshūna bihā am lahum aydin yabṭishūna bihā am lahum aʿyunun yubṣirūna bihā am lahum ādhānun yasmaʿūna bihā quli udʿū shurakāʾakum thumma kīdūni fa-lā tunẓirūni

Translation:

Do they have feet with which to walk, hands with which to grasp forcefully, eyes with which to see, or ears with which to hear? Say, “Call your partners, then scheme against me and give me no respite.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:195

This clearly illustrates the root’s concrete sense. Challenging the objects invoked alongside The God, the Qur’an names familiar living capacities: walking with feet, grasping with hands, seeing with eyes, and hearing with ears.

يَبْطِشُونَ بِهَا concerns a hand’s function. It should not directly become killing, torturing, or oppression here. The question concerns these objects’ actual capacity to act and have an effect.

Nor should it become wholly ordinary, effortless taking. Choosing baṭsh rather than a general verb such as أخذ fits the hand’s ability to grasp and exert force.

An outward representation of a limb differs from its real capacity. Something may be fashioned to resemble a being with hands yet lack the ability to grasp or act. The passage concerns ability, rather than mere outward likeness.

بِهَا refers to the hands as the instrument of action, clarifying baṭsh’s connection with a hand’s operation and strength.

Baṭsh therefore need not carry moral condemnation. The verse is not describing an oppressive hand; its ability supplies the basis for a question.

4. Forceful action and tyranny: Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:130

Qur’anic text:

وَإِذَا بَطَشْتُمْ بَطَشْتُمْ جَبَّارِينَ

wa-idhā baṭashtum baṭashtum jabbārīna

Translation:

Whenever you exert force, you do so as tyrants.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:130

This belongs to Hūd’s address to ʿĀd. After questioning their construction of buildings and installations, he addresses their conduct when exercising power.

B Ṭ SH occurs twice in one sentence. Repetition foregrounds the criticized behavior; جَبَّارِينَ specifies its character.

جَبَّارِينَ belongs to the separate J B R and here concerns tyranny and imposing power. The sentence’s entire sense of oppression should not be assigned to B Ṭ SH alone: the verse condemns exercising force tyrannically.

Grammatically, جَبَّارِينَ is an accusative circumstantial expression, describing the agents while they exercise force. It shows how they employed their power.

Early explanations give examples such as killing with swords and beating with whips. These fit the violent, tyrannical context but are not exhaustive lexical definitions: baṭsh itself names neither sword nor whip.

Nor should بَطَشْتُمْ simply become “you killed.” Killing may follow forceful action but does not exhaust the root’s meaning.

The criticism concerns how ʿĀd used power. An individual or community can use force to defend or prevent harm; when that capacity becomes an instrument of domination, suppression, and tyranny, it resembles the conduct criticized here.

The passage thus distinguishes possessing power from using it oppressively.

5. Intended force in Moses’s story: Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:19

Qur’anic text:

فَلَمَّا أَنْ أَرَادَ أَنْ يَبْطِشَ بِالَّذِي هُوَ عَدُوٌّ لَهُمَا قَالَ يَا مُوسَىٰ أَتُرِيدُ أَنْ تَقْتُلَنِي كَمَا قَتَلْتَ نَفْسًا بِالْأَمْسِ إِنْ تُرِيدُ إِلَّا أَنْ تَكُونَ جَبَّارًا فِي الْأَرْضِ وَمَا تُرِيدُ أَنْ تَكُونَ مِنَ الْمُصْلِحِينَ

fa-lammā an arāda an yabṭisha bi-alladhī huwa ʿaduwwun lahumā qāla yā Mūsā a-turīdu an taqtulanī ka-mā qatalta nafsan bi-al-amsi in turīdu illā an takūna jabbāran fī al-arḍi wa-mā turīdu an takūna mina al-muṣliḥīna

Translation:

When he intended to seize forcefully the one who was an enemy to them both, someone said, “Moses, do you intend to kill me as you killed someone yesterday? You only want to be a tyrant in the land, rather than one of those who set things right.”

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:19

This follows Moses’s intervention between two people, when his blow the previous day had caused one person’s death.

In verse 19, Moses intends forceful action against someone described as an enemy to both parties. يَبْطِشَ takes bā to identify the person concerned.

Arabic permits this construction for forcibly laying hold of, seizing, or attacking someone.

The passage distinguishes intended baṭsh from killing. يَبْطِشَ names the forceful action; تَقْتُلَنِي in the subsequent speech refers to killing.

Their relationship within one event does not make the verbs synonymous. Force may lead to death, but killing is not necessary to every baṭsh.

The action is not yet reported as accomplished either. أَرَادَ أَنْ يَبْطِشَ expresses intention; translation should preserve that distinction.

A speaker subsequently accuses Moses of wanting tyranny. A character’s accusation must not automatically be treated as the Qur’an’s final judgment of Moses’s intention without examining context.

The earlier event, the new intention, and the speaker’s judgment of motive are distinct. Lexically, baṭsh denotes a forceful, severe action against someone, rather than necessarily killing or its certain outcome.

Together with Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:130, this also distinguishes baṭsh from jabbār. In Al-Shuʿarāʾ, جَبَّارِينَ characterizes the group; in Al-Qaṣaṣ, the charge of tyranny belongs to someone speaking under a perceived threat.

6. Communities’ coercive power

Two verses use baṭsh to compare the power of generations or communities.

Al-Zukhruf 43:8

Qur’anic text:

فَأَهْلَكْنَا أَشَدَّ مِنْهُمْ بَطْشًا وَمَضَىٰ مَثَلُ الْأَوَّلِينَ

fa-ahlaknā ashadda minhum baṭshan wa-maḍā mathalu al-awwalīna

Translation:

We destroyed those greater than them in coercive power, and the precedent of earlier peoples has passed.

Al-Zukhruf 43:8

بَطْشًا, following أَشَدَّ مِنْهُمْ, specifies the respect in which they were stronger: the capacity to exert force and prevail.

The word is not confined to one assault or blow. It concerns power that a community or group can employ to dominate and coerce.

That power may involve military capacity, material resources, social organization, or dominance. The verse does not make any of these an independent lexical definition of baṭsh.

“Stronger” conveys the general idea but weakens the distinction from power in general. “Stronger in exerting force” or “possessing greater coercive power” preserves it more clearly.

Qāf 50:36

Qur’anic text:

وَكَمْ أَهْلَكْنَا قَبْلَهُمْ مِنْ قَرْنٍ هُمْ أَشَدُّ مِنْهُمْ بَطْشًا فَنَقَّبُوا فِي الْبِلَادِ هَلْ مِنْ مَحِيصٍ

wa-kam ahlaknā qablahum min qarnin hum ashaddu minhum baṭshan fa-naqqabū fī al-bilādi hal min maḥīṣin

Translation:

How many generations We destroyed before them, stronger than them in coercive power! They traveled and searched through the lands. Is there any escape?

Qāf 50:36

Again, أَشَدُّ مِنْهُمْ بَطْشًا compares a capacity whose strength varies among communities.

This is especially useful for definition: a degree of coercive power is at issue, rather than necessarily one actual violent act.

The verse recalls stronger generations or communities moving and searching through the lands, whose power did not prevent their destruction.

Neither passage presents power as a guarantee of survival. Military strength, extensive resources, and dominance do not by themselves ensure lasting security.

These uses rule out translating baṭsh everywhere as beating. Coercive might and dominance fit better here.

7. Divine baṭsh: severe action and requital

Three further verses concern divine action. Force and dominance remain, but human images of physical aggression must not be transferred without regard to the difference of agent.

Al-Dukhān 44:16

Qur’anic text:

يَوْمَ نَبْطِشُ الْبَطْشَةَ الْكُبْرَىٰ إِنَّا مُنْتَقِمُونَ

yawma nabṭishu al-baṭshata al-kubrā innā muntaqimūna

Translation:

On the day We seize with the great forceful seizure, We will indeed exact retribution.

Al-Dukhān 44:16

The verb نَبْطِشُ stands beside the related noun الْبَطْشَةَ, foregrounding the action through a noun of the same family.

الْكُبْرَىٰ expresses its greatness. Severity is conveyed both by baṭsh itself and by the accompanying description.

إِنَّا مُنْتَقِمُونَ connects the action with divine retribution or requital. N Q M is a separate root, however; its lexical meaning should not replace baṭsh.

Study of N Q M

Baṭsh emphasizes force and overwhelming severity in action; retribution specifies the response to conduct and accountability.

Historical commentaries disagree about the timing and referent of الْبَطْشَةَ الْكُبْرَىٰ. Some relate it to a major event during the Prophet’s mission, others to final requital in the hereafter. Baṭshah itself names no particular battle, place, or date.

Al-Ṭabarī: differing interpretations of the great seizure

Even if contextual study favors one referent, it should not become the root’s lexical definition.

Nor does the noun’s formation necessarily indicate one bodily blow. Here it names a great, severe action brought about through divine power.

Al-Qamar 54:36

Qur’anic text:

وَلَقَدْ أَنْذَرَهُمْ بَطْشَتَنَا فَتَمَارَوْا بِالنُّذُرِ

wa-laqad andharahum baṭshatanā fa-tamāraw bi-al-nudhuri

Translation:

Lot had certainly warned them of Our forceful seizure, but they doubted and disputed the warnings.

Al-Ṭabarī: Lot as the subject of andharahum in Al-Qamar 54:36

Al-Qamar 54:36

The verse belongs to Lot’s story. The surrounding passages concern warning, deliverance, and his people’s fate.

بَطْشَتَنَا combines baṭshah with the attached “Our,” attributing the threatened action to The God.

The action is warned of before it happens. The meaning extends beyond a momentary bodily act to a severe intervention ensuing from the community’s conduct.

فَتَمَارَوْا بِالنُّذُرِ describes their doubt and dispute over the warnings. M R Y expresses that conduct; it is not part of baṭsh’s meaning.

Study of M R Y

Baṭshatanā names the severe action; context explains the warning and the audience’s responsibility.

Attributing baṭsh to The God does not authorize equating it with human tyranny. The same word can describe human force and divine action without making their justice, motives, or nature identical.

Al-Burūj 85:12

Qur’anic text:

إِنَّ بَطْشَ رَبِّكَ لَشَدِيدٌ

inna baṭsha rabbika la-shadīdun

Translation:

Your Lord’s forceful seizure is indeed severe.

Al-Burūj 85:12

This is one of the root’s most concise uses. Baṭsh is attributed to the Lord, and شَدِيد describes its severity.

Earlier in the surah, people persecute believers for their faith and subject them to fire. Oppression and persecution therefore form the setting for the declaration of severe divine baṭsh.

Baṭsh itself does not mean torturing and should not be restricted to punishment by fire. The verse names the Lord’s severe action; the surah’s context clarifies the warning’s grounds and subject.

The surah immediately also speaks of originating and restoring creation, divine forgiveness, and love. Baṭsh does not name the entirety of The God’s relationship with creation, but a particular aspect of power and severe action in context.

The coercive force remains lexically present, but divine baṭsh should not be construed as requiring hands, bodily instruments, or combat against an equal power.

Qur’anic language can draw upon familiar vocabulary to make divine power intelligible without transferring every human bodily feature and limitation to The God.

8. Taking, striking, overpowering, and tyranny distinguished

B Ṭ SH and ʾ KH DH

Study of ʾ KH DH

ʾ KH DH is broad: taking, receiving, picking up, or encompassing something.

Baṭsh is narrower and more intense, foregrounding force, dominance, and coercion.

Taking a book can be expressed through akhdh without baṭsh. Forcefully seizing or overpowering another person fits baṭsh more closely.

The roots also approach each other in divine requital. Hūd 11:102 describes the Lord’s taking of oppressive settlements. It is related in meaning to divine baṭsh in Al-Burūj without erasing the roots’ distinction.

B Ṭ SH and Ḍ R B

Study of Ḍ R B

Ḍ R B has a broad family of meanings. Construction and context can yield striking, traveling through the land, setting forth an example, and other uses.

Baṭsh instead foregrounds power and the severity of action.

A blow may be an instance of baṭsh, but not every ḍarb is baṭsh. Baṭsh can also involve seizing or restraining without necessarily delivering a blow.

Translating it everywhere as striking consequently removes part of its range.

B Ṭ SH and Q H R

Study of Q H R

Q H R concerns overpowering and subduing. Baṭsh likewise includes dominance but foregrounds forceful, severe seizing or action.

Qahr may emphasize the outcome or condition of dominance; baṭsh more often concerns exerting force and severity in action.

This is not absolute. Ibn Fāris himself defines baṭsh through qahr and dominance. The roots are close without being completely interchangeable.

B Ṭ SH and J B R

Study of J B R

Some J B R uses concern imposing, compelling, and enforcing a will. In tyranny, a jabbār imposes his will or power upon others.

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:130, jabbārīn specifies how those exercising baṭsh behave.

Baṭsh names the forceful action and its severity; jabbārīn in that context names the agents’ tyrannical character.

Their relationship in the same verse thus distinguishes severe action from oppression, despite their proximity.

B Ṭ SH and S Ṭ W

Study of S Ṭ W

S Ṭ W concerns assaulting, attacking, or acting forcefully, and is among baṭsh’s closest neighboring families.

Al-Ḥajj 22:72 uses يَسْطُونَ for people whose anger and readiness to assault become apparent on hearing divine verses.

In such settings, saṭw and baṭsh can be very close. Qur’anic baṭsh also prominently expresses coercive capacity, as in Al-Zukhruf 43:8 and Qāf 50:36.

Their distinction should therefore not become an absolute, permanent boundary.

B Ṭ SH and B Ṭ R

Study of B Ṭ R

The roots share their first two letters but remain independent.

Qur’anic B Ṭ R concerns exultation and arrogance in responding to blessings and ability; B Ṭ SH concerns forceful action and coercive dominance.

Baṭar may lead someone to oppressive baṭsh, but this possible relationship does not establish a derivational connection.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:130 criticizes tyranny in exercising power; Al-Anfāl 8:47 describes another group’s baṭar and ostentation. Each verse employs its own word; one shared definition should not erase the distinction.

9. Morphological and structural points

All five Qur’anic verbal occurrences are Form I. يَبْطِشُونَ, بَطَشْتُمْ, يَبْطِشَ, and نَبْطِشُ belong to this family.

In وَإِذَا بَطَشْتُمْ بَطَشْتُمْ جَبَّارِينَ, repetition belongs to the conditional construction, while jabbārīn describes the subjects’ condition. It should not be counted as two separate events: the verse emphatically describes a pattern of behavior.

In يَبْطِشَ بِالَّذِي, bā identifies the person targeted. In يَبْطِشُونَ بِهَا, it relates to the hands instrumentally. The same preposition can therefore express different relationships in different constructions.

بَطْشًا in Al-Zukhruf and Qāf is accusative tamyīz, specifying the respect of greater strength. The capacity to exert force is at issue.

الْبَطْشَةَ in Al-Dukhān accompanies a cognate verb, foregrounding a great action. بَطْشَتَنَا in Al-Qamar attributes the action to The God through the attached pronoun.

بَطْشَ رَبِّكَ in Al-Burūj is a construct phrase: baṭsh is a verbal noun, and the construction attributes the action to the Lord.

One root thus expresses ability, action, and a particular forceful intervention. This variety does not require unrelated lexical cores: force and dominance remain throughout.

10. Translation proposals for Forqan

For بَطَشَ concerning a person, “seized forcefully,” “took by force,” “engaged forcefully,” or “assaulted” can fit. Construction and circumstances determine the choice.

For بَطْش, possible equivalents include “exertion of force,” “forceful action,” and “coercive dominance”; when comparing communities, “coercive might.”

For بَطْشَة, “severe, forceful action” or “fierce assault” can fit. In divine requital, “forceful seizure” preserves the central meaning without adding bodily instruments.

Specific Qur’anic proposals are:

  • Al-Aʿrāf 7:195: “hands with which to grasp forcefully,” because their capacity is questioned.
  • Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:130: “whenever you exert force, you do so as tyrants,” because oppressive use of power is at issue.
  • Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:19: “intended to seize him forcefully,” because intended severe action is expressed, rather than certainly accomplished killing.
  • Al-Zukhruf 43:8 and Qāf 50:36: “stronger in coercive power,” comparing communities’ capacity to dominate.
  • Al-Dukhān 44:16: “the great forceful seizure,” describing a great, severe divine action.
  • Al-Qamar 54:36: “Our forceful seizure,” a severe action of which they were warned.
  • Al-Burūj 85:12: “your Lord’s forceful seizure,” describing severe divine action.

These translations should not automatically turn baṭsh into killing, torture, war, punishment by fire, or injustice. Each may be a contextual example or consequence, rather than the meaning of the entire family.

Power alone is likewise insufficient everywhere: it weakens the forcefulness and dominance. Violence alone also fails to cover the full range, especially a hand’s capacity and communities’ might.

11. Conclusion

Arabic B Ṭ SH concerns taking, acting, and prevailing through force and intensity. Its concrete image is a strong hand grasping or restraining something, but usage extends beyond the hand’s bodily action.

The Qur’an extends it from grasping capacity to forceful assault, communities’ might, and severe divine action. Force and dominance in taking or acting remain the common meaning.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:195 shows baṭsh need not mean oppression; Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:130 shows force accompanied by tyranny. Al-Zukhruf 43:8 and Qāf 50:36 show that it can name a community’s coercive might without identifying one particular attack.

Divine uses express severe action and requital. They neither imply oppressive tyranny nor authorize assigning bodily instruments and limbs to The God. Agent and context clarify the nature of the action.

Final definition: B Ṭ SH denotes seizing or acting with power, intensity, and dominance; extended uses also express the capacity to coerce and overpower.

The key distinction is between forcefulness and oppression in using force. Baṭsh may express ability, tyrannical conduct, or severe divine action in different settings, while retaining its common semantic core.
