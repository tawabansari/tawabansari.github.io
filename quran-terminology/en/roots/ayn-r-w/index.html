From approaching and befalling to a handhold, connection, and dependable support

The Arabic root ʿ R W concerns approaching, reaching someone or something, befalling, and a kind of contact or connection. In verbal uses, ʿarāhu means coming to, visiting, or reaching someone. With illness, cold, sorrow, or an unwelcome event, it can express something befalling or afflicting a person.

The noun ʿurwah denotes a loop, handle, or fastening that can be grasped or used to connect and secure one thing to another. A jug’s handle, a bucket’s loop, and a garment’s button loop are familiar examples.

The Arabic family is broader than a single image. Something reaching a person, a person approaching another, a connecting loop, and support sought in need all occur in recorded usage. Independent historical evidence would be needed to establish that all originated in one concrete image.

The Quran has three occurrences in two forms: iʿtarāka in Hūd 11:54 and al-ʿurwah in al-Baqarah 2:256 and Luqmān 31:22.

These illustrate distinct uses: something reaching or befalling someone, and a handhold that a person grasps. Understanding their relationship requires examining early Arabic usages and then each verse’s construction.

1. Historical meaning and early Arabic uses

Classical ʿarā–yaʿrū expresses approaching, visiting, reaching, or befalling. The particular meaning depends on subject and object.

ʿArawtu al-rajula means “I went to or approached the man.” In some uses the visit seeks help, a gift, or the fulfillment of a need.

ʿArāhu al-amru means that something befell or happened to him.

ʿArāhu al-bardu means that cold reached or enveloped him.

ʿArāhu al-hammu means that sorrow came upon him.

These share reaching or coming upon, but differ importantly: sometimes a person deliberately visits another, while sometimes something befalls a person without their choice.

ʿArā therefore does not inherently mean attacking, harming, or making ill. Those features depend on the event and context.

Witnesses attributed to early Arabic

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī says:

أَتَيْتُكَ عَارِيًا خَلَقًا ثِيَابِي
عَلَى خَوْفٍ تُظَنُّ بِيَ الظُّنُونُ

ataytuka ʿāriyan khalaqan thiyābī / ʿalā khawfin tuẓannu biya al-ẓunūn

“I came to you in need, my clothes worn, fearful of the suspicions cast upon me.” This rendering follows the explanation of ʿāriyan as approaching in need; the alternative explanation of worn clothing is discussed below.

Classical lexical discussions cite this verse both for approaching someone in need and for worn clothing. The clothing image is therefore not merely a modern conjecture: both explanations appear in the lexical record.

The verse is useful evidence for the discussion of approaching in need, but does not independently settle its interpretation or historical derivation. The possible overlap must not erase the distinction between Quranic ʿ R W and ʿ R Y.

A clearer material witness for ʿurwah occurs in poetry attributed to Labīd ibn Rabīʿah:

فَخْمَةٌ ذَفْرَاءُ تُرْتَى بِالْعُرَى
قُرْدُمَانِيًّا وَتَرْكًا كَالْبَصَلْ

fakhmatun dhafrāʾu turtā bi-l-ʿurā / qurdumāniyyan wa-tarkan ka-l-baṣal

The verse describes armor or an armored troop and its equipment. Turtā bi-l-ʿurā concerns drawing up and securing armor through its loops: the hem is gathered to loops around the middle so it does not impede the wearer.

ʿUrā is the plural of ʿurwah: loops used to fasten, support, or gather up part of the armor.

The material sense is clear: an ʿurwah can be grasped or used to connect and secure parts.

Labīd lived both before and after the advent of Islam. The date of this particular verse has not independently been established, so it should not be labeled an unequivocally pre-Islamic witness. It nevertheless illustrates loops and fastenings on real equipment as familiar early uses of ʿurā.

ʿUrwah in everyday objects

Arabic dictionaries record several concrete uses.

ʿUrwat al-dalw is a bucket’s handle or loop, used for grasping it or attaching a rope.

ʿUrwat al-kūz is a jug’s handle or loop, facilitating grasping and lifting.

ʿUrwat al-qamīṣ is a garment’s button loop or fastening. A button enters it and keeps two parts of the garment together.

In these uses, ʿurwah is not the vessel, garment, or rope itself, but the part enabling grasping, holding, or connection.

This distinction matters for al-ʿurwah al-wuthqā. The Quranic image concerns not merely something strong, but a handhold a person grasps and holds fast to.

Plants and pastoral life

ʿUrwah also names certain plants or thickets that remain available to livestock when other pasture is scarce.

Lexical descriptions include plants retaining greenery or leaves through winter and vegetation livestock rely on when pasture fails. The emphasis is on available forage in need, not a botanical claim that every plant so named survives every drought.

In this use, ʿurwah is something dependable at a time of need.

A similar idea appears socially: a person or group supporting the needy can be likened to vegetation that sustains livestock through hard times.

These uses relate clearly to a handhold and support. Resemblance alone does not prove that a bucket loop was named after vegetation or that vegetation was named after a garment loop.

The evidence illuminates the semantic field without conclusively establishing the chronological order of its uses.

Core meaning and branches

Two principal branches are useful for this study.

The first is approaching, reaching, coming upon, and befalling, prominent in ʿarā and iʿtarā.

The second is a loop or handhold for grasping, connecting, and relying upon, seen in ʿurwah.

Contact, connection, and attachment offer a possible semantic relationship, but do not alone prove a common historical origin for every form.

Some dictionaries discuss final-weak ʿ R W and ʿ R Y forms together. That arrangement does not authorize importing nakedness or emptiness into the definitions of ʿurwah or iʿtarā without independent analysis.

2. Iʿtarāka: something befalling Hūd

After opposing Hūd’s invitation, his people claim that some of their objects of worship have harmed him.

The Quran reports their words and his response:

إِنْ نَقُولُ إِلَّا اعْتَرَاكَ بَعْضُ آلِهَتِنَا بِسُوءٍ قَالَ إِنِّي أُشْهِدُ اللَّهَ وَاشْهَدُوا أَنِّي بَرِيءٌ مِمَّا تُشْرِكُونَ

in naqūlu illā iʿtarāka baʿḍu ālihatinā bi-sūʾin qāla innī ushhidu allāha wa-shhadū annī barīʾun mimmā tushrikūn

Hūd 11:54

“We say only that some of our deities have afflicted you with harm. He said: I call The God to witness, and you too bear witness that I am free of what you associate.”

Hūd 11:54

Iʿtarāka is the Form VIII past-tense verb iʿtarā with the attached pronoun -ka. It means “reached you,” “befell you,” or “came upon you.”

Its subject is baʿḍu ālihatinā, “some of our deities.”

Bi-sūʾin specifies what Hūd’s people allege: their deities, they believe, have harmed him.

Harm and evil come from bi-sūʾin and the speakers’ allegation, not from ʿ R W alone.

If cold or sorrow is the subject of this verbal family, it can describe cold reaching or sorrow befalling someone. Iʿtirāʾ does not necessarily mean deliberate attack or punishment.

The verb’s meaning and the speakers’ claim

The verse reports Hūd’s opponents, who imagined their deities could harm him for opposing them.

Reporting that claim does not endorse it.

Hūd immediately disavows what they associate. He subsequently challenges them and declares his reliance on The God.

The passage therefore does not establish Quranic approval of the supposed deities actually causing harm.

Nor does iʿtarāka inherently mean becoming mad. Some interpretations identify the alleged harm as mental disturbance or madness, but the verb does not have that exclusive meaning.

“Some of our deities have afflicted you with harm” preserves the construction. Explaining what kind of harm the speakers imagined belongs to contextual interpretation.

Does Form VIII add a particular meaning?

Iʿtarā differs morphologically from ʿarā, but their recorded uses overlap in reaching and befalling.

The verbal pattern alone does not establish that the alleged harm was gradual, intentional, hidden, or unusually intense.

Hūd 11:54 clearly reports the people’s claim that harm from their deities reached Hūd. Their intention and belief are identified through context, not morphology alone.

This is the root’s only Quranic verbal occurrence; its Quranic family is not confined to the noun ʿurwah.

3. ʿUrwah: a handhold a person grasps

The other two Quranic occurrences use the identical expression:

الْعُرْوَةِ الْوُثْقَىٰ

al-ʿurwati al-wuthqā

Al-Baqarah 2:256; Luqmān 31:22

“The firmest handhold.”

ʿUrwah is a feminine singular noun. Both verses attach the preposition bi-, producing bi-l-ʿurwati.

Al-wuthqā belongs to W TH Q and expresses firmness and reliability. It is the feminine elative form, agreeing with ʿurwah.

The expression combines two components:

ʿUrwah is something one can grasp; wuthqā describes its exceptional firmness and trustworthiness.

Being firmest should not be transferred into the independent meaning of ʿ R W. A loop may be strong or weak; this particular handhold’s firmness comes from its adjective.

Both verses place the expression after istamsaka.

This verb belongs to M S K and means grasping firmly and holding fast. The image thus comprises a person holding fast, a handhold being grasped, and that handhold’s firmness.

The linguistic image is clear, but its referent is not a physical loop or bodily instrument. The verses concern a person holding to a truth that directs faith and conduct.

4. The firm handhold in al-Baqarah 2:256: rejecting ṭāghūt and believing in The God

Al-Baqarah says:

لَا إِكْرَاهَ فِي الدِّينِ قَدْ تَبَيَّنَ الرُّشْدُ مِنَ الْغَيِّ فَمَنْ يَكْفُرْ بِالطَّاغُوتِ وَيُؤْمِنْ بِاللَّهِ فَقَدِ اسْتَمْسَكَ بِالْعُرْوَةِ الْوُثْقَىٰ لَا انْفِصَامَ لَهَا وَاللَّهُ سَمِيعٌ عَلِيمٌ

lā ikrāha fī al-dīni qad tabayyana al-rushdu mina al-ghayyi fa-man yakfur bi-l-ṭāghūti wa-yuʾmin bi-llāhi fa-qadi istamsaka bi-l-ʿurwati al-wuthqā lā infiṣāma lahā wa-llāhu samīʿun ʿalīm

Al-Baqarah 2:256

“There is no compulsion in religion; sound guidance has become clear from error. Whoever rejects ṭāghūt and believes in The God has grasped a firm handhold that does not break. The God is Hearing, Knowing.”

Al-Baqarah 2:256

The handhold follows two conditions: rejecting ṭāghūt and believing in The God.

These establish the direction of a person’s choice: rejecting the authority of ṭāghūt and placing faith in The God.

The image is vivid. Just as someone grasps something strong for support in difficulty, a person making judgments and choices needs a dependable truth to hold to.

ʿUrwah itself, however, does not lexically mean faith or monotheism. Those concepts come from the complete verse.

The handhold is the linguistic image; rejecting ṭāghūt and believing in The God identify its referent and direction here.

Why begin with no compulsion in religion?

The verse begins by denying compulsion and distinguishing sound guidance from error, then describes someone rejecting ṭāghūt and believing in The God.

That connection matters: holding fast is associated here with understanding and choice, rather than forcing a person to accept a belief they have not embraced.

Coercion can change outward behavior, but cannot by itself create informed and genuine faith.

The image of holding fast fits responsibility for choosing. A person must grasp the handhold themselves. Others may show the way and explain the truth, but external imposition cannot substitute for that person’s grasp.

This connects directly with freedom of faith and the verse’s denial of compulsion in religion.

Lā infiṣāma lahā: the handhold’s own firmness

After the firmest handhold, the verse says:

لَا انْفِصَامَ لَهَا

lā infiṣāma lahā

Al-Baqarah 2:256

“There is no breaking of it.”

Infiṣām belongs to F Ṣ M and concerns breaking, splitting, or severing a connection.

The clause describes the handhold as unbreakable.

Its firmness must nevertheless be distinguished from the conduct of the person holding it.

The verse says the handhold does not break; it does not say that anyone who once grasped it can never let go under any circumstances.

Something’s strength and a person’s ability to release it are distinct linguistic and conceptual questions.

The phrase should therefore not become a separate assertion that every believer must remain faithful for the rest of life after believing once.

What is stated of the handhold itself is firmness and unbreakability.

5. The firm handhold in Luqmān 31:22: submitting one’s face and doing good

The other occurrence is in Luqmān:

وَمَنْ يُسْلِمْ وَجْهَهُ إِلَى اللَّهِ وَهُوَ مُحْسِنٌ فَقَدِ اسْتَمْسَكَ بِالْعُرْوَةِ الْوُثْقَىٰ وَإِلَى اللَّهِ عَاقِبَةُ الْأُمُورِ

wa-man yuslim wajhahu ilā allāhi wa-huwa muḥsinun fa-qadi istamsaka bi-l-ʿurwati al-wuthqā wa-ilā allāhi ʿāqibatu al-umūr

Luqmān 31:22

“Whoever submits his face to The God while doing good has grasped a firm handhold. To The God belongs the outcome of all matters.”

Luqmān 31:22

The same expression, “has grasped the firmest handhold,” occurs, but the preceding wording differs from al-Baqarah 2:256.

Al-Baqarah speaks of rejecting ṭāghūt and believing in The God.

Luqmān speaks of submitting one’s face to The God while doing good.

Wajh here is not merely the physical face; the expression conveys a person’s orientation and turning. Submitting one’s face expresses directing oneself toward The God and yielding to His command and guidance.

The verse immediately qualifies that submission:

وَهُوَ مُحْسِنٌ

wa-huwa muḥsin

Luqmān 31:22

“While doing good.”

This qualification matters in identifying who grasps the firm handhold.

The person both submits their face to The God and practices goodness in conduct.

The handhold cannot therefore be reduced here to a religious label, spoken claim, or outward affiliation.

Submission must accompany goodness. Claiming submission while practicing oppression, corruption, deceit, or violations of others’ rights cannot replace the qualification “while doing good.”

Al-Baqarah 2:112 and al-Nisāʾ 4:125 likewise combine submitting one’s face to The God with doing good.

Two verses, one image, complementary descriptions

The two occurrences of al-ʿurwah al-wuthqā can be read together.

Al-Baqarah 2:256 identifies the direction of faith: rejecting ṭāghūt and believing in The God.

Luqmān 31:22 identifies submission and conduct: yielding one’s face to The God while doing good.

These descriptions illuminate rather than contradict one another.

Faith cannot be merely a label people adopt; nor should goodness be disregarded when considering truth and the direction of their faith.

Both verses connect the firm handhold with a person’s relationship to The God and actual responsibility.

Luqmān ends with:

وَإِلَى اللَّهِ عَاقِبَةُ الْأُمُورِ

wa-ilā allāhi ʿāqibatu al-umūr

Luqmān 31:22

“To The God belongs the outcome of all matters.”

This places holding fast within responsibility and the outcome of actions. The handhold is not merely a means of psychological comfort or social belonging; it concerns life’s direction and accountability to The God.

These concepts arise from context, however, and should not be presented as the independent lexical definition of ʿurwah.

6. Comparison with nearby roots

ʿ R W and ʿ R Y: befalling and being unclothed

These families resemble one another in spelling and some inflected forms, particularly because both have a final weak radical.

Their Quranic uses must nevertheless be distinguished.

ʿ R W concerns reaching or befalling in iʿtarāka, and a handhold or connecting loop in ʿurwah.

By contrast, ʿ R Y appears as taʿrā in Ṭā Hā 20:118, meaning becoming unclothed.

Al-ʿarāʾ in al-Ṣāffāt 37:145 and al-Qalam 68:49 denotes an open, uncovered place.

The firmest handhold cannot therefore be translated as nakedness, disclosure, or emptying, just as taʿrā cannot mean grasping a handhold.

Possible historical proximity between branches does not make their Quranic words synonymous.

ʿ R W and ʿ R R: iʿtarā and muʿtarr

Al-Ḥajj 22:36 places al-muʿtarr alongside al-qāniʿ:

وَأَطْعِمُوا الْقَانِعَ وَالْمُعْتَرَّ

wa-aṭʿimū al-qāniʿa wa-l-muʿtarr

Al-Ḥajj 22:36

“And feed the one who is content and the one who approaches.” The exact distinction between the two is discussed in lexical and exegetical explanations; muʿtarr should not be restricted here to explicit verbal begging.

Quranic morphological analysis assigns muʿtarr to ʿ R R, not ʿ R W.

Despite related meanings of approaching and seeking help, it should not be counted as another derivative of ʿ R W.

Formal and semantic resemblance does not replace independent examination of root and morphology.

ʿ R W and M S K: the handhold and grasping it

ʿUrwah names something graspable or usable as a connection.

Istamsaka expresses firmly grasping and holding fast.

The families occur together in both al-Baqarah and Luqmān, producing the complete image of holding to a handhold.

Grasping should not be attributed entirely to ʿurwah: the noun names the handhold; istimsāk names the act of grasping it.

ʿ R W and W TH Q: the handhold and its firmness

W TH Q concerns firmness, assurance, and trustworthiness.

In al-ʿurwah al-wuthqā, it supplies the handhold’s description.

A handle or loop is not necessarily the firmest. A vessel’s handle can break and a garment’s fastening come loose.

The Quranic handhold’s exceptional firmness comes from al-wuthqā, and its unbreakability from lā infiṣāma lahā.

ʿ R W and F Ṣ M: connection and breaking

F Ṣ M concerns breaking or splitting. In al-Baqarah 2:256, lā infiṣāma lahā denies breaking.

This completes the image of a handhold fit for reliance and support.

Infiṣām is not itself a derivative of ʿ R W, and unbreakability is not an invariant property of every ʿurwah.

It is this Quranic construction that brings together a handhold, exceptional firmness, and the absence of breaking.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Each derivative should retain its own meaning.

ʿArā/yaʿrū: coming to someone, visiting, reaching, or befalling, according to subject, object, and context.

Iʿtarā: reached, befell, came upon, or affected. The verb alone specifies no particular injury or illness.

Iʿtarāka in Hūd 11:54: “reached you,” or in the complete construction, “some of our deities have afflicted you with harm.”

ʿUrwah: loop, handle, handhold, or connecting fastening; figuratively, something to rely on or hold to.

Al-ʿurwah al-wuthqā: “the firmest handhold” or “a very firm handhold.” “A firm handhold” is also natural, but the elative form should remain explicit in the semantic analysis.

In al-Baqarah 2:256 and Luqmān 31:22, the Persian dastāvīz is preferable to dastgīrah because the image concerns grasping something for support and steadiness. English “handhold” similarly conveys that image.

Modern Persian dastgīrah commonly suggests a door or vessel handle, while dastāvīz more readily accommodates the figurative sense intended here.

Directly substituting “monotheism,” “Islam,” “faith,” or “salvation” is less suitable for the base translation. These may explain the referent or outcome, but ʿurwah is not their lexical name.

Likewise, translating iʿtarāka simply as “have driven you mad” substitutes an interpretation of the alleged harm for the verb’s direct meaning.

8. Summary

Two important branches of ʿ R W are reaching or befalling, and a loop or handhold.

Verbal ʿarā and iʿtarā concern something reaching a person or a person approaching another. The encounter may be welcome or unwelcome, so harm, illness, and madness are not invariant meanings.

The noun ʿurwah names a loop or component enabling grasping, fastening, or connection: a vessel handle, bucket loop, or garment fastening. Vegetation remaining available when forage is scarce also illustrates dependable support.

Iʿtarāka occurs in the words of Hūd’s opponents. They allege harm from some of their deities; the Quran reports rather than endorses the claim.

Al-Baqarah 2:256 and Luqmān 31:22 use al-ʿurwah al-wuthqā, the image of a firm handhold that a person grasps.

Al-Baqarah connects this with rejecting ṭāghūt and believing in The God; Luqmān with submitting one’s face to The God and doing good.

Al-Baqarah also stresses the handhold’s unbreakability. This does not itself establish that a person cannot release it: the handhold’s strength and the continuity of human choice are distinct matters.

The definition should retain two branches: reaching, approaching, or befalling; and a loop or handhold for grasping, connecting, or relying upon. Contact and connection can explain their semantic relationship, but a single historical origin has not been conclusively established.

The Quranic image joins the handhold with firmness, faith, submission, and goodness. It invites reliance on an enduring truth, rather than mere labels and claims, as the basis for the direction of faith and conduct.
