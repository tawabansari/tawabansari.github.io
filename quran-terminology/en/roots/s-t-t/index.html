Core meaning: the number six, a specified quantity of six units; in sittīn, sixty, or six tens. In the Qur’an, sittah expresses the six days of creating the heavens and earth, while sittīn specifies the needy people to be fed in a particular ruling. These numbers do not themselves mean unlimited abundance, creation’s perfection, or a hidden code.

1. Introducing the family and its meaning in early Arabic

In Qur’anic morphological classification, S T T is a numerical family associated with sitt, sittah, and sittīn.

Sitt and sittah denote six: a quantity greater than five and less than seven.

Sittīn denotes sixty.

Unlike many Arabic verbal roots, this family has a numerical quantity as its established semantic core, rather than a movement, action, bodily state, or moral quality.

In ordinary Arabic, sittatu rijālin means six men and sittu nisāʾin six women.

The distinction follows the grammatical rules governing numerals and the gender of the counted noun, not a difference in quantity.

Both forms denote six.

An early Arabic witness: six months in al-Aʿshā’s poetry

A verse attributed to al-Aʿshā, the renowned poet of the pre-Islamic period close to the emergence of Islam, reads:

وَكَأَنَّهَا لَمْ تَلْقَ سِتَّةَ أَشْهُرٍ
ضُرًّا إِذَا وَضَعَتْ إِلَيْكَ حِلَالَهَا

wa-kaʾannahā lam talqa sittata ashhurin / ḍurran idhā waḍaʿat ilayka ḥilālahā

Translation:

“As though she had suffered no hardship for six months, when she sets down her baggage before you.”

Sittata ashhurin here denotes six months.

Ashhur is the plural of shahr, month.

Ḍurran concerns hardship and suffering.

The second hemistich has a transmitted variant: jilālahā instead of ḥilālahā.

The variant concerns baggage or coverings associated with travel, and does not change the number six.

The poet describes a specified duration.

Sittah does not here mean an indefinitely long time or suffering itself.

Suffering is expressed by ḍurran.

The duration is expressed by sittata ashhurin.

This witness appears in poetry attributed to al-Aʿshā and in early lexical transmission. Since the poet lived around the transition from the pre-Islamic period to Islam and this verse is not independently dated, his name alone does not establish a definite pre-Qur’anic date for it.

Nevertheless, it attests the number six in Arabic poetic language close to the period of revelation.

A historically grounded definition of the number

In early Arabic, sitt and sittah name the number six.

This meaning is clear and stable in recorded Arabic usage and the Qur’an.

Unlike roots whose initial material meanings developed into social or ethical concepts, there is no evidence here for an earlier meaning such as gathering, completion, order, or perfection that subsequently became six.

The defensible historical definition of this family is the name of the number six; its tens form sittīn denotes sixty.

This does not mean the word’s entire sound history began with these three letters.

Early Arabic accounts discuss an important relationship between S T T and S D S, which requires separate consideration.

2. The historical relationship between S T T and S D S

A notable feature of the family is the presence of two different formations associated with six:

Sitt and sittah.

Sādis and sudus.

The former contains tāʾ.

The latter contains dāl.

Early lexical analysis of the original form

Early Arabic lexical accounts propose older forms sids and sidsah underlying sitt and sittah.

On this account, sound changes and assimilation transformed a formation containing dāl and sīn into one containing doubled tāʾ.

The diminutive of sittah is also cited in explaining this relationship.

Sudaysah is treated as evidence of the formation with dāl within the family of six.

Sātt is also recorded alongside sādis for sixth.

These forms show a recognized relationship between S T T and S D S in Arabic morphological tradition.

Two claims must nevertheless be distinguished.

First, Arabic has different forms for six, sixth, and one-sixth.

Second, every historical stage converting one form into another can be reconstructed from contemporary evidence.

Language usage and the Qur’an clearly establish the first claim.

The second requires independent historical investigation.

Early morphological analysis matters, but each proposed stage should not be presented as certain sound history without independent evidence.

Why distinguish the families in Qur’anic study?

The morphological index used here places sittah and sittīn under S T T.

It places sādis and sudus under S D S.

The distinction helps examine the actual Qur’anic forms.

It does not deny their historical and semantic relationship.

All concern six, but their grammatical functions differ.

Sittah expresses a count.

Sādis identifies sixth position.

Sudus denotes one of six equal parts.

Their numerical connection is clear, but each must be translated according to its own construction and context.

3. Important forms in the family

Sitt

The name of the number six.

It is used when the counted noun is grammatically feminine.

سِتُّ نِسَاءٍ

sittu nisāʾin

“Six women.”

This ordinary Arabic form does not occur independently in this form in the Qur’an.

Sittah

The other form of six.

Under the usual rules for three through ten, the form with tāʾ is used with a masculine counted noun.

سِتَّةُ رِجَالٍ

sittatu rijālin

“Six men.”

سِتَّةُ أَيَّامٍ

sittatu ayyāmin

“Six days.”

It occurs seven times in the Qur’an, always with ayyām.

Sittūna and sittīna

Both denote sixty.

Sittūna is nominative.

Sittīna is used in the accusative and genitive.

The distinction concerns case, not meaning.

سِتُّونَ رَجُلًا

sittūna rajulan

“Sixty men.”

رَأَيْتُ سِتِّينَ رَجُلًا

raʾaytu sittīna rajulan

“I saw sixty men.”

Sittīna occurs once in the Qur’an.

Sittūna does not occur.

Sātt

Lexical records give sātt for the sixth person in a group.

It is recorded alongside sādis.

Sātt follows the formation with tāʾ, whereas sādis belongs to the formation with dāl.

Sātt does not occur in the Qur’an.

Sādis

It means sixth.

It belongs to S D S.

It occurs in Al-Kahf 18:22 and Al-Mujādilah 58:7.

It is related to six, but the count six and the ordinal sixth are distinct.

Sudus

It means one-sixth.

One part of something divided into six equal parts.

It too belongs to S D S.

In the Qur’an, it occurs in inheritance-share passages.

It should not be translated as six or sixty.

Qur’anic forms of S T T

This family has only two Qur’anic forms:

  • Sittati in Al-Aʿrāf 7:54, Yūnus 10:3, Hūd 11:7, Al-Furqān 25:59, Al-Sajdah 32:4, Qāf 50:38, and Al-Ḥadīd 57:4.
  • Sittīna in Al-Mujādilah 58:4.

Its uses therefore fall into two subject groups: six days of creation and feeding sixty needy people.

4. Numerical construction and syntax

Why sittah with ayyām?

The Qur’an uses sittati ayyāmin.

Ayyām is the plural of yawm.

Yawm is grammatically masculine.

Under ordinary counting rules, three through ten exhibit gender polarity with the singular of the counted noun.

Hence sittah accompanies ayyām.

The final tāʾ does not make day feminine.

Nor does six here specify six different kinds of thing or six kinds of day.

The quantity is six; the unit is a day.

The position of sittah in the sentence

In fī sittati ayyāmin, fī precedes the numeral.

Sittati is genitive because of fī.

Ayyāmin is also genitive, as the second term of the construct phrase.

The phrase expresses a temporal quantity.

Sittah itself does not determine the nature of that time or how it is measured.

That concerns ayyām and the creation context.

The construction of sixty

Al-Mujādilah 58:4 uses sittīna miskīnan.

Sittīna is sixty.

Miskīnan is an accusative specification identifying the people counted.

In fa-iṭʿāmu sittīna miskīnan, iṭʿāmu is the first term of the construct phrase and sittīna the genitive second term; its genitive marking is yāʾ.

This construction differs in meaning from sittati ayyāmin.

The latter counts six units of time.

The former counts sixty people described as needy.

The number itself is neither time nor a person: the accompanying noun identifies the unit counted.

5. Fī sittati ayyāmin: six days of creation

All seven occurrences of sittah concern creating the heavens and earth.

The repetition matters because the same quantity appears across different chapters and contexts.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:54: creation and sovereignty

إِنَّ رَبَّكُمُ اللَّهُ الَّذِي خَلَقَ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ فِي سِتَّةِ أَيَّامٍ ثُمَّ اسْتَوَىٰ عَلَى الْعَرْشِ يُغْشِي اللَّيْلَ النَّهَارَ يَطْلُبُهُ حَثِيثًا وَالشَّمْسَ وَالْقَمَرَ وَالنُّجُومَ مُسَخَّرَاتٍ بِأَمْرِهِ أَلَا لَهُ الْخَلْقُ وَالْأَمْرُ تَبَارَكَ اللَّهُ رَبُّ الْعَالَمِينَ

inna rabbakumu Allāhu alladhī khalaqa al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍa fī sittati ayyāmin thumma istawā ʿalā al-ʿarshi yughshī al-layla al-nahāra yaṭlubuhu ḥathīthan wa-al-shamsa wa-al-qamara wa-al-nujūma musakhkharātin bi-amrihi alā lahu al-khalqu wa-al-amru tabāraka Allāhu rabbu al-ʿālamīn

Al-Aʿrāf 7:54

Translation:

“Indeed, your Lord is The God, who created the heavens and earth in six days, then established Himself upon the Throne. He covers the day with night, which pursues it swiftly; and the sun, moon, and stars are subjected to His command. Surely, creation and command belong to Him. Blessed is The God, Lord of all worlds.”

Sittati is the number and ayyāmin its counted unit.

Khalaqa expresses creating.

The heavens and earth are the objects created.

Fī sittati ayyāmin gives the stated duration of that creation.

The verse continues with the Throne, night covering day, and the sun, moon, and stars being subject to His command.

Six thus occurs in a description of divine creation and sovereignty.

Sovereignty, governance, the Throne, and subjection should not be incorporated into the meaning of six.

They are expressed by other words.

Yūnus 10:3: creation and governance

Yūnus 10:3 likewise mentions creating the heavens and earth in six days.

It then says yudabbiru al-amr.

This attributes governance of affairs to The God.

The verse places creation alongside governance.

It also addresses intercession subject to divine permission.

None of these concepts is the meaning of six.

Sittah still denotes the quantity six.

Hūd 11:7: creation and testing humanity

After creation in six days, Hūd 11:7 mentions the Throne upon water and the testing of people.

The test’s aim is expressed by ayyukum aḥsanu ʿamalan: which of you is best in action.

The verse connects creation with practical human responsibility.

This does not make testing, righteous action, or stages of moral growth lexical meanings of six.

Testing comes from li-yabluwakum and excellence in action from aḥsanu ʿamalan.

Al-Furqān 25:59: heavens, earth, and what lies between

Al-Furqān 25:59 explicitly includes wa-mā baynahumā: what is between them.

It mentions the heavens, earth, and what lies between them.

The same context states six days.

The description explicitly includes what lies between the heavens and earth.

This broader statement of the objects does not change the quantity.

Six days are still stated.

Al-Sajdah 32:4: creation and guardianship

Al-Sajdah 32:4 likewise mentions creating the heavens, earth, and what lies between them in six days.

It continues with the Throne and the absence of any guardian or intercessor for the audience apart from The God.

Creation is thus connected with divine guardianship and authority.

The number six does not acquire these meanings.

Qāf 50:38: creation without fatigue

وَلَقَدْ خَلَقْنَا السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ وَمَا بَيْنَهُمَا فِي سِتَّةِ أَيَّامٍ وَمَا مَسَّنَا مِنْ لُغُوبٍ

wa-laqad khalaqnā al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍa wa-mā baynahumā fī sittati ayyāmin wa-mā massanā min lughūb

Qāf 50:38

Translation:

“We indeed created the heavens and earth and what lies between them in six days, and no weariness touched Us.”

Sittati ayyāmin expresses the same familiar quantity.

The closing clause adds another point:

وَمَا مَسَّنَا مِنْ لُغُوبٍ

wa-mā massanā min lughūb

Qāf 50:38

And no weariness touched Us.

This denies divine fatigue.

Lughūb belongs to L GH B and concerns weariness and exhaustion.

The Qur’an speaks of six days of creation without associating that duration with divine fatigue or a need for rest.

The denial is explicit.

The duration should not be taken to mean that The God, like humans or physical beings, used limited energy and became exhausted afterward.

Al-Ḥadīd 57:4: creation and comprehensive knowledge

Al-Ḥadīd 57:4 also states creation in six days.

It continues with divine knowledge of what enters the earth, emerges from it, descends from the sky, and ascends into it.

It also states that The God is with people wherever they are.

Creation is connected with divine knowledge and comprehensiveness.

The different subjects do not change the numerical quantity.

What the seven uses show

Sittah means six in all seven verses.

Their contexts differ.

Some emphasize sovereignty and governance.

Others emphasize testing people or divine guardianship.

Qāf 50:38 denies fatigue.

Al-Ḥadīd 57:4 addresses comprehensive divine knowledge.

The Qur’an thus uses one fixed numerical quantity across a network of subjects.

Six retains its meaning throughout; theological and philosophical subjects arise from the accompanying clauses.

6. Does six days mean six terrestrial day-and-night cycles?

An important question is precisely what kind of time fī sittati ayyāmin expresses.

Answering requires distinguishing two words.

Sittah is six.

Ayyām is the plural of yawm.

Questions about the nature of creation’s days primarily concern yawm and the creation context, not a changed meaning of six.

Day in Arabic

Yawm can denote the familiar day contrasted with night, or a span or period of time.

Qur’anic yawm is not always restricted to an identical interval of terrestrial hours.

Al-Ḥajj 22:47 speaks of a day with the Lord like a thousand years of human reckoning.

Al-Sajdah 32:5 also mentions a day measured as a thousand years.

Al-Maʿārij 70:4 gives a day’s measure as fifty thousand years.

These uses are not restricted to one uniform temporal measure.

They do not alone establish that each creation day was exactly a thousand or fifty thousand years.

Nor may a particular geological or cosmological period be assigned to each creation day without evidence.

Direct translation and analysis of duration

The direct translation of fī sittati ayyāmin is:

“In six days.”

This preserves the Arabic construction.

Analysis can then examine whether day in the creation context denotes periods not necessarily identical to the earth’s present day-and-night cycle.

Such a reading must be assessed against yawm usage, creation’s subject matter, and related Qur’anic verses.

The possibility of “period” does not itself justify changing the direct translation to “six stages of cosmic evolution.”

Stage and period may aid temporal interpretation, but sittah itself means neither stage nor evolution.

Is six a symbol of perfection?

Some numerical interpretations may propose symbolic meanings for six.

Sittah itself names a specific Arabic quantity.

That quantity recurs in seven creation verses.

Repetition does not establish an original meaning of perfection, perfect cosmic order, or an unlimited number of stages.

Creation’s order and wisdom may invite philosophical reflection, but they are not lexical meanings of six.

Repetition alone does not authorize omitting its explicit quantity from translation.

Forqan should retain six as a number and address the nature of creation’s days in separate analysis.

7. Sittīna miskīnan: sixty in Al-Mujādilah 58:4

فَمَنْ لَمْ يَجِدْ فَصِيَامُ شَهْرَيْنِ مُتَتَابِعَيْنِ مِنْ قَبْلِ أَنْ يَتَمَاسَّا فَمَنْ لَمْ يَسْتَطِعْ فَإِطْعَامُ سِتِّينَ مِسْكِينًا ذَٰلِكَ لِتُؤْمِنُوا بِاللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ وَتِلْكَ حُدُودُ اللَّهِ وَلِلْكَافِرِينَ عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ

fa-man lam yajid fa-ṣiyāmu shahrayni mutatābiʿayni min qabli an yatamāssā fa-man lam yastaṭiʿ fa-iṭʿāmu sittīna miskīnan dhālika li-tuʾminū bi-Allāhi wa-rasūlihi wa-tilka ḥudūdu Allāhi wa-li-l-kāfirīna ʿadhābun alīm

Al-Mujādilah 58:4

Translation:

“Whoever cannot find the means must fast for two consecutive months before the two touch one another; whoever is unable must feed sixty needy people. That is so you may believe in The God and His messenger. These are The God’s limits, and for the disbelievers is a painful punishment.”

Sittīna means sixty here.

Unlike the seven preceding uses, the counted subject is not days.

Miskīnan identifies the people counted.

Why sixty here?

The verse continues the ruling on ẓihār.

Al-Mujādilah 58:3 first mentions freeing a person from bondage before the spouses resume intimate contact.

The fourth verse prescribes two consecutive months of fasting for someone without the means.

It then states feeding sixty needy people for someone unable to do that.

Sixty thus occurs within a specified, conditional ruling.

The number itself is not the name of an act of worship, a ransom, or a punishment.

Sittīna only states the count.

Iṭʿāmu supplies the action and miskīnan the recipients.

Sixty needy people or sixty meals?

The Arabic construction says:

إِطْعَامُ سِتِّينَ مِسْكِينًا

iṭʿāmu sittīna miskīnan

Al-Mujādilah 58:4

“Feeding sixty needy people.”

It counts needy people, not meals.

Rendering it merely “giving sixty meals” changes the stated unit.

In the text, sixty applies to people.

The amount per person, manner of feeding, and other practical details do not follow from S T T itself.

Those require examination of the other wording and relevant evidence; a lexical study alone does not settle every question of implementation.

One lexical point is clear:

Sittīna miskīnan numerically denotes sixty people described as needy, not merely sixty unspecified units of food.

Sixty and two consecutive months

The verse mentions two consecutive months of fasting before feeding sixty needy people.

Their proximity may prompt an inference that sixty is merely equivalent to sixty days of fasting.

That cannot be extracted from sittīn itself.

Two calendar months are not invariably sixty days.

The first clause uses months as the temporal unit.

The second counts needy people.

Although the provisions have a specified order, their units should not be equated without evidence.

Does sixty mean abundance here?

Arabic numbers can sometimes serve rhetorical emphasis on abundance.

That general possibility does not suffice to turn an explicit number in a ruling into unlimited abundance.

Al-Mujādilah 58:4 gives sixty with a counted noun in a ruling.

Its direct contextual meaning is sixty.

Replacing it with “many needy people” would require independent evidence.

The verse itself supplies no such authorization.

A specified ruling and a general principle

Feeding sixty needy people here concerns the particular situation in Al-Mujādilah.

It does not establish sixty recipients for every fast, inability, or compensatory provision in the Qur’an.

The number and ruling must be understood in their context.

Al-Baqarah 2:184, for example, mentions a compensatory feeding provision in another setting.

Shared use of miskīn does not make all conditions and quantities identical.

Al-Mujādilah 58:4 explicitly gives sixty.

Al-Baqarah 2:184 requires independent examination of its own numerical and legal construction.

8. Distinguishing six, sixth, and one-sixth

Three related numerical concepts must not be conflated in translation.

Six: a quantity

Sittah denotes six.

Sittati ayyāmin counts six days.

The number itself does not identify their sequence or the sixth day’s position.

Sixth: position within an order or group

Al-Kahf 18:22 states:

سَادِسُهُمْ كَلْبُهُمْ

sādisuhum kalbuhum

Al-Kahf 18:22

Their sixth is their dog.

This gives the dog sixth position in the reported group.

Al-Mujādilah 58:7 also uses sādisuhum.

That context concerns divine knowledge of people’s private conversations.

Both uses numerically concern being sixth.

Sixth differs from six.

Six gives the total quantity.

Sixth identifies a member’s position relative to the group.

In Al-Mujādilah 58:7, the context of divine knowledge and accompaniment should not be ignored by turning sixth into bodily presence like that of a human being.

This concerns the entire verse, not the meaning of six.

One-sixth: a share of a whole

Al-Nisāʾ 4:11 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:12 use al-sudus for an inheritance share.

Sudus means one-sixth.

If property is divided into six equal parts, each is one-sixth of the whole.

One-sixth is not six itself, but a proportion obtained by division into six equal parts.

Sittah, sādis, and sudus thus share a numerical field but differ in function.

Six is a quantity; sixth is a position; one-sixth is a part-to-whole ratio.

9. Distinctions from neighboring numerical families

S T T and S D S

The families are historically and semantically related.

Qur’anic S T T expresses six and sixty.

Qur’anic S D S expresses sixth and one-sixth.

The morphological and grammatical distinction is real.

It does not establish historical independence.

Early lexical accounts propose a phonetic connection.

Preserve the index distinction without denying the historical relationship without grounds.

S T T and KH M S

Khams denotes five.

Sittah denotes six.

They are adjacent in counting but belong to different numerical families.

Al-Mujādilah 58:7 uses khamsatin, followed by sādisuhum for sixth position.

Their association shows the numerical relation between five members and a sixth.

It does not supply a new meaning for S T T.

S T T and S B ʿ

Sabʿ denotes seven.

Sittah is six.

Both are used for counting.

Seven also occurs in various Qur’anic settings, including the heavens and other specified quantities.

Their adjacency does not mean six necessarily represents an incomplete stage and seven a complete one.

Such symbolic evaluation requires independent evidence.

Direct translation must retain each quantity.

S T T and ʿ SH R

ʿAshr denotes ten.

Sittīn denotes six tens in the Arabic numerical system.

The relationship of sixty to six and ten is clear.

It does not give the word an independent meaning of abundance, perfection, or the completion of a specified period.

In Al-Mujādilah 58:4, sixty specifies the number of needy people.

That quantity must be understood within its ruling and counted unit.

10. Conclusion

Qur’anic S T T concerns six and its tens form, sixty.

Sitt and sittah are recognized Arabic forms of six.

Their ordinary use differs according to the counted noun’s grammatical gender.

Sittīn denotes sixty.

Poetry attributed to al-Aʿshā uses sittata ashhurin for six months, attesting the specified quantity in poetic Arabic close to the period of revelation.

Early lexical accounts propose sids and sidsah as older forms behind sitt and sittah.

This analysis is connected with sādis and sudus, but does not alone establish every stage of sound history.

The family occurs at eight Qur’anic locations.

Sittah occurs seven times for six days of creating the heavens and earth.

Sittīn occurs once, for sixty needy people in Al-Mujādilah 58:4.

Six remains constant across the different creation contexts.

Some emphasize divine sovereignty and governance.

Others emphasize testing humanity, guardianship, or divine knowledge.

Qāf 50:38 denies divine exhaustion.

These concepts arise from the full clauses, not six itself.

The direct translation of sittata ayyāmin is six days.

The nature of creation’s days requires independent study of yawm and the creation verses.

Sixty in Al-Mujādilah 58:4 counts needy people in a specified ruling.

Its construction does not justify replacing sixty people with sixty meals or an unspecified quantity without evidence.

Six, sixth, and one-sixth must also be distinguished.

Sittah expresses the quantity six.

Sādis identifies sixth position.

Sudus is one of six equal parts.

The Qur’anic meaning of S T T is the quantity six, and in sittīn, sixty.

The family supplies no independent moral, philosophical, or symbolic meaning that can replace the number in translation.

Six is not itself a sign of perfection or the name of a creation stage.

Sixty is not itself the name of compensation or feeding: it gives a specified count within a feeding clause.

Forqan should translate sittah as six and sittīn as sixty.

Precision here means preserving the number’s actual quantity and distinguishing it from the counted unit, grammatical construction, and contextual implications.
