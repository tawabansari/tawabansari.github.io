The Qur’anic Root Q L Y (ق ل ي)

Aversion, detestation, and turning away out of displeasure

Qur’anic forms

Qalā

Detested; 93:3
الْقَالِينَ

Al-qālīn

1. Basic meaning and linguistic background

The Arabic root Q L Y denotes aversion or detestation toward a person or thing. Such aversion can lead to distancing oneself, ending a relationship, or turning away from its object.

Poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period uses قِلَى (qilā) in human relationships and aversion toward others. Classical Arabic sources also record it as hatred, strong dislike, and the turning away that may follow. Securely attributed evidence for precisely dating its pre-Qur’anic use is limited.

Qur’anic forms examined: two occurrences, one a past-tense verb and the other a plural active participle.

  • قَلَىٰ (qalā): detested or hated.
  • الْقَالِينَ (al-qālīn): those who detest a person or thing.

Other recorded forms include قِلَى (qilā) and قَلَاء (qalāʾ), aversion or detestation; قَالٍ (qālin), one who detests; and مَقْلِيّ (maqliyy), one who is detested. These forms do not occur in the Qur’an, apart from the active participle in its plural form.

2. Poetic evidence and lexical uses

First witness: Lāmiyyat al-ʿArab, attributed to Al-Shanfarā. This poem, whose historical attribution is disputed, includes the following verse:

وَفِي الْأَرْضِ مَنْأًى لِلْكَرِيمِ عَنِ الْأَذَىٰ وَفِيهَا لِمَنْ خَافَ الْقِلَىٰ مُتَعَزَّلُ

“On earth there is a place for the noble person to escape harm, and a retreat for anyone who fears others’ hatred.”

At the poem’s opening, the speaker describes his decision to leave his people, finding in the earth’s breadth an opportunity to escape harm and unwelcome relationships.

الْقِلَى (al-qilā) denotes aversion or hatred here, while مُتَعَزَّل (mutaʿazzal) names a place where someone can withdraw from others.

The poem’s attribution to Al-Shanfarā is disputed. Although the verse clearly illustrates the word’s meaning in Arabic literary tradition, it cannot serve as conclusive evidence of pre-Qur’anic usage.

Second witness: Kuthayyir ʿAzzah, an Umayyad poet. His address to his beloved includes:

أَسِيئِي بِنَا أَوْ أَحْسِنِي لَا مَلُومَةٌ لَدَيْنَا وَلَا مَقْلِيَّةٌ إِنْ تَقَلَّتْ

“Treat us badly or well; with us you are neither blamed nor detested, even if she shows aversion.”

مَقْلِيَّة (maqliyyah) is a feminine passive participle meaning “detested.” The poet says that his beloved is not detested by him despite her displaying aversion. At the verse’s close, إِنْ تَقَلَّتْ shifts from addressing her directly to speaking about the same woman. This change of grammatical person is reflected in the translation.

This usage belongs to the period after the Qur’an’s revelation and illustrates the continued use of the sense of aversion.

Qilā is not merely the absence of affection. Someone may feel no affection for a person or thing without detesting it. Qilā expresses a negative orientation toward its object, and distancing oneself may follow from it.

Distinguishing frying and roasting

Arabic also uses قَلَى (qalā) for roasting or cooking meat or grains on a hot vessel. Dictionaries record both yāʾ-final and wāw-final forms for this sense.

مِقْلَاة (miqlāh) denotes a vessel used for frying or roasting food.

The similar form of this verb and qalā meaning detestation does not establish a common historical origin. Both Qur’anic occurrences concern aversion or detestation; neither context concerns frying or heating.

3. Qur’anic uses

Lot’s aversion to his people’s conduct

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ, after Lot urges his people to stop their conduct, they threaten to expel him:

قالوا لَئِن لَم تَنتَهِ يٰلوطُ لَتَكونَنَّ مِنَ المُخرَجينَ

Qālū la-in lam tantahi yā Lūṭu la-takūnanna mina l-mukhrajīn.

“They said: O Lot, if you do not desist, you will surely be among those expelled.”

Ash-Shu'ara 26:167

Lot responds:

قالَ إِنّى لِعَمَلِكُم مِنَ القالينَ

Qāla innī li-ʿamalikum mina l-qālīn.

“He said: Indeed, I am among those who detest your conduct.”

Ash-Shu'ara 26:168

الْقَالِينَ (al-qālīn) is the accusative or genitive sound masculine plural of قَالٍ (qālin), the active participle of قَلَى. Its nominative form is الْقَالُونَ (al-qālūn). Here, after the preposition مِنْ, it is genitive, marked by yāʾ. The original final yāʾ of the defective noun is dropped when forming the plural. The word means “those who detest,” and should not be confused with الْقَائِلِينَ (al-qāʾilīn), the plural of qāʾil, “one who says,” from Q W L.

مِنَ الْقَالِينَ means “among those who detest.” In a common analysis, this prepositional phrase functions as the predicate of inna. Another grammatical analysis supplies an omitted predicate such as qālin and treats mina l-qālīn as its modifier. The difference in syntactic analysis does not alter the meaning of Lot’s aversion to their conduct.

لِعَمَلِكُمْ identifies the object of aversion: “your conduct.” In the first analysis, it is connected with the active participle al-qālīn; in the second, with the omitted predicate.

In this sentence, Lot explicitly directs his aversion toward their conduct, and the translation must preserve that object. The wording alone does not settle all his feelings toward the people, but “your conduct” must not become “your very existence” in translation.

His response also shows that the threat of expulsion did not change his position on their conduct. He did not endorse what he considered wrong to preserve his position or security.

In the next verse, he says:

رَبِّ نَجِّنى وَأَهلى مِمّا يَعمَلونَ

Rabbi najjinī wa-ahlī mimmā yaʿmalūn.

“My Lord, save me and my family from what they do.”

Ash-Shu'ara 26:169

Here too, Lot’s prayer concerns what his people are doing.

Qalā in this context is more than disapproval or a difference in taste: it expresses Lot’s explicit aversion to their conduct.

Denying that the Lord detests Muhammad

The root’s other occurrence is in Al-Ḍuḥā:

ما وَدَّعَكَ رَبُّكَ وَما قَلىٰ

Mā waddaʿaka rabbuka wa-mā qalā.

“Your Lord has neither forsaken you nor detested you.”

Ad-Duha 93:3

The verse places two distinct verbs together: وَدَّعَكَ (waddaʿaka), from W D ʿ, and قَلَىٰ (qalā), from Q L Y.

The first expresses forsaking or leaving; the second, aversion or detestation.

قَلَىٰ is a third-person masculine singular past-tense verb in the basic form. Its implicit subject refers to the Lord. Its object is not explicitly repeated, since the addressee’s pronoun in the preceding clause makes clear that Muhammad remains the person being discussed.

The object understood from context is the addressee: your Lord has not detested you. Not repeating that pronoun also accords with the verses’ endings. This grammatical explanation does not replace the verse’s actual wording.

Forsaking and detesting differ. Someone may leave another person without hatred, or may detest someone while continuing to live alongside them.

The Qur’an separately denies both conditions in this verse.

Later verses promise the Lord’s giving and recall instances of care and guidance in Muhammad’s life:

وَلَسَوفَ يُعطيكَ رَبُّكَ فَتَرضىٰ

Wa-la-sawfa yuʿṭīka rabbuka fa-tarḍā.

“And your Lord will surely give to you, and you will be content.”

Ad-Duha 93:5

أَلَم يَجِدكَ يَتيمًا فَـٔاوىٰ

A-lam yajidka yatīman fa-āwā.

“Did He not find you an orphan, then give shelter?”

Ad-Duha 93:6

Al-Ḍuḥā 93:7 and Al-Ḍuḥā 93:8 further speak of guiding him and relieving his need.

Together with the denial of abandonment and detestation, these reminders express the Lord’s continued care for Muhammad.

Historical reports connect this verse’s revelation with an interruption in receiving revelation. The Qur’an itself does not specify the interruption’s duration or details, and understanding qalā does not depend on establishing them.

4. Comparison with nearby roots

Q L Y and B GH Ḍ: aversion and hatred

Qilā and bughḍ are closely related Arabic words in the semantic field of hatred and aversion.

Bughḍ stands opposite affection, while qilā also denotes hatred and strong displeasure. Lexical explanations of qilā also highlight the distancing or turning away that can result from hatred.

In the Qur’an, بَغْضَاء (baghḍāʾ) appears several times alongside hostility and concerns hatred in human relations. Al-Māʾidah 5:91, for example, speaks of creating hostility and hatred among people.

Qalā in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:168 concerns aversion to conduct; in Al-Ḍuḥā 93:3, it concerns the denial of aversion to a person.

These contextual differences do not confine qilā to hatred of conduct or bughḍ to hatred of people. Both can concern people or other objects in Arabic.

Q L Y and K R H: detesting and disliking

Karāhah has a broader semantic range. Someone may dislike something because it is difficult, unpleasant, harmful, or contrary to their wishes.

Al-Baqarah 2:216 speaks of disliking something that may be good for a person. Karāhah therefore does not always mean moral revulsion toward something wrong.

Qilā more strongly emphasizes aversion or detestation, going beyond simple disapproval.

Nevertheless, the roots overlap, and context determines the degree of intensity in each use.

Q L Y and W D ʿ: detestation and abandonment

Their juxtaposition in Al-Ḍuḥā 93:3 makes the distinction clear.

Tawdīʿ concerns leaving, bidding farewell, or forsaking, whereas qilā expresses aversion or detestation toward a person or thing.

Separation may happen for reasons other than hatred. Conversely, aversion does not always lead to actual separation.

Translating both verbs simply as “leaving” or “turning away” would erase an important distinction in the verse.

Q L Y and ʿ D W: aversion and hostility

Qilā expresses an attitude of aversion toward someone or something, while ʿadāwah more directly concerns a hostile relationship or opposition between parties.

Someone can detest a behavior without entering a hostile relationship with its practitioner.

Aversion, hostility, and hostile action are therefore distinct concepts. The root Q L Y itself carries no command to harm, punish, or attack the object of aversion.

5. Translation recommendations for Forqan

For قَلَىٰ in Al-Ḍuḥā 93:3, “detest” or “hate” is proposed, corresponding to the Persian equivalents بیزار شدن and دشمن داشتن.

For الْقَالِينَ in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:168, “those who detest” is suitable.

A smoother rendering of Lot’s words is:

“He said: Indeed, I detest your conduct.”

“Those who are displeased” conveys part of the meaning but less clearly expresses the aversion in this use. “Those who turn away” is also insufficient alone, since turning away can happen without hatred.

6. Conclusion

Q L Y denotes aversion or detestation toward a person or thing. It may lead to turning away or ending a relationship, but it is not identical with the act of leaving or with active hostility.

The Qur’an uses the root in two different contexts: Lot’s aversion to his people’s conduct and the denial that the Lord detests Muhammad.

In the first, human conduct is the object of aversion; in the second, the Lord’s aversion to the addressee is denied.

Proposed definition for Forqan: Aversion or detestation toward a person or thing; a condition that may lead to distancing or turning away but does not in itself mean ending a relationship or acting with hostility.
