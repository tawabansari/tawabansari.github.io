1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

The three-letter Arabic root ع س ر concerns hardship, difficulty, constraint, and a situation in which an undertaking is not easy or achieving a result meets obstacles and pressure. It stands opposite ي س ر, associated with ease, opening, available means, and an undertaking proceeding smoothly.

Qur’anic ʿusr is not limited to personal suffering or enduring calamity. A debtor’s financial hardship, difficulty fulfilling a responsibility, hard social conditions, disagreement preventing an agreement, and the hardship of the day of account all belong to this family’s uses.

The root occurs twelve times in twelve verses across nine surahs. Its principal forms are:

Quranic Arabic Corpus: twelve occurrences in six morphological groups

  • عُسْر: hardship, difficulty, or constraint. Used in the Qur’an: yes, five times.
  • عُسْرَة: a difficult or constrained situation, especially involving limited resources or demanding circumstances. Used in the Qur’an: yes, twice.
  • عَسِر: hard or difficult. Used in the Qur’an: yes, once.
  • عَسِير: hard, difficult, or burdensome. Used in the Qur’an: yes, twice.
  • عُسْرَى: a difficult matter or course, opposite يُسْرَى. Used in the Qur’an: yes, once.
  • تَعَاسَرَ / تَعَاسَرْتُمْ: agreement becoming difficult; encountering mutual difficulty or disagreement. Used in the Qur’an: yes, once.
  • عَسُرَ / يَعْسُرُ: to become hard or difficult. Used in the Qur’an: no, in this verbal form.
  • عَسَّرَ / يُعَسِّرُ: to make difficult, act harshly, or impose constraint. Used in the Qur’an: no.
  • أَعْسَرَ / إِعْسَار: to become poor or unable to pay. Used in the Qur’an: no.
  • تَعَسَّرَ: to become difficult, complicated, or obstructed. Used in the Qur’an: no.
  • مُعْسِر: someone in financial hardship or unable to pay. Used in the Qur’an: no.
  • اِعْتَسَرَ: in certain older uses, to take or put something to use forcibly or before it is ready. Used in the Qur’an: no.

Non-Qur’anic forms help establish Arabic’s lexical range, but should not be identified as derivatives occurring in the Qur’an. Each Qur’anic form must also be understood through its morphology and its verse’s context.

2. The semantic core and older Arabic usage

Hardship opposite ease

Arabic ʿusr stands opposite yusr. They describe different situations: in one an undertaking meets ease, opening, and available means; in the other there is an obstacle, pressure, blockage, or difficulty.

ʿUsr therefore goes beyond an unpleasant feeling. An undertaking can be objectively difficult even if someone performs it calmly. Conversely, someone may fear an easy undertaking without its external conditions or available means having become difficult.

عَسُرَ الْأَمْرُ concerns an undertaking becoming difficult. عَسُرَ عَلَيْهِ الْأَمْرُ explicitly relates the difficulty to someone: it has become difficult for him.

This distinction matters. Something can be difficult for one person but not another. Limited money, unavailable means, a resistant obstacle, complexity, or others’ harsh behavior may cause the difficulty.

ʿUsr should therefore not be translated only as complete inability. It can make an undertaking difficult without making it impossible. Conversely, constraint can become so severe that someone cannot fulfill a responsibility under existing conditions.

A physical illustration: blockage, resistance, and hindered movement

Some Arabic explanations also use خلاف and التواء, suggesting opposition, complication, or something failing to proceed smoothly as expected.

For an everyday illustration, imagine an undertaking whose ordinary course encounters an obstacle. Greater effort is needed, movement slows, or a result becomes harder to obtain.

This is an explanatory image, not a certain historical definition. It does not establish that every ع س ر meaning arose from a particular physical image such as twisting rope or knotting thread.

Even accounts of tangled thread record variation or differentiation between تَعَسَّرَ with ʿayn and تَغَسَّرَ with ghayn. Tangling thread should therefore not be identified without examination as the root’s certain original image.

What can be said with greater confidence is that difficulty, resistance, constraint, and difficulty obtaining a result form the shared field of this family’s principal uses.

Maqāyīs, Lisān al-ʿArab, and Al-Muḥkam: difficulty and the ʿayn/ghayn variation

Examples from older Arabic

Jarīr, the Umayyad poet, writes:

بِشْرٌ أَبُو مَرْوَانَ إِنْ عَاسَرْتَهُ
عَسِرٌ، وَعِنْدَ يَسَارِهِ مَيْسُورُ

bishrun abū marwāna in ʿāsartahu / ʿasirun wa-ʿinda yasārihi maysūru

That is: “Bishr, Abū Marwān, is hard if you deal harshly with him, and accommodating when approached with ease.”

The verse contrasts difficulty and ease in human conduct. Difficulty describes neither a mountain, road, nor physical duty, but interaction between people.

This witness postdates revelation and cannot be called pre-Islamic poetry, though it remains valuable evidence of older Arabic usage.

Al-Mubarrad’s Al-Kāmil: Jarīr and harsh versus accommodating conduct

A verse attributed to Al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī reads:

فَذَرْ ذَا وَعَدِّ إِلَى غَيْرِهِ
فَشَرُّ الْمَقَالَةِ مَا يُعْتَسَرْ

fa-dhar dhā wa-ʿaddi ilā ghayrihi / fa-sharru al-maqālati mā yuʿtasar

That is: “Leave this and turn to another subject, for the worst speech is what is forced out before it is ready.”

يُعْتَسَرْ concerns speech produced under pressure and strain rather than proceeding fluently and thoughtfully.

Al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. Without dating this particular composition, it cannot be presented as certain pre-Qur’anic evidence.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī’s witness on forced speech

Other Arabic uses include iʿsār for poverty or inadequate financial means, and taʿāsur for parties to a transaction or family relationship encountering difficulty and disagreement in reaching an agreement.

Arabic also uses أَعْسَر for a left-handed person. This should not be taken to mean that the person is incapable or deficient. The precise historical connection between naming left-handedness and difficulty also requires independent evidence.

Together, these uses show that ع س ر concerns more than suffering’s intensity. It can describe a difficult undertaking, constrained means, harsh conduct, and resistance to an affair’s ordinary progress.

3. Hardship and ease: responsibility and human capacity

Al-Baqarah 2:185: The God intends ease, not hardship

Verse text

شَهْرُ رَمَضَانَ الَّذِي أُنْزِلَ فِيهِ الْقُرْآنُ هُدًى لِلنَّاسِ وَبَيِّنَاتٍ مِنَ الْهُدَىٰ وَالْفُرْقَانِ فَمَنْ شَهِدَ مِنْكُمُ الشَّهْرَ فَلْيَصُمْهُ وَمَنْ كَانَ مَرِيضًا أَوْ عَلَىٰ سَفَرٍ فَعِدَّةٌ مِنْ أَيَّامٍ أُخَرَ يُرِيدُ اللَّهُ بِكُمُ الْيُسْرَ وَلَا يُرِيدُ بِكُمُ الْعُسْرَ وَلِتُكْمِلُوا الْعِدَّةَ وَلِتُكَبِّرُوا اللَّهَ عَلَىٰ مَا هَدَاكُمْ وَلَعَلَّكُمْ تَشْكُرُونَ

shahru ramaḍāna alladhī unzila fīhi al-qurʾānu hudan li-al-nāsi wa-bayyinātin mina al-hudā wa-al-furqāni fa-man shahida minkumu al-shahra fa-l-yaṣumhu wa-man kāna marīḍan aw ʿalā safarin fa-ʿiddatun min ayyāmin ukhara yurīdu Allāhu bikumu al-yusra wa-lā yurīdu bikumu al-ʿusra wa-li-tukmilū al-ʿiddata wa-li-tukabbirū Allāha ʿalā mā hadākum wa-laʿallakum tashkurūna

English translation

The month of Ramadan is that in which the Qur’an was sent down, as guidance for people and clear signs of guidance and discernment. Whoever among you is present for the month shall fast it; whoever is ill or on a journey, then a number of other days. The God intends ease for you and does not intend hardship for you, so that you may complete the number and magnify The God for having guided you, and so that you may give thanks.

Usage analysis

Here ʿusr stands in direct contrast with yusr:

يُرِيدُ اللَّهُ بِكُمُ الْيُسْرَ وَلَا يُرِيدُ بِكُمُ الْعُسْرَ.

The construction is clear. يُرِيدُ expresses intending; الْيُسْرَ is its object in the first clause and الْعُسْرَ is the object of the repeated verb in the second, negative clause. بِكُمُ relates each intention to the audience.

In the same context, The God prescribes fasting and provides a fitting arrangement for illness and travel. Ease therefore does not mean abolishing responsibility; it concerns prescribing responsibility in a way attentive to actual human conditions.

An undertaking’s natural difficulty should be distinguished from imposed or disproportionate hardship. Even a healthy person may experience hunger, thirst, and some difficulty while fasting. This alone does not establish that every feeling of hunger or fatigue automatically removes the prescription.

Conversely, the verse prevents unlimited hardship or disregard for illness and actual human circumstances from being identified as the divine prescription’s purpose.

ʿUsr here is therefore the hardship and constraint contrasted with the intended accommodation. Identifying situations covered by exceptions requires the fasting verses together.

The verse establishes an important principle: prescribing a responsibility does not make its deliberate complication desirable. A responsibility’s value follows from its rightness and purpose, not the amount of suffering someone can impose on himself or others.

Hardship and capacity

Al-Baqarah 2:286 mentions wusʿ and ṭāqah. Neither belongs to ع س ر, but both matter when examining difficulty and human ability.

Someone may encounter hardship yet remain able to act. Alternatively, the difficulty may exceed the person’s actual capacity.

Three situations should therefore be distinguished: an easy undertaking, a difficult but feasible undertaking, and inability to perform or bear it.

ع س ر alone does not determine when difficulty becomes inability. That judgment requires the subject, individual circumstances, and further evidence.

4. ʿUsrah: financial hardship and difficult social conditions

Al-Baqarah 2:280: a debtor in constraint

Verse text

وَإِنْ كَانَ ذُو عُسْرَةٍ فَنَظِرَةٌ إِلَىٰ مَيْسَرَةٍ وَأَنْ تَصَدَّقُوا خَيْرٌ لَكُمْ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ تَعْلَمُونَ

wa-in kāna dhū ʿusratin fa-naẓiratun ilā maysaratin wa-an taṣaddaqū khayrun lakum in kuntum taʿlamūna

English translation

If someone is in financial hardship, then allow respite until ease. To remit it as charity is better for you, if you know.

Usage analysis

ذُو عُسْرَةٍ describes someone in a difficult, constrained situation. Debt and the ability to pay form the context, specifying ʿusrah as financial hardship and limited financial capacity.

The verse then states نَظِرَةٌ إِلَىٰ مَيْسَرَةٍ: allowing time until relief and the means of payment become available.

The contrast illustrates hardship and ease in an actual life situation. A debtor may acknowledge the debt while lacking the means to pay when due.

ʿUsrah here is therefore more than feeling unhappy about paying. It concerns financial circumstances and a real lack of means.

The verse also distinguishes allowing time from remitting the debt. Respite responds to financial hardship; remission is additionally described as better.

The problem is not viewed only from the creditor’s perspective. The debtor’s actual ability also matters in determining the response.

ʿUsr can therefore describe someone needing time and relief to fulfill a legitimate obligation. Imposing further pressure on someone genuinely unable to pay is not presented as the appropriate response.

This does not establish that every debtor, regardless of financial means or conduct, may delay payment. The verse specifically concerns someone in ʿusrah.

Al-Tawbah 9:117: the time of hardship

Verse text

لَقَدْ تَابَ اللَّهُ عَلَى النَّبِيِّ وَالْمُهَاجِرِينَ وَالْأَنْصَارِ الَّذِينَ اتَّبَعُوهُ فِي سَاعَةِ الْعُسْرَةِ مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا كَادَ يَزِيغُ قُلُوبُ فَرِيقٍ مِنْهُمْ ثُمَّ تَابَ عَلَيْهِمْ إِنَّهُ بِهِمْ رَءُوفٌ رَحِيمٌ

la-qad tāba Allāhu ʿalā al-nabiyyi wa-al-muhājirīna wa-al-anṣāri alladhīna ittabaʿūhu fī sāʿati al-ʿusrati min baʿdi mā kāda yazīghu qulūbu farīqin minhum thumma tāba ʿalayhim innahu bihim raʾūfun raḥīmun

English translation

The God has indeed turned toward the Prophet, the emigrants, and the helpers who followed him during the time of hardship, after the hearts of some of them nearly swerved. Then He turned toward them; He is indeed deeply compassionate and merciful toward them.

Usage analysis

سَاعَةِ الْعُسْرَةِ denotes a difficult occasion or period. Sāʿah does not necessarily mean an exact sixty-minute unit here; it can indicate a particular time or situation.

ʿUsrah is more than a shortage of money here. The verse concerns following the Prophet under difficult conditions and some hearts nearly swerving.

The difficulty can encompass external and internal pressures: limited resources, danger, exhaustion, anxiety, or conditions testing perseverance.

ʿUsrah alone specifies none of these particular instances. The historical event’s details and conditions require independent evidence.

In Al-Baqarah 2:280, ʿusrah describes someone’s financial circumstances; in Al-Tawbah 9:117 it describes a difficult collective situation.

Its Qur’anic uses therefore cannot all be restricted to poverty or financial hardship. Constraint and difficulty are general; context determines their kind.

5. Making an undertaking difficult and encountering difficulty in agreement

Al-Kahf 18:73: do not impose difficulty upon my affair

Verse text

قَالَ لَا تُؤَاخِذْنِي بِمَا نَسِيتُ وَلَا تُرْهِقْنِي مِنْ أَمْرِي عُسْرًا

qāla lā tuʾākhidhnī bi-mā nasītu wa-lā turhiqnī min amrī ʿusran

English translation

He said: “Do not hold me accountable for what I forgot, and do not impose hardship upon me in my affair.”

Usage analysis

عُسْرًا occurs with تُرْهِقْنِي, a verb associated with imposing a burden, pressure, or something that places a person in constraint.

The speaker asks that forgetting should not bring additional difficulty upon him.

ʿUsr describes neither life generally nor financial circumstances here. It concerns difficulty arising within a particular relationship and undertaking.

Hardship can therefore result from one person’s treatment of another. An already demanding undertaking can become harder through harshness or disproportionate accountability.

Grammatically, عُسْرًا is an object specifying what is imposed. The verb and noun are not identical or complete synonyms: the verb expresses imposing pressure, while the noun identifies its burden or effect.

This does not establish that all questioning or accountability is illegitimate. The request concerns forgetting and how it is treated.

Al-Ṭalāq 65:6: taʿāsur between parties

Verse text

أَسْكِنُوهُنَّ مِنْ حَيْثُ سَكَنْتُمْ مِنْ وُجْدِكُمْ وَلَا تُضَارُّوهُنَّ لِتُضَيِّقُوا عَلَيْهِنَّ وَإِنْ كُنَّ أُولَاتِ حَمْلٍ فَأَنْفِقُوا عَلَيْهِنَّ حَتَّىٰ يَضَعْنَ حَمْلَهُنَّ فَإِنْ أَرْضَعْنَ لَكُمْ فَآتُوهُنَّ أُجُورَهُنَّ وَأْتَمِرُوا بَيْنَكُمْ بِمَعْرُوفٍ وَإِنْ تَعَاسَرْتُمْ فَسَتُرْضِعُ لَهُ أُخْرَىٰ

askinūhunna min ḥaythu sakantum min wujdikum wa-lā tuḍārrūhunna li-tuḍayyiqū ʿalayhinna wa-in kunna ulāti ḥamlin fa-anfiqū ʿalayhinna ḥattā yaḍaʿna ḥamlahunna fa-in arḍaʿna lakum fa-ātūhunna ujūrahunna waʾtamirū baynakum bi-maʿrūfin wa-in taʿāsartum fa-sa-turḍiʿu lahu ukhrā

English translation

Lodge them where you dwell, according to your means, and do not harm them to constrain them. If they are pregnant, provide for them until they give birth. If they nurse for you, give them their wages, and consult among yourselves in an accepted manner. If agreement becomes difficult between you, another woman shall nurse for him.

Usage analysis

تَعَاسَرْتُمْ is this root’s only Qur’anic verb. It is Form VI and occurs in a context of discussion and agreement between parties.

Form VI can express participation or reciprocal interaction, but morphology alone does not establish that both parties are equally at fault or deliberately seek to obstruct agreement.

Before taʿāsartum, the verse addresses housing, prohibiting harm, pregnancy expenses, nursing wages, and consultation in an accepted manner.

It then says that if agreement becomes difficult, another woman can nurse the child.

Taʿāsur is therefore more than two different opinions: it is a situation in which reaching an appropriate nursing agreement encounters difficulty.

Older Arabic also uses تَعَاسَرَ الْبَيِّعَانِ for two parties to a transaction failing to reach agreement.

This accords with the verse’s construction. Difficulty can arise not in the undertaking itself, but in relationships, proposed terms, harshness, or inability to reach a shared solution.

The verse does not leave the relationship without a solution after mentioning taʿāsur. Nursing by someone else is proposed.

ʿUsr here is thus more than a condition to endure: it is a problem whose impasse may be overcome by another arrangement.

An alternative does not cancel the parents’ other responsibilities or authorize harming the mother. Prohibiting harm, paying wages, and observing what is accepted remain within the same verse.

Al-Ṭabarī on Al-Ṭalāq 65:6: difficulty in nursing arrangements

Hardship compared with ordinary disagreement

Not every difference of opinion is taʿāsur. Disagreement can form part of discussion and seeking a suitable course.

Taʿāsur becomes prominent when disagreement or harshness makes reaching an agreement difficult.

Nor does absent agreement always mean both parties are ill-intentioned. Economic conditions, genuine differences in ability to pay, or differing needs can produce difficulty.

“Agreement becomes difficult between you” therefore preserves the contextual range better than a translation that assumes hostility or equal blame without evidence.

6. Hardship and relief after constraint

Al-Ṭalāq 65:7: ease after hardship

Verse text

لِيُنْفِقْ ذُو سَعَةٍ مِنْ سَعَتِهِ وَمَنْ قُدِرَ عَلَيْهِ رِزْقُهُ فَلْيُنْفِقْ مِمَّا آتَاهُ اللَّهُ لَا يُكَلِّفُ اللَّهُ نَفْسًا إِلَّا مَا آتَاهَا سَيَجْعَلُ اللَّهُ بَعْدَ عُسْرٍ يُسْرًا

li-yunfiq dhū saʿatin min saʿatihi wa-man qudira ʿalayhi rizquhu fa-l-yunfiq mimmā ātāhu Allāhu lā yukallifu Allāhu nafsan illā mā ātāhā sa-yajʿalu Allāhu baʿda ʿusrin yusran

English translation

Let someone with ample means spend from his abundance; someone whose provision is restricted shall spend from what The God has given him. The God charges no person beyond what He has given that person. The God will bring about ease after hardship.

Usage analysis

عُسْرٍ stands opposite يُسْرًا, but the construction differs from Al-Baqarah 2:185.

In Al-Baqarah, ease and hardship are contrasted in stating divine intent. Here relief follows constraint:

سَيَجْعَلُ اللَّهُ بَعْدَ عُسْرٍ يُسْرًا.

The temporal adverb بَعْدَ expresses sequence: ease follows a difficult period.

The sentence follows a command to spend according to actual means. Someone with greater financial capacity spends from it; someone with restricted provision is not described as obligated to spend beyond what is available.

ʿUsr here concerns limited, difficult economic means, though the word itself extends beyond poverty.

The verse does more than offer hope of future relief: it first relates financial responsibility to the person’s present circumstances.

This order matters. Promised future ease does not authorize disproportionate expenditure being imposed during hardship.

“After hardship” also specifies no fixed interval. Relief is promised, but the phrase supplies no day, month, or manner of fulfillment.

7. ʿUsrā: difficulty as the outcome of a course

Al-Layl 92:10: facilitating a course toward difficulty

Verse text

فَسَنُيَسِّرُهُ لِلْعُسْرَىٰ

fa-sa-nuyassiruhu li-al-ʿusrā

English translation

We shall therefore prepare him for the difficult course.

Usage analysis

This is one of the root’s subtler uses: a verb from ي س ر accompanies a noun from ع س ر within one sentence.

فَسَنُيَسِّرُهُ belongs to ي س ر and concerns providing, preparing, or smoothing a course. الْعُسْرَى belongs to ع س ر and stands opposite الْيُسْرَى.

Al-Layl 92:7 uses فَسَنُيَسِّرُهُ لِلْيُسْرَىٰ for those choosing generosity, mindfulness, and affirmation. By contrast, Al-Layl 92:8, Al-Layl 92:9, and Al-Layl 92:10 concern those who withhold, consider themselves self-sufficient, and deny the good.

Yusrā and ʿusrā therefore concern more than an easy or difficult physical undertaking. They describe different orientations in life and their consequences.

The construction requires care. Facilitating does not itself make the destination good; it describes preparing or smoothing the course. The destination may be desirable or undesirable.

فَسَنُيَسِّرُهُ لِلْعُسْرَىٰ therefore concerns progress toward a difficult outcome being facilitated, not ʿusrā suddenly meaning ease.

The fuʿlā pattern alone also does not establish every detail of the outcome, the punishment’s severity, or its realization. The connection with difficulty is clear; its referent and consequences come from the surah’s context.

Rather than merely reporting temporary trouble, ʿusrā here names the difficult, undesirable direction of the course described.

8. Hardship in describing the day of account

Three verses use the adjectives عَسِر or عَسِير for a difficult day.

Al-Furqān 25:26

Verse text

الْمُلْكُ يَوْمَئِذٍ الْحَقُّ لِلرَّحْمَٰنِ وَكَانَ يَوْمًا عَلَى الْكَافِرِينَ عَسِيرًا

al-mulku yawmaʾidhin al-ḥaqqu li-al-raḥmāni wa-kāna yawman ʿalā al-kāfirīna ʿasīran

English translation

True sovereignty that day belongs to the Entirely Merciful, and it will be a difficult day for the disbelievers.

Usage analysis

عَسِيرًا describes the day, while عَلَى الْكَافِرِينَ specifies for whom its difficulty is stated.

ʿAsīr therefore concerns more than an external event’s intensity: the day’s difficulty is related to people’s situation.

This prevents a difficult day being generalized to everyone with identical intensity and quality without attention to the people concerned.

Al-Qamar 54:8

Verse text

مُهْطِعِينَ إِلَى الدَّاعِ يَقُولُ الْكَافِرُونَ هَٰذَا يَوْمٌ عَسِرٌ

muhṭiʿīna ilā al-dāʿi yaqūlu al-kāfirūna hādhā yawmun ʿasirun

English translation

Hastening toward the caller, the disbelievers say: “This is a difficult day.”

Usage analysis

عَسِرٌ describes يَوْمٌ.

Where the preceding example has عَسِيرًا, this has عَسِرٌ. Both belong to the field of difficulty.

The difference between faʿil and faʿīl alone does not conclusively establish that one always means less hardship and the other more. Morphological possibilities must be assessed through context and linguistic evidence.

The disbelievers themselves speak here, describing the day before them as difficult.

The verse thus presents both the situation and the response and recognition of those encountering it.

Al-Muddaththir 74:9

Verse text

فَذَٰلِكَ يَوْمَئِذٍ يَوْمٌ عَسِيرٌ

fa-dhālika yawmaʾidhin yawmun ʿasīrun

English translation

That day will therefore be a difficult day.

Usage analysis

عَسِيرٌ again describes يَوْمٌ.

The next verse, Al-Muddaththir 74:10, specifies that it is not easy for disbelievers. Difficulty is therefore connected with those who have adopted a position of denial toward truth.

Together, these verses show ع س ر describing difficulty in a final situation, extending beyond finances or everyday undertakings.

ʿAsīr itself nevertheless supplies no details of punishment or the day’s character. The word expresses difficulty; its nature and consequences require other verses.

9. Hardship accompanied by ease: maʿa and baʿda

Al-Sharḥ 94:5

Verse text

فَإِنَّ مَعَ الْعُسْرِ يُسْرًا

fa-inna maʿa al-ʿusri yusran

English translation

So indeed, with hardship there is ease.

Al-Sharḥ 94:6

Verse text

إِنَّ مَعَ الْعُسْرِ يُسْرًا

inna maʿa al-ʿusri yusran

English translation

Indeed, with hardship there is ease.

Usage analysis

These verses establish a particular relationship between hardship and ease.

Both have definite الْعُسْرِ, whereas يُسْرًا is indefinite. Their constructions are nearly identical, and repetition emphasizes the message.

The first point concerns the adverb مَعَ. The Qur’an does not say here that ease appears only once hardship has ended; it speaks of ease accompanying hardship.

The difference from Al-Ṭalāq 65:7 is apparent: there the expression is بَعْدَ عُسْرٍ يُسْرًا; here it is مَعَ الْعُسْرِ يُسْرًا.

Baʿda foregrounds temporal sequence, while maʿa expresses accompaniment. This can be explained through relief present within difficulty; some commentators instead understand a figurative expression of relief’s nearness. Maʿa alone should therefore not establish precise simultaneity of every effect of hardship and ease, though it should not silently become baʿda in translation.

Al-Taḥrīr wa-al-Tanwīr: a figurative reading of relief’s nearness

Ease accompanying hardship does not deny hardship’s reality. A situation may remain difficult while offering a way forward, a possibility of repair, or conditions for relief within it.

These are possible illustrations of accompaniment. Yusr itself is not restricted here to one particular kind of relief.

The second point concerns repeated definite الْعُسْرِ and indefinite يُسْرًا.

In Arabic, repeating a definite noun can refer back to the same known thing. Repeating an indefinite noun can allow multiple referents or introduce an instance anew.

This alone does not yield a certain mathematical rule that each hardship has exactly two separate eases.

The familiar expression “one hardship will not overcome two eases” reflects a recognized linguistic reading, but the verses themselves supply no explicit numeral two for ease. Rhetorical inference and explicit numerical wording should be distinguished.

Maḥmūd Ṣāfī’s Al-Jadwal: adverbial maʿa and the repeated nouns

The third point is the surah’s setting. Before these verses it mentions opening the breast, lifting a burden, and raising the Prophet’s remembrance. The statement arises from a particular context.

Its wording nevertheless allows a broader, hopeful understanding of difficulty and relief.

It should not become a promise that everyone in every situation will immediately obtain precisely the outcome desired. That goes beyond the expression’s direct meaning.

These verses show that hardship and ease need not be completely separate, incompatible periods. Relief can be presented as accompanying difficulty, just as another verse places it afterward.

10. Distinguishing ع س ر from neighboring roots

ʿ S R compared with Y S R (يُسْر)

The clearest contrast is with ي س ر.

In its main use, ʿusr concerns difficulty, constraint, and resistance to carrying out an undertaking. Yusr expresses ease, opening, available means, and smooth progress.

They occur directly together in Al-Baqarah 2:185, Al-Ṭalāq 65:7, Al-Sharḥ 94:5, and Al-Sharḥ 94:6.

Yusr should not be assumed to eliminate every effort, nor ʿusr to make every movement impossible. These situations have different degrees.

Al-Layl 92:10 also directs a verb of facilitating toward ʿusrā. Preparing the course and evaluating its destination must therefore be considered separately.

Study of Y S R

ʿ S R compared with Ḍ Y Q (ضِيق)

Ḍīq primarily concerns narrowness or limited space, opportunity, or capacity; ʿusr concerns difficulty performing or advancing an undertaking.

They are related in many situations but are not complete synonyms.

Limited means, a form of ḍīq, may produce ʿusr. An undertaking can also be difficult without finances or physical space being the principal cause.

In Al-Ṭalāq 65:6, لِتُضَيِّقُوا عَلَيْهِنَّ concerns intending to constrain the women, while the later تَعَاسَرْتُمْ concerns difficulty reaching agreement.

Constraint and difficulty in a relationship are close, but their linguistic and semantic roles are different.

Study of Ḍ Y Q

ʿ S R compared with Ḥ R J (حَرَج)

Qur’anic ḥaraj concerns constraint, constriction, and difficulty that can place someone under restriction or pressure.

ʿUsr also concerns difficulty, but debt, agreement, and responsibility foreground an undertaking’s relationship with means and obstacles.

This should not become an absolute boundary that ḥaraj is always purely psychological or ʿusr always purely material. Both roots’ Qur’anic uses are broader.

Study of Ḥ R J

ʿ S R compared with SH D D (شِدَّة)

Shiddah concerns intensity, firmness, hardness, and strength. Something can be intense without that intensity necessarily meaning difficulty performing an undertaking.

ʿUsr, by contrast, usually foregrounds difficulty in relation to an undertaking, circumstances, or a person.

Severe conditions can produce hardship, for example, but the two concepts remain distinct in linguistic analysis.

Study of SH D D

ʿ S R compared with Ṭ W Q (طَاقَة)

Ṭāqah concerns ability to perform or bear something; ʿusr concerns the undertaking’s difficulty and constraint.

An undertaking can remain within someone’s capacity while being difficult. Its difficulty can also exceed that person’s actual ability.

Hardship alone therefore does not prove that capacity is absent. Conversely, having some capacity does not necessarily eliminate severe pressure or real limitations.

The distinction matters in examining religious responsibility, fasting, work, debt, and family rights.

Study of Ṭ W Q

11. Morphological and grammatical points

Several features of the root’s Qur’anic constructions should be preserved.

First, ʿusr and ʿusrah are related but distributed differently. The two ʿusrah passages describe a person’s or group’s difficult situation; ʿusr also occurs opposite ease or names an imposed burden of difficulty.

This distribution is apparent in the Qur’an, but the fuʿlah pattern does not establish that ʿusrah always lasts longer or is more severe than ʿusr.

Second, عَسِر and عَسِير are both adjectives. Different patterns have different morphological possibilities, but pattern alone does not measure difficulty’s exact intensity.

Third, عُسْرَى stands opposite يُسْرَى and can name a difficult matter or course. It should not be restricted to “most difficult” unless independent evidence establishes a superlative sense in that usage.

Fourth, تَعَاسَرْتُمْ is Form VI. It concerns difficulty in a relationship and agreement here, without establishing internal intentions or equal blame.

Fifth, accompanying words and constructions matter. مَعَ الْعُسْرِ expresses accompaniment; بَعْدَ عُسْرٍ, temporal sequence; ذُو عُسْرَةٍ, a person in difficult circumstances; عَلَى الْكَافِرِينَ عَسِيرًا relates difficulty to a particular group.

Attention to grammatical construction therefore matters as much as lexical meaning when translating this root.

12. Translation proposals for Forqan

For عُسْر, “hardship,” “difficulty,” and, when appropriate, “constraint” are natural, defensible equivalents. The final choice should fit the sentence and what the difficulty concerns.

In Al-Baqarah 2:185, “and does not intend hardship for you” appropriately renders وَلَا يُرِيدُ بِكُمُ الْعُسْرَ, preserving the direct contrast with ease.

In Al-Baqarah 2:280, ذُو عُسْرَةٍ can be “someone in financial hardship.” Debt and ability to pay support this, rather than ʿusrah always meaning poverty throughout Arabic.

In Al-Tawbah 9:117, سَاعَةِ الْعُسْرَةِ is close to “the time of hardship” or “the period of difficulty.”

In Al-Kahf 18:73, وَلَا تُرْهِقْنِي مِنْ أَمْرِي عُسْرًا can be “and do not impose hardship upon me in my affair.”

In Al-Ṭalāq 65:6, وَإِنْ تَعَاسَرْتُمْ is close to “if agreement becomes difficult between you” or “if you cannot readily agree.” The first is more concise and closer to the verbal construction.

In Al-Ṭalāq 65:7, “The God will bring about ease after hardship” appropriately renders سَيَجْعَلُ اللَّهُ بَعْدَ عُسْرٍ يُسْرًا. “After” should be preserved.

Al-Layl 92:10 requires particular care. “We shall prepare him for the difficult course” can convey فَسَنُيَسِّرُهُ لِلْعُسْرَىٰ, but “course” is contextual explanation rather than an independent word in ʿusrā. A more literal “We shall therefore prepare him for difficulty” can also be considered.

“A difficult day” naturally and clearly renders عَسِر and عَسِير in describing the day of account.

In Al-Sharḥ 94:5 and Al-Sharḥ 94:6, “with hardship there is ease” is preferable to “after every hardship ease comes.” The latter changes maʿa to baʿda and adds “every” without its direct presence in the Arabic wording.

13. Final synthesis

Arabic ع س ر concerns hardship, difficulty, constraint, and a situation in which performing an undertaking or achieving a result meets obstacles and pressure.

Limited resources, difficult conditions, complexity, a resistant obstacle, or harsh interpersonal conduct can cause this. The word itself does not specify its cause in every use.

The Qur’an uses it for difficulty opposite ease, a debtor’s financial hardship, hard collective circumstances, imposing hardship on another, difficulty reaching agreement, an undesirable course, and the difficult day of account.

The range shows that ʿusr is neither an absolute synonym of poverty, complete inability, merely a feeling of suffering, nor invariably a sign of punishment.

Some verses present hardship as requiring fitting accommodation, as with an impoverished debtor. Others concern difficulty arising within a human relationship and requiring a solution, as with nursing-related taʿāsur. Elsewhere it describes a difficult situation at the day of account or the outcome of a course.

The relationship with ي س ر is central. The Qur’an speaks both of ease after hardship and ease with hardship. Their grammatical and semantic differences should be preserved in translation.

In discussing human responsibilities, the Qur’an also does not make difficulty an end desirable in itself. Alongside commands and obligations it attends to capacity, means, actual circumstances, and suitable forms of relief.

Final definition: In its principal Qur’anic field, ع س ر denotes hardship and constraint that make performing an undertaking, enduring a situation, or obtaining a result difficult, whether arising from external conditions, limited means, or relationships. Its kind, intensity, cause, and consequence must be determined in each verse’s context.

“Hardship” and “difficulty” are therefore the basic translations; “constraint,” “financial hardship,” and “difficulty reaching agreement” fit particular uses with contextual support.

This preserves the root’s principal meaning while allowing each verse’s legal, social, ethical, and theological questions to be examined in their own place rather than imposed on the word.
