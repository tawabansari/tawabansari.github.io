The Qur’anic Root S B L

Sabīl: a way along which one can travel

The root S B L appears in the Qur’an entirely through the noun sabīl and its plural subul. Other verbs of this family known in Arabic do not occur in the Qur’anic text.

The direct meaning of sabīl is a way or path along which movement is possible. The Qur’an uses the same word both for an actual route across the land and for a method, direction, means of access, or course a person follows in life, belief, and action.

This range gives rise to expressions such as sabīl Allāh, sabīl al-rushd, sabīl al-ghayy, sabīl al-muʾminīn, sabīl al-mujrimīn, subul al-salām, and ibn al-sabīl.

Other branches of this Arabic word family concern extension, hanging down, and flowing. Forms of the family are used of a lowered curtain, falling rain, and things that extend. Sabīl has accordingly been associated with a path stretching out before someone. This shared imagery does not, by itself, establish the historical order in which these meanings developed.

The imagery is worth examining, but the clearer semantic core in the Qur’an is a traversable way and the possibility of moving in a direction. The image of rain or hanging down should not be imported into every occurrence of sabīl.

An initial definition is:

S B L: a way or path along which one can move or reach a destination; by extension, a method, direction, opportunity, or channel through which someone reaches an outcome or another person. Being good or bad is not part of the word itself: the accompanying noun, preposition, and context establish what kind of way is meant.

Pre-Islamic Arabic: physical and figurative ways

Sabīl was already a familiar word for a way before the Qur’an.

A verse attributed to al-Musayyab ibn ʿAlas reads:

خَلُّوا سَبِيلَ بَكْرِنَا إِنَّ بَكْرَنَا
يَخُدُّ سَنَامَ الأَكْحَلِ الْمُتَمَاحِلِ

Khallū sabīla bakrinā inna bakranā / yakhuddu sanāma l-akḥali l-mutamāḥili.

In khallū sabīla bakrinā, the expression khallū sabīla means to leave the way open and not obstruct movement. Understanding this use of sabīl does not require a definitive identification of what bakrinā represents in the poetic image.

This use conveys more than the existence of a road: whether a way is open or blocked determines whether movement is possible. This capacity matters in Qur’anic passages where sabīl comes to express an opportunity to act or gain access.

A verse attributed to Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd also reads:

وَكَيْفَ تَضِلُّ الْقَصْدَ وَالْحَقُّ وَاضِحٌ
وَلِلْحَقِّ بَيْنَ الصَّالِحِينَ سَبِيلُ

Wa-kayfa taḍillu l-qaṣda wa-l-ḥaqqu wāḍiḥun / wa-li-l-ḥaqqi bayna ṣ-ṣāliḥīna sabīlu.

“How can you lose the right course when truth is clear, and truth has a way among the righteous?”

Here sabīl is no longer merely a road across the land: truth, too, has a way.

The extension from a physical way to a course of conduct, thought, or purpose thus already existed in the language before the Qur’an. The Qur’an draws on that capacity across a much wider range of expressions.

Ways across the land, ways through life, and the plural subul

The physical use of sabīl is clear in the Qur’an:

وَإِنَّهَا لَبِسَبِيلٍ مُقِيمٍ

Wa-innahā la-bi-sabīlin muqīmin.

“And it lies on an established route.”

Al-Ḥijr 15:76

And:

وَأَلْقَىٰ فِي الْأَرْضِ رَوَاسِيَ أَنْ تَمِيدَ بِكُمْ وَأَنْهَارًا وَسُبُلًا لَعَلَّكُمْ تَهْتَدُونَ

Wa-alqā fī l-arḍi rawāsiya an tamīda bikum wa-anhāran wa-subulan laʿallakum tahtadūna.

“And He placed firm mountains in the earth, lest it sway with you, and rivers and paths, so that you might find your way.”

Al-Naḥl 16:15

Also:

لِتَسْلُكُوا مِنْهَا سُبُلًا فِجَاجًا

Li-taslukū minhā subulan fijājan.

“So that you may travel along its broad paths.”

Nūḥ 71:20

In these verses the way is actually traversed.

The same image carries into the direction of a person’s life:

وَإِنْ يَرَوْا سَبِيلَ الرُّشْدِ لَا يَتَّخِذُوهُ سَبِيلًا وَإِنْ يَرَوْا سَبِيلَ الْغَيِّ يَتَّخِذُوهُ سَبِيلًا

Wa-in yaraw sabīla r-rushdi lā yattakhidhūhu sabīlan wa-in yaraw sabīla l-ghayyi yattakhidhūhu sabīlan.

“If they see the way of sound guidance, they do not adopt it as a way; but if they see the way of error, they adopt it as a way.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:146

This verse matters for the root’s definition. Sabīl itself means neither guidance nor misguidance. The same word is combined first with:

الرشد

Al-rushd.

And then with:

الغي

Al-ghayy.

The accompanying nouns supply the contrasting directions.

The same neutrality appears in:

سَبِيلَ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ

Sabīla l-muʾminīna.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:115

And:

سَبِيلَ الْمُجْرِمِينَ

Sabīla l-mujrimīna.

Al-Anʿām 6:55

These expressions identify the way of the believers and the way of the offenders respectively.

Sabīl should therefore not automatically be translated as “the way of truth.” The noun denotes a way; the noun attached to it identifies its direction.

The same principle applies to the singular and plural.

Al-Anʿām 6:153 says:

وَأَنَّ هَٰذَا صِرَاطِي مُسْتَقِيمًا فَاتَّبِعُوهُ وَلَا تَتَّبِعُوا السُّبُلَ فَتَفَرَّقَ بِكُمْ عَنْ سَبِيلِهِ

Wa-anna hādhā ṣirāṭī mustaqīman fa-ttabiʿūhu wa-lā tattabiʿū s-subula fa-tafarraqa bikum ʿan sabīlihi.

“And this is My path, straight, so follow it; and do not follow the other ways, lest they separate you from His way.”

This verse might suggest that one sabīl is good while a plurality of subul is always bad. Other Qur’anic uses rule out that generalization.

The Qur’an says:

يَهْدِي بِهِ اللَّهُ مَنِ اتَّبَعَ رِضْوَانَهُ سُبُلَ السَّلَامِ

Yahdī bihi llāhu mani ttabaʿa riḍwānahu subula s-salāmi.

“Through it The God guides those who seek His approval to the ways of peace.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:16

And:

وَالَّذِينَ جَاهَدُوا فِينَا لَنَهْدِيَنَّهُمْ سُبُلَنَا

Wa-lladhīna jāhadū fīnā la-nahdiyannahum subulanā.

“As for those who strive for Us, We will surely guide them to Our ways.”

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:69

Even the bee is addressed with:

فَاسْلُكِي سُبُلَ رَبِّكِ ذُلُلًا

Fa-slukī subula rabbiki dhululan.

“Then travel the ways of your Lord, made easy to traverse.” This translation takes dhululan as describing the condition of the ways. Grammatically it can also describe the bee: “Then travel the ways of your Lord obediently.” Both analyses are compatible with subul meaning ways.

Al-Naḥl 16:69

Thus subul is simply the plural of sabīl and has no inherently negative value. Multiple ways can be “ways of peace,” or ways that lead people away from “His way.”

Sabīl Allāh: the direction of an action, not the name of one particular action

One of the most important expressions in this family is sabīl Allāh, “the way of The God.”

In some verses it accompanies fighting:

فَقَاتِلْ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ

Fa-qātil fī sabīli llāhi.

“So fight in the way of The God.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:84

But the same expression also accompanies spending:

الَّذِينَ يُنْفِقُونَ أَمْوَالَهُمْ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ

Alladhīna yunfiqūna amwālahum fī sabīli llāhi.

“Those who spend their wealth in the way of The God.”

Al-Baqarah 2:262

With migration:

وَمَنْ يُهَاجِرْ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ

Wa-man yuhājir fī sabīli llāhi.

“And whoever migrates in the way of The God…”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:100

And concerning those killed:

وَلَا تَقُولُوا لِمَنْ يُقْتَلُ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ أَمْوَاتٌ

Wa-lā taqūlū li-man yuqtalu fī sabīli llāhi amwātun.

Al-Baqarah 2:154

People can also be prevented from following the way of The God (Al-Ḥajj 22:25):

وَيَصُدُّونَ عَنْ سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ

Wa-yaṣuddūna ʿan sabīli llāhi.

And invited to it:

ادْعُ إِلَىٰ سَبِيلِ رَبِّكَ بِالْحِكْمَةِ وَالْمَوْعِظَةِ الْحَسَنَةِ

Udʿu ilā sabīli rabbika bi-l-ḥikmati wa-l-mawʿiẓati l-ḥasanati.

“Invite to the way of your Lord with wisdom and good counsel.”

Al-Naḥl 16:125

These uses show that sabīl Allāh is not a lexical synonym for warfare. Fighting can be “in the way of The God”; spending and migration can also have that same direction.

The preposition changes the relationship to the way:

فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ
Fī sabīli llāh: in the way of The God;

عَنْ سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ
ʿAn sabīli llāh: from the way of The God, in expressions of preventing or turning away;

إِلَىٰ سَبِيلِ رَبِّكَ
Ilā sabīli rabbika: to the way of your Lord, in an expression of inviting.

Sabīl supplies the direction; the sentence’s verb shows what the person does in relation to that direction.

From a way to access: sabīl ʿalā and ibn al-sabīl

The physical meaning of a way also extends in the Qur’an to the possibility of reaching someone or taking action.

Al-Nisāʾ says:

فَإِنْ أَطَعْنَكُمْ فَلَا تَبْغُوا عَلَيْهِنَّ سَبِيلًا

Fa-in aṭaʿnakum fa-lā tabghū ʿalayhinna sabīlan.

“Then if they obey you, do not seek a way against them.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:34

Here sabīl is not a road. Seeking “a way against them” means finding a channel for action, interference, or a pretext to proceed against them.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:90 also says:

فَمَا جَعَلَ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ عَلَيْهِمْ سَبِيلًا

Fa-mā jaʿala llāhu lakum ʿalayhim sabīlan.

“The God has given you no way against them.”

And in Al-Tawbah:

مَا عَلَى الْمُحْسِنِينَ مِنْ سَبِيلٍ

Mā ʿalā l-muḥsinīna min sabīlin.

“There is no way against those who do good.”

Al-Tawbah 9:91

Then:

إِنَّمَا السَّبِيلُ عَلَى الَّذِينَ يَسْتَأْذِنُونَكَ وَهُمْ أَغْنِيَاءُ

Innamā s-sabīlu ʿalā lladhīna yastaʾdhinūnaka wa-hum aghniyāʾu.

“The way [of reproach] is only against those who ask your permission while they are wealthy.”

Al-Tawbah 9:93

In such constructions, having “a way against someone” approaches the idea of a legal, moral, or practical ground for calling them to account or acting against them. The context determines its exact nature; sabīl alone does not equal “dominion,” “punishment,” or “a judicial entitlement.”

The same extension appears in:

فَخَلُّوا سَبِيلَهُمْ

Fa-khallū sabīlahum.

“Then leave their way open.”

Al-Tawbah 9:5

Leaving the way open means not obstructing their movement.

Ibn al-sabīl

The expression ibn al-sabīl repeatedly appears alongside people in need or entitled to financial support:

وَآتَى الْمَالَ عَلَىٰ حُبِّهِ ذَوِي الْقُرْبَىٰ وَالْيَتَامَىٰ وَالْمَسَاكِينَ وَابْنَ السَّبِيلِ

Wa-ātā l-māla ʿalā ḥubbihi dhawī l-qurbā wa-l-yatāmā wa-l-masākīna wa-bna s-sabīli.

Al-Baqarah 2:177

And:

وَآتِ ذَا الْقُرْبَىٰ حَقَّهُ وَالْمِسْكِينَ وَابْنَ السَّبِيلِ

Wa-āti dhā l-qurbā ḥaqqahu wa-l-miskīna wa-bna s-sabīli.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:26

In this construction, ibn does not express biological descent. Arabic can associate a person with something they closely accompany or are connected to. Ibn al-sabīl, literally “son of the way,” is someone whose situation is defined by the road and who accompanies it through travelling.

Its basic meaning is a traveller.

In passages about spending and financial rights, this traveller may be someone who needs assistance because they are far from home and their own resources. “A stranded traveller” can therefore suit many of these contexts.

However, “a stranded traveller” should not be treated as the expression’s exclusive lexical meaning. Ibn al-sabīl first identifies the person’s relationship to travel and the road; financial need emerges from the relevant passages’ context.

The grammatical gender of sabīl and its similarity to ṭarīq

In Arabic, sabīl can be either masculine or feminine. The Qur’an preserves both patterns of agreement.

In Yūsuf we read:

قُلْ هَٰذِهِ سَبِيلِي

Qul hādhihi sabīlī.

“Say: this is my way.”

Yūsuf 12:108

Hādhihi is feminine.

In Hūd as well:

الَّذِينَ يَصُدُّونَ عَنْ سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ وَيَبْغُونَهَا عِوَجًا

Alladhīna yaṣuddūna ʿan sabīli llāhi wa-yabghūnahā ʿiwajan.

“Those who turn people away from the way of The God and seek to make it crooked.”

Hūd 11:19

The pronoun -hā is feminine.

But in Al-Aʿrāf:

وَإِنْ يَرَوْا سَبِيلَ الرُّشْدِ لَا يَتَّخِذُوهُ سَبِيلًا

Wa-in yaraw sabīla r-rushdi lā yattakhidhūhu sabīlan.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:146

The pronoun -hu is masculine.

This difference does not establish two different meanings of sabīl and should not be turned into a semantic interpretation. The word itself accepts either grammatical gender in Arabic.

Ṭarīq is likewise a word that can behave as masculine or feminine. The resemblance between sabīl and ṭarīq therefore extends beyond their meaning “way”: both allow variation in grammatical gender.

This is primarily a grammatical fact rather than part of the root’s semantic core. Explaining it nevertheless helps prevent a change in pronoun or demonstrative from being mistaken for a change in meaning.

S B L (sabīl), Ṣ R Ṭ (ṣirāṭ), and Ṭ R Q (ṭarīq): three words for a way

Sabīl, ṣirāṭ, and ṭarīq can all be translated as “way” or “path,” but their uses are not identical.

Sabīl has a very broad range. It can denote a physical path, a direction in life, a group’s way, the way of The God, a way of sound guidance or error, access to something, or a channel for acting against someone. Its plural subul is also entirely natural.

Ṣirāṭ is particularly prominent in the Qur’an’s language of guidance:

الصِّرَاطَ الْمُسْتَقِيمَ

Al-ṣirāṭa l-mustaqīma.

And:

هَٰذَا صِرَاطِي مُسْتَقِيمًا

Hādhā ṣirāṭī mustaqīman.

Al-Anʿām 6:153 even places ṣirāṭ and subul alongside one another in a single construction.

This does not mean that ṣirāṭ is intrinsically right while sabīl is intrinsically neutral or misguided. The Qur’an itself speaks of subul al-salām, “ways of peace,” and subulanā, “Our ways.” Moreover, ṣirāṭ al-jaḥīm, “the path of the blazing Fire,” in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:23 shows that the destination of a ṣirāṭ is also determined by context, rather than by assuming that the word inherently denotes truth.

Ṭarīq likewise denotes a physical way:

فَاضْرِبْ لَهُمْ طَرِيقًا فِي الْبَحْرِ يَبَسًا

Fa-ḍrib lahum ṭarīqan fī l-baḥri yabasan.

“Then open for them a dry path through the sea.”

Ṭā Hā 20:77

And a figurative way:

يَهْدِي إِلَى الْحَقِّ وَإِلَىٰ طَرِيقٍ مُسْتَقِيمٍ

Yahdī ilā l-ḥaqqi wa-ilā ṭarīqin mustaqīmin.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:30

As tendencies of usage, we can say:

Sabīl is a way through which a destination, outcome, or another person is reached, and thus readily extends to “method, direction, and means of access”;

Ṭarīq more often foregrounds the traversable path itself;

And:

Ṣirāṭ is especially prominent in Qur’anic expressions of the direction of guidance.

These distinctions are not absolute. The Qur’an itself uses ṭarīq mustaqīm, sabīl al-rushd, and subul al-salām. The comparison identifies patterns in each word’s use; it should not impose boundaries that the Qur’an itself does not maintain.

Translation proposals for Forqan

In physical uses, sabīl is usually “way” or “path,” while subulan fijājan can be rendered “broad paths.”

In figurative uses, retaining “way” often best preserves the Qur’anic image: sabīl al-rushd, “the way of sound guidance”; sabīl al-ghayy, “the way of error”; sabīl al-muʾminīn, “the way of the believers”; and hādhihi sabīlī, “this is my way.”

Sabīl Allāh is best retained as “the way of The God.” In fī sabīli llāh, “in the way of The God” preserves the expression’s breadth; the sentence’s verb identifies the action.

For fa-lā tabghū ʿalayhinna sabīlan, a close rendering is “do not seek a way against them.” More idiomatic English could be “do not seek a pretext for action against them,” provided that “pretext” is understood as contextual, not a fixed meaning of sabīl.

For mā ʿalā l-muḥsinīna min sabīlin, “there is no ground for reproach against those who do good” makes the contextual meaning clear.

Ibn al-sabīl basically means “traveller.” In contexts of financial rights, it can be rendered “a traveller in need” or “a stranded traveller.” The latter must not become its exclusive lexical definition.

Conclusion

The Qur’anic core of S B L is a traversable way or path, and the possibility of moving or reaching something through it. That way can lie across the land or within belief, action, and life. From this image, sabīl extends to a method or direction, access, a channel for action, and a way to an outcome.

The word is not inherently positive or negative. The Qur’an speaks of sabīl al-rushd and sabīl al-ghayy, the ways of sound guidance and error, and sabīl al-muʾminīn and sabīl al-mujrimīn, the ways of believers and offenders. The plural subul is not inherently negative either: “ways of peace” and “Our ways” appear alongside ways that separate people from “His way.”

Sabīl Allāh does not name one particular action such as fighting. Fighting, spending, migration, and other actions can take place in this way. In sabīl ʿalā fulān, “a way against someone,” the image extends to access and action against another person; in ibn al-sabīl, the traveller is associated with the way through close involvement with it.

Grammatically, sabīl can be masculine or feminine, and the Qur’an preserves both patterns. Ṭarīq allows the same variation. This variation in grammatical gender does not create a new meaning for the word.

A concise definition of the root is:

S B L: a way or path along which one can move or reach a destination, outcome, or another person. From this physical meaning develop the uses “method, direction, means of access, and channel for action.” A sabīl can be physical or figurative, right or wrong, divine or otherwise; the sentence’s construction and context determine its direction and value.
