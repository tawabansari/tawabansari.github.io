Covering, veiling, concealment from view, and removal of a covering

The Arabic root gh-ṭ-w concerns covering, concealing, and being beneath a covering. Ghaṭā means covering or enveloping something, ghaṭṭā means covering it, and ghiṭāʾ denotes something placed over an object, concealing it from view or direct access.

Physically, a ghiṭāʾ can cover a vessel, the body, or another object. More broadly, it can describe an obstacle concealing a truth, feature, or reality from human sight and understanding.

The related expression ghaṭā al-laylu describes night becoming dark and spreading, since darkness can cover objects and make them harder to see.

The root occurs only twice in the Quran, both times as the noun ghiṭāʾ: in Al-Kahf 18:101 and Qaf 50:22.

The first describes people's eyes as covered in relation to remembrance of God. The second describes removing a covering that had prevented someone from seeing the reality now confronting them.

These uses show that the Quran can employ covering as an image for limitations of sight and understanding without requiring a physical curtain over the eyes in each case.

1. The Root in Early Arabic

Early Arabic records ghaṭā al-shayʾa for covering and concealing something. Ghiṭāʾ denotes a covering placed over something, hiding it from view or direct contact.

Placing a lid over a vessel, for example, hides its contents from direct view. The contents have not disappeared; a covering simply stands between them and the observer.

Similarly, a cloth laid over an object can obscure its shape or features. Removing the cloth adds nothing new to the object; it reveals what was already there.

This illustrates the basic sense of ghiṭāʾ: something that covers an object or prevents it from being manifest.

It does not follow that every covering serves deception or concealment of truth. Covering a vessel, body, or implement may serve protection, preservation, comfort, or another ordinary purpose.

Lexically, covering is therefore neither necessarily commendable nor blameworthy. Context determines its type, purpose, and result.

An Early Witness: Concealing Ignorance in Poetry Attributed to Hassan ibn Thabit

A verse attributed to Hassan ibn Thabit reads:

رُبَّ حِلْمٍ أَضَاعَهُ عَدَمُ الْمَالِ

rubba ḥilmin aḍāʿahu ʿadamu al-māli

وَجَهْلٍ غَطَّى عَلَيْهِ النَّعِيمُ

wa-jahlin ghaṭṭā ʿalayhi al-naʿīmu

Translation:

“Many a sound judgment has had its worth wasted by lack of wealth, and many an ignorance has been covered over by prosperity.”

The poet contrasts two situations.

Someone may be wise and forbearing, yet poverty causes their worth and standing to go unrecognized.

Conversely, someone may be ignorant, yet wealth and prosperity adorn their appearance so that others fail to notice that ignorance.

Ghaṭṭā ʿalayhi al-naʿīmu does not here mean covering a physical object with cloth.

Prosperity does not eliminate the person's ignorance; it may prevent it from becoming apparent.

This illustrates the family's use in early poetry for concealing a feature or reality as well as physically covering something.

Literary accounts attribute the verse to Hassan ibn Thabit and report that he composed it before Muhammad's commissioning. Transmission of that report in later sources does not by itself establish its date with certainty.

The witness matters because figurative covering was recognized and semantically intelligible within the early Arabic poetic tradition.

Another Witness: A Covered Face and Hidden Standing

The following verse appears in lexical sources without a firm attribution in the cited entry; it is also transmitted in Hassan ibn Thabit's collected poetry with variant wording:

أَنَا ابْنُ كِلَابٍ وَابْنُ أَوْسٍ فَمَنْ يَكُنْ

anā ibnu Kilābin wa-bnu Awsin fa-man yakun

قِنَاعُهُ مَغْطِيًّا فَإِنِّي مُجْتَلَى

qināʿuhu maghṭiyyan fa-innī mujtalā

Translation:

“I am a son of Kilab and of Aws; whoever has his veil drawn over him, I stand revealed.”

Maghṭiyyan concerns being covered and contrasts with mujtalā, meaning revealed or manifest.

The poet uses covering to discuss whether a person's standing is apparent or hidden.

The contrast distinguishes covering from being revealed.

Attribution in a collection does not independently date this verse. It should not be presented as securely pre-Islamic; it illustrates recorded Arabic usage rather than establishing when the root first arose.

2. Core Meaning and Branches of Usage

A. Ghiṭāʾ: Something That Covers

Ghiṭāʾ denotes something placed over an object to cover it.

In ordinary Arabic usage it can cover a vessel, bed, head, or another object.

Its defining feature is functioning as a covering.

When a lid covers a food container, the food remains inside but is no longer directly visible.

This distinction between existing and being apparent matters in figurative uses.

A reality may exist while an obstacle prevents someone from seeing or understanding it correctly.

Figurative use does not require a material object between the eye and the truth.

B. Ghaṭā and Ghaṭṭā: Covering and Enveloping

Arabic records ghaṭā al-shayʾa and ghaṭṭā al-shayʾa for covering something.

Ghaṭṭā is Form II and commonly describes placing a covering over an object.

Form II alone does not establish repeated covering or a particular intensity.

A single act of laying a cloth over a vessel, for example, may cover it.

Covering is the clear meaning; frequency, extent, and purpose depend on the sentence and situation.

C. Ghaṭā al-Laylu: Night-Time Darkness Spreading

Arabic records ghaṭā al-laylu for night becoming dark and its darkness spreading.

Night is pictured as a covering over objects.

Mountains, trees, and paths are easily visible in daylight, but those same objects may disappear from human view as night falls.

Darkness has not destroyed the mountain or tree; it has reduced the ability to see them.

This image relates to covering.

Night-time darkness should not be equated with deliberate concealment of truth. Night belongs to the natural order of creation, while hiding truth can be a deliberate human act.

Nor should ghaṭā al-laylu be identified with aghṭasha laylahā in An-Naziʿat 79:29. Both concern night-time darkness, but they belong to different roots.

D. Covering and Extending over Something

Arabic sources also record ghaṭā for rising and extending over something.

Related forms describe a plant whose branches grow and spread over its surroundings.

Ghaṭā al-māʾu is likewise reported for water rising and covering or enveloping something.

Some uses emphasize movement and rising, while others emphasize the result: covering a surface or the surrounding space.

The relationship is easy to picture: rising water can cover previously exposed land.

This image does not prove that every use developed from rising. Dictionaries record both elevation and covering, but their historical sequence is uncertain.

E. Ghiṭāya: An Under-Covering

Ghiṭāya is also recorded for a kind of women's under-covering or undergarment.

Here the covering conceals the body or part of it.

Lexical accounts also explain the form through substitution of yāʾ for wāw.

This is morphologically significant because Arabic sources record both wāw and yāʾ forms within the family.

This clothing-related use does not restrict ghiṭāʾ in the Quran's two occurrences to clothing.

3. Important Forms and Derivatives

Recognized Arabic forms include:

  • Ghaṭā / yaghṭū: covered or enveloped; of night, became dark.
  • Ghaṭw and ghuṭuww: covering or spreading over, depending on construction and use.
  • Ghaṭṭā / yughaṭṭī: covered something.
  • Taghṭiya: covering or placing a covering over something.
  • Ghiṭāʾ: a covering, lid, or veil.
  • Aghṭiya: coverings, the plural of ghiṭāʾ.
  • Taghaṭṭā: covered oneself or came under a covering.
  • Ightaṭā: covered oneself or became concealed.
  • Aghṭā: covered; also used for plant branches growing and spreading.
  • Ghāṭin: covering; of night, dark.
  • Ghiṭāya: an under-covering or undergarment.
  • Maghṭiyy: covered.
  • Ghaṭayān: in some uses, water rising and spreading over.

Only the noun ghiṭāʾ occurs in the Quran.

The verbal forms ghaṭā, ghaṭṭā, taghaṭṭā, and aghṭā do not occur in its text.

A Wāw or Yāʾ Root?

Arabic dictionaries discuss covering-related forms under both gh-ṭ-w and gh-ṭ-y.

The final weak radical appears as wāw in some formations and yāʾ in others; the form alone does not always determine the dictionary's underlying classification.

Ghaṭā yaghṭū displays wāw, while ghaṭṭā yughaṭṭī displays yāʾ. Such a surface yāʾ does not by itself prove an independently yāʾ-final root, since weak-letter changes must also be considered.

The Quranic morphological index places ghiṭāʾ under gh-ṭ-w.

This is a suitable organizing basis for Quranic study, but it does not prove a single historically established ancestral form for every wāw and yāʾ variant.

What is clear in the principal uses is their shared semantic field of covering.

The Form Ghiṭāʾ

Ghiṭāʾ is a noun denoting a means of covering or something placed over another object to cover it.

The Quran uses it twice:

As ghiṭāʾin in Al-Kahf 18:101.

As ghiṭāʾaka in Qaf 50:22.

In the second form, attached -ka produces the meaning “your covering.”

These are not independent derivatives but occurrences of the same noun in different syntactic constructions.

4. Ghiṭāʾin: Eyes within a Covering

Al-Kahf 18:101

الَّذِينَ كَانَتْ أَعْيُنُهُمْ فِي غِطَاءٍ عَنْ ذِكْرِي وَكَانُوا لَا يَسْتَطِيعُونَ سَمْعًا

alladhīna kānat aʿyunuhum fī ghiṭāʾin ʿan dhikrī wa-kānū lā yastaṭīʿūna samʿā

Al-Kahf 18:101

“Those whose eyes were within a covering from My remembrance, and who were unable to hear.”

Here ghiṭāʾ is associated with human eyes.

The issue is not simply covering the eyes with cloth or a physical obstacle.

Aʿyunuhum fī ghiṭāʾin ʿan dhikrī describes people's eyes as covered in relation to remembrance of God.

People possess eyes yet remain veiled in relation to a truth to which they should attend.

Why “Their Eyes Were within a Covering”?

Arabic fī basically expresses being within or amid something.

The verse does not merely say a covering was placed “over” their eyes; it says their eyes were “within a covering.”

This construction pictures their sight as situated in obscurity.

Someone standing in thick fog, for example, has eyes and can look, yet the fog prevents a clear view of distant objects.

Neither the eyes nor the objects have ceased to exist; the conditions of seeing have changed.

This can make covering tangible, but it does not mean the verse describes actual fog, a curtain, or physical matter covering their eyes.

The relevant relation is ʿan dhikrī: in relation to remembrance of God.

ʿAn Dhikrī: Veiled from Remembrance of God

ʿAn specifies the relationship between the eyes' covering and remembrance of God.

Dhikrī belongs to dh-k-r and means My remembrance.

Quranic dhikr can concern remembrance, revealed speech, and what directs people toward truth.

Ghiṭāʾ here should therefore not be translated as a physical inability to see anything whatsoever.

People are veiled in relation to remembrance of God and the truth presented to them.

Someone may observe clear signs of injustice yet be so bound by prejudice or self-interest that they refuse to acknowledge it.

The issue need not be missing information or physical blindness; the person's relationship and response to truth matter.

This illustrates the image's application to understanding. It does not automatically make every disagreement or failure to reach a particular conclusion an instance of this verse.

“And They Were Unable to Hear”

The verse continues by describing inability to hear.

Placed alongside covered eyes, this completes the picture.

Seeing and hearing are two important avenues of reception and understanding.

Their eyes are described as covered from remembrance of God, and their ability to hear is negated.

This does not establish that all these people were physically blind or deaf.

Quranic hearing sometimes extends beyond receiving physical sound to attending, accepting, and engaging seriously with speech.

Someone may hear words audibly yet refuse to attend to their reasoning and truth.

This differs from physical deafness.

The image alone does not conclusively establish each person's intention or awareness. Responsibility requires considering what they knew, what truth reached them, and how they responded.

The Verse's Context

Al-Kahf 18:100 describes Hell being presented to the disbelievers on the Day of Resurrection.

Al-Kahf 18:101 describes that group through eyes covered from remembrance of God and inability to hear.

Al-Kahf 18:102 concerns taking God's servants as protecting allies instead of Him.

Al-Kahf 18:103 and Al-Kahf 18:104 then describe those who lose most through their deeds: their effort goes astray in worldly life while they think they are doing good.

The sequence involves more than failing to see a physical fact: understanding, judgment, and confidence in one's own path are also at issue.

People may feel satisfied with their activity and believe they act correctly while the reality of their conduct differs.

This does not make every mistaken person a deliberate concealer of truth. Honest error, being deceived, and knowingly persisting in falsehood are different situations.

5. Ghiṭāʾaka: Removing the Covering on the Day of Reckoning

Qaf 50:22

لَقَدْ كُنْتَ فِي غَفْلَةٍ مِنْ هَٰذَا فَكَشَفْنَا عَنْكَ غِطَاءَكَ فَبَصَرُكَ الْيَوْمَ حَدِيدٌ

laqad kunta fī ghaflatin min hādhā fa-kashafnā ʿanka ghiṭāʾaka fa-baṣaruka al-yawma ḥadīd

Qāf 50:22

“You were indeed heedless of this; then We removed your covering from you, and today your sight is keen.”

Ghiṭāʾ here appears as ghiṭāʾaka: your covering.

Unlike Al-Kahf 18:101, the subject is removal rather than remaining covered.

Fa-kashafnā ʿanka ghiṭāʾaka means that We removed from you the covering that was over you.

It follows the description of death, the blowing of the Trumpet, and people's arrival at the reckoning.

“You Were Indeed in Heedlessness”

The verse begins with heedlessness.

Ghafla belongs to gh-f-l and concerns inattention or something escaping awareness.

Ghiṭāʾ follows it.

The words are related but distinct.

Ghafla describes the person's inattention or unawareness.

Ghiṭāʾ supplies the image of a covering preventing something from becoming apparent to them.

Someone may be heedless of a future danger, paying no attention or not taking it seriously.

Once it occurs and they see the result, they cannot remain unaware of it as before.

Removing the covering in this example means a reality previously outside clear understanding becoming manifest.

This image relates to the context of Qaf 50:22.

“Then We Removed Your Covering from You”

Kashafa belongs to the independent root k-sh-f, concerning removal of a covering and disclosure.

Fa-kashafnā expresses removal, while ghiṭāʾaka names what is removed.

The construction distinguishes covering from disclosure.

Ghiṭāʾ names the covering or obstacle.

Kashf is the act of setting it aside or removing it.

An object beneath a cloth is covered; when the cloth is moved aside, the object becomes visible again.

Removing the cloth does not create the object's reality; it enables it to be seen.

The verse likewise associates removing ghiṭāʾ with clearer sight.

This illustration does not prove that a physical curtain will necessarily be lifted from the eyes on the Day of Resurrection or that the verse describes a new bodily mechanism of sight.

Covering and its removal here convey reality becoming manifest at the reckoning.

“Today Your Sight Is Keen”

The verse then describes the person's sight as ḥadīd.

Ḥadīd here concerns sharp, penetrating sight.

It belongs to another root whose meaning should not be transferred to gh-ṭ-w.

In Arabic, sharp sight means being able to see and distinguish more clearly.

After removal of the covering, sight is described as keen and clear.

The relationship matters: the verse does not say a new truth has just been created, but that the person encounters a reality of which they were previously heedless.

Someone may have heard warnings for years about the consequences of an action without taking them seriously. When those consequences appear, their occurrence is no longer doubtful.

This illustrates the difference between prior awareness and witnessing the result.

The verse itself does not explain the nature of human senses after death or a scientific mechanism of awareness in the hereafter.

Who Is Addressed?

Qaf 50:21 describes every soul arriving with a driver and a witness.

Qaf 50:22 then uses singular forms: kunta, ʿanka, ghiṭāʾaka, and baṣaruka.

This singular address directs the scene toward a person now confronted with reality.

Two important readings of its scope deserve consideration.

One relates it to a heedless denier, since the following verses concern disbelievers and those who opposed truth.

A broader reading takes every soul's arrival at the reckoning as a setting for addressing the human being before the reality of the hereafter.

Neither reading changes ghiṭāʾ: the obstacle has been removed and reality has become clearer to the addressee.

The scope of the address requires contextual examination. The singular form does not prove that only one particular historical person is addressed.

6. The Relationship between the Two Quranic Uses

The two occurrences describe different situations.

In Al-Kahf 18:101, people's eyes are covered from remembrance of God.

In Qaf 50:22, the covering is removed and sight becomes keen.

The images are related but should not be restricted without evidence to one event or group.

The first concerns being veiled from remembrance of God.

The second concerns reality becoming manifest in a scene encountered after death and resurrection.

Neither use of ghiṭāʾ means that truth itself has been destroyed.

A reality can exist while an obstacle, heedlessness, or the person's state of understanding prevents clear perception.

Removing the covering can enable clearer sight and understanding.

Truth becoming manifest and benefiting from it are nevertheless different questions.

Someone may recognize past mistakes after witnessing their consequences; such late recognition does not necessarily permit returning and correcting everything that has passed.

As-Sajda 32:12 likewise describes people who, after witnessing their outcome, say they have now seen and heard and ask to return.

This relationship distinguishes clear perception in the hereafter from the opportunity to act in worldly life.

7. Distinctions from Related Roots

Gh-ṭ-w Compared with S-t-r: Covering and Concealing

Arabic satr concerns covering and concealing something.

Ghiṭāʾ likewise denotes a covering that hides something from view.

Their semantic fields overlap.

A cloth over a vessel concealing its contents can be described through either concept.

Ghiṭāʾ chiefly names the covering itself, while satr can emphasize covering as an act or being concealed.

This is a tendency, not an absence of overlap between the roots.

Gh-ṭ-w Compared with Ḥ-j-b: Covering and Forming a Barrier

Arabic ḥijāb denotes something that separates two sides or forms a barrier between them.

Ghiṭāʾ can also obstruct sight, but its basic image is a covering over something.

A wall between rooms can function as a ḥijāb without lying over the objects inside.

A covering over a vessel, by contrast, is a ghiṭāʾ.

A ghiṭāʾ may serve as a barrier, but not every ḥijāb is a covering laid over an object.

Al-Israʾ 17:45 mentions a concealed barrier. That usage requires study in its own root and context and should not be equated completely with ghiṭāʾ in Al-Kahf 18:101.

Gh-ṭ-w Compared with Gh-sh-w: Covering and Enveloping

Gh-sh-w also concerns covering and enveloping.

Al-Baqara 2:7 uses ghishāwa in relation to people's eyes.

The image is close to aʿyunuhum fī ghiṭāʾin in Al-Kahf 18:101.

Ghishāwa and ghiṭāʾ nevertheless belong to different roots; semantic similarity does not make all their features identical.

Each verse requires separate examination of how the covering relates to the eyes, its grammatical subject, and its context.

Gh-ṭ-w Compared with Gh-f-l: Covering and Heedlessness

Ghafla concerns inattention and something escaping awareness.

Ghiṭāʾ names a covering or obstacle that conceals something.

They occur together in Qaf 50:22.

The verse first describes the addressee's heedlessness, then removal of their covering.

Heedlessness is the person's condition; the covering images an obstacle being removed.

They may be related without having the same meaning.

Gh-ṭ-w Compared with K-sh-f: Covering and Revealing

Kashf concerns removing a covering and making something manifest.

Qaf 50:22 applies this verb to ghiṭāʾ.

Ghiṭāʾ is what covers.

Kashf expresses its removal.

This contrast establishes a clear relationship without identifying their roots.

Not every kashf removes physical cloth or a curtain, just as figurative ghiṭāʾ need not be a material object.

Gh-ṭ-w Compared with Gh-ṭ-sh: Covering and Darkness

Gh-ṭ-sh occurs in An-Naziʿat 79:29 for making the night dark.

Arabic gh-ṭ-w can also describe night-time darkness spreading.

The roots thus approach one another in the image of darkness.

Quranic ghiṭāʾ, however, denotes a covering, while aghṭasha means making dark.

Darkness may conceal something from view, but the result of being concealed is not identical to the independent meaning of darkening.

Gh-ṭ-w Compared with Gh-ṭ-ṭ: Covering and Immersing

Arabic gh-ṭ-ṭ concerns immersing or pressing something into water and, in other uses, sound and breathing.

The principal use of gh-ṭ-w concerns covering.

Something may be covered by water, but being covered and being pressed into water are different acts.

Similar consonants should not lead to identification of the roots' meanings or histories.

8. Challenges of Understanding and Translation

Is Ghiṭāʾ Always a Physical Covering?

No. It can denote a physical covering, but both Quranic uses concern human sight and understanding.

Al-Kahf 18:101 concerns being veiled from remembrance of God.

Qaf 50:22 relates removal of the covering to reality becoming manifest at the reckoning.

Neither verse warrants assuming a physical curtain over the eyes without evidence.

Does Covering Always Mean Deliberately Concealing Truth?

No. Ghiṭāʾ itself does not determine anyone's intention.

Something may be obscured by external conditions, a person may be unaware of it, or someone may conceal it deliberately.

These situations differ in responsibility.

In Quranic passages, intention, knowledge, and responsibility must be understood from the whole context, not from the image of covering alone.

Does Removing Ghiṭāʾ Create a New Truth?

The word itself has no such meaning.

A covering may prevent sight of something already existing.

Removing it changes what is manifest.

Qaf 50:22 associates this with the reality of reckoning becoming apparent; how this occurs exceeds the root's lexical meaning.

Does “Today Your Sight Is Keen” Concern Physically Sharper Eyes?

The verse describes keen sight without explaining its bodily mechanism.

Its context concerns reality becoming manifest and heedlessness ending.

The expression alone should not yield a theory of the eye, brain, or awareness after death.

Is Ghiṭāʾ the Same as Kufr?

No. Ghiṭāʾ means a covering.

Kufr belongs to an independent root whose Quranic uses require attention to their object and to a person's knowledge and conduct.

Concealing truth or deliberate denial may share an image of covering. This does not make every covering kufr or every uninformed person a deliberate denier.

9. Translation Suggestions for Forqan

In Al-Kahf 18:101, ghiṭāʾin means “a covering.”

A suitable translation:

“Those whose eyes were within a covering from My remembrance, and who were unable to hear.”

In Qaf 50:22, ghiṭāʾaka means “your covering.”

Suggested translation:

“You were indeed heedless of this; then We removed your covering from you, and today your sight is keen.”

Non-Quranic equivalents vary by sentence:

Ghaṭā: covered, enveloped; of night, became dark.

Ghaṭṭā: covered.

Ghiṭāʾ: covering, lid, or veil.

Aghṭiya: coverings.

Taghṭiya: covering.

Taghaṭṭā: covered oneself or came under a covering.

Ightaṭā: covered oneself or became concealed.

Ghāṭin: covering; of night, dark.

Ghiṭāya: a kind of under-covering or undergarment.

Maghṭiyy: covered.

Translation should distinguish physical covering, obscured understanding, spreading darkness, and other uses.

Conclusion

Arabic gh-ṭ-w concerns covering, concealing, and being under a covering.

Physically, ghiṭāʾ covers an object and conceals it from view or direct contact.

Broader uses describe spreading night-time darkness, a concealed feature, or truth hidden from human sight and understanding.

Early poetic witnesses, including the verse attributed to Hassan ibn Thabit, show covering applied to concealed human qualities as well as physical objects.

Only the noun ghiṭāʾ occurs in the Quran, in two verses.

Al-Kahf 18:101 describes people's eyes as covered from remembrance of God.

Qaf 50:22 describes removal of the covering and keen sight before the reality now confronting the person.

The uses picture an obstacle to sight and understanding, but the word alone determines neither intention, the cause of obscurity, nor ethical responsibility.

Ghiṭāʾ is related to ghafla, kufr, ḥijāb, ghishāwa, and kashf, but none is a complete equivalent of another.

The core field of gh-ṭ-w is covering and being covered so that something is concealed from direct view. Quranic uses apply this image to being veiled from reality and removal of an obstacle to sight and understanding.

A covering does not itself destroy truth; it separates truth from the ability to see or understand it. Removing it can reveal what was hidden without necessarily creating that reality anew.
