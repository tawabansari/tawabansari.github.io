Core Meaning

The root ن ك ر in its basic sense indicates unfamiliarity, alienness, roughness, reprehensibility, denial, and being changed from a familiar and recognizable state. This root stands opposite ع ر ف. If ع ر ف is the field of knowing, recognizing, familiarity, prominence, and discernible fittingness, then ن ك ر is the field of alienness, strangeness, disharmony, denial, and things that rub against sound recognition and living fitrah.

From this field comes the word munkar: something that sound fitrah, unbiased reason, truth, and guidance do not find familiar, acceptable, or harmonious with good. Munkar is not merely “what people dislike,” because a society may become alienated from truth and accustomed to falsehood. Qur'anic munkar is something that is ugly, alien, corrosive, or rejectable in the field of truth, justice, dignity, and guidance.

Important Forms

نكر in the basic form can point to finding something unfamiliar, seeing it as alien, or sensing inward strangeness:

فلما رأى أيديهم لا تصل إليه نكرهم وأوجس منهم خيفة

So when he saw that their hands did not reach toward it, he found them unfamiliar and sensed fear from them inwardly.

Hud 11:70

ينكرون in form IV points to denying, consciously pushing away, or rejecting something after recognition:

يعرفون نعمت الله ثم ينكرونها

They recognize the blessing of The God, then they deny it.

An-Nahl 16:83

منكر is a description from this root: something reprehensible, unknown, rejectable, and alien to good and truth:

وينهون عن المنكر

And they forbid munkar.

Ali 'Imran 3:104

Munkar sometimes appears for reprehensible and wrongful speech:

وإنهم ليقولون منكرا من القول وزورا

And indeed they say munkar speech and falsehood.

Al-Mujadilah 58:2

نكر and نكرا point to something hard, reprehensible, strange, or heavy from the outward angle of the matter:

لقد جئت شيئا نكرا

You have certainly brought something hard and reprehensible.

Al-Kahf 18:74

نكروا in form II points to changing something so its familiar form becomes hidden:

نكروا لها عرشها

Make her throne altered and unfamiliar for her.

An-Naml 27:41

نكير points to denial, rejection, restraining, or a severe and transforming response against corruption and denial:

فكيف كان نكير

So how was My rejecting and restraining response?

Al-Hajj 22:44

Linguistic Note

The semantic core of ن ك ر turns around alienness from sound recognition. Something is munkar when, to the eye, heart, fitrah, reason, or guidance, it does not appear familiar and acceptable. This alienness can be sensory, as when Ibrahim found the behavior of the guests unfamiliar. It can be ethical, as with munkar opposite ma'ruf. It can be creedal, as with denying the blessing of The God. It can be verbal, as with munkar speech. And it can be structural, as with changing something so it is not recognized.

In this root, the issue is not only not knowing. A human being may know something and still deny it. The Qur'an says: يعرفون نعمت الله ثم ينكرونها. Recognition exists, but acceptance, sidq, and faithfulness do not. So ن ك ر is sometimes ignorance, and sometimes denial after recognition.

There is also an important difference between نكر in the basic form and أنكر or ينكرون in form IV. In نكرهم, the issue is an inward reaction and seeing something as unfamiliar; Ibrahim, when seeing the unusual behavior of the guests, feels alienness and concern inwardly. But in ينكرونها, the issue is a conscious stance; they recognize the blessing, then deny it. This difference shows that ن ك ر can begin with sensory contraction and continue all the way to conscious rejection of truth.

Munkar in the Qur'an is the opposite of ma'ruf, but its opposite is not merely “uncommon.” A society can become used to munkar and call it ma'ruf. Therefore, the criterion of munkar in the Qur'an is alienness from truth, good, justice, dignity, and sound fitrah; not merely alienness from people's custom.

Material Image

The material image of this root can be seen in several scenes.

First, there is hard, rough, stony ground on which movement is not easy. A human being on such ground faces abrasion, caution, and insecurity. From this image, the ethical meaning of munkar becomes clearer: munkar is conduct that makes the ground of human life rough, hard, corrosive, and unsafe. Oppression, humiliation, lying, transgression, and corruption make society like stony ground on which walking is difficult.

Second, there is something that has lost its familiar form. If the form of an object is changed, its owner hesitates in recognizing it. This image is clear in نكروا لها عرشها. The throne is the same throne, but its outward signs are changed in such a way that recognizing it requires precision, insight, and passing beyond appearance.

Third, there is unfamiliar behavior. A guest enters, but does not reach for the food. This behavior does not harmonize with the familiar signs of safety and hospitality. Ibrahim finds it unfamiliar and feels concern inwardly.

In the ethical field, munkar is like an unpleasant smell or rough ground for sound fitrah. When a sound human being faces oppression, humiliation, betrayal, fahsha, baghy, transgression, or inversion of truth, he does not find it familiar with good. This alienness is the ethical core of munkar.

Classical Usage

In classical Arabic, ن ك ر points to not recognizing, denying, finding reprehensible, finding unfamiliar, and being changed from a familiar form. نكر الشيء means he did not recognize it or found it reprehensible. أنكر is close both to verbal denial and to finding something unfamiliar or reprehensible.

In Arabic, نكراء can also be used for something hard, difficult, rough, or reprehensible. This semantic field helps with the image of hard and unfamiliar ground: ground that makes movement difficult and does not place the human being in calm familiarity.

In Arabic usage, munkar refers to something that is not known in good and fittingness; something that reason, sound 'urf, or living fitrah does not accept. But in the Qur'an, this word must be measured in relation to truth and guidance, not only people's approval.

For more precise pre-Qur'anic usage, reliable evidence from pre-Qur'anic poetry or ancient Arabic usage should be examined. If reliable evidence is found, it can be added.

Recognizing Blessing, Then Denying It

The Qur'an says:

يعرفون نعمت الله ثم ينكرونها وأكثرهم الكافرون

They recognize the blessing of The God, then they deny it, and most of them are kafirun.

An-Nahl 16:83

This verse is very important for understanding the root. Denial here does not come from simple ignorance. They recognize the blessing of The God, then deny it. So ن ك ر can point to a stage in which the human being recognizes something, but does not accept it, covers it, or behaves toward it as alien.

The difference here from نكرهم is important. In the story of Ibrahim, نكرهم is a sensory reaction and finding behavior unfamiliar. But in ينكرونها, the issue is recognizing the blessing and then consciously denying it. Here ن ك ر comes close to the mechanism of kufr: truth is seen, but it is not accepted.

Finding the Guests Unfamiliar

In the story of Ibrahim, it says:

فلما رأى أيديهم لا تصل إليه نكرهم وأوجس منهم خيفة

So when he saw that their hands did not reach toward it, he found them unfamiliar and sensed fear from them inwardly.

Hud 11:70

In this verse, نكرهم means finding them unfamiliar or finding their behavior strange. They are guests, but they do not reach for the food. Their behavior does not fit the normal expectation of a host. From this unfamiliarity, a feeling of concern arises.

This use shows that ن ك ر begins from sensory and social experience: something does not harmonize with familiar signs, so the human being finds it unfamiliar. This is not necessarily intellectual denial or takdhib yet; it is a kind of inward contraction before what is unfamiliar.

Munkar as the Opposite of Ma'ruf

In the verses of commanding ma'ruf and forbidding munkar, it says:

ولتكن منكم أمة يدعون إلى الخير ويأمرون بالمعروف وينهون عن المنكر

And let there be from among you an ummah that calls to good, commands ma'ruf, and forbids munkar.

Ali 'Imran 3:104

In this verse, munkar stands opposite ma'ruf. Ma'ruf is what is recognizable in the field of good, sound recognition, and fittingness. Munkar is what is alien and unacceptable to this field.

Another verse says:

كنتم خير أمة أخرجت للناس تأمرون بالمعروف وتنهون عن المنكر وتؤمنون بالله

You were the best ummah brought out for the people: you command ma'ruf, forbid munkar, and have faith in The God.

Ali 'Imran 3:110

Here forbidding munkar is a social responsibility, but it must not become an instrument of domination, inspection, religious coercion, or control over people's faith. Munkar is not only what appears disliked to one group. Munkar is what is alien to truth, justice, good, and dignity.

The Divine Prohibition of Fahsha, Munkar, and Baghy

In Surah An-Nahl, it says:

إن الله يأمر بالعدل والإحسان وإيتاء ذي القربى وينهى عن الفحشاء والمنكر والبغي

Indeed, The God commands justice, ihsan, and giving to the near kin, and He forbids fahsha, munkar, and baghy.

An-Nahl 16:90

This verse brings munkar beside fahsha and baghy. Fahsha is open ugliness and transgression beyond purity. Munkar is what is reprehensible and alien to sound recognition. Baghy is rebellion, transgression, and power that crosses the boundary of right.

This pairing shows that munkar is not limited to small individual behaviors. Oppression, transgression, humiliation, rebellious power, structural corruption, and breaking human dignity also fall in the field of munkar, because they are alien to truth and justice.

Munkar Speech: Speech That Distorts Reality

In Surah Al-Mujadilah, it says:

وإنهم ليقولون منكرا من القول وزورا

And indeed they say munkar speech and falsehood.

Al-Mujadilah 58:2

This verse comes in the context of zihar, a practice that, through oppressive speech, distorted the real relationship and dignity of the woman. Munkar speech here is not merely an ugly word; it is speech that bends reality with language and covers a person's right.

The pairing of munkar with zur is also important. Zur is crooked, false, and lying speech. So qawl munkar is speech alien to truth and dignity; it distorts reality and may make oppression appear legal or normal through words.

Any speech that theorizes oppression, humiliates the human being, inverts right, or breaks human dignity through religious and legal words falls in the field of qawl munkar.

Munkar in the Chain of Mutual Wilayah, Salat, and Zakat

In Surah At-Tawbah, the believers are described as follows:

والمؤمنون والمؤمنات بعضهم أولياء بعض يأمرون بالمعروف وينهون عن المنكر ويقيمون الصلاة ويؤتون الزكاة

The believing men and the believing women are awliya' of one another: they command ma'ruf, forbid munkar, establish salat, and give zakat.

At-Tawbah 9:71

Here forbidding munkar comes beside the mutual wilayah of believing men and believing women, salat, and zakat. This shows that forbidding munkar is not the work of a class that dominates people. It has meaning within the field of supportive relationship and mutual responsibility among the people of faith.

Qur'anic standing against munkar is not separated from wilayah, salat, zakat, and mercy. If it becomes hostility, humiliation, coercion, inspection, or pursuit of power, it has separated from its Qur'anic context and can itself become structural munkar.

Forbidding Munkar in Prophetic Mission

In the description of the Messenger, it says:

يأمرهم بالمعروف وينهاهم عن المنكر

He commands them to ma'ruf and forbids them from munkar.

Al-A'raf 7:157

But the same context shows that this forbidding of munkar is on the path of removing burdens and shackles:

ويضع عنهم إصرهم والأغلال التي كانت عليهم

And he removes from them their heavy burden and the shackles that were upon them.

Al-A'raf 7:157

This point is very important. Forbidding munkar in the prophetic path is removing shackles and burdens, not adding new chains in the name of din. If forbidding munkar becomes imposition, constriction, guardianship over people's faith, or the making of religious shackles, it does not fit the direction of this verse.

Changing Something for a Test of Recognition

In the story of Sulayman and the Queen of Saba', it says:

نكروا لها عرشها ننظر أتهتدي أم تكون من الذين لا يهتدون

Make her throne altered and unfamiliar for her, so we may see whether she is guided or is among those who are not guided.

An-Naml 27:41

In this verse, نكروا means changing something so it comes out of its recognized form. This is not merely a simple test of intelligence; it is a test of recognition. Will the Queen of Saba' recognize the essence and reality of her own throne despite a change in appearance, or not?

This verse shows that ن ك ر also works with the relation between appearance and reality. Sometimes a thing is still the same thing, but its form has changed. Someone habituated only to appearance stops; but someone who has the power of recognition can see the original reality behind the alteration.

The connection of the verse with guidance is also important: ننظر أتهتدي. Correct recognition is related to guidance. A changed appearance can become a test of whether the human being finds a path to reality or remains at the level of outward signs.

Nukr: A Hard and Unacceptable Matter

In the story of Musa and the righteous servant, it says:

أقتلت نفسا زكية بغير نفس لقد جئت شيئا نكرا

Did you kill a pure self without another self? You have certainly brought something hard and reprehensible.

Al-Kahf 18:74

نكر here points to something very reprehensible, heavy, and outside Musa's moral recognition. From Musa's view, the outward act is not familiar with truth and justice. So he calls it نكرا.

This use shows that ن ك ر can be the ethical reaction of the human being before something that outwardly appears deeply unacceptable and strange. The continuation of the story, however, shows that Musa does not yet know all the dimensions. So نكرا here is a reaction to the outward matter, not a final judgment on the hidden reality of the event.

Nakir: Divine Rejection and Restraining Response

The Qur'an speaks several times of nakir:

فكيف كان نكير

So how was My rejecting and restraining response?

Al-Hajj 22:44

نكير here is not only verbal denial. It points to a restraining, severe, and evident divine response toward falsehood and corruption. When truth is denied and falsehood persists, divine nakir means rejection, restraint, and effective transformation.

This use shows that ن ك ر operates both at the level of the human being and at the level of divine response to corruption and denial. The human being must recognize munkar by the measure of truth; but when societies persist in denial and corruption, divine nakir appears in history as truth's rejection and restraint.

Similar Uses

ن ك ر sometimes points to a sensory reaction and finding something unfamiliar, as in نكرهم in the story of Ibrahim.

Sometimes it points to conscious denial after recognition, as in يعرفون نعمت الله ثم ينكرونها.

Sometimes it points to ethical and social munkar: something alien to ma'ruf, good, and truth.

Sometimes it relates to qawl munkar: speech that inverts truth and dignity.

Sometimes it points to changing something into unfamiliarity, as in نكروا لها عرشها.

Sometimes it points to a hard, strange, and unacceptable matter, as in شيئا نكرا.

Sometimes it points to divine nakir: the rejecting, restraining, and transforming divine response before denial and corruption.

Grammar and Structure

In يعرفون نعمت الله ثم ينكرونها, the order of recognition and then denial is important. The verse shows that denial is not always from ignorance; sometimes it happens after recognition.

In نكرهم, the pronoun هم returns to the guests. Ibrahim finds them unfamiliar, but this unfamiliarity arises from their behavior, because they do not reach for the food. Here نكر is more of an inward and sensory reaction.

In ينكرونها, form IV shows a more active stance and denial. Here denial happens after recognition of the blessing and is tied to kufr.

In ينهون عن المنكر, the particle عن shows that prohibition is restraining and distancing from munkar. The aim is to draw society out from what is alien to good and truth.

In منكرا من القول, من القول shows that munkar here is a type of speech. So language too can be a field of munkar.

In نكروا لها عرشها, form II shows active alteration: change the throne in such a way that it becomes unknown or less familiar to her.

In شيئا نكرا, نكرا describes something that, from the outward angle of the matter, is deeply unacceptable and heavy.

In كيف كان نكير, نكير points to divine response; the question with كيف emphasizes the intensity and quality of that response.

Nearby Roots

ع ر ف is the main opposite of this root. ع ر ف points to knowing, recognizing, familiarity, and discernible fittingness; ن ك ر points to unfamiliarity, alienness, denial, and reprehensibility.

ج ح د is close to denial through wrongdoing or conscious denial. Juhud usually shows more strongly the dimension of stubbornness and covering truth with knowledge. ن ك ر is broader: it includes finding something unfamiliar, denial, and ethical munkar.

ك ف ر points to covering, ingratitude, and refusing truth despite signs. In An-Nahl 16:83, denial of blessing is tied to kufr. ن ك ر can be one manifestation of kufr, but it is not identical with it.

ك ذ ب points to calling something false or speaking contrary to reality. ن ك ر can relate to denial or finding something reprehensible; takdhib is more about report, truth, and falsehood.

ر د د points to returning, rejecting, and pushing back. Denial may come with rejection, but ن ك ر emphasizes unfamiliarity, alienness, and being unacceptable.

ف ح ش points to open ugliness and going beyond the limit of purity. Fahsha is one field of munkar, but munkar is broader than fahsha.

ب غ ي points to rebellion, crossing the limit, and seeking without right. In An-Nahl 16:90, munkar and baghy come together. Baghy is a structural and transgressive form of munkar, but munkar is not only baghy.

إ ث م points to sin, moral burden, and the heavy effect of sin. Munkar is what is alien to sound recognition and good; ithm shows the burden and heavy effect of sin.

ع د و points to transgression, crossing the limit, and practical hostility. 'Udwan can be among the great munkarat, but munkar is a broader concept.

زور points to crookedness, lying, and false speech. In Al-Mujadilah 58:2, qawl munkar comes with zur: the speech is both reprehensible and false and crooked.

Translation Suggestions for Forqan

نكر: found unfamiliar, saw as alien, considered unknown, denied; depending on context.

نكرهم: he found them unfamiliar; he saw their behavior as alien; he felt inward unfamiliarity toward them.

ينكرونها: they deny it, they consciously push it away, they behave toward it as alien.

منكر: munkar, reprehensible, alien to truth, rejectable, ugly by the measure of sound recognition, an ethically rough phenomenon.

ينهون عن المنكر: they forbid munkar, they restrain from what is reprehensible and alien to truth, they restrain society from ethical roughness.

منكرا من القول: munkar speech, reprehensible speech, words alien to truth and dignity, speech that distorts reality.

نكروا لها عرشها: make her throne altered and unfamiliar for her; change its outward signs so her recognition is tested.

شيئا نكرا: something hard and reprehensible, a heavy and unacceptable matter, something outside the recognized ground of justice.

نكير: severe rejection and restraint, a rejecting and restraining response, a transforming response; depending on context.

Related Entries

This root is directly connected with the entry ع ر ف, because munkar is the opposite of ma'ruf. It is also connected with the entry أ م ر, because the Qur'anic phrase ينهون عن المنكر cannot be fully understood without understanding ن ك ر and munkar.

Summary

The root ن ك ر indicates unfamiliarity, alienness, roughness, reprehensibility, denial, and coming out of a familiar and recognizable state. Its material image is both hard and rough ground that makes movement abrasive, and something that has lost its familiar form and now requires more careful recognition.

In the Qur'an, this root is used for denying blessing after recognition, finding guests unfamiliar, ethical and social munkar, munkar speech, altering the throne, a hard and unacceptable matter, and divine nakir.

The difference between ن ك ر and ع ر ف is that ع ر ف is knowing, recognizing, and sign-based familiarity, while ن ك ر is alienness, unfamiliarity, roughness, and denial. The difference between munkar and fahsha is that fahsha is open ugliness, while munkar includes every matter alien to truth and sound recognition. The difference between munkar and baghy is that baghy is transgression and rebellion beyond the limit, while munkar is broader and can appear in speech, structure, behavior, and denial of truth.

In Forqan translation, munkar should not be translated only as “bad deed” or “socially disliked.” Depending on context, “munkar,” “a matter alien to truth,” “reprehensible,” “rejectable,” “ugly by the measure of sound recognition,” “an ethically rough phenomenon,” or “conduct disharmonious with ma'ruf” may be more precise.
