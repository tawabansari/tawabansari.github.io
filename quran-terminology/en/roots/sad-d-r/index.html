1. The root and its Qur’anic forms

The Arabic root Ṣ D R has two established semantic branches. The first concerns صَدْر (ṣadr), the chest or front of something. Expressions such as a person’s chest, the foremost place in an assembly, the opening of a book, and the beginning of the day belong to this branch. The second concerns صُدُور (ṣudūr), returning, leaving, or departing from somewhere, particularly in contrast with وُرُود (wurūd), arrival at a watering place.

The branches have resemblances, but should not be derived from a single image without historical evidence. Early lexical explanations sometimes identify them as two separate principles.

Most Qur’anic occurrences concern the human chest. The chest in these verses, however, is not always merely a bodily organ: it is associated with what people conceal within themselves, receptivity and constriction, anxiety, resentment, desires, knowledge, and decisions.

The two verbal occurrences have a different sense. In Moses’ story, يُصْدِرَ concerns taking livestock away from water; in Al-Zalzalah, يَصْدُرُ النَّاسُ expresses people departing or proceeding in the scene of resurrection.

This breadth prevents all forms of Ṣ D R from being translated as “heart,” “human interior,” or “coming out.”

Qur’anic forms

Ṣ D R occurs 46 times in 43 verses, in three morphological groups:

  • صَدْر and its plural صُدُور: 44 occurrences, comprising 10 singular and 34 plural forms, concerning the human chest and what it contains or is associated with.
  • يَصْدُرُ (yaṣduru): once, in Al-Zalzalah 99:6; a present-tense Form I verb, “departs,” “returns,” or “proceeds.”
  • يُصْدِرَ (yuṣdira): once, in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23; a present-tense Form IV verb in Ḥafṣ’s transmission from ʿĀṣim, concerning taking livestock back or away from the watering place.

The following citation forms and lexical derivatives also help explain the family. This is not the same as an inventory of Qur’anic forms: some vocalizations occur in the Qur’an in another sense, while others are citation forms introducing a verb or lexical usage.

  • صَدَرَ، يَصْدُرُ (ṣadara, yaṣduru): the citation forms of the simple verb, “to return or depart from somewhere.” Its present tense occurs in Al-Zalzalah 99:6.
  • أَصْدَرَ (aṣdara): the citation form of Form IV, “to send someone back, send them away, or take them out.” Its present tense occurs in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23.
  • صُدُور (ṣudūr): the verbal noun of ṣadara, “returning or departing.” This verbal-noun use does not occur in the Qur’an, but the same vocalization occurs as the plural of ṣadr.
  • صَدَر (ṣadar): a noun concerning return, especially from water; its vocalization differs from ṣadr, “chest.”
  • صَادِر (ṣādir): one departing or returning; this active participle from Ṣ D R does not occur in the Qur’an. Its counterpart وَارِد (wārid) belongs to W R D and occurs as وَارِدَهُمْ in Yūsuf 12:19.
  • مَصْدَر (maṣdar): a place of departure or source; also an established grammatical term for a verbal noun. The lexical meaning alone does not date the emergence of the technical sense.
  • صَدَارَة (ṣadārah): precedence or a foremost position.
  • تَصَدَّرَ (taṣaddara): to take the foremost place in an assembly or occupy a leading position.
  • صَدَّرَ (ṣaddara): to place something in front or send it ahead.
  • صِدَار (ṣidār): a covering worn over the chest and front of the body.
  • مَصْدُور (maṣdūr): lexically, someone whose chest is diseased or injured.
  • The forms مَصْدَر, صَدَارَة, تَصَدَّرَ, صَدَّرَ, صِدَار, and مَصْدُور do not occur in the Qur’an.

Both singular and plural forms of the noun occur in the Qur’an. صَدْرَكَ sometimes concerns Muhammad’s chest, and صَدْرِي Moses’ chest; صُدُورِكُمْ, صُدُورِهِمْ, and الصُّدُور concern the chests of groups of people.

The pronouns and number matter and should not disappear in translation.

2. Two semantic branches in early Arabic

Ṣadr: the front or facing part of something

An established Arabic use of ṣadr names the front of the human body and of certain animals.

This lies at the front of the body, extending from below the neck toward the abdomen.

Ṣadr is also used for the front of other things.

For example, صَدْرُ الرُّمْح denotes the forward part of a spear, صَدْرُ السَّهْم the portion near an arrow’s tip, and صَدْرُ الْمَجْلِس the foremost place in an assembly.

Arabic صَدْرُ النَّهَار also concerns the beginning of the day, and صَدْرُ الْأَمْر the beginning of an undertaking.

Being ahead or foremost connects these uses.

Being foremost is not always spatial, however. The beginning of the day has temporal precedence, while the human chest occupies a physical position at the front of the body.

A relationship can therefore be recognized without turning every use into one mechanical image.

A poetic witness from Al-Aʿshā

Sībawayh attributes this line to Al-Aʿshā and quotes it in his grammatical discussion:

وَتَشْرَقُ بِالْقَوْلِ الَّذِي قَدْ أَذَعْتَهُ
كَمَا شَرِقَتْ صَدْرُ الْقَنَاةِ مِنَ الدَّمِ

“You choke on the words you yourself have spread, as the spear’s forward shaft is choked and stained with blood.”

The poet speaks of صَدْرُ الْقَنَاة, the spear’s forward portion, covered with blood.

The witness matters because ṣadr was not confined to the human body: the forward portion of an implement such as a spear could bear the same name.

Grammatical commentaries discuss the feminine شَرِقَتْ here. صَدْرُ is the nominative subject and ṣadr itself is masculine, but it is attached to the feminine قَنَاة. The verb’s feminine agreement is explained through reference to the meaning of qanāh.

The expression thus belongs to actual poetic usage and should not be treated as something invented merely to explain a Qur’anic verse.

The precise date of this particular line’s composition must nevertheless be considered separately from its attribution to the poet.

Ṣudūr: returning from water

The other branch is exemplified by صَدَرَ عَنِ الْمَاءِ, “he departed from the water.”

Taking livestock to water was an ordinary necessity in early Arab life. People and animals reached the watering place, used it, and subsequently left.

Arrival at water was expressed through wurūd; leaving or returning from it through ṣudūr.

Two different stages are involved: arriving at the water and departing or returning from it.

Ṣadara here consequently does not mean entering the water. It concerns movement following arrival.

Usage also extends beyond water to returning from a territory, ending one’s presence somewhere, or departing from it.

A poetic witness from Labīd

A line attributed to Labīd reads:

ثُمَّ أَصْدَرْنَاهُمَا فِي وَارِدٍ
صَادِرٍ وَهْمٍ صُوَاهُ قَدْ مَثَلْ

“Then we sent the two away along a great route of approach to water and departure from it, whose markers stood clearly in view.”

أَصْدَرْنَاهُمَا here concerns sending two animals away after drinking or along their return route.

وَارِد and صَادِر also stand together, expressing the two directions of passage.

The witness matters for the root’s verbal meaning: ṣudūr is connected with an established activity of pastoral life in Arabic.

Labīd lived through both the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. Attribution to him alone therefore does not establish that this particular line was composed before Islam.

Its linguistic use within the Arabic poetic tradition is nevertheless clear.

Ṣadr and ṣudūr: did one develop from the other?

Both branches can be related in some way to being foremost or moving in relation to a position.

The chest is the body’s front, and a ṣādir is someone who leaves or returns from somewhere.

Such a resemblance does not establish a certain derivational history.

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies two principles in the family: one opposite to arrival at water, and the other the chest of humans and other things.

Even early explanations thus did not require the two meanings to be completely unified.

Qur’anic study should recognize each branch through its evidence. Verses about the human chest should not be translated through returning from water, nor verses about returning and departing through the bodily chest.

3. Arrival and departure: the two Qur’anic verbal uses

Moses and the two women at Midian’s water

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23

وَلَمَّا وَرَدَ مَاءَ مَدْيَنَ وَجَدَ عَلَيْهِ أُمَّةً مِنَ النَّاسِ يَسْقُونَ وَوَجَدَ مِنْ دُونِهِمُ امْرَأَتَيْنِ تَذُودَانِ ۖ قَالَ مَا خَطْبُكُمَا ۖ قَالَتَا لَا نَسْقِي حَتَّىٰ يُصْدِرَ الرِّعَاءُ وَأَبُونَا شَيْخٌ كَبِيرٌ

“When Moses reached the water of Midian, he found a group of people watering their livestock, and apart from them two women holding theirs back. He asked: What is your situation? They said: We do not water until the shepherds take their livestock away from the water, and our father is an elderly man.”

Both sides of the relationship between arrival and departure appear here.

Moses first reaches Midian’s water: وَرَدَ مَاءَ مَدْيَنَ.

The women then say they do not water until the shepherds take their animals away.

يُصْدِرَ is Form IV in Ḥafṣ’s transmission from ʿĀṣim. The shepherds are the subject, and context supplies the omitted object: taking livestock back or away from water.

يَصْدُرَ, with an initial a-vowel and a u-vowel on dāl, is also an established recitation of the verse, including those of Abū ʿAmr and Ibn ʿĀmir among the seven recitations. It is the simple verb and, understood intransitively, concerns the shepherds themselves departing from water. This is therefore a difference of recitation and verbal formation, rather than merely two interpretations of one vocalization.

The morphological difference is real, but both readings concern watering livestock.

The verb should therefore not be rendered simply as “the people’s turn ends” without retaining its connection with departure from water.

The shepherds finishing their task is, of course, a natural result of that departure.

Why do the women wait?

The verse itself says they held their animals back and did not water until the shepherds departed.

This picture distinguishes their situation from that of the group gathered at the water.

The precise reason for waiting should not be extracted from Ṣ D R itself.

The verb conveys taking animals away or departing from water. Difficult access, the shepherds’ presence, and the father’s old age are understood through other elements of the same verse.

Nor should the scene become a general prohibition on women’s presence alongside men. It reports a particular situation; wider social conclusions require further evidence.

يَصْدُرُ النَّاسُ: people proceeding at resurrection

Al-Zalzalah 99:6

يَوْمَئِذٍ يَصْدُرُ النَّاسُ أَشْتَاتًا لِيُرَوْا أَعْمَالَهُمْ

“That day people proceed in scattered groups, to be shown their deeds.”

يَصْدُرُ is Form I here.

النَّاسُ is its subject, while أَشْتَاتًا describes people as scattered and divided into groups.

Unlike Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23, the subject is neither shepherds nor water. Leaving and proceeding apply to the scene of resurrection.

A shared root does not establish that people return from a spiritual watering place at resurrection. That image is absent from the verse.

Early explanations offer two readings of their direction: some understand returning from the place of accounting and dispersing toward different outcomes; others emphasize coming forth and proceeding to the presentation of deeds.

The verse itself does not explicitly name each group’s final destination.

لِيُرَوْا أَعْمَالَهُمْ connects the scene’s purpose or result with people being shown their deeds.

“Proceed” or “come forth” can consequently preserve the basic meaning, while determining the movement’s exact beginning and end requires fuller study of the resurrection context.

أَشْتَاتًا also supplies “scattered” or “in groups.” This does not follow from ṣadara itself.

The two verses show both the use concerning leaving water and the broader use of departing or proceeding. They do not, by themselves, date these uses or establish the historical order in which they emerged.

4. Ṣadr: the human chest and its relationship with qalb and fuʾād

Most Qur’anic occurrences of Ṣ D R concern the human chest.

They require a distinction between the chest itself and what is located in it or attributed to it.

الْقُلُوبُ الَّتِي فِي الصُّدُورِ: hearts within chests

Al-Ḥajj 22:46

أَفَلَمْ يَسِيرُوا فِي الْأَرْضِ فَتَكُونَ لَهُمْ قُلُوبٌ يَعْقِلُونَ بِهَا أَوْ آذَانٌ يَسْمَعُونَ بِهَا ۖ فَإِنَّهَا لَا تَعْمَى الْأَبْصَارُ وَلَٰكِنْ تَعْمَى الْقُلُوبُ الَّتِي فِي الصُّدُورِ

“Have they not travelled through the land, so that they might have hearts with which to understand or ears with which to hear? It is not the eyes that become blind, but the hearts within the chests.”

This is a principal witness for the relationship between ṣadr and qalb.

The Qur’an mentions hearts and then locates them فِي الصُّدُورِ, “within the chests.”

Qalb and ṣadr are therefore two different words in this construction.

Ṣadr names the chest; qalb names something within it.

This distinction should not become complete separation of their semantic fields. Elsewhere ṣadr is associated with knowledge, inclination, fear, and human inward affairs, as qalb also is.

The verse contrasts the heart’s blindness with the eyes’ blindness.

Blindness in relation to the heart consequently concerns failing to understand and apprehend truth, rather than a bodily heart literally having eyes and sight.

الَّتِي فِي الصُّدُورِ should nevertheless not be deleted or dismissed. The Qur’an speaks of hearts within chests. The bodily image remains present, although the heart’s blindness takes its meaning from understanding and learning from experience.

The verse alone also cannot supply a medical theory locating every human cognitive activity precisely. It connects understanding and reflection with the heart, rather than describing the nervous system.

Ṣadr, qalb, and fuʾād

Qur’anic qalb is associated with change, faith, understanding, fear, and reassurance.

Fuʾād also occurs in verses concerning hearing, sight, perception, responsiveness, and firmness.

Ṣadr basically means chest, but can serve as the location of what passes in a person’s heart or inward life.

The words thus have a real proximity without being synonymous in every verse.

Nor do Qur’anic uses establish a rigid division in which ṣadr always holds emotions, qalb always faith, and fuʾād always thought.

Some verses express these same concepts through different words.

For example, the Qur’an attributes strengthening to fuʾād, elsewhere speaks of reassurance of qalb, and in other verses describes the expansion or constriction of ṣadr.

These differences must be studied in context.

Is ṣadr always figurative?

No. Its basic meaning is chest.

Al-Ḥajj 22:46 clearly connects it with the heart’s location. Expressions of constriction and expansion use the chest’s image to convey someone’s inward condition.

Figurative uses do not mean that Qur’anic ṣadr has no relationship with the actual chest.

Conversely, an expression such as “expansion of the chest” should not necessarily be imagined as physical surgery.

The accompanying verb or description determines the precise meaning.

5. What is hidden within chests

Among the most frequent Qur’anic uses are expressions such as مَا فِي الصُّدُورِ, ذَاتِ الصُّدُورِ, and مَا تُكِنُّ صُدُورُهُمْ.

The chest here becomes a location through which people’s inward affairs are expressed, including matters others may not know.

Concealing and revealing

Āl ʿImrān 3:29

قُلْ إِنْ تُخْفُوا مَا فِي صُدُورِكُمْ أَوْ تُبْدُوهُ يَعْلَمْهُ اللَّهُ ۗ وَيَعْلَمُ مَا فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَمَا فِي الْأَرْضِ ۗ وَاللَّهُ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ قَدِيرٌ

“Say: Whether you conceal what is within your chests or disclose it, The God knows it. He knows what is in the heavens and the earth, and The God has power over everything.”

مَا فِي صُدُورِكُمْ accompanies the two verbs تُخْفُوا and تُبْدُوهُ.

The subject is consequently matters that can remain concealed or be disclosed.

Ṣadr here concerns inward affairs such as desire, thought, inclination, and intention.

None of these examples should individually become its lexical meaning.

The God’s knowing what is within chests does not mean that ṣadr itself means “secret,” “intention,” or “hidden sin.”

Ṣadr is the location to which the verse’s language attributes inward affairs.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:10 also asks أَوَلَيْسَ اللَّهُ بِأَعْلَمَ بِمَا فِي صُدُورِ الْعَالَمِينَ in a context distinguishing claims of faith from responses to persecution. Ṣudūr again concerns people’s inward affairs. Knowing follows from the verb and description of knowledge, rather than the name of the chest.

This distinction should be preserved in translation.

ذَاتِ الصُّدُورِ

Several verses describe The God as عَلِيمٌ بِذَاتِ الصُّدُورِ.

The expression appears in Āl ʿImrān 3:119, Āl ʿImrān 3:154, Al-Māʾidah 5:7, Al-Anfāl 8:43, Hūd 11:5, Luqmān 31:23, Fāṭir 35:38, Al-Zumar 39:7, Al-Shūrā 42:24, Al-Ḥadīd 57:6, and Al-Taghābun 64:4.

ذَاتِ الصُّدُورِ here concerns what resides within chests or relates to their interior.

Dhāt should not be taken without examination as the philosophical term “substance” or “essence.”

A natural translation is “knowing what is within the chests.”

This clarifies the sentence without introducing later philosophical terminology.

عَلِيمٌ بِذَاتِ الصُّدُورِ also does not, by itself, say that everything within chests is wrong or sinful.

Some contexts concern ill intent and concealment; others concern conduct, responsibility, and The God’s encompassing knowledge.

Hatred expressed through mouths and hidden within chests

Āl ʿImrān 3:118

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَا تَتَّخِذُوا بِطَانَةً مِنْ دُونِكُمْ لَا يَأْلُونَكُمْ خَبَالًا وَدُّوا مَا عَنِتُّمْ قَدْ بَدَتِ الْبَغْضَاءُ مِنْ أَفْوَاهِهِمْ وَمَا تُخْفِي صُدُورُهُمْ أَكْبَرُ ۚ قَدْ بَيَّنَّا لَكُمُ الْآيَاتِ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ تَعْقِلُونَ

“Believers, do not take as confidants outsiders who spare no effort to harm you and desire your suffering. Hatred has appeared from their mouths, and what their chests conceal is greater. We have made the signs clear to you, if you use reason.”

The verse clearly contrasts mouth and chest.

أَفْوَاهِهِمْ denotes mouths, where speech becomes manifest.

صُدُورُهُمْ, by contrast, concerns what is kept hidden.

Ṣadr itself does not mean hatred. الْبَغْضَاء identifies hatred, and context shows that the hidden hostility exceeds what has appeared.

The verse also does not describe everyone outside a group in these terms without qualification. Particular conduct and characteristics accompany the sentence and determine the warning’s scope.

The chest and the disclosure of secrets

Ghāfir 40:19

يَعْلَمُ خَائِنَةَ الْأَعْيُنِ وَمَا تُخْفِي الصُّدُورُ

“He knows the eyes’ treachery and what the chests conceal.”

The verse is short but lexically clear.

Eyes appear on one side of the sentence, chests on the other.

A person’s gaze can be outwardly observable, while an accompanying motive or intention remains hidden from others.

The Qur’an places both within The God’s knowledge.

Al-Naml 27:74 and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:69 also contain مَا تُكِنُّ صُدُورُهُمْ وَمَا يُعْلِنُونَ, bringing concealment and disclosure together.

Al-Mulk 67:13 likewise connects speaking secretly or aloud with The God’s knowledge of what is within chests.

Within this semantic network, ṣadr expresses the location of hidden human affairs. The word itself does not mean lying, scheming, or betrayal.

What is within chests brought forth at resurrection

Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:10

وَحُصِّلَ مَا فِي الصُّدُورِ

“And what is within the chests is brought forth and disclosed.”

صُدُور accompanies حُصِّلَ here.

That verb belongs to another family and concerns gathering, separating, or bringing forth what lies within something.

The disclosure of inward affairs follows from the verb and resurrection context, rather than ṣadr itself.

This clearly connects with Āl ʿImrān 3:29. There The God knows what is concealed or revealed within chests; here the scene concerns what they contain being brought forth.

The image does not establish that chests must be physically split open at resurrection and their material contents removed.

The verse concerns the disclosure of inward affairs without specifying its physical manner.

6. Expansion of the chest: openness, receptivity, and inward readiness

An important Qur’anic conceptual use joins ṣadr with SH R Ḥ (ش ر ح).

Sharḥ concerns opening or expanding; ṣadr is what receives that openness.

Expanding the chest for Islam

Al-Anʿām 6:125

فَمَنْ يُرِدِ اللَّهُ أَنْ يَهْدِيَهُ يَشْرَحْ صَدْرَهُ لِلْإِسْلَامِ ۖ وَمَنْ يُرِدْ أَنْ يُضِلَّهُ يَجْعَلْ صَدْرَهُ ضَيِّقًا حَرَجًا كَأَنَّمَا يَصَّعَّدُ فِي السَّمَاءِ ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ يَجْعَلُ اللَّهُ الرِّجْسَ عَلَى الَّذِينَ لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ

“Whomever The God wishes to guide, He opens his chest to Islam; whomever He wishes to leave astray, He makes his chest tight and constricted, as though he were laboriously ascending into the sky. Thus The God places defilement upon those who do not believe.”

The verse describes two different conditions of ṣadr.

First, a chest opened to Islam.

Second, a chest that becomes narrow and severely constrained.

The accompanying verbs and descriptions distinguish these conditions.

يَشْرَحْ supplies opening, ضَيِّقًا narrowness, and حَرَجًا severe difficulty and constriction.

Ṣadr itself should consequently mean neither expansion nor constriction. A chest can be described through either condition.

The same verse includes the comparison كَأَنَّمَا يَصَّعَّدُ فِي السَّمَاءِ.

It emphasizes difficulty and pressure. It should not immediately be converted into a report on high-altitude physiology or a particular scientific theory.

Arabic can use difficult ascent to depict severe constraint.

Nor does this verse alone resolve every question concerning guidance, human will, and responsibility. Those matters require study across the verses on guidance and going astray.

Al-Zumar 39:22 likewise concerns someone whose chest The God has opened to Islam, who is upon light from his Lord. The same verse continues with الْقَاسِيَةِ قُلُوبُهُمْ. Ṣadr and qalb thus occur in a closely related context, each with its own description and construction.

Expanding the chest toward disbelief

Al-Naḥl 16:106

مَنْ كَفَرَ بِاللَّهِ مِنْ بَعْدِ إِيمَانِهِ إِلَّا مَنْ أُكْرِهَ وَقَلْبُهُ مُطْمَئِنٌّ بِالْإِيمَانِ وَلَٰكِنْ مَنْ شَرَحَ بِالْكُفْرِ صَدْرًا فَعَلَيْهِمْ غَضَبٌ مِنَ اللَّهِ وَلَهُمْ عَذَابٌ عَظِيمٌ

“Whoever disbelieves in The God after believing—except one who is compelled while his heart remains secure in faith—but those who open their chests to disbelief will incur wrath from The God, and theirs is a tremendous punishment.”

This is a principal witness preventing expansion of the chest from being considered intrinsically positive.

Al-Anʿām 6:125 describes expansion for Islam; here expansion concerns disbelief.

Openness of the chest therefore does not inherently mean guidance or goodness.

Its value depends on what the chest is opened toward.

The same verse distinguishes someone acting or speaking under compulsion while their heart remains secure in faith from someone opening their chest to disbelief.

The outward appearance of conduct is consequently not the sole standard of judgment in this context.

It does not follow that ṣadr is always the seat of disbelief and qalb always the seat of faith. Elsewhere ṣadr opens to Islam, while qalb has various conditions.

Expansion here also does not mean physically opening the chest. It concerns inward acceptance and openness toward a direction.

Muhammad’s expansion of the chest

Al-Sharḥ 94:1

أَلَمْ نَشْرَحْ لَكَ صَدْرَكَ

“Have We not expanded your chest for you?”

The address is directly to Muhammad.

نَشْرَحْ conveys expansion; صَدْرَكَ denotes his chest.

The surah continues by describing the removal of his heavy burden and the raising of his remembrance.

Expansion of the chest must therefore be understood with that context.

The verse itself does not say that Muhammad’s chest was physically split open. Such a reading depends on external reports and should not replace the verse’s direct meaning.

Conversely, expansion should not be reduced to “making someone happy.” Arabic openness of the chest can concern readiness, endurance, acceptance, and relief from constraint.

Its precise role in this surah nevertheless requires a separate reflection on the connected verses.

Moses’ prayer for expansion

Ṭā Hā 20:25

قَالَ رَبِّ اشْرَحْ لِي صَدْرِي

“He said: My Lord, expand my chest for me.”

The prayer follows Moses’ commission to go to Pharaoh.

In subsequent verses he also asks for ease in his task and the untying of the knot in his tongue.

Expansion here consequently concerns readiness to carry out a difficult mission.

Ṣadr alone does not establish that Moses speaks only of a breathing problem or physical illness.

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:13, Moses likewise speaks of his chest constricting and his tongue failing to flow freely.

Together these passages connect the mission’s difficulty, inward condition, and the request for openness and expressive ability.

7. Constriction of the chest: anxiety, difficulty, and inward limitation

Ṣadr is not only expanded in the Qur’an. Several verses describe its constriction, pressure, and unease.

Muhammad’s chest constricting at his opponents’ words

Al-Ḥijr 15:97

وَلَقَدْ نَعْلَمُ أَنَّكَ يَضِيقُ صَدْرُكَ بِمَا يَقُولُونَ

“We indeed know that your chest constricts because of what they say.”

The God directly describes the effect of opponents’ words on Muhammad.

يَضِيقُ supplies constriction, while صَدْرُكَ identifies what experiences that condition.

Constriction should not become part of ṣadr’s independent meaning.

Receiving revelation and being a Messenger thus does not mean being free of human pressure and suffering.

The Qur’an presents Muhammad’s constriction as real and continues with guidance for responding to it.

A particular medical or psychological diagnosis should not be constructed from this verse alone. It describes constriction of the chest at opponents’ words, rather than naming an illness.

Hūd 11:12 likewise describes his chest’s constriction in connection with revelation and opponents’ words. Al-Aʿrāf 7:2 asks that no constraint be in his chest concerning the revealed Book.

Ṣadr can therefore express inward pressure and difficulty in discharging a responsibility.

Moses’ chest constricting

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:13

وَيَضِيقُ صَدْرِي وَلَا يَنْطَلِقُ لِسَانِي فَأَرْسِلْ إِلَىٰ هَارُونَ

“My chest constricts and my tongue does not flow freely, so send also to Aaron.”

Moses places two matters together: constriction of the chest and difficulty speaking.

They are nevertheless not identical.

Ṣadr concerns the chest and inward condition; lisān concerns the tongue and expressive ability.

Their combination relates inward condition and difficulty speaking within Moses’ experience.

It does not make constriction of the chest lexically mean stammering.

In Ṭā Hā 20:25 and the following verses Moses prays for expansion and ease in his task. The two parts of the story clarify his situation together.

Those unwilling to fight either side

Al-Nisāʾ 4:90

إِلَّا الَّذِينَ يَصِلُونَ إِلَىٰ قَوْمٍ بَيْنَكُمْ وَبَيْنَهُمْ مِيثَاقٌ أَوْ جَاءُوكُمْ حَصِرَتْ صُدُورُهُمْ أَنْ يُقَاتِلُوكُمْ أَوْ يُقَاتِلُوا قَوْمَهُمْ ۚ وَلَوْ شَاءَ اللَّهُ لَسَلَّطَهُمْ عَلَيْكُمْ فَلَقَاتَلُوكُمْ ۚ فَإِنِ اعْتَزَلُوكُمْ فَلَمْ يُقَاتِلُوكُمْ وَأَلْقَوْا إِلَيْكُمُ السَّلَمَ فَمَا جَعَلَ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ عَلَيْهِمْ سَبِيلًا

“Except those who join a people bound to you by a treaty, or come to you with their chests constrained against fighting either you or their own people. Had The God willed, He could have given them power over you, and they would have fought you. If they withdraw, do not fight you, and offer you peace, The God has given you no way against them.”

حَصِرَتْ صُدُورُهُمْ describes people unwilling to fight either side.

Ḥaṣirat concerns constraint, inability, or difficulty acting.

It does not establish that they must be cowardly or lack courage.

The context concerns treaty, warfare, and withdrawal from conflict.

The constriction therefore involves a difficult position between two possibilities of fighting.

The verse then establishes a clear limit on conduct toward those who withdraw and offer peace.

That ruling follows from the whole verse, rather than Ṣ D R itself.

8. The chest and different human conditions

The Qur’an associates ṣadr with widely differing conditions. This diversity prevents restricting it to one feeling or moral state.

Resentment removed from chests

Al-Ḥijr 15:47

وَنَزَعْنَا مَا فِي صُدُورِهِمْ مِنْ غِلٍّ إِخْوَانًا عَلَىٰ سُرُرٍ مُتَقَابِلِينَ

“We removed whatever resentment was in their chests: as brothers, upon couches facing one another.”

غِلّ concerns inward resentment or rancor here.

Ṣadr identifies the location to which that resentment is attributed.

Ṣadr itself should therefore not be defined as resentment.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:43 uses the same image, then continues with flowing rivers, praise of The God, and attainment of the divine promise.

The removal of resentment is thus a feature of those rewarded in the hereafter.

The verses do not explain its physical manner. It should not be converted into removal of a material substance from the chest.

Relief and healing of chests

Al-Tawbah 9:14

قَاتِلُوهُمْ يُعَذِّبْهُمُ اللَّهُ بِأَيْدِيكُمْ وَيُخْزِهِمْ وَيَنْصُرْكُمْ عَلَيْهِمْ وَيَشْفِ صُدُورَ قَوْمٍ مُؤْمِنِينَ

“Fight them: The God will punish them through your hands, disgrace them, grant you victory over them, and heal the chests of a believing people.”

The expression is يَشْفِ صُدُورَ.

Healing belongs to another root, concerning improvement and relief from injury or suffering.

In the warfare context it can concern relief from a group of believers’ grief and inward pressure.

Ṣadr itself does not mean grief, and healing need not be confined to curing bodily illness.

قَوْمٍ مُؤْمِنِينَ must also be preserved. The verse describes healing the chests of a believing people, rather than presenting every war as a cure and source of calm for every believer in every age.

Teaching that heals what is within chests

Yūnus 10:57

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّاسُ قَدْ جَاءَتْكُمْ مَوْعِظَةٌ مِنْ رَبِّكُمْ وَشِفَاءٌ لِمَا فِي الصُّدُورِ وَهُدًى وَرَحْمَةٌ لِلْمُؤْمِنِينَ

“Humankind, admonition from your Lord has reached you, and healing for what is within the chests, and guidance and mercy for believers.”

Healing is again associated with ṣadr.

Unlike Al-Tawbah 9:14, however, warfare is not the subject: admonition, guidance, and mercy are.

Healing in these contexts should not be treated as entirely identical without examination.

In Yūnus 10:57, مَا فِي الصُّدُورِ can concern inward conditions improved by receiving admonition and guidance.

The verse does not establish that the Qur’an is presented as a guaranteed substitute for treatment of every bodily illness.

شِفَاءٌ لِمَا فِي الصُّدُورِ itself does not supply that claim.

Inward need, envy, and preferring others

Al-Ḥashr 59:9

وَالَّذِينَ تَبَوَّءُوا الدَّارَ وَالْإِيمَانَ مِنْ قَبْلِهِمْ يُحِبُّونَ مَنْ هَاجَرَ إِلَيْهِمْ وَلَا يَجِدُونَ فِي صُدُورِهِمْ حَاجَةً مِمَّا أُوتُوا وَيُؤْثِرُونَ عَلَىٰ أَنْفُسِهِمْ وَلَوْ كَانَ بِهِمْ خَصَاصَةٌ ۚ وَمَنْ يُوقَ شُحَّ نَفْسِهِ فَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْمُفْلِحُونَ

“Those who had settled in the abode and embraced faith before them love those who migrated to them. Within their chests they feel no need or coveting for what the migrants received, and prefer them over themselves even when they are in need. Those protected from their own soul’s avarice are the successful.”

Ṣadr accompanies حَاجَة here.

Ḥājah’s precise meaning must be understood with مِمَّا أُوتُوا and the described people’s preference for others.

It can concern the absence of coveting or resentment over what others received.

It does not mean that they had no material needs: the verse itself says they may experience خَصَاصَة, need and poverty.

The distinction is significant.

Having no inward need or coveting for another’s share differs from having no actual need in life.

Ṣadr here should also not be translated as envy. Envy may enter contextual explanations, but ṣadr itself does not name that conduct.

Fear and arrogance within chests

Al-Ḥashr 59:13 emphasizes fear of believers within a group of opponents’ chests.

Ghāfir 40:56 describes pride within the chests of those disputing The God’s signs.

Ṣadr can consequently express the location of fear or arrogance.

Neither condition is its fixed meaning.

Arrogance should also not be equated with expansion of the chest. Someone may be morally arrogant without their condition being described through sharḥ.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:51 also uses مِمَّا يَكْبُرُ فِي صُدُورِكُمْ for something that seems great and difficult in the recipients’ estimation. The context concerns renewed creation; the greatness imagined should not be understood as physical enlargement of chests.

An inward desire fulfilled through travel

Ghāfir 40:80

وَلَكُمْ فِيهَا مَنَافِعُ وَلِتَبْلُغُوا عَلَيْهَا حَاجَةً فِي صُدُورِكُمْ وَعَلَيْهَا وَعَلَى الْفُلْكِ تُحْمَلُونَ

“You have benefits in them, and through them you may attain a desire within your chests; on them and on ships you are carried.”

This occurrence is important because it prevents restricting ṣadr to emotions or spirituality.

The context concerns livestock, their benefits, and human transport.

حَاجَةً فِي صُدُورِكُمْ denotes an inward need or desire attained by using the animals.

It may concern travel, carrying goods, or reaching a destination.

Ṣadr here expresses a person’s aim or desire, rather than necessarily faith, disbelief, fear, or emotion.

This shows that the Qur’anic language of what is within chests can also concern entirely practical, everyday desires.

The final definition should take this seriously, preventing every occurrence from being reduced to one narrow psychological or theological concept.

9. Ṣadr and knowledge: clear signs within those given knowledge

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:49

بَلْ هُوَ آيَاتٌ بَيِّنَاتٌ فِي صُدُورِ الَّذِينَ أُوتُوا الْعِلْمَ ۚ وَمَا يَجْحَدُ بِآيَاتِنَا إِلَّا الظَّالِمُونَ

“Rather, it is clear signs within the chests of those given knowledge. None deny Our signs except the wrongdoers.”

This is a principal use of ṣadr in relation to knowledge and understanding.

Clear signs are within the chests of those given knowledge.

Linguistically, this may concern retaining the signs in memory as well as knowing and understanding them.

Two readings have nevertheless been proposed for the pronoun هُوَ.

One takes it to refer to the Qur’an and its signs: clear signs that knowledgeable people recognize and hold within themselves.

Another connects it with the preceding verse’s subject and signs concerning Muhammad. These signs are understood as known to those possessing knowledge of earlier scripture.

Preferring either reading requires examination of Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:48, Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:49, and the pronoun’s grammatical referent.

The disagreement does not change ṣudūr’s basic meaning.

Both readings make chests the location of something associated with knowledge and recognition.

Does the verse reject writing the Qur’an?

فِي صُدُورِ الَّذِينَ أُوتُوا الْعِلْمَ alone does not establish that Qur’anic verses should not be written or that written text has no place.

The verse concerns the signs’ presence within knowledgeable people’s chests.

Something’s presence in human memory and understanding need not conflict with its written recording.

Ṣadr should also not become a material container in which the text is written in physical letters and lines.

Qur’anic ṣadr can therefore express the location of knowing and retaining meanings or signs.

10. The chest and whispering: inward suggestion

Al-Nās 114:5

الَّذِي يُوَسْوِسُ فِي صُدُورِ النَّاسِ

“Who whispers within the chests of humankind.”

Ṣadr accompanies يُوَسْوِسُ here.

Waswasah belongs to W S W S (و س و س), concerning hidden dialogue and inward suggestion.

Ṣadr is the location to which that suggestion is attributed.

It should not itself be defined as whispering.

Nor does the verse establish that whispering must be produced as a physical sound inside the chest.

Qāf 50:16 also expresses human inner dialogue through the verb for whispering. There, however, its subject is the person’s own self.

Three matters should therefore remain distinct: ṣadr as the location of inward affairs, waswasah as a kind of hidden suggestion or dialogue, and the agent to whom that whispering is attributed.

This prevents every inward human thought from being called satanic whispering without justification.

The use in Al-Nās also should not give every Qur’anic occurrence of Ṣ D R a negative meaning.

The same word accompanies whispering in Al-Nās, clear signs and knowledge in Al-ʿAnkabūt, Moses’ prayer in Ṭā Hā, and Muhammad’s expansion of the chest in Al-Sharḥ.

11. Comparing related roots

Ṣ D R (صَدْر) and Q L B (قَلْب): chest and heart

Ṣadr basically means the chest or front of the body.

Qalb concerns the heart and is associated in the Qur’an with understanding, faith, change, and inward conditions.

Al-Ḥajj 22:46 speaks of hearts within chests.

Ṣadr and qalb consequently cannot be lexically identical.

Their figurative fields nevertheless overlap.

Āl ʿImrān 3:154 is also important. It connects مَا فِي صُدُورِكُمْ with testing and مَا فِي قُلُوبِكُمْ with purification.

The Qur’an thus uses both words in a closely related construction.

Their combination alone does not establish that ṣadr always holds hidden affairs and qalb always holds faith.

The Qur’an relates both faith and disbelief to ṣadr, while describing qalb through various conditions.

Their differences must therefore be identified in each verse’s construction and context.

Ṣ D R (صَدْر) and F ʾ D (فُؤَاد): chest and responsive heart

Arabic fuʾād names the heart and is associated in the Qur’an with reception, perception, inclination, and responsiveness.

Ṣadr also enters this inward field in some verses.

Their basic meanings are nevertheless not identical.

Ṣadr denotes the chest; fuʾād names the heart.

Verses about strengthening Muhammad’s fuʾād combine the verb for making firm with fuʾād. Verses about expanding ṣadr combine sharḥ with ṣadr.

This is a real distinction, but should not become a rigid system in which fuʾād only holds feeling and ṣadr only receptivity.

Both can concern human inward condition. Their precise differences require separate study of their full range of uses.

Ṣ D R (صَدْر) and B Ṭ N (بَطْن): front and interior

Physical baṭn concerns the abdomen, with some uses involving interiority or hiddenness.

Ṣadr is the body’s front and is also used in Arabic for the foremost part of other things.

The two words are therefore different.

The Qur’an’s description of hidden matters within chests does not make ṣadr lexically mean “the hidden interior.”

Nor is everything passing inwardly within a person necessarily expressed through ṣadr in every verse. The Qur’an also uses nafs, qalb, and fuʾād.

Ṣ D R (صَدْر) and SH R Ḥ (شَرْح): location and opening

These roots accompany one another in expressions of expansion of the chest.

Sharḥ concerns opening or expanding; ṣadr is what the action is applied to.

Ṣadr should therefore not be lexically defined as openness.

Expansion of the chest is also not invariably positive: Al-Naḥl 16:106 concerns opening it to disbelief.

The moral direction depends on what the chest is opened toward.

Ṣ D R (صَدْر) and Ḥ R J (حَرَج): chest and constraint

Ḥaraj concerns constriction, difficulty, and limitation.

Ṣadr can be described through ḥaraj, as in Al-Anʿām 6:125 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:2.

Ḥaraj itself does not name the chest, and ṣadr does not mean constraint.

The words complement one another in these constructions.

Constriction of the chest also does not always indicate disbelief. Muhammad and Moses experience it in difficult situations.

The condition consequently cannot become one fixed moral judgment.

Ṣ D R (صُدُور) and W R D (وُرُود): departure and arrival

These families have an established opposition in the language of watering places.

Wurūd is reaching water; ṣudūr is returning or departing from it.

Both appear within Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23.

Their opposition matters for يُصْدِرَ, but should not transfer to every nominal use of ṣadr.

The human chest in the Qur’an does not mean a watering place or place of return.

Nor does the opposition mean that every use of ṣadara necessarily involves water or drinking.

Al-Zalzalah 99:6 applies the verbal family to people proceeding at resurrection.

Ṣ D R (صُدُور) and R J ʿ (رُجُوع): departure and return

Arabic rujūʿ concerns returning.

The verbal branch of ṣudūr can also concern returning from somewhere.

Not every rujūʿ is ṣudūr, however, and not every ṣudūr necessarily means returning to the original starting point.

A ṣādir may be someone leaving water and taking another route.

In Al-Zalzalah 99:6, the direction should therefore be determined through context, rather than a specific destination being constructed from returning alone.

Ṣ D R (صَدْر) and Ṣ D D (صَدّ): chest and preventing

These roots have similar letters and sounds but are not identical.

Qur’anic Ṣ D D concerns preventing, turning away, and obstructing.

Nominal Ṣ D R concerns the chest; its verbal use concerns departure or return.

Their visual resemblance does not justify merging meanings.

تَصَدَّى in ʿAbasa 80:6 likewise belongs to Ṣ D Y (ص د ي) in a common morphological classification, rather than Ṣ D R, despite its similar sound.

The actual form must be checked before interpretation in root study.

12. Translation proposals for Forqan

For صَدْر and صُدُور, the basic equivalents proposed are “chest” and “chests.”

These preserve the bodily sense while allowing inward uses to be understood through context. “Breast” and “breasts” are also established literary equivalents, particularly in translations of expressions about inward life.

Some idiomatic expressions may read more fluently with “heart” or “within.” Such choices should be deliberate and must not erase significant distinctions between ṣadr, qalb, and fuʾād.

Forms concerning the chest

صَدْرَكَ: “your chest”; for example, Al-Sharḥ 94:1.

صَدْرِي: “my chest”; for example, Ṭā Hā 20:25 and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:13.

صَدْرَهُ: “his chest”; for example, Al-Anʿām 6:125.

صُدُورِكُمْ: “your chests.”

صُدُورِهِمْ: “their chests.”

الصُّدُور: “the chests.”

Expressions concerning inward affairs may be rendered “what is within the chests” or, more idiomatically, “what lies hidden in the hearts.”

If ṣadr and qalb occur together, however, “chest” and “heart” are preferable to preserve their actual distinction.

Expansion of the chest

In Al-Anʿām 6:125, “He opens his chest to Islam” is appropriate.

In Al-Naḥl 16:106, “he has opened his chest to disbelief” is more appropriate than a rendering making openness intrinsically a sign of guidance.

In Al-Sharḥ 94:1, “Have We not expanded your chest for you?” stays close to the wording.

In Ṭā Hā 20:25, “My Lord, expand my chest for me” is appropriate.

A contextual explanation may use openness of heart, inward readiness, or acceptance. It should remain clear that these meanings follow from combining sharḥ and ṣadr.

Constriction of the chest

In Al-Ḥijr 15:97, “your chest constricts because of their words” is appropriate.

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:13, “my chest constricts” is clear.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:90, “their chests are constrained against fighting you or their own people” can preserve the expression’s meaning.

These should not all be translated as “being afraid.” Constriction has a different cause and condition in each context.

Hidden matters within ṣadr

For مَا فِي الصُّدُورِ, “what is within the chests” is appropriate.

For عَلِيمٌ بِذَاتِ الصُّدُورِ, “knowing what is within the chests” is natural and clear.

For وَحُصِّلَ مَا فِي الصُّدُورِ in Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:10, “what is within the chests is brought forth and disclosed” is proposed.

Disclosure and knowing should not become part of ṣadr itself in these uses.

Verbal forms

For يُصْدِرَ in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23, the proposal is:

“Until the shepherds take their livestock away from the water.”

This accords with Form IV and the context of watering livestock.

For يَصْدُرُ in Al-Zalzalah 99:6, “proceed,” “come forth,” or, where context warrants it, “return” is appropriate.

Proposed rendering:

“That day people proceed in scattered groups, to be shown their deeds.”

Both uses require the verbal senses of departing and returning to remain distinct from the noun meaning chest.

13. Conclusion

Arabic Ṣ D R has two clear branches: ṣadr, the chest or front of things; and ṣudūr, returning or departing from somewhere, particularly opposite arrival at water.

The root occurs 46 times in the Qur’an. Forty-four are ṣadr and its plural ṣudūr, concerning the human chest and related matters; two belong to the verbal branch.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23, departure concerns taking livestock away from water. Al-Zalzalah 99:6 applies the same verbal family to people proceeding at resurrection.

Nominal ṣadr accompanies a wide field of human affairs: hidden matters, knowledge, faith and disbelief, anxiety and pressure, desires, resentment, fear, arrogance, and whispering.

None of these should become an invariant part of its lexical meaning.

A chest opened to Islam can elsewhere be opened to disbelief. The word accompanying whispering in Al-Nās also accompanies clear signs and knowledge in Al-ʿAnkabūt. In Ghāfir 40:80 it expresses a practical desire attained through travel or carrying goods.

Ṣadr therefore carries no intrinsic positive or negative moral value.

Its distinction from qalb and fuʾād must also be preserved. Ṣadr basically means chest, whereas qalb and fuʾād name the heart. Their uses overlap in expressing inward life, without making them always synonymous or assigning each an entirely separate psychological or cognitive function.

In expressions of expansion, constriction, and what is within chests, the complete construction determines the condition associated with ṣadr.

For Forqan, the final definition is: ṣadr is the chest or front of something and, in human uses, may express the location of inward affairs. Ṣudūr in the verbal branch is departure, proceeding, or returning from somewhere, especially in contrast with arriving there.

This definition preserves both principal Arabic branches and accommodates all Qur’anic uses, without equating the chest with qalb and fuʾād or imposing departure and return on every nominal occurrence.
