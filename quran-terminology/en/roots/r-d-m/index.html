Closing breaches, filling openings, and building a firm barrier

In Arabic, the root R D M denotes closing a breach, filling an opening, or covering a place left open or whose continuity has been broken. This meaning appears in blocking a passage or entrance, filling a cavity, securing a barrier, and patching worn fabric.

In this family, radm names both the act of closing and filling and the barrier or structure produced through it. Some uses also emphasize placing components upon one another and joining them.

However, radm should not always be restricted to building a wall, piling up stones, or banking earth. What the principal uses share is closing an opening or covering an exposed or damaged part. The materials, dimensions, and final strength of a structure cannot be determined from the word alone.

In the Qur’an, this root occurs only once, as رَدْمًا (radman). Responding to people who complain of the corruption of Gog and Magog, Dhū al-Qarnayn asks them to help with their strength so that he can build a barrier between the two groups.

Qur’anic form: رَدْمًا (radman), a masculine singular, indefinite accusative noun in Al-Kahf 18:95.

Arabic also records forms such as رَدَمَ (radama), يَرْدِمُ (yardimu), رَدْم (radm), رُدُوم (rudūm), رَدِيم (radīm), رَدَّمَ (raddama), مُرَدَّم (muraddam), تَرَدَّمَ (taraddama), مُتَرَدَّم (mutaraddam), رَدِيمَة (radīmah), and أَرْدَمَ (ardama). These forms do not occur as such in the Qur’an; examining them helps establish the root’s semantic range.

1. Core meaning and early Arabic usage

Closing a breach or opening

In Arabic, رَدَمَ الْبَابَ (radama al-bāba) means blocking a doorway, while رَدَمَ الثُّلْمَةَ (radama al-thulmata) means closing a breach or broken section.

A ثُلْمَة (thulmah) is a gap or breach in something such as a wall or barrier that interrupts its continuity.

Lexical explanations of this family identify closing a breach as its core meaning:

أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى سَدِّ ثُلْمَةٍ

aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā saddi thulmatin

Meaning: “A single principle denoting the closing of a breach.”

This definition clarifies the family’s central meaning.

Here the focus is not simply placing something somewhere. It is closing a section left open, with a gap between its two sides.

For example, a breach in a wall can be filled with suitable materials. An open route or entrance can be closed by constructing a barrier.

The act itself must nevertheless be distinguished from its result.

رَدَمَ (radama) expresses the act of closing or filling; رَدْم (radm) can name the barrier resulting from that act.

The root itself does not specify whether the barrier is made of stone, earth, iron, or another material. The materials must be established from evidence in each particular use.

Radm: covering and joining components

Alongside closing breaches and doorways, joining components also appears in lexical explanations of this family:

وَكُلُّ مَا لُفِّقَ بَعْضُهُ بِبَعْضٍ فَقَدْ رُدِمَ

wa-kullu mā luffiqa baʿḍuhu bi-baʿḍin fa-qad rudima

Meaning: “Anything whose parts have been joined to one another has undergone radm.”

This explanation emphasizes joining components and covering gaps.

When building a barrier, stones, pieces, or other materials may be placed beside or upon one another to close a passage.

When repairing fabric, a damaged part may similarly be covered with a patch.

The tools and materials differ, but in both cases an open or damaged area is covered by adding material and joining parts.

However, radm should not always be understood as stacking several layers. Closing an opening is the clearer core meaning, and the method can vary.

Radm as the name of an act and of a barrier

In Arabic, رَدْم (radm) functions both as a verbal noun and as a substantive noun.

In رَدَمَ الثُّلْمَةَ رَدْمًا (radama al-thulmata radman), the verbal noun radman expresses the act of closing the breach.

When radm names a structure, however, it refers to the barrier or object constructed to close an opening.

The plural رُدُوم (rudūm) is recorded for this use.

The two uses are related but must be distinguished in translation.

In Al-Kahf 18:95, radman names what Dhū al-Qarnayn intends to build. The sense of a barrier as an object is therefore prominent, rather than simply the act of closing a breach.

Radm: repairing and patching fabric

Another established use of this root concerns cloth and clothing.

Arabic records رَدَمَ الثَّوْبَ (radama al-thawba) and رَدَّمَهُ (raddamahu) for patching a garment.

ثَوْبٌ مُرَدَّمٌ (thawbun muraddamun) denotes a patched garment.

رَدِيم (radīm) also describes worn clothing in some early uses: a garment that has become old and may need patching.

رَدِيمَة (radīmah) is also recorded for two pieces of cloth sewn together.

These uses have a clear semantic connection with closing a breach.

Just as a gap in a wall can be filled with building materials, a torn or damaged part of cloth can be covered with a patch.

Patching fabric does not necessarily restore it to a completely new and flawless state.

Thus رَدَّمَ الثَّوْبَ (raddama al-thawba) means repairing or patching, rather than necessarily renewing the garment completely.

Nor should this use suggest that every instance of radm in Arabic repairs something previously built and later damaged. In the Qur’anic verse, Dhū al-Qarnayn creates a new barrier.

Closing an opening can therefore involve either repairing an existing object or creating a new barrier.

2. A pre-Islamic witness: mutaraddam in ʿAntarah’s Muʿallaqah

One of the best-known witnesses to this family occurs at the opening of the Muʿallaqah of the pre-Islamic poet ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād:

هَلْ غَادَرَ الشُّعَرَاءُ مِنْ مُتَرَدَّمِ
أَمْ هَلْ عَرَفْتَ الدَّارَ بَعْدَ تَوَهُّمِ

hal ghādara al-shuʿarāʾu min mutaraddami / am hal ʿarafta al-dāra baʿda tawahhumi

“Have the earlier poets left any place to mend or add to? Or have you recognized the dwelling after doubt and conjecture?”

The word of interest in this verse is مُتَرَدَّم (mutaraddam).

Explanations of this verse relate mutaraddam to a place for repair or patching: an area that needs mending because it has become worn or damaged.

ʿAntarah is not speaking here about an actual wall or piece of cloth.

He asks whether the poets before him have left any theme or meaning about which a later poet could say something new.

The image is that earlier poets have treated poetic subjects so thoroughly that no unspoken part seems to remain for completion or repair.

Mutaraddam thus transfers the material idea of repairing and patching into the domain of speech and poetry.

This use matters because it shows that, in language attributed to the pre-Islamic period, the family R D M was not confined to walls and barriers; it could also serve literary expression.

It does not follow that radm itself means composing poetry, completing a thought, or discovering a new meaning.

Those ideas arise from the verse’s imagery and context, rather than from the root’s basic meaning.

The variant reading of mutaraddam

Some transmissions and commentaries of the Muʿallaqah also report مُتَرَنَّم (mutarannam) instead of mutaraddam.

That form belongs to another family and relates to chanting or repeating a sound.

The variation shows why the precise reading must be identified when citing the verse as lexical evidence.

With mutaraddam, the imagery concerns repair and patching. The alternative reading requires a different semantic explanation.

This passage is therefore a valid witness to R D M on the established mutaraddam reading, while the transmitted variation should remain acknowledged.

Other early uses

Lexical usage also includes أَرْدَمَتْ عَلَيْهِ الْحُمَّى (ardamat ʿalayhi al-ḥummā): a fever continued upon someone and did not leave them.

سَحَابٌ مُرْدِمٌ (saḥābun murdimun) likewise describes a continuing, persistent cloud. Its spatial extent cannot be determined from that description alone.

Continuance and remaining with something are prominent in these uses.

A conceptual connection can be seen between covering, encompassing, and remaining upon something. That resemblance alone, however, does not establish a single historical image from which every use developed.

In secondary uses, radm is recorded for the sound of a bow and رُدَام (rudām) for passing wind or its sound. These uses differ from closing a breach.

These secondary uses show why all forms assigned to this root cannot be placed under closing breaches without separate examination.

Qur’anic research should distinguish the clear core meaning relevant to the verse from secondary uses supported by fewer examples.

3. Ajʿal baynakum wa-baynahum radman: the root in the Qur’an

The root’s sole Qur’anic occurrence is in Al-Kahf 18:95:

قَالَ مَا مَكَّنِّي فِيهِ رَبِّي خَيْرٌ فَأَعِينُونِي بِقُوَّةٍ أَجْعَلْ بَيْنَكُمْ وَبَيْنَهُمْ رَدْمًا

qāla mā makkan-nī fīhi rabbī khayrun fa-aʿīnūnī bi-quwwatin ajʿal baynakum wa-baynahum radman

“He said: What my Lord has enabled me to do is better; so help me with strength, that I may build a firm wall between you and them.”

In this verse, Dhū al-Qarnayn speaks of constructing a barrier between two groups.

Understanding radman precisely requires examining the setting and purpose of its construction in the surrounding verses.

The narrative setting

In the preceding verse, Al-Kahf 18:94, a group of people tells Dhū al-Qarnayn:

إِنَّ يَأْجُوجَ وَمَأْجُوجَ مُفْسِدُونَ فِي الْأَرْضِ

inna yaʾjūja wa-maʾjūja mufsidūna fī al-arḍi

“Gog and Magog spread corruption in the land.”

They then offer to provide payment in exchange for a barrier between themselves and those people.

The purpose of the request is clear: they want a barrier between themselves and those whose corruption they complain of.

In this context, radm concerns closing a route or opening so that passage through it is restricted or prevented.

The meaning of corruption or the identity of Gog and Magog should not be inserted into the lexical definition of radm.

The word refers to the barrier itself, rather than to the identity of those whose access it is intended to prevent.

Fa-aʿīnūnī bi-quwwatin

Dhū al-Qarnayn says in Al-Kahf 18:95; an excerpt:

فَأَعِينُونِي بِقُوَّةٍ

fa-aʿīnūnī bi-quwwatin

“So help me with strength.”

The phrase contains a request for assistance.

أَعِينُونِي (aʿīnūnī) belongs to the root ʿ W N and concerns giving help.

بِقُوَّةٍ (bi-quwwatin) refers to the strength needed for the work.

Building the radm in this narrative is therefore connected with cooperation and providing the capacity to carry out the work.

Radm itself, however, does not mean teamwork or social cooperation. That feature comes from Dhū al-Qarnayn’s command.

Nor does the verse establish that every barrier must be built in the same way in every situation.

It describes a particular event, whose execution is explained subsequently.

Ajʿal baynakum wa-baynahum radman

Here أَجْعَلْ (ajʿal) is a jussive imperfect verb expressing the consequence of complying with the request for help.

بَيْنَكُمْ وَبَيْنَهُمْ (baynakum wa-baynahum) identifies the barrier’s position: between you and them.

رَدْمًا (radman) is the verb’s object, an indefinite accusative noun.

The construction shows that Dhū al-Qarnayn intends to place something between the two groups that will serve as a barrier.

Here radm is lexically connected with closing a breach and blocking a passage.

The word alone does not specify the materials, height, length, or construction method.

Those features require examining the following verse.

4. Sadd and radm: two words within one narrative

In Al-Kahf 18:94, the people use sadd in their request; an excerpt:

أَنْ تَجْعَلَ بَيْنَنَا وَبَيْنَهُمْ سَدًّا

an tajʿala baynanā wa-baynahum saddan

“That you might build a barrier between us and them.”

Dhū al-Qarnayn responds in Al-Kahf 18:95; an excerpt:

أَجْعَلْ بَيْنَكُمْ وَبَيْنَهُمْ رَدْمًا

ajʿal baynakum wa-baynahum radman

“That I may build a firm wall between you and them.”

The lexical difference matters.

Sadd: a barrier preventing passage

The Arabic root S D D concerns closing and preventing passage.

سَدّ (sadd) can name something that closes a route or opening.

Yā Sīn 36:9 states; an excerpt:

وَجَعَلْنَا مِنْ بَيْنِ أَيْدِيهِمْ سَدًّا وَمِنْ خَلْفِهِمْ سَدًّا

wa-jaʿalnā min bayni aydīhim saddan wa-min khalfihim saddan

“And We placed a barrier before them and a barrier behind them.”

This use shows that sadd can occur in a construction concerning obstruction and enclosure without necessarily identifying particular building materials.

The application of sadd is therefore broad.

Radm: closing an opening by constructing a barrier

In its clear uses, radm is more closely associated with filling a breach and closing an opening.

In Dhū al-Qarnayn’s narrative, it names a structure erected between the two sides of a passage.

Some lexical explanations describe radm as more extensive or more built up than sadd because its components are placed upon one another. This can emphasize a more densely constructed barrier, but does not by itself establish that it is always stronger.

That explanation is compatible with the construction described in the next verse.

It should not become an absolute rule that every radm in Arabic is larger, taller, or stronger than every sadd.

Such a judgment depends on dimensions, materials, and construction, and cannot be established from the lexical difference alone.

Conversely, the people’s request for a sadd and Dhū al-Qarnayn’s response with radm show that both words serve the same practical purpose here: creating a barrier to prevent passage.

The two words should not be separated as though the people requested one structure and Dhū al-Qarnayn decided to build something wholly unrelated.

The defensible distinction is that sadd is a broader term for a barrier or obstruction, whereas radm in this context emphasizes a barrier created by filling and joining the parts of an opening.

This is a semantic tendency, rather than an absolute boundary governing every use of the two roots.

5. Constructing the radm: iron, heat, and molten copper

In Al-Kahf 18:96, the Qur’an explains the barrier’s construction; an excerpt:

آتُونِي زُبَرَ الْحَدِيدِ

ātūnī zubara al-ḥadīdi

“Bring me pieces of iron.”

زُبَر (zubar) refers to pieces or large masses, while الْحَدِيد (al-ḥadīd) identifies the material.

The use of iron is therefore conveyed by al-ḥadīd, rather than by radm itself.

This matters because radm can be created using different materials in Arabic usage.

Defining radm in Arabic as an iron wall on the basis of this passage would unjustifiably restrict the root to one particular instance.

Ḥattā idhā sāwā bayna al-ṣadafayni

Al-Kahf 18:96 continues; an excerpt:

حَتَّىٰ إِذَا سَاوَىٰ بَيْنَ الصَّدَفَيْنِ

ḥattā idhā sāwā bayna al-ṣadafayni

“Until, when he had made the space between the two mountainsides level.”

الصَّدَفَيْنِ (al-ṣadafayni) concerns two high sides or two facing mountainsides.

The verse thus describes a location with an opening between its two sides, where Dhū al-Qarnayn fills the intervening space using the materials provided.

This is clearly consistent with the lexical meaning of radm.

It is not merely an isolated wall in an unspecified setting; it is a barrier between two identified sides.

That description does not establish the structure’s exact height, width, length, or geometric shape.

The verse says that the interval between the two sides was filled and leveled, but does not provide a complete engineering plan.

Qāla unfukhū

Dhū al-Qarnayn then commands in Al-Kahf 18:96; an excerpt:

قَالَ انْفُخُوا

qāla unfukhū

“He said: Blow.”

The continuation speaks of bringing the structure or its materials to a state like fire.

Blowing is connected here with the heating process.

Radm itself, however, does not mean blowing, heating, or melting.

These acts form part of the structure’s construction in the narrative.

The verse itself does not establish the precise furnace, blowing equipment, temperature, or technology employed.

Although such details can be discussed in historical or technical reconstructions, they are not explicitly stated in the verse.

Ufrigh ʿalayhi qiṭran

The end of Al-Kahf 18:96 states; an excerpt:

آتُونِي أُفْرِغْ عَلَيْهِ قِطْرًا

ātūnī ufrigh ʿalayhi qiṭran

“Bring it to me, that I may pour molten copper upon it.”

قِطْر (qiṭr) here concerns molten copper, while أُفْرِغْ (ufrigh) expresses pouring it onto the structure.

This passage shows that the structure was built using iron and molten copper.

The boundary of the word’s meaning must again be maintained.

Radm names the barrier itself, rather than a metal, alloy, or method of pouring molten material.

What technical properties the combination of materials gave the structure, and exactly how it contributed to strength, requires separate investigation.

Without evidence, it should not be identified with a modern metalworking technology, nor should definite claims be made about the final material, chemical composition, or joining method.

What the Qur’an makes clear is the use of iron pieces, heating, and pouring molten copper during construction.

Together, these details show that Dhū al-Qarnayn’s radm was a real structure built with materials.

6. The result: preventing passage and penetration

After describing the construction, the Qur’an states its result in Al-Kahf 18:97:

فَمَا اسْطَاعُوا أَنْ يَظْهَرُوهُ وَمَا اسْتَطَاعُوا لَهُ نَقْبًا

fa-mā isṭāʿū an yaẓharūhu wa-mā istaṭāʿū lahu naqban

“So they could neither climb over it nor pierce it.”

The verse describes two practical features of the structure.

First, they could not pass over it.

Second, they could not make a breach through it.

Passing over a barrier and piercing through it

يَظْهَرُوهُ (yaẓharūhu) here concerns climbing and passing over the barrier.

نَقْبًا (naqban) belongs to N Q B and refers to piercing something or making a passage through it.

The verse therefore presents two different ways through: going over the barrier and opening a route through it.

This description shows that the structure prevented both forms of passage in the situation described.

These two features come from Al-Kahf 18:97, rather than from the independent meaning of radm.

Radm names the barrier. Preventing climbing and penetration characterizes this particular barrier within the narrative.

Is every radm impenetrable?

No.

A breach closed with weak materials may reopen.

A patch in a garment may later fail.

The act of radm therefore does not necessarily create absolute and permanent strength.

In Dhū al-Qarnayn’s narrative, the Qur’an states the effect of the structure’s strength separately.

A particular instance cannot establish that radm inherently means an impassable and indestructible barrier throughout Arabic.

Strength characterizes the structure described; it is not the root’s fixed definition.

A firm barrier, but not an everlasting one

In Al-Kahf 18:98, Dhū al-Qarnayn says; an excerpt:

هَٰذَا رَحْمَةٌ مِنْ رَبِّي

hādhā raḥmatun min rabbī

“This is a mercy from my Lord.”

He then says in the same verse, Al-Kahf 18:98; an excerpt:

فَإِذَا جَاءَ وَعْدُ رَبِّي جَعَلَهُ دَكَّاءَ

fa-idhā jāʾa waʿdu rabbī jaʿalahu dakkāʾa

“Then, when my Lord’s promise comes, He will level it.”

The structure’s strength is not equated here with permanent endurance.

Dhū al-Qarnayn describes its construction as a mercy from his Lord, while also speaking of a time when the structure will be leveled in connection with the divine promise.

Even a structure that has prevented passage and penetration in a particular situation will not necessarily remain in the same condition forever.

The exact time, physical process, and immediate agent of the structure’s removal, however, cannot be determined from the root R D M itself.

دَكَّاءَ (dakkāʾa) belongs to a different root. Leveling or collapse should not be added to the definition of radm.

7. The meaning of radm and historical identification of Dhū al-Qarnayn’s barrier

Historical and exegetical research has attempted various identifications of the location of Dhū al-Qarnayn’s barrier.

Structures in mountainous regions and historical passes have sometimes been compared with this narrative.

However, a particular geographical location cannot be identified from the lexical meaning of radm.

The word names a barrier constructed to close an opening; it does not contain the name of a country, mountain, people, or construction date.

Even the use of iron and molten copper is insufficient by itself to identify a historical structure.

Such a conclusion requires independent geographical, historical, archaeological, and textual evidence.

A root study should therefore not become a list of possible locations or unsupported theories about the barrier’s site.

Nor should radm be restricted to a water-retaining dam in the modern technical sense.

Modern dams may retain water, generate energy, or regulate river flow. The barrier in Dhū al-Qarnayn’s narrative is constructed to prevent one group from reaching or passing through to another.

That distinction follows from the purpose stated in the narrative itself.

Translating radm as a dam can convey the broad idea of a barrier, but should not lead readers to attribute the features of a modern reservoir dam to the verse.

8. Comparison with related roots

R D M (radm) and S D D (sadd)

These roots are close in the ideas of blocking a way and creating a barrier.

Sadd can name anything that obstructs passage or access.

In its clear uses, radm concerns filling an opening and creating a barrier by closing a breach.

In Dhū al-Qarnayn’s narrative, the people request a sadd, while he speaks of building a radm.

The difference can suggest that he proposes a suitably strong barrier constructed in a particular way for their purpose.

It does not establish a fixed distinction in size or strength throughout all uses of the roots.

A radm may function as a kind of sadd, whereas not every sadd is necessarily built by filling a breach.

R D M (radm) and R T Q (ratq)

In Arabic, رَتْق (ratq) concerns joining separated parts and closing a breach.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:30 states; an excerpt:

أَنَّ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ كَانَتَا رَتْقًا فَفَتَقْنَاهُمَا

anna al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍa kānatā ratqan fa-fataqnāhumā

“That the heavens and the earth were joined together, then We opened them apart.”

Ratq and radm are close in the ideas of closure and continuity.

Ratq, however, is contrasted with فَتْق (fatq), opening and separating.

In its clear use, radm concerns closing an opening by adding or joining components.

This does not eliminate overlap. Arabic can describe mending fabric or closing a breach with closely related words.

Study of R T Q

The proximity of the roots does not mean that radm in the Qur’an denotes a cosmic joining of the heavens and the earth.

Al-Kahf 18:95 concerns a real structure in a particular passage.

R D M (radm) and R Ṣ Ṣ (raṣṣ)

In Arabic, رَصّ (raṣṣ) concerns arranging components closely and firmly together.

Al-Ṣaff 61:4 states; an excerpt:

كَأَنَّهُمْ بُنْيَانٌ مَرْصُوصٌ

ka-annahum bunyānun marṣūṣun

“As though they were a tightly joined, firm structure.”

In the continuity of its components, this image is close to the construction of a radm.

Raṣṣ emphasizes how tightly the components are positioned, whereas radm primarily concerns closing a breach and creating a barrier.

Materials may be densely arranged to build a radm, but that does not make the two roots identical.

Nor does بُنْيَانٌ مَرْصُوصٌ (bunyānun marṣūṣun) establish that every radm in Qur’anic language means social unity or people standing in ranks.

Such an extension requires independent evidence.

R D M (radm) and N Q B (naqb)

Naqb concerns piercing something and making a breach in it.

In Al-Kahf 18:97, the same word occurs in the description of the completed barrier; an excerpt:

وَمَا اسْتَطَاعُوا لَهُ نَقْبًا

wa-mā istaṭāʿū lahu naqban

“And they could not pierce it.”

Practically, radm blocks passage in this context, while naqb could open a route through the barrier.

This contrast does not make radm and naqb complete opposites confined to wall construction in every Arabic use.

Naqb has other Arabic uses that require separate examination.

Study of N Q B

R D M (radm) and H D M (hadm)

Hadm concerns demolishing a structure and bringing it down.

By contrast, the primary use of radm concerns closing a breach or creating a barrier.

There is thus a distinction between building or closing on one side and demolishing on the other.

They should not, however, be treated as complete opposites in every use.

For example, radm is also recorded in Arabic for material that falls from a demolished wall.

This lexical use shows that not every form of the root can be restricted to building something new.

In the Al-Kahf passage, however, constructing a barrier is clear. Nothing supports translating radman there as the rubble of a collapsed wall.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

Translating this family requires distinguishing the act of closing, the resulting barrier, and uses concerning repair.

رَدَمَ (radama): closed a breach; filled an opening; blocked a route or entrance.

يَرْدِمُ (yardimu): closes; fills; blocks a breach or opening.

رَدْم (radm): closing a breach; filling an opening. As a substantive noun, a barrier or structure built to obstruct a passage.

رُدُوم (rudūm): barriers or closed sections; the plural of radm.

رَدَّمَ (raddama): patched; repaired; covered damaged sections.

مُرَدَّم (muraddam): patched or repaired; in clothing usage, patched fabric.

رَدِيم (radīm): old and worn, in clothing usage.

رَدِيمَة (radīmah): two pieces of cloth sewn together.

تَرَدَّمَ (taraddama): in تَرَدَّمَ الثَّوْبُ (taraddama al-thawbu), the garment became worn and needed patching; in تَرَدَّمَ الرَّجُلُ ثَوْبَهُ (taraddama al-rajulu thawbahu), the man patched his own garment. The intransitive or transitive sense must be identified from the sentence structure.

مُتَرَدَّم (mutaraddam): a place needing patching or repair; in ʿAntarah’s poetic witness, room to complete or add to speech.

أَرْدَمَتِ الْحُمَّى (ardamati al-ḥummā): the fever continued and did not leave the person.

For the sole Qur’anic occurrence, part of Al-Kahf 18:95 reads:

أَجْعَلْ بَيْنَكُمْ وَبَيْنَهُمْ رَدْمًا

ajʿal baynakum wa-baynahum radman

The existing Persian rendering in Forqan conveys:

“That I may build a firm wall between you and them.”

This rendering fits the narrative: Al-Kahf 18:96 describes building with iron pieces and molten copper, and Al-Kahf 18:97 states the barrier’s ability to prevent passage and penetration.

The lexical explanation should nevertheless make clear that firmness here is supported by the surrounding context. The root R D M does not always mean absolute strength.

For a brief account of the word, a barrier built to close a breach or an obstructing barrier is more precise.

Dam is another possible equivalent, but in contemporary Persian it may suggest a water-retaining dam. A wall closing the passage is clearer in this narrative.

Conversely, translating it as an earthwork unjustifiably restricts the meaning, since the described structure is built using iron and molten copper.

Expressions such as an eternally impenetrable wall or a structure that will never disappear are also incompatible with Al-Kahf 18:98.

A basic translation should preserve the ideas of a barrier and closing an opening, leaving the details of construction, strength, and eventual fate to explanations of the relevant verses.

10. Conclusion

At its semantic core, the root R D M denotes closing a breach, filling an opening, or covering a part left open or whose continuity has been broken.

This meaning appears in blocking doorways, filling breaches, building barriers, and patching fabric.

In Arabic, radm names both the act and the resulting barrier. Derivatives such as muraddam and mutaraddam also show its application to repair.

The opening of ʿAntarah’s Muʿallaqah is an important witness in which repair and patching are transferred to poetry and speech. That literary use should not replace the root’s basic meaning.

In the Qur’an, the root occurs only in Al-Kahf 18:95. Dhū al-Qarnayn intends to build a barrier, with the people’s assistance, between them and Gog and Magog.

In the preceding verse, the people request a sadd, while Dhū al-Qarnayn speaks of constructing a radm. This fits the ideas of closing an opening and joining a continuous barrier, but does not prove that radm is always larger or stronger than sadd throughout Arabic.

Al-Kahf 18:96 describes the use of iron pieces, heating, and pouring molten copper. These details concern the construction of that particular structure and should not enter the lexical definition of radm.

In Al-Kahf 18:97, the barrier’s strength is evident in their inability to climb over or pierce it.

Al-Kahf 18:98 also recalls that, despite its strength in that situation, the structure is neither everlasting nor indestructible.

Radm should therefore not be restricted to an iron wall, a water-retaining dam, an eternal barrier, or an indestructible structure.

Nor can this root’s meaning determine the barrier’s historical location, Dhū al-Qarnayn’s definite identity, or the precise manner of the structure’s eventual removal. Those questions require separate research.

Final definition: In its principal use, R D M denotes closing a breach, filling an opening, and covering an exposed or damaged part. Radm can name the act of closing or the barrier resulting from it. In the Qur’an, the word names the structure Dhū al-Qarnayn built to close the passage between two groups. Its materials, construction method, strength, and eventual fate are learned from the accompanying verses, rather than from the root alone.
