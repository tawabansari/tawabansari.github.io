Laughing and the expression of joy, surprise, or mockery: from human smiles to nature’s blossoming and laughter in the Qur’an

1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

The Arabic root Ḍ Ḥ K primarily concerns laughing. Laughter may appear on a person’s face, part the lips, reveal the teeth, and sometimes produce an audible sound.

A person may laugh through joy, surprise, hearing something pleasant, or seeing an unexpected event. In another situation, laughter may express mockery, contempt, or disregard for someone’s words.

Although laughing is a familiar human behavior, its causes and value are therefore not identical in every situation.

Early Arabic also uses this family for such things as teeth becoming visible, blossoms opening, lightning appearing in clouds, and a road becoming clear.

The root occurs ten times in ten Qur’anic verses. Its forms are:

  • فَضَحِكَتْ: then she laughed; Hūd 11:71.
  • فَلْيَضْحَكُوا: then let them laugh; Al-Tawbah 9:82.
  • تَضْحَكُونَ and وَتَضْحَكُونَ: you laugh; Al-Muʾminūn 23:110 and Al-Najm 53:60.
  • يَضْحَكُونَ: they laugh; Al-Zukhruf 43:47, Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:29, and Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:34.
  • أَضْحَكَ: made someone laugh or caused laughter; Al-Najm 53:43.
  • ضَاحِكًا: laughing; Al-Naml 27:19.
  • ضَاحِكَةٌ: laughing; ʿAbasa 80:39.

There are thus seven uses of the Form I verb ḍaḥika, one of the Form IV verb aḍḥaka, and two active participles, ḍāḥik and ḍāḥikah.

Other important Arabic forms include:

  • ضَحِكَ / يَضْحَكُ: laughed; laughs.
  • ضَحِك and ضَحْك: laughter and laughing.
  • ضَحْكَة: one instance of laughter.
  • ضَاحِك: laughing; in another use, a tooth exposed during laughter.
  • ضَحَّاك: frequently laughing, or someone who laughs a great deal.
  • ضَحُوك: laughing; in other uses, clear or manifest.
  • ضُحَكَة: someone who laughs frequently.
  • ضُحْكَة: someone or something at which others laugh.
  • أَضْحَكَ: made someone laugh or caused laughter.
  • ضَاحَكَ: laughed with another person or joined in their laughter.
  • تَضَاحَكَ: laughed; in some uses, affected a laughing manner.
  • اِسْتَضْحَكَ: began laughing or burst into laughter.
  • أُضْحُوكَة: someone or something made an object of laughter or ridicule.

These forms are established in Arabic, but they do not all occur in the Qur’an.

2. The semantic core and early Arabic usage

Laughter and the opening of the face

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris explains the root through al-inkishāf wa-al-burūz: becoming uncovered and appearing.

This analysis relates to one outward feature of laughter.

When someone laughs, their facial expression changes. The lips may part and the teeth become visible.

Lisān al-ʿArab also explains ḍaḥik through the front teeth becoming visible in joy.

The related word ḍāḥikah names certain teeth that appear during laughter or smiling.

This physical feature should not, however, become a comprehensive definition of all laughter.

A person may smile or laugh quietly without showing their teeth. Teeth may also be visible during speech or when opening the mouth without laughing.

Showing the teeth is therefore a common sign of laughter, rather than a necessary and sufficient condition for every use.

Ibn Fāris’s account of appearing offers an intelligible connection between several branches of the root, but does not itself establish the history of every meaning.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Lisān al-ʿArab: appearing and exposed teeth

Laughter and joy

Arabic often associates laughter with joy and happiness.

Someone may laugh on hearing good news, seeing a loved one, or experiencing something pleasant.

The verb’s basic meaning is laughing, however, and it should not be equated with joy in every use.

Joy is an inward state that may appear through smiling, laughing, speaking, or other behavior.

Laughter may also arise from surprise, anxiety, or mockery.

Not every use of ḍaḥika can therefore be translated merely as “became happy.”

A witness attributed to pre-Islamic poetry

A fragment attributed to ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād reads:

ضَحِكَتْ عُبَيْلَةُ إِذْ رَأَتْنِي عَارِيًا
خَلَقَ الْقَمِيصِ وَسَاعِدِي مَخْدُوشُ

ḍaḥikat ʿubaylatu idh raʾatnī ʿāriyan / khalaqa al-qamīṣi wa-sāʿidī makhdūshu

Translation:

“ʿUbaylah laughed when she saw me poorly clad, in a worn shirt, with my arm scratched.”

In the next line, the poet asks her to look at his courage in battle rather than laugh at his appearance.

Laughter here concerns a situation that may involve mockery.

The poet distinguishes his shabby appearance from his ability in battle.

This use shows that early Arabic laughter was not solely a sign of joy and affection: a person might laugh at someone else and belittle them.

Ḍaḥikat alone nevertheless does not conclusively establish mockery. That interpretation becomes clearer through the continuation.

Poetic collections attribute the fragment to ʿAntarah, but individual pieces in early collected poetry are not always as securely attributed as the celebrated Muʿallaqāt. The witness should be approached with this caution.

The collected poetry attributed to ʿAntarah: the ʿUbaylah fragment

Smiling and laughing

Tabassum concerns the gentle opening of the face in a smile.

In ordinary use, ḍaḥik covers a broader field of laughing and may involve more visible teeth or an audible laugh.

Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī describes smiling as the beginning or a degree of laughter.

This account shows that tabassum and ḍaḥik are closely related, without being wholly synonymous.

A verse by Kuthayyir ʿAzzah reads:

غَمْرُ الرِّدَاءِ إِذَا تَبَسَّمَ ضَاحِكًا
غَلِقَتْ لِضَحْكَتِهِ رِقَابُ الْمَالِ

ghamru al-ridāʾi idhā tabassama ḍāḥikan / ghaliqat li-ḍaḥkatihi riqābu al-māli

An approximate rendering of the sense:

“A generous man who, when he smiles with laughter, leaves wealth pledged to his generosity and good cheer.”

Tabassama and ḍāḥikan occur together in this verse.

Their combination shows that smiling may accompany laughter without necessarily constituting two separate stages in time.

Kuthayyir was an Umayyad poet. This is therefore an early Islamic Arabic witness, rather than pre-Islamic poetry.

The construction clearly relates to fa-tabassama ḍāḥikan in Al-Naml 27:19.

Lisān al-ʿArab, RDY: Kuthayyir’s witness and generosity

3. Appearing and blossoming in nature

Ḍaḥikat al-arḍ: the earth blossomed

Early Arabic uses the expression:

ضَحِكَتِ الْأَرْضُ

ḍaḥikati al-arḍu

That is, the earth revealed its plants and flowers.

The earth is not laughing like a human being. Its vegetation is blossoming and becoming visible.

When the earth becomes green after rain and flowers appear, the transformation can be likened to a face opening in laughter.

This is an illustrative, figurative use.

It clarifies a relationship between appearing and laughter, but does not establish that plant growth was the word’s earliest historical meaning.

Ḍaḥikat al-nakhlah: the palm blossom opened

Lexical sources use ضَحِكَتِ النَّخْلَةُ and أَضْحَكَتْ when the covering of the palm’s blossom opens and its interior becomes visible.

Some sources also call the newly exposed part al-ḍaḥk or al-ḍaḥāk.

This bears an illustrative resemblance to lips parting and teeth becoming visible.

In both, something previously covered appears.

The comparison does not imply that the tree has human feelings of joy or actually produces a laughing sound.

Lexical research must distinguish figurative meaning from a plant’s actual biological properties.

Lightning in clouds and a clear road

Ibn al-Aʿrābī also speaks of a cloud’s ḍaḥik.

When lightning appears within a cloud and illuminates part of it, figurative Arabic may describe the cloud as laughing.

Ṭarīq ḍaḥūk is likewise recorded for a clear, visible road.

These uses associate laughter with illumination and becoming visible.

Ibn Durayd also records ḥajar ḍāḥik for a stone whose brightness makes it conspicuous among a mountain’s stones.

These witnesses show that the family could also describe nonhuman phenomena in Arabic.

The Qur’anic uses, however, concern laughing and causing laughter. Plant blossoms or lightning in clouds should not be imported into them without evidence.

Ḍaḥk as honey and blossom

Some early sources also record al-ḍaḥk for honey, honeycomb, whiteness, and blossom.

A verse by Abū Dhuʾayb al-Hudhalī contains an expression in which lexical explanations associate al-ḍaḥk with honey or honeycomb.

Some lexicographers explain the connection through the whiteness and brightness of teeth exposed during laughter.

The precise meaning in that witness is disputed, however: suggestions include honey, honeycomb, and other white substances.

A disputed witness cannot therefore establish the family’s derivational history conclusively.

These limited uses should be recognized, while laughing remains the clear principal meaning for the Qur’anic study.

4. Laughter in the stories of Abraham and Solomon

Faḍaḥikat in Hūd 11:71

Hūd reads:

وَامْرَأَتُهُ قَائِمَةٌ فَضَحِكَتْ فَبَشَّرْنَاهَا بِإِسْحَاقَ وَمِنْ وَرَاءِ إِسْحَاقَ يَعْقُوبَ

wa-imraʾatuhu qāʾimatun fa-ḍaḥikat fa-bashsharnāhā bi-isḥāqa wa-min warāʾi isḥāqa yaʿqūba

“His wife was standing, and she laughed; then We gave her good news of Isaac, and after Isaac, Jacob.”

Hūd 11:71

Faḍaḥikat is a third-person feminine singular perfect verb.

Its subject is Abraham’s wife.

The verse mentions her laughter before reporting the good news of Isaac’s birth.

This order matters: without supporting evidence, the translation should not imply that the birth announcement first came and she then laughed.

In the preceding verse, Abraham became apprehensive at his guests’ behavior. They reassured him and said they had been sent to Lot’s people.

In this context, his wife’s laughter may relate to relief from anxiety or surprise at the events.

The good news of Isaac and Jacob then follows.

The next verse explicitly gives her astonished response to the birth announcement:

قَالَتْ يَا وَيْلَتَىٰ أَأَلِدُ وَأَنَا عَجُوزٌ وَهَٰذَا بَعْلِي شَيْخًا

qālat yā waylatā a-alidu wa-anā ʿajūzun wa-hādhā baʿlī shaykhan

“She said: Woe to me! Shall I bear a child when I am an old woman and this husband of mine is an old man?”

Hūd 11:72

Astonishment at the birth announcement is therefore clear in the continuation. The precise cause of the preceding laughter should not be determined without conclusive evidence.

Al-Ṭabarī: differing explanations of Abraham’s wife’s laughter

Does faḍaḥikat mean that she menstruated?

Some lexical and exegetical sources also report “menstruated” for ضَحِكَتِ الْمَرْأَةُ.

On this interpretation, Abraham’s wife resumed menstruation in old age and then received the announcement of a child.

Exegetical sources attribute this view to certain early commentators.

Its lexical authority is seriously disputed, however.

Al-Farrāʾ explicitly states that he had not heard ḍaḥikat meaning ḥāḍat from a trustworthy transmitter.

Al-Zajjāj likewise rejects the interpretation.

Other lexicographers support it with poetry concerning rabbits and hyenas, although those verses’ meanings are themselves disputed.

For example, a verse whose attribution varies among sources mentions ḍaḥk al-arānib, which some interpreted as menstrual blood.

For a similar expression concerning hyenas, however, Ibn Durayd and others explain that it may concern exposing the teeth or the animal’s condition while eating meat, rather than menstruation.

A report’s presence in a dictionary is therefore insufficient to prefer it conclusively in Hūd 11:71.

The familiar and stronger meaning of faḍaḥikat remains “she laughed.”

The verse’s construction and context do not require abandoning that meaning.

“Then she laughed” is consequently a suitable translation; the menstruation reading remains a disputed lexical and exegetical interpretation.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: Al-Farrāʾ and Al-Zajjāj on the disputed menstruation reading

Fa-tabassama ḍāḥikan in Al-Naml 27:19

Solomon’s story reads:

فَتَبَسَّمَ ضَاحِكًا مِنْ قَوْلِهَا

fa-tabassama ḍāḥikan min qawlihā

“Then he smiled with laughter at her words.”

Al-Naml 27:19

This expression follows the ant’s words concerning Solomon and his troops entering the valley of ants.

Two words from different families occur here:

Tabassama belongs to B S M, while ḍāḥikan belongs to Ḍ Ḥ K.

Tabassum concerns smiling.

Ḍāḥikan is an accusative active participle describing Solomon’s state while smiling.

Grammatically it is a circumstantial expression. The sentence need not be divided into separate events in which Solomon first smiled and then laughed.

It can be understood as a smile accompanied by laughter, or a smiling, laughing expression.

Solomon continues by asking his Lord to enable him to give thanks for blessings and perform righteous deeds.

Laughter here thus accompanies his reaction to the ant’s words and subsequent attention to divine blessing.

Ḍ Ḥ K itself does not mean thanksgiving, however. Thanks is expressed by ashkura later in the verse.

Nor should Solomon’s laughter be taken to mean that he belittled the ant. The context of prayer and attention to the blessing of The God does not clearly support that interpretation.

5. Laughter of joy and good news

ʿAbasa 80:39: laughing faces

ʿAbasa reads:

وُجُوهٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ مُسْفِرَةٌ
ضَاحِكَةٌ مُسْتَبْشِرَةٌ

wujūhun yawmaʾidhin musfiratun / ḍāḥikatun mustabshiratun

“Faces that day will be radiant, laughing, rejoicing in good news.”

ʿAbasa 80:38 and ʿAbasa 80:39

Ḍāḥikah describes faces in these verses.

Musfirah concerns the radiance of the faces.

Ḍāḥikah expresses their laughing state.

Mustabshirah concerns joy and receiving good news.

Together, the three expressions create an image of radiant, joyful faces.

Each word nevertheless has its own contribution.

Laughing comes from ḍāḥikah, while good news and rejoicing come from mustabshirah.

Ḍaḥika should therefore not mean receiving good news in every use.

Laughter here signals a favorable, joyful condition, whereas elsewhere it may accompany mockery.

Wa-annahu huwa aḍḥaka wa-abkā

Al-Najm reads:

وَأَنَّهُ هُوَ أَضْحَكَ وَأَبْكَىٰ

wa-annahu huwa aḍḥaka wa-abkā

“And that it is He who caused laughter and weeping.”

Al-Najm 53:43

Aḍḥaka is Form IV.

The verb expresses causing laughter: making someone laugh or bringing about the circumstances of their laughter.

Alongside it stands abkā, “caused weeping.”

The two verbs place familiar human responses together.

The next verse also reads:

وَأَنَّهُ هُوَ أَمَاتَ وَأَحْيَا

wa-annahu huwa amāta wa-aḥyā

“And that it is He who caused death and gave life.”

Al-Najm 53:44

Laughter and weeping, death and life, are thus presented in relation to the action and power of The God.

Aḍḥaka does not imply that The God morally approves every particular instance of human laughter.

A person may laugh through good, legitimate joy or hurt another through ridicule.

Attributing the possibility and occurrence of laughter and weeping to The God does not by itself endorse every human use of these abilities.

Nor does the verse describe the neural, hormonal, or biological mechanisms of laughter and weeping.

Natural science may investigate those subjects, but they do not follow from aḍḥaka’s lexical meaning.

6. Laughter expressing mockery and contempt

Al-Muʾminūn 23:110: laughing at believers

Al-Muʾminūn reads:

فَاتَّخَذْتُمُوهُمْ سِخْرِيًّا حَتَّىٰ أَنْسَوْكُمْ ذِكْرِي وَكُنْتُمْ مِنْهُمْ تَضْحَكُونَ

fa-ittakhadhtumūhum sikhriyyan ḥattā ansawkum dhikrī wa-kuntum minhum taḍḥakūna

“You made them an object of ridicule until they made you forget My remembrance, and you used to laugh at them.”

Al-Muʾminūn 23:110

Laughter accompanies sikhriyyan in this verse.

In this context, S KH R concerns mocking and belittling others.

The laughter is therefore plainly derisive here.

Mockery does not, however, arise from Ḍ Ḥ K alone.

Translating taḍḥakūna merely as “you mocked” would obscure the distinction between laughing and its derisive character.

A more precise rendering preserves both contributions: they ridiculed the believers and laughed at them.

The verse shows that laughter can become an instrument for humiliating people.

It does not follow that every instance of laughing at someone, including affectionate, harmless joking, constitutes the same condemned ridicule.

Al-Zukhruf 43:47: laughing at the signs

Moses’ story reads:

فَلَمَّا جَاءَهُمْ بِآيَاتِنَا إِذَا هُمْ مِنْهَا يَضْحَكُونَ

fa-lammā jāʾahum bi-āyātinā idhā hum minhā yaḍḥakūna

“When he brought them Our signs, there they were laughing at them.”

Al-Zukhruf 43:47

Here, laughter is directed at the signs Moses brought.

The confrontation between Moses, Pharaoh, and Pharaoh’s companions shows this laughter to be a way of belittling the signs and failing to take them seriously.

The verb yaḍḥakūna still means laughing.

Disregard and mockery are understood through the object of laughter and the audience’s conduct.

Merely laughing at a claim should not, however, be treated in every circumstance as unbelief or conscious hostility to truth.

The verse concerns a particular group’s response to signs presented by Moses in a clear context.

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:29: the wrongdoers’ laughter

Al-Muṭaffifīn reads:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ أَجْرَمُوا كَانُوا مِنَ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا يَضْحَكُونَ

inna alladhīna ajramū kānū mina alladhīna āmanū yaḍḥakūna

“Indeed, those who committed wrongdoing used to laugh at those who believed.”

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:29

The following verses explain their conduct: they exchanged gestures when passing the believers, returned merrily to their families, and called the believers misguided.

Laughter here forms part of contemptuous social behavior.

The verse suggests that ridicule need not be an individual, momentary response. It may become collective behavior in which people support one another in belittling another group.

Ḍaḥika itself does not mean group partisanship, wrongdoing, or false judgment.

These features emerge from the agents, the object of their laughter, and the surrounding verses.

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:34: a reversal of position

A few verses later comes:

فَالْيَوْمَ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا مِنَ الْكُفَّارِ يَضْحَكُونَ

fa-al-yawma alladhīna āmanū mina al-kuffāri yaḍḥakūna

“Today, those who believed laugh at the disbelievers.”

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:34

This verse contrasts with Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:29.

In the first scene, wrongdoers laughed at believers.

In the second, believers laugh at disbelievers.

The contrast shows a reversal of position and the outcome of past conduct becoming apparent.

Yaḍḥakūna means laughing in both verses, while the positions of those laughing and the object of their laughter change.

This contrast should not imply that the Qur’an approves ridicule and humiliation in worldly life without regard for truth and justice.

The second verse concerns judgment and the outcome in the Hereafter, rather than a general instruction to laugh at opponents in social life.

7. Laughter and weeping in contrast

Al-Tawbah 9:82: falyaḍḥakū qalīlan

Al-Tawbah reads:

فَلْيَضْحَكُوا قَلِيلًا وَلْيَبْكُوا كَثِيرًا جَزَاءً بِمَا كَانُوا يَكْسِبُونَ

fa-l-yaḍḥakū qalīlan wa-l-yabkū kathīran jazāʾan bi-mā kānū yaksibūna

“Let them laugh a little and weep much, as recompense for what they used to earn.”

Al-Tawbah 9:82

Laughter and weeping stand in contrast here.

Falyaḍḥakū is an imperfect verb with the imperative lām.

Its context nevertheless shows that it is not an instruction to perform worship or an encouragement to laugh.

The preceding verses describe people who failed to accompany others in difficult circumstances and rejoiced at staying behind.

“Let them laugh a little and weep much” thus warns of their present joy’s brevity and their conduct’s consequences.

The imperative form functions here as warning and threat.

The verse should not be understood as commanding all people to laugh little and weep much.

The context identifies the audience and reason for the warning.

Al-Najm 53:60: laughter before serious speech

Al-Najm reads:

أَفَمِنْ هَٰذَا الْحَدِيثِ تَعْجَبُونَ
وَتَضْحَكُونَ وَلَا تَبْكُونَ

a-fa-min hādhā al-ḥadīthi taʿjabūna / wa-taḍḥakūna wa-lā tabkūna

“Do you then marvel at this discourse, and laugh rather than weep?”

Al-Najm 53:59 and Al-Najm 53:60

In this passage, laughter accompanies astonishment and a particular response to the message.

The continuation describes people responding in a way unsuited to the message’s importance.

Laughter is therefore censured in this context.

That censure does not mean that laughing itself is objectionable in every situation.

The same surah states in Al-Najm 53:43 that The God caused laughter and weeping.

No general prohibition of joy or laughter can therefore be derived from Al-Najm 53:60.

The issue is the response to speech that should be taken seriously and considered thoughtfully.

Laughter and weeping: two different responses

Both laughter and weeping may accompany different human emotions.

Laughter may arise from joy, surprise, or mockery.

Weeping may arise from sorrow, fear, longing, joy, or regret.

Neither behavior alone is therefore a conclusive measure of piety, faith, or corruption.

The Qur’anic evaluation of laughter and weeping is understood through their context and object.

Solomon’s laughter in Al-Naml 27:19 accompanies attentiveness and thanksgiving.

Derisive laughter is condemned in Al-Muʾminūn 23:110.

The laughing faces receiving good news in ʿAbasa 80:39 appear in a favorable picture.

These differences show that Ḍ Ḥ K does not itself carry a fixed moral value.

8. Comparison with neighboring roots

Ḍ Ḥ K (ḍaḥika) and B S M (tabassama)

Tabassum concerns smiling.

Ḍaḥika concerns laughing and may take a more visible or audible form.

The two words occur in one construction in Al-Naml 27:19:

فَتَبَسَّمَ ضَاحِكًا

fa-tabassama ḍāḥikan

“He smiled with laughter.”

Their combination shows that smiling and laughing may occur together.

It does not imply that every smile must produce a laughing sound, or that every laugh necessarily exposes the teeth fully.

The distinction is a tendency: tabassum emphasizes a smile and a gentle facial expression, while ḍaḥika emphasizes laughing.

Study of B S M

Ḍ Ḥ K (ḍaḥika) and B K Y (bakā)

Bakā concerns weeping.

Al-Najm 53:43 combines the two families in aḍḥaka wa-abkā.

Laughter and weeping also stand in contrast in Al-Tawbah 9:82 and Al-Najm 53:60.

This contrast is clear, but does not mean laughter always denotes joy and weeping always denotes sorrow.

Someone may weep with longing or laugh through anxiety.

The roots express different behaviors; context identifies the inward state.

Study of B K Y

Ḍ Ḥ K (ḍaḥika) and S KH R (sakhira)

In its interpersonal use, S KH R concerns mocking and belittling another person.

Ḍaḥika concerns laughing itself.

The two concepts occur together in Al-Muʾminūn 23:110.

Someone may humiliate another through laughter, but not all laughter is mockery.

Conversely, mockery may use words, gestures, demeaning imitation, or other behavior without laughing.

Mockery and laughter are therefore related but distinct.

Study of S KH R

Ḍ Ḥ K (ḍaḥika) and H Z ʾ (istahzaʾa)

Istihzāʾ concerns ridicule and treating someone lightly.

It may involve laughter, but is not confined to it.

The Qur’an uses istihzāʾ for various verbal and social behaviors.

Ḍaḥika in verses such as Al-Naml 27:19 and ʿAbasa 80:39 has no derisive meaning.

Laughing should therefore not automatically be translated as ridiculing.

Ḍ Ḥ K (ḍaḥika) and F R Ḥ (fariḥa)

Faraḥ concerns joy and becoming happy.

Ḍaḥika concerns the response of laughing.

Someone may be happy and laugh, but inward joy does not necessarily produce visible laughter.

Someone may also laugh to mock another without their pleasure arising from a good event.

Faraḥ and ḍaḥika therefore accompany one another in some contexts without being identical.

Ḍ Ḥ K (ḍaḥika) and ʿ J B (ʿajiba)

ʿAjab concerns surprise and astonishment.

Laughter sometimes arises from an unexpected event.

Abraham’s wife’s astonishment is explicit in the subsequent verses of her story.

Astonishment and laughter also occur together in Al-Najm 53:59 and Al-Najm 53:60.

This proximity does not imply that every instance of astonishment is laughter or that every laugh arises from astonishment.

Study of ʿ J B

Ḍ Ḥ K (ḍaḥika) and Ḍ Ḥ W (ḍuḥā)

The root Ḍ Ḥ W concerns daylight and parts of the day in Arabic.

Ḍuḥā in the Qur’an identifies a time of daylight.

Ḍ Ḥ K, in contrast, concerns laughing.

Although figurative uses of ḍaḥika concern illumination or appearing, similar letters and related imagery do not make the roots identical.

Ḍuḥā should therefore not be counted as a derivative of Ḍ Ḥ K, nor should laughter fundamentally be defined as daylight.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

ضَحِكَ: laughed.

يَضْحَكُ: laughs.

ضَحِك and ضَحْك: laughter; laughing.

ضَحْكَة: one laugh.

In most of these uses, “laughing” is the clear, natural equivalent.

“Becoming happy,” “mocking,” and “being surprised” may explain the cause or character of laughter in particular verses, but should preferably not replace the verb itself.

In Hūd 11:71, “menstruated” likewise lacks sufficiently clear and undisputed support to replace the familiar meaning of laughing in the base translation.

10. Conclusion

Ḍ Ḥ K concerns laughing in Arabic. It may involve a changed facial expression, parted lips, visible teeth, and sometimes an audible laugh.

Ibn Fāris explains the family through appearing and becoming visible. Figurative uses such as flowers blossoming, palm blossoms opening, lightning appearing in clouds, and a road becoming clear relate to this image.

Such illustrative explanations should not replace the established meaning of laughing.

The root occurs ten times in the Qur’an.

In Hūd 11:71, Abraham’s wife laughs and then receives the announcement of Isaac’s birth.

In Al-Naml 27:19, Solomon smiles with laughter on hearing the ant’s words.

ʿAbasa 80:39 describes laughing faces rejoicing in good news.

In Al-Muʾminūn 23:110, Al-Zukhruf 43:47, and Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:29, laughter instead accompanies mockery and belittlement.

In Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:34, laughter appears in a scene of reversed positions and the outcome in the Hereafter.

Al-Najm 53:43 attributes both causing laughter and causing weeping to The God.

This variety shows that laughter has no fixed moral value by itself. Its cause, object, the person’s conduct, and its social consequences require separate examination.

Verses condemning laughter cannot establish a general prohibition of joy and laughing, just as laughter in a favorable situation does not endorse every kind of laughter or ridicule.

Brief root meaning for Forqan:

Laughing and the appearance of laughter in the face; figuratively, appearing, blossoming, or becoming bright and visible.

The formation and verse context determine each derivative’s precise meaning. Joy, surprise, mockery, and good news may accompany laughter, but none is the fixed meaning of every use of the root.
