Washing, bathing, removing dirt with water, and what washing leaves behind

Arabic gh s l concerns washing something with water and removing dirt. Ghasala applies to washing the body, clothing, or another object; ightasala ordinarily means washing oneself or bathing; and ghusl names bodily washing.

Ghasīl and maghsūl describe something washed; ghasūl denotes water or material used for washing; ghusālah denotes the water or matter left from washing; and mughtasal can denote a bathing place or something used for washing.

The root occurs four times in four Quranic forms: taghtasilū in Al-Nisāʾ 4:43, fa-ghsilū in Al-Māʾidah 5:6, mughtasal in Ṣād 38:42, and ghislīn in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:36.

The first three directly concern washing. Ghislīn describes food of punishment in the Hereafter and is connected in lexical sources with foul matter and what is washed from a wound or body.

Washing, becoming clean, bathing, and residual matter are not one meaning. Each derivative requires attention to its form and context.

1. Meaning in early Arabic

In early Arabic, ghasala al-shayʾa meant washing something, ordinarily with water.

This can concern hands, the face, the body, clothing, tools, or anything else that can be washed.

Water contacts the object and may remove soil, sweat, grime, or other surface matter.

The action differs from its result. Something may be washed without every impurity being removed; something already clean may also be washed to fulfil a religious instruction.

Ghasl therefore denotes washing itself. Complete cleanliness, though a usual aim or result, is not guaranteed by every use of the verb.

An early witness: washing in Imruʾ al-Qays’s poetry

The Muʿallaqah attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays describes a powerful horse:

فَعَادَى عِدَاءً بَيْنَ ثَوْرٍ وَنَعْجَةٍ

fa-ʿādā ʿidāʾan bayna thawrin wa-naʿjatin

دِرَاكًا وَلَمْ يَنْضَحْ بِمَاءٍ فَيُغْسَلِ

dirākan wa-lam yanḍaḥ bi-māʾin fa-yughsali

Translation:

“In one run it caught a wild bull and cow in succession, without sweating so as to need washing.”

The poet describes the horse’s prowess: despite its fast pursuit, it does not sweat enough to require washing.

Fa-yughsali refers here to washing the horse.

Washing an animal with water is a familiar physical activity in this use.

No religious technical term or special rite is needed to explain it: the audience understands ordinary washing after a horse has run and sweated.

The line is a familiar witness in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period and occurs in transmissions of the Muʿallaqah and early lexical and literary sources. Despite variations in Imruʾ al-Qays’s poetry, it is preserved in several recognized transmissions and commentaries.

It provides evidence for washing with water as a familiar Arabic sense independently of later ritual prescriptions.

Washing in everyday life

Washing was not confined to the human body.

Clothing, containers, tools, animals, and household objects were also washed.

For example, soiled cloth could be washed with water; a person might also wash after work and sweating.

Arabic lexicons record ghasīl for something washed and ghusālah for water or matter left from washing.

Cleaning plants and substances for washing hair and clothing were also called ghasūl or related forms.

A poem attributed to al-Rabīʿ ibn Ziyād al-ʿAbsī names a grazing plant transmitted as ghasūl and, in another version, ghaswīl. It attests an early plant name, but does not itself show use for washing in that verse.

The lexical family thus extends to means and products of washing. Recording derivatives in later dictionaries, however, cannot date the origin of every form.

2. Semantic core and branches

A. Ghasl: washing something

Ghasl is the verbal noun of ghasala and denotes washing.

Ordinarily, a person washes something with water.

This may remove grime, sweat, dust, or other matter. It may also fulfil a particular instruction even when no visible dirt is present.

Someone might wash soil from their hands after gardening; another might wash hands and face before worship according to a prescribed instruction.

Both involve washing, although their reasons and purposes differ.

The lexical sense should not be restricted to one cause or type of impurity.

B. Ghusl and ightisāl: bodily washing

Ghusl commonly denotes washing the body.

Ightasala is form VIII and ordinarily means washing oneself or bathing.

Lexicons distinguish ghasala al-shayʾa, potentially washing any object, from ightasala, generally washing one’s own body.

Quranic uses fit this distinction.

Fa-ghsilū in Al-Māʾidah 5:6 concerns washing faces and hands.

Taghtasilū in Al-Nisāʾ 4:43 concerns bathing in relation to janābah.

Form VIII alone does not supply every detail of ritual bathing. The kind of washing, conditions, and relation to janābah depend on context.

C. Ghasīl and maghsūl: what has been washed

Both forms describe something washed.

A garment washed with water may be called maghsūl.

Ghasīl can also denote washed clothing or a person whose body has been washed.

These forms concern what has undergone washing, rather than performing it.

Being washed does not itself establish that something is entirely clean, sound, or usable.

A stain may remain in washed fabric, and washing may serve a purpose other than removing ordinary dirt.

“Washed” and “completely clean” should therefore be distinguished.

D. Ghasūl: a means of washing

Ghasūl denotes water or a substance used to wash the head, body, or clothing.

Lexicons include cleaning plants and materials under this name.

Someone might wash clothing with water and a substance that helps loosen dirt.

Here ghasūl is a means of washing.

It should not be restricted to modern soap, since early Arabic knew various washing materials.

E. Ghusālah: washing water and residual matter

Ghusālah denotes water used for washing or matter removed through washing.

Washing soil-covered cloth can make the water muddy.

Three things should be distinguished: the cloth being washed, the water used, and the dirty matter or water left afterwards.

This matters for Quranic ghislīn, which lexicographers connect with matter washed away.

The resemblance does not establish the precise nature of ghislīn in the Hereafter.

3. Important forms and derivatives

Recognized Arabic forms include:

  • Ghasala / yaghsilu: washed.
  • Ghasl: washing.
  • Ghusl: bodily washing; in some uses, water or material for washing.
  • Ghisl: in some lexical uses, material for washing the head and body.
  • Ightasala / yaghtasilu: washed oneself or bathed.
  • Ightisāl: bathing or bodily washing.
  • Ghassala / yughassilu: washed; sometimes with emphasis on repetition or extent.
  • Taghsīl: washing, sometimes thorough or repeated.
  • Ghāsil: someone who washes.
  • Maghsūl: washed.
  • Ghasīl: something or someone washed.
  • Ghusālah: water or matter left from washing.
  • Ghasūl: washing material or means.
  • Mighsal / maghsal: a vessel, implement, or place for washing, according to usage.
  • Mughtasal: a bathing place, or water or means used for washing.
  • Ghislīn: lexically, something washed away; in the Quran, a substance describing food of punishment.

Only four derivatives occur in the Quran.

Form II ghassala, ghāsil, ghasīl, ghusālah, and ghasūl do not occur there.

They illuminate the family but should not be labelled Quranic derivatives.

4. Quranic occurrences

Al-Nisāʾ 4:43: taghtasilū and bodily washing

Al-Nisāʾ 4:43

يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا لَا تَقْرَبُوا ٱلصَّلَوٰةَ وَأَنتُمْ سُكَٰرَىٰ حَتَّىٰ تَعْلَمُوا مَا تَقُولُونَ وَلَا جُنُبًا إِلَّا عَابِرِي سَبِيلٍ حَتَّىٰ تَغْتَسِلُوا وَإِن كُنتُم مَّرْضَىٰ أَوْ عَلَىٰ سَفَرٍ أَوْ جَاءَ أَحَدٌ مِّنكُم مِّنَ ٱلْغَائِطِ أَوْ لَامَسْتُمُ ٱلنِّسَاءَ فَلَمْ تَجِدُوا مَاءً فَتَيَمَّمُوا صَعِيدًا طَيِّبًا فَامْسَحُوا بِوُجُوهِكُمْ وَأَيْدِيكُمْ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ كَانَ عَفُوًّا غَفُورًا

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū lā taqrabū al-ṣalāta wa-antum sukārā ḥattā taʿlamū mā taqūlūna wa-lā junuban illā ʿābirī sabīlin ḥattā taghtasilū wa-in kuntum marḍā aw ʿalā safarin aw jāʾa aḥadun minkum mina al-ghāʾiṭi aw lāmastumu al-nisāʾa fa-lam tajidū māʾan fa-tayammamū ṣaʿīdan ṭayyiban fa-msaḥū bi-wujūhikum wa-aydīkum inna allāha kāna ʿafuwwan ghafūrā

Al-Nisāʾ 4:43

“You who have believed, do not approach ṣalāt while intoxicated until you know what you are saying, nor in a state of janābah, except when passing on a way, until you bathe. If you are ill or travelling, or one of you comes from relieving oneself, or you have had contact with women and find no water, turn to a clean surface of earth and wipe your faces and hands. Indeed, The God is Pardoning, Forgiving.”

Taghtasilū is a second-person plural imperfect verb in form VIII.

It follows ḥattā and denotes washing the body.

Here washing is related to the state described by junuban.

The subject is bathing in relation to leaving that state, rather than merely washing a specified limb.

Washing and janābah are distinct

Janābah belongs to j n b and requires independent study.

Ghasl belongs to gh s l and concerns washing.

Here bathing relates to ending the impediment associated with janābah.

Taghtasilū itself should not be redefined as sexual relations or the whole body of rules about janābah.

Washing one’s body after daily activity is bathing, but it does not itself establish the person’s prior ritual state.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:43, context connects washing with janābah and approaching ṣalāt.

Does taghtasilū mean only becoming pure?

No. Ightisāl denotes bodily washing itself.

Purity may be its result or purpose, but the words are not equivalent.

Physical cleanliness may be achieved by washing.

Elsewhere the Quran discusses purity of hearts, conduct, or human relationships without bodily washing being intended.

Taghtasilū should not be rendered solely as spiritual purification, removing the actual act of washing.

The instruction when water is unavailable

The verse goes on to discuss illness, travel, and not finding water, and commands tayammum.

This shows attention to people’s actual circumstances in purification instructions.

Tayammum belongs to another root and is not lexically identical with washing.

The alternative instruction does not change taghtasilū’s meaning: bathing remains bodily washing, while tayammum is provided for specified circumstances.

Forqan’s separate study of ṣalāt discusses these instructions and their relation to prayer in greater detail.

Al-Māʾidah 5:6: fa-ghsilū and specified body parts

Al-Māʾidah 5:6

يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا إِذَا قُمْتُمْ إِلَى ٱلصَّلَوٰةِ فَٱغْسِلُوا وُجُوهَكُمْ وَأَيْدِيَكُمْ إِلَى ٱلْمَرَافِقِ وَٱمْسَحُوا بِرُءُوسِكُمْ وَأَرْجُلَكُمْ إِلَى ٱلْكَعْبَيْنِ وَإِن كُنتُمْ جُنُبًا فَٱطَّهَّرُوا وَإِن كُنتُم مَّرْضَىٰ أَوْ عَلَىٰ سَفَرٍ أَوْ جَاءَ أَحَدٌ مِّنكُم مِّنَ ٱلْغَائِطِ أَوْ لَامَسْتُمُ ٱلنِّسَاءَ فَلَمْ تَجِدُوا مَاءً فَتَيَمَّمُوا صَعِيدًا طَيِّبًا فَٱمْسَحُوا بِوُجُوهِكُمْ وَأَيْدِيكُم مِّنْهُ مَا يُرِيدُ ٱللَّهُ لِيَجْعَلَ عَلَيْكُم مِّنْ حَرَجٍ وَلَٰكِن يُرِيدُ لِيُطَهِّرَكُمْ وَلِيُتِمَّ نِعْمَتَهُ عَلَيْكُمْ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَشْكُرُونَ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū idhā qumtum ilā al-ṣalāti fa-ghsilū wujūhakum wa-aydiyakum ilā al-marāfiqi wa-msaḥū bi-ruʾūsikum wa-arjulakum ilā al-kaʿbayni wa-in kuntum junuban fa-ṭṭahharū wa-in kuntum marḍā aw ʿalā safarin aw jāʾa aḥadun minkum mina al-ghāʾiṭi aw lāmastumu al-nisāʾa fa-lam tajidū māʾan fa-tayammamū ṣaʿīdan ṭayyiban fa-msaḥū bi-wujūhikum wa-aydīkum minhu mā yurīdu allāhu li-yajʿala ʿalaykum min ḥarajin wa-lākin yurīdu li-yuṭahhirakum wa-li-yutimma niʿmatahu ʿalaykum laʿallakum tashkurūn

Al-Māʾidah 5:6

“You who have believed, when you rise for ṣalāt, wash your faces and your hands up to the elbows, and wipe your heads and your feet up to the ankles. If you are in janābah, purify yourselves. If you are ill or travelling, or one of you comes from relieving oneself, or you have had contact with women and find no water, turn to clean earth and wipe your faces and hands with it. The God does not intend to impose hardship on you, but intends to purify you and complete His favour upon you, so that you may give thanks.”

Fa-ghsilū is a plural imperative of ghasala.

Fa connects the command to wash with the preceding situation of rising for ṣalāt.

Faces and hands up to the elbows are the objects of the initial washing command.

Ghasl here concerns washing particular body parts.

Fa-ghsilū and wa-msaḥū

The verse uses two distinct verbs.

Fa-ghsilū belongs to gh s l.

Wa-msaḥū belongs to m s ḥ.

Washing and wiping do not mean the same thing.

Washing ordinarily involves applying water to the part and washing it.

Wiping concerns passing a hand or something else over a surface and can involve moisture in ritual uses.

The results may sometimes overlap, but the lexical meanings differ.

Rules of ablution require separate consideration of arjulakum’s grammatical relation to the preceding verbs and the variant readings. Gh s l alone cannot resolve that grammatical and reading question.

The distinction between washing and wiping should nevertheless remain in translation.

Washing and becoming pure

The verse continues: wa-in kuntum junuban fa-ṭṭahharū.

Fa-ṭṭahharū belongs to ṭ h r, not gh s l.

Two lexical families are therefore being used.

Washing is a particular action performed with water.

Purification concerns becoming clean or leaving an impure state.

Bathing may achieve purification, but Quranic purity has a broader scope.

Washing clothes is physical; freedom from injustice, falsehood, and betrayal is not achieved by washing clothing.

Purification should not be reduced entirely to washing, nor washing converted solely into spiritual purity.

For more detail, see Forqan’s independent study of ṭ h r.

Study of ṭ h r

Ṣād 38:42: mughtasal as water or a bathing place

Ṣād 38:42

ٱرْكُضْ بِرِجْلِكَ هَٰذَا مُغْتَسَلٌ بَارِدٌ وَشَرَابٌ

urkuḍ bi-rijlika hādhā mughtasalun bāridun wa-sharāb

Ṣād 38:42

“Strike with your foot; here is cool water for washing and drinking.”

The verse belongs to Job’s story.

The preceding verse mentions his distress and affliction.

He is then told urkuḍ bi-rijlika, and something is presented as mughtasalun bāridun wa-sharāb.

Mughtasal belongs to ightisāl’s family and may denote a bathing place or washing water.

Lexical sources record both uses.

Here bārid and sharāb indicate water or a water source for washing and drinking.

Must mughtasal name a place?

Morphologically, it can be a place noun: somewhere a person bathes.

Some Arabic forms also name the material or means of an action as well as its location.

Here mughtasal occurs together with drinking.

“Water for washing” therefore fits the context, although “bathing place” is a recognized lexical possibility.

The place-noun analysis should not be made so absolute that a washing-water or means sense becomes impossible.

Why is bārid separate?

Bārid means cool or cold.

It belongs to b r d rather than gh s l.

Coolness is not part of mughtasal’s own meaning.

People may wash with warm or cold water; the temperature is a separate feature expressed here by bārid.

Sharāb concerns drinking or drink and should not be incorporated into the lexical meaning of washing.

The phrase therefore combines washing, coolness, and drinking.

Washing and Job’s relief

The cool water follows Job’s appeal for relief from distress.

It can therefore be understood as part of the divine response.

The verse does not name his disease, identify bodily injuries, or explain the water’s composition or therapeutic mechanism.

Some historical accounts and commentaries offer extensive details about his illness, wounds, and recovery.

Such details should not be imported into the Quranic text without evidence.

The verse clearly presents something for washing and drinking in the context of Job’s affliction and the divine response.

Mughtasal cannot be translated as a specific medical treatment or a substance of some asserted composition.

5. Ghislīn: washed-away matter and food of punishment

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:36

وَلَا طَعَامٌ إِلَّا مِنْ غِسْلِينٍ

wa-lā ṭaʿāmun illā min ghislīn

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:36

“And no food except from ghislīn.”

Ghislīn names a substance in the description of food for those punished.

It is assigned to gh s l, but its use differs from washing bodies and clothes.

Lexicons connect it with matter removed during washing, such as grime, foul matter, or wound discharge.

Ghusālah also denotes water or matter left from washing.

This makes its connection to washed-away matter understandable.

The Quranic description, however, is not about ordinary washing water.

Lexical explanations of ghislīn

Several explanations are recorded.

One connects it with pus and discharge from a wound or body.

Another connects it with foul matter issuing from those undergoing punishment.

Others identify it as food of the Fire without settling its precise composition or nature.

Some lexical reports mention other explanations, such as intensely hot matter or a plant associated with punishment.

These differences show that its precise referent is not established by a single agreed explanation.

Some lexicographers analyze the form through gh s l with added yāʾ and nūn.

That analysis does not make it necessarily the name of any ordinary dirty washing water.

What the Quran states directly

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:35 describes the punished person’s lack of a close friend or supporter.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:36 identifies the food as ghislīn.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:37 says only wrongdoers eat it.

Its place within punishment and deprivation is clear.

The verses do not specify its physical constituents, production process, or chemical composition.

That absence should not be filled with extraneous stories.

Nor does a shared root establish that ghislīn, like ordinary water, is a cleansing agent.

It names food in punishment, not a washing command or a means of purification.

Why retain ghislīn in translation?

Where the precise referent is uncertain and no established equivalent exists, retaining the Arabic term with a brief explanation can prevent an unsupported interpretation from entering the translation.

Directly translating it as “pus from the bodies of Hell’s inhabitants” substitutes one lexical and exegetical explanation for the Quran’s term.

Calling it merely “washing water” also fails to convey its specialized use in the punishment context.

Retaining ghislīn with a note such as “foul, unpleasant matter described as food of punishment” is therefore preferable here.

The note does not settle its exact nature but clarifies its lexical and contextual role.

6. Comparison with related roots

Gh s l and ṭ h r: washing and purity

Ghasl concerns washing.

Ṭahārah concerns purity and purification and can apply to bodies, clothing, places, conduct, and other matters in the Quran.

Washing hands may remove soil from them.

Freedom from injustice or betrayal in human relationships is ethical and differs from bodily washing.

Al-Māʾidah 5:6 places washing and purification within one passage without merging their roots.

Washing can be a means of purification, but purification is not limited to washing.

Gh s l and m s ḥ: washing and wiping

Masḥ ordinarily concerns passing a hand or object over a surface.

Ghasl concerns washing with water.

Al-Māʾidah 5:6 uses them separately.

Passing a wet hand over a surface can be wiping.

Washing a container with water is ghasl.

The distinction does not make every wiping dry or establish a fixed form or quantity of flowing water for every washing.

The precise action requires attention to sentence structure, context, and linguistic evidence.

Gh s l and n ẓ f: washing and cleanliness

Naẓāfah concerns cleanliness in Arabic.

Washing is one action that can achieve it.

A room may be cleaned by sweeping without washing anything with water.

A person may also wash an already clean body to fulfil a religious instruction.

Cleanliness and washing are related but distinct.

N ẓ f is not attested in the Quran in its usual forms; everyday ideas of cleanliness should not manufacture a new Quranic sense for washing.

Gh s l and z k w: washing and purification with growth

Zakāh and tazkiyah concern purification, growth, and sound development in Quranic usage.

Ghasl is washing.

Inner purification can be explained through washing imagery, but the analogy does not merge the roots.

Taghtasilū directly denotes bodily washing and should not be translated as purification of the soul without evidence.

Gh s l and gh s q: independent roots

The roots look similar but differ in their third consonants.

Gh s l concerns washing.

Quranic gh s q concerns darkness and the dark part of night; ghassāq also occurs in descriptions of punishment.

Some commentaries relate ghassāq and ghislīn through foul matter or discharge associated with punishment.

Possible overlap in referents does not make the roots identical.

Ghassāq belongs to gh s q; ghislīn is assigned to gh s l.

Similar sound and related interpretations do not make them one derivative.

7. Difficulties in understanding and translation

Does washing always mean complete cleanliness?

No. Ghasl names the act of washing.

Cleanliness may result, but its extent depends on the object’s state and the washing process.

Ritual bathing may be required to leave a particular state even without visible dirt.

Washing should therefore not be restricted to removing visible grime.

Is ightisāl merely washing one body part?

It ordinarily concerns bathing.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:43 connects it to janābah and bodily washing.

The word alone does not establish the order of body parts, the number of washings, or other procedural details.

Those questions require separate evidence.

Must mughtasal be a place?

No. It can denote a bathing place or the water or means used for washing.

Bārid and sharāb in Ṣād 38:42 indicate water or a water source.

Its morphology should not be restricted to a single spatial meaning.

Does ghislīn mean cleansing water?

No. In Al-Ḥāqqah 69:36 it describes food of punishment.

If its connection with washing is accepted, lexical explanations point chiefly to matter washed away, rather than water used to cleanse.

Cleansing power should not be transferred from the root to this derivative.

Is ghislīn necessarily pus and blood from those in the Fire?

That explanation occurs in lexical and exegetical sources, but the Quran itself does not give its exact composition.

It can be presented as an early interpretation, not converted without independent evidence into a definitive physical description.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

The following equivalents are proposed for the Quranic forms.

Taghtasilū — Al-Nisāʾ 4:43: “bathe” or “wash your bodies.”

In the sentence: “until you bathe.”

Fa-ghsilū — Al-Māʾidah 5:6: “then wash.”

In the sentence: “wash your faces and your hands up to the elbows.”

Mughtasalun — Ṣād 38:42: “water for washing” or “a bathing place,” according to interpretation.

“Here is cool water for washing and drinking” is a natural rendering in this context, fitting bārid and sharāb.

Ghislīnin — Al-Ḥāqqah 69:36: “ghislīn.”

The basic translation can retain the term, with a note explaining its lexical connection to foul washings and its Quranic role as food of punishment.

For non-Quranic uses:

Ghasala: washed.

Ghasl: washing.

Ghusl: bathing or bodily washing.

Ightasala: bathed or washed oneself.

Ghassala: washed, sometimes emphasizing repetition or extent.

Ghasīl and maghsūl: washed.

Ghasūl: a washing substance or means.

Ghusālah: water or matter left from washing.

Maghsal: a washing place or implement.

Mughtasal: a place, water, or means for washing, according to usage.

Translation should distinguish the action, its agent, the object washed, the means and place, and the residual matter.

Conclusion

Arabic gh s l concerns washing with water, with evidence in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

The verb describes washing a horse in Imruʾ al-Qays’s Muʿallaqah, showing a familiar everyday action independently of its Quranic ritual uses.

The family provides forms for washing, bathing, implements and locations, washed objects, and residual matter.

There are four Quranic occurrences.

Taghtasilū in Al-Nisāʾ 4:43 concerns bathing in relation to janābah.

Fa-ghsilū in Al-Māʾidah 5:6 commands washing faces and hands.

Mughtasal in Ṣād 38:42 concerns washing water or a bathing place in Job’s story.

Ghislīn in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:36 names a substance describing food of punishment. Lexicons relate it to foul matter washed away, but its precise nature is not specified in the Quran.

The principal distinction from ṭ h r is that ghasl denotes washing itself, whereas purity and purification are broader. Washing may achieve purification without exhausting its meaning.

Washing also differs from wiping, cleanliness, and tazkiyah, although purposes or results may overlap.

The semantic core of gh s l is washing something with water. Its derivatives can denote bathing, washing means and locations, washed objects, or matter washed away.

This core should be preserved in Quranic analysis without assigning complete purity, medical treatment, a specific ritual procedure, or ghislīn’s physical nature to the root without independent evidence.
