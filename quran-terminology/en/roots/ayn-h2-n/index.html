Core meaning: wool, especially dyed wool; a fibrous material that may occur in masses, strands, or scattered fragments. Other branches of this family include slackness, bending, and certain senses of presence and availability.

The Arabic root ʿ H N is best known through ʿihn, a name for wool used especially for dyed wool in many early contexts.

Some dictionaries apply ʿihn to any wool, while some lexicographers restrict it to wool that has been dyed.

This difference matters for Quranic usage: in describing the transformation of mountains on the Day of Resurrection, the Quran compares them to ʿihn. One verse gives this comparison alone; another adds the adjective manfūsh.

ʿIhn identifies the material to which mountains are compared, while manfūsh describes its opened and separated fibers.

The root occurs only twice in the Quran, both times as al-ʿihn in Al-Maarij 70:9 and Al-Qariah 101:5.

1. The root in early Arabic

ʿIhn: wool and dyed wool

ʿIhn names wool in Arabic. A particularly familiar use refers to wool dyed in different colors.

For example, wool shorn from sheep can be cleaned, dyed, and used for yarn, cloth, coverings, or ornaments.

Bunches of wool dyed red, yellow, or other colors may be called ʿihn.

But is every kind of wool ʿihn?

Early lexicographical sources differ on this question.

In explaining the word, Al-Khalil ibn Ahmad first identifies dyed wool and also reports its use for wool generally.

Within the same lexical tradition, Arram is reported to restrict ʿihn to dyed wool.

Al-Asmai, by contrast, accepts its use for wool whether dyed or undyed.

Two points therefore emerge:

First, “dyed wool” has strong support in Arabic usage.

Second, restricting ʿihn to dyed wool is not a unanimous position among lexicographers.

A definition should preserve both recorded uses and explain the more specific sense where color matters.

A witness from pre-Islamic poetry

One of the clearest early poetic witnesses occurs in the Muallaqah attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abi Sulma:

كَأَنَّ فُتَاتَ الْعِهْنِ فِي كُلِّ مَنْزِلٍ
نَزَلْنَ بِهِ حَبُّ الْفَنَا لَمْ يُحَطَّمِ

ka-anna futāta al-ʿihni fī kulli manzilin / nazalna bihi ḥabbu al-fanā lam yuḥaṭṭami

“As though the fragments of dyed wool at every stopping place where they alighted were the red berries of the fanā plant, still unbroken.”

The poet describes a caravan of women traveling on camels.

The caravan's furnishings and coverings are ornamented with colored wool. During movement and stops, small pieces may detach and fall to the ground.

The poet compares these colored fragments to the berries of a plant called fanā, described in early explanations as bearing red berries.

The wool's redness and color matter to this image: without a definite color, the comparison with red berries would be less clear.

This witness thus illustrates the use of ʿihn for dyed wool in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

There is another point as well.

Futāt in this verse means fragments or detached pieces.

Fragmentation and scattering are therefore conveyed by futāt, rather than by ʿihn itself.

ʿIhn identifies the material, wool; futāt describes its fragments.

This distinction also matters for understanding the Quranic phrase al-ʿihn al-manfūsh.

Zuhayr is a recognized pre-Islamic poet, and the verse is transmitted in his famous Muallaqah. Attributing the poem to a pre-Islamic poet and dating this particular verse precisely are nevertheless distinct claims.

Transmitted variants include fī kulli manzilin nazalna bihi and fī kulli mawqifin waqafna bihi. Some versions also have ḥutāt instead of futāt.

These variants do not change the witness's central point: fragments of dyed wool are compared to red berries.

The material image of ʿihn

Wool consists of many fibers.

They can be compressed together, joined, made into yarn, or opened and separated.

Imagine wool stored in a compact mass after being shorn from a sheep.

If opened by hand or with a tool, its fibers spread and the mass looks fluffier and bulkier.

The quantity of material need not have increased; only the arrangement of its fibers has changed.

Wool may also have different colors, either naturally or through dyeing after shearing.

Dyed wool matters to the specific use of ʿihn, but the word alone does not establish that it has been spun, woven, or carded.

These are distinct states and processes.

Other uses of the ʿ H N family

Arabic dictionaries record several further uses alongside the name for wool.

ʿIhnah denotes a piece or bunch of wool, and ʿuhūn is recorded as the plural of ʿihn.

In another use, ʿuhnah denotes a break in a branch that has not completely separated from the tree.

For example, pressure may break a branch while some bark or fibers still keep it attached.

It may look intact from a distance, yet bend and hang at the break when moved.

Here ʿuhnah refers to that break without complete separation.

In this field, ʿāhin is recorded for something hanging slackly and, of a person, for someone sluggish or lazy.

In another use, however, ʿāhin means present, ready, or available.

For example, mālun ʿāhin denotes wealth presently available, in contrast to property not yet obtained or due in the future.

ʿAwāhin is also used in certain Arabic dialects for particular palm fronds, especially those near the palm's young central heart.

These uses show that the lexical family is not restricted to wool.

They cannot, however, all be reduced to a single concept such as softness or slackness.

Wool may be soft and a broken branch may hang slackly, but the availability of wealth is not in itself softness or breakage.

Nor does their recording in later dictionaries prove that all these meanings occurred in precisely the same forms and range before Islam.

Only ʿihn, meaning wool, occurs in the Quran.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important Arabic forms include:

ʿIhn: wool, especially dyed wool.

ʿIhnah: a piece or bunch of wool.

ʿUhūn: plural of ʿihn; wool or bunches of wool.

ʿUhnah: a break in a branch without complete separation.

ʿAhana: in certain lexical uses, remained in a place or was present and established; of a branch, suffered a break.

ʿĀhin: in one use, present and available; in another, hanging slackly or a sluggish person.

ʿAwāhin: particular palm fronds; in some uses, the sides or lateral parts of things.

ʿIhnah: also recorded as the name of a plant with a red flower.

Of these forms, only ʿihn occurs in the Quran.

The complete Quranic expression in both verses is ka-l-ʿihni.

Ka- marks comparison; al-ʿihni is a definite noun in the genitive after it.

The comparison particle and definite article do not create a new derivative of the root.

No verb, active participle, or plural from this root occurs in the Quran.

3. First Quranic use: mountains like wool

Al-Maarij 70:9

وَتَكُونُ الْجِبَالُ كَالْعِهْنِ

wa-takūnu al-jibālu ka-l-ʿihn

Al-Maʿārij 70:9

“And the mountains will be like wool.”

Al-Maarij 70:9

Al-ʿihn here refers to wool.

Ka- marks comparison, showing that mountains are likened to wool.

The Quran is not saying that mountains were originally made of wool; it describes their transformed condition in a particular scene.

The difference between mountains and wool matters to this image.

In ordinary human experience, a mountain is a large, hard mass of rock and other earth materials.

Wool, however, is fibrous material that can be opened, scattered, and reshaped under pressure or movement.

Comparing great, hard mountains to wool portrays an astonishing transformation in their condition.

The surah's context

Al-Maarij 70:8 describes a day when the sky will be like muhl.

Al-Maarij 70:9 then compares mountains to wool.

Al-Maarij 70:10 describes a situation in which one close friend does not ask about another.

Together, these images depict a day when the familiar condition of the world and human relationships changes.

In this context, the wool comparison forms part of an immense transformation of the world.

The word ʿihn itself does not mean resurrection, destruction, or collapse.

Those meanings emerge from the context and related verses about that day.

Does ʿihn here necessarily mean carded wool?

Al-Maarij 70:9 contains only ka-l-ʿihni.

The adjective manfūsh does not appear in this verse.

The direct rendering “like wool” therefore respects its wording.

Al-Qariah 101:5, however, gives the same comparison with manfūsh.

This second verse can illuminate the image in Al-Maarij, but its adjective should not be silently inserted into the literal translation of the other verse.

In Al-Maarij 70:9, ʿihn itself means wool; the opened condition is explained when examining the other verse.

Color in this comparison

A familiar Arabic use of ʿihn also denotes dyed wool.

Mountains naturally have different colors.

Fatir 35:27 mentions white and red streaks and differing colors in mountains.

Their varied colors may therefore be one aspect of the comparison with dyed wool.

Imagine bunches of red, white, and dark wool placed together, resembling different parts of a mountain landscape in color.

This resemblance should not become a categorical claim about the color of every mountain on the Day of Resurrection.

ʿIhn means wool, with dyed wool a recognized use. How strongly color features in each verse's image requires consideration of the evidence and context together.

4. Second Quranic use: opened wool

Al-Qariah 101:5

وَتَكُونُ الْجِبَالُ كَالْعِهْنِ الْمَنْفُوشِ

wa-takūnu al-jibālu ka-l-ʿihni al-manfūsh

Al-Qariah 101:5

“And the mountains will be like carded wool.”

Al-Qariah 101:5

This verse repeats the comparison in Al-Maarij 70:9, with one significant difference:

Al-manfūsh is added.

ʿIhn identifies wool, and manfūsh specifies its condition.

Manfūsh belongs to the independent root N F SH and describes something whose fibers or parts have been opened and separated.

With wool, nafsh may be performed by hand or with a tool to separate compacted fibers.

A mass of wool may initially be compressed. Opening its fibers by hand makes the same wool look more spread out, fluffy, and light.

This is the image created by al-manfūsh alongside al-ʿihn.

The two words' distinct meanings

ʿIhn identifies the material.

Manfūsh describes its opened condition.

The distinction resembles the expression “carded cotton.”

Cotton names the material, while carded describes its condition.

Saying only “cotton” does not tell us whether its fibers are compressed or opened.

Likewise, saying only “carded,” without its object, does not establish whether wool or cotton is meant.

Each word in the Quranic expression thus has an independent role.

This matters because ʿihn does not itself mean scattered, fragmented, or destroyed.

Wool can be compressed, spun, woven, or carded.

Of these conditions, this verse expressly selects its being manfūsh.

The separate study of N F SH discusses opening fibers, fluffing, and scattering in greater detail.

Study of N F SH

Why compare mountains to carded wool?

Mountains ordinarily appear large, heavy, and hard.

Carded wool has lost its previous compactness: its fibers are opened, producing a more dispersed mass.

The verse places these two images against each other.

A mountain, ordinarily a symbol of greatness and stability, is likened to something with an open and scattered structure.

The comparison portrays a tremendous change in the mountains' condition.

The comparison alone does not establish a literal conversion of rock into animal fibers.

The Quran uses a simile, comparing the mountains' condition in some respect to that of carded wool.

The simile alone also cannot determine particle size, material composition, or the physical mechanism of the mountains' transformation.

Other verses about the transformation of mountains

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:5 speaks of mountains being crushed.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:6 portrays the result as scattered dust.

Ta Ha 20:105 speaks of the mountains' nasf: uprooting and scattering them.

Ta Ha 20:106 describes the land being left level after this change.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:20 describes mountains set in motion and becoming like a mirage.

Alongside Al-Maarij 70:9 and Al-Qariah 101:5, these verses form a network of images concerning the mountains' transformation.

One describes their crushing, another their scattering and movement; Al-Qariah compares them to carded wool.

Each root nevertheless retains its own meaning.

ʿIhn names wool.

Nafsh concerns opening and separating fibers.

Bass and nasf in other verses describe different aspects of the mountains' transformation.

All these meanings should not be attributed to ʿ H N itself.

The scattering of people

Immediately before the mountain comparison, Al-Qariah 101:4 likens people to farāsh mabthūth: scattered winged creatures.

In Al-Qariah 101:5, mountains become like carded wool.

The two verses offer different images.

First, people moving like scattered creatures.

Second, mountains described as opened wool.

Mabthūth belongs to B TH TH, and manfūsh to N F SH.

They overlap in dispersion, but their kinds of scattering differ.

In the first image, creatures scatter in different directions.

In the second, the fibers of a previously compact material open apart.

This distinction means that the verses do not simply repeat each other, despite their related imagery.

5. Comparison with neighboring roots

ʿ H N (ʿihn) and Ṣ W F (ṣūf)

Ṣūf is the general Arabic name for wool.

ʿIhn also denotes wool, but one familiar early use specifies dyed wool.

Freshly shorn sheep's wool, for example, may not yet have been dyed or prepared for spinning. Such material is called ṣūf.

If that wool is dyed in different colors, its description as ʿihn becomes particularly clear.

Not all lexicographers, however, restrict ʿihn to dyed wool.

The distinction should therefore not become an absolute rule that ṣūf is always undyed and ʿihn always dyed.

Ṣūf broadly names the material, while a recognized use of ʿihn identifies it with the feature of being dyed.

Al-Naḥl 16:80 uses aṣwāfihā for livestock wool.

Al-Maarij 70:9 and Al-Qariah 101:5 instead use ʿihn in comparing mountains to wool.

This lexical choice merits study, but does not alone establish completely separate, non-overlapping Quranic meanings for the two words.

ʿ H N (ʿihn) and N F SH (nafsh)

ʿIhn names a material, whereas nafsh concerns opening and separating fibers.

Wool can undergo nafsh, but wool itself does not mean nafsh.

Wool kept compactly in a bag, for example, remains wool.

When that wool is opened and carded, it becomes manfūsh.

Al-Qariah 101:5 expresses these two meanings with two independent words.

Scattering should therefore not be made an invariable part of ʿihn's meaning.

ʿ H N (ʿihn) and F T T (futāt)

Fatta concerns crumbling or breaking something into small pieces.

Futāt denotes the resulting fragments.

In Zuhayr ibn Abi Sulma's verse, futātu al-ʿihn refers to pieces of dyed wool.

The expression combines two meanings.

Futāt identifies the pieces; ʿihn identifies their material.

This verse therefore does not establish that ʿihn itself means scattered fragments.

ʿ H N (ʿihn) and B S S (bass)

Applied to mountains, bass concerns crushing and breaking them up.

ʿIhn, however, is wool.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:5 describes crushing mountains; Al-Qariah 101:5 uses the image of carded wool.

These images may concern one tremendous transformation, but their lexical meanings differ.

Crushing describes an action upon something; carded wool supplies an image of its condition and appearance.

ʿ H N (ʿihn) and H B ʾ (habāʾ)

Habāʾ concerns very fine, dispersed particles, such as dust visible in the air.

ʿIhn names wool.

Carded wool may look scattered and light from a distance, but wool fibers and dust particles are different materials.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:6 describes the mountains as becoming scattered dust.

Al-Qariah 101:5 uses carded wool as its image.

The expressions overlap in dispersion but present different images; one name should not replace the other in translation.

6. Proposed translations for Forqan

Ka-l-ʿihni — Al-Maarij 70:9

Root: ʿ H N.

Word type: definite noun, genitive after the comparison particle ka-.

Brief root meaning: wool, especially dyed wool known in Arabic for ornaments, fabrics, and other fibrous applications.

Meaning in this verse: wool, to which mountains are compared on the Day of Resurrection.

Proposed rendering: “like wool.”

Rendering the phrase:

وَتَكُونُ الْجِبَالُ كَالْعِهْنِ

wa-takūnu al-jibālu ka-l-ʿihn

Al-Maʿārij 70:9

“And the mountains will be like wool.”

This rendering preserves the verse's explicit comparison and the word's general sense.

“Like colored wool” also fits a familiar use of ʿihn. Since its broader application to wool is recorded too, the dyed sense can be explained in the lexical discussion.

Manfūsh is absent here, so “carded” should not be added without explanation to the direct translation.

Ka-l-ʿihni — Al-Qariah 101:5

Root: ʿ H N.

Word type: definite noun, genitive after the comparison particle ka-.

Brief root meaning: wool, especially dyed wool, with a broader use for wool generally.

Meaning in this verse: wool described as al-manfūsh, to which mountains are compared.

Proposed rendering of the noun itself: “wool.”

Rendering the phrase:

وَتَكُونُ الْجِبَالُ كَالْعِهْنِ الْمَنْفُوشِ

wa-takūnu al-jibālu ka-l-ʿihni al-manfūsh

Al-Qariah 101:5

“And the mountains will be like carded wool.”

This rendering distinguishes the material from its stated condition.

“Carded” here renders al-manfūsh and is not part of ʿihn's independent meaning.

A fuller explanation could be: “Dyed wool whose fibers have been opened apart.”

This is a lexical and pictorial explanation; every element need not be inserted into the verse's short translation.

7. Conclusion

The Arabic root ʿ H N is best known as the name of wool.

In an important early use, ʿihn denotes dyed wool: the verse attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abi Sulma compares its colored fragments with red berries.

Some lexicographers, however, accept ʿihn for any wool, dyed or undyed.

Its basic meaning is therefore wool, with dyeing important to one recognized use.

Other branches record words for breakage without complete separation, slackness and hanging, and presence or availability. These should not be reduced without evidence to one certain historical core.

ʿIhn occurs only twice in the Quran, both times concerning mountains on the Day of Resurrection.

Al-Maarij 70:9 compares mountains to wool.

Al-Qariah 101:5 clarifies the image by adding al-manfūsh, portraying wool with opened fibers.

Wool thus comes from ʿihn, and carding or opening from manfūsh.

The comparison alone does not establish literal conversion of rock into animal fibers. The verse portrays the mountains' transformed condition, which is examined alongside related resurrection verses.

Final definition of ʿ H N:

“In its principal Quranic branch, wool, especially dyed wool, although Arabic also records the word for wool generally. ʿIhn names the material; fragmentation, scattering, and carding are conveyed by accompanying words. The Quran compares mountains to this material, and in Al-Qariah 101:5, manfūsh specifies its opened fibers.”
