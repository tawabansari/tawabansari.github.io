Qur’anic Root A R K

Arīkah and arāʾik: adorned couches, ease, and a place to rest

Qur’anic and lexical forms

الْأَرَائِكِ
(al-arāʾiki)
The only Qur’anic form: a definite genitive broken plural, five times after ʿalā

أَرِيكَة
(arīkah)
Lexical singular: an adorned couch or bed; this form is absent from the Qur’an

أَرِيك
(arīk)
Another collective/plural form in lexicons; not Qur’anic

أَرَاك
(arāk)
Lexical name of the arāk tree; absent from the Qur’an

أَرَاكَة
(arākah)
One arāk tree in lexical usage; not Qur’anic

أَرِكَة
(arikah)
Lexical description of land abundant in arāk; not Qur’anic

آرِكَة / أَوَارِك
(ārikah / awārik)
Lexical descriptions of camels grazing or remaining in a pasture; not Qur’anic

أَرَكَ / أُرُوك
(araka / urūk)
Lexical verb and verbal noun for remaining or residing; not Qur’anic

أَرَكَ الْجُرْحُ
(araka al-jurḥu)

The root A R K occurs in the Qur’an only as al-arāʾik, the plural of arīkah: an adorned couch or bed prepared for sitting and reclining. In early Arabic, the word frequently described a couch within a curtained enclosure or furnished with bedding and coverings.

All five Qur’anic occurrences concern blessings in the Hereafter. In three verses, the people of the Garden recline on arāʾik; in the other two, they are upon them, looking. The word thus forms part of the description of the righteous enjoying ease, provision, and an honored position.

In Arabic, however, A R K extends beyond couches and beds. Arāk names a familiar tree, while araka bi-al-makāni denotes remaining or residing somewhere. Other recorded uses concern camels, plants, and wounds healing.

Understanding arāʾik requires first establishing the noun’s own meaning and Qur’anic usage, then examining possible relationships with the root’s other branches, rather than merging every meaning into a hypothetical image from the outset.

1. Qur’anic forms and the semantic core

A R K occurs five times in the Qur’an, always in the single form al-arāʾiki:

  • Al-Kahf 18:31
  • Yā Sīn 36:56
  • Al-Insān 76:13
  • Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:23
  • Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:35

Its singular is arīkah and its familiar plural arāʾik. Another plural, arīk, is recorded in lexicons but does not occur in the Qur’an.

In every Qur’anic occurrence, al-arāʾiki is a definite broken plural in the genitive after ʿalā. This construction locates people upon those couches or beds.

In Arabic, arīkah usually denotes a couch prepared for sitting and resting. Some early descriptions also include a covering, curtain, or enclosure among its features.

The Qur’anic form’s semantic core can therefore be expressed as:

An adorned couch or bed prepared for sitting, resting, and reclining, often with a covering or special furnishing.

Ease and enjoyment naturally relate to this furnishing, but the word itself does not directly mean happiness, victory, reward, or royal status. Those meanings may arise from the verses’ context, rather than the lexical form arīkah alone.

2. Early Arabic: the arāk tree, residence, and an adorned couch

Arāk: a familiar tree in Arab life

One clear branch of the family is arāk, a tree whose branches and roots were used to make tooth-cleaning sticks, or miswāk.

The tree was familiar in the Arab environment, and camels ate its leaves and branches. Related vocabulary consequently describes arāk-covered land and camels grazing on the plant.

Arākah denotes one arāk tree, while arikah can describe land containing abundant arāk. Ārikah and awārik are also recorded for camels grazing or remaining in pastures associated with the plant.

Some lexicons even record arāʾik as a plural of arākah. This rare usage is nevertheless distinct from Qur’anic arāʾik in the setting of couches and reclining. A word’s form alone is insufficient: construction and context establish its meaning.

Although the tree name arāk belongs to A R K, it does not occur in the Qur’an.

Araka bi-al-makāni: remaining and residing

Another important branch is araka, used in some Arabic expressions for remaining or residing in a place.

Araka bi-al-makāni is recorded for residing somewhere without departing. Its verbal noun is urūk.

This use is not confined to camels or pastures: it also describes people remaining somewhere. Araka fī al-amri is additionally recorded for persisting or insisting in a matter.

Two lexical explanations overlap in the camel-related uses: some forms are connected with eating arāk, others with camels remaining in a pasture for a long time. These behaviors could coincide in pastoral life without necessarily having a single semantic origin.

Araka al-jurḥu: swelling subsiding and a wound healing

Arabic also records araka al-jurḥu for a wound or injury beginning to heal as its swelling subsides.

The expression concerns a wound whose inflammation has settled and which has reached the stage of repair.

This can conceptually be related to subsiding and becoming settled. Such an explanation should nevertheless not become a supposedly certain history of the whole family. Nor does a wound-related use make arīkah in the Qur’an a therapeutic device or a sign of bodily recovery.

This form does not occur in the Qur’an. Its value here lies in showing the lexical family’s range.

Arīkah: a couch in a furnished, curtained space

Early descriptions often place an arīkah within a ḥajalah.

Ḥajalah here denotes a curtained space or enclosure separated from its surroundings by curtains and coverings. In established definitions, arīkah thus means more than a surface to sit on: it can be a couch within an adorned, covered space prepared for rest.

Some lexicographers require a couch within a curtained enclosure for the term arīkah. Others give it a broader range, applying it to a couch, bed, or furnishing upon which someone reclines.

This difference should be preserved. It cannot be asserted that every arīkah in every Arabic use necessarily had a separate curtain and canopy. Conversely, removing every sense of furnishing and prepared bedding makes the definition overly general.

Dhū al-Rummah, an Umayyad-era poet, writes:

خُدُودًا جَفَتْ فِي السَّيْرِ حَتَّىٰ كَأَنَّمَا
يُبَاشِرْنَ بِالْمَعْزَاءِ مَسَّ الْأَرَائِكِ

khudūdan jafat fī al-sayri ḥattā ka-annamā / yubāshirna bi-al-maʿzāʾi massa al-arāʾiki

“Cheeks that had found no rest on the journey, as though upon the stony ground they felt the softness of adorned couches.”

The poet compares cheeks touching hard, stony ground with their touching restful bedding. The contrast shows that arāʾik evoked softness, ease, and prepared bedding for his audience. The image depicts travelers so tired that stony ground feels like a soft bed. Jafat fī al-sayri here is explained as finding no rest during the journey, rather than the skin of their cheeks drying out.

This is a witness from early Islamic Arabic, rather than one whose composition can be assigned to the pre-Islamic period. Establishing that every feature of the word predates the Qur’an requires independent historical evidence.

Is arīkah derived from residence or the arāk tree?

Lexicons propose two explanations for the name arīkah.

One connects it with urūk, residence, because a couch is a place for remaining and resting.

The other derives it from arāk, on the suggestion that such couches or beds may once have been made from that tree’s wood.

Neither should be treated as the certain origin of the name without independent historical evidence. The relationship between residence and a resting place is intelligible, but an intelligible connection is distinct from historical proof.

The tree’s existence and the possibility of its wood being used likewise do not establish that the Qur’an’s heavenly couches are made of that wood.

Understanding the verses does not require settling this historical question. Arīkah is an established Arabic term for an adorned couch or bed, and the Qur’anic context supports that use.

3. Qur’anic arāʾik: couches of ease and enjoyment

All five Qur’anic occurrences describe blessings in the Hereafter, although each verse places the word alongside different elements.

Al-Kahf 18:31: adorned couches among the Garden’s blessings

أُولَٰئِكَ لَهُمْ جَنَّاتُ عَدْنٍ تَجْرِي مِنْ تَحْتِهِمُ الْأَنْهَارُ يُحَلَّوْنَ فِيهَا مِنْ أَسَاوِرَ مِنْ ذَهَبٍ وَيَلْبَسُونَ ثِيَابًا خُضْرًا مِنْ سُنْدُسٍ وَإِسْتَبْرَقٍ مُتَّكِئِينَ فِيهَا عَلَى الْأَرَائِكِ نِعْمَ الثَّوَابُ وَحَسُنَتْ مُرْتَفَقًا

ulāʾika lahum jannātu ʿadnin tajrī min taḥtihimu al-anhāru yuḥallawna fīhā min asāwira min dhahabin wa-yalbasūna thiyāban khuḍran min sundusin wa-istabraqin muttakiʾīna fīhā ʿalā al-arāʾiki niʿma al-thawābu wa-ḥasunat murtafaqā

“They will have gardens of abiding residence, with rivers flowing beneath them. There they are adorned with bracelets of gold and wear green garments of fine silk and brocade, reclining upon adorned couches. How excellent the reward, and how fine a resting place!”

Al-Kahf 18:31

Here, arāʾik accompanies several other blessings: gardens, rivers, golden ornaments, and fine clothing.

Muttakiʾīna describes the state of those upon the couches. It belongs to another root, W K A, concerning leaning and support. Reclining therefore comes from muttakiʾīna, rather than from arāʾik itself.

Study of W K A

The distinction matters. Arāʾik names the place or furnishing, while muttakiʾīna describes the people’s posture.

The closing niʿma al-thawābu wa-ḥasunat murtafaqan praises the reward and resting place. The whole verse makes the adorned couches part of the image of enjoyment and rest.

Arāʾik itself, however, does not mean the reward for deeds. Their being a reward follows from the context and closing statement.

Yā Sīn 36:56: ease with spouses and shade

هُمْ وَأَزْوَاجُهُمْ فِي ظِلَالٍ عَلَى الْأَرَائِكِ مُتَّكِئُونَ

hum wa-azwājuhum fī ẓilālin ʿalā al-arāʾiki muttakiʾūn

“They and their spouses are in shade, reclining upon adorned couches.”

Yā Sīn 36:56

Here, arāʾik accompanies azwājuhum, “their spouses,” and ẓilāl, “shade.”

People rest upon adorned couches with their spouses in a shaded setting. Spouses and shade are independent elements of the description, rather than components of arāʾik’s meaning.

The verse also shows that the couches are not presented solely as seats of rule or royal thrones. People recline on them alongside their spouses, in a state associated with ease and enjoyment.

This matters for translation. Rendering arāʾik simply as royal thrones may add a picture of political rule that neither the word nor the context requires.

“Adorned couches” is clearer, preserving the furnishing without imposing kingship.

Al-Insān 76:13: adorned couches free of distressing heat and cold

مُتَّكِئِينَ فِيهَا عَلَى الْأَرَائِكِ لَا يَرَوْنَ فِيهَا شَمْسًا وَلَا زَمْهَرِيرًا

muttakiʾīna fīhā ʿalā al-arāʾiki lā yarawna fīhā shamsan wa-lā zamharīrā

“Reclining there upon adorned couches, they see neither sun nor severe cold there.”

Al-Insān 76:13

The couches here appear alongside the absence of sun and severe cold.

Zamharīr denotes intense cold and stands opposite shamsan. Their juxtaposition depicts a comfortable setting free of distressing conditions.

The absence of sun and severe cold should nevertheless not be incorporated into the definition of arāʾik. The verse does not say that the couches themselves prevent cold or sunlight. These are environmental features described alongside them.

In all three passages, Al-Kahf, Yā Sīn, and Al-Insān, arāʾik names the adorned couch or bed, while muttakiʾīna or muttakiʾūna specifies the restful posture of its occupants.

4. ʿAlā al-arāʾiki yanẓurūn: one expression in two settings

Al-Muṭaffifīn repeats ʿalā al-arāʾiki yanẓurūn twice.

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:23: the righteous enjoying blessings

عَلَى الْأَرَائِكِ يَنْظُرُونَ

ʿalā al-arāʾiki yanẓurūn

“Upon adorned couches, they look.”

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:23

This follows the declaration that the righteous enjoy blessings. ʿAlā al-arāʾiki locates them, while yanẓurūna expresses looking.

Unlike the preceding three occurrences, this verse contains no expression of reclining. A direct translation should therefore neither turn yanẓurūna into “they recline” nor omit looking.

The absence of an expression for reclining does not deny that posture. It simply shows that looking is the sentence’s focus.

The object of yanẓurūna is not stated in the verse. What exactly they look at cannot therefore be settled from this sentence alone. Context can clarify the setting, but an unstated object should not be inserted unmarked into the translation.

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:35: the same couches in another scene

عَلَى الْأَرَائِكِ يَنْظُرُونَ

ʿalā al-arāʾiki yanẓurūn

“Upon adorned couches, they look.”

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:35

The same expression recurs, but its context differs.

The preceding verses describe people who mocked believers and laughed at them in worldly life. In the Hereafter, positions are reversed, and the believers are described enjoying blessings and looking.

This context gives the repeated expression a wider significance. Arāʾik still names adorned couches, but the believers’ presence upon them now contributes to the contrast between worldly life and the Hereafter.

The two passages show how a word with a stable lexical meaning can serve different contextual roles within one surah.

The first describes the righteous enjoying blessings; the second repeats that description in the reversal of positions and the outcome of worldly conduct.

Arāʾik’s meaning has not changed. The people’s situation and the verse’s relationship to its surroundings have gained greater depth.

5. Distinguishing arāʾik from surur, furush, ʿarsh, and kursī

The Qur’an uses different words for places of sitting, resting, or rule. Some overlap, but they are not necessarily exact synonyms.

A R K (arāʾik) and S R R (surur)

Study of S R R

Sarīr and its plural surur denote a couch or place for sitting and resting.

Surur also describes heavenly couches, as in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:15 and Al-Ṭūr 52:20. Some verses qualify these couches as adorned, arranged, or facing one another.

Arīkah is lexically close to sarīr, but established Arabic descriptions generally make it a furnished couch or bed, sometimes within curtains.

Sarīr can therefore be considered the more general term, while familiar uses of arīkah evoke additional features of prepared, adorned bedding.

This should not become an absolute rule: lexicons record broader uses of arīkah, and some Qur’anic surur are likewise described as fine and adorned.

A R K (arāʾik) and F R SH (furush)

Study of F R SH

Firāsh and its plural furush concern something spread and prepared for sitting or resting.

Al-Raḥmān 55:54 describes the Garden’s people upon beds whose linings are brocade. Their comfort and furnishing relate to arāʾik, but the words approach the subject from different angles.

Furush can foreground bedding and coverings, while familiar uses of arāʾik name the couch or furnished resting arrangement as a whole.

This distinction is relative, too. Some early definitions apply arīkah to the bedding within a curtained enclosure. Couches and bedding should therefore not be treated as wholly separate objects in every Arabic use.

A R K (arāʾik) and ʿ R SH (ʿarsh)

Study of ʿ R SH

ʿArsh can denote a seat of rule or royal throne, such as the Queen of Sabaʾ’s throne in Al-Naml 27:23.

This differs significantly from arāʾik: ʿarsh in a royal setting concerns power and authority, while Qur’anic arāʾik describes heavenly ease and enjoyment.

Not all uses of ʿarsh are restricted to a human throne, however. Its application to The God requires a separate, contextual examination.

Conversely, the adornment and honored occupants of arāʾik do not by themselves make it equivalent to a royal ʿarsh.

A R K (arāʾik) and K R S (kursī)

Study of K R S

Kursī concerns a seat in Arabic. Al-Baqarah 2:255 uses it in the context of divine greatness and dominion.

Qur’anic arāʾik, however, concerns the couches of the Garden’s people at ease. Both vocabulary and context distinguish these uses.

The form kursī and the theological meaning of its verse should not be imported into the definition of arāʾik.

“Couch,” “bed,” “seat,” or “throne” may consequently overlap in translation, but a precise rendering and explanation must preserve each word’s place, use, and description.

6. Rest and ease: lexical meaning or contextual implication?

All five occurrences associate arāʾik with blessings and enjoyment. The repetition establishes its place within the Qur’anic image of the Garden as a furnishing for the righteous to rest and enjoy its blessings.

Three levels must nevertheless be distinguished.

First is arāʾik’s direct meaning: adorned couches or beds.

Second is its use in a sentence: people recline upon these couches or look from them.

Third is what emerges from the whole context: ease, enjoyment, peace, and the honored position of the Garden’s people.

The three are related but distinct.

Translating arāʾik simply as “peace” removes a particular object named by the Qur’an. Calling it “royal thrones” may impose an excessive sense of power and rule.

Likewise, the presence of arāʾik in the Garden does not establish their exact construction, wood, dimensions, curtain installation, or the precise posture of those upon them. Such conclusions exceed the word’s indication.

The Qur’an offers an intelligible image of blessing and ease. This does not deny the reality of the promise concerning the Hereafter, but neither does it authorize filling unknown details with confident speculation.

The possible relationship between arīkah and urūk, residence, can make the connection between a couch and resting linguistically intelligible. It does not make “eternal residence” the primary Qur’anic meaning of arāʾik. The permanence of blessings and the setting of the Hereafter are established through other verses and the Garden’s context, rather than an unproven etymological relationship.

7. Proposed translations for Forqan

The principal proposed Persian equivalent for al-arāʾik is takht-hā-ye ārāsteh, “adorned couches.”

It preserves the plural, names a furnishing for sitting and resting, and avoids restricting the word either to a royal throne or to a modern sofa.

“Adorned beds” is also defensible in some settings, especially given early definitions connecting arīkah with bedding within curtains. “Adorned couches” nevertheless provides a more consistent rendering across the five Qur’anic verses.

In Al-Kahf 18:31, “reclining upon adorned couches” preserves the separate contributions of arāʾik and muttakiʾīna.

In Yā Sīn 36:56, “in shade, reclining upon adorned couches” is likewise natural.

Al-Insān 76:13 should retain the same equivalent: the following absence of sun and severe cold concerns the heavenly environment and does not require changing arāʾik’s meaning.

In Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:23 and Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:35, “Upon adorned couches, they look” is appropriate. Adding “reclining” to a direct translation is unnecessary, because the verb present is yanẓurūna.

“Curtained couches” can appear in lexical explanation, but requiring it in every Qur’anic translation is uncertain. “Royal thrones,” “seats of dominion,” and “eternal stations” are likewise unsuitable direct equivalents for the word itself.

8. Conclusion

A R K has several established Arabic branches. Arāk names a familiar tree, while araka bi-al-makāni concerns remaining and residing somewhere. Other uses concern persistence, swelling subsiding, and wounds healing.

Arīkah also names an adorned couch or bed, frequently associated in familiar early uses with curtains and prepared bedding. Its historical relationship with the tree or the verb of residence remains uncertain; imagery must not substitute for evidence.

The Qur’an uses only al-arāʾiki from this whole family. It occurs five times, always denoting the adorned couches of those enjoying blessings in the Hereafter.

In Al-Kahf 18:31, Yā Sīn 36:56, and Al-Insān 76:13, people recline on these couches. In Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:23 and Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:35, they are upon them and look.

Ease, enjoyment, and the Garden’s people occupying an honored position emerge from the combined descriptions. Arāʾik itself nevertheless names a furnishing, rather than peace or reward.

Forqan’s final definition of A R K:

“A lexical family with established branches concerning the arāk tree and remaining or residing somewhere, as well as arīkah: an adorned couch or bed, often with a covering or curtained enclosure. The Qur’an uses only the plural arāʾik, denoting the adorned couches upon which the Garden’s people recline or from which they look. Ease and enjoyment emerge from the verses’ context, rather than the word’s lexical meaning alone.”
