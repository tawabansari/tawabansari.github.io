Hastening, swift movement, and advancing toward a destination

In one of its principal Arabic usages, w-f-ḍ denotes hastening, moving swiftly, and proceeding quickly toward a destination. Wafaḍa means “he hastened,” awfaḍa “he moved swiftly,” and īfāḍ denotes haste in movement.

Other branches are recorded under the same root: al-awfāḍ for scattered or mixed groups of people, and al-wafḍah for a leather bag or quiver. Their historical relationship to swift movement is not firmly established; they should not all be reduced to an invented common image.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as يُوفِضُونَ in al-Maʿārij 70:43. The verse describes people emerging swiftly from their graves, as though hastening toward an erected marker.

1. The root in early and classical Arabic

Early Arabic records wafaḍa and awfaḍa for the swift movement of people and animals. Their use is not limited to human running: they also describe camels hastening and ostriches moving rapidly.

Wafḍ means haste, and mīfāḍ describes a swift-moving creature. The expression ʿalā wafḍ is also used of someone moving in a hurry.

A verse attributed to al-Ḥuṭayʾah, a poet who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, reads:

وَقِدْرٍ إِذَا مَا أَنْفَضَ النَّاسُ أَوْفَضَتْ
إِلَيْهَا بِأَيْتَامِ الشِّتَاءِ الْأَرَامِلُ

wa-qidrin idhā mā anfaḍa al-nāsu awfaḍat / ilayhā bi-aytāmi al-shitāʾi al-arāmilu

Translation: “And a cooking pot to which, when people’s provisions ran out, widows hastened with winter’s orphans.”

The poet describes a pot toward which people in need hurry during hardship and food shortage.

Awfaḍat expresses swift movement toward a particular destination: the pot to which people come for food. Hunger and need are not meanings of the verb itself; they emerge from the verse’s complete image.

Al-Ḥuṭayʾah lived both before Islam and during the Islamic period. Since this verse’s date of composition is not independently established, it cannot serve as conclusive evidence of pre-Islamic usage. Nevertheless, his use of the verb matters for understanding language close to the period of revelation.

Another witness from this family appears in poetry attributed to al-Shanfarā, a pre-Islamic poet:

لَهَا وَفْضَةٌ فِيهَا ثَلَاثُونَ سَيْحَفًا
إِذَا آنَسَتْ أُولَى الْعَدِيِّ اقْشَعَرَّتِ

lahā wafḍatun fīhā thalāthūna sayḥafan / idhā ānasat ūlā al-ʿadiyyi iqshaʿarrati

Translation: “It has a quiver containing thirty broad arrowheads; when it perceives the first advancing attackers, it bristles.”

Wafḍah here denotes a quiver. The verse is transmitted in the poetry attributed to al-Shanfarā and in early lexical sources, showing a branch of w-f-ḍ concerning an implement for holding things. Its attribution to a pre-Islamic poet is established in the literary tradition, but the surviving copies do not independently settle the precise antiquity of every verse.

Arabic sources describe al-wafḍah as a leather bag in which a shepherd carried equipment and provisions. The same word is recorded for a leather quiver constructed without wood.

Other meanings listed under this entry include a hide beneath a millstone, a surface for placing and cutting meat, and a receptacle for water. These usages show that the entire family cannot be explained simply as “running.”

Al-awfāḍ is also recorded for scattered groups of people, particularly people gathered from different tribes. Some lexicographers connect this meaning with camels dispersing.

Three branches should therefore remain distinct: swift movement, scattered groups, and a kind of container or storage implement. The Qur’anic meaning belongs to the first. Available evidence does not suffice to establish which branch came first historically.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important recorded Arabic forms include:

  • وَفَضَ: hastened, moved swiftly, or ran.
  • وَفْض: haste or hurry.
  • أَوْفَضَ: moved swiftly; transitively, caused someone or an animal to move quickly.
  • إِيفَاض: hastening and swift movement.
  • اسْتَوْفَضَ: hastened; in some usages, bolted or moved from fear.
  • مُسْتَوْفِض: a person or animal moving swiftly, sometimes from fear or alarm.
  • مِيفَاض: swift-moving; used, among other things, of an animal.
  • أَوْفَاض: scattered groups of people; in another usage, haste or hurry.
  • وَفْضَة: a leather bag or quiver.
  • وِفَاض: plural of wafḍah in the sense of a quiver or bag.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an. Its only derivative from this family is يُوفِضُونَ.

Several semantic branches within one root do not necessarily imply a single known historical origin. Swift movement has direct linguistic support for the Qur’anic usage; it need not be derived from a quiver, a scattered group, or a storage implement.

3. Qur’anic usage: emerging swiftly from the graves

Al-Maʿārij 70:43

يَوْمَ يَخْرُجُونَ مِنَ الْأَجْدَاثِ سِرَاعًا كَأَنَّهُمْ إِلَىٰ نُصُبٍ يُوفِضُونَ

yawma yakhrujūna mina al-ajdāthi sirāʿan ka-annahum ilā nuṣubin yūfiḍūn

Al-Maʿārij 70:43

Translation: “The day they emerge swiftly from the graves, as though hastening toward an erected marker.”

يُوفِضُونَ is a masculine plural imperfect verb in Form IV. Its past form is أَوْفَضَ and its verbal noun إِيفَاض.

The verb is intransitive here: the people themselves move swiftly. Although awfaḍa can be transitive elsewhere in Arabic and mean making another hasten, the verse contains no such object.

The plural subject of يُوفِضُونَ refers to those emerging from the graves. إِلَىٰ نُصُبٍ specifies the direction of their movement within the simile.

The verse first uses سِرَاعًا to express their swift emergence. The clause كَأَنَّهُمْ إِلَىٰ نُصُبٍ يُوفِضُونَ then supplies a concrete image of that haste.

Sirāʿan and yūfiḍūn come from different roots but belong to the same semantic field here. First the speed of emergence is stated; then the movement is compared with people hastening toward a particular marker.

Imagine a group racing toward an appointed marker, each person knowing the destination and moving quickly in that direction. This can help illustrate the simile, although the verse does not necessarily describe a sporting race or an exactly comparable situation.

Yūfiḍūn means moving swiftly here; the destination comes from إِلَىٰ نُصُبٍ. The root w-f-ḍ itself does not mean marker, target, or gathering place.

4. Nuṣub in the simile

Understanding nuṣub also matters for the image, although it belongs to the distinct root n-ṣ-b.

In Arabic, nuṣub can refer to something erected, such as a stone, sign, or marker set up at a particular place. In other usages, this family also concerns erected stones and places associated with worship or religious rites.

Two familiar interpretations of al-Maʿārij 70:43 are an erected marker toward which people hasten, and cult stones or places toward which they went to perform rituals.

The readings نُصُب and نَصْب also enter the discussion of these meanings. However, the distinction between marker and cult stone should not be reduced to a rigid, exclusive correspondence with those two vocalizations.

In both interpretations, the image relevant to yūfiḍūn remains: a group moving swiftly toward a particular destination.

If nuṣub is an erected marker, the comparison concerns people hastening toward an appointed sign. If it denotes a cult stone or place of worship, the image concerns people hurrying toward something that previously mattered to them.

The simile does not by itself establish that people at the resurrection will be moving to worship those stones. That movement supplies a comparison, rather than identifying their actual destination in the Hereafter.

“An erected marker” may therefore be selected for the basic translation, while the interpretation involving cult sites is explained in commentary.

5. Haste at the resurrection: movement and human condition

Al-Maʿārij 70:43 follows the preceding verse’s description of encountering the promised day.

Al-Maʿārij 70:42

فَذَرْهُمْ يَخُوضُوا وَيَلْعَبُوا حَتَّىٰ يُلَاقُوا يَوْمَهُمُ الَّذِي يُوعَدُونَ

fa-dharhum yakhūḍū wa-yalʿabū ḥattā yulāqū yawmahumu alladhī yūʿadūn

Al-Maʿārij 70:42

Translation: “So leave them to plunge into talk and play until they encounter the day they are promised.”

Al-Maʿārij 70:43 then describes how they emerge from the graves.

Haste in this scene is not equivalent to joy or an eager welcome. The following verse describes their condition:

Al-Maʿārij 70:44

خَاشِعَةً أَبْصَارُهُمْ تَرْهَقُهُمْ ذِلَّةٌ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ الْيَوْمُ الَّذِي كَانُوا يُوعَدُونَ

khāshiʿatan abṣāruhum tarhaquhum dhillatun dhālika al-yawmu alladhī kānū yūʿadūn

Al-Maʿārij 70:44

Translation: “Their eyes downcast, humiliation enveloping them: that is the day they were promised.”

The haste described in al-Maʿārij 70:43 thus belongs to the context of reckoning, emergence from graves, and the humiliation of those who previously failed to take that day seriously.

Fear, lack of choice, or joy cannot, however, be extracted as lexical meanings of yūfiḍūn itself. The verb expresses speed of movement. Inner states and outcomes are understood from other verses in the surrounding passage.

Some Arabic usages apply mustawfiḍ to a creature that has bolted in fear and runs quickly. This does not restrict every derivative of the root to flight from fear.

Nor should swift movement be used to construct conclusions about the bodily character of resurrection or the mechanics of movement in the Hereafter that the Qur’an does not state. The verse clearly depicts rapid emergence and movement toward a destination without explaining its physical details.

6. Distinctions from related roots

W-f-ḍ (wafḍ) and s-r-ʿ (surʿah): haste and swift movement

S-r-ʿ denotes speed and haste and has a broader range of Qur’anic usages.

In al-Maʿārij 70:43, sirāʿan derives from this root and appears alongside yūfiḍūn. Both concern swift movement, but their roles differ: sirāʿan describes the manner of emergence, while yūfiḍūn describes hastening within the simile.

Their combination does not establish that one is always faster than the other. The verse does not define different degrees of speed.

Study of s-r-ʿ

W-f-ḍ (wafḍ) and w-f-d (wafd): hastening and coming into someone’s presence

In human contexts, w-f-d concerns coming before someone of standing. A wafd is a company arriving in someone’s presence.

W-f-ḍ, by contrast, emphasizes swift movement in its Qur’anic usage.

Someone arriving as part of a wafd may move slowly or quickly. Someone who yūfiḍu, meanwhile, need not belong to an official delegation or an honoured company.

The roots’ similar spelling and shared concern with movement should not cause their meanings to be conflated.

Study of w-f-d

W-f-ḍ (wafḍ) and f-y-ḍ (fayḍ): hastening and flowing

F-y-ḍ concerns flowing and overflowing. The Qur’an uses this family both for overflowing tears and for collective movement in certain settings.

For example, أَفَضْتُمْ in al-Baqarah 2:198 derives from f-y-ḍ, not w-f-ḍ.

Although both families can concern collective movement, īfāḍ from w-f-ḍ denotes hastening, whereas ifāḍah from f-y-ḍ fundamentally concerns flowing or streaming.

Yūfiḍūn should therefore not be assigned the same root as yufīḍūn or afāḍū.

Study of f-y-ḍ

W-f-ḍ (wafḍ) and f-w-ḍ (tafwīḍ): haste and entrusting

F-w-ḍ contains the same three letters in a different order.

In Ghāfir 40:44, وَأُفَوِّضُ أَمْرِي إِلَى اللَّهِ concerns entrusting one’s affair to God. Ufawwiḍu derives from f-w-ḍ, not w-f-ḍ.

The two roots have different Qur’anic meanings: one concerns entrusting an affair, the other swift movement. Similar letters do not prove semantic kinship.

W-f-ḍ (wafḍ) and h-ṭ-ʿ (ihṭāʿ): swift movement and the mover’s posture

In al-Maʿārij 70:36, مُهْطِعِينَ describes people coming toward Muḥammad. The word derives from h-ṭ-ʿ and in Qur’anic usage is associated with swift movement and attention directed toward something.

Both roots can describe group movement, but lexical explanations of h-ṭ-ʿ also concern the posture of the eyes and neck. Those features should not be transferred to yūfiḍūn.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

For wafaḍa and awfaḍa in movement contexts, natural equivalents include “hastened,” “moved swiftly,” “went quickly,” or, where appropriate, “ran swiftly.”

For īfāḍ, “haste in movement” and “moving swiftly” are suitable.

Mustawfiḍ can be rendered “hastening” or “hurried.” Where context concerns a bolting animal or fear, “frightened and hastening” may fit, but fear should not be added without contextual evidence.

For al-awfāḍ in the social branch, “scattered groups” or “groups of diverse people” is appropriate. This meaning is distinct from Qur’anic yūfiḍūn.

For al-wafḍah, “leather bag” or “leather quiver” is proposed according to the object concerned.

The proposed translation of al-Maʿārij 70:43 is:

“The day they emerge swiftly from the graves, as though hastening toward an erected marker.”

This preserves the meanings of both sirāʿan and yūfiḍūn while making the simile clear.

“They run swiftly” is also possible for the verb, but “they hasten” is preferable for the basic translation because it does not narrow the precise manner of movement beyond what the verse explicitly states.

8. Conclusion

In movement contexts, w-f-ḍ denotes hastening and going swiftly. Arabic records this sense for both people and animals.

Other branches include al-awfāḍ for scattered groups and al-wafḍah for a bag or quiver. Their historical connection is not sufficiently clear to derive them all from a single original image.

The Qur’an uses only yūfiḍūn. In al-Maʿārij 70:43 it describes the swift movement of those emerging from graves, as though hastening toward an erected marker in the simile.

Haste comes from the verb itself, the destination from إِلَىٰ نُصُبٍ, and the people’s condition from the surrounding verses. Fear, humiliation, and the manner of movement in the Hereafter should not be presented as lexical components of īfāḍ.

In Qur’anic usage, w-f-ḍ means hastening and swift movement. Yūfiḍūn in al-Maʿārij 70:43 describes hastening toward the destination specified within the simile, without the verb itself judging the movement’s motive, moral value, or physical character.
