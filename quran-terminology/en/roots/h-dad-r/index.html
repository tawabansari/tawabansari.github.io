The Qur’anic Root Ḥ Ḍ R

Presence, arrival, proximity, and bringing into presence: from everyday life to confronting deeds in the Hereafter

The three-consonant root Ḥ Ḍ R is used in the Qur’an for being present, arriving at a situation, the approach of an event, and bringing someone or something into presence. Its various forms describe people attending a distribution of property, death approaching a person, a deed being placed before its doer, and groups being brought together for an accounting.

All these uses involve arriving at or being in a particular situation, but presence does not always mean physically standing in a place. The subject may be a person attending an event, a time arriving, spatial proximity, or something becoming present that was previously neither accessible nor before the person.

The principal Qur’anic forms include ḥaḍara (حَضَرَ), yaḥḍurūni (يَحْضُرُونِ), ḥaḍarūhu (حَضَرُوهُ), uḥḍirat (أُحْضِرَتْ), la-nuḥḍirannahum (لَنُحْضِرَنَّهُمْ), aḥḍarat (أَحْضَرَتْ), ḥāḍir (حَاضِر), ḥāḍirah (حَاضِرَة), ḥāḍirī (حَاضِرِي), muḥḍar (مُحْضَر), and muḥtaḍar (مُحْتَضَر). Plural and case forms such as muḥḍarūn (مُحْضَرُونَ), muḥḍarīn (مُحْضَرِينَ), and muḥḍaran (مُحْضَرًا) belong to the same family.

1. The semantic core and early Arabic usage

In Arabic, ḥaḍara (حَضَرَ) stands in contrast to being absent. Ḥaḍara fulānun (حَضَرَ فُلَانٌ) means that someone came or became present in a situation, and ḥuḍūr (حُضُور), “presence,” contrasts with ghaybah (غَيْبَة), “absence.” In the direct application, someone previously absent arrives at the place or situation where their presence matters.

Lexicographical sources record three related expressions for this root: bringing something, its arriving, and observing or being present with it. These uses are not identical. Something may itself arrive, someone may go to where it is, or a person may bring something else into presence. The contrast between ḥaḍara (حَضَرَ) and aḥḍara (أَحْضَرَ) partly expresses this difference in the direction of the relationship.

In spatial usage, ḥaḍrah (حَضْرَة) can denote someone’s vicinity or the place of their presence. Bi-ḥaḍrati fulānin (بِحَضْرَةِ فُلَانٍ) means “in that person’s presence” or “with that person.” This relates to maḥḍar (مَحْضَر), a place of attendance or assembly.

Presence and settled life

Another familiar branch is al-ḥaḍar (الحَضَر) in contrast to al-badw (البَدْو). In early Arabic, ḥaḍar referred to life in settlements and places of permanent residence, while bādiyah (بادِيَة) concerned desert and nomadic life.

A line attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Al-Aʿshā says:

«حَوْلِي ذَوُو الْآكَالِ مِنْ وَائِلٍ
كَاللَّيْلِ مِنْ بَادٍ وَمِنْ حَاضِرِ»

“Around me are the men of Wāʾil who possess shares and privileges, spreading like the night, both desert dwellers and settled people.”

Bādin (بَادٍ) and ḥāḍir (حَاضِر) contrast with one another here. The poet speaks of two patterns of residence and life, not an ethical difference between people. In this use, ḥāḍir is someone living in a settlement and a permanent dwelling, as opposed to someone whose life is associated with the desert.

This meaning also entered words such as ḥaḍārah (حَضَارَة) and ḥaḍarī (حَضَرِيّ). Yet the modern meaning of advanced civilization, with all its cultural, scientific, industrial, and political implications, must not be projected back as the root’s original pre-Qur’anic meaning. The early sense of ḥaḍar primarily concerns a mode of residence in contrast to desert life; the broader concept of civilization developed in later usage.

The contrast between ḥaḍārah and bādiyah also appears in poetry by Al-Quṭāmī, an Umayyad-period poet. It shows continuity of linguistic usage, but because he belongs to the period after the emergence of Islam, his line must not be treated as an independent witness to pre-Islamic poetry.

Dictionaries also record al-ḥuḍr (الحُضْر) for a horse’s swift, bounding run. An explanatory connection with movement and arrival is possible, but this cannot be established as the definite origin of “presence,” nor can speed be extended to every derivative. The Qur’an does not use this root for a horse’s gallop.

In the family’s principal uses, presence therefore concerns arriving, being present, or being situated in a particular setting. Settled life, spatial proximity, and bringing into presence are also recognized branches, without requiring all of them to derive from a single unproven material image.

2. Ḥaḍara: a person’s arrival and the onset of an event

Attending an event

One of the clearest uses of ḥaḍara (حَضَرَ) occurs in Al-Nisāʾ 4:8:

«وَإِذَا حَضَرَ الْقِسْمَةَ أُولُو الْقُرْبَىٰ وَالْيَتَامَىٰ وَالْمَسَاكِينُ فَارْزُقُوهُمْ مِنْهُ وَقُولُوا لَهُمْ قَوْلًا مَعْرُوفًا»

“When relatives, orphans, and the needy are present at the distribution, give them something from it and speak to them in a proper, kindly manner.”

(Al-Nisāʾ 4:8)

Ḥaḍara al-qismata (حَضَرَ الْقِسْمَةَ) concerns people attending a distribution of property. They are present in the setting where the distribution takes place. The verb itself does not say what share they have or whether they are entitled to inherit; those questions concern the distribution and other provisions.

Presence here may also involve awareness and seeing an event, but the verb does not necessarily mean “testifying.” Someone may attend an event without serving as an official or legal witness.

Another use occurs in Al-Aḥqāf 46:29:

«وَإِذْ صَرَفْنَا إِلَيْكَ نَفَرًا مِنَ الْجِنِّ يَسْتَمِعُونَ الْقُرْآنَ فَلَمَّا حَضَرُوهُ قَالُوا أَنْصِتُوا فَلَمَّا قُضِيَ وَلَّوْا إِلَىٰ قَوْمِهِمْ مُنْذِرِينَ»

“When We directed a group of jinn toward you to listen to the Qur’an, then, when they attended its recitation, they said, ‘Be silent and listen.’ When it was finished, they returned to their people as warners.”

(Al-Aḥqāf 46:29)

Ḥaḍarūhu (حَضَرُوهُ) expresses attending the occasion of hearing the Qur’an. Listening and becoming silent are expressed by yastamiʿūna (يَسْتَمِعُونَ) and anṣitū (أَنْصِتُوا). Thus ḥaḍara must not itself be equated with listening, understanding, or accepting the message. Those actions occur in the same context but are expressed by other words.

The approach of death

Another use of the same verb occurs in Al-Baqarah 2:133:

«أَمْ كُنْتُمْ شُهَدَاءَ إِذْ حَضَرَ يَعْقُوبَ الْمَوْتُ»

“Were you witnesses when death approached Jacob?”

(Al-Baqarah 2:133; opening of the verse)

Al-mawtu (الْمَوْتُ), “death,” is the subject of ḥaḍara, while Yaʿqūba (يَعْقُوبَ), “Jacob,” is its object: death reached or approached Jacob. The sentence does not say that Jacob attended a place called death; it relates the event of death to him.

The same construction occurs in Al-Baqarah 2:180:

«كُتِبَ عَلَيْكُمْ إِذَا حَضَرَ أَحَدَكُمُ الْمَوْتُ إِنْ تَرَكَ خَيْرًا الْوَصِيَّةُ لِلْوَالِدَيْنِ وَالْأَقْرَبِينَ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ حَقًّا عَلَى الْمُتَّقِينَ»

“It is prescribed for you that, when death approaches one of you, if they leave property, they should make a bequest for parents and close relatives in a recognized, proper manner—a duty upon the mindful.”

(Al-Baqarah 2:180)

Here too, ḥaḍara concerns death’s approach. Since the verse concerns the person making a will, it describes a situation in which death has drawn near but the person can still give those instructions.

A similar construction occurs in Al-Nisāʾ 4:18 and Al-Māʾidah 5:106. In Al-Nisāʾ 4:18, someone declares repentance as death approaches; Al-Māʾidah 5:106 addresses testimony when a will is made. The subjects differ, but ḥaḍara al-mawtu (حَضَرَ الْمَوْتُ) still means death arriving or drawing near.

This does not establish that death is always conceived in the Qur’an as a physical being moving toward a person. Arabic can express an event’s onset using a verb also used for a person’s arrival. “Death approached” is therefore natural and accurate, without adding a physical image.

Unwanted presence

Al-Muʾminūn 23:97–98 gives the following prayer:

«وَقُلْ رَبِّ أَعُوذُ بِكَ مِنْ هَمَزَاتِ الشَّيَاطِينِ ۝ وَأَعُوذُ بِكَ رَبِّ أَنْ يَحْضُرُونِ»

“Say, ‘My Lord, I seek refuge in You from the promptings and incitements of the devils; and I seek refuge in You, my Lord, from their coming into my presence.’”

(Al-Muʾminūn 23:97–98)

Yaḥḍurūni (يَحْضُرُونِ) concerns the devils coming into the speaker’s presence. Unlike attending a gathering or a distribution of property, this is an unwanted presence from which the person seeks refuge in The God.

This shows that Ḥ Ḍ R has no inherently positive value. Presence may be welcome or unwelcome; its value depends on who is present and the passage’s context.

Nor should presence here be restricted, without evidence, to a particular kind of physical contact, psychological influence, or control over human will. The text expresses a request for refuge from the devils’ presence; it does not explain its mechanism.

3. Ḥāḍir and ḥāḍirah: proximity, residence, and immediate transactions

A settlement beside the sea

Al-Aʿrāf 7:163 says:

«وَاسْأَلْهُمْ عَنِ الْقَرْيَةِ الَّتِي كَانَتْ حَاضِرَةَ الْبَحْرِ»

“Ask them about the settlement that stood beside the sea.”

(Al-Aʿrāf 7:163; opening of the verse)

Ḥāḍirata al-baḥri (حَاضِرَةَ الْبَحْرِ) describes the settlement’s position relative to the sea: near or beside it. It does not picture a settlement attending a gathering of the sea as a person might.

Qaryah (قَرْيَة) names the settlement. Ḥāḍirah does not establish that it was necessarily a large city, political center, or settlement with a particular civilizational structure. Such urban definitions are known from historical and modern uses of ḥāḍirah, but here ḥāḍirata al-baḥri specifies the spatial relationship with the sea.

Ḥāḍirī al-masjidi al-ḥarām

Al-Baqarah 2:196 contains the expression:

«ذَٰلِكَ لِمَنْ لَمْ يَكُنْ أَهْلُهُ حَاضِرِي الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ»

“This is for someone whose family are not residents around the Sacred Mosque.”

(Al-Baqarah 2:196; part of the verse)

The passage concerns provisions for hajj and ʿumrah. Significantly, ḥāḍirī al-masjidi al-ḥarām (حَاضِرِي الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ) does not simply refer to temporary attendance in the mosque building, since the verse speaks of ahluhu (أَهْلُهُ), the person’s family or household.

In this context, ḥāḍirī concerns residence and proximity of the household’s dwelling to the Sacred Mosque. It draws on the same semantic capacity through which early Arabic contrasts ḥāḍir with bādī.

The root alone does not determine the exact geographical limits of this residence. How far the category extends is a question for understanding the full provision and context of hajj, not a boundary fixed by ḥāḍir itself.

Tijāratan ḥāḍirah: an immediate transaction

After instructions on recording debts and deferred transactions, Al-Baqarah 2:282 says:

«إِلَّا أَنْ تَكُونَ تِجَارَةً حَاضِرَةً تُدِيرُونَهَا بَيْنَكُمْ فَلَيْسَ عَلَيْكُمْ جُنَاحٌ أَلَّا تَكْتُبُوهَا»

“Unless it is an immediate, on-the-spot transaction that you conduct among yourselves; then there is no blame upon you if you do not write it down.”

(Al-Baqarah 2:282; part of the verse)

Ḥāḍirah qualifies tijārah (تِجَارَة), “trade” or “transaction.” The verse concerns an exchange conducted between the parties on the occasion itself, in contrast to a debt whose payment is deferred to a specified future time.

“Present trade,” if it conveys no clear meaning, is therefore an unsuitable rendering. “An on-the-spot transaction” or “an immediate transaction” communicates the sense more naturally.

Ḥāḍirah does not itself mean cash, coins, or a particular payment method. Its direct meaning concerns being present and available; applied to a transaction in contrast to deferred debt, it conveys an exchange completed immediately.

The three expressions ḥāḍirata al-baḥri, ḥāḍirī al-masjidi al-ḥarām, and tijāratan ḥāḍirah illustrate a fundamental distinction. A settlement near the sea, a household residing around a place, and an immediate transaction all relate to presence, yet cannot all be translated with one fixed equivalent.

4. Aḥḍara: bringing into presence and placing before another

Aḥḍara (أَحْضَرَ) is a Form IV verb whose established uses concern bringing something into presence or to a particular setting. Unlike ḥaḍara, where someone or something may arrive or become present, aḥḍara emphasizes causing that state of presence.

Avarice and the difficulty of reconciliation

In discussing marital discord and the possibility of reconciliation, Al-Nisāʾ 4:128 says:

«وَالصُّلْحُ خَيْرٌ وَأُحْضِرَتِ الْأَنْفُسُ الشُّحَّ»

“Reconciliation is better, and avarice has been brought into the souls’ presence.”

(Al-Nisāʾ 4:128; part of the verse)

Uḥḍirat (أُحْضِرَتْ) is the passive of Form IV. In the usual grammatical analysis, al-anfusu (الْأَنْفُسُ), “the souls,” is the passive subject, while al-shuḥḥa (الشُّحَّ), “avarice,” remains the other object. The expression therefore concerns avarice being brought into the souls’ presence, or the souls being confronted with it.

Al-shuḥḥ (الشُّحّ) concerns avarice and a strong grasping desire to retain what one wants for oneself. The verse’s context concerns reconciliation and the difficulty of relinquishing a personal right or desire. That meaning comes from al-shuḥḥ and the passage’s subject, not from Ḥ Ḍ R.

The passive also matters: this expression does not explicitly name the agent who brings it into presence. A translation should therefore not add a particular agent without grounds. Nor does the phrase establish that people must inevitably follow avarice, since the same verse speaks of reconciliation, doing good, and taqwa.

Presence here does not mean the physical transportation of an object. Avarice is not carried somewhere like a person or merchandise. The construction expresses this disposition’s presence within human souls and its influence on conduct.

What a person brings forward

Al-Takwīr 81:14 says:

«عَلِمَتْ نَفْسٌ مَا أَحْضَرَتْ»

“Each soul will know what it has brought forward.”

(Al-Takwīr 81:14)

Aḥḍarat (أَحْضَرَتْ) is active here, and nafsun (نَفْسٌ), “a soul,” is its subject. This time, the soul itself is identified as the doer.

The preceding verses describe a series of tremendous events and give this sentence as their culmination. What a person has brought forward is understood in the context of accounting and the disclosure of what they have done. Aḥḍarat itself, however, does not mean “reckoned,” “wrote a record of deeds,” or “was punished”; it emphasizes bringing forward.

This expression is semantically related to two further verses in which human deeds are found present.

Deeds becoming present before their doers

Āl ʿImrān 3:30 says:

«يَوْمَ تَجِدُ كُلُّ نَفْسٍ مَا عَمِلَتْ مِنْ خَيْرٍ مُحْضَرًا»

“The day when every soul finds whatever good it has done brought before it.”

(Āl ʿImrān 3:30; part of the verse)

Muḥḍaran (مُحْضَرًا) is a Form IV passive participle. The good deed is described as something that has been brought into presence. The verse continues by discussing evil deeds and the wish to be far from them.

Al-Kahf 18:49 says:

«وَوُضِعَ الْكِتَابُ فَتَرَى الْمُجْرِمِينَ مُشْفِقِينَ مِمَّا فِيهِ وَيَقُولُونَ يَا وَيْلَتَنَا مَالِ هَٰذَا الْكِتَابِ لَا يُغَادِرُ صَغِيرَةً وَلَا كَبِيرَةً إِلَّا أَحْصَاهَا وَوَجَدُوا مَا عَمِلُوا حَاضِرًا وَلَا يَظْلِمُ رَبُّكَ أَحَدًا»

“The Book is set down, and you see the offenders fearful of what it contains. They say, ‘Woe to us! What is this Book that leaves nothing small or great without counting it?’ They find what they have done present, and your Lord wrongs no one.”

(Al-Kahf 18:49)

The verse distinguishes aḥṣāhā (أَحْصَاهَا) from ḥāḍiran (حَاضِرًا). The first concerns counting and accurate recording; the second concerns the presence of what was done.

Ḥāḍir should therefore not simply be translated “recorded” without regard to the wording. The recording of deeds is mentioned in the same verse, but ḥāḍiran emphasizes that the deeds are present before their doers, who can now confront them.

This expression does not authorize a definitive theory about the physical form of deeds in the Hereafter. The Qur’an speaks of deeds becoming present; the root Ḥ Ḍ R alone does not explain the nature of that presence.

5. Muḥḍarūn: being brought for accounting and confronting the outcome of deeds

Prominent Qur’anic uses include the passive forms muḥḍarūn (مُحْضَرُونَ), muḥḍarīn (مُحْضَرِينَ), and muḥḍaran (مُحْضَرًا). They refer to people or things brought into presence.

Yā Sīn 36:32 says:

«وَإِنْ كُلٌّ لَمَّا جَمِيعٌ لَدَيْنَا مُحْضَرُونَ»

“All of them, without exception, will be brought before Us.”

(Yā Sīn 36:32)

Muḥḍarūn here does not describe a group voluntarily attending an ordinary gathering. Its passive form indicates that they are brought into presence. Ladaynā (لَدَيْنَا), “before Us,” specifies the destination.

Yā Sīn 36:53 repeats this meaning with reference to a single cry and everyone being brought together.

Maryam 19:68 says:

«فَوَرَبِّكَ لَنَحْشُرَنَّهُمْ وَالشَّيَاطِينَ ثُمَّ لَنُحْضِرَنَّهُمْ حَوْلَ جَهَنَّمَ جِثِيًّا»

“By your Lord, We shall gather them and the devils; then We shall bring them around Hell, on their knees.”

(Maryam 19:68)

La-naḥshurannahum (لَنَحْشُرَنَّهُمْ) and la-nuḥḍirannahum (لَنُحْضِرَنَّهُمْ) appear together here. Ḥashr emphasizes gathering, while iḥḍār emphasizes bringing into a particular setting. Ḥawla jahannama (حَوْلَ جَهَنَّمَ) specifies the scene, and jithiyyan (جِثِيًّا) describes their condition there: on their knees.

Iḥḍār does not in itself mean gathering, although the two may be connected within a process. Kneeling and being around Hell likewise come from other words and the verse’s complete construction, not from Ḥ Ḍ R itself.

Does muḥḍarūn always mean people being punished?

Al-Rūm 30:16 says:

«وَأَمَّا الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا وَكَذَّبُوا بِآيَاتِنَا وَلِقَاءِ الْآخِرَةِ فَأُولَٰئِكَ فِي الْعَذَابِ مُحْضَرُونَ»

“As for those who rejected and denied Our signs and the meeting of the Hereafter, they will be brought into punishment.”

(Al-Rūm 30:16)

Fī al-ʿadhābi (فِي الْعَذَابِ), “in punishment,” explicitly specifies the situation. Punishment comes from that phrase; it is not an independent component of muḥḍarūn.

Sabaʾ 34:38 uses the same combination for those striving against The God’s signs to render them ineffective. Al-Ṣāffāt 37:127 and 37:158 use la-muḥḍarūn (لَمُحْضَرُونَ), and Al-Ṣāffāt 37:57 uses al-muḥḍarīn (الْمُحْضَرِينَ). These contexts concern accounting, the final outcome, and punishment.

Yā Sīn 36:32 and 36:53, however, speak of everyone being brought forward. They prevent muḥḍar from being defined throughout the Qur’an as “someone punished.”

Muḥḍar denotes someone or something brought into presence. In one context it may concern accounting, in another confrontation with punishment, or something placed before its doer. The outcome and kind of presence must be understood from the verse.

Jundun muḥḍarūn: two contextual possibilities

Yā Sīn 36:74–75 says:

«وَاتَّخَذُوا مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ آلِهَةً لَعَلَّهُمْ يُنْصَرُونَ ۝ لَا يَسْتَطِيعُونَ نَصْرَهُمْ وَهُمْ لَهُمْ جُنْدٌ مُحْضَرُونَ»

“They have taken gods apart from The God, hoping to be helped. They cannot help them, while they are for them a host brought into presence.”

(Yā Sīn 36:74–75)

Identifying the referents of hum (هُمْ), “they,” and lahum (لَهُمْ), “for them,” is important here. One reading understands the worshippers as a host ready and present for their gods: people defending deities that cannot help them. Another relates the sentence to being brought into the scene of accounting or the final outcome in the Hereafter.

The construction permits these possibilities to be examined, but the lexical meaning of muḥḍarūn remains “brought into presence” in either case. The derivative itself does not identify who the host consists of, for whom they are present, or exactly when that presence occurs. Those questions depend on pronoun relationships and context.

6. Muḥtaḍar: a turn at the water, not a dying person

One of the root’s most important applications is muḥtaḍar (مُحْتَضَر) in Al-Qamar 54:28:

«وَنَبِّئْهُمْ أَنَّ الْمَاءَ قِسْمَةٌ بَيْنَهُمْ كُلُّ شِرْبٍ مُحْتَضَرٌ»

“Inform them that the water is divided among them; each turn at drinking is attended by the one whose turn it is.”

(Al-Qamar 54:28)

This verse occurs in the account of Ṣāliḥ and his people. The water is divided between them and the she-camel, with each side having an appointed turn to use it.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:155 states the matter more explicitly:

«قَالَ هَٰذِهِ نَاقَةٌ لَهَا شِرْبٌ وَلَكُمْ شِرْبُ يَوْمٍ مَعْلُومٍ»

“He said, ‘This is a she-camel. She has a turn at the water, and you have a turn at drinking on an appointed day.’”

(Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:155)

Muḥtaḍar in Al-Qamar 54:28 is a Form VIII passive participle. It describes a turn for which someone comes to make use of the water. The subject is water allocation and observance of turns, not illness or approaching death.

A familiar contemporary use of muḥtaḍar denotes a person at the point of death. This relates to expressions concerning death’s presence, but that familiar modern sense must not be carried into Al-Qamar 54:28.

Kullu shirbin muḥtaḍar (كُلُّ شِرْبٍ مُحْتَضَرٌ) does not mean “every drink is dying” or “every turn at the water is at the point of death.” Such renderings result from restricting the word to its later specialized sense and do not fit the context.

Shirb (شِرْب) here can denote a share or turn in access to water. The turn comes from shirb and the division of water, while muḥtaḍar expresses attendance to make use of it. These two contributions must remain distinct.

7. Related roots and translation choices

Ḥ Ḍ R (ḥaḍara) and SH H D (shahida)

Ḥaḍara emphasizes becoming present or arriving, while many uses of shahida (شَهِدَ) concern witnessing, observation, and giving testimony.

Both roots occur together in Al-Baqarah 2:133: am kuntum shuhadāʾa idh ḥaḍara Yaʿqūba al-mawtu (أَمْ كُنْتُمْ شُهَدَاءَ إِذْ حَضَرَ يَعْقُوبَ الْمَوْتُ). Shuhadāʾa concerns the addressees being witnesses, and ḥaḍara concerns death approaching Jacob.

Presence may provide an occasion for observation or testimony, but the words are not complete synonyms. Someone can be present without testifying; testimony, too, is not confined to physical attendance at a gathering.

Ḥ Ḍ R (ḥaḍara) and Q R B (qaruba)

Q R B expresses proximity, whether spatial, temporal, or of another kind. Ḥ Ḍ R can also express nearness, but more often emphasizes arrival or presence in a particular setting.

For example, ḥāḍirata al-baḥri (حَاضِرَةَ الْبَحْرِ) in Al-Aʿrāf 7:163 expresses proximity to the sea, while ḥaḍara al-mawtu (حَضَرَ الْمَوْتُ) in Al-Baqarah 2:180 concerns death’s approach. The roots overlap in some uses, but substituting them in every sentence would be inaccurate.

Ḥ Ḍ R (aḥḍara) and J M AYN (jamaʿa)

Jamaʿa (جَمَعَ) emphasizes gathering; aḥḍara emphasizes bringing into presence. A group may be gathered and then brought to a particular scene.

The juxtaposition of la-naḥshurannahum and la-nuḥḍirannahum in Maryam 19:68 helps illustrate this difference. Ḥashr and jamʿ emphasize gathering, while iḥḍār may foreground the destination and setting into which people are brought.

This distinction must not become an absolute rule that every bringing into presence follows a group’s assembly. In Al-Takwīr 81:14 a soul brings something forward, and in Al-Nisāʾ 4:128 avarice has been brought into the souls’ presence, without the gathering of a group being at issue.

Ḥ Ḍ R (ḥaḍara) and B D W (badā)

In the branch concerning ways of life, ḥaḍar contrasts with badw. This opposition concerns settled and desert life, however, not every application of the two roots.

Other uses of badā (بَدَا) also concern becoming apparent. The opposition of ḥaḍar and badw therefore does not mean that every presence is concealment or that every appearance is absence. The roots contrast in particular fields, but their semantic ranges do not coincide completely.

Ḥ Ḍ R (ḥaḍara) and Ḥ Ẓ R (ḥaẓara)

Ḥ Ḍ R and Ḥ Ẓ R are close in writing and sound but must not be confused. Ḥ Ẓ R concerns preventing and enclosing.

The distinction is particularly notable in Al-Qamar. Muḥtaḍarun (مُحْتَضَرٌ) in Al-Qamar 54:28 comes from Ḥ Ḍ R, whereas al-muḥtaẓiri (الْمُحْتَظِرِ) in Al-Qamar 54:31 comes from Ḥ Ẓ R. Similar-looking words do not establish identical roots or meanings.

Translation choices for Forqan

For ḥaḍara in applications concerning people, “was present” or “attended” is suitable. In ḥaḍara al-mawtu, “death approached” or “death arrived” is more natural. Yaḥḍurūni in Al-Muʾminūn 23:98 can be rendered “come into my presence.”

Ḥāḍir in Al-Kahf 18:49 means “present” or “present before the person.” Suitable renderings are “situated beside the sea” for ḥāḍirata al-baḥri, “residents around the Sacred Mosque” for ḥāḍirī al-masjidi al-ḥarām, and “an immediate, on-the-spot transaction” for tijāratan ḥāḍirah.

Render aḥḍara as “brought forward” or “brought into presence,” according to the sentence. “Has brought forward” suits aḥḍarat in Al-Takwīr 81:14. In Al-Nisāʾ 4:128, however, the passive construction and the subject of avarice call for a natural expression such as “avarice has been brought into the souls’ presence.”

Muḥḍar and muḥḍarūn should generally be rendered “brought into presence” or “are brought forward.” If punishment is specified, translate it as part of that phrase; do not make “punished” the derivative’s fixed equivalent.

Muḥtaḍar in Al-Qamar 54:28 must be translated in relation to a turn at the water, for example “a turn that is attended.” The contemporary use for someone at the point of death does not suit this verse.

8. Conclusion

Ḥ Ḍ R in the Qur’an expresses presence, arrival, and bringing into presence. A person can attend an event, death can approach someone, a settlement can stand beside the sea, a transaction can be completed immediately, and people or deeds can be brought to a particular scene.

Within this family, ḥaḍara chiefly emphasizes coming or arriving; ḥāḍir, a state of presence; aḥḍara, bringing into presence; and muḥḍar, someone or something brought forward. Muḥtaḍar in its Qur’anic application concerns an allocation of water attended by those entitled to use it.

Two distinctions are fundamental. First, presence is not always physical: it can concern an event’s onset, spatial proximity, or deeds becoming present. Second, being brought forward does not itself mean punishment or accounting. Those meanings come from context and other expressions in the verse.

The semantic core of Ḥ Ḍ R can be expressed as the arrival or presence of a person, thing, or event in a particular setting, or bringing it into that setting. This covers people’s attendance, death’s approach, spatial proximity, and deeds being brought forward, without imposing speed, observation, judgment, punishment, or a physical mode of presence on every derivative.
