Cutting off, bringing to an end, and eliminating: the meaning of حُسُومًا in the Qur’an

1. Introducing the root and its basic meaning

The triliteral Arabic root ح س م concerns cutting off, preventing, and bringing something to an end. حَسَمَ is used when something is severed, an ongoing process is stopped, or a cause or effect is eliminated.

Cutting off in this family does not always mean cutting a physical object with a sharp instrument. Blood flow may cease, a disease may be eliminated, someone’s access to something may be blocked, or an issue may be settled so that dispute or uncertainty ends.

The root’s meaning cannot therefore be confined to physical cutting. The common feature of its principal uses is cutting something off or ending its continuation and effect.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as حُسُومًا in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7. It describes the seven nights and eight days during which a destructive wind was imposed upon ʿĀd.

Lexical and exegetical sources record two important explanations of حُسُومًا: consecutive, uninterrupted days, and a punishment that destroyed the people and brought their existence to an end.

Both readings relate to the root’s semantic range, but their differences should remain clear.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the sole occurrence in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7

2. Lexical forms and semantic branches

Ḥasama: cutting off and bringing to an end

حَسَمَ يَحْسِمُ حَسْمًا is the root’s form I verb.

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris introduces the root as follows:

«الْحَاءُ وَالسِّينُ وَالْمِيمُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ، وَهُوَ قَطْعُ الشَّيْءِ عَنْ آخِرِهِ»

al-ḥāʾu wa-l-sīnu wa-l-mīmu aṣlun wāḥidun, wa-huwa qaṭʿu al-shayʾi ʿan ākhirihi

That is: “Ḥāʾ, sīn, and mīm have one semantic basis: cutting something off completely.” Here عَنْ آخِرِهِ indicates complete severance, rather than merely cutting from an object’s end.

This definition emphasizes cutting off. Ibn Fāris also relates the root’s other uses to this basis.

Ibn Fāris, Maqāyīs al-Lughah: complete severance and uses of ḥasm

Lisān al-ʿArab records حَسَمَهُ يَحْسِمُهُ حَسْمًا فَانْحَسَمَ: he cut it off, and it was cut off.

Here حَسَمَ is transitive, while انْحَسَمَ is form VII, expressing the occurrence of severance or cessation.

Ḥasm al-dāʾ: ending a disease

In early Arabic, حَسَمَ الدَّاءَ expresses eliminating a disease or ending its effect.

Some uses concern treatment by cauterization.

For example, حَسَمَ الْعِرْقَ is recorded for stopping a vessel’s bleeding through cauterization. It may be used after a vessel has been cut, but does not itself necessarily mean cutting the vessel. Al-Miṣbāḥ al-Munīr explains the intended expression as حَسَمَ دَمَ الْعِرْقِ.

In this usage, cutting off concerns stopping the blood flow. Cutting a vessel and stopping its bleeding should not be treated as the same action.

Al-Miṣbāḥ al-Munīr: stopping a vessel’s bleeding

Thus ḥasm can express stopping a harmful flow or ending its effect.

This does not make treating a disease the root’s basic meaning. Treatment is one setting in which cutting off or stopping occurs.

These lexical uses concern historical treatments; they do not by themselves establish those methods’ effectiveness or safety according to modern medical knowledge.

Ḥusām: a sharp sword

حُسَام is a familiar derivative used for a sharp, cutting sword.

Dictionaries record سَيْفٌ حُسَامٌ as a cutting sword.

Its relationship to ḥasm is clear: a sword cuts and separates.

حُسَامُ السَّيْفِ can also refer to a sword’s edge or cutting part.

This does not make every form of ح س م refer to a weapon or killing. A sword is one familiar instance of cutting, rather than a definition of the entire word family.

Maḥsūm: something whose continuation has been cut off

مَحْسُوم is the passive participle, used for something cut off or stopped.

In early Arabic, مَحْسُوم also describes a poorly nourished, undersized child. Lexicographers explain this usage through interruption or impairment of nursing and nutrition.

Here, impaired growth is related to interrupted or inadequate nourishment. That consequence or naming explanation should not become the basic meaning of every derivative.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Lisān al-ʿArab, and Tāj al-ʿArūs: maḥsūm and nutrition

انْحَسَمَ الْأَمْرُ can also mean that an issue ended or was settled.

Modern Persian and Arabic uses such as settling an issue or a decisive decision belong to this semantic range. They concern ending uncertainty, disagreement, or an ongoing situation, rather than physically cutting something.

Ḥusūm: continuity or cutting off

حُسُوم is the most important form of this family for Qur’anic study.

Dictionaries and grammatical explanations analyze it in several ways.

One analysis treats حُسُوم as the plural of حَاسِم on the pattern فُعُول, like شُهُود from شَاهِد.

Another treats حُسُوم as a verbal noun expressing cutting off or destroying.

With days and nights, حُسُوم is also explained as succession, continuing hardship, or harmful days.

Consecutive should not replace the general meaning of every derivative without evidence. It concerns a particular use, especially the description of time and an ongoing condition.

3. Early Arabic witnesses and the history of usage

A witness from Abū Khirāsh al-Hudhalī

A poem by Abū Khirāsh al-Hudhalī contains:

وَلَوْلَا نَحْنُ أَرْهَقَهُ صُهَيْبٌ
حُسَامَ الْحَدِّ مَذْرُوبًا خَشِيبًا

wa-lawlā naḥnu arhaqahu ṣuhaybun / ḥusāma al-ḥaddi madhrūban khashīban

Explanatory translation: “Had it not been for us, Ṣuhayb would have brought down upon him a keen-edged, sharpened and polished sword.”

حُسَامَ الْحَدِّ describes a very sharp-edged sword. Lexical commentary explains أَرْهَقَهُ حُسَامًا as bringing a sword upon someone; merely translating “would have reached him” omits an important part of the construction. خَشِيب is recorded both for an unfinished sword and for a sharpened or polished one; the rendering above selects one attested use.

Lisān al-ʿArab, R H Q: arhaqahu ḥusāman

The witness shows that poetic Arabic used ḥusām for a cutting instrument, clearly connecting it with cutting off.

Abū Khirāsh lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. The precise date of this line is unknown. It is valuable evidence for early Arabic, but should not be presented without evidence as certainly predating the Qur’an.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Abū Khirāsh’s witness and khashīb as polished

A witness attributed to ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz ibn Zurārah al-Kilābī

Poetry attributed to ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz ibn Zurārah al-Kilābī contains:

فَفَرَّقَ بَيْنَ بَيْنِهِمْ زَمَانٌ
تَتَابَعَ فِيهِ أَعْوَامٌ حُسُومُ

fa-farraqa bayna baynihim zamānun / tatābaʿa fīhi aʿwāmun ḥusūmu

Explanatory translation: “Time separated them, a time through which harsh, consecutive years followed one another.”

Minor variants occur in the first hemistich, but the second, أَعْوَامٌ حُسُومُ, matters for the meaning of continuity.

Here حُسُوم describes years that followed one another.

This witness shows the word being used for successive periods of time.

However, ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz ibn Zurārah was an Umayyad poet, and his poetry postdates the Qur’an. This line alone therefore cannot establish the same temporal usage before the Qur’an.

The poet may have been influenced by Qur’anic usage. The witness is evidence for the word’s later history, rather than proof of earlier usage.

Al-Kashshāf: ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz ibn Zurārah’s witness

Continuity and the image of cauterization

Al-Farrāʾ explains حُسُومًا through succession. In Lisān al-ʿArab, Abū Manṣūr al-Azharī compares this succession with repeated cauterization to stop bleeding. Attributing that entire explanation to Al-Farrāʾ himself is therefore imprecise.

In historical wound cauterization, an application might be repeated to stop bleeding or end a disease’s effects.

On this account, repeating an action without a break offers a basis for describing successive days as ḥusūm.

The semantic connection is understandable, but should not be presented as the word’s established historical origin.

Another explanation instead connects ḥusūm directly with cutting off and eradicating.

Both explanations occur in the lexical tradition. The verse’s meaning must be assessed through its syntax and Qur’anic context.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Al-Farrāʾ, Al-Azharī, and Al-Zajjāj on ḥusūm

4. The root’s sole Qur’anic occurrence

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7

سَخَّرَهَا عَلَيْهِمْ سَبْعَ لَيَالٍ وَثَمَانِيَةَ أَيَّامٍ حُسُومًا فَتَرَى الْقَوْمَ فِيهَا صَرْعَىٰ كَأَنَّهُمْ أَعْجَازُ نَخْلٍ خَاوِيَةٍ

sakhkharahā ʿalayhim sabʿa layālin wa-thamāniyata ayyāmin ḥusūman fa-tarā al-qawma fīhā ṣarʿā ka-annahum aʿjāzu nakhlin khāwiyatin

Suggested translation: “He imposed it upon them for seven nights and eight consecutive days; you would see the people lying fallen during that time, as though they were hollow palm trunks.”

The verse continues the account of ʿĀd’s destruction.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:6 identifies a fierce, overpowering wind as the means of their destruction. Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7 gives its duration: seven nights and eight days.

حُسُومًا then describes the character of that period or the wind’s effect.

The rest of the verse depicts ʿĀd lying fallen, compared with hollow palm trunks. Al-Ḥāqqah 69:8 then asks about any remnant of them in this scene of destruction.

Both the wind’s temporal continuity and its destructive outcome are therefore present in the context.

First reading: consecutive, uninterrupted days

On this reading, حُسُومًا means مُتَتَابِعَةً or مُتَوَالِيَةً: consecutive, without interruption.

Al-Ṭabarī transmits this meaning from several early interpreters and prefers it.

The destructive wind was thus imposed upon them throughout seven nights and eight consecutive days, without a respite during that period.

This reading clearly suits the word’s position after the number of nights and days.

In سَبْعَ لَيَالٍ وَثَمَانِيَةَ أَيَّامٍ حُسُومًا, it is natural to understand حُسُومًا as describing that period.

“Seven nights and eight consecutive days” therefore conveys the construction simply and directly.

An important distinction remains: uninterrupted here means no break within the punishment’s duration, rather than everlasting duration. The verse itself gives a limited period.

Al-Ṭabarī: preference for succession

Second reading: an eradicating punishment

Another reading connects حُسُومًا directly with the basic meaning of ḥasm: cutting off and eliminating.

On this account, the wind overwhelmed ʿĀd so as to eradicate them.

This also suits the continuation, which describes fallen bodies and the people’s destruction after mentioning the wind.

Al-Zajjāj and other lexicographers explain the phrase through تَحْسِمُهُمْ حُسُومًا: the wind eliminates and destroys them.

In this analysis, حُسُومًا can be an accusative verbal noun expressing eradication.

This reading has clear lexical support in ح س م and agrees with the outcome described.

It nevertheless requires a fuller syntactic explanation: the word follows the number of nights and days, and the first reading straightforwardly describes that period.

Can both meanings be connected?

The verse places two realities together: the wind’s specified duration and the destruction of ʿĀd.

Succession and eradication are therefore compatible at the level of the account. The wind could continue without a break and ultimately destroy the people.

Their compatibility does not establish that one word in a particular construction necessarily conveys both meanings equally.

For a basic translation, consecutive is preferable: it connects more directly with the numbered nights and days and the word’s position, and enjoys broad support in early explanations.

Cutting off and eradicating should remain in the lexical analysis and discussion of the second reading.

This distinction keeps the sentence’s direct meaning clear while retaining the word’s connection to its root.

The syntax of حُسُومًا

Several grammatical analyses have been proposed.

In one analysis, حُسُومًا is an accusative adjective qualifying the numerical expression سَبْعَ ... وَثَمَانِيَةَ ..., describing the nights and days together. لَيَالٍ and أَيَّامٍ are themselves genitive complements of the numbers; حُسُومًا should not be treated as a direct genitive adjective of أَيَّامٍ.

Another analysis treats حُسُومًا as an accusative verbal noun conveying the people’s elimination.

It has also been analyzed as a circumstantial expression or an accusative of purpose, bringing out its relationship to the wind and the destructive outcome.

These possibilities should not be collapsed into one definitive grammatical account.

A Persian translation need not place every grammatical disagreement within one sentence. It should express the preferred reading clearly and naturally, leaving alternatives for separate explanation.

Al-Kashshāf: plural, verbal noun, and syntactic alternatives

Connections with other verses about ʿĀd

Fuṣṣilat 41:16 describes sending a ṣarṣar wind against ʿĀd during أَيَّامٍ نَحِسَاتٍ.

Al-Qamar 54:19 likewise places a ṣarṣar wind in يَوْمِ نَحْسٍ مُسْتَمِرٍّ.

These verses describe the period of ʿĀd’s punishment through hardship and a disastrous outcome.

نَحْس belongs to the separate root ن ح س, however, and must not be identified with ح س م.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:25 describes the wind’s destructiveness and the people’s disappearance, supporting the compatibility of the eradicating reading of ḥusūm with the broader context.

Hūd 11:58 describes the rescue of Hūd and those who believed with him. The destruction of the wrongdoing people should therefore not be understood as the death of every person associated with that society while ignoring this Qur’anic exception.

Together, these verses illuminate the punishment’s continuity and destructive outcome. Each word’s precise meaning must still follow its own root, structure, and context.

5. Comparison with related words

Ḥasm and qaṭʿ

قَطَعَ has a broad range in Arabic: cutting, separating, or ending something.

حَسَمَ also concerns cutting off, but many of its uses emphasize ending a process’s continuation or eliminating its effect.

For example, حسم الداء concerns ending a disease or its effect, rather than merely cutting something.

This practical distinction does not mean that قطع never expresses a complete ending, or that حسم always means absolute annihilation.

Study of Q Ṭ ʿ

Ḥasm and batr

بَتَرَ chiefly concerns cutting or severing, and in some uses separating something from its continuation or extension.

Ḥasm can also concern stopping an effect or flow.

For example, cutting a vessel is not the same as stopping bleeding. In the historical medical use of ḥasm, stopping the bleeding is prominent, whether a cut has previously occurred or not.

The two roots overlap in some contexts, and absolute boundaries should not be imposed on all their uses.

Study of B T R

Ḥasm and faṣl

فَصَلَ concerns separating and creating distance or a distinction between things.

Ḥasm is more often used where an ending, severance, or prevention of continuation is at issue.

One action can sometimes be both faṣl and ḥasm: separating something so that a previous connection or effect ends.

Faṣl also has other Qur’anic uses extending beyond cutting; these require study within that root’s own article.

Study of F Ṣ L

Ḥasm and naḥs

Distinguishing حُسُومًا from نَحِسَاتٍ matters for understanding the story of ʿĀd.

In Qur’anic usage, نَحْس concerns a disastrous, adverse condition.

Ḥasm basically concerns cutting off and ending, and in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7 relates to a period of successive devastation.

Their proximity in the story of ʿĀd does not justify translating ḥusūm as ill-omened in every use.

This distinction also prevents those particular days of destruction from being declared inherently unlucky for everyone at all times. The root ح س م itself does not establish such a generalization.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

The following renderings suit the main forms:

حَسَمَ: cut off, ended, prevented something from continuing.

حَسْم: cutting off, ending, eliminating, or settling, according to the subject.

حَاسِم: cutting off, ending, or decisive.

حُسَام: a cutting sword; in some constructions, its cutting edge.

مَحْسُوم: cut off, or something whose flow or continuation has stopped.

انْحَسَمَ: was cut off, ended, or was settled.

حُسُوم: in the Qur’anic use, consecutive and uninterrupted; with eradication as an alternative syntactic and contextual reading.

Translating حُسُومًا in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7

The proposed rendering of:

«سَبْعَ لَيَالٍ وَثَمَانِيَةَ أَيَّامٍ حُسُومًا»

sabʿa layālin wa-thamāniyata ayyāmin ḥusūman

is:

“Seven nights and eight consecutive, uninterrupted days.”

This preserves the preferred reading.

A separate explanation may note the word’s connection with ح س م, cutting off and ending, and the interpretation of some lexicographers as an eradicating punishment.

The two meaning fields for Forqan’s word box are proposed as follows:

Word: حُسُومًا

Root: ح س م

Grammar: accusative; preferably an adjective of the accusative numerical expression describing the period, with alternative analyses as an accusative verbal noun, circumstantial expression, or accusative of purpose.

Brief root meaning: cutting off, ending something’s continuation, or eliminating its effect.

Meaning in this verse: consecutive and uninterrupted, describing the seven nights and eight days of the wind’s domination over ʿĀd.

Here, consecutive is the contextual meaning and should not replace the root’s full semantic range.

Eradicating remains in the explanation of the second possibility, without merging the readings awkwardly in the verse’s direct translation.

7. Conclusion

ح س م concerns cutting off, preventing a process from continuing, and ending something’s effect.

Its uses extend from cutting and preventing to eliminating a disease, stopping bleeding, and settling an issue.

حُسَام, a cutting sword; مَحْسُوم, something whose continuation or nourishment has been cut off; and انْحَسَمَ, being cut off or coming to an end, belong to this semantic range.

Only حُسُومًا occurs from this root in the Qur’an.

In Al-Ḥāqqah 69:7, it describes seven nights and eight days of the wind’s domination over ʿĀd.

The two principal readings are a consecutive, uninterrupted period of punishment and the wind’s eradication of the people. Both have lexical and exegetical support, but the syntax and word position favor consecutive and uninterrupted for translation.

Cutting off and eliminating should nevertheless remain in the root explanation and second possibility.

This root clearly illustrates the difference between basic meaning and contextual usage: cutting off and ending form its core range, while حُسُومًا describes a period of consecutive, destructive punishment in the verse.

Brief definition for Forqan:

Brief root meaning: ح س م means cutting off, stopping something’s continuation, and ending its effect. حُسام, a cutting sword, and حسم, cutting off or settling, belong to this family. In the Qur’an, حُسُومًا describes the seven nights and eight days of ʿĀd’s punishment and preferably means consecutive and uninterrupted, while the eradicating reading also warrants lexical and contextual consideration.
