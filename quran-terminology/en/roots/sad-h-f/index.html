Qur’anic root ص ح ف — Ṣ Ḥ F

Written sheets, writings, an extended surface and a broad dish: two semantic branches connected by openness and breadth of surface

Qur’anic forms

(ṣuḥuf)
Plural of ṣaḥīfah: written sheets and writings; eight occurrences

صِحَاف
(ṣiḥāf)
Plural of ṣaḥfah: broad food dishes; one occurrence

1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

The Arabic root ص ح ف (ṣād ḥāʾ fāʾ) is associated with breadth and an extended surface. Two important words belong to this family: صَحِيفَة (ṣaḥīfah), a sheet or something on which writing is placed, and صَحْفَة (ṣaḥfah), a broad, open vessel, particularly one for holding and serving food.

The Qur’an uses two different forms for these branches: صُحُف (ṣuḥuf) for written sheets and writings, and صِحَاف (ṣiḥāf) for broad dishes. They belong to the same lexical family but do not have the same meaning in Qur’anic usage.

The important forms in this family are:

  • صَحِيفَة (ṣaḥīfah): a sheet or leaf on which something is written; Arabic can also use it for the writing itself. This singular form does not occur in the Qur’an.
  • صُحُف (ṣuḥuf): the plural of ṣaḥīfah: writings, written sheets, or collections of recorded writings. This form occurs eight times in the Qur’an.
  • صَحَائِف (ṣaḥāʾif): another recognized Arabic plural of ṣaḥīfah, absent from the Qur’an.
  • صَحْفَة (ṣaḥfah): a broad, open vessel, usually for food. Its singular does not occur in the Qur’an.
  • صِحَاف (ṣiḥāf): the plural of ṣaḥfah: broad food dishes, occurring in Al-Zukhruf 43:71.
  • مُصْحَف (muṣḥaf): a collection of written sheets gathered into a bound volume; a recognized term in Islamic usage, though this form itself does not occur in the Qur’an.
  • تَصْحِيف (taṣḥīf): an error in reading or transmitting writing, particularly through confusion between similarly shaped letters; a lexical and scholarly use absent from the Qur’an.

The root therefore occurs nine times in the Qur’an, but its occurrences must not all be translated as “writing” or “book.” One explicitly concerns a food vessel.

2. The semantic core and older Arabic usage

Breadth of surface

Arabic lexicons record ص ح ف in association with an open, broad or extended surface. This breadth may concern the ground, a human face, something written upon, or a vessel with a broad opening.

Lexical usage records الصَّحِيف (al-ṣaḥīf) for the surface of the earth and صَحِيفَةُ الْوَجْهِ (ṣaḥīfat al-wajh) for the visible surface of the face. These uses show that this family is not confined to writing.

The existence of these different uses does not establish that they all developed along a particular historical path from a single original meaning. Breadth of surface provides a useful semantic connection, but the precise history of each branch requires independent evidence.

Ṣaḥīfah: a sheet for writing

In its familiar sense, a ṣaḥīfah is something on which writing is placed. The word itself does not specify the material. It may be made from various materials suitable for writing.

Once writing has been placed on such a sheet, ṣaḥīfah can name either the surface bearing it or the writing recorded there. This allows the word, in some contexts, to approach the meaning of a letter, document or written text.

An ordinary expression such as “I read the letter” illustrates this transfer: what is usually read is the written content, rather than the material of the paper. Arabic likewise allows ṣaḥīfah to refer to recorded content without making the material or shape of its carrier the subject of every sentence.

This distinction matters in Qur’anic usage, where the content of the written sheets is sometimes the focus, rather than their physical form.

Ṣaḥfah: a broad, open dish

The other branch is صَحْفَة (ṣaḥfah). Arabic lexical descriptions use it for a broad, relatively shallow vessel, resembling a large food bowl or platter. Its usual plural is صِحَاف (ṣiḥāf).

This use is also recorded in a lexical quotation from a poem attributed to al-Aʿshā, a poet of the late pre-Islamic period:

وَالْمَكَاكِيكُ وَالصِّحَافُ مِنَ الْفِضَّةِ

That is: “And the drinking vessels and broad food dishes are of silver.”

The continuation of the verse has variant readings, but lexical sources quote this passage to illustrate ṣiḥāf as broad vessels. Attribution to al-Aʿshā alone does not establish the precise date when this particular verse was composed.

The significance of this witness is that ṣiḥāf was a recognized Arabic word for actual vessels. Its Qur’anic use does not require a symbolic interpretation.

The relationship between the branches

Ṣaḥīfah and ṣaḥfah differ both in word form and in what they name. In the principal Qur’anic use of the former, it denotes a carrier of writing or recorded content; the latter denotes a food dish.

An extended surface can be recognized in both, but this does not establish that a food dish developed from a book leaf, or that a written sheet was originally a vessel. The available evidence does not demonstrate such an order of development.

3. Ṣuḥuf: earlier revealed writings

An important group of Qur’anic uses of ص ح ف concerns writings that the Qur’an associates with revelation and earlier Prophets.

The earlier sheets in Ṭā Hā

Ṭā Hā 20:133

وَقَالُوا لَوْلَا يَأْتِينَا بِآيَةٍ مِنْ رَبِّهِ ۚ أَوَلَمْ تَأْتِهِمْ بَيِّنَةُ مَا فِي الصُّحُفِ الْأُولَىٰ

“And they said, ‘Why does he not bring us a sign from his Lord?’ Has clear evidence of what is in the earlier written sheets not reached them?”

Here الصُّحُفِ الْأُولَىٰ (al-ṣuḥuf al-ūlā) refers to earlier writings. Their priority is expressed by al-ūlā, “earlier” or “first,” rather than supplied by ṣuḥuf itself.

The passage concerns the content of those writings and the clear evidence raised in connection with them. “The earlier written sheets” or “the earlier writings” is therefore appropriate.

Reading ṣuḥuf as “the earlier books” is understandable in this context, but “books” is a broader equivalent and partly obscures the lexical distinction carried by ṣuḥuf.

The verse does not specify the names, number or physical form of these written sheets.

The written sheets of Moses in Al-Najm

Al-Najm 53:36

أَمْ لَمْ يُنَبَّأْ بِمَا فِي صُحُفِ مُوسَىٰ

“Or has he not been informed of what is in the written sheets of Moses?”

Here ṣuḥuf is associated with Moses. بِمَا فِي (bimā fī), “of what is in,” draws attention to their content: something of which the person should have been informed.

The next verse mentions Abraham. Subsequent verses clarify the message, including that no bearer bears another’s burden and that a human being has nothing except the outcome of their own striving.

In this passage, “the written sheets of Moses” designates writings associated with revelation to Moses. The expression itself does not specify an exact number of sheets, a particular number of separate documents, or a physical format.

Nor can this word alone establish that every detail of Moses’ written sheets is identical with the present text of the Torah. That requires a separate examination of the relationships between ṣuḥuf, kitāb, the Torah and other Qur’anic expressions.

The written sheets of Abraham and Moses in Al-Aʿlā

Al-Aʿlā 87:18

إِنَّ هَٰذَا لَفِي الصُّحُفِ الْأُولَىٰ

“Indeed, this is in the earlier written sheets.”

Al-Aʿlā 87:19

صُحُفِ إِبْرَاهِيمَ وَمُوسَىٰ

“The written sheets of Abraham and Moses.”

Verse nineteen identifies “the earlier written sheets” more closely by naming Abraham and Moses.

This use is especially important because the Qur’an speaks of the written sheets of Abraham and Moses without stating their number or physical form.

In verse eighteen, هَٰذَا (hādhā), “this,” points to content mentioned previously. The nearby verses speak of purifying oneself, remembering the Lord’s name, salat, preferring worldly life, and the greater goodness and lasting nature of the Hereafter.

At least the connection between these teachings and earlier writings is therefore clear. Whether “this” refers to the entire surah or primarily to the nearby teachings is a contextual question; ṣuḥuf itself does not determine that scope.

The central point is that the Qur’an establishes a connection between its message and what was contained in earlier writings. This does not mean that the Qur’an’s entire text, in precisely its present sequence and wording, had been written in the sheets of Abraham and Moses.

4. Honoured and purified sheets: revelation and recitation

In two other uses, ṣuḥuf is accompanied by descriptions expressing honour and purification.

Honoured sheets in ʿAbasa

ʿAbasa 80:13

فِي صُحُفٍ مُكَرَّمَةٍ

“In honoured written sheets.”

This verse follows discussion of a reminder: the preceding verses speak of a reminder that whoever wishes may take to heart.

After verse thirteen, the sheets are described as مرفوعة (marfūʿah), “raised,” and مطهرة (muṭahharah), “purified.” The passage then mentions سفرة (safarah) and their descriptions.

Their honour therefore does not come from the root ص ح ف alone. Ṣuḥuf identifies the written sheets or writings; مكرمة (mukarramah) describes their value and honour.

Nor does this expression by itself establish that the reference must be to a paper book physically available to human beings. The verse expresses the value and standing of the sheets without explaining their material or physical form.

This caution must not erase the recognized meaning of ṣaḥīfah. The image of writing and recording is present in the word; what remains unspecified is the particular form and character of what it denotes here.

Purified sheets in Al-Bayyinah

Al-Bayyinah 98:2

رَسُولٌ مِنَ اللَّهِ يَتْلُو صُحُفًا مُطَهَّرَةً

“A Messenger from The God, reciting purified written sheets.”

The relationship between ṣuḥuf and يَتْلُو (yatlū), “recites,” is especially important here.

In this construction, recitation concerns content that is read and conveyed. When the Qur’an says that the Messenger recites written sheets, the statement must not be confined to reading their material surface. What is recited is their revealed content.

At the same time, ṣuḥuf must not be reduced to “speech” in a way that overlooks its connection with writing. The expression can name written sheets while referring to their recitable content.

مُطَهَّرَة (muṭahharah) comes from another root and describes the sheets as purified. Purity, immunity from error, truth or divine origin cannot be inferred from ص ح ف itself. Such standing follows from their content and the descriptions given by the passage.

The next verse, Al-Bayyinah 98:3, states that these sheets contain كُتُبٌ قَيِّمَةٌ (kutubun qayyimah). The combination of ṣuḥuf and kutub shows a relationship between sheets as a collection or carrier of writing and what is recorded within them.

The expression kutub in that verse nevertheless requires independent study. It must not be restricted in advance to several bound volumes, separate surahs or specified collections of laws.

5. Ṣuḥuf at the Resurrection and in a demand for a sign

Written sheets opened at the Resurrection

Al-Takwīr 81:10

وَإِذَا الصُّحُفُ نُشِرَتْ

“And when the written sheets are unfolded.”

This verse occurs among scenes of the Resurrection. نُشِرَتْ (nushirat) comes from ن ش ر (N SH R) and expresses unfolding or opening.

Ṣuḥuf itself names the sheets or writings; their being unfolded is contributed by the verb nushirat.

Elsewhere in the Qur’an’s descriptions of the Resurrection, it speaks of a written record or book of deeds, including Al-Isrāʾ 17:13, Al-Kahf 18:49, Al-Ḥāqqah 69:19, Al-Ḥāqqah 69:25, Al-Inshiqāq 84:7 and Al-Inshiqāq 84:10. These passages provide important context for understanding the written sheets unfolded at the Resurrection.

Interpreting ṣuḥuf in Al-Takwīr as writings or records of deeds is therefore consistent with the Qur’anic context.

The verse itself does not specify their shape, number or material. Nor does it establish that each ṣaḥīfah must be a separate leaf or a notebook in a familiar earthly form.

What stands out is the disclosure of recorded writings, rather than their material construction.

A demand for unfolded written sheets

Al-Muddaththir 74:52

بَلْ يُرِيدُ كُلُّ امْرِئٍ مِنْهُمْ أَنْ يُؤْتَىٰ صُحُفًا مُنَشَّرَةً

“Rather, every individual among them wishes to be given unfolded written sheets.”

Here صُحُفًا (ṣuḥufan) is described as مُنَشَّرَةً (munashsharatan). This description comes from ن ش ر (N SH R) and expresses the sheets’ unfolded or opened state.

The surrounding verses concern people who turn away from the reminder. Their demand is expressed here as receiving individual, unfolded written sheets.

كُلُّ امْرِئٍ مِنْهُمْ (kullu imriʾin minhum), “every individual among them,” is important to the construction. Each person among them wants this: the demand is individual, rather than merely a request for one public writing for everyone.

Ṣuḥuf here names writings they expect to receive. Their description as unfolded conveys their being open and accessible.

These sheets should not be identified with the sheets of the Resurrection in Al-Takwīr. Both passages use ن ش ر for unfolding them, but their settings differ: one describes the demand of people turning away, the other a scene of the Resurrection.

Details such as each person’s name written on a sheet, the special text of each letter, how it descends, or its precise contents are not stated in the verse itself.

6. Ṣiḥāf: broad dishes in the Qur’an

The sole use of the branch concerning food vessels occurs in Al-Zukhruf.

Al-Zukhruf 43:71

يُطَافُ عَلَيْهِمْ بِصِحَافٍ مِنْ ذَهَبٍ وَأَكْوَابٍ ۖ وَفِيهَا مَا تَشْتَهِيهِ الْأَنْفُسُ وَتَلَذُّ الْأَعْيُنُ ۖ وَأَنْتُمْ فِيهَا خَالِدُونَ

“Broad dishes of gold and cups are passed around them; in it [the Garden] is whatever souls desire and eyes delight in; and you remain therein.”

Here صِحَاف (ṣiḥāf) is the plural of صَحْفَة (ṣaḥfah), rather than of صَحِيفَة (ṣaḥīfah).

This morphological distinction matters directly to translation. Treating every form of ص ح ف as a writing or written sheet would produce an inappropriate image here, where these vessels are mentioned alongside أَكْوَاب (akwāb).

Ṣiḥāf are broad, open dishes, while akwāb names cups and drinking vessels. Their combination helps make the scene of hospitality intelligible.

مِنْ ذَهَبٍ (min dhahabin), “of gold,” specifies the dishes’ material in this verse. Gold is therefore not part of ṣiḥāf’s lexical meaning; such a vessel may be made of other materials.

“Broad food dishes,” “food platters” or “open dishes” can be used in translation. “Plates” conveys part of the meaning but may suggest small, flat modern plates. Since a ṣaḥfah in older Arabic could be a larger, open vessel, “broad food dishes” is more precise and less restrictive.

This does not specify the dimensions, depth, geometry or physical properties of vessels in the Hereafter. The Qur’an presents golden vessels and hospitality without explaining their material details.

7. Related words and translation guidance

ص ح ف (ṣaḥīfah) and ك ت ب (kitāb)

Ṣaḥīfah and kitāb approach one another in some uses, but are not identical.

In its basic use, ṣaḥīfah names a sheet or carrier of writing, and then the writing recorded there. Kitāb has a broader field: depending on construction and context, the Qur’an can use it for writing, a collection of writings, a command, a ruling, or something recorded and prescribed.

A ṣaḥīfah can therefore be related to kitāb through its written content, but the two words are not interchangeable in every verse.

Their distinction is particularly worth considering in Al-Bayyinah 98:2 and Al-Bayyinah 98:3, where ṣuḥuf and kutub occur in consecutive verses. Determining the precise meaning of كُتُبٌ قَيِّمَةٌ (kutubun qayyimah) nevertheless requires study of ك ت ب (K T B).

ص ح ف (ṣaḥīfah) and ق ر ط س (qirṭās)

قِرْطَاس (qirṭās) names writing material or a sheet suitable for writing and occurs in Al-Anʿām 6:7.

By comparison, ṣaḥīfah may encompass both the writing’s carrier and what is written on it. In revelation passages, it is therefore usually understood as more than the mere material on which one writes.

This distinction must not become an absolute boundary. In natural language, the name of a carrier of writing may be transferred to its content, and the name of a writing may also refer to its physical carrier.

ص ح ف (ṣaḥīfah) and ل و ح (lawḥ)

لَوْح (lawḥ) names a tablet or board that can carry writing. In Al-Aʿrāf 7:145, the tablets are mentioned in connection with Moses.

Both ṣaḥīfah and lawḥ can carry writing, but the Qur’an does not express them through the same word form. Lawḥ conveys a more specific image of a tablet or board, while Qur’anic ṣaḥīfah more often draws attention to writing and recorded content.

This comparison alone does not determine the physical material of either the tablets or the sheets.

Ṣuḥuf and ṣiḥāf: preserving the distinction

The most important distinction within this family is between صُحُف (ṣuḥuf) and صِحَاف (ṣiḥāf).

Qur’anic ṣuḥuf names sheets and writings: earlier revealed writings, honoured and purified sheets, or writings opened at the Resurrection.

Qur’anic ṣiḥāf names broad food dishes.

Neither should be translated with the equivalent belonging to the other.

Translation suggestions for Forqan

For صُحُف (ṣuḥuf), “written sheets” is a suitable basic equivalent. Where the focus is the written content, “writings” may be more natural. “Books” should be used only where the context supports it and no important distinction is lost.

For الصُّحُفِ الْأُولَىٰ (al-ṣuḥuf al-ūlā), “the earliest written sheets” or “the earlier written sheets” is suggested.

For صُحُفٍ مُكَرَّمَةٍ (ṣuḥufin mukarramah), “honoured written sheets” is appropriate; for صُحُفًا مُطَهَّرَةً (ṣuḥufan muṭahharah), “purified written sheets.”

In Al-Takwīr 81:10, “the written sheets are unfolded” stays close to the wording. A contextual explanation can clarify its connection with writings and records of deeds.

For صُحُفًا مُنَشَّرَةً (ṣuḥufan munashsharatan) in Al-Muddaththir 74:52, “unfolded written sheets” or “unfolded writings” is appropriate. Their being unfolded does not come from ص ح ف itself.

For صِحَافٍ مِنْ ذَهَبٍ (ṣiḥāfin min dhahabin) in Al-Zukhruf 43:71, “broad dishes of gold” or “golden food platters” is suggested.

8. Conclusion

The Qur’an uses ص ح ف in two clear branches. Ṣuḥuf names written sheets and writings; ṣiḥāf names broad food dishes. Breadth of surface is a recognizable connection between them, but must not become a claim of certain historical derivation or a single meaning imposed on every derivative.

In revelation passages, ṣaḥīfah can convey either a carrier of writing or its recorded content. At the Resurrection, the sheets are associated with the unfolding and disclosure of writings. In the food-vessel use, the reference is to actual, open objects used for hospitality.

A precise definition of this family must therefore include more than books and writing, without excluding the vessel branch. Equally, breadth of surface must not be generalized until each derivative loses its specific meaning.

In translating the Qur’an, first identify the actual word form: ṣuḥuf belongs to ṣaḥīfah, while ṣiḥāf belongs to ṣaḥfah. The descriptions, accompanying verbs and context of each verse then identify which writing, use or vessel is meant.
