The course of celestial bodies and ships sailing on water: two uses sharing consonants, with a possible connection through the imagery of movement

The root in early Arabic

The family ف ل ك has two principal uses in Arabic that should be distinguished from the outset.

فَلَك, with short a vowels after the first two consonants, denotes the orbit or circular course of celestial bodies. In broader classical Arabic usage, it also relates to roundness and circular motion.

فُلْك, with u after the first consonant and no vowel after the second, means a ship: a vessel carrying people and goods on water. It can refer to one ship or several.

Early Arabic sources also record فَلْكَة for a spindle whorl, a round piece that turns with the spindle. The same rounded shape appears in names for certain elevations of land and parts of the body.

An important illustration comes from the Muʿallaqah attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays, the pre-Islamic poet:

كَأَنَّ ذُرَى رَأْسِ الْمُجَيْمِرِ غُدْوَةً
مِنَ السَّيْلِ وَالْغُثَّاءِ فَلْكَةُ مِغْزَلِ

Ka-anna dhurā raʾsi al-Mujaymiri ghudwatan / mina al-sayli wa-al-ghuthāʾi falkatu mighzali.

“At dawn, the summit of al-Mujaymir, amid the flood and the debris it carries, seems like a spindle whorl.”

The poet compares the summit and its surrounding scene to the spindle’s round piece. فَلْكَة here names a familiar everyday object, not an astronomical term.

The verse is attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays in the transmitted tradition of pre-Islamic poetry and appears in the well-known Muʿallaqāt collection. Its preservation in later compilations does not by itself establish the composition date of every line. Its value here is that it illustrates فَلْكَة through the roundness of a tangible object.

Classical Arabic also records فَلَك for the course of stars, celestial rotation, and sometimes the whirling movement of sea waves. These uses show the importance of roundness and circular movement in one branch of the family.

Must فُلْك, “ship,” also have developed from this image of roundness or rotation?

Some lexicographers propose such a connection, suggesting that a ship may be called fulk because it moves or turns in water. This is an etymological possibility, not an established historical conclusion. A ship’s movement is not necessarily circular; its course can be straight or winding.

Proving that the name of a ship arose from roundness, rotation, or the shape of its hull would require independent historical evidence. Shared consonants and comparable images of movement are insufficient.

Qur’anic study must therefore preserve two clear meanings: فَلَك, an orbit or celestial course, and فُلْك, a ship.

Qur’anic forms and linguistic features

This family occurs 25 times in 25 Qur’anic verses, through only two nominal forms:

  • فَلَكٍ in al-Anbiyāʾ 21:33 and Yā Sīn 36:40: an orbit or celestial course.
  • فُلْك, in definite forms and different grammatical cases, in 23 verses: a ship or ships.

Forms such as فَلْكَة, أَفْلَاك, فَلَكَ, فَلَّكَ, and تَفَلَّكَ are known in Arabic, but do not occur in these forms in the Qur’an.

Although فَلَك and فُلْك share consonants, they are two nouns with different vocalizations and uses. They should not be treated as one derivative with two wholly synonymous pronunciations.

Fulk: one ship or several?

Fulk can have the same form in the singular and plural. Morphological explanations vary, including an analysis in which the plural is outwardly identical to the singular.

The Qur’an itself provides direct evidence of this usage.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:119 says:

فَأَنْجَيْنَاهُ وَمَنْ مَعَهُ فِي الْفُلْكِ الْمَشْحُونِ

Fa-anjaynāhu wa-man maʿahu fī al-fulki al-mashḥūn.

“So We saved him and those with him in the laden ship.”

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:119

الْمَشْحُونِ is masculine singular and describes a particular ship.

In Yūnus 10:22, however, كُنْتُمْ فِي الْفُلْكِ is followed by وَجَرَيْنَ بِهِمْ. The verb جَرَيْنَ is feminine plural and refers to the ships carrying the passengers.

Al-Baqarah 2:164 also has الْفُلْكِ الَّتِي تَجْرِي فِي الْبَحْرِ. The feminine relative pronoun and verb accord with Arabic usage of this noun, but feminine agreement alone does not establish that only one ship is intended.

The number of ships is therefore not always apparent from fulk alone; the verb, adjective, pronoun, and context must guide interpretation.

الْمَشْحُون means full or laden in these ship passages. It comes from another root, and fullness or heaviness should not be treated as intrinsic to fulk.

Fulk: ships in human life and livelihood

One major use of fulk concerns its role as a means of transport.

وَعَلَيْهَا وَعَلَى الْفُلْكِ تُحْمَلُونَ

Wa-ʿalayhā wa-ʿalā al-fulki tuḥmalūn.

“And on them and on ships you are carried.”

Al-Muʾminūn 23:22

The pronoun in عَلَيْهَا refers to the livestock mentioned in the preceding verse. Ships are then named separately.

The construction places two familiar means of transport together: animals used by human beings and ships moving on water.

Fulk here clearly means an actual means of carrying people.

Al-Zukhruf 43:12 likewise mentions livestock and ships together. Ghāfir 40:80, after describing benefits from livestock, speaks of being carried on them and on ships.

These uses do not permit fulk to be reduced to an abstract symbol of movement, spiritual travel, or the world’s rotation. Ships are actual vessels in these verses.

Ships in motion and use of the sea

اللَّهُ الَّذِي سَخَّرَ لَكُمُ الْبَحْرَ لِتَجْرِيَ الْفُلْكُ فِيهِ بِأَمْرِهِ وَلِتَبْتَغُوا مِنْ فَضْلِهِ وَلَعَلَّكُمْ تَشْكُرُونَ

Allāhu alladhī sakhkhara lakumu al-baḥra li-tajriya al-fulku fīhi bi-amrihi wa-li-tabtaghū min faḍlihi wa-laʿallakum tashkurūn.

“The God is the One who subjected the sea for you so that ships may sail upon it by His command, that you may seek His bounty, and that you may give thanks.”

Al-Jāthiyah 45:12

Several concepts occur together here: sea, ship, movement, divine command, seeking bounty, and gratitude.

Fulk names the vessel. تَجْرِيَ expresses its flowing movement. فِيهِ, “in it,” refers back to the sea and identifies the setting of movement, while بِأَمْرِهِ connects this possibility with divine command and the order of creation.

Fulk itself does not encompass all these features. Each part of the construction contributes its own meaning.

Al-Baqarah 2:164 also presents ships moving through the sea for people’s benefit as signs for those who reason, alongside the creation of the heavens and earth, the alternation of night and day, rainfall, and the movement of winds.

In Ibrāhīm 14:32, al-Ḥajj 22:65, al-Rūm 30:46, and Luqmān 31:31, the movement of ships likewise relates to subjection, God’s command, or His blessing.

Al-Naḥl 16:14 and Fāṭir 35:12 describe ships as مَوَاخِرَ: advancing through and cleaving the water. This image comes from mawākhir, not from fulk itself meaning “to cleave water.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:66 also connects ships being driven through the sea with seeking divine bounty.

Together, these passages show that ships are more than a means of getting from place to place. People travel in them, transport goods, gain access to resources and opportunities elsewhere, and seek livelihood and bounty through the sea.

Attributing this possibility to God does not negate human effort in building and steering ships. The Qur’an itself speaks of human shipbuilding in the account of Noah.

Ships in motion and danger at sea

Yūnus 10:22 depicts ships initially sailing with a favorable wind and passengers rejoicing, before a stormy wind and formidable waves surround them.

Fulk denotes the same ship in both situations. Changing conditions at sea do not change the word’s meaning.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:65 likewise describes those aboard ships calling upon God sincerely in danger, then changing their conduct after being saved and reaching land.

Luqmān 31:31 presents ships as divine signs. The following verse, Luqmān 31:32, describes waves rising over the passengers like canopies.

These verses place two realities together: people can build and use ships, but they do not control every natural condition.

The subjection of the sea does not mean the absence of danger or guaranteed rescue for every ship. Human benefit from creation’s possibilities exists alongside the limits of human ability.

Fulk in the accounts of Noah and Jonah

Noah’s ship: building and deliverance

وَاصْنَعِ الْفُلْكَ بِأَعْيُنِنَا وَوَحْيِنَا وَلَا تُخَاطِبْنِي فِي الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا إِنَّهُمْ مُغْرَقُونَ

Wa-iṣnaʿi al-fulka bi-aʿyuninā wa-waḥyinā wa-lā tukhāṭibnī fī alladhīna ẓalamū innahum mughraqūn.

“Build the ship under Our watch and by Our revelation, and do not address Me concerning those who have wronged; they will be drowned.”

Hūd 11:37

Fulk here is something built. The verb اصْنَعِ clearly identifies a constructed vessel used for transport and rescue.

Hūd 11:38 continues the account of shipbuilding and the chiefs of Noah’s people ridiculing him. Al-Muʾminūn 23:27 also commands the building of the ship. In al-Muʾminūn 23:28, once Noah and his companions are settled aboard, Noah is told to praise God for their deliverance.

In al-Aʿrāf 7:64 and Yūnus 10:73, the ship is the means of rescue for Noah and his companions. Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:119 describes the same deliverance through الْفُلْكِ الْمَشْحُونِ.

The ship is an actual means of preserving life in these verses. Rescue, construction, loading, and boarding are expressed through separate verbs and descriptions.

Fulk does not itself mean rescue, refuge, or a vessel reserved for Prophets.

Jonah’s ship

In al-Ṣāffāt 37:140, Jonah flees to الْفُلْكِ الْمَشْحُونِ, “the laden ship.”

This ship is also described as laden but belongs to a different narrative. Noah’s ship is the means of rescue for him and his companions; Jonah’s account proceeds through boarding, casting lots, and the subsequent event involving the fish.

Even the shared description mashḥūn does not make the ship’s role identical in every account.

Falak: the course of celestial bodies

The family’s second major Qur’anic branch is فَلَك, with short a vowels after the first two consonants.

It occurs in only two verses, both in the expression كُلٌّ فِي فَلَكٍ يَسْبَحُونَ.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:33

وَهُوَ الَّذِي خَلَقَ اللَّيْلَ وَالنَّهَارَ وَالشَّمْسَ وَالْقَمَرَ كُلٌّ فِي فَلَكٍ يَسْبَحُونَ

Wa-huwa alladhī khalaqa al-layla wa-al-nahāra wa-al-shamsa wa-al-qamara kullun fī falakin yasbaḥūn.

“He is the One who created the night and day, the sun and moon; each floats along in an orbit.”

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:33

The verse mentions night, day, sun, and moon, followed by movement in a falak.

فِي indicates being within a course. Falak names that course or orbit, while يَسْبَحُونَ expresses flowing, swimming movement.

The course must therefore be distinguished from the movement itself. Falak is not a verb meaning “to move”; it identifies the setting or course of movement in the verse’s image.

The course of stars and celestial rotation are recognized classical Arabic senses of falak. The image of roundness and rotation also relates to uses such as فَلْكَة.

Nevertheless, falak in this verse should not be directly equated with a particular astronomical model, such as a solid, transparent sphere carrying a celestial body.

Nor does the word alone specify the course’s precise geometry, speed, center, or physical cause.

Modern knowledge examines celestial paths in relation to differing conditions and forces; not every path is a perfect circle. Such knowledge may broaden our understanding of celestial movement, but modern physical details should not be presented as the direct meaning of falak in the Qur’an.

Yā Sīn 36:40

لَا الشَّمْسُ يَنْبَغِي لَهَا أَنْ تُدْرِكَ الْقَمَرَ وَلَا اللَّيْلُ سَابِقُ النَّهَارِ وَكُلٌّ فِي فَلَكٍ يَسْبَحُونَ

Lā al-shamsu yanbaghī lahā an tudrika al-qamara wa-lā al-laylu sābiqu al-nahāri wa-kullun fī falakin yasbaḥūn.

“It is not for the sun to overtake the moon, nor does the night outstrip the day; and each floats along in an orbit.”

Yā Sīn 36:40

In the surrounding passage, Yā Sīn 36:37 describes night and the appearance of darkness; Yā Sīn 36:38 describes the sun’s movement; and Yā Sīn 36:39 speaks of the moon’s phases.

Yā Sīn 36:40 then describes the relationship of sun and moon, and night and day, by denying overtaking or outstripping. كُلٌّ فِي فَلَكٍ يَسْبَحُونَ connects the sequence with the image of movement in an orbit.

Falak here does more than indicate that movement exists: it depicts movement along a course. The order and temporal relationships among the phenomena, however, come from the sentences together, rather than from the noun alone.

Do night and day also float in a falak?

Both verses mention night and day alongside sun and moon. Two readings therefore concern the scope of كُلٌّ, “each” or “all.”

One takes كُلٌّ to refer to all the phenomena mentioned. Movement of night and day then concerns their alternation and progression within creation’s order, without making them necessarily independent celestial bodies.

The other takes movement in an orbit to refer directly to sun and moon, with night and day providing the setting in which the two bodies’ movement and relationship are described.

Both readings must be assessed through syntax and context. Naming four phenomena before كُلٌّ supports a broad scope; the familiar use of celestial courses for bodies and the descriptive construction also allow examination of a focus on sun and moon.

In either case, the verse alone does not establish that night and day are two material bodies with independent orbits.

يَسْبَحُونَ also has a masculine plural subject ending. Arabic can use this rhetorical and grammatical construction when depicting celestial movement through actions resembling those of living beings. It does not by itself establish human-like awareness or independent will in celestial bodies.

Does yasbaḥūn mean swimming in water?

س ب ح has a familiar image of swimming and flowing movement. يَسْبَحُونَ alongside falak can portray the free, flowing motion of celestial bodies through an image resembling swimming.

Floating in this expression does not necessarily establish the presence of material water in the environment of movement.

A verb used for movement in water can also describe flowing movement in another setting. Applying it to celestial bodies helps portray their motion; it does not determine the substance of the environment through which they move.

Forqan’s study of the Qur’anic root س ب ح explains this distinction in greater detail.

Falak and fulk in consecutive verses of Yā Sīn

A notable feature of this family is the appearance of its two forms in consecutive verses of Yā Sīn.

In Yā Sīn 36:40, falak concerns a course of movement.

Immediately afterward, Yā Sīn 36:41 says:

وَآيَةٌ لَهُمْ أَنَّا حَمَلْنَا ذُرِّيَّتَهُمْ فِي الْفُلْكِ الْمَشْحُونِ

Wa-āyatun lahum annā ḥamalnā dhurriyyatahum fī al-fulki al-mashḥūn.

“And a sign for them is that We carried their offspring in the laden ship.”

Yā Sīn 36:41

The first verse concerns celestial movement; the second, ships and carrying people.

Their proximity allows a comparison of images: celestial bodies float along their courses, and ships move through the sea. Both scenes involve movement, but its kind and setting differ.

Yā Sīn 36:42 also mentions other means that people ride, while Yā Sīn 36:43 recalls the possibility of drowning.

The context following Yā Sīn 36:41 thus clearly concerns transport and the danger of drowning. These indications support the maritime meaning of fulk.

By contrast, Yā Sīn 36:38 and Yā Sīn 36:39 concern sun and moon and support the celestial meaning of falak.

Their proximity may invite reflection on celestial and maritime movement, but does not establish that “ship” actually names a celestial body or that the “sea” in ship passages necessarily means outer space.

If such an allegorical reading is proposed, it requires further evidence and must remain distinct from direct translation.

Related words and distinctions

ف ل ك (fulk) and س ف ن (safīnah)

Both fulk and safīnah denote ships in the Qur’an.

The Noah narrative uses fulk, while al-ʿAnkabūt 29:15 recalls the same event with safīnah. This overlap shows that they cannot be treated as entirely different vessels.

Safīnah also occurs in the account of Moses and his knowledgeable companion, including al-Kahf 18:71 and al-Kahf 18:79.

Their different roots warrant linguistic historical investigation, but both can name a vessel for transport on water in Qur’anic usage.

The difference does not establish that fulk always means a large ship and safīnah always a small boat.

For the other family, see Forqan’s study of the root س ف ن.

ف ل ك (fulk) and ج ر ي (jarā)

ج ر ي expresses flowing movement or running.

In لِتَجْرِيَ الْفُلْكُ, the ship is what moves, while tajrī describes its sailing or flowing movement.

The same family describes the sun’s movement in passages such as Yā Sīn 36:38. Jarayān can therefore express the movement of a ship or a celestial body.

Sharing a verb of movement does not establish that ships and celestial bodies have the same nature or environment.

Fulk names a ship, falak a course or orbit, and jarā flowing movement. These are independent semantic roles.

For further discussion, see Forqan’s study of the root ج ر ي.

ف ل ك (falak) and د و ر (dāra)

د و ر in Arabic concerns turning, returning, and circular movement.

Celestial falak can relate to the image of circular motion, but the noun itself is not the verb “to circle.”

The Qur’an expresses the course through falak and the movement through yasbaḥūn. A noun and verb with related imagery are not necessarily synonymous.

ف ل ك (fulk) and ب ح ر (baḥr)

Fulk is a ship, while baḥr is the body of water on which a ship can travel.

In الْفُلْكِ الَّتِي تَجْرِي فِي الْبَحْرِ, vessel, movement, and setting are three independent elements.

Forqan’s study of the root ب ح ر examines uses involving the sea, travel, provision, danger, and movement.

A ship floating on the sea can illustrate flowing movement, but baḥr should not be translated as celestial space without contextual evidence.

ف ل ك (fulk) and س خ ر (sakhkhara)

The subjection branch of س خ ر concerns making something available for use or service.

In al-Jāthiyah 45:12, the sea’s subjection accompanies the ships’ movement. The relationship expresses human benefit from the possibilities of creation.

Fulk does not mean a subjugating device or instrument of domination. A ship is a vessel; its relationship to creation’s order and divine command comes from the complete sentence.

Translation proposals for Forqan

The family’s two principal forms must remain clearly distinct in Qur’anic translation.

فَلَكٍ: “an orbit,” or where needed, “a celestial course.”

In al-Anbiyāʾ 21:33 and Yā Sīn 36:40, “floating along in an orbit” is a natural rendering of فِي فَلَكٍ يَسْبَحُونَ.

Further explanation belongs in the study or reflection: orbit here concerns the course of movement and should not be assigned a particular physical structure without evidence.

فُلْك: “ship” or “ships.”

“Ship” is appropriate where a specific vessel is discussed in the Noah and Jonah narratives.

“Ships” is more natural in general descriptions of maritime transport, such as al-Baqarah 2:164, al-Naḥl 16:14, and al-Jāthiyah 45:12.

الْفُلْكِ الْمَشْحُونِ: “the laden ship” or “the filled ship.”

الْفُلْكِ الَّتِي تَجْرِي فِي الْبَحْرِ: “the ships that sail through the sea.”

الْفُلْكَ مَوَاخِرَ: “ships cleaving the water.”

In this last construction, cleaving the water is expressed by mawākhir; it should not be presented as the meaning of ف ل ك itself.

Nor should falak be replaced in basic translation by names such as planet, star, solar system, or galaxy. In its two relevant verses, it names a course of movement, not the body moving along it.

Conclusion

ف ل ك occurs in the Qur’an through two principal nouns: falak and fulk.

Falak concerns an orbit or celestial course. In al-Anbiyāʾ 21:33 and Yā Sīn 36:40, it accompanies yasbaḥūn, depicting celestial bodies moving fluidly along their courses.

Fulk names a ship. The Qur’an uses it in the accounts of Noah and Jonah and in passages about travel, transport, trade, benefit from the sea, and the danger of drowning. It can denote one ship or several, with number determined by context.

Roundness and circular movement are recognized features of the falak branch in early Arabic. The spindle-whorl image in the verse attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays likewise illustrates roundness through a tangible object.

A historical derivation of the ship’s name from that same image has not been conclusively established. Shared consonants and comparable movements do not suffice to prove a common origin.

The proximity of falak and fulk in consecutive verses of Yā Sīn invites reflection on celestial and maritime movement, while each word retains its clear contextual meaning.

Final definition for Forqan:

ف ل ك has two nominal branches in Qur’anic usage:

فَلَك: an orbit or circular celestial course, without a definitive specification of its physical structure or exact geometry.

فُلْك: a ship, carrying people and cargo on water; it may denote one vessel or a group of vessels.

Roundness and movement offer a possible connection within the family’s imagery, but its two Qur’anic meanings must be understood independently. Their historical relationship should not be asserted beyond the available evidence.
