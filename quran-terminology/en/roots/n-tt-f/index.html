Nuṭfah in early Arabic, its connection with dripping and water, and its Qur’anic uses in human formation and growth

The three-consonant Arabic root N TT F concerns liquid dripping, moisture and water. Naṭafa describes water dripping or trickling. Nuṭfah denotes a small quantity of water, clear water and, in reproduction, the initial material from which a child develops.

The Qur’an uses only the noun nuṭfah from this root, always in connection with human formation. It sometimes places it alongside dust and subsequent developmental stages, sometimes relates it to semen, and elsewhere uses nuṭfatin amshāj, a mixed nuṭfah.

Nuṭfah should not immediately be equated with a modern biological definition. A drop of water, semen, a sperm cell, an egg and a fertilized egg are distinct terms. Understanding requires first establishing Arabic usage, then examining Qur’anic contexts, and finally considering scientific explanations that may help identify a referent without replacing the word’s meaning.

1. Semantic core and forms

Important recorded Arabic forms include:

  • نَطَفَ: dripped or trickled.
  • نَطْف: dripping, pouring or liquid forming drops.
  • نُطْفَة: a little water, clear water and, in reproduction, nuṭfah.
  • نُطَف و نِطَاف: recorded plurals of nuṭfah, with differences in use.
  • نُطُوف و نَطَفَان: dripping and trickling.
  • نَطُوف: something dripping, such as a night of continuing rain.
  • نَطَّاف و نَاطِف: forms with various uses in this family of dripping and liquids.

Related words also concern pearls, earrings and being tainted by fault or corruption. Those branches require their own treatment rather than reducing the entire family to reproduction.

Only nuṭfah occurs in the Qur’an: twelve times in twelve verses across eleven surahs. Nuṭfatin, nuṭfatan and al-nuṭfata differ in grammatical role and definiteness; they are not three independent derivatives.

The semantic core

The water-related branch includes dripping or a small amount of liquid, and water being clear and pure.

These are connected without being identical. A small amount need not be clear, while clear water can be plentiful. Lexicographers record both uses, with differing explanations of which feature predominates in particular contexts.

The reproductive-fluid use matters most for the Qur’anic occurrences. Here nuṭfah refers to material or a quantity of liquid associated with a child’s formation.

A general sense of a little water or a liquid drop can therefore be distinguished from the particular reproductive use. The Qur’anic use cannot always become ordinary water, just as every general Arabic use should not become semen.

2. Early Arabic: water in a waterskin and pearls

Water dripping

A clear use of the verb describes liquid dripping from a vessel or another object.

For example:

نَطَفَ الْمَاءُ

naṭafa al-māʾu

Translation: “The water dripped.”

A waterskin leaking through a split or hole can likewise be described as:

الْقِرْبَةُ تَنْطِفُ

al-qirbatu tanṭifu

Translation: “The waterskin drips.”

These expressions describe dripping, without necessarily specifying purity, exact quantity, temperature or value.

Laylah naṭūf is recorded for a night in which rain continues until morning. Naṭūf describes dripping or rainfall rather than one particular drop.

These uses explain the family’s connection with liquids without proving a single historical image from which every meaning originated.

Nuṭfah as a little water and clear water

Arabic sources also use nuṭfah for a little water remaining in a waterskin, bucket or other vessel.

A line attributed to ʿAbd Allāh ibn Rawāḥah, a poet of the early Islamic period, states:

هَلْ أَنْتِ إِلَّا نُطْفَةٌ فِي شَنَّةٍ

hal anti illā nuṭfatun fī shannatin

Translation: “Are you anything but a little water in an old waterskin?”

The poet addresses his own self, using nuṭfah for a small amount of liquid in a waterskin.

The witness belongs to the early Islamic period rather than pre-Islamic poetry. It shows a general, nontechnical usage close to the Qur’an’s linguistic setting.

Another Arabic report describes water from a plentiful well as a cool nuṭfah. Lexicographers cite it to show that the word can also describe abundant, clear water.

A small quantity is therefore a common meaning without making a single tiny drop the only possible Arabic use.

Naṭaf, pearls and earrings

Another branch concerns pearls and ornaments. Naṭaf and nuṭaf are recorded for clear or small pearls and earrings.

The famous Waddiʿ Hurayrata ode attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Aʿshā includes:

يَسْعَى بِهَا ذُو زُجَاجَاتٍ لَهُ نُطَفٌ

yasʿā bihā dhū zujājātin lahu nuṭafun

مُقَلِّصٌ أَسْفَلَ السِّرْبَالِ مُعْتَمِلُ

muqalliṣun asfala al-sirbāli muʿtamilu

Translation: “It is carried around by a man with glass vessels and earrings; he has hitched up the lower part of his garment and is busy at work.”

The description concerns a server carrying a drink. Nuṭaf denotes his ornaments or earrings rather than reproductive fluid.

This attests uses of the family outside the reproductive sense in pre-Islamic Arabic.

Some lexical explanations compare a small pearl or ornament to a water drop in shape and clarity. That can explain a possible semantic connection without establishing the word’s historical origin.

Several meanings being grouped under one lexical entry does not prove that all historically branched from one original sense.

Other branches

Lexical accounts also record uses concerning fault, corruption or damage. Naṭifa can describe being tainted by a defect or bad reputation.

These uses show why a very simple, supposedly comprehensive definition would not cover every branch.

One early lexical analysis distinguishes two principal branches: ornaments, and moisture or wetness. Uses concerning taint and fault are also examined as semantic extensions.

Water and reproductive fluid are central to the Qur’anic use, without justifying deletion or forced assimilation of the other branches.

3. Nuṭfah in the Qur’an: the beginning of human creation

All Qur’anic uses concern human formation, but the passages’ purposes differ. Some contrast humble beginnings with pride; others describe development or invoke creation as evidence of resurrection’s possibility.

From nuṭfah to a disputing human

خَلَقَ الْإِنسَانَ مِن نُّطْفَةٍ فَإِذَا هُوَ خَصِيمٌ مُّبِينٌ

khalaqa al-insāna min nuṭfatin fa-idhā huwa khaṣīmun mubīnun

Translation: “He created the human from a nuṭfah, and suddenly he is an open disputant.”

Al-Naḥl 16:4

The verse places the beginning from nuṭfah beside becoming a being capable of openly disputing.

Yā Sīn 36:77 has a similar construction:

أَوَلَمْ يَرَ الْإِنسَانُ أَنَّا خَلَقْنَاهُ مِن نُّطْفَةٍ فَإِذَا هُوَ خَصِيمٌ مُّبِينٌ

a-wa-lam yara al-insānu annā khalaqnāhu min nuṭfatin fa-idhā huwa khaṣīmun mubīnun

Translation: “Has the human not seen that We created him from a nuṭfah? Yet suddenly he is an open disputant.”

The following verses in Yā Sīn concern denial that decayed bones can live again and the Qur’an’s response about renewed creation.

Nuṭfah is thus more than a reproductive description here: it recalls a familiar origin to direct attention to the possibility of creation anew.

Disputing, pride and denial of resurrection do not belong to nuṭfah’s lexical meaning; the sentence and context convey them.

ʿAbasa 80:17, ʿAbasa 80:18 and ʿAbasa 80:19 likewise rebuke ungrateful humanity and ask from what it was created:

مِن نُّطْفَةٍ خَلَقَهُ فَقَدَّرَهُ

min nuṭfatin khalaqahu fa-qaddarahu

Translation: “From a nuṭfah He created him and proportioned him.”

ʿAbasa 80:19

Nuṭfah is the beginning in the verse’s argument. Fa-qaddarahu adds proportioning and ordering, rather than those meanings coming from N TT F itself.

In Al-Kahf 18:37, one speaker also challenges the other by recalling creation from dust and then nuṭfah. The context counters pride in wealth and position by recalling human origins.

Nuṭfah in formation and growth

ثُمَّ جَعَلْنَاهُ نُطْفَةً فِي قَرَارٍ مَّكِينٍ

thumma jaʿalnāhu nuṭfatan fī qarārin makīnin

Translation: “Then We placed him as a nuṭfah in a secure resting place.”

Al-Muʾminūn 23:13

Nuṭfah here accompanies fī qarārin makīn.

Qarār concerns a place of settlement; makīn describes its firmness or secure establishment. In human development, the womb is the familiar referent of this resting and growing place.

Nuṭfah does not itself mean something in the womb. The accompanying phrase identifies the location.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:14 states:

ثُمَّ خَلَقْنَا النُّطْفَةَ عَلَقَةً فَخَلَقْنَا الْعَلَقَةَ مُضْغَةً

thumma khalaqnā al-nuṭfata ʿalaqatan fa-khalaqnā al-ʿalaqata muḍghatan

Translation: “Then We formed the nuṭfah into an ʿalaqah, and formed the ʿalaqah into a muḍghah.”

Al-Muʾminūn 23:14

Nuṭfah is a stage in human formation, followed in the passage by ʿalaqah and muḍghah.

The sequence distinguishes states of development. Their particular features require separate study of AYN L Q and M DAD GH, alongside consideration of how Qur’anic descriptions relate to modern scientific terms.

Nuṭfah alone does not define a biological stage in every modern technical detail.

Al-Ḥajj 22:5 and Ghāfir 40:67 present a similar sequence: dust, then nuṭfah, then ʿalaqah, followed by further stages of human life.

Fāṭir 35:11 also mentions dust followed by nuṭfah and then people being made into pairs.

Dust and nuṭfah

Several verses place dust and nuṭfah in sequence. Does this describe every individual coming directly from dust, or humanity’s first origin followed by continued reproduction?

Al-Sajdah 32:7 and Al-Sajdah 32:8 help clarify the question: they distinguish the beginning of human creation from clay from descendants being made from an extract of lowly fluid.

“مِن تُرَابٍ ثُمَّ مِن نُّطْفَةٍ” should therefore not immediately be taken to mean that every individual’s body first existed as separate dust and then became reproductive fluid.

The contexts and parallel passages can encompass both humanity’s first origin and the continuation of its descendants.

This helps explain the sequence without changing nuṭfah’s sense as material associated with reproduction’s beginning.

4. Nuṭfah and semen: are they identical?

Two passages make nuṭfah’s relation to semen or emission clearer.

أَلَمْ يَكُ نُطْفَةً مِّن مَّنِيٍّ يُمْنَىٰ

a-lam yaku nuṭfatan min maniyyin yumnā

Translation: “Was he not a nuṭfah from emitted semen?”

Al-Qiyāmah 75:37

Nuṭfatan and maniyyin are separate words connected by min, from.

This directly relates nuṭfah to semen; it can be understood here as a quantity of seminal fluid.

They are not complete synonyms across Arabic usage. Semen names a particular reproductive fluid, while nuṭfah also has more general uses.

Al-Najm 53:45 and Al-Najm 53:46 also state:

وَأَنَّهُ خَلَقَ الزَّوْجَيْنِ الذَّكَرَ وَالْأُنثَىٰ ۝ مِن نُّطْفَةٍ إِذَا تُمْنَىٰ

wa-annahu khalaqa al-zawjayni al-dhakara wa-al-unthā / min nuṭfatin idhā tumnā

Translation: “And that He created the two counterparts, male and female, from a nuṭfah when it is emitted.”

Tumnā in Al-Najm 53:46 concerns fluid being emitted in the reproductive setting.

These occurrences are more specific than the general meaning of a little water, since the fluid relates to human reproduction.

Nuṭfah and reproductive cells

Modern biology distinguishes semen, sperm cells, eggs and fertilized eggs.

Semen contains fluids and other components, which can include sperm. An egg is a female reproductive cell, and a fertilized egg forms through the joining of reproductive cells.

None is the certain, universal equivalent of the Arabic word nuṭfah.

Nuṭfatan min maniyyin clearly connects nuṭfah with semen, without alone identifying precisely one sperm cell, all the seminal fluid or a cell after fertilization.

A nuṭfah placed in a secure resting place and undergoing subsequent development may describe a state in embryonic formation. Equating it with a precise cellular term nevertheless requires independent evidence.

It is preferable to retain nuṭfah in the basic Persian translation and explain it as an amount or material of reproductive fluid. This preserves the familiar Qur’anic use without arbitrarily restricting it to one modern technical term.

5. Nuṭfatin amshāj: a mixed nuṭfah

إِنَّا خَلَقْنَا الْإِنسَانَ مِن نُّطْفَةٍ أَمْشَاجٍ نَّبْتَلِيهِ فَجَعَلْنَاهُ سَمِيعًا بَصِيرًا

innā khalaqnā al-insāna min nuṭfatin amshājin nabtalīhi fa-jaʿalnāhu samīʿan baṣīran

Translation: “We created the human from a mixed nuṭfah, testing him, and made him hearing and seeing.”

Al-Insān 76:2

This is particularly relevant to nuṭfah’s limits because it adds amshāj as a description.

Amshāj belongs to M SH J and concerns mixing. Mashīj describes something mixed with something else; amshāj is a plural formation in the family.

Why is nuṭfah singular but amshāj plural?

Nuṭfah is singular in the phrase, while amshāj has plural form.

This can suggest that something expressed as one unit contains mingled parts or materials. Arabic can describe a single thing with a plural expression in view of its internal components.

A mixed nuṭfah is therefore a natural, defensible rendering; an explanation can add that several parts or materials may be involved.

One familiar early interpretation identifies a mixture of male and female fluid. Other explanations concern different constituents of the initial material or its changing developmental states.

Amshāj does not itself name the substances mixed. Its direct sense is mixtures or having mingled components.

The verse alone therefore does not determine a precise cellular or chemical composition, nor justify restricting mixture to one particular account without evidence.

Is mixture part of nuṭfah’s own meaning?

The phrase presents another important point.

Even if nuṭfah necessarily meant mixed material in every use, amshāj could emphasize that feature. In any case, the description comes from another root and contributes a meaning that should not automatically enter nuṭfah’s basic definition.

Word-by-word analysis should therefore distinguish:

Nuṭfah: initial reproductive material.

Amshāj: its mixed condition.

Together they describe a mixed nuṭfah, while each component retains its own meaning.

This avoids translating nuṭfah everywhere as a mixture of several materials, or ignoring amshāj in Al-Insān 76:2.

The connection with human testing

After the nuṭfah amshāj phrase, the verse states:

نَّبْتَلِيهِ فَجَعَلْنَاهُ سَمِيعًا بَصِيرًا

nabtalīhi fa-jaʿalnāhu samīʿan baṣīran

Translation: “We test him, and made him hearing and seeing.”

Al-Insān 76:3 describes showing the way and the possibility of gratitude or ingratitude.

The passage therefore concerns more than bodily formation: it describes a created being endowed with perceptual capacities and subject to testing and choice.

Testing, hearing, sight and moral choice do not belong to nuṭfah’s lexical meaning. Other clauses introduce those capacities and stages.

6. Related Qur’anic words

N TT F and M N Y: nuṭfah and semen

The Qur’anic nuṭfah concerns initial reproductive fluid, while manī denotes a familiar fluid in the same field.

Nuṭfah has a broader Arabic range, including a little liquid or clear water. Manī is a more specific name for emitted reproductive fluid in these uses.

Their pairing in Al-Qiyāmah 75:37 establishes a close relationship without erasing their linguistic differences.

Study of M N Y

N TT F and S L L: nuṭfah and an extract

Al-Sajdah 32:8 states:

ثُمَّ جَعَلَ نَسْلَهُ مِن سُلَالَةٍ مِّن مَّاءٍ مَّهِينٍ

thumma jaʿala naslahu min sulālatin min māʾin mahīnin

Translation: “Then He made his descendants from an extract of lowly fluid.”

Sulālah belongs to S L L and concerns something drawn out or extracted.

Māʾ is the material under discussion; sulālah describes something taken or separated from it.

This relates to the nuṭfah passages without making the terms complete synonyms. One concerns initial reproductive material in Qur’anic use; the other something extracted from material.

Nuṭfah should therefore not always become extract merely to preserve an image belonging to S L L.

N TT F and M W H: nuṭfah and water

Māʾ is the general word for water, with broad Qur’anic uses including rain, drinking water and fluids involved in human formation.

Nuṭfah can denote a kind or quantity of water or liquid, but not every water is called nuṭfah.

Al-Mursalāt 77:20 states:

أَلَمْ نَخْلُقكُّم مِّن مَّاءٍ مَّهِينٍ

a-lam nakhluqkum min māʾin mahīnin

Translation: “Did We not create you from lowly fluid?”

Its human-formation context clearly relates to nuṭfah’s uses.

Mahīn supplies the lowly or slight-valued description. It should not become an invariant feature of nuṭfah, nor make the word always mean impure or base fluid.

Study of M W H

N TT F and AYN L Q: nuṭfah and ʿalaqah

Formation passages place nuṭfah and ʿalaqah in sequence.

This distinguishes an initial nuṭfah state from a later ʿalaqah state.

The precise meaning of ʿalaqah requires its own root, form and uses. The later stage’s features should not be folded into nuṭfah from the outset.

The sequence alone also does not specify each stage’s exact duration.

7. Translation questions and common interpretations

Does nuṭfah always mean just one drop?

Drop is a close, natural equivalent in some uses, particularly where a small amount of fluid is intended.

Arabic also uses nuṭfah for clear water, without always restricting it to exactly one drop.

Small quantity and the connection with dripping should be explained without turning the Qur’anic image into a precise measurement unit.

Does nuṭfah always mean only sperm?

No. The Arabic word is not the technical name of one biological cell.

Some passages explicitly connect nuṭfah with semen. Others concern human beginnings and growth; Al-Insān 76:2 also adds a description of mixture.

They clearly relate nuṭfah to reproduction without warranting an unexplained restriction to sperm in every verse.

The same caution applies to identifying it universally with an egg or a fertilized egg.

Does nuṭfah itself mean lowly or impure?

Some verses describe the fluid of human beginnings as mahīn; others contrast human beginnings with subsequent pride and dispute.

Lowliness comes from the relevant adjective and context, rather than every use of N TT F.

Its use for clear water particularly cautions against making impurity, filth or contempt part of its universal meaning.

Many passages recall human origins to direct attention to creation, the Lord’s power, responsibility and resurrection. That purpose should not become an imposed negative definition of nuṭfah itself.

Does nuṭfah itself convey every developmental stage?

No. It is an initial point in a process; other words and verbs, including ʿalaqah, muḍghah, khalaqa and jaʿala, describe what follows.

Studying embryonic development can help identify natural referents and processes, but a lexical nuṭfah study should not become an account of all embryology.

The scope is first the word and Qur’anic expressions, followed, where needed, by independent scientific examination of the phenomena discussed.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

Naṭafa, concerning water or liquid, can naturally be rendered as dripped or trickled.

For general Arabic nuṭfah, a little water, a liquid drop or clear water can fit the context. All these senses should not be combined in every translation.

Since every Qur’anic occurrence concerns human formation, retaining the familiar Persian nuṭfah is the best short equivalent. It conveys reproduction without forcing a decision in each verse about exact fluid volume or cellular identity.

For example:

خَلَقَ الْإِنسَانَ مِن نُّطْفَةٍ

khalaqa al-insāna min nuṭfatin

“He created the human from a nuṭfah.”

Al-Naḥl 16:4

ثُمَّ جَعَلْنَاهُ نُطْفَةً فِي قَرَارٍ مَّكِينٍ

thumma jaʿalnāhu nuṭfatan fī qarārin makīnin

“Then We placed him as a nuṭfah in a secure resting place.”

Al-Muʾminūn 23:13

أَلَمْ يَكُ نُطْفَةً مِّن مَّنِيٍّ يُمْنَىٰ

a-lam yaku nuṭfatan min maniyyin yumnā

“Was he not a nuṭfah from emitted semen?”

Al-Qiyāmah 75:37

مِن نُّطْفَةٍ أَمْشَاجٍ

min nuṭfatin amshājin

“From a mixed nuṭfah.”

Al-Insān 76:2

Forqan’s brief explanation can distinguish two levels:

Brief root meaning: Liquid dripping; a little or clear water, and reproductive nuṭfah in its particular context.

Meaning in the Qur’anic verses: Initial reproductive material or a quantity of it from which human formation is described.

In Al-Insān 76:2, mixture should remain in the explanation of amshāj rather than become nuṭfah’s invariant definition.

In Al-Qiyāmah 75:37, the specific semen connection should be explained locally without changing every other occurrence to male semen.

9. Conclusion

N TT F has several Arabic branches. The branch relevant to the Qur’an includes dripping liquid, a little water, clear water and reproductive nuṭfah. Pearls, earrings and certain fault-related meanings also occur, without sufficient historical evidence to force them all into one certain origin.

The Qur’an uses only nuṭfah from this family, always for human formation. Some verses contrast it with the human’s later state; others place it in formation sequences. Two passages connect it with semen or fluid emission, while Al-Insān 76:2 describes it as mixed.

Nuṭfah thus concerns initial reproductive material in Qur’anic use, without independently specifying every detail of origin, composition, location, development or cellular structure. Other words, context and independent inquiry supply those details.

Final definition: In its liquid-related branch, N TT F concerns dripping, moisture and water, particularly a small quantity or clear water. The Qur’anic nuṭfah denotes initial human reproductive material or a quantity of it, variously connected with semen, mixture and development. Retaining nuṭfah is appropriate in Persian, provided it is not arbitrarily equated with one precise cell type or technical developmental stage.
