Passing years, the effects of time, and changes in food and drink

The Arabic family S N H relates to years, the passage of time, and changes occurring over time. Its attested uses concern passing years, changes in trees and crops across years, and food or drink losing its former quality through age and storage.

Under the classification adopted here, the root occurs once in the Quran, as yatasannah in Al-Baqarah 2:259. The verse describes someone whom God caused to die for a hundred years and then raised. His food and drink had not undergone the expected alteration despite that long interval.

In this usage, tasannaha means to change through the passage of time, especially for food or drink to deteriorate through remaining unused.

This relates to sanah, “year,” whose historical structure was disputed by early Arabic grammarians. Some reconstructed sanhah, others sanwah. S N H and S N W must therefore remain distinguishable, without merging their Quranic forms without examination.

1. Early Arabic usage and an ancient poetic example

Time and passing years

Early lexical analyses connect S N H principally with time and the passage of years.

Sanah denotes a year. Related expressions such as sanahat al-nakhlatu concern a date palm upon which years have passed; other forms concern changes in its annual yield.

Tasannaha can likewise describe something affected by the passage of time.

A year here is not merely a number, but a unit of time during which people, plants, food, land, and other things may change.

This relationship was tangible in farming and herding. A tree may bear plentifully one year and less the next; produce may be fresh in one season but lose quality after prolonged storage.

Nevertheless, the passage of time and undesirable alteration are distinct. Something a year or several years old is not necessarily spoiled. Deterioration becomes prominent where the verb and its subject indicate such change.

An ancient poem: palms and difficult years

A poem attributed to Suwayd ibn al-Ṣāmit, an Arab poet near the emergence of Islam, says:

لَيْسَتْ بِسَنْهَاءَ وَلَا رُجَّبِيَّةٍ
وَلَكِنْ عَرَايَا فِي السِّنِينَ الْجَوَائِحِ

laysat bi-sanhāʾa wa-lā rujjabiyyatin / wa-lākin ʿarāyā fī al-sinīni al-jawāʾiḥi

“These palms are neither irregular bearers nor palms requiring supports; rather, their fruit is granted to others in devastating years.”

Sanhāʾ in this example concerns the condition of a date palm across years.

Lexicons record it for a palm bearing one year but not the next; another explanation describes a palm affected by drought.

These explanations differ but both concern the tree's changing condition and annual circumstances.

Al-sinīn al-jawāʾiḥ in the same line denotes harsh, damaging years.

The line is attributed to Suwayd ibn al-Ṣāmit in early sources and, in some reports, to Uḥayḥah ibn al-Julāḥ. Its final word also has variants. Its attribution and wording should be understood with these limitations.

The example places sanhāʾ within the concrete setting of farming, palms, and changing annual conditions.

It does not establish drought or infertility as the original meaning of the entire family. These are particular uses related to time and its effects.

Year and drought

Alongside its general meaning “year,” al-sanah can refer to a harsh year of drought.

This is understandable in a society dependent on rain, pasture, and harvests.

Shortages of rain and food may dominate life so strongly that sanah alone, in particular constructions, denotes a difficult year.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:130 says:

وَلَقَدْ أَخَذْنَا آلَ فِرْعَوْنَ بِالسِّنِينَ وَنَقْصٍ مِنَ الثَّمَرَاتِ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَذَّكَّرُونَ

wa-laqad akhadhnā āla firʿawna bi-al-sinīna wa-naqṣin mina al-thamarāti laʿallahum yadhdhakkarūn

Al-Aʿrāf 7:130

“We certainly afflicted Pharaoh's people with harsh years and diminished fruits, so that they might take heed.”

Here al-sinīn accompanies reduced produce, supporting the meaning of harsh years and drought.

Sinīn does not mean drought throughout the Quran. In Yūsuf 12:47, it concerns seven years of sustained cultivation; in Al-Kahf 18:25, it expresses a duration of residence.

A year is therefore broader than famine or drought. Hardship derives from particular usage and context, not from the number of years itself.

Likewise, the connection of yatasannah with years does not alone establish spoilage. Its use for food and drink supplies the sense of deterioration.

2. Saniha al-ṭaʿāmu: food changing with time

Arabic lexicons record the following expressions:

Saniha al-ṭaʿāmu: the food changed through remaining stored.

Saniha al-sharābu: the drink lost its former quality.

Tasannaha al-ṭaʿāmu: the food changed or spoiled with time.

Ṭaʿāmun sanihun and khubzun mutasannihun likewise describe aged food that has deteriorated.

Lexical explanations also mention takarruj, a condition affecting bread and some drinks and associated with deterioration.

In food contexts, tasannuh thus denotes not simply passing years but their effect on the food.

Remaining stored versus spoiling

Food can remain for some time and still be usable.

Drying, suitable storage conditions, and other preservation methods may reduce or delay deterioration.

Simply remaining unused does not mean a substance has spoiled.

Tasannaha al-ṭaʿāmu, however, expresses alteration associated with age and the passage of time.

Such change may appear in smell, taste, color, appearance, or other qualities; the verb alone does not specify its exact form.

Spoilage is attested in this branch, but should not be restricted to a particular mold, fermentation, chemical reaction, or microbial process.

The verb describes a familiar, observable condition, not the scientific mechanism of every process of decay.

Is every change tasannuh?

Arabic has other verbs for general change, including taghayyara.

Tasannaha, when applied to food and drink, connects alteration with age and remaining stored.

Deliberately changing food's color, heating a drink, or crushing fruit is not necessarily tasannuh merely because something changes.

The distinguishing feature here is alteration associated with the passage of time.

This clarifies the verb in Al-Baqarah 2:259.

3. Lexical forms and the disputed structure of sanah

Under the morphological classification adopted here, S N H has one Quranic occurrence:

Yatasannah — Al-Baqarah 2:259

In the usual analysis, this is the imperfect of form V, tasannaha.

Arabic also has a broader lexical family:

  • Saniha: for food or drink to undergo alteration through age.
  • Tasannaha: changed, aged, or spoiled; alternatively, for a period of years to pass over something.
  • Sanhāʾ: describing a palm or condition associated with annual variation, irregular bearing, or drought.
  • Sānaha: conducted a transaction or relationship on a yearly basis; of a palm, bore differently in successive years.
  • Musānahah: an annual transaction, lease, or agreement.
  • Sanahāt: an attested plural of sanah.
  • Sunayhah: the diminutive of sanah under the analysis treating hāʾ as part of its historical structure.

These forms illuminate the lexical family, but do not occur in these exact forms in the Quran.

Does sanah belong to S N H or S N W?

An important grammatical disagreement concerns sanah's historical final radical.

The word ends in tāʾ marbūṭah in writing; morphological analyses reconstruct an earlier radical lost from the current form.

One analysis reconstructs sanhah with a lost hāʾ, citing sanahāt, sunayhah, and musānahah.

Another reconstructs sanwah, citing sanawāt, sunayyah, and musānāh.

Both sets of forms are attested.

Sanawāt does not invalidate every form containing hāʾ; nor does sanahāt alone prove that every use of sanah derives from a single historical root ending in hāʾ.

In the classification used here, sanah and sinīn belong to S N W, while yatasannah is studied under S N H; its existing word box preserves both proposed derivations.

This organization helps study Quranic forms but does not settle the historical grammatical dispute.

This article therefore focuses on yatasannah and directly related uses. A comprehensive study of sanah, sinīn, and other forms for years belongs separately under S N W.

4. Lam yatasannah: food and drink remaining unchanged

The root's single occurrence under this classification is in Al-Baqarah 2:259.

Part of the verse reads:

قَالَ بَلْ لَبِثْتَ مِائَةَ عَامٍ فَانْظُرْ إِلَىٰ طَعَامِكَ وَشَرَابِكَ لَمْ يَتَسَنَّهْ وَانْظُرْ إِلَىٰ حِمَارِكَ

qāla bal labithta miʾata ʿāmin fa-nẓur ilā ṭaʿāmika wa-sharābika lam yatasannah wa-nẓur ilā ḥimārika

Al-Baqarah 2:259

“He said: Rather, you remained a hundred years. Look at your food and drink: it has not changed; and look at your donkey.”

The verb's structure

Yatasannah is a third-person masculine singular imperfect of form V.

The preceding lam places it in the jussive.

Under the analysis treating hāʾ as an original radical, the verb ends with a quiescent hāʾ.

Lam yatasannah negates the occurrence of alteration in the past.

Both food and drink are mentioned, and the statement concerns what the person had with him to eat and drink.

Despite a hundred years passing, they had not undergone the change associated with age.

The verb itself does not express a hundred years; that duration comes from miʾata ʿāmin earlier in the verse.

Tasannuh does not independently mean “remaining a hundred years.” It expresses time-associated alteration, which the verse says did not occur.

Reading variants and final hāʾ

Recognized Quranic readings differ over pronouncing the final hāʾ when joining yatasannah to the following word.

Some readings retain it both when continuing and when pausing.

Others, including those of Ḥamzah and al-Kisāʾī, retain it at a pause but omit it in connected recitation.

These readings have been related to different morphological analyses, but retaining hāʾ in connected recitation does not by itself prove that it is a root radical.

One analysis treats hāʾ as an original radical and derives the verb from S N H.

Another treats it as hāʾ al-sakt, a letter associated with pausing; some readings preserve it even in connected recitation. On this analysis, the verb's connection with forms containing wāw or yāʾ in the family of sanah is examined.

Some early explanations also propose a connection with yatasannanu and S N N.

These differences do not fundamentally change the contextual sense: the food and drink had not deteriorated with time.

Reading variants matter for the verb's historical structure, but should not be turned into two separate events or distinct kinds of spoilage.

Time has passed, but the food has not changed

Before lam yatasannah, the Quran says that the person remained a hundred years.

That interval has elapsed within the narrative, although he himself thought he had remained a day or part of a day.

His attention is then directed to his food and drink.

Ordinarily, food and drink can change over time according to their substance and storage conditions.

Here, however, the Quran reports their remaining without the alteration in question.

The story places two distinct facts together:

First, a long interval has elapsed.

Second, the expected alteration in food and drink has not occurred.

Lam yatasannah does not mean that time stopped; it means that something remained unchanged although a hundred years passed.

This distinction does not justify attributing a specific physical mechanism to the verse, such as time stopping for all objects, complete freezing, storage at a particular temperature, or the absence of every chemical reaction.

The Quran states the outcome, not a technical preservation method.

Absence of spoilage or absence of every change?

Lexical explanations associate yatasannah with alteration and spoilage of food.

“Has not spoiled” is therefore a suitable equivalent for this usage.

The word nevertheless belongs to alteration over time and should not be confined to a particular kind of spoilage.

Food may lose its smell or taste, change in appearance, or become unsuitable to eat.

These illustrate altered quality, but the verb does not specify precisely what would have happened without divine preservation.

“Has not changed” provides a broader direct rendering; “has not spoiled” can clarify the familiar direction of change in lexical commentary.

5. Time, death, and restoration in the verse

Al-Baqarah 2:259 concerns more than food and drink.

It begins with someone passing a ruined settlement and asking how God will revive it after its death.

He then experiences death and being raised himself.

He is asked how long he remained and directed to his food, drink, donkey, and the bones.

The verse continues:

وَانْظُرْ إِلَى الْعِظَامِ كَيْفَ نُنْشِزُهَا ثُمَّ نَكْسُوهَا لَحْمًا

wa-nẓur ilā al-ʿiẓāmi kayfa nunshizuhā thumma naksūhā laḥmā

Al-Baqarah 2:259

“And look at the bones, how We raise them, then clothe them with flesh.”

This describes the bones being restored and clothed with flesh.

The verse thus places several conditions together: human death and revival, food and drink remaining unchanged, and the visible restoration of bones and flesh.

Their differences matter for understanding the verse.

Is passing time the sufficient cause of every change?

Many changes occur over time, but time alone does not fully explain them.

The substance, environmental conditions, and natural processes affect how things change.

Food deterioration, for example, depends on storage and the substance's properties. Time allows change to occur, but its form and rate vary.

This scientific explanation should not be imposed upon the verse.

Lam yatasannah describes an expected alteration not occurring, rather than a comprehensive theory of time, matter, and every natural law.

The context displays God's power over death, revival, and restoration.

Its lexical sense is clear; its wider message emerges from the whole story.

Preserved food is not the same as immortality

In Al-Baqarah 2:259, food and drink remain unchanged for a particular interval.

This does not mean that their substances are inherently incorruptible or eternal.

The verse describes their condition in that event.

Preservation for a hundred years should not become a general claim that every food can be preserved forever.

Nor should preservation be equated with human revival. Reviving a person, preserving food, and restoring bones are distinct events brought together to display divine power.

6. Comparison with neighboring roots

S N H (tasannuh) and S N W (sanah): years and the structure of “year”

These families are closely connected in discussion of sanah.

Lexical analyses record some year-related forms with hāʾ and others with wāw.

Sanahāt and sunayhah stand on one side, sanawāt and sunayyah on the other.

Here yatasannah is studied under S N H, while sanah and sinīn are classified under S N W. The existing word box retains the competing derivations of yatasannah.

Quranic study should therefore distinguish their actual forms and morphological analyses.

This separation does not deny a possible historical connection.

S N H (tasannuh) and A S N (asin): changes in food and water

A S N appears in the Quran as āsin in Muḥammad 47:15.

That verse describes māʾin ghayri āsinin: water not subject to undesirable alteration or staleness.

This resembles lam yatasannah in subject matter, since both concern changing material qualities, especially food and drink.

They nevertheless remain distinct roots.

Asina al-māʾu conventionally denotes undesirable changes in water, especially its smell or taste.

Tasannaha in Al-Baqarah 2:259 concerns alteration associated with years passing and food or drink remaining stored.

One foregrounds changed water, the other the effects of time upon a substance.

The separate root A S N concerns undesirable alteration and stagnation of water and should be studied independently.

S N H (tasannuh) and S N N (sunnah): year and established practice

S N N includes Quranic words such as sunnah, al-sunan, and masnūn.

Its Quranic uses concern ways, practices, and aspects of an object's form or condition.

Sanah, “year,” is a temporal unit whose historical structure is discussed under S N H and S N W.

Established practice and a year are different concepts, despite similarities between some Arabic written forms.

Sunnata Allāhi, for example, refers to God's way or established practice, not to a year belonging to God.

Masnūn in the creation passages about clay belongs conventionally to S N N; proposed senses include altered or fashioned.

This does not authorize treating masnūn and yatasannah as one root without analysis.

Some early analyses propose a connection, but their classification and structure require separate assessment.

S N H (tasannuh) and W S N (wasan): year and drowsiness

Al-Baqarah 2:255 says:

لَا تَأْخُذُهُ سِنَةٌ وَلَا نَوْمٌ

lā taʾkhudhuhu sinatun wa-lā nawm

Al-Baqarah 2:255

“Neither drowsiness nor sleep overtakes Him.”

Sinah here means drowsiness and belongs to W S N.

It differs from sanah, “year.”

Their similar appearance must not lead to merging their roots.

Yatasannah in Al-Baqarah 2:259 likewise does not concern drowsiness or sleep.

S N H (tasannuh) and ʿ W M (ʿām): two words for a year

In Al-Baqarah 2:259, miʾata ʿāmin expresses the length of the person's stay.

ʿĀm means a year and is conventionally assigned to a different root.

ʿĀm and sanah can frequently refer to the same annual duration.

Lexical and interpretive explanations sometimes distinguish them by hardship, abundance, or methods of reckoning.

These observations do not establish that sanah always means a difficult year and ʿām always an easy one. Quranic usage does not support that absolute division.

In Al-Baqarah 2:259, ʿām expresses the actual duration, while yatasannah negates alteration of food and drink during it.

They share a temporal setting without having identical grammatical or semantic roles.

S N H (tasannuh) and GH Y R (taghayyur): time-related alteration and change

Taghayyara means to change or pass from one condition to another.

This is broader than deterioration of stored food.

Water may change temperature through heating without undergoing tasannuh.

Conversely, tasannuh may appropriately describe food becoming stale or spoiled over time.

“Change” is therefore a broad explanatory equivalent, but the particular temporal connection should remain in the analysis.

7. Translation recommendations for Forqan

The root's sole Quranic form under this classification is yatasannah in Al-Baqarah 2:259.

The main phrase:

فَانْظُرْ إِلَىٰ طَعَامِكَ وَشَرَابِكَ لَمْ يَتَسَنَّهْ

fa-nẓur ilā ṭaʿāmika wa-sharābika lam yatasannah

Al-Baqarah 2:259

A suitable direct translation is:

“Look at your food and drink: it has not changed.”

This accords with the verb's attested sense and context.

“Has not spoiled” also fits the lexical use for food and drink. Both renderings require attention to time-associated deterioration; neither identifies a particular process of spoilage.

“Has not changed over the years” makes the temporal connection explicit, although “over the years” partly repeats the preceding context.

The direct rendering can remain brief, with its temporal connection explained lexically.

Suggested word information for Forqan:

Word in the verse: yatasannah.

Root studied here: S N H; its relationship with S N W remains discussed.

Brief root meaning: passing years and time-related alteration; for food and drink, aging and deterioration.

Meaning here: has not changed; the food and drink have not lost their former quality despite a hundred years passing.

Further explanation can note the lexical relationship and disagreement involving S N W in sanah's historical structure.

Principal non-Quranic forms

Saniha al-ṭaʿāmu: food changed or spoiled through age.

Tasannaha: changed over time; alternatively, for years to pass over something.

Mutasannih: something altered through age.

Sanhāʾ: of a palm, bearing differently across years or affected by drought.

Sānaha: established a yearly transaction or relationship; of a palm, referred to differing annual bearing.

Musānahah: an annual agreement, lease, or transaction.

Sunayhah: a diminutive of “year” under the analysis retaining historical hāʾ.

These forms illuminate the family; only yatasannah is classified here as Quranic.

Conclusion

S N H relates to years, time, and effects occurring in things over the years.

Plant-related forms such as sanhāʾ describe palms and changes in their annual bearing.

For food and drink, saniha and tasannaha express alteration and spoilage through age.

Under the adopted classification, yatasannah in Al-Baqarah 2:259 is the root's sole Quranic occurrence. The food and drink remained unchanged despite a hundred years passing.

This family is lexically connected with sanah, “year,” but early analyses disagree over its final radical, proposing S N H or S N W. That dispute should not be hidden or replaced with a certain historical claim.

In the classification used here, tasannuh differs from āsin, describing stale water, sunnah, “practice,” and sinah, “drowsiness.” Some early analyses connect tasannuh with S N N, but this does not justify conflating their meanings.

The resulting definition is:

The Quranic branch studied under S N H concerns alteration with passing years and time, especially food and drink deteriorating through age. Lam yatasannah in Al-Baqarah 2:259 means that the food and drink had not changed despite the long interval.

This keeps the verb in context without restricting it to one type of deterioration or an unestablished scientific explanation.

The verse's striking distinction is that time has passed, but the expected alteration has not occurred. The Quran places this alongside death, revival, and restoration of bones to show God's power to create and restore.

Yatasannah is consequently neither a name for time itself nor a name for death or life. It expresses alteration associated with time, which the Quran negates of the food and drink.
