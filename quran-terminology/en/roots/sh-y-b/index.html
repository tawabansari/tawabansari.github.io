Central meaning: hair turning white and the appearance of white hair; figuratively, whiteness or a visible change resembling graying hair.

The Arabic root SH Y B concerns hair turning white. شَابَ means that a person’s hair turned white; شَيْب denotes white hair or its whitening; and أَشْيَب describes someone whose hair has turned white.

This root is closely associated with old age, since graying hair is a familiar sign of advancing years. Yet شَيْب and old age are not identical: people may have white hair when young, and not every older person has entirely white hair.

The root occurs three times in the Quran: شَيْبًا in Maryam 19:4, شَيْبَةً in Al-Rūm 30:54, and شِيبًا in Al-Muzzammil 73:17.

All three concern white hair, but serve different roles. In Maryam it marks Zechariah’s advanced age; in Al-Rūm it is one of life’s changes; in Al-Muzzammil children turning white-haired form part of the description of the terror of Resurrection Day.

1. The root in early Arabic

شَيْب: white hair

In early Arabic, شَيْب denoted hair turning white or white hair itself, whether a small amount or much of the hair.

Lexicons record شَابَ الرَّجُلُ يَشِيبُ شَيْبًا for a person becoming white-haired.

Likewise, شَابَ رَأْسُهُ means that the hair on his head turned white.

The immediate subject is a change in hair color, rather than bodily weakness, illness, or loss of ability.

Someone may have a few white strands among black hair; this can already be described as the appearance of شَيْب.

If most of the hair turns white years later, the same word still applies.

شيب therefore specifies neither a fixed amount of white hair nor a particular age.

A poetic witness attributed to the pre-Islamic period

Poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿUbayd ibn al-Abraṣ includes:

إِمَّا قَتِيلًا وَإِمَّا هَالِكًا
وَالشَّيْبُ شَيْنٌ لِمَنْ يَشِيبُ

immā qatīlan wa-immā hālikan / wa-l-shaybu shaynun li-man yashīb

“Either slain or perishing; and white hair is a blemish to the one who grows white-haired.”

Here الشَّيْب denotes white hair and يَشِيبُ means becoming white-haired.

The poet views white hair unfavorably and sets it beside the passage of life and the human end.

The word’s lexical meaning must be distinguished from the poet’s judgment.

شَيْب does not mean a blemish, ugliness, or deficiency. It is the poet who regards graying hair as unwelcome in this poetic setting.

The same ode includes:

تَصْبُو فَأَنَّى لَكَ التَّصَابِي
أَنَّى وَقَدْ رَاعَكَ الْمَشِيبُ

taṣbū fa-annā laka al-taṣābī / annā wa-qad rāʿaka al-mashīb

“You yearn for youthful ways—but how can you play the youth? How, when white hair has alarmed you?”

الْمَشِيب here denotes graying hair, contrasted with youthful behavior and ardor.

This illustrates the recognition of white hair in early Arabic language and culture as a sign of passing youth and advancing age.

These lines are transmitted in ʿUbayd ibn al-Abraṣ’s poetry. He belongs to the pre-Islamic period, but the precise composition date of these passages is not independently established here. They are attributed poetic witnesses, rather than documents with an exact known date.

Their semantic evidence is clear: شيب denoted white hair, while مشيب could evoke the passing of youth and the approach of old age.

شَيَّبَهُ الحُزْنُ: grief making someone white-haired

Arabic lexicons record شَيَّبَهُ الْحُزْنُ and أَشَابَهُ الْحُزْنُ.

They mean that grief made someone white-haired, or affected them so deeply that they seemed aged and white-haired.

White hair was thus associated in Arabic expression not only with time passing, but also with severe emotional strain.

Someone might say after a terrible experience, “That event turned my hair white.”

Such speech can convey great distress without necessarily asserting that every hair turned white at that instant.

The effects of different factors on hair are separate from the lexical question. Such expressions can refer either to an actual change in appearance or to a literary image of intense grief.

This matters for Al-Muzzammil 73:17, where children turning white-haired describe a terrifying day.

Whiteness applied to mountains

Lexicons also record شِيب for mountains whitened by snow. Explanations of particular poetic examples include white clouds, mountains whitened by snow or dust, and a mountain name; these readings should be distinguished.

The snow-covered mountain interpretation draws on a visible resemblance to a white-haired head.

Imagine a mountain whose lower slopes are dark while its summit and upper reaches are covered in white snow.

It may resemble a head on which white hair has appeared.

The mountain has no actual hair, and its whitening does not indicate its old age.

The extension of شيب to such a scene rests on a resemblance in whiteness.

This use is recorded in Arabic lexicons, but without independent historical evidence it should not be assigned to a particular pre-Islamic date.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important Arabic forms include:

شَابَ: his hair turned white; he became white-haired.

يَشِيبُ: becomes white-haired.

شَيْب: hair turning white, or white hair itself.

شَيْبَة: graying and the appearance of white hair.

مَشِيب: graying hair; in descriptions of life, entering the period associated with it.

أَشْيَب: someone whose hair has turned white.

شِيب: white-haired people; commonly analyzed as the plural of أَشْيَب, though other morphological explanations are also recorded.

شَيَّبَ: made someone white-haired or turned hair white.

تَشْيِيب: making hair white or causing someone to become white-haired.

أَشَابَ: caused hair to turn white.

شَائِب: white-haired in some recorded lexical uses.

أَشْيَب is established for a white-haired man. شَمْطَاء, from another lexical family, is an established description for a woman with graying hair.

This difference in naming does not restrict the verb شَابَ to men; the same verbal family also describes women’s hair turning white.

The Quran uses only three nominal forms from the forms listed here:

شَيْبًا, a noun or verbal noun denoting white hair; شَيْبَةً, denoting graying hair; and شِيبًا, commonly analyzed as the plural of أَشْيَب.

They look similar in writing but differ in morphology and sentence function.

3. Quranic uses

Maryam 19:4: white hair in Zechariah’s description of old age

قَالَ رَبِّ إِنِّي وَهَنَ الْعَظْمُ مِنِّي وَاشْتَعَلَ الرَّأْسُ شَيْبًا وَلَمْ أَكُنْ بِدُعَائِكَ رَبِّ شَقِيًّا

qāla rabbi innī wahana al-ʿaẓmu minnī wa-ishtaʿala al-raʾsu shayban wa-lam akun bi-duʿāʾika rabbi shaqiyyā

Maryam 19:4

“He said: My Lord, my bones have weakened, and my head has flared with white hair; and in calling upon You, my Lord, I have never been deprived.”

Maryam 19:4

Zechariah describes his bodily condition here.

He first says:

وَهَنَ الْعَظْمُ مِنِّي

wahana al-ʿaẓmu minnī

Maryam 19:4

That is, my bones have weakened.

He then says:

وَاشْتَعَلَ الرَّأْسُ شَيْبًا

wa-ishtaʿala al-raʾsu shaybā

Maryam 19:4

That is, my head has flared with white hair.

شَيْبًا denotes white hair, while اشْتَعَلَ comes from a different root, SH ʿ L.

In its material sense, اشتعال concerns fire blazing up.

The image combines two concepts: blazing and white hair.

Why is white hair compared to blazing fire?

When fire spreads through combustible material, flames reach different parts.

Likewise, a few strands may turn white first, followed gradually by more of the hair.

The image compares the spread of white hair to a spreading blaze.

Someone may have only a few white strands in youth but a largely white head of hair in old age.

“White hair has spread over his whole head” expresses the explanatory sense.

The Quranic وَاشْتَعَلَ الرَّأْسُ شَيْبًا presents a more vivid image, as though whiteness has spread through the head like flame.

Blazing is not the meaning of SH Y B itself, however; شيب must not be defined as catching fire.

Grammatically, شَيْبًا is accusative and specifies the respect in which the depicted blazing occurs. The common analysis calls it a specifying accusative, or tamyīz.

White hair and weakened bones

Weakened bones and white hair appear together in the verse.

Their association shows that Zechariah is describing the effects of advanced age.

Yet وَهَنَ الْعَظْمُ and شَيْبًا express different things.

The first concerns bodily weakness.

The second concerns a change in hair color.

شيب must therefore not be reduced to weakened bones, inability, or even old age itself.

This distinction also matters later in Zechariah’s speech: Maryam 19:8 again mentions his advanced age and his wife’s barrenness.

White hair in verse 4 is consequently one sign of his condition, rather than its complete definition.

Al-Rūm 30:54: white hair among the changes of life

اللَّهُ الَّذِي خَلَقَكُمْ مِنْ ضَعْفٍ ثُمَّ جَعَلَ مِنْ بَعْدِ ضَعْفٍ قُوَّةً ثُمَّ جَعَلَ مِنْ بَعْدِ قُوَّةٍ ضَعْفًا وَشَيْبَةً ۚ يَخْلُقُ مَا يَشَاءُ ۖ وَهُوَ الْعَلِيمُ الْقَدِيرُ

allāhu alladhī khalaqakum min ḍaʿfin thumma jaʿala min baʿdi ḍaʿfin quwwatan thumma jaʿala min baʿdi quwwatin ḍaʿfan wa-shaybatan yakhluqu mā yashāʾu wa-huwa al-ʿalīmu al-qadīr

Al-Rūm 30:54

“The God is the One who created you from weakness, then after weakness appointed strength, then after strength appointed weakness and white hair. He creates what He wills, and He is the Knowing, the Powerful.”

Al-Rūm 30:54

The verse describes changes in human strength across life’s stages.

People begin life weak, then grow stronger; after a period of strength, they may again experience weakness with advancing age.

شَيْبَةً accompanies ضَعْفًا in this context.

The words are related but not synonymous.

ضَعْف concerns weakness and reduced strength.

شَيْبَة denotes white hair.

Someone can have white hair while remaining physically strong, or be weak and ill without having white hair.

The words’ juxtaposition does not make their meanings identical.

Does white hair always signify old age?

Here it occurs in the context of aging, but this does not mean that everyone becomes white-haired only after reaching old age.

White hair can appear at different ages.

Its extent and timing are not the same for everyone.

The verse depicts familiar changes in human life, rather than prescribing an age-specific timetable for every individual.

شَيْبَة here names white hair, a visible sign accompanying the weakness of old age, rather than being identical to age itself.

Grammatically, شَيْبَةً is coordinated with ضَعْفًا, and both belong to the construction with جَعَلَ.

This lexical and grammatical distinction should remain visible in translation.

Al-Muzzammil 73:17: a day that makes children white-haired

فَكَيْفَ تَتَّقُونَ إِنْ كَفَرْتُمْ يَوْمًا يَجْعَلُ الْوِلْدَانَ شِيبًا

fa-kayfa tattaqūna in kafartum yawman yajʿalu al-wildāna shībā

Al-Muzzammil 73:17

“Then, if you deny, how will you guard yourselves against a day that makes children white-haired?”

Al-Muzzammil 73:17

The form here is شِيبًا.

It differs morphologically from شَيْبًا in Maryam 19:4.

شَيْبًا in Maryam is a noun or verbal noun denoting white hair.

شِيبًا in Al-Muzzammil means white-haired people and is commonly analyzed as the plural of أَشْيَب.

الْوِلْدَانَ denotes children; شِيبًا describes the condition into which they are made to pass.

يَجْعَلُ الْوِلْدَانَ شِيبًا means “makes children white-haired.”

الْوِلْدَانَ is the first object of يَجْعَلُ and شِيبًا its second object.

Why speak of children becoming white-haired?

In ordinary life, white hair is chiefly associated with advancing age.

Children are at the beginning of life; signs of aging are not normally expected in them as in older people.

يَجْعَلُ الْوِلْدَانَ شِيبًا therefore creates a striking image.

It describes a day so overwhelming and terrifying that even children become white-haired.

Ordinary language likewise permits expressions such as “This event ages a person overnight.”

Such expressions convey the intensity of strain and fear.

In Al-Muzzammil, this image occurs among warnings about Resurrection Day.

Al-Muzzammil 73:14 speaks of the earth and mountains shaking.

Al-Muzzammil 73:18 speaks of the sky splitting in connection with that day.

Children becoming white-haired thus forms part of the depiction of its terror.

The phrase’s direct meaning should nevertheless be distinguished from how it is fulfilled.

Directly, it says that the day makes children white-haired.

Rhetorically, it conveys the day’s fearfulness and gravity; the word itself specifies neither a bodily mechanism nor how long such a change takes.

It should not be translated merely as “children become frightened,” since that removes the particular image of white hair.

It is preferable to preserve the direct wording and explain its role in depicting the terror of Resurrection Day separately.

4. Comparison with related roots

SH Y B (شَيْب) and SH M Ṭ (شَمَط)

شَمَط concerns white hair mixed with dark hair.

أَشْمَط describes someone whose hair contains both white and black strands.

شَيْب is broader and can denote either a little or a great deal of white hair.

Someone might still have mostly black hair with a few white strands at the temples.

شَمَط aptly describes that mixture of white and black.

شَيْب still applies when much of the hair has turned white.

The two families overlap in this domain, but not all their uses should be treated as identical.

SH Y B (شَيْب) and B Y Ḍ (بَيَاض)

بَيَاض denotes whiteness and has a broad range.

Snow, cloth, walls, and many other things can be white.

شَيْب primarily concerns white hair.

ثَوْبٌ أَبْيَضُ, for example, describes a white garment.

رَجُلٌ أَشْيَبُ describes a man whose hair has turned white.

Whiteness is shared, but شيب refers to a particular kind of whitening.

Its figurative application to snow-covered mountains likewise draws on resemblance in color.

SH Y B (شَيْب) and K B R (كِبَر)

In relation to age, كِبَر concerns growing older and advanced age.

شَيْب concerns white hair.

In Maryam 19:4 Zechariah speaks of his white hair.

In Maryam 19:8 he speaks of reaching advanced age and decrepitude.

The verses show that white hair and aging can coexist in a single situation while remaining distinct concepts.

Although شيب is a familiar sign of age, direct translations should not always substitute “old age” for it.

SH Y B (شَيْب) and SH B B (شَبَاب)

شَبَاب belongs to SH B B and concerns youth.

شَيْب belongs to SH Y B and concerns hair turning white.

Unvocalized شاب can cause confusion.

شَابَ from SH Y B means that someone’s hair turned white.

شَابّ from SH B B means a young person.

Despite their visible resemblance, they differ in root and meaning.

Nor does the cultural contrast between youth and white hair make شيب precisely equivalent to the end of youth.

Its primary meaning concerns hair turning white, though it can be regarded as a sign of passing youth.

SH Y B (شَيْب) and SH W B (شَوْب)

These roots resemble one another in letters and in some semantic associations, but are distinct.

شَوْب from SH W B concerns mixing one thing with another.

Mixing water with milk, for example, can be expressed through that family.

شَيْب from SH Y B, by contrast, concerns white hair.

Some etymological explanations associate the families through white strands becoming mixed with black ones.

This association is understandable: early white hairs may appear among dark strands.

The resemblance alone does not prove that all uses of شيب derive from شوب.

شَوْب can concern mixing two liquids, whereas شَيْب primarily describes hair color.

The roots should therefore remain distinct in Quranic analysis.

SH Y B (شَيْب) and W H N (وَهَن)

وَهَن concerns weakness and diminished strength.

Maryam 19:4 places وَهَنَ الْعَظْمُ beside اشْتَعَلَ الرَّأْسُ شَيْبًا.

Their association shows that both can be signs of advanced age.

Weakened bones and hair turning white are nevertheless different conditions.

Someone may become physically weak without having white hair.

A white-haired person may also remain physically strong.

The meaning of وهن should therefore not be imported into the translation of شيب.

5. Suggested Forqan translations

شَيْبًا — Maryam 19:4

Brief root meaning: hair turning white; graying hair.

Meaning in this verse: Zechariah’s white hair, depicted through the spread of a blaze.

Suggested translation: “white hair.”

Rendering of the phrase:

وَاشْتَعَلَ الرَّأْسُ شَيْبًا

wa-ishtaʿala al-raʾsu shaybā

Maryam 19:4

“And my head has flared with white hair.”

This preserves both the meaning of شيب and the image of blazing.

“My head has turned white with age” conveys a general sense but weakens both the blazing image and the distinction between age and white hair.

شَيْبَةً — Al-Rūm 30:54

Brief root meaning: hair turning white and the appearance of white hair.

Meaning in this verse: white hair as one of the changes associated with old age.

Suggested translation: “white hair” or “graying hair.”

Rendering of the phrase:

ثُمَّ جَعَلَ مِنْ بَعْدِ قُوَّةٍ ضَعْفًا وَشَيْبَةً

thumma jaʿala min baʿdi quwwatin ḍaʿfan wa-shaybah

Al-Rūm 30:54

“Then after strength He appointed weakness and white hair.”

ضَعْفًا and شَيْبَةً should be translated separately, since the verse expresses related but different characteristics.

شِيبًا — Al-Muzzammil 73:17

Brief root meaning: hair turning white and white hair itself.

Meaning in this verse: children becoming white-haired in the description of Resurrection Day’s terror.

Word type: an adjectival plural, commonly analyzed as the plural of أَشْيَب.

Suggested translation: “white-haired.”

Rendering of the phrase:

يَجْعَلُ الْوِلْدَانَ شِيبًا

yajʿalu al-wildāna shībā

Al-Muzzammil 73:17

“Makes children white-haired.”

شِيبًا here is an adjectival plural describing white-haired people, rather than an abstract name for graying.

“Makes children white-haired” is therefore more natural and more faithful to the construction than “places white hair in children.”

6. Conclusion

SH Y B concerns hair turning white and the appearance of white hair.

شَيْب denotes whitening or white hair itself; شَيْبَة denotes graying hair; and أَشْيَب describes a white-haired person, with شِيب as its commonly identified plural.

This meaning also appears in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period and has long been associated with passing youth and advancing age.

That association does not make white hair and old age identical.

In Maryam 19:4, شَيْبًا denotes Zechariah’s white hair through the image of a spreading blaze.

شَيْبَةً in Al-Rūm 30:54 denotes white hair alongside the weakness of old age.

شِيبًا in Al-Muzzammil 73:17 describes white-haired people and is commonly understood as the plural of أَشْيَب; it depicts children becoming white-haired amid the terror of Resurrection Day.

The three uses share a basic meaning, while their sentence roles range from describing a natural change in hair to creating an image of warning.

Final definition of SH Y B:

“Hair turning white and the appearance of white hair, whether little or abundant. Arabic associates this condition with advancing age and uses it figuratively to express intense distress, fear, or the visible whiteness of other things. In the Quran it describes Zechariah’s white hair, changes across human life, and children becoming white-haired in the depiction of Resurrection Day’s terror.”
