Central meaning: advanced age, a stage of life in which the passing of years becomes evident; socially, a title for elders, leaders, and people of standing or experience.

The Arabic root SH Y KH concerns advanced age: a stage beyond youth when the effects of growing older become apparent.

شَيْخ primarily denotes an elderly man, and شَيْخَة is its feminine form. شَاخَ means becoming old, while شَيْخُوخَة names old age.

Socially, shaykh can also designate someone respected for experience, knowledge, seniority, or standing. The language records its use for tribal elders, teachers, scholars, and holders of religious or social positions; their respective histories should not be reduced to a single assumed chronology.

The age-related meaning and the title must be distinguished. Not every elderly person is a leader or scholar, and knowledge or social standing does not necessarily imply advanced age.

The root occurs four times in the Quran: three singular instances of شَيْخ and one plural شُيُوخ. All four concern age, rather than a religious office or leadership title.

1. The root in early Arabic

شَيْخ: a man showing the effects of advancing age

In early Arabic, شَيْخ described a man past youth whose appearance or condition showed advancing age.

Lexicons explain it through استبانت فيه السن وظهر عليه الشيب: age has become evident in him and white hair has appeared.

This explanation has two important features.

First, shaykh concerns a stage of life, rather than merely a count of years since birth.

Second, white hair is a familiar sign of that stage, but شَيْخ is not identical to شَيْب, which denotes white hair itself.

A man around fifty, for example, might remain physically strong while showing signs of age in his face and hair.

A younger person might have white hair for various reasons without ordinarily being called an old man.

The primary meaning therefore concerns age and life stage, not hair color alone.

Did shaykh specify an exact age?

Lexicons record different ages for the beginning of this stage.

Some place it around fifty; others give different limits.

This variation shows that Arabic usage was not restricted to one fixed numerical boundary.

One community might regard a fifty-year-old as elderly, while another might apply that description chiefly to older people.

People also differ in bodily condition and visible signs of age.

The Quranic word shaykh cannot therefore yield an exact age.

Calling someone شيخ establishes advanced age in that context, but does not specify sixty, seventy, or eighty years.

شَيْخَة: an elderly woman

شَيْخَة is a feminine form of شَيْخ.

It also occurs in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

The ode attributed to ʿUbayd ibn al-Abraṣ includes:

كَأَنَّهَا لِقْوَةٌ طَلُوبُ
تُخْزَنُ فِي وَكْرِهَا الْقُلُوبُ

ka-annahā liqwatun ṭalūbu / tukhzanu fī wakrihā al-qulūb

“As though she were a she-eagle seeking prey, in whose nest hearts are stored.” The hearts are those of its prey. The variant تَيْبَسُ describes them drying in the nest; neither reading means that hearts become frightened there.

The poet then says:

بَاتَتْ عَلَى إِرَمٍ عَذُوبًا
كَأَنَّهَا شَيْخَةٌ رَقُوبُ

bātat ʿalā iramin ʿadhūban / ka-annahā shaykhatun raqūb

“She spent the night without food on a rocky height, like an elderly woman bereaved of her children, watching anxiously.”

The eagle is used in a comparison describing the poet’s mount; the eagle’s posture is then compared to that of an elderly woman watching in distress.

شَيْخَة means an elderly woman here. Classical explanations of رَقُوب include a woman whose children do not survive, or one bereaved of a child and keeping anxious watch; simply “waiting for her child” does not convey the full force of the description.

The witness shows the feminine form in poetry transmitted as pre-Islamic.

These lines are transmitted in ʿUbayd ibn al-Abraṣ’s ode. Attribution to a pre-Islamic poet does not by itself establish the exact date of these particular lines.

The lexical evidence is clear: شيخة denoted an elderly woman and could be used in a simile describing another creature’s appearance or posture.

Age and social seniority

In Arab tribal societies, older people could command respect through experience, family history, or social position.

This association makes the use of shaykh for a community elder or leader understandable, without establishing the precise history of every title.

Someone who had long participated in tribal affairs, settled disputes, or carried responsibilities might acquire standing and be called the tribe’s shaykh.

Age and social office nevertheless remain distinct.

Advanced age concerns years lived; leadership concerns a person’s social position and responsibilities.

A very old person might hold no leadership position.

Conversely, knowledge or ability might make someone a leader before old age.

A social title is therefore not an inseparable part of the root’s primary age-related meaning.

Uses for a religious scholar, teacher, spiritual guide, or religious officeholder require separate consideration; they do not define the word’s Quranic occurrences.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important Arabic forms include:

شَيْخ: an elderly man; as a title, an elder or person of respected standing.

شَيْخَة: an elderly woman.

شُيُوخ: plural of shaykh; elderly men or older people in context.

أَشْيَاخ: another plural of shaykh.

شِيخَان: a recorded plural of shaykh.

مَشْيَخَة and مَشَايِخ: collective or plural forms associated with shaykhs and elders.

شَاخَ: grew old.

يَشِيخُ: grows old.

شَيْخُوخَة: old age.

شَيَّخَ: grew old in one use; in another, called someone a shaykh.

تَشَيَّخَ: adopted the appearance of elders or assumed the title shaykh in recorded uses.

The Quran uses only شَيْخ and شُيُوخ from these forms.

The verb شَاخَ and forms such as شَيْخُوخَة, شَيْخَة, and مَشَايِخ do not occur in the Quran.

Morphologically, شَيْخ follows the pattern فَعْل, and شُيُوخ is its broken plural on فُعُول.

3. Quranic uses

Hūd 11:72: Abraham’s advanced age alongside his wife’s

قَالَتْ يَا وَيْلَتَىٰ أَأَلِدُ وَأَنَا عَجُوزٌ وَهَٰذَا بَعْلِي شَيْخًا ۖ إِنَّ هَٰذَا لَشَيْءٌ عَجِيبٌ

qālat yā waylatā a-alidu wa-anā ʿajūzun wa-hādhā baʿlī shaykhan inna hādhā la-shayʾun ʿajīb

Hūd 11:72

“She said: Woe to me! Shall I give birth when I am an old woman and this husband of mine is an old man? This is indeed a wondrous thing.”

Hūd 11:72

شَيْخًا describes Abraham here.

After hearing the news of a child, Abraham’s wife speaks of her own advanced age and her husband’s.

Of herself she says:

وَأَنَا عَجُوزٌ

wa-anā ʿajūz

Hūd 11:72

“And I am an old woman.”

And of her husband:

وَهَٰذَا بَعْلِي شَيْخًا

wa-hādhā baʿlī shaykhā

Hūd 11:72

“And this husband of mine is an old man.”

عَجُوز here denotes an elderly woman and شَيْخ an elderly man.

The words describe the couple’s ages within the same context.

Does shaykh here mean an elder or leader by title?

The subject is astonishment at having a child in old age.

Abraham’s wife is identifying his advanced age alongside hers, rather than identifying him as a community leader.

“Old man” or “elderly man” is therefore the clear contextual meaning.

شَيْخًا is accusative and commonly analyzed as a circumstantial expression referring to بَعْلِي, describing her husband’s condition.

Does being a shaykh mean being unable to have children?

The word itself does not mean inability to father children.

In this verse, however, the couple’s advanced age helps explain the surprise at the announcement.

In Al-Dhāriyāt 51:29, Abraham’s wife also describes herself as عَجُوزٌ عَقِيمٌ, “an old, barren woman.”

The two descriptions should be distinguished.

عَجُوز concerns age, and عَقِيم barrenness.

Likewise, شَيْخ identifies Abraham’s advanced age without independently specifying his reproductive capacity.

A contextual inference should not replace a root’s lexical meaning.

In Hūd 11:73, the divine envoys respond by questioning astonishment at The God’s command.

The passage therefore centers on the announcement of a child and surprise at its fulfillment in old age.

Yūsuf 12:78: an elderly father about whom his sons are concerned

قَالُوا يَا أَيُّهَا الْعَزِيزُ إِنَّ لَهُ أَبًا شَيْخًا كَبِيرًا فَخُذْ أَحَدَنَا مَكَانَهُ ۖ إِنَّا نَرَاكَ مِنَ الْمُحْسِنِينَ

qālū yā ayyuhā al-ʿazīzu inna lahu aban shaykhan kabīran fa-khudh aḥadanā makānahu innā narāka mina al-muḥsinīn

Yūsuf 12:78

“They said: O Aziz, he has a very elderly father, so take one of us in his place. We see you as one of those who do good.”

Yūsuf 12:78

Joseph’s brothers speak here of their father, Jacob.

They ask the Aziz of Egypt to retain one of them instead of their brother.

To explain the importance of their request, they say:

إِنَّ لَهُ أَبًا شَيْخًا كَبِيرًا

inna lahu aban shaykhan kabīrā

Yūsuf 12:78

That is, he has a very elderly father.

شَيْخًا accompanies كَبِيرًا here.

The difference between shaykh and kabīr

شَيْخ denotes an elderly man.

كَبِير belongs to K B R and has a broad range, including greatness in size, quantity, age, or standing.

With شَيْخ, it emphasizes advanced age.

شَيْخًا كَبِيرًا thus denotes a man of considerable age.

“A very elderly man” conveys the combination; when the sentence speaks of a father, “a very elderly father” is more natural.

شَيْخًا and كَبِيرًا both qualify أَبًا in this verse.

The role of age in the brothers’ request

Joseph’s brothers invoke their father’s age to explain their request.

They know that his son’s remaining with the Aziz will affect him.

Yūsuf 12:80 also mentions the pledge their father took from them.

The context therefore includes the sons’ responsibility and family circumstances as well as their father’s age.

The word shaykh itself does not mean someone requiring care, ill, or incapable.

Increased vulnerability may accompany aging, but these consequences are not the word’s direct definition.

The father’s age supplies a reason for the request; the word does not lexically mean a helpless person dependent on his children.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23: an elderly father and daughters responsible for watering

وَلَمَّا وَرَدَ مَاءَ مَدْيَنَ وَجَدَ عَلَيْهِ أُمَّةً مِنَ النَّاسِ يَسْقُونَ وَوَجَدَ مِنْ دُونِهِمُ امْرَأَتَيْنِ تَذُودَانِ ۖ قَالَ مَا خَطْبُكُمَا ۖ قَالَتَا لَا نَسْقِي حَتَّىٰ يُصْدِرَ الرِّعَاءُ ۖ وَأَبُونَا شَيْخٌ كَبِيرٌ

wa-lammā warada māʾa madyana wajada ʿalayhi ummatan mina al-nāsi yasqūna wa-wajada min dūnihimu imraʾatayni tadhūdāni qāla mā khaṭbukumā qālatā lā nasqī ḥattā yuṣdira al-riʿāʾu wa-abūnā shaykhun kabīr

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23

“When he reached the water of Midian, he found a group of people watering, and apart from them he found two women holding back. He said: What is your situation? They said: We do not water until the shepherds drive their flocks away, and our father is very elderly.”

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23

The two women are at Midian’s water source, keeping their flock away from where the others are watering.

Moses asks about their situation.

They explain that they wait until the shepherds drive their flocks away, and that their father is شَيْخٌ كَبِيرٌ.

Again, the combination means very elderly.

Why mention their father’s age?

The context places the daughters in work involving the care and watering of livestock.

They mention their father’s age when explaining their circumstances.

Their words naturally suggest that his advanced age has affected the division of family responsibilities.

For example, a father who traveled a long way to water livestock in youth might find the task harder with age.

Children can then take on some family responsibilities.

The verse does not explicitly say that he is completely incapacitated, ill, or bedridden.

Those details should not be extracted from shaykh.

Nor does this verse name the women’s father. Identifying him with a particular figure is a historical and interpretive question, not a meaning of SH Y KH.

Does shaykh mean a religious leader?

Here it describes age.

The daughters are speaking about their father’s stage of life.

The phrase وَأَبُونَا شَيْخٌ كَبِيرٌ does not by itself mean a religious scholar, Prophet, religious leader, or tribal chief.

The title’s other social uses should therefore not be imported into this verse.

“Our father is very elderly” is a clear, natural rendering.

Ghāfir 40:67: old age as a stage of human life

هُوَ الَّذِي خَلَقَكُمْ مِنْ تُرَابٍ ثُمَّ مِنْ نُطْفَةٍ ثُمَّ مِنْ عَلَقَةٍ ثُمَّ يُخْرِجُكُمْ طِفْلًا ثُمَّ لِتَبْلُغُوا أَشُدَّكُمْ ثُمَّ لِتَكُونُوا شُيُوخًا ۚ وَمِنْكُمْ مَنْ يُتَوَفَّىٰ مِنْ قَبْلُ ۖ وَلِتَبْلُغُوا أَجَلًا مُسَمًّى وَلَعَلَّكُمْ تَعْقِلُونَ

huwa alladhī khalaqakum min turābin thumma min nuṭfatin thumma min ʿalaqatin thumma yukhrijukum ṭiflan thumma li-tablughū ashuddakum thumma li-takūnū shuyūkhan wa-minkum man yutawaffā min qablu wa-li-tablughū ajalan musamman wa-laʿallakum taʿqilūn

Ghāfir 40:67

“He is the One who created you from dust, then from a drop, then from a clinging thing; then He brings you forth as a child, then so that you reach your full strength, then so that you become old. Some of you are taken in death before that, and so that you reach an appointed term, and perhaps you will reason.”

Ghāfir 40:67

شُيُوخًا is the plural of شَيْخ and refers here to older human beings.

Unlike the three preceding instances concerning particular individuals, this verse describes human life stages generally.

It mentions creation, childhood, full strength, and then old age.

The sequence places the age described by shaykh after growth and maturity.

أَشُدَّكُمْ and شُيُوخًا

أَشُدَّكُمْ belongs to SH D D and concerns reaching full strength and mature capability here.

شُيُوخًا concerns old age.

The meanings are different.

A person reaches mature growth and strength, and later may show the effects of advancing age.

This sequence does not mean that every elderly person is necessarily weak or incapable.

Some older people retain considerable bodily strength, experience, and mental ability.

Some people also become ill or disabled before old age.

The distinction concerns stages of life; shaykh does not itself mean incapable.

Does everyone reach old age?

The verse explicitly allows for death before that stage.

It continues:

وَمِنْكُمْ مَنْ يُتَوَفَّىٰ مِنْ قَبْلُ

wa-minkum man yutawaffā min qabl

Ghāfir 40:67

That is, some of you are taken in death before that.

The Quran therefore explicitly indicates that not everyone passes through all the stages listed.

A person may die in childhood, youth, or middle age without reaching old age.

This qualification matters for understanding the verse.

The stages describe a general course of life, not a program every individual completes without exception.

Nor does شيوخ specify an exact age at which life ends.

4. Comparison with related roots

SH Y KH (شَيْخ) and SH Y B (شَيْب)

These roots resemble one another in letters and usage but are distinct.

شَيْب denotes white hair.

شَيْخ denotes an elderly person.

White hair can be a sign of old age, but old age is not limited to hair whitening.

Someone may have white hair from youth without being elderly.

An older man may also retain much dark hair.

In Maryam 19:4, Zechariah speaks of the whitening of his hair.

In Hūd 11:72, Abraham’s wife describes him as a shaykh.

Both verses concern age, but one names a visible sign and the other the age-related condition itself.

SH Y KH (شَيْخ) and K H L (كَهْل)

كَهْل describes a man past youth who has reached middle age or mature adulthood.

Lexicons also differ over its age limits.

It is generally placed before old age.

Āl ʿImrān 3:46 says that Jesus will speak to people in the cradle and in mature adulthood.

كَهْلًا there denotes mature age, rather than extreme old age.

The age boundaries of kahl and shaykh may approach one another, but they do not ordinarily designate the same stage.

This distinction does not establish an exact numerical timetable for everyone’s transition into old age.

SH Y KH (شَيْخ) and K B R (كِبَر)

كِبَر denotes advanced age in age-related contexts; the broader family also concerns greatness.

K B R extends to size, quantity, standing, and importance.

شَيْخ primarily identifies an elderly person.

Yūsuf 12:78 and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23 place shaykh and kabīr together.

The combination emphasizes advanced age.

It describes a very old man in these contexts, rather than necessarily someone of great social rank.

SH Y KH (شَيْخ) and ʿ J Z (عَجُوز)

عَجُوز commonly denotes an elderly woman.

In Hūd 11:72, Abraham’s wife calls herself ʿajūz and her husband shaykh.

Both words express advanced age in that context.

عجوز is not the morphological feminine of شيخ; شيخة also exists as a feminine form.

Arabic can also apply عجوز to a very elderly man.

Its application to a woman and shaykh to a man in Hūd does not exclude other recorded uses.

SH Y KH (شَيْخ) and H R M (هَرَم)

هَرَم concerns extreme old age and age-related decrepitude.

Shaykh denotes an older person without necessarily implying the utmost weakness or deterioration.

An elderly man may still manage his responsibilities.

For someone greatly worn down whose physical strength has sharply declined, haram may be more appropriate.

This is not an absolute numerical boundary, but helps distinguish the semantic ranges.

SH Y KH (شَيْخ) and S W D (سَيِّد)

سَيِّد concerns leadership, eminence, and superior standing in a social relationship.

Shaykh primarily concerns advanced age.

Social usage may apply both to one person: an elderly tribal leader might be called both shaykh and sayyid.

Not every sayyid or leader is elderly.

Not every old man holds a position of leadership.

Their overlap as social titles does not make their primary meanings identical.

5. Shaykh as a social and religious title

In Arabic and Persian usage, shaykh can title a religious scholar, teacher, spiritual guide, tribal elder, or respected person.

These uses concern seniority and social standing.

A community may call someone shaykh for knowledge and experience even without particularly advanced age.

The title may also be formally used within a religious or social institution.

None of the four Quranic instances of SH Y KH denotes such an office.

Hūd 11:72 concerns Abraham’s advanced age.

Yūsuf 12:78 concerns Jacob’s advanced age.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23 the daughters mention their father’s advanced age.

Ghāfir 40:67 uses شيوخ for a stage of human life.

These occurrences therefore do not establish a special title for a religious scholar or authority.

This distinction does not deny the titles’ existence in linguistic history; it separates Quranic usage from social and religious terminology.

Respecting an older person or learning from their experience also differs from accepting everything they say without question.

Advanced age may accompany greater experience, but does not alone establish that all a person’s views are correct.

Youth likewise does not establish that someone’s view is mistaken.

In its primary sense shaykh describes age; scholarly or ethical credibility requires criteria appropriate to the matter concerned.

6. Suggested Forqan translations

شَيْخًا — Hūd 11:72

Brief root meaning: growing old and reaching advanced age.

Meaning in this verse: Abraham as an elderly man.

Suggested translation: “an old man.”

Rendering of the phrase:

وَهَٰذَا بَعْلِي شَيْخًا

wa-hādhā baʿlī shaykhā

Hūd 11:72

“And this husband of mine is an old man.”

Shaykh is not a social or religious title here: the context concerns surprise at having a child in old age.

شَيْخًا — Yūsuf 12:78

Brief root meaning: advanced age; a man in whom advancing years have become evident.

Meaning in this verse: a father of considerable age.

Suggested translation: “elderly.”

Rendering of the phrase:

إِنَّ لَهُ أَبًا شَيْخًا كَبِيرًا

inna lahu aban shaykhan kabīrā

Yūsuf 12:78

“He has a very elderly father.”

“Very” conveys the emphasis of شَيْخًا كَبِيرًا.

شَيْخٌ — Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23

Brief root meaning: an elderly man.

Meaning in this verse: the women’s father, who has reached advanced age.

Suggested translation: “an old man.”

Rendering of the phrase:

وَأَبُونَا شَيْخٌ كَبِيرٌ

wa-abūnā shaykhun kabīr

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23

“And our father is very elderly.”

It should not be replaced with “tribal chief,” “scholar,” or “Prophet,” since those meanings do not follow from the phrase itself.

شُيُوخًا — Ghāfir 40:67

Brief root meaning: reaching advanced age.

Meaning in this verse: older people in the stages of human life.

Word type: a broken plural of شَيْخ, accusative as the predicate of تَكُونُوا.

Suggested translation: “become old.”

Rendering of the phrase:

ثُمَّ لِتَكُونُوا شُيُوخًا

thumma li-takūnū shuyūkhā

Ghāfir 40:67

“Then so that you become old.”

This is natural and preserves the life-stage meaning.

“So that you become dignitaries” would be inappropriate, substituting social standing for age.

7. Conclusion

SH Y KH concerns reaching advanced age when the passing of years becomes evident.

شَيْخ primarily denotes an elderly man; شَيْخَة is a feminine form.

شَاخَ means growing old, and شَيْخُوخَة denotes old age.

Socially, shaykh can title a tribal elder, teacher, scholar, or respected person, but those titles are not identical to its primary age-related meaning.

All Quranic occurrences concern advanced age.

Hūd 11:72 describes Abraham as an elderly man.

Yūsuf 12:78 has Joseph’s brothers refer to their father’s age.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:23, the daughters of Midian mention their father’s age.

Ghāfir 40:67 presents old age as a stage of human life.

In these occurrences shaykh describes age, rather than a religious office, governmental title, or certain mark of knowledge.

Advanced age alone does not mean physical incapacity, illness, or inability to have children. Such conditions may be associated with age but require contextual or independent evidence.

Final definition of SH Y KH:

“Reaching advanced age and showing the effects of passing years. شَيْخ primarily denotes an elderly man and شَيْخَة an elderly woman. Socially, the word can title a respected, experienced, or prominent person; in all its Quranic occurrences, however, it denotes age rather than religious or social office.”
