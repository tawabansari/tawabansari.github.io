Power, predominance, authority, and compelling proof in Arabic and the Quran

The Arabic root S L Ṭ belongs to a semantic field of power, intensity, predominance, and effective influence. This may appear in the hardness of an object, the sharpness of speech, the ability to prevail over another, or the force and validity of an argument.

Its principal Quranic derivative is sulṭān. This does not refer exclusively to political rule: depending on context, it may mean valid proof, authority, power, dominance, or grounds on which someone can substantiate a claim or exercise a right.

These uses share power or influence that has an effect in relation to something or someone. Practical power, argumentative proof, and legitimate authority are nevertheless distinct. Sentence structure and subject matter determine the relevant sense in each verse.

1. The root in early Arabic and evidence attributed to the pre-Islamic period

Historical meaning and evidence

Early Arabic witnesses use this family to describe intensity, firmness, sharpness, and power to prevail. These qualities may occur in bodies and tools, human speech, or power exercised over another.

Salāṭah is recorded for predominance and sharpness, and saluṭa for having a sharp tongue or a tough hoof. Salīṭ can describe a forceful or sharp person or thing. These belong to a broad field of force and influence, but evidence from before Islam must be distinguished from later lexical records.

An early example attributed to Al-Aʿshā describes a powerful horse:

وَكُلِّ كُمَيْتٍ كَجِذْعِ الطَّرِيقِ
يَجْرِي عَلَى سَلَطَاتٍ لُثُمْ

wa-kulli kumaytin ka-jidhʿi al-ṭarīqi / yajrī ʿalā salaṭātin luthm

“And every bay horse, like the trunk of a tall palm, running on hard, sharp hooves that strike the ground.”

Salaṭāt here describes the horse's hard, sharp hooves. The physical sense of hardness is thus attested without directly referring to government, political authority, or religious proof. The quotation's attribution alone does not establish its exact date.

A verse attributed to Al-Mutanakhkhil Al-Hudhalī also states:

كَأَوْبِ الدَّبْرِ غَامِضَةٍ وَلَيْسَتْ
بِمُرْهَفَةِ النِّصَالِ وَلَا سِلَاطِ

ka-awbi al-dabri ghāmiḍatin wa-laysat / bi-murhafati al-niṣāli wa-lā silāṭ

“Like the returning movement of bees, their edges are fine; yet their arrowheads are not slender in build, nor are the arrows long.” This rendering follows the distinction between a finely sharpened edge and a thinly formed head.

Silāṭ here denotes long arrows, and the poem negates that description. Some forms in the family thus name or describe physical implements; their specific features must not be transferred indiscriminately to every derivative.

Salīṭ is also used of sharp, penetrating speech. Lisān salīṭ may describe an eloquent, incisive tongue or harsh, hurtful speech. Its value depends on the speaker's conduct, not simply on the root.

Is sulṭān derived from light?

Salīṭ also denotes oil used to light a lamp. Imruʾ Al-Qays says in his famous Muʿallaqah:

يُضِيءُ سَنَاهُ أَوْ مَصَابِيحُ رَاهِبٍ
أَمَالَ السَّلِيطَ بِالذُّبَالِ المُفَتَّلِ

yuḍīʾu sanāhu aw maṣābīḥu rāhibin / amāla al-salīṭa bi-al-dhubāli al-mufattal

“Its brilliance shines like a monk's lamps, whose twisted wicks have been supplied with oil.” The image describes lightning. Amāla and other wordings are transmitted; explanations of the precise action differ.

Salīṭ in this verse is lamp oil, not power or proof.

Some lexical explanations connect a lamp's light with an argument's illuminating effect. Yet two identical-looking forms do not by themselves establish that one developed historically from the other.

Lamplight may aptly illustrate a proof's illuminating effect, but it cannot be treated as the established historical origin of sulṭān.

Early poetry attests physical and descriptive branches involving firmness and sharpness. Dating the emergence of every sense of sulṭān, including proof and political authority, requires independent evidence. The noun's clear and varied Quranic usage can be examined more securely.

2. Central meaning and Quranic forms

S L Ṭ occurs 39 times in the Quran: 37 instances of sulṭān and two Form II verbs.

The main Quranic forms are:

  • Sulṭān: power, authority, proof, or entitlement, according to context.
  • Sulṭānan, sulṭānin, sulṭānun: case forms of the same noun, not independent derivatives.
  • Sulṭānuhu: his authority or power.
  • Sulṭāniyah: my authority; with the first-person possessive suffix and final hāʾ al-sakt.
  • La-sallaṭahum: He would surely have empowered them.
  • Yusalliṭu: empowers or gives the ability to prevail.

Tasalluṭ, salāṭah, sulṭah, and salīṭ are known Arabic words, but these forms do not themselves occur in the Quran. They should not be listed as actual Quranic derivatives.

Form II sallaṭa has a causative sense: someone or something is enabled to prevail or exercise influence over another. The formation does not by itself establish that such power is legitimate, unlimited, or permanent.

Sulṭān is not used in the Quran as the exclusive title of a king or political office. It is a common noun for power or supporting authority with different functions.

3. Sulṭān as proof and grounds for a claim

A prominent Quranic use concerns claims, argument, associating partners with God, and attributing statements to Him.

Many verses describe people who have no divine authorization or proof for invented deities, ranks, or beliefs.

For example, Al-Rūm 30:35 states:

أَمْ أَنْزَلْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ سُلْطَانًا فَهُوَ يَتَكَلَّمُ بِمَا كَانُوا بِهِ يُشْرِكُونَ

am anzalnā ʿalayhim sulṭānan fa-huwa yatakallamu bi-mā kānū bihi yushrikūn

Al-Rūm 30:35

“Or have We sent down to them an authority that speaks of what they associated with Him?”

Sulṭān here cannot mean merely military power or political rule. The issue is grounds for associating partners with God. The question asks whether a divinely given proof exists that articulates and supports that claim.

Fa-huwa yatakallamu, “so it speaks,” is decisive: the image presents sulṭān as something capable of speaking. It centers the capacity to articulate and support a claim.

This does not make sulṭān the lexical equivalent of “logic.” Here it functions as evidence that can explain a claim and expose it to examination. Sound reasoning can help assess such evidence.

Its relation to reason, reflection, and the assessment of claims can be examined separately; it should not turn “logic” into the fixed lexical meaning of sulṭān.

Sulṭān and invented names

In Yūsuf 12:40, Joseph explains that the objects of worship are names established by his listeners and their ancestors, for which God has sent down no authority.

The same pattern appears in Al-Aʿrāf 7:71 and Al-Najm 53:23.

The issue is not merely the existence of a name. Human naming is criticized when divine rank, religious validity, or unsupported authority is attributed to it.

Someone may name a thing, but naming alone cannot establish a right to worship, legislate, or demand religious obedience.

Al-Anʿām 6:81, Al-Aʿrāf 7:33, Al-Ḥajj 22:71, and Āl ʿImrān 3:151 likewise deny authority for claims of partnership with God. Yūnus 10:68 uses this language to reject attributing offspring to Him.

In these contexts, mā lam yunazzil bihi sulṭānan expresses the absence of divine grounds or authorization. The required proof must actually support the claim; invoking religion or ancestral custom does not automatically provide it.

Disputing without authority

Ghāfir 40:35 and Ghāfir 40:56 describe disputing God's signs without an authority that has reached the disputants. The latter connects this behavior with pride.

Sulṭān here is valid support for disputation. Insistence on a claim is not the same as having evidence for it.

In Al-Kahf 18:15, the youths ask why their people do not bring clear evidence for their deities. Al-Ṣāffāt 37:156 similarly challenges unsupported claims with a demand for clear authority.

Al-Ṭūr 52:38 challenges those claiming access to information from above to produce clear evidence.

Sulṭān can thus provide grounds beyond conjecture, reputation, imitation, or personal confidence. Yet an argument's mere presentation does not make it valid in Quranic terms: its relevance and evidence must be examined.

4. Clear authority in the mission of the prophets

Several verses speak of Moses, and in some passages Aaron with him, being sent with signs and clear authority.

Hūd 11:96 states:

وَلَقَدْ أَرْسَلْنَا مُوسَىٰ بِآيَاتِنَا وَسُلْطَانٍ مُبِينٍ

wa-laqad arsalnā Mūsā bi-āyātinā wa-sulṭānin mubīn

Hūd 11:96

“We certainly sent Moses with Our signs and clear authority.”

The expression also occurs in Al-Muʾminūn 23:45 and Ghāfir 40:23. Al-Dhāriyāt 51:38 likewise describes Moses being sent to Pharaoh with clear authority.

In Al-Dukhān 44:19, Moses affirms that he brings clear authority. Al-Nisāʾ 4:153 also states that God gave Moses clear authority.

Here sulṭān supports Moses' mission before Pharaoh and his opponents. Signs, proof, and the authority of the mission belong to a shared field. Their conjunction does not prove that “signs” and “authority” are entirely separate phenomena. One can distinguish the signs presented from the authority arising through them and the divine commission without imposing absolute separation.

Why is the authority mubīn?

Mubīn concerns being clear or making clear. Sulṭān mubīn is thus proof or authority whose validity is evident in the matter at issue.

In Ibrāhīm 14:10, opponents demand clear authority from the messengers. The response in Ibrāhīm 14:11 shows that producing it is not within their independent, unlimited power: they can bring it only by God's permission.

Prophetic standing does not remove the place of evidence here. The mission is accompanied by signs, explanation, and divine support.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:35, God responds to Moses' concern by strengthening him through his brother and giving the two authority, so that their enemies cannot reach them.

Here sulṭān is connected with the capacity to stand firm and protection from enemy domination, alongside its evidentiary aspect. Even within one prophet's story, it may concern both proof and practical support.

5. Human choice and Satan's influence

Another important use marks the limits of Satan's power over human beings.

Al-Naḥl 16:99 states:

إِنَّهُ لَيْسَ لَهُ سُلْطَانٌ عَلَى الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَعَلَىٰ رَبِّهِمْ يَتَوَكَّلُونَ

innahu laysa lahu sulṭānun ʿalā alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿalā rabbihim yatawakkalūn

Al-Naḥl 16:99

“He has no authority over those who believe and rely upon their Lord.”

Al-Naḥl 16:100 continues:

إِنَّمَا سُلْطَانُهُ عَلَى الَّذِينَ يَتَوَلَّوْنَهُ وَالَّذِينَ هُمْ بِهِ مُشْرِكُونَ

innamā sulṭānuhu ʿalā alladhīna yatawallawnahu wa-alladhīna hum bihi mushrikūn

Al-Naḥl 16:100

“His authority is only over those who take him as their patron and those who associate partners through him.”

Al-Naḥl 16:99 and Al-Naḥl 16:100 clarify two sides of the relationship: Satan's influence is not independent of human conduct and allegiance. Relying on God differs from accepting Satan as a patron. The referent of bihi in the latter verse has been read differently; “through him” is one reading, not the only possible one.

Al-Ḥijr 15:42 and Al-Isrāʾ 17:65 likewise deny Satan authority over God's servants. Sabaʾ 34:21 relates the issue to distinguishing those who believe in the Hereafter from those who doubt it.

A particularly clear witness is Satan's statement in Ibrāhīm 14:22. When the matter is settled, he says that he had no authority over his followers: he called them, and they responded.

Invitation is not identical with coercive control. Satan may tempt, promise, deceive, and prepare conditions for error; accepting his invitation does not remove human responsibility.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:30, those followed by others say:

وَمَا كَانَ لَنَا عَلَيْكُمْ مِنْ سُلْطَانٍ بَلْ كُنْتُمْ قَوْمًا طَاغِينَ

wa-mā kāna lanā ʿalaykum min sulṭānin bal kuntum qawman ṭāghīn

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:30

“We had no authority over you; rather, you were a transgressing people.”

Following false leaders does not necessarily mean being coerced by them. Yet denying their coercive control over followers does not automatically absolve deceptive leaders. Each party's responsibility depends on its actual conduct and role.

These uses distinguish accepted influence, deceptive invitation, and imposed power. That distinction matters for understanding human choice and responsibility.

6. Authority, entitlement to act, and power to prevail

Not every Quranic use concerns doctrinal proof or Satan's influence. Some concern practical authority, a recognized right, or the ability to prevail.

Authority in response to wrongful killing

Al-Isrāʾ 17:33 states:

وَلَا تَقْتُلُوا النَّفْسَ الَّتِي حَرَّمَ اللَّهُ إِلَّا بِالْحَقِّ وَمَنْ قُتِلَ مَظْلُومًا فَقَدْ جَعَلْنَا لِوَلِيِّهِ سُلْطَانًا فَلَا يُسْرِفْ فِي الْقَتْلِ إِنَّهُ كَانَ مَنْصُورًا

wa-lā taqtulū al-nafsa allatī ḥarrama Allāhu illā bi-al-ḥaqqi wa-man qutila maẓlūman fa-qad jaʿalnā li-waliyyihi sulṭānan fa-lā yusrif fī al-qatli innahu kāna manṣūrā

Al-Isrāʾ 17:33

“Do not kill the soul that The God has made inviolable, except by right. Whoever is killed wrongfully, We have granted his guardian authority; so let him not exceed the bounds in killing, for he is supported.”

Sulṭān here concerns the right and authority granted to the slain person's guardian.

It is connected with justification and legitimacy, but is more than an abstract argument: it is a right arising in response to an actual wrong.

The verse immediately limits that authority: fa-lā yusrif fī al-qatli, “let him not exceed the bounds in killing.” Possessing sulṭān therefore does not mean unlimited authority for violence or retaliation.

Sulṭān in war and defense

Al-Nisāʾ 4:91 describes people who refuse withdrawal, peace, and restraint from fighting, then speaks of clear authority against them.

In this context of war and hostility, it provides clear grounds for action against that particular group, not general permission to attack every opponent.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:144 has a different construction: a-turīdūna an tajʿalū li-llāhi ʿalaykum sulṭānan mubīnan, “Do you wish to give God clear grounds against yourselves?” Clear evidence against one's conduct is the appropriate sense. Within one surah, the noun can express grounds for action or evidence against wrongful behavior.

A valid explanation in the face of a threat

In Al-Naml 27:21, Solomon says of the hoopoe's absence:

لَأُعَذِّبَنَّهُ عَذَابًا شَدِيدًا أَوْ لَأَذْبَحَنَّهُ أَوْ لَيَأْتِيَنِّي بِسُلْطَانٍ مُبِينٍ

la-uʿadhdhibannahu ʿadhāban shadīdan aw la-adhbaḥannahu aw la-yaʾtiyannī bi-sulṭānin mubīn

Al-Naml 27:21

“I will surely punish him severely or slaughter him, unless he brings me a clear justification.”

Here sulṭān is neither government nor the hoopoe's coercive power. It is a clear reason or excuse explaining his absence.

This shows that even in a specific practical incident, sulṭān can mean a valid, defensible explanation.

Supporting authority

Al-Isrāʾ 17:80 asks God to grant sulṭānan naṣīran from Himself.

Naṣīr emphasizes assistance and support. The phrase can concern power, proof, or authority helping someone fulfill a mission and stand firm. It does not by itself designate a specific political office.

7. Empowering, the ability to pass, and the loss of authority

Taslīṭ: enabling someone to prevail

The verb sallaṭa occurs twice in the Quran.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:90 states that, had God willed, He would have empowered a group against the people addressed, and they would have fought them.

The context is confrontation and war. La-sallaṭahum ʿalaykum means that He would have put them in a position to prevail or exercise power against you.

Al-Ḥashr 59:6 states that God empowers His messengers against whomever He wills. The context concerns property obtained without charging with horses or camels.

Here too, taslīṭ concerns practical power and predominance, rather than merely presenting an argument.

The verb thus attests actual power to influence or prevail within this family. It does not establish the legitimacy of every form of human dominance or an unlimited right to rule.

Authority to pass beyond boundaries

Al-Raḥmān 55:33 states:

يَا مَعْشَرَ الْجِنِّ وَالْإِنْسِ إِنِ اسْتَطَعْتُمْ أَنْ تَنْفُذُوا مِنْ أَقْطَارِ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ فَانْفُذُوا لَا تَنْفُذُونَ إِلَّا بِسُلْطَانٍ

yā maʿshara al-jinni wa-al-insi ini istaṭaʿtum an tanfudhū min aqṭāri al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍi fa-unfudhū lā tanfudhūna illā bi-sulṭān

Al-Raḥmān 55:33

“Company of jinn and humankind, if you can pass beyond the regions of the heavens and earth, then pass; you cannot pass except with authority.”

Sulṭān accompanies capacity and passage here. Power, authority, or support enabling the passage fits the construction.

The wider context concerns God's power and judgment and the limitations of jinn and humans. Bi-sulṭānin cannot therefore be confined without evidence to space technology, political permission, or a particular material instrument.

Nor does the verse establish that every human movement in known space is absolutely impossible. It speaks of passing beyond the regions of the heavens and earth, whose scope requires contextual examination.

Sulṭān here concerns the power or authority without which the passage in question cannot occur.

The loss of authority

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:29 states:

هَلَكَ عَنِّي سُلْطَانِيَهْ

halaka ʿannī sulṭāniyah

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:29

“My authority has deserted me.”

The speaker describes losing something on which he had relied.

In the context of reckoning, sulṭān may mean worldly power and influence or the grounds and ability to defend oneself. What the speaker regarded as support no longer serves him.

In sulṭāniyah, the yāʾ marks the first-person possessive and the final hāʾ is hāʾ al-sakt. It is not a pronoun referring to another person and adds no independent lexical meaning to the root.

8. Distinctions from related roots

Several neighboring word families help clarify sulṭān.

S L Ṭ and Q D R: Q D R also concerns ability, measure, and determination. Sulṭān may involve ability, but emphasizes effective authority, influence, or support. Being capable of an act does not necessarily mean having the right or valid grounds to perform it.

S L Ṭ and M L K: M L K concerns ownership, rule, and disposal. Sulṭān can accompany government, but is not restricted to royal power in the Quran. A person may possess authority regarding a particular right without being a king or owner.

S L Ṭ and Q H R: Qahr emphasizes overpowering and domination. Sulṭān may involve such power, but its evidentiary use does not mean violence. An argument prevailing against an unsupported claim differs from military dominance.

Sulṭān compared with ḥujjah and burhān: These terms concern evidence and demonstration. Sulṭān often approaches that field, but its uses for entitlement to act, predominance, and empowerment extend beyond purely logical proof. This comparison does not decide the disputed derivation of burhān.

S L Ṭ and ʾ M R: Amr concerns command, instruction, or an affair. A command may be backed by sulṭān, but issuing a command and having the power or authorization to implement it are distinct. A command's origin in a powerful position does not by itself prove its rightness.

These distinctions caution against a single fixed equivalent in every verse. The subject determines which aspect is active.

9. Translation suggestions for Forqan

Sulṭān should be translated according to its role in the sentence.

For claims, partnership with God, and prophetic missions, “proof,” “valid evidence,” “clear proof,” and “valid grounds” may fit. Sulṭān mubīn in prophetic narratives may be rendered “clear proof” or “clear authority,” according to context.

For Satan, “authority” or “power to dominate” is more natural because the issue is influence over people and its limits.

In passages about rights and responsibility, such as Al-Isrāʾ 17:33, “authority” or “entitlement to act” may fit. In Al-Naml 27:21, “clear justification” or “valid excuse” better conveys the sentence.

The verbs in Al-Nisāʾ 4:90 and Al-Ḥashr 59:6 may be rendered “empower” or “enable to prevail.”

In Al-Raḥmān 55:33, “power” and “authority” are possibilities, but the choice must preserve the connection with capacity, passage, and the full context.

In Al-Ḥāqqah 69:29, “my power and authority” and “my grounds of defense” represent two readings worth examining. The main translation should follow the context, with an alternative explained separately when necessary.

An interpretation such as “internal logic,” “political government,” “miracle,” “authorization for war,” or “technology” must not replace sulṭān without sufficient contextual evidence.

Conclusion

S L Ṭ belongs to a field of power, intensity, firmness, influence, and predominance. Early Arabic attests physical and descriptive uses; dating every sense of sulṭān requires separate evidence.

Its two main Quranic manifestations are the noun sulṭān, expressing power, proof, authority, or effective support, and the verb sallaṭa, meaning to empower someone to prevail or exert influence.

Sulṭān may be evidence supporting a claim, authority granted in defense of a right, power supporting a prophet against enemies, or authority whose limits clarify Satan's influence.

Across these uses, sulṭān denotes effective authority or supporting grounds whose force appears in the relevant field: argument, entitlement, or power to prevail.

Such power does not always establish rightness. Possessing power differs from possessing a right; gaining influence differs from having valid proof. The Quran speaks of divine authority, human entitlement, Satan's authority over those who adopt him as patron, and claims for which no authority was revealed.

Two questions must therefore remain distinct: What power or support exists, and what determines its validity and limits? The first clarifies the lexical field; the second is answered through context and the criteria of right and justice.
