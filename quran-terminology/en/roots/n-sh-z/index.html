Rising and standing up: from elevated ground and raising bones to discord and estrangement between spouses

1. Introducing the root and its central meaning

In Arabic, the root N SH Z concerns rising, becoming elevated, or standing out above a previous surface or position.

Nashz and nashaz name an elevated part of the ground that rises above its surroundings. It may be a small hill or mound; it need not be a great mountain.

The verb nashaza is also used for getting up from a seat, something rising from its position, and a bodily part protruding or becoming prominent.

Another use concerns discord in human relationships, particularly when either spouse departs from proper marital conduct, mistreats the other, or adopts an antagonistic attitude toward them.

This branch has an intelligible figurative connection with elevation: just as a mound stands out from level ground, one person’s conduct may depart from a relationship’s ordinarily harmonious condition.

This image should not, however, become a claim about the certain historical origin of every derivative.

In marriage, nushūz does not literally mean that a woman or man rises bodily above the ground. The word has acquired an established social use in that setting.

Nor should every instance of rising or elevation be considered blameworthy. The Qur’an uses the same root in a positive command to rise from a gathering.

Thus, rising and departing from a previous position form the root’s central semantic axis; elevated ground, getting up from a seat, raising bones, and marital discord are distinct applications.

Each verse requires us to identify what rises, the condition it leaves behind, and whether the subject is bodily movement or conduct between people.

Qur’anic forms

In the reading transmitted by Ḥafṣ, N SH Z occurs five times in four verses, in three morphological categories.

Qur’anic formCountVerse
نُنْشِزُهَا1Al-Baqarah 2:259
نُشُوزَهُنَّ1Al-Nisāʾ 4:34
نُشُوزًا1Al-Nisāʾ 4:128
انْشُزُوا1Al-Mujādilah 58:11
فَانْشُزُوا1Al-Mujādilah 58:11

Nunshizuhā is Form IV and concerns raising or setting something upright.

Nushūz is a Form I verbal noun, used in two verses of Al-Nisāʾ for discordant conduct within marriage.

Unshuzū is a Form I imperative meaning “rise.”

In Al-Mujādilah 58:11, unshuzū and inshizū reflect two vocalizations of the Form I verb and retain the sense of rising. In Al-Baqarah 2:259, nunshizuhā and nunshiruhā belong to two independent roots, N SH Z and N SH R.

Arabic also has forms such as nashz, nashaz, nishāz, nāshiz, nāshizah, anshāz, nawāshiz, and inshāz. Apart from the derivatives listed above, these do not occur in the Qur’an.

2. Early Arabic usage: elevation and prominence

Nashz: elevated ground

An early lexical explanation gives the following central meaning for N SH Z:

أَصْلٌ صَحِيحٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى ارْتِفَاعٍ وَعُلُوٍّ

aṣlun ṣaḥīḥun yadullu ʿalā irtifāʿin wa-ʿuluwwin

Meaning:

“A root denoting rising and elevation.”

In the same explanation, al-nashaz is an elevated and prominent place on the ground.

In early Arabic, nashz and nashaz name ground that stands higher than its surroundings.

Elevation here is relative.

A mound may be called nashz in comparison with the level ground around it, while being very small in comparison with a mountain.

The word therefore does not specify a height in units of measurement.

Nor must nashz be rugged rock or impassable terrain. Some lexical explanations explicitly state that this elevation need not be particularly massive or rough.

Anshāz, nushūz, and nishāz are recorded plurals for such elevations.

In the two verses of Al-Nisāʾ, however, nushūz is not a plural meaning mounds: it is the verb’s verbal noun in its marital use.

Recognizing a word’s shape alone is therefore insufficient; grammar and context also matter.

A poetic example: a fox on a mound

Al-Akhṭal, a poet of the Umayyad period, describes a broad desert:

تَرَى الثَّعْلَبَ الْحَوْلِيَّ فِيهَا كَأَنَّهُ
إِذَا مَا عَلَا نَشْزًا حِصَانٌ مُجَلَّلُ

tarā al-thaʿlaba al-ḥawliyya fīhā ka-annahu / idhā mā ʿalā nashzan ḥiṣānun mujallalu

Translation:

“You see the year-old fox there as though, when it climbs a mound, it were a horse covered with a cloth.”

In this verse, nashzan names elevated ground.

Within the image of the wide desert, the poet makes a small animal appear large: the fox on a mound is likened to a covered horse. This poetic image does not establish a rule that every animal looks larger from a distance.

The example clearly illustrates the root’s spatial meaning.

Al-Akhṭal lived after the Qur’an’s revelation. His poetry is therefore evidence for classical Arabic, rather than direct evidence from the pre-Islamic period.

The verse is attributed to al-Akhṭal and aptly illustrates nashz as elevated ground.

It does not establish that nashz must always be very high; the image depends on the mound’s relative elevation.

Bodily prominence

Arabic also uses nāshiz for something protruding above the body’s surface or its usual position.

For example, ʿirqun nāshiz describes a prominent, visible vein.

Rakabun nāshiz likewise describes prominence of the pubic mound. Rakab here does not mean “knee”; the Arabic word for a knee is rukbah.

These uses are close to the literal sense of elevation and prominence.

Elevated ground and a prominent bodily part have an evident semantic connection: in each, something stands above the surrounding surface.

A prominent bodily part does not by itself indicate disease or injury. The prominence may be natural or, in another setting, indicate an unusual condition.

Disease, fear, and pain should therefore not be included in the root’s general meaning.

Nashaza al-qalb: a rising or troubled heart

Arabic poetry also uses this family to describe the heart in a state of fear.

A verse attributed to al-Farazdaq reads:

بِهِ اطْمَأَنَّتْ قُلُوبُ الْقَوْمِ إِذْ نَشَزَتْ
إِذَا الْجَبَانُ رَأَى لِلْمَوْتِ أَلْوَانًا

bihi iṭmaʾannat qulūbu al-qawmi idh nashazat / idhā al-jabānu raʾā li-al-mawti alwānan

A translation close to the sense:

“Through him the people’s hearts grew calm after rising in fear and turmoil, when the coward saw death in many forms.”

Here, nashaza concerns a heart rising or becoming disturbed through fear.

The image should not be treated as a literal assertion that the heart physically moves from its natural position.

Al-Farazdaq was also a poet of the Islamic period; the verse is consequently not pre-Qur’anic evidence.

Its relevance is that N SH Z can describe literary situations beyond terrain and sitting.

Rising from a seat

Nashaza al-rajulu min majlisihi means that a man got up from his seat.

This use is clearly attested in early Arabic.

Nashaza fī majlisihi may also mean rising, or raising oneself a little, in a gathering.

The connection with the spatial meaning is clear: a person moves from sitting to a higher, standing position.

Rising from a gathering does not in itself mean disobedience, discourtesy, or ending a relationship.

Someone may rise out of respect, get up to perform a task, or leave after a meeting has ended.

Al-Mujādilah 58:11 uses this sense in a positive command.

Describing a mount

Dābbatun nashīzah is recorded for an animal on whose back a rider or saddle does not readily remain settled.

The use may relate to unevenness or prominence in the animal’s bodily condition.

It does not establish that marital nushūz always means bodily restlessness.

A figurative connection between an unsettled position and a discordant relationship is conceivable, but each use has its own established meaning.

3. Marital nushūz: a use shared by women and men

Arabic lexical explanations record nushūz for discord and departure from proper conduct within marriage.

The crucial point is that this use is not restricted to women.

An early explanation expressly states that the word can concern either spouse:

النُّشُوزُ يَكُونُ بَيْنَ الزَّوْجَيْنِ

al-nushūzu yakūnu bayna al-zawjayni

Meaning:

“Nushūz occurs between the two spouses.”

The explanation continues by describing each spouse’s aversion and antagonism toward the other.

Lexical explanations also give the nushūz of wives and husbands after introducing elevation and prominence.

For a wife, they describe discord and a relationship becoming difficult for her husband.

For a husband, they also describe harshness and mistreatment toward his wife.

The husband’s conduct is even explained through mistreatment, inflicting harm, and harshness.

Thus, marital nushūz is not, as a lexical matter, an inherently female word.

The forms nushūzahunna and nushūzan in the two verses of Al-Nisāʾ identify different parties through pronouns and sentence structure.

That distinction belongs to context. It does not mean that one root denotes wrongdoing for a woman and something wholly unrelated for a man.

Does nushūz mean only disobedience?

Some dictionaries and works of jurisprudence explain a wife’s nushūz as disobedience or defiance toward her husband.

That explanation reflects part of the word’s historical and legal use.

Defining Qur’anic nushūz only as “a wife disobeying her husband,” however, would fail to account for its use of a husband in Al-Nisāʾ 4:128.

Lexical explanations also describe a husband’s nushūz through distancing, harshness, mistreatment, and harming his wife.

Its semantic field is thus broader than refusing a command.

Conversely, it should not be extended to every difference of preference or passing dissatisfaction.

Spouses may disagree about a decision without either mistreating the other.

One spouse may also refuse an improper or harmful demand. That refusal alone does not establish nushūz without examining the demand’s legitimacy and their mutual responsibilities.

In marriage, an appropriate explanation is therefore serious discord, harshness, or departure from proper relational conduct and commitments.

Identifying an actual instance requires examining conduct, both parties’ rights, and the relationship’s circumstances, rather than accepting one spouse’s allegation alone.

4. Al-Nisāʾ 4:34: concern about a wife’s nushūz

The verse includes:

وَاللَّاتِي تَخَافُونَ نُشُوزَهُنَّ فَعِظُوهُنَّ وَاهْجُرُوهُنَّ فِي الْمَضَاجِعِ وَاضْرِبُوهُنَّ

wa-allātī takhāfūna nushūzahunna fa-ʿiẓūhunna wa-uhjurūhunna fī al-maḍājiʿi wa-iḍribūhunna

A rendering close to the wording:

“And those women whose nushūz you fear: admonish them, leave them apart in the sleeping places, and strike them.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:34, excerpt

Here, nushūzahunna is a verbal noun with a feminine plural pronoun.

Hunna identifies the nushūz as that of the women mentioned in the sentence.

The verbal noun itself does not name the precise conduct.

A. What does nushūz describe?

The lexical evidence relates it here to serious discord or departure from proper marital relations.

Some early explanations describe strong aversion, defiance, and a wife’s mistreatment of her husband.

These explanations do not all have the same scope.

Some emphasize disobedience; others aversion and discord; still others conduct that disrupts the marital bond.

“Disobedience” may therefore convey only one recognized aspect of nushūz.

“Sexual infidelity” is also too narrow, since the word itself does not specify that particular conduct.

The Qur’an has other words and constructions for illicit sexual conduct; they should not all be equated with nushūz.

B. Takhāfūna and the possibility of conduct

The verse says:

تَخَافُونَ نُشُوزَهُنَّ

takhāfūna nushūzahunna

“You fear their nushūz.”

Takhāfūna belongs to KH W F and in this construction means fearing nushūz.

The verse thus concerns apprehension about discordant conduct occurring or continuing.

It should not be understood as permission to pass certain judgment on any personal suspicion.

Possibility and apprehension are logically distinct from proving conduct.

Determining which signs are reliable and which responses are permitted or forbidden requires examining the whole verse and other Qur’anic principles concerning justice and spouses’ rights.

The meaning of N SH Z alone cannot settle that question.

C. Nushūz and the following expressions

Several other verbs follow nushūzahunna.

Fa-ʿiẓūhunna belongs to W ʿ Ẓ.

Wa-uhjurūhunna belongs to H J R.

Wa-iḍribūhunna belongs to Ḍ R B.

Each requires independent examination of its construction and context.

The meaning of nushūz alone does not establish that every response to it is unconditionally permitted in every situation.

Nor can differences in interpreting wa-iḍribūhunna be settled merely by changing the meaning of nushūz.

That requires an independent study of Ḍ R B, the sequence of clauses, the limits of spouses’ rights, and other relevant verses.

This root study seeks to identify the conduct under discussion, rather than finally determine every rule governing marital disagreements.

D. The next verse: arbitration and reconciliation

Al-Nisāʾ 4:35 states:

وَإِنْ خِفْتُمْ شِقَاقَ بَيْنِهِمَا فَابْعَثُوا حَكَمًا مِنْ أَهْلِهِ وَحَكَمًا مِنْ أَهْلِهَا إِنْ يُرِيدَا إِصْلَاحًا يُوَفِّقِ اللَّهُ بَيْنَهُمَا

wa-in khiftum shiqāqa baynihimā fa-ibʿathū ḥakaman min ahlihi wa-ḥakaman min ahlihā in yurīdā iṣlāḥan yuwaffiqi allāhu baynahumā

Translation:

“If you fear a breach between them, appoint an arbiter from his family and an arbiter from hers. If they desire reconciliation, The God will bring agreement between them.”

Shiqāq belongs to SH Q Q and concerns a breach and discord between two parties.

Arbitration and reconciliation then follow.

This sequence shows that the marital issue is not confined to one individual’s conduct. If the dispute grows, examining and repairing the relationship between both parties also comes into view.

Nushūz and shiqāq nevertheless are not complete synonyms.

Nushūz may begin with one spouse’s conduct, whereas shiqāq highlights the breach between the two parties.

5. Al-Nisāʾ 4:128: a wife’s concern about her husband’s nushūz

وَإِنِ امْرَأَةٌ خَافَتْ مِنْ بَعْلِهَا نُشُوزًا أَوْ إِعْرَاضًا فَلَا جُنَاحَ عَلَيْهِمَا أَنْ يُصْلِحَا بَيْنَهُمَا صُلْحًا وَالصُّلْحُ خَيْرٌ وَأُحْضِرَتِ الْأَنْفُسُ الشُّحَّ وَإِنْ تُحْسِنُوا وَتَتَّقُوا فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ كَانَ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ خَبِيرًا

wa-ini imraʾatun khāfat min baʿlihā nushūzan aw iʿrāḍan fa-lā junāḥa ʿalayhimā an yuṣliḥā baynahumā ṣulḥan wa-al-ṣulḥu khayrun wa-uḥḍirati al-anfusu al-shuḥḥa wa-in tuḥsinū wa-tattaqū fa-inna allāha kāna bi-mā taʿmalūna khabīran

Translation:

“If a woman fears nushūz or withdrawal from her husband, there is no blame upon the two if they reach a settlement between themselves; settlement is better. Souls are beset by avarice. If you do good and remain mindful, surely The God is fully aware of what you do.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:128

This is the most important Qur’anic evidence that marital nushūz is not attributed only to women.

Here, a woman fears nushūz or withdrawal on her husband’s part.

A. Min baʿlihā nushūzan

Baʿl in this context means the husband.

Min baʿlihā identifies the source of the conduct feared.

Nushūz is therefore attributed to the husband in this verse.

Defining every instance as “a wife refusing her husband’s command” would conflict with one of the Qur’an’s two marital uses.

Lexical and exegetical explanations describe a husband’s nushūz through harshness, mistreatment, aversion toward his wife, and departure from proper conduct.

Early explanations also discuss a husband’s mistreatment, withholding some of his wife’s rights, and distancing himself from marital relations.

These explanations fit the word’s social scope.

They do not establish that every temporary distance or inward dissatisfaction, without harmful conduct, constitutes proven nushūz.

B. Distinguishing nushūzan and iʿrāḍan

The verse uses two separate words:

نُشُوزًا أَوْ إِعْرَاضًا

nushūzan aw iʿrāḍan

“Nushūz or withdrawal.”

Iʿrāḍ belongs to ʿ R Ḍ and here concerns turning away and inattention.

In lexical explanations of marriage, nushūz may encompass antagonistic conduct, harshness, or active mistreatment.

Iʿrāḍ places greater emphasis on distancing and turning away.

For example, a husband may hurt his wife through speech and action, or turn away from companionship and attention to her.

The two conditions may coexist in practice, but are not lexically identical.

Aw, “or,” also presents nushūz and iʿrāḍ as distinct descriptions, though their application may overlap in a particular case.

It is therefore preferable not to restrict nushūz to “inattention,” a sense also expressed by iʿrāḍ.

C. Does a husband’s nushūz mean depriving a wife of every right?

Some jurisprudential explanations mention withholding companionship, failing to provide financial entitlements, or favoring another wife.

These can be examples of marital mistreatment or harshness.

The word nushūz itself, however, does not specify a complete list of rights and duties.

The phrase nushūzan min baʿlihā alone cannot yield a maintenance amount, a division of time, or other legal details.

Those matters require independent examination of the relevant verses and evidence.

For a root study, the essential point is that nushūz can also describe a husband’s discordant and harmful conduct toward his wife.

D. Settlement and reconciliation

After mentioning a husband’s nushūz or withdrawal, the verse says:

فَلَا جُنَاحَ عَلَيْهِمَا أَنْ يُصْلِحَا بَيْنَهُمَا صُلْحًا

fa-lā junāḥa ʿalayhimā an yuṣliḥā baynahumā ṣulḥan

“There is no blame upon the two if they reach a settlement between themselves.”

Ṣulḥ and iṣlāḥ here belong to Ṣ L Ḥ.

These words should not be equated with nushūz.

Nushūz is the concern; settlement describes one response to the situation that has arisen.

The pronoun humā in ʿalayhimā refers to both parties; the pronoun itself does not mean agreement. The possibility of reaching a settlement is expressed by an yuṣliḥā baynahumā ṣulḥan.

This sentence alone does not establish that a woman must tolerate every kind of mistreatment or relinquish her rights.

The following verses also discuss justice within marriage.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:129 states:

فَلَا تَمِيلُوا كُلَّ الْمَيْلِ فَتَذَرُوهَا كَالْمُعَلَّقَةِ

fa-lā tamīlū kulla al-mayli fa-tadharūhā ka-al-muʿallaqati

“Do not incline entirely to one side, leaving her as though suspended and unresolved.”

The warning shows that merely retaining the outward form of a relationship, while one person remains deprived and in limbo, cannot be disregarded.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:130 also presents the possibility of separation.

Settlement is thus important in this context, but should not become a requirement to remain in every relationship.

E. What the two verses share

Comparing Al-Nisāʾ 4:34 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:128 establishes a clear point:

The first concerns a wife’s nushūz.

The second concerns a husband’s nushūz.

Thus, Qur’anic nushūz is not restricted to one sex.

This does not mean the sentence structures, proposed responses, and every legal dimension of the two verses are identical.

Those differences require examining each verse independently.

Lexical study can confidently establish that the same description, nushūz, can apply to discordant conduct by either spouse.

6. Al-Mujādilah 58:11: rising from a gathering

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِذَا قِيلَ لَكُمْ تَفَسَّحُوا فِي الْمَجَالِسِ فَافْسَحُوا يَفْسَحِ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ وَإِذَا قِيلَ انْشُزُوا فَانْشُزُوا يَرْفَعِ اللَّهُ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا مِنْكُمْ وَالَّذِينَ أُوتُوا الْعِلْمَ دَرَجَاتٍ وَاللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ خَبِيرٌ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū idhā qīla lakum tafassaḥū fī al-majālisi fa-ifsaḥū yafsaḥi allāhu lakum wa-idhā qīla unshuzū fa-unshuzū yarfaʿi allāhu alladhīna āmanū minkum wa-alladhīna ūtū al-ʿilma darajātin wa-allāhu bi-mā taʿmalūna khabīrun

Translation:

“You who have believed: when you are told to make room in gatherings, make room, and The God will grant you spaciousness. When you are told to rise, rise. The God raises those of you who have believed and those given knowledge by degrees; The God is fully aware of what you do.”

Al-Mujādilah 58:11

This is the most important Qur’anic evidence for the direct sense of rising in N SH Z.

A. Unshuzū fa-unshuzū

In the reading transmitted by Ḥafṣ, both verbs have a ḍammah on shīn.

Unshuzū is a plural imperative.

Fa-unshuzū repeats the same command after the conjunction fa.

Its direct meaning is “rise.”

Rising here is a positive action that is commanded.

The root therefore cannot inherently mean defiance or mistreatment.

Such a fixed meaning would make the command unshuzū difficult to explain.

The verse shows that the basic sense of rising can describe appropriate, commendable conduct in context.

B. Distinguishing tafassaḥū and unshuzū

The opening of the verse says:

تَفَسَّحُوا فِي الْمَجَالِسِ

tafassaḥū fī al-majālisi

This means to make room in gatherings.

The verb belongs to F S Ḥ and concerns making space or providing greater room.

Unshuzū, however, belongs to N SH Z and means “rise.”

Making room can happen while people remain seated: they may move a little to provide a place for someone arriving.

Rising, however, may entail leaving a seat or standing to perform another task.

The two commands are related, but are not complete synonyms.

Unshuzū should not simply be translated as “move over a little” unless a particular contextual indication supports that reading.

C. Rise to do what?

Exegetical explanations offer several readings of the command unshuzū.

Some understand rising from the gathering and leaving a seat so that others can attend.

Others mention getting up for a good deed, prayer, or an assigned duty.

Some accounts also describe rising and leaving the Prophet after remaining too long.

A broader reading takes the command to include rising in response to a call to good, without restricting it to one particular task.

The expression unshuzū itself does not name a specific task.

The base translation should therefore not replace “rise” with “rise for war” or “rise for prayer,” since that specifies more than the wording does.

The different possibilities can be examined in commentary on the verse.

D. Does yarfaʿi allāhu belong to the same root?

The verse continues:

يَرْفَعِ اللَّهُ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا مِنْكُمْ وَالَّذِينَ أُوتُوا الْعِلْمَ دَرَجَاتٍ

yarfaʿi allāhu alladhīna āmanū minkum wa-alladhīna ūtū al-ʿilma darajātin

“The God raises those of you who have believed and those given knowledge by degrees.”

Yarfaʿi belongs to R F ʿ, not N SH Z.

Bodily rising and elevation in rank have a notable semantic connection.

That connection should not lead us to define N SH Z directly as “rising in spiritual rank.”

Unshuzū commands rising.

Yarfaʿi allāhu gives a subsequent result associated with faith and knowledge.

The two meanings should remain distinct.

E. The readings unshuzū and inshizū

Ḥafṣ reads unshuzū with a ḍammah on shīn; when beginning with the word, its connecting hamzah also takes a ḍammah.

Other recognized readings also have inshizū with a kasrah on shīn; when beginning with that form, the connecting hamzah takes a kasrah.

The difference reflects two vocalizations of the Form I verb: nashaza yanshuzu and nashaza yanshizu.

Both are recorded for rising and standing up.

The different vowel on shīn therefore does not change the command’s central meaning.

Two wholly different duties should not be derived from it.

7. Al-Baqarah 2:259: raising bones

The account of the person who passed a ruined settlement includes:

وَانْظُرْ إِلَى الْعِظَامِ كَيْفَ نُنْشِزُهَا ثُمَّ نَكْسُوهَا لَحْمًا

wa-unẓur ilā al-ʿiẓāmi kayfa nunshizuhā thumma naksūhā laḥman

Translation:

“Look at the bones, how We raise them and set them together, then clothe them with flesh.”

Al-Baqarah 2:259, excerpt

Nunshizuhā here is Form IV.

The construction emphasizes raising or setting something upright.

A. The role of Form IV

Nashaza can mean rising oneself.

Anshaza can mean raising something from its position.

In the Qur’anic sentence, nunshizuhā means “We raise the bones” or “We set them upright.”

Lexical explanations also associate this use with placing bones in their positions and assembling them together.

That explanation is intelligible in context, since the verse next mentions clothing the bones with flesh.

The verb’s direct meaning should nevertheless be distinguished from explaining the process.

The direct sense is raising or setting the bones upright.

The word itself does not reveal the precise connections between bones, the sequence of tissue growth, or a biological mechanism.

B. Thumma naksūhā laḥman

Naksūhā belongs to K S W and means clothing or covering.

Laḥman specifies what the bones are clothed with: flesh.

The verse thus expresses two distinct actions:

First, raising the bones.

Then, clothing them with flesh.

Nunshizuhā should not be translated as “We grow flesh on the bones,” since flesh is expressed by the following verb and its object.

Nor should medical stages of embryonic growth or bone reconstruction be extracted from the phrase without independent evidence.

The verse concerns the power to recreate after death, rather than providing biological instructions.

C. The readings nunshizuhā and nunshiruhā

Ḥafṣ and several other recognized readings have the form with zāy:

نُنْشِزُهَا

nunshizuhā

This belongs to N SH Z and concerns raising or setting upright.

Nāfiʿ, Ibn Kathīr, Abū ʿAmr, Abū Jaʿfar, and Yaʿqūb, however, read the form with rāʾ:

نُنْشِرُهَا

nunshiruhā

This belongs to N SH R and in this context concerns restoring to life.

The difference matters.

Nunshizuhā emphasizes setting the bones upright.

Nunshiruhā concerns bringing them to life within the act of recreation.

Both readings fit the verse’s subject, but belong to different roots.

Nunshiruhā should therefore not be presented as a derivative of N SH Z.

Their similar written shapes do not establish that N SH Z and N SH R are synonymous in every application.

D. Whose bones?

Earlier in the same verse, the person’s donkey is mentioned:

وَانْظُرْ إِلَىٰ حِمَارِكَ

wa-unẓur ilā ḥimārika

“And look at your donkey.”

The bones are mentioned afterward.

This sequence strongly supports referring the bones to the donkey. The noun al-ʿiẓām alone, however, does not identify whose bones they are.

That reading is well known in early commentary; some transmitted accounts also offer other possibilities concerning the bones’ referent.

Not every narrative detail—such as bones scattered in particular directions, wind gathering them, or an angel breathing into the body—should be added to the Qur’anic text.

Those details are absent from the expression under examination.

What is clear for studying N SH Z is the raising of bones in a scene concerning recreation.

8. Comparison with related roots

N SH Z and R F ʿ: rising and raising

R F ʿ concerns lifting and raising.

Spatial uses of N SH Z also concern height and prominence.

R F ʿ nevertheless has a broad semantic field: the Qur’an uses it for elevating rank, spiritual elevation, and raising things.

Qur’anic N SH Z concerns rising, raising bones, and discord within marriage.

The two overlap in some applications but are not complete synonyms.

Study of R F ʿ

Both roots occur in Al-Mujādilah 58:11, with different roles.

N SH Z and Q W M: rising and standing

Qāma concerns standing or rising.

Nashaza can likewise describe getting up from a seat.

In Al-Mujādilah 58:11, unshuzū has been explained as qūmū, “stand up.”

N SH Z also has other uses, including prominent ground and spouses’ nushūz.

Its entire family cannot therefore be equated with Q W M.

Study of Q W M

N SH Z and N SH R: raising and reviving

The variant readings bring these roots alongside each other in Al-Baqarah 2:259.

N SH Z concerns elevation and setting upright.

N SH R concerns opening, spreading, and, in some uses, restoring to life after death.

The senses come close in the scene of recreation, but remain lexically independent.

Study of N SH R

N SH Z and N SH ʾ: prominence and origination

N SH ʾ in Arabic concerns coming into existence, beginning, growing, and bringing into existence.

N SH Z places greater emphasis on rising or becoming prominent relative to a previous condition.

Growth can sometimes involve rising, but that overlap does not make the two families identical.

For example, the Qur’an uses anshaʾa for bringing humans and gardens into existence, whereas nunshizuhā in Al-Baqarah concerns raising bones.

Study of N SH ʾ

N SH Z and SH Q Q: discord and a relational breach

Shiqāq belongs to SH Q Q and concerns a breach or division between two parties.

Marital nushūz may concern discordant conduct by one party.

Nushūz may lead to shiqāq, but the words are not identical.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:35 concerns a breach between the spouses.

This distinction shows the Qur’an using different words for discordant conduct itself and the breach that develops in the relationship.

Study of SH Q Q

N SH Z and iʿrāḍ: discord and withdrawal

These words occur together in Al-Nisāʾ 4:128.

Nushūz can involve harshness, mistreatment, and departure from proper conduct.

Iʿrāḍ more particularly concerns turning away and inattention.

The two may coexist in one behavior, but are not entirely identical in meaning.

Nushūz and ʿiṣyān: discord and disobedience

ʿIṣyān concerns refusing or disobeying a command.

Some historical explanations relate nushūz to a wife’s disobedience toward her husband.

Its use of a husband, alongside the root’s other uses, shows that Arabic nushūz is not restricted to ʿiṣyān.

Conversely, not every act of disobedience is marital nushūz.

The two should not be substituted for each other throughout the Qur’an.

9. Translation questions and proposals for Forqan

Is nushūz always blameworthy?

No.

In Al-Mujādilah 58:11, unshuzū means rising and occurs in a positive command.

Al-Baqarah 2:259 concerns raising bones.

The negative marital use should therefore not be imposed on the entire root family.

The root itself does not determine moral value, though some established social uses occur in blameworthy contexts.

Is nushūz restricted to women?

No.

The Qur’an discusses a wife’s nushūz in Al-Nisāʾ 4:34 and a husband’s nushūz in Al-Nisāʾ 4:128.

The word belongs to the same family in both cases.

That commonality does not make every detail of the conduct, rulings, or responses identical in the two verses.

Does nushūz mean any difference of opinion?

No.

A difference of viewpoint alone is insufficient to establish nushūz.

Lexical explanations of marriage discuss discord, harshness, mistreatment, and departure from proper relations.

“Difference of opinion” is therefore too broad and does not adequately convey disruption of the relationship.

“Infidelity” and “refusal of any demand by the husband” are also too restrictive or interpretive.

Does nushūz mean violence?

Some lexical explanations connect a husband’s nushūz with striking and harming his wife.

Physical violence is only one possible instance of mistreatment, rather than the word’s entire meaning.

Nushūz can also involve harshness and depriving the other person of proper rights.

The root should therefore not be restricted to “beating and assault.”

Nor does its use of a wife establish that every suspicion or dispute permits violence against her. Such a claim does not follow from the root’s lexical meaning.

For Al-Nisāʾ 4:34, a suitable proposal is:

“And those women whose serious marital discord you fear…”

For Al-Nisāʾ 4:128:

“If a woman fears discord or withdrawal from her husband…”

In these two renderings, “discord” retains the shared meaning.

Commentary should clarify that nushūz is not merely a difference of preference or every passing dissatisfaction, but concerns serious disruption in marital conduct and relations.

In Al-Baqarah 2:259, “raising the bones” is appropriate; nunshizuhā and the alternate reading nunshiruhā should be explained separately.

In Al-Mujādilah 58:11, “rise” is a concise and direct equivalent.

Non-Qur’anic uses

For established Arabic forms, the following equivalents are appropriate:

Nashz or nashaz: a mound or elevated ground.

Nashaza min majlisihi: he got up from his seat.

ʿIrqun nāshiz: a prominent vein.

Anshaza al-shayʾa: he raised something.

Nāshiz in marriage: someone whose conduct is discordant within the relationship; the precise meaning depends on context.

Nashāz in modern musical usage: discordance or a sound outside harmonious pitch.

This last use is recognized in modern Arabic, but is absent from the Qur’an and should not become the historical meaning of every derivative.

10. Conclusion

Arabic N SH Z concerns rising, becoming elevated, and standing out above a previous surface or condition.

Material uses include elevated ground, getting up from a seat, or a prominent bodily part.

Form IV concerns raising or setting something upright.

In its social use, nushūz concerns discord and departure from proper marital conduct.

Early lexical explanations attribute such conduct to wives and husbands alike.

Poetic evidence, including al-Akhṭal’s fox climbing a mound, illustrates the direct sense of elevated ground.

That evidence should not restrict social nushūz to a bodily image alone.

The root occurs five times in four Qur’anic verses.

In Al-Baqarah 2:259, nunshizuhā concerns raising bones.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:34, nushūzahunna concerns women’s marital discord.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:128, nushūzan concerns discordant conduct by a husband.

In Al-Mujādilah 58:11, unshuzū and fa-unshuzū are commands to rise.

The family N SH Z is therefore not inherently restricted to women, wrongdoing, or disobedience. The social use of marital nushūz must remain distinct from the root’s general meaning.

The root concerns elevation and rising; when used of marriage, it concerns discordant conduct and departure from proper relations.

Nushūz also overlaps with shiqāq, iʿrāḍ, ʿiṣyān, and rafʿ, but none is its complete synonym.

In the variant readings of Al-Baqarah 2:259, nunshizuhā from N SH Z must also be distinguished from nunshiruhā from N SH R.

Proposed final definition for Forqan

The root N SH Z centrally concerns something rising, becoming elevated, or standing out above its previous surface or position.

Material applications can concern elevated ground, a person rising, or setting something upright.

In marriage, nushūz concerns serious discord, harshness, or departure from proper relational conduct and commitments, conduct which can lexically be attributed to either spouse.

In the Qur’an, nunshizuhā concerns raising bones, unshuzū means rising, and nushūz concerns marital discord.

Thus, rising and elevation form this family’s central semantic axis, while marital nushūz is an established social use that should not be reduced to any disagreement or solely to a wife’s disobedience toward her husband.

Each application must be understood through its morphological form, the party involved, and the particular verse’s context.
