The Qur’anic Root Q L ʿ (ق ل ع)

Uprooting and detaching; desisting, ceasing, and passing away

Qur’anic forms

أَقْلِعِي

Aqliʿī

Cease; feminine singular imperative, 11:44

1. Basic meaning and linguistic background

The root has two prominent uses: uprooting or detaching something from its place, such as a tree or tooth; and, in form IV, desisting or ceasing, as when rainfall ends or a fever subsides.

Verses transmitted from Al-Aʿshā and Al-Shammākh illustrate sails and the passing of fever. Their precise dates of composition are uncertain, and they do not independently establish a pre-Qur’anic date for the specific sense of rainfall ceasing.

The Qur’anic form examined: أَقْلِعِي (aqliʿī), cease or desist.

Other recorded forms include قَلَعَ (qalaʿa), uproot; قَلْع (qalʿ), uprooting; اِقْتَلَعَ (iqtalaʿa), pull out; اِنْقَلَعَ (inqalaʿa), become uprooted; إِقْلَاع (iqlāʿ), ceasing or desisting; مَقْلُوع (maqlūʿ), uprooted; and قِلْع (qilʿ), a ship’s sail. These forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

2. Physical imagery and early Arabic evidence

Uprooting and detaching

The verb قَلَعَ can describe uprooting a tree, extracting a tooth, or removing a stone from the ground.

This is more than moving something: it involves detaching it from where it rested or from what it was attached to.

Classical Arabic also records forms of this root for pieces of earth that crack and separate from the surface as the ground dries.

Removing someone from office fits the same image. A person dismissed from a position may be called مَقْلُوع (maqlūʿ).

Ceasing and passing away

The form IV verb أَقْلَعَ (aqlaʿa) can describe an activity ending or a condition passing away.

أَقْلَعَ عَنِ الْأَمْرِ means to desist from something one had been doing. أَقْلَعَتْ عَنْهُ الْحُمَّى means that the fever left someone.

Al-Shammākh ibn Ḍirār, who lived across the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods, describes a person whose body has become thin through illness:

كَأَنَّ نَطَاةَ خَيْبَرَ زَوَّدَتْهُ بَكُورَ الْوِرْدِ رَيِّثَةَ الْقُلُوعِ

“It is as though Naṭāh of Khaybar supplied him with a fever that comes early and is slow to leave.”

Naṭāh is a place-name associated with Khaybar and, in early sources, with fever.

الْقُلُوع (al-qulūʿ) here refers to the fever passing away. رَيِّثَةَ الْقُلُوعِ describes a fever slow to depart.

The poem’s precise date is unknown, but its use of the root for the ending of a condition is clear.

Physical uprooting and desisting can be understood as sharing an image of separation from a previous condition. This semantic explanation does not by itself establish which meaning arose first.

Sails and the movement of ships

قِلْع (qilʿ) and قِلَاع (qilāʿ) are also recorded as names for a ship’s sail.

A verse attributed to Al-Aʿshā, a poet of the late pre-Islamic period, reads:

يَكُبُّ الْخَلِيَّةَ ذَاتَ الْقِلَاعِ وَقَدْ كَادَ جُؤْجُؤُهَا يَنْحَطِمْ

“It overturns the sailing vessel, whose prow is close to shattering.”

الْقِلَاع (al-qilāʿ) denotes the ship’s sails. Some sources transmit الشِّرَاع (al-shirāʿ) in its place.

Classical dictionaries explain أَقْلَعَ السَّفِينَةَ as raising the ship’s sail. In nautical usage, iqlāʿ can also refer to a ship setting out; the relationship between these usages does not establish a definite historical sequence.

In modern Arabic, the same word is used for an aircraft taking off.

These uses should not be confused with the Qur’anic meaning: a ship’s departure or an aircraft’s takeoff begins movement, whereas the sky’s iqlāʿ in the Qur’an means ceasing to rain.

قَلْعَة (qalʿah), a fortress or stronghold, especially one on elevated ground, is also recorded under this lexical family and has entered Persian from Arabic.

3. The Qur’anic use of أَقْلِعِي

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, at the close of the account of Noah’s flood:

وَقيلَ يٰأَرضُ ابلَعى ماءَكِ وَيٰسَماءُ أَقلِعى وَغيضَ الماءُ وَقُضِىَ الأَمرُ وَاستَوَت عَلَى الجودِىِّ وَقيلَ بُعدًا لِلقَومِ الظّٰلِمينَ

Wa-qīla yā arḍu blaʿī māʾaki wa-yā samāʾu aqliʿī wa-ghīḍa l-māʾu wa-quḍiya l-amru wa-stawat ʿalā l-Jūdiyyi wa-qīla buʿdan li-l-qawmi ẓ-ẓālimīn.

“And it was said: O earth, swallow your water, and O sky, cease. The water subsided, the matter was concluded, and it came to rest upon Al-Jūdī. And it was said: Away with the wrongdoing people.”

Hud 11:44

أَقْلِعِي (aqliʿī) is the form IV imperative of أَقْلَعَ ـ يُقْلِعُ (aqlaʿa–yuqliʿu), addressed to a feminine singular addressee. It is built on the omission of nūn because it is attached to the feminine addressee’s yāʾ. The final yāʾ is an attached subject pronoun, not a root letter. Its initial hamzah is hamzat al-qaṭʿ, and the sky is being addressed.

أَقْلَعَتِ السَّمَاءُ means that the sky ceased raining. In the verse, the context makes rainfall clear without explicitly naming the activity that is to cease.

Surah Al-Qamar describes the beginning of the same flood:

فَفَتَحنا أَبوٰبَ السَّماءِ بِماءٍ مُنهَمِرٍ

Fa-fataḥnā abwāba s-samāʾi bi-māʾin munhamir.

“So We opened the gates of the sky with pouring water.”

Al-Qamar 54:11

وَفَجَّرنَا الأَرضَ عُيونًا فَالتَقَى الماءُ عَلىٰ أَمرٍ قَد قُدِرَ

Wa-fajjarnā l-arḍa ʿuyūnan fa-ltaqā l-māʾu ʿalā amrin qad qudir.

“And We caused the earth to burst forth as springs, so the waters met for a matter already determined.”

Al-Qamar 54:12

These two verses describe water arriving from the sky and springing from the earth. In Hūd 11:44, the earth is commanded to swallow the water and the sky to cease raining.

أَقْلِعِي thus concerns the end of rainfall. It does not mean the sky’s destruction, the definite dispersal of every cloud, or the sky swallowing water into itself.

The verb alone does not determine whether the rain stops abruptly or gradually. Its direct meaning is the cessation of an activity previously in progress.

4. Ceasing rain, swallowing water, and its subsiding

Hūd 11:44 places three verbs from distinct roots together:

  • ابْلَعِي (iblaʿī), from ب ل ع: swallow.
  • أَقْلِعِي (aqliʿī), from ق ل ع: cease.
  • غِيضَ (ghīḍa), from غ ي ض: subsided.

The verbs concern one event but express different aspects of it.

Balʿ concerns taking water into the earth. Iqlāʿ concerns the cessation of water arriving from the sky, while ghayḍ expresses the water’s decrease and subsiding.

For an illustration, imagine a vessel receiving water while an outlet is also open. Stopping the inflow, the existing water leaving, and the water level falling are distinct concepts, even though they concern one situation.

Rendering all three verbs simply as “the water disappeared” would erase the distinctions between rainfall ceasing, water being swallowed, and water diminishing.

In this context, the command to the sky ends a natural event. The address to sky and earth does not, by itself, establish a definite account of their awareness, will, or mode of existence.

5. Comparison with nearby roots

Q L ʿ and N Z ʿ: uprooting and pulling out

Nazʿ emphasizes drawing or pulling out, while qalʿ emphasizes detachment from a position; both can describe extracting a tooth. This overlap does not remove the particular sense of ceasing in form IV of Q L ʿ.

Q L ʿ and J TH TH: detaching and uprooting from the base

Ijtithāth concerns uprooting from the base, especially of plants; qalʿ also applies to teeth, stones, and other objects. Their overlap in uprooting does not justify translating the sky’s iqlāʿ as destruction.

Ibrāhīm 14:26 uses ijtithāth for a tree uprooted from the earth and left without firm footing.

Q L ʿ and K F F: desisting and refraining

Kaff concerns restraining or refraining; iqlāʿ generally highlights the ending of an activity or condition already in progress. Refraining from starting a task fits kaff, while desisting from an ongoing task fits iqlāʿ. This is a tendency, not an absolute boundary.

6. Translation recommendations for Forqan

For أَقْلِعِي in Hūd 11:44, the proposed Persian equivalent is بازایست; the corresponding English rendering is “cease.”

“Cease raining” is also clear and contextually appropriate. In this wording, rainfall is supplied from context to make the meaning explicit.

“Cut off the rain” conveys the event but preserves less directly the address to the sky and the sense of its ceasing.

“Cease” retains the Arabic brevity and allows the activity to be understood from context, as in the original.

Outside the Qur’an, suitable equivalents include uprooting, pulling out, detaching, removing from office, desisting, ceasing, and passing away, according to form and context.

7. Conclusion

In physical uses, Q L ʿ denotes removing something from its attachment or resting place. In iqlāʿ, recognized senses include desisting from an activity, ceasing, and a condition passing away.

In the Qur’an, أَقْلِعِي commands the sky to cease raining at the end of Noah’s flood. Alongside the earth swallowing water and the water subsiding, it describes a distinct aspect of the flood’s ending.

Proposed definition for Forqan: Uprooting or detaching something from its place or attachment; in the iqlāʿ branch, desisting from something, ceasing an ongoing activity, or a previously existing condition passing away.
