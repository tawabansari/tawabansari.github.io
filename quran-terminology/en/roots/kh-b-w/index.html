Subsiding flames, diminishing fire, and the quieting of something previously ablaze

The Arabic root خ ب و concerns subsiding, quieting, and a reduction in a fire’s intensity. The verb خَبَا يَخْبُو describes flames growing shorter, a fire blazing less strongly, or a fire dying down.

Early Arabic extends this root beyond fire. It also describes an intense war abating or a person’s anger and vehemence subsiding. Something previously active, intense, or inflamed loses its force.

Subsiding is not always complete extinction. A fire may have no visible flames while its coals remain hot and burning. Conversely, it may go out so completely that no active burning remains. Both situations can fall within khubuww’s lexical range; the root alone does not determine whether heat remains in every use.

The Qur’an uses this root only in Al-Isrāʾ 17:97, which describes Hell: whenever it subsides, its blazing is increased for those confined there.

In this verse, khubuww stands against saʿīr. One concerns subsiding, the other intensified fire. This opposition is central to understanding the Qur’anic usage.

1. Forms and fundamental meaning

The root خ ب و occurs only once in the Qur’an, as خَبَتْ, formed from خَبَا ـ يَخْبُو.

Recognized Arabic forms include:

  • خَبَا ـ يَخْبُو: It subsided, grew quiet, or died down, especially of fire.
  • خَبْو: A verbal noun: subsiding and diminishing flames.
  • خُبُوّ: Another verbal noun of the same verb: a fire quieting or going out.
  • خَابٍ: Something subsiding or losing its intensity and brightness.
  • خَابِيَة: Adjectivally, a fire that has subsided or grown quiet.
  • أَخْبَى ـ يُخْبِي: He subdued or extinguished a fire.
  • إِخْبَاء: Subduing a fire or diminishing its intensity.
  • مُخْبٍ: Someone who subdues fire; in literary usage, someone diminishing enthusiasm or intensity.

Lexicons record these forms, but only خَبَتْ occurs in the Qur’an.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: Al-Isrāʾ 17:97, word twenty-five; Form I verb

Fundamental meaning

Al-ʿAyn explains خَبَتِ النَّارُ تَخْبُو through the fire going out.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise records fire, war, and vehemence as things that can takhbū: grow quiet and lose intensity.

The Cairo Arabic Language Academy’s definition also combines two features: fire growing quiet and its flame subsiding.

These definitions place diminishing intensity and quieting at the center of the root’s recognized uses.

Consider a wood fire burning strongly with tall flames. As fuel diminishes or conditions change, its flames shorten and the fire begins to subside.

The fire may still give off heat; its coals may even remain very hot. Its blazing, however, is no longer as intense.

At another stage, the fire may go out completely.

Khubūw should therefore not be confined to a single, precisely defined physical stage of combustion. Arabic uses it both for flames subsiding and for fire going out.

Together, these witnesses support this semantic core: subsiding and diminishing intensity in something previously ablaze or active, especially fire and its flame.

Uses concerning war, anger, and other intense conditions belong to the same field, although the intensity and manner of its decline differ in each case.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Muḥkam, and Tāj al-ʿArūs: fire, war, and vehemence subsiding

2. Early usage and poetic witnesses

A fire that fades and brightens again

The pre-Islamic Arab poet ʿAdī ibn Zayd al-ʿIbādī describes light within clouds in a poem opening أَرَوَاحٌ مُوَدِّعٌ أَمْ بُكُورُ:

وَسَطُهُ كَالْيَرَاعِ أَوْ سُرُجِ الْمَجْدَلِ
حِينًا يَخْبُو وَحِينًا يُنِيرُ

wasaṭuhu ka-al-yarāʿi aw suruji al-majdali / ḥīnan yakhbū wa-ḥīnan yunīru

Translation:

“Its inner light is like a firefly’s glow or lamps in a tower: at times it fades, at times it shines.”

The poet describes light whose intensity is neither uniform nor steady. It weakens at times and becomes visible and bright again at others.

يَخْبُو stands against يُنِيرُ.

This opposition connects khubuww with diminishing brightness or light growing faint.

Here the word does not mean the light’s permanent disappearance: the same thing that fades subsequently shines again.

ʿAdī ibn Zayd’s verse thus helps illuminate the Qur’anic usage. Both describe subsiding followed by renewed or increased intensity of light or fire.

This resemblance does not justify transferring every detail of the poem into the Qur’anic verse. The poem describes light in a cloud; the Qur’an concerns Hell and its blazing.

The witness establishes yakhbū’s Arabic meaning, rather than the nature of otherworldly fire.

Al-Ṭabarī: ʿAdī ibn Zayd’s witness and the opposition of yakhbū and yunīru

Al-Quṭāmī’s witness: a fire flaring again

Al-Quṭāmī, an Umayyad-period poet, describes discord and confrontation between two groups:

وَكُنَّا كَالْحَرِيقِ أَصَابَ غَابًا
فَيَخْبُو سَاعَةً وَيَهِبُّ سَاعَا

wa-kunnā ka-al-ḥarīqi aṣāba ghāban / fa-yakhbū sāʿatan wa-yahibbu sāʿā

Translation:

“We were like a blaze that struck a thicket: it subsides for a time, then flares for a time.”

Here yakhbū stands against yahibbu.

A thicket fire may quiet for a while, then regain intensity and blaze again.

Al-Quṭāmī uses this image for confrontations that sometimes abate and sometimes intensify again.

Fire is a natural reality in the image, but the poet uses its behavior to explain human relationships.

The image clarifies two points.

First, subsiding does not necessarily end the previous intensity forever.

Second, khubuww and renewed blazing can be successive conditions of the same fire.

The second hemistich has wording variants: some transmit وَيَهِبُّ, others وَيَشِبُّ, and another وَيَهِيجُ. These differ in the second verb but retain the opposition between subsiding and renewed intensity.

Al-Quṭāmī belongs to the Umayyad period, not the pre-Islamic era. This verse therefore witnesses early Arabic after the Qur’an’s revelation. ʿAdī ibn Zayd’s witness has a more suitable historical position for examining pre-Qur’anic usage.

War abating

Lexicons record خَبَتِ الْحَرْبُ: the war quieted or the fighting diminished.

War is not literally fire here. The word describes a condition previously marked by collision, violence, and intensity that has now grown quieter.

Two groups may, for example, be fighting before attacks diminish or hostilities cease.

One can then say that the war has subsided.

This alone does not establish lasting peace.

A temporary ceasefire may leave the underlying conflict unresolved. Alternatively, hostilities may genuinely end and the parties reach agreement.

Khabā itself does not specify how lasting the calm will be.

Abating warfare should therefore be distinguished from completely removing its cause.

The first belongs to khubuww’s semantic field; the second requires other information and evidence.

Anger and vehemence subsiding

Arabic also records خَبَتِ الْحِدَّةُ for vehemence or sharpness abating.

خَبَا لَهَبُهُ can likewise describe someone whose anger has subsided.

Lahab, “flame,” supplies the fire image, while the intended subject is intense human feeling or behavior.

Someone may begin a disagreement speaking in great anger. Later, the emotion diminishes and the person can converse more calmly.

Subsiding anger is understandable in such a situation.

It does not establish that the person has necessarily recognized the truth, corrected a mistake, or forgiven the other party.

An emotion quieting is not identical to moral reform or resolving a dispute.

In human usage, too, this root concerns diminishing intensity; its moral value and outcome follow from other circumstances.

Mukhbī contrasted with a kindler of fire

Al-Kumayt ibn Zayd, an Umayyad-period poet, praises people’s nobility:

وَمِنَّا ضِرَارٌ وَابْنَمَاهُ وَحَاجِبٌ
مُؤَجِّجُ نِيرَانِ الْمَكَارِمِ لَا الْمُخْبِي

wa-minnā ḍirārun wa-ibnamāhu wa-ḥājibun / muʾajjiju nīrāni al-makārimi lā al-mukhbī

Translation:

“Among us are Ḍirār, his two sons, and Ḥājib: kindlers of the fires of noble deeds, not their quenchers.”

مُؤَجِّج and مُخْبِي stand against each other.

Muʾajjij is someone who makes fire blaze; mukhbī is someone who subdues it.

Here fire is a literary image for nobility and generosity.

The poet means that those praised encourage noble conduct rather than suppressing it.

This witnesses a transitive form within خ ب و: someone causes fire or another intensity to subside.

By contrast, ordinary khabā is intransitive, describing the fire itself subsiding.

This distinction matters for the Qur’anic form: خَبَتْ in Al-Isrāʾ 17:97 is intransitive, and the fire or Hell is the subject of subsiding.

3. Qur’anic usage: subsiding fire and increased blazing

وَمَنْ يَهْدِ اللَّهُ فَهُوَ الْمُهْتَدِ وَمَنْ يُضْلِلْ فَلَنْ تَجِدَ لَهُمْ أَوْلِيَاءَ مِنْ دُونِهِ وَنَحْشُرُهُمْ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ عَلَىٰ وُجُوهِهِمْ عُمْيًا وَبُكْمًا وَصُمًّا مَأْوَاهُمْ جَهَنَّمُ كُلَّمَا خَبَتْ زِدْنَاهُمْ سَعِيرًا

wa-man yahdi allāhu fa-huwa al-muhtadi wa-man yuḍlil fa-lan tajida lahum awliyāʾa min dūnihi wa-naḥshuruhum yawma al-qiyāmati ʿalā wujūhihim ʿumyan wa-bukman wa-ṣumman maʾwāhum jahannamu kullamā khabat zidnāhum saʿīran

Proposed translation for this root study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Whomever The God guides is the guided one; and whomever He leads astray, you will never find protectors for them apart from Him. We shall gather them on the Day of Resurrection upon their faces, blind, mute, and deaf. Their abode is Hell: whenever it subsides, We increase the blazing for them.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:97

This is the sole Qur’anic occurrence of خ ب و.

The verse concerns guidance and misguidance, gathering on the Day of Resurrection, and the fate of a group of people.

Its ending identifies Hell as their abode, then describes fire subsiding and intensifying again.

Understanding this expression first requires examining its construction:

“كُلَّمَا خَبَتْ زِدْنَاهُمْ سَعِيرًا”

It connects two events: subsiding and increased blazing.

خَبَتْ: what subsides?

خَبَتْ is a third-person feminine singular perfect verb.

Its subject is the implicit pronoun هِيَ, referring to Hell or its fire in the preceding sentence.

The nearest referent is جَهَنَّمُ in مَأْوَاهُمْ جَهَنَّمُ.

Arabic also treats Jahannam with feminine verbs; khabat agrees with it.

Subsiding is attributed here to Hell and its fire.

The verse does not say that the people undergo khabat: it speaks of the fire subsiding.

This matters because some exegetical explanations describe bodies burning and punishment being renewed. Such explanations may seek to account for the punishment, but they should not replace khabat’s immediate subject without evidence.

The sentence’s construction describes the fire or Hell subsiding, followed by increased blazing for those confined there.

كُلَّمَا: a recurring relationship

كُلَّمَا is a temporal construction expressing a recurring condition and its accompanying consequence.

Naturally understood, whenever subsiding occurs, increased blazing follows.

This differs from a single event.

خَبَتْ alone could describe one instance of subsiding.

كُلَّمَا خَبَتْ, however, expresses a recurring relationship.

Kullamā alone does not specify each stage’s duration, the interval between conditions, or the exact number of repetitions.

The verse provides no such details.

A physical cycle with a definite timetable for otherworldly fire should therefore not be constructed from it.

زِدْنَاهُمْ سَعِيرًا: what is increased?

The verse continues:

“زِدْنَاهُمْ سَعِيرًا”

زِدْنَا comes from ز ي د and concerns adding or increasing.

هُمْ refers to those confined in Hell.

سَعِيرًا comes from س ع ر and concerns blazing fire and intense burning.

The Qur’an thus describes increased blazing for them.

Importantly, the verse contains two independent verbs: khabat and zidnā.

The first expresses subsiding; the second, increasing.

Increased blazing is consequently not khubuww’s own meaning. It is the second event the Qur’an describes after subsiding.

Translating khabat as “blazed” from the outset weakens the evident opposition between diminished and increased fire.

This matters further when examining lexical disagreements.

4. Does خَبَتْ mean complete extinction?

Lexicons give several related explanations of khabā: it quieted, its flame diminished, it died down, and it went out.

These do not describe precisely the same physical condition in every situation.

A fire may, for example, subside until visible flames disappear while hot coals remain. Given suitable fuel and air, it may blaze again.

Alternatively, a fire may go out completely, leaving no active burning.

Arabic can describe both through expressions concerning extinction and khubuww.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:97, however, the continuation matters: زِدْنَاهُمْ سَعِيرًا.

This accompaniment shows that subsiding does not finally end the fire’s condition: increased blazing follows.

Here “subsiding” is therefore more precise than “complete extinction and the end of the fire.”

Conversely, it would be wrong to claim that خ ب و never denotes complete extinction anywhere in Arabic. Lexicons explicitly include طَفِئَتْ in their explanations.

Context should determine the difference between diminished flames and complete extinction, rather than deleting one recorded meaning.

Does subsiding mean reduced punishment?

Khabat itself concerns the flame’s intensity, not directly the amount of pain or suffering experienced by those confined there.

In worldly fire, smaller flames may reduce some effects. This verse concerns Hell, however, and immediately describes increased blazing for its occupants.

Kullamā khabat therefore does not establish a definite period of reduced punishment or respite.

Other Qur’anic verses also concern continuing punishment.

Al-Zukhruf 43:75 says:

لَا يُفَتَّرُ عَنْهُمْ وَهُمْ فِيهِ مُبْلِسُونَ

lā yufattaru ʿanhum wa-hum fīhi mublisūna

That is, the punishment is not eased for them, and they despair within it.

Fāṭir 35:36 likewise describes neither death ending their state nor their punishment being lightened.

These verses caution against drawing a definitive conclusion about reduced punishment from subsiding flames.

Yet خ ب و itself does not mean increased pain or punishment either. That question belongs to the complete sentence and the network of verses.

5. Lexical disagreement: does خَبَتْ also have an opposite meaning?

Alongside the common “subsiding” meaning, some early sources record another position.

In Al-Aḍdād, Ibn al-Anbārī includes khabat among words reported to have opposing meanings.

In this account, khabat al-nāru can describe a fire quieting, or, in another reading, becoming hot and ablaze.

Some exegetical reports also explain kullamā khabat as the fire intensifying.

This disagreement merits attention, but three questions should remain distinct.

First is khabā’s familiar, well-attested Arabic meaning: flames subsiding and growing quiet.

Second is the opposite meaning’s inclusion in a book concerning words reported to bear contrary senses.

Third is a particular interpretation of a verse about Hell’s punishment.

The third alone does not establish that the opposite meaning was equally common throughout Arabic’s history.

Which reading better suits the verse’s construction?

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:97, khabat is followed by:

“زِدْنَاهُمْ سَعِيرًا”

If khabat means subsiding, two conditions stand in opposition: diminished flames followed by increased blazing.

This opposition also accords with the poetic witnesses.

In ʿAdī ibn Zayd’s verse, yakhbū contrasts with yunīru.

In Al-Quṭāmī’s verse, yakhbū contrasts with yahibbu or yashibbu.

“Subsiding” consequently has clearer lexical and contextual support in Al-Isrāʾ 17:97.

The early record of an opposite reading should nevertheless remain visible. Selecting it as the verse’s principal meaning requires stronger evidence.

A defensible proposal is therefore to translate khabat as “subsides” in the base translation and discuss the lexical disagreement in the root explanation.

Ibn al-Anbārī’s Al-Aḍdād, entry 108: the two reported meanings of khabat

Exegetical accounts concerning the occupants’ bodies

Some exegetical reports explain the fire subsiding through the occupants’ bodies burning and punishment then being renewed.

Reports transmitted by Al-Ṭabarī, for example, describe bodies being replaced and the fire blazing again.

This explanation has been connected with verses such as Al-Nisāʾ 4:56, concerning burned skins being replaced.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:97 itself, however, kullamā khabat directly concerns the fire subsiding. Complete bodily consumption and the manner of bodily renewal are not explicitly stated there.

Verses concerning continuing punishment can therefore be examined together without inserting one verse’s details into another’s translation.

Some explanations also propose that kullamā khabat means whenever the occupants wish the fire would subside.

This, too, departs from the direct wording: the expression contains no verb of wanting or wishing.

A base translation should first retain what the sentence states: subsiding each time, followed by increased blazing.

6. Distinctions from related roots

خ ب و (khubuww) and خ ب ت (khabt): subsiding and humility

These roots look very similar in writing but are morphologically and semantically independent.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:97, خَبَتْ comes from خَبَا يَخْبُو and the root خ ب و.

By contrast, أَخْبَتُوا in Hūd 11:23, الْمُخْبِتِينَ in Al-Ḥajj 22:34, and فَتُخْبِتَ in Al-Ḥajj 22:54 come from خ ب ت.

خ ب ت concerns low-lying ground and humility before The God.

خ ب و, however, concerns fire subsiding and diminishing flames.

The final tāʾ of خَبَتْ in Al-Isrāʾ 17:97 is not a root letter: it marks feminine agreement in the perfect verb.

The verb’s basic form is خَبَا.

In خ ب ت, by contrast, tāʾ is the third radical.

The visual resemblance between خَبَتْ and forms of خبت should therefore not turn subsiding fire into humility or ikhbāt.

Nor does a flame quieting imply moral or faithful humility in the fire. That would unjustifiably transfer one root’s concept into another.

Study of KH B T

خ ب و (khubuww) and خ ب أ (khabʾ): subsiding and concealing

Arabic خ ب أ concerns hiding and keeping concealed.

Khabʾ also names something hidden.

خ ب و, by contrast, concerns fire subsiding and quieting.

Some Arabic forms nevertheless exhibit morphological proximity and overlap.

For example, الْخَابِيَة, a large storage jar, has been assigned to the family of concealing and keeping something; lexicographers report the loss of its hamzah.

نَارٌ خَابِيَةٌ, however, describes a fire that has subsided.

The forms’ visual resemblance does not make their roots or concepts identical.

One concerns a vessel and storage; the other, diminished fire.

Some lexical analyses also connect خِبَاء, a tent or covering, with hiding or covering. It should not, without evidence, become the certain origin of the sense of fire subsiding.

The خ ب و study should therefore remain distinct from خ ب أ.

Study of KH B ʾ

خ ب و (khubuww) and خ م د (khumūd): subsiding and going out

Arabic khumūd concerns extinction and a fire’s intensity settling.

Lexicons frequently explain khabat al-nāru through khamadat.

This proximity is real but does not make the roots identical in every use.

Khubūw can emphasize the process of flames diminishing and subsiding.

Khumūd can likewise concern fire quieting or going out, sometimes highlighting the cessation of an active, intense condition.

No absolute rule makes khubuww always temporary weakening and khumūd always complete extinction.

Lexicons can use both when explaining a fire going out.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:97, renewed saʿīr in the context supports rendering khabat as “subsiding.”

خ ب و (khubuww) and ط ف أ (iṭfāʾ): subsiding and extinguishing

Arabic طَفِئَتِ النَّارُ describes a fire going out; أَطْفَأَ النَّارَ describes extinguishing it.

Familiar khabā, however, chiefly describes the fire’s subsiding state.

A grammatical distinction also matters.

خَبَتِ النَّارُ describes the fire itself subsiding.

أَطْفَأَ فُلَانٌ النَّارَ describes someone extinguishing it.

Both families nevertheless have intransitive and transitive forms; their distinction should not be limited to these two examples.

The Qur’an uses ط ف أ for attempts to extinguish The God’s light, including Al-Tawbah 9:32 and Al-Ṣaff 61:8.

These verses concern efforts to extinguish light.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:97, however, khabat is attributed to Hell or its fire itself.

The subject, object, and context must therefore remain distinct.

خ ب و (khubuww) and س ع ر (saʿīr): subsiding and blazing

Al-Isrāʾ 17:97 itself presents this comparison.

Khubūw concerns diminishing flames.

Saʿīr belongs to the field of blazing fire and intense burning.

In kullamā khabat zidnāhum saʿīran, the meanings form a clear opposition.

Khubūw itself does not, however, mean reduced punishment, nor does saʿīr mean a specified period of punishment.

They concern the state and intensity of fire.

Understanding the otherworldly outcome and the punishment’s nature requires the verses together and their contexts, rather than this lexical comparison alone.

خ ب و (khubuww) and و ق د (waqd): subsiding and kindling fire

و ق د concerns kindling, fuel, and making fire blaze.

Al-Humazah 104:6 contains نَارُ اللَّهِ الْمُوقَدَةُ.

Mūqadah describes a fire that has been kindled.

Khubūw, however, concerns fire subsiding.

The two can describe different conditions of the same fire.

Their opposition should not yield a particular physical process for otherworldly fire.

The verses describe kindling, intensity, and punishment’s outcome; the words themselves do not specify fuel, timing, or mechanism.

7. Translation proposal for Forqan

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:97, the most suitable Persian equivalent for خَبَتْ is “subsided.”

This rendering has several advantages.

First, it retains the recognized Arabic meaning.

Second, it suits the opposition with saʿīr later in the verse.

Third, it does not impose a definitive, permanent end to the fire.

Fourth, it makes the difference between diminished flames and renewed intensity clear.

“Died down” is also defensible, although it emphasizes movement toward extinction slightly more than “subsided.”

“Grew quiet” is lexically close, but in Persian it may not sufficiently clarify the reduction in flames.

“Went out” also has Arabic lexical support, but readers may understand it here as the complete end of burning.

“Subsided” is therefore preferable for the base translation.

The whole expression may be rendered:

“Whenever it subsides, We increase the blazing for them.”

This preserves both principal verbs and their relationship.

Khabat should not directly become “their punishment decreased”: its subject is fire or Hell, not the amount of punishment.

Nor should زِدْنَاهُمْ سَعِيرًا be confined to “We light the fire again”: the sentence describes increased saʿīr for those confined there.

Brief root meaning and meaning in the verse

خَبَتْ — Al-Isrāʾ 17:97

Root: خ ب و

Grammar: Active third-person feminine singular perfect, Form I. Its subject is the implicit هِيَ, referring to Hell or its fire; final tāʾ marks feminine agreement.

Brief root meaning: Subsiding, diminishing flames, and a fire growing quiet.

Meaning in this verse: Subsides, referring to Hell or its fire. The subsequent increase in blazing is expressed independently by زِدْنَاهُمْ and is not part of خَبَتْ itself.

This keeps the word’s meaning separate from its consequence and the verse’s second verb.

The final wāw in خ ب و is a radical even though it becomes alif in perfect خَبَا and is not visible in خَبَتْ.

This morphological point distinguishes it from خ ب ت.

8. Conclusion

Early Arabic خ ب و concerns fire subsiding and growing quiet. Extended uses also express the abatement of war, anger, and vehemence.

The poetic witnesses of ʿAdī ibn Zayd and Al-Quṭāmī show that subsiding can be temporary, followed by renewed light or fire.

They do not make every khubuww temporary. Complete extinction is also recorded in lexicons.

The Qur’an uses this root only in Al-Isrāʾ 17:97: whenever Hell or its fire subsides, increased saʿīr follows for those confined there.

Some early sources also report the opposite meaning, blazing, for khabat. Clearer Arabic evidence and the opposition with zidnāhum saʿīran nevertheless support “subsiding.”

Khabat here comes from خ ب و, not خ ب ت. Subsiding fire should therefore not be equated with humility and ikhbāt before The God.

Final definition: خ ب و signifies subsiding and diminishing intensity in something previously ablaze or active, especially fire and its flame. Arabic extends this meaning to war and anger abating. خَبَتْ in Al-Isrāʾ 17:97 concerns Hell’s fire subsiding; renewed blazing for those confined there is expressed by the independent زِدْنَاهُمْ. Brief root meaning: Subsiding, diminishing flames, and a fire growing quiet; in other uses, war and anger abating.
