The Arabic root ح ز ب concerns gathering and forming a collective. حِزْب names people joined by a common purpose, idea, position, or relationship; its plural is أَحْزَاب. تَحَزَّبَ الْقَوْمُ means that people formed groups or gathered in them, while حَازَبَ فُلَانًا concerns supporting someone’s position.

حِزْب can also name a defined portion of a whole. It is used for an assigned portion of Qur’anic recitation, while taḥzīb can mean dividing the text into portions. This differs from forming a human group, although both distinguish part of a larger whole.

Other early branches include حَزَبَهُ الْأَمْرُ for a matter becoming difficult and pressing upon someone, حَزَابِي for a stocky person or animal, and حِزْبَاء for hard, uneven terrain.

Every Qur’anic occurrence is the noun حِزْب in a singular, dual, or plural form. Its settings include religious divisions, wartime coalitions, earlier peoples, the two groups in the account of the cave, Satan’s followers, and people loyal to God and His messenger.

This range shows that حِزْب is not inherently a good or bad designation in the Qur’an.

The text speaks of both حِزْبُ اللَّهِ and حِزْبُ الشَّيْطَانِ. Elsewhere, two groups differ about the duration of an event, without the word itself implying war or hostility.

The semantic core concerns grouping and collective affiliation. The affiliation’s value depends on the group’s purpose, conduct, and orientation.

1. Forms and derivatives

The root consists of ḥāʾ, zāy, and bāʾ. It is a sound triliteral Arabic root.

Its principal forms include:

  • حِزْب: A group sharing an affiliation, purpose, or position;also a specified portion of a whole.
  • أَحْزَاب: Plural of حِزْب:groups or factions.
  • حَزَبَ ـ يَحْزُبُ: Of a matter, befell someone and became difficult or pressing.
  • حَزْب: Difficulty or pressure arising from a matter.
  • حَازِب: A difficult matter or pressing event.
  • حَزِيب: Severe or difficult, especially of a matter or situation.
  • حَوَازِب: Plural of حَازِب, concerning pressing events and difficulties.
  • حَزَّبَ ـ يُحَزِّبُ: Gathered people, formed them into groups, or divided a whole into portions.
  • تَحْزِيب: Grouping or dividing into parties;for a text, dividing into defined portions.
  • تَحَزَّبَ: Gathered as a group or joined one.
  • تَحَزُّب: Grouping or group affiliation.
  • حَازَبَ ـ يُحَازِبُ: Aligned with or supported someone.
  • مُحَازَبَة: Alignment and group support.
  • تَحَازَبَ: Allied or gathered to support one side.
  • حِزْبِيّ: Affiliated with a group or party.
  • حِزْبِيَّة: Group affiliation;in modern social and political usage, partisanship.
  • حَزَابِي / حَزَابِيَة: An early description of a stocky, relatively short person or animal;the yāʾ is undoubled.
  • حِزْبَاء / حَزْبَاء: Hard, uneven, raised ground.
  • حَيْزَبُون: An old description of an elderly woman, sometimes derogatory.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an.

The Qur’an contains only the noun حِزْب in its different forms:

حِزْبِيَّة as political or social partisanship belongs to later linguistic developments, rather than the Qur’an’s own vocabulary.

2. The semantic core and early Arabic usage

Gathering and forming a collective

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies one semantic principle:

أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ، وَهُوَ تَجَمُّعُ الشَّيْءِ

aṣlun wāḥidun wa-huwa tajammuʿu al-shayʾi

“One principle: the gathering of something.”

He then relates حِزْب to a body of people.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn explains تَحَزَّبَ الْقَوْمُ as people gathering, and حَزَّبْتُ أَحْزَابًا as bringing them together in groups.

This illuminates the root’s central image.

People may initially be scattered, then form a collective through a shared purpose or position.

Such a collective can be called a ḥizb.

The word alone does not specify numbers, scale, or organizational form.

A group may be small or large.

It may have a regular structure or simply comprise people supporting one another in a particular matter.

Its members need not all be in one place.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Kitāb al-ʿAyn: gathering and forming groups

Alignment around a shared position

Lisān al-ʿArab explains حِزْبُ الرَّجُلِ as someone’s companions and supporters sharing his opinion or undertaking.

A ḥizb therefore need not be merely a physical gathering in one place.

People separated geographically may still share beliefs, purposes, or conduct.

Their common position establishes the group relationship.

This matters for “God’s group” and “Satan’s group”: the Qur’an does not provide membership lists of registered organizations or addresses of administrative offices.

The affiliation is identified through people’s orientation and behaviour.

Agreement on one matter does not mean that every member shares all beliefs and actions.

The specific subject of alignment helps determine the group’s scope in each passage.

A poetic witness attributed to Ruʾbah ibn al-ʿAjjāj

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs attribute this verse to Ruʾbah ibn al-ʿAjjāj; the inspected Kitāb al-ʿAyn transmission attributes a variant to Al-ʿAjjāj:

لَقَدْ وَجَدْتُ مُصْعَبًا مُسْتَصْعَبًا
حِينَ رَمَى الْأَحْزَابَ وَالْمُحَزِّبَا

la-qad wajadtu muṣʿaban mustaṣʿaban / ḥīna ramā al-aḥzāba wa-al-muḥazzibā

“I found Muṣʿab difficult to overcome, when he confronted the groups and their organizer.”

الْأَحْزَاب names groups, while الْمُحَزِّب concerns the person assembling them.

Two forms occur in this witness: the groups’ name and the designation of their organizer.

It illustrates classical Arabic grouping in a setting of social or political confrontation.

Ruʾbah was an Islamic-era poet,particularly associated with the Umayyad period. This is not a pre-Islamic witness.

The sources vary between لَقَدْ وَجَدْتُ and لَقَدْ وَجَدْنَا, and between حِينَ and حَتَّى.

The attribution differences and wording variants should be retained in research.

The witness helps explain أَحْزَاب, but does not establish when the word began to be used before the Qur’an.

Lisān al-ʿArab: the Ruʾbah witness and differences from Kitāb al-ʿAyn

حَازَبَ: aligning with and supporting someone

In Asās al-Balāghah, Al-Zamakhsharī writes:

فُلَانٌ يُحَازِبُ فُلَانًا

fulānun yuḥāzibu fulānan

It means that someone supports another and stands alongside him.

The main issue is alignment in a position or action, rather than gathering alone.

Someone may defend another in a dispute or join his group to strengthen his position.

Group affiliation can thus become practical support.

مُحَازَبَة does not itself mean fighting.

Support may be political, social, or personal without armed conflict.

A ḥizb should not be lexically restricted to a fighting force.

حِزْب as a share or portion

Lisān al-ʿArab records حِزْب for a defined share as well as a body of people.

For example:

أَعْطِنِي حِزْبِي مِنَ الْمَالِ

aʿṭinī ḥizbī mina al-māli

“Give me my share of the property.”

It also concerns a turn at drawing water or an assigned portion of recitation.

This differs from a human group.

One identifies a portion of property or text; the other distinguishes a group within a larger population.

Both can be understood in terms of a portion and a collective.

Historical priority of either sense should not be asserted without evidence.

A Qur’anic ḥizb: dividing the text

Later religious terminology uses ḥizb for a particular portion of the Qur’an.

تَحْزِيبُ الْقُرْآنِ concerns dividing the text for recitation and following a reading schedule.

In the familiar contemporary system, the Qur’an is divided into thirty juzʾ and sixty ḥizb.

That division is not what حِزْب means in the Qur’anic verses.

None of its twenty Qur’anic occurrences names one of the muṣḥaf’s sixty portions.

Instructional and recitational divisions should be distinguished from the word’s own Qur’anic usage.

The term “a ḥizb of the Qur’an” does not establish a Qur’anic command to divide the text into sixty portions.

حَزَبَهُ الْأَمْرُ: pressure and difficulty

Kitāb al-ʿAyn and Lisān al-ʿArab use this expression for a difficult matter befalling and pressing upon someone.

أَمْرٌ حَازِبٌ describes a difficult matter or situation.

This branch concerns pressure on a person, rather than a human group.

Ibn Fāris treats the family under the principle of gathering.

One can imagine difficulties gathering around someone and exerting pressure.

That semantic explanation does not prove the historical origin of the sense.

It should be recognized as a separately attested usage.

حَزَبَهُ الْأَمْرُ, حَازِب, and حَزِيب do not occur in the Qur’an.

Stockiness and compactness in حَزَابِي

Lexicons use حَزَابِي and حَزَابِيَة, with undoubled yāʾ, for relatively short, heavily built people or animals.

Lisān al-ʿArab quotes this expression from Umayyah ibn Abī ʿĀʾidh al-Hudhalī:

أَوْ أَصْحَمٍ حَامٍ جَرَامِيزَهُ
حَزَابِيَةٍ حَيْدَى بِالدِّحَالِ

aw aṣḥamin ḥāmin jarāmīzahu / ḥazābiyatin ḥaydā bi-al-diḥāli

The poet depicts an animal’s build and movement. حَزَابِيَة concerns its stocky, compact body.

This has an imagistic connection with gathering and compactness.

The verse is not about a political or religious group.

Some biographical sources, following Al-Marzubānī, call Umayyah a mukhaḍram who experienced pre-Islamic times and lived into the Umayyad period. The date of this particular verse is uncertain.

It should not be labelled definitely pre-Qur’anic without evidence.

The witness shows non-group senses within the lexical family.

Khizānat al-Adab: the biographical report about Umayyah following Al-Marzubānī

حِزْبَاء: hard, uneven ground

Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Lisān al-ʿArab record حِزْبَاء for hard, rough, raised terrain.

حَزَابِي also describes certain hard or rough lands.

Compactness or density may be relevant to the image.

This does not make حِزْب mean rocky or hard ground in the Qur’an.

Its Qur’anic occurrences concern groups of people.

The terrain and bodily senses help explain the lexical family, but do not belong in translating these verses.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: undoubled yāʾ in ḥazābī and other branches

3. Groups and differences in Qur’anic usage

Al-Kahf 18:12: two groups, not necessarily religious sects

Verse text:

ثُمَّ بَعَثْنَاهُمْ لِنَعْلَمَ أَيُّ الْحِزْبَيْنِ أَحْصَىٰ لِمَا لَبِثُوا أَمَدًا

thumma baʿathnāhum li-naʿlama ayyu al-ḥizbayni aḥṣā li-mā labithū amadan

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Then We raised them so that We might know which of the two groups had best counted the duration of their stay.”

الْحِزْبَيْنِ is the dual of حِزْب.

It means two groups.

The distinction concerns calculating how long the cave’s sleepers remained.

The verse does not explicitly identify exactly who the two groups were.

Early commentaries differ on whether the two groups were among the cave’s sleepers themselves or included other people associated with them.

Lexically, the word need not designate religious sects, political parties, or armies here.

Two groups may differ on a particular question, such as calculating a period.

The noun itself does not establish mutual hostility.

This occurrence is an important witness to the word’s Qur’anic range.

Al-Ṭabarī: the two groups and differing identifications

Al-Muʾminūn 23:53: dividing a shared matter into groups

Verse text:

فَتَقَطَّعُوا أَمْرَهُمْ بَيْنَهُمْ زُبُرًا كُلُّ حِزْبٍ بِمَا لَدَيْهِمْ فَرِحُونَ

fa-taqaṭṭaʿū amrahum baynahum zuburan kullu ḥizbin bi-mā ladayhim fariḥūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“They divided their affair among themselves into separate portions; each group rejoices in what it has.”

حِزْبٍ means a group or faction here.

The preceding clause establishes the grouping’s orientation:

فَتَقَطَّعُوا أَمْرَهُمْ بَيْنَهُمْ زُبُرًا

fa-taqaṭṭaʿū amrahum baynahum zuburan

It concerns dividing a shared affair.

The groups are introduced in connection with separation and disagreement over something whose unity should have been preserved.

The criticism is not merely that several human groups exist; it concerns fragmenting the shared affair and each group’s satisfaction with what it possesses.

كُلُّ حِزْبٍ بِمَا لَدَيْهِمْ فَرِحُونَ

كُلُّ حِزْبٍ is singular in form.

لَدَيْهِمْ and فَرِحُونَ nevertheless use plural reference and agreement.

This is normal Arabic: the noun is grammatically singular but denotes multiple people.

The sentence concerns the members of each group.

Another issue is فَرِحُونَ.

It comes from ف ر ح, concerning joy or gladness.

Here each group is pleased and satisfied with what it has.

حِزْب itself does not mean self-satisfaction or joy.

That feature comes from فَرِحُونَ.

A brief root definition should therefore not say “a group that always thinks itself right.”

Collective complacency may be relevant to the context, but is not an invariant lexical meaning of حِزْب.

Al-Rūm 30:32: division in religion

Verse text:

مِنَ الَّذِينَ فَرَّقُوا دِينَهُمْ وَكَانُوا شِيَعًا كُلُّ حِزْبٍ بِمَا لَدَيْهِمْ فَرِحُونَ

mina alladhīna farraqū dīnahum wa-kānū shiyaʿan kullu ḥizbin bi-mā ladayhim fariḥūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Of those who divided their religion and became factions; each group rejoices in what it has.”

The verse repeats the ending of Al-Muʾminūn 23:53.

Its beginning clearly identifies the subject of division:

فَرَّقُوا دِينَهُمْ وَكَانُوا شِيَعًا

farraqū dīnahum wa-kānū shiyaʿan

These people divided their religion and became separate factions.

The criticism concerns religious division.

حِزْب does not itself mean fragmenting religion.

That meaning comes from فَرَّقُوا and شِيَعًا.

This distinction is necessary in root research.

Does the Qur’an condemn every group and community?

That does not follow from these two verses.

If حِزْب inherently meant a wrongful group, “God’s group” in Al-Māʾidah 5:56 and Al-Mujādilah 58:22 would be inexplicable.

Al-Kahf 18:12 also introduces two groups without directly condemning their being groups.

Being a group should be distinguished from blameworthy sectarianism.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:53 and Al-Rūm 30:32 concern religious division and each group’s satisfaction with its own possessions.

Cooperation in good work, education, social service, or defending rights is not inherently the same division.

The group’s purpose, conduct, and relationship to truth and justice determine its value.

4. أَحْزَاب: groups, coalitions, and earlier peoples

Al-Aḥzāb 33:20: the attacking coalition

Verse text:

يَحْسَبُونَ الْأَحْزَابَ لَمْ يَذْهَبُوا وَإِنْ يَأْتِ الْأَحْزَابُ يَوَدُّوا لَوْ أَنَّهُمْ بَادُونَ فِي الْأَعْرَابِ يَسْأَلُونَ عَنْ أَنْبَائِكُمْ وَلَوْ كَانُوا فِيكُمْ مَا قَاتَلُوا إِلَّا قَلِيلًا

yaḥsabūna al-aḥzāba lam yadhhabū wa-in yaʾti al-aḥzābu yawaddū law annahum bādūna fī al-aʿrābi yasʾalūna ʿan anbāʾikum wa-law kānū fīkum mā qātalū illā qalīlan

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“They suppose the confederate groups have not gone. If the confederates should return, they would wish to be out among the desert Arabs asking about your news. If they were among you, they would fight only a little.”

The noun أَحْزَاب occurs twice here: first as accusative الْأَحْزَابَ, then as nominative الْأَحْزَابُ.

The surah’s setting concerns armed confrontation with opposing forces.

The word therefore identifies groups allied against the believers.

“Confederate groups” or “the attacking coalition” suits this context.

The sense of an attacking coalition comes from the whole setting.

The Arabic noun itself is not restricted to an army.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:22: the believers’ reaction to the coalition

Verse text:

وَلَمَّا رَأَى الْمُؤْمِنُونَ الْأَحْزَابَ قَالُوا هَٰذَا مَا وَعَدَنَا اللَّهُ وَرَسُولُهُ وَصَدَقَ اللَّهُ وَرَسُولُهُ وَمَا زَادَهُمْ إِلَّا إِيمَانًا وَتَسْلِيمًا

wa-lammā raʾā al-muʾminūna al-aḥzāba qālū hādhā mā waʿadanā Allāhu wa-rasūluhu wa-ṣadaqa Allāhu wa-rasūluhu wa-mā zādahum illā īmānan wa-taslīman

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“When the believers saw the confederates, they said: This is what The God and His messenger promised us; The God and His messenger spoke truly. It increased them only in faith and submission.”

The word concerns the same allied forces in the armed confrontation.

The believers’ response differs from that described in Al-Aḥzāb 33:20.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:20 emphasizes fear and the wish to avoid danger.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:22 presents believers’ steadfastness and trust.

The contrast matters to the context.

أَحْزَاب nevertheless means the same thing in both: the allied groups present in the confrontation.

Different reactions should not lead to changing the noun’s meaning.

Does أَحْزَاب always refer to the Battle of the Trench?

No.

Islamic historical tradition also calls the Battle of the Trench “the Day of the Confederates.”

This name relates to the verses of Al-Aḥzāb.

Other Qur’anic occurrences do not concern that battle.

Ghāfir 40:30 uses يَوْمِ الْأَحْزَابِ in the words of a believing man among Pharaoh’s people.

الْأَحْزَاب should therefore not always be treated as the proper name of that battle.

Ghāfir 40:30: earlier groups

Verse text:

وَقَالَ الَّذِي آمَنَ يَا قَوْمِ إِنِّي أَخَافُ عَلَيْكُمْ مِثْلَ يَوْمِ الْأَحْزَابِ

wa-qāla alladhī āmana yā qawmi innī akhāfu ʿalaykum mithla yawmi al-aḥzābi

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“The man who believed said: My people, I fear for you a fate like the day of the earlier groups.”

الْأَحْزَاب concerns earlier peoples here.

Ghāfir 40:31 names Noah’s people, ʿĀd, Thamūd, and those after them.

The context itself distinguishes them from the coalition in Al-Aḥzāb.

They are historical groups who opposed messengers.

They need not all have lived simultaneously or assembled on one battlefield.

Peoples from different periods may resemble one another in conduct or position and be described under this common designation.

Shared orientation can therefore suffice; simultaneous presence and a joint organization are not universal conditions.

Ṣād 38:11, Ṣād 38:12, and Ṣād 38:13: groups and historical outcomes

Ṣād 38:11 speaks of a defeated force among the groups.

Ṣād 38:12 names Noah’s people, ʿĀd, and Pharaoh.

Ṣād 38:13 names Thamūd, Lot’s people, and the people of the thicket, then says:

أُولَٰئِكَ الْأَحْزَابُ

ulāʾika al-aḥzābu

Here the word identifies different historical groups.

It is not simply the name of a simultaneous coalition.

Nor does it establish that all these peoples formed a common organization across historical periods.

Their similar opposition to messengers brings them under a shared designation.

Ghāfir 40:5: the groups after Noah

Ghāfir 40:5 concerns denial by Noah’s people and the groups after them.

It continues with the communities’ attempts to seize their messengers.

الْأَحْزَاب therefore concerns earlier communities confronting their messengers.

The sense of a group or collective remains.

The accompanying verbs and context establish their opposition, rather than the noun alone.

5. Groups in religious and intellectual disagreements

Maryam 19:37 and Al-Zukhruf 43:65

Both verses contain فَاخْتَلَفَ الْأَحْزَابُ مِنْ بَيْنِهِمْ.

Both contexts concern Jesus.

The groups differ about the issue under discussion.

أَحْزَاب does not itself mean disagreement.

The verb اخْتَلَفَ expresses that.

The noun names the groups among whom disagreement arose.

Translation should retain this distinction.

Translating أَحْزَاب as “disagreements” removes the sentence’s subject.

These verses also do not establish that all followers of Jesus at every time share one position or outcome.

They concern particular groups and disagreements; assessments of beliefs and conduct require relevant evidence.

Hūd 11:17 and Al-Raʿd 13:36

Hūd 11:17 discusses those among the groups who disbelieve in the message under discussion.

Al-Raʿd 13:36 says that some among the groups reject part of what was sent down.

These uses identify groups with intellectual or religious positions.

The noun alone does not specify their doctrines.

The accompanying verbs express disbelief or rejection.

“Groups” is therefore a suitable basic equivalent; their affiliation can be explained in reflection on the verse.

6. حِزْبُ اللَّهِ and حِزْبُ الشَّيْطَانِ

Al-Māʾidah 5:56: God’s group and allegiance

Verse text:

وَمَنْ يَتَوَلَّ اللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُ وَالَّذِينَ آمَنُوا فَإِنَّ حِزْبَ اللَّهِ هُمُ الْغَالِبُونَ

wa-man yatawalla Allāha wa-rasūlahu wa-alladhīna āmanū fa-inna ḥizba Allāhi humu al-ghālibūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Whoever takes The God, His messenger, and those who believe as protectors and allies:indeed, God’s group are the ones who prevail.”

The verse contains حِزْبَ اللَّهِ.

It does not begin by naming a group.

It first speaks of يَتَوَلَّ اللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُ وَالَّذِينَ آمَنُوا.

Allegiance and alignment thus provide the setting for identifying God’s group.

يَتَوَلَّ comes from و ل ي and should be studied alongside that family’s other uses.

The full sense of wilāyah, protection, or alignment cannot be derived from حِزْب alone.

What is clear is the connection between affiliation with God, His messenger, and believers and the expression “God’s group.”

Is “God’s group” the name of a particular political organization?

The verse does not make that identification.

It provides no organizational address, administrative structure, named party leader, membership procedure, or political boundaries.

حِزْبُ اللَّهِ expresses a group affiliated with God.

Organizations in modern usage may choose this name.

The choice alone does not establish an exclusive or definitive identification with the Qur’anic expression.

Such a claim must be evaluated against Qur’anic criteria.

The expression does not confer ruling authority or immunity from criticism on every group adopting it.

Al-Mujādilah 58:22: characteristics of God’s group

Verse text:

لَا تَجِدُ قَوْمًا يُؤْمِنُونَ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ يُوَادُّونَ مَنْ حَادَّ اللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُ وَلَوْ كَانُوا آبَاءَهُمْ أَوْ أَبْنَاءَهُمْ أَوْ إِخْوَانَهُمْ أَوْ عَشِيرَتَهُمْ أُولَٰئِكَ كَتَبَ فِي قُلُوبِهِمُ الْإِيمَانَ وَأَيَّدَهُمْ بِرُوحٍ مِنْهُ وَيُدْخِلُهُمْ جَنَّاتٍ تَجْرِي مِنْ تَحْتِهَا الْأَنْهَارُ خَالِدِينَ فِيهَا رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهُمْ وَرَضُوا عَنْهُ أُولَٰئِكَ حِزْبُ اللَّهِ أَلَا إِنَّ حِزْبَ اللَّهِ هُمُ الْمُفْلِحُونَ

lā tajidu qawman yuʾminūna bi-Allāhi wa-al-yawmi al-ākhiri yuwāddūna man ḥādda Allāha wa-rasūlahu wa-law kānū ābāʾahum aw abnāʾahum aw ikhwānahum aw ʿashīratahum ulāʾika kataba fī qulūbihimu al-īmāna wa-ayyadahum bi-rūḥin minhu wa-yudkhiluhum jannātin tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhāru khālidīna fīhā raḍiya Allāhu ʿanhum wa-raḍū ʿanhu ulāʾika ḥizbu Allāhi alā inna ḥizba Allāhi humu al-mufliḥūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“You will not find people who believe in The God and the Last Day maintaining affection and allegiance with those who oppose The God and His messenger, even if they are their fathers, sons, brothers, or kin. He has inscribed faith in their hearts and strengthened them with a spirit from Him. He admits them to gardens beneath which rivers flow, remaining there. The God is pleased with them, and they with Him. They are God’s group; surely God’s group are the successful.”

The noun حِزْب occurs twice here: nominative حِزْبُ اللَّهِ first, then accusative حِزْبَ اللَّهِ.

The designation follows several characteristics.

First, faith in God and the Last Day.

Second, withholding allegiance from those opposing God and His messenger, even when they are close relations.

Third, established faith and divine support.

Fourth, mutual pleasure between God and these people.

The group is identified through characteristics and allegiance, rather than a name alone.

حَادَّ اللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُ and the boundary of allegiance

حَادَّ comes from ح د د.

It concerns opposition and confrontation.

The passage concerns those taking an opposing stand against God and His messenger.

حَادَّ does not name every person who differs religiously from a believer.

Its scope requires study of the context and the root’s other uses.

يُوَادُّونَ concerns affection and allegiance with those taking that opposing stand.

This should not become, without evidence, a denial of human rights, justice, or proper treatment of relatives.

Luqmān 31:15 instructs proper worldly companionship even with parents pressing their child towards associating partners with God.

Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:8 does not prohibit kindness and justice towards those who neither fought believers over religion nor expelled them from their homes.

Allegiance to opposition against truth should be distinguished from respecting human rights.

Study of Ḥ D D

“God’s group” does not provide permission for injustice against others.

Prevailing and success

Al-Māʾidah 5:56 describes God’s group as الْغَالِبُونَ.

Al-Mujādilah 58:22 uses الْمُفْلِحُونَ.

These belong to different roots.

غَلَبَ concerns prevailing or overcoming.

فَلَحَ concerns success or flourishing.

They should not both be reduced without examination to military victory in every battle.

حِزْب alone does not guarantee that every group claiming divine affiliation will always win a political contest or war.

The verses concern people with specified characteristics.

The designation and promised outcome should be understood in relation to those characteristics.

Fāṭir 35:6: Satan’s group and his invitation

Verse text:

إِنَّ الشَّيْطَانَ لَكُمْ عَدُوٌّ فَاتَّخِذُوهُ عَدُوًّا إِنَّمَا يَدْعُو حِزْبَهُ لِيَكُونُوا مِنْ أَصْحَابِ السَّعِيرِ

inna al-shayṭāna lakum ʿaduwwun fa-ittakhidhūhu ʿaduwwan innamā yadʿū ḥizbahu li-yakūnū min aṣḥābi al-saʿīri

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Satan is your enemy, so take him as an enemy. He only calls his group so that they may become companions of the blazing fire.”

حِزْبَهُ combines the noun and the attached pronoun هُ.

The pronoun refers to Satan.

It therefore means Satan’s group or followers.

The verb يَدْعُو matters here.

Satan calls his group.

The construction expresses a relationship of invitation and following.

It does not name a formally registered human organization with a particular outward emblem.

Al-Mujādilah 58:19: Satan’s group and forgetting God’s remembrance

Verse text:

اسْتَحْوَذَ عَلَيْهِمُ الشَّيْطَانُ فَأَنْسَاهُمْ ذِكْرَ اللَّهِ أُولَٰئِكَ حِزْبُ الشَّيْطَانِ أَلَا إِنَّ حِزْبَ الشَّيْطَانِ هُمُ الْخَاسِرُونَ

istaḥwadha ʿalayhimu al-shayṭānu fa-ansāhum dhikra Allāhi ulāʾika ḥizbu al-shayṭāni alā inna ḥizba al-shayṭāni humu al-khāsirūna

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Satan has gained mastery over them and caused them to forget the remembrance of The God. They are Satan’s group; surely Satan’s group are the losers.”

The expression naming Satan’s group occurs twice: حِزْبُ الشَّيْطَانِ followed by حِزْبَ الشَّيْطَانِ.

Their condition is described before that designation:

اسْتَحْوَذَ عَلَيْهِمُ الشَّيْطَانُ فَأَنْسَاهُمْ ذِكْرَ اللَّهِ

istaḥwadha ʿalayhimu al-shayṭānu fa-ansāhum dhikra Allāhi

In this context, the group is connected with Satan’s mastery and forgetting God’s remembrance.

The preceding verses mention false oaths and using oaths to obstruct God’s way.

A particular name or social identity is not the only issue.

Conduct and orientation provide the basis of the designation.

Does “Satan’s group” name every religious opponent?

The verse does not establish such a rule.

People may disagree over how to understand a verse.

Disagreement alone does not prove that one side is God’s group and the other Satan’s.

Al-Mujādilah 58:19 describes Satan’s mastery and forgetting God’s remembrance.

Al-Mujādilah 58:22 also specifies characteristics of God’s group.

The root alone does not justify calling particular people Satan’s group merely because they oppose a scholar, school, political party, or human leader.

Such an assessment needs clear evidence concerning their conduct and position.

7. Comparison with related roots

Ḥ Z B and J M ʿ: groups and gathering

جَمَعَ concerns gathering people or things.

حِزْب also names a collective of people.

Not every gathering is necessarily a ḥizb.

People may gather in a market or public place without sharing a position or purpose.

The familiar use of حِزْب gives greater prominence to affiliation and shared alignment.

This is not an absolute boundary: language is broad and context-dependent.

Study of J M ʿ

Ḥ Z B and SH Y ʿ: groups and factions

شِيَع comes from ش ي ع and occurs alongside حِزْب in Al-Rūm 30:32.

The families overlap in the sense of groups or factions.

شِيَع has its own Qur’anic field involving following, alignment, and factional division.

In Al-Rūm 30:32, شِيَعًا describes their divided condition, while كُلُّ حِزْبٍ identifies each resulting group.

The words are close, but perform different functions.

Ḥ Z B and F R Q: grouping and separation

فَرَّقَ concerns separating or dispersing.

A ḥizb may form after separating from a larger whole.

A group may also form without creating harmful division.

فَرَّقُوا in Al-Rūm 30:32 expresses division of religion.

Blameworthy division should not be imported into every use of حِزْب.

Study of F R Q

Ḥ Z B and F ʾ Y: groups and فِئَة

فِئَة names a group in the Qur’an. The Quranic Arabic Corpus classifies it under ف أ ي; ة is not the root’s third radical.

Some occurrences concern military confrontation.

حِزْب can likewise identify allied groups or fighting forces in that context.

فِئَة and حِزْب belong to different lexical families; the written ة in فِئَة should not be presented as a root letter.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: فِئَة classified under F ʾ Y

Neither word is invariably restricted to a modern political organization.

Ḥ Z B and J N D: group and army

جُنْد concerns a body of forces, often an army.

حِزْب is broader and can name supporters of an opinion or position.

The two groups in the cave account are not named as armies.

The groups in Al-Aḥzāb participate in a military coalition.

Context determines whether a particular group is intellectual or military.

Study of J N D

Ḥ Z B and W L Y: groups and allegiance

وَلِيَ and its derivatives concern closeness, protection, and alignment.

Al-Māʾidah 5:56 places يَتَوَلَّ in the same sentence as “God’s group.”

This associates the group with allegiance.

حِزْب is not the full meaning of wilāyah; questions of protection and leadership cannot all be derived from it.

Study of W L Y

Ḥ Z B and N Ṣ R: groups and support

نَصَرَ concerns helping.

A ḥizb may be a group of supporters.

Not every helper must belong to a particular group.

Someone may support another in a just matter without sharing all their beliefs or actions.

Affiliation and the act of helping are related but different.

Study of N Ṣ R

Ḥ Z B and Ḥ S B: grouping and reckoning

حَسِبَ comes from ح س ب, which concerns supposing and reckoning in different uses.

Al-Kahf 18:12 places الْحِزْبَيْنِ alongside أَحْصَىٰ.

The groups and the act of counting belong to different families.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:20 likewise places يَحْسَبُونَ near الْأَحْزَاب.

Similar spelling should not cause the roots to be confused.

Study of Ḥ S B

Ḥ Z B and Ḥ Z N: groups and grief

حُزْن comes from ح ز ن and concerns grief.

حِزْب ends in bāʾ and names a group.

A group may experience grief, but the noun itself does not mean grief.

Likewise, حَزَبَهُ الْأَمْرُ in its difficulty sense is not the same root as حَزِنَ, becoming sad.

Study of Ḥ Z N

Ḥ Z B and Ḥ Z M: gathering and firmness

حَزْم, from ح ز م, concerns binding or securing and, in another use, prudence.

حِزْب comes from ح ز ب.

An imaginable connection between gathering and securing does not make similar letters proof of historical kinship.

Prudence and firmness should not be added to Qur’anic حِزْب without evidence.

8. Interpretive questions and semantic development

Is Qur’anic حِزْب identical to a modern political party?

Not exclusively.

A modern political party normally has members, political objectives, a programme, and a defined structure for influencing power or government.

Qur’anic حِزْب can name groups differing on a question, historical communities, an attacking coalition, or followers of an orientation.

Its Qur’anic sense is broader than the modern institution.

The modern use is nevertheless related to grouping and alignment, as a social extension of that field.

Does a group’s name establish its truthfulness?

No.

The Qur’an identifies God’s group through people’s characteristics and relationship to God and His messenger.

A religious name alone is not proof of being right.

A group may adopt the name while acting contrary to justice, honesty, or clear Qur’anic instructions.

People without formal group titles may act in closer allegiance to truth.

Names and claims should be assessed separately from actual conduct.

Is sectarianism identical to every form of organization?

No.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:53 and Al-Rūm 30:32 criticize religious division and each group’s satisfaction with its own holdings.

Al-Māʾidah 5:56 and Al-Mujādilah 58:22 speak of God’s group.

Other verses support cooperation, consultation, faithfulness to commitments, and good work.

Criticism of sectarianism cannot by itself prohibit cooperation or forming groups.

The central concern is that affiliation should not displace truth and justice.

Can partisanship hinder recognizing truth?

Yes; human experience makes this danger understandable.

Someone who judges truth only by their group’s name may overlook its errors or reject others’ true statements.

Al-Rūm 30:32 presents satisfaction with one’s own group’s possessions in a setting of religious division.

The image bears on the danger of unjustified partisanship.

Reflection on human behaviour should be distinguished from categorical claims about every member of a group.

The verse does not establish that every affiliated person is necessarily prejudiced or incapable of recognizing truth.

People’s behaviour and judgement require evidence.

Are God’s group and Satan’s group two formally enrolled organizations?

The verses do not introduce that structure.

Al-Mujādilah 58:19 concerns Satan’s mastery and forgetting God’s remembrance.

Al-Mujādilah 58:22 concerns faith and allegiance to God and His messenger in opposition to hostility towards them.

The expressions identify different orientations.

The word itself supplies no system of enrolment, expulsion, or organizational command.

A person’s final standing cannot be soundly judged by a group name alone without knowing their beliefs and conduct.

Are the groups always contemporaneous?

No.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:20 and Al-Aḥzāb 33:22 concern allies in a particular confrontation.

Ghāfir 40:30 and Ṣād 38:13 concern historical groups of different periods.

All the groups need not have gathered at one time and place.

Similar positions or conduct may be their common feature.

This helps prevent restricting the noun to a military coalition.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

For general حِزْب, “group” or “body of people” is suitable.

Persian also recognizes the borrowed word ḥizb, but it may immediately suggest a modern political party.

“Group” can be more natural where an ordinary human collective is meant.

For الْحِزْبَيْنِ in Al-Kahf 18:12, “the two groups” is suitable.

For كُلُّ حِزْبٍ بِمَا لَدَيْهِمْ فَرِحُونَ in Al-Muʾminūn 23:53 and Al-Rūm 30:32, “each group rejoices in what it has” preserves the descriptive force.

For الْأَحْزَاب in Al-Aḥzāb 33:20 and Al-Aḥzāb 33:22, “confederate groups” or “allied forces” suits the setting.

“Earlier groups” is a contextual explanation for Ghāfir 40:30 and Ṣād 38:13, but “earlier” does not come from the noun alone.

For Maryam 19:37 and Al-Zukhruf 43:65, “groups” or “different factions” is suitable.

حِزْبُ اللَّهِ may be “God’s group,” or “God’s party” if the established expression is retained.

حِزْبُ الشَّيْطَانِ may be “Satan’s group” or “Satan’s party.”

The possessive relationship should remain; a particular political or religious organization should not replace it without evidence.

Forqan’s word box should retain two separate parts.

Brief root meaning: “Gathering and forming a collective; people joined by a shared affiliation, position, or purpose.”

Meaning in Al-Kahf 18:12: “The two groups distinguished over the duration of the cave’s sleepers’ stay.”

Meaning in Al-Muʾminūn 23:53 and Al-Rūm 30:32: “Groups divided over religion, each satisfied with its own possessions.”

Meaning in Al-Aḥzāb 33:20 and Al-Aḥzāb 33:22: “Allied groups and forces gathered in an armed confrontation.”

Meaning in Ghāfir 40:30 and Ṣād 38:13: “Earlier groups and communities who opposed their messengers.”

Meaning in Al-Māʾidah 5:56 and Al-Mujādilah 58:22: “Those aligned in allegiance with God and His messenger.”

Meaning in Fāṭir 35:6 and Al-Mujādilah 58:19: “Satan’s followers and supporters in the orientation and conduct criticized by the verses.”

These distinctions matter: one fixed equivalent such as “sect” or “political party” cannot represent every Qur’anic use.

10. Conclusion

ح ز ب concerns gathering and forming a collective.

حِزْب names people joined by a shared opinion, purpose, position, or affiliation.

It also names a defined portion of a whole, such as property or text.

Early branches include حَزَبَهُ الْأَمْرُ for difficulty and pressure, حَزَابِي for stockiness, and حِزْبَاء for hard, uneven terrain.

Every Qur’anic occurrence concerns groups of people.

These groups do not all have the same character.

Al-Kahf 18:12 distinguishes two groups over a question of duration.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:53 and Al-Rūm 30:32 link grouping with dividing religion and satisfaction with each group’s own holdings.

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:20 and Al-Aḥzāb 33:22, the word concerns allied forces in an armed confrontation.

Ghāfir and Ṣād describe historical peoples of different periods under the same designation.

Al-Māʾidah 5:56 and Al-Mujādilah 58:22 connect God’s group with allegiance to God and His messenger and the characteristics of believers.

Fāṭir 35:6 and Al-Mujādilah 58:19 describe Satan’s group through following him in conduct and orientation.

Being a group is not inherently a virtue or sin in the Qur’an. It is a collective form of affiliation whose value depends on its members’ orientation and actions.

This also matters for understanding sectarianism.

The Qur’an criticizes dividing religion and each group’s satisfaction with its own holdings. This does not establish that all cooperation, alliance, or organization is forbidden.

Conversely, choosing the name “God’s group” does not exempt any group from assessment by truth and justice.

The actual relationship and conduct matter more than the name.

For Forqan, “group” is a suitable basic equivalent, “confederates” suits wartime settings, and the possessive expressions “God’s group” and “Satan’s group” can be retained.

This preserves lexical meaning without imposing later political or sectarian uses as the exclusive meaning throughout the Qur’an.
