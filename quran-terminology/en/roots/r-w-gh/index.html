From turning aside and changing course to discreet and deliberate movement

The Arabic root R W Gh denotes inclining, turning from a direct course, and moving in another direction, often discreetly or with some strategy. It describes animal movement, evasion, changes of direction, seeking an objective, and deliberate human movement.

All its Qur’anic occurrences concern Abraham. After his people leave, he turns toward their objects of worship and then turns upon them, striking them. Elsewhere, while hosting guests, he goes discreetly to his household and brings food.

Rawgh is not inherently immoral deception or a good deed. Its core concerns movement and direction; context establishes the destination, motive, and moral character.

1. Qur’anic forms and semantic core

The root occurs three times: twice in Al-Ṣāffāt and once in Al-Dhāriyāt:

  • Al-Ṣāffāt 37:91 — فَرَاغَ إِلَىٰ آلِهَتِهِمْ
  • Al-Ṣāffāt 37:93 — فَرَاغَ عَلَيْهِمْ
  • Al-Dhāriyāt 51:26 — فَرَاغَ إِلَىٰ أَهْلِهِ

All three use rāgha, a Form I past-tense verb in the third-person masculine singular. The prefixed fa- connects and sequences events; it is not part of the root.

Yarūghu, rawgh, rawaghān, murāwaghah, and arāgha are recorded in Arabic, but none occurs in the Qur’an in those forms.

This is a hollow wāw root: its middle wāw becomes alif in rāgha and appears in yarūghu.

An early lexical analysis identifies one semantic principle:

أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى مَيْلٍ وَقِلَّةِ اسْتِقْرَارٍ.

aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā maylin wa-qillati istiqrārin

Meaning: “One principle indicating inclination and little remaining in place.”

“Little remaining in place” here concerns movement, changes of direction, and not keeping to a fixed course; it does not mean moral unreliability or indecision.

Another early explanation describes rawgh as turning aside with a measure of strategy or contrivance.

These usages support the following account of the root:

Turning or inclining from one course toward another, often indirectly, discreetly, or deliberately in pursuit of an objective.

This preserves the movement sense and distinguishes it from ordinary going without making deception, speed, or ill will indispensable.

2. Early Arabic: turning, evasion, and seeking an objective

A fox’s changes of direction

A familiar usage describes a fox’s movement:

“رَاغَ الثَّعْلَبُ يَرُوغُ رَوَغَانًا”

Meaning: “The fox twists and turns, changing its course.”

When escaping or pursuing prey, a fox may leave a straight course and turn repeatedly. Murāwaghah is used for evasive or misleading movements and changes of direction to escape pursuit or overcome an opponent.

The image combines indirect movement with avoiding disclosure of the actual route or destination.

The fox is an illustrative example, not proof that every derivative necessarily compares its subject to a fox.

A winding road and indirect movement

Ṭarīqun rāʾighun describes a road that is not straight but bends or winds.

Inclination can thus be a property of the route without deception or concealed intent.

Applied to human behavior, changing course can involve planning, bypassing an obstacle, or avoiding attention.

An early poetic witness

A line attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAdī ibn Zayd al-ʿIbādī reads:

يَوْمَ لَا يَنْفَعُ الرَّوَاغُ وَلَا يُقْدِمُ إِلَّا الْمُشَيَّعُ النَّحْرِيرُ

yawma lā yanfaʿu al-rawāghu wa-lā yuqdimu illā al-mushayyaʿu al-naḥrīru

Translation: “On a day when evasion is useless and only the brave, experienced man advances.”

The line is transmitted with attribution and wording variants. Another reading has “ولا ينفع إلا” rather than “ولا يقدم إلا”; the translation here follows the quoted reading, without claiming that every copy of the poet’s collection has identical wording.

Al-rawāgh here contrasts with advancing and confronting the situation, denoting evasion or withdrawal.

It illustrates the root’s established poetic use for evasive movement; its precise dating must remain subject to the attribution and transmission variants.

From evasion to seeking

Classical Arabic records several further branches.

Rāwagha fulānun fulānan describes mutual maneuvering, including wrestling, where each opponent tries to gain an advantage through changes of direction and strategy.

Arāgha al-shayʾa and irtāghahu are recorded for wanting, seeking, or pursuing something. The connection with finding a route is an explanation of the usage, not a requirement that every instance involve physical movement.

Tarwīgh al-luqmah also denotes coating a morsel with fat or oil; one early explanation connects it with turning the morsel in the fat.

These branches extend beyond spatial movement. Seeking, wrestling, or coating food should not be imported directly into Qur’anic rāgha. The passages emphasize turning and indirect or discreet movement.

3. Al-Ṣāffāt 37:91: turning discreetly toward the idols

فَرَاغَ إِلَىٰ آلِهَتِهِمْ فَقَالَ أَلَا تَأْكُلُونَ

fa-rāgha ilā ālihatihim fa-qāla a-lā taʾkulūna

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:91

“Then he went discreetly toward their objects of worship and said, ‘Do you not eat?’”

The preceding verse helps explain this:

فَتَوَلَّوْا عَنْهُ مُدْبِرِينَ

fa-tawallaw ʿanhu mudbirīna

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:90

“Then they turned away from him and departed.”

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:90

After his people leave, Abraham moves toward their objects of worship. Rāgha describes a more particular movement than a general verb such as “went” or “walked.”

He does not openly accompany the others. Once they have left, he changes course and approaches their objects of worship.

Ilā identifies the direction and destination: the objects his people worshipped.

He then asks:

أَلَا تَأْكُلُونَ
مَا لَكُمْ لَا تَنْطِقُونَ

a-lā taʾkulūna / mā lakum lā tanṭiqūna

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:91; Al-Ṣāffāt 37:92

“Do you not eat? What is wrong with you that you do not speak?”

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:91; Al-Ṣāffāt 37:92

Together, the verses show the movement leading to a confrontation with these objects and an exposure of their inability.

Addressing the idols and asking about eating and speaking come from the subsequent clauses, not the lexical meaning of rāgha.

Does rāgha mean deceiving?

The movement takes place out of his people’s sight, but hidden movement is not identical to lying.

The root can occur in deceptive situations without making every instance a lie or wrongdoing.

This verse describes a manner of movement. Its purpose and character must be assessed through the continuing story and related passages.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:57 also reports Abraham’s intention concerning the idols:

وَتَاللَّهِ لَأَكِيدَنَّ أَصْنَامَكُمْ بَعْدَ أَنْ تُوَلُّوا مُدْبِرِينَ

wa-tallāhi la-akīdanna aṣnāmakum baʿda an tuwallū mudbirīna

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:57

“By The God, I will certainly devise a plan against your idols after you turn away and depart.”

The movement belongs to a planned action. Kayd and rawgh nonetheless differ: the former concerns planning, while the latter here describes movement and direction.

4. Al-Ṣāffāt 37:93: turning upon the idols

فَرَاغَ عَلَيْهِمْ ضَرْبًا بِالْيَمِينِ

fa-rāgha ʿalayhim ḍarban bi-al-yamīni

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:93

“Then he turned upon them, striking with his right hand.”

The same rāgha used with ilā in verse 91 now occurs with ʿalā.

In verse 91 he approaches the idols; in verse 93 he turns upon them and strikes.

The verb remains the same; the preposition and accompanying words clarify the direction and action.

Fa-rāgha ilā ālihatihim describes movement toward a destination.

Fa-rāgha ʿalayhim, together with ḍarban, describes turning upon them to strike.

In this construction, ʿalā marks the target of the action. The confrontation and striking emerge from the whole phrase, not from the preposition alone.

Striking should not be extracted from R W Gh itself: ḍarban expresses that action.

Bi-al-yamīn directly permits “with the right hand.” Some explanations also consider an association with Abraham’s oath in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:57. That question concerns yamīn and context, not R W Gh.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:58 describes the result:

فَجَعَلَهُمْ جُذَاذًا إِلَّا كَبِيرًا لَهُمْ لَعَلَّهُمْ إِلَيْهِ يَرْجِعُونَ

fa-jaʿalahum judhādhan illā kabīran lahum laʿallahum ilayhi yarjiʿūna

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:58

“He made them fragments, except a large one among them, so that they might return to it.”

The accounts connect: after his people left, Abraham approached and broke the idols.

Rāgha describes turning and approaching; striking and breaking are specified by other words and verses.

5. Al-Dhāriyāt 51:26: discreet movement to host guests

فَرَاغَ إِلَىٰ أَهْلِهِ فَجَاءَ بِعِجْلٍ سَمِينٍ

fa-rāgha ilā ahlihi fa-jāʾa bi-ʿijlin samīnin

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:26

“Then he went to his household without drawing attention and brought a fat calf.”

This occurs in the account of Abraham’s guests.

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:24 and Al-Dhāriyāt 51:25 describe their arrival and exchange of greetings. Abraham then goes to his household and returns with a fat calf.

Rāgha again takes ilā, but the destination is now his household rather than the idols.

This demonstrates that discreet or indirect movement need not involve wrongful deception.

Abraham is providing hospitality. His movement may have been unobtrusive, without announcing what he intended to prepare.

An early explanation distinguishes this usage from simply going or returning: the movement is kept from others’ notice.

That helps explain the choice of rāgha rather than a generic verb of going.

Hūd 11:69 also describes the hospitality:

فَمَا لَبِثَ أَنْ جَاءَ بِعِجْلٍ حَنِيذٍ

fa-mā labitha an jāʾa bi-ʿijlin ḥanīdhin

Hūd 11:69

“He did not delay in bringing a roasted calf.”

The verse conveys the lack of delay in bringing food, not its precise preparation time. Speed is not rāgha’s fixed meaning; fa-mā labitha expresses the lack of delay.

The Qur’an does not explicitly state the precise reason for concealing the movement. Preparing food unobtrusively is a possible explanation, not a certain report of Abraham’s unspoken intention.

One root in two situations

Comparing the accounts clarifies the root’s range.

In Al-Ṣāffāt he approaches the idols and breaks them.

In Al-Dhāriyāt he goes to his household to provide food.

Both movements use rāgha, but lead to different actions: confronting idols and hosting guests.

The shared feature is the manner of turning and unobtrusive movement, not identical motivation or moral value.

Rendering every instance as “deceived” is therefore inaccurate; limiting the word to “went slowly” also fails to cover its range.

6. Prepositions and related roots

Rāgha ilā and rāgha ʿalā

The verb takes a preposition in all three Qur’anic occurrences.

Rāgha ilā emphasizes movement toward a destination, whether the idols or the household.

Rāgha ʿalā appears in a construction describing confrontation and striking a target.

ʿAlā alone does not mean striking, but its combination with ḍarban in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:93 clarifies the action’s attacking direction.

Outside the Qur’an, rāgha ʿan also occurs for turning away from someone or something, often discreetly.

The constructions show a root concerning turning and changing course, with the preposition and context establishing the direction.

R W Gh (rawgh) and M Y L (mayl)

Mayl has a broad range: inclination, leaning, and deviation from a previous position or course, physically, psychologically, morally, or figuratively.

The usages of rawgh examined here describe a more particular kind of turning, associated with indirect movement, evasion, or concealing a route or purpose.

Rawgh thus involves inclination and direction change, but not every mayl can be called rawgh.

R W Gh (rawgh) and Ḥ Y D (ḥayd)

Ḥayd concerns turning aside, distancing oneself, or avoiding something.

Qāf 50:19 says:

ذَٰلِكَ مَا كُنْتَ مِنْهُ تَحِيدُ

dhālika mā kunta minhu taḥīdu

Qāf 50:19

“This is what you used to avoid.”

The verse concerns the death a person sought to evade.

Ḥayd can express avoidance without necessarily involving concealment or calculated movement.

Rawgh can also involve turning aside, but the usages examined emphasize indirect movement.

R W Gh (rawgh) and Z Y Gh (zaygh)

Zaygh often describes deviation from the right course or hearts turning away from truth in the Qur’an.

Āl ʿImrān 3:8 says:

رَبَّنَا لَا تُزِغْ قُلُوبَنَا بَعْدَ إِذْ هَدَيْتَنَا

rabbanā lā tuzigh qulūbanā baʿda idh hadaytanā

Āl ʿImrān 3:8

“Our Lord, do not let our hearts deviate after You have guided us.”

The moral and spiritual deviation arises in the context of guidance and hearts.

Rawgh in Abraham’s three passages concerns movement and changing course. It does not inherently imply deviation from truth.

Closeness through inclination and deviation does not make rawgh and zaygh complete synonyms.

R W Gh (rawgh) and K Y D (kayd)

Kayd concerns planning or devising action toward an objective, with its value dependent on agent, purpose, and context.

Qur’anic rawgh primarily describes movement and turning.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:57 expresses Abraham’s plan through la-akīdanna; Al-Ṣāffāt 37:91 and Al-Ṣāffāt 37:93 describe his movements through rāgha.

The meanings connect without being identical: planning may precede the movement whose manner rawgh describes.

Fa-rāgha versus farāgh

Without vowels, Qur’anic fa-rāgha may be confused with farāgh, also borrowed into Persian, but they belong to different roots.

Fa-rāgha consists of the prefixed fa- and rāgha from R W Gh.

Farāgh, farāghah, and fārigh belong to F R Gh, concerning emptiness, completion of an occupation, or becoming free of it.

Similar spelling should not merge the two families’ meanings.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

One fixed equivalent cannot naturally convey every nuance in all three verses.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:91:

فَرَاغَ إِلَىٰ آلِهَتِهِمْ

fa-rāgha ilā ālihatihim

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:91

Proposed: “Then he went discreetly toward their objects of worship.”

“Went discreetly” conveys the particular manner better than “went” alone.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:93:

فَرَاغَ عَلَيْهِمْ ضَرْبًا بِالْيَمِينِ

fa-rāgha ʿalayhim ḍarban bi-al-yamīni

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:93

Proposed: “Then he turned upon them, striking with his right hand.”

This preserves turning toward them while assigning striking to ḍarban.

In Al-Dhāriyāt 51:26:

فَرَاغَ إِلَىٰ أَهْلِهِ فَجَاءَ بِعِجْلٍ سَمِينٍ

fa-rāgha ilā ahlihi fa-jāʾa bi-ʿijlin samīnin

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:26

Proposed: “Then he went to his household without drawing attention and brought a fat calf.”

“Without drawing attention” naturally expresses unobtrusive movement here.

A brief root description can read “turning aside or changing direction, often discreetly.” Each verse’s explanation should also consider its destination and construction. These proposals do not replace existing authored word-box explanations.

8. Conclusion

R W Gh concerns inclination, turning, changes of course, and indirect movement. Human usages often involve discretion, planning, or avoiding attention.

Early usages range from a fox’s evasive movement and winding roads to maneuvering, escape, and seeking an objective.

All three Qur’anic occurrences concern Abraham: two describe approaching and turning upon the idols, and one describes going to his household to provide food.

Discreet or indirect movement does not necessarily mean lying, betrayal, fear, or ill will. Such judgments depend on purpose, action, and context.

Rawgh does not inherently mean speed, striking, or overpowering. Those meanings, where relevant, come from accompanying words and the full construction.

Final definition for Forqan:

R W Gh denotes turning from an ordinary course or moving elsewhere indirectly, often concealing the movement or acting strategically toward an objective. Prepositions and context specify the direction and precise meaning.

Its prominent Qur’anic feature is the manner of movement and turning, not a moral judgment about the person moving.
