The Qur’anic root B D R (badr and bidār)

Hastening and anticipating, reaching fullness and completion, and the name Badr: from hurried consumption of orphans’ property to the recollection of victory at Badr

Qur’anic and lexical forms

بِدَارًا
(bidāran)
Qur’anic form III verbal noun, accusative; Al-Nisāʾ 4:6

بَدْر
(Badr)
Place name in Āl ʿImrān 3:123; counted separately from lexical derivatives

بَدَرَ
(badara)
Lexical base verb; this form does not occur in the Qur’an

بَادَرَ
(bādara)
Lexical form III verb; the verb itself is not Qur’anic

مُبَادَرَة
(mubādarah)
Lexical form III verbal noun; not Qur’anic

بَادِرَة
(bādirah)
Lexical form; not Qur’anic

بَدْر، بُدُور
(badr, budūr)
Full moon and its lexical plural; not Qur’anic in this sense

بَدْرَة
(badrah)
Lexical form used of an eye or a purse; not Qur’anic

1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

In early Arabic, B D R has two prominent semantic fields: hastening, anticipating, and acting before a particular opportunity arrives; and becoming complete or full, as illustrated by badr, the full moon.

Ibn Fāris presents these as two distinct semantic bases in Maqāyīs al-lughah. The investigation should therefore not begin by assuming that every word in this family necessarily developed from one underlying image.

An essential distinction applies to the Qur’anic occurrences:

  • وَبِدَارًا in Al-Nisāʾ 4:6 is a form III verbal noun of this root, used in a passage about hastening to consume orphans’ property.
  • بِبَدْرٍ in Āl ʿImrān 3:123 is a place name, recalling the believers’ victory there.

The Qur’anic Arabic Corpus records B D R once: بِدَارًا in Al-Nisāʾ 4:6. The proper name Badr also occurs in Āl ʿImrān 3:123, but is not included in that index’s count of lexical derivatives.

Two forms in the Qur’anic text are thus relevant to this study, but only one directly illustrates the root’s verbal sense.

Forms such as بَادَرَ (bādara), مُبَادَرَة (mubādarah), بَادِرَة (bādirah), بَدْر (badr) meaning the full moon, and بُدُور (budūr) are attested in Arabic, but do not occur in the Qur’an in these forms and senses.

The distinction matters because a place name, even if historically connected with a lexical family, does not necessarily carry that family’s descriptive meaning in a sentence.

2. Two semantic branches in early Arabic

Hastening and anticipating

Lisān al-ʿArab records:

«بَدَرْتُ إِلَى الشَّيْءِ أَبْدُرُ بُدُورًا: أَسْرَعْتُ»

badartu ilā al-shayʾi abduru budūran: asraʿtu

That is: “I hastened toward something.”

The same entry connects بَادَرَ (bādara), تَبَادَرَ (tabādara), and اِبْتَدَرَ (ibtadara) with hastening and getting ahead.

For example, اِبْتَدَرُوا السِّلَاحَ describes people hurrying to take up weapons. تَبَادَرَ الْقَوْمُ can refer to members of a group hastening ahead of one another.

These uses concern more than the speed of movement alone. In many cases, someone seeks to act before another person or before a particular time arrives.

For example, a person may hurry to assist someone in need rather than miss the opportunity. Another may anticipate a right-holder’s claim in order to seize what belongs to that person.

Both involve haste and anticipation, but their moral value differs.

B D R does not intrinsically mean greed, injustice, or deception. Those features emerge from the action’s purpose and circumstances.

Bādirah: a sudden utterance or action

بَادِرَة (bādirah) can also designate an utterance or action that issues suddenly from a person, especially in anger.

بَوَادِرُ الْغَضَبِ refers to reactions that emerge before sufficient reflection under the force of anger.

Anticipation and haste remain discernible, but the subject is no longer the speed of walking or running. What occurs rapidly is a person’s speech or action.

Lexical sources also record bādirah for the first signs of plant growth and certain other early appearances. Such uses relate to precedence and early emergence, but should not all be treated as stages of a proven derivational history without historical investigation.

Completion and fullness: badr as the full moon

The family’s other branch appears in بَدْر (badr), the full moon.

Ibn Fāris writes:

«وَسُمِّيَ الْبَدْرُ بَدْرًا لِتَمَامِهِ وَامْتِلَائِهِ»

wa-summiya al-badru badran li-tamāmihi wa-imtilāʾihi

That is: “The full moon was called badr because of its completeness and fullness.”

The same semantic field includes غُلَامٌ بَدْرٌ, a youth whose physical growth has reached fullness, and عَيْنٌ بَدْرَةٌ, a full or prominent eye, as recorded in lexical sources.

بَدْرَة (badrah) is also used for a purse containing a specified amount of money and for certain leather containers. Lexical reports give different amounts for the money purse, so no single number should be treated as its universal definition. Ibn Fāris explains the usage through capacity and a complete quantity, whereas Lisān al-ʿArab connects the purse’s name with a kid’s hide. Linking all these uses with fullness is therefore a lexical explanation, not proof of a definite derivational history.

Was the full moon named for fullness or anticipation?

Another explanation of the full moon’s name also appears in the sources.

Lisān al-ʿArab transmits the explanation that the full moon was called badr because it anticipates the sun’s setting by rising.

This account relates to the familiar observation that the full moon rises near sunset. The sources also explain the name through the completeness and fullness of its disc.

These two reports do not establish with certainty which was the word’s original historical basis or whether one explanation developed later from the other.

The name may have been explained through the moon’s fullness, while other lexicographers perceived a link with its anticipating sunset by rising. The availability of both explanations does not establish the derivational history.

Forqan should therefore recognize the two fields of haste and completeness without forcing one to be the proven origin of the other.

3. Early poetic evidence

A witness for hastening and anticipating

Lisān al-ʿArab attributes this verse to Abū al-Muthallam al-Hudhalī:

فَيَبْدُرُهَا شَرَائِعَهَا فَيَرْمِي

fa-yabduruhā sharāʾiʿahā fa-yarmī

مَقَاتِلَهَا فَيَسْقِيهَا الزُّؤَامَا

maqātilahā fa-yasqīhā al-zuʾāmā

Translation: “He reaches their watering places before them, shoots at the fatal spots of their bodies, and makes them drink sudden death.” The poem’s context concerns a hunter and ibexes.

فَيَبْدُرُهَا indicates anticipating and arriving first. Lisān al-ʿArab explains that the usual construction could have included إِلَى before شَرَائِعَهَا, but the poet omitted the preposition.

The witness thus relates B D R, in a concrete action, to getting ahead and hastening to arrive.

The attribution requires caution: Dīwān al-Hudhaliyyīn places the verse in Ṣakhr al-Ghayy’s elegy for his son Talīd, whereas Lisān al-ʿArab names Abū al-Muthallam. Some sources classify Abū al-Muthallam as pre-Islamic and others as a poet spanning the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods; the verse’s own composition date is not independently established. It is an early poetic witness, but neither its attribution to Abū al-Muthallam nor its composition before revelation should be treated as certain.

A witness for fullness and prominence

A poem attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays describes a horse’s body and eyes:

وَعَيْنٌ لَهَا حَدْرَةٌ بَدْرَةٌ

wa-ʿaynun lahā ḥadratun badratun

وَشُقَّتْ مَآقِيهَا مِنْ أُخَرْ

wa-shuqqat maʾāqīhā min ukhar

Sense translation: “She has a full, prominent eye, with open corners.”

The verse occurs in the Dīwān and early literary sources with variant wording. بَدْرَة itself has also received different explanations: some connect it with the eye’s fullness and size, others with its keen, anticipating gaze. After reporting these possibilities, Lisān al-ʿArab prefers Ibn al-Aʿrābī’s explanation through the gaze’s anticipation. Fullness in the translation is therefore one transmitted interpretation, not an agreed meaning.

The witness is useful for the family’s linguistic range, but cannot by itself establish that every use of badrah belongs exclusively to one semantic branch.

Together, the poetic and lexical witnesses attest haste, precedence, fullness, and completeness in this Arabic family. The Qur’anic بِدَارًا belongs to the branch of hastening and anticipating, rather than the moon’s becoming full.

4. وَبِدَارًا أَنْ يَكْبَرُوا: anticipating the orphans’ maturity by consuming their property

Al-Nisāʾ 4:6

وَابْتَلُوا الْيَتَامَىٰ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا بَلَغُوا النِّكَاحَ فَإِنْ آنَسْتُمْ مِنْهُمْ رُشْدًا فَادْفَعُوا إِلَيْهِمْ أَمْوَالَهُمْ ۖ وَلَا تَأْكُلُوهَا إِسْرَافًا وَبِدَارًا أَنْ يَكْبَرُوا ۚ وَمَنْ كَانَ غَنِيًّا فَلْيَسْتَعْفِفْ ۖ وَمَنْ كَانَ فَقِيرًا فَلْيَأْكُلْ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ ۚ فَإِذَا دَفَعْتُمْ إِلَيْهِمْ أَمْوَالَهُمْ فَأَشْهِدُوا عَلَيْهِمْ ۚ وَكَفَىٰ بِاللَّهِ حَسِيبًا

wa-btalū al-yatāmā ḥattā idhā balaghū al-nikāḥa fa-in ānastum minhum rushdan fa-dfaʿū ilayhim amwālahum wa-lā taʾkulūhā isrāfan wa-bidāran an yakbarū wa-man kāna ghaniyyan fa-l-yastaʿfif wa-man kāna faqīran fa-l-yaʾkul bi-l-maʿrūfi fa-idhā dafaʿtum ilayhim amwālahum fa-ashhidū ʿalayhim wa-kafā bi-llāhi ḥasīban

Translation: “Test the orphans until they reach marriageable age. Then, if you discern sound judgment in them, hand over their property to them. Do not consume it excessively and in haste before they grow up. Whoever is wealthy should abstain, and whoever is poor should consume according to what is right. When you hand over their property to them, call witnesses over them; and The God suffices as Reckoner.”

The morphology of bidāran

بِدَارًا (bidāran) is an accusative form III verbal noun, corresponding to بَادَرَ يُبَادِرُ (bādara yubādiru). It concerns hastening, anticipating, and acting to get ahead of another person or a particular event.

Its distinction from badr, the full moon, is clear. Here the pattern is فِعَال (fiʿāl), and the sense is haste, rather than fullness or completeness.

وَلَا تَأْكُلُوهَا إِسْرَافًا وَبِدَارًا contains two accusative verbal nouns: إِسْرَافًا from S R F and بِدَارًا from B D R.

The former concerns excess and exceeding limits; the latter expresses haste and anticipation.

Syntactically, بِدَارًا أَنْ يَكْبَرُوا expresses hastening to get ahead of the orphans’ growing up. أَنْ يَكْبَرُوا is a clause functioning as a verbal noun that identifies what is being anticipated.

In Jāmiʿ al-bayān, al-Ṭabarī likewise explains بِدَارًا through مُبَادَرَة and relates the construction to hurried consumption of the property before the orphans reach adulthood.

Why is haste condemned here?

The context concerns property belonging to orphans and held under another person’s guardianship until they attain the necessary sound judgment.

A guardian may know that once the owner grows up and sound judgment is established, the assets must be handed over. If the guardian hastens to consume them for personal benefit beforehand, bidār is realized in this context.

Imagine a guardian responsible for safeguarding a child’s assets. The property belongs to the child from the outset; guardianship creates no right to wrongful personal consumption. Knowing that administration must pass to the owner upon maturity and verified sound judgment, the guardian hastens to consume or dissipate the property beforehand.

Two aspects are distinguishable: wrongful use of another person’s assets, and haste to do so before the owner can take control of them.

The second feature is expressed by بِدَارًا.

“In haste” is therefore an appropriate rendering, but a significant part of the construction is lost if its relationship with أَنْ يَكْبَرُوا is overlooked.

The issue is not every kind of speed in action, but anticipating the time for handing the assets over by consuming them first.

The distinction between isrāfan and bidāran

These two words occur in the same sentence, but describe different aspects of the conduct.

Isrāf focuses on exceeding a limit. Someone may consume assets excessively or wrongfully.

Bidār focuses on haste and anticipation relative to a particular approaching time. Someone seeks to act before losing the opportunity to do so.

A person may therefore consume property gradually but excessively. Another may show no conspicuous extravagance yet hurry to seize or consume a specified amount belonging to someone else.

The verse condemns both forms of conduct in relation to an orphan’s property.

Bidār should therefore not be made synonymous with isrāf or tabdhīr, wasteful dissipation. Their related subject matter in one verse does not make the roots identical.

Does “do not eat it” refer only to eating?

In وَلَا تَأْكُلُوهَا, تَأْكُلُوا is from ʾ K L, and the attached pronoun هَا refers to the orphans’ assets.

“Eating property” here concerns consuming or benefiting from assets; it does not mean that the guardian literally eats the orphan’s banknotes, coins, or food goods.

This semantic extension belongs to أَكَلَ and its financial usage, rather than to B D R.

Bidār should accordingly not be defined as “devouring property.” It expresses haste and anticipation; the object of the wrongful consumption is specified by the sentence’s verb and object.

Does the verse forbid every use of an orphan’s assets by a guardian?

After prohibiting isrāf and bidār, the verse itself distinguishes wealthy and poor guardians:

«وَمَنْ كَانَ غَنِيًّا فَلْيَسْتَعْفِفْ وَمَنْ كَانَ فَقِيرًا فَلْيَأْكُلْ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ»

wa-man kāna ghaniyyan fa-l-yastaʿfif wa-man kāna faqīran fa-l-yaʾkul bi-l-maʿrūfi

The wealthy guardian is instructed to abstain, while use by a poor guardian is addressed within the bounds of maʿrūf, what is recognized as right.

The prohibition of bidār should therefore not become an absolute ban on every use by a poor guardian. Conversely, the provision for need does not permit unlimited consumption or a race to appropriate assets before the orphan grows up.

The limits of بِالْمَعْرُوفِ must be considered within the context of justice, trustworthiness, genuine need, and the owner’s rights.

The opening also distinguishes reaching marriageable age from demonstrating sound judgment. Physical maturity and the ability to administer property should therefore not simply be collapsed into one condition.

The contribution of B D R remains specific: hastening to get ahead of the orphan’s reaching the stage at which control of the property is restored to its owner.

5. Badr in Āl ʿImrān 3:123: a place name

Āl ʿImrān 3:123

وَلَقَدْ نَصَرَكُمُ اللَّهُ بِبَدْرٍ وَأَنْتُمْ أَذِلَّةٌ ۖ فَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَشْكُرُونَ

wa-la-qad naṣarakumu llāhu bi-badrin wa-antum adhillatun fa-ttaqū llāha laʿallakum tashkurūna

Translation: “The God certainly helped you at Badr when you were weak. So remain mindful of The God, that you may give thanks.”

What does Badr mean in this verse?

بِبَدْرٍ consists of the preposition بِـ and the proper name Badr.

Badr here names the recognized place where the believers’ victory occurred. The verse itself speaks of The God’s help there and the addressees’ weakness.

Grammatically, Badr is genitive after بِـ; the phrase identifies the location of the event.

Early historical and geographical sources identify Badr as a watering place or locality between Mecca and Medina. The battle bearing its name occurred in that area.

بِبَدْرٍ should therefore not be translated as “at the full moon,” “at fullness,” or “in haste.”

Its meaning in this sentence is “at Badr.”

Was the place named after the full moon?

Historical reports disagree about the origin of the place name.

Al-Ṭabarī transmits the account that a well or watering place belonged to a man named Badr and came to be known by his name.

He also transmits an opposing account: people familiar with the region rejected that attribution and regarded Badr as the locality’s own name.

The place cannot therefore confidently be said to have been named for its resemblance to the full moon. Even the account deriving its name from a person was contested in the historical sources.

Three matters must be distinguished: badr as the full moon, Badr as a personal name, and Badr as a place name.

Their shared form alone does not establish an identical reason for naming them.

Does Badr here also mean haste or complete victory?

The linguistic form itself establishes no such meaning.

The Qur’an says that The God helped the believers at Badr. The reference to victory and their relative weakness occurs in other parts of the verse.

An attempt to connect the name Badr with completion, fullness, or haste as a literary association would first require valid contextual evidence.

Without it, adding fullness and completeness to the place name remains a possible interpretation, rather than a direct translation.

This caution matters because root studies sometimes return every proper name to a supposed root meaning and then derive symbolic or theological conclusions. That method requires independent evidence.

The verse’s focus: help in weakness

وَأَنْتُمْ أَذِلَّةٌ, in the context of the victory at Badr, refers to the believers’ weak position and limited strength.

“When you were weak” or “when you were in a weak position” thus makes the contextual meaning clear.

The verse recalls The God’s help and then calls for mindfulness and gratitude.

Mentioning Badr does not define the name lexically; it recalls an event familiar to the addressees.

The occurrence is relevant to this root study, but must be classified correctly: Badr here is a proper name, not direct evidence for hastening or becoming complete.

6. Distinctions from related roots

B D R (bidār) and S R ʿ (surʿah)

Surʿah emphasizes speed of movement or action.

Bidār also concerns haste, but in uses such as Al-Nisāʾ 4:6, anticipation and action before a particular time are more prominent.

An action may be performed quickly without competition or precedence over another, whereas bidār often suggests such a relationship.

This distinction should not become an absolute boundary: the two roots overlap in expressing haste.

Study of S R ʿ

B D R (bidār) and ʿ J L (ʿajal)

ʿ J L also concerns hastening and, depending on context, can express hurried conduct or an early request for something.

ʿAjalah and mubādarah are often close in sense. In Al-Nisāʾ 4:6, however, bidār is specifically accompanied by أَنْ يَكْبَرُوا and expresses anticipation of the orphans’ growing up.

The distinctive contribution emerges from the root’s construction with its complement, rather than from any rule that all ʿajalah is blameworthy and all mubādarah praiseworthy.

Study of ʿ J L

B D R (bidār) and S B Q (sabq)

Sabq concerns getting ahead and taking precedence, sometimes as the result of haste.

Someone may get ahead through mubādarah, but the two words do not focus on precisely the same aspect.

Mubādarah emphasizes haste in acting, whereas sabq can express precedence itself or the resulting position ahead.

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:6, the issue is anticipating the orphans’ maturity by consuming their property. Although getting ahead is involved, bidār itself does not name a race or victory in it.

Study of S B Q

B D R (bidār) and S R F (isrāf)

These words occur together in one verse but differ in meaning.

Isrāf expresses exceeding limits in conduct or consumption, and bidār haste to act before an approaching time.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:6 prohibits both forms of conduct in consuming orphans’ assets.

Study of S R F

B D R (bidār) and B D ʾ (badʾ)

B D ʾ concerns beginning; the verbal branch of B D R concerns hastening and anticipating.

Someone may be the first to begin an action, but beginning alone does not indicate haste or impatience.

Conversely, someone may hurry to perform an action without being its original initiator.

Sharing the letters bāʾ and dāl does not make these roots identical in meaning.

Study of B D ʾ

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

In uses involving haste, “hasten,” “anticipate,” and “hurry to get ahead of something” are available equivalents.

For Qur’anic بِدَارًا in Al-Nisāʾ 4:6, however, the translation should preserve its relationship with the orphans’ growing up.

A short gloss for the word itself may be “in haste” or “in anticipation.”

وَلَا تَأْكُلُوهَا إِسْرَافًا وَبِدَارًا أَنْ يَكْبَرُوا can be rendered:

“Do not consume that property excessively and in haste before they grow up.”

The explanation should make clear that the haste anticipates the orphans’ reaching the stage at which their assets must be handed over.

“Theft,” “embezzlement,” and “appropriation of assets” should not appear as independent meanings of بِدَارًا in the word box, even though the conduct described in context may violate another person’s property rights.

For Badr in Āl ʿImrān 3:123, the English equivalent is simply “Badr.”

The proper name should not become “the full moon” or “completeness” in translation. Historical information about the locality should remain separate from the word’s lexical definition.

8. Conclusion

In early Arabic, B D R has two prominent semantic fields: haste and anticipation, and reaching fullness and completeness.

In the first branch, verbs such as بَدَرَ (badara) and بَادَرَ (bādara) express hastening and getting ahead. In the second, بَدْر (badr) names the full moon, and expressions involving the eye’s fullness, physical growth, and a complete quantity are also recorded.

The existence of these branches does not prove that one was the other’s historical origin. Early lexicographers offered different explanations of the full moon’s name, and the available evidence does not establish a definite derivational sequence.

The Qur’an’s sole direct morphological use of this root is بِدَارًا in Al-Nisāʾ 4:6. It concerns hastening to consume orphans’ property before they grow up.

The conduct’s condemnation comes from its object and context. Haste in itself is neither a sin nor a virtue; its value depends on what someone hastens to do.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:123, Badr is a place name whose meaning is not replaced by the full moon or the concept of haste. Historical reports also differ about the origin of the locality’s name.

A suitable final definition for Forqan:

In Arabic, B D R has two recognized semantic branches: hastening and anticipating in action, and reaching fullness and completeness, as illustrated by the full moon’s name. Qur’anic بِدَارًا in Al-Nisāʾ 4:6 concerns hastening to consume orphans’ assets before they grow up. Badr in Āl ʿImrān 3:123 is a place name; neither haste nor fullness should be imposed on it without independent evidence.
