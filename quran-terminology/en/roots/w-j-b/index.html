Wajaba: falling and settling, becoming established and binding, and its relation to obligation

1. Meaning in early Arabic

W J B concerns falling, settling, and becoming established. In one principal use, wajaba yajibu means to fall or collapse.

For example, a wall collapsing to the ground can be described as wajaba al-ḥāʾiṭu.

The same family can describe an animal falling after a blow or being killed.

Early sources record wajaba al-baytu for a house collapsing and wajabat al-shamsu for the sun setting.

The latter concerns its descent in the observer's horizon, not the sun physically falling onto the ground.

These uses describe a body falling or a structure collapsing; the sunset expression extends the image to disappearance below the horizon.

This is not the root's only branch.

Wajaba al-shayʾu also means that something became established and binding. Wajaba al-bayʿu describes a transaction becoming final and binding.

This concerns an established effect and obligation, rather than a body falling.

Some early philologists connect the meanings: as a fallen body settles, a finalized matter becomes established and binding.

The explanation makes their semantic relationship understandable without proving the precise history of the obligatory sense.

A witness from Qays ibn al-Khaṭīm

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Qays ibn al-Khaṭīm describes warfare between groups of Aws and Khazraj:

أَطَاعَتْ بَنُو عَوْفٍ أَمِيرًا نَهَاهُمُ

aṭāʿat banū ʿawfin amīran nahāhum

عَنِ السِّلْمِ حَتَّى كَانَ أَوَّلَ وَاجِبِ

ʿani al-silmi ḥattā kāna awwala wājibi

“Banū ʿAwf obeyed a commander who kept them from peace, until he himself became the first to fall.”

Wājib here means someone fallen and killed.

The commander had prevented his people from making peace and drawn them into continued fighting, only to become its first casualty.

Wājib here therefore does not mean a duty that must be performed.

Awwala wājibi means the first person to fall and die in battle.

The family thus had a clear early poetic use concerning falling and death.

The verse is recorded in the poetry attributed to Qays and in early dictionaries. Qays was a pre-Islamic poet, and the poem concerns the pre-Hijrah battle of Buʿāth. Its precise composition date and earliest recorded wording are nevertheless not independently certain.

Together with the Quranic use and early lexical accounts, this witness supports falling to the ground as a meaning near the period of revelation.

The semantic evidence

Familiar uses yield two principal branches:

First, falling, settling, and reaching the ground.

Second, becoming established, definite, or binding.

Uses concerning the sound of a fall, heartbeat, and other conditions are also recorded.

They should not all be reduced to “falling” or “compulsion” without separate examination.

The sole Quranic occurrence directly means falling and settling, not becoming obligatory in the legal sense.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important recorded forms include:

  • Wajaba: fell or settled; alternatively, became established and binding.
  • Yajibu: falls; alternatively, becomes binding.
  • Wajb and wujūb: falling; in another branch, becoming binding or definite.
  • Wajbah: a fall, sometimes with a heavy sound.
  • Wājib: fallen or killed; alternatively, required or binding.
  • Awjaba: made binding or imposed an obligation.
  • Ījāb: establishing an obligation.
  • Wajjaba: in some uses, caused to fall or struck firmly onto the ground; alternatively, made binding.
  • Tawjīb: causing a fall or imposing an obligation, according to context.
  • Istawjaba: became deserving or entitled to something.
  • Mūjib: something that makes a consequence necessary or creates an obligation.
  • Mūjab: what is required or prescribed; in other uses, a consequence or implication.
  • Wajīb: throbbing or beating, especially of the heart.
  • Wajbah: in another use, a meal or occasion of eating.

These forms are recorded in Arabic, but not all occur in the Quran.

Only wajabat occurs there.

Falling and settling

This branch denotes a movement through which something falls or collapses from its previous condition.

A wall may collapse through deterioration or a blow.

It no longer occupies its former upright position.

An animal may likewise fall after being killed and cease moving.

The focus is the fall and the body's position on the ground.

Wajaba does not always mean dying: it can describe a wall, which is not alive.

Death arises where a living subject and its context support that outcome.

Becoming binding and definite

Wajaba al-shayʾu also means something became established and binding.

A finalized transaction with established commitments can thus be described through wujūb.

No merchandise or money is falling to the ground.

The transaction has moved from proposal or possibility to established consequences and commitments.

Istawjaba likewise describes someone becoming deserving of a consequence through an action.

That consequence may be reward or something adverse.

Entitlement and obligation here concern a definite relationship between action and result.

This does not mean that Arabic necessarily uses this root for every certain or inevitable event.

The sound of a fall

Wajbah is also recorded for the heavy sound of something falling.

A large object falling from a height may produce a tremendous noise on impact.

Arabic can describe that sound as wajbah.

The fall and its sound are related in this use.

That relationship does not require every use of wajaba to involve loud noise.

Sunset, for example, can be described by wajaba without physical impact or its sound.

Heartbeat

Wajaba al-qalbu and wajību al-qalbi are recorded for the heart's beating.

Wajīb concerns the motion and throbbing of the heartbeat.

Some early philologists relate this branch to the movement or sound accompanying a fall.

Others suggest a relationship between wajīb and wajīf through a change of sound.

The heartbeat sense therefore cannot confidently be derived from a body falling to the ground.

For this Quranic study, the relevant point is that wajīb is recorded for beating but does not occur in the Quran.

It should not be identified with wajilat qulūbuhum, which belongs to another root.

3. Quranic use: Al-Ḥajj 22:36

W J B occurs only once in the Quran.

Al-Ḥajj 22:36

وَالْبُدْنَ جَعَلْنَاهَا لَكُمْ مِنْ شَعَائِرِ اللَّهِ لَكُمْ فِيهَا خَيْرٌ ۖ فَاذْكُرُوا اسْمَ اللَّهِ عَلَيْهَا صَوَافَّ ۖ فَإِذَا وَجَبَتْ جُنُوبُهَا فَكُلُوا مِنْهَا وَأَطْعِمُوا الْقَانِعَ وَالْمُعْتَرَّ ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ سَخَّرْنَاهَا لَكُمْ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَشْكُرُونَ

wa-al-budna jaʿalnāhā lakum min shaʿāʾiri Allāhi lakum fīhā khayrun fa-dhkurū isma Allāhi ʿalayhā ṣawāffa fa-idhā wajabat junūbuhā fa-kulū minhā wa-aṭʿimū al-qāniʿa wa-al-muʿtarra kadhālika sakhkharnāhā lakum laʿallakum tashkurūn

Al-Ḥajj 22:36

“We made the sacrificial camels among the symbols of The God for you; in them is good for you. Mention the name of The God over them as they stand in rows. When their sides fall, eat from them and feed the contented and the one who approaches asking. Thus We subjected them to you, so that you may give thanks.”

Wajabat describes the animals' sides falling to the ground.

The verse concerns livestock within the divine rites, followed by eating their meat and feeding others.

The structure of wajabat

Wajabat is the third-person feminine singular past tense of the basic verb.

Its root is W J B.

The final tāʾ marks feminine agreement.

Its subject is junūbuhā.

Junūb is the plural of janb, meaning sides or flanks.

The pronoun hā refers to the animals mentioned at the beginning.

Arabic permits a nonhuman plural to take a feminine singular verb; the singular verbal form therefore agrees with plural junūb.

Wajabat here means:

Fell, sank down, or came to rest on the ground.

Fa-idhā wajabat junūbuhā thus means when the animals' sides have fallen to the ground.

Why mention their sides?

The preceding phrase is ʿalayhā ṣawāffa.

Ṣawāffa belongs to Ṣ F F and concerns being arranged in rows or an orderly standing position.

God's name is mentioned over the animals in that condition.

The verse then speaks of their sides falling.

A clear change occurs: formerly standing animals fall after the act concerned.

Wajabat describes that fall and settling.

Familiar Arabic usage applies this expression to an animal dying and falling after slaughter.

Wajaba itself, however, does not mean slaughtering.

Slaughter is the act performed upon the animal; wajabat junūbuhā describes its sides falling.

The stages are semantically distinct.

Does wajabat mean died?

Some early explanations understand wajabat junūbuhā as the animals' sides falling to the ground.

Others explain it through the animal's death and bodily collapse.

Their contextual outcomes are close: after slaughter, the animal falls, and eating its meat follows.

The direct meaning and the event's outcome must nevertheless be distinguished.

Wajabat here expresses falling.

Death is understood from the slaughter and meat-eating context.

A person who faints and falls has fallen without necessarily dying.

An animal dying after slaughter and collapsing onto its side has both fallen and died.

The latter is the context in Al-Ḥajj.

“They died” can explain the contextual result, but “their sides fell to the ground” renders the Arabic more directly.

Fa-idhā and the sequence of events

Fa-idhā introduces the time when the next condition is fulfilled.

First comes mentioning God's name over the animals.

Then their sides fall to the ground.

The command to eat and feed others follows.

The sentence thus has a clear sequence:

Mentioning God's name, the animals falling after the relevant act, and then eating and feeding others from them.

Wajabat alone does not supply all technical slaughtering details, the time until bodily movement ceases, or meat preparation methods.

It describes falling and settling.

Fa-kulū minhā wa-aṭʿimū: eating and feeding

After wajabat junūbuhā, the Quran says:

«فَكُلُوا مِنْهَا وَأَطْعِمُوا الْقَانِعَ وَالْمُعْتَرَّ»

fa-kulū minhā wa-aṭʿimū al-qāniʿa wa-al-muʿtarr

Al-Ḥajj 22:36

Two commands appear:

Eating from the animals and feeding others.

Al-qāniʿ and al-muʿtarr are distinct descriptions of people receiving food in this context.

The current Forqan translation renders them “the contented” and “the one who approaches asking.”

The verse thus addresses both ritual action and people's use and sharing of the meat.

These commands must not be merged into wajaba's meaning.

Eating comes from fa-kulū.

Feeding comes from wa-aṭʿimū.

The animals' sides falling comes from wajabat junūbuhā.

Each part contributes independently.

Connection with Al-Ḥajj 22:37

The next verse explains that neither the animals' meat nor their blood reaches God; people's reverence and moral vigilance do.

The livestock rites should therefore not be reduced to physical acts disconnected from taqwā.

Al-Ḥajj 22:36 describes mentioning God's name, benefiting from a provision, and feeding others.

Al-Ḥajj 22:37 emphasizes taqwā as the important inner reality of the conduct.

Together, the verses connect ritual action, beneficial provision, and moral responsibility.

Their broader implications should not be inserted into the lexical meaning of W J B.

A broader study of the rites must examine the complete context and its relationship to reverence and moral responsibility, separately from this lexical study.

4. Distinguishing wajaba from the technical term wājib

Wājib is among this family's best-known words in present-day Persian.

Calling an act wājib normally means it is required and must not be omitted.

In legal discourse it also became a category of religious duty.

That technical sense should not be transferred to every early Arabic use of the root.

From establishment to obligation

Wajaba al-bayʿu describes a finalized transaction.

The matter has become established and binding, with its consequences taking effect.

In this semantic field, wājib can denote something requiring performance or observance.

Someone accepting a commitment in a valid contract, for example, may become bound to fulfil it.

Wājib here concerns obligation and responsibility, not a falling body.

The obligatory sense is real and established in Arabic, but it is not the direct Quranic use in Al-Ḥajj 22:36.

Does wājib occur in the Quran?

Wājib as a binding legal ruling does not occur in the Quranic text.

The only occurrence of W J B is wajabat in Al-Ḥajj 22:36.

This does not establish that the Quran contains no binding commands or duties.

It uses other words and constructions for commands, duties, prohibitions, and prescriptions.

Kutiba ʿalaykumu al-ṣiyāmu in Al-Baqarah 2:183, for example, expresses the prescription of fasting.

Al-Baqarah 2:197 also uses faraḍa fīhinna al-ḥajja.

Other verses contain direct commands and prohibitions whose scope and force require examination of their wording and context.

The absence of the legal term wājib therefore does not imply the absence of duty.

Conversely, calling an act wājib does not by itself prove that the Quran commands all its associated details.

Determining an act's ruling requires examining the text, audience, kind of command, and conditions.

Are wājib and farḍ always synonymous?

Later legal discourse developed differing accounts of their relationship.

Some legal systems use them closely or synonymously; others distinguish them.

These technical classifications must not define W J B's Quranic meaning.

Farḍ belongs to F R Ḍ and has its own Quranic uses.

Wajaba belongs to another lexical family.

Overlap in certain legal or religious uses does not make their roots or full ranges identical.

5. Comparisons with nearby roots

W J B (wajaba) and S Q Ṭ (saqaṭa): falling

Saqaṭa denotes falling in Arabic and the Quran.

It can describe a body falling or something leaving its previous position.

Wajaba overlaps with this semantic field in its falling branch.

In Al-Ḥajj 22:36, wajabat concerns the animals' sides falling.

Saqaṭa has a broader range of Quranic applications.

This verse does not restrict wajaba throughout Arabic to dead animals or saqaṭa to inanimate objects.

They overlap in falling while differing in forms and semantic branches.

W J B (wajaba) and KH R R (kharra): falling and collapsing

Kharra concerns falling down.

Maryam 19:90 speaks of mountains collapsing.

The family also describes people falling in prostration in worship.

Wajaba in Al-Ḥajj 22:36 concerns the animals' sides falling.

Some early uses associate wajaba with a fall and its heavy sound.

This does not establish a rule that kharra always means a quiet fall and wajaba a noisy one.

Wajabat in Al-Ḥajj does not explicitly describe the sound.

W J B (wajaba) and TH B T (thabata): establishment and stability

Thabāt concerns firmness and remaining stable.

In its obligatory branch, wajaba also describes something becoming established and binding.

Its falling branch, however, concerns a fall.

The words may overlap in establishment without all their derivatives becoming synonymous.

A wall may collapse and settle on the ground; a fall has occurred.

A wall standing firmly for years is also stable without having fallen.

Stability should therefore not replace the direct meaning of wajaba in Al-Ḥajj 22:36.

W J B (wajaba) and F R Ḍ (faraḍa): becoming binding and prescribing

Legal and religious wujūb concerns becoming binding.

Faraḍa is used in the Quran for prescribing or determining something.

The roots nevertheless have independent forms and histories of use.

A rule may be prescribed for someone, making compliance binding.

Prescription and obligation are related here without being the same concept.

Wajabat in Al-Ḥajj 22:36 does not belong to this legal field.

“When sacrifice became obligatory for them” is therefore incompatible with the wording.

W J B (wajaba) and K T B (kataba): obligation and writing

Kataba denotes writing and, in particular Quranic constructions, prescribing.

Al-Baqarah 2:183 uses kutiba ʿalaykumu al-ṣiyāmu for fasting.

The construction concerns a prescribed duty.

Wajabat in Al-Ḥajj 22:36 concerns the animals' sides falling.

Although both families may enter modern discussions of obligation, their Quranic meanings require separate treatment.

W J B (wajaba) and W J L (wajila): falling and trembling hearts

The roots differ only in their final letter.

Al-Ḥajj 22:35 uses wajilat.

Wajilat qulūbuhum describes hearts trembling in awe and being affected when God is mentioned.

Wajabat junūbuhā in Al-Ḥajj 22:36 describes the animals' sides falling.

Their proximity in successive verses should not merge the roots.

W J L concerns fear and inward trembling.

The Quranic W J B concerns falling and settling.

The lexical expression wajīb al-qalb also does not make W J B and W J L identical.

W J B (wajaba) and J W B (jāba): falling and responding

J W B occurs frequently in the Quran, including ajāba and istajāba.

W J B is a separate root.

Their similar letters should not transfer responding or answering into wajaba.

Wajabat in Al-Ḥajj 22:36 has no connection with answering or granting a request.

6. Challenges in interpretation and translation

Does Quranic wajaba mean becoming obligatory?

No. Its sole occurrence describes the animals' sides falling.

The family has an obligatory sense, but it is not used here.

Translation must retain the derivative's actual meaning in its sentence.

Does wajabat junūbuhā merely mean the animals died?

Their sides falling is connected here with their lives ending.

The verb itself expresses falling.

“When they died” conveys the contextual outcome but loses the specific image of falling sides.

“When their sides fell to the ground” is therefore more precise.

Does wajaba always involve a loud noise?

No. Wajbah can denote a falling sound without every wajaba expressing such a noise.

Al-Ḥajj 22:36 does not mention the sound of the fall.

Adding “with a tremendous noise” would introduce meaning beyond the text.

Is the animals' sides falling the name of a devotional act?

No. Wajabat junūbuhā does not name an independent rite or kind of worship.

It describes the animals' condition after the relevant slaughtering act.

Mentioning God's name and feeding others are addressed separately in the same verse.

These acts should not all be merged into W J B's meaning.

Does this verse define obligatory and recommended acts?

No. W J B does not express obligation here.

The force of a command requires examination of the command, audience, conditions, and related verses.

Neither a particular legal obligation nor the absence of binding rulings can be inferred merely from this verb or the Quran's lack of technical wājib.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Suitable equivalents for established branches include:

Wajaba: fell or settled; alternatively, became established and binding.

Yajibu: falls; alternatively, becomes binding.

Wajb and wujūb: falling; in the obligatory branch, becoming binding or definite.

Wajbah: a fall; alternatively, a meal.

Wājib: fallen or killed; alternatively, required or binding.

Awjaba: made binding or obligatory.

Ījāb: making binding, establishing an obligation.

Istawjaba: became deserving or entitled.

Mūjib: a cause, consequence-producing factor, or binding requirement.

Wajīb: throbbing or beating.

The sole Quranic occurrence

Al-Ḥajj 22:36: wajabat

Principal equivalent: “fell,” or in this sentence, “fell to the ground.”

The complete phrase:

«فَإِذَا وَجَبَتْ جُنُوبُهَا»

fa-idhā wajabat junūbuhā

Al-Ḥajj 22:36

“When their sides fall,”

This retains the verb's direct meaning and the verse's image.

“When they died” conveys the contextual result but omits the falling sides.

“When sacrifice became obligatory” is incorrect, substituting a technical obligatory sense for the verb's actual meaning here.

On this point, the current Forqan translation is suitable and needs no change.

8. Conclusion

Early Arabic W J B concerns falling and settling, with another branch for becoming established and binding.

The witness attributed to Qays describes someone fallen and killed in battle as wājib.

Dictionaries record forms for walls collapsing, sunset, falling sounds, heartbeat, and transactions becoming binding.

Some early philologists suggest falling and settling as a shared basis. This can clarify semantic connections without establishing the certain historical origin of every branch.

Only wajabat occurs in the Quran.

In Al-Ḥajj 22:36 it describes the animals' sides falling in the context of slaughter and eating their meat.

Wājib as a binding legal ruling is another use of the family, absent from this verse.

Final definition for Forqan:

W J B denotes falling and settling in one principal branch, and becoming established, definite, or binding in another. Falling sounds and heartbeat are also recorded, but each form requires separate consideration.

Its sole Quranic occurrence, wajabat, means “fell”; fa-idhā wajabat junūbuhā concerns the animals' sides falling after slaughter.

This must remain distinct from technical wājib in legal systems.
