The Qur’anic Root W J H

From the face and facing to direction, intention, and wajh Allāh

The root W J H has a concrete, basic meaning: wajh, the face, the part of a person that faces another. The idea of facing helps explain the connections with other branches: the front, taking a direction, turning toward a destination, the form or manner of something, and, in certain constructions, a person’s whole orientation.

The Qur’an uses several forms from this family: wajh, wijhah, wajīh, wajjaha, and tawajjaha. In some verses wajh is the physical face; elsewhere a person turns their wajh toward religion or The God. Wijhah expresses the direction of facing, tawajjaha movement toward a destination, and wajīh standing or distinction.

Physical and nonphysical uses of wajh occur alongside one another. Its meaning as the face is clear in passages about the body; in other constructions, facing and having a direction help explain its broader uses.

An initial definition can be stated as follows:

W J H: the face or front of a person or thing; connected with this core are turning toward, taking a direction, the side ahead, the form or manner of something, and, in certain constructions, a person’s overall orientation.

The face: the root’s concrete meaning

In many verses, wajh is the physical face.

The passage on washing before prayer states:

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِذَا قُمْتُمْ إِلَى الصَّلَاةِ فَاغْسِلُوا وُجُوهَكُمْ وَأَيْدِيَكُمْ إِلَى الْمَرَافِقِ

Yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū idhā qumtum ilā al-ṣalāti fa-ghsilū wujūhakum wa-aydiyakum ilā al-marāfiqi.

“O you who believe, when you rise for Salat, wash your faces and your hands up to the elbows.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:6

In the account of Jacob, Joseph’s shirt is placed over his wajh:

فَلَمَّا أَنْ جَاءَ الْبَشِيرُ أَلْقَاهُ عَلَىٰ وَجْهِهِ فَارْتَدَّ بَصِيرًا

Fa-lammā an jāʾa al-bashīru alqāhu ʿalā wajhihi fa-rtadda baṣīran.

“When the bearer of good news arrived, he cast it over his face, and he regained his sight.”

Yūsuf 12:96

The same physical sense appears in verses concerning faces being struck or covered with fire, falling upon the face, and washing or wiping it.

In these passages, the concrete meaning of the face is directly connected with washing, covering, or contact with it.

Wajh and dubur: front and back

One of the clearest Qur’anic indications of the core idea of facing is the contrast between wajh and dubur.

Al-Nisāʾ states:

مِنْ قَبْلِ أَنْ نَطْمِسَ وُجُوهًا فَنَرُدَّهَا عَلَىٰ أَدْبَارِهَا

Min qabli an naṭmisa wujūhan fa-naruddahā ʿalā adbārihā.

“Before We efface faces and turn them back upon their backs.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:47

Elsewhere:

وَلَوْ تَرَىٰ إِذْ يَتَوَفَّى الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا الْمَلَائِكَةُ يَضْرِبُونَ وُجُوهَهُمْ وَأَدْبَارَهُمْ

Wa-law tarā idh yatawaffā alladhīna kafarū al-malāʾikatu yaḍribūna wujūhahum wa-adbārahum.

“If only you could see when the angels take those who disbelieved at death, striking their faces and their backs.”

Al-Anfāl 8:50

The same construction recurs in Muḥammad 47:27.

In these verses, wajh and dubur are opposite sides of the body: the front and the back.

Here the lexical meanings of wajh and dubur concern the two sides of the body. The ethical significance of turning toward or away, such as acceptance or denial, comes from the sentence’s context. Honesty, responsibility, and denial are not inherent meanings of these two words.

Their lexical distinction is:

Wajh is the facing or front side; dubur is the back.

The face as an expression of a person’s condition

The Qur’an describes the face not only as a bodily part, but also as the place where a person’s condition becomes visible.

It says:

يَوْمَ تَبْيَضُّ وُجُوهٌ وَتَسْوَدُّ وُجُوهٌ

Yawma tabyaḍḍu wujūhun wa-taswaddu wujūhun.

“On the day when some faces become white and other faces become black.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:106

And:

وُجُوهٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ نَاضِرَةٌ

Wujūhun yawmaʾidhin nāḍiratun.

“Faces on that day will be radiant.”

Al-Qiyāmah 75:22

In contrast:

وَوُجُوهٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ بَاسِرَةٌ

Wa-wujūhun yawmaʾidhin bāsiratun.

“And faces on that day will be grim.”

Al-Qiyāmah 75:24

Also:

وُجُوهٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ خَاشِعَةٌ

Wujūhun yawmaʾidhin khāshiʿatun.

“Faces on that day will be humiliated and downcast.”

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:2

These uses present the face as the place where a person’s condition becomes apparent. Joy, fear, humiliation, distress, and a person’s fate are manifested in their wajh.

The face here expresses the person’s condition. This role helps explain figurative uses of the word, although “soul,” “inner self,” and “entire being” are not fixed lexical meanings of wajh.

Wajh al-nahār: the first part of something

The uses of wajh extend beyond the human body.

Āl ʿImrān states:

آمِنُوا بِالَّذِي أُنْزِلَ عَلَى الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَجْهَ النَّهَارِ وَاكْفُرُوا آخِرَهُ

Āminū bi-alladhī unzila ʿalā alladhīna āmanū wajha al-nahāri wa-kfurū ākhirahu.

“Believe in what was sent down to those who believe at the beginning of the day, and disbelieve at its end.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:72

Wajha al-nahāri, “the face of the day,” stands opposite ākhirahu, “its end.”

The face of the day is therefore its first part or beginning.

The application is clear: a day has no physical face, but it has a first part. The idea expressed by “face” in a person extends, in relation to time, to the beginning or foremost part.

Arabic lexical sources also record wajh al-nahār for the beginning of the day. In this verse, the contrast with ākhirahu makes that meaning clear.

ʿAlā wajhihā: the proper form or manner of something

Al-Māʾidah states:

ذَٰلِكَ أَدْنَىٰ أَنْ يَأْتُوا بِالشَّهَادَةِ عَلَىٰ وَجْهِهَا

Dhālika adnā an yaʾtū bi-al-shahādati ʿalā wajhihā.

“That is more likely to make them give testimony in its proper form.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:108

Wajh here does not refer to a bodily part. The subject is the proper manner or form of giving testimony.

This use illustrates another branch of the same idea: an affair can have a wajh, a form or aspect through which it is presented and understood.

Thus ʿalā wajhihā can be understood as “in its proper form” or “in its proper manner.”

From face to direction: wajjahtu wajhī

The relationship between face and direction is clear in the Qur’an.

Abraham says:

إِنِّي وَجَّهْتُ وَجْهِيَ لِلَّذِي فَطَرَ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ حَنِيفًا وَمَا أَنَا مِنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ

Innī wajjahtu wajhiya li-alladhī faṭara al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍa ḥanīfan wa-mā anā mina al-mushrikīna.

“I have turned my face toward the One who originated the heavens and the earth, as a ḥanīf, and I am not among those who associate others with Him.”

Al-Anʿām 6:79

A verb and a noun from the same root occur together:

وَجَّهْتُ وَجْهِي

Wajjahtu wajhī.

Turning the wajh is more than a movement of the face. The continuation, with ḥanīfan and the rejection of associating others with The God, shows that the subject is the person’s orientation.

The face turns in a direction, and that movement also represents the course the person adopts.

The same connection appears in the verb tawajjaha:

وَلَمَّا تَوَجَّهَ تِلْقَاءَ مَدْيَنَ قَالَ عَسَىٰ رَبِّي أَنْ يَهْدِيَنِي سَوَاءَ السَّبِيلِ

Wa-lammā tawajjaha tilqāʾa Madyana qāla ʿasā rabbī an yahdiyanī sawāʾa al-sabīli.

“When he turned toward Midian, he said, ‘Perhaps my Lord will guide me to the right way.’”

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:22

Moses does not merely turn his face toward Midian; he travels in that direction.

Tawajjaha can therefore express turning toward something, setting out for it, and moving toward that destination.

Al-Naḥl also states:

أَيْنَمَا يُوَجِّهْهُ لَا يَأْتِ بِخَيْرٍ

Aynamā yuwajjihhu lā yaʾti bi-khayrin.

“Wherever he sends him, he brings no good.”

Al-Naḥl 16:76

Here yuwajjihhu means sending him in a direction or directing him toward a destination.

These uses display the following semantic relationship, without necessarily establishing the historical order in which the meanings developed:

Face → turning toward → taking a direction → moving or sending toward a destination.

Wijhah: the direction of turning

The form wijhah occurs once in the Qur’an:

وَلِكُلٍّ وِجْهَةٌ هُوَ مُوَلِّيهَا فَاسْتَبِقُوا الْخَيْرَاتِ

Wa-li-kullin wijhatun huwa muwallīhā fa-stabiqū al-khayrāti.

“For each there is a direction toward which he turns; so race one another in good deeds.”

Al-Baqarah 2:148

Wijhah is the side toward which one turns, or a direction.

Its connection with wajh is direct: the wijhah is the direction toward which a person turns their wajh.

The word itself does not specify that this direction must always be geographical, religious, or ethical. The context determines what kind of direction is meant.

Wijhah expresses direction and destination. Broader rulings and implications concerning the qiblah or religious law depend on the context and the relevant passages as a whole, not on this word’s structure alone.

Aslama wajhahu: surrendering one’s whole orientation

Several Qur’anic passages use the expression aslama wajhahu.

Al-Baqarah states:

بَلَىٰ مَنْ أَسْلَمَ وَجْهَهُ لِلَّهِ وَهُوَ مُحْسِنٌ فَلَهُ أَجْرُهُ عِنْدَ رَبِّهِ

Balā man aslama wajhahu li-Allāhi wa-huwa muḥsinun fa-lahu ajruhu ʿinda rabbihi.

“Yes indeed: whoever surrenders their face to The God while doing good shall have their reward with their Lord.”

Al-Baqarah 2:112

The same construction occurs in Al-Nisāʾ:

وَمَنْ أَحْسَنُ دِينًا مِمَّنْ أَسْلَمَ وَجْهَهُ لِلَّهِ وَهُوَ مُحْسِنٌ

Wa-man aḥsanu dīnan mimman aslama wajhahu li-Allāhi wa-huwa muḥsinun.

“Who is better in religion than one who surrenders their face to The God while doing good?”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:125

And in Luqmān:

وَمَنْ يُسْلِمْ وَجْهَهُ إِلَى اللَّهِ وَهُوَ مُحْسِنٌ فَقَدِ اسْتَمْسَكَ بِالْعُرْوَةِ الْوُثْقَىٰ

Wa-man yuslim wajhahu ilā Allāhi wa-huwa muḥsinun fa-qadi istamsaka bi-al-ʿurwati al-wuthqā.

“Whoever surrenders their face to The God while doing good has grasped the firmest handhold.”

Luqmān 31:22

Reading “surrendered his face” merely as a reference to the physical face narrows the sentence’s meaning. In this construction, wajh conveys the person’s orientation: the person turns toward The God and entrusts themselves to Him.

This usage retains a connection with the physical image. Someone facing another places their face toward them; language uses that image to express surrender and the person’s whole orientation.

In this construction, wajh represents personal orientation. Its reference to the person as a whole is understood from the sentence; “the entirety of a person’s inner and outer being” is not a separate lexical definition of wajh.

Aqim wajhaka li-al-dīn

Al-Rūm states:

فَأَقِمْ وَجْهَكَ لِلدِّينِ حَنِيفًا

Fa-aqim wajhaka li-al-dīni ḥanīfan.

“So set your face firmly toward the religion, as a ḥanīf.”

Al-Rūm 30:30

This command connects turning toward the religion with firmness and constancy.

And in Yūnus:

وَأَنْ أَقِمْ وَجْهَكَ لِلدِّينِ حَنِيفًا

Wa-an aqim wajhaka li-al-dīni ḥanīfan.

Yūnus 10:105

The subject again is more than keeping a facial position unchanged. The wajh is directed toward the religion and held firm.

Establishing the wajh can therefore be understood as maintaining a person’s orientation firmly.

The Qur’anic forms wajjahtu, yuwajjihhu, tawajjaha, and wijhah support this reading: wajh in this construction expresses personal direction, rather than serving as an independent philosophical term for “inner being.”

Yakhlu lakum wajhu abīkum: wajh and a person’s attention

Joseph’s brothers say:

اقْتُلُوا يُوسُفَ أَوِ اطْرَحُوهُ أَرْضًا يَخْلُ لَكُمْ وَجْهُ أَبِيكُمْ

Uqtulū Yūsufa awi iṭraḥūhu arḍan yakhlu lakum wajhu abīkum.

“Kill Joseph, or cast him away into some land, so that your father’s face may be free for you.”

Yūsuf 12:9

Their concern is not Jacob’s physical face. The preceding verse speaks of his greater love for Joseph and his brother.

Their father’s wajh is therefore connected with his attention and favor: they want him to turn exclusively toward them.

This is a particularly clear extension of wajh from the face to a person’s turning toward another.

The sentence concerns the father’s attention and favor becoming theirs alone. Wajh conveys that relationship through the image of turning toward someone.

Wajīh: a person of standing

The Qur’an uses wajīh twice.

Of Jesus, it says:

وَجِيهًا فِي الدُّنْيَا وَالْآخِرَةِ وَمِنَ الْمُقَرَّبِينَ

Wajīhan fī al-dunyā wa-al-ākhirati wa-mina al-muqarrabīna.

“Distinguished in this world and the Hereafter, and among those brought near.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:45

And of Moses:

وَكَانَ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ وَجِيهًا

Wa-kāna ʿinda Allāhi wajīhan.

“He was distinguished with The God.”

Al-Aḥzāb 33:69

Wajīh in these verses does not concern facial beauty. Its clear meaning is standing and esteem.

Arabic lexical sources place wajīh in the wajh family. Its Qur’anic use denotes distinction and esteem; determining the precise historical route from “face” to “standing” requires evidence beyond these two verses.

Wajīh: possessing standing and esteem.

Wajh Allāh in different Qur’anic constructions

Wajh occurs in several different constructions in relation to The God.

These differences prevent us from rendering every instance uniformly as a “physical face,” “essence,” “pleasure,” “reward,” or “presence.”

Seeking the wajh

Al-Baqarah states:

وَمَا تُنْفِقُونَ إِلَّا ابْتِغَاءَ وَجْهِ اللَّهِ

Wa-mā tunfiqūna illā ibtighāʾa wajhi Allāhi.

“You spend only in seeking the wajh of The God.”

Al-Baqarah 2:272

In Al-Raʿd:

وَالَّذِينَ صَبَرُوا ابْتِغَاءَ وَجْهِ رَبِّهِمْ

Wa-alladhīna ṣabarū ibtighāʾa wajhi rabbihim.

“Those who remained patient, seeking the wajh of their Lord.”

Al-Raʿd 13:22

Al-Anʿām 6:52 and Al-Kahf 18:28 also say:

يُرِيدُونَ وَجْهَهُ

Yurīdūna wajhahu.

“They desire His wajh.”

And in Al-Insān:

إِنَّمَا نُطْعِمُكُمْ لِوَجْهِ اللَّهِ لَا نُرِيدُ مِنْكُمْ جَزَاءً وَلَا شُكُورًا

Innamā nuṭʿimukum li-wajhi Allāhi lā nurīdu minkum jazāʾan wa-lā shukūran.

“We feed you only for the wajh of The God; we desire neither reward nor thanks from you.”

Al-Insān 76:9

This verse helps clarify the construction. Li-wajhi Allāh stands in contrast with seeking reward and gratitude from people.

The act is directed toward The God; another human being is not its ultimate object.

In this group of verses, wajh is connected with the act’s aim and intended object. “Seeking the pleasure of The God” is one interpretive explanation of the construction. Retaining wajh in a study translation also allows comparison with its other uses.

Kullu shayʾin hālikun illā wajhahu

Al-Qaṣaṣ states:

كُلُّ شَيْءٍ هَالِكٌ إِلَّا وَجْهَهُ ۚ لَهُ الْحُكْمُ وَإِلَيْهِ تُرْجَعُونَ

Kullu shayʾin hālikun illā wajhahu; lahu al-ḥukmu wa-ilayhi turjaʿūna.

“Everything is perishing except His wajh; judgment belongs to Him, and to Him you will be returned.”

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:88

The sentence contrasts:

كُلُّ شَيْءٍ هَالِكٌ

Kullu shayʾin hālikun.

With:

إِلَّا وَجْهَهُ

Illā wajhahu.

“Everything is perishing” and “except His wajh.”

Restricting wajh here to a bodily organ independent of its possessor does not fit the course of the sentence. What remains in contrast to the perishing of everything is “His wajh.”

Interpreting wajh as “He Himself” or “His essence” is a recognized explanation of this verse. That explanation should be distinguished from the verse’s wording: the Qur’an says wajh, and a study translation can preserve this image while presenting its interpretation separately.

In this reading, wajh refers to The God Himself, while the verbal image of the face and of facing remains in the text.

Wa-yabqā wajhu rabbika

Al-Raḥmān presents the same contrast more explicitly:

كُلُّ مَنْ عَلَيْهَا فَانٍ ۝ وَيَبْقَىٰ وَجْهُ رَبِّكَ ذُو الْجَلَالِ وَالْإِكْرَامِ

Kullu man ʿalayhā fānin. Wa-yabqā wajhu rabbika dhū al-jalāli wa-al-ikrāmi.

“Everyone upon it is passing away, and the wajh of your Lord, possessed of majesty and honor, remains.”

Al-Raḥmān 55:26–27

Here too, passing away is contrasted with the permanence of wajhu rabbika, “the wajh of your Lord.”

The grammar is also noteworthy. Wajhu is nominative, as is dhū al-jalāli wa-al-ikrām, with nominative dhū. Rabbika, by contrast, is genitive because of the possessive construction.

In the commonly recited reading, dhū al-jalāli wa-al-ikrām qualifies wajh and agrees with it in the nominative case.

This grammatical relationship matters for interpretation. Substituting “pleasure” or “reward” for wajh requires an independent explanation; it does not follow simply from the word’s lexical meaning.

The grammatical construction alone does not establish whether the referent is bodily. Other uses, such as ibtighāʾa wajhi Allāh and yurīdūna wajhahu, show that understanding wajh depends on the context of each expression.

Fa-thamma wajhu Allāh: human direction and the wajh of The God

Al-Baqarah 2:115 is a central passage for this root:

وَلِلَّهِ الْمَشْرِقُ وَالْمَغْرِبُ ۚ فَأَيْنَمَا تُوَلُّوا فَثَمَّ وَجْهُ اللَّهِ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ وَاسِعٌ عَلِيمٌ

Wa-li-Allāhi al-mashriqu wa-al-maghribu; fa-aynamā tuwallū fa-thamma wajhu Allāhi; inna Allāha wāsiʿun ʿalīmun.

“To The God belong the east and the west; wherever you turn, there is the wajh of The God. The God is all-encompassing and knowing.”

Al-Baqarah 2:115

The whole verse concerns direction and turning:

The east and the west;
tuwallū, “you turn”;
aynamā, “wherever”;
and wajhu Allāh.

This expression does not by itself assign The God a particular location.

فَأَيْنَمَا تُوَلُّوا

Fa-aynamā tuwallū.

Means “wherever you turn.”

If wajh Allāh were a particular geographical coordinate, aynamā would not have this unrestricted scope.

Elsewhere in the same surah, when a direction for human action is specified, the wording is different:

فَوَلِّ وَجْهَكَ شَطْرَ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ

Fa-walli wajhaka shaṭra al-masjidi al-ḥarāmi.

“Turn your face toward the Sacred Mosque.”

Al-Baqarah 2:144

Here the human face is turned toward a particular direction.

Verse 148 also states:

وَلِكُلٍّ وِجْهَةٌ هُوَ مُوَلِّيهَا

Wa-li-kullin wijhatun huwa muwallīhā.

“For each there is a direction toward which he turns.”

And in 2:177:

لَيْسَ الْبِرَّ أَنْ تُوَلُّوا وُجُوهَكُمْ قِبَلَ الْمَشْرِقِ وَالْمَغْرِبِ

Laysa al-birra an tuwallū wujūhakum qibala al-mashriqi wa-al-maghribi.

“Righteousness does not consist in turning your faces toward the east and the west.”

These passages together yield a clear distinction:

A person’s face may be turned toward a specified direction, but Al-Baqarah 2:115 does not confine the wajh of The God to that geographical direction.

The distinction can be expressed as follows:

The qiblah specifies the direction in which a person turns; specifying the qiblah does not mean assigning a location to The God.

This distinction emerges from reading the expressions of direction and turning together across these verses.

The ethical, social, and religious implications of the qiblah depend on the passages concerning the House, pilgrimage, and the qiblah as a whole. Studying W J H clarifies only part of the linguistic background to that discussion.

Face, being before someone, and presence: a semantic comparison

In other Semitic languages, words for the face and the front can also refer to being before someone or to presence. Hebrew pānīm and Akkadian pānu, for example, have such a range.

These words are not direct cognates of Arabic W J H. Comparing them illustrates a similar semantic extension; it does not establish a shared derivation with wajh.

The comparisons show that a semantic relationship between face and presence is possible; they do not by themselves establish the earliest meaning of Arabic wajh.

Within the Qur’an, “presence” may sometimes help explain a context, such as fa-thamma wajhu Allāh or yakhlu lakum wajhu abīkum, but should not become the root’s fixed equivalent.

In the uses examined here, facing, turning toward, and direction connect the branches more clearly. Explanations involving presence or being before someone must be assessed in the context of the particular verse.

Wajh in relation to yad and ʿayn

The Qur’an uses yad, “hand,” ʿayn, “eye,” and wajh, “face,” in relation to The God. Interpretive explanations may associate these words with power, care, or presence in certain contexts, but each word’s range extends beyond that explanation.

For example, explaining yad through power or ʿayn through care in one context does not establish that these are their meanings in every verse. The same limitation applies to explaining wajh through presence or intention.

These uses do not yield a fixed system of one-to-one symbolic equivalents.

Each word must first be examined within its own linguistic family and then within the context of the verse.

The wajh family is connected through the face, facing, direction, and turning toward. Understanding wajh Allāh likewise depends on these linguistic uses and the context of each verse, rather than on a symbolic division of functions among bodily parts.

Translation proposals for Forqan

One fixed rendering cannot serve every use of wajh.

For:

فَاغْسِلُوا وُجُوهَكُمْ

Fa-ghsilū wujūhakum.

“Wash your faces” is natural.

For:

وَجْهَ النَّهَارِ

Wajha al-nahāri.

“The beginning of the day” conveys the meaning more effectively.

For:

عَلَىٰ وَجْهِهَا

ʿAlā wajhihā.

“In its proper form” or “in its proper manner” is suitable.

For:

وَجَّهْتُ وَجْهِيَ

Wajjahtu wajhiya.

Retaining “face” preserves the image, while the context should make clear that the expression concerns the person’s orientation.

For:

أَسْلَمَ وَجْهَهُ لِلَّهِ

Aslama wajhahu li-Allāhi.

“Surrendered his face to The God” can be retained, with an explanation of the person’s whole orientation. “Surrendered himself” conveys the general intent, but removes the Qur’anic image of wajh.

For:

وَلِكُلٍّ وِجْهَةٌ

Wa-li-kullin wijhatun.

“Direction” or “a direction of turning” is suitable.

For:

وَكَانَ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ وَجِيهًا

Wa-kāna ʿinda Allāhi wajīhan.

“He was distinguished with The God” conveys the meaning clearly.

In expressions of wajh Allāh, this study retains wajh, the Arabic word whose basic meaning is “face,” so that each verse can be explained in its own context.

“Face,” “essence,” “pleasure,” “presence,” and “reward” may each help explain an aspect of a particular use, but none is a fixed equivalent for every occurrence of wajh Allāh.

Conclusion

W J H has a concrete, basic meaning: the face or front of a person or thing.

The Qur’anic contrast between wajh and dubur clarifies this core: wajh is the front and dubur the back. The ethical significance of turning toward or away comes from the context, not from the bodily parts themselves.

The face is also where a person’s condition becomes visible. The Qur’an speaks of radiant, grim, white, black, and humiliated, downcast faces. This capacity helps explain figurative extensions, without making “soul” or “inner self” lexical definitions of wajh.

From the idea of the foremost part, wajh al-nahār denotes the beginning of the day, while ʿalā wajhihā expresses the form or manner of an affair.

Turning the face also connects with direction: wajjaha directs someone toward a place, tawajjaha expresses turning and moving toward a destination, and wijhah is the direction toward which one turns.

In expressions such as aslama wajhahu li-Allāh and aqim wajhaka li-al-dīn, wajh represents personal orientation. In Joseph’s story, the father’s wajh is connected with his attention and favor. Wajīh describes a person of standing and distinction.

Wajh Allāh must be examined verse by verse within these relationships. In ibtighāʾa wajhi Allāh and yurīdūna wajhahu, it concerns the aim and intended object of an act. In kullu shayʾin hālikun illā wajhahu and wa-yabqā wajhu rabbika, it is connected with the permanence of The God. In fa-aynamā tuwallū fa-thamma wajhu Allāh, it is not restricted to one geographical direction.

A concise definition can be stated as follows:

W J H: the face or front of a person or thing; connected uses include the foremost part, turning toward, taking a direction, a destination, the form or manner of an affair, and, in certain constructions, personal orientation. Wajh Allāh should likewise be understood through these relationships and each verse’s context, neither reduced to a physical face or geographical coordinate nor replaced without supporting grounds by one fixed equivalent such as essence, pleasure, presence, or reward.
