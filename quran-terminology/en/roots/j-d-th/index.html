The Qur’anic Root J D TH

Jadath and ajdāth: graves, the resting places of the dead, and coming forth at the Resurrection

Qur’anic and lexical forms

(al-ajdāthi)

Arabic «جَدَث» (jadath) means a grave, the place where a dead person is buried. Its familiar plural is «أَجْدَاث» (ajdāth). Only the plural occurs in the Qur’an, and all three occurrences concern people coming forth from graves at the Resurrection.

Arabic qabr likewise names this place, and dictionaries generally explain jadath through qabr. Their Qur’anic settings, however, are not identical. Qabr and its family concern burial, the dead’s resting places, and resurrection in various passages; ajdāth occurs in three scenes of people coming forth at the Resurrection.

This distribution matters, but it does not establish movement, resurrection, or emergence as the word’s original meaning. It means graves; emerging, haste, and dispersal come from the surrounding clauses.

The study should therefore first examine the familiar Arabic meaning, then compare its Qur’anic use with neighboring terms, particularly qabr.

1. Forms and basic meaning

The Qur’an uses J D TH in only one nominal form: «الْأَجْدَاث» (al-ajdāth).

It is the broken plural of jadath on the pattern afʿāl. In all three verses, it follows the preposition min, in «مِنَ الْأَجْدَاثِ» (mina al-ajdāthi).

Quranic Arabic Corpus: three plural noun occurrences

No verb, verbal noun, or other adjective from this root occurs in the Qur’an.

Early Arabic sources record the following forms:

Jadath means grave; ajdāth is its plural; ajduth is recorded as another plural in some dictionaries; and «اجْتَدَثَ» (ijtadatha) means making or preparing a grave, including for oneself.

«مُجْتَدِث» (mujtadith) is also recorded for someone who digs or prepares a grave.

These are recorded Arabic forms, but only ajdāth occurs in the Qur’an.

Semantic core

Al-Khalīl’s Kitāb al-ʿAyn explains the family concisely:

«الْأَجْدَاثُ: الْقُبُورُ، وَاحِدُهَا جَدَثٌ.»

al-ajdāthu al-qubūru wāḥiduhā jadathun

Meaning: “Ajdāth are graves; their singular is jadath.”

Ibn Fāris likewise writes in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

«الْجِيمُ وَالدَّالُ وَالثَّاءُ كَلِمَةٌ وَاحِدَةٌ: الْجَدَثُ الْقَبْرُ، وَجَمْعُهُ أَجْدَاثٌ.»

al-jīmu wa-al-dālu wa-al-thāʾu kalimatun wāḥidatun al-jadathu al-qabru wa-jamʿuhu ajdāthun

Meaning: “Jīm, dāl, and thāʾ have one word: jadath is the grave, and its plural is ajdāth.”

Al-ʿAyn and Maqāyīs: jadath as grave

This establishes the familiar meaning of the jadath/ajdāth branch. Some dictionaries also record a sound-related use of jadathah; Ibn Fāris’s statement should therefore not be treated as denying every other lexical use across all sources.

Jadath does not name the dead person, dying, or the process of resurrection. It names the place where the dead person is buried.

The basic, defensible meaning in Qur’anic usage is a grave or burial place for the dead. Brief root meaning: A grave; the place where a dead person is buried.

2. Uses in early Arabic

The grave as the place where the dead are laid

A grave is the place where a dead person’s body is laid.

Arabic records both jadath and qabr for such a place.

Al-Zamakhsharī’s Asās al-Balāghah has:

«غَيَّبُوهُ فِي الْجَدَثِ»

ghayyabūhu fī al-jadathi

Meaning: “They concealed him in the grave.”

Asās al-Balāghah: concealment in a jadath

This shows the familiar use of jadath for a place where a dead body is hidden from view.

Concealment is also expressed by the verb ghayyabūhu. The noun’s entire meaning should therefore not be turned into the act of hiding.

Nor does jadath determine a grave’s precise shape. Graves may differ in depth, construction, or preparation.

The word alone does not specify how its walls are built, whether stones cover it, or how high the earth is mounded above it.

Such features require a separate description of the particular grave.

Preparing a grave

Dictionaries record ijtadatha for preparing or making a grave, including for oneself.

The verb belongs to Form VIII.

Here, the noun jadath provides the basis for a verb concerning preparation of a burial place.

Its existence in Arabic does not mean that the Qur’an uses the verb.

In the three Qur’anic passages, ajdāth is a noun, and no verb from this root occurs in the sentence.

Emerging is expressed by yakhrujūna, and movement by yansilūna, rather than a J D TH verb.

The name of the grave should therefore be distinguished from coming out of it.

Jadaf: another form meaning grave

Lexical sources also record jadaf, with fāʾ, for a grave.

Al-Jawharī and Ibn Manẓūr explain the difference through substitution of fāʾ for thāʾ. Some sources attribute jadath to the people of Tihāmah and jadaf to those of Najd.

These accounts document two neighboring Arabic forms for the grave.

Their precise relationship is disputed: some lexicographers treat jadaf as an altered form of jadath, and discuss whether it has a plural of its own.

For example, Lisān al-ʿArab under jadath emphasizes ajdāth and denies ajdāf; under jadaf, however, the same book records ajdāf and reports some lexicographers’ objection to it. Tāj al-ʿArūs likewise reflects differing accounts across the two entries. One passage’s position should therefore not be imposed uniformly on these dictionaries as a whole.

Lisān and Tāj under jadaf: ajdāf and disagreement about it

The disagreement does not establish that both forms had identical status in every dialect and period.

For Qur’anic study, the relevant point is that its form is ajdāth, with thāʾ.

Jadaf or other meanings of J D F should not be transferred to Qur’anic jadath without evidence.

Jadathah: sound-related uses

Al-Muḥīṭ and Tāj al-ʿArūs record jadathah for the sound of a hoof, a soft foot such as a camel’s, and chewing meat. This should not be generalized to the footfall of every animal.

Al-Muḥīṭ and Tāj: mujtadith and sound-related jadathah

Its written form belongs with J D TH, but its semantic field differs from the grave.

Connecting hoofbeats or chewing with the grave would require independent historical evidence.

Shared letters alone do not prove that one form developed from another.

Thus, the recorded animal-foot sound does not make Qur’anic ajdāth mean the hurried sound of people coming forth.

That would merge the word’s meaning with the scene’s imagery.

The Qur’an expresses haste and movement with separate verbs and descriptions. Ajdāth still means graves.

An older poetic witness and the place-name question

A verse attributed to Al-Mutanakhkhil al-Hudhalī says:

«عَرَفْتُ بِأَجْدُثٍ فَنِعَافِ عِرْقٍ
عَلَامَاتٍ كَتَحْبِيرِ النِّمَاطِ»

ʿaraftu bi-ajduthin fa-niʿāfi ʿirqin / ʿalāmātin ka-taḥbīri al-nimāṭi

Meaning: “At Ajduth and Niʿāf ʿIrq I recognized traces like the embroidered patterns of cloth.”

Ajduth appears alongside another place-name and is identified in lexical sources as a location.

Al-Jawharī quotes it when discussing ajduth, while Ibn Sīdah examines the name’s form and its possible relationship with a plural of jadath.

The relevant point is its function as a geographical name in this line.

Its appearance in older poetry does not by itself prove that the poet used it as an ordinary noun meaning graves.

Ajduf, with fāʾ, is also transmitted for the name. Tāj al-ʿArūs additionally reports Al-Sukkarī’s reading with ḥāʾ, aḥduth. This concerns the place-name and does not alter Qur’anic ajdāth.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Muḥkam, Lisān, and Tāj: Al-Mutanakhkhil’s witness and the place-name

The witness helps document linguistic forms and place-names, but it is not independent, conclusive evidence for the ordinary meaning grave in pre-Islamic poetry.

The witnesses examined here did not establish an independent poetic example with secure pre-Qur’anic attribution and dating as well as an unambiguous ordinary use meaning grave. This study therefore rests on reliable lexical sources and the Qur’anic usage.

3. Ajdāth in the Resurrection scene of Yā Sīn

Yā Sīn 36:51

وَنُفِخَ فِي الصُّورِ فَإِذَا هُم مِّنَ الْأَجْدَاثِ إِلَىٰ رَبِّهِمْ يَنسِلُونَ

wa-nufikha fī al-ṣūri fa-idhā hum mina al-ajdāthi ilā rabbihim yansilūna

“And the trumpet will be blown, and suddenly they will hurry from the graves to their Lord.”

The verse describes the trumpet’s blowing and people subsequently coming forth from graves.

Al-ajdāthi follows the preposition min.

Mina al-ajdāthi specifies the starting point: from the graves.

Ilā rabbihim specifies the direction: toward their Lord.

Yansilūna expresses their movement and haste.

Study of N S L

These three components should be recognized separately.

Ajdāth itself does not mean haste, nor does min alone express resurrection. The scene emerges from the trumpet’s blowing, departure from graves, and movement toward their Lord together.

The grave’s name and the act of emerging

Ajdāth here names the burial place; this description does not assert a regularly derived morphological place-noun pattern. The Qur’an does not say the word originally means rising or returning to life.

Resurrection and emergence are understood from the sentence’s context.

Nor should ilā rabbihim be turned without grounds into travel along a geographical route toward a location occupied by The God.

The Qur’an describes turning and returning to The God in various settings. How this movement occurs at the Resurrection requires a separate study of verses concerning presence, gathering, and accounting.

The grave’s meaning is clear; the nature of the hereafter’s event is not supplied by jadath alone.

Marqadinā: the connection with the next verse

قَالُوا يَا وَيْلَنَا مَن بَعَثَنَا مِن مَّرْقَدِنَا ۜ هَٰذَا مَا وَعَدَ الرَّحْمَٰنُ وَصَدَقَ الْمُرْسَلُونَ

qālū yā waylanā man baʿathanā min marqadinā hādhā mā waʿada al-raḥmānu wa-ṣadaqa al-mursalūna

“They will say: Woe to us! Who has raised us from our resting place? This is what the Entirely Merciful promised, and the messengers spoke the truth.” (Yā Sīn 36:52)

Those who have come forth refer to marqadinā, our resting place.

Marqad belongs to R Q D and concerns sleeping and resting.

The sequence of ajdāth and marqad in Yā Sīn 36:51 and Yā Sīn 36:52 matters. The first names the graves from which they emerge; the second presents the previous condition as a resting place in the raised people’s speech.

Those words alone do not establish that every dead person experiences awareness and time between death and resurrection exactly like a sleeping person.

Marqad concerns resting, and the speakers’ words belong to the Resurrection scene. Al-Ṭabarī also transmits an explanation involving rest between the two trumpet blasts; that is a contextual interpretation rather than part of jadath’s lexical definition.

Al-Ṭabarī: ajdāth, marqad, and the interval between trumpet blasts

The nature of death, awareness afterward, and the manner of resurrection require further independent evidence.

Jadath itself specifies the burial place alone.

Study of R Q D

4. Graves, dispersal, and haste in the other two passages

Al-Qamar 54:7

خُشَّعًا أَبْصَارُهُمْ يَخْرُجُونَ مِنَ الْأَجْدَاثِ كَأَنَّهُمْ جَرَادٌ مُّنتَشِرٌ

khushshaʿan abṣāruhum yakhrujūna mina al-ajdāthi ka-annahum jarādun muntashirun

“they will emerge from the graves with downcast eyes, as though they were scattered locusts,”

Al-ajdāth again refers to graves.

Here, instead of yansilūna, the expression is yakhrujūna mina al-ajdāthi.

Yakhrujūna belongs to KH R J and expresses coming forth.

The verse also describes the eyes and compares those emerging to scattered locusts.

The comparison suggests a great multitude and dispersed movement.

Those features do not belong to ajdāth itself.

Jarād names locusts, and muntashir expresses dispersal. The image describes the people coming forth from graves.

The next verse, Al-Qamar 54:8, describes movement toward the caller.

Together, the verses depict emergence, dispersal, and response to a summons.

Jadath here still means a grave, rather than a dispersed company, gathering place, or exit route.

Does the locust comparison change the meaning of grave?

The Qur’an can use familiar natural phenomena to illustrate features of a scene.

Scattered locusts can suggest great numbers and widespread movement.

Here, the comparison concerns the people coming forth, rather than the graves themselves.

The sentence does not describe graves scattered like locusts; it describes those emerging as resembling scattered locusts.

That grammatical distinction matters.

Nor should the comparison be used to derive exact numbers, a precise physical movement, or the details of their dispersal.

The image is clear, but details beyond what is stated should not be declared certain.

Al-Maʿārij 70:43

يَوْمَ يَخْرُجُونَ مِنَ الْأَجْدَاثِ سِرَاعًا كَأَنَّهُمْ إِلَىٰ نُصُبٍ يُوفِضُونَ

yawma yakhrujūna mina al-ajdāthi sirāʿan ka-annahum ilā nuṣubin yūfiḍūna

“the day they emerge swiftly from the graves, as though rushing toward an erected marker,”

Its opening resembles Al-Qamar 54:7.

Both passages have:

«يَخْرُجُونَ مِنَ الْأَجْدَاثِ»

yakhrujūna mina al-ajdāthi

Meaning: “They come forth from the graves.”

Their continuations differ.

Al-Qamar 54:7 compares those emerging to scattered locusts; Al-Maʿārij 70:43 compares their haste to people rushing toward an erected marker. Al-Ṭabarī transmits both a marker and a stone worshipped as interpretations of nuṣub. Forqan currently has an erected marker; this interpretive difference does not alter ajdāth’s meaning.

Al-Ṭabarī: graves, haste, and interpretations of nuṣub

Sirāʿan explicitly expresses haste, and yūfiḍūna likewise concerns swift movement.

Haste is therefore stated with words of its own.

Defining ajdāth as a place of haste or running would transfer its accompanying verbs and descriptions into the noun for graves.

Here too, ajdāth names the place from which people emerge.

Why do all three passages involve emergence and movement?

All three Qur’anic occurrences share a setting: people emerging or moving at the Resurrection.

Yā Sīn 36:51 describes the trumpet’s blowing and movement toward their Lord.

Al-Qamar 54:7 compares coming forth from graves with dispersed locusts.

Al-Maʿārij 70:43 describes the emerging people’s haste and movement toward an erected marker.

This consistency of usage is clear.

It does not establish an absolute linguistic rule that jadath is exclusively a grave from which the dead rise while qabr merely names a burial place.

Qabr also occurs in Qur’anic settings of resurrection and the dead’s emergence.

The difference in usage should be recognized without turning it into an absolute difference in nature lacking independent evidence.

5. Distinguishing J D TH from neighboring roots

J D TH (jadath) and Q B R (qabr)

Arabic qabr means the burial place of a dead person.

In familiar usage, jadath and qabr name the same kind of place.

Q B R has a broader Qur’anic range, including verbs as well as the noun and its plurals.

ʿAbasa 80:21 has thumma amātahu fa-aqbarahu, concerning death and provision of a burial place.

Al-Infiṭār 82:4 describes graves being overturned.

Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:9 mentions bringing forth what is in the graves.

Al-Ḥajj 22:7 also describes those in graves being raised.

Qabr can therefore occur in the context of renewed life and resurrection too.

It cannot be claimed that the Qur’an reserves qabr for worldly life and jadath for the hereafter.

The observable difference is narrower: ajdāth always accompanies emergence at the Resurrection in its three Qur’anic uses; qabr has a broader Qur’anic distribution.

The distribution differs, while the basic meaning of both is the dead person’s burial place.

Study of Q B R

J D TH (jadath) and L Ḥ D (laḥd)

Arabic laḥd concerns inclining and departing from a direction.

In burial terminology, it names a recess prepared on one side of the grave to receive the body.

That constructional term is more specific than jadath.

Jadath alone does not specify a particular cavity, wall, or position for the body.

Qur’anic ajdāth should therefore not definitively be translated as the lateral recesses of graves.

Qur’anic L Ḥ D also concerns deviation; its uses should not all be limited to grave construction.

Lexical sources: laḥd as a lateral grave recess

Study of L Ḥ D

J D TH (jadath) and R M S (rams)

Arabic rams concerns covering and concealing.

It is also recorded for a grave, where the dead person is covered and concealed.

The connection between covering and the name of the grave is more explicit here.

Lexical sources do not establish the same independent derivational explanation as certain for jadath.

Covering should not be transferred into J D TH merely because jadath and rams can be synonymous.

Lisān al-ʿArab: covering, rams, and the grave

J D TH (jadath) and J D F (jadaf)

Jadaf means grave in some Arabic dialects.

The forms are phonetically close, and some lexicographers explain them through substitution between fāʾ and thāʾ.

J D F has other uses, including ingratitude and belittling a favor.

Those meanings should not be transferred to jadath.

Overlap in one restricted meaning does not establish that every derivative and semantic branch is identical.

Lexical sources: tajdīf and ingratitude

J D TH (jadath) and Ḥ D TH (ḥadath)

Ḥ D TH concerns happening, coming into being, and newness.

Persian also knows ḥādithah and iḥdāth from this family.

Qur’anic ajdāth, however, belongs to J D TH rather than Ḥ D TH.

Written and phonetic resemblance should not turn graves into events, incidents, or newly appearing things.

These are distinct roots with different Qur’anic fields of meaning.

Study of Ḥ D TH

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Two natural Persian equivalents of jadath are gūr and qabr.

Gūr is a short, familiar Persian word; qabr is a widely used Arabic loanword in Persian.

Both can convey jadath’s basic meaning.

All three Qur’anic occurrences have plural al-ajdāth. Graves is therefore a defensible equivalent.

Forqan’s current Persian translations use gūrhā, graves, in all three verses.

In Yā Sīn 36:51:

“And the trumpet will be blown, and suddenly they will hurry from the graves to their Lord.”

In Al-Qamar 54:7:

“they will emerge from the graves with downcast eyes, as though they were scattered locusts,”

In Al-Maʿārij 70:43:

“the day they emerge swiftly from the graves, as though rushing toward an erected marker,”

The three renderings preserve the noun’s meaning and convey haste, emergence, and dispersal through the other expressions.

A brief root explanation can read:

“J D TH: a grave; the place where a dead person is buried.”

For the Qur’anic form’s contextual meaning:

“Al-ajdāth: graves, the burial places from which people are described as emerging at the Resurrection.”

The first part gives the lexical meaning; the second identifies its use in these Qur’anic passages.

Rapid emergence, a place of resurrection, or movement toward accounting should not replace jadath’s independent definition. Those features come from the three verses’ contexts.

The verses describe emergence from graves, but jadath itself does not explain how the dead return to life or how those not buried in the usual manner are raised.

Such questions require a separate study of death, raising, and resurrection.

Conclusion

Arabic J D TH has a relatively limited semantic family. Jadath means grave, and its familiar plural is ajdāth.

Lexical sources also record ijtadatha for preparing a grave and jadaf as an equivalent form; the Qur’an uses only al-ajdāth.

All three Qur’anic occurrences concern the Resurrection. Yā Sīn 36:51 describes movement from graves toward their Lord after the trumpet blast; Al-Qamar 54:7 compares the emerging people with scattered locusts; Al-Maʿārij 70:43 emphasizes their haste.

Ajdāth thus consistently appears in the image of the dead coming forth at the Resurrection.

Jadath does not itself mean resurrection, movement, haste, or dispersal. Those features come from accompanying verbs, descriptions, and comparisons.

Nor does the difference between jadath and qabr establish two entirely different kinds of resting place. The Qur’an also uses qabr in settings of raising and bringing forth the dead.

The direct, defensible Qur’anic meaning of J D TH is grave. Ajdāth are the graves from which people are described as emerging in three Resurrection scenes.

This preserves the familiar Arabic meaning and its particular Qur’anic usage without assigning unknown details of resurrection or other verbs’ meanings to the root itself.
