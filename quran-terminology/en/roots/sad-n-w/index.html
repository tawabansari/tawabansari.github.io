Sharing a root and closeness of origin: from palms growing from one base to brothers, relatives, and counterparts

1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

In Arabic, Ṣ N W concerns a shared root, proximity, and connection between things arising from one origin or closely related in origin and position.

Its best-known use concerns palms whose two or more trunks emerge from one common base. Each trunk is called صِنْو (ṣinw), and together they are صِنْوَان (ṣinwān).

The same word also describes brothers, close relatives, and people sharing an origin. More broadly, ṣinw can mean a counterpart or peer.

The root occurs in the Qur’an only in Al-Raʿd 13:4, in two forms:

  • صِنْوَانٌ (ṣinwānun): palms sharing a base and root; nominative.
  • صِنْوَانٍ (ṣinwānin): palms sharing a base and root; genitive after ghayr.

These are two occurrences of the same word, rather than two derivatives with separate meanings.

The family’s known Arabic forms include:

  • صِنْو (ṣinw): one of two or more trunks or trees growing from one origin; in human usage, a brother or close counterpart.
  • صِنْوَان (ṣinwān): two ṣinws; also the plural for several trunks or palms sharing a root.
  • أَصْنَاء (aṣnāʾ): another plural of ṣinw; counterparts or those sharing an origin.
  • صِنْوَة (ṣinwah): the feminine form of ṣinw; the offshoot sense is also recorded as صَنْوَة (ṣanwah), with fatḥah on ṣād.
  • صُنْو (ṣunw): another lexical form of ṣinw recorded in some sources.
  • صُنَيّ (ṣunayy): a diminutive of ṣinw; in a particular usage, a small hollow or water basin.
  • أَصْنَى (aṣnā): of palms, developed several branches or trunks from one base.

Of these forms, only ṣinwān occurs in the Qur’an.

2. The semantic core in early Arabic

Closeness and connection in origin

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris traces Ṣ N W to closeness between two things, whether in kinship or in distance and location.

He counts brotherhood and kinship among the family’s uses, then explains that its familiar underlying image is palms growing from a common base.

This analysis clarifies two features: a connection in origin and several distinguishable things arising from that origin.

For example, when two palm trunks grow from one base, each may have its own stem, leaves, and fruit clusters while remaining connected below.

Being a ṣinw therefore does not mean complete identity between two things.

If the trunks were literally one indistinguishable thing, speaking of two ṣinws would make no sense. The word concerns distinguishable things closely connected with one another.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: proximity in kinship or distance and the palm image

Palms growing from one base

Lisān al-ʿArab states that when two or more palms grow from one origin, each is the other’s ṣinw.

For example, offshoots may emerge around a palm’s base and develop into separate trunks over time.

Each trunk can grow upward and bear leaves and clusters while their lower parts belong to a common base.

This condition is familiar in date palms, which can produce offshoots around their bases.

Not every palm grows this way. Some grow separately, each with its own independent base and roots.

The specific meanings of ṣinwān and ghayr ṣinwān arise from this difference.

Spatial proximity alone is insufficient for palms to be ṣinwān: two palms may stand very close together but grow from independent bases.

Conversely, two trunks arising from one base remain ṣinwān even if their branches and crowns spread apart.

In the principal palm-related usage, a shared origin therefore matters more than the trunks’ apparent proximity.

One origin, different branches

Lexical explanations sometimes describe ṣinwān as palms whose “origin is one and branches are diverse.”

This expression clarifies an important feature of the word.

A common base does not mean that every part of the trees is identical. Trunks may differ in size, height, leaf number, yield, and growth condition.

This lexical description does not establish that all palms sharing a base necessarily produce differently flavored fruit.

Ṣinwān describes their growth relationship and shared root. Fruit quality is a separate subject introduced later in the verse.

3. Ṣinw in human kinship and resemblance

Brothers of one family origin

Arabic also uses صِنْو (ṣinw) for a brother.

In Kitāb al-ʿAyn, Al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad explains the word through two brothers sharing a father and mother.

Such a brother is also called a shaqīq.

The connection with palms sharing a root is clear: as two trunks arise from one base, two brothers arise from one family origin.

This analogy should not become a claim that the brothers are completely identical.

Brothers can differ in appearance, temperament, ability, belief, and conduct. Being a ṣinw expresses kinship, rather than identical personalities or destinies.

Maqāyīs, citing Al-Khalīl: a brother sharing father and mother

A paternal uncle as the father’s ṣinw

Arabic recognizes the expression عَمُّ الرَّجُلِ صِنْوُ أَبِيهِ (ʿammu al-rajuli ṣinwu abīhi).

It means that a person’s paternal uncle is their father’s brother, both arising from one family origin.

This usage shows that ṣinw is not restricted to brothers of the same age or two completely similar people.

The underlying connection is common origin.

The expression compares an uncle with the father; it does not make the uncle replace the father in every respect or automatically receive all the father’s rights and responsibilities.

Kinship and the legal rulings relating to it are separate questions.

Counterpart and peer

In broader usage, ṣinw may denote a counterpart or peer.

The common feature may then be similarity in attributes, standing, or intellectual and social origin, rather than actual kinship.

Two people close in knowledge or character, for example, may be described as counterparts in literary language.

The original sense of a shared root must nevertheless be distinguished from this broader resemblance.

Two similar things are not necessarily ṣinwān in the precise botanical sense. Nor does outward resemblance alone establish a common origin.

“Counterpart” or “peer” may therefore suit some contexts, but “sharing a root” is more precise for Al-Raʿd 13:4.

4. Other uses in early Arabic

Two neighboring wells

Abū Zayd al-Anṣārī also records a use of Ṣ N W for two wells near each other.

رَكِيَّتَانِ صِنْوَانِ (rakiyyatāni ṣinwāni) describes two wells so close together that little space separates them.

Some lexical explanations describe wells supplied by one underground source.

This use illustrates the family’s spatial-proximity branch.

A shared base and root stand out in palms; for two wells, location and sometimes a shared water source matter.

Not every ṣinw usage therefore necessarily denotes two objects sharing one biological or material root.

A small hollow or water basin

Lexical sources also record ṣinw or related forms for a small hollow or water basin in the ground or between elevations.

Ibn Fāris separates this from the principal sense of closeness and kinship, treating it as an irregular use within the family.

This caution matters: an artificial connection with palms and brotherhood should not be invented for every secondary use.

A historical word may have several semantic branches whose precise relationship remains unclear.

Al-Muḥkam, Al-Muḥīṭ, Tāj al-ʿArūs, and Asās al-Balāghah: wells, hollows, and lexical forms

A witness from Laylā al-Akhyaliyyah

The Umayyad-era poet Laylā al-Akhyaliyyah addresses Al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī:

أَنَابِغَ لَمْ تَنْبَغْ وَلَمْ تَكُ أَوَّلًا
وَكُنْتَ صُنَيًّا بَيْنَ صُدَّيْنِ مَجْهَلَا

a-nābigha lam tanbagh wa-lam taku awwalan / wa-kunta ṣunayyan bayna ṣuddayni majhalā

Approximate translation:

“Nābighah, you had not distinguished yourself or been foremost; you were a small, unknown water hollow between two heights.”

Lexical commentary relates صُنَيًّا (ṣunayyan) here to a small hollow or water basin.

To disparage her addressee, the poet compares him with a small, unknown pool.

The poem has variant readings. Some transmissions give وشيلا (washīlan) instead of صنيا (ṣunayyan), also concerning a small amount of water.

The witness must therefore be examined with its variant wording in mind.

Laylā al-Akhyaliyyah was an Umayyad-era poet, so this is not a pre-Islamic witness.

It nevertheless matters for understanding the family’s secondary uses in early Arabic.

The water-hollow sense should not be transferred to Qur’anic ṣinwān, since Al-Raʿd 13:4 explicitly concerns palms.

Ibn Qutaybah’s Al-Shiʿr wa-al-Shuʿarāʾ: the washīlan variant in Laylā’s reply to Al-Nābighah

5. Qur’anic usage: ṣinwānun wa-ghayru ṣinwānin

The root’s only Qur’anic verse is in Al-Raʿd:

وَفِي الْأَرْضِ قِطَعٌ مُتَجَاوِرَاتٌ وَجَنَّاتٌ مِنْ أَعْنَابٍ وَزَرْعٌ وَنَخِيلٌ صِنْوَانٌ وَغَيْرُ صِنْوَانٍ يُسْقَىٰ بِمَاءٍ وَاحِدٍ وَنُفَضِّلُ بَعْضَهَا عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ فِي الْأُكُلِ إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَآيَاتٍ لِقَوْمٍ يَعْقِلُونَ

wa-fī al-arḍi qiṭaʿun mutajāwirātun wa-jannātun min aʿnābin wa-zarʿun wa-nakhīlun ṣinwānun wa-ghayru ṣinwānin yusqā bi-māʾin wāḥidin wa-nufaḍḍilu baʿḍahā ʿalā baʿḍin fī al-ukuli inna fī dhālika la-āyātin li-qawmin yaʿqilūna

“And on earth are neighboring tracts, gardens of grapes, crops, and palms sharing a root and growing separately, watered with one water. We favor some over others in their edible yield. In this are signs for people who reason.”

Al-Raʿd 13:4

Ṣinwān in this verse

Ṣinwān is the plural of ṣinw here, denoting palms with several trunks from a common base.

Ghayr ṣinwān, by contrast, denotes palms without this shared base, each growing independently.

The verse thus places two different forms of palm growth alongside each other.

Ṣinwān palms may be connected below and grow from one origin, whereas ghayr ṣinwān palms have independent bases.

Ghayr ṣinwān does not mean the palms must stand far apart. Two independent palms may be very close together.

The central distinction is therefore whether their growth shares a base, rather than their apparent distance alone.

Why is ṣinwān plural here?

Ṣinwān is an interesting Arabic form: it can denote two ṣinws or function as the plural for several.

In Al-Raʿd 13:4, its first form is صِنْوَانٌ (ṣinwānun), and its second صِنْوَانٍ (ṣinwānin).

The tanwīn and changing final vowel show that both occurrences are plural here.

A dual form would have a different inflectional construction.

Nakhīlun ṣinwānun should therefore not be restricted to “two palms from one root.”

Two, three, or several trunks may arise from one common base.

The word itself does not specify a number in this verse.

Al-Ṭabarī: palms sharing a base and the plural/dual inflectional distinction

Wa-ghayru ṣinwānin

Ghayr here expresses difference from ṣinwān.

Ṣinwān describes palms sharing a base, while ghayr ṣinwān includes palms lacking that feature.

The Arabic construction contrasts two conditions.

It does not establish that ṣinwān palms must exceed ghayr ṣinwān palms in worth, quality, or yield.

The verse gives no fixed judgment of this kind about the two groups. It subsequently describes differences in produce and favoring some over others, but does not restrict that difference to sharing or not sharing a root.

6. Shared water and different produce

Yusqā bi-māʾin wāḥidin

The verse continues:

يُسْقَىٰ بِمَاءٍ وَاحِدٍ

yusqā bi-māʾin wāḥidin

“Watered with one water.”

Shared water is set alongside varied plants and produce.

Neighboring land, grape gardens, crops, and different palms may draw on one water source without producing identical yields.

This diversity is apparent in nature. Plants differ in structure, growth pattern, leaf shape, fruit characteristics, ripening time, and biological needs.

Even plants growing on one tract of land may produce different crops.

The verse’s statement must nevertheless be distinguished from modern scientific explanations.

The verse describes common water and differing produce. It does not itself state that every other factor, including soil, light, temperature, plant age, inheritance, and growth conditions, is exactly the same.

Conditions the verse does not state should therefore not be added to establish a scientific marvel.

Wa-nufaḍḍilu baʿḍahā ʿalā baʿḍin fī al-ukuli

It then states:

وَنُفَضِّلُ بَعْضَهَا عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ فِي الْأُكُلِ

wa-nufaḍḍilu baʿḍahā ʿalā baʿḍin fī al-ukuli

“And We favor some over others in their edible yield.”

Al-ukul here concerns edible produce and the food it provides.

The preference mentioned therefore concerns produce and nourishment.

Fruits may differ, for example, in sweetness, flavor, quality, quantity, and other characteristics relevant to consumption.

The verse does not say that every palm sharing a root is better than every independently rooted palm, or the reverse.

Nor should preference in edible yield become an absolute superiority of the entire plant or its value as a being.

A difference in one quality does not mean superiority in every quality.

Does sharing a root mean identical produce?

Ṣinwān palms grow from one base, but the word itself makes no definite judgment about the quantity or quality of their produce.

Trunks arising from one origin may differ in yield because of growth condition, light, environmental circumstances, or other factors.

Conversely, palms with independent bases may produce very similar fruit.

Lexically, therefore, being ṣinwān means sharing a root, rather than guaranteeing identical fruit.

The verse presents this shared root as one example of diversity in creation, alongside the broader subject of differences in produce.

This image within Al-Raʿd

The preceding verse describes the spreading of the earth, mountains, rivers, fruits, and night covering day.

Al-Raʿd 13:4 draws attention to differences in neighboring environments: adjoining land, gardens, crops, and palms with shared or independent bases.

The verse ends:

إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَآيَاتٍ لِقَوْمٍ يَعْقِلُونَ

inna fī dhālika la-āyātin li-qawmin yaʿqilūna

“In this are signs for people who reason.”

Attention to nature is thus connected with reasoning and examining signs.

People are invited to notice real differences as well as shared features.

This is compatible with scientific understanding of nature: examining factors affecting growth and yield is part of understanding these phenomena.

The verse does not establish that all natural differences occur without causes or mechanisms, or that it has stated every botanical cause of differing produce.

Al-Ṭabarī: shared water and differences in edible produce

7. Common origin and difference: extending the idea

The use of ṣinw for palms sharing a base and brothers sharing an origin helps illuminate a broader principle: common origin does not necessarily mean every characteristic is identical.

Two brothers may belong to one family yet differ in knowledge, character, ability, and decisions.

People of one community may likewise share history and environment while differing in conduct and choices.

These examples can clarify how ṣinw’s lexical meaning relates to human experience.

Ṣinwān itself in Al-Raʿd 13:4, however, does not refer to people. The verse directly concerns actual palms.

Its meaning should therefore not become “people with one religion,” “groups with one leader,” or “people with different destinies.”

Such comparisons may serve reflection or analogy, but are not the root’s direct meaning.

Nor does common origin alone confer moral superiority or remove individual responsibility.

Kinship, shared community, and a common upbringing do not replace assessing each person’s conduct.

8. Distinctions from related roots

Ṣ N W (ṣinw) and A Ṣ L (aṣl)

Aṣl in Arabic concerns the base, foundation, or root of something.

Ibrāhīm 14:24 states:

كَشَجَرَةٍ طَيِّبَةٍ أَصْلُهَا ثَابِتٌ وَفَرْعُهَا فِي السَّمَاءِ

ka-shajaratin ṭayyibatin aṣluhā thābitun wa-farʿuhā fī al-samāʾi

“Like a wholesome tree whose root is firm and whose branch is in the sky.”

Aṣl here denotes the tree’s foundation.

Ṣinw, however, denotes one of several trunks or branches arising from one base.

Aṣl and ṣinw are therefore related but different: one denotes the base or origin, the other the relationship among parts arising from it.

Study of A Ṣ L

Ṣ N W (ṣinw) and A KH W (akh)

The Qur’an uses akh for a brother and, more broadly, for social and communal bonds.

Ṣinw in human usage can mean a brother, but its underlying image emphasizes a shared root and common origin.

Both can describe two brothers, but their semantic ranges differ.

Akh has broad social uses in the Qur’an. Ṣinw, however, is never applied to people in the Qur’anic text; it occurs only concerning palms.

Human uses of ṣinw can therefore be established through lexical evidence, but should not be presented as the direct meaning of Al-Raʿd’s verse.

Study of A KH W

Ṣ N W (ṣinw) and SH Q Q (shaqīq)

In kinship usage, shaqīq denotes a closely related brother, especially one sharing both father and mother.

Ṣinw expresses the same meaning in some Arabic uses.

SH Q Q, however, has the broader field of splitting and division, whereas Ṣ N W concerns closeness and sharing a root.

The words may overlap in describing a family relationship, but their semantic paths differ.

The use of shaqīq for a brother does not establish that ṣinw always means a full brother.

Study of SH Q Q

Ṣ N W (ṣinwān) and Q N W (qinwān)

These words are close in spelling and sound, but belong to different roots.

Al-Anʿām 6:99 states:

وَمِنَ النَّخْلِ مِنْ طَلْعِهَا قِنْوَانٌ دَانِيَةٌ

wa-mina al-nakhli min ṭalʿihā qinwānun dāniyatun

“And from the palm, from its spathe, clusters within reach.”

Qinwān is the plural of qinw and denotes date clusters.

Ṣinwān in Al-Raʿd 13:4 instead concerns palms growing from one base.

Qinwān therefore concerns a palm’s fruit clusters, while ṣinwān concerns the relationship between trunks and their base.

The words’ resemblance should not make date clusters and palms sharing a root identical.

Ṣ N W (ṣinw) and Ṣ N F (ṣinf)

Ṣinf in Arabic denotes a kind, category, or group of similar things.

Ṣinw instead concerns a shared root, closeness, and, in broader use, resemblance.

Two palms may belong to one ṣinf without being ṣinwān: they may be of one kind but have independent bases.

Conversely, several ṣinwān trunks may grow from one base without ṣinw itself naming their botanical type or classification.

Ṣinf and ṣinw should therefore not be assigned one root because of their similar sound or notions of closeness.

9. Translation suggestions for Forqan

صِنْو (ṣinw): sharing a root and base; in human usage, a brother, close relative, or counterpart.

صِنْوَان (ṣinwān): palms sharing a root, or several trunks arising from one base.

غَيْرُ صِنْوَانٍ (ghayru ṣinwānin): palms without a common base, growing separately.

أَصْنَاء (aṣnāʾ): counterparts or those sharing an origin; in non-Qur’anic usage.

صَنْوَة (ṣanwah): an offshoot or young plant; distinct from صِنْوَة (ṣinwah), the feminine form of ṣinw.

Tāj al-ʿArūs: ṣanwah with fatḥah for an offshoot and ṣinwah as the feminine form

صُنَيّ (ṣunayy): a small hollow or water basin; a secondary early Arabic use.

A suitable rendering of the principal phrase in Al-Raʿd 13:4 is:

“And palms sharing a root and growing separately.”

This expresses the two growth conditions briefly and naturally.

Lexical explanation should clarify that “sharing a root” concerns several trunks arising from one base. “Growing separately” here concerns independent bases, rather than necessarily standing far apart.

“Twin palms” is unsuitable because ṣinwān here is not restricted to two trunks.

“Similar and dissimilar palms” also fails to convey the principal meaning: outward resemblance and actually sharing a root are different.

“Grafted palms” may suggest people joining two independent plants, whereas the word concerns growth from one common base.

10. Conclusion

In Arabic, Ṣ N W concerns proximity, a shared root, and connection between things with a common origin or closely related in origin and position.

Its best-known use concerns palms with several trunks arising from one base. Each trunk is the other’s ṣinw, and together they are ṣinwān.

Human uses describe brothers, close relatives, and counterparts. Secondary Arabic uses also apply forms of the family to neighboring wells and a small hollow or water basin.

Only ṣinwān occurs in the Qur’an: twice in Al-Raʿd 13:4, once in nakhīlun ṣinwānun and once in ghayru ṣinwānin.

The verse places palms with shared and independent bases alongside one another, then describes irrigation with one water and differences in produce.

Sharing a root, spatial proximity, and identical produce are three different matters. Being a ṣinw concerns the relationship of origin; fruit quality and quantity do not follow from the root itself.

Human uses of ṣinw also permit reflection on connections and differences among people sharing an origin. Such extensions should not replace its direct Qur’anic meaning.

Brief root meaning for Forqan:

Sharing a root, proximity, and connection in origin; especially several trunks or palms growing from one base, and, in human usage, a close relative or counterpart.

Meaning in Al-Raʿd 13:4:

صِنْوَانٌ (ṣinwānun): palms whose several trunks grow from one common base.

صِنْوَانٍ (ṣinwānin) in وَغَيْرُ صِنْوَانٍ (wa-ghayru ṣinwānin): palms sharing a base; ghayr negates that feature, indicating palms with independent bases.
