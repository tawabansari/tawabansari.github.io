Core meaning: suspending or attaching something to something else, so that it appears as an appendage or identifying mark; in human descriptions, someone affiliated with a group without originally belonging to it, or someone recognizable by a particular trait.

1. Introduction and meaning in pre-Islamic Arabic

The Arabic root Z N M concerns hanging, attachment, and marking. Its concrete image is an appendage of skin hanging from an animal’s ear or beneath its throat, sometimes serving to identify it.

زَنَمَة denotes such an appendage. It may occur naturally in some animals or be created by cutting part of the ear’s skin and leaving it hanging.

زَنَّمَ الْبَعِيرَ means marking a camel in this way.

Two human applications are associated with this concrete image:

First, زَنِيم can describe someone attached or attributed to a people without originally belonging to them, compared to an appendage attached to something else.

Second, it has been explained as someone recognizable by a trait or behavior, particularly notoriety and wrongdoing, as an animal is recognized by an ear or throat marking.

These uses are related but not identical.

Affiliation concerns attribution and social position, while being known for wrongdoing concerns conduct and reputation.

One early lexical definition describes the root as follows:

أَصْلٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى تَعْلِيقِ شَيْءٍ بِشَيْءٍ

aṣlun yadullu ʿalā taʿlīqi shayʾin bi-shayʾ

“An origin indicating the suspension of one thing from another.”

This connects the physical appendage, animal marking, and human applications.

It does not imply that everything marked, or everyone joining a group, is inherently objectionable.

The evaluative force must be examined in each word and context.

First pre-Islamic quotation: marked camels in Zuhayr ibn Abi Sulma

Zuhayr ibn Abi Sulma says in his Muʿallaqah:

فَأَصْبَحَ يَجْرِي فِيهِمْ مِنْ تِلَادِكُمْ
مَغَانِمُ شَتَّى مِنْ إِفَالٍ مُزَنَّمِ

fa-aṣbaḥa yajrī fīhim min tilādikum / maghānimu shattā min ifālin muzannami

Translation:

“From your inherited possessions, various benefits now pass among them in the form of young, marked camels.”

The verse praises people who assumed payments and blood-money obligations to end warfare and establish peace.

تِلَاد denotes long-held, inherited possessions.

إِفَال is the plural of أَفِيل and denotes young camels.

In one early explanation, مُزَنَّم describes camels recognizable by a mark on the ear.

Such markings were associated particularly with valuable camels. Part of the ear was cut but left hanging, allowing the animal to be identified.

The wording and interpretation vary: one account reads a description of marked camels; another gives the name of a male camel to which the young camels are attributed.

The quotation establishes the form in pre-Islamic poetry, while its textual and interpretive differences must remain visible.

On the descriptive reading, it illustrates animal marking. The proper-name reading alone would not prove that meaning, although the lexical family is attested in the poem.

On the marked-camel interpretation, the mark is not a defect; it may distinguish a valuable animal.

The physical marking sense is therefore not inherently disparaging.

Second quotation: muzannam in al-Mutalammis

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Mutalammis al-Dubaʿi reads:

وَإِنَّ نِصَابِي إِنْ سَأَلْتَ وَأُسْرَتِي
مِنَ النَّاسِ حَيٌّ يَقْتَنُونَ الْمُزَنَّمَا

wa-inna niṣābī in saʾalta wa-usratī / mina al-nāsi ḥayyun yaqtanūna al-muzannamā

Translation:

“If you ask about my lineage and family, I belong to a people who keep marked camels.”

On this reading, الْمُزَنَّم concerns an animal recognized by a mark.

The poet speaks of his family and social standing, presenting ownership of such livestock as a feature of his people.

The animal reading is not disparaging. An alternative transmitted explanation understands مُزَنَّم of a person held in servitude; the translation above follows the livestock interpretation.

Together with Zuhayr’s verse, this supports the early marking branch when read of livestock; the competing explanation should not be suppressed.

Evidence concerning human affiliation

A verse attributed in literary records sometimes to al-Khatim al-Tamimi and sometimes to Hassan ibn Thabit reads:

زَنِيمٌ تَدَاعَاهُ الرِّجَالُ زِيَادَةً
كَمَا زِيدَ فِي عَرْضِ الْأَدِيمِ الْأَكَارِعُ

zanīmun tadāʿāhu al-rijālu ziyādatan / kamā zīda fī ʿarḍi al-adīmi al-akāriʿu

Translation:

“An attached outsider whom men claim as an addition, like the limb-portions left as additions along a hide’s edge.”

Here زَنِيم describes a person attributed to a group whose connection the poet considers inauthentic.

أَكَارِع refers to limbs or their portions; the image of peripheral parts attached to the hide conveys an addition. It does not necessarily describe sewing separate patches onto leather.

The quotation helps establish the human meaning, though its attribution is disputed.

Since it is attributed both to a pre-Islamic poet and to Hassan, who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, it cannot be treated as unequivocally pre-Islamic.

The usage nevertheless establishes a human description involving affiliation or attribution to a group.

What the pre-Quranic evidence establishes

The evidence supports the following account, with different levels of certainty for the various branches:

The concrete branch concerns an attached appendage or bodily mark, natural or created for livestock identification. Early poetic evidence supports animal marking; human applications involving affiliation or recognition are also recorded, but not every such sense has an equally secure pre-Islamic date.

The pre-Islamic quotations support the marked-livestock interpretation, subject to the interpretive differences already noted.

The human use is clear in lexical and literary records, but the earliest dates of notoriety and social-attribution senses cannot all be established with equal confidence.

The physical and human meanings should remain distinct, without constructing a definite derivational history for every use.

2. Important forms and derivatives

زَنَمَ يَزْنُمُ

In livestock marking, cutting part of an animal’s ear and leaving it hanging.

زَنَمَ الْبَعِيرَ

zanama al-baʿīra

“He cut part of the camel’s ear and left it hanging as a mark.”

In this explanation, it differs from removing the whole ear: the hanging portion remains. Some lexical accounts distinguish related marking practices differently.

زَنَمَة

An appendage of skin hanging from the ear or beneath an animal’s throat.

Some goats naturally have such appendages on either side beneath the throat.

Camels could also be given hanging ear marks by cutting part of the ear’s skin.

The word can therefore concern a natural appendage or an artificial identifying mark.

Their origins differ, but their hanging shape is similar.

زَنَمَتَا الْأُذُنِ

Two projections or parts near the ear.

This expression is explained as two small parts beside the earlobe, opposite the ridge. It should be distinguished from the broader animal-appendage uses of زَنَمَة.

The varying descriptions caution against limiting every use to one appendage in an identical anatomical location.

زَنَّمَ يُزَنِّمُ

A Form II verb meaning to mark an animal by creating a hanging appendage.

زَنَّمَ الْبَعِيرَ

zannama al-baʿīra

“He marked the camel by leaving a hanging part of its ear.”

Another recorded use concerns affiliating someone with a group to which they did not originally belong.

Here attachment or attribution is prominent.

تَزْنِيم

The verbal noun of زَنَّمَ, denoting creating such a mark or appendage on an ear.

In human applications, it can concern attaching or affiliating someone with another group.

It should not be confused with تَسْنِيم from S N M in Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:27, which has a different root and semantic field.

زَنِم and أَزْنَم

Descriptions of an animal possessing an appendage.

بَعِيرٌ أَزْنَمُ

baʿīrun aznamu

“A camel possessing a hanging mark or appendage.”

This feature does not itself imply illness, defect, or worthlessness.

زَنْمَاء

The feminine form of أَزْنَم.

It describes a female animal possessing an appendage.

In livestock descriptions, it can concern a natural feature or an artificial mark.

مُزَنَّم

The passive participle of زَنَّمَ: something or an animal that has been marked.

Early Arabic uses it of camels with ear marks.

Some lexical explanations associate the mark with valuable camels.

It therefore need not be negative when describing animals.

The form is also recorded of a person attributed or affiliated to a people.

زَنِيم

In its physical use, an animal possessing a hanging appendage.

Two human explanations are prominent in this discussion.

One concerns someone affiliated to a people without originally belonging to them, described as an addition.

The other concerns someone known by a particular trait, especially notoriety and wrongdoing, as an animal is recognized by its appendage.

Some uses also describe a base or ill-behaved person.

This disparaging human application should not be extended to every derivative, including marked camels.

زُنْمَة

A plant name is also recorded, associated with a shape resembling an animal’s appendage.

Descriptions of the plant are not uniform.

The resemblance may have informed the name, but its precise naming history is unclear.

Of these forms, only زَنِيم occurs in the Quran.

3. Quranic usage

The root occurs once, at Al-Qalam 68:13.

Al-Qalam 68:13

عُتُلٍّ بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ زَنِيمٍ

ʿutullin baʿda dhālika zanīmin

Al-Qalam 68:13

Proposed rendering on the notoriety interpretation:

“Harsh, and beyond that, notorious for evil.”

زَنِيمٍ is a singular description in the genitive case.

It concerns the person whose qualities are introduced from Al-Qalam 68:10 onward.

عُتُلٍّ describes someone harsh, brutal, or severe.

بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ adds a description beyond those already mentioned.

زَنِيمٍ is the final description in this sequence.

Both lexical meanings and preceding descriptions matter to its interpretation.

Context: someone known for objectionable conduct

Al-Qalam 68:10 mentions someone given to frequent oaths and baseness.

Al-Qalam 68:11 describes faultfinding and going about spreading malicious tales.

Al-Qalam 68:12 concerns withholding good, transgression, and sinfulness.

Al-Qalam 68:13 adds harshness and being زَنِيم.

In Al-Qalam 68:15, when divine signs are recited to him, he calls them tales of the ancients.

The sequence portrays someone opposing truth and behaving badly toward others.

This supports the reading someone recognizable by wrongdoing and baseness, without conclusively excluding the affiliation reading.

On that interpretation, his conduct identifies him as an animal’s mark identifies it.

This does not make زَنِيم directly mean sinner or wrongdoer in every Arabic use.

Here its negative force relates to the preceding descriptions and the person’s conduct.

Can it mean someone attached to another people?

An established lexical meaning concerns someone affiliated to a people without originally belonging to them.

Some Quran translations and explanations apply it to Al-Qalam 68:13.

This reading describes the person’s social or genealogical attribution as an addition to a group.

It is linguistically attested and should not be excluded merely because the expression is uncomfortable.

Two issues nevertheless remain distinct.

First, the word can describe an affiliated outsider in Arabic.

Second, whether this verse specifically addresses family descent or notoriety and a recognizable quality.

The verse itself names no person, father, mother, or people.

The preceding criticism also concentrates on faultfinding, malicious tale-bearing, withholding good, transgression, and sinfulness.

This supports the notoriety reading, but contextual suitability does not itself disprove the other recorded interpretation.

The phrase alone does not conclusively establish a particular person’s lineage.

Does it mean a child born outside marriage?

Some lexical and exegetical accounts apply it to someone attributed to a man other than their actual father, or born outside marriage.

This relates to the affiliation branch.

That interpretation cannot be presented without qualification as the verse’s indisputable meaning.

Affiliation to another people is not identical to birth outside marriage: guardianship, alliances, migration, and other social relationships can also connect someone to a group.

The verse supplies no explicit account of the person’s birth or their parents’ conduct; interpretations connecting the word with descent remain disputed.

Al-Anʿām 6:164 states that no one bears another’s burden of sin.

Al-Najm 53:38 and Al-Najm 53:39 likewise connect responsibility with one’s own conduct.

A parent’s possible wrongdoing therefore cannot become a child’s inherent guilt.

Recording a historical insult about descent does not amount to endorsing its moral judgment.

Al-Qalam 68:10, Al-Qalam 68:11, Al-Qalam 68:12, Al-Qalam 68:13, Al-Qalam 68:14, and Al-Qalam 68:15 present conduct and circumstances relevant to the criticism.

This article adopts the notoriety reading as a contextual proposal. The affiliation reading remains a substantial recorded interpretation, rather than an option disproved by the ethical context.

4. A bodily mark or a mark of conduct?

Does the verse refer to an actual bodily feature, or describe personality and social position?

Arabic can use زَنِيم of an animal with an appendage.

Al-Qalam 68:13 places it among human descriptions.

These concern conduct, character, and dealings with others.

A social and ethical description is therefore a serious possibility.

Some early accounts say the person had an identifying feature on the ear or neck.

The verse does not explicitly describe such a feature; that identification comes from a particular interpretation and accompanying reports.

A specific bodily appendage cannot therefore be conclusively established from the wording alone.

Comparing a person to a marked animal to convey recognizability is linguistically intelligible.

On this reading, the person need not literally have a goat’s or camel’s appendage; his misconduct is as recognizable as a visible mark.

This preserves the root’s concrete image without making a definite anatomical claim.

5. The connection with marking in Al-Qalam 68:16

Al-Qalam 68:16 says:

سَنَسِمُهُ عَلَى الْخُرْطُومِ

sa-nasimuhu ʿalā al-khurṭūm

Al-Qalam 68:16

Translation:

“We shall mark him upon the snout.”

سَنَسِمُهُ belongs to W S M and denotes marking.

In Al-Qalam 68:13, زَنِيم belongs to a family whose concrete branch concerns a mark or hanging appendage on an animal.

The proximity of these images invites attention.

In verse 13, one interpretation of the description involves being marked and recognizable.

Verse 16 announces marking the snout.

The roots nevertheless differ.

W S M concerns marking and identifying something.

Z N M concerns a hanging appendage or a particular kind of mark in its concrete branch.

زَنِيم should therefore not simply be reduced to “marked” as an exact equivalent of مَوْسُوم.

Their coexistence does not prove that the later mark is the same bodily appendage or a particular livestock mark.

Literarily, a connection between notoriety and the promised mark is understandable.

On that reading, someone known for his conduct encounters a consequence and sign of that condition.

This should be presented as contextual reflection, not a definitive lexical definition.

6. Distinctions from related roots

Z N M (زَنَمَ) and W S M (وَسَمَ)

W S M concerns identifying something by a mark.

The concrete Z N M branch concerns a hanging appendage or mark on an animal.

Taznīm can therefore be a kind of marking, but not every marking is taznīm.

Animals may be identified through ear cutting, branding, or other methods.

The appendage is only one bodily marking form.

Z N M (زَنِيم) and L S Q (لَصِيق)

L S Q concerns sticking and attachment.

Lexical explanations sometimes describe زَنِيم as لَصِيقٌ بِالْقَوْمِ, someone attached to a people.

The words are close in this use.

L S Q has a broader field of sticking and joining, while زَنِيم also carries the specific image of an appendage and human attribution or recognizability.

Social affiliation does not necessarily mean wrongdoing.

Attachment alone therefore cannot explain the entire condemnatory force of the context.

Z N M (زَنِيم) and AYN T L (عُتُلّ)

These descriptions occur together in one verse.

عُتُلّ concerns harshness, brutality, and severe behavior.

One contextually suitable reading of زَنِيم concerns someone known for wrongdoing.

The two descriptions are not identical.

Someone may be harsh without every action being publicly known.

Someone may also be notorious without every characteristic being covered by عُتُلّ.

Both contribute to the negative portrait in Al-Qalam 68:13.

Z N M (زَنِيم) and H M Z (هَمْز)

In human conduct, H M Z concerns faultfinding and disparagement.

هَمَّازٍ in Al-Qalam 68:11 belongs to this root.

زَنِيم does not name the act of faultfinding itself; it may describe someone recognizable by a trait or conduct.

Faultfinding may contribute to notoriety without exhausting the meaning of زَنِيم.

Z N M (زَنِيم) and L ʾ M (لُؤْم)

لُؤْم concerns baseness and objectionable conduct.

Some lexical uses explain زَنِيم as base or known for baseness.

The terms can therefore overlap in human descriptions.

Yet زَنِيم also has a lexical background involving identifying marks and affiliation.

Translating every use as “base” without those distinctions obscures the word’s range.

Z N M (زَنَمَ) and Z N Y (زَنَى)

Their first two consonants coincide, but their structures and meanings differ.

Z N M concerns appendages, marking, affiliation, and recognizability.

Z N Y concerns illicit sexual relations.

Some accounts apply زَنِيم to a child of uncertain attribution or birth outside marriage, but that does not merge the roots.

زَنِيم belongs to Z N M, not Z N Y.

This morphological and lexical distinction should be preserved in translation and analysis.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

The following equivalents distinguish important forms. These proposals do not replace installed verse translations or existing authored word-box explanations.

زَنَمَ: marked an animal’s ear by cutting a piece and leaving it hanging.

زَنْم: creating a hanging mark in the relevant animal use.

زَنَمَة: an appendage of skin on an ear or beneath the throat, sometimes an identifying mark.

زَنَّمَ: marked; in another use, affiliated someone with a group.

تَزْنِيم: creating a hanging mark or marking in this manner.

زَنِم: possessing a hanging appendage.

أَزْنَم: possessing a hanging mark or appendage.

زَنْمَاء: the feminine of أَزْنَم.

مُزَنَّم: marked; in livestock usage, an animal marked for identification.

زَنِيم: possessing an appendage; in human uses, an affiliated outsider or someone recognizable by a particular quality or wrongdoing.

Proposed translation of Al-Qalam 68:13

عُتُلٍّ بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ زَنِيمٍ

ʿutullin baʿda dhālika zanīmin

Al-Qalam 68:13

Proposed translation:

“Harsh, and beyond that, notorious for evil.”

This adopts one recorded reading. If “marked by evil” is used instead, “marked” should explain recognizability, not add a separate physical characteristic to the verse.

The explanation can clarify someone recognizable by wrongdoing, as an animal is recognized by its appendage.

The affiliation reading should also be discussed, without turning it into an unsupported claim about a particular person’s family descent.

A brief root summary can say:

“An attached appendage and identifying mark; human applications involve affiliation or recognition by a particular trait.”

For the verse-specific meaning:

“On one interpretation, notorious for wrongdoing; another understands an outsider attached to a people.”

An unqualified “child born outside marriage” would present one disputed explanation as certain. This article therefore does not select it as the direct translation, while retaining it in the account of recorded interpretations.

Likewise, a bodily appendage should not be asserted without qualification. The anatomical interpretation is recorded, but the verse does not explicitly describe that feature or the person’s identity.

8. Conclusion

Z N M concerns suspending or attaching one thing to another.

زَنَمَة denotes a skin appendage hanging from an ear or beneath an animal’s throat, either natural or created as a mark.

The pre-Islamic verses of Zuhayr and al-Mutalammis support the marked-livestock sense on that interpretation, with the textual and interpretive variations already discussed.

Human uses concern an affiliated outsider or someone recognizable by a particular trait, especially wrongdoing and baseness.

Only زَنِيمٍ occurs in the Quran, at Al-Qalam 68:13.

It stands among negative descriptions involving faultfinding, malicious tale-bearing, withholding good, transgression, sinfulness, and harshness.

The notoriety reading fits that context, while remaining an interpretation rather than an undisputed definition.

Social attribution also belongs to the lexical field, but the wording alone does not conclusively establish the person’s birth or family descent.

Criticizing conduct should not become a claim of inherent guilt through birth, family, or lineage. The Quran connects responsibility with personal conduct.

The central image is an appendage attached to something else. Human uses include affiliation and recognizability. In Al-Qalam 68:13 the context is condemnatory, but the precise description remains subject to the recorded interpretations.

This preserves the concrete lexical evidence and distinguishes the main readings, without imposing uncertain historical reports or judgments about descent as the verse’s definitive meaning.
