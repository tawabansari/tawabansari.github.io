The root f-ṣ-ḥ — Clear speech, eloquence, and intelligible expression

Qur’anic and lexical forms

أَفْصَحُ
(afsahu)
More eloquent in speech

فَصَاحَة / فَصِيح
(fasahah / fasih)
Eloquence and clear speech

أَفْصَحَ / إِفْصَاح
(afsaha / ifsah)
Expressing something clearly

The triliteral Arabic root ف ص ح relates to clarity, becoming apparent, and freedom from what obstructs clarity. In speech, فَصَاحَة denotes clear and fluent expression through which speakers convey their meaning effectively and listeners understand it.

فَصِيح may describe a person whose speech is fluent and clear, or speech whose words and structure are intelligible to its audience.

The same family also describes nonlinguistic phenomena in Arabic, such as dawn becoming bright or milk becoming clear when its foam subsides.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as أَفْصَحُ in Al-Qasas 28:34. Moses says that his brother Aaron is more eloquent than he is, and asks God to send Aaron with him to support and confirm him.

Eloquence does not mean truthfulness, greater knowledge, or moral superiority. Someone may express falsehood very clearly and fluently, while an honest and knowledgeable person may struggle to communicate.

In this verse, Aaron’s eloquence is his ability to express himself—an ability Moses recognizes as valuable for fulfilling their shared mission.

1. The root in early Arabic

Clarity and fluency of speech

In attested Arabic, فَصُحَ الرَّجُلُ means that a person’s speech became clear, fluent, and intelligible, or that he possessed a good ability to express himself.

لِسَانٌ فَصِيحٌ is a tongue that articulates speech clearly and fluently.

كَلَامٌ فَصِيحٌ is speech that is clear and sound in its expression.

Early lexicons explain eloquence through بَيَان and طَلَاقَةُ اللِّسَان.

بَيَان concerns making one’s meaning clear.

طَلَاقَةُ اللِّسَان refers to ease and fluency in speaking.

These qualities are closely related, but not identical.

Someone may speak rapidly and without pauses, yet leave listeners unsure of the intended meaning.

Conversely, someone may speak slowly and with pauses while communicating clearly and intelligibly.

Eloquence should therefore not be reduced to speed of speech.

In linguistic usage, clarity, fluency, and the ability to convey meaning matter.

Clarity and purity in material things

Early lexicons also record فَصُحَ اللَّبَنُ.

This describes milk whose foam has gone, leaving the milk itself apparent.

Likewise, أَفْصَحَ الصُّبْحُ means that the brightness of dawn became apparent.

In the first example, the removal of foam makes the milk itself more visible.

In the second, dawn’s light emerges as darkness begins to recede.

These examples relate to becoming apparent and removing an obstacle to seeing or recognizing something.

In speech, ambiguity, complexity, or difficulties with articulation may prevent the speaker’s meaning from being understood.

Clearer speech reduces such obstacles.

This semantic relationship is understandable, but it does not prove that the word for eloquence was first coined for milk and only later applied to language.

In Maqayis al-Lughah, Ibn Faris identifies purity and freedom from admixture as the root’s core. This is a valuable lexical analysis, but by itself it does not establish a definitive history of every use of the root.

Early poetic evidence and its historical limits

Studying pre-Qur’anic language requires distinguishing pre-Islamic poetry from Islamic-period poetry and lexical definitions recorded in later centuries.

Lexicons quote the following rajaz fragment from Abu al-Najm al-‘Ijli:

«أَعْجَمَ فِي آذَانِهَا فَصِيحًا»

A'jama fi adhaniha fasihan

The expression concerns a donkey’s voice: unintelligible to humans perhaps, but clear and understandable to other donkeys.

This illustrates an important point about language: a message’s clarity depends not only on its sounds, but also on the recipient’s familiarity and ability to understand.

Abu al-Najm al-‘Ijli was an Umayyad-period poet. This fragment therefore cannot be presented as pre-Islamic poetry or independent evidence from before the Qur’an’s revelation.

Another verse attributed to Nadlah al-Sulami contains وَتَحْتَ الرَّغْوَةِ اللَّبَنُ الْفَصِيحُ, meaning “beneath the foam lies clear milk.”

Some versions have الصريح instead of الفصيح. The precise date of the verse’s composition is also uncertain.

It is useful evidence for the lexical senses of clarity and purity, but should not be treated without qualification as conclusive evidence from the pre-Islamic period.

Pre-Islamic poetry also contains الفِصْح, a religious festival name borrowed into Arabic from other Semitic languages and known as the feast of Fisḥ.

Its shared consonants with ف ص ح do not establish that it derives from the Arabic root of eloquence.

The available poetic evidence therefore has limits for dating every branch of this root precisely.

Nevertheless, أَفْصَحُ in Al-Qasas 28:34 demonstrates that the sense of fluent, clear speech was recognized in Arabic at the time of revelation.

2. Linguistic forms and the core meaning

Important forms in this Arabic family include:

  • فَصُحَ: spoke clearly and fluently; possessed eloquence.
  • فَصَاحَة: clarity and fluency of speech.
  • فَصِيح: an articulate person, or clear and fluent speech.
  • فُصَحَاء: plural of فصيح; eloquent speakers.
  • أَفْصَحَ: expressed one’s meaning openly and clearly.
  • إِفْصَاح: making one’s meaning explicit; clear expression.
  • أَفْصَح: as an elative, more eloquent or clearer in speech.
  • تَفَصَّحَ: spoke eloquently, or sought to adopt eloquent speech.
  • تَفَاصَحَ: in some uses, displayed or affected eloquence.
  • مُفْصِح: one who makes something explicit or expresses it clearly.
  • فُصْحَى: the feminine of أفصح, also used in naming standard Arabic.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an.

Only أَفْصَحُ occurs there.

In Al-Qasas 28:34 this is an elative, not a Form IV past-tense verb.

The distinction matters:

أَفْصَحَ, with a final fatḥah, may be a past-tense verb meaning that someone spoke clearly or made their meaning explicit.

But أَفْصَحُ in the verse is an elative meaning “more eloquent.”

The core meaning relevant to the Qur’anic usage can be defined as:

Clear and fluent expression that enables a speaker to communicate their meaning effectively through language.

This concerns clarity and expressive ability, rather than whether the content is true or false.

3. Qur’anic usage: Aaron is more eloquent than Moses

Al-Qasas 28:34

وَأَخِي هَارُونُ هُوَ أَفْصَحُ مِنِّي لِسَانًا فَأَرْسِلْهُ مَعِيَ رِدْءًا يُصَدِّقُنِي ۖ إِنِّي أَخَافُ أَنْ يُكَذِّبُونِ

Wa-akhi Harunu huwa afsahu minni lisanan fa-arsilhu ma'iya rid'an yusaddiquni inni akhafu an yukadhdhibun

Translation: “And my brother Aaron is more eloquent in speech than I am. So send him with me as a support to confirm me. I fear that they will deny me.”

This is the root’s sole Qur’anic occurrence.

Moses has been commissioned to go to Pharaoh and his leading men.

But he fears being killed and having his message rejected.

In Al-Qasas 28:33, Moses recalls having killed one of their people and fears that they will kill him.

He then speaks in verse 34 of Aaron’s ability and asks for his brother to accompany him in the mission.

Eloquence is thus discussed in a real and difficult situation: communicating a message before a power that may reject it and endanger its bearer’s life.

أَفْصَحُ مِنِّي: comparing two people’s abilities

أَفْصَحُ is an elative.

مِنِّي specifies the person with whom the comparison is made.

لِسَانًا specifies the respect in which one is better.

The sentence therefore means that Aaron is better than Moses at fluent speech and expression.

But the comparison does not establish several other things.

First, it does not say that Moses had no ability to speak.

Second, it does not say that Aaron surpassed Moses in every field of knowledge or ability.

Third, it does not say that Aaron surpassed Moses in faith, standing before God, or honesty.

The verse compares one specific ability: eloquence of speech and expression.

For example, someone may understand a subject very well yet struggle to explain it to a group. Another person may be better at conversation, explanation, and answering questions.

This difference does not make either person superior in every respect.

Recognizing such a difference, Moses asks God to send his brother with him.

لِسَانًا: specifying the comparison

In هُوَ أَفْصَحُ مِنِّي لِسَانًا, the word لِسَانًا is an accusative specification, or tamyiz.

It clarifies the respect in which the comparison is made.

Thus “tongue” here does not refer only to the physical organ: it includes the ability to speak through it.

Both “more eloquent than I am” and “clearer in speech than I am” are therefore defensible renderings.

Saying only “Aaron is better than I am” omits the specific basis of comparison.

The Qur’an does not declare Aaron absolutely superior to Moses; it says أَفْصَحُ مِنِّي لِسَانًا.

4. Moses’ difficulty in speaking: what does the Qur’an say?

Understanding Moses’ request also requires examining other verses about his speech.

Ta-Ha 20:27

وَاحْلُلْ عُقْدَةً مِنْ لِسَانِي

Wa-uhlul 'uqdatan min lisani

Translation: “And loosen a knot from my tongue.”

Moses asks God to remove an impediment from his tongue.

عُقْدَة belongs to the root ع ق د and relates to a knot or binding.

Applied to the tongue, it may refer to difficulty expressing oneself or speaking fluently.

The verse does not specify the precise nature of that difficulty.

It may concern a limitation in articulation, or difficulty arising when speaking in a sensitive situation.

The phrase عقدة من لساني alone cannot establish a medical diagnosis or historical cause.

Ta-Ha 20:28

يَفْقَهُوا قَوْلِي

Yafqahu qawli

Translation: “So that they may understand my speech.”

This verse explains the purpose of Moses’ request.

He does not merely want to speak more beautifully or quickly; he wants his audience to understand him.

This distinction matters.

Someone may speak loudly and rapidly, yet the listener may not understand the intended meaning.

Conversely, calm and simple speech may be very clear and intelligible.

Moses seeks the removal of an obstacle affecting his audience’s understanding.

Difficulty speaking under pressure

Ash-Shu‘ara 26:13

وَيَضِيقُ صَدْرِي وَلَا يَنْطَلِقُ لِسَانِي فَأَرْسِلْ إِلَىٰ هَارُونَ

Wa-yadiqu sadri wa-la yantaliqu lisani fa-arsil ila Harun

Translation: “And my breast tightens, and my tongue does not flow freely; so send to Aaron.”

Moses describes two conditions together: tightness in his breast and difficulty speaking fluently.

He says this in the context of fearing rejection.

His difficulty speaking may therefore also relate to the mission’s demanding circumstances.

Yet their association alone does not prove that the difficulty was necessarily psychological or exclude a physical cause.

The Qur’an does not specify its precise nature.

What is clear is that Moses recognized his difficulty and sought God’s help in fulfilling the mission.

Does the Qur’an contain the story of the burning coal?

Some later exegetical accounts say that Moses placed a burning coal in his mouth as a child and injured his tongue.

This story is not told in the Qur’an.

The Qur’an speaks of عقدة من لساني and ولا ينطلق لساني, but does not attribute the difficulty to a particular childhood incident.

That account therefore cannot be incorporated into the root’s definition as a definitive Qur’anic explanation of Aaron’s eloquence or Moses’ difficulty speaking.

Furthermore, in Ta-Ha 20:36, God tells Moses that his request has been granted.

We must distinguish Moses’ initial difficulty, his request for its removal, and his situation after the divine response.

The verses do not allow us to determine with certainty whether every difference between Moses’ and Aaron’s speaking abilities subsequently disappeared.

They do show that God did not leave Moses’ request unanswered.

5. Pharaoh’s statement about Moses’ speech

Az-Zukhruf 43:52

أَمْ أَنَا خَيْرٌ مِنْ هَٰذَا الَّذِي هُوَ مَهِينٌ وَلَا يَكَادُ يُبِينُ

Am ana khayrun min hadha alladhi huwa mahinun wa-la yakadu yubin

Translation: “Or am I better than this man, who is lowly and can scarcely express himself clearly?”

These are Pharaoh’s words about Moses.

In the preceding verse, Pharaoh speaks of his dominion over Egypt and the waterways under his control.

In verse 52, he then seeks to portray himself as superior to Moses.

One of his claims is that Moses لَا يَكَادُ يُبِينُ—can scarcely make his meaning clear.

This expression belongs to ب ي ن, not ف ص ح.

It nevertheless relates to clarity and fluency of speech.

Is Pharaoh’s judgment a measure of truth?

No.

The Qur’an reports Pharaoh’s words without thereby endorsing all his judgments about Moses.

In the same sentence, Pharaoh calls Moses مَهِين, lowly and insignificant.

This description serves his attempt to demean the Messenger and present himself as superior.

Pharaoh’s words should therefore not be treated uncritically as an impartial report of Moses’ speaking ability.

Even if Moses experienced genuine difficulty speaking, that would not make his message false.

Likewise, Pharaoh’s fluency and skill in political oratory do not establish his honesty or moral superiority.

Clear expression and a true message are different

The story brings out a fundamental distinction.

Someone may express falsehood fluently, confidently, and persuasively.

Another person may struggle to express something true.

Listeners should therefore not judge truth solely by vocal elegance, speed of speech, or oratorical skill.

The content and its supporting reasons require independent examination.

In Moses’ story, divine signs and proofs stand on one side and Pharaoh’s claims on the other.

Neither speaker’s eloquence or social influence is, by itself, the measure of truth.

6. Aaron’s eloquence and cooperation in conveying the message

In Al-Qasas 28:34, Moses does more than say that Aaron is more eloquent; he makes a specific request:

«فَأَرْسِلْهُ مَعِيَ رِدْءًا يُصَدِّقُنِي»

Fa-arsilhu ma'iya rid'an yusaddiquni

That is: send him with me as a helper to confirm me.

رِدْء belongs to the separate root ر د أ and denotes a helper or supporter.

يُصَدِّقُنِي belongs to ص د ق and concerns confirmation and affirmation.

Three matters therefore appear in the sentence:

Aaron’s eloquence, his assistance, and his confirmation of Moses.

They are related concepts, but not identical.

Was Aaron merely Moses’ spokesperson?

In Al-Qasas 28:34, Moses asks God to send Aaron with him.

Ta-Ha 20:29, Ta-Ha 20:30, Ta-Ha 20:31, and Ta-Ha 20:32 also describe Moses’ request for a helper from his family and for Aaron to share in his mission.

These verses show that Aaron’s role was not limited to explaining Moses’ difficult words.

He was also a helper and partner in the mission.

His eloquence was one ability that could contribute to their cooperation.

For example, in research, one person may be good at understanding a problem or gathering evidence, while another excels at explaining the findings clearly to an audience.

If both cooperate in the service of truth, their different abilities can improve their joint work.

This example does not imply that Moses lacked knowledge or that Aaron was merely his translator.

Both have roles in the divine mission in the Qur’an.

God’s response to Moses’ request

Al-Qasas 28:35

قَالَ سَنَشُدُّ عَضُدَكَ بِأَخِيكَ وَنَجْعَلُ لَكُمَا سُلْطَانًا فَلَا يَصِلُونَ إِلَيْكُمَا ۚ بِآيَاتِنَا أَنْتُمَا وَمَنِ اتَّبَعَكُمَا الْغَالِبُونَ

Qala sa-nashuddu 'adudaka bi-akhika wa-naj'alu lakuma sultanan fa-la yasiluna ilaykuma bi-ayatina antuma wa-mani ittaba'akuma al-ghalibun

Translation: “He said: We shall strengthen your arm through your brother and grant you both authority, so that they will not reach you. Through Our signs, you two and those who follow you will prevail.”

God grants Moses’ request and speaks of strengthening him through his brother.

This response shows that asking for help does not in itself indicate inability to fulfill a responsibility or lack of trust in God.

Moses recognizes his brother’s ability and draws on it to carry out the mission.

Recognizing one’s limitations and drawing on others’ abilities can therefore be part of acting responsibly.

This is a reading of the wider story; أفصح itself states only Aaron’s greater eloquence.

7. Eloquence compared with clarification, delivery, understanding, and truthfulness

ف ص ح (فَصِيح) and ب ي ن (بَيَّنَ)

ب ي ن relates to becoming apparent, clarifying, and making the truth of a matter distinct.

In Qur’anic usage, ف ص ح focuses on clear and fluent speech.

Speech may be eloquent and also help clarify a matter.

Yet not every act of clarification requires exceptional speaking ability.

Sometimes a clear document or evident fact removes ambiguity even when the person presenting it is not a skilled speaker.

Conversely, someone may speak fluently without clarifying the truth of the matter.

Eloquence and clarification are therefore related, but are not one concept.

ف ص ح (فَصِيح) and ب ل غ (بَلَغَ)

بَلَاغ concerns conveying a message.

فَصَاحَة concerns the clarity and fluency of speech.

A message may reach its audience yet be partly misunderstood because of difficulties in its expression.

Equally, a very clear statement may never reach its intended audience.

In the mission of the Messengers, both conveying the message and making it clear matter.

Eloquence itself does not mean delivery, and delivery is not confined to having an eloquent tongue.

ف ص ح (فَصِيح) and ف ق ه (فَقِهَ)

In Qur’anic usage, فِقْه concerns understanding and grasping meaning.

In Ta-Ha 20:28, Moses says:

«يَفْقَهُوا قَوْلِي»

Yafqahu qawli

That is: so that they may understand my speech.

Eloquence concerns the speaker’s expressive ability.

Understanding concerns the audience’s reception of meaning.

Clear speech can assist understanding, but understanding does not depend on the speaker alone.

Listeners must also pay attention, know the language, and make an effort to grasp the meaning.

A statement may be clear to one person but difficult for another who is unfamiliar with the subject.

Eloquence and understanding are related, but not identical.

ف ص ح (فَصِيح) and ن ط ق (نَطَقَ)

نُطْق concerns speaking and producing speech.

فَصَاحَة concerns that speech’s clarity and fluency.

Someone may be able to speak yet experience difficulties articulating words.

Someone may also speak in a way others do not understand.

The ability to speak is therefore different from the degree of eloquence.

This matters in comparing Moses and Aaron: Aaron’s greater eloquence does not mean Moses completely lacked speech.

ف ص ح (فَصِيح) and ص د ق (صَدَقَ)

صِدْق concerns truthfulness and the correspondence of speech with truth.

فَصَاحَة concerns clear and fluent expression.

An honest person may have little skill in speaking.

Conversely, a skilled liar may deceive an audience with very fluent speech.

Eloquence cannot replace truthfulness.

Al-Qasas 28:34 reflects this distinction: Moses speaks of Aaron’s eloquence, then of being confirmed and of his fear that his message will be rejected.

Eloquence and truthfulness are related but distinct matters.

ف ص ح (فَصِيح) and ب ل غ (بَلِيغ)

In later literary disciplines, بَلَاغَة also concerns conveying meaning through speech suited to the situation and audience.

Those disciplines developed various technical definitions of eloquence in words and sentences, and of rhetoric.

These definitions are useful for studying the history of literary disciplines, but should not all be treated as the direct meaning of أفصح in Al-Qasas 28:34.

The verse compares Moses’ and Aaron’s speaking abilities; it does not present the specialist classifications of the later disciplines of meanings, figurative expression, and rhetorical embellishment.

ف ص ح (فَصِيح) and ف س ر (فَسَّرَ)

ف س ر concerns explanation and clarification of meaning.

In Qur’anic usage, ف ص ح concerns fluent and eloquent speech.

Someone may be very eloquent yet offer insufficient explanation of a subject.

A researcher may also interpret and clarify a difficult matter well in simple language without literary embellishment.

Eloquence can assist the quality of interpretation, but interpretation and eloquence are not the same activity.

8. Is eloquence confined to Arabic?

Some early lexical definitions associate eloquence with speaking Arabic correctly.

For example, فَصُحَ الْأَعْجَمِيُّ sometimes describes someone who has learned Arabic and whose speech has become intelligible to Arabic speakers.

This does not mean that only Arabic can support clear and eloquent expression.

The same lexicons also use eloquence for intelligible expression, including non-Arabic speech or sounds understood by their particular recipients.

Furthermore, Ar-Rum 30:22 presents the diversity of human languages as one of God’s signs.

Eloquence should therefore not be turned into a claim of inherent ethnic or linguistic superiority.

Someone may speak Persian, Arabic, English, or another language very clearly and precisely.

Conversely, someone may struggle to express their meaning even in their native language.

The Qur’anic expression أفصح مني لسانا compares two people, not two languages or peoples.

This verse therefore provides no basis for ranking people’s human worth by their native language.

9. Morphological and grammatical observations

ف ص ح is a sound triliteral root.

The attested verb فَصُحَ يَفْصُحُ concerns becoming eloquent and fluent in speech.

The family also includes Form IV أَفْصَحَ, which can mean expressing one’s meaning openly and clearly.

But the Qur’anic أَفْصَحُ is not a verb.

In Al-Qasas 28:34 it is an elative on the pattern أَفْعَل.

The sentence is structured as follows:

وَأَخِي هَارُونُ — my brother Aaron.

هُوَ أَفْصَحُ — he is more eloquent.

مِنِّي — than I am.

لِسَانًا — in tongue or speech.

هُوَ is a pronoun referring to Aaron; in one grammatical analysis it is a subject whose predicate is أَفْصَحُ.

Grammatically, لِسَانًا is an accusative specification that clarifies the respect in which he excels.

The sentence’s meaning is thus limited and precise: Aaron surpasses Moses in eloquence of speech.

In فَأَرْسِلْهُ مَعِيَ رِدْءًا يُصَدِّقُنِي, رِدْءًا expresses Aaron’s supporting role.

The recognized readings differ in the inflection of يُصَدِّقُنِي.

In Hafs’s reading from ‘Asim, the verb is indicative and may form an explanatory clause or a description of Aaron’s role.

In some other readings, it is jussive as a response to the request فأرسله.

This grammatical difference does not remove the central meaning of Aaron’s assistance and confirmation of Moses.

However, يصدقني is not itself derived from ف ص ح.

Nor does Aaron’s eloquence imply that he merely repeated Moses’ words. The Qur’an also presents him as a helper and partner in the mission.

10. Difficulties in understanding and translation

The first error is equating eloquence with truthfulness.

Eloquent speech may be true or false. Its truth requires evaluation through evidence and reasoning.

The second error is turning أفصح مني لسانا into a claim of Aaron’s absolute superiority to Moses.

The verse compares speaking ability, not their entire knowledge, faith, or standing.

The third error is inferring that Moses was completely unable to speak.

أفصح is an elative describing a greater degree of ability in Aaron, not the total absence of that ability in Moses.

The fourth error is attributing a specific medical cause or the burning-coal incident to Moses’ speech on the basis of the Qur’an itself.

The Qur’an mentions his difficulty but does not identify its precise cause.

The fifth error is ignoring the divine response to Moses’ request.

In Ta-Ha 20:36, God states that his request has been granted. We should therefore not assume that Moses’ situation remained entirely unchanged.

The sixth error is reducing eloquence to literary ornament and complex vocabulary.

Speech may be very simple and nevertheless clear and eloquent.

Difficult words and long sentences, by contrast, may obscure the meaning for an audience.

The seventh error is restricting eloquence to Arabic and using it to rank speakers of one language above others.

The meaning of clear speech does not itself establish such a hierarchy.

Finally, expressive ability must not replace responsibility toward truth.

A good speaker remains responsible for what they say. Eloquence can help convey truth, but it can also serve deception.

11. Suggested translations for Forqan

In general usage, فَصُحَ can be rendered “spoke clearly and fluently,” “became eloquent,” or “possessed fluency of speech.”

فَصِيح can be translated as “articulate,” “fluent,” “eloquent,” or “clear in speech.”

فَصَاحَة can be “eloquence,” “clarity of expression,” or “fluency of speech,” according to context.

إِفْصَاح in its established usage concerns “expressing openly” and “making one’s meaning clear.”

In Al-Qasas 28:34, however, the Qur’anic form is an elative.

For the expression:

«هُوَ أَفْصَحُ مِنِّي لِسَانًا»

Huwa afsahu minni lisanan

Two suitable renderings are:

“He is clearer in speech than I am.”

Or:

“He is more eloquent than I am.”

The first makes the basis of comparison more explicit.

The second is shorter and natural, conveying the ability to speak effectively; its Persian counterpart likewise conveys verbal ability.

For Forqan’s base translation, the following is suitable:

“And my brother Aaron is more eloquent in speech than I am. So send him with me as a support to confirm me. I fear that they will deny me.”

This preserves Aaron’s greater eloquence while separately expressing Moses’ request for assistance and confirmation.

A brief lexical explanation can distinguish the root’s general meaning from its meaning in this verse as follows:

Word form: أَفْصَحُ

Root: ف ص ح

Brief root meaning: Clear and fluent speech; intelligible expression.

Meaning in this verse: More eloquent and clearer in speech; an elative comparing Aaron’s speaking ability with Moses’.

The brief explanation focuses on this occurrence; Moses’ difficulty speaking and the history of rhetoric are separate discussions.

12. Conclusion

The Arabic root ف ص ح relates to clarity, becoming apparent, and eloquence of speech.

Early lexicons attest this family for clear speech, a fluent tongue, the appearance of dawn, and milk becoming clear after its foam is removed.

These uses relate to clarity and the removal of an obstacle, although independent pre-Qur’anic evidence for their chronological development is limited.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as أَفْصَحُ in Al-Qasas 28:34.

Moses says that Aaron is more eloquent in speech and asks for his brother to accompany him in confronting Pharaoh.

Other verses show that Moses was concerned about his lack of fluency and asked God to remove the obstacle to understanding his speech.

The Qur’an does not specify a medical or historical cause of this difficulty.

Moreover, أفصح indicates only Aaron’s relative superiority in speaking ability—not absolute superiority to Moses, nor Moses’ complete inability to speak.

The root’s core meaning is clear and fluent expression through which a speaker can convey their meaning intelligibly.

This ability relates to knowledge, truthfulness, delivery, and the audience’s understanding, but is identical to none of them.

Someone may know the truth yet struggle to express it. Another person may have greater speaking ability and help make the same truth clear.

In Moses’ and Aaron’s story, this difference leads to cooperation and support.

Instead of ignoring his brother’s ability or concealing his own difficulty, Moses asks God to place Aaron alongside him.

This shows that recognizing people’s different abilities and using them appropriately can contribute to fulfilling a shared responsibility.

Yet eloquence or oratorical power is not the ultimate criterion for a message’s truth: eloquence helps communicate a statement clearly, but its validity must be judged by truth and evidence.
