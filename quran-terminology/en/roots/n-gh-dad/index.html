Naghḍ in early Arabic and the Qur’an: loose teeth, moving heads and the response to resurrection

The Arabic root ن غ ض concerns movement, trembling and unstable or repeated motion. It describes heads, loose teeth, camel saddles and bodily parts. Related words also describe birds’ head movements, dense clouds and similar conditions.

Its characteristic emphasis is not merely displacement, but shaking, trembling or rising and falling. A loose tooth moving in place and a head repeatedly moving up and down can both be described through naghḍ.

The root occurs once in the Qur’an: fa-sa-yunghiḍūna in Al-Isrāʾ 17:51. Those doubting resurrection are told that their original creator can recreate them; they move their heads toward the messenger and ask when it will happen.

Head movement can form part of a response to speech. Naghḍ itself does not mean denial, mockery or surprise; these follow from circumstances, accompanying words and context.

1. Semantic Core and Forms

Important Arabic forms include:

  • نَغَضَ (naghaḍa): moved, trembled or shook; in some constructions, moved something.
  • نَغْض و نُغُوض (naghḍ, nughūḍ): movement and trembling.
  • نَغَضَان (naghaḍān): shaking, tremulous or repeated movement.
  • أَنْغَضَ (anghaḍa): caused something, particularly a head, to move.
  • تَنَغَّضَ (tanaghghaḍa): moved, trembled or shook.
  • نَغَّاض (naghghāḍ): frequently moving or trembling, also describing moving clouds.
  • نَغْض و نِغْض (naghḍ, nighḍ): names for a male ostrich, particularly with reference to head movement while walking.
  • نَاغِض و نُغْض (nāghiḍ, nughḍ): names of shoulder or neck-junction parts associated with movement.
  • نَغُوض (naghūḍ): in some lexical accounts, a description of a large-humped she-camel.

Only the Form IV imperfect yunghiḍūna occurs in the Qur’an, with fa- and sa- attached as fa-sa-yunghiḍūna.

Semantic Core

Lexical explanations identify moving or causing movement as central and also emphasize trembling and unstable motion.

This is visible across the uses.

A loose tooth moves in place without changing location. A camel saddle shakes with the animal; a head can move up and down or repeatedly back and forth.

These uses share movement involving shaking, trembling or instability.

The definition should nevertheless not be restricted to vertical head motion. Other things are described through naghḍ, and direction varies.

The core is movement and shaking, especially tremulous or repeated motion. Raising and lowering the head is one clear application, not the whole family’s exclusive definition.

2. Early Arabic Uses

Head Movement

Moving the head is among the root’s familiar uses.

One expression is:

نَغَضَ بِرَأْسِهِ

naghaḍa bi-raʾsihi

Translation: “He moved his head.”

Another is:

أَنْغَضَ رَأْسَهُ

anghaḍa raʾsahu

Translation: “He caused his head to move.”

This can be upward or downward movement. Lexical explanations refer to this raising and lowering.

Head movement need not convey one fixed message. Someone might be surprised, reject a statement, mock its speaker or lean toward someone to understand.

Lexical accounts describe these varied settings.

The direct meaning is movement; intention and response require contextual evidence.

A Poetic Witness: Responding by Moving the Head

A rajaz line of uncertain authorship says:

لَمَّا رَأَتْنِي أَنْغَضَتْ لِيَ الرَّأْسَا

lammā raʾatnī anghaḍat liya al-raʾsā

Translation: “When she saw me, she moved her head toward me.”

The movement responds to seeing someone.

The witness illustrates anghaḍa with raʾs as its object. It does not itself fully explain whether the movement expressed surprise, acceptance or mockery.

That requires the full setting and the people’s relationship.

Early lexical and exegetical accounts transmit the line, but its exact authorship and date are unclear. It should not be assigned to pre-Islamic times without independent evidence.

An Ostrich’s Head Movement

Naghḍ is also an Arabic name for a male ostrich.

The explanation connects it with the bird raising and lowering its head when moving quickly.

Al-ʿAjjāj, an early Islamic poet, describes a male ostrich:

أَصَكَّ نَغْضًا لَا يَنِي مُسْتَهْدِجًا

aṣakka naghḍan lā yanī mustahdijan

Translation: “A sturdy male ostrich continually advancing with hurried, tremulous steps.”

Naghḍ names the male ostrich here, within an image of rapid movement.

The accompanying description has transmitted variants aṣakk and asakk. Their difference does not change the basic reference of naghḍ.

The animal’s name can thus be connected with a familiar movement. The lexical explanation of that connection does not by itself establish the name’s historical origin.

Loose Teeth and Camel Saddles

Arabic uses naghḍ for a tooth loose and moving in its place:

نَغَضَتِ السِّنُّ

naghaḍati al-sinnu

Translation: “The tooth loosened and moved.”

The movement usually follows loss of firmness, but weakness and old age are not necessary parts of the root’s meaning.

Naghaḍa raḥlu al-baʿīr means the camel’s saddle or riding equipment moved.

The root is therefore not confined to voluntary human motion. Outside force or loss of stability may make something shake.

“Deliberately moving something” should not define every use.

Clouds and Other Uses

Ghaymun naghghāḍun describes dense clouds whose parts move among one another without the entire cloud necessarily traveling rapidly across the sky.

This too concerns movement and shaking.

Nāghiḍ can also name a moving part near the shoulder or neck junction. It names a bodily part associated with movement, rather than the action itself.

Extended uses also describe advancing toward an enemy. This should not define every derivative: the expression may involve rising or moving forward in that particular setting.

The family extends beyond heads while retaining movement, shaking and instability in many branches.

3. Qur’anic Use: Al-Isrāʾ 17:51

أَوْ خَلْقًا مِّمَّا يَكْبُرُ فِي صُدُورِكُمْ فَسَيَقُولُونَ مَن يُعِيدُنَا قُلِ الَّذِي فَطَرَكُمْ أَوَّلَ مَرَّةٍ فَسَيُنْغِضُونَ إِلَيْكَ رُءُوسَهُمْ وَيَقُولُونَ مَتَىٰ هُوَ قُلْ عَسَىٰ أَن يَكُونَ قَرِيبًا

aw khalqan mimmā yakburu fī ṣudūrikum fa-sa-yaqūlūna man yuʿīdunā quli alladhī faṭarakum awwala marratin fa-sa-yunghiḍūna ilayka ruʾūsahum wa-yaqūlūna matā huwa qul ʿasā an yakūna qarīban

Translation: “Or a creation that seems greater in your minds. They will say: Who will restore us? Say: The One who originated you the first time. Then they will move their heads toward you and say: When is it? Say: Perhaps it is near.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:51

This continues an exchange about life after death.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:49, those finding resurrection unlikely ask how people could be recreated after becoming bones and decayed fragments.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:50 replies:

قُلْ كُونُوا حِجَارَةً أَوْ حَدِيدًا

qul kūnū ḥijāratan aw ḥadīdan

Translation: “Say: Be stones or iron.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:51 continues with any other creation that seems great and difficult to them.

The focus is the creator’s ability to restore the people He first brought into being.

They respond by moving their heads toward the messenger and asking matā huwa, “when is it?”

Fa-sa-yunghiḍūna

Yunghiḍūna is a masculine plural imperfect verb in Form IV.

Fa- links it to the preceding speech; sa- marks futurity.

Yunghiḍūna then expresses moving or causing to move, with futurity in the full construction.

Its object is ruʾūsahum, their heads.

The direct sense is that they will move their heads.

Ilayka

Ilā in fa-sa-yunghiḍūna ilayka ruʾūsahum specifies direction toward the addressee.

This adds to mere head movement. A head could tremble without reference to anyone; here it moves toward the messenger in response to his statement.

Ilā itself nevertheless means neither hostility nor mockery. These follow from the exchange and the speakers’ conduct.

Does Naghḍ Mean Mockery?

Many interpretations understand the movement here as mockery, denial or surprise.

This fits the setting of people finding recreation unlikely. After the messenger’s answer, they move their heads and ask when resurrection will occur.

Matā huwa is thus not simply a neutral request for a date; it continues their denial and incredulity.

Mockery and denial should nevertheless not become the independent lexical meaning of naghḍ.

Arabic also uses head movement in surprise or questioning. Here too the verb gives the movement and context identifies the response.

“They will mockingly move their heads” can explain the context, but a shorter direct rendering is:

“Then they will move their heads toward you.”

The preceding exchange and following statement indicate the character of their response.

The Qur’anic Reply

The verse ends:

قُلْ عَسَىٰ أَن يَكُونَ قَرِيبًا

qul ʿasā an yakūna qarīban

Translation: “Say: Perhaps it is near.”

After the disbelieving response, the Qur’an supplies another answer.

The exchange shifts from the possibility of recreation to its timing.

The first answer rests on the creator’s ability: He who first created can restore.

The final response draws attention to the event possibly being near.

Head movement forms part of their response to the resurrection argument. Naghḍ itself contains no meaning about its date, the possibility of renewed life or an assertion’s truth.

Those concerns follow from the passage as a whole.

4. Related Roots

N GH Ḍ and H Z Z

Hazz concerns moving and shaking things in various ways.

Maryam 19:25 says:

وَهُزِّي إِلَيْكِ بِجِذْعِ النَّخْلَةِ

wa-huzzī ilayki bi-jidhʿi al-nakhlati

Translation: “Shake the palm trunk toward yourself.”

The object being shaken is a tree trunk.

Naghḍ can also concern shaking, with a stronger characteristic emphasis on tremulous, unstable or repeated movement.

Both concern motion without having identical ranges.

N GH Ḍ and R J F

Rajf concerns trembling, strong shaking or movement. Qur’anic uses include the earth shaking and frightening scenes.

Naghḍ can approach rajf, as with a loose tooth or shaking camel saddle.

Rajf may describe wider, stronger trembling, whereas head-related naghḍ is more localized and specific.

This is a tendency, not an absolute rule governing all Arabic uses.

N GH Ḍ and N H Ḍ

Nahḍ concerns rising and moving, especially getting up to act.

Naghḍ is more characteristically associated with shaking and motion.

Advancing toward an enemy through naghḍ can approach nahḍ without making every sense identical.

Their phonetic resemblance alone also establishes no historical relationship.

N GH Ḍ and N Q Ḍ

Naqḍ concerns undoing, unraveling or disrupting a structure, as in breaking a covenant.

Naghḍ concerns movement and shaking.

Something may loosen through shaking and eventually collapse, but that consequence does not make the roots identical.

A loose tooth may be described with naghḍ without meaning covenant-breaking or the unraveling of a structure.

5. Translation Proposals for Forqan

For naghaḍa in movement-related uses, “moved,” “trembled” or “shook” can fit.

Naghḍ and naghaḍān can be rendered “movement,” “tremulous motion” or “trembling,” according to context.

Anghaḍa raʾsahu naturally means “he moved his head.”

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:51:

فَسَيُنْغِضُونَ إِلَيْكَ رُءُوسَهُمْ

fa-sa-yunghiḍūna ilayka ruʾūsahum

“Then they will move their heads toward you.”

This preserves the action, its direction and its object.

Explanation can add that the head movement accompanies mockery or disbelief in the resurrection setting.

“They will mock,” “they will deny” or “they will arrogantly shake their heads” should not wholly replace the action. Doing so removes the physical head movement or introduces a feature not given by the root itself.

For Forqan’s word box:

Brief root meaning: moving, shaking or causing to move, especially tremulous or repeated motion.

Meaning in Al-Isrāʾ 17:51: they will move their heads toward the addressee, contextually a disbelieving or mocking response to resurrection.

The verb’s meaning thus remains distinct from contextual interpretation.

6. Conclusion

N GH Ḍ concerns movement, trembling and shaking, as with heads, loose teeth, camel saddles and bodily parts.

The male ostrich’s name, a moving shoulder part and descriptions of dense clouds show that it is not confined to voluntary human motion.

Its sole Qur’anic occurrence is fa-sa-yunghiḍūna in Al-Isrāʾ 17:51. People doubting resurrection respond to the messenger by moving their heads toward him and asking when it will happen.

The setting implies denial and mockery, but the root itself does not mean either. Direction, addressee and tense likewise follow from the full construction rather than the independent root meaning.

Final definition: N GH Ḍ denotes movement and shaking, particularly tremulous, unstable or repeated motion. Qur’anic fa-sa-yunghiḍūna describes moving heads in response to the messenger; in the resurrection-denial setting, it expresses an incredulous or mocking reaction.
