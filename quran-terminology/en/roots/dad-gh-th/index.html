From bundles of plants to confused dreams

In early Arabic, the root ض غ ث concerns gathering and bringing together components, especially stalks and plants. ضِغْث denotes a bundle or handful of grass, plants, or slender twigs that can be held in one hand. Its components may be of one kind or consist of different plants and stalks.

A further usage draws on this tangible image: things whose components have become mixed and difficult to distinguish or separate. The expression أَضْغَاثُ أَحْلَامٍ, used for confused, intermingled dreams, belongs to this semantic field.

Both usages are clearly attested in the language of the Quranic period. Evidence also occurs in poetry attributed to pre-Islamic poets and poets who lived across the advent of Islam, although the dates of composition of these individual verses are not independently established. The material sense is well supported in early Arabic, but this does not prove that every semantic branch was equally widespread before Islam.

1. Early meaning and Arabic evidence

Gathering grass to feed animals, holding a bundle of stalks, and collecting fragrant plants provide everyday settings for the word ضِغْث.

A verse attributed to ʿAwf ibn al-Khariʿ al-Taymī reads:

وَأَسْفَلَ مِنِّي نَهْدَةً قَدْ رَبَطْتُهَا
وَأَلْقَيْتُ ضِغْثًا مِنْ خَلًى مُتَطَيِّبِ

wa-asfala minnī nahdatan qad rabaṭtuhā / wa-alqaytu ḍighthan min khalan mutaṭayyibi

Translation: “Below me I had tethered a sturdy mare, and I cast her a handful of choice herbage.”

Here ضِغْث is an actual bundle of plants: something that can be picked up and used as animal fodder. The word conveys neither confusion nor a negative judgment.

A verse attributed to Ibn Muqbil, a poet who lived both before and after the advent of Islam, contains the plural:

خَوْدٌ كَأَنَّ فِرَاشَهَا وُضِعَتْ بِهِ
أَضْغَاثُ رَيْحَانٍ غَدَاةَ شَمَالِ

khawdun ka-anna firāshahā wuḍiʿat bihi / aḍghāthu rayḥānin ghadāta shamāli

Translation: “A young woman whose bedding seems to have been strewn with bundles of fragrant herbs on a morning of north wind.”

Here أَضْغَاث is the plural of ضِغْث and refers to bundles of fragrant plants. Again, the word does not necessarily imply disorder, worthlessness, or corruption. A bundle of fragrant herbs may be pleasant and desirable, just as a bundle of grass may be gathered as fodder.

Ibn Muqbil lived into the Islamic period, but the individual dates of these verses have not been independently established in this study. They should therefore not be treated as securely dated evidence predating the Quran. They remain valuable witnesses to early Arabic and the tangible meaning of the word.

Arabic also records the proverbial expression ضِغْثٌ عَلَى إِبَّالَةٍ. An إِبَّالَة is a larger bundle of firewood; adding a ضِغْث to it figuratively represents one trouble added to another. This illustrates how gathering and adding one bundle to another can supply a figurative image. Its appearance in later sources alone does not establish the proverb’s precise date of origin.

2. Semantic core and forms

Three features should be distinguished when considering the principal semantic field of ض غ ث:

First, gathering several components, as when stalks or plants are collected into a bundle.

Second, bringing components together or intermingling them, thereby forming a collection.

Third, making a collection’s components difficult to distinguish, a feature prominent in figurative usage, especially descriptions of confused dreams.

The third feature is not essential to every usage. A bundle of fragrant herbs may be orderly and readily identifiable. “Confusion” alone is therefore not a comprehensive definition of this root.

Arabic lexical sources also record these forms:

  • ضِغْث: a bundle or handful of plants, grass, or slender stalks.
  • أَضْغَاث: the plural of ضِغْث; bundles or intermingled collections.
  • ضَغَثَ: to gather something, mix components, or grasp and press something by hand, depending on its object.
  • ضَغَّثَ: to arrange plants in bundles.
  • أَضْغَثَ الرُّؤْيَا: to recount a dream in a confused, intermingled manner.
  • ضَغُوث: a description recorded in connection with a she-camel whose hump is examined by hand to assess her condition.

The camel-hump usage deserves attention. Someone grasps, feels, or presses the hump to determine its condition. This involves handling and pressure but does not necessarily mean making a bundle or mixing several things. The recorded branches should not all be forced into a single narrow image.

In the Quran, this family appears only as the singular noun ضِغْث and its plural أَضْغَاث. The other verbs and descriptions, though recorded in Arabic, do not occur in the Quranic text.

3. Quranic usages

The root occurs three times in two forms: ضِغْثًا once and أَضْغَاثُ twice. The singular illustrates the tangible meaning of a bundle of plants; both plurals occur in the expression أَضْغَاثُ أَحْلَامٍ.

A. ضِغْث: a bundle of stalks in the account of Job

Ṣād 38:44

وَخُذْ بِيَدِكَ ضِغْثًا فَاضْرِبْ بِهِ وَلَا تَحْنَثْ ۗ إِنَّا وَجَدْنَاهُ صَابِرًا ۚ نِعْمَ الْعَبْدُ ۖ إِنَّهُ أَوَّابٌ

wa-khudh bi-yadika ḍighthan fa-ḍrib bihi wa-lā taḥnath innā wajadnāhu ṣābiran niʿma al-ʿabdu innahu awwāb

Ṣād 38:44

Translation: “Take a bundle of stalks in your hand, strike with it, and do not break your oath. We found him patient. What an excellent servant! He was ever returning.”

Here ضِغْثًا is a singular noun and the object of خُذْ. The phrase بِيَدِكَ indicates something that can be held in the hand. The pronoun in بِهِ then refers back to that ضِغْث.

This is clear Quranic evidence for the tangible meaning. Here ضِغْث is neither a confused dream nor an obscure matter, but stalks or plants gathered into a bundle held in the hand.

The verse continues Job’s account. The preceding verses speak of his suffering, his appeal to his Lord, and divine mercy towards him. Here the command to take a ضِغْث accompanies وَلَا تَحْنَثْ, concerning the breaking of an oath.

The command thus belongs to a particular situation involving an oath. The verse itself, however, does not state what Job had sworn, why he had sworn it, whom the striking concerned, or how many stalks the bundle contained.

Some exegetical accounts connect this incident with Job’s oath to strike his wife and the use of a hundred twigs. His wife, the number one hundred, and the reason for the oath are not stated in this verse. These details should not be made part of the lexical meaning of ضِغْث or treated as explicit Quranic information.

Linguistically, ضِغْث identifies an implement gathered into a bundle. Its connection with an oath and a command to strike comes from the complete sentence, not the root itself. Nor does this account alone establish a general rule authorizing punishment of people.

B. أضغاث أحلام: the king’s dream in Joseph’s account

Yūsuf 12:44

قَالُوا أَضْغَاثُ أَحْلَامٍ ۖ وَمَا نَحْنُ بِتَأْوِيلِ الْأَحْلَامِ بِعَالِمِينَ

qālū aḍghāthu aḥlāmin wa-mā naḥnu bi-taʾwīli al-aḥlāmi bi-ʿālimīn

Yūsuf 12:44

Translation: “They said: ‘Intermingled dreams; we have no knowledge of the interpretation of dreams.’”

Here أَضْغَاث is the plural of ضِغْث, combined with أَحْلَام. The image of bundles of plants is extended to dreams whose components appear intermingled and difficult to interpret.

A decisive distinction is needed: these words are spoken by the king’s entourage; they are not a divine judgment on the true nature of the dream.

In the preceding verse, the king describes seven fat cows eaten by seven lean cows, along with green and dry ears of grain. His entourage, unable to interpret the dream, calls it أَضْغَاثُ أَحْلَامٍ.

Later, Joseph interprets that same dream, describing years of plentiful harvest, difficult years, and preparations for them. The dream dismissed as confused and meaningless thus had a clear interpretation.

This distinction matters for understanding the root’s usage. In this context, أَضْغَاثُ أَحْلَامٍ is a description applied by the speakers. Their label does not establish that the dream itself was meaningless.

Nor does أَضْغَاث by itself mean lies. Intermingling, confusion, and difficulty discerning meaning underlie this usage. Whether a dream is true or false, or admits interpretation, is a separate question.

The verse illustrates how people may call something meaningless or confused simply because they cannot understand it. Their inability does not prove an absence of meaning.

C. أضغاث أحلام: the opponents’ charge against revelation

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:5

بَلْ قَالُوا أَضْغَاثُ أَحْلَامٍ بَلِ افْتَرَاهُ بَلْ هُوَ شَاعِرٌ فَلْيَأْتِنَا بِآيَةٍ كَمَا أُرْسِلَ الْأَوَّلُونَ

bal qālū aḍghāthu aḥlāmin bali iftarāhu bal huwa shāʿirun fa-l-yaʾtinā bi-āyatin ka-mā ursila al-awwalūn

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:5

Translation: “Rather, they said: ‘Confused dreams! Rather, he fabricated it! Rather, he is a poet! Let him bring us a sign, as the earlier ones were sent.’”

The same expression أَضْغَاثُ أَحْلَامٍ occurs here, but the subject is not the king’s dream. The Messenger’s opponents are judging what is revealed to him.

The preceding context shows their resistance to divine reminder and to a human being from among them being sent. Verse five presents several successive accusations:

First they describe what they hear as confused dreams. Then they call it fabrication, and then describe the Messenger as a poet.

The repetition of بَلْ and the succession of different descriptions indicate accusations rather than a stable, demonstrated understanding.

Lexically, أَضْغَاث retains the image of intermingling and confusion. Contextually, it serves to discredit revelation. The opponents try to portray the discourse confronting them as a collection of confused, incoherent impressions rather than a divine message.

This verse and Yūsuf 12:44 share an important feature: both quote أَضْغَاثُ أَحْلَامٍ as the judgment of speakers whose assessment the Quranic text does not endorse.

In Joseph’s account, the subsequent interpretation reveals the error in the entourage’s assessment. In Al-Anbiyāʾ, the context likewise presents the claim about revelation as the opponents’ objection and denial.

Neither occurrence should therefore be read as Quranic endorsement of the king’s dream being meaningless or revelation being confused. The root contributes an image of confusion and intermingling; the context determines whether that image accurately describes the subject.

4. Tangible meaning and figurative usage

A clear semantic relationship connects ضِغْثًا in Ṣād with أَضْغَاثُ أَحْلَامٍ in Yūsuf and Al-Anbiyāʾ.

In tangible usage, several stalks or twigs form a bundle. Each is part of the collection, but the whole is designated ضِغْث.

In dreams, different images and experiences may come together in ways that make their relationship difficult to discern. The expression أَضْغَاثُ أَحْلَامٍ describes this situation.

The two usages nevertheless do not share every feature. In Ṣād the bundle is tangible and graspable; in the dream expression, the subject is a collection of mental impressions and an assessment of their confusion.

The tangible sense does not imply that ضغث always describes a disorderly or worthless collection. As the poetic evidence shows, bundles of fragrant herbs can also be called أَضْغَاث.

Thus gathering and intermingling components is a prominent semantic field within this family. Confusion, difficulty discerning meaning, and a judgment of meaninglessness become prominent in the particular expression أَضْغَاثُ أَحْلَامٍ.

5. Comparison with related meanings

ض غ ث (ḍighth) compared with خ ل ط (khalṭ)

خ ل ط concerns mixing things and has a broad scope. In Al-Tawbah 9:102, خَلَطُوا عَمَلًا صَالِحًا وَآخَرَ سَيِّئًا describes mixing righteous action with bad action.

The tangible usage of ضِغْث supplies a more specific image: plants and stalks gathered into a bundle or handful. Figuratively, that image is extended to intermingled dreams.

A ضِغْث may involve gathering or intermingling, but not every خَلْط is a ضِغْث. Mixing morally different actions does not directly denote a bundle of stalks.

ض غ ث (ḍighth) compared with ق ب ض (qabḍ)

ق ب ض denotes grasping, taking hold, or taking possession. Hand-grasping is an important feature of the tangible sense of ضِغْث, but that noun also identifies the gathered collection itself.

In Ṣād 38:44, بِيَدِكَ makes the use of the hand explicit, while ضِغْثًا identifies what is to be taken. These meanings work together in the sentence but are distinct.

ض غ ث (ḍighth) compared with ح ل م (ḥulm)

حُلْم concerns dreams and what a person sees while asleep. By contrast, the tangible sense of ضِغْث is a handful of plants. Some dictionaries also record ضِغْث as a confused dream, a figurative extension that should not be generalized to every usage.

In أَضْغَاثُ أَحْلَامٍ, أَحْلَام explicitly supplies the reference to dreams, while أَضْغَاث characterizes their intermingled or confused condition.

In translating this construction, “dreams” corresponds to أَحْلَام and “intermingled” or “confused” corresponds to أَضْغَاث. A judgment that the dream is truly meaningless should not be added to the direct translation without regard to its speakers and context.

ض غ ث (ḍighth) compared with ض غ ن (ḍighn)

ض غ ن is a different root associated with resentment and concealed hostility. Its plural أَضْغَان also occurs in the Quran.

The resemblance between أَضْغَاث and أَضْغَان must not lead to their identification. The former belongs to ض غ ث and the latter to ض غ ن; their semantic fields are distinct.

6. Suggested translations for Forqan

For ضِغْثًا in Ṣād 38:44, “a bundle of stalks” is clear and appropriate. “A bundle of plants” is also possible, but “stalks” provides a more tangible image in the command to take and strike. “A handful of grass” conveys the quantity graspable in the hand but may overemphasize soft vegetation.

For أَضْغَاثُ أَحْلَامٍ in Yūsuf 12:44 and Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:5, “intermingled dreams,” “jumbled dreams,” and “confused dreams” are defensible equivalents.

“Intermingled dreams” makes the connection with the root’s image more explicit; “confused dreams” is a concise, natural alternative.

“False dreams” or “meaningless dreams” go beyond what the root alone establishes as a direct translation. They may reflect the speakers’ assessment, but the translation should not lead readers to think the Quran endorses it.

“Intermingled dreams” is a precise choice for both verses: it preserves the linguistic image while leaving questions about the dream’s truth or meaning to contextual consideration.

7. Conclusion

In tangible usage, ض غ ث concerns gathering stalks, grasses, and plant components into a bundle or handful. Early Arabic evidence and Ṣād 38:44 clearly illustrate this meaning.

Figuratively, a collection of intermingled components supplies an image for confused dreams. The expression أَضْغَاثُ أَحْلَامٍ in Yūsuf 12:44 and Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:5 illustrates that usage.

Intermingling is nevertheless distinct from falsity, worthlessness, or absence of meaning. Both verses quote speakers who consider something confused; Joseph’s account subsequently shows that assessment to have been mistaken.

A summary of this root’s principal field, encompassing its two Quranic usages, is:

ض غ ث: gathering or bringing components together, especially stalks and plants, into a bundle or handful; figuratively, the intermingling of components that become difficult to distinguish and separate.

This description explains both the tangible meaning of ضِغْث and the figurative expression أَضْغَاثُ أَحْلَامٍ without extending the negative assessment of one construction to the entire root family.
