Being occupied, engaged, and having one’s attention and time taken up: from early Arabic usage to the joyful occupation of Paradise’s inhabitants and the excuse of those who stayed behind

The root SH GH L in early Arabic concerns being occupied or engaged in an activity or matter that takes up a person’s time, attention, or capacity, limiting the opportunity or attention available for other matters. Shughl, or shughul, names this state or the activity occupying someone; shaghala means to occupy someone or keep them from attending to something else.

This usage is not necessarily about paid work or a profession. A person may be occupied with business, family care, travel, worry, recreation, study, or something enjoyable. In each case, something takes up part of their time and attention.

Being occupied does not itself determine moral value. A person may engage in something useful and necessary, or spend time on something futile and harmful. The same occupation may be appropriate in one situation and inappropriate in another if it prevents a more important responsibility from being fulfilled.

The root occurs in the Qur’an in only two forms: شُغُلٍ in Yā Sīn 36:55 and شَغَلَتْنَا in Al-Fatḥ 48:11.

In the first verse, the inhabitants of Paradise are joyfully occupied. In the second, some who stayed behind claim that their possessions and families occupied them and kept them from accompanying the Prophet.

These uses show that shughl fundamentally denotes engagement in something; whether it is pleasant, difficult, necessary, or a valid excuse cannot be established from the word alone.

1. Early Arabic usage and poetic witnesses

Occupation in contrast to free time

Arabic lexical explanations present the basic meaning of SH GH L in contrast to farāgh.

In one of its uses, farāgh concerns being free of an activity and having time available. Shughl, by contrast, describes someone whose time or attention is engaged in something.

شَغَلْتُ فُلَانًا means “I occupied someone.”

شُغِلَ فُلَانٌ بِكَذَا means that someone became occupied with a particular matter, or that it engaged them.

شُغِلْتُ عَنْكَ بِكَذَا is used when engagement with something keeps a person from attending to someone or something else.

These constructions distinguish what occupies a person from what they are kept from doing.

For example, someone may be caring for a sick person and consequently be unable to visit a friend at the appointed time. Caring for the patient is their occupation; visiting the friend is what they have been kept from doing.

The verb itself does not say which activity matters more or whether the person chose correctly.

Arabic records شُغْل, شُغُل, شَغْل, and شَغَل with different vowel patterns in this semantic family. Plurals such as أَشْغَال and شُغُول are also attested.

These pronunciation variants do not constitute separate roots in their basic use.

A poetic witness attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays says:

حَلَّتْ لِيَ الْخَمْرُ وَكُنْتُ امْرَأً

ḥallat liya al-khamru wa-kuntu imraʾan

عَنْ شُرْبِهَا فِي شُغُلٍ شَاغِلِ

ʿan shurbihā fī shughulin shāghili

Translation:

“Wine has now become permissible to me; previously I was a man kept from drinking it by an absorbing occupation.”

The literary account associated with this poem relates it to avenging the poet’s father: he is said to have forbidden himself wine until taking revenge, then to have ended that abstention. This narrative explains the poetic setting; the verse alone does not independently establish every detail of the event.

Here, شُغُلٍ شَاغِلِ denotes something important enough to keep the poet from pursuing another pleasure or desire.

The expression matters because it does not restrict shughl to daily work or an economic profession. The occupation may be a major absorbing objective that shapes someone’s attention and conduct.

The combination shughl shāghil emphasizes how absorbing that occupation is.

Shāghil here describes what occupies someone and keeps them from attending to something else.

The attribution to Imruʾ al-Qays is familiar in transmitted pre-Islamic poetry, although an independent date of composition is not established here. The poet’s statement about wine becoming permissible expresses his own situation and decision; it is not a religious ruling or evidence that wine is lawful.

A poetic witness attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā says:

وَقُلْتُ لَهُ سَدِّدْ وَأَبْصِرْ طَرِيقَهُ

wa-qultu lahu saddid wa-abṣir ṭarīqahu

وَمَا هُوَ فِيهِ عَنْ وَصَاتِيَ شَاغِلُهْ

wa-mā huwa fīhi ʿan waṣātiya shāghiluh

Translation:

“I told him: aim straight and watch its path; what he is engaged in distracts him from my advice.”

The verse belongs to a hunting scene.

The poet addresses a young attendant who is occupied with managing a horse or pursuing the quarry while receiving instructions.

Shāghiluhu here describes what occupies his attention and distracts him from the advice.

In this explanation, mā in وَمَا هُوَ فِيهِ is relative: “that which he is engaged in,” rather than a negative particle. The verse therefore affirms distraction; it does not deny it.

The witness depicts a particular situation of competing demands on attention. It does not establish that every occupation completely prevents attention to anything else.

A person can, in other situations, be occupied and still hear someone, make a decision, or undertake another responsibility.

The distinction between being occupied and total incapacity remains valid, but this verse should not be cited as a direct denial of distraction.

An absorbing concern that intervenes between desires

A verse attributed to al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī also says:

وَقَدْ حَالَ هَمٌّ دُونَ ذَلِكَ شَاغِلٌ

wa-qad ḥāla hammun dūna dhālika shāghilun

مَكَانَ الشُّغَافِ تَبْتَغِيهِ الْأَصَابِعُ

makāna al-shughāfi tabtaghīhi al-aṣābiʿu

Translation:

“An absorbing sorrow has intervened between me and that desire, in the place of the pain called shughāf, which fingers seek out.”

The source of occupation here is not physical work or a profession, but sorrow and worry.

Distress so engages the poet’s mind and heart that it stands between him and another desire.

Thus shāghil may describe something occupying a person inwardly. In the cited lexical reading, shughāf names an illness or pain, and the fingers are physicians’ fingers seeking its location.

This usage shows that early Arabic shughl could embrace outward practical matters and inward states of thought and feeling.

Al-Nābighah was a pre-Islamic poet, but an independent date for this verse is not established here. Its transmission includes shāghil and wālij and differing expressions involving shughāf. This article retains shāghil and the reading makāna al-shughāfi; the illness imagery must be distinguished from the meaning “the heart’s covering.”

Shughl and ishtighāl in Arabic

شَغَلَ is the basic three-consonant verb and may take a direct object, as when an activity occupies someone.

اشْتَغَلَ belongs to Form VIII and commonly describes becoming engaged in an activity.

For example, اشْتَغَلَ بِالْعَمَلِ may describe someone attending to work.

By contrast, شَغَلَهُ الْعَمَلُ presents the work as what occupies that person.

This changes the sentence’s perspective, but morphology alone does not make every ishtighāl voluntary or every shughl involuntary.

A person may freely begin a task and become so engaged in it that less time remains for other matters.

Arabic also uses مَشْغُول for someone occupied with an activity or situation.

شَاغِل, by contrast, may name the person or thing causing that occupation.

Modern Persian commonly uses shāghel for someone who has a job. In early Arabic, the form could describe something occupying another person.

Modern Persian likewise usually uses shoghl for paid work or a profession, whereas its historical and Qur’anic meaning is broader.

2. Qur’anic forms and the semantic core

The root SH GH L occurs in the Qur’an in two forms.

The first is the noun شُغُلٍ in Yā Sīn 36:55.

The second is the verb شَغَلَتْنَا in Al-Fatḥ 48:11.

شُغُلٍ is an indefinite genitive noun following the preposition فِي.

The Ḥafṣ reading underlying this site has شُغُل, with a ḍamma on ghayn. Other recognized readings also have شُغْل, with ghayn unvowelled. Both retain the basic meaning of occupation.

شَغَلَتْنَا is formed from the basic past-tense verb شَغَلَ. Its tāʾ marks the feminine singular, while نَا is the object pronoun meaning “us.”

In the same verse, أَمْوَالُنَا وَأَهْلُونَا supplies the subject and its coordinated noun: “our possessions and our families” are presented as what occupied the speakers.

Forms such as يَشْغَلُ, اشْتَغَلَ, مَشْغُول, شَاغِل, أَشْغَال, and شُغُول are known in Arabic but do not occur in the Qur’anic text.

The root’s semantic core can be defined as follows:

Becoming engaged, or engaging someone, in an activity or situation that takes up part of their time, attention, or capacity and may limit their opportunity to attend to other matters.

This covers both the occupied state and what causes it.

The root itself does not determine whether the occupation is voluntary or forced, beneficial or harmful, enjoyable or difficult, or a valid excuse for leaving another responsibility unfulfilled.

In the two Qur’anic occurrences, such features are supplied by context.

3. Shughul in Yā Sīn 36:55: joyful occupation

Yā Sīn 36:55 says:

إِنَّ أَصْحَابَ الْجَنَّةِ الْيَوْمَ فِي شُغُلٍ فَاكِهُونَ

inna aṣḥāba al-jannati al-yawma fī shughulin fākihūn

Yā Sīn 36:55

Translation:

“Indeed, the inhabitants of Paradise today are joyfully occupied.”

The verse continues the description of a day when people are brought forward and their deeds are judged.

Yā Sīn 36:54 states that no soul will be wronged that day and that people will be recompensed only for what they have done.

Yā Sīn 36:55 then describes the inhabitants of Paradise.

Yā Sīn 36:56, Yā Sīn 36:57, and Yā Sīn 36:58 describe their ease beside their spouses, reclining on couches, enjoying fruit and what they desire, and receiving a greeting of peace from a merciful Lord.

Their occupation therefore appears in a setting of ease, enjoyment, and joy.

The meaning of فِي شُغُلٍ

In this expression, فِي introduces the noun شُغُلٍ.

The inhabitants of Paradise are in a state of engagement: they are occupied with something.

The construction alone does not identify their particular activity.

In modern Persian, saying that someone is “busy working” may suggest economic or professional activity.

But shughl in this verse does not necessarily mean work, toil, or a task performed to earn a living.

The setting of Paradise concerns enjoyment and ease.

Consequently, rendering it “the inhabitants of Paradise are working” would narrow the meaning if readers understood a profession or daily toil.

Nor does the word imply purposeless inactivity. The Qur’an describes occupation, but in a setting different from the difficult efforts of earthly life.

The role of فَاكِهُونَ

فَاكِهُونَ belongs to F K H and here concerns joy, pleasure, and delightful enjoyment.

It clarifies the state of Paradise’s inhabitants as they are occupied.

The verse thus contains two connected ideas: occupation and joy.

Shughul does not mean joy, just as fākihūn does not mean being occupied.

One person may enjoy an earthly activity, while another suffers through an activity occupying them.

The description فَاكِهُونَ and the context of Paradise’s blessings make this occupation pleasant.

Joy should therefore not be treated as an invariant part of shughl’s lexical meaning.

Is the inhabitants’ activity specified?

The verse does not name a particular activity.

In Yā Sīn 36:56, Paradise’s inhabitants recline with their spouses in shade upon couches.

Yā Sīn 36:57 speaks of fruit and the enjoyment of what they desire.

These verses portray ease and enjoyment, but do not by themselves restrict all instances of shughul in Yā Sīn 36:55 to one activity.

For example, the expression alone cannot establish that their occupation consists exclusively of eating, conversation, recreation, or relations with spouses.

These matters may have a place within the wider description of Paradise’s blessings, but shughl itself does not exclusively designate any one of them.

The basic translation should therefore preserve occupation, while the following verses explain their pleasant state.

Occupation and ease in Paradise

An important difference between earthly life and the description of Paradise concerns occupation’s relationship to hardship and necessity.

On earth, people may undertake difficult work to secure food, shelter, and safety. Some necessary activities involve fatigue.

In Yā Sīn 36:55, however, occupation is described amid joy and blessing.

This use shows that shughl does not necessarily mean toil or pressure.

Nor does it imply that every activity must address a deficiency or produce income.

People may be occupied with reading, visiting friends, learning, playing, or a chosen activity without primarily seeking income.

These examples illustrate the breadth of occupation; they should not be presented without evidence as a definitive list of activities in Paradise.

4. Shaghalatnā in Al-Fatḥ 48:11: occupation offered as an excuse

The root’s other Qur’anic occurrence is in Al-Fatḥ 48:11:

سَيَقُولُ لَكَ الْمُخَلَّفُونَ مِنَ الْأَعْرَابِ شَغَلَتْنَا أَمْوَالُنَا وَأَهْلُونَا فَاسْتَغْفِرْ لَنَا يَقُولُونَ بِأَلْسِنَتِهِمْ مَا لَيْسَ فِي قُلُوبِهِمْ قُلْ فَمَنْ يَمْلِكُ لَكُمْ مِنَ اللَّهِ شَيْئًا إِنْ أَرَادَ بِكُمْ ضَرًّا أَوْ أَرَادَ بِكُمْ نَفْعًا بَلْ كَانَ اللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ خَبِيرًا

sa-yaqūlu laka al-mukhallafūna mina al-aʿrābi shaghalatnā amwālunā wa-ahlūnā fa-staghfir lanā yaqūlūna bi-alsinatihim mā laysa fī qulūbihim qul fa-man yamliku lakum mina allāhi shayʾan in arāda bikum ḍarran aw arāda bikum nafʿan bal kāna allāhu bi-mā taʿmalūna khabīrā

Al-Fatḥ 48:11

Translation:

“Those of the desert Arabs who stayed behind will say to you: ‘Our possessions and our families occupied us, so ask forgiveness for us.’ They say with their tongues what is not in their hearts. Say: ‘Who can avail you anything against The God if He intends harm for you, or intends benefit for you?’ Rather, The God is fully aware of what you do.”

Some desert Arabs who did not accompany the Prophet offer an excuse for their absence.

They say that their possessions and families occupied them.

The Qur’an immediately distinguishes their words from what is in their hearts.

The meaning of شَغَلَتْنَا

In شَغَلَتْنَا, the pronoun nā refers to the speakers.

أَمْوَالُنَا وَأَهْلُونَا names the two matters presented as occupying them.

Their claim directly means:

“Our possessions and our families kept us occupied.”

The sentence has a clear linguistic meaning.

Attending to possessions, livestock, land, trade, and family affairs can take up time and energy.

Caring for children, a spouse, parents, or other family members may also be a genuine and necessary responsibility.

But the intelligibility of a sentence must be distinguished from the truth of its speaker’s claim.

That “our possessions and families occupied us” makes sense does not prove that this occupation actually prevented those individuals from accompanying the Prophet.

Does the Qur’an endorse their stated occupation?

The same verse continues:

يَقُولُونَ بِأَلْسِنَتِهِمْ مَا لَيْسَ فِي قُلُوبِهِمْ

yaqūlūna bi-alsinatihim mā laysa fī qulūbihim

Al-Fatḥ 48:11

Translation:

“They say with their tongues what is not in their hearts.”

This identifies a discrepancy between their outward words and their inward reality.

Their statement should not be quoted as though the Qur’an unreservedly endorsed financial and family occupation as the real cause of their conduct.

Shaghalatnā is part of their own claim.

Al-Fatḥ 48:12 reveals another cause:

بَلْ ظَنَنْتُمْ أَنْ لَنْ يَنْقَلِبَ الرَّسُولُ وَالْمُؤْمِنُونَ إِلَى أَهْلِيهِمْ أَبَدًا

bal ẓanantum an lan yanqaliba al-rasūlu wa-l-muʾminūna ilā ahlīhim abadan

Al-Fatḥ 48:12

Translation:

“Rather, you thought that the Messenger and the believers would never return to their families.”

Their expectations concerning the fate of the Prophet and believers played a role in their conduct.

The issue is therefore more than being busy with possessions and family: their stated excuse differs from the motive the Qur’an reveals.

Shughl in Al-Fatḥ 48:11 does not mean lying or hypocrisy. The truth or falsity of this particular claim is established by the next statement and the surrounding verses.

Is caring for possessions and family blameworthy?

The verse does not establish that attending to property or family responsibilities is inherently blameworthy.

Elsewhere, the Qur’an identifies respecting family rights, providing necessities, caring for children, and treating relatives well as human responsibilities.

Thus not everyone occupied with family or business has turned away from religious duties.

The question is whether someone properly fulfilled or evaded a responsibility in their particular circumstances, and whether their stated excuse was genuine and defensible.

For example, someone may need to care for a seriously ill child and consequently be unable to join a public activity.

Another person might invoke the same “family care” while actually refraining out of fear, self-interest, or unwillingness to accept responsibility.

Both may linguistically describe occupation, but their excuses do not have equal validity.

Al-Fatḥ 48:17 explicitly removes blame from the blind, the lame, and the sick. It shows that actual capacity and circumstances matter in assessing responsibility.

Al-Fatḥ 48:11 therefore does not justify rejecting every family, bodily, or economic excuse without examination.

Excuse and responsibility are distinct

شَغَلَتْنَا concerns being occupied.

فَاسْتَغْفِرْ لَنَا expresses a request for forgiveness.

These expressions have different meanings.

Being occupied may explain how someone spent their time or opportunity, but does not by itself establish an excuse for failing another responsibility.

A person may truly have been busy yet still have been able to fulfill another duty through planning and appropriate priorities.

Conversely, a situation may be so urgent and beyond their control that the second responsibility cannot be fulfilled.

Assessing an excuse therefore requires knowledge of the facts, capacity, freedom of choice, and kind of responsibility.

In Al-Fatḥ 48:11, the Qur’an explicitly states that this group’s words do not match their hearts. That judgment about them must not become a general definition of shughl.

Attending to the verse’s audience

The verse speaks of الْمُخَلَّفُونَ مِنَ الْأَعْرَابِ: those who stayed behind among the desert Arabs.

It does not extend its judgment to all desert Arabs or everyone who ever misses a collective undertaking.

Al-Fatḥ 48:15 describes these same people’s response to the prospect of spoils, and Al-Fatḥ 48:16 describes another summons addressed to them.

This continuity shows the Qur’an assessing a particular group’s conduct in different situations.

Shughl should not become the basis of a theory about the inherent nature of desert Arabs, people with families, or property owners.

5. Distinctions from related roots

SH GH L and ʿ M L: occupation and action

ʿAmal, from ʿ M L, concerns doing something or acting.

Shughl describes a person engaged in a task or matter that takes up part of their time and attention.

The concepts are related but not identical.

An action may take little time and not prevent someone from attending to other matters.

Someone’s mind may also be deeply occupied by worry or anticipation without a particular outward action at that moment.

In Al-Fatḥ 48:11, those who stayed behind speak of being occupied by possessions and family. The verse ends by stating that The God is aware of what they “do,” تَعْمَلُونَ.

Shughl is the subject of their excuse; ʿamal concerns their actual conduct.

Claimed occupation and actual behavior therefore need separate assessment.

Forqan’s study of ʿ M L separately examines action and its relation to responsibility and moral value.

Study of ʿ M L

SH GH L and F R GH: occupation and being free

In one of its uses, farāgh concerns becoming free, an occupation ending, or time becoming available.

Shughl, by contrast, expresses engagement in an activity or situation.

Early lexical explanations present them as contrasting states.

Yet farāgh is not always absolute inactivity, and shughl is not always pressure and suffering.

Al-Sharḥ 94:7 says:

فَإِذَا فَرَغْتَ فَانْصَبْ

fa-idhā faraghta fa-nṣab

Al-Sharḥ 94:7

Translation:

“So when you are free, exert yourself.”

Becoming free here is connected with beginning another effort.

The end of one occupation can create an opportunity to attend to something else.

Shughl and farāgh must therefore be understood in relation to someone’s activity and situation, rather than as absolute states, one always good and the other always bad.

SH GH L and L H W: occupation and diversion

Lahw concerns engaging with something that may divert attention from something else.

Shughl has a wider field and may describe engagement with any activity or situation.

Not every occupation is lahw.

Caring for a patient, doing necessary work, or studying may occupy someone, but cannot be called lahw merely because they are occupations.

Al-Takāthur 102:1 says:

أَلْهَاكُمُ التَّكَاثُرُ

alhākumu al-takāthur

Al-Takāthur 102:1

Translation:

“Rivalry in increase has diverted you.”

Here, striving for more is presented as a cause of distraction.

Al-Nūr 24:37 also describes men whom trade and sale do not divert from remembrance of The God, establishing ṣalāh, and giving zakāh.

That verse does not condemn trade in itself. It describes people who do not let economic activity divert them from other responsibilities.

An occupation can therefore be legitimate and beneficial, while in a particular situation engagement in one matter may prevent a more important responsibility from being fulfilled.

SH GH L and GH F L: occupation and heedlessness

Ghaflah concerns inattention, something being absent from awareness, or failing to attend to a matter.

Shughl concerns engagement in an activity or situation.

They may coincide but are not identical.

A person may be so occupied with one matter that they become heedless of another.

Yet someone may remain aware of other responsibilities while working and attend to them at the appropriate time.

Not every occupation is therefore heedlessness.

Conversely, heedlessness does not necessarily result from too much work. Someone with abundant free time may fail to attend to an important matter.

Forqan’s study of GH F L clarifies this distinction through the Qur’anic uses of heedlessness.

Study of GH F L

SH GH L and F K H: occupation and enjoyment

In Yā Sīn 36:55, F K H concerns joy and enjoyment.

Shughl in the same verse concerns occupation.

They describe aspects of the same situation without having the same meaning.

On earth, a person may enjoy an activity or become tired and sorrowful through it.

In Yā Sīn 36:55, فَاكِهُونَ makes clear that Paradise’s inhabitants are joyfully occupied.

SH GH L alone therefore does not establish that every occupation is enjoyable.

SH GH L and SH GH F: occupation and deep attachment

Shughl and shaghaf resemble each other in writing and sound but belong to distinct roots.

Shughl concerns engagement in an activity or situation.

In its Qur’anic use, shaghaf concerns deep attachment as love reaches into someone’s heart.

Deep attachment to someone may also occupy a person’s mind and time, but this overlap does not make the roots identical.

Someone may be engaged in a task without feeling deeply attached to it.

Someone deeply attached to another person may also regulate their conduct and keep that feeling from displacing all their responsibilities.

Occupation and attachment are distinct concepts that can influence each other in some circumstances.

Study of SH GH F

6. Occupation and responsibility: being busy versus having a valid excuse

An important distinction is between “being occupied” and “being entitled to leave something else undone.”

People face many responsibilities. Economic work, family, health, learning, caring for others, and social commitments may all require time and energy.

Not all responsibilities can always be fulfilled simultaneously or equally.

Sometimes priorities must be set.

For example, someone may work to provide for their family. That activity is a proper responsibility in its place.

But if excessive interest in accumulating wealth leads them to disregard family members’, employees’, or others’ rights, a different issue arises.

Economic activity itself is not the wrong; the person’s respect for rights and priorities must be assessed.

Conversely, someone may genuinely face an emergency and be unable to fulfill a responsibility. Actual incapacity should not be equated with making excuses.

Al-Fatḥ 48:11 gives a specific example of occupation offered as an excuse, but explicitly states that these speakers’ words do not match their inward reality.

Yā Sīn 36:55, by contrast, describes occupation amid joy and enjoyment.

Taken together, the verses do not establish that shughl always causes heedlessness or that every occupation is commendable.

Its value depends on the activity, the person’s circumstances, and its relation to other responsibilities.

Being occupied is a state; whether a person’s choice and behavior are right is a separate question.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Shughl may be rendered “occupation,” “engagement,” “what someone is occupied with,” or, where appropriate, “being involved in a task.”

In modern Persian, shoghl commonly means a profession or paid work. Using that word without explanation can therefore narrow the Arabic meaning in some verses.

In Yā Sīn 36:55, “occupation” is more appropriate than “work” understood as a profession.

Proposed translation:

“Indeed, the inhabitants of Paradise today are joyfully occupied.”

In this rendering, “occupied” conveys شُغُل, while “joyfully” conveys فَاكِهُونَ.

Explanatory prose may say that Paradise’s inhabitants are engaged in desired enjoyments, but the basic translation should not replace shughl with a specific activity the verse does not name.

For شَغَلَتْنَا in Al-Fatḥ 48:11, “occupied us” is an appropriate rendering.

Translation of the expression:

“Our possessions and our families occupied us, so ask forgiveness for us.”

“Excuse” should not be inserted into the verb’s translation: shaghalatnā does not itself mean to make an excuse.

The insincerity of their words is established by the continuation of the verse.

“Occupied us” also does not necessarily mean completely incapacitated us. Explaining that they presented occupation as the cause of staying behind depends on the whole statement and their circumstances.

For non-Qur’anic forms, shāghil may mean “occupying” or “keeping someone from another task,” while mashghūl describes someone engaged in an activity or situation.

These uses should preserve the distinction between occupation generally and an economic profession.

8. Conclusion

In early Arabic, SH GH L concerns being occupied and having one’s time, attention, or capacity taken up.

It contrasts with free time, but is not necessarily restricted to paid work or physical activity.

Poetic witnesses attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays, Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā, and al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī show occupation involving an important objective, practical activity, or worry and sorrow.

The root occurs in only two forms in the Qur’an.

In Yā Sīn 36:55, shughul describes the occupied state of Paradise’s inhabitants, while فَاكِهُونَ establishes their joyful enjoyment.

In Al-Fatḥ 48:11, shaghalatnā occurs in the words of some who stayed behind and presented possessions and family as occupying them. The continuation makes clear that their words do not match their hearts.

These uses show that being occupied is not itself a virtue or a sin.

Shughl is related to action, free time, diversion, heedlessness, and deep attachment, but is not fully synonymous with any of them. Someone may be occupied while respecting other responsibilities, or use a real or claimed occupation to set responsibility aside.

The resulting definition is:

SH GH L denotes becoming engaged, or engaging someone, in an activity, matter, or situation that takes up their time, attention, or capacity and may limit their opportunity to attend to other matters. Shughl names this occupation, and shaghala is the verb “to occupy.” The kind of activity, its pleasantness or unpleasantness, and the rightness or wrongness of conduct are not determined by the root itself; they must be established from context.

For Forqan’s Persian translation, mashghūliyyat, “occupation,” and mashghūl sākhtan, “to occupy,” are clear equivalents of shughul and shaghala. A profession, pleasant recreation, failure to fulfill responsibility, or a valid excuse should be specified only where sentence structure and context support it.
