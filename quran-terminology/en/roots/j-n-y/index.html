Qur’anic Root J N Y

Picking, harvesting, and obtaining the yield

The Arabic root ج ن ي concerns picking or obtaining something from where it grows or originates. Its clearest image is picking fruit from a tree when the fruit can be harvested and enjoyed. Other Arabic uses extend to gathering a yield, acquiring something, and even committing an act with harmful consequences.

The Qur’an uses the forms جَنِيًّا and جَنَى. Both concern fruit and enjoying it: fresh dates in Mary’s story, and the fruit of the two gardens of Paradise.

Quranic Arabic Corpus:two nominal and adjectival uses

1. Forms and derivatives

The family’s basic verb is جَنَى ـ يَجْنِي: picking fruit or obtaining something from its source. It is a final-weak yā verb: its third radical is yā, written as alif maqṣūrah in the perfect جَنَى.

Important derivatives include:

  • جَنَى ـ يَجْنِي: picked, harvested, obtained.
  • جَنْي: the act of picking and harvesting.
  • جَنًى: what is gathered or harvested, especially fruit and tree produce.
  • جَنِيّ: freshly picked fruit, or fruit at a suitable stage for gathering.
  • اِجْتَنَى ـ يَجْتَنِي: picked, gathered, harvested from its source.
  • أَجْنَى الشَّجَرُ: the tree’s fruit became ready for picking.
  • جَانٍ: someone who gathers, or in another branch commits a harmful act.
  • جِنَايَة: committing an offense, wrong, or act bringing harm and responsibility.
  • تَجَنَّى عَلَيْهِ: attributed a sin or wrong to someone who had not committed it.

Of these, the Qur’an uses only the noun جَنَى and the adjective جَنِيًّا. The identically written perfect verb جَنَى, active participle جَانٍ, noun جِنَايَة, and the other listed derivatives do not occur in the Qur’an.

Both Qur’anic uses retain the sense of fruit and its harvesting.

2. Semantic core and early Arabic usage

Gathering something from its source

The root’s clear core is taking or gathering something from its source. In the principal physical use, a person picks fruit from a branch. What is gathered is called جَنَى; when fresh or gathered at the appropriate time, it can be described as جَنِيّ. Brief root meaning: Picking and gathering a yield from its source; Qur’anic uses concern pickable fruit and fruit fresh or ready for gathering.

Ibn Fāris in Maqāyīs al-Lughah identifies the root’s primary sense as taking fruit from its tree. Al-Jawharī in Al-Ṣiḥāḥ likewise describes janā as what is gathered from a tree or another source.

Maqāyīs:picking fruit and gathering at its proper time

Classical Arabic does not restrict this use to tree fruit. The family also describes gathering desert truffles, honey, and even gold from a mine. The central act remains taking or collecting something from where it originates or is stored, although these things are not all plucked from branches.

Lexical sources:fruit,truffles,honey,and gold

Janā is therefore not simply the name of one fruit or crop. It can indicate a gathered yield; what is gathered determines the precise sense.

Early linguistic witnesses

Lexical sources record the proverb:

هَذَا جَنَايَ وَخِيَارُهُ فِيهِ

hādhā janāya wa-khiyāruhu fīhi

That is: “This is what I have gathered, and its best remains within it.”

The saying concerns gathering produce without withholding its best part from another. Some sources attribute it to ʿAmr ibn ʿAdī al-Lakhmī; that attribution and its accompanying story should nevertheless not, without independent evidence, be treated as a securely documented pre-Islamic occurrence.

Ibn Qutaybah:the hādhā janāya proverb and transmitted story

Another witness, by the mukhaḍram poet Abū Dhuʾayb al-Hudhalī, appears in the Dīwān of the Hudhalī Poets:

وَكِلَاهُمَا قَدْ عَاشَ عِيشَةَ مَاجِدٍ
وَجَنَى الْعَلَاءَ لَوْ أَنَّ شَيْئًا يَنْفَعُ

wa-kilāhumā qad ʿāsha ʿīshata mājidin / wa-janā al-ʿalāʾa law anna shayʾan yanfaʿu

That is: “Both lived the life of a noble man and attained eminence—if anything could have availed against death.”

Here جَنَى no longer means picking fruit: it concerns acquiring eminence and nobility. The poet uses the harvesting verb for human achievement.

Abū Dhuʾayb lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, but this particular line’s date is not independently established here. It is evidence for Arabic near the period of revelation, rather than definite proof of this expression’s pre-Qur’anic use.

Dīwān of the Hudhalī Poets:janā al-ʿalāʾ and the gloss about escaping death

Lexical sources also record جَنَى عَلَى for causing harm or bringing an adverse consequence upon another. The preposition عَلَى and the verb’s complement are important in determining the meaning.

From gathering a yield to committing an offense

An important branch of this family appears in جِنَايَة.

As people gather a crop from a tree, they can figuratively obtain the outcome of their conduct. That outcome is not always beneficial: an act may bring harm upon oneself or others.

In جَنَى عَلَى نَفْسِهِ, someone acts so that the harm and consequences return upon themselves. In جَنَى عَلَى غَيْرِهِ, the harm falls upon another.

The related word جِنَايَة is established in Arabic for criminal or harmful conduct. جَانٍ then names the person committing it.

تَجَنَّى عَلَيْهِ has another sense: attributing an error or offense to someone who did not commit it. Wrongful accusation differs from actually committing an offense.

The semantic relationship with gathering and obtaining a yield can be explained, but cannot establish a definite history for every derivative. Arabic dictionaries record the meanings; their chronological development is not fully established in every case.

Al-Rāghib and lexical sources:yield and jināyah

Most importantly, the legal and moral sense of jināyah must not be transferred to Qur’anic جَنَى and جَنِيّ, whose contexts differ entirely.

3. Qur’anic uses

Maryam 19:25: fresh dates ready for picking

Verse text:

وَهُزِّي إِلَيْكِ بِجِذْعِ النَّخْلَةِ تُسَاقِطْ عَلَيْكِ رُطَبًا جَنِيًّا

wa-huzzī ilayki bi-jidhʿi al-nakhlati tusāqiṭ ʿalayki ruṭaban janiyyan

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“Shake the trunk of the date palm toward yourself, so that fresh, ripe dates fall upon you.”

جَنِيًّا qualifies رُطَبًا. Ruṭab names fresh, soft dates; janiyy adds their freshness and fitness for gathering at the appropriate time. Classical explanations also analyze it as faʿīl with a passive sense, “freshly gathered,” alongside “fit to be picked.”

Lexically, جَنِيّ can also describe freshly picked fruit. Here the dates fall after the tree is shaken, so the word need not mean they were picked before that event. Ripe fruit ready to be gathered suits the scene; describing it as fresh when gathered does not imply an earlier harvest.

Al-Ṭabarī:janiyy as majniyy and freshness when gathered

Two features appear together: ruṭab specifies the dates’ type and condition, while janiyy emphasizes freshness and readiness for gathering.

The combination depicts fruit becoming available to Mary in good condition at the appropriate time.

The context of Maryam 19:23, Maryam 19:24, Maryam 19:25, and Maryam 19:26 also matters. Mary is in distress, and the verse commands her to shake the palm trunk. Fresh dates fall as a result. The image is thus more than the existence of a fruit-bearing tree: it concerns obtaining ready food in that situation.

The lexical meaning of janiyy alone does not determine the kind of miracle, the exact season, or the biological process by which the dates ripened. These require separate examination and independent evidence.

Al-Raḥmān 55:54: fruit close at hand

Verse text:

مُتَّكِئِينَ عَلَىٰ فُرُشٍ بَطَائِنُهَا مِنْ إِسْتَبْرَقٍ وَجَنَى الْجَنَّتَيْنِ دَانٍ

muttakiʾīna ʿalā furushin baṭāʾinuhā min istabraqin wa-janā al-jannatayni dānin

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“They recline on couches whose linings are of istabraq, and the pickable fruit of the two gardens is near.”

جَنَى is a noun here, not the identically written perfect verb جَنَى. It means the yield or fruit that can be gathered from the two gardens’ trees.

Grammatically, جَنَى is the subject and دَانٍ its predicate. الْجَنَّتَيْنِ is the genitive complement of janā: the fruit of the two gardens.

The sentence therefore means that the gardens’ harvestable produce is near.

The roles of جَنَى and دَانٍ must remain distinct. Janā, from ج ن ي, concerns fruit and harvestable produce; dān, from د ن و, supplies proximity.

Nearness and accessibility are consequently not the independent meaning of ج ن ي: دَانٍ adds that feature to the sentence.

The preceding verses describe gardens, branches, springs, and kinds of fruit. Al-Raḥmān 55:54 completes the picture with the fruit’s proximity. The gardens’ yield is not merely present and abundant, but near enough to enjoy.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:23 contains قُطُوفُهَا دَانِيَةٌ, while Al-Insān 76:14 depicts nearby fruit made easy to reach. Their imagery of gathering and accessing produce relates to Al-Raḥmān 55:54, although the lexical roots differ.

جَنَى الْجَنَّتَيْنِ دَانٍ should therefore not be reduced to fruit merely existing in two gardens: it combines pickable produce and its proximity.

Al-Ṭabarī:pickable fruit and its proximity

4. Distinguishing related roots

ج ن ي (جَنَى) and ث م ر (ثَمَر): harvesting and produce

ث م ر concerns fruit, yield, and the outcome of growth. ثَمَر can name fruit on a tree or the result of fertility, whether already picked or not.

جَنَى presents fruit and produce from the perspective of picking and harvesting. Both words can thus refer to the same thing with different emphases.

Thamar emphasizes the yield itself; janā foregrounds its relationship to harvesting.

This does not mean every janā was previously picked or every thamar is still on a tree. Context establishes the produce’s actual stage.

For a broader examination of fruit and yield, see Forqan’s study of ث م ر.

Study of TH M R

ج ن ي (جَنَى) and ق ط ف (قَطَفَ): two expressions for picking

قَطَفَ also concerns picking fruit; قُطُوف can indicate fruit or clusters available for gathering.

Janā and qaṭf genuinely overlap. The difference should not be made absolute, as though one concerned only picking and the other only produce: both families can concern the act and its yield.

In Al-Raḥmān 55:54, janā names the pickable yield, whereas قُطُوف in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:23 uses another family for harvestable fruit.

Both receive a description of nearness, creating similar images of easily accessible produce.

ج ن ي (جَنَى) and ج ن ن (جَنَّ): written similarity, different roots

Study of J N N

A key distinction separates janā from جَنَّة and جِنّ.

Janā consists of jīm, nūn, and yā; jannah and jinn belong to ج ن ن.

Al-Raḥmān 55:54 places the families together: جَنَى and الْجَنَّتَيْنِ.

Their pleasing sound pattern does not make them the same root. Janā indicates pickable fruit; al-jannatayn identifies the two gardens.

Janiyy in Maryam must likewise not be confused with جِنِّيّ, a being of the jinn. Different vowels and root structure distinguish them.

ج ن ي (جَنَى) and ج ر م (جَرَمَ): offense and wrongdoing

Study of J R M

Arabic جِنَايَة concerns crime and harm. Some uses of ج ر م likewise concern wrongdoing and committing an offense.

The overlap of jināyah and jurm in legal language does not make every derivative of the roots equivalent.

Both Qur’anic uses of ج ن ي concern fruit, not crime. Qur’anic ج ر م must likewise be studied through its own constructions and contexts.

5. Points for understanding and translation

First, distinguish the noun جَنَى from the verb جَنَى. Their written forms coincide, but in Al-Raḥmān 55:54 it is a noun and cannot be translated “picked.”

Second, janiyy does not always mean only “ripe.” Lexical sources also include freshness and gathering at the appropriate time. Maryam 19:25 should therefore preserve the dates’ ripeness and freshness as far as possible.

Third, janā in Al-Raḥmān 55:54 concerns harvestable fruit, while dān expresses its nearness. Translation must not attribute dān’s meaning to ج ن ي itself.

Fourth, the family’s semantic branch jināyah, important in Arabic usage, provides no basis for inserting crime, sin, or punishment into its two Qur’anic verses.

Fifth, janā’s appearance beside al-jannatayn does not establish a shared root. Similar sound and the relationship between fruit and garden differ from common derivation.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

For رُطَبًا جَنِيًّا in Maryam 19:25, “fresh, ripe dates” is natural and appropriate. Lexical explanation can add janiyy’s emphasis on freshness and readiness for picking.

For جَنَى الْجَنَّتَيْنِ دَانٍ in Al-Raḥmān 55:54, “the pickable fruit of the two gardens is near” more explicitly preserves the root’s sense. “The fruit of the two gardens is near” is also natural, though less explicit about harvesting.

The principal lexical equivalents are “picking,” “harvesting,” and “pickable yield.” “Obtaining” suits broader figurative uses, but must not replace the specific fruit sense in the Qur’anic verses.

7. Conclusion

The root ج ن ي centers on picking or gathering a yield from its source. Tree fruit supplies its clearest physical image; Arabic also uses it for gathering other things and acquiring achievements, without this relationship alone establishing a precise chronology.

The jināyah branch shows that ethical and legal uses can concern harmful conduct and its consequences. That branch nevertheless does not determine the root’s two Qur’anic meanings.

In Maryam 19:25, جَنِيًّا describes fresh dates ready for gathering. In Al-Raḥmān 55:54, جَنَى names the gardens’ pickable fruit, with دَانٍ describing it as near and accessible.

Both verses thus concern fruit and enjoying a yield: fresh dates fall for Mary in one; the fruit of two gardens is near in the other. Both preserve the root’s clear, tangible Qur’anic sense.
