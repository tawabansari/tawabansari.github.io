Scattering and becoming scattered: from strewing seeds and pearls to dispersed dust and stars

1. Introducing the root and its semantic core

The Arabic root N TH R concerns scattering, strewing, and dispersing objects or parts across different places. نَثَرَ means scattered, sprinkled, or strewed; اِنْتَثَرَ means became scattered or dispersed.

Clear images include seeds scattered on the ground, pearls dispersed across a place, and fine particles spread through the air.

In these uses, objects or parts occupy different places. This does not necessarily mean destruction, worthlessness, or disorder. Seeds scattered for planting can be useful; scattered pearls remain valuable; airborne dust presents a different image of dispersion. Nor does the word alone establish that the objects were previously strung together or arranged in a particular way.

Scattering and becoming scattered form a defensible semantic core, rather than destruction or loss of value. Other features depend on what is scattered and the context.

Qur’anic forms

N TH R occurs three times in the Qur’an, in two morphological forms:

  • مَنْثُورًا: scattered or strewn; Al-Furqān 25:23 and Al-Insān 76:19.
  • اِنْتَثَرَتْ: became scattered or dispersed; Al-Infiṭār 82:2.

مَنْثُور is a Form I passive participle; اِنْتَثَرَتْ is a Form VIII perfect verb.

Arabic also attests نَثْر, نِثَار, نُثَارَة, نَاثِر, مُنَثَّر, تَنَاثَرَ, and اِسْتَنْثَرَ, but none of these forms occurs in the Qur’an.

The simple verb نَثَرَ is also absent. All three Qur’anic occurrences therefore concern becoming scattered or the state of something scattered, rather than directly describing a person strewing something through the simple verb.

2. Early Arabic usage and branches of meaning

Scattering objects and strewing seeds

Early Arabic uses nathr for scattering objects such as seeds, walnuts, almonds, coins, and pearls.

Scattering something by hand is one clear use. The semantic core can be expressed in Arabic as follows:

أَصْلٌ صَحِيحٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى إِلْقَاءِ شَيْءٍ مُتَفَرِّقٍ

aṣlun ṣaḥīḥun yadullu ʿalā ilqāʾi shayʾin mutafarriqin

Meaning: “A sound root denoting casting something in a dispersed manner.”

نَثَرَ الْحَبَّ means strewing seeds. In cultivation, seeds move from a gathered location to different points in the soil.

In نَثَرَ الدَّرَاهِمَ, coins are scattered; in نَثَرَ اللُّؤْلُؤَ, pearls are strewn or scattered.

These examples share the separation of items from a gathered state and their dispersion.

Their purposes nevertheless differ. Someone may sow seeds, scatter coins among people, or strew pearls over a surface. The root itself does not determine the purpose.

Nathr does not necessarily mean forceful throwing. Seeds gently released from a farmer’s hand can also be described this way.

Nithār and scattering objects at social occasions

نِثَار can name the act of scattering and sometimes the things scattered among people on an occasion.

شَهِدْتُ نِثَارَ فُلَانٍ means being present at someone’s occasion where things were scattered among the participants.

These might include sweets, food, or other objects.

Scattering thus also has ordinary social uses without necessarily implying damage, waste, or destruction.

An attested classical use does not alone establish the precise origin of every associated custom. Its details cannot all be assigned to the pre-Islamic period without independent evidence.

Nuthārah: what has become scattered or detached

نُثَارَة names things detached and scattered from a larger collection.

Examples include crumbs falling around a meal and grains spilling from a container.

Attention shifts here from the act of scattering to its result.

The scattered material may have little value, but low value is not necessary to nuthārah or the root itself. Elsewhere, scattering can concern precious pearls.

This matters especially for the two Qur’anic uses of مَنْثُورًا.

A poetic witness

A transmitted half-line contains نَثَرًا:

حَدَّ النَّهَارِ تُرَاعِي ثِيرَةً نَثَرًا

ḥadda al-nahāri turāʿī thīratan natharan

Approximate sense:

“During the day, she watches scattered cattle.”

نَثَرًا describes a group of animals as scattered. Dispersion is therefore not restricted to seeds or small objects.

The incomplete lexical quotation does not establish the poet or its date. It should not be assigned unconditionally to a pre-Islamic poet or treated as securely dated pre-Qur’anic evidence. Here it illustrates a use preserved in Arabic lexical tradition. The incomplete witness also does not justify fixing its opening time expression as “until the end of the day.”

Other uses of the root

Arabic also uses nathr for material coming from an animal’s nose. نَثِير names something resembling a sneeze in certain animals; اِسْتَنْثَرَ concerns expelling water or other material from the nose.

نَثُور can describe a woman who bears many children; نَثَرَ الْكَلَامَ concerns producing abundant speech.

Releasing or dispersing something from one location offers an intelligible connection between these uses. They should nevertheless not all be assigned a single certain historical origin.

In literary terminology, نَثْر also means discourse not bound by poetic meter and rhyme, in contrast to verse.

The image behind the contrast with naẓm is clear: naẓm strings or arranges parts together, whereas prose lacks that particular metrical and rhyming bond. Prose does not therefore mean confused or structureless speech; it can have precise organization and coherence.

None of the root’s Qur’anic occurrences concerns literary prose. All three concern scattering or becoming scattered.

3. Qur’anic uses of N TH R

A. Al-Furqān 25:23: scattered dust

وَقَدِمْنَا إِلَىٰ مَا عَمِلُوا مِنْ عَمَلٍ فَجَعَلْنَاهُ هَبَاءً مَنْثُورًا

wa-qadimnā ilā mā ʿamilū min ʿamalin fa-jaʿalnāhu habāʾan manthūran

Translation:

“We turn to whatever deeds they have done and make them scattered dust.”

Al-Furqān 25:23

مَنْثُورًا describes هَبَاءً here.

Habāʾ refers to fine dust particles, sometimes visible in a beam of light, suspended or dispersed in the air.

Manthūran describes their state: dust that has been scattered.

The words should therefore be distinguished. Habāʾ names the dust or particles; manthūr describes their dispersion.

Translating manthūran directly as “destroyed” would assign it a meaning it does not express independently.

Scattered dust and human deeds

The verse concerns a group’s deeds. The preceding verses discuss people who do not expect to meet The God and make arrogant demands.

In this context, verse 23 presents their deeds’ outcome through the image of scattered dust.

The image conveys deeds becoming fruitless in divine judgment. Their performers may have expected value or benefit from them, yet they do not obtain the desired result in that scene.

The image’s meaning should nevertheless be distinguished from the verse’s moral judgment.

Dispersion is not inherently worthlessness. Here, scattered dust together with the judgment of deeds conveys their fruitlessness.

This does not establish that everything dispersed in nature has been destroyed or rendered worthless.

Nor does it establish an unlimited rule that every apparently good deed by every person is necessarily worthless, regardless of intention, faith, justice, circumstances, and divine judgment. The context concerns a particular group; broader questions about deeds require consideration of the Qur’an as a whole.

Do deeds actually become material particles?

The verse says:

فَجَعَلْنَاهُ هَبَاءً مَنْثُورًا

fa-jaʿalnāhu habāʾan manthūran

“We make it scattered dust.”

The construction vividly presents deeds being made into scattered dust.

It does not justify concluding without evidence that deeds must undergo a physical process turning them into dust particles in the hereafter.

It concerns the outcome and yield of deeds in divine judgment, expressed through the image of scattered dust.

The image should remain in translation without being expanded into unstated material or scientific details.

B. Al-Insān 76:19: scattered pearls

وَيَطُوفُ عَلَيْهِمْ وِلْدَانٌ مُخَلَّدُونَ إِذَا رَأَيْتَهُمْ حَسِبْتَهُمْ لُؤْلُؤًا مَنْثُورًا

wa-yaṭūfu ʿalayhim wildānun mukhalladūna idhā raʾaytahum ḥasibtahum luʾluʾan manthūran

Translation:

“Everlasting youths circulate among them; when you see them, you would think them scattered pearls.”

Al-Insān 76:19

This is the second occurrence of manthūran.

It described dust in Al-Furqān; here it describes pearls.

لُؤْلُؤًا names pearls, and مَنْثُورًا describes their scattered state.

In حَسِبْتَهُمْ, the pronoun hum is the first object, while لُؤْلُؤًا is the second; مَنْثُورًا modifies the pearls. The syntax distinguishes those being perceived from the image through which they appear.

The comparison refers to the youths mentioned at the beginning: they appear to the viewer like scattered pearls.

Why scattered pearls?

Pearls were familiar and valuable in Arabic life. Their beauty and luster provided a fitting basis for comparison.

The pearls are described as scattered rather than strung together.

This suits the youths circulating among the Garden’s inhabitants. They are not fixed at one point, but present and moving about in the surroundings.

The scattered pearls, together with their beauty and luster, can thus picture the youths’ presence in different places.

Their exact number, movement, and distribution cannot be extracted from manthūran.

Has manthūr acquired another meaning here?

Its basic meaning remains the same in both verses: scattered.

What differs is the thing being described.

In Al-Furqān 25:23, scattered dust conveys the fruitlessness of deeds.

In Al-Insān 76:19, scattered pearls form part of an image of blessing, beauty, and the Garden’s abundance.

Defining manthūr as “worthless” or “destroyed” would therefore be contradicted by the pearls.

Defining it as “beautiful and shining” would likewise fail to explain the dust: these features are not the root’s independent meaning.

Manthūr is neither inherently blameworthy nor praiseworthy. It means scattered; the value and position of what is scattered follow from the noun and context.

These verses demonstrate why all Qur’anic uses should be considered before settling a root’s meaning.

C. Al-Infiṭār 82:2: celestial bodies becoming scattered

وَإِذَا الْكَوَاكِبُ انْتَثَرَتْ

wa-idhā al-kawākibu intatharat

Translation:

“When the stars become scattered.”

Al-Infiṭār 82:2

Unlike the preceding occurrences, this one is a verb.

اِنْتَثَرَتْ is Form VIII and concerns the kawākib becoming scattered.

Kawākib is a nonhuman plural, with a feminine singular verb, consistent with a familiar Arabic agreement pattern.

The subject is celestial bodies becoming scattered, rather than someone strewing seeds, coins, or pearls.

Connection with the surrounding verses

The opening of Al-Infiṭār helps explain this use:

إِذَا السَّمَاءُ انْفَطَرَتْ

idhā al-samāʾu infaṭarat

“When the sky splits open.”

Al-Infiṭār 82:1

وَإِذَا الْكَوَاكِبُ انْتَثَرَتْ

wa-idhā al-kawākibu intatharat

“When the stars become scattered.”

Al-Infiṭār 82:2

وَإِذَا الْبِحَارُ فُجِّرَتْ

wa-idhā al-biḥāru fujjirat

“When the seas are opened and set flowing.”

Al-Infiṭār 82:3

وَإِذَا الْقُبُورُ بُعْثِرَتْ

wa-idhā al-qubūru buʿthirat

“When the graves are turned over.”

Al-Infiṭār 82:4

عَلِمَتْ نَفْسٌ مَا قَدَّمَتْ وَأَخَّرَتْ

ʿalimat nafsun mā qaddamat wa-akhkharat

“Each soul will know what it sent ahead and what it left behind.”

Al-Infiṭār 82:5

The sequence describes changes in the sky, celestial bodies, seas, and graves, then turns to a person’s awareness of their deeds.

The scattered kawākib belong to this tremendous change preceding the reckoning.

The other verses’ details should not all be inserted into intatharat. The sky’s splitting comes from infaṭarat, the graves’ upheaval from buʿthirat, and the celestial bodies’ dispersion from intatharat.

Does intatharat mean falling?

Some translations render it as “falling down” or “falling away.”

Scattering and falling is also a recognized explanation in lexical and interpretive discussion of this context, rather than necessarily an error. Yet the shared meaning is dispersion; the verb alone does not establish a particular direction or that the stars fall toward the earth.

Scattering does not necessarily have a fixed direction. Something may disperse outward without necessarily moving downward.

Intatharat alone therefore does not establish that the stars fall toward the earth.

Nor does it directly mean extinguishing, exploding, complete destruction, or becoming fine particles.

Various possibilities about the nature of this change can be discussed, but should remain distinct from the word’s clear meaning.

Does the verse express an astronomical theory?

It describes a change concerning the sky and celestial bodies within the scene of the hereafter.

It does not explain their motion, the forces involved, distances, duration, or physical mechanism.

The word alone cannot prove a theory about cosmic expansion, gravitational collapse, stellar explosions, or the destruction of celestial systems.

Conversely, uncertainty about the physical mechanism does not justify turning the entire image into an unsupported psychological or social allegory.

It is preferable to retain the direct meaning of celestial bodies becoming scattered, without claiming certainty about the unstated mechanism.

4. Morphological constructions and the role of context

Nathara: scattering something

The Form I verb can be transitive: an agent scatters something.

In نَثَرَ الْحَبَّ, for example, someone scatters seeds.

The act and the thing scattered are distinguishable in this construction.

Manthūr: something scattered

Manthūr is a passive participle describing something subjected to scattering or in a scattered state.

It describes dust in Al-Furqān 25:23 and pearls in Al-Insān 76:19.

The participle alone does not identify the agent, time, or cause.

In Al-Furqān, فَجَعَلْنَاهُ states The God’s role in the deeds’ outcome. Al-Insān does not identify an agent scattering the pearls used in the comparison, since it describes an appearance rather than reporting an actual act of scattering pearls.

Intathara: becoming scattered

Intathara expresses the occurrence of dispersion.

In Al-Infiṭār 82:2, the kawākib undergo this event.

Unlike transitive nathara, the sentence directly describes their becoming scattered rather than an agent scattering them.

Form VIII does not establish that the celestial bodies scatter voluntarily or independently of any other cause.

The construction determines how the event is expressed; it does not itself settle its physical agent or ultimate cause.

Nor does Form VIII necessarily express force, speed, or complete destruction. The event’s magnitude in Al-Infiṭār follows from its subject and the surrounding verses, rather than its verb pattern alone.

5. Comparison with related roots

N TH R (نَثَرَ) and N SH R (نَشَرَ): scattering and spreading out

The roots resemble one another in sound and part of their semantic fields, but differ.

N TH R more often concerns dispersing parts or objects.

N SH R can mean spreading, unfolding, publishing, and, in other uses, bringing to life.

The Qur’an says:

وَإِذَا الصُّحُفُ نُشِرَتْ

wa-idhā al-ṣuḥufu nushirat

“When the records are unfolded and spread out.”

Al-Takwīr 81:10

Nushirat belongs to N SH R rather than N TH R.

The records are opened and spread out; the verse does not describe their parts being scattered like seeds or pearls.

This illustrates a difference of emphasis without making the roots completely unrelated. Spreading and scattering can produce similar results in some Arabic uses.

The precise distinction depends on the object and context.

N TH R (نَثَرَ) and B TH TH (بَثَّ): scattering and dispersing

B TH TH also expresses scattering and dispersal in the Qur’an.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:6 says of the mountains:

فَكَانَتْ هَبَاءً مُنْبَثًّا

fa-kānat habāʾan munbaththan

“They became dispersed dust.”

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:6

This closely resembles هَبَاءً مَنْثُورًا in Al-Furqān 25:23.

Both combine habāʾ with a description of dispersion.

Al-Wāqiʿah concerns mountains whose crumbling is mentioned in the preceding verses; Al-Furqān concerns human deeds and their outcome in divine judgment.

Nathr and bathth can therefore convey closely related results in certain contexts.

The two verses alone cannot establish an absolute boundary, such as nathr always describing tiny objects while bathth always describes dispersal over enormous distances.

Other Qur’anic uses of bathth concern creatures and people being dispersed. Nathr also concerns celestial bodies in Al-Infiṭār and is not restricted to small objects.

Study of B TH TH

N TH R (نَثَرَ) and B ʿ TH R (بَعْثَرَ): dispersion and upheaval

Their occurrence near one another at the beginning of Al-Infiṭār makes this comparison useful.

Intatharat in verse 2 describes the celestial bodies becoming scattered.

Buʿthirat in verse 4 concerns graves being turned over and their contents brought to light.

Both contribute to an image of change and disruption, but belong to separate roots.

Baʿthara emphasizes overturning and disturbing a state; intithār emphasizes dispersion.

Their presence in one surah does not mean every intithār necessarily involves upheaval or emptying a container.

Study of B ʿ TH R

N TH R (نَثَرَ) and N Ẓ M (نَظَمَ): scattering and stringing together

Arabic N Ẓ M concerns arranging parts together and stringing them.

نَظَمَ اللُّؤْلُؤَ means stringing pearls together.

نَثَرَ اللُّؤْلُؤَ, by contrast, means scattering them.

This provides a clear contrast: naẓm joins parts in a particular arrangement, while nathr scatters them. Manthūr does not necessarily imply that a string has broken or that the pearls were previously strung together; that would require the particular context.

This should not become a value judgment. Scattered pearls can remain beautiful and valuable, as the Qur’an’s depiction of the Garden’s blessings in Al-Insān 76:19 shows.

6. Translation challenges and proposals for Forqan

The central issue is preserving the distinction between the word’s meaning and the outcome conveyed by context.

For ordinary Arabic nathara, “scattered,” “sprinkled,” and “strewed” are suitable equivalents.

Intathara can be rendered “became scattered” or “dispersed.” Wording such as “came apart” is more suitable when the context includes the disruption of a previous state.

“Scattered” or “strewn” are natural and sufficient for manthūr.

These renderings suit the three Qur’anic occurrences:

VerseExpressionProposed rendering
Al-Furqān 25:23هَبَاءً مَنْثُورًاScattered dust
Al-Insān 76:19لُؤْلُؤًا مَنْثُورًاScattered pearls
Al-Infiṭār 82:2الْكَوَاكِبُ انْتَثَرَتْThe stars became scattered

Al-Furqān does not require translating manthūran as “destroyed.” The deeds’ fruitlessness can be explained in context.

Al-Insān should not render manthūran merely as “shining,” “very beautiful,” or “abundant.” These may follow from the pearls and the scene, but are not its independent meaning.

“Become scattered” is a clear basic rendering for Al-Infiṭār. “Fall away” is a recognized contextual reading, but should not be expanded into “fall toward the earth.” Exploding and complete destruction likewise introduce details not established by the verb alone.

For Forqan, it is preferable to preserve dispersion across all three uses and explain their different messages contextually.

7. Conclusion

N TH R denotes scattering and becoming scattered. Its clearest material uses include strewing seeds, dispersing objects, and separating items from a gathered state.

Its other branches include scattering objects at social occasions, describing detached material, and uses concerning expelling substances or producing speech. Literary prose belongs to the same family, but its technical meaning should not be imposed on the verses.

The Qur’an uses N TH R three times: manthūran twice and intatharat once.

In Al-Furqān 25:23, scattered dust conveys a group’s deeds becoming fruitless in divine judgment.

In Al-Insān 76:19, scattered pearls belong to an image of beauty and abundance for the Garden’s inhabitants.

In Al-Infiṭār 82:2, the celestial bodies’ dispersion forms part of the tremendous heavenly change in the hereafter.

These uses place dispersion within contexts of blame, praise, or a tremendous event. Value, beauty, destruction, and magnitude are not fixed meanings of the root; they follow from what is scattered and the whole verse.

Proposed final definition for Forqan

N TH R means scattering, sprinkling, or strewing something, or its parts becoming dispersed across different places. This can result from deliberate action or be described without naming an agent. The root does not necessarily imply destruction, worthlessness, forceful motion, or a particular direction.

In Qur’anic usage, manthūr means “scattered” and intathara means “became scattered.”

Scattering and becoming scattered thus form the shared semantic core of its Qur’anic uses, explaining dispersed dust, scattered pearls, and scattered celestial bodies without adding an invented meaning.
