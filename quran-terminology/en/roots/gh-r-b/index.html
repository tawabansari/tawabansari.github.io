The Qur’anic Root GH R B

From sunset and disappearance from view to the west, estrangement, and other branches

In the Qur’an, the root GH R B is most readily associated with sunset, the place of sunset, and the western direction. The sun gharabat, “set,” and taghrubu, “sets”; its disappearance at the horizon is called ghurūb. The place or direction of that setting is maghrib, and something associated with that direction is described as gharbiyy or gharbiyyah, “western.”

The family GH R B is not confined to this branch. In early Arabic, the same lexical entry also includes going far away, being away from one’s homeland, and becoming a stranger. In lexical classification, ghurāb, the name of the crow, and gharābīb, describing intense blackness, are also treated under this entry; both occur in the Qur’an.

A historical connection linking all these forms to a single original meaning has not been established. The branch comprising sunset, maghrib, and the western direction is clear, while distance and estrangement have independent support in Arabic usage. Ghurāb and gharābīb must be examined in light of their particular uses.

The principal Qur’anic branch can be described as follows: the sun setting and disappearing from view at the horizon; associated with this event are sunset, the place and direction of setting, and the west. Arabic also uses the same lexical family for distance and estrangement, but not all its forms can confidently be derived from this one image.

Distance and estrangement

Early Arabic uses another branch of GH R B for going far away and being far from home. Ghurbah denotes absence from one’s homeland, and gharīb describes someone far from their people or native land.

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Mutalammis al-Ḍubaʿī reads:

أَلَا أَبْلِغَا أَفْنَاءَ سَعْدِ بْنِ مَالِكٍ
رِسَالَةَ مَنْ قَدْ صَارَ فِي الْغُرْبِ جَانِبُهْ

Alā ablighā afnāʾa Saʿdi bni Mālikin / risālata man qad ṣāra fī al-ghurbi jānibuh.

“Convey to the clans of Saʿd ibn Mālik the message of one who has come to dwell far from home.”

Lisān al-ʿArab interprets al-ghurb in this verse as distance from one’s homeland. Ibn al-Anbārī’s commentary on early poetry attributes the verse to al-Mutalammis but explains al-gharb as the western region where the poet was staying. The transmitted attribution is thus explicit, while the precise interpretation varies: estrangement and distance from home, or the western region of the poet’s residence.

The translation above follows the explanation in terms of estrangement; on the geographical reading, its ending would be “one who has settled in the western region.” On either reading, the verse describes the poet’s location away from his addressees, not sunset. Lexicons also record tagharrub and ightirāb for going away and leaving familiar surroundings; the date of each usage must be assessed separately.

Sunset and estrangement have an intelligible semantic connection: the sun passes out of sight when it sets, and the stranger is far from home and familiar surroundings. Some early lexicographers similarly explain the setting of the sun through its withdrawal from view.

This proximity of meaning does not establish a definite historical chain. It cannot demonstrate that ghurbah was necessarily a metaphor derived from sunset, or that sunset originally meant “the sun becoming a stranger.” What can confidently be retained is the attested branch of distance within the same lexical family.

Gharabat: sunset as an event

One of the clearest verbal uses occurs in the account of the Companions of the Cave:

وَتَرَى الشَّمْسَ إِذَا طَلَعَتْ تَزَاوَرُ عَنْ كَهْفِهِمْ ذَاتَ الْيَمِينِ وَإِذَا غَرَبَتْ تَقْرِضُهُمْ ذَاتَ الشِّمَالِ

Wa-tarā al-shamsa idhā ṭalaʿat tazāwaru ʿan kahfihim dhāta al-yamīni wa-idhā gharabat taqriḍuhum dhāta al-shimāl.

“You see the sun, when it rises, incline away from their cave to the right, and when it sets, pass them by on the left.”

Al-Kahf 18:17

The Qur’an places ṭalaʿat, “rose,” and gharabat, “set,” opposite one another within a single sentence. The sun first rises and then sets. Here the verb describes an event observed at the horizon; the sentence does not establish the historical order in which the meanings “sunset” and “west” developed.

The imperfect verb retains the same meaning in the account of Dhū al-Qarnayn:

حَتَّىٰ إِذَا بَلَغَ مَغْرِبَ الشَّمْسِ وَجَدَهَا تَغْرُبُ فِي عَيْنٍ حَمِئَةٍ وَوَجَدَ عِنْدَهَا قَوْمًا

Ḥattā idhā balagha maghriba al-shamsi wajadahā taghrubu fī ʿaynin ḥamiʾatin wa-wajada ʿindahā qawmā.

“Until, when he reached the setting place of the sun, he found it setting in a muddy spring, and found a people near it.”

Al-Kahf 18:86

Taghrubu expresses the setting of the sun. The verb by itself does not say that the sun physically enters a spring; that question concerns the scene as a whole and the narrative’s perspective. At the level of the root, what is clear is sunset and the sun’s disappearance from the visible horizon.

Ṭulūʿ and ghurūb: the two ends of a day

Ṭā Hā states:

وَسَبِّحْ بِحَمْدِ رَبِّكَ قَبْلَ طُلُوعِ الشَّمْسِ وَقَبْلَ غُرُوبِهَا

Wa-sabbiḥ bi-ḥamdi rabbika qabla ṭulūʿi al-shamsi wa-qabla ghurūbihā.

“And glorify your Lord with praise before the rising of the sun and before its setting.”

Ṭā Hā 20:130

And Qāf states:

وَسَبِّحْ بِحَمْدِ رَبِّكَ قَبْلَ طُلُوعِ الشَّمْسِ وَقَبْلَ الْغُرُوبِ

Wa-sabbiḥ bi-ḥamdi rabbika qabla ṭulūʿi al-shamsi wa-qabla al-ghurūb.

“And glorify your Lord with praise before the rising of the sun and before sunset.”

Qāf 50:39

In these two verses, ṭulūʿ, “rising,” and ghurūb, “setting,” are opposite events. At sunrise the sun appears, and at sunset it disappears from the visible horizon.

This contrast also helps explain the relationship between SH R Q and GH R B. The solar branch of SH R Q concerns rising and shining, while the solar branch of GH R B concerns setting and disappearing from view.

This natural contrast does not itself constitute a philosophical system. The two roots do not establish that east intrinsically symbolizes truth and presence, or that west intrinsically symbolizes darkness and absence. Such associations may develop in later literature and thought, but they are not the Qur’anic words’ lexical definitions.

Maghrib: the place or direction of sunset

The relationship between verb and noun is particularly clear in Al-Kahf 18:86. The Qur’an first mentions maghriba al-shams, “the setting place of the sun,” and then taghrubu, “it sets.” Maghrib is the place or direction in which the sun is seen setting.

The same relationship is evident in Abraham’s words:

قَالَ إِبْرَاهِيمُ فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ يَأْتِي بِالشَّمْسِ مِنَ الْمَشْرِقِ فَأْتِ بِهَا مِنَ الْمَغْرِبِ

Qāla Ibrāhīmu fa-inna Allāha yaʾtī bi-l-shamsi mina al-mashriqi faʾti bihā mina al-maghrib.

“Abraham said: ‘The God brings the sun from the east; then bring it from the west.’”

Al-Baqarah 2:258

Mashriq is the direction of rising, and maghrib the direction of setting. The two geographical designations are associated with two natural events.

Maghrib can therefore designate the geographical direction “west.” This is a real and established usage; studying the root also reveals the image of sunset underlying the directional name.

One maghrib, two maghribs, and many maghārib

Maghrib is not always singular in the Qur’an. Al-Raḥmān states:

رَبُّ الْمَشْرِقَيْنِ وَرَبُّ الْمَغْرِبَيْنِ

Rabbu al-mashriqayni wa-rabbu al-maghribayn.

“Lord of the two easts and Lord of the two wests.”

Al-Raḥmān 55:17

In Al-Maʿārij we read:

فَلَا أُقْسِمُ بِرَبِّ الْمَشَارِقِ وَالْمَغَارِبِ إِنَّا لَقَادِرُونَ

Fa-lā uqsimu bi-rabbi al-mashāriqi wa-l-maghāribi innā la-qādirūn.

“So I swear by the Lord of the easts and the wests: We are indeed able.”

Al-Maʿārij 70:40

Al-Aʿrāf also states:

وَأَوْرَثْنَا الْقَوْمَ الَّذِينَ كَانُوا يُسْتَضْعَفُونَ مَشَارِقَ الْأَرْضِ وَمَغَارِبَهَا الَّتِي بَارَكْنَا فِيهَا

Wa-awrathnā al-qawma alladhīna kānū yustaḍʿafūna mashāriqa al-arḍi wa-maghāribahā allatī bāraknā fīhā.

“And We made the people who had been oppressed inherit the eastern and western regions of the land in which We had placed blessing.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:137

The Qur’an uses maghrib in the singular, dual, and plural. In Al-Aʿrāf 7:137, the subject is land inherited by a people: mashāriqa al-arḍi wa-maghāribahā refers in this context to that land’s eastern and western regions. Not every plural occurrence should therefore be restricted to points of sunset.

In the traditional explanation of Al-Raḥmān 55:17, the two wests are associated with the two furthest limits of sunset over the course of a year. The many wests in Al-Maʿārij 70:40 are associated with the different places where the sun sets on successive days. This explanation accords with the observable change in sunset’s position along the horizon, but these verses themselves specify neither seasons nor a particular number of sunset points.

Maghrib can express a place of sunset, the western direction, or a western region. Its singular, dual, and plural forms should be preserved, and the referent of each established from the verse’s context. This range does not conflict with the ordinary use of “west” as a geographical direction.

Al-gharbiyy and gharbiyyah: the western direction

Al-Qaṣaṣ states:

وَمَا كُنْتَ بِجَانِبِ الْغَرْبِيِّ إِذْ قَضَيْنَا إِلَىٰ مُوسَى الْأَمْرَ وَمَا كُنْتَ مِنَ الشَّاهِدِينَ

Wa-mā kunta bi-jānibi al-gharbiyyi idh qaḍaynā ilā Mūsā al-amra wa-mā kunta mina al-shāhidīn.

“You were not on the western side when We entrusted the command to Moses, nor were you among the witnesses.”

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:44

Al-gharbiyy is a spatial adjective here, meaning “western.” Just as sharqiyy relates something to the east, gharbiyy relates it to the west.

This usage shows the semantic connection between sunset and geographical direction: the sun sets, the place or direction of setting is called maghrib, and something associated with that direction is “western.” This semantic relationship alone should not be treated as a record of the historical sequence in which the words developed.

Lā sharqiyyatin wa-lā gharbiyyah: neither eastern nor western

The Verse of Light states:

يُوقَدُ مِنْ شَجَرَةٍ مُبَارَكَةٍ زَيْتُونَةٍ لَا شَرْقِيَّةٍ وَلَا غَرْبِيَّةٍ يَكَادُ زَيْتُهَا يُضِيءُ وَلَوْ لَمْ تَمْسَسْهُ نَارٌ

Yūqadu min shajaratin mubārakatin zaytūnatin lā sharqiyyatin wa-lā gharbiyyatin yakādu zaytuhā yuḍīʾu wa-law lam tamsashu nār.

“It is kindled from a blessed tree, an olive, neither eastern nor western, whose oil would almost shine even if no fire touched it.”

Al-Nūr 24:35

Gharbiyyah qualifies zaytūnah, “olive tree,” and is paired with its opposite, sharqiyyah. At the lexical level, “western” means associated with the direction of the west.

The root alone does not explain why the olive tree is described as “neither eastern nor western.” Explanations involving the tree’s location, exposure to sunlight, or position attempt to identify the full image’s referent; they should not be incorporated into the basic meaning of gharbiyyah.

Nor does this phrase alone establish that the verse rejects every geographical boundary for divine light. That may be a reflection on the complete image, but it is not the direct meaning of the adjective gharbiyyah.

For the basic translation, “neither eastern nor western” remains the more faithful rendering.

A Semitic comparison: the sun “entering”

In Akkadian, an ancient Semitic language, the verb erēbu, “to enter,” is also used of the sun setting. The expression erēb šamši denotes sunset: the same event is described through the image of the sun “entering.”

This is historically significant because it shows that Semitic speakers did not describe sunset only through the image of “going down.” The sun’s “entering” at the horizon could also express the event.

Comparative studies have examined this Akkadian usage alongside Arabic gharaba. Comparing two forms, or considering their possible cognacy, is not the same as claiming that the Arabic word was derived directly from Akkadian. Assessing the historical relationship requires regular consonant correspondences and evidence from each language, not semantic similarity alone.

The comparison thus helps illuminate an ancient image of sunset in the Semitic language family; it does not conclusively determine the origin of Arabic GH R B.

Is “darkness” the core meaning?

Sunset accompanies nightfall and diminishing light; a crow is commonly black; and ghirbīb denotes intense blackness. These associations may yield a compelling literary image, but they are insufficient to place “darkness” at the center of the root.

The branch concerned with estrangement means distance, not darkness. Maghrib is the place of sunset, not a name for darkness. Other lexical uses of GH R B are also attested which cannot be explained through darkness.

Describing estrangement as “social darkness” may be a literary metaphor, but it is not evidence for the root’s historical meaning. Neither sunset’s association with darkness nor the crow’s blackness independently reduces all branches of the family to a single meaning, “darkness.”

The attested branches are better retained as the language records them, even when they cannot all be derived from a single image.

Ghurāb: the name of the crow

In the account of Adam’s two sons, the Qur’an states:

فَبَعَثَ اللَّهُ غُرَابًا يَبْحَثُ فِي الْأَرْضِ لِيُرِيَهُ كَيْفَ يُوَارِي سَوْءَةَ أَخِيهِ قَالَ يَا وَيْلَتَىٰ أَعَجَزْتُ أَنْ أَكُونَ مِثْلَ هَٰذَا الْغُرَابِ فَأُوَارِيَ سَوْءَةَ أَخِي

Fa-baʿatha Allāhu ghurāban yabḥathu fī al-arḍi li-yuriyahu kayfa yuwārī sawʾata akhīhi qāla yā waylatā aʿajaztu an akūna mithla hādhā al-ghurābi fa-uwāriya sawʾata akhī.

“Then The God sent a crow scratching in the earth to show him how to cover his brother’s body. He said: ‘Woe to me! Was I unable to be like this crow and cover my brother’s body?’”

Al-Māʾidah 5:31

Ghurāb names the bird in this verse and is translated “crow.”

The noun’s placement in the lexical family GH R B does not by itself establish that the bird’s name derives from sunset, west, or estrangement. Dictionaries record connections between ghurāb and certain other branches, but understanding this Qur’anic usage does not require reconstructing the origin of the bird’s name.

In this verse’s context, the word refers to the bird scratching in the ground. The name ghurāb does not itself add the meaning of west, sunset, or a hidden symbol of darkness to the sentence.

Gharābību sūd: intense blackness

Fāṭir states:

وَمِنَ الْجِبَالِ جُدَدٌ بِيضٌ وَحُمْرٌ مُخْتَلِفٌ أَلْوَانُهَا وَغَرَابِيبُ سُودٌ

Wa-mina al-jibāli judadun bīḍun wa-ḥumrun mukhtalifun alwānuhā wa-gharābību sūd.

“And among the mountains are streaks of white and red, of different shades, and [parts] of intense blackness.”

Fāṭir 35:27

Gharābīb is the plural of ghirbīb, a word used in Arabic for intense blackness. The expression aswadu ghirbībun is also attested for “intensely black.”

In the lexical tradition, including al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī’s explanation, ghirbīb is described as black like a ghurāb, a crow. This evidence clarifies a connection between the two words through color; a lexical explanation of color should not, by itself, be treated as conclusive proof of a historical path of derivation.

In the Qur’an, then, gharābību sūd concerns the deeply black coloring of mountains, not the geographical west or sunset.

This branch is an important reminder that apparent membership in a root family, or the grouping of words under a single lexical entry, does not establish one proven historical image underlying them all.

SH R Q compared with GH R B: rising and setting

The comparison between these two families is clearest in their solar branches.

The solar branch of SH R Q concerns rising and shining, while GH R B concerns the sun’s setting and disappearance from view. Associated with these two events, mashriq and maghrib designate two geographical directions.

The Qur’an maintains this contrast at several levels: ṭulūʿ al-shams wa-ghurūbihā, “the sun’s rising and setting”; al-mashriq wa-l-maghrib, “the east and west”; al-mashriqayn wa-l-maghribayn, “the two easts and two wests”; and al-mashāriq wa-l-maghārib, “the easts and wests.”

This is an attested linguistic contrast, but it carries no fixed moral value. The Qur’an does not say that east is intrinsically good and west intrinsically bad, or that one represents spiritual light and the other spiritual darkness.

The clearest distinction remains the natural event: mashriq is the direction of rising, and maghrib the direction of setting.

Al-Baqarah 2:115: from east and west to “wherever”

Al-Baqarah 2:115 places east and west together:

وَلِلَّهِ الْمَشْرِقُ وَالْمَغْرِبُ ۚ فَأَيْنَمَا تُوَلُّوا فَثَمَّ وَجْهُ اللَّهِ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ وَاسِعٌ عَلِيمٌ

Wa-li-llāhi al-mashriqu wa-l-maghribu fa-aynamā tuwallū fa-thamma wajhu Allāhi inna Allāha wāsiʿun ʿalīm.

“To The God belong the east and the west. Wherever you turn, there is the Face of The God. Indeed, The God is All-Encompassing, Knowing.”

Al-Baqarah 2:115

In this sentence, mashriq is the direction of sunrise and maghrib the direction of sunset. They are among the most readily perceived limits of the human horizon.

The verse does not stop with naming them. It continues:

فَأَيْنَمَا تُوَلُّوا

Fa-aynamā tuwallū.

“So wherever you turn.”

East and west are therefore not an exhaustive list of possible directions here. Two familiar sides of the horizon are named, and aynamā, “wherever,” then extends the statement to every direction in which a person turns.

East and west both belong to The God; a person does not leave His domain by turning in a different direction. This follows from the verse’s construction without requiring east and west to become further philosophical symbols.

Translation proposals for Forqan

For gharabati al-shams, “the sun set” is natural and accurate. The root discussion can explain that setting involves the sun’s disappearance from view at the horizon.

For ghurūbihā and al-ghurūb, “its setting” and “sunset” are established, natural English expressions. No more elaborate substitute is needed.

For maghrib al-shams, “the setting place of the sun” preserves the image of the expression; “where the sun sets” can also explain its meaning.

Al-maghrib, when paired with mashriq, naturally translates as “the west.” The image behind the root need not be expanded into “the direction in which the sun goes down” every time it occurs in a basic translation.

Al-maghribayn is “the two wests,” and al-maghārib “the wests”; their difference in number should be preserved.

For jānib al-gharbiyy, “the western side” is natural; for lā sharqiyyatin wa-lā gharbiyyah, “neither eastern nor western.”

Ghurāb in Al-Māʾidah 5:31 is “crow.” No meaning connected with sunset or estrangement should be inserted into its translation.

For gharābību sūd, “intensely black” or “deep black” conveys the meaning. If gharābīb itself is retained in a lexical discussion, a brief explanation of its emphasis on blackness is needed.

Conclusion

GH R B is a family with several branches; its forms should not all be reduced to one image.

Its clearest Qur’anic branch concerns the sun. Gharabat and taghrubu express the sun setting, and ghurūb names the event itself. The Qur’an directly contrasts rising, ṭulūʿ, with setting, ghurūb.

Maghrib denotes the place or direction of sunset, as well as west; gharbiyy and gharbiyyah express geographical association with that direction. Singular, dual, and plural forms occur in the Qur’an. Their referents depend on context: for example, the maghārib of Al-Aʿrāf 7:137 are the western regions of a land, not simply sunset points.

Early Arabic uses another branch of this lexical family for distance and estrangement. It is semantically close to sunset through going away and disappearing from view, but that resemblance alone does not establish their precise historical relationship.

Akkadian erēbu shows that an ancient Semitic language could describe sunset through the image of “entering.” This comparison helps us understand ancient expressions for sunset, but does not by itself establish direct borrowing into Arabic from Akkadian or a precise path of semantic development.

Ghurāb names the crow, and gharābīb expresses intense blackness in the Qur’an. The lexical tradition explains ghirbīb through the crow’s black coloring. On its own, this explanation neither settles the history of derivation nor makes “darkness” the core of every meaning associated with sunset, estrangement, and the crow.

The principal distinction from SH R Q is clear: shurūq concerns the sun’s rising and shining, and ghurūb its setting and disappearance from view. Mashriq and maghrib designate the two geographical directions associated with those events, without this natural contrast automatically becoming a moral or philosophical opposition.

GH R B: in its principal Qur’anic branch, the sun setting and disappearing from view, with the associated meanings of sunset, the place and direction of setting, and west. In early Arabic, the same lexical entry also includes distance and estrangement. Ghurāb and gharābīb are other branches that must be understood through their own uses; without evidence, they should not be reduced to sunset, estrangement, or a hypothetical common core such as darkness.
