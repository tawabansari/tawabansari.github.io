Qur’anic Root A D D

Idd: something profoundly appalling, extraordinary, and wrongful; from the gravity of an event to condemnation of a claim concerning The God

1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic form

The Arabic root A D D is associated with something exceptionally great, appalling, difficult, or astonishing. Idd describes something whose magnitude or horror provokes attention and astonishment. In a condemnatory context, it can characterize something profoundly reprehensible, wrongful, and shocking.

Within this family, iddah is recorded for a major event or calamity, add for intensity and strength, and adīd for clamor and loud noise. Other uses include verbs describing a camel’s moaning, the reverberation of its voice within its chest, and livestock bolting or moving away.

These meanings should not all be derived from a single original image merely because they belong to the same root. For example, the intensity shared by a loud noise and a horrific event does not by itself establish that the meaning “a wrongful matter” originally developed from a camel’s voice.

In the Qur’an, this root occurs only once:

Maryam, 19:89

لَّقَدْ جِئْتُمْ شَيْئًا إِدًّا

la-qad jiʾtum shayʾan iddan

“You have indeed uttered something profoundly appalling and wrongful.”

In this verse, iddan qualifies shayʾan, “something.” The Qur’an characterizes the claim reported in the preceding verse as profoundly appalling and wrongful.

That claim attributes a child to Al-Raḥmān.

The root A D D itself, however, does not mean a child, childbirth, associating partners with The God, or disbelief. Its basic meaning concerns the intensity and appalling nature of a matter; the surrounding verses identify what receives that description.

Recorded forms

A D D is a geminate Arabic root: its final two consonants are identical.

Important forms recorded in the lexicons include:

  • إِدّ (idd): something exceptionally great, appalling, astonishing, or wrongful; the root’s only Qur’anic form.
  • أَدّ (add): intensity, predominance, or strength; in other uses, a camel’s voice or certain heavy sounds.
  • إِدَّة (iddah): a major, difficult event or an appalling calamity.
  • إِدَد / إِدَاد (idad / idād): recorded plurals of forms such as iddah and idd.
  • آدّ (ādd): a description of someone or something associated with a great and appalling matter; used in place of idd in some Arabic expressions.
  • أَدَّ، يَؤُدُّ / يَئِدُّ (adda, yaʾuddu / yaʾiddu): in one use, to afflict someone with a major ordeal; in others, to make a sound or stretch something.
  • أَدِيد (adīd): clamor, noise, or loud tumult.
  • أَدَّتِ النَّاقَةُ (addati al-nāqah): the she-camel reverberated and prolonged its moaning within its chest.
  • أَدَّتِ الْإِبِلُ (addati al-ibil): in another use, the camels bolted or moved away from their place.

Apart from idd, which appears in the accusative form iddan in Maryam 19:89, the other forms in this list do not occur in the Qur’an.

The broader range of Arabic usage must therefore be distinguished from the specific meaning of the Qur’anic word.

2. Intensity, horror, and astonishment in early Arabic

Idd: something great and shocking

Arabic lexicons explain al-idd and al-iddah through expressions such as al-amr al-ʿaẓīm, “a great matter”; al-amr al-faẓīʿ, “an appalling matter”; al-dāhiyah, “a calamity”; and al-ʿajab, “astonishment.”

These expressions occupy a related semantic field, but they are not identical in meaning.

ʿAẓīm emphasizes the greatness or importance of something.

Faẓīʿ foregrounds its appalling and distressing character.

Dāhiyah denotes a major event or calamity that may overwhelm someone and leave them helpless.

ʿAjab concerns astonishment and the extraordinary nature of something.

Together, these explanations show that idd is not simply a description of physical size. It can characterize a matter or event whose intensity, gravity, or horror exceeds the ordinary.

For example, an exceptionally large building may be ʿaẓīm without necessarily being called idd.

Conversely, a claim considered profoundly wrongful and shocking by its speaker or evaluator may receive this description even if it consists of only a few words.

In the Qur’anic passage, translating shayʾan iddan merely as “something very great” therefore fails to convey its horror and condemnation adequately.

An example from Arabic rajaz verse

In his explanation of Maryam 19:89, Al-Ṭabarī quotes the following Arabic rajaz verse:

قَدْ لَقِيَ الْأَعْدَاءُ مِنِّي نُكْرَا
دَاهِيَةً دَهْيَاءَ إِدًّا إِمْرَا

qad laqiya al-aʿdāʾu minnī nukrā / dāhiyatan dahyāʾa iddan imrā

“My enemies have encountered something terrible from me: a formidable calamity, immense, astonishing, and dreadful.”

Several related terms appear together in this witness: nukr, dāhiyah, idd, and imr.

Their combination shows that idd was recognized in Arabic poetic usage as a way of expressing the intensity and horror of an event.

They should not, however, all be treated as exact synonyms.

A poet can combine several related expressions to intensify an image without each word having precisely the same lexical meaning as the others.

The poet and exact date of composition are not identified in the transmission examined here. Without independent evidence, this passage cannot be presented as securely pre-Islamic poetry.

Its value lies in documenting the word’s use within the Arabic linguistic tradition.

Idd and iddah: a grave event or major calamity

Iddah is also used in Arabic for an exceptionally great matter or a difficult calamity.

The expression addat fulānan dāhiyatun means that a grave event has overtaken and afflicted someone.

This use concerns the intensity and gravity of the event.

It does not establish that every idd must be a natural disaster or physical affliction.

Maryam 19:89 concerns a claim about The God, rather than a flood, war, illness, or physical event.

An utterance or wrongful attribution can therefore be described as appalling.

Add: intensity and strength

Lexicons also define al-add, with a fatḥah on the hamzah, as intensity, predominance, or strength.

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris quotes a verse in this connection with the reading shirrah. The form shiddah used below is recorded in Lisān al-ʿArab. These transmissions do not identify the poet or the exact date of composition:

نَضَوْنَ عَنِّي شِدَّةً وَأَدًّا
مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا كُنْتُ صُمُلًّا نَهْدَا

naḍawna ʿannī shiddatan wa-addā / min baʿdi mā kuntu ṣumullan nahdā

“After I had been strong and sturdy, they stripped me of my vigor and strength.”

In the version quoted here, addan stands beside shiddatan and concerns bodily strength and toughness. The reading shirratan instead foregrounds impetuosity, ardor, and unruly vigor; this textual variation does not change the meaning of add as strength.

There is thus a semantic proximity between the intensity of strength and the magnitude or difficulty of an event.

“Strength” and “a wrongful matter” nevertheless have different meanings.

Nor does this poetic witness establish that iddan in Maryam 19:89 means bodily strength or physical ability.

There, the construction shayʾan iddan and the surrounding claim of having a child support the meaning “appalling and wrongful.”

Adīd: sound and clamor

In Arabic, adīd concerns clamor and tumult.

Addati al-nāqah is also used of a camel that reverberates and prolongs its moaning within its chest.

Another explanation connects addu al-baʿīr with a camel’s loud voice.

Sound and its intensity are central to these uses.

In Al-Mufradāt, Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī attempts to explain idd in the Maryam passage through this field of sound: a profoundly reprehensible matter accompanied by clamor and a forceful reaction.

This is an intelligible proposal for a semantic connection.

It does not establish that idd itself means a loud noise, a scream, or an explosion in the verse.

Maryam 19:89 does not describe a particular sound. It concerns the appalling nature of a claim voiced by a group of people.

If a relationship between idd and adīd is accepted, it should therefore be understood as a lexical explanation involving intensity, rather than a replacement of the verse’s direct meaning with sound.

Camels bolting or moving away

Ibn Fāris records another branch of the root: addati al-ibil, used when camels bolt or move away from their place.

On its face, this differs from an appalling matter or a loud noise.

Loud sounds and fleeing animals may sometimes occur together in real experience, but such an imagined connection does not establish the history of the word’s derivation.

Bolting is therefore best recorded as a distinct use within the family, without imposing it on Maryam 19:89.

The verse itself does not discuss bolting, flight, or the physical movement of creatures.

3. La-qad jiʾtum shayʾan iddan: the only Qur’anic occurrence

Maryam, 19:88

وَقَالُوا اتَّخَذَ الرَّحْمَٰنُ وَلَدًا

wa-qālū ittakhadha al-raḥmānu waladan

“And they said, ‘Al-Raḥmān has taken a child for Himself.’”

Maryam, 19:89

لَّقَدْ جِئْتُمْ شَيْئًا إِدًّا

la-qad jiʾtum shayʾan iddan

“You have indeed uttered something profoundly appalling and wrongful.”

These two verses establish both the object and the intensity of the condemnation.

The first reports a claim: ittakhadha al-raḥmānu waladan, “Al-Raḥmān has taken a child.”

The second condemns that same claim with the expression shayʾan iddan.

The syntax of the verse

In this construction, la-qad emphasizes the statement and presents it as realized.

Jiʾtum is a perfect-tense verb addressed to a plural “you”: “you have brought,” or, in this context of speech, “you have put forward such a statement.”

Shayʾan is the object of the verb.

Iddan is an accusative adjective qualifying shayʾan and expressing the intensity and appalling nature of that “something.”

Iddan is therefore neither the word for a child nor the name of a particular doctrine. It describes the reported claim.

The plural form jiʾtum also matters: the Qur’an addresses the group making that claim.

The plural form alone, however, cannot identify every historical individual or community intended.

Identifying the speakers requires examining the surrounding verses and other passages about attributing a child to The God.

Does the verse concern only one religious community?

In Maryam 19:88, the Qur’an says wa-qālū, “and they said.”

This expression does not explicitly name a particular community.

Elsewhere, the Qur’an discusses attributing a child to The God in different settings, including Al-Baqarah 2:116, Al-Tawbah 9:30, Yūnus 10:68, and passages concerning the attribution of daughters or angels to The God.

The root A D D itself therefore provides no basis for applying wa-qālū indiscriminately to everyone bearing a particular religious designation.

Nor does this verse establish that everyone born into a religious community or bearing a religious name has necessarily and consciously adopted the condemned claim.

The verse critiques a specific claim.

For a root study, the distinction is clear: iddan describes the appalling nature of that claim; it is neither a group name nor a criterion for determining each individual’s responsibility.

Shayʾan iddan: an utterance or an action?

Shayʾan is broad in Arabic and can denote a matter, thing, or subject.

Here, the preceding verb qālū, “they said,” identifies the object of condemnation as an uttered claim.

The contextual meaning can therefore be rendered in translation as:

“You have uttered something profoundly appalling.”

Or:

“You have put forward a profoundly wrongful claim.”

These renderings make the contextual meaning of shayʾan explicit.

Idd itself, however, should not be defined as “an utterance.” The preceding verse and the sentence construction identify the matter as speech; idd conveys the intensity of the condemnation.

4. The claim’s appalling nature in the following Maryam verses

The following verses directly illuminate the intensity of the description iddan.

The heavens, the earth, and the mountains

Maryam, 19:90

تَكَادُ السَّمَاوَاتُ يَتَفَطَّرْنَ مِنْهُ وَتَنْشَقُّ الْأَرْضُ وَتَخِرُّ الْجِبَالُ هَدًّا

takādu al-samāwātu yatafaṭṭarna minhu wa-tanshaqqu al-arḍu wa-takhirru al-jibālu haddan

“The heavens are close to splitting apart because of it, the earth to cleaving open, and the mountains to falling in utter collapse.”

This verse expresses an immense reaction through the image of the heavens, the earth, and the mountains.

The heavens approaching rupture, the earth splitting, and the mountains collapsing show the gravity of the condemned matter.

The roots used in this verse must nevertheless be distinguished.

Yatafaṭṭarna belongs to F Ṭ R and concerns splitting.

Tanshaqqu belongs to SH Q Q.

Takhirru belongs to KH R R and concerns falling down.

Haddan belongs to H D D and concerns collapse and breaking apart.

None of these forms derives from A D D.

The similar sound of iddan in verse 89 and haddan in verse 90 likewise does not make them the same root.

Their placement in consecutive verses is nevertheless significant for the passage’s rhythm and intensity of expression.

Have the heavens actually split apart?

The verse begins with takādu, “nearly” or “are close to.”

It should therefore not be converted, without qualification, into a report that all the heavens actually tore apart at that moment.

The language describes an immense event approaching occurrence.

This construction can express the exceptional horror and gravity of a matter.

It does not follow that the Qur’an has used a meaningless or merely ornamental expression.

The image of the heavens, the earth, and the mountains emphasizes the magnitude of the claim that the Qur’an declares wrongful.

Determining precisely how this image relates to the created world requires a separate study of this verse and comparable uses.

For the root study, the essential point is that verse 90 clarifies the force of the condemnation iddan; it does not change the lexical meaning of idd into “the heavens splitting apart.”

The reason for this condemnation

Maryam, 19:91

أَنْ دَعَوْا لِلرَّحْمَٰنِ وَلَدًا

an daʿaw li-al-raḥmāni waladan

“Because they attributed a child to Al-Raḥmān.”

This verse makes the reason for the preceding condemnation explicit.

The Qur’an identifies attributing a child to Al-Raḥmān as the subject of that appalling utterance.

Idd here therefore characterizes the gravity and wrongfulness of the claim attributing a child to Al-Raḥmān; it does not name a natural event.

Waladan belongs to W L D and concerns a child and the relationship of birth or offspring.

This meaning must not be merged with A D D.

Wa-mā yanbaghī li-al-raḥmān

Maryam, 19:92

وَمَا يَنْبَغِي لِلرَّحْمَٰنِ أَنْ يَتَّخِذَ وَلَدًا

wa-mā yanbaghī li-al-raḥmāni an yattakhidha waladan

“It is neither fitting nor appropriate for Al-Raḥmān to take a child for Himself.”

This verse explicitly rejects the reported claim.

Mā yanbaghī denies that the attribution is fitting or appropriate.

This expression, too, does not belong to A D D.

The lexical meaning of idd should therefore not be reduced to “unfitting.”

The claim’s unfitness emerges from the surrounding passage and the explicit negation in verse 92; idd expresses its intensity and appalling nature.

All creatures in the position of servants

Maryam, 19:93

إِنْ كُلُّ مَنْ فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ إِلَّا آتِي الرَّحْمَٰنِ عَبْدًا

in kullu man fī al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍi illā ātī al-raḥmāni ʿabdan

“There is no one in the heavens and the earth who does not come to Al-Raḥmān as a servant.”

Continuing the rejection of attributing a child to The God, this verse describes the position of creatures.

The appalling nature of the preceding claim is thus situated within a set of verses about the oneness of The God and His relationship with creatures.

The meaning of ʿabd must nevertheless not be transferred to A D D.

ʿAbd belongs to a different family and concerns the relationship of servanthood.

Each word has its own role in this passage: walad expresses the claimed relationship of offspring; idd, the appalling nature of that claim; and ʿabd, the position of creatures before Al-Raḥmān.

5. Distinguishing idd from related words

Idd and imr: a great and astonishing matter

Study of A M R

In the Qur’an, imr occurs in the account of Moses and his companion.

Al-Kahf, 18:71

فَانْطَلَقَا حَتَّىٰ إِذَا رَكِبَا فِي السَّفِينَةِ خَرَقَهَا ۖ قَالَ أَخَرَقْتَهَا لِتُغْرِقَ أَهْلَهَا لَقَدْ جِئْتَ شَيْئًا إِمْرًا

fa-inṭalaqā ḥattā idhā rakibā fī al-safīnati kharaqahā; qāla a-kharaqtahā li-tughriqa ahlahā la-qad jiʾta shayʾan imran

“The two then set out until, when they boarded the boat, his companion made a hole in it. Moses said, ‘Have you made a hole in it to drown its passengers? You have indeed done something extraordinary and grave.’”

The construction la-qad jiʾta shayʾan imran here closely resembles Maryam 19:89.

The two passages nevertheless use different words: imran and iddan.

Lexical explanations connect imr with a great, astonishing, and sometimes objectionable matter.

Idd occupies a related field, but its established usage can foreground horror and atrocity more strongly.

The difference between the words does not, however, establish that one always ranks numerically higher than the other.

The context matters when assessing the gravity of an event.

In Al-Kahf, moreover, Moses’ statement is his response to the boat being damaged. A further explanation of that event is given later in the account.

The speaker’s initial judgment and the event’s full reality should therefore be distinguished.

Idd and nukr: horror and reprehensible unfamiliarity

Study of N K R

Al-Kahf, 18:74

فَانْطَلَقَا حَتَّىٰ إِذَا لَقِيَا غُلَامًا فَقَتَلَهُ قَالَ أَقَتَلْتَ نَفْسًا زَكِيَّةً بِغَيْرِ نَفْسٍ لَقَدْ جِئْتَ شَيْئًا نُكْرًا

fa-inṭalaqā ḥattā idhā laqiyā ghulāman fa-qatalahu qāla a-qatalta nafsan zakiyyatan bi-ghayri nafsin la-qad jiʾta shayʾan nukran

“The two then set out until, when they encountered a youth, his companion killed him. Moses said, ‘Have you killed an innocent person without that being in retaliation for a life? You have indeed done something profoundly wrongful and reprehensible.’”

The same construction, la-qad jiʾta shayʾan, appears here, but its adjective is nukran.

Nukr belongs to N K R and concerns something unfamiliar, unexpected, or reprehensible.

In this context, condemnation of the deed is prominent.

Nukr should nevertheless not be treated as precisely equivalent to idd in every Arabic use.

Nor does the occurrence of imr and nukr at two stages of the account, by itself, create a fixed scale for measuring every action in the world.

In Maryam 19:89, idd concerns a claim about The God.

In Al-Kahf 18:71 and Al-Kahf 18:74, the other words express Moses’ response to particular events.

These differences of speaker, subject, and context must be preserved.

Idd and faẓīʿ: horror and extreme reprehensibility

In Arabic, faẓīʿ describes something profoundly reprehensible or appalling.

Lexicons repeatedly explain idd as al-amr al-faẓīʿ, “an appalling matter.”

The semantic proximity of the words is therefore clear.

This explanation does not make all uses of the two word families identical.

“Appalling” is appropriate for translating idd. In Maryam 19:89, however, its application to a wrongful claim makes “profoundly appalling and wrongful” a fuller rendering.

Idd and ʿaẓīm: greatness and horror

Study of ʿ Ẓ M

ʿAẓīm can express physical size, rank, worth, or importance.

Idd also concerns magnitude, but in Maryam 19:89 that magnitude accompanies condemnation and horror.

The word cannot therefore simply be translated as “great” without regard to the context.

A “great statement” can even sound complimentary in English, just as the corresponding Persian expression can. “Something profoundly appalling and wrongful” conveys the verse’s judgment more clearly.

Idd and hadd: two different roots

Study of H D D

Maryam 19:89 has iddan; the following verse has haddan.

Their sounds are similar, but their roots differ.

A D D concerns horror, intensity, and the gravity of a matter.

H D D in the following verse concerns collapse and breaking apart.

Idd should therefore not be translated as “collapse,” nor hadd as “a wrongful matter.”

Their similar sounds may intensify the verses’ imagery while their lexical distinction remains intact.

Idd and awd: horror and a burden’s weight

Study of A W D

The Arabic expression ādahu al-ḥimlu yaʾūduhu means that a load weighs heavily on someone and makes it difficult to bear.

This use belongs to A W D.

Some earlier explanations propose a connection between that family’s weight and difficulty and the intensity of idd.

This resemblance should not be taken to mean that the two roots are identical.

Nor should idd in Maryam be translated merely as “a heavy burden.” The passage concerns an appalling claim and contains no image of a physical load.

Idd and adāʾ: horror and conveying or fulfilling

Study of A D Y

Addā and adāʾ belong to A D Y and concern conveying, paying, or bringing something to completion.

Their spelling and sound resemble adda, but they do not share its root.

Iddan in the Qur’an therefore does not mean performing an obligation, paying, or delivering a trust.

The root A Y D, concerning strength and support, is likewise distinct from A D D, although some lexical explanations bring their fields of intensity and power close together.

6. Proposed translation for Forqan

Three aspects need to be considered together in Maryam 19:89: the gravity of the matter, its appalling nature, and the wrongfulness of the claim under discussion.

“Something great” stays close to the wording but does not fully convey the intensity of the condemnation.

“Something strange” may likewise suggest only something unusual or astonishing to an English reader, just as its Persian counterpart can, without conveying the claim’s wrongfulness.

“An ugly statement” expresses condemnation but weakens the intensity and gravity of idd.

The principal proposal is therefore:

Maryam, 19:89

لَّقَدْ جِئْتُمْ شَيْئًا إِدًّا

la-qad jiʾtum shayʾan iddan

“You have indeed uttered something profoundly appalling and wrongful.”

In this rendering, “uttered” comes from the preceding verse’s context: the Qur’an reports a claim and then describes it.

A rendering closer to the literal construction would be:

“You have indeed brought forth something profoundly appalling and wrongful.”

The first version is more natural in Persian; the English “uttered” likewise makes the speech context clear.

Brief meaning for the word-by-word box

Iddan: profoundly appalling and wrongful; describing a grave and shocking matter. In this verse, it qualifies the claim attributing a child to Al-Raḥmān.

This explanation keeps the root’s meaning distinct from its contextual application.

The brief box need not include all the meanings involving a camel’s voice, intensity of strength, or fleeing livestock. Those belong in the root article rather than the short explanation of this word in its verse.

Iddan should also not be translated as “associating partners with The God,” “disbelief,” “having a child,” or “the heavens splitting apart.”

These concepts occur in the surrounding passage but do not constitute the word’s independent meaning.

Conclusion

The Arabic root A D D is associated with intensity, greatness, and horror.

Idd describes something exceptionally great, shocking, or wrongful. Iddah concerns an appalling event or calamity; add, in another use, concerns intensity and strength.

Other recorded branches include loud sound, a camel’s moaning, clamor, stretching something, and livestock bolting.

Some of these uses can be connected through intensity, movement, or sound, but all of them should not be traced to a single supposedly certain historical image.

In the Qur’an, the root occurs only once: Maryam 19:89.

There, iddan qualifies shayʾan and characterizes the claim attributing a child to Al-Raḥmān as profoundly appalling and wrongful.

The claim appears in verse 88. The following verses express the intensity of its condemnation through the heavens approaching rupture, the earth splitting, and the mountains collapsing.

The Qur’an then explicitly explains the reason for the condemnation and rejects attributing a child to Al-Raḥmān.

Idd itself, however, does not mean a child, disbelief, splitting, or collapse.

Nor should it be equated with imr, nukr, or hadd. These words approach the field of intensity and condemnation in some uses, but they have distinct roots and semantic roles.

For Forqan, the basic definition of this family is: idd describes something exceptionally great, difficult, astonishing, or appalling; in a condemnatory context, it can characterize something profoundly wrongful and shocking. Other branches concern intensity of strength, sound, and certain movements, but these meanings do not replace the description of horror in Maryam 19:89.

Accordingly, “profoundly appalling and wrongful” is the most suitable equivalent for this root’s only Qur’anic occurrence.
