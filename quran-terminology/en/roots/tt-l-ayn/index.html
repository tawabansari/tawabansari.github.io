Qur’anic Root Ṭ L ʿ (ط ل ع)

Rising: emerging, becoming visible, the emergence of plant produce, and gaining access to what was hidden

1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

The Arabic root ط ل ع (ṭāʾ–lām–ʿayn) concerns rising, appearing, and becoming visible. طَلَعَتِ الشَّمْسُ (ṭalaʿati al-shamsu) means “the sun rose”; طَلَعَ النَّبَاتُ (ṭalaʿa al-nabātu) describes plants emerging; and اِطَّلَعَ عَلَى الْأَمْرِ (iṭṭalaʿa ʿalā al-amri) means becoming acquainted with something previously unknown to the person.

This family has concrete uses as well as uses involving sight and knowledge. The sun rises and appears on the horizon; a plant bud or cluster emerges from its place; someone surveys the surroundings from a height; and, in another use, a person learns a secret previously concealed from them.

A connection with emergence and gaining access to something can be seen in these uses. This does not mean that every derivative necessarily describes physical movement upward. Learning about something need not involve climbing, just as sunrise does not mean an increase in someone’s knowledge.

The root occurs 19 times in 18 Qur’anic verses. Using the vocalization of the transmission of Ḥafṣ, these occurrences can be organized into the following eight morphological and lexical groups. This does not mean that there are eight independent verb forms or morphological patterns:

  • طَلَعَ (ṭalaʿa) and its verbal forms: twice, describing sunrise, in Al-Kahf 18:17 and Al-Kahf 18:90.
  • طُلُوع (ṭulūʿ): twice, meaning the rising of the sun, in Ṭā Hā 20:130 and Qāf 50:39.
  • مَطْلِع (maṭliʿ): once, in مَطْلِعَ الشَّمْسِ (maṭliʿa al-shamsi), Al-Kahf 18:90.
  • مَطْلَع (maṭlaʿ): once, in مَطْلَعِ الْفَجْرِ (maṭlaʿi al-fajri), Al-Qadr 97:5.
  • طَلْع (ṭalʿ): four times, three concerning palms and one the tree of Zaqqūm: Al-Anʿām 6:99, Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:148, Al-Ṣāffāt 37:65, and Qāf 50:10.
  • أَطْلَعَ (aṭlaʿa), Form IV: once, meaning to make someone aware, in Āl ʿImrān 3:179.
  • اِطَّلَعَ (iṭṭalaʿa) and its verbal forms, Form VIII: seven times, involving observation, looking in, becoming aware, or reaching something.
  • مُطَّلِعُونَ (muṭṭaliʿūn): once, the plural active participle of Form VIII, in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:54.

Other familiar Arabic forms include طَالِع (ṭāliʿ), “rising” or “one that rises”; طَلِيعَة (ṭalīʿah), a scout or advance guard; اِسْتِطْلَاع (istiṭlāʿ), seeking information or reconnaissance; and طَلْعَة (ṭalʿah), someone’s appearance or countenance. These particular forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

The breadth of this family shows why translating it everywhere simply as “rising” is inadequate. Sometimes something appears on the horizon, sometimes tree produce first emerges, and sometimes someone gains visual or intellectual access to something.

2. Semantic core and early Arabic usage

Appearance and emergence from concealment

Arabic lexical studies identify appearance and emergence as particularly clear themes of this root.

At sunrise, the sun becomes visible above the horizon to an observer on earth. When someone appears from behind a rise or an obstruction, Arabic can say طَلَعَ عَلَيْنَا (ṭalaʿa ʿalaynā): “he appeared before us” or “he suddenly came into view.”

Here, rising is not limited to upward movement. Someone may arrive along a road, emerge from behind an obstacle, or suddenly become visible. What stands out is the transition from being unseen to appearing within view.

Accordingly, Arabic uses طَلَعَ (ṭalaʿa) for the sun, moon, and stars. These bodies become visible to the observer when they rise and leave the observer’s field of view when they set.

The actual movement of a body must nevertheless be distinguished from how it appears. The linguistic expression “sunrise” does not by itself present a theory about the movement of the sun or earth. It describes an observed phenomenon.

Reaching an elevated vantage point

Another use connects the root with ascending, reaching a commanding position, and looking out from it.

Lexical explanations use طِلْعُ الْأَكَمَةِ (ṭilʿu al-akamati) for a part of a hill or rise from which, once reached, a person can see the surroundings.

Similarly, طَلِيعَةُ الْجَيْشِ (ṭalīʿatu al-jayshi) refers to those who go ahead of an army to learn about the route and the enemy’s movements.

In this use, rising and observation meet. Someone who reaches a vantage point can see something hidden from their previous position.

This image does not mean that اِطِّلَاع (iṭṭilāʿ) always requires physical ascent. Someone may learn something by hearing a report without moving from where they are.

Iṭṭilāʿ: something becoming accessible to a person’s awareness

The Arabic expression أَطْلَعْتُكَ عَلَى الْأَمْرِ (aṭlaʿtuka ʿalā al-amri) means “I made you aware of the matter.”

Something previously unclear to the person becomes accessible to their awareness. The connection with appearance is recognizable, but here something becomes clear to human understanding; a physical object need not appear before the eyes.

The verb اِطَّلَعَ (iṭṭalaʿa) can likewise mean becoming aware, observing, or gaining access to something.

There is an important difference between أَطْلَعَهُ (aṭlaʿahu) and اِطَّلَعَ (iṭṭalaʿa). In the transitive use of Form IV, someone or something makes another person aware. In Form VIII, the subject gains access to observation or knowledge.

This morphological distinction does not establish an absolute rule that Form VIII always conveys a difficult, secret effort to uncover a mystery. The Qur’an uses this form for direct looking and even for fire reaching hearts.

The precise meaning must therefore be determined from the verb, preposition, object, and sentence context.

Ṭalʿ: the first emergence of palm produce

An important branch of this family is طَلْع (ṭalʿ), associated with the date palm.

In Arabic, طَلْعُ النَّخْلِ (ṭalʿu al-nakhli) denotes the structure that emerges from the palm at the beginning of its inflorescence and produce. In its initial stages it is enclosed in a covering for which Arabic records names including كُفُرَّى (kufurrā) and كَافُور (kāfūr).

When this covering opens, the strands and branches bearing flowers and, subsequently, developing fruits become visible.

Thus, in palm terminology, ṭalʿ can refer to the initial flowering and fruiting structure. Lexical usage sometimes extends the name to its covering or to what is inside that covering.

These stages matter. Ṭalʿ should not always be equated with ripe dates. Ripe dates belong to a later stage of growth, whereas ṭalʿ concerns the early emergence of the produce.

This name has a clear connection with appearance and emergence: ṭalʿ is something that emerges from the palm.

Evidence from poetry and early Arabic life

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Aws ibn Ḥajar describes a bow:

كَتُومٌ طِلَاعُ الْكَفِّ لَا دُونَ مِلْئِهَا
وَلَا عَجْسُهَا عَنْ مَوْضِعِ الْكَفِّ أَفْضَلَا

Its sense is: “A sound, uncracked bow, its middle exactly filling the palm: neither smaller than the hand’s capacity nor extending beyond the place of the palm.” In the explanation of this verse, كَتُوم (katūm) is understood as a bow without cracks or defects; the word’s lexical entry also records the meaning of a quiet bow.

In this example, طِلَاعُ الْكَفِّ (ṭilāʿu al-kaffi) concerns filling the palm’s capacity. Lexical sources record this use under the root, showing that the family’s early Arabic semantic field was not confined to sunrise or acquiring knowledge.

The sense of fullness or measure should not be extended to every derivative. This is a particular expression describing a bow’s dimensions and shape; its connection with appearance or projection does not, by itself, establish its derivational history.

Arabic sources also record scouting, observation from a height, and palm ṭalʿ. Recording an expression in a classical dictionary does not necessarily prove its presence in a particular pre-Islamic poem. Each witness requires its own assessment of date, attribution, and actual meaning.

Taken together, the Qur’an and lexical evidence show several fields of use: the appearance of celestial bodies or people, observation from a vantage point, making information known, and the emergence of palm produce. The poetic expression ṭilāʿu al-kaffi also concerns measure and fullness. Dictionary attestation alone cannot establish a definite pre-Qur’anic date for every one of these expressions.

3. Sunrise: appearance on the horizon and the marking of time

The sun in the account of the Companions of the Cave

Al-Kahf, 18:17

وَتَرَى الشَّمْسَ إِذَا طَلَعَتْ تَزَاوَرُ عَنْ كَهْفِهِمْ ذَاتَ الْيَمِينِ وَإِذَا غَرَبَتْ تَقْرِضُهُمْ ذَاتَ الشِّمَالِ وَهُمْ فِي فَجْوَةٍ مِنْهُ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ مِنْ آيَاتِ اللَّهِ ۗ مَنْ يَهْدِ اللَّهُ فَهُوَ الْمُهْتَدِ ۖ وَمَنْ يُضْلِلْ فَلَنْ تَجِدَ لَهُ وَلِيًّا مُرْشِدًا

“You see the sun, when it rises, inclining away from their cave to the right, and when it sets, passing them on the left, while they are in an open space within it. That is among the signs of The God. Whoever The God guides is the rightly guided one; and whoever He leads astray, you will find no guiding protector for him.”

Here, طَلَعَتْ (ṭalaʿat) refers to the sun and stands in contrast to غَرَبَتْ (gharabat), “it set.”

Their pairing clarifies the verb: rising is the sun’s emergence and appearance on the horizon; setting is its descent and disappearance from the horizon.

However, تَزَاوَرُ (tazāwaru) and تَقْرِضُهُمْ (taqriḍuhum) belong to other roots. The apparent movement of the light and the cave’s position are understood from the combination of these verbs and clauses, rather than from Ṭ L ʿ alone.

Consequently, rising should not be defined here as inclining, turning rightward, or shining into the cave.

Nor does this verse alone establish the cave’s definite geographical location, its latitude and longitude, or the precise orientation of its entrance. Such details require additional evidence.

Here, sunrise is a concrete, observable phenomenon. The verse concerns the sun’s position relative to the cave and the companions’ circumstances, rather than presenting a new definition of sunrise.

Rising in contrast to setting

Ṭā Hā, 20:130

فَاصْبِرْ عَلَىٰ مَا يَقُولُونَ وَسَبِّحْ بِحَمْدِ رَبِّكَ قَبْلَ طُلُوعِ الشَّمْسِ وَقَبْلَ غُرُوبِهَا ۖ وَمِنْ آنَاءِ اللَّيْلِ فَسَبِّحْ وَأَطْرَافَ النَّهَارِ لَعَلَّكَ تَرْضَىٰ

“So be steadfast in the face of what they say, and glorify your Lord with praise before the rising of the sun and before its setting. Glorify Him also during hours of the night and at the edges of the day, so that you may be content.”

Here, طُلُوعِ الشَّمْسِ (ṭulūʿi al-shamsi) means the rising of the sun.

طُلُوع (ṭulūʿ) is the verbal noun of طَلَعَ (ṭalaʿa), denoting the event of rising itself. The word قَبْل (qabl), “before,” locates the commanded action relative to that event.

Rising is also paired with setting in this verse. The contrast identifies two clear markers in the course of the day.

Qāf, 50:39

فَاصْبِرْ عَلَىٰ مَا يَقُولُونَ وَسَبِّحْ بِحَمْدِ رَبِّكَ قَبْلَ طُلُوعِ الشَّمْسِ وَقَبْلَ الْغُرُوبِ

“So be steadfast in the face of what they say, and glorify your Lord with praise before the rising of the sun and before sunset.”

Here too, ṭulūʿ has the same natural and temporal meaning.

Both verses connect the command to glorify with the times of sunrise and sunset. The meanings of ṣalāh and tasbīḥ, and their relationship with times of worship, require separate study.

The root alone cannot establish the number of daily prayers, how they are performed, or a complete system of worship times. Its role in these two verses is to identify sunrise as a time marker.

Maṭliʿa al-shamsi: a place in the direction of sunrise

Al-Kahf, 18:90

حَتَّىٰ إِذَا بَلَغَ مَطْلِعَ الشَّمْسِ وَجَدَهَا تَطْلُعُ عَلَىٰ قَوْمٍ لَمْ نَجْعَلْ لَهُمْ مِنْ دُونِهَا سِتْرًا

“Until, when he reached the region of the sun’s rising, he found it rising upon a people for whom We had provided no covering against it.”

This verse contains two forms from the family: مَطْلِعَ (maṭliʿa) and تَطْلُعُ (taṭluʿu).

مَطْلِع (maṭliʿ) can indicate a place of rising, while taṭluʿu is the imperfect verb “rises” or “is rising.”

The context concerns Dhū al-Qarnayn’s journey. Maṭliʿ al-shams therefore identifies a direction or region toward sunrise, without necessarily specifying a physical point where the sun emerges from the earth or the end of the world.

This can be compared with the ordinary spatial use of “the east.” Speaking of a journey eastward means traveling in the direction from which the sun rises, rather than claiming that the sun is born at a location on earth.

The clause وَجَدَهَا تَطْلُعُ عَلَىٰ قَوْمٍ (wajadahā taṭluʿu ʿalā qawmin) also states that Dhū al-Qarnayn found the sun rising upon a people.

The root itself does not establish exactly where those people lived, which natural or social conditions their lack of covering involved, or the geographical route of Dhū al-Qarnayn’s journey.

The lexical meaning of maṭliʿ should therefore not be turned into a definite claim about world geography or the structure of the cosmos.

4. Maṭlaʿi al-fajri: daybreak and its difference from sunrise

Al-Qadr, 97:5

سَلَامٌ هِيَ حَتَّىٰ مَطْلَعِ الْفَجْرِ

“That night is peace and tranquility until the breaking of dawn.”

This verse contains مَطْلَعِ الْفَجْرِ (maṭlaʿi al-fajri).

In the transmission of Ḥafṣ from ʿĀṣim, مَطْلِعَ الشَّمْسِ (maṭliʿa al-shamsi) in Al-Kahf 18:90 differs in vocalization from مَطْلَعِ الْفَجْرِ (maṭlaʿi al-fajri) in Al-Qadr 97:5: the lām has kasrah in the former and fatḥah in the latter. Among the ten readings, Al-Kisāʾī and Khalaf the Tenth read the word in Al-Qadr 97:5 with kasrah on the lām. This vocalic distinction therefore cannot be generalized to every reading.

Arabic morphological explanations connect both مَطْلَع (maṭlaʿ) and مَطْلِع (maṭliʿ) with the event or place of rising. Some analyses favor the form with fatḥah for the verbal noun and that with kasrah for a place noun. Arabic usage and variant vocalizations nevertheless prevent an absolute distinction covering every occurrence.

In Al-Qadr, حَتَّىٰ (ḥattā), “until,” marks the end of a period. The appropriate contextual meaning is therefore the event of daybreak, rather than a geographical location.

This differs importantly from Al-Kahf 18:90. There, Dhū al-Qarnayn reaches a region toward sunrise. In Al-Qadr, the description of the night continues until dawn begins.

فَجْر (fajr) and شَمْس (shams) are also different words. Fajr concerns the appearance of light at the beginning of the day; sunrise is the appearance of the sun on the horizon.

Maṭlaʿ al-fajr should consequently not be translated casually as “sunrise.” They are distinct events in time.

Nor does maṭlaʿ itself mean peace, mercy, or blessing. Those descriptions come from the surah’s verses and the clause سَلَامٌ هِيَ (salāmun hiya). This root serves only to mark the temporal endpoint of the description.

5. Ṭalʿ: emerging palm produce and the beginning of fruiting

The uses of طَلْع (ṭalʿ) provide particularly clear examples of the root’s connection with emergence from concealment.

Ṭalʿ and date clusters

Al-Anʿām, 6:99

وَهُوَ الَّذِي أَنْزَلَ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً فَأَخْرَجْنَا بِهِ نَبَاتَ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ فَأَخْرَجْنَا مِنْهُ خَضِرًا نُخْرِجُ مِنْهُ حَبًّا مُتَرَاكِبًا وَمِنَ النَّخْلِ مِنْ طَلْعِهَا قِنْوَانٌ دَانِيَةٌ وَجَنَّاتٍ مِنْ أَعْنَابٍ وَالزَّيْتُونَ وَالرُّمَّانَ مُشْتَبِهًا وَغَيْرَ مُتَشَابِهٍ ۗ انْظُرُوا إِلَىٰ ثَمَرِهِ إِذَا أَثْمَرَ وَيَنْعِهِ ۚ إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكُمْ لَآيَاتٍ لِقَوْمٍ يُؤْمِنُونَ

“He is the One who sent water down from the sky. With it We brought forth every kind of vegetation; from it We brought forth green growth, from which We produce grain arranged in layers. And from the palms, from their ṭalʿ, come near, hanging clusters; and gardens of grapes, olives, and pomegranates, some alike and some unlike. Look at their fruit when it bears and at its ripening. In these there are indeed signs for people who believe.”

Here, طَلْعِهَا (ṭalʿihā) refers to a stage or structure in the emergence of palm produce.

قِنْوَان (qinwān), the plural of قِنْو (qinw), concerns clusters or bunches of palm fruit. Although ṭalʿ and qinwān are connected in the development of the produce, they are distinct words.

Here, ṭalʿ concerns the initial flowering and fruiting structure, while qinwān denotes the clusters that emerge from it.

دَانِيَة (dāniyah) describes the clusters as near and hanging. This meaning does not come from ṭalʿ itself.

In some contexts, “date cluster” may help readers approach the intended meaning of ṭalʿ, but it does not fully explain the difference from qinwān.

For closer study, “emerging palm produce” or “early palm inflorescence” is nearer the lexical meaning. In general Persian, the equivalent of “date-cluster bud” is more familiar and less technical.

The end of the verse also draws attention to fruit and its ripening. The Qur’an presents the emergence and growth of produce as a connected picture, but all of these stages should not be assigned to the word ṭalʿ as independent meanings.

Ṭalʿ described as haḍīm

Al-Shuʿarāʾ, 26:148

وَزُرُوعٍ وَنَخْلٍ طَلْعُهَا هَضِيمٌ

“And cultivated fields and palms whose ṭalʿ is soft and tender.”

The verse occurs among the blessings recalled for the people of Thamūd.

طَلْعُهَا (ṭalʿuhā) refers to the initial structure of palm produce, but هَضِيم (haḍīm) is a description from a different lexical family.

Several understandings of haḍīm are recorded in Arabic usage and early explanations: soft and tender; abundant and compact; closely packed; or produce that has reached ripeness and softness.

This variation shows why haḍīm should not be restricted to a precise stage of date growth without separate investigation.

For a basic translation, “soft and tender” is defensible, although some evidence also emphasizes the compactness and close arrangement of the ṭalʿ structure.

For the present root, the central point is that softness, tenderness, and density come from haḍīm and the context, rather than from ṭalʿ itself.

The verse also shows the Qur’an using ṭalʿ for palm produce in the familiar agricultural world. Understanding its natural meaning should begin with these concrete uses.

Ṭalʿ described as naḍīd

Qāf, 50:10

وَالنَّخْلَ بَاسِقَاتٍ لَهَا طَلْعٌ نَضِيدٌ

“And the tall palms, bearing ṭalʿ arranged in closely ordered layers.”

Here, بَاسِقَات (bāsiqāt) describes the palms’ height, while نَضِيد (naḍīd) concerns the layering and arrangement of the parts of their ṭalʿ.

Neither the trees’ height nor the orderly arrangement of the clusters should therefore be built into the lexical meaning of ṭalʿ.

Ṭalʿ is the structure associated with the emergence of the produce; naḍīd describes how its parts are arranged.

This verse follows a description of rain, gardens, and cultivated grain. The next verse also mentions provision for the servants and the revival of dead land.

Ṭalʿ is therefore part of the picture of plant growth and fertility. Provision, arrangement, and the tree’s growth are each understood from the combined verses.

The ṭalʿ of Zaqqūm: a use extending beyond palms

Al-Ṣāffāt, 37:65

طَلْعُهَا كَأَنَّهُ رُءُوسُ الشَّيَاطِينِ

“Its emerging fruit is as though it were the heads of devils.”

This occurrence is particularly important for understanding the root: unlike the previous three examples, ṭalʿ here does not describe palms.

The preceding verses speak of the tree of Zaqqūm. The suffix ـهَا (-hā), “its,” in طَلْعُهَا (ṭalʿuhā) therefore refers to that tree.

In this context, ṭalʿ denotes what emerges from the tree as its produce or emerging fruit.

This establishes that Qur’anic ṭalʿ need not be limited to palms. Although the word is particularly associated with palms in Arabic agriculture, the Qur’an also uses it for the outgrowths of Zaqqūm.

This does not establish that Zaqqūm necessarily has the same botanical structure of flowers and fruit as a palm. Its referent belongs here to a description of the Hereafter, and its actual nature is not fully explained.

Comparing its ṭalʿ to devils’ heads is also a separate description. The ugliness or terror of the image comes from the comparison, rather than from the lexical meaning of ṭalʿ.

Nor does the verse define the actual shape of devils’ heads so that the physical structure of Zaqqūm’s fruit could be reconstructed from it.

Ṭalʿ here names something emerging from the tree; the following comparison presents a terrifying image of it.

6. Iṭṭalaʿa: observing, looking in, and becoming aware

Form VIII is one of the root’s more extensive branches of Qur’anic usage.

In this form, someone may observe something, look toward something, or learn something hidden; the verb may also describe something reaching a particular place.

These uses are related but should not be reduced to a single fixed translation.

Becoming aware of something hidden in others’ conduct

Al-Māʾidah, 5:13

فَبِمَا نَقْضِهِمْ مِيثَاقَهُمْ لَعَنَّاهُمْ وَجَعَلْنَا قُلُوبَهُمْ قَاسِيَةً ۖ يُحَرِّفُونَ الْكَلِمَ عَنْ مَوَاضِعِهِ وَنَسُوا حَظًّا مِمَّا ذُكِّرُوا بِهِ ۚ وَلَا تَزَالُ تَطَّلِعُ عَلَىٰ خَائِنَةٍ مِنْهُمْ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا مِنْهُمْ ۖ فَاعْفُ عَنْهُمْ وَاصْفَحْ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُحِبُّ الْمُحْسِنِينَ

“Because they broke their covenant, We removed them from mercy and made their hearts hard. They alter words from their proper places and forgot a portion of what they had been reminded of. You continue to become aware of treachery from them, except for a few of them. So pardon them and overlook; indeed, The God loves those who do good.”

Here, تَطَّلِعُ عَلَىٰ (taṭṭaliʿu ʿalā) denotes becoming aware of something.

Its semantic object is خَائِنَة (khāʾinah). The subject of the awareness is therefore treachery or treacherous conduct becoming apparent to the addressee.

The meaning here is closer to awareness than to physical ascent. A person learns something that was previously unclear to them.

Two important qualifications must nevertheless be preserved.

First, the verse speaks of treachery; this does not justify defining the root itself as “uncovering treachery.” Awareness of treachery results from the verb’s combination with its object in this particular verse.

Second, إِلَّا قَلِيلًا مِنْهُمْ (illā qalīlan minhum), “except for a few of them,” limits the statement’s scope. The Qur’an does not include every member of that group in a judgment without exception.

Lexically, this awareness could arise through observation, a report, or conduct becoming apparent. The verse does not specify the exact means of learning, so the verb should not be restricted to spying, covert surveillance, or organized investigation.

Observing the Companions of the Cave

Al-Kahf, 18:18

وَتَحْسَبُهُمْ أَيْقَاظًا وَهُمْ رُقُودٌ ۚ وَنُقَلِّبُهُمْ ذَاتَ الْيَمِينِ وَذَاتَ الشِّمَالِ ۖ وَكَلْبُهُمْ بَاسِطٌ ذِرَاعَيْهِ بِالْوَصِيدِ ۚ لَوِ اطَّلَعْتَ عَلَيْهِمْ لَوَلَّيْتَ مِنْهُمْ فِرَارًا وَلَمُلِئْتَ مِنْهُمْ رُعْبًا

“You would think them awake, while they are asleep. We turn them to the right and to the left, while their dog stretches out its forelegs at the entrance. Had you looked in upon them, you would have turned away from them in flight and been filled with terror at them.”

Here, اطَّلَعْتَ عَلَيْهِمْ (iṭṭalaʿta ʿalayhim) concerns direct observation.

If the hypothetical observer had gained access to the companions’ condition and seen them, that observer would have fled in fear.

لَوِ اطَّلَعْتَ (lawi iṭṭalaʿta) introduces a conditional construction. The verse does not say that the addressee actually saw them or fled; it states what would follow if they were observed.

Fear and flight are also not meanings of the verb itself. They are consequences assigned to the observation by the conditional sentence.

This use shows that Qur’anic iṭṭilāʿ is not always knowledge of an abstract secret. It can refer to directly seeing people and their circumstances.

Has someone gained knowledge of the unseen?

Maryam, 19:78

أَطَّلَعَ الْغَيْبَ أَمِ اتَّخَذَ عِنْدَ الرَّحْمَٰنِ عَهْدًا

“Has he gained knowledge of the unseen, or taken a covenant with the Entirely Merciful?”

This verse follows discussion of someone claiming future prosperity for himself.

The Qur’an’s question tests the basis of that claim: has the person actually learned the unseen, or does he possess a covenant from The God?

Here, أَطَّلَعَ (aṭṭalaʿa) means gaining access to knowledge, and الْغَيْبَ (al-ghayba), “the unseen,” is its subject matter. This is an interrogative hamzah followed by اِطَّلَعَ (iṭṭalaʿa), Form VIII: the verb’s connecting hamzah is omitted after the interrogative hamzah, and the tāʾ of Form VIII has assimilated to ṭāʾ. It must not be confused with أَطْلَعَ (aṭlaʿa), Form IV, “to make someone aware.”

This resembles Al-Māʾidah 5:13 in that both concern knowing something. In Al-Māʾidah, however, the subject is people’s conduct; in Maryam, it is the unseen and a claim to know the future.

Here the verb appears without عَلَى (ʿalā). This illustrates the different syntactic constructions in which iṭṭalaʿa can occur.

The question does not affirm the person’s knowledge of the unseen. On the contrary, the context challenges the basis of his claim.

The presence of the verb therefore must not be taken as evidence that the person actually gained knowledge of the unseen.

Looking out from a vantage point

Al-Ṣāffāt, 37:54

قَالَ هَلْ أَنْتُمْ مُطَّلِعُونَ

“He said, ‘Would you like to look?’”

Al-Ṣāffāt, 37:55

فَاطَّلَعَ فَرَآهُ فِي سَوَاءِ الْجَحِيمِ

“Then he looked and saw him in the midst of the blazing Fire.”

These two verses offer particularly clear examples of observation in this family.

In the first, مُطَّلِعُونَ (muṭṭaliʿūn) is the plural active participle of Form VIII. In the second, the verb فَاطَّلَعَ (fa-iṭṭalaʿa) occurs, followed by its result in فَرَآهُ (fa-raʾāhu), “and he saw him.”

The relationship between these verbs matters. Fa-iṭṭalaʿa concerns turning to observe and gaining a view; fa-raʾāhu states the seeing of the particular person.

Iṭṭilāʿ and رُؤْيَة (ruʾyah), seeing, are consequently very close here. They occur in a connected construction, however, and their distinct roles should be preserved.

In this context, someone among the people of Paradise speaks of a former companion and then sees him in the Fire.

The verb alone cannot establish a definite theory about the structure of Paradise and the Fire, the distance between them, or the means of observation between them.

The verse states that observation occurs, rather than giving physical details of how it happens.

7. Iṭṭilāʿ in Pharaoh’s speech: ascending toward the sky or gaining a view?

Al-Qaṣaṣ, 28:38

وَقَالَ فِرْعَوْنُ يَا أَيُّهَا الْمَلَأُ مَا عَلِمْتُ لَكُمْ مِنْ إِلَٰهٍ غَيْرِي فَأَوْقِدْ لِي يَا هَامَانُ عَلَى الطِّينِ فَاجْعَلْ لِي صَرْحًا لَعَلِّي أَطَّلِعُ إِلَىٰ إِلَٰهِ مُوسَىٰ وَإِنِّي لَأَظُنُّهُ مِنَ الْكَاذِبِينَ

“Pharaoh said, ‘O chiefs, I know of no deity for you other than myself. So kindle a fire over clay for me, Hāmān, and build me a lofty structure, so that I may look toward the deity of Moses. I consider him one of the liars.’”

The verb أَطَّلِعُ (aṭṭaliʿu) occurs here in Pharaoh’s speech.

Building a lofty structure provides the setting. The connection between iṭṭilāʿ, reaching an elevated position, and looking is therefore clear in this use.

Pharaoh wants a structure built from which to look toward the deity of Moses, or presents the act as a way to test Moses’ claim.

Pharaoh’s speech does not establish that the Qur’an locates The God in a physical place above the sky. The statement is attributed to Pharaoh in the context of his denial and opposition to Moses.

The construction أَطَّلِعُ إِلَىٰ (aṭṭaliʿu ilā), with ilā, also directs attention toward the direction or object of the looking.

The root itself does not mean “seeing The God.” What Pharaoh wishes to observe is supplied by the verb’s complement and the complete sentence.

Pharaoh’s speech repeated in Ghāfir

Ghāfir, 40:37

أَسْبَابَ السَّمَاوَاتِ فَأَطَّلِعَ إِلَىٰ إِلَٰهِ مُوسَىٰ وَإِنِّي لَأَظُنُّهُ كَاذِبًا ۚ وَكَذَٰلِكَ زُيِّنَ لِفِرْعَوْنَ سُوءُ عَمَلِهِ وَصُدَّ عَنِ السَّبِيلِ ۚ وَمَا كَيْدُ فِرْعَوْنَ إِلَّا فِي تَبَابٍ

“The ways of the heavens, so that I may look toward the deity of Moses; I consider him a liar. Thus Pharaoh’s evil conduct was made attractive to him, and he was turned away from the way. Pharaoh’s scheming leads only to ruin.”

This verse continues Pharaoh’s speech in the preceding verse, where he asks Hāmān to build him a lofty structure so that he may reach the ways of the heavens.

Here too, فَأَطَّلِعَ (fa-aṭṭaliʿa) occurs with إِلَىٰ (ilā).

Together, the two verses make clear that iṭṭilāʿ in Pharaoh’s speech concerns reaching a position from which to look, or gaining the visual access he claims to seek.

The verb’s meaning should not be restricted to tower building or physical ascent. The lofty structure and the ways of the heavens belong to the context. In this construction, the verb itself concerns turning toward something to observe it.

Another distinction matters. In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:38, Pharaoh says لَعَلِّي أَطَّلِعُ (laʿallī aṭṭaliʿu). In Ghāfir 40:37, the transmission of Ḥafṣ from ʿĀṣim has فَأَطَّلِعَ (fa-aṭṭaliʿa), with the verb in the subjunctive, analyzed as the response to the hope expressed by laʿallī. The remaining readings among the ten canonical readings have the indicative فَأَطَّلِعُ (fa-aṭṭaliʿu) in Ghāfir, coordinating it with أَبْلُغُ (ablughu), “I reach.” The inflectional difference therefore also depends on the reading; in both contexts, what Pharaoh seeks is access to observation.

Neither verse says that Pharaoh actually reached or observed the deity of Moses. They report his own wish and claim.

8. Li-yuṭliʿakum ʿalā al-ghaybi: making people aware and the limits of knowledge of the unseen

Āl ʿImrān, 3:179

مَا كَانَ اللَّهُ لِيَذَرَ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ عَلَىٰ مَا أَنْتُمْ عَلَيْهِ حَتَّىٰ يَمِيزَ الْخَبِيثَ مِنَ الطَّيِّبِ ۗ وَمَا كَانَ اللَّهُ لِيُطْلِعَكُمْ عَلَى الْغَيْبِ وَلَٰكِنَّ اللَّهَ يَجْتَبِي مِنْ رُسُلِهِ مَنْ يَشَاءُ ۖ فَآمِنُوا بِاللَّهِ وَرُسُلِهِ ۚ وَإِنْ تُؤْمِنُوا وَتَتَّقُوا فَلَكُمْ أَجْرٌ عَظِيمٌ

“The God would not leave the believers in the condition you are in until He distinguishes the impure from the pure. Nor would The God make you aware of the unseen; rather, The God chooses whom He wills from His Messengers. So believe in The God and His Messengers. If you believe and remain mindful, you will have a great reward.”

لِيُطْلِعَكُمْ (li-yuṭliʿakum) in this verse is Form IV.

Its distinction from اِطَّلَعَ (iṭṭalaʿa) deserves attention. In Form VIII, the subject gains access to observation or knowledge; in أَطْلَعَهُ عَلَى الْأَمْرِ (aṭlaʿahu ʿalā al-amri), someone makes another person aware of a matter.

Here the subject is The God, and the suffix ـكُمْ (-kum) refers to the addressees. The subject matter of the awareness is الْغَيْب (al-ghayb), the unseen.

The clause therefore means making the addressees aware of the unseen, rather than physically raising them to a hidden location.

The verse continues by describing the choosing of Messengers. This connection shows that, in this context, awareness of unseen matters depends on divine will and selection.

The verse does not establish that every Messenger, simply by being a Messenger, possesses independent and unlimited knowledge of everything unseen. It makes no such claim.

The difference between “making aware” and “becoming aware” should also be preserved. The God can make someone aware of a matter without that person being the independent source of the knowledge.

Comparison with Maryam 19:78 is also instructive. Maryam questions someone’s claim to knowledge of the unseen; Āl ʿImrān concerns the limits of making the unseen known by The God.

Despite their different contexts, both show that iṭṭilāʿ can concern intellectual access, rather than only seeing with the eyes.

9. Taṭṭaliʿu ʿalā al-afʾidati: fire reaching the hearts

Al-Humazah, 104:7

الَّتِي تَطَّلِعُ عَلَى الْأَفْئِدَةِ

“That fire which reaches the hearts.”

This is an important and distinctive use of the root.

The preceding verses speak of الْحُطَمَة (al-ḥuṭamah), subsequently identified as نَارُ اللَّهِ الْمُوقَدَةُ (nāru Allāhi al-mūqadatu), “the kindled fire of The God.” The relative pronoun الَّتِي (allatī), “which,” therefore refers to that kindled fire.

Here, تَطَّلِعُ (taṭṭaliʿu) is followed by عَلَى الْأَفْئِدَةِ (ʿalā al-afʾidati), “upon the hearts.”

In some Arabic uses, iṭṭilāʿ can concern something reaching or extending into a place, in addition to observation and becoming aware.

Since fire is the subject here, reaching and penetrating fit the context better than “becoming aware.”

“Fire which reaches the hearts” therefore gives a more natural translation.

It should not be changed into “fire that learns people’s thoughts.” That meaning has no clear fit with the subject or the construction describing punishment.

At the same time, fire reaching hearts should not be turned into a specific physical theory about how organs burn. The verse describes the intensity and extent of the fire’s effect without detailing its bodily mechanism.

This use matters lexically because it shows that Form VIII is not limited to human observation. It can sometimes indicate something reaching an interior or a particular place.

The connection with the root’s central branch is gaining access to or reaching something. Here, however, the subject is not knowledge or understanding.

10. Comparison with related roots

Ṭ L ʿ (ṭulūʿ) and Ẓ H R (ẓuhūr): rising and appearing

Arabic ظُهُور (ẓuhūr) concerns something becoming apparent and has a broad semantic field.

Ṭulūʿ can also involve appearance. In its familiar uses, however, it often emphasizes something emerging from the horizon, behind an obstruction, or its previous position.

Sunrise, plant emergence, and someone appearing from behind a rise are examples.

This does not establish that every ṭulūʿ must involve vertical movement or that every ẓuhūr must be appearance without movement. Arabic usage as a whole does not support such a fixed boundary.

Ṭ L ʿ (ṭulūʿ) and Sh R Q (shurūq): sunrise and the east

The root ش ر ق (shīn–rāʾ–qāf) concerns the east, brightness, and the direction of sunrise.

Ṭulūʿ describes the event of the sun appearing, while مَشْرِق (mashriq), in its spatial use, denotes the direction or place of its rising.

In Al-Kahf 18:90, maṭliʿ al-shams approaches the concept of the east in terms of direction.

This proximity does not mean that maṭliʿ and mashriq are completely synonymous in every verse. Maṭliʿ can concern a place or time of rising, and mashriq belongs to a lexical family with its own uses.

Ṭ L ʿ (ṭulūʿ) and Gh R B (ghurūb): appearing and disappearing

The two words occur together in Al-Kahf 18:17 and Ṭā Hā 20:130.

Ṭulūʿ is the sun’s rising, and غُرُوب (ghurūb) its sinking from the horizon.

This contrast helps mark the parts of the day. It does not mean that every use of either root always has a temporal relationship with the sun.

For example, iṭṭilāʿ meaning “becoming aware” does not have a simple, direct opposite in ghurūb.

Ṭ L ʿ (iṭṭilāʿ) and ʿ L M (ʿilm): gaining awareness and knowing

عِلْم (ʿilm) belongs to a very extensive Qur’anic family concerning knowledge and awareness.

In some uses, iṭṭilāʿ also concerns becoming aware or making something known.

Iṭṭilāʿ, however, more prominently involves access to something previously unapparent, while ʿilm can simply state knowledge of a fact.

This distinction should not be made absolute. Arabic iṭṭilāʿ can also refer to established awareness, and ʿilm may have been acquired through discovery and observation.

Their precise distinction in verses about the unseen must be assessed through sentence structure and context.

Ṭ L ʿ (iṭṭilāʿ) and N Ẓ R (naẓar): looking and observing

نَظَر (naẓar) can mean looking in its primary use, extending in some contexts to reflection and examination.

Iṭṭilāʿ sometimes concerns looking from a commanding position, looking in, or gaining a view.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:54 and Al-Ṣāffāt 37:55, iṭṭilāʿ accompanies seeing; in Maryam 19:78, the subject is knowledge of the unseen.

Consequently, every instance cannot be translated simply as “looking with the eyes.”

Ṭ L ʿ (ṭalʿ) and N B T (nabāt): emerging produce and plant growth

نَبَات (nabāt), from ن ب ت (nūn–bāʾ–tāʾ), concerns plant growth.

In palm usage, ṭalʿ denotes a particular part of the beginning of its flowering and fruiting.

Every ṭalʿ therefore relates to plant growth, but not every instance of plant growth is called ṭalʿ.

A leaf bud, an ear of wheat, and palm ṭalʿ, for example, do not have the same botanical structure.

Ṭalʿ is also used for Zaqqūm in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:65. Palm agricultural terminology should therefore not be imposed on every occurrence without qualification.

Ṭ L ʿ (ṭalʿ) and Th M R (thamar): the emergence of produce and fruit

ثَمَر (thamar), in its familiar use, concerns a tree’s produce and fruit.

Ṭalʿ concerns a stage and structure from which that produce emerges.

In Al-Anʿām 6:99, the Qur’an speaks of palm ṭalʿ and subsequently of fruit and its ripening. The context shows that the two families are related but distinct.

This does not mean ṭalʿ can never include developing fruit. Arabic naming can encompass adjacent stages. Nevertheless, “fully ripe dates” is not its precise, invariable meaning.

11. Translation recommendations for Forqan

Translations of the main forms should follow the word’s actual use in its particular verse.

طَلَعَ (ṭalaʿa) and طُلُوع (ṭulūʿ): for the sun, “rise” or “rising” is appropriate. The ordinary Persian expression for “sunrise” is also clear and familiar.

مَطْلِعَ الشَّمْسِ (maṭliʿa al-shamsi): in Al-Kahf 18:90, “the direction of sunrise” or “the region of the sun’s rising” is suitable. The translation should not create an image of reaching the physical end of the world.

مَطْلَعِ الْفَجْرِ (maṭlaʿi al-fajri): in Al-Qadr 97:5, “daybreak” or “the breaking of dawn” is recommended. It is distinct from sunrise.

طَلْع (ṭalʿ): for palms, the Persian equivalent of “date-cluster bud” is natural and understandable. A lexical explanation can clarify that this is the initial structure in the emergence of palm flowers and produce.

In Al-Anʿām 6:99 and Qāf 50:10, where greater precision is needed, “palm ṭalʿ” with a short explanation can be used.

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:148, “the palm’s soft, tender ṭalʿ” is suggested, while preserving the qualification that the precise meaning of haḍīm requires its own discussion.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:65, “its emerging fruit” or “its ṭalʿ” is preferable. The translation should not turn Zaqqūm into a date palm without evidence.

أَطْلَعَ (aṭlaʿa): in Āl ʿImrān 3:179, “make aware” is appropriate. لِيُطْلِعَكُمْ عَلَى الْغَيْبِ (li-yuṭliʿakum ʿalā al-ghaybi) can be rendered “to make you aware of the unseen.”

اِطَّلَعَ (iṭṭalaʿa): depending on context, it can be translated as “become aware,” “observe,” “look,” “look in,” or “gain a view.”

In Al-Māʾidah 5:13, “you become aware” is appropriate; in Al-Kahf 18:18, “had you looked in upon them”; in Maryam 19:78, “has he gained knowledge of the unseen?”; and in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:55, “then he looked.”

For Pharaoh’s speech in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:38 and Ghāfir 40:37, “I may look” or “I may observe” fits the context of constructing a lofty building.

مُطَّلِعُونَ (muṭṭaliʿūn): in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:54, “would you like to look?” is natural and suitable.

In Al-Humazah 104:7, “reaches the hearts” is preferable for تَطَّلِعُ عَلَى الْأَفْئِدَةِ (taṭṭaliʿu ʿalā al-afʾidati). “Becomes aware of the hearts” does not fit the context of fire.

Throughout, the basic translation should be short and natural. The image of rising and appearing can be explained in the root study, but invented Persian compounds equivalent to “emergent-awareness” or “epistemic peering” should not replace clear, familiar wording.

12. Conclusion

The Arabic root Ṭ L ʿ concerns rising, appearing, and becoming visible. This is clear in sunrise, the emergence of palm produce, and people coming into view.

In another branch of the same family, iṭṭilāʿ concerns looking, observing, and gaining awareness. Its connection with appearance is recognizable: something previously hidden becomes apparent to a person.

This connection should not collapse every use into a single action. Sunrise, observation from a vantage point, learning about the unseen, and fire reaching hearts each have a different construction and context.

In the Qur’an, the two Form I verbal occurrences and the two occurrences of ṭulūʿ concern the sun. Maṭliʿ/maṭlaʿ occurs once in a journey toward the region of sunrise and once in marking the night’s endpoint at daybreak.

Ṭalʿ occurs four times. Three concern palms and the emergence of their produce; one concerns Zaqqūm. The fourth prevents restricting its Qur’anic meaning everywhere to “palm dates.”

In uses concerning awareness, aṭlaʿa means making aware, and iṭṭalaʿa concerns observation or becoming aware. In Al-Humazah 104:7, however, the same family occurs in a construction describing fire reaching hearts. “Knowing” must therefore not be imposed on every Form VIII occurrence either.

For Forqan, the family’s final definition is:

In its familiar semantic core, Ṭ L ʿ concerns rising and becoming apparent. In natural uses, it describes the sun appearing and plant produce emerging. In human uses, it can concern observing or learning something previously beyond a person’s access. In some constructions, it conveys reaching a particular place.

This definition preserves the connections between the branches without making them identical. Qur’anic rising is not always awareness; iṭṭilāʿ is not always physical ascent; and ṭalʿ is not always ripe dates. Each form’s precise meaning begins with the word and its construction and becomes clearer through its verse’s context.
