The root f-ḍ-w — Spaciousness, reaching another, and direct intimacy

Qur’anic and lexical forms

أَفْضَىٰ
(afḍā)
Reaching marital intimacy

فَضَاء / فَاضٍ
(faḍāʾ / fāḍin)
Open space and spaciousness

إِفْضَاء
(ifḍāʾ)
Reaching and close contact

The triliteral Arabic root ف ض و concerns spaciousness, openness, and the absence of obstruction within a space. فَضَاء denotes an open, extensive place with few obstacles or restrictions on movement or presence.

Within this family, أَفْضَى إِلَىٰ means to reach someone or something and gain access to their domain. In this usage, distance or a barrier between two parties gives way, making access or close contact possible.

Ifḍāʾ also has an established marital usage, referring to the intimacy and private relationship of husband and wife, which can include sexual intercourse.

The root ف ض و occurs only once in the Qur’an, as أَفْضَىٰ in al-Nisa 4:21. God questions and reproaches men who, after marital intimacy, seek to reclaim property they have given their wives.

The essential distinction is that ifḍāʾ generally concerns reaching and drawing close; in a marital context, however, it refers to the closest private relationship between spouses. It should therefore neither be reduced to two people merely occupying one place nor treated as a root whose entire meaning is sexual intercourse.

1. The root in early Arabic

فَضَاء: open, extensive space

In early Arabic, فَضَا الْمَكَانُ يَفْضُو describes a place being spacious and open.

الفَضَاء denotes a broad, open place without obstacles to movement or presence.

مَكَانٌ فَاضٍ and مَكَانٌ مُفْضٍ also describe a spacious, open place.

For example, someone emerging from a narrow passage into an open plain no longer experiences the passage’s constriction.

This contrast between restricted and open space is a clear application of the ف ض و family.

Yet spaciousness does not itself mean safety, beauty, or comfort. A desert can be very open yet dangerous to a traveler without water or shelter.

The root’s direct meaning thus concerns openness and lack of obstruction, rather than every quality an open space might possess.

أَفْضَى إِلَيْهِ: reaching and gaining access

In another usage, أَفْضَى فُلَانٌ إِلَىٰ فُلَانٍ means that someone reached another person or entered their domain.

Lisan al-Arab explains this usage as entering another’s space and reaching them.

In Maqayis al-Lughah, Ibn Faris likewise identifies spaciousness and openness as the family’s basic meaning and uses this background to explain two people reaching one another.

This connection is intelligible.

Distance or a barrier between two people limits their close contact.

When the barrier gives way and one reaches the other, access and proximity become possible.

Nevertheless, ifḍāʾ need not always involve removing a wall or physical barrier.

Someone may approach another person without a wall ever having stood between them.

What matters in أفضى إليه is reaching another and attaining close contact.

أَفْضَى إِلَيْهِ بِسِرِّهِ: confiding a secret

In established Arabic usage, أَفْضَى إِلَيْهِ بِسِرِّهِ means that someone disclosed a secret to another, informing them of a private matter.

This usage does not necessarily involve bodily contact between the two people.

Closeness arises in trust and shared knowledge.

Someone might, for example, share a personal matter kept hidden from others with a trusted friend.

The friend thereby gains access to part of that person’s private life and knowledge.

This usage shows that ifḍāʾ extends beyond physical access and can describe close, confidential communication.

It does not follow, however, that أفضى بعضكم إلى بعض in the Qur’an refers only to sharing secrets or emotional trust. Each occurrence must be understood in its own sentence and context.

2. Pre-Islamic poetic evidence: Zuhayr ibn Abi Sulma

In one of his wisdom verses, the renowned pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abi Sulma says:

وَمَنْ يُوفِ لَا يُذْمَمْ وَمَنْ يُفْضِ قَلْبُهُ
إِلَىٰ مُطْمَئِنِّ الْبِرِّ لَا يَتَجَمْجَمِ

Wa-man yūfi lā yudh'mam wa-man yufḍi qalbuhu / ilā muṭmaʾinni l-birri lā yatajamjami.

Translation: “Whoever keeps faith is not reproached; and whoever’s heart reaches the secure ground of goodness does not waver in uncertainty.”

In the reading يُفْضِ قَلْبُهُ إِلَىٰ, the heart reaches a secure place; قَلْبُهُ is the subject of the verb.

The usage is not material. It describes the heart reaching a settled state of goodness, rather than a body moving to a particular place.

In that state, a person does not hesitate or waver in doing what is right.

This evidence helps illuminate the sense of reaching or gaining access.

Variant readings of the verse

The transmitted versions of Zuhayr’s poem contain an important variant here.

Some versions read:

«وَمَنْ يُفْضِ قَلْبُهُ»

Wa-man yufḍi qalbuhu.

Other transmissions instead have:

«ومن يهد قلبه»

Wa-man yahdi qalbahu (variant wording).

This is an alternative reading.

The second wording belongs to the root ه د ي and concerns the heart being guided toward goodness; it is distinct from the يُفْضِ reading.

The reading يفض appears in lexical accounts, while يهد is found in other transmissions of the poem.

The verse can therefore provide historical evidence for يفض, but the variant must not be concealed, nor should all early versions be claimed to share one reading.

In the يفض reading, the verb concerns reaching or gaining access, a sense consistent with Qur’anic أفضى and early lexical accounts.

The verse itself is not about marriage. It cannot establish by itself that marital ifḍāʾ means exclusively intercourse or exclusively private seclusion.

3. Other Arabic usages

Direct contact

Lexical sources record أَفْضَى بِيَدِهِ إِلَى الْأَرْضِ as direct contact between the palm and the ground.

The person brings a hand to the ground, with no barrier remaining between them.

The example clarifies the physical, unmediated aspect of reaching.

Direct contact, however, does not always mean a sexual relationship.

Someone may place a hand on the ground or an object, with the same verb describing access and contact.

An affair reaching a particular stage or outcome

أَفْضَى الْأَمْرُ إِلَىٰ كَذَا is also an established Arabic expression.

It means that an affair reached a particular outcome, condition, or stage.

A decision may, for example, lead through several stages to a certain consequence.

Here ifḍāʾ concerns a process reaching a particular point, rather than material space or bodily contact.

Faḍāʾ in modern language

Today, faḍāʾ in Arabic, and its borrowed Persian form, can describe open space, the surrounding environment, and even outer space.

The technical meaning “outer space” should not be projected into pre-Qur’anic Arabic without evidence.

The historical usage under discussion concerns an open, spacious, unobstructed place.

The modern cosmic sense is a later extension in scientific language.

4. Linguistic forms and Qur’anic usage

Known forms in this family include:

  • فَضَا: became spacious or open.
  • يَفْضُو: becomes spacious or is open.
  • فَضَاء: an open, extensive place.
  • فَاضٍ: spacious, open, or exposed.
  • أَفْضَى: reached someone or something; gained access to their domain.
  • يُفْضِي: reaches or enters into close contact.
  • إِفْضَاء: reaching, gaining access, and, in marriage, private intimacy.
  • مُفْضٍ: the active participle of أفضى; its contextual senses include spacious or leading to something.
  • مُفْضًى إِلَيْهِ: someone or something toward whom access or contact is directed.

Some dictionaries also record forms such as فَضًى for things that are scattered or mixed.

Examples include expressions for mixed dates and raisins or articles placed together without a particular arrangement.

The precise historical relationship of these usages to open space and reaching another is not conclusively established.

Shared root letters alone do not prove that every meaning developed from a single original image.

The Qur’anic form أَفْضَىٰ

Only one form of this root occurs in the Qur’an:

أَفْضَىٰ — al-Nisa 4:21.

It is a Form IV perfect verb, third-person masculine singular.

Its root is ف ض و: the third radical in morphological analysis is waw.

The final alif maqsurah in أفضى should not lead to the mistaken root assignment ف ض ي.

In this verse, the verb occurs with the preposition إِلَىٰ.

This construction identifies the direction of reaching and proximity.

5. Qur’anic usage: marital intimacy and a solemn covenant

Al-Nisa 4:21

وَكَيْفَ تَأْخُذُونَهُ وَقَدْ أَفْضَىٰ بَعْضُكُمْ إِلَىٰ بَعْضٍ وَأَخَذْنَ مِنْكُمْ مِيثَاقًا غَلِيظًا

Wa-kayfa taʾkhudhūnahu wa-qad afḍā baʿḍukum ilā baʿḍin wa-akhadhna minkum mīthāqan ghalīẓā.

Translation: “And how can you take it back, when you have reached marital intimacy with one another, and they have taken a solemn covenant from you?”

This is the root’s only Qur’anic occurrence.

The verse concerns marriage and reclaiming property a man has given his wife.

The preceding verse must also be read to understand the subject.

Al-Nisa 4:20

وَإِنْ أَرَدْتُمُ اسْتِبْدَالَ زَوْجٍ مَكَانَ زَوْجٍ وَآتَيْتُمْ إِحْدَاهُنَّ قِنْطَارًا فَلَا تَأْخُذُوا مِنْهُ شَيْئًا ۚ أَتَأْخُذُونَهُ بُهْتَانًا وَإِثْمًا مُبِينًا

Wa-in aradtumu stibdāla zawjin makāna zawjin wa-ātaytum iḥdāhunna qinṭāran fa-lā taʾkhudhū minhu shayʾā. A-taʾkhudhūnahu buhtānan wa-ithman mubīnā.

Translation: “And if you wish to replace one wife with another, and have given one of them abundant wealth, do not take any of it back. Would you take it back through slander and manifest sin?”

Verse 20 concerns a man who wishes to take another wife and reclaim property given to his former wife.

The Qur’an reproaches this conduct.

Verse 21 then raises two matters to emphasize why it is wrong:

The spouses’ marital intimacy, and the solemn covenant the woman has taken from the man.

Afḍā here is therefore inseparable from the subject of married life and the woman’s financial rights.

بَعْضُكُمْ إِلَىٰ بَعْضٍ: mutual intimacy

The verse does not say only that the man has reached the woman.

Its wording is:

«أَفْضَىٰ بَعْضُكُمْ إِلَىٰ بَعْضٍ»

Afḍā baʿḍukum ilā baʿḍ.

That is, some of you have reached others among you.

In the marital context, this construction refers to the close relationship between husband and wife.

Its reciprocal expression of the relationship matters.

Spouses develop a private relationship unlike ordinary social contact.

They may have shared the closest bodily and personal situations.

Their relationship is more than a passing encounter between strangers.

Nevertheless, reciprocity in this expression should not be turned into a claim that all marital rights and responsibilities are identical in every respect.

The verse speaks of mutual intimacy, then identifies a particular commitment women have taken from men.

6. Does ifḍāʾ refer only to sexual intercourse?

This is a central question in interpreting the root.

Early lexical and exegetical explanations contain two recognized understandings.

In the first, أفضى إلى امرأته refers to intercourse and sexual contact between spouses.

In the second, the expression can also include their private seclusion and intimacy, even without intercourse.

Linguistically, the general meaning of أفضى إليه concerns reaching and close contact.

In marriage, however, the same expression can serve as a euphemism for sexual relations.

Distinguishing a root’s meaning from its particular application

Sexual intercourse does not exhaust the basic meaning of ifḍāʾ: it also occurs in reaching someone, touching the ground with a hand, and confiding a secret.

That fact does not justify disregarding the verse’s marital meaning.

For comparison, the Persian verb meaning “to reach” also takes different senses according to its sentence.

“I reached the city” describes spatial movement.

“I reached a conclusion” describes the outcome of a process.

“I gained access to the most private sphere of another’s life” can describe intimate, personal contact.

The Qur’anic أفضى بعضكم إلى بعض occurs in a marital context, making marital intimacy prominent.

Can the word establish a legal ruling about seclusion and intercourse?

Private seclusion and actual intercourse are linguistically distinct.

Some early commentators restricted ifḍāʾ in this verse to intercourse.

Others also included the spouses’ private seclusion among its applications.

This disagreement alone does not determine the entire legal framework of marital financial rights.

Other verses require separate examination when considering spouses’ rights in different circumstances, including al-Baqarah 2:237 on separation before marital contact.

Al-Baqarah 2:229 likewise discusses reclaiming what a man has given a woman and an exceptional situation.

All rules of divorce, the marriage gift, and marital relations should not be derived from one word.

What is clear in al-Nisa 4:21 is that the Qur’an invokes spouses’ private intimacy to emphasize the man’s responsibility toward his wife’s financial rights.

7. وَأَخَذْنَ مِنْكُمْ مِيثَاقًا غَلِيظًا

After mentioning marital intimacy, the verse says:

«وَأَخَذْنَ مِنْكُمْ مِيثَاقًا غَلِيظًا»

Wa-akhadhna minkum mīthāqan ghalīẓā.

That is, the women have taken a solemn covenant from you.

أَخَذْنَ is a feminine plural verb referring to the women.

مِنْكُمْ refers to the men being addressed.

مِيثَاقًا غَلِيظًا is a firm, serious commitment.

This part does not present marriage merely as bodily intimacy; it places intimacy alongside a solemn covenant.

Intimacy and commitment

In marriage, a person gains access to another’s private life.

This intimacy may involve trust, living together, and shared responsibilities.

Bodily closeness alone, however, does not explain all ethical and financial responsibilities.

The verse accordingly mentions a solemn covenant after afḍā.

The two expressions highlight two related aspects:

أفضى بعضكم إلى بعض — intimacy and a private bond.

وأخذن منكم ميثاقا غليظا — a solemn commitment.

These should not be merged into one meaning.

Ifḍāʾ does not itself mean making a covenant, nor is mithaq simply a name for sexual relations.

Together, these expressions show that unjustly reclaiming a woman’s property conflicts with the intimacy and commitment previously shared by the parties.

Does ending a marriage invalidate earlier commitments?

Within the context of al-Nisa 4:20 and al-Nisa 4:21, the answer is clear.

A man may decide to end a marriage, but that decision does not permit him to reclaim property he has given his wife by inventing pretexts.

The verse emphasizes preserving the woman’s financial right even when married life ends.

This also relates to al-Nisa 4:19 and its instruction to treat women honorably.

Past intimacy must therefore not become a means of violating rights, applying pressure, or humiliating someone after separation.

This reading arises from the verses’ overall context, rather than the lexical meaning of ف ض و alone.

8. Distinguishing ifḍāʾ from related roots

ف ض و (أَفْضَىٰ) and ف س ح (فَسَحَ)

Fasḥ concerns spaciousness and making room.

The faḍāʾ branch of ف ض و also concerns openness and open space.

In its Qur’anic usage, however, afḍā concerns reaching another and private intimacy.

In al-Mujadilah 58:11, فافسحوا concerns making room in gatherings.

In al-Nisa 4:21, أفضى concerns the marital relationship.

Their neighboring senses of spaciousness do not make the two verbs interchangeable in translation.

ف ض و (أَفْضَىٰ) and ف ض ض (انْفَضَّ)

These roots may be confused because they share their first two letters.

Yet ف ض ض is a separate, doubled root.

In Al Imran 3:159, لَانْفَضُّوا مِنْ حَوْلِكَ refers to people dispersing from around the Messenger.

In al-Jumuah 62:11, انْفَضُّوا إِلَيْهَا likewise describes people leaving the gathering and turning toward trade.

In these usages, infaḍḍa concerns separation and dispersal.

Afḍā in al-Nisa 4:21, however, concerns reaching and intimacy.

Coming together and drawing close should not be confused with dispersing and separating merely because the letters resemble one another.

ف ض و (أَفْضَىٰ) and ق ر ب (قَرُبَ)

Qurb means being or drawing near.

Ifḍāʾ can also express reaching and closeness, but أفضى إليه highlights gaining access to another’s domain and close contact with them.

Two people may be spatially very close without any private relationship or direct contact.

By contrast, the spouses in al-Nisa 4:21 have attained an intimacy unlike ordinary interpersonal contact.

Qurb therefore has a broader field of proximity; not every application is ifḍāʾ.

ف ض و (أَفْضَىٰ) and م س س (مَسَّ)

Mass concerns touching and one thing coming into contact with another.

In al-Baqarah 2:237, it occurs in a context of marital contact.

Ifḍāʾ in al-Nisa 4:21 likewise refers to marital intimacy.

The general meanings of these two roots are nevertheless different.

Mass concerns contact in many Arabic usages.

Ifḍāʾ concerns reaching, entering a domain, and unmediated closeness.

In marriage, the expressions may refer to shared situations, but that overlap does not make the roots absolute synonyms.

ف ض و (أَفْضَىٰ) and ب ش ر (بَاشَرَ)

Mubasharah belongs to ب ش ر and, in marital usage, concerns bodily contact and relations.

بَاشِرُوهُنَّ occurs in this context in al-Baqarah 2:187.

Ifḍāʾ can likewise express bodily intimacy and marital relations, but its general semantic field concerns reaching and gaining access.

The words thus approach one another in marital contexts, without every sense of afḍā being restricted to bashara.

ف ض و (أَفْضَىٰ) and خ ل و (خَلَا)

The root خ ل و concerns emptiness or being alone in seclusion.

Two people can be in a secluded place without sexual relations occurring.

Marital ifḍāʾ can relate to private seclusion, but al-Nisa 4:21 is not merely describing two people alone in a room.

The context concerns marriage, spouses’ intimacy, and a solemn commitment.

أفضى بعضكم إلى بعض should therefore not be reduced to the mere absence of a third person from a place.

9. Challenges in understanding and translation

The first error is translating afḍā only as ordinary reaching.

“You reached one another” is lexically defensible, but can be highly ambiguous for readers who overlook the marital context.

Private, marital intimacy should be recognizable in the translation or explanation of this verse.

A second error is equating every usage of the root with intercourse.

Arabic uses afḍā for reaching someone, touching the ground with a hand, and confiding a secret as well.

Sexual intercourse is therefore not the general meaning of all the root’s derivatives.

A third error is separating marital intimacy from responsibility and the woman’s financial rights.

The verse uses afḍā to reinforce its condemnation of unjustly reclaiming her property.

Lexical analysis should therefore remain connected to the subject of al-Nisa 4:20 and al-Nisa 4:21.

A fourth error is treating “solemn covenant” as the meaning of ifḍāʾ itself.

Intimacy and covenant are distinct matters that the Qur’an places alongside one another.

A fifth error is reading unilateral ownership into the marital relationship.

بعضكم إلى بعض expresses mutual contact. The following words also present women as those who have taken a solemn covenant from men.

The verse therefore does not present the woman as an item of property that can be reclaimed.

A sixth error is deriving every legal detail of marriage and separation from afḍā alone.

The linguistic disagreement about seclusion and intercourse deserves recognition, but other verses also matter when assessing financial rights in different circumstances.

Finally, early Arabic faḍāʾ should not be assigned the technical meaning of outer space or a theory of the universe’s physical dimensions without evidence.

The Qur’anic occurrence concerns close human contact, rather than the structure of the universe.

10. Translation proposals for Forqan

For physical فَضَا, appropriate renderings include “became spacious,” “opened out,” and “became an open space.”

فَضَاء means “open space,” “a spacious place,” or “an extensive area.”

In general usage, أَفْضَى إِلَىٰ can be rendered “reached something,” “gained access to it,” or “came into close contact with it.”

In the usage involving a secret, “confided a secret to another” is appropriate.

In al-Nisa 4:21, however, marital intimacy must remain clear.

For the expression:

«وَقَدْ أَفْضَىٰ بَعْضُكُمْ إِلَىٰ بَعْضٍ»

Wa-qad afḍā baʿḍukum ilā baʿḍ.

Several renderings can be considered:

“When you have drawn close to one another.”

This is brief and close to the root’s general meaning, but leaves the kind of closeness unclear.

“When you have had marital relations with one another.”

This is natural and understandable, but retains less of the image of reaching and gaining access to another’s private sphere.

“When you have reached marital intimacy with one another.”

This better balances the lexical meaning and the verse’s context.

The proposed base translation for Forqan is therefore:

“And how can you take it back, when you have reached marital intimacy with one another, and they have taken a solemn covenant from you?”

This preserves three features: the reproachful question about reclaiming property, mutual marital intimacy, and the solemn covenant women have taken from men.

A brief lexical explanation

Word form: أَفْضَىٰ

Root: ف ض و

Brief root meaning: Spaciousness and openness; reaching and gaining access to another’s domain or private sphere.

Meaning in this verse: Husband and wife attaining intimacy and private marital relations; a Form IV perfect verb.

A brief explanation focuses on this occurrence; lexical variation, seclusion and intercourse, and their relationship to financial rights are separate aspects of the fuller study.

11. Conclusion

In early Arabic, ف ض و concerns spaciousness, open space, and lack of obstruction.

Within the same family, أفضى إلى describes reaching someone or something, gaining access to their domain, and establishing close contact.

In the lexical transmission of Zuhayr’s poem, يُفْضِ قَلْبُهُ describes the heart reaching secure ground, although transmitted versions differ in their wording.

Arabic ifḍāʾ can also describe direct contact, confiding a secret, and marital intimacy.

The Qur’an uses this root only once, in al-Nisa 4:21.

After prohibiting the reclamation of property given to a wife, God recalls the spouses’ intimacy and the solemn covenant of marriage.

Afḍā in this context therefore refers to a close, private marital relationship.

The root concerns spaciousness and openness; in أفضى إلى, it concerns reaching and gaining access to another, as distance or a barrier to close contact gives way.

This general sense takes the form of private intimacy between spouses in marriage, but the root itself should not be treated merely as a term for intercourse.

Nor does the verse present marital intimacy apart from responsibility and the parties’ rights.

The Qur’an recalls that, after such a relationship, a man cannot disregard the covenant and his wife’s financial rights by unjustly seeking to reclaim what he gave her.

The word’s meaning should thus be preserved at two levels: reaching and intimacy at the linguistic level, and responsibility arising from relationship and commitment at the contextual level.

This distinction preserves lexical precision without reducing the verse’s message to a merely bodily account of the spouses’ relationship.
