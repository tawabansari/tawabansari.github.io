Bolting, fleeing, driving away, and scattering: from runaway camels in early Arabic to deterrence against covenant-breaking in the Quran

In early Arabic, SH R D concerns bolting, fleeing, leaving one's place or group, and dispersal. شَرَدَ البَعِيرُ describes a camel running away from its owner or herd. شَارِد describes an animal or person that has fled or strayed; شَرِيد can describe someone driven away and separated from their place or community.

شَرَّدَ means driving away or scattering another. It explicitly introduces an agent causing departure or dispersal, whereas شَرَدَ describes flight without necessarily naming an outside cause; it need not be wholly spontaneous.

شَرَّدَ بِفُلَانٍ is also established Arabic. It can describe publicizing someone's conduct or faults, making their story or treatment known. This matters for Al-Anfal 8:57, which says فَشَرِّدْ بِهِمْ مَنْ خَلْفَهُمْ rather than simply شَرِّدْهُمْ.

The root occurs only once in the Quran, as فَشَرِّدْ in Al-Anfal 8:57, within warfare, broken covenants, and deterring repeated betrayal.

Its core is fleeing or departing from a place or group, or causing others to flee and disperse. In the Quranic context, action against covenant-breakers in battle is to scatter and deter others from continuing the same conduct.

1. The root in early Arabic

Animals bolting and fleeing

A clear early usage concerns camels and other livestock.

شَرَدَ البَعِيرُ describes a camel bolting, leaving its owner or herd, and escaping ordinary control.

شَرُود describes an animal inclined to flee or difficult to keep under control.

The animal leaves its usual place or group and ordinary control or companionship; its movement may involve bolting or flight.

These features should not be treated as identical.

Not every moving animal is shārid, nor must every camel leaving its herd be fleeing human pursuit.

شَرَدَ highlights flight or departure; شَرَّدَ can describe an agent driving another away and causing flight.

A pre-Islamic witness: runaway camels in ʿAbd Manāf ibn Ribʿ al-Hudhalī

The pre-Islamic Hudhayl poet ʿAbd Manāf ibn Ribʿ describes a group driven in battle into Qutāʾidah:

حَتَّى إِذَا أَسْلَكُوهُمْ فِي قُتَائِدَةٍ
شَلًّا كَمَا تَطْرُدُ الْجَمَّالَةُ الشُّرُدَا

ḥattā idhā aslakūhum fī Qutāʾidatin / shallan kamā taṭrudu al-jammālatu al-shurudā

The line's sense is:

“Until they drove them into Qutāʾidah, driving them hard as camel-herders drive runaway camels.”

Qutāʾidah is explained as a known mountain pass or ascent, and also as a mountain. Its particular narrowness is not established by the name alone. Shall means driving or urging onward; al-jammālah means camel-herders.

الشُّرُد describes camels that have bolted and left their ordinary relation to herd or herders.

The poet compares driving people to driving runaway camels.

The witness connects animal flight with people being driven and dispersed in warfare.

Older versions also record الشَّرَد at the line's end. Both readings concern runaway camels, without materially changing the sense discussed here.

Its attribution to a pre-Islamic poet supports the early poetic connection between this root, flight, and driving away. The exact date of the line, however, is not independently established here.

The root itself does not thereby mean war, military defeat, or punishment: those belong to the scene described.

The word still concerns flight and departure from a place or group.

Shārid and sharīd: fleeing and being driven away

Shārid commonly describes someone or something that has bolted or fled.

Sharīd can describe someone driven away and separated from their place or community.

طَرِيدٌ شَرِيدٌ combines neighboring descriptions of a person expelled and displaced.

Arabic can describe this condition both through the person's movement and through another agent's conduct.

Someone may flee home out of fear of an enemy. Flight describes their movement; forcible expulsion adds an external cause.

It does not follow that shārid always flees voluntarily or sharīd is always forcibly expelled. These two forms overlap; construction and context determine the precise sense.

Some lexical uses also make sharīd a small remainder, such as water left in a container. The image concerns a part remaining isolated from a larger amount.

That sense cannot be generalized: the Quranic use does not mean a small remainder.

Sharrada bihi: making someone's conduct known

Older lexicons record شَرَّدَ بِهِ for publicizing someone's faults or conduct.

A line of uncertain authorship says:

أُطَوِّفُ بِالْأَبَاطِحِ كُلَّ يَوْمٍ
مَخَافَةَ أَنْ يُشَرِّدَ بِي حَكِيمُ

uṭawwifu bi-l-abāṭiḥi kulla yawmin / makhāfata an yusharrida bī Ḥakīm

“I wander the gravelly valleys every day, fearing that Ḥakīm will publicize my conduct.”

Accounts explaining the line identify Ḥakīm as a man charged with restraining certain people from misconduct.

يُشَرِّدَ بِي here need not mean that Ḥakīm will expel the speaker from town; it can concern exposing their behavior to others.

A variant with يُؤَدِّبَني is also reported. Neither authorship nor exact date has been independently established here, so it should not be treated as a securely dated pre-Quranic witness.

Nevertheless, its preservation in older lexicons shows that شَرَّدَ بِفُلَانٍ can function differently from شَرَّدَ فُلَانًا.

In one recorded use, شَرَّدَ بِفُلَانٍ publicizes a person's conduct or treatment, whereas شَرَّدَ فُلَانًا can take that person as the direct object of expulsion. This is not an absolute boundary: the prepositional construction also has uses involving driving away and dispersal. The complete sentence determines the sense.

This grammatical distinction helps examine Al-Anfal 8:57.

2. Important forms and derivatives

The following forms belong to this Arabic family.

شَرَدَ / يَشْرُدُ: bolted, fled, or left a place or group; used of humans and animals.

شَرْد، شِرَاد، شُرُود: verbal nouns for bolting, flight, and departure.

شَارِد: runaway, fleeing, or straying from one's place.

شَرُود: an animal or person prone to frequent flight.

شَرِيد: expelled, fleeing, or displaced; in some uses, an isolated remainder.

شَرَّدَ: drove away, scattered, or caused another to flee.

تَشْرِيد: driving away, dispersing, or removing from a place or group.

مُشَرِّد: an agent who drives away or scatters others.

مُشَرَّد: someone or something driven away or dispersed.

أَشْرَدَ: in recorded usage, drove away or caused another to flee.

تَشَرَّدَ: dispersed, fled, or became separated from a place or community.

Literary Arabic also uses قَافِيَةٌ شَرُودٌ for a rhyme or poem circulating widely among people and lands.

Movement away from an initial location supplies the image for poetic circulation. Circulation is a literary extension, rather than the family's universal meaning.

Persian equivalents such as “displaced” and “displacing” render some modern Arabic uses of this family. Their connection to expulsion should not restrict every historical use to homelessness or forced migration.

Only فَشَرِّدْ occurs in the Quran: an imperative of Form II.

3. The Quranic occurrence: Al-Anfal 8:57

The Quran says:

فَإِمَّا تَثْقَفَنَّهُمْ فِي الْحَرْبِ فَشَرِّدْ بِهِمْ مَنْ خَلْفَهُمْ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَذَّكَّرُونَ

fa-immā tathqafannahum fī al-ḥarbi fa-sharrid bihim man khalfahum laʿallahum yadhdhakkarūn

Al-Anfāl 8:57

“If you gain mastery over them in war, scatter through them those behind them, so that they may take heed.”

This is the root's only Quranic occurrence.

Understanding it requires identifying the pronoun's referent and the circumstances of the command.

The group concerned: covenant-breakers in warfare

Al-Anfal 8:56 says:

الَّذِينَ عَاهَدْتَ مِنْهُمْ ثُمَّ يَنْقُضُونَ عَهْدَهُمْ فِي كُلِّ مَرَّةٍ وَهُمْ لَا يَتَّقُونَ

alladhīna ʿāhadta minhum thumma yanquḍūna ʿahdahum fī kulli marratin wa-hum lā yattaqūn

Al-Anfāl 8:56

“Those among them with whom you made a covenant, who then break their covenant every time and do not exercise restraint.”

The group is identified by repeated breach of a covenant they have entered.

Al-Anfal 8:57 concerns encountering that same group in war.

The command is therefore not addressed against every opponent, non-Muslim, or person disagreeing with the Prophet's beliefs.

It concerns people with a record of covenant-breaking encountered under wartime conditions.

فِي الْحَرْبِ is an explicit qualification: the verse concerns wartime action, not arbitrary action against people outside warfare.

This qualification and the preceding verse determine the command's scope. Removing them detaches the verse from its subject.

Tathqafannahum: gaining mastery in war

The verse begins:

فَإِمَّا تَثْقَفَنَّهُمْ فِي الْحَرْبِ

fa-immā tathqafannahum fī al-ḥarb

Al-Anfāl 8:57

“If you gain mastery over them in war…”

تَثْقَفَنَّهُمْ belongs to TH Q F and here means finding, gaining access to, or prevailing over the opposing group.

إِمَّا is the conditional in combined with an emphatic mā. The construction makes the following command dependent on this situation's occurrence.

It does not give unrestricted permission to pursue and scatter them at any time or place; it specifies gaining mastery over them in war.

The verb alone does not establish that every member has been killed, captured, or put to flight. Such details require context and independent evidence.

Fa-sharrid bihim: scattering others through them

The central phrase is:

فَشَرِّدْ بِهِمْ مَنْ خَلْفَهُمْ

fa-sharrid bihim man khalfahum

Al-Anfāl 8:57

“Then scatter through them those behind them.”

Three components need to be distinguished.

شَرِّدْ is an imperative expressing dispersal or causing others to flee.

بِهِمْ refers to the group encountered in war. Bāʾ marks an instrumental or causal relationship.

مَنْ خَلْفَهُمْ refers to those behind that group.

Grammatically, مَنْ خَلْفَهُمْ is the verb's object, while بهم marks the means or cause of the effect.

The command therefore does more than say “scatter them”: treatment of one group is to affect and scatter those behind it.

The first group becomes a means of deterrence for the second.

This relates to the recorded use of شَرَّدَ بِفُلَانٍ, where someone's treatment or story becomes known and influences others.

Older lexical explanations accordingly connect the phrase with warning, restraint, and making an example.

These explanations should not displace the root's core. Driving away and dispersal are central here; taking heed is the contextual outcome rather than the universal meaning of every derivative. The dispersal is to deter repeated warfare and covenant-breaking.

Man khalfahum: those behind them

Literally, مَنْ خَلْفَهُمْ denotes those behind the first group.

In warfare, this may concern forces or groups supporting them, following them, or potentially continuing the same course.

Some readings extend it to people repeating their conduct afterward.

Khalfahum alone does not identify these as necessarily their children, future generations, or everyone living later.

Nor does the verse independently identify every such person.

The construction establishes that action against the first group should deter another group behind it.

For example, firm and publicly known treatment of a group defeated in battle after repeated attacks and treaty breaches may deter other forces from repetition.

This illustrates the relation between the groups. It does not authorize every treatment of defeated people or make all their associates responsible for combatants' conduct.

Laʿallahum yadhdhakkarūn: the aim of deterrence

The verse ends:

لَعَلَّهُمْ يَذَّكَّرُونَ

laʿallahum yadhdhakkarūn

Al-Anfāl 8:57

“So that they may take heed.”

This clarifies the intended result.

Those witnessing or hearing of the outcome should recognize the consequences and refrain from similar conduct.

يَذَّكَّرُونَ belongs to DH K R and here concerns remembering, recognizing, and taking heed.

The verse does not present purposeless dispersal or deterrence.

The command responds to covenant-breaking and warfare, with taking heed and avoiding repetition as its intended result.

It does not guarantee that everyone behind them will heed the warning. Laʿalla presents an intended or hoped-for outcome, not certainty about every person's reaction.

4. Warfare, covenants, and peace in related verses

Deterrence and the limits of punishment

Some older commentaries explain fa-sharrid bihim as making the first group an example to others.

This fits the wartime context, where action against covenant-breakers is to deter others from repeating their conduct.

It should not become a command for unrestricted killing, torture, displacement of families, or punishment of people uninvolved in fighting and covenant-breaking.

Sharrid itself specifies no particular punishment, weapon, number of deaths, or treatment of captives.

Al-Anfal 8:57 calls for effective wartime action, whose details and limits must be examined alongside other relevant passages.

For example, Al-Baqarah 2:190 says:

وَقَاتِلُوا فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ الَّذِينَ يُقَاتِلُونَكُمْ وَلَا تَعْتَدُوا

wa-qātilū fī sabīli allāhi alladhīna yuqātilūnakum wa-lā taʿtadū

Al-Baqarah 2:190

“Fight in the way of The God those who fight you, and do not transgress.”

This principle prevents deterrence from becoming a substitute name for aggression and injustice.

Equally, Al-Anfal 8:57 should not be reduced to a verbal warning. It concerns prevailing over a covenant-breaking enemy in war and taking action that actually deters others.

Both its wartime force and its limits must be retained.

Breach of covenant and openly ending an agreement

Al-Anfal 8:58 immediately follows:

وَإِمَّا تَخَافَنَّ مِنْ قَوْمٍ خِيَانَةً فَانْبِذْ إِلَيْهِمْ عَلَىٰ سَوَاءٍ إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يُحِبُّ الْخَائِنِينَ

wa-immā takhāfanna min qawmin khiyānatan fa-nbidh ilayhim ʿalā sawāʾin inna allāha lā yuḥibbu al-khāʾinīn

Al-Anfāl 8:58

“If you fear treachery from a people, cast the covenant back to them on equal terms; The God does not love the treacherous.”

Fear of treachery does not license secret betrayal or ending an agreement while concealing that fact from the other party.

فَانْبِذْ إِلَيْهِمْ عَلَىٰ سَوَاءٍ concerns openly renouncing the covenant so that the other side knows its status too.

Al-Anfal 8:57 should therefore not be detached from the next verse and read as permission for surprise attacks against any group suspected of possible betrayal.

Al-Anfal 8:57 concerns repeated covenant-breaking and warfare. Al-Anfal 8:58 concerns feared treachery and openly addressing the agreement.

This distinction matters for the command's scope.

Deterrence and defensive preparedness

Al-Anfal 8:60 says:

وَأَعِدُّوا لَهُمْ مَا اسْتَطَعْتُمْ مِنْ قُوَّةٍ وَمِنْ رِبَاطِ الْخَيْلِ تُرْهِبُونَ بِهِ عَدُوَّ اللَّهِ وَعَدُوَّكُمْ

wa-aʿiddū lahum mā istaṭaʿtum min quwwatin wa-min ribāṭi al-khayli turhibūna bihi ʿaduwwa allāhi wa-ʿaduwwakum

Al-Anfāl 8:60

“Prepare against them whatever strength and ready horses you can, thereby striking fear into the enemy of The God and your enemy.”

Preparedness and strength can deter attack and continued hostility.

This relates to Al-Anfal 8:57, though their constructions differ.

Al-Anfal 8:57 presents deterrence through action against covenant-breakers in war.

Al-Anfal 8:60 presents preparedness and defensive strength as deterrents to hostile action.

Deterrence is therefore not limited to action during warfare; defensive capability can also help prevent attack.

تُرْهِبُونَ belongs to R H B and concerns causing fear; sharrid concerns driving away and scattering.

Their outcomes may overlap without making them lexically identical.

Ending hostility and inclining toward peace

Al-Anfal 8:61 says:

وَإِنْ جَنَحُوا لِلسَّلْمِ فَاجْنَحْ لَهَا وَتَوَكَّلْ عَلَى اللَّهِ

wa-in janaḥū li-l-salmi fa-jnaḥ lahā wa-tawakkal ʿalā allāh

Al-Anfāl 8:61

“If they incline toward peace, incline toward it too, and rely on The God.”

Action against an enemy and restraint of aggression should not turn into endless hostility.

The same context that addresses preparedness and covenant-breakers also addresses an inclination toward peace.

Al-Anfal 8:57 should thus be understood alongside ending wartime danger and preventing repeated treachery, rather than as a command to perpetuate hostility after its cause has ended.

5. Distinctions from neighboring roots

SH R D compared with H R B

Harab means fleeing or escaping.

It overlaps with sharad, whose early uses emphasize bolting, leaving a place or herd, and dispersal.

Harab naturally describes a person escaping to save their life.

When a camel bolts from its herd, sharad also evokes separation from its ordinary place and companionship.

Al-Anfal 8:57 concerns more than one person's escape: action against one group causes others to disperse. This fits the image of sharrid, without exhaustively explaining the choice of wording.

Harab is not always individual, nor sharad always collective. Both can concern individuals or groups.

SH R D compared with Ṭ R D

Ṭard concerns driving away or expelling.

Tashrīd belongs to this field and is explained by ṭard in older lexicons.

SH R D also evokes bolting and estrangement, while ṭard can simply describe another's act of expulsion.

Removing someone from a place is ṭard.

If the expelled person becomes separated from their community and wanders, sharīd may also describe them.

Ṭard emphasizes expulsion; tashrīd can emphasize the resulting separation or dispersal.

This is not an absolute boundary: the roots are synonymous in certain uses.

SH R D compared with F R Q

Tafrīq means separating or distinguishing parts of a whole.

Tashrīd may scatter a group, with driving away, flight, or departure more prominent.

Dividing people into teams for different tasks is separation or division, but not necessarily tashrīd.

When an agent causes a group to flee and scatter through fear or pursuit, tashrīd is appropriate for that causing action; shurūd or tasharrud describes the group's own flight and dispersal.

In Al-Anfal 8:57, dispersal is connected with wartime action and deterrence, going beyond simple division into groups.

SH R D compared with N F R

N F R concerns bolting, moving away, and setting out.

نَفَرَ البَعِيرُ can describe a camel bolting and overlaps with sharada.

Quranic N F R also includes departure or collective movement for a particular purpose.

Al-Tawbah 9:122, for example, says:

فَلَوْلَا نَفَرَ مِنْ كُلِّ فِرْقَةٍ مِنْهُمْ طَائِفَةٌ

fa-lawlā nafara min kulli firqatin minhum ṭāʾifah

Al-Tawbah 9:122

“Why does a group from every division of them not go forth…”

This concerns purposeful departure, rather than bolting in fear.

Not every nafr is shurūd. N F R covers a broader range of movement, while SH R D has a more specific connection to flight and separation.

SH R D compared with N K L

Nakāl concerns restraint, an exemplary outcome, and punishment that deters others from repetition.

Al-Baqarah 2:66 says:

فَجَعَلْنَاهَا نَكَالًا لِمَا بَيْنَ يَدَيْهَا وَمَا خَلْفَهَا

fa-jaʿalnāhā nakālan li-mā bayna yadayhā wa-mā khalfahā

Al-Baqarah 2:66

“We made it a deterrent example for what was before it and what was behind it.”

This outcome relates to Al-Anfal 8:57: one group's conduct or fate can affect others.

Nakāl and tashrīd nevertheless differ lexically.

Nakāl emphasizes deterrence and exemplary effect; tashrīd emphasizes driving away and scattering.

In Al-Anfal 8:57, deterrence emerges from sharrid together with بهم من خلفهم and لعلهم يذكرون.

Making an example is thus a contextual explanation of the command's effect, not an independent definition of every SH R D derivative.

SH R D compared with SH T T and SH DH R

SH T T concerns dispersal and diversity, including the Quranic forms شَتَّى and أَشْتَاتًا.

In Al-Nur 24:61, ashtātan describes eating separately; in Ta Ha 20:53, shattā concerns diverse plants.

Dispersal and diversity therefore need not involve escape, pursuit, or compulsion.

SH R D, particularly of runaway animals or expelled people, emphasizes movement away from a place.

SH DH R also concerns dispersal in expressions such as شذر مذر, but is a separate root.

Semantic resemblance does not merge these families or make every dispersal tashrīd.

6. Morphology and translation proposals for Forqan

The Quranic form is:

فَشَرِّدْ

fa-sharrid

Al-Anfāl 8:57

It combines فَـ, linking the conditional result to the preceding clause, with شَرِّدْ, a second-person masculine singular imperative of Form II.

The basic verb is شَرَدَ; شَرَّدَ is its Form II derivative.

Form I can express a person's or animal's flight without naming an outside agent.

Form II can be transitive, expressing causing another to flee or disperse.

Form II alone establishes neither unlimited severity, necessary repetition, nor a particular punishment. Those details require construction and context.

In Al-Anfal 8:57, مَنْ خَلْفَهُمْ is the object and بِهِمْ marks means or cause.

“Scatter them” therefore omits part of the construction: the effect on those behind them also matters.

Conversely, “punish them in the harshest possible way” substitutes an interpretation for the lexical meaning and imposes an unsupported degree of severity.

Suggested equivalents

شَرَدَ: “bolted,” “fled,” or “departed from a place or group.”

شُرُود and شِرَاد: “bolting,” “flight,” or “departure.”

شَارِد: “runaway,” “fleeing,” or “estranged.”

شَرُود: “prone to flight” or “frequently bolting.”

شَرِيد: “expelled,” “displaced,” or “isolated.”

شَرَّدَ: “drove away,” “scattered,” or “caused to flee.”

تَشْرِيد: “driving away,” “scattering,” or, where appropriate, “displacing.”

مُشَرَّد: “driven away,” “scattered,” or “displaced.”

تَشَرَّدَ: “dispersed,” “became separated,” or “became displaced.”

For شَرَّدَ بِفُلَانٍ, “publicized their conduct” or “made them an example to others” can explain the expression's contextual effect. Neither is a universal equivalent for the verb.

Rendering the Quranic occurrence

Al-Anfal 8:57

فَإِمَّا تَثْقَفَنَّهُمْ فِي الْحَرْبِ فَشَرِّدْ بِهِمْ مَنْ خَلْفَهُمْ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَذَّكَّرُونَ

fa-immā tathqafannahum fī al-ḥarbi fa-sharrid bihim man khalfahum laʿallahum yadhdhakkarūn

Al-Anfāl 8:57

“If you gain mastery over them in war, scatter through them those behind them, so that they may take heed.”

This preserves the verse's basic construction. The proposal does not alter the installed verse translation.

An explanation should identify the first group as covenant-breakers encountered in war, whose treatment is to scatter and deter those behind them from repetition.

An expanded paraphrase of the contextual result could read:

“If you gain mastery over them in war, let your treatment of them cause those behind them to disperse and take heed against repeating that conduct.”

This is more explanatory, adding “your treatment of them,” and belongs in commentary rather than replacing the direct translation.

7. Conclusion

SH R D concerns bolting, flight, separation from a place or group, and causing others to disperse.

The witness attributed to ʿAbd Manāf ibn Ribʿ shows runaway camels supplying an image for people driven in battle; its early attribution does not establish an exact date for this particular line.

Arabic distinguishes sharada, fleeing, from sharrada, driving away or scattering. Sharrada bi-fulān can also publicize someone's conduct or treatment with an effect on others.

The Quran has only fa-sharrid, in Al-Anfal 8:57.

Its context concerns repeated covenant-breakers encountered in war. Action against them is to deter those behind them from continuing or repeating similar conduct.

The command is neither purposeless dispersal nor a specification of punishment's type and extent. Its deterrent aim must be read alongside passages on covenants, nontransgression, and accepting peace.

The defensible core is flight and departure from a place or group; transitively, driving others away and causing dispersal.

In فَشَرِّدْ بِهِمْ مَنْ خَلْفَهُمْ, action against one covenant-breaking group in battle becomes a means of scattering and deterring groups behind it.

For Forqan, “scatter” is the basic equivalent, provided the relation between bihim and man khalfahum is preserved. Deterrence and taking heed emerge from the whole construction and context, rather than being imposed on the root itself.
