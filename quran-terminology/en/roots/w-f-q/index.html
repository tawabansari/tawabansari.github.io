Compatibility, correspondence, harmony, and enabling action

The Arabic root w-f-q denotes compatibility and correspondence between two or more things: they suit one another, share a direction, or come into harmony. Wafq describes something fitting another thing; wifāq denotes agreement and correspondence; tawfīq concerns bringing about the compatibility and coordination needed for an undertaking.

This compatibility need not involve conscious agreement between two people. Events may coincide in time, objects may correspond in size or characteristics, people may agree about an issue, or human effort may coincide with the necessary conditions and means to achieve a desired result.

The Qur’an uses this root four times: bringing two parties into harmony, a claim to have sought reconciliation, divine enabling in the pursuit of reform, and recompense corresponding to deeds.

1. Early and classical Arabic evidence

In the Arabic linguistic tradition, wafq describes what suits or corresponds to something else. It applies to human relationships, measurement, timing, and the movement of objects.

For example, wafqa al-ʿiyāl describes food or provision matching a household’s needs: sufficient to meet them, without necessarily exceeding what is needed.

Wāfaqtu fulānan can mean meeting or encountering someone at a place. Wāfaqtuhu ʿalā amrin, however, denotes two people agreeing about an issue.

In the first usage, the encounter may be accidental; in the second, coordination may arise through discussion and joint decision. Deliberate agreement and accidental coincidence are distinct applications within this root’s semantic field.

A poetic witness attributed to Ruʾbah ibn al-ʿAjjāj, a poet of the Umayyad period, reads:

يَهْوِينَ شَتَّى وَيَقَعْنَ وَفْقًا

yahwīna shattā wa-yaqaʿna wafqā

Translation: “They descend separately, yet land in accord.”

This witness is transmitted as a description of a horse’s legs, but early literary criticism questioned the accuracy of its depiction of movement. Its value here is evidence for wafqan meaning “together, in accord,” rather than a precise account of a horse’s gait.

This shows that wafq does not require all components to be identical. Different things can correspond in a particular respect.

A verse by al-Rāʿī al-Numayrī, a poet of the first Islamic century, reads:

أَمَّا الْفَقِيرُ الَّذِي كَانَتْ حَلُوبَتُهُ
وَفْقَ الْعِيَالِ فَلَمْ يُتْرَكْ لَهُ سَبَدُ

ammā al-faqīru alladhī kānat ḥalūbatuhu / wafqa al-ʿiyāli fa-lam yutrak lahu sabadu

Translation: “As for the poor man whose milch animal supplied just enough for his household, nothing was left to him.”

Wafqa al-ʿiyāl expresses the correspondence between the milk available and the family’s needs. It means an amount fitting a particular need, rather than unlimited abundance.

This also helps explain jazāʾan wifāqan in the Qur’an: correspondence does not necessarily mean abundance or outward identity between two things.

Both witnesses belong to poets of the Islamic period and should not be presented as securely pre-Islamic poetry. The witnesses presented here do not independently establish a pre-Qur’anic date for a particular poetic usage. This limitation should be respected when discussing the pre-Islamic period. Nevertheless, early lexicographical records together with the Qur’an’s own usages clearly establish compatibility, proportion, and agreement within the root’s range.

Lexical discussions also mention fitting an arrow’s nock to a bowstring as an illustration of two things matching. Some lexicographers, however, treat awfaqa al-sahma as a metathesized form of afwaqa, belonging to f-w-q. This example should therefore not be declared the certain historical origin of w-f-q without examining its derivation.

The defensible core is correspondence and compatibility between things that align or match in a particular respect.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important forms recorded in Arabic include:

  • وَفْق: fitting, compatible, or corresponding to something else.
  • وَفِقَ: found something suitable or fitting; some usages also concern understanding or comprehending.
  • وَافَقَ: agreed with, matched, or coincided with someone or something.
  • مُوَافَقَة: agreement, correspondence, or coincidence.
  • وِفَاق: compatibility, harmony, and correspondence.
  • تَوَافَقَ: two or more parties came into agreement.
  • اتَّفَقَ: reached agreement, or matched and coincided.
  • وَفَّقَ: brought about compatibility or provided conditions suitable for an undertaking.
  • تَوْفِيق: bringing about harmony or enabling an undertaking; its moral direction is determined by context.
  • مُوَفَّق: someone granted success or helped to carry out a task properly.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an.

The forms actually used in the Qur’an are:

يُوَفِّقِ in al-Nisāʾ 4:35, تَوْفِيقًا in al-Nisāʾ 4:62, تَوْفِيقِي in Hūd 11:88, and وِفَاقًا in al-Nabaʾ 78:26.

Yuwaffiqi and the forms tawfīqan and tawfīqī belong to Form II. Wifāqan belongs to the family of muwāfaqah and is a Form III verbal noun.

The distinction matters. Wifāq expresses compatibility or correspondence itself, while tawfīq can concern bringing about that compatibility or enabling its realization.

3. Qur’anic usages

1. Yuwaffiqi: bringing two parties into harmony

Al-Nisāʾ 4:35

وَإِنْ خِفْتُمْ شِقَاقَ بَيْنِهِمَا فَابْعَثُوا حَكَمًا مِنْ أَهْلِهِ وَحَكَمًا مِنْ أَهْلِهَا إِنْ يُرِيدَا إِصْلَاحًا يُوَفِّقِ اللَّهُ بَيْنَهُمَا إِنَّ اللَّهَ كَانَ عَلِيمًا خَبِيرًا

wa-in khiftum shiqāqa baynihimā fa-bʿathū ḥakaman min ahlihi wa-ḥakaman min ahlihā in yurīdā iṣlāḥan yuwaffiqi Allāhu baynahumā inna Allāha kāna ʿalīman khabīrā

Al-Nisāʾ 4:35

Translation: “If you fear a breach between the two, appoint an arbiter from his family and an arbiter from hers. If they desire reconciliation, The God will bring about harmony between them. Indeed, The God is Knowing, Aware.”

The verse concerns disagreement between husband and wife and efforts to repair their relationship.

Shiqāq, from sh-q-q, concerns division and separation. Here the disagreement has reached a point where a breach in the relationship is feared.

To address the disagreement, the Qur’an calls for two arbiters: one from the man’s family and one from the woman’s.

It then states:

«إِنْ يُرِيدَا إِصْلَاحًا يُوَفِّقِ اللَّهُ بَيْنَهُمَا»

in yurīdā iṣlāḥan yuwaffiqi Allāhu baynahumā

Al-Nisāʾ 4:35

In this phrase, yuwaffiqi denotes bringing two parties into harmony. The verb is Form II, and God is its subject.

Baynahumā, “between them,” also matters. Tawfīq here is relational harmony, rather than an individual achievement isolated from others.

There are different interpretations of the dual pronouns. Yurīdā may refer to the two arbiters, just mentioned, or to the spouses. Baynahumā has likewise been understood as harmony between the spouses or agreement between the arbiters in their effort to reconcile them. These interpretations differ over the participants, while retaining the central meaning of bringing about accord in the process of repair.

In either case, iṣlāḥan is central to the condition. The verse speaks of intending repair, rather than merely attending a meeting or conducting formal arbitration.

For example, two families may meet to resolve a marital disagreement. If each side only seeks to condemn the other, the meeting may be called reconciliation without a genuine intention to repair. When those responsible sincerely work toward a solution, the possibility of agreement improves.

This illustrates the verse’s social meaning; the verb yuwaffiqi alone does not determine the quality or final outcome of every dispute.

Tawfīq here does not mean that God forcibly removes disagreement without human intention, effort, or responsibility. The verse itself mentions the intention to repair and the practical step of appointing arbiters.

Yuwaffiqi Allāhu baynahumā thus means that God brings about harmony between them, in the context of efforts to repair a damaged relationship.

2. Tawfīqan: a claim of reconciliation and good intention

Al-Nisāʾ 4:62

فَكَيْفَ إِذَا أَصَابَتْهُمْ مُصِيبَةٌ بِمَا قَدَّمَتْ أَيْدِيهِمْ ثُمَّ جَاءُوكَ يَحْلِفُونَ بِاللَّهِ إِنْ أَرَدْنَا إِلَّا إِحْسَانًا وَتَوْفِيقًا

fa-kayfa idhā aṣābat-hum muṣībatun bi-mā qaddamat aydīhim thumma jāʾūka yaḥlifūna bi-Allāhi in aradnā illā iḥsānan wa-tawfīqā

Al-Nisāʾ 4:62

Translation: “How will it be when a calamity strikes them because of what their hands have sent ahead, and then they come to you, swearing by The God: We intended nothing but good and reconciliation?”

This verse illustrates another use of tawfīq.

In al-Nisāʾ 4:35, God is the subject of yuwaffiqi, in the context of repairing marital discord. In al-Nisāʾ 4:62, tawfīq appears in the speech of people whose conduct the Qur’an criticizes.

The preceding passage concerns people who claim faith but turn away when called to judgment and to what God has revealed.

When the consequences of their conduct overtake them, they come to the Messenger and swear that they intended only iḥsānan wa-tawfīqan.

Iḥsān and tawfīq are different words. Iḥsān concerns doing good, while tawfīq here concerns bringing about accord or reconciliation.

The speakers may mean that they sought agreement between different views or parties and wanted a peaceful resolution.

The Qur’an, however, does not accept a mere claim of good intention and reconciliation as proof that their conduct was right.

This usage matters lexically: tawfīq does not itself establish that the speaker is right or that God approves of the action.

Someone may claim to work for harmony and compromise while acting inconsistently with truth and justice.

The lexical meaning of tawfīq must therefore not be restricted everywhere to “divine guidance” or “God-approved success.” Here it names an objective people claim to justify their conduct; the truth of that claim is a separate issue.

3. Tawfīqī: divine enabling in the pursuit of reform

Hūd 11:88

قَالَ يَا قَوْمِ أَرَأَيْتُمْ إِنْ كُنْتُ عَلَىٰ بَيِّنَةٍ مِنْ رَبِّي وَرَزَقَنِي مِنْهُ رِزْقًا حَسَنًا ۚ وَمَا أُرِيدُ أَنْ أُخَالِفَكُمْ إِلَىٰ مَا أَنْهَاكُمْ عَنْهُ ۚ إِنْ أُرِيدُ إِلَّا الْإِصْلَاحَ مَا اسْتَطَعْتُ ۚ وَمَا تَوْفِيقِي إِلَّا بِاللَّهِ ۚ عَلَيْهِ تَوَكَّلْتُ وَإِلَيْهِ أُنِيبُ

qāla yā qawmi araʾaytum in kuntu ʿalā bayyinatin min rabbī wa-razaqanī minhu rizqan ḥasanan wa-mā urīdu an ukhālifakum ilā mā anhākum ʿanhu in urīdu illā al-iṣlāḥa mā istaṭaʿtu wa-mā tawfīqī illā bi-Allāhi ʿalayhi tawakkaltu wa-ilayhi unīb

Hūd 11:88

Translation: “He said: My people, consider: if I stand upon clear evidence from my Lord, and He has provided me with good provision from Himself? I do not wish to do myself what I forbid you. I desire only reform, as far as I am able; and my enabling is only through The God. In Him I have placed my trust, and to Him I turn.”

Shuʿayb speaks these words in response to people who oppose his call.

In the preceding verses, he has spoken about honest dealings, respecting people’s rights, and avoiding corruption. His opponents ask why they should accept restrictions on how they deal with their possessions.

Shuʿayb first emphasizes his clear evidence. He then says that he does not wish to do what he forbids others.

This relates conceptually to w-f-q: correspondence between speech and conduct.

If someone forbids shortchanging but does it himself, his words and actions conflict. Shuʿayb disavows such conduct.

He then states his purpose:

«إِنْ أُرِيدُ إِلَّا الْإِصْلَاحَ مَا اسْتَطَعْتُ»

in urīdu illā al-iṣlāḥa mā istaṭaʿt

Hūd 11:88

He pursues reform to the extent of his ability.

He follows this with:

«وَمَا تَوْفِيقِي إِلَّا بِاللَّهِ»

wa-mā tawfīqī illā bi-Allāh

Hūd 11:88

Here tawfīq concerns the help and enabling means by which Shuʿayb’s efforts can proceed rightly toward their outcome.

Two points appear together: human effort within one’s ability, and dependence on God for enabling that effort.

Shuʿayb does not say that divine enabling relieves him of responsibility to act. Before speaking of it, he emphasizes his intention to reform and the extent of his ability.

Conversely, human effort does not imply complete independence from conditions and means beyond one’s control.

Someone may seek to address a social problem with knowledge, effort, and sound intention. The outcome also depends on other factors, including cooperation, existing circumstances, and consequences outside that individual’s control.

In Shuʿayb’s words, tawfīq connects responsible human effort with God’s help, rather than setting responsibility aside.

Nor does it mean merely outward success or achieving a personal wish. Shuʿayb’s stated aim is reform. The success he seeks must therefore accord with truth and reform, rather than merely defeating opponents or obtaining personal advantage.

The distinction matters in contemporary language too. Someone may be highly successful in gaining wealth, power, or fame without thereby being shown to follow truth and reform.

Shuʿayb connects tawfīq with reform, effort within his ability, trust in God, and turning to Him. This combination clarifies the particular Qur’anic usage.

4. Wifāqan: recompense corresponding to deeds

Al-Nabaʾ 78:26

جَزَاءً وِفَاقًا

jazāʾan wifāqā

Al-Nabaʾ 78:26

Translation: “A fitting recompense.”

Wifāqan belongs to the family of muwāfaqah and characterizes the recompense in terms of correspondence and proportion.

This differs from the preceding three occurrences. Those concern harmony between people or enabling an undertaking. Here the relationship is between recompense and that for which it is given.

The preceding passage concerns the outcome of transgressors. After describing their condition, the Qur’an speaks of jazāʾan wifāqan. Al-Nabaʾ 78:27 and al-Nabaʾ 78:28 connect that outcome with disregard for reckoning and denial of the signs.

The correspondence in question is therefore between conduct and recompense.

For example, if two people have different responsibilities and their wrongs differ in nature, consequences, and circumstances, proportionate judgment need not mean identical punishment.

Jazāʾan wifāqan emphasizes fitting correspondence, but these two words alone do not yield a numerical system or precise formula for calculating punishment.

Wifāqan does not itself mean “extremely severe punishment.” Severity and manner follow from the surrounding verses, whereas the word concerns the recompense’s correspondence to conduct.

This also shows that w-f-q does not necessarily carry a positive evaluation. The root that can describe harmony in repairing a relationship here describes punishment corresponding to transgressors’ deeds.

Harmony and correspondence are thus linguistic concepts; the goodness or badness of what corresponds is determined by subject and context.

4. Three important settings of Qur’anic usage

The four occurrences can be understood within three main settings.

Harmony between people: in al-Nisāʾ 4:35, yuwaffiqi Allāhu baynahumā concerns bringing about accord in a relationship troubled by disagreement.

Enabling effort and action: in Hūd 11:88, tawfīqī concerns divine help in pursuing reform. In al-Nisāʾ 4:62, tawfīqan appears in a claim to have worked toward reconciliation.

Correspondence between recompense and conduct: in al-Nabaʾ 78:26, wifāqan describes recompense fitting the deeds.

Each usage concerns a relationship between two sides: people, effort and outcome, or conduct and recompense.

Yet each verse expresses that relationship differently. The derivatives cannot all be translated simply as “success,” or reduced to “agreement between two people.”

5. Tawfīq and human responsibility

Contemporary usage often equates tawfīq with success or attaining a desired outcome. This fits some contexts, but carrying it into the Qur’an indiscriminately obscures much of the root’s range.

In al-Nisāʾ 4:35, tawfīq concerns accord between two parties. In al-Nisāʾ 4:62, people claim that they intended goodness and reconciliation. In Hūd 11:88, Shuʿayb attributes his enabling to God.

These differences require distinguishing the word itself from its moral direction.

Divine enabling should also not be turned into compulsion or the ineffectiveness of human will. The arbitration verse speaks of intending repair, and Shuʿayb explicitly pursues reform to the extent of his ability.

Human intention and action have not been removed in either usage.

Conversely, tawfīq should not be treated solely as the product of individual effort independent of God. Shuʿayb works for reform without considering himself independent of divine help.

His words thus preserve both human responsibility and dependence on the Lord.

6. Distinctions from related roots

W-f-q (wafq) and ṣ-l-ḥ (ṣalāḥ): compatibility and reform

Ṣalāḥ concerns becoming sound, improving, and removing corruption. Wafq concerns correspondence and harmony between things.

In al-Nisāʾ 4:35, iṣlāḥan names the effort’s aim, while yuwaffiqi describes bringing the parties into harmony.

The words are related but are not exact synonyms.

Two people may agree to do something wrong. Their agreement is a form of accord linguistically, but not necessarily reform. Repairing a dispute, meanwhile, may require agreement respecting the parties’ rights and responsibilities.

Study of ṣ-l-ḥ

W-f-q (wafq) and w-f-y (wafāʾ): correspondence and full discharge

Despite similar spelling, these roots are independent.

Qur’anic w-f-y concerns rendering in full, discharging rights completely, fulfilling covenants, and withholding nothing from an account or commitment.

W-f-q concerns compatibility and correspondence.

For example, أَوْفُوا الْكَيْلَ in al-Isrāʾ 17:35 concerns giving full measure, whereas جَزَاءً وِفَاقًا concerns recompense corresponding to deeds.

Something may be paid in full without fitting a sound standard. Conversely, things may correspond in quantity or quality without any question of fulfilling a covenant or discharging a right.

Tawfīq thus derives from w-f-q, not w-f-y.

Study of w-f-y

W-f-q (wafq) and ʿ-d-l (ʿadl): correspondence and justice

Qur’anic ʿadl concerns justice, sound assessment, and respecting rights. Wafq emphasizes compatibility and correspondence.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:26 describes recompense fitting deeds. This relates to justice, but wifāq does not encompass the entire meaning of justice.

Agreement between two people likewise does not guarantee a just outcome. They may agree to commit an injustice; agreement alone does not make it just.

Study of ʿ-d-l

W-f-q (wafq) and q-d-r (qadr): correspondence and measure

Qadr concerns measure, quantity, and determining a limit. Wafq concerns compatibility between things.

Saying that food matches a family’s needs involves both the amount of food and its relationship to those needs.

Qadr can emphasize the food’s quantity, whereas wafq emphasizes that quantity’s suitability to the need.

This distinction also helps explain jazāʾan wifāqan: wifāq expresses correspondence, not necessarily a numerical quantity of recompense.

Study of q-d-r

W-f-q (wafq) and kh-l-f (khilāf): agreement and opposition

These families can stand in contrast in usages involving agreement and disagreement.

Muwāfaqah denotes accord, while mukhālafah denotes disagreement or going against something.

In Hūd 11:88, Shuʿayb first disavows doing what he forbids others, then speaks of reform and divine enabling.

This sequence shows how consistency between speech and action fits his reforming call, while each word’s lexical meaning should remain distinct.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

For wafq, “corresponding,” “fitting,” and “compatible” are appropriate according to context.

For wāfaqa, use “agreed,” “matched,” or, in contexts of coincidence, “coincided with” and “encountered.”

For wifāq, principal equivalents include “compatibility,” “agreement,” “harmony,” and “correspondence.”

For waffaqa, suitable renderings include “brought into harmony,” “coordinated,” or, for effort and action, “enabled” and “provided the means of success.”

For tawfīq in interpersonal relationships, “bringing about harmony” or “reconciliation” is clearer. In contexts of human effort and divine help, “enabling” or “help to act rightly” fits better.

The following renderings are proposed for the four Qur’anic occurrences:

Al-Nisāʾ 4:35

“If they desire reconciliation, The God will bring about harmony between them.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:62

“We intended nothing but good and reconciliation.”

Hūd 11:88

“And my enabling is only through The God.”

Al-Nabaʾ 78:26

“A fitting recompense.”

These equivalents preserve the differences in usage. Rendering everything as “success” or “agreement” without context narrows the root’s range.

8. Conclusion

W-f-q denotes compatibility, proportion, and correspondence between two or more things. This can concern time, quantity, interpersonal relationships, efforts to carry out a task, or the relationship between conduct and recompense.

Arabic wafq describes what suits something else, while wifāq expresses compatibility and correspondence. Tawfīq concerns bringing about or enabling the harmony needed for an undertaking.

The Qur’an has four distinct usages. In al-Nisāʾ 4:35, tawfīq concerns bringing two parties into harmony. In al-Nisāʾ 4:62, people claim goodness and reconciliation as their intention. In Hūd 11:88, Shuʿayb attributes the enabling of his reforming effort to God. In al-Nabaʾ 78:26, wifāq concerns recompense corresponding to deeds.

These usages show that wafq does not inherently mean goodness, guidance, or moral success. Accord can concern a right or wrong action; its value depends on subject and context.

Shuʿayb connects tawfīq with reform, effort within his ability, trust in God, and turning to Him. People whose conduct the Qur’an criticizes use it for their claim of reconciliation and good intention. In the verse of recompense, wifāq concerns consequences fitting deeds.

The root w-f-q means compatibility and correspondence between things; tawfīq concerns bringing about or enabling the coordination needed for an undertaking. Whether that undertaking is good or bad, and whether a claim of tawfīq is true or false, is determined through context and clear Qur’anic criteria rather than the root alone.
