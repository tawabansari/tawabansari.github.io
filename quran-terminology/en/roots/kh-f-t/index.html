Khufūt, quiet speech, and subsiding sound: from less audible speech to private conversation

In Arabic, خ ف ت concerns lowering the voice, quieting speech, and reducing its audibility. This family is used when someone speaks in a low, subdued voice, or people speak together so quietly that others cannot easily hear them.

Lexical uses extend beyond speech. Related forms describe sound fading through weakness, a weakened person whose speech ceases, and certain conditions of feebleness and decline.

The root appears in three Qur’anic verses: once opposed to jahr, making the voice open and loud, and twice describing quiet conversation between people.

The essential point is that khufūt is not identical with absolute silence. Speech may still exist but be quiet and less audible. Private conversation also does not necessarily imply lying, plotting, or wrongful intentions. These depend on its content and context.

1. Root forms and derivatives

خ ف ت is a sound triliteral Arabic root. Important forms include:

  • خَفَتَ ـ يَخْفِتُ (khafata–yakhfitu): a sound became lower, quiet, or weak; in other uses, something became still or declined.
  • خَفْت (khaft): speaking quietly and privately, opposed to jahr.
  • خُفُوت (khufūt): subsiding or quieting sound; in some uses, weakness and stillness.
  • خُفَات (khufāt): weakness and, in certain uses, sudden death.
  • خَافِت (khāfit): low-voiced or subdued; outside sound contexts, weak or diminished.
  • خَفِيت (khafīt): an adjective for a low, weak sound.
  • خَافَتَ ـ يُخَافِتُ (khāfata–yukhāfitu): lowered the voice and spoke or recited quietly.
  • مُخَافَتَة (mukhāfatah): speaking quietly or lowering the voice.
  • تَخَافَتَ ـ يَتَخَافَتُ (takhāfata–yatakhāfatu): conversed quietly or privately, particularly among several people.
  • تَخَافُت (takhāfut): quiet, private conversation; in some lexical uses, subsiding or displaying weakness.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an.

Its Qur’anic derivatives comprise only two forms:

تُخَافِتْ (tukhāfit) in Al-Isrāʾ 17:110, Form III, and يَتَخَافَتُونَ (yatakhāfatūna) in Ṭā Hā 20:103 and Al-Qalam 68:23, Form VI.

The root therefore occurs three times in three verses, but has only two verbal forms.

The distinction matters: tukhāfit concerns lowering the voice during an activity, whereas yatakhāfatūna describes quiet conversation between people.

Form III does not itself require reciprocal action. Nor does Form VI establish every detail of the participants’ interaction without context.

2. Semantic core and early Arabic use

Subsiding sound and speech kept less audible

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris gives this root one principle:

أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ، وَهُوَ إِسْرَارٌ وَكِتْمَانٌ

aṣlun wāḥidun wa-huwa isrārun wa-kitmānun

That is, “one principle: secrecy and concealment.”

He then explains khaft as isrār al-nuṭq, expressing speech quietly or privately.

In speech contexts, this concerns restricting sound’s audibility.

Kitmān in this lexical explanation should not be taken as consciously concealing truth or refusing to declare what is right in every use. Someone may speak ordinary, truthful words quietly without concealing a truth from anyone.

Lisān al-ʿArab explains خَفَتَ الصَّوْتُ خُفُوتًا as sound becoming quiet or still. خَافَتَ بِصَوْتِهِ means lowering one’s voice, and mukhāfatah concealing or quieting it.

These witnesses allow two uses to be distinguished:

In خَفَتَ الصَّوْتُ, the sound itself becomes lower or subsides.

In خَافَتَ بِصَوْتِهِ, someone lowers their voice.

The distinction is between the sound’s condition and the speaker’s action.

For group conversations, تَخَافَتَ الْقَوْمُ describes people speaking quietly among themselves or consulting privately.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: quiet speech and the opposition of jahr and khaft

Does khufūt mean complete silence?

In some lexical uses, khafata reaches the sense of becoming silent or still. In speech-related mukhāfatah and takhāfut, however, speaking usually continues while sound becomes less audible.

Two simple situations clarify this distinction.

Someone saying nothing is silent. Someone speaking very quietly is still speaking, even though a person farther away may not hear.

Both conditions can relate to quietness and stillness in Arabic, but they are not identical.

The Qur’an makes this distinction clear. In Ṭā Hā 20:103, people yatakhāfatūna while saying something definite among themselves. In Al-Qalam 68:23, their quiet conversation continues, with its content stated in the following verse.

Translating these as “they remain silent” is therefore inaccurate: the verses report conversation.

Weakness and stillness beyond speech

Lisān al-ʿArab records meanings besides lowering sound.

Al-khaft and al-khufāt sometimes concern weakness from hunger and similar causes. Khufūt can also describe someone’s voice when severe weakness prevents speaking at normal volume.

Here low sound results from physical weakness, not necessarily a conscious decision to conceal speech.

خَفَتَ مِنَ النُّعَاسِ also denotes becoming quiet or still through drowsiness.

زَرْعٌ خَافِتٌ describes weak or stunted vegetation that has not reached its usual growth. Other explanations concern the softness and flexibility of young plants.

Khāfit is also recorded for a cloud without water, highlighting weakness or the lack of its effective characteristic.

Subsiding and weakness therefore extend beyond sound.

Their precise historical relationships should nevertheless not be assumed certain. Physical weakness may lower a voice, while an adjective may also function across related settings without a clear chronology for all its uses.

Khufūt describing death

Some Arabic uses give خَفَتَ الرَّجُلُ the meaning “the man died.”

Lisān al-ʿArab explains this through the cessation of speech and the person’s silence: someone who no longer speaks is described through khufūt.

Some lexical transmissions also give khufāt the sense of sudden death.

This does not make death the root’s basic definition.

Qur’anic tukhāfit and yatakhāfatūna concern speaking and reduced sound; neither means dying.

Lisān al-ʿArab: sound, weakness, plants, and silence in death

3. Poetic and early linguistic witnesses

The opposition of jahr and khufūt in Arabic poetry

Ibn Fāris and Ibn Manẓūr quote a verse without securely identifying its poet:

أُخَاطِبُ جَهْرًا إِذْ لَهُنَّ تَخَافُتٌ
وَشَتَّانَ بَيْنَ الْجَهْرِ وَالْمَنْطِقِ الْخَفْتِ

ukhāṭibu jahran idh lahunna takhāfutun / wa-shattāna bayna al-jahri wa-al-manṭiqi al-khafti

Translation:

“I speak openly, while their speech is quiet; how different are open speech and subdued utterance.”

Three words matter here:

Jahran denotes speaking openly; takhāfutun conveys quiet conversation; and al-manṭiq al-khaft describes low, less audible speech.

Their opposition places khaft against jahr within the field of speech.

Both sides concern speaking: one party speaks audibly, the other more quietly.

Thus khufūt here is not a complete absence of speech, but a quality of how it is heard.

The poet’s name and the verse’s precise date are unclear in the examined transmissions. It documents a use recorded in early Arabic sources, but should not be called securely pre-Islamic without independent evidence.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: the unnamed poetic witness for jahr and takhāfut

Khufāt in poetry attributed to Al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī

Lisān al-ʿArab attributes the following to Al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī:

وَلَسْتُ وَإِنْ عَزُّوا عَلَيَّ بِهَالِكٍ
خُفَاتًا وَلَا مُسْتَهْزِمٍ ذَاهِبِ الْعَقْلِ

wa-lastu wa-in ʿazzū ʿalayya bi-hālikin / khufātan wa-lā mustahzimin dhāhibi al-ʿaqli

Approximate translation, following the reading of khufātan as suddenly:

“Although they are dear to me, I will not collapse into sudden death, nor become distraught and lose my reason.” On the weakness-and-abasement reading, the first part concerns collapsing in weakness and humiliation. These explanations should not be treated as a single certain meaning.

Two lexical readings of khufātan are transmitted.

Abū ʿAmr takes it as suddenly, without warning; Abū Manṣūr interprets it as weakness and abasement. Mustahzim is also glossed here as jazūʿ, distraught or impatient under distress.

This disagreement matters: a form of the family in a particular witness can be interpreted between meanings associated with decline and destruction.

Sudden death should therefore not become the certain meaning of every form of khufūt without regard to the competing explanations.

Al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī was a mukhaḍram poet, living across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. That alone does not establish that this particular verse predates the Qur’an’s revelation.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Al-Jaʿdī’s witness and two explanations of khufātan

Less audible speech in social life

تَخَافَتَ الْقَوْمُ describes people consulting privately among themselves.

Takhāfut can thus characterize group communication, keeping speech quiet enough to remain within the group.

This may occur in private consultation, cautious conversation, or concealing a decision from others.

The verb alone does not reveal the purpose. Fear, caution, respect, privacy, or wrongful intent requires further evidence.

This distinction also matters in both Qur’anic occurrences of yatakhāfatūna.

4. Qur’anic uses

A. A middle course between jahr and mukhāfatah — Al-Isrāʾ 17:110

قُلِ ادْعُوا اللَّهَ أَوِ ادْعُوا الرَّحْمَٰنَ أَيًّا مَا تَدْعُوا فَلَهُ الْأَسْمَاءُ الْحُسْنَىٰ وَلَا تَجْهَرْ بِصَلَاتِكَ وَلَا تُخَافِتْ بِهَا وَابْتَغِ بَيْنَ ذَٰلِكَ سَبِيلًا

quli idʿū allāha awi idʿū al-raḥmāna ayyan mā tadʿū fa-lahu al-asmāʾu al-ḥusnā wa-lā tajhar bi-ṣalātika wa-lā tukhāfit bihā wa-ibtaghi bayna dhālika sabīlan

Proposed translation for the root study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Say, ‘Call upon The God, or call upon the Entirely Merciful; whichever you call upon, His are the finest names.’ Do not make your ṣalāh loud, nor perform it in an excessively low voice; seek a way between the two.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:110

Tukhāfit is a second-person masculine singular imperfect, Form III, made jussive by prohibitive lā.

It accompanies bi-hā. The feminine pronoun hā refers to ṣalātika and identifies the activity whose sound is lowered.

Opposite it stands tajhar, from ج ه ر, making sound open and loud.

The structure prohibits jahr on one side and mukhāfatah on the other, then asks for a course between them.

What does tukhāfit mean here?

Lexically, it concerns lowering and subduing the voice.

Set against jahr, it describes a voice so low and less audible that it stands at the opposite end from loudness.

The verse thus presents two limits and asks for a middle course.

The verb itself does not show that every low voice is objectionable. The prohibition concerns a condition set against jahr, with a path between them requested.

Moderation follows from the whole construction, particularly وَابْتَغِ بَيْنَ ذَٰلِكَ سَبِيلًا, rather than khaft fundamentally meaning excess.

Khufūt and ṣalāh

Ṣalātika is the activity addressed. For this root study, خ ف ت concerns sound’s quality; the full range of ṣalāh requires separate examination of that word and its Qur’anic uses.

The verse begins with “call upon” and divine names, connecting it with calling, supplication, and remembering The God.

Tukhāfit alone nevertheless cannot establish that ṣalāh always means only reciting a supplication or reading aloud throughout the Qur’an.

This verse clearly calls for a way between two contrasting manners of making sound open and lowering it during ṣalāh.

The verse does not itself specify a numerical volume, listener distance, or a division of prayers into times with distinct sound rules.

Such details should not be added to khaft’s lexical meaning.

Al-Ṭabarī: loudness, subdued speech, and a course between them

B. Quiet conversation about the duration of a stay — Ṭā Hā 20:103

يَتَخَافَتُونَ بَيْنَهُمْ إِنْ لَبِثْتُمْ إِلَّا عَشْرًا

yatakhāfatūna baynahum in labithtum illā ʿashran

Proposed translation for the root study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“They converse quietly among themselves: ‘You stayed no more than ten [units of time].’”

Ṭā Hā 20:103

Yatakhāfatūna is a masculine plural imperfect, Form VI. In both occurrences, it is third-person masculine plural and indicative, marked by retention of nūn; wāw is the plural subject pronoun.

Unlike Al-Isrāʾ 17:110, this construction describes conversation among several people.

Baynahum strengthens this: the speech passes among themselves.

The context is the day the trumpet is blown and the criminals are gathered. A group discusses how long they remained.

The final part of Ṭā Hā 20:103 gives their words:

إِنْ لَبِثْتُمْ إِلَّا عَشْرًا

in labithtum illā ʿashran

Proposed translation of this excerpt: “You stayed no more than ten.”

The unit of time is not explicitly named. Whether ten refers to days, nights, or another duration requires contextual examination.

Ṭā Hā 20:104 gives another statement describing their stay as only one day.

This continuity shows people viewing their previous stay as short or discussing its length.

Yatakhāfatūna does not itself mean miscalculating time. It describes how they converse; the following expression supplies the content.

Does their quiet speech arise from fear?

The Resurrection setting and the criminals’ condition may suggest fear, anxiety, and confusion.

Quiet conversation is understandable in such a setting.

The root itself does not establish fear as the definite cause.

People may speak quietly from fear, but quiet speech does not linguistically entail being afraid.

Here the word denotes less audible conversation, with the Resurrection furnishing its setting.

Does takhāfut mean speech hidden from The God?

Part of Ṭā Hā 20:104 reads:

نَحْنُ أَعْلَمُ بِمَا يَقُولُونَ

naḥnu aʿlamu bi-mā yaqūlūna

Proposed translation of this excerpt: “We know best what they say.”

Their quiet conversation is therefore not hidden from divine knowledge.

Lowering a voice in human communication must be distinguished from actually concealing speech from divine knowledge.

Takhāfut expresses the former here, not the latter.

Al-Ṭabarī: quiet conversation and duration of stay

C. The garden owners’ private conversation — Al-Qalam 68:23

فَانْطَلَقُوا وَهُمْ يَتَخَافَتُونَ

fa-inṭalaqū wa-hum yatakhāfatūna

Proposed translation for the root study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“They set out, conversing quietly among themselves.”

Al-Qalam 68:23

This occurs in the story of the garden owners.

They intend to leave early to harvest. Earlier verses describe their decision and oath; subsequent verses clarify what they say.

As in Ṭā Hā 20:103, yatakhāfatūna is Form VI and denotes quiet conversation among people.

The sentence construction, however, differs.

وَهُمْ يَتَخَافَتُونَ is a circumstantial clause describing their condition while setting out.

They are moving and simultaneously speaking quietly together.

The next verse supplies the content:

أَنْ لَا يَدْخُلَنَّهَا الْيَوْمَ عَلَيْكُمْ مِسْكِينٌ

an lā yadkhulannahā al-yawma ʿalaykum miskīnun

Proposed translation of Al-Qalam 68:24: “No needy person must enter it upon you today.”

Al-Qalam 68:24

Their conversation concerns their decision to prevent needy people from coming.

Quiet speech thus forms part of conduct aimed at excluding the needy from sharing in the produce.

This moral significance comes from their words and decision, rather than yatakhāfatūna itself.

Were the same verb used of people privately consulting about helping someone in need, its lexical meaning would remain quiet conversation, while the moral value would differ.

A manner of speaking can serve different purposes; this distinction matters for the root.

Al-Ṭabarī: the garden owners’ quiet conversation and its content

Connections and differences among the three Qur’anic uses

All three involve low, restricted audibility, but differ in construction and situation.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:110, tukhāfit concerns how ṣalāh is voiced and contrasts with tajhar.

In Ṭā Hā 20:103, yatakhāfatūna describes people’s quiet conversation in the Resurrection scene.

In Al-Qalam 68:23, it describes the garden owners moving while privately discussing their decision.

The principal distinction is lowering one’s voice in an activity versus several people conversing quietly together.

The thematic or moral difference between Ṭā Hā and Al-Qalam comes from content and context, not morphology: both use precisely yatakhāfatūna.

5. Distinctions from related roots

خ ف ت (khufūt) compared with ج ه ر (jahr)

Jahr is khaft’s clearest Qur’anic opposite.

ج ه ر concerns becoming manifest, making something visible, and expressing something openly and loudly.

Part of Al-Isrāʾ 17:110 sets them against each other:

وَلَا تَجْهَرْ بِصَلَاتِكَ وَلَا تُخَافِتْ بِهَا

wa-lā tajhar bi-ṣalātika wa-lā tukhāfit bihā

Jahr here is making the voice open and loud; khufūt is lowering and subduing it.

This does not establish that jahr is always wrong and khaft always good, or the reverse.

The verse itself prohibits both limits and calls for a middle way.

Study of J H R

خ ف ت (khufūt) compared with خ ف ي (khafāʾ)

خ ف ي concerns being hidden or concealed, whether speech, thought, action, or something else.

خ ف ت in its principal Qur’anic uses concerns a low, less audible voice.

Quiet speech may be hidden from others, but concealment does not always result from lowering the voice.

Someone may conceal a thought, hide a document, or act out of public view without sound being involved.

Khafāʾ therefore has a broader range of objects, while all three Qur’anic uses of khaft concern speech and sound.

خ ف ت (khufūt) compared with خ ف ض (khafḍ)

خ ف ض denotes lowering and reducing.

It can concern position, limbs, a condition, or other things.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:24, وَاخْفِضْ لَهُمَا جَنَاحَ الذُّلِّ concerns lowering the wing of humility for one’s parents.

Khaft in speech concerns sound subsiding or being subdued.

Khafḍ al-ṣawt and mukhāfatat al-ṣawt can be very close in a particular situation, but khafḍ is not confined to sound.

Luqmān 31:19 also uses وَاغْضُضْ مِنْ صَوْتِكَ for lowering the voice. This belongs to غ ض ض, showing that the Qur’an uses more than خ ف ت to regulate sound.

خ ف ت (khufūt) compared with ن ج و (najwā)

Najwā concerns private conversation, confidential speech, and discussion kept apart from others.

Al-Mujādilah 58:9, for example, evaluates private conversation’s content in terms of goodness, mindfulness, sin, and aggression.

Khaft likewise describes less audible conversation in two Qur’anic uses.

There is, however, a significant distinction.

Takhāfut foregrounds low volume; najwā emphasizes the privacy of communication between people.

They may occur together: people conversing privately may also speak quietly.

They remain distinct roots with related semantic fields and should not be treated as completely synonymous without examining construction and context.

As Al-Mujādilah 58:9 itself shows, private conversation can have good or wrongful content. Privacy alone does not determine moral value.

Study of N J W

خ ف ت (khufūt) compared with ه م س (hams)

Hams also concerns quiet, less audible sound.

Ṭā Hā 20:108 reads:

يَوْمَئِذٍ يَتَّبِعُونَ الدَّاعِيَ لَا عِوَجَ لَهُ وَخَشَعَتِ الْأَصْوَاتُ لِلرَّحْمَٰنِ فَلَا تَسْمَعُ إِلَّا هَمْسًا

yawmaʾidhin yattabiʿūna al-dāʿiya lā ʿiwaja lahu wa-khashaʿati al-aṣwātu li-al-raḥmāni fa-lā tasmaʿu illā hamsan

Proposed translation for the root study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“On that day they follow the caller, for whom there is no crookedness; voices fall quiet before the Entirely Merciful, so you hear only a faint sound.”

Ṭā Hā 20:108

Hamsan denotes a very low, less audible sound here.

Some lexical uses concern the soft sound of movement or contact, so its field is not necessarily confined to verbal conversation.

Yatakhāfatūna in Ṭā Hā 20:103, by contrast, clearly concerns people conversing.

These verses belong to one surah and both concern the Resurrection, but use different words.

Yatakhāfatūna in Ṭā Hā 20:103 expresses quiet conversation; hamsan in Ṭā Hā 20:108 names what is heard when voices have subsided.

Semantic proximity should not erase differences in construction and grammatical role.

خ ف ت (khufūt) compared with ك ت م (kitmān)

Ibn Fāris mentions kitmān when explaining khaft, but this does not make the two roots identical in every use.

Kitmān may concern concealing knowledge, truth, or information that someone withholds from expression.

In takhāfut, someone may express that information, but quietly or within a limited group.

In Ṭā Hā 20:103, for example, people actually speak. Their speech is not absent; it is quietly exchanged among themselves.

Takhāfut may therefore help conceal speech from others, but does not establish that speakers are withholding a truth they are obliged to declare.

Study of K T M

خ ف ت (khufūt) compared with خ ف ف (khiffah)

خ ف ف is another root, concerning lightness, becoming lighter, and reducing weight.

Despite the phonetic resemblance of khafata and khaffa, they are not one root.

Qur’anic خ ف ف concerns lightening and being light; خ ف ت concerns lowering sound and quiet conversation in its three occurrences.

Tukhāfit therefore does not mean making ṣalāh lighter, shortening it, or reducing its duration. In Al-Isrāʾ 17:110 it concerns manner of voicing and sound.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Speaking quietly, lowering the voice, and quiet conversation are suitable Persian equivalents.

The distinction between the two Qur’anic forms should nevertheless be maintained.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:110, tukhāfit contrasts with jahr. “Performing in a very low voice” or “subduing the voice excessively” can clarify the contextual meaning.

“Excessively” follows from the two limits and the call for a middle course; it is not a fixed part of the verb itself.

In Ṭā Hā 20:103 and Al-Qalam 68:23, yatakhāfatūna denotes people conversing quietly together. “They converse quietly among themselves” is more natural than “they remain silent.”

Brief root meaning

Sound becoming quiet and less audible, speaking softly, and conversing privately; in other uses, weakness and subsiding.

Meanings in the verses

تُخَافِتْ — Al-Isrāʾ 17:110: performing ṣalāh in a very low, subdued voice.

يَتَخَافَتُونَ — Ṭā Hā 20:103: they converse quietly among themselves.

يَتَخَافَتُونَ — Al-Qalam 68:23: while moving, they speak quietly among themselves.

These equivalents preserve each form’s morphology, sentence role, and context.

Conclusion

In Arabic, خ ف ت concerns subsiding sound, less audible speech, and quiet speaking. Elsewhere it may express weakness and stillness, describing a weakened person, stunted vegetation, or the cessation of speech.

All three Qur’anic uses concern speech and sound.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:110, tukhāfit contrasts with tajhar, with a way sought between loudness and subdued sound. In Ṭā Hā 20:103 and Al-Qalam 68:23, yatakhāfatūna denotes quiet conversation between people.

Khufūt need not mean absolute silence; in speech, it means reduced loudness and audibility. Takhāfut can describe private conversation without itself determining its cause or moral value.

The root’s basic meaning can therefore be described as sound subsiding or being lowered, and, in speech contexts, speaking quietly and less audibly. Brief root meaning: Sound becoming quiet and less audible, speaking softly, and conversing privately; in other uses, weakness and subsiding.
