Core meaning: confinement, detention, and restricting freedom to move or leave a situation. In human usage, it means holding someone where they cannot freely leave. Sijn denotes prison and masjūn an imprisoned person. Qur’anic uses principally concern Joseph's imprisonment, Pharaoh's threat to imprison Moses, and sijjīn in connection with the record of the wicked. Sijjīn is associated with confinement, but its precise nature requires examination of the language and context of Al-Muṭaffifīn.

1. The root in early Arabic

Arabic S J N concerns confinement, detention, and restrictions on freedom.

Sajana al-rajula means imprisoning or detaining a man.

Sajanahu sajnan describes the act of confinement.

Al-sijn is a place where people are imprisoned.

Al-masjūn is someone confined, whose freedom to leave or move is restricted.

The central point is not merely being inside an enclosed place, but a restriction preventing a person from leaving freely.

Living in a house, room, or enclosure does not itself constitute imprisonment when one is free to leave.

Conversely, holding someone against their will without permission to leave constitutes confinement.

Walls, gates, and guards may be involved, but building design, wall materials, and means of detention do not belong to the root's basic meaning.

A verse attributed to early poetry: confining the tongue

Arabic literary sources transmit this verse:

لَعَمْرُكَ مَا شَيْءٌ عَلِمْتُ مَكَانَهُ
أَحَقُّ بِسِجْنٍ مِنْ لِسَانٍ مُذَلَّلِ

la-ʿamruka mā shayʾun ʿalimtu makānahu / aḥaqqu bi-sijnin min lisānin mudhallal

Translation:

“By your life, of all the things I know, nothing deserves confinement more than a tongue readily put to use.”

Sijn here concerns restraint and confinement.

The poet is not discussing imprisoning a person, but restraining the tongue from uncontrolled speech.

Confinement is figurative here.

The tongue is not placed in a prison building; a person restrains it from improper speech.

Some sources attribute the verse to Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd, while others attribute it to Abū al-Aswad al-Duʾalī. It should not be treated as securely attributed to Ṭarafah without an independent examination of the transmission.

It therefore cannot unconditionally serve as certain pre-Islamic evidence.

Its presence in Arabic literary tradition nevertheless illustrates the extension from bodily detention to restraint of speech.

Another poetic example: confining sorrow

Lexical sources transmit another verse without a secure historical attribution:

وَلَا تَسْجُنَنَّ الْهَمَّ إِنَّ لِسَجْنِهِ
عَنَاءً وَحَمِّلْهُ الْمَهَارَى النَّوَاجِيَا

wa-lā tasjunanna al-hamma inna li-sajnihi / ʿanāʾan wa-ḥammilhu al-mahārā al-nawājiyā

Translation:

“Do not imprison sorrow within you: holding it is a hardship. Load it onto swift travelling camels.”

Tasjunanna means to confine or hold within.

Al-hamm is sorrow or anxiety.

Al-mahārā al-nawājī refers to swift camels capable of travelling; another transmission reads al-maṭiyya al-nawājiya, swift riding camels.

The poet urges a person not to hold sorrow within, but to seek release from it.

Sorrow is imagined as something that can be confined inside a person.

Sajn itself does not mean sorrow or psychological suffering.

Sorrow is the object being confined.

This verse is recorded in lexicons, but its attribution and date are not secure enough to make it certain pre-Qur’anic evidence.

Defining the root historically

The evidence considered here does not securely date a particular poetic example to the pre-Islamic period.

Confinement and detention are nevertheless established meanings in classical Arabic, and the Qur’an itself provides early evidence for them.

A defensible definition is therefore:

S J N concerns confining or restraining someone or something from free movement, departure, or release. For people, it can involve holding them in a particular place; figuratively, it can extend to restraining the tongue or keeping sorrow within.

This fits the Qur’anic prison passages.

Sijjīn in Al-Muṭaffifīn requires separate examination, since the immediate subject is not a person being imprisoned in this world.

2. Important forms and derivatives

سَجَنَ يَسْجُنُ

The family's Form I verb.

It means to confine, imprison, or prevent someone from leaving freely.

سَجَنَ الرَّجُلَ

sajana al-rajula

Meaning: “He imprisoned a man.”

The subject performs the detention; the object is the person whose freedom is restricted.

The Qur’an uses this verb in both active and passive forms.

سَجْن

A verbal noun of Form I, denoting the act of imprisonment.

Arabic distinguishes sajn with a fatḥah from sijn with a kasrah.

Sajn principally concerns the act of confinement.

Sijn commonly denotes the place of confinement.

The distinction is not absolute in all Arabic usage, but matters for studying the Qur’anic forms.

سِجْن

Prison or a place of detention.

دَخَلَ السِّجْنَ

dakhala al-sijna

Meaning: “He entered prison.”

خَرَجَ مِنَ السِّجْنِ

kharaja mina al-sijni

Meaning: “He left prison.”

Prison here is a place where people are held with restrictions on leaving.

Its plural is sujūn.

Al-sijn occurs in the Qur’anic account of Joseph.

سَجِين

A description meaning imprisoned or confined.

Used for someone held in confinement.

Sujanāʾ and sajnā are also recorded plurals for prisoners.

This differs from sijjīn with a doubled jīm.

مَسْجُون

The passive participle of Form I.

Imprisoned or confined.

Its sound masculine plural is masjūnūn, with masjūnīn in the accusative and genitive.

Al-masjūnīn occurs in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:29.

سَجَّان

A prison guard or someone responsible for keeping prisoners.

An established Arabic word, but absent from the Qur’an in this form.

سَجَّنَ and تَسْجِين

In imprisonment-related usage, this construction concerns confining or holding someone in prison.

Tasjīn is the verbal noun of Form II.

It is also familiar in administrative and legal Arabic.

The Qur’an does not use Form II verbs for imprisonment.

سِجِّين

With doubled jīm, it differs structurally from sajīn, a prisoner.

Lexicons connect sijjīn with confinement, constriction, and a place of detention.

It occurs only in Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:7 and Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:8.

There it concerns the record of the wicked.

It may name a particular place or register containing those records, or an encompassing recorded document itself.

Shared root letters alone do not determine its precise nature.

Forms actually found in the Qur’an

This family occurs twelve times:

  • Three Form I verbs: Yūsuf 12:25, Yūsuf 12:32, and Yūsuf 12:35.
  • Six occurrences of al-sijn: Yūsuf 12:33, Yūsuf 12:36, Yūsuf 12:39, Yūsuf 12:41, Yūsuf 12:42, and Yūsuf 12:100.
  • One occurrence of al-masjūnīn: Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:29.
  • Two occurrences of sijjīn: Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:7 and Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:8.

Most concern actual human confinement; the two sijjīn passages concern records and the fate of the wicked.

3. Prison in Joseph's story: threats, pressure, and restricted freedom

Joseph's story contains the largest group of Qur’anic uses.

Prison appears both as a place and as an instrument of threats, pressure, and power.

These features come from the narrative and its characters, rather than injustice or abuse of power being the lexical meaning of sajn.

The proposed imprisonment in Yūsuf 12:25

After Joseph and the official's wife race to the door and his shirt is torn from behind, she says to her husband in Yūsuf 12:25:

مَا جَزَاءُ مَنْ أَرَادَ بِأَهْلِكَ سُوءًا إِلَّا أَنْ يُسْجَنَ أَوْ عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ

mā jazāʾu man arāda bi-ahlika sūʾan illā an yusjana aw ʿadhābun alīm

Yūsuf 12:25

What is the recompense of someone who intended harm to your household, except imprisonment or painful punishment?

Yusjana is a passive imperfect verb.

It means “be imprisoned.”

The woman proposes prison or painful punishment in response to her accusation.

An accusation must be distinguished from proof.

The following narrative presents the shirt torn from behind as evidence for discerning what happened.

Her statement in Yūsuf 12:25 is therefore not the Qur’an's final judgment that Joseph is guilty.

It is speech by a character in the story.

Lexically, yusjana expresses being imprisoned.

Whether imprisonment is just or unjust depends on its circumstances and grounds.

The explicit threat in Yūsuf 12:32

قَالَتْ فَذَٰلِكُنَّ الَّذِي لُمْتُنَّنِي فِيهِ وَلَقَدْ رَاوَدْتُهُ عَنْ نَفْسِهِ فَاسْتَعْصَمَ وَلَئِنْ لَمْ يَفْعَلْ مَا آمُرُهُ لَيُسْجَنَنَّ وَلَيَكُونًا مِنَ الصَّاغِرِينَ

qālat fa-dhālikunna alladhī lumtunnanī fīhi wa-laqad rāwadtuhu ʿan nafsihi fa-istaʿṣama wa-laʾin lam yafʿal mā āmuruhu la-yusjananna wa-la-yakūnan mina al-ṣāghirīn

Yūsuf 12:32

Translation:

“She said: This is the one concerning whom you blamed me. I did seek to seduce him, but he resisted. If he does not do what I command, he will certainly be imprisoned and become one of the humbled.”

La-yusjananna is an emphatic passive imperfect.

The woman presents Joseph's imprisonment as a serious threat.

Wa-la-yakūnan mina al-ṣāghirīn also speaks of his humiliation.

The expressions are connected but not synonymous.

La-yusjananna expresses being imprisoned.

Al-ṣāghirīn concerns humiliation.

Prison may be used to humiliate, but S J N itself does not mean humiliation.

Here prison becomes an instrument for compelling Joseph to fulfil the woman's desire.

Restriction of freedom can thus serve an abuse of power.

That conclusion comes from the threat and her conduct, rather than the word prison itself.

4. “Prison is dearer to me”: Joseph's choice under pressure

قَالَ رَبِّ السِّجْنُ أَحَبُّ إِلَيَّ مِمَّا يَدْعُونَنِي إِلَيْهِ وَإِلَّا تَصْرِفْ عَنِّي كَيْدَهُنَّ أَصْبُ إِلَيْهِنَّ وَأَكُنْ مِنَ الْجَاهِلِينَ

qāla rabbi al-sijnu aḥabbu ilayya mimmā yadʿūnanī ilayhi wa-illā taṣrif ʿannī kaydahunna aṣbu ilayhinna wa-akun mina al-jāhilīn

Yūsuf 12:33

Translation:

“He said: My Lord, prison is dearer to me than what they invite me to. Unless You turn their plotting away from me, I shall incline toward them and be among the ignorant.”

Yūsuf 12:33

Al-sijn is a noun referring to prison.

Joseph faces two situations.

On one side are pressure and the threat of imprisonment.

On the other is a demand he refuses to accept.

He declares that he prefers prison to what they invite him to.

Did Joseph love prison in itself?

Aḥabbu ilayya mimmā expresses a comparison.

He does not say prison is desirable in every situation for everyone.

He compares two specific alternatives.

When complying would mean wrongdoing, he prefers prison.

The verse describes an ethical choice under pressure.

This is not a general praise of suffering, deprivation, or imprisonment.

Joseph also asks God to turn their plotting away from him.

The subject is preserving himself against pressure toward wrongdoing, rather than an attraction to suffering.

Prison and inner freedom

Joseph may lose freedom of movement while retaining the ability to accept or refuse a wrongful demand.

The story distinguishes external restriction from personal moral choice.

Prison can restrict bodily movement; imprisonment alone does not establish the disappearance of thought, faith, or judgment.

This reflection fits the story, but sajn lexically concerns confinement and restricted freedom.

Al-sijn and al-sajn

The Ḥafṣ reading followed here has al-sijn with kasrah.

This commonly denotes prison as a place.

The transmitted reading of Yaʿqūb has al-sajn with fatḥah in this verse.

That reading can denote the act of imprisonment itself.

The distinction between prison and being imprisoned thus has a morphological and lexical basis.

Both readings retain Joseph's preference for confinement over the demand he resists.

5. Actual imprisonment: detention, prison life, and Joseph's release

Imprisonment despite seeing the signs

Yūsuf 12:35 says:

ثُمَّ بَدَا لَهُمْ مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا رَأَوُا الْآيَاتِ لَيَسْجُنُنَّهُ حَتَّىٰ حِينٍ

thumma badā lahum min baʿdi mā raʾaw al-āyāti la-yasjununnahu ḥattā ḥīn

Yūsuf 12:35

Translation:

“Then, after they had seen the signs, it seemed right to them to imprison him for a time.”

La-yasjununnahu is active and emphatic.

Unlike the passive la-yusjananna in Yūsuf 12:32, it attributes the decision and action to a group.

The pronoun -hu refers to Joseph.

Ḥattā ḥīn indicates a period envisaged for his detention.

It does not specify the exact length.

The decision follows their seeing the signs.

His imprisonment should therefore not be taken as proof of guilt.

The Qur’an distinguishes the truth of the situation from the powerful people's decision to imprison him.

Prison itself is not evidence that the prisoner committed a crime.

Two young men enter prison

In Yūsuf 12:36, two young men enter prison with Joseph.

Al-sijn identifies the place entered.

Prison in this story is an actual place for holding people.

The young men then tell Joseph their dreams and ask him to interpret them.

Sijn itself makes no judgment about their crime, guilt, or innocence.

They are introduced as people present with Joseph in prison.

“My two prison companions”

Joseph addresses them as yā ṣāḥibayi al-sijn in Yūsuf 12:39 and Yūsuf 12:41.

Ṣāḥibay is dual, referring to two companions.

Al-sijn specifies where they are companions.

The expression means “O my two prison companions.”

Sharing prison does not necessarily imply the same accusation, crime, belief, or fate.

In Yūsuf 12:39, Joseph speaks to them about separate lords and The God, the One.

In Yūsuf 12:41, he interprets their dreams.

Their conversations show that imprisonment has not entirely prevented thought, discussion, or sharing knowledge.

This follows from their conduct, not from the lexical meaning of prison.

Remaining in prison for several years

Yūsuf 12:42 describes Joseph remaining in prison for several years.

Fa-labitha fī al-sijni biḍʿa sinīn expresses the continuation of his confinement.

Fī al-sijn identifies where he stayed.

Biḍʿa sinīn gives the duration as several years.

The duration comes from the time expression, not because sijn means permanent imprisonment.

Leaving prison in Yūsuf 12:100

After his circumstances change and he reunites with his family, Joseph recalls in Yūsuf 12:100 his Lord's kindness in bringing him out of prison.

أَخْرَجَنِي مِنَ السِّجْنِ

akhrajanī mina al-sijni

Yūsuf 12:100

He brought me out of prison.

Akhrajanī expresses bringing him out.

Mina al-sijn identifies the place from which he was brought out.

Prison was a situation from which he was eventually freed.

He himself counts release among his Lord's kindnesses.

Preferring prison in Yūsuf 12:33 therefore does not praise confinement itself or devalue freedom.

Under pressure he preferred prison to wrongdoing; later he recognizes release as a divine blessing.

6. Al-masjūnīn: Pharaoh threatens Moses with prison

قَالَ لَئِنِ اتَّخَذْتَ إِلَٰهًا غَيْرِي لَأَجْعَلَنَّكَ مِنَ الْمَسْجُونِينَ

qāla laʾini ittakhadhta ilāhan ghayrī la-ajʿalannaka mina al-masjūnīn

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:29

Translation:

“He said: If you take a deity other than me, I will certainly make you one of the prisoners.”

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:29

Pharaoh threatens Moses here.

Al-masjūnīn is the sound masculine plural of masjūn.

It means people who have been imprisoned.

Pharaoh does not merely threaten to restrict discussion; he threatens to place Moses among the prisoners.

Prison as an instrument of power

Moses speaks in the preceding verses about the Lord of the heavens and earth.

Pharaoh responds with a threat of imprisonment.

Prison can thus become a ruler's instrument for silencing opposition or imposing obedience.

The passage does not present imprisonment as a response to a justly proven crime.

Pharaoh makes the threat in response to Moses's position.

This is a use of prison linked to political power and imposed belief.

That feature should not become the lexical meaning of every S J N derivative.

Masjūn means imprisoned; justice or injustice depends on the grounds and circumstances.

Comparison with the official's wife's threat

In Yūsuf 12:32, she threatens that Joseph will be imprisoned.

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:29, Pharaoh threatens Moses with prison.

Both use restricted freedom to compel compliance with a powerful person's wishes.

The settings differ.

Joseph's story involves personal desire and abuse of social position.

Moses's story presents Pharaoh imposing religious and political authority through prison.

The same instrument can operate in different settings.

The shared meaning is deprivation of freedom.

7. Sijjīn: the record of the wicked in Al-Muṭaffifīn

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:7 says:

كَلَّا إِنَّ كِتَابَ الْفُجَّارِ لَفِي سِجِّينٍ

kallā inna kitāba al-fujjāri la-fī sijjīn

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:7

Translation:

“No indeed! The record of the wicked is surely in Sijjīn.”

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:8 says:

وَمَا أَدْرَاكَ مَا سِجِّينٌ

wa-mā adrāka mā sijjīn

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:8

Translation:

“And what has made you know what Sijjīn is?”

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:9 continues:

كِتَابٌ مَرْقُومٌ

kitābun marqūm

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:9

Translation:

“An inscribed record.”

Sijjīn occurs twice in these verses.

It is structurally associated with al-sijn, prison, but has a different form.

Sijjīn has doubled jīm and the pattern fiʿʿīl.

Lexicons connect the form with intensified confinement and constriction.

The pattern alone cannot determine whether it names a place, book, condition, or something in the unseen world.

Does Sijjīn name a prison?

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:7 places the record of the wicked in Sijjīn.

Fī expresses a relationship between the record and Sijjīn.

A spatial reading takes it as a place containing that record.

This connects with confinement and keeping something in a place.

Some older explanations identify a location in hell, a very low place, or a place beneath the earth.

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:7, Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:8, and Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:9 do not themselves specify geographical coordinates, layers of earth, or physical structure.

The root alone cannot establish a prison of a particular design and location.

Is Sijjīn a book or register of deeds?

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:9 says kitābun marqūm.

Grammatically this can be read as explaining Sijjīn.

That reading directly associates Sijjīn with an inscribed document or register.

The record of the wicked may then be their recorded deeds contained in a larger register.

The relation is linguistically conceivable, as individual records can be kept within an encompassing collection.

The subject concerns accountability and human destiny; its form should not be equated with modern paper books or administrative files.

Does kitābun marqūm refer back to the record of the wicked?

Another grammatical reading exists.

It takes kitābun marqūm as a further explanation or predicate concerning kitāba al-fujjār.

The statement then places their record in Sijjīn and describes that record as inscribed.

Sijjīn may still name a location or condition associated with it.

The difference concerns which earlier expression kitābun marqūm explains.

Both possibilities merit examination of the construction.

Neither authorizes unsupported details about the unseen world.

Sijjīn and ʿIlliyyīn

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:18 says:

كَلَّا إِنَّ كِتَابَ الْأَبْرَارِ لَفِي عِلِّيِّينَ

kallā inna kitāba al-abrāri la-fī ʿilliyyīn

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:18

No indeed! The record of the righteous is surely in ʿIlliyyīn.

The record of the righteous is placed in ʿIlliyyīn.

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:19 follows with a question parallel to the question about Sijjīn.

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:20 repeats kitābun marqūm.

The parallel structure matters.

One side pairs the record of the wicked with Sijjīn.

The other pairs the record of the righteous with ʿIlliyyīn.

Both concern records of deeds.

Sijjīn is semantically associated with confinement and constriction.

ʿIlliyyīn is associated with height and elevation.

This contrast can illuminate the differing standing and destiny of the wicked and righteous.

It does not, without evidence, supply a physical map of where deeds are recorded in the unseen world.

The inscribed record and responsibility for deeds

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:7 concerns the record of the wicked.

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:18 concerns that of the righteous.

This connects with wider Qur’anic passages about recorded deeds.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:13 and Al-Isrāʾ 17:14 describe a record a person will encounter and be asked to read.

Al-Kahf 18:49 places the record before people, leaving out neither small nor great deeds.

Recording deeds is linked to responsibility and reckoning.

Sijjīn itself does not mean sin, reckoning, or punishment.

These concepts arise from the passages together.

In Al-Muṭaffifīn, Sijjīn is a designation associated with confinement and constriction in the context of the record and destiny of the wicked. Whether it names a place, register, or particular condition requires grammatical and contextual examination, without declaring unseen details that the text does not specify.

8. Distinctions from nearby roots

S J N: sajn, and Ḥ B S: ḥabs

Ḥabs means restraining, holding, or preventing movement or release.

Sajn belongs to the same general field.

Both can concern restriction of human freedom.

Ḥabs also has broader uses, including stopping something from moving or holding it for a purpose.

The clear Qur’anic uses of sajn mainly concern imprisoning people and the place of imprisonment.

This does not mean ḥabs never describes prisoners or sajn never applies to anything other than people.

It is a tendency in usage, not an absolute boundary.

S J N: sajn, and ʾ S R: asr

Asr concerns taking someone captive and bringing them under control.

Qur’anic captivity can occur in warfare or conflict.

Sajn also expresses restriction of human freedom.

Captivity and imprisonment are not necessarily the same situation.

Someone may be captured in war before being transferred to prison.

Someone may also be imprisoned without being a prisoner of war.

Joseph exemplifies imprisonment outside wartime captivity.

The families therefore require contextual examination.

S J N: sajn, and W TH Q: wathāq

Wathāq concerns binding and securing.

Muḥammad 47:4 speaks of firmly binding captives, expressed by shadd al-wathāq.

This can restrict freedom of movement.

Binding limbs or using restraints is not the whole meaning of prison.

A person may be imprisoned without bodily restraints.

Tying something with rope or another device need not mean imprisoning a person.

Despite practical overlap, wathāq and sajn have distinct semantic fields.

S J N: sijjīn, and S J L: sijill

The families resemble one another in writing and sound but have different root letters.

S J N ends in nūn.

S J L ends in lām.

Sijjīn concerns the record of the wicked in Al-Muṭaffifīn.

Al-sijill in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:104 concerns a scroll of writings.

Writing in both contexts does not establish a common root or history.

Sound resemblance alone does not prove that sijjīn derives from sijill in the sense of recording.

S J N: sijjīn, and S J L: sijjīl

Sijjīl accompanies stones in Hūd 11:82, Al-Ḥijr 15:74, and Al-Fīl 105:4.

Sijjīn concerns the record of the wicked in Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:7 and Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:8.

Some lexicons discuss proximity or interchange between nūn and lām in these forms.

Resemblance does not establish historical common derivation.

Qur’anic sijjīl concerns stone material or type; sijjīn concerns confinement and the record of the wicked.

One word's usage should not determine the other's meaning.

S J N: sijn, and KH Z N: khazn

Khazn concerns keeping and storing something.

Sajn concerns detention and restricted freedom.

Prisoners may be guarded and supervised, but this does not turn all keeping into imprisonment.

Storing wealth, keeping food, or preserving something in a particular place need not be sajn.

Imprisonment may involve guarding and control, but its central subject is restricted freedom.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

Principal meanings

Sajana: imprisoned or held in confinement.

Yasjunu: imprisons.

Sajn: confinement or imprisonment.

Sijn: prison or a place of confinement.

Sajīn: a prisoner or confined person.

Masjūn: imprisoned or confined.

Masjūnīn: prisoners, those held in confinement.

Sajjān: a prison guard.

Sajjana: imprisoned, in the relevant usage.

Tasjīn: imprisonment.

Sijjīn: in Al-Muṭaffifīn, a designation connected with confinement, constriction, and the record of the wicked; its precise nature requires examination.

Proposed translations of the Qur’anic forms

For yusjana, la-yusjananna, and la-yasjununnahu in Yūsuf, natural renderings are “be imprisoned,” “he will certainly be imprisoned,” and “they would certainly imprison him.”

Al-sijn is naturally “prison”; no new equivalent is required.

Al-masjūnīn in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:29 means “prisoners.”

For Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:7 and Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:8, retaining Sijjīn in the base translation is preferable here.

Rendering it without explanation as hell, an underground prison, a register of deeds, or an imprisoned book would substitute one interpretation for the term itself.

Brief root meaning and verse-specific meanings

For the brief root box:

“Confinement and detention; restriction of freedom to move or leave.”

For Yūsuf 12:25:

“Being imprisoned: the punishment sought by the official's wife for the accused person.”

For Yūsuf 12:32:

“An emphatic threat that Joseph will be imprisoned if he refuses the woman's command.”

For Yūsuf 12:33:

“Prison as the condition Joseph prefers to accepting the wrongful demand.”

For Yūsuf 12:35:

“An emphatic decision to imprison Joseph for an unspecified time.”

For Yūsuf 12:36, Yūsuf 12:39, and Yūsuf 12:41:

“Prison as the place occupied by Joseph and his two companions.”

For Yūsuf 12:42:

“The place where Joseph remained confined for several years.”

For Yūsuf 12:100:

“Prison as the place from which Joseph was brought out through his Lord's kindness.”

For Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:29:

“The prisoners among whom Pharaoh threatens to place Moses.”

For Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:7:

“Sijjīn: a designation associated with confinement and constriction, in which the record of the wicked is said to be.”

For Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:8:

“The Sijjīn of the preceding verse, whose nature is now questioned.”

These boxes should distinguish sijjīn from sajīn, a prisoner. These proposals do not replace existing authored verse explanations.

The brief root meaning should not become a definitive description of Sijjīn's location or physical structure.

10. Conclusion

Arabic S J N concerns confinement, detention, and restricted freedom.

In human usage it concerns imprisoning a person or the place where prisoners are held.

Figurative Arabic uses extend to restraining the tongue or keeping sorrow within.

The poetic examples are transmitted in literary sources, but their pre-Islamic attribution is not equally secure. An uncertain attribution should not establish the precise age of a use.

The root occurs twelve times in the Qur’an.

Most occurrences belong to Joseph's story.

Yūsuf 12:25 and Yūsuf 12:32 present imprisonment as a threat.

In Yūsuf 12:33, Joseph prefers prison to accepting a wrongful demand.

In Yūsuf 12:35, the powerful decide to imprison him after seeing the signs.

Later, prison is the setting shared with his two companions; Yūsuf 12:100 recalls release among his Lord's kindnesses.

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:29, Pharaoh also threatens Moses with prison.

Prison can appear as an instrument of pressure and imposition by the powerful.

S J N itself means neither injustice nor justice.

Confinement is an act; its value and legitimacy depend on its grounds, circumstances, and the actor's responsibility.

Being imprisoned does not by itself prove that someone committed a crime.

Joseph's story illustrates this distinction clearly.

Sijjīn occurs in Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:7 and Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:8.

Associated with confinement and constriction, it appears in the context of the record of the wicked.

The construction permits examination of Sijjīn as a place or a register of deeds.

These verses do not specify its coordinates, physical structure, or complete nature.

The contrast with ʿIlliyyīn helps distinguish the standing and destiny of the wicked and righteous, without itself mapping the unseen world physically.

S J N means confinement, detention, and restriction of a person or thing's freedom to leave, move, or be released.

In direct human usage, this concerns imprisoning people and the place of imprisonment.

In Sijjīn, confinement and constriction are connected with recorded deeds and the destiny of the wicked; explanation of its precise nature should remain within the Qur’anic evidence.

“Prison,” “imprison,” and “prisoners” clearly render the forms in Joseph's story and Pharaoh's threat.

Retaining Sijjīn in Al-Muṭaffifīn, alongside lexical explanation and examination of contextual possibilities, avoids imposing an unproven definitive interpretation.

The central distinction is that restricting freedom is the lexical meaning of confinement, while the prisoner's guilt, the legitimacy of imprisonment, and Sijjīn's nature in the unseen world are separate questions requiring their own evidence and context.
