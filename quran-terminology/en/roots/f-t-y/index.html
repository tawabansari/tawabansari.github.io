The three-consonant root ف ت ي has two recognized semantic branches in Arabic: one concerns youth, freshness of age, and youthful strength; the other concerns answering, clarifying a matter, and offering an opinion or ruling on a question raised.

The first branch includes فَتًى, فَتَاة, فِتْيَة, and فُتُوَّة. The second includes أَفْتَى, يُفْتِي, اِسْتَفْتَى, فُتْيَا, and فَتْوَى.

The root occurs 21 times in 19 Qur’anic verses. Its uses range from the young people of the cave and Moses’ companion to women within systems of service and slavery, dream interpretation, political consultation, and divine answers to legal questions.

The essential point is that the verbs of iftāʾ in Qur’anic language are not confined to religious rulings: they also occur in answering the Egyptian king’s dream and in the Queen of Sheba’s request for advice. The noun fatwā itself does not occur in the Qur’an.

1. The Root in Early Arabic and Pre-Islamic Evidence

Youth, Freshness, and Strength

In early Arabic, فَتًى designated a young person past childhood, in a period of youth and strength. Its feminine form was فَتَاة.

فَتَاء referred to youth and freshness of age. فَتِيّ also described a young human or animal, in contrast to an old animal.

Before any moral judgment, this meaning describes age and a stage of life. A young person may be brave, generous, ignorant, believing, or oppressive. None of these qualities is inherent in the age-related definition of fatā.

Nevertheless, early Arabic language and culture associated youth with strength, readiness for action, courage, and sometimes generosity. This provided a setting for the approving use of fatā for a brave, generous, and chivalrous man.

Evidence from Pre-Islamic Poetry

The pre-Islamic poet Tarafah ibn al-‘Abd says in his Mu‘allaqah:

إِذَا الْقَوْمُ قَالُوا مَنْ فَتًى خِلْتُ أَنَّنِي
عُنِيتُ فَلَمْ أَكْسَلْ وَلَمْ أَتَبَلَّدِ

Idhā al-qawmu qālū man fatan khiltu annanī / ʿunītu fa-lam aksal wa-lam ataballadi.

Translation: “When the people asked, ‘Who is the gallant man?’ I thought they meant me; I neither grew lazy nor stood bewildered.”

Here مَنْ فَتًى is not merely a question about someone’s age. The people seek someone ready for a difficult task, and the poet proudly considers himself the person addressed.

The verse shows that fatā in pre-Islamic language could go beyond age to convey a capable young man ready to help.

It does not prove that every fatā in every sentence is necessarily chivalrous or righteous. That favorable connotation comes from convention and context.

Al-Nabighah al-Dhubyani’s Mu‘allaqah also contains:

وَاحْكُمْ كَحُكْمِ فَتَاةِ الْحَيِّ إِذْ نَظَرَتْ
إِلَى حَمَامٍ سِرَاعٍ وَارِدِ الثَّمَدِ

Wa-uḥkum ka-ḥukmi fatāti al-ḥayyi idh naẓarat / ilā ḥamāmin sirāʿin wāridi al-thamadi.

Translation: “Judge as the young woman of the tribe judged when she looked at swift pigeons approaching the scant water.”

فَتَاةِ الْحَيِّ describes a young woman known for keen observation and accurate calculation. The story accompanying the poem concerns her accurate counting of pigeons.

This shows that fatāh was an established feminine form and did not necessarily designate a female servant or an enslaved woman.

A verse attributed to al-Rabi‘ ibn Dab‘ al-Fazari also reads:

إِذَا عَاشَ الْفَتَى مِائَتَيْنِ عَامًا
فَقَدْ ذَهَبَ اللَّذَاذَةُ وَالْفَتَاءُ

Idhā ʿāsha al-fatā miʾatayni ʿāman / fa-qad dhahaba al-ladhādhatu wa-al-fatāʾu.

Translation: “If a man lives two hundred years, pleasure and youth have departed from him.”

الفتاء here refers to youth. However, the second hemistich has different transmitted readings, and reports about the poet’s lifetime vary. The verse is useful evidence of the recorded word, but its composition should not be declared certainly pre-Islamic without independent investigation.

The verses of Tarafah and al-Nabighah, alongside established Arabic usage, provide clearer support for defining the branch concerning youth.

Answering and Clarifying a Matter

The family’s second branch concerns إفتاء and فُتيا.

أَفْتَاهُ فِي الْأَمْرِ means clarifying a matter for a questioner and answering them.

اِسْتَفْتَاهُ means asking someone for an answer, explanation, or opinion on a matter.

فُتْيَا and فَتْوَى name the answer or opinion given in response to such a question.

This usage is clearly present in the Qur’an. Independent historical evidence is nevertheless needed to establish exactly when and how the answering branch became connected with youth.

Some later lexical explanations suggest that an answer strengthens and clarifies an obscure question, just as a young person has reached strength and capability. This can make a semantic connection intelligible, but does not by itself establish the history of fatwā’s formation.

It is therefore better to recognize two established branches without reducing all their meanings to an unproven historical image.

2. Forms and Derivatives

Important Arabic forms in this family include:

  • فَتًى: a young person; in some uses, a capable or chivalrous young man.
  • فَتَاة: a girl or young woman; in some social contexts, a female servant or enslaved woman.
  • فِتْيَة and فِتْيَان: plurals of fatā; young people.
  • فَتَيَان: two young men.
  • فُتُوَّة: youth and qualities associated with chivalry.
  • أَفْتَى: answered; offered an explanation or opinion on a question.
  • إِفْتَاء: answering a question.
  • اِسْتَفْتَى: requested an answer, explanation, or opinion.
  • فُتْيَا and فَتْوَى: an answer or opinion offered on a question.

The Qur’an contains forms relating to youth and verbal forms of aftā and istaftā. The nouns fatwā, futyā, and muftī themselves do not occur in its text.

The two semantic branches can be defined as follows:

First branch: youth, freshness of age, and associated qualities, with social uses for companions and servants.

Second branch: giving a clarifying answer to a question, or requesting such an answer.

Historical study considers these branches together, but should not impose one branch’s meaning on the other.

3. فتى, فتية, and فتيان in the Qur’an

A. The Young People of the Cave

Al-Kahf 18:13

نَحْنُ نَقُصُّ عَلَيْكَ نَبَأَهُم بِالْحَقِّ ۚ إِنَّهُمْ فِتْيَةٌ آمَنُوا بِرَبِّهِمْ وَزِدْنَاهُمْ هُدًى

Naḥnu naquṣṣu ʿalayka nabaʾahum bi-al-ḥaqqi; innahum fityatun āmanū bi-rabbihim wa-zidnāhum hudā.

Translation: “We recount their news to you in truth. They were young people who believed in their Lord, and We increased them in guidance.”

فِتْيَة is a plural of fatā and here refers to young people who believed.

Al-Kahf 18:10 uses the same designation in its definite form, الْفِتْيَةُ, when the young people take refuge in the cave and ask their Lord for mercy and a right course.

Faith and guidance are not extracted from the word fityah itself. The Qur’an separately states آمَنُوا بِرَبِّهِمْ and وَزِدْنَاهُمْ هُدًى.

Youth describes their age; their faith and steadfastness are qualities the verse attributes to their actions and choices.

Al-Kahf 18:14 also makes their courageous conduct clear: they stand by their belief and declare their Lord’s oneness.

This context may show moral courage and nobility, but does not make fityah mean courageous believers in every Qur’anic occurrence.

B. Abraham as a فَتًى

Al-Anbiya 21:60

قَالُوا سَمِعْنَا فَتًى يَذْكُرُهُمْ يُقَالُ لَهُ إِبْرَاهِيمُ

Qālū samiʿnā fatan yadhkuruhum yuqālu lahu Ibrāhīm.

Translation: “They said: ‘We heard a young man mentioning them, called Abraham.’”

These words are spoken by people seeking to identify the person who had spoken about their idols.

فَتًى designates Abraham here and indicates his youth.

His courage in confronting idol worship is understood from the narrative and his actions, not because fatā alone means brave or truth-seeking.

Since his opponents speak these words, an approving intention of praising his chivalry should not be attributed to them without evidence.

C. Two Young Men in Prison with Joseph

Yusuf 12:36 states:

«وَدَخَلَ مَعَهُ السِّجْنَ فَتَيَانِ»

Wa-dakhala maʿahu al-sijna fatayāni.

Meaning: “And two young men entered the prison with him.”

فَتَيَانِ is the dual of fatā, referring to two young men.

The verse does not call them righteous or wicked simply because they are young. They recount their dreams to Joseph and ask him to explain their interpretation.

The same story also contains the second branch in Yusuf 12:41: تَسْتَفْتِيَانِ.

Thus one narrative uses fatayān for two young men and istiftāʾ for requesting an answer on a matter.

Their proximity illustrates the two branches; it does not prove that asking for an answer derives from the questioner’s youth.

D. Moses’ Young Companion

Al-Kahf 18:60 and al-Kahf 18:62 use لِفَتَاهُ for Moses’ companion.

In the first verse, Moses tells his young companion that he will not stop traveling until he reaches the meeting point of the two seas.

In the second, after passing that place, he asks the same young man to bring their food.

فَتَاهُ here refers to a young man accompanying Moses and helping him on the journey.

“His young companion” is therefore a natural rendering.

Some later interpretations identify this young man by name, but the two verses do not name him. Nor can the word fatā alone establish that he was Moses’ slave.

E. Joseph’s Young Men and Servants

Yusuf 12:62 states:

«وَقَالَ لِفِتْيَانِهِ اجْعَلُوا بِضَاعَتَهُمْ فِي رِحَالِهِمْ»

Wa-qāla li-fityānihi ijʿalū biḍāʿatahum fī riḥālihim.

Meaning: “And he told his young attendants: ‘Put their merchandise in their baggage.’”

فِتْيَانِهِ refers here to people carrying out Joseph’s administrative instructions.

The context indicates servants or staff. Their social role is established by their relationship to Joseph and the work they perform.

The lexical meaning of fityān still concerns young people; it does not independently establish their occupation or legal status.

F. فَتَاهَا in the Story of the Egyptian Dignitary’s Wife

In Yusuf 12:30, the women of the city say of the dignitary’s wife:

«امْرَأَتُ الْعَزِيزِ تُرَاوِدُ فَتَاهَا عَنْ نَفْسِهِ»

Imraʾatu al-ʿazīzi turāwidu fatāhā ʿan nafsihi.

Meaning: “The dignitary’s wife is trying to seduce the young man of her household.”

فَتَاهَا refers to Joseph in this sentence.

In the narrative, Joseph had previously been sold and brought into the dignitary’s household. The word therefore occurs in a setting involving ownership and service.

This does not make fatā mean slave throughout Arabic. The same word describes Abraham in al-Anbiya 21:60 and Moses’ companion in al-Kahf 18:60.

4. فَتَيَات: Young Women and Their Social Status

فَتَيَات is the feminine plural of fatāh.

This form occurs twice in the Qur’an, both times referring to women within social and legal structures of dependence and slavery.

Al-Nisa 4:25

The verse states:

«فَمِنْ مَا مَلَكَتْ أَيْمَانُكُمْ مِنْ فَتَيَاتِكُمُ الْمُؤْمِنَاتِ»

Fa-min mā malakat aymānukum min fatayātikumu al-muʾmināti.

Meaning: “Then from among your believing young women whom you own…”

مَا مَلَكَتْ أَيْمَانُكُمْ establishes these women’s social and legal status.

فَتَيَاتِكُمُ does not independently mean enslaved women, but in this construction refers to those women under ownership.

The verse then discusses marrying them, their families’ or guardians’ permission, paying their marriage dues, and observing what is right.

Here fatāh is not merely an age designation, but a term also used in that social setting for young female servants or enslaved women.

Al-Nur 24:33

The verse states:

«وَلَا تُكْرِهُوا فَتَيَاتِكُمْ عَلَى الْبِغَاءِ إِنْ أَرَدْنَ تَحَصُّنًا»

Wa-lā tukrihū fatayātikum ʿalā al-bighāʾi in aradna taḥaṣṣunan.

Meaning: “And do not compel your young women under your control into prostitution if they desire chastity.”

The context concerns sexual exploitation and coercion of women under others’ control.

Fatayātikum here refers to women whom those possessing power and control might force into prostitution for profit.

إِنْ أَرَدْنَ تَحَصُّنًا highlights women who desire chastity. It should not be inverted into permission to compel prostitution under other circumstances.

Again, the social and legal meaning comes from the whole verse and the relationships described, not from slavery or exploitation being inherent in ف ت ي.

Translation should therefore distinguish fatāh’s lexical meaning from the woman’s social position. In a slavery context, “enslaved woman” or “young woman under ownership” can clarify the contextual sense; in al-Nabighah’s verse, “young woman” is appropriate.

5. إفتاء in the Qur’an: Answering and Clarifying

The second branch appears in two verbal forms:

أَفْتَى, form IV, means giving an answer or explanation about a question.

اِسْتَفْتَى, form X, means requesting an answer, explanation, or opinion.

The subject of the question is crucial. An answer may concern law, dream interpretation, a political decision, or a disputed claim.

A. A Divine Answer Concerning Women

Al-Nisa 4:127 states:

«وَيَسْتَفْتُونَكَ فِي النِّسَاءِ قُلِ اللَّهُ يُفْتِيكُمْ فِيهِنَّ»

Wa-yastaftūnaka fī al-nisāʾi quli Allāhu yuftīkum fīhinna.

Meaning: “And they ask you concerning women. Say: ‘The God answers you concerning them.’”

People here ask Muhammad about a matter concerning women.

The response begins with قُلِ اللَّهُ يُفْتِيكُمْ.

This construction presents Muhammad as the recipient of the question and conveyor of the divine answer. Its content is stated by The God.

The continuation concerns orphaned women, their rights, vulnerable children, and establishing justice.

Here يُفْتِيكُمْ is a clarifying answer concerning people’s actual rights and responsibilities, not merely a personal opinion.

B. A Divine Answer Concerning Kalalah

Al-Nisa 4:176 again states:

«يَسْتَفْتُونَكَ قُلِ اللَّهُ يُفْتِيكُمْ فِي الْكَلَالَةِ»

Yastaftūnaka quli Allāhu yuftīkum fī al-kalālati.

Meaning: “They ask you for an answer. Say: ‘The God answers you concerning kalalah.’”

The Qur’an then sets out inheritance provisions for the situation described.

The end of the same verse says:

«يُبَيِّنُ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ أَنْ تَضِلُّوا»

Yubayyinu Allāhu lakum an taḍillū.

The connection between yuftīkum and yubayyinu matters.

Here iftāʾ directly concerns clarifying the question and preventing error in understanding and action.

This does not confine every Qur’anic use of iftāʾ to inheritance or religious law. Its uses in Yusuf and al-Naml rule out that restriction.

C. Interpreting the Egyptian King’s Dream

In Yusuf 12:43, after recounting his dream, the Egyptian king says:

«يَا أَيُّهَا الْمَلَأُ أَفْتُونِي فِي رُؤْيَايَ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ لِلرُّؤْيَا تَعْبُرُونَ»

Yā ayyuhā al-malaʾu aftūnī fī ruʾyāya in kuntum li-al-ruʾyā taʿburūna.

Meaning: “O dignitaries, answer me concerning my dream, if you can interpret dreams.”

This use is decisive in understanding Qur’anic iftāʾ.

The king does not ask his courtiers for a ruling on the permitted and forbidden. He wants the meaning of his dream explained.

أَفْتُونِي here means answering and clarifying an obscure matter.

تَعْبُرُونَ belongs to another root and concerns interpreting dreams.

Iftāʾ describes requesting and giving an answer, while dream interpretation is the subject and specific skill needed to answer.

In Yusuf 12:46, someone also comes to Joseph and says:

«يُوسُفُ أَيُّهَا الصِّدِّيقُ أَفْتِنَا»

Yūsufu ayyuhā al-ṣiddīqu aftinā.

Meaning: “Joseph, O truthful one, answer us.”

He then recounts the king’s dream.

This second example confirms the same meaning of seeking an answer about a dream.

In Yusuf 12:41, after explaining the two prisoners’ destinies, Joseph says:

«قُضِيَ الْأَمْرُ الَّذِي فِيهِ تَسْتَفْتِيَانِ»

Quḍiya al-amru alladhī fīhi tastaftiyāni.

Meaning: “The matter about which you sought an answer has been decided.”

تَسْتَفْتِيَانِ is a dual imperfect verb, referring to those two prisoners.

These three uses show that Qur’anic iftāʾ and istiftāʾ can concern nonlegal subjects.

D. The Queen of Sheba’s Request for Political Advice

Al-Naml 27:32

قَالَتْ يَا أَيُّهَا الْمَلَأُ أَفْتُونِي فِي أَمْرِي مَا كُنْتُ قَاطِعَةً أَمْرًا حَتَّىٰ تَشْهَدُونِ

Qālat yā ayyuhā al-malaʾu aftūnī fī amrī mā kuntu qāṭiʿatan amran ḥattā tashhadūni.

Translation: “She said: ‘O dignitaries, give me your opinion about my affair. I do not decide a matter until you are present.’”

The Queen of Sheba speaks here, consulting her people’s dignitaries after receiving Solomon’s letter.

أَفْتُونِي فِي أَمْرِي requests advice and guidance about a political decision.

In the next verse, al-Naml 27:33, the dignitaries mention their strength and power, but leave the final decision to the queen.

Iftāʾ here means offering advice, not issuing a binding command by those holding power.

This shows that Qur’anic iftāʾ is broader than the technical juristic fatwa of later periods.

6. Istiftāʾ as Inquiry, Examination, and Challenge

Not every use of istiftāʾ seeks an answer from someone whom the questioner regards as more knowledgeable.

Sometimes a question exposes a claim’s falsehood or prompts the addressee to think.

A. A Question about Creation

Al-Saffat 37:11

فَاسْتَفْتِهِمْ أَهُمْ أَشَدُّ خَلْقًا أَمْ مَنْ خَلَقْنَا ۚ إِنَّا خَلَقْنَاهُمْ مِنْ طِينٍ لَازِبٍ

Fa-istaftihim a-hum ashaddu khalqan am man khalaqnā; innā khalaqnāhum min ṭīnin lāzibin.

Translation: “Then ask them: ‘Are they stronger in creation, or those We have created?’ We created them from sticky clay.”

فَاسْتَفْتِهِمْ here is a command addressed to Muhammad.

Those questioned are people being engaged in discussion about creation and resurrection.

The point is not that Muhammad needs their knowledge to understand creation. The question confronts them with a subject they must consider and answer.

Istiftāʾ can therefore be a question used in reasoning.

B. A Question about Attributing Daughters to The God

Al-Saffat 37:149

فَاسْتَفْتِهِمْ أَلِرَبِّكَ الْبَنَاتُ وَلَهُمُ الْبَنُونَ

Fa-istaftihim a-li-rabbika al-banātu wa-lahumu al-banūna.

Translation: “Then ask them: ‘Does your Lord have daughters while they have sons?’”

This question challenges the addressees’ claim.

Here too, fastaftihim does not request a religious fatwa from them; it exposes an inconsistency in their belief.

Thus the occurrence of istiftāʾ does not necessarily mean the questioner accepts the other party’s scholarly or religious authority.

C. The Prohibition on Asking about the People of the Cave

In al-Kahf 18:22, after reporting disagreements about the number of the people of the cave, Muhammad is told:

«وَلَا تَسْتَفْتِ فِيهِمْ مِنْهُمْ أَحَدًا»

Wa-lā tastafti fīhim minhum aḥadan.

Meaning: “And do not ask any of them for an answer concerning them.”

Istiftāʾ here means seeking an explanation or opinion about the people of the cave.

The context shows disagreement over their number and statements based on conjecture.

The same verse says their Lord knows their number best.

The prohibition here therefore concerns a particular subject about which human answers are unreliable or unnecessary.

It cannot establish that asking knowledgeable people about every subject is forbidden, just as other occurrences of istiftāʾ cannot make every answer valid and binding.

An answer’s value depends on its correctness, knowledge, and support, not merely on calling it a fatwa.

7. Qur’anic Usage and the Later Technical Meaning of Fatwa

In later periods, fatwa became an established term in jurisprudence: a jurist’s answer to a question about a religious ruling.

This relates to one Qur’anic branch but does not encompass its entire meaning.

In the Qur’an, requests for answers can concern inheritance, women’s rights, dreams, political decisions, or opponents’ claims.

Answering a question does not by itself mean legislating or making law.

In al-Nisa, when a divine ruling is sought, the answer is attributed to The God: قُلِ اللَّهُ يُفْتِيكُمْ.

In al-Naml, however, the Queen of Sheba asks her advisers for an opinion; in Yusuf, the Egyptian king asks for dream interpretation.

These differences show that an answer’s authority is not derived from the verb aftā itself.

An answer may come from The God, a knowledgeable person may explain a matter, or someone may offer advice. Its correctness and authority must be assessed through its subject, speaker, and evidence.

Religious discussion should distinguish three things: stating a ruling present in the Qur’an, human inference from it, and making a new ruling and attributing it to The God.

The name fatwa alone does not distinguish these three.

In its linguistic sense, fatwa is a means of giving an answer; its name alone does not guarantee correctness, justice, or legislative authority.

8. Morphology and Syntax

Fatā and Its Plurals

فَتًى is a noun whose final radical appears as an alif maqsurah in its usual form.

Its feminine is فَتَاة, with the feminine plural فَتَيَات.

فِتْيَة and فِتْيَان are two established plurals of fatā.

Both occur in the Qur’an: فِتْيَة describes the people of the cave, while فِتْيَان appears in لِفِتْيَانِهِ for Joseph’s young attendants in Yusuf 12:62.

فَتَيَانِ in Yusuf 12:36 is dual and refers to two young men.

The plural fityān and dual fatayān should therefore not be confused without considering vocalization, spelling, and sentence structure.

Forms IV and X

أَفْتَى is form IV and in Qur’anic usage expresses answering or clarifying a matter.

اِسْتَفْتَى is form X; its Qur’anic uses mainly concern requesting an answer or opinion.

The distinction is clear in al-Nisa 4:127 and al-Nisa 4:176:

يَسْتَفْتُونَكَ — they ask you for an answer.

يُفْتِيكُمْ — He answers you.

The morphological difference does not make every answer authoritative, or every question an acceptance of the respondent’s authority.

In al-Saffat 37:11 and al-Saffat 37:149, istiftāʾ takes the form of questions used in reasoning.

In al-Naml 27:32, iftāʾ requests an opinion on a political decision.

Morphology indicates the action’s general direction; context identifies the kind of question and answer.

9. Comparison with Related Roots

ف ت ي and ف ت أ

These roots may be confused because their spelling is similar.

ف ت ي concerns youth and answering.

ف ت أ in Yusuf 12:85, تَفْتَؤُا تَذْكُرُ يُوسُفَ, concerns not ceasing and continuing an activity in a construction with understood negation.

Similar initial consonants do not make them the same root.

ف ت ي and غ ل م

Ghulām in Qur’anic language can mean a boy, adolescent, or young man.

Yusuf 12:19 uses هَٰذَا غُلَامٌ of Joseph.

The same narrative calls him فَتَاهَا in Yusuf 12:30.

Both can designate a young person, but their contexts differ. Ghulām sometimes emphasizes a younger age, while fatā may name a young man with strength and a social role.

This difference should not be turned into an exact, exceptionless age boundary.

ف ت ي and س أ ل

Suʾāl broadly means requesting, asking, or seeking something.

Istiftāʾ is a more specific inquiry requesting an answer or opinion on a matter.

The boundary is not absolute: an ordinary question may seek an explanation, and istiftāʾ may approach questioning used in reasoning.

The subject, purpose, and sentence structure clarify the main difference.

ف ت ي and ب ي ن

Bayān concerns becoming clear and making something clear.

In al-Nisa 4:176, يُفْتِيكُمْ occurs at the answer’s beginning and يُبَيِّنُ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ at the verse’s end.

Their proximity shows how iftāʾ here relates to clarification.

Not every clarification is a fatwa: a truth can be explained without anyone having asked about it.

Istiftāʾ and iftāʾ foreground the relationship between question and answer.

ف ت ي and ف ت ح

ف ت ح concerns opening and, in some Qur’anic uses, judgment and settlement.

The iftāʾ branch of ف ت ي focuses more on answering and clarifying.

An answer may help settle a dispute, but giving a fatwa and making a final judgment are not identical concepts.

The divine epithet al-Fattāḥ does not make muftī and Fattāḥ derivatives of the same root.

10. Challenges in Understanding and Translation

The first challenge is confining fatā to moral chivalry.

That sense is established in some early usage but does not explain every Qur’anic occurrence. The prisoners in Yusuf 12:36, Moses’ companion in al-Kahf 18:60, and fatayāt in al-Nisa 4:25 each have a different context.

The second challenge is equating every form of fatā and fatāh with an enslaved person.

This meaning fits some social settings, but the lexical form alone does not establish the status. Accompanying expressions and narrative establish the relationship.

The third challenge is restricting iftāʾ to juristic fatwas.

Yusuf and al-Naml show that the same verb can express answers about dreams and political consultation.

The fourth challenge is equating every answer with an authoritative ruling. The Qur’an mentions divine answers and requests for human opinions. In the king’s dream story, his courtiers cannot even provide the answer he needs.

Requesting iftāʾ therefore proves neither the respondent’s competence nor the answer’s correctness.

Conversely, the verb’s broad usage should not obscure its clearly legal and legislative context in al-Nisa 4:127 and al-Nisa 4:176. Those questions truly concern people’s rights and rulings.

The root should retain its actual range, while each derivative’s meaning is determined in context.

11. Translation Proposals for Forqan

In the youth branch, فَتًى generally means “a young person” or “a young man,” and فَتَاة means “a girl” or “a young woman.”

فِتْيَة and فِتْيَان can mean “young people,” “young companions,” or “young attendants,” according to context.

In al-Anbiya 21:60, “a young man called Abraham” is suitable.

In al-Kahf 18:13, “they were young people” preserves the direct meaning.

In al-Kahf 18:60 and al-Kahf 18:62, “Moses’ young companion” is more accurate than asserting enslavement without evidence.

In Yusuf 12:62, “his young attendants” clarifies the contextual meaning.

For فَتَيَات in al-Nisa 4:25 and al-Nur 24:33, the social structure makes “young women under ownership” or “enslaved women” suitable. The explanation should make clear that this restriction comes from context, not a fixed root meaning.

For the answering branch, proposed renderings are:

أَفْتَى: answered, explained, offered an opinion, or stated a ruling.

اِسْتَفْتَى: requested an answer, explanation, or opinion.

أَفْتُونِي in Yusuf 12:43: “interpret my dream for me” or “answer me concerning my dream.”

أَفْتِنَا in Yusuf 12:46: “explain to us” or “answer us.”

أَفْتُونِي in al-Naml 27:32: “give me your opinion about my affair.”

يَسْتَفْتُونَكَ in al-Nisa 4:127 and al-Nisa 4:176: “they ask you for an answer.”

يُفْتِيكُمْ in those verses: “He answers you” or “He clarifies the ruling for you.”

فَاسْتَفْتِهِمْ in al-Saffat 37:11 and al-Saffat 37:149: “then ask them.”

وَلَا تَسْتَفْتِ in al-Kahf 18:22: “do not seek an answer or opinion.”

In the base translation, “fatwa” is suitable only if readers understand its broad sense of answering. In contemporary Persian, the word usually suggests a religious or juristic ruling; for dreams, consultation, and questions used in reasoning, “answer,” “explanation,” and “opinion” are therefore more natural and accurate.

12. Conclusion

ف ت ي has two established Arabic branches: youth and fresh strength, and answering and clarifying a matter.

In the first, fatā and fatāh designate a young person and young woman. Some social uses apply them to companions, servants, and people under ownership. Chivalry, courage, and generosity can accompany futuwwah, but are not obligatory meanings of every derivative.

In the second, aftā gives an answer and istaftā requests one. Qur’anic uses show that the subject can be legal, religious, political, dream interpretation, or a question challenging a claim.

A final definition must preserve both branches: youth and its associated qualities; and answering or requesting an answer to a matter needing explanation, an opinion, or clarification.

Neither can every use be reduced to youth, nor can broad iftāʾ be confined to later juristic fatwas. Translation must follow the actual form, sentence structure, and context of each verse.
