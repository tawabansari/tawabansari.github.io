A study of naqb in early Arabic and the Qur’an: from piercing walls and traversing lands to investigating affairs and a naqīb’s responsibility among people

The three-letter root N Q B concerns piercing, opening a way, passing through something, and searching to reach what is inaccessible or not apparent. Its forms describe piercing a wall, opening a mountain passage, or travelling through different lands.

The root also concerns knowledge and social responsibility. Someone investigating hidden matters or a subject’s details may be called naqqāb. In Arabic social usage, naqīb denotes someone familiar with a group’s affairs who learns about its circumstances and holds responsibility for, or represents, its members.

The root occurs three times in the Qur’an, in three different forms: naqīban in Al-Māʾidah 5:12, naqban in Al-Kahf 18:97, and fa-naqqabū in Qāf 50:36.

These illustrate three familiar aspects of the family: responsibility for and knowledge of a group, breaching a barrier, and traversing and searching through lands.

They should not all be reduced indiscriminately to “penetrating” or “discovering truth.” Piercing a wall, travelling through lands, and attending to a community’s affairs are different activities. Their semantic relationship can be examined, but each derivative’s precise meaning follows from attested usage and the verse’s context.

1. Forms and Semantic Core

Important Arabic forms in this family include:

  • نَقَبَ: To pierce, breach, or create a way through something; in some uses, to pass through a place.
  • نَقْب: A hole, breach, or passage through something; also the act of piercing.
  • نَقْب and مَنْقَب: A passage or route, especially through mountains.
  • نَقَّبَ: To travel through a place, search, or investigate something.
  • تَنْقِيب: Searching, investigation, or exploration; in physical uses, piercing and opening a way.
  • نَقِيب: A supervisor, representative, or person responsible for a group, familiar with its affairs and charged with following them up.
  • نُقَبَاء: The plural of naqīb.
  • نِقَابَة: The responsibility or position of a naqīb among a group.
  • نَقَّاب، نِقَاب and مِنْقَب: A knowledgeable investigator; minqab also denotes a piercing implement.
  • مَنْقَبَة and مَنَاقِب: In geographical usage, a passage; in another branch, praiseworthy deeds or qualities.
  • نَقِبَ: In some uses, to become thin or damaged, especially the sole of a person’s foot or a camel’s footpad.
  • نَقْبَة and نُقْبَة: Forms associated with holes and skin damage; nuqbah, with initial u, also names a garment.
  • نِقَاب: A covering that conceals part of the face.

These forms are attested in Arabic, but only naqīb, naqb, and naqqabū occur in the Qur’an.

Nuqabāʾ, tanqīb, niqābah, manāqib, and niqāb are attested Arabic derivatives but do not themselves occur in the Qur’an.

The semantic core: opening a way through something

One lexical analysis associates N Q B with opening something.

Piercing a wall is a clear example.

نَقَبَ الْحَائِطَ

naqaba al-ḥāʾiṭa

Translation: “He pierced the wall.”

A person creates a gap or way through a previously closed or continuous object.

Naqb can also name a route through mountains or through an obstacle.

These uses directly concern opening a way and passing through something.

In investigation, someone moves beyond initial awareness and examines details. Arabic expresses this through tanqīb ʿan al-shayʾ.

The relationship between physical piercing and investigating a subject should not become a certain claim about the word’s historical origin.

Both can be explained as gaining access to something not initially within direct reach. This conceptual explanation does not establish that pre-Qur’anic Arabic speakers derived every use from the image of piercing a wall.

Naqīb also has an established social meaning. It does not simply mean someone who makes holes in people’s affairs or penetrates their inner selves. Those are neither natural equivalents nor precise definitions of the role.

Two clear fields should therefore be distinguished: opening a way and passing through, and knowing and attending to affairs. Their relationship is intelligible, but each derivative retains its particular meaning.

2. N Q B in Early Arabic

Piercing and creating a passage

Clear uses include piercing walls, skin, and other physical objects.

Arabic says:

نَقَبَ الْجِدَارَ

naqaba al-jidāra

Translation: “He made a breach in the wall.”

Likewise:

نَقَبَ فِي الْجَبَلِ

naqaba fī al-jabali

Translation: “He opened a passage or breach in the mountain.”

Naqb may denote the act of piercing or the hole and passage it produces.

The meanings are close but not identical: one names an action, the other its result or location.

Manqab also denotes a mountain passage: a place through which one can travel among mountains.

Such a passage need not be a human-built tunnel. A natural gap or mountain route may also bear this name.

Not every use of naqb and manqab should therefore be restricted to intentional excavation or tunnel construction.

Pierced skin and worn feet

Lexical accounts also use N Q B for damage or perforation of the foot’s covering.

For example:

نَقِبَ خُفُّ الْبَعِيرِ

naqiba khuffu al-baʿīri

Translation: “The camel’s footpad became thin and damaged.”

Walking or the animal’s physical condition may cause its natural footpad to thin and wear away.

Expressions concerning human feet also describe thin skin, blisters, or walking injuries.

Naqbah can likewise concern certain skin wounds or injuries. Early pastoral accounts attest it for the onset of some skin diseases in camels.

The family is therefore not confined to making holes with tools. Injury, wear, or disease may compromise a bodily covering.

Not every use nevertheless means disease or deterioration. Piercing a wall may create a useful passage, while perforated skin may be harmful. Context determines the act’s value and outcome.

Traversing lands

Early Arabic uses naqqaba fī al-bilād for travelling through different lands, following varied routes, and reaching distant regions.

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays reads:

وَقَدْ نَقَّبْتُ فِي الْآفَاقِ حَتَّىٰ
رَضِيتُ مِنَ السَّلَامَةِ بِالْإِيَابِ

wa-qad naqqabtu fī al-āfāqi ḥattā / raḍītu mina al-salāmati bi-al-iyābi

Translation: “I travelled far through the horizons, until I was content simply to return safely.”

Naqqabtu here concerns movement and travel through different regions.

The poet describes journeys and travel experience, rather than necessarily digging a hole in the ground.

This helps explain fa-naqqabū fī al-bilād in Qāf: both constructions join the verb with fī and a geographical expression.

The wording varies. Some versions begin laqad naqqabtu, while the second line is also transmitted as mina al-ghanīmati bi-al-iyāb.

Early lexical and exegetical accounts attribute the verse to Imruʾ al-Qays, but any definite claim about its original wording must account for these variants.

The transmitted versions establish the use of naqqaba fī al-āfāq for extensive travel through different regions.

Investigation and knowledge of affairs

Another branch concerns careful searching and investigation.

Naqqaba ʿan al-akhbār means seeking out and examining reports.

The object may be something not apparent or requiring effort to understand.

Naqqāb also describes someone knowledgeable, perceptive, and skilled in understanding details.

The pre-Islamic poet Aws ibn Ḥajar praises a person as follows:

نَجِيحٌ مَلِيحٌ أَخُو مَأْقِطٍ
نِقَابٌ يُحَدِّثُ بِالْغَائِبِ

najīḥun malīḥun akhū maʾqiṭin / niqābun yuḥaddithu bi-al-ghāʾibi

Translation: “Successful, sound in judgment, seasoned in difficult encounters: a perceptive man who speaks of matters out of sight.”

Niqāb here, with initial i and an undoubled qāf, describes a knowledgeable investigator. Naqqāb is also attested for knowing and examining affairs. The poem’s actual form should therefore be distinguished from the form used to explain it.

This need not imply knowledge of the unseen in a theological sense. The poet praises someone whose experience, examination, and perceptiveness reveal things unclear to others.

Early literary transmission attributes the verse to Aws ibn Ḥajar. Some words vary, including najīḥ malīḥ versus karīm jawād, and yuḥaddithu versus yukhbiru.

Despite these differences, niqāb clearly describes someone knowledgeable and perceptive in difficult affairs.

The example helps explain the connection between naqīb and familiarity with a group’s affairs, without establishing that every naqīb necessarily knows everything hidden about its members.

Naqīb: a group’s representative, supervisor, or responsible person

Naqību al-qawm denotes someone with an acknowledged position among a group who attends to its affairs.

Lexical explanations use terms such as ʿarīf, amīn, kafīl, shāhid, and ḍamīn.

They emphasize different aspects of responsibility.

ʿArīf denotes someone familiar with the group’s members and circumstances.

Amīn describes someone trustworthy.

Kafīl undertakes responsibility or a guarantee.

Shāhid and ḍamīn emphasize the naqīb’s role as witness or responsible party concerning the group’s commitments.

These terms are not fully synonymous. A naqīb in every historical setting need not simultaneously have all a guarantor’s legal duties, a ruler’s powers, and a representative’s responsibilities.

Familiar usage denotes someone who knows the community’s affairs and holds a position for representing, supervising, or attending to it.

This relates intelligibly to investigation and knowledge, but naqīb is itself an established social title.

Manāqib and niqāb: other branches

Manqabah can denote a praiseworthy deed or quality, with the plural manāqib.

Persian consequently uses manāqib for a person’s virtues or good qualities.

One etymological explanation connects this use with a good quality becoming manifest and recognized.

The semantic connection is interesting without proving a certain historical origin. Manqabah as a virtue also differs from manqabah as a mountain passage, despite their placement in one lexical family.

Niqāb as a face covering is also familiar in Arabic. Lexical accounts describe different ways of covering the face and how much of the eyes remains visible.

Its presence in N Q B does not establish that every use derives from piercing a wall. Etymological analyses also express uncertainty over how the face covering relates to the proposed semantic core.

Similarity between naqb and niqāb should therefore generate neither a new dress ruling nor a new Qur’anic interpretation. Niqāb itself does not occur in the Qur’an.

3. Naqīb in the Qur’an: Responsibility among the Children of Israel

وَلَقَدْ أَخَذَ اللَّهُ مِيثَاقَ بَنِي إِسْرَائِيلَ وَبَعَثْنَا مِنْهُمُ اثْنَيْ عَشَرَ نَقِيبًا

wa-laqad akhadha Allāhu mīthāqa banī isrāʾīla wa-baʿathnā minhum ithnay ʿashara naqīban

Translation: “Indeed, The God took the covenant of the Children of Israel, and We raised twelve naqībs from among them.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:12

This is the Qur’an’s only use of naqīb.

It places two matters together: The God taking the Children of Israel’s covenant, and twelve naqībs raised from among them.

The meaning of naqīb here

Familiar Arabic usage indicates someone holding social responsibility among the people, familiar with the group’s affairs and potentially involved in following up its covenant and commitments.

Early exegetical accounts variously describe the twelve as representatives of Israelite groups or as responsible for, or guaranteeing, their fulfilment of the covenant.

These explanations fit the term’s familiar semantic field.

The verse does not list each naqīb’s duties in detail. The word alone therefore establishes neither unlimited political power, legislative authority, nor spiritual infallibility.

Baʿathnā minhum shows that they were raised from among the Children of Israel. The verb matters for their appointment, but their precise office follows from naqīb and the context.

Why twelve naqībs?

The verse explicitly numbers them as twelve.

The number should retain its actual numerical value rather than become “many” or “a complete group” without evidence.

Twelve naqībs in this event do not independently establish a universal rule for the number of religious or political leaders in other periods.

Twelve is a feature of this particular account; applying it to later institutions or individuals requires independent evidence.

Naqīb should also not be equated merely by its use with rasūl, nabī, or imām. These words have different Qur’anic uses and roles.

The naqībs and the covenant

The verse continues:

وَقَالَ اللَّهُ إِنِّي مَعَكُمْ لَئِنْ أَقَمْتُمُ الصَّلَاةَ وَآتَيْتُمُ الزَّكَاةَ وَآمَنتُم بِرُسُلِي وَعَزَّرْتُمُوهُمْ وَأَقْرَضْتُمُ اللَّهَ قَرْضًا حَسَنًا لَّأُكَفِّرَنَّ عَنكُمْ سَيِّئَاتِكُمْ

wa-qāla Allāhu innī maʿakum la-in aqamtumu al-ṣalāta wa-ātaytumu al-zakāta wa-āmantum bi-rusulī wa-ʿazzartumūhum wa-aqraḍtumu Allāha qarḍan ḥasanan la-ukaffiranna ʿankum sayyiʾātikum

Translation: “The God said: I am with you. If you establish ṣalāh, give zakāh, believe in My messengers and support them, and lend The God a goodly loan, I will surely cover your misdeeds.”

The passage principally concerns the Children of Israel’s commitment before The God.

Naqībs appear at its opening, but fidelity to the covenant is not confined to their title or position.

The verse closes with:

فَمَن كَفَرَ بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ مِنكُمْ فَقَدْ ضَلَّ سَوَاءَ السَّبِيلِ

fa-man kafara baʿda dhālika minkum fa-qad ḍalla sawāʾa al-sabīli

Translation: “Whoever among you disbelieves after that has strayed from the right way.”

Having naqībs and a social structure does not by itself guarantee the people’s fidelity.

The naqīb’s responsibility makes sense within the covenant, but the title alone does not prove that every decision by its holder is correct.

Baʿathnā also does not mean that every later person called a naqīb or representative is necessarily chosen by The God.

This verse concerns a particular event among the Children of Israel.

4. Naqb in the Qur’an: Breaching Dhū al-Qarnayn’s Barrier

فَمَا اسْطَاعُوا أَنْ يَظْهَرُوهُ وَمَا اسْتَطَاعُوا لَهُ نَقْبًا

fa-mā isṭāʿū an yaẓharūhu wa-mā istaṭāʿū lahu naqban

Translation: “They could neither climb over it nor make a breach in it.”

Al-Kahf 18:97

The verse continues the account of Dhū al-Qarnayn constructing a barrier between two mountain sides.

The preceding verses describe people asking him to build a barrier between themselves and Gog and Magog.

Using pieces of iron and molten metal, he creates a structure difficult to cross.

The Qur’an then states two inabilities: they could neither pass over it nor breach it.

The precise meaning of نَقْبًا

Naqban concerns a hole or breach made through a barrier to enable passage.

This direct meaning agrees with the familiar naqaba al-ḥāʾiṭ.

If a wall obstructs passage, someone may climb over it or make a hole through it.

The verse presents both routes and the inability to use either.

Naqb here is therefore not merely spiritual penetration, influence, or investigation, but a breach in a physical barrier.

أَنْ يَظْهَرُوهُ and نَقْبًا

Yaẓharūhu here concerns climbing and passing over the barrier.

Naqban instead concerns opening a way through it.

The distinction separates two methods of passage.

Naqb does not always mean passing under a structure. Arabic permits a naqb through a wall, mountain, or another object; its direction depends on the obstacle’s position and shape.

The inability to breach is clear here, but the precise excavation route, location of the attempt, and tools are unspecified.

Does naqb mean destroying the barrier?

No. Making a breach need not destroy a whole structure.

A hole may be made in a wall while much of it remains standing.

Conversely, a barrier may be entirely destroyed without that act being expressed through naqb.

Al-Kahf 18:97 describes the inability to breach it.

Al-Kahf 18:98 states:

قَالَ هَٰذَا رَحْمَةٌ مِّن رَّبِّي فَإِذَا جَاءَ وَعْدُ رَبِّي جَعَلَهُ دَكَّاءَ وَكَانَ وَعْدُ رَبِّي حَقًّا

qāla hādhā raḥmatun min rabbī fa-idhā jāʾa waʿdu rabbī jaʿalahu dakkāʾa wa-kāna waʿdu rabbī ḥaqqan

Translation: “He said: This is a mercy from my Lord. When my Lord’s promise comes, He will make it level and crushed; and my Lord’s promise is true.”

Al-Kahf 18:98

The verse concerns the barrier’s eventual fate.

Dakkāʾ is from another root; crushing or levelling should not become part of naqb’s meaning.

The inability to breach at the stated time also does not establish that the barrier remains impassable forever under all conditions. The following verse itself describes a future change.

Can naqb identify the barrier’s material or location?

No. Naqb states only the act or result of breaching.

It cannot establish the barrier’s geographical location, dimensions, mountain height, structural thickness, or precise construction technology.

The preceding verses do state some features, such as iron and molten metal.

Details left unspecified should not be assigned to naqb on the basis of present historical or technical knowledge.

This particularly matters in studying Dhū al-Qarnayn and Gog and Magog: identifying the barrier’s historical or geographical location is independent research that N Q B alone cannot resolve.

5. فَنَقَّبُوا فِي الْبِلَادِ: Traversing and Searching through Lands

وَكَمْ أَهْلَكْنَا قَبْلَهُم مِّن قَرْنٍ هُمْ أَشَدُّ مِنْهُم بَطْشًا فَنَقَّبُوا فِي الْبِلَادِ هَلْ مِن مَّحِيصٍ

wa-kam ahlaknā qablahum min qarnin hum ashaddu minhum baṭshan fa-naqqabū fī al-bilādi hal min maḥīṣin

Translation: “How many generations before them We destroyed, mightier than they, who travelled extensively through the lands: was there any escape?”

Qāf 50:36

This is the third and last Qur’anic occurrence of N Q B.

The Qur’an describes generations and societies preceding the present audience and stronger than them in power and capacity.

That strength did not protect them from destruction.

Fa-naqqabū fī al-bilād appears in this setting.

The meaning of نَقَّبُوا

Naqqabū is a plural Form II perfect verb.

Here it concerns traversing lands, travelling through different regions, and reaching distant areas.

Early Arabic attests naqqaba fī al-bilād for movement and searching through lands. The verse attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays illustrates the same use.

The construction need not mean piercing the soil or excavating the ground.

Fī and al-bilād identify lands and regions as the sphere of activity.

“Travelled through the lands,” “traversed the lands,” or “searched through different regions” are therefore suitable equivalents.

Form II alone does not establish that they travelled to every part of the world, discovered all lands, or conducted systematic scientific research.

The form fits extensive or repeated movement, but does not independently determine its extent, motive, or precise manner.

هُمْ أَشَدُّ مِنْهُم بَطْشًا

The verse begins by describing the earlier generations as mightier.

Baṭsh here concerns capacity and strength expressed in action and domination.

The subject is therefore more than a group travelling: it concerns stronger societies with extensive movement and activity in the lands.

Naqqabū nevertheless does not establish that they ruled every land they traversed.

Travel, searching, gaining access, and governing are different concepts.

A group may move through extensive regions without politically controlling them all.

Their strength follows from ashaddu minhum baṭshan, rather than from naqqabū independently.

هَلْ مِن مَّحِيصٍ: was there any escape?

The verse ends by asking:

هَلْ مِن مَّحِيصٍ

hal min maḥīṣin

“Was there any escape?”

This follows the account of powerful earlier generations being destroyed.

Their travels and power evidently could not save them from the outcome they faced.

Naqqabū itself does not, however, mean “they fled.”

Traversing lands may involve seeking safety, but escape follows from maḥīṣ and the warning context.

Rendering the whole expression merely as “they fled” would lose the image of travelling and searching.

Does naqqabū mean scientific research?

Modern tanqīb may describe archaeological excavation, exploration for natural resources, or scientific investigation.

Qāf 50:36 does not require such a specialized modern meaning.

The verse describes earlier generations moving through lands. They may have learned about routes, regions, resources, or opportunities, but the Qur’an does not specify their activities in detail.

“They conducted scientific research” or “discovered underground resources” is therefore unsuitable as a direct translation.

Nor does the verse condemn exploration, travel, or knowledge of lands in itself.

The warning concerns the inability of power and extensive activity to spare people and societies from destruction or responsibility before The God.

Searching and travelling do not independently carry a negative moral value.

Variant readings and sentence structure

The familiar reading fa-naqqabū has a doubled qāf with a and is perfect: they travelled through the lands.

Some early reading reports attest fa-naqqibū, with i on qāf. It is understood as an imperative: “Then travel through the lands.”

This changes a report about earlier generations into a challenge and warning to the audience.

Early accounts also transmit forms with an undoubled qāf.

The distinctions matter morphologically and syntactically, identifying a report about earlier people or a direct command.

The familiar reading nevertheless remains fa-naqqabū as a perfect verb.

The alternatives should not be placed on an equal footing in the base translation without examining their recitational standing and transmission.

In any case, travelling and ranging through lands remain familiar meanings in this construction.

6. N Q B and Related Roots

N Q B and TH Q B

The roots approach one another in uses concerning piercing.

Thaqb concerns making a hole or opening. Some uses also relate to penetration and light.

Naqb likewise pierces physical objects, but its family includes other uses such as traversing lands and the title naqīb.

The roots may overlap in physical usage.

An absolute distinction between thaqb as always a small hole and naqb as always a large passage would therefore be misleading. Size and kind depend on the object and context.

In Al-Kahf 18:97, naqb concerns breaching a barrier obstructing passage.

Study of TH Q B

N Q B and Ḥ F R

Ḥafr more particularly concerns digging the ground or making a hollow or pit.

Naqb may also require digging and piercing, but in breaching a wall the intended result is an opening or route through it.

Someone may dig a well in the ground, while another person breaches a wall to reach its far side.

This should not become an exceptionless rule. Excavation can create a passage, and breaching can require digging.

The main distinction concerns the action and intended result of each expression.

Study of Ḥ F R

N Q B and B Ḥ TH

Baḥth concerns searching and investigating and may physically describe digging or probing the ground.

Al-Māʾidah 5:31 states:

فَبَعَثَ اللَّهُ غُرَابًا يَبْحَثُ فِي الْأَرْضِ

fa-baʿatha Allāhu ghurāban yabḥathu fī al-arḍi

Translation: “The God sent a raven searching through the earth.”

Baḥth here concerns probing the ground.

Naqqaba in expressions such as fa-naqqabū fī al-bilād instead concerns traversing and searching through lands.

Both families can express searching, but their precise meanings are not identical in every construction.

Qāf 50:36 particularly concerns more than digging soil.

Study of B Ḥ TH

N Q B and N F DH

Nafadha principally concerns passing through and reaching the other side or a destination.

Naqb can create an opening that enables passage.

The roots therefore have a practical connection: naqb sometimes creates a route, while nafadha can express passing along it or through an obstacle.

That connection is not their invariable definition.

Someone can pass through an open gate without making a breach. A hole may also be made in a wall before anyone passes through it.

The roots are independent, and each construction should preserve the distinction between creating an opening and passing through.

Study of N F DH

N Q B and ʿ R F

ʿArafa has familiar uses concerning knowing and becoming aware.

A naqīb is likewise familiar with a group’s affairs in social usage.

Naqīb nevertheless means more than a knowledgeable person: it is an established title for someone responsible for or representing a group.

Lexical accounts consequently explain it through ʿarīf, although the titles have not been identical in every period or circumstance.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:12, “scholar” alone would not convey naqīb, since the social role matters within the covenant.

Study of ʿ R F

N Q B and K F L

Kafālah in some uses concerns undertaking responsibility, guaranteeing, or supervising.

Lexical accounts sometimes describe a naqīb as the group’s kafīl or guarantor.

The overlap matters in understanding responsibility without making the roots identical.

A guarantee may concern a particular person or matter without the guarantor necessarily representing a group.

A naqīb’s social role, by contrast, concerns community affairs and following up its circumstances.

The covenant in Al-Māʾidah 5:12 strengthens the connection, but the naqībs’ precise responsibilities require the whole verse and related evidence.

7. Translation Proposals for Forqan

No single fixed Persian equivalent adequately conveys every use of the root.

For physical naqaba al-ḥāʾiṭ, “pierced the wall” or “made a breach in the wall” is suitable.

For naqb as a route through something, “breach,” “hole,” or in some settings “passage” is appropriate.

For naqqaba fī al-bilād, “travelled through the lands,” “traversed the lands,” or “searched through different regions” is natural.

For the social title naqīb, “representative,” “supervisor,” or “person responsible for a group” is defensible. Where both attending to affairs and representation matter, retaining naqīb with a brief explanation may be more precise.

Al-Māʾidah 5:12

وَبَعَثْنَا مِنْهُمُ اثْنَيْ عَشَرَ نَقِيبًا

wa-baʿathnā minhum ithnay ʿashara naqīban

Proposed translation:

“We raised twelve naqībs from among them.”

A note may define naqīb as a group’s representative or responsible person who follows up its affairs and commitments.

If the Arabic title is not retained, “twelve representatives and supervisors” is intelligible, but should not imply all the powers of independent rulers.

Al-Kahf 18:97

وَمَا اسْتَطَاعُوا لَهُ نَقْبًا

wa-mā istaṭāʿū lahu naqban

Proposed translation:

“They could not make a breach in it.”

“Breach” fits a barrier through which a hole or passage could enable crossing.

Digging a tunnel could be one example, but the verse does not specify the excavation method or passage’s shape.

Qāf 50:36

فَنَقَّبُوا فِي الْبِلَادِ هَلْ مِن مَّحِيصٍ

fa-naqqabū fī al-bilādi hal min maḥīṣin

Proposed translation:

“They travelled extensively through the lands: was there any escape?”

The traversal follows from naqqabū and the question about escape from hal min maḥīṣ.

Naqqabū should not be restricted to flight, conquest, or scientific exploration, since those qualities are not expressly stated in the verb.

Brief root meaning and meanings in each verse

Brief root meaning: Piercing and opening a breach or route; in extended uses, traversing and searching through lands, and knowing and attending to affairs.

Al-Māʾidah 5:12: A naqīb: a representative or responsible person among the Children of Israel, introduced in the setting of the divine covenant.

Al-Kahf 18:97: A breach or hole enabling passage through a barrier; here, the inability to create it in Dhū al-Qarnayn’s structure.

Qāf 50:36: They travelled extensively and traversed the lands; describing powerful earlier generations whose strength and wide activity did not save them from destruction.

8. Conclusion

N Q B has familiar uses concerning piercing, breaching, and opening a way through something. These appear in walls, mountain passages, and injuries perforating skin or foot coverings.

Other uses extend to traversing lands and investigating affairs. Naqqāb describes a knowledgeable investigator, and naqīb a group’s representative or responsible person.

Forms such as niqāb and manāqib also have established meanings that should not all be derived from one simple image without historical evidence.

The Qur’an uses the root in three different settings.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:12, naqīb is a social title among the Children of Israel in the divine covenant’s setting. It does not independently establish prophethood, infallibility, or unlimited power.

In Al-Kahf 18:97, naqb concerns breaching a physical barrier. The verse describes the inability both to cross over and to open a way through it, without naqb itself specifying technical details or geographical location.

In Qāf 50:36, naqqabū concerns traversing and searching through lands. The Qur’an places this beside earlier generations’ strength and destruction, showing that extensive power and resources alone do not provide escape from the consequences of conduct.

N Q B should therefore neither be restricted everywhere to piercing nor generalized vaguely as penetration and profound knowledge.

Final definition: The physical branch of N Q B concerns piercing, breaching, and making a way through something. Movement uses concern traversing and searching through lands. In knowledge and social responsibility, forms such as naqqāb and naqīb describe someone knowledgeable and attentive to affairs or responsible for a group. The Qur’an illustrates these fields in naqban, fa-naqqabū, and naqīban respectively. Each must be understood through its construction and context, without imposing one branch’s qualities on the entire family.
