Audible sound and voice: from human and animal sounds to loudness, calling, and reputation

1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

In Arabic, Ṣ W T concerns sound and what reaches the ear. It may come from a person, animal, instrument, or objects striking each other. Speech, shouting, singing, animal calls, and the collision of two objects can all belong to this field.

Human sound enables speech and communication, but sound itself does not necessarily convey meaningful speech. People may shout, laugh, cry, or sing without every sound being a sentence with a specific meaning.

The root occurs eight times in five Qur’anic verses. Every occurrence belongs to the noun ṣawt:

  • بِصَوْتِكَ (bi-ṣawtika): with your voice; Al-Isrāʾ 17:64.
  • الْأَصْوَاتُ (al-aṣwātu): sounds or voices; Ṭā Hā 20:108.
  • صَوْتِكَ (ṣawtika): your voice; Luqmān 31:19.
  • الْأَصْوَاتِ (al-aṣwāti): sounds or voices; Luqmān 31:19.
  • لَصَوْتُ (la-ṣawtu): surely the sound of; Luqmān 31:19.
  • أَصْوَاتَكُمْ (aṣwātakum): your voices; Al-Ḥujurāt 49:2.
  • صَوْتِ النَّبِيِّ (ṣawti al-nabiyyi): the Prophet’s voice; Al-Ḥujurāt 49:2.
  • أَصْوَاتَهُمْ (aṣwātahum): their voices; Al-Ḥujurāt 49:3.

Other forms are also known in Arabic:

  • صَوْت (ṣawt): sound, voice, or an audible call.
  • أَصْوَات (aṣwāt): sounds or voices.
  • صَاتَ / يَصُوتُ (ṣāta / yaṣūtu): produced sound, called out, or summoned.
  • صَوَّتَ (ṣawwata): produced sound, called someone, or raised a call.
  • تَصْوِيت (taṣwīt): producing sound or calling.
  • صَائِت (ṣāʾit): someone or something producing sound; in a particular use, someone shouting.
  • صَيِّت (ṣayyit): pleasant-voiced, or having a loud, clear voice, depending on usage.
  • صَات (ṣāt): loud-voiced in some early uses.
  • صِيت (ṣīt): reputation, fame, or a name that has spread among people.
  • صِيتَة (ṣītah): reputation or fame; another form of ṣīt.

These forms have related Arabic meanings, but should not all be counted as Qur’anic forms. Only the noun ṣawt and its plural occur in the Qur’an.

2. The semantic core and early Arabic usage

Sound: what reaches the ear

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris defines ṣawt as follows:

وَهُوَ جِنْسٌ لِكُلِّ مَا وَقَرَ فِي أُذُنِ السَّامِعِ

wa-huwa jinsun li-kulli mā waqara fī udhuni al-sāmiʿi

“A general category for everything that settles in the listener’s ear.”

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: ṣawt and what reaches the listener’s ear

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise states:

الصَّوْتُ: الْجَرْسُ

al-ṣawtu: al-jarsu

“Ṣawt is audible sound.”

Ibn al-Sikkīt explicitly includes human and nonhuman sounds rather than restricting ṣawt to people.

This broad definition clarifies the root’s central point. Ṣawt names an audible phenomenon whose source, quality, loudness, and meaning may vary.

A person’s speaking voice, for example, differs from their footsteps. Birdsong, camel calls, colliding stones, and a musical instrument also differ in quality.

All can nevertheless be called sound.

Modern science relates sound to vibration and waves propagating through a material medium. This helps explain its physics, but should not be presented as the historically proven meaning behind every early Arabic use of the root.

Lexically, the established core is audible sound.

Al-ʿAyn, Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, and Lisān al-ʿArab: human and nonhuman sound

Human sound: from speech to shouting

Arabic uses صَاتَ (ṣāta) and صَوَّتَ (ṣawwata) for producing sound or calling out.

In صَوَّتَ بِفُلَانٍ (ṣawwata bi-fulānin), someone calls to another person and summons them.

صَائِت (ṣāʾit) may likewise denote someone calling or shouting.

These uses concern sound, but sound itself and the act of summoning are distinct.

Someone may make a sound without a particular addressee. When calling another person, however, sound serves as the means of summoning.

Nor is every sound a shout. Shouting is one loud, intense form of sound.

Ṣawt should therefore not always be translated as “shouting” unless the context actually indicates an intense call or cry.

A pre-Islamic witness: singing and animal calls

The pre-Islamic poet Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd describes a woman’s singing in his Muʿallaqah:

إِذَا رَجَّعَتْ فِي صَوْتِهَا خِلْتَ صَوْتَهَا
تَجَاوُبَ أَظْآرٍ عَلَى رُبَعٍ رَدِي

idhā rajjaʿat fī ṣawtihā khilta ṣawtahā / tajāwuba aẓʾārin ʿalā rubaʿin radī

Translation:

“When she repeated the notes of her voice, you imagined it as the answering calls of she-camels mourning a lost calf.”

Ṣawt appears twice here, both times concerning the woman’s singing.

The poet compares her voice with the calls of she-camels mourning their calf.

Human and animal sounds thus both belong to ṣawt’s general field. The poet nevertheless distinguishes their qualities, using animal calls to describe the singing’s melody and emotional character.

Al-Zawzanī’s commentary relates tarjīʿ to repeating and returning to the voice’s melodic notes.

Ṣawt here is therefore more than speaking; it concerns vocal sound, melody, and audible quality.

The witness also shows that ṣawt was known for singing and animal calls in pre-Qur’anic Arabic poetry.

Al-Zawzanī’s commentary: Ṭarafah’s witness and repeating the voice

Human and nonhuman sound

Arabic sound may come from living beings or inanimate objects.

For example, lexical sources record أَصَاتَ الْقَوْسَ (aṣāta al-qawsa), making a bow produce sound.

The sound then comes from an instrument’s movement and effect, rather than human speech.

Ṣawt is thus not restricted to speech organs, language, or conscious beings.

Hearing something’s sound does not by itself establish that it possesses speech, understanding, or will.

Human speech normally involves sound, but its meaning comes from words and linguistic structure rather than loudness or melody alone.

Ṣīt: reputation and renown

صِيت (ṣīt) denotes a person’s reputation or renown among people.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ records it especially for a good name and favorable reputation. Other sources also show broader usage for fame and being known.

This meaning has an intelligible connection with sound spreading. As sound travels beyond its source to others’ ears, a person’s name and reports about them may spread among people.

Ṣīt is nevertheless distinct from audible sound.

Someone may be famous in a distant country whose people have never heard their voice.

Labīd ibn Rabīʿah writes:

وَكَمْ مُشْتَرٍ مِنْ مَالِهِ حُسْنَ صِيتَةٍ
لِآبَائِهِ فِي كُلِّ مَبْدًى وَمَحْضَرِ

wa-kam mushtarin min mālihi ḥusna ṣītatin / li-ābāʾihi fī kulli mabdin wa-maḥḍari

Translation:

“Many acquire, through their wealth, a good reputation for their forebears, in every place of nomadic or settled life.”

Ṣītah here means reputation and a good name, rather than a person’s loud voice.

Labīd lived before and after Islam. Without dating this particular verse, it should therefore not be presented as certainly pre-Islamic.

Modern Persian likewise distinguishes a pleasant voice from a good reputation: one concerns sound quality, the other someone’s name and standing.

The distinction exists in Arabic too; reputation should not be incorporated into every definition of ṣawt.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab: ṣīt, reputation, and Labīd’s witness

3. Qur’anic uses: sound, calling, and subdued voices

Al-Isrāʾ 17:64: bi-ṣawtika

Al-Isrāʾ addresses Satan:

وَاسْتَفْزِزْ مَنِ اسْتَطَعْتَ مِنْهُمْ بِصَوْتِكَ

wa-istafziz mani istaṭaʿta minhum bi-ṣawtika

“And stir up whomever you can among them with your voice.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:64

Bi-ṣawtika consists of the preposition bi, the noun ṣawt, and the second-person pronoun ka.

Sound is attributed to Satan here and accompanies istafziz.

Istafziz concerns provoking, stirring up, and unsettling.

The verse therefore describes influence and incitement through sound.

Ṣawt’s own meaning must nevertheless be distinguished from its function in this context.

Ṣawt alone does not mean temptation, falsehood, music, or deception. Those ideas arise from the agent, accompanying verb, and context.

Must Satan’s voice be physically audible?

The verse calls upon Satan to stir people through his voice.

The expression can be understood in connection with calling, temptation, and incitement to wrongdoing.

The remainder mentions mobilizing cavalry and infantry, sharing in wealth and children, and deceptive promises.

Together these describe varied tools and methods of incitement and misguidance.

Bi-ṣawtika itself nevertheless does not specify that Satan’s voice always appears as a physical sound audible to humans.

Nor should it be restricted without evidence to singing, an instrument, music, or a particular medium.

Some early commentaries identify singing and instruments among instances of Satan’s voice. Such applications are not the lexical meaning of Ṣ W T.

The verse itself names neither music nor a particular instrument. The presence of ṣawt alone therefore does not establish a general prohibition on music or every kind of singing.

The clear meaning concerns employing sound or a call to stir people toward temptation and deception.

Al-Ṭabarī: readings of Satan’s voice and calling to disobedience

Ṭā Hā 20:108: wa-khashaʿati al-aṣwātu

Ṭā Hā states:

يَوْمَئِذٍ يَتَّبِعُونَ الدَّاعِيَ لَا عِوَجَ لَهُ وَخَشَعَتِ الْأَصْوَاتُ لِلرَّحْمَٰنِ فَلَا تَسْمَعُ إِلَّا هَمْسًا

yawmaʾidhin yattabiʿūna al-dāʿiya lā ʿiwaja lahu wa-khashaʿati al-aṣwātu li-al-raḥmāni fa-lā tasmaʿu illā hamsan

“On that day they follow the caller, for whom there is no deviation; voices subside before the Entirely Merciful, and you hear only a faint sound.”

Ṭā Hā 20:108

Al-aṣwāt is the plural of ṣawt.

The verb khashaʿat expresses the voices subsiding here.

Khushūʿ in this context concerns becoming calm, humble, and subdued before the majesty of the Entirely Merciful.

The verse describes voices subsiding until only hams is heard.

Ṣawt and hams

The verse continues:

فَلَا تَسْمَعُ إِلَّا هَمْسًا

fa-lā tasmaʿu illā hamsan

“And you hear only a faint sound.”

Hams belongs to H M S and concerns very quiet or concealed sound.

Early commentaries offer two principal explanations: quiet speech and the sound of footsteps on the ground.

Both belong to faint sound’s field.

A basic translation should therefore avoid restricting hams to a particular form of speech unless contextual evidence supports that preference.

Khashaʿati al-aṣwātu likewise does not mean every sound disappears absolutely: the verse immediately mentions hams being heard.

The central image is therefore loud voices subsiding and quietness and faint sound prevailing.

Ṣawt here denotes audible sound itself; khushūʿ describes its quality and condition in the resurrection context.

Al-Ṭabarī: hams, subdued sound, and footsteps

4. Human voice and manners of speech

Luqmān 31:19: wa-ughḍuḍ min ṣawtika

Luqmān counsels his son:

وَاقْصِدْ فِي مَشْيِكَ وَاغْضُضْ مِنْ صَوْتِكَ إِنَّ أَنْكَرَ الْأَصْوَاتِ لَصَوْتُ الْحَمِيرِ

wa-iqṣid fī mashyika wa-ughḍuḍ min ṣawtika inna ankara al-aṣwāti la-ṣawtu al-ḥamīri

“Be moderate in your walking and lower your voice; surely the most disagreeable of sounds is the sound of donkeys.”

Luqmān 31:19

Ṣ W T occurs three times here.

First, ṣawtika denotes the addressee’s voice.

Al-aṣwāti then denotes sounds in the plural.

Finally, la-ṣawtu al-ḥamīri denotes the sound of donkeys.

The three occurrences show ṣawt’s breadth: human voices and animal sounds both fall under it.

Wa-ughḍuḍ min ṣawtika

Ghaḍḍ belongs to GH Ḍ Ḍ and here concerns lowering or reducing.

The command ughḍuḍ min ṣawtika means lowering one’s voice.

The verse does not say never to speak or always to make the quietest possible sound.

This accompanies a command for moderation in walking.

In the preceding verse, Luqmān also forbids arrogance, contemptuously turning away from people, and walking ostentatiously.

Lowering the voice therefore concerns manners, moderation, and avoiding arrogant conduct.

Loudness nevertheless does not have the same moral significance in every situation.

Someone may need to raise their voice to warn a person in danger, seek help, address a large gathering, or prevent harm.

The verse should therefore not become an absolute prohibition on every loud sound. Luqmān’s counsel emphasizes avoiding misplaced shouting and hurtful or ostentatious conduct.

Ankara al-aṣwāti

The verse ends by presenting donkeys’ sound as disagreeable.

Ankar belongs to N K R and here describes a very unpleasant or objectionable sound.

This concerns the animal’s sound quality rather than making the animal morally sinful.

A donkey makes its natural call; it is not responsible, in human terms, for a moral evaluation of that sound.

Its sound illustrates the effect of loud, disagreeable calling in human conduct.

Ṣawt itself here means sound. Its disagreeableness comes from ankar and the comparison’s subject, rather than Ṣ W T.

Not every sound is therefore unpleasant, nor is every loud sound morally objectionable.

Human conduct’s value depends on the situation, purpose, manner of expression, and effect on others.

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:2: aṣwātakum fawqa ṣawti al-nabiyyi

Al-Ḥujurāt states:

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَا تَرْفَعُوا أَصْوَاتَكُمْ فَوْقَ صَوْتِ النَّبِيِّ وَلَا تَجْهَرُوا لَهُ بِالْقَوْلِ كَجَهْرِ بَعْضِكُمْ لِبَعْضٍ أَنْ تَحْبَطَ أَعْمَالُكُمْ وَأَنْتُمْ لَا تَشْعُرُونَ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū lā tarfaʿū aṣwātakum fawqa ṣawti al-nabiyyi wa-lā tajharū lahu bi-al-qawli ka-jahri baʿḍikum li-baʿḍin an taḥbaṭa aʿmālukum wa-antum lā tashʿurūna

“You who have believed, do not raise your voices above the Prophet’s voice or speak loudly to him as you do to one another, lest your deeds become fruitless without your realizing it.”

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:2

Ṣ W T occurs twice here:

أَصْوَاتَكُمْ (aṣwātakum) and صَوْتِ النَّبِيِّ (ṣawti al-nabiyyi).

Both denote human voices, but their possessors differ.

The addressees’ voices appear first, followed by the Prophet’s voice.

The verse commands the addressees not to raise their voices above his.

Ṣawt and qawl in one verse

After aṣwātakum and ṣawti al-nabiyyi, the same verse uses another expression:

وَلَا تَجْهَرُوا لَهُ بِالْقَوْلِ

wa-lā tajharū lahu bi-al-qawli

“And do not speak loudly to him.”

Qawl belongs to Q W L and concerns speech and what is said.

Ṣawt concerns audible sound.

Jahr here indicates the open, loud manner of expressing speech.

Their proximity helps distinguish the words.

Ṣawt and qawl may accompany each other in human speech, but are not complete synonyms.

Two people may utter the same sentence, one quietly and the other shouting. The content of their qawl is then similar, while their ṣawt differs in quality.

Human speech is also more than an acoustic phenomenon: words and sentence structure carry meaning and a message.

The verse should therefore be analyzed by distinguishing vocal loudness from spoken content.

The command’s scope

The command addresses believers interacting with the Prophet.

It concerns how they speak to Muhammad and respect him.

Without independent evidence, every historical or social situation should therefore not be treated as exactly equivalent to being before the Prophet.

Ṣawt itself does not mean obedience, truth, or authority. The Prophet’s due respect and the consequences of disregarding it come from the address and context.

The surah continues by describing people who called Muhammad from behind the chambers and would have done better to wait until he came out.

This continuity shows the subject to be social manners and how to address him.

Such manners are compatible with asking, understanding, and respectful discussion. Nor should the verse itself be made to yield an entire detailed system concerning disagreement and dialogue.

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:3: yaghuḍḍūna aṣwātahum

The next verse states:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ يَغُضُّونَ أَصْوَاتَهُمْ عِنْدَ رَسُولِ اللَّهِ أُولَٰئِكَ الَّذِينَ امْتَحَنَ اللَّهُ قُلُوبَهُمْ لِلتَّقْوَىٰ لَهُمْ مَغْفِرَةٌ وَأَجْرٌ عَظِيمٌ

inna alladhīna yaghuḍḍūna aṣwātahum ʿinda rasūli allāhi ulāʾika alladhīna imtaḥana allāhu qulūbahum li-al-taqwā lahum maghfiratun wa-ajrun ʿaẓīmun

“Those who lower their voices before the Messenger of The God are those whose hearts God has tested for mindfulness. For them are forgiveness and a great reward.”

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:3

Aṣwātahum here denotes a group of believers’ voices.

Yaghuḍḍūna expresses lowering and controlling the voice.

The verse continues the preceding subject: raising voices above the Prophet’s is first forbidden, then those lowering theirs before him are praised.

Ṣawt therefore has the same meaning in both verses. The command, conduct, and moral consequence differ.

Lowering the voice here shows good manners and respect for the Prophet.

Ṣawt alone, however, does not mean mindfulness. The following clause connects the conduct with mindfulness.

Lowering the voice also differs from complete silence: someone may speak quietly, ask, or answer.

The verse therefore preserves ṣawt’s general meaning while describing how to use the voice in a particular setting.

5. Ṣawt compared with related words

Ṣ W T (ṣawt) and Q W L (qawl)

Qawl concerns speech and what is said.

Ṣawt denotes audible sound.

Ordinary human speech involves sound, but not every sound is speech.

An animal’s call, breaking wood, or colliding stones may be ṣawt without being human qawl.

Both families appear together in Al-Ḥujurāt 49:2, clarifying the distinction.

Study of Q W L

Ṣ W T (ṣawt) and N Ṭ Q (naṭaqa)

Nuṭq concerns speaking and expression.

Ṣawt has a broader field, potentially encompassing any audible sound.

Luqmān 31:19, for example, calls donkeys’ sound ṣawt without meaning that they speak a human language.

This does not deny every form of animal communication or perception. It shows that ṣawt itself does not imply linguistic speech.

Ṣ W T (ṣawt) and S M ʿ (samiʿa)

Samʿ concerns hearing and receiving sound.

Ṣawt is what may be heard.

Ṭā Hā 20:108 states:

وَخَشَعَتِ الْأَصْوَاتُ (wa-khashaʿati al-aṣwātu), followed by فَلَا تَسْمَعُ إِلَّا هَمْسًا (fa-lā tasmaʿu illā hamsan).

Sounds are the objects of hearing, while tasmaʿu expresses auditory reception.

Producing and hearing sound are therefore distinct concepts.

The existence of sound also does not establish that every addressee heard it or understood its meaning.

Study of S M ʿ

Ṣ W T (ṣawt) and N D W (nidāʾ)

Nidāʾ concerns calling and summoning.

Ṣawt may be the means of a call, but not every sound is a call.

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:4 states:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ يُنَادُونَكَ مِنْ وَرَاءِ الْحُجُرَاتِ أَكْثَرُهُمْ لَا يَعْقِلُونَ

inna alladhīna yunādūnaka min warāʾi al-ḥujurāti aktharuhum lā yaʿqilūna

“Those who call you from behind the chambers—most of them do not reason.”

Yunādūnaka here expresses calling Muhammad.

In Al-Ḥujurāt 49:2 and Al-Ḥujurāt 49:3, however, aṣwāt concerns raising or lowering voices.

Nidāʾ therefore emphasizes summoning, while ṣawt emphasizes audible sound.

Study of N D W

Ṣ W T (ṣawt) and J H R (jahara)

Jahr in speech-related uses concerns speaking openly and loudly.

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:2 places tajharū lahu bi-al-qawli alongside aṣwātakum.

Loud speech involves sound, but sound itself is not necessarily loud.

Quiet and loud sounds are both ṣawt.

Loudness and openness therefore come from accompanying verbs and descriptions, rather than Ṣ W T’s fixed meaning.

Study of J H R

Ṣ W T (ṣawt) and Ṣ Y Ḥ (ṣāḥa)

Ṣayḥah concerns a cry or shout.

The Qur’an also uses this family for intense cries and sudden events.

Ṣawt is broader, since it can describe quiet, ordinary, or loud sound.

Every ṣayḥah thus belongs to sound’s field, but not every ṣawt is a ṣayḥah.

Bi-ṣawtika in Al-Isrāʾ 17:64 does not alone mean an intense shout; al-aṣwāt in Ṭā Hā 20:108 likewise accompanies subsiding and quietness.

Ṣ W T (ṣawt) and Ṣ M T (ṣamata)

Ṣamt concerns remaining silent and not speaking.

Ṣawt names audible sound.

They can contrast in human speech: someone makes a sound or remains silent.

A person’s lack of speech does not, however, mean the absence of every environmental sound.

Remaining silent also does not itself establish inability to speak or absence of thought.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:193 contrasts ṣāmitūna with calling, while the Ṣ W T verses concern sound itself and its qualities.

Study of Ṣ M T

6. Sound and the moral value of conduct

Qur’anic uses of Ṣ W T show that sound has no fixed moral value in itself.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:64 presents it in Satan’s incitement and misguidance.

Ṭā Hā 20:108 describes voices subsiding before the Entirely Merciful.

Luqmān 31:19 asks a person to lower their voice and avoid disagreeable calling.

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:2 and Al-Ḥujurāt 49:3 concern manners: not raising one’s voice above the Prophet’s and lowering it before him.

These uses share a lexical meaning, while context supplies their value and moral consequences.

A loud voice may be hurtful, threatening, or arrogant. Elsewhere the same loudness may be needed to warn, save someone’s life, or convey a message.

A quiet voice is not always good either: someone may quietly deceive or conceal the truth.

An absolute moral rule about loudness or quietness therefore cannot be derived from Ṣ W T.

What matters is the speech’s subject, its manner, the addressee’s situation, and the conduct’s consequences.

A pleasant, melodious, or resonant voice likewise does not itself establish the spoken content’s truth.

Someone may say something false in a delightful voice or express a clear truth in an ordinary voice.

Distinguishing vocal sound from the truth of its content is an important boundary in studying this root.

7. Translation suggestions for Forqan

صَوْت (ṣawt): sound, voice, or call, according to context.

أَصْوَات (aṣwāt): sounds or voices.

صَاتَ (ṣāta): made a sound or called out.

صَوَّتَ (ṣawwata): made a sound, called someone, or summoned aloud.

صَائِت (ṣāʾit): producing sound or shouting.

صَيِّت (ṣayyit): pleasant-voiced, loud-voiced, or resonant.

صِيت (ṣīt): renown, reputation, or a good name.

تَصْوِيت (taṣwīt): producing sound or calling. Modern Arabic also uses it for voting; that later meaning should not be transferred to Qur’anic verses.

inna alladhīna yaghuḍḍūna aṣwātahum ʿinda rasūli allāhi

“Those who lower their voices before the Messenger of The God.”

“Sound” or “voice” is the principal, natural equivalent throughout these uses.

“Singing” may suit a melodic voice, “a call” a loud sound, and “a shout” an intense sound. Such equivalents should replace ṣawt only where context supports that particular feature.

8. Conclusion

The Arabic root Ṣ W T concerns audible sound, whether produced by people, animals, or objects.

Early Arabic uses related verbs for producing sound, shouting, and calling another. Ṣīt also concerns reputation and renown, associated with a person’s name and reports spreading among people.

Every Qur’anic occurrence belongs to the noun ṣawt; no derived verb appears.

Its varied verses concern sound attributed to Satan, subdued voices on resurrection day, human speaking voices, animal sound, and the manners of lowering one’s voice before the Prophet.

This variety shows that sound itself is neither good nor bad; nor is it always meaningful speech, shouting, or singing.

In human usage, sound may enable speech and communication, but the root alone does not establish the speech’s truth, the speaker’s intention, or the conduct’s value.

In Luqmān 31:19, Al-Ḥujurāt 49:2, and Al-Ḥujurāt 49:3, lowering the voice concerns manners and respect. It does not mean an absolute prohibition on speaking loudly in every setting.

Attributing ṣawt to Satan in Al-Isrāʾ 17:64 likewise does not justify restricting it to music or a particular instrument.

Brief root meaning for Forqan:

Audible sound, voice, and call; in other uses, producing sound and summoning, and, in the ṣīt branch, reputation and renown.

The source, quality, and sentence context determine each occurrence’s precise meaning. Loudness, melody, unpleasantness, and spoken content are not fixed parts of the root.
