The three-letter Arabic root ف ق ر concerns need, deficiency, and lacking what is necessary for living or accomplishing a task. In economic usage, فَقْر (faqr) describes a situation in which a person's resources and possessions are insufficient to meet their needs. A فَقِير (faqīr) is someone who faces a shortfall in meeting those needs.

The root's semantic range, however, is not limited to a lack of money. Someone may be financially well-off yet depend for life, health, security, and other conditions of existence on things beyond themselves.

Thus, in Fatir 35:15, the Qur’an describes all human beings as الْفُقَرَاءُ إِلَى اللَّهِ: those in need of The God. Here, poverty does not mean an absence of possessions; it expresses humanity's fundamental dependence on its Creator.

Another branch of the root concerns the vertebrae, gaps, and injury. فَاقِرَة (fāqirah) in Al-Qiyamah 75:25 denotes a devastating, backbreaking calamity. It must therefore be distinguished from financial poverty.

The root occurs fourteen times in fourteen Qur’anic verses: فَقْر once, singular and plural forms of فَقِير twelve times, and فَاقِرَة once.

The core of the human-related usages is a need or deficiency whose remedy depends on something beyond one's available resources. In the fāqirah branch, the emphasis is instead on a backbreaking injury or calamity that overwhelms one's endurance.

1. The root in early Arabic

Faqr: need contrasted with sufficiency

In early Arabic, فَقْر is contrasted with غِنًى (ghinā).

Ghinā concerns having sufficient means and being free of need.

Faqr denotes deficiency and need.

In economic usage, رَجُلٌ فَقِيرٌ describes a man who lacks sufficient means to sustain his life.

Its plural is فُقَرَاء (fuqarāʾ), and its feminine form is فَقِيرَة (faqīrah).

Early lexicons use the expression الفَقْرُ الْحَاجَةُ: faqr means need.

Need does not always mean having absolutely nothing.

Someone may possess a small home, tools, or some food, while these resources remain insufficient to support them and their family.

Someone may also meet today's needs but lack any support for tomorrow, leaving them exposed to serious deprivation.

Poverty should therefore not be restricted to having not even a single coin or possession.

What matters is the relationship between a person's actual needs and the resources available to meet them.

A pre-Islamic poetic example: Hatim al-Ta’i

Hatim al-Ta’i, the well-known pre-Islamic poet, says:

وَإِنِّي لَعَفُّ الْفَقْرِ مُشْتَرَكُ الْغِنَى
وَوُدُّكَ شَكْلٌ لَا يُوَافِقُهُ شَكْلِي

Wa-innī la-ʿaffu l-faqri mushtaraku l-ghinā / wa-wudduka shaklun lā yuwāfiquhu shaklī.

Translation: “I am restrained in poverty and share my wealth in prosperity; but your manner of friendship does not accord with my character and way.”

The verse contrasts two conditions:

الفَقْر: poverty and need.

الغِنَى: prosperity and sufficient means.

The poet describes his behavior in both conditions.

When poor, he strives to preserve his dignity and restraint.

When prosperous, he does not keep his possessions exclusively for himself, but allows others to benefit as well.

This is linguistically significant because it shows faqr used in its economic and social sense of need in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

Restraint and generosity, however, are not components of the lexical meaning of poverty.

The poet describes his own conduct in poverty and prosperity; he is not declaring all poor people restrained or all wealthy people generous.

The verse survives in the collection attributed to Hatim al-Ta’i and serves as evidence of pre-Islamic Arabic. Like other pre-Islamic poetry, however, its text has reached us through later transmission and manuscripts.

Need and the vertebrae

Lexicons also record this root family in connection with the bones of the back.

فَقَار (faqār) denotes the vertebrae of the spine.

فَقَارَة (faqārah) and فِقْرَة (fiqrah) name an individual vertebra.

In some early usages, فَقِير also describes someone whose backbone has been injured or broken.

In Maqayis al-Lughah, Ibn Faris identifies an opening or separation within the structure of things as the family's core, connecting it with the spaces between vertebrae.

In that explanation, he mentions the view that calling a needy person faqīr evokes someone whose backbone has seemingly been broken by severe need.

The image is understandable.

Severe poverty can reduce a person's ability and practical independence as though the support of their life had been broken.

This figurative explanation should not, however, be presented as the established history of the word faqīr.

The resemblance between the two usages does not establish whether the economic meaning or the vertebral meaning arose first.

Both branches are known in early Arabic, and their precise historical relationship requires independent evidence.

A pre-Islamic poetic example: Tarafah ibn al-‘Abd

Tarafah, the well-known pre-Islamic poet, says:

وَإِذَا تَلْسُنُنِي أَلْسُنُهَا
إِنَّنِي لَسْتُ بِمَوْهُونٍ فَقِرْ

Wa-idhā talsununī alsunuhā / innanī lastu bi-mawhūnin faqir.

Translation: “When she reproaches me with her tongue, I can answer her, for I am not a weak, broken-backed man.”

Here, فَقِرْ occurs beside مَوْهُون (mawhūn).

Mawhūn describes someone weak and feeble.

In this construction, faqir has been understood in relation to injury or breakage of the back.

The poet means that he is not powerless or defenseless and can respond for himself.

Some transmissions of Tarafah's poetry read غمر instead of فَقِرْ. The wording is therefore not without variation.

Nevertheless, the faqr reading is known in literary and lexical sources and is significant for studying the branch concerning a broken or weakened back.

This example does not concern financial poverty and should not be taken as evidence that the poet is describing his economic hardship.

Fāqirah: a backbreaking calamity

Within this semantic field, فَاقِرَة is recorded for a great and grievous calamity.

Lexicons explain it as an affliction that seems to break a person's vertebrae.

The image does not entail a literally broken spine in every usage.

Persian likewise uses “a backbreaking calamity” for a hardship that is exceedingly difficult to bear, even when no physical injury to the back has occurred.

In the Qur’an, fāqirah appears in Al-Qiyamah 75:25 with this sense of a devastating calamity.

Understanding the root's full range thus requires retaining two important branches: need, and backbreaking injury or calamity.

2. Arabic forms and Qur’anic derivatives

Important Arabic forms in this family include:

  • فَقْر (faqr): need and deficiency; in another branch, a gap or hollow.
  • فَقِير (faqīr): needy; in another usage, someone with an injured back.
  • فَقِيرَة (faqīrah): the feminine of faqīr.
  • فُقَرَاء (fuqarāʾ): the plural of faqīr; needy people.
  • اِفْتَقَرَ (iftaqara): became needy or came to need something.
  • اِفْتِقَار (iftiqār): need and dependence on something.
  • أَفْقَرَ (afqara): made someone poor; in other usages, lent someone an animal to ride.
  • فَقَار (faqār): the vertebrae.
  • فَقَارَة (faqārah) and فِقْرَة (fiqrah): an individual vertebra.
  • فَاقِرَة (fāqirah): a devastating, backbreaking calamity.
  • فَوَاقِر (fawāqir): the plural of fāqirah; backbreaking afflictions.
  • مَفْقُور (mafqūr): in early usage, injured in the vertebrae.
  • مَفَاقِر (mafāqir): needs and various aspects of deficiency.
  • تَفَقَّرَ (tafaqqara): in تَفَقَّرَتِ الْأَرْضُ, the ground developed many hollows.
  • مُتَفَاقِر (mutafāqir): someone who presents themselves as needy.

Arabic also records فَقَرَ for digging the earth, making a hole, and piercing beads or objects strung into a necklace.

These branches concern openings and hollows, but do not occur in the Qur’an.

Qur’anic forms

Three main forms of this family occur in the Qur’an:

فَقْر: once, in Al-Baqarah 2:268.

فَقِير and its plural فُقَرَاء: twelve times.

فَاقِرَة: once, in Al-Qiyamah 75:25.

The root therefore occurs fourteen times in fourteen verses altogether.

No verb such as فَقَرَ, اِفْتَقَرَ, or أَفْقَرَ occurs in the Qur’anic text.

This distinction matters: a root study may examine known Arabic forms, whereas a Qur’anic index records only forms actually present in the Qur’an.

3. “Satan promises you poverty”: fear of need

Al-Baqarah 2:268

الشَّيْطَانُ يَعِدُكُمُ الْفَقْرَ وَيَأْمُرُكُمْ بِالْفَحْشَاءِ ۖ وَاللَّهُ يَعِدُكُمْ مَغْفِرَةً مِنْهُ وَفَضْلًا ۗ وَاللَّهُ وَاسِعٌ عَلِيمٌ

Ash-shayṭānu yaʿidukumu l-faqra wa-yaʾmurukum bi-l-faḥshāʾi wa-llāhu yaʿidukum maghfiratan minhu wa-faḍlan wa-llāhu wāsiʿun ʿalīm.

Translation: “Satan promises you poverty and commands you to indecency; The God promises you forgiveness from Him and bounty. The God is All-Encompassing, Knowing.”

This verse occurs among passages about spending and the use of possessions.

Here, الْفَقْرَ means economic need and poverty.

The subject, however, is not merely being poor; it also concerns the fear of becoming poor.

Actual poverty and fear of poverty

Someone may genuinely lack sufficient resources for food, housing, or other necessities of life.

That is actual poverty.

Someone else may possess sufficient wealth yet continually fear for their financial future.

That fear may lead them to refuse help to the needy, even when such help would not endanger their own livelihood.

In this verse, Satan's promise of poverty accompanies a command to indecency.

The issue is therefore not economic concern alone, but concern that can prevent right conduct or lead to wrongdoing.

Is every financial concern a satanic temptation?

No.

Someone responsible for feeding a family, paying debts, or caring for vulnerable people must consider their actual resources and responsibilities.

Al-Baqarah 2:286 states that The God does not burden anyone beyond their capacity.

Al-Baqarah 2:233 also connects providing food and clothing with capacity and what is recognized as right.

Financial planning and avoiding actions that endanger dependents' livelihoods are therefore not inherently wrong.

What Al-Baqarah 2:268 condemns is the frightening promise that draws a person toward indecency and wrongdoing.

Conversely, the promise of divine bounty should not become a claim that every act of giving immediately produces a measurable increase in personal wealth.

The verse speaks of forgiveness and bounty, not a financial contract with a fixed return.

4. Fuqarāʾ: needy people and the responsibility to give

Giving charity openly and privately

Al-Baqarah 2:271

إِنْ تُبْدُوا الصَّدَقَاتِ فَنِعِمَّا هِيَ ۖ وَإِنْ تُخْفُوهَا وَتُؤْتُوهَا الْفُقَرَاءَ فَهُوَ خَيْرٌ لَكُمْ ۚ وَيُكَفِّرُ عَنْكُمْ مِنْ سَيِّئَاتِكُمْ ۗ وَاللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ خَبِيرٌ

In tubdū ṣ-ṣadaqāti fa-niʿimmā hiya wa-in tukhfūhā wa-tuʾtūhā l-fuqarāʾa fa-huwa khayrun lakum wa-yukaffiru ʿankum min sayyiʾātikum wa-llāhu bi-mā taʿmalūna khabīr.

Translation: “If you disclose your charitable gifts, that is good; if you conceal them and give them to the needy, that is better for you, and He will expiate some of your misdeeds. The God is aware of what you do.”

Here, الْفُقَرَاءَ is the plural of faqīr.

The subject is people who need help and resources.

The Qur’an also calls open charity good, while describing private giving to the needy as better.

Public visibility does not, by itself, prove ostentation.

For example, a group may publicly collect money for disaster victims so that others can participate.

Such conduct is not necessarily showing off.

Private help, in contrast, can better preserve the needy person's dignity and privacy.

The word fuqarāʾ itself does not mean people whose needs must be exposed and displayed publicly.

They are human beings with rights and dignity who face insufficient resources.

Need accompanied by self-respect

Al-Baqarah 2:273

لِلْفُقَرَاءِ الَّذِينَ أُحْصِرُوا فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ لَا يَسْتَطِيعُونَ ضَرْبًا فِي الْأَرْضِ يَحْسَبُهُمُ الْجَاهِلُ أَغْنِيَاءَ مِنَ التَّعَفُّفِ تَعْرِفُهُمْ بِسِيمَاهُمْ لَا يَسْأَلُونَ النَّاسَ إِلْحَافًا ۗ وَمَا تُنْفِقُوا مِنْ خَيْرٍ فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ بِهِ عَلِيمٌ

Li-l-fuqarāʾi lladhīna uḥṣirū fī sabīli llāhi lā yastaṭīʿūna ḍarban fī l-arḍi yaḥsabuhumu l-jāhilu aghniyāʾa mina t-taʿaffufi taʿrifuhum bi-sīmāhum lā yasʾalūna n-nāsa ilḥāfan wa-mā tunfiqū min khayrin fa-inna llāha bihi ʿalīm.

Translation: “For the needy who are constrained in the way of The God and cannot travel through the land. The uninformed person thinks them wealthy because of their restraint. You recognize them by their signs; they do not ask people insistently. Whatever good you spend, The God knows it.”

This is one of the most important uses of ف ق ر concerning human need.

The Qur’an describes people whose restraint, despite their poverty, leads an uninformed observer to consider them wealthy.

A tidy appearance, not asking, or not complaining therefore does not prove actual sufficiency.

Poverty without begging

الْفُقَرَاء and لَا يَسْأَلُونَ النَّاسَ إِلْحَافًا are two distinct expressions.

The first identifies their condition of need.

The second describes their manner of asking or their lack of insistence.

Faqīr therefore does not fundamentally mean a beggar or someone who asks people for money.

Someone may be poor yet ask others for nothing because of dignity, fear of humiliation, or not knowing how to obtain help.

Conversely, someone may ask for help without genuinely needing it.

Asking or not asking is thus not, on its own, a conclusive criterion of poverty.

“They cannot travel through the land”

The verse speaks of people unable to travel through the land in search of resources.

This inability may arise from social restrictions, living conditions, external difficulties, or physical limitations.

The verse itself does not restrict the cause to a particular illness or bodily condition.

The poor people mentioned should therefore not all be understood as physically unable to walk.

Nor can the verse establish that every poor person is unable to work.

It concerns a particular group of needy people who face a further restriction in addition to poverty.

Recognizing need without intrusion or humiliation

The verse says:

«تَعْرِفُهُمْ بِسِيمَاهُمْ»

Taʿrifuhum bi-sīmāhum.

That is, you recognize them by their signs.

This expression concerns recognizing a condition from available signs.

It should not become a license to spy on needy people's private lives.

Helping someone in need does not grant an unjustified right to intrude into their home, possessions, relationships, or personal secrets.

The aim should be to recognize genuine need and provide suitable help, not to humiliate people or force them to display their poverty.

5. Faqīr and miskīn: related but not entirely identical descriptions

At-Tawbah 9:60

إِنَّمَا الصَّدَقَاتُ لِلْفُقَرَاءِ وَالْمَسَاكِينِ وَالْعَامِلِينَ عَلَيْهَا وَالْمُؤَلَّفَةِ قُلُوبُهُمْ وَفِي الرِّقَابِ وَالْغَارِمِينَ وَفِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ وَابْنِ السَّبِيلِ ۖ فَرِيضَةً مِنَ اللَّهِ ۗ وَاللَّهُ عَلِيمٌ حَكِيمٌ

Innamā ṣ-ṣadaqātu li-l-fuqarāʾi wa-l-masākīni wa-l-ʿāmilīna ʿalayhā wa-l-muʾallafati qulūbuhum wa-fī r-riqābi wa-l-ghārimīna wa-fī sabīli llāhi wa-bni s-sabīli farīḍatan mina llāhi wa-llāhu ʿalīmun ḥakīm.

Translation: “Charitable contributions are only for the needy, the distressed, those administering them, those whose hearts are reconciled, for freeing those in bondage, debtors, in the way of The God, and the stranded traveler: an obligation from The God. The God is Knowing, Wise.”

Here, الفقراء and المساكين appear alongside one another.

If they were entirely synonymous, their inclusion together in such a list would require explanation.

Their difference, however, should not be converted without examination into a fixed, numerical ranking.

Lexicographers' disagreement over the severity of faqr and maskanah

Early lexical sources contain two different understandings.

Some lexicographers describe the faqīr as someone with limited means of support, while the miskīn is needier and more deprived.

Others, conversely, regard the faqīr as more severely deprived than the miskīn.

Some, for example, cite Al-Kahf 18:79, where people described as masākīn possess a boat with which they work at sea.

This shows that a miskīn may possess tools or property.

Owning a boat, however, does not establish that all their needs were met.

Nor does this verse alone establish that a miskīn is wealthier than a faqīr in every usage.

In Arabic, maskanah can concern distress, a weak position, and inability to meet needs.

Faqr emphasizes deficiency and need.

A person may therefore be both faqīr and miskīn; in another situation, one description may express a more particular aspect of their hardship.

Does the Qur’an establish a fixed financial poverty threshold?

The verses containing ف ق ر provide no fixed numerical threshold for identifying poverty.

For example, they do not say that anyone with less than a specified amount is poor, while anyone with slightly more is no longer poor.

In actual life, the resources needed depend on a person's circumstances and dependents.

A family may require more resources than a person living alone to provide food, housing, healthcare, and children's education.

Living costs and access to work also vary between environments.

These examples clarify how the concept of need applies today; they should not be treated as a numerical definition issued by the verse itself.

In Qur’anic language, faqr is a condition of need; identifying its instances requires understanding a person's actual shortfall and living conditions.

6. The needy in ritual and the distribution of resources

Feeding someone in need

Al-Hajj 22:28

لِيَشْهَدُوا مَنَافِعَ لَهُمْ وَيَذْكُرُوا اسْمَ اللَّهِ فِي أَيَّامٍ مَعْلُومَاتٍ عَلَىٰ مَا رَزَقَهُمْ مِنْ بَهِيمَةِ الْأَنْعَامِ ۖ فَكُلُوا مِنْهَا وَأَطْعِمُوا الْبَائِسَ الْفَقِيرَ

Li-yashhadū manāfiʿa lahum wa-yadhkurū sma llāhi fī ayyāmin maʿlūmātin ʿalā mā razaqahum min bahīmati l-anʿāmi fa-kulū minhā wa-aṭʿimū l-bāʾisa l-faqīr.

Translation: “So that they may witness benefits for themselves and mention the name of The God on known days over the livestock He has provided them. Eat of them and feed the distressed, needy person.”

Two descriptions appear together here:

الْبَائِسَ (al-bāʾis)

and

الْفَقِيرَ (al-faqīr).

Bāʾis concerns hardship, suffering, and a difficult condition.

Faqīr denotes need.

Together, they depict someone who both faces hardship and lacks access to adequate resources.

This should not be taken to mean that only someone meeting technical, numerical definitions of both terms is entitled to receive food.

The verse occurs in the context of rites and using livestock that The God has provided for people.

The command is that enjoyment of this blessing should not remain exclusively personal; needy people should also benefit.

Emigrants who have lost their homes and possessions

Al-Hashr 59:8

لِلْفُقَرَاءِ الْمُهَاجِرِينَ الَّذِينَ أُخْرِجُوا مِنْ دِيَارِهِمْ وَأَمْوَالِهِمْ يَبْتَغُونَ فَضْلًا مِنَ اللَّهِ وَرِضْوَانًا وَيَنْصُرُونَ اللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُ ۚ أُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الصَّادِقُونَ

Li-l-fuqarāʾi l-muhājirīna lladhīna ukhrijū min diyārihim wa-amwālihim yabtaghūna faḍlan mina llāhi wa-riḍwānan wa-yanṣurūna llāha wa-rasūlahu ulāʾika humu ṣ-ṣādiqūn.

Translation: “For the needy emigrants who were expelled from their homes and possessions, seeking bounty and approval from The God and supporting The God and His Messenger. Those are the truthful.”

Here, fuqarāʾ describes emigrants who have lost their homes and possessions.

Their poverty was therefore not simply the result of an original lack of resources.

Before displacement, they may have had homes, land, tools, or property; expulsion from their homeland deprived them of access to those resources.

This is an important situation in human life.

Someone may own a home and have a job in their city, yet after war, threats, or forced expulsion arrive somewhere without immediate means of support.

Their need in such circumstances is real, even if they were formerly prosperous.

The verse does not, conversely, say that every emigrant is necessarily poor.

It concerns a particular group of needy emigrants expelled from their homes and possessions.

The next verse, Al-Hashr 59:9, describes people who love the emigrants and prefer them over themselves, even while experiencing need themselves.

This connection clarifies social responsibility toward displaced and needy people.

7. Poverty, financial responsibility, and justice

A poor guardian and an orphan's property

An-Nisa 4:6

وَابْتَلُوا الْيَتَامَىٰ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا بَلَغُوا النِّكَاحَ فَإِنْ آنَسْتُمْ مِنْهُمْ رُشْدًا فَادْفَعُوا إِلَيْهِمْ أَمْوَالَهُمْ ۖ وَلَا تَأْكُلُوهَا إِسْرَافًا وَبِدَارًا أَنْ يَكْبَرُوا ۚ وَمَنْ كَانَ غَنِيًّا فَلْيَسْتَعْفِفْ ۖ وَمَنْ كَانَ فَقِيرًا فَلْيَأْكُلْ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ ۚ فَإِذَا دَفَعْتُمْ إِلَيْهِمْ أَمْوَالَهُمْ فَأَشْهِدُوا عَلَيْهِمْ ۚ وَكَفَىٰ بِاللَّهِ حَسِيبًا

Wa-btalū l-yatāmā ḥattā idhā balaghū n-nikāḥa fa-in ānastum minhum rushdan fa-dfaʿū ilayhim amwālahum wa-lā taʾkulūhā isrāfan wa-bidāran an yakbarū wa-man kāna ghaniyyan fa-l-yastaʿfif wa-man kāna faqīran fa-l-yaʾkul bi-l-maʿrūfi fa-idhā dafaʿtum ilayhim amwālahum fa-ashhidū ʿalayhim wa-kafā bi-llāhi ḥasībā.

Translation: “Test the orphans until they reach marriageable age; if you perceive sound judgment in them, hand over their property to them. Do not consume it extravagantly and hastily before they grow up. Whoever is wealthy should abstain; whoever is poor may partake according to what is right. When you hand over their property, take witnesses over them. The God suffices as Reckoner.”

Here, poor and wealthy describe the person responsible for safeguarding and administering an orphan's property.

The issue is the guardian's own need and its relationship to the owner's financial rights.

The Qur’an distinguishes a wealthy guardian from a poor one.

The wealthy guardian is asked to exercise restraint and not benefit personally from the orphan's property.

A poor guardian, however, is permitted to benefit within the bounds of maʿrūf, what is recognized as right.

Does poverty authorize unlimited use of another person's property?

No.

The verse has already expressly prohibited extravagant and hasty consumption of the orphan's property.

It then qualifies the poor guardian's allowance with بِالْمَعْرُوفِ.

A person's need therefore does not make them the owner of all the property entrusted to their care.

Maʿrūf must accord with proper conduct, genuine need, responsibility fulfilled, and the property owner's rights.

The verse also emphasizes returning property to its owners and taking witnesses.

A guardian's poverty therefore does not remove their responsibility to honor the trust.

Poor and wealthy before justice

An-Nisa 4:135

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا كُونُوا قَوَّامِينَ بِالْقِسْطِ شُهَدَاءَ لِلَّهِ وَلَوْ عَلَىٰ أَنْفُسِكُمْ أَوِ الْوَالِدَيْنِ وَالْأَقْرَبِينَ ۚ إِنْ يَكُنْ غَنِيًّا أَوْ فَقِيرًا فَاللَّهُ أَوْلَىٰ بِهِمَا ۖ فَلَا تَتَّبِعُوا الْهَوَىٰ أَنْ تَعْدِلُوا ۚ وَإِنْ تَلْوُوا أَوْ تُعْرِضُوا فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ كَانَ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ خَبِيرًا

Yā ayyuhā lladhīna āmanū kūnū qawwāmīna bi-l-qisṭi shuhadāʾa li-llāhi wa-law ʿalā anfusikum awi l-wālidayni wa-l-aqrabīna in yakun ghaniyyan aw faqīran fa-llāhu awlā bihimā fa-lā tattabiʿū l-hawā an taʿdilū wa-in talwū aw tuʿriḍū fa-inna llāha kāna bi-mā taʿmalūna khabīrā.

Translation: “You who have believed, stand firmly for justice as witnesses for The God, even against yourselves, your parents, or close relatives. Whether someone is wealthy or poor, The God has a greater claim concerning both. Do not follow desire and turn away from justice. If you distort or turn aside, The God is aware of what you do.”

Poverty and wealth here are economic conditions that must not override just judgment.

Someone's wealth and influence may incline a person to testify in their favor.

Conversely, sympathy for a poor person may lead someone to speak falsely about the facts of a financial dispute.

The Qur’an warns against both tendencies.

Being poor does not itself prove truthfulness or innocence, just as being wealthy does not prove dishonesty or oppression.

Justice must rest on truth and people's rights, not on their wealth or poverty.

This does not make the poor person's need unimportant. Helping someone in need is one responsibility; testifying truthfully is another.

One must not be violated in order to fulfill the other.

8. Poverty and the possibility of marriage

An-Nur 24:32

وَأَنْكِحُوا الْأَيَامَىٰ مِنْكُمْ وَالصَّالِحِينَ مِنْ عِبَادِكُمْ وَإِمَائِكُمْ ۚ إِنْ يَكُونُوا فُقَرَاءَ يُغْنِهِمُ اللَّهُ مِنْ فَضْلِهِ ۗ وَاللَّهُ وَاسِعٌ عَلِيمٌ

Wa-ankiḥū l-ayāmā minkum wa-ṣ-ṣāliḥīna min ʿibādikum wa-imāʾikum in yakūnū fuqarāʾa yughnihimu llāhu min faḍlihi wa-llāhu wāsiʿun ʿalīm.

Translation: “Arrange marriage for the unmarried among you and the suitable among your male and female slaves. If they are needy, The God will enrich them from His bounty. The God is All-Encompassing, Knowing.”

Here, fuqarāʾ is the plural of faqīr.

The subject is people for whom insufficient financial means may make marriage difficult.

The Qur’an does not present poverty as a barrier that must permanently deprive someone of the possibility of marriage.

At the same time, the promise of divine bounty should not be made into an absolute guarantee that every couple will become wealthy immediately after marrying.

An-Nur 24:33 also calls those without the means to marry to exercise restraint until The God enriches them from His bounty.

These two verses must therefore be understood together.

One speaks of facilitating marriage and hoping for divine bounty.

The other considers those who do not yet have the means to marry.

The first verse should therefore not be taken to mean accepting any financial commitment without considering actual capacity and responsibilities.

Nor should poverty become a permanent barrier leaving a needy person no route to forming a family.

9. “I am in need of whatever good You have sent down to me”: Moses' need after helping others

Al-Qasas 28:24

فَسَقَىٰ لَهُمَا ثُمَّ تَوَلَّىٰ إِلَى الظِّلِّ فَقَالَ رَبِّ إِنِّي لِمَا أَنْزَلْتَ إِلَيَّ مِنْ خَيْرٍ فَقِيرٌ

Fa-saqā lahumā thumma tawallā ilā ẓ-ẓilli fa-qāla rabbi innī li-mā anzalta ilayya min khayrin faqīr.

Translation: “He watered for the two of them, then withdrew to the shade and said: My Lord, I am in need of whatever good You have sent down to me.”

This verse concerns Moses after his arrival in Midian.

He has left Egypt and finds himself in difficult circumstances.

In Al-Qasas 28:23, he sees two women who cannot water their livestock until the shepherds have moved away.

Moses first helps them.

He then withdraws to the shade and expresses his need before The God.

Poverty here is not merely a lack of money

Moses says:

«لِمَا أَنْزَلْتَ إِلَيَّ مِنْ خَيْرٍ فَقِيرٌ»

Li-mā anzalta ilayya min khayrin faqīr.

That is, I am in need of whatever good You have sent down to me.

مِنْ خَيْرٍ gives the request a broad scope.

The expression itself does not establish that Moses wanted only food, only money, or only a spouse.

Someone in such circumstances may need food, security, shelter, work, peace, or other assistance.

Later in the narrative, Moses gains the opportunity to connect with a family in Midian, followed by work and marriage.

These later events, however, should not restrict the initial meaning of khayr in his prayer to just one of them.

Need is compatible with serving others

Moses helps the two women before expressing his own need.

A person can therefore be needy while remaining useful to others.

Poverty does not mean having no ability or social worth.

Someone with little money may possess knowledge, physical strength, skill, or an opportunity that enables them to help another person.

Conversely, helping people does not mean being entirely free of need for others' assistance.

In Moses' story, service and need appear in the same context.

This prevents portraying a poor person solely as a passive recipient of help.

10. “You are those in need of The God”: the fundamental need of all humanity

Fatir 35:15

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّاسُ أَنْتُمُ الْفُقَرَاءُ إِلَى اللَّهِ وَاللَّهُ هُوَ الْغَنِيُّ الْحَمِيدُ

Yā ayyuhā n-nāsu antumu l-fuqarāʾu ilā llāhi wa-llāhu huwa l-ghaniyyu l-ḥamīd.

Translation: “People, you are those in need of The God, and The God is the Self-Sufficient, the Praiseworthy.”

This verse has a broader application than economic poverty.

It addresses all people:

«يَا أَيُّهَا النَّاسُ»

Yā ayyuhā n-nās.

It then says:

«أَنْتُمُ الْفُقَرَاءُ إِلَى اللَّهِ»

Antumu l-fuqarāʾu ilā llāh.

That is, you are those in need of The God.

This includes both financially wealthy and poor people.

What does human need of The God mean?

For life itself, its continuation, their abilities, and their resources, human beings depend on factors not all independently under their control.

Someone may have great wealth, extensive knowledge, or high social standing, yet still need health, air, water, security, and the conditions that sustain life.

Even their wealth cannot eliminate all these needs.

The verse contrasts human need of The God with divine self-sufficiency.

الْغَنِيُّ means the One free of need.

The issue is therefore more than a comparison between two economic situations.

The Qur’an speaks of the creature's fundamental dependence on the Creator and of The God's freedom from need.

Does poverty here mean that human beings are worthless?

No.

Human need of The God does not mean human worthlessness.

Nor does divine self-sufficiency make human effort and responsibility unimportant.

The Qur’an itself speaks of human dignity, knowledge, righteous action, and accountability.

A human being can depend on The God for their creation while also possessing choice, ability, and responsibility in life.

These two concepts are compatible.

Human need and divine self-sufficiency in giving

Muhammad 47:38

هَا أَنْتُمْ هَٰؤُلَاءِ تُدْعَوْنَ لِتُنْفِقُوا فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ فَمِنْكُمْ مَنْ يَبْخَلُ ۖ وَمَنْ يَبْخَلْ فَإِنَّمَا يَبْخَلُ عَنْ نَفْسِهِ ۚ وَاللَّهُ الْغَنِيُّ وَأَنْتُمُ الْفُقَرَاءُ ۚ وَإِنْ تَتَوَلَّوْا يَسْتَبْدِلْ قَوْمًا غَيْرَكُمْ ثُمَّ لَا يَكُونُوا أَمْثَالَكُمْ

Hā antum hāʾulāʾi tudʿawna li-tunfiqū fī sabīli llāhi fa-minkum man yabkhalu wa-man yabkhal fa-innamā yabkhalu ʿan nafsihi wa-llāhu l-ghaniyyu wa-antumu l-fuqarāʾu wa-in tatawallaw yastabdil qawman ghayrakum thumma lā yakūnū amthālakum.

Translation: “Here you are, called to spend in the way of The God, yet some of you withhold. Whoever withholds does so only against themselves. The God is Self-Sufficient, and you are those in need. If you turn away, He will replace you with another people, and they will not be like you.”

Here too, The God is described as ghanī and human beings as fuqarāʾ.

The context, however, concerns giving and miserliness.

This clarifies a point: The God does not ask people for property to meet a personal need.

Giving is an action humans undertake within their own world and society.

Refusing to help the needy or fulfill a proper financial responsibility does not leave The God needy or helpless.

Rather, the person deprives themselves of the opportunity to do good and of its consequences.

“Spend in the way of The God” therefore does not mean that The God needs people's money and possessions like a poor human being.

The verse expressly emphasizes divine self-sufficiency against such a conception.

11. “The God is poor and we are wealthy”: attributing need to The God

Al Imran 3:181

لَقَدْ سَمِعَ اللَّهُ قَوْلَ الَّذِينَ قَالُوا إِنَّ اللَّهَ فَقِيرٌ وَنَحْنُ أَغْنِيَاءُ ۘ سَنَكْتُبُ مَا قَالُوا وَقَتْلَهُمُ الْأَنْبِيَاءَ بِغَيْرِ حَقٍّ وَنَقُولُ ذُوقُوا عَذَابَ الْحَرِيقِ

La-qad samiʿa llāhu qawla lladhīna qālū inna llāha faqīrun wa-naḥnu aghniyāʾu sa-naktubu mā qālū wa-qatlahumu l-anbiyāʾa bi-ghayri ḥaqqin wa-naqūlu dhūqū ʿadhāba l-ḥarīq.

Translation: “The God has heard the speech of those who said: The God is poor and we are wealthy. We shall record what they said and their killing of the Prophets without right, and We shall say: Taste the burning punishment.”

Here, faqīr occurs within the speech of people making a claim about The God.

It reports a condemned claim; it does not endorse divine neediness.

Elsewhere, The God is expressly described as الْغَنِيُّ.

“The God is poor” must therefore not be detached from its original speakers and presented as the Qur’an's description of The God.

Calling for giving differs from divine need

Someone might imagine that if The God calls people to give, He must need their possessions.

The Qur’an, however, distinguishes these matters.

The God asks people to use their property responsibly and help those in need.

This command does not imply divine financial need.

Muhammad 47:38 states this distinction explicitly.

The claim “The God is poor and we are wealthy” thus reverses the relationship between Creator and creature.

Is this claim attributed to every member of a group?

The verse itself says:

«الَّذِينَ قَالُوا»

Alladhīna qālū.

That is, those who said.

The immediate subject is therefore the people who uttered those words.

Without evidence, this claim should not be attributed to every member of a people or all followers of a religion throughout history.

The verse condemns particular speech and conduct.

Each person's responsibility must likewise be assessed in relation to their own speech, actions, and knowledge.

12. Fāqirah: a backbreaking calamity, not financial poverty

Al-Qiyamah 75:25

تَظُنُّ أَنْ يُفْعَلَ بِهَا فَاقِرَةٌ

Taẓunnu an yufʿala bihā fāqirah.

Translation: “It expects that a backbreaking calamity will be inflicted upon it.”

This is the only occurrence of fāqirah in the Qur’an.

The preceding verse, Al-Qiyamah 75:24, speaks of scowling faces.

Al-Qiyamah 75:25 then describes their condition as expecting something devastating.

Fāqirah here denotes a great, backbreaking calamity.

Fāqirah and the backbone

Lexicons describe fāqirah as an affliction that seems to break a person's vertebrae.

This image conveys the calamity's severity.

The word itself, however, does not mean a literal breaking of the back or spine in this verse.

Nor should it be translated as financial poverty.

A person may be economically poor, but fāqirah here does not denote financial deprivation.

The subject is facing a terrifying outcome at the Resurrection.

Does taẓunnu mean certainty?

In Arabic and the Qur’an, ẓann concerns supposition and belief; in certain contexts it can denote a strong belief approaching certainty.

Al-Qiyamah 75:25 speaks of expecting a grave event.

Fāqirah itself, however, means neither certainty nor fear.

Taẓunnu expresses the person's cognitive state and expectation.

Fāqirah identifies the kind of calamity expected.

These two concepts must remain distinct.

13. Comparison with related roots

Faqr and ghinā: ف ق ر and غ ن ي

Ghinā concerns having sufficient resources and being free of need.

Faqr concerns need and insufficient resources.

In economic usage, the two contrast with one another.

In Fatir 35:15, however, divine self-sufficiency is compared with the fundamental need of all humanity.

The meaning of ghinā and faqr must therefore be understood in relation to the subject of each context.

Financial prosperity does not free a person from dependence on The God for existence.

Faqr and maskanah: ف ق ر and س ك ن

Maskanah concerns distress, diminished standing, and need.

Faqr emphasizes insufficient resources and need.

The two may coincide in one person, but are not lexically identical.

Both descriptions appear together in At-Tawbah 9:60.

Their juxtaposition suggests a difference in their fields of application, but the verse itself yields no fixed, definitive ranking of the severity of need.

Faqr and ʿaylah: ف ق ر and ع ي ل

In Qur’anic language, ʿaylah concerns financial hardship and economic need.

At-Tawbah 9:28 mentions fear of poverty.

Ad-Duha 93:8 also says:

«وَوَجَدَكَ عَائِلًا فَأَغْنَىٰ»

Wa-wajadaka ʿāʾilan fa-aghnā.

This describes finding someone in need and then making them free of need.

ʿAylah and faqr are close in the field of economic need, but do not belong to the same root.

Similarity of meaning does not establish identical usage for all their derivatives.

Faqr and imlāq: ف ق ر and م ل ق

In the Qur’an, imlāq concerns poverty and severe financial deprivation.

Al-An’am 6:151 prohibits killing children because of poverty.

Al-Isra 17:31 likewise addresses killing children out of fear of poverty.

These usages show that poverty or fear of it does not authorize violating another person's life.

Imlāq, however, must not be treated as a derivative of ف ق ر.

The roots are independent, although their meanings converge in economic matters.

Faqr and baʾs: ف ق ر and ب أ س

In certain Qur’anic usages, baʾs denotes hardship, severity, and suffering.

Al-Hajj 22:28 uses الْبَائِسَ الْفَقِيرَ.

This expression combines suffering and hardship on one side with need on the other.

A person may be poor without, at that moment, facing another accident or severe affliction.

A wealthy person may likewise experience baʾs through illness or a great calamity.

Suffering and poverty are thus related but distinct concepts.

Faqr and ʿawaz: ف ق ر and ع و ز

In Arabic, ʿawaz concerns deficiency and need.

Its meaning is close to faqr.

The root ع و ز in this form, however, does not occur in the Qur’an.

Faqr, meanwhile, occurs in verses concerning economic need, social position, and fundamental dependence on The God.

ʿAwaz can therefore help explain deficiency generally, but cannot replace examination of the actual Qur’anic forms of ف ق ر.

Faqr and faqd: ف ق ر and ف ق د

Faqd denotes losing something or being deprived of it.

Faqr concerns deficiency and need.

Someone may become poor through losing their possessions.

Not every loss, however, amounts to poverty.

Someone may lose a low-value object while all their needs remain met.

Someone may also have lacked sufficient possessions from the outset, without losing anything in particular.

The roots are therefore close in their letters but differ in meaning and usage.

14. Morphology and syntax

ف ق ر is a sound triliteral root.

All its Qur’anic occurrences are nominal, not verbal.

فَقْر (faqr)

In Al-Baqarah 2:268, الْفَقْرَ is an object of يَعِدُكُمُ.

Satan promises people poverty.

This construction concerns frightening people with poverty.

Faqr itself, however, does not name fear; it is the subject of the frightening promise.

فَقِير (faqīr)

Faqīr follows the pattern فَعِيل (faʿīl).

In economic usage, it describes someone in need.

This Arabic pattern can express an established quality.

The pattern alone, however, does not establish that a person's poverty is permanent or unchangeable.

Someone may be poor for a time and subsequently obtain sufficient resources.

فُقَرَاء (fuqarāʾ)

Fuqarāʾ is the broken plural of faqīr.

Al-Baqarah 2:271 and Al-Baqarah 2:273 introduce needy people in the context of giving.

In At-Tawbah 9:60, they are among the recipients of charitable contributions.

In Al-Hashr 59:8, the term describes emigrants deprived of their homes and possessions.

In Fatir 35:15 and Muhammad 47:38, the same plural expresses humanity's fundamental need of The God.

Contextual meaning thus varies with the subject and sentence relationships, not through a change in the word's root.

فَاقِرَة (fāqirah)

Fāqirah is the feminine of fāqir and follows the pattern فاعلة (fāʿilah).

In Al-Qiyamah 75:25, it functions as a noun for a devastating calamity.

Its meaning here belongs to the root's backbreaking-injury branch.

It must therefore not be treated as the feminine of faqīr.

The feminine of faqīr is فقيرة, not فاقرة.

This morphological distinction matters for the root's accurate presentation in Forqan.

15. Challenges in understanding and translation

The first error is equating poverty with begging.

A faqīr is someone who experiences need and deficiency, not necessarily someone who asks people for money.

Al-Baqarah 2:273 clearly describes needy people considered wealthy because of their restraint.

A second error is assuming that every poor person has no possessions or abilities.

Someone may possess tools, a small home, or a valuable skill, yet still need help to sustain their life.

A third error is treating faqīr and miskīn as identical in every usage, or assigning them a fixed ranking of poverty.

Early lexicons express differing views on the distinction. At-Tawbah 9:60 likewise provides no numerical threshold for either.

A fourth error is humiliating the needy and turning poverty into a sign of moral worthlessness.

The Qur’an speaks of needy people's rights, caring for them, and observing justice.

Poverty does not itself signify sin, laziness, or distance from divine mercy.

A fifth error is treating economic deprivation as inherently sacred.

The Qur’an speaks of helping the needy and attaining sufficiency. A lack of food, housing, or essential resources should therefore not be regarded as a virtue to preserve forever.

A sixth error is equating everyone's need of The God with everyone's financial poverty.

Fatir 35:15 concerns humanity's fundamental dependence on the Creator. This differs from individuals' particular economic conditions.

A seventh error is attributing financial need to The God on the basis of the command to give.

Muhammad 47:38 expressly states that The God is Self-Sufficient and human beings are needy.

An eighth error is translating fāqirah as economic poverty.

Al-Qiyamah 75:25 concerns a backbreaking calamity, not a lack of money.

Finally, the promise of divine bounty does not establish that every needy person will immediately become prosperous without effort, planning, or using real opportunities.

The Qur’an both draws attention to divine bounty and holds humans accountable for action, responsibility, and others' rights.

16. Suggested renderings for Forqan

For economic فَقْر, “poverty,” “need,” and “insufficient means of living” are suitable equivalents.

For فَقِير, “needy” or “poor” is natural.

For فُقَرَاء, “needy people” is more appropriate than an equivalent that portrays them solely as beggars.

In Fatir 35:15 and Muhammad 47:38, however, “in need of The God” should be understood in the broader sense of human dependence.

For فَاقِرَة, “a backbreaking calamity” or “a devastating affliction” is suitable.

Renderings of the main forms

Al-Baqarah 2:268 — الْفَقْرَ

“Satan promises you poverty.”

Al-Baqarah 2:271 — الْفُقَرَاءَ

“If you conceal them and give them to the needy, that is better for you.”

Al-Baqarah 2:273 — لِلْفُقَرَاءِ

“For the needy who are constrained in the way of The God.”

An-Nisa 4:6 — فَقِيرًا

“Whoever is poor may partake according to what is right.”

An-Nisa 4:135 — فَقِيرًا

“Whether someone is wealthy or poor, The God has a greater claim concerning both.”

At-Tawbah 9:60 — لِلْفُقَرَاءِ

“Charitable contributions are only for the needy and the distressed …”

Al-Hajj 22:28 — الْفَقِيرَ

“Feed the distressed, needy person.”

An-Nur 24:32 — فُقَرَاءَ

“If they are needy, The God will enrich them from His bounty.”

Al-Qasas 28:24 — فَقِيرٌ

“My Lord, I am in need of whatever good You have sent down to me.”

Fatir 35:15 — الْفُقَرَاءُ

“People, you are those in need of The God.”

Muhammad 47:38 — الْفُقَرَاءُ

“The God is Self-Sufficient, and you are those in need.”

Al-Hashr 59:8 — لِلْفُقَرَاءِ الْمُهَاجِرِينَ

“For the needy emigrants who were expelled from their homes and possessions.”

Al-Qiyamah 75:25 — فَاقِرَةٌ

“It expects that a backbreaking calamity will be inflicted upon it.”

General root meaning and contextual meanings

Brief root meaning: Need and deficiency contrasted with sufficiency; in another branch, injury to the vertebrae and a backbreaking calamity.

Meaning in verses about giving: A person who needs adequate means of living.

Meaning in Fatir 35:15 and Muhammad 47:38: Humanity's fundamental need of The God, not merely financial poverty.

Meaning in Al-Qiyamah 75:25: A devastating, backbreaking calamity.

The meaning of each form depends on its verse's context; economic need, fundamental dependence, and a backbreaking calamity must remain distinct.

17. Conclusion

The Arabic root ف ق ر concerns need and deficiency; another branch concerns the vertebrae, gaps, and backbreaking injury.

Hatim al-Ta’i's pre-Islamic poetry illustrates the contrast between poverty and prosperity in economic life. Poetry attributed to Tarafah also preserves the usage concerning weakness and injury of the back.

The root appears fourteen times in the Qur’an in three main forms.

Faqr in Al-Baqarah 2:268 denotes poverty in the context of fearing need.

Faqīr and fuqarāʾ in verses about giving, charitable contributions, justice, financial rights, marriage, and migration denote people facing insufficient resources.

In Al-Qasas 28:24, Moses expresses his need for whatever good The God provides for him.

Fatir 35:15 and Muhammad 47:38 use poverty in a broader sense: all humans need The God, whereas The God is Self-Sufficient.

Fāqirah in Al-Qiyamah 75:25 denotes a devastating, backbreaking calamity.

The core of the root's main branch is need and insufficient resources to meet it; its vertebral branch also includes gaps and backbreaking injury.

These meanings are linguistically related, but should not all be reduced to one narrow definition.

The Qur’an also shows that economic poverty does not determine a person's moral condition.

A poor person may be restrained and dignified; someone in need may also help others. A wealthy person may become arrogant because of their possessions or, conversely, use them to serve people.

Need should likewise not become an excuse to violate others' rights or distort the truth in judgment.

In verses about charitable giving, the aim is to recognize need and help people, not to humiliate them or turn poverty into a permanent identity.

Finally, an important distinction remains between two kinds of need:

Economic poverty can be remedied by providing resources; fundamental human need of The God arises from the relationship between creature and Creator.

A person may become financially independent while remaining dependent on The God for existence itself and continued life.

Preserving this distinction clarifies faqīr throughout its Qur’anic usages and prevents confusing financial need, dependence for existence, and a backbreaking calamity.
