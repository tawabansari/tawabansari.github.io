Gesture, conveying meaning without explicit speech, and meaningful movements

Brief root meaning: Signaling and conveying an intention through movements of the lips, eyes, eyebrows, hands, or other body parts instead of ordinary, explicit speech. Early Arabic can also call a very faint, indistinct sound ramz. Other branches concern movement, stirring, and agitation.

1. Introducing the root and its Quranic form

The three-consonant Arabic root R M Z forms a family of words associated with gesture, movement, communication, and sometimes faint sound.

In a familiar use of ramz, someone conveys an intention through the lips, eyes, eyebrows, head, or hands rather than through ordinary explicit speech.

A movement can carry meaning for its recipient: a person may answer with a nod, point in a direction, or draw someone's attention through an eye movement.

Communication takes place in each case, although explicit speech is not necessarily its medium.

Early Arabic also attests ramz for a very faint sound or speech not clearly heard or understood.

The root should therefore not be restricted to wholly soundless gesture.

Lexical records also contain a movement branch. Irtamaza denotes moving or stirring, and katībatun rammāzah describes a force in vigorous motion.

This family is therefore broader than modern senses of symbols and codes.

The root occurs only once in the Quran, in Zechariah's story: his sign is that for three days he will speak to people only by gesture.

The form examined and its Quranic occurrence

رَمْزًا — Āl ʿImrān 3:41

Ramzan is an indefinite verbal noun from ramaza, in the accusative case.

Here it describes Zechariah's way of communicating with people: conveying meaning through gesture instead of ordinary conversation.

Other forms are also known in Arabic:

  • رَمَزَ — ramaza: Signaled or conveyed an intention through movement or a sign.
  • يَرْمِزُ، يَرْمُزُ — yarmizu, yarmuzu: Signals; two attested imperfect forms.
  • رَمْز — ramz: Gesture or signaling, and in some usages very faint speech.
  • رَامِز — rāmiz: Someone conveying an intention by gesture.
  • رَمَّاز، رَمَّازَة — rammāz, rammāzah: Frequent signaling; in another usage, abundant movement.
  • تَرَمَّزَ — tarammaza: Signaled, or in another branch moved or stirred.
  • تَرَامَزَ — tarāmaza: Communicated with one another by signs.
  • ارْتَمَزَ — irtamaza: Moved or stirred, including after a blow or injury.
  • ارْمَأَزَّ — irmaʾazza: Moved or departed in some expressions; staying in place or contracting is also attested, so context is essential.
  • رَمِيز — ramīz: In different lexical usages, someone very active or someone firm and sound in judgment.
  • رَامُوز — rāmūz: An early name for the sea.

These forms establish the range of Arabic usage, but only ramzan occurs in the Quran in the forms listed here.

The Quranic study centers on gesture and communication apart from ordinary speech in Āl ʿImrān 3:41. Other derivatives establish the family's historical and semantic range.

2. The semantic core and early Arabic usage

Movement and agitation: an early lexical analysis

One early lexical analysis proposes a single semantic foundation for R M Z:

أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى حَرَكَةٍ وَاضْطِرَابٍ

aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā ḥarakatin wa-iḍṭirābin

Translation: “A single foundation indicating movement and agitation.”

The analysis illustrates this with an army whose sides move and surge.

It also places irtamaza within the field of movement.

This helps explain relationships among certain derivatives.

Signaling commonly involves movement: someone moves the lips, eyebrows, eyes, or hand to convey meaning.

There is an intelligible connection between movement and gesture.

That connection does not make every bodily movement ramz.

A hand moved without any communicative intention is not necessarily ramz in its communicative sense.

Conversely, a tiny, brief movement may carry a clear meaning for a familiar recipient.

Movement belongs to the root's wider field, but communicative ramz also depends on the movement conveying an intention.

The lexical analysis alone does not prove that all senses historically arose from one specific kind of movement.

What is securely attested is the presence of movement and signaling branches within the Arabic family.

Ramz: signaling with the lips

One principal recorded use is moving the lips without distinctly articulating speech.

Someone may move their lips so that a recipient understands an intention without an ordinary, explicit sentence being uttered.

This differs from ordinary speaking.

Ordinary conversation uses audible words with recognizable pronunciation. Lip signaling may replace part of that speech with movement.

Not every lip movement, however, is ramz.

A movement used as a communicative sign differs from an accidental movement.

The recipient need not identify every word through lip movements; a brief movement may convey the message.

For example, lip and facial movements can ask someone to be quiet or direct their attention elsewhere.

Such examples clarify communicative ramz, but the Quranic context must determine what can be said about the particular gesture in the verse.

Signaling with the eyes, eyebrows, head, and hands

Lexical records do not restrict ramz to lip movement.

Movements of the eyes, eyebrows, head, and hands can also belong to this field.

Ramaza ilayhi describes someone conveying an intention to another person through a sign.

Ramazathu bi-ʿaynihā concerns signaling with the eye.

These usages show that a particular signaling body part is not a fixed element of the root's definition.

The gesture may use the lips, hand, head, or eyes.

They share the use of movement or a sign to communicate meaning.

The general equivalent “gesture” is therefore appropriate in Āl ʿImrān 3:41, which names no particular signaling body part.

Ramz and faint sound

An early lexical account also records this use:

تَصْوِيتٌ خَفِيٌّ بِاللِّسَانِ كَالْهَمْسِ

taṣwītun khafiyyun bi-al-lisāni ka-al-hamsi

Translation: “A faint sound made with the tongue, like a whisper.”

Here, someone may produce a very weak sound unlike ordinary clear speech.

This is close to, but distinct from, silent lip movement.

One involves sound, however faint; the other may involve only visible movement.

The distinction within ramz is therefore not always absolute sound versus absolute silence.

It can concern clear expression versus less explicit speech or gesture.

The existence of the faint-sound sense does not prove that Zechariah was whispering words in Āl ʿImrān 3:41.

His communication must be understood through the relevant verses of the story.

Rammāzah: frequent signaling and vigorous movement

Arabic sources attest rammāzah for someone who signals frequently with the eyes or other body parts.

Some social usages concern particular kinds of signaling between people.

Its moral significance depends on context.

A gesture can convey useful news, offer guidance, or draw attention. Elsewhere it can serve mockery, deception, or improper communication.

R M Z itself therefore does not mean deception, wrongdoing, or trickery.

In the movement branch, katībatun rammāzah describes a large military force whose many members cause its different parts to move and surge.

Here rammāzah describes the crowd's motion, rather than soldiers exchanging coded messages.

A single word form can thus occur in branches related in form but different in function.

Irtamaza: moving and stirring

Arabic irtamaza concerns movement or stirring.

Irtamaza mina al-ḍarbah describes someone stirring or convulsing after a blow.

The motion may be a bodily response to pain or injury.

This differs from ramz as communicative gesture.

Someone moving in response to a blow is not necessarily trying to communicate.

Not every use of irtamaza can therefore be translated as signaling.

This usage also supports the lexical observation that movement and agitation occur within the family.

Ramz as a sign and in modern terminology

Modern Persian and Arabic give ramz a broad range of uses.

It can be a conventional symbol, such as a mathematical sign, a map symbol, or a scientific notation.

A password is likewise a string of signs used for identification or access.

In literature, a symbol can be an image or word representing something beyond its surface meaning.

These usages have an intelligible connection with conveying meaning through signs.

Modern technical senses should not be projected back onto the Quran.

Ramzan in Āl ʿImrān 3:41 does not mean a password, an encrypted message, or a symbol containing a hidden scientific meaning.

It describes communication with people that replaces ordinary speech.

A sign being ramz does not necessarily make it secret.

A gesture may be understood by everyone present, or only by someone already familiar with it.

Secrecy and the need for a decoding key are not fixed elements of the root's meaning.

3. Early poetry and the semantic range of ramz

Ramz and subdued speech in an early witness

An exegetical and lexical tradition attributes the following verse to Juʾayyah ibn ʿĀʾidh:

وَكَانَ تَكَلُّمُ الْأَبْطَالِ رَمْزًا
وَهَمْهَمَةً لَهُمْ مِثْلَ الْهَدِيرِ

wa-kāna takallumu al-abṭāli ramzan / wa-hamhamatan lahum mithla al-hadīri

Translation: “The warriors' speech was subdued signaling, and their murmuring resembled a rumbling roar.”

Ramz occurs beside hamhamah, murmuring.

Hamhamah is audible sound whose words may not be distinct.

The pairing shows that ramz here is not confined to hand gestures: subdued expression or communication approaching a murmur is also present.

Early works transmit this witness, but its attribution and date are not secure enough to identify it categorically as pre-Islamic poetry.

Its value lies in attesting ramz for subdued speech, rather than establishing the exact date when that sense arose.

Finger gestures in al-Ṭirimmāḥ's poetry

A poem by al-Ṭirimmāḥ ibn Ḥakīm al-Ṭāʾī states:

إِذَا مَا رَآهُ الْكَاشِحُونَ تَرَمَّزُوا
حِذَارًا وَأَوْمَوْا كُلُّهُمْ بِالْأَنَامِلِ

idhā mā raʾāhu al-kāshiḥūna tarammazū / ḥidhāran wa-awmaw kulluhum bi-al-anāmili

Translation: “When his enemies see him, they signal to one another in fear, all gesturing with their fingertips.”

Tarammazū occurs beside awmaw.

Īmāʾ concerns gesturing, and bi-al-anāmil identifies the fingertips as its instrument here.

The verse shows that ramz can involve a visible bodily movement without an uttered sentence.

The enemies' gestures accompany fear and apprehension.

Fear is not part of the lexical meaning of tarammazū; it comes from ḥidhāran and the poem's context.

Al-Ṭirimmāḥ belongs to the Umayyad period, so this is early Arabic evidence after Quranic revelation, rather than pre-Quranic evidence.

Rammāzah: an army's movement in Sāʿidah ibn Juʾayyah's poetry

A poem attributed to Sāʿidah ibn Juʾayyah al-Hudhalī states:

تَحْمِيهِمْ شَهْبَاءُ ذَاتُ قَوَانِسٍ
رَمَّازَةٌ تَأْبَى لَهُمْ أَنْ يُحْرَبُوا

taḥmīhim shahbāʾu dhātu qawānisin / rammāzatun taʾbā lahum an yuḥrabū

Approximate translation: “A gray-hued army with helmets protects them, surging in motion and refusing to let them be plundered.”

Rammāzah describes the army.

Lexical explanations connect it with the movement and surging of the army's sides because of its numerous members.

It does not mean that the army communicates through secret codes.

The word belongs here to the movement and agitation branch.

Sāʿidah is generally regarded as a poet who lived across the transition to Islam. The date of this verse is not independently established, so it should not be assigned unconditionally to the pre-Islamic period.

Together, these witnesses illustrate three uses: subdued speech, bodily gesture, and the motion of a large group.

They do not prove that all three necessarily developed from one historical image.

4. Quranic usage: illā ramzan in Zechariah's story

Āl ʿImrān 3:41

Verse:

قَالَ رَبِّ اجْعَلْ لِي آيَةً ۖ قَالَ آيَتُكَ أَلَّا تُكَلِّمَ النَّاسَ ثَلَاثَةَ أَيَّامٍ إِلَّا رَمْزًا ۗ وَاذْكُرْ رَبَّكَ كَثِيرًا وَسَبِّحْ بِالْعَشِيِّ وَالْإِبْكَارِ

qāla rabbi ijʿal lī āyatan qāla āyatuka allā tukallima al-nāsa thalāthata ayyāmin illā ramzan wa-udhkur rabbaka kathīran wa-sabbiḥ bi-al-ʿashiyyi wa-al-ibkāri

Āl ʿImrān 3:41

Translation:

“He said, ‘My Lord, appoint a sign for me.’ He said, ‘Your sign is that for three days you will not speak to people except by gesture. Remember your Lord abundantly and glorify Him in the evening and early morning.’”

The meaning of ramzan in the verse

Here ramzan is a verbal noun in the accusative case.

It denotes signaling and conveying an intention without ordinary conversation.

It follows illā, which introduces an exception.

Allā tukallima al-nāsa concerns not speaking to people.

The construction means that for three days Zechariah will not converse normally with people, communicating instead through gesture.

This agrees with the attested Arabic meaning of ramz.

The verse does not specify whether his gesture used the eyes, lips, eyebrows, head, or hands.

“Gesture” is therefore more precise than translations that supply a specific body part without evidence.

Is gesture a kind of speech?

Ramz appears as an exception to speaking to people.

This structure matters for the meaning.

If speaking means ordinary explicit utterance, gesture is a different act.

If speaking is understood more broadly as conveying a message, gesture can belong to communication with others.

Early grammatical analysis accordingly discusses whether the exception is connected or disconnected.

On one analysis, ramz is not of the same kind as ordinary speech, making the exception different in kind.

On another, because ramz conveys an intention, it can be included within the broader field of communication.

This difference does not overturn the verse's clear sense.

On either reading, ordinary conversation is limited while gesture remains available.

The sentence should therefore not be understood as a loss of every means of communication. A gesture can be entirely soundless while still conveying meaning.

The restriction concerns speaking to people

In Āl ʿImrān 3:41, the restriction concerns al-nās, people.

It is conversation with people that is at issue.

The same verse continues:

وَاذْكُرْ رَبَّكَ كَثِيرًا

wa-udhkur rabbaka kathīran

Āl ʿImrān 3:41

“Remember your Lord abundantly.”

It then commands glorification in the evening and early morning.

The restriction on ordinary communication with people does not sever Zechariah's relationship with his Lord.

He remains commanded to remember and glorify Him.

The verse itself does not specify exactly how remembrance was performed; remembrance may take different forms.

This sentence alone does not prove either that Zechariah's tongue functioned normally during remembrance or that all his remembrance was silent.

What is clear is the distinction between restricted conversation and continued remembrance of The God.

A sign for Zechariah

In Āl ʿImrān 3:38, Zechariah asks his Lord for good offspring.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:39, the angels announce John to him.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:40, he asks how he can have a child given his age and his wife's barrenness.

After the divine answer, Āl ʿImrān 3:41 records his request for a sign.

The restriction on speech is connected with the promised child and the appointed sign.

His request does not by itself establish that he sinned by asking and was being punished.

Some early exegetical reports offer that reading, but the verse does not itself label his request a sin or disobedience.

Nor does requesting a sign or greater reassurance always signify denial of truth in the Quran.

For example, Al-Baqarah 2:260 records Abraham asking to see how the dead are brought to life, distinguishing faith from greater reassurance of the heart.

Zechariah's story should likewise be described in the Quran's own terms: he requested a sign, and one was appointed.

The exact bodily condition and reasons beyond what the verses state should not be settled through an unsupported narrative.

5. Connection with Maryam: gesture in action

Maryam 19:10

Verse:

قَالَ رَبِّ اجْعَلْ لِي آيَةً ۚ قَالَ آيَتُكَ أَلَّا تُكَلِّمَ النَّاسَ ثَلَاثَ لَيَالٍ سَوِيًّا

qāla rabbi ijʿal lī āyatan qāla āyatuka allā tukallima al-nāsa thalātha layālin sawiyyan

Maryam 19:10

Translation:

“He said, ‘My Lord, appoint a sign for me.’ He said, ‘Your sign is that for three nights you will not speak to people, while sound.’”

This records the same request in Maryam.

Instead of thalāthata ayyām, three days, it uses thalātha layālin, three nights.

One counts the period by days and the other by nights.

Arabic can name an associated period by its days or nights, so these need not describe two different durations.

Maryam also includes sawiyyan.

On a familiar grammatical reading, this describes Zechariah's condition as sound and healthy.

His restricted speech should therefore not be attributed without evidence to an ordinary illness or established bodily defect.

Some early analyses understand sawiyyan as three consecutive nights; describing Zechariah as sound nevertheless fits the sentence and its context as a sign.

Was Zechariah unable to speak?

Āl ʿImrān 3:41 and Maryam 19:10 present his condition as a special sign.

Some commentators understand a temporary inability to converse normally despite bodily soundness.

Other readings emphasize abstaining from speech as the sign; the distinction between voluntary abstention and inability is an interpretive question, not the lexical meaning of ramz.

The verses establish restricted ordinary communication and the possibility of gesture; Zechariah's soundness follows the familiar reading of sawiyyan.

The exact bodily mechanism cannot be determined from R M Z itself.

The root describes the alternative communication.

Ramz should not be translated as stuttering, muteness, a neurological disorder, or permanent inability to speak.

Maryam 19:11

Verse:

فَخَرَجَ عَلَىٰ قَوْمِهِ مِنَ الْمِحْرَابِ فَأَوْحَىٰ إِلَيْهِمْ أَنْ سَبِّحُوا بُكْرَةً وَعَشِيًّا

fa-kharaja ʿalā qawmihi mina al-miḥrābi fa-awḥā ilayhim an sabbiḥū bukratan wa-ʿashiyyan

Maryam 19:11

Translation:

“He came out from the sanctuary to his people and signaled to them to glorify in the morning and evening.”

This is important evidence for ramz in Āl ʿImrān 3:41.

After leaving the sanctuary, Zechariah conveys a message to his people.

The verb is fa-awḥā ilayhim.

Awḥā belongs to W Ḥ Y, which has varied Quranic uses.

Here the subject is Zechariah conveying a message to his people.

Given the preceding restriction on ordinary conversation, this could take place through gesture and signs.

Exegetical discussions also mention writing the message.

The verse itself does not specify the instrument of communication.

Awḥā here concerns conveying a message; the preceding restriction suggests communication apart from ordinary speech. That inference comes from context, rather than making every use of awḥā mean bodily gesture.

The connection shows that limited speech did not prevent him from conveying his intention.

He could make his people understand that they should glorify in the morning and evening.

Distinguishing ramz and waḥy in this story

Ramz and waḥy belong to different roots.

Ramz in Āl ʿImrān 3:41 describes communication replacing ordinary conversation.

Awḥā in Maryam 19:11 expresses the conveying of a message.

The two can concern the same event: Zechariah conveys a message through gesture.

Not every Quranic use of waḥy is therefore restricted to bodily signaling.

Waḥy has a wider field, including divine communication, inspiration, and other kinds of conveying messages.

Conversely, ramz in the present verse is not itself a name for divine revelation.

The distinction allows each word to be understood in its own role.

Mary's gesture in Maryam 19:29

Mary's story also contains a clear instance of gesture.

Maryam 19:29

Verse:

فَأَشَارَتْ إِلَيْهِ ۖ قَالُوا كَيْفَ نُكَلِّمُ مَنْ كَانَ فِي الْمَهْدِ صَبِيًّا

fa-ashārat ilayhi qālū kayfa nukallimu man kāna fī al-mahdi ṣabiyyan

Maryam 19:29

Translation:

“She pointed to him. They said, ‘How can we speak to someone who is a child in the cradle?’”

Mary points to the child.

Fa-ashārat belongs to Sh W R, not R M Z.

As an act of communication, it clearly illustrates gesture in place of speech.

Mary directs her listeners' attention to the child, and they understand an intention from her gesture.

Nonverbal communication can therefore carry a clear message.

Mary's story and Zechariah's nevertheless describe different circumstances.

Maryam 19:26 connects Mary's not speaking to people with a vow of ṣawm; Zechariah's restricted conversation is a sign connected with the announcement of a child.

Their shared use of gesture should not erase the differences between their situations.

6. Related roots and translation questions

R M Z (ramz) and Sh W R (ishārah)

Arabic ishārah concerns showing or directing through a sign or movement.

Quranic ramz also denotes a kind of gesture.

Maryam 19:29 uses ashārat for Mary's pointing to the child, not for Mary herself moving toward him.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:41, ramzan describes Zechariah's communication with people.

The words overlap in nonverbal communication.

There is no absolute rule that ishārah must always be public and ramz always concealed.

A gesture may be conspicuous or meaningful only to a particular recipient.

The distinction in each usage depends on context.

R M Z (ramz) and Gh M Z (ghamz)

Arabic ghamz can concern signaling with the eyes, eyebrows, or eyelids.

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:30 uses yataghāmazūn.

The signaling there takes place in a context of mocking believers.

Ramz can also use the eyes and eyebrows, but is not limited to them.

Ghamz also has other physical senses, such as pressing.

Their shared use for eye gestures does not make them historically or semantically identical.

Mockery in Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:30 comes from the conduct described in context; not every eye gesture is inherently mocking.

R M Z (ramz) and L M Z (lamz)

Quranic lamz concerns faultfinding and disparagement.

Al-Tawbah 9:58 describes people finding fault over charitable distributions.

Lexicons sometimes connect lamz with gestures or subdued speech.

Similarity in how an act is performed does not equate the roots' meanings.

Quranic ramz describes a means of communication.

Lamz in its relevant verses expresses disparaging faultfinding.

Not every gesture can therefore be called lamz.

R M Z (ramz) and H M Z (hamz)

Arabic hamz concerns pressing or impelling, and in social uses disparagement.

Al-Humazah 104:1 contains humazatin lumazah.

Lexical accounts show overlap between hamz and lamz, sometimes distinguishing open from concealed disparagement.

That division is not absolute across every usage.

Ramz can communicate through movement or subdued sound, without itself meaning disparagement.

Ramzan in Āl ʿImrān 3:41 should therefore not be translated as biting speech, contempt, or disparagement.

R M Z (ramz) and H M S (hams)

Hams concerns very faint sound.

Ṭā Hā 20:108 uses hamsan.

Some lexical uses of ramz also approach faint sound.

Communicative ramz can, however, occur entirely through movement without producing a sound.

The familiar use of hams concerns faint sound or a whisper.

This is a tendency rather than an absolute boundary: Arabic usage can overlap in the fields of faint sound and gesture.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:41, illā ramzan, together with Maryam 19:11, supports the general translation “by gesture.”

R M Z (ramz) and R Q M (raqm)

Arabic raqm concerns writing, marking, and recording signs on a surface.

Ramz can also convey meaning, but need not be written or recorded.

Zechariah communicates through ramz in Āl ʿImrān 3:41; the word itself does not prove that he wrote anything.

By contrast, kitābun marqūm in Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:9 and Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:20 concerns a recorded writing.

Writing uses signs, but not every gesture or symbol is written.

Similar consonants also do not establish a historical relationship between the roots.

R M Z (ramz) and R M S (rams)

Arabic rams concerns covering and concealment, especially covering something with earth.

Some usages extend this family to concealing news.

Ramz does not itself mean concealment.

A person may convey a message openly through gesture.

Secret encoding and hiding information are particular extended uses of the concept, rather than its necessary Quranic meaning.

Āl ʿImrān 3:41 concerns communicating with people in place of ordinary conversation, not burying, covering, or concealing a message.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan and conclusions

Translating the Quranic form

رَمْزًا — Āl ʿImrān 3:41

Primary equivalent: “gesture.”

Possible explanatory renderings: “by gesture,” “by signs,” or “through signaling.”

In the full construction:

أَلَّا تُكَلِّمَ النَّاسَ ثَلَاثَةَ أَيَّامٍ إِلَّا رَمْزًا

allā tukallima al-nāsa thalāthata ayyāmin illā ramzan

Āl ʿImrān 3:41

Suggested translation:

“For three days you will not speak to people except by gesture.”

The supplied rendering likewise uses “by gesture,” a lexically defensible choice.

This preserves the established sense, avoids restricting the gesture to one body part without evidence, and excludes modern technical senses of encoding.

“By secret code,” “with coded words,” and “through encrypted messages” are unsuitable here.

“By whispering” reflects one lexical use, but the context of Zechariah's story and Maryam 19:11 favors the clearer “by gesture.”

A proposed Forqan word box

Word: رَمْزًا

Root: R M Z

Brief root meaning: Gesture and communication through movements of the lips, eyes, eyebrows, hands, or a sign; in some usages, very faint sound.

Meaning in this verse: Zechariah communicating with people by gesture instead of ordinary conversation during the three days appointed as his sign.

The full article can explain movement, an army's motion, movement caused by a blow, and later technical uses.

The verse box should focus on Zechariah's communication with people. These proposals do not replace existing authored explanations in verse boxes.

Conclusions and final definition

Arabic R M Z contains a family of senses concerning gesture, movement, and communication.

Communicative ramz can be a movement of the lips, eyes, eyebrows, head, or hands conveying an intention.

Some usages also denote very faint, subdued sound.

Lexical records additionally attest movement and agitation: irtamaza can mean to stir, and katībatun rammāzah describes an army in vigorous motion.

One early analysis places movement and agitation at the center of the family. This can explain some derivatives, but does not make every bodily movement ramz or establish a single signaling act as the origin of all usages.

Early poetry reflects the range: one witness pairs ramz with murmuring, al-Ṭirimmāḥ depicts finger gestures, and the verse attributed to Sāʿidah uses rammāzah for an army's movement.

The root occurs only once in the Quran: ramzan in Āl ʿImrān 3:41.

In response to his request for a sign, Zechariah is placed in a condition in which for three days he communicates with people only through gesture.

Maryam 19:10 describes the condition using three nights and sawiyyan.

Maryam 19:11 records him conveying a message to his people: communication remains possible despite restricted ordinary conversation.

Ramz here therefore does not mean a complete breakdown of communication or a secret coded language; the gesture itself may nevertheless be entirely silent.

Final definition of R M Z:

This family has two important fields:

1. Signaling and conveying an intention through movement or a sign instead of ordinary explicit speech. It can involve the lips, eyes, eyebrows, head, hands, or very faint sound.

2. Movement and agitation, as when a person stirs or a large group surges.

Their connection with movement does not make every detail identical.

The Quranic usage belongs to the first field.

Ramzan in Āl ʿImrān 3:41 therefore means “by gesture.”

It describes communication that does not require ordinary speech but still enables understanding.

The root alone does not establish Zechariah's exact gesture, a medical condition, the secrecy of the message, or a motive beyond what the Quran states.

The appropriate contextual core for Forqan is “conveying meaning through gesture in place of explicit speech.”

This preserves the attested Arabic meaning and distinguishes movement, signaling, subdued speech, and modern technical encoding.
