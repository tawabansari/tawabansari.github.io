Stepwise movement, steps, and rank: degrees and istidrāj in the Qur’an

1. Introducing the root and its semantic field

The Arabic triliteral root D R J concerns movement, traversing a path, passing between stages, and successive levels. This field includes darajah, a step or rank; tadarruj, proceeding in stages; and istidrāj, progressively leading someone onward.

Early Arabic uses daraja for a child’s first walking, following a route, and people or generations passing away. A darajah is also a step connecting levels; this image extends to differences of social or conceptual standing.

Movement, steps, and rank are nevertheless distinct. Movement need not ascend; steps allow ascent or descent; rank positions someone relative to others in a particular order.

The Qur’an uses two principal branches: darajah/darajāt and the verb sa-nastadrijuhum.

Degrees concern people, prophets, worldly circumstances, deeds, and standing before The God. Istidrāj, however, concerns deniers progressively led toward an unfavorable outcome through a course they do not perceive.

These branches are linguistically related without identical moral value. Higher standing in one respect need not imply greater moral worth; gradual advancement need not constitute improvement or approach to good.

Research should therefore distinguish gradual movement, rank, worldly and otherworldly differences, and istidrāj as an unfavorable process within its particular Qur’anic contexts.

2. Qur’anic forms and early Arabic meaning

D R J occurs twenty times in twenty Qur’anic verses, in two principal morphological families.

Darajah and darajāt: the noun meaning a step or rank and its plural; eighteen occurrences, comprising four singulars and fourteen plurals.

These appear singular and plural, definite and indefinite, with different cases, including darajatun, darajatan, darajātin, darajātun, and al-darajātu.

Such differences concern grammar and number rather than independent derivation.

Sa-nastadrijuhum: a Form X imperfect verb with future sa- and object pronoun hum; twice, in Al-Aʿrāf 7:182 and Al-Qalam 68:44.

Arabic also records the following forms:

دَرَجَ يَدْرُجُ: he walked, moved, or followed his route.

دَرْج and دُرُوج: movement and traversing a route; in some uses, passing or elapsing.

دَرَجَة: a step or rank.

دَرَج: steps, levels, or a path of movement, according to construction and context.

مَدْرَج and مَدْرَجَة: a road, passage, or place of movement.

دَرَّجَ: to advance something in stages or give it degrees.

تَدَرَّجَ: to proceed gradually or pass between levels.

اِسْتَدْرَجَ: to lead someone or something progressively onward or toward a condition.

أَدْرَجَ: to wrap, enclose, or insert one thing within another.

دُرْج: a receptacle or place for keeping something.

Daraja, darraja, tadarraja, and adraja do not occur in these forms in the Qur’an. Nor do the verbal nouns tadarruj and istidrāj; the latter family appears as sa-nastadrijuhum.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: eighteen nouns and two verbs

The semantic core in Maqāyīs al-Lughah

Ibn Fāris identifies this principle:

أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى مُضِيِّ الشَّيْءِ وَالْمُضِيِّ فِي الشَّيْءِ

aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā muḍiyyi al-shayʾi wa-al-muḍiyyi fī al-shayʾi

“One principle denoting something passing onward and proceeding through something.”

This begins with movement and progression rather than social standing or moral rank.

Ibn Fāris illustrates it through a child walking, traversing a path, and people passing away.

He nevertheless suggests that durj as a receptacle and idrāj as wrapping may concern another principle of covering or enclosing.

This caution matters: words collected in one dictionary entry should not all derive from one physical image without evidence.

Lisān al-ʿArab explains darajah as both a step and standing, while darj concerns human and animal movement, paths, generations passing, and enclosing one thing within another.

Al-Rāghib distinguishes martabah and darajah in Al-Mufradāt: darajah is a rank understood through ascent, like ladder steps.

That clarifies the connection with levels without making every use of D R J necessarily upward movement.

3. Tangible uses in early Arabic

A child walking and following a route

Daraja al-ṣabiyyu describes a child beginning to walk.

It concerns movement step by step: the child has begun to progress without yet walking like an experienced, capable person.

Dictionaries also record the verb for an elderly person’s movement.

Moving along a route is therefore one tangible setting for this family.

The child’s movement should not define every derivative. Human degrees in the verses concern standing rather than bodily walking.

Steps and successive levels

Darajah can name a step used to go up or down.

Steps together form successive levels.

The same relationship enables describing different positions through darajah.

Two people may have differing degrees within a system without physically standing on a building’s stairs.

Not every physical property of a step transfers with this usage.

Social or moral degrees need not have measurable height or equal intervals.

The step image illuminates differentiated ranks without establishing a definite numerical or geometrical structure.

A route and returning along it

Rajaʿa adrājahu means returning by the route one came.

The pre-Islamic poet Salāmah ibn Jandal says:

وَكَرُّنَا خَيْلَنَا أَدْرَاجَهَا رُجُعًا
كُسَّ السَّنَابِكِ مِنْ بَدْءٍ وَتَعْقِيبِ

wa-karrunā khaylanā adrājahā rujuʿan / kussa al-sanābiki min badʾin wa-taʿqībi

“And we turned our horses back along their tracks, their hooves worn by advancing and returning.”

Adrājahā concerns the horses’ route and return.

This shows that the family is not confined to upward movement; returning along a previous route also belongs here. Lisān al-ʿArab transmits adrājanā as a variant of adrājahā. The supplied wording agrees with Al-Mufaḍḍaliyyāt and is retained here.

Al-Mufaḍḍaliyyāt: Salāmah ibn Jandal’s witness

Not every form should consequently become ascent or improvement.

Generations passing and life ending

Daraja al-qawmu can describe people who have died or whose lineage has ended.

Qabīlah dārijah can likewise describe a tribe whose traces and descendants have disappeared.

Passing and elapsing can explain its connection with movement.

People pass through life and generations come and go successively.

Darj does not, however, intrinsically mean death. Death is a particular use identified by the sentence’s subject.

Wrapping and enclosing

Adraja al-kitāba concerns rolling or folding a written document.

Adraja al-shayʾa fī al-shayʾi concerns enclosing or incorporating one thing within another.

Later Arabic similarly uses it for including something in a larger collection.

Ibn Fāris treats this branch’s historical relationship with movement and following a route cautiously.

Gradual wrapping and layering may resemble staged movement without proving common historical origin.

The Qur’an does not use the branch concerning wrapping documents or including one thing within another.

Qur’anic study should therefore focus on degrees and istidrāj, without importing every non-Qur’anic meaning.

4. Darajah and darajāt: differences of standing

The Qur’anic noun expresses differences of position, standing, or rank.

These may concern deeds and otherworldly reward, prophets and other people, or social conditions and worldly life.

Darajah does not itself name a reward or spiritual station.

One context may concern good standing before The God, another differing circumstances or resources.

Prophets’ differing standing: Al-Baqarah 2:253

Al-Baqarah 2:253 says:

تِلْكَ الرُّسُلُ فَضَّلْنَا بَعْضَهُمْ عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ

tilka al-rusulu faḍḍalnā baʿḍahum ʿalā baʿḍin

“Those messengers: We favored some over others.”

The verse then mentions The God speaking with some and raising others in degrees.

Darajāt concerns different standing among prophets.

The noun alone does not determine whether mission, responsibility, signs, standing before The God, or some combination explains the difference.

Those questions require the full construction and related verses.

The plural also supplies neither a fixed number of prophetic levels nor a definitive ranking list.

It conveys differing standing without containing all criteria for that difference.

Degrees and knowledge: Al-Anʿām 6:83 and Yūsuf 12:76

After mentioning the proof given to Abraham, Al-Anʿām 6:83 says:

نَرْفَعُ دَرَجَاتٍ مَنْ نَشَاءُ

narfaʿu darajātin man nashāʾu

“We raise whom We will in degrees.”

Abraham’s standing is connected here with proof and divine knowledge.

Yūsuf 12:76 repeats the expression and adds:

وَفَوْقَ كُلِّ ذِي عِلْمٍ عَلِيمٌ

wa-fawqa kulli dhī ʿilmin ʿalīmun

“Above every possessor of knowledge is one who knows more.”

The proximity of degrees and knowledge matters. The reading with tanwīn has darajātin; Al-Qurṭubī also records the possessive construction darajāti man nashāʾu in these passages in Al-Anʿām and Yūsuf. The former raises persons; the latter raises their degrees. The syntactic difference preserves the connection between rank and elevation.

This association does not make darajah lexically mean knowledge.

Knowledge, proof, and wisdom can affect standing, while darajah itself conveys differences of rank.

This matters in claims of intellectual superiority: actual knowledge may relate to higher standing, but claiming knowledge or an academic title does not itself conclusively establish standing before The God.

Al-Qurṭubī: tanwīn and possessive readings of narfaʿu darajāt

Degrees and deeds: Al-Nisāʾ 4:95 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:96

Al-Nisāʾ 4:95 distinguishes those striving with wealth and lives in The God’s path from those not participating in that situation.

It states:

فَضَّلَ اللَّهُ الْمُجَاهِدِينَ بِأَمْوَالِهِمْ وَأَنْفُسِهِمْ عَلَى الْقَاعِدِينَ دَرَجَةً

faḍḍala Allāhu al-mujāhidīna bi-amwālihim wa-anfusihim ʿalā al-qāʿidīna darajatan

“The God has favored those striving with their wealth and lives over those remaining seated by a degree.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:96 then speaks of darajātin minhu.

Darajah can thus express differences of reward or standing based on action.

Verse 95 importantly qualifies the comparison with ghayru ulī al-ḍarar, distinguishing those with impairments or impediments.

The difference should not become a simple judgment of everyone who did not participate in a particular activity.

Circumstances, ability, responsibility, and the action’s nature matter contextually.

Darajah does not itself mean warfare or striving; independent words and constructions provide those meanings.

Degrees of deeds: Al-Anʿām 6:132 and Al-Aḥqāf 46:19

Al-Anʿām 6:132

وَلِكُلٍّ دَرَجَاتٌ مِمَّا عَمِلُوا وَمَا رَبُّكَ بِغَافِلٍ عَمَّا يَعْمَلُونَ

wa-li-kullin darajātun mimmā ʿamilū wa-mā rabbuka bi-ghāfilin ʿammā yaʿmalūna

“For each are degrees according to what they have done; your Lord is not unaware of what they do.”

Al-Aḥqāf 46:19 uses the same central construction:

وَلِكُلٍّ دَرَجَاتٌ مِمَّا عَمِلُوا

wa-li-kullin darajātun mimmā ʿamilū

“For each are degrees according to what they have done.”

Their relationship with deeds is explicit in both passages.

Degree here concerns evaluation and differing standing according to conduct.

Darajāt should not thereby belong exclusively to the virtuous.

Both surrounding passages describe groups with different deeds and outcomes, allowing differing levels within judgment.

Conduct and context identify someone’s superiority or inferiority, rather than the plural darajāt alone.

Hum darajātun ʿinda Allāh: Āl ʿImrān 3:163

Āl ʿImrān 3:163

هُمْ دَرَجَاتٌ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ وَاللَّهُ بَصِيرٌ بِمَا يَعْمَلُونَ

hum darajātun ʿinda Allāhi wa-Allāhu baṣīrun bi-mā yaʿmalūna

“They have differing degrees before The God; The God sees what they do.”

This follows a comparison between someone pursuing The God’s pleasure and someone incurring His anger.

Differing degrees concern people’s orientations and deeds here.

Arabic hum darajātun literally says they are degrees; natural Persian more clearly conveys differing degrees of standing.

It should not imply that people physically turn into stairs.

Darajah here names rank and standing.

Good standing before The God

Al-Anfāl 8:4 describes believers with particular qualities:

لَهُمْ دَرَجَاتٌ عِنْدَ رَبِّهِمْ

lahum darajātun ʿinda rabbihim

“They have degrees before their Lord.”

Ṭā Hā 20:75 similarly grants al-darajāt al-ʿulā to those arriving as believers with righteous deeds.

Al-Tawbah 9:20 describes greater standing for those believing, migrating, and striving with wealth and lives. Al-Ḥadīd 57:10 also grants greater standing to those who spent and fought before the opening than to those doing so afterward; timing and circumstances matter in this comparison.

These concern good standing before The God.

Their positive value comes from context, deeds, and accompanying descriptions. D R J alone means neither faith, piety, nor reward.

Otherworldly degrees: Al-Isrāʾ 17:21

Al-Isrāʾ 17:21

انْظُرْ كَيْفَ فَضَّلْنَا بَعْضَهُمْ عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ وَلَلْآخِرَةُ أَكْبَرُ دَرَجَاتٍ وَأَكْبَرُ تَفْضِيلًا

unẓur kayfa faḍḍalnā baʿḍahum ʿalā baʿḍin wa-la-al-ākhiratu akbaru darajātin wa-akbaru tafḍīlan

“Look how We favored some over others; the hereafter is greater in degrees and greater in preference.”

The verse distinguishes worldly differences from otherworldly levels.

Worldly differences may involve resources, opportunities, and benefits, while the verse declares otherworldly degrees greater.

Worldly standing should not become the final measure of worth before The God.

Greater wealth, influence, or social position does not itself establish superiority in the hereafter.

The contrast comes from context, without making darajah two unrelated lexical meanings.

5. Worldly degrees: standing, resources, and responsibility

Al-Anʿām 6:165: degrees as a setting for testing

Al-Anʿām 6:165 says:

وَهُوَ الَّذِي جَعَلَكُمْ خَلَائِفَ الْأَرْضِ وَرَفَعَ بَعْضَكُمْ فَوْقَ بَعْضٍ دَرَجَاتٍ لِيَبْلُوَكُمْ فِي مَا آتَاكُمْ

wa-huwa alladhī jaʿalakum khalāʾifa al-arḍi wa-rafaʿa baʿḍakum fawqa baʿḍin darajātin li-yabluwakum fī mā ātākum

“He made you successors on earth and raised some above others in degrees, to test you through what He has given you.”

Differing degrees accompany li-yabluwakum fī mā ātākum.

Greater resources or higher standing therefore become a setting for testing.

This can concern people’s differing opportunities and responsibilities.

Darajāt does not establish that every wealthier, more powerful, or socially prominent person is better before The God.

The verse connects different situations with testing rather than guaranteed moral merit.

Those with greater resources are accountable for their use; deprivation or limited means likewise does not intrinsically signify lesser human worth.

Al-Zukhruf 43:32: differing social degrees

Al-Zukhruf 43:32

أَهُمْ يَقْسِمُونَ رَحْمَتَ رَبِّكَ نَحْنُ قَسَمْنَا بَيْنَهُمْ مَعِيشَتَهُمْ فِي الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا وَرَفَعْنَا بَعْضَهُمْ فَوْقَ بَعْضٍ دَرَجَاتٍ لِيَتَّخِذَ بَعْضُهُمْ بَعْضًا سُخْرِيًّا وَرَحْمَتُ رَبِّكَ خَيْرٌ مِمَّا يَجْمَعُونَ

a-hum yaqsimūna raḥmata rabbika naḥnu qasamnā baynahum maʿīshatahum fī al-ḥayāti al-dunyā wa-rafaʿnā baʿḍahum fawqa baʿḍin darajātin li-yattakhidha baʿḍuhum baʿḍan sukhriyyan wa-raḥmatu rabbika khayrun mimmā yajmaʿūna

“Do they distribute your Lord’s mercy? We have apportioned their livelihood in worldly life and raised some above others in degrees, so some may employ others in service; your Lord’s mercy is better than what they gather.”

Degrees here concern livelihood and social relationships.

People differ in ability, resources, skills, and position; these differences can enable cooperation and mutual dependence.

Darajāt itself does not mean ownership of one human by another or a right to exploit others.

Li-yattakhidha baʿḍuhum baʿḍan sukhriyyan contributes independently and requires study through S KH R and the verse’s context.

Nor does differentiated livelihood necessarily morally endorse unjust social structures, economic corruption, or depriving people of rights.

Describing differing positions does not authorize every use of power and resources.

The ending also places the Lord’s mercy above the possessions people accumulate.

Worldly advantage is consequently not humanity’s final measure of worth or success.

Degrees and knowledge: Al-Mujādilah 58:11

Al-Mujādilah 58:11 says:

يَرْفَعِ اللَّهُ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا مِنْكُمْ وَالَّذِينَ أُوتُوا الْعِلْمَ دَرَجَاتٍ

yarfaʿi Allāhu alladhīna āmanū minkum wa-alladhīna ūtū al-ʿilma darajātin

“The God raises those among you who believe and those given knowledge in degrees.”

Knowledge appears alongside faith and elevation in degrees.

Knowledge can therefore matter for standing before The God.

Darajah does not itself mean knowledge, however.

An academic title, claimed knowledge, or educational position does not conclusively establish those degrees without examining the knowledge’s reality and its possessor’s conduct.

The verse also concerns believers’ social conduct and making room in gatherings. Knowledge and degrees should therefore remain connected with behavioral and moral responsibility.

Rafīʿ al-darajāt: Ghāfir 40:15

Ghāfir 40:15

رَفِيعُ الدَّرَجَاتِ ذُو الْعَرْشِ يُلْقِي الرُّوحَ مِنْ أَمْرِهِ عَلَىٰ مَنْ يَشَاءُ مِنْ عِبَادِهِ لِيُنْذِرَ يَوْمَ التَّلَاقِ

rafīʿu al-darajāti dhū al-ʿarshi yulqī al-rūḥa min amrihi ʿalā man yashāʾu min ʿibādihi li-yundhira yawma al-talāqi

“Exalted in rank, possessor of the Throne; He casts the spirit by His command upon whom He wills among His servants, to warn of the day of meeting.”

Rafīʿ al-darajāt describes The God here.

Rafīʿ belongs to R F ʿ, while darajāt belongs to D R J.

Two semantic directions have been proposed: exalted divine standing, and The God’s raising degrees.

Darajāt still concerns ranks; deciding whether the whole expression principally describes exalted standing or raising ranks requires its possessive construction and context.

Al-darajāt alone should not become a divine name; it forms part of a descriptive construction.

Nor does the expression establish a definite number of heavenly layers or bodily locations.

6. Wa-li-al-rijāli ʿalayhinna darajah: Al-Baqarah 2:228

This important, disputed use occurs in Al-Baqarah 2:228:

وَلَهُنَّ مِثْلُ الَّذِي عَلَيْهِنَّ بِالْمَعْرُوفِ وَلِلرِّجَالِ عَلَيْهِنَّ دَرَجَةٌ وَاللَّهُ عَزِيزٌ حَكِيمٌ

wa-lahunna mithlu alladhī ʿalayhinna bi-al-maʿrūfi wa-li-al-rijāli ʿalayhinna darajatun wa-Allāhu ʿazīzun ḥakīmun

“Women have rights corresponding fairly to their obligations, and men have a degree over them; The God is mighty and wise.”

Darajatun is singular and indefinite here.

It directly conveys a rank or higher standing within the relationship discussed.

The noun alone does not specify the exact right, responsibility, or authority involved.

Understanding it requires context.

Divorce and reciprocal rights

Al-Baqarah 2:228 concerns divorced women and their waiting period.

It also discusses taking women back within that period, qualified by an intention to reconcile.

Reciprocal rights according to what is recognized as fair precede the mention of a degree for men.

The expression should not be detached from divorce and marital relationships to become an unlimited claim about all men’s intrinsic worth over all women.

Conversely, wa-li-al-rijāli ʿalayhinna darajah should not disappear or be treated as expressing no difference.

The text explicitly names a degree within this relationship.

Research must ask what that degree includes and how far it extends.

Does degree mean men’s intrinsic superiority?

D R J itself says nothing about gender, intellectual capacity, human worth, or a right to rule.

Darajah expresses a difference of rank.

Elsewhere the same noun concerns prophets, deeds, worldly resources, and otherworldly standing.

The noun itself therefore does not establish that every man surpasses every woman in intelligence, piety, character, merit, or human worth.

Such a claim would require independent evidence.

Al-Baqarah 2:228 itself recognizes reciprocal rights through wa-lahunna mithlu alladhī ʿalayhinna bi-al-maʿrūf.

Interpreting degree should not nullify that explicit clause.

Does degree concern taking a spouse back during the divorce waiting period?

The same verse says:

وَبُعُولَتُهُنَّ أَحَقُّ بِرَدِّهِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ إِنْ أَرَادُوا إِصْلَاحًا

wa-buʿūlatuhunna aḥaqqu bi-raddihinna fī dhālika in arādū iṣlāḥan

“Their husbands have greater right to take them back during that period, if they intend reconciliation.”

This is significant contextual evidence for a legal difference.

The degree may relate to the man’s particular authority or position in restoring the marital relationship.

Exegetical sources also offer financial responsibility, particular powers, or a man’s forgoing some of his own rights.

This disagreement shows that darajah’s lexical meaning does not alone determine its exact application.

Root research should preserve the expressed difference while leaving its legal limits to an independent study of the verse and marriage and divorce passages.

The root establishes neither unlimited intrinsic male superiority nor a freely chosen substitute for the degree without argument.

Darajah’s meaning is clear; its precise scope in this ruling exceeds lexical definition.

Al-Ṭabarī: differing readings of degree and reciprocal rights

7. Sa-nastadrijuhum: progressive leading toward an unfavorable outcome

Sa-nastadrijuhum is D R J’s second Qur’anic branch.

It belongs to Form X and concerns staged progression.

Arabic istadraja can convey gradually bringing someone closer or drawing them into a condition, rather than a sudden single transition.

Some human uses involve deception or gradual enticement, but human deceit is not necessary to every lexical use of the form.

The Qur’an uses it twice: Al-Aʿrāf 7:182 addresses those denying Our signs, while Al-Qalam 68:44 addresses someone denying this discourse. These expressions relate to the divine message contextually, but their wording and objects of denial differ.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:182

Al-Aʿrāf 7:182

وَالَّذِينَ كَذَّبُوا بِآيَاتِنَا سَنَسْتَدْرِجُهُمْ مِنْ حَيْثُ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ

wa-alladhīna kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā sa-nastadrijuhum min ḥaythu lā yaʿlamūna

“Those who denied Our signs: We will lead them onward gradually from where they do not know.”

The verse identifies a particular group: those denying The God’s signs.

It then introduces sa-nastadrijuhum.

Its subject is We, its object them, and sa- marks futurity.

Min ḥaythu lā yaʿlamūn indicates their unawareness of the process’s course or direction.

This is gradual progression whose aims are not clear to the group concerned.

The verb alone does not specify its exact resources, events, or stages.

Istidrāj and granting respite

Al-Aʿrāf 7:183

وَأُمْلِي لَهُمْ إِنَّ كَيْدِي مَتِينٌ

wa-umlī lahum inna kaydī matīnun

“I grant them respite; My plan is firm.”

Respite appears alongside istidrāj.

Umlī belongs to another root and should not become identical to istidrāj itself.

Granting respite means allowing more opportunity or time.

Contextual istidrāj is the gradual process unfolding through that time.

These concepts are related but distinct.

A person may receive time, while their use of it and their conduct’s consequences determine their direction.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:182 concerns deniers unaware of the process bringing them toward their outcome.

Al-Qalam 68:44 and Al-Qalam 68:45

Al-Qalam 68:44

فَذَرْنِي وَمَنْ يُكَذِّبُ بِهَٰذَا الْحَدِيثِ سَنَسْتَدْرِجُهُمْ مِنْ حَيْثُ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ

fa-dharnī wa-man yukadhdhibu bi-hādhā al-ḥadīthi sa-nastadrijuhum min ḥaythu lā yaʿlamūna

“Leave Me with whoever denies this discourse; We will lead them onward gradually from where they do not know.”

The next verse repeats the respite theme:

Al-Qalam 68:45

وَأُمْلِي لَهُمْ إِنَّ كَيْدِي مَتِينٌ

wa-umlī lahum inna kaydī matīnun

“I grant them respite; My plan is firm.”

Repeating the construction in two chapters shows the close contextual relationship between istidrāj and respite.

Neither verse makes istidrāj itself the name of a specific economic, political, or bodily instrument.

Opportunity, resources, or continued power might play a part, but their identification requires separate evidence.

Does istidrāj always mean increasing blessings and wealth?

Some exegetical explanations describe progressively increased blessings while someone persists in wrongdoing.

This can be compared with other Qur’anic passages.

Al-Anʿām 6:44 describes people forgetting reminders, having doors of everything opened to them, rejoicing in what they receive, and then being suddenly seized.

Āl ʿImrān 3:178 likewise does not present unbelievers’ respite as guaranteed good for them.

These passages can illuminate istidrāj.

They do not make every increase of wealth, power, opportunity, or apparent success necessarily istidrāj.

The Qur’an also describes increased blessings in response to gratitude, including Ibrāhīm 14:7.

Increased resources have no single fixed moral value.

They may enable gratitude and good action, create a new test, or accompany heedlessness, denial, and an approaching unfavorable outcome in a particular situation.

Another person’s wealth or success alone therefore cannot establish their standing before The God.

Does istidrāj mean deceit on The God’s part?

Some human uses involve deception and gradually drawing another into an unfavorable situation.

Human deceit’s every characteristic should not transfer to divine action.

Al-Aʿrāf and Al-Qalam announce a process of which the deniers are unaware; min ḥaythu lā yaʿlamūn conveys that unawareness.

The verse does not say The God has told them a lie or deceptively turned guidance’s signs into falsehood.

Rather, Al-Aʿrāf identifies denial of signs, while Al-Qalam identifies denial of this discourse.

Contextual istidrāj thus means progressively leading them in a condition whose outcome they do not perceive, rather than equating the lexical meaning with human lying or moral deceit.

The process belongs within the network of responsibility, respite, heedlessness, and actions’ consequences.

Does istidrāj mean lowering?

People can use stairs to ascend or descend.

Stepwise movement in D R J therefore need not mean ascent.

Qur’anic istidrāj concerns gradual movement toward an unfavorable outcome.

Someone’s visible resources may increase while responsibility and consequences bring them closer to a worse condition.

Quantitative increase is consequently not identical to moral improvement.

The base translation should not directly replace istadraja with casting into Hell or destroying, however.

Those may concern contextual outcomes; the verb’s direct meaning is progressively leading onward.

Nor is every sin or further opportunity certainly istidrāj. The Qur’an also permits repentance and reform; judging an individual requires more than their visible circumstances.

Early dictionaries: istidrāj, progression, and contextual outcome

8. Distinguishing nearby roots

D R J and R F ʿ: rank and raising

These occur together in several verses.

R F ʿ conveys raising; darajah conveys a rank differing from others.

In narfaʿu darajātin, narfaʿu expresses raising, while darajāt specifies its rank or extent.

The raising verb’s whole meaning should not merge into the noun darajah.

Darajah can ordinarily mean rank or level; the construction and context express ascent.

Study of R F ʿ

D R J and F Ḍ L: rank and preference

Qur’anic F Ḍ L concerns superiority, abundance, and additional favor.

Darajah can likewise express a difference of superiority within a sentence.

The roots are not complete synonyms.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:21 places darajāt and tafḍīl together.

Darajah conveys standing and differentiated ranks, while tafḍīl foregrounds preference or the associated advantage.

Superiority in one respect does not necessarily mean superiority in all respects.

Study of F Ḍ L

D R J and R T B: rank and ordering

Arabic rutbah and martabah concern order and a position within a sequence.

Darajah can likewise identify a position within levels.

Its linguistic image connects with steps and passing between levels; rutbah can describe someone’s or something’s place within an order.

They overlap in many conceptual uses.

R T B in this form does not occur in the Qur’an, so this is an Arabic lexical comparison rather than two frequently used Qur’anic families.

D R J and D R K: degree and reaching

D R K concerns reaching, apprehending, and in some uses a lower level.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:145 describes al-dark al-asfal, the lowest level in the Fire.

Dictionaries compare darajāt for higher levels with darakāt for lower ones.

That tendency should not become an absolute rule identifying darajah invariably with Paradise and dark invariably with Hell.

Al-Anʿām 6:132 and Al-Aḥqāf 46:19 use darajāt amid differences in conduct and outcomes.

Other D R K forms independently concern reaching and apprehending.

The roots should remain separate despite similar spelling.

D R J and Z Y D: rank and increase

Increase and degree may connect in a given setting.

Greater resources, knowledge, or power may alter someone’s standing.

Mere quantitative increase does not itself elevate moral or otherworldly rank.

Degree concerns position within an order or comparison; increase concerns something becoming more.

This distinction matters for istidrāj: wealth may grow without bringing its possessor closer to divine approval.

Increase and elevation in rank should not always substitute for one another.

Study of Z Y D

D R J and M L W: istidrāj and respite

Al-Aʿrāf 7:182 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:183 juxtapose istidrāj and granting respite.

Istidrāj expresses progressively leading onward.

Imlāʾ contextually concerns allowing time.

They may accompany one process, one naming gradual movement and the other continued opportunity and time.

Rendering sa-nastadrijuhum merely as granting respite loses the progressive movement.

Nor does every reprieve necessarily involve istidrāj toward an unfavorable outcome.

9. Suggested translations for Forqan

Persian equivalents should follow morphology and context.

Darajah and darajāt

Degree, degrees, and ranks are usually natural equivalents.

Persian already uses darajah, so preserving the root’s image requires no invented substitute.

A degree suits Al-Baqarah 2:228, expressing a difference within a particular relationship.

Degrees according to their deeds clarifies rank and action in Al-Anʿām 6:132 and Al-Aḥqāf 46:19.

Degrees or high ranks suit the rewards in Al-Anfāl 8:4 and Ṭā Hā 20:75.

Degrees above others suits worldly positions and resources in Al-Anʿām 6:165 and Al-Zukhruf 43:32.

Ghāfir 40:15 requires the full rafīʿ al-darajāt construction, without making darajāt alone a divine name.

Paradise, spiritual station, political power, and wealth should not universally replace degrees; context supplies such applications.

Sa-nastadrijuhum

For Al-Aʿrāf 7:182 and Al-Qalam 68:44, a base equivalent is:

“We will lead them onward gradually.”

This preserves the staged process.

“We will lead them onward step by step” is also clear and natural.

“We will destroy them gradually” replaces the verb with an interpreted outcome.

“We will deceive them” also introduces more human deceit than the construction itself establishes.

“We will raise them step by step” can suggest positive improvement, contrary to the unfavorable contextual process.

Explanation can add that, with respite and the deniers’ unawareness, this progression brings them toward an outcome they overlook.

Such explanation should remain distinct from direct translation.

Non-Qur’anic family equivalents

Lexical daraja can mean walked, advanced, or passed away.

Tadarraja means proceeded stage by stage.

Darraja can mean divided into stages or progressively advanced something.

Adraja in the enclosing branch means inserted, incorporated, or wrapped something within another.

Durj can name a receptacle or drawer.

These illuminate historical usage without being Qur’anic derivatives.

10. Conclusion

Arabic D R J concerns movement, progression, traversing a path, and differentiated ranks. Walking, steps, and passages illuminate its extension into social and conceptual standing.

Dictionaries also record wrapping and enclosing, but a common historical origin for every use through movement or steps is not conclusively established.

The Qur’an uses the root twenty times in two main families: darajah/darajāt and sa-nastadrijuhum.

Darajah describes differing standing among prophets, people, deeds, worldly circumstances, and otherworldly degrees.

Otherworldly degrees may concern faith, knowledge, deeds, and reward; worldly differences can instead provide testing and responsibility without guaranteeing moral superiority.

Darajah in Al-Baqarah 2:228 expresses a difference within spouses’ legal relationship in a divorce context. The root does not determine its exact nature or independently establish unlimited intrinsic male superiority.

The two istidrāj verses concern deniers of the divine message, progressively led by a course they do not perceive, with respite and an unfavorable contextual outcome.

Istidrāj does not itself mean becoming wealthy, being deceived in every setting, or sudden destruction. Related verses and particular circumstances must establish those matters.

Greater resources and apparent advancement alone establish neither divine approval nor certain istidrāj.

Proposed final definition: D R J’s familiar core concerns movement, traversing a route, and passing between stages. Darajah is a rank within levels; istidrāj progressively leads someone or something onward. Qur’anic degrees may express differing worldly or otherworldly standing, while istidrāj amid denial describes gradual progression toward an unfavorable outcome. Construction and context determine each usage’s direction and value. Brief root meaning: Stepwise movement and passage; steps and rank; istidrāj: progressively leading onward.
