Principal meanings: antiquity and long standing; release from bondage or dependence; nobility, fine quality, and excellence. In the Quran, the root occurs only in the description «الْبَيْتِ الْعَتِيقِ».

1. The root in early Arabic

The three-letter root ع ت ق has several established branches, chiefly antiquity, freedom from bondage, and nobility or excellence.

«عَتِيق» can describe something surviving from the distant past, a person freed from slavery, or an animal, person, or object of noble origin, fine quality, or distinction.

These meanings have connections, but historical evidence is needed before reducing them to a single original meaning.

A fine camel may be «عتيق» without being very old. Someone freed from slavery can be young. Conversely, an ancient object may be called عتيق without being intrinsically free or excellent.

Thus antiquity, freedom, and nobility are three established senses that must be distinguished in context.

First poetic witness: nobility and quality

The Muʿallaqa attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Ṭarafa ibn al-ʿAbd says:

تُبَارِي عِتَاقًا نَاجِيَاتٍ وَأَتْبَعَتْ

tubārī ʿitāqan nājiyātin wa-atbaʿat

وَظِيفًا وَظِيفًا فَوْقَ مَوْرٍ مُعَبَّدِ

waẓīfan waẓīfan fawqa mawrin muʿabbadi

Translation: His she-camel races alongside fine, swift camels, advancing one leg after another over a trodden road.

The poet describes his camel competing with other camels in movement.

«عِتَاقًا» is a plural of «عَتِيق» and here denotes fine, well-bred camels.

«نَاجِيَاتٍ» expresses their swiftness.

Here عتاق therefore means fine and valuable camels, not simply old ones.

This witnesses the pre-Islamic use of the family for noble stock, excellence, and superior quality.

The verse survives in established versions of Ṭarafa's Muʿallaqa and its early commentaries, which explain عتاق as fine, noble camels.

Second witness: antiquity

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Aws ibn Ḥajar says:

عَلَيَّ أَلِيَّةٌ عَتُقَتْ قَدِيمًا

ʿalayya aliyyatun ʿatuqat qadīman

فَلَيْسَ لَهَا وَإِنْ طُلِبَتْ مَرَامُ

fa-laysa lahā wa-in ṭulibat marāmu

Translation: An ancient oath binds me; even if a way around it is sought, there is no escape from it.

«أَلِيَّة» means an oath.

«عَتُقَتْ» describes an oath established long ago and still binding.

The following verse speaks of the poet's commitment not to betray his neighbor.

Here the verb concerns long standing and the passage of time.

«قَدِيمًا» reinforces that sense.

The witness illustrates this family's application to something surviving from the distant past in poetry attributed to a pre-Islamic poet.

The verse appears in the collection attributed to Aws and in earlier literary transmission. The attribution supports the usage's antiquity, although the verse's precise date is not independently established.

The freedom branch

Another branch concerns release from bondage and freedom.

«عَتَقَ الْعَبْدُ» means that an enslaved person became free.

«أَعْتَقَهُ» means that someone freed him.

«عِتْق» and «عَتَاقَة» likewise express freedom from slavery in this setting.

A freed person can be described as «عَتِيق».

Arabic dictionaries clearly record this usage. The poetic witnesses concerning fine camels and an ancient oath do not, however, establish when the freedom sense arose.

Nor do the three branches prove that freedom from slavery came first and then developed into antiquity and nobility, or the reverse.

Establishing related meanings differs from proving their historical sequence.

Freedom should therefore be recognized as an established Arabic branch without guessing its chronology from semantic resemblance.

2. Word family and semantic branches

Principal recorded forms include «عَتَقَ», «عَتُقَ», «عِتْق», «عَتَاق», «عَتَاقَة», «عَتِيق», «عَتِيقَة», «عِتَاق», «عَاتِق», «أَعْتَقَ», «إِعْتَاق», «عَتَّقَ», «تَعْتِيق», «مُعْتَق», and «عُتَقَاء».

عَتَقَ and عَتُقَ: becoming free or old

The verbs occur in several semantic branches.

«عَتَقَ الْعَبْدُ» describes an enslaved person becoming free.

The person leaves a condition of being owned and controlled by another.

«عَتُقَ الشَّيْءُ» and related forms describe something on which much time has passed.

One branch concerns a change in human legal status; another concerns elapsed time and antiquity.

These senses should not be merged in translation.

عِتْق and إِعْتَاق: emancipation

In the human context, «عِتْق» names freedom from slavery.

The Form IV verb «أَعْتَقَ» means to free an enslaved person.

«إِعْتَاق» is its verbal noun, naming the act of emancipation.

The distinction between intransitive and transitive forms matters:

In «عَتَقَ الْعَبْدُ», the enslaved person becomes free.

In «أَعْتَقَ السَّيِّدُ الْعَبْدَ», the owner frees that person.

Thus عتق can describe becoming free, whereas إعتاق describes freeing someone.

«مُعْتَق» describes someone freed; «عُتَقَاء» denotes freed persons.

This legal usage should not be imposed on every occurrence of عتيق.

Calling a camel عتيق does not necessarily mean it has been released from someone's ownership.

عَتِيق: ancient, freed, or noble

The adjective follows «فَعِيل».

It has three prominent uses in Arabic.

Temporally, it describes something old or ancient.

Legally, it describes someone freed from slavery.

Qualitatively, it describes something of noble stock, fine quality, goodness, or distinction.

For example, «فَرَسٌ عَتِيقٌ» can mean a fine, well-bred horse.

«عَبْدٌ عَتِيقٌ» refers to a freed person in the slavery context.

«شَيْءٌ عَتِيقٌ» can mean an ancient object.

The pattern فعيل does not determine one exclusive meaning across all these uses.

The described noun and context determine the precise sense.

عِتَاق: fine or noble specimens

«عِتَاق» is an established plural of «عَتِيق».

Pre-Islamic poetry particularly uses it for fine camels and horses.

«عِتَاقُ الطَّيْرِ» is also recorded for powerful, prized hunting birds, such as hawks.

Lexical explanations describe them as birds that hunt but are not readily hunted.

This may suggest independence from domination, but the practical sense here is the bird's excellence and capability.

عتاق cannot therefore always be translated as “freed persons.”

عَتَّقَ: aging and maturing

Form II «عَتَّقَ», when applied to objects, concerns keeping something so that it ages or matures over time.

A familiar illustration is «الْخَمْرُ الْعَتِيقَةُ», aged wine.

Here time can alter the substance's qualities.

«تَعْتِيق» names the process of aging or maturing.

In some uses, antiquity may thus accompany a desirable quality rather than describe age alone.

This does not imply that every old thing is better or more valuable.

Age and quality remain distinct features that may coexist.

عَاتِق: a young woman and the shoulder region

«عَاتِق» has different uses within this family.

«جَارِيَةٌ عَاتِقٌ» describes a young woman who has reached maturity; some lexical definitions also specify that she has not yet moved from her family's home to a husband.

Some explanations connect the designation with leaving childhood and entering a new condition.

The word also names the upper shoulder region between the neck and shoulder, where a garment's strap or edge may rest.

These senses are recorded, but their precise historical connections with freedom, antiquity, or nobility are not established.

There is no need to force shoulder anatomy or maturity into a derivation from liberation merely to maintain a seemingly unified definition.

3. Quranic uses

The root occurs twice, in a single lexical form.

Both occurrences are the adjective «الْعَتِيقِ» in «الْبَيْتِ الْعَتِيقِ», in Al-Ḥajj.

Verbs such as «عَتَقَ», «أَعْتَقَ», and «عَتَّقَ», and nouns such as «عِتْق» and «عِتَاق», do not occur in the Quran.

Quranic usage is therefore limited to describing a particular House.

A. الْبَيْتِ الْعَتِيقِ: the Ancient House and circumambulation

ثُمَّ لْيَقْضُوا تَفَثَهُمْ وَلْيُوفُوا نُذُورَهُمْ وَلْيَطَّوَّفُوا بِالْبَيْتِ الْعَتِيقِ

thumma l-yaqḍū tafathahum wa-l-yūfū nudhūrahum wa-l-yaṭṭawwafū bi-l-bayti al-ʿatīq

Al-Ḥajj 22:29

Then let them remove their unkemptness, fulfill their vows, and circumambulate the Ancient House.

Al-Ḥajj 22:29

«الْعَتِيقِ» modifies «الْبَيْتِ».

Both are definite and genitive.

«بِالْبَيْتِ» relates to «لْيَطَّوَّفُوا», while the adjective describes the House.

Syntactically, عتيق is an adjective here, not an independent noun or verb.

Translation must select an established sense appropriate to the noun and context.

Why “the Ancient House”?

One clear Arabic meaning of عتيق is old or ancient.

Other verses concerning the House's history support this interpretation.

Āl ʿImrān 3:96 speaks of the first House established for humanity:

إِنَّ أَوَّلَ بَيْتٍ وُضِعَ لِلنَّاسِ لَلَّذِي بِبَكَّةَ مُبَارَكًا وَهُدًى لِلْعَالَمِينَ

inna awwala baytin wuḍiʿa li-l-nāsi la-lladhī bi-bakkata mubārakan wa-hudan li-l-ʿālamīn

Āl ʿImrān 3:96

The first House established for humanity is indeed the one at Bakkah, blessed and a guidance for all peoples.

Āl ʿImrān 3:96

This verse matters for understanding the adjective.

The House is described as the first established for humanity.

“The Ancient House” therefore accords both with the lexical sense of antiquity and with the Quran's account of its background.

Al-Ḥajj 22:26 describes The God assigning Abraham the place of the House.

Al-Baqarah 2:127 mentions Abraham and Ishmael raising its foundations.

These verses connect the House with a distant past and Abraham's way.

Nevertheless, the House's antiquity does not mean its physical materials and structure have remained unchanged since the beginning.

The passages concern its background and standing, without detailing every construction phase.

“The Ancient House” is therefore a clear, defensible translation without an added claim of structural immutability.

Could العتيق mean a free House?

Freedom from slavery and bondage is also an established branch of ع ت ق.

Earlier lexical explanations accordingly propose freedom from others' domination or ownership for «البيت العتيق».

Some accounts explain the name by saying that no tyrant could overpower it.

Another explanation holds that the House is not subject to exclusive human ownership.

But the lexical idea of freedom differs from a historical claim about the House's fortunes.

The adjective alone cannot prove that no ruler ever controlled the House or its surroundings.

Nor does it independently determine the legal status of the land or building, or every rule of administration.

Freedom from monopolization and human domination may nevertheless be examined alongside other verses.

Al-Ḥajj 22:25 presents the Sacred Mosque as for humanity, with equality between the resident and the person arriving from outside. «الباد» is not restricted to a nomadic desert-dweller.

Al-Baqarah 2:125 describes the House as a place of return and security for people.

Together, these verses support reflection on public access, security, and resistance to monopolization.

“The House free from exclusive control” can therefore be discussed as a Quranic reflection, but should not be presented without explanation as the adjective's certain and exclusive meaning.

For direct translation, this study prefers “the Ancient House” on lexical and Quranic grounds, without treating the other recorded readings as linguistically impossible.

Could العتيق mean an honored House?

Arabic also uses عتيق for what is noble, fine, or esteemed.

Pre-Islamic poetry supports this sense, as with عتاق describing fine camels.

Āl ʿImrān 3:96 calls the House blessed and a guidance for all peoples.

Al-Māʾidah 5:97 connects the Kaaba with sustaining people's lives.

The House's honored status thus accords with Quranic descriptions.

This agreement alone does not prove that العتيق in Al-Ḥajj 22:29 must mean only “honored.”

A house may be ancient and also esteemed.

Both qualities can coexist, while a basic translation must make its chosen reading clear.

This study proposes “the Ancient House” for direct translation; its honored status also emerges from other verses.

Circumambulation in the verse

Al-Ḥajj 22:29 commands circumambulation alongside removing «تَفَث» and fulfilling vows. Here تفث is understood as bodily unkemptness and related grooming in the pilgrimage setting, not every kind of moral impurity.

«لْيَطَّوَّفُوا» belongs to ط و ف and concerns going around something.

«البيت العتيق» specifies what is circumambulated.

The construction clearly establishes circumambulation of the House in the Quran.

The adjective عتيق does not specify the number of circuits, direction, starting point, or other ritual details.

Those questions require their own evidence.

The contribution of ع ت ق is the description of the House; details about circumambulation or vows should not be added to its lexical meaning.

B. الْبَيْتِ الْعَتِيقِ: the Ancient House and the rites' destination

لَكُمْ فِيهَا مَنَافِعُ إِلَىٰ أَجَلٍ مُسَمًّى ثُمَّ مَحِلُّهَا إِلَى الْبَيْتِ الْعَتِيقِ

lakum fīhā manāfiʿu ilā ajalin musamman thumma maḥilluhā ilā al-bayti al-ʿatīq

Al-Ḥajj 22:33

You have benefits in them until an appointed term; then their place of completion is at the Ancient House.

Al-Ḥajj 22:33

Again, «الْعَتِيقِ» modifies «الْبَيْتِ».

Its form and basic meaning are unchanged.

The surrounding construction differs, however.

Al-Ḥajj 22:29 connects the House with circumambulation.

Al-Ḥajj 22:33 connects it with «مَحِلُّهَا» and an appointed term.

مَحِلُّهَا: place or point of completion

«مَحِلّ» belongs to ح ل ل, whose uses include settling, the loosening of a restriction, and the arrival of the time or place for an action.

In the reading concerning sacrificial animals, «مَحِلُّهَا» denotes their place of sacrifice. It has also been understood more broadly as the culmination of the rites; the word should not be reduced to an unspecified “place.”

The surrounding verses help identify the pronoun's referent.

Al-Ḥajj 22:32 speaks of honoring The God's rites.

Al-Ḥajj 22:28 and Al-Ḥajj 22:34 mention livestock and invoking The God's name over them.

Al-Ḥajj 22:36 identifies the large sacrificial animals among His rites.

The immediate context therefore strongly supports a connection with sacrificial animals, although interpretation of the pronoun and scope of شعائر is not uniform.

The adjective العتيق itself does not mean a slaughtering place; it describes the House.

The preposition إِلَى

«إِلَى الْبَيْتِ الْعَتِيقِ» contains إِلَى.

The preposition can express direction or arrival at a destination.

It does not itself state that the action occurs inside the House's structure.

The House is identified as the destination or spatial reference; the precise location of ritual acts requires contextual and further evidence.

Neither the House's name nor the preposition should be made to supply unstated spatial rules.

This study again takes العتيق as ancient or long-established.

The House's different roles in Al-Ḥajj 22:29 and Al-Ḥajj 22:33 do not require a different adjective meaning.

Connecting the two occurrences

Both occur within the same passage of Al-Ḥajj.

Al-Ḥajj 22:25 opens with the Sacred Mosque and people's access to it.

Al-Ḥajj 22:26 assigns Abraham the place of the House and speaks of avoiding association with The God and keeping the House pure.

Al-Ḥajj 22:27 announces the pilgrimage to humanity.

Al-Ḥajj 22:28 discusses benefits for people, remembrance of The God, and feeding those in need.

Al-Ḥajj 22:29 then connects the Ancient House with circumambulation, and Al-Ḥajj 22:33 with the culmination of the rites.

The passage, together with Al-Baqarah 2:125, shows that the Ancient House is more than an old building: it is a recognized place connected with Abraham's way, worship of The God alone, pilgrimage, security, public benefit, and divine rites.

Each of these ideas comes from its own words and passages.

The root ع ت ق should not be made to carry this entire Quranic network by itself.

4. Related roots and distinctions

عَتِيق and قَدِيم: antiquity

Some uses of ق د م concern precedence and being ahead.

«قَدِيم» describes what has existed from the past.

One principal sense of «عَتِيق» is likewise old or ancient.

The two words are close in this respect.

But ع ت ق also includes freedom and nobility; قديم does not itself mean emancipated from slavery.

Aged wine may be عتيق and, temporally, قديم.

Someone recently freed is legally عتيق without necessarily being old.

Yā Sīn 36:39 uses «قَدِيم» for an old date-cluster stalk.

Al-Ḥajj uses العتيق to describe the House.

Both can be translated “old” or “ancient,” but their entire semantic ranges differ.

عِتْق and حُرِّيَّة: release and freedom

ح ر ر concerns freedom and release from slavery.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:92 mentions «تَحْرِيرُ رَقَبَةٍ»: freeing a person from slavery.

Al-Mujādilah 58:3 expresses the same concept in another context.

The Quran does not use ع ت ق for this act.

عِتْق and تَحْرِير can overlap in emancipation contexts, but differ in root and form.

Verses containing تحرير رقبة should therefore not be counted as occurrences of ع ت ق.

Nor does the legal freedom sense require translating البيت العتيق as a House emancipated from slavery.

A house is not an enslaved person; the application of the adjective requires independent examination.

عَتِيق and كَرِيم: nobility and excellence

ك ر م concerns nobility, esteem, and value.

The descriptive sense of عتيق can likewise mean well-bred, fine, or esteemed.

Ṭarafa's poem uses عتاق for fine camels.

كريم can describe a person, speech, provision, or other valued things.

Some uses of عتيق additionally associate excellence with antiquity or noble stock.

كريم does not itself express age.

“The Honored House” accords with the House's Quranic standing, but it selects the nobility branch and does not convey the antiquity expressed by this study's preferred reading.

عَتِيق and أَوَّل: ancient and first

أَوَّل denotes first position in an order.

Āl ʿImrān 3:96 identifies the House at Bakkah as the first established for humanity.

عتيق can denote antiquity, but being old and being first are different concepts.

Several things can be ancient while only one is first.

The adjective alone does not establish that the House is first.

That information comes from the separate expression «أَوَّلَ بَيْتٍ».

Translating البيت العتيق as “the First House” would substitute wording drawn from another verse for this adjective.

“The Ancient House” is more precise for this reading; its firstness is explained through Āl ʿImrān 3:96.

عَتِيق and حَرَام: antiquity and sanctity

«الْبَيْتِ الْعَتِيقِ» and «الْبَيْتَ الْحَرَامَ» are Quranic descriptions of the recognized House.

Al-Māʾidah 5:97 contains «الْكَعْبَةَ الْبَيْتَ الْحَرَامَ».

حَرَام belongs to ح ر م and concerns sanctity and restrictions against violation.

In the branch considered here, عتيق concerns antiquity.

The same House can be both ancient and sacred.

Antiquity comes from ع ت ق; sanctity comes from ح ر م.

These descriptions should not be treated as synonyms, nor sanctity added to the lexical meaning of عتيق.

عَتِيق and عَتِيد: antiquity and readiness

The roots look similar but differ in their final consonant.

ع ت ق chiefly concerns antiquity, freedom, and nobility.

ع ت د concerns presence and readiness for a use or event.

In Qāf 50:18, عَتِيد means present and ready.

In Al-Ḥajj 22:29, عَتِيق describes the Ancient House.

They belong to different roots and have different meanings.

Their resemblance does not justify translating the Ancient House as a ready or prepared house.

5. Morphological and semantic observations

The Quranic form الْعَتِيقِ

It is a singular masculine definite genitive adjective from ع ت ق.

In both Al-Ḥajj verses it modifies الْبَيْتِ.

The two phrases have the same construction.

There is no change of verbal form, number, or morphological meaning between them.

Both describe the House.

Using the same equivalent in both is appropriate unless clear evidence supports a difference.

The pattern فَعِيل

عَتِيق follows فَعِيل.

Across Arabic vocabulary, the pattern can serve different semantic roles.

It may express a lasting quality or approach a passive-participle meaning.

When describing a freed person, عتيق can express the result of emancipation.

When describing an old object, it need not mean someone or something liberated.

The pattern alone therefore cannot establish that البيت العتيق must mean a House someone has freed.

Lexical usage and context determine the intended sense.

Is عتيق always positive?

The nobility and excellence branch can express positive evaluation.

In the antiquity branch, age alone does not establish superiority or moral value.

An ancient thing may be valuable, such as an artifact that helps us understand the past.

An old custom may also be mistaken or harmful.

Thus antiquity alone does not establish truth or correctness.

The Quran's House has standing beyond its age: other verses connect it with worship of The God alone, guidance, security, reverence, and public benefit.

These provide independent grounds for understanding its standing.

The adjective should not be used to claim divine approval for every old practice associated with the House.

Freedom from monopolization and human responsibility

The freedom branch may inform reflection on the Ancient House.

Al-Ḥajj 22:25 discusses public access to the Sacred Mosque and equality between residents and those arriving from elsewhere.

Al-Ḥajj 22:26 connects keeping the House pure with avoiding association with The God and making it available to worshippers.

These verses do not restrict the House to a particular social, ethnic, or political group.

One may infer that people's rights concerning the House should not be eliminated through claims of exclusive religious or political ownership.

But this is a reflection based on several verses, not the adjective's certain and exclusive lexical meaning.

This study recommends “ancient” in translation and discussion of freedom from monopolization in the accompanying explanation.

6. Translation recommendations for Forqan

The three principal branches should remain distinct.

عَتِيق, temporally: ancient, old, long-established.

عَتِيق, legally: freed from slavery.

عَتِيق, qualitatively: well-bred, fine, good, esteemed.

عِتْق: freedom from slavery; in other uses, nobility or antiquity according to context.

أَعْتَقَ: freed someone, in the slavery context.

إِعْتَاق: emancipation.

عِتَاق: fine or noble specimens, particularly camels and horses.

عَتَّقَ: aged or matured; kept something so that it became old.

عَاتِق: in some uses, a young woman who has reached maturity; anatomically, the upper shoulder region.

Al-Ḥajj 22:29

For «بِالْبَيْتِ الْعَتِيقِ» in its circumambulation context:

“Around the Ancient House.”

In the full verse:

“Then let them remove their unkemptness, fulfill their vows, and circumambulate the Ancient House.”

This retains antiquity and agrees with the House's description in Āl ʿImrān 3:96.

Al-Ḥajj 22:33

For «إِلَى الْبَيْتِ الْعَتِيقِ»:

“To the Ancient House” — rendered “at the Ancient House” in the following contextual translation.

In the full verse:

“You have benefits in them until an appointed term; then their place of completion is at the Ancient House.”

The same equivalent, “the Ancient House,” should be retained.

Brief meanings for word boxes

Brief root meaning: antiquity; release from bondage; nobility and fine quality.

الْعَتِيقِ — Al-Ḥajj 22:29

Contextual meaning: ancient or long-established; describing the House to be circumambulated.

الْعَتِيقِ — Al-Ḥajj 22:33

Contextual meaning: ancient or long-established; describing the same House in connection with the rites' destination.

7. Conclusion

The Arabic root has several branches, chiefly antiquity, freedom, and nobility.

Ṭarafa's attributed poetry uses عِتَاق for fine camels, illustrating quality and noble stock in pre-Islamic language.

The poem attributed to Aws ibn Ḥajar uses عَتُقَتْ for an ancient oath, illustrating antiquity and elapsed time.

Another branch concerns emancipation from slavery, including عِتْق, أَعْتَقَ, and مُعْتَق.

These branches should not be reduced without evidence to one historical meaning. Age, freedom, and nobility are distinct qualities that may become connected in particular uses.

The Quran uses the root only twice, both as الْعَتِيقِ in الْبَيْتِ الْعَتِيقِ.

Al-Ḥajj 22:29 connects the Ancient House with circumambulation.

Al-Ḥajj 22:33 connects it with the culmination of the rites.

In both verses the word modifies the House; this study prefers “ancient” or “long-established.”

Āl ʿImrān 3:96 supports this reading by describing the House at Bakkah as the first established for humanity.

Age alone does not explain the House's entire standing. The Quran connects it with Abraham's way, rejection of association with The God, security, guidance, pilgrimage, reverence, and public benefit.

The freedom branch can also inform reflection, especially in light of the equality of residents and visitors in Al-Ḥajj 22:25. It should not become an unproven claim that the House has always been immune to every form of human domination.

Its honored status accords with Quranic descriptions, but should remain distinct from antiquity and the House's other qualities.

Final definition: ع ت ق has three principal Arabic branches: antiquity, release from bondage or dependence, and nobility or excellence. Depending on its noun and context, عَتِيق may mean ancient, emancipated, or fine and esteemed. The Quran uses it only in الْبَيْتِ الْعَتِيقِ. On lexical grounds and in light of passages about the House's background, this study recommends “the Ancient House” for direct translation, while recognizing other recorded explanations.
