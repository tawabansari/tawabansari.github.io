1. Meaning and Structure of the Word

In Arabic, surādiq (سُرَادِق) denotes a pavilion, curtain, or covering erected around a tent, house, or defined space. Made of cloth or other materials, it can enclose, cover, or separate a space from its surroundings.

In early usage, surādiq sometimes denotes an awning stretched over a courtyard, sometimes curtains surrounding a tent, and sometimes the pavilion or large tent itself.

Al-ʿAyn, among the earliest comprehensive Arabic dictionaries, defines surādiq as something surrounding another thing, such as curtains around a tent or a wall enclosing a space.

Lisān al-ʿArab gives a similar definition: “السُّرَادِقُ: مَا أَحَاطَ بِالْبِنَاءِ”—a surādiq is what surrounds a building.

Al-ʿAyn and Lisān al-ʿArab: enclosure and covering a building

This definition clarifies an important feature: surādiq is a covering that, by standing around or above something, creates an enclosed or covered space.

Its uses should nevertheless not be restricted to one kind of structure. In some witnesses it is a roofless surrounding curtain; in others it is a covering stretched above a courtyard.

Having or lacking a roof is therefore not an invariant part of the meaning. The principal uses share the provision of covering, enclosure, or separation of a defined area.

Qur’anic morphological indexes register S R D Q (س ر د ق) as a quadriliteral entry. This morphological classification does not itself establish the word’s historical origin. An Iranian background and borrowing into Arabic are prominent, well-established proposals for surādiq; direct transmission from Iranian, as opposed to transmission through Aramaic, remains uncertain.

Arabic sardaqa (سَرْدَقَ) means to erect a surādiq or cover and enclose a space with one. Forms such as musardaq (مُسَرْدَق) are derived within this verbal paradigm.

The existence of these verbs does not establish that the noun surādiq originally arose from a native Arabic verbal root. Arabic can adopt a borrowed word and then form verbs and descriptive forms from it according to its own morphology.

2. Historical Origin and Linguistic Forms

Is Surādiq a Persian Word?

Many early lexicographers considered surādiq an Arabicized Persian word: one that entered Arabic from Persian and was adapted to Arabic pronunciation and structure.

Al-Rāghib al-Isfahānī states explicitly in Al-Mufradāt: “السُّرَادِقُ فَارِسِيٌّ مُعَرَّبٌ”—“Surādiq is an Arabicized Persian word.”

He also points to its unusual structure: surādiq has an alif in third position followed by two letters, a configuration he regards as atypical of native Arabic singular nouns.

This is morphological evidence, but it cannot alone establish historical origin. Its association with lexical accounts of Persian provenance provides a stronger basis for the proposal.

Tāj al-ʿArūs reports from al-Jawālīqī that surādiq is an Arabicized form of sarādār (سَرادار) or sarāṭāq (سَراطاق). These reported forms should be treated as proposals concerning its source, rather than as an established historical original.

Al-Rāghib and Tāj al-ʿArūs: Arabicization and proposed source forms

Other sources propose forms such as sarāpardah or sarābardah. Some scholars have also examined a connection with Persian sarā and sarāy, words associated with a house or dwelling.

These proposals are semantically close: all concern a house, covered space, curtain, or pavilion.

None should, however, be selected as the certain source without examining its historical evidence and precise recorded form.

For example, the resemblance between surādiq and sarāpardah, and their close meanings, are apparent; that resemblance alone does not establish the phonological and historical route of transmission.

What can be said with greater confidence is that surādiq was an established word in early Arabic and that respected lexicographers regarded it as Arabicized. Its precise Iranian source remains open to investigation. Arthur Jeffery also examines an Iranian background in The Foreign Vocabulary of the Qur’ān, while leaving direct transmission from Iranian versus transmission through Aramaic unresolved.

Arthur Jeffery, The Foreign Vocabulary of the Qur’ān, pp.167–168: Iranian background and possible Aramaic transmission

Borrowing does not diminish a word’s status in the Arabic of the Qur’anic period. Once accepted and understood, a word belongs to that community’s vocabulary even if its history reaches another language.

Arabic Forms and Derivatives

The basic noun is surādiq (سُرَادِق).

سُرَادِق — surādiq: a pavilion, surrounding covering, curtain, or structure enclosing a space.

سُرَادِقَات — surādiqāt: the plural of surādiq, meaning pavilions or enclosing coverings. Sībawayh notes that the masculine noun surādiq is pluralized with the ending otherwise characteristic of the sound feminine plural.

سَرْدَقَ — sardaqa: a quadriliteral verb: he erected a surādiq or covered a space with a pavilion.

سَرْدَقَة — sardaqah: the verbal noun of sardaqa: erecting a surādiq or covering and enclosing a space with one.

مُسَرْدَق — musardaq: a passive participle describing a house or space covered or enclosed with a surādiq.

Lexicons record baytun musardaqun (بَيْتٌ مُسَرْدَقٌ), a house or tent whose covering has been erected and secured.

These forms show that surādiq entered Arabic’s morphological system rather than remaining an isolated, unchanging name.

In the Qur’an, however, only the noun surādiq with an attached pronoun occurs. No verb or plural from this family appears in the text.

3. Surādiq in Early Arabic Evidence

Pavilions and Enclosed Spaces

In early Arab life, a tent was not always merely a cloth roof. Curtains could be erected around large tents, rulers’ quarters, or gathering places to create a space separated from the outside.

Such coverings could consist of joined lengths of cloth surrounding a tent or courtyard.

In this arrangement the surādiq need not be the principal tent itself; it can establish a surrounding enclosure.

Al-Muṭarrizī describes this use clearly in Al-Mughrib: “مَا يُدَارُ حَوْلَ الْخَيْمَةِ مِنْ شُقَقٍ بِلَا سَقْفٍ”—lengths of cloth arranged around a tent without a roof.

Al-Mughrib and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: roofless curtain and courtyard covering

This evidence helps explain enclosure in surādiq. In this use it covers the sides of an area without necessarily providing an overhead covering.

Al-Jawharī, by contrast, records surādiq in Al-Ṣiḥāḥ for a covering stretched above a courtyard.

Arabic usage thus recognizes both a surrounding screen and an awning above a space.

The difference does not mean that the lexicographers give wholly incompatible accounts. Both uses involve covering and enclosing a defined space, although the structures differ.

A Witness from Labīd’s Poetry

Lisān al-ʿArab quotes the following from Labīd ibn Rabīʿah, who lived through the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods:

رَفَعْنَ سُرَادِقًا فِي يَوْمِ رِيحٍ
يُصَفِّقُ بَيْنَ مَيْلٍ وَاعْتِدَالِ

rafaʿna surādiqan fī yawmi rīḥin / yuṣaffiqu bayna maylin wa-ʿtidāli

“On a windy day they raised a surrounding curtain of dust, fluttering between leaning and standing upright.”

The verse describes wild asses raising dust as they move. Labīd lived both before and after the advent of Islam; without dating this particular verse, it should not be presented as certain evidence from before the Qur’an.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Labīd’s witness describing wild asses

Here surādiq does not denote an actual tent or curtain. Rising dust is likened to a pavilion spread through the air, creating a covered scene.

The witness shows that early Arabic could extend surādiq from a constructed covering to a natural covering dispersed in the air.

The distinction between curtain and dust remains. The semantic transfer rests on their resemblance in surrounding and covering the scene.

Surādiq should therefore not be restricted to cloth or any particular material.

This comparison nevertheless does not establish that any dust or smoke can be called surādiq. Its enveloping, curtain-like appearance makes the expression possible here.

Surādiq in Praise and Grandeur

In poetry from the Islamic period, surādiq also expresses a person’s standing and grandeur.

A rajaz passage attributed to Ruʾbah ibn al-ʿAjjāj reads:

يَا حَكَمُ بْنَ الْمُنْذِرِ بْنِ الْجَارُودْ
سُرَادِقُ الْفَضْلِ عَلَيْكَ مَمْدُودْ

yā Ḥakamu bna al-Mundhiri bni al-Jārūd / surādiqu al-faḍli ʿalayka mamdūd

“O Ḥakam, son of al-Mundhir, son of al-Jārūd: the canopy of excellence is stretched over you.”

The surādiq here is not a physical covering. Excellence is pictured as a pavilion enveloping the person.

The attribution is disputed: Lisān al-ʿArab also reports attribution to al-Kadhdhāb al-Ḥirmāzī. The wording in al-Ṭabarī is surādiqu al-faḍl, whereas Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab have surādiqu al-majd, “the canopy of glory.” This study retains al-faḍl as transmitted in al-Ṭabarī, without treating the other reading as an error.

Al-Ṭabarī: al-faḍl and the reported wording and attribution variants

The passage is nevertheless recorded in literary and lexical sources and illustrates the word’s figurative use.

The image has no negative meaning here. Whereas the Qur’anic occurrence describes fire, this enveloping covering is associated with excellence and personal distinction.

Surādiq itself therefore denotes neither punishment nor, necessarily, grandeur. Those associations arise from what the surādiq belongs to.

A House Described as Musardaq

A verse attributed to Salāmah ibn Jandal also uses baytin musardaq (بَيْتٍ مُسَرْدَقٍ):

هُوَ الْمُولِجُ النُّعْمَانَ بَيْتًا سَمَاؤُهُ
صُدُورُ الْفُيُولِ بَعْدَ بَيْتٍ مُسَرْدَقِ

huwa al-mūliju al-Nuʿmāna baytan samāʾuhu / ṣudūru al-fuyūli baʿda baytin musardaqi

Explanatory rendering: “He is the one who brought al-Nuʿmān, after a house furnished with a pavilion, into a house whose roof was the breasts of elephants.” The verse contrasts the earlier covered house with the dreadful situation described in the account of al-Nuʿmān’s death. Quoting the poem does not establish the historical accuracy of every detail of that account.

Lexicographers explain musardaq here as a house furnished with a surādiq.

Al-Ṭabarī transmits the opening as huwa al-mūliju, while Lisān al-ʿArab has huwa al-mudkhilu, both meaning “he who brings in.” Sources also report differing attribution to Salāmah ibn Jandal or al-Aʿshā. The supplied reading al-mūliju is retained here. None of these variations establishes every detail of the associated historical narrative.

Al-Ṭabarī: al-mūliju and variants of the musardaq house verse

The morphology of musardaq is clear: it describes something provided with, or enclosed by, a surādiq.

Together with the other uses, this witness shows that the noun surādiq is associated with related verbs and descriptive forms in Arabic. That morphological relationship alone does not establish precisely when each form arose.

4. Qur’anic Usage in Al-Kahf 18:29

The entry S R D Q occurs only once in the Qur’an, in Al-Kahf.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: one occurrence in Al-Kahf 18:29

Al-Kahf 18:29

وَقُلِ الْحَقُّ مِنْ رَبِّكُمْ فَمَنْ شَاءَ فَلْيُؤْمِنْ وَمَنْ شَاءَ فَلْيَكْفُرْ إِنَّا أَعْتَدْنَا لِلظَّالِمِينَ نَارًا أَحَاطَ بِهِمْ سُرَادِقُهَا وَإِنْ يَسْتَغِيثُوا يُغَاثُوا بِمَاءٍ كَالْمُهْلِ يَشْوِي الْوُجُوهَ بِئْسَ الشَّرَابُ وَسَاءَتْ مُرْتَفَقًا

wa-quli al-ḥaqqu min rabbikum fa-man shāʾa fa-l-yuʾmin wa-man shāʾa fa-l-yakfur innā aʿtadnā li-l-ẓālimīna nāran aḥāṭa bihim surādiquhā wa-in yastaghīthū yughāthū bi-māʾin ka-l-muhli yashwī al-wujūha biʾsa al-sharābu wa-sāʾat murtafaqan

Proposed Persian translation, rendered here in English:

“And say: The truth is from your Lord; so whoever wills, let him believe, and whoever wills, let him deny. We have prepared for the wrongdoers a fire whose pavilion encompasses them. If they seek relief, they will be given relief with water like molten metal that roasts faces. What an evil drink, and what an evil resting place!”

The Structure of Surādiquhā

Surādiquhā (سُرَادِقُهَا) consists of the noun surādiq and the attached pronoun hā (هَا).

The pronoun refers to nāran (نَارًا), “a fire.” The expression therefore concerns the surādiq of the fire prepared for the wrongdoers.

Grammatically, surādiquhā is the subject of aḥāṭa (أَحَاطَ), “encompassed”; bihim (بِهِمْ) refers to those whom this surādiq encompasses.

The semantic order is therefore: the fire’s surādiq has encompassed them.

The distinction matters: the verse does not simply say that fire surrounds them; it speaks of the fire’s surādiq as the thing producing that enclosure.

Surādiq already carries the sense of covering and enclosure in Arabic. Its combination with aḥāṭa makes this feature especially prominent in the verse.

What Does the Fire’s Surādiq Express?

In ordinary usage, a surādiq can be a curtain covering the sides of a tent or separating a space from its surroundings.

In Al-Kahf, this same noun is associated with fire.

The image can accordingly be understood as fire, or a covering belonging to it, placing the wrongdoers within an enclosed space like an enveloping pavilion.

Aḥāṭa bihim, “encompassed them,” describes surrounding enclosure. The description itself does not present a route of escape or separation from that environment.

The clear meaning of the verse must nevertheless be distinguished from possible details of its imagery.

Some commentarial accounts explain the fire’s surādiq as a wall of fire; others identify it as smoke or a dark covering surrounding those undergoing punishment.

Al-Ṭabarī: accounts of a wall of fire and surrounding smoke

Both images fit enclosure and encompassing. The noun itself, however, does not specify whether the pavilion consists of a solid wall, smoke, flame, or another substance.

A direct translation should therefore retain “pavilion” or “surrounding covering” without presenting details of its construction as the word’s certain meaning.

Does Surādiq Mean a Protective Covering?

In ordinary life, a surādiq can provide shade, protect a space, or create a calm, separate setting.

Protection, however, is not an invariant and necessary meaning of the word.

A surādiq is fundamentally a covering that encloses an area. It may protect against heat, wind, or outside observation, but may simply separate spaces or display grandeur and power.

In Al-Kahf 18:29 the surādiq neither protects the wrongdoers nor provides them a refuge.

It instead appears in the description of a fire that encloses them.

This use shows why the beneficial functions of ordinary pavilions should not be imposed on every lexical use of surādiq.

The shared meaning is an enveloping covering or enclosure. Whether it benefits or harms depends on context.

Enclosure, Relief, and a Resting Place

Later in Al-Kahf 18:29, those undergoing punishment seek relief and are given a drink that scorches faces.

This continuation makes their suffering and helplessness clearer.

First the fire’s surādiq encompasses them. Then, when they ask for help or relief from suffering, the response described in the verse is itself part of that suffering.

The ending, sāʾat murtafaqan (سَاءَتْ مُرْتَفَقًا), describes that place as an evil and undesirable resting place.

The ending fits the earlier setting. In ordinary life a person might seek comfort and rest in a pavilion or covered area; here the enveloping covering accompanies suffering and the absence of comfort.

This contrast should not be added to the lexical definition. Surādiq itself does not mean suffering or discomfort; the verse places it in that setting.

Human Choice and Its Consequence

The verse begins with the command to proclaim the truth: al-ḥaqqu min rabbikum (الْحَقُّ مِنْ رَبِّكُمْ), “the truth is from your Lord.”

It then says “whoever wills, let him believe, and whoever wills, let him deny,” immediately followed by the consequence of wrongdoing. Al-Ṭabarī understands this construction as a threat and warning. It should therefore not, by itself, be treated as moral permission to disbelieve or as indifference to the consequences of choices.

Al-Ṭabarī: the opening construction as threat and warning

Mention of human willing and choice in this context accompanies responsibility and warning. A full account of the relationship between human choice and divine will requires a separate examination of other verses.

A lexical study of surādiq should not become a separate treatment of every aspect of choice, belief, disbelief, and divine justice.

For understanding the word, the key point is that the fire’s surādiq describes a consequence: an enveloping covering surrounding the wrongdoers.

The word alone cannot establish the precise time of punishment, the physical construction of the Hereafter, or every detail of entering and leaving it.

5. Comparison with Related Words

Surādiq and Khaymah

Khaymah (خَيْمَة) names a tent or shelter, usually made of cloth or other materials.

A surādiq can be part of a tent’s structure or a covering around it.

In some uses surādiq also denotes an entire tent or large pavilion. The two words therefore overlap; more specifically, surādiq highlights covering and enclosing a space.

The plural of khaymah occurs as al-khiyām (الْخِيَامِ) in Al-Raḥmān 55:72, whereas surādiq appears only in Al-Kahf 18:29.

Surādiq and Fusṭāṭ

Fusṭāṭ (فُسْطاط) denotes a tent or a large erected lodging in Arabic.

A surādiq may stand around a fusṭāṭ and enclose its space.

Accordingly, some lexicographers define surādiq as a curtain or enclosure installed around a fusṭāṭ.

In certain uses a large pavilion itself is also called surādiq. An entirely fixed boundary between the two words cannot therefore be maintained.

Fusṭāṭ does not appear in the Qur’an, but knowing its Arabic use helps explain a surādiq’s structure.

Surādiq and Ḥijāb

Ḥijāb (حِجَاب) denotes something establishing a barrier or covering between two sides.

A surādiq can create a ḥijāb or barrier around a space, but not every ḥijāb is a surādiq.

A ḥijāb can be a curtain, obstacle, or separation between two sides; a surādiq usually concerns a structure or covering enveloping a defined area.

Thus ḥijāb emphasizes obstruction and separation, while surādiq more clearly evokes a pavilion and spatial enclosure.

Study of Ḥ J B

Surādiq and Sūr

Sūr (سُور) denotes a wall or enclosure surrounding a space.

Al-Ḥadīd 57:13 mentions a wall placed between two groups and having a gate.

In establishing a boundary and separation, it can overlap with surādiq.

A surādiq need not, however, be a hard wall made of stone or building materials. A cloth curtain, tent covering, or even enveloping dust in figurative usage can be called surādiq.

Translating surādiquhā as “its wall” may therefore express one interpretive reading, but narrows the word’s broader meaning.

Study of S W R

Surādiq and Ẓill

Ẓill (ظِلّ) concerns shade, the condition produced by being screened from light.

A surādiq may create shade, but is not itself necessarily shade.

In Al-Kahf 18:29 the surādiq belongs to fire; there is no reason to translate it solely as shade.

Commentarial accounts connecting the fire’s surādiq with smoke or dark shade should likewise not erase the independent meaning of surādiq.

Surādiq and Iḥāṭah

Iḥāṭah (إِحَاطَة) concerns surrounding and encompassing something.

In Al-Kahf 18:29, aḥāṭa appears together with surādiq. Their meanings are related but not identical.

Iḥāṭah expresses the act or state of encompassing, whereas surādiq names a covering or structure that can bring about that enclosure.

Surādiq should therefore not be reduced to the abstract word “encompassing” in translation.

For example, rendering the sentence simply as “fire surrounds them” retains the general idea of enclosure but loses the pavilion image used by the Qur’an.

Distinction from the Root S R D

S R D Q (س ر د ق) should not be identified with the triliteral root S R D (س ر د).

Sard (سَرْد) concerns continuity and ordered connection and, in some uses, weaving or joining the rings of armor.

Sabaʾ 34:11 uses sard in the context of making armor.

The three shared initial letters do not establish that surādiq derives from sard, particularly given the independent lexical evidence for surādiq’s non-Arabic origin.

An explanation such as “a surādiq is made by joining curtains, so it derives from sard” would not be a defensible etymology without reliable historical evidence.

6. Conclusion and Suggested Translation

In Arabic, surādiq names a pavilion, awning, or covering erected around or above a defined space. Its principal uses concern covering and enclosing an area.

Early dictionaries record curtains around tents, courtyard coverings, large pavilions, and even enclosing walls.

Poetic witnesses show figurative extension as well: enveloping dust likened to a pavilion, or excellence pictured as a canopy stretched over a person.

Historically, surādiq is very probably an Arabicized word with an Iranian background. Its precise source—proposals include sarādār, sarāṭāq, and sarāpardah—and direct transmission versus transmission through Aramaic remain disputed or uncertain.

The family appears only once in the Qur’an, as surādiquhā in Al-Kahf 18:29.

Here the surādiq belongs to fire and accompanies aḥāṭa. An enveloping covering or enclosure therefore fits the context clearly.

The verse describes the fire’s surādiq encompassing the wrongdoers. The word itself does not specify whether that covering consists of wall, smoke, flame, or another substance.

Brief root meaning: A pavilion or covering erected around or above a defined space and enclosing it, such as a curtain around a tent, an awning, or an enveloping enclosure.

Meaning of the Qur’anic form surādiquhā: The pavilion or enveloping covering of that fire, surrounding the wrongdoers.

Suggested translation: “its pavilion.”

Translation of the Qur’anic phrase:

نَارًا أَحَاطَ بِهِمْ سُرَادِقُهَا

nāran aḥāṭa bihim surādiquhā

“A fire whose pavilion encompasses them.”

This rendering preserves the established meaning of surādiq without adding unknown details about its construction.

Surādiq intrinsically means neither punishment nor protection. It names a covering or structure enclosing a space. In ordinary life it can provide comfort; in poetry it can represent grandeur; in the Qur’anic description of fire it depicts enclosure and confinement.

The shared feature is covering and encompassing a defined space. The material, purpose, and consequences of that covering depend on context.
