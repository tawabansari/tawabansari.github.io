Khuḍūʿ: lowering oneself, bowing, and yielding in conduct and speech

In Arabic, the root خ ض ع is associated with descending, bending, humility, and submission. This meaning becomes visible in human conduct, especially in bending the neck and bowing the head, but its use is not confined to physical movement. A person may submit to another’s power, pressure, needs, or wishes, with that condition appearing in conduct, speech, or circumstances.

This root also describes softening one’s speech and speaking in a manner that expresses gentleness and flexibility toward the listener. Accordingly, khuḍūʿ does not carry the same moral value in every use. Sometimes it expresses submission to power; sometimes it refers to a particular softness in speech that may encourage desire or misunderstanding.

In the Qur’an, خ ض ع occurs twice, in two forms and two verses from two surahs: once in describing necks that submit to a divine sign, and once in an instruction to the Prophet’s wives about their manner of speaking. These uses display two important aspects of the root’s semantic field.

1. Forms and derivatives

The basic verb is خَضَعَ ـ يَخْضَعُ (khaḍaʿa–yakhḍaʿu). Its verbal noun is خُضُوع (khuḍūʿ), and important derivatives include خَاضِع (khāḍiʿ), أَخْضَع (akhḍaʿ), خَضْعَاء (khaḍʿāʾ), خُضَعَة (khuḍaʿah), and اِخْتَضَعَ (ikhtaḍaʿa).

خَضَعَ can mean to descend, bend, humble oneself, or submit. خَاضِع describes someone in that condition. In bodily descriptions, أَخْضَع and its feminine خَضْعَاء may describe a person or animal with a bent neck or posture. اِخْتَضَعَ denotes humbling or lowering oneself.

Arabic also records forms such as أَخْضَعَ for making someone else submit. For example, أَخْضَعَتْنِي إِلَيْكَ الْحَاجَةُ means “Need brought me low before you and made me submit.”

Only two forms of this root occur in the Qur’an:

  • خَاضِعِينَ (khāḍiʿīna): a masculine sound plural active participle, in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:4.
  • تَخْضَعْنَ (takhḍaʿna): a second-person feminine plural imperfect verb, Form I, in Al-Aḥzāb 33:32.

Although widely used in Arabic, the verbal noun khuḍūʿ does not itself occur in the Qur’anic text.

2. The semantic core: from bending the neck to submission and yielding

Lowering and bowing the head

One of the root’s clearest physical uses concerns bending the neck and lowering the head.

In Arabic, خَضَعَ عُنُقُهُ describes a neck that has bent or lowered. رَجُلٌ أَخْضَعُ may describe a man with a bent neck, and عُنُقٌ خَضْعَاءُ a neck with that characteristic.

This use also applies to animals. When an animal lowers its head or bends its neck toward the ground, forms of this family have been used to describe its posture.

This physical image helps explain human submission: someone who bows before another’s power may express submission or humility by lowering the head or bending the neck.

Nevertheless, this image must be distinguished from the word’s full meaning. Khuḍūʿ does not always require an actual bending of the neck. Someone may yield to another in decisions, conduct, or speech without any visible bodily movement.

Nor can we confidently claim that the physical meaning historically preceded every social and psychological meaning of this root. Lexical evidence shows the relationship between these uses, but does not establish their definite chronological order.

Submission, humility, and compliance

An important Arabic use of خَضَعَ is yielding to another’s power or position.

In خَضَعَ لِغَرِيمِهِ, a person submits or humbles himself before his creditor. In أَخْضَعَهُ الْفَقْرُ, poverty is presented as the cause of someone’s abasement.

These examples show that submission need not originate in a free decision motivated by respect. Need, poverty, fear, or another’s power may also bring someone to a position of bowing before another.

Conversely, not every instance of khuḍūʿ means humiliation or moral degradation. Its moral value depends on its cause and object. Humility before truth, yielding from fear, and submission to force differ in motive and value, although words of this family may describe them.

Its lexical meaning should therefore not be restricted from the outset to either “commendable humility” or “reprehensible abasement.”

Yielding in speech

Another important use of the root concerns the manner of speaking.

Arabic records خَضَعَ بِالْقَوْلِ and expressions such as خَضَعَ الرَّجُلُ لِلْمَرْأَةِ بِكَلَامِهِ for someone making his speech soft and accommodating, in a way that may express inclination, attraction, or an effort to draw the listener’s attention.

Here, the neck or body is not bending; the quality of speech changes.

Its relationship to the broader meaning of submission can be explained as follows: just as someone may lower his position before another, he may make his tone particularly soft and inclined toward the listener.

However, not every gentle or courteous utterance counts as khuḍūʿ in this particular sense. Softness of speech can arise from kindness, courtesy, calmness, fear, a request, attraction, or other purposes. The expression’s construction and context establish the precise meaning.

Another branch: sound and clamor

Classical Arabic dictionaries do not confine the family خ ض ع to bending and submission.

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies two separate principles for this entry: taṭāmun, lowering or stooping, and a kind of sound.

In the latter branch, خَضِيعَة (khaḍīʿah) denotes a sound heard from the belly of a horse or other animal in motion. خَيْضَعَة (khayḍaʿah) also describes mingled sounds and clamor, particularly in battle.

Sources record other meanings for khayḍaʿah, including the fighting itself, the battlefield, and even its dust. Ibn Fāris, however, does not accept the dust explanation as an independently well-grounded meaning, suggesting that it might arise through the proximity of dust to the clamor of battle.

This branch should not be forced out of the image of a bent neck. Words sharing a consonantal family do not by that fact alone prove that all their meanings developed from a single physical image.

Both Qur’anic forms, خَاضِعِينَ and تَخْضَعْنَ, belong to the branch of lowering, submission, and yielding, rather than an animal’s belly sounds or the clamor of war.

3. Early Arabic witnesses

Descending stars in poetry attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays

A poem attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays contains the following:

بَعَثْتُ إِلَيْهَا وَالنُّجُومُ خَوَاضِعٌ
بِلَيْلٍ حِذَارًا أَنْ تَهُبَّ وَتُسْمَعَا

baʿathtu ilayhā wa-al-nujūmu khawāḍiʿun / bi-laylin ḥidhāran an tahubba wa-tusmaʿā

Translation:

“At night, while the stars were descending, I sent word to her, fearing that she might rise and her sound be heard.”

The wording used here is that transmitted in Maqāyīs al-Lughah: بِلَيْلٍ حِذَارًا أَنْ تَهُبَّ وَتُسْمَعَا. Other transmissions differ in the second hemistich; Akhbār Abī al-Qāsim al-Zajjājī has ضَوَاجِع in place of خَوَاضِع. This wording must therefore be attributed to its particular source. In Ibn Fāris’s transmission, وَالنُّجُومُ خَوَاضِعٌ is relevant to the root’s use. تُسْمَعَا is passive here: her sound might be heard, rather than she herself might hear.

Akhbār Abī al-Qāsim al-Zajjājī: the alternative transmission with ḍawājiʿ

خَوَاضِع describes the stars. It does not mean that they are morally humble or obedient; it describes their apparent downward movement toward disappearance at the horizon.

This witness shows that khuḍūʿ can describe something descending through space without implying obedience, fear, or respect.

Attribution of this verse to Imruʾ al-Qays in literary sources does not by itself establish its precise date of composition. Its value here is as evidence of the recorded use of خَوَاضِع to describe the stars’ movement.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: the poetic transmission with khawāḍiʿ and bi-laylin ḥidhāran

Yielding in speech: a witness from Al-Kumayt

Al-Kumayt ibn Zayd, an Umayyad poet, says of women whose chastity he praises:

إِذْ هُنَّ لَا خُضُعُ الْحَدِيثِ
وَلَا تَكَشَّفَتِ الْمَفَاضِلْ

idh hunna lā khuḍuʿu al-ḥadīthi / wa-lā takashshafati al-mafāḍil

Translation:

“When they showed no enticing softness in speech, and their limbs were not exposed.”

خُضُعُ الْحَدِيثِ refers here to people who exhibit a particular yielding softness in speech.

This witness is important because it documents yielding in speech as a recognized Arabic use. Physical bending is not the subject; the quality of communication with another person is.

Al-Kumayt was an Islamic-era poet, not a pre-Islamic one. This verse is evidence from the literary Arabic of the early Islamic centuries and should not be presented as poetry predating the Qur’an.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Al-Kumayt’s witness and yielding in speech

Submission as abasement: a witness from Al-ʿAjjāj

A rajaz passage attributed to Al-ʿAjjāj reads:

وَصِرْتُ عَبْدًا لِلْبَعُوضِ أَخْضَعَا
يَمُصُّنِي مَصَّ الصَّبِيِّ الْمُرْضِعَا

wa-ṣirtu ʿabdan li-al-baʿūḍi akhḍaʿā / yamuṣṣunī maṣṣa al-ṣabiyyi al-murḍiʿā

Translation:

“I became a lowly slave before the mosquitoes; it sucks at me as a child sucks milk from a nursing woman.”

Through literary exaggeration, the poet expresses his helplessness before the torment of mosquitoes. In مَصَّ الصَّبِيِّ الْمُرْضِعَا, the child is the one doing the sucking, while الْمُرْضِع is the nursing woman and its object; the latter word is not an adjective describing the child.

أَخْضَع here describes abasement and lowliness, rather than a naturally bent neck or commendable moral humility.

This witness also belongs to Islamic-era Arabic. It shows that submission can result from pressure and helplessness, even when that pressure is expressed through a humorous or exaggerated image.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: Al-ʿAjjāj’s witness and abasement

Taken together, these witnesses show three distinct settings: physical descent, yielding in speech, and abasement under pressure. These settings are related, but context determines the particular meaning of each.

4. Qur’anic uses

A. Necks submitting before a divine sign — Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:4

إِنْ نَشَأْ نُنَزِّلْ عَلَيْهِمْ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ آيَةً فَظَلَّتْ أَعْنَاقُهُمْ لَهَا خَاضِعِينَ

in nashaʾ nunazzil ʿalayhim mina al-samāʾi āyatan fa-ẓallat aʿnāquhum lahā khāḍiʿīna

Proposed translation for the root study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“If We will, We can send down upon them a sign from heaven before which their necks would remain bowed.”

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:4

خَاضِعِينَ is a masculine sound plural active participle of خ ض ع, describing people who bow before a divine sign.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:3 speaks of the Prophet’s grief at his listeners’ failure to believe. The fourth verse then describes The God’s power to send a sign before which they would submit.

This connection matters. Here, submission is a response to a sign so manifest and powerful that necks would bow before it.

خَاضِعِينَ does not by itself specify whether these people submit from love, understanding, fear, or psychological pressure. The context establishes submission before a divine sign, but their entire inward relationship with truth cannot be deduced from this word alone.

Why is أَعْنَاقُهُمْ followed by خَاضِعِينَ?

A significant grammatical feature is that أَعْنَاقُهُمْ, “their necks,” is followed by the masculine plural adjective or predicate خَاضِعِينَ, rather than خَاضِعَاتٍ.

Syntactically, خَاضِعِينَ is the predicate of ظَلَّتْ and refers to the condition of those to whom submission is attributed.

In Arabic, bending the neck can represent the submission of the person. Saying that their necks bowed does not merely describe a change in one body part’s position; the submission of those to whom the necks belong is also understood.

Accordingly, many early language scholars explain that the expression begins with necks, while خَاضِعِينَ looks to their owners. The verse thus connects submission’s bodily sign with the people themselves.

Some early analyses also propose another meaning for أَعْنَاق: leading figures, chiefs, or groups of people. This reading draws on other Arabic uses of ʿunuq and accounts for the masculine plural خَاضِعِينَ in that way.

Nevertheless, understanding “neck” as the body part also has clear lexical and grammatical support and fits the general image of bowing. There is therefore no need to discard the direct meaning of neck merely to resolve the grammatical question.

Al-Ṭabarī: necks, their owners, and other grammatical analyses

Is the verse about compelled faith?

The context shows The God’s power to send a sign that would bring the listeners to submission. However, the verse begins with إِنْ نَشَأْ, “if We will,” expressing a conditional situation.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:5 then speaks of their turning away from fresh reminders, and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:6 refers to their denial.

The fourth verse should therefore not be detached from its opening condition and surrounding verses to conclude that every submission before divine signs necessarily becomes conscious and enduring faith.

This expression describes submitting, rather than every stage of belief, understanding, choice, and perseverance in truth.

Distinguishing outward submission from conscious acceptance of truth matters here, although خَاضِعِينَ alone does not definitively judge anyone’s inward intention.

B. Yielding in speech — Al-Aḥzāb 33:32

يَا نِسَاءَ النَّبِيِّ لَسْتُنَّ كَأَحَدٍ مِنَ النِّسَاءِ إِنِ اتَّقَيْتُنَّ فَلَا تَخْضَعْنَ بِالْقَوْلِ فَيَطْمَعَ الَّذِي فِي قَلْبِهِ مَرَضٌ وَقُلْنَ قَوْلًا مَعْرُوفًا

yā nisāʾa al-nabiyyi lastunna ka-aḥadin mina al-nisāʾi ini ittaqaytunna fa-lā takhḍaʿna bi-al-qawli fa-yaṭmaʿa alladhī fī qalbihi maraḍun wa-qulna qawlan maʿrūfan

Proposed translation for the root study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“O wives of the Prophet, you are not like any other women, if you remain mindful; so do not be soft in speech, lest the one in whose heart is sickness should become desirous; and speak fitting words.”

Al-Aḥzāb 33:32

تَخْضَعْنَ is a second-person feminine plural imperfect verb following the prohibitive lā. Its final nūn is nūn al-niswah, the feminine plural subject pronoun, and is therefore retained. Attached to that pronoun, the verb is built on sukūn and occupies the syntactic position of a jussive after prohibitive lā; this nūn is not the inflectional nūn of the “five verbs.”

بِالْقَوْلِ specifies that the prohibition concerns a yielding manner of speech, rather than bodily submission or bending the neck.

Addressing the Prophet’s wives, the verse prohibits a kind of yielding speech that may encourage desire in someone with sickness in his heart.

Three components should be read together:

First, فَلَا تَخْضَعْنَ بِالْقَوْلِ prohibits a manner of speaking.

Second, فَيَطْمَعَ الَّذِي فِي قَلْبِهِ مَرَضٌ states the consequence being addressed.

Third, وَقُلْنَ قَوْلًا مَعْرُوفًا sets fitting, recognized good speech against the prohibited manner.

Does this prohibit every kind of gentleness in speech?

Treating فَلَا تَخْضَعْنَ بِالْقَوْلِ as an absolute prohibition of gentle speech would broaden the semantic field of khuḍūʿ too far.

In Arabic, submissive softness in speech may involve inclination, drawing attention, or encouraging the listener’s desire. Al-Kumayt’s witness and lexical explanations of yielding in speech also document the expression in this setting.

Moreover, the prohibition is followed by the instruction وَقُلْنَ قَوْلًا مَعْرُوفًا.

The verse’s purpose is therefore not silence, verbal harshness, or abandoning courtesy. Speech must still be maʿrūf, fitting and good, but without the kind of yielding that, in this context, encourages inappropriate desire.

The distinction between respectful speech and submissive speech that encourages desire matters here.

For example, someone can speak calmly, kindly, and respectfully in an ordinary conversation without seeking to arouse inappropriate desire or display a particular inclination toward the listener.

Natural courtesy and calmness should therefore not be equated with the prohibited yielding without contextual evidence.

The listener’s role and مَرَضٌ in the verse

The verse attributes desire to someone فِي قَلْبِهِ مَرَضٌ, “in whose heart is sickness.” This shows that the issue is not confined to an outward quality of speech; the listener’s moral condition and intention also matter.

Consequently, a listener’s inappropriate desire does not by itself prove that the speaker has engaged in the prohibited form of yielding.

The verse prohibits a manner of speaking and mentions a listener with sickness of heart. These should not be turned into a rule placing all responsibility for the listener’s wrongful conduct and intentions upon the speaker.

The direct addressees are also نِسَاءَ النَّبِيِّ, “the Prophet’s wives.” Any discussion of extending the ruling to others must separately examine Qur’anic evidence and the address’s scope. The root خ ض ع does not itself establish such an extension.

Al-Ṭabarī: the kind of softness in speech and fitting words

Do the two Qur’anic uses have the same meaning?

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:4, submission responds to a divine sign and is expressed through the image of bowed necks.

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:32, yielding is attributed to the manner of speech.

These uses are related through the broad notions of lowering, submission, and yielding, but they are not identical.

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ, bowing and submission before the sign’s power are prominent. In Al-Aḥzāb, the quality of speech and the relationship with the listener matter.

Mechanically translating both as “humility” therefore fails to make all their contextual differences clear.

5. Distinctions from related roots

خ ض ع (khuḍūʿ) compared with خ ش ع (khushūʿ)

Khuḍūʿ and khushūʿ are close in Arabic, and Persian translations sometimes render both as humility or bowing.

Their uses, however, are not entirely identical.

Lexical witnesses particularly associate khuḍūʿ with lowering the neck, submission, and compliance. Khushūʿ also expresses humility and lowliness, but in the Qur’an applies across voices, eyes, limbs, the earth, and the human condition.

For example, part of Ṭā Hā 20:108 reads:

وَخَشَعَتِ الْأَصْوَاتُ لِلرَّحْمَٰنِ فَلَا تَسْمَعُ إِلَّا هَمْسًا

wa-khashaʿati al-aṣwātu li-al-raḥmāni fa-lā tasmaʿu illā hamsan

Proposed translation of this excerpt: “And voices will fall quiet before the Entirely Merciful, so you will hear only a whisper.”

Here khushūʿ is attributed to voices.

In Fuṣṣilat 41:39, the earth is described as خَاشِعَةً, in a condition that changes when water reaches it.

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:4, by comparison, خَاضِعِينَ concerns people and their necks; in Al-Aḥzāb 33:32, khuḍūʿ describes a manner of speaking.

Thus, khuḍūʿ in its principal uses has a marked association with bowing, submitting, and yielding before another, whereas Qur’anic khushūʿ also applies to a lowered or humble state in various settings.

This should not become an absolute rule, such as claiming that khushūʿ is exclusively inward and khuḍūʿ exclusively outward. Such a rigid division does not fit all the linguistic evidence.

Study of KH SH ʿ

خ ض ع (khuḍūʿ) compared with ذ ل ل (dhull)

The root ذ ل ل concerns abasement, tameness, and compliance. It can apply to humans, animals, or things made tractable or accessible.

In some contexts, dhull and khuḍūʿ are very close, as when someone bows under another’s power.

Dhull may highlight the condition of abasement or tractability itself, whereas خضع in its clear uses emphasizes lowering oneself, yielding, or displaying submission.

Conversely, ذَلُول can describe an animal or the earth as tractable and ready for use without implying moral humiliation.

These roots therefore overlap in some instances, but every use of khuḍūʿ should not be replaced with dhull.

Study of DH L L

خ ض ع (khuḍūʿ) compared with خ ف ض (khafḍ)

The root خ ف ض means lowering or reducing. An important distinction is that khafḍ can describe lowering something without implying that it submits or becomes humble.

For example, part of Al-Isrāʾ 17:24 reads:

وَاخْفِضْ لَهُمَا جَنَاحَ الذُّلِّ مِنَ الرَّحْمَةِ

wa-ikhfiḍ lahumā janāḥa al-dhulli mina al-raḥmati

Proposed translation of this excerpt: “And lower for them the wing of humility, out of mercy.”

اخْفِضْ commands lowering, while جَنَاحَ الذُّلِّ establishes its quality and imagery.

In Luqmān 31:19, وَاغْضُضْ مِنْ صَوْتِكَ concerns lowering the voice, although ghaḍḍ belongs to a different root.

Khafḍ or ghaḍḍ in such contexts can express reducing height or intensity. Yielding in speech, as in Al-Aḥzāb 33:32, is not confined to low volume; it also involves a particular softness and inclination in the manner of speaking.

Quiet speech is not necessarily submissive speech, just as submissive speech need not consist solely of lowering one’s voice.

خ ض ع (khuḍūʿ) compared with خ ن ع (khunūʿ)

Khunūʿ in Arabic refers to abasement, humility accompanied by lowliness, and a humiliating kind of submission. In some lexical uses, it carries a more negative meaning approaching servility.

Khuḍūʿ can also describe lowliness and abasement, but is not confined to them.

For example, the submission of necks in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:4 does not necessarily carry that same humiliating connotation. Its principal subject is bowing before a divine sign.

Although these words are close in certain settings, khunūʿ’s negative moral connotations cannot be transferred to every form of khuḍūʿ.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

The root خ ض ع has no single Persian equivalent that is equally precise in every construction.

For uses concerning the neck and body, “bending,” “bowing the head,” or “lowering” is appropriate.

In human relationships, “submitting,” “yielding,” or “showing submission” may convey the meaning more accurately.

For speech, its softness and particular quality must be considered, rather than merely calmness or lowering the voice.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:4 — خَاضِعِينَ

This derivative means being submissive before a divine sign.

“Their necks would remain bowed before it” conveys the general meaning, but the lexical explanation should clarify that خَاضِعِينَ is masculine plural and also expresses the condition of the people themselves.

A reflection might explain:

“Their necks bow before that sign; that is, the people to whom those necks belong submit before it.”

This sentence explains the verse’s meaning and syntax; it need not be inserted into the direct translation.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:32 — تَخْضَعْنَ

“Do not be soft in speech” conveys one principal meaning of the expression, but may suggest that all softness, calmness, or courtesy in speaking is prohibited.

To convey the lexical distinction, the explanation can use “submissive softness in speech”: a softness and inclination in one’s manner that relates to the desire mentioned in the verse.

بِالْقَوْلِ is an important limitation here. The verse concerns the manner of speech; it does not turn the general concept of submission into an absolute ruling on every kind of conduct.

Brief root meaning

Lowering oneself, bowing, and submitting; in speech, a yielding softness and inclination toward another.

This definition suits the principal family of Qur’anic uses. The lexical branch concerning sound and clamor should be considered separately, but since it is not used in the Qur’an, it need not be included in a brief definition directed to these verses.

Conclusion

In Arabic, خ ض ع concerns descending, bending, humility, and submission. This is especially visible in bending the neck and bowing the head, but is not limited to physical movement: it can also express someone’s condition before power, need, or an interlocutor.

Arabic witnesses show this family describing descending stars, abasement under pressure, and a particular yielding softness in speech. Classical dictionaries also record a branch concerning sound and clamor, which should not be derived from bending and submission without evidence.

In the Qur’an, خَاضِعِينَ in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:4 expresses submission before a divine sign through the image of bowed necks. تَخْضَعْنَ in Al-Aḥzāb 33:32 refers to a kind of yielding in speech that, in context, relates to the listener’s inappropriate desire.

These uses show that khuḍūʿ does not always mean commendable humility, nor necessarily humiliation and abasement. Yielding speech should likewise not be equated with every kind of gentle, respectful utterance.

The root’s basic meaning can therefore be described as “lowering oneself and bowing, and, in human conduct, submitting or yielding before another.” Whether this is physical, behavioral, verbal, voluntary, or produced by pressure depends on the context of each use. Brief root meaning: Lowering oneself, bowing, and submitting; in speech, a yielding softness and inclination toward another.
