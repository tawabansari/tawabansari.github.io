From wābil, heavy rain, to wabāl, harmful consequences, and wabīl, burdensome and damaging severity

1. Meaning in early Arabic

The Arabic root و ب ل has several branches in which intensity, weight, and impact are prominent.

وَبْل and وَابِل denote heavy rain with large drops. Arabic can call abundant, heavy rainfall wābil.

In another branch, وَبِيل describes something burdensome, harsh, or unwholesome. Food or water that disagrees with the body and causes discomfort can receive this description.

وَبَال denotes heaviness, hardship, or an unpleasant consequence arising from an action or condition.

These branches are related but distinct. Heavy rain need not be harmful, and not every harmful consequence concerns rain or water.

Understanding the root requires first identifying its actual early Arabic uses.

First witness: wābil in al-Nābighah's poetry

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī contains:

وَبَاتَ ضَيْفًا لِأَرْطَاةٍ وَأَلْجَأَهُ

wa-bāta ḍayfan li-arṭātin wa-aljaʾahu

مَعَ الظَّلَامِ إِلَيْهَا وَابِلٌ سَارِ

maʿa al-ẓalāmi ilayhā wābilun sāri

“It spent the night as a guest of an arṭā shrub; heavy rain moving through the darkness had driven it there for shelter.”

This passage describes a wild bull on a difficult night.

Rain and darkness force it to seek shelter. An arṭāh is a desert tree or shrub beneath which it takes refuge.

Wābil here is heavy rain exposing the animal to difficult conditions.

The witness shows wābil naming actual heavy rainfall in early poetry.

The hardship comes from rain, darkness, and the need for shelter together. Wābil itself does not mean danger or punishment.

The verse is recorded in the collection attributed to al-Nābighah. Although the poet belongs to the pre-Islamic period, this verse's exact date is not independently established. Within that limit, it witnesses an early rain sense.

Second witness: mustawbal in Zuhayr's poetry

The Muʿallaqah attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā contains:

فَقَضَّوْا مَنَايَا بَيْنَهُمْ ثُمَّ أَصْدَرُوا

fa-qaḍḍaw manāyā baynahum thumma aṣdarū

إِلَى كَلَإٍ مُسْتَوْبَلٍ مُتَوَخَّمِ

ilā kalaʾin mustawbalin mutawakhkhami

“They brought deaths upon one another, then returned to pasture whose yield was unwholesome and harmful.”

Zuhayr discusses warfare and its heavy consequences.

كَلَأ denotes pasture vegetation; مُسْتَوْبَل describes something whose use or consumption proves disagreeable and harmful.

The unwholesome pasture pictures the fate of people caught in war and its consequences.

Mustawbal does not denote heavy rain here, but an unpleasant outcome or something harmful to use.

This illustrates the second branch: early Arabic used the family not only for rainfall but also for the harsh, disagreeable consequences of a situation.

The verse occurs in Zuhayr's well-known Muʿallaqah, with variants in wording and order. The poem belongs to the pre-Islamic tradition, though this verse's precise date cannot be independently established.

What the historical witnesses show

The two witnesses establish two clear senses:

Wābil denotes abundant, heavy rain.

Mustawbal describes something whose yield or consequence is unpleasant and harmful.

Some early lexicographers identified intensity and heaviness as common to the family.

This is conceptually understandable: both heavy rain and harmful consequences may involve intensity and burden.

It does not prove that wabāl historically developed from rain or that wābil originally named punishment.

A defensible historical definition: Early Arabic shows at least two clear branches: heavy, large-drop rain; and harmful harshness or unwholesomeness in something or its outcome.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important recorded forms include:

  • وَبَلَتِ السَّمَاءُ: the sky poured heavy rain.
  • وَبْل: abundant, heavy rain.
  • وَابِل: heavy rain with large drops.
  • مَوْبُول: something or land on which heavy rain has fallen.
  • وَبُلَ: became unwholesome or disagreeable, of food or pasture.
  • وَبِيل: harsh, heavy, unwholesome, or harmful, depending on context.
  • وَبِيلَة: in one use, unwholesome or harmful land or pasture.
  • وَبَال: hardship, harmful consequence, or damage resulting from conduct.
  • وَبَالَة: heaviness and unwholesomeness.
  • اِسْتَوْبَلَ: found something or somewhere disagreeable or unwholesome.
  • مُسْتَوْبَل: something whose yield is disagreeable or harmful.

Arabic sources also record وَبِيل and مَوْبِل for a thick stick, and وَبْل in certain uses for striking or driving forcefully.

This variety does not prove that every sense arose from one particular tool or physical action.

Quranic forms

The root occurs eight times in seven verses, in three forms:

  • وَابِلٌ: three times: Al-Baqarah 2:264 and twice in Al-Baqarah 2:265.
  • وَبَالَ: four times: Al-Māʾidah 5:95, Al-Ḥashr 59:15, Al-Taghābun 64:5, and Al-Ṭalāq 65:9.
  • وَبِيلًا: once: Al-Muzzammil 73:16.

No inflected verb from the root occurs in the Quran.

Three Quranic branches

Wābil is a noun for heavy rain. Its form is also compatible with an active participle, but Quranic usage names rainfall.

Wabāl is a noun associated with أَمْر in all four occurrences, expressing an action's burdensome, harmful consequence.

Wabīl is an adjective on the فَعِيل pattern. In Al-Muzzammil 73:16, it describes the severity of Pharaoh's seizure.

A single equivalent cannot therefore serve all three forms despite their shared root.

3. Wābil: heavy rain in two Quranic comparisons

Al-Baqarah 2:264: rain washing soil from stone

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَا تُبْطِلُوا صَدَقَاتِكُمْ بِالْمَنِّ وَالْأَذَىٰ كَالَّذِي يُنْفِقُ مَالَهُ رِئَاءَ النَّاسِ وَلَا يُؤْمِنُ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ ۖ فَمَثَلُهُ كَمَثَلِ صَفْوَانٍ عَلَيْهِ تُرَابٌ فَأَصَابَهُ وَابِلٌ فَتَرَكَهُ صَلْدًا ۖ لَا يَقْدِرُونَ عَلَىٰ شَيْءٍ مِمَّا كَسَبُوا ۗ وَاللَّهُ لَا يَهْدِي الْقَوْمَ الْكَافِرِينَ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū lā tubṭilū ṣadaqātikum bi-al-manni wa-al-adhā ka-alladhī yunfiqu mālahu riʾāʾa al-nāsi wa-lā yuʾminu bi-allāhi wa-al-yawmi al-ākhiri fa-mathaluhu ka-mathali ṣafwānin ʿalayhi turābun fa-aṣābahu wābilun fa-tarakahu ṣaldan lā yaqdirūna ʿalā shayʾin mimmā kasabū wa-allāhu lā yahdī al-qawma al-kāfirīn

Al-Baqarah 2:264

“You who have believed, do not invalidate your charitable gifts through reproach and harm, like someone who spends his wealth to be seen by people and does not believe in The God and the Last Day. His likeness is that of a smooth rock with soil upon it: heavy rain strikes it and leaves it bare and hard. They have no power over anything they have earned; and The God does not guide the disbelieving people.”

Wābil here is heavy rain falling on smooth stone.

The stone has a layer of soil that may initially look like ground ready for growth.

The soil merely covers stone, however; beneath it is no suitable bed for roots.

Heavy rain washes the surface soil away, leaving bare rock.

Wābil expresses the rain's force.

The washing away and exposed stone come from the sentence as a whole, especially صَفْوَانٍ, تُرَابٌ, فَأَصَابَهُ, and فَتَرَكَهُ صَلْدًا.

Wābil itself does not mean destroying or washing away soil.

Meaning of the comparison

The Quran applies this image to charity invalidated through reproach and harm, or giving intended for display.

Someone may give money and be thought generous. If they seek only praise, or later hurt the recipient through reproach and humiliation, the act's reality differs from its appearance.

Like soil on stone suggesting fertile ground, such giving may outwardly seem good.

The important point is that rain is not inherently an agent of evil or destruction.

It washes soil from smooth stone here; in the next verse, the same rain reaches a suitable garden and accompanies increased yield.

The comparison therefore concerns the receiving ground as well as the rain's intensity.

This distinction is central to understanding the two verses.

Al-Baqarah 2:265: rain making a garden fruitful

وَمَثَلُ الَّذِينَ يُنْفِقُونَ أَمْوَالَهُمُ ابْتِغَاءَ مَرْضَاتِ اللَّهِ وَتَثْبِيتًا مِنْ أَنْفُسِهِمْ كَمَثَلِ جَنَّةٍ بِرَبْوَةٍ أَصَابَهَا وَابِلٌ فَآتَتْ أُكُلَهَا ضِعْفَيْنِ فَإِنْ لَمْ يُصِبْهَا وَابِلٌ فَطَلٌّ ۗ وَاللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ بَصِيرٌ

wa-mathalu alladhīna yunfiqūna amwālahumu ibtighāʾa marḍāti allāhi wa-tathbītan min anfusihim ka-mathali jannatin bi-rabwatin aṣābahā wābilun fa-ātat ukulahā ḍiʿfayni fa-in lam yuṣibhā wābilun fa-ṭallun wa-allāhu bi-mā taʿmalūna baṣīr

Al-Baqarah 2:265

“And the likeness of those who spend their wealth seeking The God's pleasure and strengthening themselves from within is that of a garden on high ground: heavy rain reaches it, and it yields twice its fruit. If heavy rain does not reach it, then light rain; and The God sees what you do.”

Wābil again means heavy rain.

This time it falls on a garden situated on raised ground.

جَنَّة denotes a garden, and رَبْوَة describes elevated ground.

Unlike the previous smooth stone, this garden provides suitable ground for plant growth.

When heavy rain falls, it gives twice its yield.

The rain has the same lexical meaning in both verses, but different results.

In Al-Baqarah 2:264, it washes soil from stone.

In Al-Baqarah 2:265, it reaches a fruitful garden and increases its yield.

The differing results arise from the rainfall together with the ground receiving it.

فَإِنْ لَمْ يُصِبْهَا وَابِلٌ فَطَلٌّ

The continuation contrasts wābil with ṭall.

Ṭall denotes very light, fine rain; some usages also associate it with dew or gentle moisture.

The contrast here is clear:

Wābil is heavy, abundant rain.

Ṭall is light, gentle rain.

The Quran thus presents differing quantities or intensities of rainfall.

In the first case, abundant rain accompanies the garden's doubled yield.

In the second, light rain is mentioned if heavy rain does not reach it.

Fruitfulness therefore does not depend solely on rainfall intensity. Ground, roots, and favorable growing conditions also affect the result.

The verse does not establish that every garden in every condition produces the same yield with any amount of moisture. It pictures a suitably situated garden rather than stating a universal agricultural rule.

The message of the comparisons

Al-Baqarah 2:264 concerns charity mixed with display, reproach, and harm.

Al-Baqarah 2:265 concerns spending to seek The God's pleasure and strengthen oneself inwardly.

In the latter, increased yield relates to the giver's right intention and inner firmness.

Rain that leaves no yield on smooth stone can increase a garden's yield on suitable ground.

Giving's worth therefore depends on more than amount or outward appearance: intention and its actual basis also matter.

Forqan discusses this further in the principles on giving from one's resources and giving without reproach or humiliation.

4. Wabāl: an action's burdensome, harmful consequence

Wabāl represents another branch of و ب ل.

Arabic uses it for hardship, harm, and unpleasant consequences arising from conduct or circumstances.

An action may initially seem profitable, yet later bring serious harm.

Its result has then returned against the person who performed it.

All four Quranic occurrences associate wabāl with أَمْر, indicating the consequence or burden of the conduct concerned.

Al-Māʾidah 5:95: tasting a violation's consequences

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَا تَقْتُلُوا الصَّيْدَ وَأَنْتُمْ حُرُمٌ ۚ وَمَنْ قَتَلَهُ مِنْكُمْ مُتَعَمِّدًا فَجَزَاءٌ مِثْلُ مَا قَتَلَ مِنَ النَّعَمِ يَحْكُمُ بِهِ ذَوَا عَدْلٍ مِنْكُمْ هَدْيًا بَالِغَ الْكَعْبَةِ أَوْ كَفَّارَةٌ طَعَامُ مَسَاكِينَ أَوْ عَدْلُ ذَٰلِكَ صِيَامًا لِيَذُوقَ وَبَالَ أَمْرِهِ ۗ عَفَا اللَّهُ عَمَّا سَلَفَ ۚ وَمَنْ عَادَ فَيَنْتَقِمُ اللَّهُ مِنْهُ ۗ وَاللَّهُ عَزِيزٌ ذُو انْتِقَامٍ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū lā taqtulū al-ṣayda wa-antum ḥurumun wa-man qatalahu minkum mutaʿammidan fa-jazāʾun mithlu mā qatala mina al-naʿami yaḥkumu bihi dhawā ʿadlin minkum hadyan bāligha al-kaʿbati aw kaffāratun ṭaʿāmu masākīna aw ʿadlu dhālika ṣiyāman li-yadhūqa wabāla amrihi ʿafā allāhu ʿammā salafa wa-man ʿāda fa-yantaqimu allāhu minhu wa-allāhu ʿazīzun dhū intiqām

Al-Māʾidah 5:95

“You who have believed, do not kill game while in consecration. Whoever among you kills it deliberately owes recompense from livestock equivalent to what he killed, judged by two just people among you: an offering reaching the Kaʿbah, or expiation by feeding needy people, or its equivalent in fasting, so that he may taste his action's consequence. The God has pardoned what is past; whoever returns, The God will exact retribution from him. The God is Mighty, Possessor of retribution.”

وَبَالَ أَمْرِهِ concerns the consequence of a deliberate act.

The subject is killing game while in consecration.

The Quran states compensation or expiation, then says:

لِيَذُوقَ وَبَالَ أَمْرِهِ

The person is to taste the harmful consequence of the act.

Tasting here means experiencing its effect, rather than eating something with the mouth.

Wabāl names neither the game nor the killed animal; it refers to the violation's consequence.

مُتَعَمِّدًا is also important. The stated rule concerns deliberate violation; intention and error should not be conflated without examining other passages.

Wabāl here concerns responsibility for one's action, not every hardship or loss in life.

Al-Ḥashr 59:15: tasting the consequence of conduct

كَمَثَلِ الَّذِينَ مِنْ قَبْلِهِمْ قَرِيبًا ۖ ذَاقُوا وَبَالَ أَمْرِهِمْ وَلَهُمْ عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ

ka-mathali alladhīna min qablihim qarīban dhāqū wabāla amrihim wa-lahum ʿadhābun alīm

Al-Ḥashr 59:15

“Like those shortly before them, who tasted their conduct's consequence; and for them is a painful punishment.”

وَبَالَ أَمْرِهِمْ expresses a group's harmful consequence.

ذَاقُوا is past tense: they have tasted the result of their conduct.

The Quran then says:

وَلَهُمْ عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ

This separately states a painful punishment for them.

Wabāl and painful punishment should not be treated as completely synonymous repetition without attending to the sentence.

Tasting the consequence and painful punishment are distinguishable parts of the statement.

Wabāl does not name one specific penalty; it expresses the result's burden and unpleasantness.

Al-Taghābun 64:5: the consequence of disbelief and rejection

أَلَمْ يَأْتِكُمْ نَبَأُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا مِنْ قَبْلُ فَذَاقُوا وَبَالَ أَمْرِهِمْ وَلَهُمْ عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ

a-lam yaʾtikum nabaʾu alladhīna kafarū min qablu fa-dhāqū wabāla amrihim wa-lahum ʿadhābun alīm

Al-Taghābun 64:5

“Has news not reached you of those who disbelieved before? They tasted the harmful consequence of their conduct, and for them is a painful punishment.”

فَذَاقُوا وَبَالَ أَمْرِهِمْ uses the same combination as Al-Ḥashr 59:15.

Both concern experiencing a harmful consequence.

Here the group is identified as الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا مِنْ قَبْلُ.

Al-Taghābun 64:6 explains that Messengers brought clear signs, but the people turned away and disbelieved.

Wabāl therefore relates to a group's wrongful response to guidance presented to them.

This does not establish the same fate for every person of differing belief without establishing awareness and responsibility.

Wabāl does not mean disbelief itself, but the harmful consequence of the conduct discussed.

Al-Ṭalāq 65:9: wabāl and outcome

فَذَاقَتْ وَبَالَ أَمْرِهَا وَكَانَ عَاقِبَةُ أَمْرِهَا خُسْرًا

fa-dhāqat wabāla amrihā wa-kāna ʿāqibatu amrihā khusrā

Al-Ṭalāq 65:9

“So it tasted the harmful consequence of its conduct, and its conduct's outcome was loss.”

Two expressions stand together:

وَبَالَ أَمْرِهَا

and

عَاقِبَةُ أَمْرِهَا.

Both concern consequences, but their sentence functions differ.

فَذَاقَتْ وَبَالَ أَمْرِهَا expresses experiencing a burdensome, harmful result.

وَكَانَ عَاقِبَةُ أَمْرِهَا خُسْرًا identifies the ultimate outcome as loss.

Al-Ṭalāq 65:8 mentions settlements rebelling against their Lord's command and His Messengers.

The feminine singular agreement in فَذَاقَتْ and أَمْرِهَا refers grammatically to the singular قَرْيَةٍ in the preceding verse's كَأَيِّنْ مِنْ قَرْيَةٍ.

That construction conveys numerous settlements while قَرْيَة remains grammatically singular and feminine. Agreement follows that word, not a nonhuman plural noun in the text.

Wabāl can therefore appear in a community's collective conduct.

Collective consequences do not imply identical knowledge, intention, or responsibility for everyone.

Those causing harmful conduct, those following it, and those suffering its effects must be distinguished.

5. Wabīl: burdensome, damaging severity

Al-Muzzammil 73:16: Pharaoh's severe seizure

فَعَصَىٰ فِرْعَوْنُ الرَّسُولَ فَأَخَذْنَاهُ أَخْذًا وَبِيلًا

fa-ʿaṣā firʿawnu al-rasūla fa-akhadhnāhu akhdhan wabīlā

Al-Muzzammil 73:16

“Pharaoh disobeyed the Messenger, so We seized him with a severe seizure.”

وَبِيلًا modifies أَخْذًا.

أَخْذًا is an accusative verbal noun from أ خ ذ, reinforcing فَأَخَذْنَاهُ.

وَبِيلًا describes the nature of that seizure.

Here it means harsh, heavy, and grievous.

The verse mentions Pharaoh disobeying the divine Messenger, then the severity of his seizure.

Wabīl does not mean heavy rain here, despite sharing a family with wābil.

Nor does the adjective specify the exact method of seizure.

Other Quranic verses describe Pharaoh's fate and drowning, but those details should not become the adjective's lexical meaning.

Here it describes severity and harshness.

Wabīl and wabāl

Wabīl is an adjective describing weight, intensity, or unwholesomeness.

Wabāl is a noun expressing an action's harmful consequence in Quranic usage.

Describing a blow as heavy concerns its intensity.

Speaking of a decision's wabāl directs attention to its harmful result.

وَبِيلًا in Al-Muzzammil 73:16 cannot therefore be rendered simply as “the consequence of conduct”: it modifies أَخْذًا.

“A severe seizure” better preserves the construction.

6. Comparison with related roots

و ب ل and ط ل ل: heavy and light rain

Wābil denotes heavy, large-drop rain.

Ṭall concerns fine, light rain and sometimes dew.

Al-Baqarah 2:265 places them in one sentence.

Their juxtaposition clarifies the difference in rainfall intensity.

Wābil should not therefore be assumed always more beneficial: both relate here to a garden suited to growth.

Rainfall's effect and value also depend on ground and plants.

و ب ل and م ط ر: heavy rain and rainfall

Maṭar concerns rain and raining.

Its scope is broader and does not itself specify a particular intensity.

Wābil names a kind of heavy rain.

Several Quranic maṭar contexts concern punishment of earlier peoples; this does not make rain intrinsically synonymous with punishment in Arabic.

Wābil itself appears in Al-Baqarah 2:264 and Al-Baqarah 2:265 with differing results.

Rainfall intensity and an event's moral significance are distinct concepts.

و ب ل and غ ي ث: rain and relieving rain

Ghayth concerns rain and, in some uses, relief or help arriving.

Al-Shūrā 42:28 describes rain being sent down after people have despaired.

Rain there is associated with mercy and the relief of hardship.

Wābil primarily expresses rainfall intensity.

In these uses, wābil emphasizes a feature of the rain, while ghayth can occur in contexts of relief and needed rainfall.

This is not absolute: heavy rain can also relieve drought.

و ب ل and و د ق: heavy rain and rainfall

Wadq is used for rainfall in the Quran.

Al-Nūr 24:43 describes clouds and rain emerging from them.

Wadq there concerns rainfall itself, while wābil describes heavy, intense rain.

Every wābil is rainfall, but wadq does not necessarily convey wābil's intensity.

و ب ل and ع ق ب: harmful consequence and outcome

ʿĀqibah concerns a result or eventual outcome.

It may be good or bad, as context determines.

Quranic wabāl concerns harmful consequences.

Their juxtaposition in Al-Ṭalāq 65:9 clarifies the distinction.

Wabāl expresses the result's unpleasantness; ʿāqibah points to the eventual outcome, identified as loss through خُسْرًا.

Not every use of ʿāqibah should therefore be reduced to wabāl.

و ب ل and و ب ق: harmful consequence and destruction

و ب ق concerns perishing and causing destruction.

In Al-Shūrā 42:34, يُوبِقْهُنَّ concerns possible destruction of ships.

Quranic wabāl instead denotes a harmful consequence of conduct.

Such a consequence may cause death, but not every wabāl involves bodily destruction.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:95, the offender experiences a consequence through compensation and expiation; the verse does not itself concern the offender's physical destruction.

The roots should not be equated merely because their letters and harmful associations are close.

و ب ل and و ب أ: unwholesomeness and disease

Some Arabic uses apply wabīl to food or water that disagrees with the body.

و ب أ instead concerns epidemic disease and unhealthy or disease-producing conditions.

Some early usages bring the fields close, with reported phonetic variation between forms.

This does not make Quranic wabāl mean infectious disease or wabīl necessarily mean poisonous.

Each form's meaning comes from its construction and context.

7. Questions of interpretation and translation

Is intensity the whole root's core?

Intensity and heaviness offer a useful conceptual link between branches.

Heavy rain, a heavy blow, unwholesome food, and harmful consequences can be related in this respect.

Intensity alone is nevertheless insufficient: many other roots also concern intensity and weight.

A precise account defines each branch according to its evidence.

For wābil, rainfall's intensity and abundance are decisive.

For wabīl, harshness, burden, or unwholesomeness is prominent.

For wabāl, an action's burdensome, harmful consequence is central in the Quran.

Keeping these distinct is more accurate than a single definition erasing their differences.

Is wābil always harmful rain?

No. Two consecutive verses themselves show different results of heavy rain.

It can wash soil off stone or help a garden bear fruit.

Wābil alone therefore does not mean punishment, destruction, or blessing.

Those outcomes come from circumstances and context.

Does wabāl mean every unpleasant event?

No. A person may suffer others' oppression or a natural disaster.

Such harm does not itself prove they are tasting their own conduct's wabāl.

Quranic وَبَالَ أَمْرِهِ and وَبَالَ أَمْرِهِمْ explicitly connect the consequence with the persons' or groups' conduct.

That connection foregrounds responsibility but cannot be extended without evidence to every suffering or event.

In collective events, innocent people may suffer others' conduct. That does not equate their moral responsibility with the perpetrators'.

Does wabīl mean punishment in the hereafter?

Al-Muzzammil 73:16 uses it for Pharaoh's severe seizure.

The adjective expresses harshness and burden.

It is not thereby the name of a specific otherworldly punishment in every use.

In Al-Ḥashr 59:15 and Al-Taghābun 64:5, wabāl stands alongside painful punishment.

The harmful consequence, severity of treatment, and punishment mentioned in each context must therefore be distinguished.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

Suitable equivalents for the established branches include:

وَبَلَ: poured heavy rain; elsewhere, struck or drove forcefully.

وَبْل: abundant, heavy rain.

وَابِل: heavy, intense rain.

مَوْبُول: rained upon; land receiving heavy rain.

وَبُلَ: became unwholesome or disagreeable, of food or pasture.

وَبِيل: harsh, heavy, or unwholesome, according to context.

وَبَال: harmful consequence, hardship, or damage arising from conduct.

وَبَالَة: heaviness, unwholesomeness, and unpleasantness.

اِسْتَوْبَلَ: found something disagreeable or unwholesome.

مُسْتَوْبَل: yielding a harmful benefit or consequence.

Quranic forms

Al-Baqarah 2:264 — وَابِلٌ

“Heavy rain.”

Al-Baqarah 2:265 — وَابِلٌ

“Heavy rain.”

Both preserve the same rain sense; differing results arise from context and the receiving ground.

Al-Māʾidah 5:95 — وَبَالَ أَمْرِهِ

“His action's consequence.”

Al-Ḥashr 59:15 — وَبَالَ أَمْرِهِمْ

“Their conduct's consequence.”

Al-Taghābun 64:5 — وَبَالَ أَمْرِهِمْ

“The harmful consequence of their conduct.”

Al-Ṭalāq 65:9 — وَبَالَ أَمْرِهَا

“The harmful consequence of its conduct.”

“Harmful consequence” explains wabāl more precisely in all four. Where the surrounding sentence makes the harm unmistakable, “consequence” can suffice in a fluent rendering.

Al-Muzzammil 73:16 — وَبِيلًا

“Severe.”

In the full construction:

فَأَخَذْنَاهُ أَخْذًا وَبِيلًا

“So We seized him with a severe seizure.”

This preserves the verbal noun and its adjective.

9. Conclusion

The root has several established early Arabic branches.

Wābil is heavy, large-drop rain, witnessed in poetry attributed to al-Nābighah and used in both comparisons in Al-Baqarah.

Wabīl expresses harshness, burden, and unwholesomeness; Quranically, it describes the severity of Pharaoh's seizure.

Wabāl is an action's burdensome, harmful consequence, appearing in four verses with أَمْر.

The Zuhayr witness also illustrates early Arabic familiarity with unwholesomeness and unpleasant results.

Intensity, weight, and impact conceptually connect these branches, without sufficient evidence that one historically originated all the others.

The two Al-Baqarah verses use wābil with the same meaning but different effects on stone and garden. Rain's value and result are not determined by the word alone.

The four wabāl occurrences connect harmful consequence with human or communal conduct. Every misfortune should not therefore be labeled the sufferer's own consequence without evidence.

Final definition for Forqan:

In its rainfall branch, و ب ل denotes abundant, heavy rain with large drops. Its harshness branch describes something burdensome, disagreeable, or harmful. Quranic wabāl denotes an action's harmful, burdensome consequence.

The family spans nature, human experience, and moral responsibility; each derivative's meaning must be determined through its own construction and context.
