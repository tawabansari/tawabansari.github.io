Qur’anic Root A F L

Setting, disappearing, and passing out of sight: from the setting of heavenly bodies to Ibrāhīm’s argument about the true object of worship

Qur’anic and lexical forms

أَفَلَ
(afala)
Masculine singular active perfect; two Qur’anic occurrences concerning the kawkab and moon

أَفَلَتْ
(afalat)
Feminine singular active perfect; one Qur’anic occurrence concerning the sun

الْآفِلِينَ
(al-āfilīna)
Sound masculine plural active participle, accusative object of uḥibbu; one Qur’anic occurrence

يَأْفُلُ / يَأْفِلُ
(yaʾfulu / yaʾfilu)

أَفْل / أُفُول
(afl / ufūl)

آفِل / آفِلَة
(āfil / āfilah)

آفِلُونَ / أُفَّل
(āfilūna / uffal)

(afīl / afīlah)

(ifāl)

Arabic A F L concerns becoming absent or hidden from sight. Its most familiar use concerns stars, the moon, and the sun when a heavenly body passes out of an observer’s field of view after being visible. أَفَلَ النَّجْمُ (afala al-najmu) means the star disappeared; أَفَلَتِ الشَّمْسُ (afalati al-shamsu) means the sun set.

The Qur’an uses this root in Ibrāhīm’s story, in three consecutive ayahs of Al-Anʿām. He first observes a bright celestial body, a kawkab, in the night sky. When it disappears, he says لَا أُحِبُّ الْآفِلِينَ (lā uḥibbu al-āfilīna), “I do not love those that set.” He then sees the moon rising and, when it sets, speaks of needing his Lord’s guidance. Finally, after observing the sun and its setting, he declares himself free of associating partners with The God.

Setting is more than an isolated natural phenomenon in these ayahs: its observation contributes to Ibrāhīm’s argument. Something visible for a time and then hidden, however bright or great, is not the Creator of the heavens and earth toward whom he turns at the conclusion.

The lexical meaning of أُفُول (ufūl) must nevertheless be distinguished from Ibrāhīm’s conclusion. Setting does not mean the heavenly body’s destruction, the end of its light, or its ceasing to exist. The root alone also does not judge createdness, dependence, or fitness for worship; these come from the argument and context.

1. Qur’anic forms and semantic core

A F L occurs four times in three ayahs, comprising two principal morphological forms.

In Al-Anʿām 6:76, أَفَلَ (afala) is a masculine singular perfect verb referring to the kawkab’s disappearance. The same ayah has الْآفِلِينَ (al-āfilīna), a masculine plural active participle in lā uḥibbu al-āfilīna. It is a sound masculine plural in the accusative, the object of uḥibbu.

In Al-Anʿām 6:77, afala is again a masculine singular perfect verb, this time concerning the moon.

In Al-Anʿām 6:78, أَفَلَتْ (afalat) is a feminine singular perfect verb concerning the sun’s setting.

There are thus three verbal occurrences and one active participle. Afala and afalat differ according to the subject’s grammatical gender; the principal meaning remains the same.

Arabic has أَفَلَ يَأْفُلُ (afala/yaʾfulu). The imperfect يَأْفِلُ (yaʾfilu) is also recorded, with verbal nouns أَفْل (afl) and ufūl.

آفِل (āfil) is the masculine singular active participle, آفِلَة (āfilah) the feminine, and آفِلُونَ (āfilūna) or آفِلِينَ (āfilīna) its masculine plural. Arabic also records other plurals such as أُفَّل (uffal).

Of the active-participle forms, only plural al-āfilīna occurs in the Qur’an. The lexical singulars and other plurals should not be counted as additional Qur’anic occurrences.

The Qur’anic branch’s semantic core may be expressed as:

Becoming absent and passing out of sight after being visible, especially the disappearance of heavenly bodies from an observer’s visible horizon.

Three things must remain distinct: the body itself, its being seen, and the loss of the opportunity to observe it.

When a star sets, what is directly reported is its disappearance from sight. The verb itself does not say that the star has been destroyed or no longer exists.

2. Earlier Arabic: absence, setting, and other branches

أَفَلَ النَّجْمُ (afala al-najmu): a star becoming absent

Earlier Arabic uses afala for stars, the moon, and the sun.

Afala al-najmu means the star became hidden from sight.

أَفَلَ الْقَمَرُ (afala al-qamaru) means the moon disappeared or set.

Afalati al-shamsu means the sun set.

These uses concern leaving the field of observation. For heavenly bodies, this is often recognized through their descending and disappearing at the visible reach of sky and earth.

The word is not limited to a particular astronomical explanation. It describes the observational situation: a body previously seen in the sky is no longer seen.

A broader lexical use, أَفَلَ فُلَانٌ عَنِ الْبَلَدِ (afala fulānun ʿani al-baladi), concerns someone leaving or becoming absent from a town or land.

Absence is thus broader than a star’s setting, although heavenly bodies remain especially prominent in ufūl’s familiar use.

A poetic example after revelation: the Pleiades’ conjunction and disappearance

A poem by Kuthayyir ʿAzzah says:

فَدَعْ عَنْكَ سُعْدَى إِنَّمَا تُسْعِفُ النَّوَىٰ
قِرَانَ الثُّرَيَّا مَرَّةً ثُمَّ تَأْفِلُ

fa-daʿ ʿanka suʿdā innamā tusʿifu al-nawā / qirāna al-thurayyā marratan thumma taʾfilu

“Leave Suʿdā aside: separation grants a meeting only once, at the Pleiades’ conjunction [with the lunar crescent]; then the Pleiades disappear from sight.”

An older explanation relates “the Pleiades’ conjunction” to their apparent closeness to the lunar crescent. The poet compares a rare, brief meeting with the beloved, followed by separation, to this conjunction and disappearance. The subject extends beyond ordinary nightly rising and setting.

Taʾfilu here concerns passing out of sight, rather than the Pleiades’ destruction.

This matters for the root’s meaning. After disappearing, the Pleiades have not ceased to exist; they are no longer observable in that situation.

The line is also transmitted in older accounts of the times when stars appear and disappear. This transmission’s Suʿdā and taʾfilu are retained; Arabic also records taʾfulu as a lexical form, distinct from this witness’s wording.

Kuthayyir ʿAzzah is an Umayyad-period poet. His line therefore illustrates older Arabic after revelation, rather than pre-Islamic poetry, though its verb agrees with familiar lexical usage. It alone does not establish a pre-Qur’anic date for the setting sense; the Zuhayr witness below concerns young camels, rather than a star’s setting.

Disappearance of milk

Another recorded use, أَفَلَتِ الْمُرْضِعُ (afalati al-murḍiʿu), concerns a milk-giving animal whose milk has ceased. Lexical transmissions also give أَفِلَتِ الْمُرْضِعُ (afilati al-murḍiʿu), with kasrah on fāʾ.

What disappears here is the milk, rather than the animal itself.

This can relate to disappearance or loss in a general sense, but the historical origin should not be asserted beyond the evidence.

It does not make Qur’anic afala mean drying up, losing ability, or a heavenly body’s power ceasing.

Al-Anʿām concerns observing the kawkab, moon, and sun and their disappearance, rather than the loss of their power or substance.

Other uses unrelated to setting

Some Arabic sources record أَفِلَ الرَّجُلُ (afila al-rajulu) for a lively, active person.

أَفَلَ الْحَمْلُ فِي الرَّحِمِ (afala al-ḥamlu fī al-raḥimi) concerns a fetus becoming established in the womb; āfil can also describe a pregnant animal.

These differ from the Qur’anic disappearance of heavenly bodies.

Some branches may have historical connections, but identical root letters alone do not prove one shared semantic image.

Without evidence, human liveliness cannot be derived from a star’s setting, or a fetus’s establishment from the sun’s disappearance.

Recording lexical meaning must remain distinct from explaining its historical origin.

أَفِيل (afīl) and إِفَال (ifāl): young camels

Another independent branch recorded in older Arabic is afīl and its plural ifāl.

Afīl denotes a young camel, especially one in its early years of growth. أَفِيلَة (afīlah) is its feminine.

Lexicographers differ over the precise age. Some limit it to a particular age, while others use it for a broader range of young camels.

The Muʿallaqah of Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā, a poet before revelation, transmits this wording:

فَأَصْبَحَ يَجْرِي فِيهِمْ مِنْ تِلَادِكُمْ
مَغَانِمُ شَتَّى مِنْ إِفَالٍ مُزَنَّمِ

fa-aṣbaḥa yajrī fīhim min tilādikum / maghānimu shattā min ifālin muzannami

“Thus various gains in marked young camels began flowing among them from your longstanding, inherited wealth.”

The line concerns wealth and camels paid to end bloodshed and conflict. In this wording, muzannam describes a camel’s marking; for the wording with al-muzannam, a particular stallion’s name is also transmitted as an explanation.

Ifāl in this wording denotes young camels.

The line has transmitted variants. The first phrase differs in some versions, and another has نِتَاج (nitāj) instead of ifāl. The recorded use must therefore be distinguished from certainty over this particular witness’s wording. Its precise composition date is unknown, and the extant transmission should be read with those variants in mind.

Afīl as a young camel should not be derived from a star’s setting.

Older lexical tradition also distinguishes absence and disappearance from the name for a young camel as two branches of this root.

None of the young-camel forms occurs in the Qur’an. All Qur’anic occurrences belong to heavenly bodies’ setting and disappearance.

3. فَلَمَّا أَفَلَ: Ibrāhīm’s first observation

Al-Anʿām 6:76 says:

فَلَمَّا جَنَّ عَلَيْهِ اللَّيْلُ رَأَىٰ كَوْكَبًا قَالَ هَٰذَا رَبِّي فَلَمَّا أَفَلَ قَالَ لَا أُحِبُّ الْآفِلِينَ

fa-lammā janna ʿalayhi al-laylu raʾā kawkaban qāla hādhā rabbī fa-lammā afala qāla lā uḥibbu al-āfilīna

“Then, when night covered him, he saw a kawkab and said, ‘This is my Lord.’ Then, when it disappeared from sight, he said, ‘I do not love those that set.’”

Al-Anʿām 6:76

The ayah first presents the darkness of night.

Janna ʿalayhi al-laylu describes night’s darkness covering him. Ibrāhīm then observes a kawkab.

Arabic كَوْكَب (kawkab) denotes a bright celestial body visible in the sky. It should not be restricted to a named star or planet without independent evidence.

After observing it, Ibrāhīm says هَٰذَا رَبِّي (hādhā rabbī), “This is my Lord.”

When it sets, he gives another judgment: lā uḥibbu al-āfilīna.

What exactly happens in the ayah’s afala?

Afala is a masculine singular perfect verb whose subject refers to that kawkab.

The ayah describes a body visible to Ibrāhīm and then disappearing from his sight.

This change from being visible to disappearing is setting’s direct meaning here.

The ayah does not say the kawkab was destroyed, its light ended forever, or it ceased to exist in the sky.

Nor must its disappearance necessarily be attributed to an unusual astronomical event.

A kawkab’s disappearance can be a familiar phenomenon in the movement and observation of heavenly bodies. Disappearance after visibility suffices for afala’s lexical meaning.

لَا أُحِبُّ الْآفِلِينَ: why a plural active participle?

Ibrāhīm first speaks of one kawkab. After it sets, rather than merely saying “I do not love this,” he uses al-āfilīna.

Āfil is the active participle of afala and denotes something or someone that sets or disappears.

Al-āfilīna is its masculine plural.

The shift from one particular kawkab to a group sharing a description matters. His statement extends beyond that body: he makes setting a criterion for judging a range of things with that condition.

The masculine plural does not establish that stars are being presented as humans or beings with reason and will.

Arabic can describe things with forms also used for persons when they are treated as objects of worship or as having personal standing.

Here the kawkab is considered in a statement about lordship, making al-āfilīna understandable within that setting.

The form itself does not determine heavenly bodies’ nature or awareness.

لَا أُحِبُّ (lā uḥibbu): dislike or rejection of fitness for worship?

Uḥibbu belongs to Ḥ B B and concerns loving.

The direct translation is “I do not love those that set.”

The subject is more than personal liking or displeasure toward a natural phenomenon.

Ibrāhīm has just spoken of the kawkab’s being his Lord. His rejection of those that set continues that judgment about its fitness to occupy the position of Lord.

This does not mean people should not find stars beautiful or enjoy the sky.

Nor does it make loving every transient or mortal thing morally wrong. People can love parents, children, and other created beings without treating them as objects of worship.

Ordinary affection for creatures is not the question; placing a phenomenon in the position of Lord is.

Lā uḥibbu’s direct meaning should be preserved while its purpose is explained within lordship and worship.

4. فَلَمَّا أَفَلَ: the moon and guidance

Al-Anʿām 6:77 says:

فَلَمَّا رَأَى الْقَمَرَ بَازِغًا قَالَ هَٰذَا رَبِّي فَلَمَّا أَفَلَ قَالَ لَئِنْ لَمْ يَهْدِنِي رَبِّي لَأَكُونَنَّ مِنَ الْقَوْمِ الضَّالِّينَ

fa-lammā raʾā al-qamara bāzighan qāla hādhā rabbī fa-lammā afala qāla la-in lam yahdinī rabbī la-akūnanna mina al-qawmi al-ḍāllīna

“Then, when he saw the moon rising, he said, ‘This is my Lord.’ But when it disappeared from sight, he said, ‘Unless my Lord guides me, I shall surely be among the people who go astray.’”

Al-Anʿām 6:77

Two expressions concerning a heavenly body’s observation occur together: بَازِغًا (bāzighan) and afala.

Bāzighan comes from B Z GH and concerns appearing or rising. Morphologically, it is an accusative active participle describing the moon’s state when observed.

Afala belongs to A F L and concerns becoming hidden and leaving the field of view.

These two forms describe different conditions within one course of observation.

The moon first rises and becomes visible, then sets and disappears.

Rising and setting: two directions within observation

بُزُوغ (buzūgh) here expresses the beginning of a heavenly body’s appearance.

Ufūl marks that appearance’s end within an observer’s field of view.

The concepts are connected but not identical.

Translating both bāzighan and afala as “moved” would erase the distinction between the moon’s rising and disappearance.

Bāzighan also does not mean the moon was created: it existed before appearing within Ibrāhīm’s view.

Likewise, afala does not mean the moon was destroyed.

The ayah presents two observational conditions, rather than the moon’s beginning and end of existence.

لَئِنْ لَمْ يَهْدِنِي رَبِّي: what changes in Ibrāhīm’s speech?

After the kawkab sets in the first ayah, Ibrāhīm says lā uḥibbu al-āfilīna.

After the moon sets in the second, he speaks of needing his Lord’s guidance.

This change in speech matters.

The first ayah presents his judgment of those that set.

The second explicitly brings guidance into the discussion.

Rabbī in la-in lam yahdinī rabbī denotes the Lord from whom Ibrāhīm seeks guidance.

This raises a question: is he still seeking to know his Lord, or conducting an argument before his people so that their gods’ inadequacy becomes clear?

A F L alone cannot settle this.

The preceding and following contexts must be considered together. In either reading, the moon’s setting is a principal element directing the argument.

At the end of observing the moon, Ibrāhīm does not accept it as the final answer to lordship.

Does the moon’s setting mean guidance is lost?

The ayah itself does not say so.

The moon can be useful in darkness for observation, timekeeping, and orientation. Its disappearance does not mean all these benefits are destroyed.

Ibrāhīm is discussing its lordship, rather than examining it merely as a useful natural phenomenon.

A heavenly body’s natural benefit should therefore not be equated with its fitness for worship.

Something may be highly useful to human life without that usefulness establishing its lordship.

5. فَلَمَّا أَفَلَتْ: the sun and the argument’s conclusion

Al-Anʿām 6:78 says:

فَلَمَّا رَأَى الشَّمْسَ بَازِغَةً قَالَ هَٰذَا رَبِّي هَٰذَا أَكْبَرُ فَلَمَّا أَفَلَتْ قَالَ يَا قَوْمِ إِنِّي بَرِيءٌ مِمَّا تُشْرِكُونَ

fa-lammā raʾā al-shamsa bāzighatan qāla hādhā rabbī hādhā akbaru fa-lammā afalat qāla yā qawmi innī barīʾun mimmā tushrikūna

“Then, when he saw the sun rising, he said, ‘This is my Lord; this is greater.’ But when it set, he said, ‘My people, I am free of what you associate as partners.’”

Al-Anʿām 6:78

Buzūgh and ufūl are again placed opposite one another.

This time the heavenly body is the sun.

The sun is prominent in brightness and apparent size within earthly observation. Ibrāhīm’s speech includes hādhā akbaru, “this is greater.”

Nevertheless, the sun also sets.

Its size and brightness do not remove its setting.

Why is أَفَلَتْ (afalat) feminine?

The preceding two ayahs have afala.

For the sun, the form is afalat.

The difference comes from the subject’s grammatical gender.

Kawkab and qamar are masculine in these constructions, whereas Arabic shams is feminine.

Afalat is therefore the same perfect verb agreeing with a feminine subject.

This morphological difference does not make the sun’s setting a different kind of destruction or change.

All three occurrences concern a heavenly body’s disappearance from view.

هَٰذَا أَكْبَرُ (hādhā akbaru): does size establish lordship?

Ibrāhīm says of the sun, hādhā akbaru.

This follows his observation of the kawkab and moon in the narrative.

Attention is therefore directed to its size in comparison with what has already been seen.

The ayah shows that apparent size does not suffice to identify the Lord.

Like the kawkab and moon, the sun appears within sight and then disappears.

This does not establish that Ibrāhīm scientifically compared every body’s actual dimensions.

The Qur’an does not name the first kawkab or supply distances or numerical dimensions for these bodies.

Hādhā akbaru concerns the sun’s evident feature within this scene of observation.

Making this an astronomical theory about the absolute sizes of stars, the moon, and the sun exceeds the ayah’s statement.

يَا قَوْمِ (yā qawmi): the argument’s audience becomes explicit

After the sun sets, Ibrāhīm says:

Yā qawmi innī barīʾun mimmā tushrikūna, “My people, I am free of what you associate as partners.”

He now explicitly addresses his people.

This matters for understanding all three ayahs: the observations occur within discourse connected with his people’s beliefs.

Ibrāhīm explicitly dissociates himself from what they associate as partners.

Barīʾun belongs to B R A and expresses dissociation and separating himself from that belief.

This should not become part of afalat’s meaning.

Afalat reports the sun’s setting; barīʾun states Ibrāhīm’s position toward associating partners with The God.

Their relationship matters, while each makes its own semantic contribution.

6. Was Ibrāhīm seeking personally or arguing with his people?

Al-Anʿām 6:76, Al-Anʿām 6:77, and Al-Anʿām 6:78 require attention to the story’s beginning and continuation.

Al-Anʿām 6:74 presents Ibrāhīm criticizing his father and his father’s people for taking idols as gods, considering them in manifest error.

Al-Anʿām 6:75 speaks of showing Ibrāhīm the dominion of the heavens and earth in connection with certainty.

The observation of the kawkab, moon, and sun then begins.

Al-Anʿām 6:80 also states that Ibrāhīm’s people disputed with him.

Finally, Al-Anʿām 6:83 calls the argument a proof The God gave him against his people.

The observation is therefore connected with his argument about lordship and association of partners, rather than an isolated account of watching nature.

Two readings to examine

One reading takes Ibrāhīm to discover, step by step through observation and comparison, that treating these bodies as Lord is wrong.

The other takes him already to reject his people’s belief and to say hādhā rabbī argumentatively: he temporarily accepts their premise in speech, then challenges it through setting, exposing their gods’ inadequacy.

The preceding context, yā qawmi, and the final characterization as a proof give the second reading substantial support.

A prior theological conception should not erase hādhā rabbī’s direct meaning or suggest that the phrase is absent from the Qur’an.

Conversely, reporting the phrase alone does not establish that Ibrāhīm actually believed the kawkab, moon, and sun to be Lord at every stage, moving from one worship to another.

The ayahs report his speech and argument. Identifying every inward stage of knowledge and belief requires further evidence.

Both readings preserve one point: the setting of the kawkab, moon, and sun plays a central role in rejecting their fitness to occupy the position of Lord.

The root study should clarify that defensible meaning without changing the word’s lexical sense to settle every interpretive disagreement.

7. From setting heavenly bodies to turning toward their Creator

The next ayah says:

إِنِّي وَجَّهْتُ وَجْهِيَ لِلَّذِي فَطَرَ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ حَنِيفًا وَمَا أَنَا مِنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ

innī wajjahtu wajhiya li-lladhī faṭara al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍa ḥanīfan wa-mā anā mina al-mushrikīna

“I have turned my face toward Him who originated the heavens and earth, with undivided devotion (ḥanīf); I am not among those who associate partners.”

Al-Anʿām 6:79

This gives the argument’s clear conclusion.

In the preceding three ayahs, Ibrāhīm observes bodies appearing in the sky and then setting.

He now speaks of alladhī faṭara al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍa, Him who originated the heavens and earth.

The distinction between the created and the Creator becomes evident.

The kawkab, moon, and sun belong within the heavens-and-earth order. Ibrāhīm finally directs himself toward its Originator.

The argument therefore extends beyond “something disappeared, so it is not a god.”

Its ending supplies a broader criterion: the true object of worship is the Creator of the heavens and earth, rather than one observable phenomenon within the world.

Does setting mean destruction or dependence?

Philosophy can discuss beings’ changeability, dependence, and limits.

A F L itself does not directly mean existential dependence, needing a cause, or destruction.

Qur’anic setting is an observable phenomenon: a heavenly body disappears from sight.

Ibrāhīm uses this observation in his argument and finally turns toward the Creator of the heavens and earth.

The passage can support an argument distinguishing the created from the Creator. Every stage of that argument should not be presented as ufūl’s lexical meaning.

A heavenly body’s disappearance differs from The God’s being unseen

This distinction especially matters.

Ibrāhīm says he does not love those that set. This does not make everything invisible to human eyes unworthy of attention or belief.

The kawkab, moon, and sun appear and disappear within observation.

The God is identified at the argument’s conclusion as Creator of the heavens and earth.

Lā uḥibbu al-āfilīna therefore does not require the true object of worship to remain physically visible before human eyes.

That inference would place an observed body’s setting on the same level as The God’s being unseen, whereas the ayahs distinguish their subjects.

Setting ends a body’s appearance within sight. The God’s being unseen cannot be reduced to that visual phenomenon.

Does Ibrāhīm consider the sun and moon worthless?

No. Rejecting a phenomenon’s lordship does not deny its existence or benefit.

Other Qur’anic ayahs count the moon and sun among The God’s signs. They contribute to human life, timekeeping, and creation’s order.

Usefulness does not itself establish fitness for worship.

Fuṣṣilat 41:37 explicitly directs people not to prostrate to sun or moon, but to The God who created them.

This agrees with Ibrāhīm’s conclusion.

The sun and moon are signs, rather than worship’s final destination.

Ufūl in Ibrāhīm’s story thus contributes to an argument distinguishing creatures from their Creator.

8. Distinguishing A F L from related roots

A F L (أَفَلَ, afala) and GH R B (غَرَبَ, gharaba)

Study of GH R B

Arabic gharaba concerns going away, becoming hidden, and, for heavenly bodies, setting.

Afala also concerns setting and disappearance for the kawkab, moon, and sun.

Older Arabic sometimes explains the verbs through one another.

GH R B has a broader family, including west, gharīb, and ghurbah: a stranger and estrangement or being away from home.

Familiar ufūl particularly describes a heavenly body’s disappearance.

“Set” can therefore translate afala in some sentences without making every GH R B derivative identical with A F L.

A F L (أَفَلَ, afala) and GH Y B (غَابَ, ghāba)

Study of GH Y B

Ghāba concerns becoming hidden or absent from observation or knowledge.

Afala also expresses a kind of absence.

Absence can concern a person, object, event, or hidden matter, whereas setting prominently concerns heavenly bodies disappearing.

Every heavenly body that sets becomes absent from view, but not everything absent has necessarily set.

Qur’anic ghayb also has a broader semantic field and should not be equated with a star’s setting.

A F L (أَفَلَ, afala) and B Z GH (بَزَغَ, bazagha)

Study of B Z GH

Arabic bazagha concerns rising and appearing.

Al-Anʿām 6:77 and Al-Anʿām 6:78 place it opposite afala.

Bāzighan and bāzighatan describe the moon and sun rising.

Afala and afalat indicate their disappearance.

The verbs therefore represent two sides of observation here: appearing and disappearing from view.

This contrast does not restrict every Arabic buzūgh to astronomical rising or every ufūl to setting in the west.

Each use requires its own context.

A F L (أَفَلَ, afala) and A F Q (أُفُق, ufuq)

Study of A F Q

The roots are close in letters but independent.

Ufuq belongs to A F Q and denotes a region or reach of the visible expanse.

Afala belongs to A F L and expresses a heavenly body’s disappearance.

A body may disappear at the horizon. The horizon names the region of observation; setting names the event.

Afala should therefore not mean “became a horizon” or “traversed the horizon.”

Similar forms also do not prove that the words historically developed from one shared root.

A F L (أَفَلَ, afala) and KH F Y (خَفِيَ, khafiya)

Study of KH F Y

Khafiya concerns being hidden or not manifest.

Something may be hidden from an observer from the outset without having set in that situation.

For heavenly bodies, afala highlights disappearance after a stage of visibility and observation.

“Disappearing” can therefore explain setting, but “being hidden” does not convey its entire sense.

Ibrāhīm first observes a heavenly body and then sees its setting, making this distinction clear.

A F L (أُفُول, ufūl) and F N Y (فَنَاء, fanāʾ)

Study of F N Y

Fanāʾ concerns passing away or the end of something’s existence or continuance.

For heavenly bodies, ufūl concerns disappearance from sight.

The sun is not destroyed by the fact of its setting.

The moon’s disappearance likewise does not necessarily end its existence.

Setting and annihilation are therefore not synonyms.

“Decline” can describe fading power, fame, or glory metaphorically. Such a metaphor is intelligible, but should not replace afala’s direct meaning in Al-Anʿām.

A F L (أَفَلَ, afala) and J N N (جَنَّ, janna)

Study of J N N

Before the kawkab is seen in Al-Anʿām 6:76, night covers Ibrāhīm.

Janna belongs to J N N and here concerns covering and enveloping in darkness.

Afala concerns the kawkab’s disappearance.

The verbs can share a scene of darkness and observing the sky without meaning the same thing.

Janna al-laylu expresses night coming and covering with darkness; afala al-kawkabu describes the kawkab’s disappearance from view.

These events should not all become a general expression such as “it grew dark.”

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

Two principal English equivalents suit the Qur’anic uses of A F L: “setting” and “disappearing from sight.”

“Setting” is natural and familiar, especially for the sun and moon.

“Disappearing from sight” clarifies absence after visibility and can also apply to the kawkab and other heavenly bodies.

“Being destroyed,” “dying,” or “vanishing forever” are unsuitable equivalents for the verb itself.

Al-Anʿām 6:76

For fa-lammā afala, the principal proposal is:

“Then, when it disappeared from sight.”

In the complete sentence:

“Then, when night covered him, he saw a kawkab and said, ‘This is my Lord.’ Then, when that kawkab disappeared from sight, he said, ‘I do not love those that set.’”

For al-āfilīna, “those that set” preserves the plural description, extending Ibrāhīm’s statement beyond one particular kawkab.

“Those that disappear” is also defensible, but may suggest a broader sense than the ayah’s particular celestial use.

The explanation can clarify that these are bodies appearing and then setting in this context.

Al-Anʿām 6:77

For fa-lammā afala concerning the moon, the proposed translation is:

“But when the moon disappeared from sight.”

“When the moon set” is also entirely natural.

Since bāzighan appears earlier, the translation should preserve the distinction between the moon’s rising and disappearance.

Al-Anʿām 6:78

For fa-lammā afalat concerning the sun, the proposed translation is:

“But when the sun set.”

This matches the verb’s familiar solar use and natural English expression.

The explanation can add that setting concerns the sun’s disappearance from view, rather than its destruction.

آفِل (āfil) and أُفُول (ufūl) outside the Qur’an

For heavenly bodies, āfil can mean “setting” or “disappearing from sight.”

Ufūl principally means “setting” or “disappearance from view.”

In metaphors of fame or power, decline may be explained as waning, fading, or losing a previous position. Those meanings should not directly enter the kawkab, moon, and sun ayahs.

Arabic afīl names a young camel and belongs in the separate lexical discussion, rather than the translation of Al-Anʿām’s ayahs.

10. Conclusion

In its principal Qur’anic branch, A F L concerns becoming absent and disappearing from sight.

Familiar Arabic usage applies this particularly to stars, the moon, and the sun. After setting, a body previously visible in the sky is outside the observer’s view.

Setting does not destroy the heavenly body or end its existence.

Arabic records other branches under this root, including a milk-giving animal’s milk ceasing, a fetus becoming established, and the name of a young camel. A shared historical origin should not be imposed without evidence.

The Qur’an uses the root four times in three consecutive ayahs of Al-Anʿām.

In Al-Anʿām 6:76, a kawkab sets, and Ibrāhīm says he does not love those that set.

In Al-Anʿām 6:77, the moon sets after rising, and Ibrāhīm speaks of needing his Lord’s guidance.

In Al-Anʿām 6:78, the sun also sets after rising, and Ibrāhīm declares himself free of associating partners with The God.

The verb’s meaning remains stable across the three ayahs. The observed body and Ibrāhīm’s response to its setting change.

Al-āfilīna extends his judgment from a particular kawkab to a broader description. This does not make every transient being worthless: the subject is heavenly phenomena’s fitness to occupy the position of Lord.

At the conclusion, Ibrāhīm turns his face toward the Creator of the heavens and earth, clarifying the distinction between observable creatures and their Creator.

Ufūl’s lexical meaning should remain absence and disappearance, while the argument’s conclusion about divine oneness comes from the ayahs together.

Forqan’s final definition of A F L:

“Becoming absent and passing out of sight after being visible, especially heavenly bodies’ disappearance or setting. Qur’anic afala and afalat describe the setting of the kawkab, moon, and sun; al-āfilīna is the plural description of those that set. The root itself does not express destruction, annihilation, or existential incapacity. In Ibrāhīm’s argument, however, the setting of heavenly bodies is an observation he uses in rejecting their lordship and turning toward the Creator of the heavens and earth.”
