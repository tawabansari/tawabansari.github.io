Springing forth from a source: emerging water, springs, and their origins

1. Introducing the root and its semantic core

The Arabic root N B ʿ concerns water or another liquid emerging, welling up, or springing from its source or outlet. نَبَعَ means “emerged” or “welled up”; نَبْع and نُبُوع are verbal nouns expressing this occurrence.

A clear example is water emerging from the ground and appearing as a spring. The place from which water emerges is also called يَنْبُوع.

The relationship between the liquid and its outlet matters. Water has been somewhere and then emerges or flows from there. The root alone, however, does not establish that its emergence is sudden, forceful, permanent, or under great pressure. Such features require a governing verb, description, or context.

Arabic usage extends beyond water to sweat emerging from the body. The basic meaning is therefore not simply “groundwater”; it also includes liquid welling up or seeping from its outlet.

Qur’anic forms

N B ʿ occurs only twice in the Qur’an, in two nominal forms:

  • يَنْبُوعًا: a spring; Al-Isrāʾ 17:90.
  • يَنَابِيعَ: springs; Al-Zumar 39:21.

يَنْبُوع is a singular noun with the pattern yafʿūl; يَنَابِيع is its broken plural.

No verb directly belonging to this root, such as نَبَعَ or يَنْبَعُ, occurs in the Qur’an. Both occurrences name a spring or springs rather than expressing the act of welling up.

Other Arabic forms include نَبْع, نُبُوع, نَابِع, مَنْبَع, and أَنْبَعَ, but these do not occur in the Qur’an.

The distinction matters: the root’s verbal meaning helps explain the Qur’anic nouns, but verbs absent from the text should not be presented as forms found in its verses.

2. Early Arabic usage and branches of meaning

Water and other liquids emerging

نَبَعَ الْمَاءُ is used when water emerges from somewhere such as the ground or a spring.

يَنْبُوع derives from this verb and names the place where water emerges. The event and its location should therefore be distinguished: nabʿ concerns emerging or welling up, while yanbūʿ names a spring from which water comes forth.

A yanbūʿ is nevertheless more than an empty hole or opening. Ordinary usage includes the spring itself and the water emerging from it.

Arabic attests variants of the verb with different vowels. These reflect morphological variation, but do not by themselves establish separate meanings.

A poetic witness attributed to the pre-Islamic period

The Muʿallaqah attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād contains this verse:

يَنْبَاعُ مِنْ ذِفْرَى غَضُوبٍ جَسْرَةٍ
زَيَّافَةٍ مِثْلِ الْفَنِيقِ الْمُكْدَمِ

yanbāʿu min dhifrā ghaḍūbin jasratin / zayyāfatin mithli al-fanīqi al-mukdami

Translation:

“From behind the ear of a powerful, spirited she-camel, quick in her gait and like a sturdy male camel, [sweat] seeps forth.”

The preceding verse compares the camel’s sweat to a thick, dark liquid. What emerges here is therefore the animal’s sweat, not spring water.

A familiar analysis takes يَنْبَاعُ as a lengthened form of يَنْبَعُ: the vowel on bāʾ is prolonged for the meter, producing an alif. Another analysis derives it from inbāʿa in the B W ʿ family, relating it to flowing and winding movement. Under the latter analysis, the form is not a direct derivative of N B ʿ, so the dispute should not be left unexplained.

On the familiar attribution to ʿAntarah and the first morphological analysis, the verse illustrates liquid emerging beyond the context of groundwater in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period. Attribution and morphology should remain explicit, rather than treating an unqualified conclusion about the exact date and root as certain.

The poetic verb يَنْبَاعُ should not be confused with the noun يَنْبُوع. The first is a verb in this verse; the second names a spring.

The verse belongs to the familiar transmitted text of ʿAntarah’s Muʿallaqah. Some wording varies between versions, while the liquid’s emerging or flowing is clear in the familiar reading.

نَوَابِع and the places where sweat emerges

نَوَابِع also names places on a camel’s body from which sweat emerges.

This is clearly related to emerging and seeping: just as a spring is an outlet for water, parts of the body are outlets for sweat.

The liquid should nevertheless be distinguished from its outlet. In this usage, nawābiʿ refers to the places where sweat emerges, rather than necessarily naming the sweat itself.

This usage again connects the root with observing liquids emerging from their locations, rather than solely with one kind of underground spring.

نَبْع as a tree name

نَبْع is also the name of a tree whose wood was used for bows and sometimes arrows.

This mattered in everyday Arabic life, since the quality of wood affected bows and tools for hunting or warfare.

The tree name’s appearance under the same root does not prove that it originated from water welling up.

Two distinct uses should be distinguished: liquid welling up or emerging, and the name of the nabʿ tree.

This distinction encourages caution in etymology. Shared letters alone do not justify a story that the tree was named for seeping water or sap, or for growing beside springs.

Such a historical connection requires independent evidence. Without it, the tree name is best presented as an attested Arabic branch, rather than forced into the image of water.

The Qur’anic usage belongs solely to the branch concerning water and springs.

3. Qur’anic uses of N B ʿ

A. Al-Isrāʾ 17:90: demanding a spring from the ground

وَقَالُوا لَنْ نُؤْمِنَ لَكَ حَتَّىٰ تَفْجُرَ لَنَا مِنَ الْأَرْضِ يَنْبُوعًا

wa-qālū lan nuʾmina laka ḥattā tafjura lanā mina al-arḍi yanbūʿan

Translation:

“They said: We will never believe you until you cause a spring to gush forth for us from the ground.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:90

يَنْبُوعًا is singular and indefinite here. It refers to a spring from which water emerges.

The main verb, تَفْجُرَ, however, belongs to F J R rather than N B ʿ. Their meanings should be distinguished.

يَنْبُوعًا names the spring, while تَفْجُرَ expresses opening and setting water flowing. The speakers demand that a spring be brought forth for them from the ground.

Any force suggested by “causing to gush” belongs more directly to تَفْجُرَ. يَنْبُوعًا does not necessarily mean an immense, torrential, or inexhaustible spring.

The demand in its surrounding context

The verse begins with وَقَالُوا. The Qur’an is therefore reporting the words of a group opposing the Messenger.

The following verses help explain their intention. Al-Isrāʾ 17:91, Al-Isrāʾ 17:92, and Al-Isrāʾ 17:93 mention further demands: a garden of palms and vines with rivers flowing through it, bringing down the sky, bringing The God and the angels, a house of gold, and ascending into the sky.

The Messenger’s response at the end of this sequence is:

قُلْ سُبْحَانَ رَبِّي هَلْ كُنْتُ إِلَّا بَشَرًا رَسُولًا

qul subḥāna rabbī hal kuntu illā basharan rasūlan

Translation:

“Say: Glory be to my Lord! Have I been anything but a human messenger?”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:93

This sequence shows that the demand for a spring is one of the conditions the opponents impose for believing.

Verse 90 should therefore not be read as the Qur’an making faith conditional on seeing a spring produced. The condition is quoted from the opponents, and the Messenger’s response follows.

Lexically, yanbūʿ still means a spring. Whether their condition is reasonable or acceptable does not change the word’s meaning.

Nor should the spring be forced into meaning a “spring of knowledge,” a “spiritual truth,” or something else. Their words concern a spring emerging from the ground; their other demands likewise concern tangible phenomena.

The verse establishes neither that natural springs are unimportant nor that every request for a miracle validates its speaker. The immediate issue is the opponents’ conditions imposed on the Messenger.

B. Al-Zumar 39:21: springs, rain, and plant growth

أَلَمْ تَرَ أَنَّ اللَّهَ أَنْزَلَ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً فَسَلَكَهُ يَنَابِيعَ فِي الْأَرْضِ ثُمَّ يُخْرِجُ بِهِ زَرْعًا مُخْتَلِفًا أَلْوَانُهُ ثُمَّ يَهِيجُ فَتَرَاهُ مُصْفَرًّا ثُمَّ يَجْعَلُهُ حُطَامًا ۚ إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَذِكْرَىٰ لِأُولِي الْأَلْبَابِ

a-lam tara anna allāha anzala mina al-samāʾi māʾan fa-salakahu yanābīʿa fī al-arḍi thumma yukhriju bihi zarʿan mukhtalifan alwānuhu thumma yahīju fa-tarāhu muṣfarran thumma yajʿaluhu ḥuṭāman inna fī dhālika la-dhikrā li-ulī al-albābi

Translation:

“Have you not seen that The God sends down water from the sky, then conducts it as springs in the earth? Then through it He brings forth crops of different colors; then they wither and you see them turn yellow, and then He makes them dry, broken fragments. In that there is surely a reminder for people of understanding.”

Al-Zumar 39:21

This is the root’s second and final Qur’anic occurrence.

يَنَابِيعَ is the plural of يَنْبُوع. The verse concerns springs connected with the water sent down from the sky.

First it speaks of water descending, followed by فَسَلَكَهُ, which concerns introducing or conducting it through courses. يَنَابِيعَ فِي الْأَرْضِ then places springs within this process.

The construction relates water from the sky to the earth and springs. The word’s basic equivalent is “springs” or “places where water emerges”; discussion of the verse may also address the water’s courses through the earth. Yet this does not make yanābīʿ directly equivalent to rain, every groundwater reserve, or the complete underground water system.

Distinguishing يَنَابِيعَ from فَسَلَكَهُ

سَلَكَ concerns entering, passing through, or placing something along a course.

The pronoun هُ in فَسَلَكَهُ refers to the water. The subject is therefore the same water whose descent was mentioned at the beginning.

يَنَابِيعَ, however, is a broken plural and names the springs.

Several grammatical analyses are possible. It may function as a circumstantial expression: the water is conducted as springs in the earth. Alternatively, salaka may carry the sense of placing or making, with yanābīʿ as a second object. An omitted preposition is another proposed analysis.

These grammatical differences do not fundamentally alter the image: the descending water makes its way through the earth and becomes accessible as springs.

One possibility should therefore not be made the verse’s sole certain meaning without sufficient reason.

Does the verse discuss the water cycle?

Al-Zumar 39:21 clearly connects descending water, its passage through the earth, springs, and plant growth.

This is compatible with part of the natural water cycle: precipitation can infiltrate the ground, replenish groundwater, and emerge again through springs.

The verse is nevertheless not a complete scientific account of the water cycle. It does not specify geological layers, reservoir depths, underground pressure, or travel times, and these details should not be extracted from it.

Nor should it be taken as a comprehensive scientific classification of the origins of every groundwater body and spring worldwide. It describes descending water and its movement through the earth, rather than classifying every water source.

Its clear subject is water’s relationship to the earth and plant growth, followed by those plants changing and drying.

Springs and the stages of plant life

After mentioning springs, the verse says:

ثُمَّ يُخْرِجُ بِهِ زَرْعًا مُخْتَلِفًا أَلْوَانُهُ

thumma yukhriju bihi zarʿan mukhtalifan alwānuhu

“Then through it He brings forth crops of different colors.”

بِهِ refers to water, emphasizing its role in plant growth.

After growth, the verse describes plants withering, yellowing, and becoming broken fragments.

The sequence is descending water, its being conducted as springs in the earth, plant growth, and finally drying and disintegration.

The passage ends by presenting this as a reminder for people of understanding.

It is possible to reflect on change and the transience of life through this sequence. Such a wider reflection should not replace the direct meaning of يَنَابِيعَ.

The springs are material realities whose role in plant life is described. The reminder arises from the verse as a whole, rather than from N B ʿ alone.

4. Distinguishing yanbūʿ, ʿayn, and manbaʿ

One challenge is the closeness of several words in Arabic and Persian.

يَنْبُوع refers to a spring or outlet of water. عَيْن also means a spring in one of its senses. مَنْبَع names a point of emergence or source, with broader uses in Arabic and Persian.

In many sentences, yanbūʿ and ʿayn can refer to the same thing: a water spring.

That overlap does not make them identical in every use. ʿAyn has a wider semantic family and occurs in the Qur’an for the eye, a spring, and other uses.

Manbaʿ in present-day language may mean the source of an idea, report, wealth, or knowledge. Yanbūʿ in both Qur’anic verses, however, concerns a water spring.

The customary and contextual meaning of each word should be preserved, rather than transferring every modern use of “source” into the two verses.

Is a yanbūʿ always abundant and permanent?

Some lexical and interpretive explanations describe a yanbūʿ as a plentiful or flowing spring.

The pattern yafʿūl alone, however, cannot establish size, abundant water, or a permanent flow.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:90, تَفْجُرَ can emphasize opening and setting water flowing. In Al-Zumar 39:21, سَلَكَ and its relationship to descending water clarify the context of flow.

“Spring” is therefore a suitable basic equivalent. Further features such as abundance, pressure, duration, or force require the particular verse or reliable linguistic evidence.

5. Comparison with related roots

N B ʿ (نَبَعَ) and N B Ṭ (نَبَطَ): water welling up and reaching water

These roots are semantically close and both concern water in Arabic.

N B ʿ more often denotes water emerging or welling up from its outlet.

N B Ṭ also concerns water emerging, but constructions such as اِسْتَنْبَطَ emphasize the effort of finding or extracting water from a concealed place.

For water emerging from the ground itself, nabʿ is appropriate; when someone reaches water by digging a well, istinbāṭ can be used.

This is a tendency rather than an absolute boundary. Both families have uses concerning water emerging; the precise distinction depends on the verb, its form, and the context.

Their Qur’anic fields also differ: N B ʿ concerns actual springs in two verses, while N B Ṭ appears as يَسْتَنْبِطُونَهُ in Al-Nisāʾ 4:83 for obtaining knowledge of a matter.

This does not make N B ʿ inherently material and N B Ṭ inherently mental. The distinction describes their Qur’anic occurrences, rather than Arabic as a whole.

Study of N B Ṭ

N B ʿ (نَبَعَ) and F J R (فَجَرَ): emerging and opening a watercourse

These roots are brought together in Al-Isrāʾ 17:90.

N B ʿ concerns emerging water and springs; F J R, in water-related uses, concerns opening or splitting a course for water to flow.

In تَفْجُرَ لَنَا مِنَ الْأَرْضِ يَنْبُوعًا, the verb therefore belongs to F J R and the spring’s name to N B ʿ.

The entire meaning of “causing to gush forcefully” should consequently not be attributed to N B ʿ.

Other verses, such as Al-Baqarah 2:60, describe water emerging through فَانْفَجَرَتْ without using yanbūʿ. The Qur’an can therefore discuss emerging water through F J R without naming a spring.

Study of F J R

N B ʿ (نَبَعَ) and ʿ Y N (عَيْن): a spring and an outlet of water

One Qur’anic meaning of ʿayn is a water spring.

In this use, ʿayn and yanbūʿ may refer to the same natural reality. When springs with flowing water are discussed, both lexical families can approach the same referent.

ʿAyn does not always mean a spring; it can have other meanings according to context.

Yanbūʿ, by contrast, clearly concerns springs in both Qur’anic occurrences.

This does not make one word superior in meaning to the other. Each should be examined within its own range of uses.

Study of ʿ Y N

N B ʿ (نَبَعَ) and N H R (نَهَرَ): a spring and a river’s flow

نَهْر names a river and its flowing watercourse; يَنْبُوع names a spring from which water emerges.

A spring may feed a stream or river, but this natural relationship does not make the words identical.

Not every river begins with one individual spring, and not every spring produces a large river.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:91, immediately after the demand for a spring in verse 90, mentions making rivers flow through a garden. Both demands concern water, but one concerns a spring emerging from the ground and the other rivers flowing through a garden.

Preserving the distinction between yanbūʿ and nahr therefore supports a more precise reading.

6. Translation challenges and proposals for Forqan

The main translation issue is to avoid replacing the clear, simple meaning “spring” with added explanations.

For يَنْبُوع, “spring” is natural, precise, and sufficient. If a lexical explanation is needed, one can say “a spring from which water emerges.”

The latter is an explanation, rather than an equivalent that must be repeated in every translation.

For Al-Isrāʾ 17:90, the proposed translation is:

“They said: We will never believe you until you cause a spring to gush forth for us from the ground.”

For فَسَلَكَهُ يَنَابِيعَ فِي الْأَرْضِ in Al-Zumar 39:21, the proposal is:

“Then He conducted it as springs in the earth.”

This preserves the plural yanābīʿ and relates it to the water mentioned previously.

Renderings such as “groundwater sources,” “water reservoirs,” “underground veins of water,” or “the earth’s water supply system” may appear in scientific explanations or reflections, but are not direct equivalents of yanābīʿ.

Nor should “springs of wisdom,” “springs of knowledge,” or “a source of guidance” replace the Qur’anic word without a contextual indication. Both verses concern water springs.

Short, natural equivalents also suit the non-Qur’anic derivatives: نَبَعَ means welled up or emerged; نُبُوع means welling up or emerging; مَنْبَع means a source or outlet; نَابِع means emerging or welling up.

These equivalents should follow the sentence’s subject, since nābiʿ may require different wording in Persian for water, sweat, or another subject.

7. Conclusion

N B ʿ concerns liquid emerging, welling up, or seeping from its outlet. Water springs provide its clearest use, while early usage also extends to sweat.

The verse attributed to ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād illustrates this use for an animal’s bodily fluid. Arabic also has the tree name نَبْع, but its historical connection with emerging water is unproven and should not be turned into an invented etymological story.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an, both times as a noun:

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:90, يَنْبُوعًا names a spring whose emergence the opponents impose as a condition for believing the Messenger.

In Al-Zumar 39:21, يَنَابِيعَ names springs in the sequence of descending water and its passage through the earth, followed by water’s role in plant growth and change.

Both verses clearly concern actual, material springs. The demand for a miracle in the first and the reminder and reflection in the second arise from the complete sentences and their contexts, rather than from the root independently.

Proposed final definition for Forqan:

N B ʿ denotes liquid emerging, welling up, or seeping from its outlet. In water-related usage, يَنْبُوع names a spring from which water emerges, and يَنَابِيع is its plural. The root alone does not establish a spring’s size, the force of its flow, or its permanence; these features require contextual or accompanying descriptions.

“Spring” and “springs” are therefore precise and sufficient equivalents for the root’s two Qur’anic forms.
