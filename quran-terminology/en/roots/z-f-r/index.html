Core meaning: in the sound branch, heavy, audible, groaning breath, often associated with strain; in another branch, carrying and bearing a heavy load.

1. The root in early Arabic

The Arabic root Z F R has two recognized branches: heavy breathing, sighing, and sounds of strain or suffering; and carrying loads, bearing weight, and undertaking responsibilities.

In the first branch, زَفِير denotes a sound accompanying forceful or groaning breath. It may occur in sorrow, pain, fatigue, or severe distress.

In the second branch, زِفْر denotes a load or something carried. The verb زَفَرَ also means carrying in some Arabic uses.

An early lexical analysis distinguishes two roots of meaning:

أَحَدُهُمَا يَدُلُّ عَلَى حِمْلٍ، وَالْآخَرُ عَلَى صَوْتٍ مِنَ الْأَصْوَاتِ

aḥaduhumā yadullu ʿalā ḥimlin wa-al-ākharu ʿalā ṣawtin mina al-aṣwāt

That is: “One denotes carrying, and the other a kind of sound.”

This distinction matters: without historical evidence, one cannot establish that breathing and groaning necessarily developed from carrying loads.

Heavy loads and labored breathing may be visibly associated, but that association alone does not establish the history of the two meanings.

Only the sound branch occurs in the Qur’an, through زَفِير.

The load-bearing branch before the Qur’an

One established example occurs in a lament by the pre-Islamic poet Aʿshā Bāhilah, praising a man’s generosity and ability:

أَخُو رَغَائِبَ يُعْطِيهَا وَيَسْأَلُهَا
يَأْبَى الظُّلَامَةَ مِنْهُ النَّوْفَلُ الزُّفَرُ

akhū raghāʾiba yuʿṭīhā wa-yasʾaluhā / yaʾbā al-ẓulāmata minhu al-nawfalu al-zufaru

Sense of the verse:

“A man of great gifts, which he gives and seeks; that generous bearer of burdens refuses injustice.”

الزُّفَر describes a man capable of bearing heavy responsibilities and burdens.

In early Arab social life, an influential and well-resourced person might undertake responsibilities involving settlements, financial obligations, or assistance to others.

Here زُفَر is not restricted to physical strength: it also concerns bearing financial and social burdens.

This transmitted pre-Islamic example supports the carrying-and-bearing branch.

It does not establish that زفير, meaning heavy breath or groaning, necessarily developed from this social use.

The sound branch in early Arabic

Lexical accounts use زَفَرَ يَزْفِرُ for heavy breathing and for releasing breath after drawing it out or prolonging it.

One recorded definition reads:

زَفَرَ يَزْفِرُ زَفْرًا وَزَفِيرًا: أَخْرَجَ نَفَسَهُ بَعْدَ مَدِّهِ إِيَّاهُ

zafara yazfiru zafran wa-zafīran akhraja nafasahu baʿda maddihi iyyāhu

That is: “He released his breath after prolonging it.”

In this field, زفرة denotes a heavy breath or sigh from the chest.

Arabic poetry also uses زفرات for sighs and breaths of sorrow, as in this fragment:

فَتَسْتَرِيحَ النَّفْسُ مِنْ زَفْرَاتِهَا

fa-tastarīḥa al-nafsu min zafrātihā

“So that the soul may find relief from its heavy sighs.”

The fragment is transmitted in early lexical and grammatical discussions without a consistent attribution. It should not be presented as securely dated pre-Islamic evidence.

Heavy breathing and sighing are established early Arabic meanings. These examples, however, do not establish the date of their first appearance or prove which semantic branch arose first.

A historically cautious definition

The examined evidence supports two fields in early Arabic:

First, carrying and bearing a load: lifting something heavy or accepting a difficult responsibility. Second, heavy, audible breathing: breath associated with strain, sorrow, or suffering that may become a groan or sigh.

The carrying branch has a transmitted pre-Islamic poetic witness. Breathing and sound are also attested in early lexical and literary accounts, although the precise history of all their uses remains uncertain.

This identifies the meanings before examining the Qur’anic passages, without forcing both branches into a hypothetical single origin.

2. Important forms and derivatives

زَفَرَ يَزْفِرُ

In the sound branch, this means to breathe heavily, sigh, or release breath with force and sound.

زَفَرَ الرَّجُلُ

zafara al-rajulu

That is: “The man gave a heavy breath.”

Sorrow, pain, fatigue, or difficult circumstances may give the breath audible force.

زَفْر and زَفِير

Both occur in the field of breathing and sound.

زَفِير is particularly associated with heavy breathing and groaning sound.

Some accounts also use it for the beginning of a donkey’s bray.

That comparison does not make every زفير a donkey’s sound. It describes a drawn-out, heavy sound that may arise under strain or agitation.

زَفْرَة and زُفْرَة

In the breathing branch, these denote a sigh or heavy breath.

The plural of زَفْرَة is زَفَرَات.

زفرات can describe repeated sighs and sorrowful breaths.

Another use of زُفْرَة denotes a horse’s middle, particularly its flanks and belly.

Context must distinguish these uses.

إِزْفِير

This is another recorded verbal noun or noun associated with heavy breathing.

It is recorded lexically but does not occur in the Qur’an.

زَفَرَ الْحِمْلَ

In the carrying branch, this means to lift or carry a load.

زَفَرَ الْحِمْلَ

zafara al-ḥimla

That is: “He carried the load.”

This differs from heavy breathing, although carrying a heavy load may in practice involve labored breath.

اِزْدَفَرَ

This means to lift or carry a load; the action itself is expressed, not merely the ability to perform it.

اِزْدَفَرَ الْحِمْلَ

izdafara al-ḥimla

That is: “He took up the load.”

The construction concerns actually carrying, rather than simply possessing physical strength.

زِفْر and أَزْفَار

زِفْر, with a kasrah on the first consonant, means a load; أَزْفَار is its recorded plural.

It is also used for a waterskin or leather vessel for carrying water.

This places the load and the water-carrying vessel in a related semantic field, without by itself establishing the direction of historical derivation.

زَافِر and زَافِرَة

زافر can denote someone carrying a load or helping to carry it.

Socially, زافرة denotes a person’s kin, supporters, or helpers who assist in difficulty and responsibility.

This relates to sharing burdens and responsibilities.

For bodies and equipment, زافرة is also recorded for particular parts, such as the withers and sections of an arrow or spear. The exact referent depends on the expression.

زُفَر

This describes a strong, generous, or prominent man.

Early uses associate it with bearing heavy burdens and financial or social obligations.

زفر is also a personal name, but a name alone does not assign all the root’s semantic qualities to its bearer.

زُفْرَةُ الْفَرَسِ

This denotes the middle and flank region of a horse.

A verse of al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī describes a horse whose middle is so full and broad that it seems to remain expanded as in breathing.

The image relates breathing to a broad chest and belly.

Al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. Without dating the particular verse, it cannot be classified as securely pre-Islamic.

زَفَرَتِ النَّارُ

This is also used for a fire making an audible, heavy sound as it flares up.

The use helps explain Al-Furqān 25:12, where زفير is attributed to the fire itself.

Of the derivatives discussed, only زَفِير occurs in the Qur’an.

3. Qur’anic occurrences

The root Z F R occurs three times in the Qur’an, each as زَفِير.

Two passages concern people in the fire; the third attributes زفير to the fire itself.

That distinction is important for understanding the Qur’anic usage.

Hūd 11:106: زَفِير and شَهِيق

فَأَمَّا الَّذِينَ شَقُوا فَفِي النَّارِ لَهُمْ فِيهَا زَفِيرٌ وَشَهِيقٌ

fa-ammā alladhīna shaqū fa-fī al-nāri lahum fīhā zafīrun wa-shahīq

Hūd 11:106

Translation:

“As for those who became wretched, they are in the fire; there they have heavy groaning breaths and drawn-out gasps.”

This verse places زَفِير beside شَهِيق.

Both concern breathing and sound, but belong to different roots.

زفير relates to heavy breath, sighing, and groaning.

شهيق is associated with drawing breath and with a loud, prolonged sound.

Some lexical accounts identify زفير with breathing out and شهيق with breathing in; other accounts reverse those directions.

A single lexical definition therefore cannot conclusively establish the direction of airflow intended here.

The accounts converge on intense, difficult, audible breathing.

The relationship to suffering and sorrow

In Arabic, زفير may occur under strain or in sorrow.

A person in severe distress may respond with heavy sighs, labored breath, or groans.

That relationship helps explain the verse’s imagery.

زفير itself does not mean punishment. Punishment and suffering come from the context.

فَفِي النَّارِ identifies their situation.

لَهُمْ فِيهَا زَفِيرٌ وَشَهِيقٌ describes one aspect of their condition.

Replacing زفير with “punishment” or “torment” would therefore lose the word’s direct meaning.

Are these two fixed stages of breathing?

Some explanations restrict زفير to exhalation and شهيق to inhalation.

That can illuminate part of the linguistic image, but variation among lexical accounts cautions against imposing an exact physiological division on the verse.

Arabic also associates both words with intense, groaning sounds.

The verse can thus be understood without specifying the detailed movement of air through a respiratory system.

It describes the harsh and audible condition of those in the fire.

Nor does the expression alone establish the bodily nature of life in the hereafter, the structure of lungs, or the physical mechanism of breathing there.

The verse reports زفير and شهيق without explaining their bodily mechanism.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:100: زَفِير and not hearing

لَهُمْ فِيهَا زَفِيرٌ وَهُمْ فِيهَا لَا يَسْمَعُونَ

lahum fīhā zafīrun wa-hum fīhā lā yasmaʿūn

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:100

Translation:

“There they have heavy groaning breaths, and there they do not hear.”

زفير is again attributed to those in the fire.

This time it is not paired with شهيق.

Instead, the verse includes وَهُمْ فِيهَا لَا يَسْمَعُونَ.

That clause states that they do not hear.

The verse therefore presents two separate features: having زفير and not hearing.

Is making sound incompatible with not hearing?

Someone can produce sound without being able to hear.

Someone may also be surrounded by noise and confusion without hearing a particular voice or sound.

Linguistically, زفير therefore does not necessarily contradict لا يسمعون.

The verse does not explain the cause of their not hearing.

Without decisive evidence, one cannot assert that their ears have physically ceased functioning, that زفير masks every other sound, or that a particular additional punishment prevents hearing.

These are possible explanations of the condition, not the direct meaning of the verse.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:101 and Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:102 contrast this with those for whom good has already been decreed: they are kept far from the fire and do not even hear its sound.

Hearing and not hearing thus occur in the descriptions of the two groups’ different situations.

زفير itself, however, occurs in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:100. The later verses describe the other group’s not hearing the fire; they do not contain a second use of زفير.

Al-Furqān 25:12: the fire’s own زَفِير

إِذَا رَأَتْهُمْ مِنْ مَكَانٍ بَعِيدٍ سَمِعُوا لَهَا تَغَيُّظًا وَزَفِيرًا

idhā raʾathum min makānin baʿīdin samiʿū lahā taghayyuẓan wa-zafīran

Al-Furqān 25:12

Translation:

“When it sees them from a distant place, they hear its fury and heavy roaring.”

This passage presents a different use of the root.

In Hūd 11:106 and Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:100, زفير belongs to those in the fire.

In Al-Furqān 25:12, لَهَا refers to the fire.

The fire itself is thus described through تَغَيُّظ and زَفِير.

تغيظ expresses anger and intense fury.

زفير expresses a heavy, formidable sound.

This relates to the lexical expression زَفَرَتِ النَّارُ, a fire making an audible sound as it blazes.

Does fire breathe like a human?

Arabic sometimes applies verbs and descriptions of living beings to natural phenomena.

Wind, sea, or fire may be described as roaring, crying, or groaning.

Al-Furqān 25:12 attributes زفير to fire, so the word cannot be limited to human breathing.

The attribution does not establish that the fire of the hereafter has human lungs, a respiratory system, or human anatomy.

The verse speaks of the fire seeing, raging, and producing زفير.

Its description should be preserved without equating its ultimate reality with familiar biological mechanisms.

Nor does lexical analysis alone settle whether every aspect of the fire’s seeing and anger must be read as purely figurative. The verse does not fully explain how these realities belong to the hereafter.

For the root study, the point is that زفير denotes a formidable, audible sound attributed to the fire itself.

4. The relationship between the three occurrences

All three Qur’anic uses of زفير concern the fire and punishment of the hereafter.

The source of the sound is not the same in each.

Hūd 11:106 attributes زفير and شهيق to those in the fire.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:100 also attributes زفير to them, alongside their not hearing.

Al-Furqān 25:12 presents زفير heard from the fire itself.

Qur’anic زفير is therefore not confined to human breathing.

The shared feature is an intense, heavy sound associated with a dreadful condition.

The dread in these passages does not mean that Z F R intrinsically denotes divine punishment.

Arabic can describe someone’s sighing in sorrow, pain, or fatigue, and can use the word for the sound of a blazing fire.

In the Qur’an, the context of punishment gives the word its particular terrifying force.

The lexical meaning of the sound must therefore be distinguished from the punitive situation in which it occurs.

5. Differences from related roots

Z F R (زَفِير) and Sh H Q (شَهِيق)

The words appear together in Hūd 11:106 and both concern breathing and sound.

Lexical descriptions often associate زفير with heavy, groaning breath and شهيق with drawing breath or a high, prolonged sound.

Their exact assignment to inhalation and exhalation varies in the accounts.

Translation should therefore preserve their breathing and sound qualities without asserting a fixed direction of airflow in every use.

Their pairing alone does not establish two entirely separate, nonoverlapping physiological stages.

Z F R (زَفِير) and ʾ N N (أَنِين)

أنين denotes groaning or a sound arising from pain, suffering, or discomfort.

زفير can also involve groaning, but its association with heavy breath is more prominent.

Human زفير may have a groaning quality, but not every groan is necessarily زفير.

This matters in translation: “groaning” alone may understate the breathing element.

Z F R (زَفِير) and Ṣ Y Ḥ (صَيْحَة)

صيحة denotes a cry or shout.

The sound branch of زفير concerns heavy breath and the sound arising with it.

Both can be loud or terrifying, but their linguistic images differ.

صيحة emphasizes the cry itself, whereas زفير often describes sound associated with breathing or internal strain.

When Al-Furqān 25:12 attributes زفير to fire, however, a human respiratory mechanism cannot simply be assumed.

Z F R (زَفْر) and Ḥ M L (حَمْل)

حمل denotes lifting, bearing, or carrying something.

One branch of زفر has that meaning, particularly with heavy loads and bearing them.

حمل has a broader range, including carrying loads, pregnancy, and undertaking responsibility.

زفر also has its distinct breathing-and-sound branch.

The roots overlap in some uses but are not interchangeable throughout.

Z F R (زَفِير) and Z F F (زَفّ)

Despite similar letters, these are different roots.

Z F F is a doubled root associated with movement, haste, and forms of advancing.

The Qur’anic branch of Z F R concerns heavy, groaning sound.

Similar spelling and sound do not establish a shared history or meaning.

6. Translation suggestions for Forqan

The following equivalents suit the main forms. These study suggestions do not replace installed verse translations or existing authored word-box explanations.

زَفَرَ يَزْفِرُ: breathed heavily, sighed, or forcefully released breath.

زَفْر and زَفِير: heavy, audible breath; a sigh or groan associated with strain or suffering.

زَفْرَة: a sigh or heavy breath.

زَفَرَات: repeated heavy sighs.

إِزْفِير: heavy or groaning breath.

زِفْر: a load or something carried.

أَزْفَار: loads; in the relevant use, waterskins.

زَفَرَ الْحِمْلَ: carried the load.

اِزْدَفَرَ الْحِمْلَ: took up and carried the load.

زَافِر: a carrier or helper in bearing a load.

زَافِرَة: socially, kin and supporters.

زُفَر: someone strong and capable of bearing burdens and responsibilities.

Suggested verse renderings

In Hūd 11:106:

لَهُمْ فِيهَا زَفِيرٌ وَشَهِيقٌ

lahum fīhā zafīrun wa-shahīq

Hūd 11:106

“There they have heavy groaning breaths and drawn-out gasps” preserves both the sound and breathing elements. The distinction between زفير and شهيق can be explained without reducing both to a single “groan” or asserting an exact physiological division.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:100:

لَهُمْ فِيهَا زَفِيرٌ

lahum fīhā zafīr

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:100

“There they have heavy groaning breaths” is suitable.

In Al-Furqān 25:12:

سَمِعُوا لَهَا تَغَيُّظًا وَزَفِيرًا

samiʿū lahā taghayyuẓan wa-zafīran

Al-Furqān 25:12

“They hear its fury and heavy roaring” keeps the two words distinct. Commentary can clarify that زفير here is the formidable sound of the fire itself.

For the brief root-meaning box:

“Heavy, audible breathing; in another branch, carrying loads.”

For verse-specific meanings:

In Hūd 11:106 and Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:100: “The heavy sighs and groaning breaths of those in the fire.”

In Al-Furqān 25:12: “The heavy, formidable sound of the fire.”

This preserves the different sources of the sound without restricting every use to human breathing.

7. Conclusion

Z F R has two important Arabic branches: carrying and bearing loads, and heavy, audible breathing.

The carrying branch has a transmitted pre-Islamic poetic witness. زُفَر there describes a capable person bearing difficult burdens and responsibilities.

The sound branch concerns sighs, heavy breaths, and groans. Sound under strain is prominent, though the precise date of its earliest uses is not fully established.

Only زفير occurs in the Qur’an, and all three occurrences concern the fire of the hereafter.

Hūd 11:106 and Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:100 attribute it to those in the fire.

Al-Furqān 25:12 attributes it to the fire itself.

The Qur’anic meaning should therefore not be limited to a particular bodily process such as releasing air from human lungs.

In Qur’anic usage, زفير denotes a heavy, intense, groaning sound: attributed twice to those in the fire and once to the fire itself.

The suffering and dread associated with it come from the contexts; punishment is not itself the root’s lexical definition.

Attributing زفير to fire also does not authorize a definitive account of the physical nature of the fire of the hereafter.

A precise account preserves both historically attested branches while focusing on sound in the Qur’anic passages. Neither load-bearing nor interpretive accounts of punishment should replace the meaning of زفير itself.
