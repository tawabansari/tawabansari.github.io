Core meaning: dragging, driving, or moving someone with force and violence; descriptively, coarseness, hardness, and harshness in conduct.

1. The root in early Arabic

The three-letter root ع ت ل concerns severity, roughness, and forceful action. Two prominent uses are «عَتَلَ», dragging or driving someone forcibly, and «عُتُلّ», a coarse, harsh, hard-handed person.

In the first sense, movement involves dragging, a firm grip, or forcible driving. Seizing someone's clothing or body and dragging that person somewhere against their will illustrates «عَتْل».

The verb was also used for pulling a camel's rein and leading it roughly. Instead of guiding the animal gently, someone pulls hard and forces it to move.

Thus عتل is more than moving or accompanying: it involves force, roughness, and pressure.

Descriptively, «عُتُلّ» denotes someone hard, coarse, and harsh in dealings: someone who may use force, lack gentleness or flexibility, or pressure others through severity.

Dictionaries also record the description for someone strong and stout, and for hard or thick objects such as a solid mountain or thick spear.

Physical strength and hardness should therefore be distinguished from violent conduct. A powerful, large person is not necessarily harsh or unjust; a small person can behave violently.

These fields overlap linguistically, but their moral implications differ.

An early poetic witness: powerful bows

A poem attributed in earlier sources to Abū al-Ṣalt al-Thaqafī, and in some transmissions to his son Umayya ibn Abī al-Ṣalt, says:

يَرْمُونَ عَنْ عَتَلٍ كَأَنَّهَا غُبُطٌ

yarmūna ʿan ʿatalin ka-annahā ghubuṭun

بِزَمْخَرٍ يُعْجِلُ الْمَرْمِيَّ إِعْجَالَا

bi-zamkharin yuʿjilu al-marmiyya iʿjālā

Translation: They shoot from powerful bows resembling wooden camel-saddle frames, with arrows that give the person under fire no respite.

«عَتَل» here is a plural of «عَتَلَة», naming Persian bows.

«غُبُط» is a plural of «غَبِيط» and denotes wooden camel-saddle frames or a kind of riding apparatus.

The comparison concerns the bows' size and curvature. «زَمْخَر» refers here to arrows and their long, slender shafts; «يُعْجِلُ الْمَرْمِيَّ» describes their effect on the person being shot at, not merely their speed toward a target.

The witness matters because عتل names a weapon associated with hardness, strength, and resilience.

The poem is transmitted in connection with Sayf ibn Dhī Yazan. Early sources differ over attribution to the father or son, and its composition date is not independently certain. It is a witness from a tradition associated with pre-Islamic times, rather than indisputable evidence predating the Quran.

The bow sense is nevertheless clear, showing that this family was not restricted to harsh human conduct.

Another material use: a heavy, hard implement

«عَتَلَة» also named a strong, hard implement.

Dictionaries record an iron bar or tool for digging, breaking walls, uprooting trees, or removing stone, as well as a thick club.

Such implements work through considerable pressure and force, moving, dislodging, or breaking things.

Their association with the verb is intelligible: both involve force and pressure.

The available evidence does not conclusively establish whether the implement's name or the verbal sense came first.

Nor does every forcible movement involve this implement. It is one lexical use, not a definition of every derivative.

A defensible historical account

Classical Arabic displays three broad fields: strong, hard implements or objects; forcible dragging or driving; and harsh conduct.

The attributed poem illustrates the implement branch. Early dictionaries also record the verb for dragging people and roughly leading animals.

The Quran directly witnesses the branches of violent dragging and harsh character in the language of revelation.

Accordingly, severity and roughness connect these uses, while the verb highlights forced movement and the adjective highlights harsh conduct or physical hardness.

2. Word family and semantic extensions

Recorded forms include «عَتَلَ», «يَعْتِلُ», «يَعْتُلُ», «عَتْل», «عَاتِل», «مِعْتَل», «عُتُلّ», «عَتِل», «عَتِيل», «عَتَلَة», «عَتَّال», and «انْعَتَلَ».

عَتَلَ: dragging or driving violently

This basic triliteral verb, in its movement sense, means dragging or driving someone forcibly.

Both imperfect forms «يَعْتِلُ» and «يَعْتُلُ» are recorded.

«عَتَلَهُ إِلَى السِّجْنِ» describes taking someone violently toward prison.

«عَتَلَ النَّاقَةَ» describes roughly pulling a camel's rein and making it move.

The examples clarify the verb's relation to the person or animal being moved.

Both involve externally applied force and movement under pressure.

It does not necessarily mean striking, wounding, or killing: someone can be dragged violently without receiving a separate blow.

Nor is it restricted to pulling backward. Movement may be forward, sideways, or toward another destination. Its distinguishing feature is a rough, coercive manner of movement.

عَتْل: forcible dragging

«عَتْل» is the verbal noun for dragging or driving violently.

It can concern moving a person, animal, or heavy object.

In dragging someone to prison, it conveys the harsh method of transfer, not merely arrival.

Translation should preserve that feature.

“Take” or “convey” may express the result without conveying the full sense.

عَاتِل and مِعْتَل: one who drags forcibly

«عَاتِل» is the active participle and can denote someone who forcibly drags or drives another.

It is also recorded for a harsh officer or enforcer.

«مِعْتَل» is recorded for a person capable of forcible dragging or movement.

The horse verse quoted nearby in some dictionaries illustrates the verb «نَعْتِلُهُ», not an application of the adjective «مِعْتَل» to a horse. These witnesses should not be conflated.

The forms concern action or capacity, without necessarily making identical moral judgments about the agent.

A person's considerable strength and the way that person uses it are separate matters.

عُتُلّ: a harsh, coarse person

This is one of the family's best-known descriptions.

Applied to people, it can mean hard, harsh, coarse, and ungentle in dealings.

Some dictionaries also record severity in antagonism, bad conduct, bodily coarseness, and strength.

Those features should not be merged indiscriminately.

One use may describe a strong, hard-bodied person; another emphasizes harsh conduct.

In the Quran it stands among condemned traits, making harsh character particularly relevant to the context.

Some explanations also associate it with voracious eating and withholding generosity. Those associations are not essential meanings of every derivative.

عَتِل: hastening toward wrongdoing

Arabic also records «عَتِلَ إِلَى الشَّرِّ».

It describes someone hastening eagerly toward wrongdoing.

Though related to behavioral severity, this must be distinguished from transitive «عَتَلَهُ».

In one, the person moves eagerly toward an action; in the other, someone moves a person or thing violently.

Prepositions and sentence structure therefore matter.

عَتَلَة: a strong implement

This word names an iron bar or tool for digging, breaking, and dislodging hard objects.

It also denotes a thick club and, in another use, a Persian bow.

These implements share hardness, resilience, or the capacity to exert force.

Some dictionaries also record the word for a she-camel that does not conceive, with emphasis on retained physical strength.

This sense should not be imported into Quranic عُتُلّ, which describes a person in a moral context.

عَتِيل and عَتَّال: worker and porter

Some Arabic dialects use «عَتِيل» for a servant or hired worker.

«عَتَّال» is also used for a porter who carries or moves heavy loads; this occupational usage should be distinguished from the Quranic forms and their period of attestation.

It relates to physical force and moving loads.

Neither carrying loads as an occupation nor the strength to do so is morally negative.

The harsh character conveyed by some uses of عُتُلّ should not be transferred to terms for work and carrying.

3. Quranic uses

The root occurs twice, in two verses and two morphological forms.

«فَاعْتِلُوهُ» in Al-Dukhān 44:47 is an imperative; «عُتُلٍّ» in Al-Qalam 68:13 describes a person.

Other lexical forms such as عَتَلَة, عَتِيل, and عَتَّال do not occur in the Quran.

The two occurrences illustrate violent movement of someone and harshness of character and conduct.

A. فَاعْتِلُوهُ: dragging violently

خُذُوهُ فَاعْتِلُوهُ إِلَىٰ سَوَاءِ الْجَحِيمِ

khudhūhu fa-ʿtilūhu ilā sawāʾi al-jaḥīm

Al-Dukhān 44:47

Seize him, then drag him violently into the midst of the blazing fire.

Al-Dukhān 44:47

«فَاعْتِلُوهُ» is a plural imperative of the basic triliteral verb عَتَلَ.

The sentence commands seizing someone and dragging him toward a specified place; seizing is expressed by the separate verb خُذُوهُ.

«خُذُوهُ» belongs to أ خ ذ and means “seize him.”

«فَاعْتِلُوهُ» belongs to ع ت ل and means “drag or drive him violently.”

«إِلَىٰ سَوَاءِ الْجَحِيمِ» specifies the destination: the midst of the blazing fire.

The sentence thus contains seizing, violent movement, and a destination.

خُذُوهُ and فَاعْتِلُوهُ

The verbs are not identical in meaning.

أَخْذ can concern taking, acquiring, or bringing something into one's possession.

Here عَتْل expresses forcible dragging or driving.

Someone may take another person's hand and lead them gently: taking occurs without necessarily involving عتل.

If someone grips another firmly and drags them violently against their will, that second act goes beyond taking and illustrates عتل.

In the verse, خُذُوهُ expresses seizing control of the person, while فَاعْتِلُوهُ specifies how he is moved.

“Seize him and take him away” therefore loses part of the second verb's meaning.

إِلَىٰ سَوَاءِ الْجَحِيمِ

إِلَىٰ expresses direction or destination.

سَوَاء here means the midst or central part.

الْجَحِيم concerns blazing fire.

The phrase therefore means “into the midst of the blazing fire.”

سواء here concerns location, not social equality or justice.

The verb itself does not identify the destination; the preposition and following words do so.

Who is dragged?

The preceding verses identify the pronoun هُ in the two commands.

Al-Dukhān 44:43 mentions the tree of Zaqqūm.

Al-Dukhān 44:44 calls it the food of «الأَثِيم», the sinful person.

Al-Dukhān 44:45 and Al-Dukhān 44:46 describe the food boiling in the belly and its intense heat.

Al-Dukhān 44:47 then commands seizing the person and dragging him into the midst of Hell's fire.

The pronoun refers to that sinful person.

Al-Dukhān 44:48 commands pouring the punishment of scalding water over his head.

Al-Dukhān 44:49 addresses him in words that, within his punishment, convey rebuke and irony.

The context therefore places فاعتلوه in a scene of punishment in the afterlife.

Nevertheless, the verb itself does not mean to punish: it describes the manner of movement. Punishment comes from the context and destination.

Does عتل mean torture or killing?

The movement is violent and situated in a punishment context.

Lexically, however, عَتَلَ alone does not mean killing, burning, or torturing with a particular instrument.

In «عَتَلَهُ إِلَى السِّجْنِ», someone is forcibly taken to prison. The verb describes the transfer without specifying what happens after arrival.

Likewise, the verse's violent dragging should be distinguished from other descriptions of punishment.

The afterlife passage should not be turned into a general command for people to treat opponents violently. Its addressees and situation belong to a particular scene of judgment and punishment.

The readings فَاعْتِلُوهُ and فَاعْتُلُوهُ

Established Quranic readings pronounce the verb in two ways:

«فَاعْتِلُوهُ», with kasra on ت.

«فَاعْتُلُوهُ», with ḍamma on ت.

Both derive from the basic triliteral عَتَلَ and its two established conjugations.

Both retain forcible dragging or driving as their principal meaning.

The initial hamza in «اِعْتِلُوا» or «اُعْتُلُوا» is the imperative's connecting hamza.

It does not make the verb Form VIII: this is a basic triliteral imperative, and ت is a root consonant.

B. عُتُلٍّ: a harsh person

عُتُلٍّ بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ زَنِيمٍ

ʿutullin baʿda dhālika zanīm

Al-Qalam 68:13

Coarse and harsh—and, beyond that, zanīm.

Al-Qalam 68:13

«عُتُلٍّ» is singular masculine and genitive, continuing the descriptions governed by «كُلَّ حَلَّافٍ» in Al-Qalam 68:10. Its grammatical connection does not stop at the verse boundary.

It stands within a series of descriptions of conduct.

To understand it, begin with Al-Qalam 68:10.

That verse commands not obeying an excessive swearer who is contemptible.

Al-Qalam 68:11 concerns fault-finding and going about spreading malicious gossip.

Al-Qalam 68:12 describes withholding good, transgression, and sinfulness.

Al-Qalam 68:13 then adds عُتُلٍّ.

The setting among condemned traits highlights moral harshness.

What kind of person is عُتُلّ?

Arabic can apply it to someone hard-bodied, powerful, or coarse in character.

Here the preceding descriptions concern gossip, withholding good, and transgression.

It is therefore appropriate to understand it as harsh and ungentle in dealings. Bullying and unfair conduct may illustrate that trait in context; they are not separate, fixed equivalents in every use.

Such a person may use force, pressure, and threats to get what they want, or lack flexibility and kindness.

This connects with the family: the verb describes roughly dragging another person, while the adjective emphasizes rough conduct.

The adjective need not mean literally grabbing others by their clothes; it can describe a broader disposition.

Does a large physique establish this trait?

Some dictionaries describe عتل as a stout, strong, physically coarse man.

Al-Qalam, however, condemns conduct and objectionable traits.

Body size or strength alone should not establish that description in its moral sense.

A very strong, large person may treat others kindly and fairly.

A physically weak person may use office, wealth, or social power harshly.

Thus the moral use concerns conduct and the use of power, not physical size alone.

This fits the context of Al-Qalam 68:13 among descriptions of behavior.

بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ: beyond the preceding traits

The phrase follows عُتُلٍّ.

Here it can mean “beyond that” or “in addition to those qualities.”

Another feature is added to the preceding descriptions.

The verse therefore adds a quality rather than simply repeating one.

The following word is زَنِيمٍ.

It belongs to ز ن م, which dictionaries connect with something hanging from or attached to something else; personal uses concern an attributed affiliation or identifying characteristic.

Explanations of its precise referent in the verse differ.

It is an independent word, whose meaning should not be transferred into عتل.

Illegitimate descent, tribal affiliation, or family attribution should therefore not enter the definition of ع ت ل.

عُتُلّ concerns harsh conduct here; زنيم is another description requiring its own root study.

Wealth and social standing

Al-Qalam 68:14 mentions wealth and children.

In Al-Qalam 68:15, the person calls divine verses tales of the ancients when they are recited to him.

The sequence places عتل among descriptions of someone enjoying resources and standing while responding wrongly to divine verses.

Possessing wealth or children is not the meaning of عتل, nor evidence of moral harshness by itself.

A wealthy person may help others, just as someone with little wealth may behave harshly or coercively.

The condemnation concerns conduct; it does not make every possession of wealth or family inherently wrong.

عُتُلّ and مَنَّاعٍ لِلْخَيْرِ

Al-Qalam 68:12 calls the person مَنَّاعٍ لِلْخَيْرِ, one who persistently withholds or obstructs good.

Al-Qalam 68:13 adds عُتُلّ.

These descriptions are related but distinct.

The first emphasizes withholding or preventing good.

The second emphasizes harsh dealings and conduct.

Someone may violently obstruct good reaching others, combining both traits.

But عُتُلّ does not always mean withholding good, and مناع does not necessarily mean dragging others forcibly.

The different roots describe different aspects of one person's conduct.

4. Related roots and distinctions

عَتَلَ and دَعَّ: dragging and pushing forcibly

د ع ع concerns forceful pushing or driving.

Al-Ṭūr 52:13 describes people driven forcibly toward Hell's fire.

دَعّ can emphasize a hard push or forceful driving.

عَتْل likewise concerns violent movement, with firm dragging and taking someone against their will prominent in established uses.

Pushing someone hard from behind illustrates دع.

Gripping someone's collar or arm and dragging them forcibly illustrates عتل.

The verbs overlap in force and coerced movement; the distinction should not be made absolute.

عَتَلَ and سَحَبَ: pulling and violent dragging

س ح ب concerns pulling something across a surface or along a course.

Ghāfir 40:71 mentions people being dragged with shackles and chains.

Al-Qamar 54:48 mentions people dragged on their faces in the fire.

سحب expresses pulling; its degree of force may come from circumstances and context.

In its human movement sense, عتل itself includes force and roughness.

عتل may involve dragging, but not every act of pulling is عتل.

Gently pulling an implement across the ground may be سحب without the force and roughness characteristic of عتل.

عُتُلّ and غَلِيظ: coarseness and hardness

غ ل ظ concerns thickness, coarseness, and severity.

Āl ʿImrān 3:159 places غَلِيظَ الْقَلْبِ beside فَظًّا.

There, harsh dealings can cause people to disperse from someone's company.

The moral use of عُتُلّ likewise concerns rough conduct.

غلظ can also describe material thickness or density.

In ع ت ل, the verbal branch concerns violent movement and the descriptive branch coarse character, alongside the physical uses discussed earlier.

They overlap in behavioral harshness without sharing every use.

عُتُلّ and فَظّ: harsh dealings

فَظّ describes a coarse, harsh person.

It closely overlaps with عُتُلّ in moral description.

ع ت ل also has a clear connection with pressure and forcible dragging, making practical violence and coercion prominent in some human uses.

فظاظة can appear in harsh speech or unpleasant dealings without bodily movement or dragging.

This is a tendency, not an absolute boundary; both descriptions can apply to the same person.

عُتُلّ and عُتُوّ: harshness and defiance

ع ت و concerns defiance, arrogance, and exceeding limits.

Al-Furqān 25:21 describes great defiance.

Al-Mulk 67:21 places عتو beside aversion.

عُتُلّ can describe a harsh or bullying person, whereas عُتُوّ more directly emphasizes defiance of a limit or command.

Someone may deliberately defy a command in a calm, calculated manner, without displaying outward violence.

Conversely, someone may be harsh in dealings without being defiant about a particular matter.

The roots differ despite their similar sound and possible overlap in conduct.

عَتَلَ and بَطَشَ: dragging and forceful seizure

ب ط ش concerns forceful seizure and intense exertion of power.

Al-Dukhān 44:16 contains الْبَطْشَةَ الْكُبْرَىٰ.

بطش can denote forceful seizure or power, while the movement sense of عتل specifies forcible dragging or driving.

بطش may occur without moving a person; in فَاعْتِلُوهُ, movement toward a destination belongs to the construction.

“Drag him forcibly” therefore fits better than merely “seize him hard.”

عَتَلَ and عَطَّلَ: violence and leaving unused

ع ط ل concerns emptiness, abandonment, or cessation of use.

Al-Takwīr 81:4 mentions valuable camels being left unattended.

Al-Ḥajj 22:45 describes a well as مُعَطَّلَة.

This family resembles ع ت ل in spelling but is independent in root and meaning.

عَتَلَ concerns violent dragging or driving; عَطَّلَ concerns abandoning or leaving something unused.

Similar sound should not be treated as proof of shared meaning.

5. Translation recommendations for Forqan

Translation should distinguish the movement verb from the character description.

عَتَلَ: dragged forcibly, hauled violently, drove roughly.

عَتْل: forcible dragging or driving.

عَاتِل: someone who drags or drives another forcibly.

مِعْتَل: a person capable of dragging or moving something forcefully.

عُتُلّ: coarse, harsh, hard-handed; in some lexical uses, hard-bodied or powerful.

عَتِل: in the wrongdoing construction, hastening eagerly toward evil.

عَتَلَة: a strong implement for digging, breaking, or dislodging; also a kind of bow.

عَتَّال: a porter carrying or moving heavy loads.

Al-Dukhān 44:47

“Seize him, then drag him violently into the midst of the blazing fire.”

This separates seizure from violent dragging and retains the direction of movement.

“Drag him into the midst of the blazing fire” is also natural, but adding “violently” makes the verb's manner more explicit.

Al-Qalam 68:13

“Coarse and harsh—and, beyond that, zanīm.”

The adjective عُتُلّ receives a behavioral equivalent.

Zanīm is retained provisionally as an independent word to avoid merging it with عتل. This is a translation proposal focused on the present root, not a complete translation of every word in the verse.

The explanation can clarify that عتل concerns harsh conduct here rather than strength or bodily size alone.

Brief meanings for word boxes

Brief root meaning: dragging or driving forcibly; descriptively, coarseness, hardness, and harsh conduct.

فَاعْتِلُوهُ — Al-Dukhān 44:47

Contextual meaning: drag him violently; a command to move the sinful person into the midst of the blazing fire.

عُتُلٍّ — Al-Qalam 68:13

Contextual meaning: coarse and harsh; describing someone whose severe dealings accompany other condemned traits.

6. Conclusion

The root concerns severity, coarseness, and forceful action.

Its verbal branch expresses forcibly dragging or driving someone. Movement alone is insufficient: pressure and roughness are integral to its manner.

The adjective عُتُلّ can describe a coarse, harsh person; Arabic also applies it to physically strong, hard-bodied people or things.

Materially, عَتَلَة names a strong implement for digging, breaking, or dislodging heavy objects, and also a kind of bow.

The verse attributed to Abū al-Ṣalt or Umayya illustrates the bow sense. Disputed attribution and uncertain dating prevent treating it as conclusive evidence for every pre-Islamic sense of the root.

The Quran uses the root twice.

In Al-Dukhān 44:47, فَاعْتِلُوهُ commands dragging someone violently into the midst of the fire. The verb conveys the violent manner; إِلَىٰ سَوَاءِ الْجَحِيمِ identifies the destination.

In Al-Qalam 68:13, عُتُلٍّ describes harsh character alongside fault-finding, malicious gossip, withholding good, transgression, and sinfulness.

The relationship is clear: one use expresses violence in dragging, the other harshness as a trait of conduct.

This does not make every strong, stout person or owner of a hard implement morally harsh. Physical strength, capacity to exert pressure, and choosing harsh conduct are different matters.

The family overlaps with دع, سحب, غلظ, فظاظة, عتو, and بطش without being fully interchangeable with them. Its verbal branch emphasizes forced movement and its descriptive branch harsh dealings.

Final definition: ع ت ل chiefly denotes forcibly dragging or driving someone. Descriptively, عُتُلّ denotes a hard, coarse, harsh person; some lexical uses emphasize physical hardness or strength. In the Quran, فَاعْتِلُوهُ expresses violent dragging, while عُتُلٍّ describes harsh human conduct in a condemnatory context.
