From the khamṭ plant and its fruit to changes in food flavor, skinning, the roar of the sea, and unruliness: examining ukulin khamṭin in the story of Sabaʾ

The Arabic root KH M Ṭ has several semantic fields that cannot all be reduced to a simple meaning such as bitterness or roughness. In botanical usage, khamṭ names a kind of tree or fruit; in a broader usage, it denotes vegetation whose taste has become so bitter that it is difficult to use as food. In food vocabulary, this family also concerns changes in the flavor and smell of milk and other drinks. In other branches of Arabic, forms from the same root express skinning and roasting, turbulent waves, an animal’s roaring, and harsh human behavior.

An important feature of this family is the diversity of its semantic fields and the different connections proposed by lexicographers. Some uses concern the quality of fruit and food, others the removal of a covering, and others surging, violent movement, and dominance. Their presence within one lexical family does not itself prove that they all developed from a single material image.

The Qur’an contains only the form khamṭin, in Sabaʾ 34:16. It describes the two gardens that replaced the people’s former gardens after they turned away. Understanding it requires distinguishing the name of a tree, the quality of its fruit, and the larger picture of the gardens’ transformation.

1. Forms and derivatives of the root

KH M Ṭ is a sound triliteral root. The following forms are recorded in the lexicons:

  • Khamṭ: a kind of tree or fruit; elsewhere, food or vegetation that is bitter, sour, or unpleasant in flavor.
  • Khamaṭa–yakhmiṭu: to skin an animal and roast it; in some uses, to roast meat.
  • Khamīṭ: roasted meat, particularly meat whose skin has been removed before roasting.
  • Khammāṭ: a person who roasts meat; in another usage, someone harsh or boisterous.
  • Khāmiṭ: something whose smell or taste has changed, especially milk, drink, or a container.
  • Khamṭah: a particular smell of a plant or drink; also a drink whose flavor and smell have begun to change.
  • Khamiṭat al-khamru: the drink underwent a change in smell or taste.
  • Takhammaṭa: to roar, become agitated, behave harshly, or show unruliness.
  • Mutakhammiṭ: a harsh, angry, boisterous, or arrogant person.
  • Khimāṭ: in some poetry, a description of drink whose flavor or quality is at a stage of maturation.

Early sources record some of these forms with different vocalizations and related meanings. For example, in a botanical setting khamṭ can refer to the tree or its fruit, whereas in food vocabulary bitterness or a change in flavor may be at issue.

Likewise, khammāṭ as a roaster of meat belongs to a different setting of usage from khammāṭ as a boisterous or harsh person.

Only khamṭin occurs in the Qur’an. In the common reading, it follows ukulin and helps describe the produce of the two gardens.

2. Semantic fields in early Arabic

Ibn Fāris: two different principles

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies two principles for this root:

أَحَدُهُمَا الِانْجِرَادُ وَالْمَلَاسَةُ، وَالْآخَرُ التَّسَلُّطُ وَالصِّيَالُ

aḥaduhumā al-injirādu wa-al-malāsatu wa-al-ākharu al-tasalluṭu wa-al-ṣiyālu

That is: “One is becoming bare and smooth; the other is domination and assault.”

This division matters because Ibn Fāris does not begin by limiting the root to the bitterness of fruit.

In the first branch, he discusses removing an animal’s skin and the smoothness of a surface. In the second, he mentions an animal’s roaring and the turbulence of the sea.

However, his account of the first branch also associates khamṭ with something thornless, whereas other lexicographers describe khamṭ as a thorny tree.

This disagreement shows that even the physical characteristics of the plant called khamṭ were not defined in one universally accepted way.

Although Ibn Fāris’s division helps identify usages, it cannot establish that all the meanings descend from two historically proven origins.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: the two principles of becoming bare and assault

Khamṭ: a tree or its fruit?

One of the best known meanings of khamṭ in Arabic sources concerns plants.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn describes khamṭ as an edible kind of arāk. Lisān al-ʿArab also quotes Al-Layth as describing a tree with edible fruit. Attribution of this particular report should be distinguished from the general attribution of Kitāb al-ʿAyn to Al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad.

In explaining the verse in Sabaʾ, Al-Farrāʾ likewise identifies khamṭ as the fruit of the arāk tree, called barīr.

Arāk is a familiar tree of hot, dry regions. Its twigs have also been used as tooth cleaning sticks, and it produces small fruits.

In this interpretation, khamṭ names a particular botanical referent rather than simply describing any bitter fruit.

Al-Zajjāj offers a broader explanation: khamṭ is used of vegetation whose taste has become so bitter that eating it is difficult or impossible.

Abū ʿUbaydah also includes bitter, thorny trees among the referents of khamṭ.

Lisān al-ʿArab transmits other identifications: a tree resembling sidr, a thorny tree, the scant fruit of certain trees, and even vegetation or fruit offering little appreciable food value.

These reports yield three main possibilities.

First, khamṭ can name a tree or a group of trees.

Second, it can refer to the tree’s fruit.

Third, in broader usage, it can describe the bitter or undesirable quality of a plant and its produce.

These possibilities overlap, but they are not identical.

If khamṭ names a tree, “khamṭ fruit” is that tree’s produce. If it names the fruit itself, the tree need not be included in the word’s definition. If it describes produce quality, its scope may extend beyond a single plant species.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn: khamṭ and arāk

Must khamṭ be poisonous or inedible?

Some early explanations associate khamṭ with very bitter or even harmful vegetation. These reports are not uniform.

The same sources also mention the arāk tree, whose fruit is edible.

One report describing an inedible plant therefore cannot establish that everything called khamṭ was poisonous or deadly.

Bitterness, sourness, and limited food value are also three different qualities.

A fruit may be bitter yet eaten; another may be sour and unpleasant. A plant may bear little fruit without that fruit being inherently poisonous.

In the verse in Sabaʾ, the central issue is the replacement of productive gardens with gardens yielding less desirable produce. Understanding the verse does not require making khamṭ necessarily deadly or wholly unusable.

Equally, a basic translation should not ignore these disagreements and replace the Qur’anic word with a single species name without qualification.

Khamṭ: changes in the flavor and smell of food

Another branch of this family concerns the taste and smell of milk and other drinks.

Arabic records labanun khamṭun and labanun khāmiṭun for milk whose smell has changed or acquired a new character.

Such a change does not always mean that the milk has completely spoiled.

In some reports in Lisān al-ʿArab, khāmiṭ milk has a pleasant smell, even one compared to lote fruit or apples. A similar description is also given for khamṭah as a drink. These are distinct uses with comparable descriptions of smell.

Other reports associate khamṭ with sourness and an undesirable change in flavor.

Al-khamṭah is also recorded for the scent of vine blossoms and certain other plants: a pleasant fragrance that is not exceedingly strong.

These examples show that a change in taste or smell need not mean becoming bitter, spoiled, or harmful.

In some cases, the substance has not fully matured, and its taste or smell is still developing.

Another recorded usage follows this pattern: something fresh that has acquired a flavor but has not fully matured or become established may be called khamṭ.

This helps map the root’s semantic field, but it should not be imposed on khamṭ in Sabaʾ 34:16 without evidence, since that verse specifically concerns garden produce.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab: milk, scent, drink, and flavor changes

Skinning and roasting

Early Arabic records khamaṭa al-shāta and khamaṭa al-ḥamala for skinning and roasting an animal.

In this use, the animal’s skin is removed and the meat is prepared for cooking.

Khamīṭ can refer to roasted meat.

Some sources distinguish khamṭ from samṭ: the former involves skinning and roasting, while the latter concerns removing the animal’s hair or wool. Alongside this familiar explanation, khamīṭ is also recorded for meat roasted with its skin. The reports should not all be treated as identical.

One lexical explanation also associates khamṭ with fire and samṭ with water.

These distinctions show that the words overlap in food preparation without being complete synonyms.

Here khamṭ does not mean bitter or unpleasant meat. The action concerns removing the skin and preparing or roasting the meat.

Bitter fruit and skinning an animal therefore should not be made necessary stages of one meaning without evidence.

Al-ʿUbāb: differing accounts of roasting with and without skin

Roaring and dominance: turbulent seas and human conduct

The second branch in Ibn Fāris’s division concerns roaring and forceful movement.

In Arabic, takhammaṭa al-faḥlu describes a male camel becoming excited and roaring loudly.

Takhammaṭa al-baḥru likewise refers to the sea becoming turbulent and its waves colliding.

The emphasis is on vigorous movement and the collision of waves, rather than the water’s quiet motion.

This family is also used of people.

Takhammaṭa al-rajulu can describe anger, unruliness, arrogance, and harsh behavior.

Al-Aṣmaʿī also connects takhammuṭ in certain uses with domination and taking something by force.

What these uses share is intensity and overpowering force in movement or conduct.

This branch does not make khamṭ in the Sabaʾ verse mean anger, arrogance, or rebellion. The Qur’anic word concerns garden produce and must be understood within that botanical setting.

3. Evidence from early Arabic poetry

Khamṭah: freshness and biting speech

A verse attributed to Khālid ibn Zuhayr al-Hudhalī reads:

وَلَا تَسْبِقَنَّ النَّاسَ مِنِّي بِخَمْطَةٍ
مِنَ السُّمِّ مَذْرُورٍ عَلَيْهَا ذُرُورُهَا

wa-lā tasbiqanna al-nāsa minnī bi-khamṭatin / mina al-summi madhrūrin ʿalayhā dhurūruhā

Approximate translation:

“Do not be the first to spread biting words about me among people, like fresh poison dusted with its poisonous powder.”

Commentaries offer two related but distinct explanations of khamṭah here.

One connects it with the freshness and sharpness of something, as though fresh material has a more powerful effect.

Al-Sukkarī, the commentator on Hudhalī poetry, instead relates khamṭah here to blame and ugly speech.

In this reading, the poet speaks of hurtful words whose sharp effect resembles poison.

This example shows that khamṭ and its derivatives could also describe a sharp or unpleasant quality.

The verse alone does not establish that poison or ugly speech is the original meaning of the whole family. Al-Aghānī transmits al-nāsa, while Lisān al-ʿArab has li-al-nāsi. The quotation retains the former reading.

Al-Aghānī: Khālid’s witness with al-nāsa

Khālid ibn Zuhayr is an early Hudhalī poet. The sources examined did not provide clear support for definitively classifying him as a mukhadram, a poet who lived in both the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. This particular verse is also not independently dated, so it is not described here as certainly pre-Qur’anic.

Khamiṭ al-tayyār: the roar of the sea

A verse attributed to Suwayd ibn Abī Kāhil al-Yashkurī reads:

ذُو عُبَابٍ زَبَدٍ آذِيُّهُ
خَمِطُ التَّيَّارِ يَرْمِي بِالْقَلَعْ

dhū ʿubābin zabadin ādhiyyuhu / khamiṭu al-tayyāri yarmī bi-al-qalaʿ

Translation:

“A sea with swelling, foaming waves and a raging current that casts great rocks.”

Khamiṭ al-tayyār here describes a current moving with force and turbulence.

The subject is not the water’s taste or smell, but the power of its movement.

This witness matters for the second branch because it applies khamṭ to a force of nature.

Some transmissions give the verbal yakhmiṭu al-tayyāra instead of khamiṭ al-tayyāri. This variation does not fundamentally alter the association with roaring and turbulence.

Suwayd ibn Abī Kāhil is known as a mukhadram. The verse is therefore evidence from early poetry attributed to him, rather than necessarily a witness with a securely established pre-Qur’anic date.

Tāj al-ʿArūs, Q L ʿ: large rocks in Suwayd’s witness

Khimāṭ: changes in the flavor of drink

A poem attributed to Al-Mutanakhkhil al-Hudhalī uses al-khimāṭ to describe drink.

One lexical transmission reads:

مُشَعْشَعَةٌ كَعَيْنِ الدِّيكِ، فِيهَا
حُمَيَّاهَا مِنَ الصُّهْبِ الْخِمَاطِ

mushaʿshaʿatun ka-ʿayni al-dīki fīhā / ḥumayyāhā mina al-ṣuhbi al-khimāṭi

Translation of the sense:

“A diluted drink, like a rooster’s eye in color, bearing the sharp force of reddish khimāṭ wines.”

Other transmissions differ in their words and even the construction of the second hemistich. The text above should not be treated as the sole, definitive version.

Lexical commentary also treats khimāṭ as the plural of khamṭah: drink that has acquired a smell but has not fully matured. Al-Sukkarī’s explanation also emphasizes its intoxicating effect and its taking hold of the drinker’s reason. These accounts should not be reduced to one fixed technical stage.

Alongside the uses of khamṭah for milk and drink, this witness shows that the field of flavor and smell existed in early Arabic independently of explanations of the Sabaʾ verse.

The available evidence does not establish a definite history of semantic development from plant to drink or from drink to plant.

Tāj al-ʿArūs: the poetic transmission and explanations of khimāṭ

4. Qur’anic usage: ukulin khamṭin in Sabaʾ 34:16

فَأَعْرَضُوا فَأَرْسَلْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ سَيْلَ الْعَرِمِ وَبَدَّلْنَاهُمْ بِجَنَّتَيْهِمْ جَنَّتَيْنِ ذَوَاتَيْ أُكُلٍ خَمْطٍ وَأَثْلٍ وَشَيْءٍ مِنْ سِدْرٍ قَلِيلٍ

fa-aʿraḍū fa-arsalnā ʿalayhim sayla al-ʿarimi wa-baddalnāhum bi-jannatayhim jannatayni dhawātay ukulin khamṭin wa-athlin wa-shayʾin min sidrin qalīlin

Proposed translation for this article, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Then they turned away, so We sent the flood of ʿArim upon them and replaced their two gardens with two other gardens bearing khamṭ fruit, athl trees, and a few lote trees.”

Sabaʾ 34:16

This is the only Qur’anic occurrence of KH M Ṭ.

The verse belongs to the story of the people of Sabaʾ. The preceding verse describes two gardens on the right and left, provision from their Lord, and a good land:

جَنَّتَانِ عَنْ يَمِينٍ وَشِمَالٍ

jannatāni ʿan yamīnin wa-shimālin

It then calls them to enjoy that provision and give thanks.

Verse sixteen describes their turning away, the flood of ʿArim, and the replacement of their gardens with two gardens yielding different produce.

Khamṭ thus belongs to a context of changed natural conditions and agricultural prosperity.

Does khamṭ name the tree or the fruit in this verse?

This is one of the verse’s main lexical questions.

Early explanations offer two prominent interpretations.

In the first, khamṭ names a tree such as arāk. Ukuli khamṭin then refers to that tree’s produce or fruit.

In the second, khamṭ names the fruit itself or describes its quality, especially fruit that is bitter, sour, or undesirable.

These interpretations also relate to different readings and grammatical constructions of the phrase.

The common reading is:

ذَوَاتَيْ أُكُلٍ خَمْطٍ

dhawātay ukulin khamṭin

Here ukulin has tanwīn and is followed by khamṭin. Khamṭ may describe the produce or stand in apposition, identifying its kind.

Abū ʿAmr’s reading is also transmitted with a genitive construction:

ذَوَاتَيْ أُكُلِ خَمْطٍ

dhawātay ukuli khamṭin

Ukuli is here placed in a genitive construction with khamṭin, making the sense “khamṭ produce” more explicit.

Al-Ṭabarī explains that with tanwīn, khamṭ can itself identify the produce, whereas in the genitive construction the produce is attributed to the tree or plant kind.

The structural difference is therefore more than a difference in sound: it affects the explanation of the relationship between ukul and khamṭ.

Both readings nevertheless occupy a shared semantic field: two gardens whose produce is identified with khamṭ.

Al-Durr al-Maṣūn: Abū ʿAmr’s genitive reading and grammatical analysis

Ukul and its importance for understanding khamṭ

Ukul belongs to the root ʾ K L. In reference to gardens and trees, it means produce and what is eaten from a plant.

Study of ʾ K L

Here khamṭ follows ukul.

Their connection shows that the verse concerns more than a tree’s presence in the landscape. The quality or kind of produce also matters.

A rendering such as “two gardens containing khamṭ” leaves part of the construction unexpressed because it does not clearly convey ukul.

“Bearing khamṭ fruit” or “bearing bitter, undesirable produce” better represents the relationship between the words.

These two translations are not exactly equivalent.

“Khamṭ fruit” preserves the name of the plant or produce.

“Bitter, undesirable fruit” relies on the lexical explanations of produce quality.

For a direct translation, preserving khamṭ and briefly explaining its bitterness or undesirable quality is lexically more cautious.

Al-Ṭabarī: khamṭ, arāk, and the distinction between the genitive construction and tanwīn

Why are the new gardens described through khamṭ, athl, and sidr?

Two more plant names follow in the verse:

وَأَثْلٍ وَشَيْءٍ مِنْ سِدْرٍ قَلِيلٍ

wa-athlin wa-shayʾin min sidrin qalīlin

Lexicons connect athl with a tree resembling ṭarfāʾ, or tamarisk.

Study of ʾ TH L

Sidr also names a familiar tree that can bear fruit.

These two names alongside khamṭ show that the verse concerns the gardens’ vegetation and produce.

This does not mean that all these trees are inherently useless or harmful.

Some can grow in dry, difficult conditions, provide animal fodder, or serve other human purposes.

The verse itself says shayʾin min sidrin qalīlin: a small amount of sidr.

The picture is therefore not the absolute destruction of every plant or the total absence of any produce.

Rather, the former gardens, presented as a sign of blessing and prosperity, have been replaced by gardens with different produce and vegetation.

Does khamṭ mean “worthless produce”?

In context, the replacement gardens represent a less desirable condition than the former gardens.

“Worthless,” however, is a relative judgment. It should not become an unexplained fixed definition of every use of khamṭ.

If khamṭ refers to a kind of arāk, for example, that tree can itself benefit people or animals. Its fruit is also edible in some circumstances.

Yet arāk or a plant bearing bitter produce does not replace a garden of abundant, desirable fruit with an equivalent benefit.

Something may be useful in one setting while remaining an undesirable replacement for a lost blessing.

This distinction matters in the story of Sabaʾ.

The verse does not declare every thorny, bitter, or desert plant useless. Nor does it establish a general judgment about the value of plants native to dry regions.

Its subject is the particular change in the people of Sabaʾ’s situation and the loss of their former prosperity.

Must khamṭ be restricted to one modern botanical species?

Some modern studies identify khamṭ with arāk, the toothbrush tree, Salvadora persica.

The lexical reports connecting khamṭ with arāk make this identification worth examining.

Early accounts do not identify khamṭ uniformly: some describe a tree, others arāk fruit, others bitter vegetation, and others a different plant.

Assigning one definitive modern scientific name to every use of khamṭ would therefore exceed the available evidence.

The modern arāk tree bears edible fruit. Botanical sources describe it as juicy and sweet with a peppery pungency. This description of the modern species does not by itself establish the referent of khamṭ in the verse.

World Agroforestry: edible fruit with a sweet, pungent flavor

Even if the verse’s khamṭ is connected with arāk, it does not follow without separate investigation that every arāk fruit is bitter or poisonous at every stage of growth.

Early plant names did not necessarily follow the precise boundaries of modern scientific taxonomy.

“A kind of arāk or bitter produce associated with it” is consequently more cautious lexically than identifying a scientific species with certainty.

Kew: modern arāk and the use of its fruit as food

Khamṭ, turning away, and ingratitude

Sabaʾ 34:15 places provision and gratitude together.

Sabaʾ 34:16 describes turning away, the flood, and the replacement of the gardens.

Sabaʾ 34:17 also says:

ذَٰلِكَ جَزَيْنَاهُمْ بِمَا كَفَرُوا

dhālika jazaynāhum bi-mā kafarū

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt: “That is how We recompensed them for their rejection.”

The connection between the people’s behavior and their changed circumstances is thus expressed by the surrounding verses.

This story does not establish that every flood, drought, or decline in harvest necessarily punishes people for rejection or ingratitude.

The Qur’an speaks about a particular people and situation. Extending that judgment to other people’s intentions or wrongdoing requires separate evidence.

KH M Ṭ itself does not mean ingratitude or punishment.

Khamṭ here identifies the kind or quality of produce. Its connection with recompense comes from the story as a whole.

5. Distinctions from related roots

KH M Ṭ (khamṭ) and M R R (marr): bitterness and a plant name

In one of its familiar uses, M R R concerns bitterness.

Murr can describe a bitter taste.

In its Qur’anic usage, khamṭ also refers to a kind of plant or produce, beyond its association with bitterness.

Marr and khamṭ are therefore not complete synonyms.

Explaining ukulin khamṭin as bitter produce draws on khamṭ’s lexical usage in that context; it does not make khamṭ the equivalent of the adjective murr in every construction.

Bitterness is not a necessary feature of the branches concerning a roaring sea or skinning an animal.

KH M Ṭ (khamṭ) and KH M R (khamr): changing flavor and drink

In the KH M Ṭ family, khamṭah can describe a drink whose smell or taste has changed.

In the KH M R family, khamr is the familiar name for intoxicating drink, and some forms concern the maturation and transformation of material.

The families overlap in some food related uses, but they have different roots.

Not every khāmiṭ drink is khamr, and not every khamr is necessarily at the stage called khamṭah.

Their similarity should not make their meanings identical or establish a shared historical derivation.

Study of KH M R

KH M Ṭ (khamṭ) and S M Ṭ (samṭ): preparing an animal for food

One area of early Arabic connects khamṭ and samṭ with preparing an animal for food.

In the familiar explanation, khamṭ involves skinning and roasting, whereas samṭ concerns removing hair or wool.

Some accounts describe khamṭ through fire and samṭ through water.

These distinctions are not recorded identically in every lexicon.

The roots can therefore overlap within a practical activity while remaining independent in form and semantic range.

The Sabaʾ verse does not concern roasting meat. The construction involving garden produce clearly favors the botanical branch.

KH M Ṭ (khamṭ) and KH Ḍ D (khaḍḍ): thorns and branches

In Arabic, KH Ḍ D concerns bending, breaking, cutting branches, and removing thorns.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:28 uses sidrin makhḍūdin of lote trees.

In Sabaʾ 34:16, however, KH M Ṭ names or describes a kind of plant and its produce.

Some lexical accounts connect khamṭ with a thorny tree, while another associates it with thornlessness.

That disagreement prevents thorns alone from establishing that khamṭ and khaḍḍ mean the same thing.

Makhḍūd describes the result of khaḍḍ, whereas khamṭ in the Sabaʾ verse identifies a kind or quality of produce.

Even when both verses concern trees, their roots do not have identical meanings.

Study of KH Ḍ D

KH M Ṭ (takhammuṭ) and Ṭ GH Y (ṭughyān): roaring and unruliness

In some Arabic uses, takhammuṭ describes roaring, anger, arrogance, and overpowering behavior.

In the Qur’an, ṭughyān also concerns exceeding bounds and rebellion.

These fields may overlap in describing some human conduct, without becoming complete synonyms.

Takhammaṭa al-baḥru, for example, concerns turbulent waves. The phrase itself makes no moral judgment about rebellion or violating another’s rights.

In Sabaʾ 34:16, khamṭ concerns garden produce. Resemblance to words for rebellion should not introduce unruliness into the plant name.

Study of Ṭ GH Y

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

No single short Persian equivalent for KH M Ṭ can contain all its recorded branches.

In botanical usage, khamṭ can name a tree or produce with a bitter, undesirable quality.

Depending on context, the flavor and smell branch may be conveyed by “becoming sour,” “a change in taste and smell,” or “an immature, changing food quality.”

For meat, “skinning and roasting” is more specific.

For the sea and human behavior, “roaring,” “becoming turbulent,” or “behaving harshly and forcefully” will fit the particular referent.

In the sole Qur’anic occurrence, however, the botanical setting is clear.

For the phrase:

ذَوَاتَيْ أُكُلٍ خَمْطٍ

dhawātay ukulin khamṭin

Proposed translation:

“Bearing khamṭ fruit.”

A lexical explanation can add:

“Khamṭ names a kind of tree or produce associated in early explanations with arāk and bitter or undesirable fruit.”

If a fluent translation needs to convey produce quality, “bearing bitter, undesirable fruit” is defensible. It does, however, turn a plant name into a quality description and move past some of the lexicographers’ disagreement.

For Forqan’s basic text, retaining khamṭ and explaining it in the root article allows greater precision.

Brief root meaning

In botanical usage, a kind of tree or bitter, undesirable produce; elsewhere, changes in flavor and smell, skinning, and roaring or forceful movement.

Meaning in this verse

Khamṭin — Sabaʾ 34:16: a kind of plant produce associated in early sources with the arāk tree and its bitter or undesirable fruit; here it replaces the produce of the people of Sabaʾ’s former gardens.

These definitions should remain separate. The brief root meaning describes the lexical family’s range, while the contextual meaning concerns only its botanical use in the story of Sabaʾ.

Conclusion

KH M Ṭ has several semantic fields in Arabic. They cannot all be explained by one short, definitive definition.

Ibn Fāris gives the material two principles: removal of a covering and smoothness, and roaring and dominance. Other dictionaries record the uses involving plants, fruit, bitterness, sourness, and changes in taste and smell in detail.

In its botanical branch, khamṭ sometimes names arāk or its fruit and sometimes bitter, undesirable vegetation or produce. Conflicting reports prevent restricting it in every case to one definite species or necessarily poisonous fruit.

The Qur’an uses the root only in Sabaʾ 34:16. Ukulin khamṭin describes the two gardens that replaced the people’s former gardens after they turned away. The context concerns changed blessing and prosperity, while the word itself still concerns plant produce.

Accordingly, Qur’anic khamṭ can be understood as a kind of plant produce with a bitter or undesirable quality, while recognizing that early explanations do not entirely separate the plant name from the fruit’s quality.

The other meanings, such as skinning, changes in a drink’s smell, and turbulent seas, matter for the history of Arabic. They should not be introduced into the meaning of the Sabaʾ verse without evidence.
