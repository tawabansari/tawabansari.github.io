The Qur’anic root KH W Ḍ: entering, mingling, and engaging in discourse

From wading in water and stirring it to entering conversations, engaging in matters, and discourse concerning divine signs

Arabic KH W Ḍ concerns entering something, moving within it, and becoming involved in it. Its clearest material image is someone entering water and wading through it. The movement involves passing through water and may also set the water in motion.

This lexical family extends beyond crossing water. Early Arabic also uses khawḍ for mixing a drink, entering a difficult undertaking, engaging with a subject, and participating in conversation.

Khāḍū fī al-ḥadīthi describes a group entering conversation and discussion about a subject. It may concern ordinary conversation, but in some contexts describes discourse involving frivolity, falsehood, mockery, or indifference to truth.

The Qur’an uses this root only in discourse and related conduct. All its occurrences belong to settings of criticism, yet an important distinction remains: the Qur’an uses the same verb both for blameworthy engagement with divine signs and for entering “another conversation.”

This shows that khawḍ does not fundamentally mean lying, mocking, or false discourse; it describes entering and engaging with a subject or conversation. The wrongdoing emerges from the subject, manner of engagement, and context.

1. Forms and semantic core

KH W Ḍ is a triliteral root with a medial wāw: its second radical is wāw, which becomes alif in some inflected forms.

Its basic verb is khāḍa–yakhūḍu.

The main Arabic forms include:

  • Khāḍa–yakhūḍu: to enter water and move through it; by extension, to enter an undertaking or address a subject.
  • Khawḍ: the verbal noun, entering and moving within something; in discourse, engaging in conversation.
  • Khiyāḍ: another verbal noun, particularly in material usages.
  • Khāʾiḍ: someone wading or entering and occupying themselves with a subject.
  • Khawwāḍ: someone frequently entering or deeply engaging in something; the precise meaning depends on the object.
  • Akhāḍa: to cause another, such as a horse or camel, to enter water.
  • Khawwaḍa: to move and mix something in water or a drink; some uses involve intensity or repetition.
  • Takhawwaḍa: to enter water or become involved in an undertaking.
  • Ikhtāḍa: to enter water; elsewhere, of pasture becoming dense and interwoven with vegetation.
  • Takhāwaḍa: to enter conversation with one another and discuss a subject.
  • Makhāḍah: a shallow water crossing traversable by people or animals.
  • Mikhwaḍ: an implement for stirring and mixing a drink or thin food.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an.

KH W Ḍ occurs twelve times in nine verses. Morphologically, the occurrences comprise the Form I verb, the verbal noun khawḍ, and the active participle khāʾiḍīn.

Verbal forms such as yakhūḍūna, yakhūḍū, nakhūḍu, khuḍtum, and khāḍū differ in tense, person, and number, but all belong to the same Form I verb.

Khawḍihim and khawḍin are the two verbal noun occurrences, differing in their possessive construction and inflection.

Al-khāʾiḍīna is a sound masculine plural active participle and occurs once.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: nine verbs, two verbal nouns, and one active participle in nine verses

The semantic core in Maqāyīs al-Lughah

Ibn Fāris gives this root one principle:

الْخَاءُ وَالْوَاوُ وَالضَّادُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى تَوَسُّطِ شَيْءٍ وَدُخُولٍ.

al-khāʾu wa-al-wāwu wa-al-ḍādu aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā tawassuṭi shayʾin wa-dukhūlin

Translation:

“Khāʾ, wāw, and ḍād have one principle indicating entering and being in the midst of something.”

He then states:

خُضْتُ الْمَاءَ وَغَيْرَهُ

khuḍtu al-māʾa wa-ghayrahu

That is: “I entered water and other things.”

He also explains takhāwaḍū fī al-ḥadīthi wa-al-amri through conversation and the interweaving of people’s speech.

The fundamental point is that khawḍ means more than approaching something or observing it from outside. It involves entering and occupying oneself within it.

Someone wading is not standing outside the water. Likewise, someone entering a subject or conversation is involved in it in some way, rather than merely a distant listener.

This connection between material and discursive usages does not make every conversation necessarily confused or disordered. Just as entering water does not always make it muddy, entering discourse need not produce corruption or frivolity.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: entering, being in the midst, and conversation

2. Material usages in early Arabic

Entering water and moving through it

Arabic khāḍa al-māʾa means entering water and moving through it.

Lisān al-ʿArab records:

خَاضَ الْمَاءَ يَخُوضُهُ خَوْضًا وَخِيَاضًا

khāḍa al-māʾa yakhūḍuhu khawḍan wa-khiyāḍan

It explains this through:

مَشَى فِيهِ

mashā fīhi

That is: “He walked in it.”

The image concerns someone entering water on foot or by another means and passing through it.

This differs from swimming. Swimming may place all or much of the body in water and involve movement of the arms and legs; ordinary khawḍ across water may involve walking along a riverbed or pool bottom.

Khāḍa al-māʾa therefore does not necessarily mean swimming or becoming completely submerged.

Crossing water may also disturb mud, sand, or sediment, but making the water muddy is not necessary to the verb’s meaning.

Someone walking in clear, shallow water can still be said to perform khawḍ even without visibly clouding it.

This distinction matters for the root’s extensions: movement and entry into something are more fundamental, while disturbance and confusion may be consequences.

Makhāḍah: a water crossing

Makhāḍah names a place where people and animals can cross water.

Lexicons describe a river section whose depth or conditions allow people to cross on foot or mounted.

Knowing such crossings had practical importance for travelers, herders, and caravans in early Arabic life.

A deep, forceful river section may make direct crossing difficult. In a shallower section or one with a suitable bed, people and animals can enter the water and pass through.

That place is called a makhāḍah.

It names the location enabling khawḍ, rather than the action itself.

Some dictionaries also record makhāḍ as a plural of makhāḍah or a name for the crossing. This should be distinguished from makhāḍ as labor pains, which belongs to a different lexical family.

Similar word forms do not by themselves establish that every meaning belongs to one root.

Causing an animal to enter water

Akhāḍa farasahu al-māʾa means making one’s horse enter water.

The subject causes the animal to perform khawḍ.

Akhāḍa al-qawmu is likewise used of a group taking their horses into water.

The distinction between khāḍa and akhāḍa matters here.

Khāḍa al-farasu al-māʾa means the horse entered the water.

Akhāḍa al-rajulu farasahu means the man made the horse enter it.

In the first, the animal is the subject of entering; in the second, the person causes its entry.

Morphology can thus change the relationship between the agent and the object while preserving the main semantic field.

Stirring and mixing a drink

Early Arabic also records khāḍa al-sharāba and khawwaḍahu for stirring and mixing a drink.

Something enters the liquid and moves its components so that they mingle.

Mikhwaḍ names an implement used for this.

Sawīq, for example, was prepared from roasted and ground grain or flour and could be mixed with water. An implement was used to stir it and disperse the ingredients through the liquid.

Arabic could call this action khawḍ.

Unlike crossing a river, the aim is not to pass from one side of a liquid to another, but to move and mix its components.

The shared element is entry and movement within something.

Khawḍ should nevertheless be distinguished from its result: not every khawḍ combines materials, though stirring liquid is one usage.

Entering difficult undertakings

Arabic uses the expression:

خَاضَ الْغَمَرَاتِ

khāḍa al-ghamarāti

That is: “He entered difficulties and dangers.”

Ghamarāt here can concern hard circumstances surrounding a person.

Entering deep water or a difficult current naturally provides an image for undertaking something dangerous.

Khawḍ in affairs consequently extends to entering and engaging with a matter.

Entering a difficult undertaking does not itself establish moral courage or recklessness. Someone may face danger for a sound purpose or enter it without considering the consequences.

The action’s value follows from its aim and circumstances, rather than from khāḍa itself.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Miṣbāḥ, and Al-Mughrib: water, ford, and stirring implement

3. Evidence from early Arabic poetry

Mixing a drink: a witness attributed to Al-Ḥuṭayʾah

A verse by Al-Ḥuṭayʾah reads:

وَقَالَتْ شَرَابٌ بَارِدٌ فَاشْرَبَنَّهُ
وَلَمْ يَدْرِ مَا خَاضَتْ لَهُ فِي الْمَجَادِحِ

wa-qālat sharābun bāridun fa-ishrabannahu / wa-lam yadri mā khāḍat lahu fī al-majādiḥi

Translation:

“She said: ‘A cold drink, so be sure to drink it’; he did not know what she had mixed for him with the stirring implements.”

The poem’s setting concerns a woman mixing something harmful into her husband’s drink.

Khāḍat does not mean the woman entered the drink herself. It concerns stirring and mixing its contents.

Al-majādiḥ is the plural of mijdaḥ, an implement for stirring drink or thin food.

The witness illustrates khawḍ in everyday drink preparation.

Movement within something can thus become moving and mixing its contents in another usage.

The mixing has a wrongful purpose in the verse, but that wrongness comes from the story and the woman’s intention, rather than from khāḍa itself.

The same verb can describe stirring an ordinary, beneficial drink.

Al-Ḥuṭayʾah is a mukhadram who lived before and after Islam’s emergence. The witness belongs to early Arabic, but without independent evidence this particular verse cannot be dated definitively to the pre-Islamic period.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Al-Ḥuṭayʾah and mixing a drink with majādiḥ

Ikhtiyāḍ in pasture: Salamah ibn al-Khurshub’s poetry

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Salamah ibn al-Khurshub al-Anmārī reads:

وَمُخْتَاضٍ تَبِيضُ الرُّبْدُ فِيهِ
تُحُومِيَ نَبْتُهُ فَهْوَ الْعَمِيمُ

wa-mukhtāḍin tabīḍu al-rubdu fīhi / tuḥūmiya nabtuhu fa-huwa al-ʿamīmu

Translation:

“And many a dense pasture where grey ostriches lay their eggs, its vegetation left ungrazed until it has become abundant and luxuriant.”

Mukhtāḍ here describes pasture with dense, interwoven vegetation.

Ibn al-Sikkīt explains ikhtāḍa al-marʿā as the pasture’s vegetation becoming abundant and intertwined.

Commentary on Al-Mufaḍḍaliyyāt also explains that remoteness and difficult access reduced grazing, allowing the vegetation to grow abundantly.

Its relationship with mingling and entering the midst of things can be examined, but it should not be identified precisely with crossing water.

It is unclear whether dense vegetation developed directly from the image of moving through water or constituted a related, relatively independent branch.

Unlike Al-Ḥuṭayʾah’s example, this witness is recorded in poetry attributed to a pre-Islamic poet and appears in Al-Mufaḍḍaliyyāt. The poet’s period alone does not, however, establish the exact date of this verse.

Al-Mufaḍḍaliyyāt: the dense pasture witness

A water usage

In Lisān al-ʿArab, Ibn al-Aʿrābī quotes this rajaz:

كَأَنَّهُ فِي الْغَرْضِ إِذْ تَرَكَّضَا
دُعْمُوصُ مَاءٍ قَلَّ مَا تَخَوَّضَا

ka-annahu fī al-gharḍi idh tarakkaḍā / duʿmūṣu māʾin qalla mā takhawwaḍā

Approximate translation:

“As it moves, it seems like a small creature in clear water in which there has been little movement or passing through.”

Duʿmūṣ names a small creature living in water.

Khawḍ concerns movement in water here, and the lexical explanation describes water remaining clear when little disturbed. The examined transmission has the active takhawwaḍā, which should not be confused with the passive vocalization tukhuwwiḍā. Lisān al-ʿArab gives al-gharḍ in the first hemistich, while Al-Muḥkam has al-farḍ. The quotation retains the former.

The examined transmission does not securely attribute the rajaz to a specific poet. It cannot therefore be called a definitively pre-Islamic witness.

Its value lies in recording the root’s material use in early Arabic.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Muḥkam: takhawwaḍā and the gharḍ/farḍ variant

These examples show three usages: moving through water, mixing drink, and pasture with dense vegetation. This range clarifies the linguistic background of the root’s uses in conversation and nonmaterial affairs.

4. Khawḍ in discourse: conversation or involvement in falsehood?

From movement through water to addressing a subject

Khāḍa al-qawmu fī al-ḥadīthi means a group entered conversation and discussed a subject.

Takhāwaḍū fī al-amri likewise describes people discussing an undertaking or matter together.

This is not literal immersion in water. An image of entering and engaging with something is transferred to discourse.

The verb itself does not conclusively judge the speech as true or false.

People may discuss a scientific, social, or practical question; others may lie, mock someone, or address a subject without knowledge.

Both involve conversation, but its quality and value differ.

Lisān al-ʿArab explains khāḍa al-qawmu fī al-ḥadīthi wa-takhāwaḍū through conversation and exchanged speech, while also recording khawḍ for discourse involving falsehood and untruth.

Neither usage should erase the other from the language’s history.

Although many Qur’anic instances are blameworthy, the root should not initially be defined as lying or mocking.

Entering conversation and confused speech

In some usages, khawḍ concerns conversation whose subjects are mixed and disordered.

This relates to the image of movement through water and its disturbance.

Yet moving through water need not make it muddy, just as entering conversation need not confuse it.

Translating khāḍa fī al-ḥadīthi requires determining whether the context concerns ordinary conversation, serious discussion, idle speech, or falsehood and mockery.

Without a specified setting, “entered conversation” or “engaged in discourse” is lexically more cautious.

Where the verse describes mockery, denial, and treating truth as play, its contextual meaning can be explained through idle and false discourse.

A decisive Qur’anic distinction

Al-Anʿām 6:68 uses a verb from this root twice:

يَخُوضُونَ فِي آيَاتِنَا

yakhūḍūna fī āyātinā

And:

يَخُوضُوا فِي حَدِيثٍ غَيْرِهِ

yakhūḍū fī ḥadīthin ghayrihi

The first part criticizes a group’s conduct; the second makes a change of conversational subject the endpoint of the command to turn away.

Khawḍ therefore cannot inherently and invariably mean mockery or false discourse.

If it did, engaging in another conversation would necessarily express the same wrong conduct, obscuring the change of subject’s role in the command.

This shows that the subject and manner of entering conversation fundamentally shape khawḍ’s contextual meaning.

Al-Ṭabarī: blameworthy discourse and another conversation

5. Qur’anic usages

A. Discourse concerning divine signs: Al-Anʿām 6:68

وَإِذَا رَأَيْتَ الَّذِينَ يَخُوضُونَ فِي آيَاتِنَا فَأَعْرِضْ عَنْهُمْ حَتَّىٰ يَخُوضُوا فِي حَدِيثٍ غَيْرِهِ وَإِمَّا يُنْسِيَنَّكَ الشَّيْطَانُ فَلَا تَقْعُدْ بَعْدَ الذِّكْرَىٰ مَعَ الْقَوْمِ الظَّالِمِينَ

wa-idhā raʾayta alladhīna yakhūḍūna fī āyātinā fa-aʿriḍ ʿanhum ḥattā yakhūḍū fī ḥadīthin ghayrihi wa-immā yunsiyannaka al-shayṭānu fa-lā taqʿud baʿda al-dhikrā maʿa al-qawmi al-ẓālimīna

Proposed translation for this article, without changing the installed verse translation:

“When you see those engaging in discourse concerning Our signs, turn away from them until they engage in another conversation. If Satan makes you forget, do not sit with the wrongdoing people after remembering.”

Al-Anʿām 6:68

Khawḍ occurs twice in this verse.

Yakhūḍūna is a masculine plural imperfect verb referring to those engaging in discourse concerning divine signs.

Yakhūḍū is the same verb following ḥattā and concerns entering another conversation.

In the first part, discourse concerning the signs is accompanied by the command to turn away.

The preceding context, particularly Al-Anʿām 6:66, describes denying the truth. Al-Anʿām 6:70 describes people treating their religion as play and diversion.

The discourse here is consequently more than studying or discussing the Qur’an. It concerns wrongful engagement and treating truth as play.

The verse nevertheless uses the general verb khawḍ for this behavior, whose blameworthy quality emerges from context.

Why until they enter another conversation?

Ḥattā yakhūḍū fī ḥadīthin ghayrihi is particularly important.

A change of subject marks the temporal endpoint of the command to turn away.

If criticism concerned speaking itself, changing the subject would make no difference. The verse attends to what is discussed.

Discourse concerning divine signs here involves wrongdoing, whereas another conversation can create a different situation.

This does not authorize every other wrongful conversation. The verse concerns the end of this particular conduct, rather than approving every possible discussion.

Al-Anʿām 6:69 also raises accountability and reminding. Turning away should therefore not become a denial of reminding itself or responsibility to state the truth.

Are questioning and criticizing the Qur’an blameworthy khawḍ?

The word itself does not establish that conclusion.

Questions seeking understanding, comparison of translations, assessment of arguments, and evidence based discussion of verses differ from deliberately treating truth as play.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:82 invites reflection on the Qur’an, for example, while Al-Zumar 39:18 mentions those who listen to discourse and follow its best.

Serious Qur’anic discussion should not be treated as blameworthy khawḍ merely because it concerns verses.

Al-Anʿām 6:68 concerns the manner of engagement within a setting of denial and treating truth as play.

This does not make every statement labeled “research” or “a question” automatically correct. Claims and conversational methods should be assessed through evidence and sound principles.

B. Sitting in a gathering of rejection and mockery: Al-Nisāʾ 4:140

وَقَدْ نَزَّلَ عَلَيْكُمْ فِي الْكِتَابِ أَنْ إِذَا سَمِعْتُمْ آيَاتِ اللَّهِ يُكْفَرُ بِهَا وَيُسْتَهْزَأُ بِهَا فَلَا تَقْعُدُوا مَعَهُمْ حَتَّىٰ يَخُوضُوا فِي حَدِيثٍ غَيْرِهِ إِنَّكُمْ إِذًا مِثْلُهُمْ إِنَّ اللَّهَ جَامِعُ الْمُنَافِقِينَ وَالْكَافِرِينَ فِي جَهَنَّمَ جَمِيعًا

wa-qad nazzala ʿalaykum fī al-kitābi an idhā samiʿtum āyāti allāhi yukfaru bihā wa-yustahzaʾu bihā fa-lā taqʿudū maʿahum ḥattā yakhūḍū fī ḥadīthin ghayrihi innakum idhan mithluhum inna allāha jāmiʿu al-munāfiqīna wa-al-kāfirīna fī jahannama jamīʿan

Proposed translation for this article, without changing the installed verse translation:

“He has already sent down to you in the Book that, when you hear the signs of The God rejected and mocked, you should not sit with them until they engage in another conversation; otherwise you would be like them. The God will surely gather the hypocrites and disbelievers together in Hell.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:140

This verse describes the criticized behavior more explicitly than Al-Anʿām 6:68.

It contains two verbs:

يُكْفَرُ بِهَا

yukfaru bihā

And:

يُسْتَهْزَأُ بِهَا

yustahzaʾu bihā

The divine signs are being rejected and mocked.

The verse then commands not sitting with those people until they enter another conversation.

The prohibition thus concerns sitting in a gathering where the signs are rejected and mocked, rather than discussing the Qur’an as such.

This clearly illuminates the contextual meaning of khawḍ in Al-Anʿām 6:68.

Yakhūḍū fī ḥadīthin ghayrihi again shows that khawḍ can describe another conversational subject.

Accordingly, khawḍ is the broader word; rejection and mockery are particular behaviors accompanying it here.

The verse also concerns knowingly continuing in such a gathering. Innakum idhan mithluhum should be read with the preceding command, rather than made a judgment on every accidental or uninformed presence.

Al-Ṭabarī: rejection, mockery, and sitting with the speakers

C. Discourse excused as play: Al-Tawbah 9:65

وَلَئِنْ سَأَلْتَهُمْ لَيَقُولُنَّ إِنَّمَا كُنَّا نَخُوضُ وَنَلْعَبُ قُلْ أَبِاللَّهِ وَآيَاتِهِ وَرَسُولِهِ كُنْتُمْ تَسْتَهْزِئُونَ

wa-la-in saʾaltahum la-yaqūlunna innamā kunnā nakhūḍu wa-nalʿabu qul a-bi-allāhi wa-āyātihi wa-rasūlihi kuntum tastahziʾūna

Proposed translation for this article, without changing the installed verse translation:

“If you ask them, they will surely say: ‘We were only engaging in discourse and playing.’ Say: ‘Were you mocking The God, His signs, and His Messenger?’”

Al-Tawbah 9:65

Nakhūḍu is the first person plural imperfect of khāḍa.

The speakers describe their conduct through two verbs:

نَخُوضُ وَنَلْعَبُ

nakhūḍu wa-nalʿabu

They had entered conversation and regarded it as play.

The Qur’anic response describes that conduct through tastahziʾūna.

This matters because khawḍ and laʿib are not lexically identical to mockery. Here, however, discourse presented as play or amusement involves mocking The God, His signs, and His Messenger.

Calling conduct “a joke” or “play” does not itself change its nature.

Al-Tawbah 9:66 also rejects their response and describes rejection after faith.

The criticism concerns the discourse’s content and excusing mockery as play, rather than simply entering conversation.

Nakhūḍu should not be translated directly as “we were mocking,” because the verse uses the separate verb tastahziʾūna for mockery.

Both verbs occur in the same verse, and their distinction should remain.

D. Discourse resembling earlier peoples’ conduct: Al-Tawbah 9:69

كَالَّذِينَ مِنْ قَبْلِكُمْ كَانُوا أَشَدَّ مِنْكُمْ قُوَّةً وَأَكْثَرَ أَمْوَالًا وَأَوْلَادًا فَاسْتَمْتَعُوا بِخَلَاقِهِمْ فَاسْتَمْتَعْتُمْ بِخَلَاقِكُمْ كَمَا اسْتَمْتَعَ الَّذِينَ مِنْ قَبْلِكُمْ بِخَلَاقِهِمْ وَخُضْتُمْ كَالَّذِي خَاضُوا أُولَٰئِكَ حَبِطَتْ أَعْمَالُهُمْ فِي الدُّنْيَا وَالْآخِرَةِ وَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْخَاسِرُونَ

ka-alladhīna min qablikum kānū ashadda minkum quwwatan wa-akthara amwālan wa-awlādan fa-istamtaʿū bi-khalāqihim fa-istamtaʿtum bi-khalāqikum ka-mā istamtaʿa alladhīna min qablikum bi-khalāqihim wa-khuḍtum ka-alladhī khāḍū ulāʾika ḥabiṭat aʿmāluhum fī al-dunyā wa-al-ākhirati wa-ulāʾika humu al-khāsirūna

Proposed translation for this article, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Like those before you: they were stronger than you and had more wealth and children. They enjoyed their share, and you enjoyed yours as those before you enjoyed theirs; you engaged in discourse as they did. Their deeds came to nothing in this world and the Hereafter, and they are the losers.”

Al-Tawbah 9:69

The root occurs twice:

خُضْتُمْ

khuḍtum

And:

خَاضُوا

khāḍū

The first verb concerns the addressees, the second earlier groups.

The verse compares their behavior and shows a resemblance in a kind of khawḍ.

Different grammatical analyses are proposed for alladhī in ka-alladhī khāḍū. In one familiar explanation, alladhī with its clause functions like a verbal noun: the comparison is with their khawḍ.

خُضْتُمْ خَوْضًا كَخَوْضِهِمْ

khuḍtum khawḍan ka-khawḍihim

Under this analysis, the comparison concerns the kind of conduct, rather than merely the people’s number or identity. It should not be taken to deny every other grammatical analysis.

For translation, “you engaged as they engaged” makes this construction clearer than assigning alladhī to one particular person without attending to the understood verbal noun.

The context concerns enjoying worldly life and following earlier groups’ conduct, connecting khawḍ with their wrongful ways.

The root itself does not mean imitating predecessors. That meaning follows from the comparison and context.

Al-Ṭabarī: comparing discourse with earlier peoples

E. Khawḍ as an occupation people are immersed in: Al-Anʿām 6:91

The end of Al-Anʿām 6:91 states:

قُلِ اللَّهُ ثُمَّ ذَرْهُمْ فِي خَوْضِهِمْ يَلْعَبُونَ

quli allāhu thumma dharhum fī khawḍihim yalʿabūna

Proposed translation of the quoted excerpt, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Say: ‘The God’; then leave them playing in their discourse.”

Al-Anʿām 6:91

Khawḍihim is a verbal noun with a plural possessive pronoun.

Unlike verbal occurrences describing an action, khawḍ itself is presented as a condition in which people are situated.

The context concerns the Book sent down to Moses. A group has claimed that The God sent nothing down to any human.

The verse then asks about the Book Moses brought, described as light and guidance for people.

In the same setting, it mentions revealing and concealing portions of the Book.

Khawḍihim at the end therefore belongs to their discourse and treatment of revelation.

The verbal noun alone cannot identify precisely which claim or sentence is intended. The context clarifies denial of revelation and treating the matter as play.

Yalʿabūna is another independent verb describing their conduct.

Khawḍ resembles a field in which people occupy themselves here, but being “in khawḍ” does not mean literal immersion in liquid.

This is a nonmaterial use of a family fundamentally connected with entering the midst of something.

Al-Ṭabarī: revelation, Moses’ Book, and their discourse

F. Khawḍ and play in Al-Ṭūr 52:12

الَّذِينَ هُمْ فِي خَوْضٍ يَلْعَبُونَ

alladhīna hum fī khawḍin yalʿabūna

Proposed translation for this article, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Those who play amid idle discourse.”

Al-Ṭūr 52:12

The preceding verse mentions deniers, so this describes that group.

Fī khawḍin, like Al-Anʿām 6:91, presents khawḍ as a condition or sphere in which people find themselves.

Here khawḍin is indefinite and has no possessive pronoun.

Yalʿabūna also describes their conduct.

The surah concerns denial and facing its outcome, so this khawḍ belongs to idle, wrongful behavior.

Khawḍ and laʿib should nevertheless be distinguished.

Khawḍ expresses entering and engaging with a subject.

Laʿib expresses play and amusement.

They may combine in one behavior, but not every khawḍ is play, nor is every play a khawḍ in discourse.

The Qur’an places them together here to describe the deniers’ condition.

G. Discourse and play until the promised day: Al-Zukhruf 43:83 and Al-Maʿārij 70:42

Al-Zukhruf 43:83 states:

فَذَرْهُمْ يَخُوضُوا وَيَلْعَبُوا حَتَّىٰ يُلَاقُوا يَوْمَهُمُ الَّذِي يُوعَدُونَ

fa-dharhum yakhūḍū wa-yalʿabū ḥattā yulāqū yawmahumu alladhī yūʿadūna

Proposed translation for this article, without changing the installed verse translation:

“So leave them to engage in discourse and play until they encounter the day they are promised.”

The same wording occurs in Al-Maʿārij 70:42.

Yakhūḍū in both is a Form I verb placed alongside yalʿabū.

In Al-Zukhruf, the preceding context concerns attributing offspring to The God and making false statements about Him.

Al-Maʿārij’s context concerns rejecters and their response to the divine call.

The subject of khawḍ in both surahs thus connects with wrongful discourse and conduct.

Fa-dharhum itself commands leaving them until their encounter with the promised day.

This does not approve their khawḍ, nor establish a general rule to abandon every conversation with opponents.

Both contexts concern groups persisting in denial and treating truth as play.

Khawḍ can therefore be ongoing behavior, but duration follows from construction and context, rather than being an invariable root meaning.

H. Joining those engaged in khawḍ: Al-Muddaththir 74:45

وَكُنَّا نَخُوضُ مَعَ الْخَائِضِينَ

wa-kunnā nakhūḍu maʿa al-khāʾiḍīna

Proposed translation for this article, without changing the installed verse translation:

“We used to engage in discourse with those engaged in it.”

Al-Muddaththir 74:45

This verse is morphologically significant because it brings two forms of the root into one sentence.

Nakhūḍu is a first person plural imperfect verb.

Al-khāʾiḍīna is a sound masculine plural active participle, here the genitive complement of maʿa, marked by yāʾ. Maʿa is an accusative adverbial noun expressing accompaniment, rather than a preposition.

The construction shows that the speakers do more than report others’ khawḍ: they identify themselves as joining the same behavior.

The preceding verses ask what brought them into Saqar.

Their answer mentions not establishing ṣalāh and not feeding the needy. They then mention joining those engaged in khawḍ, followed by denial of the Day of Recompense.

Khawḍ is thus one part of a group of wrongful behaviors.

All those behaviors should not be placed inside the word khāʾiḍīn itself.

Lexically, khāʾiḍīn are those engaged in khawḍ. Context makes clear that this khawḍ is blameworthy.

The role of maʿa

Maʿa is an accusative adverbial noun expressing accompaniment here, related to the verb nakhūḍu.

The speakers say they accompanied those engaged in khawḍ and performed the same behavior.

The verse therefore describes participation, beyond simply hearing others’ wrongful speech.

Three situations can be distinguished: hearing discourse, attending a gathering where it occurs, and joining its speakers in that conduct.

Nakhūḍu maʿa al-khāʾiḍīna in Al-Muddaththir 74:45 emphasizes participation.

This can be compared with Al-Nisāʾ 4:140, criticizing continued sitting with people rejecting and mocking the signs.

Both address individual conduct within a group, but their linguistic constructions differ.

Al-Muddaththir does not make every association with wrongdoers automatically identical to their khawḍ. The expression concerns joining the act itself.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: maʿa as an accusative adverbial noun

6. Distinctions from related roots

KH W Ḍ (khawḍ) and KH L Ṭ (khalṭ): entering and mixing

KH L Ṭ concerns mixing two or more things.

KH W Ḍ fundamentally involves entering and moving within something.

These roots overlap in some uses.

Stirring a drink with an implement involves both movement within liquid and mixing its components.

The two lexical families may describe different aspects of that action.

Wading through water, however, does not necessarily mix two substances.

Engaging with a subject or conversation likewise does not necessarily mingle truth and falsehood.

Khawḍ should therefore not be translated as mixing in every verse.

Study of KH L Ṭ

KH W Ḍ (khawḍ) and J D L (jadal): conversation and argument

Discursive jadal concerns discussion and argumentative contention between parties.

Khawḍ may also occur in conversation, but does not necessarily mean reasoned debate.

People may discuss a subject without clear evidence, or even treat it as play. That can be khawḍ without organized, reasoned argument.

Not every jadal is blameworthy either.

Al-Naḥl 16:125 commands argument in a better manner.

Ghāfir 40:35, however, criticizes disputing divine signs without proof or authority.

A conversation’s ethical value thus depends on its method and subject.

Criticism of khawḍ does not make every discussion, question, or argument about the Qur’an forbidden.

Study of J D L

KH W Ḍ (khawḍ) and L ʿ B (laʿib): addressing a subject and treating it as play

Laʿib concerns play and amusement.

Several verses place khawḍ and laʿib together, including Al-Tawbah 9:65, Al-Zukhruf 43:83, Al-Ṭūr 52:12, and Al-Maʿārij 70:42.

Both words can describe the same behavior together.

If khawḍ inherently meant the same as laʿib, the two elements’ semantic distinction would disappear.

Khawḍ concerns entering and addressing a subject; laʿib concerns a manner of behavior involving play and amusement.

Both can fit someone treating a serious matter as play.

Playing in a wholesome activity need not be khawḍ in falsehood, just as seriously addressing a scholarly question is not laʿib.

KH W Ḍ (khawḍ) and L GH W (laghw): idle discourse and engagement in it

Laghw describes discourse or action lacking the necessary value in meaning or result.

Khawḍ can describe entering such discourse, but has a broader meaning.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:3 describes believers turning away from laghw.

This conceptually relates to turning away from blameworthy khawḍ in Al-Anʿām 6:68.

The roots are nevertheless different.

Laghw primarily describes a characteristic of speech or action, while khawḍ describes entering and occupying oneself with it.

Khawḍ should not become laghw in every case, though idle speech may explain it in a blameworthy context.

KH W Ḍ (khawḍ) and Ḥ W R (ḥiwār): dialogue and participation in discourse

Ḥiwār and muḥāwarah involve returning and exchanging speech between parties.

Al-Kahf 18:34 uses yuḥāwiruhu for dialogue between two people.

Takhāwaḍū is likewise recorded for several people entering conversation.

Khawḍ also carries an image of entering and engaging with a subject, whereas muḥāwarah emphasizes exchanged and returned speech.

The words can therefore concern one conversation in some situations without being complete synonyms.

This matters for Qur’anic usage: khawḍ often appears amid frivolity and denial, while ḥiwār itself does not establish that fixed evaluation.

Study of Ḥ W R

KH W Ḍ (khawḍ) and KH W F (khawf): entering danger and fearing

KH W F is an independent root concerning fear.

KH W Ḍ concerns entering and engaging with something.

Khāḍa al-ghamarāti can describe entering danger and difficulty, but does not mean fearing.

Someone may enter danger fearfully or face it confidently and courageously.

The thematic proximity of danger and fear does not make their roots identical.

Study of KH W F

KH W Ḍ (khawḍ) and KH W R (khuwār): engagement and an animal call

KH W R is another root, used in the Qur’an for the calf’s khuwār.

KH W Ḍ concerns entering and moving within something, and Qur’anically engaging in discourse.

The calf’s khuwār in Al-Aʿrāf 7:148 and Ṭā Hā 20:88 therefore has no direct lexical connection with khawḍ concerning verses or discourse.

Sharing the first two letters does not establish identical roots or meanings.

Study of KH W R

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

No fixed Persian equivalent for KH W Ḍ is precise in every use.

Material khāḍa al-māʾa can be rendered “entered and moved through water” or “passed through water.”

For food, “mixing and stirring” is clearer.

For affairs, “addressing a subject” or “engaging in an undertaking” may fit.

Qur’anic cases all concern discourse or related behavior. “Engaging in discourse,” “entering conversation,” and, where blameworthy, “indulging in idle or false discourse” are more appropriate equivalents.

Falsehood, mockery, play, and denial are not independent elements of khawḍ in every case. Many verses convey them through context and accompanying words.

For example, in Al-Anʿām 6:68:

يَخُوضُونَ فِي آيَاتِنَا

yakhūḍūna fī āyātinā

A basic translation could be:

“They engage in discourse concerning Our signs.”

A more natural contextual Persian rendering would be:

“They engage in idle discourse concerning Our signs.”

This conveys the contextual sense.

The same verse contains:

حَتَّىٰ يَخُوضُوا فِي حَدِيثٍ غَيْرِهِ

ḥattā yakhūḍū fī ḥadīthin ghayrihi

This is better rendered:

“Until they engage in another conversation.”

This is not a contradiction: the verb’s lexical meaning takes different contextual forms as the subject changes.

In Al-Tawbah 9:65, nakhūḍu wa-nalʿabu can be rendered “we were engaging in discourse and playing,” with commentary clarifying that the actual discourse involved mockery.

In Al-Muddaththir 74:45, nakhūḍu maʿa al-khāʾiḍīna can be rendered “we joined those indulging in false discourse,” while explaining that context establishes the conduct’s falsehood.

Brief root meaning

Entering and moving within something; in discourse, entering a discussion or conversation and engaging in it.

Conclusion

Arabic KH W Ḍ concerns entering and moving within something, clearly illustrated by passing through water. This field also extends to stirring drink, entering difficult undertakings, and conversation.

Early Arabic witnesses show ordinary material actions such as wading or preparing a drink. Discursive usage can concern entering discussion and exchanging speech without the root itself establishing wrongdoing.

All Qur’anic occurrences belong to critical settings, but the whole expression establishes the blameworthy meaning.

In particular, Al-Anʿām 6:68 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:140 use the same verb for another conversation. Khawḍ therefore cannot invariably mean lying, mockery, or opposition to the signs.

Al-Tawbah 9:65 joins khawḍ with play, and the Qur’anic response describes the speakers’ conduct as mockery. Al-Muddaththir 74:45 records their admission of joining those engaged in khawḍ. Other verses place it within denial, treating truth as play, and continuing idle discourse.

Accordingly, the fundamental meaning is entering and engaging within something; its Qur’anic meaning concerns addressing and occupying oneself with discourse or a subject, often accompanied contextually by falsehood, mockery, and frivolity.

Discussion, questioning, critical examination, and supported argument should not be called blameworthy khawḍ solely because they concern divine signs.

The principal distinction in Qur’anic usage concerns how truth is treated and how the conversation is approached, rather than conversation alone.

This preserves the lexical meaning while allowing the Qur’anic criticism of idle discourse, mockery, and participation in wrongdoing to be understood accurately. Brief root meaning: Entering and moving within something; in discourse, entering a discussion or conversation and engaging in it.
