Standing in a row and arranging side by side: from human ranks and birds’ outspread wings to objects placed in rows

The Arabic root Ṣ F F concerns things standing beside one another, aligning, and being arranged along an extent. People may stand in one or more rows, objects may be placed side by side, and a bird may open its wings and hold them outspread.

This family includes ṣaff, a row or rank; ṣufūf, rows; ṣāff, someone or something forming a rank or holding its limbs outspread; maṣfūf, arranged in rows; and iṣṭifāf, forming ranks.

The root occurs 14 times in 12 Qur’anic verses: ṣaffan seven times, ṣāffāt three times, al-ṣāffūn once, ṣawāffa once, and maṣfūfah twice.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: fourteen occurrences in five forms

Its uses concern human beings, angels, birds, livestock, and arranged objects.

An important distinction is that being in ranks does not necessarily mean obedience, agreement in thought, or a rightful purpose. Ṣaff basically describes how people or things are positioned. Whether a rank is formed for worship, warfare, a display of power, or another purpose follows from context.

When applied to birds, ṣāffāt likewise need not mean flocking in a single row; it can refer to opening the wings and holding them outspread.

1. The semantic core: alignment along an extent

Ṣaff: standing side by side

Ibn Fāris writes about this family in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

الصَّادُ وَالْفَاءُ يَدُلُّ عَلَى أَصْلٍ وَاحِدٍ، وَهُوَ اسْتِوَاءٌ فِي الشَّيْءِ وَتَسَاوٍ بَيْنَ شَيْئَيْنِ فِي الْمَقَرِّ

al-ṣādu wa-al-fāʾu yadullu ʿalā aṣlin wāḥidin, wa-huwa istiwāʾun fī al-shayʾi wa-tasāwin bayna shayʾayni fī al-maqarri

The family thus refers to evenness, being level, and two things occupying equal or aligned positions.

He explains:

وَقَفَا صَفًّا، إِذَا وَقَفَ كُلُّ وَاحِدٍ إِلَى جَنْبِ صَاحِبِهِ

waqafā ṣaffan, idhā waqafa kullu wāḥidin ilā janbi ṣāḥibihi

Two people form a rank when each stands beside the other.

Two features matter in this definition: being side by side and having some order in extent or position.

For example, several people standing beside one another and forming a row can be said to have formed a rank.

If the same people gather at a place without arrangement, their mere gathering is not necessarily a rank.

Ṣaff therefore concerns more than gathering: the arrangement of a group’s members also matters.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: alignment and equality of position

Must a rank always be a perfectly straight line?

In the clear use of ṣaff, alignment and the arrangement of components matter.

The word itself does not establish that every rank must have perfect geometric precision, equal spacing, or a specified length.

People may form an orderly rank without every interval being exactly equal.

Ṣaff can also refer to one row or a collection of rows.

In some Arabic uses, the singular ṣaff has a collective sense: it can describe a group’s formation even when its members occupy several rows.

The number of ranks therefore requires separate evidence.

Ṣaff and arranging objects

Arabic records the expression:

صَفَفْتُ الْقَوْمَ فَاصْطَفُّوا

ṣafaftu al-qawma fa-iṣṭaffū

It means: I drew the people up in a rank, and they formed ranks.

Also:

صَفَفْتُ الْأَشْيَاءَ

ṣafaftu al-ashyāʾa

It means: I arranged the things side by side in rows.

In the first use, people are the subject of the arrangement.

In the second, inanimate objects are placed beside one another.

The root is therefore not confined to one kind of being.

Accordingly, the Qur’an uses it both for human and angelic ranks and for couches and cushions arranged in rows.

2. Other uses in early Arabic

Ṣaffa al-ṭāʾir: opening and holding the wings outspread

Arabic uses ṣaffa al-ṭāʾir when a bird opens its wings in flight and holds them outspread for a time.

Lisān al-ʿArab explains this use through spreading the wings without continuously moving them.

Human beings forming ranks are not directly at issue here.

What connects this use with the root is the extension and spread of the wings in a relatively steady position.

With birds, therefore, two conditions should be distinguished: birds flying in one row and a bird holding its wings outspread.

These conditions may occur together, but they are not identical.

In Al-Nūr 24:41 and Al-Mulk 67:19, opening and holding the wings outspread has clearer lexical and contextual support.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Miṣbāḥ al-Munīr: outspread wings

Ṣaffat al-ibil qawāʾimahā: aligning the limbs

Arabic uses ṣaffat al-ibil qawāʾimahā for camels placing their forelegs and hind legs in an aligned, orderly position.

Ṣāffah and ṣawāff can likewise describe such animals.

This use matters for Al-Ḥajj 22:36, where ṣawāffa describes animals associated with the rites of pilgrimage.

The alignment of limbs does not, however, by itself specify which leg must be bound or the precise angle at which the animal stands.

Such details, where discussed, concern the manner of performing the act, rather than the root’s independent meaning.

Ṣafūf: a she-camel yielding abundant milk

Al-Jawharī’s Al-Ṣiḥāḥ also records ṣafūf for a she-camel yielding enough milk to place two or three milking vessels side by side.

Another explanation applies ṣafūf to a camel keeping its forelegs aligned during milking.

Two related uses are thus recorded: arranging milk vessels in a row and the position of the camel’s forelegs.

These uses show the root’s place in Arab pastoral life.

Ṣafūf does not, however, establish that milking or abundant milk is the basic meaning of Ṣ F F.

Those meanings arise from the word’s particular setting.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: two explanations of the she-camel called ṣafūf

Ṣafīf: meat spread out for cooking

Arabic uses ṣafīf for meat spread out or cut into pieces in preparation for drying or roasting.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ describes arranging the meat over fire or embers to roast it.

Some lexicographers emphasize spreading and thinning the pieces; others emphasize arranging them side by side.

Ibn Fāris regards this use as less closely connected with the root’s central sense and cautions against forcing an artificial connection between every derivative and the root’s foundation.

This methodological caution matters: semantic resemblance should rest on evidence, rather than freely invented imagery.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: caution against forcing ṣafīf into the root’s foundation

A pre-Islamic poetic witness: Imruʾ al-Qays

Imruʾ al-Qays says in his Muʿallaqah:

فَظَلَّ طُهَاةُ اللَّحْمِ مِنْ بَيْنِ مُنْضِجٍ
صَفِيفَ شِوَاءٍ أَوْ قَدِيرٍ مُعَجَّلِ

fa-ẓalla ṭuhātu al-laḥmi min bayni munḍijin / ṣafīfa shiwāʾin aw qadīrin muʿajjali

Translation: “The cooks spent the time preparing the meat: some roasting spread-out slices, others quickly cooking meat in a pot.”

Ṣafīfa shiwāʾin here concerns meat prepared and spread out for roasting.

The witness shows that ṣafīf had a real culinary use in pre-Islamic Arabic.

This use must not, however, be transferred to human ranks in the Qur’an.

Nor does ṣafīf establish that all derivatives of the family concern cooking or drying things.

Gharīb al-Ḥadīth, Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, and Lisān al-ʿArab: Imruʾ al-Qays’s witness

Ṣuffah: a covered or projecting part of a building

Arabic sources record ṣuffah for part of a house or building, such as a covered space or a seating area beside the structure.

The same word also has a recorded use concerning saddles and riding equipment.

This form is assigned to the Ṣ F F family.

Ṣuffah must not, however, be confused with ṣifah, a characteristic or description.

Ṣifah in the sense of description belongs to W Ṣ F.

This distinction can be obscured in Persian, where Arabic vowel marks are usually omitted.

3. Principal morphological forms

Ṣaffa and ṣaff

Ṣaffa is a Form I verb. Different uses can mean forming ranks, drawing others into ranks, or extending limbs.

Ṣaff is a verbal noun and also the name of a row or rank.

Ṣaffan occurs seven times in the Qur’an.

The form can function adverbially or describe a condition.

Ṣāff and ṣāffah

Ṣāff is the active participle of ṣaffa.

Ṣāffah is its feminine form.

Its sound masculine plural is ṣāffūn and its sound feminine plural is ṣāffāt.

Ṣāffāt does not, however, mean a row of human beings in every sentence.

When applied to a bird, it can refer to spreading the wings.

Morphology alone therefore does not determine the contextual meaning.

Ṣawāff

Ṣawāff is a broken plural of ṣāffah.

Al-Ḥajj 22:36 uses this form for livestock.

Morphologically, ṣawāff belongs to Ṣ F F.

It must not be equated with ṣawāfī from Ṣ F W or ṣawāfin from Ṣ F N.

Maṣfūf and maṣfūfah

Maṣfūf is the Form I passive participle: something arranged in a row beside other things.

Maṣfūfah is its feminine form.

The Qur’an uses this word twice for arranged objects.

Iṣṭaffa, taṣāffa, and muṣāffah

Iṣṭaffa refers to forming ranks or taking a place in a rank.

Taṣāffa is recorded for groups forming ranks and, in some uses, for two groups taking positions opposite one another.

Muṣāffah likewise refers in the language of warfare to two groups positioned in opposing ranks.

These forms do not occur in the Qur’an, but matter for understanding the family’s range.

4. Human and angelic ranks in the Qur’an

Al-Kahf 18:48: presented in ranks

وَعُرِضُوا عَلَىٰ رَبِّكَ صَفًّا لَقَدْ جِئْتُمُونَا كَمَا خَلَقْنَاكُمْ أَوَّلَ مَرَّةٍ بَلْ زَعَمْتُمْ أَلَّنْ نَجْعَلَ لَكُمْ مَوْعِدًا

wa-ʿuriḍū ʿalā rabbika ṣaffan la-qad jiʾtumūnā ka-mā khalaqnākum awwala marratin bal zaʿamtum allan najʿala lakum mawʿidan

Translation: And they will be presented before your Lord in a row. You have indeed come to Us as We created you the first time; yet you claimed We would never appoint a meeting for you.

Ṣaffan describes the condition of those presented before God.

The preceding verse speaks of gathering everyone.

This formation therefore belongs to a context of human beings assembled and facing accountability.

Ṣaff itself, however, means neither judgment, reckoning, nor punishment.

Those concepts follow from other parts of the verses.

Nor does the singular ṣaffan determine the precise number of human rows.

What is clear is their orderly arrangement when presented.

Ṭā Hā 20:64: forming ranks for confrontation

فَأَجْمِعُوا كَيْدَكُمْ ثُمَّ ائْتُوا صَفًّا وَقَدْ أَفْلَحَ الْيَوْمَ مَنِ اسْتَعْلَىٰ

fa-ajmiʿū kaydakum thumma iʾtū ṣaffan wa-qad aflaḥa al-yawma mani istaʿlā

Translation: So bring your scheme together, then come in ranks; today whoever gains the upper hand has succeeded.

The verse belongs to Moses’ confrontation with the magicians.

Ṣaffan here refers to forming ranks or appearing as an orderly group.

Al-Ṭabarī regards it as a verbal noun conveying coming in ranks.

Some lexical explanations also use al-ṣaff for a gathering place or place of prayer.

In this confrontation, however, come in ranks is clear and natural.

This use matters because forming ranks in Arabic is not inherently a sign of being right.

Here it forms part of the plan of a group intending to confront Moses.

A group’s mere formation in ranks therefore does not establish that its moral or religious position is right.

Al-Ṭabarī: ṣaff as a verbal noun and its prayer-place use

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:1: wa-al-ṣāffāti ṣaffan

وَالصَّافَّاتِ صَفًّا

wa-al-ṣāffāti ṣaffan

Translation: By those who form ranks, in ordered ranks.

Two forms of the same root occur in this verse.

Al-ṣāffāti is the sound feminine plural of the active participle.

Ṣaffan is a verbal noun emphasizing the act of forming ranks.

Early commentaries, including Al-Ṭabarī, interpret al-ṣāffāt as angels.

This reading relates to Al-Ṣāffāt 37:164 and Al-Ṣāffāt 37:165, which speak of known stations and forming ranks.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:1 itself, however, does not explicitly name angels.

Those who form ranks therefore remains closer to the wording in direct translation. Identifying them as angels belongs in contextual explanation.

Nor does ṣāffāt itself establish that birds must be intended: its application to birds elsewhere concerns spreading wings in a different context.

Al-Ṭabarī: al-ṣāffāt interpreted as angels

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:165: al-ṣāffūn

وَإِنَّا لَنَحْنُ الصَّافُّونَ

wa-innā la-naḥnu al-ṣāffūna

Translation: And surely it is we who form ranks.

Al-ṣāffūna is the sound masculine plural of the active participle.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:164 says that each has a known station.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:166 also speaks of these same speakers glorifying God.

This continuity connects forming ranks here with known stations and glorification.

Early exegetical reports identify the speakers as angels.

Al-ṣāffūn itself, however, does not mean angels; it describes those who form ranks.

The distinction between a word’s meaning and the identification of its referent must remain.

Al-Ṭabarī: those forming ranks and glorifying

Al-Ṣaff 61:4: ranks in fighting

إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُحِبُّ الَّذِينَ يُقَاتِلُونَ فِي سَبِيلِهِ صَفًّا كَأَنَّهُمْ بُنْيَانٌ مَرْصُوصٌ

inna allāha yuḥibbu alladhīna yuqātilūna fī sabīlihi ṣaffan ka-annahum bunyānun marṣūṣun

Translation: Indeed, God loves those who fight in His way in ranks, as though they were a firm, closely joined structure.

Ṣaffan describes the condition of those engaged in fighting.

Bunyānun marṣūṣun then likens them to a firm, closely joined structure.

Marṣūṣ comes from R Ṣ Ṣ, not Ṣ F F.

Forming ranks must therefore be distinguished from the quality of firmness and close connection.

Forming ranks describes how the people are positioned.

The comparison with a closely joined building also highlights their firmness and cohesion.

Order and coordination are therefore clear in this context, but must not become necessary components of every use of the root.

Fī sabīlihi also specifies the purpose of the fighting.

Divine love in the verse does not attach merely to any rank formed in any war. The verse concerns those fighting in God’s way in a firm, orderly formation.

Nor can this verse, without examination of others, establish that every orderly war or military command is automatically legitimate and divine.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:38: the Spirit and angels in ranks

يَوْمَ يَقُومُ الرُّوحُ وَالْمَلَائِكَةُ صَفًّا لَا يَتَكَلَّمُونَ إِلَّا مَنْ أَذِنَ لَهُ الرَّحْمَٰنُ وَقَالَ صَوَابًا

yawma yaqūmu al-rūḥu wa-al-malāʾikatu ṣaffan lā yatakallamūna illā man adhina lahu al-raḥmānu wa-qāla ṣawāban

Translation: The day the Spirit and the angels stand in ranks; they do not speak, except one permitted by the All-Merciful who speaks rightly.

Ṣaffan refers to the standing position of the Spirit and angels.

The verse continues with speaking only by permission and saying what is right.

The formation here thus accompanies a situation of order and waiting.

Ṣaff itself, however, means neither obtaining permission nor remaining silent.

Those concepts follow from other expressions in the verse.

Nor does the verse specify the precise number of ranks or their spacing.

Al-Fajr 89:22: ṣaffan ṣaffan

وَجَاءَ رَبُّكَ وَالْمَلَكُ صَفًّا صَفًّا

wa-jāʾa rabbuka wa-al-malaku ṣaffan ṣaffan

Translation: And your Lord comes, and the angels, rank upon rank.

Ṣaffan is repeated twice.

The repetition emphasizes formation in multiple or successive ranks.

Rank upon rank is therefore a natural rendering close to the expression’s structure.

The repetition itself does not specify the number of ranks.

How jāʾa rabbuka is to be understood is also a separate question. Ṣ F F cannot determine the manner of the Lord’s coming.

The root clarifies al-malak’s formation in ranks, rather than the manner of divine presence or action.

5. Ṣāffāt applied to birds: outspread wings

Al-Nūr 24:41: birds with wings outspread

أَلَمْ تَرَ أَنَّ اللَّهَ يُسَبِّحُ لَهُ مَنْ فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ وَالطَّيْرُ صَافَّاتٍ كُلٌّ قَدْ عَلِمَ صَلَاتَهُ وَتَسْبِيحَهُ وَاللَّهُ عَلِيمٌ بِمَا يَفْعَلُونَ

a-lam tara anna allāha yusabbiḥu lahu man fī al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍi wa-al-ṭayru ṣāffātin kullun qad ʿalima ṣalātahu wa-tasbīḥahu wa-allāhu ʿalīmun bi-mā yafʿalūna

Translation: Have you not seen that whoever is in the heavens and earth, and the birds with wings outspread, glorify God? Each has known its prayer and glorification; and God knows what they do.

Ṣāffātin describes the birds’ condition.

In established Arabic usage, a bird is ṣāff when it opens its wings and holds them outspread.

Birds with wings outspread is therefore more suitable than birds standing in ranks.

The verse itself says nothing about birds positioned beside one another in one row.

Nor does ṣāffātin alone mean glorification or prayer.

Those concepts are expressed separately through yusabbiḥu, ṣalātahu, and tasbīḥahu.

Spreading the wings should therefore not, without evidence, be treated as the whole meaning of birds’ prayer or glorification.

The verse places the birds’ posture and glorification in one context; their precise relationship should be assessed alongside other evidence.

Al-Mulk 67:19: spreading and drawing in the wings

أَوَلَمْ يَرَوْا إِلَى الطَّيْرِ فَوْقَهُمْ صَافَّاتٍ وَيَقْبِضْنَ مَا يُمْسِكُهُنَّ إِلَّا الرَّحْمَٰنُ إِنَّهُ بِكُلِّ شَيْءٍ بَصِيرٌ

a-wa-lam yaraw ilā al-ṭayri fawqahum ṣāffātin wa-yaqbiḍna mā yumsikuhunna illā al-raḥmānu innahu bi-kulli shayʾin baṣīrun

Translation: Have they not looked at the birds above them, spreading and drawing in their wings? None holds them except the All-Merciful; indeed, He sees everything.

This verse is particularly important for understanding ṣāffāt because it places yaqbiḍna beside it.

Qabaḍa concerns taking hold and drawing together.

Al-Ṭabarī explains that the birds sometimes hold their wings outspread and sometimes draw them in.

Ṣāffātin wa-yaqbiḍna thus contrasts two wing positions: spread and drawn in.

This clearly supports outspread wings here, rather than merely several birds forming ranks beside one another.

Al-Ṭabarī: outspread and drawn-in wings

Does ṣāffāt mean that birds never flap their wings?

No.

The verse immediately mentions drawing the wings in.

Ṣāffāt must therefore not become a permanent condition in which birds never move their wings.

In actual flight, birds can have periods of spreading their wings and gliding, and at other times flap or draw in their wings.

This is consistent with the verse’s image.

Ṣāffāt itself, however, is not a complete explanation of aerodynamics, air currents, or the generation of lift.

The verse invites observation of birds and reflection on their being held aloft; the scientific details of flight are a separate subject of study.

Birds described as ṣāffāt and the opening of Al-Ṣāffāt

In Al-Nūr 24:41 and Al-Mulk 67:19, ṣāffāt describes birds and clearly concerns outspread wings.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:1, however, the same form describes a group forming ranks, usually understood as angels in early commentaries.

Identical word forms must therefore not force one fixed image upon every verse.

The same construction can express different established uses of the family in different contexts.

6. Ṣawāff: livestock in the rites of pilgrimage

Al-Ḥajj 22:36

وَالْبُدْنَ جَعَلْنَاهَا لَكُمْ مِنْ شَعَائِرِ اللَّهِ لَكُمْ فِيهَا خَيْرٌ فَاذْكُرُوا اسْمَ اللَّهِ عَلَيْهَا صَوَافَّ فَإِذَا وَجَبَتْ جُنُوبُهَا فَكُلُوا مِنْهَا وَأَطْعِمُوا الْقَانِعَ وَالْمُعْتَرَّ كَذَٰلِكَ سَخَّرْنَاهَا لَكُمْ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَشْكُرُونَ

wa-al-budna jaʿalnāhā lakum min shaʿāʾiri allāhi lakum fīhā khayrun fa-udhkurū isma allāhi ʿalayhā ṣawāffa fa-idhā wajabat junūbuhā fa-kulū minhā wa-aṭʿimū al-qāniʿa wa-al-muʿtarra ka-dhālika sakhkharnāhā lakum laʿallakum tashkurūna

Translation: And the large sacrificial animals We have made among God’s rites for you; in them is good for you. So mention God’s name over them as they stand aligned. Then, when their sides fall to the ground, eat of them and feed the contented and the one who asks. Thus We have subjected them to you, so that you may give thanks.

Ṣawāffa is a plural of ṣāffah.

Applied to livestock, it describes standing with the limbs in an orderly arrangement.

Early exegetical reports commonly explain this through camels standing during sacrifice.

Al-Ṭabarī transmits from Ibn ʿAbbās and others that a camel stands in this condition and may have one foreleg bound.

Lexical meaning must nevertheless be distinguished from practical details.

Ṣawāffa basically describes orderly, aligned standing.

The word alone does not specify which foreleg or hind leg must be bound or precisely how many limbs support the animal.

Those details occur in reports about practice and interpretation, rather than in the root itself.

Distinguishing ṣawāff, ṣawāfin, and ṣawāfī

Sources on readings and interpretation report three forms for this verse.

Ṣawāffa is the familiar reading, from Ṣ F F. It concerns standing aligned.

Ṣawāfin, found in some transmitted readings, comes from Ṣ F N. Applied to horses or camels, this family concerns a particular stance, supported on some limbs with another bent or raised.

Ṣawāfī, also transmitted in some reports, comes from Ṣ F W. It means pure ones and is explained here through dedicating the act purely to God.

Despite their similar sounds, the three forms do not share one root.

Ṣawāff must therefore not be translated directly as purely for God, because that meaning belongs to Ṣ F W.

Nor should the details associated with ṣawāfin immediately become part of ṣawāff’s morphological meaning.

Al-Ṭabarī: ṣawāff, ṣawāfin, and ṣawāfī

The role of fa-idhā wajabat junūbuhā

The verse continues:

فَإِذَا وَجَبَتْ جُنُوبُهَا

fa-idhā wajabat junūbuhā

It means: “Then, when their sides fall to the ground.”

The falling of their sides is presented after the act under discussion.

This continuity fits the reading that the animals had been standing before they fell.

Wajabat itself, however, comes from W J B and concerns falling or occurring.

The falling of their sides must not be transferred into Ṣ F F.

The verse then mentions eating the meat and feeding others. Those commands are independent of ṣawāff’s lexical meaning.

Does ṣawāff prescribe an identical practice for every kind of livestock?

The verse itself describes al-budn as ṣawāff.

Arabic usage and exegetical sources apply budn to large sacrificial animals, particularly camels.

Ṣawāff alone, however, cannot establish identical practical rulings for every kind of animal without regard to bodily differences.

How different kinds of livestock are sacrificed should be examined in an independent study of rulings and rites.

For this root study, the principal meaning remains standing aligned.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Gharīb al-Ḥadīth: animals’ limb positions and distinct roots

Study of B D N

7. Maṣfūfah: objects arranged in rows

Al-Ṭūr 52:20: couches arranged in rows

مُتَّكِئِينَ عَلَىٰ سُرُرٍ مَصْفُوفَةٍ وَزَوَّجْنَاهُمْ بِحُورٍ عِينٍ

muttakiʾīna ʿalā sururin maṣfūfatin wa-zawwajnāhum bi-ḥūrin ʿīnin

Translation: Reclining on couches arranged in rows; and We have paired them with wide-eyed companions.

Maṣfūfatin describes sururin.

Surur is the plural of sarīr and denotes couches.

Maṣfūfah shows that the couches are arranged in rows.

Some exegetical explanations relate this arrangement to the people of the Garden facing one another.

Maṣfūfah alone, however, cannot establish that the couches form a circle or any particular geometric shape.

The word indicates only their arrangement in rows and the order of that arrangement.

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:15: cushions arranged in rows

وَنَمَارِقُ مَصْفُوفَةٌ

wa-namāriqu maṣfūfatun

Translation: And cushions arranged in rows.

Maṣfūfatun describes namāriqu.

Namāriq denotes cushions or small supports for reclining.

Here neither people form ranks nor a bird spreads its wings: objects are placed beside one another.

This use displays the root’s physical, general meaning.

It also shows that an orderly arrangement does not inherently mean unity, obedience, or cooperation.

Those concepts arise only when the members of the formation can act and will, and the context warrants them.

8. Distinguishing related roots

Ṣ F F (ṣaffa) and R Ṣ Ṣ (raṣṣa)

Raṣṣa concerns pressing together, strengthening, and arranging firmly.

Ṣaffa concerns aligning and placing along an extent.

Al-Ṣaff 61:4 brings both fields together: people are ṣaffan and are likened to bunyānun marṣūṣun.

The combination shows that forming ranks and the firmness of the connection between components are related but different qualities.

Every rank therefore cannot automatically be considered a firm building.

Ṣ F F (ṣaffa) and N Ẓ M (naẓama)

Naẓama concerns ordering and placing things in a particular sequence.

Ṣaffa likewise expresses a kind of spatial order.

Order, however, may concern speech, a plan, time, or relationships that form no physical row.

Ṣaff in its principal use concerns components positioned in a row or along an extent.

Every rank can therefore have a kind of order, but not every order is a rank.

Ṣ F F (ṣaffa) and J M ʿ (jamaʿa)

Jamaʿa concerns gathering.

Ṣaffa may likewise apply to a group that has assembled.

Gathering alone, however, is not forming ranks.

People may gather in a square without standing in a definite row.

Jamʿ thus highlights number or gathering, while ṣaff highlights how the components are positioned.

Study of J M ʿ

Ṣ F F (ṣaffa) and Ṣ F W (ṣafā)

These roots may look similar in unvocalized Persian and Arabic writing, but they are distinct.

Ṣ F F concerns alignment and forming ranks.

Ṣ F W concerns purity, unmixedness, and selection.

Ṣawāff in Al-Ḥajj 22:36 belongs to the first root; ṣawāfī belongs to the second.

Muṣṭafā and iṣṭafā likewise belong to Ṣ F W, rather than Ṣ F F.

Forming ranks and being chosen therefore do not mean the same thing.

Study of Ṣ F W

Ṣ F F (ṣaffa) and Ṣ F N (ṣafana)

Applied to a horse, ṣafana refers to a particular stance, such as resting on three limbs while bending or raising the fourth.

Ṣaffa, applied to livestock, describes aligning the limbs.

The roots overlap in describing an animal’s standing posture, but differ in root letters and semantic details.

This distinction matters when examining ṣawāff and ṣawāfin in Al-Ḥajj 22:36.

Ṣ F F (ṣaffa) and Ṣ F Ṣ F

Ṭā Hā 20:106 uses ṣafṣafan for the ground.

Lexical sources explain ṣafṣaf as smooth, level ground.

Ibn Fāris relates the word to the foundation of evenness and firmness in ṣaff.

The Qur’an’s morphological index, however, records ṣafṣaf under the quadriliteral Ṣ F Ṣ F.

Despite a possible semantic relationship, it therefore must not be added to the 14 occurrences of triliteral Ṣ F F.

9. Challenges in understanding and translation

Is forming ranks always a sign of being right?

No.

Ṭā Hā 20:64 places it within the magicians’ preparations to confront Moses.

Al-Ṣaff 61:4, however, praises firm ranks in God’s way.

One outward behavior can therefore occur in two settings with different aims.

The rightness of the aim follows from context, rather than Ṣ F F itself.

Does being in ranks always mean complete unity?

No.

A rank may indicate coordination in position and movement.

Two people may nevertheless stand in one row while disagreeing on other matters.

Conversely, people may share intellectual and moral aims without standing in a physical row.

Unity and solidarity may therefore result from or accompany a rank, but they are not necessary components of its lexical meaning.

Does ṣāffāt always describe angels?

No.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:1, early commentaries commonly identify the referents as angels.

In Al-Nūr 24:41 and Al-Mulk 67:19, however, ṣāffāt describes birds.

A meaning from one verse must therefore not be transferred to another without considering the subject and context.

Does ṣāffāt applied to birds mean flying in one row?

Not necessarily.

In Al-Mulk 67:19, the pairing with yaqbiḍna indicates spreading and drawing in the wings.

The verse therefore pictures the wings’ condition, rather than the birds’ collective arrangement in the sky.

Does maṣfūfah specify a precise geometric layout?

No.

Maṣfūfah refers to being arranged in rows.

It does not determine their number, precise spacing, complete geometric shape, or direction.

Al-Ṭūr 52:20 and Al-Ghāshiyah 88:15 explicitly describe couches and cushions arranged in rows.

Is ṣawāff in the pilgrimage context the act of sacrifice itself?

No.

Ṣawāff describes the animals’ condition.

Mentioning God’s name, the falling of their sides, and eating and feeding others are expressed by separate verbs and phrases in the same verse.

All stages of the act must therefore not be included in the definition of one word.

10. Translation proposals for Forqan

In Al-Kahf 18:48, Ṭā Hā 20:64, Al-Ṣaff 61:4, and Al-Nabaʾ 78:38, ṣaffan can be translated as in ranks or standing in rows.

For Al-Ṣāffāt 37:1, a rendering close to the wording is:

“By those who form ranks, in ordered ranks.”

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:165:

“And surely it is we who form ranks.”

In Al-Fajr 89:22:

“And your Lord comes, and the angels, rank upon rank.”

In Al-Nūr 24:41 and Al-Mulk 67:19, ṣāffātin is better rendered with wings outspread or spreading their wings.

In Al-Ḥajj 22:36, ṣawāffa applied to livestock means standing in orderly formation or standing aligned.

In Al-Ṭūr 52:20 and Al-Ghāshiyah 88:15, maṣfūfah means arranged in rows or placed side by side.

These equivalents distinguish three principal uses: groups forming ranks, wings spreading, and objects arranged side by side.

11. Conclusion

The Arabic root Ṣ F F concerns alignment, being side by side, and things arranged along an extent.

Applied to human beings, ṣaff may describe a group standing or moving in orderly ranks.

Applied to birds, ṣāff can describe a bird opening its wings and holding them outspread.

Applied to livestock, the family can describe standing posture and the alignment of limbs.

Applied to objects, maṣfūf describes something arranged in a row beside other things.

The family occurs 14 times in 12 Qur’anic verses, displaying this same variety.

Ṣaff in its basic meaning nevertheless judges neither the rightness of the aim, the participants’ faith, nor the moral worth of their action.

Ṭā Hā 20:64 concerns the magicians’ confrontation with Moses; Al-Ṣaff 61:4 concerns fighting in God’s way. The angelic passages concern their stations and standing; the bird passages concern outspread wings.

Concise root definition: Ṣ F F denotes people or things positioned beside one another along an extent or row. Applied to birds and livestock, it can describe extended or aligned limbs. Brief root meaning: Aligning and arranging side by side; spreading wings or aligning limbs; maṣfūf: arranged in rows

The essential distinction is that forming ranks, spreading wings, and objects being arranged in rows are three related but different uses.

Each form’s precise meaning must follow from its subject, object, and context; the whole family must not be reduced to order, unity, or people standing in line.
