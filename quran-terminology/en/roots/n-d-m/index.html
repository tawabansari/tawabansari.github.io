Regret about the past: from recognising mistakes and grieving over consequences to distinguishing regret from repentance and repair

1. Introducing the root and its central meaning

The Arabic root «ن د م» denotes regret for something done or an opportunity lost. «نَدِمَ» describes someone who, after an action, decision, or event, feels displeasure and sorrow about it and wishes the past had turned out differently.

«نَدَم» and «نَدَامَة» are verbal nouns denoting regret. «نَادِم» describes someone who regrets, and «نَادِمِين» is its plural form.

Regret directs attention back to the past. Someone may realise that a decision was mistaken, an action harmful, an opportunity lost, or an outcome contrary to expectations.

These situations nevertheless need to be distinguished.

Someone may regret an action after recognising that it was wrong and harmed another person. Someone else may regret the same action solely because it harmed their own interests.

The word regret can apply to both, but the moral character of their regret differs.

The central meaning is therefore regret and displeasure about something in the past, not necessarily repentance, changed behaviour, or a return to what is right.

This distinction matters particularly in the Quran: regret describes both wrongdoers who still have an opportunity to respond and people regretting their past when they see punishment.

Qur’anic forms

The root occurs seven times in the Quran in two main forms.

«نَادِمِينَ» and «النَّادِمِينَ» occur five times: Al-Māʾidah 5:31 and Al-Māʾidah 5:52, Al-Muʾminūn 23:40, Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:157, and Al-Ḥujurāt 49:6.

«النَّدَامَةَ» occurs twice, at Yūnus 10:54 and Sabaʾ 34:33.

«نادم» is the active participle of the basic triliteral verb «نَدِمَ». «نادمين» is a sound masculine plural in the accusative or genitive, while «الندامة» is a definite verbal noun.

The Quran has no verb from this root such as «نَدِمَ», «يَنْدَمُ», «تَنَدَّمَ», or «أَنْدَمَ». Other attested Arabic forms, including «نَدِيم», «نَدْمَان», «مُنَادَمَة», and «مَنْدَمَة», likewise do not occur in the Quran.

All Qur’anic occurrences therefore describe either a person regretting or the state of regret itself.

2. Early Arabic uses

Nadam and nadāmah: painful reconsideration of the past

Lexical explanations treat nadam and nadāmah as synonymous and describe the nādim as someone feeling sorrow and regret about the past.

The following expression has also been used to explain the root’s central meaning:

كَلِمَةٌ تَدُلُّ عَلَى تَفَكُّنٍ لِشَيْءٍ قَدْ كَانَ

kalimatun tadullu ʿalā tafakkunin li-shayʾin qad kāna

It describes a state of distress and regret about something that has already occurred.

This highlights an important feature: regret generally concerns something that has happened or an opportunity whose time has passed.

Arabic can say:

نَدِمَ عَلَى مَا فَعَلَ

nadima ʿalā mā faʿala

“He regretted what he had done.”

Likewise:

نَدِمَ عَلَى تَرْكِهِ

nadima ʿalā tarkihi

“He regretted leaving it undone.”

Regret is therefore not confined to doing something wrong. Failing to do something later considered necessary or beneficial can also occasion regret.

This differs from an ordinary wish for the future. A person regretting looks back at something that occurred or was lost; a wish can concern something still possible.

Regret alone does not establish that someone has recognised the truth. A person may regret doing something right because its consequences conflict with their interests or desires.

The rightness or wrongness of what is regretted must therefore be assessed separately.

An Arabic proverbial witness: Al-Kusaʿī’s regret

«نَدَامَةُ الْكُسَعِيِّ», “Al-Kusaʿī’s regret,” is a well-known expression in Arabic proverbial tradition.

In the familiar story, Al-Kusaʿī worked hard to make a bow. He then shot at game on a dark night. Seeing sparks as his arrows struck rock, he thought that he had missed.

In anger and despair, he broke his bow.

The next morning he discovered that the arrows had struck the animals and reached the rock only after passing through their bodies. He then regretted breaking the bow.

Lines attributed to him say:

نَدِمْتُ نَدَامَةً لَوْ أَنَّ نَفْسِي
تُطَاوِعُنِي إِذًا لَقَطَعْتُ خَمْسِي

nadimtu nadāmatan law anna nafsī / tuṭāwiʿunī idhan la-qaṭaʿtu khamsī

تَبَيَّنَ لِي سَفَاهُ الرَّأْيِ مِنِّي
لَعَمْرُ أَبِيكَ حِينَ كَسَرْتُ قَوْسِي

tabayyana lī safāhu al-raʾyi minnī / la-ʿamru abīka ḥīna kasartu qawsī

Translation:

“I regretted it so deeply that, had my own self obeyed me, I would have cut off my fingers.

It became clear to me, by your father’s life, how foolish my judgement had been when I broke my bow.”

The story appears in Arabic collections of proverbs and literary material, making Al-Kusaʿī a familiar example of intense regret.

Its details and the lines’ wording vary, however, and the historical attribution of every line to Al-Kusaʿī himself has not been independently established with certainty.

The witness is therefore chiefly useful for understanding a familiar Arabic proverb, rather than proving every historical detail of the story.

The semantic point is that, after learning the actual outcome, Al-Kusaʿī reassessed his earlier decision.

Regret can thus arise from recognising a mistaken decision, even where religious sin or moral responsibility towards someone else is not the issue.

Al-Farazdaq: regretting a personal decision

Al-Farazdaq, a poet of the Islamic period, says of separating from his wife Nawār:

نَدِمْتُ نَدَامَةَ الْكُسَعِيِّ لَمَّا
غَدَتْ مِنِّي مُطَلَّقَةً نَوَارُ

nadimtu nadāmata al-kusaʿiyyi lammā / ghadat minnī muṭallaqatan nawāru

Translation:

“I regretted it with Al-Kusaʿī’s regret when Nawār became divorced from me.”

Regret here concerns a personal, family decision.

After the separation, the poet sees the lost relationship differently and regrets his choice.

Regret in Arabic was therefore not confined to religious faults or criminal behaviour.

Al-Farazdaq lived after the Quran’s revelation, however, so his poetry is not direct evidence for pre-Qur’anic Arabic.

It does help show the continued use of expressions of regret in Arabic language and literature.

Nadīm and munādamah: companionship in drinking

Another branch is represented by «نَدِيم» and «مُنَادَمَة».

«نَدِيم» denoted a companion at a drinking gathering; «مُنَادَمَة» concerned keeping company and participating in such gatherings.

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Burj ibn Musʹhir al-Ṭāʾī says:

وَنَدْمَانٍ يَزِيدُ الْكَأْسَ طِيبًا
سَقَيْتُ وَقَدْ تَغَوَّرَتِ النُّجُومُ

wa-nadmānin yazīdu al-kaʾsa ṭīban / saqaytu wa-qad taghawwarati al-nujūmu

Translation:

“A drinking companion who made the cup more pleasant: I served him when the stars had sunk.”

«نَدْمَان» here denotes a companion in drinking.

The poet does not necessarily mean someone regretting an action.

«نَدْمَان» in this use and «نَادِم» meaning “regretful” therefore differ despite sharing their root consonants.

Is nadīm derived from nadāmah?

Lexical tradition offers several explanations of their relationship.

One suggests that drinking companions might say or do something while intoxicated that they later regretted, leading to the name nadīm.

Another treats munādamah as a transposed form of mudāmanah, referring to continual, repeated companionship in drinking.

Both explanations have been recorded in lexical discussions, along with doubts about their correctness.

Shared letters and a plausible story do not establish a word’s historical origin.

An old relationship between nadīm and regret may exist, but the established gathering-related meaning is “companion,” not “a regretful person.”

Neither nadīm nor munādamah occurs in the Quran.

The companionship branch belongs in lexical study but should not be transferred into verses about regret.

3. The first Qur’anic use: regret after killing

Al-Māʾidah 5:31: one of Adam’s two sons

فَبَعَثَ اللَّهُ غُرَابًا يَبْحَثُ فِي الْأَرْضِ لِيُرِيَهُ كَيْفَ يُوَارِي سَوْءَةَ أَخِيهِ قَالَ يَا وَيْلَتَا أَعَجَزْتُ أَنْ أَكُونَ مِثْلَ هَٰذَا الْغُرَابِ فَأُوَارِيَ سَوْءَةَ أَخِي فَأَصْبَحَ مِنَ النَّادِمِينَ

fa-baʿatha allāhu ghurāban yabḥathu fī al-arḍi li-yuriyahu kayfa yuwārī sawʾata akhīhi qāla yā waylatā a-ʿajaztu an akūna mithla hādhā al-ghurābi fa-uwāriya sawʾata akhī fa-aṣbaḥa mina al-nādimīna

Translation:

“The God sent a crow scratching in the earth to show him how to cover his brother’s body. He said: Woe to me! Was I unable to be like this crow and cover my brother’s body? Then he became one of the regretful.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:31

The verse belongs to the story of Adam’s two sons.

The preceding verse says:

فَطَوَّعَتْ لَهُ نَفْسُهُ قَتْلَ أَخِيهِ فَقَتَلَهُ فَأَصْبَحَ مِنَ الْخَاسِرِينَ

fa-ṭawwaʿat lahu nafsuhu qatla akhīhi fa-qatalahu fa-aṣbaḥa mina al-khāsirīna

Translation:

“His own self made killing his brother easy for him; he killed him and became one of the losers.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:30

The passage first describes killing and loss, then the killer’s realisation, on seeing the crow, of his inability to cover his brother’s body.

What does he regret?

The killer’s own words explicitly concern covering his brother’s body:

أَعَجَزْتُ أَنْ أَكُونَ مِثْلَ هَٰذَا الْغُرَابِ فَأُوَارِيَ سَوْءَةَ أَخِي

a-ʿajaztu an akūna mithla hādhā al-ghurābi fa-uwāriya sawʾata akhī

He speaks of being unable to do what the crow had done.

The verse then states more generally:

فَأَصْبَحَ مِنَ النَّادِمِينَ

fa-aṣbaḥa mina al-nādimīna

“Then he became one of the regretful.”

Did he regret only his inability to cover the body, or the killing itself as well?

The narrative connects his regret with killing his brother, since the need to cover the body directly results from it.

His own statement explicitly concerns covering the body, however, and the verse does not specify how far his regret involved recognising the killing itself as wrong.

Details of his inner intention should therefore not be made more certain than the text supports.

Does his regret mean repentance?

The verse says that he regretted, but does not explicitly say that he repented, sought forgiveness from The God, or undertook corrective action for the killing.

The difference matters.

Someone can regret an action without yet properly accepting responsibility or taking steps to change and make amends.

The Quran reports the killer’s regret without explicitly presenting it as accepted repentance.

The verses themselves do not name the brothers. Their familiar names in outside narratives are therefore not necessary to explain this root.

Lexically, «النادمين» means “the regretful.” Assessing this person’s regret morally requires further contextual examination and evidence.

4. Regret after decisions and their consequences

A. Al-Māʾidah 5:52: regret over what was concealed within

فَتَرَى الَّذِينَ فِي قُلُوبِهِمْ مَرَضٌ يُسَارِعُونَ فِيهِمْ يَقُولُونَ نَخْشَىٰ أَنْ تُصِيبَنَا دَائِرَةٌ فَعَسَى اللَّهُ أَنْ يَأْتِيَ بِالْفَتْحِ أَوْ أَمْرٍ مِنْ عِنْدِهِ فَيُصْبِحُوا عَلَىٰ مَا أَسَرُّوا فِي أَنْفُسِهِمْ نَادِمِينَ

fa-tarā alladhīna fī qulūbihim maraḍun yusāriʿūna fīhim yaqūlūna nakhshā an tuṣībanā dāʾiratun fa-ʿasā allāhu an yaʾtiya bi-al-fatḥi aw amrin min ʿindihi fa-yuṣbiḥū ʿalā mā asarrū fī anfusihim nādimīna

Translation:

“You see those with sickness in their hearts hastening towards them, saying: We fear that a misfortune will strike us. It is hoped that The God will bring victory or a matter from Him, and they will come to regret what they concealed within themselves.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:52

The verse concerns people whose relationships and allegiances are shaped by fear of the future.

They fear adverse events and hasten towards groups from whom they expect possible support.

The verse places before the audience an opening or a matter from The God, followed by their regret: a development that can overturn their concealed calculations.

Regret for the behaviour or for failed calculations?

Regret can arise when circumstances change and a calculation proves mistaken.

People may take a position or form an allegiance to protect their interests, then regret it when events turn out differently.

Such regret does not necessarily involve recognising their behaviour as morally wrong.

They may merely realise that the decision did not bring the expected benefit.

«نادمين» therefore cannot be translated directly as “those who repent.”

«فَعَسَى اللَّهُ» here concerns expectation or a promise of an opening from The God and the regret following it, rather than a report that everyone had already regretted at that moment. The construction should not simply be reduced to uncertain probability or doubt about a promise’s fulfilment.

This differs from Al-Māʾidah 5:31: the killing narrative reports regret after the event, while Al-Māʾidah 5:52 places future regret following an opening and changed circumstances before the audience.

B. Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:157: Thamūd’s regret

فَعَقَرُوهَا فَأَصْبَحُوا نَادِمِينَ

fa-ʿaqarūhā fa-aṣbaḥū nādimīna

Translation:

“They hamstrung her and became regretful.”

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:157

The verse concerns Thamūd and the she-camel whom Ṣāliḥ had warned them not to harm.

The preceding verse says:

وَلَا تَمَسُّوهَا بِسُوءٍ فَيَأْخُذَكُمْ عَذَابُ يَوْمٍ عَظِيمٍ

wa-lā tamassūhā bi-sūʾin fa-yaʾkhudhakum ʿadhābu yawmin ʿaẓīmin

Translation:

“Do not touch her with harm, lest the punishment of a tremendous day seize you.”

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:156

They nevertheless harmed her and then regretted it.

The next verse says:

فَأَخَذَهُمُ الْعَذَابُ

fa-akhadhahumu al-ʿadhābu

“Then the punishment seized them.”

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:158, opening of the verse

The narrative places regret after the hamstringing and before the report of punishment arriving.

This order matters: not every Qur’anic occurrence of regret should be confined to regret after seeing punishment.

Why did Thamūd regret?

The verse connects their regret with hamstringing the she-camel.

It does not explicitly say whether they recognised Ṣāliḥ’s message as true, feared punishment, or saw other consequences.

Interpretive explanations have proposed both fear of punishment and regret for the action itself.

Neither should become the lexical meaning of nādimīn without adequate evidence.

The following narrative shows that the reported regret did not prevent their punishment.

That alone does not prove that none of them ever felt real regret. What is clear is that this passage does not report accepted repentance or changed behaviour.

The existence of regret and the acceptance of repentance are separate matters.

Their regret should neither erase their responsibility by assumption nor be denied despite the explicit report.

C. Al-Muʾminūn 23:40: predicted regret

قَالَ عَمَّا قَلِيلٍ لَيُصْبِحُنَّ نَادِمِينَ

qāla ʿammā qalīlin la-yuṣbiḥunna nādimīna

Translation:

“He said: Before long they will become regretful.”

Al-Muʾminūn 23:40

This follows the story of a messenger whom his people deny.

Earlier, leading men call the messenger a human like themselves and deny re-creation after death.

At Al-Muʾminūn 23:39, the messenger asks his Lord for help:

قَالَ رَبِّ انْصُرْنِي بِمَا كَذَّبُونِ

qāla rabbi unṣurnī bi-mā kadhdhabūni

“He said: My Lord, help me against their denial.”

Verse 40 then answers that they will soon regret.

The next verse reports punishment and their destruction.

The occurrence therefore concerns future regret among people who, while denying, do not yet fully recognise their behaviour’s consequences.

«لَيُصْبِحُنَّ» has an emphatic lām and nūn, stressing that the event will occur. Its nearness in time comes from the separate phrase «عَمَّا قَلِيلٍ».

Aṣbaḥa need not mean that regret arises precisely the following morning; it can also mean entering a new state.

“Before long they will become regretful” is therefore natural.

The Quran can present regret as the future result of a wrong course without equating it with repentance or reform.

5. Regret on seeing punishment

Both Qur’anic occurrences of the noun «الندامة» concern seeing punishment.

Their wording is closely related, sharing the phrase:

وَأَسَرُّوا النَّدَامَةَ لَمَّا رَأَوُا الْعَذَابَ

wa-asarrū al-nadāmata lammā raʾaw al-ʿadhāba

Their contexts nevertheless differ.

A. Yūnus 10:54: regret and the wish for release

وَلَوْ أَنَّ لِكُلِّ نَفْسٍ ظَلَمَتْ مَا فِي الْأَرْضِ لَافْتَدَتْ بِهِ وَأَسَرُّوا النَّدَامَةَ لَمَّا رَأَوُا الْعَذَابَ وَقُضِيَ بَيْنَهُمْ بِالْقِسْطِ وَهُمْ لَا يُظْلَمُونَ

wa-law anna li-kulli nafsin ẓalamat mā fī al-arḍi la-iftadat bihi wa-asarrū al-nadāmata lammā raʾaw al-ʿadhāba wa-quḍiya baynahum bi-al-qisṭi wa-hum lā yuẓlamūna

Translation:

“If every self that had done wrong possessed everything on earth, it would offer it as ransom. They concealed regret when they saw the punishment; judgement was passed between them with justice, and they are not wronged.”

Yūnus 10:54

Regret is directly connected with seeing punishment:

لَمَّا رَأَوُا الْعَذَابَ

lammā raʾaw al-ʿadhāba

“When they saw the punishment.”

The verse first states that a wrongdoer possessing all earthly property would give it to secure release.

Regret accompanies the disclosure of the final consequences of human behaviour.

«الندامة» does not itself mean punishment: punishment is what they see, while regret is their response.

The willingness to offer property for release comes from «لَافْتَدَتْ بِهِ», not from the meaning of regret.

«وَأَسَرُّوا النَّدَامَةَ»: concealed or revealed?

The familiar Arabic meaning of «أَسَرَّ» is to conceal.

The direct reading is therefore:

“They concealed their regret.”

Lexical and interpretive traditions also transmit another reading, taking «أسروا» here as “they revealed.”

Some lexical explanations treat it as a word capable of opposite meanings in different uses.

Other explanations concern signs of regret becoming visible on their faces.

Concealment is nevertheless the clearer, more familiar meaning and is preferred for the base translation.

The verse does not specify precisely why every person conceals regret.

Some interpretations describe misleading leaders concealing it from their followers. This can be examined in connection with leaders’ and followers’ responsibility, but should not enter the translation as the pronoun’s sole certain referent.

The preferable approach is to retain “they concealed their regret” while explaining the attested difference separately.

Study of S R R

Is regret on seeing punishment the same as repentance?

Here regret arises when punishment has become apparent.

The verse does not say that it restores their former lives, repairs their actions, or changes their outcome.

It instead concerns just judgement and responsibility for the past.

Regret can thus be real yet arise when the opportunity to repair what was done has passed.

Lexically it remains regret; its effect depends on the circumstances and timing.

B. Sabaʾ 34:33: regret among leaders and followers

وَقَالَ الَّذِينَ اسْتُضْعِفُوا لِلَّذِينَ اسْتَكْبَرُوا بَلْ مَكْرُ اللَّيْلِ وَالنَّهَارِ إِذْ تَأْمُرُونَنَا أَنْ نَكْفُرَ بِاللَّهِ وَنَجْعَلَ لَهُ أَنْدَادًا وَأَسَرُّوا النَّدَامَةَ لَمَّا رَأَوُا الْعَذَابَ وَجَعَلْنَا الْأَغْلَالَ فِي أَعْنَاقِ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا هَلْ يُجْزَوْنَ إِلَّا مَا كَانُوا يَعْمَلُونَ

wa-qāla alladhīna ustuḍʿifū li-alladhīna istakbarū bal makru al-layli wa-al-nahāri idh taʾmurūnanā an nakfura bi-allāhi wa-najʿala lahu andādan wa-asarrū al-nadāmata lammā raʾaw al-ʿadhāba wa-jaʿalnā al-aghlāla fī aʿnāqi alladhīna kafarū hal yujzawna illā mā kānū yaʿmalūna

Translation:

“Those who had been kept weak said to those who had acted arrogantly: Rather, it was your scheming by night and day, when you commanded us to disbelieve in The God and assign rivals to Him. They concealed regret when they saw the punishment. We placed shackles on the necks of those who disbelieved. Are they repaid for anything except what they used to do?”

Sabaʾ 34:33

Regret follows a conversation between two groups: people who lived under others’ influence and weakness, and people who acted arrogantly.

The preceding verses show them assigning responsibility for misguidance to each other at the reckoning.

Followers accuse leaders of scheming and commanding disbelief; leaders place responsibility for the followers’ choices and actions back on them.

Then comes «وَأَسَرُّوا النَّدَامَةَ».

Does the regret belong only to followers?

The context involves both groups in discussion, followed by a statement of regret.

The regret can therefore be understood as concerning those facing their actions’ consequences in this scene collectively.

The sentence does not explicitly confine regret to only one group.

Some interpretations assign concealing regret to leaders unwilling to let their followers know about it.

That possibility can be examined, but restricting the pronoun to leaders requires a separate contextual argument.

The base translation should retain the plural “they concealed their regret.”

Regret and shifting responsibility

This is an important example of regret in relationships of power and following.

People may trust leaders, groups, or influential figures and shape their behaviour according to their commands or persuasion.

When consequences become apparent, they may try to place all responsibility on their leaders.

Leaders, in turn, may deny their role in misleading others.

The verse ends by emphasising responsibility for actions:

هَلْ يُجْزَوْنَ إِلَّا مَا كَانُوا يَعْمَلُونَ

hal yujzawna illā mā kānū yaʿmalūna

“Are they repaid for anything except what they used to do?”

Regret alone does not eliminate responsibility for the past.

Blaming others, even where they helped mislead, does not necessarily remove a person’s own responsibility.

The responsibility of leaders and followers must nevertheless be assessed according to their actual conduct and circumstances, rather than deriving a uniform judgement about all power relationships from the word regret.

6. Al-Ḥujurāt 49:6: preventing regret before harm occurs

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِنْ جَاءَكُمْ فَاسِقٌ بِنَبَإٍ فَتَبَيَّنُوا أَنْ تُصِيبُوا قَوْمًا بِجَهَالَةٍ فَتُصْبِحُوا عَلَىٰ مَا فَعَلْتُمْ نَادِمِينَ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū in jāʾakum fāsiqun bi-nabaʾin fa-tabayyanū an tuṣībū qawman bi-jahālatin fa-tuṣbiḥū ʿalā mā faʿaltum nādimīna

Translation:

“You who have believed, if a wrongdoer brings you news, investigate and clarify it, lest you harm a people in ignorance and then regret what you have done.”

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:6

This verse occupies a distinctive place among the root’s uses.

Most verses examined concern regret after an action or after consequences become apparent.

Here the Quran warns beforehand that hasty behaviour may lead to regret.

Regret and investigating news

The verse concerns news arriving.

It then commands investigation:

فَتَبَيَّنُوا

fa-tabayyanū

The verb belongs to «ب ي ن» and concerns becoming clear or making a matter clear.

Other accepted recitations have «فَتَثَبَّتُوا», emphasising careful assessment and establishing the report’s reliability.

Both readings concern avoiding hasty action based on unreliable news.

The verse then states a possible consequence of carelessness:

أَنْ تُصِيبُوا قَوْمًا بِجَهَالَةٍ

an tuṣībū qawman bi-jahālatin

“Lest you harm a people in ignorance.”

Then:

فَتُصْبِحُوا عَلَىٰ مَا فَعَلْتُمْ نَادِمِينَ

fa-tuṣbiḥū ʿalā mā faʿaltum nādimīna

“And then regret what you have done.”

Regret is a consequence whose causes should be prevented beforehand.

Is regret here a sign of virtue?

Regret after harming others may show recognition that the behaviour was wrong.

The command’s immediate purpose, however, is not merely to produce a feeling of regret.

It calls for investigating news before acting, so that no harm occurs which later brings regret.

The verse therefore emphasises preventing wrongdoing.

A person may sincerely regret a bad decision afterwards without undoing the harm inflicted on others.

Someone might accuse another person, damage their reputation, or make a harmful decision against them on the basis of an unverified report.

When the truth emerges, the regret can be real, while restoring the reputation or repairing every consequence may have become difficult or impossible.

The contextual message therefore extends beyond a feeling: human responsibility begins before action, not only after regret.

That conclusion comes from the relationship between investigating news, preventing harm, and regret, rather than from the root’s independent meaning.

Comparison with Thamūd’s regret

At Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:157, Thamūd regrets after a harmful action.

At Al-Ḥujurāt 49:6, the audience is warned to investigate before acting so as not to incur regret.

The uses complement one another.

One shows regret after action; the other shows how to prevent regret arising from ignorant action.

Not all human regret can therefore be prevented by checking news. The verse concerns a particular situation in which action on a report can harm others.

7. Regret, repentance, and repair: related but distinct

A major challenge is treating regret and repentance as identical.

Religious discourse sometimes regards regret as a condition or sign of repentance.

The connection is morally understandable: someone feeling no dissatisfaction with their behaviour may lack motivation to leave it and repair the past.

In the Quran, however, these are different roots whose meanings must be examined independently.

Regret: a response to the past

Regret describes someone who regrets an action taken or an opportunity lost.

It may involve recognising a mistake or merely responding to an unpleasant consequence.

A wrongdoer may regret the wrongdoing itself, or may only resent the punishment or personal loss it caused.

Both can be called regret, but their moral assessment differs.

Repentance: returning from a wrong course

The Qur’anic root «ت و ب» concerns returning.

In human conduct, repentance concerns returning from error and changing direction.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:17 says:

إِنَّمَا التَّوْبَةُ عَلَى اللَّهِ لِلَّذِينَ يَعْمَلُونَ السُّوءَ بِجَهَالَةٍ ثُمَّ يَتُوبُونَ مِنْ قَرِيبٍ فَأُولَٰئِكَ يَتُوبُ اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِمْ

innamā al-tawbatu ʿalā allāhi li-alladhīna yaʿmalūna al-sūʾa bi-jahālatin thumma yatūbūna min qarībin fa-ulāʾika yatūbu allāhu ʿalayhim

Translation:

“Repentance with The God is for those who do wrong in ignorance, then return soon afterwards; to them The God turns.”

Al-Nisāʾ 4:17, part of the verse

The next verse describes people continuing in wrongdoing until death arrives, then saying they have repented.

Timing and circumstances of return therefore matter in Qur’anic judgement.

Al-Baqarah 2:160 also pairs repentance with repair and making the truth clear:

إِلَّا الَّذِينَ تَابُوا وَأَصْلَحُوا وَبَيَّنُوا فَأُولَٰئِكَ أَتُوبُ عَلَيْهِمْ

illā alladhīna tābū wa-aṣlaḥū wa-bayyanū fa-ulāʾika atūbu ʿalayhim

Translation:

“Except those who returned, made amends, and clarified; to them I turn.”

Return here accompanies corrective action and clarification of the truth.

In some wrongs, an inward feeling or declared regret is therefore insufficient; corrective behaviour matters too.

One verse should not become a mechanical formula for every kind of wrong: the repair must fit the behaviour and the right that was violated.

Is every regret repentance?

The Qur’anic uses do not support that conclusion.

Thamūd regretted, yet punishment overtook them.

Yūnus 10:54 and Sabaʾ 34:33 likewise describe regret at seeing punishment without treating that state alone as accepted return.

At Al-Māʾidah 5:31, the brother’s killer becomes regretful, but the verse does not explicitly say he repented.

Regret and repentance can be connected without being synonymous.

Regret can begin reconsideration; repentance concerns returning from a wrong course; repair, where required, concerns changed behaviour and addressing the consequences of wrongdoing.

The distinction must remain in translation.

«نادمين» means “regretful people,” not “those who repent.”

«الندامة» means “regret,” not “repentance.”

8. Related roots

N D M and Ḥ S R: regret and sorrow over loss

Qur’anic «ح س ر» concerns exhaustion, depletion, and, in some forms, grief and sorrow over loss.

«حَسْرَة» can describe deep sorrow over something lost or a consequence no longer changeable.

The Quran says:

يَا حَسْرَتَىٰ عَلَىٰ مَا فَرَّطْتُ فِي جَنْبِ اللَّهِ

yā ḥasratā ʿalā mā farraṭtu fī janbi allāhi

Translation:

“Alas for my neglect of what is due to The God.”

Al-Zumar 39:56, part of the verse

The sorrow is connected with past neglect.

It comes close to regret, since both look back to the past and its outcome.

This does not make ḥasrah always more intense than regret, or regret always the first stage and ḥasrah the final one.

The uses do not establish such a fixed sequence.

The general difference in emphasis is that nadāmah more directly concerns regretting past decisions or behaviour, while ḥasrah can foreground sorrow over a lost opportunity or outcome.

The two can occur together.

Study of Ḥ S R

N D M and ʾ S F: regret and sorrow

«أ س ف» concerns grief and sorrow and sometimes anger accompanied by sorrow.

The Quran says:

وَتَوَلَّىٰ عَنْهُمْ وَقَالَ يَا أَسَفَىٰ عَلَىٰ يُوسُفَ

wa-tawallā ʿanhum wa-qāla yā asafā ʿalā yūsufa

Translation:

“He turned away from them and said: Alas for Yūsuf!”

Yūsuf 12:84, part of the verse

The sorrow concerns Yaʿqūb’s separation from Yūsuf.

It does not necessarily mean that Yaʿqūb regretted a wrong decision of his own.

Regret, by contrast, usually has a clearer connection with reassessing an action or decision.

Asaf can nevertheless accompany regret in some Arabic uses, and the boundary is not absolute.

“Sorrow” may therefore fit some sentences, but “regret” more precisely conveys the root’s Qur’anic connection with a person’s past decisions or behaviour.

Study of ʾ S F

N D M and L W M: regret and blame

«ل و م» concerns blaming or reproaching.

A person may blame someone else or reproach themselves for their behaviour.

The Quran says:

وَلَا أُقْسِمُ بِالنَّفْسِ اللَّوَّامَةِ

wa-lā uqsimu bi-al-nafsi al-lawwāmati

Translation:

“And I swear by the frequently reproaching self.”

Al-Qiyāmah 75:2

«لوامة» describes repeated reproach.

Self-reproach can connect with regret: someone regretting may blame themselves for past behaviour.

The concepts are nevertheless different.

Someone may reproach themselves without yet deciding to change. Another may regret an outcome but refuse responsibility and blame others.

Blame is a reproachful action or judgement; regret is a state of dissatisfaction about the past.

No fixed psychological sequence follows in which every regret must come after blame or every blame before repentance.

N D M and T W B: regret and return

Repentance is the closest moral comparison with regret.

A nādim regrets the past.

A tāʾib turns back from a course.

Regret can motivate that return, but the root «ن د م» does not itself denote carrying it out.

The Quran does not call everyone described as regretful a person who repents.

The roots should therefore not replace one another in translation.

Study of T W B

N D M and N W Ḥ: regret and mourning

Arabic lamentation and wailing can concern grief and bereavement.

Mourning and regret, however, are different states.

Someone may grieve a loved one’s death without regretting their own action.

Conversely, someone may regret a bad decision without crying or mourning.

Outward responses such as tears, lamentation, or wailing are not fixed components of regret’s meaning.

Qur’anic regret is sometimes concealed, while nādimīn describes a person’s state. Neither form inherently requires crying or wailing.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

The Persian pashīmānī, “regret,” is the most natural, precise equivalent.

«نَدِمَ» can be rendered “he regretted,” and «نَدَامَة» as “regret.”

«نَادِم» means “regretful,” and «نَادِمِين» means “regretful people.”

Expressions such as “felt sorrow” or “lamented” may fit some contexts, but should not reduce the central meaning to general grief.

The following renderings suit the Qur’anic occurrences:

VerseQur’anic expressionProposed rendering
Al-Māʾidah 5:31فَأَصْبَحَ مِنَ النَّادِمِينَThen he became one of the regretful
Al-Māʾidah 5:52فَيُصْبِحُوا عَلَىٰ مَا أَسَرُّوا فِي أَنْفُسِهِمْ نَادِمِينَAnd they will regret what they concealed within themselves
Yūnus 10:54وَأَسَرُّوا النَّدَامَةَThey concealed regret
Al-Muʾminūn 23:40لَيُصْبِحُنَّ نَادِمِينَThey will soon become regretful
Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:157فَأَصْبَحُوا نَادِمِينَThey became regretful
Sabaʾ 34:33وَأَسَرُّوا النَّدَامَةَThey concealed regret
Al-Ḥujurāt 49:6فَتُصْبِحُوا عَلَىٰ مَا فَعَلْتُمْ نَادِمِينَAnd then regret what you have done

Aṣbaḥa and its other forms are not mechanically translated “reached morning,” since these constructions can describe entering a new state.

At Yūnus and Sabaʾ, «الندامة» is rendered “regret,” not “punishment,” “repentance,” or “disgrace.”

The lexical disagreement about «أَسَرُّوا» can be explained, while “concealed” remains preferable for the base translation.

At Al-Ḥujurāt 49:6, regret remains a possible consequence of ignorant action. The command concerns investigating news; regret must not itself become investigation or clarification.

At Al-Māʾidah 5:31, “became one of the regretful” is more accurate than “repented,” since that is the word the Quran uses.

At Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:157, “became regretful” is sufficient. Adding “sincerely repented” or “regretted only from fear of punishment,” without adequate evidence, replaces the wording with an interpretation.

For non-Qur’anic forms, nadīm denotes a companion or drinking companion, and munādamah companionship at drinking gatherings. These uses should remain distinct from regret.

10. Conclusion

The Arabic root «ن د م» denotes regret about the past. A regretful person is dissatisfied with an action, decision, or lost opportunity and may wish that things had happened differently.

Regret can arise from recognising wrongdoing, harm to others, lost advantage, or an adverse consequence.

It does not inherently guarantee recognition of truth, acceptance of moral responsibility, or corrective behaviour.

Another Arabic branch concerns nadīm and munādamah, companionship at drinking gatherings. Its historical relationship with regret is disputed; a possible story should not become a certain etymology.

The root occurs seven times in the Quran: five instances of the participial description nādimīn and two of the noun nadāmah.

They present several different situations.

At Al-Māʾidah 5:31, regret follows killing a brother and seeing the crow.

At Al-Māʾidah 5:52, regret follows an opening and changed circumstances that frustrate concealed calculations and allegiances.

At Al-Muʾminūn 23:40, the future regret of a group of deniers is foretold.

At Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:157, Thamūd regrets after hamstringing the she-camel, but punishment follows.

At Yūnus 10:54 and Sabaʾ 34:33, regret arises on seeing punishment and is expressed by «وَأَسَرُّوا النَّدَامَةَ».

At Al-Ḥujurāt 49:6, the Quran warns before harm occurs that action on an unexamined report can lead to regret.

This last occurrence differs significantly: it aims not merely to describe regret but to prevent behaviour that may produce it.

Together the verses show that regret can follow recognition of wrongdoing or its consequences, while its moral value and outcome depend on what is regretted, when regret arises, and how the person responds afterwards.

Regret also connects with repentance and repair without being identical to either.

Regret looks back to the past; repentance concerns returning from a wrong course; repair, where required, concerns changed behaviour and addressing harm.

A proposed final definition for Forqan

«ن د م» means regret and dissatisfaction about an action taken, a past decision, or an opportunity lost. It may follow recognition of wrongdoing or an unpleasant consequence. The root itself does not determine regret’s moral cause or the person’s action to repair the past.

In the Quran, «نَادِمِين» means “regretful people” and «النَّدَامَة» means “regret.”

Regret can prepare the way for return and repair, but does not alone mean accepted repentance, making amends, or release from responsibility.

In another Arabic branch, nadīm denotes a companion or drinking companion, a use distinct from Qur’anic regret.

Regret about the past is therefore the shared central meaning of all Qur’anic occurrences. Constructive regret, regret about unwanted consequences, and belated regret must be distinguished from each verse’s context.
