Making, crafting, and producing the result of work: from crafts and tools to nurturing people, planning, and conduct

1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

The Arabic root Ṣ N ʿ concerns doing work, making something, and producing a result through work and attention. In familiar usage, a maker uses knowledge, skill, or planning to construct something or accomplish a task.

The family extends beyond material objects. Building a ship, making armor, raising a horse, nurturing a person, arranging a deception, and social conduct can all be expressed through its derivatives.

Early usage also connects the family with skill and fine workmanship. The Qur’an nevertheless applies the same root to wrong and harmful deeds. Skill or purposefulness therefore does not establish moral rightness.

Ṣ N ʿ occurs twenty times in nineteen Qur’anic verses, in five groups:

  • صَنَعَ، صَنَعُوا، يَصْنَعُ، يَصْنَعُونَ، تَصْنَعُونَ، اِصْنَعْ (ṣanaʿa, ṣanaʿū, yaṣnaʿu, yaṣnaʿūna, taṣnaʿūna, iṣnaʿ): making, doing, or producing work; fifteen Form I occurrences overall.
  • وَاصْطَنَعْتُكَ (wa-iṣṭanaʿtuka): choosing and nurturing for a special purpose; Ṭā Hā 20:41.
  • صُنْع / صُنْعًا (ṣunʿ / ṣunʿan): making, workmanship, or the result of work; Al-Kahf 18:104 and Al-Naml 27:88.
  • صَنْعَة (ṣanʿah): craft and skill in making; Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:80.
  • مَصَانِع (maṣāniʿ): buildings and constructed structures; Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:129.

Arabic also has ṣāniʿ, maṣnūʿ, ṣināʿah, ṣanīʿah, istiṣnāʿ, taṣannuʿ, and muṣānaʿah, but these particular forms do not occur in the Qur’anic text.

2. The semantic core and early Arabic usage

Making and doing work

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris describes Ṣ N ʿ through ʿamalu al-shayʾi ṣunʿan: doing or making something.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise explains ṣanaʿahu through ʿamilahu.

This establishes the general field: someone making something works upon it and produces a result. Familiar ṣunʿ nevertheless often accompanies attention, planning, or skill.

A carpenter makes a door or table from wood, a smith makes metal tools, and a weaver arranges threads into cloth.

Such work is more than bodily movement: it shapes something or brings about a result.

This should not become an absolute rule requiring every ṣunʿ to produce a new object. The Qur’an also applies it to human behavior, arrangements, and deeds.

Skill and craftsmanship

Arabic uses rajulun ṣanaʿun and imraʾatun ṣanāʿun for a man or woman skilled in a task.

Ṣanaʿu al-yadi or ṣanāʿu al-yadi likewise describes someone capable and skilled in manual work.

Lexicons record women skilled in sewing, piercing leather, making tools, and preparing household equipment.

Ṣināʿah also names an occupation or activity mastered through learning and practice.

Arabic ṣināʿah therefore was not restricted to factories or modern industrial production. Carpentry, metalworking, weaving, leatherwork, and other manual crafts were familiar examples.

Modern Persian ṣanʿat also has broader uses encompassing manufacturing, machinery, factories, and advanced technology.

That later extension should not be imposed on Qur’anic verses. Each context determines whether the word concerns making something, occupational skill, or another action.

Skilled work without guaranteed goodness

A witness transmitted in Maqāyīs al-Lughah reads:

خَرْقَاءُ بِالْخَيْرِ لَا تَهْدِي لِوِجْهَتِهِ
وَهْيَ صَنَاعُ الْأَذَى فِي الْأَهْلِ وَالْجَارِ

kharqāʾu bi-al-khayri lā tahdī li-wijhatihi / wa-hiya ṣanāʿu al-adhā fī al-ahli wa-al-jāri

Translation:

“Clumsy in doing good, unable to find its way, yet highly skilled in harming family and neighbors.”

Ṣanāʿ here expresses skill in causing harm.

Ability and skill therefore need not have positive moral value. Someone may skillfully make something useful or use the same planning and ability to harm others.

Early lexicons transmit the witness, but the consulted citations do not establish its authorship and date sufficiently to declare it certainly pre-Islamic.

Its linguistic point matters: ṣunʿ and related skill words can describe an objectionable activity.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: the witness concerning skill in harming others

Ṣanīʿah: doing good and caring for another

Arabic also records ṣanīʿah for a good deed, kindness, or service performed for someone.

Ṣanaʿa ilayhi maʿrūfan means doing someone a kindness.

Elsewhere fulānun ṣanīʿatu fulānin describes someone another person has nurtured, supported, or helped attain a position.

Making here does not mean carving or producing a body. It concerns care and conditions enabling a person to grow or prepare for responsibility.

This matters for understanding wa-iṣṭanaʿtuka li-nafsī in Moses’ story.

Ṣanīʿah’s positive sense of kindness should nevertheless not be transferred to every derivative. General ṣunʿ can concern good or bad conduct.

Nurturing and preparing

Arabic sources use ṣanaʿa farasahu for caring for, feeding, keeping, and preparing a horse.

A horse raised with good care can be called farasun ṣanīʿun.

A line attributed to ʿAdī ibn Zayd concerns a horse’s care and upbringing:

فَنَقَلْنَا صَنْعَهُ حَتَّى شَتَا
نَاعِمَ الْبَالِ لَجُوجًا فِي السَّنَنِ

fa-naqalnā ṣanʿahu ḥattā shatā / nāʿima al-bāli lajūjan fī al-sanani

A rendering close to the sense:

“We continued his care until he wintered at ease, vigorous and persistent in his course.”

Ṣanʿahu here concerns nurturing and keeping rather than constructing the horse’s body from materials.

ʿAdī ibn Zayd belongs to the period close to Islam’s emergence, but this particular line’s date is not independently established.

Ṣ N ʿ can therefore concern sustained attention to a living creature, resulting in growth, readiness, or an improved condition.

Lisān al-ʿArab: ʿAdī ibn Zayd’s witness concerning care for a horse

Building and providing water

Arabic maṣnaʿah is recorded for a basin, reservoir, water-storage structure, and certain buildings.

Its plural is maṣāniʿ.

Lexicons sometimes relate it to reservoirs built to collect and store rainwater, and also to buildings, fortifications, and palaces.

Labīd ibn Rabīʿah says:

بَلِينَا وَمَا تَبْلَى النُّجُومُ الطَّوَالِعُ
وَتَبْقَى الدِّيَارُ بَعْدَنَا وَالْمَصَانِعُ

balīnā wa-mā tablā al-nujūmu al-ṭawāliʿu / wa-tabqā al-diyāru baʿdanā wa-al-maṣāniʿu

Translation:

“We wear away, while the rising stars do not; homes and structures remain after us.”

Maṣāniʿ here denotes enduring buildings or structures.

Labīd lived through both the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. Without dating this particular line, it should not be declared certainly pre-Islamic.

The witness establishes maṣāniʿ as a familiar name for human constructions, unrestricted to modern factories.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Lisān al-ʿArab, and Asās al-Balāghah: making, nurturing, and Labīd’s maṣāniʿ witness

3. Building tools and structures in the Qur’an

Noah’s ship

Hūd says:

وَاصْنَعِ الْفُلْكَ بِأَعْيُنِنَا وَوَحْيِنَا

wa-iṣnaʿi al-fulka bi-aʿyuninā wa-waḥyinā

“Build the ship under Our observation and through Our revelation.”

Hūd 11:37

Wa-iṣnaʿi is a Form I imperative commanding construction of the ship.

Its direct object is al-fulk: the ship.

Making here is clearly physical. Noah is commanded to prepare something that will provide rescue in the coming situation.

Bi-aʿyuninā wa-waḥyinā also relates the construction to divine observation and revelation.

It should not imply God supervises construction with human tools or bodily organs. The verse concerns divine guidance and observation rather than a bodily manner of divine action.

The next verse says:

وَيَصْنَعُ الْفُلْكَ وَكُلَّمَا مَرَّ عَلَيْهِ مَلَأٌ مِنْ قَوْمِهِ سَخِرُوا مِنْهُ

wa-yaṣnaʿu al-fulka wa-kullamā marra ʿalayhi malaʾun min qawmihi sakhirū minhu

“He was building the ship, and whenever leaders of his people passed him, they mocked him.”

Hūd 11:38

Yaṣnaʿu now describes the work being carried out.

The first verse commands construction; the second reports it.

The command also appears in Al-Muʾminūn 23:27:

أَنِ اصْنَعِ الْفُلْكَ بِأَعْيُنِنَا وَوَحْيِنَا

ani iṣnaʿi al-fulka bi-aʿyuninā wa-waḥyinā

“Build the ship under Our observation and through Our revelation.”

All three uses concern actual, practical construction.

Ṣ N ʿ itself nevertheless specifies neither shipbuilding methods, wood, dimensions, nor engineering design. Such details require independent evidence.

David’s armor-making craft

Al-Anbiyāʾ says:

وَعَلَّمْنَاهُ صَنْعَةَ لَبُوسٍ لَكُمْ لِتُحْصِنَكُمْ مِنْ بَأْسِكُمْ فَهَلْ أَنْتُمْ شَاكِرُونَ

wa-ʿallamnāhu ṣanʿata labūsin lakum li-tuḥṣinakum min baʾsikum fa-hal antum shākirūna

“We taught him the making of a covering for you, to protect you against the harm of your warfare. Will you then be grateful?”

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:80

Ṣanʿah concerns the craft and skill of making.

Its object, labūs, denotes protective battle clothing in context.

Sabaʾ also says of David:

أَنِ اعْمَلْ سَابِغَاتٍ وَقَدِّرْ فِي السَّرْدِ

ani iʿmal sābighātin wa-qaddir fī al-sardi

“Make ample coats of armor and keep due measure in linking the rings.”

Sabaʾ 34:11

Protective equipment thus involves skill, measurement, and joining components.

Ṣanʿah here is more than a movement: it concerns practical knowledge that can be taught and used to make something beneficial.

Ṣanʿah alone nevertheless does not mean armor. Armor follows from labūs and the verses’ context.

Study of L B S

Its making here protects people, but Ṣ N ʿ itself means neither defense nor warfare.

Maṣāniʿ: ʿĀd’s enduring structures

Al-Shuʿarāʾ says:

وَتَتَّخِذُونَ مَصَانِعَ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَخْلُدُونَ

wa-tattakhidhūna maṣāniʿa laʿallakum takhludūna

“You erect structures, as though hoping to remain forever.”

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:129

Maṣāniʿ is the plural of maṣnaʿah.

Exegetical sources offer two principal explanations: large buildings, palaces, and strong fortifications; or reservoirs and structures for collecting and storing water.

Both are established Arabic uses. Without evidence, the verse cannot therefore be restricted to manufacturing factories or one particular building type.

The preceding verse says:

أَتَبْنُونَ بِكُلِّ رِيعٍ آيَةً تَعْبَثُونَ

a-tabnūna bi-kulli rīʿin āyatan taʿbathūna

“Do you build a sign on every height, acting without purpose?”

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:128

The following verse says:

وَإِذَا بَطَشْتُمْ بَطَشْتُمْ جَبَّارِينَ

wa-idhā baṭashtum baṭashtum jabbārīna

“When you exert force, you do so tyrannically.”

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:130

The context places ʿĀd’s building alongside purposelessness, an imagined permanence, and tyrannical conduct.

The condemnation should not extend to construction, engineering, or water storage as such. Shelter and water provision can benefit people.

The concern is a people using power and resources with a rebellious orientation.

Maṣāniʿ names their structures rather than an inherently objectionable moral act.

Lisān al-ʿArab: maṣāniʿ as buildings and water-storage structures

4. Ṣunʿ in nurturing and choosing Moses

Wa-li-tuṣnaʿa ʿalā ʿaynī

In Ṭā Hā, God recalls events in Moses’ early life:

وَأَلْقَيْتُ عَلَيْكَ مَحَبَّةً مِنِّي وَلِتُصْنَعَ عَلَىٰ عَيْنِي

wa-alqaytu ʿalayka maḥabbatan minnī wa-li-tuṣnaʿa ʿalā ʿaynī

“I cast upon you love from Me, so that you might be nurtured under My observation.”

Ṭā Hā 20:39

Li-tuṣnaʿa is a Form I passive imperfect verb.

It does not concern making an implement or inanimate object. Moses is addressed amid protection, return to his mother, upbringing, and life events.

Ṣunʿ therefore concerns a person’s nurturing and preparation here.

Early Arabic also expressed care for horses and nurturing people with this root.

ʿAlā ʿaynī conveys special divine care and observation in context, without establishing a bodily or spatial manner of God’s observation.

Study of ʿ Y N

Wa-iṣṭanaʿtuka li-nafsī

Two verses later:

وَاصْطَنَعْتُكَ لِنَفْسِي

wa-iṣṭanaʿtuka li-nafsī

“I nurtured you for Myself.”

Ṭā Hā 20:41

Iṣṭanaʿtuka is Form VIII.

Arabic iṣṭanaʿa fulānan li-nafsihi can describe bringing someone close, choosing them for a special task, or preparing them for responsibility through care and upbringing.

This connects with ṣanīʿah for someone under another’s support and nurturing.

In Moses’ story, wa-iṣṭanaʿtuka li-nafsī follows recollections of rescue from the river, return to his mother, relief from distress, trials, and residence in Midian.

The next verse commands Moses and his brother to go with divine signs; they are then sent to Pharaoh.

Moses’ nurturing and selection therefore connect with a particular responsibility.

Form VIII alone, however, does not make iṣṭināʿ invariably mean choosing a prophet or conferring spiritual rank.

Those features follow from the addressee, li-nafsī, and Moses’ wider story.

Does making a person here concern their physical body?

Li-tuṣnaʿa ʿalā ʿaynī and iṣṭanaʿtuka li-nafsī address a living person.

Ṣ N ʿ therefore extends beyond making material objects.

Nurturing and preparing a person can belong to this family.

These verses should nevertheless not imply that people are made and used like tools without will or choice.

Moses’ story itself includes experience, decisions, fear, choices, speech, and responsibility. Divine guidance and nurturing here do not alone negate those qualities.

Iṣṭināʿ here also does not mean the initial creation of Moses’ body: it concerns preparation and nurturing during his life.

Al-Ṭabarī: choosing Moses for a mission and responsibility

5. Ṣunʿ in human conduct: from deception to responsibility

The magicians’ deception

Ṭā Hā describes Moses facing the magicians:

وَأَلْقِ مَا فِي يَمِينِكَ تَلْقَفْ مَا صَنَعُوا إِنَّمَا صَنَعُوا كَيْدُ سَاحِرٍ

wa-alqi mā fī yamīnika talqaf mā ṣanaʿū innamā ṣanaʿū kaydu sāḥirin

“Cast what is in your right hand: it will swallow what they have made. What they have made is only a magician’s trick.”

Ṭā Hā 20:69

Ṣanaʿū occurs twice in this verse.

First it identifies what the magicians have produced, then identifies its nature as kaydu sāḥirin.

This matters for the root’s meaning.

Ṣunʿ can concern a skilled, arranged activity with a result, while that skill serves deception.

Defining ṣunʿ as making morally good things or doing what is right therefore does not fit the verse.

The magicians’ possible technical skill does not establish their work’s rightness.

Those who think they are doing good work

Al-Kahf says:

الَّذِينَ ضَلَّ سَعْيُهُمْ فِي الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا وَهُمْ يَحْسَبُونَ أَنَّهُمْ يُحْسِنُونَ صُنْعًا

alladhīna ḍalla saʿyuhum fī al-ḥayāti al-dunyā wa-hum yaḥsabūna annahum yuḥsinūna ṣunʿan

“Those whose striving in worldly life has gone astray, while they think they are doing good work.”

Al-Kahf 18:104

Ṣunʿan concerns their work and its result.

Yuḥsinūna is a separate verb expressing doing well.

If ṣunʿ already invariably meant doing work well, yuḥsinūna’s role would be weakened.

The verse also distinguishes a person’s assessment of their work from its actual result.

Someone may think they are doing right while their effort goes astray.

The verse alone does not assign every person in error one intention. Some may knowingly follow a wrong course; others may mistakenly consider their conduct good.

Lexically, however, it clearly concerns the work and result: good or bad does not follow from the root alone.

Conduct that is condemned

Al-Māʾidah 5:63 says:

لَبِئْسَ مَا كَانُوا يَصْنَعُونَ

la-biʾsa mā kānū yaṣnaʿūna

“How bad was what they were doing.”

The verse initially criticizes learned leaders who fail to restrain people from sinful speech and illicit gain.

Its final yaṣnaʿūna therefore belongs to condemnation of conduct and neglected responsibility.

Al-Māʾidah 5:14 describes people forgetting part of their reminder, then says God will inform them of what they were doing.

Al-Naḥl 16:112 also places a town’s conduct alongside ingratitude for blessings and its social consequences.

Al-Raʿd 13:31 describes calamity striking disbelievers bi-mā ṣanaʿū: because of what they have done.

Qur’anic ṣunʿ therefore applies to social and moral conduct as well as physical manufacture.

Al-Nūr 24:30, Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:45, and Fāṭir 35:8 likewise concern God’s knowledge of human actions.

What they manufacture could wrongly restrict such passages to physical objects. What they do or their conduct is more natural.

Work’s result and responsibility

Hūd 11:16 says:

وَحَبِطَ مَا صَنَعُوا فِيهَا وَبَاطِلٌ مَا كَانُوا يَعْمَلُونَ

wa-ḥabiṭa mā ṣanaʿū fīhā wa-bāṭilun mā kānū yaʿmalūna

“What they have done in it has come to nothing, and what they used to do is futile.”

Ṣanaʿū and yaʿmalūna occur together here.

The families therefore overlap in speaking of human conduct and its results.

This one occurrence should not establish an absolute rule that ṣunʿ always concerns results and ʿamal always process.

Both describe human behavior, and their exact differences require the complete Qur’anic usage.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:137 also describes the destruction of what Pharaoh and his people were making and raising.

This may include their constructions and established systems, but ṣunʿ itself does not mean oppression or Pharaonic conduct without contextual evidence.

6. Ṣunʿa Allāh: divine making and ordering

Al-Naml says:

وَتَرَى الْجِبَالَ تَحْسَبُهَا جَامِدَةً وَهِيَ تَمُرُّ مَرَّ السَّحَابِ صُنْعَ اللَّهِ الَّذِي أَتْقَنَ كُلَّ شَيْءٍ إِنَّهُ خَبِيرٌ بِمَا تَفْعَلُونَ

wa-tarā al-jibāla taḥsabuhā jāmidatan wa-hiya tamurru marra al-saḥābi ṣunʿa allāhi alladhī atqana kulla shayʾin innahu khabīrun bi-mā tafʿalūna

“You see the mountains and think them motionless, while they pass like clouds: the work of The God, who has made everything firm. He is aware of what you do.”

Al-Naml 27:88

Ṣunʿa Allāh concerns divine action and workmanship.

Ṣunʿa is accusative in the familiar reading. A common grammatical analysis treats it as an accusative verbal noun explaining or emphasizing the preceding action: what has been described belongs to God’s making.

The verse continues:

الَّذِي أَتْقَنَ كُلَّ شَيْءٍ (alladhī atqana kulla shayʾin)

Who has made everything firm.

Atqana belongs to another root and expresses making firm and sound.

Firmness and itqān are explicit here, but itqān’s entire meaning should not become an invariant component of Ṣ N ʿ.

Divine making and the mountains’ movement

The verse describes mountains thought motionless that nevertheless move like clouds.

Early exegesis generally relates this to resurrection and the mountains’ transformation. The nearby context mentions the trumpet being blown and creatures’ alarm.

Linguistically, the image of motion where stillness is perceived can also prompt reflection on movements within nature.

Assessing these readings requires the whole construction and context, other mountain-movement verses, and independent evidence.

Ṣ N ʿ itself specifies neither speed, physical cause, nor the movement’s time.

Ṣunʿa Allāh alone therefore should not yield a scientific theory of Earth’s movement or cosmic mechanisms. Conversely, a natural or figurative reading should not be dismissed simply for differing from early exegesis.

Divine and human making

Ṣunʿ is attributed to both God and people.

Human making may depend on skill, materials, tools, time, teaching, and experience.

Noah builds a ship, and David learns the skill of making protective equipment.

The same root applied to God does not establish divine action as dependent on tools, prepared materials, or a human craftsperson’s bodily limitations.

A shared word does not establish identical modes of action or agents’ abilities.

Ṣunʿa Allāh also does not make every human product or action morally approved by God.

An action’s existence and possibility within creation should be distinguished from the moral value of human decisions and conduct.

Al-Ṭabarī: firm workmanship, moving mountains, and resurrection context

7. Comparing related roots

Ṣ N ʿ (ṣanaʿa) and ʿ M L (ʿamila)

Qur’anic ʿamal covers a broad range of work and good or bad deeds.

Ṣunʿ likewise covers good or bad actions, while familiar uses connect more closely with making, crafting, and producing a result.

Hūd 11:16 places both together:

مَا صَنَعُوا (mā ṣanaʿū) and مَا كَانُوا يَعْمَلُونَ (mā kānū yaʿmalūna).

Sabaʾ 34:11 also uses iʿmal for making armor, while Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:80 describes the skill as ṣanʿata labūsin.

Making an object can therefore be both ʿamal and ṣunʿ.

ʿAmal cannot be excluded from craftsmanship, nor ṣunʿ restricted to producing objects.

The difference is a tendency, understood through each verse’s construction and context.

Study of ʿ M L

Ṣ N ʿ (ṣanaʿa) and F ʿ L (faʿala)

Fiʿl is among the broadest Arabic roots for something happening or being done.

The Qur’an uses the family for many kinds of action.

Ṣunʿ also concerns doing something, while familiar uses give greater prominence to making or attending to a task.

Al-Naml 27:88 contains both families:

صُنْعَ اللَّهِ (ṣunʿa allāhi) and بِمَا تَفْعَلُونَ (bi-mā tafʿalūna).

The verse first concerns divine making, then human actions.

This distinction in use does not reserve ṣunʿ for God and fiʿl for humans. Many verses also attribute ṣunʿ to people.

Fiʿl applied to a natural or unintended event likewise does not make every use devoid of intention or awareness.

Study of F ʿ L

Ṣ N ʿ (ṣanaʿa) and KH L Q (khalaqa)

Qur’anic khalaqa concerns creating beings and things. Other Arabic uses also relate the family to measuring and determining shape.

Ṣunʿ concerns making, crafting, and accomplishing work.

Human making often uses existing materials and resources, as with ships or armor.

Divine khalaqa expresses the creation of beings and the order of existence.

This should nevertheless not become an absolute rule that khalaqa always means creation from nothing and ṣunʿ always manufacture from prior materials.

Applying such a restriction everywhere requires independent evidence.

The Qur’an also attributes khalaqa to someone other than God in certain contexts, such as تَخْلُقُ مِنَ الطِّينِ كَهَيْئَةِ الطَّيْرِ (takhluqu mina al-ṭīni ka-hayʾati al-ṭayri) in Al-Māʾidah 5:110.

The roots’ difference should therefore follow their combined uses rather than a predetermined philosophical definition.

Study of KH L Q

Ṣ N ʿ (ṣanaʿa) and T Q N (atqana)

Itqān concerns making firm, sound, and carefully formed.

Al-Naml 27:88 combines divine ṣunʿ with itqān.

Making and making firm can therefore accompany one another without being identical expressions.

A work may be technically skilled and carefully structured yet morally wrong, as in ṣanaʿū kaydu sāḥirin.

Skill, firmness, and moral value should remain distinct.

Study of T Q N

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

Ṣanaʿa: made, did, or produced, according to object and context.

Yaṣnaʿu: makes or does.

Yaṣnaʿūna: make or do; in social conduct, what they do.

Iṣnaʿi: build or make.

Ṣunʿ: making, workmanship, product, or conduct.

Ṣunʿan: work and its result; in Al-Kahf 18:104, doing work well within yuḥsinūna ṣunʿan.

Ṣanʿah: craft, technique, and manufacturing skill.

Maṣāniʿ: buildings or constructed structures; in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:129, large or strong structures.

Li-tuṣnaʿa: so that you may be nurtured or prepared for a purpose.

Iṣṭanaʿtuka: I nurtured and chose you for Myself; in Ṭā Hā 20:41.

Ṣāniʿ: maker or craftsperson; established Arabic, but this particular form does not occur in the Qur’an.

Maṣnūʿ: made; an established Arabic form not present as such in the Qur’an.

Suitable renderings include:

وَاصْنَعِ الْفُلْكَ (Wa-iṣnaʿi al-fulka) — and build the ship.

وَيَصْنَعُ الْفُلْكَ (Wa-yaṣnaʿu al-fulka) — and he was building the ship.

وَعَلَّمْنَاهُ صَنْعَةَ لَبُوسٍ (Wa-ʿallamnāhu ṣanʿata labūsin) — and We taught him to make a covering.

وَتَتَّخِذُونَ مَصَانِعَ (Wa-tattakhidhūna maṣāniʿa) — and you erect structures.

وَلِتُصْنَعَ عَلَىٰ عَيْنِي (Wa-li-tuṣnaʿa ʿalā ʿaynī) — so that you may be nurtured under My observation.

وَاصْطَنَعْتُكَ لِنَفْسِي (Wa-iṣṭanaʿtuka li-nafsī) — and I nurtured you for Myself.

إِنَّمَا صَنَعُوا كَيْدُ سَاحِرٍ (Innamā ṣanaʿū kaydu sāḥirin) — what they have made is only a magician’s trick.

وَهُمْ يَحْسَبُونَ أَنَّهُمْ يُحْسِنُونَ صُنْعًا (Wa-hum yaḥsabūna annahum yuḥsinūna ṣunʿan) — while they think they are doing good work.

صُنْعَ اللَّهِ الَّذِي أَتْقَنَ كُلَّ شَيْءٍ (Ṣunʿa allāhi alladhī atqana kulla shayʾin) — the work of The God, who has made everything firm.

In Persian, sākhtan, making, suits clear physical constructions. For human conduct, doing or deeds is more natural. Nurturing and preparing for a special purpose better convey Moses’ contextual uses.

9. Conclusion

Ṣ N ʿ concerns making, doing work, and producing its result. Familiar uses connect with skill, attention, and planning, but not every derivative is restricted to material objects.

Noah builds a ship, David learns protective-equipment making, ʿĀd erect structures, and God describes nurturing Moses and preparing him for a special mission.

Other uses describe human behavior, magicians’ deception, condemned deeds, and efforts people may mistakenly consider good.

Making, technical skill, and moral goodness are distinguishable. Skill can accompany ṣunʿ, but the root alone does not establish a work’s value.

Ṣunʿa Allāh in Al-Naml 27:88 concerns divine action and workmanship, accompanied by atqana kulla shayʾin. It expresses firm divine workmanship without reducing God’s action to human tools and limitations.

Brief root meaning for Forqan:

Making, crafting, and doing work that produces a result or effect; in other uses, nurturing, preparing, and performing various deeds.

Each derivative’s precise meaning follows its form, the work’s subject, and the verse’s context. Skill and careful execution may accompany ṣunʿ, while moral goodness or a beneficial result is not an invariant part of the root.
