1. The meaning in early Arabic

Arabic بَنَان (banān) denotes fingers and toes, or their end portions. Some lexicographers apply it to the entire digit; others particularly mean the fingertip. In extended usage it can also concern bodily extremities and their joints.

In Kitāb al-ʿAyn, al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad describes banān as the tips of fingers and toes. Abū al-Haytham, meanwhile, explains that بَنَانَة (banānah) can denote the whole finger or its upper segment.

Restricting banān to the smallest part of the very tip therefore does not encompass every recorded use.

Three levels should be distinguished: the digit itself, its end portion, and the extremities of hands and feet. The first two senses are clearer and more common in lexical sources; the third appears particularly in explanations of certain Qur’anic passages and extended expressions.

The family also has a particular morphological feature. Banān is a collective generic noun whose individual unit is banānah. In a phrase such as kull banān, it need not mean one individual finger: it can refer collectively to the digits, with kull emphasising comprehensiveness.

Al-Miṣbāḥ, Lisān, and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: digit, fingertip, and the unit of banān

Is the root’s core meaning remaining or being settled?

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris examines the doubled root B N N under اللُّزُومُ وَالْإِقَامَةُ (al-luzūmu wa-al-iqāmah): remaining attached and staying in a place.

He discusses أَبَنَّ بِالْمَكَانِ (abanna bi-al-makāni), remaining at a place, and أَبَنَّتِ السَّحَابَةُ (abannati al-saḥābatu), a cloud remaining in position.

التَّبْنِين (al-tabnīn) is also recorded for making something firm or secure in a matter.

The focus in this branch is on remaining, adhering, or becoming settled.

Ibn Fāris and some other lexicographers seek to connect the name banān with that principle: fingers make the work necessary for life possible, allowing people to meet their needs and organise a settled life.

The functional connection is intelligible. People grasp, build, write, and use tools with their fingers.

It does not, however, prove that the name for fingers historically arose from the verb of remaining in a place.

Such a claim requires independent evidence about the word’s emergence and development. A functional resemblance between fingers and settled life is not sufficient evidence by itself.

This study therefore defines banān through its attested sense of digits and fingertips. Its association with remaining or settledness is a derivational explanation, not a definitive replacement for the word’s meaning.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: adherence, residence, and derivational explanation

2. Other lexical branches of the family

Early sources record other forms in this family, some resembling banān in writing while differing in meaning.

Bannah: smell and fragrance

بَنَّة (bannah) denotes smell or scent. Some lexicographers restrict it to a pleasant fragrance; others also record an unpleasant smell.

Sībawayh and others mention a pleasant scent, whereas al-Aṣmaʿī allows both pleasant and unpleasant smells.

For example, بَنَّةُ الْغَنَمِ (bannatu al-ghanami) concerns the smell of sheep’s resting places.

Its plural is بِنان (binān). The initial vowel matters: this form has kasrah under bāʾ, whereas بَنَان (banān), digits, has fatḥah.

Identical written consonants should therefore not cause the two distinct senses to be confused.

Ibn Fāris also seeks to relate smell to remaining and adherence, since a scent can cling to something and persist.

This too is an intelligible image, but does not by itself establish the historical direction of derivation.

Bunānah: a meadow

Some sources record بُنَانَة (bunānah) for a meadow or an attractive green tract of land. They also report بَنانة (banānah), with initial fatḥah, for the same meadow sense.

Ibn Fāris relates this use to the pleasant smell of plants.

This meaning does not occur in the Qur’an and differs semantically from banānah meaning an individual digit. Where both have initial fatḥah, context distinguishes the uses.

Al-Muḥkam and Tāj: two meadow vocalisations and distinction from a digit

Such forms show different lexical branches under B N N. A Qur’anic definition should recognise them without importing any of them into the meaning of digits without evidence.

B N N and making firm

Lexical sources record بَنَّنَ (bannana) for keeping or tethering livestock to fatten them. تَبَنَّنَ (tabannana) can also concern being deliberate and avoiding haste in judgement.

These uses belong to the field of remaining or firmness, but do not occur with those meanings in the Qur’an.

Al-Muʿjam al-Wasīṭ: keeping livestock for fattening

Qur’anic banān therefore cannot be translated directly as stabilisation, residence, smell, or meadow.

3. Banān in early Arabic poetry

Poetic witnesses show that banān was an established word for digits in pre-Qur’anic Arabic.

Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī: delicate fingers

Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī describes a beautiful hand:

بِمُخَضَّبٍ رَخْصٍ كَأَنَّ بَنَانَهُ
عَنَمٌ يَكَادُ مِنَ اللَّطَافَةِ يُعْقَدُ

bi-mukhaḍḍabin rakhṣin ka-anna banānahu / ʿanamun yakādu mina al-laṭāfati yuʿqadu

Translation: “With a soft, henna-dyed hand whose fingers are like ʿanam, so delicate and supple that they seem capable of being tied into a knot.”

Banānahu here refers to the fingers of the hand.

The poet compares them to delicate, reddish branches or parts of a plant. The image concerns their softness, delicacy, and beauty.

The word therefore does not mean power, ownership, or social standing here; it directly denotes fingers.

This is a clear pre-Islamic witness to banān. Lexical sources also transmit the ending عَنَمٌ عَلَىٰ أَغْصَانِهِ لَمْ يُعْقَدِ (ʿanamun ʿalā aghṣānihi lam yuʿqadi). The present text and translation follow يَكَادُ مِنَ اللَّطَافَةِ يُعْقَدُ (yakādu mina al-laṭāfati yuʿqadu).

Al-Nābighah’s Diwan: the witness on delicate fingers

ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād: fingers grasping a sword

A poem attributed to ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād reads:

وَأَنَّ الْمَوْتَ طَوْعُ يَدِي إِذَا مَا
وَصَلْتُ بَنَانَهَا بِالْهِنْدُوَانِي

wa-anna al-mawta ṭawʿu yadī idhā mā / waṣaltu banānahā bi-al-hinduwāni

Translation: “And death obeys my hand when I join its fingers to the Indian sword.”

The fingers here enable the grasping and wielding of the sword.

The poet places banān alongside yad, but the two words are not identical.

Yad is the hand, and banān its fingers: the parts through which tools are grasped and controlled.

The fingers’ role in holding the sword is clear. Banān itself does not mean fighting or striking; that function comes from its association with the sword and the poem’s context.

The verse is attributed to ʿAntarah in the collected poetry and literary sources, although the attribution of the complete ode differs among transmissions. This should remain visible without altering the word’s clear sense. In the Diwan transmission edited by Muḥammad Saʿīd Mawlawī, al-Aṣmaʿī attributes the ode to Kuthayyir ibn ʿUrwah al-Nahshalī.

Transmitted ʿAntarah Diwan, Mawlawī edition: disputed attribution

Both witnesses share a point: banān means fingers, whether the poet describes their beauty and delicacy or their ability to grasp and use tools.

4. Banān in battle: Al-Anfāl 8:12

Verse text:

إِذْ يُوحِي رَبُّكَ إِلَى الْمَلَائِكَةِ أَنِّي مَعَكُمْ فَثَبِّتُوا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا ۚ سَأُلْقِي فِي قُلُوبِ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا الرُّعْبَ فَاضْرِبُوا فَوْقَ الْأَعْنَاقِ وَاضْرِبُوا مِنْهُمْ كُلَّ بَنَانٍ

idh yūḥī rabbuka ilā al-malāʾikati annī maʿakum fa-thabbitū alladhīna āmanū sa-ulqī fī qulūbi alladhīna kafarū al-ruʿba fa-iḍribū fawqa al-aʿnāqi wa-iḍribū minhum kulla banānin

Translation:

“When your Lord revealed to the angels: I am with you, so make firm those who believe. I shall cast terror into the hearts of those who disbelieve; so strike above the necks and strike all their digits.”

Al-Anfāl 8:12

The verse concerns battle and armed confrontation. The surrounding verses discuss divine support, angels, strengthening believers, and confrontation with the opposing group.

Banān refers here to bodily parts involved in work and particularly in grasping and using tools.

Fingers matter for holding swords, spears, shields, reins, and other implements of warfare.

Striking them can therefore reduce a combatant’s capacity to continue fighting.

This functional explanation must not change the lexical meaning. Banān does not mean fighting capacity; it names the digits whose function matters in this setting.

Does banān mean only fingertips here?

Some lexical explanations restrict banān to fingertips.

Broader views also occur in explanations of Al-Anfāl 8:12.

Al-Ṭabarī relates banān to fingers and toes in his lexical account, and to bodily extremities and their joints in explaining the verse.

Some transmitted explanations gloss kull banān as kull mafṣil: every joint.

Al-Zajjāj likewise extends its scope here beyond digits to other bodily parts.

Two levels must therefore remain distinct.

First is the familiar meaning of banān itself: digits and their end portions.

Second is its contextual extension to joints or limbs where a blow impedes movement and fighting.

The first is lexically clearer and better attested. The broader reading can be examined within early explanations and usage, but should not wholly replace the main meaning without justification.

“Digits” is therefore suitable in the base translation. A separate explanation can mention the extension to extremities and joints accepted by some lexicographers and commentators.

Al-Ṭabarī: digits, extremities, and joints

The role of kulla banānin

بَنَانٍ (banānin) follows كُلَّ (kulla) in this verse.

Kull emphasises comprehensiveness; the expression does not concern only one particular finger.

It concerns the full range of digits, or their parts, made the object of striking within this confrontation.

Kull alone does not establish a prescribed identical movement to be performed on every finger of every person. In the battle context, the sentence expresses the scope of the target.

Who receives the command?

The opening reports divine speech to the angels:

«إِذْ يُوحِي رَبُّكَ إِلَى الْمَلَائِكَةِ»

idh yūḥī rabbuka ilā al-malāʾikati

“When your Lord revealed to the angels.”

The command فَثَبِّتُوا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا (fa-thabbitū alladhīna āmanū) then follows.

This continuity of address matters in determining the addressees of the subsequent commands.

Early commentaries nevertheless differ over whether fa-iḍribū remains addressed directly to angels or concerns the believers present in the battle.

Al-Zajjāj, Maʿānī al-Qurʾān: address and the scope of banān

The disagreement does not change the meaning of banān.

The verse concerns a particular armed confrontation. Its battle command should not be removed from that setting and turned into a general instruction for dealing with everyone of a different belief.

For this root study, banān means digits and associated extremities. The command’s subject and scope must be examined through the battle passages as a whole.

5. Banān in human re-creation: Al-Qiyāmah 75:4

Verse text:

بَلَىٰ قَادِرِينَ عَلَىٰ أَنْ نُسَوِّيَ بَنَانَهُ

balā qādirīna ʿalā an nusawwiya banānahu

Translation:

“Yes indeed, We are able even to form his digits properly.”

Al-Qiyāmah 75:4

The verse continues a question about human re-creation after death.

Al-Qiyāmah 75:3 asks whether the human being thinks that The God will not gather his bones.

Al-Qiyāmah 75:4 answers with بَلَىٰ (balā), affirming the ability to perform taswiyat al-banān.

Unlike Al-Anfāl, the digits are not targets in battle here; they occur in the context of creation and the body’s reconstruction.

Why are digits mentioned after bones?

Bones are firm components of the body’s structure. Digits are comparatively small and intricate parts, whose precise movement depends on coordinated bones, joints, muscles, and other components.

When the subject is gathering a person’s bones after death, mentioning banān can emphasise the ability to reconstruct the body’s finer parts as well.

On this reading, divine power extends beyond gathering large bodily components: even the digits, with their detailed structure, can be re-created.

This accords with the connection between Al-Qiyāmah 75:3 and Al-Qiyāmah 75:4.

Banān itself nevertheless does not mean small bones. Digits contain bones, joints, skin, and other components; mentioning them can concern their entire structure.

Two readings of nusawwiya banānahu

Study of S W Y

Early analyses offer two important readings of this expression.

First reading: the precise reconstruction of the digits

Here نُسَوِّيَ (nusawwiya) concerns properly forming, arranging, and coordinating the digits’ structure.

Ibn Qutaybah and al-Zajjāj explain this in connection with reconstructing the body’s smaller components.

If The God can restore the digits with their small details, gathering the larger bones is not beyond His power.

This reading directly relates to the preceding question about gathering bones.

Second reading: the ability to alter the digits’ structure

Another explanation, transmitted by al-Ṭabarī and other early commentators, understands taswiyat al-banān as the ability to join the digits into a single part, like an animal’s hoof or undivided foot.

It emphasises that The God can even give the digits a form different from the familiar human structure.

Separate fingers allow grasping, opening, closing, and delicate work. Combining them into one part would change many of these abilities.

This reading emphasises divine power to alter bodily structure.

The possibility of such a meaning should nevertheless be distinguished from establishing it conclusively.

The verse does not say that human fingers will necessarily become animal hooves at resurrection. Commentaries use the image to explain the scope of divine power.

Since the main question concerns gathering bones after death, precise reconstruction relates more directly to resurrection.

The base translation should therefore render nusawwiya as “form properly,” leaving the other reading for the explanation.

This avoids imposing a particular interpretation, such as transformation into a hoof, on the translated text.

Al-Qurṭubī: two readings of forming banān, citing Ibn Qutaybah and al-Zajjāj

Is the verse about fingerprints?

Today, the skin patterns of fingertips have scientific and practical importance in identifying people.

This can inform contemporary reflection on the intricacy of the fingers’ structure.

The verse itself, however, uses no term for skin patterns, fingertip ridges, or identification through fingerprints.

Arabic banānahu denotes his digits or fingertips; it is not a technical term for fingerprints.

The verse should therefore not be said directly to mean reconstructing identifying ridge patterns, or explicitly to teach the modern method of fingerprint identification.

Conversely, modern knowledge of fingertip skin patterns can be included when the aim is to illustrate the wonders of creation.

A scientific fact can illustrate the complexity and precision of the digits’ structure without determining the verse’s lexical meaning or exclusive intention.

The verse clearly concerns divine power to re-create the human being and properly form the digits again.

A grammatical note

Banānahu is the object of nusawwiya, and its attached pronoun -hu refers to the human being under discussion.

Nusawwiya is an imperfect verb in the subjunctive after أَنْ (an).

قَادِرِينَ (qādirīna) is an accusative masculine plural. In the familiar grammatical analysis, it is circumstantial, describing divine ability in response to the human assumption.

Al-Ṭabarī: re-creation, banān, and qādirīn

This structure connects the answer to the preceding question.

The verse should therefore not be interpreted in isolation from the question about gathering bones.

6. Banān and neighbouring words

B N N, banān, and Ṣ B ʿ, iṣbaʿ

Study of SAD B AYN

إِصْبَع (iṣbaʿ) is the name of a digit.

Banān also denotes digits and, in some uses, their tips.

The two belong to a similar semantic field but differ in their patterns of use.

Iṣbaʿ can denote one particular finger, whereas banān commonly refers collectively to the digits or their end portions, with banānah as its individual unit.

There should nevertheless be no absolute rule that banān can never include the whole digit. Lexical reports do not support that restriction.

B N N, banān, and N M L, anmulah

Study of N M L

أَنْمُلَة (anmulah) particularly concerns a joint or end portion of a finger. The Qur’anic root index places anāmil under N M L; the initial hamzah of anmulah should not be assumed to be an independent root consonant without analysis.

Quranic Corpus: anāmil indexed under N M L

Banān can also denote a fingertip, but sometimes has a wider recorded range.

When only the smallest terminal segment is intended, anmulah may be more precise; not every use of banān should be restricted to it.

B N N, banān, and Y D Y, yad

Study of Y D Y

Yad names the hand and can have a wider range than the palm in some Arabic constructions.

Banān denotes digits or their end portions.

The poem attributed to ʿAntarah uses yad and banān together, illustrating this distinction.

Translating banān as digits in Al-Anfāl 8:12 likewise preserves the particular body part; rendering it as hands changes its scope.

B N N, banān, and F Ṣ L, mafṣil

Study of F SAD L

مَفْصِل (mafṣil) is a place of connection and movement between bodily parts. It belongs to F Ṣ L; initial mīm is part of the noun’s derived pattern.

Some explanations of Al-Anfāl 8:12 extend banān to joints, but the words are not complete synonyms.

Each digit has segments and joints, but not every bodily joint is in a digit.

Explaining banān through joints within a battle context should therefore not become the root’s general definition.

B N N, banān, and B N Y, bināʾ

Study of B N Y

B N N and B N Y are different roots.

In its construction-related use, B N Y concerns building and erecting.

Banān, by contrast, names digits.

Fingers may be tools for building, but that functional connection does not make the roots identical.

Nor should two shared consonants turn banān directly into “structure” or “the body’s construction.”

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

The principal equivalent for banān is “digits.”

“Fingertips” is also suitable when the end portion matters in context.

For banānah, “one digit” or “a fingertip” may fit the sentence.

The proposed Qur’anic renderings are as follows.

Al-Anfāl 8:12

«وَاضْرِبُوا مِنْهُمْ كُلَّ بَنَانٍ»

wa-iḍribū minhum kulla banānin

“And strike all their digits.”

This preserves the main sense. Possible inclusion of extremities or joints is examined separately in the explanation.

Al-Qiyāmah 75:4

«بَلَىٰ قَادِرِينَ عَلَىٰ أَنْ نُسَوِّيَ بَنَانَهُ»

balā qādirīna ʿalā an nusawwiya banānahu

“Yes indeed, We are able to form his digits properly.”

Nusawwī concerns arranging and properly forming. Re-creation and the gathering of bodily components are understood from the preceding context.

The base translation should not replace banān with fingerprints, fingertip ridges, bodily joints, human working ability, or the smallest bones.

These ideas may occur in scientific, lexical, or contextual explanation, but they are not the word’s direct meaning.

8. Conclusion

Arabic B N N has several uses. Ibn Fāris examines it under remaining and residence; some lexicographers seek to connect the name of digits with the human ability to carry out life’s tasks.

This proposed derivation is not clear enough to replace banān’s main meaning with settledness or residence.

Its familiar Qur’anic sense is digits, particularly their end portions.

Pre-Islamic poetry supports this: al-Nābighah describes delicate, beautiful fingers, while the poem attributed to ʿAntarah presents them grasping and wielding a sword.

The root occurs twice in the Qur’an.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: two uses of banān

Al-Anfāl 8:12 places the digits within armed confrontation. Some lexicographers extend the expression to extremities and joints, but that extension should not remove the familiar digit sense from translation.

Al-Qiyāmah 75:4 concerns digits within human re-creation. Mentioning them after the gathering of bones emphasises the ability to reconstruct the body’s finer parts.

Two readings of taswiyat al-banān are discussed: forming the digits again and the ability to alter their structure. The first relates more directly to re-creation; the second concerns divine ability to change their form.

Modern knowledge of fingertip skin patterns can also draw attention to creation’s intricacy, but the verse does not directly describe fingerprint identification.

Final definition in Qur’anic usage: Banān denotes fingers and toes, particularly their end portions. In some extended uses it can also include bodily extremities.

In the Qur’an the word names a particular bodily part whose role changes with context. One verse highlights grasping and using tools in battle; the other highlights bodily intricacy and the ability to re-create it.

Digits is therefore the most suitable basic equivalent for both Qur’anic uses, allowing neither a battle-related interpretation, a proposed etymology, nor modern knowledge to replace the word’s direct meaning.
