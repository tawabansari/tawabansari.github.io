1. Introducing the Root

The three-consonant Arabic root J M L has several important semantic branches. The first concerns parts gathering into a whole and, in some uses, large bodily structure. Jumlah denotes a collection or entirety, and ajmala can mean collecting or totaling. Ibn Fāris also tentatively connects jamal, “camel,” to this branch through bodily size.

The second branch concerns beauty, goodness, and agreeableness. Jamāl names beauty, while jamīl can describe something beautiful, gracious conduct, a pleasing response, or an appropriate way of acting.

The branches approach one another in some uses but should not be equated without evidence. Something large may be neither beautiful nor good; something small can be beautiful. Conduct can also be morally good without involving bodily appearance or visual beauty.

This distinction particularly matters in the Qur’an.

Al-Naḥl 16:6 uses jamāl for livestock’s beauty. In Yūsuf 12:18 and Yūsuf 12:83, jamīl describes patience. Al-Ḥijr 15:85 describes forbearance with the same adjective. Al-Aḥzāb 33:28 and Al-Aḥzāb 33:49 concern releasing wives graciously; Al-Muzzammil 73:10 concerns gracious withdrawal; Al-Maʿārij 70:5 likewise describes patience as jamīl.

By contrast, jumlatan wāḥidatan in Al-Furqān 25:32 concerns the Qur’an being sent down all at once. Al-jamal in Al-Aʿrāf 7:40 means a camel, while jimālat in Al-Mursalāt 77:33 appears in a comparison involving sparks of fire.

Thus, beauty, size, the camel, and a unified whole are related subjects within J M L, but do not share one identical meaning.

Accurate understanding requires examining the word’s form, vowels, morphology, and each verse’s context.

Qur’anic Forms and Occurrence Counts

J M L occurs eleven times in the Qur’an, in five principal forms:

Quranic Arabic Corpus:eleven occurrences in five forms

  • Jamālun once in Al-Naḥl 16:6, meaning beauty.
  • Al-jamalu once in Al-Aʿrāf 7:40, meaning a camel.
  • Jimālatun once in Al-Mursalāt 77:33, comparing sparks to camels, with lexical and reading differences.
  • Jumlatan once in Al-Furqān 25:32, meaning all together as a whole.
  • Jamīl with different case endings seven times: Yūsuf 12:18, Yūsuf 12:83; Al-Ḥijr 15:85; Al-Aḥzāb 33:28, Al-Aḥzāb 33:49; Al-Maʿārij 70:5; and Al-Muzzammil 73:10.

Verbs such as jamula, jammala, ajmala, and tajammala do not occur in the Qur’an.

The breadth of the early Arabic family should therefore not be confused with the actual number of Qur’anic derivatives. Brief root meaning: Beauty and goodness; in another branch, a whole and largeness; jamal names the camel.

2. Semantic Cores in Early Arabic

Two Principles in Maqāyīs al-Lughah

Ibn Fāris writes concerning this root:

الْجِيمُ وَالْمِيمُ وَاللَّامُ أَصْلَانِ: أَحَدُهُمَا تَجَمُّعٌ وَعِظَمُ الْخَلْقِ، وَالْآخَرُ حُسْنٌ.

al-jīmu wa-al-mīmu wa-al-lāmu aṣlāni aḥaduhumā tajammuʿun wa-ʿiẓamu al-khalqi wa-al-ākharu ḥusnun

Translation: “Jīm, mīm, and lām have two principles: gathering and greatness of bodily structure, and goodness.”

This division is central to the study.

The first principle concerns being a whole and size.

The second concerns beauty and agreeableness.

Ibn Fāris discusses jumlah under the first branch:

أَجْمَلْتُ الشَّيْءَ، وَهَذِهِ جُمْلَةُ الشَّيْءِ.

ajmaltu al-shayʾa wa-hādhihi jumlatu al-shayʾi

That is: “I collected the thing together, and this is its entirety.”

He also allows that the camel’s name may relate to this branch through its large bodily structure.

This should be understood as the possibility he proposes.

His connection between the camel’s name and bodily size is an etymological analysis, not definitive proof that jumlah existed first and the camel’s name was subsequently formed from it.

For the second branch, Ibn Fāris writes:

وَالْأَصْلُ الْآخَرُ الْجَمَالُ، وَهُوَ ضِدُّ الْقُبْحِ.

wa-al-aṣlu al-ākharu al-jamālu wa-huwa ḍiddu al-qubḥi

Translation: “The other principle is jamāl, the opposite of ugliness.”

Maqāyīs:two principles and the camel-name hypothesis

The Arabic lexical tradition therefore considers beauty and collection within one family without necessarily assigning them one semantic origin.

Did Every Meaning Develop from Becoming Complete?

One might hypothesize that assembling parts, completing a structure, and beauty all developed from one original image.

When parts fit together correctly and harmoniously, for example, something may look beautiful.

That conceptual relationship is imaginable.

Harmony of parts does not always produce beauty, however. Arabic jamāl can also describe conduct and speech, which do not necessarily have a large physical structure.

Defending root unity should therefore not produce a definitive theory of structural perfection to which every usage is reduced.

A more precise approach preserves the two branches recorded in early sources, treating possible connections only as far as evidence permits.

3. Beauty and Goodness: The Jamāl Branch

Jamāl: Beauty

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

الْجَمَالُ: الْحُسْنُ.

al-jamālu al-ḥusnu

Translation: “Jamāl means beauty and goodness.”

This short definition clarifies the root’s second branch.

Arabic can apply jamāl to a being’s or object’s appearance.

People, animals, clothes, buildings, and landscapes may be considered visually beautiful.

Jamāl’s scope is not confined to appearance, however.

Ibn Sīdah explains in Al-Muḥkam:

الْجَمَالُ الْحُسْنُ يَكُونُ فِي الْفِعْلِ وَالْخَلْقِ.

al-jamālu al-ḥusnu yakūnu fī al-fiʿli wa-al-khalqi

Translation: “Jamāl is goodness found in action and physical constitution.”

The distinction matters.

A face’s beauty differs from an action’s goodness.

A face is perceived through form, proportion, color, and other visual characteristics.

Conduct can be good or objectionable through its manner, respect for rights and others, and consequences.

Arabic can describe both with J M L.

Ṣabrun jamīlun in the Qur’an consequently cannot become patience with a pretty face.

Beauty there concerns conduct’s quality and appropriateness.

Jamula: Was Beautiful or Good

Jamula follows the faʿula pattern.

Arabic uses it for a person or thing being beautiful or good.

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

جَمُلَ الرَّجُلُ جَمَالًا، فَهُوَ جَمِيلٌ.

jamula al-rajulu jamālan fa-huwa jamīlun

Translation: “The man became or was beautiful; he is therefore jamīl.”

Lisān al-ʿArab:goodness in action and constitution

Jamīl does not describe human appearance alone.

It may also describe someone’s speech, conduct, action, or reply.

The Qur’an makes this extension clear: all seven jamīl occurrences concern conduct or manner of action rather than facial beauty.

Jamīl: Beautiful and Gracious

Depending on what it describes, jamīl can mean beautiful, good, pleasing, or appropriate.

A phrase about a face may concern visual beauty.

A phrase about patience concerns the commendable quality of steadfastness.

A phrase about forbearance concerns forgiving graciously.

A phrase about separation concerns behaving properly when ending a relationship.

One fixed equivalent cannot therefore suit every use of jamīl.

Their relationship remains clear: the subject is described as good or pleasing in some respect.

Jammala and Tajmīl

Jammala is Form II and means beautifying or adorning.

Tajmīl is its verbal noun.

Modern tajmīl concerns beautifying faces, buildings, and other things.

Those modern uses should not constitute its entire historical meaning.

Improving an action or conduct can also relate to this family in Arabic.

Tajammala: Adornment or Gracious Conduct

Tajammala can concern adorning oneself or adopting a good appearance or manner.

Some uses concern preserving dignity and behaving graciously in difficulty.

Someone facing grief or another person’s objectionable behavior may try to avoid harshness and unrestrained distress in responding.

Early Arabic tajammul is not identical to modern Persian luxurious living.

Modern Persian tajammul often concerns costly consumption, extensive adornment, and displaying wealth.

That specialized use should not be projected into early Arabic without evidence.

Lexical sources also record tajammala for eating rendered fat; this will be considered separately.

Mujāmalah: Gracious Treatment and Consideration for a Relationship

Jāmalahu concerns treating someone well and maintaining a pleasing manner.

Some lexical accounts describe mujāmalah as refraining from a harsh response to preserve a relationship despite having the ability to respond harshly.

Beauty of conduct can thus concern one’s treatment of others.

Mujāmalah does not always mean complete sincerity, justice, or goodwill, however.

Someone may behave gently from courtesy or expediency. Elsewhere, an outwardly pleasing manner may conceal another intention.

The conduct’s actual value requires evidence and circumstances.

The root itself expresses the manner’s goodness or agreeableness in the relevant use.

Asās and Lisān:tajammul and mujāmalah

4. The Camel: The Jamal Branch

Jamal: A Male Camel

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

الْجَمَلُ: الذَّكَرُ مِنَ الْإِبِلِ.

al-jamalu al-dhakaru mina al-ibili

Translation: “Jamal is the male of the camels.”

This gives the familiar meaning.

Nāqah is the Arabic name for a female camel.

Jamal predominantly denotes a male camel.

Lexical sources differ about the age at which a male is called jamal.

Some set one age threshold and others another.

Jamal alone should therefore not supply a precise age or weight in every verse.

Rare applications to a female camel are also reported, but do not erase the common male-camel sense.

Jimāl and Ajmāl

Jimāl and ajmāl are familiar plurals of jamal.

Other recorded forms include jimālah and jimālāt.

Some sources distinguish a collection of male camels from a herd containing both sexes.

Those distinctions are not uniform across lexical accounts.

Al-Mursalāt 77:33 consequently requires separate attention to the precise Qur’anic form and its morphological and reading differences.

Jāmil: A Herd of Camels

Some Arabic uses call a camel herd jāmil.

Some lexicographers include the camels’ owners or keepers in the body designated.

Other explanations allow both male and female camels.

A family member can thus extend from the name of one animal to a body of such animals.

Jāmil itself does not occur in the Qur’an.

Jumālī: Large-Bodied

Rajulun jumāliyyun is recorded for a large-bodied man.

Nāqatun jumāliyyatun describes a female camel resembling a male in bodily size and strength.

The camel serves as a familiar example of bodily size.

Large size and beauty are distinct, however.

A large-bodied person may not be described as jamīl in appearance or conduct.

Conversely, a small-bodied person may be beautiful.

Verbal resemblance should therefore not turn the relationship of jamal and jamāl into a definitive rule concerning camels’ beauty.

Al-Jummal: A Thick Rope

Lexical sources also record al-jummal, with ḍammah on jīm and doubled mīm, for a large, thick rope.

This matters for readings of Al-Aʿrāf 7:40.

Some accounts describe a thick ship’s rope.

One can see a resemblance between that rope’s thickness and a camel’s size, but it is not the established historical cause of all root usages.

The familiar Al-Aʿrāf 7:40 text has al-jamalu, a camel.

Reading variants and the lexical rope account belong in their own discussion rather than replacing the familiar wording without explanation.

Lisān:camel,plurals,herd,and rope

5. Jumlah: A Collection and Wholeness

Jumlah: Parts Gathered Together

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

وَالْجُمْلَةُ: جَمَاعَةُ الشَّيْءِ.

wa-al-jumlatu jamāʿatu al-shayʾi

Translation: “Jumlah is a thing’s collection or entirety.”

Another explanation states:

وَالْجُمْلَةُ: جَمَاعَةُ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ بِكَمَالِهِ مِنَ الْحِسَابِ وَغَيْرِهِ.

wa-al-jumlatu jamāʿatu kulli shayʾin bi-kamālihi mina al-ḥisābi wa-ghayrihi

Translation: “Jumlah is the complete collection of something, whether in accounting or otherwise.”

Lisān:the whole and accounting

Several parts are treated as a whole.

Several goods, figures, or parts of a subject may exist separately. Bringing them into one account or report permits speaking of a jumlah.

In this usage, jumlah fundamentally concerns a collection and being together.

Ajmala al-Ḥisāba: Totaled an Account

Ajmala al-ḥisāba concerns bringing the account’s parts together and presenting their total.

This relates to modern Persian ijmāl.

Persian bi-ijmāl generally means briefly, without detailed explanation.

Presenting an account’s total differs from shortening an explanation, however.

An account may have several sections whose figures someone combines into one final amount.

Ijmāl then concerns the final total.

Brevity may instead omit details without properly collecting all the subject’s parts.

Jumlah in Al-Furqān 25:32 should therefore not directly become “brief” or “summary.”

Jumlah in Grammar

Arabic grammar uses jumlah for a combination of words linked grammatically.

Modern usage consequently calls a unit of speech or writing a sentence, or jumlah.

This technical use relates to words coming together in one structure.

The later grammatical meaning should not be transferred to every early occurrence.

Al-Furqān 25:32 does not concern a grammatical sentence or short phrase.

It concerns the Qur’an being sent down as a whole.

6. Other Early Arabic Uses

Al-Jamīl: Rendered Fat

A less familiar use of this family is al-jamīl, rendered fat.

Lisān al-ʿArab records it for fat or oil obtained by melting animal fat.

Jamala al-shaḥma likewise means rendering fat.

Some explanations emphasize the melted fat collecting in a vessel or on bread.

Ibn Qutaybah also suggests that some uses concerning facial beauty may relate to the shine and smoothness of fat.

That suggestion should not become a certain history of the beauty sense.

Maqāyīs:Ibn Qutaybah’s fat-and-beauty proposal

Jamīl in the patience and forbearance verses does not mean rendered fat.

A form’s having two uses does not permit meanings to be exchanged without context.

Ijtamala and Tajammala in Food Usages

Arabic sources record ijtamala for preparing or eating rendered fat.

Some accounts also use tajammala in that setting.

These differ from tajammala meaning adorning oneself or behaving graciously.

The verb’s complement and sentence subject matter determine whether an early text concerns food or conduct and adornment.

Jamālaka: Adopt Gracious Conduct

Jamālaka is recorded as an exhortation to preserve gracious behavior, dignity, and self-restraint.

It also appears in poetry, meaning “adopt a good manner” or “maintain gracious conduct.”

This relates to jamāl as goodness of conduct.

It should not be confined to facial adornment or wearing ornaments.

7. Witnesses from Pre-Islamic and Early Arabic Poetry

Camels and the Herd in Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd’s Poetry

Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd, a familiar pre-Islamic poet, mentions jāmil and nīb.

Nīb denotes mature female camels in Arabic.

Its association with jāmil shows that jāmil could designate a body of camels, sometimes including females.

This helps clarify pastoral vocabulary.

It does not make jamal the ordinary name for every male and female camel.

Jamal, nāqah, jimāl, and jāmil each have their own structure and range.

Lisān:Ṭarafah on jāmil and nīb

Camel Color in Al-Aʿshā’s Poetry

Al-Aʿshā Maymūn ibn Qays says:

تِلْكَ خَيْلِي مِنْهُ وَتِلْكَ رِكَابِي
هُنَّ صُفْرٌ أَوْلَادُهَا كَالزَّبِيبِ

tilka khaylī minhu wa-tilka rikābī / hunna ṣufrun awlāduhā ka-al-zabībi

Translation: “These are my horses from him, and these my riding camels; they are ṣufr, their young like raisins.”

Rikāb denotes camels used for riding and travel.

Ṣufr concerns a color also recorded by lexicographers for dark camels tinged with yellow or brown.

Awlāduhā ka-al-zabībi concerns the young camels’ dark color.

This helps examine Al-Mursalāt 77:33, where ṣufrun also accompanies a camel plural. The poetic line itself contains no J M L derivative; it witnesses the color word rather than directly attesting the root.

Jamharat al-Lughah:Al-Aʿshā’s color witness

It does not make ṣufr exclusively “black” throughout Arabic.

Yellow is also a familiar meaning of this color family.

The exact color depends on the object and contextual evidence.

Gracious Conduct in Abū Dhuʾayb al-Hudhalī’s Poetry

Abū Dhuʾayb al-Hudhalī says:

جَمَالَكَ أَيُّهَا الْقَلْبُ الْقَرِيحُ
سَتَلْقَىٰ مَنْ تُحِبُّ فَتَسْتَرِيحُ

jamālaka ayyuhā al-qalbu al-qarīḥu / sa-talqā man tuḥibbu fa-tastarīḥu

Translation: “Wounded heart, maintain gracious self-restraint; you will meet the one you love and find relief.”

Jamālaka concerns preserving graciousness and dignity in grief.

Ibn Sīdah explains that one should behave well and avoid objectionable, unrestrained distress.

This helps illuminate Qur’anic ṣabrun jamīlun.

It does not mean gracious patience excludes grief or forbids every tear and expression of pain.

The poem concerns how one responds to grief.

Abū Dhuʾayb lived through pre-Islamic times and early Islam. This is early Arabic evidence close to revelation, but cannot be declared definitely pre-Islamic without dating its composition.

Sharḥ Shawāhid al-Mughnī:Abū Dhuʾayb’s witness

Al-Jamīl in Abū Khirāsh al-Hudhalī’s Poetry

Lisān al-ʿArab transmits this line from Abū Khirāsh:

نُقَابِلُ جُوعَهُمْ بِمُكَلَّلَاتٍ
مِنَ الْفُرْنِيِّ يَرْعَبُهَا الْجَمِيلُ

nuqābilu jūʿahum bi-mukallalātin / mina al-furniyyi yarʿabuhā al-jamīlu

Translation: “We meet their hunger with thick furnī loaves covered in rendered fat.”

Lisān:furnī bread and poetic variants

Al-jamīl here means rendered fat, not moral or visual beauty. Other transmissions differ in the opening verb; the supplied nuqābilu is supported in Lisān’s J M L entry and is retained here.

Lisān:nuqābilu and rendered fat

One lexical form can therefore have a food sense distinct from the familiar adjective jamīl.

Methodologically, this prevents one meaning being generalized to all resembling forms.

All seven Qur’anic jamīl occurrences describe conduct or manner of action; none concerns rendered fat.

Findings from the Poetic Witnesses

The poetry clarifies three different fields.

The camel poetry concerns the animal and its color.

Abū Dhuʾayb’s poem concerns gracious conduct and dignity.

Abū Khirāsh uses jamīl as the name of a food substance.

Root research must consequently distinguish resembling forms and their different meanings.

Shared consonants alone do not make beauty, camels, and rendered fat mean the same thing in every sentence.

8. Qur’anic Jamāl: Livestock’s Beauty

Al-Naḥl 16:6

وَلَكُمْ فِيهَا جَمَالٌ حِينَ تُرِيحُونَ وَحِينَ تَسْرَحُونَ

wa-lakum fīhā jamālun ḥīna turīḥūna wa-ḥīna tasraḥūna

Existing Persian Forqan translation, rendered here in English: “There is beauty for you in them when you bring them home and when you take them out to pasture.”

This concerns livestock introduced in the preceding verse.

Al-Naḥl 16:5 mentions warmth, benefits, and food obtained from livestock.

Al-Naḥl 16:6 introduces their beauty.

Al-Naḥl 16:7 also mentions carrying burdens to distant lands.

Livestock are thus considered beyond food and transport: their presence and movement’s beauty is also an aspect of human enjoyment.

Wa-Lakum Fīhā Jamālun

Jamālun means beauty here.

Fīhā refers to livestock.

Beauty concerns the same beings whose practical benefits were previously mentioned.

This matters.

The Qur’an does not oppose beauty to usefulness.

Livestock may serve food, clothing, transport, and other needs while also appearing beautiful in human life.

Jamāl itself does not make every economic benefit merely an aspect of beauty.

The verses name these features separately.

Ḥīna Turīḥūna wa-Ḥīna Tasraḥūna

Turīḥūna here concerns bringing livestock back from pasture.

Tasraḥūna concerns sending or taking them out to graze.

These describe movement at the beginning and end of grazing.

Animals leaving collectively for pasture and returning can be striking sights in pastoral life.

Their collective movement, sounds, colors, and bodily condition may contribute to the beauty.

The verse identifies no single feature, such as color, size, or number, as its sole cause.

Jamāl should therefore not be reduced to pride in a large herd or displaying wealth.

Many animals may confer social standing in a society, but the verse speaks directly of beauty.

Is Jamāl Here Only Visual Beauty?

The subject is actual livestock and their movement, making sensory and visual beauty clear.

A scene’s beauty may also accompany awareness of its usefulness, order, or tranquility.

Those aspects of experience may inform analysis without all becoming jamāl’s lexical meaning.

“Beauty” is accurate and sufficient in the base translation.

This also does not establish that only animals useful to humans can be beautiful.

The verse concerns one blessing rather than an exhaustive definition of the world’s beauty.

9. Qur’anic Al-Jamal: The Camel and the Needle’s Eye

Al-Aʿrāf 7:40

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ كَذَّبُوا بِآيَاتِنَا وَاسْتَكْبَرُوا عَنْهَا لَا تُفَتَّحُ لَهُمْ أَبْوَابُ السَّمَاءِ وَلَا يَدْخُلُونَ الْجَنَّةَ حَتَّىٰ يَلِجَ الْجَمَلُ فِي سَمِّ الْخِيَاطِ ۚ وَكَذَٰلِكَ نَجْزِي الْمُجْرِمِينَ

inna alladhīna kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā wa-istakbarū ʿanhā lā tufattaḥu lahum abwābu al-samāʾi wa-lā yadkhulūna al-jannata ḥattā yalija al-jamalu fī sammi al-khiyāṭi wa-kadhālika najzī al-mujrimīna

Proposed translation based on this study: “Those who denied Our signs and arrogantly turned from them will not have heaven’s gates opened to them, nor enter the garden until the camel passes through the needle’s eye; thus We recompense the criminals.”

Al-jamalu here denotes a camel.

Sammi al-khiyāṭi is the narrow eye of a needle.

The camel’s large body stands against the needle’s very small opening.

In ordinary language, this image expresses impossibility, not merely a difficult but normally achievable task.

Why a Camel?

The camel was a familiar, large animal in Arabic life.

A needle is a small tool with a very narrow eye.

Placing them together makes the difference in size clear.

Jamal itself does not mean impossibility, however.

It names the animal.

Impossibility emerges from the camel, the needle’s eye, and the sentence’s construction together.

Reading Differences: Camel or Thick Rope?

Lexical and reading sources also report al-jummal and related forms for thick rope, particularly a ship’s rope.

Their vowels and consonantal doubling differ from al-jamal.

Two related images consequently appear:

In the familiar reading, a large camel would pass through the needle’s eye.

In the rope reading, a thick rope would pass through it.

Both depend on the incompatibility of a large body or thick rope with the narrow opening.

A similar implication does not make the words identical.

The familiar Qur’anic text has al-jamalu, “camel,” and Forqan’s translation preserves this.

Thick-rope accounts belong under reading differences or lexical analysis rather than replacing the adopted text without explanation.

Al-Ṭabarī:camel and thick-rope readings

Does the Verse Concern Every Sinner?

The verse identifies a group through denial of the signs and arrogance toward them.

Al-jamal or the impossibility image alone does not make every erring person indiscriminately subject to that judgment.

The judgment’s scope requires the surrounding phrases.

For root research, jamal’s direct meaning remains camel.

10. Jimālatun Ṣufrun: Camels or Thick Ropes?

Al-Mursalāt 77:33

كَأَنَّهُ جِمَالَتٌ صُفْرٌ

ka-annahu jimālatun ṣufrun

Proposed translation based on this study: “As though they were yellowish camels.”

This belongs to the description of fire’s sparks.

The preceding verse states:

Al-Mursalāt 77:32

إِنَّهَا تَرْمِي بِشَرَرٍ كَالْقَصْرِ

innahā tarmī bi-shararin ka-al-qaṣri

Proposed translation based on this study: “It throws out sparks like great buildings.”

Verse 33 then compares the sparks to jimālatun ṣufrun.

The comparison’s subject is consequently sparks of fire.

First Reading: Camels

In the familiar reading, jimālat is a plural related to jamal.

The large sparks are then compared to camels.

Their resemblance may involve size, form, movement, or color.

The comparison alone does not explain all these features individually.

One should not specify a particular camel’s head, neck, legs, or movement without evidence.

Ṣufrun: Yellow or Dark with a Yellow Tinge?

Ṣufr is the plural of aṣfar and generally concerns yellow.

Early Arabic also calls some very dark camels ṣufr because their dark hide or hair is mixed with yellow or brown.

Al-Aʿshā’s ṣufr camels and dark young are one witness.

Some early commentators consequently explain jimālatun ṣufrun as dark camels tinged with yellow.

This does not make aṣfar primarily mean black throughout Arabic.

Color contributes to the comparison; identifying its precise shade requires linguistic and contextual evidence.

Second Reading: Thick Ropes

Lexical and exegetical reports also present another reading.

It takes jimālat or related forms as thick ropes used on ships and bridges.

Several strands are twisted or gathered into one large, thick rope.

This can likewise supply an image of large, elongated sparks.

Sources attribute this account to Ibn ʿAbbās, Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, and others.

Attribution to an early commentator alone does not establish decisive superiority.

The form in the particular reading, plural structure, and Arabic usage must be assessed.

Reading Differences

Reading sources report jimālah and jimālāt, as well as forms with ḍammah on jīm.

The familiar Ḥafṣ reading has jimālatun.

After reporting camel and rope accounts, Al-Ṭabarī prefers camels as the more familiar Arabic usage.

Al-Ṭabarī:preferring camels and discussing ṣufr

This matters, while remaining a scholar’s lexical and exegetical judgment.

A root article should report both documented accounts and explain the grounds for preferring the familiar reading.

Does Al-Mursalāt 77:33 Concern Camels’ Beauty?

No.

Although jimālat and jamāl belong to one family, the verse does not concern livestock’s beauty.

Jamāl in Al-Naḥl 16:6 means beauty.

Jimālat in Al-Mursalāt 77:33 names camels or, in another reading, thick ropes.

Shared consonants therefore do not license “beautiful sparks.”

The nominal structure and reading differences determine the word’s meaning.

11. Jumlatan Wāḥidatan: The Qur’an Sent Down All at Once

Al-Furqān 25:32

وَقَالَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا لَوْلَا نُزِّلَ عَلَيْهِ الْقُرْآنُ جُمْلَةً وَاحِدَةً ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ لِنُثَبِّتَ بِهِ فُؤَادَكَ ۖ وَرَتَّلْنَاهُ تَرْتِيلًا

wa-qāla alladhīna kafarū lawlā nuzzila ʿalayhi al-qurʾānu jumlatan wāḥidatan kadhālika li-nuthabbita bihi fuʾādaka wa-rattalnāhu tartīlan

Existing Persian Forqan translation, rendered here in English: “Those who disbelieved said: Why was the Qur’an not sent down to him all at once? Thus, to strengthen your heart through it; and We recited it deliberately and continuously.”

Jumlatan wāḥidatan concerns being together as one whole.

The opponents ask why the Qur’an was not sent down as a single body.

Jumlah therefore means neither a grammatical sentence nor a short phrase.

The Role of Wāḥidatan

Wāḥidatan describes jumlatan.

It emphasizes the collection’s unity.

Together, jumlatan wāḥidatan conveys all together in one body.

Persian yek-bāreh, “all at once,” is natural here.

The expression itself specifies neither pages, surahs, nor text size.

The subject is the manner and unity of sending down.

Being Together and Being Complete

Jumlatan wāḥidatan concerns being together.

Kāmil instead comes from K M L and concerns completeness or reaching completion.

These may coincide without sharing one meaning.

Something complete may reach people in stages.

Something delivered all at once may still be incomplete.

The phrase consequently does not make the Qur’an’s staged revelation imply incompleteness.

The verse itself answers the opponents’ question by introducing strengthening the Prophet’s heart through this manner of revelation.

Wa-Rattalnāhu Tartīlan

The verse continues with rattalnāhu.

This comes from R T L and concerns arrangement, coherence, and the manner of reciting or presenting speech.

Jumlah and tartīl must remain distinct.

Jumlatan wāḥidatan occurs in the opponents’ question about sending down all at once.

Rattalnāhu tartīlan belongs to the answer and the manner of presenting the Qur’an.

J M L itself does not mean ordered recitation, gradualness, or instruction.

Those concepts emerge from other phrases.

Does Jumlah Mean a Summary?

Modern ijmāl can mean speaking briefly.

That sense does not fit this verse.

The opponents ask why a whole body of Qur’an was not sent down, rather than why a summary was not revealed.

“All at once” is therefore more accurate than “summary.”

12. Ṣabrun Jamīlun: Gracious Steadfastness

Yūsuf 12:18

وَجَاءُوا عَلَىٰ قَمِيصِهِ بِدَمٍ كَذِبٍ ۚ قَالَ بَلْ سَوَّلَتْ لَكُمْ أَنْفُسُكُمْ أَمْرًا ۖ فَصَبْرٌ جَمِيلٌ ۖ وَاللَّهُ الْمُسْتَعَانُ عَلَىٰ مَا تَصِفُونَ

wa-jāʾū ʿalā qamīṣihi bi-damin kadhibin qāla bal sawwalat lakum anfusukum amran fa-ṣabrun jamīlun wa-Allāhu al-mustaʿānu ʿalā mā taṣifūna

Existing Persian Forqan translation, rendered here in English: “They brought false blood upon his shirt. He said: Rather, your own selves have made a matter seem fair to you; so, gracious patience. The God is the One whose help is sought against what you describe.”

Jamīlun describes ṣabrun.

It concerns patience’s kind or quality.

Jacob does not unquestioningly accept his sons’ account; he objects to their conduct.

He then speaks of gracious patience and seeking The God’s help.

Gracious patience therefore does not necessarily mean endorsing another’s false statement.

Nor does it establish that a patient person feels no grief or concern.

Yūsuf 12:83

قَالَ بَلْ سَوَّلَتْ لَكُمْ أَنْفُسُكُمْ أَمْرًا ۖ فَصَبْرٌ جَمِيلٌ ۖ عَسَى اللَّهُ أَنْ يَأْتِيَنِي بِهِمْ جَمِيعًا ۚ إِنَّهُ هُوَ الْعَلِيمُ الْحَكِيمُ

qāla bal sawwalat lakum anfusukum amran fa-ṣabrun jamīlun ʿasā Allāhu an yaʾtiyanī bi-him jamīʿan innahu huwa al-ʿalīmu al-ḥakīmu

Existing Persian Forqan translation, rendered here in English: “He said: Rather, your own selves have made a matter seem fair to you; so, gracious patience. Perhaps The God will bring them all to me; He is the Knowing, the Wise.”

This is the second ṣabrun jamīlun in Jacob’s narrative.

Later, Jacob expresses grief and asks his sons to search for Joseph and his brother.

His gracious patience therefore does not mean permanent silence, abandoning pursuit, or having no grief.

Its beauty requires examining steadfastness’s quality, the response to events, and preserving a proper direction in conduct.

Al-Maʿārij 70:5

فَاصْبِرْ صَبْرًا جَمِيلًا

fa-iṣbir ṣabran jamīlan

Proposed translation based on this study: “So remain steadfast with gracious patience.”

Ṣabran is the verbal noun of the imperative fa-iṣbir.

Jamīlan describes that verbal noun.

The verse therefore describes patience’s quality rather than merely commanding patience.

The surah concerns punishment the opponents consider distant, while the Qur’an presents it as near.

Patience here relates to the Prophet’s response to their denial and demands for haste.

Jamīl itself does not mean distant punishment, silence, or waiting a specified time.

Such features require other contextual expressions.

Does Gracious Patience Mean Not Complaining?

Some commentaries explain it as patience without complaint.

What complaint means must be specified, however.

Jacob’s narrative explicitly describes grief and speech about his pain.

Gracious patience cannot therefore be confined to an absolute ban on expressing grief or requesting help.

It may concern avoiding unjust protest, improper conduct, or abandoning what is right.

That explanation requires comparison with all relevant patience verses.

Al-Ṭabarī:the no-complaint account of gracious patience

Forqan’s independent article “Patience in the Qur’an” examines this more fully.

Forqan’s independent study of patience

For J M L, the key point is that jamīl describes patience’s quality rather than supplying patience’s entire definition.

13. Al-Ṣafḥ al-Jamīl: Gracious Forbearance

Al-Ḥijr 15:85

وَمَا خَلَقْنَا السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ وَمَا بَيْنَهُمَا إِلَّا بِالْحَقِّ ۗ وَإِنَّ السَّاعَةَ لَآتِيَةٌ ۖ فَاصْفَحِ الصَّفْحَ الْجَمِيلَ

wa-mā khalaqnā al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍa wa-mā baynahumā illā bi-al-ḥaqqi wa-inna al-sāʿata la-ātiyatun fa-iṣfaḥi al-ṣafḥa al-jamīla

Existing Persian Forqan translation, rendered here in English: “We created the heavens, the earth, and what lies between them only in truth; the Hour is surely coming, so turn away with gracious forbearance.”

Al-jamīla describes al-ṣafḥa.

Ṣafḥ comes from Ṣ F Ḥ and here concerns forgiving or turning away from another’s objectionable conduct.

Jamīl describes that forbearance’s quality.

The verse thus commands gracious forbearance rather than forbearance alone.

Does Gracious Forbearance Mean Disregarding Rights?

Jamīl itself does not mean that.

Forbearance may involve declining retaliation or a harsh reply.

It does not establish that every wrong must go unaddressed or the injured party’s rights disappear.

Different Qur’anic verses discuss justice and forgiveness together.

Forbearance’s beauty therefore requires attention to rights, circumstances, and manner of treatment.

Jamīl shows that even forbearance can be practiced well or poorly.

Someone may apparently forgive another’s error while continuing to humiliate them or using it repeatedly to cause harm.

That differs morally from gracious forbearance.

The example explains conduct’s quality rather than directly defining every word in the verse.

14. Sarāḥan Jamīlan: Gracious Separation

Al-Aḥzāb 33:28

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّبِيُّ قُلْ لِأَزْوَاجِكَ إِنْ كُنْتُنَّ تُرِدْنَ الْحَيَاةَ الدُّنْيَا وَزِينَتَهَا فَتَعَالَيْنَ أُمَتِّعْكُنَّ وَأُسَرِّحْكُنَّ سَرَاحًا جَمِيلًا

yā ayyuhā al-nabiyyu qul li-azwājika in kuntunna turidna al-ḥayāta al-dunyā wa-zīnatahā fa-taʿālayna umattiʿkunna wa-usarriḥkunna sarāḥan jamīlan

Existing Persian Forqan translation, rendered here in English: “Prophet, tell your wives: If you desire worldly life and its adornment, come: I will provide for you and release you graciously.”

Jamīlan describes sarāḥan.

Sarāḥ comes from S R Ḥ and here concerns releasing and ending the marital relationship.

Jamīl describes the manner of doing so.

Goodness concerns ending the relationship, rather than the wives’ bodily beauty or the amount of worldly adornment.

The verse itself also mentions providing for the women before releasing them.

Separation here therefore extends beyond uttering words to end the relationship.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:49

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِذَا نَكَحْتُمُ الْمُؤْمِنَاتِ ثُمَّ طَلَّقْتُمُوهُنَّ مِنْ قَبْلِ أَنْ تَمَسُّوهُنَّ فَمَا لَكُمْ عَلَيْهِنَّ مِنْ عِدَّةٍ تَعْتَدُّونَهَا ۖ فَمَتِّعُوهُنَّ وَسَرِّحُوهُنَّ سَرَاحًا جَمِيلًا

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū idhā nakaḥtumu al-muʾmināti thumma ṭallaqtumūhunna min qabli an tamassūhunna fa-mā lakum ʿalayhinna min ʿiddatin taʿtaddūnahā fa-mattiʿūhunna wa-sarriḥūhunna sarāḥan jamīlan

Existing Persian Forqan translation, rendered here in English: “You who believe, when you marry believing women and then divorce them before touching them, you have no waiting period to count for them; provide for them and release them graciously.”

Sarāḥan jamīlan occurs here as well.

Its circumstances differ from Al-Aḥzāb 33:28.

The subject is divorce before marital contact and the absence of the waiting period specified here.

Provision and gracious release then follow.

Jamīlan describes the quality of separation in both verses, while their legal and family circumstances differ.

Beauty in Ending a Relationship

A human or family relationship may end, yet its manner of ending remains an ethical responsibility.

People can respect the other party’s rights and avoid humiliation, harm, and excess.

Separation may instead involve oppression, humiliation, or denying rights.

Jamīl here concerns the manner’s goodness.

The adjective alone does not determine every rule of divorce, dowry, waiting periods, or financial rights.

These come from other relevant words and verses.

Sarāḥan jamīlan should consequently mean gracious separation or release, without absorbing every rule on ending marriage into jamīl.

15. Hajran Jamīlan: Gracious Withdrawal

Al-Muzzammil 73:10

وَاصْبِرْ عَلَىٰ مَا يَقُولُونَ وَاهْجُرْهُمْ هَجْرًا جَمِيلًا

wa-iṣbir ʿalā mā yaqūlūna wa-uhjurhum hajran jamīlan

Proposed translation based on this study: “Remain steadfast despite what they say, and withdraw from them graciously.”

Jamīlan describes hajran.

Hajr comes from H J R and concerns leaving or distancing oneself.

Jamīl describes that distancing’s quality.

The verse therefore does not establish that every broken relationship is good.

Withdrawal may involve justice and avoiding fruitless conflict, or arise from spite and an intention to harm.

Jamīl distinguishes these manners.

The Relationship of Patience and Withdrawal

The verse begins with wa-iṣbir and then introduces hajran jamīlan.

Patience here accompanies gracious withdrawal.

This matters because patience does not always mean remaining in a dispute and answering every opposing statement.

Remaining steadfast on a proper course may involve withdrawing from argument.

That conclusion comes from the two commands together, not from jamīl meaning withdrawal or patience meaning ending a relationship.

Does Gracious Withdrawal Mean Complete Silence?

The phrase alone does not determine that.

Hajr can concern avoiding company, distancing oneself from argument, or not prolonging an objectionable encounter.

Whether it involves silence, a short reply, or other conduct depends on context and actual circumstances.

The base translation “gracious withdrawal” is therefore more precise than adding detailed instructions on speaking.

16. Beauty: Appearance or Ethical Quality?

The eleven Qur’anic occurrences reveal an important distinction.

Al-Naḥl 16:6 concerns livestock’s beauty, something sensory and visible.

All seven jamīl occurrences instead attribute beauty to conduct’s quality.

Patience can be jamīl.

Forbearance can be jamīl.

Separation can be jamīl.

Withdrawal can be jamīl as well.

Qur’anic beauty therefore extends beyond objects’ shape and color.

This does not make every action pleasing to its agent necessarily jamīl.

An action may please its agent while oppressing or harming another.

In ethical use, conduct’s goodness should therefore be assessed through truth, justice, and responsibility.

J M L alone does not define all those criteria.

Jamīl describes goodness; why an act is good emerges from its subject, context, and other Qur’anic criteria.

This also matters in the independent study of Ḥ S N.

17. Distinguishing J M L from Related Roots

J M L (Jamāl) and Ḥ S N (Ḥusn)

Study of Ḥ S N

Ḥusn concerns goodness, beauty, and agreeableness in Arabic.

Jamāl’s second branch likewise concerns beauty and goodness.

The two are very close in many uses.

Ḥ S N has a broad Qur’anic field including conduct, recompense, judgment, human form, and good deeds.

J M L also has separate branches concerning camels and a unified whole.

Overlap in goodness and beauty should therefore not equate all derivatives.

Jamīl can be translated “beautiful” in some places and “gracious” or “good” in others.

J M L (Jamāl) and Z Y N (Zīnah)

Zīnah concerns adornment and what enhances beauty.

Jamāl denotes beauty itself.

Adornment may increase beauty, but beauty does not always require added ornament.

Livestock are beautiful in Al-Naḥl 16:6 without any mention of artificial adornment.

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:28, zīnatahā concerns worldly life’s adornment, while sarāḥan jamīlan concerns gracious separation.

The two families can therefore play different roles even within one verse.

J M L (Jamīl) and Ṭ Y B (Ṭayyib)

Study of Ṭ Y B

Ṭayyib concerns purity, pleasantness, agreeableness, and suitability.

Jamīl can likewise describe goodness.

They are not synonyms in every use, however.

Something visually beautiful may not be wholesome or suitable to eat or use.

Something pure and useful may not appear beautiful to everyone.

Visual beauty alone should not establish that something is ṭayyib or lawful in Qur’anic research.

J M L (Jamāl) and B H W (Bahāʾ)

Bahāʾ concerns radiance, splendor, and beauty in Arabic.

Lexical sources sometimes explain jamāl through bahāʾ.

The proximity is clear in descriptions of landscapes and sensory beings.

Ethical jamīl, as in patience and forbearance, does not concern visual radiance alone.

Not every jamāl is therefore confined to light and visible brilliance.

J M L (Jumlah) and J M ʿ (Jamʿ)

Study of J M ʿ

Jamʿ comes from J M ʿ and concerns gathering things.

Jumlah comes from J M L and denotes a thing’s collection or entirety.

Their collection senses are close.

Their consonants differ, however.

J M L ends in lām; J M ʿ ends in ʿayn.

Jumlah in Al-Furqān 25:32 also concerns the Qur’an as one whole rather than every human assembly or accumulation of wealth.

J M L (Jumlah) and K M L (Kamāl)

Study of K M L

Kamāl concerns becoming complete and reaching completion.

Jumlah concerns a body or being together.

These may approach one another without sharing one meaning.

Something complete may be presented in stages.

Something else may be presented all at once yet remain incomplete.

Al-Furqān 25:32 concerns sending the Qur’an down all at once, which should not become a question of its completeness or incompleteness.

J M L (Jamīl) and Ṣ B R (Ṣabr)

Study of Ṣ B R

Ṣabr concerns steadfastness and holding oneself to a course.

Jamīl describes its gracious quality.

Ṣabrun jamīlun contains two independent words.

Ṣabr names the conduct or state.

Jamīl describes its goodness.

Jamīl itself therefore does not mean patience, nor can every instance of patience be declared jamīl without examination.

J M L (Jamal) and N W Q (Nāqah)

Jamal principally denotes a male camel.

Nāqah names a female camel.

They concern the same animal kind but are distinct words.

The Qur’an also uses nāqah in Ṣāliḥ’s narrative.

Al-jamal in Al-Aʿrāf 7:40 should not therefore become a female camel without evidence.

J M L (Jamal) and ʾ B L (Ibil)

Study of ʾ B L

Ibil names a body of camels, including males and females.

Jamal ordinarily means a male camel.

Their naming scope consequently differs.

Persian shotor, “camel,” can suit both in general usage.

Root research should nevertheless recognize their Arabic distinction.

J M L (Jamal) and B ʿ R (Baʿīr)

Study of B ʿ R

Baʿīr denotes a camel and can often be either male or female.

Jamal predominantly denotes a male.

Baʿīr can therefore have a broader scope.

A particular animal’s sex and exact age still require context.

J M L (Jamāl) and J L L (Jalāl)

Study of J L L

Jamāl concerns beauty and goodness.

Jalāl comes from J L L and concerns greatness and majesty.

Beauty and majesty can accompany one another.

A large building may be both magnificent and beautiful, for example.

Greatness and majesty alone do not establish beauty.

An action’s beauty likewise need not involve physical or social grandeur.

The roots require separate examination.

J M L (Jamāl) and J M M (Jamm)

Study of J M M

Jamm comes from J M M and means abundant or plentiful in Al-Fajr 89:20.

Jamāl comes from J M L and means beauty in Al-Naḥl 16:6.

Their consonants resemble one another but do not form one root.

Beauty and abundance are also distinct.

Something may be plentiful without beauty, or beautiful yet small in number or amount.

18. Questions of Understanding and Translation

Does Jamal in Al-Aʿrāf 7:40 Mean Rope?

In the familiar reading, al-jamalu means a camel.

Sources record other readings concerning thick rope.

These differences should be recognized without silently displacing the familiar form and its clear meaning.

Does Jimālat in Al-Mursalāt 77:33 Necessarily Mean Camels?

Camels is the familiar reading with strong lexical support.

Reports also concern thick ropes and different recited forms.

Research should report the differences and distinguish the base translation from interpretive possibilities.

Is Qur’anic Jamīl Exclusively Visual Beauty?

No.

Its seven uses concern patience, forbearance, separation, and withdrawal.

They describe conduct’s good and gracious quality.

Arabic beauty therefore extends beyond objects’ shape and color.

Does Gracious Patience Mean No Grief?

The adjective alone does not establish that.

Jacob’s gracious patience accompanies grief, prayer, and a search for his children.

Gracious patience should not become a denial of human feelings or abandonment of responsibility.

Does Gracious Forbearance Mean Relinquishing Every Right?

No.

Jamīl describes forbearance’s quality.

Forgiveness, justice, and responsibility’s limits require their other relevant verses.

Does Gracious Separation Always Mean No Disagreement?

No.

Two people may disagree over the underlying matter yet end a relationship respectfully while honoring one another’s rights.

The goodness concerns how separation is carried out, not necessarily the absence of all disagreement.

Does Jumlatan Wāḥidatan Mean a Summary of the Qur’an?

No.

The phrase in Al-Furqān 25:32 concerns the whole being together.

The subject is the manner of revelation, not summarizing its content.

Does Livestock’s Beauty Come Only from Its Owner’s Wealth?

Al-Naḥl 16:6 locates beauty in the livestock when returning and going to pasture.

Livestock may signal wealth in some societies, but the verse explicitly says jamāl.

Beauty should therefore not become a display of property or social standing without evidence.

19. Translation Proposals for Forqan

“Beauty” suits jamāl in Al-Naḥl 16:6.

“Camel” is clear and natural for al-jamalu in Al-Aʿrāf 7:40.

“Camels” suits jimālatun in Al-Mursalāt 77:33 following the familiar recitation and reading, while lexical and reading differences concerning thick ropes belong in the study’s explanation.

“All at once” or “together” suits jumlatan wāḥidatan in Al-Furqān 25:32.

Jamīl’s equivalent depends on what the adjective describes.

Ṣabr jamīl in Yūsuf 12:18 and Yūsuf 12:83 means gracious patience.

Al-ṣafḥ al-jamīl in Al-Ḥijr 15:85 means gracious or beautiful forbearance.

Sarāḥan jamīlan in Al-Aḥzāb 33:28 and Al-Aḥzāb 33:49 means releasing graciously.

Ṣabran jamīlan in Al-Maʿārij 70:5 concerns gracious patience.

Hajran jamīlan in Al-Muzzammil 73:10 means gracious withdrawal.

These equivalents distinguish gracious conduct from visual beauty.

Non-Qur’anic derivatives also require different equivalents.

Jamula means being beautiful or good.

Jammala means beautifying or adorning.

Tajammala in behavioral use can concern good bearing and preserving dignity.

Jāmalahu concerns gracious treatment or consideration for a relationship.

Ajmala al-ḥisāba means bringing the account together as one total.

Pastoral jāmil denotes a camel herd.

Jumālī describes a large-bodied person or animal.

Al-jummal in its particular use means thick rope.

Al-jamīl in some food usages names rendered fat.

These meanings should not be exchanged across Qur’anic uses without evidence.

20. Conclusion

J M L has two important Arabic semantic branches: gathering and large bodily structure, and goodness and beauty.

Ibn Fāris presents them independently.

In the first, jumlah denotes a collection, and ajmala can mean gathering parts or totaling an account.

Jamal names the camel; Ibn Fāris proposes its connection to bodily size as a lexical possibility.

In the second, jamāl means beauty and jamīl can describe a person’s, thing’s, or action’s good quality.

Early Arabic evidence likewise illustrates this breadth.

Camel poetry concerns the animal and its characteristics.

Abū Dhuʾayb relates jamāl to gracious conduct and self-restraint.

Abū Khirāsh uses al-jamīl for rendered fat.

Shared consonants, and even identical written forms, consequently do not always imply identical usage.

The Qur’an uses the root eleven times.

Jamāl in Al-Naḥl 16:6 is livestock’s beauty.

Jamal in Al-Aʿrāf 7:40 denotes a camel.

Jimālat in Al-Mursalāt 77:33 compares sparks, with recorded lexical and reading differences concerning camels or thick ropes.

Jumlatan wāḥidatan in Al-Furqān 25:32 concerns the Qur’an as one whole in the opponents’ question.

The other seven occurrences are forms of jamīl describing conduct’s good quality.

Jacob’s patience is jamīl.

The commanded forbearance in Al-Ḥijr is described as jamīl.

Releasing wives can be practiced graciously.

Patience in Al-Maʿārij and withdrawal in Al-Muzzammil take the same adjective.

These uses show that Qur’anic beauty is not confined to objects’ form and appearance; the manner of acting can also be good and beautiful.

Jamīl should nevertheless not become the complete definition of patience, forgiveness, separation, or withdrawal.

Each has its independent root and semantic field.

Jamīl describes their good and gracious quality.

“Beauty” suits jamāl; “camel” suits jamal; “all together” or “all at once” suits jumlatan wāḥidatan; and “beautiful,” “gracious,” or “good” suits jamīl according to context.

J M L also requires distinction from related roots such as J M ʿ, J M M, K M L, Ḥ S N, and J L L.

Its defensible semantic cores therefore cannot be reduced to an invented, exceptionless concept.

One branch of J M L concerns a whole and size, while another concerns beauty and goodness; each derivative’s meaning emerges from its morphology and actual context.

Preserving these distinctions allows livestock’s beauty, camels and thick ropes, the Qur’an as one whole, and gracious patience, forbearance, and separation to be studied without semantic confusion or adding unstated interpretation to the verses.
