Cutting and separating: from advancing property for repayment to the good loan in the Qur’an

Cutting, separating, and removing part of something are important material meanings of Q R Ḍ in Arabic. Recorded usages concern cutting fabric, chewing off pieces of food, traversing a route, and setting aside part of one’s property for another. Their semantic connections are intelligible, but do not by themselves prove a precise historical sequence of development.

In financial relationships, qarḍ denotes property advanced to another with repayment of its equivalent expected. The family extends beyond monetary transactions: Arabic also uses it for good or bad actions that receive a response or recompense.

The Qur’an uses this root in two different settings: the sun passing by the companions of the cave, and a “good loan” to The God. In the latter, people are invited to give and spend their resources for good, with divine recompense promised, without The God needing their wealth.

Understanding the root requires keeping three matters distinct: physical cutting, the conventional financial loan, and Qur’anic lending to The God.

1. Qur’anic forms and basic meaning

Q R Ḍ occurs thirteen times in the Qur’an: six nominal occurrences of qarḍan, six form-IV verbs, and one basic-form verb, taqriḍuhum.

Qarḍan denotes a loan or what is given for repayment or recompense. It occurs in qarḍan ḥasanan. In grammatical analysis, it can function as a noun replacing the verbal noun of lending; its form should not be confused with the regular form-IV verbal noun iqrāḍ.

Aqraḍa is the form-IV verb meaning to lend or advance something for repayment. Its Qur’anic forms are yuqriḍu, aqraḍtumu, aqraḍū, tuqriḍū, and aqriḍū.

Taqriḍuhum is a basic-form imperfect verb in Al-Kahf 18:17, describing the sun’s movement past the companions of the cave.

These occurrences are distributed across seven verses: Al-Baqarah 2:245, Al-Māʾidah 5:12, Al-Kahf 18:17, Al-Ḥadīd 57:11, Al-Ḥadīd 57:18, Al-Taghābun 64:17, and Al-Muzzammil 73:20.

Other lexical forms include qaraḍa, miqrāḍ, qurāḍah, qarīḍ, qirāḍ, muqāraḍah, iqtaraḍa, and inqaraḍa. They belong to the family but do not occur in those exact forms in the Qur’an.

The root’s material core can be described as follows:

Cutting and separating part of something. Its extended uses include passing by and leaving behind, or advancing property or a deed for repayment or recompense; each has its own construction and context.

This clarifies the semantic connection between cutting and a financial loan. Repayment belongs to the financial usage and the parties’ relationship; not every separation is a loan.

2. Early Arabic meanings and pre-Qur’anic evidence

Cutting and separating

In early Arabic, qaraḍa al-shayʾa means cutting something, whether with a tool, teeth, or another means.

Qaraḍat al-faʾratu al-thawba concerns a mouse gnawing cloth and removing small pieces with its teeth.

Al-qurāḍah denotes scraps left by cutting, such as fabric clippings or fragments of gold.

Al-miqrāḍ names a cutting tool. Its membership in this family makes the physical cutting sense clear.

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAdī ibn Zayd al-ʿIbādī says:

كُلُّ صَعْلٍ كَأَنَّمَا شَقَّ فِيهِ
سَعَفَ الشَّرْيِ شَفْرَتَا مِقْرَاضِ

kullu ṣaʿlin ka-annamā shaqqa fīhi / saʿafa al-sharyi shafratā miqrāḍi

In describing a small-headed creature, the poet evokes cutting with two scissor blades. Shafratā miqrāḍ means “the two blades of a pair of scissors.”

The relevant evidence is miqrāḍ as a cutting tool. Attribution to a pre-Islamic poet supports the antiquity of the name and image, although the exact date of this verse is not independently established.

Here qarḍ concerns cutting that separates a portion from a whole. This helps explain related usages, particularly setting aside a portion of property.

Qarḍ in financial and social relationships

In early and classical Arabic, al-qarḍ denotes property a person sets aside and advances to another for return of its equivalent.

Two stages are involved: advancing the property and repayment.

In the classical financial usage, the lender advances property from current holdings and the recipient owes its equivalent. For money, this generally concerns the corresponding amount and kind, rather than necessarily the identical coins originally handed over. This is a lexical description of the relationship, not a ruling on every modern contract.

This differs from an outright gift. An ordinary gift does not require the recipient to return its equivalent; repayment is integral to a loan relationship.

Arabic also records a broader use for an action done for another that receives a reciprocal response or recompense. Its connection with lending is clear, without establishing the exact historical order of the two usages.

Qarḍ as an action calling for reciprocation

A verse by Labīd ibn Rabīʿah says:

وَإِذَا جُوزِيتَ قَرْضًا فَاجْزِهِ
إِنَّمَا يَجْزِي الْفَتَى لَيْسَ الْجَمَلْ

wa-idhā jūzīta qarḍan fa-jzihi / innamā yajzī al-fatā laysa al-jamal

“When a kindness is done to you, repay it; it is the person of character who reciprocates, not the camel.”

Qarḍ here is not confined to a monetary transaction. The verse concerns conduct and kindness deserving a fitting response.

Labīd lived both before and after the emergence of Islam. Since this verse’s date is not independently established, it cannot confidently be classified as pre-Qur’anic; it nevertheless illustrates the wider use of qarḍ in his poetry and the literary tradition.

A verse attributed to Umayyah ibn Abī al-Ṣalt also says:

كُلُّ امْرِئٍ سَوْفَ يُجْزَى قَرْضَهُ حَسَنًا
أَوْ سَيِّئًا وَمَدِينًا كَالَّذِي دَانَا

kullu imriʾin sawfa yujzā qarḍahu ḥasanan / aw sayyiʾan wa-madīnan ka-alladhī dānā

“Every person will receive recompense for what he has advanced, whether good or bad, and will be dealt with as he has dealt with others.”

Here qarḍ extends to conduct and its recompense. It can be good or bad in this usage, so positive moral value is not an invariable part of the word itself.

The attribution and dating of portions of Umayyah’s poetry are debated. This verse can be used as an example transmitted in Arabic literature, but its authenticity and date would need establishing before claiming that this use certainly preceded the Qur’an.

These lexical and poetic usages show both physical cutting and applications to property and reciprocal conduct. The Qur’an employs the same semantic field in the expression “good loan” in the relationship between humans and The God.

Other branches

Qarīḍ can mean poetry, and qaraḍa al-shiʿra is attested for composing poetry. A connection with cutting or measuring speech is explicable, but poetry should not therefore be translated literally as “something cut.”

Qirāḍ and muqāraḍah in commercial usage denote an arrangement in which an owner supplies capital for another to trade with, sharing profit as agreed. This differs from an ordinary loan: it concerns commercial activity and profit-sharing rather than merely returning an equivalent advance.

Inqaraḍa is also used for a group or generation coming to an end, as though its continuity had been severed.

These attested Arabic branches should not be imposed without contextual support on the verses about a good loan or the sun’s movement.

3. First Qur’anic usage: taqriḍuhum and the companions of the cave

Al-Kahf 18:17

وَتَرَى الشَّمْسَ إِذَا طَلَعَتْ تَزَاوَرُ عَنْ كَهْفِهِمْ ذَاتَ الْيَمِينِ وَإِذَا غَرَبَتْ تَقْرِضُهُمْ ذَاتَ الشِّمَالِ وَهُمْ فِي فَجْوَةٍ مِنْهُ

wa-tarā al-shamsa idhā ṭalaʿat tazāwaru ʿan kahfihim dhāta al-yamīni wa-idhā gharabat taqriḍuhum dhāta al-shimāli wa-hum fī fajwatin minhu

Al-Kahf 18:17

“You see the sun, when it rises, inclining away from their cave toward the right, and when it sets, passing them on the left and leaving them aside, while they are in a spacious part of it.”

Taqriḍuhum belongs to the basic verb qaraḍa, not the form-IV financial lending construction.

Arabic records qaraḍa al-makāna for traversing or passing a place. Qaraḍahu dhāta al-yamīn or dhāta al-shimāl can describe passing to its right or left, leaving it aside from the course of movement.

The verse uses tazāwaru for sunrise and taqriḍuhum for sunset. The former concerns inclining away; the latter highlights passing by and leaving behind.

Dhāta al-shimāl specifies the direction. “Left” comes from that phrase, not from Q R Ḍ itself.

Lexical explanations gloss taqriḍuhum as leaving them aside or passing by; related constructions also describe moving alongside one side. The shared feature is the sun’s movement relative to their location, not cutting their bodies.

“The sun cut them” or “the sun’s rays cut them off” would therefore misrepresent the passage: it does not concern bodily cutting or severing light.

“Passed them on the left” or “passed by toward their left, leaving them aside” better suits this context.

The root alone cannot establish the cave entrance’s exact compass direction, the amount of sunlight, or its architecture. The verse describes the sun’s movement relative to their location; further details require other parts of the verse or independent evidence.

This usage shows that Qur’anic Q R Ḍ is not confined to transactions and loans; its movement branch is also present.

4. Second Qur’anic usage: lending to The God

In the other six verses, the root appears in lending to The God. Each occurrence of the loan expression includes ḥasanan, good.

Al-Baqarah 2:245: a loan multiplied many times

مَنْ ذَا الَّذِي يُقْرِضُ اللَّهَ قَرْضًا حَسَنًا فَيُضَاعِفَهُ لَهُ أَضْعَافًا كَثِيرَةً

man dhā alladhī yuqriḍu Allāha qarḍan ḥasanan fa-yuḍāʿifahu lahu aḍʿāfan kathīrah

Al-Baqarah 2:245

“Who will lend The God a good loan, so that He may multiply it for him many times over?”

The verse invites a person to advance something from their resources in His way and promises multiplied recompense.

This is not an ordinary loan between two humans. The God does not need wealth or request it to remedy a financial deficiency.

Fāṭir 35:15 says:

يَا أَيُّهَا النَّاسُ أَنْتُمُ الْفُقَرَاءُ إِلَى اللَّهِ وَاللَّهُ هُوَ الْغَنِيُّ الْحَمِيدُ

yā ayyuhā al-nāsu antumu al-fuqarāʾu ilā Allāhi wa-Allāhu huwa al-ghaniyyu al-ḥamīd

Fāṭir 35:15

“People, you are the ones in need of The God, and The God is the Self-Sufficient, the Praiseworthy.”

Lending to The God therefore uses a familiar loan relationship to express the value of human action and the assurance of its recompense.

Someone giving from their property for good may appear to lose part of their holdings, but the Qur’an does not present such giving as fruitless in relation to divine reward.

Al-Baqarah 2:245 also has a context: Al-Baqarah 2:244 concerns fighting in The God’s way, and spending to support that way is intelligible here. Other occurrences show, however, that the good loan is not restricted to expenditure on fighting.

The verse continues:

وَاللَّهُ يَقْبِضُ وَيَبْسُطُ وَإِلَيْهِ تُرْجَعُونَ

wa-Allāhu yaqbiḍu wa-yabsuṭu wa-ilayhi turjaʿūn

Al-Baqarah 2:245

“The God constricts and expands, and to Him you will be returned.”

This connects property and giving with The God’s authority over constriction and expansion, and with humanity’s final return to Him.

Aḍʿāfan kathīratan does not establish that every donation will return to the giver’s worldly finances at a fixed rate. The verse promises multiplied recompense, not a commercial contract guaranteeing earthly profit.

Al-Māʾidah 5:12: the good loan in the covenant with the Children of Israel

وَآمَنْتُمْ بِرُسُلِي وَعَزَّرْتُمُوهُمْ وَأَقْرَضْتُمُ اللَّهَ قَرْضًا حَسَنًا لَأُكَفِّرَنَّ عَنْكُمْ سَيِّئَاتِكُمْ

wa-āmantum bi-rusulī wa-ʿazzartumūhum wa-aqraḍtumu Allāha qarḍan ḥasanan la-ukaffiranna ʿankum sayyiʾātikum

Al-Māʾidah 5:12

“And believe in My messengers, support them, and lend The God a good loan, I will surely remove your misdeeds from you.”

This is part of the covenant with the Children of Israel. The verse also mentions establishing ṣalāh, giving zakāh, believing in the messengers, and supporting them.

The good loan stands alongside religious, ethical, and social commitments. This shows that professed belief or loyalty alone is not the whole covenant; action and giving from one’s resources are included.

Aqraḍtumu Allāha qarḍan ḥasanan follows ātaytumu al-zakāh. The two expressions should not be treated as completely identical without evidence: zakāh is named and the good loan is mentioned separately.

Their separate mention does not by itself establish a distinct financial obligation with a fixed amount. That would require further evidence.

The expression is not restricted in the Qur’anic account to the Prophet’s contemporary Muslim community; it also appears in the covenant with the Children of Israel.

Al-Ḥadīd 57:11 and Al-Ḥadīd 57:18: lending, giving, and a generous reward

Al-Ḥadīd 57:11 says:

مَنْ ذَا الَّذِي يُقْرِضُ اللَّهَ قَرْضًا حَسَنًا فَيُضَاعِفَهُ لَهُ وَلَهُ أَجْرٌ كَرِيمٌ

man dhā alladhī yuqriḍu Allāha qarḍan ḥasanan fa-yuḍāʿifahu lahu wa-lahu ajrun karīm

Al-Ḥadīd 57:11

“Who will lend The God a good loan, so that He may multiply it for him, and he will have a generous reward?”

Its structure closely resembles Al-Baqarah 2:245, but ends by mentioning ajrun karīm, a generous reward.

Al-Ḥadīd 57:7 also says:

وَأَنْفِقُوا مِمَّا جَعَلَكُمْ مُسْتَخْلَفِينَ فِيهِ

wa-anfiqū mimmā jaʿalakum mustakhlafīna fīhi

Al-Ḥadīd 57:7

“Spend from that over which He has made you successors.”

The good loan in this surah thus concerns giving from resources and property placed at human disposal.

Al-Ḥadīd 57:18 says:

إِنَّ الْمُصَّدِّقِينَ وَالْمُصَّدِّقَاتِ وَأَقْرَضُوا اللَّهَ قَرْضًا حَسَنًا يُضَاعَفُ لَهُمْ وَلَهُمْ أَجْرٌ كَرِيمٌ

inna al-muṣṣaddiqīna wa-al-muṣṣaddiqāti wa-aqraḍū Allāha qarḍan ḥasanan yuḍāʿafu lahum wa-lahum ajrun karīm

Al-Ḥadīd 57:18

“The men and women who give charity and lend The God a good loan—it will be multiplied for them, and they will have a generous reward.”

Men and women appear together and both receive the promise of reward.

Charitable giving alongside the good loan establishes a close connection with generosity and spending. Their association alone does not prove that every charitable gift, whatever its motive or manner, is the good loan praised here.

Ḥasan remains part of the expression and raises the question of the action’s quality.

Al-Taghābun 64:17: the good loan and forgiveness

إِنْ تُقْرِضُوا اللَّهَ قَرْضًا حَسَنًا يُضَاعِفْهُ لَكُمْ وَيَغْفِرْ لَكُمْ وَاللَّهُ شَكُورٌ حَلِيمٌ

in tuqriḍū Allāha qarḍan ḥasanan yuḍāʿifhu lakum wa-yaghfir lakum wa-Allāhu shakūrun ḥalīm

Al-Taghābun 64:17

“If you lend The God a good loan, He will multiply it for you and forgive you; The God is Appreciative, Forbearing.”

Forgiveness is mentioned alongside multiplication.

The preceding verse, Al-Taghābun 64:16, says:

فَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ مَا اسْتَطَعْتُمْ وَاسْمَعُوا وَأَطِيعُوا وَأَنْفِقُوا خَيْرًا لِأَنْفُسِكُمْ

fa-ittaqū Allāha mā istaṭaʿtum wa-ismaʿū wa-aṭīʿū wa-anfiqū khayran li-anfusikum

Al-Taghābun 64:16

“Be mindful of The God as far as you are able; listen, obey, and spend—it is better for yourselves.”

The good loan thus appears within mindfulness, obedience, and spending.

Khayran li-anfusikum makes clear that the benefit of such giving returns to the giver. Giving in The God’s way adds nothing to His self-sufficiency; it sends good ahead for the person.

Al-Muzzammil 73:20: the good loan and sending good ahead

وَأَقِيمُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَآتُوا الزَّكَاةَ وَأَقْرِضُوا اللَّهَ قَرْضًا حَسَنًا

wa-aqīmū al-ṣalāta wa-ātū al-zakāta wa-aqriḍū Allāha qarḍan ḥasanan

Al-Muzzammil 73:20

“Establish ṣalāh, give zakāh, and lend The God a good loan.”

It continues:

وَمَا تُقَدِّمُوا لِأَنْفُسِكُمْ مِنْ خَيْرٍ تَجِدُوهُ عِنْدَ اللَّهِ هُوَ خَيْرًا وَأَعْظَمَ أَجْرًا

wa-mā tuqaddimū li-anfusikum min khayrin tajidūhu ʿinda Allāhi huwa khayran wa-aʿẓama ajran

Al-Muzzammil 73:20

“Whatever good you send ahead for yourselves, you will find it with The God, better and greater in reward.”

This makes the connection between the good loan and advancing good explicit.

People devote some of their resources to good today, while its fruit is preserved with The God.

As in Al-Māʾidah 5:12, the good loan appears alongside ṣalāh and zakāh. This repeated association connects worship, giving zakāh, and good giving.

The expression itself does not specify an amount, payment date, recipient, or single form of giving. Those details must come from the relevant rulings and contexts, not from Q R Ḍ alone.

5. Qarḍan ḥasanan: what makes the loan good?

The expression has two elements: qarḍ concerns what is given for repayment or recompense, and ḥasan specifies its good and worthy quality.

Goodness should not be reduced to a large amount. It concerns what is given, intention, manner of giving, and accompanying conduct.

Verses on spending help explain this quality.

Good giving is not only a matter of quantity

Al-Baqarah 2:267 says:

أَنْفِقُوا مِنْ طَيِّبَاتِ مَا كَسَبْتُمْ

anfiqū min ṭayyibāti mā kasabtum

Al-Baqarah 2:267

“Spend from the good things you have earned.”

The quality of what is given matters. Merely handing over something one considers undesirable or worthless does not make it equal to the finest gift.

Al-Baqarah 2:262 says:

ثُمَّ لَا يُتْبِعُونَ مَا أَنْفَقُوا مَنًّا وَلَا أَذًى

thumma lā yutbiʿūna mā anfaqū mannan wa-lā adhan

Al-Baqarah 2:262

“Then they do not follow what they have spent with reminders of their generosity or injury.”

Conduct after giving also matters. Humiliation, reproach, or injury can damage the ethical value of a gift.

Al-Baqarah 2:264 also forbids nullifying charity through reminders and injury and warns against giving to be seen by people.

Read alongside these verses, the good loan accords with goodness in motive, property, and conduct. These features arise from reading the verses together, not from supposing that qarḍ alone lexically contains every one of them.

Is the good loan only financial giving?

Its association with spending and giving property is clear in the six verses about lending to The God. Their principal contextual sense should not be detached from material giving.

Arabic also uses qarḍ more broadly for good or bad actions receiving recompense. After commanding the good loan, Al-Muzzammil 73:20 speaks of whatever good people send ahead for themselves.

Recompense and advancing good therefore belong to the expression’s wider field, but that breadth should not erase the clear financial setting of the spending passages.

Lending to The God and the nature of His promise

In a financial loan between people, the lender has a claim to repayment and the borrower undertakes to return an equivalent.

In the Qur’anic expression, The God is not a borrower through need or deficiency. He Himself promises multiplied recompense for good giving.

The distinction is fundamental.

Giving property does not place The God under a human commercial contract or entitle someone to demand a specified return at a preferred time.

The good loan concerns trust in the divine promise and sending good ahead. Reward is promised by The God, not negotiated through a commercial contract between two parties sharing a need.

Multiplied reward is not equivalent to monetary interest. Fa-yuḍāʿifahu speaks of increased divine recompense and cannot be converted into a contractual return on human loans.

6. Distinctions from related roots

Q R Ḍ and Q Ṭ ʿ

Qaṭʿ has the broad meanings of cutting, separating, and severing.

Qarḍ in its physical branch is also cutting, with familiar usages emphasizing removing portions, gnawing, and cutting away pieces; financial usage concerns advancing part of one’s resources.

Physical qarḍ involves a kind of cutting, but not every cutting is called qarḍ.

Qaṭʿ can concern cutting off a route, ending a relationship, or completely separating something; a financial loan concerns advancing property for repayment.

Q R Ḍ and D Y N

Dayn in financial usage denotes an obligation owed by a person and due for discharge.

Al-Baqarah 2:282 says:

إِذَا تَدَايَنْتُمْ بِدَيْنٍ إِلَىٰ أَجَلٍ مُسَمًّى فَاكْتُبُوهُ

idhā tadāyantum bi-daynin ilā ajalin musamman fa-uktubūhu

Al-Baqarah 2:282

“When you contract a debt with one another for a specified term, write it down.”

Qarḍ and dayn are related but not fully synonymous.

Qarḍ is advancing property for return of its equivalent; dayn is the resulting obligation owed. Debt can arise from a loan, but also from another source, such as an unpaid purchase price.

A financial loan therefore ordinarily creates a repayment obligation, but not every debt originates in borrowing.

Q R Ḍ and N F Q

Qur’anic infāq concerns spending or disbursing property or resources.

Someone spending uses or transfers resources; infāq alone does not express an obligation on the recipient to repay.

The good loan to The God belongs to this field of giving and spending, but uses the image of a loan and promised recompense.

The difference is one of emphasis: infāq highlights disbursement, while the good loan highlights giving’s value and the divine promise of recompense.

Q R Ḍ and Ṣ D Q

Ṣadaqah in financial usage concerns a gift associated with goodness, sincerity, and helping others.

Its recipient is not ordinarily required to repay it. In the good-loan expression, the Qur’an uses lending imagery to promise the giver recompense from The God.

Al-Ḥadīd 57:18 places both together, showing their close connection.

This does not turn every charitable gift into a financial loan, or require a needy recipient to repay what was given as a good loan to The God.

Q R Ḍ and S L F

Salafa concerns preceding or having occurred earlier.

The Qur’an uses this family in expressions such as mā qad salafa for what has already passed.

A loan may be advanced now and repaid later, but the basic element of S L F is temporal precedence rather than a particular financial obligation.

Some commercial usages of salaf and qarḍ can converge without their basic meanings becoming identical.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Translations should distinguish the movement usage in Al-Kahf from the financial and ethical usages elsewhere.

Taqriḍuhum in Al-Kahf 18:17 can be rendered “passes them by,” “leaves them behind,” or, including the direction supplied by the verse, “passes them on the left, leaving them aside.”

Yuqriḍu Allāha in Al-Baqarah 2:245 and Al-Ḥadīd 57:11 directly means “lends The God.” The contextual explanation can clarify this without replacing “lending” with “spending” in the translation itself.

“A good loan” or “a worthy loan” suits qarḍan ḥasanan. “Good giving in hope of divine reward” is a conceptual explanation, not a direct translation of every word.

Aqraḍtumu Allāha in Al-Māʾidah 5:12 is morphologically past-tense, but its conditional setting can call for “you lend The God.” Aqraḍū Allāha in Al-Ḥadīd 57:18 means “they lent The God”; tuqriḍū Allāha in Al-Taghābun 64:17 is conditional “you lend The God”; aqriḍū Allāha in Al-Muzzammil 73:20 is the command “lend The God.”

For non-Qur’anic forms, physical qaraḍa means “cut” or “cut off a piece”; miqrāḍ, “scissors”; qurāḍah, “cuttings”; qarīḍ, “poetry”; and inqaraḍa, “came to an end” or “became extinct.”

Keeping “loan” in the good-loan verses preserves the linguistic image connecting giving and divine recompense. Replacing every occurrence with “charity” or “spending” would remove part of that image.

Conclusion

The material core of Q R Ḍ is cutting and separating. Arabic records tool-cutting, chewing off pieces, traversing a course, and setting aside property within this family. These semantic connections do not alone establish a certain chronological derivation for every branch.

A financial loan advances property for repayment of its equivalent. More broadly, qarḍ can denote an action for which someone receives a response or recompense.

Taqriḍuhum in the cave narrative concerns the sun passing by its occupants. It cannot be reduced to a financial loan or bodily cutting.

Six other verses speak of a good loan to The God. This concerns giving and advancing good, particularly from one’s resources, with divine recompense promised. Some verses explicitly mention multiplication; Al-Māʾidah 5:12 emphasizes removal of misdeeds and entry into gardens.

Ḥasan highlights the quality of the giving. The spending verses show that goodness involves more than quantity: the quality of what is given, intention, avoiding reproach and injury, and respect for others’ rights matter too.

Lending to The God does not imply divine need or make Him a human financial debtor. The familiar relationship of lending and repayment expresses the value of good giving and the assurance of divine recompense.

Final definition for Forqan:

The physical core of Q R Ḍ concerns cutting and separating a portion. In financial usage, qarḍ is property advanced from one’s holdings for repayment. Other usages concern passing by and leaving behind, or an action or gift that receives recompense.

In Qur’anic qarḍan ḥasanan, the familiar loan meaning combines with goodness and the promise of divine recompense: people advance some of their resources for good, and The God promises its reward.
