Going astray, departing from sound judgment, and failing to find the right way: from Adam's lapse to Satan's misleading and human responsibility in following

In early Arabic, gh-w-y concerns going astray, departing from the right path, and taking a course that does not lead to sound judgment or a desirable outcome. In its familiar use, ghayy contrasts with rushd: someone may not recognize a sound and beneficial way, depart from it, or knowingly follow a wrong course.

Ghawā means going astray, leaving the right path, and, in some uses, failing. Ghāwī describes someone in that condition; aghwā concerns misleading another or drawing them onto a wrong path.

Arabic sources also record corruption and deterioration in this family. For example, ghawiya is used of a young camel or lamb harmed and weakened by an unsuitable intake of milk.

These fields—departure from sound judgment and deterioration of a condition—have an intelligible relationship. Without historical evidence, however, they should not be reduced to one original meaning.

The Quran uses several forms of this root, including ghayy, ghawā, ghāwī, ghawiyy, and aghwā.

Their contexts vary considerably. The Quran discusses the distinction between rushd and ghayy, Adam's lapse, Satan's misleading, people who lead others astray, following such leaders, and the outcome for those who remain on a wrong path.

A central distinction is that going astray, leading someone else astray, and knowingly persisting in error are not identical situations.

Someone may lapse through ignorance or deception and then correct the error. Another person may recognize a wrong path, follow it, and draw others in the same direction.

Both involve departure from the right way, but moral responsibility depends on knowledge, the choices made, and the response to truth.

1. The root in early Arabic and ancient witnesses

Early Arabic uses ghayy in opposition to rushd. Rushd concerns recognizing the right course, acting judiciously, and reaching an outcome conducive to well-being; ghayy denotes departure from that course.

In some uses, ghawā also concerns failure or not attaining a desired outcome. This shows that its field is not restricted to ignorance of a route: the unsuccessful result of a choice may also be relevant.

A poetic witness attributed to Al-Muraqqish al-Aṣghar

Al-Muraqqish al-Aṣghar, a pre-Islamic poet, says:

فَمَنْ يَلْقَ خَيْرًا يَحْمَدِ النَّاسُ أَمْرَهُ
وَمَنْ يَغْوِ لَا يَعْدَمْ عَلَى الْغَيِّ لَائِمَا

fa-man yalqa khayran yaḥmadi al-nāsu amrahu / wa-man yaghwi lā yaʿdam ʿalā al-ghayyi lāʾimā

Translation: “Whoever encounters good has his conduct praised by people; whoever goes astray will not lack someone to blame him for his error.”

Here, yaghwi and al-ghayy stand against good and commendable conduct. Some lexical explanations emphasize failure in one's pursuit, so the verse should not be restricted to deliberate moral deviation.

The poet compares someone whose course and action lead to good with someone who strays or fails.

This witness shows that early ghayy was not confined to losing a geographical route. It could concern human conduct and choices.

The negative outcome is also prominent: the person who goes astray exposes themselves to blame.

The verse is recorded in early poetry collections among poems attributed to Al-Muraqqish al-Aṣghar. Its attribution is established in literary tradition, but the surviving text comes through later compilations; a contemporary record of its composition has not been established here.

It is therefore valuable evidence for usage attributed to pre-Quranic Arabic, without establishing the root's precise date of origin.

Recognizing error yet continuing on a wrong course

Durayd ibn al-Ṣimmah, an Arab poet and warrior who lived through the pre-Islamic period and the beginning of Islam, says of accompanying his tribe:

وَمَا أَنَا إِلَّا مِنْ غَزِيَّةَ إِنْ غَوَتْ
غَوَيْتُ وَإِنْ تَرْشُدْ غَزِيَّةُ أَرْشُدِ

wa-mā anā illā min Ghaziyyata in ghawat / ghawaytu wa-in tarshud Ghaziyyatu arshudi

Translation: “I am only one of Ghaziyyah: if it goes astray, I go astray; if Ghaziyyah takes the right course, I take it too.”

This verse is particularly important for understanding ghayy.

The poet speaks of remaining with his tribe even when its course is wrong.

Ghawat contrasts with tarshud, and ghawaytu with arshudi.

The opposition between ghayy and rushd is therefore explicit in the poem's construction.

There is another point: the speaker is not describing only ignorance of the right way. He acknowledges that his tribe may go astray while still identifying himself with it.

This example shows how ghayy may relate to group loyalty, partisanship, and continuing on a wrong path.

Going astray does not always mean complete ignorance. Someone may recognize right and wrong yet remain on a wrong course because of family ties, tribal affiliation, or personal interest.

The verse is transmitted in historical and literary accounts of Durayd ibn al-Ṣimmah. An account relates its composition to an event in his pre-Islamic life, though the surviving text still comes through later compilations.

It is thus an important witness to the early opposition of ghayy and rushd, without treating its earliest recorded wording as certain.

Another branch: deterioration in a young camel's condition

Early Arabic sources also record ghawiya al-faṣīlu of a young camel.

Faṣīl primarily names a young camel separated from its mother at weaning. In the milk-related examples, its age or stage must be understood from the account; the word does not simply mean every suckling animal.

Some lexical explanations describe a young camel suffering heaviness and internal harm after drinking too much milk.

Others use the form for one receiving insufficient milk, becoming weak and thin, and possibly collapsing.

This difference concerning the physical cause matters.

Some lexicographers describe overfeeding and its harm; others describe insufficient milk and resulting weakness.

One cannot therefore confidently identify this branch's original meaning exclusively with overeating or hunger.

The shared element is harm and deterioration in the animal's normal condition.

This use concerns a living being's physical condition, not religious guidance or moral choice.

It does not follow that all Quranic uses of ghawā originally mean hunger, overeating, or digestive illness.

Nor can the moral sense of going astray be claimed to have necessarily developed from a young camel's illness without sufficient historical evidence.

Early sources record both branches together, but their historical order is unclear.

What the historical evidence shows

The linguistic witnesses suggest two main fields:

First, departure from rushd and the right course, whether through a mistaken understanding, a wrong choice, or following a path with an undesirable outcome.

Second, corruption or deterioration of a condition, especially in recorded descriptions of harm to a living being.

The first branch is central in Quranic usage.

Failure and an undesirable outcome also matter in some verses; going astray should not be reduced to not knowing the way.

2. Core meaning and root forms

Gh-w-y contains two weak radicals. Its wāw and yāʾ appear in different forms according to the verb's morphological pattern.

The basic verbal pattern for going astray is:

غَوَى يَغْوِي غَيًّا

That is, to go astray, depart from the right way, or take a wrong course.

The family includes the following forms:

غَيّ: going astray and departure from rushd; in some contexts, the resulting adverse outcome.

غَوَى: went astray, followed a wrong course, or failed to attain the right outcome.

غَوَايَة: misguidance and the condition of departing from the right way.

غَاوٍ: someone who has departed from the right course.

غَاوُون and غَاوِينَ: plural forms of ghāwī; those astray or on a wrong path.

غَوِيّ: someone astray; a description that can convey the intensity or evident character of the deviation in context.

أَغْوَى: misled someone or drew them onto a wrong course.

يُغْوِي: leads astray.

إِغْوَاء: misleading another and drawing them onto a wrong path.

مُغْوٍ: one who leads astray.

تَغَاوَى: in some Arabic uses, a group's gathering or joining together in wrongful activity.

مَغْوَاة: a recorded term for a pit prepared by a hunter to trap an animal.

Ghawiya is also recorded concerning deterioration in the condition of young camels and other living beings.

These uses should not be merged into a single moral definition without examination.

Five principal Quranic forms

The root occurs 22 times in 18 Quranic verses, in five morphological groups: three Form I verbs, eight Form IV verbs, four instances of ghayy, six of ghāwī, and one of ghawiyy.

Ghayy occurs in Al-Baqarah 2:256, Al-Aʿrāf 7:146, Al-Aʿrāf 7:202, and Maryam 19:59.

Ghawā occurs in Ṭā Hā 20:121, Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:63, and Al-Najm 53:2.

Aghwā and its inflections occur in Al-Aʿrāf 7:16, Hūd 11:34, Al-Ḥijr 15:39, Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:63, Al-Ṣāffāt 37:32, and Ṣād 38:82.

Ghāwūn and ghāwīn occur in Al-Aʿrāf 7:175, Al-Ḥijr 15:42, Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:91, Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:94, Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:224, and Al-Ṣāffāt 37:32.

Ghawiyy occurs in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:18.

An important morphological distinction runs through these uses:

Ghawā concerns the person who has gone astray.

Aghwā concerns an agent who leads someone else astray.

Ghāwī describes the person on the wrong course.

Ghayy denotes the condition of misguidance or, in some contexts, its consequence.

A single undifferentiated translation should not erase these roles: the acting subject, the misleading agent, and the person who has gone astray.

3. Ghayy opposed to rushd: right and wrong courses

Al-Baqarah 2:256

لَا إِكْرَاهَ فِي الدِّينِ ۖ قَدْ تَبَيَّنَ الرُّشْدُ مِنَ الْغَيِّ ۚ فَمَنْ يَكْفُرْ بِالطَّاغُوتِ وَيُؤْمِنْ بِاللَّهِ فَقَدِ اسْتَمْسَكَ بِالْعُرْوَةِ الْوُثْقَىٰ لَا انْفِصَامَ لَهَا ۗ وَاللَّهُ سَمِيعٌ عَلِيمٌ

lā ikrāha fī al-dīni qad tabayyana al-rushdu mina al-ghayyi fa-man yakfur bi-al-ṭāghūti wa-yuʾmin bi-allāhi fa-qadi istamsaka bi-al-ʿurwati al-wuthqā lā infiṣāma lahā wa-allāhu samīʿun ʿalīm

Al-Baqarah 2:256

Translation: “There is no compulsion in religion; the way of sound guidance has become distinct from error. Whoever rejects false authority and believes in The God has grasped the firmest handhold, which cannot break. The God is Hearing, Knowing.”

This is among the clearest Quranic witnesses for ghayy, because it stands directly against al-rushd.

Rushd concerns recognizing and following a right, judicious course.

Ghayy denotes a course that departs from such sound direction.

Here, ghayy is not merely lack of knowledge: it contrasts with a clear course about which a person can decide.

Rushd and ghayy are different paths

Qad tabayyana al-rushdu mina al-ghayyi speaks of a clear distinction between sound guidance and error.

The absence of compulsion in religion therefore does not imply that truth and falsehood are identical.

People can have freedom of choice while remaining responsible for the rightness or wrongness of their course.

After distinguishing rushd from ghayy, the verse speaks of rejecting false authority and believing in The God.

Human choice in relation to guidance and false authority thus becomes significant.

Ghayy itself, however, does not mean compulsion, false authority, or disbelief. These are expressed elsewhere in the verse and are thematically connected.

An uninformed person, an inquirer, or someone to whom the truth is not yet clear should not be labeled knowingly astray merely for lacking a particular belief.

Knowledge and choice matter when assessing moral responsibility.

For further study of compulsion and choice, the complete context of Al-Baqarah 2:256 should be considered alongside its distinction between rushd and ghayy.

The path of ghayy in Al-Aʿrāf 7:146

Al-Aʿrāf 7:146 describes people who do not adopt the path of sound guidance when they see it, but adopt the path of error when they see that.

Sabīla al-ghayyi stands against sabīla al-rushdi.

This use shows that ghayy can describe the direction a person chooses.

The context is not simply someone unaware of the right way: these people encounter divine signs yet choose a wrong course through rejection and heedlessness.

In this situation, going astray involves conduct and choice.

Continuing in ghayy in Al-Aʿrāf 7:202

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:201, the mindful remember when touched by a prompting from Satan and regain clear sight.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:202 then describes being drawn further into error without restraint. Explanations differ over the pronouns' referents; the shared point is continuing assistance in misguidance, so every pronoun should not simply be assigned to the misled humans.

Fī al-ghayyi describes a condition in which deviation continues.

The contrast between these situations matters.

Someone may face temptation or error yet correct their course as soon as they recognize it.

Another may accept the prompting, continue the wrong behavior, and go further astray.

Merely encountering temptation, or even beginning to lapse, is not identical to persisting in ghayy.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:175 also describes someone given divine signs who detached himself from them; Satan then pursued him, and he became one of those astray.

This is another example of deviation after receiving signs.

It does not justify one judgment about the intentions of everyone who errs. The verse describes an individual with particular conduct and circumstances.

4. Ghawā: Adam's lapse and the denial of error concerning Muhammad

A notable Quranic feature is that ghawā is used of Adam but negated concerning Muhammad.

Both uses must be understood within their respective contexts.

Adam disobeyed and went astray

Ṭā Hā 20:121

فَأَكَلَا مِنْهَا فَبَدَتْ لَهُمَا سَوْآتُهُمَا وَطَفِقَا يَخْصِفَانِ عَلَيْهِمَا مِنْ وَرَقِ الْجَنَّةِ ۚ وَعَصَىٰ آدَمُ رَبَّهُ فَغَوَىٰ

fa-akalā minhā fa-badat lahumā sawʾātuhumā wa-ṭafiqā yakhṣifāni ʿalayhimā min waraqi al-jannati wa-ʿaṣā Ādamu rabbahu fa-ghawā

Ṭā Hā 20:121

Translation: “They both ate from it, and their nakedness became apparent to them. They began covering themselves with leaves from the garden. Adam disobeyed his Lord and went astray.”

Fa-ghawā is a past-tense verb from gh-w-y.

The particle fa- connects it to Adam's disobedience.

ʿAṣā belongs to ʿ-ṣ-y and concerns disobedience.

Ghawā concerns deviation and the adverse outcome of that behavior.

The verbs are semantically close but not identical.

Disobedience was Adam's action against his Lord's command; ghayy describes the resulting wrong condition or outcome.

Ṭā Hā 20:115 mentions Adam's forgetting and the absence of resolve in him.

Ṭā Hā 20:120 describes Satan's prompting and promise of the tree of immortality and an unfailing dominion.

Adam's lapse thus occurs amid forgetting, temptation, desire for something attractive, and disobedience.

Fa-ghawā should not be detached from that context and taken to mean that Adam had always been an enemy of truth or one who misled others.

The verse reports a real error and deviation, but it does not settle Adam's whole destiny.

Adam's return and guidance

Ṭā Hā 20:122

ثُمَّ اجْتَبَاهُ رَبُّهُ فَتَابَ عَلَيْهِ وَهَدَىٰ

thumma ijtabāhu rabbuhu fa-tāba ʿalayhi wa-hadā

Ṭā Hā 20:122

Translation: “Then his Lord chose him, turned toward him, and guided him.”

This verse immediately follows the account of Adam's disobedience and ghayy.

It describes his being chosen, The God's turning toward him, and his guidance.

Adam's condition of ghayy is therefore not presented as permanent and unchangeable.

He lapsed, but the story did not end with the lapse.

This distinction matters for the root:

Ghawā may describe a real lapse without implying that the person remains in that condition forever.

In Al-Baqarah 2:37, Adam also receives words from his Lord, and The God turns toward him.

The connection gives recognition of error, return, and guidance a real place in the continuation of the story.

The use of ghawā for Adam does not place everyone who has lapsed on the same level as those who knowingly lead others astray.

Denying ḍalāl and ghayy concerning Muhammad

Al-Najm 53:2

مَا ضَلَّ صَاحِبُكُمْ وَمَا غَوَىٰ

mā ḍalla ṣāḥibukum wa-mā ghawā

Al-Najm 53:2

Translation: “Your companion has not strayed, nor has he taken a wrong course.”

Two verbs appear together:

Ḍalla, from ḍ-l-l.

Ghawā, from gh-w-y.

The Quran negates both concerning Muhammad.

Their combination places them in neighboring semantic fields without requiring exact synonymy.

Ḍalāl can concern losing or lacking the right way.

Ghayy often concerns departing from rushd and following a wrong course.

In Al-Najm, this denial is followed by discussion of revelation and receiving the divine message.

The central context is therefore the soundness of Muhammad's course and message in receiving and conveying revelation.

This lexical discussion alone does not settle every theological question about all his human statements, decisions, or conduct. The verse must be understood in its revelatory context alongside other evidence.

Other verses directly guide, warn, and, in some matters, correct him; examples include Al-Tawbah 9:43 and Al-Taḥrīm 66:1.

The denial of error in this context should therefore be distinguished from a comprehensive discussion of later theological doctrines concerning protection from human error.

“You are clearly astray” in the story of Moses

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:18, Moses addresses the man who had sought his help the previous day and is involved in another conflict:

إِنَّكَ لَغَوِيٌّ مُبِينٌ

innaka la-ghawiyyun mubīn

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:18

That is: “You are clearly on a wrong course.”

Ghawiyy describes someone whose behavior Moses regards as plainly misguided.

The statement is reported from Moses in a particular situation.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:15, the same man requested help, and Moses' intervention led to another man's death.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:18, he is again involved in a conflict and asking for help.

Moses is responding to conduct repeated before him.

Ghawiyy can thus be a critical description of someone's behavior.

This one sentence does not establish that person's entire fate in the afterlife or hidden inner reality.

Nor does ghawiyy mean enemy or killer; those ideas come from other actions and statements in the narrative.

5. Ighwāʾ: misleading and the responsibility of agent and follower

Form IV aghwā moves beyond someone's being on a wrong path to an agent's leading another person onto it.

Three elements must be distinguished: the misleading agent, the person exposed to misguidance, and that person's choice or response.

Iblis's claim about his own misguidance

Al-Aʿrāf 7:16

قَالَ فَبِمَا أَغْوَيْتَنِي لَأَقْعُدَنَّ لَهُمْ صِرَاطَكَ الْمُسْتَقِيمَ

qāla fa-bimā aghwaytanī la-aqʿudanna lahum ṣirāṭaka al-mustaqīm

Al-Aʿrāf 7:16

Translation: “He said: Because You have led me astray, I will surely lie in wait for them on Your straight path.”

Aghwaytanī combines the verb aghwā with the object pronoun -nī, “me.”

Iblis is speaking to The God.

He attributes his misguidance to The God and announces that he will lie in wait on humanity's straight path.

The crucial point is that the sentence reports Iblis's speech.

Reporting his words does not by itself endorse every claim and attribution he makes about The God.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:12, Iblis had claimed superiority over Adam.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:13 describes the consequence of his arrogance and refusal.

His words in Al-Aʿrāf 7:16 must be read in that sequence.

On the reading followed here, he shifts responsibility to The God rather than accepting his own conduct, then resolves to mislead others. This is a contextual interpretation, not the verb's entire lexical meaning.

Al-Ḥijr 15:39 develops the same theme further.

Making falsehood attractive and misleading others

Al-Ḥijr 15:39

قَالَ رَبِّ بِمَا أَغْوَيْتَنِي لَأُزَيِّنَنَّ لَهُمْ فِي الْأَرْضِ وَلَأُغْوِيَنَّهُمْ أَجْمَعِينَ

qāla rabbi bi-mā aghwaytanī la-uzayyinanna lahum fī al-arḍi wa-la-ughwiyannahum ajmaʿīn

Al-Ḥijr 15:39

Translation: “He said: My Lord, because You have led me astray, I will surely make things attractive to them on earth and lead them all astray.”

Two forms of gh-w-y occur here:

Aghwaytanī concerns Iblis's attribution of his own misguidance.

La-ughwiyannahum concerns his determination to mislead human beings.

Between them appears uzayyinanna.

That verb belongs to z-y-n and concerns making something attractive.

Here, Iblis intends to make things appear appealing and draw people onto a wrong course.

Adorning and misleading operate together, but their lexical meanings are not identical.

Adorning makes something appear attractive.

Ighwāʾ draws another person astray.

Something may appear beautiful yet lead to harm. Someone may likewise promise advantage or status to induce wrongful conduct.

An alluring appearance may then serve as a means of misleading.

Not every beautiful thing or attractive statement is ighwāʾ. Identifying misguidance also requires examining whether the direction and outcome are right or wrong.

In Ṣād 38:82, Iblis again swears to mislead human beings.

Ṣād 38:83 then states the exception of The God's chosen servants.

Iblis's claim to mislead everyone therefore does not establish that he will actually succeed with everyone.

The limits of Iblis's power

Al-Ḥijr 15:42

إِنَّ عِبَادِي لَيْسَ لَكَ عَلَيْهِمْ سُلْطَانٌ إِلَّا مَنِ اتَّبَعَكَ مِنَ الْغَاوِينَ

inna ʿibādī laysa laka ʿalayhim sulṭānun illā mani ittabaʿaka mina al-ghāwīn

Al-Ḥijr 15:42

Translation: “You have no authority over My servants, except those of the astray who follow you.”

Al-ghāwīn denotes those who follow a wrong course.

The sentence also contains ittabaʿaka: “follows you.”

That distinction matters.

Iblis may tempt, adorn falsehood, and invite a person onto a wrong path, but the verse also speaks of the person's following.

Misleading and following a misleader are distinct roles.

This verse does not establish that everyone is an involuntary victim of Iblis's independent power without responsibility.

Conversely, the deceptive influence of the misleader should not be ignored.

Ignorance, fear, self-interest, or misplaced trust may expose someone to deception. Their circumstances matter in assessing responsibility.

Yughwiyakum in Noah's speech

Hūd 11:34

وَلَا يَنْفَعُكُمْ نُصْحِي إِنْ أَرَدْتُ أَنْ أَنْصَحَ لَكُمْ إِنْ كَانَ اللَّهُ يُرِيدُ أَنْ يُغْوِيَكُمْ ۚ هُوَ رَبُّكُمْ وَإِلَيْهِ تُرْجَعُونَ

wa-lā yanfaʿukum nuṣḥī in aradtu an anṣaḥa lakum in kāna allāhu yurīdu an yughwiyakum huwa rabbukum wa-ilayhi turjaʿūn

Hūd 11:34

Translation: “My counsel will not benefit you, even if I wish to advise you sincerely, if The God wills to lead you astray. He is your Lord, and to Him you will be returned.”

Yughwiyakum is Form IV, expressing the leading of the addressees astray in relation to divine will. Early explanations also include destruction or deprivation of the desired good; the rendering must therefore be distinguished from the wider interpretive debate.

The speaker is Noah.

His words continue a confrontation with people rejecting his call and disputing the threatened punishment.

The conditional construction in kāna allāhu yurīdu is important.

It should not be removed to produce an unconditional claim that The God misleads everyone for no reason and without relation to their conduct.

Nor should the explicit yughwiyakum be ignored or rewritten to erase divine will from the sentence.

Understanding divine will and human responsibility also requires considering other verses about guidance and error.

In Al-Ṣaff 61:5, after a group deviated, The God caused their hearts to deviate.

Al-Baqarah 2:26 discusses The God's leading the defiantly disobedient astray through Quranic examples.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:69 describes guidance for those who strive in The God's cause.

These connections show why divine misguidance should not be examined apart from conduct, choice, and divine patterns of dealing.

For further study, Al-Ṣaff 61:5, Al-Baqarah 2:26, and Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:69 should each be considered in their own contexts.

6. Misleaders, followers, and the outcome of ghayy

Several verses use this root for those who lead others astray and whose responsibility, together with their followers', is raised at the reckoning.

Misleading in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:63

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:63

قَالَ الَّذِينَ حَقَّ عَلَيْهِمُ الْقَوْلُ رَبَّنَا هَٰؤُلَاءِ الَّذِينَ أَغْوَيْنَا أَغْوَيْنَاهُمْ كَمَا غَوَيْنَا ۖ تَبَرَّأْنَا إِلَيْكَ ۖ مَا كَانُوا إِيَّانَا يَعْبُدُونَ

qāla alladhīna ḥaqqa ʿalayhimu al-qawlu rabbanā hāʾulāʾi alladhīna aghwaynā aghwaynāhum kamā ghawaynā tabarraʾnā ilayka mā kānū iyyānā yaʿbudūn

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:63

Translation: “Those against whom the word has come into effect will say: Our Lord, these are the ones we led astray. We led them astray as we ourselves went astray. We disavow them before You; they were not worshipping us.”

Three occurrences of gh-w-y appear here:

Aghwaynā: “we led astray.”

Aghwaynāhum: “we led them astray.”

Ghawaynā: “we ourselves went astray.”

These forms clarify the relationship between the misleading agent and the person astray.

The speakers acknowledge that they themselves were on a wrong course and drew others onto it.

They then disavow their followers.

The scene shows how misguidance may spread between individuals or groups.

Someone may accept a false belief and then use influence, credibility, or power to encourage others to accept it.

Two responsibilities arise: that of the person promoting the false claim and that of the person accepting it.

The responsibility of every follower cannot be treated as identical without examining circumstances.

One may knowingly follow a false claim to preserve an advantage; another may be deceived and lack clear understanding.

Ighwāʾ itself does not settle these differences. They must be understood through context, evidence, and people's actual circumstances.

The misleaders' admission in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:32

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:32

فَأَغْوَيْنَاكُمْ إِنَّا كُنَّا غَاوِينَ

fa-aghwaynākum innā kunnā ghāwīn

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:32

Translation: “So we led you astray; we ourselves were astray.”

Aghwaynākum and ghāwīn occur together.

The first describes misleading others.

The second describes the misleaders' own condition.

Someone may therefore both be astray and lead others astray.

But not everyone astray necessarily misleads others.

One person's error may concern only themselves, while another turns the same error into a social or intellectual method of attracting followers.

The scope of their effects and responsibilities differs.

The verses around Al-Ṣāffāt 37:32 portray dispute between groups related through following and influence in worldly life.

Their claims about one another become apparent at the reckoning.

A misleader's existence does not eliminate the follower's responsibility, just as being followed does not remove the misleader's responsibility.

Ghayy as an outcome

Maryam 19:59

فَخَلَفَ مِنْ بَعْدِهِمْ خَلْفٌ أَضَاعُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَاتَّبَعُوا الشَّهَوَاتِ فَسَوْفَ يَلْقَوْنَ غَيًّا

fa-khalafa min baʿdihim khalfun aḍāʿū al-ṣalāta wa-ittabaʿū al-shahawāti fa-sawfa yalqawna ghayyā

Maryam 19:59

Translation: “Then successors came after them who neglected prayer and followed desires; they will therefore encounter the outcome of error.”

Ghayyan is the object of yalqawna.

The construction describes encountering something in the future.

Early exegesis offers several explanations, including punishment, the outcome of misguidance, and, in some reports, a place in Hell.

The root gh-w-y itself does not name a particular location in Hell.

The verse mentions neglect of prayer and following desires before speaking of encountering ghayy.

Understanding ghayyan as the consequence of misguidance is therefore an interpretation suited to the construction and context.

Maryam 19:60 excepts those who repent, believe, and do righteous deeds.

This connection matters: the possibility of return and reform remains part of the discussion of misguidance's outcome.

These verses do not establish that everyone who has ever erred has an unchangeable destiny with no possibility of repentance and reform.

Ghāwīn at the reckoning

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:91, the blazing fire is brought into view for the ghāwīn.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:94 describes them and the others under discussion being cast into it.

These uses place ghāwīn in a scene of the final outcome.

Lexically, however, ghāwīn does not name fire or punishment. It describes people on a misguided course.

The outcome in the afterlife is conveyed by the surrounding statements and the reckoning context.

7. Al-ghāwūn and following poets

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:224

وَالشُّعَرَاءُ يَتَّبِعُهُمُ الْغَاوُونَ

wa-al-shuʿarāʾu yattabiʿuhumu al-ghāwūn

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:224

Translation: “As for the poets, those astray follow them.”

Al-ghāwūn is the plural of ghāwin.

It denotes people on a wrong course.

The verse should not be read apart from those that follow.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:225 describes the poets as wandering in every valley.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:226 speaks of their saying what they do not do.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:227 then excepts those who believe, do righteous deeds, remember The God often, and defend themselves after suffering injustice.

The passage cannot therefore be treated as a condemnation of all poets, poems, or readers of poetry.

Is poetry inherently ghayy?

Neither the root nor the context supports that conclusion.

Ghayy denotes misguidance and departure from the right course.

Poetry names a form of linguistic and literary expression.

The two concepts are not identical.

Poetry can express truth, justice, grief, love, or human experience. It can also serve deception, lies, partisanship, or praise of oppression.

Its moral value in such cases concerns its content and effects, rather than simply the presence of meter and rhyme.

The passage describes poets and followers inclined to error, explains wrongful conduct, and then excepts a group of poets.

Al-ghāwūn therefore cannot be translated as “all listeners to poetry.”

Nor should every poet or artist be deemed astray merely for literary activity.

This again shows that the name of an activity and the moral character of its practitioner are separate questions.

8. Distinguishing ghayy from neighboring roots

Gh-w-y (ghayy) and r-sh-d (rushd)

Quranic rushd concerns the right course, judicious understanding, and movement toward what is sound.

Ghayy directly contrasts with it in Al-Baqarah 2:256 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:146.

This opposition is the clearest guide to the root's central meaning.

Rushd is not simply abundant information, and ghayy is not always complete ignorance of truth.

Someone may possess information yet fail to use it well in choices and conduct.

Another may err through ignorance and correct their course after recognizing the truth.

Knowledge and conduct must therefore be considered together in human usage.

Gh-w-y (ghayy) and ḍ-l-l (ḍalāl)

Ḍalāl and ghayy share a semantic field in the Quran.

Both are negated concerning Muhammad in Al-Najm 53:2.

Ḍalāl can mean losing the way, failing to find it, or departing from guidance.

Ghayy's principal uses concern departure from rushd and following a wrong course.

Some contexts associate ghayy with persistence in falsehood or failure resulting from a choice.

Nevertheless, one should not make a rule that ḍalāl is always an unintentional error and ghayy always a deliberate sin.

The roots overlap in use.

Their differences must be explained through constructions and contexts, without imposing an absolute boundary that confines every verse to one definition.

Gh-w-y (ghayy) and h-d-y (hudā)

Hudā concerns guiding and showing the right way, and in some uses accepting and following it.

Ghayy concerns being on a wrong course.

Guidance and ghayy may stand opposed in Quranic meaning, but the roots do not perform identical roles.

A person may be shown the right way yet decline to follow it.

Guidance being available does not by itself guarantee a right choice.

Conversely, a person may return to guidance after error, as in Adam's story.

Gh-w-y (ghayy) and h-w-y (hawā)

One Quranic branch of hawā concerns inclination and desire.

Some verses contrast following desire with following guidance.

Hawā and ghayy do not have the same meaning.

Hawā may express attraction or inclination; ghayy concerns departure from sound direction.

Following a desire may lead someone astray, but that relationship depends on context and the choice involved.

Maryam 19:59 joins following desires with neglecting prayer, then describes encountering ghayy.

This association shows how wrongly following desires may relate to misguidance.

Not every natural human desire should be called ghayy.

Desire for food, comfort, family, or blessings is not inherently misguidance. Conduct is assessed through its direction, limits, and consequences.

Gh-w-y (ghayy) and gh-r-r (ghurūr)

Gh-r-r concerns deception, risk, and misleading appearances.

Deception can sometimes cause ghayy.

For example, a false promise may draw someone onto a wrong course.

Deception is then a means of misleading.

Ghayy itself does not mean deceiving: someone may depart from sound direction through their own wrong choice without another person's direct deception.

Nor is every deceived person necessarily as responsible as the deceiver.

The method of deception, the act of misleading, and the condition of the person astray must remain distinct.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

Useful equivalents include “misguidance,” “going astray,” “departure from sound judgment,” “leading astray,” and, where context supports it, “failure” or “an adverse outcome.”

One equivalent is insufficient for every verse.

Ghayy

In Al-Baqarah 2:256:

الْغَيِّ — “error” or “the wrong path”

Both equivalents are defensible.

“The wrong path” makes the contrast with rushd and the right way more concrete.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:146:

سَبِيلَ الْغَيِّ — “the path of error” or “the wrong way”

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:202:

فِي الْغَيِّ — “in misguidance”

In Maryam 19:59, yalqawna and the recompense context support “the outcome of error” as an explanatory equivalent.

A shorter rendering may retain “error,” leaving its relationship to consequence and punishment for the commentary.

Ghawā

In Ṭā Hā 20:121:

فَغَوَىٰ — “so he went astray” or “so he took a wrong course”

“Went astray” is a defensible translation here.

There is no need to add “became an enemy of truth” or “remained astray forever,” since the next verse describes return and guidance.

In Al-Najm 53:2:

وَمَا غَوَىٰ — “nor has he taken a wrong course”

Alongside mā ḍalla, this wording helps preserve the two verbs' distinction.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:63:

غَوَيْنَا — “we went astray”

The verb describes the speakers' own condition, distinct from the verbs for misleading others.

Aghwā

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:16:

أَغْوَيْتَنِي — “You led me astray”

This preserves Iblis's direct speech.

In Al-Ḥijr 15:39:

لَأُغْوِيَنَّهُمْ — “I will surely lead them astray”

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:63:

أَغْوَيْنَاهُمْ — “we led them astray”

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:32:

فَأَغْوَيْنَاكُمْ — “so we led you astray”

These transitive constructions distinguish the misleading agent from the person led astray.

In Hūd 11:34, yughwiyakum occurs within a conditional construction concerning divine will. That construction must be retained.

Ghāwī and ghawiyy

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:224:

الْغَاوُونَ — “those astray”

In Al-Ḥijr 15:42:

الْغَاوِينَ — “those astray”

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:18:

لَغَوِيٌّ مُبِينٌ — “clearly astray” or “plainly on a wrong course”

Ghawiyy describes a person in a specific situation.

It should not be rendered “one who misleads others” without evidence; that meaning is directly expressed by Form IV and mughwin.

Brief root meanings in Forqan word boxes

For ghayy and forms concerning deviation:

Brief root meaning: “Going astray and departure from sound judgment and the right course.”

For ghawā:

Form meaning: “Going astray and departing from the right course.”

For aghwā:

Form meaning: “Misleading and drawing another onto a wrong course.”

For ghāwī:

Form meaning: “Someone on a misguided course.”

In preparing a verse box, the general root summary and the contextual meaning of the particular form must be separate. The form descriptions above do not replace the general root summary.

For example, fa-ghawā in Ṭā Hā 20:121 refers to Adam's lapse after disobedience.

Al-ghāwīn in Al-Aʿrāf 7:175 concerns someone who detached himself from divine signs and whom Satan then pursued.

La-ughwiyannahum in Al-Ḥijr 15:39 expresses Iblis's intention to mislead others.

Ghayyan in Maryam 19:59 concerns the adverse outcome for those who neglected prayer and followed desires.

These differences must not be replaced by one repetitive generic explanation. These are editorial proposals: installing this study does not rewrite existing verse boxes.

10. Conclusion

Early Arabic gh-w-y concerns going astray, departing from rushd, and following a course that does not reach a sound outcome.

Early poetry places ghayy against rushd and applies it to conduct and choice, including knowingly following a group's wrong direction.

Lexical sources also record failure and deterioration. The injured young camel illustrates another branch that should not be imposed on the verses' moral meaning without historical evidence.

The principal Quranic uses concern sound direction and error, human lapses, and misleading others.

Al-Baqarah 2:256 declares rushd clearly distinguished from ghayy.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:146 describes people who reject the path of rushd but choose that of ghayy.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:175 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:202 concern detachment from divine signs and continuing misguidance.

In Ṭā Hā 20:121, Adam experiences ghayy after disobedience; in Ṭā Hā 20:122, his Lord chooses him, turns toward him, and guides him.

A lapse is therefore not necessarily a permanent condition.

Al-Najm 53:2 negates ḍalāl and ghayy concerning Muhammad in the context of his mission.

In the accounts of Iblis, misleading accompanies temptation, making falsehood attractive, and inviting others onto a wrong path.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:63 and Al-Ṣāffāt 37:32, people who were themselves astray speak of misleading others.

In Maryam 19:59, the outcome of ghayy relates to neglect of prayer and following desires.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:224 describes those astray as followers of poets, but the following verses do not condemn every poet or every artistic expression.

Several essential distinctions emerge:

First, going astray is not identical to complete ignorance. A person may lack knowledge of a truth or knowingly follow a wrong course through interest or attachment.

Second, going astray is not identical to misleading another. One person may err, while another uses influence and power to draw others into the same error.

Third, a lapse differs from persistence in error. Adam's story shows that return and guidance remain possible after disobedience and ghayy.

Fourth, a misleader's presence does not automatically remove a follower's responsibility. Conversely, deception, ignorance, and knowing compliance should not be judged identically without examination.

The family may be defined in two branches:

Gh-w-y:

1. Departure from rushd and the right course: going astray, following a wrong path, or making a choice that does not lead to what is sound or beneficial; transitively, drawing another onto that path.

2. Corruption and deterioration of a condition: harm and loss of a sound or healthy state, recorded of some living beings in Arabic but not to be imposed on every verse without contextual support.

The first meaning is central in Quranic usage.

Thus, ghayy does not simply name an error: it can describe the course, conduct, and outcome of departure from sound direction.

The Quran does not place everyone who has ever erred in one category.

Adam lapsed and was then guided. Iblis refused the command and resolved to mislead others. Some detached themselves from divine signs and followed ghayy. At the resurrection, groups disavow those who misled them.

Throughout these situations, departure from the right course remains central, while responsibility and outcome vary with knowledge, choices, actions, and responses.

The question is not only whether someone ever lapses, but also whether, on recognizing a wrong course, they return to rushd or knowingly persist in ghayy.
