The Arabic root w-ḍ-ʿ concerns placing something somewhere, lowering it, setting it down, and lifting a burden from someone. Other uses concern childbirth, assigning a position, establishing something, and even moving swiftly.

The root has a broad Qur’anic range. God establishes the balance and places the earth for creatures; the record is laid out on Judgment Day; a heavy burden is lifted from Muhammad; a pregnant woman gives birth; people lay aside clothing or weapons; and words are distorted from their proper positions.

Al-Tawbah 9:47 contains another use: أَوْضَعُوا means moving swiftly. This specialized sense should not be equated with putting something in a place.

The root’s principal, familiar core is placing something in a position, or lowering and laying it down. Each derivative’s precise meaning depends on its construction, object, prepositions, and context.

1. The root in early Arabic

Putting and setting in place

Early Arabic uses وَضَعَ الشَّيْءَ for putting something down from the hand, placing it somewhere, and setting down a load.

In this use, waḍaʿa contrasts with rafaʿa.

Rafʿ can mean lifting, while waḍʿ means lowering and placing.

But waḍʿ need not mean dropping something from above. One may place an object on a table, set a load on the ground, or position something in a particular place.

“Putting” is therefore broader than “dropping.”

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAmr ibn Qamīʾah reads:

وَضَعَ الْمَنِيحَ وَكَانَ حَظُّهُمُ

waḍaʿa al-manīḥa wa-kāna ḥaẓẓuhumu

فِي الْمُنْقِيَاتِ يُقِيمُهَا يُسُرُهْ

fī al-munqiyāti yuqīmuhā yusuruh

A cautious rendering is: “He set down the manīḥ arrow; their share was among the fat camels provided through the drawing of lots.” The detailed sense of yusuruhu and the actors in the scene requires separate explanation; generosity should not be inserted as the certain meaning of that word.

This passage describes a group’s generosity and conduct during hardship.

Al-manīḥ names an arrow used in the familiar Arabian practice of drawing lots.

Here waḍaʿa accompanies a particular object and concerns putting it down or setting it aside in that activity; the precise action requires the poem’s context.

The witness supports a concrete use of the root in poetry attributed to pre-Islamic times, without making every detail of its interpretation certain.

The verse alone cannot establish all the procedures of the drawing or the arrow’s exact position. It should not bear more weight than its textual and contextual evidence allows.

Mawḍūʿ and marfūʿ as a mount’s gaits

Another branch occurs in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd:

مَوْضُوعُهَا زَوْلٌ وَمَرْفُوعُهَا

mawḍūʿuhā zawlun wa-marfūʿuhā

كَمَرِّ صَوْبٍ لَجِبٍ وَسْطَ رِيحْ

ka-marri ṣawbin lajibin wasṭa rīḥ

“Her slower gait is marvelous, and her faster gait is like a rushing, clamorous downpour amid the wind.”

The poet describes his mount’s movement.

Mawḍūʿ and marfūʿ here denote two degrees or kinds of movement, not simply putting down and lifting an object.

Lexicons identify mawḍūʿ as a gait slower than marfūʿ.

The verse has variant readings. Ibn Barrī prefers a version reversing mawḍūʿuhā and marfūʿuhā.

That changes which gait receives which comparison, but does not remove the words’ specialized use for a mount’s movement.

Arabic also records وَضَعَ الْبَعِيرُ for a camel’s swift movement, and أَوْضَعَ الرَّاكِبُ دَابَّتَهُ for a rider urging a mount onward quickly.

This matters for Al-Tawbah 9:47.

A shared root does not make placing and hastening identical throughout Arabic. Movement is an established branch with its own sense.

Setting down and reducing a burden

In classical Arabic, وَضَعَ عَنْهُ الْحِمْلَ means removing a load from someone.

وَضَعَ عَنْهُ الدَّيْنَ is also recorded for reducing or remitting a debt.

Something previously borne by a person is removed.

The physical image is clear: someone carries a load and another lowers it.

Yet this image should not determine every nonphysical use of the root.

Remitting a debt, for instance, reduces a financial obligation rather than physically moving a load.

The Qur’an likewise uses this family for removing burdens and constraints that weigh upon people.

Childbirth and delivery

وَضَعَتِ الْمَرْأَةُ وَلَدَهَا means that a woman gave birth to her child.

وَضَعَتْ حَمْلَهَا likewise expresses delivery and the ending of pregnancy.

Its semantic relationship to laying down a burden is intelligible: before birth, the fetus is carried within the mother.

Nevertheless, childbirth is an established meaning of this construction in its own right.

A translation of a birth passage should not force an unnatural expression such as “the mother put her burden on the ground” merely to preserve the root’s image.

“Gave birth” or “delivered” is appropriate.

Classical lexicons cite early usages and speech attributed to the mother of Taʾabbaṭa Sharran. The reports’ dating and wording are insufficient here to establish an independently certain pre-Qur’anic occurrence. The Qur’an itself, however, clearly attests the childbirth sense in the language of revelation.

Historical meaning: a conclusion

Arabic evidence establishes several uses: putting an object down, lowering a load, positioning something, childbirth, and a mount’s particular gait.

Placing and laying down form the family’s broad, clear core.

Hastening is a specialized movement branch; it should not be derived from “putting down” without evidence.

2. Forms and Qur’anic constructions

Important Arabic forms include:

وَضَعَ: he put, laid down, lowered, or positioned something.

يَضَعُ: he puts, lays down, or places.

وَضْع: putting, placing, or laying down.

مَوْضِع: a position or place where something is situated.

مَوَاضِع: plural of mawḍiʿ; positions or locations.

مَوْضُوع: something laid down or positioned.

وَاضِع: someone who puts or places something.

أَوْضَعَ: in the movement branch, he hastened or urged a mount forward swiftly.

إِيضَاع: hastening or urging a mount onward.

وَضِيع: a person of low social standing; this social sense belongs to the branch concerning lowered rank.

وَضِيعَة: in some commercial uses, loss or reduction of capital in a transaction.

تَوَاضَعَ: he acted humbly or did not put himself above others.

تَوَاضُع: humility, familiar in Arabic ethical usage.

مُوَاضَعَة: agreement on something or an arrangement between two parties, with specialized legal and commercial applications.

These are recorded Arabic forms, but not all occur in the Qur’an.

The Qur’an contains four principal structures from this family: twenty-six occurrences across twenty-four verses.

  • The basic verb وَضَعَ in perfect and imperfect, active and passive forms.
  • Form IV أَوْضَعَ, appearing as لَأَوْضَعُوا.
  • The place noun مَوَاضِع, appearing as مَوَاضِعِهِ.
  • The feminine passive participle مَوْضُوعَة.

The root’s initial wāw drops in يَضَعُ and related imperfect forms. This is a recognized inflectional development in certain Arabic verbs beginning with wāw.

تَضَعُ and يَضَعْنَ therefore still belong to w-ḍ-ʿ.

Semantic core

The family’s principal core is:

Putting something in a position or laying it down; in suitable constructions, lowering, setting aside, lifting from someone, or leaving it in a specified place.

In the childbirth branch, this concerns birth and the end of pregnancy.

Mawḍiʿ emphasizes where something is, or ought to be.

Awḍaʿa carries the specialized movement sense of hastening.

A precise definition preserves these actual branches instead of reducing every derivative to “change of state.”

3. Placing and establishing in the Qur’an

Āl ʿImrān 3:96 — The house established for people

إِنَّ أَوَّلَ بَيْتٍ وُضِعَ لِلنَّاسِ لَلَّذِي بِبَكَّةَ مُبَارَكًا وَهُدًى لِلْعَالَمِينَ

inna awwala baytin wuḍiʿa li-al-nāsi la-alladhī bi-Bakkata mubārakan wa-hudan li-al-ʿālamīn

Āl ʿImrān 3:96

“The first house established for people is indeed the one at Bakkah, blessed and a guidance for all worlds.”

وُضِعَ is the passive perfect of w-ḍ-ʿ.

The construction concerns بَيْتٍ, a house.

لِلنَّاسِ expresses its relationship to people.

Here placing means establishing a house with a particular position and purpose.

The verb alone does not identify its building materials, who laid its first stone, or how often it was rebuilt.

Such details do not follow from the root.

أَوَّلَ بَيْتٍ وُضِعَ لِلنَّاسِ must be read with the qualification li-al-nās. The verse identifies the first house established for people in this particular role; waḍʿ alone cannot determine the history of all buildings worldwide.

“Blessed” and “guidance for all worlds” clarify the house’s standing, but are not part of waḍʿ’s lexical meaning.

Al-Raḥmān 55:7 — Establishing the balance

وَالسَّمَاءَ رَفَعَهَا وَوَضَعَ الْمِيزَانَ

wa-al-samāʾa rafaʿahā wa-waḍaʿa al-mīzān

Al-Raḥmān 55:7

“The heaven—He raised it, and established the balance.”

Two important verbs occur together:

رَفَعَهَا belongs to r-f-ʿ.

وَوَضَعَ belongs to w-ḍ-ʿ.

Rafʿ concerns raising.

Waḍʿ here concerns placing and establishing the balance.

The pairing presents distinct actions, but does not imply that the balance was physically dropped like a heavy object.

Al-Raḥmān 55:8 speaks of not exceeding bounds in the balance.

Al-Raḥmān 55:9 commands maintaining weight with equity and not diminishing the balance.

Establishing the balance here concerns the availability of correct, equitable measurement.

Mīzān itself belongs to w-z-n, not w-ḍ-ʿ.

Measurement comes from the balance; waḍaʿa expresses establishing it.

The root itself should therefore not be redefined as “justice.” Justice and equity emerge from context and separate words.

Al-Raḥmān 55:10 — Placing the earth for creatures

وَالْأَرْضَ وَضَعَهَا لِلْأَنَامِ

wa-al-arḍa waḍaʿahā li-al-anām

Al-Raḥmān 55:10

“The earth—He placed it for creatures.”

وَضَعَهَا is perfect with a feminine pronoun referring to the earth.

لِلْأَنَامِ identifies those for whom it is placed.

Here placing concerns providing and establishing the earth as a place of life.

The following verses mention fruit, date palms, grain, and plants.

The earth is thus presented among creatures’ blessings and means of life.

Waḍaʿa itself does not mean creating all these plants, physically spreading the earth, or determining its geometry.

The Qur’an uses distinct expressions for creation, spreading, and preparing the earth. Their differences should be preserved.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:47 — Setting up equitable balances

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:47 states:

وَنَضَعُ الْمَوَازِينَ الْقِسْطَ لِيَوْمِ الْقِيَامَةِ

wa-naḍaʿu al-mawāzīna al-qisṭa li-yawmi al-qiyāmah

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:47

“We shall set up equitable balances for the Day of Resurrection.”

نَضَعُ is an imperfect form of w-ḍ-ʿ.

Its object is الْمَوَازِينَ الْقِسْطَ.

The verse continues with no soul suffering injustice and even a mustard seed’s weight being accounted for.

Setting up the balances belongs to a context of equitable judgment and accounting.

The verb alone cannot determine the balances’ physical construction in the hereafter.

Without evidence, one should neither insist on an earthly two-pan scale nor erase the explicit image of a balance altogether.

The Qur’anic statement establishes measurement with equity.

Al-Kahf 18:49 and Al-Zumar 39:69 — The record laid out

Both Al-Kahf 18:49 and Al-Zumar 39:69 contain:

وَوُضِعَ الْكِتَابُ

wa-wuḍiʿa al-kitāb

Al-Kahf 18:49; Al-Zumar 39:69

“The record was laid out.”

Wuḍiʿa is passive perfect in both.

The context places the record in the scene of accounting and judgment.

In Al-Kahf 18:49, the guilty are fearful of what it contains.

In Al-Zumar 39:69, the Prophets and witnesses are brought after the record is laid out, and people are judged in truth.

The expression concerns presenting the record in the scene of accounting.

Waḍʿ itself does not mean writing the record.

Nor does the passive verb establish a particular material form, such as a modern paper notebook placed on a desk or platform.

The passages present the record in its role in accounting and judgment.

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:14 — Cups set out

وَأَكْوَابٌ مَوْضُوعَةٌ

wa-akwābun mawḍūʿah

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:14

“And cups set out.”

مَوْضُوعَةٌ is a feminine passive participle of w-ḍ-ʿ.

Akwāb is a nonhuman plural, taking a feminine singular description.

The cups have been set in place.

The context concerns Paradise’s blessings, so readiness and accessibility fit the scene.

Mawḍūʿah itself does not specify that they are full, their material, or their contents.

It must not be confused with مَوْضُونَة, from the separate root w-ḍ-n, describing couches in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:15.

4. Laying aside and removing burdens

Al-Nisāʾ 4:102 — Laying aside weapons

Al-Nisāʾ 4:102, concerning salat during danger, includes:

أَنْ تَضَعُوا أَسْلِحَتَكُمْ

an taḍaʿū asliḥatakum

Al-Nisāʾ 4:102

“To lay aside your weapons.”

Placing here means setting down or laying aside weapons.

The permission is tied to particular circumstances, including distress from rain or illness.

The verse also commands continued caution and readiness.

Laying aside weapons here therefore means neither the end of all danger nor a general abandonment of defense.

Taḍaʿū expresses the action; the other phrases establish its conditions and limits.

Al-Nūr 24:58 — Laying aside clothing at rest

Al-Nūr 24:58 describes one occasion requiring permission before entering private space:

وَحِينَ تَضَعُونَ ثِيَابَكُمْ مِنَ الظَّهِيرَةِ

wa-ḥīna taḍaʿūna thiyābakum mina al-ẓahīrah

Al-Nūr 24:58

“When you lay aside your clothes at midday.”

تَضَعُونَ concerns laying aside clothing.

Midday and rest matter to the context.

The verb does not specify removing every garment or becoming completely naked.

Clothing and privacy must be understood contextually.

The verse concerns respecting privacy, but w-ḍ-ʿ itself does not mean privacy.

Al-Nūr 24:60 — Setting aside clothing

Of older women who no longer expect marriage, Al-Nūr 24:60 says:

أَنْ يَضَعْنَ ثِيَابَهُنَّ

an yaḍaʿna thiyābahunna

Al-Nūr 24:60

“To lay aside their garments.”

The qualification غَيْرَ مُتَبَرِّجَاتٍ بِزِينَةٍ follows: without displaying adornment.

It matters to the scope of the permission.

Yaḍaʿna alone cannot establish permission for complete nakedness.

Nor should a particular historical interpretation of the clothing’s type and limits be inserted into the verb without considering the whole verse.

Waḍʿ means setting aside; the practical ruling depends on the complete sentence, audience, and qualifications.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:157 — Removing burdens and shackles

Al-Aʿrāf 7:157 says of the Messenger:

وَيَضَعُ عَنْهُمْ إِصْرَهُمْ وَالْأَغْلَالَ الَّتِي كَانَتْ عَلَيْهِمْ

wa-yaḍaʿu ʿanhum iṣrahum wa-al-aghlāla allatī kānat ʿalayhim

Al-Aʿrāf 7:157

“He removes from them their heavy burden and the shackles that were upon them.”

The construction waḍaʿa ʿan is significant.

With ʿan, waḍaʿa can express removing something from another’s shoulders or responsibility.

Iṣr concerns a heavy burden or obligation.

Aghlāl denotes shackles or fetters.

The image is of removing pressure and constraints from people.

Waḍʿ itself, however, means neither forgiving all sins nor abolishing every responsibility. Object and context determine the burdens concerned.

The verse also does not make every religious command or responsibility inherently a shackle.

It concerns the burdens and shackles named in the passage.

Al-Sharḥ 94:2 — Muhammad’s burden lifted

وَوَضَعْنَا عَنْكَ وِزْرَكَ

wa-waḍaʿnā ʿanka wizrak

Al-Sharḥ 94:2

“We lifted your burden from you.”

Waḍaʿa ʿan occurs here too.

The object is wizraka: your burden.

Wizr belongs to w-z-r, not w-ḍ-ʿ.

Al-Sharḥ 94:3 identifies it as weighing upon Muhammad’s back.

Waḍaʿnā therefore expresses removing a burden that weighed upon someone.

The verb does not determine precisely what that burden was.

Contextual study may consider the mission’s difficulties, heavy responsibilities, or other defensible possibilities.

The action is lifting the burden; identifying it requires separate examination.

Muḥammad 47:4 — War laying down its burdens

Muḥammad 47:4 includes:

حَتَّىٰ تَضَعَ الْحَرْبُ أَوْزَارَهَا

ḥattā taḍaʿa al-ḥarbu awzārahā

Muḥammad 47:4

“Until war lays down its burdens and equipment.”

The language is figurative.

War is not a person carrying a load and setting it down.

Arabic nevertheless expresses war’s ending by its laying down burdens and equipment.

Awzār here can concern war’s equipment and burdens, a usage also known in early poetry.

Taḍaʿa thus participates in an expression concerning the cessation of warfare.

Waḍʿ itself does not mean peace, ceasefire, or pardon. War’s ending comes from the complete expression and the established use of awzār al-ḥarb.

Nor can every rule concerning warfare and captives be extracted from this phrase without examining the rest of the verse.

5. Delivery and childbirth

Childbirth is a frequent Qur’anic branch of this root.

Here the verb accompanies the child or the pregnancy’s burden.

Āl ʿImrān 3:36 — Mary’s birth

The root occurs three times in Āl ʿImrān 3:36:

فَلَمَّا وَضَعَتْهَا

fa-lammā waḍaʿathā

Āl ʿImrān 3:36

إِنِّي وَضَعْتُهَا أُنْثَىٰ

innī waḍaʿtuhā unthā

Āl ʿImrān 3:36

وَاللَّهُ أَعْلَمُ بِمَا وَضَعَتْ

wa-Allāhu aʿlamu bi-mā waḍaʿat

Āl ʿImrān 3:36

The first phrase concerns giving birth to the child.

In the second, the wife of ʿImrān—Mary’s mother—tells God that she has delivered a girl.

The third expresses God’s knowledge of what was delivered.

The forms differ in person and pronoun but concern the same birth.

وَضَعَتْهَا and وَضَعْتُهَا differ in verbal person and speaker.

The pronoun hā refers to the female child.

In bi-mā waḍaʿat, the familiar Ḥafṣ reading uses the third-person feminine. A first-person reading is also recorded, changing the sentence’s speaker while retaining the childbirth sense.

Waḍʿ here means giving birth, not setting the child aside or abandoning responsibility for her.

Her being female is expressed by unthā, not by w-ḍ-ʿ.

Al-Ḥajj 22:2 — Delivery amid overwhelming terror

Al-Ḥajj 22:2 includes:

وَتَضَعُ كُلُّ ذَاتِ حَمْلٍ حَمْلَهَا

wa-taḍaʿu kullu dhāti ḥamlin ḥamlahā

Al-Ḥajj 22:2

“Every pregnant one delivers what she carries.”

The verse depicts the overwhelming terror of the Day of Resurrection.

Dhāti ḥamlin denotes one who is pregnant.

Ḥamlahā is the object of taḍaʿu.

In ordinary usage this construction concerns delivery and the emergence of what is carried.

Here it forms part of a scene of pervasive terror.

The image alone should not become a medical theory about all pregnant women or the physiology of the hereafter.

The terror’s intensity is clearly conveyed through the image of delivery.

Fāṭir 35:11 and Fuṣṣilat 41:47 — God’s knowledge of pregnancy and birth

Both verses include:

وَمَا تَحْمِلُ مِنْ أُنْثَىٰ وَلَا تَضَعُ إِلَّا بِعِلْمِهِ

wa-mā taḥmilu min unthā wa-lā taḍaʿu illā bi-ʿilmih

Fāṭir 35:11; Fuṣṣilat 41:47

“No female conceives or gives birth except with His knowledge.”

Taḥmilu and taḍaʿu are placed alongside one another.

Ḥaml concerns pregnancy and carrying the fetus.

Waḍʿ concerns delivery and the ending of that condition.

The distinction confirms the Qur’anic childbirth use of waḍʿ.

God’s knowledge is expressed by illā bi-ʿilmihi, not by taḍaʿu itself.

The verses concern the extent of God’s knowledge; reproductive biology cannot be extracted from waḍʿ alone.

Al-Aḥqāf 46:15 — The hardship of pregnancy and delivery

Al-Aḥqāf 46:15 includes:

حَمَلَتْهُ أُمُّهُ كُرْهًا وَوَضَعَتْهُ كُرْهًا

ḥamalathu ummuhu kurhan wa-waḍaʿathu kurhā

Al-Aḥqāf 46:15

“His mother carried him with hardship and gave birth to him with hardship.”

Two distinct stages are described.

First, pregnancy.

Then, delivery.

Kurhan occurs in both clauses, expressing their accompanying difficulty and hardship.

The root w-ḍ-ʿ does not itself mean suffering.

Childbirth may involve pain, but here hardship is stated separately.

Translation should render waḍaʿathu as “she gave birth to him,” and convey kurhan independently as hardship.

Al-Ṭalāq 65:4 — Delivery ending the waiting period

Al-Ṭalāq 65:4 includes:

وَأُولَاتُ الْأَحْمَالِ أَجَلُهُنَّ أَنْ يَضَعْنَ حَمْلَهُنَّ

wa-ulātu al-aḥmāli ajaluhunna an yaḍaʿna ḥamlahunna

Al-Ṭalāq 65:4

“For pregnant women, their term is until they deliver what they carry.”

Delivery marks the end of a specified period within a family ruling.

Waḍʿ al-ḥaml has its familiar childbirth sense, while its legal role comes from the complete sentence.

The root itself means neither the waiting period, divorce, nor termination of a marriage contract.

Al-Ṭalāq 65:6 — Providing until delivery

Al-Ṭalāq 65:6 likewise includes:

حَتَّىٰ يَضَعْنَ حَمْلَهُنَّ

ḥattā yaḍaʿna ḥamlahunna

Al-Ṭalāq 65:6

“Until they deliver what they carry.”

This concerns financial responsibility toward pregnant women.

The same birth thus marks the waiting period’s end in one verse and relates to continuing financial responsibility in another.

The difference belongs to their legal contexts, not to a change in waḍʿ’s meaning.

6. Mawāḍiʿ: words’ proper positions and distortion

Mawāḍiʿ is the plural of mawḍiʿ.

Its primary sense is where something is, or ought to be.

This may be physical, as with an object’s location.

Speech likewise allows us to discuss the position of a word, phrase, or concept.

A sentence may be clear in its own context but change meaning when detached or replaced by another expression.

Three Qur’anic verses use mawāḍiʿihi concerning words.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:46 — Moving words from their proper positions

Al-Nisāʾ 4:46 includes:

يُحَرِّفُونَ الْكَلِمَ عَنْ مَوَاضِعِهِ

yuḥarrifūna al-kalima ʿan mawāḍiʿih

Al-Nisāʾ 4:46

“They distort words from their proper positions.”

Two independent roots occur here.

Yuḥarrifūna belongs to ḥ-r-f.

Mawāḍiʿihi belongs to w-ḍ-ʿ.

Yuḥarrifūna expresses distortion.

Mawāḍiʿihi identifies the positions from which words are displaced.

Mawḍiʿ itself does not mean distortion.

A word may occupy its proper position, or someone may distort it away from that position.

A word’s position is not only its written location

Al-Nisāʾ 4:46 follows the phrase with examples of speech and twisting the tongue.

Some people utter expressions in ways that make them misleading or insulting.

More appropriate formulations are then presented.

Distorting words from their positions can therefore concern expression, usage, and meaning.

Mawāḍiʿihi alone does not prove that a written manuscript has necessarily been altered in every case.

Altering written text and distorting meaning are distinguishable actions. Each requires contextual evidence.

The verse also concerns some of those who became Jews, rather than accusing every member of a people or every follower of a religion throughout history of deliberate distortion.

Al-Māʾidah 5:13 — Distorting words

Al-Māʾidah 5:13 likewise includes:

يُحَرِّفُونَ الْكَلِمَ عَنْ مَوَاضِعِهِ

yuḥarrifūna al-kalima ʿan mawāḍiʿih

Al-Māʾidah 5:13

The wording matches Al-Nisāʾ 4:46.

Its context concerns breaking the covenant and forgetting part of what they had been reminded of.

Mawāḍiʿ again means positions.

The type of distortion, the actors’ motives, and its consequences do not come from the noun alone.

Al-Māʾidah 5:41 — Min baʿdi mawāḍiʿihi

Al-Māʾidah 5:41 uses different wording:

يُحَرِّفُونَ الْكَلِمَ مِنْ بَعْدِ مَوَاضِعِهِ

yuḥarrifūna al-kalima min baʿdi mawāḍiʿih

Al-Māʾidah 5:41

“They distort words after they have been set in their proper positions.”

The earlier two passages used ʿan.

This one uses min baʿdi.

Translation should retain the difference.

ʿAn mawāḍiʿihi emphasizes displacement from the positions.

Min baʿdi mawāḍiʿihi presents distortion after the words’ positions have been established.

This grammatical difference alone does not establish two completely separate, unrelated kinds of distortion.

All three concern diverting words from their proper use or position.

How is a word’s proper position known?

A word’s position in language is more than its physical place on a page.

Its meaning relates to sentence, audience, verbal object, prepositions, and context.

Someone may quote a sentence correctly but omit its conditions and convey a different meaning.

A statement about a particular group may be generalized to everyone.

A command for a particular situation may become a permanent rule when its qualifications are omitted.

The written words can remain unchanged while their meaning is displaced from its proper context.

These examples illustrate context’s importance; they do not prove that every Qur’anic instance of distortion took only this form.

Mawāḍiʿ means positions; identifying the alteration requires relevant evidence.

7. Awḍaʿū: hasty movement and fomenting disorder

Al-Tawbah 9:47

لَوْ خَرَجُوا فِيكُمْ مَا زَادُوكُمْ إِلَّا خَبَالًا وَلَأَوْضَعُوا خِلَالَكُمْ يَبْغُونَكُمُ الْفِتْنَةَ وَفِيكُمْ سَمَّاعُونَ لَهُمْ ۗ وَاللَّهُ عَلِيمٌ بِالظَّالِمِينَ

law kharajū fīkum mā zādūkum illā khabālan wa-la-awḍaʿū khilālakum yabghūnakumu al-fitnata wa-fīkum sammāʿūna lahum wa-Allāhu ʿalīmun bi-al-ẓālimīn

Al-Tawbah 9:47

“Had they gone out among you, they would have added nothing but disorder, and would have hurried about among you, seeking turmoil for you. Among you are those who listen to them, and The God knows the wrongdoers.”

La-awḍaʿū is Form IV.

It belongs to the established branch of rapid movement.

Arabic records awḍaʿa for urging a mount swiftly onward or hastening in travel.

It does not mean “putting something among you” here.

The role of khilālakum

Khilālakum refers to the midst and spaces within the addressed group.

The phrase pictures rapid movement among them.

The purpose comes next:

يَبْغُونَكُمُ الْفِتْنَةَ

yabghūnakumu al-fitnah

Al-Tawbah 9:47

“Seeking turmoil and disorder for you.”

Hastening comes from awḍaʿū, while fomenting disorder comes from the following clause.

The root w-ḍ-ʿ itself should not be defined as turmoil or hypocrisy.

The verse’s context

Al-Tawbah 9:46 discusses people who had not made preparations to go out.

Al-Tawbah 9:47 says that accompanying the group would only have increased its disorder.

Someone’s presence in a group is not necessarily beneficial.

Instead of helping, a person may create conflict and distrust.

That conclusion comes from the whole context.

Awḍaʿū expresses haste; the purpose and conduct determine its moral value.

The passage also shows why all derivatives cannot share one fixed translation. Waḍaʿa al-mīzān and la-awḍaʿū khilālakum belong to one family but different branches of usage.

8. Comparison with related roots

W-ḍ-ʿ (waḍaʿa) and r-f-ʿ (rafaʿa)

In physical uses, these roots can express opposite directions.

Rafʿ concerns lifting and raising.

Waḍʿ concerns lowering and laying down.

Both occur in Al-Raḥmān 55:7:

وَالسَّمَاءَ رَفَعَهَا وَوَضَعَ الْمِيزَانَ

wa-al-samāʾa rafaʿahā wa-waḍaʿa al-mīzān

Al-Raḥmān 55:7

This does not make every rafʿ a physical upward movement or every waḍʿ a physical downward movement.

Both have broader nonphysical uses.

Al-Sharḥ 94:2 describes Muhammad’s burden being lifted, while Al-Sharḥ 94:4 describes his remembrance being raised.

The actions differ conceptually, and their objects determine their precise meanings.

W-ḍ-ʿ (waḍaʿa) and ḥ-ṭ-ṭ (ḥaṭṭa)

Ḥ-ṭ-ṭ concerns lowering and laying down a load.

It overlaps with one branch of w-ḍ-ʿ.

Waḍʿ has wider uses in positioning objects, assigning places, childbirth, and establishing the balance.

The two therefore overlap without being fully equivalent.

W-ḍ-ʿ (waḍaʿa) and ḥ-m-l (ḥamala)

Ḥ-m-l concerns carrying something upon or within oneself.

Pregnancy passages place ḥaml and waḍʿ together.

In Al-Aḥqāf 46:15, the mother first carries her child, then gives birth.

Fāṭir 35:11 and Fuṣṣilat 41:47 also state pregnancy and delivery separately.

They are distinct stages here.

This does not make every Qur’anic waḍʿ refer to childbirth.

Object and context decide the sense.

W-ḍ-ʿ (waḍaʿa) and j-ʿ-l (jaʿala)

The Qur’an uses jaʿala for placing, making, and bringing about various conditions.

Waḍʿ can also concern placing something somewhere.

The roots nevertheless differ.

Waḍaʿa al-mīzān emphasizes establishing the balance.

Other uses of jaʿala can emphasize transformation, assigning a role, or placing something in a new condition.

Both may be translated as “placed,” but their individual usages require examination.

W-ḍ-ʿ (mawḍiʿ) and ḥ-r-f (taḥrīf)

Mawḍiʿ names a position.

Taḥrīf concerns distortion or displacement from the proper course or position.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:46 and Al-Māʾidah 5:13 combine these families.

But position itself does not mean distortion.

A statement’s proper position and the act of distorting it are separate matters, especially when examining words and meaning.

W-ḍ-ʿ (awḍaʿa) and s-r-ʿ (surʿah)

In the mount’s movement branch, awḍaʿa concerns hastening.

Surʿah likewise concerns speed.

Awḍaʿa has a particular background in a mount’s movement, applied in Al-Tawbah 9:47 to rapid movement through a group.

“They moved swiftly” is therefore suitable, but this sense should not spread to every form of w-ḍ-ʿ.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

Several principal equivalents are needed according to the verb’s object. These proposals do not replace existing verse word boxes.

Putting and establishing

In Āl ʿImrān 3:96:

“The first house established for people.”

In Al-Raḥmān 55:7:

“He established the balance.”

In Al-Raḥmān 55:10:

“He placed the earth for creatures.”

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:47:

“We shall set up equitable balances for the Day of Resurrection.”

In Al-Kahf 18:49 and Al-Zumar 39:69:

“The record was laid out.”

In Al-Ghāshiyah 88:14:

“And cups set out.”

Setting aside and lifting burdens

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:102:

“To lay aside your weapons.”

In Al-Nūr 24:58:

“When you lay aside your clothes at midday.”

In Al-Nūr 24:60:

“To lay aside their garments.”

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:157:

“He removes their heavy burden and shackles from them.”

In Al-Sharḥ 94:2:

“We lifted your burden from you.”

In Muḥammad 47:4:

“Until war lays down its burdens and equipment.”

Childbirth

In Āl ʿImrān 3:36:

“When she gave birth to her.”

In Al-Ḥajj 22:2:

“Every pregnant one delivers what she carries.”

In Fāṭir 35:11 and Fuṣṣilat 41:47:

“No female conceives or gives birth except with His knowledge.”

In Al-Aḥqāf 46:15:

“His mother carried him with hardship and gave birth to him with hardship.”

In Al-Ṭalāq 65:4 and Al-Ṭalāq 65:6:

“Until they deliver what they carry.”

Position

In Al-Nisāʾ 4:46 and Al-Māʾidah 5:13:

“They distort words from their proper positions.”

In Al-Māʾidah 5:41:

“They distort words after they have been set in their proper positions.”

Hasty movement

In Al-Tawbah 9:47:

“They would have hurried about among you.”

Preserving the root’s core does not mean repeating one Persian or English word throughout every verse.

“Putting” is natural in many constructions, but childbirth, lifting burdens, and hasty movement each need their appropriate equivalent.

10. Conclusion

Arabic w-ḍ-ʿ concerns putting, laying down, lowering, and positioning something.

Poetry attributed to pre-Islamic times illustrates concrete placement and a mount’s gaits, while the witnesses’ details require separate assessment.

Qur’anic forms concern establishing a house and balance, laying out the record, putting aside clothes and weapons, removing burdens, childbirth, words’ positions, and hasty movement.

This range prevents restricting the root to “putting down.”

Some uses emphasize placing something appropriately.

Others emphasize removing or setting it aside.

In childbirth, the verb denotes delivery.

Mawāḍiʿ concerns something’s positions.

Awḍaʿa belongs to the specialized branch of rapid movement.

The family’s definition can preserve two principal branches:

Placement: putting or setting something in a position, lowering or setting it aside, with established applications in lifting burdens and childbirth.

Movement: hastening or urging movement onward, especially in the old use concerning a mount, seen in Al-Tawbah 9:47.

Mawḍiʿ belongs to the positional branch: where something is, or ought to be.

A central result is the distinction between waḍʿ and mawḍiʿ. Placing a word appropriately differs from distorting it away from its place. In the distortion verses, yuḥarrifūna supplies the wrongful action; mawāḍiʿ identifies the positions from which words are displaced.

In the balance passages, waḍʿ itself does not mean justice. The balance, equity, and the command against exceeding bounds each contribute independently.

In birth passages, waḍʿ denotes delivery; preserving a load-lowering image should not produce an unnatural translation.

Forqan’s final translation criterion is to recognize the root while determining each derivative’s meaning from its construction and verse context. This preserves linguistic relationships without imposing one form’s meaning on another.
