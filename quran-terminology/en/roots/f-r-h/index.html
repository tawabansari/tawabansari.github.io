The root ف ر ح — Joy, gladness, and exultation: commendable and blameworthy happiness

Qur’anic and lexical forms

فَرِحَ / يَفْرَحُ
(fariḥa / yafraḥu)
Form I verb; rejoicing

فَلْيَفْرَحُوا
(fa-l-yafraḥū)
Command to rejoice in bounty and mercy

فَرِحٌ / فَرِحُونَ / فَرِحِينَ
(fariḥun / fariḥūna / fariḥīna)
Adjective joyful; contextual evaluation

The triliteral Arabic root ف ر ح denotes joy, happiness, and satisfaction at a desirable occurrence. فَرِحَ describes someone delighted by a blessing, good news, success, or a welcome event.

Such joy may arise from divine bounty and mercy, another person’s success, or something good. Alternatively, people may exult in wealth and power, delight in others’ defeat, or become so attached to their possessions and beliefs that they disregard truth.

The Qur’an uses ف ر ح in both settings. Sometimes it commands rejoicing; elsewhere it condemns a group’s rejoicing. Even within one surah, believers’ joy can be commended while partisan groups’ satisfaction is criticized.

Thus faraḥ is neither a moral virtue nor a sin in itself: its cause, object, and accompanying conduct disclose its value.

The root occurs 22 times in 21 Qur’anic verses, all within the semantic field of joy. The main differences concern human situations and responses, not independent roots.

1. Early Arabic meanings and the historical standing of the evidence

In early Arabic, فَرِحَ يَفْرَحُ فَرَحًا means to rejoice or feel happy. Faraḥ contrasts with ḥuzn, sorrow or grief.

Early lexical explanations sometimes define it as خِفَّةُ الْقَلْب: a lightness of heart, moving away from sorrow or a burdensome situation.

This is emotional imagery: good news or fulfilled wishes bring a sense of openness, vitality, and lightness.

Alongside this general sense, some Arabic uses express excessive delight, exultation, or self-importance. The same semantic field can therefore convey natural happiness and joy mixed with pride.

A poetic example attributed to the pre-Islamic period

Lines attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm say:

لَا تَلُومِينِي فَإِنِّي مُتْلِفٌ
كُلَّ مَا تَحْوِي يَمِينِي وَشِمَالِي

Lā talūmīnī fa-innī mutlifun; kulla mā taḥwī yamīnī wa-shimālī.

لَسْتُ إِنْ أُطْرِفْتُ مَالًا فَرِحًا
وَإِذَا أَتْلَفْتُهُ لَسْتُ أُبَالِي

Lastu in uṭriftu mālan fariḥan; wa-idhā atlaftuhu lastu ubālī.

Translation: “Do not blame me, for I spend and give away everything in my hands. If I acquire new wealth, I am not carried away by it; if I expend it, I do not worry.”

These lines appear in the collection attributed to ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm and in the transmission associated with Thaʿlab.

The poet describes his attitude toward possessions: acquiring wealth does not intoxicate him, nor does losing it unsettle him.

Fariḥan concerns joy at obtaining wealth. Within the poet’s praise of his generosity, the point is independence from possessions and preservation of nobility.

This usage shows how language attributed to the pre-Islamic period could express joy as a response to acquiring wealth, potentially accompanied by attachment and exultation.

The attribution reaches us through later transmission without independent contemporary documentation of composition. It is useful evidence of early usage, but cannot alone establish a definitive chronology of every meaning.

Faraḥ: natural happiness and exultation

Arabic faraḥ can express simple, natural joy.

Someone may rejoice at a loved one’s return, recovery from illness, or good news.

In another context, joy may move beyond satisfaction into pride, ingratitude, or superiority.

Such qualities do not always come from the root itself. Context, accompanying adjectives, and conduct may disclose them.

Its historical definition cannot therefore be restricted to “blameworthy exultation,” just as it cannot always be translated as “pure, commendable joy.”

Another Arabic branch: weight and pressure

Early dictionaries also record another use under ف ر ح:

«أَفْرَحَهُ الدَّيْنُ»

Afraḥahu al-dayn.

That is: “Debt burdened and constrained him.”

Mufraḥ can here describe someone weighed down by debt or heavy responsibility.

An early poetic line illustrates this branch:

إِذَا أَنْتَ لَمْ تَبْرَحْ تُؤَدِّي أَمَانَةً
وَتَحْمِلُ أُخْرَى أَفْرَحَتْكَ الْوَدَائِعُ

Idhā anta lam tabraḥ tuʾaddī amānatan; wa-taḥmilu ukhrā afraḥatka al-wadāʾiʿ.

Translation: “If you keep discharging one trust and carrying another, the entrusted obligations will eventually weigh you down.”

Afraḥatka here does not mean “made you happy,” but “burdened you.”

Lexical accounts preserve this use, although the poem’s exact date and pre-Qur’anic origin have not been securely established here.

It should therefore be presented as an attested Arabic meaning, without making it the proven origin of the sense of happiness.

Ibn Fāris also distinguishes two branches: happiness opposed to sorrow, and heaviness or the imposition of a burden.

The Qur’an contains no occurrence of the burdening branch. All its forms of ف ر ح concern happiness.

2. Linguistic forms and Qur’anic derivatives

Important Arabic forms include:

  • فَرِحَ: he rejoiced or became happy.
  • يَفْرَحُ: he rejoices.
  • فَرَح: joy or gladness.
  • فَرِح: happy or joyful; in a suitable context, exultant or proud.
  • فَرِحُونَ and فَرِحِينَ: plural forms of the adjective fariḥ.
  • فَرْحَة and فُرْحَة: joy or happiness.
  • فَرْحَان: happy.
  • مِفْرَاح: someone frequently joyful.
  • فَرَّحَ: made someone happy.
  • تَفْرِيح: causing happiness.
  • أَفْرَحَ: made happy; in the other lexical branch, burdened.
  • مُفْرَح: in certain early uses, someone distressed or heavily burdened.

The Qur’an uses only the basic verb fariḥa in different tense, person, and command constructions, and the adjective fariḥ with its plural forms.

Forms such as tafrīḥ, farḥa, farḥān, and mifrāḥ do not occur in the Qur’an.

The central Qur’anic meaning can be defined as:

Joy and satisfaction in response to something a person regards as desirable, pleasant, or gratifying.

This deliberately avoids assigning the root a fixed ethical value. People may desire something genuinely good, or rejoice in something harmful to others.

The object of joy and the person’s conduct determine its evaluation.

3. Commendable joy in the Qur’an

A. A direct command to rejoice in divine bounty and mercy

Yūnus 10:58

قُلْ بِفَضْلِ اللَّهِ وَبِرَحْمَتِهِ فَبِذَٰلِكَ فَلْيَفْرَحُوا هُوَ خَيْرٌ مِمَّا يَجْمَعُونَ

Qul bi-faḍli Allāhi wa-bi-raḥmatihi fa-bi-dhālika fa-l-yafraḥū; huwa khayrun mimmā yajmaʿūn.

Translation: “Say: In the bounty of The God and His mercy—in that let them rejoice. It is better than what they accumulate.”

This clearly shows that the Qur’an does not condemn joy in itself.

فَلْيَفْرَحُوا is an imperfect verb with the lām of command: an instruction to rejoice.

The passage therefore actively invites joy, rather than merely allowing it.

Its object is divine bounty and mercy.

Yūnus 10:57 mentions an admonition from the Lord, healing for what is in hearts, guidance, and mercy for believers.

Bounty and mercy here therefore relate to guidance and what benefits people’s inner lives and conduct.

The verse ends:

«هُوَ خَيْرٌ مِمَّا يَجْمَعُونَ»

Huwa khayrun mimmā yajmaʿūn.

This blessing is better than what people accumulate.

Blessings are thus not valued solely by the quantity of money or possessions.

Someone with little wealth may enjoy health, security, knowledge, guidance, love, or peace. These too can bring joy.

The broad instances of mercy should not, however, become independent definitions of faraḥ. Faraḥ is joy; bounty and mercy are its object and cause.

B. The joy of those killed in the way of The God

Āl ʿImrān 3:170

فَرِحِينَ بِمَا آتَاهُمُ اللَّهُ مِنْ فَضْلِهِ وَيَسْتَبْشِرُونَ بِالَّذِينَ لَمْ يَلْحَقُوا بِهِمْ مِنْ خَلْفِهِمْ أَلَّا خَوْفٌ عَلَيْهِمْ وَلَا هُمْ يَحْزَنُونَ

Fariḥīna bi-mā ātāhumu Allāhu min faḍlihi wa-yastabshirūna bi-alladhīna lam yalḥaqū bihim min khalfihim allā khawfun ʿalayhim wa-lā hum yaḥzanūn.

Translation: “Rejoicing in what The God has given them of His bounty, and receiving glad tidings concerning those behind them who have not yet joined them: no fear shall be upon them, nor shall they grieve.”

This follows Āl ʿImrān 3:169, concerning those killed in the way of The God who live and receive provision with their Lord.

فَرِحِينَ describes their joyful condition.

Its cause is also explicit:

«بِمَا آتَاهُمُ اللَّهُ مِنْ فَضْلِهِ»

Bi-mā ātāhumu Allāhu min faḍlihi.

They rejoice in the divine bounty they have received.

The same sentence uses يَسْتَبْشِرُونَ, relating to glad tidings and welcome news.

The two words are close but not identical.

Fariḥīn describes their joy; yastabshirūn emphasizes receiving or welcoming glad tidings concerning others.

Qur’anic joy is thus not limited to worldly life.

The word fariḥīn alone does not disclose bodily conditions, mechanisms of awareness, or the manner of life with the Lord. Those require separate study of the passages on life and death.

C. Believers’ joy at victory

Al-Rūm 30:4 says:

«وَيَوْمَئِذٍ يَفْرَحُ الْمُؤْمِنُونَ»

Wa-yawmaʾidhin yafraḥu al-muʾminūn.

That is: “And on that day the believers will rejoice.”

Al-Rūm 30:5 connects this joy with بِنَصْرِ اللَّهِ, the help or victory of The God.

The context concerns the Romans’ defeat and subsequent victory.

Believers’ joy relates to a historical event and fulfillment of a promise stated in the preceding verses.

Joy can therefore arise from an outward event, victory, or the confirmation of a report.

This also shows that faraḥ itself does not mean pride, ingratitude, or boasting.

D. Rejoicing in revelation

Al-Raʿd 13:36 says:

«وَالَّذِينَ آتَيْنَاهُمُ الْكِتَابَ يَفْرَحُونَ بِمَا أُنْزِلَ إِلَيْكَ»

Wa-alladhīna ātaynāhumu al-kitāba yafraḥūna bi-mā unzila ilayk.

That is: “Those to whom We have given the Book rejoice in what has been sent down to you.”

This is another example of commendable joy.

Its object is the revelation sent down.

The continuation also mentions factions rejecting part of it.

All People of the Book should therefore not be assigned one intellectual or ethical position. The verse itself distinguishes those rejoicing in revelation from factions denying some of it.

The distinction comes from context, not from faraḥ belonging exclusively to a particular religious community.

4. Natural joy: an emotion evaluated alongside subsequent responses

Joy at a favorable wind during a sea journey

Yūnus 10:22 describes travelers whose ships sail with a favorable wind:

«وَجَرَيْنَ بِهِمْ بِرِيحٍ طَيِّبَةٍ وَفَرِحُوا بِهَا»

Wa-jarayna bihim bi-rīḥin ṭayyibatin wa-fariḥū bihā.

That is: “The ships carried them along with a favorable wind, and they rejoiced in it.”

Their happiness is understandable: favorable sailing conditions delight travelers at sea.

The verse then describes a violent wind and waves suddenly surrounding them.

They call upon The God sincerely and promise gratitude.

Yūnus 10:23 describes their response after rescue, when they return to wrongdoing.

The problem is therefore not delight at the favorable wind. It is conduct in prosperity and danger, and faithfulness or unfaithfulness to acknowledged commitments.

Joy at receiving mercy

Al-Rūm 30:36 says:

«وَإِذَا أَذَقْنَا النَّاسَ رَحْمَةً فَرِحُوا بِهَا»

Wa-idhā adhaqnā al-nāsa raḥmatan fariḥū bihā.

That is: “When We let people taste mercy, they rejoice in it.”

The same verse describes their response to adversity: they suddenly despair.

Al-Shūrā 42:48 offers a similar example: a person rejoices in mercy, but may become ungrateful when suffering the consequences of their own wrongdoing.

These verses place happiness alongside the response to hardship.

Joy in prosperity is not necessarily sinful. The problem emerges when one’s outlook rests entirely on the present moment: in comfort, acting as though hardship were impossible; in hardship, forgetting every blessing and possibility of good.

The passages thus distinguish natural joy from unconsidered attachment to passing circumstances.

5. Blameworthy joy: injustice, pride, and disregard for truth

A. Rejoicing in harm to others

Āl ʿImrān 3:120 says:

«وَإِنْ تُصِبْكُمْ سَيِّئَةٌ يَفْرَحُوا بِهَا»

Wa-in tuṣibkum sayyiʾatun yafraḥū bihā.

That is: “If adversity afflicts you, they rejoice in it.”

The verse concerns people displeased by the audience’s good fortune and delighted by its suffering.

Al-Tawba 9:50 describes similar conduct: good reaching Muhammad displeases them, but his adversity satisfies them, and they turn away rejoicing.

In both cases, another’s harm is central to the joy.

This differs from natural pleasure in one’s own blessing. A person can enjoy success without desiring others’ failure; here another’s suffering itself brings delight.

Such a response can indicate hostility and resentment.

The verses should not be used to condemn every joy in competition, including fair competition. They address others’ harm and misfortune in a hostile setting.

B. Joy at evading responsibility

Al-Tawba 9:81 says:

«فَرِحَ الْمُخَلَّفُونَ بِمَقْعَدِهِمْ خِلَافَ رَسُولِ اللَّهِ»

Fariḥa al-mukhallafūna bi-maqʿadihim khilāfa rasūli Allāh.

That is: “Those who remained behind rejoiced in staying seated and not accompanying the Messenger of The God.”

The continuation describes their reluctance to strive with possessions and lives in the divine cause, and their urging others not to go out in the heat.

The criticism does not concern mere rest or avoiding danger, but a particular group’s conduct in a setting of collective responsibility and opposition to the mission under discussion.

Faraḥ itself therefore cannot establish that delight in rest, comfort, or escape from danger is always blameworthy.

The assessment arises from the full context.

C. Joy mixed with exultation over blessings

Al-Anʿām 6:44 concerns people who forgot the reminders they received and were then granted abundant opportunities:

«حَتَّىٰ إِذَا فَرِحُوا بِمَا أُوتُوا أَخَذْنَاهُمْ بَغْتَةً»

Ḥattā idhā fariḥū bi-mā ūtū akhadhnāhum baghtatan.

That is: “Until, when they rejoiced in what they had been given, We seized them suddenly.”

Their joy occurs amid forgotten admonition, extensive prosperity, and subsequent surprise.

It would be wrong to detach the criticism from this context and conclude that everyone happy at receiving a blessing deserves punishment.

Al-Raʿd 13:26 also says:

«وَفَرِحُوا بِالْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا»

Wa-fariḥū bi-al-ḥayāti al-dunyā.

That is: “They rejoiced in worldly life and were satisfied with it.”

The verse then calls worldly life a limited enjoyment compared with the Hereafter.

The issue is an outlook confined to present advantages, as though current possessions exhausted life’s whole reality.

This connects with Hūd 11:10, which describes someone after receiving a blessing as فَرِحٌ فَخُورٌ, joyful and boastful.

Fariḥ expresses joy; fakhūr expresses boasting. The meaning of boasting should not be transferred to every occurrence of faraḥ.

D. Qārūn: joy in wealth and self-importance

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:76

إِنَّ قَارُونَ كَانَ مِنْ قَوْمِ مُوسَىٰ فَبَغَىٰ عَلَيْهِمْ وَآتَيْنَاهُ مِنَ الْكُنُوزِ مَا إِنَّ مَفَاتِحَهُ لَتَنُوءُ بِالْعُصْبَةِ أُولِي الْقُوَّةِ إِذْ قَالَ لَهُ قَوْمُهُ لَا تَفْرَحْ إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يُحِبُّ الْفَرِحِينَ

Inna Qārūna kāna min qawmi Mūsā fa-baghā ʿalayhim wa-ātaynāhu mina al-kunūzi mā inna mafātiḥahu la-tanūʾu bi-al-ʿuṣbati ulī al-quwwati idh qāla lahu qawmuhu lā tafraḥ; inna Allāha lā yuḥibbu al-fariḥīn.

Translation: “Qārūn was of Moses’s people, but he oppressed them. We gave him treasures whose keys weighed heavily upon a group of strong men. His people said to him: Do not exult arrogantly; indeed, The God does not love the exultant.”

This is a key passage for distinguishing natural joy from blameworthy exultation.

Reading only لَا تَفْرَحْ إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يُحِبُّ الْفَرِحِينَ might suggest that The God dislikes happiness.

But the verse first says:

«فَبَغَىٰ عَلَيْهِمْ»

Fa-baghā ʿalayhim.

Qārūn acted oppressively and arrogantly toward his people.

The person described is not merely wealthy and happy: his abundant wealth accompanies transgression.

In al-Qaṣaṣ 28:77, his people advise him to seek the Hereafter through what The God has given him, remember his worldly share, do good to others, and avoid corruption on earth.

This continuation does not prohibit possessions, enjoyment of life, or happiness.

Qārūn could benefit from his wealth while doing good and avoiding corruption.

The criticism concerns joy mixed with pride and oppression: wealth becomes an instrument of self-importance and injustice rather than a means of living and service.

In this context, “Do not exult arrogantly” conveys lā tafraḥ more precisely than an unqualified “Do not be happy.”

E. Joy in gifts and financial standing

In al-Naml 27:36, Solomon responds to the Queen of Sheba’s delegation and gift:

«بَلْ أَنْتُمْ بِهَدِيَّتِكُمْ تَفْرَحُونَ»

Bal antum bi-hadiyyatikum tafraḥūn.

That is: “Rather, it is you who rejoice in your gift.”

The statement responds to a financial proposal.

Solomon first says that what The God has given him is better than what they have received.

In this context he refuses to let a financial gift determine his decision.

Faraḥ denotes pleasure in a gift or material advantage; the ethical implication comes from the gift’s role in the exchange and Solomon’s response.

The verse does not establish that giving or enjoying gifts is inherently wrong.

F. Satisfaction with one’s beliefs and knowledge, even when confronted with truth

Al-Muʾminūn 23:53 says:

«كُلُّ حِزْبٍ بِمَا لَدَيْهِمْ فَرِحُونَ»

Kullu ḥizbin bi-mā ladayhim fariḥūn.

That is: “Each faction rejoices in what it has.”

Al-Rūm 30:32 repeats the expression concerning people who divided their religion and became separate factions.

The issue is not merely pleasure in social belonging, but factionalism and religious division.

Enjoying membership in a family, community, or circle of friends is not inherently blameworthy.

But when group loyalty replaces the search for truth, and a belief is deemed superior and beyond examination merely because it belongs to one’s group, delight in belonging approaches fanaticism.

Ghāfir 40:83 also says:

«فَرِحُوا بِمَا عِنْدَهُمْ مِنَ الْعِلْمِ»

Fariḥū bi-mā ʿindahum mina al-ʿilm.

That is: “They rejoiced in the knowledge they possessed.”

The context places this response at the arrival of their Messengers with clear evidence.

Natural pleasure in learning is not the problem. Confronted with clear evidence, they clung to existing knowledge and rejected the message.

Knowledge can be a great blessing. Yet regarding it as so complete that fresh evidence need not be examined can turn it into an instrument of pride.

G. Delight in unearned praise and credit

Āl ʿImrān 3:188 concerns those who rejoice in what they have done and want praise for what they have not done.

Two behaviors appear together:

Pleasure in what one has done or acquired, and the desire for praise for something not done.

The latter is crucial to understanding the criticism.

Someone may rightly be glad at doing good. Seeking credit for work not actually performed, however, concerns self-display and dishonest attribution of achievements.

بِمَا أَتَوْا here also differs from بِمَا أُوتُوا in al-Anʿām 6:44: the first concerns what they did, the second what they were given. Similar spelling should not erase the difference.

H. Wrongful rejoicing and boastful revelry

Ghāfir 40:75

ذَٰلِكُمْ بِمَا كُنْتُمْ تَفْرَحُونَ فِي الْأَرْضِ بِغَيْرِ الْحَقِّ وَبِمَا كُنْتُمْ تَمْرَحُونَ

Dhālikum bi-mā kuntum tafraḥūna fī al-arḍi bi-ghayri al-ḥaqqi wa-bi-mā kuntum tamraḥūn.

Translation: “That is because you rejoiced wrongfully on earth and indulged in boastful revelry.”

The qualification بِغَيْرِ الْحَقِّ is decisive.

The Qur’an does not merely say “you were happy”; it speaks of wrongful joy.

تَمْرَحُونَ belongs to another root and concerns exultant behavior, self-display, and excessive exuberance.

Faraḥ and maraḥ should therefore not be collapsed into a single word.

Their juxtaposition allows joy, its quality, and its accompanying outward conduct to be considered separately.

Again, the criticism targets wrongful joy and overbearing conduct, not the human capacity for happiness itself.

6. Al-Ḥadīd 57:23: does the Qur’an prohibit happiness and sorrow?

Al-Ḥadīd 57:23

لِكَيْلَا تَأْسَوْا عَلَىٰ مَا فَاتَكُمْ وَلَا تَفْرَحُوا بِمَا آتَاكُمْ ۗ وَاللَّهُ لَا يُحِبُّ كُلَّ مُخْتَالٍ فَخُورٍ

Li-kaylā taʾsaw ʿalā mā fātakum wa-lā tafraḥū bi-mā ātākum; wa-Allāhu lā yuḥibbu kulla mukhtālin fakhūr.

Translation: “So that you do not become consumed by grief over what has escaped you, nor exult in what He has given you. The God does not love any conceited boaster.”

This might be read as requiring neither grief at loss nor happiness at a blessing.

That conclusion, however, does not fit the full range of Qur’anic uses.

Yūnus 10:58 commands rejoicing, and Āl ʿImrān 3:170 describes the joy of those receiving divine bounty.

The Qur’an also describes Jacob’s sorrow in Joseph’s story without making the mere feeling of grief an independent sin.

Understanding al-Ḥadīd 57:23 therefore requires its preceding verse and its own ending.

Al-Ḥadīd 57:22 speaks of calamities and divine foreknowledge.

Al-Ḥadīd 57:23 then invites an outlook that maintains balance through losses and gains.

It ends:

«وَاللَّهُ لَا يُحِبُّ كُلَّ مُخْتَالٍ فَخُورٍ»

Wa-Allāhu lā yuḥibbu kulla mukhtālin fakhūr.

The God does not love a conceited boaster.

This ending clarifies the direction of the criticism.

Someone receiving wealth, status, health, or success can rejoice and give thanks.

Using that blessing as a measure of superiority to belittle others moves from natural happiness into boasting.

Grief at loss is also natural. The concern is that loss should not destroy all understanding and hope.

Al-Ḥadīd 57:23 thus addresses our relationship to blessings and losses, warning against exultation and conceit rather than demanding insensibility.

7. Distinctions from nearby roots

ف ر ح (فَرَح) and س ر ر (سُرُور)

Both concern joy and satisfaction.

Surūr can express happiness and contentment in the Qur’an. Al-Insān 76:11 describes the joy of those receiving divine mercy.

Faraḥ frequently appears as a response to events, blessings, news, or acquisitions.

This does not justify an absolute rule that surūr is always calm and enduring while faraḥ is fleeting or blameworthy.

Faraḥ in Āl ʿImrān 3:170 concerns life with the Lord, not merely transient worldly joy.

In al-Inshiqāq 84:13, masrūran describes someone happy in their former life who held a mistaken view of returning.

Neither root therefore carries an unchanging ethical value.

ف ر ح (فَرَح) and ب ط ر (بَطَر)

Baṭar in Qur’anic usage concerns exultation and an improper response to blessings, ability, or prosperity.

Faraḥ has a broader range, embracing natural, commendable, or blameworthy joy.

Someone can therefore enjoy a blessing without succumbing to baṭar.

If prosperity produces pride, ingratitude, and thoughtless conduct, joy becomes associated with baṭar.

This distinction matters for al-Qaṣaṣ 28:76 and Hūd 11:10.

ف ر ح (فَرَح) and م ر ح (مَرَح)

Maraḥ in Qur’anic ethical contexts concerns exultation, self-display, and proud behavior.

Tafraḥūn and tamraḥūn appear together in Ghāfir 40:75.

Their association shows that joy and exultant conduct can coexist without being exact synonyms.

Faraḥ expresses gladness; maraḥ here more closely concerns overbearing, self-displaying conduct.

ف ر ح (فَرَح) and ف خ ر (فَخَرَ)

Fakhr concerns boasting of advantages and displaying superiority.

فَرِحٌ فَخُورٌ in Hūd 11:10 shows that a person can be both happy and boastful.

Joy alone is not boasting.

Parents may rejoice in their child’s success. If they use it to belittle other children, their happiness becomes mixed with boastful conduct.

The example distinguishes the concepts; it does not condemn every pleasure in personal or family success.

ف ر ح (فَرَح) and ب ش ر (بُشْرَى)

Bishāra concerns good news and awareness of a welcome event.

Faraḥ is the joyful response to such news or an occurrence.

Fariḥīn and yastabshirūn appear together in Āl ʿImrān 3:170.

They can reinforce one another, but not every joy follows fresh news, and glad tidings do not themselves denote the emotion of joy.

ف ر ح (فَرَح) and ح ز ن (حُزْن)

Ḥuzn concerns sorrow and grief, contrasting with joy in the emotional field.

People can nevertheless feel both about different matters simultaneously: happiness at a loved one’s recovery and grief at another person’s loss, for example.

The contrast should not be turned into two permanently separate human conditions.

Both are human emotions. Their ethical assessment must distinguish involuntary feeling from intentional attitudes and conduct; an emotion’s occurrence alone does not establish moral responsibility.

8. Morphology and syntax

Qur’anic ف ر ح forms belong to the basic Form I verb and the adjective fariḥ.

فَرِحَ is a perfect verb, as in al-Tawba 9:81. By contrast, لَفَرِحٌ in Hūd 11:10 consists of an emphatic lām and the adjective fariḥ; it is not a perfect verb.

يَفْرَحُ is an imperfect verb, as in al-Rūm 30:4.

فَلْيَفْرَحُوا in Yūnus 10:58 has directive force through the lām of command: “Let them rejoice.”

لَا تَفْرَحْ in al-Qaṣaṣ 28:76 is a prohibition whose context concerns Qārūn’s exultant pride.

فَرِحُونَ and فَرِحِينَ are plural forms of the adjective fariḥ.

Nominative and accusative inflections do not change the underlying sense of joy; they reflect syntactic function.

The preposition bāʾ

Many Qur’anic uses of the verb take the preposition bāʾ.

For example:

فَرِحُوا بِمَا أُوتُوا — al-Anʿām 6:44.

فَرِحِينَ بِمَا آتَاهُمُ اللَّهُ — Āl ʿImrān 3:170.

فَرِحُوا بِالْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا — al-Raʿd 13:26.

فَرِحُوا بِمَا عِنْدَهُمْ مِنَ الْعِلْمِ — Ghāfir 40:83.

Bāʾ connects the verb with the object or cause of joy.

The preposition alone does not determine whether the joy is commendable. Divine bounty, worldly life, possessions, and knowledge each require contextual assessment.

Translation should therefore not impose one fixed equivalent while overlooking the cause of joy.

9. Challenges in understanding and translation

The first error condemns all happiness.

Yūnus 10:58, Āl ʿImrān 3:170, al-Rūm 30:4, and al-Raʿd 13:36 show that joy may arise from bounty, mercy, guidance, and good fulfilled.

The second error approves every kind of joy.

Āl ʿImrān 3:120 and al-Tawba 9:50 describe delight in others’ harm. Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:76 concerns Qārūn’s oppression, and Ghāfir 40:75 condemns wrongful rejoicing.

The third error makes faraḥ equivalent to baṭar or boasting everywhere.

Baṭar, fakhr, maraḥ, and conceit are distinct words. They can accompany happiness, but should not enter its lexical definition without contextual grounds.

The fourth error prohibits enjoyment of worldly blessings.

Worldly life offers blessings and opportunities for good. The Qur’an does not universally deny happiness in blessings; it warns about our response to them.

The fifth error equates delight in group membership with faith and truth.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:53 and al-Rūm 30:32 describe factions pleased with their own positions amid religious division.

Satisfaction with a belief or social affiliation alone does not prove the belief true.

Finally, al-Ḥadīd 57:23 should not become a theory of eliminating human emotion. The Qur’an calls for understanding, gratitude, steadfastness, and responsible responses to happiness and sorrow.

10. Translation proposals for Forqan

For general فَرِحَ, “rejoiced,” “was happy,” or “became glad” is suitable.

Depending on context, فَرِحٌ and فَرِحُونَ may be “joyful,” “happy,” “pleased,” or, where warranted, “exultant and proud.”

In Yūnus 10:58:

“In that let them rejoice.”

In Āl ʿImrān 3:170:

“Rejoicing in what The God has given them of His bounty.”

In al-Rūm 30:4:

“And on that day the believers will rejoice.”

In al-Qaṣaṣ 28:76:

“Do not exult arrogantly; indeed, The God does not love the exultant.”

In al-Muʾminūn 23:53 and al-Rūm 30:32:

“Each faction rejoices in what it has.”

In Ghāfir 40:75:

“You rejoiced wrongfully.”

In al-Ḥadīd 57:23:

“Nor exult in what He has given you.”

These renderings distinguish basic meaning from contextual evaluation. “Exultation” or “pride” may clarify a sentence where its context warrants them, but condemnation alone does not make pride part of every occurrence’s meaning.

11. Conclusion

Early Arabic ف ر ح concerns joy and happiness. Poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period and lexical accounts show responses to prosperity, success, or welcome events, sometimes approaching exultation.

Arabic sources also record a branch meaning to burden. Qur’anic occurrences all belong to the joy branch; the two should not be merged into one historical image without evidence.

The Qur’an evaluates joy through its cause and accompanying conduct.

Joy in bounty, mercy, guidance, and good fulfilled can be commendable. Delight in others’ harm, intoxication with wealth and power, intellectual arrogance, and factional attachment are criticized in their respective contexts.

Two verses are especially significant: Yūnus 10:58 commands rejoicing, while al-Qaṣaṣ 28:76 prohibits Qārūn’s arrogant exultation.

They do not conflict, since the object and quality of joy differ.

The central Qur’anic meaning of ف ر ح is joy and satisfaction at something regarded as desirable. Its ethical value is understood through its cause, object, and accompanying conduct, not the root alone.

Translation should distinguish natural happiness, commendable joy, and blameworthy exultation. This preserves both the Qur’an’s invitation to rejoice in blessings and its warnings about pride, ingratitude, hostility, and boasting.
