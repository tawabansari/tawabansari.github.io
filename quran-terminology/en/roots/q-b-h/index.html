Qubḥ: ugliness or objectionable appearance and conduct; maqbūḥ: someone condemned, despised, or rejected

1. Meaning and background in early Arabic

Q B Ḥ concerns ugliness and objectionableness, opposite to ḥusn. Qabuḥa means to be ugly or objectionable; qabīḥ describes something judged unpleasant in appearance, conduct, or quality.

Qubḥ is not confined to people's or objects' outward form. Speech, behaviour, decisions, and deeds can also be described through this family.

Someone may, for example, find a face or image unattractive. That is a judgment about appearance.

When someone harms others through lying, betrayal, or oppression, their conduct can be called qabīḥ. Here ugliness concerns conduct and moral value, not physical form.

These uses share a linguistic field but must remain distinct. An unattractive appearance does not establish wrongdoing, just as facial beauty does not establish justice or integrity.

Early Arabic sources define qubḥ in contrast to ḥusn, applying the contrast to both outward form and human actions.

Another branch concerns condemnation, rejection, and removal from good.

Qabaḥahu Allāhu is recorded for removing someone from good and placing them in a condemned condition. Maqbūḥ in this use denotes someone condemned and rejected.

Three concepts must therefore be distinguished: being ugly or objectionable; judging something ugly and condemning it; and rejecting or removing someone.

A witness from al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī

A poem attributed to al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī reads:

وَلَيْسَتْ بِشَوْهَاءَ مَقْبُوحَةٍ

wa-laysat bi-shawhāʾa maqbūḥatin

تُوَافِي الدِّيَارَ بِوَجْهٍ غَبِرْ

tuwāfī al-diyāra bi-wajhin ghabir

“She is not ugly and despised, coming to the dwellings with a dust-covered face.”

Maqbūḥah appears beside shawhāʾ.

Shawhāʾ concerns ugliness or ill-formed appearance; maqbūḥah adds condemnation and disapproval.

Their pairing illustrates the family's use for an objectionable appearance in Arabic poetry.

Maqbūḥ is not limited to physical ugliness in every sentence: the family also condemns conduct and describes rejecting people.

Al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī lived before and after the rise of Islam. Early dictionaries and poetry attributed to him record this verse, but its precise composition date is unclear.

It is therefore not an unqualified, certainly pre-Quranic witness. Its value lies in documenting maqbūḥah within the early Arabic tradition.

A witness from al-Ḥuṭayʾah

A poem attributed to al-Ḥuṭayʾah reads:

أَرَى لَكَ وَجْهًا قَبَّحَ اللَّهُ شَخْصَهُ

arā laka wajhan qabbaḥa Allāhu shakhṣahu

فَقُبِّحَ مِنْ وَجْهٍ وَقُبِّحَ حَامِلُهْ

fa-qubbiḥa min wajhin wa-qubbiḥa ḥāmiluh

“I see a face of yours—may God make its form ugly! Condemned be that face, and condemned its bearer!”

This is an example of satire and condemnation in Arabic poetry.

The poet uses several forms of Q B Ḥ to express scorn and condemnation.

Qabbaḥa and qubbiḥa are not merely neutral descriptions of appearance; they occur in condemnatory speech.

The witness illustrates a movement from describing objectionableness to condemning and expressing aversion.

Al-Ḥuṭayʾah also lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, and the verse's date is not independently established. It witnesses the family's continued early use, not its certain pre-Islamic origin.

Historical findings

The linguistic and poetic witnesses show two principal fields.

First, qubḥ contrasts with ḥusn and concerns objectionable appearance, speech, or conduct.

Second, the family's verbs can express condemnation, treating someone as repugnant, and rejection.

Some early philologists identify removal from good as the central meaning of qabaḥahu Allāhu.

Independent evidence is needed to determine whether rejection came first historically or ugliness developed into condemnation and rejection.

A defensible historical definition: early Arabic Q B Ḥ concerns ugliness or objectionableness in form and conduct; its transitive uses also concern condemning, despising, and removing someone from good.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important recorded forms include:

  • Qabuḥa: was ugly or objectionable.
  • Yaqbuḥu: is ugly or objectionable.
  • Qubḥ: ugliness or objectionableness, opposite to ḥusn.
  • Qabāḥah: ugliness or objectionableness.
  • Qabīḥ: ugly, objectionable, or blameworthy.
  • Qabīḥah: feminine of qabīḥ.
  • Qabāʾiḥ: ugly things or objectionable deeds.
  • Qabaḥa: transitively, condemned or removed from good.
  • Qubūḥ: a recorded verbal noun concerning condemnation and rejection.
  • Maqbūḥ: condemned, despised, or rejected.
  • Qabbaḥa: judged ugly, condemned, or exposed something's ugliness.
  • Taqbīḥ: judging ugly or condemning.
  • Muqabbaḥ: judged ugly or condemned.
  • Aqbaḥa: performed an ugly deed. The separate comparative adjective aqbaḥ means uglier or more objectionable.
  • Istaqbaḥa: judged ugly or objectionable.
  • Istiqbāḥ: disapproval, opposite to istiḥsān.
  • Muqābaḥah: mutual condemnation or exchange of offensive words.

Some early sources also record qabīḥ as a name for part of the upper-arm bone near the elbow. This anatomical use should not be treated without evidence as the origin of all the family's moral or visual meanings.

Not all these forms occur in the Quran.

Its only Quranic derivative is al-maqbūḥīn.

Distinguishing qabuḥa, qabaḥa, and qabbaḥa

They belong to one family but differ in structure and use.

Qabuḥa is intransitive. Qabuḥa al-fiʿlu means the act was ugly or objectionable.

The act itself bears that quality.

Transitive qabaḥa can concern condemning or removing someone.

Qabbaḥa is Form II and is used for judging ugly, condemning, or exposing ugliness.

A behaviour being objectionable and someone judging it objectionable are different matters.

Someone may act wrongly, making their conduct qabīḥ.

Another person condemning it performs taqbīḥ.

Condemnation does not always prove actual wrongness: ignorance, prejudice, or hostility may cause someone to disapprove of a good act.

The conduct, the judgment about it, and that judgment's correctness must therefore be distinguished.

Distinguishing qabīḥ from maqbūḥ

Qabīḥ describes something regarded as ugly or objectionable.

Maqbūḥ is the passive participle of transitive qabaḥa, on the mafʿūl pattern.

Applied to a person, it denotes someone subjected to condemnation and rejection.

A behaviour may be qabīḥ, while someone condemned for that behaviour can be maqbūḥ.

The distinction matters in the root's sole Quranic occurrence.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:42 does not say hum mina al-qabīḥīn; it uses al-maqbūḥīn.

The derivative therefore includes being placed in a condemned and rejected condition, beyond merely possessing an objectionable quality.

3. Quranic use: Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:42

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:42

وَأَتْبَعْنَاهُمْ فِي هَٰذِهِ الدُّنْيَا لَعْنَةً ۖ وَيَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ هُمْ مِنَ الْمَقْبُوحِينَ

wa-atbaʿnāhum fī hādhihi al-dunyā laʿnatan wa-yawma al-qiyāmati hum mina al-maqbūḥīn

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:42

“We made a curse follow them in this world, and on the Day of Resurrection they will be among the despised.”

This follows the account of Pharaoh and his troops, their conduct, and their fate.

Understanding al-maqbūḥīn requires identifying the people concerned and the basis for their stated outcome.

The context: Pharaoh's conduct

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:4 describes Pharaoh exalting himself in the land, dividing its people into groups, and weakening one group.

The same verse describes killing the oppressed group's sons and sparing its women.

Pharaoh is thus portrayed through oppression, discrimination, and corruption, beyond merely possessing political power.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:38 he tells his notables that he knows of no deity for them other than himself.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:39 then describes his and his troops' unjustified arrogance in the land.

It also states that they thought they would not be returned to God.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:40 they face the consequences and are cast into the water.

The passage thus concerns power accompanied by domination, oppression, killing, and arrogance, rather than mere differences of belief or knowledge.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:41: leaders inviting to the Fire

The preceding verse, Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:41, presents Pharaoh and his associates as leaders inviting to the Fire.

Aʾimmah here describes leaders with a wrongful direction.

The title imam or leader therefore does not by itself establish truth or legitimacy.

Someone may lead others toward truth and justice.

Someone else may use the same social position to draw people into oppression, corruption, and misguidance.

A position of leadership must therefore be distinguished from the truth and direction of that leadership.

This context matters for Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:42: al-maqbūḥīn concerns people whose conduct and roles the preceding verses have identified.

Wa-atbaʿnāhum fī hādhihi al-dunyā laʿnatan

The opening states that a curse was made to follow them in this world.

Laʿnah belongs to L ʿ N and concerns rejection and removal from good and mercy.

The meaning of laʿnah requires its own lexical study and should not simply replace maqbūḥ.

The curse follows Pharaoh's and his troops' conduct and fate.

It should not be reduced to insults other people utter after their deaths.

The Quran attributes this condition to God.

Understanding laʿnah alone is nevertheless insufficient for maqbūḥ, since the verse uses two distinct words.

Wa-yawma al-qiyāmati hum mina al-maqbūḥīn

The second part moves to the Day of Resurrection.

Yawma al-qiyāmah is an expression of time.

Hum is a plural pronoun referring to the same people discussed previously.

Min is a preposition placing them among a particular group here.

Al-maqbūḥīn is the sound masculine plural of maqbūḥ, in the genitive after min.

The sentence therefore means:

On the Day of Resurrection they will belong among those despised, condemned, and rejected.

What does maqbūḥ mean?

Early Arabic sources explain it in this verse through several familiar descriptions.

One is someone removed and driven away from good.

Another is someone regarded as despised and condemned.

The ugliness or objectionableness of the person's condition also belongs within the family.

These meanings are close, but should not all become a theory about physical appearance.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:42 concerns divine judgment of Pharaoh and his oppressive associates.

In this context, al-maqbūḥīn therefore chiefly concerns their despised and rejected condition, rather than facial or bodily unattractiveness alone.

Some early exegetical explanations also associate it with altered appearance in the Hereafter.

The verse itself does not specify their skin colour, eyes, or precise outward features.

Such details cannot be extracted as the word's certain meaning.

Is maqbūḥ identical to malʿūn?

The verse pairs a curse in this world with being maqbūḥ on the Day of Resurrection.

The concepts are closely related.

Laʿnah emphasizes exclusion from mercy and good.

Maqbūḥ includes condemnation and being despised alongside its association with rejection.

The verse thus presents two aspects of their outcome.

A curse follows them in this world.

On the Day of Resurrection they are among the despised and rejected.

This does not restrict laʿnah to this world or qubḥ to the Hereafter. The roots do not establish such an absolute division.

Their timing and placement come from this verse's construction.

Why are they described as despised?

The answer requires returning to their described conduct.

Pharaoh had weakened a group of people.

Their sons were being killed.

The population had been divided into groups.

Pharaoh and his troops behaved arrogantly without right.

They also stood as leaders inviting people to the Fire.

Their condemnation thus has a stated context and basis.

Someone may enjoy power, wealth, troops, and social standing, with others bowing before them; these do not establish their worth before God.

Pharaoh ruled in this world, but the Quran describes his and his oppressive associates' outcome through curse and condemnation.

Public deference to a powerful authority does not by itself establish its legitimacy.

Nor does being despised on the Day of Resurrection imply that everyone hated in this world is condemned before God.

A just person may be hated by oppressors for resisting injustice.

An oppressive ruler may be loved by followers.

Divine judgment must therefore be distinguished from social popularity or unpopularity.

4. Physical ugliness and moral condemnation

The family's scope includes both appearance and conduct.

Ordinary language can apply ugliness to shape or form.

One person may find an image beautiful and another unattractive.

Such judgment partly reflects taste, knowledge, and visual criteria.

Moral use concerns behaviour and its consequences.

Oppressing the defenceless, unjust killing, betrayal, and deception can be objectionable even when their perpetrator is physically attractive or socially respected.

Conversely, a person's facial difference, disability, illness, or outward condition does not itself establish moral ugliness.

The Quran likewise does not determine human worth solely through appearance.

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:11 prohibits mocking and belittling others.

Al-maqbūḥīn describing oppressors in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:42 therefore does not license degrading people's appearance or attributing divine judgment to their physical characteristics.

The group's outcome is connected to its conduct and history.

This distinction is essential: one lexical family may operate in both fields while the criteria of judgment differ.

5. Comparisons with nearby roots

Q B Ḥ (qubḥ) and Ḥ S N (ḥusn): ugliness and goodness

Ḥusn concerns beauty, goodness, and approval.

Qubḥ contrasts with it, expressing ugliness or disapproval.

Visually, both can describe shape and appearance.

Morally, they concern human conduct.

Speech may be good in content and manner.

Other speech may contain lies, contempt, or harm and be objectionable.

Only al-maqbūḥīn from Q B Ḥ occurs in the Quran, however.

Their lexical contrast does not place every Quranic ḥusn opposite qubḥ or make these two terms express all Quranic moral distinctions.

Q B Ḥ (qubḥ) and S W ʾ (sūʾ): ugliness and evil

Sūʾ is used for evil, unpleasantness, and harmful outcomes.

Its broad family can concern conduct, conditions, or punishment.

Ghāfir 40:45 mentions sūʾ al-ʿadhāb concerning Pharaoh's people.

Sūʾ there expresses the punishment's grievous quality.

Al-maqbūḥīn in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:42 describes the people themselves under judgment.

In these uses, sūʾ concerns badness and adversity, while maqbūḥ denotes someone condemned and despised.

The meanings can relate without being identical.

Q B Ḥ (qubḥ) and L ʿ N (laʿn): condemnation and exclusion

Laʿn concerns driving away from good and mercy.

Qubḥ principally concerns ugliness and objectionableness; personal uses also include condemnation and rejection.

They appear together in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:42.

A curse in this world, and condemnation on the Day of Resurrection.

Both concern an adverse outcome here while contributing distinct meanings.

Laʿnah more clearly emphasizes exclusion and removal.

Maqbūḥ highlights condemnation, being despised, and a rejected condition.

The translation should therefore preserve two distinct equivalents.

Q B Ḥ (qubḥ) and M Q T (maqt): disapproval and intense abhorrence

Maqt concerns intense aversion and abhorrence.

Al-Ṣaff 61:3 expresses great abhorrence before God at saying what one does not do.

It concerns a behaviour's intense objectionableness.

Qubḥ can likewise involve aversion and condemnation, but extends to physical ugliness in Arabic.

The roots are therefore not completely synonymous.

Al-maqbūḥīn in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:42 denotes people counted among the despised and condemned.

The word alone does not specify an exact degree or kind of aversion.

Q B Ḥ (qubḥ) and KH Z Y (khizy): ugliness and disgrace

Khizy concerns humiliation, disgrace, and a shameful condition.

Al-Zumar 39:26 speaks of tasting disgrace in worldly life.

Maqbūḥ under judgment can likewise be associated with humiliation and condemnation.

Khizy emphasizes disgrace, while Q B Ḥ also concerns disapproval, judging ugly, and rejection.

Someone may become both disgraced and despised through their conduct.

Disgrace and being despised are nevertheless distinct concepts.

Q B Ḥ (qubḥ) and SH W H (shawah): ugliness and ill-formed appearance

SH W H concerns ugliness or ill-formed appearance in Arabic.

Al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī pairs shawhāʾ with maqbūḥah.

They can thus overlap in describing appearance.

Qubḥ also applies to conduct and condemnation.

A visual poetic witness alone should therefore not restrict Quranic al-maqbūḥīn to “the misshapen” or “the ugly-faced.”

The context of Pharaoh and Resurrection supports condemnation and being despised.

Q B Ḥ (qubḥ) and Q B R (qabr): separate roots

Q B R concerns graves and burial.

Q B Ḥ concerns ugliness and condemnation.

Only their final letters differ.

Quranic al-maqbūḥīn is not lexically connected to graves, burial, or being dead.

Written similarity should not merge the families.

6. Challenges in interpretation and translation

Does maqbūḥ only mean ugly-faced?

No. Arabic sources also apply it to someone condemned, despised, or removed from good.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:42 concerns Pharaoh and his associates and their outcome on Resurrection.

“The ugly-faced” would restrict the verse to an outward feature it does not specify.

Does being maqbūḥ mean being destroyed?

Some early explanations interpret al-maqbūḥīn through destruction as well.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:40 has already described Pharaoh's and his troops' destruction in water.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:42 then addresses their condition on Resurrection.

“The destroyed” alone would not adequately convey the family's condemnation and rejection.

“The despised” or “the rejected” better suits this context.

Is everyone hated by people also maqbūḥ before God?

No. Social hatred and condemnation in divine judgment differ.

Resistance to injustice can make someone hated by oppressors.

An oppressor may conversely enjoy popularity and many followers.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:42 presents God's judgment within the specific conduct of Pharaoh and his troops.

Popularity or unpopularity alone therefore does not measure people's final standing before God.

Does qubḥ mean physical impurity?

No. Its visual and behavioural uses do not make moral qubḥ necessarily denote bodily contamination.

Calling conduct qabīḥ concerns its wrongness or objectionableness, not material pollution of the body.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:42 does not explain the bodily composition or precise appearance of Pharaoh and his associates.

Is qubḥ always a certain moral judgment?

No. People's ordinary judgments of disapproval may be mistaken.

Cultural, linguistic, or visual differences may prompt some to judge another person's conduct or traits ugly.

Such disapproval alone does not constitute a divine moral ruling.

The Quran offers clearer criteria concerning oppression, corruption, and unjust killing.

Personal disapproval should therefore be distinguished from actual wrongdoing in human uses.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:42 concerns divine judgment of a group with specified deeds, rather than mere human taste or hatred.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Suitable equivalents include:

Qabuḥa: was ugly or objectionable.

Yaqbuḥu: is ugly or objectionable.

Qubḥ: ugliness or objectionableness.

Qabāḥah: ugliness or objectionableness.

Qabīḥ: ugly, objectionable, blameworthy.

Qabīḥah: ugly or objectionable, feminine.

Qabāʾiḥ: ugly things or objectionable deeds.

Qabaḥa: transitively, condemned or removed from good.

Qabbaḥa: judged ugly or condemned.

Taqbīḥ: judging ugly or condemning.

Maqbūḥ: despised, condemned, rejected.

Muqabbaḥ: judged ugly or condemned.

Istaqbaḥa: judged ugly or objectionable.

Istiqbāḥ: disapproval, opposite to istiḥsān.

The sole Quranic occurrence

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:42: al-maqbūḥīn

Principal equivalent: “the despised.”

The existing Forqan translation reads:

“We made a curse follow them in this world, and on the Day of Resurrection they will be among the despised.”

This preserves condemnation and being despised, fitting divine judgment in the passage.

“The rejected” is also defensible, especially in light of qabaḥahu Allāhu as removal from good.

Since laʿnatan already conveys exclusion here, “the despised” better maintains the distinction.

“The ugly-faced” and “the misshapen” are unsuitable restrictions to physical appearance.

“The destroyed” also fails to convey the full condemnation and rejection.

On this lexical point, the current Forqan translation needs no change.

8. Conclusion

Arabic Q B Ḥ concerns ugliness and objectionableness, opposite to ḥusn.

This may apply to appearance, speech, or conduct.

Its other branch concerns condemning, judging ugly, and rejecting.

Early poetic witnesses, including maqbūḥah attributed to al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī and al-Ḥuṭayʾah's condemnatory expressions, document both description and condemnation.

The precise historical emergence of each branch remains unclear. Neither rejection nor physical ugliness should become the certain origin of every derivative without evidence.

Only al-maqbūḥīn occurs in the Quran.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:42, after the account of Pharaoh and his troops and their worldly curse, it describes their condition on Resurrection.

Here it concerns being despised, condemned, and rejected, rather than bodily unattractiveness alone.

The judgment is connected to their specified oppression, domination, killing, and misleading leadership.

Final definition for Forqan:

Q B Ḥ principally denotes ugliness or objectionableness in appearance, speech, or conduct. Its condemnatory branch includes judging ugly, despising, and removing someone from good.

Qabīḥ describes something regarded as ugly or objectionable; personal maqbūḥ denotes someone subjected to condemnation and rejection.

Its sole Quranic occurrence, al-maqbūḥīn, means “the despised”: people whose condemnation and outcome in divine judgment are connected to their deeds.
