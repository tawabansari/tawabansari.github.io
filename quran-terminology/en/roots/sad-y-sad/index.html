Fortresses and means of defence: from animal horns and a cock’s spur to weaving implements and fortified refuges

1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

Arabic ص ي ص (Ṣ Y Ṣ) has several recorded uses, the most prominent concerning defence, resistance, and taking shelter. صِيصِيَة (ṣīṣiyah) can name a bovine horn, a cock’s thorn or spur, a pointed weaving implement, or a fortress.

Protection and resistance offer a semantic connection: cattle defend themselves with their horns, a cock uses its spur in fighting, and people shelter behind fortress walls against attack.

Not every use is confined to defence. Arabic sources also record صِيص (ṣīṣ) for poorly developed, inferior dates. Lexicographers also disagree over the derivational structure of ṣīṣiyah.

The Qur’an uses this root only once:

صَيَاصِيهِمْ (ṣayāṣīhim): their fortresses; Al-Aḥzāb 33:26.

The word combines ṣayāṣī with the pronoun hum, “their.” Ṣayāṣī is the broken plural of ṣīṣiyah or ṣīṣah and refers in this verse to fortified refuges and strongholds.

Recorded Arabic forms include:

  • صِيصِيَة (ṣīṣiyah): a cock’s thorn or spur, an animal horn, a pointed weaving implement, or a fortress, depending on the subject.
  • صِيصَة (ṣīṣah): a shorter recorded form, especially a means of defence or shelter.
  • صَيَاصِي (ṣayāṣī): plural of ṣīṣiyah or ṣīṣah: horns, weaving implements, or fortresses.
  • صِيص (ṣīṣ): poorly developed or inferior dates in a dialectal use.
  • صِيصَاء (ṣīṣāʾ): recorded for inferior dates and for poorly filled colocynth seeds.
  • صَيَّصَتِ النَّخْلَةُ (ṣayyaṣati al-nakhlatu): the date palm produced poorly developed, inferior fruit, in a recorded lexical use.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an. Its only form is ṣayāṣīhim, directly denoting fortresses and defensive refuges in the verse.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the sole occurrence at Al-Aḥzāb 33:26

2. The semantic core: protection and resistance

Ibn Fāris’s analysis

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris explains this family through something people or animals use to protect themselves.

He mentions fortresses, the cock’s spur, and bovine horns, identifying their shared defensive function.

This semantic analysis is readily understandable.

A fortress protects people behind its walls and fortifications. Horns and spurs can likewise be used against an attacking animal.

Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī’s Al-Mufradāt also discusses this connection under ṣayāṣī, identifying the common concept as something used for shelter or defence.

Established meanings must nevertheless be distinguished from the history of the word’s emergence.

The defensive function of both fortress and horn makes their semantic connection understandable, but does not independently establish that ṣīṣiyah first named a horn and was then transferred to a fortress, or the reverse.

Some early explanations propose a direction of transfer; establishing it as certain derivational history requires further evidence.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah, under Ṣ Y: fortresses and defensive means

Bovine horns and those of other animals

Arabic صَيَاصِي الْبَقَرِ (ṣayāṣī al-baqari) denotes the horns of cattle.

A horn is a body part that may serve in defence, fighting, and repelling danger.

Lexical sources report the use of bovine or gazelle horns to make implements. A horn could even be fitted to a spear in place of its head.

Here the horn is detached from the animal and becomes a human implement for work or defence.

Ṣayāṣī can thus name a natural animal organ or, in another use, an implement made from it.

This does not mean that every horn is necessarily used in warfare or that every horned animal behaves identically in defence.

The lexical usage identifies the organ; its actual function varies with the animal and situation.

The cock’s thorn or spur

Arabic صِيصِيَةُ الدِّيكِ (ṣīṣiyatu al-dīki) names the thorn or spur at the back of a cock’s leg.

The projection may be used in fighting other animals.

Some sources describe it as a thorn, others as a claw or talon, and others as an additional digit at the back of the foot.

These descriptions identify a particular defensive organ without giving entirely identical anatomical accounts.

“A cock’s spur” is a clear present-day equivalent for this use.

Its relationship to a bovine horn is understandable: both are projecting organs that animals can use in conflict and defence.

Their natural structures are nevertheless different. A shared name does not imply biological identity.

Al-ʿAyn, Al-Mufradāt, and Lisān al-ʿArab: horns, spurs, and refuge

3. Ṣīṣiyah as a weaving implement

The weaver’s pointed implement

Arabic also uses صِيصِيَة (ṣīṣiyah) for an implement employed in weaving cloth.

It could be made from an animal horn and used to arrange, separate, or move warp and weft threads.

Lisān al-ʿArab describes it as a thorn or pointed implement used to even the woven threads.

Abū al-Haytham explains that women could use a small piece of gazelle horn for this purpose.

Its material connects it with the animal’s horn, while its function is no longer defence against an enemy.

The weaver presses on the threads, moves them, and arranges the weave.

When ṣīṣiyah denotes a weaving implement, “a means of defence” is therefore insufficient as a translation. The particular use means a weaving implement.

Lisān al-ʿArab, under Ṣ Y ʾ: the gazelle-horn weaving implement

A poetic witness from Durayd ibn al-Ṣimmah

A poem attributed to Durayd ibn al-Ṣimmah concerning his brother ʿAbd Allāh says:

فَجِئْتُ إِلَيْهِ وَالرِّمَاحُ تَنُوشُهُ
كَوَقْعِ الصَّيَاصِي فِي النَّسِيجِ الْمُمَدَّدِ

fa-jiʾtu ilayhi wa-al-rimāḥu tanūshuhu / ka-waqʿi al-ṣayāṣī fī al-nasīji al-mumaddadi

Translation:

“I came to him while spears were reaching him, like the striking of pointed weaving implements into stretched cloth.”

Al-ṣayāṣī here denotes weaving implements.

The poet compares repeated spear strikes to weaving implements entering and moving among the cloth’s threads.

The image shows that the implement was familiar in Arabic and that its shape and movement could illustrate spear strikes.

Durayd ibn al-Ṣimmah was a pre-Islamic poet and warrior who lived into the early Islamic period. Early sources attribute this verse to him, but its precise composition date is not independently established.

Khizānat al-Adab: Durayd’s witness in the elegy for his brother

Animal horns in weaving

Another verse states:

فَأَصْبَحَتِ الثِّيرَانُ غَرْقَى وَأَصْبَحَتْ
نِسَاءُ تَمِيمٍ يَلْتَقِطْنَ الصَّيَاصِيَا

fa-aṣbaḥati al-thīrānu gharqā wa-aṣbaḥat / nisāʾu tamīmin yaltaqiṭna al-ṣayāṣiyā

Translation:

“The wild cattle lay drowned, and the women of Tamīm began gathering their horns.”

The lexical explanation says the women took the horns for use in weaving.

This witness connects two uses: the animal horn and the pointed implement made from it.

Some sources attribute the verse to Suḥaym, the enslaved poet of Banū al-Ḥasḥās, and others to Al-Nābighah al-Jaʿdī.

The attribution is therefore uncertain, and the verse should not be presented as a firmly dated pre-Islamic witness without independent examination.

Its lexical value lies in ṣayāṣī naming horns used to make implements.

Lisān al-ʿArab: the horn witness and attribution to Suḥaym or Al-Nābighah

4. Fortress and refuge: the Qur’anic use

Ṣīṣiyah as a means of fortification

Arabic also uses ṣīṣiyah for a fortress or place where people take shelter and protect themselves against attack.

Its plural is ṣayāṣī.

Al-Farrāʾ glosses the Qur’anic ṣayāṣīhim as ḥuṣūnihim, “their fortresses.”

Al-Zajjāj likewise relates ṣayāṣī to things people use to defend themselves and take shelter.

A fortress in this established sense is therefore more than an ordinary dwelling: its defensive and protective function matters.

It may have high walls, strong gates, or a position difficult to approach, but ṣīṣiyah itself does not specify its exact form.

A fortress may stand on high or level ground, with walls of stone, earth, or other materials.

The shared feature is the possibility of shelter and resistance to attack.

Is every house or palace a ṣīṣiyah?

Arabic sources sometimes use ṣayāṣī for palaces and fortified settlements.

This does not mean that every ordinary house, village, or palace is called ṣīṣiyah.

Their protective role places such buildings in this field.

A palace with defensive walls and fortifications where people can shelter may, for example, belong to it.

A house built only for daily life, without a particular defensive feature, need not bear this designation.

“Fortress” is therefore more precise than “house” or “palace” for the Qur’anic use.

Defensive implement and refuge: two forms of protection

A bovine horn and a human fortress differ greatly in size, structure, and material.

Both may nevertheless help protect a creature against danger.

The horn is an implement of the animal’s defence; the fortress is a place where people shelter behind fortifications.

This connection offers a reasonable account of their inclusion in one lexical family.

Defence is nevertheless only one possible function of a horn, and taking shelter does not describe every feature of a historical fortress.

The verse should therefore not replace “fortresses” with the general “defensive implements”: its context concerns bringing people out of fortified places.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Al-Farrāʾ and Al-Zajjāj on fortresses

5. The Qur’anic use: min ṣayāṣīhim

This family’s sole Qur’anic occurrence is in Al-Aḥzāb:

وَأَنْزَلَ الَّذِينَ ظَاهَرُوهُمْ مِنْ أَهْلِ الْكِتَابِ مِنْ صَيَاصِيهِمْ وَقَذَفَ فِي قُلُوبِهِمُ الرُّعْبَ فَرِيقًا تَقْتُلُونَ وَتَأْسِرُونَ فَرِيقًا

wa-anzala alladhīna ẓāharūhum min ahli al-kitābi min ṣayāṣīhim wa-qadhafa fī qulūbihimu al-ruʿba farīqan taqtulūna wa-taʾsirūna farīqan

“He brought down from their fortresses those among the People of the Book who had supported them, and cast terror into their hearts; you were killing one group and taking another captive.”

Al-Aḥzāb 33:26

The meaning of ṣayāṣīhim

Ṣayāṣīhim combines the noun ṣayāṣī and the pronoun hum.

Ṣayāṣī is a broken plural; the pronoun refers to the group introduced at the verse’s beginning.

The preceding min indicates the point of departure or the place from which they were brought down.

مِنْ صَيَاصِيهِمْ (min ṣayāṣīhim) therefore means “from their fortresses.”

Actual buildings and defensive refuges are meant here.

Dictionaries also use ṣayāṣī for horns and weaving implements, but neither sense fits this context.

People are brought out of their fortresses, rather than out of cattle horns or weaving implements.

Its connection with wa-anzala

The verse begins:

وَأَنْزَلَ الَّذِينَ ظَاهَرُوهُمْ

wa-anzala alladhīna ẓāharūhum

It means that He brought down those who had supported them.

أنزل (anzala) belongs to N Z L and concerns bringing down.

Alongside min ṣayāṣīhim, the verb portrays a group being brought out of its strong refuges.

The fortresses may stand high, but the expression alone does not give their precise elevation.

Being “brought down from fortresses” can also concern losing a defensive position and being brought out of it.

The construction does not establish that all the fortresses necessarily stood on mountains.

Study of N Z L

Who was brought out of the fortresses?

The verse says:

الَّذِينَ ظَاهَرُوهُمْ مِنْ أَهْلِ الْكِتَابِ

alladhīna ẓāharūhum min ahli al-kitābi

It means those among the People of the Book who had supported them.

This qualification matters.

The verse does not treat all the People of the Book as one hostile group. It concerns those who supported the opposing side in a particular confrontation.

Historical accounts connect the event with Banū Qurayẓah and the aftermath of the Battle of the Confederates.

The immediate Qur’anic context supports this connection. Al-Aḥzāb 33:25 describes the attackers being turned back; the next verse reports their supporters being brought down from their fortresses.

A particular event in a historical confrontation is thus at issue.

Ṣayāṣīhim’s meaning does not establish a general ruling about all the People of the Book, all fortress holders, or every community in conflict with another.

Al-Ṭabarī: fortresses, supporters of the Confederates, and Banū Qurayẓah

Fortresses and a sense of security

A fortress functions as a refuge and a means of resisting outside threats.

Here being brought out of fortresses accompanies terror being cast into hearts:

وَقَذَفَ فِي قُلُوبِهِمُ الرُّعْبَ

wa-qadhafa fī qulūbihimu al-ruʿba

It means that He cast terror into their hearts.

The events are connected in meaning: a group occupying defensive refuges loses that position and becomes afraid.

Ṣayāṣī itself nevertheless does not mean fear or psychological defeat.

Fortresses name the defensive places; terror is expressed by al-ruʿb.

Translation and analysis should preserve each word’s contribution.

The connection with the next verse

Al-Aḥzāb 33:27 states:

وَأَوْرَثَكُمْ أَرْضَهُمْ وَدِيَارَهُمْ وَأَمْوَالَهُمْ

wa-awrathakum arḍahum wa-diyārahum wa-amwālahum

“He caused you to inherit their land, dwellings, and possessions.”

This verse concerns the transfer of land, dwellings, and possessions.

Diyār and ṣayāṣī may identify buildings belonging to the same group in a historical setting without being lexically identical.

Diyār concerns dwellings and places of residence.

Ṣayāṣī in the preceding verse identifies fortresses and defensive works.

A fortress may also have been a residence, but this overlap does not erase the difference between the designations.

6. Other uses and caution about derivation

Ṣīṣ and poorly developed dates

Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Ṣiḥāḥ record الصِّيص (al-ṣīṣ) in the dialect of Banū al-Ḥārith ibn Kaʿb for inferior, poorly developed dates.

The term is also associated with شِيص (shīṣ): dates that failed to develop properly or yield desirable fruit because of problems with pollination.

Ṣīṣāʾ is also recorded for poorly filled colocynth seeds.

Their subject differs from fortresses, horns, and weaving implements.

Shared letters alone do not establish that poorly developed dates derive from protection or a defensive implement.

Arabic Ṣ Y Ṣ and SH Y Ṣ also differ in their first consonant.

A similar dialectal form does not justify identifying the entire histories of the two families.

The date-related use should therefore be recorded as a lexical branch without altering ṣayāṣī’s principal Qur’anic meaning.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab: ṣīṣ and ṣīṣāʾ for dates and seeds

Disagreement over ṣīṣiyah’s structure

Lexical sources give ṣīṣiyah and ṣīṣah for an animal’s spur, horns, weaving implements, and fortresses.

Their derivational structure is not unanimously agreed upon.

Discussing the weaving implement, Ibn Barrī says ṣīṣiyah belongs among weak roots because, in his analysis, its final radical is yāʾ rather than ṣād.

A word’s placement in a dictionary section therefore does not independently establish an undisputed historical analysis.

The Qur’anic morphological index records ṣayāṣīhim under Ṣ Y Ṣ, a classification that can be used to organize Qur’anic roots.

Historical study should nevertheless recognize other possible analyses and acknowledge real disagreement.

This disagreement does not make the verse’s meaning uncertain.

Despite the derivational disagreement, ṣayāṣīhim clearly means “their fortresses” in Al-Aḥzāb 33:26.

7. Comparison with related roots

Ṣ Y Ṣ (ṣayāṣī) and Ḥ Ṣ N (ḥiṣn)

Arabic ḥiṣn concerns a fortress, fortification, or place where people shelter.

Ṣayāṣī likewise denotes fortresses in its Qur’anic use.

The two words can thus be equivalent in a particular context.

The Ḥ Ṣ N family nevertheless has broader uses concerning immunity, protection, and a state involving inviolability or restriction.

For example, Qur’anic muḥṣanāt occurs in contexts concerning women and their legal situations.

Those uses should not be transferred to Ṣ Y Ṣ.

“Fortresses” suitably translates ṣayāṣī without making every derivative of Ḥ Ṣ N and Ṣ Y Ṣ identical in history and meaning.

Study of Ḥ Ṣ N

Ṣ Y Ṣ (ṣayāṣī) and M N ʿ (manaʿa)

Manʿ concerns withholding, preventing, and obstructing.

A fortress may prevent an enemy’s entry, while ṣayāṣī itself names the defensive place.

Manʿ expresses an act or effect of prevention; ṣayāṣī in the verse denotes buildings.

Nor is every obstacle a fortress. A law, distance, wall, or someone’s conduct may prevent an action without being called ṣīṣiyah.

Study of M N ʿ

Ṣ Y Ṣ (ṣayāṣī) and L W DH (malādh)

Malādh and expressions concerning taking refuge relate to seeking a safe place.

Qur’anic ṣayāṣī names places where people can shelter.

Taking refuge is an action, while a fortress is its place or means.

This difference shows that not every word concerning refuge is lexically identical with ṣayāṣī.

Ṣ Y Ṣ (ṣīṣiyah) and Q R N (qarn)

Arabic qarn is an established word for an animal’s horn.

Ṣīṣiyah is also used for bovine horns and those of certain other animals.

The words overlap in this use.

Qarn nevertheless has other uses in the Qur’an and Arabic, including human generations or communities.

Sharing the horn sense does not transfer all these uses to Ṣ Y Ṣ.

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:26, ṣayāṣī means fortresses rather than horns.

Ṣ Y Ṣ (ṣīṣiyah) and SH W K (shawkah)

Shawkah concerns a thorn or something pointed; other uses may denote strength and the ability to oppose.

When describing a cock, ṣīṣiyah concerns its thorn or spur.

The terms thus approach one another in describing that animal organ.

Shawkah names thorns or pointed objects in a broader field, while ṣīṣiyah has established uses for particular organs, weaving implements, and fortresses.

In Al-Anfāl 8:7, dhātu al-shawkati concerns confrontation with a militarily powerful group. This should not replace ṣayāṣīhim’s direct meaning in Al-Aḥzāb 33:26.

Ṣ Y Ṣ (ṣīṣ) and SH Y Ṣ (shīṣ)

Arabic shīṣ denotes dates that have not developed properly or remain poorly filled.

Ṣīṣ is also recorded with this sense in a dialect.

The two forms nevertheless have different initial consonants.

Similarity of sound alone should not merge every meaning of Ṣ Y Ṣ and SH Y Ṣ into one root.

Neither date-related use occurs in Al-Aḥzāb 33:26.

8. Translation suggestions for Forqan

صِيصِيَة (ṣīṣiyah): fortress, animal horn, cock’s spur, or pointed weaving implement, depending on usage.

صِيصَة (ṣīṣah): a means of defence and protection; a fortress in an architectural use.

صَيَاصِي (ṣayāṣī): fortresses; horns or pointed implements in other uses.

صَيَاصِيهِمْ (ṣayāṣīhim): their fortresses.

صِيص (ṣīṣ): poorly developed or inferior dates in a dialectal use.

صِيصَاء (ṣīṣāʾ): a poorly filled seed or fruit, in certain early uses.

For Al-Aḥzāb 33:26, the existing Forqan rendering is appropriate:

وَأَنْزَلَ الَّذِينَ ظَاهَرُوهُمْ مِنْ أَهْلِ الْكِتَابِ مِنْ صَيَاصِيهِمْ

wa-anzala alladhīna ẓāharūhum min ahli al-kitābi min ṣayāṣīhim

“He brought down from their fortresses those among the People of the Book who had supported them.”

“Their fortresses” is more precise here than “their houses,” preserving the buildings’ defensive function.

“Their refuges” is also defensible but may suggest simple or natural shelters, whereas a fortress is a strong defensive building.

“Their defensive implements” is unsuitable for the basic translation because it replaces particular buildings with the general concept of a means of defence.

Bovine horns, a cock’s spur, and weaving implements should likewise not enter this verse’s translation. Those uses illuminate the lexical field without being the verse’s referent.

9. Conclusion

Established Arabic uses of Ṣ Y Ṣ concern defensive implements, protection, and resistance.

Ṣīṣiyah names a bovine horn and a cock’s spur, organs that animals may use in self-defence.

The family also names a pointed weaving implement, sometimes made from an animal horn.

In architectural use, ṣīṣiyah denotes a fortress or refuge where people defend themselves behind fortifications.

These semantic connections are clear, but the historical order of all the uses is uncertain. Sources also record disagreement over ṣīṣiyah’s morphological analysis and a separate use for poorly developed dates.

The Qur’an uses only ṣayāṣīhim.

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:26 it denotes the fortresses of a group among the People of the Book who supported the Muslims’ opponents in events connected with the Confederates.

Being brought out of those fortresses accompanies terror being cast into their hearts. Ṣayāṣī thus names their actual places of defence and refuge.

The word’s lexical meaning should not be replaced with fear, defeat, warfare, or captivity: these are separate matters and events understood from the context.

Brief root meaning for Forqan:

A fortress or means of defence or protection; in other uses, an animal horn, a cock’s spur, or a pointed weaving implement.

Meaning in Al-Aḥzāb 33:26:

Ṣayāṣīhim: their fortresses, the strong defensive places where they had taken refuge and from which they were brought out.
