Smoke, its rising and dispersal; darkness and murkiness in linguistic usage

1. Introducing the root and its semantic field

The Arabic triliteral root D KH N principally concerns smoke: material rising from burning, dispersing through the air, and potentially darkening a space or limiting visibility.

Dukhān names smoke, while dakhanat al-nār describes a fire emitting it. This tangible meaning also underlies recorded uses concerning dark color, altered food smells and tastes, smoke-like dust, and troubled human relationships.

These uses are related but distinct. Smoke is perceptible; dark coloration can resemble its appearance; relational murkiness is figurative and concerns the loss of clarity and harmony.

The root occurs twice in the Qur’an, both times as the noun dukhān.

Fuṣṣilat 41:11 describes heaven in a state of being dukhān, within an account of creation and the ordering of heaven and earth.

Al-Dukhān 44:10 announces a day when heaven brings manifest smoke, while the following verse describes its enveloping people.

Both concern heaven but have different contexts: one describes a state during creation, the other an event expressed as a warning.

Sharing the noun does not necessarily make them the same phenomenon in time, origin, and precise nature.

Nor should the familiar meaning of smoke be restricted without evidence to a particular scientific theory, such as primordial cosmic gases, or a specific historical incident.

Root research should first establish Arabic meaning, then examine each Qur’anic construction and context independently.

2. Forms and derivatives

D KH N occurs twice, in two verses, exclusively as dukhān:

Fuṣṣilat 41:11: دُخَانٌ

Al-Dukhān 44:10: بِدُخَانٍ

These are the same lexical noun. Their difference concerns the preposition bi- and grammatical case, rather than independent derivation.

Family verbs such as dakhana, adkhana, and dakhkhana do not occur in the Qur’an.

Early and classical Arabic records these important forms:

دُخَان: smoke, rising from burning and dispersing in air.

دَخَنَتِ النَّارُ: the fire emitted smoke.

دُخُون: a verbal noun concerning smoke rising.

دَخَّنَ: to produce smoke or expose something to it.

تَدَخَّنَ: to expose oneself to smoke or incense.

دُخْنَة: aromatic material or incense used for fumigation; in color descriptions, a dusky blackish hue.

أَدْخَن: having a dark, smoke-like color.

دَخْنَاء: the feminine of the color adjective adkhan.

دَخِنُ الخُلُق: someone whose disposition is troubled, corrupted, or disagreeable.

دَوَاخِن and أَدْخِنَة: recorded plurals for smoke or related uses.

دُخْن: a small-grained cereal identified in lexical sources as jāwars, millet.

This last form requires distinction in root writing. Dukhn, the grain, is not dukhān, smoke. Dictionaries placing them under one entry does not prove that the grain’s name derives from smoke or that its color or shape historically produced the name.

Only dukhān occurs in the Qur’an. Other branches illuminate the linguistic field but should not enter verse translation without evidence.

3. The semantic core in early Arabic

Smoke rising from burning

Ibn Fāris relates the family to one principle in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

وَهُوَ الَّذِي يَكُونُ عَنِ الْوَقُودِ

wa-huwa alladhī yakūnu ʿani al-waqūdi

“That which results from burning.”

He explains other uses, including certain kinds of darkness and murkiness, through resemblance to smoke.

The familiar starting point is therefore smoke itself, rather than abstractions such as corruption, darkness, or obscurity.

Lisān al-ʿArab also states:

وَالدُّخَانُ الْعُثَانُ، دُخَانُ النَّارِ مَعْرُوفٌ

wa-al-dukhānu al-ʿuthānu dukhānu al-nāri maʿrūfun

“Dukhān is ʿuthān; fire’s smoke is familiar.”

ʿUthān belongs to another lexical family and concerns smoke and its dispersal.

Lexical sources continue:

دَخَنَتِ النَّارُ تَدْخُنُ ... ارْتَفَعَ دُخَانُهَا

dakhanati al-nāru tadkhunu … irtafaʿa dukhānuhā

“The fire smoked: its smoke rose.”

Ordinary Arabic dukhān thus primarily means smoke rising from fire.

The familiar meaning nevertheless differs from requiring every usual condition of its production.

Describing something as dukhān alone does not identify the material burned, its temperature, or surrounding conditions. Figurative use may also describe something as smoke-like because of its appearance or dispersal.

Dukhān in Fuṣṣilat 41:11 therefore does not conclusively establish earthly fire, burning wood, or combustion in its everyday form.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: smoke and semantic extensions

Rising and dispersing

Rising and spreading through air are perceptible features of smoke.

Dictionaries also record dakhana al-dukhān: smoke rose and became apparent.

The same construction can describe dust:

دَخَنَ الْغُبَارُ

dakhana al-ghubāru

That is, dust rose.

The verb can thus extend to dust through similarity of movement and dispersal.

Smoke and dust should not thereby become identical substances.

Smoke ordinarily concerns burning; dust may come from soil, sand, or dry material dispersed by wind or movement.

Shared features may include rising, dispersal, darkening space, or reducing visibility.

Comparing dust to smoke therefore does not necessarily make the dust a product of combustion.

Dark coloration

Arabic dukhnah can describe a dusky, blackish color.

Its lexical explanation is:

كُدْرَةٌ فِي سَوَادٍ

kudratun fī sawādin

“Murkiness in blackness.”

Adkhan also describes certain animals in this usage.

Kabshun adkhan and shātun dakhnāʾ, for example, concern animals with dark, smoke-like coloration.

Actual smoke rising from fire is not the subject; the animal resembles smoke through color.

This extension is understandable because smoke can appear gray, dark, or blackish.

Dukhān does not, however, name a color in every use. Darkness is one feature enabling related adjectives.

Wa-hiya dukhānun in Fuṣṣilat 41:11 should therefore not become merely “heaven was black.” The verse uses the noun dukhān, not the adjective adkhan.

Smoke altering smell and taste

Dakhina al-ṭaʿām describes food whose smell or taste has been affected by smoke.

The family is used, for example, when cooking or roasting smoke overwhelms food and gives it an unpleasant smell.

Sharābun dakhin also describes a drink whose smell has changed.

Smoke’s effect on smell and taste, rather than only its visible presence, matters in these expressions.

They do not restrict dukhān’s general meaning to spoiled or foul-smelling food.

Smoke has different effects in different conditions. In the food expression, the undesirable alteration is objectionable; smoke does not intrinsically mean corruption or impurity in every Arabic use.

Incense and perfuming

Dukhnah also names aromatic material burned to spread smoke or fragrance through a house or over clothing.

Here smoke serves perfuming rather than necessarily signifying pollution.

This prevents treating the entire root family as inherently negative.

Smoke may be irritating, useful for fragrance, or simply a perceptible natural phenomenon in different settings.

Each instance’s value and effect depend on its conditions.

4. Arabic witnesses and extensions of smoke imagery

Battle dust in a poem attributed to Al-Aʿshā

A poem attributed to Al-Aʿshā, who lived in the pre-Islamic period, says:

تُبَارِي الزِّجَاجَ مَغَاوِيرُهَا
شَمَاطِيطَ فِي رَهَجٍ كَالدَّخَنِ

tubārī al-zijāja maghāwīruhā / shamāṭīṭa fī rahajin ka-al-dakhani

“Its raiders advance in scattered groups through dust like smoke, keeping pace with the spear butts.”

Rahaj describes dust raised by movement and battle.

The poet calls it ka-al-dakhan: like smoke.

This witness matters for understanding the root.

First, familiar smoke supplies an image for another phenomenon.

Second, dust and smoke are compared rather than identified, through the comparative kāf.

Third, darkness, dispersal, and dust filling the battlefield probably motivate the comparison.

Smoke could thus provide an early Arabic comparison for suspended, dark masses.

The line’s composition date has not been independently established. Al-Aʿshā’s pre-Islamic life does not itself securely date this passage before Qur’anic revelation. Zijāj is the plural of zujj, the metal fitting at a spear’s lower end, rather than its upper spearhead.

Lisān al-ʿArab, Z J J: a spear’s lower metal fitting

Altered smell in a poem attributed to Labīd

A verse attributed to Labīd ibn Rabīʿah says:

وَفِتْيَانِ صِدْقٍ قَدْ غَدَوْتُ عَلَيْهِمُ
بِلَا دَخِنٍ وَلَا رَجِيعٍ مُجَنَّبِ

wa-fityāni ṣidqin qad ghadawtu ʿalayhimu / bi-lā dakhinin wa-lā rajīʿin mujannabi

“Many a company of true companions I visited in the morning, with no drink of altered smell, nor stale drink left aside.”

Lexical explanations connect dakhin here with an undesirable change of smell.

The family concerns not only visible smoke but its effect upon a drink’s quality.

The tangible smoke meaning can thus remain relevant to smell and taste.

Labīd lived both before and after Islam; this line should not automatically be called pre-Islamic without dating it.

Its interpretation also needs literary explanation. Dakhin alone does not reconstruct every detail of the gathering and its drinks.

Tahdhīb al-Lughah: Labīd’s witness and altered drink smell

Darkness in a sword description

A line attributed to Al-Muʿaṭṭal al-Hudhalī describes a sword:

عَضْبٌ حُسَامٌ لَا يُلِيقُ ضَرِيبَةً
فِي مَتْنِهِ دَخَنٌ وَأَثَرٌ أَحْلَسُ

ʿaḍbun ḥusāmun lā yulīqu ḍarībatan / fī matnihi dakhanun wa-atharun aḥlasu

“A sharp, cutting sword that leaves nothing it strikes without severing it; its blade bears a dusky hue and dark patterning.”

Dakhan concerns darkness visible on the sword’s surface.

Its connection with smoke concerns color and appearance, rather than rising from fire.

Some lexical explanations identify blackish darkness; others identify the sword’s surface pattern or appearance.

Dating and precise transmission need further study, so the line should remain a dictionary-attested witness. Under Ḥ L S, Tāj al-ʿArūs corrects its attribution to Abū Qilābah al-Ṭābikhī and its opening to ʿaḍbun ḥusāmun. That corrected opening is adopted here. Lā yulīqu ḍarībatan means leaving nothing struck without cutting it, rather than a blow not sticking in the blade.

Tāj al-ʿArūs, Ḥ L S: corrected attribution and sword wording

Murkiness in human relationships

Arabic dakhan also describes troubled or impure relations between people.

A line attributed to Qaʿnab says:

وَقَدْ عَلِمْتُ عَلَى أَنِّي أُعَاشِرُهُمْ
لَا نَفْتَأُ الدَّهْرَ إِلَّا بَيْنَنَا دَخَنُ

wa-qad ʿalimtu ʿalā annī uʿāshiruhum / lā naftaʾu al-dahra illā baynanā dakhanu

“Although I live among them, I know that some resentment always remains between us.”

Dakhan here does not mean physical smoke.

It concerns troubled relationships lacking complete harmony and trust.

The image resembles smoke darkening a space: resentment removes clarity and harmony from relationships.

That explanatory image should not become a conclusive historical derivation.

Hadith transmissions and dictionaries also describe a truce or reconciliation accompanied by dakhan: outward calm with lingering trouble or impurity.

Such witnesses illuminate linguistic history without determining the Qur’anic verses’ meaning.

Both Qur’anic occurrences concern dukhān rather than moral corruption or troubled relationships.

5. First Qur’anic use: Fuṣṣilat 41:11

Fuṣṣilat 41:11

ثُمَّ اسْتَوَىٰ إِلَى السَّمَاءِ وَهِيَ دُخَانٌ فَقَالَ لَهَا وَلِلْأَرْضِ ائْتِيَا طَوْعًا أَوْ كَرْهًا قَالَتَا أَتَيْنَا طَائِعِينَ

thumma istawā ilā al-samāʾi wa-hiya dukhānun fa-qāla lahā wa-li-al-arḍi iʾtiyā ṭawʿan aw karhan qālatā ataynā ṭāʾiʿīna

“Then He turned to heaven while it was smoke, and said to it and to the earth: Come willingly or unwillingly. They said: We come in obedience.”

This is the root’s first occurrence in the order of the muṣḥaf.

Dukhānun is an indefinite nominative noun, functioning as the predicate of wa-hiya dukhānun.

Hiya refers back to al-samāʾ.

The direct construction therefore presents heaven as dukhān in that state.

Unlike the second occurrence, this describes heaven within creation passages rather than smoke arising and enveloping people.

The verse within the creation account

Fuṣṣilat 41:9 describes creating the earth in two days.

Fuṣṣilat 41:10 concerns mountains, blessing, and measuring out provisions.

Fuṣṣilat 41:11 then introduces heaven as dukhān.

Fuṣṣilat 41:12 describes ordering seven heavens, assigning each heaven its affair, and adorning the nearest heaven with lamps.

This sequence places dukhān within a stage of heaven’s ordering.

The word alone does not specify that state’s duration, material composition, or correspondence with a scientific stage of cosmic history.

Those questions must remain distinct from lexical meaning.

Is this smoke produced by combustion?

Ordinary Arabic smoke concerns burning.

Fuṣṣilat 41:11, however, names no particular fire or burning material.

It therefore does not establish that heaven necessarily arose from burning wood, earthly materials, or a specific fuel.

Conversely, smoke should not disappear into translations such as vacuum, space, or light.

The Qur’an uses a word familiar to Arabic speakers through smoke and a smoke-like condition.

Al-Rāghib explains the usage in Al-Mufradāt as heaven resembling smoke, taking the image to indicate a lack of cohesion and the firmness of its eventual state.

This lexical and exegetical reading deserves consideration, but does not conclusively specify the stage’s physics.

Retaining smoke in the base translation is preferable; the state’s actual nature requires separate explanation.

Al-Rāghib’s Al-Mufradāt: the smoke-like reading in Fuṣṣilat

Dukhān and primordial cosmic matter

Modern scientific knowledge describes the early universe as passing through very different material states.

Cosmology discusses hot, dense matter, particles, plasma, and later atoms, gases, and larger structures.

NASA: material stages in cosmic history

A modern reader may perceive similarities between Qur’anic dukhān and some early cosmic material states.

Such similarity does not prove that Qur’anic Arabic dukhān specifically named plasma, hydrogen gas, a molecular cloud, or a particular cosmological epoch.

Scientific smoke concerns fine particles and gases associated with thermal decomposition and burning. It is not identical to every gas or plasma environment.

NIST: the scientific definition of smoke

Conversely, describing a heavenly state as smoke does not require its material to match earthly fire smoke in every property.

Three matters should therefore remain distinct: familiar Arabic smoke, the Qur’anic description of heaven at that stage, and scientific models of cosmic structure formation.

An independent study may compare them, but should not identify them automatically.

Wa-hiya dukhānun and fa-qaḍāhunna sabʿa samāwāt

Fuṣṣilat 41:11 uses the singular al-samāʾ.

Fuṣṣilat 41:12 then speaks of seven heavens.

This change of expression matters and deserves independent study of samāʾ and samāwāt.

D KH N alone does not determine the precise relationship between singular heaven and seven heavens.

Dukhān consequently does not make the seven heavens necessarily seven smoke layers, gas strata, or physical stages of one substance.

Their relationship requires the full network of heaven-creation verses.

Fuṣṣilat 41:11 also ends with divine command and heaven and earth’s response.

This forms part of creation’s obedience, but dukhān does not mean obedience, awareness, or will.

6. Second Qur’anic use: Al-Dukhān 44:10

Al-Dukhān 44:10

فَارْتَقِبْ يَوْمَ تَأْتِي السَّمَاءُ بِدُخَانٍ مُبِينٍ

fa-irtaqib yawma taʾtī al-samāʾu bi-dukhānin mubīnin

“So await a day when heaven brings manifest smoke.”

This is the root’s second and final Qur’anic occurrence.

Bi-dukhānin combines the preposition bi- with the noun dukhān.

Mubīnin qualifies it and conveys the smoke’s manifest character.

The construction differs clearly from Fuṣṣilat 41:11.

There, heaven is described as being smoke.

Here, heaven brings manifest smoke.

Neither construction should be transformed into the other without attention to verbs and context.

Dukhānin mubīn: manifest smoke

Mubīn belongs to B Y N and here adds the quality of being evident and apparent.

Dukhān names the smoke; mubīn is an independent description.

Its manifest character therefore comes from mubīn, rather than D KH N invariably meaning a phenomenon apparent to every human being.

This matters when examining different readings.

A reading identifying the smoke solely with a mental condition or hunger-induced perception needs to explain its relation to manifest and the subsequent verses.

Conversely, a phenomenon being evident does not itself determine its date or precise physics.

Yaghshā al-nās: enveloping people

Al-Dukhān 44:11

يَغْشَى النَّاسَ هَٰذَا عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ

yaghshā al-nāsa hādhā ʿadhābun alīmun

“Enveloping the people; this is a painful punishment.”

Yaghshā belongs to GH SH Y and conveys covering or enveloping.

Al-nās identifies those whom the phenomenon envelops.

The two verses thus describe smoke as brought by heaven, manifest, and enveloping people.

These features should not all become independent meanings of D KH N.

Heaven is the sentence’s subject, mubīn qualifies the smoke, and yaghshā expresses its enveloping action.

Verse 11 also names painful punishment, placing the smoke within warning and suffering.

Dukhān does not itself mean punishment in Arabic; that follows from the passage and the separate statement hādhā ʿadhābun alīm.

Was this smoke a historical event?

Early exegesis includes a reading relating Al-Dukhān 44:10 to severe famine and hunger among the Prophet’s opponents.

In the report attributed to Ibn Masʿūd, extreme hunger made people see something smoke-like when looking toward heaven. Some lexical explanations separately compare dust rising from parched land to smoke.

These sources attribute the first reading to Ibn Masʿūd and other early exegetes. Without evidence, the dust explanation should not become part of that particular report.

Arabic does compare dust with smoke, as the Al-Aʿshā witness illustrated.

Figurative smoke therefore cannot be excluded merely for being figurative.

Linguistic possibility nevertheless differs from establishing that a particular historical event was intended.

Al-Dukhān 44:10 itself names neither Quraysh, a famine’s date, the severity of hunger, nor a specific incident in Muḥammad’s life.

Manifest smoke and enveloping people portray an evident, encompassing phenomenon. Restricting this to hunger-induced visual disturbance requires further evidence.

The reading can thus be examined as a historical and exegetical possibility, but should not become the root’s lexical definition without independent evidence.

Al-Ṭabarī: Ibn Masʿūd’s report and readings of the smoke

Is the smoke a sign of future events?

Exegetical sources also present a reading identifying the smoke as a future event and a sign of major occurrences before resurrection.

On this view, encompassing smoke will appear at a future time.

The imperative fa-irtaqib and expression yawma taʾtī al-samāʾ suit warning of an awaited event.

Dukhān itself does not specify its exact date, duration, geographic extent, or bodily effects.

This verse mentions neither forty days, particular illnesses, chemical composition, nor differing effects on religious groups.

Some external reports provide such details, but they are neither direct root meaning nor explicit verse information.

Nor does the chapter’s name Al-Dukhān establish a particular future date for the event.

A chapter name does not replace examination of its verses.

Why subsequent verses matter

In Al-Dukhān 44:12, people ask The God to remove the punishment and declare themselves believers.

Al-Dukhān 44:13 and Al-Dukhān 44:14 concern remembrance after a messenger has come and their turning away from him.

Al-Dukhān 44:15 then announces temporary removal of the punishment and their return.

This continuity matters.

The passage concerns not only a heavenly phenomenon but also responses to warning, requests for relief, and returning to former conduct.

Historical and future-oriented interpretations should therefore consider Al-Dukhān 44:10, Al-Dukhān 44:11, Al-Dukhān 44:12, Al-Dukhān 44:13, Al-Dukhān 44:14, and Al-Dukhān 44:15 together.

An interpretation relying on one report or phrase without explaining temporary relief and the addressees’ return has not yet presented a complete argument.

That continuity alone nevertheless does not settle the smoke’s date or nature conclusively.

Could it be dust or something smoke-like?

Arabic compares smoke and dust through dispersal and darkness.

Figurative dukhān for a dark, enveloping mass is consequently linguistically possible.

That possibility does not remove the word’s ordinary meaning.

Smoke remains the most suitable base equivalent.

If independent research supplies sufficient evidence for a figurative or historical reading, explanation can present it; dukhān should not become famine, hunger, dust, or darkness without justification.

Nor should this smoke be conclusively identified with forest fires, volcanic eruption, an impact from space, nuclear war, or another scientific phenomenon.

These are conceivable natural occurrences, but the verse names none of them.

7. Distinguishing nearby words

D KH N and ʿ TH N: smoke and dispersal

Dictionaries identify ʿuthān as a close equivalent of dukhān.

Both can name smoke.

The ʿ TH N family also foregrounds spreading and dispersal in lexical definitions.

Ibn Fāris describes ʿ TH N through something spreading out, with smoke as one instance.

The families overlap concerning smoke without making every derivative equivalent.

The Qur’an uses dukhān, not ʿuthān.

D KH N and GH B R: smoke and dust

Both may appear as airborne masses of suspended particles.

Their usual origins differ.

Smoke principally concerns burning; dust can arise from dispersed soil and dry material.

Arabic poetry and prose sometimes compare battle dust to smoke.

That comparison demonstrates visual proximity rather than complete lexical identity.

Replacing smoke directly with dust in Al-Dukhān 44:10 requires sufficient contextual, linguistic, or historical evidence.

D KH N and S Ḥ B: smoke and clouds

Qur’anic saḥāb concerns clouds and associated phenomena.

A cloud can spread across heaven and resemble a mass of smoke to an observer.

Clouds and smoke are nevertheless distinct phenomena.

Ordinary atmospheric clouds consist of tiny water droplets or ice crystals; familiar smoke concerns particles and gases produced by burning.

Visual resemblance does not make saḥāb and dukhān identical in meaning.

Fuṣṣilat 41:11 uses dukhān, which should not be rendered merely as cloud.

Likewise, modern science calling a cosmic structure a gas cloud does not itself establish that Qur’anic dukhān precisely names that structure.

Study of S Ḥ B

D KH N and GH M M: smoke and cloud cover

Ghamām belongs to GH M M and names cloud masses in the Qur’an, including Al-Baqarah 2:57.

Smoke and ghamām may look similar because both can obscure heaven or human sight.

Ghamām nevertheless names cloud cover, while dukhān conveys smoke.

Covering and enveloping may be effects of either, without making them synonyms.

In Al-Dukhān 44:11, yaghshā specifies the smoke’s enveloping action; that meaning should not replace the noun dukhān itself.

Study of GH M M

D KH N and Q T R: smoke and darkened faces

Qatarah in ʿAbasa 80:41 concerns darkened, dust-covered faces.

Yūnus 10:26 also mentions qatar alongside abasement.

This family can concern darkness, dust, and an unpleasant condition.

The color branch of D KH N can likewise convey blackish darkness.

That resemblance does not make qatar actual smoke in every verse or make dukhān invariably denote darkened faces.

Human condition and appearance are central in the face passages; heaven and smoke are the subjects of dukhān’s two occurrences.

D KH N and N W R: smoke and light

Fire may involve both smoke and light, but their meanings differ.

Light concerns illumination; smoke is material or a mass arising from burning and sometimes reducing illumination.

Nār names fire, while dukhān names smoke.

A fire may produce abundant smoke or very little, depending on conditions.

Dukhān in Fuṣṣilat 41:11 therefore does not directly determine a type of fire, brightness, or heat at that stage.

Fire and light require independent evidence.

Study of N W R

D KH N and D KH L: distinct roots

These share their first two letters but differ in the third.

D KH N concerns smoke and related darkness or murkiness.

D KH L principally concerns entering something.

Expressions about internal corruption and troubled conditions may establish semantic connections between some words of both families.

A shared subject does not establish a shared root.

Dukhān should therefore not derive from dukhūl or entering; smoke penetrating a place does not itself establish its name’s historical origin.

8. Suggested translations for Forqan

Only dukhān occurs from this family in the Qur’an. Its primary, natural Persian equivalent is smoke.

That choice suits both occurrences despite their different contexts.

Fuṣṣilat 41:11

وَهِيَ دُخَانٌ

wa-hiya dukhānun

Suitable rendering:

“While it was smoke.”

This preserves dukhān without adding a claim about heaven’s physical composition.

Gas is unsuitable as a replacement: it is a broader modern scientific term, not necessarily the early Arabic meaning of smoke.

“While it was a mass of smoke” can explain the image, but mass is not independently stated in Arabic.

“While heaven was plasma” is a proposed scientific interpretation rather than a lexical translation.

Forqan’s current rendering of this portion is therefore appropriate.

Al-Dukhān 44:10

بِدُخَانٍ مُبِينٍ

bi-dukhānin mubīnin

Suitable rendering:

“With manifest smoke.”

The current Forqan rendering also suits the whole sentence:

“So await a day when heaven brings manifest smoke.”

This preserves smoke and independently conveys mubīn.

Manifest famine, enveloping darkness, heavy dust, and end-time smoke should not replace it as direct lexical translations.

Any supported proposal belongs in contextual and historical analysis.

Other family equivalents

Non-Qur’anic forms receive Persian equivalents according to usage:

Dakhanat al-nār: the fire emitted smoke.

Dakhkhana: produced smoke or exposed something to smoke.

Dukhnah: incense or material for fumigation; in color descriptions, a smoky darkness.

Adkhan: having a dark, blackish color.

Dakhin al-khuluq: having a troubled or unhealthy disposition.

Dukhn in its botanical sense: a small-grained cereal.

These meanings should not all transfer uniformly to Qur’anic dukhān.

9. Conclusion

D KH N principally concerns smoke, ordinarily rising from burning and dispersing through air in Arabic usage.

Early dictionaries also record dark coloration, altered smell and taste, smoke-like dust, and troubled human relationships within this tangible field.

These have figurative or experiential connections with smoke without identical meanings.

Qur’anic D KH N occurs twice, both times as dukhān.

Fuṣṣilat 41:11 describes heaven as smoke within its creation and ordering. The word does not determine the combustion source, material composition, or exact scientific cosmological correspondence.

Al-Dukhān 44:10 concerns manifest smoke brought by heaven, enveloping people in the next verse, within warning and punishment.

Historical and future-oriented readings differ over its nature and timing. The root alone does not decide between a past famine, future actual smoke, or another kind of occurrence.

Smoke remains the most faithful and natural equivalent in both verses.

Accompanying features should remain distinct from word meaning. Heavenly origin, manifest character, enveloping action, and association with punishment come from independent expressions and constructions, not D KH N alone.

Resemblance to modern scientific phenomena should not directly turn smoke in creation or warning passages into plasma, gas cloud, cosmic dust, volcanic smoke, or industrial pollution.

Proposed final definition: D KH N’s familiar core denotes smoke rising from burning. Its tangible qualities support uses concerning rising and dispersal, dark coloration, altered smell, and murkiness. Qur’anic dukhān concerns heaven in two different contexts and directly means smoke; the origin, precise nature, and timing of each described event require independent evidence from its context. Brief root meaning: Smoke and its rising; in other uses, darkness, the effects of smoke, and murkiness.
