Censure and faultfinding; dhimmah, covenant, obligation, and the right to protection

The Arabic root DH M M has two important semantic branches, both represented in the Qur’an. The first concerns censure, faultfinding, and judging a person or conduct unfavorably. ذَمَّ stands opposite مَدَحَ, “to praise,” while مَذْمُوم describes someone censured.

The second appears in ذِمَّة and ذِمَام and concerns covenants, obligations, rights, inviolability, and protection that a person or group must uphold. It describes a relationship requiring fidelity, whose resulting rights and security must not be violated.

Early lexical explanations connect the branches: violating an obligation or another person’s right to protection brings censure. This connection does not make dhimmah mean censure in the Qur’an, or madhmūm mean a covenant-holder. Each word has its established use.

The root occurs five times in the Qur’an as two words with different case endings: ذِمَّةً in Al-Tawbah 9:8 and Al-Tawbah 9:10, and مَذْمُوم in Al-Isrāʾ 17:18, Al-Isrāʾ 17:22, and Al-Qalam 68:49.

The five occurrences belong to two fields. Al-Tawbah concerns respecting covenants and protected rights between groups. In Al-Isrāʾ, censure appears as a consequence of conduct; in Al-Qalam, the same description belongs to an unrealized conditional outcome.

1. Core meaning and early Arabic usage

Dhamm: censure opposite praise

One lexical analysis places the core meaning of the doubled root DH M M opposite ḥamd, praise:

الذَّالُ وَالْمِيمُ فِي الْمُضَاعَفِ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ كُلُّهُ عَلَى خِلَافِ الْحَمْدِ

al-dhālu wa-al-mīmu fī al-muḍāʿafi aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu kulluhu ʿalā khilāfi al-ḥamd

Meaning: Dhāl and mīm in this doubled root express one principle, entirely opposed to praise.

Arabic uses the following expressions:

ذَمَّ فُلَانًا: he censured someone.

ذَمَّهُ عَلَى فِعْلِهِ: he reproached him for his conduct.

هُوَ مَذْمُومٌ: he is censured.

الْمَذَمَّة: censure, or what gives rise to censure.

Another definition states الذَّمُّ نَقِيضُ الْمَدْحِ: dhamm is the opposite of madḥ, praise.

The opposition is clear: praise expresses admirable qualities, while censure expresses unfavorable ones.

There is nevertheless a distinction between censuring someone and that person actually deserving censure.

Someone may correctly criticize another’s wrongful conduct. Someone may also censure another unjustly through hostility, ignorance, or self-interest.

ذَمَّ itself reports censure occurring; it does not alone guarantee that the person censuring has judged correctly.

The Qur’anic occurrences of madhmūm appear within divine judgment. Its authority is understood from the speaker and the verse’s construction, rather than from an assumption that every act of censure in Arabic is justified.

A poetic witness: praise and censure opposed

In verses attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm as a response to al-Nuʿmān ibn al-Mundhir’s threat, he says:

أَلَا أَبْلِغِ النُّعْمَانَ عَنِّي رِسَالَةً
فَمَدْحُكَ حَوْلِيٌّ وَذَمُّكَ قَارِحُ

alā ablighi al-nuʿmāna ʿannī risālatan / fa-madḥuka ḥawliyyun wa-dhammuka qāriḥ

Translation: Convey a message from me to al-Nuʿmān: my praise of you is like a yearling horse, but my censure of you is like a strong, mature horse.

The poet sets praise against censure, using two stages of an animal’s development and strength to describe their difference.

حَوْلِيّ concerns an animal one year old, while قَارِح, when applied to a horse, denotes a stage of maturity and development.

In the context of the threat and the poet’s response, the point is the poet praising or censuring al-Nuʿmān: praise is pictured as weak and censure as stronger. It should not be reversed without evidence into praise and censure delivered by al-Nuʿmān himself.

The verse is transmitted as ʿAmr ibn Kulthūm’s. Some transmissions vary slightly in the vocalization or writing of حَوْلِيٌّ. Literary attribution should be distinguished from independently establishing an exact date of composition.

This witness places dhamm opposite praise in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period. The poet presents censure as a social act that can affect someone’s reputation and standing.

Damage to reputation is not therefore the lexical meaning of dhamm. It may result from censure, while the word itself denotes faultfinding and reproach.

Dhamm and refraining from blameworthy conduct

تَذَمَّمَ also describes refraining from something out of fear of censure or a feeling of shame.

The following expression is recorded in lexical usage:

لَوْ لَمْ أَتْرُكِ الْكَذِبَ تَأَثُّمًا لَتَرَكْتُهُ تَذَمُّمًا

law lam atruki al-kadhiba taʾaththuman la-taraktuhu tadhammuman

Meaning: If I did not refrain from lying to avoid sin, I would at least refrain from it to avoid censure.

The use reveals an important distinction: a person may refrain because they believe an act wrong, or because they do not want to be reproached or discredited.

The outward result may be the same, while the motives differ.

The sentence is recorded in dictionaries, but its earliest use is not established. It should not be presented as securely dated pre-Islamic poetry or speech.

2. Dhimmah: covenant, inviolability, and protection

The Arabic meaning of dhimmah

In this branch, ذِمَّة concerns a covenant, obligation, guarantee, or right that a person or group must uphold.

One definition of this branch reads:

الذِّمَّةُ الْعَهْدُ وَالْكَفَالَةُ

al-dhimmatu al-ʿahdu wa-al-kafālah

Meaning: Dhimmah is covenant and guarantee.

ذِمَام likewise denotes a right, protected claim, or obligation that must be maintained.

Arabic uses the following expressions:

لَهُ ذِمَّةٌ: he has a covenant or right that must be respected.

أَعْطَاهُ الذِّمَّةَ: he granted him a covenant and assurance of protection.

حَفِظَ ذِمَامَهُ: he upheld the protected claim and obligation concerning him.

Dhimmah is therefore not confined to a verbal promise. Some uses concern a relationship of trust and responsibility requiring fidelity in practice.

For example, when someone enters another’s protection, dhimmah can concern the obligation to protect their life and security.

Not every dhimmah is a written contract or a formal political institution. Obligations, neighboring rights, refuge, and guarantees can also fall within the word’s field.

A poetic witness: dhimmah and protecting a person seeking refuge

Al-Aʿshā, a prominent poet of the pre-Islamic tradition, praises fidelity and protection in a poem beginning شُرَيْحُ لَا تَتْرُكَنِّي بَعْدَمَا عَلِقَتْ:

جَارُ ابْنِ حَيَّا لِمَنْ نَالَتْهُ ذِمَّتُهُ
أَوْفَى وَأَمْنَعُ مِنْ جَارِ ابْنِ عَمَّارِ

jāru ibni ḥayyā li-man nālat-hu dhimmatuhu / awfā wa-amnaʿu min jāri ibni ʿammār

Explanatory translation: Whoever comes within Ibn Ḥayyā’s dhimmah finds greater fidelity and stronger protection in his refuge than in Ibn ʿAmmār’s.

Dhimmah is directly connected here with protection and fidelity.

The poet describes someone who enters another’s refuge and gains a right to protection. The comparison concerns the protector’s fidelity and the strength of that protection, rather than an inherent virtue of the person seeking refuge.

The following verse mentions a strong fortress and a protector who does not betray. The subject is security and fidelity in danger, rather than a verbal promise alone.

The poem is recorded in early transmissions as al-Aʿshā’s.

The second hemistich has variants: some transmissions read أَوْفَى وَأَمْنَعُ, “more faithful and stronger in protection,” and others أَوْفَى وَأَكْرَمُ, “more faithful and more generous.” Both retain the connection with fidelity and protection, though one emphasizes protective strength and the other the protector’s generosity.

The witness places dhimmah in a relationship of covenant and protection within the pre-Islamic poetic tradition. The poet’s lifetime alone does not date this particular verse precisely. Nevertheless, the early evidence does not support treating this basic sense as solely a creation of later legal systems.

The connection between dhimmah and censure

One explanation connects this branch with censure by saying that a covenant is called ذِمَام because a person is censured for neglecting it.

This provides a clear semantic connection: someone who accepts an obligation must uphold it and becomes liable to reproach if they violate it.

A semantic explanation should nevertheless be distinguished from proving a history of derivation.

That violating dhimmah brings dhamm does not establish with certainty that dhimmah originally arose from the verb “to censure.”

The evidence clearly records both uses: censure, and an obligation whose neglect brings censure.

Dhimmah should not consequently be translated as “reproach” in the Qur’anic passages. Its established sense there concerns covenant and protected rights.

Dhimmah and the expression ahl al-dhimmah

In later legal and jurisprudential usage, ahl al-dhimmah became a designation for groups of non-Muslims under the covenant and protection of Islamic government, with specified rights and duties.

This historical use is connected with covenant and protection, but all later legal conditions and classifications should not be transferred into the Qur’anic meaning of ذِمَّة.

In Al-Tawbah 9:8 and Al-Tawbah 9:10, the word itself denotes covenant and protected rights. The passages concern groups’ conduct in particular circumstances; the word is not the name of a legal category elaborated in later centuries.

The lexical meaning, “a covenant or obligation requiring respect,” therefore takes precedence in this study over the historical technical designation ahl al-dhimmah.

3. “Nor a covenant”: disregarding obligations and protected rights

Al-Tawbah 9:8: power combined with covenant-breaking

Al-Tawbah 9:8 reads:

كَيْفَ وَإِنْ يَظْهَرُوا عَلَيْكُمْ لَا يَرْقُبُوا فِيكُمْ إِلًّا وَلَا ذِمَّةً يُرْضُونَكُمْ بِأَفْوَاهِهِمْ وَتَأْبَىٰ قُلُوبُهُمْ وَأَكْثَرُهُمْ فَاسِقُونَ

kayfa wa-in yaẓharū ʿalaykum lā yarqubū fīkum illan wa-lā dhimmatan yurḍūnakum bi-afwāhihim wa-taʾbā qulūbuhum wa-aktharuhum fāsiqūn

Translation: How, when, if they overcome you, they respect neither kinship nor covenant concerning you? They please you with their mouths, while their hearts refuse; most of them are defiantly disobedient.

ذِمَّةً denotes a covenant or obligation that must be respected.

لَا يَرْقُبُوا belongs to R Q B and here concerns observing and respecting something.

لَا يَرْقُبُوا فِيكُمْ ... ذِمَّةً therefore means that they do not uphold the relevant protected claim and obligation concerning you.

This does not necessarily mean that no covenant ever existed. It describes people who would not remain faithful to the obligation if they gained the upper hand.

Ill and dhimmah

إِلًّا and ذِمَّةً appear together in this verse.

Ill is an independent word with several recorded meanings, including kinship, covenant, and oath. Some explanations also connect it with a name for The God.

Dhimmah in this context concerns a covenant, pact, or protected obligation that must be respected.

“Kinship and covenant” is a defensible lexical reading of the two words and agrees with Forqan’s current Persian translation.

The lexical disagreement over ill means that not every early explanation limits it to kinship.

The clear point is that the verse concerns disregarding bonds and obligations that should restrain aggression.

The surrounding discussion of covenants

The opening verses of Al-Tawbah provide context for dhimmah here.

Al-Tawbah 9:4 commands that covenants with associators who have not violated them be fulfilled until their appointed end.

Al-Tawbah 9:7 likewise requires fidelity while the other party remains upright in the covenant.

The chapter therefore distinguishes groups faithful to their covenants from groups who would violate obligations if they gained power.

This distinction is fundamental.

The censure in Al-Tawbah 9:8 cannot be turned into a general judgment that every person or group with a different belief is inherently unfaithful to covenants.

The chapter itself recognizes fidelity among groups of associators and requires their covenants to be respected.

The qualification وَإِنْ يَظْهَرُوا عَلَيْكُمْ in Al-Tawbah 9:8 matters: the passage concerns the group’s conduct if it gains the upper hand.

The verse continues by distinguishing their speech from their inward disposition: their mouths offer pleasing words, while their hearts do not agree.

The subject is therefore not simply differing belief. The context emphasizes unfaithfulness and the gap between outward speech and actual intention.

Al-Tawbah 9:10: disregarding dhimmah and transgression

Al-Tawbah 9:10 reads:

لَا يَرْقُبُونَ فِي مُؤْمِنٍ إِلًّا وَلَا ذِمَّةً وَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْمُعْتَدُونَ

lā yarqubūna fī muʾminin illan wa-lā dhimmatan wa-ulāʾika humu al-muʿtadūn

Translation: They respect neither kinship nor covenant concerning any believer; those are the transgressors.

The verse repeats إِلًّا وَلَا ذِمَّةً.

Unlike Al-Tawbah 9:8, which states the condition of gaining the upper hand, this verse describes the group’s behavior directly.

It ends with وَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْمُعْتَدُونَ, “those are the transgressors.”

مُعْتَدُونَ belongs to ʿ D W and concerns exceeding limits and transgressing.

Disregarding a covenant and protected rights is thus connected with transgression in this context.

Dhimmah itself does not mean aggression or transgression. It denotes a covenant or obligation; violating or disregarding it is the censured conduct.

Al-Tawbah 9:12 and Al-Tawbah 9:13 continue the discussion with broken oaths, hostile action, and the initiation of enmity.

The opening passages therefore concern actual relationships between groups, their covenants, fidelity, and covenant-breaking.

Dhimmah is an important ethical and legal concept here: a right that must be respected and does not lose validity merely because the other party gains power.

4. Madhmūm: censure as a consequence and in a conditional construction

Al-Isrāʾ 17:18: censured and driven away

Al-Isrāʾ 17:18 reads:

مَنْ كَانَ يُرِيدُ الْعَاجِلَةَ عَجَّلْنَا لَهُ فِيهَا مَا نَشَاءُ لِمَنْ نُرِيدُ ثُمَّ جَعَلْنَا لَهُ جَهَنَّمَ يَصْلَاهَا مَذْمُومًا مَدْحُورًا

man kāna yurīdu al-ʿājilata ʿajjalnā lahu fīhā mā nashāʾu li-man nurīdu thumma jaʿalnā lahu jahannama yaṣlāhā madhmūman madḥūrā

Translation: Whoever desires the immediate life, We hasten in it whatever We will for whomever We intend; then We assign Hell to him, which he enters to burn, censured and driven away.

مَذْمُومًا is a passive participle from DH M M, describing someone who is censured.

It appears alongside مَدْحُورًا in this verse.

مَدْحُورًا belongs to D Ḥ R and means driven away or expelled. The descriptions are related, but do not express exactly the same thing.

Study of D Ḥ R

Madhmūm concerns the person’s censure, while madḥūr concerns being driven away.

Censure appears here within the context of human choice and its outcome.

Desiring the immediate life

The verse begins مَنْ كَانَ يُرِيدُ الْعَاجِلَةَ.

الْعَاجِلَةَ concerns the immediate life or its passing benefits and is set against concern for the Hereafter.

The following verse, Al-Isrāʾ 17:19, describes someone who desires the Hereafter, makes the appropriate effort for it, and believes.

The contrast is therefore not only between this life and the Hereafter as two periods. The direction of human desire and effort also matters.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:18 should not be taken to mean that everyone who enjoys worldly blessings or desires comfort, provision, and security is necessarily madhmūm.

The verse concerns a direction of desire and choice contrasted with striving for the Hereafter.

The verse itself also states that worldly benefit depends on The God’s will: مَا نَشَاءُ لِمَنْ نُرِيدُ, “whatever We will for whomever We intend.”

Worldly benefit alone therefore does not establish The God’s final approval, nor does lacking something in this world establish a person’s blameworthiness.

Madhmūm describes the outcome under discussion, rather than defining every form of worldly life.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:22: censured and forsaken

Al-Isrāʾ 17:22 reads:

لَا تَجْعَلْ مَعَ اللَّهِ إِلَٰهًا آخَرَ فَتَقْعُدَ مَذْمُومًا مَخْذُولًا

lā tajʿal maʿa allāhi ilāhan ākhara fa-taqʿuda madhmūman makhdhūlā

Translation: Do not set another deity alongside The God, lest you remain censured and without a helper.

مَذْمُومًا appears again, but the second description differs from Al-Isrāʾ 17:18.

Here مَخْذُولًا belongs to KH DH L and concerns being forsaken and left without support.

Study of KH DH L

Madhmūm accompanies madḥūr in Al-Isrāʾ 17:18 and makhdhūl in Al-Isrāʾ 17:22.

Translation should preserve the difference.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:22 explicitly prohibits setting another deity alongside The God. It states two consequences: censure and being left without support.

فَتَقْعُدَ in this construction concerns remaining or being left in that condition; it should not be confined to physically sitting on the ground.

Madhmūm itself does not mean associating others with The God. That behavior is understood from لَا تَجْعَلْ مَعَ اللَّهِ إِلَٰهًا آخَرَ, while censure is a consequence stated for it.

Every error, intellectual disagreement, or wrongful act should not therefore be identified without evidence with this particular act of setting up a divine counterpart.

Al-Qalam 68:49: censure in a conditional construction

Al-Qalam 68:49 reads:

لَوْلَا أَنْ تَدَارَكَهُ نِعْمَةٌ مِنْ رَبِّهِ لَنُبِذَ بِالْعَرَاءِ وَهُوَ مَذْمُومٌ

lawlā an tadārakahu niʿmatun min rabbihi la-nubidha bi-al-ʿarāʾi wa-huwa madhmūm

Translation: Had a blessing from his Lord not reached him, he would have been cast into the open, censured.

مَذْمُومٌ is nominative here, within the circumstantial clause وَهُوَ مَذْمُومٌ.

The verse follows the mention of صَاحِبِ الْحُوتِ, “the companion of the fish,” meaning Jonah, in Al-Qalam 68:48.

The central point is the conditional construction لَوْلَا.

The verse does not say that Jonah was ultimately abandoned in a censured state. It says that, had his Lord’s blessing not reached him, he would have been cast out with that description. This conditional outcome did not occur because the blessing reached him.

The distinction matters because Al-Ṣāffāt 37:145 describes Jonah actually being cast into the open while ill.

In Al-Qalam 68:49, being censured belongs to the conditional absence of that blessing. The other account describes the actual event with illness.

The phrases should not be merged without attending to their different constructions.

Madhmūm does not here mean ill, exhausted, or without shelter. Those conditions have their own expressions.

Al-Qalam 68:50 adds that Jonah’s Lord chose him and placed him among the righteous.

Together, the verses present the possibility of censure and the role of divine mercy and favor in the person’s outcome.

This should not become a rule that a person censured in one situation must remain censured forever. The Qur’an itself speaks of favor, election, and righteousness in Jonah’s case.

5. Distinctions from related roots

DH M M and DH ʾ M: censure and disparagement

Study of DH ʾ M

DH M M and DH ʾ M are very close in meaning.

ذَأَمَ is likewise explained through faultfinding and censure; some lexical uses treat the roots as synonymous.

Their morphological structures nevertheless differ.

مَذْمُوم comes from DH M M, while مَذْءُوم comes from DH ʾ M.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:18 uses مَذْءُومًا مَدْحُورًا concerning Iblīs.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:18 instead uses مَذْمُومًا مَدْحُورًا for the person’s outcome under discussion.

The expressions are semantically close, but their different roots must be preserved.

It cannot be asserted without sufficient evidence that madhʾūm is always stronger than madhmūm. Some lexical explanations propose differences of intensity or nuance, but these require examination against actual usage.

DH M M and L W M: censure and reproach

Lawm concerns reproaching someone and calling their conduct to account.

Dhamm likewise concerns censure and unfavorable judgment.

The words may refer to the same situation in many uses. Lawm sometimes emphasizes addressing and reproaching the person, while dhamm can also concern an unfavorable assessment of conduct or character.

This distinction is not absolute. Dhamm can be spoken directly to someone, and lawm can concern a negative assessment of conduct.

Madhmūm can therefore be rendered “censured,” and in some contexts “reproached,” without treating every use of the two families as interchangeable.

DH M M and ʿ H D: dhimmah and covenant

Study of ʿ H D

ʿAhd is used in the Qur’an for covenants, injunctions, obligations, and relationships requiring observance.

Dhimmah likewise concerns covenants and binding protected claims in Al-Tawbah.

The concepts are close in the field of covenant and responsibility, while dhimmah in Arabic can also emphasize protection, guarantee, and security.

Al-Tawbah 9:4 and Al-Tawbah 9:7 concern fidelity to covenants; Al-Tawbah 9:8 and Al-Tawbah 9:10 concern failing to respect dhimmah.

The connection shows that breaching obligations and violating the rights arising from them are shared subjects of these verses.

The words’ difference does not establish that every ʿahd lacks a guarantee or every dhimmah is a formal political protection contract. Such a classification cannot be derived from these limited Qur’anic occurrences.

DH M M and Ḥ R M: dhimmah and inviolability

Study of Ḥ R M

Ḥurmah concerns something requiring respect, against which violation is not permitted.

Dhimmah can likewise involve a person’s or group’s rights and inviolability, especially when covenant or protection creates a specific responsibility.

Not everything entitled to respect is necessarily the subject of a particular contract or dhimmah.

Conversely, dhimmah may establish a specific relationship of obligation and protection that the responsible party must respect.

The families overlap in human rights and relationships, without being completely synonymous.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

The two Qur’anic words require different equivalents.

In Al-Tawbah 9:8 and Al-Tawbah 9:10, ذِمَّةً is best rendered “covenant” or “pact.”

“Right to protection” or “obligation requiring respect” can also explain the lexical and contextual meaning.

Forqan’s current Persian rendering uses “covenant” in both verses and agrees with the Qur’anic usage.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:18 and Al-Isrāʾ 17:22, مَذْمُومًا means “censured.”

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:18, مَذْمُومًا مَدْحُورًا can be rendered “censured and driven away.”

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:22, مَذْمُومًا مَخْذُولًا means “censured and without a helper.”

In Al-Qalam 68:49, وَهُوَ مَذْمُومٌ means “while censured,” with the qualification that it belongs to a lawlā conditional construction. The hypothetical state should not be converted without reason into an actual event.

Madhmūm alone does not mean an inhabitant of Hell, an associator, someone ill, or someone expelled. Where present, those qualities are understood from other words and clauses.

Dhimmah in Al-Tawbah should likewise not be translated directly as ahl al-dhimmah or a particular legal class. The word denotes an obligation and protected claim, rather than naming a group.

7. Conclusion

DH M M has two important Arabic branches, both represented in the Qur’an.

The first is censure. A person or behavior is criticized, and madhmūm describes someone censured. It occurs in Al-Isrāʾ 17:18, Al-Isrāʾ 17:22, and Al-Qalam 68:49.

The second is dhimmah and obligation. It concerns a covenant, right, inviolability, or guarantee of protection that a person or group must uphold. It occurs in Al-Tawbah 9:8 and Al-Tawbah 9:10.

Early Arabic witnesses place censure opposite praise and use dhimmah for covenants and protection of those seeking refuge.

Lexical explanations connect the branches: breaking a covenant and neglecting another’s right bring censure. This semantic connection is defensible without making it the proven historical origin of every word in the family.

In the Qur’an, censure concerns people’s conduct and outcomes, while dhimmah in human and social relationships concerns respecting covenants and obligations.

Translation should preserve the distinction.

Final definition: In its first branch, DH M M denotes censure and faultfinding concerning a person or conduct, and madhmūm describes someone censured. In its second branch, dhimmah denotes a covenant, obligation, right, or inviolability requiring respect, particularly in relationships of protection and guarantee.

Upholding a covenant and deserving censure are connected within this family, but dhimmah itself does not mean reproach, nor does madhmūm mean a covenant-holder.

Understanding the Qur’anic uses requires distinguishing the word’s own meaning, people’s conduct, and the ethical or legal consequence.
