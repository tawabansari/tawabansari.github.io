The Qur’anic Root A Y M (أ ي م)

Being without a spouse: a condition shared by women and men, without necessarily implying previous marriage, poverty, or widowhood

Qur’anic and lexical forms

أَيِّم
(ayyim)
Lexical singular: a woman or man without a spouse; this form is not Qur’anic

الْأَيَامَىٰ
(al-ayāmā)
Sole Qur’anic form; plural; Al-Nūr 24:32

آمَ / يَئِيمُ
(āma / yaʾīmu)
Lexical verb: becoming without a spouse; not Qur’anic

تَأَيَّمَ / تَأَيُّم
(taʾayyama / taʾayyum)
Verb and verbal noun: remaining or becoming without a spouse; not Qur’anic

أَيْمَة / أُيُوم
(aymah / uyūm)
Lexical nouns or verbal nouns concerning being without a spouse; not Qur’anic

مَأْيَمَة
(maʾyamah)
A cause of becoming without a spouse, such as war; not Qur’anic

أَيْم / أَيِّم
(aym / ayyim)
A name for a kind of snake; a separate branch; not Qur’anic

إِيَام
(iyām)
Smoke; disputed root affiliation; not Qur’anic

In Arabic usage concerning marriage, the root A Y M denotes the condition of someone who has no spouse. Ayyim (أَيِّم) can describe a woman or a man who has never married or who, for any reason, no longer has a spouse after a marriage. Its familiar plural is ayāmā (أَيَامَى).

In the Qur’an, this family occurs only in Al-Nūr, where the community is commanded to facilitate marriage for people without spouses. Contrary to a reading restricted to widows, the Arabic word al-ayāmā (الأَيَامَىٰ) can include both women and men without spouses.

The description states a person’s marital situation, rather than moral worth, age, financial means, or the cause of being without a spouse. A widowed woman, a divorced woman, a man who has lost his spouse, and someone who has never married can all fall within its semantic range.

1. Linguistic forms and earlier Arabic usage

The Qur’an contains only the form al-ayāmā (الْأَيَامَىٰ). Its singular is ayyim (أَيِّم), used in Arabic for both sexes.

Lexicons also record aymah (أَيْمَة), uyūm (أُيُوم), taʾayyum (تَأَيُّم), taʾayyama (تَأَيَّمَ), āma (آمَ), and yaʾīmu (يَئِيمُ) concerning the state of having no spouse. These forms do not themselves occur in the Qur’an, but help explain the word’s linguistic family.

Ayyim: a woman or man without a spouse

Arabic uses rajulun ayyimun (رَجُلٌ أَيِّمٌ) for a man who has no wife. Similarly, imraʾatun ayyimun (امْرَأَةٌ أَيِّمٌ) describes a woman who has no husband.

The use of the same form for women and men matters. Ayyim is not inherently exclusive to women, even though some conversational or legal contexts use it predominantly of women.

In Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Jawharī includes men and women among those without spouses and explicitly allows either a previous marriage or none. Ibn Sīdah likewise includes both a woman who has never married and one previously married within its broad meaning.

This usage separates two questions: does the person currently have a spouse, and has the person ever been married?

In its broad sense, ayyim concerns the first question. The word itself does not answer the second.

The verbs āma and taʾayyama

In Arabic, āmat al-marʾatu (آمَتِ الْمَرْأَةُ) can express a woman’s becoming without a spouse. In some uses, it concerns a woman whose husband has died and who has not subsequently remarried.

Taʾayyama (تَأَيَّمَ) can also describe a woman or man who remains without a spouse for a period.

The verb’s meaning must be distinguished from the cause of the condition. A spouse’s death can cause someone to be without a spouse, but it is not the only cause. Separation, the end of a marriage, or never having married can leave a person in the same condition.

Some sources use al-aymah (الأَيْمَة) for a period without a spouse and maʾyamah (مَأْيَمَة) for something that causes others to become without spouses. For example, war in which men are killed and their wives become widows can be described in this way.

These uses place the root within family relationships and social circumstances. Nevertheless, losing a spouse through war or death must not become the definition of every derivative.

The plural ayāmā and its disputed morphological explanation

Ayāmā (أَيَامَى) is the plural of ayyim. Some sources also record ayāʾim (أَيَائِم).

Different morphological explanations exist for ayāmā. Some treat it as involving a rearrangement within the plural’s structure; others regard it as an established plural whose components should not all be mechanically derived from one simple pattern.

This disagreement does not change the Qur’anic meaning. Ayāmā is plural and refers to a group of people without spouses.

The plural’s resemblance to yatāmā (يَتَامَى) must not merge the two families. Ayāmā comes from A Y M, whereas yatāmā comes from Y T M. The first concerns marital status; the second concerns being an orphan.

2. Poetry and social usage

A verse transmitted in earlier lexicons reads:

فَإِنْ تَنْكِحِي أَنْكِحْ وَإِنْ تَتَأَيَّمِي
يَدَا الدَّهْرِ مَا لَمْ تَنْكِحِي أَتَأَيَّمُ

fa-in tankiḥī ankiḥ wa-in tataʾayyamī / yadā al-dahri mā lam tankiḥī ataʾayyamu

Its sense is: if you marry, I shall marry too; and if you remain without a spouse, I too shall remain without a spouse, however long time passes, until you marry.

Here tataʾayyamī (تَتَأَيَّمِي) addresses a feminine singular subject, while ataʾayyamu (أَتَأَيَّمُ) is first person. The former explicitly concerns a woman; the latter does not itself determine the speaker’s sex. The line illustrates this verbal family for remaining without a spouse; explicit lexical definitions independently establish its application to both women and men.

The line is transmitted with variant readings, and neither its attribution nor its exact composition date is certain. It attests recognized usage in the Arabic linguistic tradition, rather than definitively dating the word’s emergence to the pre-Islamic period.

In another line preserved in earlier sources, the poet addresses Fāṭimah:

أَفَاطِمُ إِنِّي هَالِكٌ فَتَأَيَّمِي
وَلَا تَجْزَعِي كُلُّ النِّسَاءِ تَئِيمُ

a-fāṭimu innī hālikun fa-taʾayyamī / wa-lā tajzaʿī kullu al-nisāʾi taʾīmu

“O Fāṭimah, I am passing away; remain without a spouse, and do not grieve beyond measure: all women become without spouses.” Here fa-taʾayyamī (فَتَأَيَّمِي) is an imperative, rather than a prediction. The second hemistich generalizes in consolation about the prospect of separation; it is not an exceptionless statistical statement about every woman.

Here the woman becomes without a spouse following her husband’s death. This is an example of the word used in a situation of widowhood.

One instance of widowhood does not establish that ayyim always means only “widow.” The preceding example described the addressee and speaker remaining without spouses under different circumstances.

A poem by Jamīl ibn Maʿmar, an Umayyad poet, also uses al-ayāmā and ayyim in connection with Buthaynah’s being without a spouse. This witness postdates the beginning of Islam: it helps establish continued usage, rather than independently proving pre-Islamic antiquity.

Together, these examples show that being without a spouse was a recognized condition in Arabic, and that different forms of this family could describe both women and men.

3. Al-ayāmā in the Qur’an: a command to facilitate marriage

The root’s sole Qur’anic occurrence is in Al-Nūr:

وَأَنكِحُوا الْأَيَامَىٰ مِنكُمْ وَالصَّالِحِينَ مِنْ عِبَادِكُمْ وَإِمَائِكُمْ إِن يَكُونُوا فُقَرَاءَ يُغْنِهِمُ اللَّهُ مِن فَضْلِهِ وَاللَّهُ وَاسِعٌ عَلِيمٌ

wa-ankiḥū al-ayāmā minkum wa-l-ṣāliḥīna min ʿibādikum wa-imāʾikum in yakūnū fuqarāʾa yughnihimu llāhu min faḍlihi wa-llāhu wāsiʿun ʿalīmun

“Arrange marriage for those among you who have no spouse, and the worthy among your enslaved men and women. If they are poor, The God will enrich them from His bounty; and The God is All-Encompassing, Knowing.” (Al-Nūr 24:32)

Here al-ayāmā is the object of ankiḥū (أَنكِحُوا). The verb is a plural imperative addressed to people who can help make marriage possible for others.

Ankiḥū is form IV of N K Ḥ. In this construction, it means marrying others off or facilitating their marriage. Grammatically, it therefore differs from “get married” addressed directly to the people without spouses themselves.

The verse concerns more than individuals’ circumstances: it also gives other addressees responsibility for helping make marriage possible.

Al-ayāmā minkum: whom does it include?

In its broad lexical sense, al-ayāmā includes women and men without spouses.

The word alone cannot mean that the addressees should arrange marriages only for widows, or that unmarried men fall outside its scope.

Nor does ayyim necessarily imply previous marriage. Someone who has never married can also fall under this description.

In the verse, minkum (مِنْكُمْ), “among you,” relates the scope to the community addressed. Another group is then mentioned: wa-l-ṣāliḥīna min ʿibādikum wa-imāʾikum (وَالصَّالِحِينَ مِنْ عِبَادِكُمْ وَإِمَائِكُمْ).

This distinction has led some exegetical explanations to understand al-ayāmā minkum here as free women and men without spouses, with the following phrase concerning enslaved people in that society.

Such a distinction can be examined through the sentence’s construction and its social setting. Lexically, however, ayyim does not itself mean “a free person.” Being free or enslaved is a social and legal status; being without a spouse is a marital condition.

The word’s semantic range must therefore be distinguished from its referents within this sentence.

Distinguishing al-ayāmā from imāʾikum

Two visually similar forms occur in this verse: al-ayāmā (الْأَيَامَىٰ) and imāʾikum (إِمَائِكُمْ).

They do not share a root.

Al-ayāmā is the plural of ayyim and concerns being without a spouse. Imāʾikum is the plural of amah (أَمَة) with the second-person plural possessive suffix; here it denotes enslaved women. Its root in the Qur’anic morphological index is A M W (أ م و).

Study of A M W

Imāʾ must therefore not be treated as another form of ayāmā.

This distinction matters especially when both words occur in the same verse. Similar spelling cannot replace morphological analysis.

Al-ṣāliḥīn: a separate description

In the verse’s second part, al-ṣāliḥīn (الصَّالِحِينَ) precedes min ʿibādikum wa-imāʾikum.

This description comes from Ṣ L Ḥ and concerns righteousness or fitness. In the sentence, it relates to the group of enslaved men and women whose marriage is to be facilitated.

Ayyim itself, however, does not mean righteous or suitable. A person may have no spouse while differing in ability, readiness, or living conditions relevant to marriage.

Conversely, the absence of al-ṣāliḥīn immediately after al-ayāmā does not establish that no other conditions or considerations apply to the first group. Conditions of marriage require examination of the relevant verses concerning marriage, consent, maturity, justice, and responsibility together.

For example, Al-Nisāʾ 4:6 speaks of reaching marriageable age and recognizing sound judgment in the administration of orphans’ property. These concepts must be examined in their own setting; age, puberty, sound judgment, and fitness must not be treated as interchangeable without evidence.

A Y M indicates only that someone has no spouse, rather than establishing that every condition for marriage has been met.

4. Being without a spouse, personal choice, and economic circumstances

Does the command ankiḥū authorize forced marriage?

In Al-Nūr 24:32, a plural audience is commanded to act to facilitate marriage for people without spouses.

Ankiḥū does not itself mean imposing marriage against a person’s wishes. Arranging or facilitating a marriage and forcing someone to accept a spouse are different concepts.

In Al-Baqarah 2:232, when discussing women whose previous marriages have ended, the Qur’an forbids preventing their marriage when there is mutual agreement in accordance with what is right.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:19 also prohibits inheriting women against their will and putting them under pressure to recover part of what was given to them; the verse states an exception concerning the latter prohibition.

These verses matter for understanding choice and consent in marital relations. A social command to facilitate marriage should therefore not be turned, without evidence, into permission to control a woman’s or man’s personal decision.

At the same time, the lexical meaning of ayyim cannot yield every legal detail of consent and the marriage contract. Those questions belong to a separate study of marriage and its relevant rulings.

If they are poor: poverty is not inherent in being without a spouse

The verse’s final part states that if the people in question are poor, The God will enrich them from His bounty.

This sentence shows that poverty can affect decisions about marriage or its feasibility; poverty is not part of the meaning of ayyim.

A woman or man without a spouse may have financial means. Someone else may have a spouse and be poor. These conditions are independent.

The verse addresses poverty as an obstacle that should not, by itself, deprive suitable people of the opportunity to marry.

Nevertheless, the statement about divine bounty should not be turned, without examining the related verses, into a guarantee that every person immediately becomes wealthy after marriage. Ayyim itself specifies neither the timing nor the form or means of financial relief.

Al-Nūr 24:33, immediately after this command, instructs those unable to find the means of marriage to remain chaste until The God enriches them from His bounty.

This adjacent verse shows that the Qur’an also recognizes the reality that marriage may be inaccessible to some people.

Being without a spouse is therefore neither necessarily a sign of poverty nor automatically a sin or moral defect. Ayyim primarily names a condition; conduct within that condition and others’ responsibilities are separate matters.

5. Other branches recorded in the lexicons

In earlier dictionaries, the family A Y M is not confined to marriage.

Ibn Fāris records three distinct branches within this group: being without a spouse, a kind of snake, and smoke.

Al-aym: a name for a kind of snake

Arabic also uses al-aym (الأَيْم), and sometimes al-ayyim (الأَيِّم), for a kind of snake. Some lexicographers describe a slender or white snake, while others give the term a wider range.

Arabic poetry also attests aym meaning a snake.

Its linguistic form resembles ayyim meaning someone without a spouse, but its meaning is entirely different.

No reliable evidence establishes that people without spouses were named after a resemblance to snakes, or that the snake’s name arose from human marital status.

A story or imagined image should not be invented merely to give these branches an appearance of unity.

Al-iyām: smoke

Lexicons also record al-iyām (الإِيَام) meaning smoke. A familiar use concerns smoking a beehive to drive out the bees and collect honey.

Ibn Fāris includes this use in the same group. Some lexicographers, however, connect the verb for smoking to A W M and regard the yāʾ of iyām as a transformation of wāw.

This disagreement shows that placing forms under one dictionary entry does not by itself establish a shared historical root.

For Qur’anic study, the relevant point is clear: al-ayāmā in Al-Nūr 24:32 concerns people without spouses. Snake and smoke meanings have no direct role in translating this verse and must not be imposed on its discussion of marriage.

6. Distinctions from related roots and words

A Y M (ayyim) compared with ʿ Z B (ʿazaba)

Study of ʿ Z B

In Arabic, ʿazab (عَزَب) and ʿuzbah (عُزْبَة) belong to a family concerning distance as well as the unmarried state. In some everyday usage, ʿāzib describes someone who has never married.

In its broad sense, ayyim is not confined to someone who has never married: a person who has lost a spouse or separated from one can also be ayyim.

This difference in common usage is useful, though words concerning ʿuzūbah have not, in every period of Arabic, been restricted to people who have never married.

The Qur’anic use of ʿ Z B concerns being distant or hidden, rather than describing someone without a spouse. A familiar meaning in later Arabic must therefore not be transferred to the Qur’anic text without examination.

A Y M (ayyim) compared with TH Y B (thayyib)

Study of TH Y B

In contrast with bikr, thayyib concerns prior conjugal experience. One familiar lexical definition describes a woman separated from her husband after marriage and intercourse. Merely having entered a marriage contract is therefore not a complete definition without this distinction.

Al-Taḥrīm 66:5 places thayyibāt (ثَيِّبَات) and abkār (أَبْكَار) alongside each other.

In its general sense, ayyim does not carry this opposition. A woman without a spouse may be a virgin or may have been married before.

Thayyib thus concerns prior conjugal experience and, in some definitions, previous marriage and separation, whereas ayyim highlights the present condition of having or not having a spouse.

Some specific uses do apply ayyim to a previously married woman. This narrower usage does not remove its broader meaning in Al-Nūr 24:32.

A Y M (ayyim) compared with B K R (bikr)

Study of B K R

Bikr concerns firstness and, in marital usage, someone who has not previously had conjugal relations.

Ayyim is neutral in this respect: it neither establishes nor denies such a history.

A person may be both a virgin and without a spouse, but not every person without a spouse is a virgin.

This distinction prevents translating al-ayāmā in Al-Nūr 24:32 exclusively as “virgin girls” or exclusively as “previously married women.”

A Y M (ayyim) compared with Y T M (yatīm)

Study of Y T M

In its primary human usage, yatīm describes someone whose father died before the person reached puberty; the designation may sometimes continue afterward in reference to the earlier condition. For animals, the mother’s loss is relevant. Ayyim concerns having no spouse.

The resemblance between the plurals ayāmā and yatāmā does not establish identical meanings.

A person may have been orphaned yet have a spouse; someone without a spouse may have both parents alive.

This distinction must be preserved in Al-Nūr 24:32 and the verses concerning orphans.

A Y M (ayyim) compared with N K Ḥ (nikāḥ)

Study of N K Ḥ

Nikāḥ concerns marriage and conjugal relations in the relevant usages.

Ayyim, however, describes the condition of having no spouse. Both families concern a social subject, but one names a condition and the other concerns the act or relationship of marriage.

The distinction is clear in Al-Nūr 24:32: al-ayāmā denotes people without spouses, while ankiḥū commands facilitating their marriage.

Al-ayāmā therefore cannot be redefined as “people who must necessarily marry.” Necessity, possibility, conditions, and personal choice require examination of the command and context as a whole; they do not follow from the name of the condition itself.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

For ayyim, a suitable general rendering is “without a spouse” or, where clear, “unmarried.” Both apply to women and men without deciding whether they have previously been married.

“Single” can also work, but everyday English sometimes associates it especially with people who have never married. “Without a spouse” makes the Arabic word’s broad scope explicit.

“Widow” or “widower” is suitable only when the loss of a spouse, especially through death, is established by context. These renderings must not be generalized to every occurrence of ayyim.

For al-ayāmā minkum in Al-Nūr 24:32, the principal proposal is:

“Those among you who have no spouse…”

This includes both women and men and describes their situation without adding unwarranted restrictions.

The complete first clause can be rendered:

“Arrange marriage for those among you who have no spouse…”

A contextual explanation can clarify that a community is being addressed to help make marriage possible for these people. Consent, conditions of marriage, and individual responsibilities should not, without evidence, be inserted into the lexical meaning of al-ayāmā itself.

For non-Qur’anic forms of this family, taʾayyum can mean “becoming without a spouse” or “remaining without a spouse for a period,” while aymah can denote the condition or period of being without a spouse.

The other meanings, aym for a snake and iyām for smoke, should remain separate in historical study and have no role in translating Al-Nūr 24:32.

Conclusion

In Qur’anic usage, A Y M concerns being without a spouse. Ayyim can describe a woman or man currently without a spouse, whether previously married or not.

Al-ayāmā is its plural and occurs in Al-Nūr 24:32 within a command to facilitate marriage.

The designation itself determines neither age, sex, marital history, poverty, fitness, nor consent to marriage. Each of these questions requires its own evidence and examination.

Earlier Arabic also records similar forms for snakes and smoke. These branches must not be merged with the marital sense into an invented definition, especially since lexicographers dispute the historical affiliation of some of them.

In its Qur’anic semantic field, ayyim precisely denotes a woman or man who has no spouse. Ayāmā is the plural of such people, without itself determining the cause of their condition or the conditions for their marriage.
