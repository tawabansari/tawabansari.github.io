The root ف ر ر — Fleeing, escaping danger, and seeking refuge

The triliteral Arabic root ف ر ر concerns fleeing, moving swiftly away, and leaving a situation in which a person or animal does not wish to remain or which it does not wish to face. فَرَّ is used when someone withdraws from danger, an enemy, an unwanted situation, or something they avoid confronting.

Such flight does not always arise from fear and is not necessarily cowardice or wrongdoing. Someone may escape a tyrant to preserve life, a fighter may retreat to reposition, and another person may leave to evade responsibility.

The Qur’an also uses this root in a distinctive expression: فَفِرُّوا إِلَى اللَّهِ, “So flee to God.” Here flight means turning toward God as destination and refuge, rather than moving away from Him.

The root occurs eleven times in ten Qur’anic verses, through forms of the verb فَرَّ, the verbal noun فِرَار, and the noun مَفَرّ. These uses range from Moses escaping the threat of death and wild animals fleeing to people turning away from the Messengers’ call, evading responsibility, fleeing death, and seeking refuge at the Resurrection.

1. The root in early Arabic and the historical standing of its evidence

In early Arabic, فَرَّ يَفِرُّ فِرَارًا means fleeing and moving away from someone or something.

In فَرَّ مِنَ الْعَدُوِّ, someone flees an enemy. In فَرَّ مِنَ الْمَوْتِ, someone avoids death and tries not to encounter it.

By contrast, فَرَّ إِلَيْهِ can express hastening toward someone and seeking refuge with them.

The two constructions are connected. Fleeing something usually entails moving elsewhere, but a sentence may specify only what is left behind or only the destination.

Dictionaries explain فِرَار through هَرَب and رَوَغَان. The former means flight; the latter concerns evasive movement and withdrawing from confrontation.

Yet flight does not always mean hiding. Someone may openly and rapidly leave a danger behind. Nor does flight necessarily mean final defeat; it can form part of a calculated maneuver.

Evidence attributed to pre-Islamic poetry

In the Muʿallaqah attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imru’ al-Qays, he describes his horse:

مِكَرٍّ مِفَرٍّ مُقْبِلٍ مُدْبِرٍ مَعًا
كَجُلْمُودِ صَخْرٍ حَطَّهُ السَّيْلُ مِنْ عَلِ

Mikarrin mifarrin muqbilin mudbirin maʿan / ka-julmūdi ṣakhrin ḥaṭṭahu s-saylu min ʿali.

Translation: “Ready to charge and retreat, to advance and withdraw, like a mass of rock swept down from above by a torrent.”

In this line, مِكَرّ contrasts with مِفَرّ.

كَرّ concerns turning back and advancing, while فَرّ concerns withdrawing. The surrounding passage describes a hunting horse’s agility; these same movements can also be employed in battle.

The poet praises his horse’s agility and capability in both movements.

Thus مِفَرّ does not mark the horse’s fear or weakness. It describes a valued capacity for quick movement and retreat.

مَعًا gathers these capacities in a single description. It need not assert literal motion in opposite directions at the same instant; commentators also associate the expression with speed and readiness to perform both movements.

The line is preserved in the Muʿallaqah attributed to Imru’ al-Qays and its early commentaries. Although transmitted through later narrations, its place in this recognized poem provides important evidence for investigating the root’s pre-Qur’anic use.

A clear semantic point follows: flight names a movement or act of withdrawal; its moral value depends on the reason and circumstances of that movement.

Flight in Arab social life

In early Arab tribal life and warfare, flight could respond to threats, defeat, pursuit, hostility, or fear of being killed.

Leaving a battlefield could be regarded as disgraceful, whereas retreating to preserve strength and return could form part of martial skill.

Escaping an oppressive ruler, a powerful enemy, or a threat to life likewise differed from evading responsibility and abandoning others.

Early poetry also reflects such distinctions, but the root alone does not identify which flight is courageous and which is blameworthy.

The danger, the person’s aim and responsibility, and the consequences of their behavior require independent examination.

Another lexical branch: Exposing an animal’s teeth

Early dictionaries record another use of فَرَّ:

«فَرَّ الدَّابَّةَ»

Farra d-dābbata.

It means exposing an animal’s teeth by drawing back its lips to determine its age.

This construction is transitive and acts upon the animal or its mouth, whereas فَرَّ مِنْهُ concerns fleeing something.

Some lexicographers identify exposing or uncovering as the central meaning of this branch.

اِفْتَرَّ can also refer to teeth becoming visible in a smile or laugh.

These uses do not establish that the meaning of flight necessarily developed from exposing an animal’s teeth.

Establishing such a historical development requires independent evidence. Exposing teeth or removing a covering should therefore not be artificially inserted into the Qur’anic definition of flight.

2. Linguistic forms and the semantic core

The main forms of this family include:

  • فَرَّ: fled or moved away.
  • يَفِرُّ: flees.
  • فِرَار: fleeing or flight.
  • فَارّ: one who flees.
  • فَرَّار and فَرُور: someone who flees repeatedly or is known for flight.
  • مَفَرّ: flight, or contextually a way or possibility of escape; lexicographers also record مَفِرّ for a place of flight.
  • أَفَرَّ: caused another to flee.
  • مِفَرّ: a horse or rider capable and agile in retreat and flight.

The Qur’an uses derivatives concerning flight, not those concerning the exposure of an animal’s teeth.

The Qur’anic verbal forms are فَفَرَرْتُ, فَرَرْتُمْ, فَفِرُّوا, تَفِرُّونَ, فَرَّتْ, and يَفِرُّ.

The verbal noun فِرَار occurs in definite and indefinite forms, while مَفَرّ occurs once.

The shared semantic core of the Qur’anic uses is:

Moving away or fleeing from something one does not wish to face or remain within; and, in another construction, hastening toward a destination to seek refuge.

This brings physical flight, rejection of a call, and turning toward God into a shared semantic field without treating them as identical bodily actions.

3. Flight to preserve life and escape danger

A. Moses’ flight from Pharaoh and his people

Al-Shuʿarā’ 26:21

فَفَرَرْتُ مِنْكُمْ لَمَّا خِفْتُكُمْ فَوَهَبَ لِي رَبِّي حُكْمًا وَجَعَلَنِي مِنَ الْمُرْسَلِينَ

Fa-farartu minkum lammā khiftukum fa-wahaba lī rabbī ḥukman wa-jaʿalanī mina l-mursalīn.

Translation: “So I fled from you when I feared you; then my Lord granted me judgment and made me one of the Messengers.”

Moses says this in reply to Pharaoh.

In the preceding verses, Pharaoh recalls Moses’ past and what he had done.

After referring to his condition at that time, Moses replies that he fled when he feared them.

The verse places two distinct concepts together:

فَفَرَرْتُ مِنْكُمْ — I fled from you.

لَمَّا خِفْتُكُمْ — when I feared you.

خَوْف describes fear, whereas فِرَار is Moses’ action in response to it.

The root ف ر ر thus does not itself mean fear, although fear can cause flight.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:20 and al-Qaṣaṣ 28:21 clarify the background. Moses learns that a group is deliberating about killing him; he then leaves the city, fearful and alert to danger.

These passages show that Moses fled to preserve his life against a real threat.

The Qur’an does not label this flight itself cowardice or disobedience.

Moses subsequently receives his commission as a Messenger and confronts the same political power again.

Escaping danger at one stage of life therefore does not necessarily mean permanently abandoning responsibility or being incapable of confronting oppression.

A person may withdraw from danger to survive and later undertake a greater responsibility when conditions allow.

B. Fleeing the frightening scene of the companions of the cave

Al-Kahf 18:18

وَتَحْسَبُهُمْ أَيْقَاظًا وَهُمْ رُقُودٌ ۚ وَنُقَلِّبُهُمْ ذَاتَ الْيَمِينِ وَذَاتَ الشِّمَالِ ۖ وَكَلْبُهُمْ بَاسِطٌ ذِرَاعَيْهِ بِالْوَصِيدِ ۚ لَوِ اطَّلَعْتَ عَلَيْهِمْ لَوَلَّيْتَ مِنْهُمْ فِرَارًا وَلَمُلِئْتَ مِنْهُمْ رُعْبًا

Wa-taḥsabuhum ayqāẓan wa-hum ruqūd. Wa-nuqallibuhum dhāta l-yamīni wa-dhāta sh-shimāli wa-kalbuhum bāsiṭun dhirāʿayhi bi-l-waṣīd. Lawi ṭṭalaʿta ʿalayhim la-wallayta minhum firāran wa-la-muli’ta minhum ruʿbā.

Translation: “You would think them awake, though they are asleep. We turn them to the right and to the left, while their dog stretches its forelegs across the threshold. Had you looked upon them, you would have turned away from them in flight and been filled with terror of them.”

Here فِرَارًا accompanies two other expressions:

لَوَلَّيْتَ — you would have turned away.

رُعْبًا — intense terror.

The three expressions serve different roles.

The verb لَوَلَّيْتَ expresses turning away and presenting one’s back to the scene.

فِرَار expresses fleeing and moving away from it.

رُعْب is the intense fear that overtakes the observer.

The Qur’an thus describes a reaction in which an observer would rapidly withdraw from the scene in terror.

The verse does not explain precisely what made the young men’s appearance frightening.

فِرَارًا alone cannot establish that their bodies had a particular unnatural form, that frightening beings inhabited the cave, or that a specific physical phenomenon caused the flight.

The clear meaning is flight from a scene whose observation brings terror.

C. Wild animals fleeing

Al-Muddaththir 74:51

فَرَّتْ مِنْ قَسْوَرَةٍ

Farrat min qaswarah.

Translation: “Fleeing from a lion.”

The verse continues the image in al-Muddaththir 74:50, comparing those who turn away from the reminder to startled wild asses.

فَرَّتْ is a feminine singular past verb referring to the animals collectively.

Flight here is animal behavior.

An animal perceiving a threat flees to escape danger. The description does not concern the animal’s religious or moral responsibility.

Lexical and exegetical accounts interpret قَسْوَرَة as a lion, hunters, or powerful pursuers. “Lion” is selected for the direct translation here, while these alternatives belong in the discussion. All describe startled animals fleeing a threatening agent.

In context, this image describes people who turn away from the reminder.

The image itself must therefore be distinguished from the people it illustrates.

The animal’s flight is a natural response to danger; the verses morally evaluate the human rejection of truth.

The criticism comes from the context of human conduct, not from declaring flight bad in every creature.

4. Fleeing a call, truth, and responsibility

A. Noah’s people fleeing his call

Nūḥ 71:6

فَلَمْ يَزِدْهُمْ دُعَائِي إِلَّا فِرَارًا

Fa-lam yazidhum duʿā’ī illā firārā.

Translation: “But my calling only increased their flight.”

Noah describes his people’s response to his call.

In Nūḥ 71:5, he says that he called his people night and day.

Nūḥ 71:6 then states that this calling, instead of bringing them closer, met with increased flight and withdrawal.

Nūḥ 71:7 describes their conduct further: they put their fingers in their ears, covered themselves with their garments, and persisted in their position.

This context shows that flight need not mean physically running away or leaving a location.

It can take the form of refusing to listen, avoiding conversation, and declining to face the call.

Someone may remain physically present but act so that no argument or explanation reaches them.

This use extends the meaning of flight from physical movement to social and intellectual behavior.

Nevertheless, ف ر ر does not itself mean knowingly rejecting truth. Knowledge and intention must be established through other contextual evidence.

In Noah’s account, the neighboring verses explicitly describe persistence and refusal to listen. That context makes their flight blameworthy rejection.

B. Evading responsibility in wartime

Al-Aḥzāb 33:13

وَإِذْ قَالَتْ طَائِفَةٌ مِنْهُمْ يَا أَهْلَ يَثْرِبَ لَا مُقَامَ لَكُمْ فَارْجِعُوا ۚ وَيَسْتَأْذِنُ فَرِيقٌ مِنْهُمُ النَّبِيَّ يَقُولُونَ إِنَّ بُيُوتَنَا عَوْرَةٌ وَمَا هِيَ بِعَوْرَةٍ ۖ إِنْ يُرِيدُونَ إِلَّا فِرَارًا

Wa-idh qālat ṭā’ifatun minhum yā ahla Yathriba lā muqāma lakum fa-rjiʿū. Wa-yasta’dhinu farīqun minhumu n-nabiyya yaqūlūna inna buyūtanā ʿawratun wa-mā hiya bi-ʿawratin. In yurīdūna illā firārā.

Translation: “And when a group of them said: People of Yathrib, there is no place for you to remain, so go back. A party of them sought the Prophet’s permission, saying: Our homes are exposed. They were not exposed; they wanted nothing but flight.”

Here flight concerns a group’s conduct in difficult defensive circumstances.

The Qur’an is not speaking about everyone or everyone worried about their family. طَائِفَةٌ مِنْهُمْ and فَرِيقٌ مِنْهُمْ restrict the statement to particular groups.

One group sought permission to leave by claiming that their homes were unprotected.

The Qur’an expressly states, however:

«وَمَا هِيَ بِعَوْرَةٍ»

Wa-mā hiya bi-ʿawrah.

Their houses were not exposed in the way they claimed.

Their objective is then stated: إِنْ يُرِيدُونَ إِلَّا فِرَارًا.

The criticism concerns more than physical departure: it addresses making excuses and abandoning responsibility in a specific situation.

The next verse, al-Aḥzāb 33:14, considers the same group’s likely conduct under pressure and trial.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:15 mentions their prior covenant not to turn their backs.

Responsibility, honesty in one’s claims, and fidelity to commitments are therefore also at issue.

This does not imply that anyone escaping danger to protect family or life is a hypocrite or evading responsibility.

The verse exposes the falsity of a particular claim. If a home truly faces danger, its situation must be assessed on its actual merits.

C. Can fleeing death be beneficial?

Al-Aḥzāb 33:16

قُلْ لَنْ يَنْفَعَكُمُ الْفِرَارُ إِنْ فَرَرْتُمْ مِنَ الْمَوْتِ أَوِ الْقَتْلِ وَإِذًا لَا تُمَتَّعُونَ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا

Qul lan yanfaʿakumu l-firāru in farartum mina l-mawti awi l-qatli wa-idhan lā tumattaʿūna illā qalīlā.

Translation: “Say: Flight will not benefit you if you flee death or killing; even then, you would enjoy life only briefly.”

The root occurs twice in this verse:

الْفِرَارُ — flight.

فَرَرْتُمْ — you fled.

This repetition emphasizes the sentence’s main subject.

The audience consists of those avoiding the defensive situation described in the preceding context.

The Qur’an’s answer is that flight guarantees neither freedom from death or killing nor lasting enjoyment of life.

This must not become a denial of the value of every effort to preserve life.

The Qur’an itself reports Moses escaping the threat of death. Elsewhere, preserving life, migration, and avoiding destruction have a meaningful place.

The distinction is that people should not manufacture excuses to evade responsibility and imagine that abandoning a duty guarantees their ultimate destiny.

Flight here does not itself mean hypocrisy. Its condemnation arises from the particular context of covenant, responsibility, and the addressed group’s conduct.

5. Fleeing death and the inability to escape one’s ultimate end

Al-Jumuʿah 62:8

قُلْ إِنَّ الْمَوْتَ الَّذِي تَفِرُّونَ مِنْهُ فَإِنَّهُ مُلَاقِيكُمْ ۖ ثُمَّ تُرَدُّونَ إِلَىٰ عَالِمِ الْغَيْبِ وَالشَّهَادَةِ فَيُنَبِّئُكُمْ بِمَا كُنْتُمْ تَعْمَلُونَ

Qul inna l-mawta lladhī tafirrūna minhu fa-innahu mulāqīkum. Thumma turaddūna ilā ʿālimi l-ghaybi wa-sh-shahādati fa-yunabbi’ukum bi-mā kuntum taʿmalūn.

Translation: “Say: The death from which you flee will certainly meet you. Then you will be returned to the Knower of the unseen and the witnessed, and He will inform you of what you used to do.”

Here تَفِرُّونَ مِنْهُ concerns fleeing death.

Death cannot be avoided forever by changing one’s location.

Someone may survive an accident, treat an illness, or escape a danger, but these successes do not abolish mortality itself.

فَإِنَّهُ مُلَاقِيكُمْ indicates that death will ultimately meet a person.

It does not declare care for health or saving lives futile. Escaping death in a particular incident differs from escaping death forever.

The verse ends with return to God and people being informed of their deeds.

Fleeing death in this context thus also connects with responsibility and reckoning.

People may avoid confronting death’s reality, but mental avoidance or physical relocation cannot abolish that ultimate end.

6. مَفَرّ: Seeking escape at the Resurrection

Al-Qiyāmah 75:10

يَقُولُ الْإِنْسَانُ يَوْمَئِذٍ أَيْنَ الْمَفَرُّ

Yaqūlu l-insānu yawma’idhin ayna l-mafarr.

Translation: “On that day the human being will say: Where is escape?”

مَفَرّ belongs to ف ر ر and here concerns flight and the possibility of escape. With the middle consonant vocalized a, it can be understood as a mīm-prefixed verbal noun, although the question also evokes a route or place of escape.

Lexicographers distinguish مَفَرّ, “flight,” from مَفِرّ, “place of flight,” also reported as a reading. These should not be confused with مِفَرّ, the horse’s description in the poem.

“Where is a way of escape?” is consequently a natural contextual rendering.

Al-Qiyāmah 75:11 answers that there is no refuge.

Al-Qiyāmah 75:12 places the ultimate resting place with the Lord.

This connection answers the question about escape with the inability to find a way out of that situation.

In worldly life, flight may save someone from danger or create another opportunity. In this scene of the Resurrection, relocation and refuge no longer remove the reality of reckoning.

Thus مَفَرّ is more than the name of a location here: the question also concerns the possibility of escaping confrontation with one’s end.

The word itself does not mean punishment, Hell, or condemnation. Those ideas, where relevant, must come from context and other verses.

7. Fleeing one’s closest relatives at the Resurrection

ʿAbasa 80:34

يَوْمَ يَفِرُّ الْمَرْءُ مِنْ أَخِيهِ

Yawma yafirru l-mar’u min akhīh.

Translation: “The day a person flees from his brother.”

يَفِرُّ is a present verb, with الْمَرْءُ as its subject.

The person flees أَخِيهِ, his brother.

The following verses, ʿAbasa 80:35 and ʿAbasa 80:36, also name mother, father, spouse, and children.

ʿAbasa 80:37 clarifies the general reason: everyone that day has a concern sufficient to absorb them.

Flight from relatives thus relates to the intensity of distress and the importance of each person’s own situation that day.

The verse does not say that everyone comes to hate their relatives at the Resurrection or that all family affection was worthless.

Nor does flight alone mean denying kinship or ending affection.

In difficult circumstances, someone may be so occupied with their own survival that they lack the opportunity or capacity to attend even to those closest to them.

Here too, flight is explained by the particular situation of the Resurrection, when personal responsibility and destiny dominate one’s attention.

The image does not make love for family unimportant in worldly life. Other verses command kindness to parents, respect for relatives’ rights, and responsibility toward family.

ʿAbasa 80:34 concerns a particular situation in which ordinary social ties cannot relieve each person of their own burden.

8. فَفِرُّوا إِلَى اللَّهِ: Fleeing toward God

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:50

فَفِرُّوا إِلَى اللَّهِ إِنِّي لَكُمْ مِنْهُ نَذِيرٌ مُبِينٌ

Fa-firrū ilā llāhi innī lakum minhu nadhīrun mubīn.

Translation: “So flee to The God. I am a clear warner to you from Him.”

This verse illustrates a different use of ف ر ر.

In most of the preceding verses, someone flees from something:

An enemy, danger, death, a call, or a frightening situation.

Here, however, the destination of flight is specified:

«إِلَى اللَّهِ»

Ilā llāh.

That is, “toward God.”

This is not bodily movement toward a geographical location. Traveling an earthly distance does not bring a person to God.

The subject is reorienting one’s life and turning toward God.

The difference between مِنْ and إِلَى

These prepositions play important roles in the root’s usage.

In al-Shuʿarā’ 26:21, Moses says:

«فَفَرَرْتُ مِنْكُمْ»

Fa-farartu minkum.

That is, “I fled from you.”

مِنْ identifies what is fled from.

In al-Dhāriyāt 51:50, however:

«فَفِرُّوا إِلَى اللَّهِ»

Fa-firrū ilā llāh.

إِلَى identifies the destination and direction.

Both preserve the idea of flight and departure from a previous situation, but the first emphasizes escaping a threat, while the second emphasizes moving toward refuge.

Immediately afterward, al-Dhāriyāt 51:51 forbids placing another god alongside God.

This clarifies the command’s direction: people should abandon setting up rivals alongside God and reliance on other gods, and turn toward the one God.

فَفِرُّوا إِلَى اللَّهِ is therefore more than an invitation to fear. It calls for choosing the right destination and breaking attachments invested with divine standing.

Can one flee from God to God?

Some later mystical and religious expressions speak of “fleeing from God to God.”

That expression can be examined in philosophical or spiritual discussion, but it is not the direct wording of al-Dhāriyāt 51:50.

The Qur’an says:

«فَفِرُّوا إِلَى اللَّهِ»

Fa-firrū ilā llāh.

That is, “Flee to God.”

In this sentence, God is the destination of flight, not what one flees from.

The speaker then presents himself as a warner from God. The warning accompanies an invitation to turn toward God.

Consequently, applying this to flight from sin, oppression, associating partners, misguidance, or dependence on fabricated powers requires support from context and other verses.

The verb فِرُّوا alone cannot yield every detail of repentance, migration, worship, or spiritual practice.

The verse’s image is nevertheless clear and powerful: recognizing danger, people withdraw and seek safety. In this command, their turning is directed toward God.

This use shows that flight can be not only permissible but, in an appropriate context, something positively commanded.

9. Morphological and grammatical points

ف ر ر is a doubled root: its second and third consonants are both rā’.

In the masculine singular past form, the two merge as فَرَّ.

When endings such as تُ or تُمْ attach, the two consonants appear separately:

فَرَرْتُ — I fled.

فَرَرْتُمْ — you fled.

The present forms include يَفِرُّ and تَفِرُّونَ.

فَفِرُّوا is a plural imperative with the prefixed conjunction فَـ, meaning “so flee.”

فِرَار is the verbal noun, expressing flight itself.

It appears in several Qur’anic constructions.

In al-Kahf 18:18, فِرَارًا accompanies turning away and terror.

In Nūḥ 71:6, فِرَارًا expresses the people’s increasing withdrawal in response to Noah’s call.

In al-Aḥzāb 33:13, فِرَارًا is what the group seeks.

In al-Aḥzāb 33:16, الْفِرَارُ is definite and functions as the subject of يَنْفَعَكُمُ: flight will not benefit them.

The verbal noun retains the meaning of flight, but its grammatical role and the reason for flight differ across the verses.

مَفَرّ in al-Qiyāmah 75:10 likewise concerns flight and the possibility of escape.

It should not be equated with مِفَرّ in Imru’ al-Qays’s poem. The poem describes a horse agile in flight and retreat, whereas الْمَفَرّ in al-Qiyāmah concerns the search for escape.

Vocalization and word formation matter here.

10. Comparison with related roots

ف ر ر (فَرَّ) and ه ر ب (هَرَبَ)

هَرَب in Arabic concerns fleeing and leaving danger or an unwanted situation.

Its meaning is close to فِرَار, and both can often be translated “flight.”

The Qur’an uses فِرَار and its verbal family for bodily flight, turning away from a call, and turning toward God.

ه ر ب also occurs in the Qur’an: al-Jinn 72:12 contains هَرَبًا in a statement that one cannot elude God through flight.

The shared meaning of escape is therefore visible in the Qur’an itself. This does not establish an absolute distinction governing all uses of the two roots; historical comparison requires broader Arabic evidence.

ف ر ر (فَرَّ) and و ل ي (وَلِيَ)

تَوَلِّي in certain constructions concerns turning away or turning one’s back.

In al-Kahf 18:18, لَوَلَّيْتَ accompanies فِرَارًا.

Turning away and fleeing complement one another here.

Not every turning away is flight. Someone may discontinue a conversation, leave a place, or direct attention elsewhere without escaping danger.

Ordinarily, فِرَار emphasizes withdrawal as escape, whereas the family of و ل ي has a wider range of orientation, approach, and turning away according to construction.

ف ر ر (فَرَّ) and خ و ف (خَافَ)

خَوْف concerns fear and apprehension of danger.

فِرَار is the action of leaving a situation.

Moses places the two together in al-Shuʿarā’ 26:21.

Someone may fear without fleeing. Someone may also withdraw without intense fear, to prevent danger or make a calculated retreat.

Fear and flight are connected but are not one concept.

ف ر ر (فَرَّ) and ر ع ب (رُعْب)

رُعْب concerns intense terror.

In al-Kahf 18:18, it is the observer’s state upon encountering a frightening scene, while flight is the response described as following that encounter.

رُعْب expresses the intensity of fear; فِرَار expresses withdrawal.

This distinction prevents restricting every use of flight to terror or severe fear.

ف ر ر (فَرَّ) and ن ف ر (نَفَرَ)

In Qur’anic Arabic, نَفْر can concern setting out, leaving, and mobilizing.

Al-Tawbah 9:122, for example, discusses a group setting out, gaining understanding of religion, and warning their people. The contextual interpretation of who gains that understanding requires separate discussion; the lexical point here is setting out and movement.

نَفْر can describe group movement or departure for a specific purpose, whereas فِرَار ordinarily emphasizes escaping something or fleeing toward refuge.

Not every rapid movement or migration is therefore flight.

ف ر ر (فَرَّ) and ه ج ر (هَجَرَ)

Migration concerns leaving a former place or environment and going elsewhere.

Flight may also involve leaving a place, but not all migration is flight and not all flight constitutes migration.

In Moses’ story, escaping the threat of death leads to leaving the city.

Verses about migration may concern preserving religion, life, dignity, and the possibility of responsible living.

Flight emphasizes withdrawal from an unwanted situation, whereas migration emphasizes leaving an environment and relocating elsewhere.

The moral value of both depends on circumstances and purpose.

ف ر ر (فَرَّ) and ك ر ر (كَرَّ)

These roots contrast in Imru’ al-Qays’s poem.

In battle, كَرّ concerns turning back and charging.

فَرّ concerns retreat and withdrawal from confrontation.

A fighter may retreat to reposition and then attack again.

The contrast marks direction of movement, rather than making one invariably courage and the other invariably cowardice.

11. Challenges in understanding and translation

A major error is equating all flight with fear and cowardice.

The Qur’an describes Moses fleeing the threat of death. In transmitted pre-Islamic poetry, readiness to withdraw could also be part of a horse’s praised agility, with applications in hunting and warfare.

Withdrawal from danger is therefore not intrinsically blameworthy.

The second error is approving every escape.

In al-Aḥzāb 33:13, a group uses a false claim to abandon responsibility. In Nūḥ 71:6, Noah’s people turn away from his call.

Condemnation in these cases comes from the conduct and context, not from the verb of flight alone.

The third error confines flight to physical movement.

In Nūḥ 71:6, flight can mean refusing to listen and withdrawing from the call.

In al-Dhāriyāt 51:50, fleeing toward God is likewise not ordinary spatial movement. It involves turning to Him and leaving what keeps a person from the right destination.

The fourth error turns فَفِرُّوا إِلَى اللَّهِ into a vague expression of constant fear of God.

God is the destination of flight and refuge in the verse. The image should not be transformed into fleeing God Himself or placing Him in the role of an enemy to escape.

The fifth error infers that preserving life has no value from verses declaring the impossibility of ultimately escaping death.

The fact that death eventually meets a person does not make treatment, prevention of danger, escape from oppression, or saving others futile.

Nor does al-Aḥzāb 33:16 prohibit every military retreat or every effort to escape danger. Al-Anfāl 8:15 and al-Anfāl 8:16 distinguish wrongful turning away from maneuvering for battle or joining another group.

Finally, intellectual withdrawal should not be labeled deliberate concealment of truth without evidence. People may withdraw through fear, ignorance, painful experience, or lack of clear evidence. Responsibility must be assessed through their knowledge, intention, and actual behavior.

12. Suggested translations for Forqan

For فَرَّ in general usage, “fled,” “escaped,” or “withdrew from the situation” may be appropriate.

فِرَار can be translated “flight,” “fleeing,” or “escape.”

مَفَرّ in the Resurrection context is “escape,” “a way of escape,” or “a place to flee.”

In al-Shuʿarā’ 26:21:

“So I fled from you when I feared you.”

In al-Kahf 18:18:

“Had you looked upon them, you would have turned away from them in flight and been filled with terror of them.”

In Nūḥ 71:6:

“But my calling only increased their flight.”

In al-Aḥzāb 33:13:

“They wanted nothing but flight.”

In al-Aḥzāb 33:16:

“Flight will not benefit you if you flee death or killing.”

In al-Jumuʿah 62:8:

“The death from which you flee will certainly meet you.”

In al-Qiyāmah 75:10:

“Where is escape?”

In ʿAbasa 80:34:

“The day a person flees from his brother.”

In al-Dhāriyāt 51:50:

“So flee to The God.”

In the last verse, “Seek refuge with God” naturally conveys the destination but retains less of the image of urgency and flight than “flee.”

For Forqan’s base translation, “So flee to The God” better preserves the verb’s image and the direction expressed by إِلَى. Explaining its relationship with abandoning partners and turning toward God belongs in contextual analysis.

Brief word-box information could distinguish:

Brief root meaning: Fleeing, leaving danger or an unwanted situation, and seeking refuge toward a destination.

Meaning in the verse: According to form and context, physical escape, withdrawal from a call, fleeing death, or turning toward God.

Each contextual explanation should give only the meaning relevant to that verse, while retaining a separate brief statement of the root’s general meaning.

13. Conclusion

In early Arabic, ف ر ر concerns fleeing and moving away from someone or something. The famous example from Imru’ al-Qays’s Muʿallaqah praises a horse’s capacity to advance and withdraw. It shows that readiness to retreat need not signify cowardice, although the poem’s immediate context is hunting.

Dictionaries also record a branch involving exposing an animal’s teeth. A definite historical derivation of flight from that sense has not been established and should not be forced into the Qur’anic definition.

The Qur’anic occurrences illustrate a broad range of flight.

Moses escapes danger to preserve life; wild animals flee a threat; Noah’s people withdraw from his call; a wartime group invents excuses to evade responsibility; humans flee death but ultimately meet it; and at the Resurrection they withdraw from relatives and find no escape from reckoning.

The command in al-Dhāriyāt 51:50 is distinctive: فَفِرُّوا إِلَى اللَّهِ. Flight here means turning toward a destination and refuge.

The root’s core is flight and withdrawal from a situation one does not wish to remain in or confront. In فَرَّ إِلَى, movement toward a destination for refuge comes to the foreground.

Flight is not invariably cowardice or courage, nor intrinsically sin or virtue. One person may escape oppression and survive, another may evade responsibility, and another may recognize truth, abandon misguided attachments, and turn toward God.

What determines the action’s value is what a person flees from, the destination they seek, and the responsibilities they preserve or abandon along the way.
