Pledging, collateral, commitment to an obligation, and being held in pledge for one’s deeds

Brief root meaning: Something remaining fixed or held in connection with a right or obligation; placing property or another thing as a pledge or security; and, in extended uses, a person or thing being bound by deeds, a debt, or a condition from which release depends on something being fulfilled.

1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

The three-letter Arabic root ر ه ن concerns stability, holding, pledging, and making the release of something dependent on satisfying a right.

In financial usage, رَهْن denotes something provided as security to ensure repayment of a debt or fulfillment of an obligation. Someone might, for example, place property in a creditor’s possession to secure the creditor’s right until repayment.

The pledged property is connected with a particular obligation. While that obligation remains, release of the property is connected with its status.

In another usage, رَهِين describes someone or something held in pledge. It can refer to a hostage or someone bound by an obligation and, by extension, express a person’s relationship to their deeds and consequences.

Both fields occur in the Qur’an.

In Al-Baqarah 2:283, رِهَان occurs in the context of debt, travel, and the absence of a scribe. In Al-Ṭūr 52:21, every person is described as held in pledge for what they have earned. In Al-Muddaththir 74:38, every soul is likewise held in pledge for its deeds, while the following verse makes an exception for أَصْحَاب الْيَمِين, the companions of the right.

These uses are related, but their subjects and outcomes should not be treated as identical.

Pledging property with a creditor is a social and economic relationship. Being held in pledge for one’s deeds concerns responsibility and accountability. The shared image is being bound or held in connection with a particular right or matter; the nature of the pledge, conditions of release, and consequences depend on each verse’s context.

Forms examined and Qur’anic derivatives

The root ر ه ن occurs three times in three verses, in three word forms, in the Qur’an:

  • فَرِهَانٌ: The plural of رَهْن with the connective fa-, meaning pledges or securities; Al-Baqarah 2:283.
  • رَهِينٌ: On the faʿīl pattern, meaning held in pledge and bound by what one has earned; Al-Ṭūr 52:21.
  • رَهِينَةٌ: On the faʿīlah pattern, meaning held in pledge in connection with deeds; Al-Muddaththir 74:38.

Other members of this family recorded in Arabic include رَهَنَ, رَهْن, رُهُون, رُهُن, رِهَان, رَاهِن, مَرْهُون, رَهِين, رَهِينَة, رَهَائِن, ارْتَهَنَ, ارْتِهَان, مُرْتَهِن, رَاهَنَ, مُرَاهَنَة, and إِرْهَان.

These forms have different uses in Arabic: some concern pledges and security, some holding and continuance, and others wagering and competition.

In the Ḥafṣ reading used as the basis here, the three Qur’anic forms are رِهَان, رَهِين, and رَهِينَة. The alternative reading رُهُن is discussed below.

2. The semantic core and early Arabic usage

Stability and being held: an early lexical analysis

An early lexical analysis proposes this principle for the root:

الرَّاءُ وَالْهَاءُ وَالنُّونُ أَصْلٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى ثَبَاتِ شَيْءٍ يُمْسَكُ بِحَقٍّ أَوْ غَيْرِهِ

al-rāʾu wa-al-hāʾu wa-al-nūnu aṣlun yadullu ʿalā thabāti shayʾin yumsaku bi-ḥaqqin aw ghayrihi

Translation: “Rāʾ, hāʾ, and nūn indicate the stability of something held by a right or otherwise.”

The definition connects two features: remaining fixed or being held, and a relationship that produces that condition.

In a financial pledge, this relationship is clear. Someone provides property as security, and it remains pledged until the obligation is resolved.

More broadly, something can remain fixed or established without any financial agreement.

Arabic uses the expression:

رَهَنَ الشَّيْءُ

rahana al-shayʾu

Meaning: “The thing remained fixed or established.”

In one early usage, رَاهِن likewise describes something stable and enduring.

The semantic field of ر ه ن therefore extends beyond financial pledges, although pledging and security are among its best-known branches.

Stability should not, however, be taken to mean absolute immutability or eternal permanence.

Something can remain fixed for a time and later change. A pledge does not inherently mean that the property will remain with its recipient forever.

رَهْن: property securing an obligation

An early lexical explanation of رَهْن states:

الرَّهْنُ مَا وُضِعَ عِنْدَ الْإِنْسَانِ مِمَّا يَنُوبُ مَنَابَ مَا أُخِذَ مِنْهُ

al-rahnu mā wuḍiʿa ʿinda al-insāni mimmā yanūbu manāba mā ukhidha minhu

Translation: “A pledge is something placed with a person to stand in place of what has been taken from them.”

This definition concerns the financial usage.

Someone might borrow money and leave property with the creditor as security.

Several matters remain distinct: the debt, the pledged property, the person pledging it, and the person receiving it as security.

In Arabic transaction terminology, the person pledging the property is the rāhin, and its recipient is the murtahin.

The pledged property is marhūn.

This distinction matters because a pledge is not the debt itself.

The property’s value may be greater or less than the debt. Providing security does not itself mean that the debt has been repaid.

A pledge helps secure fulfillment of an obligation; it does not extinguish that obligation merely by being provided.

رَهَنَ and أَرْهَنَ: differences in verbal usage

Early Arabic records رَهَنَ الشَّيْءَ for pledging something.

The usage of أَرْهَنَ has been disputed by lexicographers.

Some early lexicographers rejected using أَرْهَنَ in place of رَهَنَ to mean pledging.

Others supplied examples supporting that usage in Arabic.

In some uses, أَرْهَنَ فِي السِّلْعَةِ concerns paying in advance for goods or offering a high price for them.

This is related to providing security, but it is not precisely the same act.

Such differences show why a verb’s pattern alone cannot determine every use’s meaning.

One construction can denote pledging, another remaining fixed, and another continuing or enduring.

The verb’s complements and the sentence’s context determine the appropriate sense.

رَهِين and رَهِينَة: what is held in pledge

Lexicons use مَرْهُون and رَهِين for something held in pledge.

رَهِينَة belongs to the same family and can denote a pledge or a person or thing held in pledge.

The word is also used for a hostage.

The general meaning “held in pledge” should be distinguished from the particular use “human hostage.”

Property can be pledged as security; the subject is then an object or asset.

A person can be held by someone else; the subject is then that person’s situation and freedom.

A person may also be described figuratively as bound by their deeds, promise, debt, or attachment.

Thus, not every رَهِين is a physical captive or an actual prisoner.

Al-Ṭūr 52:21 and Al-Muddaththir 74:38 concern people being held in pledge for what they earn and do, rather than property deposited with a creditor.

فَكُّ الرَّهْن: release from a pledge

In financial usage, فَكُّ الرَّهْن refers to releasing pledged property.

The expression is established in Arabic.

Fulfilling the obligation secured by the property provides the basis for releasing the pledge.

The image can also be related to releasing a person from confinement or dependence; this conceptual connection does not by itself establish the historical sequence of the usages.

But being held in pledge and being released from it are distinct stages.

The word رَهِين alone does not establish that someone can never be released or must be released at a particular time.

That conclusion depends on the conditions and other statements.

The distinction matters in Al-Muddaththir 74:38 and Al-Muddaththir 74:39, where the statement that every soul is held in pledge is followed by an exception.

رِهَان: pledges and wagering

رِهَان can be the plural of رَهْن.

For example, several assets provided as security can be called رِهَان.

In another usage, رِهَان also concerns competition and wagering, especially horse racing.

In this branch, مُرَاهَنَة concerns a contest whose participants stake property or something valuable on its outcome.

Both uses relate to placing something in pledge in connection with an outcome or obligation.

They should nevertheless remain distinct.

A financial pledge secures a debt or right.

A wager stakes something on the outcome of a contest or an uncertain event.

Thus, فَرِهَانٌ مَقْبُوضَةٌ in Al-Baqarah 2:283 does not mean wagering or horse racing.

The context concerns debt, recording agreements, and protecting the parties’ rights.

3. Pre-Islamic poetic evidence and the social use of pledges

Pledging children in verse attributed to Uḥayḥah ibn al-Julāḥ

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Uḥayḥah ibn al-Julāḥ says:

يُرَاهِنُنِي فَيَرْهَنُنِي بَنِيهِ
وَأَرْهَنُهُ بَنِيَّ بِمَا أَقُولُ

yurāhinunī fa-yarhanunī banīhi / wa-arhanuhu baniyya bi-mā aqūlu

Translation: “He wagers with me and pledges his sons to me; and I pledge my sons to him for what I say.”

The verse brings together يُرَاهِنُنِي, فَيَرْهَنُنِي, and أَرْهَنُهُ. Here أَرْهَنُهُ is a first-person imperfect, “I pledge to him,” not the past-tense أَرْهَنَ of Form IV.

The subject is a commitment and the placing of something valuable in pledge for it.

What is pledged is not ordinary property, however, but the participants’ children.

This usage illustrates how people could themselves be placed as pledges or guarantees in some early Arab tribal and social relationships.

Recording such a usage in poetry does not endorse it morally or legally.

Poetry supplies evidence about language and historical relationships; it does not establish permission to take people hostage.

That distinction matters in lexical research.

The language of wagering in this attributed verse also supplies evidence for this branch in pre-Islamic poetry.

Refusing to surrender hostages in al-Aʿshā’s poetry

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Aʿshā says:

آلَيْتُ لَا أُعْطِيهِ مِنْ أَبْنَائِنَا
رَهْنًا فَيُفْسِدَهُمْ كَمَنْ قَدْ أَفْسَدَا

ālaytu lā uʿṭīhi min abnāʾinā / rahnan fa-yufsidahum ka-man qad afsadā

Translation: “I swore not to give him any of our sons as a pledge, lest he ruin them as he has ruined others.”

The verse is transmitted in a context of conflict and political pressure between Arab tribes and an outside power.

Historical explanations connect it with a Persian king’s demand for hostages; that contextual report is distinct from the word’s lexical meaning.

Here رَهْن refers to a person held by another power to guarantee a commitment. Another transmitted wording has the plural رُهُنًا in place of رَهْنًا; both readings concern human pledges.

This differs significantly from a financial pledge.

A financial pledge places an asset as security for an obligation.

Human hostage-taking exposes a person’s freedom and safety to danger.

Al-Aʿshā’s verse explicitly expresses concern about harm to the children.

The pledge in this example is consequently associated with the danger of abuse of power.

But رَهْن itself does not mean corruption or torture: the harmful consequence is expressed by the verb يُفْسِدَهُمْ, “he ruins them.”

The heart as a pledge in Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā’s poetry

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā says:

وَفَارَقْتُكِ بِرَهْنٍ لَا فَكَاكَ لَهُ
يَوْمَ الْوَدَاعِ فَأَمْسَى الرَّهْنُ قَدْ غَلِقَا

wa-fāraqtuki bi-rahnin lā fakāka lahu / yawma al-wadāʿi fa-amsā al-rahnu qad ghaliqā

Translation: “I parted from you on the day of farewell with a pledge that cannot be redeemed; the pledge had become irredeemable.” The image concerns losing the possibility of redeeming a pledge, not merely physically locking it away.

Here the pledge is not property deposited in a transaction.

The poet describes his heart’s attachment and its remaining with the beloved.

The pledge image expresses attachment and the difficulty of release.

لَا فَكَاكَ لَهُ, “it has no release,” reinforces this.

The second hemistich is transmitted with variants including فَأَمْسَى الرَّهْنُ قَدْ غَلِقَا and فَقَلْبِي مُبْسَلٌ غَلِقَا.

Both versions convey a similar image: a heart caught in attachment and difficult to release.

This attributed example supplies evidence for figurative pledging as attachment or confinement in pre-Islamic verse.

The poetic sense of pledging one’s heart should not be imported into every Qur’anic use.

Al-Ṭūr 52:21 concerns a person’s relationship with their own deeds, not romantic attachment.

رَاهِن: fixed and continuing

In verse attributed to al-Aʿshā, رَاهِنَة describes wine that continues to be available and does not run out.

This usage is recorded in early lexicons.

It concerns neither financial security nor human hostages, but the continuation and stability of a condition.

This example relates to the lexical analysis in terms of stability and continuance.

It does not mean that every instance of رَاهِن denotes collateral.

Lexicons also record رَاهِن for an emaciated, exhausted person or animal unable to move on.

That usage concerns remaining in place and inability to move; the meaning of a financial pledge should not be derived from it.

Together, the examples show that the family encompasses pledging, commitment, stability, continuance, and attachment in early Arabic; they do not by themselves establish the historical direction of semantic development.

4. فَرِهَانٌ مَقْبُوضَةٌ: collateral and financial rights in Al-Baqarah 2:283

The verse

Al-Baqarah 2:283

وَإِنْ كُنْتُمْ عَلَىٰ سَفَرٍ وَلَمْ تَجِدُوا كَاتِبًا فَرِهَانٌ مَقْبُوضَةٌ ۖ فَإِنْ أَمِنَ بَعْضُكُمْ بَعْضًا فَلْيُؤَدِّ الَّذِي اؤْتُمِنَ أَمَانَتَهُ وَلْيَتَّقِ اللَّهَ رَبَّهُ ۗ وَلَا تَكْتُمُوا الشَّهَادَةَ ۚ وَمَنْ يَكْتُمْهَا فَإِنَّهُ آثِمٌ قَلْبُهُ ۗ وَاللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ عَلِيمٌ

wa-in kuntum ʿalā safarin wa-lam tajidū kātiban fa-rihānun maqbūḍatun fa-in amina baʿḍukum baʿḍan fa-l-yuʾaddi alladhī uʾtumina amānatahu wa-l-yattaqi Allāha rabbahu wa-lā taktumū al-shahādata wa-man yaktumhā fa-innahu āthimun qalbuhu wa-Allāhu bi-mā taʿmalūna ʿalīmun

Al-Baqarah 2:283

Translation:

“And if you are on a journey and find no scribe, then pledges taken into possession. If one of you trusts another, let the one entrusted discharge their trust and be mindful of The God, their Lord. Do not conceal testimony; whoever conceals it has a sinful heart. The God knows what you do.”

The meaning of فَرِهَانٌ

فَرِهَانٌ consists of the connective fa- and the broken plural رِهَان.

رِهَان is the plural of رَهْن and here refers to pledges or securities.

Grammatically, the noun occurs in an elliptical construction: a predicate such as “required” may be understood, or an omitted subject such as “the security” may be supplied. These analyses explain the clause rather than adding words to the Qur’anic text.

The sense can thus be explained as requiring pledges to be taken into possession in the stated situation.

A direct translation should preserve the nominal construction as far as natural language allows.

The word does not name the debt or contract; it names what is provided to secure a right.

The plural does not necessarily require several assets to be pledged in every transaction.

The verse addresses people collectively and discusses financial relationships among them.

In such a construction, the plural can reflect multiple transactions and participants.

مَقْبُوضَةٌ: pledges taken into possession

مَقْبُوضَةٌ belongs to the root ق ب ض.

It is a feminine singular passive participle qualifying رِهَان.

In Arabic grammar, a nonhuman plural may take a feminine singular adjective.

Thus, رِهَانٌ مَقْبُوضَةٌ means pledges received or taken into possession.

This qualification matters.

Promising to pledge something differs from actually delivering it into possession.

Someone might promise property as a pledge without yet handing it over.

مَقْبُوضَةٌ emphasizes receipt and possession of the security.

The word alone does not establish all legal details concerning possession, custody, use, or sale of pledged property.

Those matters depend on the relevant rules and obligations.

Pledging and transferring ownership

Pledging property differs from giving or selling it.

A pledge provides property as security for an obligation.

The word رَهْن alone does not imply that ownership is permanently transferred to the pledge’s recipient.

If property is pledged for a debt, the act of pledging does not by itself make the creditor its final owner regardless of the parties’ rights and obligations.

This distinction follows from the relationship between the pledge and the obligation.

Al-Baqarah 2:283 does not, however, explain in detail who may use, hold, or sell a pledge in every situation.

A root study should therefore not replace a separate examination of contracts and ownership rules.

The context of Al-Baqarah 2:282: recording debt and protecting both parties

Al-Baqarah 2:283 follows a verse concerning written debt records, witnesses, and justice in documenting obligations.

Al-Baqarah 2:282 discusses writing down debt with a specified term, dictation by the person owing the obligation, a scribe and witnesses, and avoiding harm to them.

Verse 283 then presents the situation of someone traveling without access to a scribe.

A pledge can provide security and protect a right in that situation.

This connection establishes the financial-rights context of رِهَان, rather than wagering or competition.

The context should not be read as protecting creditors alone.

Both parties’ rights matter when debt and testimony are recorded.

A pledge should likewise not become a means of wrongfully taking property or imposing an obligation beyond what is due.

Are pledges permitted only during travel?

The verse explicitly mentions travel and the inability to find a scribe.

That situation is therefore a clear textual instance of using a pledge.

Mentioning one situation does not alone establish a prohibition on pledging in every other situation.

A universal prohibition would require independent evidence.

Conversely, this verse alone does not explain every condition governing pledge agreements in all times and places.

We should distinguish the situation explicitly stated in the Qur’an from the ruling’s scope in other situations.

This prevents a clear Qur’anic example from becoming an absolute restriction without supporting evidence.

The readings فَرِهَانٌ and فَرُهُنٌ

In the commonly used Ḥafṣ reading, the phrase is:

فَرِهَانٌ مَقْبُوضَةٌ — “then pledges taken into possession.”

Other recognized readings also transmit فَرُهُنٌ مَقْبُوضَةٌ, with the same basic meaning.

رِهَان is an established plural of رَهْن.

The morphology of رُهُن has been analyzed differently: some treat it as a direct plural of رَهْن, others as a plural of رِهَان.

Some early analyses prefer رِهَان as the more straightforward plural formation; that grammatical preference does not negate the transmission of رُهُن in recognized readings.

Both forms refer to pledges in their basic meaning here.

The reading variation does not turn the financial pledge into something else, such as a wager.

فَإِنْ أَمِنَ بَعْضُكُمْ بَعْضًا: trust alongside collateral

The verse continues:

فَإِنْ أَمِنَ بَعْضُكُمْ بَعْضًا

Meaning: if some of you trust others.

It then commands the entrusted person to discharge the trust.

This connection is significant.

After mentioning pledges and security, the Qur’an also speaks of trust.

Financial relationships therefore do not rest only on holding another person’s assets as guarantees; trustworthiness and honoring commitments also have a place.

Trust does not extinguish the financial right itself.

If someone extends trust without taking a pledge, the other party’s obligation remains.

Accordingly, the verse requires the entrusted person to discharge the trust.

Thus, collateral does not replace honesty, and its absence does not license violating another’s right.

This conclusion follows from the connections between the clauses, rather than from the word رِهَان alone.

وَلَا تَكْتُمُوا الشَّهَادَةَ: do not conceal testimony

The latter part of the verse prohibits concealing testimony.

That instruction relates to protecting financial rights and obligations.

Someone may know about a transaction, debt, or right, and their testimony may help establish the truth.

Knowingly concealing that testimony can cause another person’s right to be lost.

Collateral, written agreements, and testimony each protect rights in a different way.

But رَهْن itself does not mean giving testimony.

The verse expresses testimony with a separate word; related concepts should not all be added to the meaning of ر ه ن.

5. رَهِينٌ: a person held in pledge for their deeds in Al-Ṭūr 52:21

The verse

Al-Ṭūr 52:21

وَالَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَاتَّبَعَتْهُمْ ذُرِّيَّتُهُمْ بِإِيمَانٍ أَلْحَقْنَا بِهِمْ ذُرِّيَّتَهُمْ وَمَا أَلَتْنَاهُمْ مِنْ عَمَلِهِمْ مِنْ شَيْءٍ ۚ كُلُّ امْرِئٍ بِمَا كَسَبَ رَهِينٌ

wa-alladhīna āmanū wa-ittabaʿathum dhurriyyatuhum bi-īmānin alḥaqnā bihim dhurriyyatahum wa-mā alatnāhum min ʿamalihim min shayʾin kullu imriʾin bi-mā kasaba rahīnun

Al-Ṭūr 52:21

Translation:

“Those who believed and whose descendants followed them in faith—We joined their descendants with them and diminished nothing of their deeds; every person is held in pledge for what they have earned.”

The meaning of رَهِينٌ

رَهِينٌ follows the faʿīl pattern.

Here it is the predicate of كُلُّ امْرِئٍ, “every person.”

It describes someone held in pledge or whose destiny is connected with what they have earned.

بِمَا كَسَبَ identifies what this dependence concerns.

The person is held in pledge for what they themselves have earned.

No property is being deposited with a creditor, and no financial contract between two people is being discussed.

The pledge image is extended to human deeds and responsibility.

The extension remains connected with the underlying meaning.

Just as pledged property is connected with a right, people have a connection with their deeds and acquisitions that cannot be disregarded.

كُلُّ امْرِئٍ: each person’s responsibility

كُلُّ emphasizes every individual.

امْرِئٍ refers to a person.

The clause consequently concerns individual responsibility.

Individual responsibility does not, however, exclude relationships or mutual influence between people.

People can help one another, influence one another, and contribute to social good or harm.

Nevertheless, each person’s own share in their conduct and acquisition remains.

Al-Anʿām 6:164 likewise states that no bearer bears another’s burden.

This principle relates to كُلُّ امْرِئٍ بِمَا كَسَبَ رَهِينٌ, “every person is held in pledge for what they have earned.”

It does not mean that everyone lives completely apart from others in all social events.

Individual responsibility and the collective effects of conduct can coexist.

Descendants and parents in the verse

Al-Ṭūr 52:21 begins with those who believed and whose descendants followed them in faith.

It then mentions joining their descendants with them without diminishing their deeds.

This relates to the final clause.

The Qur’an speaks of family connection and divine mercy while preserving individual responsibility at the end.

Joining descendants with parents does not mean that one person’s deeds are transferred to another without qualification.

The verse itself says بِإِيمَانٍ, “in faith.”

That qualification matters and must not be omitted.

وَمَا أَلَتْنَاهُمْ مِنْ عَمَلِهِمْ مِنْ شَيْءٍ states that nothing of their deeds is diminished through this joining.

Family connection should therefore not be taken to erase personal responsibility or the value of each individual’s deeds.

Does رَهِين mean imprisoned in Hell?

Some interpretive explanations describe رَهِين as being held because of one’s deeds.

In Al-Ṭūr 52:21, however, the clause occurs amid blessings for believers and the joining of their descendants with them.

It should therefore not be translated directly as “imprisoned in Hell.”

The broader meaning is being held in pledge for one’s deeds.

بِمَا كَسَبَ allows examination of human deeds and acquisitions for good or evil.

The phrase itself does not specify sin alone. Some interpretations connect its final statement particularly with those detained for evil deeds, while others emphasize its general wording; these explanations must remain distinct from the word’s lexical meaning.

“Every person is held in pledge for what they have earned” is consequently more precise than “everyone is imprisoned for their sin.”

The latter substitutes a narrower meaning for the Qur’an’s general wording.

Does being held in pledge negate divine mercy and grace?

The same verse begins with believing descendants being joined with their parents.

The context is therefore not solely a mechanical reckoning of deeds; divine mercy and grace are also present.

The final clause, meanwhile, states individual responsibility.

The two should not be set against one another.

Mercy and grace can have a role in a person’s outcome without rendering deeds and responsibility meaningless.

Individual responsibility likewise does not imply that The God judges solely by a human calculation devoid of mercy.

Earlier verses in Al-Ṭūr speak of blessings, divine favor, and protection of the mindful from punishment.

رَهِين should be understood within this setting: a person’s relationship with their deeds, rather than the denial of all grace and mercy.

6. رَهِينَةٌ: every soul held in pledge for its deeds in Al-Muddaththir 74:38

The verse

Al-Muddaththir 74:38

كُلُّ نَفْسٍ بِمَا كَسَبَتْ رَهِينَةٌ

kullu nafsin bi-mā kasabat rahīnatun

Al-Muddaththir 74:38

Translation:

“Every soul is held in pledge for what it has earned.”

The difference between رَهِين and رَهِينَة

Al-Ṭūr 52:21 uses رَهِينٌ.

Al-Muddaththir 74:38 uses رَهِينَةٌ.

This difference is significant morphologically.

رَهِين follows the faʿīl pattern; when used with passive meaning, this pattern can serve both masculine and feminine referents without changing form.

On that basis, some early grammarians explain رَهِينَة here as more than a feminine adjective agreeing with نَفْس: it can be an independent noun meaning a pledge or something pledged.

This noun-based analysis is supported by Arabic poetic usage, although lexicons also record رَهِينَة as a feminine form. The possible analyses should be distinguished.

The formal difference should not be made into an unsupported essential distinction between women and men, or between a soul and a person.

Both verses fundamentally concern being held in pledge for deeds.

بِمَا كَسَبَتْ: the soul’s connection with its deeds

بِمَا كَسَبَتْ in Al-Muddaththir 74:38 expresses the same relationship as بِمَا كَسَبَ in Al-Ṭūr 52:21.

The verb’s morphological difference concerns its referent.

Al-Ṭūr uses امْرِئ, with the corresponding verb كَسَبَ.

Al-Muddaththir uses نَفْس, with the feminine verb كَسَبَتْ.

Both concern a person’s deeds and acquisitions.

Acquisition in the Qur’an is not limited to obtaining wealth.

Through conduct, choices, and actions, someone can acquire what matters in their reckoning and outcome.

رَهِينَة here therefore does not concern financial collateral in a commercial agreement.

The soul is described as held in pledge for its deeds.

إِلَّا أَصْحَابَ الْيَمِينِ: the important exception

The next verse says:

Al-Muddaththir 74:39

إِلَّا أَصْحَابَ الْيَمِينِ

illā aṣḥāba al-yamīni

Al-Muddaththir 74:39

Translation:

“Except the companions of the right.”

This exception is essential for understanding رَهِينَة.

To say that every soul remains pledged forever without any possibility of release would ignore or empty the following exception of meaning.

The basic connection between people and their deeds must therefore be distinguished from their final condition.

Everyone is responsible for their deeds, but not everyone is assigned the same outcome.

These verses except the companions of the right from the stated condition.

Early interpretations identify this group differently, including believers, the inhabitants of the Garden, and those released from the consequences of wrongful deeds.

In the immediate context, the following verses place them in gardens.

The exception thus relates to release and a good outcome.

Does being held in pledge always have a negative outcome?

In Arabic, a pledge can describe a relationship whose outcome is not yet settled.

Pledged property may be released after the obligation is fulfilled.

In relation to deeds, رَهِين and رَهِينَة likewise do not alone determine someone’s entire outcome.

In Al-Muddaththir, the exception for the companions of the right and the subsequent description of the guilty show differing conditions.

In Al-Ṭūr 52:21, رَهِين also occurs in a setting of believers and divine mercy.

The root’s definition should therefore not be restricted to “condemned to punishment.”

Nor should responsibility be weakened until “held in pledge for one’s deeds” becomes an empty expression.

The image connects conduct with a person’s outcome and with accountability.

What has brought the guilty into their predicament?

Al-Muddaththir 74:42 asks what brought the guilty into Saqar.

Their answer identifies several actions and positions.

Al-Muddaththir 74:43 mentions not being among those who perform ṣalāh.

Al-Muddaththir 74:44 mentions failing to feed the needy.

Al-Muddaththir 74:45 describes joining those who indulged in vain and false discourse.

Al-Muddaththir 74:46 mentions denying the Day of Recompense.

Together these show that being held in pledge for deeds is not merely an abstract label: real actions and choices relate to people’s outcomes.

Yet رَهِينَة itself does not name any of those actions individually.

The following verses specify them.

The root’s meaning must therefore remain distinct from contextual examples and explanations.

Can intercession eliminate human responsibility?

Al-Muddaththir 74:48 says:

فَمَا تَنْفَعُهُمْ شَفَاعَةُ الشَّافِعِينَ

fa-mā tanfaʿuhum shafāʿatu al-shāfiʿīna

Al-Muddaththir 74:48

Translation:

“So the intercession of intercessors does not benefit them.”

This continues the discussion of the guilty.

Within this context, intercession is not presented as a way to rescue them from their deeds’ consequences.

Conversely, رَهِينَة does not itself negate every kind of mercy, forgiveness, or intercession in all Qur’anic settings.

Those concepts require examination through their own relevant verses.

Here, however, one principle is clear: people cannot make responsibility for their deeds insignificant by relying on others’ names or support.

Lexically, pledging supplies an image of connection and dependence; details of the outcome and release are learned from the relevant accountability passages.

7. Comparison with related roots

ر ه ن (rahn) and ح ب س (ḥabs)

حَبْس concerns holding and preventing release or movement.

رَهْن can also involve being held, but its familiar usage emphasizes a particular right or obligation.

Property may, for example, be pledged for a debt.

Holding it then relates to a financial right.

Not everything held is necessarily a pledge.

Someone might remain in a place, or an object might be stored for ordinary safekeeping, without any relationship of security and obligation.

Holding can therefore explain one feature of a pledge, but not its entire meaning.

ر ه ن (rahn) and و ث ق (wathāq)

وَثَاق concerns binding and securing.

In social and legal usage, security can be something that strengthens an obligation.

Accordingly, the Persian equivalent “security” is appropriate for رَهْن in relevant contexts.

But وَثَاق and رَهْن are not lexically identical.

وَثَاق can denote a bond or means of binding.

In financial usage, رَهْن concerns something provided to secure a right and obligation.

A financial pledge can serve as security, but not every kind of binding and fastening is a financial pledge.

In Al-Baqarah 2:283, رِهَان denotes financial pledges, while مَقْبُوضَةٌ describes their having been taken into possession.

ر ه ن (rahn) and أ م ن (amānah)

Amānah in the Qur’an concerns trust and honoring an entrusted right.

Al-Baqarah 2:283 mentions pledging and trust in the same verse.

Their juxtaposition clarifies the difference.

A pledge can provide tangible security for an obligation.

Trustworthiness concerns the proper conduct of the person trusted.

Someone may be trusted without pledging property, yet remain obliged to discharge the other person’s right.

Conversely, the presence of a pledge does not dispense with trustworthiness.

Pledging and trust are therefore complementary concepts, not two names for the same thing.

ر ه ن (rahn) and د ي ن (dayn)

In financial usage, دَيْن means debt.

رَهْن is something that can secure a debt.

Al-Baqarah 2:282 and Al-Baqarah 2:283 connect the two matters.

Debt and collateral nevertheless remain distinct.

Someone may owe a debt without having pledged property.

Property may also be provided as security without itself constituting the debt’s principal subject.

Translating رِهَان as “debts” is consequently incorrect.

“Pledges” or “securities” is appropriate.

ر ه ن (rahn) and ك س ب (kasb)

كَسْب in the Qur’an concerns acquiring and the outcome of human deeds.

Al-Ṭūr 52:21 connects رَهِين with بِمَا كَسَبَ, while Al-Muddaththir 74:38 connects رَهِينَة with بِمَا كَسَبَتْ.

The constructions show that a person is held in pledge for what they have earned.

The root ر ه ن does not itself mean acquiring or acting.

Acquisition identifies what the dependence concerns; رَهِين or رَهِينَة describes the person’s condition in relation to it.

These concepts should not be merged into a single definition.

ر ه ن (rahn) and ب س ل (ibsāl)

إِبْسَال can concern surrendering, consigning, or leaving someone exposed to the consequences of deeds.

Al-Anʿām 6:70 says:

أَنْ تُبْسَلَ نَفْسٌ بِمَا كَسَبَتْ

an tubsala nafsun bi-mā kasabat

Al-Anʿām 6:70

Translation:

“Lest a soul be consigned to its consequences because of what it has earned.”

This clearly relates to كُلُّ نَفْسٍ بِمَا كَسَبَتْ رَهِينَةٌ, “every soul is held in pledge for what it has earned.”

Both connect the soul’s condition with its own conduct and acquisitions.

But إِبْسَال and رَهْن belong to independent roots.

Lexical explanations also describe إِبْسَال in terms of surrendering someone to consequences or placing them in confinement.

رَهْن evokes being pledged and bound.

The two can approach one another in accountability contexts without sharing all their meanings.

ر ه ن (rahn) and ف ك ك (fakk)

فَكّ concerns opening and releasing something from a bond or connection.

Financial language uses فَكُّ الرَّهْن for releasing a pledge.

Al-Balad 90:13 uses فَكُّ رَقَبَةٍ.

The expression refers to freeing a human being from enslavement and confinement.

But فَكّ and رَهْن are distinct words.

رَهْن describes the pledge or binding relationship; فَكّ can name the act of release from it.

Freeing someone from slavery and releasing property from a financial pledge are also different events whose conditions and rulings should not all be equated.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

فَرِهَانٌ — Al-Baqarah 2:283

Suitable equivalents are “pledges,” “securities,” or, in an expanded explanation, “assets provided as collateral.”

For فَرِهَانٌ مَقْبُوضَةٌ, “then pledges taken into possession” preserves the direct meaning and nominal construction.

“Received securities” is also lexically possible, although it may suggest modern technical legal terminology more strongly.

The proposed Persian rendering, “pledges taken into possession,” is clear and defensible.

رَهِينٌ — Al-Ṭūr 52:21

Suitable equivalents include “held in pledge,” “bound by one’s deeds,” or the explanation “held in pledge for what one has earned.”

In the complete clause:

“Every person is held in pledge for what they have earned.”

This preserves the pledge image without importing imprisonment in Hell or permanent deprivation into the word.

رَهِينَةٌ — Al-Muddaththir 74:38

A suitable equivalent is “held in pledge.”

In the complete construction:

“Every soul is held in pledge for what it has earned.”

This rendering fits the root meaning and the verse’s construction.

Persian does not need a distinct feminine adjective to represent رَهِينَة.

The article explains the Arabic morphological difference, while a natural Persian translation conveys the sentence’s meaning.

Proposals for Forqan word boxes

Word: فَرِهَانٌ — Al-Baqarah 2:283

Meaning in this verse: Then pledges or securities; in the context of debt and the absence of a scribe during travel, qualified as having been taken into possession.

Meaning in this verse: Held in pledge for one’s deeds; describing each soul’s condition, from which the following verse excepts the companions of the right.

The family’s brief general meaning should remain separate from each derivative’s contextual meaning in these boxes.

9. Synthesis and final definition

The Arabic root ر ه ن concerns remaining fixed, being held, pledging, and making release dependent on fulfilling an obligation.

Financially, رَهْن is something placed with another person to secure a debt or right.

Transaction terminology calls the pledger rāhin and the recipient murtahin.

مَرْهُون and رَهِين can describe what is pledged.

Other branches use رَاهِن for something fixed and continuing, and مُرَاهَنَة for competition and wagering.

These uses relate to holding something in connection with a right, commitment, or outcome, but they are not identical.

Verse attributed to pre-Islamic poets supplies evidence for pledging in early Arab social and political relationships.

The verse attributed to Uḥayḥah ibn al-Julāḥ speaks of pledging children for a commitment.

Al-Aʿshā’s verse refuses to surrender children as hostages.

Zuhayr’s verse uses pledging to express the heart’s attachment and difficulty of release.

The examples show the family’s range across property, security, human beings, and attachment.

Its three Qur’anic occurrences illustrate two important fields.

In Al-Baqarah 2:283, رِهَان concerns protecting financial rights and obligations associated with debt.

In Al-Ṭūr 52:21, رَهِين expresses each person’s relationship with their deeds.

In Al-Muddaththir 74:38, رَهِينَة describes each soul in connection with what it has earned; the next verse makes an exception for a particular group.

Financial pledging and being held in pledge for one’s deeds differ in subject, while sharing the image of dependence and being held.

Final definition of ر ه ن:

The family concerns remaining fixed or being held. In its pledging branch, it concerns being held in connection with a right or obligation, with release depending on resolving that relationship; not every use denoting stability requires a debt or condition of release.

Financially, رَهْن is property or something else pledged to secure an obligation.

In human and ethical contexts, رَهِين and رَهِينَة can describe someone bound by their deeds, debt, or commitment.

The three Qur’anic usages can therefore be distinguished as follows:

1. رِهَان: Financial pledges or securities in the context of protecting rights and obligations.

2. رَهِين: A person held in pledge; in Al-Ṭūr 52:21, for what they themselves have earned.

3. رَهِينَة: A soul or person held in pledge in connection with their deeds, with attention to the following exception in Al-Muddaththir.

The root does not by itself mean financial indebtedness, imprisonment, sinfulness, punishment, or eternal deprivation.

Where relevant, those features are established by context.

Being held in pledge does not necessarily exclude release. Release or continued confinement depends on the relationship and conditions expressed in the sentence and related verses.

For Forqan’s research and translation, an appropriate short definition is therefore:

“Pledge and security; being held in connection with a right or obligation; and being bound by one’s deeds or acquisitions.”

This preserves the financial sense of رِهَان while clarifying its connection with human responsibility in رَهِين and رَهِينَة.

In passages concerning deeds, the pledge image recalls that people cannot separate themselves from responsibility for their actions. That responsibility does not negate mercy, forgiveness, or possible release; each must be examined within the full network of Qur’anic passages and according to its evidence.

Understanding ر ه ن therefore requires preserving three distinctions: the pledge itself, the right to which it relates, and the conditions that determine whether it remains pledged or is released.

These distinctions keep the lexical meaning clear and prevent financial rulings, metaphors of responsibility, and details of the final outcome from being merged into one definition without evidence.
