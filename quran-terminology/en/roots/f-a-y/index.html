The root F ʾ Y: a group, company, and supporting force

Qur’anic and lexical forms

The Qur’anic family indexed as F ʾ Y is represented by fiʾah, a group or company of people. It may be present in battle, assembled to support others, or distinguished from another group by a shared position or view.

Fiʾah denotes a company, group, or section of people. In military contexts it can describe troops standing together and supporting one another. That does not exhaust its meaning: the Qur’an also uses it for believers divided in opinion and for people expected to defend someone.

Fiʾah is not inherently positive or negative. One group may fight in God’s way while another opposes it. A small group may defeat a large one, and a large group may be unable to defend its members.

Size, legitimacy, capacity, and outcome are not fixed components of the word’s meaning. Adjectives, verbs, and context specify them.

1. Meaning in early Arabic

Fiʾah: a company of people

Arabic lexical definitions explain al-fiʾah as al-ṭāʾifatu mina al-nāsi or al-jamāʿatu mina al-nāsi: a group of people.

That is its direct, established meaning.

A fiʾah can form part of a larger community, without that larger community needing to be named. What matters is identifying people as a group.

Two companies of troops may confront one another in battle. People sharing a view in a social disagreement may likewise constitute a fiʾah.

These illustrate groups united in action and groups distinguished by a position or opinion.

The lexical connection with splitting and separation

Early Arabic records faʾawtu and faʾaytu for splitting something open.

For example:

«فَأَوْتُ رَأْسَهُ فَأْوًا وَفَأَيْتُهُ فَأْيًا»

faʾawtu raʾsahu faʾwan wa-faʾaytuhu faʾyan

“I split his head.”

Faʾw can also denote a cleft in a mountain, a gap between mountains, or an opening among elevated landforms.

The field includes something opening apart or a gap between its parts.

Some lexical analyses connect fiʾah with this sense, treating a group as a portion distinguished from a larger body.

On that account it approaches firqah, a division or section of people.

This possible derivation must remain separate from actual Qur’anic meaning. Fiʾah means a group, not the act of splitting.

An early poetic witness

A poem attributed to Al-Namir ibn Tawlab, who lived across the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods, includes this half-line:

فَأْوٌ مِنَ الْأَرْضِ مَحْفُوفٌ بِأَعْلَامِ

faʾwun mina al-arḍi maḥfūfun bi-aʿlāmin

“An open hollow of land surrounded by heights.”

The phrase describes land enclosed by elevations.

Faʾw here names an opening or lower tract between heights, attesting the field of gaps and openings in land.

Al-Namir’s lifetime makes his poetry relevant to language close to the Qur’anic period. The exact date of this verse is uncertain, so it should not be labeled unconditionally pre-Qur’anic.

The quoted half-line attests faʾw, not the social meaning of fiʾah. Connecting open terrain with a separated group is an etymological explanation, not direct evidence of that social use.

Another account: a group whose members return to one another

Other analyses relate fiʾah to F Y ʾ and fāʾa yafīʾu, to return.

The group’s members are understood as returning to one another for assistance.

One lexical explanation expresses this as:

«الْجَمَاعَةُ الْمُتَظَاهِرَةُ الَّتِي يَرْجِعُ بَعْضُهُمْ إِلَىٰ بَعْضٍ فِي التَّعَاضُدِ»

al-jamāʿatu al-mutaẓāhiratu allatī yarjiʿu baʿḍuhum ilā baʿḍin fī al-taʿāḍudi

“A mutually supporting company whose members turn to one another for assistance.”

This captures a feature of social groups: members may rely on one another when they need support.

It particularly suits the military expression mutaḥayyizan ilā fiʾatin in Al-Anfāl 8:16.

The word’s historical formation remains disputed. Some propose an underlying fiʾwah connected with splitting, while others connect it with returning. Analyses discuss the loss of wāw or yāʾ and the position of the hamzah.

These are different proposed derivational routes. Similarity of meaning does not prove both.

This study retains F ʾ Y, following the site’s Qur’anic index. That editorial classification does not settle the historical relationship between F ʾ Y, F ʾ W, and F Y ʾ.

What the historical evidence supports

Two conclusions should be distinguished.

First, classical Arabic and the Qur’an use fiʾah for a group of people. That meaning is established.

Second, lexical analyses propose connections with separation from a larger body and with mutual return for support.

This article does not present an independently dated pre-Islamic occurrence of fiʾah that settles the competing derivations. That limitation does not prove that no such evidence exists elsewhere.

Neither image should therefore be presented here as a conclusively established original concept in pre-Islamic speakers’ minds.

“A group of people” is a secure definition for Qur’anic reading. Mutual support is also established by context where relevant.

2. Qur’anic forms and occurrences

The indexed root occurs eleven times in nine verses.

All instances are forms of fiʾah. Their differences concern singular or dual number, grammatical case, and attached pronouns, not separate verbs or independent semantic derivatives.

The forms are:

Fiʾah: a group of people, with case endings such as fiʾatun, fiʾatin, and fiʾatan.

Fiʾatayni: two groups; the accusative or genitive dual.

Al-fiʾatāni: the two groups; the definite nominative dual.

Fiʾatukum: your group; the noun with the second-person plural possessive suffix.

The plurals fiʾāt and fiʾūn are recorded in Arabic but do not occur in the Qur’an.

Nor do the splitting verbs faʾaytu and faʾawtu occur in those forms.

The Qur’anic uses fall into three broad settings: opposing groups, groups differing in judgment, and groups offering or expected to offer support.

3. Fiʾah in confrontation and battle

A small group and a large group

Al-Baqarah 2:249 includes:

كَمْ مِنْ فِئَةٍ قَلِيلَةٍ غَلَبَتْ فِئَةً كَثِيرَةً بِإِذْنِ اللَّهِ وَاللَّهُ مَعَ الصَّابِرِينَ

kam min fiʾatin qalīlatin ghalabat fiʾatan kathīratan bi-idhni Allāhi wa-Allāhu maʿa al-ṣābirīna

Al-Baqarah 2:249

“How many a small group has overcome a large group by The God’s permission! The God is with the steadfast.”

This appears in the story of Ṭālūt and his troops, when some doubt their ability to confront Jālūt and his army.

Fiʾah here identifies people involved in confrontation.

Qalīlah and kathīrah specify relative numbers. Fiʾah itself means neither a small nor a large group.

Ghalabat states the outcome. Victory is not part of fiʾah’s meaning.

Numbers alone do not guarantee victory; a small group can overcome a larger one.

That does not make every minority right merely because it is small, or ensure victory in every confrontation.

Bi-idhni Allāhi and wa-Allāhu maʿa al-ṣābirīna connect the victory with divine permission and steadfastness, not an inherent advantage in small numbers.

Al-Baqarah 2:250 describes steadfastness and a plea for firm footing; Al-Baqarah 2:251 reports the defeat of Jālūt and his troops.

Fiʾah remains “group.” Faith, perseverance, numbers, and victory come from other words and the narrative.

Two groups with different positions

Āl ʿImrān 3:13 states:

قَدْ كَانَ لَكُمْ آيَةٌ فِي فِئَتَيْنِ الْتَقَتَا فِئَةٌ تُقَاتِلُ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ وَأُخْرَىٰ كَافِرَةٌ يَرَوْنَهُمْ مِثْلَيْهِمْ رَأْيَ الْعَيْنِ وَاللَّهُ يُؤَيِّدُ بِنَصْرِهِ مَنْ يَشَاءُ إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَعِبْرَةً لِأُولِي الْأَبْصَارِ

qad kāna lakum āyatun fī fiʾatayni iltaqatā fiʾatun tuqātilu fī sabīli Allāhi wa-ukhrā kāfiratun yarawnahum mithlayhim raʾya al-ʿayni wa-Allāhu yuʾayyidu bi-naṣrihi man yashāʾu inna fī dhālika la-ʿibratan li-ulī al-abṣāri

Āl ʿImrān 3:13

“There was a sign for you in two groups that met: one fighting in God’s way and another disbelieving. They saw them with their own eyes as twice their number. The God strengthens with His help whomever He wills. In this is a lesson for those with insight.”

Fiʾatayni initially identifies two groups.

Each then receives a separate description: one fights in God’s way, while the other disbelieves.

Fiʾah itself means neither believer nor disbeliever.

Its application to both opposing sides supports that neutrality; their positions are specified by the accompanying descriptions.

It is a collective social term applicable to different sides of a confrontation.

Iltaqatā states that they met. Their encounter comes from the verb, not from fiʾah.

Steadfastness when meeting a group

Al-Anfāl 8:45 states:

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِذَا لَقِيتُمْ فِئَةً فَاثْبُتُوا وَاذْكُرُوا اللَّهَ كَثِيرًا لَعَلَّكُمْ تُفْلِحُونَ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū idhā laqītum fiʾatan fa-thbutū wa-udhkurū Allāha kathīran laʿallakum tufliḥūna

Al-Anfāl 8:45

“You who believe, when you meet a group, stand firm and remember God much, so that you may succeed.”

Fiʾatan occurs in a military confrontation here.

Fa-thbutū commands steadfastness during that encounter.

Fiʾah itself does not identify a particular religion, ethnicity, or moral character. Context identifies the opposing force.

It should therefore not be lexically restricted to “a group of disbelievers,” even though the passage concerns an opposing force.

When the two groups saw one another

Al-Anfāl 8:48 states:

وَإِذْ زَيَّنَ لَهُمُ الشَّيْطَانُ أَعْمَالَهُمْ وَقَالَ لَا غَالِبَ لَكُمُ الْيَوْمَ مِنَ النَّاسِ وَإِنِّي جَارٌ لَكُمْ فَلَمَّا تَرَاءَتِ الْفِئَتَانِ نَكَصَ عَلَىٰ عَقِبَيْهِ وَقَالَ إِنِّي بَرِيءٌ مِنْكُمْ إِنِّي أَرَىٰ مَا لَا تَرَوْنَ إِنِّي أَخَافُ اللَّهَ وَاللَّهُ شَدِيدُ الْعِقَابِ

wa-idh zayyana lahumu al-shayṭānu aʿmālahum wa-qāla lā ghāliba lakumu al-yawma mina al-nāsi wa-innī jārun lakum fa-lammā tarāʾati al-fiʾatāni nakaṣa ʿalā ʿaqibayhi wa-qāla innī barīʾun minkum innī arā mā lā tarawna innī akhāfu Allāha wa-Allāhu shadīdu al-ʿiqābi

Al-Anfāl 8:48

“When Satan made their deeds attractive to them and said, ‘No one among the people will overcome you today, and I am your protector,’ then, when the two groups came into sight of one another, he turned on his heels and said, ‘I am free of you; I see what you do not see; I fear God, and God is severe in punishment.’”

Al-fiʾatāni is definite and dual, identifying the two known groups in the confrontation.

Tarāʾati expresses mutual sight: they came into view of one another.

Fiʾah still merely names the group. Satan’s deception, the promised victory, and the retreat are expressed elsewhere in the sentence.

Together with Āl ʿImrān 3:13, this shows that the dual can name opposing forces without judging either side’s legitimacy.

Numbers and inability to help

Al-Anfāl 8:19 states:

إِنْ تَسْتَفْتِحُوا فَقَدْ جَاءَكُمُ الْفَتْحُ وَإِنْ تَنْتَهُوا فَهُوَ خَيْرٌ لَكُمْ وَإِنْ تَعُودُوا نَعُدْ وَلَنْ تُغْنِيَ عَنْكُمْ فِئَتُكُمْ شَيْئًا وَلَوْ كَثُرَتْ وَأَنَّ اللَّهَ مَعَ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ

in tastaftiḥū fa-qad jāʾakumu al-fatḥu wa-in tantahū fa-huwa khayrun lakum wa-in taʿūdū naʿud wa-lan tughniya ʿankum fiʾatukum shayʾan wa-law kathurat wa-anna Allāha maʿa al-muʾminīna

Al-Anfāl 8:19

“If you sought victory, victory has come to you. If you desist, it is better for you; if you return, We return. Your group will avail you nothing, however numerous it may be. God is with the believers.”

Fiʾatukum means your group.

The suffix -kum identifies its relationship with the addressees.

They rely on its numbers and strength, but the verse warns that even a large group will not avail them.

Wa-law kathurat allows for a numerous group. Numbers do not establish the rightness of its position or its outcome.

This is not merely a counted collection of individuals: it is a group the addressees expect to act for their benefit.

That expectation is not established by the noun or suffix alone. Lan tughniya ʿankum supplies the relevant context.

4. Differences of opinion and joining a group

Two groups among believers

Al-Nisāʾ 4:88 states:

فَمَا لَكُمْ فِي الْمُنَافِقِينَ فِئَتَيْنِ وَاللَّهُ أَرْكَسَهُمْ بِمَا كَسَبُوا أَتُرِيدُونَ أَنْ تَهْدُوا مَنْ أَضَلَّ اللَّهُ وَمَنْ يُضْلِلِ اللَّهُ فَلَنْ تَجِدَ لَهُ سَبِيلًا

fa-mā lakum fī al-munāfiqīna fiʾatayni wa-Allāhu arkasahum bi-mā kasabū a-turīdūna an tahdū man aḍalla Allāhu wa-man yuḍlili Allāhu fa-lan tajida lahu sabīlan

Al-Nisāʾ 4:88

“What is the matter with you, divided into two groups concerning the hypocrites, when God has turned them back because of what they earned? Do you seek to guide those God has left astray? For anyone God leaves astray, you will find no way.”

This is important evidence of fiʾah’s range.

Fa-mā lakum fī al-munāfiqīna fiʾatayni addresses people divided over the hypocrites.

The two groups are therefore the addressees with differing positions, not two groups of hypocrites.

Fiʾatayni need not denote two armies. Differences in judgment, opinion, or practical decision can distinguish two groups.

A fiʾah does not necessarily require a formal organization, military commander, or specified membership count.

The word alone does not imply that all disagreement is blameworthy.

The rebuke concerns this dispute’s circumstances. Elsewhere, disagreement may arise from differing information, assessments, or understanding.

Fiʾah should not therefore be defined as a misguided sect, fanatical group, or deviant faction.

Joining a group during battle

Al-Anfāl 8:16 states:

وَمَنْ يُوَلِّهِمْ يَوْمَئِذٍ دُبُرَهُ إِلَّا مُتَحَرِّفًا لِقِتَالٍ أَوْ مُتَحَيِّزًا إِلَىٰ فِئَةٍ فَقَدْ بَاءَ بِغَضَبٍ مِنَ اللَّهِ وَمَأْوَاهُ جَهَنَّمُ وَبِئْسَ الْمَصِيرُ

wa-man yuwallihim yawmaʾidhin duburahu illā mutaḥarrifan li-qitālin aw mutaḥayyizan ilā fiʾatin fa-qad bāʾa bi-ghaḍabin mina Allāhi wa-maʾwāhu jahannamu wa-biʾsa al-maṣīru

Al-Anfāl 8:16

“Whoever turns his back on them that day, except while maneuvering for battle or joining a group, has incurred God’s wrath. His refuge is Jahannam—an evil destination.”

Fiʾah appears in mutaḥayyizan ilā fiʾatin.

Mutaḥayyizan concerns moving aside to another position; ilā indicates the direction of movement or joining.

The phrase describes changing position to join another group.

In battle, that may be a supporting force joined to receive assistance, regroup, or continue fighting.

The two exceptions differ: mutaḥarrifan li-qitālin describes maneuvering for battle, while mutaḥayyizan ilā fiʾatin describes joining a group.

Fiʾah alone cannot establish all legal conditions concerning retreat or military orders. Those depend on the whole verse and related provisions.

Its meaning here is clear: people one can join and stand alongside during confrontation.

The group may provide practical support, rather than merely function as a social label.

5. The limits of human support

The owner of two gardens

Al-Kahf 18:43 states:

وَلَمْ تَكُنْ لَهُ فِئَةٌ يَنْصُرُونَهُ مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ وَمَا كَانَ مُنْتَصِرًا

wa-lam takun lahu fiʾatun yanṣurūnahu min dūni Allāhi wa-mā kāna muntaṣiran

Al-Kahf 18:43

“He had no group to help him against God, nor could he defend himself.”

This belongs to the story of a man who relied on his gardens, wealth, and strength.

In Al-Kahf 18:34, he had claimed greater wealth and stronger numbers than his companion.

After his property’s destruction, Al-Kahf 18:43 describes the absence of a group able to support him.

Fiʾah identifies people expected to come to someone’s aid.

Yanṣurūnahu specifies the relationship: helping him.

Fiʾah need not mean an army. It can describe associates or supporters whose strength a person relies on.

The noun is grammatically singular, but yanṣurūnahu is plural. Agreement follows its collective meaning: one group comprising multiple people.

The verse states two limitations: no group could defend him, and he could not defend himself.

External support and personal capacity are distinct, both absent or ineffective here.

Qārūn and the group unable to save him

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:81 states:

فَخَسَفْنَا بِهِ وَبِدَارِهِ الْأَرْضَ فَمَا كَانَ لَهُ مِنْ فِئَةٍ يَنْصُرُونَهُ مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ وَمَا كَانَ مِنَ الْمُنْتَصِرِينَ

fa-khasafnā bihi wa-bi-dārihi al-arḍa fa-mā kāna lahu min fiʾatin yanṣurūnahu min dūni Allāhi wa-mā kāna mina al-muntaṣirīna

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:81

“We caused the earth to swallow him and his dwelling. He had no group to help him against God, nor was he among those able to defend themselves.”

The subject is Qārūn.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:76 describes his abundant treasures; Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:78 describes his reliance on knowledge he claims for himself.

At his downfall, neither wealth nor supporters save him.

Min fiʾatin yanṣurūnahu closely resembles Al-Kahf 18:43.

Both refer to a group a person might imagine as a source of support.

Fiʾah itself means neither wealth, political power, nor relatives. Such things may provide support in particular situations, but the noun means a group.

These verses broaden the picture beyond military settings.

A fiʾah may be a force in battle or a company of social supporters. The common feature is people forming a group, not a necessary military function.

Groups and imagined security

Al-Kahf 18:43, Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:81, and Al-Anfāl 8:19 reveal a recurring relationship.

People may rely on their companions’ numbers and assume that support will always suffice.

These verses show that a group or its size does not alone guarantee safety and victory.

That is a contextual lesson, not a new lexical meaning for fiʾah.

Fiʾah remains a group of people. Its ability to help depends on its capacity, circumstances, conduct, and actual limitations.

6. Comparison with related roots

F ʾ Y and F R Q: a group and separation

F R Q concerns separation, distinction, and differentiation.

Firqah and farīq can also name a group or section of people.

Fiʾah overlaps with them, especially where a group is distinguished within a larger body.

It does not itself mean disagreement, conflict, or hostility.

Two groups may fight, as in Āl ʿImrān 3:13, or differ over a question, as in Al-Nisāʾ 4:88.

That does not prove every fiʾah arises through enmity or division.

Nor does farīq invariably mean an enemy group; its context must be examined.

The families should not be divided absolutely into united versus divided groups.

F ʾ Y and Ṭ W F: a group and a section of a community

Qur’anic ṭāʾifah can denote a group or part of a community.

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:9 includes:

وَإِنْ طَائِفَتَانِ مِنَ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ اقْتَتَلُوا فَأَصْلِحُوا بَيْنَهُمَا

wa-in ṭāʾifatāni mina al-muʾminīna iqtatalū fa-aṣliḥū baynahumā

Al-Ḥujurāt 49:9

“If two groups of believers fight, make peace between them.”

Ṭāʾifatāni names two groups of believers engaged in conflict.

This approaches fiʾatayni in Āl ʿImrān 3:13: both can name confronting groups.

Ṭāʾifah also has many nonmilitary uses for people speaking, acting, or forming an identifiable part of a larger body.

Fiʾah is likewise not limited to war.

Both may therefore be translated as group in some contexts, without equating their full derivational histories or all their forms.

F ʾ Y and J N D: a group and an army

Qur’anic jund concerns troops, a mobilized force, or a body assembled under command or for a purpose.

The Ṭālūt narrative in Al-Baqarah 2:249 uses al-junūd for troops and fiʾah in the same verse.

They can occupy one semantic setting without being completely synonymous.

Jund more explicitly identifies a military force, while fiʾah can describe either a fighting group or a nonmilitary one.

A fighting force may be an appropriate contextual rendering, but army should not become an obligatory equivalent everywhere.

F ʾ Y and Ḥ Z B: a group and shared allegiance

Ḥizb also names people sharing a bond and direction.

The Qur’an speaks of ḥizb Allāh and ḥizb al-shayṭān. These expressions identify a group through allegiance and conduct.

Fiʾah can share a common position without necessarily being a political party or formally organized body.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:88 does not establish two permanent formal parties merely by calling the addressees two groups.

Group is a more suitable base equivalent for fiʾah; ḥizb should be considered separately in its own contexts.

F ʾ Y and F Y ʾ: a group and returning

Fayʾ belongs to F Y ʾ and concerns returning.

Qur’anic uses include returning to God’s command, the movement of shadows, and property obtained or restored under specified circumstances.

Some analyses connect fiʾah to this family because members may turn back to one another in danger.

Semantic closeness alone does not establish a single certain historical derivation for fiʾah and fayʾ.

Returning should not be inserted into every translation of fiʾah.

Fiʾatun tuqātilu fī sabīli Allāhi in Āl ʿImrān 3:13 describes a fighting group, not necessarily one returning anywhere.

In Al-Anfāl 8:16, however, joining the group can contextually involve returning to or joining a supporting force.

Possible etymology must not replace each verse’s direct meaning.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Group is the natural base equivalent of fiʾah.

Company or band may also fit, especially for a distinct group within a larger body.

Force or fighting group can clarify military context without becoming universal equivalents.

Suitable renderings for the Qur’anic occurrences are:

  • Fiʾatin qalīlatin: a small group — Al-Baqarah 2:249.
  • Fiʾatan kathīratan: a large group — Al-Baqarah 2:249.
  • Fiʾatayni iltaqatā: two groups that met — Āl ʿImrān 3:13.
  • Fiʾatun tuqātilu fī sabīli Allāhi: a group fighting in God’s way — Āl ʿImrān 3:13.
  • Fiʾatayni: two groups — Al-Nisāʾ 4:88.
  • Mutaḥayyizan ilā fiʾatin: moving to join a group — Al-Anfāl 8:16.
  • Fiʾatukum: your group — Al-Anfāl 8:19.
  • Idhā laqītum fiʾatan: when you meet a group — Al-Anfāl 8:45.
  • Al-fiʾatāni: the two groups — Al-Anfāl 8:48.
  • Wa-lam takun lahu fiʾatun: he had no group — Al-Kahf 18:43.
  • Fa-mā kāna lahu min fiʾatin: he had no group at all — Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:81.

The base translation should not automatically become party, sect, army, relatives, believers, or disbelievers.

Those may identify a particular group in context but are not its direct lexical meaning.

Nor does it mean a small group. Separate adjectives specify small and large in Al-Baqarah 2:249.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:88 should make clear that the addressees are divided about the hypocrites, not necessarily that the hypocrites form two groups.

Ilā fiʾatin in Al-Anfāl 8:16 must be read with its preposition and accompanying expression. Joining is conveyed by the phrase, not fiʾah alone.

Conclusion

Fiʾah names a group of people. It can be present in battle, distinguished by its position, or a source of support.

Two prominent derivational accounts connect it with separation from a body and with mutual return for assistance.

Both are semantically intelligible, but competing morphological accounts and limited historical evidence prevent either being treated as conclusively established here.

The Qur’an contains eleven occurrences, all forms of fiʾah.

They show its moral neutrality: one group can fight in God’s way, another oppose it, and other groups disagree over an issue.

Al-Baqarah 2:249 uses it for small and large groups; Āl ʿImrān 3:13 for opposing sides; Al-Nisāʾ 4:88 for addressees divided about hypocrites.

Al-Anfāl 8:16 presents a group to join during battle; Al-Kahf 18:43 and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:81 emphasize the absence of effective supporters.

The supported definition is:

Fiʾah is a group of people recognized as a distinct body. Depending on context, they may share a position, confront another group, or support one another.

Being a group establishes neither virtue nor fault, truth nor error. Numbers likewise guarantee neither value nor effectiveness.

These uses direct attention beyond names, numbers, and claims of strength to a group’s position, conduct, perseverance, purpose, and outcome.

Group is Forqan’s best base equivalent, preserving the word’s meaning while leaving each group’s particular features to its context.
