Core meaning: inclining or deviating from a direction or course; in thought and conduct, turning away from the right path; in vision, the gaze departing from its direction or object.

1. The root in classical Arabic and the question of earlier usage

The Arabic root Z Y GH denotes inclining or deviating from a direction or course. It applies both to perceptible movement and to intellectual or practical orientation.

“زَاغَ عَنِ الطَّرِيقِ” means that someone departed from the road and went in another direction.

“زَاغَتِ الشَّمْسُ” describes the sun inclining from its previous position in its apparent daily movement.

“زَاغَ الْبَصَرُ” means that the gaze departed from its direction or object.

“زَاغَ عَنِ الْحَقِّ” describes turning away from truth and leaving the right course.

An early lexical definition states:

الزَّاءُ وَالْيَاءُ وَالْغَيْنُ أَصْلٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى مَيْلِ الشَّيْءِ

al-zāʾu wa-l-yāʾu wa-l-ghaynu aṣlun yadullu ʿalā mayli al-shayʾ

Meaning:

“Zāy, yāʾ, and ghayn form a root denoting the inclination of something.”

This definition identifies a shared feature: something inclines away from the direction or condition against which it is being considered.

Yet these inclinations are not identical.

The sun moving from its previous position describes a natural condition.

The eye departing from its object describes the behavior or condition of the gaze.

A person departing from truth raises questions about thought, choice, and conduct.

Zaygh fundamentally denotes deviation; its moral significance depends on its object and context.

Pre-Qur’anic meaning and historical evidence

Classical lexicons record zāgha–yazīghu as inclining or departing. They illustrate it through roads, the sun, the gaze, and human orientation.

The expression “زاغت الشمس” is associated with the sun inclining and the shadow extending again after a stage of the day.

“زاغ عن الطريق” describes someone turning from the road in another direction.

These usages show that classical Arabic does not restrict the root to religious deviation: it also conveys a perceptible change of direction.

Determining its precise pre-Islamic usage requires distinguishing a phrase recorded in a classical dictionary from an independently dated pre-Islamic witness.

This article supplies no independently dated poetic witness securely establishing pre-Islamic usage of zaygh, zāgha, or azāgha. That limitation of the evidence presented does not establish that no such witness exists.

Some lexical accounts quote azwagh in poetry under the neighboring root Z W GH. This should not be presented as an independently dated witness to Z Y GH without explaining its form and transmission.

Despite this historical limitation, the Arabic lexical meaning is clear and agrees with the Qur’anic usages.

A defensible lexical definition is: Z Y GH denotes something inclining or deviating from a direction, road, or condition relative to which it stands.

In moral contexts this becomes departure from truth and guidance; in vision and natural movement, that moral judgment is not necessarily present.

2. Important forms and derivatives

زَاغَ يَزِيغُ — zāgha, yazīghu

This Form I verb means to incline, deviate, or turn away from a course.

زَاغَ عَنِ الطَّرِيقِ

zāgha ʿani al-ṭarīq

Meaning: “He departed from the road.”

زَاغَ عَنِ الْحَقِّ

zāgha ʿani al-ḥaqq

Meaning: “He turned away from truth.”

زَاغَ الْبَصَرُ

zāgha al-baṣar

Meaning: “The gaze deviated.”

These usages share a semantic field, but the thing described as deviating determines the sentence’s particular sense.

زَيْغ — zaygh

A familiar verbal noun of the root, denoting inclination or deviation.

In thought and conduct:

فِي قَلْبِهِ زَيْغٌ

fī qalbihi zaygh

Meaning: “There is deviation in his heart.”

This does not describe a physically crooked heart: the person’s inward orientation has departed from the right course.

This nominal form occurs in Āl ʿImrān 3:7.

زَيَغَان، زُيُوغ، زَيْغُوغَة — zayaghān, zuyūgh, zayghūghah

These verbal nouns are recorded in lexical accounts.

All three concern inclination or deviation.

Their different structures do not independently establish distinct kinds of misguidance or moral deviation.

These forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

زَائِغ — zāʾigh

The active participle of zāgha.

It describes someone or something inclining or deviating.

رَجُلٌ زَائِغٌ عَنِ الْحَقِّ

rajulun zāʾighun ʿani al-ḥaqq

Meaning: “A man departing from truth.”

بَصَرٌ زَائِغٌ

baṣarun zāʾigh

Meaning: “A deviating or wandering gaze.”

The first usage concerns moral or intellectual deviation.

The second describes the gaze.

Thus zāʾigh does not necessarily mean doctrinally misguided in every sentence.

زَاغَة، زَوَائِغ — zāghah, zawāʾigh

Zāghah is recorded as a plural of zāʾigh.

Zawāʾigh is a broken plural of the feminine form zāʾighah; it is not a sound feminine plural.

Neither form occurs in the Qur’an.

أَزَاغَ يُزِيغُ — azāgha, yuzīghu

A Form IV verb.

It means to cause to incline, turn aside, or deviate.

أَزَاغَهُ عَنِ الطَّرِيقِ

azāghahu ʿani al-ṭarīq

Meaning: “He caused him to depart from the road.”

أَزَاغَ قَلْبَهُ

azāgha qalbahu

Meaning: “He caused his heart to deviate.”

Zāgha describes deviation occurring; azāgha introduces an agent causing it.

This distinction is central to Al-Ṣaff 61:5, where both verbs occur together.

إِزَاغَة — izāghah

The verbal noun of azāgha.

It means causing deviation or turning something from the right direction.

إِزَاغَةُ الْقَلْبِ

izāghatu al-qalb

Meaning: “Causing the heart to deviate.”

Lexical accounts record this sense both for departure from a physical road and for departure from truth.

تَزَيَّغَ، تَزَيُّغ، تَزَايُغ — tazayyagha, tazayyugh, tazāyugh

Inclining or swaying is recorded for this family. The spellings tazayyugh and tazāyugh must nevertheless be distinguished when attributing a particular usage.

التَّزَايُغُ

al-tazāyugh

Meaning: “Inclining or swaying.”

The specific description of crooked or unevenly inclined teeth is recorded under tazāyugh.

Here the bodily condition and alignment of teeth are at issue, rather than moral deviation.

زَيَّغَ يُزَيِّغُ — zayyagha, yuzayyighu

A Form II verb.

Some lexical accounts record it for correcting crookedness or restoring something from deviation.

For example, “زَيَّغْتُ فُلَانًا” is explained as setting someone’s deviation right.

This is noteworthy because Form II does not necessarily express causing deviation in this usage.

This limited usage must not be transferred to Qur’anic azāgha.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:8 and Al-Ṣaff 61:5, the Qur’anic form is Form IV and concerns causing deviation.

الزَّاغ — al-zāgh

Lexical accounts describe a small kind of crow by this name.

Its Arabic or borrowed origin has been questioned.

It is also discussed under the neighboring family Z W GH in some accounts.

The bird’s name should therefore not be derived from the central sense of Z Y GH without evidence.

Among these forms, the Qur’an uses the noun zaygh and inflections of the Form I verb zāgha and the Form IV verb azāgha.

3. Deviation of the heart: inward orientation

Āl ʿImrān 3:7: deviation in approaching Qur’anic verses

هُوَ الَّذِي أَنْزَلَ عَلَيْكَ الْكِتَابَ مِنْهُ آيَاتٌ مُحْكَمَاتٌ هُنَّ أُمُّ الْكِتَابِ وَأُخَرُ مُتَشَابِهَاتٌ فَأَمَّا الَّذِينَ فِي قُلُوبِهِمْ زَيْغٌ فَيَتَّبِعُونَ مَا تَشَابَهَ مِنْهُ ابْتِغَاءَ الْفِتْنَةِ وَابْتِغَاءَ تَأْوِيلِهِ وَمَا يَعْلَمُ تَأْوِيلَهُ إِلَّا اللَّهُ وَالرَّاسِخُونَ فِي الْعِلْمِ يَقُولُونَ آمَنَّا بِهِ كُلٌّ مِنْ عِنْدِ رَبِّنَا وَمَا يَذَّكَّرُ إِلَّا أُولُو الْأَلْبَابِ

huwa alladhī anzala ʿalayka al-kitāba minhu āyātun muḥkamātun hunna ummu al-kitābi wa-ukharu mutashābihātun fa-ammā alladhīna fī qulūbihim zayghun fa-yattabiʿūna mā tashābaha minhu ibtighāʾa al-fitnati wa-ibtighāʾa taʾwīlihi wa-mā yaʿlamu taʾwīlahu illā Allāhu wa-l-rāsikhūna fī al-ʿilmi yaqūlūna āmannā bihi kullun min ʿindi rabbinā wa-mā yadhdhakkaru illā ulū al-albāb

Āl ʿImrān 3:7

Translation:

“He is the One who sent down the Book to you. Some of it consists of firm verses, the mother of the Book, and others are ambiguous. Those in whose hearts is deviation follow what is ambiguous in it, seeking discord and seeking its interpretation. None knows its interpretation except The God; and those firmly grounded in knowledge say: We believe in it; all is from our Lord. None takes heed except people of understanding.”

Here zaygh is a noun and verbal noun. The translation above pauses after “except The God.” Another grammatical reading coordinates those grounded in knowledge with the divine name in knowing the interpretation. That question requires its own examination and is not settled by defining zaygh.

“فِي قُلُوبِهِمْ زَيْغٌ” describes inward deviation.

It does not mean the physical heart bending or changing its position in the chest. Here the heart concerns intellectual and moral orientation.

The verse does more than mention ambiguous verses: it describes a particular group’s approach to them.

It first identifies deviation in their hearts.

It then describes their conduct: following ambiguous passages in pursuit of discord and their interpretation.

Zaygh here is therefore linked to how someone approaches truth and what they seek through that inquiry.

Zaygh and the use of ambiguous verses

At the beginning, the firm verses are called “أُمُّ الْكِتَابِ.”

This expression identifies their foundational place in understanding the Book.

The ambiguous verses are also part of that same Book.

Their presence does not make them false or worthless.

The warning concerns following them with an orientation toward discord.

For example, someone might select a phrase allowing several interpretations, set aside firm and clear verses, and build a categorical judgment on that phrase alone.

If the aim is truth, other evidence must also be examined and conclusions corrected when error becomes clear.

If the aim is confirming a prior assumption, preserving power, or imposing a predetermined conclusion, even a verse of guidance can become an instrument of discord.

This is an application to inquiry, not a claim that every interpretive disagreement or effort to understand ambiguous passages is itself zaygh.

Uncertainty about a verse, differing interpretations, or proposing a scholarly possibility does not alone establish deviation of the heart.

The verse emphasizes inward orientation and the pursuit of discord.

Outward disagreement does not disclose inward intention with certainty. Accusing a particular person of deliberately seeking discord requires more than a different interpretation.

Āl ʿImrān 3:8: seeking steadfastness after guidance

رَبَّنَا لَا تُزِغْ قُلُوبَنَا بَعْدَ إِذْ هَدَيْتَنَا وَهَبْ لَنَا مِنْ لَدُنْكَ رَحْمَةً إِنَّكَ أَنْتَ الْوَهَّابُ

rabbanā lā tuzigh qulūbanā baʿda idh hadaytanā wa-hab lanā min ladunka raḥmatan innaka anta al-wahhāb

Āl ʿImrān 3:8

Translation:

“Our Lord, do not cause our hearts to deviate after You have guided us, and grant us mercy from Yourself; indeed, You are the abundant Giver.”

Tuzigh is a Form IV imperfect verb in the jussive after petitionary lā. The construction is prohibitive in form but functions here as supplication.

Its subject is the implicit “You,” addressing The God, and qulūbanā is its object. Its jussive sign is the final sukūn; the medial weak letter drops because two vowelless consonants would otherwise meet.

The preceding verse identifies a group with deviation of the heart.

Here servants of The God ask Him not to cause their hearts to deviate after guidance.

Being guided at one stage does not eliminate the continuing need for care, steadfastness, and divine mercy.

A person may recognize truth but later depart from it under pressure from fear, advantage, prejudice, or desire.

Conversely, someone may recognize an error, return, and correct their course.

The prayer thus relates to human responsibility for maintaining a sound orientation.

Because tuzigh directly addresses The God’s action, it should not be translated merely as “may we ourselves not deviate.”

The verse explicitly petitions concerning divine action.

Its relationship to human choice and conduct must be examined within the wider network of guidance and misguidance verses.

4. Initial human deviation and divine izāghah

Al-Ṣaff 61:5: the explicit sequence of two verbs

وَإِذْ قَالَ مُوسَىٰ لِقَوْمِهِ يَا قَوْمِ لِمَ تُؤْذُونَنِي وَقَدْ تَعْلَمُونَ أَنِّي رَسُولُ اللَّهِ إِلَيْكُمْ فَلَمَّا زَاغُوا أَزَاغَ اللَّهُ قُلُوبَهُمْ وَاللَّهُ لَا يَهْدِي الْقَوْمَ الْفَاسِقِينَ

wa-idh qāla Mūsā li-qawmihi yā qawmi lima tuʾdhūnanī wa-qad taʿlamūna annī rasūlu Allāhi ilaykum fa-lammā zāghū azāgha Allāhu qulūbahum wa-Allāhu lā yahdī al-qawma al-fāsiqīn

Al-Ṣaff 61:5

Translation:

“And when Moses said to his people: My people, why do you hurt me while you know that I am The God’s messenger to you? Then, when they deviated, The God caused their hearts to deviate; and The God does not guide defiantly disobedient people.”

This is a particularly important occurrence because two different verbal forms appear together:

فَلَمَّا زَاغُوا

fa-lammā zāghū

Al-Ṣaff 61:5

“Then, when they deviated.”

And:

أَزَاغَ اللَّهُ قُلُوبَهُمْ

azāgha Allāhu qulūbahum

Al-Ṣaff 61:5

“The God caused their hearts to deviate.”

In the first phrase, zāghū is Form I, and deviation is attributed to the people themselves.

In the second, azāgha is Form IV, and The God is its subject.

This morphological distinction accompanies the verse’s sequence of meaning.

Moses first reminds his people that they hurt him despite knowing his mission.

Their deviation is then stated.

After that, The God causes their hearts to deviate.

Finally, they are described as defiantly disobedient.

Knowledge, conduct, and deviation

The phrase “وَقَدْ تَعْلَمُونَ” is significant.

Moses questions them about a matter they are said to know.

In context, their deviation is not presented as mere ignorance, lack of access to the message, or an unintended mistake.

Despite knowing, they pursue harm and opposition.

Their conduct is then described through zāghū.

The verse thus connects knowledge with responsibility.

That conclusion concerns this group and situation.

It does not follow that everyone with an incorrect understanding or departure from truth previously knew the truth clearly and deliberately rejected it.

People differ in knowledge and intention; judging these requires specific evidence.

Does The God cause hearts to deviate without a reason?

The verse attributes the initial deviation to the people:

فَلَمَّا زَاغُوا

fa-lammā zāghū

Al-Ṣaff 61:5

It then speaks of The God’s action:

أَزَاغَ اللَّهُ قُلُوبَهُمْ

azāgha Allāhu qulūbahum

Al-Ṣaff 61:5

Divine izāghah is therefore not presented without a preceding history here.

This connects with Al-Baqarah 2:26, where being led astray through divine parables concerns the defiantly disobedient.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:155 likewise speaks of hearts being sealed in connection with covenant-breaking and disbelief.

Al-Anʿām 6:110 connects the turning of hearts and eyes with their initial failure to believe.

Together these passages show a relationship between conduct and inward condition.

Someone who knowingly persists in denial and opposition may reach a stage at which accepting truth becomes more difficult.

Al-Ṣaff 61:5 nevertheless explicitly attributes this consequence to The God’s action.

“أزاغ الله قلوبهم” should not therefore be translated as only an automatic psychological process, omitting its divine agent.

Nor should the stated sequence be removed to conclude that The God first caused their deviation without any preceding human responsibility and then forced them to oppose.

The verse places their deviation before the divine causing of deviation.

Zaygh and human choice

The Qur’an connects guidance and misguidance with divine knowledge and will while repeatedly affirming responsibility for human conduct.

Al-Raʿd 13:27 connects divine guidance with turning toward The God.

Muḥammad 47:17 says He increases the guidance of those who are guided.

In the opposite direction, Al-Ṣaff 61:5 connects the people’s knowing deviation with their hearts being caused to deviate.

These passages describe two courses: turning toward guidance and its increase, or turning from truth and encountering the consequences of deviation.

They do not offer an exhaustive empirical or bodily explanation of divine action in the heart.

What is explicit is the attribution of guidance and izāghah to The God alongside human responsibility and orientation.

5. Zaygh under hardship and approaching a lapse

Al-Tawbah 9:117

لَقَدْ تَابَ اللَّهُ عَلَى النَّبِيِّ وَالْمُهَاجِرِينَ وَالْأَنْصَارِ الَّذِينَ اتَّبَعُوهُ فِي سَاعَةِ الْعُسْرَةِ مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا كَادَ يَزِيغُ قُلُوبُ فَرِيقٍ مِنْهُمْ ثُمَّ تَابَ عَلَيْهِمْ إِنَّهُ بِهِمْ رَءُوفٌ رَحِيمٌ

laqad tāba Allāhu ʿalā al-nabiyyi wa-l-muhājirīna wa-l-anṣāri alladhīna ittabaʿūhu fī sāʿati al-ʿusrati min baʿdi mā kāda yazīghu qulūbu farīqin minhum thumma tāba ʿalayhim innahu bihim raʾūfun raḥīm

Al-Tawbah 9:117

Translation:

“The God has indeed turned toward the Prophet, the emigrants, and the helpers who followed him in the hour of hardship, after the hearts of a group of them almost deviated. Then He turned toward them; indeed, He is compassionate and merciful toward them.”

Yazīghu is a Form I imperfect verb.

Its subject is “قُلُوبُ فَرِيقٍ مِنْهُمْ,” the hearts of a group of them.

The qualification “a group of them” matters: the verse does not say all the emigrants and helpers.

The verb is also preceded by:

كَادَ

kāda

Al-Tawbah 9:117

Meaning: “Almost.”

The expression describes approaching deviation, rather than reporting its complete and final occurrence for everyone mentioned.

Approaching deviation differs from persisting in it

In Al-Ṣaff 61:5, the group knowingly hurts Moses, and the Qur’an then states that they deviated.

In Al-Tawbah 9:117, the people followed the Prophet during the “hour of hardship.”

Severe difficulty can impose bodily, psychological, and social pressure.

Fear, fatigue, limited resources, or concern about the outcome may test a person’s steadfastness.

Such pressures do not independently establish a knowing denial of truth.

Al-Tawbah 9:117 describes a group’s hearts almost deviating and then describes The God’s merciful turning toward them.

Zaygh can therefore be an approaching danger without the person necessarily persisting in it or losing the possibility of return.

This distinction matters when considering responsibility.

Someone experiencing doubt, fear, or weakness under hardship should not be equated with someone deliberately persisting in opposition to truth without examining their conduct and outcome.

“ثُمَّ تَابَ عَلَيْهِمْ” — then He turned toward them

The verse concludes with:

ثُمَّ تَابَ عَلَيْهِمْ

thumma tāba ʿalayhim

Al-Tawbah 9:117

This describes The God turning toward them.

When attributed to The God, tāba does not mean returning from sin or error. It concerns merciful attention and acceptance of the servants’ return.

Here it concerns those who followed the Prophet under hardship and among whom some hearts almost deviated.

The verse therefore contains mercy and the possibility of correction as well as a warning about faltering.

Its difference from Al-Ṣaff 61:5 shows that zaygh cannot always be reduced to a fixed pattern of deliberate deviation followed by punishment.

6. Departing from the command in Solomon’s story

Sabaʾ 34:12

وَلِسُلَيْمَانَ الرِّيحَ غُدُوُّهَا شَهْرٌ وَرَوَاحُهَا شَهْرٌ وَأَسَلْنَا لَهُ عَيْنَ الْقِطْرِ وَمِنَ الْجِنِّ مَنْ يَعْمَلُ بَيْنَ يَدَيْهِ بِإِذْنِ رَبِّهِ وَمَنْ يَزِغْ مِنْهُمْ عَنْ أَمْرِنَا نُذِقْهُ مِنْ عَذَابِ السَّعِيرِ

wa-li-Sulaymāna al-rīḥa ghuduwwuhā shahrun wa-rawāḥuhā shahrun wa-asalnā lahu ʿayna al-qiṭri wa-mina al-jinni man yaʿmalu bayna yadayhi bi-idhni rabbihi wa-man yazigh minhum ʿan amrinā nudhiqhu min ʿadhābi al-saʿīr

Sabaʾ 34:12

Translation:

“And for Solomon, the wind: its morning journey was a month and its evening journey a month. We caused the spring of molten copper to flow for him; and among the jinn were those working before him by his Lord’s permission. Whoever among them departs from Our command, We shall make him taste the punishment of the blazing fire.”

Yazigh is an imperfect verb in the jussive within a conditional construction. Its final sukūn marks the jussive, while the medial weak letter drops to avoid two vowelless consonants meeting.

مَنْ يَزِغْ مِنْهُمْ

man yazigh minhum

Sabaʾ 34:12

Meaning: “Whoever among them deviates.”

The preposition ʿan in “عَنْ أَمْرِنَا” identifies what the departure is from.

Here zaygh means departing from the command.

The verse concerns jinn working for Solomon by his Lord’s permission.

In describing deviation, however, the Qur’an says:

عَنْ أَمْرِنَا

ʿan amrinā

Sabaʾ 34:12

Meaning: “From Our command.”

This should not be generalized without qualification to disobeying any personal wish of Solomon or any human order.

The explicit reference is to the divine command.

How does departure from a command differ from deviation of the heart?

Āl ʿImrān 3:7 locates deviation in the human heart.

Sabaʾ 34:12 attributes the verb to someone departing from a command.

The root is therefore not merely the name of an inward condition.

Sometimes inward orientation is at issue.

Sometimes conduct and departure from a particular command are described.

Yazigh in Sabaʾ 34:12 cannot therefore be restricted to “experience inward doubt.”

The verse concerns opposition to the command.

Man is conditional here: the wording does not establish that all the jinn working for Solomon actually deviated.

The ruling applies to whoever commits that departure.

7. Deviation of the eye: not necessarily moral misguidance

The Qur’an uses this root three times concerning eyes and vision.

These occurrences matter because zāgha does not always mean doctrinal deviation or sin.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:10: unsettled eyes in danger

إِذْ جَاءُوكُمْ مِنْ فَوْقِكُمْ وَمِنْ أَسْفَلَ مِنْكُمْ وَإِذْ زَاغَتِ الْأَبْصَارُ وَبَلَغَتِ الْقُلُوبُ الْحَنَاجِرَ وَتَظُنُّونَ بِاللَّهِ الظُّنُونَا

idh jāʾūkum min fawqikum wa-min asfala minkum wa-idh zāghati al-abṣāru wa-balaghati al-qulūbu al-ḥanājira wa-taẓunnūna bi-Allāhi al-ẓunūnā

Al-Aḥzāb 33:10

Translation:

“When they came upon you from above you and from below you, and when eyes swerved and hearts reached throats, and you entertained various thoughts about The God.”

Zāghat is a past verb whose subject is al-abṣār, the eyes.

The context describes confrontation with enemies and severe danger.

In such conditions the gaze may depart from its ordinary state.

A frightened person may look around, lose visual focus, or attend anxiously to different directions.

The Qur’an describes the condition through “زاغت الأبصار.”

The following image, hearts reaching throats, expresses the intensity of fear.

Deviation of the eye here is thus associated with fear and anxiety.

This visual condition does not itself mean disbelief or deliberate departure from guidance.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:11 speaks of believers being tested and severely shaken.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:12 separately addresses hypocrites and those with sickness in their hearts.

Fear and a wandering gaze in danger should therefore not by themselves be taken as evidence of hypocrisy, nor should everyone’s inward response be judged identical.

The verse describes a difficult situation in which people are tested.

Ṣād 38:63: eyes not finding the people expected

أَتَّخَذْنَاهُمْ سِخْرِيًّا أَمْ زَاغَتْ عَنْهُمُ الْأَبْصَارُ

a-ttakhadhnāhum sikhriyyan am zāghat ʿanhumu al-abṣār

Ṣād 38:63

Translation:

“Did we take them in mockery, or have our eyes swerved away from them?”

Zāghat is again attributed to al-abṣār.

The phrase ʿanhum adds that the eyes have swerved away from those people.

In Ṣād 38:62, a group in Hell asks why they do not see men whom they counted among the wicked in worldly life.

The next verse expresses two questions in their speech.

Had they taken those people in mockery?

Or have their eyes swerved away so they fail to see them?

Here deviation of the eye concerns not seeing people whose presence the speakers expect.

The verb zāghat does not itself mean mocking.

Mockery is expressed by sikhriyyan.

“زاغت عنهم الأبصار” concerns the eyes departing from them.

The verse does not independently establish that the people sought really are in Hell but merely hidden from the speakers’ view.

The speakers’ own question reveals a problem with their previous judgment of others.

People’s judgment that others are wicked should not be equated with The God’s definitive judgment.

Al-Najm 53:17: a gaze that did not deviate

مَا زَاغَ الْبَصَرُ وَمَا طَغَىٰ

mā zāgha al-baṣaru wa-mā ṭaghā

Al-Najm 53:17

Translation:

“The gaze did not swerve, nor did it exceed the limit.”

The verb zāgha is negated by mā.

Al-baṣar is its subject.

The following verb ṭaghā belongs to Ṭ GH Y.

Two features are therefore separately negated:

First, the gaze swerving.

Second, exceeding the limit.

This distinction matters.

Zāgha concerns the gaze departing from its intended direction or object.

Ṭaghā concerns crossing the limit.

The verse has not simply placed two exact synonyms together.

Al-Najm 53:13, Al-Najm 53:14, Al-Najm 53:15, Al-Najm 53:16, Al-Najm 53:17, and Al-Najm 53:18 concern witnessing and the Lord’s great signs.

Within that setting, the Qur’an says that the gaze neither swerved nor exceeded the limit.

This conveys the precision and steadiness of observation within what was shown to the observer.

The verse does not explain the bodily mechanism of seeing, the exact distance, or how the observation occurred.

Its negation of visual deviation does not alone constitute a universal statement about every visual state throughout the Prophet’s life; it concerns the observation in this passage.

8. Distinguishing zaygh from slipping, misguidance, and inclination

Z Y GH (zaygh) and M Y L (mayl)

Mayl denotes inclination or leaning and has a broad semantic field.

A person may incline toward something desirable or away from something.

Zaygh also concerns inclination and deviation, but in many usages involving roads and guidance it highlights departure from the right direction.

In vision, this departure can occur without moral judgment.

Not every inclination is zaygh, and not every zaygh is religious deviation.

Z Y GH (zaygh) and Z L L (zalal)

Zalal concerns slipping and losing one’s footing or stability.

In moral contexts it may describe error or a lapse.

Zaygh places more emphasis on changing direction and departing from a course.

Someone may slip under pressure or through ignorance.

Someone may also knowingly change direction and persist in deviation.

Zaygh alone does not establish deliberateness in every instance, just as zalal does not always establish absence of responsibility.

Al-Tawbah 9:117 describes some believers’ hearts almost deviating under hardship.

Al-Ṣaff 61:5 presents deviation alongside knowledge and opposition to the messenger.

The difference comes from context, not from the root being intrinsically involuntary in one verse and deliberate in another.

Z Y GH (zaygh) and Ḍ L L (ḍalāl)

Qur’anic ḍalāl concerns losing or failing to find the right way.

Zaygh denotes inclining or departing from a direction.

The senses overlap in some situations.

Someone departing from the right road may become lost.

Yet failing to reach the right way is not necessarily the result of knowingly abandoning it after recognizing it.

A person may not yet know the way or may be confused by conflicting signs.

Al-Ṣaff 61:5, by contrast, places the people’s knowledge and deviation in one context.

A person’s level of knowledge matters when judging their deviation morally.

Z Y GH (zaygh) and Ḥ Y D (ḥayd)

Ḥayd denotes moving aside or turning from a course.

Qāf 50:19 uses Ḥ Y D in connection with evading or turning away from death.

Zaygh also concerns deviation and changing direction.

Some usages of ḥayd emphasize moving aside or away, whereas zaygh can describe the inclination of direction or gaze itself.

This is a tendency rather than an absolute boundary.

Z Y GH (zaygh) and Ṭ GH Y (ṭughyān)

Ṭughyān concerns exceeding a limit.

Zaygh concerns departing from a direction or course.

Both occur in Al-Najm 53:17:

مَا زَاغَ الْبَصَرُ وَمَا طَغَىٰ

mā zāgha al-baṣaru wa-mā ṭaghā

Al-Najm 53:17

Here the gaze neither departs from its object nor exceeds its limit.

The Qur’an expresses two different aspects together.

Someone may leave a main course without the same notion of exceeding a limit being applicable.

Someone may also continue along a course yet exceed permitted limits.

Z Y GH (zaygh) and Z W GH (zawgh)

The two families are closely related in letters and meaning.

Lexical accounts record departure from the road with both zaygh and zawgh, with some preferring the yāʾ form.

In Qur’anic morphology, inflections of zāgha–yazīghu are classified under Z Y GH.

A witness transmitted with wāw should not be presented as certain evidence of the yāʾ form without explanation and historical examination.

This particularly matters when using early poetry.

For a brief root definition:

“Inclining or deviating from a direction or course.”

In contexts involving the heart:

“An inward orientation departing from guidance and the right way.”

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:10:

“The gaze swerving or wandering under intense fear.”

In Ṣād 38:63:

“The gaze departing from the people being sought.”

In Al-Najm 53:17:

“The gaze not departing from what is being observed.”

In Sabaʾ 34:12:

“Turning away and departing from the divine command.”

For azāgha in Al-Ṣaff 61:5:

“The God causing hearts to deviate after the people’s own deviation.”

These contextual meanings preserve the shared root sense while identifying the differing objects and constructions.

10. Conclusion

Z Y GH in Arabic concerns inclining or departing from a direction or course.

In perceptible usage, this appears in the sun inclining and the gaze swerving.

In thought and conduct, it extends to departure from truth, guidance, or a rightful command.

Classical lexical accounts define the family through inclination. This article does not supply independently dated pre-Islamic poetic evidence for all its forms; later dictionary records should not be assigned to that period without qualification.

The root occurs nine times in eight Qur’anic verses, in three morphological groups: the noun zaygh, inflections of Form I zāgha, and inflections of Form IV azāgha.

Āl ʿImrān 3:7 concerns the inward orientation of those following ambiguous passages in pursuit of discord.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:8, servants of The God ask Him not to cause their hearts to deviate after guidance.

Al-Tawbah 9:117 describes some believers’ hearts almost deviating under hardship, followed by The God’s merciful turning toward them.

Sabaʾ 34:12 concerns departure from the divine command.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:10, Ṣād 38:63, and Al-Najm 53:17 use the root concerning eyes and the direction of the gaze.

Al-Ṣaff 61:5 combines zāghū and azāgha: first the people’s own deviation, then The God causing their hearts to deviate.

These usages show that zaygh does not always mean deliberate denial of truth.

A gaze may swerve under intense fear.

A heart may almost lose steadfastness under hardship.

Someone may disobey a particular command.

A group may knowingly turn from truth despite recognizing it.

The moral judgment and degree of responsibility depend on the context and evidence about the people concerned, not on the verb alone.

The root denotes inclining or departing from a direction or course. The Qur’an applies it to eyes and vision as well as hearts, conduct, and obedience to commands.

Thus not every zaygh is sinful, and not every deviation establishes ignorance or lack of choice.

The distinctions between a swerving gaze, a heart nearing a lapse, a knowing departure from guidance, and divine izāghah must be preserved.

They allow the Qur’anic warning about departure from truth to be understood without imposing unwarranted judgments on fear, doubt, honest inquiry, or correctable human mistakes.
