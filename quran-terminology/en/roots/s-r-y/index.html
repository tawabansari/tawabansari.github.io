The Arabic root S R Y concerns movement and passage. One of its best-known expressions is the verbal noun surā: traveling by night. Arabic uses the verbs sarā and asrā for nocturnal movement, particularly journeys and departures during the darkness of night.

In the Qur’an, this family occurs chiefly in accounts of prophets and their companions traveling by night. Lot’s departure with his family, Moses’ movement with the Children of Israel, and the servant’s night journey from the Sacred Mosque to the Farthest Mosque are expressed with derivatives of this root.

Another use occurs in the oath “وَاللَّيْلِ إِذَا يَسْرِ,” describing the night as it passes. The form sariyyan in Mary’s story also has an established Arabic meaning, “a stream.” Another meaning, a noble or distinguished person, has likewise been proposed.

This variety cautions against reducing every form to a single meaning without historical and contextual examination. Nocturnal travel, the passing night, flowing water, and human nobility each require their own evidence.

1. Meaning in early Arabic

Surā: traveling by night

In early Arabic, al-surā names movement and travel at night. Sarā yasrī means to travel by night, while asrā also belongs to this semantic field.

The word had a concrete, familiar meaning for people who traveled at night for journeys, trade, migration, and desert crossings.

In hot, dry regions, nocturnal travel could offer practical advantages: cooler air, reduced water needs on the journey, and movement during hours when daytime heat would otherwise make progress difficult. These circumstances help explain the word’s setting; surā itself does not mean escaping heat, concealment, or desert travel.

Its direct meaning is movement and travel at night.

Early Arabic dictionaries record a close relationship between saraytu and asraytu in the sense of nocturnal travel. Both can describe a traveler making a journey by night.

Some lexicographers report differences in their temporal scope, such as connecting surā with traveling through the night or most of it. Yet the form alone does not establish that movement necessarily occupies the entire night. Its precise duration depends on context and accompanying expressions.

Evidence from early language

A verse attributed to al-Shammākh ibn Ḍirār, a poet who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, reads:

سَرَتْ مِنْ أَعَالِي رَحْجَانَ فَأَصْبَحَتْ
بِفَيْدٍ وَبَاقِي لَيْلِهَا مَا تَحَسَّرَا

sarat min aʿālī Raḥjāna fa-aṣbaḥat / bi-Faydin wa-bāqī laylihā mā taḥassarā

“She traveled by night from the heights of Raḥjān and was at Fayd at morning, while the remaining night had not yet lifted.”

The verse describes nocturnal movement and arrival at Fayd as morning approached, while the remaining darkness had not yet lifted. Sarat, “she traveled by night,” and fa-aṣbaḥat, “she came to be at morning,” connect the journey with the transition toward dawn.

This witness helps establish the verb’s early use. Since al-Shammākh lived across both periods, however, the verse cannot be assigned conclusively to pre-Islamic times without dating its composition.

Night journeys and arrival at dawn recur in Arabic poetry. Nevertheless, poems mentioning only darkness, travel, or desert crossings are not direct witnesses to this root’s meaning unless they actually contain it.

Sariyy: a small stream

Sariyy is an Arabic name for a stream or watercourse carrying flowing water.

The Muʿallaqah attributed to Labīd ibn Rabīʿah, who lived in pre-Islamic times and subsequently became Muslim, includes:

فَتَوَسَّطَا عَرْضَ السَّرِيِّ وَصَدَّعَا
مَسْجُورَةً مُتَجَاوِرًا قُلَّامُهَا

fa-tawassaṭā ʿarḍa al-sariyyi wa-ṣaddaʿā / masjūratan mutajāwiran qullāmuhā

“The two entered the side of the stream and parted a full pool whose surrounding plants grew close together.”

Here the poet describes two animals moving toward water. Al-sariyy names the stream or watercourse; the pool and surrounding vegetation clarify its meaning.

This is significant evidence because it attests sariyy as “stream” in early Arabic poetry independently of interpreting the verse in Maryam.

Labīd’s Muʿallaqah is attributed to the pre-Islamic period, although variants of some words have been transmitted. It therefore offers strong evidence for the antiquity of sariyy as a watercourse, without establishing the exact date when the word originated.

The stream’s name might be associated with moving, flowing water. This semantic explanation alone, however, does not prove that it historically developed from the verb for nocturnal travel. Water flows by day and night; nocturnality is not part of sariyy’s meaning.

Sariyy: a noble or distinguished person

Lexical sources also record sariyy as a noble, distinguished person of honorable character.

This meaning has a clear lexical association with S R W, the family concerning nobility, greatness, and elevation. Al-sarāt likewise denotes the leading or distinguished members of a community.

The spoken form sariyy can thus belong to two different semantic settings: a flowing stream or a noble person.

Their identical form does not justify deriving human nobility from nocturnal movement or flowing water. Early lexicographers recognized different semantic branches among words containing sīn, rā, and a weak final radical; these do not necessarily admit a single shared definition.

This distinction is especially relevant to sariyyan in Maryam 19:24.

2. Qur’anic forms and derivatives

Under the usual morphological classification, S R Y occurs eight times in eight Qur’anic verses, in three principal forms.

Sarā yasrī: the basic triliteral verb, meaning to travel by night or to pass and move onward. The form yasri occurs once, in Al-Fajr 89:4.

Asrā yusrī: the Form IV verb, meaning to travel by night or to take someone along on a nocturnal journey. In the base reading used here, it occurs six times: once as the past-tense asrā and five times as the imperative asri or fa-asri.

Sariyy: a noun meaning a stream, and, in another lexical branch, an adjective meaning noble or distinguished. Sariyyan occurs once, in Maryam 19:24.

Sarā is intransitive, while asrā does not necessarily mean taking someone by force. The construction asrā bi-fulānin concerns taking a person or group along or setting them in motion.

Qur’anic verbs from this family are often accompanied by bi-, as in “أَسْرِ بِأَهْلِكَ” and “أَسْرِ بِعِبَادِي.”

The preposition belongs to the verbal construction and identifies those taken along. Its presence alone does not specify a vehicle, a mode of transport, or how much choice the companions had.

3. Lot’s night departure with his family

Hūd 11:81 states:

قَالُوا يَا لُوطُ إِنَّا رُسُلُ رَبِّكَ لَنْ يَصِلُوا إِلَيْكَ فَأَسْرِ بِأَهْلِكَ بِقِطْعٍ مِنَ اللَّيْلِ وَلَا يَلْتَفِتْ مِنْكُمْ أَحَدٌ إِلَّا امْرَأَتَكَ إِنَّهُ مُصِيبُهَا مَا أَصَابَهُمْ إِنَّ مَوْعِدَهُمُ الصُّبْحُ أَلَيْسَ الصُّبْحُ بِقَرِيبٍ

qālū yā Lūṭu innā rusulu rabbika lan yaṣilū ilayka fa-asri bi-ahlika bi-qiṭʿin mina al-layli wa-lā yaltafit minkum aḥadun illā imraʾataka innahu muṣībuhā mā aṣābahum inna mawʿidahumu al-ṣubḥu a-laysa al-ṣubḥu bi-qarībin

Hūd 11:81

“They said: Lot, we are messengers of your Lord; they will never reach you. Take your family along during a part of the night, except your wife, and let none of you turn back. What strikes them will strike her. Their appointed time is morning; is morning not near?”

The command fa-asri bi-ahlika concerns departure and travel by night with the family. The translation above follows the interpretation that excepts Lot’s wife from those to be taken away; the placement of the exception has also been discussed in interpretation and recitation studies.

Bi-qiṭʿin mina al-layl places the departure within part of the night. This qualification helps show that asrā does not itself mean traveling for the whole night.

Here nocturnal movement is associated with approaching danger, leaving a threatening environment, and escaping the consequences of the people’s conduct. Escape from danger is nevertheless supplied by the circumstances, not by the lexical definition of asrā.

Al-Ḥijr 15:65 also states:

فَأَسْرِ بِأَهْلِكَ بِقِطْعٍ مِنَ اللَّيْلِ وَاتَّبِعْ أَدْبَارَهُمْ وَلَا يَلْتَفِتْ مِنْكُمْ أَحَدٌ وَامْضُوا حَيْثُ تُؤْمَرُونَ

fa-asri bi-ahlika bi-qiṭʿin mina al-layli wa-ittabiʿ adbārahum wa-lā yaltafit minkum aḥadun wa-imḍū ḥaythu tuʾmarūna

Al-Ḥijr 15:65

“Take your family along during a part of the night, follow behind them, let none of you turn back, and proceed where you are commanded.”

The command again concerns night travel, but adds further instructions: following behind the companions, not turning back, and going in the commanded direction.

“وَامْضُوا حَيْثُ تُؤْمَرُونَ” shows that asri does not itself contain a particular destination. Other parts of the command establish the destination and direction.

The prohibition on turning back is likewise a separate instruction for this event, not the lexical meaning of isrāʾ.

These verses show that nocturnal travel may provide escape from danger, while the root is not confined to that situation.

4. Moses’ nocturnal movement with the Children of Israel

Ṭā Hā 20:77 states:

وَلَقَدْ أَوْحَيْنَا إِلَىٰ مُوسَىٰ أَنْ أَسْرِ بِعِبَادِي فَاضْرِبْ لَهُمْ طَرِيقًا فِي الْبَحْرِ يَبَسًا لَا تَخَافُ دَرَكًا وَلَا تَخْشَىٰ

wa-la-qad awḥaynā ilā Mūsā an asri bi-ʿibādī fa-iḍrib lahum ṭarīqan fī al-baḥri yabasan lā takhāfu darakan wa-lā takhshā

Ṭā Hā 20:77

“We indeed revealed to Moses: Take My servants along by night and make a dry path for them through the sea, without fearing pursuit or being afraid.”

Asri bi-ʿibādī commands Moses to take God’s servants along by night. In the reading of Ḥafṣ, lā takhāfu is indicative, describing freedom from fear; it is not a separate jussive prohibition.

The rest of the verse concerns making a dry path through the sea and not fearing pursuit. In this event, nocturnal movement accompanies a collective departure from Pharaoh’s domination and passage through a natural obstacle.

Crossing the sea is not contained within asri itself. That instruction uses another verb, fa-iḍrib.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:52 states:

وَأَوْحَيْنَا إِلَىٰ مُوسَىٰ أَنْ أَسْرِ بِعِبَادِي إِنَّكُمْ مُتَّبَعُونَ

wa-awḥaynā ilā Mūsā an asri bi-ʿibādī innakum muttabaʿūna

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:52

“We revealed to Moses: Take My servants along by night; you will indeed be pursued.”

The context here makes the importance of nocturnal movement clearer: “إِنَّكُمْ مُتَّبَعُونَ,” you will be pursued.

Al-Dukhān 44:23 also states:

فَأَسْرِ بِعِبَادِي لَيْلًا إِنَّكُمْ مُتَّبَعُونَ

fa-asri bi-ʿibādī laylan innakum muttabaʿūna

Al-Dukhān 44:23

“Take My servants along by night; you will indeed be pursued.”

Here laylan, “by night,” explicitly follows the verb.

Laylan alongside fa-asri shows that Qur’anic Arabic can clarify or emphasize time by mentioning night together with a verb already expressing nocturnal movement. The combination should not be dismissed as meaningless repetition.

In all three verses, Moses is instructed to lead a group, not merely to undertake an individual journey.

Asrā itself, however, does not inherently mean liberation from oppression, religious migration, or deliverance from Pharaoh. Those meanings arise from the story and its related verses.

Distinguishing word meaning from context allows both lexical precision and appreciation of the event’s broader significance.

5. The servant’s night journey and the passing night

The night journey in Al-Isrāʾ 17:1

The opening of Al-Isrāʾ states:

سُبْحَانَ الَّذِي أَسْرَىٰ بِعَبْدِهِ لَيْلًا مِنَ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ إِلَى الْمَسْجِدِ الْأَقْصَى الَّذِي بَارَكْنَا حَوْلَهُ لِنُرِيَهُ مِنْ آيَاتِنَا إِنَّهُ هُوَ السَّمِيعُ الْبَصِيرُ

subḥāna alladhī asrā bi-ʿabdihi laylan mina al-masjidi al-ḥarāmi ilā al-masjidi al-aqṣā alladhī bāraknā ḥawlahu li-nuriyahu min āyātinā innahu huwa al-samīʿu al-baṣīru

Al-Isrāʾ 17:1

“Glory to the One who took His servant by night from the Sacred Mosque to the Farthest Mosque, whose surroundings We blessed, to show him some of Our signs. He is indeed the All-Hearing, the All-Seeing.”

Asrā is a past-tense verb with God as its subject. Bi-ʿabdihi identifies the person for whom this journey was brought about.

Unlike the commands to Lot and Moses, this is a report of an act attributed to God.

The verse presents three clear elements: nocturnal movement, its starting point and destination, and its purpose.

“مِنَ الْمَسْجِدِ الْحَرَامِ” gives the starting point, while “إِلَى الْمَسْجِدِ الْأَقْصَى” gives the destination. “لِنُرِيَهُ مِنْ آيَاتِنَا” states the purpose: to show him some of Our signs.

Asrā alone therefore does not contain the mosque’s name, the route, the purpose of seeing signs, or the manner of the journey. Separate phrases supply these details.

Laylan emphasizes the event’s time but does not establish its exact duration. Neither travel throughout the entire night nor precise starting and ending times can be inferred simply from this word.

Different historical interpretations address how the event occurred. A lexical study of S R Y alone cannot determine how the movement happened, what conveyed the traveler, or every detail of the servant’s experience.

In the Qur’anic text, the word expresses a night journey. Its nature and manner require a separate study of the whole verse and related passages.

The expression miʿrāj does not occur in this verse. The meaning of S R Y should not be equated with the full body of later narratives and terminology concerning the ascent.

“By the night as it passes”

Al-Fajr 89:4 states:

وَاللَّيْلِ إِذَا يَسْرِ

wa-al-layli idhā yasri

Al-Fajr 89:4

“And by the night as it passes.”

Yasri is a present-tense verb from sarā yasrī. The final yā is absent from the written form and from the reading of Ḥafṣ used here. Other readings retain it in particular recitational conditions; the omission should not be generalized to every reading or treated as evidence of a jussive caused by idhā.

The expression evokes movement, but its subject is neither a person nor a caravan: it is the night itself.

This use shows that the verb can also express the night’s passage and elapsing.

The surrounding oaths invoke dawn, nights, the even and the odd, and night as it passes. Rendering the phrase as “when the night passes” fits this sequence.

Figuratively, the night’s movement may resemble that of a traveler covering a route. This image should not be turned into a scientific claim about night moving physically as an independent entity.

Yasri here does not mean a person traveling at night. Assigning the verb to night changes its application: night itself undergoes passage.

“Passes” or “elapses” is thus a more natural and precise rendering than “travels by night.”

6. Sariyyan in Mary’s story: a stream or a noble person?

Maryam 19:24 states:

فَنَادَاهَا مِنْ تَحْتِهَا أَلَّا تَحْزَنِي قَدْ جَعَلَ رَبُّكِ تَحْتَكِ سَرِيًّا

fa-nādāhā min taḥtihā allā taḥzanī qad jaʿala rabbuki taḥtaki sariyyan

Maryam 19:24

“Then he called to her from beneath her: Do not grieve; your Lord has placed a stream beneath you.”

This verse is particularly important for studying S R Y because sariyyan has two established Arabic meanings, both proposed in its interpretive history.

First interpretation: a stream

On this interpretation, sariyyan means a stream or small watercourse.

The context provides substantial support. In Maryam 19:23, labor pains bring Mary to the palm trunk, and her distress leads her to wish she had died.

A voice then tells her not to grieve and that her Lord has placed a stream beneath her.

Maryam 19:25 describes shaking the palm trunk and fresh dates falling; Maryam 19:26 commands:

فَكُلِي وَاشْرَبِي وَقَرِّي عَيْنًا

fa-kulī wa-shrabī wa-qarrī ʿaynan

Maryam 19:26

“Eat, drink, and be comforted.”

Connecting “eat” with fresh dates and “drink” with the stream creates a clear contextual relationship. On this reading, Mary receives food and drink in her difficult condition after childbirth.

Labīd’s verse also shows that sariyy as a stream was an actual Arabic usage, not merely an interpretation invented for this verse.

Second interpretation: a noble or distinguished person

Another Arabic meaning of sariyy is a noble, distinguished person of honorable character.

On this basis, “قَدْ جَعَلَ رَبُّكِ تَحْتَكِ سَرِيًّا” may refer to Mary’s child: your Lord has placed a distinguished person beneath or near you.

This interpretation considers Mary’s situation and Jesus’ birth. Her distress is not only physical pain; the story also reveals concern about facing people and their accusations.

News of her child’s standing could therefore be appropriate consolation.

This interpretation nevertheless raises two matters requiring separate consideration.

First, sariyy as a noble person is lexically associated with S R W and nobility. It should not be derived without evidence from flowing water or nocturnal travel.

Second, the command wa-shrabī, “drink,” in Maryam 19:26 connects more directly with a stream. This does not make the noble-person meaning linguistically impossible, but it adds weight to the stream interpretation.

Which meaning fits better?

Both meanings are established in Arabic, but the context favors “stream.”

Independent poetic evidence supports this meaning. Placing sariyyan beneath Mary, together with commands to eat and drink, provides a coherent picture of her needs being met.

The noble-person meaning is lexically defensible, yet its connection with drinking is less direct.

“Stream” is therefore preferable for Forqan’s base translation. The alternative can be discussed as an interpretation to consider, without combining both meanings into one translation.

The caller’s identity in “فَنَادَاهَا مِنْ تَحْتِهَا” must also be distinguished from sariyyan’s meaning. Who speaks is a separate question; neither issue should be settled conclusively solely on the basis of the other.

7. Comparison with related roots

S R Y and S Y R: nocturnal movement and movement generally

Sayr concerns movement and travel, which may occur by day or night.

For example, Al-Naml 27:69 states:

قُلْ سِيرُوا فِي الْأَرْضِ فَانْظُرُوا كَيْفَ كَانَ عَاقِبَةُ الْمُجْرِمِينَ

qul sīrū fī al-arḍi fa-unẓurū kayfa kāna ʿāqibatu al-mujrimīna

Al-Naml 27:69

“Say: Travel through the earth and see how the offenders ended.”

Sīrū is a general command to move and travel through the earth. The verb itself does not specify nighttime.

By contrast, sarā in its familiar application to human movement concerns nocturnal travel.

The principal distinction in these uses is thus that sayr expresses movement broadly, whereas surā identifies a type of movement by its time.

These are not wholly separate kinds of movement. Nocturnal travel remains travel, with a particular temporal feature.

S R Y and J R Y: passage and flow

Jarā concerns flowing and continuous movement. The Qur’an uses it for water, rivers, ships, the sun, and other phenomena.

Sariyy as a stream may have a semantic association with flowing water. This similarity does not make sarā and jarā one root or two forms of the same word.

Jarā concerns flow and movement without inherently specifying nighttime.

Sarā as a verb applied to travelers expresses nocturnal movement; sariyy as a noun names a stream.

An overlap in movement is insufficient to identify the two roots.

S R Y and S R R: nocturnal travel and concealment

S R R concerns concealment, hiddenness, and other meanings such as joy.

Since night journeys often occur in darkness, there can be a practical connection between nocturnal movement and remaining unseen. Lot’s and Moses’ night departures, for example, are associated with leaving danger and pursuit behind.

Concealment, however, is not sarā’s basic meaning.

A person can travel by night without intending to hide. Equally, someone can conceal a statement in daylight without moving at night.

Their formal and semantic proximity does not justify transferring one root’s features to the other.

S R Y and ʾ S R: night travel and captivity

Asrā in Al-Isrāʾ 17:1 belongs to S R Y and concerns nocturnal movement.

Asrā in Al-Anfāl 8:67 and al-asrā in Al-Anfāl 8:70, however, belong to ʾ S R and concern captives and captivity.

Their written forms can resemble one another, while their roots and meanings differ.

This distinction is essential in translation and root classification. Asrā bi-ʿabdihi does not mean taking the servant captive; it describes taking him on a night journey.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

The base equivalents for these verbs should express nocturnal movement naturally and clearly. Choosing between traveling by night, taking someone along by night, and setting someone in motion by night depends on the subject, construction, and context.

Sarā: “traveled by night” or “journeyed at night.”

Asrā bi-ʿabdihi: “took His servant by night.”

Fa-asri bi-ahlika: “take your family along by night.”

An asri bi-ʿibādī: “take My servants along by night.”

Wa-al-layli idhā yasri: “and by the night as it passes.”

Sariyyan in Maryam 19:24: “a stream.”

Without contextual support, asrā should not be replaced with “made them flee,” “escaped secretly,” “transported rapidly,” or “conveyed by an extraordinary vehicle.”

Escape, danger, speed, means of travel, and manner of movement are established by each event’s context, not the root alone.

In Al-Fajr 89:4, “the night travels” might preserve the Arabic image, but “the night passes” is clearer in natural translation.

In Maryam 19:24, “stream” is preferred for the base text. Discussion of “a noble person” belongs in the study’s explanation, rather than a combined rendering such as “a stream and a noble person.”

Conclusion

In its verbal uses, S R Y concerns nocturnal movement and passage. Sarā and asrā are established Arabic verbs for traveling at night, and the Qur’an employs them for Lot, Moses and the Children of Israel, and the servant’s night journey.

In Al-Fajr 89:4, the verb describes the passing night. Night itself, rather than a human traveler, is the subject. This illustrates Arabic’s capacity to portray time passing without establishing a physical theory of night’s movement.

Sariyy as a stream also has independent early Arabic evidence. In Maryam 19:24, it fits particularly well with childbirth and the commands to eat and drink.

By contrast, sariyy as a noble person belongs with the lexical family of nobility and greatness and should not be identified with nocturnal movement without historical evidence.

A single artificial definition cannot conclusively generate every meaning. The final definition must preserve the branches:

In Qur’anic verbal usage, S R Y expresses nocturnal movement and travel; when applied to night, it describes its passage. The noun sariyy is also established as a stream, while the identically pronounced word for a noble person is associated with a different lexical branch.

A significant feature of its Qur’anic use is that nocturnal movement may, depending on context, provide escape from danger, collective departure from oppression, or a journey to witness divine signs. None of these purposes is a fixed part of the word’s meaning; each arises from its verse’s subject and construction.

Maintaining these distinctions preserves early meanings and makes the Qur’an’s varied uses intelligible without imposing one theory on all of them.
