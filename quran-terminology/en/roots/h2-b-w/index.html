Habāʾ: fine dust and the rising and scattering of particles of earth

1. Meaning in early Arabic

The Arabic root hā–bāʾ–wāw concerns fine dust that rises from the ground, disperses in the air, or settles on surfaces. Its principal characteristics are the smallness and lightness of its particles, which wind or moving objects can readily lift and scatter.

Within this family, هَباء denotes very fine particles of earth and dust. A familiar example is the motes visible in a room when sunlight enters through an opening. The particles already exist before the light reaches them; illumination makes their movement and dispersion more visible.

Arabic habāʾ is not restricted to dust visible in sunlight. Dust rising from the ground, fine particles dispersed by wind, and dust raised by horses or other moving bodies belong to the same semantic field.

To understand the background of this usage, early poetic witnesses must be distinguished from accounts in dictionaries compiled centuries later. Habāʾ and related forms are recorded in early linguistic sources and attested in old poetry, but each verse’s date and attribution require separate assessment.

An early Arabic poetic witness

A verse attributed to ʿAmr ibn Aḥmar al-Bāhilī, a poet whose life spanned the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, describes the wind:

لَهَا مُنْخُلٌ تَذْرِي إِذَا عَصَفَتْ بِهِ

lahā munkhulun tadhrī idhā ʿaṣafat bihi

أَهَابِيَّ سَفْسَافٍ مِنَ التُّرْبِ تَوْأَمِ

ahābiyya safsāfin mina al-turbi tawʾami

“It is as though the wind has a sieve through which, when it blows, it scatters fine dust.”

The poet likens the wind to someone separating and scattering fine particles with a sieve. Ahābī here concerns fine dust, while safsāf emphasizes its small, fine particles.

The witness illustrates this word family describing a tangible natural phenomenon: earth fine enough for wind to lift and scatter.

Some lexical transmissions attribute the verse to Aws ibn Ḥajar, while others, including al-Qālī’s account, attribute it to Ibn Aḥmar. Since Ibn Aḥmar lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods and the verse’s precise date is unknown, it cannot securely establish pre-Islamic usage. Alongside the lexical accounts and the Quran, however, it illustrates the family’s early semantic field.

A defensible definition of this branch in early Arabic is therefore:

Very fine, soft dust, especially particles of earth that rise and disperse in the air.

2. Forms, derivatives, and semantic core

Arabic linguistic sources record the following forms:

  • هَبَا يَهْبُو: of dust, to rise and become visible in the air.
  • هَبْو and هُبُوّ: verbal nouns associated with dust rising.
  • هَباء: fine dust and small scattered particles.
  • هَبْوَة: dust and the obscurity it produces.
  • هَابٍ: in descriptions of earth, fine dust or dust that has risen.
  • أَهْبَى: to raise or stir up dust.
  • إِهْباء and أَهَابِي: forms recorded in descriptions of raised dust.

These forms are recorded in Arabic, but only هَباءً occurs in the Quran.

The semantic core

Three characteristics stand out in uses involving earth and dust:

Fineness: habāʾ denotes dust with very small particles, distinguishing it from large clods, pebbles, and sand.

Rising: dust can rise from the ground or other surfaces and become visible in the air. In this use, هَبَا يَهْبُو concerns dust rising.

Dispersion: fine dust readily spreads through the air. This characteristic matters in the Quranic images too, but مَنْثُورًا and مُنْبَثًّا independently express scattering.

The entire meaning of هَباءً مَنْثُورًا should therefore not be assigned to habāʾ alone. Habāʾ identifies the material, while its accompanying adjective specifies the state of dispersion.

Other Arabic uses

Lexicons also record هَبَا الرَّمَادُ: ash mixing with earth and becoming extinguished or still; related accounts describe fire having become ash.

The soft, earth-like state of the material connects this use with the field of dust. Nevertheless, Quranic habāʾ does not specifically mean ash produced by burning.

Some lexical accounts also record senses such as fleeing, dying, or walking slowly for habā. Without independent evidence, these restricted uses cannot all be derived from one image of dust.

The Quranic sense belongs to the clear, familiar branch concerning fine dust, not to every disparate meaning collected under the dictionary entry.

3. Quranic occurrences

The root occurs twice in the Quran, both times as هَباءً:

  • Al-Furqān 25:23: هَباءً مَنْثُورًا.
  • Al-Wāqiʿah 56:6: هَباءً مُنْبَثًّا.

One occurrence describes the fate of mountains, while the other depicts the fate of human deeds.

Both rely on the same lexical meaning, but one concerns transformation of the physical world and the other portrays judgment of deeds.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:5: mountains crushed

وَبُسَّتِ الْجِبَالُ بَسًّا

wa-bussati al-jibālu bassā

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:5

“And the mountains are thoroughly crushed.”

This verse prepares for habāʾ in the next verse. The mountains lose their stability and structure in that momentous event.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:6: mountains becoming dust

فَكَانَتْ هَبَاءً مُنْبَثًّا

fa-kānat habāʾan munbaththā

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:6

“And become dispersed dust.”

The implicit subject of فَكَانَتْ refers to the mountains in the preceding verse. Although “mountains” is plural, a feminine singular verb is normal Arabic agreement with a nonhuman plural.

Habāʾ describes the mountains’ new state: those immense, solid bodies become very fine particles of dust.

Munbaththan belongs to bāʾ–thāʾ–thāʾ and expresses the particles’ dispersion.

The image presents two transformations: the loss of the mountains’ solid, integrated structure through crushing, and the dispersal of their material as fine dust.

What was once a large, stable mountain no longer retains its former structure. Instead of remaining joined in one body, its particles are described as dispersed.

The physical image is clear. Yet habāʾ alone cannot determine exact particle sizes, physical composition, or a scientific mechanism for the mountains’ transformation. The passage speaks of crushing and dispersion without explaining the event’s detailed mechanics.

Nor should habāʾ here be equated directly with the absolute annihilation of matter. Dust is itself real material. The image depicts the loss of the mountains’ former structure and their transformation into dispersed particles.

Al-Furqān 25:23: deeds becoming scattered dust

وَقَدِمْنَا إِلَىٰ مَا عَمِلُوا مِنْ عَمَلٍ فَجَعَلْنَاهُ هَبَاءً مَنْثُورًا

wa-qadimnā ilā mā ʿamilū min ʿamalin fa-jaʿalnāhu habāʾan manthūrā

Al-Furqān 25:23

“We turn to whatever deeds they have done and make them scattered dust.”

The subject is now human deeds and their outcome in divine judgment, rather than mountains or another natural phenomenon.

Habāʾ retains its meaning of fine dust, but now functions in a figurative image.

People may do many things during their lives, labor over them, take pride in them, and imagine that they have accumulated a valuable asset. When their deeds are assessed before The God, however, what they considered valuable may not yield the outcome they expected.

The Quran depicts this condition through fine, scattered dust: something one cannot hold as a stable, reliable possession.

The object pronoun in فَجَعَلْنَاهُ refers to the deeds under discussion, not to the people themselves. The image concerns the deeds’ fate and worth.

Manthūran comes from nūn–thāʾ–rāʾ and means scattered or strewn. It completes the dust image: dispersed particles that no longer form a stable whole within a person’s grasp.

Habāʾ therefore does not by itself mean “invalid deed.” Its lexical meaning remains fine dust; the deeds’ lack of benefit emerges from the sentence and the context of judgment.

Connections with other verses

In Ibrāhīm 14:18, the deeds of those who reject their Lord are likened to ash scattered by a strong wind on a stormy day; they cannot secure anything from what they have earned.

This image clearly resembles Al-Furqān 25:23, but the words differ. Ibrāhīm 14:18 uses ramād, “ash,” whereas Al-Furqān 25:23 uses habāʾ, “fine dust.”

In Al-Nūr 24:39, the deeds of disbelievers are likewise compared to a mirage that a thirsty person mistakes for water, only to find nothing upon reaching it.

These passages use different images to illuminate a truth about judging deeds: their final worth and outcome need not match their appearance or their doer’s assessment of them.

This conclusion should not be turned into an unrestricted claim that every human effort, or every good deed by someone of a different belief, is worthless. Judgment of individuals and their deeds involves the Quran’s broader criteria concerning faith, intention, action, knowledge, choice, and responsibility. Al-Furqān 25:23 must be read within its particular context, rather than as an unqualified verdict on all people.

4. Comparison with related words

Habāʾ and ghāʾ–bāʾ–rāʾ (ghabarah)

Both families concern earth and dust in some uses, but their predominant images differ.

Habāʾ chiefly denotes fine dust particles capable of rising and dispersing.

Ghabarah also means dust. For example, ʿAbasa 80:40 describes faces covered with dust.

In that use, dust rests on faces, whereas habāʾ in the passages examined here accompanies dispersion.

This distinction is not an absolute rule assigning all airborne dust to habāʾ and all settled dust to ghabarah. Their usage overlaps.

Habāʾ and rāʾ–mīm–dāl (ramād)

Ramād is ash left by burning. Habāʾ denotes fine dust particles and need not result from combustion.

Ibrāhīm 14:18 exposes ash to a powerful wind. In Al-Furqān 25:23, deeds become scattered dust.

Both images can express people’s inability to retain the benefit they expected, but they use different materials.

“Ash” is therefore not an accurate direct rendering of habāʾ in these Quranic verses.

Habāʾ and dhāl–rāʾ–rāʾ (dharrah)

In Quranic uses including Al-Zalzalah 99:7 and Al-Zalzalah 99:8, dharrah conveys an extremely small quantity.

Habāʾ, however, names fine dust; it is not a specific unit of quantity or weight.

The fact that habāʾ consists of tiny particles does not make it identical to dharrah. Nor should the modern scientific concept of an atom be imposed without evidence on either word’s historical meaning.

Nathara and baththa in the two Quranic expressions

Al-Furqān 25:23 uses manthūran: scattered or strewn.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:6 uses munbaththan: dispersed or scattered.

Both express dispersion, but derive from different roots.

Nathr can describe strewing and scattering something. Bathth likewise concerns spreading and dispersion in different directions.

In both passages, the adjective accompanying habāʾ emphasizes scattering. Without evidence, the difference between the adjectives does not establish two wholly distinct kinds of dust or two different physical mechanisms.

5. Challenges in understanding and translation

Does habāʾ mean “nothing” or “nonexistence”?

In contemporary Persian, saying that everything became habāʾ ordinarily means that the result of an effort was lost and came to nothing.

This usage relates to the Quranic image, but its figurative sense should not replace the root’s lexical definition.

Habāʾ originally denotes something real: dust and fine particles of earth.

When the Quran describes mountains becoming habāʾ, the physical image of dust is central. When it describes deeds becoming habāʾ, the same image depicts their fate.

Worthlessness, invalidity, absolute annihilation, and fruitlessness are not the lexical definition of habāʾ itself. Such ideas may emerge from a particular sentence and context.

Is habāʾ only dust visible in sunlight?

Some lexical explanations describe habāʾ as the particles visible in a shaft of sunlight through a window.

This example effectively conveys the particles’ fineness and lightness, but treating it as an exclusive definition would narrow the word’s meaning.

Habāʾ does not come into existence only when sunlight shines. Dust particles exist in darkness too, and wind or moving objects can disperse them.

In this example, light makes the particles visible; it is not an essential part of the word’s meaning.

Is Quranic habāʾ exclusively figurative?

No. Al-Wāqiʿah 56:6 also uses the word to describe the transformation of mountains.

Al-Furqān 25:23 uses dust to depict the fate of deeds, but this figurative application should not erase the word’s physical meaning.

The contrast shows how a word can retain a definite lexical meaning while producing different images and implications in different contexts.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

The principal natural equivalent of habāʾ is “fine dust.”

Because the verses accompany it with adjectives expressing dispersion, “dust” can suffice in the translation, with the fineness of its particles explained in the word note.

In Al-Furqān 25:23:

فَجَعَلْنَاهُ هَبَاءً مَنْثُورًا

fa-jaʿalnāhu habāʾan manthūrā

Al-Furqān 25:23

“And make it scattered dust.”

In Al-Wāqiʿah 56:6:

فَكَانَتْ هَبَاءً مُنْبَثًّا

fa-kānat habāʾan munbaththā

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:6

“And become dispersed dust.”

In each case, “dust” translates habāʾ itself, while “scattered” or “dispersed” renders the accompanying adjective.

“Ash,” “nothing,” “utterly annihilated,” “invalid deed,” or “atomic particles” should not replace the word in the base translation.

For a more explicit description of the physical image, “fine particles of dust” also works; for a fluent Quran translation, “dust” is sufficient.

7. Conclusion

In its main dust-related branch, hā–bāʾ–wāw denotes very fine, soft particles of earth that can rise from the ground, become visible in the air, and disperse.

Early Arabic evidence supports this semantic field, although historical limitations affect assigning some poetic witnesses to the pre-Islamic period.

Only the form habāʾan occurs in the Quran: once for mountains crushed into dispersed dust, and once for deeds becoming like scattered dust in divine judgment.

The lexical meaning remains the same in both occurrences. Their subjects and contexts differ: one describes the transformation of the mountains’ physical bodies, and the other depicts the fate of human deeds.

Final definition for Forqan:

In its dust-related branch, hā–bāʾ–wāw refers to very fine, soft particles of earth that rise and disperse readily. Habāʾ names this dust. In figurative usage, fruitlessness or loss of value emerges from context, rather than from the root itself.

This distinction preserves the word’s physical meaning while allowing the different Quranic images to be understood.
