Softness, humility, and needy entreaty: from the udder and resemblance to humble supplication before The God and the plant ḍarīʿ

1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

The Arabic root Ḍ R ʿ has several familiar uses. The most important concern softness, weakness, humility, expressing need, and seeking help.

When faced with hardship or serious need, a person may abandon pride and obstinacy and humbly ask for help. Arabic can describe this as taḍarruʿ.

However, the family is not limited to human conduct. Ḍarʿ denotes the udder of a milk-producing animal, while muḍāraʿah concerns resemblance between two things. Ḍarīʿ is also the name of an unpleasant plant of little value as food, mentioned in the Qur’an.

Research on this root must distinguish these uses rather than collapse them into one meaning without evidence.

Ḍ R ʿ occurs eight times in eight Qur’anic verses:

Qur’anic formVerseApproximate meaning
يَتَضَرَّعُونَAl-Anʿām 6:42Humbly entreat; turn in humility
تَضَرَّعُواAl-Anʿām 6:43Entreated; showed humility
تَضَرُّعًاAl-Anʿām 6:63With entreaty and humility
تَضَرُّعًاAl-Aʿrāf 7:55With entreaty and humility
يَضَّرَّعُونَAl-Aʿrāf 7:94Humbly entreat
تَضَرُّعًاAl-Aʿrāf 7:205With entreaty and humility
يَتَضَرَّعُونَAl-Muʾminūn 23:76They humbly entreat
ضَرِيعٍAl-Ghāshiyah 88:6An unpleasant plant unsuitable as food

Seven of these occurrences concern humble entreaty and expressed humility. One, ḍarīʿ, names a food in the description of an outcome in the hereafter.

Other important Arabic forms include:

  • ضَرَعَ / يَضْرَعُ: became humble and needy; asked submissively.
  • ضَرِعَ / ضَرُعَ: became weak, lean, or feeble, according to use.
  • ضَرَاعَة: humility, submissiveness, and expressed need.
  • تَضَرَّعَ: entreated and asked with humility.
  • تَضَرُّع: expressed humility and need; needy entreaty and petition.
  • ضَارِع: humble and submissive; in another use, lean and weak.
  • ضَرَع: in one use, a weak or inexperienced person; with different vowel marking, an animal’s udder.
  • ضَرْع: the udder of sheep, goats, cattle, or other milk-producing livestock.
  • ضُرُوع: plural of ḍarʿ; udders.
  • أَضْرَعَتِ النَّاقَةُ: the she-camel’s udder filled or its milk came down near parturition.
  • ضَرِيع / ضَرِيعَة: describing certain animals, having a large udder.
  • مُضَارَعَة: likeness and resemblance.
  • ضَارَعَ: resembled or was similar to something.
  • مُضَارِع: resembling; in grammar, the term for the imperfect verb.
  • ضَرِيع: a plant commonly associated in early sources with unsuitable fodder and harmful thorns.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an. Its main focus is humble entreaty, while ḍarīʿ is a distinct use requiring separate examination.

2. The semantic core in early Arabic

Ibn Fāris’s analysis: softness and flexibility

Ibn Fāris introduces the root’s core in Maqāyīs al-Lughah as follows:

الضَّادُ وَالرَّاءُ وَالْعَيْنُ أَصْلٌ صَحِيحٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى لِينٍ فِي الشَّيْءِ

al-ḍādu wa-al-rāʾu wa-al-ʿaynu aṣlun ṣaḥīḥun yadullu ʿalā līnin fī al-shayʾi

“Ḍād, rāʾ, and ʿayn form a sound root indicating softness in something.”

He then relates ḍaraʿa al-rajulu ḍarāʿatan to a person becoming humble and submissive, and also mentions the animal’s udder.

This analysis connects physical softness with a softening of human conduct in an understandable way.

Language may describe someone who abandons obstinacy and pride before others as gentle or humble.

The udder also has soft tissue and belongs within this semantic field.

Yet this semantic connection must not become a definite historical claim that every derivative first developed from the name of the udder, or originally meant the softness of a physical substance.

Ibn Fāris offers a lexical analysis; establishing the precise historical development requires broader evidence.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: softness, the udder, and resemblance

Softness, weakness, and humility

Softness and weakness may approach one another in some Arabic uses, but are not identical.

An object can be soft without being weak.

A person can likewise be humble in conduct while remaining physically, intellectually, or morally strong.

Conversely, a weak person may remain arrogant and refuse to accept the truth.

The proposed core of līn, softness, therefore does not make taḍarruʿ invariably a sign of powerlessness or a lack of will.

In human uses, ḍarāʿah can concern abandoning obstinacy and revealing need.

Other uses, however, apply ḍāriʿ to someone lean, feeble, inexperienced, or dependent.

Each form’s meaning must therefore be understood through its structure and sentence context.

Taḍarruʿ: expressing need and asking humbly

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

وَتَضَرَّعَ إِلَى اللَّهِ أَيِ ابْتَهَلَ

wa-taḍarraʿa ilā allāhi ayi ibtahala

“He humbly supplicated The God; that is, he turned in entreaty and petition.”

Early sources associate taḍarruʿ with submissiveness, reverent humility, expressed need, and seeking help.

Entreaty does not necessarily mean crying.

Someone may petition with tears and lamentation, but may also express need and helplessness quietly or inwardly.

Taḍarruʿ is therefore not limited to a particular kind of sound, crying, or bodily movement.

What stands out is abandoning a posture of self-sufficiency and turning with humility and need.

The Qur’anic use of humble supplication before The God must also remain distinct from one person humiliating another.

A person may acknowledge their limits and need for the Lord without denying their own personality and human dignity.

3. Ḍarʿ: the udder and the use of milk

The bodily meaning of ḍarʿ

Arabic ḍarʿ denotes the milk-producing organ of livestock.

Lisān al-ʿArab records the word for sheep, camels, and other milk-producing livestock.

Its plural is ḍurūʿ.

Milk is produced in the udder and passes out through particular channels.

In pastoral life, this organ concerns feeding the young animal and human use of milk.

However, the organ’s name differs from the name of the substance it produces.

Ḍarʿ denotes the udder; laban names milk.

Ḍarʿ should therefore not be translated directly as “milk.”

Aḍraʿati al-nāqah: the udder filling near parturition

Arabic records:

أَضْرَعَتِ النَّاقَةُ

aḍraʿati al-nāqatu

Meaning that the she-camel’s udder filled or became ready to produce milk near parturition.

This concerns changes in the animal’s body as birth approaches.

Some sources record a similar form for sheep.

The verb itself, however, does not mean giving birth.

The udder may change and milk become available before the young animal is born.

Arabic also uses shātun ḍarīʿ for a ewe with a large udder.

This should not be confused with ḍarīʿ naming the plant in Al-Ghāshiyah 88:6, despite the identical written form.

Early lexicons: the udder, its plural, and approaching parturition

Is taḍarruʿ derived from the udder?

Al-Rāghib’s Al-Mufradāt mentions ḍaraʿa used of a lamb or kid taking its mother’s udder.

It then suggests that human ḍarāʿah may have developed from this condition of need and dependence.

The image is understandable: the newborn needs its mother for nourishment, and a person in need turns to someone for help.

Yet a semantic image must be distinguished from proof of historical derivation.

The image of suckling or an udder need not be present in every use of taḍarruʿ.

In the Qur’an, taḍarruʿ directly concerns humility, petition, and turning away from obstinacy.

It is therefore better to record both the organ’s name and the expression of need without forcing either to be the meaning of the other.

4. Poetry and human uses in early Arabic

Physical weakness and inexperience

In a well-known verse recorded in lexical sources, a poet says:

أَنَاةً وَحِلْمًا وَانْتِظَارًا بِهِمْ غَدًا
فَمَا أَنَا بِالْوَانِي وَلَا الضَّرَعِ الْغُمْرِ

anātan wa-ḥilman wa-intiẓāran bihim ghadan / fa-mā anā bi-al-wānī wa-lā al-ḍaraʿi al-ghumri

An approximate rendering:

“I wait until tomorrow out of patience and self-restraint, not because I am sluggish or weak and inexperienced.”

Here al-ḍaraʿ al-ghumr concerns a weak and inexperienced person.

The poet clarifies that his lack of haste comes from forbearance, rather than powerlessness.

This witness matters for distinguishing weakness from self-restraint.

A capable, informed person may choose not to react hastily to others’ provocation.

Their composure and forbearance do not then mean weakness.

Early sources sometimes attribute this verse to Ibn Waʿlah and, in some versions, to Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd.

The attribution therefore requires caution. The verse clearly uses ḍaraʿ for weakness and inexperience, but the poet’s identity is disputed.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn: attribution to Ṭarafah, alongside Ibn Waʿlah in Maqāyīs

Entreaty before another person

Al-Aʿshā says in a poem about a group taken captive:

سَائِلْ تَمِيمًا بِهِ أَيَّامَ صَفْقَتِهِمْ
لَمَّا أَتَوْهُ أُسَارَى كُلُّهُمْ ضَرَعَا

sāʾil tamīman bihi ayyāma ṣafqatihim / lammā atawhu usārā kulluhum ḍaraʿā

An approximate rendering:

“Ask Tamīm about him and the days of their bargain, when they came to him as captives, all humble and imploring.”

Here ḍaraʿā concerns the captives’ humility and helplessness.

This shows that Arabic did not use ḍarāʿah only for prayer to The God.

Someone may also submit before another person out of intense need or weakness.

This use does not mean that every instance of human entreaty is commendable.

Humility may arise from conscious choice, or from pressure, fear, and humiliation.

The lexical meaning describes submissiveness and need; judging their cause and form ethically is a separate matter.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Al-Aʿshā’s witness concerning captives and humility

Ḍarāʿah and petition

Lexical sources sometimes associate taḍarruʿ with istighāthah.

Istighāthah concerns seeking help in hardship.

Taḍarruʿ can therefore be part of a plea for help.

But not every request is taḍarruʿ.

A person can calmly ask someone for something without feeling helpless.

Someone who asks in entreaty and acknowledges need is in a different state.

Nor does every taḍarruʿ necessarily lead to a particular spoken request.

A person may humbly return to The God and inwardly feel their need without uttering a long sentence.

This distinction matters in verses about remembrance and prayer.

5. Qur’anic uses: hardship and the invitation to humble entreaty

Al-Anʿām 6:42: hardship as an occasion for returning

Al-Anʿām states:

وَلَقَدْ أَرْسَلْنَا إِلَىٰ أُمَمٍ مِنْ قَبْلِكَ فَأَخَذْنَاهُمْ بِالْبَأْسَاءِ وَالضَّرَّاءِ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَتَضَرَّعُونَ

wa-laqad arsalnā ilā umamin min qablika fa-akhadhnāhum bi-al-baʾsāʾi wa-al-ḍarrāʾi laʿallahum yataḍarraʿūna

“We sent to communities before you, then afflicted them with hardship and suffering, so that they might humbly entreat.”

Al-Anʿām 6:42

Yataḍarraʿūna is a plural imperfect verb of Form V here.

It refers to entreaty and humility before The God.

Al-baʾsāʾ and al-ḍarrāʾ specify the situation: hardship and suffering.

However, al-ḍarrāʾ belongs to Ḍ R R, rather than Ḍ R ʿ.

The two families should not be identified merely because they occur together.

Hardship and suffering can make a person aware of the limits of their abilities and resources.

They may then move away from pride and return to the Lord.

Yet hardship is not itself taḍarruʿ.

A person may suffer while continuing to persist in misconduct.

Al-Anʿām 6:43 makes this distinction clear.

Al-Anʿām 6:43: humble entreaty contrasted with hardheartedness

The following verse states:

فَلَوْلَا إِذْ جَاءَهُمْ بَأْسُنَا تَضَرَّعُوا وَلَٰكِنْ قَسَتْ قُلُوبُهُمْ وَزَيَّنَ لَهُمُ الشَّيْطَانُ مَا كَانُوا يَعْمَلُونَ

fa-lawlā idh jāʾahum baʾsunā taḍarraʿū wa-lākin qasat qulūbuhum wa-zayyana lahumu al-shayṭānu mā kānū yaʿmalūna

“Why, when Our adversity came to them, did they not humbly entreat? Instead, their hearts hardened, and Satan made what they were doing attractive to them.”

Al-Anʿām 6:43

Taḍarraʿū is a plural perfect verb here.

Fa-lawlā in this construction is a reproachful question about something that should have happened but did not.

Here the contrast with taḍarruʿ is qasat qulūbuhum: their hearts hardened.

This contrast relates to Ibn Fāris’s analysis in terms of softness.

Taḍarruʿ here accompanies a softening of conduct and the abandonment of obstinacy.

Hardness of heart, by contrast, describes unwillingness to reconsider one’s conduct even after experiencing hardship and receiving a warning.

The verse then describes Satan making their deeds attractive to them.

It is therefore more than a failure to make sounds of entreaty: the verse reports rejecting the warning and continuing misconduct.

Taḍarruʿ should accordingly not be treated as merely a vocal or outward state.

Here it concerns readiness to return, accept a warning, and abandon obstinacy.

These meanings arise from the verse as a whole; they are not all parts of the lexical definition of taḍarruʿ.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:94: yaḍḍarraʿūna

Al-Aʿrāf states:

وَمَا أَرْسَلْنَا فِي قَرْيَةٍ مِنْ نَبِيٍّ إِلَّا أَخَذْنَا أَهْلَهَا بِالْبَأْسَاءِ وَالضَّرَّاءِ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَضَّرَّعُونَ

wa-mā arsalnā fī qaryatin min nabiyyin illā akhadhnā ahlahā bi-al-baʾsāʾi wa-al-ḍarrāʾi laʿallahum yaḍḍarraʿūna

“We sent no prophet to a settlement without afflicting its people with hardship and suffering, so that they might humbly entreat.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:94

This closely resembles Al-Anʿām 6:42 in content.

There is, however, a morphological difference.

Al-Anʿām has:

يَتَضَرَّعُونَ

yataḍarraʿūna

“They humbly entreat.”

Al-Aʿrāf has:

يَضَّرَّعُونَ

yaḍḍarraʿūna

“They humbly entreat.”

The second form also belongs to Form V, but its tāʾ has assimilated into the ḍād.

Yaḍḍarraʿūna therefore has neither a distinct root nor an independent meaning from yataḍarraʿūna.

Both concern humble entreaty.

Here too hardship and suffering are presented as an occasion for people’s attention and return.

The following verse, however, says that after their circumstances changed and ease replaced hardship, some regarded these events simply as vicissitudes that had also befallen their forebears.

The issue is therefore more than experiencing hardship: people’s response and understanding of events also matter.

6. Taḍarruʿ in prayer and remembrance of The God

Al-Anʿām 6:63: prayer in danger

Al-Anʿām states:

قُلْ مَنْ يُنَجِّيكُمْ مِنْ ظُلُمَاتِ الْبَرِّ وَالْبَحْرِ تَدْعُونَهُ تَضَرُّعًا وَخُفْيَةً لَئِنْ أَنْجَانَا مِنْ هَٰذِهِ لَنَكُونَنَّ مِنَ الشَّاكِرِينَ

qul man yunajjīkum min ẓulumāti al-barri wa-al-baḥri tadʿūnahu taḍarruʿan wa-khufyatan la-in anjānā min hādhihi la-nakūnanna mina al-shākirīna

“Say: Who saves you from the darkness of land and sea, as you call Him in humble entreaty and secretly: ‘If He saves us from this, we will surely be among the grateful’?”

Al-Anʿām 6:63

Tadʿūnahu belongs to D ʿ W and concerns calling upon The God.

Taḍarruʿan describes the manner of this calling.

Khufyatan also emphasizes its secrecy.

Prayer and taḍarruʿ are therefore connected but distinct concepts.

Here prayer is calling and seeking help.

Taḍarruʿ describes doing so with humility and need.

Khufyah indicates secrecy.

This construction does not mean that taḍarruʿ must always be loud, since it accompanies secrecy in the same verse.

The verse continues with the promise of gratitude after rescue.

Al-Anʿām 6:64, however, says that The God saves them from distress, yet they subsequently return to associating partners.

This sequence shows that entreaty in danger does not by itself ensure continued commitment to truth once the danger passes.

A person may turn to The God in helplessness but resume their former conduct and orientation when ease arrives.

Sincere entreaty in a moment of need and subsequent fidelity to truth are therefore connected matters that must be considered together.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:55: calling the Lord humbly and secretly

Al-Aʿrāf states:

ادْعُوا رَبَّكُمْ تَضَرُّعًا وَخُفْيَةً إِنَّهُ لَا يُحِبُّ الْمُعْتَدِينَ

udʿū rabbakum taḍarruʿan wa-khufyatan innahu lā yuḥibbu al-muʿtadīna

“Call upon your Lord in humble entreaty and secretly; He does not love those who transgress.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:55

Taḍarruʿ appears here as a quality of calling upon the Lord.

Recognizing their need, a person calls Him and moves away from arrogance.

Khufyah also expresses secrecy.

This combination shows that supplication does not depend on public display, raising one’s voice, or attracting others’ attention.

A person can pray humbly in a quiet voice or in private.

The verse ends by addressing transgression.

This qualification shows that even in petition and worship, one should not exceed proper bounds.

However, Ḍ R ʿ itself does not mean moderation or observing every boundary: that point comes from the verse’s final sentence.

The next verse also states:

وَادْعُوهُ خَوْفًا وَطَمَعًا

wa-udʿūhu khawfan wa-ṭamaʿan

“Call upon Him with fear and hope.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:56

These verses show that calling The God can accompany humble entreaty, secrecy, fear, and hope.

But these words are not synonyms.

Taḍarruʿ describes humility and need, while fear and hope indicate two different directions of human expectation and feeling.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:205: humble entreaty in inward remembrance

Near the end of Al-Aʿrāf, we read:

وَاذْكُرْ رَبَّكَ فِي نَفْسِكَ تَضَرُّعًا وَخِيفَةً وَدُونَ الْجَهْرِ مِنَ الْقَوْلِ بِالْغُدُوِّ وَالْآصَالِ وَلَا تَكُنْ مِنَ الْغَافِلِينَ

wa-udhkur rabbaka fī nafsika taḍarruʿan wa-khīfatan wa-dūna al-jahri mina al-qawli bi-al-ghuduwwi wa-al-āṣāli wa-lā takun mina al-ghāfilīna

“Remember your Lord within yourself, in humble entreaty and fear, without raising your voice, in the mornings and evenings; do not be among the heedless.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:205

This verse is particularly important for understanding taḍarruʿ.

The main verb here is udhkur, remember, rather than udʿu, call.

Taḍarruʿ therefore accompanies more than requests for help and prayer: it also appears in the remembrance of the Lord.

Fī nafsika concerns remembering within oneself.

Taḍarruʿan describes humility and need.

Khīfatan indicates fear.

Dūna al-jahri mina al-qawl also concerns avoiding a raised voice.

This construction makes clear that taḍarruʿ does not depend on shouting or audible lamentation.

A person may remember the Lord inwardly, with humility and attention.

Remembrance and taḍarruʿ are also distinct concepts.

Dhikr concerns remembering; taḍarruʿ describes a state and manner of remembrance.

The verse concludes by warning against heedlessness.

Here taḍarruʿ therefore concerns conscious attention and avoiding heedlessness, beyond merely moving the tongue or repeating words.

Al-Ṭabarī: humility, inward remembrance, and avoiding a raised voice

7. Al-Muʾminūn 23:76: humble entreaty and obstinacy

Al-Muʾminūn states:

وَلَقَدْ أَخَذْنَاهُمْ بِالْعَذَابِ فَمَا اسْتَكَانُوا لِرَبِّهِمْ وَمَا يَتَضَرَّعُونَ

wa-laqad akhadhnāhum bi-al-ʿadhābi fa-mā istakānū li-rabbihim wa-mā yataḍarraʿūna

“We certainly afflicted them with punishment, yet they did not humble themselves before their Lord, nor do they humbly entreat.”

Al-Muʾminūn 23:76

Two expressions appear together here:

فَمَا اسْتَكَانُوا

fa-mā istakānū

“Yet they did not humble themselves.”

وَمَا يَتَضَرَّعُونَ

wa-mā yataḍarraʿūna

“Nor do they humbly entreat.”

This combination shows that istikānah and taḍarruʿ are close in meaning but distinct words.

Istikānah concerns relinquishing obstinacy and acknowledging a higher situation or authority.

Taḍarruʿ emphasizes expressed need and the petitioner’s humility.

The preceding verse also states that if The God showed them mercy and removed their suffering, they would continue in their transgression.

The issue is therefore more than experiencing suffering: it includes persisting in obstinacy during and after it.

One should not conclude that everyone necessarily becomes humble and willing to change as soon as hardship strikes.

Suffering can offer an opportunity to reconsider, but human responses differ.

Al-Anʿām 6:42, Al-Anʿām 6:43, and Al-Aʿrāf 7:94 also show this.

Hardship may provide an occasion for attention, but humble entreaty and returning are the human response.

8. Is taḍarruʿ always a sign of faith and good conduct?

Arabic sources sometimes use taḍarruʿ and ḍarāʿah for submissiveness before wealthy or powerful people.

A person may entreat another to obtain wealth, gain support, or escape danger.

This may lexically be taḍarruʿ, but its moral value requires separate assessment.

Someone may seek help out of genuine need; someone else may be forced into outward humility through fear and humiliation.

A person may also deceive others with humble words while failing to uphold truth and justice in practice.

Taḍarruʿ as an outward state is therefore not by itself evidence of faith, sincerity, or reformed conduct.

In the Qur’anic uses, the intended taḍarruʿ accompanies turning to the Lord and abandoning obstinacy.

Even here, however, momentary entreaty in danger must be distinguished from lasting commitment to truth.

Al-Anʿām 6:63 and Al-Anʿām 6:64 provide a clear example: people call The God in humble entreaty during danger but return to associating partners after rescue.

The presence of entreaty and prayer alone therefore does not establish that a person has changed for the better.

Conversely, humility before The God does not mean humiliating people or denying their dignity.

Acknowledging one’s limits and responsibility toward truth can accompany humility and continued firmness against injustice and wrongdoing.

9. Ḍarīʿ: an unpleasant plant in Al-Ghāshiyah 88:6

Al-Ghāshiyah states:

لَيْسَ لَهُمْ طَعَامٌ إِلَّا مِنْ ضَرِيعٍ
لَا يُسْمِنُ وَلَا يُغْنِي مِنْ جُوعٍ

laysa lahum ṭaʿāmun illā min ḍarīʿin / lā yusminu wa-lā yughnī min jūʿin

“They have no food except ḍarīʿ, which neither nourishes nor relieves hunger.”

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:6 and Al-Ghāshiyah 88:7

Ḍarīʿ in Arabic

Lexical sources name ḍarīʿ as a familiar plant, often associated with thorns, dryness, an unpleasant smell, and unsuitability for grazing.

Lisān al-ʿArab records several explanations.

One view identifies ḍarīʿ as a dry plant associated with shibriq.

Al-Farrāʾ says that the people of the Hijaz call shibriq ḍarīʿ when it dries.

Another account from Ibn al-Aʿrābī relates the word to a kind of ʿawsaj and its stages of drying.

Some sources, however, report a different sequence of names for the plant’s fresh and dry stages.

Without independent historical botanical research, these accounts should not all be collapsed into one precise and undisputed definition.

What is clearer in the lexical evidence is the association of ḍarīʿ with an unpleasant plant unsuitable for nourishment.

A poetic witness for ḍarīʿ

Qays ibn ʿAyzārah al-Hudhalī says of camels kept on poor pasture:

وَحُبِسْنَ فِي هَزْمِ الضَّرِيعِ فَكُلُّهَا
حَدْبَاءُ دَامِيَةُ الْيَدَيْنِ حَرُودُ

wa-ḥubisna fī hazmi al-ḍarīʿi fa-kulluhā / ḥadbāʾu dāmiyatu al-yadayni ḥarūdu

An approximate rendering:

“They were kept among the broken growth of ḍarīʿ, so all became lean, their forelegs bloodied and their milk scant.”

Hazm al-ḍarīʿ here concerns the plant’s broken and crushed parts.

The poet describes the camels’ poor condition.

Bad pasture and difficult circumstances have left these animals thin and yielding little milk.

Ḍarīʿ thus names a real plant in an early Arabic environment, associated with unsuitable fodder and livestock in poor condition.

This matters because it shows that ḍarīʿ was more than an unfamiliar name occurring only in accounts of the hereafter.

The verse cannot, however, establish the plant’s precise scientific species, chemical composition, or every natural characteristic.

Nor does it establish that ḍarīʿ was completely inedible to every animal at every stage of growth. Early accounts differ about how much livestock could use it.

Lisān al-ʿArab: ḍarīʿ, hazm, and Qays ibn ʿAyzārah’s witness

Is the Qur’anic ḍarīʿ the same as the earthly plant?

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:6 and Al-Ghāshiyah 88:7 use ḍarīʿ for food that neither nourishes people nor relieves their hunger.

This clearly relates to the lexical meaning of a plant unsuitable for nourishment.

Yet these verses do not specify its botanical species, exact appearance, growing place, or the composition of the substance in the hereafter.

The word can therefore be said to use the name of an unpleasant plant unsuitable as food; one should not assert that its substance in the hereafter has precisely the same natural structure as a familiar plant from a particular earthly region.

Nor does the plant’s unpleasantness alone justify treating ḍarīʿ here as merely metaphorical, with no actual referent.

The verse itself speaks of food and describes its properties.

Its precise quality and nature lie beyond the lexical information.

Al-Ṭabarī: accounts of ḍarīʿ and shibriq

Ḍarīʿ and the root’s semantic core

Ibn Fāris regards ḍarīʿ as a use that does not fit readily within the proposed core of softness and weakness.

He suggests that its name might relate to the family through its poor nutritional value or inability to fatten animals.

This is a semantic possibility, rather than conclusive proof of the route of derivation.

Arabic also uses shātun ḍarīʿ for a ewe with a large udder.

Yet an identical written form does not establish that the plant’s name derives directly from a large udder.

Root research should therefore keep three uses distinct:

Taḍarruʿ for expressed need and humility; ḍarʿ for an animal’s udder; and ḍarīʿ for a plant with familiar characteristics making it unsuitable as food.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: qualifying the connection of ḍarīʿ with the root’s core

10. Muḍāraʿah: resemblance and the name of the imperfect verb

Resemblance between two things

Arabic muḍāraʿah concerns likeness and resemblance.

One says:

هَذَا يُضَارِعُ هَذَا

hādhā yuḍāriʿu hādhā

Meaning: “This resembles that.”

The subject here is not humility or entreaty.

Two things may resemble each other in structure, size, conduct, or another quality.

Some early sources offer an image to explain the name: the two things are as though they had suckled from one udder and were close for that reason.

Yet this image does not prove a definite historical origin of muḍāraʿah.

The word’s established use is likeness and resemblance.

Why is it called the imperfect verb?

Arabic grammar calls verbs indicating the present or future fiʿl muḍāriʿ, the imperfect verb.

Lexicographers and grammarians explain the name through this verbal structure’s resemblance to nouns in certain grammatical properties.

For example, under specified conditions, the imperfect takes inflection and may have different grammatical endings, as nouns do.

Muḍāriʿ in grammar therefore does not mean submissive or imploring.

The grammatical term draws on resemblance.

Nor should the grammatical term be equated with taḍarruʿ in Qur’anic verses, although both are studied under the consonantal family Ḍ R ʿ.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: resemblance and the imperfect verb’s name

11. Related roots

Ḍ R ʿ (taḍarruʿ) and D ʿ W (duʿāʾ)

Duʿāʾ fundamentally concerns calling and summoning.

In worship, a person calls The God and petitions Him.

Taḍarruʿ describes the humility and need of this turning.

Both concepts occur together in Al-Anʿām 6:63 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:55.

Duʿāʾ and taḍarruʿ are therefore related, but not complete synonyms.

A person can call someone to do something without humbly entreating them.

They may also turn inwardly to The God in humility and need without uttering a loud request.

Study of D ʿ W

Ḍ R ʿ (taḍarruʿ) and KH SH ʿ (khushūʿ)

Khushūʿ concerns humility, becoming subdued, and attentive reverence.

Taḍarruʿ also concerns humility, but its Qur’anic uses often emphasize expressed need and the petitioner’s turning.

Someone may show reverent humility when hearing truthful speech without having a particular request at that moment.

Someone in danger who prays for rescue, by contrast, may turn to The God in humble entreaty.

The two concepts can therefore accompany one another without being identical.

Study of KH SH ʿ

Ḍ R ʿ (taḍarruʿ) and KH Ḍ ʿ (khuḍūʿ)

Khuḍūʿ concerns humility and lowering a defiant posture.

Taḍarruʿ often describes a kind of submissiveness accompanied by need and petition.

Khuḍūʿ, however, may also occur before a law, power, or another person and does not always mean entreaty.

Outward submission may also arise from fear or compulsion.

Its moral quality therefore requires examination of the context.

Study of KH Ḍ ʿ

Ḍ R ʿ (taḍarruʿ) and DH L L (dhull)

Dhull also concerns falling from a position of might or entering a state of abasement and obedience.

Some Arabic dictionaries explain ḍarāʿah through tadhallul.

Yet not every use of humility should be treated as humiliation and the loss of human dignity.

In prayer verses, taḍarruʿ concerns recognizing need and being humble before the Lord.

This meaning does not itself command accepting humiliation at the hands of other people.

Study of DH L L

Ḍ R ʿ (taḍarruʿ) and Ḍ R R (ḍarar)

These roots share their first two consonants, but their third consonants differ.

The Qur’anic use of Ḍ R ʿ concerns humble entreaty and expressed need.

Ḍ R R concerns harm, injury, and suffering.

Al-Anʿām 6:42 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:94 contain both families: al-ḍarrāʾ and yataḍarraʿūna or yaḍḍarraʿūna.

Their proximity shows that suffering can provide an occasion for humble entreaty, but the roots do not mean the same thing.

A person may suffer harm without humbly entreating.

They can also call The God with humility and awareness of need without experiencing a severe calamity.

Study of Ḍ R R

Ḍ R ʿ (muḍāraʿah) and SH B H (shabah)

Shabah concerns likeness and resemblance.

Muḍāraʿah also denotes resemblance between two things in its lexical use.

The families overlap in this respect.

But semantic similarity does not establish identity of roots.

Nor should the grammatical meaning of the imperfect verb be transferred to every form of Ḍ R ʿ.

Ḍ R ʿ (ḍarīʿ) and SH B R Q (shibriq)

Arabic sources name shibriq as a thorny plant.

Many explanations also associate ḍarīʿ with this plant or its dried stage.

Accounts differ, however, about which name applied to the fresh and which to the dry stage.

Ḍarīʿ in Al-Ghāshiyah 88:6 should therefore not be treated, without qualification or explanation, as simply the name of one scientifically established species.

It is more appropriate to preserve the original name and explain its familiar characteristics lexically.

ضَرِعَ / ضَرُعَ: became weak or lean, according to use.

ضَرَاعَة: humility, submissiveness, and expressed need.

تَضَرَّعَ: entreated; turned with humility and need.

ضُرُوع: udders.

أَضْرَعَتِ النَّاقَةُ: the she-camel’s udder filled or became ready to produce milk near parturition.

مُضَارَعَة: likeness and resemblance.

مُضَارِع: resembling; in grammar, the imperfect verb.

“They have no food except ḍarīʿ.”

For the taḍarruʿ uses, Persian zārī, entreaty, is a concise and defensible equivalent.

However, present-day readers may restrict zārī to crying and audible lamentation. The word explanation should therefore clarify that taḍarruʿ also concerns humility, expressed need, and the petitioner’s turning.

For ḍarīʿ, it is better to retain the plant’s name in translation and discuss an unpleasant plant unsuitable as food in the lexical explanation.

Renderings such as “a particular poisonous plant,” “fiery thorns,” or “dried shibriq” add details that cannot be established conclusively from the Qur’anic wording itself.

13. Conclusion

Arabic Ḍ R ʿ concerns softness, weakness, humility, and expressed need.

Ibn Fāris identifies softness as its semantic core. This allows a semantic connection between a softening of human conduct and the soft organ that produces milk.

Yet the connection should not become a claim that every derivative developed from one fixed physical image.

In human uses, taḍarruʿ concerns abandoning obstinacy and turning with humility and need.

In bodily uses, ḍarʿ names the udder.

In the field of resemblance, muḍāraʿah denotes likeness, and the grammatical name of the imperfect verb also comes from this field.

Ḍarīʿ names a plant associated in early sources with unpleasant characteristics and little nutritional value. Its precise derivational relationship with softness and weakness is uncertain.

Seven Qur’anic occurrences in this family concern humble entreaty.

Al-Anʿām 6:42, Al-Anʿām 6:43, and Al-Aʿrāf 7:94 present hardship and suffering in the context of inviting people to humility and reconsideration.

In Al-Anʿām 6:63 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:55, taḍarruʿ accompanies calling upon the Lord.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:205, it accompanies inward remembrance of The God.

Al-Muʾminūn 23:76 places the absence of taḍarruʿ beside the absence of humility and continued obstinacy.

Al-Ghāshiyah 88:6 uses ḍarīʿ for a food that the following verse describes as failing to nourish or relieve hunger.

These uses show that taḍarruʿ is more than shouting, crying, or lamentation. A person can express humility and need through audible entreaty or quiet inward attention.

Nor does hardship alone reform a person, who may remain obstinate in the face of warnings.

Brief root meaning for Forqan:

Softness, humility, and expressed need; in human uses, entreaty and humble turning. Other uses concern the udder, resemblance, and the unpleasant plant called ḍarīʿ.

Meaning in the taḍarruʿ verses:

Taḍarruʿ: entreaty and needy humility; abandoning obstinacy and turning with a sense of need.

Meaning in Al-Ghāshiyah 88:6:

Ḍarīʿin: ḍarīʿ, the name of an unpleasant plant presented in context as food that does not relieve hunger.
