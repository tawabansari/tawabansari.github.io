Perception and awareness, seeking signs, a faint sound, and destruction

1. Introducing the root and its semantic range

The triliteral Arabic root ح س س has several semantic branches. Its best-known branch concerns sensing, perceiving, and becoming aware of something’s presence or condition. Within this family, أَحَسَّ expresses perceiving or noticing, تَحَسَّسَ seeking and obtaining news, and حِسّ and حَسِيس a sound or perceptible sign.

This does not exhaust the root’s range. In early Arabic, حَسَّ also means killing, eliminating, or destroying. This meaning is clear in the Qur’an in Āl ʿImrān 3:152.

The root ح س س should therefore not be defined solely as feeling. Nor should an artificial abstract meaning be imposed on all its uses in a way that erases their real differences.

The root occurs six times in the Qur’an, in four morphological categories: form I, form IV, form V, and the noun حَسِيس.

  • تَحُسُّونَهُمْ: from the form I verb حَسَّ, killing or destroying enemies; Āl ʿImrān 3:152.
  • أَحَسَّ، أَحَسُّوا، تُحِسُّ: form IV, perceiving, noticing, or sensing a presence; Āl ʿImrān 3:52, Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:12, and Maryam 19:98.
  • تَحَسَّسُوا: form V, searching and seeking news or signs; Yūsuf 12:87.
  • حَسِيسَهَا: the noun حَسِيس with an attached pronoun, its sound; Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:102.

These four morphological categories belong to one root family, but do not mean the same thing in every verse.

2. Semantic branches in early Arabic

Sensing and perceiving

In Arabic, حَسَّ بِالشَّيْءِ and أَحَسَّ بِهِ express perceiving or becoming aware of something.

Lisān al-ʿArab explains this use with شَعَرَ بِهِ: someone notices something’s presence or condition.

This perception may occur through sight, hearing, touch, or other signs that make someone aware of a reality.

For example, أَحْسَسْتُ بِالشَّيْءِ means that I perceived something’s presence or effect. Whether this happened through sight, hearing, touch, or recognizing signs depends on context.

Lexical explanations sometimes also relate أَحَسَّ to عَلِمَ and وَجَدَ: knowing or realizing that something exists.

Nevertheless, perception and knowledge are not identical in every case. Someone may perceive a sign and then infer something’s existence or condition. Sometimes direct perception and the resulting recognition are so closely connected that one verb expresses both.

In Arabic, الحَوَاسّ also names human sensory faculties, such as sight, hearing, smell, taste, and touch. This use directly concerns perception through the senses.

The plural حواس does not occur in the Qur’an, however. The classification of five senses should not be treated as a complete definition of all the root’s Qur’anic uses.

Taḥassus: seeking news and signs

The form V verb تَحَسَّسَ is used lexically for searching for something and trying to obtain news of it.

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

«وَتَحَسَّسَ الْخَبَرَ: تَطَلَّبَهُ وَتَبَحَّثَهُ»

wa-taḥassasa al-khabara: taṭallabahu wa-tabaḥḥathahu

That is: “He sought the news and investigated it.”

Lisān al-ʿArab, Ḥ S S: taḥassus and seeking news

The difference between أَحَسَّ and تَحَسَّسَ is clear in this usage.

أَحَسَّ can express perceiving or noticing something, whereas تَحَسَّسَ emphasizes seeking and making an effort to obtain news or signs of it.

Someone might suddenly notice another person’s presence. أَحَسَّ can describe that situation.

When that person asks others about someone missing, examines clues, and seeks news, تَحَسَّسَ is more appropriate.

This difference should not become an absolute rule. Depending on construction and context, تَحَسَّسَ may approach searching, examining, or obtaining news.

Ḥiss and ḥasīs: a sound or audible sign

حِسّ and حَسِيس are also used for sound, particularly a faint sound indicating something’s presence, movement, or activity.

Lisān al-ʿArab explains الحِسّ and الحَسِيس as الصَّوْتُ الْخَفِيّ: a quiet or faint sound.

Someone may not see a nearby movement, but hear its sound and become aware of a presence.

This use relates to perception and awareness because sound is one way of detecting signs of something’s existence or movement.

In such expressions, however, حَسِيس names the sound itself, rather than the act of hearing or becoming aware. Translation should preserve the difference between what is heard and the hearer’s action.

Ḥassa: killing and destroying

Another branch of this family in early Arabic concerns killing, destroying, and eliminating.

حَسَّ الْعَدُوَّ means killing or destroying the enemy.

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

«الْحَسُّ: الْقَتْلُ الذَّرِيعُ»

al-ḥassu: al-qatlu al-dharīʿu

That is: “Ḥass means severe, extensive killing.”

حَسَّ الْبَرْدُ النَّبَاتَ is also used of cold that destroys vegetation.

Some Arabic uses of this branch concern removing or clearing something away, such as brushing dust from an animal’s body.

A possible common feature is removal or disappearance, but this does not establish that all these uses developed from one original meaning.

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris gives this family two broad bases: overcoming something through killing or otherwise, and an utterance of pain or distress.

He also connects some uses of perception and awareness with the first basis. This represents a lexicographer’s effort to relate the meanings, rather than conclusive evidence of the root’s historical development.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: the two bases of Ḥ S S

In Al-Mufradāt, Al-Rāghib al-Isfahānī offers another explanation, relating killing to damage to sensation or the senses.

These analyses are not necessarily identical. Lexical research should treat the recorded semantic branches as firmer evidence than theories about their common origin.

Al-Rāghib, Al-Mufradāt: perception and injury to a sensory faculty

Pain, reaction, and sympathy

حَسِّ is also recorded as an exclamation at sudden pain or injury, such as touching something hot.

Within this family, حَسِسْتُ لَهُ also expresses tenderness or compassion toward someone.

Here, a person is affected by someone else’s suffering and feels sympathy.

This has an understandable connection with perceiving pain and emotional responsiveness, but every Qur’anic use of ح س س should not be restricted to suffering, compassion, or emotion.

For example, أَحَسَّ عِيسَىٰ مِنْهُمُ الْكُفْرَ concerns Jesus perceiving disbelief, rather than simply feeling emotionally hurt by their conduct.

3. Evidence from early Arabic poetry and usage

A witness attributed to Aws ibn Ḥajar

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Aws ibn Ḥajar contains:

فَمَا جَبُنُوا أَنَّا نَسُدُّ عَلَيْهِمْ
وَلَكِنْ لَقُوا نَارًا تَحُسُّ وَتَسْفَعُ

fa-mā jabunū annā nasuddu ʿalayhim / wa-lākin laqū nāran taḥussu wa-tasfaʿu

Explanatory translation following the commentary on نَسُدُّ: “They did not draw back through fear of an equal fight; rather, they encountered a fire that destroyed and scorched.”

Lisān al-ʿArab, S D D: commentary on fair combat in Aws’s witness

Here, تَحُسُّ accompanies fire and means destroying or consuming.

This witness matters because it shows that the destructive use of حَسَّ was not confined to describing the killing of people.

The poet uses fire to describe destructive force in battle. تَحُسُّ beside تَسْفَعُ emphasizes severe harm and destruction.

The line is transmitted in Aws ibn Ḥajar’s Dīwān, as evidence attributed to pre-Islamic poetry. Under سدد, Lisān al-ʿArab reports an explanation of نَسُدُّ as meeting an opponent fairly in combat; under حسس it also transmits نَشُدُّ. Thus “we had blocked their way” is not a secure translation of the reading نَسُدُّ.

Aws ibn Ḥajar’s Dīwān: the readings nasuddu and taḥussu

A witness attributed to Al-Afwah al-Awdī

Poetry attributed to Al-Afwah al-Awdī also contains:

نَفْسِي لَهُمْ عِنْدَ انْكِسَارِ الْقَنَا
وَقَدْ تَرَدَّى كُلُّ قِرْنٍ حَسِيسْ

nafsī lahum ʿinda inkisāri al-qanā / wa-qad taraddā kullu qirnin ḥasīs

Explanatory translation: “May my life be their ransom when spears break and every mighty opponent has fallen slain.”

Here, حَسِيس means slain.

This differs from حَسِيس meaning a faint sound. Although the word has the same form, the battle context and تَرَدَّى كُلُّ قِرْنٍ indicate a slain opponent falling, rather than his sound being heard.

Literary sources identify Al-Afwah al-Awdī as a pre-Islamic poet, though the authenticity of some poetry attributed to him has been questioned. This line is important evidence from the Arabic literary and lexical tradition; its historical attribution should nevertheless remain qualified.

Lisān al-ʿArab: Al-Afwah’s witness and ḥasīs meaning slain

A witness attributed to Abū Zubayd al-Ṭāʾī

Poetry attributed to Abū Zubayd al-Ṭāʾī also shows a form of this root meaning perceiving or becoming aware:

خَلَا أَنَّ الْعِتَاقَ مِنَ الْمَطَايَا
حَسِينَ بِهِ فَهُنَّ إِلَيْهِ شُوسُ

khalā anna al-ʿitāqa mina al-maṭāyā / ḥasīna bihi fa-hunna ilayhi shūsu

“Except that the noble riding-camels perceived him [the lion], and look askance toward him.”

Some accounts explain حَسِينَ بِهِ here as a form of حَسِسْنَ بِهِ: they perceived or noticed him.

Another transmission has أَحَسْنَ بِهِ, explained as originating in أَحْسَسْنَ بِهِ: those camels perceived his presence. This differs from أَحَسَّنَ, with a doubled sīn, in the original draft.

This witness illustrates two points: early Arabic used the root for perception, and a geminate verb could sometimes change one of its identical consonants to ease pronunciation.

Historically, Abū Zubayd al-Ṭāʾī lived across the transition to Islam. The line is evidence of early Arabic, but cannot unconditionally be dated to the pre-Islamic period.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Lisān al-ʿArab: Abū Zubayd’s lion witness and variant forms

These witnesses show that the different semantic branches of ح س س occur in the Arabic linguistic tradition. They do not by themselves establish their chronological order or which meaning developed from another.

4. Qur’anic uses of the root

A. Perceiving and becoming aware

Āl ʿImrān 3:52

فَلَمَّا أَحَسَّ عِيسَىٰ مِنْهُمُ الْكُفْرَ قَالَ مَنْ أَنْصَارِي إِلَى اللَّهِ قَالَ الْحَوَارِيُّونَ نَحْنُ أَنْصَارُ اللَّهِ آمَنَّا بِاللَّهِ وَاشْهَدْ بِأَنَّا مُسْلِمُونَ

fa-lammā aḥassa ʿīsā minhumu al-kufra qāla man anṣārī ilā allāhi qāla al-ḥawāriyyūna naḥnu anṣāru allāhi āmannā bi-allāhi wa-shhad bi-annā muslimūna

Suggested translation: “When Jesus perceived disbelief from them, he said: Who are my helpers toward God? The disciples said: We are God’s helpers; we believe in God, and bear witness that we submit.”

Here, أَحَسَّ means perceiving or noticing.

The object perceived is الْكُفْرَ, and مِنْهُمُ refers to those from whom this disbelief became apparent.

أَحَسَّ alone does not establish that Jesus directly knew all their hidden intentions without any sign. The verb is also consistent with recognizing something from conduct, speech, or visible indications.

Conversely, it should not be reduced to an emotional impression or groundless supposition. The verse describes perceiving disbelief and then Jesus’s response.

Thus “perceived” or “noticed” can express the meaning more naturally than the Persian “felt,” which in modern Persian may suggest only emotion or a subjective impression.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:12

فَلَمَّا أَحَسُّوا بَأْسَنَا إِذَا هُمْ مِنْهَا يَرْكُضُونَ

fa-lammā aḥassū baʾsanā idhā hum minhā yarkuḍūna

Suggested translation: “When they perceived Our severe punishment, suddenly they fled from there.”

أَحَسُّوا is a third-person masculine plural past verb, form IV.

بَأْسَنَا identifies what they perceived. In this context, بأس concerns severity and divine punishment.

When they perceived this reality, they reacted immediately and fled.

أَحَسُّوا need not mean physically touching the punishment. It may describe perceiving its first signs or witnessing its occurrence.

The verb alone does not conclusively determine whether the punishment had fully overtaken them before they fled or they had detected its approach. That question requires examining Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:11, Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:12, Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:13, Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:14, and Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:15.

The main point is that أَحَسُّوا describes perceiving a situation, while يَرْكُضُونَ describes their practical response.

Maryam 19:98

وَكَمْ أَهْلَكْنَا قَبْلَهُمْ مِنْ قَرْنٍ هَلْ تُحِسُّ مِنْهُمْ مِنْ أَحَدٍ أَوْ تَسْمَعُ لَهُمْ رِكْزًا

wa-kam ahlaknā qablahum min qarnin hal tuḥissu minhum min aḥadin aw tasmaʿu lahum rikzan

Suggested translation: “How many generations We destroyed before them! Do you perceive anyone of them, or hear even a faint sound from them?”

تُحِسُّ is the present tense of أَحَسَّ and concerns perceiving someone’s presence.

The question هَلْ تُحِسُّ مِنْهُمْ مِنْ أَحَدٍ emphasizes the absence of any sign of their presence.

In this interrogative construction, مِنْ أَحَدٍ emphasizes the scope of the expected negative answer: can you find even one of them?

The next phrase is أَوْ تَسْمَعُ لَهُمْ رِكْزًا. رِكْز belongs to the separate root ر ك ز and here refers to a faint sound.

The juxtaposition of تُحِسُّ and تَسْمَعُ shows that perceiving someone’s existence is not linguistically confined to hearing. The verse then separately specifies one sign of presence: hearing a sound.

This does not mean تُحِسُّ must refer exclusively to sight. Perceiving or finding a sign of presence suits the context; hearing is specifically expressed in the second clause.

The verse clearly illustrates the breadth of perception in Arabic: it can extend beyond one particular sense, even when mediated through perceptible signs.

B. Taḥassus: seeking news

Yūsuf 12:87

يَا بَنِيَّ اذْهَبُوا فَتَحَسَّسُوا مِنْ يُوسُفَ وَأَخِيهِ وَلَا تَيْأَسُوا مِنْ رَوْحِ اللَّهِ إِنَّهُ لَا يَيْأَسُ مِنْ رَوْحِ اللَّهِ إِلَّا الْقَوْمُ الْكَافِرُونَ

yā baniyya idhhabū fa-taḥassasū min yūsufa wa-akhīhi wa-lā tayʾasū min rawḥi allāhi innahu lā yayʾasu min rawḥi allāhi illā al-qawmu al-kāfirūna

Suggested translation: “My sons, go and seek news of Joseph and his brother, and do not despair of God’s relief; only disbelieving people despair of God’s relief.”

This is the sole Qur’anic occurrence of تَحَسَّسَ.

Jacob commands his sons to seek Joseph and his brother and obtain news of their condition or whereabouts.

فَتَحَسَّسُوا is a second-person masculine plural imperative, form V. Here it expresses active searching and an effort to find news.

مِنْ يُوسُفَ وَأَخِيهِ identifies the search’s subject. Here مِنْ accompanies taḥassus to express seeking Joseph and his brother or obtaining news about them.

Simply translating it as “feel” would obscure Jacob’s practical command. He is not merely asking his sons to consult their feelings; he tells them to go and search.

Nor is taḥassus here limited to asking a single question. Seeking news can include examining clues, asking others, and pursuing available information.

This breadth follows from the verb and narrative situation; it does not mean that the word separately names every possible method.

The relationship between searching and hope

The same verse pairs فَتَحَسَّسُوا with وَلَا تَيْأَسُوا مِنْ رَوْحِ اللَّهِ.

The connection matters. Jacob commands his sons to make a real effort and search, while also forbidding despair.

In this context, hope does not replace searching, and searching is not separated from hope.

This conceptual conclusion follows from the clauses together. The root ح س س does not itself mean hoping, and رَوْحِ اللَّهِ does not derive from it.

Translation and research should preserve this distinction.

C. Killing and destroying

Āl ʿImrān 3:152

وَلَقَدْ صَدَقَكُمُ اللَّهُ وَعْدَهُ إِذْ تَحُسُّونَهُمْ بِإِذْنِهِ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا فَشِلْتُمْ وَتَنَازَعْتُمْ فِي الْأَمْرِ وَعَصَيْتُمْ مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا أَرَاكُمْ مَا تُحِبُّونَ مِنْكُمْ مَنْ يُرِيدُ الدُّنْيَا وَمِنْكُمْ مَنْ يُرِيدُ الْآخِرَةَ ثُمَّ صَرَفَكُمْ عَنْهُمْ لِيَبْتَلِيَكُمْ وَلَقَدْ عَفَا عَنْكُمْ وَاللَّهُ ذُو فَضْلٍ عَلَى الْمُؤْمِنِينَ

wa-laqad ṣadaqakumu allāhu waʿdahu idh taḥussūnahum bi-idhnihi ḥattā idhā fashiltum wa-tanāzaʿtum fī al-amri wa-ʿaṣaytum min baʿdi mā arākum mā tuḥibbūna minkum man yurīdu al-dunyā wa-minkum man yurīdu al-ākhirata thumma ṣarafakum ʿanhum li-yabtaliyakum wa-laqad ʿafā ʿankum wa-allāhu dhū faḍlin ʿalā al-muʾminīna

Suggested translation: “God indeed fulfilled His promise to you when, by His permission, you were killing them, until you lost courage, disputed over the matter, and disobeyed after He had shown you what you loved. Some of you desired this world, and some desired the Hereafter. Then He turned you away from them to test you. He has indeed pardoned you; God possesses bounty toward the believers.”

تَحُسُّونَهُمْ derives from the form I verb حَسَّ and means killing or destroying the enemy on the battlefield.

Its meaning clearly differs from أَحَسَّ in Āl ʿImrān 3:52.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:52, Jesus perceived disbelief.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:152, the people addressed were killing their opponents.

Identical root letters do not justify giving these verbs the same meaning.

In تَحُسُّونَهُمْ, هُمْ is the object pronoun referring to the opposing combatants. بِإِذْنِهِ occurs within the context of divine promise and assistance.

Lexically, حَسَّ in this branch can convey severe killing and destruction. Its intensity does not establish a historical claim that every enemy was completely annihilated.

The verse concerns a particular battle. A verb meaning killing in that setting does not by itself constitute a general command to kill others or authorize unrestricted conduct in warfare.

Furthermore, تَحُسُّونَهُمْ here reports an event; it is not an imperative.

Distinguishing a verb’s meaning from the kind of sentence in which it occurs is essential.

D. Ḥasīs: a faint sound

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:102

لَا يَسْمَعُونَ حَسِيسَهَا وَهُمْ فِي مَا اشْتَهَتْ أَنْفُسُهُمْ خَالِدُونَ

lā yasmaʿūna ḥasīsahā wa-hum fī mā ishtahat anfusuhum khālidūna

Suggested translation: “They do not hear its faint sound, and they remain in what their souls desire.”

حَسِيسَهَا here refers to the sound of the fire.

The pronoun هَا refers back to Jahannam, mentioned in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:98; Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:99, Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:100, and Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:101 continue referring to the same place. Its antecedent and context establish the reference to the fire’s sound; the word نار does not occur in those preceding verses.

حَسِيس can denote a faint sound or a sound arising from something’s movement or activity.

Its antecedent makes “the fire’s sound” appropriate here. The word حسيس itself does not mean fire, burning, or punishment.

It also differs from حَسِيس meaning slain in some early poetic witnesses.

The syntax is straightforward: لَا يَسْمَعُونَ negates hearing, while حَسِيسَهَا identifies what is not heard.

The statement occurs in a context describing that group’s distance from the fire. Denying that they hear its sound also contributes to the wider picture of distance and safety.

That conclusion follows from Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:101, Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:102, and Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:103; the lexical meaning of حسيس remains sound.

5. Comparison with related words

Ḥ S S and J S S

A particularly important comparison concerns تَحَسَّسَ and تَجَسَّسَ.

فَتَحَسَّسُوا, with ḥāʾ, occurs in Yūsuf 12:87.

By contrast, وَلَا تَجَسَّسُوا, with jīm, occurs in Al-Ḥujurāt 49:12.

Some lexical explanations associate taḥassus more with seeking something desirable, and tajassus more with searching for faults or hidden matters.

This suits the two contexts: Jacob tells his sons to seek Joseph, while Al-Ḥujurāt 49:12 prohibits spying.

The distinction should not be made absolute.

Lisān al-ʿArab reports Ibn al-Aʿrābī’s view that تَجَسَّسْتُ الْخَبَرَ and تَحَسَّسْتُهُ can have the same meaning.

Lexicographers also do not agree completely on the precise difference. Some relate it to searching for oneself or for someone else, others to listening and investigating hidden matters.

Lisān al-ʿArab: lexical views on taḥassus and tajassus

One therefore cannot declare every activity called taḥassus inherently good and every activity called tajassus inherently bad.

The ethics of a search also depend on its subject, purpose, methods, and others’ rights.

ح س س and ج س س are distinct roots. Similarity of meaning and structure does not demonstrate that they are historically one root.

Study of J S S

Ḥ S S and SH ʿ R

شَعَرَ also concerns noticing and becoming aware.

Some lexicographers explain أَحَسَّ through شَعَرَ بِهِ. This proximity shows that both roots can concern perceiving a reality.

Nevertheless, each Qur’anic word must be examined in its own construction.

Shuʿūr can concern awareness of a reality someone has not noticed. Iḥsās can express perceiving something through its presence or signs.

This overlap does not make all derivatives of the two roots interchangeable.

Ḥ S S and S M ʿ

سَمِعَ concerns hearing and thus overlaps with part of the semantic range of ح س س.

In Maryam 19:98, however, تُحِسُّ and تَسْمَعُ occur separately.

Their juxtaposition shows that perceiving someone’s presence and hearing their sound are different expressions.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:102, حَسِيس is the sound itself that is not heard.

Hearing, an audible sound, and perceiving a presence should therefore be distinguished.

Study of S M ʿ

Ḥ S S and W J D

وَجَدَ can refer to finding, perceiving, or encountering something.

In some uses, أَحَسَّ and وَجَدَ are very close, especially when perceiving something’s presence or condition.

Nevertheless, وجد has other, broader meanings that should not all be reduced to feeling.

For example, in Maryam 19:98, هَلْ تُحِسُّ مِنْهُمْ مِنْ أَحَدٍ can naturally be translated “Can you find anyone of them?” This translation choice does not establish complete identity between the roots.

Study of W J D

Distinction from Ḥ S N, Ḥ S B, and Ḥ S M

ح س س resembles roots such as ح س ن, ح س ب, and ح س م in writing, but must not be confused with them.

ح س ن concerns beauty, goodness, and what is pleasing.

ح س ب concerns counting, reckoning, and related meanings.

ح س م concerns cutting off, severing, and ending in Arabic usage.

Some instances may overlap semantically, such as destroying or ending something. That similarity does not make the roots identical or authorize transferring their meanings automatically.

Study of Ḥ S B

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Translation should follow morphological form and context. A single fixed Persian equivalent cannot adequately express all this root’s uses.

حَسَّ: in the destructive branch, killed, eliminated, or destroyed.

أَحَسَّ: perceived, noticed, or sensed something’s presence or condition.

تَحَسَّسَ: searched, sought news, or made inquiries to find someone or something.

حِسّ: sensory perception, or a sound, depending on context.

حَسِيس: a faint sound; slain is also recorded in other early Arabic uses.

حَاسَّة and حَوَاسّ: a sensory faculty and the senses; Arabic forms not used in the Qur’an.

Translating the six Qur’anic occurrences

Āl ʿImrān 3:52 — أَحَسَّ

“Perceived” or “noticed.”

Brief root meaning: sensing and perceiving something’s existence or condition.

Meaning in this verse: Jesus perceived and became aware of their disbelief.

Āl ʿImrān 3:152 — تَحُسُّونَهُمْ

“You were killing them.”

Brief root meaning: in this branch, killing and destroying.

Meaning in this verse: you were killing opposing combatants on the battlefield.

Yūsuf 12:87 — فَتَحَسَّسُوا

“Then search” or “Then seek news.”

Brief root meaning: searching and making an effort to obtain news or signs.

Meaning in this verse: seek news of Joseph and his brother and make inquiries about them.

Maryam 19:98 — تُحِسُّ

“Do you perceive,” “sense his presence,” or, in a fluent rendering of the clause, “find.”

Brief root meaning: perceiving something’s existence or signs.

Meaning in this verse: do you perceive even one of them or find any sign of his presence?

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:12 — أَحَسُّوا

“They perceived” or “they noticed.”

Brief root meaning: becoming aware of something’s existence or occurrence.

Meaning in this verse: they perceived the severity of divine punishment or its signs.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:102 — حَسِيسَهَا

“Its faint sound.”

Brief root meaning: a faint sound or an audible sign of something’s movement or activity.

Meaning in this verse: the faint sound of the fire.

Translating all six occurrences as feeling would produce errors. In Āl ʿImrān 3:152 especially, it would erase the verb’s clear meaning.

In Yūsuf 12:87, translating “feel” instead of “search” would turn a practical command into a mental state.

Conversely, in Maryam 19:98 and Āl ʿImrān 3:52, equivalents such as perceiving and noticing express the meaning naturally without confining it to emotional or bodily sensation.

7. Conclusion

ح س س has several semantic branches in Arabic, all of which should be recognized in studying it.

One branch concerns sensing, perceiving, and becoming aware of something’s presence or condition. أَحَسَّ expresses perceiving, and تَحَسَّسَ seeking and trying to obtain news.

Another concerns sound and sensory reaction. حِسّ and حَسِيس can denote a faint sound; cries of pain and sympathy are also recorded within this family in early Arabic.

The branch concerning killing and destruction is evident in تَحُسُّونَهُمْ in Āl ʿImrān 3:152, and is also supported by early poetic witnesses.

All six Qur’anic occurrences fall into four morphological categories. Three instances of أَحَسَّ concern perception; تَحَسَّسُوا expresses seeking news; تَحُسُّونَهُمْ refers to killing opposing combatants; and حَسِيسَهَا conveys the fire’s sound.

This variety shows why every form of a root should not be interpreted through one familiar modern derivative.

In modern Persian, “feeling” is chiefly associated with sensory or emotional perception; the range of ح س س in early Arabic is broader.

Conversely, attempts to relate every branch to an original image should not distract from each word’s actual contextual meaning.

Final definition for Forqan:

Brief root meaning: In Arabic, ح س س has several semantic branches: perceiving and becoming aware of something; seeking news and signs; sound and sensory reaction; and killing or destroying. In the Qur’an, each form’s meaning follows its morphology and context. أَحَسَّ concerns perceiving, تَحَسَّسَ seeking news, حَسِيس a faint sound, and حَسَّ in a battle context killing and destroying.
