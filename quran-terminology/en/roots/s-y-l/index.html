Flowing and running: from floods and valleys to molten metal and the Quranic parable of truth and falsehood

1. The root in early Arabic

The Arabic root س ي ل denotes flowing, running and extending in motion along a course. سَالَ يَسِيلُ means that something flowed from one place to another. It is used especially of water and other liquids, but is not restricted to water.

سَالَ الْمَاءُ means that water flowed; سَالَ الْوَادِي means that water ran through a valley or wadi. In the latter, flow is attributed to the valley, although its water is what actually moves.

As a noun, سَيْل denotes abundant running water, particularly rainwater flowing through valleys and natural channels. “Flood” or “torrent” is a familiar equivalent.

سَيَلَان and سَيْل should nevertheless be distinguished. The former names flowing as an action, while the latter can name the flowing water or torrent itself. Not every liquid flow is a flood; سَيْل can also function as a verbal noun in Arabic.

The transitive أَسَالَ means to make something flow. سَيَّلَ is also recorded with the sense of setting something flowing.

The distinction is clear: in سَالَ الْمَاءُ the water flows; in أَسَالَ الْمَاءَ someone or something causes it to flow.

Historical meaning and early poetic evidence

The Muʿallaqa attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays describes a horse's swift, powerful movement:

مِكَرٍّ مِفَرٍّ مُقْبِلٍ مُدْبِرٍ مَعًا
كَجُلْمُودِ صَخْرٍ حَطَّهُ السَّيْلُ مِنْ عَلِ

mikarrin mifarrin muqbilin mudbirin maʿan / ka-julmūdi ṣakhrin ḥaṭṭahu al-saylu min ʿali

That is: charging, retreating, advancing and turning all together, like a great rock swept down from above by a torrent.

السَّيْلُ here denotes a powerful flow capable of moving a boulder.

In language attributed to the pre-Islamic period, the image identifies a familiar water flow capable of carrying or dislodging objects.

A poem attributed to Tarafah ibn al-ʿAbd also states:

فَهْيَ مَوْتَى، لَعِبَ الْمَاءُ بِهَا
فِي غُثَاءٍ سَاقَهُ السَّيْلُ، عُدَدْ

fa-hiya mawtā laʿiba al-māʾu bihā / fī ghuthāʾin sāqahu al-saylu ʿudad

In the preceding verse, the poet describes spiny-tailed lizards whose burrows have flooded. Here they are dead, tossed about by the water among debris carried by the torrent. The image concerns animal bodies, not unspecified remnants.

سَاقَهُ السَّيْلُ means that the torrent drove and carried it along.

This illustrates another aspect of a torrent: flowing water can carry floating matter, debris and other objects.

Both poems are attributed to pre-Islamic poets in later literary collections. They are valuable evidence of early usage, but a poet's pre-Islamic identity alone does not establish the precise date of every verse.

Despite that limitation, the meaning of سَيْل is clear in these examples and compatible with its Quranic use.

The semantic field in dictionaries

Ibn Faris describes the root's central meaning as follows:

أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى جَرَيَانٍ وَامْتِدَادٍ

aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā jarayānin wa-imtidād

That is: “A single basic meaning indicating flow and extension.”

The definition combines two features: flowing and extending.

Flow describes continuing movement; extension suggests its continuation along a course. Neither requires every occurrence to denote very fast, prolonged or destructive motion.

Arabic dictionaries record مَسِيل for a watercourse or flood channel. السَّائِلَة can also describe an extended white marking along a horse's forehead and nose, connected by lexicographers with the marking's length.

السَّيَال is recorded for a thorny tree, and السِّيلان for a metal part of a sword or knife. Their inclusion in a lexical family does not establish historical derivation from flowing water.

The clear central verbal sense is consequently flowing. Uses involving extension, shapes and particular names require their own evidence.

2. Quranic forms and occurrences

In the Hafs reading used here, س ي ل occurs four times in three Quranic verses.

Its Quranic forms are:

  • فَسَالَتْ in al-Raʿd 13:17: a form-I perfect verb, “flowed.”
  • السَّيْلُ in al-Raʿd 13:17: a noun, “torrent” or abundant flowing water.
  • وَأَسَلْنَا in Saba 34:12: a form-IV perfect verb, “We caused to flow.”
  • سَيْلَ in Saba 34:16: a noun, “flood.”

These describe three different situations: water flowing through valleys, making a substance other than water flow, and a flood with destructive effects.

The root cannot therefore be restricted to a destructive flood. Flow may be a useful natural phenomenon, a process involving molten material or a destructive event.

Other Arabic forms include يَسِيلُ, سَيَلَان, مَسِيل, مَسَال, سَيَّلَ and تَسْيِيل, but these do not occur independently in the Quran.

3. فَسَالَتْ أَوْدِيَةٌ: water flowing in valleys

Al-Raʿd 13:17 states:

أَنْزَلَ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً فَسَالَتْ أَوْدِيَةٌ بِقَدَرِهَا فَاحْتَمَلَ السَّيْلُ زَبَدًا رَابِيًا

anzala mina al-samāʾi māʾan fa-sālat awdiyatun bi-qadarihā fa-iḥtamala al-saylu zabadan rābiyā

Al-Raʿd 13:17

He sent down water from the sky; valleys flowed according to their capacity, and the torrent carried swelling foam.

The root occurs twice: first in the verb فَسَالَتْ and then in the noun السَّيْلُ.

Why is flowing attributed to valleys?

In فَسَالَتْ أَوْدِيَةٌ, the grammatical subject is أَوْدِيَةٌ: valleys or wadis.

What actually flows, however, is their water.

This is natural Arabic usage. Saying that a valley flowed does not mean that the ground or valley bed moved, but that water flowed within it.

Lexical analysis must therefore distinguish grammatical construction from the phenomenon it describes.

A verb can grammatically be attributed to a location while the actual movement belongs to the material within it.

بِقَدَرِهَا and the measure of flow

The verse says:

فَسَالَتْ أَوْدِيَةٌ بِقَدَرِهَا

fa-sālat awdiyatun bi-qadarihā

Al-Raʿd 13:17

Then valleys flowed according to their measure.

بِقَدَرِهَا expresses the measure of the flow in relation to the valleys.

Valleys differ in width, depth and capacity. When rain falls, the quantity of water flowing through different channels can vary.

The image connects flow with the size and condition of its channel.

سالت does not itself mean measuring or having capacity. That dimension comes from بِقَدَرِهَا.

Reflection on the parable may consider different capacities. But without evidence, أودية should not be declared to lexically mean human hearts, nor بقدرها necessarily to mean individuals' measure of faith.

The immediate image is water actually flowing through valleys. Its figurative application becomes explicit later in the verse.

The torrent carrying floating matter

The passage continues:

فَاحْتَمَلَ السَّيْلُ زَبَدًا رَابِيًا

fa-iḥtamala al-saylu zabadan rābiyā

Al-Raʿd 13:17

Then the torrent carried swelling foam.

السَّيْلُ is the subject of فَاحْتَمَلَ: the torrent carries something along.

زَبَدًا denotes foam or froth on the water. رَابِيًا describes it as rising or swelling.

Three distinct ideas are present: the flowing torrent, the foam accompanying it and the foam's swelling.

They must not be merged into the meaning of سَيْل itself.

A torrent can carry debris, sediment and other materials. The Quranic image foregrounds swelling foam moving with the current.

This would be familiar to people living among valleys and seasonal watercourses: rainwater gathers in channels, flows and sometimes carries floating matter.

The word سَيْل does not require the water always to be muddy, polluted or destructive. Those features depend on conditions.

4. The torrent in the parable of truth and falsehood

Al-Raʿd 13:17 goes beyond describing water and a torrent. It introduces another phenomenon:

وَمِمَّا يُوقِدُونَ عَلَيْهِ فِي النَّارِ ابْتِغَاءَ حِلْيَةٍ أَوْ مَتَاعٍ زَبَدٌ مِثْلُهُ

wa-mimmā yūqidūna ʿalayhi fī al-nāri ibtighāʾa ḥilyatin aw matāʿin zabadun mithluhu

Al-Raʿd 13:17

And from what they heat in the fire, seeking ornaments or useful goods, comes similar froth.

Two physical images are placed alongside one another.

In the first, water runs through valleys and carries foam.

In the second, material is heated to make ornaments or implements, and froth-like matter appears on its surface.

Both involve material rising or separating out. Water and metal nevertheless remain different substances, and the processes are not identical.

The Quran then states:

كَذَٰلِكَ يَضْرِبُ اللَّهُ الْحَقَّ وَالْبَاطِلَ

kadhālika yaḍribu allāhu al-ḥaqqa wa-al-bāṭil

Al-Raʿd 13:17

Thus The God illustrates truth and falsehood.

It continues:

فَأَمَّا الزَّبَدُ فَيَذْهَبُ جُفَاءً وَأَمَّا مَا يَنْفَعُ النَّاسَ فَيَمْكُثُ فِي الْأَرْضِ

fa-ammā al-zabadu fa-yadhhabu jufāʾan wa-ammā mā yanfaʿu al-nāsa fa-yamkuthu fī al-arḍ

Al-Raʿd 13:17

As for the foam, it passes away, cast aside; but what benefits people remains in the earth.

What is central to the parable?

What appears on the surface is not necessarily the useful, enduring part.

Foam can spread widely and attract attention without serving the water's purpose. Likewise, dross may appear on heated material, while its useful part is what is needed for ornaments or implements.

The parable distinguishes outward appearance from real value, and the transient from the beneficial and enduring.

The Quran uses this image to explain truth and falsehood.

Yet سَيْل does not itself lexically mean truth, nor does زَبَد acquire every meaning of falsehood merely by appearing beside the torrent.

سَيْل names flowing water; زَبَد names foam. Their relationship to truth and falsehood comes from the parable's structure.

This matters in a root study. Treating س ي ل as inherently meaning truth, guidance or reforming power would substitute interpretation for lexical meaning.

Likewise, treating every natural flood as a symbol of falsehood or impurity because it carries foam would exceed the parable's scope.

The torrent is a natural phenomenon used here to explain a moral and epistemic truth.

The meanings of زَبَد for water and heated metal can be examined separately, without adding the meaning of foam to the definition of سَيْل.

5. وَأَسَلْنَا لَهُ عَيْنَ الْقِطْرِ: making another substance flow

Saba 34:12 states:

وَلِسُلَيْمَانَ الرِّيحَ غُدُوُّهَا شَهْرٌ وَرَوَاحُهَا شَهْرٌ وَأَسَلْنَا لَهُ عَيْنَ الْقِطْرِ

wa-li-sulaymāna al-rīḥa ghuduwwuhā shahrun wa-rawāḥuhā shahrun wa-asalnā lahu ʿayna al-qiṭr

Sabaʾ 34:12

For Solomon, the wind: its morning journey was a month and its evening journey a month; and We caused a spring of copper to flow for him.

The root appears here in أَسَلْنَا.

It is a form-IV perfect verb meaning “We caused to flow.”

Its difference from فَسَالَتْ in al-Raʿd 13:17 is clear. There the flowing is described; here causing something to flow is attributed to God.

Is flow restricted to water?

عَيْنَ الْقِطْرِ refers to a spring or source of قِطْر. This has an established sense of copper, presented here as flowing.

Copper is normally solid, but can become liquid and flow when melted.

أَسَلْنَا therefore demonstrates that flow is not restricted to water: molten metal can also flow.

Defining the root exclusively as water moving in a riverbed would not accommodate this verse.

A broader and more accurate definition is the movement of material capable of flowing, whether water or another substance.

The verb's meaning must again be distinguished from the event's mechanism. أَسَلْنَا specifies no equipment, temperature or particular process.

The Quran states the result: a spring of copper was made to flow for Solomon.

The expression does not establish a particular smelting technology, unknown industrial apparatus or unproven physical property.

Work and the making of implements

Saba 34:12 continues with members of the jinn working for Solomon by his Lord's permission.

Saba 34:13 mentions the making of lofty chambers, statues, large bowls and fixed cauldrons.

Flowing copper thus appears among the blessings and resources connected with Solomon's rule and works.

Not every object named in the next verse can be assumed to be copper: the Quran does not specify all their materials.

For this root study, the clear point is that causing flow applies to a material beyond running water.

6. سَيْلَ الْعَرِمِ: Saba's destructive flood

Saba 34:16 states:

فَأَعْرَضُوا فَأَرْسَلْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ سَيْلَ الْعَرِمِ وَبَدَّلْنَاهُمْ بِجَنَّتَيْهِمْ جَنَّتَيْنِ ذَوَاتَيْ أُكُلٍ خَمْطٍ وَأَثْلٍ وَشَيْءٍ مِنْ سِدْرٍ قَلِيلٍ

fa-aʿraḍū fa-arsalnā ʿalayhim sayla al-ʿarimi wa-baddalnāhum bi-jannatayhim jannatayni dhawātay ukulin khamṭin wa-athlin wa-shayʾin min sidrin qalīl

Sabaʾ 34:16

They turned away, so We sent upon them the flood of al-ʿarim, and replaced their two gardens with two bearing bitter produce, tamarisk and a little lote.

سَيْل is a noun here, combined with الْعَرِمِ.

The surrounding verses link this flood with a severe change in Saba's life and environment.

Distinguishing سَيْل from عَرِم

سَيْل retains its meaning of abundant flowing water.

الْعَرِمِ is another word, treated under ع ر م. Its meaning should not be imported into سَيْل without examination.

Lexicographical and exegetical sources record several interpretations of عَرِم.

One identifies a dam or embankment built across a valley to restrain and store water. سَيْلَ الْعَرِمِ then denotes the flood associated with that structure.

Another connects عَرِم with the flood's force and unruliness: a severe, unrestrained torrent.

It has also been taken as the name of a particular valley or place.

These readings warrant historical and linguistic examination, but س ي ل alone decides none of them.

“The flood of the dam” therefore fits one established reading. It does not mean that سَيْل itself denotes a dam breaking or stored water being released.

The flood in Saba's story

Saba 34:15 describes two gardens to the right and left of the people's dwelling:

لَقَدْ كَانَ لِسَبَإٍ فِي مَسْكَنِهِمْ آيَةٌ جَنَّتَانِ عَنْ يَمِينٍ وَشِمَالٍ

laqad kāna li-sabaʾin fī maskanihim āyatun jannatāni ʿan yamīnin wa-shimāl

Sabaʾ 34:15

There was indeed a sign for Saba in their dwelling: two gardens, to the right and left.

The verse speaks of life's blessings and invites enjoyment and gratitude.

Saba 34:16 then describes their turning away and the flood being sent. The gardens change and their produce deteriorates.

The sequence connects the flood with the loss of earlier benefits.

Saba 34:17 also states:

ذَٰلِكَ جَزَيْنَاهُمْ بِمَا كَفَرُوا وَهَلْ نُجَازِي إِلَّا الْكَفُورَ

dhālika jazaynāhum bi-mā kafarū wa-hal nujāzī illā al-kafūr

Sabaʾ 34:17

Thus We recompensed them for their ingratitude; do We recompense in this way anyone but the deeply ungrateful?

The narrative explicitly relates their behavior to the resulting consequence.

That judgment concerns this particular event. It does not establish that every contemporary flood or natural disaster is divine punishment of its victims.

Such a judgment about a contemporary event requires independent evidence. Floods have recognizable natural causes, and harmful construction, environmental damage, inadequate water management or other human actions may worsen their impact.

Recognizing natural causes and human responsibilities is compatible with examining the story's moral message. Neither warrants confidently declaring divine anger or punishment upon disaster victims without a textual basis.

Comparing the floods in al-Raʿd and Saba

In al-Raʿd 13:17, the torrent appears in a depiction of water flow and a parable of truth and falsehood. Besides carrying foam, the water is associated with what benefits people and remains.

In Saba 34:16, the same word occurs in a destructive event.

Restricting سَيْل to destruction would therefore obscure al-Raʿd 13:17.

Conversely, treating a torrent as inherently signifying mercy or blessing would neglect Saba's story.

The root has no fixed moral value. A flood's effects depend on water quantity, flow strength, route, environment and living conditions. The Quran employs the phenomenon in two different contexts.

7. Distinguishing related roots

س ي ل and ج ر ي

Both roots concern movement and flow.

جَرْي has a broad Arabic range, applying to water, ships, the sun, the moon and other phenomena.

سَيْل is more closely associated with flowing liquid and especially abundant water.

Quranic derivatives of ج ر ي describe rivers, ships and heavenly bodies, whereas س ي ل concerns water and a substance such as copper being made to flow.

This is a tendency, not an absolute rule assigning only orderly motion to جَرْي and only disorderly motion to سَيَلَان.

The broader uses of ج ر ي require their own examination; similarity in flow does not make the two families identical.

س ي ل and ف ي ض

فَيْض concerns abundance, overflowing and flowing forth.

Water can increase until it overflows its container or place. The family also extends to overflowing emotion and, physically, abundant tears.

سَيَلَان need not involve exceeding capacity: liquid may flow within its channel without spilling beyond it.

Some uses of فَيْض, by contrast, emphasize excess and overflow.

Flowing and overflowing can coincide, but are not identical meanings.

س ي ل and س ي ح

س ي ح has Arabic uses for flowing and moving through the land. Applied to people, ساح في الأرض means roaming or traveling through the land.

س ي ل is more often used of liquids, water and fluid materials.

They share aspects of movement and flow, but remain different roots.

Features of a torrent, such as its force or carrying debris, cannot all be transferred to سِيَاحَة.

س ي ل and س ي ب

Some uses of س ي ب concern flowing or free movement. Another major branch concerns releasing, setting free or leaving something unrestrained.

سَيَلَان describes flow itself, while transitive تَسْيِيب can describe removing restraint and allowing freedom.

Water may flow whether or not someone deliberately removed a barrier. An animal can be released without its movement being termed سَيْل.

Their semantic proximity must not erase their actual differences.

س ي ل and ص ب ب

صَبّ denotes pouring. Water being poured from a vessel can be expressed by صَبَّ.

سَالَ, however, describes flowing.

Someone may pour water, which then runs over the ground. Pouring and flowing are related stages, but distinct meanings.

Water can also flow without someone pouring it from a vessel.

ʿAbasa 80:25 states:

أَنَّا صَبَبْنَا الْمَاءَ صَبًّا

annā ṣababnā al-māʾa ṣabbā

ʿAbasa 80:25

We poured down the water abundantly.

In al-Raʿd 13:17, after water descends from the sky, its flow through valleys is expressed by فَسَالَتْ.

Different words thus express different aspects of water's movement.

س ي ل and س أ ل

These roots may be confused in writing, especially when the hamzah is omitted.

س ي ل concerns flowing.

س أ ل concerns asking, requesting or seeking something.

سَائِل can describe something flowing, but سَائِل meaning a questioner or petitioner belongs to س أ ل.

For example, al-Duha 93:10 states:

وَأَمَّا السَّائِلَ فَلَا تَنْهَرْ

wa-ammā al-sāʾila fa-lā tanhar

al-Duha 93:10

As for the petitioner, do not drive him away harshly.

السَّائِلَ here means the petitioner, not flowing liquid.

In al-Maʿarij 70:1, the familiar reading سَأَلَ سَائِلٌ concerns asking. Some readings pronounce the first verb سَالَ without hamzah. Analyses differ between another form of asking and a possible connection with flowing.

This reading difference must not lead to assigning every سال or سائل to س ي ل without examining morphology and context.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

Suitable equivalents for سَالَ يَسِيلُ include “flowed,” “ran” and “began to flow.”

In al-Raʿd 13:17:

فَسَالَتْ أَوْدِيَةٌ بِقَدَرِهَا

fa-sālat awdiyatun bi-qadarihā

Al-Raʿd 13:17

A rendering close to the Arabic structure is:

“Then valleys flowed according to their measure.”

An explanation can clarify that water flowed within the valley beds.

For greater fluency, “Then water flowed through the valleys according to their capacity” makes the meaning explicit. However, “water” is understood from the previous clause rather than repeated in this phrase.

For:

فَاحْتَمَلَ السَّيْلُ زَبَدًا رَابِيًا

fa-iḥtamala al-saylu zabadan rābiyā

Al-Raʿd 13:17

A suitable rendering is:

“Then the torrent carried swelling foam.”

“Torrent” suits السَّيْل, while carrying the foam is expressed by فَاحْتَمَلَ.

In Saba 34:12:

وَأَسَلْنَا لَهُ عَيْنَ الْقِطْرِ

wa-asalnā lahu ʿayna al-qiṭr

Sabaʾ 34:12

One can render it:

“We caused a spring of copper to flow for him.”

This preserves both the causative meaning and its object. The copper's molten condition can be discussed in the lexical and contextual explanation.

In Saba 34:16:

فَأَرْسَلْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ سَيْلَ الْعَرِمِ

fa-arsalnā ʿalayhim sayla al-ʿarim

Sabaʾ 34:16

A basic rendering is:

“So We sent upon them the flood of al-ʿarim.”

An explanation can discuss readings connecting عَرِم with a dam, a valley or the flood's intensity.

“The flood of the dam” is also defensible on one established reading, but that sense does not come from س ي ل itself.

In modern Persian, “seyl” and “seylab” usually describe strong, abundant water flow. “Sayalan” is broader, applying to liquids and other flowing materials.

The family's verbs should therefore not always be rendered as becoming a flood. “Flow” is often more accurate.

Conclusion

In early Arabic, س ي ل denotes flowing and extending in motion along a course. Water is a clear application, but not the only one.

سَيْل names abundant running water, especially a torrent in a valley. سَيَلَان describes the action of flowing, and إِسَالَة causing something to flow.

The Quran uses this family in three different situations.

In al-Raʿd 13:17, water descends, valleys flow according to their measure and the torrent carries swelling foam. Together with an image of heated materials, this becomes a parable of truth and falsehood.

In Saba 34:12, أَسَلْنَا concerns a spring of copper being made to flow. Flow is not restricted to water and can apply to molten material.

In Saba 34:16, سَيْلَ الْعَرِمِ occurs in Saba's story alongside damaged and transformed gardens. The precise meaning of عَرِم requires separate study; the event's every feature must not be absorbed into سَيْل.

Flow itself therefore does not inherently mean destruction, blessing or guidance. Running water may benefit people or cause great harm under particular conditions. Materials other than water can also flow.

Final definition: س ي ل denotes flowing and the extension of material along a course. سَيْل commonly names abundant water flow, and أَسَالَ means to make something flow. Intensity, quantity, benefit or destruction and the material's characteristics come from context and accompanying words, not from the root alone.

In al-Raʿd 13:17, this natural phenomenon illustrates truth and falsehood: what looks large and prominent on the surface is not necessarily what is useful and lasting. Real value differs from a conspicuous, passing appearance; what benefits people has another standing.

That conclusion comes from the complete Quranic parable, while the root's lexical meaning remains distinct and clear: flowing and running.
