Settlement, stability, and movement coming to rest in its place

Arabic R S W denotes becoming fixed, settling, and remaining firm in place. It describes firmly established mountains, a ship coming to rest, a large cooking pot remaining in place, and something secured in the ground.

Within this family, rasā means became fixed or settled, arsā means made fixed or settled, and rāsī describes something firmly established in place.

The Qur’an uses the root in several fields. Rawāsi describes firm mountains; rāsiyāt describes cooking pots in Solomon’s story; mursāhā concerns Noah’s ship coming to rest and the time of the Hour’s occurrence; and arsāhā expresses establishing the mountains firmly.

These uses share settlement and stability, but the kind of settlement differs. A mountain’s firmness, a ship’s stopping, a pot’s remaining fixed, and an event’s occurrence are four different situations.

The root occurs fourteen times in fourteen Qur’anic verses: nine instances of rawāsi, one of rāsiyāt, three of mursāhā, and one of arsāhā.

Qur’anic forms: رَوَاسِيَ (rawāsiya): Al-Raʿd 13:3, Al-Ḥijr 15:19, Al-Naḥl 16:15, Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:31, Al-Naml 27:61, Luqmān 31:10, Fuṣṣilat 41:10, Qāf 50:7, Al-Mursalāt 77:27; رَاسِيَاتٍ (rāsiyātin): Sabaʾ 34:13; مُرْسَاهَا (mursāhā): Al-Aʿrāf 7:187, Hūd 11:41, Al-Nāziʿāt 79:42; أَرْسَاهَا (arsāhā): Al-Nāziʿāt 79:32.

Other known Arabic forms include رَسَا (rasā), يَرْسُو (yarsū), رُسُوّ (rusūw), رَاسٍ (rāsin), رَاسِيَة (rāsiyah), أَرْسَى (arsā), إِرْسَاء (irsāʾ), مَرْسَى (marsā), مِرْسَاة (mirsāh), and مَرَاسِي (marāsī). These matter for Arabic lexical study but do not occur in precisely these forms in the Qur’an.

1. Core meaning and early Arabic uses

Rasā: becoming fixed and firm

Lexical explanation identifies stability as the semantic core:

الرَّاءُ وَالسِّينُ وَالْحَرْفُ الْمُعْتَلُّ أَصْلٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى ثَبَاتٍ

al-rāʾu wa-al-sīnu wa-al-ḥarfu al-muʿtallu aṣlun yadullu ʿalā thabātin

Meaning: “Rāʾ, sīn, and the weak radical have a core denoting firmness.”

The explanation continues:

رَسَا الشَّيْءُ يَرْسُو، إِذَا ثَبَتَ

rasā al-shayʾu yarsū idhā thabata

“Something rasā: it became fixed and firm.”

Arabic rasā al-jabalu describes a mountain standing firm in place.

Rasat qadamāhu is also recorded of someone whose feet remain firmly planted.

This can describe maintaining one’s position in confrontation or while carrying out a task.

The common feature is firmness and stability.

Firmness does not always mean absolute immobility.

Someone whose feet stand firm in battle may move, speak, or change position in another situation. Firmness here concerns maintaining one’s position rather than losing it.

Likewise, describing a mountain as firmly established in the earth does not by itself establish that it is unchangeable at every time and under every condition.

Rusūw therefore concerns settlement and stability, rather than the absolute absence of movement or change.

Arsā: making something settled and firm

Arsā is Form IV and, in its familiar use, means making something fixed and settled.

The distinction between rasā and arsā is clear in this construction.

Rasā al-shayʾu means something settled.

Arsāhu means someone or some cause made it settled.

For example, rasat al-safīnatu means the ship stopped and settled, whereas arsā al-safīnata means bringing it to rest and settling it.

Arsaytu al-watada is also recorded for driving a peg firmly into the ground.

Driving it in causes the peg to become fixed in place.

This example does not make irsāʾ in every use mean inserting an object into the earth or driving it by striking.

A ship may settle through anchoring, a mountain may stand firmly in place, and an event may occur at a particular time.

The cause and manner of settlement must be understood from usage and context.

Ships and anchoring

One clear Arabic use of the root concerns ships.

Lexical explanation describes a ship as rasat when it reaches a resting place and ceases moving.

Mirsāh is also recorded for a device attached to a ship that holds it in place.

This device is an anchor.

Its function is to prevent the ship’s unrestricted movement and hold it at a location.

Rusūw as settlement and mirsāh as the instrument of settlement are therefore clearly related.

This does not mean every use of R S W necessarily concerns a physical anchor.

The Qur’an uses the family for mountains, cooking pots, and the time of the Hour as well.

These uses cannot be restricted to casting an anchor into water.

Nor does every ship necessarily require a device of one particular design to come to rest.

A ship may stop at a shore, beside a quay, or otherwise. Rusūw expresses its settled condition; how that condition is achieved is a separate question.

Clouds settling and camels becoming still

Arabic also records the following expression:

أَلْقَتِ السَّحَابَةُ مَرَاسِيَهَا

alqat al-saḥābatu marāsiyahā

“The cloud cast its anchors.”

This means the cloud remained over a place and continued raining.

It is an image of a cloud staying in one place, without giving it a ship’s literal anchor.

Rasā al-faḥlu bi-shawlihi describes a male camel whose calls and behavior gather dispersed she-camels and make them settle.

The emphasis is a group of camels becoming still and returning to a settled condition.

Rasawtu bayna al-qawmi is also reported for reconciling people and establishing peace among them.

Here stability extends to human relationships: discord and unrest give way to agreement and calm.

This does not make peace, justice, or resolving disputes the meaning of R S W in every use.

These are particular uses in which the core of settlement and stability concerns a different subject.

Retaining and relating speech

رَسَوْتُ لَهُ مِنَ الْحَدِيثِ (rasawtu lahu mina al-ḥadīthi) is recorded for relating part of a report or story to someone.

رَسَا الْحَدِيثَ فِي نَفْسِهِ (rasā al-ḥadītha fī nafsihi) is also recorded for repeating speech inwardly; retaining and establishing it in the mind explains a result of that repetition.

These uses have an understandable connection with meaning becoming established in the mind or with relating speech.

The R S S family also includes some similar uses concerning reports and speech.

Semantic proximity alone should not merge R S W and R S S.

Nor should establishing speech make Qur’anic rawāsi mean firm statements or established ideas.

In the verses concerning the earth, rawāsi concerns mountains and must be understood in that setting.

2. Early poetry: firm mountains and a steadfast soul

Al-jibāla al-rawāsiyā in Zuhayr’s poetry

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā includes:

أَلَا لَا أَرَى عَلَى الْحَوَادِثِ بَاقِيًا
وَلَا خَالِدًا إِلَّا الْجِبَالَ الرَّوَاسِيَا

alā lā arā ʿalā al-ḥawādithi bāqiyan / wa-lā khālidan illā al-jibāla al-rawāsiyā

“Indeed, I see nothing surviving the turns of fortune, nothing enduring except the firmly established mountains.”

Al-rawāsiyā describes mountains here.

The poet discusses time, death, and disappearance, contrasting firm mountains with brief and unstable human life.

This shows mountains described as rawāsi in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

The word’s meaning must nevertheless be distinguished from the poet’s belief.

Rawāsi here means firmly established mountains.

The entire verse conveys the poet’s view of mountains as exceptionally lasting or enduring through events.

The lexical meaning of rawāsi therefore does not establish that mountains are intrinsically eternal or indestructible.

The distinction also matters in the Qur’an, which calls mountains rawāsi in one setting and elsewhere describes their transformation and collapse.

Present firmness and possible transformation in another event are not semantically inconsistent.

Tarsū in poetry attributed to ʿAntarah

A verse attributed to ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād states:

فَصَبَرْتُ نَفْسًا عِنْدَ ذَٰلِكَ حُرَّةً
تَرْسُو إِذَا نَفْسُ الْجَبَانِ تَطَلَّعُ

fa-ṣabartu nafsan ʿinda dhālika ḥurratan / tarsū idhā nafsu al-jabāni taṭallaʿu

“Then I made a free soul endure, one that stands firm when the coward’s soul grows restless in fear and seeks escape.” In the second hemistich, nafs means the soul or self, not nafas meaning breath.

Tarsū concerns the soul’s firmness and steadfastness here.

The poet describes himself confronting fear and agitation.

The verse has variant transmissions, including the beginning of its first hemistich. The quoted form is known, but this study does not independently establish the precise date of its composition.

The witness matters because rasā applies beyond mountains, pegs, and ships to human steadfastness.

This does not make courage or moral virtue intrinsic to every use of R S W.

Courage emerges from the contrast between the steadfast and fearful souls and the poem’s context.

Tarsū expresses remaining firm.

Limits of poetic evidence

These two witnesses illustrate firmness in mountains and in a person’s condition.

The word extends beyond physical objects while retaining the core of stability and steadfastness.

They do not establish that the root’s entire historical meaning originated with mountains or human conduct in battle.

Nor should Zuhayr’s and ʿAntarah’s poetry be the final authority for interpreting Qur’anic verses.

Early poetry witnesses Arabic usage; the precise meaning of each Qur’anic occurrence must be assessed through its construction and other verses.

3. Rawāsi and arsāhā: firm mountains in the Qur’an

The root’s most extensive Qur’anic use concerns mountains.

Rawāsi occurs nine times in verses about the earth, and arsāhā once expresses establishing the mountains firmly.

The structure of rawāsi

Rawāsi is a broken plural of rāsiyah.

Rāsiyah is the feminine of rāsin, the active participle of rasā.

Its basic meaning is therefore something firm and established.

In the verses about the earth, it applies to mountains.

Some do not explicitly include al-jibāl in the same phrase, but established Arabic usage and other Qur’anic verses clarify the referent of rawāsi.

For example, Al-Nāziʿāt 79:32 states:

وَالْجِبَالَ أَرْسَاهَا

wa-al-jibāla arsāhā

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:32

“And the mountains He established firmly.”

This directly connects mountains with R S W.

Rawāsi in the verses about the earth therefore refers to firmly established mountains.

This does not make rawāsi exclusively a name for mountains in every Arabic sentence.

Sabaʾ uses another plural of the same description for cooking pots.

Wa-jaʿala fīhā rawāsiya: mountains in the earth

Al-Raʿd 13:3 states:

وَهُوَ الَّذِي مَدَّ الْأَرْضَ وَجَعَلَ فِيهَا رَوَاسِيَ وَأَنْهَارًا

wa-huwa alladhī madda al-arḍa wa-jaʿala fīhā rawāsiya wa-anhāran

Al-Raʿd 13:3

“And He is the One who spread out the earth and placed in it firm mountains and rivers.”

Here rawāsi appears alongside anhāran.

The verse discusses the earth, mountains, and rivers before mentioning other signs of creation.

Rawāsi describes firm mountains, whereas anhār means rivers.

They appear together, but neither is part of the other’s meaning.

Rawāsi itself therefore does not mean rivers, flowing water, or fertility.

Al-Ḥijr 15:19 also states:

وَالْأَرْضَ مَدَدْنَاهَا وَأَلْقَيْنَا فِيهَا رَوَاسِيَ وَأَنْبَتْنَا فِيهَا مِنْ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ مَوْزُونٍ

wa-al-arḍa madadnāhā wa-alqaynā fīhā rawāsiya wa-anbatnā fīhā min kulli shayʾin mawzūnin

Al-Ḥijr 15:19

“And the earth We spread out, and placed in it firm mountains, and caused to grow in it something of every balanced kind.”

The mountains’ firmness appears alongside the growth of plants and other created things.

The juxtaposition of rawāsi and anbatnā does not make plant growth or nature’s measured order the root’s meaning.

These are different parts of the description of the earth.

Fuṣṣilat 41:10 also states:

وَجَعَلَ فِيهَا رَوَاسِيَ مِنْ فَوْقِهَا وَبَارَكَ فِيهَا

wa-jaʿala fīhā rawāsiya min fawqihā wa-bāraka fīhā

Fuṣṣilat 41:10

“And He placed in it, above it, firm mountains, and blessed it.”

Min fawqihā describes the mountains’ location relative to the earth.

That phrase should not be added to the lexical definition of rawāsi.

Rawāsi expresses firmness; their location is conveyed by fīhā and min fawqihā.

An tamīda bikum: firmness and the earth’s movement

Al-Naḥl 16:15 states:

وَأَلْقَىٰ فِي الْأَرْضِ رَوَاسِيَ أَنْ تَمِيدَ بِكُمْ وَأَنْهَارًا وَسُبُلًا لَعَلَّكُمْ تَهْتَدُونَ

wa-alqā fī al-arḍi rawāsiya an tamīda bikum wa-anhāran wa-subulan laʿallakum tahtadūna

Al-Naḥl 16:15

“And He placed firm mountains in the earth lest it sway with you, and rivers and paths, so that you might find your way.”

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:31 has a similar expression:

وَجَعَلْنَا فِي الْأَرْضِ رَوَاسِيَ أَنْ تَمِيدَ بِهِمْ وَجَعَلْنَا فِيهَا فِجَاجًا سُبُلًا لَعَلَّهُمْ يَهْتَدُونَ

wa-jaʿalnā fī al-arḍi rawāsiya an tamīda bihim wa-jaʿalnā fīhā fijājan subulan laʿallahum yahtadūna

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:31

“And We placed firm mountains in the earth lest it sway with them, and made in it broad passages as paths, so that they might find their way.”

Luqmān 31:10 also states:

وَأَلْقَىٰ فِي الْأَرْضِ رَوَاسِيَ أَنْ تَمِيدَ بِكُمْ

wa-alqā fī al-arḍi rawāsiya an tamīda bikum

Luqmān 31:10

“And He placed firm mountains in the earth lest it sway with you.”

These three verses have similar constructions.

Each connects rawāsi with the earth and follows it with an tamīda.

The meaning of mayd

Mayd belongs to M Y D and concerns swaying, oscillation, and unsettled movement.

In these verses, an tamīda occurs in a construction expressing prevention of that movement.

The grammatical explanation understands an here as averting an unwelcome event, yielding li-allā tamīda: lest the earth sway or become unsettled.

This comes from the sentence construction and Arabic usage.

The verse therefore states a purpose for placing firm mountains.

Mayd is not part of R S W’s own meaning.

Rawāsi concerns the mountains’ firmness, while tamīda concerns the earth’s unsettled movement.

Together they express a relationship between mountains and the earth’s stability.

Does the Qur’an say the earth has no movement?

The root R S W itself establishes no such conclusion.

Mayd in these verses concerns unsettled movement and swaying.

The earth’s movement can involve different meanings and kinds of motion.

Movement along a path, rotation, crustal displacement, local shaking, and unsettled oscillation are not conceptually or physically identical.

An tamīda bikum should therefore not be taken as denying every kind of movement of the earth.

Nor do these verses alone offer a precise geological theory of the earth’s crust, the origins of earthquakes, or every mountain’s physical function.

The roles mountains play in the earth’s structure require independent scientific investigation, rather than turning a simple comparison or lexical inference into a definite physical law.

Conversely, earthquakes do not justify removing the mountains’ literal firmness and treating rawāsi merely as a symbol of social or religious stability.

These verses discuss the actual earth and mountains.

Their natural, physical meaning should be retained without adding details the verses do not state.

Rawāsiya shāmikhātin

Al-Mursalāt 77:27 states:

وَجَعَلْنَا فِيهَا رَوَاسِيَ شَامِخَاتٍ وَأَسْقَيْنَاكُمْ مَاءً فُرَاتًا

wa-jaʿalnā fīhā rawāsiya shāmikhātin wa-asqaynākum māʾan furātan

Al-Mursalāt 77:27

“And We placed in it firm, lofty mountains and gave you fresh water to drink.”

Rawāsi appears with the adjective shāmikhāt.

Shāmikhāt belongs to SH M KH and here concerns the mountains’ height.

The combination matters because it distinguishes firmness from height.

Rawāsi expresses firmness.

Shāmikhāt expresses their height.

Height should therefore not become an invariable part of R S W’s meaning.

A mountain can be tall and firm, but these are distinct qualities.

The verse then mentions fresh drinking water.

The juxtaposition of mountains and water does not, without independent evidence, establish a complete mechanism for rivers or groundwater forming.

The Qur’an recalls several blessings and features of the earth, each with its own meaning.

Other occurrences of rawāsi

Al-Naml 27:61 places firm mountains alongside the earth as a resting place, rivers, and a barrier between two seas.

Qāf 50:7 connects rawāsi with the earth’s expanse and the growth of plants.

In both verses, mountains form part of the description of the earth and the signs of creation.

Rawāsi itself means neither rivers, plant growth, a resting place, nor a barrier between two seas.

Other words convey those features.

Wa-al-jibāla arsāhā

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:32 states:

وَالْجِبَالَ أَرْسَاهَا

wa-al-jibāla arsāhā

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:32

“And the mountains He established firmly.”

Arsāhā is a Form IV past verb from R S W.

The pronoun hā refers to al-jibāl.

The verb is transitive here, expressing the mountains being established firmly.

This clearly relates to rawāsi in other verses.

Those verses describe the mountains’ firmness; this one expresses the act of establishing them firmly.

Arsā alone does not specify the method of creating mountains, their geological sequence of formation, or their constituent materials.

Nor should it be restricted to driving mountains into the earth: that would turn one limited physical image into a theory of how mountains originate.

The verse expresses establishing them firmly; its physical manner requires other appropriate evidence.

4. Wa-qudūrin rāsiyātin: firmly set cooking pots in Solomon’s story

Sabaʾ contains a particularly useful contrasting use: rāsiyāt here describes cooking pots rather than mountains.

Sabaʾ 34:13 states:

يَعْمَلُونَ لَهُ مَا يَشَاءُ مِنْ مَحَارِيبَ وَتَمَاثِيلَ وَجِفَانٍ كَالْجَوَابِ وَقُدُورٍ رَاسِيَاتٍ

yaʿmalūna lahu mā yashāʾu min maḥārība wa-tamāthīla wa-jifānin ka-al-jawābi wa-qudūrin rāsiyātin

Sabaʾ 34:13

“They made for him whatever he wished: structures, images, basins like reservoirs, and firmly set cooking pots.”

The verse discusses things made for Solomon.

The morphology of rāsiyāt

Rāsiyāt is the sound feminine plural of rāsiyah.

Rawāsi is another plural in the same descriptive family.

The two forms therefore share a closely related semantic core.

Their Qur’anic subjects differ, however.

Rawāsi in the verses about the earth concerns mountains.

Rāsiyāt in Sabaʾ describes qudūr, cooking pots.

This makes clear that rāsiyah is not intrinsically a name for a mountain.

The word expresses firmness; context identifies the thing described.

Pots that remain fixed in place

Qidrun rāsiyatun is recorded for a pot that stays in place and is not easily moved.

Early explanations of qudūrin rāsiyātin say these pots were not removed from their places because of their size.

Other explanations also emphasize their size and weight.

This fits the context, which has just mentioned vessels as large as reservoirs.

The rāsiyāt pots can therefore be understood as large pots firmly set in place and difficult to move.

Lexical meaning should nevertheless be distinguished from contextual explanation.

Lexically, rāsiyāt means firmly set or established.

The pots’ size and weight come from the object, context, and lexical explanations of this use.

The word itself establishes neither their precise dimensions, weight, metal, nor capacity.

Did the pots stay there forever?

The verse does not establish that conclusion.

Rāsiyāt expresses their settled, fixed condition.

They may have been positioned in particular places and not moved in ordinary use.

This does not mean moving them was physically impossible under every condition or that they remained there forever.

Nor does the verse say they were necessarily carved into mountains or made of a particular material.

Some exegetical reports give such details, but rāsiyāt itself does not convey them.

This use is especially useful for defining the root because it shows firmness applying to a human-made object as well as mountains.

5. Majrāhā wa-mursāhā: Noah’s ship moving and coming to rest

Another use occurs in Noah’s story, in Hūd 11:41:

وَقَالَ ارْكَبُوا فِيهَا بِسْمِ اللَّهِ مَجْرَاهَا وَمُرْسَاهَا إِنَّ رَبِّي لَغَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ

wa-qāla irkabū fīhā bi-smi allāhi majrāhā wa-mursāhā inna rabbī la-ghafūrun raḥīmun

Hūd 11:41

“And he said: Board it; in the name of The God are its course and its coming to rest. My Lord is indeed forgiving and merciful.”

Mursāhā here concerns the ship.

Understanding it requires considering majrāhā and mursāhā together.

Movement and stopping: two different conditions

Majrāhā belongs to J R Y and concerns movement and flowing.

Mursāhā belongs to R S W and here concerns the ship stopping and settling.

Two conditions thus appear together: moving and coming to rest.

Arabic rasat al-safīnatu describes a ship ceasing to move and settling in place.

Mursāhā therefore directly concerns the ship’s stopping and resting here.

It should not necessarily be restricted to casting a particular anchor, since the verse does not identify the means of stopping.

Nor should mursāhā be restricted to reaching shore.

The ship can settle at a shore or elsewhere; the root alone does not identify its destination.

Reading and structure of mursāhā

The familiar Ḥafṣ reading has majrāhā wa-mursāhā.

Established readings of majrāhā differ between fatḥah and ḍammah on its initial mīm.

Mursāhā in the well-known readings has ḍammah on mīm and is associated with Form IV arsā.

Morphologically, mursā can be a mīm-prefixed verbal noun or a noun of time or place; lexical explanations apply it to settling, its time, or its location.

Here it concerns the ship and is paired with its movement in one construction.

The appropriate meaning is therefore the ship’s settlement or being brought to rest.

The syntactic position of majrāhā wa-mursāhā also has differing analyses, including expressing the ship’s movement and being brought to rest in the name of The God, or expressing the time of its movement and settlement.

These differences do not change the basic meaning of R S W.

Wa-istawat ʿalā al-jūdiyyi

Later in the story, Hūd 11:44 states:

وَقِيلَ يَا أَرْضُ ابْلَعِي مَاءَكِ وَيَا سَمَاءُ أَقْلِعِي وَغِيضَ الْمَاءُ وَقُضِيَ الْأَمْرُ وَاسْتَوَتْ عَلَى الْجُودِيِّ

wa-qīla yā arḍu iblaʿī māʾaki wa-yā samāʾu aqliʿī wa-ghīḍa al-māʾu wa-quḍiya al-amru wa-istawat ʿalā al-jūdiyyi

Hūd 11:44

“And it was said: Earth, swallow your water; sky, cease. The water subsided, the matter was concluded, and the ship settled upon al-Jūdī.”

Istawat belongs to S W Y, not R S W.

Contextually, the ship’s settling upon al-Jūdī relates to its coming to rest.

Hūd 11:41 discusses the ship’s movement and settlement.

Hūd 11:44 describes that movement’s outcome and the place where the ship settles.

This connection presents the ship’s settlement as an actual event in the story.

It does not make mursāhā lexically mean al-Jūdī.

Al-Jūdī names the location in another verse; mursā concerns settlement or its location.

Does the ship’s settlement mean every passenger is certainly saved?

Noah’s ship is associated with saving a group of people and creatures in the story.

Mursāhā does not lexically mean salvation.

Rescue, faith, drowning, and individuals’ fates emerge from other parts of the story.

R S W itself should therefore not be made to mean rescue, salvation, or divine shelter.

The ship’s settlement is indeed part of divine blessing and mercy in the story, but that contextual feature should not replace the word’s basic meaning.

6. Ayyāna mursāhā: when the Hour occurs

The other two Qur’anic instances of mursāhā concern the Hour.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:187 states:

يَسْأَلُونَكَ عَنِ السَّاعَةِ أَيَّانَ مُرْسَاهَا قُلْ إِنَّمَا عِلْمُهَا عِنْدَ رَبِّي لَا يُجَلِّيهَا لِوَقْتِهَا إِلَّا هُوَ

yasʾalūnaka ʿani al-sāʿati ayyāna mursāhā qul innamā ʿilmuhā ʿinda rabbī lā yujallīhā li-waqtihā illā huwa

Al-Aʿrāf 7:187

“They ask you about the Hour: when will it occur? Say: Knowledge of it is only with my Lord; none but He reveals it at its time.”

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:42 also states:

يَسْأَلُونَكَ عَنِ السَّاعَةِ أَيَّانَ مُرْسَاهَا

yasʾalūnaka ʿani al-sāʿati ayyāna mursāhā

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:42

“They ask you about the Hour: when will it occur?”

In both verses, mursāhā concerns al-sāʿah, the Hour.

From spatial settlement to occurrence in time

In the ship’s case, mursā concerns its stopping and settling.

The Hour, however, is not a ship or object moving through water and then anchoring.

The root is therefore applied here to an event’s occurrence and realization.

Ayyāna asks about time.

Ayyāna mursāhā in this setting therefore asks when the Hour will arise or occur.

An early explanation compares this use to a ship settling: just as a moving ship comes to rest somewhere, an event’s occurrence can be expressed through rusūw and settlement.

In that explanation, the Hour arising does not mean a person’s bodily standing; it means its realization and manifestation.

This fits the verse’s context.

Mursāhā as the appointed time of occurrence

Mursāhā here can concern the time of the Hour’s settlement or realization.

The word alone does not give a particular date.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:187 asks about its time and responds by assigning knowledge of it exclusively to The God.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:187 also continues:

لَا تَأْتِيكُمْ إِلَّا بَغْتَةً

lā taʾtīkum illā baghtatan

Al-Aʿrāf 7:187

“It comes upon you only suddenly.”

The verse thus speaks both of exclusive divine knowledge of its time and of its sudden arrival for those addressed.

Mursā does not imply that humans can calculate the Hour through celestial movements, historical cycles, or a hidden code.

The verse itself endorses no such claim.

Ilā rabbika muntahāhā

Following the question about the Hour, Al-Nāziʿāt 79:43 and Al-Nāziʿāt 79:44 state:

فِيمَ أَنْتَ مِنْ ذِكْرَاهَا ۝ إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ مُنْتَهَاهَا

fīma anta min dhikrāhā / ilā rabbika muntahāhā

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:43; Al-Nāziʿāt 79:44

“What have you to do with recalling its time? To your Lord belongs its ultimate end.”

This response shows that determining the Hour’s time is not in the audience’s hands.

Muntahāhā belongs to N H Y and here concerns an end or limit.

It is semantically related to mursāhā but is not from the same root.

Mursāhā should therefore not be translated as end in every Arabic use.

For the ship, mursā concerns stopping and settling; for the Hour, the time it arises and occurs.

Context establishes the difference.

Does the Hour anchor somewhere?

The verse does not establish that understanding.

Rusūw concerning a ship has a clear spatial meaning.

The expression about the Hour concerns an event in time.

The physical image of a ship anchoring should therefore not be imposed on the Hour.

Nor does this use establish a theory of time stopping, the earth becoming stationary, or every celestial body ceasing to move.

The verse asks when the Hour will occur and arise, without explaining its physical mechanism.

Mursāhā here therefore concerns the event’s realization and definite arrival; the root alone establishes neither its manner nor its precise time.

7. Comparison with related roots

R S W (rusūw) and R S KH (rusūkh)

R S KH also concerns firmness and stability.

Āl ʿImrān 3:7 includes:

وَالرَّاسِخُونَ فِي الْعِلْمِ

wa-al-rāsikhūna fī al-ʿilmi

Āl ʿImrān 3:7

“And those firmly grounded in knowledge.”

Rusūkh here concerns knowledge becoming established and firm within someone.

R S W also expresses settlement and stability.

The two roots differ in structure and range of use.

Qur’anic R S KH concerns firmness in knowledge; R S W concerns mountains, pots, ships, and the Hour’s occurrence.

Al-rāsikhūna fī al-ʿilmi should therefore not be assigned to R S W through semantic resemblance.

Nor does that resemblance make scholarly firmness precisely the same as a mountain standing in the earth.

Both involve stability, but their subjects and kinds of use differ.

Study of R S KH

R S W (rusūw) and R S S (rass)

Arabic R S S also concerns firmness and becoming established.

It is a doubled root, with sīn as both its second and third radicals.

R S W instead has wāw as its third radical.

Qur’anic R S S occurs only in the designation the people of al-Rass; R S W describes mountains, ships, pots, and the Hour’s occurrence.

Similarity in stability does not merge these roots.

Nor should rass meaning well be equated with marsā meaning a ship’s resting place.

Each requires its own evidence.

Study of R S S

R S W (rusūw) and TH B T (thabāt)

Arabic thabata means being firm and established.

It is therefore very close to rasā in meaning.

Al-Baqarah 2:250 includes:

وَثَبِّتْ أَقْدَامَنَا

wa-thabbit aqdāmanā

Al-Baqarah 2:250

“And make our feet firm.”

Firmness here concerns human steadfastness and maintaining one’s position.

Arabic rasat aqdāmuhum can likewise describe their feet remaining firm.

The roots therefore overlap in some uses for human steadfastness.

There should not be an absolute division whereby R S W always means physical stability and TH B T always means inward stability.

Both have differing uses.

In the Qur’an, R S W is particularly prominent in describing the settlement of things and events.

Study of TH B T

R S W (rusūw) and Q R R (qarār)

Qarār concerns settling, becoming still, and having a place of rest.

Al-Naml 27:61 states:

أَمَّنْ جَعَلَ الْأَرْضَ قَرَارًا وَجَعَلَ خِلَالَهَا أَنْهَارًا وَجَعَلَ لَهَا رَوَاسِيَ

amman jaʿala al-arḍa qarāran wa-jaʿala khilālahā anhāran wa-jaʿala lahā rawāsiya

Al-Naml 27:61

“Or who made the earth a resting place, placed rivers through it, and made for it firm mountains?”

Qarāran and rawāsiya occur in one setting.

The first presents the earth as a resting place; the second concerns firm mountains.

Their juxtaposition shows semantic proximity without making them one word with an identical complete meaning.

The earth as a resting place comes from qarāran, and the mountains’ quality from rawāsi.

Noah’s ship also settles upon al-Jūdī, but the location and manner of settling come from the whole sentence, not R S W itself.

Study of Q R R

R S W (rusūw) and W T D (watad)

Arabic watad names a peg or device that holds something in place.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:7 states:

وَالْجِبَالَ أَوْتَادًا

wa-al-jibāla awtādan

Al-Nabaʾ 78:7

“And the mountains as pegs.”

This is conceptually related to wa-al-jibāla arsāhā in Al-Nāziʿāt 79:32.

One describes mountains as awtād; the other expresses establishing them firmly.

Watad and rasā are nevertheless lexically different.

Watad names something used for securing; rasā expresses becoming settled.

Combining these verses does not establish precise mountain roots, their underground dimensions, or the mechanisms of every geological phenomenon without independent scientific evidence.

The linguistic image should be retained without turning it into a definite scientific claim extending beyond the text.

R S W (rusūw) and S W Y (istiwāʾ)

Arabic istiwāʾ has various uses, including becoming straight or level, reaching completion, or settling upon something.

Hūd 11:44 includes:

وَاسْتَوَتْ عَلَى الْجُودِيِّ

wa-istawat ʿalā al-jūdiyyi

Hūd 11:44

“And it settled upon al-Jūdī.”

This concerns the ship’s stopping, but istawat belongs to S W Y.

It should therefore not be counted as a morphological occurrence of R S W.

The story presents the ship’s settlement from two angles: mursāhā in Hūd 11:41 and istawat ʿalā al-jūdiyyi in Hūd 11:44.

They concern a connected event but have different roots and morphological structures.

Study of S W Y

8. Mountains’ firmness and their transformation at the resurrection

An important test of the definition of R S W is the verses in which mountains change.

The Qur’an calls mountains rawāsi and also says about the resurrection in Al-Kahf 18:47:

وَيَوْمَ نُسَيِّرُ الْجِبَالَ

wa-yawma nusayyiru al-jibāla

Al-Kahf 18:47

“And the day We set the mountains in motion.”

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:5 and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:6 also state:

وَبُسَّتِ الْجِبَالُ بَسًّا ۝ فَكَانَتْ هَبَاءً مُنْبَثًّا

wa-bussati al-jibālu bassan / fa-kānat habāʾan munbaththan

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:5; Al-Wāqiʿah 56:6

“And the mountains are utterly crushed, becoming scattered dust.”

These verses show that the mountains’ firmness should not mean their absolute indestructibility.

A mountain can be firm in the earth’s familiar condition and transformed in another event.

These propositions are not linguistically inconsistent.

A building, for example, can be firm yet perish under very different conditions.

Firmness does not mean that change is absolutely impossible.

Zuhayr’s depiction of mountains as enduring witnesses the use of rawāsi, but the poet’s belief in their permanence should not override the Qur’an’s account of their transformation.

The final definition should therefore accommodate both mountain stability in creation verses and mountain transformation at the resurrection without contradiction.

9. Translation proposals for Forqan

Translation requires distinguishing the description of firmness, the act of making firm, and the place or time of settlement.

رَسَا: Rasā: settled, became firm, took its place.

يَرْسُو: Yarsū: settles, becomes fixed in place.

رُسُوّ: Rusūw: settlement, becoming fixed; of a ship, stopping and anchoring.

رَاسٍ: Rāsin: firm, settled, established.

رَاسِيَة: Rāsiyah: firm and fixed; of mountains, a firm mountain; of pots, a pot remaining fixed in place.

رَوَاسِي: Rawāsi: firm mountains, in the Qur’anic uses concerning the earth.

رَاسِيَات: Rāsiyāt: firmly established things; in Sabaʾ 34:13, a description of pots.

أَرْسَى: Arsā: made firm or settled; of a ship, brought it to rest and settled it.

إِرْسَاء: Irsāʾ: making firm or settled; in maritime use, anchoring and bringing a ship to rest.

مَرْسَى: Marsā: a resting place; the time or place of stopping, according to use.

مُرْسَى: Mursā: settlement or bringing to rest; in the relevant constructions, a time or place of settlement or something brought to rest.

مِرْسَاة: Mirsāh: an anchor, a device holding a ship in place.

مَرَاسِي: Marāsī: anchors or places of settlement, according to context.

The mountain verses

وَجَعَلَ فِيهَا رَوَاسِيَ

wa-jaʿala fīhā rawāsiya

Al-Raʿd 13:3

“And He placed in it firm mountains.”

وَجَعَلْنَا فِيهَا رَوَاسِيَ شَامِخَاتٍ

wa-jaʿalnā fīhā rawāsiya shāmikhātin

Al-Mursalāt 77:27

“And We placed in it firm, lofty mountains.”

وَالْجِبَالَ أَرْسَاهَا

wa-al-jibāla arsāhā

Al-Nāziʿāt 79:32

“And the mountains He established firmly.”

Firm and established firmly are natural equivalents in these uses.

Rawāsi should not be directly translated as the earth’s anchors, giant pegs of the earth, or stabilizers of tectonic plates.

Such images may arise in studying other verses or scientific research, but basic translation should rest on the word’s clear meaning.

Sabaʾ 34:13

وَقُدُورٍ رَاسِيَاتٍ

wa-qudūrin rāsiyātin

Sabaʾ 34:13

“And firmly set cooking pots.”

An explanation can add that these were large pots that stayed in place and were not easily moved.

Size and weight should not be added to rāsiyāt so as to make every use necessarily describe a large object.

Hūd 11:41

بِسْمِ اللَّهِ مَجْرَاهَا وَمُرْسَاهَا

bi-smi allāhi majrāhā wa-mursāhā

Hūd 11:41

“In the name of The God are its course and its coming to rest.”

Or, more fluently:

“In the name of The God, when it moves and comes to rest.”

Precise syntax and the meaning of majrāhā must follow the selected reading.

Here mursāhā concerns the ship’s settlement, not the time of the Hour.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:187 and Al-Nāziʿāt 79:42

يَسْأَلُونَكَ عَنِ السَّاعَةِ أَيَّانَ مُرْسَاهَا

yasʾalūnaka ʿani al-sāʿati ayyāna mursāhā

Al-Aʿrāf 7:187; Al-Nāziʿāt 79:42

“They ask you about the Hour: when will it occur?”

This rendering fits both verses’ contexts.

Mursāhā here concerns the time the Hour occurs and arises.

When will it anchor? reflects a physical image, but is unsuitable as a natural translation: the Hour is not a ship, and the question concerns when it occurs.

10. Conclusion

Arabic R S W denotes becoming fixed, settling, and remaining firm in place.

This appears in physical uses such as a firm mountain, a ship at rest, a fixed pot, and a peg secured in the earth.

Early Arabic also uses this family for feet standing firm in confrontation, clouds remaining, camels settling, and even reconciling people.

Poetic witnesses likewise show rawāsi for firm mountains and tarsū for human steadfastness.

The Qur’an uses the root fourteen times in fourteen verses.

Its most extensive use concerns mountains. Rawāsi describes firm mountains in nine verses, and arsāhā in Al-Nāziʿāt 79:32 expresses establishing them firmly.

Some verses connect this firmness with preventing mayd, the earth’s swaying, but that does not deny all movement of the earth or establish a precise geological theory.

Rāsiyāt in Sabaʾ 34:13 describes large, fixed cooking pots in Solomon’s story, showing the word can describe manufactured objects as well as mountains.

Mursāhā in Hūd 11:41 concerns Noah’s ship, its movement and stopping.

The same form concerns the Hour in Al-Aʿrāf 7:187 and Al-Nāziʿāt 79:42. These ask about the time the Hour occurs and arises, rather than a physical object’s spatial settlement.

The shared root meaning should therefore be preserved without transferring each use’s physical details into the others.

Firmness is not eternal duration either. The Qur’an describes mountains as firm and also depicts their transformation and collapse at the resurrection.

Rāsī therefore should not mean something that never changes or perishes.

Comparison with R S KH, R S S, TH B T, Q R R, and W T D should likewise distinguish their shared stability from differing word structures and uses.

Final definition: R S W denotes settling, becoming fixed, and remaining firm in a place or condition. Rasā expresses becoming settled; arsā expresses making something settled and firm. Qur’anic uses concern firm mountains, fixed cooking pots, a ship coming to rest, and the time the Hour occurs. The kind of settlement, its means, duration, effects, and physical manner must be understood from the word form and context rather than a single physical image imposed on every derivative.
