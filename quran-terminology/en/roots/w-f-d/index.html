Coming into someone’s presence, an arriving delegation, and honored reception

In its human application, the early Arabic root w-f-d concerns coming before someone of standing, such as a king or ruler. A wāfid is someone who comes for a visit, to convey a message, or to pursue a matter; wafd denotes a company of such arrivals. This presence commonly involves a purpose and a recognized capacity, so wafd is not identical to any crowd that happens to enter a place.

Arabic also records branches of the root concerning advancing, rising, and being prominent. These connect with the image of coming forward and becoming visible, but historical evidence is needed before claiming that they all developed from one original meaning.

The root occurs in the Qur’an only as وَفْدًا in Maryam 19:85, which describes gathering the God-conscious toward the Entirely Merciful.

1. The root in early and classical Arabic

Accounts of pre-Islamic Arabian society describe tribal leaders and representatives visiting rulers. Groups might seek assistance, address a dispute, convey a message, establish relations, or pursue tribal affairs. Reports of a group of Banū ʿĀmir visiting al-Nuʿmān ibn al-Mundhir with the young Labīd ibn Rabīʿah illustrate these relationships. These reports survive in works written centuries later, however, and do not independently establish that every expression in the narrative was used at that earlier date.

Early dictionaries’ definition of wāfid fits this social setting: someone coming to a king on behalf of a group to raise a matter or pursue an undertaking. Wafd therefore involves more than movement between places; the manner of presence and the relationship to the person approached also matter.

A verse attributed to Tamīm ibn Muqbil, who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, states:

إِلَّا الْإِفَادَةَ فَاسْتَوَلَتْ رَكَائِبُنَا
عِنْدَ الْجَبَابِيرِ بِالْبَأْسَاءِ وَالنِّعَمِ

illā al-ifādata fa-stawlat rakāʾibunā / ʿinda al-jabābīri bi-al-baʾsāʾi wa-al-niʿami

Here al-ifādah is recorded as an alternative form of al-wifādah in the sense of coming into someone’s presence. The reference to rulers, hardship, and favors places it within that social setting. Its secure lexical value is this use of ifādah; the line alone should not be made to establish the details of a return journey or the outcome of a particular mission.

Tamīm ibn Muqbil lived both before and after the emergence of Islam. Since the date of this verse is not independently established, it is not decisive evidence of pre-Qur’anic usage, although it is relevant to language near the period of revelation.

Another branch appears in a poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Aʿshā:

رَأَتْ رَجُلًا غَائِرَ الْوَافِدَيْنِ
مُخْتَلِفَ اللَّوْنِ أَعْشَى ضَرِيرَا

raʾat rajulan ghāʾira al-wāfidayni / mukhtalifa al-lawni aʿshā ḍarīrā

Translation: “She saw a man with sunken cheekbones, altered in complexion, dim-sighted and afflicted in vision.”

Al-wāfidān here denotes the two prominent cheekbones. This differs from the presence of a delegation and shows that the early root family was not confined to social relationships. The exact date of composition has not been independently established, although attribution to the pre-Islamic poet is well known.

Lexicons also record wafd for the high, conspicuous crest of a sand ridge, and wāfid for an animal that moves ahead of the others. These concern prominence, coming forward, and taking the lead. Some lexicographers identify this image as the root’s shared core, but that analysis does not establish the historical sequence of meanings.

For the human application, the clear sense “coming before someone of standing, commonly for a particular purpose or mission” provides a useful basis. Evidence for dating every detail of that meaning to a specific pre-Qur’anic period is not equally secure.

2. Forms and semantic core

Important recorded Arabic forms include:

  • وَفَدَ: he came into someone’s presence or arrived.
  • وَافِد: someone who comes before another; socially, a representative or envoy.
  • وَفْد: a company of arrivals or a delegation coming before someone.
  • وُفُود: delegations or arriving companies.
  • وِفَادَة: coming into someone’s presence or visiting a person of standing.
  • أَوْفَدَ: he sent someone before another; in another branch, something rose or became prominent.
  • تَوَفَّدَ: in some lexical usages, coming forward, taking the lead, or becoming prominent.

These forms do not all occur in the Qur’an. The family’s sole Qur’anic form is وَفْدًا.

Two recorded fields should be distinguished: arrival before a person, and coming forward, becoming prominent, or moving ahead. A shared image can be discerned, but the Qur’anic word must be interpreted through the meaning appropriate to its form and context.

3. Qur’anic use: wafd in Maryam

Maryam 19:85

يَوْمَ نَحْشُرُ الْمُتَّقِينَ إِلَى الرَّحْمَٰنِ وَفْدًا

yawma naḥshuru al-muttaqīna ilā al-Raḥmāni wafdā

Maryam 19:85

Translation: “On the day We gather the God-conscious toward the Entirely Merciful as a delegation.”

وَفْدًا is accusative here and expresses the condition of the God-conscious as they are gathered. Morphologically, it has been analyzed as a verbal noun used collectively or as a collective noun; it has also been treated as a plural of wāfid. These analyses all point to a company of arrivals rather than a single person.

نَحْشُرُ expresses gathering. الْمُتَّقِينَ identifies those gathered, and إِلَى الرَّحْمَٰنِ gives the direction of the gathering. وَفْدًا describes the manner of their presence.

In human relationships, an aimless crowd differs from a delegation coming before someone important. A delegation may be respectfully received, accommodated, and have its requests considered. Wafd evokes such a recognized, purposeful presence.

The Qur’an uses this familiar image for the God-conscious at the resurrection. They are presented as a delegation gathered toward the Entirely Merciful, rather than an anonymous crowd. The association with al-muttaqīn and al-Raḥmān conveys their honored reception in this scene.

Nevertheless, not every feature of human delegations should be transferred to the verse. A political wafd may represent others or petition a king. The verse does not say that the God-conscious represent another group or assemble for a political mission. Such features belong to familiar social uses, not to the verse’s direct statement.

Some early interpretations also describe wafdan as honored riders. Delegations arriving on mounts were familiar, but وَفْدًا itself does not name a mount or specify a mode of travel. The verse gives no further description of their means of conveyance.

4. Wafd and wird in consecutive verses

The following verse is particularly important for understanding وَفْدًا.

Maryam 19:86

وَنَسُوقُ الْمُجْرِمِينَ إِلَىٰ جَهَنَّمَ وِرْدًا

wa-nasūqu al-mujrimīna ilā Jahannama wirdā

Maryam 19:86

Translation: “And We drive the offenders toward Hell, thirsty.”

The two verses place distinct images side by side:

In verse 85, the God-conscious are gathered toward the Entirely Merciful as a delegation. In verse 86, the offenders are driven toward Hell in the condition described as wirdan.

وَفْدًا belongs to w-f-d, whereas وِرْدًا belongs to w-r-d. Their resemblance in writing and sound does not make them identical in meaning.

W-r-d concerns reaching water or a watering place. Arabic wird is associated with a company coming for water and with thirst. In Maryam 19:86, thirst is prominent in the offenders’ movement toward Hell.

Study of w-r-d

The contrast therefore involves both destination and manner of arrival: one group is gathered as a delegation toward the Entirely Merciful; the other is driven toward Hell in thirst.

This should not become an absolute rule about the verbs ḥashr and sawq. Al-Zumar 39:73 also speaks of the God-conscious being driven toward the Garden. Sawq itself does not invariably mean humiliation or punishment. In Maryam, the distinction emerges from the verb, the people, the destination, and their stated condition together.

5. Challenges in understanding and translation

One challenge is that the Persian equivalent heyʾat, like English “delegation,” often has an administrative or political coloring today. Readers may picture ambassadors or government officials. Arabic wafd is not confined to modern political institutions; it can encompass a company intentionally coming before someone important.

“Honored guests” also conveys part of the image well, emphasizing respect and hospitality. Yet “guest” does not always convey everything in wāfid: someone may be a guest without arriving as part of a delegation or for a formal audience.

Another difficulty is transferring every feature of a human delegation to the God-conscious at the resurrection. The word does not establish the number in each group, the kinds of mounts, the arrangement of rows, or the precise manner of presence before The God. The verse directly states their gathering as wafd toward the Entirely Merciful.

Modern usage sometimes narrows wafd to a political or diplomatic delegation. That restriction should not determine its Qur’anic meaning.

6. Comparison with related roots

W-f-d (wafd) and w-r-d (wurūd): a delegation’s presence and arrival

W-r-d principally concerns reaching or arriving, especially in connection with water. Human w-f-d emphasizes coming before someone in a recognized capacity. A group can arrive somewhere without being a wafd. The contrast between Maryam 19:85 and Maryam 19:86 is particularly useful in distinguishing them.

W-f-d (wafd) and ḥ-sh-r (ḥashr): coming before someone and gathering

Ḥashr denotes gathering beings or people. Wafd describes a particular kind of collective presence. In Maryam 19:85, naḥshuru expresses the gathering, while wafdan specifies the condition of those gathered. They complement one another rather than functioning as synonyms.

W-f-d (wafd) and q-d-m (qudūm): arrival and arrival in a recognized capacity

Qudūm concerns coming or arriving and can apply to an individual or a group. Human wafd adds the image of presence before someone important and sometimes representation of others. Every wāfid arrives, but not every arrival is necessarily a wāfid.

W-f-d (wafd) and w-f-ḍ (īfāḍ): presence and rapid movement

W-f-ḍ concerns haste and rapid movement. In Al-Maʿārij 70:43, يُوفِضُونَ describes hurrying.

W-f-d and w-f-ḍ look similar but are separate roots. Although some non-Qur’anic uses of w-f-d also involve taking the lead or haste, that overlap does not make them one root. وَفْدًا in Maryam 19:85 does not itself specify the speed of the God-conscious.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

For human وَفَدَ, suitable equivalents include “came into someone’s presence,” “came before someone,” and, where appropriate, “came as a representative.”

وَافِد can be rendered “one arriving,” “one who comes before another,” or “representative,” depending on the person’s role.

For وَفْد, “delegation” and “arriving company” are basic equivalents. Where the context emphasizes respect and reception, “honored delegation” is also suitable.

The proposed rendering of Maryam 19:85 is:

“On the day We gather the God-conscious toward the Entirely Merciful as a delegation.”

This preserves the collective sense of wafd. Honor and respectful reception, understood from the familiar image and context, can be clarified in the explanation.

“Honored guests” is useful for explaining the scene, but retaining “delegation” in the base translation helps preserve the lexical distinction.

8. Conclusion

In its human use, w-f-d concerns coming before someone of standing. A wāfid comes into another’s presence; wafd is a company of such arrivals. Representation, a mission, a request, or a visit may accompany this presence, but none of these subsidiary features is obligatory in every occurrence.

Other branches record advancing, rising, and being prominent. They can be connected at the level of imagery, but their historical sequence is uncertain.

In the Qur’an, وَفْدًا occurs only in Maryam 19:85, describing the God-conscious as they are gathered toward the Entirely Merciful. Delegation, alongside al-muttaqīn and the destination al-Raḥmān, creates an image of honored arrival.

The contrast with وِرْدًا in the following verse also matters: one group is gathered as a delegation, while the offenders are driven thirsty toward Hell.

In its Qur’anic use, wafd denotes a company coming before someone of standing. In Maryam 19:85, it conveys the honored position of the God-conscious in the gathering at the resurrection, without specifying the material or ceremonial details of their arrival.
