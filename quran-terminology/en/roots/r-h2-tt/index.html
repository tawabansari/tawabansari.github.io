A small group, close relatives, and bonds of mutual support

Brief root meaning: A relatively small group, especially men connected by kinship, tribal affiliation, or association. In family contexts, rahṭ denotes close relatives who may provide social support. Other recorded branches include certain leather coverings, clusters of trees, and parts of an animal’s burrow.

1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

The triliteral root R H Ṭ is best known in Arabic for a group of people and their connections.

Rahṭ denotes a relatively small group linked by kinship, tribal affiliation, or association. In a possessive expression such as رَهْطُ فُلَانٍ, it commonly refers to someone’s close relatives and associates.

In Arab tribal life, such a group could protect an individual, deter attacks, and sustain social standing. Membership in a powerful family or group could provide protection unavailable without that connection.

The word’s lexical meaning must nevertheless be distinguished from the group’s social function.

Rahṭ does not itself mean protecting, fighting, or defending someone. It identifies people; whether they support or oppose a person depends on their actual relationships and circumstances.

The Qur’an uses this root in two different settings.

In Shuʿayb’s story, his rahṭ are relatives whose standing deters his opponents from directly attacking him.

In Ṣāliḥ’s story, nine men spread corruption in the city and plot a night attack on him and his family.

Rahṭ therefore does not inherently denote righteous or oppressive people. Their conduct determines their moral character.

Forms examined and Qur’anic occurrences

R H Ṭ occurs three times in three Qur’anic verses, always as the noun rahṭ:

  • رَهْطُكَ: Your kin or close group; Hūd 11:91.
  • أَرَهْطِي: Are my kin…?; Hūd 11:92.
  • رَهْطٍ: A group of people; Al-Naml 27:48.

No verb from this root occurs in the Qur’an.

Other Arabic forms and uses include:

  • رَهْط: A small group; in family contexts, close relatives.
  • أَرْهُط: One plural of rahṭ.
  • أَرْهَاط: Another plural.
  • أَرَاهِط: A plural recorded in early poetry and lexical works.
  • رَهْطِيّ: Related to a rahṭ.
  • ارْتِهَاط: In an early usage, being or gathering in groups.
  • تَرْهِيط: In another branch, taking large mouthfuls and eating vigorously.
  • رِهَاط: Leather coverings made of strips; recorded both as a plural and, in another account, a singular noun.
  • الرَّاهِطَاء: A part of a jerboa’s burrow.
  • رَهْطُ الشَّجَر: In certain usages, a cluster of closely growing trees or shrubs.

These forms illuminate the broader lexical family, but only rahṭ in different constructions appears in the Qur’an.

2. Core meaning and early Arabic usage

People gathering: A central meaning

An early lexical analysis proposes the following principle for R H Ṭ:

الرَّاءُ وَالْهَاءُ وَالطَّاءُ أَصْلٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى تَجَمُّعٍ فِي النَّاسِ وَغَيْرِهِمْ

al-rāʾu wa-al-hāʾu wa-al-ṭāʾu aṣlun yadullu ʿalā tajammuʿin fī al-nāsi wa-ghayrihim

Translation: “Rāʾ, hāʾ, and ṭāʾ indicate gathering among people and other things.”

This analysis places gathering and forming a group at the center of the family.

Not every large gathering, however, is called a rahṭ.

In familiar usage, rahṭ more often denotes a limited group; in family or tribal contexts, closeness and affiliation matter.

“Population” alone is therefore inadequate: a population may number thousands without close family or social ties between its members.

Conversely, kinship is not essential to every use. A group may be called rahṭ without the verse or sentence specifying family relationships.

How many people constitute a rahṭ?

Early Arabic lexicons give several numerical descriptions.

One defines it as three to ten people.

Another gives seven to ten and uses nafar for smaller groups.

Some accounts allow a broader range.

These differences show that rahṭ is not an entirely fixed numerical unit.

It generally suggests a relatively small group, but its precise size cannot be established without context.

For example, رَهْطُكَ in Hūd 11:91 does not disclose exactly how many relatives Shuʿayb had.

Al-Naml 27:48, by contrast, explicitly gives the number nine.

That count comes from تِسْعَةُ rather than from rahṭ alone.

Rahṭ as a collective noun

Rahṭ is an Arabic collective noun: its form appears singular, but it denotes several people.

Like certain other collective nouns, it has no singular of the same wording for each member.

Rahṭ is not simply the name for one person from which a plural is made by adding a suffix.

Nevertheless, the collective itself has plurals, including أرهط, أرهاط, and أراهط.

Early grammatical discussion notes the unusual formation of arāhiṭ and differing analyses of its relation to rahṭ and arhuṭ.

Such forms show that a noun denoting a group can itself be pluralized.

Being plural in meaning and possessing a morphological plural are distinct matters.

This matters for tisʿatu rahṭ: the noun’s collective meaning does not require the verse to mean nine separate groups.

Does rahṭ refer only to men?

Some early lexical definitions specify a group of men, excluding women from that count.

This describes established usage within certain Arab social and tribal settings.

Many groups responsible for defense, military confrontation, and tribal protection were identified through their male members.

That usage does not mean women were outside family, kinship, or social relationships.

Nor must every member of a person’s family be confined to the particular group called his rahṭ.

Rahṭ may emphasize a group of close kin, while ahl, ʿashīrah, and other terms have broader or different ranges.

Hūd 11:91 concerns Shuʿayb’s social backing.

Al-Naml 27:48 concerns nine corrupt individuals.

Translation must fit each verse’s referent.

A person’s rahṭ: Close kin

An established lexical definition reads:

رَهْطُ الرَّجُلِ: قَوْمُهُ وَقَبِيلَتُهُ

rahṭu al-rajuli qawmuhu wa-qabīlatuhu

Translation: “A man’s rahṭ are his people and tribe.”

The expression رَهْطُهُ دِنْيَةً likewise refers to close relatives.

An early explanation says that “the descendants of so-and-so are this person’s rahṭ” means his nearest relatives.

This background helps explain why Shuʿayb’s kin matter to his opponents.

Even if they regard him as personally weak, attacking him could provoke his relatives.

Such a relationship could deter violence in a tribal society.

Yet kinship does not mean sharing the same beliefs.

Someone may differ from relatives in belief, conduct, or social position while retaining family ties and protection.

It therefore cannot be assumed without evidence that everyone in Shuʿayb’s rahṭ believed his message.

3. Other lexical branches and early poetic evidence

Rahṭ in poetry attributed to Saʿd ibn Mālik

A poem attributed to Saʿd ibn Mālik al-Bakrī, a pre-Islamic notable and poet, contains:

يَا بُؤْسَ لِلْحَرْبِ الَّتِي
وَضَعَتْ أَرَاهِطَ فَاسْتَرَاحُوا

yā buʾsa li-al-ḥarbi allatī / waḍaʿat arāhiṭa fa-istarāḥū

A close rendering: “Woe to the war that brought groups low, and they found rest.” Early explanations differ: some understand disgrace and withdrawal from war, others death and release from worldly life.

The line belongs to a poem about tribal conflict.

Under one early explanation, the poet reproaches groups that withdrew from warfare and preferred ease to confrontation; another reads the line as referring to those killed. Neither interpretation changes arāhiṭ’s basic meaning, “groups.”

Arāhiṭ here denotes groups of men.

The line attests this plural in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period; the attribution and interpretation require their own examination.

Arāhiṭ itself means neither warriors nor cowards.

The poem’s context supplies their conduct; the noun identifies groups of people.

Early accounts attribute the line to Saʿd ibn Mālik, while some give ʿAmr ibn Marthad. The disagreement should be retained when naming its author.

For this root study, the secure point is the recorded use of arāhiṭ in poetry attributed to pre-Islamic times.

Arhuṭ in Ruʾbah ibn al-ʿAjjāj’s poetry

Ruʾbah ibn al-ʿAjjāj says:

هُوَ الذَّلِيلُ نَفَرًا فِي أَرْهُطِهِ

huwa al-dhalīlu nafaran fī arhuṭihi

Translation: “He is lowly in his following among his groups.”

Arhuṭihi refers to groups with which the person is associated.

The pairing of nafar and arhuṭ is significant: both concern people and human groups.

They are nevertheless not identical in every use.

Arhuṭ is a plural of rahṭ, while nafar itself names a group.

Ruʾbah lived in the Umayyad period and into the beginning of the Abbasid period. This is evidence of early Islamic Arabic, not pre-Islamic poetry.

Rahṭ and rihāṭ: Leather coverings

Early sources also record rahṭ as a kind of leather covering.

It was made from pieces or narrow strips of leather and worn by children or women in particular circumstances in some Arabian regions.

Rihāṭ is recorded as its plural; another account treats rihāṭ as a singular with the plural arhiṭah. It should therefore not be labeled uniformly singular or plural.

A verse attributed to Abū al-Muthallam al-Hudhalī illustrates this usage:

مَتَى مَا أَشَأْ غَيْرَ زَهْوِ الْمُلُوكِ
أَجْعَلْكَ رَهْطًا عَلَى حُيَّضِ

matā mā ashaʾ ghayra zahwi al-mulūki / ajʿalka rahṭan ʿalā ḥuyyaḍi

A close rendering: “Whenever I wish, without the vaunting of kings, I shall make you a leather covering upon menstruating women.”

The poet humiliates his addressee through an image involving a covering familiar in that culture.

Rahṭ here does not name a human group.

The same written form can thus denote a material object in another branch.

This does not make the Qur’anic rahṭ a reference to clothing.

The contexts in Hūd and Al-Naml clearly concern people.

Nor can the covering’s name confidently be derived from the human group, or vice versa, without further historical evidence.

Al-rāhiṭāʾ: A part of an animal’s burrow

Lexical sources record al-rāhiṭāʾ for a part of a jerboa’s burrow.

Descriptions differ: some identify an excavated chamber; another identifies earth placed at the burrow’s entrance, leaving a small opening for light.

One account says the animal conceals its young in a chamber.

Some lexical explanations connect the name with gathering or concealment; others compare the entrance’s opening to gaps in a leather covering. These analyses must be distinguished.

Such explanations do not establish the name’s historical origin with certainty.

Rāhiṭāʾ does not occur in the Qur’an, and the burrow’s features should not be transferred to rahṭ in verses about people.

Clusters of trees and other uses

The expression رَهْطُ سِدْرٍ is also recorded for closely growing lote trees.

Here rahṭ denotes a cluster of nearby trees or shrubs.

The use is readily connected with gathering and forming a collection.

Tarhīṭ, by contrast, is recorded for taking large mouthfuls and eating vigorously.

This might be explained through gathering food in the mouth, but that association is not a demonstrated history of derivation.

Together, these attestations show a lexical family wider than the name of a human group.

The Qur’an uses only the branch concerning people.

4. Rahṭuka: Shuʿayb’s kin in Hūd 11:91

The verse

Hūd 11:91

قَالُوا يَا شُعَيْبُ مَا نَفْقَهُ كَثِيرًا مِمَّا تَقُولُ وَإِنَّا لَنَرَاكَ فِينَا ضَعِيفًا ۖ وَلَوْلَا رَهْطُكَ لَرَجَمْنَاكَ ۖ وَمَا أَنْتَ عَلَيْنَا بِعَزِيزٍ

qālū yā shuʿaybu mā nafqahu kathīran mimmā taqūlu wa-innā la-narāka fīnā ḍaʿīfan wa-lawlā rahṭuka la-rajamnāka wa-mā anta ʿalaynā bi-ʿazīzin

Hūd 11:91

Translation:

“They said: Shuʿayb, we do not understand much of what you say, and we consider you weak among us. Were it not for your kin, we would stone you; you have no power over us.”

The meaning of rahṭuka

Rahṭuka consists of the noun rahṭ and the second-person singular suffix -ka.

The suffix refers to Shuʿayb.

Here rahṭ denotes his relatives or close affiliated group.

The following clause explains why they matter.

Shuʿayb’s opponents say:

وَلَوْلَا رَهْطُكَ لَرَجَمْنَاكَ

wa-lawlā rahṭuka la-rajamnāka

Hūd 11:91

That is, were it not for your relatives, we would stone you.

Lawlā shows that the presence of his kin prevents the threatened act.

Their standing therefore makes his opponents take their possible response into account.

This follows from the whole sentence; rahṭ itself does not mean a barrier, protector, or military force.

Personal weakness and the power of kin

The opponents begin by saying:

وَإِنَّا لَنَرَاكَ فِينَا ضَعِيفًا

wa-innā la-narāka fīnā ḍaʿīfan

Hūd 11:91

That is, they regard Shuʿayb as weak among them.

They then mention his kin.

The contrast matters.

They consider him personally unable to deter an attack, yet his family connection prevents them from readily harming him.

Personal capacity and social support are therefore different things.

Someone may lack physical, financial, or political strength while enjoying family or social backing.

The verse makes that distinction clear.

It does not follow that Shuʿayb actually lacked every kind of ability.

La-narāka reports the opponents’ perception and assessment.

The verse quotes their words without necessarily endorsing all their judgments about him.

Wa-lawlā rahṭuka: Social support and deterrence

The threat of stoning shows that opposition to Shuʿayb was more than a calm intellectual disagreement.

His opponents raise the prospect of violence against him.

His relatives’ presence, however, stands in their way.

In such a society, family or tribal affiliation could offer a measure of protection.

Sometimes it deterred an attack through the relatives’ power and possible response rather than recognition of the individual’s rights.

This distinction matters.

People may refrain from oppression because they fear retaliation, even without being committed to justice.

A rahṭ can thus deter an attack without every member sharing the protected person’s beliefs or conduct.

Did Shuʿayb’s kin believe?

The verse does not say that all his relatives accepted his message.

Their protective standing against his opponents is what the passage establishes.

Rahṭuka should therefore not be translated as “your believing followers.”

That would conflate kinship and faith.

Kinship can produce social protection even amid religious disagreement within a family.

Family connection alone is not a Qur’anic criterion of faith or truth.

“Your relatives” is therefore more accurate here than “your followers.”

Wa-mā anta ʿalaynā bi-ʿazīz

At the verse’s end, the opponents say Shuʿayb is not ʿazīz in their eyes.

This follows their reference to his kin.

The distinction between the individual and his relatives is emphasized again.

They deny him the standing or power to deter them, yet his kin possess standing they cannot ignore.

This fits a rendering such as “you have no power over us.”

ʿAzīz has a broader range involving strength, standing, esteem, and difficulty of access.

Power and social standing are connected in this context.

Rahṭ alone does not express those meanings; its relationship to ḍaʿīfan and bi-ʿazīz reveals the group’s social position.

5. A-rahṭī: Shuʿayb’s response concerning kinship and truth

Hūd 11:92

Verse text:

قَالَ يَا قَوْمِ أَرَهْطِي أَعَزُّ عَلَيْكُمْ مِنَ اللَّهِ وَاتَّخَذْتُمُوهُ وَرَاءَكُمْ ظِهْرِيًّا ۖ إِنَّ رَبِّي بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ مُحِيطٌ

qāla yā qawmi a-rahṭī aʿazzu ʿalaykum mina Allāhi wa-ittakhadhtumūhu warāʾakum ẓihriyyan inna rabbī bi-mā taʿmalūna muḥīṭun

Hūd 11:92

Translation:

“He said: My people, are my kin dearer to you than The God, whom you have cast behind your backs? My Lord encompasses what you do.”

The structure of a-rahṭī

A-rahṭī has three components:

The interrogative hamzah a-, the noun rahṭ, and the first-person suffix -ī.

It literally means “Are my kin…?” within the complete question.

Shuʿayb repeats the word his opponents used in the preceding verse.

They had said they refrained from stoning him because of his kin.

He now asks:

أَرَهْطِي أَعَزُّ عَلَيْكُمْ مِنَ اللَّهِ

a-rahṭī aʿazzu ʿalaykum mina Allāhi

Hūd 11:92

That is, are my relatives dearer to you than The God?

The question shifts attention from social power to standards of value and responsibility before The God.

Are relatives dearer than The God?

Shuʿayb challenges their regard for his relatives’ standing at the expense of truth and The God’s command.

They refrain from attacking him, but their stated reason is not respect for his message’s truth.

His rahṭ is what stops them.

He therefore questions their standard of judgment.

If human relatives can deter them, why do they show no comparable regard for The God and the truth conveyed from Him?

The question does not deny the value of family or kinship.

Other Qur’anic verses command respect for relatives’ rights.

Family ties must nevertheless not displace truth and justice.

Social standing and truth

In these two verses, Shuʿayb’s opponents effectively apply two different assessments.

They regard him as weak and insufficiently powerful.

They regard his relatives as influential enough to prevent an attack.

Neither assessment determines whether his message is true.

A socially weak individual can speak truthfully.

A powerful group can support wrongdoing.

Rahṭ therefore does more than name relatives in this narrative: the context also reveals their social power and support.

The word itself, however, means neither absolute power nor a sound argument.

Wa-ittakhadhtumūhu warāʾakum ẓihriyyan

Shuʿayb then rebukes them for casting The God behind their backs.

The expression connects with his preceding question.

They take his relatives seriously while disregarding the truth he conveys from The God.

The issue goes beyond personal disrespect for Shuʿayb to neglecting a divine standard in their conduct.

Hūd 11:84 and Hūd 11:85 also show him calling his people to worship The God, use honest measures and scales, and avoid corruption on earth.

Their opposition concerns economic and social behavior as well as an individual’s standing.

These points come from the narrative context; they are not additional lexical meanings of rahṭ.

Kinship as social backing, not the final criterion of truth

Shuʿayb’s story reveals an important distinction.

Family, communal, or social support can preserve life and deter harm in dangerous circumstances.

The group’s power, however, is not the final measure of right and wrong.

Respecting someone’s rights only out of fear of their family, while denying the same rights to an unsupported person, is not consistent justice.

Such conduct makes rights depend on relatives’ power.

Shuʿayb’s question exposes this faulty standard.

That conclusion emerges from the two verses together, not because rahṭ itself means justice, partisanship, or injustice.

6. Tisʿatu rahṭ: Nine corrupt men in Ṣāliḥ’s story

Al-Naml 27:48

Verse text:

وَكَانَ فِي الْمَدِينَةِ تِسْعَةُ رَهْطٍ يُفْسِدُونَ فِي الْأَرْضِ وَلَا يُصْلِحُونَ

wa-kāna fī al-madīnati tisʿatu rahṭin yufsidūna fī al-arḍi wa-lā yuṣliḥūna

Al-Naml 27:48

Translation:

“There were nine men in the city who spread corruption on earth and did not set things right.”

The meaning of tisʿatu rahṭ

Here rahṭ occurs with the numeral tisʿah.

Tisʿah supplies the number nine; rahṭ denotes the human group.

Grammatically, tisʿatu is the first term of a construct phrase, and rahṭin is its genitive complement.

A collective noun can occur with a numeral in this attested construction; this is not a rule to apply indiscriminately to every collective noun.

In its established use here, tisʿatu rahṭ denotes nine individuals.

Because rahṭ is collective, some exegetes have also suggested nine groups or bands.

Under that reading, each group might have its own members or following.

The reading “nine individuals” nevertheless has clearer support in the construction and context.

Grammatical and exegetical explanations likewise identify nine persons, not necessarily nine independent tribes.

Forqan’s rendering “nine men” is therefore appropriate and defensible.

The expression alone does not establish that each man led a separate tribe or commanded a particular number of followers.

Why not translate rahṭ here as relatives?

In Hūd 11:91 and Hūd 11:92, the noun is connected possessively to Shuʿayb.

Rahṭuka and rahṭī refer to his close group and kin.

Al-Naml 27:48 instead places rahṭ with nine, without a possessive suffix identifying someone’s kin.

The context concerns people in the city engaged in corruption.

It does not state that they were brothers, cousins, or close relatives.

“Nine relatives” would introduce an unspecified qualification.

“Nine men” or “nine individuals forming a group” is preferable.

A possessive or numerical construction can thus give rahṭ different contextual referents.

Yufsidūna fī al-arḍi wa-lā yuṣliḥūn

The verse next identifies the nine men’s conduct.

They spread corruption on earth and do not set things right.

Yufsidūna and yuṣliḥūna come from different roots and are contrasted here.

The group is characterized by corrupt conduct.

Corruption does not come from rahṭ itself.

People can gather to help and repair, or to oppress and corrupt.

Here their actions establish the group’s moral character.

The imperfect yufsidūna can describe ongoing or habitual conduct in this context; the form alone does not establish an uninterrupted or permanent habit.

The context characterizes them through corruption and failure to reform, rather than merely naming an isolated mistake.

Their subsequent plot against Ṣāliḥ and his family further clarifies that description.

Al-Naml 27:49: A joint plan for a night attack

Verse text:

قَالُوا تَقَاسَمُوا بِاللَّهِ لَنُبَيِّتَنَّهُ وَأَهْلَهُ ثُمَّ لَنَقُولَنَّ لِوَلِيِّهِ مَا شَهِدْنَا مَهْلِكَ أَهْلِهِ وَإِنَّا لَصَادِقُونَ

qālū taqāsamū bi-Allāhi la-nubayyitannahu wa-ahlahu thumma la-naqūlanna li-waliyyihi mā shahidnā mahlika ahlihi wa-innā la-ṣādiqūna

Al-Naml 27:49

Translation:

“They said: Swear to one another by The God that we will attack him and his family by night, then tell his guardian: We did not witness his family’s destruction, and we speak the truth.”

This verse explains the conduct of the group introduced immediately before it.

They agree on a night attack against Ṣāliḥ and his family.

They also prepare to deny responsibility.

Their corruption therefore extends beyond verbal opposition.

They coordinate violence and concealment of the truth.

Their small number does not prevent them from being effective.

Nine people acting together can seriously threaten an individual and a family.

That follows from their conduct, not from any inherent meaning of rahṭ as conspirators.

Rahṭ and ahl in the same narrative

Al-Naml 27:48 uses rahṭ for the group of nine.

Al-Naml 27:49 uses ahl for Ṣāliḥ’s family.

The distinction matters.

Rahṭ in the first verse identifies people engaged in common corrupt conduct.

Ahl in the next refers to Ṣāliḥ’s family or close household.

Although the words can overlap in other human settings, they play different roles here.

Verse 49 also mentions his walī, a person with standing to inquire or make a claim concerning Ṣāliḥ and his family’s fate.

Social bonds and obligations toward relatives matter here too, but rahṭ alone cannot establish the society’s entire legal or tribal structure.

Were these nine men leaders of Ṣāliḥ’s people?

Some early exegetical accounts call them chiefs or notables.

Names are also transmitted for them.

The names and details, however, vary.

Al-Naml 27:48 itself gives the expression tisʿatu rahṭ and describes their conduct.

It does not separately call them malaʾ, chiefs, or nobles.

They may have possessed influence, but the verse does not fully specify their authority, office, or individual tribal affiliations.

Nor does their presence make everyone in the city equally involved in the plot.

Al-Naml 27:53 explicitly mentions saving those who believed and were mindful of The God.

The corrupt group’s responsibility must therefore be distinguished from everyone else’s position.

A small group’s role in social corruption

Al-Naml 27:48 characterizes nine individuals through corruption.

The following verse shows them coordinating violence and concealment.

Social corruption need not involve every member of a society directly participating in the same act.

A limited group can play a substantial role in producing or spreading it.

Access to resources, influence, or violent means can make even a small group dangerous.

The example does not make every small or closely connected group inherently threatening.

Being a group is a social condition.

Its moral character depends on its members’ actions.

In Hūd 11:91, Shuʿayb’s kin deter an attack; in Al-Naml 27:48, the nine men belong to the group that plots against Ṣāliḥ.

The contrast confirms that rahṭ carries no fixed moral judgment by itself.

7. Related roots, translation proposals, and conclusions

R H Ṭ (rahṭ) and Q W M (qawm)

Qawm has a broad Qur’anic range, including a people or community with historical, social, or territorial connections.

Rahṭ in familiar usage is narrower and more often denotes a small group.

In Hūd 11:92, Shuʿayb says “my people” and then speaks of “my kin.”

Both terms occur in one verse without identifying the same set of people.

Qawm is his wider audience.

Rahṭ is his close kin whom his opponents take seriously.

The difference in scope is clear here.

It does not follow that qawm always means a large group or rahṭ exactly nine people.

Numbers and relationships must be determined separately in each use.

R H Ṭ (rahṭ) and N F R (nafar)

Nafar also denotes a relatively small group.

Early descriptions of the numbers covered by nafar and rahṭ sometimes overlap.

Some lexicographers distinguish them numerically, but the accounts are not uniform.

In Al-Kahf 18:34, أَعَزُّ نَفَرًا concerns superiority in the numbers or strength of one’s following.

Rahṭ can likewise identify people who provide social backing.

Shared associations with numbers and support do not make the words synonymous in every context.

The broader N F R family also involves movement and setting out, whereas rahṭ more directly identifies the group and its connections.

R H Ṭ (rahṭ) and ʿ Sh R (ʿashīrah)

ʿAshīrah denotes a person’s kin or clan.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:214 contains عَشِيرَتَكَ الْأَقْرَبِينَ, “your nearest relatives.”

Rahṭ in family contexts can similarly denote close kin.

Early lexical explanations use these terms to explain one another.

ʿAshīrah can nevertheless have a wider social scope than a small band.

Rahṭ, in familiar numerical descriptions, usually denotes a limited group.

The precise distinction depends on context and the relevant social structure.

In Shuʿayb’s story, rahṭī denotes his close supporting group; “my kin” conveys its main meaning.

R H Ṭ (rahṭ) and ʾ H L (ahl)

Ahl has a broad Qur’anic range.

It can denote family, household members, inhabitants of a community, or people associated with a subject.

Ahl al-bayt, ahl al-qaryah, and ahl al-kitāb, for example, have different referents.

Family uses of rahṭ can overlap with ahl without covering all its meanings.

Ṣāliḥ’s story uses rahṭ in Al-Naml 27:48 and ahlahu in Al-Naml 27:49.

The Qur’an uses distinct terms for the corrupt group and the threatened person’s family.

Rahṭ should therefore not always be translated as ahl or “family.”

R H Ṭ (rahṭ) and F Ṣ L (faṣīlah)

Faṣīlah refers to close kin and a branch of a family or tribe.

Al-Maʿārij 70:13 contains فَصِيلَتِهِ الَّتِي تُؤْوِيهِ, “his kin who shelter him.”

The context concerns relatives to whom a person belongs and among whom he can find refuge and support.

This is close to rahṭ in Shuʿayb’s story.

Faṣīlah and rahṭ nevertheless have different forms and lexical histories.

Some lexical classifications place faṣīlah closer to the individual than rahṭ.

Such classifications should not be treated as a uniform system for every tribal society.

R H Ṭ (rahṭ) and M L ʾ (malaʾ)

Malaʾ often denotes notables, influential people, or those with social standing in the Qur’an.

In prophetic narratives, such groups sometimes oppose the message of truth.

A rahṭ may also consist of influential people.

The word itself, however, does not mean nobles or rulers.

The nine men in Al-Naml 27:48 may have had influence, but the verse does not call them malaʾ.

Political authority or social leadership should not be added to R H Ṭ’s definition without evidence.

Translation proposals for Forqan

رَهْطُكَ — Hūd 11:91

Suitable equivalents include “your kin,” “your close relatives,” or “your family group.”

“Were it not for your kin, we would stone you” naturally renders وَلَوْلَا رَهْطُكَ لَرَجَمْنَاكَ.

It preserves the family connection and the group’s contextual social role.

أَرَهْطِي — Hūd 11:92

A suitable rendering begins “Are my kin…?”

“Are my kin dearer to you than The God?” suitably renders أَرَهْطِي أَعَزُّ عَلَيْكُمْ مِنَ اللَّهِ.

The question is retained, and the suffix -ī refers to Shuʿayb.

تِسْعَةُ رَهْطٍ — Al-Naml 27:48

For the complete phrase, suitable renderings include “nine men” or “nine individuals.” Nine comes from tisʿah, not from rahṭ alone.

“There were nine men in the city who spread corruption on earth and did not set things right” is defensible in meaning and context.

“Nine tribes” or “nine clans” would change the phrase’s scope without sufficient support.

Suggested word-box wording for Forqan

Root: R H Ṭ

Brief root meaning: A relatively small group; in family contexts, close kin and people affiliated with an individual.

Word: رَهْطُكَ — Hūd 11:91

Meaning here: Shuʿayb’s close kin, whose presence and standing deterred his opponents from attacking him.

Word: أَرَهْطِي — Hūd 11:92

Meaning here: “Are my kin…?” in Shuʿayb’s question about valuing his relatives more highly than The God.

Word: رَهْطٍ — Al-Naml 27:48

Meaning here: People forming a group, numbered as nine by the preceding word; the context describes their corruption and subsequent plot against Ṣāliḥ’s family. These proposals do not replace existing authored verse-box explanations.

Conclusions and final definition

R H Ṭ is associated with gathering and forming a group.

In its principal human use, rahṭ denotes a relatively small group.

Its members may be linked by kinship, tribal affiliation, or association.

In family contexts, it denotes close relatives and may appear where their social support is emphasized.

Early sources differ over its numerical range: some give three to ten, others a narrower range, and some a broader one.

The noun itself therefore does not determine an exact fixed number.

Rahṭ is a collective noun with plurals such as arhuṭ, arhāṭ, and arāhiṭ.

Poetry attributed to pre-Islamic times attests the word and its plurals in transmitted early Arabic; the attribution and date of each witness require separate evaluation.

Other branches include leather coverings, burrow features, and clusters of trees.

Their forms and imagery can be compared, but one should not derive all these meanings from one another without historical evidence.

Rahṭ occurs three times in the Qur’an.

Hūd 11:91 presents Shuʿayb’s kin as deterring his opponents from attacking him.

Hūd 11:92 challenges opponents who take his relatives seriously while disregarding The God’s truth and command.

Al-Naml 27:48 mentions nine corrupt men; the next verse describes their plot for a night attack on Ṣāliḥ and his family.

These occurrences show that rahṭ itself labels neither a righteous nor a corrupt group.

The members’ actions establish its moral character.

Shuʿayb’s story emphasizes kinship and social protection; Ṣāliḥ’s emphasizes a small group’s coordinated corruption.

Final definition of R H Ṭ:

In its main human use, the family denotes a relatively small group connected by kinship, affiliation, or association.

In a family setting, rahṭ identifies close relatives whose protective role may be evident from context.

More generally, it can denote a limited group without establishing kinship among all its members.

Its two main Qur’anic uses are therefore:

1. Close kin and social backing: Shuʿayb’s rahṭ in Hūd 11:91 and Hūd 11:92.

2. A particular small group: The nine men in Al-Naml 27:48.

The root itself does not mean support, justice, corruption, conspiracy, or political power. Conduct and context supply those features.

A suitable brief meaning for Forqan’s study and translation is:

“A small group of people, especially an individual’s close kin and associates.”

This preserves the semantic connection between the three occurrences while distinguishing Shuʿayb’s relatives from the nine men in Ṣāliḥ’s story.
