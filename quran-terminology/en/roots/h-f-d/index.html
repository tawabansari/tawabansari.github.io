The Qur’anic Root Ḥ F D

Qur’anic and lexical forms

(ḥafadatan)

(ḥafada)

From prompt work and service to helpers and grandchildren in the family

The Arabic root ح ف د concerns promptness in performing work, serving, and assisting. حَفَدَ describes someone who acts quickly and nimbly, hastens to serve another, or assists them in a task. Within the same family, حَافِد denotes a helper, and حَفَدَة a group of such persons.

The root also acquired family usages. Arabic uses حَفَدَة for children, grandchildren, sons-in-law, and others who assist older family members. حَفِيد likewise means a child’s child: a grandchild.

The root’s basic meaning must not be restricted to grandchildren or sons-in-law, however. Someone may serve another without being related to them and be called a ḥāfid. Conversely, kinship alone does not establish actual service or assistance.

The Qur’an uses the root only once, as حَفَدَةً in Al-Naḥl 16:72. The verse mentions spouses, children, ḥafadah, and good provisions as divine gifts. Understanding ḥafadah requires considering its lexical meaning alongside the family relationship expressed in the verse.

Qur’anic form: حَفَدَةً, a plural of حَافِد, in Al-Naḥl 16:72.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the sole occurrence of ḥafadah, Al-Naḥl 16:72

Other forms, including حَفَدَ, يَحْفِدُ, حَفْد, حَفَدَان, حَافِد, حَفِيد, حُفَدَاء, احْتَفَدَ, and مَحْفُود, are recorded in Arabic but do not occur in these forms in the Qur’an.

1. The semantic core and important derivatives

Ḥafada: promptness and agility in work

In Al-ʿAyn, Al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad explains ḥafd as agility and promptness in work and service. In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris likewise presents two connected features: الْخِفَّةُ فِي الْعَمَلِ and التَّجَمُّع, agility in work and gathering together. He explains helpers through the connection between these features.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: agility in work and gathering together

Lisān al-ʿArab also records حَفَدَ يَحْفِدُ for hastening in work, serving, and moving quickly.

Speed here does not always mean running or rapid bodily movement. Someone may be active, ready, and nimble in performing a task and be described through this root.

Two usages must therefore be distinguished: hastening in movement, and agility in work or service.

حَفَدَ الْبَعِيرُ, for example, describes a camel moving quickly. حَفَدَ فُلَانٌ لِفُلَانٍ can describe someone hastening to work for or serve another.

The first foregrounds movement itself. The second places someone in a relationship with another person, performing work for them.

Ḥafd should therefore not always be translated only as running or only as serving.

Ḥafd and ḥafadān: rapid movement

حَفْد, حُفُود, and حَفَدَان are recorded verbal nouns of this family.

In descriptions of quadrupeds and birds, ḥafadān expresses quick, successive steps.

Lexical works differ over whether this movement is faster than ordinary walking or approaches the gait called خَبَب.

Khabab is faster than ordinary walking. Disagreement over ḥafadān’s exact speed nevertheless shows that it should not be treated as one fixed, measurable kind of running.

Their shared feature is speed and successive movement.

احْتَفَدَ likewise expresses hastening or acting nimbly in a task. This form extends the family’s usage from bodily movement to varied activities.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn: speed, service, and iḥtifād

Ḥāfid and ḥafadah: people who assist

حَافِد is the active participle of حَفَدَ. In service usages, it denotes someone who works for another, hastens to assist them, or is nimble in serving them.

حَفَدَة is a plural of ḥāfid, like طَلَبَة for ṭālib and كَتَبَة for kātib.

Explaining the word, Ibn ʿArafah states that ḥafadah in Arabic are assistants; someone performing work for another and hastening to assist or serve can be called a ḥāfid.

The definition has a fundamental implication: in its broad lexical sense, ḥafadah does not necessarily mean children, grandchildren, or sons-in-law. Their shared feature is assistance and service.

It can therefore describe people with no kinship to the person concerned.

In Al-Naḥl 16:72, however, مِنْ أَزْوَاجِكُمْ provides an important clue that must be examined in identifying the verse’s referent.

Ḥafīd: a grandchild

حَفِيد means a child’s child, a grandchild. حُفَدَاء and أَحْفَاد are also recorded plurals.

Some dictionaries likewise use ḥafadah of grandchildren.

The connection with the root’s semantic field is understandable: grandchildren may care for and assist older family members. This explanation does not mean all grandchildren necessarily serve others or that their worth depends on how much service they perform.

Kinship must also be distinguished from actual conduct. Ḥafīd can be a family title even if the person has performed no particular service for a grandparent.

The grandchild sense is therefore one recognized branch of the family, but does not determine every derivative’s meaning.

Maḥfūd: someone who is served

مَحْفُود is a passive participle describing someone whom others serve or assist with their work.

Some usages describe a person whose companions hasten to serve and obey them.

This meaning likewise concerns the relationship between someone serving and someone benefiting from that service.

Serving and obeying are not wholly identical concepts, however. Someone may help another out of affection and cooperation without submitting to every command.

Service and obedience occur together in some historical usages, but absolute obedience cannot be inferred from the root itself.

Other Arabic usages

Lexical works record مِحْفَد for a vessel or implement into which animal feed is placed, as well as a measuring vessel.

Some reports use مَحْفِد for origin or ancestry, or the base of a camel’s hump. Usages concerning a fabric’s pattern or decoration are also recorded within the family.

These senses are less clearly relevant to the Qur’anic usage than promptness and service. Shared root letters alone do not justify deriving them all from hastening to serve.

Their presence in Arabic should therefore be recognized, without constructing one certain derivational history for all of them in the absence of independent historical evidence.

2. Early linguistic witnesses: speed, service, and cooperation

Hastening in service

A poetic witness transmitted in early lexical sources reads:

حَفَدَ الْوَلَائِدُ حَوْلَهُنَّ وَأُسْلِمَتْ
بِأَكُفِّهِنَّ أَزِمَّةُ الْأَجْمَالِ

ḥafada al-walāʾidu ḥawlahunna wa-uslimat / bi-akuffihinna azimmatu al-ajmāli

Meaning: Female servants hurried about their tasks around them, and the camels’ reins were placed in their hands.

Ḥafada describes women acting nimbly in service here.

Al-walāʾid denotes women serving, while أَزِمَّةُ الْأَجْمَالِ means the camels’ reins.

This witness shows that ḥafd is not only speed of movement, but can also concern practical human work and service.

The verse appears in Al-ʿAyn, Lisān al-ʿArab, and other lexical sources. Its wording varies, including بَيْنَهُنَّ in place of حَوْلَهُنَّ, and its attribution is not uniform.

It can therefore be used as a witness to recorded Arabic usage, but should not be presented as certain pre-Islamic evidence without establishing its wording and date.

Sharḥ Maqāmāt al-Ḥarīrī: the al-walāʾid witness and differing wording and attribution

Hastening in movement

Another verse transmitted in literary sources reads:

كَلَّفْتُ مَجْهُولَهَا نُوقًا يَمَانِيَةً
إِذَا الْحُدَاةُ عَلَى أَكْسَائِهَا حَفَدُوا

kallaftu majhūlahā nūqan yamāniyatan / idhā al-ḥudātu ʿalā aksāʾihā ḥafadū

Meaning: I made Yemeni camels traverse the unknown route, while the camel drivers hurried behind them. Aksāʾihā refers to their hind parts or rear; it does not describe riding on the camels’ backs.

Al-Ṭabarī, poetic-witness note: aksāʾ as the rear or hind parts

Ḥafadū here concerns quick movement and the camel drivers’ activity.

The witness is sometimes attributed to Al-Aʿshā and sometimes to Al-Rāʿī. Some textual discussions note that it was not found in certain editions of the diwans attributed to these poets. Failure to find a verse in one edition does not prove its absence from every manuscript; Al-Aṣmaʿī’s Al-Ibil, for example, attributes the second hemistich to Al-Rāʿī.

Al-Aṣmaʿī, Al-Ibil: attribution of the hemistich to Al-Rāʿī

The attribution should therefore not be treated as certain. The witness matters because lexical sources record ḥafadū in the sense of speed and activity.

The expression wa-ilayka nasʿā wa-naḥfidu

An expression from a supplication transmitted in early sources reads:

وَإِلَيْكَ نَسْعَى وَنَحْفِدُ

wa-ilayka nasʿā wa-naḥfidu

Meaning: Toward You we strive, and we hasten to serve and obey You.

Nasʿā and naḥfidu stand alongside one another here.

Saʿy concerns effort and movement toward an objective. In this context, ḥafd emphasizes promptness in service and performing the intended work.

Lexicographers such as Abū ʿUbayd cite the expression as evidence for service and work.

The expression is not part of the Qur’anic text. Its value here is to demonstrate naḥfidu’s linguistic usage, rather than establish a ritual ruling or an independent religious status.

Abū ʿUbayd, Gharīb al-Ḥadīth: the supplication and service

Together, these witnesses show a real connection between speed, service, and assistance within ح ف د. Each usage must nevertheless be understood through its own structure and context.

3. The Qur’anic occurrence: Al-Naḥl 16:72

وَاللَّهُ جَعَلَ لَكُمْ مِنْ أَنْفُسِكُمْ أَزْوَاجًا وَجَعَلَ لَكُمْ مِنْ أَزْوَاجِكُمْ بَنِينَ وَحَفَدَةً وَرَزَقَكُمْ مِنَ الطَّيِّبَاتِ أَفَبِالْبَاطِلِ يُؤْمِنُونَ وَبِنِعْمَتِ اللَّهِ هُمْ يَكْفُرُونَ

wa-allāhu jaʿala lakum min anfusikum azwājan wa-jaʿala lakum min azwājikum banīna wa-ḥafadatan wa-razaqakum mina al-ṭayyibāti a-fa-bi-l-bāṭili yuʾminūna wa-bi-niʿmati allāhi hum yakfurūna

Translation: And God appointed for you spouses from among yourselves, and from your spouses appointed sons and helpers for you, and provided you with good things. Do they then believe in falsehood and deny God’s blessing?

Al-Naḥl 16:72

حَفَدَةً is an indefinite accusative broken plural of حَافِد. In the direct-coordination analysis, وَ joins it to بَنِينَ. It should not be confused with the technical morphological category of a collective noun.

The verse first mentions spouses, then children and ḥafadah, followed by good provisions.

This sequence places the relationship between spouses, the expanding family, and access to life’s resources within the context of divine gifts.

The meaning of min azwājikum

In the analysis coordinating حَفَدَةً directly with بَنِينَ, وَجَعَلَ لَكُمْ مِنْ أَزْوَاجِكُمْ بَنِينَ وَحَفَدَةً expresses a relationship through which children and ḥafadah arise through spouses or become connected to the family.

This qualification matters in identifying ḥafadah’s referent.

Taking the word generally for all servants raises the question of how unrelated servants connect with “from your spouses.”

By contrast, if it concerns children, grandchildren, sons-in-law, or other helpers connected with the family, the relationship with spouses is clearer.

This clue alone does not establish that only grandchildren or only sons-in-law are intended. Nor does it mean every helper must be a biological child of the same couple: kinship can also arise through marriage.

Min azwājikum is therefore an important clue to the referent in the family reading, without resolving every disagreement. Al-Ṭabarī, however, does not restrict ḥafadah to one family group and includes unrelated persons who serve. Restricting every reading of the verse to relatives is therefore not a certain consequence of the word itself.

Al-Ṭabarī: the plural of ḥāfid and family versus broader referents

The difference between banīna and ḥafadatan

بَنِينَ belongs to ب ن ي and here means sons.

حَفَدَةً belongs to a different root and lexically concerns helpers and persons serving.

The root distinction matters: merely translating ḥafadah as sons would leave their coordination insufficiently explained.

The Qur’an can nevertheless place overlapping groups side by side. Coordination alone therefore does not prove that no member of the first group can belong to the second.

A son may help his family and be an ibn by descent and a ḥāfid in conduct.

The different titles nevertheless show that they should not be treated as complete synonyms.

Banīna emphasizes the relationship of offspring here, whereas ḥafadah may concern those who assist within the family or are known in Arabic by such a family title.

Does ḥafadah mean only grandchildren?

Ḥafīd means a grandchild in Arabic, and some usages apply ḥafadah to grandchildren.

“Sons and grandchildren” is therefore a recognized reading with lexical support.

It also fits the verse’s structure, which discusses spouses, children, and extending generations.

Ḥafadah is not exclusive to grandchildren in Arabic, however. Early dictionaries also clearly record its usage for helpers and persons serving.

One family usage alone cannot therefore establish that ḥafadah must mean grandchildren in the Qur’an.

A translation using only “grandchildren” does not inform the reader of the broader sense of service and assistance within the word’s lexical family.

Conversely, “servants” alone can weaken the verse’s family connection.

The distinction between lexical meaning and the verse’s possible referent should therefore remain.

Are the ḥafadah sons-in-law?

Some early explanations interpret ḥafadah as أَخْتَان or أَصْهَار.

Akhtān here means sons-in-law: daughters’ husbands.

This interpretation also relates to family context. Children arise through marriage, and daughters’ marriages create further relationships by affinity.

Sons-in-law may cooperate with and assist the family and thus be instances of ḥafadah.

The word’s basic meaning is not exclusive to the son-in-law relationship, however. If it were, its application to servants, children, and grandchildren would remain unexplained.

Sons-in-law are therefore one proposed referent, rather than the root’s certain, exclusive meaning.

Daughters, or a wife’s children from an earlier marriage?

Some lexical reports apply ḥafadah to daughters helping their parents at home.

Another explanation applies it to a woman’s children from an earlier marriage.

These usages show that early sources did not limit family referents to grandchildren through sons.

None of these referents should become a legal or ethical rule requiring daughters, young children, or relatives to be servants of other family members.

The reports describe linguistic usages and social understandings. The word itself cannot establish a family group’s compulsory duty.

The Qur’an mentions ḥafadah as a gift here, but does not specify their mutual rights or the form of family cooperation.

An appropriate range for ḥafadah in the verse

Considering lexical evidence alongside the verse’s structure lets us distinguish two points.

First, Arabic uses ḥafadah for helpers and those acting nimbly in work and service.

Second, in the family reading of Al-Naḥl 16:72, it concerns those arising from the marital relationship or connected to the family. This contextual preference must remain distinct from the broader reading of helpers accepted by Al-Ṭabarī.

“Family helpers” can therefore preserve both the lexical meaning and the contextual clue.

It is a contextual explanation, however, and must not imply that family is a separate, fixed component of the root itself.

Grandchildren, sons-in-law, and other relatives may be instances of this title. The verse does not explicitly designate only one of these groups.

4. Ḥafadah in the context of divine gifts

Family as a gift

Al-Naḥl 16:72 places three subjects alongside one another: spouses, children and ḥafadah, and good provisions.

This shows that family relationships are not presented solely in terms of reproduction. Through family, people can also benefit from companionship, cooperation, and others’ assistance.

Ḥafadah’s meaning should nevertheless not be expanded to every social effect of family. Affection, upbringing, economic cooperation, and emotional support can exist within family relationships, but are not all contained in the word itself.

Ḥafadah’s lexical meaning concerns assistance and service. Other aspects of family emerge from context and life experience.

Being a gift differs from ownership of persons

The verse includes spouses, children, and ḥafadah among God’s gifts.

This does not mean someone owns their spouse, child, or grandchild, or possesses unlimited authority over them.

The verse says جَعَلَ لَكُمْ: God has appointed these relationships and resources for you. That verb alone does not determine the legal relationship between a person and their family members.

Just as children can bring their parents benefit and assistance, they are themselves persons with responsibility, choice, and rights.

Ḥafadah should therefore not be translated as though the word itself expressed enslavement or unconditional obedience. Some early explanations include enslaved persons serving among its referents; their legal status is not established by the word ḥafadah itself.

Service could occur within unequal social relationships in early Arabic society. The existence of that usage does not itself establish the legitimacy of every such relationship.

The distinction between lexical meaning, historical reality, and ethical judgment must remain.

Gifts and denial

The verse ends by questioning belief in falsehood and denial of God’s blessing.

This ending places family and provision within a wider argument about divine gifts.

Al-Naḥl 16:71 considers differences in provision. Al-Naḥl 16:73 discusses worshipping things that possess no power to provide sustenance.

Al-Naḥl 16:72 therefore stands within verses directing people to recognize the source of gifts and avoid attributing divine power to anything other than God.

The broader discussion must not change ḥafadah’s meaning. The word does not itself mean gratitude, disbelief, divine unity, or a gift.

In the family reading, its role is to identify helpers connected to the family whose presence and assistance are counted among the gifts. The broader reading also includes other persons serving.

5. Distinctions from related roots

Ḥ F D (ḥafd) and R F D (rafd)

Rafd concerns assistance, support, and providing help.

Ḥafd likewise approaches assistance in human usage, but its lexical witnesses foreground promptness and agility in serving.

Someone may provide financial assistance, for example, within rafd’s semantic range. A ḥāfid, in familiar usage, hastens to assist another in work and service.

The difference is not absolute. Both roots can occur in cooperation and support, but differ in structure and range.

Ḥ F D (ḥafd) and Kh D M (khidmah)

Khidmah concerns performing work for another.

Many usages of ḥafd approach this sense, but early explanations also include promptness and agility.

Not every service is performed quickly. Work may be slow and careful and remain service.

Ḥafd’s familiar emphasis on nimbleness in work can therefore distinguish it from khidmah, although some usages bring them very close.

Ḥ F D (ḥafd) and ʿ W N (ʿawn)

ʿAwn concerns help and assistance across varied tasks.

Lexical works also explain ḥafadah through أَعْوَان, helpers.

ʿAwn nevertheless names assistance more broadly, whereas familiar ḥafd usages connect with service and prompt action.

“Helpers” can appropriately render ḥafadah in Al-Naḥl 16:72, with an explanation that it occurs within family context.

Study of ʿ W N

Ḥ F D (ḥafd) and W L D (walad)

Walad concerns birth and the relationship of offspring.

One family usage of ḥafīd means a child’s child, but ح ف د does not itself mean birth.

The distinction is that walad identifies descent, while ḥafd’s familiar lexical meaning concerns prompt service and assistance.

Ḥafīd meaning grandchild is a family title within this lexical family. Not all its derivatives can nevertheless be translated as children or descendants.

This matters in Al-Naḥl 16:72 because banīna and ḥafadah occur together and should not replace one another without evidence.

Study of W L D

Ḥ F D (ḥafd) and Ḥ F Ẓ (ḥifẓ)

Ḥifẓ concerns keeping, caring for, and preventing loss.

Ḥafd concerns promptness in work and service.

Someone may hasten to help protect another, but this conduct combines two concepts: assisting and protecting.

A ḥāfid therefore does not itself mean a guard or keeper; nor is a ḥāfiẓ necessarily a servant or helper.

Study of Ḥ F Ẓ

Ḥ F D (ḥafd) and Ḥ F F (ḥaff)

Ḥaff concerns surrounding or being around something.

Al-Zumar 39:75 uses حَافِّينَ for angels surrounding the Throne.

Ḥāfid, however, belongs to ح ف د and concerns promptness and service.

The visual similarity of ḥāfid and ḥāff must not cause their meanings to be identified. Surrounding and assisting are different actions, even if helpers may also be present around someone.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Translating حَفَدَةً in Al-Naḥl 16:72 requires two considerations: its familiar Arabic lexical meaning and the family relationship expressed through مِنْ أَزْوَاجِكُمْ.

“Grandchildren” is natural Persian and conveys a recognized usage. It also fits the verse’s discussion of spouses and children.

It does not convey the broader scope recorded in early dictionaries for helpers, children serving, and other relatives, however.

“Servants” likewise has lexical support, but in modern Persian can suggest an employer–servant relationship and weaken the verse’s family connection.

For Forqan’s base translation, “helpers” is therefore defensible:

“And God appointed for you spouses from among yourselves, and from your spouses appointed sons and helpers for you, and provided you with good things. Do they then believe in falsehood and deny God’s blessing?”

“Helpers” preserves ḥafadah’s lexical meaning, while “from your spouses” supplies the family clue within the same sentence.

Commentary should explain that these family helpers may be children, grandchildren, sons-in-law, or other relatives. The Arabic does not identify one of these referents by its exclusive name.

Banīna here should also be recognized as sons. This does not deny daughters’ existence or worth: Al-Shūrā 42:49 mentions the giving of both daughters and sons.

A lexical translation should not alter banīna or ḥafadah without evidence to resolve a social question. The words’ distinctions should remain, with contextual explanation in its own place.

Outside the Qur’an, حَفَدَ may mean “he hurried” or “hastened to serve,” حَافِد “a nimble helper,” حَفِيد “a grandchild,” and مَحْفُود “someone who is served.”

Conclusion

The Arabic root ح ف د concerns speed, agility in work, and serving. In descriptions of movement, it emphasizes quick, successive steps; in human relationships, it concerns someone working for another or hastening to assist with a task.

Within the same family, ḥāfid denotes a helper and ḥafadah a group of helpers. In family usage, the title also applies to children, grandchildren, sons-in-law, and others assisting within the household.

Only حَفَدَةً occurs in the Qur’an, in Al-Naḥl 16:72. It follows banīna and connects with مِنْ أَزْوَاجِكُمْ. Assistance must therefore be considered alongside the family clue.

Grandchildren are one recognized possible referent, but restricting the word to them does not reflect its entire lexical range. “Servants” also has lexical support, but disregarding “from your spouses” can extend the verse’s scope too far.

Brief root meaning: Speed and agility in performing work and, in human relationships, service and assistance. In the family reading of the verse, ḥafadah concerns those assisting within the household or known by such a title; this is not the only transmitted interpretation.

This meaning must not become a compulsory duty for children and grandchildren to serve. The word describes a familiar relationship and role, rather than ownership of persons or their unconditional obedience.

For Forqan, “helpers” can preserve the basic meaning, with “from your spouses” supporting the family reading. Lexical commentary should make clear that grandchildren, sons-in-law, and other relatives are proposed referents rather than the root’s exclusive meaning; early sources also transmit the broader reading of persons serving.
