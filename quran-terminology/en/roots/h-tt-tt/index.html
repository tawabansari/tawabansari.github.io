1. Short Introduction to the Root

The Arabic root Ḥ Ṭ Ṭ moves around lowering, setting down, taking something off the shoulders, and reducing. Its clearest material use is when a load is taken down from the back of an animal or from a person’s shoulder and placed on the ground.

From that image, several related uses arise: bringing something down from a higher place, coming down, reducing a price or debt, a rank being lowered, and setting down the burden or consequence of sin.

Yet the Qur’an uses only one form from this wider family:

حِطَّة

This word appears twice, in Al-Baqarah 2:58 and Al-A‘raf 7:161, in a command addressed to the Children of Israel. They were commanded, while entering humbly through the gate, to say حِطَّة; then they were promised that their errors would be forgiven.

The Qur’anic use of the root is therefore narrow and focused. The Qur’an does not use the verb حَطَّ, the form انحطّ, the noun محطّة, or other derivatives from this root.

Short meaning: lowering and setting down; and in the Qur’anic use of حِطَّة, asking for the burden of errors to be set down and forgiven.

2. Important Forms and Derivatives of the Root

حِطَّة

This is the only Qur’anic form of the root, and it appears twice:

وَقُولُوا حِطَّةٌ

Al-Baqarah 2:58

Al-A‘raf 7:161

The word is a nominative noun. Semantically, it points to setting something down, reducing it, or having it removed. Its connection with the following clause:

نَغْفِرْ لَكُمْ خَطَايَاكُمْ

shows that, in this context, what is being set down is the burden and consequence of errors.

حَطَّ / يَحُطُّ

This is the simple and known Arabic verb, but it does not occur in the Qur’an. Its main Arabic meanings include bringing something down, setting down a load from an animal, reducing an amount or price, and, in explanatory usage, removing a burden or weight from someone.

انْحَطَّ

This intransitive form points to coming down, descending, or being reduced. It does not occur in the Qur’an. The Persian word “inhitāt” comes from this branch, but the later meaning of moral or civilizational decline should not control the Qur’anic use of حِطَّة.

حَطِيطَة

In Arabic, this can mean the amount reduced from a price, debt, or account. This form does not occur in the Qur’an.

مَحَطّ and مَحَطَّة

These refer to a place of descent or a place where loads are set down. These forms are not Qur’anic either.

3. Reference Card for the Qur’anic Derivative

FormUsed in the Qur’an: yes

حِطَّة

A request or declaration that the burden of errors be set down; the word the Children of Israel were commanded to say while entering humbly through the gate, after which forgiveness of their errors was promised.

This root has no other Qur’anic derivative card. Forms such as حَطَّ, انحطَّ, حطيطة, and محطّة belong to Arabic usage, but they do not occur in the Qur’anic text.

4. The Semantic Core of the Root

The semantic core of Ḥ Ṭ Ṭ may be stated as: bringing something down from a higher place, or from a state of being carried, and setting it down; or reducing its amount and weight.

This meaning expands in three directions. First, material lowering: a load is taken down from a camel or mount. Second, quantitative reduction: part of a price, debt, or account is reduced. Third, the setting down of an attributed burden: a burden, sin, or consequence that weighs upon a person is removed or reduced.

The Qur’anic use of حِطَّة is close to the third direction, while drawing on the first image: errors are like a burden, and the human being asks that they be set down.

This image should not be turned into a claim that sin is literally a material object on a person’s shoulder. The Qur’an speaks about responsibility and moral consequence through human and tangible language.

Likewise, حِطَّة itself does not fully mean “repentance,” “submission,” “disarmament,” or “entry into a new government.” Such ideas may relate to the larger atmosphere of the command, but they are not established as the direct meaning of the word.

5. The Material Image of the Root

The clearest material image of the root is a caravan stopping at a station while the loads are lowered from the backs of the animals.

حَطَّ الحِمْلَ عَنِ البَعِير

He set the load down from the camel.

As long as the load remains on the animal, its weight is being carried. With حطّ, the load is brought down from its elevated position and placed on the ground.

This image comes close to the request for forgiveness. The error and its consequence are understood like a burden upon a person, and حِطَّة is the request that it be set down.

But the details of caravan life should not be inserted into the meaning of the verse. The Qur’an does not mention camels, caravans, the end of a journey, or material loads in these two verses. The image is useful only for clarifying the lexical field of the root.

Another claim to avoid is the story of “gates of humility” with a low arch. The Qur’an says:

وَادْخُلُوا الْبَابَ سُجَّدًا

but it does not say that the height of the gate forced them to bend. سُجَّدًا expresses their state of humility and submission; the architecture of the gate is not specified in the text.

6. Use of the Root in Early Arabic

In early Arabic, حَطَّ is first of all connected with bringing down and setting down.

حَطَّ الحِمْلَ

He set down the load.

حَطَّ الرَّحْلَ

He set down the travel load or saddle.

حَطَّ مِنَ الثَّمَن

He reduced the price.

حَطَّ عَنْهُ وِزْرَهُ

He removed the burden or weight from him.

المَحَطّ was also used for a station or place of descent, because travelers would set down their loads there.

These uses show that the root is not merely “falling” or “decline.” An agent may consciously set something down, reduce a debt, or remove a burden from someone else. The root can therefore be positive or negative depending on the object and context: lowering a load brings relief; lowering a rank may be negative; reducing a debt may be an act of goodness; setting down a burden can be connected with forgiveness.

7. Translation Challenges

Many translations render حِطَّة with meanings such as “forgiveness,” “forgive us,” “set down our sins,” or “remove the burden of our sins.”

The translation “forgiveness” is influenced by the result stated later in the verse:

نَغْفِرْ لَكُمْ خَطَايَاكُمْ

That rendering communicates the general direction of the verse, but it hides the root image. حِطَّة is not directly from the root GH F R; it points to setting down and lowering.

At the same time, an overly long translation such as “we seek lightening and the setting down of the burdens of our rebellion” inserts more than the word itself carries. Rebellion, arrogance, lightness, and the pronoun “we” must be explained from context or grammatical reconstruction, not placed wholly inside a single word.

A balanced rendering may be: “Set down the burden of our errors.” Or: “We ask for the setting down of our errors.” In a base translation, the Qur’anic word itself may be kept: “And say: Ḥiṭṭah; set down the burden of our errors.” This preserves the special Qur’anic word while making its meaning clear to the reader.

8. Methodological Note

This root is an important example of separating three levels: the lexical meaning, the contextual meaning, and the promised result.

The lexical meaning is setting down, lowering, and reducing. The contextual meaning is the setting down of errors and the removal of their consequence. The promised result is forgiveness of errors and increase for the doers of good.

These three levels are connected, but they are not identical. The result of the verse should not be placed in the word itself by saying that حِطَّة literally means “forgiveness.” Nor should the image of the root be expanded until it becomes “laying down civilizational arrogance,” “moving from nomadic life,” or “accepting governmental order.”

It is also necessary to distinguish Qur’anic data from external reports. The Qur’an only says:

فَبَدَّلَ الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا مِنْهُمْ قَوْلًا غَيْرَ الَّذِي قِيلَ لَهُمْ

They replaced the saying that had been said to them with another saying. The Qur’an does not name that replacement. Therefore, the claim that حِطَّة was definitely changed into حِنْطَة, wheat, or a specific phrase is not obtained from the Qur’an itself and should not be presented in the root article as a Qur’anic fact.

9. Qur’anic Uses of the Root

Ḥiṭṭah in Al-Baqarah

وَإِذْ قُلْنَا ادْخُلُوا هَٰذِهِ الْقَرْيَةَ فَكُلُوا مِنْهَا حَيْثُ شِئْتُمْ رَغَدًا وَادْخُلُوا الْبَابَ سُجَّدًا وَقُولُوا حِطَّةٌ نَغْفِرْ لَكُمْ خَطَايَاكُمْ وَسَنَزِيدُ الْمُحْسِنِينَ

And when We said: enter this settlement and eat from it wherever you wish in abundance; and enter the gate humbly, and say: set down the burden of our errors; We will forgive your errors for you, and We will increase the doers of good.

Al-Baqarah 2:58

The verse places several commands and promises beside one another: entering the settlement, eating freely from its provision, entering humbly through the gate, saying حِطَّة, forgiveness of errors, and increase for the doers of good.

حِطَّة stands between the bodily act of humble entry and the promise of forgiveness. This shows that their entry was not to be marked by arrogance, seizure, or ingratitude; it was to be accompanied by acknowledgment of their need for the setting down of their errors.

Yet the word itself does not carry all these meanings alone. Humility is understood from سُجَّدًا, forgiveness from نغفر, and goodness from المحسنين.

Replacing the Command

فَبَدَّلَ الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا قَوْلًا غَيْرَ الَّذِي قِيلَ لَهُمْ فَأَنْزَلْنَا عَلَى الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا رِجْزًا مِنَ السَّمَاءِ بِمَا كَانُوا يَفْسُقُونَ

Then those who did wrong replaced the saying that had been said to them with another saying; so We sent down upon those who did wrong a punishment from the heaven because they were defiantly departing.

Al-Baqarah 2:59

The issue is not merely a phonetic alteration of one word. The verse describes their behavior through wrongdoing, replacing the command, and fisq. The change of speech is an outward sign of disobedience. But the Qur’an does not state the content of the replacement, so this article should not add the “wheat” report to the Qur’anic text or build a theory of materialistic mentality upon it.

Ḥiṭṭah in Al-A‘raf

وَإِذْ قِيلَ لَهُمُ اسْكُنُوا هَٰذِهِ الْقَرْيَةَ وَكُلُوا مِنْهَا حَيْثُ شِئْتُمْ وَقُولُوا حِطَّةٌ وَادْخُلُوا الْبَابَ سُجَّدًا نَغْفِرْ لَكُمْ خَطِيئَاتِكُمْ سَنَزِيدُ الْمُحْسِنِينَ

And when it was said to them: dwell in this settlement and eat from it wherever you wish; and say: set down the burden of our errors; and enter the gate humbly; We will forgive your errors for you, and We will increase the doers of good.

Al-A‘raf 7:161

This verse restates the same meaning with differences in order and wording. In Al-Baqarah, entering the gate is mentioned before saying حِطَّة; in Al-A‘raf, saying حِطَّة comes before entering the gate humbly. This shift shows that the two commands are connected without requiring that one must always come after the other in time.

Al-Baqarah uses خطاياكم, while Al-A‘raf uses خطيئاتكم. Both point to their errors, and this article does not need to create an artificial distinction between the two plurals.

Replacing the Command in Al-A‘raf

فَبَدَّلَ الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا مِنْهُمْ قَوْلًا غَيْرَ الَّذِي قِيلَ لَهُمْ فَأَرْسَلْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ رِجْزًا مِنَ السَّمَاءِ بِمَا كَانُوا يَظْلِمُونَ

Then those among them who did wrong replaced the saying that had been said to them with another saying; so We sent upon them a punishment from the heaven because they were doing wrong.

Al-A‘raf 7:162

In Al-Baqarah the verse ends with بِمَا كَانُوا يَفْسُقُونَ; in Al-A‘raf it ends with بِمَا كَانُوا يَظْلِمُونَ. Together, the two statements show that replacing the command was both fisq and wrongdoing.

10. Examination of the Qur’anic Derivative

حِطَّة

This is the only Qur’anic derivative of the root. In both occurrences, it comes after قولوا, it is nominative, it is connected with humble entry through the gate, and it is immediately followed by the promise of forgiveness of errors.

This structural stability shows that حِطَّة is a commanded utterance and a request for the setting down of errors. It should not be reduced to a meaningless slogan or ritual code. Its lexical meaning fits the continuation of the verse: they speak of errors being set down, and The God promises their forgiveness.

At the same time, it should not be treated as a complete sentence whose omitted parts can be reconstructed with certainty. Reconstructions such as مَسْأَلَتُنَا حِطَّةٌ or أَمْرُنَا حِطَّةٌ are possible, or the word may simply be quoted after قولوا. The general meaning is clear, but the omitted structure should not be over-specified beyond the evidence.

11. Morphological and Grammatical Notes

The doubled root

Ḥ Ṭ Ṭ is a doubled root; its second and third radicals are the same. In the verb حَطَّ, the two ṭā’s merge. This morphological fact should not be turned into a theory about the “compression of burden” or “intensity of fall.”

The pattern of حِطَّة

حِطَّة is approximately on the pattern فِعَّة from a doubled root. It should not be simply equated with forms like جِلْسَة and مِشْيَة in order to conclude that it must be a noun of bodily posture. Its meaning is gained from the root, noun formation, and context.

The nominative form

The common reading is حِطَّةٌ, with nominative ending. Several analyses are possible: a predicate for an omitted subject, the word quoted after قولوا, or a compressed phrase conveying the request for setting down. The nominative form does not prove that the Children of Israel were commanded to perform a special bodily posture; the bodily state is separately expressed by سُجَّدًا.

قولوا حطة

The verb قولوا shows that they were to say this meaning aloud. But speech in the Qur’an is not always separated from action. In this context, speech is joined with humble entry and doing good. The verse therefore teaches neither a magical pronunciation nor the replacement of speech by an inward state alone.

نغفر لكم

The phrase نَغْفِرْ لَكُمْ follows the preceding commands. It shows that obedience to the command is accompanied by the promise of forgiveness. But GH F R and Ḥ Ṭ Ṭ are distinct roots: Ḥ Ṭ Ṭ is setting down and reducing; GH F R is covering and forgiving. In the verse they meet, but they should not be treated as complete synonyms.

12. Ḥ Ṭ Ṭ Compared with W Ḍ ‘

وضع means placing or putting something somewhere. حطّ emphasizes bringing something down and then setting it down.

As a tendency: W Ḍ ‘ says that something was placed somewhere; Ḥ Ṭ Ṭ says that something was brought down from a higher position or from being carried and then set down. A load may be حطّ from an animal and then وضع on the ground, though in natural usage the two fields overlap.

In the moral field, the Qur’an uses the expression of laying down a burden, while Arabic also uses حطّ الوزر. This closeness shows that definitions should not become mechanical borders.

13. Ḥ Ṭ Ṭ Compared with N Z L

نزول is connected with descending, coming down, or arriving at a lodging place. حطّ more strongly highlights bringing something down or setting something down.

As a tendency: N Z L says that someone or something came down or settled in a place; Ḥ Ṭ Ṭ says that something was brought down or a load was set down. A traveler may نزل at a station and set down his load there. But N Z L does not always mean being a guest, and Ḥ Ṭ Ṭ is not always joined to settlement. These features must be understood from context.

14. Ḥ Ṭ Ṭ Compared with H B Ṭ

هبوط relates to going down or moving from one level to a lower one. حطّ can be transitive: someone brings something down.

As a tendency: H B Ṭ says that the subject came down; Ḥ Ṭ Ṭ says that an agent brought something down or set it down. Still, in Arabic, حطّ may also be used for descending, and انحطّ is its intransitive branch. The border is therefore not completely closed.

اهْبِطُوا مِصْرًا

In this example, the issue is moving down or moving into a place, not setting down a load.

15. Ḥ Ṭ Ṭ Compared with GH F R

This comparison is especially important for the Qur’anic use of حِطَّة. Ḥ Ṭ Ṭ carries the image of removing and setting down a burden. GH F R is connected with covering, protecting, and forgiving.

In the two verses, the command قولوا حطة is joined with the promise:

نغفر لكم خطاياكم

One may say: they ask that their errors be set down, and The God promises to forgive them. But these two stages should not be collapsed into one. حِطَّة is not the name of forgiveness, even though the result in the verse is forgiveness.

16. Ḥ Ṭ Ṭ Compared with Ḥ M L

حمل means carrying or bearing something. One of the clearest uses of حطّ is its practical opposite: a carried load is put down.

Simply stated: Ḥ M L is taking up and carrying a burden; Ḥ Ṭ Ṭ is bringing that burden down and setting it down. This contrast helps explain حِطَّة. Errors and their consequences can be understood as a burden upon a person; the request for ḥiṭṭah is a request for release from carrying that burden.

Still, the Qur’an does not use the root Ḥ M L in the two verses under discussion. This relationship is lexical explanation, not the direct wording of the verses.

17. Translation Suggestions for Forqan

حِطَّة

The main suggestion is: “Set down the burden of our errors.” This keeps the root meaning, makes the link with خطاياكم / خطيئاتكم clear, and avoids needlessly lengthening the word.

Another option is: “We ask for the setting down of our errors.” This is closer to an analysis of the word as a nominative noun, but it is more formal and less smooth in English.

Keeping the word in translation

One may write: “And say: Ḥiṭṭah; set down the burden of our errors.” This method is suitable for Forqan, because it preserves the distinctive Qur’anic word while allowing the reader to understand it without needing a separate note.

Translations that carry only part of the meaning

“Forgive us” communicates the result of the verse, but it removes the root image. “Repentance” brings in more than the word itself says; the root of repentance is returning, not setting down. “Forgiveness” agrees with the context, but it is not a direct synonym of حِطَّة.

Translations to avoid

Translations or explanations such as disarmament, civilizational lightening, leaving behind desert arrogance, a rite of purification, or transition from desert order to urban rule should be avoided. These ideas are either not in the verse or are broad theorizing beyond the meaning of the word.

18. Conclusion

The root Ḥ Ṭ Ṭ in Arabic moves around lowering, setting down, and reducing. The proposed final definition is: bringing something down from a higher place or from a state of being carried and setting it down; and, in the Qur’anic use of حِطَّة, asking that the burden of errors be set down.

The Qur’an uses only the word حِطَّة from this root, twice, in one shared narrative about the Children of Israel. They were commanded to enter or dwell in the settlement, eat from its provision, enter the gate humbly, and say حِطَّة. Then the promise was given:

نَغْفِرْ لَكُمْ خَطَايَاكُمْ

or:

نَغْفِرْ لَكُمْ خَطِيئَاتِكُمْ

From this connection it becomes clear that حِطَّة is related to the setting down of errors and their burden. But the word should not be reduced to “forgiveness”; forgiveness is the result expressed through the root GH F R. A better translation should preserve the image of setting down.

Nor should حِطَّة be turned into a complex system about disarmament, civilizational transition, gate ritual, bodily purification, or consumer mentality. The Qur’an does not say that the gate was low, that the Children of Israel were forced to dismount, or that the word was changed into “wheat.” It says only that the wrongdoers replaced the commanded saying with another saying.

The claim that this root is linked with Hebrew חטא is also incorrect. That family is closer to the field of error and sin and is compared with the Arabic root KH Ṭ ’, not with Ḥ Ṭ Ṭ. Limited phonetic similarity is not evidence of etymological kinship.

The pattern of حِطَّة by itself also does not prove that the word is a noun of bodily posture or a command to a specific body position. The bodily state of humility is expressed separately in the verse by سُجَّدًا.

The core of the article should remain simple and clear: a burden is upon the person; the person asks that it be set down; and The God promises forgiveness of errors. For Forqan, the suitable translation is: “And say: Ḥiṭṭah; set down the burden of our errors.”
