Hard rock: from a landmark and a concealed location to Thamūd’s rock-cutting

Ṣ KH R concerns hard stone and masses of rock in Arabic. Size and solidity are prominent in characteristic uses; functions such as shelter, concealment, or building material are established by context.

The root occurs three times in three Qur’anic verses: al-ṣakhrati in Al-Kahf 18:63, ṣakhratin in Luqmān 31:16, and al-ṣakhra in Al-Fajr 89:9.

1. The semantic core in Arabic

A large, hard stone

Ibn Fāris writes in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

الصَّخْرَةُ: الْحَجَرَةُ الْعَظِيمَةُ

al-ṣakhratu: al-ḥajaratu al-ʿaẓīmatu

That is, a ṣakhrah is a large stone.

Lisān al-ʿArab gives a more specific definition:

الصَّخْرَةُ: الْحَجَرُ الْعَظِيمُ الصُّلْبُ

al-ṣakhratu: al-ḥajaru al-ʿaẓīmu al-ṣulbu

That is, a large, hard stone.

These definitions emphasize stony substance and solidity, together with the size of the rock mass in characteristic uses.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Lisān al-ʿArab: defining ṣakhrah

“Large” should not become a fixed numerical condition applying to every use. A fragment detached from a rock can still consist of ṣakhr, and a rock need not be mountain-sized.

Compared with pebbles and loose materials, ṣakhr characteristically foregrounds a hard, coherent rock mass.

Ṣakhr and ṣakhrah

Arabic sources use ṣakhr for hard stone as a material and as a collective generic noun. Ṣakhrah can designate one unit of it.

Ṣakhrah usually refers to a particular mass or piece of rock.

Lexicographers therefore explain ṣakhrah as a unit of ṣakhr, as adding tāʾ marbūṭah in some Arabic noun families identifies one member of a collection or kind.

Ṣukhūr and ṣakharāt are recorded as plurals. Ṣakhar, with fatḥah on the khāʾ, is also a pronunciation variant of ṣakhr; the vowel difference does not itself create a plural with an entirely separate meaning.

Both forms occur in the Qur’an. Al-ṣakhr in Al-Fajr 89:9 describes the rock Thamūd cut; ṣakhrah in Al-Kahf 18:63 and Luqmān 31:16 refers to a rock mass.

The distinction matters for translation, without making ṣakhr and ṣakhrah wholly separate: both belong to the same material family.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Al-Miṣbāḥ al-Munīr: collective, unit and plurals

2. Rock in early Arabic and everyday life

Rock in Imruʾ al-Qays’s poetry

A clear pre-Qur’anic witness occurs in the Muʿallaqah of the renowned pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays:

مِكَرٍّ مِفَرٍّ مُقْبِلٍ مُدْبِرٍ مَعًا
كَجُلْمُودِ صَخْرٍ حَطَّهُ السَّيْلُ مِنْ عَلِ

mikarrin mifarrin muqbilin mudbirin maʿan / ka-julmūdi ṣakhrin ḥaṭṭahu al-saylu min ʿali

Translation: “A horse agile in charging and retreating, advancing and turning away; like a hard boulder swept down from a height by the torrent.”

The poet compares his horse’s speed and force to a great stone rolled down from above by floodwater.

Julmūd denotes a large, hard stone; ṣakhr specifies its rock substance.

The stone’s hardness and coherence matter: even while moving, it remains a solid mass rather than soft earth or scattered material.

Ṣakhr itself does not mean rapid movement or descent. These come from the full simile and ḥaṭṭahu al-saylu min ʿali, the torrent casting it down from above.

The verse shows ṣakhr as a familiar pre-Islamic word for hard stone, available for poetic imagery.

Dīwān Imruʾ al-Qays: the julmūd ṣakhr witness and commentary

Rocky ground

Makānun ṣakhirun and makānun muṣkhirun describe a place abundant in stones and rocks.

The word extends from a rock’s name to a description of surroundings containing many rocks.

Later lexicons also record aṣkhara al-makānu for a place having abundant rock: Dozy, for example, transmits it from Muḥīṭ al-Muḥīṭ. This record should not be treated without evidence as a securely pre-Qur’anic witness.

Dozy citing Muḥīṭ al-Muḥīṭ: later attestation of aṣkhara al-makānu

The family can therefore describe the character of terrain as well as an individual object.

Rocky ground is not necessarily uninhabitable or uncultivable. Those qualities depend on soil, water, and other conditions and should not be included in the root’s definition.

Stone in building and cutting

People used hard stone to build walls, houses, enclosures, tools, and other structures.

Some rock masses are large and coherent enough for people to open fissures, cut through them, or carve parts of them.

This material use matters for Al-Fajr 89:9.

The stone itself should nevertheless be distinguished from cutting or carving it.

Ṣakhr names the material; cutting and carving come from accompanying verbs.

It should not be lexically restricted to cut stone, a stone house, or a rock-cut structure.

A rock can be entirely natural or worked by people. Context establishes the difference.

3. Qur’anic uses

Al-Kahf 18:63: a rock as a landmark

قَالَ أَرَأَيْتَ إِذْ أَوَيْنَا إِلَى الصَّخْرَةِ فَإِنِّي نَسِيتُ الْحُوتَ وَمَا أَنْسَانِيهُ إِلَّا الشَّيْطَانُ أَنْ أَذْكُرَهُ وَاتَّخَذَ سَبِيلَهُ فِي الْبَحْرِ عَجَبًا

qāla a-raʾayta idh awaynā ilā al-ṣakhrati fa-innī nasītu al-ḥūta wa-mā ansānīhu illā al-shayṭānu an adhkurahu wa-ittakhadha sabīlahu fī al-baḥri ʿajaban

Translation: He said, “Do you remember when we took shelter beside the rock? I forgot the fish, and none but Satan made me forget to mention it. It took its way into the sea in a wondrous manner.”

Al-ṣakhrati appears in Moses’s journey with his young companion.

In Al-Kahf 18:60, Moses declares his intention to reach the meeting of the two seas. In Al-Kahf 18:61, the fish takes its way into the sea. In verse 63, his companion recalls the rock and the forgotten incident concerning the fish.

The rock is a particular place along their journey where they took shelter or stopped beside it.

The role of ilā al-ṣakhrah

Awaynā ilā al-ṣakhrati combines the verb awā with the preposition ilā.

Awā ilā can express turning toward, seeking shelter, or lodging beside something.

Stopping or taking shelter therefore comes from awaynā, rather than Ṣ KH R.

Study of ʾ W Y

Al-ṣakhrati identifies the location of that act.

Its definite article likewise points to a particular rock or place familiar to speaker and listener.

Their shared journey may explain this familiarity. The article does not establish that the rock was a universally famous or sacred site.

Was the rock a special location?

Some transmitted commentaries propose a specific geographical location.

The verse itself gives no name, size, shape, or precise coordinates.

It establishes that the rock lay on their route and that recalling it helped identify the place of the fish incident.

In Al-Kahf 18:64, Moses says this is what they were seeking, and the two retrace their steps.

The rock’s narrative role is therefore to identify a recognizable place.

This does not make ṣakhrah inherently mean a sign, meeting place, travel guide, or sacred site.

Those roles emerge from the narrative; lexically, the word still denotes a rock mass.

Al-Ṭabarī: the rock on Moses’s journey

Luqmān 31:16: a concealed location difficult to access

يَا بُنَيَّ إِنَّهَا إِنْ تَكُ مِثْقَالَ حَبَّةٍ مِنْ خَرْدَلٍ فَتَكُنْ فِي صَخْرَةٍ أَوْ فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ أَوْ فِي الْأَرْضِ يَأْتِ بِهَا اللَّهُ إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَطِيفٌ خَبِيرٌ

yā bunayya innahā in taku mithqāla ḥabbatin min khardalin fa-takun fī ṣakhratin aw fī al-samāwāti aw fī al-arḍi yaʾti bihā allāhu inna allāha laṭīfun khabīrun

Translation: My dear son, if it weighs as much as a mustard seed and is within a rock, or in the heavens, or in the earth, The God will bring it forth. Indeed, The God is Subtle, Fully Aware.

Continuing his advice to his son, Luqmān describes something so small that, even within a rock or in the heavens or earth, it remains within God’s knowledge and power.

Ṣakhratin is indefinite: a rock, rather than a particular, recognized rock.

The contrast with Al-Kahf 18:63 matters: there it is al-ṣakhrati, whereas Luqmān has ṣakhratin without the article.

This fits their different roles: one identifies a particular rock on a journey, while the other illustrates the difficulty of finding something extremely small.

Why does the example mention a rock?

The verse describes something weighing as much as a mustard seed.

Something so slight can remain unseen or difficult to access within a large, hard mass.

A rock’s solidity and coherence make it a suitable example.

Ṣakhrah alone does not mean a hiding place or an impenetrable location. The image comes from the tiny amount, its placement within rock, and the statement about divine knowledge.

Fī ṣakhratin matters too: fī places it within a rock, rather than merely beside or upon it.

Does it mean a rock beneath the earth?

Some commentaries transmit an interpretation identifying a particular subterranean rock.

The verse itself contains no such specification.

It does not say “in the rock beneath the earth,” but:

فِي صَخْرَةٍ

fī ṣakhratin

Aw, “or,” then introduces two other locations: the heavens and the earth.

A cosmic rock, the earth’s supporting pillar, or a stone bearing the world is therefore not specified by the wording.

The direct reading is that something extremely small remains within God’s knowledge even inside a rock.

Is the point merely finding a lost object?

Luqmān’s advice concerns more than locating a material object.

Luqmān 31:13 discusses associating partners and great injustice. Luqmān 31:15 concerns following, proper conduct, and returning to God. Verse 16 then emphasizes that even something a mustard seed in weight remains within divine knowledge.

This continuity reinforces human responsibility and God’s knowledge of conduct.

Study of KH R D L

That moral message should not be added to ṣakhrah’s lexical definition.

The rock remains hard stone; the tiny thing within it illustrates the scope of divine knowledge and power.

Nor should yaʾti bihā be used, without examining the full construction, to derive details of how deeds become manifest or how final accounting operates.

Al-Ṭabarī: tiny deeds and differing accounts of the rock

Al-Fajr 89:9: the rock Thamūd cut

وَثَمُودَ الَّذِينَ جَابُوا الصَّخْرَ بِالْوَادِ

wa-thamūda alladhīna jābū al-ṣakhra bi-al-wādi

Translation: And Thamūd, those who cut the rock in the valley.

Al-ṣakhra describes the material Thamūd worked.

Unlike the preceding uses, the rock is the object of human activity, rather than simply a stopping place or an illustration of concealment.

Jābū derives from J W B and here concerns cutting, opening, or hewing through rock.

Ṣakhr does not itself mean cutting or carving. Jābū expresses the action, and al-ṣakhra identifies the material acted upon.

Study of J W B

Rock and skill in working it

Thamūd are characterized by their ability to cut or open up rock.

Hard stone generally requires force and suitable tools to cut and shape. Such work can therefore indicate technical ability and experience in working rock.

Al-Ḥijr 15:82 also states:

وَكَانُوا يَنْحِتُونَ مِنَ الْجِبَالِ بُيُوتًا آمِنِينَ

wa-kānū yanḥitūna mina al-jibāli buyūtan āminīna

That is, they carved houses from the mountains and lived securely.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:149 likewise describes carving houses from mountains.

Together with Al-Fajr 89:9, these verses identify work on great rock masses and rock-cut dwellings as familiar features of Thamūd.

Jābū al-ṣakhra alone does not say that every cut stone necessarily became a house. Construction emerges from the other verses and wider context.

Al-Ṭabarī: jābū al-ṣakhr and carved dwellings

Does the verse identify Thamūd’s precise location?

Bi-al-wādi refers to the valley.

The phrase itself gives no particular geographical name for it.

It may relate to rocky regions associated with Thamūd, but definitive identification requires separate geographical and historical evidence.

Ṣ KH R itself supplies no name for a particular land, city, or civilization.

Does hard rock symbolize Thamūd’s stubbornness?

Al-Fajr 89:11 describes the transgression of the groups previously introduced; Al-Fajr 89:12 mentions their spreading corruption in the lands.

Literary reflection may see a resemblance between rock’s hardness and human stubbornness.

That resemblance is not ṣakhr’s lexical meaning or the fundamental reason for its use in the verse.

The verse first concerns a material fact about Thamūd: cutting and hewing rock.

The following verses give the moral assessment of their conduct. It should not be extracted from stone’s material composition.

4. Important Arabic forms

Ṣakhr

Ṣakhr names hard stone or rock as a material.

In Al-Fajr 89:9 it has the article and is the object of jābū.

Here the noun can refer to rock as a substance without specifying a single stone.

Ṣakhrah

Ṣakhrah denotes a particular piece or mass of rock.

Al-Kahf 18:63 uses the definite al-ṣakhrati.

Luqmān 31:16 has the indefinite ṣakhratin.

Arabic also records ṣakharah, with a vowel on the khāʾ. This pronunciation difference does not itself create an entirely different meaning.

Ṣukhūr and ṣakharāt

Ṣukhūr is a familiar plural of ṣakhr and ṣakhrah, rendered as rocks or large stones.

Ṣakharāt is another plural form.

Neither occurs in the Qur’an, but Arabic uses them for several stones or rock masses.

Ṣakhir and muṣkhir

Ṣakhir and muṣkhir describe a place with abundant stones or rocks.

They describe a location’s character rather than necessarily one rock.

Later lexicons also record aṣkhara al-makānu for a place having abundant rock. This verbal use should be examined separately from the early adjectives ṣakhir and muṣkhir.

None of these forms occurs in the Qur’an.

Ṣakhr as a personal name

Ṣakhr has also been an Arabic personal name, including Ṣakhr ibn ʿAmr, brother of Al-Khansāʾ, the renowned Arab woman poet.

Here Ṣakhr is a proper name.

Choosing such a name may relate to the image of hard, solid rock. That does not mean the person’s name should be translated as “hard stone” in every sentence.

In actual usage it names an individual, distinct from the root’s common meaning.

Less widespread lexical uses

Some sources record al-ṣākhir for iron striking iron, and al-ṣākhirah for an earthenware vessel.

Al-ṣakhīr also names a plant.

Lexicons place these forms under the same entry, but their precise historical relationship to the core stone meaning is not fully established.

A story should not be invented to force the family into apparent unity, as though iron’s sound, an earthenware vessel, and a plant name necessarily arose from one original image of hard rock.

None of these uses appears in the Qur’an.

5. Comparison with related roots

Ṣ KH R (ṣakhr) and Ḥ J R (ḥajar)

Ḥajar is the familiar general Arabic word for stone, with a broad range.

Ṣakhr also means rock, with size and hardness particularly prominent in early definitions.

Al-ḥijārah denotes stones in Al-Baqarah 2:74.

Study of Ḥ J R

The same verse describes stones from which rivers burst forth or water emerges.

Ḥajar can therefore also concern large, hard stones. It should not be claimed that ḥajar always means a small stone and ṣakhr always a large one.

The distinction is a tendency in usage: ḥajar is more general, while characteristic ṣakhr uses emphasize a great, hard rock mass.

Ḥ J R also has branches involving restriction and preventing access. Those meanings do not belong to Ṣ KH R.

Ṣ KH R (ṣakhr) and J B L (jabal)

Jabal means a mountain.

A mountain can consist of rock masses, but mountain and rock are not identical concepts.

Al-Ḥijr 15:82 describes carving houses from mountains; Al-Fajr 89:9 calls the material Thamūd cut al-ṣakhra.

These expressions can concern closely related realities while describing them from different perspectives.

Jabal foregrounds a great, elevated landform; ṣakhr concerns hard rock as substance or mass.

Translating al-ṣakhr as mountain in every use is therefore inaccurate.

Study of J B L

Ṣ KH R (ṣakhr) and J L M D (julmūd)

Julmūd denotes a great, hard boulder.

Imruʾ al-Qays uses the two words together:

كَجُلْمُودِ صَخْرٍ

ka-julmūdi ṣakhrin

The combination shows their semantic closeness.

Early poetic commentaries explain julmūd as hard, relatively large stone, with ṣakhr specifying its substance.

The verse does not establish that every rock is necessarily a julmūd, or that a julmūd must have a fixed size.

Julmūd itself does not occur in the Qur’an.

Ṣ KH R (ṣakhr) and Ṣ L D (ṣald)

Applied to stone or a rock surface, ṣald can describe hardness, smoothness, and being bare of soil. It does not mean that nothing can ever rest on the surface under any circumstances.

Al-Baqarah 2:264 uses ṣaldan in an example of stone whose covering soil is removed by heavy rain, exposing its surface.

The stone is called ṣafwān; ṣaldan describes its bare, hard condition.

Ṣakhr can likewise be hard, but does not necessarily denote an entirely smooth, soil-free surface.

The words overlap in hardness: one names rock, while the other can describe a surface’s condition or quality.

Ṣ KH R (ṣakhr) and S KH R (sakhkhara)

These roots require separate examination despite their written resemblance.

Ṣ KH R begins with ṣād and concerns stone or rock in the Qur’an.

S KH R begins with sīn. Its Qur’anic forms concern subjecting things to use within an order, as well as mockery and ridicule.

Ibrāhīm 14:32, for example, describes subjecting ships and rivers; Al-Ḥujurāt 49:11 prohibits mocking others.

Neither use should be assigned to Ṣ KH R.

Even if lexicons report rare forms or sound alternations among similar words, that does not justify merging these Qur’anic families.

All three Ṣ KH R verses clearly refer to actual rock, with no contextual basis for replacing it with subjection or mockery.

Study of S KH R

6. Challenges in understanding and translation

Must a rock be exceptionally large?

Lexical definitions emphasize size and hardness.

These should not become a fixed measurement.

Al-Kahf 18:63 gives no dimensions for its rock, nor does Luqmān 31:16.

In Al-Fajr 89:9, Thamūd’s stone-working context suggests the size and solidity of rock masses.

“Large rock mass” can therefore suit some sentences, and “rock” others.

Does ṣakhrah mean shelter?

In Al-Kahf 18:63, Moses’s companion recalls taking shelter or lodging beside the rock.

Taking shelter comes from awaynā, rather than al-ṣakhrah.

A rock can provide natural shelter in one situation and be an obstacle, building material, or simply part of the terrain in another.

These functions should not become the word’s lexical definition.

Does rock imply absolute inaccessibility?

In Luqmān 31:16, something a mustard seed in size within rock illustrates concealment and difficulty of access.

Ṣakhrah does not inherently mean an absolutely inaccessible place.

Rock can be split, carved, or removed: Al-Fajr 89:9 itself describes Thamūd cutting it.

Even within these three verses, rock can be hard and obstructive without being represented as wholly unchangeable or impenetrable.

Is rock always a symbol of firmness and resistance?

Hardness and solidity can provide literary comparisons.

The Qur’an’s three uses of this root, however, concern actual rock.

None requires translating ṣakhr as firm faith, a hard heart, moral resistance, or stubbornness.

Where another verse uses stone to describe a hard heart or something enduring, that meaning comes from comparison and context.

The root’s material meaning should not be abandoned merely because metaphorical uses are possible.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

“Rock” is a natural, precise equivalent for ṣakhrah in most uses.

According to context, ṣakhr can be rendered as stone, hard rock, or a rock mass.

For Al-Kahf 18:63:

إِذْ أَوَيْنَا إِلَى الصَّخْرَةِ

idh awaynā ilā al-ṣakhrati

A suitable rendering:

“When we took shelter beside the rock.”

This identifies the particular rock and preserves awaynā’s meaning.

For Luqmān 31:16:

فَتَكُنْ فِي صَخْرَةٍ

fa-takun fī ṣakhratin

A suitable rendering:

“And is within a rock.”

This retains indefiniteness without adding a particular rock or an unstated geographical location.

For Al-Fajr 89:9:

الَّذِينَ جَابُوا الصَّخْرَ بِالْوَادِ

alladhīna jābū al-ṣakhra bi-al-wādi

A suitable rendering:

“Those who cut the rock in the valley.”

Rock adequately renders al-ṣakhr. Cutting comes from jābū and the location from bi-al-wādi.

“Those who carved houses in the mountains” can explain a connection with other verses. It is not a direct translation of this expression, since Al-Fajr 89:9 contains neither houses nor mountains.

8. Conclusion

Ṣ KH R has a relatively limited, clear Arabic family centered on stone and rock. Early definitions emphasize great size and hardness without establishing mandatory dimensions or shape.

Ṣakhr was a familiar pre-Islamic word. Imruʾ al-Qays uses it for a hard boulder rolled down from above by a torrent.

The Qur’an uses the root three times. Al-Kahf 18:63 identifies a familiar stopping place on Moses’s journey. Luqmān 31:16 uses rock to illustrate concealment and difficult access to something tiny. In Al-Fajr 89:9, rock is the material Thamūd cut and hewed.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: three nominal occurrences

All three retain the rock’s substance and solidity, while other sentence elements establish its particular role.

Short definition: Ṣ KH R concerns hard stone and rock masses, particularly those notable for size, hardness, or coherence. Ṣakhr can denote the material or a collection of rocks, while ṣakhrah identifies a particular mass or piece. Brief root meaning: Hard stone and rock; a large or coherent rock mass.

The basic meaning is therefore hard stone and rock, rather than shelter, concealment, cutting, carving, moral resistance, or stubbornness. Different contexts can associate rock with these functions without replacing the word’s direct meaning.
