M R R in early Arabic and the Qur’an: passage and time, bitter experience, tightly twisted strands, strength, and persistence

The three-letter Arabic root M R R forms a broad family with several semantic branches. One principal use concerns passing, crossing, or elapsing. Someone passes a settlement, a cloud moves across the sky, time passes, or an event occurs once and then again.

Another branch concerns bitterness, such as the bitter taste of a plant or food, and difficult experiences figuratively described as bitter.

A third concerns strength, solidity, and firmness. A tightly twisted rope, someone with firm resolve and ability, or an affair remaining established can be described through this family.

These fields belong to one lexical family but are not identical. A passing cloud, bitter food, and a being’s strength are different realities. Historical or figurative relationships can be investigated, but the meanings should not be forced into an invented single definition without evidence.

M R R occurs thirty-five times in thirty-two Qur’anic verses. Its uses include passing signs, mountains passing like clouds, events occurring once or repeatedly, the strength described in Al-Najm, the appointed Hour’s bitterness and severity, and persistence in Al-Qamar.

Four closely resembling forms especially need to be distinguished:

مَرَّ means “he passed” or “went by.”

مَرَّة means one occasion or one time.

مِرَّة means strength and firmness.

مُرّ and أَمَرّ mean bitter and more bitter.

Their short vowels distinguish real differences in meaning and should not be overlooked in translation or analysis.

1. Forms and semantic branches

Important Arabic forms include:

  • مَرَّ، يَمُرُّ: Passed, went by, or passed beside something.
  • مَرّ and مُرُور: Passing, crossing, and elapsing.
  • مَمَرّ: A place of passage or route; sometimes the act of passing.
  • مَارّ: One passing or going by.
  • مَرَّة: Once, one occasion, or one occurrence of an act.
  • مَرَّات and مِرَار: Times or recurring occasions.
  • مُرّ: Bitter, as opposed to sweet.
  • مَرَارَة: Bitterness, literally or in unpleasant experience.
  • أَمَرّ: More bitter, harsher, or more grievous.
  • مَرِير: Bitter; in another branch, firm or strong.
  • مِرَّة: Strength, firmness, and ability; sometimes soundness and balance of constitution.
  • مَرِيرَة: A tightly twisted rope or strand; also firm will and resolve.
  • مِرَار and مَرَائِر: In some uses, ropes or twisted strands.
  • أَمَرَّ: Made something bitter; in another use, made it move or pass; also twisted a rope firmly.
  • اِسْتَمَرَّ: Continued, persisted, or proceeded along a course; in some early uses, became strong or firm.
  • مُسْتَمِرّ: Continuing or persistent; elsewhere passing or strong and firm, depending on construction and context.
  • اِمْتَرَّ: Passed or went by in some usages.
  • مَارَّهُ: Accompanied someone in movement; in another branch, opposed or disputed with them.

مُرَّة also names certain bitter plants; مُرَار denotes a bitter plant, and مُرّ is attested for a bitter medicinal substance.

مَرَارَة also names the gallbladder, associated with bile.

These botanical and bodily uses do not occur in the Qur’an in those forms. They belong to the lexical range, rather than directly identifying the referents of all the verses.

Two principal semantic fields

An early lexical classification identifies two principal fields in this family.

First, passing and elapsing.

Second, the opposite of sweetness and pleasantness: bitterness.

The branch of strength and tightly twisted rope is also discussed among the root’s established uses.

This distinction reflects a significant linguistic fact: passage and bitterness are recognized fields, and neither must be forcibly derived from the other.

There is a clearer figurative connection between tightly twisted rope and firm resolve. Just as twisting strengthens strands, a person’s will can be described as firm and steadfast.

Even here, semantic explanation must be distinguished from proof of the historical order of derivatives.

2. Early Arabic uses and poetic witnesses

Passing and going by

Arabic uses the expression:

مَرَّ بِالْمَكَانِ

marra bi-al-makāni

Translation: “He passed that place.”

Also:

مَرَّ عَلَيْهِ

marra ʿalayhi

Translation: “He passed by him or over it.”

This can describe people, animals, clouds, wind, and other things moving along a course.

Murūr denotes the passage itself, without specifying speed, destination, or cause.

Someone may pass a place slowly or quickly, with a particular goal or simply while following a route.

Marra does not itself mean hurrying, fleeing, or disregarding.

An early poetic witness: an arrow’s movement

In a poem beginning with an address to a woman named Tayyā, Al-Aʿshā describes an arrow’s movement:

فَمَرَّ نَضِيُّ السَّهْمِ تَحْتَ لَبَانِهِ
وَجَالَ عَلَىٰ وَحْشِيِّهِ لَمْ يُثَمْثِمِ

fa-marra naḍiyyu al-sahmi taḥta labānihi / wa-jāla ʿalā waḥshiyyihi lam yuthamthimi

Translation: “The arrow’s shaft passed beneath his chest, and he wheeled toward his outer flank without pausing.” نَضِيّ here denotes the arrow’s shaft; the latter half concerns the animal’s movement.

فَمَرَّ expresses the arrow’s passing movement.

The poet describes an arrow’s path, not the passage of time or repetition of an act.

The witness illustrates a concrete use in poetry transmitted from near the period of revelation.

Al-Aʿshā lived across the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods, with most of his life before Islam. The line itself does not establish the poem’s exact date. Its attribution and its date relative to the Qur’an must therefore be considered separately.

Bitterness and sweetness

In another branch, Arabic distinguishes مُرّ from حُلْو.

طَعَامٌ مُرٌّ

ṭaʿāmun murrun

Translation: “Bitter food.”

طَعَامٌ حُلْوٌ

ṭaʿāmun ḥulwun

Translation: “Sweet food.”

Here bitterness is a property of taste.

The family can likewise describe a bitter plant or an unpleasant-tasting medicine.

Linguistically, bitterness does not necessarily mean harmfulness.

Some bitter foods or plants may be beneficial, while some sweet substances can be harmful in particular circumstances.

Bitterness describes taste, not a conclusive judgment about a substance’s goodness, badness, permissibility, or prohibition.

Al-Aʿshā’s poetry: bitter plants

In the same poem, Al-Aʿshā describes an animal with access to fresh, appealing vegetation:

رَعَى الرَّوْضَ وَالْوَسْمِيَّ حَتَّىٰ كَأَنَّمَا
يَرَىٰ بِيَبِيسِ الدَّوِّ أَمْرَارَ عَلْقَمِ

raʿā al-rawḍa wa-al-wasmiyya ḥattā ka-annamā / yarā bi-yabīsi al-dawwi amrāra ʿalqami

Translation: “It grazed the meadow and the growth of the season’s early rains, until the desert’s dry herbage seemed to it like bitter colocynth.” وَسْمِيّ concerns the early seasonal rains and their growth, not specifically spring rain.

أَمْرَار occurs here in the field of bitter plants.

The poet contrasts fresh, appealing fodder with dry, unpalatable vegetation.

This witness helps establish the bitterness branch.

أَمْرَار should not be confused with Qur’anic أَمَرُّ. The former concerns bitter plants or their plural designation; the latter is comparative in Al-Qamar 54:46, meaning more bitter or harsher.

Bitter human experience

Arabic extends bitterness from taste to human experience.

For example:

عَيْشٌ مُرٌّ

ʿayshun murrun

Translation: “A bitter, difficult life.”

Life does not literally have a taste sampled by the tongue.

The expression means that living conditions have become painful and unpleasant.

الأَمَرَّانِ is also attested for two great hardships, though accounts differ about precisely which two.

Some identify poverty and old age; others offer different explanations.

These uses show that bitterness can describe severe suffering and unpleasantness.

The extension does not make the root invariably refer to moral suffering or punishment.

Literal bitterness and bitter experience are distinct uses within one branch.

Tightly twisted rope and firmness

مَرِير and مَرِيرَة are attested for a firmly twisted strand or rope.

The strands are joined through twisting, increasing their strength.

أَمَرَّ الْحَبْلَ means to twist a rope firmly.

The image also appears in uses concerning resolve and strength.

اِسْتَمَرَّتْ مَرِيرَتُهُ can mean that someone’s will became firm and their decision strengthened.

No actual rope is the subject here; a person’s firmness is described through a tightly twisted strand.

An early witness: firmness of resolve

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Laqīṭ ibn Yaʿmur al-Iyādī includes:

حَتَّى اسْتَمَرَّتْ عَلَىٰ شَزْرٍ مَرِيرَتُهُ
مُسْتَحْكَمَ السِّنِّ لَا قَمْحًا وَلَا ضَرْعَا

ḥattā istamarrat ʿalā shazrin marīratuhu / mustaḥkama al-sinni lā qamḥan wa-lā ḍarʿā

Translation: “Until his resolve was firmly twisted and established; a mature, steadfast man, neither inexperienced nor weak.”

مَرِيرَتُهُ concerns the person’s firm ability and resolve.

اِسْتَمَرَّتْ here expresses becoming strong and established.

The witness shows that early istimrār was not limited to temporal continuation; it could also concern strength and firmness.

The poem is transmitted in the collection attributed to Laqīṭ ibn Yaʿmur. The attribution and transmission of very early poetry are not uniformly certain. The line is evidence within the received poetic tradition, not a document whose date and every wording are beyond dispute.

3. Passing in the Qur’an: people and mountains

Forms of the simple verb مَرَّ occur ten times in the Qur’an.

Most concern passing, while each context determines its particular kind.

Al-Baqarah 2:259: passing a ruined settlement

أَوْ كَالَّذِي مَرَّ عَلَىٰ قَرْيَةٍ وَهِيَ خَاوِيَةٌ عَلَىٰ عُرُوشِهَا

aw ka-alladhī marra ʿalā qaryatin wa-hiya khāwiyatun ʿalā ʿurūshihā

Al-Baqarah 2:259

Translation:

“Or like the one who passed a settlement that had collapsed upon its roofs.”

مَرَّ عَلَىٰ قَرْيَةٍ concerns passing beside or through the site of a settlement.

The verse continues with a question about its return to life after death and ruin.

Marra itself does not mean reflecting on resurrection.

Passing is the initial event; reflection on the ruin is described afterward.

Nor does marra establish that the person stayed there or inspected every part.

Hūd 11:38: passing and mocking

وَكُلَّمَا مَرَّ عَلَيْهِ مَلَأٌ مِنْ قَوْمِهِ سَخِرُوا مِنْهُ

wa-kullamā marra ʿalayhi malaʾun min qawmihi sakhirū minhu

Hūd 11:38

Translation:

“Whenever a group of leaders from his people passed him, they mocked him.”

The context is Noah building the ark.

مَرَّ عَلَيْهِ describes a group passing him.

Mocking is expressed separately by سَخِرُوا.

Marra should therefore not be translated as “they mocked.”

Repetition comes from كُلَّمَا, not from marra itself.

Their passing was accompanied by mockery on each occasion.

Yūsuf 12:105: passing signs

وَكَأَيِّنْ مِنْ آيَةٍ فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ يَمُرُّونَ عَلَيْهَا وَهُمْ عَنْهَا مُعْرِضُونَ

wa-ka-ayyin min āyatin fī al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍi yamurrūna ʿalayhā wa-hum ʿanhā muʿriḍūna

Yūsuf 12:105

Translation:

“How many signs in the heavens and earth they pass, while turning away from them.”

Passing and turning away appear together here.

Marra concerns passing and encountering signs.

Iʿrāḍ concerns turning away or disregarding.

These meanings should remain distinct.

Someone can pass something attentively, or pass the same place while ignoring its signs.

The verse describes the latter situation.

Passing is not inherently heedlessness; turning away is expressed independently in وَهُمْ عَنْهَا مُعْرِضُونَ.

Al-Furqān 25:72: passing idle talk with dignity

وَإِذَا مَرُّوا بِاللَّغْوِ مَرُّوا كِرَامًا

wa-idhā marrū bi-al-laghwi marrū kirāman

Al-Furqān 25:72

Translation:

“When they pass idle talk, they pass with dignity.”

The verb occurs twice.

The first occurrence describes passing idle talk.

The second specifies the manner through كِرَامًا.

Passing can here concern encountering worthless speech or conduct, rather than necessarily passing a particular physical object.

“With dignity” is not an independent meaning of the root; it comes from كِرَامًا.

The verse also does not require everyone to pass every incorrect statement without examination or an informed response.

The subject is laghw; its meaning and context determine the response being described.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:137: passing the traces of former peoples

وَإِنَّكُمْ لَتَمُرُّونَ عَلَيْهِمْ مُصْبِحِينَ

wa-innakum la-tamurrūna ʿalayhim muṣbiḥīna

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:137

Translation:

“You pass by them in the morning.”

This follows the account of Lot’s people.

Passing refers to their site or surviving traces.

The next verse also mentions nighttime.

The sequence indicates a route familiar to the addressees.

Marra itself does not mean closely inspecting remains, understanding history, or learning a lesson.

Those may follow from passing, but are established through the statements and context.

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:30: passing and mocking gestures

وَإِذَا مَرُّوا بِهِمْ يَتَغَامَزُونَ

wa-idhā marrū bihim yataghāmazūna

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:30

Translation:

“When they passed them, they exchanged mocking gestures.”

The passage concerns people who mocked believers.

Marra expresses passing alone.

يَتَغَامَزُونَ specifies the behavior accompanying it.

The same verb can thus accompany dignified conduct in one passage and mockery in another.

The root carries no fixed moral judgment by itself.

Yūnus 10:12: going on after distress is removed

فَلَمَّا كَشَفْنَا عَنْهُ ضُرَّهُ مَرَّ كَأَنْ لَمْ يَدْعُنَا إِلَىٰ ضُرٍّ مَسَّهُ

fa-lammā kashafnā ʿanhu ḍurrahu marra ka-an lam yadʿunā ilā ḍurrin massahu

Yūnus 10:12

Translation:

“When We removed his distress, he went on as though he had never called upon Us about the distress that touched him.”

Here marra concerns behavior after relief from affliction.

The person calls upon The God in difficulty, then acts afterward as though the earlier appeal had been forgotten.

In this context, marra can mean going on one’s way and returning to former conduct.

The verb itself does not mean ingratitude.

Ingratitude emerges from comparing behavior during and after the distress.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:189: carrying on with pregnancy

فَلَمَّا تَغَشَّاهَا حَمَلَتْ حَمْلًا خَفِيفًا فَمَرَّتْ بِهِ فَلَمَّا أَثْقَلَتْ دَعَوَا اللَّهَ رَبَّهُمَا

fa-lammā taghashshāhā ḥamalat ḥamlan khafīfan fa-marrat bihi

Al-Aʿrāf 7:189

Translation:

“When he lay with her, she bore a light burden and carried on with it; when she grew heavy, both called upon The God, their Lord.”

This differs from spatial passage.

Early explanations chiefly understand فَمَرَّتْ بِهِ as carrying on with pregnancy through its initial stage.

At first the pregnancy is a light burden and the woman can continue ordinary activities. As it becomes heavier, her situation changes.

Some early explanations instead connect the expression with uncertainty about whether pregnancy has occurred.

That explanation has no clearer support in the direct sense of marra than carrying on with the pregnancy.

It should not be translated as the woman passing beside the fetus; that does not fit the sentence.

The verse illustrates use of marra for continuing in a state over time.

Al-Naml 27:88: mountains moving like clouds

وَتَرَى الْجِبَالَ تَحْسَبُهَا جَامِدَةً وَهِيَ تَمُرُّ مَرَّ السَّحَابِ صُنْعَ اللَّهِ الَّذِي أَتْقَنَ كُلَّ شَيْءٍ إِنَّهُ خَبِيرٌ بِمَا تَفْعَلُونَ

wa-tarā al-jibāla taḥsabuhā jāmidatan wa-hiya tamurru marra al-saḥābi ṣunʿa Allāhi alladhī atqana kulla shayʾin innahu khabīrun bi-mā tafʿalūna

Al-Naml 27:88

Forqan translation:

“You see the mountains, thinking them motionless, while they pass like clouds—the work of The God, who has perfected everything. He is aware of what you do.”

The root occurs twice here:

تَمُرُّ, an imperfect verb.

مَرَّ, a verbal noun.

مَرَّ السَّحَابِ compares their passing movement to that of clouds.

The sentence therefore describes the mountains moving and passing.

The comparison alone does not determine exact speed, direction, or physical mechanism.

Resurrection or the earth’s present motion?

The preceding verse, Al-Naml 27:87, speaks of the trumpet being blown and fear among heaven’s and earth’s inhabitants.

A reading concerning the mountains’ transformation at resurrection therefore has clear contextual support.

Verse 88 uses the imperfect verbs تَرَى, تَحْسَبُهَا, and تَمُرُّ and then speaks of perfected divine workmanship.

Some use these features to consider a reading connected with the present order of creation. Yet an imperfect verb can depict a future scene vividly; it does not by itself establish present time.

Neither observation independently proves a particular scientific mechanism.

The earth’s motion, crustal movement, and the displacement of mountains are studied in natural science. Marra itself does not restrict the movement to rotation, plate motion, or another particular scientific phenomenon.

The base translation should preserve the clear image: mountains pass like clouds.

Its temporal setting and mechanism belong to separate interpretive study informed by context and knowledge of nature.

4. Marrah: once, an occasion, and recurrence

مَرَّة is one of this root’s most widespread Qur’anic uses.

It denotes an occurrence or occasion.

An act occurring once can be described as مَرَّةً وَاحِدَةً; twice as مَرَّتَيْنِ; larger counts use the plural مَرَّات.

This relates to the passage of time, since each occurrence is one occasion.

Marrah does not itself specify a duration such as an hour, day, or year.

One act may last moments and another several days.

It principally counts occurrences, rather than measuring duration.

Al-Baqarah 2:229: two occasions of divorce

الطَّلَاقُ مَرَّتَانِ فَإِمْسَاكٌ بِمَعْرُوفٍ أَوْ تَسْرِيحٌ بِإِحْسَانٍ

al-ṭalāqu marratāni fa-imsākun bi-maʿrūfin aw tasrīḥun bi-iḥsānin

Al-Baqarah 2:229

Translation:

“Divorce is twice; then retention with propriety or release with kindness.”

مَرَّتَانِ is dual and denotes two occasions.

It belongs to M R R, not Ṭ L Q.

Al-ṭalāq names the act counted; marratān gives its number of occurrences.

Marratān alone cannot establish every legal detail of divorce.

Conditions, timing, the possibility of resuming the relationship, and the effects of successive divorces require the related passages.

The lexical meaning here is two times or occasions.

Al-Nūr and other counting uses

Al-Nūr 24:58 states:

ثَلَاثَ مَرَّاتٍ

thalātha marrātin

Al-Nūr 24:58

“Three times.”

The verse concerns three occasions for requesting permission to enter private space.

مَرَّات is the plural of مَرَّة.

It does not itself name the three particular times; these are specified later in the verse.

Al-Tawbah 9:126 states:

فِي كُلِّ عَامٍ مَرَّةً أَوْ مَرَّتَيْنِ

fī kulli ʿāmin marratan aw marratayni

Al-Tawbah 9:126

“Once or twice in every year.”

One or two occurrences here concern testing or affliction.

Marrah is not the trial itself; it counts its occurrences.

Awwala marrah: the first time

The expression occurs in several verses.

In Al-Anʿām 6:94:

كَمَا خَلَقْنَاكُمْ أَوَّلَ مَرَّةٍ

ka-mā khalaqnākum awwala marratin

Al-Anʿām 6:94

“As We created you the first time.”

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:51:

قُلِ الَّذِي فَطَرَكُمْ أَوَّلَ مَرَّةٍ

quli alladhī faṭarakum awwala marratin

Al-Isrāʾ 17:51

“Say: the One who originated you the first time.”

In Yā Sīn 36:79:

قُلْ يُحْيِيهَا الَّذِي أَنْشَأَهَا أَوَّلَ مَرَّةٍ

qul yuḥyīhā alladhī anshaʾahā awwala marratin

Yā Sīn 36:79

“Say: the One who first brought them into being will give them life.”

Marrah identifies the first occasion in these constructions.

The word does not explain how creation occurred or the stages of human emergence.

The expression also occurs in Al-Anʿām 6:110, Al-Tawbah 9:13, Al-Tawbah 9:83, Al-Isrāʾ 17:7, Al-Kahf 18:48, and Fuṣṣilat 41:21.

In some passages the first time concerns creation; in others, an earlier act or event.

It is a counting construction whose subject comes from its verb and context.

Marratan ukhrā: another time

Ṭā Hā 20:37 states:

وَلَقَدْ مَنَنَّا عَلَيْكَ مَرَّةً أُخْرَىٰ

wa-la-qad manannā ʿalayka marratan ukhrā

Ṭā Hā 20:37

Translation:

“We indeed bestowed favor upon you on another occasion.”

مَرَّةً أُخْرَىٰ identifies a further occurrence or occasion.

The favor’s nature emerges from the continuation of Moses’ story.

Marrah does not establish that the favor must be of the same kind as another favor.

Reward twice over, or punishment twice

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:54 states:

أُولَٰئِكَ يُؤْتَوْنَ أَجْرَهُمْ مَرَّتَيْنِ بِمَا صَبَرُوا

ulāʾika yuʾtawna ajrahum marratayni bi-mā ṣabarū

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:54

“They receive their reward twice over because they persevered.” “Twice over” here can convey double reward and does not necessarily specify two separate times of payment.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:31 also uses مَرَّتَيْنِ for reward.

In Al-Tawbah 9:101, the same form concerns punishment:

سَنُعَذِّبُهُمْ مَرَّتَيْنِ ثُمَّ يُرَدُّونَ إِلَىٰ عَذَابٍ عَظِيمٍ

sa-nuʿadhdhibuhum marratayni thumma yuraddūna ilā ʿadhābin ʿaẓīmin

Al-Tawbah 9:101

“We shall punish them twice; then they will be returned to a tremendous punishment.”

Marratayn expresses twofoldness: two occasions in a context of repeated punishment, or reward twice over in a context of recompense.

The nature, timing, and manner of receiving the reward or punishment do not follow from the word alone. “Twice” does not invariably establish two distinct times of payment.

Al-Tawbah 9:101, for example, mentions two punishments, but identifying each precisely requires other evidence.

Corruption twice and repeated covenant-breaking

Al-Isrāʾ 17:4 states:

لَتُفْسِدُنَّ فِي الْأَرْضِ مَرَّتَيْنِ

la-tufsidunna fī al-arḍi marratayni

Al-Isrāʾ 17:4

“You will surely cause corruption in the land twice.”

Marratayn counts the occasions of corruption.

The word itself does not specify its kind, agents, or historical details.

Al-Anfāl 8:56 states:

ثُمَّ يَنْقُضُونَ عَهْدَهُمْ فِي كُلِّ مَرَّةٍ

thumma yanquḍūna ʿahdahum fī kulli marratin

Al-Anfāl 8:56

“Then they break their covenant on every occasion.”

Kulla marrah indicates repetition on the occasions concerned.

The count or recurrence comes from the construction involving marrah; the conduct’s moral value comes from the verbs and context.

Sabʿīna marrah: seventy times

Al-Tawbah 9:80 states:

إِنْ تَسْتَغْفِرْ لَهُمْ سَبْعِينَ مَرَّةً فَلَنْ يَغْفِرَ اللَّهُ لَهُمْ

in tastaghfir lahum sabʿīna marratan fa-lan yaghfira Allāhu lahum

Al-Tawbah 9:80

“If you seek forgiveness for them seventy times, The God will not forgive them.”

Marrah counts requests for forgiveness.

Seventy can emphasize abundance in some Arabic rhetorical constructions.

Its precise function here depends on context and Qur’anic numerical usage.

Marrah itself neither means unlimited abundance nor alters the number seventy.

A direct translation should retain “seventy times.”

5. Mirrah in Al-Najm: strength and firmness

عَلَّمَهُ شَدِيدُ الْقُوَىٰ ۝ ذُو مِرَّةٍ فَاسْتَوَىٰ

ʿallamahu shadīdu al-quwā / dhū mirratin fa-istawā

Al-Najm 53:5; Al-Najm 53:6

Translation:

“One mighty in power taught him—possessed of strength and firmness; then he became established.”

Al-Najm 53:5; Al-Najm 53:6

مِرَّة here has a kasrah beneath the mīm.

It differs from مَرَّة, one time.

In established Arabic usage, ذُو مِرَّةٍ describes a person or being with strength, ability, and firmness.

Lexically, that strength can involve bodily solidity, capacity, or even firmness of judgment.

Does mirrah mean beauty?

Two principal explanations of ذُو مِرَّةٍ appear in the interpretive tradition.

One emphasizes strength and power.

The other concerns sound constitution, firm formation, and well-proportioned appearance.

Some early explanations prefer soundness and firmness of constitution while also recording the strength reading.

These are not necessarily complete opposites.

A soundly and firmly constituted body can also be described as strong.

Yet “beauty” without qualification should not be the primary equivalent: established usage is closer to strength and solidity.

Dhū mirrah also does not mean occurring once or being a single occasion.

Who bears the description in Al-Najm?

Al-Najm 53:5 and Al-Najm 53:6 describe a powerful teacher.

The familiar interpretation identifies the teacher as Gabriel, the angel of revelation.

Gabriel is not named in these two verses themselves.

The identification relates to other revelation passages and interpretive explanations.

The root study’s principal concern is mirrah as strength and firmness.

That meaning should remain distinct from the identity of its bearer and the interpretation of fa-stawā.

Connection with tightly twisted rope

مَرِيرَة and مَرِير denote firmly twisted strands or rope in Arabic.

This also supplies an image for firm resolve and personal strength.

It helps explain dhū mirrah.

The verse should not, however, be translated “possessing a tightly twisted rope”; that substitutes the lexical image for the adjective’s established meaning.

“Possessed of strength and firmness” is appropriate in Al-Najm 53:6.

6. Continuance and bitterness in Al-Qamar

M R R has three occurrences in Al-Qamar: two uses of mustamirr, whose interpretation is discussed, and amarr in the bitterness branch.

Al-Qamar 54:2: siḥrun mustamirr

وَإِنْ يَرَوْا آيَةً يُعْرِضُوا وَيَقُولُوا سِحْرٌ مُسْتَمِرٌّ

wa-in yaraw āyatan yuʿriḍū wa-yaqūlū siḥrun mustamirrun

Al-Qamar 54:2

Forqan translation:

“If they see a sign, they turn away and say: continuing magic.”

مُسْتَمِرّ is an active participle of Form X.

Its precise sense here has been debated.

Three important lexical readings are offered:

First, continuing or persistent.

Second, passing and destined to disappear.

Third, strong, firm, or powerful.

These come from attested branches of M R R, but differ in meaning.

The first takes the opponents to describe the sign as continuing magic.

The second takes them to call it magic that will pass away.

The third emphasizes the force of the alleged magic.

The passing-away reading is recorded in early interpretation.

Continuance and intensity are also attested explanations.

Morphologically, mustamirr is compatible with continuation, but Form X alone does not settle one meaning.

The context identifies these words as the claim of people rejecting the divine sign and calling it magic.

Choosing “continuing magic” therefore does not mean the Qur’an affirms actual magic here; it reports the opponents’ allegation.

“Continuing magic” is defensible as a base rendering. The lexical study should also recognize passing and powerful, without merging all three into one translation.

Al-Qamar 54:19: naḥsin mustamirr

إِنَّا أَرْسَلْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ رِيحًا صَرْصَرًا فِي يَوْمِ نَحْسٍ مُسْتَمِرٍّ

innā arsalnā ʿalayhim rīḥan ṣarṣaran fī yawmi naḥsin mustamirrin

Al-Qamar 54:19

Forqan translation:

“We sent upon them a fierce, cold wind on a day of continuing misfortune.”

مُسْتَمِرّ qualifies نَحْس here.

On the familiar reading, it describes the continuance of the adverse condition affecting ʿĀd.

Two other possibilities also appear in lexical and interpretive explanations.

One is the intensity and firmness of the calamity.

The other is its bitterness and unpleasantness.

These possibilities concern different branches of M R R.

The context nevertheless concerns a punishment sent upon ʿĀd.

Fuṣṣilat 41:16 also states:

فَأَرْسَلْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ رِيحًا صَرْصَرًا فِي أَيَّامٍ نَحِسَاتٍ

fa-arsalnā ʿalayhim rīḥan ṣarṣaran fī ayyāmin naḥisātin

Fuṣṣilat 41:16

“We sent upon them a fierce, cold wind on days of misfortune.”

The connection shows that the adversity was not restricted to a brief instant.

Continuing or persistent is therefore suitable for mustamirr in Al-Qamar 54:19.

The verse does not establish that a particular weekday or calendar date is inherently unlucky and harmful for everyone.

It describes a day when punishment befell a particular people.

Permanent, intrinsic bad luck attached to calendar days does not follow from mustamirr.

Al-Qamar 54:46: adhā wa-amarr

بَلِ السَّاعَةُ مَوْعِدُهُمْ وَالسَّاعَةُ أَدْهَىٰ وَأَمَرُّ

bali al-sāʿatu mawʿiduhum wa-al-sāʿatu adhā wa-amarru

Al-Qamar 54:46

Forqan translation:

“Rather, the Hour is their appointed time, and the Hour is more grievous and more bitter.”

أَمَرُّ is a comparative from the bitterness branch.

Its direct meaning is “more bitter.”

The subject, however, is the Hour, not food or drink.

Bitterness thus describes a difficult, unpleasant experience.

Alongside أَدْهَىٰ, which stresses greater severity and dreadfulness, أَمَرُّ highlights greater distress.

This confirms the bitterness branch’s presence in the Qur’an.

Amarr does not establish that a bitter substance will be fed to someone at that Hour.

It concerns the event’s severity and unpleasantness.

Nor does it mean more transitory or more continuous; its form and context belong to bitterness.

7. Distinctions from related roots

M R R and J W Z

Jāza concerns passing and crossing.

Marra shares that field.

Jawāz and ijtiyāz can emphasize crossing a boundary, obstacle, or location, while marra also broadly includes passing and elapsing.

A cloud can be described as passing, and time is associated with an occasion’s passing.

This is a tendency of usage, not an absolute boundary.

M R R and ʿ B R

ʿAbara in physical usage concerns crossing from one side to another.

This is close to marra.

The ʿ B R family also includes moving from outward appearance to meaning, taking a lesson, and interpretation.

M R R in the Qur’an includes passing, counting occasions, strength, and bitterness.

The roots overlap in some spatial uses, but their complete semantic families differ.

M R R and M Ḍ Y

Maḍā concerns going, passing, and elapsing.

It is close to marra, particularly with time.

Marrah, however, denotes an occurrence rather than merely elapsed time.

Marratayn can mean doing something twice, whereas maḍā may simply describe a period or event passing.

Every marrah should not be converted into a fixed duration.

M R R and K R R

These roots meet in the field of repetition.

Karrara means to repeat or bring something back.

Marrah is a unit for counting occurrences.

Marrah emphasizes an occasion itself; repetition concerns returning to or doing something again.

An act may happen only once: marrah is appropriate, though repetition has not occurred.

Repeating something several times implies more than one occurrence.

The distinction should be retained in passages using marratayn and thalātha marrāt.

M R R and Ḥ L W

In the taste branch, murr contrasts with ḥulw.

مُرّ is bitter; حُلْو is sweet.

Bitter and sweet lives can also describe contrasting human experiences.

Figurative bitterness and sweetness do not imply a literal taste in an event.

Amarr in Al-Qamar 54:46 expresses the appointed Hour’s greater grievousness.

Its imagery relates to bitter food, but the verse does not concern tasting a dish.

M R R and Q W Y

Q W Y concerns ability and strength.

Mirrah in Al-Najm 53:6 also denotes strength and firmness.

M R R additionally includes passing, bitterness, and counting occasions.

Strength is therefore only one branch.

Dhū mirrah in Al-Najm should not be translated “possessing repetition” or “one who passes.”

M R R and D W M

Dawām concerns endurance and continuation.

Istimrār likewise often means continuation.

Yet early mustamirr can also concern strength and firmness in certain constructions.

Istimrār and dawām are therefore not exact synonyms everywhere.

This breadth underlies the different explanations of Al-Qamar 54:2.

In ordinary modern Persian and Arabic, mustamirr and istimrār chiefly concern continuity.

That modern meaning should not become the only possible early meaning without historical examination.

8. Interpretive challenges and translation proposals

Distinguishing four similar forms

Translations and word-by-word explanations should preserve these distinctions:

مَرَّ: Passed or went by.

مَرَّة: Once or one occasion.

مِرَّة: Strength and firmness.

أَمَرُّ: More bitter or more grievous.

They share a root but not one fixed equivalent.

The mīm vowel in marrah and mirrah is especially decisive.

مَرَّتَانِ in Al-Baqarah 2:229 denotes two occasions.

ذُو مِرَّةٍ in Al-Najm 53:6 denotes strength.

Ignoring the distinction completely changes the passages’ meanings.

Passing

“Passed” or “passed by” suits the verb in Al-Baqarah 2:259, Hūd 11:38, Yūsuf 12:105, Al-Ṣāffāt 37:137, and Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:30.

“Went on” fits Yūnus 10:12.

“Passing with dignity” fits Al-Furqān 25:72, but dignity comes from kirāman.

“Pass” or “move past” clearly renders tamurru in Al-Naml 27:88.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:189, “continued with the pregnancy” or “carried on with that light burden” is more appropriate than mechanically saying “passed beside it.”

Counting occasions

“Time” or “occasion” is suitable for counting marrah.

أَوَّلَ مَرَّةٍ means “the first time.”

مَرَّتَيْنِ means “twice.”

ثَلَاثَ مَرَّاتٍ means “three times.”

فِي كُلِّ مَرَّةٍ means “on every occasion.”

The event’s nature and duration come from the surrounding passage.

Strength and firmness

In Al-Najm 53:6:

ذُو مِرَّةٍ فَاسْتَوَىٰ

dhū mirratin fa-istawā

Al-Najm 53:6

Proposed translation:

“Possessed of strength and firmness; then he became established.”

An explanation may note ability and solidity, and the early reading of sound, firm constitution.

These should not all be crowded into a brief translation as incompatible alternatives.

Continuance

In Al-Qamar 54:2:

سِحْرٌ مُسْتَمِرٌّ

siḥrun mustamirrun

Al-Qamar 54:2

Forqan’s existing rendering:

“Continuing magic.”

This fits one established lexical meaning.

The explanation should also note the early readings of passing and strong or firm.

In Al-Qamar 54:19:

نَحْسٍ مُسْتَمِرٍّ

naḥsin mustamirrin

Al-Qamar 54:19

“With continuing misfortune.”

Persistence of the adverse condition is the preferred contextual sense.

Bitterness

In Al-Qamar 54:46:

وَالسَّاعَةُ أَدْهَىٰ وَأَمَرُّ

wa-al-sāʿatu adhā wa-amarru

Al-Qamar 54:46

Forqan translation:

“And the Hour is more grievous and more bitter.”

The rendering preserves both adjectives and the established meaning of amarr.

Reducing amarr to “more severe” would lose the image of bitterness.

Brief root meaning and contextual meanings

Brief root meaning: Passing and elapsing; in other branches, bitterness and unpleasantness, and strength and firmness. An occasion or one time is also associated with the field of passing.

In passage verses: Passing a place, person, or sign; in Al-Naml 27:88, mountains moving and passing like clouds.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:189: Carrying on with a light pregnancy.

In marrah and marrāt passages: One or several occasions; with additional numerical expressions, a count or recurrence of an act, and in reward passages the possibility of recompense twice over.

In Al-Najm 53:6: Strength and firmness.

In Al-Qamar 54:2: Continuing or persistent, alongside alternative lexical readings of passing or powerful.

In Al-Qamar 54:19: Continuance of the adverse punishment affecting ʿĀd.

In Al-Qamar 54:46: More bitter and grievous.

9. Synthesis

M R R has several established Arabic branches that should not be reduced to one simple meaning.

First is passing and elapsing, visible in the movement of people, animals, clouds, and other phenomena.

Second is an occurrence or one time. Marrah is associated with the field of passage and occurrence and is frequently used to count occasions in the Qur’an. This semantic connection alone does not establish the historical order in which the meanings developed.

Third is bitterness, both literal taste and its extension to harsh experience.

Fourth is strength and firmness, found in tightly twisted rope, firm resolve, and dhū mirrah.

The branches have linguistic connections, but cannot all be derived from one original image without historical evidence.

Passing is clear in the Qur’anic movement of people and mountains. In counting passages, marrah denotes an event or one performance of an act.

Mirrah in Al-Najm 53:6 describes strength and firmness and must not be confused with marrah, once.

Mustamirr in Al-Qamar has a broader range and appears in two contexts. Continuity is defensible in both, while early lexical explanations also attest passing or firmness.

Amarr in the same surah expresses the bitterness branch in describing the appointed Hour’s severity.

Mustamirr should not invariably mean forever, just as it should not invariably mean transitory or strong.

The image of moving mountains in Al-Naml 27:88 should be retained. Its temporal setting and kind of motion are questions separate from defining marra.

Final definition: M R R has principal fields of passing and bitterness, with an established branch of strength and firmness. Marrah belongs to counting occurrences within the passage field; amarr expresses a more bitter experience; mirrah expresses strength within the firmness field. The precise Qur’anic meaning depends on vowels, morphology, and context. Passing, an occasion, bitterness, and strength should remain distinct meanings within one family, rather than being reduced to one fixed rendering. Semantic connections alone do not establish the historical order of derivation.
