Qur’anic Root

The Qur’anic Root L Q Y

Meeting, encounter, facing, two things reaching one another, and receiving something in the moment of encounter

Morphological forms of this root

The root ل ق ي (L Q Y) does not simply mean seeing. Seeing may happen from a distance, but liqāʾ usually carries nearness, contact, arrival, or encounter. When a person yalqā something, he does not merely see it; he faces it, reaches it, or encounters its effect and reality.

General meaning of the root L Q Y

The root ل ق ي (L Q Y) points to arriving, meeting, encountering, and facing. In this root, two sides or two things usually reach one another in such a way that distance is removed and an encounter occurs.

This encounter may be simple and outward, like two people meeting. It may be social and historical, like two groups clashing. It may be inward and cognitive, like receiving a word that reaches the heart and understanding. And it may be existential and final, like the human being facing his Lord, his account, or the outcome of his own action.

So the core of this root is not only seeing; it is reaching the point of contact and encounter. In liqāʾ, something leaves distance and becomes present before the human being.

Usage of the root in Arabic

Arabic says لَقِيَ فلانٌ فلانًا (laqiya flānun flānanā): so-and-so met so-and-so or faced him. This meeting may be friendly, accidental, or may happen in a field of conflict and battle.

One says لَقِيَ العدوَّ (laqiya al-ʿdwwa): he encountered the enemy. This is not merely seeing the enemy; it is reaching the field of confrontation. One may also say لَقِيَ خيرًا (laqiya khyranā) or لَقِيَ شرًّا (laqiya shrranā): he encountered good or evil and experienced it.

Thus Arabic can say that a person met someone, encountered hardship, faced death, or met the result of an action. This breadth shows that the root begins from contact and meeting, then extends to experience, receiving, and confrontation with consequence.

The material and linguistic image of liqāʾ

The main image of this root is two things reaching one another. Two roads may meet. Two people may run into one another on a path. Two armies may meet in battle. A word may reach the ear or heart. A human being may face the result of what he has done.

In this image, liqāʾ means that distance has been removed. As long as something is far away, one may only hear about it or imagine it. But when one yalqā it, that thing becomes present before him. It is no longer merely report or imagination; it is encounter.

This matters for understanding the Qur’an. Liqāʾ Allāh is not merely a mental or doctrinal phrase; it means that the human being will one day face a reality of which he may now be heedless.

Alqā and bringing something into the field of contact

One important branch of this root is أَلْقَى (alqā). In material usage, this form can mean to cast, throw, or place something; something leaves its prior position and comes into contact with the ground, hand, ear, heart, or field of receiving.

When speech, remembrance, fear, love, or a word is ulqiya, it is as though something from beyond the human being enters his field of reception. Speech does not remain in the air; it lands in the ear, heart, or field of understanding.

This is why ألقى السمع (alqā al-samʿ) is a beautiful expression. The human being does not merely hear; he places his hearing in the path of the word and lets the word arrive in his attention.

Examples outside the Qur’an

Ordinary Arabic says لقيتُه في الطريق (lqytuh fy al-ṭryq): I met him on the road. Here liqāʾ is a simple outward encounter.

It says لقي الجيشُ الجيشَ (lqy al-jyshu al-jysha): the army met the army. Here liqāʾ is not merely seeing; it is serious field confrontation.

It also says لقيَ الرجلُ شدّةً (lqya al-rjlu shddah): the man encountered hardship. In this usage, what stands opposite the human being is not a person, but a condition. He “meets” hardship, meaning he experiences it and becomes engaged with it.

Morphological and semantic forms

The simple verbal form is لَقِيَ / يَلْقَى (laqiya / yalqā): he met, faced, or encountered something. لَقُوا (laqū) belongs to this same form.

لِقَاء (liqāʾ) points to meeting or encounter itself. In the Qur’an, it appears many times concerning liqāʾ Allāh and liqāʾ al-ākhirah.

تَلَقَّى (talaqqā) highlights receiving. When you receive something through talaqqī, you do not only face it; you take it and bring it into the field of your understanding or being.

أَلْقَى (alqā) outwardly means to cast or place, but in root sense it sends something into a place where it will be encountered or received.

The difference between seeing, meeting, and encounter

To understand this root, one must distinguish seeing from meeting. Seeing may happen from a distance and may not produce deep impact. But liqāʾ is usually nearer. In liqāʾ, a person or thing reaches the point of contact with another.

Raʾā points more to seeing and observing. Laqiya points to facing and encounter. A human being may see something without truly facing it. But when he yalqā it, a kind of contact, receiving, or encounter takes place.

For this reason, laqū should not always be translated only as “they saw.” Sometimes “they met” is better, sometimes “they encountered,” sometimes “they faced,” and sometimes “they met its consequence.”

Usage of the root in the Qur’an

In the Qur’an, the root ل ق ي (L Q Y) is used for human meetings, the confrontation of groups, receiving revelation or words, casting something, and facing God, account, death, or punishment.

In some verses, liqāʾ is simple and human, as when a group meets another group. In some verses, it is historical and field-based, as when two gatherings or two armies confront each other. In some verses, it is cognitive, as in receiving words. In other verses, liqāʾ is decisive and final, as in liqāʾ Allāh.

Laqū and outward meeting

The word لَقُوا (laqū) appears in the Qur’an with the meaning “they met” or “they faced.” In some contexts, it refers to an outward meeting between people, such as when a group meets the believers and says something, but when they withdraw to their own group, they show another face.

Here, laqū is not merely an ordinary meeting. The meeting becomes the scene where doubleness is exposed. In meeting others, a person shows his tongue, face, and direction.

The contrast between laqū and khalaw in hypocrisy

In 2:14, there is a precise contrast between لَقُوا (laqūā) and خَلَوْا (khalawā): وَإِذَا لَقُوا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا قَالُوا آمَنَّا وَإِذَا خَلَوْا إِلَىٰ شَيَاطِينِهِمْ قَالُوا إِنَّا مَعَكُمْ (waidhā laqūā alladhīna āmanūā qālū āmannā waidhā khalawā ilā shayāṭīnihim qālū innā maʿakum).

Laqū is the scene of encounter, where they face the believers in public space and say what fits that setting. But khalaw is withdrawal into privacy, where they leave the public field and become alone with their devils.

This contrast shows that hypocrisy has two faces: one in encounter and one in private withdrawal. In liqāʾ, a face suitable to the environment is made; in privacy, the inner attachment and direction become visible.

Talaqqā and conscious receiving

The form تَلَقَّى (talaqqā) in the Qur’an points to receiving and taking something. In 2:37, Adam receives words from his Lord: فَتَلَقَّىٰ آدَمُ مِنْ رَبِّهِ كَلِمَاتٍ (fatalaqqaā ādamu min rabbihi kalimātin).

Here Adam does not merely face the words. He receives them, accepts them, and those words become part of his return. Talaqqī is therefore a meeting that includes receiving and acceptance.

Alqā as casting or bringing into encounter

The form أَلْقَى (alqā) is broad in the Qur’an. Sometimes it means casting a staff, throwing something, or placing something in a field of sight and encounter. Sometimes it means casting speech, fear, love, or meaning.

In 50:37, the wording is: أَوْ أَلْقَى السَّمْعَ وَهُوَ شَهِيدٌ (aw alqaā al-samʿa wahuwa shahīdun). This shows the human being bringing his hearing into the field of receiving the word and listening with presence.

Liqāʾ Allāh and facing the final reality

One of the deepest Qur’anic uses of this root is لِقَاء الله (liqāʾ Allāh). This expression should not be reduced to simple “seeing.” Liqāʾ Allāh means the human being facing the final reality, his Lord, the account, the result of life, and a reality that can no longer be denied or escaped.

In this world, a human being may speak about God, believe in Him, deny Him, or be heedless of Him. But liqāʾ is the moment when distance is removed and reality stands before the human being.

In 2:46, the humble are those who believe they will meet their Lord: أَنَّهُمْ مُلَاقُو رَبِّهِمْ (annahum mulāqū rabbihim).

The difference between liqāʾ Allāh and physical seeing

In the discussion of liqāʾ Allāh, liqāʾ must be distinguished from physical seeing. Ruʾyah more directly points to seeing and observing, and at the bodily level it may suggest visual grasp. But the Qur’an says about God: لَا تُدْرِكُهُ الْأَبْصَارُ (lā tudrikuhu al-abṣāru).

For this reason, liqāʾ Allāh should not be reduced to physical seeing or visual enclosure. Liqāʾ means standing in the presence of truth, the removal of heedlessness, facing the account, and encountering the Lord toward whom the human being had faith, denial, heedlessness, or responsibility in this world.

Translation suggestions for Forqan

لَقِيَ / لَقُوا (laqiya / laqūā): he/they met; faced; encountered.

وَإِذَا لَقُوا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا (waidhā laqūā alladhīna āmanūā): when they met those who believed; when they entered the field of encounter with the believers.

لِقَاء (liqāʾ): meeting; encounter; facing.

لِقَاء الله (liqāʾ allāh): facing God; meeting the Lord; encounter with the final reality and account.

تَلَقَّى (talaqqaā): received; accepted; took something and faced it.

أَلْقَى (alqaā): cast; placed; conveyed; brought something into the field of receiving or encounter.

أَلْقَى السَّمْعَ (alqaā al-samʿa): listened attentively; placed his hearing in the path of receiving speech.

مُلَاقُو رَبِّهِمْ (mulāqū rabbihim): those who will surely meet their Lord; those who will stand before Him.

Summary

The root ل ق ي (L Q Y) in Arabic indicates meeting, encounter, arrival, receiving, and facing. It begins from a simple image: two things reach one another and the distance between them is removed.

In the Qur’an, the root becomes much broader. Human beings meet one another, groups confront one another, words are received, things are cast, and finally the human being faces God, the account, and the result of his own action.

So laqū does not merely mean “they saw.” More accurately, it means “they met” or “they faced”: the moment when distance is removed and the human being enters a scene of encounter, speech, receiving, or responsibility.
