The Arabic root F Ḍ Ḍ concerns breaking something, separating its parts, and dispersing a whole that was previously connected or assembled. Faḍḍa means to break and separate; infaḍḍa means to disperse or break up.

The same lexical family includes fiḍḍah, the name of silver: a valuable metal used for ornaments, vessels, and exchangeable assets.

In the Qur’an, this root occurs in two distinct semantic fields. The verb infaḍḍa describes people dispersing from around the Prophet, turning toward trade and amusement, and an attempt to break up the company accompanying the Messenger. Fiḍḍah, by contrast, names silver, sometimes as worldly wealth and sometimes in descriptions of blessings in the hereafter.

Dictionaries place these branches in one family, but their shared letters alone do not prove that both historically developed from a single meaning. In Qur’anic usage, infiḍāḍ concerns dispersal, whereas fiḍḍah denotes a particular metal.

1. The root in early Arabic

Breaking and separating parts

Classical Arabic records faḍḍa al-shayʾa for breaking something and separating its parts.

For example, when a whole object is divided into pieces by a blow or pressure, the verb faḍḍa may be used.

Here, breaking involves separation. Accordingly, fuḍāḍ denotes fragments or pieces resulting from breakage.

Faḍḍa al-khātama also means to break or open a seal: a seal placed on a letter or document to keep it closed until opened.

This usage shows that faḍḍ does not necessarily mean destroying something completely. Breaking a seal may open access to something previously closed.

The central meaning here is nevertheless the breaking of continuity or the removal of a closed, intact condition, rather than reading the letter or learning its contents.

Lexical sources also record faḍḍa al-qawma for dispersing people who have gathered.

No material object is broken in this case. People assembled in one place or around a center cease to be gathered there.

The verbal branch thus includes two related uses: breaking the continuity of something, and dispersing or breaking up a gathering of people.

A witness from pre-Islamic poetry

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī contains this couplet:

يَطِيرُ فُضَاضًا بَيْنَهَا كُلُّ قَوْنَسٍ
وَيَتْبَعُهَا مِنْهُمْ فَرَاشُ الْحَوَاجِبِ

yaṭīru fuḍāḍan baynahā kullu qawnasin / wa-yatbaʿuhā minhum farāshu al-ḥawājibi

“Every helmet crest flies in fragments among them—the swords—and fragments of the bones around their brows follow.”

The poet describes the force of clashing swords and the shattering of the warriors’ helmets and bones.

Qawnas denotes the upper part of the head or the raised crest of a helmet; fuḍāḍan describes its fragmented, scattered condition.

This witness matters because fuḍāḍ does not simply mean the dispersal of members of a group here. Something material is broken by a blow, and its parts separate.

Breaking and scattering into fragments therefore occur in the language of early Arabic poetry.

The couplet is transmitted in a recognized poem of al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī and preserved in literary and lexical works with similar readings. Although the poet belongs to the pre-Islamic period, the exact date of this couplet is not independently established. It is a witness from the transmitted pre-Islamic poetic tradition, not evidence that by itself dates the origin of the root.

Infiḍāḍ: the breakup of a gathering

In Arabic, infaḍḍa al-qawmu means that a group of people dispersed.

When people gather at a meeting, around a person, or for a task, and their gathering subsequently breaks up, one may say infaḍḍū.

The difference from breaking an object is clear. In physical breakage, parts of one body separate; in the dispersal of people, their spatial or social assembly comes apart.

That assembly may involve attending a meeting, following a leader, cooperating in a task, or accompanying others in a social situation.

The meaning does not necessarily imply hostility, disagreement in belief, or a permanent rupture between group members. People may disperse because their work has ended, news has arrived, an amusement has appeared, or their reason for staying has weakened.

Nor does dispersing from a center always mean moving in different directions. People may leave someone’s surroundings while many of them head toward the same new place.

The central element in infiḍāḍ is the breakup of the former gathering. The cause, direction, and outcome are supplied by the sentence and its context.

Fiḍḍah: the name of silver

Fiḍḍah is the Arabic name of silver, a white, lustrous metal long used for ornaments, vessels, and valuable possessions.

This meaning is direct and clear in the Qur’an. Silver is named alongside gold as familiar wealth, and other verses mention silver vessels and ornaments.

Its historical relationship to breaking and dispersing nevertheless requires caution.

One can envisage a semantic connection between silver’s name and dividing metal to make ornaments, but that possibility alone is not evidence of the word’s historical origin.

This is a conceivable semantic connection: metal can be divided into smaller pieces, melted, and used to make various objects.

However, this is not unique to silver. Gold, copper, and other metals can also be broken, separated, or shaped.

Moreover, listing fiḍḍah beside faḍḍa in a dictionary does not by itself prove that the metal’s name historically arose from the verb for breaking.

Two matters should therefore remain distinct: fiḍḍah was a recognized name for silver in the Arabic of the Qur’an’s revelation, while its precise origin and historical relationship to faḍḍa remain uncertain.

This article supplies no independently dated pre-Qur’anic witness that establishes the origin of the name for silver. The Qur’an clearly attests the name in the Arabic of its revelation, but does not determine when it first arose.

2. Qur’anic forms and derivatives

The root F Ḍ Ḍ occurs nine times in nine Qur’anic verses.

These occurrences fall into two main forms.

Infaḍḍa: a form VII verb meaning to disperse. It occurs three times, as la-infaḍḍū, infaḍḍū, and yanfaḍḍū.

Fiḍḍah: the noun for silver, occurring six times in definite and indefinite forms.

In la-infaḍḍū, the perfect verb is preceded by the lām introducing the result of the condition.

Infaḍḍū is a masculine plural perfect verb.

Yanfaḍḍū is a masculine plural imperfect verb. In Al-Munāfiqūn 63:7, it follows ḥattā in the subjunctive, with the final nūn omitted.

Fiḍḍah, however, is a noun. Its case forms include al-fiḍḍati, al-fiḍḍata, and fiḍḍatin. These inflectional differences do not produce separate derivatives.

Other Arabic forms include faḍḍa, faḍḍ, fuḍāḍ, faḍīḍ, mafḍūḍ, and tafaḍḍaḍa. They help explain the root’s semantic range but do not occur in the Qur’an in these forms.

Faḍḍaḍa is also recorded in the silver-related sense of silvering or adorning something with silver. This usage should be distinguished from breaking and dispersing.

The Qur’an thus uses two clear branches of the family: the dispersal of a gathering and the metal silver.

3. Infiḍāḍ: dispersal and the loss of company

The Prophet’s gentleness and the people gathered around him

Āl ʿImrān 3:159 states:

فَبِمَا رَحْمَةٍ مِنَ اللَّهِ لِنْتَ لَهُمْ وَلَوْ كُنْتَ فَظًّا غَلِيظَ الْقَلْبِ لَانْفَضُّوا مِنْ حَوْلِكَ فَاعْفُ عَنْهُمْ وَاسْتَغْفِرْ لَهُمْ وَشَاوِرْهُمْ فِي الْأَمْرِ فَإِذَا عَزَمْتَ فَتَوَكَّلْ عَلَى اللَّهِ إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُحِبُّ الْمُتَوَكِّلِينَ

fa-bi-mā raḥmatin mina Allāhi linta lahum wa-law kunta faẓẓan ghalīẓa al-qalbi la-infaḍḍū min ḥawlika fa-ʿfu ʿanhum wa-staghfir lahum wa-shāwirhum fī al-amri fa-idhā ʿazamta fa-tawakkal ʿalā Allāhi inna Allāha yuḥibbu al-mutawakkilīna

Āl ʿImrān 3:159

“Through mercy from The God, you were gentle with them. Had you been harsh and hard-hearted, they would have dispersed from around you. So pardon them, seek forgiveness for them, and consult them in the matter. Then, when you have resolved, trust in The God. Indeed, The God loves those who trust in Him.”

La-infaḍḍū here refers to people dispersing from around the Prophet.

Min ḥawlika specifies the direction: away from your surroundings.

The verb’s meaning must nevertheless be distinguished from the cause of the dispersal.

Infaḍḍū does not mean “they would resent you” or “they would become your enemies.” These might result from harshness, but the verb itself describes the breakup of the gathering around someone.

The hypothetical cause appears earlier in the verse: faẓẓan ghalīẓa al-qalbi, “harsh and hard-hearted.”

Faẓẓ belongs to F Ẓ Ẓ and concerns harshness. That root differs from F Ḍ Ḍ, although this verse clearly connects harsh conduct with people dispersing.

Faẓẓ describes a person’s conduct; la-infaḍḍū names the result that such conduct could have produced.

A grammatical point: hypothetical dispersal, not an accomplished event

Wa-law kunta … la-infaḍḍū is a conditional construction.

The verse does not say that people actually dispersed because the Prophet was harsh. It says that had he been harsh and hard-hearted, this would have happened.

The distinction is essential in translation.

Here, la-infaḍḍū means “they would have dispersed,” not “they dispersed.”

The verse thus shows how mercy and gentleness help sustain people’s company.

It then commands the Prophet to pardon them, seek forgiveness, and consult them.

This connection shows that gentleness here is more than an emotional trait or personal preference. It concerns conduct, repairing relationships, and involving others in decision-making.

Consultation and pardon are nevertheless not part of the lexical meaning of infiḍāḍ. They are actions prescribed in the same verse for preserving and repairing relationships.

Dispersing toward trade and amusement

Al-Jumuʿah 62:11 states:

وَإِذَا رَأَوْا تِجَارَةً أَوْ لَهْوًا انْفَضُّوا إِلَيْهَا وَتَرَكُوكَ قَائِمًا قُلْ مَا عِنْدَ اللَّهِ خَيْرٌ مِنَ اللَّهْوِ وَمِنَ التِّجَارَةِ وَاللَّهُ خَيْرُ الرَّازِقِينَ

wa-idhā raʾaw tijāratan aw lahwan infaḍḍū ilayhā wa-tarakūka qāʾiman qul mā ʿinda Allāhi khayrun mina al-lahwi wa-mina al-tijārati wa-Allāhu khayru al-rāziqīna

Al-Jumuʿah 62:11

“When they saw trade or amusement, they dispersed toward it and left you standing. Say: What is with The God is better than amusement and trade, and The God is the best of providers.”

Infaḍḍū here describes the dispersal of people previously assembled in a shared setting.

Unlike Āl ʿImrān 3:159, this is a statement about conduct that occurred.

Ilayhā indicates the direction of movement. They left the former gathering and went toward what had attracted their attention.

Infiḍāḍ therefore does not necessarily mean movement in different directions. People may leave one center and move toward a new attraction.

Here, the new attraction is trade or amusement.

Tijāratan aw lahwan identifies what drew their attention; neither trade nor amusement is part of the meaning of infaḍḍū itself.

Wa-tarakūka qāʾiman also states the result: they left the Prophet standing.

Infiḍāḍ does not itself mean abandoning prayer or a sermon. Such details must be established from context and independent evidence rather than from the root’s definition.

Permitted and censured dispersal

Al-Jumuʿah 62:10 states:

فَإِذَا قُضِيَتِ الصَّلَاةُ فَانْتَشِرُوا فِي الْأَرْضِ وَابْتَغُوا مِنْ فَضْلِ اللَّهِ وَاذْكُرُوا اللَّهَ كَثِيرًا لَعَلَّكُمْ تُفْلِحُونَ

fa-idhā quḍiyati al-ṣalātu fa-ntashirū fī al-arḍi wa-btaghū min faḍli Allāhi wa-udhkurū Allāha kathīran laʿallakum tufliḥūna

Al-Jumuʿah 62:10

“When the ṣalāh has ended, disperse through the land and seek the bounty of The God, and remember The God often, so that you may prosper.”

Here, dispersing after ṣalāh is expressed by fa-ntashirū.

In Al-Jumuʿah 62:11, however, infaḍḍū occurs in the context of leaving the Prophet for trade or amusement.

The two actions should not be assigned the same ethical value.

Verse ten concerns moving through the land after ṣalāh and seeking divine bounty. Verse eleven censures people who, on seeing trade or amusement, abandon the gathering and leave the Prophet standing.

Leaving a place is therefore not inherently wrong. Its timing, cause, and the person’s responsibility in that situation matter to its assessment.

Nor should the verbs’ difference be reduced to “good dispersal” and “bad dispersal.” Their lexical imagery differs too: intishār emphasizes spreading over an area, whereas infiḍāḍ emphasizes the breakup of a former gathering.

This distinction fits the two verses, but the final ethical judgment comes from the whole statement.

Economic pressure to disperse the Messenger’s companions

Al-Munāfiqūn 63:7 states:

هُمُ الَّذِينَ يَقُولُونَ لَا تُنْفِقُوا عَلَىٰ مَنْ عِنْدَ رَسُولِ اللَّهِ حَتَّىٰ يَنْفَضُّوا وَلِلَّهِ خَزَائِنُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ وَلَٰكِنَّ الْمُنَافِقِينَ لَا يَفْقَهُونَ

humu alladhīna yaqūlūna lā tunfiqū ʿalā man ʿinda rasūli Allāhi ḥattā yanfaḍḍū wa-li-llāhi khazāʾinu al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍi wa-lākinna al-munāfiqīna lā yafqahūna

Al-Munāfiqūn 63:7

“They are the ones who say: Do not spend on those with the Messenger of The God until they disperse. To The God belong the treasuries of the heavens and the earth, but the hypocrites do not understand.”

Yanfaḍḍū here refers to the dispersal of people accompanying the Messenger.

There is nevertheless an important difference from the previous two uses.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:159, hypothetical harshness could cause dispersal. In Al-Jumuʿah 62:11, attention to trade or amusement led people to leave. In Al-Munāfiqūn 63:7, a group seeks to break up the Prophet’s company by withholding financial assistance.

Lā tunfiqū commands withholding expenditure, and ḥattā yanfaḍḍū states the intended outcome.

Dispersal is not reported as an accomplished result; it is the aim of those making the statement.

Infiḍāḍ and financial pressure

The verse shows that a group’s breakup need not result from disagreement in belief or open hostility among its members.

Some may try to make continued company impossible or difficult by cutting off funds and economic support.

Economic pressure can thus become a means of breaking a social bond and dispersing people.

This relationship comes from the context of Al-Munāfiqūn 63:7. Infiḍāḍ itself does not mean an economic boycott.

Tunfiqū also belongs to N F Q, not F Ḍ Ḍ. The visual similarity of tunfiqū and yanfaḍḍū does not make them one lexical family.

The verse places two distinct actions in a means-and-purpose relationship: withholding expenditure so that the Messenger’s companions disperse.

The verse then assigns ownership of the treasuries of the heavens and earth to God. This response exposes the limited economic power of those who imagine that withdrawing their support can conclusively determine a community’s fate.

Taken together, the three verbal uses concern a gathering breaking up, with different contextual causes: harshness, the attraction of trade and amusement, or economic pressure.

4. Fiḍḍah: silver as worldly wealth and a blessing of the hereafter

The second Qur’anic branch of this family names silver.

Unlike the verb infaḍḍū, fiḍḍah names a particular material. Its meaning should be understood through the verses’ actual use of silver, not through the image of breaking and dispersing.

Silver alongside gold and valuable possessions

Āl ʿImrān 3:14 states:

زُيِّنَ لِلنَّاسِ حُبُّ الشَّهَوَاتِ مِنَ النِّسَاءِ وَالْبَنِينَ وَالْقَنَاطِيرِ الْمُقَنْطَرَةِ مِنَ الذَّهَبِ وَالْفِضَّةِ وَالْخَيْلِ الْمُسَوَّمَةِ وَالْأَنْعَامِ وَالْحَرْثِ ذَٰلِكَ مَتَاعُ الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا وَاللَّهُ عِنْدَهُ حُسْنُ الْمَآبِ

zuyyina li-l-nāsi ḥubbu al-shahawāti mina al-nisāʾi wa-al-banīna wa-al-qanāṭīri al-muqanṭarati mina al-dhahabi wa-al-fiḍḍati wa-al-khayli al-musawwamati wa-al-anʿāmi wa-al-ḥarthi dhālika matāʿu al-ḥayāti al-dunyā wa-Allāhu ʿindahu ḥusnu al-maʾābi

Āl ʿImrān 3:14

“Love of desirable things has been made attractive to people: women, sons, accumulated heaps of gold and silver, marked horses, livestock, and cultivated land. That is the enjoyment of worldly life; with The God is the goodness of the return.”

Al-fiḍḍati appears here beside al-dhahabi.

Both name valuable metals that can be accumulated and kept as assets.

Fiḍḍah does not itself mean wealth, money, or jewelry. It names silver, which can take various forms as an asset or means of exchange.

Al-qanāṭīri al-muqanṭarati conveys the abundance of the possessions. Their quantity and accumulation come from other words in the verse, not from fiḍḍah itself.

The verse then presents these possessions as part of the enjoyment of worldly life.

This does not make silver impure or inherently objectionable. The ethical value of handling wealth concerns how it is acquired, kept, and spent.

Hoarding gold and silver

Al-Tawbah 9:34 states:

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِنَّ كَثِيرًا مِنَ الْأَحْبَارِ وَالرُّهْبَانِ لَيَأْكُلُونَ أَمْوَالَ النَّاسِ بِالْبَاطِلِ وَيَصُدُّونَ عَنْ سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ وَالَّذِينَ يَكْنِزُونَ الذَّهَبَ وَالْفِضَّةَ وَلَا يُنْفِقُونَهَا فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ فَبَشِّرْهُمْ بِعَذَابٍ أَلِيمٍ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū inna kathīran mina al-aḥbāri wa-al-ruhbāni la-yaʾkulūna amwāla al-nāsi bi-al-bāṭili wa-yaṣuddūna ʿan sabīli Allāhi wa-alladhīna yaknizūna al-dhahaba wa-al-fiḍḍata wa-lā yunfiqūnahā fī sabīli Allāhi fa-bashshirhum bi-ʿadhābin alīmin

Al-Tawbah 9:34

“You who have believed, many scholars and monks consume people’s wealth wrongfully and bar others from the path of The God. Those who hoard gold and silver and do not spend it in the path of The God—give them tidings of a painful punishment.”

Al-fiḍḍata is coordinated with al-dhahaba, the object of yaknizūna; both metals are what is hoarded.

Kanz concerns gathering and retaining wealth, whereas fiḍḍah names the metal itself.

The verse does not merely mention silver’s existence or ownership. It addresses people who hoard gold and silver without spending it in God’s path.

Fiḍḍah should therefore not be redefined as “forbidden property” or “illicit wealth.” The metallic material is distinct from human conduct.

Nor should the verse be taken to mean that every instance of keeping silver, regardless of circumstances and financial responsibilities, is automatically the censured conduct.

The issue is the relationship between hoarding wealth and withholding expenditure in God’s path.

Al-Tawbah 9:35 describes the outcome of this conduct. The connection shows that wealth can entail responsibility and accountability, but this follows from the verses’ context, not from the lexical meaning of silver.

Silver and worldly luxury

Al-Zukhruf 43:33 states:

وَلَوْلَا أَنْ يَكُونَ النَّاسُ أُمَّةً وَاحِدَةً لَجَعَلْنَا لِمَنْ يَكْفُرُ بِالرَّحْمَٰنِ لِبُيُوتِهِمْ سُقُفًا مِنْ فِضَّةٍ وَمَعَارِجَ عَلَيْهَا يَظْهَرُونَ

wa-lawlā an yakūna al-nāsu ummatan wāḥidatan la-jaʿalnā li-man yakfuru bi-al-raḥmāni li-buyūtihim suqufan min fiḍḍatin wa-maʿārija ʿalayhā yaẓharūna

Al-Zukhruf 43:33

“Were it not that people would become one community, We would have provided those who disbelieve in the Entirely Merciful with silver roofs for their houses and stairways upon which they ascend.”

Silver is mentioned here as material for house roofs.

This shows that fiḍḍah is not simply money or storable wealth: it is a metal from which different objects can be made.

Al-Zukhruf 43:34 and Al-Zukhruf 43:35 then mention doors, couches, and further adornments, presenting them all as enjoyment of worldly life.

The context shows that abundant silver and material luxury do not conclusively measure someone’s worth before God.

Silver nevertheless remains the same metal. Its lexical meaning cannot be changed to luxury-seeking or disbelief.

In particular, the sentence is conditional. It should not become a report implying that all disbelievers’ houses actually have silver roofs.

Silver vessels in descriptions of the Garden

Al-Insān 76:15 states:

وَيُطَافُ عَلَيْهِمْ بِآنِيَةٍ مِنْ فِضَّةٍ وَأَكْوَابٍ كَانَتْ قَوَارِيرَا

wa-yuṭāfu ʿalayhim bi-āniyatin min fiḍḍatin wa-akwābin kānat qawārīrā

Al-Insān 76:15

“Vessels of silver and cups that are crystal will be passed around among them.”

Here, silver is the material of vessels included among the Garden’s blessings.

Āniyah refers to vessels; fiḍḍah names the material from which they are made.

Fiḍḍah here is neither a term for money nor the name of a separate reward; it specifies the vessels’ material.

Al-Insān 76:16 states:

قَوَارِيرَ مِنْ فِضَّةٍ قَدَّرُوهَا تَقْدِيرًا

qawārīra min fiḍḍatin qaddarūhā taqdīran

Al-Insān 76:16

“Crystal of silver, which they have measured precisely.”

The expression is striking: qawārīr concerns crystal vessels, while fiḍḍah concerns silver.

Resolving the expression does not justify redefining fiḍḍah as an unknown substance or constructing a certain theory about the physical properties of otherworldly blessings.

The verse speaks of crystal of silver. The full nature of this description in the hereafter cannot be determined from F Ḍ Ḍ alone.

Silver ornaments

Al-Insān 76:21 states:

عَالِيَهُمْ ثِيَابُ سُنْدُسٍ خُضْرٌ وَإِسْتَبْرَقٌ وَحُلُّوا أَسَاوِرَ مِنْ فِضَّةٍ وَسَقَاهُمْ رَبُّهُمْ شَرَابًا طَهُورًا

ʿāliyahum thiyābu sundusin khuḍrun wa-istabraqun wa-ḥullū asāwira min fiḍḍatin wa-saqāhum rabbuhum sharāban ṭahūran

Al-Insān 76:21

“Upon them are green garments of fine silk and brocade. They are adorned with bracelets of silver, and their Lord gives them a pure drink.”

Silver here is used for bracelets.

Asāwira identifies the ornament; min fiḍḍatin specifies its material.

This use relates to the silver vessels in the same chapter without changing the word’s meaning.

Together, the three verses of Al-Insān present silver as the material of vessels and ornaments among the hereafter’s blessings.

These uses should be read alongside the verses about worldly wealth.

Āl ʿImrān 3:14 lists silver among desired possessions. Al-Tawbah 9:34 censures hoarding it while withholding expenditure. Al-Zukhruf 43:33 places it in an image of worldly luxury. In Al-Insān, the same metal’s name appears in descriptions of the Garden’s blessings.

Positive or negative ethical value therefore does not arise from silver itself. Its place and use in each context are what matter.

5. The relationship between the branches and nearby roots

Was silver named from breaking?

This family has two recognized meanings: breaking and dispersing, and the name of silver.

One can envisage a semantic connection between silver’s name and dividing metal to make ornaments, but that possibility alone is not evidence of the word’s historical origin.

This explanation is not an established historical origin.

Deriving all meanings of F Ḍ Ḍ from one image would require independent evidence that silver’s name actually arose from breaking and separating.

The lexical connection presented here does not by itself establish that historical development.

The final definition can therefore retain two branches without either denying or asserting a historically uncertain relationship between them.

F Ḍ Ḍ and F R Q: breaking continuity and separating

F R Q concerns separation, distinction, and a distance or difference between things.

The verbal branch of F Ḍ Ḍ also concerns separating parts and dispersing a gathering.

Faḍḍa emphasizes breaking continuity or breaking up a whole, whereas faraqa can simply separate two things or distinguish them.

One can distinguish two topics without breaking an object or dispersing a gathering.

When people infaḍḍū from around someone, however, their former assembly breaks up.

This is a tendency, not an absolute boundary covering every use of both roots.

F Ḍ Ḍ and N SH R: dispersal and spreading

Nashr concerns spreading and scattering in Arabic.

In Al-Jumuʿah 62:10, fa-ntashirū fī al-arḍi concerns dispersing and moving through the land after ṣalāh ends.

In Al-Jumuʿah 62:11, infaḍḍū ilayhā concerns leaving the former gathering and turning toward trade or amusement.

The first emphasizes movement and spreading through the land; the second emphasizes breaking up the assembly and leaving the previous setting.

Both can be rendered “disperse,” but their lexical imagery and contexts differ.

F Ḍ Ḍ and F Ẓ Ẓ: dispersal and harshness

These roots look similar in writing, but differ in their letters and meanings.

F Ẓ Ẓ concerns harshness; F Ḍ Ḍ concerns breaking and dispersing.

Both appear in Āl ʿImrān 3:159: faẓẓan describes harsh conduct, and la-infaḍḍū states its hypothetical result.

Their proximity in this verse does not authorize treating their roots as identical.

Fiḍḍah and dhahab: silver and gold

Gold and silver appear together in Āl ʿImrān 3:14 and Al-Tawbah 9:34.

Both are valuable metals used as assets, ornaments, and means of exchange.

Dhahab names gold and fiḍḍah names silver. Their shared economic value does not make the words synonymous.

Nor should their financial value become the lexical meaning of their roots. Value, economic use, and the owner’s conduct are distinct from the name of a substance.

Fiḍḍah and faḍl: silver and abundance

F Ḍ L concerns excess, superiority, and having more; it has a broad Qur’anic range.

Fiḍḍah, however, names silver.

The words’ visual resemblance, especially without vowel marks, should not lead to their confusion.

In Al-Jumuʿah 62:10, faḍli Allāhi refers to divine bounty, not silver. In the metal-related verses, fiḍḍah does not mean bounty or abundance.

This distinction is essential for classifying roots and translating the Qur’an accurately.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

One Persian equivalent cannot adequately cover both Qur’anic branches.

The basic equivalent for infiḍāḍ is “disperse”; depending on context, “the gathering breaks up” or “disperse from around” may be more precise.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:159, la-infaḍḍū min ḥawlika is best rendered:

“They would have dispersed from around you.”

This preserves the conditional construction and the direction of dispersal.

In Al-Jumuʿah 62:11, infaḍḍū ilayhā can be rendered:

“They dispersed toward it.”

The movement toward trade or amusement is supplied by the preposition and pronoun.

In Al-Munāfiqūn 63:7, ḥattā yanfaḍḍū is rendered:

“Until they disperse.”

This preserves the aim of those seeking to break up the Messenger’s company by withholding expenditure.

For fiḍḍah, the clear, consistent equivalent is “silver.”

Al-dhahabi wa-l-fiḍḍati means “gold and silver.”

Suqufan min fiḍḍatin means “roofs of silver.”

Āniyatin min fiḍḍatin means “vessels of silver.”

Qawārīra min fiḍḍatin means “crystal of silver.”

Asāwira min fiḍḍatin means “bracelets of silver.”

These renderings should not replace the metal’s name with “money,” “coins,” “ornaments,” “wealth,” or “a shining substance”: each may represent only one use or feature of silver.

Nor should infaḍḍū become “they became enemies,” “they disbelieved,” “they fled,” or “they went to war” without supporting evidence.

Such conduct may accompany dispersal in some circumstances, but is not the root’s direct meaning.

Conclusion

The Arabic root F Ḍ Ḍ has two clear branches.

Its verbal branch concerns breaking continuity, separating parts, and dispersing a gathering. The poetic witness attributed to al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī illustrates breaking and scattering fragments in early Arabic.

In the Qur’an, this branch occurs only through infaḍḍa.

Āl ʿImrān 3:159 describes hypothetical dispersal resulting from harshness. Al-Jumuʿah 62:11 describes people leaving a gathering on seeing trade or amusement. Al-Munāfiqūn 63:7 describes an attempt to disperse the Messenger’s companions through economic pressure.

All three concern the breakup of a gathering; cause, direction, and outcome come from context.

The second branch is fiḍḍah, silver.

Silver appears among worldly valuables, in the censure of hoarding without expenditure, and in descriptions of the Garden’s vessels and ornaments.

These different uses show that silver itself carries no fixed ethical judgment. The value and responsibility of human conduct involving it depend on its use and the verses’ context.

A connection between silver’s name and breaking is conceivable as a semantic possibility, but this article offers no historical evidence sufficient to derive every meaning of the family from a single image.

The final definition should therefore preserve both branches clearly:

In verbal usage, F Ḍ Ḍ concerns breaking continuity, separating parts, and dispersing a gathering. Fiḍḍah names silver; its historical relationship to breaking and dispersal is uncertain.

For Forqan’s translation, “disperse” for the verbal forms and “silver” for the nouns are the two basic, accurate equivalents.

A central lesson of the Qur’anic verbal uses is that gathering together does not by itself ensure continued company. Harsh behavior, immediate attractions, and economic pressure can contribute to a gathering’s breakup.

Conversely, Āl ʿImrān 3:159 presents mercy, gentleness, pardon, and consultation as conduct connected with preserving and repairing human relationships. This conclusion comes from the verse’s context and must not replace the root’s lexical meaning.
