Gathering, taking possession, moving aside, and joining another side

The Arabic root ح و ز concerns gathering, taking into one’s possession, separating from one company, and joining another side. The words حَيازَة (ḥiyāzah), حَوْزَة (ḥawzah), اِنْحِياز (inḥiyāz), and تَحَيُّز (taḥayyuz) belong to this family.

When someone draws something to themselves and takes possession of it, they have حاز (ḥāza) it. When people move away from their previous position and gather elsewhere, the terms inḥiyāz and taḥayyuz can describe this movement. A ḥawzah can also be an area within which something or someone is situated, or which is under someone’s control and protection.

In the Qur’an, this root appears in مُتَحَيِّزًا إِلَىٰ فِئَةٍ (mutaḥayyizan ilā fiʾatin): someone moving toward and joining another group during a military encounter. This use shows that leaving one position does not necessarily mean fleeing and abandoning responsibility. It may be a movement to join a group and continue that responsibility.

1. Forms and the root’s semantic core

The root ح و ز occurs in the Qur’an only in مُتَحَيِّزًا (mutaḥayyizan), in Al-Anfāl 8:16. This is the active participle of تَحَيَّزَ (taḥayyaza).

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the root’s sole occurrence in Al-Anfāl 8:16

The Arabic family contains a broader range of forms:

  • حَازَ ـ يَحُوزُ (ḥāza–yaḥūzu): to gather something, take possession of it, or bring it within one’s domain.
  • حَوْز and حِيَازَة (ḥawz and ḥiyāzah): gathering and possessing something, especially wealth, land, or other property.
  • حَوْزَة (ḥawzah): an area, region, or something under a person’s control and protection.
  • حَيِّز (ḥayyiz): a place or space occupied by something. Lexica sometimes discuss this form separately as well.
  • حَوَّزَ (ḥawwaza): to gather something or drive it toward a destination.
  • اِحْتَازَ (iḥtāza): to appropriate something or take possession of it.
  • اِنْحَازَ (inḥāza): to move aside, leave a position, or turn toward and join another group or place.
  • تَحَوَّزَ (taḥawwaza): to draw back, move away from somewhere, or incline to one side.
  • تَحَيَّزَ (taḥayyaza): to move to a side or group and take one’s place within its area or company.
  • مُتَحَيِّز (mutaḥayyiz): someone who has moved aside or joined a group or position.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an. The only Qur’anic form in this family is مُتَحَيِّزًا (mutaḥayyizan).

Semantic core

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies one principle for ح و ز: الْجَمْعُ وَالتَّجَمُّعُ (al-jamʿ wa-l-tajammuʿ), gathering and coming together.

He explains حَوْز (ḥawz) and حَوْزَة (ḥawzah) through the same meaning. Someone who gathers something to themselves has حاز (ḥāza) it.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: gathering and coming together

Two main directions appear within this semantic field:

First, something scattered or outside an area is gathered toward a center and brought into someone’s possession.

Second, a person or group leaves one position and moves toward another area or company.

These directions are connected: moving toward one side means moving away from another. Yet this relationship does not mean that every use necessarily contains all the stages of separation, movement, gathering, and ownership.

For example, حاز المال (ḥāza al-māl) can mean taking possession of property without describing its physical movement. Conversely, انحاز عن القوم (inḥāza ʿan al-qawm) may simply describe withdrawing from a group, without acquiring property or territory.

The root’s semantic core can therefore be understood as gathering or taking a place within a particular area or side, with possible departure from a previous position or acquisition of something.

This definition explains the connection between the branches. Each derivative’s particular meaning must still be established from its own formation and use.

2. The root in early Arabic

Driving livestock and gathering them

One early use of ح و ز concerns driving and directing livestock.

Arabic dictionaries record حَازَ الإِبِلَ يَحُوزُهَا (ḥāza al-ibila yaḥūzuhā) for driving camels. Lexicographical accounts describe both gentle, unhurried driving and forceful driving.

This variation shows that neither force nor gentleness is a constant feature of every use. In the camel example, the central action is directing a collection of animals along a route.

حَوَّزَ الإِبِلَ إِلَى الْمَاءِ (ḥawwaza al-ibila ilā al-māʾ) also means driving camels toward water.

The animals are moved toward a destination. Gathering and directing may occur together, but water and drinking are not part of the root’s meaning: they come from the stated destination.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ and Tāj al-ʿArūs: driving camels, gently or forcefully

A poetic witness from Al-Quṭāmī

A verse attributed to the Umayyad poet Al-Quṭāmī reads:

تَحَوَّزُ عَنِّي خِيفَةَ أَنْ أَضِيفَهَا
كَمَا انْحَازَتِ الْأَفْعَى مَخَافَةَ ضَارِبِ

taḥawwazu ʿannī khīfata an aḍīfahā / kamā inḥāzati al-afʿā makhāfata ḍāribi

Translation:

“She draws away from me, afraid that I might become her guest, just as a viper withdraws in fear of someone who would strike it.”

The lexica explain this verse as describing a woman with whom the poet seeks hospitality. She moves away to avoid receiving him.

Here تَحَوَّزُ (taḥawwazu) means moving aside and away. اِنْحَازَتِ الْأَفْعَى (inḥāzati al-afʿā) likewise describes the snake moving away from danger.

This witness shows that ح و ز is not confined to collecting property or ownership. It can also describe the movement and withdrawal of a person or animal.

Another transmitted reading of the first hemistich has تَحَيَّزُ مِنِّي (taḥayyazu minnī). Some transmissions also have خَشْيَةَ (khashyata) instead of خِيفَةَ (khīfata). The variation matters because taḥawwuz and taḥayyuz have closely related uses here. In this verse, أَضِيفَهَا (aḍīfahā) comes from ضافَ ـ يَضِيفُ (ḍāfa–yaḍīfu), meaning to become her guest; the initial hamzah therefore takes fatḥah, not ḍammah.

Lisān al-ʿArab, Ḍ Y F: ḍiftuhu aḍīfuhu and Al-Quṭāmī’s witness

Al-Quṭāmī was an Umayyad poet. This is consequently a witness to early Arabic close to the early Islamic period, rather than a securely pre-Islamic witness.

Tāj al-ʿArūs: taḥawwazu ʿannī khīfata and the poet’s request for hospitality

A domain and what is in someone’s possession

The lexica use حَوْزَة (ḥawzah) for an area or domain that a person controls or protects.

حَوْزَةُ الْمُلْكِ (ḥawzatu al-mulk) is also recorded for a territory or domain of rule.

In relation to land, حَوْز (ḥawz) can mean marking out and taking possession of an area.

These examples show that gathering and acquiring may also take the form of defining a domain.

Nevertheless, every ḥawzah should not be translated solely as legal ownership. Sometimes it simply means an area, location, or space occupied by something.

Moving away and joining

Classical Arabic uses اِنْحَازَ عَنْهُ (inḥāza ʿanhu) for withdrawing from someone or something, and اِنْحَازَ إِلَيْهِ (inḥāza ilayhi) for joining or turning toward them.

The difference between these constructions matters.

In the first, عَنْ (ʿan) marks movement away. In the second, إِلَى (ilā) identifies the destination or party toward which someone moves.

The root can thus concern movement toward a particular side. What someone leaves, or whom they join, is established by the preposition and the sentence’s construction.

This distinction is central to understanding the Qur’anic مُتَحَيِّزًا إِلَىٰ فِئَةٍ (mutaḥayyizan ilā fiʾatin).

3. Qur’anic use: مُتَحَيِّزًا إِلَىٰ فِئَةٍ (mutaḥayyizan ilā fiʾatin)

The root ح و ز appears in Al-Anfāl 8:16. The preceding verse is also relevant to its meaning.

The verse’s setting

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِذَا لَقِيتُمُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا زَحْفًا فَلَا تُوَلُّوهُمُ الْأَدْبَارَ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū idhā laqītumu alladhīna kafarū zaḥfan fa-lā tuwallūhumu al-adbāra

Proposed translation:

“You who have believed, when you encounter those who have disbelieved advancing for battle, do not turn your backs to them.”

Al-Anfāl 8:15

This verse concerns an encounter on the battlefield. زَحْفًا (zaḥfan) establishes that setting.

In that situation, the believers are commanded not to turn their backs on the opposing side. The next verse, however, excepts two circumstances.

The verse

وَمَنْ يُوَلِّهِمْ يَوْمَئِذٍ دُبُرَهُ إِلَّا مُتَحَرِّفًا لِقِتَالٍ أَوْ مُتَحَيِّزًا إِلَىٰ فِئَةٍ فَقَدْ بَاءَ بِغَضَبٍ مِنَ اللَّهِ وَمَأْوَاهُ جَهَنَّمُ وَبِئْسَ الْمَصِيرُ

wa-man yuwallihim yawmaʾidhin duburahu illā mutaḥarrifan li-qitālin aw mutaḥayyizan ilā fiʾatin fa-qad bāʾa bi-ghaḍabin mina allāhi wa-maʾwāhu jahannamu wa-biʾsa al-maṣīru

Proposed translation:

“Whoever turns his back to them on that day, except while maneuvering for battle or joining a group, has incurred anger from The God, and his abode is Hell. What an evil destination!”

Al-Anfāl 8:16

Two different circumstances appear together: مُتَحَرِّفًا لِقِتَالٍ (mutaḥarrifan li-qitālin) and مُتَحَيِّزًا إِلَىٰ فِئَةٍ (mutaḥayyizan ilā fiʾatin).

Both can involve changing position on the battlefield, but their word formation and direction of movement differ.

مُتَحَرِّفًا (mutaḥarrifan), from ح ر ف, concerns changing direction or position. لِقِتَالٍ (li-qitālin) specifies its purpose: for battle.

مُتَحَيِّزًا (mutaḥayyizan), however, comes from ح و ز. With إِلَىٰ فِئَةٍ (ilā fiʾatin), it describes someone moving toward a group and joining it.

The verse therefore distinguishes turning away to flee from changing position in order to continue the battle.

The role of إِلَىٰ فِئَةٍ (ilā fiʾatin)

إِلَىٰ فِئَةٍ (ilā fiʾatin) is decisive for understanding مُتَحَيِّزًا (mutaḥayyizan).

فِئَة (fiʾah) means a group or company of people. In this setting, the group is connected with the same confrontation and battle.

Here إِلَىٰ (ilā) identifies the direction: toward a group.

In this construction, mutaḥayyiz is consequently more than someone moving away from the scene: it is someone heading toward a group to join them.

For example, someone might move back from their current position during battle and join another group of allied forces, to return to the battle with them. This example fits the verse’s meaning, but the text does not specify the type of military unit, distance traveled, or method of organizing the forces.

The main point is that not every backward movement has the same purpose or outcome.

In one situation, someone leaves the scene to evade responsibility. In another, they change position to join a group and continue the battle.

This difference explains the importance of the exception أَوْ مُتَحَيِّزًا إِلَىٰ فِئَةٍ (aw mutaḥayyizan ilā fiʾatin).

Al-Ṭabarī: maneuvering for battle and joining the company of believers

Does mutaḥayyiz mean a fugitive?

Taḥayyuz may involve leaving a position, but the word does not necessarily mean fleeing from responsibility.

In Al-Anfāl 8:16, translating mutaḥayyizan simply as “fleeing” would erase the verse’s distinction between blameworthy flight and moving to join another group.

Conversely, translating it merely as “a fighter” or “an attacker” would overlook the central movement and joining.

In this context, the appropriate meaning is someone moving from one position toward another group and joining it.

The group’s connection with the battle, and the movement’s relation to continuing the confrontation, come from the context of Al-Anfāl 8:15 and Al-Anfāl 8:16. They do not mean that the root ح و ز inherently means fighting.

Does this ruling concern every kind of retreat?

The verse describes a particular situation: a military encounter and turning one’s back on the day of battle.

It cannot therefore establish, by itself, that every retreat in personal, social, or political life is blameworthy or forbidden.

Nor does the verse independently address every operational detail, such as repositioning when continuing the battle is impossible, resources are unavailable, or people are wounded.

Examining those situations requires a broader study of warfare and responsibility in the Qur’an. This root study establishes only that mutaḥayyiz here means changing position to join a group.

Linguistically, this movement is different from fleeing and abandoning the field altogether.

4. The morphology of مُتَحَيِّزًا (mutaḥayyizan)

ح و ز is a hollow wāw root: its middle radical is wāw.

Although the Qur’anic مُتَحَيِّزًا (mutaḥayyizan) is written with yāʾ, its root in the established morphological analysis is ح و ز.

In the analysis transmitted from Sībawayh, تَحَيَّزَ (taḥayyaza) follows تَفَيْعَلَ (tafayʿala), with تَحَيْوَزَ (taḥaywaza) as the form before sound change. When yāʾ and wāw meet, with the preceding yāʾ unvoweled, wāw changes to yāʾ and the two yāʾs assimilate. The analytical pattern of مُتَحَيِّز (mutaḥayyiz) is therefore given as مُتَفَيْعِل (mutafayʿil). This differs from تَحَوَّزَ (taḥawwaza), on تَفَعَّلَ (tafaʿʿala).

The yāʾ in mutaḥayyiz therefore does not necessarily make ح ي ز its root.

The lexica record both taḥawwaza and taḥayyaza, sometimes with the same meaning. Both, for example, can describe a snake or person moving aside.

Nevertheless, this form’s morphological analysis should be distinguished from the lexicographical classification of related forms such as حَيِّز (ḥayyiz). Similarity of use does not, by itself, settle every historical or morphological disagreement.

Lisān al-ʿArab: tafayʿala, the wāw–yāʾ sound change, and circumstantial syntax

Active participle and syntactic function

مُتَحَيِّزًا (mutaḥayyizan) is an indefinite, masculine singular active participle in the accusative.

In Al-Anfāl 8:16, it identifies one of the two circumstances excepted from the ruling on turning one’s back on the enemy.

مُتَحَرِّفًا (mutaḥarrifan) and مُتَحَيِّزًا (mutaḥayyizan) are both accusative and are joined by أَوْ (aw), “or.”

In an established syntactic analysis, they can be understood as two circumstances excepted from the general situation of turning away. The verse thus describes the conditions and direction of someone’s action when changing position, rather than simply assigning two groups a permanent identity.

This syntax does not mean that someone who once joined a group in battle acquires a permanent moral or military designation. The word describes the person’s situation during that action.

5. Comparison with related roots and words

ح و ز (ḥawz) and ح و ذ (ḥawdh): gathering and mastery

ح و ز and ح و ذ are close in sound and in some early uses.

The lexica transmit some forms for driving and controlling livestock with either zāy or dhāl. For example, a rajaz witness attributed to Al-ʿAjjāj has variant readings from these two families.

This historical overlap matters, but the two roots should not be merged into a single morphological family without examination.

In the Qur’anic use of ح و ز, مُتَحَيِّزًا إِلَىٰ فِئَةٍ (mutaḥayyizan ilā fiʾatin), movement toward and joining a group are prominent.

In اسْتَحْوَذَ عَلَيْهِمُ الشَّيْطَانُ (istaḥwadha ʿalayhimu al-shayṭān), in Al-Mujādilah 58:19, however, mastery and bringing others under an influence are prominent.

نَسْتَحْوِذْ عَلَيْكُمْ (nastaḥwidh ʿalaykum), in Al-Nisāʾ 4:141, likewise appears in a claim to influence or advantage over others.

Mutaḥayyiz should therefore not be equated with mustaḥwidh in Qur’anic translation. In its context, the first concerns joining a group; the second concerns mastery or influence over another.

The distinction comes from each word’s use and construction, rather than an assumption that the two roots never overlapped in early Arabic.

Study of Ḥ W DH

ح و ز (ḥawz) and ح ر ف (ḥarf): joining and changing position

This comparison is especially relevant in Al-Anfāl 8:16, where mutaḥarrifan and mutaḥayyizan occur in the same sentence.

Here taḥarruf concerns changing direction or position for battle.

In the same context, taḥayyuz concerns moving toward a group and joining it.

One thus emphasizes changing position, and the other joining a group.

Yet this difference should not become an absolute rule that taḥarruf always means individual movement and taḥayyuz always means collective movement. The construction and complements of each verb must also be examined in other uses.

Study of Ḥ R F

ح و ز (ḥawz) and ج م ع (jamʿ): gathering and entering a domain

Jamʿ means gathering scattered things or bringing them together.

Ḥāza likewise means gathering in many uses, but can also emphasize acquiring something or bringing it within one’s domain.

For example, حاز المال (ḥāza al-māl) can concern taking possession of property, beyond simply placing items next to one another.

Nevertheless, ownership must not be treated as the constant meaning of every derivative of ح و ز. Someone joining a group does not acquire ownership of it or necessarily gain control of its members.

Gathering connects the two semantic fields, but the object and sentence structure clarify the difference.

Study of J M ʿ

ح و ز (ḥawz) and ح ج ز (ḥajz): an area and restraint

ح ج ز concerns interposing between two things, separating, and restraining.

حَوْزَة (ḥawzah) can also denote a defined area, but defining an area does not necessarily mean erecting an obstacle or preventing passage.

In one use, a person may possess land; in another, an obstacle is placed between two things. These situations may be connected in practice, but ḥāza and ḥajaza do not have the same basic meaning.

Protecting a domain is likewise understood from the combination of an area under someone’s control with the act of guarding it. Not every form of ح و ز independently means defending.

6. Semantic developments of taḥayyuz and ḥawzah

In contemporary Arabic and some Persian writing, taḥayyuz means unfair partiality, prejudice, or bias, particularly in scholarship, social discussion, and judgment.

This use is connected with inclining toward one side, but its negative connotation should not be transferred to every early use of the root.

In Al-Anfāl 8:16, mutaḥayyiz is neither a prejudiced person nor a judge who favors a group without justification. It concerns changing position and joining a group in battle.

This difference clearly illustrates why Qur’anic use must be distinguished from familiar terminology in today’s language.

Ḥawzah also has several uses in contemporary Persian and Arabic. A geographical area, a sphere of responsibility, and a ḥawzah ʿilmiyyah can all be connected with the idea of a domain or field.

Yet the technical meaning of ḥawzah ʿilmiyyah, a religious educational institution, is not the root’s historical definition. It developed through the word’s extended uses and should not enter the translation of mutaḥayyiz in the Qur’an.

Similarly, ḥiyāzah can be a specific legal term for acquiring property or taking possession. Later legal rules are not a necessary part of every form’s meaning in Qur’anic Arabic.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

In Al-Anfāl 8:16, the key phrase is:

أَوْ مُتَحَيِّزًا إِلَىٰ فِئَةٍ (aw mutaḥayyizan ilā fiʾatin)

Several translations can be considered.

“Or while joining a group” is natural, fluent, and close to the result of the movement.

“Or while moving aside toward a group” makes the direction clearer, but is somewhat less fluent in Persian.

The Persian equivalent using “becoming attached to a group” is also accurate and understandable, though more formal than the ordinary word for joining.

By contrast, “or while showing partiality to a group” is unsuitable here. It foregrounds the later meaning of bias and obscures movement and joining on the battlefield.

“Or while seeking refuge with a group” also requires explanation. Refuge may be one result of joining in some situations, but the Qur’anic phrase does not necessarily describe incapacity, fear, or requesting protection.

For Forqan’s base translation, “or while joining a group” is preferable.

The explanation can clarify that this joining involves a change of battlefield position, as distinct from turning away to abandon the battle.

8. Conclusion

The Arabic root ح و ز concerns gathering, acquiring, moving aside, and joining a particular side. Driving livestock, collecting property, defining a domain, and separating from a group illustrate different branches of this semantic field.

In the Qur’an, the root occurs only as مُتَحَيِّزًا (mutaḥayyizan). In Al-Anfāl 8:16, it describes someone changing position during battle to join a group.

The meaning of joining becomes clear through the construction of mutaḥayyiz with إِلَىٰ فِئَةٍ (ilā fiʾatin). The root alone means neither fighting, flight, unfair partiality, nor ownership in every use.

This example also shows that movement on the battlefield cannot be assessed without considering a person’s purpose and situation. The verse itself distinguishes blameworthy turning away, maneuvering for battle, and joining another group.

Final definition: ح و ز concerns gathering and bringing something within a particular area or side. Its derivatives also express acquisition, moving aside, and joining. In its Qur’anic use, مُتَحَيِّزًا (mutaḥayyizan) describes someone moving toward a group and joining it, a meaning established by the word’s construction, the preposition ilā, and the battlefield context. Brief root meaning: Gathering and taking possession; in its derivatives, moving aside and joining a side or group.
