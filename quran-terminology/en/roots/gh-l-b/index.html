Prevailing, victory, defeat, and superior power: from conflict and competition to prevailing will and sturdy trees in the Qur’an

Early Arabic GH L B has two important semantic fields: overcoming another and prevailing against an opposing force; and physical thickness, stoutness, and robustness, especially of human or animal necks and sturdy trees.

In the first sense, ghalaba describes a person, group, or force overcoming an opponent in confrontation, competition, or conflicting circumstances. Ghalabah names this ascendancy; ghālib is the one who prevails; maghlūb is someone defeated or deprived of effective resistance by another force.

Such ascendancy need not involve war or physical force. A group may win a battle, a person outdo another in competition, a power control a decision or course of events, or an inner condition so dominate someone that their ability to decide and resist diminishes.

In the second sense, aghlab describes a thick, powerful neck or bodily structure. Within this field, ghulb describes stout trees and densely wooded gardens.

The Qur’an uses this root both for prevailing and defeating and for gardens of sturdy trees. Human victory, military defeat, an overwhelming inner condition, The God’s mastery over His affair, and the stoutness of trees must each be understood in their own setting.

The essential point is that prevailing does not itself mean being right. Greater strength, deception, pressure, or resources can enable someone to prevail without truth or justice being on their side. Equally, defeat alone does not prove a person’s position wrong.

1. Early Arabic usage and lexical evidence

Overcoming and defeating

In established Arabic usage, ghalabahu yaghlibuhu means to overcome or defeat someone.

This can occur in battle, conflict, competition, or circumstances in which two parties seek different outcomes.

Ghalabah and ghalab are verbal nouns of the verb; ghilāb and mughālabah concern struggle and the effort to overcome another.

If two groups fight for control of territory and one breaks the other’s military capacity, the victor can be called ghālib and the defeated party maghlūb.

The same vocabulary may describe competition between poets or participants in a discussion. Here, prevailing need not involve physical injury; it concerns one party gaining the upper hand.

A force may also overwhelm a person, such as grief affecting thought and behavior, or laughter that is difficult to control.

Thus the main sense in this branch is effective ascendancy within a relationship, rather than simply fighting or using force.

A poetic witness attributed to Abū al-Muthallam al-Hudhalī

An elegy attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Abū al-Muthallam al-Hudhalī for Ṣakhr al-Ghayy contains these lines:

رَبَّاءُ مَرْقَبَةٍ مَنَّاعُ مَغْلَبَةٍ

rabbāʾu marqabatin mannāʿu maghlabatin

رَكَّابُ سَلْهَبَةٍ قَطَّاعُ أَقْرَانِ

rakkābu salhabatin qaṭṭāʿu aqrāni

Translation:

“A watcher from high lookouts, a warder-off of defeat; a rider of tall mounts, a breaker of adversaries.”

Maghlabah here concerns defeat and an opponent’s ascendancy.

The poet describes someone who stood against enemies and resisted being overcome. Extending this protection to his group is a contextual interpretation rather than part of maghlabah itself.

Mannāʿu maghlabatin describes someone who wards off defeat and enemy domination.

This use connects the root with effective ascendancy in confrontation and defeat in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

Abū al-Muthallam is known as a pre-Islamic Hudhalī poet, and the line is transmitted in Ṣakhr al-Ghayy’s elegy. Its independent date of composition has not been established here; its historical attribution should therefore be kept within these limits.

The relevant meaning is clear: being overcome is a situation in which advantage and the capacity to resist shift toward the other party.

Prevailing in competition and speech

Ghālabahu mughālabatan describes two parties competing or struggling for ascendancy.

This is not restricted to warfare.

Two poets, for example, may compete in composition or response, with one judged superior by those versed in the language.

In some early uses, shāʿirun mughallab describes a poet repeatedly defeated in such contests. Another recorded use denotes someone judged to have prevailed.

This variation shows why a morphological form cannot be assigned the same meaning everywhere without attending to its attested usage.

Ghalaba ʿalā al-shayʾ can also mean gaining mastery or control over something, such as territory or an undertaking.

The verb alone does not reveal whether this control was legitimate, consensual, or achieved through coercion and aggression.

Those matters depend on the circumstances and evidence of the particular event.

The predominance of a trait or inner condition

Arabic ghalabah is not limited to relations between two people.

A characteristic can become so prominent that it predominates over someone’s other qualities.

Ghalaba ʿalayhi al-karamu, for example, can describe a person whose conduct and character are dominated by generosity.

Istaghlaba ʿalayhi al-ḍaḥiku is recorded for laughter becoming overwhelming and difficult to control.

These uses involve neither an army nor an enemy defeated, but a condition that takes hold of someone.

This branch matters for Al-Muʾminūn 23:106, where people say that their wretchedness overcame them.

Ghulb: stout necks and bodily structures

Another branch of GH L B concerns physical thickness and stoutness.

Aghlab describes a person or animal with a thick, powerful neck. Its feminine is ghalbāʾ, and one plural is ghulb.

Asadun aghlab describes a thick-necked lion.

Here, a physical feature is described, rather than victory in a contest.

A thick neck may be associated with bodily strength, but aghlab in this construction does not become “victorious” simply because the animal is powerful.

The description is also recorded for a large, prominent hill or rocky height.

Haḍbatun ghalbāʾ describes a large, prominent elevation; ḥadīqatun ghalbāʾ describes a garden with stout, densely growing trees.

This branch is distinct from prevailing in competition, although strength, stoutness, and superiority may provide a conceptual connection.

A poetic witness attributed to ʿAmr ibn Maʿdīkarib

A line attributed to ʿAmr ibn Maʿdīkarib reads:

يَمْشِي بِهَا غُلْبُ الرِّقَابِ كَأَنَّهُمْ

yamshī bihā ghulbu al-riqābi ka-annahum

بُزْلٌ كُسِينَ مِنَ الْكُحَيْلِ جِلَالَا

buzlun kusīna mina al-kuḥayli jilālā

Translation:

“Thick-necked ones walk there, as though they were mature camels clothed in coverings of tar.” Kuḥayl here denotes a tar-like substance used to coat camels, not merely any black material.

Early explanations understand the line as describing powerful lions.

Ghulbu al-riqāb refers to the thickness and stoutness of their necks.

Buzl denotes mature camels; the comparison emphasizes the animals’ size and strength.

The witness helps establish the physical meaning of ghulb.

ʿAmr ibn Maʿdīkarib lived across the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods. The date of this line has not been established here, so it cannot be called certainly pre-Qur’anic without independent evidence.

It nevertheless shows that ghulb describes stout, powerful necks in early poetic usage.

This meaning also appears in the Qur’an’s description of gardens.

2. Central senses and Qur’anic forms

GH L B occurs thirty-one times in twenty-eight Qur’anic verses, in five morphological groups: the basic verb ghalaba, the verbal noun ghalab, the adjective ghulb, the active participle ghālib, and the passive participle maghlūb.

The verb occurs in active and passive forms, past and present, and with different person and number endings: ghalabat, ghulibat, sa-yaghlibūn, yaghlibū, yughlabūn, and la-aghlibanna, for example.

Ghālib occurs in singular and plural, definite and indefinite forms, including ghālibun, ghāliba, al-ghālibūn, and al-ghālibīn.

Maghlūb occurs in Al-Qamar 54:10.

The noun ghalab occurs in Al-Rūm 30:3; ghulban describes gardens in ʿAbasa 80:30.

Forms such as ghallāb, mughālabah, taghallaba, taghlīb, mughallab, and ighlawlaba are known in Arabic but do not occur in the Qur’an.

Two central senses should remain distinct:

In the ascendancy branch, ghalabah means a force, person, group, or condition prevailing over another, shifting the capacity to resist or determine the outcome toward the prevailing side.

In the physical branch, ghulb concerns stoutness, thickness, and robustness, especially of necks and tree trunks.

The association between strength and thickness may explain their conceptual proximity, but it does not establish a single original physical image for all uses without historical evidence.

3. Prevailing in battle: victory, defeat, and unequal forces

A small group against a large one

Al-Baqarah 2:249 states:

كَمْ مِنْ فِئَةٍ قَلِيلَةٍ غَلَبَتْ فِئَةً كَثِيرَةً بِإِذْنِ اللَّهِ

kam min fiʾatin qalīlatin ghalabat fiʾatan kathīratan bi-idhni allāh

Al-Baqarah 2:249

Translation:

“How often a small group has overcome a large group by The God’s permission!”

These words occur in the story of Saul and his troops.

After the river test, some soldiers feel unable to confront Goliath’s large force.

Those who believe they will meet The God recall that small numbers need not mean defeat.

Ghalabat is an active past-tense verb here, describing one group’s victory over another.

Fiʾatan kathīratan identifies the group overcome.

The relationship is clear: a small group can prevail over a large one.

Ghalab itself means neither few nor many. Those features come from qalīlah and kathīrah.

Bi-idhni allāh connects victory with divine permission through an accompanying phrase, rather than through GH L B itself.

Does victory always depend on greater numbers?

Al-Anfāl 8:65 states:

إِنْ يَكُنْ مِنْكُمْ عِشْرُونَ صَابِرُونَ يَغْلِبُوا مِائَتَيْنِ

in yakun minkum ʿishrūna ṣābirūna yaghlibū miʾatayn

Al-Anfāl 8:65

Translation:

“If there are twenty steadfast among you, they will overcome two hundred.”

The same verse then mentions a hundred against a thousand.

Al-Anfāl 8:66 gives another ratio:

فَإِنْ يَكُنْ مِنْكُمْ مِائَةٌ صَابِرَةٌ يَغْلِبُوا مِائَتَيْنِ

fa-in yakun minkum miʾatun ṣābiratun yaghlibū miʾatayn

Al-Anfāl 8:66

Translation:

“So if there are a hundred steadfast among you, they will overcome two hundred.”

It then mentions a thousand against two thousand.

The beginning of Al-Anfāl 8:66 also states:

الْآنَ خَفَّفَ اللَّهُ عَنْكُمْ وَعَلِمَ أَنَّ فِيكُمْ ضَعْفًا

al-āna khaffafa allāhu ʿankum wa-ʿalima anna fīkum ḍaʿfā

Al-Anfāl 8:66

“Now The God has lightened your burden, knowing that there is weakness in you.”

These verses make the forces’ actual condition relevant to their capacity for confrontation; the instruction and expectation take account of weakness and strength.

The numerical ratios should retain their actual meanings. They do not, however, create a universal rule that every believing group, at every time and in every circumstance, must defeat a fixed number of opponents.

Steadfastness, actual capacity, the troops’ condition, and divine permission are all present in the context.

Yaghlibū itself expresses prevailing. The reasons and conditions come from the verses as a whole.

Victory and death in battle

Al-Nisāʾ 4:74 states:

وَمَنْ يُقَاتِلْ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ فَيُقْتَلْ أَوْ يَغْلِبْ فَسَوْفَ نُؤْتِيهِ أَجْرًا عَظِيمًا

wa-man yuqātil fī sabīli allāhi fa-yuqtal aw yaghlib fa-sawfa nuʾtīhi ajran ʿaẓīmā

Al-Nisāʾ 4:74

Translation:

“Whoever fights in The God’s way and is killed or prevails, We will grant him a great reward.”

Here, yaghlib is contrasted with being killed in battle.

Reward in this context is therefore not made dependent solely on military victory.

A person may fight in what the Qur’an calls fī sabīli allāh and be killed, or prevail over an opponent.

Reward is stated in both circumstances.

Outward victory alone cannot determine the struggle’s ethical value; equally, being killed or apparently defeated does not itself prove one’s cause wrong.

Assessing a struggle’s legitimacy also requires examining “in The God’s way,” the limits of fighting, and the verses on aggression and defense.

The Romans’ defeat and victory

Al-Rūm 30:2 states:

غُلِبَتِ الرُّومُ

ghulibati al-rūm

Al-Rūm 30:2

Translation:

“The Romans have been defeated.”

Al-Rūm 30:3 continues:

وَهُمْ مِنْ بَعْدِ غَلَبِهِمْ سَيَغْلِبُونَ

wa-hum min baʿdi ghalabihim sa-yaghlibūn

Al-Rūm 30:3

Translation:

“Yet after their defeat, they will prevail.”

The two clauses clearly distinguish being defeated from prevailing.

Ghulibat is a passive past-tense verb: the Romans have suffered defeat.

Sa-yaghlibūn is an active present-tense verb marked for the future: the same Romans will later prevail over their opponent.

Ghalabihim between these verbs is a verbal noun added to its object: the defeat inflicted on them. The surrounding active and passive forms determine this meaning.

A group can therefore be defeated at one time and victorious at another.

Being victorious or defeated is not necessarily a fixed, unchangeable quality of a people or political power.

Al-Rūm 30:4 and Al-Rūm 30:5 connect the future victory with divine authority and the believers’ joy. That does not imply divine ethical approval of every act and policy of the Roman Empire.

Military victory and the rightness of all a government’s conduct are distinct matters.

The standard reading followed here has passive ghulibat and active sa-yaghlibūn. Reports also transmit other active/passive readings; they are not the basis of this article’s translation. The selected reading and its roles must remain clear.

Prevailing despite financial and military resources

Of those spending their wealth to obstruct The God’s way, Al-Anfāl 8:36 states:

ثُمَّ تَكُونُ عَلَيْهِمْ حَسْرَةً ثُمَّ يُغْلَبُونَ

thumma takūnu ʿalayhim ḥasratan thumma yughlabūn

Al-Anfāl 8:36

Translation:

“Then it will become a source of regret for them, and then they will be defeated.”

Yughlabūn is passive here.

The verse describes a group using financial resources against The God’s way whose expenditure fails to secure its desired result.

Wealth and resources therefore do not by themselves guarantee victory.

GH L B itself does not mean financial loss or economic defeat. Being overcome is the outcome stated for this group; its reasons and setting come from the surrounding verses.

Āl ʿImrān 3:12 likewise addresses those who disbelieve:

سَتُغْلَبُونَ وَتُحْشَرُونَ إِلَىٰ جَهَنَّمَ

sa-tughlabūna wa-tuḥsharūna ilā jahannam

Āl ʿImrān 3:12

“You will be defeated and gathered toward Hell.”

Defeat and gathering are two separate statements. Worldly defeat should not be identified with the punishment of the Hereafter itself.

4. Competition, argument, and attempts to silence truth

Pharaoh’s magicians and claims of victory

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:113, the magicians speak to Pharaoh about their reward:

إِنَّ لَنَا لَأَجْرًا إِنْ كُنَّا نَحْنُ الْغَالِبِينَ

inna lanā la-ajran in kunnā naḥnu al-ghālibīn

Al-Aʿrāf 7:113

Translation:

“Surely we shall have a reward if we are the victors.”

In the Ḥafṣ reading, Al-Aʿrāf 7:113 has the declarative inna. Some other readings add an interrogative hamzah. Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:41 expresses the corresponding request as a question; these forms should be distinguished in translation.

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:40, some people say:

لَعَلَّنَا نَتَّبِعُ السَّحَرَةَ إِنْ كَانُوا هُمُ الْغَالِبِينَ

laʿallanā nattabiʿu al-saḥarata in kānū humu al-ghālibīn

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:40

Translation:

“Perhaps we may follow the magicians if they are the victors.”

Prevailing can thus be an object of expectation, competition, and self-interest.

The magicians seek victory and reward, while some people make following them conditional on the contest’s outcome.

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:44, the magicians say:

بِعِزَّةِ فِرْعَوْنَ إِنَّا لَنَحْنُ الْغَالِبُونَ

bi-ʿizzati firʿawna innā la-naḥnu al-ghālibūn

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:44

Translation:

“By Pharaoh’s might, we shall surely be the victors.”

The claim does not establish that victory has occurred.

After the confrontation’s outcome becomes clear, Al-Aʿrāf 7:119 states:

فَغُلِبُوا هُنَالِكَ وَانْقَلَبُوا صَاغِرِينَ

fa-ghulibū hunālika wa-inqalabū ṣāghirīn

Al-Aʿrāf 7:119

Translation:

“Thus they were defeated there and turned back humbled.”

Those who imagined themselves victorious are defeated.

Claiming victory, confidence, and political backing do not themselves establish actual success.

The defeat in this scene is not an ordinary military battle, but a confrontation between a magical display and the sign brought by Moses.

Prevailing can therefore concern the demonstrated superiority of an act or sign.

Prevailing by silencing another’s speech

Fuṣṣilat 41:26 states:

لَا تَسْمَعُوا لِهَٰذَا الْقُرْآنِ وَالْغَوْا فِيهِ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَغْلِبُونَ

lā tasmaʿū li-hādhā al-qurʾāni wa-lghaw fīhi laʿallakum taghlibūn

Fuṣṣilat 41:26

Translation:

“Do not listen to this Qur’an; make disruptive noise during it, so that you may prevail.”

The sentence quotes those opposing the Qur’an.

Instead of hearing and examining its message, they urge others not to listen and to disrupt its hearing.

Taghlibūn concerns the ascendancy they seek through interference with the message’s reception.

This has social and epistemic significance.

Someone unable to answer an argument may nevertheless prevent it from being heard through shouting, threats, censorship, or disruption.

They may apparently dominate the discussion without having the better argument.

Prevailing here therefore cannot mean “demonstrating the truth.”

Dominating a discussion is not the same as being right.

The context makes this distinction clear: the opponents propose refusal to listen and disruptive speech rather than examination of evidence.

Promised ascendancy in Moses’ story

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:35, The God addresses Moses and Aaron:

بِآيَاتِنَا أَنْتُمَا وَمَنِ اتَّبَعَكُمَا الْغَالِبُونَ

bi-āyātinā antumā wa-man ittabaʿakumā al-ghālibūn

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:35

Translation:

“Through Our signs, you two and those who follow you will prevail.”

This rendering connects bi-āyātinā with the following promise. Another syntactic analysis connects it with the preceding clause, saying that they will not reach the two through Our signs. In either analysis, the promise of prevailing belongs to a context of divine support.

This differs from the magicians’ claim. They invoked Pharaoh’s might, whereas this verse promises victory to Moses and Aaron.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:116 likewise states:

وَنَصَرْنَاهُمْ فَكَانُوا هُمُ الْغَالِبِينَ

wa-naṣarnāhum fa-kānū humu al-ghālibīn

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:116

Translation:

“We helped them, and they were the victors.”

The verse follows the account of Moses, Aaron, and their people.

Naṣarnāhum and al-ghālibīn occur in the same sentence.

Helping and prevailing are related but do not mean the same thing.

Naṣr concerns help and support; ghalabah concerns ascendancy and the outcome of confrontation.

5. Control of an affair and overwhelming inner conditions

Prevailing in a decision or undertaking

Al-Kahf 18:21 states:

قَالَ الَّذِينَ غَلَبُوا عَلَىٰ أَمْرِهِمْ لَنَتَّخِذَنَّ عَلَيْهِمْ مَسْجِدًا

qāla alladhīna ghalabū ʿalā amrihim la-nattakhidhanna ʿalayhim masjidā

Al-Kahf 18:21

Translation:

“Those who prevailed over their affair said, ‘We will certainly establish a place of worship over them.’”

The passage follows the discovery of the people of the cave and discussion about them.

Ghalabū ʿalā amrihim identifies those who gained superior authority or influence in the disputed matter.

Here, prevailing need not involve defeating a military enemy; it concerns decision-making or determining a matter’s outcome.

Social or political power and influence may enable a group to make its decision prevail.

Prevailing alone does not establish that decision’s legitimacy or correctness.

The verse quotes the group’s intention to establish a place of worship at the cave’s site. Ghalabū alone cannot establish Qur’anic approval of every decision by a dominant group or a judgment that power makes them right.

Reporting the words of those in power and ethically or religiously endorsing their decision are separate matters.

Wretchedness overcoming a person

Al-Muʾminūn 23:106 states:

قَالُوا رَبَّنَا غَلَبَتْ عَلَيْنَا شِقْوَتُنَا وَكُنَّا قَوْمًا ضَالِّينَ

qālū rabbanā ghalabat ʿalaynā shiqwatunā wa-kunnā qawman ḍāllīn

Al-Muʾminūn 23:106

Translation:

“They said, ‘Our Lord, our wretchedness overcame us, and we were a people astray.’”

The prevailing agent here is not a person or army.

The subject of ghalabat is shiqwatunā, “our wretchedness.”

ʿAlaynā indicates that this condition overcame the speakers themselves.

The verb thus describes a condition taking hold of people.

This clearly relates to the root’s main sense: a force or condition prevails and the person comes under its influence.

The confrontation here is not external; the speakers describe a condition in their lives and conduct.

Does overwhelming wretchedness remove responsibility?

The speakers themselves say that their wretchedness overcame them.

They speak in the setting of reckoning in the Hereafter.

In Al-Muʾminūn 23:107, they ask to be taken out of the Fire and say that if they return to their former conduct, they will indeed be wrongdoers.

Their statement does not establish that they had no choice or responsibility, or that all their conduct was predetermined and unavoidable.

Ghalabah denotes the ascendancy of a force or condition. Its origin, the role of human choice, and the possibility of resistance or reform cannot be determined from this verb alone.

They are explaining their previous condition. Assessing responsibility requires considering the verses on actions, warnings, choices, and reckoning together.

An overwhelming inner condition therefore does not itself establish a complete loss of agency.

6. The God’s ascendancy: governance, help, and final victory

“The God prevails over His affair”

Yūsuf 12:21 states:

وَاللَّهُ غَالِبٌ عَلَىٰ أَمْرِهِ وَلَٰكِنَّ أَكْثَرَ النَّاسِ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ

wa-allāhu ghālibun ʿalā amrihi wa-lākinna akthara al-nāsi lā yaʿlamūn

Yūsuf 12:21

Translation:

“The God prevails over His affair, but most people do not know.”

This follows Joseph’s arrival in Egypt and entry into the household of the person who purchased him.

Joseph’s brothers had plotted to remove him. Yet events brought him to Egypt and prepared the next stages of his life.

Ghālibun here concerns The God’s mastery over the course of the affair.

ʿAlā amrihi specifies the object of that mastery.

The context supports understanding The God’s affair and governance in relation to limited human plans. The pronoun can also be examined as referring to Joseph’s affairs; either way, divine mastery over the outcome is emphasized.

This use shows that prevailing extends beyond warfare.

Divine ascendancy and human choice

The God’s mastery over His affair does not imply ethical approval of every human act, including oppression and betrayal.

Joseph’s brothers decide to harm him and separate him from his father.

Their conduct is wrong, even though the subsequent story shows The God bringing about His purpose through those difficult circumstances.

An event occurring within divine power and governance does not itself endorse the wrongdoing of those involved.

The God’s ultimate power and human moral responsibility are distinct questions.

This distinction is fundamental to understanding the phrase.

“I and My Messengers will surely prevail”

Al-Mujādilah 58:21 states:

كَتَبَ اللَّهُ لَأَغْلِبَنَّ أَنَا وَرُسُلِي إِنَّ اللَّهَ قَوِيٌّ عَزِيزٌ

kataba allāhu la-aghlibanna anā wa-rusulī inna allāha qawiyyun ʿazīz

Al-Mujādilah 58:21

Translation:

“The God has decreed: ‘I and My Messengers will surely prevail.’ The God is indeed strong, invincible.”

La-aghlibanna contains an emphatic lām and the heavy emphatic nūn.

The verb is first-person singular, with an implicit subject. Anā reinforces that subject, and rusulī is coordinated with it: The God and His Messengers are named as those who prevail.

Qawiyy and ʿazīz are divine descriptions associated with strength and unassailable might.

The verse does not establish that no Prophet or Messenger ever faced persecution, apparent defeat, exile, or death in worldly life.

The Qur’an itself speaks of harm to Prophets and the killing of some of them.

The promised ascendancy must therefore be understood alongside the verses on messengership, truth, final outcomes, and help, rather than reduced to immediate, exceptionless victory in every worldly encounter.

The God’s party and prevailing

Al-Māʾidah 5:56 states:

فَإِنَّ حِزْبَ اللَّهِ هُمُ الْغَالِبُونَ

fa-inna ḥizba allāhi humu al-ghālibūn

Al-Māʾidah 5:56

Translation:

“Surely The God’s party are the ones who prevail.”

This follows discussion of allegiance to The God, the Messenger, and the believers.

In this context, “The God’s party” concerns attachment and loyalty to Him and an orientation He approves.

It does not automatically identify every political or religious group adopting that name as the certain beneficiary of the promise.

Human naming does not replace the Qur’anic criterion.

Nor does ghālibūn mean a permanent entitlement to dominate others.

The promised victory must be understood through the passage’s allegiance, faith, and conduct.

The God’s troops and prevailing

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:173 states:

وَإِنَّ جُنْدَنَا لَهُمُ الْغَالِبُونَ

wa-inna jundanā la-humu al-ghālibūn

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:173

Translation:

“And Our troops will surely be the victors.”

This follows the divine promise concerning the Messengers.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:171 and Al-Ṣāffāt 37:172 recall The God’s prior word for His Messengers; the victory of His troops follows.

Jundunā is thus linked with prevailing.

This phrase alone cannot identify every armed group claiming to fight in The God’s name as His actual troops.

True allegiance must be assessed by Qur’anic criteria.

Ghalabah itself also cannot establish the legitimacy of every war, occupation, or imposition of belief.

Prevailing and divine help

Āl ʿImrān 3:160 states:

إِنْ يَنْصُرْكُمُ اللَّهُ فَلَا غَالِبَ لَكُمْ

in yanṣurkumu allāhu fa-lā ghāliba lakum

Āl ʿImrān 3:160

Translation:

“If The God helps you, none can overcome you.”

Naṣr and ghalabah are related here.

Naṣr is helping; ghalabah is overcoming another.

The verse presents divine help as decisive for the outcome He determines.

Its conditional statement should not become an unconditional promise for every decision and battle of people who call themselves believers.

In Al-Anfāl 8:48, Satan tells a group:

لَا غَالِبَ لَكُمُ الْيَوْمَ مِنَ النَّاسِ

lā ghāliba lakumu al-yawma mina al-nās

Al-Anfāl 8:48

Translation:

“Today no one among the people can overcome you.”

Later in that verse, however, he retreats when the two groups see one another.

A promise or claim of victory does not become true merely because it is spoken.

Āl ʿImrān 3:160 concerns The God’s help, whereas Al-Anfāl 8:48 reports Satan’s deceptive speech.

The speakers do not have equal credibility, although ghālib means “one who overcomes” in both statements.

Is every outward power truly victorious?

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:44 states:

أَفَهُمُ الْغَالِبُونَ

a-fa-humu al-ghālibūn

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:44

Translation:

“Are they, then, the ones who prevail?”

The question follows discussion of prolonged enjoyment and changes affecting the land.

Its interrogative form challenges their claim to superiority and power.

A group may imagine that its current resources and strength guarantee lasting ascendancy.

Prevailing at one time does not mean immunity from future change or defeat.

The Roman account likewise shows that a group may suffer defeat and later prevail.

7. Maghlūb: when resistance is overcome

Al-Qamar 54:10 states:

فَدَعَا رَبَّهُ أَنِّي مَغْلُوبٌ فَانْتَصِرْ

fa-daʿā rabbahu annī maghlūbun fa-ntaṣir

Al-Qamar 54:10

Translation:

“He called upon his Lord: ‘I am overcome, so help me and vindicate me.’”

These words occur in Noah’s story.

The preceding verse describes his people’s denial and treatment of him.

After confronting their opposition, Noah describes himself as maghlūb and seeks his Lord’s help. His long period among his people is stated separately in Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:14, rather than in maghlūb itself.

Maghlūb is the passive participle of GH L B: someone whom another force has overcome.

It does not necessarily mean the final defeat of truth or the falsity of Noah’s call.

Someone can be overcome in resources, social power, or capacity to resist pressure while being right in their position.

Noah’s condition is accompanied by a request for help.

An opponent’s ascendancy at one stage therefore does not end the possibility of help and changed circumstances.

Once again, prevailing and being overcome describe the actual relationship of forces, rather than by themselves pronouncing a final judgment on truth and falsehood.

8. Ghulban: gardens of stout, densely growing trees

ʿAbasa 80:30 states:

وَحَدَائِقَ غُلْبًا

wa-ḥadāʾiqa ghulbā

ʿAbasa 80:30

Translation:

“And gardens of stout, densely growing trees.”

This follows the description of plant growth and earthly blessings.

ʿAbasa 80:24 invites people to consider their food.

The passage then mentions rain, the earth splitting, grain, grapes, edible plants, olives, palms, and gardens.

Ghulban describes ḥadāʾiq in this context.

The meaning of ghulban

Ghulb is a plural of aghlab and ghalbāʾ.

In bodily descriptions, aghlab concerns a thick, stout neck.

Applied to gardens and trees, the vocabulary concerns large, sturdy trees and their dense growth.

Ghulban in ʿAbasa 80:30 therefore does not describe gardens victorious over other gardens.

The subject is neither battle nor competition.

The verse describes gardens with stout, densely growing trees.

Ḥadāʾiq is the plural of ḥadīqah, garden.

Ghulban emphasizes features of the trees and the garden’s structure.

Does ghulban mean only abundant foliage?

Lexical explanations describe ḥadīqatun ghalbāʾ as a garden of large, stout, densely growing trees.

Abundant branches and leaves may accompany such a garden, but the word should not be restricted to numerous leaves.

Thick trunks and large trees also belong to its descriptive field.

“Dense gardens” conveys part of the sense; “gardens of stout, densely growing trees” makes it more explicit.

The description does not identify a particular tree species, numerical height, or specific biological structure.

Stoutness and ascendancy

Thick necks, large bodies, and greater strength may be associated with the ability to prevail.

That conceptual connection does not establish a single certain historical origin for all GH L B uses.

In battle passages, prevailing concerns one force overcoming another.

In ʿAbasa 80:30, ghulban describes the size and stoutness of trees.

The branches can be conceptually close through strength and prominence, but neither meaning should be imposed on the other.

9. Comparison with neighboring roots

GH L B and N Ṣ R: prevailing and helping

Ghalab concerns overcoming and gaining the upper hand.

Naṣr concerns helping and supporting against an enemy, pressure, or weakness.

They often relate because effective help can lead to victory.

Yet help does not necessarily mean immediate victory, nor does every victory require a third party’s assistance.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:116 places both concepts together:

وَنَصَرْنَاهُمْ فَكَانُوا هُمُ الْغَالِبِينَ

wa-naṣarnāhum fa-kānū humu al-ghālibīn

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:116

That is, they were helped and became the victors.

The construction connects help and its outcome without making the roots complete synonyms.

Forqan’s separate study of N Ṣ R examines this distinction through the meaning of help.

Study of N Ṣ R

GH L B and Q H R: prevailing and subduing

Qahr concerns mastery with the power to compel or subjugate.

Ghalab can accompany coercion and domination, but Qur’anic usage is not restricted to forcing people.

It can concern competition, battle, decisions, discussion, or an overwhelming inner condition.

In Al-Muʾminūn 23:106, wretchedness overwhelms the speakers; in Fuṣṣilat 41:26, opponents seek ascendancy by disrupting the Qur’an’s hearing.

These uses show why prevailing should not everywhere be translated as applying physical force.

Qahr and ghalabah in descriptions of The God may relate through supreme power, but each root’s distinctions should remain intact.

GH L B and Ẓ F R: prevailing and attaining success

Ẓafar concerns attaining what is sought, succeeding, and winning.

Ghalab focuses more on overcoming another party.

Someone may defeat a rival and reach their goal; here, prevailing and attaining success coincide.

A person may also attain a wish without confronting a rival.

Attainment and success can therefore occur without overcoming another.

Conversely, someone may defeat a rival at one stage without reaching the ultimate goal.

Prevailing and attaining the desired end should not always be equated.

GH L B and Ẓ H R: prevailing and becoming manifest

Ẓ H R includes becoming apparent and, in other uses, superiority and support.

GH L B concerns prevailing over another party.

Some contexts bring ẓuhūr and ghalabah close, such as a group’s superiority becoming evident or its prevailing in confrontation.

Ẓ H R also includes the back, an outer surface, and the appearance of something previously hidden.

Not every appearance is victory, nor is every victory simply appearance.

Forqan’s study of Ẓ H R examines this semantic field.

Study of Ẓ H R

GH L B and Q W Y: prevailing and strength

Quwwah means ability and strength.

Ghalab concerns one force prevailing over another.

Greater force may provide the conditions for victory, so the concepts are closely related.

Being strong, however, does not necessarily mean winning every encounter.

A group with greater military resources and numbers may lose because of other circumstances.

A less well-equipped group may prevail in a particular situation.

Al-Baqarah 2:249, Al-Anfāl 8:65, and Al-Anfāl 8:66 illustrate the contextual distinction between outward strength, steadfastness, and the outcome.

GH L B and GH L Ẓ: stoutness and thickness

Ghilẓ concerns thickness, coarseness, hardness, and, in some uses, harsh conduct.

The ghulb branch also concerns stoutness and robustness.

These roots overlap in descriptions of physical thickness.

A thick-trunked tree can be described through ghilẓ, while ḥadāʾiqa ghulban also concerns gardens of stout trees.

The semantic proximity does not make ghulban a derivative of GH L Ẓ.

Nor should victory in GH L B be confused with severity or harshness from GH L Ẓ.

10. Questions of interpretation and social application

Power and rightness are different measures

A central issue is distinguishing the ability to prevail from being in the right.

Political office, wealth, military strength, or social influence may enable someone to impose their wishes.

That ability does not itself establish that their wishes are right.

A ruler may silence opponents through force and practically control public space, but silencing them does not prove the policy correct.

A group may similarly make its narrative dominant through wealth and extensive publicity.

This is ascendancy in power and influence, not a necessary change in truth in the group’s favor.

Fuṣṣilat 41:26 clearly illustrates seeking ascendancy by preventing an opposing message from being heard.

Successfully imposing a claim should not be equated with establishing its intellectual or moral validity.

Prevailing and being overcome: active and passive perspectives

One party’s victory often accompanies another’s defeat.

The sentence’s perspective nevertheless matters.

Ghalaba Zaydun ʿAmran presents Zayd as the agent who prevails and ʿAmr as the defeated party.

Ghuliba ʿAmrun presents the same event from the perspective of the defeated person.

Qur’anic ghulibati al-rūm and sa-yaghlibūn likewise illustrate passive and active voice.

Ghālib and maghlūb also occupy different positions in the relationship.

Ignoring this morphology can reverse the victor and defeated party in translation.

Prevailing and moral responsibility

Difficult circumstances may reduce someone’s ability to cope or resist.

One may say that pressure or difficulty has overwhelmed them.

Without further examination, that statement does not establish a complete absence of choice or responsibility for every decision.

Intense fear, for example, can affect decision-making, but responsibility depends on actual circumstances, the extent of choice, and available possibilities.

The wrongdoers’ statement about overwhelming wretchedness in Al-Muʾminūn 23:106 should also be examined with these distinctions in mind.

Pressure and overwhelming inner conditions are real; the degree of responsibility requires separate consideration.

Divine ascendancy and the responsibility of power holders

The God’s mastery over His affair cannot grant moral immunity to rulers, commanders, or victorious groups.

An oppressive government may control a territory for a time.

Its success in retaining power does not imply divine approval of its oppression.

The Qur’an itself describes Pharaoh’s power and wrongdoing.

Practical power and moral judgment must therefore remain distinct.

The promise of The God’s and His Messengers’ victory should not license attributing human groups’ personal decisions, violence, or domination to His certain will.

11. Translation recommendations for Forqan

Ghalaba can be rendered “prevailed,” “overcame,” “won,” or, where appropriate, “defeated.”

“Won” or “was victorious” is natural in military encounters and competition.

“Overcame” has a broader range, including a condition, force, or will taking hold.

Passive ghuliba can be rendered “was overcome” or “was defeated.”

Ghālib can be “prevailing,” “one who overcomes,” or “victorious.”

Maghlūb is “overcome,” “defeated,” or “one over whom another has prevailed.”

The following renderings suit important Qur’anic uses:

Al-Baqarah 2:249

“How often a small group has overcome a large group by The God’s permission!”

Al-Rūm 30:2 and Al-Rūm 30:3

“The Romans have been defeated; yet after their defeat, they will prevail.”

Al-Muʾminūn 23:106

“Our Lord, our wretchedness overcame us, and we were a people astray.”

Yūsuf 12:21

“The God prevails over His affair, but most people do not know.”

Al-Qamar 54:10

“I am overcome, so help me and vindicate me.”

Fuṣṣilat 41:26

“Do not listen to this Qur’an; make disruptive noise during it, so that you may prevail.”

ʿAbasa 80:30

“And gardens of stout, densely growing trees.”

In wa-allāhu ghālibun ʿalā amrihi, “prevails” or “has mastery” should be retained. “The God does whatever He wills” may express an implication, but weakens the Arabic construction’s image of mastery and ascendancy.

Conversely, ghulban in ʿAbasa must not become “victorious gardens” or “prevailing gardens”; it describes stoutness and robustness.

Nor should ghālib become “rightful” or “deserving victory” everywhere. Rightness and moral worth require independent evidence.

12. Conclusion

Early Arabic GH L B has two important branches: overcoming another, and physical thickness and stoutness.

In the first, ghalaba describes a force, person, group, or condition prevailing over another. Ghalabah names that ascendancy, ghālib the prevailing party, and maghlūb someone defeated or overcome.

Poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period illustrates defeat, resistance, and competition. In the physical branch, aghlab and ghalbāʾ describe thick necks, robust structures, and stout trees.

The Qur’an uses prevailing in diverse situations.

In Saul’s story, a small group can defeat a large one by The God’s permission.

Al-Anfāl connects victory with troop numbers, capacity, and steadfastness.

In Al-Rūm, a group is first defeated and later prevails; victory and defeat can change with circumstances.

In Moses’ story, the magicians and Pharaoh’s supporters speak of victory but are themselves overcome.

In Fuṣṣilat 41:26, a group seeks ascendancy by obstructing hearing and creating disturbance. Prevailing can thus accompany deception and disruption without proving rightness.

In Al-Muʾminūn 23:106, wretchedness overwhelms the speakers; prevailing can concern an inner condition.

In Al-Kahf 18:21, a group gains control over the affair and decision concerning the cave’s inhabitants, but decision-making power alone does not establish correctness.

Yūsuf 12:21 describes The God as prevailing over His affair; Al-Mujādilah 58:21 promises His and His Messengers’ victory.

Divine ascendancy should not become endorsement of every political power, war, or dominant conduct. Events occurring within divine power are not identical with ethical approval of oppression.

In Al-Qamar 54:10, Noah calls himself overcome and asks for help. Being overcome does not by itself show that one’s path is wrong.

Finally, ghulban in ʿAbasa 80:30 describes gardens of stout, densely growing trees, distinct from victory and defeat.

The Qur’anic definition therefore has two branches:

In its ascendancy branch, GH L B denotes a force, person, group, or condition prevailing over another, shifting resistance or control of the outcome toward the prevailing party. This can occur in battle, competition, decision-making, or an inner condition. In the physical branch, ghulb denotes thickness, stoutness, and robustness, especially of necks and tree trunks.

For Persian Forqan, equivalents of “prevail,” “overcome,” and “defeat” suit the first branch, while “sturdy” and “stout” suit ʿAbasa 80:30.

Prevailing expresses effective ascendancy within a relationship; the word itself does not determine the truth, justice, or legitimacy of that ascendancy.
