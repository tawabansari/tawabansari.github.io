Entrusting and handing an affair over to another: from authority and responsibility in human relations to entrusting one’s own fate to The God

1. The root in early Arabic

In its branch concerning the delegation of an affair, the triliteral Arabic root F W Ḍ concerns entrusting a matter, or authority to deal with it, to someone else.

فَوَّضَ إِلَيْهِ الْأَمْرَ means that someone entrusted an affair to another and gave that person authority to handle or decide it.

Classical lexical sources explain this meaning as follows:

فَوَّضَ إِلَيْهِ الْأَمْرَ: صَيَّرَهُ إِلَيْهِ وَجَعَلَهُ الْحَاكِمَ فِيهِ

Fawwaḍa ilayhi l-amra: ṣayyarahu ilayhi wa-jaʿalahu l-ḥākima fīh.

“He entrusted the affair to him: he placed it in his hands and gave him the decision concerning it.”

This use has three elements: the person entrusting, the affair entrusted, and the person to whom it is entrusted.

For example, someone may entrust the management of part of their property to a trusted person. Ownership need not change, but authority to deal with it within specified limits passes to another.

The head of an organization may likewise entrust a decision to someone else. Here delegation is more than asking for help: it involves giving authority or responsibility for handling the affair.

Nevertheless, delegation does not always transfer full ownership or unlimited authority, or release the delegator from every responsibility. Its limits depend on the subject and conditions of the arrangement.

In the familiar use فَوَّضَ أَمْرَهُ إِلَيْهِ, entrusting the affair and handing over its handling or decision are prominent.

Historical evidence and the meaning of فَوْضَى

Another old form in this family is فَوْضَى.

In classical Arabic, it describes people who are mixed together, lack a defined leader or order, or hold their affairs and possessions in common without a clear division.

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Afwah al-Awdi reads:

لَا يَصْلُحُ النَّاسُ فَوْضَى لَا سَرَاةَ لَهُمْ
وَلَا سَرَاةَ إِذَا جُهَّالُهُمْ سَادُوا

Lā yaṣluḥu n-nāsu fawḍā lā sarāta lahum / Wa-lā sarāta idhā juhhāluhum sādū.

“People cannot prosper in disorder, without leaders to manage their affairs; nor does leadership avail when their ignorant ones rule.”

Here فَوْضَى describes a community without clear order or leadership.

The verse is attributed to al-Afwah al-Awdi in classical lexical sources. He is known as a pre-Islamic poet, but the attribution alone does not establish the verse’s precise date of composition.

With that qualification, this example helps clarify the old sense of فَوْضَى.

In another recorded lexical use, مَتَاعُهُمْ فَوْضَى بَيْنَهُمْ means that their possessions or goods are held in common without a specified division.

This relates to the modern sense “chaos,” but is not identical to it. In early Arabic, فَوْضَى could denote sharing, intermingling, or the absence of a defined leader; today its familiar Arabic sense is disorder or chaos.

Was فَوْضَى derived from delegation?

Some lexicographers connect the meanings of delegation and فَوْضَى.

On this account, when people hand their affairs to one another without clearly assigning responsibility or authority, their situation can become confused and disordered.

This helps explain a semantic relationship, but does not prove that فَوْضَى historically developed from the act of delegation.

Nor does every act of delegation cause chaos. Clear assignment of responsibility can, on the contrary, improve order.

Historically, فَوْضَى can be examined in the poetry attributed to al-Afwah, while entrusting an affair is clearly attested in the Qur’an. Establishing which branch arose first requires independent evidence.

An invented physical image therefore cannot define this family. The Qur’anic sense of entrusting an affair is clear and provides the starting point.

2. Forms of the root and its Qur’anic occurrence

Important Arabic forms include:

فَوَّضَ, “he delegated”; يُفَوِّضُ, “he delegates”; تَفْوِيض, the entrusting of an affair or authority; مُفَوِّض, the person delegating; مُفَوَّض, a person or affair to whom or for which authority or handling has been delegated; and فَوْضَى, an intermixed or disordered state.

تَفَاوَضَ also occurs in Arabic for joint discussion, exchanging views, and dealing with a matter together. In legal and commercial usage, مُفَاوَضَة has described forms of partnership and mutual delegation of authority.

These uses belong to the family’s broader history; their later technical meanings should not be read into the Qur’an.

The root F W Ḍ occurs only once in the Qur’an:

وَأُفَوِّضُ — Ghafir 40:44

This is a Form II verb. أُفَوِّضُ is first-person singular imperfect, meaning “I entrust” or “I commit.”

The initial conjunction connects the clause to the preceding speech. The verb’s root letters are fā, wāw, and ḍād; doubling the middle radical marks Form II.

The Qur’an contains neither this family’s basic verb nor the nouns تَفْوِيض and فَوْضَى, nor the participle مُفَوَّض.

Its Qur’anic meaning must therefore be understood through this single occurrence.

A defensible semantic core here is:

Entrusting an affair or concern of one’s own to someone to whom its handling and final decision are committed.

The subject, limits, and recipient’s standing emerge from the complete sentence.

3. Ghafir 40:44: entrusting an affair to The God

فَسَتَذْكُرُونَ مَا أَقُولُ لَكُمْ وَأُفَوِّضُ أَمْرِي إِلَى اللَّهِ إِنَّ اللَّهَ بَصِيرٌ بِالْعِبَادِ

Fa-sa-tadhkurūna mā aqūlu lakum wa-ufawwiḍu amrī ilā llāhi inna llāha baṣīrun bi-l-ʿibād.

“You will remember what I tell you; and I entrust my affair to The God. Indeed, The God sees the servants.”

Ghafir 40:44

This is the root’s only Qur’anic occurrence.

The speaker is a believing man from Pharaoh’s household, introduced in Ghafir 40:28. He had concealed his faith, but speaks out when the killing of Moses is proposed.

He defends Moses, recalls the danger of wrongdoing and denial, and calls his people toward the right way and deliverance.

In Ghafir 40:41, he calls them to deliverance and asks why they instead call him to the Fire.

In Ghafir 40:42, he speaks of their call to disbelief and to associating with The God something of which he has no knowledge.

Then, in Ghafir 40:44, he declares that he entrusts his affair to The God.

This sequence matters.

Before entrusting his affair, the believer has neither remained silent nor abandoned his responsibility. He has spoken, reasoned, and taken a stand against a threatening power.

Having conveyed his message, he now entrusts his own affair to The God.

أُفَوِّضُ: a deliberate act of entrusting

أُفَوِّضُ is first-person singular imperfect.

The speaker says, “I entrust.”

This expresses his own decision and action. He entrusts his affair to The God; others have not removed it from his control against his will.

Form II here denotes entrusting or handing over an affair. The form alone does not establish his precise psychological condition, his degree of calm, or how The God will deal with the affair.

The imperfect can also express the speaker’s present decision and stance. It should not be restricted without evidence to a promise of a future act.

أَمْرِي: what is entrusted?

أَمْرِي combines أَمْر with the first-person possessive suffix: “my affair” or “the matter concerning me.”

أَمْر has a broad Arabic semantic range, including an affair, situation, matter, or command.

Here, however, the verb أُفَوِّضُ and the context of danger and confrontation identify أَمْرِي as the speaker’s own affair and situation.

He says that he entrusts the outcome of his affair to The God.

This does not mean commanding The God. أَمْرِي here is not an order whose execution the speaker previously controlled over The God and now transfers to Him.

Nor does it imply abandoning all everyday decisions or human responsibilities.

The clear contextual sense is entrusting his affair in the face of the threat and opposition he encounters.

إِلَى: the direction of entrusting

The preposition إِلَى is important here.

It identifies the direction in which the affair is entrusted.

The speaker entrusts his affair to The God, rather than Pharaoh, the community’s leaders, or the power seeking to determine his fate.

In human relations, delegation may transfer authority someone previously held.

In the human relationship with The God, however, this cannot mean that He lacked authority beforehand and acquires it through a person’s declaration.

The Qur’an repeatedly identifies The God as possessing dominion, judgment, and authority. By entrusting his affair, the person turns to that power and judgment.

The linguistic sense of entrusting remains, but the relationship differs from a limited transfer of authority between two human beings.

Divine sight of the servants: the ground of trust

The verse ends by recalling that The God sees the servants.

This clause clearly relates to the act of entrusting.

The believer has addressed people who may have greater power and influence. He knows that intentions, conduct, and what lies behind their opposition are not hidden from The God.

بَصِيرٌ belongs to B Ṣ R and expresses divine sight and awareness.

Yet seeing is not the lexical meaning of entrusting. This attribute explains the ground of the speaker’s trust in The God.

Nor does بِالْعِبَادِ restrict divine sight to the believer. The God sees the servants: both the person speaking against wrongdoing and those plotting against him.

4. Entrusting and its outcome in the next verse

فَوَقَاهُ اللَّهُ سَيِّئَاتِ مَا مَكَرُوا وَحَاقَ بِآلِ فِرْعَوْنَ سُوءُ الْعَذَابِ

Fa-waqāhu llāhu sayyiʾāti mā makarū wa-ḥāqa bi-āli firʿawna sūʾu l-ʿadhāb.

“Then The God protected him from the evils of what they plotted, and grievous punishment encompassed Pharaoh’s household.”

Ghafir 40:45

The next verse reports an important outcome in the story.

The believer entrusted his affair to The God, who protected him from the evils of his opponents’ plotting.

فَوَقَاهُ belongs to W Q Y and concerns guarding and protecting against harm.

مَكَرُوا refers to the plotting and planning employed by the opponents.

The narrative thus connects entrusting with divine protection.

Nevertheless, the two verbs belong to different roots:

أُفَوِّضُ expresses entrusting the affair to The God.

فَوَقَاهُ expresses divine protection of the person.

Protection is therefore not the direct lexical meaning of entrusting; it is the outcome reported next in this particular story.

Nor does the event imply that anyone saying “I entrust my affair to The God” must become immune to every bodily, financial, or social danger.

The Qur’an also speaks of Prophets and believers harmed in the cause of truth.

The result is specified in the story of the believer from Pharaoh’s household, but the manner and timing of divine help cannot be fixed in one pattern for everyone.

5. Entrusting does not mean abandoning responsibility

Entrusting an affair to The God is sometimes understood as though a person should no longer decide, strive, or defend themselves against danger.

The believer’s story does not present that picture.

Before declaring his trust, he has fulfilled his responsibility: opposing the proposed killing of Moses, warning his people, and inviting them to deliverance.

Entrusting comes at the end of this effort, rather than replacing it.

This distinction also matters in everyday life.

Someone facing injustice may speak the truth, use legitimate means of self-preservation, and try to prevent harm, while recognizing that not every consequence of others’ behavior is under their control.

They can entrust their affair to The God without evading their responsibilities.

Conversely, someone who has violated others’ rights cannot escape redress and obligations merely by saying, “I have left the affair to The God.”

This contemporary application draws on the Qur’an’s wider treatment of responsibility and action; it is not derived entirely from F W Ḍ alone.

6. Distinctions from related roots

F W Ḍ and W K L: entrusting an affair and relying

F W Ḍ and W K L are among the closest semantic families in this context.

تَفْوِيض emphasizes committing and entrusting an affair.

In Qur’anic usage, تَوَكُّل concerns trust in and reliance upon The God.

Both can occur in the same action. Someone entrusting an affair to The God may also rely on His knowledge and power.

Lexically, however, entrusting an affair and relying are not identical.

In Ghafir 40:44, the speaker uses أُفَوِّضُ, emphasizing the entrusting of his affair.

In Al Imran 3:159, reliance follows consultation and resolve. This sequence shows that trust in The God is compatible with making decisions and fulfilling human responsibility.

In Yusuf 12:67, Jacob likewise speaks of reliance on The God after practical advice to his sons.

Entrusting and reliance are thus closely connected, but one’s precise meaning should not be absorbed into the other.

For a fuller discussion, see Forqan’s study of the Qur’anic root W K L.

F W Ḍ and S L M: entrusting and submitting

تَسْلِيم belongs to S L M. Its Qur’anic uses can concern accepting a judgment, yielding, and submitting to The God.

تَفْوِيض concerns entrusting an affair.

A person may submit to the divine command and also entrust their affair to The God in difficulty.

The two actions nevertheless have different orientations.

Submission foregrounds acceptance and yielding.

Entrusting foregrounds committing the affair to another.

For example, Al-Nisa 4:65 concerns accepting the Messenger’s judgment and submitting to it. In Ghafir 40:44, the person entrusts his own affair to The God.

Thus أُفَوِّضُ cannot simply be translated “I submit,” although entrusting an affair to The God may accompany submission to Him.

F W Ḍ and A M R: entrusting an affair and the affair itself

In Ghafir 40:44, أُفَوِّضُ and أَمْرِي belong to different roots.

F W Ḍ expresses the act of entrusting or committing.

A M R identifies the matter entrusted.

Entrusting should therefore not be equated with the affair itself. One may entrust an affair, decide about it, or assign its execution to someone. The verb and sentence structure specify the relationship.

In relation to The God, أَمْرِي likewise concerns the speaker’s affair and situation; it does not imply authority to command Him.

F W Ḍ and F Y Ḍ: two distinct roots

These families resemble each other in writing and sound, but are not the same.

F W Ḍ, with wāw as its middle radical, concerns entrusting an affair in its Qur’anic occurrence.

F Y Ḍ, with yā as its middle radical, concerns overflowing, flowing, and spreading in its respective uses.

In Ghafir 40:44, أُفَوِّضُ belongs to F W Ḍ.

The form تُفِيضُونَ, by contrast, belongs to F Y Ḍ.

Modern تَفَاوُض concerns negotiation and exchanging views, but should not be confused with إِفَاضَة or فَيْضَان from F Y Ḍ.

Forqan’s study of the Qur’anic root F Y Ḍ discusses this distinction further.

F W Ḍ and ʿ Z M: deciding and entrusting the outcome

عَزْم concerns firm resolve and the will to act.

تَفْوِيض concerns entrusting the affair.

In many situations a person must decide and act, then, recognizing their limited ability, entrust the outcome to The God.

This relationship should not be turned into an obligatory sequence for every Arabic use.

Entrusting may itself form part of a decision. A person may also rely on The God from the outset and retain their responsibility throughout.

In Ghafir 40:44, the believer entrusts his affair after speaking and warning. This order emerges from the story, not from any intrinsic meaning of F W Ḍ as a stage following resolve.

7. Later technical uses

In later periods, تَفْوِيض acquired technical uses in law, administration, theology, and mysticism.

In legal and administrative affairs, it may designate delegating decision-making authority or a particular responsibility.

In some theological discussions, it has expressed views about human choice, the relationship between human acts and divine power, or how an affair is delegated.

Some mystical usages concern entrusting one’s affair completely to The God and accepting its outcome.

These uses relate to the semantic field of entrusting, but none should predetermine the meaning of Ghafir 40:44.

The verse does not formulate a technical theory of the creation of human acts, the transfer of divine power, or a particular stage of mystical practice.

Its clear subject is a believer entrusting his affair to The God amid opposition and plotting.

Nor does this verse alone settle every philosophical question about human freedom and divine will. Those questions require examining the relevant verses on choice, action, judgment, will, and human responsibility together.

8. Translation recommendations for Forqan

For فَوَّضَ, the main equivalents are “entrusted,” “committed,” or, where appropriate, “delegated authority to deal with an affair.”

For تَفْوِيض, “entrusting an affair” or “delegation” is suitable.

مُفَوِّض denotes the delegator; depending on syntax, مُفَوَّض may describe someone given authority or an affair entrusted to another.

For فَوْضَى concerning society, “disorder,” “intermingling,” or “a leaderless state” may fit. “Chaos” is a familiar modern equivalent.

These nouns in these forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

Translating the Qur’anic form

The following recommendation concerns Ghafir 40:44; the complete verse and its translation appear in section 3.

أُفَوِّضُ — Ghafir 40:44

Suggested rendering of the clause: “And I entrust my affair to The God.”

This is clear, natural, and consistent with the Arabic construction.

أُفَوِّضُ is rendered “I entrust,” أَمْرِي “my affair,” and إِلَى with the following divine name “to The God.”

Another possibility is:

“And I commit my affair to The God.”

In Persian, “my affair” stays closer to the Arabic wording, while the everyday equivalent “my business” can sound more natural to a general reader.

“I rely on The God” is close to the thought, but does not preserve the distinction between entrusting and reliance.

“I abandon all my responsibilities” is incorrect: neither the verb nor its context supports that sense.

“I make The God my agent” also substitutes a different relationship for the Qur’anic expression, which speaks of entrusting an affair.

The recommended Forqan rendering therefore remains:

“And I entrust my affair to The God.”

Summary

In the branch relevant to the Qur’anic verse, F W Ḍ concerns entrusting an affair or the authority to handle it.

In human relations, this may concern decision-making, administration, or a particular responsibility. It does not always transfer full ownership or remove every responsibility from the delegator.

Early Arabic also records فَوْضَى for intermingling, sharing without a clear division, or a community without a defined leader. Its semantic relationship to entrusting can be examined, but the historical priority of either branch is not conclusively established.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as وَأُفَوِّضُ in Ghafir 40:44.

The speaker is a believer from Pharaoh’s household who has opposed the killing of Moses, addressed his people, and called them toward the right path. After fulfilling this responsibility, he entrusts his affair to The God.

The next verse reports that The God protected him from the evils of their plotting.

Together, these verses show entrusting accompanied by speech, reasoning, and responsibility. It stands alongside human action rather than replacing it.

Entrusting is closely related to reliance and submission, but is not fully synonymous with them. It emphasizes committing the affair; reliance emphasizes trust; submission emphasizes acceptance and yielding.

Nor should entrusting an affair to The God be imagined as transferring authority He previously lacked. The person commits their affair to divine knowledge, power, and judgment.

Final root definition for Forqan:

F W Ḍ: in the branch of entrusting, committing an affair to another and handing over its handling or decision, within limits determined by the parties’ relationship and the matter involved.

فَوَّضَ denotes performing this act, تَفْوِيض the act itself, and مُفَوِّض the delegator. فَوْضَى belongs to a branch concerning intermingling, sharing without a clear division, or disorder; it should not be equated with deliberate entrusting.

In Ghafir 40:44, أُفَوِّضُ expresses entrusting one’s own affair to The God after speaking the truth and fulfilling responsibility, trusting that He sees the servants and their actions.
