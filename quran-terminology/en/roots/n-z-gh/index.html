Harmful incitement: from pricking and hurtful speech to stirring hostility and disrupting human relationships

1. Introducing the root and its semantic core

The Arabic root N Z GH concerns provoking, inciting, and exerting a harmful influence that may draw someone into wrongful conduct or produce discord and hostility between people.

One established use is inciting two or more people against one another. «نَزَغَ بَيْنَهُمْ» describes someone who corrupts relationships through words or actions and turns people against each other.

In another use, «نَزْغ» approaches pricking, inserting a pointed object into the body, or provoking with an irritating jab. Arabic records «نَزَغَهُ» for pricking or provoking someone and «نَزَغَهُ بِكَلِمَةٍ» for hurting or taunting someone with words.

This image can illuminate a connection between physical provocation and psychological or social incitement. Just as a small jab or prick may make an animal move suddenly, provocative words can arouse anger and draw someone into an ill-considered reaction.

However, this analogy should not be presented as the certain historical origin of every meaning of the root. Corrupting relations between people is independently recorded in early usage, and sufficient evidence does not establish that all social uses necessarily arose from physical pricking.

Another important feature is its negative direction in established usage. Unlike the general word “incitement,” which can concern encouragement toward good or evil, nazgh in the Qur’an consistently concerns harmful incitement, satanic prompting, and disrupted relationships.

Thus, “harmful provocation and incitement, especially toward corruption in conduct and relationships” is a defensible semantic core for its Qur’anic uses.

Yet nazgh should not be restricted at the outset to disagreement between two people. In two Qur’anic verses, it concerns an incitement someone may encounter inwardly before an outward dispute has occurred.

Qur’anic forms

The root N Z GH occurs six times in four Qur’anic verses, in just two principal grammatical categories:

  • «يَنْزَغَنَّكَ»: Al-Aʿrāf 7:200 and Fuṣṣilat 41:36.
  • «نَزْغٌ»: Al-Aʿrāf 7:200 and Fuṣṣilat 41:36.
  • «نَزَغَ»: Yūsuf 12:100.
  • «يَنْزَغُ»: Al-Isrāʾ 17:53.

All four verbal occurrences belong to Form I. The other two occurrences are the verbal noun «نَزْغ».

Arabic also has forms such as «نَازِغ», «نَزَّاغ», «مِنْزَغ», «نَزْغَة», «نَزِيغَة», and «نَوَازِغ», but they do not occur in the Qur’an.

No Form III, VI, or X verb from this root occurs in the Qur’an.

This matters because the presence of disagreement or contention does not justify adding forms of tanāzuʿ to N Z GH. Tanāzuʿ belongs to the separate root N Z ʿ.

2. Early Arabic usage and semantic branches

Nazgh: inciting people against one another

Early Arabic records nazgh for inciting people against one another and corrupting their relationships.

An established expression is:

نَزَغَ فُلَانٌ بَيْنَهُمْ نَزْغًا

nazagha fulānun baynahum nazghan

Meaning:

“So-and-so stirred discord and corruption among them.”

Here, someone’s intervention damages an existing relationship between other people.

This intervention may involve speech, stirring emotions, disparagement, or relaying statements that turn the parties against each other.

The root itself does not establish that nazgh always takes the form of lying or malicious tale-bearing. Someone might also inflame conflict by exaggerating a true remark, choosing an inappropriate moment, provoking anger, or distorting another person’s intention.

What stands out is the incitement and its corrupting effect, rather than one fixed method.

One lexical definition of the root is:

النُّونُ وَالزَّاءُ وَالْغَيْنُ كَلِمَةٌ تَدُلُّ عَلَى إِفْسَادٍ بَيْنَ اثْنَيْنِ

al-nūnu wa-al-zāʾu wa-al-ghaynu kalimatun tadullu ʿalā ifsādin bayna ithnayni

Translation:

“This root denotes causing corruption between two people.”

This definition clarifies one of the most important uses of nazgh.

However, “between two” should not impose a fixed numerical limit. Arabic also uses nazgh among a group, potentially involving several people or communities.

Its Qur’anic use for inciting one person also shows that its meaning is not confined to a dispute already manifest between two people.

Nazgh: speech that corrupts relations among people

Another lexical explanation of nazgh is:

النَّزْغُ: الْكَلَامُ الَّذِي يُغْرِي بَيْنَ النَّاسِ

al-nazghu al-kalāmu alladhī yughrī bayna al-nāsi

Meaning:

“Nazgh is speech that incites people against one another.”

This use matters socially: speech can do more than communicate information and become an instrument of provocation and hostility.

For example, someone may relay a remark while omitting important context. If the purpose or evident effect is to arouse resentment and hostility, that conduct approaches the semantic field of nazgh.

Taunting, disparagement, and hurtful speech can likewise be forms of such incitement.

But not every report or identification of wrongdoing is nazgh.

Someone may provide an accurate, documented account of harmful conduct to prevent injustice. That does not become nazgh merely because the report is unwelcome or upsets the person criticized.

Truthful clarification, substantiated criticism, and corrupting incitement must be distinguished.

This distinction follows from the root’s meaning and its role in human relationships; it does not mean that all unwelcome statements are equally prohibited.

Nazgh: pricking and physical provocation

Arabic also records «نَزَغَهُ» for pricking, inserting a pointed object into the body, or delivering a provoking jab.

Its physical sense is explained as follows:

وَالنَّزْغُ شِبْهُ الْوَخْزِ وَالطَّعْنِ

wa-al-nazghu shibhu al-wakhzi wa-al-ṭaʿni

Translation:

“Nazgh resembles pricking and piercing with a pointed object.”

Also:

نَزَغَهُ بِكَلِمَةٍ

nazaghahu bi-kalimatin

Meaning:

“He taunted or hurt him with a remark.”

These uses show a semantic proximity between a physical prick and a verbal barb.

A physical prick may cause stimulation, pain, or a sudden reaction. A verbal barb can similarly cause anger or hurt and provoke a response.

However, this does not establish that every verbal nazgh is as painful as a physical prick or that every satanic prompting in the Qur’an involves an actual object entering the body.

The image is useful only for understanding part of the root’s semantic field.

Is low intensity part of the meaning of nazgh?

Some lexical explanations use «حَرَّكَهُ أَدْنَى حَرَكَةٍ»: he stirred him with the slightest movement.

This shows that nazgh can begin with a small influence.

Sometimes a short sentence, a contemptuous look, or a sarcastic remark is enough to arouse anger and start a dispute.

But this does not mean that nazgh is always mild or insignificant.

A small provocation can have far-reaching consequences.

Nor does nazgh itself necessarily specify the degree or intensity of incitement. Context establishes its intensity and consequences.

The definition should therefore emphasize harmful incitement, treating a small beginning as one possibility rather than a necessary condition.

A poetic witness: enemies’ provocative words

Ruʾbah ibn al-ʿAjjāj, a poet of the Islamic period, says in rajaz verse:

وَاحْذَرْ أَقَاوِيلَ الْعُدَاةِ النُّزَّغِ

wa-iḥdhar aqāwīla al-ʿudāti al-nuzzaghi

عَلَيَّ إِنِّي لَسْتُ بِالْمُزَغْزَغِ

ʿalayya innī lastu bi-al-muzaghzaghi

أَغْدُو وَعِرْضِي لَيْسَ بِالْمُمَشَّغِ

aghdū wa-ʿirḍī laysa bi-al-mumashshaghi

Translation:

“Beware the words of disparaging, troublemaking enemies against me; I am no fitting object of ridicule or attacks on my honor, and I begin the day with my reputation unstained.”

Here, «النُّزَّغ» refers to people whose words stir discord and disparagement.

«المُزَغْزَغ» describes someone mocked or disparaged in lineage and standing; «المُمَشَّغ» here concerns honor that has been stained or impugned.

This witness matters because it places nazgh within actual human relationships, disparagement, and attempts to damage another person’s reputation.

But Ruʾbah ibn al-ʿAjjāj lived after the Qur’an’s revelation. This is therefore not direct evidence of pre-Qur’anic usage.

Nor should every similar form in these lines be assigned to N Z GH. «مُزَغْزَغ» belongs to Z GH Z GH, and «مُمَشَّغ» to M SH GH; both must be distinguished from «النُّزَّغ».

The verse shows that nazgh concerned inflammatory speech and disparagement in Arabic poetry, but does not by itself date the first emergence of that meaning.

This study has not established an independent pre-Islamic poetic witness for N Z GH with secure wording and attribution. An expression’s presence in a dictionary alone does not establish that it predates the Qur’an.

Other established derivatives

Arabic uses «مِنْزَغ» and «نَزَّاغ» for someone who creates hostility and discord among people.

«نَازِغ» can describe someone who disparages another or speaks to harm them.

«نَزْغَة» can mean a prick, jab, or incitement, while «نَزِيغَة» denotes a bad or hurtful remark.

These forms show that N Z GH concerns both physical provocation and verbal or social incitement in Arabic.

None of these derivatives occurs in the Qur’an. Its actual Qur’anic forms consist only of Form I verbs and the verbal noun nazgh.

3. The first Qur’anic passage: Al-Aʿrāf 7:200

وَإِمَّا يَنْزَغَنَّكَ مِنَ الشَّيْطَانِ نَزْغٌ فَاسْتَعِذْ بِاللَّهِ إِنَّهُ سَمِيعٌ عَلِيمٌ

wa-immā yanzaghannaka mina al-shayṭāni nazghun fa-istaʿidh bi-allāhi innahu samīʿun ʿalīmun

Translation:

“If an incitement from Satan should provoke you, seek refuge in The God; He is indeed Hearing, Knowing.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:200

In the order of the muṣḥaf, this is the first verse containing the root N Z GH.

The verb «يَنْزَغَنَّكَ» and verbal noun «نَزْغٌ» occur together in one sentence here.

Understanding the preceding verse is essential to its meaning.

A. Forgiveness and turning away from the ignorant

Al-Aʿrāf 7:199 says:

خُذِ الْعَفْوَ وَأْمُرْ بِالْعُرْفِ وَأَعْرِضْ عَنِ الْجَاهِلِينَ

khudhi al-ʿafwa wa-mur bi-al-ʿurfi wa-aʿriḍ ʿani al-jāhilīna

Translation:

“Practice forgiveness, command what is recognized as good, and turn away from the ignorant.”

This verse describes three behaviors: forgiveness, calling for good, and turning away from the ignorant.

Verse 200 immediately follows with a warning about satanic nazgh.

This continuity matters.

When faced with ignorance, insults, or opposition, someone may grow angry and want to respond in kind.

In that situation, incitement can turn them away from forgiveness and measured conduct.

Nazgh in this context can thus concern an incitement toward rash retaliation, anger, or wrongful conduct.

But it should not be restricted to anger. Anger is one understandable setting for this incitement; the root itself has a wider field.

Nor does the verse say that every feeling of anger or human hurt is necessarily satanic nazgh.

It warns against incitement that diverts people from right conduct.

B. The construction «يَنْزَغَنَّكَ مِنَ الشَّيْطَانِ نَزْغٌ»

Here, «يَنْزَغَنَّكَ» is an imperfect verb with the heavy emphatic nūn.

The pronoun «كَ» is its object; the Prophet is the addressee.

The grammatical subject, however, is the nominative «نَزْغٌ».

«مِنَ الشَّيْطَانِ» identifies the source of the incitement.

A rendering close to the construction would therefore be:

“If an incitement from Satan should incite you.”

This is close in general message to “if Satan should incite you,” but the grammatical construction is different.

The Qur’an makes the incitement itself the subject here and attributes its origin to Satan.

«وَإِمَّا» also introduces a condition: if the situation occurs, the addressee must respond in a specified way.

The condition does not establish that the addressee has already committed wrongdoing.

The verse concerns encountering incitement and responding to it, rather than a sin whose occurrence has already been established.

C. The meaning of «نَزْغٌ»

«نَزْغٌ» is the verbal noun of nazagha.

Here, it denotes harmful incitement originating from Satan.

Lexical explanations also describe satanic nazgh as whispering and pricking the heart.

This expression should not be taken as physically inserting something into the human heart.

Nor does it establish a specific neurological, psychological, or physical mechanism.

What the Qur’an states here is the possibility of satanic incitement and the need to seek refuge in The God against it.

The word itself does not explain precisely how that influence operates.

D. «فَاسْتَعِذْ بِاللَّهِ»: responding to incitement

After mentioning nazgh, the command follows:

فَاسْتَعِذْ بِاللَّهِ

fa-istaʿidh bi-allāhi

“Then seek refuge in The God.”

Seeking refuge belongs to the root ʿ W DH.

This command shows that the person facing incitement is not presented as wholly without agency.

Although incitement may reach them, their response remains a matter of command and responsibility.

The next verse says:

إِنَّ الَّذِينَ اتَّقَوْا إِذَا مَسَّهُمْ طَائِفٌ مِنَ الشَّيْطَانِ تَذَكَّرُوا فَإِذَا هُمْ مُبْصِرُونَ

inna alladhīna ittaqaw idhā massahum ṭāʾifun mina al-shayṭāni tadhakkarū fa-idhā hum mubṣirūna

Translation:

“Those who are mindful, when a passing prompting from Satan touches them, remember, and suddenly they see clearly.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:201

This verse sets remembrance and restored insight against satanic incitement.

The two verses together show that responding properly to nazgh involves more than naming Satan: renewed attention to truth and avoiding a wrongful reaction also matter.

This conclusion comes from the verses together. The root nazgh itself does not mean forgetting or losing one’s sight.

4. Yūsuf 12:100: nazgh and the brothers’ disrupted relationship

The account of Joseph says:

وَقَدْ أَحْسَنَ بِي إِذْ أَخْرَجَنِي مِنَ السِّجْنِ وَجَاءَ بِكُمْ مِنَ الْبَدْوِ مِنْ بَعْدِ أَنْ نَزَغَ الشَّيْطَانُ بَيْنِي وَبَيْنَ إِخْوَتِي إِنَّ رَبِّي لَطِيفٌ لِمَا يَشَاءُ إِنَّهُ هُوَ الْعَلِيمُ الْحَكِيمُ

wa-qad aḥsana bī idh akhrajanī mina al-sijni wa-jāʾa bikum mina al-badwi min baʿdi an nazagha al-shayṭānu baynī wa-bayna ikhwatī inna rabbī laṭīfun li-mā yashāʾu innahu huwa al-ʿalīmu al-ḥakīmu

Translation:

“He was good to me when He brought me out of prison and brought you from the desert, after Satan had stirred corruption and discord between me and my brothers. My Lord is indeed subtle in what He wills; He is the Knowing, the Wise.”

Yūsuf 12:100, part of the verse

«نَزَغَ» is a perfect verb here.

Its subject is «الشَّيْطَانُ».

«بَيْنِي وَبَيْنَ إِخْوَتِي» identifies the relationship in which the nazgh occurred.

The direct sense is therefore corrupting relations and stirring discord between Joseph and his brothers.

A. Does nazgh mean an ordinary disagreement?

In Joseph’s story, the issue extends beyond an ordinary difference of opinion.

His brothers resented their father’s affection and regard for Joseph and his brother, and discussed removing Joseph.

Yūsuf 12:9 says:

اقْتُلُوا يُوسُفَ أَوِ اطْرَحُوهُ أَرْضًا يَخْلُ لَكُمْ وَجْهُ أَبِيكُمْ

uqtulū yūsufa awi iṭraḥūhu arḍan yakhlu lakum wajhu abīkum

Translation:

“Kill Joseph or cast him away in some land, so that your father’s attention will be yours alone.”

This indicates envy and harmful conduct that could lead to killing Joseph or sending him away.

Nazgh in Yūsuf 12:100 therefore concerns real corruption in family relationships, rather than a mere difference of taste or viewpoint.

Yet killing, envy, and banishing someone are not independent lexical meanings of nazgh.

Those behaviors are understood from the narrative and its statements.

B. Does attributing nazgh to Satan remove the brothers’ responsibility?

In the phrase under discussion, Satan is the subject of nazagha.

Yet the Qur’an also attributes the brothers’ actions to the brothers themselves.

In Yūsuf 12:91, the brothers say:

تَاللَّهِ لَقَدْ آثَرَكَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْنَا وَإِنْ كُنَّا لَخَاطِئِينَ

ta-allāhi la-qad ātharaka allāhu ʿalaynā wa-in kunnā la-khāṭiʾīna

Translation:

“By The God, The God has indeed favored you over us, and we were truly in the wrong.”

Here, the brothers accept responsibility for their past conduct.

In the next verse Joseph says:

لَا تَثْرِيبَ عَلَيْكُمُ الْيَوْمَ

lā tathrība ʿalaykumu al-yawma

“There is no reproach upon you today.”

Yūsuf 12:92, part of the verse

This continuity shows that attributing corrupting incitement to Satan need not conflict with human responsibility.

Satan can be identified as an inciting agent while people remain responsible for their choices and conduct.

Thus, Joseph’s mention of satanic nazgh does not mean that his brothers bore no responsibility for wronging him.

Conversely, “Satan” should not be omitted from the translation, reducing nazgh to the brothers’ natural envy alone.

Both satanic incitement and actual human conduct are present in the text.

C. Why does Joseph speak of nazgh?

Joseph says this after being reunited with his family.

Past conflict and separation have ended in this scene, and the family has gathered again.

Rather than recounting the details of his brothers’ actions and reproaching them again, he speaks of Satan’s nazgh between him and them.

This manner of speaking can be connected with the reconciliation and forgiveness surrounding the scene.

It does not follow that nazgh lexically means forgiveness or reconciliation.

Nazgh still denotes corrupting incitement. Forgiveness and reconciliation are understood from Joseph’s conduct and the broader narrative.

Nor does «نَزَغَ الشَّيْطَانُ بَيْنِي وَبَيْنَ إِخْوَتِي» by itself say that Joseph and his brothers were equally at fault for the conflict.

“Between me and my brothers” locates the damaged relationship; it does not divide responsibility equally.

That distinction matters for an accurate understanding of the story.

5. Al-Isrāʾ 17:53: nazgh and responsibility in speech

وَقُلْ لِعِبَادِي يَقُولُوا الَّتِي هِيَ أَحْسَنُ إِنَّ الشَّيْطَانَ يَنْزَغُ بَيْنَهُمْ إِنَّ الشَّيْطَانَ كَانَ لِلْإِنْسَانِ عَدُوًّا مُبِينًا

wa-qul li-ʿibādī yaqūlū allatī hiya aḥsanu inna al-shayṭāna yanzaghu baynahum inna al-shayṭāna kāna li-al-insāni ʿaduwwan mubīnan

Translation:

“Tell My servants to say what is better. Satan indeed stirs discord and corruption among them; Satan has indeed been an evident enemy to the human being.”

Al-Isrāʾ 17:53

This is among the clearest social uses of N Z GH.

The verse begins with how people speak, then explains why that matters through the danger of satanic nazgh among them.

A. «يَقُولُوا الَّتِي هِيَ أَحْسَنُ»

Servants are asked here to choose the better expression.

«أَحْسَنُ» belongs to Ḥ S N and means better.

It is not nazgh, and their meanings should not be merged.

Nevertheless, the connection between the clauses shows the importance of how people speak in preventing discord.

Two people may disagree on an issue. If each explains their intention clearly and respectfully, the disagreement can remain a discussion of the matter itself.

But contempt, taunting, accusation, or incitement makes the same conversation more likely to turn into hostility.

This fits nazgh: an intervention or incitement that damages relationships.

B. Is better speech always gentle and agreeable?

The verse speaks of «الَّتِي هِيَ أَحْسَنُ», rather than merely what a listener finds pleasant.

Telling the truth, criticizing injustice, or warning against wrongdoing may be unwelcome to the person addressed.

Being unwelcome does not by itself make speech nazgh or a source of corruption.

Nor does a gentle surface guarantee truthfulness or goodwill.

Someone may calmly spread a lie or use an apparently friendly manner to incite others against a person.

Tone alone is therefore insufficient when examining nazgh. Truthfulness, intention, context, manner of expression, and likely effects on relationships also matter.

Better speech should not be equated with flattery or withholding the truth; equally, speaking the truth plainly does not authorize contempt and corrupting incitement.

This conclusion follows from the connection between better speech and the warning against nazgh, rather than from the root N Z GH in isolation.

C. «إِنَّ الشَّيْطَانَ يَنْزَغُ بَيْنَهُمْ»

Here, Satan is the subject, and «يَنْزَغُ» is an imperfect verb.

«بَيْنَهُمْ» identifies where nazgh has its effect: relationships between people.

The imperfect can concern conduct that may recur.

The imperfect alone does not establish that satanic nazgh necessarily occurs in every human conversation.

The verse warns of a danger requiring vigilance in speech and conduct.

Nor is nazgh restricted to two people here. «بَيْنَهُمْ» can concern relationships within a group of servants.

The verse’s message about speech thus extends beyond family relationships or individual conversations, although it should not become an automatic ruling about every social disagreement.

6. Fuṣṣilat 41:36: nazgh, perseverance, and the better response

وَإِمَّا يَنْزَغَنَّكَ مِنَ الشَّيْطَانِ نَزْغٌ فَاسْتَعِذْ بِاللَّهِ إِنَّهُ هُوَ السَّمِيعُ الْعَلِيمُ

wa-immā yanzaghannaka mina al-shayṭāni nazghun fa-istaʿidh bi-allāhi innahu huwa al-samīʿu al-ʿalīmu

Translation:

“If an incitement from Satan should provoke you, seek refuge in The God; He indeed is the Hearing, the Knowing.”

Fuṣṣilat 41:36

This verse closely resembles Al-Aʿrāf 7:200 in construction.

Both place «يَنْزَغَنَّكَ» and «نَزْغٌ» together, followed by the command «فَاسْتَعِذْ بِاللَّهِ».

Their endings, however, differ in wording.

Al-Aʿrāf says:

إِنَّهُ سَمِيعٌ عَلِيمٌ

innahu samīʿun ʿalīmun

Fuṣṣilat says:

إِنَّهُ هُوَ السَّمِيعُ الْعَلِيمُ

innahu huwa al-samīʿu al-ʿalīmu

In Fuṣṣilat, «هُوَ» and the definiteness of the two attributes add emphasis.

But the meaning of nazgh does not change between the two verses.

A. Responding to wrongdoing

Fuṣṣilat 41:34 says:

وَلَا تَسْتَوِي الْحَسَنَةُ وَلَا السَّيِّئَةُ ادْفَعْ بِالَّتِي هِيَ أَحْسَنُ فَإِذَا الَّذِي بَيْنَكَ وَبَيْنَهُ عَدَاوَةٌ كَأَنَّهُ وَلِيٌّ حَمِيمٌ

wa-lā tastawī al-ḥasanatu wa-lā al-sayyiʾatu idfaʿ bi-allatī hiya aḥsanu fa-idhā alladhī baynaka wa-baynahu ʿadāwatun ka-annahu waliyyun ḥamīmun

Translation:

“Good and evil are not equal. Respond with what is better, and suddenly the one between whom and you there is hostility is as though a close friend.”

This verse describes how to respond to wrongdoing and hostility.

The next verse says:

وَمَا يُلَقَّاهَا إِلَّا الَّذِينَ صَبَرُوا وَمَا يُلَقَّاهَا إِلَّا ذُو حَظٍّ عَظِيمٍ

wa-mā yulaqqāhā illā alladhīna ṣabarū wa-mā yulaqqāhā illā dhū ḥaẓẓin ʿaẓīmin

Translation:

“This is granted only to those who persevere, and granted only to one who has a great share.”

Fuṣṣilat 41:35

The warning against satanic nazgh then follows.

The semantic sequence is clear.

Responding to wrongdoing with better conduct requires perseverance and restraint of rash reactions.

Against that background, nazgh can incite someone toward an angry, vengeful, or wrongful response.

The warning thus continues the call to perseverance and a better response.

Yet nazgh here should not be restricted to revenge. Revenge is one fitting contextual example, rather than the root’s entire meaning.

B. Does the better response mean accepting injustice?

These verses present good in response to evil. They do not establish that every injustice must go unanswered or that people should cease defending their own rights or others’ rights.

The issue is choosing how to respond.

Forgiveness and forbearance may suit one situation, while preventing harm, seeking a right, or resorting to fair judgment may be necessary in another.

This distinction must be examined alongside the Qur’an’s broader principles of justice, forgiveness, and preventing corruption.

Nazgh concerns incitement that diverts someone from a measured, proper response in this context.

Defending a right should therefore not automatically be called nazgh, nor every silence a sign of perseverance.

C. Is seeking refuge in The God merely saying a phrase?

Both Al-Aʿrāf and Fuṣṣilat command seeking refuge.

Lexically, istiʿādhah concerns asking for refuge.

In Fuṣṣilat, however, this command follows the call to a better response and perseverance.

That continuity suggests that seeking refuge against incitement also involves returning to proper conduct.

Yet no particular act or sentence should be declared the only valid form of seeking refuge without independent evidence.

Nor does the verse establish that saying a phrase of refuge removes responsibility for controlling one’s conduct.

Seeking refuge and responsibility for conduct need not conflict in the Qur’anic text.

7. Comparing N Z GH with related roots

N Z GH (نَزَغَ) and N Z ʿ (نَزَعَ): corrupting incitement and drawing or removing

These roots are close in letters and sound but differ importantly in meaning.

Arabic nazaʿa concerns drawing, extracting, and removing something from its place.

Qur’anic nazʿ concerns Moses drawing out his hand, taking away authority, removing resentment, and disagreement between people.

Nazagha, by contrast, concerns harmful incitement and corruption in conduct or relationships.

For example, Al-Nisāʾ 4:59 says:

فَإِنْ تَنَازَعْتُمْ فِي شَيْءٍ فَرُدُّوهُ إِلَى اللَّهِ وَالرَّسُولِ

fa-in tanāzaʿtum fī shayʾin fa-ruddūhu ilā allāhi wa-al-rasūli

“If you disagree over something, refer it to The God and the Messenger.”

Tanāzuʿ here names a disagreement between people.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:53, however, says:

إِنَّ الشَّيْطَانَ يَنْزَغُ بَيْنَهُمْ

inna al-shayṭāna yanzaghu baynahum

“Satan indeed stirs discord and corruption among them.”

Nazgh here concerns incitement and corrupting the relationship.

In some situations nazgh may lead to tanāzuʿ. That is a possible relationship between two behaviors, rather than an identical lexical meaning.

Moreover, tanāzuʿ is not always blameworthy in the Qur’an. In Al-Ṭūr 52:23, «يَتَنَازَعُونَ» concerns the people of the Garden passing a cup among themselves.

All Qur’anic instances of nazgh, by contrast, occur in the context of satanic, corrupting incitement.

Thus, Qur’anic nazgh has a more specifically negative direction, while nazʿ and tanāzuʿ have a broader semantic field whose value depends on context.

Study of N Z ʿ

N Z GH (نَزَغَ) and W S W S (وَسْوَسَ): incitement and whispering

Arabic waswasah concerns hidden, recurring speech or prompting.

The Qur’an says:

فَوَسْوَسَ لَهُمَا الشَّيْطَانُ

fa-waswasa lahumā al-shayṭānu

“Then Satan whispered to them both.”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:20

Al-Nās also says:

مِنْ شَرِّ الْوَسْوَاسِ الْخَنَّاسِ

min sharri al-waswāsi al-khannāsi

“From the evil of the retreating whisperer.”

Al-Nās 114:4

In Qāf 50:16, whispering is attributed to the human self.

These uses show that waswasah may concern inward speech or prompting; the Qur’an does not confine it to a single form of satanic influence.

Nazgh overlaps with waswasah, but emphasizes stirring corruption and hostility, especially in uses concerning human relationships.

Nazgh can also concern incitement toward an immediate wrongful reaction.

This does not mean that waswasah is always gentle and nazgh always intense. That boundary is not absolute.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:200, nazgh can begin with a small prompting and be connected with waswasah in that context.

The roots are close, but translating every nazgh as “whispering” without regard to context would be too restrictive.

Study of W S W S

N Z GH (نَزَغَ) and H M Z (هَمَزَ): incitement and taunting

Arabic H M Z concerns pricking, taunting, and inciting.

Al-Muʾminūn says:

وَقُلْ رَبِّ أَعُوذُ بِكَ مِنْ هَمَزَاتِ الشَّيَاطِينِ

wa-qul rabbi aʿūdhu bika min hamazāti al-shayāṭīni

“Say: My Lord, I seek refuge in You from the incitements and promptings of the devils.”

Al-Muʾminūn 23:97

This is close in subject to satanic nazgh.

Both concern harmful incitement and seeking refuge in The God against it.

But hamz also has another Qur’anic use:

وَيْلٌ لِكُلِّ هُمَزَةٍ لُمَزَةٍ

waylun li-kulli humazatin lumazatin

“Woe to every faultfinder and taunter.”

Al-Humazah 104:1

Hamz here concerns human faultfinding and taunting.

Nazgh too can operate through hurtful words, but its Qur’anic uses highlight satanic incitement and corruption in relationships.

These roots therefore overlap without being identical in every use.

Study of H M Z

N Z GH (نَزَغَ) and W Q ʿ (وَقَعَ): incitement and the occurrence of discord

Some Arabic uses connect nazgh with malicious tale-bearing and creating waqīʿah between people.

The general sense of W Q ʿ, however, concerns occurring or something coming to rest.

In one usage, waqīʿah concerns disparaging people and damaging their reputation.

In that sense it may be an instrument of nazgh.

But an event’s occurrence, disparaging someone, and corrupting incitement should not be treated as fully synonymous.

Nazgh may operate through disparagement but is not restricted to it.

N Z GH (نَزَغَ) and GH W Y (غَوَىٰ): incitement and going astray

Qur’anic GH W Y concerns going astray and departing from the right way.

In some verses Satan speaks of attempting to lead humans astray.

Nazgh may be one form of inciting people toward wrongful conduct and, in that respect, relate to leading astray.

But nazgh and ighwāʾ are not identical.

Nazgh emphasizes harmful incitement or corruption in conduct and relationships.

Ighwāʾ concerns misleading someone or directing them onto a wrong path.

A nazgh may lead to straying, but nazgh itself does not necessarily express the entire process or its outcome.

Nor should attributing these behaviors to Satan be taken to eliminate human responsibility for choosing how to act.

8. Interpretive questions and proposed Forqan renderings

Does nazgh only mean causing disagreement between two people?

In Yūsuf 12:100 and Al-Isrāʾ 17:53, nazgh concerns relationships between people.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:200 and Fuṣṣilat 41:36, it concerns an incitement that may reach one person.

Defining the root solely as “causing disagreement between two people” is therefore too narrow.

That explains the two social uses but does not adequately cover the other two.

A broader, more accurate definition treats nazgh as harmful incitement that can divert someone from right conduct or create corruption and hostility between people.

This covers all four verses without making their subjects identical.

Does nazgh mean Satan has complete control over a person?

The Qur’an attributes nazgh to Satan.

None of the four verses, however, says that people entirely lose their agency and responsibility after nazgh.

Al-Aʿrāf and Fuṣṣilat command the addressee to seek refuge in The God against it.

Al-Isrāʾ commands servants to choose better speech.

Joseph’s brothers also accept responsibility for their past conduct.

Nazgh can thus be an inciting factor, but does not by itself explain every human decision and action.

It should not be translated directly as coercion, lack of agency, or the loss of will.

Is every unwelcome thought or disagreement satanic nazgh?

The verses do not support that conclusion.

Fatigue, fear, error, insufficient information, or conflicting interests may cause hurt and disagreement.

The Qur’an itself uses different roots when discussing human disagreements and decisions.

Every instance of anger, doubt, disagreement, or an unwelcome reaction cannot therefore be declared satanic nazgh without evidence.

All four Qur’anic passages explicitly connect nazgh with Satan. This does not justify assigning every psychological or social occurrence to a single, certain hidden cause.

Nor should the meaning of nazgh be used to diagnose people’s psychological condition or judge their hidden intentions.

What can be examined is their conduct, words, incitement, and consequences for human relationships.

Proposed renderings

Suitable equivalents for N Z GH include “to incite,” “to stir corruption,” “to sow discord,” and, in specific cases, “to whisper temptation.”

None of these alone is sufficient for every Qur’anic construction.

Al-Aʿrāf and Fuṣṣilat highlight incitement directed at a person.

Yūsuf and Al-Isrāʾ more clearly concern corrupting relations between people.

For «نَزْغٌ» alone, “harmful incitement,” or “satanic incitement” in its satanic context, is suitable.

“A whisper from Satan” is also possible in Al-Aʿrāf and Fuṣṣilat, but conceals some of the sense of provoking and inciting.

In Yūsuf and Al-Isrāʾ, “whispered” alone does not adequately convey the social effect: «بَيْنَهُمْ» or «بَيْنِي وَبَيْنَ إِخْوَتِي» concerns relationships between people.

Conversely, “sowed discord” is natural in those two verses, but restricting the root to that sense leaves the incitement of an individual inadequately explained.

Forqan should therefore preserve two principal renderings: “to incite” for nazgh directed at a person, and “to stir discord and corruption” for nazgh between people.

There is no need to introduce complete control, loss of agency, psychological illness, or compulsion to sin into any of these renderings.

9. Conclusion

Arabic N Z GH concerns harmful provocation and incitement.

Physical uses can concern pricking, piercing with a pointed object, or causing sudden movement.

Verbal uses concern taunting, disparagement, and speech that incites people against one another.

Socially, nazgh denotes causing corruption, stirring hostility, and damaging relationships.

A physical prick and verbal or psychological incitement can be related as images; available evidence does not establish one as the certain historical origin of the other.

The root occurs six times in four Qur’anic verses, always directly connected with Satan.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:200 follows commands to forgive, promote good, and turn away from the ignorant, warning against incitement that may divert someone from measured conduct.

In Yūsuf 12:100, satanic nazgh concerns corruption and discord between Joseph and his brothers. This attribution does not remove the brothers’ responsibility.

In Al-Isrāʾ 17:53, nazgh accompanies the command to speak better, highlighting the importance of communication in preventing hostility and corruption.

In Fuṣṣilat 41:36, the warning follows the call to respond to wrongdoing with what is better and persevere in that conduct.

Together, these verses show that nazgh is not confined to overt disagreement between two people. It may first arise as incitement within an individual toward a wrongful reaction.

Nazgh also has semantic connections with waswasah, hamz, and ighwāʾ, but none of these roots is fully synonymous in every use.

Its distinction from nazʿ must also be preserved. Nazʿ concerns drawing, removing, and, in certain constructions, contention or exchange; Qur’anic nazgh concerns satanic incitement and corruption.

Proposed final definition for Forqan

N Z GH denotes harmful provocation and incitement that can lead someone toward wrongful conduct or create discord, hostility, and corruption between people.

Arabic also records pricking, taunting, and inciting through speech in this family. These physical and verbal images should not be made the certain historical origin of all its meanings.

In the Qur’an, «يَنْزَغَنَّكَ» concerns incitement reaching a person, while «نَزَغَ» and «يَنْزَغُ» with «بَيْنَ» concern stirring corruption and discord between people.

The root itself does not mean complete control of the human will, compulsion to sin, or the removal of responsibility.

Thus, “harmful, corrupting incitement” is a defensible common semantic core for Qur’anic N Z GH. Translation and explanation should preserve the distinctions between individual incitement, whispering, and disrupted relationships according to each verse’s construction and context.
