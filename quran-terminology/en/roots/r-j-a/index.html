Postponing, granting respite, deferring a decision, and the distinction from hope

In early Arabic, R J ʾ concerns putting off an action, deferring a decision or consideration of a matter, and leaving it until another time. A matter is available for action or decision, but its handling is deferred for the present.

For example, irjāʾ can describe a person’s case remaining undecided and being left for later consideration. أَرْجَأَ can also apply when someone is provisionally set aside without action being taken concerning them.

The root is close in sound and morphology to R J W, associated with hope. Some Arabic forms omit or replace the hamzah of R J ʾ. Nevertheless, deferring something and hoping it will occur are different meanings, and one should not replace the other without evidence.

In distinguishing hope and the side of something from the hamzah-bearing branch, the following lexical distinction is made:

وَأَمَّا الْمَهْمُوزُ فَإِنَّهُ يَدُلُّ عَلَى التَّأْخِيرِ.

wa-ammā al-mahmūzu fa-innahu yadullu ʿalā al-taʾkhīr

That is: “The form with hamzah, however, indicates postponement.”

This distinction is central to the present study.

The semantic family of irjāʾ appears in four Qur’anic passages: Al-Aʿrāf 7:111, Al-Tawbah 9:106, Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:36, and Al-Aḥzāb 33:51. In Ḥafṣ’s reading, the forms are أَرْجِهْ in two verses, مُرْجَوْنَ, and تُرْجِي. Other recognized readings also have corresponding forms with hamzah.

Some morphological indexes consequently group these forms with R J W. Lexically, however, the postponement branch should be examined separately from hope.

1. Core meaning: postponing and deferring

Irjāʾ: putting off

Arabic uses the expression:

أَرْجَأْتُ الْأَمْرَ

arjaʾtu al-amr

That is: “I postponed the matter.”

The verb describes an action not being performed now, but being left for another time.

A delay can have different reasons. Someone may need more time to investigate, await suitable conditions, or be unwilling to decide.

Irjāʾ itself does not specify the reason for postponement.

For example, a judge might defer consideration of a case until further evidence arrives. Such a delay is not necessarily unjust.

Conversely, someone responsible for a complaint might deliberately delay despite sufficient evidence, seeking to avoid responsibility.

Both involve postponement, but their reasons and moral character differ.

Thus, irjāʾ is not lexically either necessarily right or necessarily wrong. Its subject, reason, and consequences must be assessed from context.

Postponement differs from cancellation

Putting something off does not mean it has been cancelled forever.

A decision may be made later, consideration resumed, or someone provisionally set aside restored to a former position.

Arabic distinguishes أَرْجَأَ, to postpone, from أَبْطَلَ, to invalidate or deprive of effect.

Deferring a decision also differs from determining its outcome. Someone whose case is delayed may ultimately be pardoned or held accountable.

Al-Tawbah 9:106 makes this distinction clear.

Irjāʾ is not identical with waiting

In waiting, someone looks for an event to occur. In irjāʾ, a person or other agent leaves an action or a case’s resolution until the future.

Someone awaiting a response to a request is waiting; someone putting off consideration of the request performs the act of deferral.

The conditions can coexist, but the relationship between the agent and the action differs.

2. Forms and uses in early Arabic

أَرْجَأَ يُرْجِئُ: to postpone

أَرْجَأَ is a Form IV verb.

Its familiar uses concern postponing, granting respite, or leaving something until another time.

Its verbal noun is إِرْجَاء.

It is normally transitive: the matter being deferred is its object.

In أَرْجَأَ الْأَمْرَ, an affair is deferred. In أَرْجَأَهُ, the object can be a person whose case or treatment is postponed.

Some uses are also explained through the closely related sense of granting respite.

Respite, however, does not necessarily mean pardon or disregard for responsibility.

أَرْجَى يُرْجِي: the form without hamzah

أَرْجَيْتُ الْأَمْرَ is also attested for “I postponed the matter.”

Both forms are recorded together in the sense of postponement:

أَرْجَأْتُ الْأَمْرَ وَأَرْجَيْتُهُ إِذَا أَخَّرْتَهُ.

arjaʾtu al-amra wa-arjaytuhu idhā akhkhartahu

That is: “Both أَرْجَأْتُ and أَرْجَيْتُ are used for deferring a matter.”

An explanation of the readings of أَرْجِهْ attributes the hamzah-bearing form to some Qays tribes, and the form without hamzah to Tamīm and Asad.

Grammatical explanations nevertheless do not all agree about the two forms’ morphological origins.

Some treat أَرْجَى as a development of أَرْجَأَ; others consider the possibility of two distinct formations converging in the sense of delay.

Some explanations of forms without hamzah also propose a connection with the root of hope.

A precise study must therefore distinguish two questions: whether both forms mean delay in a particular use, and whether they necessarily share the same historical origin.

The first is clearly supported; the second is not resolved identically in every lexical account.

مُرْجَأ and مُرْجَؤُون: deferred

مُرْجَأ is a Form IV passive participle.

It describes a person or matter whose consideration or handling has been delayed.

Its sound masculine plural can be مُرْجَؤُونَ.

The form without hamzah is مُرْجَوْنَ.

Both occur in the readings of Al-Tawbah 9:106.

The passive participle describes a delay affecting a person or affair, rather than necessarily identifying that person as its agent.

Accordingly, Qur’anic مُرْجَوْنَ in the postponement sense does not mean people who have themselves decided to put something off.

مُرْجِئ: someone who postpones

مُرْجِئ is the active participle of أَرْجَأَ.

Its direct sense is someone who postpones an action or leaves a decision for another time.

مُرْجٍ is also recorded without hamzah.

These forms must remain distinct from the passive participle.

مُرْجِئ performs the deferral; مُرْجَأ is the person or thing whose case is deferred.

Usage concerning approaching birth

Early Arabic records أَرْجَأَتِ النَّاقَةُ for a she-camel approaching the time of giving birth.

أَرْجَأَتِ الْحَامِلُ is likewise recorded for a pregnant woman nearing childbirth.

At first sight, this differs from postponing.

Some explanations connect it with an approaching event and the expectation or hope of birth.

This analysis does not establish a single historical origin for every use.

Approaching birth should therefore be recorded as an attested use, rather than the basic meaning of R J ʾ.

أَرْجَأَ الصَّائِدُ: returning without game

Some lexical accounts record أَرْجَأَ الصَّائِدُ for a hunter who has caught nothing.

The same use is also reported without hamzah as أَرْجَى.

A connection might be imagined between an unsuccessful hunt and continued expectation of obtaining game, but that explanation alone does not establish the usage’s historical origin.

The sense is absent from the Qur’anic passages and should not be transferred to أَرْجِهْ or تُرْجِي.

3. Evidence from early Arabic

أَرْجَأَتْ in Dhū al-Rummah’s poetry

A verse by Dhū al-Rummah reads:

نَتُوجٍ وَلَمْ تُقْرِفْ لِمَا يُمْتَنَى لَهُ
إِذَا أَرْجَأَتْ مَاتَتْ وَحَيَّ سَلِيلُهَا

natūjin wa-lam tuqrif li-mā yumtanā lahu / idhā arjaʾat mātat wa-ḥayya salīluhā

Approximate sense of this version: “An egg bearing a chick, though it has not itself approached a male; when the chick’s emergence draws near, the egg breaks and its offspring lives.” The broken egg explains the image of its death; the alternative readings are considered below.

The image concerns an ostrich egg and a chick emerging, rather than a camel or another animal giving birth directly. In the explanation of the أَرْجَأَتْ version, the word concerns the approaching emergence of the chick; the egg’s “death” depicts its breaking as the chick emerges.

Some accounts end the verse with وَعَاشَ سَلِيلُهَا. More importantly, إِذَا نُتِجَتْ and إِذَا أَنْتَجَتْ are also transmitted instead of إِذَا أَرْجَأَتْ, and one textual correction favors إِذَا نُتِجَتْ. The verse is therefore not an undisputed witness for أَرْجَأَتْ.

The witness shows that, in one transmitted version, hamzah-bearing أَرْجَأَتْ is associated with a chick’s emergence. The variation must nevertheless remain explicit when using it as lexical evidence.

The usage does not mean “an approaching time” in every setting.

In particular, it does not establish that Qur’anic irjāʾ means an event drawing near: postponement fits the contexts of those passages more clearly.

Dhū al-Rummah was an Umayyad poet. The verse is therefore Islamic-period evidence, rather than pre-Islamic poetry.

Deferral in handling affairs and trade

أَرْجَأَ الْأَمْرَ is a known Arabic expression for postponing a matter.

مُرْجًى occurs in accounts of trade for something whose payment or delivery is deferred. Some versions also record the doubled form مُرَجًّى; the difference between the formations must be retained.

For example, a price might be agreed now while payment is left for later.

Here, irjāʾ concerns the timing of an obligation’s performance, rather than whether the transaction itself is valid.

The use shows that deferral could apply to financial, legal, and social matters in Arabic.

The root alone does not determine whether a deferred transaction is permitted or prohibited. That depends on the transaction’s conditions and independent grounds.

Limits of the pre-Qur’anic evidence

Among the witnesses examined, أَرْجَأَ in the sense of postponement is clearly recorded in early lexical accounts and explanations of Qur’anic readings.

However, no independent pre-Islamic verse with sufficiently established wording and attribution was found using this verb for postponement.

Expressions preserved only in lexical accounts should therefore not be presented without evidence as securely dated pre-Islamic poetry.

Despite its lexical value, Dhū al-Rummah’s witness also belongs to the Islamic period.

This limitation does not prevent identifying the postponement sense, but it matters when reconstructing the precise history of the hamzah-bearing and hamzah-less forms.

4. أَرْجِهْ وَأَخَاهُ: deferring action concerning Moses and Aaron

The expression occurs in two Qur’anic passages.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:111 reads:

قَالُوا أَرْجِهْ وَأَخَاهُ وَأَرْسِلْ فِي الْمَدَائِنِ حَاشِرِينَ

qālū arjih wa-akhāhu wa-arsil fī al-madāʾini ḥāshirīn

“They said: Defer dealing with him and his brother, and send gatherers into the cities.”

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:36 likewise reads:

قَالُوا أَرْجِهْ وَأَخَاهُ وَابْعَثْ فِي الْمَدَائِنِ حَاشِرِينَ

qālū arjih wa-akhāhu wa-ibʿath fī al-madāʾini ḥāshirīn

“They said: Defer dealing with him and his brother, and dispatch gatherers into the cities.”

The difference lies in the following verb: أَرْسِلْ in Al-Aʿrāf, and ابْعَثْ in Al-Shuʿarāʾ.

أَرْجِهْ itself has the same sense in both.

The morphology of أَرْجِهْ

In Ḥafṣ’s reading, أَرْجِهْ is a masculine singular imperative with the object pronoun هُ, pronounced here with a still h as هْ.

The verb is related to the hamzah-bearing form أَرْجِئْهُ.

Pharaoh is addressed; the speakers are a group around him.

The pronoun in أَرْجِهْ refers to Moses, while وَأَخَاهُ includes Aaron in the same proposed handling.

Lexically, the verb describes postponing action concerning them.

Why did Pharaoh’s associates seek a delay?

The preceding verses provide the context.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:107 and Al-Aʿrāf 7:108, Moses throws his staff and reveals his hand.

In the following verses, leading members of Pharaoh’s people describe him as a skilled magician.

They then propose deferring the matter of Moses and his brother while sending people to gather magicians from the cities.

The same sequence appears in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:34, Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:35, Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:36, Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:37, and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:38.

Deferral here therefore concerns preparing for another confrontation.

Rather than acting immediately against Moses, Pharaoh’s associates want to assemble magicians to oppose him.

However, أَرْجِهْ does not itself mean inviting magicians. Their dispatch is expressed by أَرْسِلْ and ابْعَثْ.

Does أَرْجِهْ mean imprisoning?

Some interpretive accounts explain أَرْجِهْ through اِحْبِسْهُ: restrain or hold him.

That explanation exists in early interpretation, but should be assessed against the basic postponement sense.

In a political or judicial setting, deferring someone’s case might accompany holding them. The two actions nevertheless are not necessarily identical.

The verse does not explicitly state that Moses and Aaron were imprisoned at this point.

The order to gather magicians and the story’s continuation suit deferral of a decisive confrontation.

Thus, “defer dealing with them” is lexically more precise than “imprison them.”

Detention is an interpretive possibility that should not be inserted into the direct translation without independent evidence.

5. وَآخَرُونَ مُرْجَوْنَ لِأَمْرِ اللَّهِ: a decision not yet announced

Al-Tawbah 9:106

وَآخَرُونَ مُرْجَوْنَ لِأَمْرِ اللَّهِ إِمَّا يُعَذِّبُهُمْ وَإِمَّا يَتُوبُ عَلَيْهِمْ وَاللَّهُ عَلِيمٌ حَكِيمٌ

wa-ākharūna murjawna li-amri allāhi immā yuʿadhdhibuhum wa-immā yatūbu ʿalayhim wa-allāhu ʿalīmun ḥakīm

“And others have their case left for The God’s command: either He punishes them or turns toward them; The God is Knowing, Wise.”

The meaning of مُرْجَوْنَ

مُرْجَوْنَ describes a group whose case has been deferred.

Readings with hamzah have مُرْجَؤُونَ, a Form IV passive participle.

The people’s case has therefore not yet reached its final resolution.

The continuation clarifies this condition:

إِمَّا يُعَذِّبُهُمْ وَإِمَّا يَتُوبُ عَلَيْهِمْ

immā yuʿadhdhibuhum wa-immā yatūbu ʿalayhim

“Either He punishes them or turns toward them.”

These alternatives show that irjāʾ here does not mean certain pardon.

Nor does it mean certain condemnation.

Resolution is deferred, while the final outcome is not announced in this phrase.

Connection with preceding verses

Al-Tawbah 9:102 describes people who acknowledge their sins and have mixed a good deed with another bad one.

Al-Tawbah 9:106 introduces another group whose case is left for The God’s command.

The surah thus distinguishes the group described in Al-Tawbah 9:102 from those whose case is deferred in Al-Tawbah 9:106; these verses are not immediately consecutive.

Historical interpretive accounts connect the latter group with people who remained behind from participation in a confrontation and whose case was deferred.

Al-Tawbah 9:118 also describes three who were left behind, followed by divine acceptance and turning toward them.

This connection can be examined in the surah’s interpretation, but مُرْجَوْنَ itself neither names those three nor recounts their whole story.

Lexically, it describes only the deferral of their case.

Does irjāʾ imply that The God does not know the outcome?

No such meaning follows from the root.

The verse ends with two divine descriptions:

وَاللَّهُ عَلِيمٌ حَكِيمٌ

wa-allāhu ʿalīmun ḥakīm

“And The God is Knowing, Wise.”

Deferring the announcement or execution of a decision does not mean its outcome is unknown.

A matter may remain unclear to the audience while The God knows its reality.

Irjāʾ should therefore not be translated as divine doubt, uncertainty, or ignorance.

Nor should every deferred case be assumed to remain forever unanswered.

6. تُرْجِي مَنْ تَشَاءُ مِنْهُنَّ: deferral in the Prophet’s family relationships

Al-Aḥzāb 33:51

تُرْجِي مَنْ تَشَاءُ مِنْهُنَّ وَتُؤْوِي إِلَيْكَ مَنْ تَشَاءُ وَمَنِ ابْتَغَيْتَ مِمَّنْ عَزَلْتَ فَلَا جُنَاحَ عَلَيْكَ ذَٰلِكَ أَدْنَىٰ أَنْ تَقَرَّ أَعْيُنُهُنَّ وَلَا يَحْزَنَّ وَيَرْضَيْنَ بِمَا آتَيْتَهُنَّ كُلُّهُنَّ وَاللَّهُ يَعْلَمُ مَا فِي قُلُوبِكُمْ وَكَانَ اللَّهُ عَلِيمًا حَلِيمًا

turjī man tashāʾu minhunna wa-tuʾwī ilayka man tashāʾu wa-mani ibtaghayta mimman ʿazalta fa-lā junāḥa ʿalayka dhālika adnā an taqarra aʿyunuhunna wa-lā yaḥzanna wa-yarḍayna bi-mā ātaytahunna kulluhunna wa-allāhu yaʿlamu mā fī qulūbikum wa-kāna allāhu ʿalīman ḥalīmā

“You defer whichever of them you wish and receive near you whichever you wish. There is no blame on you concerning anyone you seek from those you had set aside. That is likelier to comfort their eyes, prevent their grief, and make them all content with what you have given them. The God knows what is in your hearts; The God is Knowing, Forbearing.”

The meaning of تُرْجِي

تُرْجِي is a masculine singular second-person Form IV imperfect verb.

Readings with hamzah have تُرْجِئُ.

The Prophet is the subject, and مَنْ تَشَاءُ مِنْهُنَّ specifies whom he may defer.

The basic meaning remains postponing or provisionally setting aside.

The verse immediately contrasts it with another verb:

وَتُؤْوِي إِلَيْكَ مَنْ تَشَاءُ

wa-tuʾwī ilayka man tashāʾ

“And receive near you whichever you wish.”

تُؤْوِي belongs to ʾ W Y, concerning sheltering, accommodating, or bringing someone close.

The two verbs stand in contrast: deferring or setting aside, and receiving or bringing close.

This contrast clarifies تُرْجِي within the passage.

Study of ʾ W Y

What is being deferred?

Early interpretations offer several explanations of this discretion’s scope.

One reading concerns arranging turns and time spent with the Prophet’s wives: he could defer some turns and receive others near him.

Another reading concerns setting aside or restoring a wife; some accounts interpret the discretion as releasing a wife from marriage or retaining her. These are readings of the verse’s subject, rather than necessary lexical equivalents of تُرْجِي itself.

Explanations also connect the verse with other women whom he might marry.

These details cannot all be derived from تُرْجِي alone.

The verb describes deferral. Which right, turn, or type of relationship is involved must be assessed from the full context in Al-Aḥzāb.

In particular, تُرْجِي should not by itself be equated with “you divorce”: divorce is a separate act, and this verb does not necessarily end the relationship.

وَمَنِ ابْتَغَيْتَ مِمَّنْ عَزَلْتَ

This phrase is particularly important.

It speaks of someone previously set aside who may later be sought again.

Setting aside here therefore does not necessarily mean permanently ending the relationship.

This suits irjāʾ’s core meaning: deferring or setting something aside need not eliminate it forever.

The feature should not, however, be turned into a general ruling for all family relationships. The verse has a specific addressee and scope; extending its rulings requires independent grounds.

The purpose stated in the continuation

The verse continues:

ذَٰلِكَ أَدْنَىٰ أَنْ تَقَرَّ أَعْيُنُهُنَّ وَلَا يَحْزَنَّ وَيَرْضَيْنَ بِمَا آتَيْتَهُنَّ كُلُّهُنَّ

dhālika adnā an taqarra aʿyunuhunna wa-lā yaḥzanna wa-yarḍayna bi-mā ātaytahunna kulluhunna

“That is likelier to comfort their eyes, prevent their grief, and make them all content with what you have given them.”

The phrase connects contentment and freedom from grief with the arrangement of the relationship.

Contentment, ease, and relief from grief are not the lexical meaning of irjāʾ.

They follow from the purpose expressed in the continuation.

A translation of تُرْجِي should retain deferral, leaving the assessment of family rights and responsibilities to a separate interpretation of the verse.

7. Irjāʾ, hope, and later terminology

R J ʾ (irjāʾ) compared with R J W (rajāʾ)

R J W is familiar for hope, expectation, and looking for something to occur.

R J ʾ concerns postponing an action.

Someone may hope for victory: this concerns rajāʾ.

Deferring a decision about holding a confrontation until the following week is irjāʾ.

Hope and postponement can nevertheless occur together. Someone might defer a decision hoping conditions will improve.

Their coexistence does not make the concepts synonymous.

In some readings and Arabic uses, omitting hamzah brings forms of postponement very close to those of R J W.

Some indexes therefore list أَرْجِهْ, تُرْجِي, and مُرْجَوْنَ beside forms involving hope.

However, مَرْجُوّ, something hoped for, differs in structure and meaning from مُرْجَأ, something deferred.

أَرْجَائِهَا in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:17 concerns sides or edges and belongs to the R J W branch of sides and margins, rather than irjāʾ as postponement.

The distinctions prevent superficially similar forms from being confused.

R J ʾ (irjāʾ) compared with R B Ṣ (tarabbuṣ)

Tarabbuṣ concerns waiting and watching for an event.

Irjāʾ concerns deferring an action or resolution.

Al-Tawbah 9:106 calls a group مُرْجَوْنَ: their case has been deferred.

They may also await the result, but مُرْجَوْنَ describes their position in relation to the deferred decision.

A person waiting and someone whose case has been deferred therefore need not occupy the same role.

Study of R B Ṣ

R J ʾ (irjāʾ) compared with ʾ KH R (taʾkhīr)

أَخَّرَ belongs to ʾ KH R. In putting something back or placing it at a later time or position, it is clearly close to أَرْجَأَ.

Irjāʾ is consequently often explained through taʾkhīr, postponement.

However, ʾ KH R has a broader Qur’anic field, including another or later, an ending or final term, and falling behind.

Irjāʾ in the passages examined chiefly concerns deferring a decision, action, or resolution.

The roots can thus be synonymous in some constructions without every derivative being identical.

Study of ʾ KH R

R J ʾ (irjāʾ) compared with N S ʾ (nasʾ)

N S ʾ also concerns postponement in Arabic.

Al-Tawbah 9:37 reads:

إِنَّمَا النَّسِيءُ زِيَادَةٌ فِي الْكُفْرِ

innamā al-nasīʾu ziyādatun fī al-kufr

“Nasīʾ is only an increase in disbelief.”

Nasīʾ here concerns changing or shifting the sacred months.

Irjāʾ and nasīʾ can be close in their connection with delay.

However, nasīʾ in Al-Tawbah 9:37 has a particular historical and calendrical setting; it should not become the general meaning of every delay.

Nor does its association with disbelief establish that every irjāʾ or deferral in the Qur’an is blameworthy or unbelieving.

Study of N S ʾ

R J ʾ (irjāʾ) compared with ʾ J L (ajal)

Ajal concerns a final term or appointed time.

An action may be deferred and assigned a new deadline.

Irjāʾ does not itself mean specifying a particular date.

Al-Tawbah 9:106 describes a deferred case, but neither the word nor the sentence states an exact ending date.

Delay and an appointed term are related, but distinct.

Study of ʾ J L

The emergence of the term مُرْجِئَة

In the history of Islamic thought, مُرْجِئَة became a name for groups and positions differing over faith, action, and judgment of people who committed grave sins.

Lexical explanations connect the name with irjāʾ as postponement.

Some explain it through deferring judgment of persons; others through placing faith first and relegating the standing of action.

These historical explanations must not be equated with the word’s Qur’anic meaning.

مُرْجَوْنَ in Al-Tawbah 9:106 is not the name of a theological school. It describes people whose case is left for The God’s command.

Nor does using irjāʾ to explain a historical position prove that everyone later called Murjiʾah held precisely the same beliefs.

Later sectarian terminology should therefore remain distinct from Qur’anic linguistic usage.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

Two main equivalents suit the root’s central meaning: “postpone” and “leave for another time.”

Where a person or matter is provisionally set aside, “set aside” can also suit the context, provided this is not necessarily understood as permanent.

For أَرْجِهْ in Al-Aʿrāf 7:111 and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:36, “defer dealing with him” is more natural than “imprison him.”

For مُرْجَوْنَ in Al-Tawbah 9:106, “those whose case has been deferred” conveys the meaning precisely. A smoother rendering can say “those whose case is left for The God’s command,” provided the word explanation retains postponement.

For تُرْجِي in Al-Aḥzāb 33:51, “you defer” is the closest direct equivalent. “You set aside” is also defensible in context, while “you divorce” inserts a specific action not necessarily conveyed by the word itself.

Forqan’s brief root meaning can be:

Postponing, putting back, and leaving an action until another time.

The verse-specific meaning should nevertheless be written separately for each form. Deferring action concerning Moses, leaving a group’s case unresolved, and provisionally setting someone aside within a family relationship differ in subject and context.

9. Concluding findings

R J ʾ in early Arabic concerns postponing, granting respite, and leaving a decision or action until another time.

Lexical explanations distinguish the hamzah-bearing form from the hope and side senses of R J W, identifying postponement as its meaning.

Both أَرْجَأَ and أَرْجَى are attested for postponement. Disagreement over their morphological origin, alongside readings with and without hamzah, shows that their sound distinction was not maintained uniformly in early usage.

The semantic family appears in four Qur’anic passages.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:111 and Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:36 propose deferring action concerning Moses and Aaron.

Al-Tawbah 9:106 describes people whose case is left for The God’s command, without the final outcome being announced in the phrase.

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:51, تُرْجِي contrasts with تُؤْوِي and concerns setting aside or deferral within the Prophet’s family relationships.

The subjects differ, but a shared concept remains: immediate action or resolution does not occur, and the matter is left for another time.

Irjāʾ itself is neither pardon nor condemnation, permanent cancellation, nor the waiting of someone whose case is deferred. Its forms without hamzah should also not be equated with rajāʾ as hope merely because they look alike.

A final definition proposed for Forqan:

R J ʾ concerns deferring an action, decision, or treatment of something or someone until another time. Hamzah-bearing أَرْجَأَ and hamzah-less أَرْجَى are both attested in this sense in early Arabic. In the Qur’an, the family describes deferring action concerning Moses and Aaron, leaving a group’s case for The God’s command, and setting aside or deferring within a family relationship. Context determines each delay’s outcome and moral character; the root itself does not.
