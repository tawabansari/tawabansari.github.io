In early Arabic, Q S M has two prominent semantic branches: dividing something and assigning a share to each part or person; and swearing an oath to affirm a statement or strengthen a commitment.

The oath branch also appears in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period. Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā says in his Muʿallaqah:

أَلَا أَبْلِغِ الْأَحْلَافَ عَنِّي رِسَالَةً / وَذُبْيَانَ هَلْ أَقْسَمْتُمْ كُلَّ مُقْسَمِ

Convey a message from me to the allies and to Dhubyān: you have indeed sworn every kind of oath.

In the context of a covenant and an end to tribal hostilities, the poet recalls their oaths. Aqsamtum denotes swearing, while muqsam here names the oath itself. The interrogative wording has an affirmative force in this reading, recalling an undertaking already made. The line is transmitted with variant openings.

Division and the assignment of shares are also clearly recorded in classical Arabic lexicons, and Qur’anic usage establishes their presence in the language of revelation. This study, however, has not established an independently datable pre-Islamic poetic witness for that branch; this is a limit of the evidence presented, not proof that none exists.

Forms examined: قَسَمَ, قَسْم, قِسْم, قِسْمَة, قَسَم, قَسَّمَ, قَاسَمَ, أَقْسَمَ, إِقْسَام, تَقَاسَمَ, اِقْتَسَمَ, اِسْتَقْسَمَ, مَقْسُوم, مُقَسِّم, مُقْسِم, مُقْتَسِم, and قَسَامَة.

Qur’anic forms: قَسَمَ, قِسْمَة, قَسَم, قَاسَمَ, أَقْسَمَ, تَقَاسَمَ, اِسْتَقْسَمَ, مَقْسُوم, مُقَسِّمَات, and مُقْتَسِمِين. The root occurs 33 times in 31 verses. Two occurrences each appear in 43:32 and 24:53.

1. Core meanings and semantic branches

Dividing and assigning shares

When property held in common is divided among several people, a portion is assigned to each. Arabic expresses this action with the verb qasama.

Qasm names the act of dividing; qism is the resulting share or portion; and qismah denotes division, apportionment, or the manner in which shares are assigned.

Maqsūm describes something divided or assigned a share, while muqassim names the one who carries out the division.

Iqtasama l-qawmu l-māl means that the people divided the property among themselves and each took a share.

Division does not necessarily mean equal shares. Something may be divided into equal or unequal portions, and division may be just or unjust. Its fairness depends on the criteria used to assign the shares.

Oaths

Qasam, with a short a after both qāf and sīn, means an oath; aqsama means to swear an oath.

This word family is used when someone swears to affirm a statement or strengthen a commitment.

In the oath branch, qāsamahu means to swear to another person; taqāsama l-qawmu denotes members of a group swearing to one another and entering a mutual undertaking.

Some Arabic lexicographers discuss the relationship between swearing and qasāmah.

Qasāmah is a historical legal term connected with repeated oaths in certain homicide disputes. Lexicographical explanations of its relationship to qasam vary: one derives swearing from qasāmah, while another treats qasāmah as a noun of swearing. Dividing a number of oaths among people does not by itself establish the historical development from division to oath-taking.

A lexical explanation of this kind is therefore insufficient, by itself, to prove the historical origin of the oath sense.

This family also has a branch concerning beauty and proportion in the face. Qasīm al-wajh describes a handsome person, and qasamāt al-wajh denotes facial features or parts. This branch is not used in the Qur’an.

2. Division in the Qur’an: resources, property, and means of living

Dividing livelihoods among people

أَهُم يَقسِمونَ رَحمَتَ رَبِّكَ نَحنُ قَسَمنا بَينَهُم مَعيشَتَهُم فِى الحَيوٰةِ الدُّنيا وَرَفَعنا بَعضَهُم فَوقَ بَعضٍ دَرَجٰتٍ لِيَتَّخِذَ بَعضُهُم بَعضًا سُخرِيًّا وَرَحمَتُ رَبِّكَ خَيرٌ مِمّا يَجمَعونَ

A-hum yaqsimūna raḥmata rabbik? Naḥnu qasamnā baynahum maʿīshatahum fī l-ḥayāti l-dunyā wa-rafaʿnā baʿḍahum fawqa baʿḍin darajātin li-yattakhidha baʿḍuhum baʿḍan sukhriyyā. Wa-raḥmatu rabbika khayrun mimmā yajmaʿūn.

Is it they who divide your Lord’s mercy? We have divided their livelihood among them in the worldly life and raised some of them above others in degrees, so that some may take others into service. Your Lord’s mercy is better than what they gather.

Az-Zukhruf 43:32

The root appears twice in this verse as a form I verb.

The first occurrence challenges human authority to allocate the Lord’s mercy; the second attributes the distribution of livelihoods to The God.

In the preceding verse, 43:31, the opponents ask why the Qur’an was not sent down to a great man from one of the two towns. They treat social standing and worldly means as a criterion of fitness to receive revelation.

Yet wealth, power, and social standing do not give a person the right to determine who receives revelation.

Differences in ability, knowledge, resources, and circumstances make people dependent on one another. A farmer needs a physician, a physician needs a teacher, and a teacher needs those who provide other necessities of life.

This interdependence does not imply the intrinsic superiority of one group over another or legitimize exploitation.

Nor should the divine distribution of livelihoods be read as approval of every existing distribution of wealth, including distributions produced by injustice, corruption, or the usurpation of rights. The Qur’an calls people to uphold justice and give others their due.

Dividing property and attending to those in need

وَإِذا حَضَرَ القِسمَةَ أُولُوا القُربىٰ وَاليَتٰمىٰ وَالمَسٰكينُ فَارزُقوهُم مِنهُ وَقولوا لَهُم قَولًا مَعروفًا

Wa-idhā ḥaḍara l-qismata ulū l-qurbā wa-l-yatāmā wa-l-masākīnu fa-rzuqūhum minhu wa-qūlū lahum qawlan maʿrūfā.

When relatives, orphans, and the needy are present at the division, provide for them from it and speak to them with fitting words.

An-Nisa 4:8

Al-qismah here refers to the division of property left by a deceased person.

Alongside the assignment of inheritance shares, the Qur’an attends to relatives, orphans, and the needy who are present. It commands that they receive something from that property and be addressed appropriately.

Division is not merely an accounting operation here. People retain social responsibilities when dividing property and must not disregard the needs and dignity of others.

Dividing water: assigning turns of access

وَنَبِّئهُم أَنَّ الماءَ قِسمَةٌ بَينَهُم كُلُّ شِربٍ مُحتَضَرٌ

Wa-nabbiʾhum anna l-māʾa qismatun baynahum. Kullu shirbin muḥtaḍar.

Inform them that the water is apportioned among them; each turn of drinking is attended.

Al-Qamar 54:28

In the account of Ṣāliḥ and Thamūd, qismah concerns the allocation of access to water.

This need not mean physically dividing water into containers or equal quantities. Access to a shared resource is assigned by turns.

Verse 26:155 likewise mentions the she-camel’s turn of drinking and the people’s turn on a specified day.

This usage shows that division can concern rights of use as well as the physical substance of property.

A familiar modern example is farmers sharing a water source while each has the right to use it at an assigned time.

An unjust division

تِلكَ إِذًا قِسمَةٌ ضيزىٰ

Tilka idhan qismatun ḍīzā.

That, then, would be an unjust division.

An-Najm 53:22

The preceding verse, 53:21, questions those who assign males to themselves and females to The God.

Ḍīzā describes such a division as unjust and improper.

This is clear evidence that justice is not inherent in the meaning of qismah. Its description as unjust shows that the word itself does not guarantee a fair division.

Qismah and qisṭ should therefore not be treated as wholly interchangeable words.

3. Maqsūm, muqassimāt, and muqtasimīn

Maqsūm: an assigned portion

لَها سَبعَةُ أَبوٰبٍ لِكُلِّ بابٍ مِنهُم جُزءٌ مَقسومٌ

Lahā sabʿatu abwābin li-kulli bābin minhum juzʾun maqsūm.

It has seven gates; for each gate there is an assigned portion of them.

Al-Hijr 15:44

Maqsūm is the passive participle of qasama and modifies juzʾ, a part.

Juzʾ denotes part of a whole, while maqsūm indicates that this part has been determined and assigned.

The verse concerns Hell and portions of those who enter it. The number of gates and each portion’s relationship to a gate are supplied by the whole verse, not by the lexical meaning of maqsūm alone.

Muqassimāt: those distributing the affair

فَالمُقَسِّمٰتِ أَمرًا

Fa-l-muqassimāti amrā.

And by those distributing the affair.

Adh-Dhariyat 51:4

Muqassimāt is a feminine plural active participle of form II. It denotes agents or phenomena that carry out division.

The opening of Sūrat al-Dhāriyāt brings together a series of actions and descriptions: scattering, carrying a load, flowing, and distributing.

The referent of muqassimāt has been interpreted in different ways. One reading understands angels distributing and administering affairs by The God’s command; another relates the expression to winds involved in moving and distributing clouds and rain.

The verse itself does not explicitly identify the distributors.

In either reading, the lexical sense remains distribution and apportionment. Identifying the distributors and explaining how they act is a separate question from defining the root.

Muqtasimīn: those who divide

كَما أَنزَلنا عَلَى المُقتَسِمينَ

Ka-mā anzalnā ʿalā l-muqtasimīn.

Just as We sent down upon those who divided.

Al-Hijr 15:90

الَّذينَ جَعَلُوا القُرءانَ عِضينَ

Alladhīna jaʿalū l-Qurʾāna ʿiḍīn.

Those who made the Qur’an into fragments.

Al-Hijr 15:91

Muqtasimīn is a masculine plural active participle of form VIII.

Iqtisām can mean dividing something among people. Some lexical uses also connect it with mutual undertakings and oaths.

Different views identify these people and their division differently. Some understand people who divided divine scripture, accepting part and denying part; others describe groups who divided tasks among themselves or conspired against Muhammad’s message.

Verse 15:91 is an important contextual guide to 15:90: it identifies the dividers as those who made the Qur’an into fragments.

This fragmentation does not refer to arranging the Qur’an into surahs, verses, or reading sections. The context concerns an approach to the divine message that prevents its acceptance as a whole.

Verse 2:85 also censures accepting part of the Book while denying another part.

Someone who accepts only the portions of a book that suit prior beliefs or interests, setting the rest aside, makes personal desire the criterion for accepting that book.

4. Istiqsām with arrows: seeking one’s share and fate without a sound basis

In 5:3, istiqsām by means of arrows is listed among prohibited practices.

Tastaqsimū is an imperfect form X verb. Istiqsām can denote seeking division or the assignment of a share and, in another use, seeking knowledge of what has been allotted to a person.

Azlām is the plural of zalam, wooden arrows or shafts used in some Arab practices of decision-making, lot-drawing, and divination.

Historical accounts describe people consulting these shafts when deciding about travel, marriage, trade, or other affairs, seeking what they believed to be good, bad, or destined for them.

In this usage, istiqsām means trying to discover one’s allotted share, fate, or the soundness of a decision from the shaft that emerges.

Verse 5:90 also places azlām alongside wine, gambling, and cultic stones, commanding people to avoid them.

Divination with arrows should be distinguished from drawing lots to assign turns or allocate a shared opportunity.

When several people have an equal claim to a resource and draw lots to decide the order of use, the draw serves a practical allocation problem rather than knowledge of the unseen or discovery of The God’s will.

Verse 3:44 mentions casting pens to determine Mary’s guardian, while 37:141 mentions Jonah’s participation in a draw.

The presence of a random element alone is therefore insufficient to identify every drawing of lots with the prohibited practice of divination by arrows. This lexical distinction does not determine the permissibility of every specific modern arrangement.

5. Oaths in human relationships

An oath as a means of establishing trust

وَقاسَمَهُما إِنّى لَكُما لَمِنَ النّٰصِحينَ

Wa-qāsamahumā innī lakumā la-mina l-nāṣiḥīn.

He swore to them both: “I am indeed one of your sincere advisers.”

Al-A'raf 7:21

Here Iblīs swears to Adam and his wife, presenting himself as their sincere adviser.

Qāsamahumā is a past-tense form III verb. Its dual pronoun refers to Adam and his wife.

Despite the form III construction, the verse does not say that Adam and his wife also swore to Iblīs. This Arabic construction can express one person swearing to another.

Its use in the account of Iblīs highlights an essential distinction: affirming that a statement is true is not the same as proving it true.

Someone can swear to a falsehood and exploit people’s trust in oaths to deceive them.

Emphatic oaths and human conduct

وَأَقسَموا بِاللَّهِ جَهدَ أَيمٰنِهِم لَئِن أَمَرتَهُم لَيَخرُجُنَّ قُل لا تُقسِموا طاعَةٌ مَعروفَةٌ إِنَّ اللَّهَ خَبيرٌ بِما تَعمَلونَ

Wa-aqsamū bi-llāhi jahda aymānihim la-in amartahum la-yakhrujunna. Qul lā tuqsimū. Ṭāʿatun maʿrūfah. Inna llāha khabīrun bi-mā taʿmalūn.

They swore by The God their most solemn oaths that, if you commanded them, they would surely go out. Say: “Do not swear; fitting obedience [is required]. The God is fully aware of what you do.”

An-Nur 24:53

The verb of swearing appears twice: first in reporting their oath, then in the command not to swear.

The expression jahda aymānihim emphasizes the solemnity of their oaths. The elliptical phrase ṭāʿatun maʿrūfah admits different readings; the translation follows the demand for fitting obedience, with the supplied predicate marked in brackets.

The verse’s ending shifts attention from the intensity of the oath to human conduct. The God knows what they do.

The same construction appears elsewhere:

In 5:53, people solemnly swear that they stand with the believers. In 6:109, some swear that they will believe if a sign comes to them. In 35:42, some promise that, if a warner comes, they will be better guided than other communities; yet his arrival only increases their aversion.

In 16:38, people solemnly swear that the dead will not be raised.

These verses show that the strength of someone’s conviction or assertion cannot take the place of evidence and truth.

In 7:49, an oath that a group will be excluded from divine mercy is contradicted. In 14:44, wrongdoers are reminded of their earlier oath that they would not pass away. In 30:55, the guilty swear about how long they remained, although their perception is mistaken.

An oath may arise from deliberate deception or the speaker’s own mistaken belief. Neither changes the truth.

Oaths in testimony and adjudication

In 5:106, the Qur’an discusses the oath of two witnesses concerning a bequest when doubt arises.

Verse 5:107 describes two other witnesses replacing them and swearing when wrongdoing by the first witnesses comes to light.

In both verses, fa-yuqsimāni is a dual imperfect form IV verb, denoting two people swearing.

Here the oath belongs to a process intended to safeguard trust, prevent the concealment of testimony, and address a dispute.

The possibility of examining and replacing the first witnesses shows that an oath does not, by itself, prevent a claim from being reviewed or exposed as false.

Taqāsum: mutual oaths

In 27:49, opponents of Ṣāliḥ swear to one another and conspire to harm him and his family.

Taqāsamū is form VI. In this context it expresses mutual swearing and the strengthening of a common undertaking.

Grammatically, the form can be an imperative addressed by members of the group to one another: swear to one another. It has also been construed as a past-tense verb describing their mutual undertaking. These are alternative grammatical analyses, not necessarily different transmitted readings.

The oath here strengthens commitment to a wrongful act.

In 68:17, the owners of a garden swear to harvest it in the morning. The subsequent account shows that they intended to exclude the needy from its produce.

In both accounts, firmness of intention does not make the intention right. An oath cannot legitimize injustice, deprivation, or wrongdoing.

6. Oaths in divine speech

The Qur’an also uses oath constructions to draw attention to phenomena, truths, and matters for reflection.

فَلا أُقسِمُ بِمَوٰقِعِ النُّجومِ

Fa-lā uqsimu bi-mawāqiʿi l-nujūm.

No indeed! I swear by the positions of the stars.

Al-Waqi'ah 56:75

وَإِنَّهُ لَقَسَمٌ لَو تَعلَمونَ عَظيمٌ

Wa-innahu la-qasamun law taʿlamūna ʿaẓīm.

It is indeed a tremendous oath, if only you knew.

Al-Waqi'ah 56:76

In the first verse, uqsimu expresses the act of swearing and bi-mawāqiʿi l-nujūm names what is sworn by. The next verse names it qasam and describes it as tremendous.

The text then speaks of a noble Qur’an in 56:77.

The construction relates what is sworn by to the truth that the oath affirms. It invites attention and reflection on the object of the oath.

The construction lā uqsimu

لا أُقسِمُ بِيَومِ القِيٰمَةِ

Lā uqsimu bi-yawmi l-qiyāmah.

No indeed! I swear by the Day of Resurrection.

Al-Qiyamah 75:1

وَلا أُقسِمُ بِالنَّفسِ اللَّوّامَةِ

Wa-lā uqsimu bi-l-nafsi l-lawwāmah.

And no indeed! I swear by the self-reproaching soul.

Al-Qiyamah 75:2

Several linguistic analyses explain lā in this construction.

One reading takes lā as a rejection of an opposing claim or assumption, followed by the oath: no, that is not so; I swear.

Another takes lā as emphatic within the oath construction, without negating the oath itself.

A further reading retains negation: the truth is so clear, or the object so great, that no oath is needed.

Verse 56:76 is especially relevant: immediately after fa-lā uqsimu, the Qur’an identifies it as a tremendous oath.

This supports an emphatic understanding of the construction. The precise function of lā must nevertheless be considered in the context of each verse.

The construction also occurs in 69:38 concerning what people see, in 70:40 concerning the Lord of the easts and wests, in 81:15 concerning al-khunnas, in 84:16 concerning twilight, and in 90:1 concerning that land.

An oath for those with understanding

هَل فى ذٰلِكَ قَسَمٌ لِذى حِجرٍ

Hal fī dhālika qasamun li-dhī ḥijr.

Is there not in that an oath for one endowed with understanding?

Al-Fajr 89:5

This verse follows the opening oaths of Sūrat al-Fajr.

Dhī ḥijr denotes someone endowed with understanding and discernment.

Here the oath does more than add verbal emphasis. It invites reflection on what is sworn by and its relationship to the subject of the verses.

7. Comparison with related roots

Q S M (qismah) and Q S Ṭ (qisṭ)

Qismah concerns division and the assignment of shares, whereas qisṭ in its justice branch emphasizes giving people their due and acting fairly.

Someone may divide common property among several people but assign shares according to personal preference. Division has occurred, yet justice has not been observed.

The unjust division in 53:22 makes this distinction clear.

Justice in division does not necessarily mean identical numerical shares. People’s rights, needs, or obligations may differ.

Q S M (qismah) and J Z ʾ (juzʾ)

Juzʾ denotes a part of a whole; qismah concerns dividing and assigning the portions.

The distinction is visible in juzʾun maqsūm, an assigned portion, in 15:44.

Something may have parts without those parts having been distributed among people.

Q S M (qismah) and N Ṣ B (naṣīb)

Naṣīb denotes a share or portion belonging to someone.

Qismah places greater emphasis on the act or arrangement of division; naṣīb on the share obtained.

Qism also means a share in Arabic and overlaps with naṣīb in this respect.

Q S M (qasam) and Ḥ L F (ḥalif)

Both qasam and ḥalf are used for swearing an oath.

Many Qur’anic uses of Ḥ L F concern deceptive oaths. This tendency does not establish that ḥalf always means a false oath and qasam always a truthful one.

The Qur’an uses Q S M for Iblīs’s oath in 7:21 and for the oath of those denying resurrection in 16:38.

The truth or falsehood of an oath must therefore be judged from its content and context, not merely from the choice between these roots.

Q S M (iqsām) and Y M N (yamīn)

Yamīn can mean either the right hand or an oath.

In the oath formula used in 24:53, iqsām denotes the act of swearing, while aymān, the plural of yamīn, names the oaths themselves.

Qasam can likewise name the oath itself, as in 56:76.

The two families overlap in the field of oaths, but the division branch of Q S M and the right-hand branch of Y M N remain distinct.

Q S M (qasam) and ʿ H D (ʿahd)

ʿAhd concerns a covenant, commitment, or responsibility undertaken; qasam is an oath affirming a statement or strengthening a commitment.

A person can enter a covenant without swearing, or swear about a past event without creating a future obligation.

An oath may strengthen a covenant, but not every oath is a covenant, nor every covenant an oath.

8. Translation choices for Forqan

In the division branch, render qasama as divided; qismah as division or apportionment; maqsūm as divided or assigned; muqassimāt as those distributing; and muqtasimīn as those who divided.

In the oath branch, render qasam as oath; aqsama as swore; qāsamahumā as swore to them both; and taqāsamū as they swore to one another or swear to one another, according to the grammatical reading.

In 5:3, the construction involving tastaqsimū and azlām may be rendered as seeking an allotted share or fate by means of divining arrows.

Distinguish muqsim, one who swears, from muqassim, one who divides, when vocalizing and analysing the Arabic forms.

Brief root meaning: Dividing, separating portions, and assigning a share to each part or person; in another branch, swearing an oath to affirm speech or strengthen a commitment.

Conclusion

Q S M has two main Qur’anic branches: division and apportionment, and oaths.

The first concerns livelihoods, property, water, assigned portions, and the fragmentation of the divine message. The second appears in human oaths, mutual undertakings, testimony, and divine oaths.

Division does not in itself guarantee justice, just as an oath does not in itself guarantee truthfulness.

Division calls for respecting rights and justice; an oath calls attention to the truth of the statement and faithfulness to the undertaking.

Naming an unjust division does not make it just, and swearing to a falsehood does not turn it into truth.
