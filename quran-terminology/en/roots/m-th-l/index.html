Qur’anic Root

The Qur’anic Root M TH L

Likeness, example, manifestation, and bringing meaning near to human understanding through a visible form

Important Forms from This Root

مَثَل

mathal; an example, sample, prominent description, or intelligible image of a meaning.

مِثْل

mithl; like, similar, comparable, in the level of likeness.

مَثِيل

mathīl; a similar one, counterpart, or equivalent.

أَمْثَال

amthāl; examples, samples, or clarifying forms of meaning.

تَمَثَّلَ

tamaththala; appeared in the form of something; became manifest in a perceivable form.

مَثُلَات

mathulāt; visible punishments or historical examples that remain as warnings.

Central Meaning

The root M TH L means being like, being a sample, likeness, appearing, giving an example, and portraying a meaning in an intelligible form.

This root is not merely “example” in the simple modern sense. It includes likeness, sample, pattern, description, model, representation, and bringing a clear form so that a hidden or difficult meaning becomes nearer to human understanding.

One point must always be preserved: an example is not the truth itself. It is a way toward the truth. It brings meaning near to the mind, but the meaning must not be imprisoned inside the example.

Linguistic Note

The semantic core of M TH L is bringing one thing near to another through likeness, manifestation, or giving a sample.

The human being does not understand every meaning directly. Some realities are too high, hidden, or complex for the mind to grasp without mediation. Language therefore uses examples.

An example builds a bridge between what is known and what is not yet known. It places something familiar before the human being so that through it he may reach something he does not yet understand clearly. In this sense, M TH L is not only comparison; it is an instrument of understanding.

Muthūl and Manifestation

One important layer of this root is muthūl: standing forth, being present, appearing, and coming into view.

In Arabic, one may say that a person “appeared before” someone; that is, he was present, stood, and became visible before him. From this layer we can see that mathal is not merely mental similarity. It is a meaning that becomes present in a form that can be seen, understood, or imagined.

Simply put, a mathal brings a meaning out of hiddenness and places it before the eye of reason.

Material Image

The material image of this root can be seen in a sample or marker. When someone wants to explain something unseen, he brings a sample. When he wants to explain a complex reality, he may bring it near through light, water, tree, house, road, trade, farming, or a scale.

An example is like a mirror. The mirror does not create the truth, but it shows one direction of it. If the human being sees the mirror correctly, he passes from it toward the truth. If he treats the mirror as the truth itself, he stops inside the image.

This is why the Qur’anic use of example is precise. The Qur’an gives examples so meaning can be seen, not so truth becomes small or limited.

Classical Arabic Note

In classical Arabic, the root M TH L is tied to likeness, sample, description, condition, presence, appearance, and proverb. Ancient Arabs used proverbs and examples to transmit experience, wisdom, warning, blame, honor, and insight into human behavior.

An example in Arabic culture was not merely a beautiful sentence. It was a form of social memory. Experiences of tribe, war, travel, trade, loyalty, betrayal, courage, fear, and error could be preserved in examples.

The Qur’an takes this familiar linguistic tool and lifts it from tribal memory into the service of guidance, oneness, moral awakening, and recognition of truth.

Mathal and Mithl

A careful distinction is needed between مَثَل and مِثْل. Mithl points more to likeness: something resembles another thing in a certain direction or stands in the level of similarity with it.

Mathal, however, points more to an example, image, model, prominent description, or representation brought to make a meaning understood. Simply: mithl means like or comparable; mathal means an example or model that shows meaning.

This difference is crucial in theology. When the Qur’an says لَيْسَ كَمِثْلِهِ شَيْءٌ 42:11, it means nothing is like Him; nothing stands in the level of likeness with His essence and reality.

Qur’anic Usage

The Qur’an repeatedly uses mathal. One important expression is:

وَتِلْكَ الْأَمْثَالُ نَضْرِبُهَا لِلنَّاسِ 29:43

This shows that examples are given to bring meaning near to people’s understanding. The Qur’an does not lower truth; it creates a path for the human being to rise beyond ordinary understanding.

So mathal in the Qur’an is not merely a literary device. It is a method of education and awakening. Through example, the Qur’an moves reason, awakens conscience, and forces the human being to distinguish between the visible image and the meaning behind it.

Tamaththul: Appearing in a Perceivable Form

One precise Qur’anic use of this root is tamaththul:

فَأَرْسَلْنَا إِلَيْهَا رُوحَنَا فَتَمَثَّلَ لَهَا بَشَرًا سَوِيًّا 19:17

Here, tamaththul means that a reality beyond ordinary experience became manifest to Mary in a perceivable and receivable form. This does not mean that the essence of the Spirit became an ordinary human being. It means that for human reception, it appeared in human form.

Mathulāt: Visible Punishments and Warnings

Another form of this root in the Qur’an is مَثُلَات:

وَقَدْ خَلَتْ مِن قَبْلِهِمُ الْمَثُلَاتُ 13:6

Mathulāt may be understood as punishments, visible histories, or warning examples: events that do not merely happen, but remain in history as signs.

From this view, history in the Qur’an is not only a report of the past. It is full of examples that stand before the human being so he may take warning.

Example and the Test of Understanding

An example is not always only simplification. Sometimes the example tests the human being. One who approaches the Qur’an with humility and a search for truth passes through the example and sees the meaning. One who approaches with arrogance, mockery, or shallowness may get stuck in the surface of the example and belittle it.

One of the great dangers in understanding M TH L is treating the example as the reality itself. If the Qur’an brings a reality near through light, that reality must not be reduced to physical light alone. If it brings something near through trade, religion must not be reduced to transaction. The example is a window, not a wall.

M TH L and Knowing The God

This root is sensitive in theology. The Qur’an says: لَيْسَ كَمِثْلِهِ شَيْءٌ 42:11. In His essence and reality, The God resembles no created thing. No form, body, mind, imagination, power, king, father, physical light, or being can be like The God.

At the same time, the Qur’an uses words such as light, mercy, dominion, throne, book, path, and other images to bring meaning near to human understanding. These indicate the direction of meaning; they do not make the essence of The God similar to created things.

Within this care, وَلِلَّهِ الْمَثَلُ الْأَعْلَىٰ 16:60 may be understood as: the highest description of perfection belongs to The God, not that The God is limited within created examples.

Difference from Nearby Roots

SH B H is more tied to resemblance and sometimes confusion. S W Y is closer to equality, leveling, and balance. N D D is closer to making a rival, equal, or partner, especially in shirk. Ṣ W R is closer to giving form, shape, and visible image. Āyah is a sign that guides the human being to a reality beyond itself.

M TH L is more tied to sample, likeness, model, intelligible image, and making meaning appear in the form of example.

Example and Shirk

One of the great Qur’anic dangers is that the human being makes a likeness, example, or equal for The God. If the example remains in its proper place, it can be an instrument of understanding. But if the example leaves its place and sits where truth belongs, it approaches shirk.

The deep problem of idolatry was not only that people saw stone or wood. It was that an image, sign, or intermediary was placed where truth belongs and was given authority.

Here M TH L comes near to Ṭ GH Y. When the example crosses its limit and occupies the place of truth, a kind of semantic transgression occurs.

Connection to Life Today

Today the human being thinks through examples and images more than ever: models, charts, films, advertising, media, simulations, political narratives, and educational examples.

These can clarify truth, but they can also distort it. A good example opens the path of understanding. A bad example imprisons the mind inside a false impression.

So the root M TH L is not only a lexical matter. It shows how the human being understands, how he is deceived, and how he can pass from image to truth.

Forqan Translation Suggestions

مَثَل (mathal): example, sample, model, image, representation, intelligible description.

مِثْل (mithl): like, similar, comparable, equal in likeness.

أَمْثَال (amthāl): examples, samples, images of meaning, models of understanding.

ضَرَبَ مَثَلًا: brought an example; struck an image; clarified a meaning through a sample.

تَمَثَّلَ (tamaththala): appeared; became manifest in the form of something.

مَثُلَات (mathulāt): visible punishments, warning histories, historical examples.

لَيْسَ كَمِثْلِهِ شَيْءٌ: nothing is like Him; nothing stands in the level of likeness with Him.

Summary

The root M TH L shows that the human being understands many high meanings through likeness, sample, image, model, and example. An example is a path by which the mind moves from what is familiar toward a deeper reality.

But the example is both a blessing and a test. If the human being passes through the example and seeks truth, the example becomes a lamp. If he stops inside the example or puts it in the place of truth, the example becomes a veil.

In the Qur’an, mathal is not meant to imprison truth. It is meant to bring the human being near to truth. The Qur’an speaks through examples, but asks the human being not to remain inside them: he must see, think, pass through, and come nearer to truth.
