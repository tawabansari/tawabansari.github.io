Shaking, trembling, and agitation

The Arabic root R J J concerns shaking, setting in motion, and causing agitation. The movement may affect a solid object such as a wall, an expanse such as the sea, or a large gathering such as an army. Movement and unrest connect these uses, while their intensity, manner, and result depend on the word and context.

The root occurs in only one Qur’anic verse, in Al-Wāqiʿah, but in two forms within that verse: the verb rujjati and the verbal noun rajjan. Together they describe the earth being shaken in the scene of an immense event.

Qur’anic forms: rujjati and rajjan, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:4.

Other forms, including rajja, irtajja, irtijāj, rajjah, and rajāj, are recorded in Arabic but do not occur in the Qur’an. The related family rajrajah and rajrājah also helps illustrate the material’s early range of uses.

1. Core Meaning and Early Arabic Uses

Shaking and causing agitation

Early Arabic dictionaries illustrate rajj with «رَجَجْتُ الْحَائِطَ رَجًّا»: “I shook the wall.”

This shows that rajj is not confined to earthquakes or the earth’s movement. Someone may shake an object, which may tremble as a result; the consequences vary with its material and condition.

The same family also records «رَجَّهُ فَارْتَجَّ»: “He shook it, and it began trembling or moving in agitation.”

Here, rajja denotes the act of shaking, and irtajja the resulting condition. In other uses, irtajja can simply mean becoming agitated or moving, without a direct cause being expressed.

«اِرْتَجَّ الْبَحْرُ» is also established Arabic: “the sea became turbulent.”

This concerns agitated water rather than a shaking wall. A turbulent sea loses its calm surface as waves move. The root’s core meaning thus extends beyond a particular object, without requiring all movements to have the same form or cause.

Movement within large gatherings

Early Arabic uses katībatun rajrājah for a large, surging army whose many people and equipment form a moving, unsettled whole.

A line attributed to al-Aʿshā states:

وَرَجْرَاجَةٌ تَغْشَى النَّوَاظِرَ فَخْمَةٌ

wa-rajrājatun taghshā al-nawāẓira fakhmah

That is: “And a vast, imposing army filling the gaze.”

Transmissions differ in the word following taghshā, but lexical sources also independently record rajrājah for a large, moving body.

This differs from shaking a single object. An army’s movement arises from its many members and components, appearing to an observer as a surging whole.

Rajrājah belongs to related repetitive, four-consonant formations. The semantic connection does not place every property of a large army, such as numbers or military strength, within rajj itself.

Other Arabic uses

Several other recorded uses help explain the family’s range.

Rajjah: a shake or agitation. Rajjatu al-qawm can denote people’s mixed voices, and rajjatu al-raʿd is recorded for thunder’s sound. Shaking accompanying sound, however, does not make sound a necessary feature of every form of the root.

Irtijāj: trembling or agitation arising in something. Material uses, such as a turbulent sea, are especially clear, but irtajja al-kalāmu is also recorded for speech becoming confused or tangled. It extends perceptible agitation to disorder in expression.

Rajāj and rajājah: some early uses apply rajāj to weak, thin people or livestock, and rajājah to a weak sheep. Some lexical explanations relate this to a frail body’s instability and agitation, but that is not a proven historical origin. Each form’s attested meaning should be recognized independently.

Rijrijah: also used for the muddy, turbid water remaining in a trough. This is an established meaning within the lexical family, but its precise historical route to the core idea of agitation cannot be demonstrated without evidence.

Rajj clearly centers on shaking and agitation, yet related uses should not all be reduced to forceful bodily movement through an invented image.

2. The Root’s Qur’anic Use

All Qur’anic occurrences of the root appear in the fourth verse of Al-Wāqiʿah:

إِذَا رُجَّتِ الْأَرْضُ رَجًّا

idhā rujjati al-arḍu rajjā

“When the earth is shaken violently.”

The verse appears near the beginning of Al-Wāqiʿah, describing an immense event that transforms the world’s familiar order.

The opening verses are Al-Wāqiʿah 56:1, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:2, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:3, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:4, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:5, and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:6:

إِذَا وَقَعَتِ الْوَاقِعَةُ ۝ لَيْسَ لِوَقْعَتِهَا كَاذِبَةٌ ۝ خَافِضَةٌ رَافِعَةٌ ۝ إِذَا رُجَّتِ الْأَرْضُ رَجًّا ۝ وَبُسَّتِ الْجِبَالُ بَسًّا ۝ فَكَانَتْ هَبَاءً مُنْبَثًّا

idhā waqaʿati al-wāqiʿatu / laysa li-waqʿatihā kādhibatun / khāfiḍatun rāfiʿatun / idhā rujjati al-arḍu rajjan / wa-bussati al-jibālu bassan / fa-kānat habāʾan munbaththā

“When the Event occurs; there will be no denying its occurrence. Bringing low, raising high. When the earth is shaken violently, and the mountains are thoroughly crushed, becoming scattered dust.”

Three points in this passage matter for understanding rajj.

First: the earth’s movement, rather than movement in general

Rajj basically means shaking. Here, however, al-arḍu is the grammatical subject of a passive verb and the thing being shaken. The scene concerns the earth, rather than a small object’s movement or localized unrest.

That extent follows from what is shaken and the context, rather than rajj always meaning the whole earth trembling throughout Arabic.

Second: emphasizing the shaking itself

In rujjati al-arḍu rajjan, rujjati is a passive perfect verb, and rajjan its verbal noun functioning as an emphatic cognate accusative.

The construction highlights and emphasizes the act of shaking.

Rajjan is a verbal noun, not an independent verb or separate report of another event. Two occurrences of the root do not therefore mean two separate shakes.

The cognate accusative alone likewise specifies neither the number of tremors, their duration, nor the earth’s precise movement. The event’s great intensity is inferred from the emphatic construction together with the surah’s context.

R J J is also a doubled root: its second and third consonants are identical. The doubled jīm in rujjati and rajjan follows from this structure and should not itself be treated as proof of repetition or extraordinary intensity.

Third: shaking differs from being crushed

After the earth’s shaking, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:5 concerns the mountains:

وَبُسَّتِ الْجِبَالُ بَسًّا

wa-bussati al-jibālu bassā

“And the mountains are thoroughly crushed.”

Then Al-Wāqiʿah 56:6 states:

فَكَانَتْ هَبَاءً مُنْبَثًّا

fa-kānat habāʾan munbaththā

“Becoming scattered dust.”

The semantic sequence matters: rujjati describes the earth’s shaking; bussati concerns the mountains being crushed; and habāʾan munbaththan describes the scattered resulting state.

Translating rajj itself as crushing, annihilation, or turning into dust would merge the verb’s meaning with information conveyed by other verses.

The mountains’ disintegration belongs to the larger scene, but is not rajj’s lexical definition.

3. Comparison with Other Qur’anic Verses

Although R J J occurs in only one verse, other verses also discuss the trembling of earth and mountains. They help clarify context and distinguish roots.

R J J (rajj) and R J F (rajf)

Al-Muzzammil 73:14 states:

يَوْمَ تَرْجُفُ الْأَرْضُ وَالْجِبَالُ وَكَانَتِ الْجِبَالُ كَثِيبًا مَهِيلًا

yawma tarjufu al-arḍu wa-al-jibālu wa-kānati al-jibālu kathīban mahīlā

“The day the earth and mountains tremble, and the mountains become a collapsing heap of sand.”

Tarjufu here comes from R J F, not R J J.

Both roots concern trembling and agitation and describe the immense event of Resurrection. A shared referent does not, however, make all their lexical possibilities identical.

In rujjati al-arḍu, the earth is semantically what is shaken and grammatically the subject of a passive verb. In tarjufu al-arḍu wa-al-jibālu, the earth and mountains are the active verb’s subject and coordinated subject. This clear syntactic difference alone should not be made into two conclusively different kinds of earth movement.

The independent range of R J F must be examined through all its own Qur’anic uses.

R J J (rajj) and Z L Z L (zalzalah)

Study of Z L Z L

Al-Zalzalah 99:1 states:

إِذَا زُلْزِلَتِ الْأَرْضُ زِلْزَالَهَا

idhā zulzilati al-arḍu zilzālahā

“When the earth is shaken with its shaking.”

This resembles idhā rujjati al-arḍu rajjan structurally. Both concern the earth and combine a passive verb with an emphatic verbal noun.

Zalzalah and rajj nevertheless belong to distinct lexical roots. Their proximity in this scene does not restrict every rajj to an earthquake.

“Violent shaking” may give rajj a broader sense closer to its Arabic uses, while “earthquake” is an intelligible contextual rendering in Al-Wāqiʿah because the thing being shaken is the earth.

R J J (rajj) and B S S (bass)

Study of B S S

These roots appear beside each other in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:4 and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:5.

Rajj means setting in motion and agitation, while bass here concerns crushing and dispersing mountains.

The adjacent verbs create a continuous scene, but their meanings should not be transferred between them. Shaking itself is not crushing, nor does every rajj necessarily involve disintegration.

4. Lexical Meaning and the Description of Resurrection

The Al-Wāqiʿah verse describes an immense future scene: the earth is violently shaken, and the mountains enter a different condition.

What the verse states must be distinguished from details a reader might imagine to explain it.

Rajj alone cannot establish horizontal or vertical movement, its frequency, duration, or geological mechanism.

Nor do lexical uses concerning thunder and mixed human voices prove that rujjati al-arḍu describes a sound of a particular quality.

Conversely, the verse’s absence of those details does not authorize stripping the earth’s trembling of its material meaning and treating it solely as psychological, social, or political disturbance.

It names the earth and then the mountains. Its direct, natural sense concerning earth and mountains should therefore be preserved. Any figurative reading or conceptual extension requires independent support and cannot replace that basic sense.

5. Translation Proposals for Forqan

Translation should distinguish the word’s basic meaning from its particular use in the verse.

Rajja: shook; caused to tremble.

Irtajja: began trembling; became agitated or turbulent.

Rajj: shaking, jolting, setting in motion.

Rujjati: was shaken; was made to tremble.

Rajjan: a shaking or jolting. In the verse’s emphatic construction, “violently” or “with a violent shaking” fits the context.

The proposed rendering of Al-Wāqiʿah 56:4 is:

إِذَا رُجَّتِ الْأَرْضُ رَجًّا

idhā rujjati al-arḍu rajjā

“When the earth is shaken violently.”

This naturally preserves the passive verb, physical shaking, and emphasis without adding the subsequent verses’ consequences.

6. Conclusion

R J J primarily concerns shaking and causing agitation or movement. Arabic uses range from shaking a wall to the sea’s turbulence. The related lexical family also includes broader descriptions of large bodies in motion, confused speech, and certain conditions of weakness or instability, each of which should be understood in its own setting.

The Qur’an uses the root only in idhā rujjati al-arḍu rajjan. The passive verb and emphatic verbal noun highlight the earth’s shaking in the scene of the Event.

Rajj should not be identified with the mountains’ crushing or dispersal, a possible sound, or the event’s physical mechanism. Where the verses mention such matters, they are understood from other parts of the text.

Final definition: R J J concerns shaking, causing to tremble, and the arising of agitation and movement. In its Qur’anic use, the earth is violently shaken when the Event occurs, but the root alone does not establish the shaking’s precise nature or consequences.
