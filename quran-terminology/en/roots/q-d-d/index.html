The Qur’anic Root Q D D

Splitting lengthwise, tearing, and the emergence of separate pieces and paths

In early Arabic, Q D D concerns cutting or splitting something, particularly along its length. Cutting hide into narrow strips, tearing cloth, and slicing meat into long pieces are established usages.

This semantic field is clearly recorded in classical Arabic sources, and the Qur’anic occurrences attest it in the period of revelation. Lexical registration alone does not establish a precise pre-Islamic date; no independently dated pre-Islamic witness is used as the basis of this discussion.

Labīd ibn Rabīʿah, a poet who lived through both the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods, says in an elegy for his brother Arbad:

لَمْ يُبْلِغِ الْعَيْنَ كُلَّ نَهْمَتِهَا
لَيْلَةَ تُمْسِي الْجِيَادُ كَالْقِدَدِ

Lam yublighi l-ʿayna kulla nahmatihā / laylata tumsī l-jiyādu ka-l-qidadi.

On a night when swift horses become like leather strips, the eye had still not fulfilled all its desire to weep.

Qidad here denotes strips cut from hide. The poet compares horses worn thin by travel and exertion to those strips. The transmitted historical account places this elegy after the emergence of Islam; it is therefore not treated here as an independently dated pre-Islamic witness.

Forms examined: qadda, yaquddu, qadd, qudda, qaddada, inqadda, taqaddada, maqdūd, qidd, qiddah, qidad, and qadīd.

Qur’anic forms: the verb qadda in qaddat and qudda, and the adjective qidad. Qaddat occurs once, qudda three times, and qidad once: five occurrences in total.

1. Core meaning and physical illustration

When a piece of hide or cloth is cut or split lengthwise, Arabic describes the action with qadda.

Qadd names the act of cutting or splitting. Qiddah denotes a piece cut and separated from something; its plural is qidad.

Qidd also denotes a leather thong cut from hide, especially untanned hide. Such strips were used to bind things, make sandal straps, and serve other purposes.

Qadīd denotes meat cut into long pieces and dried for preservation.

Qadd, meaning stature or bodily proportions, is also familiar in Arabic and Persian. Ḥasanu l-qadd describes a person’s well-proportioned build.

Another usage can be understood through cutting and the separation of pieces: describing groups with different paths, inclinations, and beliefs. This semantic connection does not by itself establish the historical sequence of these usages.

This usage occurs in Al-Jinn.

2. Joseph’s torn shirt: physical evidence

وَاسْتَبَقَا الْبَابَ وَقَدَّتْ قَمِيصَهُ مِنْ دُبُرٍ وَأَلْفَيَا سَيِّدَهَا لَدَا الْبَابِ ۚ قَالَتْ مَا جَزَاءُ مَنْ أَرَادَ بِأَهْلِكَ سُوءًا إِلَّا أَنْ يُسْجَنَ أَوْ عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ

Wa-stabaqā l-bāba wa-qaddat qamīṣahu min duburin wa-alfayā sayyidahā ladā l-bāb. Qālat mā jazāʾu man arāda bi-ahlika sūʾan illā an yusjana aw ʿadhābun alīm.

And they both raced toward the door, and she tore his shirt from behind, and they found her master at the door. She said, “What should be the recompense of someone who intended evil against your household, except imprisonment or a painful punishment?”

Yusuf 12:25

Qaddat is a third-person feminine singular active perfect verb. The woman is its subject, and Joseph’s shirt its object.

Q D D expresses the tearing of the shirt; min dubur specifies that the tear was at the back.

This distinction matters because a torn shirt alone would not establish which person’s claim was true. The location of the tear and its relationship to their movements became decisive in this incident.

قَالَ هِيَ رَاوَدَتْنِي عَنْ نَفْسِي ۚ وَشَهِدَ شَاهِدٌ مِنْ أَهْلِهَا إِنْ كَانَ قَمِيصُهُ قُدَّ مِنْ قُبُلٍ فَصَدَقَتْ وَهُوَ مِنَ الْكَاذِبِينَ

Qāla hiya rāwadatnī ʿan nafsī, wa-shahida shāhidun min ahlihā: in kāna qamīṣuhu qudda min qubulin fa-ṣadaqat wa-huwa mina l-kādhibīn.

He said, “She sought to seduce me.” And a witness from her household testified: “If his shirt was torn from the front, she has spoken the truth, and he is among the liars.

Yusuf 12:26

وَإِنْ كَانَ قَمِيصُهُ قُدَّ مِنْ دُبُرٍ فَكَذَبَتْ وَهُوَ مِنَ الصَّادِقِينَ

Wa-in kāna qamīṣuhu qudda min duburin fa-kadhabat wa-huwa mina l-ṣādiqīn.

But if his shirt was torn from behind, she has lied, and he is among the truthful.”

Yusuf 12:27

In these two verses, qudda is passive. The focus shifts from the person who tore the shirt to the shirt itself and its condition.

The witness presents two possibilities. A tear at the front supports one account; a tear at the back supports the other.

The next verse gives the result of examining this physical evidence:

فَلَمَّا رَأَىٰ قَمِيصَهُ قُدَّ مِنْ دُبُرٍ قَالَ إِنَّهُ مِنْ كَيْدِكُنَّ ۖ إِنَّ كَيْدَكُنَّ عَظِيمٌ

Fa-lammā raʾā qamīṣahu qudda min duburin qāla innahu min kaydikunna, inna kaydakunna ʿaẓīm.

Then, when he saw his shirt torn from behind, he said, “This is from your scheming; indeed, your scheming is great.”

Yusuf 12:28

The tear was a physical trace of what happened before the two reached the door. Examining that trace helped clarify the truth.

This part of Joseph’s story illustrates the importance of observation, reasoning, and examining evidence when judging claims. Where two people make conflicting claims, available evidence can help distinguish truth from falsehood.

The force of this reasoning comes from the combined circumstances of that incident, not from a universal rule about the location of clothing tears in every event.

3. Ṭarāʾiqa qidadā: differing paths and inclinations

وَأَنَّا مِنَّا الصَّالِحُونَ وَمِنَّا دُونَ ذَٰلِكَ ۖ كُنَّا طَرَائِقَ قِدَدًا

Wa-annā minnā l-ṣāliḥūna wa-minnā dūna dhālika, kunnā ṭarāʾiqa qidadā.

And among us are the righteous, and among us are those below that; we have followed diverse paths.

Al-Jinn 72:11

Qidadan is the broken plural of qiddah, on the pattern fiʿal. It functions here as an adjective qualifying ṭarāʾiq.

Ṭarīqah denotes a path, method, or way a person follows. Qidad expresses the distinction and separation of these paths.

Just as separate strips can be cut from one piece of hide, members of a community can have different paths, ideas, and inclinations.

This illustration explains a semantic relationship within the root. The verse does not necessarily describe a community that first held one belief and later split into groups. It states that different paths and inclinations exist among the jinn.

The speakers do not assign one moral condition to everyone in their community. Some are righteous, while others fall below that level.

A later verse makes this difference clearer:

وَأَنَّا مِنَّا الْمُسْلِمُونَ وَمِنَّا الْقَاسِطُونَ ۖ فَمَنْ أَسْلَمَ فَأُولَٰئِكَ تَحَرَّوْا رَشَدًا

Wa-annā minnā l-muslimūna wa-minnā l-qāsiṭūna, fa-man aslama fa-ulāʾika taḥarraw rashadā.

And among us are those who submit, and among us are the unjust. Whoever submits—those are the ones who have sought right guidance.

Al-Jinn 72:14

The Qur’an does not portray the jinn as a community with identical conduct, beliefs, and moral destinies. Just as humans include righteous and unrighteous individuals, the jinn display moral and religious differences.

Ṭarāʾiqa qidadā expresses this diversity and distinction of paths. Each path’s value depends on its substance, not merely on being different from others.

4. Distinguishing qadda, qaddada, and the particle qad

Q D D is a doubled Arabic root: its second and third consonants are identical.

Qadda is a basic form I verb in which the two dāl consonants merge. The shaddah in qaddat and qudda therefore does not indicate form II.

The form II verb is qaddada, which can express intensity, repetition, or cutting something into pieces.

The shaddah in qaddat alone does not establish that the woman tore Joseph’s shirt repeatedly or reduced it to multiple pieces.

The particle qad, used before perfect and imperfect verbs in the Qur’an, is also distinct from qadda and the other derivatives of Q D D.

Qad is a grammatical particle whose function depends on the verb and sentence structure. The meaning of cutting or splitting should not be imposed on it.

5. Comparison with related roots

Q D D (qadd) and Q Ṭ ʿ (qaṭʿ)

Qaṭʿ has a broader semantic range, concerning cutting, separating, or severing a connection. It can refer to a physical object, family bond, ongoing activity, or relationship between people.

Material qadd particularly concerns splitting or cutting lengthwise.

The same sūrah uses Q Ṭ ʿ for the women cutting their hands, in Yūsuf 12:31 and Yūsuf 12:50, but Q D D for the tearing of Joseph’s shirt.

Tearing a shirt does not necessarily mean detaching a piece completely. Qadd can create a split in cloth without severing every connection between its parts.

Q D D (qadd) and M Z Q (tamzīq)

Tamzīq concerns tearing apart, disintegrating, and scattering something’s components.

In Sabaʾ 34:7 it concerns the disintegration of the human body after death; in Sabaʾ 34:19 it concerns a community’s dispersal.

Qadd foregrounds splitting or cutting, while tamzīq emphasizes disintegration and the dispersal of components.

Q D D (qadd) and SH Q Q (shaqq)

Shaqq concerns splitting something or opening a fissure in it. The Qur’an uses this root for the earth, sky, stone, and other phenomena.

Material qadd overlaps with shaqq, but lengthwise cutting, especially of hide and cloth, is a prominent characteristic in Arabic usage.

Q D D (qidad) and F R Q (farq)

F R Q concerns separating and distinguishing things, people, groups, and concepts.

Firqah denotes a group within a community, and farīq a group of people. Qidad can likewise describe different groups and paths.

In ṭarāʾiqa qidadā, however, the emphasis falls on the diversity and separation of paths and inclinations.

This distinction does not exclude overlap. Both word families can express social distinctions and separation among groups.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Qaddat in Yūsuf 12:25 can be rendered “she tore,” and qudda in Yūsuf 12:26, Yūsuf 12:27, and Yūsuf 12:28 as “was torn” or “torn,” according to the sentence.

In Al-Jinn 72:11, ṭarāʾiqa qidadā can be rendered “diverse paths and inclinations” or “different, separate ways.”

Brief root meaning: Cutting or splitting something, especially lengthwise; pieces separated from something; and, by extension, different and distinct paths and groups.

Conclusion

Q D D concerns cutting, splitting, and the emergence of separate parts.

The Qur’an uses it in two semantic settings: the physical tearing of Joseph’s shirt and the existence of different paths and inclinations among the jinn.

The first concerns a split in a physical object; the second, distinctions among paths and inclinations.

Qadd is semantically close to qaṭʿ, shaqq, and farq. In physical usage it emphasizes lengthwise splitting; qidad emphasizes separate parts and paths.
