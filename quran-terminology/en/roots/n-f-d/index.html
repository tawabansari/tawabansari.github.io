Coming to an end, running out, and exhausting a supply or capacity

The Arabic root N F D denotes something coming to an end or being exhausted, particularly when a limited supply, quantity, or resource is depleted through consumption, time, or use until none remains. Provisions, well water, and wealth running out are familiar examples.

The Qur’an uses this root to express a significant contrast: what people possess runs out, while what is with The God remains; a sea imagined as writing ink could be exhausted, but the Lord’s words cannot; and the provision described for the people of the Garden does not run out.

In these uses, nafād is not confined to an object’s physical destruction. It can denote a supply running out, a resource reaching its end, or the exhaustion of something whose continuation or use is at issue. This breadth should not become the vague definition “any change or ending.” The important element in the Qur’anic uses is that the subject reaches its end and no longer remains.

1. Qur’anic forms and the semantic core

The root N F D occurs five times in four Qur’anic verses, in two principal structures: four Form I verbs and one verbal noun.

The Qur’anic forms are:

  • يَنْفَدُ: runs out, comes to an end; Al-Naḥl 16:96.
  • لَنَفِدَ: would certainly run out; Al-Kahf 18:109.
  • تَنْفَدَ: be exhausted; Al-Kahf 18:109.
  • نَفِدَتْ: came to an end; in the verse’s negated hypothetical construction, “would not be exhausted”; Luqmān 31:27.
  • نَفَاد: exhaustion, coming to an end, running out; Ṣād 38:54.

Other Arabic forms include أَنْفَدَ, اسْتَنْفَدَ, نَافِد, and مُنَافَدَة, but these do not occur in the Qur’an.

The basic meaning

The basic verb is نَفِدَ يَنْفَدُ (nafida yanfadu). Nafād and nafad are both recorded as its verbal nouns.

Arabic dictionaries explain it with words such as فَنِيَ, ذَهَبَ, and اِنْقَطَعَ: something ended, disappeared, or ceased to continue.

Lexical explanations identify the root’s central sense as something ending or passing away, and place nafād in the semantic field of fanāʾ. The Qur’anic uses are likewise explained through this sense of coming to an end.

This proximity does not make nafād, fanāʾ, and inqiṭāʿ completely identical. In different constructions, each may emphasize a particular aspect of ending or disappearance.

2. Uses in early Arabic

In Arabic, نَفِدَ الشَّيْءُ is said when something has ended or its supply has run out.

Examples recorded in dictionaries clarify the root’s range.

Provisions and wealth running out: أَنْفَدَ القَوْمُ means that a people’s provisions or wealth have been exhausted. نَفِدَ زَادُهُمْ likewise means that their provisions ran out.

Well water running out: أَنْفَدَتِ الرَّكِيَّةُ describes a well whose water is exhausted. This example matters because it shows a usable resource reaching its end.

Exhausting what one has: اِسْتَنْفَدَ مَا عِنْدَهُ means to use up what one possesses. اِسْتَنْفَدَ وُسْعَهُ likewise denotes expending all one’s ability or capacity.

These examples concern a supply or capacity that may be consumed gradually until nothing remains. Gradual depletion is not an inherent requirement of the root: nafād expresses reaching the end, rather than the speed or manner of reaching it.

A poetic witness and use in disputation

Ibn Harmah, a poet of the Umayyad and Abbasid periods, says in describing a generous person:

أَغَرُّ كَمِثْلِ الْبَدْرِ يَسْتَمْطِرُ النَّدَى
وَيَهْتَزُّ مُرْتَاحًا إِذَا هُوَ أَنْفَدَا

agharru ka-mithli al-badri yastamṭiru al-nadā / wa-yahtazzu murtāḥan idhā huwa anfadā

“Radiant like the full moon, he seeks generosity as rain; and when his means run out, he stirs with a willing heart.”

The description compares the person to the full moon and speaks of generosity and readiness to give, even when his available means have run out.

Anfadā here concerns the exhaustion of one’s possessions, illustrating the root’s connection with wealth and giving. Ibn Harmah lived in the second Islamic century, however, so the line is evidence from literary Arabic after the Qur’an; it cannot be presented as pre-Islamic evidence.

In another use, مُنَافَدَة concerns mutual disputation and argument. A خَصْمٌ مُنَافِدٌ is an opponent who seeks, through debate, to exhaust the other party’s argument and capacity to reason.

This shows that exhaustion is not confined to water, food, or wealth. Arguments, ability, and the capacity to reason can also be involved.

Some early transmissions also record expressions such as نَفَدَنِي بَصَرُهُ, meaning that someone’s gaze reached and passed beyond a person. This limited use stands close to N F DH in lexical discussion and cautions against turning the root’s dominant meaning into an absolute rule for every historical form. Nevertheless, all five Qur’anic uses of N F D express coming to an end or being exhausted.

3. Qur’anic uses: exhaustion contrasted with remaining

A. Al-Naḥl 16:96: what you possess runs out

مَا عِنْدَكُمْ يَنْفَدُ وَمَا عِنْدَ اللَّهِ بَاقٍ وَلَنَجْزِيَنَّ الَّذِينَ صَبَرُوا أَجْرَهُمْ بِأَحْسَنِ مَا كَانُوا يَعْمَلُونَ

mā ʿindakum yanfadu wa-mā ʿinda allāhi bāqin wa-la-najziyanna alladhīna ṣabarū ajrahum bi-aḥsani mā kānū yaʿmalūna

Translation: What you possess runs out, and what is with The God remains; and We will certainly reward those who persevered according to the best of what they used to do.

Yanfadu is an imperfect verb here, referring to what people possess being subject to exhaustion.

It stands opposite bāqin, from B Q Y. The contrast is clear: one comes to an end, while the other remains.

The preceding verse, Al-Naḥl 16:95, warns against selling The God’s covenant for a small price. The exhaustion of human possessions is therefore part of an evaluation: will a person abandon a covenant and responsibility for a limited, passing benefit?

The subject is not merely money or food running out. “What you possess” may include various limited benefits and resources in life. These instances should not, however, be presented as the lexical definition of yanfadu.

Nafād here expresses being subject to exhaustion. Which possession, opportunity, or benefit is exhaustible is understood from مَا عِنْدَكُمْ and the context.

Nor does the exhaustibility of human possessions mean that every worldly benefit is worthless. The verse concerns preferring a limited gain over a covenant and lasting reward; it does not prohibit using life’s resources.

B. Al-Kahf 18:109: the sea runs out before the Lord’s words do

قُلْ لَوْ كَانَ الْبَحْرُ مِدَادًا لِكَلِمَاتِ رَبِّي لَنَفِدَ الْبَحْرُ قَبْلَ أَنْ تَنْفَدَ كَلِمَاتُ رَبِّي وَلَوْ جِئْنَا بِمِثْلِهِ مَدَدًا

qul law kāna al-baḥru midādan li-kalimāti rabbī la-nafida al-baḥru qabla an tanfada kalimātu rabbī wa-law jiʾnā bi-mithlihi madadan

Translation: Say: If the sea were ink for my Lord’s words, the sea would run out before my Lord’s words were exhausted, even if We brought another like it as replenishment.

The same root occurs twice in this verse:

لَنَفِدَ الْبَحْرُ: the sea would certainly run out.

قَبْلَ أَنْ تَنْفَدَ كَلِمَاتُ رَبِّي: before my Lord’s words were exhausted.

The difference lies in what the verb concerns. However vast, the sea is imagined as a limited resource. If all of it were used to write the Lord’s words, it would eventually be exhausted.

The Lord’s words are not described as subject to the same limitation.

وَلَوْ جِئْنَا بِمِثْلِهِ مَدَدًا expands the picture: adding another sea like the first would still not remove the material resource’s exhaustibility.

Nafād should not be equated with writing here. Writing is suggested by midādan and its relationship with kalimāt. Nafād itself denotes only the resource’s exhaustion.

Nor should كَلِمَاتُ رَبِّي be confined without evidence to written sentences in a book. The Qur’an speaks of The God’s words in various contexts. Establishing the full scope of this concept requires a separate study of kalimah and kalimāt.

The image should not be turned into a physical calculation of ink volume or a countable number of divine words. It uses an immense but exhaustible resource to express the inexhaustible breadth of the Lord’s words.

Grammatically, the lām in لَنَفِدَ belongs to the answer to law and affirms that result; it is not the negative particle lan. The phrase therefore does not mean “the sea would not run out.” It affirms that the sea would run out under the stated supposition.

C. Luqmān 31:27: all trees as pens, and seven seas

وَلَوْ أَنَّمَا فِي الْأَرْضِ مِنْ شَجَرَةٍ أَقْلَامٌ وَالْبَحْرُ يَمُدُّهُ مِنْ بَعْدِهِ سَبْعَةُ أَبْحُرٍ مَا نَفِدَتْ كَلِمَاتُ اللَّهِ إِنَّ اللَّهَ عَزِيزٌ حَكِيمٌ

wa-law anna-mā fī al-arḍi min shajaratin aqlāmun wa-al-baḥru yamudduhu min baʿdihi sabʿatu abḥurin mā nafidat kalimātu allāhi inna allāha ʿazīzun ḥakīmun

Translation: If every tree on earth were a pen, and the sea were replenished afterward by seven more seas, The God’s words would not be exhausted. The God is indeed Invincible, Wise.

This verse is closely related in meaning to Al-Kahf 18:109, but expands the image.

The sea alone is not the whole picture: every tree on earth is imagined as a pen, and seven further seas replenish the first.

Despite this expansion, the result is the same: مَا نَفِدَتْ كَلِمَاتُ اللَّهِ.

Nafidat is a perfect verb negated by mā within the hypothetical construction wa-law. The sentence therefore speaks of The God’s words not being exhausted even under that supposition.

The verse explicitly mentions seven. That number must remain in translation. Replacing it with “infinitely many seas” or “any number of seas” would not directly translate the text.

Seven also helps convey the breadth of the imagined writing resources, but the actual numerical expression should not be omitted.

The preceding verse, Luqmān 31:26, speaks of The God’s ownership of what is in the heavens and earth and of His self-sufficiency. The following verse, Luqmān 31:28, considers human creation and resurrection in relation to His power.

The image thus belongs to a network of verses about The God’s ability, knowledge, wisdom, and self-sufficiency. The root N F D itself does not mean knowledge, wisdom, or power; it expresses whether the subject is exhausted or remains inexhaustible.

D. Ṣād 38:54: provision that does not run out

إِنَّ هَٰذَا لَرِزْقُنَا مَا لَهُ مِنْ نَفَادٍ

inna hādhā la-rizqunā mā lahu min nafādin

Translation: This is indeed Our provision; it has no exhaustion.

The verbal noun nafād occurs here rather than the verb nafida.

مَا لَهُ مِنْ نَفَادٍ means that there is no coming to an end or running out of it.

In this negative construction, min emphasizes the comprehensiveness of the negation. The verse does not merely call this provision abundant; it denies that it runs out.

The difference matters. Something may be very abundant yet eventually be exhausted. By contrast, the provision described as مَا لَهُ مِنْ نَفَادٍ is not presented as exhaustible.

The preceding verses, Ṣād 38:49, Ṣād 38:50, Ṣād 38:51, Ṣād 38:52, and Ṣād 38:53, speak of the good destination of the mindful, Gardens of Eden, fruit, drinks, and the promise of the Day of Reckoning.

Rizqunā therefore occurs in the context of blessings in the hereafter.

Nafād alone, however, does not determine this provision’s bodily qualities or how it continues. The verse reports that it does not run out; the details of how cannot be extracted from the root.

This use also shows that nafād is not necessarily limited to an act of consumption. The verbal noun may express the state of coming to an end. Negating that state conveys continuation and inexhaustibility.

4. Distinctions from related roots

N F D (nafida) and N F DH (nafadha): running out and passing through

These roots can easily be confused because of their similarity in writing and sound, particularly in Persian.

N F D, with dāl, concerns running out and coming to an end in its principal Qur’anic uses.

N F DH, with dhāl, concerns passing, crossing, finding a way through, or penetrating something.

Al-Raḥmān 55:33 says:

إِنِ اسْتَطَعْتُمْ أَنْ تَنْفُذُوا مِنْ أَقْطَارِ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ فَانْفُذُوا

ini istaṭaʿtum an tanfudhū min aqṭāri al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍi fa-unfudhū

Translation: If you can pass beyond the regions of the heavens and earth, then pass beyond them.

Tanfudhū and fa-unfudhū belong to N F DH, not N F D. They mean passing or crossing, not being exhausted.

Similarly, in ordinary Arabic, نَفَذَ السَّهْمُ describes an arrow penetrating or passing through something, whereas نَفِدَ الزَّادُ describes provisions running out.

The main distinction is clear: one focuses on passage, the other on reaching an end.

As the lexical witnesses showed, some early uses overlap. These limited instances do not justify merging the roots in the Qur’an.

Study of N F DH

N F D (nafida) and F N Y (faniya): exhaustion of a supply and passing away

Fanāʾ and nafād are very close in meaning; dictionaries sometimes explain one through the other.

In clear examples of nafād, however, the subject is usually considered in terms of its supply, quantity, or capacity reaching an end.

Well water, provisions, wealth, and ink can all run out.

Fanāʾ, in its wider use, can refer to something or someone passing out of existence.

Al-Raḥmān 55:26 says:

كُلُّ مَنْ عَلَيْهَا فَانٍ

kullu man ʿalayhā fānin

Translation: Everyone upon it is passing away.

Fānin concerns beings here, rather than their supplies running out.

This comparison identifies a tendency, not an absolute boundary without exceptions. In some Arabic contexts, nafād and fanāʾ can mean almost the same thing.

N F D (nafida) and N Q Ṣ (naqaṣa): running out and diminishing

Naqṣ concerns a reduction in the quantity, quality, or part of something.

Nafād, in its dominant use, concerns reaching the end.

If a well’s water decreases, some water may still remain. When it is exhausted, one can speak of its nafād.

Exhaustion may thus involve a decrease in quantity, but not every decrease has reached exhaustion.

The distinction matters in translation. Yanfadu in Al-Naḥl 16:96 does not merely mean “decreases”; it means “comes to an end.”

Study of N Q Ṣ

N F D (nafida) and B Q Y (baqiya): exhaustion and remaining

The two roots stand in direct contrast in Al-Naḥl 16:96:

مَا عِنْدَكُمْ يَنْفَدُ وَمَا عِنْدَ اللَّهِ بَاقٍ

mā ʿindakum yanfadu wa-mā ʿinda allāhi bāqin

“What you possess runs out, and what is with The God remains.”

Yanfadu and bāqin form the two sides of the contrast.

Nafād concerns coming to an end; baqāʾ concerns remaining.

Remaining does not necessarily mean absolute eternity in every context. Something may remain after one event yet end under other circumstances. The duration and nature of its remaining must be understood from context.

In Al-Naḥl 16:96, the contrast between exhaustible human possessions and what remains with The God clarifies the verse’s meaning.

Nor does baqāʾ mean remaining fixed without any movement or change merely because it stands opposite nafād. The verse speaks of remaining; it does not determine every philosophical characteristic of what remains.

Study of B Q Y

5. Translation proposals for Forqan

“Come to an end,” “run out,” and “have no end” are accurate, natural renderings in this root’s Qur’anic uses.

In Al-Naḥl 16:96, yanfadu is well rendered “comes to an end” or “runs out,” preserving the natural contrast with bāqin.

In Al-Kahf 18:109, لَنَفِدَ الْبَحْرُ can be rendered “the sea would come to an end” or “the sea would run out.” تَنْفَدَ كَلِمَاتُ رَبِّي means “my Lord’s words were exhausted.” Using closely related renderings for both verbs makes the intended contrast clearer.

In Luqmān 31:27, مَا نَفِدَتْ كَلِمَاتُ اللَّهِ is appropriately rendered “The God’s words would not be exhausted.” “Would not come to an end” is also defensible. In Persian research prose, the equivalent of “come to an end” is smoother and clearer than the everyday equivalent of “be finished.”

In Ṣād 38:54, مَا لَهُ مِنْ نَفَادٍ can be rendered “it has no exhaustion” or “it is inexhaustible.” The first is closer to the Arabic structure.

“Run out” is also useful for material uses of the root, such as provisions or well water. For verses about The God’s words, “come to an end” is a natural, less ambiguous choice; the corresponding Persian idiom for a supply running out is better suited to material examples.

This root should not be equated in every case with penetration, decrease, the complete destruction of existence, or physical disappearance.

6. Conclusion

In its Qur’anic uses, N F D denotes something running out or coming to an end. This may concern a resource’s supply, limited means, or something whose continuation is at issue.

In early Arabic, recorded uses include provisions, wealth, well water, and even argumentative capacity running out. Anfada and istanfada likewise concern bringing something to an end or using it up, although these derivatives do not occur in the Qur’an.

Four Qur’anic verses employ the root along two clear lines: exhaustible human possessions contrasted with what remains with The God, and the inexhaustibility of divine words and provision contrasted with limited, exhaustible resources.

The root alone determines neither the material nature of destruction, the duration of consumption, the initial quantity of a resource, nor the nature of what does not run out.

Final definition: in Qur’anic use, N F D means something reaching its end or running out, so that the relevant supply or continuation ends. Nafād names this exhaustion; negating it expresses inexhaustibility.

This definition fits human possessions in Al-Naḥl 16:96, the sea and the Lord’s words in Al-Kahf 18:109, The God’s words in Luqmān 31:27, and inexhaustible provision in Ṣād 38:54.
