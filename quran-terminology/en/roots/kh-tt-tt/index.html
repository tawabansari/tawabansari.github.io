Drawing lines, marking, and writing: from leaving a trace on the ground to recording speech by hand

In Arabic, خ ط ط concerns making a line, drawing a mark, and leaving an extended trace on a surface. This may occur on earth, stone, cloth, or a writing surface. Related uses include drawing lines, writing, marking land, defining a place’s boundaries, and tracing a route.

Within this family, khaṭṭ denotes both a line itself and writing. Producing ordered marks on a surface with a pen or another implement may be called khaṭṭa. When these marks are letters and words, making lines becomes writing.

Not every use is confined to writing. Arabic also uses this family for a path’s line, a land boundary, patterns on cloth, and even lines made by feet on the ground.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, in Al-ʿAnkabūt. وَلَا تَخُطُّهُ بِيَمِينِكَ concerns writing by hand. The verse distinguishes reciting a book from writing it and recalls the absence of both activities during a specified period of the Prophet’s life.

1. Root forms and semantic core

خ ط ط is a doubled triliteral root: its second and third radicals are identical. In Form I, they are often assimilated into a single geminated letter.

Important forms include:

  • خَطَّ ـ يَخُطُّ (khaṭṭa–yakhuṭṭu): drew a line, marked a surface, or wrote something.
  • خَطّ (khaṭṭ): a line, extended trace, route, or writing.
  • خُطُوط (khuṭūṭ): plural of khaṭṭ, lines.
  • خَطَّطَ ـ يُخَطِّطُ (khaṭṭaṭa–yukhaṭṭiṭu): ruled or drew lines; in extended uses, planned.
  • تَخْطِيط (takhṭīṭ): tracing or drawing lines; in later uses, designing and planning.
  • مَخْطُوط (makhṭūṭ): something written or marked with lines.
  • مُخَطَّط (mukhaṭṭaṭ): something bearing drawn lines or patterns, or for which a plan has been determined.
  • اِخْتَطَّ (ikhtaṭṭa): marked out a line or boundaries for oneself, particularly land or a building site.
  • خُطَّة (khuṭṭah): an affair, situation, course, or measure before someone; in extended uses, a plan.
  • خِطَّة (khiṭṭah): a bounded plot designated for residence or use.
  • خَطَّاط (khaṭṭāṭ): someone engaged in writing and calligraphy.

These forms do not all have the same range or history. Some are known in early Arabic; others became more specialized later, particularly with the development of writing, urban settlement, and administrative terminology.

The Qur’an has only تَخُطُّهُ: an active second-person masculine singular imperfect, Form I, with the object pronoun hu.

Thus خُطَّة, تَخْطِيط, مَخْطُوط, and خَطَّاط do not occur as derivatives of this root in the Qur’an, although they help explain the family’s semantic field.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the sole occurrence in Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:48

The semantic core: a trace extending along a surface

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies the family’s basic meaning as:

أَثَرٌ يَمْتَدُّ امْتِدَادًا

atharun yamtaddu imtidādan

That is, “a trace that extends.”

This definition highlights a feature shared by many uses: a line is a trace produced on a surface along a particular length or extension.

Such a trace may be produced with a pen, finger, stick, writing implement, or even the movement of human or animal feet.

Here a line need not consist of letters and words. A groove in soil, an extended mark on stone, or a trace of movement can also be a khaṭṭ.

When such traces take the form of letters and words, they acquire the meaning of writing.

Three related concepts should therefore be distinguished:

First, making a line or extended trace, the root’s principal physical field.

Second, marking and defining boundaries, an application of lines to a place or surface.

Third, writing and recording letters and words, a particular application in written language.

They are related but not wholly synonymous. Writing in script involves marking, but not every act of drawing lines records words.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: the principle of an extended trace and writing

2. Lines, paths, land, and writing in early Arabic

A line as a trace or route

Lisān al-ʿArab explains al-khaṭṭ through a route or an extended form within something.

For example, when rain has not covered all the ground evenly and vegetation appears along parts of it, the growth can be described as khuṭūṭ, lines, across the land.

Here no person has drawn the line with a pen; the natural landscape takes a linear form.

Khaṭṭ is also recorded for a road or route followed by humans or animals. Its distinct extension over the ground connects it with the physical image of a line.

This shows that the Arabic meaning was not confined to a writing surface.

Lines and writing

Arabic dictionaries record خَطَّ الشَّيْءَ يَخُطُّهُ خَطًّا for writing something with a pen or another implement.

خَطَّ الْقَلَمُ is also used of a pen writing.

Here drawing lines becomes recording linguistic signs. Separate or connected strokes form letters, and letters form words and expressions.

Accordingly, خَطَّ الْكِتَابَ can mean writing a book or text.

In writing contexts, however, khaṭṭa does not necessarily establish whether the writer composed the text, recorded someone else’s words, or copied another manuscript. The object, accompanying expressions, and context must establish these distinctions.

Someone may write their own words, record speech they heard, or copy an existing text. All three may count physically as writing.

Marking out land

اِخْتَطَّ فُلَانٌ أَرْضًا describes someone marking out a plot and indicating its boundaries.

Here a line defines a boundary and separates an area from its surroundings.

Someone identifying a home site or land for use may draw lines, cut grooves, or place marks to determine its limits.

Hence khiṭṭah came to be used for land or a plot whose boundaries were marked.

Later, khiṭaṭ also denoted designated urban sections or plots. Historical accounts of the formation and growth of cities such as Kufa and Basra use the term.

The lexical basis must be distinguished from historical application. Defining land through lines is a clear use of the root, but particular systems of urban subdivision and property regulation are not part of its original meaning.

Lines and patterns on objects

Arabic dictionaries use ثَوْبٌ مُخَطَّطٌ for a garment bearing lines or linear patterns.

The adjective also describes certain animals, fruits, and visible forms.

These uses do not concern writing a text, but lines or patterns in something’s appearance.

They matter because they show the physical meaning of a line remaining independent alongside the writing sense.

Drawing on earth and divinatory uses

Some lexical accounts of early Arabic use khaṭṭ for drawing lines on earth or sand.

In one social practice, people claiming to predict events or know hidden matters drew lines and interpreted their arrangement or number.

Khaṭṭ still means drawing lines here. Divination or interpreting signs is a separate activity performed using those lines.

Thus khaṭṭ should not be defined fundamentally as fortune-telling. Divination was one particular application in certain social settings, alongside writing, marking land, and patterning cloth.

Recording this practice in language sources does not endorse its truth or legitimacy.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: lines, land, patterns, and drawing on earth

3. Poetic and early linguistic witnesses

خُطَّة as a situation or course ahead

Poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Taʾabbaṭa Sharran contains:

هُمَا خُطَّتَا إِمَّا إِسَارٌ وَمِنَّةٌ
وَإِمَّا دَمٌ وَالْقَتْلُ بِالْحُرِّ أَجْدَرُ

humā khuṭṭatā immā isārun wa-minnatun / wa-immā damun wa-al-qatlu bi-al-ḥurri ajdaru

Translation:

“Two courses lie ahead: captivity and release through another’s favor, or blood and death; and death is more fitting for the free man.”

The poet presents himself as facing captivity or possible death.

خُطَّتَا refers to two situations or courses ahead, rather than two lines written on paper or two plots of land.

This witness shows the family expressing a condition or affair confronting someone in early Arabic, in addition to physical traces.

It does not show that khuṭṭah in pre-Islamic Arabic meant exactly today’s “management planning” or “implementation plan.” “Two situations or courses ahead” is clearer here.

Literary transmissions differ over the wording and grammatical analysis of khuṭṭatā, including omission of the dual nūn. These differences do not fundamentally change the sense of two situations or choices.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: Taʾabbaṭa Sharran’s witness and omitted dual nūn

Drawing on earth in Dhū al-Rummah’s poetry

Dhū al-Rummah, an Umayyad poet, says:

عَشِيَّةَ مَا لِي حِيلَةٌ غَيْرَ أَنَّنِي
بِلَقْطِ الْحَصَى وَالْخَطِّ فِي التُّرْبِ مُولَعُ

ʿashiyyata mā lī ḥīlatun ghayra annanī / bi-laqṭi al-ḥaṣā wa-al-khaṭṭi fī al-turbi mūlaʿu

Translation:

“That evening, I have no recourse except to occupy myself with picking up pebbles and drawing lines in the earth.”

الْخَطِّ فِي التُّرْبِ denotes drawing lines in the earth.

A pen, paper, or written text is therefore unnecessary for an act of khaṭṭ. Someone can draw on earth with a finger or another implement.

Drawing lines sometimes had a social connection with divination, but that meaning should not be imposed on every example. Al-Jāḥiẓ quotes this witness under khaṭṭ al-ḥazīn, the grieving person’s drawing of lines. Its context thus fits sorrow and occupying the hands; al-khaṭṭ directly means drawing lines.

Al-Jāḥiẓ’s Al-Ḥayawān: the same witness as a grieving person drawing lines

Dhū al-Rummah belongs to the Islamic period, so this should not be called pre-Islamic poetry. It documents early Arabic close to Islam’s first centuries.

Dīwān of Dhū al-Rummah: the fī al-turb transmission

Lines and routes in Arabic poetry

Lexical sources quote other poets using khaṭṭ for a road or route.

They also record expressions likening the traces of feet on earth to writing.

Such images display the relationship between a route, a trace of movement, and drawing lines. The ground is imagined as a page marked by a passing human or animal.

The writing sense is not detached from the tangible image of leaving a surface trace, although these uses cannot all be reconstructed in a definite historical sequence.

4. خُطَّة: from an affair and intention to planning

In modern Arabic, khuṭṭah is a familiar word for a plan. Its Arabic history should guide the discussion; it should not be equated immediately with every use of the Persian word kheṭṭeh today.

Distinguishing khuṭṭah, an affair or situation, from khiṭṭah, a marked plot of land, and takhṭīṭ, tracing or planning, is essential.

In early Arabic, khuṭṭah could denote an affair, situation, difficulty, business, or a course confronting someone.

خُطَّةُ سُوءٍ is recorded for an undesirable condition or affair.

Uses involving intention describe someone with a khuṭṭah “in his head”: a matter or purpose he has in mind and has resolved upon.

These uses relate to modern planning, but are not identical with it.

Planning today usually involves defining goals, stages, timing, resources, and the order of implementation. This broad technical sense should not be attributed to every early occurrence without historical evidence.

Takhṭīṭ, tracing lines and defining a structure, can naturally also apply to designing a plan. Defining a project’s boundaries and course before carrying it out resembles drawing a design.

Establishing the historical emergence of modern planning senses, however, requires evidence from each period. Semantic affinity alone does not establish a precise chronology.

Planning is thus a recognized extension of this family, but not the meaning of its sole Qur’anic occurrence.

5. The Qur’anic use: وَلَا تَخُطُّهُ بِيَمِينِكَ

The root’s sole Qur’anic occurrence is in Al-ʿAnkabūt:

وَمَا كُنْتَ تَتْلُو مِنْ قَبْلِهِ مِنْ كِتَابٍ وَلَا تَخُطُّهُ بِيَمِينِكَ إِذًا لَارْتَابَ الْمُبْطِلُونَ

wa-mā kunta tatlū min qablihi min kitābin wa-lā takhuṭṭuhu bi-yamīnika idhan la-irtāba al-mubṭilūna

Proposed translation for the root study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“Before it, you did not recite any book, nor did you write it with your right hand; otherwise, those promoting falsehood would have doubted.”

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:48

The verse distinguishes two acts: reciting a book and writing it.

تَتْلُو belongs to ت ل و, reciting or reading; تَخُطُّهُ belongs to خ ط ط and means writing here.

These are related but independent activities. Someone may read a document without having written it, and may write something heard or learned from another.

Both activities are negated here in relation to a book and an earlier period.

The precise meaning of تَخُطُّهُ

Morphologically, تَخُطُّهُ consists of a verb and an object pronoun, with the root’s doubled structure visible within the verb:

تَخُطُّ is the second-person masculine singular imperfect of khaṭṭa, while attached hu denotes the object being written. The two final radicals assimilate as geminated ṭāʾ. Lā here is negative, not prohibitive; the verb is indicative, marked by ḍammah on the geminated ṭāʾ. This therefore reports the absence of writing rather than commanding “Do not write.”

In تَخُطُّهُ بِيَمِينِكَ, the verb accompanies bi-yamīnika, “with your right hand.”

This clarifies writing. The verse concerns writing by hand, rather than planning, marking land boundaries, or merely thinking about a subject.

تَخُطُّهُ can therefore appropriately be rendered “you wrote it.”

The verb does not itself identify the writing instrument. A pen, ink, skin, paper, or another implement is not named in the expression.

Khaṭṭa does not lexically mean composing. Someone may write another person’s composition or create their own text; the act of making lines and writing does not itself establish that distinction.

The direct meaning here is writing a text by hand, not necessarily composing, copying, or dictating it to others.

The importance of مِنْ قَبْلِهِ

A decisive qualification is min qablihi, “before it.”

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:47 speaks of the Book being sent down to the Prophet. “Before it” therefore concerns the period preceding this Book’s coming.

This specifies the scope of the statement.

The verse states that before it, the Prophet did not recite a book or write it with his own hand.

Two propositions, however, must be distinguished:

That someone had not performed an activity in the past.

That the person was absolutely incapable of performing it.

Here, with negation and a temporal qualification, the verb addresses not performing an act. The verb itself does not mean “being able” or “being unable.”

Thus “you did not write” should not become “you never possessed the ability to write” without independent argument.

Conversely, the verse does not by itself prove that the Prophet could read and write. That conclusion also exceeds what this expression alone states.

The clear meaning is the negation of reciting and writing a book in the specified period before its revelation. The extent of the Prophet’s literacy, education, and practical ability is a broader subject requiring separate study of relevant verses and evidence.

Al-Ṭabarī: negated recitation and writing in the context of the revealed Book

بِيَمِينِكَ: writing directly by hand

Bi-yamīnika means “with your right hand.”

Yamīn can have extended senses, such as power, in some constructions. Here its combination with writing clearly supports the literal right hand.

The verse describes writing directly by hand.

Mentioning the right hand does not mean the verse is establishing a ruling on writing with either hand. It concerns the ordinary manner of writing and denies its occurrence in the situation discussed.

Nor does the qualification establish that the Prophet was necessarily right-handed or had never written with his left hand. Such questions lie outside the verse’s purpose and direct import.

Writing, recitation, and the doubts of those promoting falsehood

The verse ends:

إِذًا لَارْتَابَ الْمُبْطِلُونَ

idhan la-irtāba al-mubṭilūna

That is, “otherwise, those promoting falsehood would have doubted.”

The Qur’an connects the Prophet’s past with the possibility of casting doubt on the Book’s source.

Had the Prophet previously been engaged in reciting and writing books, opponents could have used that background to cast doubt on the source of what he brought.

The verse denies such a background.

Khaṭṭa still means writing. The discussion of opponents’ doubts is the consequence and purpose of the argument, not part of the root’s lexical definition.

The verb should therefore not be translated as “learning from earlier books.” Learning or borrowing is distinct from writing and is raised through the verse’s broader argument.

Connections with surrounding verses

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:47 concerns the Book’s revelation and different groups’ responses.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:48 addresses the prior history of reciting and writing a book before the Qur’an’s revelation.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:49 speaks of clear signs in the breasts of those given knowledge.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:51 again emphasizes the revealed Book recited to its listeners.

This continuity shows that the central subject is the Book’s source, reception, and communication.

تَخُطُّهُ thus appears within a discussion of a book, making its writing sense clear.

Distinguishing reciting from writing does not establish that the Qur’an has no relationship with written recording or that its written transmission is unimportant. The verse makes no such claim; it concerns the Prophet’s specified situation before receiving the Book.

6. تَخُطُّهُ and the allegation اكْتَتَبَهَا

Al-Furqān reports an allegation by opponents:

وَقَالُوا أَسَاطِيرُ الْأَوَّلِينَ اكْتَتَبَهَا فَهِيَ تُمْلَىٰ عَلَيْهِ بُكْرَةً وَأَصِيلًا

wa-qālū asāṭīru al-awwalīna iktatabahā fa-hiya tumlā ʿalayhi bukratan wa-aṣīlan

Proposed translation for the root study, without changing the installed verse translation:

“They said, ‘Tales of the ancients which he wrote or had put into writing; they are dictated to him morning and evening.’”

Al-Furqān 25:5

اكْتَتَبَهَا belongs to ك ت ب, rather than خ ط ط.

Comparing it with تَخُطُّهُ is nevertheless useful, since both concern allegations about writings and the Qur’an’s source.

Iktatabahā can denote writing or obtaining something in writing, including having another person write it. Its precise range requires examining Form VIII and the context.

In Al-Furqān, however, this is the opponents’ allegation, not an account the Qur’an accepts as factual.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:48, by contrast, describes and denies the Prophet’s prior reciting and writing of a book.

Two points emerge:

First, writing and obtaining writing may relate linguistically to distinct activities, including direct recording, copying, and dictation. These should not be conflated without reason.

Second, reporting an accusation in the Qur’an does not establish its truth. The opponents’ words must be distinguished from the Qur’an’s response or judgment.

This matters for the context of تَخُطُّهُ, but the lexical article should not become a separate study of every historical claim about the Prophet’s literacy.

Al-Ṭabarī: the opponents’ allegation of obtaining writing and dictation

7. Distinctions from related roots

خ ط ط (khaṭṭ) compared with ك ت ب (katb): lines and writing

ك ت ب concerns writing and recording, with broader uses such as prescribing and registering an affair.

خ ط ط in its physical field emphasizes making a line or extended trace.

The roots overlap in writing, since ordinary writing produces linear forms.

Not every line, however, constitutes writing. A line can be drawn on the ground without recording letters or words.

Some Qur’anic uses of kataba mean prescribing or making obligatory, as in كُتِبَ عَلَيْكُمُ الصِّيَامُ in Al-Baqarah 2:183. That meaning cannot be transferred directly to khaṭṭa.

Thus, although تَخُطُّهُ in Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:48 is translated as “you wrote,” it does not contain the entire semantic field of kitāb and kitābah.

Study of K T B

خ ط ط (khaṭṭ) compared with س ط ر (saṭr): a line and a row of writing

س ط ر concerns a row, arranging writing in a line, and recording it.

Qur’anic expressions include وَالْقَلَمِ وَمَا يَسْطُرُونَ in Al-Qalam 68:1 and وَكِتَابٍ مَسْطُورٍ in Al-Ṭūr 52:2.

Saṭr particularly concerns the row and arrangement of writing; khaṭṭ denotes the linear trace and the act of drawing it.

They can be very close in actual writing. Producing written strokes creates rows of text.

Their distinction should not become absolute separation. Each highlights an aspect of written recording.

خ ط ط (khaṭṭ) compared with ر س م (rasm): a line and a remaining trace

Rasm can denote a mark, trace, or remainder. It also describes drawing and depicting in writing and image-making contexts.

Their uses, however, are not identical.

Khaṭṭ’s physical sense highlights an extended linear trace, whereas rasm may denote a form, pattern, or the remaining traces of something.

In Arabic poetry, rusūm al-diyār denotes surviving traces of dwellings and settlements, which need not be writing or lines of a particular form.

Later writing terminology gives rasm and khaṭṭ specialized uses. Those developments should not be imposed on the Qur’anic root’s basic meaning without historical examination.

خ ط ط (khaṭṭ) compared with خ ط و (khaṭw): a line and a step

خ ط و concerns stepping and movement step by step.

Human or animal movement over soft earth may leave a trace or path. That result can relate to a line, but stepping and drawing lines are not the same act.

Khaṭw denotes movement and a step; khaṭṭ denotes an extended trace or mark.

Their resemblance and the possible relationship between movement and footprints do not make them lexically one root.

Study of KH Ṭ W

خ ط ط (khaṭṭ) compared with خ ط أ (khaṭaʾ): a line and an error

خ ط أ concerns error, mistake, and missing the target or correct course.

Despite sharing two initial letters, خ ط ط and خ ط أ are separate roots.

Some dictionaries place khaṭīṭah, land left without rain while its surroundings receive it, under خ ط ط. Ibn Fāris, however, excludes it from this entry and connects it with akhṭaʾa, as though the rain had missed that land. This is his etymological analysis, not an established historical derivation universally accepted by lexicographers.

Formal similarity alone therefore does not establish a word’s historical root affiliation.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: Ibn Fāris’s analysis of khaṭīṭah and akhṭaʾa

Qur’anic research should likewise not transfer error or mistake from خ ط أ into تَخُطُّهُ.

Study of KH Ṭ ʾ

8. Translation proposal for Forqan

Writing is the most appropriate equivalent for the root’s sole Qur’anic use.

For part of Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:48:

وَمَا كُنْتَ تَتْلُو مِنْ قَبْلِهِ مِنْ كِتَابٍ وَلَا تَخُطُّهُ بِيَمِينِكَ

wa-mā kunta tatlū min qablihi min kitābin wa-lā takhuṭṭuhu bi-yamīnika

Proposed translation of this excerpt:

“Before it, you did not recite any book, nor did you write it with your right hand.”

تَخُطُّهُ is rendered as writing because the subject is a book and bi-yamīnika specifies writing by hand.

A more literal “you did not draw lines” displays the root’s physical field, but may mislead in modern Persian. Readers may understand only drawing on paper or ground, whereas this context means writing a text.

“You did not compose” is also imprecise: composing concerns producing and organizing content, which does not necessarily follow from khaṭṭa.

“You were illiterate” or “you could not write” is not a direct equivalent either. These judge someone’s ability, whereas the verb here addresses not performing an activity during a specified period.

Brief root meaning

Making a line or extended trace on a surface; marking and writing.

Meaning in the verse

تَخُطُّهُ — Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:48: you wrote it by hand.

The lexical explanation can identify a second-person masculine singular imperfect from the doubled root خ ط ط, with attached hu specifying its object.

Conclusion

خ ط ط concerns making a line, mark, or extended trace on a surface. This can occur in drawing on earth, defining routes and land boundaries, patterning objects, and writing letters and words.

Related words include khiṭṭah for a marked plot and khuṭṭah for an affair, situation, or course ahead. Modern senses of designing and planning relate to part of the family, but should not become every use’s original and fixed meaning.

The Qur’an has only تَخُطُّهُ in Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:48, meaning writing a book by hand. Its combination with reciting, a book, and the right hand shows that written recording is the subject, rather than marking land, designing, or planning.

The verse denies reciting and writing a book before the Qur’an’s revelation, relating that to the doubts of those promoting falsehood. Not performing an act must nevertheless be distinguished from absolute inability; they are linguistically different.

The root’s basic meaning can therefore be described as “making lines and extended marks on a surface, and, in writing, recording letters and words.” Writing is accurate and natural for its sole Qur’anic use, while the physical image connects it with the wider family خ ط ط. Brief root meaning: Making a line or extended trace on a surface; marking and writing.
