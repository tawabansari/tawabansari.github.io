Qur’anic root ر أ ف

Compassion and raʾūf: tenderness, kindness and consideration for another, from human feeling to descriptions of The God and the Prophet in the Qur’an

The three-letter Arabic root ر أ ف concerns compassion and kindness accompanied by gentleness and tenderness. Someone who has compassion for another does not disregard their suffering and difficulty, and inclines towards considering their condition, easing their hardship or treating them gently. This meaning is close to رحمة, though lexical explanations often associate رأفة with greater gentleness and tenderness.

In the Qur’an, رَءُوف most often describes The God, but also describes the Prophet. رَأْفَة occurs once for compassion in the hearts of Jesus’s followers and once in a verse that concerns implementing a ruling and preventing compassion from interfering with it. These uses show that compassion is not simply a commendable feeling with the same result in every situation. The compassion may be real, while how it should influence conduct also depends on the situation and other people’s rights.

This root occurs 13 times in the Qur’an, in two nominal derivatives: رَأْفَة twice and رَءُوف eleven times. Forms such as رَأَفَ, رَؤُفَ, يَرْأَفُ, رَائِف and رَآفَة are known in Arabic but do not occur in the Qur’an in these forms. Of the eleven occurrences of رَءُوف, ten describe The God and one describes the Messenger.

1. The semantic core and usage in early Arabic

Compassion: gentleness and concern towards another

Arabic lexical sources explain رَأْفَة through رَحْمَة. They record رَأَفَ بِهِ and رَؤُفَ بِهِ for showing mercy, feeling compassion and treating another kindly.

Two features appear particularly in explanations of this root: mercy and tenderness. Tenderness here concerns gentleness and delicacy in dealing with another, in contrast to hardness of heart and indifference to their suffering.

For example, a father who is kind to his child and whose treatment becomes gentler when he sees the child suffer can be described as رَءُوف. This does not mean that he is weak or unable to make decisions; it concerns compassion and consideration for the child’s condition.

The Arabic verb also occurs with the preposition bāʾ: رَأَفَ بِهِ means that he showed compassion towards him. This construction directs compassion towards the person or being who receives it.

Some roots have an initial image concerning a specific material action, such as cutting, covering or carrying. In this investigation, no independently supported material image was found for ر أ ف. The root’s principal field concerns emotional and ethical dispositions and conduct. Making softened matter, a broken hard object or an opened heart the historical origin of compassion therefore lacks adequate lexical support.

Is compassion the same as mercy?

Lexical sources show two tendencies in explaining the relation between رأفة and رحمة.

One explanation identifies compassion with mercy. This rests on the words’ extensive closeness in language use.

Another presents compassion as a gentler, more tender or more intense kind of mercy. Some lexicographers describe it as more specific than mercy; others as a stronger degree of mercy.

These differences should remain visible. “More specific” and “more intense” are different claims. A word can denote a particular kind of kindness without being more intense than another word in every situation.

Lexical sources also offer another distinction: mercy may involve something unpleasant for a person’s benefit, while compassion is usually associated with gentleness and avoiding hardship. For example, someone may prevent another from doing something out of concern for their good, even if that restraint is unpleasant for them.

This distinction may clarify a tendency of the two words, but should not become an absolute rule. Al-Nūr 24:2 places compassion against implementing a ruling involving hardship and suffering. Compassion and mercy therefore need to be understood through their range of uses, rather than through one lexical definition alone.

Compassion in Arabic poetry

A poem attributed to Kaʿb ibn Mālik al-Anṣārī, a poet who lived during the period of revelation, says:

«نُطِيعُ نَبِيَّنَا وَنُطِيعُ رَبًّا
هُوَ الرَّحْمَنُ كَانَ بِنَا رَءُوفًا»

“We obey our Prophet and obey a Lord who is the Most Merciful and has been compassionate and kind towards us.”

رَءُوفًا describes the Lord here. Its association with الرَّحْمَن and the statement about obedience illustrate compassion’s use in the religious language of that period. رَءُوف itself does not mean obedience or following.

Ibn Hishām attributes this piece to Kaʿb in the account of the journey towards Ṭāʾif. On the basis of that attribution and narrative setting, the evidence belongs to the period of revelation. The later date of the poem’s transmission and recording must nevertheless be distinguished from its attributed date of composition.

This evidence is connected to religious and Qur’anic language. It therefore cannot be treated as independent evidence for reconstructing the root’s meaning before the Qur’an.

The Qur’an itself supplies direct evidence for the human use of رَءُوف: Al-Tawbah 9:128 applies it to the Messenger. Establishing this human use therefore does not require poetry from periods after revelation.

This investigation found no pre-Qur’anic poetic witness for this root with sufficiently secure attribution and wording. This reports a limitation of the evidence examined, rather than proving that such a use did not exist before the Qur’an. The verses themselves are the principal basis for understanding its Qur’anic meaning; lexical accounts are examined to compare uses.

2. رَءُوف: a description of The God in the Qur’an

In Ḥafṣ’s recitation, رَءُوف follows فَعُول, a pattern that can express the prominence or intensity of a quality. Abū ʿAmr, Shuʿbah, Ḥamzah, al-Kisāʾī, Yaʿqūb and Khalaf the Tenth read the shorter رَؤُف, following فَعُل. This recitation difference does not add a new independent semantic derivative to the eleven occurrences of this description.

The فَعُول pattern of the longer form alone does not establish a precise quantity of compassion, its duration or the unlimited extent of all its effects. These need to be understood through the identity of the one described and the context.

The Qur’an attributes رَءُوف to The God ten times. Eight occurrences accompany رَحِيم, and two use بِالْعِبَادِ.

The God’s compassion in the qiblah episode

Al-Baqarah 2:143 says:

«وَمَا كَانَ اللَّهُ لِيُضِيعَ إِيمَانَكُمْ إِنَّ اللَّهَ بِالنَّاسِ لَرَءُوفٌ رَحِيمٌ»

“The God will never let your faith go to waste. The God is indeed deeply compassionate and merciful towards people.”

(Al-Baqarah 2:143; the end of the verse)

This occurs within the discussion of the qiblah. The Qur’an speaks of following the Messenger, the difficulty of the change of qiblah for some of those addressed, and their faith not being wasted.

The ending recalls The God’s compassion and mercy. These descriptions thus appear in a context of reassurance for people whom the qiblah question could have caused difficulty or concern.

بِالنَّاسِ also matters. The Qur’an describes His compassion towards people here, without restricting it to one particular group.

This should not mean that all divine dealings with every person are identical in every situation. The same surah also discusses guidance, turning away and human responsibility. Yet بِالنَّاسِ itself does not restrict the description to a religious group.

Compassion in the blessings of life

Al-Naḥl 16:7 says:

«وَتَحْمِلُ أَثْقَالَكُمْ إِلَىٰ بَلَدٍ لَمْ تَكُونُوا بَالِغِيهِ إِلَّا بِشِقِّ الْأَنْفُسِ إِنَّ رَبَّكُمْ لَرَءُوفٌ رَحِيمٌ»

“They carry your loads to a land you could reach only with great hardship. Your Lord is indeed deeply compassionate and merciful.”

(Al-Naḥl 16:7)

The subject is livestock carrying human loads and reducing the difficulty of travel.

The God’s compassion here is connected to a concrete benefit in life. People have capacities and means for transporting loads and travelling long distances that reduce life’s hardship.

This use allows divine compassion to be understood beyond forgiveness of sin or reward in the hereafter. The Qur’an also describes The God as compassionate and merciful when recalling natural blessings and the means of living.

Al-Ḥajj 22:65, after discussing what on earth has been made serviceable to people, ships moving at sea and the holding of the sky, also says:

«إِنَّ اللَّهَ بِالنَّاسِ لَرَءُوفٌ رَحِيمٌ»

“The God is indeed deeply compassionate and merciful towards people.”

(Al-Ḥajj 22:65; the end of the verse)

Compassion and mercy are again expressed in relation to blessings that benefit people. This shows that Qur’anic kindness is not necessarily confined to an inward emotional state; its effects can appear in human life and the benefits people receive.

Compassion in guidance and bringing people out of darkness

Al-Ḥadīd 57:9 says:

«هُوَ الَّذِي يُنَزِّلُ عَلَىٰ عَبْدِهِ آيَاتٍ بَيِّنَاتٍ لِيُخْرِجَكُمْ مِنَ الظُّلُمَاتِ إِلَى النُّورِ وَإِنَّ اللَّهَ بِكُمْ لَرَءُوفٌ رَحِيمٌ»

“He is the one who sends down clear signs upon His servant to bring you out of darkness into light. The God is indeed deeply compassionate and merciful towards you.”

(Al-Ḥadīd 57:9)

This verse situates compassion within the sending down of signs and human guidance. The God sends down clear signs so that those addressed may be brought out of darkness into light.

Divine compassion here is therefore connected to illuminating the way and providing an opportunity for guidance. This use of kindness extends beyond removing bodily hardship: people’s being astray and needing guidance are also at issue.

رَءُوف itself does not mean guiding or sending down signs. Those actions are expressed by يُنَزِّلُ and لِيُخْرِجَكُمْ. رَءُوف describes The God in this context and retains the meaning of compassion and kindness.

Compassion and accepting a person’s return

Al-Tawbah 9:117 says:

«لَقَدْ تَابَ اللَّهُ عَلَى النَّبِيِّ وَالْمُهَاجِرِينَ وَالْأَنْصَارِ الَّذِينَ اتَّبَعُوهُ فِي سَاعَةِ الْعُسْرَةِ مِنْ بَعْدِ مَا كَادَ يَزِيغُ قُلُوبُ فَرِيقٍ مِنْهُمْ ثُمَّ تَابَ عَلَيْهِمْ إِنَّهُ بِهِمْ رَءُوفٌ رَحِيمٌ»

“The God has indeed turned towards the Prophet, the emigrants and the helpers who followed him in the hour of hardship, after the hearts of some of them had nearly swerved. Then He turned towards them. He is indeed deeply compassionate and merciful towards them.”

(Al-Tawbah 9:117)

This describes people who followed the Prophet in a difficult situation, when some of their hearts nearly swerved from the course.

The ending places The God’s compassion and mercy after ثُمَّ تَابَ عَلَيْهِمْ. In this context, compassion is therefore connected to divine favour returning to people exposed to slipping.

This use should not be confined to the lexical meaning of forgiveness. Turning back and compassion are separate concepts connected in one verse. Compassion may appear in accepting someone’s return and treating them kindly, but ر أ ف itself does not mean repentance or returning.

Compassion in a prayer for forgiveness and freedom from resentment

In Al-Ḥashr 59:10, those who came after the earlier groups say:

«وَالَّذِينَ جَاءُوا مِنْ بَعْدِهِمْ يَقُولُونَ رَبَّنَا اغْفِرْ لَنَا وَلِإِخْوَانِنَا الَّذِينَ سَبَقُونَا بِالْإِيمَانِ وَلَا تَجْعَلْ فِي قُلُوبِنَا غِلًّا لِلَّذِينَ آمَنُوا رَبَّنَا إِنَّكَ رَءُوفٌ رَحِيمٌ»

“Those who came after them say: Our Lord, forgive us and our brothers who preceded us in faith, and place no resentment in our hearts towards those who believe. Our Lord, You are indeed deeply compassionate and merciful.”

(Al-Ḥashr 59:10)

Here رَءُوفٌ رَحِيمٌ is part of a human prayer and describes the Lord. Requests for forgiveness and for resentment to be absent from the heart use separate verbs. This association places compassion within believers’ relations with one another and a request for divine favour, but does not make رَءُوف itself mean forgiveness or an absence of resentment.

3. Compassion alongside warning, reckoning and responsibility

Examining only verses about blessing and guidance might suggest that compassion always means the absence of warning, hardship or an unpleasant consequence. The Qur’an also uses this description in contexts concerning reckoning and the possibility of people being seized.

وَاللَّهُ رَءُوفٌ بِالْعِبَادِ

Āl ʿImrān 3:30 says:

«يَوْمَ تَجِدُ كُلُّ نَفْسٍ مَا عَمِلَتْ مِنْ خَيْرٍ مُحْضَرًا وَمَا عَمِلَتْ مِنْ سُوءٍ تَوَدُّ لَوْ أَنَّ بَيْنَهَا وَبَيْنَهُ أَمَدًا بَعِيدًا وَيُحَذِّرُكُمُ اللَّهُ نَفْسَهُ وَاللَّهُ رَءُوفٌ بِالْعِبَادِ»

“On the day when every soul finds present the good it has done, and wishes that there were a great distance between it and the evil it has done. The God warns you of Himself, and The God is deeply compassionate towards the servants.”

(Āl ʿImrān 3:30)

The verse concerns people encountering their deeds and a divine warning. It nevertheless ends with وَاللَّهُ رَءُوفٌ بِالْعِبَادِ.

This association matters. The God’s compassion here is not opposed to informing people of their deeds’ consequences. The Qur’an both warns and describes Him as compassionate.

One can understand warning someone about their conduct’s consequences as compatible with compassion towards them. Yet this association does not make compassion lexically mean warning or punishing.

Al-Baqarah 2:207 also says:

«وَمِنَ النَّاسِ مَنْ يَشْرِي نَفْسَهُ ابْتِغَاءَ مَرْضَاتِ اللَّهِ وَاللَّهُ رَءُوفٌ بِالْعِبَادِ»

“Among people is one who sells his life seeking the pleasure of The God, and The God is deeply compassionate towards the servants.”

(Al-Baqarah 2:207)

This follows the description of people whose speech is pleasing but who cause corruption in their actions. Against them, the verse describes someone who seeks The God’s pleasure.

رَءُوفٌ بِالْعِبَادِ directs The God’s compassion towards the servants in both verses. One highlights warning and reckoning; the other a person’s striving for His pleasure. Compassion should therefore not be defined through just one result or kind of divine action.

فَإِنَّ رَبَّكُمْ لَرَءُوفٌ رَحِيمٌ

Al-Naḥl 16:47 says:

«أَوْ يَأْخُذَهُمْ عَلَىٰ تَخَوُّفٍ فَإِنَّ رَبَّكُمْ لَرَءُوفٌ رَحِيمٌ»

“Or that He seizes them while they are afraid? Your Lord is indeed deeply compassionate and merciful.”

(Al-Naḥl 16:47)

Alongside fear and apprehension, explanations of عَلَىٰ تَخَوُّفٍ also propose gradual seizure or reduction step by step. This difference concerns خ و ف and that expression’s construction.

On either reading, this continues warnings about people who devise evil plans being seized. The conclusion of these warnings describes The God as compassionate and merciful.

Their association should not make divine compassion negate all reckoning or punishment. Nor should compassion itself be interpreted as punishment. The two subjects occur in one context, but make different linguistic contributions.

These verses connect Qur’anic compassion to gentleness and kindness, without making it remove every responsibility and consequence of human conduct.

Compassion in a context of reform and avoiding accusations

Al-Nūr 24:20 says:

«وَلَوْلَا فَضْلُ اللَّهِ عَلَيْكُمْ وَرَحْمَتُهُ وَأَنَّ اللَّهَ رَءُوفٌ رَحِيمٌ»

“If it were not for The God’s bounty upon you and His mercy, and that The God is compassionate and merciful…”

(Al-Nūr 24:20)

The verse begins with وَلَوْلَا and does not explicitly state the condition’s consequence. The translation should therefore not attribute a definite unstated result to the text without explanation.

The preceding verses concern slander, repeating statements without knowledge and spreading accusations among believers. In this context, the Qur’an recalls The God’s bounty and mercy and describes Him as compassionate and merciful.

This again shows that compassion differs from unconditionally overlooking wrongful conduct. The preceding verses condemn particular actions and discuss the speakers’ responsibility while recalling divine bounty and mercy.

Compassion here therefore appears within a combination of warning, correcting conduct and divine kindness, rather than against the principle of human moral responsibility.

4. رَءُوفٌ رَحِيمٌ describing the Prophet

Al-Tawbah 9:128 says:

«لَقَدْ جَاءَكُمْ رَسُولٌ مِنْ أَنْفُسِكُمْ عَزِيزٌ عَلَيْهِ مَا عَنِتُّمْ حَرِيصٌ عَلَيْكُمْ بِالْمُؤْمِنِينَ رَءُوفٌ رَحِيمٌ»

“A Messenger from among yourselves has indeed come to you. Your suffering weighs heavily upon him; he is deeply concerned for your good, and is deeply compassionate and merciful towards the believers.”

(Al-Tawbah 9:128)

This is the Qur’an’s only occurrence of رَءُوف that explicitly describes a human being, the Messenger.

The construction matters for understanding the description. First, the verse says that the people’s suffering and hardship weigh heavily upon the Messenger: عَزِيزٌ عَلَيْهِ مَا عَنِتُّمْ. It then expresses his concern for them through حَرِيصٌ عَلَيْكُمْ. Finally, it describes him as رَءُوفٌ رَحِيمٌ towards the believers.

This sequence associates the Messenger’s compassion with caring about others’ hardship and striving for their good.

رَءُوف itself nevertheless means neither intensely concerned nor afflicted by suffering. Those descriptions are understood through separate words in the verse. Compassion is the kindness and tenderness expressed within this relationship.

Does raʾūf describe only The God?

Al-Tawbah 9:128 answers clearly: the Qur’an uses the description for both The God and the Messenger.

Their sharing it does not make all divine and human qualities and capacities identical. In human use, compassion can involve emotional experience, distress at another’s hardship and efforts to help. In divine use, it describes The God’s kind dealings and relation with His servants, and should not be confined to bodily experience or emotional need like a human being’s.

The word belongs to the same semantic family in both uses, while the subject and level of attribution differ.

بِالْمُؤْمِنِينَ and the extent of the Messenger’s compassion

بِالْمُؤْمِنِينَ precedes رَءُوفٌ رَحِيمٌ at the end of the verse. This clearly identifies believers as those towards whom the Messenger’s compassion is described.

This qualification does not prove that the Prophet had no compassion for any other person. The verse makes a particular statement about believers; denying compassion towards others is a separate claim requiring independent evidence.

Nor should the shared رَءُوفٌ رَحِيمٌ in descriptions of The God and the Prophet create a divine status for the Messenger or remove the distinction between Creator and created. The verse speaks of the Messenger’s ethical and behavioural quality, while other verses attribute the same description to The God.

5. رَأْفَةً وَرَحْمَةً: compassion in the hearts of Jesus’s followers

Al-Ḥadīd 57:27 says:

«ثُمَّ قَفَّيْنَا عَلَىٰ آثَارِهِمْ بِرُسُلِنَا وَقَفَّيْنَا بِعِيسَى ابْنِ مَرْيَمَ وَآتَيْنَاهُ الْإِنْجِيلَ وَجَعَلْنَا فِي قُلُوبِ الَّذِينَ اتَّبَعُوهُ رَأْفَةً وَرَحْمَةً وَرَهْبَانِيَّةً ابْتَدَعُوهَا مَا كَتَبْنَاهَا عَلَيْهِمْ إِلَّا ابْتِغَاءَ رِضْوَانِ اللَّهِ فَمَا رَعَوْهَا حَقَّ رِعَايَتِهَا فَآتَيْنَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا مِنْهُمْ أَجْرَهُمْ وَكَثِيرٌ مِنْهُمْ فَاسِقُونَ»

“Then We sent Our Messengers following in their footsteps, and sent Jesus son of Mary after them, and gave him the Gospel. We placed compassion and mercy in the hearts of those who followed him. They themselves introduced monasticism, which We had not prescribed for them; they introduced it seeking The God’s pleasure, but did not observe it as it should have been observed. We gave those among them who believed their reward, while many of them were disobedient.”

(Al-Ḥadīd 57:27)

This is the Qur’an’s second occurrence of رَأْفَة and matters in several respects.

First, compassion is presented as something placed in human hearts: وَجَعَلْنَا فِي قُلُوبِ الَّذِينَ اتَّبَعُوهُ رَأْفَةً وَرَحْمَةً.

Compassion and mercy are attributed to Jesus’s followers here. The Qur’an therefore does not confine compassion to The God or the Prophet of Islam; other people can also have this quality.

Second, رَأْفَةً and رَحْمَةً are coordinated. Their association places the expressions within one semantic field, but alone establishes neither complete separation nor synonymy.

The third point is the difference between compassion and mercy and رَهْبَانِيَّة.

Compassion and mercy compared with introduced monasticism

After compassion and mercy, the verse mentions رَهْبَانِيَّةً ابْتَدَعُوهَا: monasticism they themselves introduced. It then says مَا كَتَبْنَاهَا عَلَيْهِمْ, “We had not prescribed it for them.”

The syntactic connection of رهبانية to the preceding sentence and إِلَّا ابْتِغَاءَ رِضْوَانِ اللَّهِ is disputed. The translation above follows the reading of a disconnected exception: they introduced monasticism themselves, without its being prescribed, and their motive was seeking The God’s pleasure. Another analysis understands the expression after إِلَّا as identifying what was prescribed: seeking His pleasure. This difference in analysing monasticism does not change the meaning of compassion and mercy; their being placed in Jesus’s followers’ hearts is expressed through جَعَلْنَا.

Compassion should therefore not mean monasticism, nor should its presence in human hearts make every particular practice of religious austerity and withdrawal a divine command.

The verse distinguishes a person’s ethical and behavioural quality from a way of religious living that they introduce.

فَمَا رَعَوْهَا حَقَّ رِعَايَتِهَا should also not suggest that compassion and mercy were wrongful qualities from the outset. The criticism concerns failing to observe what is discussed in the context of monasticism.

The ending distinguishes those who believed from many who were disobedient. The Qur’an therefore does not give all Jesus’s followers an identical moral description.

Compassion in human hearts: feeling or conduct?

فِي قُلُوبِ shows that compassion can concern an inward human state and disposition. Its being in the heart does not make it unrelated to conduct.

Arabic compassion can be a feeling of concern and also appear in gentle conduct and consideration for another. Someone, for example, may be affected by another person’s suffering and consequently act to help them or ease their hardship.

This verse itself nevertheless does not identify particular actions by Jesus’s followers or all their social relations. It states the presence of compassion and mercy, rather than listing what each person did.

6. وَلَا تَأْخُذْكُمْ بِهِمَا رَأْفَةٌ: compassion and the bounds of implementing a ruling

Al-Nūr 24:2 says:

«الزَّانِيَةُ وَالزَّانِي فَاجْلِدُوا كُلَّ وَاحِدٍ مِنْهُمَا مِائَةَ جَلْدَةٍ وَلَا تَأْخُذْكُمْ بِهِمَا رَأْفَةٌ فِي دِينِ اللَّهِ إِنْ كُنْتُمْ تُؤْمِنُونَ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ وَلْيَشْهَدْ عَذَابَهُمَا طَائِفَةٌ مِنَ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ»

“The woman and man who commit zinā: lash each of them a hundred times. In the religion of The God, do not let compassion for them keep you from implementing this ruling, if you believe in The God and the Last Day. Let a group of believers witness their punishment.”

(Al-Nūr 24:2)

This verse is especially significant for understanding compassion because, unlike most uses of the root, it prohibits compassion’s influence on conduct in this setting.

رَأْفَةٌ here concerns compassion towards two people whose punishment is at issue. لَا تَأْخُذْكُمْ بِهِمَا رَأْفَةٌ means that compassion for them must not overcome those addressed and prevent implementation of the ruling.

This does not change compassion’s meaning into weakness, injustice or sin. It remains compassion; what is prohibited here is allowing it to affect implementation of the stated ruling.

Does this verse condemn compassion itself?

The verse speaks of a particular ruling and discusses compassion within that framework. It therefore cannot become a general rule that compassion towards wrongdoers is always wrong.

Elsewhere, the Qur’an describes The God as compassionate and presents compassion in Jesus’s followers’ hearts as placed there by Him. The Messenger is also described as compassionate towards believers.

Compassion itself is therefore not a blameworthy quality. Al-Nūr describes a situation in which it might cause a ruling prescribed by the text to be set aside.

This distinction between a word’s meaning and its place in a ruling must be preserved.

Compassion and justice

The verse raises an important question: does compassion always accord with justice?

Lexically, compassion concerns considering someone’s condition and responding gently to their suffering. Judging rights and responsibilities may involve other considerations as well.

In a dispute over rights, for example, compassion for one party should not cause the other party’s right to be disregarded. This example illustrates a possible relation between compassion and justice, but is not part of the root’s lexical definition.

Al-Nūr 24:2 itself presents a situation in which compassion must not suspend the ruling. This verse alone nevertheless does not explain all the conditions for establishing an offence and conducting proceedings. Verses 4 and 6–9 of the same surah also concern accusation, testimony and related oaths.

The lexical discussion of compassion should therefore not authorise disregarding evidentiary conditions or carrying out punishment on one’s own initiative. Those matters require a complete examination of the rulings and the relation between verses.

Can mercy involve hardship?

Some lexical explanations say that mercy can involve something unpleasant for someone’s benefit, while compassion is more associated with gentleness and avoiding hardship.

Al-Nūr can help clarify this tendency: compassion for the two people’s suffering, if it prevents the ruling’s implementation, is not accepted in this situation.

This should not make mercy invariably punish and compassion invariably set punishment aside. That formula follows neither from this verse nor from the full range of the two roots’ uses.

The difference between compassion and mercy needs cautious expression. Both concern kindness and tender concern, while compassion’s established uses place greater emphasis on gentleness and consideration for another. This is a semantic tendency, rather than a rule without exceptions.

7. Comparison with related roots and translation choices

ر أ ف (رَأَفَ) and ر ح م (رَحِمَ)

Mercy has a broad Qur’anic semantic field. The God gives people life’s blessings, illuminates the way of guidance, provides opportunities to return and receive forgiveness, and extends His mercy to the servants.

Compassion is connected to this field, while its lexical explanations more strongly emphasise gentleness and tender concern.

The Qur’an repeats رَءُوفٌ رَحِيمٌ nine times: eight for The God and once for the Messenger. Al-Ḥadīd 57:27 also places رَأْفَةً وَرَحْمَةً together.

This recurrence shows a close connection between the words. Their association alone nevertheless does not prove that compassion is always mercy’s first stage, a higher degree of it or an entirely separate kind.

Across verses, one expression may emphasise an aspect of kindness and the other a broader field of the same meaning. The available evidence nevertheless does not suffice for an absolute boundary between them.

ر أ ف (رَأَفَ) and ش ف ق (أَشْفَقَ)

Arabic شفقة concerns concern, fear and compassionate attention to another’s condition.

Al-Ṭūr 52:26 says:

«قَالُوا إِنَّا كُنَّا قَبْلُ فِي أَهْلِنَا مُشْفِقِينَ»

“They said: previously, among our families, we were fearful and apprehensive.”

(Al-Ṭūr 52:26)

مُشْفِقِينَ denotes fear and apprehension here. In other uses too, شفقة can involve fear of an action’s consequence.

Compassion and شفقة can be close in concern for another, but many Qur’anic uses of the latter have a more evident element of fear and apprehension. رَءُوف, by comparison, emphasises gentleness and kindness.

Both should therefore not receive one fixed equivalent in every verse. “Compassion” may suit both in some instances, but the difference in context and subject must remain clear.

ر أ ف (رَأَفَ) and ل ط ف (لَطُفَ)

لطف concerns gentleness and delicacy, and Qur’anic uses can also involve precise knowledge and subtle management.

Al-Mulk 67:14 says:

«أَلَا يَعْلَمُ مَنْ خَلَقَ وَهُوَ اللَّطِيفُ الْخَبِيرُ»

“Does the one who created not know, while He is the Subtle, the Aware?”

(Al-Mulk 67:14)

اللَّطِيفُ accompanies الْخَبِيرُ here, placing the meaning in relation to The God’s knowledge and awareness.

Compassion can also involve gentle and kind conduct, but the word itself does not denote subtle knowledge or hidden management.

Gentleness therefore brings لطف and compassion close, while their full ranges of use differ.

Compassion, pardoning and overlooking

Qur’anic عفو concerns pardoning and, in some uses, removing an effect or not pursuing a wrongdoing.

Someone may pardon another’s wrongdoing out of compassion, but compassion and pardoning differ lexically. Compassion is tenderness and kindness; pardoning concerns passing over something.

Al-Nūr 24:2 also shows that compassion’s presence does not necessarily mean setting punishment aside. Conversely, in verses about returning and forgiveness, The God’s compassion can accompany accepting a person’s return.

Not every instance of compassion therefore leads to pardon, nor does every pardon necessarily involve the same human feeling of tender concern. Their relation needs to be understood in each context.

رَءُوف in divine descriptions

The Qur’an uses رَءُوف without the definite article and sometimes with emphatic lām, as in لَرَءُوفٌ رَحِيمٌ. The definite الرَّءُوف does not occur among this root’s Qur’anic uses.

This morphological fact should not become an absolute theological conclusion that lacking ال prevents the word from describing The God. The Qur’an itself repeatedly attributes it to Him.

Nor should ال be added to the Qur’anic form to align it with later lists of divine names and descriptions. The actual Qur’anic wording needs to be recorded and studied as it stands.

Al-Tawbah 9:128 also shows that رَءُوف is not linguistically exclusive to The God. Its meaning concerns tenderness and kindness in both divine and human uses, while the identity of the one described and the context determine the kind and level of attribution.

Translation choices for Forqan

For رَأْفَة, “compassion” is natural and appropriate. Al-Ḥadīd 57:27, رَأْفَةً وَرَحْمَةً, can be rendered “compassion and mercy.” This preserves their closeness without forcing two wholly separate meanings upon them.

رَأْفَةٌ in Al-Nūr 24:2 is also well rendered “compassion.” The sentence can be translated: “Do not let compassion for them keep you from implementing the ruling.” The prohibition on compassion’s influence comes from the whole construction, rather than making compassion itself mean injustice or weakness.

For رَءُوف, “deeply compassionate” or “very kind” can be appropriate. Al-Tawbah 9:128 describes the Messenger, and “deeply compassionate and merciful towards the believers” conveys its meaning clearly.

In divine uses, رَءُوفٌ رَحِيمٌ can be translated “deeply compassionate and merciful.” When the difference between the descriptions matters, it is better discussed in the study than forced into an unnatural, invented equivalent for each word.

رَءُوف should not invariably become “forgiving,” “giving wealth,” “protecting from every danger” or “never punishing.” Some contexts may associate these with the effects of mercy and compassion, but they are not the root’s direct meaning.

“Pitying” can suggest a condescending attitude towards a vulnerable person in some English sentences. “Compassionate” and “kind” convey the meaning more naturally in most Qur’anic uses without adding that implication.

8. Conclusion

Arabic ر أ ف concerns compassion and kindness accompanied by gentleness and tenderness. It is close to mercy, though some lexical explanations more strongly associate compassion with delicacy and consideration for another. Lexicographers do differ over identifying compassion with mercy, making it more specific or making it more intense. One definition should not become an absolute rule without examining the full range of uses.

Qur’anic رَءُوف usually describes The God, and once also describes the Messenger. Divine compassion occurs in contexts of life’s blessings, guidance, accepting a return, reckoning, warning and easing difficulties. It should therefore not be confined to forgiving sin or removing bodily suffering.

Al-Tawbah 9:128 describes the Messenger as compassionate and merciful towards believers. Al-Ḥadīd 57:27 places compassion and mercy in Jesus’s followers’ hearts. These show that Qur’anic compassion can be a human quality as well as a divine description.

Al-Nūr 24:2 uses the same word where compassion’s influence on implementing a ruling is prohibited. This does not make its meaning negative; it shows that compassion alone does not determine every decision’s correctness. Its place needs to be assessed with the subject of the ruling, others’ rights and the verse’s full construction.

The semantic core of ر أ ف can be expressed as compassion and kindness accompanied by gentleness and consideration for another, especially in their suffering and hardship: a disposition that can appear in gentle conduct, easing difficulty or striving for another’s good.

This encompasses human compassion and the description رَءُوف for The God and the Messenger, without bodily human feeling and conduct being attributed to The God.

The main distinction is that compassion lexically means tender concern, while its result is not always identical. One verse associates it with blessing and ease, another with guidance and return, and another prevents its influence on implementing a ruling. Understanding the root must preserve these differences, rather than confining compassion to emotionalism, perpetual overlooking or an absolute, fixed degree of mercy.
