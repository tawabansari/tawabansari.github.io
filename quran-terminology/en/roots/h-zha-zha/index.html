The Qur’anic Root Ḥ Ẓ Ẓ

From shares and portions to material and spiritual endowment

The Arabic root ح ظ ظ concerns a share, portion, or allotment that reaches someone or is assigned to them. حَظّ can express a specified share of property, access to life’s resources, a portion of good, or an endowment of virtue. Within the same family, مَحْظُوظ describes someone who has received a favorable portion, while أَحَظّ compares two people and describes the one with the greater share.

The Qur’an uses this root in different settings. It sometimes concerns children’s or siblings’ inheritance shares, sometimes a portion of divine reminders that a group has forgotten, and sometimes worldly wealth or moral virtue.

This variety shows that ḥaẓẓ does not itself name wealth or an afterlife reward; nor do “luck” and “chance” provide a uniform equivalent in every usage. Its shared Qur’anic meaning is a share or portion, whose value, size, and kind are determined by context.

Qur’anic form: حَظّ, in seven verses: Āl ʿImrān 3:176, Al-Nisāʾ 4:11, Al-Nisāʾ 4:176, Al-Māʾidah 5:13, Al-Māʾidah 5:14, Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:79, and Fuṣṣilat 41:35.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the seven nominal occurrences of ḥaẓẓ

Other forms, including حُظُوظ, حَظِيظ, مَحْظُوظ, أَحَظّ, and the verb حَظَّ, are recorded in Arabic but do not occur in these forms in the Qur’an.

1. The semantic core and early Arabic usage

Ḥaẓẓ: a share or portion

In Al-ʿAyn, Al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad defines ḥaẓẓ as a share of bounty and good. Ibn Fāris likewise identifies one principle for the family in Maqāyīs al-Lughah: النَّصِيبُ وَالْجَدُّ, a share or portion, together with the sense of favorable endowment or fortune.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: a portion, fortune, and verbal forms

Lisān al-ʿArab records both senses, explaining that ḥaẓẓ can concern someone’s share of something as well as their favorable circumstances.

Lisān al-ʿArab: a share and favorable endowment

In the first usage, the main subject is an amount or part belonging to someone. When property is divided among several people, for example, each person’s share can be called their ḥaẓẓ.

In the second, the subject is someone’s possession of something considered desirable or beneficial, such as wealth, standing, or life’s resources.

The senses are related but not identical. Someone’s share of an asset can be established by a rule or agreement, whereas their access to life’s resources may be described in ordinary language in terms of fortune or luck.

Studying ḥaẓẓ therefore requires distinguishing three matters: the share itself, its value to its recipient, and the factor responsible for its reaching them.

The word does not necessarily determine all three.

Ḥaẓẓ and the sense of luck or fortune

Early Arabic also explains ḥaẓẓ through جَدّ. In this usage, jadd concerns fortune and favorable circumstances, rather than the effort and seriousness associated with the word in modern Persian.

فُلَانٌ ذُو حَظٍّ may mean that someone has a good portion of life’s advantages, wealth, or standing.

In this setting, مَحْظُوظ describes a fortunate or well-endowed person, while حَظِيظ is recorded for someone possessing wealth or resources.

This usage does not mean the root fundamentally describes an independent, random power called chance.

In modern Persian, “lucky” usually describes someone to whom a favorable event has happened. That sense overlaps with part of the ordinary usage of maḥẓūẓ, but cannot be transferred to every Qur’anic occurrence.

The inheritance share in Al-Nisāʾ 4:11, for example, is established through the rule stated in the verse. It cannot simply be called the heir’s luck.

Likewise, ذُو حَظٍّ عَظِيمٍ in Fuṣṣilat 41:35 occurs in a context of perseverance and good conduct. Rendering it merely as “a very lucky person” weakens its connection with conduct and moral virtue.

Important Arabic derivatives

حُظُوظ is a familiar plural of حَظّ. Lexical works also record forms such as أَحُظّ and أَحَاظٍ.

حَظِيظ describes someone with a favorable portion. Some lexical reports use it for a wealthy, well-provided person.

مَحْظُوظ likewise describes someone fortunate or favorably endowed.

أَحَظّ is a comparative form, describing the person with a greater share when two people are compared.

Lexical sources also record the verb حَظَّ. حَظِظْتُ فِي الْأَمْرِ, for example, means that someone has obtained a share or favorable fortune in a matter.

Reports differ, however, concerning this family’s verbal usage. Al-ʿAyn states that an independent verb for ḥaẓẓ had not been heard, whereas Ibn Fāris and other lexicographers transmit verbal forms.

A form’s absence from one lexicographer’s experience therefore does not necessarily mean that it did not exist in Arabic.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn: the report of not hearing a verb, compared with other lexicographers’ attestations

2. Early poetic witnesses: prosperity and differing circumstances

A passage attributed to Suwayd ibn Khadhāq al-ʿAbdī, a pre-Islamic poet, reads:

مَتَى مَا يَرَ النَّاسُ الْغَنِيَّ وَجَارُهُ
فَقِيرٌ يَقُولُوا عَاجِزٌ وَجَلِيدُ

matā mā yara al-nāsu al-ghaniyya wa-jāruhu / faqīrun yaqūlū ʿājizun wa-jalīdu

وَلَيْسَ الْغِنَى وَالْفَقْرُ مِنْ حِيلَةِ الْفَتَى
وَلَكِنْ أَحَاظٍ قُسِّمَتْ وَجُدُودُ

wa-laysa al-ghinā wa-l-faqru min ḥīlati al-fatā / wa-lākin aḥāẓin qussimat wa-judūdu

The two verses mean: When people see a wealthy person beside his poor neighbor, they call one capable and the other incapable. Yet wealth and poverty cannot be attributed solely to a person’s resourcefulness or ability; in the poet’s view, they are shares and fortunes distributed among people.

أَحَاظٍ here is a plural of ḥaẓẓ, referring to people’s different shares or allotments.

The witness matters because ḥaẓẓ is not used only for an agreed or legal share, but also to describe actual differences in life and economic provision.

The poet criticizes people’s hasty judgment that the wealthy must be capable and the poor must be incapable.

The word’s lexical meaning must nevertheless not be identified with the poet’s entire worldview. It does not itself establish how far wealth and poverty result from effort, opportunity, social conditions, or other factors.

Ibn Durayd’s transmission attributes the passage to Suwayd ibn Khadhāq, but Lisān al-ʿArab also reports its attribution to Al-Maʿlūṭ ibn Badal al-Qurayʿī. Although the witness belongs to poetry attributed to pre-Islamic times, the conflicting attribution must be preserved, and this particular passage’s date should not be treated as certain.

Lisān al-ʿArab: the two-verse witness and differing attributions to Suwayd and Al-Maʿlūṭ

Another poetic witness transmitted by Ibn Jinnī uses حِظَاظ for shares. This likewise shows plural forms of the root expressing differing portions and endowments.

Al-Muḥkam: Ibn Jinnī’s transmitted witness for ḥiẓāẓ

These witnesses clarify the word’s semantic range, but do not establish that ḥaẓẓ always means a benefit received without effort or entitlement.

3. Ḥaẓẓ in inheritance: a specified, ascertainable share

Al-Nisāʾ 4:11: children’s shares

Al-Nisāʾ 4:11 states:

لِلذَّكَرِ مِثْلُ حَظِّ الْأُنْثَيَيْنِ

li-l-dhakari mithlu ḥaẓẓi al-unthayayni

Translation: For the son is the equivalent of the share of two daughters.

The expression stands near the beginning of a verse discussing inheritance for children and certain other heirs.

Ḥaẓẓ here denotes a specified inheritance share. In حَظِّ الْأُنْثَيَيْنِ, the share of two daughters supplies the basis for expressing the son’s proportion.

The meaning is clear and legal: an amount or portion of property belonging to someone within the inheritance rule.

It does not mean random luck or fortune. The proportion stated is a particular rule for distributing an inheritance.

The word’s lexical meaning must also be distinguished from the size of the share. Ḥaẓẓ does not itself say that a person’s share is a half, double another’s, or a third. The proportion comes from the sentence containing it.

The two-to-one ratio should therefore not be incorporated into the root’s definition.

The expression also concerns inheritance in a particular relationship of kinship. It cannot alone establish that every man’s share is always twice every woman’s in all financial and legal situations.

Such a generalization does not follow from ḥaẓẓ’s meaning and must be assessed separately against the Qur’an’s own variety of inheritance situations.

Al-Ṭabarī, Al-Nisāʾ 4:11: shares in the context of children’s inheritance

Al-Nisāʾ 4:176: siblings’ shares

Al-Nisāʾ 4:176 likewise states:

وَإِنْ كَانُوا إِخْوَةً رِجَالًا وَنِسَاءً فَلِلذَّكَرِ مِثْلُ حَظِّ الْأُنْثَيَيْنِ

wa-in kānū ikhwatan rijālan wa-nisāʾan fa-li-l-dhakari mithlu ḥaẓẓi al-unthayayni

Translation: And if they are siblings, male and female, then for the male is the equivalent of the share of two females.

The expression occurs within the discussion of inheritance in a kalālah situation.

Ḥaẓẓ again means a specified share, but the persons under discussion differ from those in Al-Nisāʾ 4:11.

The first verse concerns children’s inheritance; the second concerns brothers and sisters in the situation described.

The repeated expression ḥaẓẓ al-unthayayni shows the word’s suitability for expressing proportional shares. It does not itself determine the proportion’s reason or amount.

In both verses, ḥaẓẓ concerns part of a common asset distributed among heirs according to a particular rule.

“Share” is therefore the most precise and natural Persian equivalent here. Translating it as “luck,” “fortune,” or even “happiness” alters the expression’s legal meaning.

4. Ḥaẓẓ in divine reminders: a forgotten portion

Al-Māʾidah 5:13: a portion of what had been recalled

After mentioning covenant-breaking and moving words from their places, Al-Māʾidah 5:13 states:

وَنَسُوا حَظًّا مِمَّا ذُكِّرُوا بِهِ

wa-nasū ḥaẓẓan mimmā dhukkirū bihi

Translation: And they forgot a portion of what they had been reminded of.

حَظًّا here does not mean a financial share or a specified amount of property.

مِمَّا ذُكِّرُوا بِهِ makes clear that the subject is part of the reminders and teachings that had reached the persons addressed.

مِنْ expresses part of a whole in this construction. Ḥaẓẓan therefore concerns a portion of what had been recalled, not necessarily all of it.

The distinction matters. “They forgot everything they had been reminded of” expands the statement’s scope without justification.

Ḥaẓẓ does not itself mean forgetting. نَسُوا expresses forgetting or neglecting, while ḥaẓẓan identifies the portion of reminders that was forgotten.

Covenant-breaking, distortion, and forgetting a portion of the reminders occur in one context, but must not all be treated as synonyms.

In particular, ḥaẓẓ alone cannot establish people’s intention or awareness. Whether conduct was deliberate, negligent, or involved concealment requires the verbs, qualifications, and other evidence.

Later in Al-Māʾidah 5:13, إِلَّا قَلِيلًا مِنْهُمْ occurs within the statement concerning treachery. The exception prevents extending that report to everyone, but should not automatically be treated as an exception to every preceding description, including forgetting.

Al-Ṭabarī, Al-Māʾidah 5:13: the exception belongs to the treachery clause

Al-Māʾidah 5:14: forgetting part of the covenant and reminders

وَمِنَ الَّذِينَ قَالُوا إِنَّا نَصَارَىٰ أَخَذْنَا مِيثَاقَهُمْ فَنَسُوا حَظًّا مِمَّا ذُكِّرُوا بِهِ فَأَغْرَيْنَا بَيْنَهُمُ الْعَدَاوَةَ وَالْبَغْضَاءَ إِلَىٰ يَوْمِ الْقِيَامَةِ وَسَوْفَ يُنَبِّئُهُمُ اللَّهُ بِمَا كَانُوا يَصْنَعُونَ

wa-mina alladhīna qālū innā naṣārā akhadhnā mīthāqahum fa-nasū ḥaẓẓan mimmā dhukkirū bihi fa-aghraynā baynahumu al-ʿadāwata wa-l-baghḍāʾa ilā yawmi al-qiyāmati wa-sawfa yunabbiʾuhumu allāhu bi-mā kānū yaṣnaʿūna

Translation: And from those who said, “We are Christians,” We also took their covenant. They then forgot a portion of what they had been reminded of, so We stirred enmity and hatred among them until the Day of Resurrection. God will inform them of what they used to do.

The same expression, فَنَسُوا حَظًّا مِمَّا ذُكِّرُوا بِهِ, appears here.

This time, the subject is a group describing themselves as Christians, and the verse discusses a covenant taken from them.

Ḥaẓẓ means the same in both verses: a portion of what they had been reminded of.

The consequences and contextual details nevertheless differ. Al-Māʾidah 5:13 also discusses covenant-breaking and distortion; Al-Māʾidah 5:14 emphasizes forgetting a portion of the reminders and the arising of enmity and hatred among them.

These usages show that ḥaẓẓ can refer to part of a nonmaterial whole. Teachings, reminders, and covenantal responsibilities are not physical assets, but the word can describe a portion of them.

The root’s meaning is therefore not restricted to shares of property or wealth.

Nor does ḥaẓẓ itself establish the size or precise content of the forgotten portion here. Understanding the subject requires considering the covenant and the teachings under discussion.

5. Ḥaẓẓ in worldly and afterlife provision

Āl ʿImrān 3:176: having no share in the Hereafter

وَلَا يَحْزُنْكَ الَّذِينَ يُسَارِعُونَ فِي الْكُفْرِ إِنَّهُمْ لَنْ يَضُرُّوا اللَّهَ شَيْئًا يُرِيدُ اللَّهُ أَلَّا يَجْعَلَ لَهُمْ حَظًّا فِي الْآخِرَةِ وَلَهُمْ عَذَابٌ عَظِيمٌ

wa-lā yaḥzunka alladhīna yusāriʿūna fī al-kufri innahum lan yaḍurrū allāha shayʾan yurīdu allāhu allā yajʿala lahum ḥaẓẓan fī al-ākhirati wa-lahum ʿadhābun ʿaẓīmun

Translation: Let those who hasten into disbelief not grieve you; they will not harm God at all. God wills to assign them no share in the Hereafter, and for them is a great punishment.

حَظًّا occurs here with يَجْعَلَ and فِي الْآخِرَةِ.

The subject is a portion assigned to people in the Hereafter.

In this context, ḥaẓẓ means a desirable portion of afterlife good. Its negation expresses deprivation of such a share.

The word’s meaning must nevertheless be distinguished from the reason for that deprivation. Ḥaẓẓ does not itself mean reward, Paradise, or punishment. The verse discusses the absence of a share in the Hereafter and a great punishment; identifying those concerned requires الَّذِينَ يُسَارِعُونَ فِي الْكُفْرِ and the context.

Ḥaẓẓ’s lexical meaning alone therefore cannot establish that the same judgment applies to every person with different beliefs or anyone who has not clearly recognized the truth.

The judgment’s scope is determined by the verse’s descriptions and conditions, rather than the root itself.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:79: a great portion in the eyes of worldly admirers

فَخَرَجَ عَلَىٰ قَوْمِهِ فِي زِينَتِهِ قَالَ الَّذِينَ يُرِيدُونَ الْحَيَاةَ الدُّنْيَا يَا لَيْتَ لَنَا مِثْلَ مَا أُوتِيَ قَارُونُ إِنَّهُ لَذُو حَظٍّ عَظِيمٍ

fa-kharaja ʿalā qawmihi fī zīnatihi qāla alladhīna yurīdūna al-ḥayāta al-dunyā yā layta lanā mithla mā ūtiya qārūnu innahu la-dhū ḥaẓẓin ʿaẓīmin

Translation: Then Qārūn came out before his people in his adornment and splendor. Those who desired worldly life said, “If only we had the like of what Qārūn has been given! He certainly possesses a great portion.”

ذُو حَظٍّ عَظِيمٍ occurs here in the speech of people captivated by Qārūn’s worldly advantages.

This is decisive for understanding the verse. Ḥaẓẓ ʿaẓīm here is not the Qur’an’s direct judgment of Qārūn’s ultimate worth. It is the statement of people who see his wealth and splendor and wish they possessed similar resources.

Ḥaẓẓ in this context concerns a large share of worldly wealth and resources.

That endowment does not itself establish moral superiority or God’s approval of Qārūn’s conduct.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:80, those given knowledge object to this outlook, declaring God’s reward better for people of faith and righteous action.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:81 then describes Qārūn’s fate. In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:82, the very people who had wished for his position reconsider their judgment.

This sequence shows that substantial worldly advantages do not in themselves measure someone’s ultimate success.

Ḥaẓẓ ʿaẓīm therefore has a favorable meaning from the speakers’ viewpoint, but what they consider great and desirable is reassessed as the account continues.

Al-Ṭabarī, Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:79: the speech of those wishing for Qārūn’s advantages

Fuṣṣilat 41:35: a great portion in perseverance and goodness

وَمَا يُلَقَّاهَا إِلَّا الَّذِينَ صَبَرُوا وَمَا يُلَقَّاهَا إِلَّا ذُو حَظٍّ عَظِيمٍ

wa-mā yulaqqāhā illā alladhīna ṣabarū wa-mā yulaqqāhā illā dhū ḥaẓẓin ʿaẓīmin

Translation: None receive it except those who have persevered, and none receive it except someone possessing a great portion.

ذُو حَظٍّ عَظِيمٍ appears again: the same expression that worldly admirers used for Qārūn in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:79.

The two verses’ contexts differ, however.

Fuṣṣilat 41:34 commands repelling evil by a better response, so that even enmity between two parties can become a close and friendly relationship.

Fuṣṣilat 41:35 attributes attainment of this conduct to those who have persevered and possess a great portion.

Ḥaẓẓ ʿaẓīm here therefore does not concern wealth and material splendor, but occurs in the context of a moral ability and virtue.

وَمَا يُلَقَّاهَا refers back to that good conduct and better way of repelling evil. The verse indicates that attaining this quality is not easy or universal and is connected to perseverance.

Early commentaries explain ḥaẓẓ ʿaẓīm here as an abundant share of good or afterlife reward. Some also interpret it as Paradise.

Al-Ṭabarī, Fuṣṣilat 41:35: a portion of good and the interpretation as Paradise

Lexically, however, ḥaẓẓ does not itself mean Paradise. Such an interpretation identifies the great portion’s referent rather than defining the word itself.

In context, it can be understood as a great endowment of virtue, moral ability, and good: an endowment expressed in conduct that may also have consequences in the Hereafter.

Nor should ḥaẓẓ be reduced to random luck here. The verse explicitly mentions الَّذِينَ صَبَرُوا, people who have persevered along the course being discussed.

An endowment of virtue is connected here with conduct and perseverance.

Comparing the two instances of dhū ḥaẓẓin ʿaẓīmin

Comparing Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:79 and Fuṣṣilat 41:35 provides particularly clear evidence for understanding this root.

The same construction, ذُو حَظٍّ عَظِيمٍ, occurs in both, but the endowment’s object differs.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ, a group considers Qārūn’s wealth a great portion. In Fuṣṣilat, substantial endowment occurs in the context of perseverance and responding well to evil.

ʿAẓīm alone therefore does not determine the kind of portion. A great share may concern wealth or moral good and virtue.

This shows that ḥaẓẓ does not necessarily mean wealth or an afterlife reward. The shared meaning is a share or endowment; context establishes what someone has been endowed with.

6. Distinctions from related roots

Ḥ Ẓ Ẓ (ḥaẓẓ) and N Ṣ B (naṣīb)

In the Qur’an, naṣīb concerns a share or part assigned to someone or belonging to them.

Ḥaẓẓ overlaps with it in this semantic field.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:11 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:176 use ḥaẓẓ for an inheritance share. Al-Nisāʾ 4:7 uses naṣīb for men’s and women’s inheritance shares.

These usages show that both words can concern a financial or legal share.

In Arabic, however, ḥaẓẓ also connects with fortune and favorable circumstances. Naṣīb, in its structure and usage, emphasizes an assigned share or a portion that reaches someone.

This is a tendency rather than an absolute distinction. In some contexts, both can be translated as a share or portion.

Ḥ Ẓ Ẓ (ḥaẓẓ) and Q S M (qasm)

Q S M concerns dividing and separating shares.

Ḥaẓẓ chiefly concerns the share or portion someone possesses.

Dividing property, for example, can establish a specified share for each heir. The act of division belongs to Q S M; the resulting share can be called ḥaẓẓ.

Ḥaẓẓ does not necessarily imply that a distributor is identified in the same sentence.

Likewise, where virtue or life’s resources are concerned, the word alone cannot establish the distribution’s kind or mechanism.

Ḥ Ẓ Ẓ (ḥaẓẓ) and R Z Q (rizq)

Rizq has a broad Qur’anic range concerning provision, giving, and what is placed at creatures’ disposal.

Ḥaẓẓ may be someone’s share of provision, but is not identical to provision itself.

Someone may possess financial resources and food, for example, and ḥaẓẓ can express the extent of their endowment.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:13 and Al-Māʾidah 5:14, however, ḥaẓẓ concerns a portion of divine reminders rather than material provision.

Not all its usages can therefore be restricted to economic provision or food.

Study of R Z Q

Ḥ Ẓ Ẓ (ḥaẓẓ) and J D D (jadd)

Early dictionaries explain ḥaẓẓ through jadd in the sense of fortune or favorable circumstances.

This differs from jadd meaning effort and seriousness. Identical written forms in Arabic do not make all their usages identical.

Dhū ḥaẓẓ emphasizes someone’s endowment. In ordinary language, this can be accompanied by the sense of fortune or good luck.

In the inheritance verses, however, the share follows a specified rule; in Fuṣṣilat 41:35, the great portion occurs in a context of virtue and perseverance.

Jadd meaning fortune can therefore supply one lexical explanation of ḥaẓẓ, but does not appropriately replace all its Qur’anic translations.

Ḥ Ẓ Ẓ (ḥaẓẓ) and Ḥ Ẓ W (ḥaẓwah)

حَظْوَة and حَظِيَ concern standing, closeness, and a favorable position with another person.

حظي عند فلان means enjoying a position of standing or favor with someone.

This family is semantically close to ḥaẓẓ, but should not be identified with ح ظ ظ in root analysis without investigation.

Ibn Fāris examines ح ظ و separately. Standing and closeness to someone influential are among its principal usages.

By contrast, ح ظ ظ in the Qur’an concerns a share or portion, whether financial, a part of reminders, or an endowment of virtue.

Semantic overlap between the families does not alone establish a direct, certain derivational relationship.

Early lexicons: ḥaẓwah and the distinction between related roots

Ḥ Ẓ Ẓ (ḥaẓẓ) and Ḥ Ẓ R (ḥaẓr)

Ḥaẓr concerns preventing and creating a barrier to access.

Ḥaẓẓ concerns a share or portion.

The roots share their first two letters but have different final letters and distinct semantic fields.

Someone may be prevented from receiving a share. Such a situation combines two separate concepts: a benefit that could have reached them and a barrier preventing its arrival.

Maḥẓūẓ therefore means favorably endowed, whereas maḥẓūr means prohibited or withheld. Their visual similarity must not cause mistranslation.

Study of Ḥ Ẓ R

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

For ḥaẓẓ in Al-Nisāʾ 4:11 and Al-Nisāʾ 4:176, “share” is the most appropriate Persian equivalent. These verses concern a specified inheritance amount; “the share of two daughters” is more precise than “the luck of two daughters.”

In Al-Māʾidah 5:13 and Al-Māʾidah 5:14, “a portion of what they had been reminded of” is natural and precise. Ḥaẓẓ here concerns part of a body of teachings and reminders, rather than wealth or luck.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:176, “a share in the Hereafter” is appropriate. It preserves the lexical meaning and leaves its referent to context without adding a particular instance such as Paradise or reward.

In Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:79, “possessing a great portion” is appropriate for dhū ḥaẓẓin ʿaẓīmin. Since admirers of Qārūn’s wealth are speaking, commentary can explain that they praise his substantial worldly advantages.

In Fuṣṣilat 41:35, “possessing a great portion” likewise preserves the literal sense. Its object in context, however, concerns virtue and moral perseverance. Commentary can speak of “a great portion of good and virtue.”

The base translation should not replace ḥaẓẓ ʿaẓīm with “abundant wealth” in Al-Qaṣaṣ or “Paradise” in Fuṣṣilat: these identify instances or contextual interpretations rather than the word’s direct meaning.

Nor are “chance” and “good luck” suitable for translating every Qur’anic occurrence. Today, these expressions can suggest accident or randomness, whereas Qur’anic ḥaẓẓ also concerns legal shares, moral endowment, and afterlife outcomes.

For Forqan, the principal Persian equivalents should be “share” and “portion,” chosen according to context.

Conclusion

The Arabic root ح ظ ظ concerns a share, portion, or endowment. In early language, the family also connects with fortune and favorable circumstances; recorded forms include ḥuẓūẓ, ḥaẓīẓ, maḥẓūẓ, and aḥaẓẓ.

Only the noun ḥaẓẓ occurs in the Qur’an: twice for inheritance shares, twice for forgotten portions of reminders, once for deprivation of a share in the Hereafter, and twice for a great endowment.

Comparing dhū ḥaẓẓin ʿaẓīmin in Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:79 and Fuṣṣilat 41:35 shows that a portion’s greatness does not necessarily establish its moral worth. In Al-Qaṣaṣ, worldly admirers consider Qārūn’s wealth a great portion; in Fuṣṣilat, the great endowment occurs in the context of perseverance and responding well to evil.

The inheritance usages also show that ḥaẓẓ need not concern luck or accident. A share may follow a specified rule. The Al-Māʾidah usages show that the word is not exclusive to property and material possessions.

Brief root meaning: A share or portion that reaches someone or is assigned to them. It may be material, legal, spiritual, or related to the Hereafter, and may vary in amount and value.

The word itself neither establishes the cause of that portion’s arrival, proves its recipient’s worthiness, nor guarantees that worldly prosperity signals ultimate success.

Precise translation requires identifying what the ḥaẓẓ is a share of, whom it belongs to, how it functions in the sentence, and who is speaking. Preserving these distinctions lets “share” and “portion” convey the root’s main meaning appropriately in Qur’anic usage.
