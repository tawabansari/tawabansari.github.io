Pressing, pinching, signalling with the eyes or eyebrows, fault-finding, and covert disparagement

The Arabic root غ م ز concerns pressing something, signalling with the eyes or eyebrows, and finding fault. Physical غَمَزَ means applying pressure with a hand or finger. As a gesture, it describes moving the eye, eyelid, or eyebrow to communicate. Socially, it can mean criticizing, finding fault with, or belittling someone.

غَمْز names pressing or signalling; غَمْزَة a particular pressure or gesture; غَمَّاز someone who targets others through gestures or fault-finding; and مَغْمَز a point vulnerable to criticism.

These uses are related but distinct. Finger pressure, a communicative wink, and a disparaging gesture are different acts. Sentence structure and circumstances establish the precise meaning.

The root occurs once in the Quran, as يَتَغَامَزُونَ in Mutaffifin 83:30. This Form VI verb concerns people signalling to one another with their eyes or similar movements.

Here the gestures mock and belittle believers. But تَغَامُز should not be restricted to mockery: in other circumstances, eye or eyebrow signals can communicate, attract attention, warn, or express understanding.

1. Meaning in early Arabic

Ghamz: pressing with the hand

A familiar use of غَمَزَ is pressing something with the hand.

Someone might press a bodily area with a finger to examine tenderness or its condition.

Arabic can describe that act with غَمَزَ.

Related forms also describe pressing or kneading parts of the body.

Pressing another person's back muscles, for instance, may be called ghamz.

This need not mean striking hard or injuring. Pressure may be light and controlled, intended to examine or relieve stiffness.

Ghamz should therefore not always be understood as harm or a painful pinch.

Conversely, it is not merely surface contact: the pressing sense involves pressure on the area concerned.

Lexicons record غَمَزْتُ الشَّيْءَ بِيَدِي for this act.

They also describe examining an animal's condition by applying pressure with the hand.

The root therefore has a tangible physical meaning independent of mockery or fault-finding.

An early witness: pressing and testing a spear

Ziyad al-Aʿjam says:

وَكُنْتُ إِذَا غَمَزْتُ قَنَاةَ قَوْمٍ

wa-kuntu idhā ghamaztu qanāta qawmin

كَسَرْتُ كُعُوبَهَا أَوْ تَسْتَقِيمُ

kasartu kuʿūbahā aw tastaqīmu

Translation:

“Whenever I pressed a people's spear-shaft, I broke its joints or made it straighten.”

قَنَاة refers to a spear or its shaft.

غَمَزْتُ supplies the physical image of pressure applied to test or alter something.

The poet uses this to assert his strength against opponents: pressure reveals weakness and breaks their shaft, or forces it straight.

Ghamz does not mean winking here; physical pressure supplies the figurative image.

Early grammatical and lexical sources transmit the verse with different endings: تَسْتَقِيمُ in some versions, and the subjunctive تَسْتَقِيمَ, written تَسْتَقِيمَا with the rhyme alif, in others.

Ziyad al-Aʿjam belongs to the early Islamic and Umayyad period. This is post-Quranic Arabic evidence, not pre-Islamic poetry.

It confirms the pressing sense and illustrates its figurative extension to confrontation and testing strength.

Ghamz: signalling with the eye, eyelid, or eyebrow

Another important branch concerns eye signals and subtle facial movements.

غَمَزَهُ بِعَيْنِهِ and غَمَزَ بِجَفْنِهِ concern signalling with the eye or eyelid.

Someone in a gathering may want to draw a companion's attention without speaking.

They may make a brief eye or eyebrow movement.

If the companion understands, a message passes without spoken words.

Arabic can call this ghamz.

The movement is small, but its purpose is communicating meaning.

It may merely attract attention.

It may warn.

It may express agreement or mutual understanding.

Or it may ridicule and belittle a third person.

The movement alone does not establish intention.

Ghamz as fault-finding

Social غَمَزَ فُلَانًا can describe finding fault with or belittling someone.

Physical pressure is not intended here.

A gesture, word, or other action may attribute a fault or seek to lower someone's standing.

Someone listening to a speaker might wink at a companion to suggest that the speaker is ignorant or worthless.

The eye gesture then accompanies fault-finding and disparagement.

Someone may also seek weaknesses in another's argument to criticize it.

غَمِيزَة and مَغْمَز accordingly denote a fault or vulnerable point.

A genuine flaw must nevertheless be distinguished from disparaging fault-finding.

Criticism may be sound and supported, or a person may seek groundless pretexts to undermine someone.

غَمْز alone does not establish whether an alleged fault is real.

An early witness: ghamiza in Hassan ibn Thabit

A poem attributed to Hassan ibn Thabit reads:

وَأَنْ لَيْسَ لِلْأَعْدَاءِ عِنْدِي غَمِيزَةٌ

wa-an laysa li-al-aʿdāʾi ʿindī ghamīzatun

وَلَا طَافَ لِي مِنْهُمْ بِوَحْشِيَ صَائِدُ

wa-lā ṭāfa lī minhum bi-waḥshiya ṣāʾidu

Translation:

“My enemies have no fault against me, nor has any hunter among them circled to catch me unawares.”

غَمِيزَة denotes a fault or weakness an enemy could exploit for disparagement.

The poet praises and defends himself, claiming that enemies have found no fault in him.

Early poetry thus attests the family for faults and criticism as well as eye signals and hand pressure.

Hassan ibn Thabit wrote before and after Islam. The verse is transmitted under his name, and lexical editions compare their wording with the collected poems attributed to him.

Its precise date is not independently certain. Some versions begin فَمَا وَجَدَ الْأَعْدَاءُ فِيَّ غَمِيزَةً.

It is useful evidence for early غميزة, but the poet's identity alone does not prove that this particular verse is pre-Islamic.

2. Semantic branches and derivatives

A. Local pressure and its effect

Physical ghamz concerns pressure on a particular spot.

One might press a patch of skin with a finger.

The pressure may examine, knead, or draw someone's attention.

In each case the act of pressing is distinct from its purpose.

Pressure may also leave a small mark or alter a surface.

A connection with impressions or shallow indentations is therefore conceivable.

It does not establish the history of every derivative.

B. Brief signals communicating a message

The signalling sense involves the eye, eyelid, eyebrow, or sometimes the hand.

Two people may be sitting together; a brief eyebrow movement tells one that it is time to leave.

Others may not understand, while the signal has a definite meaning between them.

Ghamz here means signalling.

The gesture is not necessarily mockery, deception, or fault-finding.

Its message may be ordinary or useful.

C. Criticism and identifying weakness

Social ghamz may involve fault-finding and diminishing another's credibility.

Rather than examining useful speech, someone may point to the speaker's accent, clothes, or personal characteristics to provoke laughter.

The gesture becomes a means of belittling the person.

Yet not every criticism is disparaging ghamz.

Showing a specific factual or behavioural error with evidence is not inherently mockery or malice.

Manner, purpose, and evidence distinguish sound criticism from humiliating fault-finding.

D. A weakness or fault

غَمِيزَة also names a flaw or weakness.

An argument may contain a defect that a critic can demonstrate.

Such a vulnerable point can be called a مَغْمَز.

A flaw does not make a person's entire argument or character worthless.

One part of an argument may be mistaken while others are correct.

Applied to a person, غميز can denote weakness of judgment.

This approaches the fault sense but should not turn limited understanding into moral corruption or deliberate deceit.

E. Impaired movement in animals

Lexicons record غَمَزَتِ الدَّابَّةُ for an animal limping or moving weakly.

Some explanations specify a slight limp.

A horse may place one leg differently, showing weakness or discomfort in walking.

This concerns movement, not an eye signal or social criticism.

Its connection with pressure and local weakness can be examined, but resemblance alone cannot establish its history.

Important forms

Established forms include:

  • غَمَزَ / يَغْمِزُ: pressed; signalled with the eye or eyebrow; socially, found fault.
  • غَمْز: pressing, signalling, or fault-finding, depending on context.
  • غَمْزَة: a particular pressure or signal, especially a wink.
  • غَمَّاز: someone who signals frequently or targets others through gestures and criticism.
  • غَمَّازَة: feminine of غماز, with uses concerning gestures and pressing bodily parts.
  • تَغَامَزَ / يَتَغَامَزُ: exchanged / exchanges signals or winks.
  • تَغَامُز: exchanging signals, especially with the eyes.
  • مُتَغَامِز: a participant in such signalling.
  • أَغْمَزَ: socially, belittled someone or exploited their weakness.
  • اِغْتَمَزَ: found or criticized a fault in someone's words or conduct.
  • غَمِيزَة: a fault or weakness.
  • غَمِيز: weak or deficient, depending on use.
  • مَغْمَز: a point open to criticism.
  • مَغَامِز: faults or vulnerable points.
  • مَغْمُوز: criticized or attributed a fault.
  • غَمُوز: of a she-camel, having a little hump fat detectable by hand pressure.

Only يَتَغَامَزُونَ occurs in the Quran.

غَمْز, غَمْزَة, غَمَّاز, غَمِيزَة, مَغْمَز, and the other forms help establish the lexical field but do not independently occur there.

3. The morphology of يَتَغَامَزُونَ

يَتَغَامَزُونَ is a Form VI imperfect verb.

Its perfect is تَغَامَزَ and verbal noun تَغَامُز.

The Quranic form is third-person masculine plural.

The role of Form VI

Form VI can express participation or reciprocal action between several parties.

That fits people signalling to one another in تَغَامُز.

Three people may be sitting together when a fourth enters; one signals with the eyes and another responds with a facial movement.

Nonverbal communication takes place between them.

The form does not prove that everyone signals equally, simultaneously, or identically.

Some may signal while others respond with a glance, smile, or brief reaction.

Nor does the form itself establish an intention to mock.

Two people may exchange eye signals to warn of danger without belittling anyone.

In Mutaffifin 83:30, mockery emerges from the surrounding verses.

Distinguishing غَمَزَ and تَغَامَزَ

غَمَزَ may describe one person's act, such as an eye signal or hand pressure.

تَغَامَزَ, used of people, describes signalling among them.

One person's wink to a companion can be described with غمز.

When several exchange messages through looks and gestures, تغامز is more appropriate.

This clarifies the Quranic verb's communicative character.

The verse concerns people exchanging signals in the presence of, or while passing, another group rather than one isolated person.

4. يَتَغَامَزُونَ in the Quran

Mutaffifin 83:30

وَإِذَا مَرُّوا بِهِمْ يَتَغَامَزُونَ

wa-idhā marrū bihim yataghāmazūn

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:30

“And when they passed by them, they would wink at one another.”

The passage describes the conduct of wrongdoers toward those who believed.

Mutaffifin 83:29 says that those who committed wrongdoing used to laugh at believers.

Mutaffifin 83:30 introduces another action: winking at one another.

Mutaffifin 83:31 describes their return to their families or associates in a merry state.

Mutaffifin 83:32 says they called believers misguided when they saw them.

Mutaffifin 83:33 reminds them that they were not sent as guardians over the believers.

These verses portray disparagement through laughter, signals, amusement, and accusations of misguidance against those being mocked.

Winking to belittle

Ordinarily a wink can express understanding, warning, humour, or a request for attention.

Here those winking are the people who laughed at believers in the preceding verse.

The next verse also describes their merriment on returning to their families.

The gestures are therefore mocking and disparaging in context.

A student may enter a classroom while classmates exchange eye or eyebrow signals, laugh, and mock them without addressing them directly.

The target may not fully understand, while those brief gestures reinforce the others' shared disparagement.

A simple signal becomes a means of belittling another person.

This illustrates something close to the Quranic use of يتغامزون.

If the same students use eye signals for ordinary coordination, the gesture alone is not blameworthy.

In Mutaffifin 83:30, signalling is the verb's meaning; mockery characterizes their conduct in context.

Who passes whom?

وَإِذَا مَرُّوا بِهِمْ contains two plural pronouns.

Grammatically, the subject of مَرُّوا can be the wrongdoers, with بِهِمْ referring to believers.

The meaning is then that the wrongdoers winked at one another when passing believers.

Another possible reading has believers passing the wrongdoers, who signal to each other on seeing them.

In either reading, the context identifies the mockers as those exchanging gestures.

The difference in who passes whom does not change يتغامزون's basic meaning.

The subject is disparaging signals exchanged about believers.

Must taghamuz be secret?

Eye and eyebrow signals differ from loud speech and can pass between people who understand them.

تغامز alone does not prove that nobody else notices.

Two people may signal openly, and their target may fully understand the mockery.

The communication is nonverbal without necessarily remaining hidden.

“Covert signalling” can suit some contexts, but complete secrecy is not essential to the root.

Laughter versus taghamuz

Mutaffifin 83:29 uses يَضْحَكُونَ.

Mutaffifin 83:30 uses يَتَغَامَزُونَ.

The verbs are not identical.

Laughter can be an overt reaction.

Taghamuz describes signals exchanged between people.

Someone may laugh at another without winking at a companion.

Others may belittle someone through brief gestures without audible laughter.

The passage combines both, showing mockery through laughter and nonverbal signals.

This does not make every laugh in the Quran or daily life mockery.

Its value depends on cause, object, and context.

Accusing another of misguidance

In Mutaffifin 83:32, the same group calls the believers misguided.

Their mockery thus accompanies a disparaging judgment about others' beliefs and path.

It is that group's claim, not the Quran's endorsed judgment of believers.

People may imagine that wealth, power, or social standing makes anyone who disagrees ignorant or misguided.

They may then use looks, laughter, and derogatory labels to silence others.

Mockery can become a means of social pressure.

This does not mean that everyone who considers another belief mistaken has committed taghamuz.

A belief can be criticized respectfully with evidence.

The passage addresses disparagement and mocking judgment, not a prohibition of all disagreement.

5. Fault-finding and social disparagement

Signals understood by companions

Taghamuz can communicate without a direct statement.

In a meeting, two people may exchange a brief look and mocking smile to show they take a speaker unseriously.

Neither may explicitly call the speaker ignorant, yet their conduct conveys it.

The gesture communicates; the situation and purpose establish the contempt.

Modern equivalents can take other forms, such as posting a mocking image or symbol in a group conversation while someone speaks.

These are contemporary examples of disparaging communication, not direct references of the Quranic verb to modern writing or devices.

Fault-finding versus sound criticism

Lexical ghamz can concern finding fault with another.

But identifying a fault is not always humiliating.

If someone miscalculates and a colleague respectfully demonstrates the mistake, that can help correct it.

If the colleague instead winks and laughs to make the person seem worthless, the conduct is different.

The first may seek understanding and correction.

The second may aim at, or result in, belittling the person and damaging their standing.

Reasoned criticism and humiliating fault-finding are therefore distinct despite apparent similarity.

The word غميزة for a fault does not prove every allegation true.

Someone may invent a flaw or exaggerate a minor matter to humiliate another.

The truth of a claim and the manner of stating it are distinguishable questions.

6. Distinctions from related roots

غ م ز versus ر م ز: signalling and communication

ر م ز concerns conveying meaning through signs rather than ordinary speech.

Al Imran 3:41 uses رَمْزًا for Zakariyya's communication with people.

There, gestures communicate rather than belittle.

Ghamz too can convey meaning through eyes, eyelids, or eyebrows.

The families are close in their signalling uses.

But ghamz also includes pressure and fault-finding.

In Mutaffifin 83:30 signalling accompanies mockery; in Al Imran 3:41 it communicates a message.

Signalling is therefore not inherently mocking or wrong.

غ م ز versus ه م ز: disparaging gestures and fault-finding

ه م ز appears in Quranic forms such as هَمَّاز and هُمَزَة.

It concerns fault-finding, disparagement, and diminishing others' reputation.

Some lexical uses of ghamz approach that field.

Yet يتغامزون in Mutaffifin 83:30 specifically describes people exchanging signals.

هَمَّاز in Qalam 68:11 describes a habitual fault-finder or disparager.

Their semantic closeness does not make يتغامزون the same grammatical form or full meaning as هماز.

Nor do their two shared final letters make them the same root.

غ م ز versus ل م ز: signalling and criticism

ل م ز in the Quran concerns finding fault, criticizing, or disparaging others.

Hujurat 49:11 prohibits تَلْمِزُوا.

Fault-finding appears there alongside mockery and offensive nicknames.

Social ghamz too may accompany contempt and criticism.

But taghamuz in Mutaffifin focuses on signals exchanged between people, while lamz in Hujurat concerns fault-finding itself.

The behaviours can coincide.

Someone might wink at a companion to make another's trait a subject of laughter.

There is then both a signal and fault-finding.

Not every signal is lamz, however, nor does every lamz involve a wink.

غ م ز versus س خ ر: gestures and mockery

سخرية concerns ridiculing and belittling someone.

Ghamz as signalling can be a means of mockery.

The context of Mutaffifin 83:30 establishes that relationship.

Mockery, however, takes many forms.

Imitating a voice, insulting speech, laughter, or other behaviour may ridicule without winking.

Conversely, a wink can serve ordinary communication without ridicule.

Mockery is thus one possible use of a gesture, not the necessary meaning of every ghamz.

غ م ز versus ن ب ز: gestures and derogatory names

ن ب ز occurs in Hujurat 49:11.

The verse prohibits calling one another offensive nicknames.

Taghamuz in Mutaffifin describes signalling among people.

Tanabuz in Hujurat concerns derogatory naming.

People might exchange winks upon seeing someone, then call them a nickname devised to humiliate them.

These behaviours can coexist but differ lexically.

One foregrounds nonverbal signals.

The other foregrounds offensive names.

غ م ز versus ض ح ك: winking and laughter

ضحك concerns laughing in the Quran.

Ghamz can be a brief eye or facial signal.

They occur in successive verses, Mutaffifin 83:29 and Mutaffifin 83:30.

Their combination shows distinct behaviours sharing a purpose.

Here that purpose is mocking believers.

Elsewhere Quranic laughter may express joy or another reaction.

Every laugh or wink should not be equated lexically or ethically with mockery.

غ م ز versus غ م ض: signalling and closing the eyes

غ م ض concerns closing or partly closing the eyes, and in other uses overlooking or letting something pass.

Signalling ghamz involves eye or eyelid movement to communicate.

The roots look similar and both concern the eye, but remain distinct.

Someone may close their eyes from fatigue without communicating anything.

Another may briefly move an eyelid to signal a companion.

تُغْمِضُوا in Baqarah 2:267 belongs to غ م ض, not غ م ز.

Phonetic similarity must not erase the distinction.

7. Questions of interpretation and translation

Does غمز always mean a wink?

No.

Arabic ghamz can mean hand pressure, eye or eyebrow signals, or fault-finding.

Eye signalling fits Mutaffifin 83:30 because of its context of group behaviour and mockery.

That sense should not be imposed on every non-Quranic use.

Does يتغامزون mean laughing?

Not exactly.

It concerns people exchanging signals.

The preceding verse expresses laughter with يضحكون.

Both may serve mockery here, but they are lexically distinct.

Is taghamuz necessarily malicious?

No.

Exchanged signals can serve different purposes.

In Mutaffifin 83:30, the surrounding verses show gestures disparaging believers.

Its wrongness emerges from this specific context.

Is taghamuz necessarily secret?

Eye signals are usually subtler than explicit speech.

But the verb does not establish that nobody notices them.

The target may see and understand them.

Complete secrecy is therefore not an essential meaning.

Is taghamuz the same as fault-finding?

In Mutaffifin the gestures accompany disparagement.

But taghamuz itself describes a kind of signalling and communication.

Fault-finding is one social use, not the meaning of every form in the family.

Is every criticism or disagreement ghamz?

No.

Reasoned criticism, examination of error, and disagreement should not be equated with disparaging gestures or groundless fault-finding.

Someone may respectfully reject an argument with evidence.

Another may ignore its content and use laughter and gestures to discredit the speaker.

Their purposes, methods, and results differ.

Is disparaging signalling wrong only against believers?

The targets in Mutaffifin are those who believed.

That specifies the immediate context.

Hujurat 49:11 also instructs believers not to mock others, find fault, or use derogatory nicknames.

A person's religious label alone cannot make mockery and humiliation right or wrong.

This ethical connection should not change the lexical meaning of ghamz or condemn every gesture in every situation.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

In Mutaffifin 83:30, “they would wink at one another” is a precise, natural equivalent for يَتَغَامَزُونَ.

The full verse:

“And when they passed by them, they would wink at one another.”

This preserves the gestural action.

Contextual explanation can clarify that the winks mocked and belittled believers.

Rendering it only as “they mocked the believers” conveys the contextual intention but loses the specific eye gesture. “They mocked one another” would also misidentify the targets.

Preserving “would wink at one another” is therefore preferable.

For non-Quranic uses:

غَمَزَ: pressed; signalled with the eye; socially, found fault.

غَمْز: pressing, signalling, or fault-finding, according to context.

غَمْزَة: a pressure or wink.

غَمَّاز: a frequent signaller; socially, a fault-finder or disparager.

تَغَامَزَ: exchanged winks or communicated through gestures.

تَغَامُز: winking or signalling among people.

مُتَغَامِز: someone participating in exchanged signals.

اِغْتَمَزَ: found fault or identified a vulnerable point.

غَمِيزَة: a flaw or weakness.

مَغْمَز: a point open to criticism.

مَغَامِز: faults or vulnerable points.

مَغْمُوز: criticized or attributed weakness.

Translation must distinguish physical pressure, nonverbal signalling, fault-finding, and mockery.

They belong to one lexical family but cannot all receive one fixed equivalent.

Conclusion

غ م ز has several familiar Arabic uses: hand pressure, eye or eyebrow signals, and finding faults or weaknesses.

Its physical sense involves pressure on a specific spot.

Its gestural sense involves eye, eyelid, or eyebrow movement to communicate.

Socially, it can serve disparagement and humiliation.

Early evidence, including Hassan ibn Thabit's attributed poem, attests غميزة for a flaw. Ziyad al-Aʿjam's verse preserves pressure in an image of testing and bending a spear.

The exact history of all branches is unclear. Eye signals and fault-finding cannot confidently be dated as developments from physical pressing in a particular period.

The Quran uses only يَتَغَامَزُونَ, in Mutaffifin 83:30.

This Form VI verb describes eye signals exchanged among people mocking believers.

The preceding verse describes laughter at believers; the following verses describe returning merrily and calling believers misguided.

Winking therefore serves ridicule and disparagement here.

But not every wink or nonverbal signal is wrong: gestures can communicate, warn, or coordinate.

Humiliating fault-finding also differs from reasoned criticism. Someone may demonstrate an error to correct it, or ignore truth and seek only to belittle another.

Established semantic branches of غ م ز include local pressure, brief signalling with eyes or other body parts, and finding fault with or belittling another.

The Quranic يَتَغَامَزُونَ specifically means: they signalled to one another with their eyes or similar gestures; here, to mock and belittle believers.

“They would wink at one another” preserves the word's image, while the explanation of mockery belongs in contextual reflection.
