Binding and securing, persistence, bitter cold, and shrill sound: a family with several semantic branches

The Arabic root Ṣ R R has several branches involving binding and securing, holding to a decision, intense cold, shrill or loud sound, and certain uses involving compactness and gathering.

Within this family, ṣarra is used for binding and securing; aṣarra for persisting and refusing to desist; ṣirr for severe, damaging cold; ṣarrah for a loud cry or gathered group; and ṣarīr for a prolonged, shrill sound.

This root occurs six times in six Qur’anic verses: ṣirrun in Āl ʿImrān 3:117, yuṣirrū in Āl ʿImrān 3:135, yuṣirru in Al-Jāthiyah 45:8, ṣarratin in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:29, yuṣirrūna in Al-Wāqiʿah 56:46, and aṣarrū in Nūḥ 71:7.

These uses fall into three main areas: bitter cold, a loud cry, and persistence in conduct or a position.

A key distinction is that binding something and keeping it secure can have a semantic connection with persistence, but intense cold and shrill sound cannot be derived from that initial image without sufficient historical evidence.

Ibn Fāris likewise identifies several separate semantic principles for this family in Maqāyīs al-Lughah. Careful study must therefore preserve the distinctions between its branches and identify each form’s meaning from its structure and actual use.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: six occurrences in three derived forms

1. Binding, securing, and holding firmly

Ṣarra: binding something securely

One established Arabic use of this root is binding and securing.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah records:

صَرَّ الدَّرَاهِمَ يَصُرُّهَا صَرًّا

ṣarra al-darāhima yaṣurruhā ṣarran

That is, he gathered the coins in a cloth and tied it.

Here ṣarra describes gathering several things inside a covering and fastening it so that its contents do not fall out or scatter.

From the same family, ṣurrah, with ḍammah on ṣād, names a bundle or bag containing money or other things.

Persian also uses ṣurrah for a money bag or bundle.

Ṣurrah itself does not necessarily mean money: it may be a bag holding money. Its material and contents depend on the particular use.

Ṣirār: binding an animal’s udder

In Arab animal husbandry, ṣirār named a band tied around a she-camel’s or another milk animal’s udder to prevent its young from suckling or the milk from escaping.

Lisān al-ʿArab records ṣarra al-nāqata for this action.

An animal whose udder has been bound in this way is called maṣrūrah or muṣarrarah.

The material action is clear: the band blocks the release of milk or restricts the young animal’s access to it.

This example does not mean that all uses of Ṣ R R involve blocking milk or preventing a substance from escaping.

It is one instance of the branch involving binding and securing.

Gathering and compactness

Arabic sources also record ṣarrah for a group of people or animals gathered together.

Ibn Fāris discusses this use alongside binding and gathering.

The relevant feature is the gathering and closeness of the parts, without necessarily involving an actual band or rope.

Ḥāfirun maṣrūrun is also recorded for a contracted hoof.

In one lexical use, ṣurṣūr also names a large herd of camels.

These forms show that gathering, compactness, and contraction occur within the Ṣ R R family.

Nevertheless, a herd’s gathering and the tying of a money bag are different actions. Both involve things coming together, but this similarity alone cannot establish a historical path of derivation.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: binding, groups, a contracted hoof, and a camel herd

2. Iṣrār: persisting and refusing to abandon a position

From secure binding to holding to a decision

Iṣrār comes from aṣarra. In established Arabic usage it describes persisting, continuing, and refusing to abandon a decision or conduct.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah records:

الْإِصْرَارُ: الثَّبَاتُ عَلَى الشَّيْءِ

al-iṣrāru al-thabātu ʿalā al-shayʾi

That is, iṣrār means remaining firm upon something.

Ibn Fāris connects this use with binding and securing, since both involve firmness and holding on.

The connection is understandable: just as something securely tied does not readily come undone, a persistent person does not relinquish a position or behavior.

This image does not mean that Arabic speakers necessarily imagined tying a bag or knotting a rope whenever they used aṣarra.

The behavioral meaning of iṣrār is independently established in Arabic.

Is persistence always blameworthy?

No.

In Arabic a person can persist in either a right or a wrong decision.

Lexicons explain aṣarra ʿalā al-amri as remaining firm and not desisting from something.

They also record aṣarra ʿalā al-dhanbi for remaining in sin and not abandoning it.

In the second expression the object of persistence is sin, but aṣarra by itself does not mean committing a sin.

This distinction matters because all the Qur’anic verbal uses occur in contexts condemning persistence in wrongdoing.

That feature of the Qur’anic uses does not make all persistence inherently blameworthy throughout Arabic.

The basic meaning of iṣrār is remaining firm and not desisting; its moral value depends on the object of persistence and the context.

Persistence and continuing

Aṣarra ʿalā is not limited to holding a firm belief in the mind.

The verb can describe continuing conduct that a person refuses to abandon.

A passing decision therefore differs from persistence.

Iṣrār generally highlights maintaining a position, continuing conduct, or refusing to turn back.

It does not necessarily imply repeating an action a specified number of times.

A person may repeatedly do wrong, or may continue defending a single past action and refuse to correct it.

Depending on context, either situation can involve persistence.

Iṣrār should therefore not be defined solely by the number of times a sin is repeated.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Al-Mufradāt: persistence and firmness

3. Severe, damaging cold

Ṣirr: cold and frost

Another branch of this family is ṣirr, with kasrah on ṣād.

Lisān al-ʿArab records:

الصِّرُّ: شِدَّةُ الْبَرْدِ

al-ṣirru shiddatu al-bardi

That is, ṣirr means intense cold.

Another definition applies the word to cold that damages plants.

Rīḥun ṣirrun and rīḥun fīhā ṣirrun are also recorded in Arabic for a wind containing intense cold.

This use relates to agricultural experience: severe cold can damage plants, blossoms, and crops.

The word alone does not identify an exact temperature or a particular kind of frost.

Ṣirr in this branch means bitter cold; its effects may vary with conditions.

Is ṣirr also used for intense heat?

Alongside bitter cold, Ibn Fāris records uses involving intense heat and thirst.

He notes that some lexicographers use al-ṣārrah for the intensity of the sun’s heat.

Other sources also record al-ṣārrah and al-ṣarīrah for thirst.

These witnesses show that the Ṣ R R family includes branches involving harsh conditions of heat and cold.

They do not mean that every occurrence of ṣirr simultaneously expresses the opposite meanings of cold and heat.

Each construction needs its own evidence.

In the Qur’anic rīḥin fīhā ṣirrun, intense cold has clearer lexical and contextual support.

Does ṣirr mean only the sound of wind?

Some lexical explanations also record intense sound as a meaning of ṣirr.

A strong wind can produce a sharp, sustained sound, making the proximity of the two branches understandable.

Sound and cold nevertheless remain distinct qualities.

A wind may be cold without much sound, or very noisy without being cold.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:117, the wind’s destructive quality matters in an agricultural comparison. Bitter cold is therefore the more direct and suitable meaning.

The auditory reading can be documented in a lexical study, but should not be selected as the verse’s certain and sole meaning.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: cold, fire, and the auditory reading

4. Sound and a loud cry

Ṣarīr: a prolonged, shrill sound

Arabic also uses ṣarra for the sound made by certain things.

Early sources record:

صَرَّ الْجُنْدَبُ صَرِيرًا

ṣarra al-jundabu ṣarīran

That is, the jundub insect made a prolonged, shrill sound.

Ṣarra al-bābu is also recorded for a creaking door, and ṣarra al-qalamu for the sound of a pen as it writes.

In these uses ṣarīr names a sound that may be sharp, drawn out, and sustained.

It need not be very loud, since a pen’s sound or a door’s creak may be quiet.

The sound branch of Ṣ R R is therefore not confined to intense shouting.

Other forms in the family, such as ṣarrah, can refer to a loud, sudden cry.

Ṣarrah: a loud cry

Ibn Fāris writes of this branch:

الصَّرَّةُ: شِدَّةُ الصِّيَاحِ

al-ṣarratu shiddatu al-ṣiyāḥi

That is, ṣarrah means intense crying out.

Lisān al-ʿArab likewise records ṣarrah for clamor, a loud shout, and a cry produced by a person or animal.

Unlike a pen’s quiet sound, the intensity and audibility of the cry are prominent here.

The word itself does not necessarily identify joy, fear, pain, surprise, or anger as the cause.

The cause of the cry must be determined from context.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: ṣarīr and intense crying out

5. A pre-Islamic poetic witness: ṣarrah in Imruʾ al-Qays’s Muʿallaqah

Imruʾ al-Qays’s Muʿallaqah includes:

فَأَلْحَقَنَا بِالْهَادِيَاتِ وَدُونَهُ
جَوَاحِرُهَا فِي صَرَّةٍ لَمْ تَزَيَّلِ

fa-alḥaqanā bi-al-hādiyāti wa-dūnahu / jawāḥiruhā fī ṣarratin lam tazayyali

A close rendering of the sense: “The horse brought us up to the leaders of those animals, while their stragglers were still gathered in a group and had not dispersed.”

In this part of the Muʿallaqah the poet describes his horse’s speed and ability in pursuing wild animals.

Al-hādiyāt are the animals in front, while jawāḥiruhā are those lagging behind.

Lam tazayyali also refers to their not dispersing.

Several lexical explanations have been offered for ṣarrah in this line.

In one reading it means a gathered group: the animals remained together and had not scattered.

In another reading it refers to the animals’ clamor and sound.

Some early commentaries also mention intense movement and agitation within the group.

These differences show that, depending on the feature in view, the word in this poem can concern either a group or sound.

Lam tazayyali clearly supports gathering and non-dispersal here, although early commentaries also record the auditory meaning.

The witness matters because both the group and sound branches were known in early Arabic.

The existence of this line alone does not establish that ṣarrah in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:29 must mean a group: the verse has a different context.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: Imruʾ al-Qays’s witness and the group meaning

6. Qur’anic uses of the root

Āl ʿImrān 3:117: rīḥin fīhā ṣirrun

مَثَلُ مَا يُنْفِقُونَ فِي هَٰذِهِ الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا كَمَثَلِ رِيحٍ فِيهَا صِرٌّ أَصَابَتْ حَرْثَ قَوْمٍ ظَلَمُوا أَنْفُسَهُمْ فَأَهْلَكَتْهُ وَمَا ظَلَمَهُمُ اللَّهُ وَلَٰكِنْ أَنْفُسَهُمْ يَظْلِمُونَ

mathalu mā yunfiqūna fī hādhihi al-ḥayāti al-dunyā ka-mathali rīḥin fīhā ṣirrun aṣābat ḥartha qawmin ẓalamū anfusahum fa-ahlakathu wa-mā ẓalamahumu allāhu wa-lākin anfusahum yaẓlimūna

Translation: The likeness of what they spend in this worldly life is that of a wind containing bitter cold: it strikes the crops of people who have wronged themselves and destroys them. The God has not wronged them; rather, they wrong themselves.

Here ṣirrun is an indefinite noun in the expression rīḥin fīhā ṣirrun.

Rīḥ names the wind, while ṣirr expresses a particular quality of it.

The predominant lexical and exegetical explanation identifies that quality as severe, damaging cold.

Why does bitter cold matter in this comparison?

The verse describes a wind reaching a field and destroying its crops.

Plants need suitable growing conditions, and severe cold can destroy a crop by damaging plant tissue.

The connection between bitter cold and the field’s destruction is therefore clear in this comparison.

Ṣirr should not, however, be translated directly as “destroying crops.”

Ṣirr expresses the wind’s quality; aṣābat its reaching the crops; and fa-ahlakathu the resulting destruction.

These three components must remain distinct.

Does it mean intense cold or fire?

Exegetical sources transmit several explanations of ṣirr.

The predominant reading is intense cold. Al-Ṭabarī likewise prefers this meaning after transmitting different reports.

Some reports also explain ṣirr as fire or an intensely scorching wind.

Certain lexicographers also connect it with the wind’s intense sound.

Contextually, damaging cold clearly fits the comparison with destroyed crops, and independent lexical uses also attest it.

Bitter cold is therefore more suitable for the basic translation, while the other meanings can be documented in the lexical discussion.

Al-Ṭabarī: intense cold in the crop comparison

The role of ẓalamū anfusahum

The verse identifies the crops as belonging to people who have wronged themselves.

Ẓalamū anfusahum does not mean a particular kind of faulty farming technique.

It does not establish that they necessarily sowed at the wrong time, chose unsuitable land, or failed to follow farming principles.

Those possibilities go beyond the text.

In the verse, wronging oneself appears alongside the comparison concerning expenditure and the destruction of its expected fruit.

Unstated narrative details should therefore not be added to ṣirr or the verse’s other words.

Agricultural loss alone also does not establish that every farmer suffering damage is a wrongdoer. The verse uses a particular comparison to describe the outcome of the conduct under discussion; it does not give a rule for judging every natural loss.

Āl ʿImrān 3:135: wa-lam yuṣirrū ʿalā mā faʿalū

وَالَّذِينَ إِذَا فَعَلُوا فَاحِشَةً أَوْ ظَلَمُوا أَنْفُسَهُمْ ذَكَرُوا اللَّهَ فَاسْتَغْفَرُوا لِذُنُوبِهِمْ وَمَنْ يَغْفِرُ الذُّنُوبَ إِلَّا اللَّهُ وَلَمْ يُصِرُّوا عَلَىٰ مَا فَعَلُوا وَهُمْ يَعْلَمُونَ

wa-alladhīna idhā faʿalū fāḥishatan aw ẓalamū anfusahum dhakarū allāha fa-istaghfarū li-dhunūbihim wa-man yaghfiru al-dhunūba illā allāhu wa-lam yuṣirrū ʿalā mā faʿalū wa-hum yaʿlamūna

Translation: And those who, when they commit an indecency or wrong themselves, remember The God and seek forgiveness for their sins—and who forgives sins except The God?—and do not knowingly persist in what they have done.

Yuṣirrū here is a Form IV imperfect verb in the jussive after lam.

Lam yuṣirrū means that they did not persist in what they had done or knowingly refuse to correct it.

ʿAlā mā faʿalū identifies the object of persistence.

The verb by itself therefore does not mean committing a sin; it describes continuing in conduct already undertaken.

Committing a wrong and persisting in it

The verse begins by allowing that the people being praised may commit an indecency or wrong themselves.

It then describes their response: they remember God, seek forgiveness, and do not persist in what they have done.

This structure shows that committing a wrong and persisting in it are different situations.

If committing a sin itself equaled persistence, the verse’s distinction between sinning and not persisting would be meaningless.

Al-Ṭabarī likewise explains persistence here in terms of deliberately remaining in sin and abandoning repentance.

A person may thus slip and do wrong, but then act to correct it and return after recognizing it.

Another person may recognize the wrong yet remain in it and refuse to change.

This distinction is important for understanding iṣrār.

The role of wa-hum yaʿlamūna

The ending reads:

وَهُمْ يَعْلَمُونَ

wa-hum yaʿlamūna

That is, “while they know.”

This phrase highlights the importance of awareness in evaluating conduct.

A distinction must therefore be made between someone acting in ignorance or error and someone knowingly persisting after recognizing the action’s wrongness.

Wa-hum yaʿlamūna alone does not specify every degree of awareness, intention, and personal responsibility.

Those matters require examining the person’s actual circumstances and other evidence.

The verse also does not say that a repentant person will never do anything wrong again.

It concerns the person’s response to wrongdoing and refusal to remain knowingly in it.

Persistence should therefore not be defined solely by a numerical repetition of conduct; seeking forgiveness should likewise not be reduced to words detached from a decision to change.

Al-Ṭabarī: not deliberately remaining in sin

Al-Jāthiyah 45:8: persistence accompanied by arrogance

يَسْمَعُ آيَاتِ اللَّهِ تُتْلَىٰ عَلَيْهِ ثُمَّ يُصِرُّ مُسْتَكْبِرًا كَأَنْ لَمْ يَسْمَعْهَا فَبَشِّرْهُ بِعَذَابٍ أَلِيمٍ

yasmaʿu āyāti allāhi tutlā ʿalayhi thumma yuṣirru mustakbiran ka-an lam yasmaʿhā fa-bashshirhu bi-ʿadhābin alīmin

Translation: He hears the signs of The God recited to him, then persists arrogantly, as though he had not heard them. So give him tidings of a painful punishment.

Yuṣirru here is a masculine singular imperfect verb.

Unlike Āl ʿImrān 3:135, the object of persistence is not repeated with ʿalā in the same sentence.

The context nevertheless describes someone to whom God’s signs are recited, who continues to maintain his position.

Al-Jāthiyah 45:7 mentions a sinful fabricator; verse 8 then describes his response to hearing the signs.

Persistence here thus means maintaining a wrong position despite hearing the signs.

Persistence and arrogance

The verse says:

ثُمَّ يُصِرُّ مُسْتَكْبِرًا

thumma yuṣirru mustakbiran

Yuṣirru expresses persistence, while mustakbiran expresses the state of arrogance.

Arrogance is therefore not part of aṣarra’s independent meaning: it is a quality accompanying persistence in this verse.

A person may persist in something without being arrogant. In this context, however, the person refuses to heed the signs and reconsider, and the conduct is accompanied by arrogance.

Ka-an lam yasmaʿhā

This expression also matters.

The verse does not say that he has genuinely not heard the signs: its opening explicitly says that he hears them.

It then says that he behaves as though he had not heard.

A message’s not reaching someone therefore differs from disregard for a message already heard.

This distinction comes from the whole verse’s structure and should not simply be added to the lexical definition of persistence.

Al-Ṭabarī: hearing the signs and persisting arrogantly

Al-Dhāriyāt 51:29: fī ṣarratin

فَأَقْبَلَتِ امْرَأَتُهُ فِي صَرَّةٍ فَصَكَّتْ وَجْهَهَا وَقَالَتْ عَجُوزٌ عَقِيمٌ

fa-aqbalati imraʾatuhu fī ṣarratin fa-ṣakkat wajhahā wa-qālat ʿajūzun ʿaqīmun

Translation: Then his wife turned with a loud cry, struck her face, and said, “A barren old woman!”

Ṣarratin here is an indefinite noun following the preposition fī.

The verse belongs to the account of Abraham receiving good news of a child.

In Al-Dhāriyāt 51:28, the messengers give Abraham good news of a knowledgeable son. Verse 29 then describes his wife’s response.

In the well-known reading of this context, ṣarrah refers to a loud cry and an intense vocal response.

Does ṣarrah mean a cry or a group of women?

Lexicons record both meanings for ṣarrah.

First, a loud cry or shout.

Second, a gathered group.

Al-Ṭabarī prefers ṣayḥah in explaining this verse and transmits numerous supporting reports from early commentators.

Some other reports suggest that Abraham’s wife was among a group of women.

The verse itself, however, mentions no such group.

Fa-ṣakkat wajhahā and wa-qālat ʿajūzun ʿaqīmun also indicate a response involving surprise.

A cry or shout therefore fits the context more closely.

Was the cry caused by fear or surprise?

The verse describes Abraham’s wife’s response after the good news of a child.

Hūd 11:72 also reports her words expressing surprise at bearing a child in old age.

The vocal response can therefore be connected with surprise at the announcement.

Ṣarrah itself does not mean fear, happiness, or disbelief.

Those states are understood from the story’s context.

Fa-ṣakkat wajhahā also describes a separate action: striking the face should not be incorporated into the meaning of Ṣ R R.

Must fa-aqbalat mean movement from one place to another?

Arabic aqbala can describe physically turning or approaching, but in some constructions it also means beginning speech or conduct.

Al-Ṭabarī takes this use to mean beginning to cry out and explicitly states that it does not concern moving from one location to another.

The verb alone therefore cannot establish Abraham’s wife’s distance from the guests or her precise location in the house.

What is clear is her response after hearing the good news.

Al-Ṭabarī: a loud cry and aqbalat as beginning an action

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:46: wa-kānū yuṣirrūna ʿalā al-ḥinthi al-ʿaẓīmi

وَكَانُوا يُصِرُّونَ عَلَى الْحِنْثِ الْعَظِيمِ

wa-kānū yuṣirrūna ʿalā al-ḥinthi al-ʿaẓīmi

Translation: And they used to persist in grave sin.

Yuṣirrūna here is a plural Form IV imperfect verb.

Kānū yuṣirrūna expresses ongoing or habitual persistence in a past context.

ʿAlā al-ḥinthi al-ʿaẓīmi identifies what they persisted in.

Persistence here therefore concerns remaining in grave sin.

What does ḥinth add to the meaning?

Ḥinth comes from Ḥ N TH and in Arabic relates to breaking an oath and committing sin.

Al-Ṭabarī explains al-ḥinthi al-ʿaẓīmi here as grave sin; some reports identify associating partners with God as its instance.

In Al-Wāqiʿah 56:47, the same group questioningly deny being raised after dying and becoming dust and bones.

This continuity clarifies the background of belief to their persistence.

Nevertheless, grave sin comes from al-ḥinthi al-ʿaẓīmi, not from yuṣirrūna itself.

The verb expresses only persistence and refusal to desist.

The verse also does not mean that all persistence, even in something right, is equivalent to grave sin.

Al-Ṭabarī: persistence in grave sin

Study of Ḥ N TH

Nūḥ 71:7: wa-aṣarrū

وَإِنِّي كُلَّمَا دَعَوْتُهُمْ لِتَغْفِرَ لَهُمْ جَعَلُوا أَصَابِعَهُمْ فِي آذَانِهِمْ وَاسْتَغْشَوْا ثِيَابَهُمْ وَأَصَرُّوا وَاسْتَكْبَرُوا اسْتِكْبَارًا

wa-innī kullamā daʿawtuhum li-taghfira lahum jaʿalū aṣābiʿahum fī ādhānihim wa-istaghshaw thiyābahum wa-aṣarrū wa-istakbarū istikbāran

Translation: And whenever I called them so that You might forgive them, they put their fingers in their ears, drew their garments over themselves, persisted, and acted with great arrogance.

Aṣarrū here is a plural perfect verb.

Its object or complement is not separately stated, but the context of Noah’s call makes clear that they maintained their opposition and refusal to accept the invitation.

Persistence, refusing to hear, and arrogance

The verse places several kinds of conduct alongside one another.

Putting fingers in the ears is an action associated here with refusing to hear Noah’s call.

Covering themselves with their garments is another action.

Aṣarrū expresses persistence and not desisting.

Wa-istakbarū istikbāran also highlights arrogance and self-importance.

These are connected, but are not synonyms.

Someone may not hear something because it genuinely never reached them. Here, however, Noah describes people who take action to avoid hearing and accepting his invitation.

Their persistence is also accompanied by arrogance, but arrogance is not an essential part of iṣrār in every use.

The verse shows how persistence can become a pattern in which someone repeatedly encounters a call to reform yet continues to maintain a previous position.

Al-Ṭabarī: covering themselves, persistence, and arrogance

7. The relationship between Ṣ R R and Ṣ R Ṣ R

The Qur’an uses ṣarṣar three times concerning wind:

  • Fuṣṣilat 41:16
  • Al-Qamar 54:19
  • Al-Ḥāqqah 69:6

In these uses the wind is described as harsh and destructive, and in some lexical explanations as very cold or noisy.

The Qur’anic morphological index nevertheless classifies ṣarṣar under the quadriliteral root Ṣ R Ṣ R, rather than the triliteral Ṣ R R.

This distinction must be preserved.

Was ṣarṣar formed by repeating ṣarr?

Some lexical explanations connect ṣarṣar with repetition of sound or intensification of the quality expressed by ṣarr.

Al-Zajjāj likewise explains a relationship between ṣarr and ṣarṣar involving repetition or intensity of sound.

Other lexicographers treat it as an independent quadriliteral form or one of Arabic’s reduplicated constructions.

The semantic relationship between ṣirr and ṣarṣar can therefore be studied, but it does not make them one morphological root for counting Qur’anic occurrences.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:117, ṣirr expresses a quality of the wind.

In Al-Ḥāqqah 69:6, ṣarṣar qualifies the wind, and ʿātiyah adds another quality.

These combinations do not establish each wind’s precise degree of cold, speed, or sound level.

Nor should ṣarṣar be restricted to one physical property when lexicons discuss both intense cold and intense sound.

Study of Ṣ R Ṣ R

8. Important Arabic forms

Ṣarra and ṣarr

In the material branch, ṣarra is used for binding and securing.

In the sound branch, it also means making a sound or crying out.

Ṣarr can be the verb’s verbal noun; certain vocalizations also involve intense cold or sound.

Vocalization and word structure matter when identifying these uses.

Ṣurrah and ṣarrah

Ṣurrah, with ḍammah on ṣād, in its established use names a bag or tied bundle.

Ṣarrah, with fatḥah on ṣād, is recorded for an intense cry, clamor, or gathered group.

The two look identical in unvocalized writing, but their lexical uses do not necessarily share one meaning.

The Qur’an uses ṣarratin with fatḥah; in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:29 it refers to a loud cry.

Translating it as a money bag or bundle is therefore incorrect.

Ṣirr

Ṣirr, with kasrah on ṣād, occurs in Āl ʿImrān 3:117, where intense cold is the preferred meaning.

Some Arabic uses also connect this form with intense sound.

The particular quality in each use must nevertheless be identified from context.

Ṣarīr

Ṣarīr names a sharp or prolonged sound, such as that of a pen, a door, or certain animals.

This form does not occur in the Qur’an.

Iṣrār

Iṣrār is the verbal noun of aṣarra and refers to persistence, remaining firm, and refusing to desist from something.

The verbal noun itself does not occur in the Qur’an, but four verbal forms from this pattern do.

Ṣirār

In animal husbandry ṣirār names a band tied around a milk animal’s udder.

This word does not occur in the Qur’an.

Ṣarūrah

Arabic records ṣarūrah for someone who has not married or has not performed the pilgrimage.

Some historical reports connect it with the person’s social or ritual status.

It cannot simply be reduced to a general meaning of binding or persisting without separate examination.

This form also does not occur in the Qur’an.

9. Comparison with related roots

Ṣ R R (ṣarra) and ʿ Q D (ʿaqada)

Arabic ʿaqada concerns knotting, binding, and securing.

Ṣarra likewise refers to binding in its material branch.

The two families can overlap in some uses, such as tying a bag or securing something.

ʿ Q D also has a broad family of meanings involving knots, covenants, and contracts.

Ṣ R R, by contrast, includes separate branches involving intense cold, sound, and persistence.

All the meanings of ʿaqd therefore cannot be transferred to ṣarr, or vice versa.

Study of ʿ Q D

Ṣ R R (aṣarra) and TH B T (thabata)

Arabic thabata means remaining firm or standing fast.

In behavioral use aṣarra likewise concerns holding to a decision or conduct.

Iṣrār usually highlights persistence and refusal to desist.

Thabāt, however, may simply express the state of something remaining established.

The Qur’an also praises firmness in some contexts, whereas the four verbal uses of aṣarra concern blameworthy persistence.

That difference comes from the Qur’anic contexts; it does not make firmness inherently good and persistence inherently bad.

Study of TH B T

Ṣ R R (aṣarra) and ʿ Z M (ʿazama)

ʿAzama concerns deciding and firm resolve.

Aṣarra also involves firmness of decision, but places greater emphasis on maintaining it and not desisting.

ʿAzm can therefore highlight the decision, and iṣrār the continuation of that decision or conduct.

This difference should not become a fixed sequence requiring ʿazm to precede iṣrār in every sentence.

The two families overlap in some uses.

Ṣ R R (aṣarra) and ʿ N D (ʿānada)

Arabic ʿinād concerns opposition and obstinacy toward another.

Iṣrār can likewise occur in contexts of opposition and refusal to change.

Persistence does not necessarily mean opposing someone else.

A person may maintain a decision, plan, or belief without being engaged in opposition to anyone.

In Al-Jāthiyah 45:8 and Nūḥ 71:7, persistence accompanies arrogance and rejection of the call, bringing it close to obstinacy.

That contextual proximity does not make the two roots fully equivalent.

Ṣ R R (ṣirr) and B R D (bard)

Bard is the Arabic name for cold.

In its weather-related use ṣirr likewise expresses severe cold.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:117 this cold relates to damage to crops.

Not every use of bard necessarily means damaging or destructive cold.

Ṣirr here highlights a more severe, harmful quality, but the distinction between the two words cannot be converted into a fixed numerical temperature difference.

Study of B R D

Ṣ R R (ṣarīr) and Ṣ W T (ṣawt)

Ṣawt is the general name for sound.

Ṣarīr describes a particular sharp or prolonged sound, or one produced by friction and movement.

Every ṣarīr is therefore sound, but not every sound is ṣarīr.

In Al-Dhāriyāt 51:29, ṣarrah refers to an intense cry, but this meaning does not encompass every use of ṣawt.

Ṣ R R (ṣarra) and S R R (sirr)

Despite the resemblance between their letters, these are separate roots.

Qur’anic Ṣ R R concerns bitter cold, a cry, and persistence.

S R R, however, involves concealment, secrets, joy, and certain other forms.

Iṣrār from Ṣ R R means persistence; isrār from S R R, in an established use, concerns concealment.

The difference between ṣād and sīn distinguishes two independent linguistic families.

Persisting in sin and concealing a matter should therefore not be conflated in lexical analysis.

Study of S R R

10. Challenges of interpretation and translation

The main difficulty is that all the forms in this family cannot be translated with a single Persian equivalent.

Ṣirr in Āl ʿImrān 3:117 refers to bitter cold.

Ṣarrah in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:29 concerns a loud cry.

The other four, verbal uses clearly mean persisting and not desisting.

The root’s definition must preserve these distinctions.

Does persistence always establish full awareness?

Aṣarra itself indicates persistence, but does not by itself establish the person’s degree of awareness or intention.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:135, wa-hum yaʿlamūna highlights awareness.

In Al-Jāthiyah 45:8, the person hears the signs and arrogantly maintains his position.

In Nūḥ 71:7, persistence accompanies refusal to hear the call and arrogance.

These verses therefore concern conduct with indications of awareness and resistance to reform.

When judging actual people, however, persistence alone should not be treated as conclusive proof that they fully know their position to be wrong.

Initial ignorance also differs from knowing persistence after an error has become clear.

Does persistence mean repeating a sin several times?

Not necessarily.

Repetition can indicate persistence, but is not its complete definition.

Someone may do wrong and then insist that the conduct was right, refuse to change, and continue defending the same position.

Another person may repeatedly slip while trying to correct a weakness, yet make a real effort each time to return and improve.

The number of repetitions alone does not establish that both situations involve the same persistence.

Āl ʿImrān 3:135 clarifies the distinction through remembering God, seeking forgiveness, and not remaining in wrongdoing.

Does all persistence indicate bigotry?

No.

Persistence may accompany bigotry, but the two are not synonymous.

Bigotry or arrogance can prevent someone from reconsidering, as in the settings of Al-Jāthiyah 45:8 and Nūḥ 71:7.

A person can also maintain a sound position after examining reliable evidence.

Persistence’s moral value must therefore be assessed through its object, the available evidence, willingness to change, and the consequences of the conduct.

11. Translation proposals for Forqan

In Āl ʿImrān 3:117:

كَمَثَلِ رِيحٍ فِيهَا صِرٌّ

ka-mathali rīḥin fīhā ṣirrun

Preferred rendering:

“Like a wind containing bitter cold.”

Here cold comes from ṣirr, and wind from rīḥ.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:135:

وَلَمْ يُصِرُّوا عَلَىٰ مَا فَعَلُوا وَهُمْ يَعْلَمُونَ

wa-lam yuṣirrū ʿalā mā faʿalū wa-hum yaʿlamūna

Preferred rendering:

“And do not knowingly persist in what they have done.”

This preserves the relationship of persistence to the previous act and to awareness.

In Al-Jāthiyah 45:8:

ثُمَّ يُصِرُّ مُسْتَكْبِرًا

thumma yuṣirru mustakbiran

Preferred rendering:

“Then he persists arrogantly.”

Arrogance here comes from mustakbiran, not from Ṣ R R itself.

In Al-Dhāriyāt 51:29:

فَأَقْبَلَتِ امْرَأَتُهُ فِي صَرَّةٍ

fa-aqbalati imraʾatuhu fī ṣarratin

Preferred rendering:

“Then his wife turned with a loud cry.”

“With a loud shout” is also defensible.

In Al-Wāqiʿah 56:46:

وَكَانُوا يُصِرُّونَ عَلَى الْحِنْثِ الْعَظِيمِ

wa-kānū yuṣirrūna ʿalā al-ḥinthi al-ʿaẓīmi

Preferred rendering:

“And they used to persist in grave sin.”

In Nūḥ 71:7:

وَأَصَرُّوا

wa-aṣarrū

Preferred rendering:

“And they persisted.”

The object of persistence is clear from Noah’s call, so no additional explanation is needed within the verb’s own translation.

For uses outside the Qur’an, ṣarra al-ṣurrata can be rendered “he securely tied the bag”; ṣarīr as “creaking or a prolonged, shrill sound”; and iṣrār as “persisting and not desisting.”

12. Conclusion

The Arabic root Ṣ R R has several semantic branches: binding and securing, gathering and compactness, persisting and holding to a decision, bitter cold, and shrill or intense sound.

Binding and persistence share a possible connection through firmness and holding on, but without sufficient historical evidence all the root’s branches cannot be restricted to that image.

Its six Qur’anic uses fall into three main areas.

Ṣirr in Āl ʿImrān 3:117 refers to severe, damaging cold.

Ṣarrah in Al-Dhāriyāt 51:29 concerns a loud cry.

The four verbal forms of aṣarra in Āl ʿImrān 3:135, Al-Jāthiyah 45:8, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:46, and Nūḥ 71:7 concern persistence in wrong conduct or a wrong position.

Persistence is blameworthy in these verses because of its object and context, rather than because all persistence is sinful in Arabic.

Short definition of the root: Ṣ R R is a family with several branches, expressing binding and securing, remaining firm and persisting, bitter cold, and shrill sound or an intense cry in different uses. Brief root meaning: Binding and securing; persisting and not desisting; bitter cold; shrill sound or a loud cry

The main Qur’anic distinction is that ṣirr expresses bitter cold, ṣarrah an intense cry, and aṣarra persistence and refusal to desist.

These differences show that sharing root letters does not necessarily make all derivatives identical in meaning. Each form’s precise meaning must be identified through linguistic evidence, morphology, and the verse’s context.
