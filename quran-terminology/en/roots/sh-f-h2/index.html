The lip, the mouth’s edge and direct speech: from bodily meaning in early Arabic to the Quran’s reminder of two lips

In early Arabic, SH F H concerns the lip, the soft, movable part above or below the mouth. Shafah names one lip and shafatān two. The family also includes direct conversation, oral speech, contact with the lip, and certain usages concerning drinking or consumption.

Physically, shafah names a part of the mouth involved in opening and closing it, taking food and drink, producing certain sounds and expressing facial states. Through its association with speaking, the lip can also stand for speech; mushāfahah denotes direct conversation.

Early lexicons also record shafah for an object’s edge, such as a vessel’s rim. This does not establish that the root originally meant any boundary. The clear, familiar meaning is the mouth’s lip; other usages require their own evidence.

The root occurs only once in the Quran, as shafatayn in Al-Balad 90:9. Together with the two eyes in Al-Balad 90:8 and the tongue, the lips are recalled among the faculties granted to humanity.

The context connects these faculties with awareness, choice and responsibility. Shafatayn itself, however, simply means two lips. Guidance, speaking, choice and moral responsibility come from their respective words and contexts rather than all belonging to the lexical definition of a lip.

1. Meaning in early Arabic

Shafah: the mouth’s lip

In early Arabic, shafah named one of the two soft, movable parts enclosing the mouth above and below and meeting when closed.

Early descriptions call the lips ṭabaqā al-fam: the two parts covering the mouth or closing upon one another.

This clarifies the physical meaning.

The lip is distinct from the tongue, teeth and mouth as a whole, though they cooperate in eating, drinking and speaking.

When drinking from a vessel, the lips contact its opening and help control incoming liquid. Some speech sounds also involve the lips approaching or touching.

Those functions are not the lexical definition: shafah names the body part, while its functions are related but distinct.

An early poetic witness: a horse’s lips in Abu Duʾad al-Iyadi

Abu Duʾad al-Iyadi, a pre-Islamic poet known for describing horses, is quoted in early lexical sources:

فَبِتْنَا عُرَاةً لَدَى مُهْرِنَا
نُنَزِّعُ مِنْ شَفَتَيْهِ الصَّفَارَا

fa-bitnā ʿurātan ladā muhrinā / nunazziʿu min shafatayhi al-ṣafārā

The line’s sense is:

“We spent the night attending to our young horse, removing the dry, prickly grass clinging to its lips.” Here ʿurātan is understood as devoting themselves to the task.

Ṣafār here refers to dried grass whose prickles can cling around a horse’s mouth.

Shafatayhi is the dual of shafah with an attached pronoun: its two lips.

The poet applies the familiar human body-part term to part of a horse’s mouth.

Some lexical explanations treat this as an extension or metaphor from the human lip.

The witness shows that early poetry could use shafah for the corresponding animal part.

It does not establish that every animal was described with this term. Specialized names such as jaḥfalah also existed for particular animals.

The first hemistich has variant readings: julūsan, qiyāman and ʿurātan. The relevant expression in the second, min shafatayhi al-ṣafārā, is preserved.

The line illustrates the bodily meaning in poetry attributed to an early poet; textual variants and attribution should still be recognized.

Shafah and the edges of objects

Lexicons also record shafatu al-dalw, the lip or rim of a bucket.

A well bucket’s opening resembles a mouth’s lip in shape and position.

Here a body-part name is extended to an object’s edge.

Shafatu al-ināʾ and comparable expressions are also intelligible as a vessel’s rim.

Shafatu al-jabal is recorded for a mountain’s edge or margin.

The age of every such usage is uncertain. One early lexicographer expressly questioned whether the bucket usage was heard from Arabs or was terminology used by Abu ʿUbayd’s teachers.

Bucket and mountain edges should therefore not be presented without independent evidence as established, widespread pre-Islamic meanings.

The physical meaning and Abu Duʾad’s attributed verse attest the lip term. Its extension to objects may rest on resemblance in shape and position, but the precise chronology is unclear.

Lips and speech

Because lips participate in speaking, they also occur in expressions about speech.

Mā kallamtuhu bi-binti shafatin means “I did not speak even a word to him.”

Bint shafah here denotes a word or utterance.

Mā samiʿtu minhu dhāta shafatin likewise means hearing not even a word from someone.

The lip stands for speech produced with the speech organs.

The association is understandable: speech comes from the mouth, and lip movement contributes to some sounds.

It does not establish that shafah originally meant “word,” “language” or “speech.”

A body part and an activity in which it participates are distinct.

Recording an expression in later lexicons also does not by itself date its circulation before the Quran.

Shāfih: thirsty

Rajulun shāfihun is recorded for a thirsty man without enough water to moisten his lip.

Tamim ibn Muqbil, who lived across the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods, says:

فَكَمْ وَطِئْنَا بِهَا مِنْ شَافِهٍ بَطَلٍ
وَكَمْ أَخَذْنَا مِنْ أَنْفَالٍ نُفَادِيهَا

fa-kam waṭiʾnā bihā min shāfihin baṭalin / wa-kam akhadhnā min anfālin nufādīhā

“How many a thirsty warrior we overcame with it, and how many captives we took to release for ransom.” This renders the general martial sense of the line.

Shāfih describes a thirsty person in this line.

The connection with lips is clear: thirst may accompany dry lips and a need to drink.

Here shāfih describes the thirsty person rather than naming the lip itself.

The poem’s period must also be treated carefully. Ibn Muqbil lived across both periods, and this line’s exact date is uncertain; it cannot be assumed to be pre-Quranic.

What the early witnesses establish

The physical use in the verse attributed to Abu Duʾad is a clear witness to early language.

Speech, object edges and thirst are also recorded in the lexicons.

Together these support the mouth’s lip, a particular body part, as the central meaning of the family.

Direct speech, lip contact, thirst and object edges are associated usages, but their history should not all be derived from a single imagined original image without evidence.

2. Important forms and derivatives

Shafah

A singular noun meaning one lip.

The noun is feminine and names either human lip.

Shafatāni and shafatayni

Dual forms of shafah: two lips.

Shafatāni is nominative; shafatayni is accusative or genitive.

The Quran uses shafatayni.

Shifāh

The broken plural of shafah: lips.

It may refer to several people’s lips or multiple lips, but does not occur in this form in the Quran.

Shafaha

In the bodily usage, affected or struck someone’s lip.

Other recorded usages concern persistent requests and consuming or exhausting something.

Shāfaha

Spoke directly to someone.

The lexical explanation depicts speech passing directly from one person’s mouth to another; it does not require their lips to touch.

Mushāfahah

Direct, face-to-face conversation.

It is also commonly contrasted with communication in writing.

Shafahiyy and shafawiyy

Relating to the lip; in speech contexts, oral or spoken.

Lexical accounts differ over these forms. Some prefer shafahiyy on morphological grounds; others accept shafawiyy too.

Persian shafāhī commonly describes spoken rather than written communication.

Shufāhiyy

Another Arabic usage describes someone with large or thick lips.

Its similar spelling does not make it identical to the modern term for oral communication.

Shufayhah

The diminutive of shafah: a little lip.

This form also matters in analyzing the word’s structure.

Shāfih

One recorded sense is a thirsty person lacking water to moisten the lip.

Mashfūh

Māʾun mashfūhun describes water frequented by many drinkers.

It also describes someone beset by many requests whose resources are strained by others’ needs.

The image of many lips at one water source helps explain the usage, but does not prove the historical origin of every verbal sense.

Shafh

Recorded meanings include occupying someone, keeping them from something, or exhausting a resource through consumption and demands.

This branch is further removed from the body-part name, and its precise historical relationship is less clear.

None of these forms occurs in the Quran except shafatayni.

3. The structure of shafah and its third radical

The root has a morphological feature not fully visible in the singular.

Shafah visibly contains shīn and fāʾ followed by the feminine ending.

In the familiar Basran analysis, the third radical is hāʾ, omitted in the singular while the feminine marker remains.

That analysis posits shafahah as the underlying form. This is a morphological reconstruction, not a separately dated attestation proving that speakers once universally used it.

Two forms support the analysis:

The plural shifāh, which displays hāʾ.

The diminutive shufayhah, which also retains it.

Many lexicographers therefore assign the word to SH F H.

Some accounts also record shafawāt and use such forms to argue for wāw as the omitted radical.

Thus not all accounts analyze the word’s structure identically.

The familiar morphological classification and Quranic index place shafatayni under SH F H.

The tāʾ in shafatayni is not an additional root letter.

Shafah is feminine, and its ending appears as tāʾ in the dual:

Shafah → shafatāni / shafatayni

The dual therefore does not imply a root SH F T.

Disagreement about the singular’s structure does not change the Quranic meaning: two lips.

4. The Quranic occurrence: Al-Balad 90:9

The Quran says:

Al-Balad 90:8

أَلَمْ نَجْعَلْ لَهُ عَيْنَيْنِ

a-lam najʿal lahu ʿaynayn

Al-Balad 90:8

“Have We not given him two eyes?”

Al-Balad 90:9

وَلِسَانًا وَشَفَتَيْنِ

wa-lisānan wa-shafatayn

Al-Balad 90:9

“And a tongue and two lips?”

Al-Balad 90:10

وَهَدَيْنَاهُ النَّجْدَيْنِ

wa-hadaynāhu al-najdayn

Al-Balad 90:10

“And guided him to the two elevated paths?”

These verses are connected.

The sequence recalls eyes, then tongue and lips, then guidance to two paths, following the familiar interpretation of al-najdayn as the paths of good and evil.

Shafatayni: the two human lips

In Al-Balad 90:9, shafatayni names the human lips.

Lexically it means neither speech, the tongue nor two ways of life.

The Quran names a body part.

The upper and lower lips participate with the tongue, teeth and other mouth parts in several bodily functions.

They help control incoming liquid, take and retain food, and contribute through movement and contact to certain sounds.

Their mention does not imply that speaking is their sole function.

Nor can speech be attributed entirely to them: the tongue, airflow, larynx and other organs also participate.

The verse thus names two actual, specific body parts.

Their significance in the surah connects with ability and responsibility, but this must not replace the word’s direct meaning.

Why are tongue and lips mentioned together?

The verse says:

وَلِسَانًا وَشَفَتَيْنِ

wa-lisānan wa-shafatayn

Al-Balad 90:9

“And a tongue and two lips.”

Lisān belongs to L S N and physically names the tongue.

Shafatayni belongs to SH F H and names the two lips.

Both belong to the mouth and cooperate in speech, but differ in structure and role.

For example, the sounds b and m involve contact between the lips. In f, the lower lip approaches the upper teeth.

Many other sounds involve the tongue moving or contacting different parts of the mouth.

Mentioning tongue and lips together can therefore draw attention to the bodily means of speech.

The verse does not establish that the tongue symbolizes truth while the lips symbolize falsehood or silence.

That division is not stated in the text.

Nor does having two lips mean one was created for truth and the other for falsehood.

Al-Balad 90:10 mentions two paths, but proximity does not assign a separate moral meaning to each lip.

The lips in Al-Balad 90:9 are bodily; the paths in Al-Balad 90:10 concern guidance.

Eyes and human guidance

Al-Balad 90:8 recalls the two eyes.

Eyes contribute to perceiving the environment and seeing the world.

Al-Balad 90:9 names the tongue and lips, which participate in taking food and communicating.

Al-Balad 90:10 then concerns guidance to two paths.

The sequence recalls bodily faculties within life, understanding and choice, rather than merely listing anatomy.

Possessing a faculty and using it well are different matters.

Eyes alone do not guarantee correct perception or sound judgment.

Tongue and lips do not guarantee that every utterance is true and just.

The same organs can serve truthful explanation and help, or false and harmful speech.

The difference belongs to human conduct and intention, rather than a lip being inherently good or bad.

The preceding context: imagined self-sufficiency

Al-Balad 90:5 says:

أَيَحْسَبُ أَنْ لَنْ يَقْدِرَ عَلَيْهِ أَحَدٌ

a-yaḥsabu an lan yaqdira ʿalayhi aḥad

Al-Balad 90:5

“Does he think no one will have power over him?”

Al-Balad 90:6 speaks of someone saying he has squandered abundant wealth.

Al-Balad 90:7 says:

أَيَحْسَبُ أَنْ لَمْ يَرَهُ أَحَدٌ

a-yaḥsabu an lam yarahu aḥad

Al-Balad 90:7

“Does he think no one has seen him?”

The Quran then recalls human eyes, tongue and lips.

The connection matters.

Someone relying on strength or wealth and imagining freedom from accountability is reminded of faculties they did not create.

They possess eyes, tongue and lips without being their creator.

Ability should therefore not become a claim to absolute independence from God.

Yet shafatayni itself does not mean dependence or responsibility; that conclusion arises from the connected verses.

Faculties and the difficult path of good

After recalling human faculties and guidance, the Quran says:

Al-Balad 90:11

فَلَا اقْتَحَمَ الْعَقَبَةَ

fa-lā iqtaḥama al-ʿaqabah

Al-Balad 90:11

“Yet he did not brave the steep ascent.”

The following verses explain it through freeing someone from bondage and feeding people on a day of hunger.

Al-Balad 90:15 and Al-Balad 90:16 mention a related orphan and a destitute person.

Al-Balad 90:17 adds faith and mutual exhortation to patience and mercy.

This connection clarifies the surah’s message.

Human beings possess many faculties, but conduct is assessed through their use and the choices made, rather than mere possession.

Someone may have eyes yet overlook another’s need.

They may use tongue and lips for humiliation or deception rather than truthful speech.

The same faculties can recognize need, defend a right and help someone in bondage.

These examples extend the surah’s message; they are not the lexical definition of a lip.

Shafatayni names two bodily organs; moral responsibility comes from the human situation described across the verses.

5. Distinctions from related roots

SH F H compared with L S N

Physically, lisān means tongue.

Its family also concerns a people’s language or manner of expression in the Quran.

Al-Naḥl 16:103, for example, says:

وَهَٰذَا لِسَانٌ عَرَبِيٌّ مُبِينٌ

wa-hādhā lisānun ʿarabiyyun mubīn

Al-Naḥl 16:103

“And this is a clear Arabic tongue.”

Lisān here concerns Arabic language rather than simply the bodily organ.

Shafatayni in Al-Balad 90:9 names the lips themselves.

Although tongue and lips cooperate in speech, the words belong to independent families.

Translating shafatayni merely as “the ability to speak” would therefore omit the named body parts.

SH F H compared with F W H

Fam means mouth.

Afwāh is its plural and occurs in Quranic speech and mouth contexts.

For example, Āl ʿImrān 3:167 says:

يَقُولُونَ بِأَفْوَاهِهِمْ مَا لَيْسَ فِي قُلُوبِهِمْ

yaqūlūna bi-afwāhihim mā laysa fī qulūbihim

Āl ʿImrān 3:167

“They say with their mouths what is not in their hearts.”

The mouth is associated here with speech.

But a mouth is not identical to a lip.

Lips, tongue, teeth and other parts contribute to the mouth’s structure and functions.

Shafah names the lip rather than the whole mouth.

Figurative afwāh can emphasize overt speech, while shifāh directly names lips.

The physical imagery is related, but the families are not complete synonyms without contextual evidence.

SH F H compared with Q W L

Qawl belongs to Q W L and denotes speech or utterance.

Shafah names a body part.

A speaker uses speech organs, but the organ and the utterance do not mean the same thing.

Bint shafah, for example, figuratively connects the lip with a word.

Al-Balad 90:9 uses shafatayni, not qawl or kalām.

It should not be translated as “two utterances” or “two kinds of speech.”

SH F H compared with SH F W

Shafā, assigned to SH F W, denotes an edge in the Quran.

Āl ʿImrān 3:103 says:

وَكُنْتُمْ عَلَىٰ شَفَا حُفْرَةٍ مِنَ النَّارِ فَأَنْقَذَكُمْ مِنْهَا

wa-kuntum ʿalā shafā ḥufratin mina al-nāri fa-anqadhakum minhā

Āl ʿImrān 3:103

“You were on the edge of a pit of fire, and He rescued you from it.”

Shafā names the pit’s edge.

A lip and a pit’s edge resemble one another as boundaries.

The Quranic morphological index nevertheless assigns shafatayni to SH F H and shafā to SH F W.

They should not be merged without historical investigation.

Using shafah for some objects’ edges does not itself prove a shared historical development for every form of shafah and shafā.

SH F H compared with SH F Y

Shifāʾ belongs to SH F Y and concerns healing in the Quran.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:80 says:

وَإِذَا مَرِضْتُ فَهُوَ يَشْفِينِ

wa-idhā mariḍtu fa-huwa yashfīn

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:80

“When I become ill, He heals me.”

Al-Naḥl 16:69 also mentions a drink containing healing for people.

The family resembles SH F H in letters but differs in meaning.

Shifāh means lips; shifāʾ means healing.

The different final consonants matter; similar spelling or pronunciation does not make the words identical.

Shafatayni in Al-Balad 90:9 therefore conveys no direct meaning of healing.

SH F H compared with N Ṭ Q

Nuṭq concerns speaking or producing speech.

The Quran applies this family to human and other creatures’ speech.

Shafah names a body part contributing to some sounds.

Nuṭq is an activity; shafah is an organ’s name.

A person may have lips yet have difficulty speaking because of tongue movement or other parts of speech production.

Having two lips alone does not guarantee complete speech ability.

An organ’s function should therefore not automatically replace its name’s lexical meaning.

6. Morphology and translation proposals for Forqan

The Quranic form is:

وَشَفَتَيْنِ

wa-shafatayn

Al-Balad 90:9

It consists of the conjunction wa and the dual of shafah.

Shafatayni is accusative and coordinated with lisānan.

ʿAynayni in Al-Balad 90:8 is also accusative.

The combined sentence reads:

أَلَمْ نَجْعَلْ لَهُ عَيْنَيْنِ ۝ وَلِسَانًا وَشَفَتَيْنِ

a-lam najʿal lahu ʿaynayn / wa-lisānan wa-shafatayn

Al-Balad 90:8; Al-Balad 90:9

“Have We not given him two eyes, a tongue and two lips?”

Najʿal in Al-Balad 90:8 governs all these coordinated objects.

Shafatayni is dual; its translation should express two lips, not a single lip or an unspecified plural.

Its indefiniteness fits the introduction of these organs; it does not independently establish moral value or an exceptional property of the lips.

Translation proposals

Shafah: “lip.”

Shafatāni / shafatayni: “two lips.”

Shifāh: “lips.”

Shafaha: in the bodily sense, “affected or struck the lip”; elsewhere, “pressed with requests” or “exhausted through consumption and demands,” according to context.

Shāfaha: “spoke face to face” or “addressed directly.”

Mushāfahah: “direct conversation” or “speaking face to face.”

Shafahiyy: “labial” in anatomy or phonetics; “oral” in speech contexts.

Shāfih: “thirsty and lacking water to moisten the lip.”

Mashfūh: “subject to many demands”; of water, “frequented by many drinkers.”

Shafh: “occupying” or “exhausting a resource through many demands,” in its recorded usages.

For Al-Balad 90:9, the direct rendering is:

وَلِسَانًا وَشَفَتَيْنِ

wa-lisānan wa-shafatayn

Al-Balad 90:9

“And a tongue and two lips.”

This is concise, natural and faithful to the Arabic structure. The proposal does not change the installed verse translation.

The base translation need not add “for speaking,” “for eating” or “for distinguishing truth from falsehood,” none of which appears in this phrase.

Physical functions and their connection to responsibility can be discussed in reflection on the verse.

“Two speaking lips” is also unsuitable: speech involves several organs, and shafatayni contains no such description.

7. Conclusion

SH F H concerns the mouth’s lip. Shafah names one lip; shafatāni or shafatayni names two.

The verse attributed to Abu Duʾad applies the word to a young horse’s lips, providing an early bodily usage.

The family also has recorded usages for direct speech, oral communication, thirst and heavy demands on water or other resources. These can relate functionally or figuratively to lips, without establishing the exact history of every development.

The familiar morphological analysis assigns hāʾ as the third radical, supported by shifāh and shufayhah. Other analyses of the structure are also reported.

Only shafatayni in Al-Balad 90:9 occurs in the Quran.

It names the human lips, recalled with eyes and tongue among bodily faculties.

The surah continues with guidance, braving the steep ascent, freeing someone from bondage, feeding the needy, and mutual exhortation to patience and mercy.

The reminder of human organs thus belongs to a wider account of gifts, abilities and responsibility.

Shafatayni itself nevertheless means neither speech, guidance, choice nor responsibility.

A defensible core meaning is the lip, the soft, movable part above or below the mouth. Speech and object-edge usages occupy a related semantic field.

“Two lips” is the direct and sufficient Forqan equivalent of shafatayni.

The surah draws attention to faculties placed at humanity’s disposal. Possession alone is not virtue; their use in conduct matters.

Lips can contribute to truthful speech, reassurance and help, as well as false or harmful speech. Moral evaluation concerns the person’s choices, not the bodily organ itself.

Understanding the root therefore requires distinguishing the organ’s name, its functions and human responsibility for using it. They are connected but are not one lexical or Quranic meaning.
