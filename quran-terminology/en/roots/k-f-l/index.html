The Arabic root K F L concerns encompassing something, taking it upon oneself, and accepting responsibility for someone or something. A person who undertakes another’s kafālah accepts responsibility for their care or for fulfilling a commitment. Within this family, kafīl means a guarantor or one responsible, kāfil a guardian or provider, and kifl a share or portion.

The Qur’an uses this root for Mary’s guardianship, the care of Moses in infancy, guaranteeing a covenant, requesting the transfer of a sheep, sharing in the consequences of actions, and receiving divine mercy. Dhū al-Kifl is also the name of a figure whom the Qur’an includes among the patient and the good.

A connection between these uses can be recognized in accepting something within one’s responsibility or allotted share. Nevertheless, caring for people, guaranteeing an undertaking, and sharing in a reward or consequence are distinct uses that should not receive an identical translation.

1. The root in early Arabic

Historical definition and semantic core

In early Arabic, the K F L family is associated with two recognizable fields: encompassing and holding something in place, and accepting a responsibility, commitment, or portion that falls to a person.

Lexicons record kifl as a covering or cloth fastened around a camel’s hump or toward the rear of the riding position, providing a secure place for a rider to sit. Kafal is also recorded for the hindquarters of a human or animal.

Ibn Fāris describes the root’s core as follows:

أَصْلٌ صَحِيحٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى تَضَمُّنِ الشَّيْءِ لِلشَّيْءِ

Aṣlun ṣaḥīḥun yadullu ʿalā taḍammuni l-shayʾi li-l-shayʾ.

“A sound root indicating one thing’s encompassing another.”

In this definition, taḍammun means containing or including. When something lies within another thing or its covering, the latter encompasses and holds it.

This image suggests a relationship between a covering that supports a rider and human responsibility: a kafīl takes responsibility for another and becomes answerable for fulfilling an undertaking or safeguarding that person’s interests.

Ibn Fāris’s definition is nevertheless a lexical analysis compiled centuries after the Qur’an. Lexicons record the riding cover, but they do not establish the precise date of that usage. Nor can all the senses of guardianship and sharing be traced with historical certainty to the name of this cover.

Evidence from early poetry

A verse attributed to al-Aʿshā, the renowned poet whose life extended from the pre-Islamic period into the early Islamic era, reads as follows. Attribution alone does not establish the precise date of this particular verse:

غَيْرَ مِيلٍ وَلَا عَوَاوِيرَ فِي الْهَيْجَا
وَلَا عُزَّلٍ وَلَا أَكْفَالِ

Ghayra mīlin wa-lā ʿawāwīra fī l-hayjā / wa-lā ʿuzzalin wa-lā akfāl.

“Neither unsteady riders nor weaklings in battle, neither unarmed men nor riders unable to keep their seat.”

Akfāl in this verse is the plural of kifl. It describes riders who lack a secure seat or sufficient riding skill. The verse itself does not specify the direction in which they slip.

The witness places the word in the setting of riding and warfare. Here kifl does not mean guardian or guarantor; it concerns the rider’s seat and ability.

Lexicons also record kafala bihi for guaranteeing someone, kafala yatīman for undertaking an orphan’s care, and takaffala bi-l-shayʾi for accepting responsibility for a matter.

The precise age of each expression requires a distinction between an independent pre-Qur’anic attestation and its recording in a later dictionary. The riding verse does not by itself establish the sense of legal guarantee. Clear Qur’anic uses of kafālah, however, show that responsibility and guardianship were recognized senses at the time of revelation.

From encompassing to responsibility and a share

In human relationships, kafālah involves more than simply being alongside someone. A kafīl accepts a responsibility: caring for a child, meeting someone’s needs, guaranteeing a commitment, or undertaking a specified duty.

When kifl means a share, it concerns a portion assigned to a person, whether a share in good or in a harmful consequence.

These branches should not be collapsed into an artificial single definition. Commitment and responsibility stand out in kafālah; in kifl as an allotment, the emphasis is the share a person receives.

2. Qur’anic forms

The root K F L occurs ten times in the Qur’an in five morphological categories:

Basic verb, Form I: yakfulu, yakfuluhu, and yakfulūnahu, in Āl ʿImrān 3:44, Ṭā Hā 20:40, and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:12.

Form II: kaffalahā, in Āl ʿImrān 3:37, placing Mary in Zechariah’s guardianship.

Form IV: akfilnīhā, in Ṣād 38:23, requesting that something be entrusted to the speaker’s responsibility or control.

The noun kifl: kiflun, kiflayni, and al-kifli, in An-Nisāʾ 4:85, Al-Ḥadīd 57:28, Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:85, and Ṣād 38:48. The last two occur within the name Dhū al-Kifl.

The noun kafīl: kafīlan, in An-Naḥl 16:91, in the context of guaranteeing and honoring a covenant.

Other forms, including kāfil, kafālah, takafful, makfūl, and kufalāʾ, occur in Arabic but not in these forms in the Qur’an.

3. Qur’anic guardianship: Mary and Moses

Āl ʿImrān 3:37: Zechariah becomes responsible for Mary

فَتَقَبَّلَهَا رَبُّهَا بِقَبُولٍ حَسَنٍ وَأَنْبَتَهَا نَبَاتًا حَسَنًا وَكَفَّلَهَا زَكَرِيَّا ۖ كُلَّمَا دَخَلَ عَلَيْهَا زَكَرِيَّا الْمِحْرَابَ وَجَدَ عِنْدَهَا رِزْقًا ۖ قَالَ يَا مَرْيَمُ أَنَّىٰ لَكِ هَٰذَا ۖ قَالَتْ هُوَ مِنْ عِنْدِ اللَّهِ ۖ إِنَّ اللَّهَ يَرْزُقُ مَنْ يَشَاءُ بِغَيْرِ حِسَابٍ

Fa-taqabbalahā rabbuhā bi-qabūlin ḥasanin wa-anbatahā nabātan ḥasanan wa-kaffalahā Zakariyyā. Kullamā dakhala ʿalayhā Zakariyyā l-miḥrāba wajada ʿindahā rizqan. Qāla yā Maryamu annā laki hādhā. Qālat huwa min ʿindi llāh. Inna llāha yarzuqu man yashāʾu bi-ghayri ḥisāb.

“So her Lord accepted her with a gracious acceptance, nurtured her with a good growth, and placed her in Zechariah’s care. Whenever Zechariah entered the sanctuary where she was, he found provision with her. He said, ‘Mary, where does this come to you from?’ She said, ‘It is from The God. Indeed, The God provides for whomever He wills without reckoning.’”

Al-Imran 3:37

Here, guardianship forms part of Mary’s good upbringing. Her mother had made a vow before her birth, and The God accepted Mary graciously. Zechariah then undertook her guardianship.

In the widely used reading transmitted by Ḥafṣ, kaffalahā is a Form II verb. Its subject refers to The God: He made Zechariah responsible for Mary.

In Nāfiʿ’s reading, the expression is kafalahā Zakariyyāʾu, with the basic verb and Zechariah as its subject: Zechariah undertook Mary’s care.

These readings express related aspects: Mary’s placement in Zechariah’s care and Zechariah’s undertaking that care. The difference lies in the verb’s structure and the role of its subject.

Guardianship here does not mean that Zechariah owned Mary. It expresses responsibility for care and upbringing, not unrestricted power over the life and will of a person in one’s care.

The verse also distinguishes Zechariah’s guardianship from the provision Mary received from The God. Zechariah was her guardian, while the ultimate source of provision is attributed to The God.

Āl ʿImrān 3:44: determining Mary’s guardian

ذَٰلِكَ مِنْ أَنْبَاءِ الْغَيْبِ نُوحِيهِ إِلَيْكَ ۚ وَمَا كُنْتَ لَدَيْهِمْ إِذْ يُلْقُونَ أَقْلَامَهُمْ أَيُّهُمْ يَكْفُلُ مَرْيَمَ وَمَا كُنْتَ لَدَيْهِمْ إِذْ يَخْتَصِمُونَ

Dhālika min anbāʾi l-ghaybi nūḥīhi ilayka wa-mā kunta ladayhim idh yulqūna aqlāmahum ayyuhum yakfulu Maryama wa-mā kunta ladayhim idh yakhtaṣimūn.

“This is among the accounts of the unseen that We reveal to you. You were not with them when they cast their pens as to which of them would care for Mary, nor were you with them when they disputed.”

Al-Imran 3:44

Here yakfulu Maryama means undertaking responsibility for Mary’s guardianship.

Several people disputed this responsibility, and the Qur’an describes their casting pens to determine who would undertake it.

Guardianship was thus a specific responsibility whose assumption was disputed. This usage shows that responsibility can be the subject of agreement, selection, or an appointment.

The word kafala alone does not establish absolute authority over another person. The responsibility being undertaken must be understood from its object and context.

Ṭā Hā 20:40 and Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:12: caring for Moses in infancy

In Ṭā Hā 20:40, the account of Moses’ infancy describes his sister offering to identify someone who would undertake his care.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:12 relates the same event in greater detail:

وَحَرَّمْنَا عَلَيْهِ الْمَرَاضِعَ مِنْ قَبْلُ فَقَالَتْ هَلْ أَدُلُّكُمْ عَلَىٰ أَهْلِ بَيْتٍ يَكْفُلُونَهُ لَكُمْ وَهُمْ لَهُ نَاصِحُونَ

Wa-ḥarramnā ʿalayhi l-marāḍiʿa min qablu fa-qālat hal adullukum ʿalā ahli baytin yakfulūnahu lakum wa-hum lahu nāṣiḥūn.

“And We had already barred wet nurses from him. Then she said, ‘Shall I direct you to a household who will care for him for you and be sincerely concerned for his welfare?’”

Al-Qasas 28:12

Yakfulūnahu here means undertaking responsibility for the child’s care.

Because the verse mentions wet nurses, nursing and feeding Moses were important parts of this responsibility. Yet K F L does not by itself mean only breastfeeding. Guardianship may also include care, meeting needs, and attending to a child.

The expression wa-hum lahu nāṣiḥūn, “and they are sincerely concerned for his welfare,” also matters. Nāṣiḥūn belongs to a different root and expresses their goodwill. Responsibility for care and sincere concern are conveyed through two related but separate expressions.

The narrative then describes Moses being returned to his mother. Guardianship in this setting therefore does not sever the child’s bond with his mother or transfer ownership of him. It is a responsibility for care that can be entrusted to an appropriate person or household.

These four verses concerning Mary and Moses show that human kafālah is principally responsible care for another person’s affairs and needs.

4. Kafīl and guarantee: commitment before The God and people

An-Naḥl 16:91: The God as guarantor of a covenant

وَأَوْفُوا بِعَهْدِ اللَّهِ إِذَا عَاهَدْتُمْ وَلَا تَنْقُضُوا الْأَيْمَانَ بَعْدَ تَوْكِيدِهَا وَقَدْ جَعَلْتُمُ اللَّهَ عَلَيْكُمْ كَفِيلًا ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ يَعْلَمُ مَا تَفْعَلُونَ

Wa-awfū bi-ʿahdi llāhi idhā ʿāhadtum wa-lā tanquḍū l-aymāna baʿda tawkīdihā wa-qad jaʿaltumu llāha ʿalaykum kafīlan. Inna llāha yaʿlamu mā tafʿalūn.

“Fulfill the covenant of The God when you make a covenant, and do not break oaths after confirming them, having made The God a guarantor over you. Indeed, The God knows what you do.”

An-Nahl 16:91

Here kafīlan occurs in the setting of covenants and oaths.

When people make a covenant and confirm it in the name of The God, they invoke Him as witness and guarantor of their commitment. Deliberately breaking it is consequently more than disloyalty to the other party: it disregards a commitment confirmed in His name.

In this usage, a kafīl is one to whom guarantee and responsibility are attributed. Attributing this role to The God should nevertheless not be equated with a limited legal guarantee between human beings.

The verse concludes by describing The God as knowing what people do. His role as guarantor in this context is thus associated with knowledge and accountability concerning covenants and their fulfillment.

This is a descriptive use of kafīl concerning The God. The verse uses the indefinite form kafīlan, not the definite title al-Kafīl. His description as guarantor can therefore be discussed in this setting without constructing a separate list of divine names from definiteness or indefiniteness alone.

Ṣād 38:23: akfilnīhā and a request for transfer

إِنَّ هَٰذَا أَخِي لَهُ تِسْعٌ وَتِسْعُونَ نَعْجَةً وَلِيَ نَعْجَةٌ وَاحِدَةٌ فَقَالَ أَكْفِلْنِيهَا وَعَزَّنِي فِي الْخِطَابِ

Inna hādhā akhī lahu tisʿun wa-tisʿūna naʿjatan wa-liya naʿjatun wāḥidatun fa-qāla akfilnīhā wa-ʿazzanī fī l-khiṭāb.

“This is my brother. He has ninety-nine ewes, and I have one ewe. He said, ‘Entrust her to me,’ and overpowered me in speech.”

Sad 38:23

Akfilnīhā is a Form IV imperative. It requests that the ewe be entrusted to the speaker’s responsibility or control.

The man with ninety-nine ewes asks the owner of one ewe to hand that one over as well. Here the request is more than an offer to care for the ewe voluntarily on its owner’s behalf: it also implies adding her to what the other man controls.

The following verse states:

قَالَ لَقَدْ ظَلَمَكَ بِسُؤَالِ نَعْجَتِكَ إِلَىٰ نِعَاجِهِ ۖ وَإِنَّ كَثِيرًا مِنَ الْخُلَطَاءِ لَيَبْغِي بَعْضُهُمْ عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ إِلَّا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَعَمِلُوا الصَّالِحَاتِ وَقَلِيلٌ مَا هُمْ ۗ وَظَنَّ دَاوُودُ أَنَّمَا فَتَنَّاهُ فَاسْتَغْفَرَ رَبَّهُ وَخَرَّ رَاكِعًا وَأَنَابَ

Qāla laqad ẓalamaka bi-suʾāli naʿjatika ilā niʿājihi wa-inna kathīran mina l-khulaṭāʾi la-yabghī baʿḍuhum ʿalā baʿḍin illā lladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū l-ṣāliḥāti wa-qalīlun mā hum. Wa-ẓanna Dāwūdu annamā fatannāhu fa-staghfara rabbahu wa-kharra rākiʿan wa-anāb.

“He said, ‘He has certainly wronged you by asking for your ewe to add to his ewes. Many partners indeed wrong one another, except those who believe and do righteous deeds—and few they are.’ David realized that We had tested him, so he sought his Lord’s forgiveness, fell down bowing, and turned back.”

Ṣād 38:24

This connection makes clear that the dispute concerned a request to add another person’s property to one’s own and overpowering them in speech.

The two verses describe ewes and a request to transfer one of them. They do not identify the ewes as a certain number of wives or connect the dispute to a particular personal episode in David’s life; such details cannot be established from the wording of these verses.

This usage shows that K F L alone does not determine whether an action is good or bad. Caring for Mary and Moses appears in a favorable context, whereas the request to transfer another’s property in David’s story involves injustice.

5. Kifl as a share or portion

An-Nisāʾ 4:85: a share in harmful shafāʿah

مَنْ يَشْفَعْ شَفَاعَةً حَسَنَةً يَكُنْ لَهُ نَصِيبٌ مِنْهَا ۖ وَمَنْ يَشْفَعْ شَفَاعَةً سَيِّئَةً يَكُنْ لَهُ كِفْلٌ مِنْهَا ۗ وَكَانَ اللَّهُ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ مُقِيتًا

Man yashfaʿ shafāʿatan ḥasanatan yakun lahu naṣībun minhā wa-man yashfaʿ shafāʿatan sayyiʾatan yakun lahu kiflun minhā wa-kāna llāhu ʿalā kulli shayʾin muqītā.

“Whoever joins in good shafāʿah will have a share in it; whoever joins in bad shafāʿah will have a portion of it. The God is watchful and powerful over all things.”

An-Nisa 4:85

Here kifl means a share or portion.

The Qur’an distinguishes two kinds of shafāʿah—joining in support or intervention: good shafāʿah, directed toward a right action and helping another, and harmful shafāʿah, through which someone participates in wrongdoing by supporting or intervening for it.

Naṣīb is used for good shafāʿah and kifl for harmful shafāʿah.

The difference in vocabulary deserves attention, but it does not mean that kifl always denotes a share of sin. Al-Ḥadīd 57:28 uses the same word for a portion of mercy.

In An-Nisāʾ 4:85, the negative character of the share comes from the adjective sayyiʾah, “bad,” and the context of harmful shafāʿah, not from the noun kifl itself.

The verse also extends individual responsibility into social conduct. Someone who intervenes for or assists a wrongful action cannot regard themselves as wholly separate from its consequences. They have a share in the action they helped bring about.

Al-Ḥadīd 57:28: two portions of mercy

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا اتَّقُوا اللَّهَ وَآمِنُوا بِرَسُولِهِ يُؤْتِكُمْ كِفْلَيْنِ مِنْ رَحْمَتِهِ وَيَجْعَلْ لَكُمْ نُورًا تَمْشُونَ بِهِ وَيَغْفِرْ لَكُمْ ۚ وَاللَّهُ غَفُورٌ رَحِيمٌ

Yā ayyuhā lladhīna āmanū ittaqū llāha wa-āminū bi-rasūlihi yuʾtikum kiflayni min raḥmatihi wa-yajʿal lakum nūran tamshūna bihi wa-yaghfir lakum wa-llāhu ghafūrun raḥīm.

“O you who believe, be mindful of The God and believe in His Messenger. He will give you two portions of His mercy, provide you with a light by which you walk, and forgive you. The God is Forgiving, Merciful.”

Al-Hadid 57:28

Kiflayni is the dual of kifl and means two shares or two portions.

The share here is in the mercy of The God. The same word used for a share in harmful shafāʿah in An-Nisāʾ 4:85 is used here for receiving mercy.

Together, these uses show that kifl has no fixed moral value. Whether the share is good or bad depends on what it is a share of.

The word kiflayni itself expresses two portions. It does not specify why they are apportioned in this way or connect each one independently with a particular act. Identifying the two portions requires evidence beyond the dual form.

Nor should the dual automatically be understood as twice the amount of reward received by every other person. The verse speaks of two portions of mercy and also promises light and forgiveness.

6. Dhū al-Kifl: a Qur’anic name whose identity is not explained

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:85

وَإِسْمَاعِيلَ وَإِدْرِيسَ وَذَا الْكِفْلِ ۖ كُلٌّ مِنَ الصَّابِرِينَ

Wa-Ismāʿīla wa-Idrīsa wa-dhā l-kifli kullun mina l-ṣābirīn.

“And Ishmael, Idris, and Dhū al-Kifl—all were among the patient.”

Al-Anbiya 21:85

Ṣād 38:48

وَاذْكُرْ إِسْمَاعِيلَ وَالْيَسَعَ وَذَا الْكِفْلِ ۖ وَكُلٌّ مِنَ الْأَخْيَارِ

Wa-dhkur Ismāʿīla wa-l-Yasaʿa wa-dhā l-kifli wa-kullun mina l-akhyār.

“And remember Ishmael, Elisha, and Dhū al-Kifl; all were among the good.”

Sad 38:48

Dhū al-Kifl combines dhū, “possessor” or “one who has,” with al-kifl. Its linguistic structure may be connected with “possessor of a share” or “one bearing a commitment or responsibility.”

The Qur’an does not, however, explain why he bears this name.

These two verses do not establish that he undertook a particular task, made a specified covenant, or assumed the guardianship of a people. Such details cannot be presented as the certain meaning of his name on the basis of these verses.

What the Qur’an does make clear is his inclusion alongside Ishmael, Idris, and Elisha, and his description in terms of patience and goodness. Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:86 also includes them among the righteous.

In these two passages the Qur’an does not explicitly apply the title Prophet to Dhū al-Kifl, although it places his name alongside Prophets.

The components of the name can thus be examined, but his historical identity and the definite reason for the name cannot be extracted from its etymology.

7. Comparison with related roots

K F L (kafl) and W K L (wakl)

Wakālah and tawkīl concern assigning a task to another or entrusting someone with affairs.

Accepting responsibility and guaranteeing are prominent in kafālah; in tawkīl, the emphasis is entrusting a matter to another.

Someone may undertake a child’s care and become their guardian, while in another matter a person appoints an agent to perform a particular task.

These relationships may coexist in practice, but kafālah should not be treated simply as agency or acting on another’s behalf.

K F L (kafl) and R AYN Y (r-ʿ-y; raʿy)

Raʿy basically concerns pasturing and caring for livestock; its extended uses also include care and attending to affairs.

Kafālah can likewise involve care, but accepted responsibility and guarantee are more prominent in it.

A person may care for someone without assuming formal guardianship or financial responsibility. Conversely, a guardian may accept responsibility for meeting someone’s needs without personally performing every act of care.

K F L (kafl) and Ḍ M N (ḍamn)

The Arabic root Ḍ M N covers encompassing and guaranteeing, making it close to K F L in this respect.

Not every use of K F L should nevertheless be translated as a financial guarantee. Caring for a child, sharing in mercy, and requesting the transfer of a ewe belong to different branches of its meaning.

Guaranteeing is one clear use of kafālah, not its entire meaning.

K F L (kafl) and N Ṣ B (naṣb)

Naṣīb belongs to N Ṣ B and denotes a share or portion in Qur’anic usage.

An-Nisāʾ 4:85 uses naṣīb and kifl for two kinds of shafāʿah. Their occurrence together shows their closeness in expressing a share.

Yet alongside a share or portion, K F L includes verbs of guardianship and guarantee whose meanings do not follow from the word naṣīb itself.

8. Translation choices for Forqan

For yakfulu, yakfuluhu, and yakfulūnahu in the verses about Mary and Moses, suitable translations include “cares for,” “undertakes his care,” and “they assume responsibility for caring for him.”

For wa-kaffalahā Zakariyyā in the widely used reading, “and placed her in Zechariah’s care” preserves the causative structure of Form II.

For akfilnīhā in Ṣād 38:23, “Entrust her to me” is natural. The contextual explanation can clarify that the request concerns transferring the ewe into the speaker’s control and property.

For kafīlan in An-Naḥl 16:91, “guarantor” or “one standing as surety” suits the connection with a covenant.

For kiflun in An-Nisāʾ 4:85, “share” or “portion” is suitable; for kiflayni in Al-Ḥadīd 57:28, “two shares” or “two portions.”

Dhū al-Kifl should remain a proper name. Possible explanations of its components belong in the root article, not in the direct translation of the name.

Conclusion

The Arabic root K F L is associated with encompassing and accepting responsibility. In human relationships, kafālah means undertaking care, guardianship, or a guarantee concerning someone or something. The noun kifl also carries the recorded sense of a share or portion.

The Qur’an uses the root for caring for Mary and Moses, commitment to a covenant, requesting the transfer of a ewe, sharing in the consequences of shafāʿah, and receiving a portion of divine mercy.

Kafālah does not by itself imply ownership, domination, or superiority over the person in one’s care. The value of the action depends on the responsibility, how it is fulfilled, and respect for the other party’s rights.

Nor is kifl as a share inherently positive or negative. A person may have a portion of the harmful consequences of an action or receive two portions of the mercy of The God.

Dhū al-Kifl is a Qur’anic name of someone among the patient and the good. The Qur’an does not explain the precise reason for the name or give the details of his life.

Forqan’s concluding definition:

K F L has a semantic core of encompassing and accepting something within one’s responsibility. In human relationships it concerns undertaking guardianship, care, or a guarantee; kifl denotes a share or portion that falls to someone. The exact sense of each form is determined by its structure and context.
