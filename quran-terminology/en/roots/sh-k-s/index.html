Contentiousness, difficult temperament and obstructive dealings: from trade and social relations in early Arabic to the Quran's conflicting partners

In early Arabic, SH K S concerns difficult temperament, incompatibility and troublesome dealings with others. Shakis or shaks describes someone difficult in social dealings, trade or agreement. Shakāsah names the trait; tashākus denotes mutual disagreement and conflict between two or more parties.

It is not limited to an unpleasant disposition. It can appear in unreasonable bargaining, resistance to reasonable agreement, repeated quarrels or insistence on opposition instead of cooperation. These are examples, not necessary intentions encoded in every use.

Older dictionaries explain it through difficulty and bad temperament. Early poetry also applies it to a cramped, difficult place. This may relate to constriction, but historical evidence is needed before making physical narrowness the origin of every interpersonal meaning.

The root occurs only once in the Quran, as mutashākisūn in Al-Zumar 39:29. It describes partners who disagree with one another concerning a person jointly under their control.

The Quran contrasts this with another person belonging exclusively to one man, then asks whether the two conditions are equal.

The parable concerns monotheism and devotion exclusively to God. Its lexical meaning, the dependent person's situation and its theological conclusion should be distinguished.

The core is difficulty and contentiousness in dealings; in reciprocal usage, opposition and conflict that impede agreement and cooperation.

1. Meaning in early Arabic

Difficult temperament and troublesome dealings

Rajulun shakisun describes someone of difficult temperament who is not accommodating in dealings with others.

Trade and transactions are a prominent setting in lexical explanations.

Two people negotiating a sale may disagree over price, delivery or quality. Such differences alone are not shakas.

Unreasonably rejecting every agreement, repeatedly changing conditions or deliberately obstructing the other party may illustrate it. Deliberate intent to cause difficulty is not necessarily part of every use's lexical meaning.

The emphasis is on obstructive contentiousness that makes interaction difficult, beyond a simple difference of opinion.

Early dictionaries explain shakas through difficulty and bad temperament.

Tashākasa al-qawmu describes a group behaving contentiously toward one another in trade or other dealings.

This establishes its place in interpersonal and social language.

This study has not established an independently datable pre-Islamic witness for every form. Older dictionaries clarify usage without proving when each form first arose.

A poetic witness: a cramped encampment in al-Hudhalī

In a verse attributed to ʿAbd Manāf ibn Ribʿ al-Hudhalī, traditionally classified as pre-Islamic, the speaker describes a night raid:

وَأَنَا الَّذِي بَيَّتُّكُمْ فِي فِتْيَةٍ
بِمَحَلَّةٍ شَكْسٍ وَلَيْلٍ مُظْلِمِ

wa-anā alladhī bayyattukum fī fityatin / bi-maḥallatin shaksin wa-laylin muẓlim

“I am the one who raided you at night with a band of young men, in a cramped encampment and a dark night.”

Bayyattukum denotes a night attack.

Maḥallah is a place of residence or encampment.

Shaks qualifies the place as cramped and difficult.

The line presents a constricted location and darkness. Detailed reconstruction of the attack and defense goes beyond its explicit wording.

This attributed early witness applies the family to a place, not only human temperament.

On the recorded lexical explanation, maḥallatin shaksin describes the place's constriction, not ill-tempered inhabitants.

That distinction should be retained.

A semantic analogy is possible: passage through a cramped place is difficult, as interaction with a contentious person meets obstacles.

That analogy does not prove that interpersonal meanings historically developed from the spatial sense.

Both usages are recorded; their exact historical sequence is unclear.

The attribution and traditional classification make this an important early witness, but neither independently establishes the exact date of the individual line.

A rajaz witness: a frowning, difficult person

A line transmitted in older dictionaries reads:

شَكْسٌ عَبُوسٌ عَنْبَسٌ عَذَوَّرُ

shaksun ʿabūsun ʿanbasun ʿadhawwar

“Contentious, frowning, fierce and rough.”

Shaks appears alongside several other descriptions.

ʿAbūs concerns a frowning face.

ʿAnbas is associated with the lion and here contributes fierceness and roughness.

ʿAdhawwar also concerns severity and rough behavior.

Together these terms portray someone difficult to deal with.

The transmitted citation does not securely identify its poet or date; it should not be presented as certainly pre-Islamic.

Its value is lexical: shaks denotes difficult temperament and troublesome interaction.

Frowning, violence and fierceness should not all be built into its fixed meaning. A contentious person need not frown, while illness or fatigue may produce a frown without contentious dealings.

Contentiousness and stinginess

An older Arabic description says of a person:

شَكِسٌ ضَبِسٌ أَلَدُّ مِلْحَسٌ

shakisun ḍabisun aladdu milḥas

“Difficult, severe, obstinately contentious and greedy.”

It continues that he draws back when asked for something but seizes an opportunity when given something.

This places shakis alongside severity, obstinate disputation and greed.

Some dictionaries also record shaks for a stingy person. That separate usage should not be inferred solely from the adjacent adjectives in this description.

It should not reduce the root's entire meaning to financial stinginess.

Someone may be difficult in transactions without being financially stingy, or reluctant to give money while being agreeable in other relationships.

Stinginess can accompany this trait in certain dealings without exhausting the family's meaning.

In human relations, shakas concerns difficulty and contentiousness manifested in dealings with others.

2. Important forms and derivatives

Principal recorded forms include:

Shakisa / yashkasu: to be or become difficult and contentious in temperament or dealings.

Shakas: difficult temperament or contentious dealings; variants in vocalization are recorded.

Shakāsah: difficult temperament and troublesome social or commercial dealings.

Shaks, shakis and shakus: adjectives describing a difficult, contentious person. Their recorded vocalizations should not all be presented as Quranic forms.

Shuks: a plural adjective for difficult, contentious people.

Shākis: someone behaving contentiously or obstructively toward others.

Shākasa: to quarrel or behave contentiously toward another.

Mushākasah: conflict, opposition or obstructive dealings with someone.

Tashākasa: two or more people behaved contentiously toward one another.

Tashākus: mutual opposition and contentiousness.

Mutashākis: involved in mutual disagreement and conflict.

Mutashākisūn: the sound masculine plural; people in conflict with one another. This is the Quranic form.

Mishkas: another recorded description of a difficult, contentious person.

Maḥallatun shaksun describes a cramped, difficult location.

A lunar usage for the period shortly before the crescent appears is also reported in some dictionaries. Its dating and relationship to contentiousness are not established here, so it is not used to reconstruct a certain common origin.

Only mutashākisūn occurs in the Quran.

Shaks, shakāsah, mushākasah, shākis and the verb tashākasa do not occur in those forms there.

3. Individual temperament and mutual contentiousness

Two situations should be distinguished.

First, one person's difficult temperament.

An individual may make trade, social relations or cooperation difficult by resisting agreement, refusing reasonable flexibility or behaving badly.

Second, mutual conflict among several parties.

Here two or more people clash, with conduct or demands conflicting.

The situations are related but not identical.

A difficult person may nevertheless agree with others on a particular matter.

Conversely, individuals who are pleasant on their own may enter a contentious relationship over a shared transaction or decision.

Mutashākisūn should therefore not be explained merely by pluralizing “ill-tempered.”

Form VI matters: disagreement occurs among multiple parties.

If those responsible for a shared undertaking reject one another's decisions and refuse cooperation, tashākus is an apt description.

If differing views are reconciled through discussion and consultation, plurality alone is not tashākus.

Tashākus is not mere diversity of people or views, but contentiousness that makes agreement and interaction difficult.

This is crucial in Al-Zumar 39:29.

4. Al-Zumar 39:29: the contentious partners

The Quran says:

ضَرَبَ اللَّهُ مَثَلًا رَجُلًا فِيهِ شُرَكَاءُ مُتَشَاكِسُونَ وَرَجُلًا سَلَمًا لِرَجُلٍ هَلْ يَسْتَوِيَانِ مَثَلًا الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ بَلْ أَكْثَرُهُمْ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ

ḍaraba allāhu mathalan rajulan fīhi shurakāʾu mutashākisūna wa-rajulan salaman li-rajulin hal yastawiyāni mathalan al-ḥamdu li-llāhi bal aktharuhum lā yaʿlamūn

Al-Zumar 39:29

“The God sets forth a parable: a man shared by contentious partners, and a man belonging exclusively to one man. Are the two equal in comparison? Praise belongs to The God; rather, most of them do not know.”

Two situations are contrasted.

In the first, a man is under several partners who conflict with one another.

In the second, a man belongs exclusively to one person.

The Quran asks whether their conditions are equal.

Several contentious owners

The first part reads:

رَجُلًا فِيهِ شُرَكَاءُ مُتَشَاكِسُونَ

rajulan fīhi shurakāʾu mutashākisūn

Al-Zumar 39:29

“A man shared by contentious partners.”

Shurakāʾ belongs to SH R K and denotes those sharing an interest in something.

Mutashākisūn belongs to SH K S and describes their relationship.

The phrase therefore combines plurality of partners and their contentiousness.

This distinction matters.

The verse does not merely mention several partners; it explicitly describes their mutual conflict.

Partners who agree and clearly divide responsibilities differ from those who continually oppose one another.

The depicted difficulty is not only the number of owners: their incompatibility is an essential element.

Living under conflicting commands

Imagine someone under the control of several owners.

One orders an act, another forbids it and a third demands something incompatible with both.

The person faces difficulty.

Obeying one may displease another; satisfying all may become practically impossible.

The difficulty is not merely workload, but conflict among those claiming authority.

Mutashākisūn is therefore decisive.

Removing it leaves a man with multiple partners but erases the emphasized conflict.

The adjective must not disappear behind a paraphrase such as “a man with several owners.”

Belonging exclusively to one person

The second part reads:

وَرَجُلًا سَلَمًا لِرَجُلٍ

wa-rajulan salaman li-rajul

Al-Zumar 39:29

“And a man belonging exclusively to one man.”

Salaman belongs to S L M and here expresses exclusive belonging.

Salaman is the site's base Ḥafṣ reading; another established reading is sāliman. Both concern exclusive belonging without competing ownership in this context.

Translating it merely as peace or inward tranquility would obscure the comparison between conflicting shared control and exclusive belonging.

The second person does not face several conflicting owners.

His relationship of authority and responsibility is with one identified party.

That can remove confusion caused by contradictory commands.

The phrase itself does not specify that this owner is kind, just or benevolent.

The comparison emphasizes unified authority against incompatible authorities.

The verse uses a parable drawn from the historical institution of slavery. Employing this image alone neither endorses every slavery relationship nor establishes human ownership as an ideal social model.

The image explains another matter, clarified by the surah's context.

Are the two equal in comparison?

The Quran asks:

هَلْ يَسْتَوِيَانِ مَثَلًا

hal yastawiyāni mathalā

Al-Zumar 39:29

“Are the two equal in comparison?”

Yastawiyāni is a dual imperfect from S W Y.

The question concerns equality between the conditions.

One person is under several contentious owners.

The other has a single owner.

Their conditions differ regarding authority and the ability to fulfill obligations.

The question does not condemn every partnership, collective administration or disagreement.

Its subject is contentious partners whose conflicting demands can entangle the dependent person.

Cooperative plurality is a different case on which this verse does not pronounce a separate general judgment.

5. The monotheistic meaning within Al-Zumar

The preceding passages clarify the parable.

Al-Zumar 39:2 says:

فَاعْبُدِ اللَّهَ مُخْلِصًا لَهُ الدِّينَ

fa-uʿbudi allāha mukhliṣan lahu al-dīn

Al-Zumar 39:2

“Worship The God, making devotion exclusively His.”

Al-Zumar 39:3 also says:

أَلَا لِلَّهِ الدِّينُ الْخَالِصُ

a-lā li-llāhi al-dīnu al-khāliṣ

Al-Zumar 39:3

“Indeed, exclusive devotion belongs to The God.”

It then describes those who take protectors apart from God and say:

مَا نَعْبُدُهُمْ إِلَّا لِيُقَرِّبُونَا إِلَى اللَّهِ زُلْفَىٰ

mā naʿbuduhum illā li-yuqarribūnā ilā allāhi zulfā

Al-Zumar 39:3

“We worship them only so that they may bring us nearer to The God.”

The issue is not simply denial of God's existence.

People may acknowledge God and claim to seek nearness to Him while giving other beings a place in worship. Applying the image to human claims of ultimate religious authority is a further contextual interpretation, not the lexical meaning of shakas.

Such divided worship conflicts with exclusive devotion.

Al-Zumar 39:29 explains this through an accessible image.

Serving several independent, incompatible authorities differs from having one ultimate object of devotion.

Mutashākisūn thus participates in a discussion of associating partners in worship, but does not itself mean polytheism.

Shurakāʾ names the partners.

Mutashākisūn describes their contentiousness.

Salaman expresses the other person's exclusive belonging.

Together these concepts complete the parable.

Monotheism and ultimate authority

Within Quranic monotheism, God is the sole deity and ultimate object of devotion.

People nevertheless relate to parents, families, communities, officials, teachers and specialists.

Those relationships are not inherently association of partners with God.

Consultation, expert advice and cooperation do not inherently conflict with monotheism either.

Al-Shūrā 42:38 describes consultation:

وَأَمْرُهُمْ شُورَىٰ بَيْنَهُمْ

wa-amruhum shūrā baynahum

Al-Shūrā 42:38

“Their affairs are conducted by consultation among them.”

Al-Zumar 39:29 does not require rejecting everyone else's independent judgment or placing all social affairs under one human being.

The proposed application concerns elevating a human or invented authority to independent authority alongside God.

For example, someone may know that bribery, oppression or violating rights is wrong yet justify it to satisfy family, a leader or a group.

The favored person's demands then conflict with what is right.

Knowingly abandoning right out of loyalty raises questions about authority and obedience, beyond ordinary respect or affection.

This illustrates how conflicting commands and loyalties can arise in life.

Not every disagreement between personal wishes and others' views constitutes association of partners with God. Such a judgment requires examination of the right at issue, the authority claimed and the person's response.

Conflicting authorities and responsibility

The parable makes the consequences of conflicting ownership tangible while addressing devotion to multiple objects of worship.

When authorities make separate demands without an ultimate criterion, a person may be caught between incompatible wishes.

An identified ultimate criterion allows other claims to be assessed.

In Al-Zumar, this concerns God and devotion exclusively to Him.

The parable should not be turned into unconditional obedience to a human leader.

The material image depicts one owner; its monotheistic application concerns God's uniqueness in worship and ultimate devotion.

This distinction prevents treating the verse as a justification of a ruler's, leader's or religious institution's despotism.

Exclusive devotion to God is not the same as granting independent divine authority to a human being.

The surrounding verses

Before the parable, Al-Zumar 39:27 says:

وَلَقَدْ ضَرَبْنَا لِلنَّاسِ فِي هَٰذَا الْقُرْآنِ مِنْ كُلِّ مَثَلٍ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَتَذَكَّرُونَ

wa-la-qad ḍarabnā li-l-nāsi fī hādhā al-qurʾāni min kulli mathalin laʿallahum yatadhakkarūn

Al-Zumar 39:27

“We have set forth for people in this Quran every kind of parable, so that they may take heed.”

Al-Zumar 39:28 then describes an Arabic Quran without crookedness.

Al-Zumar 39:29 supplies one such parable.

The conflicting partners help the hearer understand the conditions and reflect on the object of devotion.

Al-Zumar 39:30 and Al-Zumar 39:31 discuss death and disputing on the Day of Resurrection.

This brings responsibility and the outcome of human choices back into view.

Takhtaṣimūn in Al-Zumar 39:31 belongs to KH Ṣ M, not SH K S. Conceptual proximity does not make the roots identical.

6. Distinguishing related roots

SH K S and KH L F: disagreement

Ikhtilāf may describe differences between people, views, conduct or conditions.

Such differences are not necessarily blameworthy.

Two people may disagree about the best approach yet reach a shared decision through discussion.

There is disagreement without necessarily tashākus.

Quranic tashākus concerns mutual contentiousness that impedes agreement and cooperation.

Contentiousness may involve disagreement, but not every disagreement is contentiousness.

The partners in Al-Zumar 39:29 do not merely have different preferences; they are mutashākisūn.

SH K S and KH Ṣ M: disputation

Khuṣūmah concerns conflict and confrontation between parties.

It overlaps with tashākus in that respect.

Shakas can also describe difficult temperament before a particular dispute arises.

Tashākus approaches disputation when multiple parties clash.

Khuṣūmah can also concern litigation or presenting a dispute for judgment.

Two people defending competing ownership claims with evidence are not thereby both ill-tempered or mutashākis.

The two terms overlap in conflict without being identical.

SH K S and N Z ʿ: contention

Nizāʿ concerns conflict between parties.

Āl ʿImrān 3:152 mentions disputing over a matter; Al-Anfāl 8:46 forbids contention that leads to weakness and lost strength.

Such conflict has practical consequences for cooperation.

Tashākus likewise concerns mutual conflict, with a close connection to difficult temperament and obstructed agreement.

Partners may dispute one decision; mutashākisūn becomes especially apt when their conduct prevents accommodation.

This is not a fixed hierarchy: tashākus need not always precede nizāʿ, nor must nizāʿ always be more severe.

Context determines the conflict's nature and intensity.

SH K S and SH R S: roughness

Sharāsah concerns fierceness and rough behavior.

Dictionaries sometimes explain shaks and sharis through one another.

Sharāsah may emphasize rough temperament, while transaction-related shakas also emphasizes obstructiveness. This is a tendency in usage, not an absolute boundary.

Loud, harsh speech may illustrate sharāsah.

A quiet person may also continually obstruct fair solutions, illustrating shakas without overt aggression.

The families are close, but shakas should not be confined to visible anger or harshness.

SH K S and SH Ḥ Ḥ: greed and stinginess

Shuḥḥ concerns greed, stinginess and withholding.

Some dictionaries also describe a stingy person as shaks, since withholding benefits may involve obstructive dealings.

The terms are not identical.

Shuḥḥ emphasizes possessiveness and reluctance to relinquish or share benefit.

Shakas concerns difficult, contentious dealings.

They may coexist, as when greed drives continual obstruction in transactions.

Neither trait alone proves the other.

SH K S and SH R K: partnership

The roots occur together in Al-Zumar 39:29 but remain distinct.

Shurakāʾ, from SH R K, means partners.

Mutashākisūn, from SH K S, means mutually contentious.

Partnership itself is not shakas.

Partners can manage an undertaking cooperatively and faithfully.

The Quranic partners are specifically described as contentious.

The image's theological application concerns associating partners in worship; shakas retains its lexical meaning of contentiousness.

SH K S must also be distinguished from SH K K, doubt. Conflict can cause uncertainty, but mutashākisūn does not mean doubters.

7. Morphology and proposed Forqan renderings

The Quranic form is:

مُتَشَاكِسُونَ

mutashākisūn

Al-Zumar 39:29

“Contentious with one another.”

It is the sound masculine plural of the active participle of Form VI tashākasa.

The morphological sequence is:

  • Basic verb: shakisa
  • Form VI verb: tashākasa
  • Singular active participle: mutashākis
  • Sound masculine plural: mutashākisūn

In Al-Zumar 39:29 it is nominative and qualifies shurakāʾ.

It therefore refers to the partners, not the man under their control.

This grammatical point matters.

The verse describes the owners' mutual conflict, not the first man's difficult temperament.

It should not be rendered as “a contentious man who has several partners.”

The role of Form VI

Form VI can indicate reciprocal action between two or more parties.

That reciprocity is important in tashākasa.

Shākasahu can describe one person's contentiousness toward another, whereas tashākasā portrays the two in a mutually contentious relationship.

Mutashākisūn places several parties in that relationship.

The form alone does not assign equal responsibility or equal intensity to every participant.

Some may initiate a conflict and others enter it in response.

Morphology indicates reciprocity here, not each party's share of responsibility.

Suggested renderings

Shakisa: became difficult, acted contentiously or behaved obstructively.

Shakas and shakāsah: difficult temperament, contentiousness or troublesome dealings.

Shaks / shakis / shakus: difficult, contentious or ill-tempered.

Shākasa: acted contentiously or obstructively toward another.

Mushākasah: contentiousness, conflict or obstructive dealings.

Tashākasa: acted contentiously toward one another or entered mutual conflict.

Tashākus: mutual contentiousness or conflict between parties.

Mutashākis: involved in mutual conflict or incompatible with the other parties.

Mutashākisūn: contentious parties or people in conflict with one another.

The principal proposed rendering in Al-Zumar 39:29 is:

رَجُلًا فِيهِ شُرَكَاءُ مُتَشَاكِسُونَ

rajulan fīhi shurakāʾu mutashākisūn

Al-Zumar 39:29

“A man shared by contentious partners.”

This is natural and preserves the basic construction. These proposals do not change the site's installed verse translations.

To make reciprocity more explicit:

“A man in whom several partners, in conflict with one another, have shares.”

That longer formulation is less fluent in Persian.

“Contentious partners” therefore suits the base translation, with a lexical explanation clarifying mutual conflict rather than merely individual ill temper.

“Different partners” is inadequate, since difference need not entail conflict or obstruction.

“Hostile partners” may sometimes approximate the result, but the word does not necessarily establish lasting enmity or intent to harm.

“Oppressive partners” likewise adds injustice not established by mutashākisūn alone.

“Contentious partners” best preserves their mutual incompatibility in demands and conduct.

8. Conclusion

SH K S concerns difficult temperament, contentiousness and troublesome interaction in early Arabic.

Applied to a person, it describes difficulty in social dealings, transactions or agreement. Reciprocally, tashākus concerns conflict among two or more parties.

The verse attributed to ʿAbd Manāf ibn Ribʿ al-Hudhalī applies it to a cramped, difficult place; other lexical witnesses concern human behavior.

A semantic relationship between spatial and interpersonal difficulty is conceivable, but historical priority and a common origin are not certain.

Only mutashākisūn occurs in the Quran, in Al-Zumar 39:29.

It describes partners who conflict over a person jointly under their control.

The Quran contrasts this with a man belonging exclusively to one owner and asks whether the two are equal.

The first image combines multiple partners and their conflict; the second emphasizes exclusive belonging.

Within exclusive devotion to God, the parable contrasts conflicting claims with one ultimate object of worship.

SH K S itself does not mean polytheism, disbelief or misguidance; those concerns arise from the theological context.

The parable does not condemn every disagreement or partnership, nor identify unconditional obedience to a human with monotheism.

Its defensible core is difficult temperament or interaction and, reciprocally, conflict that impedes agreement and coordination.

The Persian equivalent meaning incompatible or contentious is a natural base rendering, conveying troublesome behavior and lack of coordination.

Al-Zumar 39:29 makes contentiousness part of the image, not merely plurality. Omitting the adjective or reducing it to simple disagreement erases an essential element.

The image concerns devotion: several independent authorities claiming incompatible ultimate control can divide loyalty, while monotheism identifies the ultimate object of devotion.

This theological meaning comes from the parable and context; SH K S independently retains its meaning of difficult temperament and mutual contentiousness.
