Knowing and becoming aware: the limits of human knowledge and informing another

1. Introducing the root and its semantic field

Arabic D R Y concerns knowing, becoming aware, and recognizing something. Darā means to know or become aware; adrā means to inform or convey knowledge to another.

Early Arabic dirāyah names knowledge and awareness, acquired through observation, reports, examination, experience, or other means. The word alone does not specify the method or completeness of that knowledge.

Dictionaries also record seeking game, approaching it stealthily, targeting a place, and using a pointed implement. Their historical relationship with knowing is not equally clear in every case.

The Qur’an uses two principal structures: Form I, such as tadrī and adrī, and Form IV, such as adrāka and yudrīka.

In the first, someone knows or does not know; in the second, someone is informed or given knowledge.

The familiar wa-mā adrāka and wa-mā yudrīka belong here. They introduce momentous, unknown, or explanation-requiring subjects and matter to Qur’anic expression.

Qur’anic uses repeatedly acknowledge limited human knowledge: what tomorrow will bring, where one will die, or the reality of certain events.

Such limits also appear in addressing Muḥammad. He is commanded to say “I do not know” concerning some matters while learning others through revelation.

The root should not become absolute ignorance or omniscience. Dirāyah concerns knowing a specified matter; its negation denies that knowledge within the sentence’s scope.

2. Qur’anic forms and early Arabic meanings

In the text of Ḥafṣ from ʿĀṣim, the root occurs 29 times in 28 verses, in two morphological structures.

Form I: twelve occurrences, including tadrūna, tadrī, adrī, nadrī, and adri.

These occur in Al-Nisāʾ 4:11, Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:109, Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:111, Luqmān 31:34, Al-Shūrā 42:52, Al-Jāthiyah 45:32, Al-Aḥqāf 46:9, Al-Ṭalāq 65:1, Al-Ḥāqqah 69:26, Al-Jinn 72:10, and Al-Jinn 72:25. Luqmān 31:34 contains two occurrences.

Form IV: seventeen occurrences, including adrāka, adrākum, and yudrīka.

These occur in Yūnus 10:16, Al-Aḥzāb 33:63, Al-Shūrā 42:17, Al-Ḥāqqah 69:3, Al-Muddaththir 74:27, Al-Mursalāt 77:14, ʿAbasa 80:3, Al-Infiṭār 82:17, Al-Infiṭār 82:18, Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:8, Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:19, Al-Ṭāriq 86:2, Al-Balad 90:12, Al-Qadr 97:2, Al-Qāriʿah 101:3, Al-Qāriʿah 101:10, and Al-Humazah 104:5.

Changes in tense, person, and attached pronouns do not create a new root.

Other important Arabic forms include:

Darā–yadrī: knew, became aware, or recognized something.

Dirāyah: awareness, knowledge, and knowing.

Dary and darayān: recorded verbal nouns for knowing.

Diryah: knowledge or awareness; another use concerns seeking game.

Adrā: informed someone or conveyed knowledge.

Idrāʾ: informing, the verbal noun associated with adrā.

Dārā: treated someone gently or considerately; in some early uses, dealt cautiously or stealthily with another.

Al-dariyyah: an animal or device used to approach game unseen; elsewhere, a target for practicing shooting or spear thrusts.

Al-midrā and al-midrāh: a pointed hairdressing implement; also an animal’s horn in some uses.

Iddarā and tadarrā: sought, targeted, or cautiously approached game in certain early constructions.

These illuminate the family, but only Form I and Form IV verbs occur in the Qur’an.

The noun dirāyah does not occur there in that form, nor do mudārāh, midrā, or dariyyah.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: twelve Form I and seventeen Form IV verbs

Basic meaning: knowing and becoming aware

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

دَرَى الشَّيْءَ ... عَلِمَهُ

darā al-shayʾa ... ʿalimahu

“He darā the thing,” meaning he knew it.

For Form IV it states:

وَأَدْرَاهُ بِهِ: أَعْلَمَهُ

wa-adrāhu bihi: aʿlamahu

“He informed him about it.”

These explain the principal Qur’anic distinction.

Darā attributes knowing to its subject; adrā describes a person or cause making someone else aware.

“I know this” differs from “I informed you about this.” The latter conveys knowledge or provides the means for knowing.

Form IV alone does not establish whether speech, writing, observation, or revelation informs the person. Context determines that.

Yūnus 10:16 concerns informing people about the Qur’an. Mā adrāka asks what could have made the addressee aware of a subject.

Informing is clear, while the method and extent of knowledge vary by verse.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Muḥkam: knowing and informing

Does dirāyah mean knowledge acquired through a stratagem?

Al-Rāghib defines it in Al-Mufradāt as:

الْمَعْرِفَةُ الْمُدْرَكَةُ بِضَرْبٍ مِنَ الْحِيَلِ

al-maʿrifatu al-mudrakatu bi-ḍarbin mina al-ḥiyali

Knowledge attained through some means of resourcefulness or contrivance.

This might initially seem to require a stratagem or indirect effort in every case.

Early Arabic ḥīlah, however, is not always morally deceptive; it can mean a means or strategy for reaching a goal.

Even accepting that explanation, it should not become necessary to every use of the root.

The Qur’an allows awareness through revelation. Yūnus 10:16 connects informing people of the Qur’an with divine will; mā adrāka expressions introduce matters explained afterward.

Such uses do not require the addressee to have acquired knowledge through personal searching or ingenuity.

“Knowing or becoming aware,” and “informing” for Form IV, therefore fit Qur’anic usage better.

Effort, strategy, or searching may occur in historical uses and particular contexts without becoming compulsory meanings of every derivative.

Al-Rāghib’s Mufradāt: dirāyah and resourcefulness

3. Other early Arabic uses

Intending and seeking a target

Ibn Fāris examines two principles for the non-hamzated D R Y family in Maqāyīs al-Lughah.

One concerns intending something and seeking it; the other concerns sharpness or pointed objects.

The first includes targeting a place for attack or movement toward it.

Forms of iddarā describe a group targeting a place for a raid.

This concerns intending a particular target, rather than knowing a report.

The branch also appears in hunting uses.

Hunting and dariyyah

Dariyyah could name an animal used to conceal a hunter approaching game.

The hunter might hide behind a domestic animal so the game would not notice.

The hunter could then approach within range and shoot.

This involves targeting, watching carefully, and approaching stealthily.

It does not make Qur’anic dirāyah mean ambushing, deceiving game, or concealing oneself.

Lexicographers differ over connecting non-hamzated dariyyah with D R Y or hamzated darīʾah with D R ʾ.

Some assign the hunting word to the first; others to the second through pushing forward or screening oneself.

Similar-looking forms should not all be assigned to one root without derivational examination.

An early poetic witness

A verse attributed to Suḥaym ibn Wathīl al-Riyāḥī reads:

أَتَتْنَا عَامِرٌ مِنْ أَرْضِ رَامٍ
مُعَلَّقَةَ الْكَنَائِنِ تَدَّرِينَا

atatnā ʿāmirun min arḍi rāmin / muʿallaqata al-kanāʾini taddarīnā

“ʿĀmir came to us from the land of Rām, with quivers hanging, intending us as their target.”

Taddarīnā concerns intending or targeting here.

It differs from knowing a subject.

Suḥaym lived across the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods. Without dating this particular verse, it cannot securely be called pre-Qur’anic.

It nevertheless witnesses a traditional Arabic use involving intending and pursuing a target.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: targeting, hunting, and the taddarīnā witness

Sharp horns and a hairdressing implement

Dictionaries describe al-midrā as a pointed implement for untangling and arranging matted hair.

It could be made from wood, iron, or another material.

Midrā also names a sharp animal horn.

Al-Nābighah al-Dhubyānī says:

شَكَّ الْفَرِيصَةَ بِالْمِدْرَى فَأَنْفَذَهَا
طَعْنَ الْمُبَيْطِرِ إِذْ يَشْفِي مِنَ الْعَضَدِ

shakka al-farīṣata bi-al-midrā fa-anfadhahā / ṭaʿna al-mubayṭiri idh yashfī mina al-ʿaḍadi

“He pierced the soft flank with his sharp horn and drove it through, like a veterinarian’s thrust when treating disease.”

Midrā names the animal’s sharp horn here.

The verse is transmitted in Al-Nābighah’s collected poetry and early commentaries, illustrating the sharpness branch.

It does not prove that Qur’anic dirāyah derives from a horn or hairdressing implement.

Ibn Fāris treats this branch separately from intending and becoming aware.

The family is better presented through documented branches than a single invented material origin.

Al-Nābighah’s Dīwān: the sharp horn and midrā witness

Considerate treatment and knowledge

Modern Persian mudārā concerns gentleness, consideration, and measured conduct.

Arabic dārāhu likewise concerns gentleness and cautious treatment.

The history and derivation of some dārā and dāraʾa forms are disputed.

Hamzated dāraʾa belongs to D R ʾ and can concern repelling or resisting.

Some non-hamzated uses instead describe kindness and consideration.

Mudārāh and dirāyah should not become equivalents in every use.

Knowledge may inform considerate behavior, but Qur’anic darā does not itself mean treating someone gently.

4. Knowing and not knowing in Qur’anic usage

Limited knowledge of relatives’ benefit: Al-Nisāʾ 4:11

After inheritance rulings, Al-Nisāʾ 4:11 states:

آبَاؤُكُمْ وَأَبْنَاؤُكُمْ لَا تَدْرُونَ أَيُّهُمْ أَقْرَبُ لَكُمْ نَفْعًا

ābāʾukum wa-abnāʾukum lā tadrūna ayyuhum aqrabu lakum nafʿan

You do not know which of your fathers and sons is nearer to you in benefit.

Lā tadrūna limits human knowledge concerning a particular comparison.

People may know much about parents and children, including their needs, capacities, and some benefits.

Yet this does not suffice for a definitive judgment about all their future benefit.

This matters within inheritance rulings: affection, immediate interests, or personal estimates of relatives’ worth may influence how someone wishes to determine their rights.

Tadrūna simply means “you know.” The justice of inheritance distribution follows from the whole verse.

It does not deny all reliable knowledge about benefit in life.

The negation concerns knowing decisively which relative is more beneficial in the comparison at issue.

The future and place of death: Luqmān 31:34

Luqmān 31:34

إِنَّ اللَّهَ عِنْدَهُ عِلْمُ السَّاعَةِ وَيُنَزِّلُ الْغَيْثَ وَيَعْلَمُ مَا فِي الْأَرْحَامِ وَمَا تَدْرِي نَفْسٌ مَاذَا تَكْسِبُ غَدًا وَمَا تَدْرِي نَفْسٌ بِأَيِّ أَرْضٍ تَمُوتُ إِنَّ اللَّهَ عَلِيمٌ خَبِيرٌ

inna Allāha ʿindahu ʿilmu al-sāʿati wa-yunazzilu al-ghaytha wa-yaʿlamu mā fī al-arḥāmi wa-mā tadrī nafsun mādhā taksibu ghadan wa-mā tadrī nafsun bi-ayyi arḍin tamūtu inna Allāha ʿalīmun khabīrun

“With The God is knowledge of the Hour; He sends down rain and knows what is in the wombs. No soul knows what it will earn tomorrow, and no soul knows in what land it will die. The God is Knowing, Aware.”

Tadrī occurs twice here.

The first concerns not knowing what someone will earn tomorrow.

The second concerns not knowing where death will occur.

Both address limited knowledge of the future.

This does not prohibit planning or deny every reliable forecast.

Experience, evidence, and present conditions can support strong probabilities about future events.

Forecasting differs from certain knowledge of every condition and actual outcome.

Knowing a person’s illness, age, or health does not give certain knowledge of the exact place and time of death.

The verse ends by describing God as ʿalīmun khabīrun.

Human limitations are contrasted with divine knowledge.

Dirāyah should not therefore mean inherently incomplete knowledge, nor ʿilm inherently complete knowledge in every Arabic use.

Extent follows the subject, object, and context, rather than the roots alone.

Not knowing what follows divorce: Al-Ṭalāq 65:1

Al-Ṭalāq 65:1 states:

لَا تَدْرِي لَعَلَّ اللَّهَ يُحْدِثُ بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ أَمْرًا

lā tadrī laʿalla Allāha yuḥdithu baʿda dhālika amran

You do not know; perhaps God will bring about a new situation afterward.

Context concerns divorce, counting the waiting period, and not expelling women from their homes during the prescribed period.

Lā tadrī recalls limited knowledge about the relationship’s future and circumstances after divorce.

Someone deciding now may not know the eventual result, and a new situation may arise later.

Context can connect this new situation with possible reconciliation.

Lā tadrī does not itself guarantee reunion. Possibility and hope arise from laʿalla and context.

It should not become a definite promise of a specific event.

Knowledge before revelation: Al-Shūrā 42:52

Al-Shūrā 42:52

وَكَذَٰلِكَ أَوْحَيْنَا إِلَيْكَ رُوحًا مِنْ أَمْرِنَا مَا كُنْتَ تَدْرِي مَا الْكِتَابُ وَلَا الْإِيمَانُ وَلَٰكِنْ جَعَلْنَاهُ نُورًا نَهْدِي بِهِ مَنْ نَشَاءُ مِنْ عِبَادِنَا وَإِنَّكَ لَتَهْدِي إِلَىٰ صِرَاطٍ مُسْتَقِيمٍ

wa-kadhālika awḥaynā ilayka rūḥan min amrinā mā kunta tadrī mā al-kitābu wa-lā al-īmānu wa-lākin jaʿalnāhu nūran nahdī bihi man nashāʾu min ʿibādinā wa-innaka la-tahdī ilā ṣirāṭin mustaqīmin

“Thus We revealed to you a spirit of Our command. You did not know what the Book or faith was, but We made it a light through which We guide whom We will of Our servants. You indeed guide toward a straight path.”

Mā kunta tadrī addresses Muḥammad.

It describes a prior lack of knowledge concerning the Book and faith in the setting of revelation.

Later theological assumptions should not empty this direct statement of meaning: the Qur’an describes a stage of not knowing in his life.

Its object must also be retained: what the Book and faith are.

Within revelation and guidance, it cannot establish without further study that he previously knew no moral truth, belief in God, or upright conduct.

Conversely, it should not be narrowed until no actual lack of knowledge about the Book and faith remains.

New knowledge and guidance through revelation are clearly stated.

Here too dirāyah concerns knowing, with revelation expanding it.

Not knowing the outcome: Al-Aḥqāf 46:9

Al-Aḥqāf 46:9

قُلْ مَا كُنْتُ بِدْعًا مِنَ الرُّسُلِ وَمَا أَدْرِي مَا يُفْعَلُ بِي وَلَا بِكُمْ إِنْ أَتَّبِعُ إِلَّا مَا يُوحَىٰ إِلَيَّ وَمَا أَنَا إِلَّا نَذِيرٌ مُبِينٌ

qul mā kuntu bidʿan mina al-rusuli wa-mā adrī mā yufʿalu bī wa-lā bikum in attabiʿu illā mā yūḥā ilayya wa-mā anā illā nadhīrun mubīnun

“Say: I am no unprecedented messenger, and I do not know what will be done with me or with you. I follow only what is revealed to me, and I am only a clear warner.”

Muḥammad is commanded to acknowledge not knowing the outcome specified.

The following clause states what he follows: what is revealed to him.

Being a messenger does not mean independently knowing every future event.

Nor does the verse deny every knowledge of the future, since revelation may communicate some of it.

Independent knowledge differs from knowledge received through reports or revelation.

Wa-mā adrī genuinely states limited knowledge of a specified subject.

Acknowledging ignorance: Al-Jāthiyah 45:32 and Al-Ḥāqqah 69:26

In Al-Jāthiyah 45:32, a group responds to news of the Hour by saying they do not know what it is and have only conjecture.

Mā nadrī is that group’s speech.

It is not Qur’anic endorsement that the Hour’s reality is unknowable to everyone.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:26 depicts someone receiving their record and wishing they had never known their account.

Wa-lam adri mā ḥisābiyah concerns knowledge of that account and its result.

Knowing and not knowing can thus appear in speech shaped by emotion and judgment.

Darā itself does not mean doubt, fear, regret, or wishing.

Those come from other parts of the discourse.

Jinn’s ignorance and limited knowledge of the future

Al-Jinn 72:10 states:

وَأَنَّا لَا نَدْرِي أَشَرٌّ أُرِيدَ بِمَنْ فِي الْأَرْضِ أَمْ أَرَادَ بِهِمْ رَبُّهُمْ رَشَدًا

wa-annā lā nadrī a-sharrun urīda bi-man fī al-arḍi am arāda bihim rabbuhum rashadan

“We do not know whether harm is intended for those on earth or their Lord intends right guidance for them.”

This speech is attributed to jinn.

Their ignorance concerns the cause or purpose of an event they encountered.

Jinn are therefore not presented as possessing unlimited knowledge.

Al-Jinn 72:25 also commands Muḥammad to say he does not know whether the promised event is near or his Lord appoints a longer term.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:109 and Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:111 likewise use wa-in adrī.

In these expressions in is negative: “I do not know,” rather than necessarily introducing a condition.

Together these distinguish a messenger’s and other beings’ knowledge from what has not been disclosed to them.

5. Adrā: informing another

Yūnus 10:16: becoming aware of the Qur’an

Yūnus 10:16

قُلْ لَوْ شَاءَ اللَّهُ مَا تَلَوْتُهُ عَلَيْكُمْ وَلَا أَدْرَاكُمْ بِهِ فَقَدْ لَبِثْتُ فِيكُمْ عُمُرًا مِنْ قَبْلِهِ أَفَلَا تَعْقِلُونَ

qul law shāʾa Allāhu mā talawtuhu ʿalaykum wa-lā adrākum bihi fa-qad labithtu fīkum ʿumuran min qablihi a-fa-lā taʿqilūna

“Say: Had The God willed, I would not have recited it to you, nor would He have made it known to you. I lived among you a lifetime before it. Will you not reason?”

Adrākum is Form IV.

It concerns informing the audience.

Its subject is understood within divine will through the construction and context.

Law shāʾa Allāhu opens the statement, relating recitation and awareness of the Qur’an to God’s will.

Talawtuhu and adrākum bihi differ.

Recitation concerns reading and presenting the text.

Adrākum concerns informing the audience about it.

These may coincide within one event without sharing identical meanings.

A discourse may be recited without listeners grasping every meaning; informing someone is also not restricted to one manner of recitation.

This does not establish two wholly separate temporal stages. Both appear in one construction describing the Qur’an reaching people.

Al-Qurṭubī: the subject of informing and reading variation in Yūnus 10:16

Study of T L W

Does adrā guarantee complete understanding?

Arabic explains adrāhu through aʿlamahu, informed him.

Informing does not guarantee complete understanding.

Someone may learn that something exists without knowing every detail.

Yūnus 10:16 likewise does not give every hearer the same degree of understanding.

The Qur’an distinguishes hearing, knowing, understanding, believing, and acting.

Form IV concerns informing, while the audience’s comprehension and response require further context.

6. Wa-mā adrāka and wa-mā yudrīka

These two questioning expressions are among the root’s most prominent Qur’anic uses.

Both are Form IV and concern informing.

Their tense and structure genuinely differ.

Adrāka is past: what made you aware?

Yudrīka is present: what makes you aware?

Natural Persian can use expressions equivalent to “what do you know of...” or “what has informed you that...,” according to context.

Wa-mā adrāka: a question that highlights its subject

Mā adrāka occurs thirteen times: twelve with wa-, and once with thumma in Al-Infiṭār 82:18.

These occur in Al-Ḥāqqah 69:3, Al-Muddaththir 74:27, Al-Mursalāt 77:14, Al-Infiṭār 82:17, Al-Infiṭār 82:18, Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:8, Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:19, Al-Ṭāriq 86:2, Al-Balad 90:12, Al-Qadr 97:2, Al-Qāriʿah 101:3, Al-Qāriʿah 101:10, and Al-Humazah 104:5.

A subject is named and a question about the addressee’s knowledge draws attention to its importance and character.

For example:

Al-Qadr 97:2

وَمَا أَدْرَاكَ مَا لَيْلَةُ الْقَدْرِ

wa-mā adrāka mā laylatu al-qadri

“And what has made you know what the Night of Decree is?”

Al-Qadr 97:3 then states its superiority to a thousand months.

The question is not merely seeking an answer from the addressee; it highlights the subject and prepares for further explanation.

Al-Ṭāriq 86:2 asks about al-ṭāriq; Al-Ṭāriq 86:3 introduces al-najmu al-thāqibu.

Al-Humazah 104:5 asks about al-ḥuṭamah; Al-Humazah 104:6 describes God’s kindled fire.

In Al-Qāriʿah 101:10, wa-mā adrāka mā hiyah is followed in Al-Qāriʿah 101:11 by nārun ḥāmiyah.

These examples connect wa-mā adrāka with explanations in following verses.

Does every wa-mā adrāka supply a complete definition?

A familiar lexical and exegetical observation says that this expression is followed by information about its subject.

Many examples support this literary observation.

Some explanation nevertheless differs from a complete and final definition.

Al-Mursalāt 77:14 asks about the Day of Decision, followed in Al-Mursalāt 77:15 by woe to that day’s deniers.

This clarifies its importance and consequences without necessarily defining every aspect of its reality.

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:8 and Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:19 ask about Sijjīn and ʿIlliyyūn; Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:9 and Al-Muṭaffifīn 83:20 connect them with kitābun marqūmun. The exact referent of that phrase is debated, and the brief explanation does not disclose every detail of either concept.

The expression generally prepares for further introduction or explanation, rather than invariably giving every final detail.

D R Y itself does not mean wonder, grandeur, punishment, or resurrection. These arise from the subject and literary setting.

Al-Rāghib’s Mufradāt: explanation after mā adrāka and contrast with mā yudrīka

Wa-mā yudrīka: attention to uncertainty and possibility

This expression occurs three times:

  • Al-Aḥzāb 33:63
  • Al-Shūrā 42:17
  • ʿAbasa 80:3

ʿAbasa 80:3

وَمَا يُدْرِيكَ لَعَلَّهُ يَزَّكَّىٰ

wa-mā yudrīka laʿallahu yazzakkā

“And what makes you know? Perhaps he will purify himself.”

The question addresses limited knowledge about another person.

Laʿallahu introduces possibility.

The outcome is not already certain to the addressee.

This matters ethically in ʿAbasa: outward appearance and social standing may influence judgments about someone’s readiness or worthiness of attention.

The verse recalls the possibility of growth and purification.

That follows from the whole context; yudrīka alone does not mean purification.

Wa-mā yudrīka and knowledge of the Hour

Al-Shūrā 42:17

اللَّهُ الَّذِي أَنْزَلَ الْكِتَابَ بِالْحَقِّ وَالْمِيزَانَ وَمَا يُدْرِيكَ لَعَلَّ السَّاعَةَ قَرِيبٌ

Allāhu alladhī anzala al-kitāba bi-al-ḥaqqi wa-al-mīzāna wa-mā yudrīka laʿalla al-sāʿata qarībun

“The God sent down the Book with truth and the balance; what makes you know? Perhaps the Hour is near.”

Al-Aḥzāb 33:63 similarly describes people asking about the Hour and recalls limited knowledge.

Wa-mā yudrīka accompanies its possible nearness in these cases.

Laʿalla does not fix a definite time.

The verse can call for taking possible nearness seriously without specifying a date.

The expression should not therefore establish that the Hour necessarily occurs shortly after the first revelation.

Precise temporal nearness or distance does not follow from the root.

Comparing the expressions

Wa-mā adrāka often appears as a question followed by introducing or explaining a significant subject.

The three wa-mā yudrīka uses concern limited knowledge about a possible or future matter.

This is a real tendency in Qur’anic usage.

Tense alone should not become an absolute rule that mā adrāka always supplies complete knowledge while mā yudrīka makes future knowledge impossible.

The following verses determine what information is given.

Both concern becoming aware. Wonder and heightened emphasis are functions of the question and context, rather than independent root meanings.

7. D R Y and neighboring roots

D R Y and ʿ L M: dirāyah and knowledge

ʿ L M is among D R Y’s closest semantic neighbors.

Both can concern knowing.

Dictionaries commonly explain darā al-shayʾa through ʿalimahu.

Qur’anic ʿ L M has a wide field encompassing divine knowledge, human knowledge, teaching, signs, and other subjects.

All Qur’anic D R Y uses instead fall within the verbs knowing and informing.

Lā tadrī and mā adrī repeatedly express limited human awareness.

This does not make dirāyah inherently incomplete and ʿilm inherently complete.

The Qur’an also uses ʿ L M for human ignorance.

The roots should not become a fixed hierarchy of knowledge’s worth or degree.

Darā concerns knowing or not knowing specified matters; adrā concerns informing someone else.

Study of ʿ L M

D R Y and D R K: knowing and attaining

Spelling and modern Persian meaning may cause confusion.

D R Y ends in yāʾ; D R K ends in kāf.

D R K fundamentally concerns reaching and attaining, including death overtaking, pursuers catching up, or visual apprehension.

Dirāyah in Qur’anic usage concerns knowing and awareness.

The fields can overlap because attaining knowledge can be understood as reaching an object.

Yet their Qur’anic functions remain distinct.

Al-Anʿām 6:103 concerns eyes’ apprehension; Luqmān 31:34 concerns knowing the future.

Idrāk should not always mean understanding, nor dirāyah physical arrival.

Study of D R K

D R Y and ʿ R F: awareness and recognition

ʿ R F concerns knowing and recognizing.

Someone may recognize a face, mark, or characteristic and distinguish a person from others.

Darā can likewise concern knowledge of something.

Qur’anic D R Y more often concerns knowing a particular report, reality, or situation.

A person does not know what tomorrow will bring or where they will die.

Some Qur’anic ʿ R F uses concern recognizing things through their distinguishing features.

This is a tendency, not an absolute rule for every Arabic use.

Study of ʿ R F

D R Y and SH ʿ R: awareness and sensing

Qur’anic SH ʿ R concerns awareness or perception and often occurs negatively.

Lā yashʿurūna can describe people unaware of a reality or their behavior’s consequences.

It is semantically close to lā yadrūna.

SH ʿ R has a broader Arabic field involving sensing, subtle perception, and signs.

Qur’anic darā concerns knowing a particular matter.

Lā tadrī should not invariably become “you do not feel.”

Al-Ṭalāq 65:1 concerns not knowing what may happen later, rather than merely lacking an inward feeling.

Study of SH ʿ R

D R Y and KH B R: knowing and information

Khabar and khabīr concern awareness of reality.

Luqmān 31:34 follows limited human dirāyah with God described as ʿalīmun khabīrun.

Knowing and being informed therefore share a broad cognitive field.

D R Y itself does not mean receiving a report from someone else.

Knowledge can arise through reports, observation, or another method.

Divine khabīr should not be reduced to someone knowing an everyday piece of news.

Study of KH B R

D R Y and D R ʾ: awareness and repelling

The roots look similar but have different final consonants.

Qur’anic D R Y concerns knowing and informing.

Qur’anic D R ʾ concerns repelling or warding off.

Al-Raʿd 13:22 describes repelling evil with good.

Adrākum in Yūnus 10:16 belongs to D R Y, not D R ʾ.

Some lexical and reading reports discuss a hamzated form, but resemblance does not replace informing with repelling.

Adrākum here concerns making the Qur’an known to people, rather than driving them away from it.

Study of D R ʾ

D R Y and D R S: knowing and studying

Qur’anic D R S concerns reading and studying books.

Dirāyah concerns knowing and becoming aware.

Study can lead to knowledge without guaranteeing correct understanding.

Someone may repeatedly study a book and misunderstand it.

Someone may also know through experience or observation without studying a book.

Neither study nor dirāyah should be restricted to the other.

Study of D R S

Durriyy belongs to another family

Durriyyun in Al-Nūr 24:35 describes a brilliant star.

The familiar explanation of the unhamzated reading connects it with durr, pearl, and D R R; sources also allow simplification of hamzah from D R ʾ.

Some hamzated readings are likewise explained through D R ʾ.

Spelling resemblance should not make durriyy mean knowledgeable or aware through D R Y.

These three families require distinction.

Al-Qurṭubī: pearl derivation of durriyy and possible hamzah simplification

Study of D R R

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

The most suitable general Persian equivalent for Form I is knowing.

Form IV is suitably rendered informing or making aware.

Tadrī, tadrūna, adrī, and nadrī

Retain tense, person, and affirmation or negation.

Lā tadrī: you do not know, singular.

Lā tadrūna: you do not know, plural.

Mā adrī: I do not know.

Lā nadrī: we do not know.

Mā kunta tadrī: you did not know.

With future events, “you do not predict” is not always appropriate: prediction and certain knowledge differ.

Nor should knowing always become understanding. The issue may be missing information about the future rather than inability to comprehend.

Adrākum

In Yūnus 10:16:

“He made it known to you.”

This preserves the distinction from recitation.

Wa-mā adrāka

A question may be rendered:

“And what has made you know...?”

This stays close to Form IV.

More idiomatic Persian equivalents of “what do you know of what ... is?” can convey the emphatic question, while the lexical explanation retains informing.

Great, terrifying, astonishing, or mysterious should not be inserted as direct meanings of adrāka. They depend on the question’s subject and context.

Wa-mā yudrīka

In ʿAbasa 80:3, Al-Aḥzāb 33:63, and Al-Shūrā 42:17, natural equivalents include:

“And what makes you know?”

Or, more idiomatically:

“And how do you know?”

The sentence and its connection with laʿalla should guide selection.

With the Hour, the expression does not declare a certain imminent date; it draws attention to limited knowledge concerning its time and possibility.

Dirāyah in Persian

Although the Arabic noun does not occur in the Qur’an, dirāyat is familiar in Persian and Dari.

It can include sound judgment, understanding a situation, and wise decision-making beyond knowing.

These Persian extensions should not automatically enter every Qur’anic verb.

Mā adrī in Al-Aḥqāf 46:9 means “I do not know,” not “I lack wisdom or sound judgment.”

Admitting ignorance of one matter does not necessarily imply lacking reason or comprehension.

9. Conclusion

Qur’anic D R Y principally concerns knowing and becoming aware. Form I describes knowing a subject; Form IV concerns informing another.

Other recorded Arabic forms concern targeting, seeking game, and pointed implements.

Ibn Fāris examines several branches. A single material origin should not be invented without adequate evidence.

Nor must every Qur’anic dirāyah involve strategy or ingenuity. Revelation, reports, observation, and other means may provide knowledge.

The root occurs 29 times in 28 verses.

Form I particularly highlights limited human awareness: future benefit from relatives, tomorrow’s earnings, the place of death, and other events not certainly known.

Muḥammad is also repeatedly commanded to acknowledge limits. Being a messenger does not mean independent, unlimited knowledge of all events; revealed knowledge must be distinguished from what has not been disclosed.

Form IV emphasizes informing.

Mā adrāka occurs thirteen times—twelve with wa- and once with thumma—generally introducing a subject emphatically and preparing for further explanation.

The three wa-mā yudrīka occurrences concern limited awareness of a possible or future matter.

This does not make one always supply complete knowledge and the other categorically prohibit later awareness.

Dirāyah relates to knowledge, apprehension, recognition, sensing, information, and study without completely matching any in every use.

Knowing a subject does not itself mean understanding all its dimensions; not knowing one subject does not mean lacking reason or all awareness.

Proposed final root definition: Qur’anic D R Y concerns knowing or being aware of a specified matter. Form IV extends this to informing another. The kind, source, degree, and limits of knowledge follow the subject, object, and context rather than the root alone. Brief root meaning: Knowing or being aware of a matter; in Form IV, informing another.
