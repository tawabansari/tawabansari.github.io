Height, loftiness, and conspicuous elevation: from tall mountains to human uses in Arabic

In early Arabic, SH M KH concerns height, elevation, and towering stature. Shamakha al-jabalu describes a mountain standing high, while shāmikh describes something conspicuous in height.

This usage highlights noticeable height, rather than merely one thing being above another. A mountain rising above the surrounding land, visibly prominent to an observer, may be called shāmikh.

The same family also describes people. Shamakha bi-anfihi means that someone raised his head and nose in self-importance and behaved arrogantly. Physical elevation here becomes associated with an inward attitude and social conduct.

The Quran uses this root only as shāmikhātin in Al-Mursalāt 77:27. It describes mountains and their height. Its Quranic meaning should therefore first be understood in this physical setting, without importing pride, spiritual greatness, or moral dignity into it.

1. The root in early Arabic

The height and loftiness of mountains

Arabic dictionaries record shamakha al-jabalu yashmakhu shumūkhan for a mountain rising or standing high.

Jabalun shāmikhun and jabalun shammākhun likewise describe a tall mountain; al-jibālu al-shawāmikhu means lofty, towering mountains.

The physical sense is clear: the height of something rising above its surroundings.

For example, among mountains of different sizes, one may be visible from afar because of its striking height. Arabic can describe such a mountain as shāmikh.

This description does not specify the summit’s shape, the slope, the rock, or how the mountain formed. Its summit might be pointed, broad, or multiple; shāmikh highlights height.

The root’s physical image can thus be described as towering stature and conspicuous elevation.

Limits of the historical evidence

Early Arabic dictionaries clearly record height and loftiness, while the Quranic shāmikhātin directly attests this meaning in the Arabic of revelation.

However, a meaning recorded in dictionaries compiled centuries later does not by itself prove that every form and usage was current in precisely that form before Islam.

An earlier literary quotation, presented without a poet’s name in the lexicographical passage examined, reads:

نَرَى شُمَّخَ الْأَطْوَادِ مِنْ شُمِّ خِنْدِفٍ

narā shummakha al-aṭwādi min shummi khindifin

ذُرَاهُنَّ فِي ضَحْضَاحِ بَحْرِكَ تَغْرَقُ

dhurāhunna fī ḍaḥḍāḥi baḥrika taghraqu

“We see the lofty nobles of Khindif as towering mountains whose summits sink in the shallows of your sea.”

The verse likens the lofty nobles of Khindif to towering mountains whose summits sink even in the shallows of the addressee’s sea. This is hyperbolic praise; reading the sea specifically as generosity is an interpretation of the image, not the lexical meaning of SH M KH.

Shummakha al-aṭwād evokes towering mountains. In this poetic setting, their height serves as an image of the stature of the men being compared to them.

The quotation examined supplies no poet’s name, and this study has not established its date. It cannot therefore serve as conclusive evidence of pre-Quranic usage. It does show a literary usage recorded in the Arabic lexicographical tradition.

Conspicuous elevation is thus clear in the Arabic of revelation, while claims about when every branch arose must remain within the available evidence.

Human loftiness: from raised posture to arrogance

Shamakha bi-anfihi and shamakha anfuhu describe someone raising his head and nose out of pride and self-importance.

A bodily action here becomes associated with an inward attitude.

Raising the head can mean different things in different situations. Someone may look upward to see something or stand upright confidently. In the established idiom shamakha bi-anfihi, however, arrogance belongs to the idiomatic usage itself.

When shumūkh describes an arrogant person, it is connected with mountain height, but its moral force comes from the human usage and the established expression.

This does not make everything shāmikh morally arrogant. A mountain neither feels pride nor intends to show off. The mountain description concerns physical height; the human description concerns conduct and attitude.

Extended expressions such as ḥasabun shāmikhun or nasabun shāmikhun describe elevated family or social standing.

Physical height is here extended to social rank. Distinguished ancestry or status does not by itself establish moral superiority.

Distance and extent

Some lexicographical accounts record niyyatun shamakhun and related forms for a distant destination or journey.

This usage concerns extent and distance. Height and remoteness may be associated through spatial distance, but that resemblance does not establish which historically arose from the other.

The Quran does not use SH M KH for distance or travel. This lexical branch helps show the language’s range but should not be added to shāmikhātin in Al-Mursalāt.

2. Core meaning and Quranic forms

Only shāmikhātin from SH M KH occurs in the Quran.

It is the sound feminine plural of shāmikhah, the feminine of shāmikh. Shāmikh is the active participle of the basic triliteral verb shamakha.

Other Arabic forms include shamakha, yashmakhu, shumūkh, shāmikh, shammākh, and shawāmikh, but these exact forms do not occur in the Quran.

In Al-Mursalāt 77:27, shāmikhātin modifies rawāsiya.

Rawāsiya refers to firmly established mountains; shāmikhātin expresses their height.

Grammatically, shāmikhātin is an accusative adjective. As a sound feminine plural, it takes kasrah as its accusative marker. In connected recitation its ending therefore has kasrah and tanwīn, rather than fatḥah.

Its feminine plural form does not make mountains biologically or humanly female; it belongs to Arabic grammar.

The Quranic core may be defined as:

The conspicuous height of something rising above the surrounding surface or environment.

This is natural for mountains. In human expressions, pride, self-importance, or social standing must be identified separately from the construction and context.

3. Shāmikhāt: lofty mountains in the Quran

Al-Mursalāt 77:27 states:

وَجَعَلْنَا فِيهَا رَوَاسِيَ شَامِخَاتٍ وَأَسْقَيْنَاكُمْ مَاءً فُرَاتًا

wa-jaʿalnā fīhā rawāsiya shāmikhātin wa-asqaynākum māʾan furātā

Al-Mursalāt 77:27

Translation:

“And We placed in it firmly established, lofty mountains, and gave you fresh water to drink.”

This verse continues the description of the earth and the provisions made for human life.

Al-Mursalāt 77:25 describes the earth as kifātan: a place that gathers and contains beings.

Al-Mursalāt 77:26 mentions the living and the dead. Al-Mursalāt 77:27 then mentions firm, lofty mountains and fresh water.

Shāmikhāt thus belongs to a set of concrete descriptions of the earth.

Rawāsī and shāmikhāt

Rawāsiya shāmikhātin combines two words from different roots.

Rawāsiya comes from R S W, concerning settlement, firmness, and stability. In verses about the earth it refers to firmly established mountains.

Shāmikhātin comes from SH M KH and emphasizes those mountains’ height and loftiness.

The descriptions complement one another without being complete synonyms.

Firmness concerns placement and stability; shumūkh concerns height and conspicuous prominence.

A large rock, for example, can be very firmly placed without being particularly tall. Conversely, something tall can lack structural or positional stability.

The Quranic expression attributes both features to the mountains.

This distinction is also discussed in the root study of R S W.

Study of R S W

Why add shāmikhāt to rawāsī?

Elsewhere rawāsiya appears without shāmikhātin, including Al-Raʿd 13:3, Al-Ḥijr 15:19, and Qāf 50:7.

Al-Mursalāt 77:27 adds shāmikhāt, highlighting another aspect of the mountains.

The reader encounters not only firmly established mountains but also their conspicuous height.

This makes the earth’s description more tangible: a land containing tall, towering mountains.

The added adjective does not make height the lexical cause of stability, nor does the expression define a particular physical relationship between mountain height and the earth’s stability.

Two features are described; their scientific or geological relationship is a separate subject.

Does shāmikhāt specify mountain shape?

Shāmikh describes considerable height, not a particular geometry.

It does not require every mountain mentioned to have a pointed summit, vertical slopes, or the same shape.

Nor does it specify a numerical height. In ordinary language, the relevant degree of height may vary with the surroundings and subject.

“Tall, lofty mountains” is therefore appropriate. “Pointed mountains,” “vertical mountains,” or mountains of a prescribed height add details absent from the word.

Mountains and fresh water

The same verse continues:

وَأَسْقَيْنَاكُمْ مَاءً فُرَاتًا

wa-asqaynākum māʾan furātā

Al-Mursalāt 77:27

“And gave you fresh water to drink.”

Mountains and fresh water appear together as part of the earth’s description and its provisions.

Mountains can be associated with rainfall, snow, springs, rivers, and water flow. But their juxtaposition with fresh water here does not alone supply a complete theory of the origins or formation of all fresh water.

Water storage, spring formation, and river guidance should especially not be extracted from SH M KH itself.

Shāmikhāt describes mountain height; furāt describes the water. The wider relationship between these provisions in nature is a separate subject of study.

Mountain height and transformation

Some Quranic verses describe firm, lofty mountains; others describe their transformation and the disappearance of their current condition.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:5, for example, speaks of mountains being crushed. Al-Nabaʾ 78:20 describes them being set in motion and becoming a mirage.

These descriptions do not lexically contradict shāmikhāt.

A mountain’s being lofty in one condition does not mean it can never change.

Shāmikhāt describes height, not a promise of everlasting existence. The root establishes neither indestructibility nor the physical mechanism of the mountains’ transformation at resurrection.

4. Comparison with related roots

SH M KH and R S W

Quranic shumūkh concerns striking height and loftiness.

Rusūw concerns settlement and stability.

In Al-Mursalāt 77:27, rawāsiya shāmikhātin combines both concepts for mountains.

Stability should not be inferred from shumūkh, nor striking height treated as an indispensable part of rusūw.

SH M KH and ʿ L W

ʿUluww has a broad range, concerning being above, superiority, and elevation in space or rank.

Shumūkh likewise expresses height, but its familiar uses more readily evoke towering stature and conspicuous elevation.

ʿĀlī and shāmikh may thus be close in describing a mountain without being interchangeable throughout the language.

ʿUluww can concern two things’ spatial relationship, social position, or claimed superiority. Quranic shumūkh focuses on the mountains’ perceptible height.

This is a semantic tendency, not an absolute rule for every Arabic usage.

SH M KH and R F ʿ

Rafʿ often concerns raising, lifting, or moving something to a higher position.

The description shāmikh primarily expresses the condition of standing tall.

Lifting something from the ground can be described with rafʿ. A high mountain can be called shāmikh without the word explaining how its height came about.

Shāmikhāt in Al-Mursalāt 77:27 therefore describes lofty mountains, rather than meaning “mountains that were raised.”

SH M KH and B S Q

Busūq concerns height and upward growth, with a familiar application to date palms.

Qāf 50:10 calls the palms bāsiqātin: tall date palms.

Shāmikhāt also expresses height, but describes mountains in Al-Mursalāt 77:27.

Both involve elevation. In the familiar palm usage, busūq can highlight upward growth and extension; shumūkh describes a mountain’s conspicuous height and loftiness.

This distinction is not exclusive: B S Q is not confined to plants, nor SH M KH to mountains.

For a wider discussion, see Forqan’s root study of B S Q.

Study of B S Q

SH M KH and K B R

K B R concerns greatness or largeness in various uses and, in certain human constructions, self-importance and arrogance.

Shumūkh concerns physical elevation when describing a mountain. In shamakha bi-anfihi it can describe arrogant human behavior.

Human shumūkh can thus be associated with arrogance, but that association should not give Quranic shāmikhāt the meaning of arrogance.

The mountains are lofty; they are not behaving arrogantly.

5. Challenges of understanding and translation

In modern Persian, words meaning “magnificent,” “honored,” “proudly dignified,” and “stately” sometimes accompany shāmikh.

In some sentences these may describe beauty or grandeur, but they are not all direct equivalents of shāmikh.

For example, Persian sarafrāz generally has a positive evaluative force when applied to someone who lives with honor and dignity.

The Arabic usage of shāmikh concerning arrogance can instead carry a negative value.

These differences show why every use of shumūkh should not be assigned one fixed moral value.

For mountains, “high,” “lofty,” and “tall” are closer to the physical meaning.

A personifying expression such as “tall in stature” can suit literary prose, but “high” and “lofty” are clearer for direct translation.

“Great mountains” does not fully convey shāmikhāt: greatness can concern volume, extent, or overall size, whereas shumūkh focuses on height.

A mountain can be broad and massive yet less prominent in height.

Height should therefore remain in the translation; grandeur inferred from the setting or reflection can be discussed separately.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Suitable equivalents of shāmikh include “high,” “lofty,” and “tall and prominent.”

For mountains, “lofty” preserves both height and the natural image of conspicuous elevation.

Two suitable renderings of shāmikhātin in Al-Mursalāt 77:27 are:

“Tall and lofty”

Or, more briefly:

“High”

Since it accompanies rawāsiya, both descriptions should remain clear in translation.

A proposed translation of the complete verse is:

“And We placed in it firmly established, lofty mountains, and gave you fresh water to drink.”

“Firmly established” conveys rawāsī, while “lofty” conveys shāmikhāt. These proposals do not alter the installed verse translations.

In another text describing human arrogance, “haughtiness,” “self-importance,” or “raising one’s head arrogantly” may suit shumūkh. Such meanings should not be transferred to the Quran’s lofty mountains.

Conclusion

SH M KH concerns height, elevation, and loftiness. A tall, prominent mountain is shāmikh. Human uses may concern self-importance, arrogance, or elevated status, but each usage’s exact meaning and value depend on its subject and construction.

Lexical evidence clearly records elevation and loftiness, and the Quran directly uses the root for mountains. The precise historical origin of every semantic branch cannot be established from later dictionary explanations alone.

The Quran uses SH M KH only in Al-Mursalāt 77:27, as shāmikhātin.

It modifies rawāsiya and emphasizes mountain height. “Firmly established” and “lofty” express distinct features: stability and conspicuous elevation.

This usage does not specify a geometric shape, measurement, geological mechanism, or moral value.

The final definition for the Quranic usage is therefore:

SH M KH expresses conspicuous height and loftiness; shāmikhātin in Al-Mursalāt 77:27 describes mountains rising prominently above the surrounding land.

Translation should preserve this meaning. Other features—stability, material, shape, and natural function—must be established from other words and independent evidence.
