Hunting and taking game: from pursuing free-ranging animals to sea game, land game, and its prohibition during consecration

1. Introducing the root and its Qur’anic forms

The Arabic root ص ي د (Ṣ Y D) is chiefly associated with pursuing and taking animals that live freely in nature and are not readily under human control. صاد (ṣāda) means to take or hunt; صائد (ṣāʾid) denotes the hunter; and صيد (ṣayd) names both hunting and the animal hunted or taken as game.

Hunting may involve the hands, traps, nets, spears, arrows, or trained animals. Its aim here is to obtain something moving freely, usually by following it or using an implement to capture it.

In early Arabic, this family also occurs in connection with collecting birds’ eggs and certain other things. Figurative uses can describe attracting people, seizing an opportunity, or finding something sought.

Ṣ Y D occurs six times in five Qur’anic verses, all in Al-Māʾidah:

  • الصَّيْدِ (al-ṣaydi): hunting or game; Al-Māʾidah 5:1 and Al-Māʾidah 5:94.
  • فَاصْطَادُوا (fa-iṣṭādū): then hunt; Al-Māʾidah 5:2.
  • الصَّيْدَ (al-ṣayda): game or an animal hunted; Al-Māʾidah 5:95.
  • صَيْدُ الْبَحْرِ (ṣaydu al-baḥri): sea game; Al-Māʾidah 5:96.
  • صَيْدُ الْبَرِّ (ṣaydu al-barri): land game; Al-Māʾidah 5:96.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: five nouns and one Form VIII verb

Other forms are also known in Arabic:

صَادَ / يَصِيدُ (ṣāda / yaṣīdu): he hunted or hunts.

صَيْد (ṣayd): hunting; also an animal hunted or already taken as game.

صَائِد (ṣāʾid): a hunter.

اِصْطَادَ (iṣṭāda): he went hunting or hunted.

تَصَيَّدَ (taṣayyada): he sought game or made efforts or plans to capture it.

مُصْطَاد (muṣṭād): a hunter; in other constructions, something hunted.

مَصِيد (maṣīd): hunted or captured. Forms recorded for a hunting implement include مِصْيَد (miṣyad) and مَصْيَد (maṣyad).

مِصْيَدَة (miṣyadah): a trap or hunting implement.

صَيُود (ṣayūd): an animal that takes prey or hunts frequently, such as a hunting dog or bird.

صَيَّاد (ṣayyād): a hunter; someone who engages in hunting.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Miṣbāḥ al-Munīr: hunter, captured game, and hunting implements

Another branch of this family connects صَيَد (ṣayad) and أَصْيَد (aṣyad) with holding the head upright, seldom turning aside, or a condition making it difficult to turn the neck. These meanings are examined separately below.

2. Hunting in early Arabic

Ṣāda: pursuing and taking game

Lisān al-ʿArab explains صاد الصيد (ṣāda al-ṣayda) as taking game. اصطاده (iṣṭādahu) and تصيّده (taṣayyadahu) are also recorded in this field.

Ṣayd is therefore not confined to searching or pursuit: capturing and obtaining the animal is one of its principal uses.

Someone catching a gazelle in a trap or a bird in a net has hunted. The same verb can describe taking an animal with a spear or arrow.

Hunting, however, does not necessarily mean killing.

An animal may be captured alive or killed during the hunt. Whether killing forms part of the act depends on the method and the sentence’s context.

This distinction matters in the Qur’an because Al-Māʾidah 5:95 uses تقتلوا (taqtulū), “kill,” separately alongside الصيد (al-ṣayd), “game.”

Ṣayd: the activity and the game itself

The Arabic verbal noun صَيْد (ṣayd) can denote the activity of hunting.

The same word also denotes the animal that is the object of hunting.

Ibn Jinnī treats this as a verbal noun used in place of its object: the activity’s name identifies what the activity affects.

For example, خرج إلى الصيد (kharaja ilā al-ṣaydi) can mean going out to hunt.

By contrast, هذا صيد (hādhā ṣaydun) can describe an animal already taken or one belonging to the category of game.

Lisān al-ʿArab reports Ibn al-Aʿrābī’s view that any wild animal hunted may be called ṣayd, whether already captured or still free.

A narrower explanation is also recorded, restricting the designation to free, huntable, unowned animals.

This difference shows that the word itself does not establish that an animal has already been captured.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:94 and Al-Māʾidah 5:95, ṣayd refers to animals that can still be reached and hunted.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:96, both hunting and what is obtained from the sea belong to the expression’s semantic field.

Lisān al-ʿArab: verbal noun, game animal, and differing scopes of ṣayd

Ṣayd and free-ranging animals

In familiar Arabic usage, ṣayd chiefly concerns animals living freely in nature whose capture requires pursuit, traps, or implements.

Gazelles, wild birds, wild asses, and fish are examples of creatures that may be hunted or caught.

A sheep kept at home or in a flock is usually not the object of hunting in this familiar sense, even when killed for its meat.

Hunting and slaughtering domestic livestock are therefore distinct.

An animal’s death alone does not make every killing an instance of ṣayd.

Nor should the term be extended to every animal in every circumstance without examining how it is obtained and how the language is used.

Taking something without killing an animal

Kitāb al-ʿAyn and Tahdhīb al-Lughah record expressions such as نصيد بيض النعام (naṣīdu bayḍa al-naʿāmi) and نصيد الكمأة (naṣīdu al-kamʾata): collecting ostrich eggs or desert truffles.

Here the verb concerns obtaining or finding something sought in nature.

An ostrich egg is not a running animal, and a desert truffle is not an animal at all.

These examples show that Arabic extended the verb beyond pursuing animals to searching for and obtaining other things in certain situations.

This linguistic extension does not, however, make صيد البر (ṣaydu al-barri) in Al-Māʾidah 5:96 encompass every plant, truffle, or object gathered from the ground.

The consecration passages concern hunting animals. Broader Arabic usages should be understood in their own settings rather than transferred into a ruling without evidence.

Lisān al-ʿArab citing Al-Tahdhīb: ostrich eggs and desert truffles

3. Hunting implements and figurative uses

Miṣyadah: a trap or hunting implement

In Arabic, مِصْيَدَة (miṣyadah) denotes an implement used to capture game.

It may be a snare, trap, or another device prepared for that purpose.

Early sources record its plural as مَصَايِد (maṣāyid), without a hamzah.

Early records give different vowel patterns for this word, but its principal use concerns a hunting implement.

Miṣyadah does not name the animal caught; it identifies the instrument used to catch it.

This distinction matters when identifying derivatives: ṣāʾid is the hunter, maṣīd can denote what is hunted, and miṣyadah is the hunting implement.

Lisān al-ʿArab: hunting-implement forms and maṣāyid without hamzah

Animals trained to hunt

A dog or bird able to take prey can be described in Arabic as صيود (ṣayūd).

For example, a hunting dog can help its handler by pursuing and capturing an animal.

A hunting bird can likewise obtain prey by flying and attacking.

Hunting here need not be a direct action of the human hand: a person may employ a trained animal.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:4, the Qur’an discusses الجوارح (al-jawāriḥ), hunting animals, and their training.

Although this verse contains no word from Ṣ Y D, it concerns hunting and includes capture by trained animals within the discussion of food rules.

A witness from early Arabic

Early sources record the familiar proverb:

كُلُّ الصَّيْدِ فِي جَوْفِ الْفَرَا

kullu al-ṣaydi fī jawfi al-farā

“All the game is in the wild ass’s belly.”

Explanations tell of people who went hunting. One caught a small animal such as a hare, another a gazelle, while someone else took a wild ass.

The account values the larger catch so highly that the smaller catches seem insignificant beside it.

The expression describes something great surpassing lesser examples.

Here ṣayd denotes both the animal taken and the result of the hunt.

Accounts of the proverb’s historical origin differ. It witnesses established Arabic usage, but the details of its origin should not be presented as certain pre-Islamic history.

Faṣl al-Maqāl: the hunting proverb and a differing origin narrative

Figurative taṣayyada

Arabic تَصَيَّدَ (taṣayyada) can also concern searching and striving to obtain something.

Broader uses include hunting for opportunities, finding someone’s faults, or attracting people through careful means.

For example, تصيد الفرصة (taṣayyada al-furṣata) describes taking advantage of a suitable opportunity.

These expressions draw on the image of hunting and obtaining something without necessarily referring to animals.

They should not replace the direct meaning of الصيد (al-ṣayd) in the consecration verses.

Al-Māʾidah concerns animals and restrictions on taking them, rather than seeking opportunities or pursuing other people’s faults.

4. Another branch: ṣayad and aṣyad

Holding the head upright and seldom turning aside

Early Arabic also records صَيَد (ṣayad), with a fatḥah on the yāʾ, for holding the head and neck in a way that involves little looking around.

أَصْيَد (aṣyad) can describe someone who holds the head high and pays little attention to either side.

Some early usages apply this description to kings.

Lexicographers explain that such a person turns aside little because of his greatness and position.

Another recorded use describes someone whose neck condition prevents easy turning of the head.

The causes differ: one may concern conduct or disposition, the other a physical condition.

Aṣyad therefore does not in every instance establish arrogance or illness.

Ibn Fāris’s analysis

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris attempts to explain this family through holding the head straight and moving without turning aside.

He also connects hunting with a free animal’s movement: the animal moves on its course and a hunter captures it.

This explanation can establish an image connecting the two branches.

Research must nevertheless distinguish semantic analysis from proof of a word’s historical origin.

Describing a hunted animal as moving straight without turning does not establish that the name for hunting originated in this feature.

Ṣāda in familiar usage means hunting; “holding the neck straight” need not be imported into its translation.

The dictionaries thus record two fields of usage under one root. A single proposed image does not make their precise historical relationship certain.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: proposed link with moving straight and seldom turning

5. Qur’anic uses: hunting and consecration

Al-Māʾidah 5:1: not treating hunting as lawful during consecration

The opening of Al-Māʾidah states:

أُحِلَّتْ لَكُمْ بَهِيمَةُ الْأَنْعَامِ إِلَّا مَا يُتْلَىٰ عَلَيْكُمْ غَيْرَ مُحِلِّي الصَّيْدِ وَأَنْتُمْ حُرُمٌ

uḥillat lakum bahīmatu al-anʿāmi illā mā yutlā ʿalaykum ghayra muḥillī al-ṣaydi wa-antum ḥurumun

“Livestock animals have been made lawful for you, except what is recited to you; do not treat hunting as lawful while you are in consecration.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:1

Here الصيد (al-ṣayd) occurs in غَيْرَ مُحِلِّي الصَّيْدِ (ghayra muḥillī al-ṣaydi).

مُحِلِّي (muḥillī) belongs to Ḥ L L and concerns treating something as lawful or permitted.

The verse distinguishes the lawfulness of livestock animals from the restriction on hunting during consecration.

Al-ṣayd names hunting or game; its lawfulness or prohibition comes from the construction and the condition of consecration.

Ṣ Y D itself therefore does not mean “forbidden.”

Nor does the verse say that every game animal is inherently and permanently forbidden.

The restriction here accompanies the addressees’ particular state.

Study of Ḥ L L

Al-Māʾidah 5:2: fa-iṣṭādū

The next verse states:

وَإِذَا حَلَلْتُمْ فَاصْطَادُوا

wa-idhā ḥalaltum fa-iṣṭādū

“And when you leave consecration, hunt.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:2

فَاصْطَادُوا (fa-iṣṭādū) is a plural imperative of Form VIII.

In this construction, iṣṭāda concerns going after game and hunting.

Arabic usage can emphasize the effort and action involved in capturing game through this verb.

Form VIII alone does not establish that every instance of iṣṭiyād is harder than every use of the basic verb ṣāda.

Early sources often treat ṣāda and iṣṭāda as equivalent.

Does fa-iṣṭādū make hunting obligatory?

This imperative follows the restriction on hunting during consecration.

Its function must therefore be examined in the context of lifting that restriction.

وإذا حللتم (wa-idhā ḥalaltum) specifies the condition: when you have left consecration.

Fa-iṣṭādū here accordingly expresses permission to hunt after the restricted state ends.

It does not by itself say that everyone leaving consecration must hunt an animal.

The imperative’s grammatical form and the kind of ruling it expresses in context are distinct matters.

Al-Ṭabarī: permission to hunt after consecration

The same verse also discusses refraining from transgression motivated by hostility and cooperating in goodness and reverence.

Permission to hunt again therefore does not abolish all ethical and legal principles governing human conduct.

6. Al-Māʾidah 5:94: game as a test

Al-Māʾidah 5:94 states:

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَيَبْلُوَنَّكُمُ اللَّهُ بِشَيْءٍ مِنَ الصَّيْدِ تَنَالُهُ أَيْدِيكُمْ وَرِمَاحُكُمْ لِيَعْلَمَ اللَّهُ مَنْ يَخَافُهُ بِالْغَيْبِ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū la-yabluwannakumu allāhu bi-shayʾin mina al-ṣaydi tanāluhu aydīkum wa-rimāḥukum li-yaʿlama allāhu man yakhāfuhu bi-al-ghaybi

“You who have believed, The God will surely test you with some game within reach of your hands and spears, so that He may know who fears Him unseen.”

The meaning of ṣayd here

Al-ṣayd here refers to accessible game.

تَنَالُهُ أَيْدِيكُمْ وَرِمَاحُكُمْ (tanāluhu aydīkum wa-rimāḥukum) shows that the verse concerns creatures reachable by hand or hunting implements.

“Hands” and “spears” identify two different means of reaching them.

Some early explanations associate taking by hand with small game and reaching with spears with larger animals.

This distinction is conceivable, but the verse does not explicitly divide game into two fixed size categories.

What is clear is the game’s accessibility and the possibility of capturing it.

Al-Ṭabarī: game within reach of hands and spears

A test when game is accessible

This verse connects game with trial or testing.

Something within reach may increase a person’s desire to use it.

Yet when an act is prohibited in a particular state, its nearness or ease does not remove the restriction.

The test here concerns respecting a limit when there is a real opportunity to act.

Ṣayd alone does not imply that all hunting presents the same moral test in every situation.

The test in this verse is understood through the command, the addressees’ state, and the sentence’s stated purpose.

Nor does mention of divine testing authorize people deliberately to harm others or animals. Neither the root nor the verse’s construction establishes that conclusion.

7. Al-Māʾidah 5:95: hunting and killing game

The next verse states:

لَا تَقْتُلُوا الصَّيْدَ وَأَنْتُمْ حُرُمٌ

lā taqtulū al-ṣayda wa-antum ḥurumun

“Do not kill game while you are in consecration.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:95

Why does taqtulū matter?

Here الصيد (al-ṣayd) is the object of تقتلوا (taqtulū).

قتل (qatl) belongs to Q T L and concerns killing.

صيد (ṣayd) belongs to Ṣ Y D and here names the animal hunted.

Their combination shows that the concepts can be distinguished.

If ṣayd itself meant killing here, lā taqtulū, “do not kill,” would contribute nothing separately to the sentence.

The translation should therefore make clear that the verse prohibits killing game animals during consecration.

Deliberate killing and compensation

It continues:

وَمَنْ قَتَلَهُ مِنْكُمْ مُتَعَمِّدًا فَجَزَاءٌ مِثْلُ مَا قَتَلَ مِنَ النَّعَمِ

wa-man qatalahu minkum mutaʿammidan fa-jazāʾun mithlu mā qatala mina al-naʿami

“Whoever among you deliberately kills it owes compensation from livestock equivalent to what he killed.”

The qualification مُتَعَمِّدًا (mutaʿammidan), “deliberately,” matters.

The verse discusses someone who deliberately kills game.

It then mentions compensation, assessment by two just persons, an offering reaching the Kaʿbah, feeding those in need, or an equivalent in fasting.

These details concern rules for violation and compensation rather than ṣayd’s lexical meaning.

The qualification of intent should not be omitted, nor should accidental, mistaken, or unintended killing be assigned precisely the same ruling without separate argument.

Conversely, ṣayd itself does not determine responsibility in every unintentional case.

That question requires independent examination of the ruling’s construction and related verses.

Al-Ṭabarī: deliberate killing of game, compensation, and differing rulings

Game and domestic livestock

The verse mentions النَّعَم (al-naʿam), livestock, as compensation for game killed.

This distinction identifies the game animal and the livestock used for compensation as different categories.

A wild animal may be comparable in size or other features to a domestic animal, but that resemblance does not make the terms identical.

Ṣayd should therefore not simply be translated as “livestock,” just as naʿam does not itself name wild game.

8. Al-Māʾidah 5:96: sea game and land game

Al-Māʾidah 5:96 states:

أُحِلَّ لَكُمْ صَيْدُ الْبَحْرِ وَطَعَامُهُ مَتَاعًا لَكُمْ وَلِلسَّيَّارَةِ وَحُرِّمَ عَلَيْكُمْ صَيْدُ الْبَرِّ مَا دُمْتُمْ حُرُمًا

uḥilla lakum ṣaydu al-baḥri wa-ṭaʿāmuhu matāʿan lakum wa-li-al-sayyārati wa-ḥurrima ʿalaykum ṣaydu al-barri mā dumtum ḥuruman

“Sea game and its food have been made lawful for you, as provision for you and travellers; land game has been forbidden to you for as long as you are in consecration.”

The verse uses ṣayd twice: once in ṣaydu al-baḥri and once in ṣaydu al-barri.

Ṣaydu al-baḥri

The direct meaning of صيد البحر (ṣaydu al-baḥri) concerns sea game or fishing.

Arabic baḥr can denote a large expanse of water; some early usages also apply it to large rivers.

It should therefore not be confined to salty ocean water in every historical use without examining the context.

Here ṣaydu al-baḥri concerns animals caught in an aquatic environment and the activity of obtaining them.

Fish are among its clearest examples.

Ṣayd does not, however, name one animal species or independently establish a complete list of permitted or forbidden aquatic creatures.

Wa-ṭaʿāmuhu

Another word follows ṣaydu al-baḥri in this expression:

وَطَعَامُهُ

wa-ṭaʿāmuhu

“And its food.”

Early commentaries report several interpretations of the distinction between sea game and its food.

Some identify ṣayd with what people catch alive or fresh from the water and ṭaʿām with what the sea itself casts ashore.

Others also associate sea food with dried or salted fish.

A broader interpretation takes ṭaʿām to encompass food obtained from the sea.

This disagreement shows that ṭaʿāmuhu’s precise meaning cannot be extracted from Ṣ Y D itself.

Ṣayd and ṭaʿām are different words, each requiring examination in its own construction.

Al-Ṭabarī: interpretations of sea game and its food

Are sea game and sea food identical?

Linguistically, sea game can refer to catching an aquatic animal or to the catch itself.

Ṭaʿāmuhu concerns food associated with the sea.

The fields overlap because many creatures caught at sea are used as food.

Overlap nevertheless does not make the words wholly identical.

The Qur’an includes both in one sentence; the distinction should be preserved in study.

This verse alone also does not issue detailed rulings on spoilage, poisoning, harmfulness, or every method of obtaining sea food.

The basic permission concerning sea game is clear, while other principles concerning health, justice, and preventing harm require examination in their own right.

Ṣaydu al-barri

The verse sets land game against sea game.

بَرّ (barr) here denotes land as opposed to the aquatic environment.

صيد البر (ṣaydu al-barri) therefore concerns hunting land animals.

It does not name one particular animal or apply only to a few familiar species such as gazelles and wild asses.

Nor does it establish that every land creature, regardless of domestic or wild status, is equally an instance of game in all circumstances.

The lexical criterion concerns hunting and its object; the ruling’s limits require consideration of the relevant verses together.

The qualification mā dumtum ḥuruman

The portion about land game ends with:

مَا دُمْتُمْ حُرُمًا

mā dumtum ḥuruman

“For as long as you are in consecration.”

This qualification expresses the prohibition’s temporal and situational limits.

The verse therefore does not establish a permanent prohibition on land hunting for everyone in every circumstance.

Al-Māʾidah 5:2 itself grants permission to hunt after leaving consecration.

The two verses illuminate one another.

Permission to hunt in ordinary circumstances nevertheless does not authorize every treatment of animals, environmental destruction, or infringement of others’ rights.

Such conduct must be assessed through independent principles concerning harm, corruption, and human responsibility.

9. Hunting and food rules

Al-Māʾidah 5:4 states:

وَمَا عَلَّمْتُمْ مِنَ الْجَوَارِحِ مُكَلِّبِينَ تُعَلِّمُونَهُنَّ مِمَّا عَلَّمَكُمُ اللَّهُ فَكُلُوا مِمَّا أَمْسَكْنَ عَلَيْكُمْ وَاذْكُرُوا اسْمَ اللَّهِ عَلَيْهِ

wa-mā ʿallamtum mina al-jawāriḥi mukallibīna tuʿallimūnahunna mimmā ʿallamakumu allāhu fa-kulū mimmā amsakna ʿalaykum wa-udhkurū isma allāhi ʿalayhi

“Eat from what the trained hunting animals hold for you—you train them from what God has taught you—and mention God’s name over it.”

This verse concerns hunting with trained animals.

جوارح (jawāriḥ) belongs to J R Ḥ rather than Ṣ Y D.

أمسكن (amsakna) belongs to M S K and concerns catching and holding.

Ṣayd itself does not occur in this verse, but hunting is clear from the context.

Study of J R Ḥ

The verse shows that the Qur’an also examines hunting in terms of how food is obtained.

Al-Māʾidah 5:3 discusses animals affected by strangling, blows, falling, goring, or attack by another animal, and includes the exception إِلَّا مَا ذَكَّيْتُمْ (illā mā dhakkaytum), “except what you duly slaughter.”

Hunting, killing, proper slaughter, and food thus belong to a shared practical field without being identical in lexical meaning or stated rulings.

Nor does the lawfulness of one category of food establish that all others are forbidden.

The Qur’an discusses both the lawfulness of livestock animals and that of sea game and its food.

Conversely, general permission to hunt does not override the explicit consecration restrictions or other Qur’anic qualifications.

10. Comparison with related roots

Ṣ Y D (ṣayd) and Q N Ṣ (qanṣ)

Arabic قنص (qanṣ) concerns hunting and pursuing animals.

Ṣayd occupies the same field and is equivalent to qanṣ in some uses.

In the Qur’an and Arabic, however, ṣayd extends beyond one particular method and can denote either the activity or the game animal.

The difference should not be turned into an absolute rule assigning one term exclusively to arrows and the other to traps.

Established usage does not support that restriction.

Ṣ Y D (ṣāda) and ʾ KH DH (akhadha)

أخذ (akhdh) concerns taking or obtaining something.

Ṣāda can also express taking something, but its familiar use concerns pursuit and hunting.

For example, taking a book from a table may be akhdh in Arabic, but ordinarily not ṣayd.

Catching a gazelle in a trap can involve both concepts: one emphasizes taking, the other hunting.

Study of ʾ KH DH

Ṣ Y D (ṣayd) and Q T L (qatala)

قتل (qatl) concerns killing.

Ṣayd can name hunting or the animal hunted.

Al-Māʾidah 5:95 brings both concepts into one expression:

لا تقتلوا الصيد (lā taqtulū al-ṣayda), “Do not kill game.”

Their combination shows that hunting and killing are not completely synonymous.

Hunting can involve killing, but ṣayd itself does not always mean killing.

Study of Q T L

Ṣ Y D (ṣayd) and DH B Ḥ (dhabaḥa)

In familiar usage, ذبح (dhabḥ) concerns cutting at the throat and killing an animal in a particular manner.

Ṣayd concerns hunting and capturing an animal.

Game may be slaughtered after capture or may die during the hunt.

Dhabḥ is thus a method of killing an animal, while ṣayd is a broader designation for obtaining game.

The meaning of dhabḥ alone cannot determine all hunting rules.

Study of DH B Ḥ

Ṣ Y D (ṣayd) and Ṭ R D (ṭarada)

طرد (ṭard) concerns driving away and pursuing.

An animal may also be pursued or driven during a hunt.

Pursuing or driving alone nevertheless does not mean that game is captured.

Someone may follow an animal without succeeding in taking it.

Ṭard may be part of hunting but does not encompass ṣayd’s entire meaning.

Study of Ṭ R D

Ṣ Y D (ṣayd) and Ḥ R M (ḥaruma)

Ḥ R M concerns inviolability, prohibition, and a state involving restrictions.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:1, Al-Māʾidah 5:95, and Al-Māʾidah 5:96, the state of ḥurum accompanies ṣayd.

Ṣayd and ḥurum nevertheless belong to different roots.

Ṣayd names hunting and game; ḥurum expresses a state or restriction affecting conduct.

The prohibition on hunting during consecration is therefore understood through the ruling and its qualification, rather than through ṣayd’s meaning itself.

Study of Ḥ R M

11. Translation suggestions for Forqan

صَادَ (ṣāda): hunted or captured; in broader uses, obtained something.

يَصِيدُ (yaṣīdu): he hunts.

صَيْد (ṣayd): hunting; also an animal hunted or taken as game.

صَائِد (ṣāʾid): a hunter.

اِصْطَادَ (iṣṭāda): he hunted or engaged in hunting.

فَاصْطَادُوا (fa-iṣṭādū): then hunt; in Al-Māʾidah 5:2, permission to hunt after leaving consecration.

تَصَيَّدَ (taṣayyada): went after game or made efforts or plans to capture it.

مِصْيَدَة (miṣyadah): a trap or hunting implement.

صَيُود (ṣayūd): taking prey or hunting frequently.

صَيَّاد (ṣayyād): a hunter.

صَيَد (ṣayad): holding the head upright or seldom turning aside; in another use, a neck condition.

أَصْيَد (aṣyad): someone holding the head high and turning aside little, sometimes because of conduct and sometimes because of physical condition.

In the Qur’anic uses:

Al-Māʾidah 5:1

غَيْرَ مُحِلِّي الصَّيْدِ وَأَنْتُمْ حُرُمٌ

ghayra muḥillī al-ṣaydi wa-antum ḥurumun

“Do not treat hunting as lawful while you are in consecration.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:2

وَإِذَا حَلَلْتُمْ فَاصْطَادُوا

wa-idhā ḥalaltum fa-iṣṭādū

“And when you leave consecration, hunt.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:94

بِشَيْءٍ مِنَ الصَّيْدِ تَنَالُهُ أَيْدِيكُمْ وَرِمَاحُكُمْ

bi-shayʾin mina al-ṣaydi tanāluhu aydīkum wa-rimāḥukum

“With some game within reach of your hands and spears.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:95

لَا تَقْتُلُوا الصَّيْدَ وَأَنْتُمْ حُرُمٌ

lā taqtulū al-ṣayda wa-antum ḥurumun

“Do not kill game while you are in consecration.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:96

أُحِلَّ لَكُمْ صَيْدُ الْبَحْرِ وَطَعَامُهُ

uḥilla lakum ṣaydu al-baḥri wa-ṭaʿāmuhu

“Sea game and its food have been made lawful for you.”

وَحُرِّمَ عَلَيْكُمْ صَيْدُ الْبَرِّ مَا دُمْتُمْ حُرُمًا

wa-ḥurrima ʿalaykum ṣaydu al-barri mā dumtum ḥuruman

“Land game has been forbidden to you for as long as you are in consecration.”

In Persian, shekār is the principal natural equivalent of ṣayd.

Where the animal itself is the sentence’s object, as in لا تقتلوا الصيد (lā taqtulū al-ṣayda), the lexical explanation can clarify that a game animal is meant.

The basic translation need not use the lengthy expression “a wild animal that can be hunted” in every instance.

Nor should ṣayd always be confined to “killing an animal,” “game meat,” or “obtaining food”: hunting can occur without killing and the language also records other uses.

12. Conclusion

Arabic Ṣ Y D concerns hunting, pursuing and capturing free-ranging animals, and the game itself.

Early usage names both the activity and what is hunted. The family also has established derivatives for traps and hunting implements, hunters, and animals that take prey.

Another branch connects ṣayad and aṣyad with holding the head upright and seldom turning aside. Ibn Fāris proposes a semantic connection with hunting, but this explanation should not be presented as the family’s certain historical origin.

The Qur’an uses Ṣ Y D six times, all in Al-Māʾidah.

Al-Māʾidah 5:1 and Al-Māʾidah 5:2 state the restriction on hunting during consecration and permission after leaving it.

Al-Māʾidah 5:94 places access to game in a context of testing and reverence for God.

Al-Māʾidah 5:95 discusses deliberate killing of game during consecration and compensation for it.

Al-Māʾidah 5:96 distinguishes sea game from land game, qualifying the land-game prohibition by the state of consecration.

Together these passages show that hunting, killing, lawful food, and consecration restrictions are related but distinct concepts.

Brief root meaning for Forqan:

Hunting, pursuing, and capturing free-ranging animals; also the animal hunted or taken as game. Another branch concerns holding the head upright and seldom turning aside.

In Qur’anic usage, ṣayd denotes hunting and game animals, while iṣṭādū concerns hunting as an action. Permission or prohibition is understood from each verse’s command and qualifications rather than a fixed meaning of the root.
