Root w-ṣ-b — Continuity, illness, and lasting suffering

Qur’anic and lexical forms

وَاصِبًا
(wāṣiban)
Always; Qur’anic accusative form

وَاصِبٌ
(wāṣibun)
Continuing; Qur’anic nominative form

وَصَبَ
(waṣaba)
It endured; non-Qur’anic verb

وَصِبَ
(waṣiba)
He became ill; non-Qur’anic verb

أَوْصَاب
(awṣāb)
Illnesses; non-Qur’anic plural

وُصُوب
(wuṣūb)
Continuity; non-Qur’anic verbal noun

The Arabic root w-ṣ-b has two established semantic branches: endurance, continuity, and persistence; and illness, pain, and bodily exhaustion.

These branches can be related in meaning because illness and pain sometimes afflict a person persistently. Historical evidence is nevertheless needed before claiming that either meaning necessarily developed from the other.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an, both times as وَاصِب.

In Al-Naḥl 16:52, وَاصِبًا concerns dīn and obedience to God, expressing their enduring character.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:9, وَاصِبٌ describes punishment and expresses its continuity.

These uses prevent restricting the root to illness and pain. Conversely, the sense of endurance does not justify removing illness and exhaustion from the family.

1. The root in early Arabic

Waṣab: illness, pain, and bodily suffering

One established early Arabic use of waṣab concerns illness and pain afflicting a person.

This appears in poetry attributed to al-Aʿshā, a poet associated with the pre-Islamic period:

تَقُولُ بِنْتِي وَقَدْ قَرَّبْتُ مُرْتَحَلًا

taqūlu bintī wa-qad qarrabtu murtaḥalan

يَا رَبِّ جَنِّبْ أَبِي الْأَوْصَابَ وَالْوَجَعَا

yā rabbi jannib abī al-awṣāba wa-al-wajaʿā

“My daughter said, when I had brought my riding camel near for departure: My Lord, keep illnesses and pain away from my father.”

The poet describes preparing to travel. His daughter, concerned for him, asks God to protect him from illness and suffering.

الْأَوْصَابَ is the plural of وَصَب and refers to illnesses and bodily afflictions.

الْوَجَعَا appears alongside it.

Their association places waṣab and wajaʿ in a shared semantic field, without proving that they are exactly identical in every use.

Wajaʿ concerns the sensation of pain, while waṣab can express illness and its associated suffering and exhaustion.

Arabic sources also record waṣab for illness, persistent pain, severe weariness, and bodily weakness.

Their juxtaposition does not establish that waṣab always means prolonged illness and wajaʿ always means brief pain. Such an absolute distinction would require an independent study of both families’ uses.

Al-Aʿshā’s witness supports the sense of illness and bodily suffering in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic tradition, though the attribution alone does not precisely date the verse.

An elegy attributed to Aʿshā Bāhilah also says:

لَا يَغْمِزُ السَّاقَ مِنْ أَيْنٍ وَلَا وَصَبٍ

lā yaghmizu al-sāqa min aynin wa-lā waṣabin

وَلَا يَعَضُّ عَلَى شُرْسُوفِهِ الصَّفَرُ

wa-lā yaʿaḍḍu ʿalā shursūfihi al-ṣafaru

“He does not rub his leg from exhaustion or pain; nor does ṣafar gnaw at his rib.”

The verse praises endurance and bodily vigor. Ṣafar evokes an old conception of an abdominal affliction or creature associated with hunger; this is the poem’s imagery, not an established medical account.

Ayn concerns fatigue and exhaustion, and waṣab bodily pain and suffering.

This wording is transmitted in early sources, but other versions have نَصَب instead of وَصَب. The second witness must therefore be assessed with its textual variation in view.

Despite that variation, the first witness supports the illness-and-pain branch in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic tradition; its precise historical dating remains a separate question.

Endurance and continuity

The other branch concerns something’s endurance and continuity.

وَصَبَ الشَّيْءُ is recorded for something continuing or remaining in place.

وَصَبَ الرَّجُلُ عَلَى الْأَمْرِ describes a person persisting in an undertaking.

Kuthayyir ʿAzzah, an Umayyad-period poet, says:

أَشَاقَكَ بَرْقٌ آخِرَ اللَّيْلِ وَاصِبُ

ashāqaka barqun ākhira al-layli wāṣibu

تَضَمَّنَهُ فَرْشُ الْجَبَا فَالْمَسَارِبُ

taḍammanahu farshu al-jabā fa-al-masāribu

“Did persistent lightning late at night, appearing over those places, awaken longing in you?”

Here وَاصِب describes lightning.

The lightning keeps appearing or flashes repeatedly.

The word thus concerns a phenomenon’s persistence, rather than illness or suffering.

This witness is Umayyad and should not be called pre-Islamic. Its historical value lies in showing the sense of continuity in poetry relatively close to Islam’s beginnings. أَهَاجَكَ is also transmitted instead of أَشَاقَكَ at the opening.

Continuity is also the reading adopted here for both Qur’anic occurrences.

Lexicons record further examples of this branch.

أَوْصَبَ الْقَوْمُ عَلَى الشَّيْءِ describes people persisting in something.

وَصَبَ الشَّحْمُ concerns an animal retaining its fat or plumpness.

مَفَازَةٌ وَاصِبَةٌ describes an exceedingly distant, extensive desert whose end is not readily apparent.

These examples foreground continuation or extension, whether a state persists or a route extends over a great distance.

Their inclusion in dictionaries does not, by itself, precisely date their use to the pre-Islamic period.

How continuity relates to illness

The branches can be connected semantically: continuing illness and pain can wear a person down and weaken them.

Continuity and suffering then form part of one experience.

Two questions nevertheless need to remain distinct.

First, whether continuity and lasting pain are related in meaning.

Second, whether one historically gave rise to the other.

The second claim lacks sufficient evidence.

We cannot assert that waṣab originally meant only prolonged pain and subsequently acquired continuity, or that continuity came first and later extended to long illnesses.

Both Qur’anic uses fit the continuity branch well, although the pain-and-suffering branch also merits consideration when explaining the punishment verse.

2. Forms and semantic centers

Important Arabic forms include:

وَصَبَ: it endured or remained; he persisted in an undertaking.

وُصُوب: endurance and continuity.

وَاصِب: enduring, continuing, persistent.

وَصِبَ: he became ill or suffered bodily pain.

وَصَب: illness, pain, suffering, and exhaustion.

أَوْصَاب: the plural of waṣab; illnesses and pains.

وَصِب: ill or suffering bodily pain.

وَصَّبَ: in a recorded transitive use, he tended or nursed someone in illness; the form also occurs among expressions associated with illness and weakness.

أَوْصَبَ: in one branch, it continued or endured; in another, it made someone ill or afflicted them with pain.

تَوَصَّبَ: he felt illness or pain.

مُوصَب: ill or afflicted with pain.

مُوَصَّب: someone experiencing many or recurrent pains and illnesses.

These forms are recorded in Arabic, but not all occur in the Qur’an.

Actual Qur’anic forms

The Qur’an uses only one derivative from this family: the active-participle form وَاصِب.

It has two case endings:

وَاصِبًا in Al-Naḥl 16:52.

وَاصِبٌ in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:9.

The distinction concerns case and grammatical function, rather than two independent derivatives with different meanings.

No perfect, imperfect, imperative, or verbal noun from this family occurs in the Qur’an.

Awṣāb, waṣab as a noun for illness, and the forms denoting becoming ill do not occur there either.

Knowing these forms assists linguistic study, but the Qur’anic article must remain centered on the two actual occurrences of wāṣib.

Semantic centers

Two branches can be established:

Continuity: continuing, remaining, or persisting in a state or activity.

Illness and suffering: bodily pain, illness, and exhaustion, especially where the condition continues.

The branches can approach one another in particular circumstances, but illness should not be imported into every occurrence of wāṣib.

Dīn wāṣib, for example, does not mean an ill or painful religion.

Nor is something described as wāṣib necessarily unpleasant: continuity can concern something desirable or undesirable.

3. Al-Naḥl 16:52 — Enduring obedience to God

وَلَهُ مَا فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ وَلَهُ الدِّينُ وَاصِبًا ۚ أَفَغَيْرَ اللَّهِ تَتَّقُونَ

wa-lahu mā fī al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍi wa-lahu al-dīnu wāṣiban a-fa-ghayra Allāhi tattaqūn

Al-Naḥl 16:52

“To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth, and obedience belongs to Him always. Will you then be mindful of other than The God?”

Here وَاصِبًا is accusative and expresses the continuing state of الدِّينُ.

Grammatically, it can be understood as a circumstantial qualifier of al-dīn.

The continuity thus concerns dīn and obedience.

Al-dīn in this context

Qur’anic dīn has a broad range of uses connected with obedience, service, judgment, reckoning, and recompense.

Each occurrence must nevertheless be understood in its own context.

Al-Naḥl 16:51 prohibits taking two gods: the object of worship is one, and He alone is to be feared.

Al-Naḥl 16:52 attributes ownership of everything in the heavens and earth to God, then speaks of dīn and obedience.

Al-Naḥl 16:53 traces every blessing to God and recalls how people cry out to Him in distress.

This sequence connects al-dīn in Al-Naḥl 16:52 with obedience and service to the one God.

It should not be reduced without examination to the modern sense of membership in a denomination or a collection of religious institutions.

What does wāṣiban add?

وَلَهُ الدِّينُ alone would express that dīn and obedience belong to God.

وَاصِبًا emphasizes the continuing, enduring character of that belonging.

God does not deserve obedience only at particular times or in particular circumstances.

His ownership of the heavens and earth does not vanish when human circumstances change; His worthiness of worship does not depend on any group’s power or approval.

People may obey at one time and disobey at another.

One community may accept truth while another turns away.

Changes in human conduct do not change God’s position.

Wāṣiban therefore emphasizes here that dīn and obedience continually belong to God.

Does wāṣiban mean obligatory?

Arabic also records وَصَبَ الدِّينُ in the sense of becoming binding or obligatory.

Explanations of this verse include “obligatory” alongside “continuing.”

The senses can be related in this context.

If obedience belongs to God alone and does so continually, His right to obedience is not temporary.

Yet a recorded lexical or exegetical explanation should be distinguished from this article’s preferred translation.

Continuity and persistence are well-attested senses of wāṣib.

“Obligatory” is also a transmitted explanation of this verse; it should not be presented merely as the author’s inference. Continuity and exclusive belonging can nevertheless help explain its connection with the context.

This study prefers “always” or “continually” for wāṣiban, while retaining obligation as a recorded alternative explanation.

Does wāṣiban mean sincerely?

Lexical and exegetical explanations also propose “exclusive” or “pure” here.

The exclusivity of obedience can also be understood from its belonging to God and the context of His oneness.

Other clear uses of the root support continuity and endurance.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:9 uses the same word for punishment, where “sincere” is not a suitable explanation.

Sincerity should therefore not be treated as wāṣib’s fixed primary meaning.

In Al-Naḥl 16:52, this study favors “continually” or “always” on lexical and contextual grounds.

Must obedience mean an uninterrupted act at every moment?

Obedience’s enduring belonging to God is not the same as performing one devotional act without interruption.

The verse does not command repeating a single act throughout the day and night without sleeping, working, resting, or attending to family life.

Its subject is that obedience and service always belong to God.

People can work, sleep, have families, and enjoy life’s blessings while respecting God’s limits and commands in their choices and conduct.

Wāṣiban should not be made into a new instruction about a ritual’s frequency or uninterrupted duration.

An enduring right to obedience differs from the uninterrupted repetition of a physical act.

Connection with God’s oneness

The verse concludes with the question:

أَفَغَيْرَ اللَّهِ تَتَّقُونَ

a-fa-ghayra Allāhi tattaqūn

Al-Naḥl 16:52

“Will you then be mindful of other than The God?”

This follows the statement of God’s ownership and the enduring belonging of obedience to Him.

The issue is more than the persistence of an abstract concept: why should people give someone other than God the ultimate place in their mindfulness and service when everything belongs to Him?

That conclusion arises from the verse and its context, rather than from w-ṣ-b alone.

4. Al-Ṣāffāt 37:9 — Continuing punishment

دُحُورًا وَلَهُمْ عَذَابٌ وَاصِبٌ

duḥūran wa-lahum ʿadhābun wāṣib

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:9

“To drive them away; and for them is continuing punishment.”

وَاصِبٌ is nominative here and modifies عَذَابٌ.

Unlike the circumstantial qualifier in Al-Naḥl 16:52, it directly describes punishment.

The preferred meaning remains continuity, but what continues is different.

In the first verse, obedience continually belongs to God.

In the second, punishment is described as continuing.

The context

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:6 speaks of the nearest heaven being adorned with stars.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:7 describes its protection from rebellious devils.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:8 says they cannot listen to the highest assembly and are pelted from every side.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:9 then mentions their expulsion and wāṣib punishment.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:10 makes an exception for one who snatches something and is pursued by a piercing flame.

عَذَابٌ وَاصِبٌ thus has a specific context concerning rebellious devils.

That qualification matters: their punishment’s description should not be extended without evidence to all people or beings in every circumstance.

Duḥūran and wāṣib: distinct meanings

دُحُورًا belongs to d-ḥ-r.

That root concerns driving away and expelling.

Wāṣib belongs to w-ṣ-b and, in the reading adopted here, expresses continuity.

Two distinct meanings occur in the same verse:

Driving away and expulsion.

Continuing punishment.

Expulsion and punishment may be connected here, but that does not make the roots identical.

Duḥūran does not itself describe punishment’s duration, and wāṣib does not itself name the act of expulsion.

Can wāṣib mean painful here?

Because another branch of w-ṣ-b concerns illness and pain, “painful punishment” is also proposed for ʿadhāb wāṣib.

That reading merits consideration within the family’s semantic range.

Two points nevertheless matter.

ʿAdhāb itself already concerns suffering and punishment; pain and unpleasantness are present in the noun.

The other Qur’anic occurrence, Al-Naḥl 16:52, strongly supports continuity in the reading adopted here.

Arabic uses describing persistent lightning and continuing states also support that sense.

This study therefore prefers “continuing” or “lasting” for wāṣib in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:9.

That rendering adds something the noun “punishment” does not express alone: continuity.

Rendering it only “painful” may leave that feature unexpressed. This consideration favors continuity here without proving that the recorded alternative is impossible.

Does wāṣib determine every feature of punishment?

No.

In the preferred reading, it concerns continuation and persistence.

The adjective does not determine the punishment’s bodily form, moment-by-moment intensity, or mechanism.

Nor should all questions about the duration of punishment in the hereafter be considered settled by a single adjective.

Its extent and outcome require examining each verse’s subjects, temporal qualifications, and expressions concerning abiding, absence of relief, respite, ending, or continuation.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:9 directly describes continuing punishment for the group under discussion.

The description should be preserved at the scope the verse gives it, neither weakened nor expanded into a theory of every Qur’anic punishment.

5. Comparing the two Qur’anic uses

The expressions can be placed side by side:

وَلَهُ الدِّينُ وَاصِبًا — Al-Naḥl 16:52

وَلَهُمْ عَذَابٌ وَاصِبٌ — Al-Ṣāffāt 37:9

In the reading adopted here, wāṣib expresses continuity in both.

What it describes changes the complete meaning of each sentence.

In the first, continuity concerns dīn and obedience.

In the second, it concerns punishment.

The root itself therefore carries no fixed moral value.

Continuity can describe something desirable, such as obedience to God.

It can also describe something unwelcome, such as punishment.

The distinction comes from what is described and its context.

Grammatical distinction

In Al-Naḥl 16:52, وَاصِبًا is accusative and qualifies the state of al-dīn.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:9, وَاصِبٌ is nominative and modifies punishment.

The different ending reflects grammar; it does not establish one root for endurance and a separate root for illness.

Both use the active-participle form wāṣib.

Does continuity always mean repetition?

A state’s continuation and an event’s recurrence can be related without being identical.

A lamp’s light may remain uninterrupted for a time.

Lightning can instead appear repeatedly without remaining a steady light at every moment.

Both can appropriately be described through continuity in suitable circumstances.

Wāṣib alone does not specify the number of recurrences, intervals between them, or precise manner of continuation.

Al-Naḥl 16:52 concerns dīn’s enduring belonging to God.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:9 describes punishment as continuing.

The kind of continuity must be understood from the sentence’s subject.

6. Comparison with related roots

w-ṣ-b (waṣab) and d-w-m (dawām)

D-w-m concerns something continuing and remaining.

The Qur’an uses forms of this family for the continuation of certain states and blessings.

Al-Raʿd 13:35, for example, describes the garden’s enduring provision and shade.

Wāṣib likewise concerns continuity in the Qur’anic reading adopted here.

The families overlap in this respect.

W-ṣ-b also has a branch concerning illness and bodily pain, which is not part of continuity’s general definition.

Dāʾim can therefore explain wāṣib well in the two verses without exhausting w-ṣ-b’s semantic range.

w-ṣ-b (waṣab) and l-z-m (luzūm)

L-z-m concerns remaining attached to or accompanying something without separation.

W-ṣ-b’s continuity branch can likewise express a state’s persistence.

Attachment or obligation and duration are nevertheless not identical.

Something may accompany or bind a person while the duration depends on other conditions.

Continuity foregrounds a state’s continuing existence.

“Binding” or “obligatory” obedience is also a recorded explanation of وَلَهُ الدِّينُ وَاصِبًا. Its relation to continuity can be understood through the enduring belonging of obedience to God, without dismissing it as merely an inference.

Not every wāṣib should therefore be translated simply as “obligatory.”

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:9, “obligatory punishment” does not convey “continuing punishment” without additional explanation.

w-ṣ-b (waṣab) and n-ṣ-b (naṣab)

One branch of n-ṣ-b concerns weariness, suffering, and toil.

The Qur’an uses naṣab for suffering and exhaustion.

In Al-Kahf 18:62, Moses speaks of weariness from the journey.

Al-Ḥijr 15:48 and Fāṭir 35:35 deny weariness and suffering in describing the people of the garden.

This approaches w-ṣ-b’s illness-and-exhaustion branch.

The roots nevertheless have different consonants.

N-ṣ-b begins with nūn; w-ṣ-b begins with wāw.

Naṣb also has branches such as setting up and erecting that should not be transferred to waṣab.

Words concerning suffering and exhaustion must therefore retain their actual roots.

w-ṣ-b (waṣab) and w-j-ʿ (wajaʿ)

Wajaʿ concerns pain and bodily suffering in Arabic.

Waṣab likewise has a branch involving pain, illness, and exhaustion.

Al-Aʿshā’s verse brings them together:

الْأَوْصَابَ وَالْوَجَعَا

al-awṣāba wa-al-wajaʿā

Their association invites comparison of their meanings.

Wajaʿ foregrounds the sensation of pain.

Waṣab can express illness and the exhausting condition associated with pain.

This does not establish that every wajaʿ is brief or every waṣab permanent.

Some uses overlap; their boundaries must be assessed through actual evidence.

w-ṣ-b (waṣab) and m-r-ḍ (maraḍ)

Maraḍ concerns illness and impaired health, with bodily and figurative Qur’anic uses.

Waṣab is also recorded for illness and pain in Arabic.

The Qur’an does not use waṣab as a noun for illness; it uses only wāṣib, understood here as continuing.

The lexical illness branch does not establish that al-dīn wāṣiban or ʿadhāb wāṣib directly describes bodily illness.

Nor does the Qur’an’s figurative illness of the heart automatically create a new meaning for waṣab.

w-ṣ-b (waṣab) and w-ẓ-b (waẓab)

W-ẓ-b concerns perseverance and persistence in an undertaking.

Its forms include وَاظَبَ and مُوَاظَبَة.

Wāṣib and wāẓib can approach one another in expressing persistence and sustained effort.

They nevertheless belong to independent roots.

Qur’anic w-ṣ-b uses ṣād, whereas w-ẓ-b uses ẓāʾ.

Similarity of sound and meaning must not collapse their distinction.

Modern Persian movāẓebat commonly means care and regular attention; that usage is not the direct definition of Qur’anic wāṣib.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

Two principal Persian equivalents suit Qur’anic wāṣib:

“Continuing.”

“Always.”

“Enduring” and “lasting” can also convey the sense in suitable constructions.

The final choice must fit what the adjective describes and the sentence’s structure. These proposals do not replace existing verse word boxes.

Al-Naḥl 16:52

Suggested translation:

“To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth, and obedience belongs to Him always. Will you then be mindful of other than The God?”

“Always” renders wāṣiban suitably here.

It makes clear that dīn and obedience continually belong to God.

“Enduring obedience belongs to Him” is also defensible, though “belongs to Him always” follows the preferred Persian construction more naturally.

“A painful religion belongs to Him” or “an exhausting religion” does not fit the context and the meaning preferred here.

Translating wāṣiban only as “obligatory” can leave continuity less apparent.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:9

Suggested translation:

“To drive them away; and for them is continuing punishment.”

“Continuing” fits more naturally than “always” here because it directly modifies punishment.

“Lasting punishment” is also possible.

“Painful punishment” alone does not clearly express the continuity supported by the other occurrence and lexical witnesses.

Conversely, wāṣib itself supplies no further details about the punishment’s type, intensity, or bodily mechanism.

Other equivalents in the family

Non-Qur’anic forms should be translated according to context.

وَصَبَ الشَّيْءُ means “something continued” or “remained.”

وَصَبَ عَلَى الْأَمْرِ means “he persisted in an undertaking.”

وُصُوب means endurance or continuity.

وَصَب in the illness branch means pain, illness, or bodily suffering.

أَوْصَاب means illnesses or pains.

وَصِب means ill or suffering bodily pain.

مُوصَب means ill or afflicted with pain.

وَاصِب in the continuity branch means continuing, enduring, or constant.

Sharing a root does not make all its derivatives identical in meaning.

8. Conclusion

Arabic w-ṣ-b has two main branches: endurance and continuity; and illness and bodily suffering.

Poetry attributed to al-Aʿshā uses awṣāb for illnesses and pains. Kuthayyir ʿAzzah uses wāṣib for persistent lightning; the latter witness is Umayyad and should not be called pre-Islamic.

Arabic lexicons record continuity, persistence, sustained activity, pain, and illness within this family.

The precise historical relationship between the branches remains unclear. Neither can confidently be declared the original source of the other.

The Qur’an uses only wāṣib, once in Al-Naḥl 16:52 and once in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:9.

In the preferred reading of Al-Naḥl 16:52, wāṣiban expresses that dīn and obedience enduringly belong to God, fitting the context of His oneness, ownership, and mindfulness of Him.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:9, wāṣib modifies punishment and, in this study’s preferred reading, expresses its continuity.

Both are understood here through continuity, but their subjects and value differ: obedience to God occurs in a positive context of His oneness, while continuing punishment concerns rebellious devils.

The two branches can be defined as follows:

Endurance and continuity: continuing, remaining, or persisting in a state or activity. This is the meaning preferred here for both Qur’anic occurrences, while acknowledging the recorded alternative explanations.

Illness and suffering: bodily pain, illness, and exhaustion, particularly an affliction that can continue. This branch is established in early Arabic, but the Qur’an does not use a separate form explicitly naming illness from this root.

Not every wāṣib means ill or painful; nor should the illness branch be deleted to force a uniform definition.

For Forqan, “always” in Al-Naḥl 16:52 and “continuing” in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:9 are suitable, natural equivalents.

The central distinction is that continuity is not inherently good or bad. What persists may be obedience, light, blessings, illness, or punishment. Its character and value depend on what is described and the context.

Understanding w-ṣ-b therefore requires distinguishing a state’s duration from its nature. The Qur’an uses the same root for enduring dīn and continuing punishment while describing two quite different subjects.
