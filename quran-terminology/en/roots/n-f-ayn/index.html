The Qur’anic Root N F AYN (N F ʿ)

Nafaʿa: benefiting someone and producing a favorable outcome

The root ن ف ع, N F AYN, occurs in the Qur’an in the principal forms نَفَعَ / يَنْفَعُ, nafaʿa / yanfaʿu, نَفْع, nafʿ, and مَنَافِع, manāfiʿ. The singular manfaʿah and derivatives such as nāfiʿ and intafaʿa do not occur in the Qur’anic text.

The clearest way to identify this root’s field is its repeated opposition to ض ر ر, Ḍ R R. The Qur’an repeatedly places benefit and harm together. In this opposition, nafʿ is an effect that produces benefit, advantage, or a favorable outcome for a person or thing, while ḍarr produces harm or loss.

This meaning is distinct from moral goodness and extends beyond financial profit. One person can benefit another; a ship can carry something beneficial; livestock and iron can have benefits; a reminder can benefit someone; and even something the Qur’an judges negatively overall can have real benefits.

The root’s core can therefore be stated as follows:

N F AYN: producing benefit, advantage, or a favorable outcome for someone or something. Benefit is relational: we must ask what benefits whom, under which circumstances, and with what result.

The evidence examined here does not establish a single, certain, original physical image for this root. Nafʿ primarily describes the kind of effect or result that something produces in relation to its beneficiary.

The same field was recognized in older Arabic. Nafaʿahu was used of something that produced a beneficial result for a person, and nafʿ stood opposite ḍarr.

A poem attributed to al-Ḥārith ibn Ḥillizah states:

يَخْلِطُونَ البَرِيءَ مِنَّا بِذِي الذَّنْبِ
وَلَا يَنْفَعُ الخَلِيَّ الخَلَاءُ

Yakhliṭūna l-barīʾa minnā bi-dhī dh-dhanbi / wa-lā yanfaʿu l-khaliyya l-khalāʾ.

“They mix the innocent among us with the guilty, and innocence does not benefit the innocent.”

This usage makes an important point. The person is indeed innocent, but in that situation innocence does not produce the desired effect of release from accusation. Benefit is therefore not just about a thing’s nature; it concerns the result that thing produces in a particular relationship and situation.

Benefit is not identical to moral goodness

One of the most important ayahs for defining this root’s boundaries is Al-Baqarah 2:219:

يَسْأَلُونَكَ عَنِ الْخَمْرِ وَالْمَيْسِرِ ۖ قُلْ فِيهِمَا إِثْمٌ كَبِيرٌ وَمَنَافِعُ لِلنَّاسِ وَإِثْمُهُمَا أَكْبَرُ مِنْ نَفْعِهِمَا

Yasʾalūnaka ʿani l-khamri wa-l-maysiri qul fīhimā ithmun kabīrun wa-manāfiʿu li-n-nāsi wa-ithmuhumā akbaru min nafʿihimā.

“They ask you about khamr and maysir. Say: ‘In both there is great sin and benefits for people, but their sin is greater than their benefit.’”

Al-Baqarah 2:219

This ayah prevents us from simply equating nafʿ with goodness or something commendable. The Qur’an acknowledges benefits while making a broader negative judgment in the same sentence.

Something can genuinely benefit people, yet that benefit may not suffice to determine its overall value.

The distinction is fundamental to understanding the root:

Nafʿ describes a beneficial effect, not a final moral judgment.

The same ayah also places the two forms مَنَافِع, manāfiʿ, and نَفْع, nafʿ, alongside one another. It first says:

مَنَافِعُ لِلنَّاسِ

Manāfiʿu li-n-nās.

There are benefits and advantages for people.

It then says:

إِثْمُهُمَا أَكْبَرُ مِنْ نَفْعِهِمَا

Ithmuhumā akbaru min nafʿihimā.

Here it speaks of “the benefit of the two” in general terms.

Manāfiʿ here can be understood as referring to multiple benefits, while nafʿ gathers their beneficial aspect into a more general expression. This one ayah, however, should not establish an absolute rule that the plural manāfiʿ always expresses particulars and nafʿ always expresses the overall outcome.

The Qur’an uses the plural مَنَافِع, manāfiʿ, in a variety of domains.

Concerning livestock:

وَالْأَنْعَامَ خَلَقَهَا لَكُمْ فِيهَا دِفْءٌ وَمَنَافِعُ وَمِنْهَا تَأْكُلُونَ

Wa-l-anʿāma khalaqahā lakum fīhā difʾun wa-manāfiʿu wa-minhā taʾkulūn.

“He created livestock for you; in them you have warmth and benefits, and from them you eat.”

Al-Naḥl 16:5

In Al-Muʾminūn:

وَلَكُمْ فِيهَا مَنَافِعُ كَثِيرَةٌ وَمِنْهَا تَأْكُلُونَ

Wa-lakum fīhā manāfiʿu kathīratun wa-minhā taʾkulūn.

“You have many benefits in them, and from them you eat.”

Al-Muʾminūn 23:21

Concerning iron:

وَأَنْزَلْنَا الْحَدِيدَ فِيهِ بَأْسٌ شَدِيدٌ وَمَنَافِعُ لِلنَّاسِ

Wa-anzalnā l-ḥadīda fīhi baʾsun shadīdun wa-manāfiʿu li-n-nās.

“We sent down iron, in which there is formidable force and benefits for people.”

Al-Ḥadīd 57:25

And concerning Hajj:

لِيَشْهَدُوا مَنَافِعَ لَهُمْ

Li-yashhadū manāfiʿa lahum.

“So that they may witness benefits for themselves.”

Al-Ḥajj 22:28

In none of these instances does manāfiʿ mean merely financial profit. The benefit may be material, social, devotional, or practical. The word itself indicates an advantage or beneficial outcome; context determines its kind.

In Hajj in particular, manāfiʿ should not be restricted without justification either to trade alone or to spiritual reward alone. The ayah uses a broader designation.

Benefit is relational: person, time, and situation matter

Qur’anic usage shows that being beneficial is not necessarily a fixed, independent property of something. We must ask whom it benefits, and in what circumstances.

In Yūsuf’s story, it is said:

عَسَىٰ أَنْ يَنْفَعَنَا أَوْ نَتَّخِذَهُ وَلَدًا

ʿAsā an yanfaʿanā aw nattakhidhahu waladā.

“Perhaps he will benefit us, or we may take him as a son.”

Yūsuf 12:21

The same expression also occurs concerning Mūsā:

عَسَىٰ أَنْ يَنْفَعَنَا أَوْ نَتَّخِذَهُ وَلَدًا

ʿAsā an yanfaʿanā aw nattakhidhahu waladā.

Al-Qaṣaṣ 28:9

One human being can benefit another.

In Al-Baqarah, ships likewise:

تَجْرِي فِي الْبَحْرِ بِمَا يَنْفَعُ النَّاسَ

Tajrī fī l-baḥri bi-mā yanfaʿu n-nās.

“Sail upon the sea with what benefits people.”

Al-Baqarah 2:164

Benefit therefore passes between created beings within ordinary life. A person, an animal, a tool, goods, knowledge, or an action can benefit another under certain circumstances.

That relationship may change, however, when the time and circumstances change.

Concerning faith, it is said:

يَوْمَ يَأْتِي بَعْضُ آيَاتِ رَبِّكَ لَا يَنْفَعُ نَفْسًا إِيمَانُهَا لَمْ تَكُنْ آمَنَتْ مِنْ قَبْلُ

Yawma yaʾtī baʿḍu āyāti rabbika lā yanfaʿu nafsan īmānuhā lam takun āmanat min qabl.

“On the day when some of your Lord’s signs come, a person’s faith will not benefit them if they had not believed before.”

Al-Anʿām 6:158

In this ayah, the timing of coming to faith affects whether it benefits someone. It does not establish that the quality and reality of faith are identical in all these situations.

The same point appears in Ghāfir:

فَلَمْ يَكُ يَنْفَعُهُمْ إِيمَانُهُمْ لَمَّا رَأَوْا بَأْسَنَا

Fa-lam yaku yanfaʿuhum īmānuhum lammā raʾaw baʾsanā.

“Their faith did not benefit them once they had seen Our severe punishment.”

Ghāfir 40:85

By contrast, concerning the people of Yūnus:

فَلَوْلَا كَانَتْ قَرْيَةٌ آمَنَتْ فَنَفَعَهَا إِيمَانُهَا إِلَّا قَوْمَ يُونُسَ

Fa-lawlā kānat qaryatun āmanat fa-nafaʿahā īmānuhā illā qawma Yūnus.

“Why was there no town that believed and benefited from its faith, except the people of Yūnus?”

Yūnus 10:98

“Faith” benefits in one situation and fails to produce the intended outcome in another. The same designation does not establish that all its conditions and qualities are identical.

This feature becomes even clearer in ayahs concerning the Hereafter:

يَوْمَ لَا يَنْفَعُ مَالٌ وَلَا بَنُونَ

Yawma lā yanfaʿu mālun wa-lā banūn.

“The day when neither wealth nor sons will benefit.”

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:88

This ayah does not say that wealth and children have no benefit in this life. Elsewhere, the Qur’an itself speaks of life’s benefits. The point is that on that day, they do not have the power to save.

Al-Mumtaḥanah likewise states:

لَنْ تَنْفَعَكُمْ أَرْحَامُكُمْ وَلَا أَوْلَادُكُمْ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ

Lan tanfaʿakum arḥāmukum wa-lā awlādukum yawma l-qiyāmah.

“Your relatives and your children will not benefit you on the Day of Resurrection.”

Al-Mumtaḥanah 60:3

An excuse does not benefit in that situation either:

فَيَوْمَئِذٍ لَا يَنْفَعُ الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا مَعْذِرَتُهُمْ

Fa-yawmaʾidhin lā yanfaʿu lladhīna ẓalamū maʿdhiratuhum.

“On that day, the excuse of those who did wrong will not benefit them.”

Al-Rūm 30:57

Intercession, too, has no independent, unconditional effect:

يَوْمَئِذٍ لَا تَنْفَعُ الشَّفَاعَةُ إِلَّا مَنْ أَذِنَ لَهُ الرَّحْمَٰنُ وَرَضِيَ لَهُ قَوْلًا

Yawmaʾidhin lā tanfaʿu sh-shafāʿatu illā man adhina lahu r-raḥmānu wa-raḍiya lahu qawlā.

“On that day, intercession will not benefit, except the intercession of someone whom the All-Merciful has permitted and whose speech He has approved.”

This rendering takes man, “someone,” to refer to the intercessor. Another analysis takes it to concern the person for whom intercession is permitted. In both analyses, intercession’s effect is conditional upon permission and approval.

Ṭā Hā 20:109

In contrast, other things do benefit people in that same setting:

هَٰذَا يَوْمُ يَنْفَعُ الصَّادِقِينَ صِدْقُهُمْ

Hādhā yawmu yanfaʿu ṣ-ṣādiqīna ṣidquhum.

“This is the day when the truthful will benefit from their truthfulness.”

Al-Māʾidah 5:119

These examples show that the central question in nafʿ is not simply “Is this thing good?” It is:

Does this thing produce a beneficial outcome for this person in this situation?

Giving benefit is different from possessing power over benefit

One of this root’s most important Qur’anic distinctions is between benefit occurring through a means and possessing power over benefit.

The Qur’an clearly allows that created beings and means can benefit under certain circumstances. Yūsuf may benefit a household; a ship carries something beneficial; livestock and iron have benefits; and a reminder can benefit believers.

Concerning constructed gods, by contrast, it says:

قُلْ أَتَعْبُدُونَ مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ مَا لَا يَمْلِكُ لَكُمْ ضَرًّا وَلَا نَفْعًا

Qul a-taʿbudūna min dūni llāhi mā lā yamliku lakum ḍarran wa-lā nafʿā.

“Say: ‘Do you worship, apart from The God, something that possesses no power of harm or benefit for you?’”

Al-Māʾidah 5:76

Concerning the calf, it likewise says:

أَفَلَا يَرَوْنَ أَلَّا يَرْجِعُ إِلَيْهِمْ قَوْلًا وَلَا يَمْلِكُ لَهُمْ ضَرًّا وَلَا نَفْعًا

A-fa-lā yarawna allā yarjiʿu ilayhim qawlan wa-lā yamliku lahum ḍarran wa-lā nafʿā.

“Do they not see that it returns no answer to them and possesses no power of harm or benefit for them?”

Ṭā Hā 20:89

Even the Prophet says:

قُلْ لَا أَمْلِكُ لِنَفْسِي نَفْعًا وَلَا ضَرًّا إِلَّا مَا شَاءَ اللَّهُ

Qul lā amliku li-nafsī nafʿan wa-lā ḍarran illā mā shāʾa llāh.

“Say: ‘I possess no power of benefit or harm for myself, except what The God wills.’”

Al-Aʿrāf 7:188

The key expression in these constructions is يَمْلِكُ / أَمْلِكُ, yamliku / amliku, from the separate root م ل ك, M L K, concerning possession, ownership, and power of disposal. This verb is not part of N F AYN; it specifies a person’s relationship to benefit and harm. The qualification “independent” is inferred from these ayahs together and from context, especially إِلَّا مَا شَاءَ اللَّهُ, illā mā shāʾa llāh, “except what The God wills.” It is not part of the lexical meaning of yamliku itself.

The distinction becomes clear here:

Something can be a means of benefit without independently possessing power over benefit.

A ship can benefit people, but does not independently control their destiny. One person can benefit another, but does not possess absolute power over that person’s outcome and final destination.

Ayahs describing the benefits of created things therefore do not conflict with ayahs denying constructed gods—or even the Prophet—independent possession of benefit and harm. They address two different levels: having an effect among other causes and possessing authority and control over benefit and harm.

The distinction also matters to the Qur’an’s reasoning about worship. The point is not that a thing or person can never provide any benefit. It is that they cannot be assigned a position in which they are thought independently to possess such authority over benefit and harm.

N F AYN (nafʿ), Ḍ R R (ḍarar), and KH Y R (khayr): benefit, harm, and good

The clearest Qur’anic opposite of nafʿ is ضَرّ, ḍarr, from the family Ḍ R R. The two repeatedly appear in a single construction:

نَفْعًا وَلَا ضَرًّا

Nafʿan wa-lā ḍarrā.

This opposition reveals the two sides of a relationship:

Nafʿ is an effect producing a beneficial or favorable outcome for someone;

Ḍarar is an effect producing loss or an adverse outcome for someone.

The opposition concerns the kind of effect, not necessarily the moral value of the thing producing it.

The same point distinguishes nafʿ from khayr, good.

خ ي ر, KH Y R (خَيْر, khayr), can refer in the Qur’an to goodness, superiority, desirability, or something judged better. Nafʿ primarily concerns the benefit something produces for someone. Khayr, too, is not always a strictly moral term: in some contexts it refers to wealth or material good. The distinction between the two therefore needs to be assessed in each particular usage.

Al-Baqarah 2:219 clearly illustrates this boundary. Khamr and maysir have “benefits,” but those benefits do not make the overall judgment of them positive.

Thus something may:

Be beneficial without being good overall;

Or be good, yet under particular circumstances no longer produce the expected benefit for a person.

Benefit is not identical to pleasure either. Something can be pleasant without producing a beneficial outcome, or beneficial even though the immediate experience is unpleasant. These distinctions emerge from context and outcome, not from the root alone.

As general tendencies:

Nafʿ is an advantage or beneficial outcome for someone;

Ḍarar is a harmful outcome or loss for someone;

And:

Khayr belongs to the broader field of goodness and evaluative desirability.

These fields can overlap, but they are not completely synonymous.

Translation proposals for Forqan

The verb نَفَعَ / يَنْفَعُ, nafaʿa / yanfaʿu, is usually naturally rendered “to benefit” or “to be useful.” For example, يَنْفَعُ النَّاسَ, yanfaʿu al-nāsa, can be “it benefits people”; عَسَىٰ أَنْ يَنْفَعَنَا, ʿasā an yanfaʿanā, “perhaps he will benefit us”; and لَا يَنْفَعُ مَالٌ وَلَا بَنُونَ, lā yanfaʿu mālun wa-lā banūna, “neither wealth nor sons will benefit.”

نَفْع, nafʿ, is usually “benefit” or “advantage.” In نَفْعًا وَلَا ضَرًّا, nafʿan wa-lā ḍarran, “benefit or harm” is natural within the negative construction. In Al-Baqarah 2:219, وَإِثْمُهُمَا أَكْبَرُ مِنْ نَفْعِهِمَا, wa-ithmuhumā akbaru min nafʿihimā, can be “their sin is greater than their benefit.”

مَنَافِع, manāfiʿ, can be “benefits,” “advantages,” or “uses,” according to context. “Various benefits” is natural concerning livestock and iron. In Hajj, the word’s breadth should be preserved without restricting it to trade, income, or a particular spiritual reward.

In constructions such as هَٰذَا يَوْمُ يَنْفَعُ الصَّادِقِينَ صِدْقُهُمْ, hādhā yawmu yanfaʿu al-ṣādiqīna ṣidquhum, it is better to preserve the sentence’s direct relationship: “This is the day when the truthful will benefit from their truthfulness.” This is clearer than renderings that dissolve nafʿ into broader moral concepts.

Conclusion

N F AYN denotes the benefit and favorable outcome that something produces for someone. Its clearest Qur’anic opposite is harm, but benefit is not identical to moral goodness. Khamr and maysir can have benefits while being judged negatively overall. Nafʿ therefore describes a beneficial effect rather than giving a final judgment of a thing’s value.

Another important feature is the relational character of benefit. Whether something benefits depends on the person, time, and situation. Faith benefits someone in one situation, but no longer produces the same result after the relevant opportunity has passed. Wealth, children, excuses, and intercession may likewise cease to benefit under the conditions the Qur’an describes. Truthfulness and reminders, by contrast, can benefit in the appropriate setting.

Benefiting someone must also be distinguished from possessing power over benefit. People and things can benefit through ordinary causes, but yamliku belongs to another root and concerns possession and control. The Qur’an denies constructed gods independent possession of benefit and harm, and the Prophet does not claim such possession for himself. This distinction preserves both the real benefit of ordinary means and their ultimate dependence on The God’s will.

A concise definition of the root can be stated as follows:

N F AYN: producing benefit, advantage, or a favorable outcome for someone or something. Benefit is relational, dependent on person, time, and situation, and does not in itself amount to moral goodness. Something can be a means of benefit without independently possessing power over benefit; its kind and extent must be assessed through each ayah’s construction and context.
