Abundance, completeness, and remaining undiminished

The Arabic root w-f-r denotes abundance, completeness, and freedom from diminution. Wafr is used for abundant wealth and possessions; wāfir for something plentiful or complete; and mawfūr for something preserved intact or provided in full.

The root has two closely related aspects: abundance in quantity, and preservation of entirety without anything being deducted. These meanings overlap but are not always identical. Something may be abundant yet fall short of the expected total; or it may remain completely intact without being especially large in quantity.

In the Qur’an, this root occurs only once, as مَوْفُورًا in al-Isrāʾ 17:63. It describes recompense that will be complete and undiminished.

1. The root in early and classical Arabic

Poetry attributed to pre-Islamic poets uses wafr for abundant wealth and wafara for keeping something intact. These usages show that the root’s semantic range in the early literary tradition encompasses both abundance and freedom from diminution.

A verse attributed to Ḥātim al-Ṭāʾī reads:

وَمَا دَارِعٌ إِلَّا كَآخَرَ حَاسِرٍ
وَمَا مُقْتِرٌ إِلَّا كَآخَرَ ذِي وَفْرِ

wa-mā dāriʿun illā ka-ākhara ḥāsirin / wa-mā muqtirun illā ka-ākhara dhī wafri

Translation: “The armoured man is like another without armour; the poor man is like another possessing abundant wealth.”

In a passage about death, the poet compares the armoured with the unarmoured and the poor with the wealthy. His point is that these differences do not deliver anyone from death.

Dhī wafr here refers to someone possessing abundant wealth. Its contrast with muqtir, a person in straitened circumstances, shows that wafr in this usage concerns material prosperity and plentiful possessions.

Another witness, attributed to al-Hudhayl al-Akbar al-Taghlibī, reads:

أَلِكْنِي وَفِرْ لِابْنِ الْغُرَيْرَةِ عِرْضَهُ
إِلَى خَالِدٍ مِنْ آلِ سَلْمَى بْنِ جَنْدَلِ

aliknī wa-fir li-bni al-Ghurayrati ʿirḍahu / ilā Khālidin min āli Salmā bni Jandali

Translation: “Carry my message to Khālid of the family of Salmā ibn Jandal, and preserve Ibn al-Ghurayrah’s honour.”

Here, وَفِرْ commands that someone’s honour be left intact. The point is not to increase his honour, but to avoid harming or diminishing it.

This usage matters because it shows that w-f-r does not concern quantity alone: it can also express preserving something’s entirety.

Both verses are attributed to poets classified as pre-Islamic in the Arabic literary tradition. Their texts, however, survive through later collections and commentaries, and the precise date of each verse has not been independently established. They therefore support our understanding of early usage without conclusively dating the emergence of the root’s meanings.

Arabic dictionaries also record other concrete examples. أَرْضٌ وَفْرَاءُ describes land with abundant, undiminished vegetation. مَزَادَةٌ وَفْرَاءُ describes a large waterskin made without cutting away any of its hide.

These examples clarify the distinction between the root’s two aspects. Abundance stands out in richly vegetated land; in a waterskin made from a whole hide, what matters is preserving the material’s entirety.

Neither image, however, can be treated as the original source of all the root’s meanings without historical evidence. Its defensible semantic core is abundance and completeness.

2. Forms and derivatives

Important forms recorded in Arabic include:

  • وَفْر: abundance, especially plentiful wealth and possessions.
  • وُفُور: being abundant, complete, or undiminished.
  • وَفَرَ: to become abundant; transitively, to preserve something in full or keep it intact.
  • وافِر: abundant, complete, or plentiful.
  • مَوْفُور: something provided in full or preserved undiminished.
  • وَفَّرَ: to make something abundant or complete; to preserve it against diminution.
  • تَوْفِير: making abundant, providing fully, or preserving against diminution.
  • تَوَفَّرَ: to become abundant or available; in some constructions, to direct one’s attention and effort to a task.
  • وَفْرَة: abundance; also a name for hair extending to around the ears.
  • وَفْرَاء: a feminine adjective for something abundant or complete.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an. Its sole form from this family is مَوْفُورًا.

Wafrah as a term for hair also illustrates naming associated with quantity and fullness. Dictionaries do not all report the same precise length for this hairstyle. That variation does not affect the Qur’anic meaning of mawfūr.

Morphologically, مَوْفُور is a passive participle on the pattern مَفْعُول from transitive وَفَرَ. It should not be confused with مُوَفَّر, the Form II passive participle, although the two structures can have closely related meanings in some usages.

3. Qur’anic usage: complete and undiminished recompense

Al-Isrāʾ 17:63

قَالَ اذْهَبْ فَمَن تَبِعَكَ مِنْهُمْ فَإِنَّ جَهَنَّمَ جَزَاؤُكُمْ جَزَاءً مَوْفُورًا

qāla idhhab fa-man tabiʿaka minhum fa-inna Jahannama jazāʾukum jazāʾan mawfūrā

Al-Isrāʾ 17:63

Translation: “He said: Go! Whoever among them follows you—Hell will indeed be your recompense, a complete and undiminished recompense.”

This verse continues the exchange between God and Iblīs.

In al-Isrāʾ 17:62, Iblīs speaks of his intention to lead Adam’s descendants astray. In al-Isrāʾ 17:63, the divine response is clear: whoever among them follows him will face the consequence of that following.

فَمَن تَبِعَكَ مِنْهُمْ establishes a decisive condition. The statement concerns those who follow Iblīs, rather than all of Adam’s descendants.

جَزَاؤُكُمْ states the recompense of Iblīs and his followers, while جَزَاءً مَوْفُورًا specifies its character.

The word جَزَاءً is repeated and accompanied by the adjective مَوْفُورًا. This repetition emphasizes the recompense and draws attention to its completeness.

Here مَوْفُورًا modifies جَزَاءً. The verse thus describes recompense that will be full and undiminished.

Of the root’s two aspects—abundance and completeness—the latter is especially significant in this phrase, since the recompense corresponds to human following and conduct.

To clarify the difference, suppose someone is entitled to a specified wage for work. If less than that amount is paid, the payment is incomplete, even if others consider the sum large. Full payment, by contrast, means that everything due is rendered without deduction.

This example only distinguishes a plentiful quantity from a complete quantity. The verse concerns punishment, and that sense is determined by its context.

Consequently, جَزَاءً مَوْفُورًا should not be restricted to “a very great punishment.” Abundance belongs to the root’s range, but mawfūr in this verse also denotes the fullness and undiminished character of the recompense.

Conversely, mawfūr by itself does not determine the punishment’s duration, equal severity for every individual, or infinite intensity. Such matters require separate Qur’anic evidence. The word characterizes the recompense without specifying every detail of its execution.

4. Complete recompense and responsibility for following

The following verse, al-Isrāʾ 17:64, speaks of Iblīs’s efforts to incite, deceive, and make promises to human beings. Al-Isrāʾ 17:65 then defines the limit of his power, stating that he has no authority over God’s servants.

This continuity further clarifies the significance of the condition فَمَن تَبِعَكَ, “whoever follows you.”

Iblīs can invite and tempt, but his invitation does not make human compliance inevitable. His promises are described as deceptive in the same context.

The recompense stated in al-Isrāʾ 17:63 therefore concerns the relationship between a misleading invitation and following it. Both the one issuing the invitation and the one responding to it are present as responsible agents.

Nevertheless, mawfūr does not imply that Iblīs and all his followers bear equal responsibility or receive an identical measure of punishment. The verse associates them in the general outcome without using this word to specify each individual’s responsibility.

Nor does the lexical meaning of mawfūr establish an independent moral judgment about every person. Censure follows from the context of following Iblīs and recompense for deeds, rather than from w-f-r itself. Arabic also uses this root for abundant possessions, a full blessing, or preserving someone’s honour.

5. Distinctions from related roots

W-f-r (wafr) and w-f-y (wafāʾ): abundance and completeness alongside full discharge of a right

These roots are close in spelling and meaning, but they remain distinct.

W-f-r emphasizes abundance, entirety, and freedom from diminution. In Qur’anic usage, w-f-y concerns rendering something in full, fulfilling a covenant, discharging a right, or taking or delivering something in its entirety.

For example, أَوْفُوا الْكَيْلَ in al-Isrāʾ 17:35 commands giving full measure. The act of rendering what is due is prominent here. By contrast, جَزَاءً مَوْفُورًا characterizes the recompense itself as complete and undiminished.

The roots overlap in completeness but differ in formation and Qur’anic usage. مَوْفُور derives from w-f-r, not w-f-y.

Study of w-f-y

W-f-r (wafr) and k-th-r (kathrah): abundance and completeness

Kathrah denotes a large number or quantity. Wufūr can likewise express abundance, but in usages such as mawfūr, completeness and freedom from deduction become prominent.

Something may be plentiful while its full amount is not present. A large harvest from an orchard can be called kathīr; yet if part of the crop has been lost, the entire crop has not been preserved.

This distinction does not establish an absolute boundary in every use of the two roots, but it helps explain مَوْفُورًا in al-Isrāʾ 17:63.

Study of k-th-r

W-f-r (wafr) and t-m-m (tamām): completeness and reaching completion

Tamām concerns something becoming complete or reaching its endpoint and full extent. Wufūr can express completeness and preservation of a quantity.

These meanings converge when describing something undiminished. Completing a task and possessing abundant wealth, however, are different usages.

In جَزَاءً مَوْفُورًا, completeness is an appropriate meaning. All derivatives of w-f-r should nevertheless not be translated solely as “completing,” since abundance has an independent place in Arabic usage.

Study of t-m-m

W-f-r (wafr) and n-q-ṣ (naqṣ): completeness and deficiency

Naqṣ refers to diminution or deficiency. Where mawfūr means complete and undiminished, naqṣ stands in contrast to it.

This contrast is especially clear for something expected to remain at its full quantity. Yet absence of deficiency does not always mean exceptional abundance: something may be small while that small amount remains entirely intact.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

For wafr in the context of possessions, “abundant wealth” or “plentiful property” is appropriate.

For wāfir, “abundant,” “complete,” or “full” can be used according to context.

For waffara, suitable equivalents include “made abundant,” “provided in full,” or “preserved against diminution.” Modern usages involving saving resources also require attention to the verb’s object: economy or saving is not present in every use of the root.

For mawfūr, the principal equivalents are “complete,” “full,” and “undiminished.” Where context emphasizes a large quantity, “abundant” may also fit.

For al-Isrāʾ 17:63, the proposed translation is:

“He said: Go! Whoever among them follows you—Hell will indeed be your recompense, a complete and undiminished recompense.”

“Complete and undiminished” conveys both entirety and the absence of deduction. A shorter rendering, “full recompense,” also preserves the central meaning.

7. Conclusion

The Arabic root w-f-r has two closely related aspects: abundance and completeness. Something wāfir may be plentiful, while something mawfūr may be provided or preserved in full, without deficiency.

Poetic witnesses attributed to the pre-Islamic period illustrate wafr both for abundant possessions and for preserving someone’s honour. Concrete examples recorded in Arabic dictionaries support the same semantic range.

In the Qur’an, the root occurs only in جَزَاءً مَوْفُورًا. This phrase describes complete and undiminished recompense. Its precise amount, duration, and manner cannot be derived from mawfūr alone.

The distinction from w-f-y must also be preserved. In Qur’anic usage, wafāʾ commonly concerns rendering a right in full or fulfilling a commitment, whereas mawfūr in al-Isrāʾ 17:63 describes the completeness of the recompense itself.

The semantic core of w-f-r is abundance and completeness, emphasizing that something is present or preserved in its full amount. In its Qur’anic usage, مَوْفُورًا refers to complete and undiminished recompense.
