Departure, travel, and the traveler’s equipment

The Arabic root R Ḥ L concerns setting out, leaving a place of residence for another, and the things carried on a journey. Riḥlah denotes travel or departure, raḥl the traveler’s equipment and baggage, and rāḥilah a mount suited to travel.

Despite their shared root, these words do not mean the same thing. Riḥlah concerns the journey and movement between places, while raḥl may denote riding equipment, the traveler’s baggage and belongings, or even a dwelling or place of residence.

The root occurs four times in two principal forms in the Qur’an. Riḥlah appears once in Quraysh; raḥl appears three times, in singular and plural forms, in Yūsuf.

Qur’anic forms: riḥlata (Quraysh 106:2), riḥālihim (Yūsuf 12:62), raḥli (Yūsuf 12:70), and raḥlihi (Yūsuf 12:75).

Other established Arabic forms include raḥala, raḥīl, rāḥil, rāḥilah, raḥḥala, taraḥḥala, irtaḥala, irtiḥāl, raḥḥāl and raḥḥālah for a frequent traveler, riḥālah for a kind of riding equipment, and marḥalah. These forms do not occur as such in the Qur’an; their lexical uses must be distinguished from the actual Qur’anic forms.

1. Core meaning and early Arabic usage

Leaving a place of residence

Raḥala al-rajulu means that a man set out and left his residence to travel. Irtaḥala al-qawmu is used when a group departs from its place and heads elsewhere.

The root’s core has been explained as muḍiyyun fī safarin: going and proceeding on a journey.

This meaning has a clear connection with nomadic Arabic life. On moving, people gathered their belongings and loads, fastened them to mounts, left their previous stopping place, and settled at another.

Words for departure and travel equipment thus belong to one lexical family.

Established usage must nevertheless be distinguished from a certain history of the words’ origins.

The connection between raḥl, riḥlah, and irtiḥāl is clear. Without sufficient historical evidence, however, we cannot determine whether the riding equipment was named first and the verb of departure developed later, or the reverse.

The basic definition should rest on actual usage, rather than an imagined story about humanity’s first migration or first traveling equipment.

Raḥl: riding equipment and travel baggage

In early Arabic, raḥl named a structure or device placed on a camel’s back so that someone could sit on it and travel.

Its function was close to that of a horse’s saddle, but its construction and use were not necessarily identical.

Established plurals of raḥl include arḥul and riḥāl.

In early usage, raḥl sometimes denotes only the main wooden frame of the riding equipment, and sometimes the whole apparatus, straps, coverings, and accompanying fittings.

Its scope was therefore not always restricted to a particular component.

Alongside its use for riding equipment, raḥl also named the baggage and equipment carried by a traveler.

This equipment might include vessels, bundles, coverings, and other items prepared for a journey.

Raḥl can consequently denote either the riding equipment itself or the traveler’s collection of baggage.

This breadth matters for its Qur’anic use in Joseph’s story, where objects are placed in riḥāl.

Restricting raḥl everywhere to a saddle’s wooden frame would fail to convey these verses properly.

Raḥl: a dwelling or stopping place

Raḥl al-rajul can also denote a person’s dwelling or place of residence.

One lexical explanation relates this use to the things a traveler carries, which provide for lodging and life when stopping.

The word also denotes the person’s dwelling while resident.

Raḥl can thus mean a home or shelter in some expressions, even when its owner is not traveling at that moment.

It does not follow that raḥl always means a house.

Where the Qur’an speaks of putting goods or a cup in a raḥl, baggage and traveling equipment fit the context better.

One root may have several related uses without all of them being present in every sentence.

Rāḥilah: a mount suited to travel

In early Arabic, rāḥilah was used for a camel suitable for travel and carrying loads.

It was not restricted to female camels. Reliable lexical accounts apply it to either male or female camels when suitable for riding and covering a route.

Raḥīl is also recorded for a strong camel capable of travel, and raḥliyyah for a she-camel strong in covering a route.

Some derivatives therefore express strength and readiness for movement.

This quality must not be extended to the whole root. Riḥlah does not itself mean an easy, fast, or safe journey.

Nor does a mount’s suitability establish that the journey is necessarily long or has a destination at a fixed distance.

2. Early poetic witnesses: saddling, departure, and stopping

Early Arabic poetry uses this root both for preparing a mount and for movement and stopping during travel.

Fastening equipment on a camel

The pre-Islamic poet al-Muthaqqib al-ʿAbdī describes his camel:

إِذَا مَا قُمْتُ أَرْحَلُهَا بِلَيْلٍ
تَأَوَّهَ آهَةَ الرَّجُلِ الْحَزِينِ

idhā mā qumtu arḥaluhā bi-laylin / taʾawwaha āhata al-rajuli al-ḥazīni

“Whenever I rise at night to fasten her travel equipment, she sighs like a sorrowful man.”

Arḥaluhā here concerns preparing the camel for travel and fastening equipment on it.

The poet portrays the camel as exhausted by repeated journeys, groaning like a sorrowful person when prepared to depart.

This witness illustrates an important distinction: transitive raḥala can mean equipping and saddling a mount, while intransitive raḥala means setting out or departing.

The same poem also speaks of stopping and setting down the equipment:

ثَنَيْتُ زِمَامَهَا وَوَضَعْتُ رَحْلِي
وَنُمْرُقَةً رَفَدْتُ بِهَا يَمِينِي

thanaytu zimāmahā wa-waḍaʿtu raḥlī / wa-numruqatan rafadtu bihā yamīnī

“I turned her rein and set down my traveling equipment, and a cushion on which I supported my right hand.”

Raḥlī here denotes the traveler’s equipment, set down when stopping.

Together these witnesses show the practical relationship between saddling, movement, and stopping.

This does not restrict all R Ḥ L derivatives to camels. The camel was a principal mount in early Arabic travel, but the linguistic meaning of travel is not confined to one animal.

Departure in al-Aʿshā’s poetry

A poem attributed to al-Aʿshā includes:

رَحَلَتْ سُمَيَّةُ غُدْوَةً أَجْمَالَهَا
غَضْبَى عَلَيْكَ فَمَا تَقُولُ بَدَا لَهَا

raḥalat sumayyatu ghudwatan ajmālahā / ghaḍbā ʿalayka fa-mā taqūlu badā lahā

“Sumayyah saddled her camels in the morning, angry with you. What do you say has occurred to her?”

Raḥalat here takes ajmālahā, her camels, as its object.

The meaning is that Sumayyah prepared the camels for departure, rather than abandoning the camels themselves.

This again shows how syntax helps determine a verb’s meaning.

Al-Aʿshā lived in the pre-Islamic period and on the threshold of Islam. The poem’s attribution is established in his collected poetry, but without independent evidence its precise composition should not be dated with certainty before Qur’anic revelation.

Together these witnesses connect R Ḥ L with practical activities of travel: preparing a mount, fastening baggage, setting out, stopping, and carrying belongings.

3. Riḥlata al-shitāʾi wa-al-ṣayfi: travel in Quraysh

The only Qur’anic use of riḥlah occurs in Quraysh 106:2:

إِيلَافِهِمْ رِحْلَةَ الشِّتَاءِ وَالصَّيْفِ

īlāfihim riḥlata al-shitāʾi wa-al-ṣayfi

“Their familiarity with the journey of winter and summer.”

The expression should be read with Quraysh 106:1, Quraysh 106:2, Quraysh 106:3, and Quraysh 106:4:

لِإِيلَافِ قُرَيْشٍ ۝ إِيلَافِهِمْ رِحْلَةَ الشِّتَاءِ وَالصَّيْفِ ۝ فَلْيَعْبُدُوا رَبَّ هَٰذَا الْبَيْتِ ۝ الَّذِي أَطْعَمَهُمْ مِنْ جُوعٍ وَآمَنَهُمْ مِنْ خَوْفٍ

li-īlāfi qurayshin / īlāfihim riḥlata al-shitāʾi wa-al-ṣayfi / fa-l-yaʿbudū rabba hādhā al-bayti / alladhī aṭʿamahum min jūʿin wa-āmanahum min khawfin

“For the familiarity of Quraysh; their familiarity with the journey of winter and summer; let them worship the Lord of this House, who fed them against hunger and made them safe from fear.”

Riḥlah in this verse

Riḥlah is a noun meaning a journey or departure.

Here it stands in a possessive construction with the two seasons al-shitāʾ and al-ṣayf: the journey of winter and summer.

The construction indicates travel associated with these seasons.

Riḥlah is singular, but that does not mean Quraysh traveled only once or made only one journey in its entire history.

An Arabic singular noun or verbal noun can name an activity repeated on different occasions.

Mentioning winter and summer places the travel in two seasonal settings. Familiar or recurring winter and summer journeys are therefore consistent with the construction.

Riḥlah does not itself specify the exact number of journeys or caravans, or the duration of each journey.

Does riḥlah mean trade?

Historical and exegetical accounts commonly connect Quraysh’s journeys with trade.

This explanation fits accounts of Quraysh’s economic position and Arabian trade routes.

The lexical meaning of riḥlah must nevertheless be distinguished from a journey’s purpose.

Riḥlah basically denotes travel, rather than buying, selling, profit, capital, or commerce.

A journey may be for trade, but trade is not an invariable part of the word’s meaning.

The basic translation should therefore not replace riḥlah with “winter and summer trade,” since the Arabic expression does not contain the word for trade.

A historical explanation can connect these journeys with trade in familiar accounts, while remaining distinct from the literal translation.

The destinations of Quraysh’s journeys

Exegetical accounts commonly describe a winter journey toward Yemen and a summer journey toward Syria.

Other accounts of Quraysh’s residence and seasonal routes also exist.

The surah itself names neither Yemen nor Syria nor another city.

It speaks only of riḥlata al-shitāʾi wa-al-ṣayfi.

Exact routes, destinations, and historical details require historical research; they should not be added to the root’s meaning or the direct translation.

Travel, familiarity, and security

Riḥlah is connected here with īlāfihim.

Īlāf comes from ʾ L F and concerns familiarity and becoming accustomed. Its full semantic range requires a separate study.

Quraysh’s winter and summer journeys are presented in connection with familiarity and their known conditions of life.

The surah then mentions being fed against hunger and made safe from fear.

This connection matters because human travel, especially for obtaining necessities, may depend on routes, transport, security, and access to resources.

Security and food, however, come from the fourth verse rather than from riḥlah itself.

Nor does the surah establish that every journey is religiously obligatory or that every form of trade and travel is inherently virtuous.

It presents Quraysh’s situation in a reminder of blessings and a call to worship the Lord of this House.

Riḥlah thus retains its meaning of travel, while the complete passage supplies its significance and message.

4. Riḥālihim: travelers’ baggage in Joseph’s story

The root’s other three occurrences are in Yūsuf.

These name the baggage and belongings travelers carry, rather than the journey itself.

Returning the goods in the brothers’ baggage

Yūsuf 12:62 states:

وَقَالَ لِفِتْيَانِهِ اجْعَلُوا بِضَاعَتَهُمْ فِي رِحَالِهِمْ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَعْرِفُونَهَا إِذَا انْقَلَبُوا إِلَىٰ أَهْلِهِمْ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَرْجِعُونَ

wa-qāla li-fityānihi ijʿalū biḍāʿatahum fī riḥālihim laʿallahum yaʿrifūnahā idhā inqalabū ilā ahlihim laʿallahum yarjiʿūna

“He told his young men: Put their goods in their baggage, so that they may recognize them when they return to their family, and perhaps return.”

Riḥālihim is the plural of raḥl with a plural possessive pronoun, denoting Joseph’s brothers’ baggage and traveling equipment.

Riḥāl here is where the biḍāʿah is placed.

Biḍāʿah belongs to another root and concerns goods or assets used in exchange. Its meaning should not become part of raḥl itself.

Ijʿalū is the verb of placing, and fī expresses a spatial relationship.

The verse thus speaks of placing something in the travelers’ baggage; raḥl itself does not mean hiding or returning goods.

Part of Yūsuf 12:65 continues:

وَلَمَّا فَتَحُوا مَتَاعَهُمْ وَجَدُوا بِضَاعَتَهُمْ رُدَّتْ إِلَيْهِمْ

wa-lammā fataḥū matāʿahum wajadū biḍāʿatahum ruddat ilayhim

“When they opened their belongings, they found their goods returned to them.”

What had been put in the baggage was discovered when they opened their belongings.

Riḥālihim in verse 62 is consequently close to traveling baggage and bundles.

Translating it only as “their camels’ saddles” may narrow it unnecessarily: the passage concerns baggage into which objects have been placed.

“Their houses,” on the other hand, fits the travelers’ situation and their return to their families less well.

“Their baggage” or “their traveling belongings” therefore suits this verse.

Raḥli akhīhi: Joseph’s brother’s baggage

The second occurrence is in Yūsuf 12:70:

فَلَمَّا جَهَّزَهُمْ بِجَهَازِهِمْ جَعَلَ السِّقَايَةَ فِي رَحْلِ أَخِيهِ ثُمَّ أَذَّنَ مُؤَذِّنٌ أَيَّتُهَا الْعِيرُ إِنَّكُمْ لَسَارِقُونَ

fa-lammā jahhazahum bi-jahāzihim jaʿala al-siqāyata fī raḥli akhīhi thumma adhdhana muʾadhdhinun ayyatuhā al-ʿīru innakum la-sāriqūna

“When he had provided their supplies, he put the cup in his brother’s baggage. Then a caller proclaimed: Caravan, you are thieves.”

Raḥli akhīhi refers to the baggage accompanying Joseph’s brother.

Jaʿala al-siqāyata fī raḥli akhīhi means that he put the cup in his baggage.

Again, raḥl is where an object is placed, rather than the object itself.

This agrees with riḥālihim in verse 62.

The first verse concerns several people’s baggage and uses plural riḥāl; this verse concerns one person’s baggage and uses singular raḥl.

The difference in number and possessive construction does not change the word’s core meaning.

Verse 70 also contains jahhazahum bi-jahāzihim.

Jahāz concerns equipment and supplies provided for departure; preparing them is expressed by the verb jahhaza. This family comes from J H Z and should not be equated with raḥl.

Preparing supplies, placing a cup in the baggage, and calling to the caravan are three separate events.

Raḥl itself therefore does not mean preparing, packing, or traveling. It denotes the baggage and equipment used in this situation.

Man wujida fī raḥlihi: searching a person’s baggage

Yūsuf 12:75 continues:

قَالُوا جَزَاؤُهُ مَنْ وُجِدَ فِي رَحْلِهِ فَهُوَ جَزَاؤُهُ كَذَٰلِكَ نَجْزِي الظَّالِمِينَ

qālū jazāʾuhu man wujida fī raḥlihi fa-huwa jazāʾuhu ka-dhālika najzī al-ẓālimīna

“They said: Its penalty is [the detention of] the person in whose baggage [the cup] is found; he himself is its penalty. Thus we recompense wrongdoers.”

Raḥlihi denotes the baggage of the person in whose belongings the cup is found.

This again fits traveling baggage and belongings.

The place where an object is found must be distinguished from a person’s responsibility for its being there.

Fī raḥlihi conveys only the spatial relationship: something is found in the person’s baggage.

The expression alone does not prove that the owner knowingly took the object or committed theft.

The story has already stated that the cup was placed in Joseph’s brother’s baggage.

Neither raḥl’s lexical meaning nor man wujida fī raḥlihi independently establishes a general rule for proving theft or criminal responsibility.

Those matters require examining the whole story and the legal arrangement expressed in it.

For this root study, the main point is that raḥl identifies the baggage and equipment belonging to a traveler.

5. Raḥl and wiʿāʾ: two words in one scene

Part of the following verse, Yūsuf 12:76, uses another word for the same search:

فَبَدَأَ بِأَوْعِيَتِهِمْ قَبْلَ وِعَاءِ أَخِيهِ ثُمَّ اسْتَخْرَجَهَا مِنْ وِعَاءِ أَخِيهِ

fa-badaʾa bi-awʿiyatihim qabla wiʿāʾi akhīhi thumma istakhrajahā min wiʿāʾi akhīhi

“He began with their containers and bundles before his brother’s container, then brought it out of his brother’s container.”

The preceding verses used raḥl and riḥāl; this one uses wiʿāʾ and awʿiyah.

Wiʿāʾ comes from W ʿ Y and, in physical usage, denotes a container or something that holds objects.

Raḥl and wiʿāʾ are therefore close here, but their core meanings differ.

Raḥl concerns travel, baggage, and the traveler’s equipment.

Wiʿāʾ emphasizes containment and holding contents.

A traveler’s bundle may be described from either perspective: as traveling baggage, raḥl; as something holding an object, wiʿāʾ.

This distinction helps clarify the text.

The changed word in verse 76 need not mean two entirely different objects, as though the cup had first been put in a camel saddle and then moved to another container.

The verses describe no such separate transfer.

The different names may reflect a different aspect of the same object, rather than a change of object.

Yūsuf 12:62, Yūsuf 12:70, and Yūsuf 12:75 concern the traveler’s baggage; Yūsuf 12:76 highlights the containers being searched.

This fits the text without inventing details about the bundles’ shape or how they were carried.

6. Comparison with related roots

R Ḥ L (raḥl) and S F R (safar)

The Qur’an uses safar for traveling or being on a journey.

For example, part of Al-Baqarah 2:185 states:

وَمَنْ كَانَ مَرِيضًا أَوْ عَلَىٰ سَفَرٍ فَعِدَّةٌ مِنْ أَيَّامٍ أُخَرَ

wa-man kāna marīḍan aw ʿalā safarin fa-ʿiddatun min ayyāmin ukhara

“Whoever is ill or traveling, then [the fast is] a number of other days.”

Safar here denotes the condition of traveling.

Riḥlah can also denote a journey and overlaps with it in this respect.

R Ḥ L additionally has an established usage concerning saddling and the traveler’s equipment.

Riḥlah may thus present a departure or journey as a movement or occasion of travel; raḥl denotes the belongings accompanying it.

No absolute rule should be made that safar always involves no baggage, while riḥlah always involves a camel and heavy loads.

Both concern travel and movement; their precise difference emerges from construction and context.

Study of S F R

R Ḥ L (irtiḥāl) and Ẓ ʿ N (ẓaʿn)

Ẓaʿn also concerns departing and moving from one place to another in Arabic.

Part of Al-Naḥl 16:80 states:

تَسْتَخِفُّونَهَا يَوْمَ ظَعْنِكُمْ وَيَوْمَ إِقَامَتِكُمْ

tastakhiffūnahā yawma ẓaʿnikum wa-yawma iqāmatikum

“You find them light and manageable on your day of departure and your day of residence.”

Ẓaʿn here stands opposite iqāmah.

The contrast concerns moving from a place and remaining in it.

Early Arabic irtiḥāl also concerns departing and leaving a dwelling, and in poetry often contrasts with ḥall, stopping or settling.

The two roots have related uses, but should not be equated completely.

R Ḥ L also includes names for travel equipment, baggage, and mounts. Ẓ ʿ N should be understood through its own independent usages.

R Ḥ L (riḥlah) and S Y R (sayr)

Sayr concerns movement and covering a route.

Part of Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:20 states:

قُلْ سِيرُوا فِي الْأَرْضِ فَانْظُرُوا

qul sīrū fī al-arḍi fa-unẓurū

“Say: Travel through the earth and observe.”

The command to move through the earth here is connected with observing signs.

Sayr can express movement and covering a route, while riḥlah names a journey or departure as an activity in its established usage.

It does not follow that sayr is always short and riḥlah always long.

Neither root encodes a fixed duration or distance.

R Ḥ L (raḥl) and M T ʿ (matāʿ)

Joseph’s story uses riḥālihim in Yūsuf 12:62 and matāʿahum in Yūsuf 12:65.

Matāʿ concerns goods, belongings, and things people use or carry.

Raḥl in this context emphasizes traveling baggage and equipment.

Something may thus be both matāʿ and part of traveling baggage, without the words having identical meanings.

In Yūsuf 12:65, opening the belongings leads to finding the returned goods. This agrees with riḥāl in Yūsuf 12:62, without requiring every matāʿ in Arabic to be called raḥl.

Study of M T ʿ

R Ḥ L (raḥl) and J H Z (jahāz)

Jahāz appears alongside raḥl in Yūsuf 12:70.

Jahhazahum bi-jahāzihim concerns preparing their belongings and needed supplies.

Raḥli akhīhi instead identifies Joseph’s brother’s baggage.

Jahāz thus tends to concern prepared equipment, while raḥl concerns the traveler’s accompanying baggage and equipment.

This is a tendency of usage, rather than an absolute boundary excluding overlap in every Arabic sentence.

7. Later developments and translation proposals for Forqan

Members of R Ḥ L have also developed broad uses in later Arabic.

Raḥḥāl and raḥḥālah describe someone who travels frequently or is experienced in travel.

Early marḥalah concerned a stopping place or the distance between two stops. Later it came to describe stages in time, science, education, and other processes.

These later meanings should not be imposed on riḥlah in Quraysh or raḥl in Yūsuf.

Expressions such as a journey of knowledge, a spiritual journey, and stages of growth occur in literary and philosophical prose, but do not directly define the root in these verses.

The following equivalents suit this family:

Raḥala: departed, set out, or traveled; transitively, fastened equipment to a mount or prepared it to depart.

Raḥl: traveling baggage, a traveler’s equipment, or camel riding equipment; in some non-Qur’anic uses, a dwelling or shelter.

Riḥāl: loads or traveling equipment; the plural of raḥl.

Riḥlah: a journey, departure, an occasion of travel, or the activity of traveling.

Raḥīl: departure, movement, or leaving a residence.

Irtiḥāl: departing, preparing to move, and leaving a dwelling.

Rāḥilah: a mount ready and suitable for travel.

Raḥḥāl: a frequent traveler.

Marḥalah: a stopping place, stage, or distance on a journey; in later usage, a stage of a process.

Qur’anic renderings should fit the individual verse, for example Quraysh 106:2:

رِحْلَةَ الشِّتَاءِ وَالصَّيْفِ

riḥlata al-shitāʾi wa-al-ṣayfi

“The journey of winter and summer.”

Historical destinations or the word “trade” need not be added to the basic translation. Part of Yūsuf 12:62 also states:

اجْعَلُوا بِضَاعَتَهُمْ فِي رِحَالِهِمْ

ijʿalū biḍāʿatahum fī riḥālihim

“Put their goods in their baggage.” Part of Yūsuf 12:70 states:

جَعَلَ السِّقَايَةَ فِي رَحْلِ أَخِيهِ

jaʿala al-siqāyata fī raḥli akhīhi

“He put the cup in his brother’s baggage.” Part of Yūsuf 12:75 states:

مَنْ وُجِدَ فِي رَحْلِهِ

man wujida fī raḥlihi

“The person in whose baggage [the cup] is found.”

In the three Yūsuf occurrences, “baggage” is natural and suitable: it retains the connection with travel equipment without unnecessarily specifying a kind of saddle, chest, or bag.

The lexical explanation can clarify that early raḥl also denoted riding equipment, accompanying baggage, and certain uses concerning a dwelling.

8. Conclusion

R Ḥ L concerns travel, departure, and the equipment carried when setting out.

Two principal branches appear in the Qur’an.

First, riḥlah means a journey or departure. In Quraysh it denotes the winter and summer journey. The verse specifies the seasons, without specifying exact destinations, the number of journeys, trade routes, or historical details. Those details should not become part of the word’s meaning or direct translation.

Second, raḥl means the traveler’s baggage and equipment. Yūsuf uses it three times for the baggage in which goods or the cup are placed or sought.

In early Arabic, raḥl also named camel riding equipment, with uses extending to traveling baggage and a dwelling.

Early poetic witnesses show established uses for saddling a mount, departing, and stopping.

These derivatives should not be reduced to one meaning. Riḥlah is the journey, while raḥl may be its accompanying equipment or baggage. Not every journey involves a camel, nor does every raḥl mean a house or place of residence.

Differences from safar, ẓaʿn, sayr, matāʿ, and jahāz should be understood through actual usage rather than absolute, unsupported boundaries.

Final definition: R Ḥ L concerns departure from a residence on a journey and the accompanying equipment and belongings. Riḥlah names the journey or departure. Raḥl denotes riding equipment, accompanying baggage or belongings, and in some usages a dwelling or shelter. The Qur’an uses riḥlah for Quraysh’s seasonal journeys and raḥl for Joseph’s brothers’ baggage. Destinations, purposes, mounts, contents, and the owner’s responsibility cannot be derived from the root alone; they must be understood from context.
