Radiance, intense heat, and brilliance arising from fire and shining light

In Arabic, the root w-h-j denotes radiance, heat emanating from fire or the sun, and intense brilliance. وَهَجَتِ النَّارُ means that a fire blazed or grew hot; وَهْج denotes its glow and heat; تَوَهَّجَ means to blaze or shine; and وَهَّاج describes something with powerful radiance.

Heat and light are closely related within this family, but they are not equally prominent in every use. Heat stands out when speaking of the glow of fire or the sun, whereas brilliance is central when describing sparkling jewels. The family is also recorded for the diffusion of fragrance.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as وَهَّاجًا in Al-Nabaʾ 78:13. It qualifies سِرَاجًا: an intensely radiant lamp, whose clear referent, in light of other Qur’anic verses, is the sun.

1. Meaning in early Arabic and transmitted evidence

In early Arabic usage, وَهَجَتِ النَّارُ describes a fire that has blazed up. وَهْج and وَهَج likewise concern the heat and glow of fire.

Lexical sources record وَهَجُ الشَّمْسِ for the sun’s heat, وَهَجُ النَّارِ for the heat of fire, and وَهَجَانُ الْجَمْرِ for the intense glow of embers.

These uses show that the root means more than illuminating one’s surroundings. Fire produces both light and heat, but this family particularly emphasizes its blazing condition and the intensity of its radiance or heat.

For example, someone may stand at a distance from a fire and feel its heat without direct contact with the flame. Arabic also uses wahj for this heat emanating from fire.

يَوْمٌ وَهِجٌ and يَوْمٌ وَهْجَانٌ describe a very hot day. لَيْلَةٌ وَهِجَةٌ and لَيْلَةٌ وَهْجَانَةٌ similarly describe a very hot night.

Heat is central in these expressions. A day or night need not blaze like firewood to receive this description.

One poetic fragment transmitted in lexical sources reads:

مُصْمَقِرُّ الْهَجِيرِ ذُو وَهَجَانِ

muṣmaqirru al-hajīri dhū wahajāni

Translation: “The midday heat is fierce and scorching, intensely hot.”

الْهَجِير concerns the intense heat of midday, and وَهَجَان emphasizes its severity.

This witness extends the root’s semantic range beyond blazing fire to intense atmospheric heat. However, the poet and date of composition are not reliably established in the account examined. It cannot therefore be presented as definite pre-Islamic evidence.

Brilliance in early poetry

A verse attributed to Abū Dhuʾayb al-Hudhalī reads:

كَأَنَّ ابْنَةَ السَّهْمِيِّ دُرَّةُ قَامِسٍ
لَهَا بَعْدَ تَقْطِيعِ النُّبُوحِ وَهِيجُ

ka-anna ibnata al-Sahmiyyi durratu qāmisin / lahā baʿda taqṭīʿi al-nubūḥi wahīju

Translation: “The daughter of al-Sahmī is like a diver’s pearl, shining after the dogs’ barking has ceased.”

The poet compares a woman’s beauty to the brilliance of a pearl.

وَهِيج here means the pearl’s sparkle. A pearl does not burn like fire or generate intense heat, but its surface shines in the light.

This usage matters because it shows how radiance can extend from fire and heat to the brilliance of something that is not burning.

Another transmitted version reads دُرَّةُ غَائِصٍ. The variation concerns the description of the diver or pearl and does not alter the use of وَهِيج for brilliance.

Abū Dhuʾayb was a mukhaḍram poet: one who lived through both the pre-Islamic period and the early Islamic years. The date of this verse is not independently established, however, so it cannot confidently be treated as predating the Qur’anic revelation.

The poem is useful evidence for usage near the period of revelation, but does not establish which meaning—heat or brilliance—developed first.

The diffusion of fragrance

Classical Arabic also records وَهَجُ الطِّيبِ for the diffusion of fragrance and تَوَهَّجَتْ رَائِحَةُ الطِّيبِ for a scent spreading.

This refers to fragrance spreading through the surroundings, not to perfume burning or producing heat.

One can see a figurative connection between spreading heat and diffusing fragrance: each originates in something and makes its effect felt around it.

Such an analogy does not establish the word’s history. We cannot claim that pre-Islamic Arabs necessarily understood every usage through one unified mental image.

Independent, datable evidence is also insufficiently clear to demonstrate that the fragrance sense was common before Islam. It is recorded in classical Arabic, but its date of emergence remains open.

A historical definition must therefore distinguish established classical meanings from the independent evidence available for dating each branch to the pre-Islamic period.

A defensible core of this family is blazing and intense heat associated with radiance, with extended uses for brilliance and the diffusion of an effect.

2. Forms and derivatives

The principal recorded Arabic forms include:

  • وَهَجَ: it blazed, became incandescent, or grew hot.
  • يَهِجُ and تَهِجُ: it burns or blazes, with the form appropriate to its subject.
  • وَهْج: blazing, radiance, and heat emanating from fire.
  • وَهَج: intense heat from fire or the sun; in some uses, brilliance.
  • وَهَجَان: intense or spreading heat or radiance.
  • وَهِيج: blazing or sparkling; in another use, the diffusion of fragrance.
  • وَهِج: describing a day or atmosphere of intense heat.
  • وَهْجَان: very hot or scorching.
  • وَهَّاج: intensely radiant, brilliant, or characterized by strong heat or radiance.
  • وَاهِج: blazing or radiant.
  • تَوَهَّجَ: it blazed, shone intensely, or grew hotter.
  • تَوَهُّج: the condition of blazing or radiance.
  • مُتَوَهِّج: blazing, radiant, or very brilliant.
  • وَهَّجَ: he made something blaze or intensified its radiance.
  • أَوْهَجَ: he kindled a fire or made it blaze.

Some sources distinguish the vocalization and verbal patterns of وَهَجَ and وَهِجَ. Their vowels and meanings should not be disregarded by treating the forms as identical.

Transitive forms such as وَهَّجَ and أَوْهَجَ are also recorded in Arabic, but do not occur in the Qur’an.

The sole Qur’anic derivative is وَهَّاجًا.

وَهَّاج follows the pattern فَعَّال, which can express the intensity or abundance of a quality.

Here, intense radiance is prominent.

The pattern alone does not establish that the object repeatedly flares up, switches on and off, or produces heat through any particular mechanism.

Morphology conveys the intensity of the description; the way that quality arises is a separate question.

3. Qur’anic use: سِرَاجًا وَهَّاجًا

Al-Nabaʾ 78:13

وَجَعَلْنَا سِرَاجًا وَهَّاجًا

wa-jaʿalnā sirājan wahhājā

Al-Nabaʾ 78:13

Translation: “And We made an intensely radiant lamp.”

This is the root’s only Qur’anic occurrence.

وَجَعَلْنَا speaks of making or bringing about a condition.

سِرَاجًا is an accusative noun functioning as the verb’s object.

وَهَّاجًا is likewise accusative, qualifying سِرَاجًا.

The verse therefore describes one object, not a lamp and a separate radiant body. Wahhāj qualifies that same lamp.

Sirāj names a lamp or source of illumination; wahhāj expresses the intensity of its radiance.

Translating only “lamp” loses an important description. Conversely, defining sirāj itself as intense heat obscures its distinction from wahhāj.

“An intensely radiant lamp” is a clear rendering. The lexical explanation should add that this radiance is closely associated with powerful shining and heat.

Is the sun intended?

Al-Nabaʾ 78:13 does not itself contain the word shams, “sun.”

Elsewhere, however, the Qur’an explicitly calls the sun a sirāj.

In Nūḥ 71:16, the moon is called light and the sun a lamp.

Al-Furqān 25:61 likewise mentions a lamp in the sky alongside a shining moon.

These usages identify the sun as the clear referent of sirāj in passages about the heavens.

The context of Al-Nabaʾ reinforces this understanding.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:6 concerns the earth; Al-Nabaʾ 78:7, mountains; Al-Nabaʾ 78:8, the creation of humans in pairs; Al-Nabaʾ 78:9, sleep; Al-Nabaʾ 78:10, night; and Al-Nabaʾ 78:11, day.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:12 then speaks of seven strong structures built above, followed in Al-Nabaʾ 78:13 by the intensely radiant lamp.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:14 speaks next of sending down water, while Al-Nabaʾ 78:15 and Al-Nabaʾ 78:16 describe grain, plants, and gardens growing.

This sequence places sirājan wahhājan within a description of creation and the conditions for human life on earth.

The sun illuminates the earth, contributes to its warmth, and plays an important role in natural cycles and plant growth.

This knowledge accords with the Qur’anic image. The verse does not, however, explain the scientific mechanism generating solar energy, the sun’s internal composition, or precise quantities of heat and radiation.

Identifying the sun as the referent comes from the context and the network of Qur’anic passages. Modern knowledge of the sun’s structure should not be presented as the lexical meaning of wahhāj.

4. Does وَهَّاج mean only light?

Wahhāj should not be reduced to “bright.”

Arabic nūr can denote light without intense heat being essential to the word itself.

Wahj, however, is closely associated with blazing, heat, and intense brilliance.

Moonlight, for instance, can illuminate the earth, but does not convey the heat people feel from sunlight in the same way.

Describing the sun as wahhāj therefore fits its heat and radiance.

Nevertheless, not every Arabic use of wahj is restricted to actual burning.

Abū Dhuʾayb’s pearl image shows that wahīj can describe the brilliance of something that is not ablaze.

Defining wahhāj necessarily as a substance burning in fire would thus be too restrictive.

Conversely, translating it merely as “luminous” loses some of its intensity and radiance.

In Al-Nabaʾ 78:13, “intensely radiant” preserves the strength of the shining and accords with the semantic range of heat and blazing.

An explanation can add that the lamp gives powerful light and heat.

5. وَهَّاجًا and ثَجَّاجًا: two descriptions in successive verses

The verse after sirājan wahhājan contains another word with a similar morphological pattern.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:14

وَأَنْزَلْنَا مِنَ الْمُعْصِرَاتِ مَاءً ثَجَّاجًا

wa-anzalnā mina al-muʿṣirāti māʾan thajjājā

Al-Nabaʾ 78:14

Translation: “And We sent down abundantly pouring water from the rain-bearing clouds.”

ثَجَّاجًا belongs to th-j-j and concerns the abundant pouring and flow of water.

Both وَهَّاجًا and ثَجَّاجًا follow فَعَّال; each emphasizes the intensity of its respective quality.

The objects being described, however, differ.

Wahhāj concerns the radiant lamp; thajjāj concerns the water being poured down.

One describes radiance and shining; the other, abundant rainfall.

Morphological pattern alone therefore does not determine a word’s meaning. Both patterns can indicate intensity, but the root and the object described determine what kind of intensity is meant.

Both verses also belong to the surrounding account of signs in creation.

The sun and rain both contribute to life on earth. Sunlight and warmth, alongside water and growing vegetation, form a broad picture of the conditions of life.

Their juxtaposition does not mean these expressions explain every detail of the water cycle, evaporation, cloud condensation, and rainfall.

Modern understanding can relate these phenomena, while the lexical meanings of wahj and thajj remain distinct.

6. Comparison with related roots

w-h-j and w-q-d: radiance and kindling

W-q-d concerns kindling fire, blazing up, and providing fuel for fire.

W-h-j also concerns flame and heat, but its principal usages emphasize intense radiance, heat, and brilliance.

أَوْقَدَ النَّارَ describes kindling a fire, whereas وَهَجُ النَّارِ can describe that fire’s heat and glow.

A fire may be kindled but give little heat. Calling it wahhāj instead directs attention to its powerful radiance.

In some Arabic usages, however, wahj and waqd are very close and are even used to explain each other.

An absolute boundary should therefore not be imposed between them.

The Qur’an uses w-q-d for material fire, fuel, kindling, and figurative fire imagery. W-h-j occurs only as the description of the radiant lamp in Al-Nabaʾ.

For a fuller examination of kindling, Forqan’s study of the root w-q-d examines its usages separately.

Study of w-q-d

w-h-j and s-r-j: radiance and the lamp

Sirāj names a lamp or source of illumination.

Wahhāj describes something with intense radiance.

The two occur together in Al-Nabaʾ 78:13.

Sirāj identifies something serving as a lamp, while wahhāj describes its quality.

Not every lamp is equally radiant: one may give weak light while another shines intensely.

Wahj is also recorded for intense daytime heat, so its meaning is not limited to the presence of an artificial lamp.

Forqan’s study of s-r-j is also relevant to the sun as a lamp and to other figurative uses of sirāj.

Study of s-r-j

w-h-j and ḍ-w-ʾ: radiance and illumination

Ḍiyāʾ concerns light and radiance.

In Yūnus 10:5, the sun is described as ضِيَاء and the moon as نُور.

In Al-Nabaʾ 78:13, that same sun is identified through the image of sirājan wahhājan.

The descriptions concern one phenomenon from different expressive perspectives.

Ḍiyāʾ emphasizes illumination; wahj is associated with radiance and intense heat and brilliance.

This does not mean that ḍiyāʾ never accompanies heat or that wahj always requires a visible flame.

Comparison must rest on actual usages, not an invented absolute separation between light and heat.

w-h-j and n-w-r: radiance and light

Nūr has a wide Qur’anic range, from perceptible light to guidance and the disclosure of truth.

The Qur’anic wahj describes the intense radiance of a heavenly lamp.

Nūḥ 71:16 calls the moon light and the sun a lamp; Al-Nabaʾ 78:13 qualifies this lamp as wahhāj.

This distinction does not establish that nūr always means reflected light. Elsewhere the Qur’an uses nūr for various concepts.

Nor should wahhāj automatically be treated as a scientific term for a body generating light internally.

Scientific knowledge of the source of sunlight and the reflection of moonlight comes from studying nature, not merely from the contrast between two words.

w-h-j and ḥ-r-r: heat and radiance

Ḥarr concerns warmth and heat.

Wahj can likewise denote intense heat, but remains closely associated with radiance and blazing.

A stone may grow hot in sunlight without emitting noticeable light of its own. Its warmth can be described through the family of ḥarr.

The sun, by contrast, is described as wahhāj: people experience both light and heat from it.

This distinction helps avoid reducing wahj to “heat” alone.

Nevertheless, يَوْمٌ وَهْجَانٌ shows that this family can also describe intense heat without emphasizing visible flame.

w-h-j and l-h-b: radiance and flame

Lahab concerns flame and tongues of fire.

Wahj concerns blazing, heat, and brilliance.

In Al-Masad 111:3, لَهَب describes flaming fire.

In Al-Nabaʾ 78:13, however, wahhāj qualifies an intensely radiant lamp without specifying the shape or size of flames.

It should therefore not be used to construct a definite image of wood-fire-like flames on the sun’s surface.

The verse expresses intense radiance; its physical character requires separate investigation.

w-h-j and b-h-j: brilliance and beauty

The two roots differ only in their first consonant.

B-h-j concerns beauty, flourishing freshness, and pleasing appearance.

In Al-Naml 27:60, ذَاتَ بَهْجَةٍ describes gardens.

W-h-j concerns blazing and radiance.

Something can be both beautiful and brilliant, but that overlap does not make the roots identical.

A garden’s beauty is expressed through b-h-j, and a lamp’s radiance through w-h-j. Features of one should not be transferred to the other without evidence.

7. Challenges of interpretation and translation

One difficulty is reducing وَهَّاج to “a bright lamp.”

Sirāj itself denotes the lamp. Rendering wahhāj merely as “bright” does not adequately convey the description’s intensity.

Conversely, the Persian equivalent of “a burning lamp” may suggest a lamp intended to burn people or destroy something.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:13 concerns signs of creation: a heavenly lamp alongside day, night, rain, and vegetation, rather than punitive fire or an instrument of destruction.

“Scorching” can clarify intense heat, but “intensely radiant” is more natural as the basic translation.

Another difficulty is turning the Qur’anic solar image into a specific scientific theory.

Modern science identifies internal nuclear processes as the source of solar radiation, unlike the burning of wood or lamp oil.

Al-Nabaʾ 78:13 uses the familiar image of a lamp for illumination and wahhāj for its intense radiance.

These words do not disclose the number of solar layers, surface and core temperatures, or types of nuclear reactions.

Conversely, discovering modern scientific mechanisms does not invalidate the luminous and thermal meanings of wahj.

The Qur’an uses natural language to identify a phenomenon people experience through light and heat. Modern science can deepen knowledge of that phenomenon, but does not replace the verse’s lexical meaning.

Nor does applying wahhāj to the sun imply that all stars are identical in size, temperature, or physical properties.

نَجْمٌ وَهَّاجٌ is recorded in Arabic for a brilliant, radiant star, but the Qur’anic occurrence describes only the lamp mentioned in this creation passage.

8. Suggested renderings for Forqan

For وَهَجَ in relation to fire, suitable renderings are “blazed,” “became incandescent,” or “burned intensely.”

For وَهْج, use “radiance,” “warm glow,” or “heat emanating from fire,” according to context.

For وَهَج describing daytime heat, “intense heat” or “scorching heat” is more natural.

For وَهَجَان, “intense heat” or “intense radiance” is appropriate.

For وَهِيج concerning jewels, “sparkle” or “brilliance” is suggested; for perfume, “the diffusion of fragrance” is more appropriate.

For تَوَهَّجَ, use “blazed,” “shone intensely,” or “became radiant.”

For مُتَوَهِّج, use “radiant,” “blazing,” or “brilliant,” according to what it describes.

For وَهَّاج, use “intensely radiant,” “brilliantly shining,” or, where appropriate, “possessing intense radiance and heat.”

For Al-Nabaʾ 78:13, the suggested rendering is:

“And We made an intensely radiant lamp.”

This preserves the distinction between the noun sirāj and its qualifier wahhāj, conveying the intensity of the radiance.

If the thermal aspect needs clarification, the explanation can read:

“This intensely radiant lamp is the sun, the source of the light and warmth the earth receives from its radiation.”

“A hot and radiant lamp” is also lexically defensible, but “an intensely radiant lamp” is preferable for a fluent direct translation. Heat can be explained in the reflection.

“A bright lamp” is too general, while “a lamp with nuclear reactions” substitutes scientific interpretation for the Qur’anic wording.

9. Conclusion

W-h-j concerns blazing, radiance, and intense heat. These meanings appear in descriptions of fire, embers, the sun, and hot weather.

Other recorded uses concern sparkling jewels and diffusing fragrance, showing that the root is not limited to physical burning.

There is insufficient independent evidence to determine which branch came first or precisely when each sense emerged. Classical meanings can be identified, but not every recorded usage can be assigned to the pre-Islamic period without evidence.

The only Qur’anic form is وَهَّاجًا in Al-Nabaʾ 78:13.

It qualifies سِرَاجًا, expressing the lamp’s intense radiance.

Nūḥ 71:16 explicitly calls the sun a sirāj. Together with the context of Al-Nabaʾ, this makes the sun the clear referent of the intensely radiant lamp.

The distinction remains essential: sirāj names the lamp, while wahhāj describes its intense radiance.

The description concerns sunlight and heat, but does not by itself specify the physical mechanism of radiation, a fuel type, or the sun’s internal structure.

The root also has no inherent moral valuation. Radiance can provide useful light and warmth, or under other circumstances cause harm. Its value and consequences are not determined by the word alone.

W-h-j denotes blazing and radiance associated with intense heat, with extended uses for brilliance and the diffusion of an effect. Qur’anic وَهَّاجًا describes a lamp of powerful radiance. The network of verses identifies this lamp as the sun, without turning the description into a theory of its scientific mechanism.
