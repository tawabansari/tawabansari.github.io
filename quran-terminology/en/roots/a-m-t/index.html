Qur’anic Root A M T

Unevenness, differences in elevation, and departure from a level surface: from raised ground to estimating distance and weakness

Qur’anic and lexical forms

أَمْتًا
(amtan)
Masculine singular indefinite accusative noun, coordinated with ʿiwajan; sole Qur’anic occurrence, Ṭā Hā 20:107

أَمْت
(amt)
Lexical base noun: unevenness, with other branches of slackness, deficiency, or doubt

إِمَات / أُمُوت
(imāt / umūt)
Reported lexical plurals; absent from the Qur’an

أَمَتَ يَأْمِتُ
(amata yaʾmitu)
Lexical Form I verb: estimating or measuring; not Qur’anic

أَمَّتَ
(ammata)
Lexical Form II verb associated with estimation; not Qur’anic

مَأْمُوت
(maʾmūt)
Lexical passive participle: estimated, determined, or in another transmission known; not Qur’anic

Arabic A M T has several uses. In its best-known spatial use, أَمْت, amt, denotes uneven terrain: a small rise, low mound, or depression interrupting a level surface. Other uses concern weakness, deficiency, variation in a thing’s condition, and even measuring or estimating.

The Qur’an uses the root only once, in Ṭā Hā 20:107, with وَلَا أَمْتًا. Following the transformation of the mountains on the Day of Resurrection, the verse describes ground leveled after their removal, with neither crookedness nor differences in elevation visible.

Amt does not name raised ground alone. Early sources record it for both elevations and depressions, or differences in level. Restricting it to “a hill” or “a hollow” in every use is therefore inaccurate.

A separate branch concerns estimating and approximate measurement. It should not be transferred to Ṭā Hā without evidence. There, amt accompanies ʿiwaj in a description of level ground; unevenness and differences in elevation have more direct support.

1. Qur’anic form and semantic core

A M T occurs only once in the Qur’an:

أَمْتًا — Ṭā Hā 20:107

This is an indefinite accusative singular noun, coordinated with عِوَجًا in لَا تَرَىٰ فِيهَا عِوَجًا وَلَا أَمْتًا.

Arabic amt denotes unevenness, an elevation, depression, or difference in level. The plurals إِمَات and أُمُوت are also recorded.

The same family has أَمَتَ يَأْمِتُ for measuring and estimating. أَمَّتَ has a similar recorded meaning, while مَأْمُوت can describe something whose quantity, distance, or time has been estimated or determined.

Arabic dictionaries also recognize amt for weakness, deficiency, and uncertainty.

None of these other verbal forms or derivatives occurs in the Qur’an. Only amtan appears, describing the ground.

The following core can be identified for the Qur’anic meaning:

Unevenness or a difference in the level of a surface, especially ground: an elevation, depression, or local irregularity interrupting its levelness.

This defines the root’s spatial use, rather than every lexical branch.

Estimating distance or uncertainty elsewhere does not make Qur’anic amt mean measurement or doubt. The meanings are recorded in Arabic, but Ṭā Hā 20:107 concerns the ground’s surface.

2. Amt in early Arabic: elevations and depressions

Amt: uneven terrain and differences in level

Early Arabic used amt for ground differing in level from its surroundings.

Some lexical explanations identify raised or elevated ground.

Amt can be a small rise in otherwise fairly level terrain, such as a low mound or raised patch of earth.

Other explanations identify a depression between two elevations.

Where two rises lie beside one another with lower ground between them, that depression is also called amt in some Arabic uses.

A broader definition likewise records a difference in level, where one part is higher and another lower.

Amt therefore does not name only one direction of an elevation difference. The issue is unevenness and local differences in level.

“Uneven terrain” is consequently more comprehensive for the Qur’anic verse than either “a hill” or “a hollow.”

Nabk and nibāk: small mounds

Early explanations also use نَبْك and its plural نِبَاك to explain amt.

Nabk denotes a small hill or raised patch of ground.

It can stand within otherwise fairly level terrain, rising above its surroundings.

Amt is therefore not confined to mountains or major elevations. Even a small rise can fall within its scope.

This matters for Ṭā Hā 20:107.

The preceding account describes removing mountains, but eliminating major mountains alone could still leave small hills and irregularities.

Amt shows that the verse’s description extends beyond the absence of large mountains.

Wahdah: a depression between elevations

Another Arabic use applies amt to a hollow or depression between raised areas.

وَهْدَة, wahdah, denotes low-lying ground or a hollow.

Where ground between two elevations lies lower, the surface is no longer uniform.

Both a small mound and a hollow between mounds can therefore fall within amt’s semantic field.

These uses are not necessarily contradictory. Both involve differences in level relative to the surrounding ground.

This does not establish that early Arabs necessarily derived every A M T use from one geometrical conception.

The witnesses establish uses for elevations, depressions, and unevenness; their historical sequence is less clear.

3. Slackness, deficiency, and irregularity in other things

Amt’s usage is not confined to the ground.

Arabic also records the noun for slackness, deficiency, or a lack of uniformity in other objects.

Amt in a water-skin

One early use concerns slackness and unevenness in a water-skin.

If the skin is not fully filled or firmly distended, one part can remain sunken and another raised.

Its surface is then neither uniformly stretched nor even.

Conversely, when it is filled so that no slackness or depression remains, its filling can be described as having no amt.

This relates unevenness to slackness and incomplete distension of a surface.

It does not make Qur’anic amt a name for a water-skin or water container.

Ṭā Hā concerns the ground. The water-skin example only shows that Arabic could also use the word for unevenness and slackness in other surfaces.

سَيْرٌ لَا أَمْتَ فِيهِ: movement without weakness

Arabic records سِرْنَا سَيْرًا لَا أَمْتَ فِيهِ for travelling without slackening or weakness.

Amt here denotes a deficiency or weakness that can reduce the continuity and strength of movement.

A rajaz line commonly attributed to Al-ʿAjjāj reads:

مَا فِي انْطِلَاقِ رَكْبِهِ مِنْ أَمْتِ

mā fī inṭilāqi rakbihi min amti

“There is no slackening or weakness in his caravan’s advance.”

Amt here does not directly describe uneven terrain; the subject is the caravan’s movement.

The expression shows that the word could also describe weakness and slackening in carrying out an action.

Slackness in an object, an uneven surface, and weakness in movement can be conceptually related: each departs from an expected uniformity or firmness.

This similarity nevertheless does not establish the historical origin of every use.

Nor should amtan in Ṭā Hā 20:107 be directly translated as “fatigue” or “weakness of movement.”

The verse concerns the ground, rather than a caravan or human ability to move.

Sources attribute this witness to Al-ʿAjjāj, while another transmission assigns it to Ruʾbah. Wordings such as مَا فِي انْجِذَابِ سَيْرِهِ مِنْ أَمْتِ also occur. Given this attributional variation, it should not be presented as a securely dated pre-Islamic poem. The poetic witnesses examined did not establish a secure pre-revelation date for this usage.

Amt as a defect or deficiency

Some Arabic uses record amt for a defect in a mouth, garment, or stone.

The issue may be a physical irregularity or deficiency in an object’s condition.

Arabic also transmits أَمْتٌ فِي الْحَجَرِ لَا فِيكَ. Literally it places irregularity or damage in the stone rather than the addressee. An explanation of the proverb takes it as wishing the person to endure even after stones decay. It therefore does more than curse a stone or report its defect.

This shows how the word can approach defect and deficiency beyond material unevenness.

“Defect” nevertheless has a wider meaning than uneven terrain.

Something can be defective without a raised or sunken surface.

Translating Qur’anic amt simply as “a defect” would therefore remove the clear image of level ground.

4. Measuring and estimating: أَمَتَ الشَّيْءَ

Arabic records another meaning for amata: measuring or estimating a quantity or distance.

أَمَتَ الشَّيْءَ can mean estimating a thing’s quantity or measuring its extent.

أَمَتَ الْقَوْمَ is likewise recorded for estimating a group’s number or size.

Uneven terrain is not the subject here. Someone tries to estimate a size or amount not directly and precisely available to them.

Maʾmūt: something with an estimated measure or distance

مَأْمُوت is one of the forms recorded in this branch.

It can describe something whose size, amount, or distance has been estimated. Another transmission records شَيْءٌ مَأْمُوتٌ for something known or recognized.

أَجَلٌ مَأْمُوتٌ denotes a fixed or appointed term. Maʾmūt is explained here with mawqūt; it does not necessarily mean an approximate or uncertain deadline.

This use concerns determining a quantity or appointed time, rather than uneven terrain.

Ruʾbah’s witness: estimating the distance to water in the desert

Ruʾbah ibn Al-ʿAjjāj says in his rajaz:

فِي بَلْدَةٍ يَعْيَا بِهَا الْخِرِّيتُ
رَأْيُ الْأَدِلَّاءِ بِهَا شَتِيتُ
أَيْهَاتَ مِنْهَا مَاؤُهَا الْمَأْمُوتُ

fī baldatin yaʿyā bihā al-khirrītu / raʾyu al-adillāʾi bihā shatītu / ayhāta minhā māʾuhā al-maʾmūtu

“In a land where even the skilled guide is confounded and the guides’ opinions are scattered: how far away is its water, whose distance they estimate!”

Al-khirrīt denotes a skilled, experienced guide.

Al-adillāʾ are route guides who judge paths and distances.

Al-maʾmūt in this wording concerns water whose distance has been estimated. Its remoteness comes from ayhāta rather than from maʾmūt itself.

The poet describes difficult country where even experienced guides disagree about routes and distances.

Maʾmūt here therefore does not mean spoiled or shallow water. It concerns estimating the distance to water.

This establishes an Arabic A M T branch concerning estimation and measurement.

It should not be historically derived from uneven terrain without evidence.

Uneven country may make estimating distance harder, but that possible connection does not prove that estimation developed from a hill or hollow sense.

Ruʾbah belongs to the Umayyad and early Abbasid periods. The poem is reported from his collection as praising Maslamah ibn ʿAbd Al-Malik. The last line also begins with هَيْهَاتَ in place of أَيْهَاتَ in another transmission. It is consequently post-revelation evidence, rather than certain evidence of pre-Islamic usage.

Amt and uncertainty

Some lexical accounts also record amt for doubt and uncertainty.

لَيْسَ فِيهِ أَمْتٌ can, in an appropriate context, mean absence of doubt or deficiency.

Some lexicographers connect this use with estimation, since an estimate can involve a degree of uncertainty.

That explanation can be examined, but is not a proven historical law.

Nor does every estimate necessarily involve doubt or indecision.

Amt meaning doubt elsewhere does not itself make that the meaning in Ṭā Hā 20:107.

That verse concerns the ground and its level surface, rather than human certainty or doubt.

The sense should therefore be recorded in the word’s history without being transferred into the Qur’anic translation.

5. لَا تَرَىٰ فِيهَا عِوَجًا وَلَا أَمْتًا: ground without unevenness

The root’s sole Qur’anic occurrence appears in Ṭā Hā:

لَّا تَرَىٰ فِيهَا عِوَجًا وَلَا أَمْتًا

lā tarā fīhā ʿiwajan wa-lā amtā

“You see in it neither crookedness nor any difference in elevation.”

Ṭā Hā 20:107

The short verse forms part of a description of transformed ground and mountains in the resurrection scene.

Understanding amtan requires examining the two preceding verses.

The mountains’ fate on the Day of Resurrection

Ṭā Hā 20:105 states:

وَيَسْأَلُونَكَ عَنِ الْجِبَالِ فَقُلْ يَنْسِفُهَا رَبِّي نَسْفًا

wa-yasʾalūnaka ʿani al-jibāli fa-qul yansifuhā rabbī nasfā

“They ask you about the mountains. Say: My Lord will uproot and scatter them completely.”

Ṭā Hā 20:105

The question concerns the mountains.

The Qur’an answers that the Lord يَنْسِفُهَا نَسْفًا.

Nasf belongs to N S F and here concerns uprooting, removing, and scattering the mountains.

The verse therefore describes a major transformation of the ground’s form, rather than the mountains remaining as they are.

The next verse states:

فَيَذَرُهَا قَاعًا صَفْصَفًا

fa-yadharuhā qāʿan ṣafṣafā

“Then He leaves it an open, level plain.”

Ṭā Hā 20:106

Qāʿ here denotes open, level ground.

Ṣafṣaf likewise denotes a smooth, even expanse whose elevations have been removed.

The words contribute to one image, emphasizing level ground.

Ṭā Hā 20:107 then makes that image more precise by negating ʿiwaj and amt.

What does the pronoun in fīhā refer to?

Hā in لَا تَرَىٰ فِيهَا relates to the ground leveled after the mountains’ removal.

The relationship between the pronouns has several syntactic analyses.

One takes the pronoun in verse 106 as referring to the earth, understood from the context although not explicitly named there.

Another relates it to the site or place of the mountains that have been removed.

Both converge on a similar final image: the surface remaining after the mountains’ removal is level and free from irregularities.

Fīhā in verse 107 identifies this described surface or ground.

The verse consequently concerns absence of unevenness in the ground, rather than doubt in a human heart or deficiency in a ruling.

Lā tarā: negating what is seen in the ground

Tarā belongs to R A Y and here concerns seeing.

Lā tarā states that no crookedness or unevenness is seen in the described ground.

The verb does not make amt merely an optical error or illusion.

The preceding verses describe a real transformation of the mountains and leveling of the ground. The absence of unevenness continues that description.

Conversely, the verse does not explain scientific surveying methods, instruments, or a numerical amount of elevation difference.

The Qur’an here presents a clear image of uniform ground without differences in elevation.

Modern geometrical details or measuring technologies should not be imposed on the word to demonstrate its precision.

6. ʿIwaj and amt in one verse

Ṭā Hā 20:107 places two related words in one negative construction:

ʿIwajan and amtan.

Their juxtaposition matters for understanding the root.

Translating both merely as “unevenness” would conceal their possible distinction from the reader.

Mentioning them separately nevertheless does not establish that they never overlap.

ʿIwaj: crookedness and departure from a straight course

ʿIwaj belongs to ʿ W J.

The root concerns crookedness, bending, and departure from a straight or level condition.

Arabic records distinctions between ʿiwaj with kasrah and ʿawaj with fatḥah. One familiar explanation associates the former more with abstract crookedness and the latter more with crooked material objects.

This division is not an exceptionless law of Arabic usage.

In Ṭā Hā 20:107, the Qur’an itself uses ʿiwajan for the ground.

Its kasrah therefore cannot establish that the verse concerns only ethical or abstract crookedness.

Crookedness and irregularity of the ground’s surface are clear in this context.

Amt: elevations, depressions, and local differences in level

Attested amt can denote a small hill, rise, or hollow between elevations.

Compared with ʿiwaj, its aspect of differing elevations and surface levels is therefore more apparent.

The distinction can be understood as follows:

ʿIwaj emphasizes crookedness and departure from levelness.

Amt highlights rises, depressions, or local differences in surface level.

This is a tendency, rather than an absolute boundary.

Some early explanations use ʿiwaj and amt to explain each other. Others assign one to a depression and the other to an elevation.

Some lexicographers also apply amt to both.

It is therefore more precise to place the words within a shared field of unevenness and explain each contribution through context.

Is amt only a small elevation?

Some earlier explanations apply amt even to a slight rise.

The word therefore also encompasses small irregularities.

It should not nevertheless be confined to a fixed size.

Lexical sources give definitions such as elevated ground, small hill, hollow, and difference in level.

Smallness can characterize some instances, but does not establish a numerical measure or fixed limit for every use.

After the mountains’ removal in Ṭā Hā 20:107, negating amt emphasizes the absence of remaining irregularities, whether rises or depressions.

Why negate both?

Saying that the ground has no ʿiwaj would negate crookedness and departure from levelness.

Adding wa-lā amtan also highlights differences in elevation and local unevenness.

The resulting image has neither winding irregularities nor any visible hill, hollow, or uneven patch.

This agrees with qāʿan ṣafṣafan in the preceding verse.

Explaining the two words should nevertheless not invent a distinction such as ʿiwaj meaning only large irregularities and amt only small ones.

Such a precise division has not been established across Arabic witnesses.

7. ʿIwaj in the next verse: level ground and following the caller

After describing the ground, Ṭā Hā 20:108 states:

يَوْمَئِذٍ يَتَّبِعُونَ الدَّاعِيَ لَا عِوَجَ لَهُ وَخَشَعَتِ الْأَصْوَاتُ لِلرَّحْمَٰنِ فَلَا تَسْمَعُ إِلَّا هَمْسًا

yawmaʾidhin yattabiʿūna al-dāʿiya lā ʿiwaja lahu wa-khashaʿati al-aṣwātu li-l-raḥmāni fa-lā tasmaʿu illā hamsā

“On that day they follow the caller without deviating in following him. Voices fall humbled and quiet before the Entirely Merciful, and you hear only a faint sound.”

Ṭā Hā 20:108

ʿIwaj recurs here, while amt does not.

This difference helps identify the two roots’ roles.

Ṭā Hā 20:107 uses both words to describe the ground.

Ṭā Hā 20:108 instead uses ʿiwaj in following the caller.

ʿ W J can therefore describe both a material condition and a course of following.

Amt’s Qur’anic use remains confined to uneven ground.

This does not mean that Arabic amt has no nonmaterial uses: weakness, deficiency, and doubt show otherwise.

The lexical range of a root must simply remain distinct from its actual Qur’anic use.

Nor does ʿiwaj recurring in successive verses make uneven ground and deviating while following one phenomenon.

The Qur’an uses a root in two settings, with each meaning established by its object and context.

8. A M T and related roots

A M T (amt) and ʿ W J (ʿiwaj)

Study of ʿ W J

ʿIwaj concerns crookedness and deviation.

Amt, where the ground is concerned, denotes unevenness, rises, and depressions.

Their juxtaposition in Ṭā Hā 20:107 reinforces the image of entirely level ground.

They nevertheless do not have wholly separate semantic boundaries: lexical sources sometimes explain amt through ʿiwaj itself.

Their distinction should therefore be treated as a tendency.

A M T (amt) and N SH Z (nashz)

Study of N SH Z

Arabic nashz concerns raised ground above its surroundings.

Amt can likewise express a rise in the ground.

Its recorded range also includes depressions and irregular surface levels, however.

A nashz can therefore exemplify spatial amt without exhausting its meaning.

Human uses of N SH Z also develop another field that should not be transferred to A M T without evidence.

A M T (amt) and W H D (wahdah)

Wahdah concerns a hollow or low-lying ground.

Some Arabic uses likewise apply amt to a depression between two elevations.

The words are close in that instance.

Amt is not confined to a hollow, however: rises and differences in level are also part of its range.

“A hollow” alone therefore does not fully translate amt in Ṭā Hā 20:107.

A M T (amt) and S W Y (istawā)

Study of S W Y

In material usage, istiwāʾ can concern becoming even or level.

Amt describes a condition incompatible with a completely level surface.

There is therefore a clear opposition between irregularities and levelness.

This does not make amt the opposite of every use of istiwāʾ.

Qur’anic S W Y has a wider range, also concerning proportion, proper formation, and other conditions.

The comparison in Ṭā Hā concerns the ground’s surface.

A M T (amt) and Ṣ F Ṣ F (ṣafṣaf)

Study of Ṣ F Ṣ F

Ṣafṣaf in Ṭā Hā 20:106 concerns smooth, even ground.

Amt in the next verse denotes unevenness and differences in level.

They contrast within the same landscape image.

The Qur’anic morphological index places ṣafṣaf under the quadriliteral Ṣ F Ṣ F, rather than A M T or directly under triliteral Ṣ F F. Its similarity to the family of rows and levelness does not eliminate this morphological distinction.

Ṣafṣaf describes level ground; amt names the unevenness whose presence is denied.

A M T (amata) and Q D R (qaddara)

Study of Q D R

In the measurement branch, amata concerns estimating quantity or distance.

Qaddara can also denote measuring or determining an amount.

Qur’anic Q D R nevertheless has a wider range involving ability, measure, determination, and other matters.

The verbs’ proximity in estimation does not justify transferring all Q D R meanings to A M T.

Nor does the A M T measurement branch occur in the Qur’an.

A M T (amt) and KH R Ṣ (kharṣ)

Study of KH R Ṣ

Arabic kharṣ also concerns estimation; its Qur’anic uses can relate to conjecture and unsupported speech.

Lexical amata likewise concerns approximate measurement.

Overlap in estimation nevertheless does not make the roots identical.

Nor is every estimate necessarily a lie or baseless conjecture.

Recording doubt for some amt uses should therefore not equate every estimate with falsehood.

9. Does Ṭā Hā 20:107 describe the earth’s present form?

The verse describes a condition following the mountains’ removal.

لَا تَرَىٰ فِيهَا عِوَجًا وَلَا أَمْتًا should therefore not be detached from the preceding verses and treated as a description of the earth’s present form.

The earth as known today has mountains, valleys, hills, and many differences in surface elevation.

Ṭā Hā 20:105 instead describes the mountains’ transformation. Ṭā Hā 20:106 presents the result as level ground, and Ṭā Hā 20:107 describes no crookedness or unevenness in that condition.

The subject is therefore the earth’s transformation in the resurrection scene.

These verses should also not be taken as explaining every scientific detail of the earth’s structure after resurrection.

The verse describes geographical features and the resulting surface in intelligible language, without separately specifying the earth’s material, dimensions, or physical mechanism of mountain transformation.

This caution conversely does not turn the entire description into a metaphor about human ethics or belief.

The question concerns mountains, and the answer concerns removing them and leveling the ground.

Amt’s spatial and material meaning must therefore remain intact.

A reader may also understand the present world’s impermanence and The God’s power to transform creation. That contextual inference should not replace the root’s lexical meaning.

10. Translation proposals for Forqan

For Qur’anic amt, the basic English equivalent is “difference in elevation.”

“Unevenness” also naturally conveys the word’s breadth.

“Rises and depressions” can make its distinction from ʿiwaj more explicit.

“A hill” is too narrow, since Arabic also records the word for a hollow.

“A depression” alone likewise omits elevations and small hills.

“Defect,” “weakness,” and “doubt” are other recorded family meanings, but do not fit Ṭā Hā 20:107.

Proposed translation of Ṭā Hā 20:107

For the expression:

لَا تَرَىٰ فِيهَا عِوَجًا وَلَا أَمْتًا

The proposed translation is:

“You see in it neither crookedness nor any difference in elevation.”

This keeps ʿiwaj and amt distinct and accords with the level ground in the preceding verse.

A more explicit material rendering is:

“You see in it no crookedness and no rise or depression.”

Both renderings are defensible.

The first reads more naturally as continuous prose; the second spells out amt’s spatial sense more explicitly.

For Forqan’s basic translation, the first is preferable: it preserves both meanings without turning the Qur’an’s short sentence into a lengthy explanation.

Non-Qur’anic forms

أَمَتَ الشَّيْءَ can be rendered “he estimated its size” or “he assessed its quantity.”

Maʾmūt, where distance is concerned, can be translated “estimated.”

سَيْرٌ لَا أَمْتَ فِيهِ can be rendered “movement without slackening.”

In a negative expression about defect or deficiency, “flawless” or “without deficiency” can convey the intended meaning.

These equivalents should nevertheless not replace amtan in Ṭā Hā 20:107.

11. Conclusion

Arabic A M T has several semantic branches.

Spatial amt concerns unevenness and differences in level, such as a small hill, rise, or depression between two elevations.

Another branch uses the word for slackness, weakness, or deficiency in objects or movement.

In the measurement branch, amata concerns estimating or determining an amount, distance, or appointed time.

Some other uses also record defect and doubt.

The branches can be conceptually close without a definite shared historical origin. Distance estimation especially cannot be derived solely from uneven terrain, nor doubt solely from assessing an amount.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an.

In Ṭā Hā 20:107, amtan accompanies ʿiwajan in describing ground leveled after the mountains’ removal.

ʿIwaj emphasizes crookedness and departure from levelness; amt foregrounds rises, depressions, and irregular surface levels.

The words overlap semantically, but their combination makes the image of level ground clearer.

The verse also shows that the absence of large mountains does not exhaust the description. The surface is described so that even its rises and depressions are not seen.

This comes from Ṭā Hā 20:105, Ṭā Hā 20:106, and Ṭā Hā 20:107 together, rather than amt itself meaning resurrection or the mountains’ destruction.

Ṭā Hā 20:108 also repeats ʿiwaj in following the caller without repeating amt. The two roots therefore have different ranges despite their proximity in describing the ground.

Forqan’s final definition of A M T:

“In the spatial branch, unevenness and differences in level, such as an elevation, depression, or local irregularity in the ground. Qur’anic amt appears beside ʿiwaj, describing the absence of any differences in elevation in ground leveled after the mountains’ removal. Other Arabic uses of this family concern weakness, deficiency, estimating quantities and distances, and sometimes doubt. These meanings should not be transferred to the Qur’anic use without evidence.”
