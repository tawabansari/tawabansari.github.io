Heaping up, placing one part upon another, and forming accumulated masses

Brief meaning: Gathering things and placing one part upon another to form a heap or accumulated collection. Qur’anic uses concern piled clouds and gathering together what shares a common fate.

1. Introduction and Qur’anic forms

Arabic R K M concerns things gathering and lying upon one another. Its family describes sand, earth, clouds, or other objects forming an accumulated mass.

رَكَمَ means gathering and heaping up. رُكام names a mass formed by accumulation; مَرْكُوم describes something heaped up.

تَرَاكَمَ also concerns gathering and accumulation. Its related noun is familiar in modern Persian in expressions concerning population density, cloud accumulation, and accumulated materials.

The Qur’anic root should not, however, be equated with every modern technical use of accumulation or density.

In scientific usage, density can express mass per unit volume. Economic and social language also speaks of concentrated capital, population growth, and accumulated problems. Such uses relate to accumulation generally but are not invariant meanings of the Qur’anic word.

The root occurs three times in the Qur’an: once for heaping up الْخَبِيث and twice concerning accumulated clouds.

Forms examined and Qur’anic derivatives

The three occurrences appear in three verses and three forms:

  • فَيَرْكُمَهُ: A basic-form imperfect verb in the subjunctive, with fa and an object pronoun; Al-Anfāl 8:37.
  • رُكَامًا: An accusative noun, an accumulated mass; Al-Nūr 24:43.
  • مَرْكُومٌ: A basic-form passive participle, heaped up; Al-Ṭūr 52:44.

Other recorded forms include رَكَمَ, يَرْكُمُ, رَكْم, رُكَام, رَكَم, رُكْمَة, رَاكِم, مَرْكُوم, تَرَاكَمَ, تَرَاكُم, مُتَرَاكِم, ارْتَكَمَ, and مُرْتَكِم.

These help establish the lexical family, but only the three previously identified forms occur in the Qur’an.

2. The semantic core and early Arabic usage

Gathering and piling one thing upon another

An early lexical account identifies one semantic core for R K M:

الرَّاءُ وَالْكَافُ وَالْمِيمُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى تَجَمُّعِ الشَّيْءِ

al-rāʾu wa-al-kāfu wa-al-mīmu aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā tajammuʿi al-shayʾi

Translation: “Rāʾ, kāf, and mīm constitute one root meaning that indicates something gathering together.”

It then explains رَكَمَ الشيءَ through placing one part of something upon another.

Another early explanation defines rakm as gathering things upon one another, as with sand and clouds.

This means more than several things being present in one place.

Many objects can stand side by side without forming a heap.

In the root’s clear uses, gathering involves superposition: the parts form an accumulated whole.

The root does not itself establish whether the parts follow an exact geometric arrangement or gather irregularly.

Rakm can describe orderly or irregular accumulation; the arrangement must be determined from context.

Rukām: the mass resulting from accumulation

رُكام describes what results from parts being gathered upon one another.

It can describe sand, earth, clouds, and other materials.

رُكام الرَّمل means accumulated sand; رُكام السَّحاب means clouds gathered upon one another.

The materials differ, but accumulation is shared.

Sand consists of solid grains, while clouds have a different structure. A shared word does not establish identical materials or formation processes.

The root conveys piling up; it does not transfer sand’s physical properties to clouds or those of clouds to every other mass.

Rukmah: accumulated earth and mud

رُكْمَة is recorded for gathered earth or mud.

It concerns accumulated ground materials.

When earth or mud gathers in one place and forms a mass, this family can describe it.

The word does not specify whether wind, water, human activity, or another process caused it.

The cause of gathering is distinct from the root meaning.

Rukmah need not mean ruins, rubble, or wreckage. An earth mound may form naturally or be built for a purpose.

تَراكَمَ and ارْتَكَمَ: accumulating

تَرَاكَمَ الشيءُ and ارْتَكَمَ الشيءُ describe something whose parts gather upon one another.

They emphasize the occurrence of accumulation.

For example, تَرَاكَمَ السَّحابُ means clouds gathered upon one another.

رَكَمَ can express the act of gathering and heaping, while تراكم describes accumulation occurring. Its form alone does not establish that it continues for a specified duration.

Form VI does not by itself imply that all parts independently intend or reciprocally perform the action.

Clouds, sand, and earth involve gathering parts, not independently choosing components.

Markūm: something heaped up

مَرْكُوم is the passive participle of رَكَمَ.

It describes something subjected to heaping or existing in an accumulated state.

سَحابٌ مَرْكُومٌ, for example, is cloud whose parts gather upon one another.

The participle alone does not identify who or what caused the accumulation.

People might gather objects in one situation; wind or another natural process might cause accumulation in another.

No particular agent or mechanism should therefore be extracted from markūm without evidence.

مُرْتَكَم الطريق: a well-traveled route

مُرْتَكَمُ الطَّرِيقِ is also recorded for the main track of a road.

An early lexical explanation connects this with travelers gathering and passing along it in succession.

The road is not necessarily a heap of earth or stone: it is a place where people gather and pass.

The field can thus extend from accumulated material objects to people gathering along a route.

This explanation should not define every derivative.

Markūm in Al-Ṭūr 52:44 concerns cloud, while yarkumahu in Al-Anfāl 8:37 concerns piling up a collection in a scene of final destiny. Neither means road.

3. Early poetry and the image of accumulation

Accumulated sand in Imruʾ al-Qays’s poetry

One transmitted version of a celebrated verse by the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays reads:

فَلَمَّا أَجَزْنَا سَاحَةَ الْحَيِّ وَانْتَحَى
بِنَا بَطْنُ حِقْفٍ ذِي رُكَامٍ عَقَنْقَلِ

fa-lammā ajaznā sāḥata al-ḥayyi wa-intaḥā / binā baṭnu ḥiqfin dhī rukāmin ʿaqanqali

Translation: “When we had passed beyond the tribal settlement’s open space, and the hollow of a curved dune with tangled heaps of sand lay across our way…”

In this version, ذِي رُكَامٍ concerns sand piled up.

Ḥiqf concerns a curved dune or sand formation; ʿaqanqal describes interwoven or closely entangled parts.

The scene depicts crossing terrain in which sand has accumulated in heaps.

The verse has differing transmitted readings.

A more familiar version of the Muʿallaqah reads:

بِنَا بَطْنُ خَبْتٍ ذِي قِفَافٍ عَقَنْقَلِ

binā baṭnu khabtin dhī qifāfin ʿaqanqali

This reading does not contain rukām. The phrase describes a low-lying tract with interlocking rises of ground.

بطن حقف ذي ركام عقنقل is recorded in early commentaries as an alternative. It cannot be presented without qualification as the sole certain original wording.

The example matters because rukām for accumulated sand appears in an early transmission of pre-Islamic poetry.

The variant readings nevertheless prevent certainty about the exact age of this wording or the historical sequence of the root’s meanings.

Rukām and murtakam in Ruʾbah ibn al-ʿAjjāj

Poetry by Ruʾbah ibn al-ʿAjjāj contains:

لَوْ كَانَ مِنْ دُونِ رُكَامِ الْمُرْتَكَمْ
وَأَرْمُلِ الدَّهْنَا وَصَمَّانِ الْوَجَمْ

law kāna min dūni rukāmi al-murtakam / wa-armuli al-dahnā wa-ṣammāni al-wajam

Approximate translation: “If there lay between us the accumulated heaps, the sands of al-Dahnāʾ, and the rocky elevations of al-Ṣammān…” The excerpt names intervening terrain; precise direction should not be fixed from it alone. The transmitted vowel in أَرْمُلِ has also been corrected.

Rukām and murtakam appear together here.

Both belong to the field of gathering and accumulation.

The pairing strengthens the image of masses whose parts lie upon one another, appearing as a prominent or large obstacle.

Ruʾbah belonged to the Umayyad era and also lived into the beginning of the Abbasid period, after the Qur’an’s revelation. His poetry is evidence of early Islamic Arabic, not pre-Islamic usage.

It illustrates the family’s established use in Arabic poetry.

Abundance without necessary disorder

Lexical accounts also use rukām to describe a large, numerous group or herd.

An early poetic citation supports this use by mentioning a large group alongside women wearing soft garments.

Its precise poet and date are uncertain, so it cannot be treated as definitely pre-Islamic.

The recorded usage nevertheless shows that rukām can express the size and abundance of a collection.

This need not imply disorder, confusion, or corruption.

A large gathering of people or animals need not be morally or socially objectionable.

Rukām has no inherent negative value.

4. فَيَرْكُمَهُ: heaping up in the separation of impure and pure

Al-Anfāl 8:37

Verse text:

لِيَمِيزَ اللَّهُ الْخَبِيثَ مِنَ الطَّيِّبِ وَيَجْعَلَ الْخَبِيثَ بَعْضَهُ عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ فَيَرْكُمَهُ جَمِيعًا فَيَجْعَلَهُ فِي جَهَنَّمَ ۚ أُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْخَاسِرُونَ

li-yamīza allāhu al-khabītha mina al-ṭayyibi wa-yajʿala al-khabītha baʿḍahu ʿalā baʿḍin fa-yarkumahu jamīʿan fa-yajʿalahu fī jahannama ulāʾika humu al-khāsirūna

Al-Anfāl 8:37

Translation:

“So that The God may distinguish the impure from the pure, place the impure one part upon another, heap it all together, and put it in Hell. Those are the losers.”

The meaning of فَيَرْكُمَهُ

فَيَرْكُمَهُ comes from the basic verb رَكَمَ يَرْكُمُ.

It is a subjunctive imperfect, coordinated with the preceding subjunctive verbs under the purpose construction introduced by لِـ in لِيَمِيزَ. The fa alone should not be presented as the cause of its subjunctive form.

Its subject is The God, and the object pronoun refers to الْخَبِيثَ.

It directly means “then heap it up.”

رَكَمَ is transitive here and expresses heaping.

It describes more than gathering things in one place, following بَعْضَهُ عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ.

That phrase explicitly presents one part upon another.

فَيَرْكُمَهُ جَمِيعًا emphasizes gathering and heaping the whole collection concerned.

Gathering and heaping up

The verse contains two related expressions:

First, الْخَبِيثَ بَعْضَهُ عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ: placing parts of the impure upon other parts.

Then, فَيَرْكُمَهُ جَمِيعًا: heaping the whole collection.

These are closely related constructions presenting one image. They need not establish two separate physical processes with independently measurable durations.

The first specifies the relationship of parts: one lies upon another.

The second presents the resulting collective accumulation.

Rakm here does not simply mean separating or setting aside.

Separation is expressed by لِيَمِيزَ; the subsequent wording describes piling and accumulation.

What does “the impure” refer to here?

Al-Anfāl 8:36 concerns people spending their wealth to obstruct the way of The God.

It also speaks of their defeat and the unbelievers being gathered toward Hell.

Thus, in Al-Anfāl 8:37, the distinction between الْخَبِيث and الطَّيِّب and the placing of the impure in Hell relate contextually to the fate of people opposing the truth.

Early interpretations often understand the impure as unbelievers and the pure as believers.

Because the preceding verse concerns wealth spent obstructing the divine way, some readings also connect the heaping image with that wealth.

Both contextual clues should be considered when identifying the referent.

وَالَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا إِلَىٰ جَهَنَّمَ يُحْشَرُونَ in the preceding verse and أُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْخَاسِرُونَ at this verse’s end provide clear contextual support for the reading concerning people and their fate.

The lexical meaning of فَيَرْكُمَهُ is not restricted to either referent.

It means heaping up; the sentence and setting identify who or what is heaped.

Does the verse describe a physical heap of people?

بَعْضَهُ عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ clearly depicts superposition.

That image should not be removed from translation.

It does not, however, establish the precise physical arrangement of people in Hell, the heap’s size, individual order, or bodily mechanism.

The verse describes separating, gathering, and placing the impure in Hell.

It may evoke actual accumulation, but it is not a detailed account of the hereafter’s structure.

Conversely, uncertainty about that world does not justify converting the entire image into “ethical summation” or “conceptual accumulation of evil.”

The verse describes divine action and a specified outcome. The outcome’s exact nature exceeds the scope of this word.

Impure and pure: value comes from context

R K M is not intrinsically an ethical root.

The same verb can describe sand, clouds, and other things.

In Al-Anfāl 8:37, ethical force comes from الْخَبِيث, الطَّيِّب, and human conduct in the surrounding context.

Rakm cannot therefore be defined as accumulating sins or gathering wrongdoers.

Those are possible referents within particular situations.

The general meaning remains heaping and piling up.

5. رُكَامًا: clouds forming an accumulated mass

Al-Nūr 24:43

Verse text:

أَلَمْ تَرَ أَنَّ اللَّهَ يُزْجِي سَحَابًا ثُمَّ يُؤَلِّفُ بَيْنَهُ ثُمَّ يَجْعَلُهُ رُكَامًا فَتَرَى الْوَدْقَ يَخْرُجُ مِنْ خِلَالِهِ وَيُنَزِّلُ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مِنْ جِبَالٍ فِيهَا مِنْ بَرَدٍ فَيُصِيبُ بِهِ مَنْ يَشَاءُ وَيَصْرِفُهُ عَنْ مَنْ يَشَاءُ ۖ يَكَادُ سَنَا بَرْقِهِ يَذْهَبُ بِالْأَبْصَارِ

a-lam tara anna allāha yuzjī saḥāban thumma yuʾallifu baynahu thumma yajʿaluhu rukāman fa-tarā al-wadqa yakhruju min khilālihi wa-yunazzilu mina al-samāʾi min jibālin fīhā min baradin fa-yuṣību bihi man yashāʾu wa-yaṣrifuhu ʿan man yashāʾu yakādu sanā barqihi yadhhabu bi-al-abṣāri

Al-Nūr 24:43

Translation:

“Have you not seen that The God drives a cloud, then joins its parts together, then makes it a piled-up mass, and you see rain emerging from within it? He sends down hail from the sky, from mountains within it, striking with it whomever He wills and turning it away from whomever He wills. The flash of its lightning nearly takes away sight.”

The meaning of رُكَامًا here

رُكَامًا describes the accumulated mass of cloud.

Grammatically, it is accusative in يَجْعَلُهُ رُكَامًا, functioning as the second object of “make” and describing the resulting state.

The subject of يَجْعَلُ is The God; its object pronoun refers to cloud.

The sentence describes the cloud becoming a piled-up mass.

Rukām here does not name rain, hail, or lightning.

Those phenomena are mentioned separately later in the verse.

The word itself concerns gathering and superposition within the cloud.

Three distinct descriptions of cloud

The verse opens with three related verbal constructions.

First, يُزْجِي سَحَابًا: moving or driving a cloud.

Then يُؤَلِّفُ بَيْنَهُ: bringing the cloud’s parts together.

Finally يَجْعَلُهُ رُكَامًا: making it an accumulated mass.

They are not lexically identical.

Moving cloud does not necessarily mean heaping it up.

Joining separated parts may bring them together, while rukām specifically highlights an accumulated mass.

Rukām therefore contributes a distinct meaning rather than merely repeating yuʾallifu.

بَيْنَهُ: joining parts of the cloud

In ثُمَّ يُؤَلِّفُ بَيْنَهُ, saḥāb is singular in form but can refer to a collection of clouds or cloud portions.

Its collective sense makes baynahu natural for relationships among its parts.

No special hypothesis about the number or type of clouds is needed to explain the construction.

The verse describes cloud portions being gathered and joined.

The resulting collection is then described as rukām.

Does rukām necessarily mean an extremely tall vertical cloud?

Accumulating cloud portions may form large, prominent masses in the sky.

Some clouds visibly possess striking height and extent.

That is compatible with the image of rukām.

The word itself is not the name of a specific modern meteorological cloud classification.

It alone cannot establish exact height, layer thickness, internal temperature, or airflow speed.

Nor does it imply that every cloud forms through an identical process.

The verse presents a natural image of cloud movement, joining, and accumulation.

Relating it to observed weather phenomena can help understanding, but new scientific data should not become the root’s lexical meaning.

Accumulated cloud and rain

The verse continues:

فَتَرَى الْوَدْقَ يَخْرُجُ مِنْ خِلَالِهِ

wa-yunazzilu mina al-samāʾi min jibālin fīhā min baradin

Al-Nūr 24:43

That is, rain emerges from within the cloud.

الْوَدْقَ means rain; خِلَالِهِ concerns the midst or spaces within the cloud mass.

Cloud accumulation is thus linked with rain emerging.

Rukām itself does not mean rain-bearing.

Clouds may appear accumulated without rain falling at that particular time and place.

Here rain’s emergence is expressed in a separate clause after accumulation.

Rainfall is a result described by the context, not an invariant part of the word’s definition.

Gathering and accumulation across the verses

Al-Rūm 30:48 also describes winds moving clouds and their spreading through the sky.

Fāṭir 35:9 likewise mentions winds stirring clouds.

These contribute to the Qur’anic account of cloud movement and rain.

Rukām in Al-Nūr 24:43 highlights a more particular feature: cloud portions lying upon one another to form an accumulated mass.

Al-Nūr 24:40 contains ظُلُمَاتٌ بَعْضُهَا فَوْقَ بَعْضٍ, darknesses one above another.

That expression shares an image of superposition but does not belong to R K M.

A shared image does not make words share a root or every meaning.

6. مَرْكُومٌ: accumulated cloud in the deniers’ speech

Al-Ṭūr 52:44

Verse text:

وَإِنْ يَرَوْا كِسْفًا مِنَ السَّمَاءِ سَاقِطًا يَقُولُوا سَحَابٌ مَرْكُومٌ

wa-in yaraw kisfan mina al-samāʾi sāqiṭan yaqūlū saḥābun markūmun

Al-Ṭūr 52:44

Translation:

“Even if they see a fragment falling from the sky, they will say: It is an accumulated cloud.”

The meaning of مَرْكُومٌ

مَرْكُومٌ is a passive participle from R K M.

It qualifies سَحَابٌ here.

It means a cloud whose parts have accumulated upon one another.

Its semantic connection with rukām in Al-Nūr 24:43 is clear.

The forms’ structural difference should nevertheless be retained.

Rukām names a mass or accumulated state.

Markūm describes something that has been heaped up.

سحاب مركوم is thus an accumulated cloud, while يجعله ركاما means making it an accumulated mass.

Natural Persian renderings may bring both close to “accumulated cloud,” but their grammatical roles differ.

Who says this?

“Accumulated cloud” here appears in the speech of people resisting the signs.

This matters for interpretation.

In Al-Nūr 24:43, the Qur’anic narration itself describes cloud accumulation.

In Al-Ṭūr 52:44, a group describes an event as “accumulated cloud.”

The scenes should not be equated without considering the speaker.

Al-Ṭūr presents a conditional possibility: even if they saw a fragment falling from the sky, they would call it an accumulated cloud.

This occurs within criticism of their denial and turning away.

Connection with demands for fragments of the sky to fall

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:187, opponents ask the messenger to make fragments of the sky fall on them if he is truthful.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:92 also records a demand for fragments of the sky to fall.

Al-Ṭūr 52:44 relates to that kind of demand.

Even witnessing an event corresponding to their demand need not produce acceptance; they may dismiss it through another explanation.

“Accumulated cloud” is the description they apply to what they see.

The expression does not establish that the event was actually only ordinary cloud.

Nor does the conditional establish that a fragment of the sky actually fell at that time.

The verse presents a hypothetical situation revealing the group’s stance and behavior.

Does markūm mean compressed or hardened?

Markūm concerns accumulation.

Accumulation may increase compactness in some materials, but the word does not mean becoming one hard, solid body.

Cloud differs in composition and structure from a heap of stone or earth.

سحاب مركوم should therefore not be rendered “petrified cloud,” “solid cloud,” or “compressed celestial body.”

Such meanings do not follow from the root or sentence.

كِسْفًا at the verse’s beginning means a fragment or portion and belongs to another family.

The object seen in the conditional must be distinguished from the description assigned to it by the speakers.

Natural accumulation and persistent denial

Seeing accumulated cloud is not itself objectionable in Al-Ṭūr 52:44.

Cloud accumulation is natural, and Al-Nūr 24:43 presents it among signs of creation.

The issue is people continuing to explain away and deny even an event of the kind they demanded.

Markūm itself carries no negative ethical value here.

Criticism comes from their conduct, speech, and the setting of denial.

This prevents “accumulated” from being redefined as “deceptive,” “false,” or “excuse-making.”

7. Related roots and translation proposals

R K M (rakm) and R K B (rukūb)

They share two consonants and sometimes have neighboring semantic fields.

One branch of R K B concerns things lying upon one another and parts being combined.

Al-Anʿām 6:99 uses حَبًّا مُتَرَاكِبًا for grain arranged upon grain.

R K M concerns gathering and accumulating parts.

Rukāman in Al-Nūr 24:43 describes accumulated cloud.

تراكب السحاب and تراكم السحاب can describe similar conditions.

Combination and accumulation are nevertheless not identical in every use.

Combination can concern parts joining into a whole; accumulation emphasizes their piling up.

This is a tendency, not an absolute boundary.

R K M (rakm) and J M ʿ (jamʿ)

Jamʿ has a broad field of gathering.

People, wealth, or objects may gather in one place without being piled upon one another.

Rakm in its clear uses concerns gathering together with superposition.

بَعْضَهُ عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ in Al-Anfāl 8:37 makes this visible.

Gathering may therefore express part of rakm without conveying its complete image.

Where superposition matters, “heaping up” is more precise.

R K M (rakm) and ʾ L F (ilf)

Taʾlīf can mean joining and bringing separated parts together.

In Al-Nūr 24:43, يُؤَلِّفُ بَيْنَهُ precedes يَجْعَلُهُ رُكَامًا.

Here joining concerns cloud portions coming together, while accumulation concerns their piled-up state.

The sequence does not establish that every kind of joining necessarily produces accumulation.

Things or people may be connected without forming a heap.

The relationship here is clear, but the meanings are not identical.

R K M (rakm) and N Ḍ D (naḍd)

Naḍd concerns arranging things upon one another.

Qāf 50:10 uses نَضِيدٌ for the closely arranged parts of a palm’s fruit-bearing cluster.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:29 also uses مَنْضُودٍ.

Rakm shares superposition, but gathering and forming a mass are especially prominent in its clear uses.

Some uses of naḍd make the arrangement of parts within a collection more prominent.

It does not follow that naḍd always involves precise geometry or rakm always disorder.

They can overlap, and distinctions require evidence from each use.

R K M (rakm) and R K D (rukūd)

R K D concerns settling or ceasing to move.

R K M concerns accumulation.

Sand may be accumulated and stationary, but these are different properties.

Cloud can likewise be accumulated while moving.

Rukāman in Al-Nūr 24:43 does not therefore mean the cloud stops moving.

Rawākid in Al-Shūrā 42:33 does not mean piled-up ships; it means ships that have stopped moving.

Similar consonants should not cause the roots to be conflated.

Suitable equivalents include “then heap it up,” “then accumulate it all,” and “then gather that collection in a heap.”

Within the complete verse, “and heap it all together” is natural and precise.

رُكَامًا — Al-Nūr 24:43

Suitable equivalents include “an accumulated mass,” “a piled-up mass,” or, with the verb, “He heaps it up.”

The supplied rendering corresponding to “then He heaps it up” conveys the meaning well.

The article can further explain cloud portions forming masses upon one another.

مَرْكُومٌ — Al-Ṭūr 52:44

Suitable equivalents include “accumulated,” “heaped up,” and “piled together.”

“An accumulated cloud” is concise and clear for سَحَابٌ مَرْكُومٌ.

It preserves the lexical image without restricting it to a particular cloud type.

Suggested word-box wording for Forqan

Root: R K M

Brief root meaning: Gathering and piling things upon one another to form a heap or accumulated collection.

Word: فَيَرْكُمَهُ — Al-Anfāl 8:37

Meaning here: Then heap it all together, following separation of impure from pure and placing parts of the impure upon one another.

Word: رُكَامًا — Al-Nūr 24:43

Meaning here: An accumulated mass; the cloud’s state after its movement and the joining of its parts.

Word: مَرْكُومٌ — Al-Ṭūr 52:44

Meaning here: Heaped up; qualifying the cloud invoked in the deniers’ speech.

8. Conclusions and final definition

Arabic R K M concerns gathering and placing things upon one another.

Sand, earth, mud, cloud, and other materials can gather into a mass.

رَكَمَ expresses heaping; رُكام names the mass or accumulated state; مَرْكُوم describes what has accumulated.

Broader uses can describe large gatherings or accumulated matters.

Early evidence establishes this field: a variant of Imruʾ al-Qays’s poetry uses rukām for piled sand, and Ruʾbah pairs rukām with murtakam.

The three Qur’anic occurrences share a meaning while appearing in different settings.

In Al-Anfāl 8:37, فَيَرْكُمَهُ describes heaping الْخَبِيث within separation and final destiny.

In Al-Nūr 24:43, رُكَامًا describes clouds forming an accumulated mass after movement and joining.

In Al-Ṭūr 52:44, مَرْكُومٌ qualifies the cloud that deniers invoke to explain what they see.

The root has no inherent positive or negative ethical value. Cloud accumulation can feature among creation’s signs, while the same lexical field describes a group’s fate elsewhere.

Rakm does not mean a specified physical density, definite arrangement, or cessation of motion. Those properties require independent evidence.

Final definition of R K M:

Its main field is gathering and placing parts or things upon one another so that a heap or accumulated collection forms.

Rakm is the act of heaping; rukām the resulting mass or condition; markūm something in that state.

The Qur’anic uses retain superposition and accumulation while context supplies subject and outcome: one concerns final destiny, and two concern clouds.

For Forqan’s study and translation, the most suitable core is “heaping up and forming a mass of gathered parts,” without treating material, cause, ethical value, or consequence as invariant components of the root.
