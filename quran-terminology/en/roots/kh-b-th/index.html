The Qur’anic Root KH B TH

Impurity, poor quality, corruption, and unworthiness: from land and property to speech, conduct, and moral judgment

Qur’anic and lexical forms

(khabutha)
Qur’anic verb: was poor in quality; Al-Aʿrāf 7:58

خَبِيث
(khabīth)
Qur’anic adjective or noun: inferior, impure, or unworthy according to context

(khabīthah)

The Arabic root KH B TH stands in opposition to Ṭ Y B. Khabīth can describe something inferior, unsound, impure, corrupt, or unworthy. Its precise meaning depends on what it describes. Khabīth land is unsuitable for growth; khabīth property may be of poor quality or obtained wrongfully; and khabīth speech or conduct refers to wrongful, objectionable words or actions.

In the Qur’an, this root is not confined to physical contamination or forbidden food. It describes unproductive land, goods unsuitable for charitable giving, false speech, foul actions, and people whose conduct is unworthy. This range shows why khabīth cannot be translated throughout with a single equivalent such as “ritually impure,” “forbidden,” or “foul-smelling.”

These uses share an undesirable quality or deficiency brought out by contrast with ṭayyib. Yet undesirability itself requires care. What an individual or culture dislikes is not necessarily khabīth in Qur’anic usage; conversely, something may attract people or bring them profit while the Qur’an judges it morally khabīth.

1. Qur’anic forms and the semantic core

The Qur’an uses the verb خَبُثَ (khabutha), the forms خَبِيث (khabīth), خَبِيثَة (khabīthah), الْخَبِيثُونَ (al-khabīthūn), الْخَبِيثِينَ (al-khabīthīn), الْخَبِيثَات (al-khabīthāt), and the plural الْخَبَائِث (al-khabāʾith). The root occurs 16 times in 10 verses across nine surahs: one khabutha verb, nine khabīth forms including its masculine plurals, two khabīthah forms, two khabīthāt forms, and two khabāʾith forms.

The verb khabutha occurs in Al-Aʿrāf 7:58 concerning land. Khabīth and its related forms occur in Al-Baqarah 2:267, Āl ʿImrān 3:179, Al-Nisāʾ 4:2, Al-Māʾidah 5:100, Al-Anfāl 8:37, and Al-Nūr 24:26. Khabīthah describes both a word and a tree in Ibrāhīm 14:26, while al-khabāʾith occurs in Al-Aʿrāf 7:157 and Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:74.

Together, these forms encompass material things and conditions as well as human speech and conduct. Not every form recorded in earlier Arabic occurs in the Qur’an, however. خُبْث (khubth), خَبَاثَة (khabāthah), مُخْبِث (mukhbith), and خَبَثُ الْحَدِيد (khabathu al-ḥadīd) are established lexical forms and expressions, but must not be listed as attested Qur’anic derivatives.

The basic Arabic meaning

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies KH B TH fundamentally as the opposite of ṭayyib. Other Arabic dictionaries record this opposition too. In Lisān al-ʿArab, khabīth describes inferior or corrupt things as well as people and their conduct.

This is a useful starting point: ṭayyib and khabīth directly oppose one another in several Qur’anic verses. Yet “the opposite of ṭayyib” does not settle each occurrence’s precise meaning. We must identify what is called ṭayyib or khabīth in the sentence and which of its qualities is at issue.

Arabic uses خَبُثَ الشَّيْءُ (khabutha al-shayʾu) of something that has an undesirable or objectionable quality. This may concern substance and quality, taste and smell, effect and outcome, or moral worth. Applied to a person, it may indicate bad conduct, impure intentions, or corrupt behavior.

The root’s semantic core therefore lies in undesirability, corruption, poor quality, and unworthiness, rather than one particular feature present in every referent.

2. Earlier Arabic usage and the root’s material imagery

In the Arabic lexicographical tradition, khabīth is not solely a moral adjective. This family describes things of undesirable quality, disagreeable food and drink, contamination, and objectionable behavior or character.

One familiar material expression is خَبَثُ الْحَدِيد (khabathu al-ḥadīd), the impurities and inferior material separated from metal during smelting. Here, khabath is the part removed from the desired metal and of no value for the principal purpose.

This image helps illustrate the opposition between khabīth and ṭayyib. Just as inferior material is separated from metal during smelting, the Qur’an speaks of distinguishing khabīth from ṭayyib.

This resemblance does not establish the furnace and metal dross as the certain historical origin of every meaning of the root. Dictionaries record the metallurgical expression, but that evidence alone does not establish its chronological priority. Nor does every Qur’anic separation of khabīth from ṭayyib necessarily evoke metal smelting.

An example from poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period

The Muʿallaqah attributed to ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād contains this line:

نُبِّئْتُ عَمْرًا غَيْرَ شَاكِرِ نِعْمَتِي
وَالْكُفْرُ مَخْبَثَةٌ لِنَفْسِ الْمُنْعِمِ

nubbiʾtu ʿamran ghayra shākiri niʿmatī / wa-al-kufru makhbathatun li-nafsi al-munʿimi

The line means: “I have been told that ʿAmr is ungrateful for my kindness; ingratitude sours the benefactor’s spirit.”

مَخْبَثَة (makhbathah) here denotes something that causes displeasure, aversion, or an unwelcome change in a person’s state. Kufr in this context means ingratitude for a favor.

This example matters because the KH B TH family describes a human emotional condition in poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period. The subject is neither physical impurity nor forbidden food: ingratitude distresses the giver.

This transmitted poetic example uses KH B TH to describe an undesirable quality in behavior and human relationships, independently of later legal terminology. Attribution to a pre-Islamic poet does not by itself establish the precise date of each line or the word’s first use.

3. Khabīth land and property: poor or unsuitable quality

وَالَّذِي خَبُثَ (wa-alladhī khabutha): land whose growth is difficult

Surah Al-Aʿrāf states:

وَالْبَلَدُ الطَّيِّبُ يَخْرُجُ نَبَاتُهُ بِإِذْنِ رَبِّهِ وَالَّذِي خَبُثَ لَا يَخْرُجُ إِلَّا نَكِدًا كَذَٰلِكَ نُصَرِّفُ الْآيَاتِ لِقَوْمٍ يَشْكُرُونَ

wa-al-baladu al-ṭayyibu yakhruju nabātuhu bi-idhni rabbihi wa-alladhī khabutha lā yakhruju illā nakidan ka-dhālika nuṣarrifu al-āyāti li-qawmin yashkurūna

“The good land brings forth its vegetation by its Lord’s permission, while that which is poor brings forth only with difficulty and scant yield. Thus We explain the signs in varied ways for people who give thanks.” (Al-Aʿrāf 7:58)

Here, khabutha contrasts with al-baladu al-ṭayyibu. Good land produces vegetation, while growth from khabīth land is difficult and meager.

Khabīth thus concerns the land’s condition and capacity for growth. “Poor land,” “land unsuitable for cultivation,” or, where the context is clear, “unproductive land” are fitting equivalents.

“Ritually impure land” changes the meaning: the verse is not discussing a ruling on the land’s ritual purity. “Forbidden land” is likewise unrelated to the sentence’s construction.

This is especially clear evidence that khabīth is not inherently a technical legal term. Land may naturally have unsuitable qualities without impurity or sin being at issue.

The following word نَكِدًا (nakidan) clarifies the difficulty and poor outcome of growth. Its entire meaning should not be absorbed into khabutha: khabīth characterizes the land, while nakid describes the quality of its yield.

الْخَبِيثَ (al-khabīth) in charitable giving: what you would not accept yourself

In Al-Baqarah 2:267, believers are commanded to give from the good things they have earned and from the earth’s produce, rather than deliberately select khabīth goods to give away—goods they would accept themselves only by overlooking their defects.

The verse clarifies khabīth through a practical criterion: people should not choose for others something they would dislike receiving and would accept only by overlooking its poor quality.

Here, al-khabīth may denote inferior, defective goods or things unsuitable for charitable giving.

This is not identical with “forbidden.” Property may be lawfully owned yet of very poor quality; giving it to someone in need while keeping the best for oneself may be objectionable conduct.

Conversely, property may appear valuable and excellent yet have been obtained through oppression or wrongful appropriation. That raises a separate question about its lawful acquisition.

Khabīth in Al-Baqarah 2:267 should therefore not be confined without evidence to illicit property. By mentioning goods one would accept only by overlooking their defects, the verse also emphasizes poor quality.

وَلَا تَتَبَدَّلُوا الْخَبِيثَ بِالطَّيِّبِ (wa-lā tatabaddalū al-khabītha bi-al-ṭayyib): orphans’ property

Surah Al-Nisāʾ states:

وَآتُوا الْيَتَامَىٰ أَمْوَالَهُمْ وَلَا تَتَبَدَّلُوا الْخَبِيثَ بِالطَّيِّبِ وَلَا تَأْكُلُوا أَمْوَالَهُمْ إِلَىٰ أَمْوَالِكُمْ إِنَّهُ كَانَ حُوبًا كَبِيرًا

wa-ātū al-yatāmā amwālahum wa-lā tatabaddalū al-khabītha bi-al-ṭayyibi wa-lā taʾkulū amwālahum ilā amwālikum innahu kāna ḥūban kabīran

“Give the orphans their property; do not take the bad in exchange for the good, and do not consume their property together with your own. That is indeed a great sin.” (Al-Nisāʾ 4:2)

The opposition between khabīth and ṭayyib concerns orphans’ property. Two interpretive directions merit consideration.

First, putting a guardian’s inferior property in place of an orphan’s good property: the person managing it takes the good item and leaves something less valuable or defective in return.

Second, exchanging rightful access to property for wrongful access: rather than protecting the orphan’s rights and using one’s own property lawfully, a person takes another’s property.

Both readings relate to the verse’s general setting. Choosing between them requires examining tatabaddalū, the preposition bāʾ, and the connection with the following prohibition. The word khabīth alone cannot decide between them independently of the sentence’s structure.

In either case, the verse concerns property rights and preventing encroachment on an orphan’s wealth. Khabīth here does not mean “foul-smelling” or “ritually impure.”

This illustrates how khabīth can carry moral significance within an economic relationship. The property’s undesirable quality, or its wrongful appropriation, bears on other people’s rights.

4. Khabīth speech and conduct: corruption in meaning and action

كَلِمَةٍ خَبِيثَةٍ (kalimatin khabīthatin): a false, unsound word

Surah Ibrāhīm states:

وَمَثَلُ كَلِمَةٍ خَبِيثَةٍ كَشَجَرَةٍ خَبِيثَةٍ اجْتُثَّتْ مِنْ فَوْقِ الْأَرْضِ مَا لَهَا مِنْ قَرَارٍ

wa-mathalu kalimatin khabīthatin ka-shajaratin khabīthatin ijtuththat min fawqi al-arḍi mā lahā min qarārin

“The likeness of a bad word is that of a bad tree, uprooted from the surface of the earth, with no stability.” (Ibrāhīm 14:26)

Khabīthah occurs twice here, describing kalimah, a word, and shajarah, a tree.

The bad word contrasts with the good word likened to a firmly rooted tree in Ibrāhīm 14:24. In this contrast, the bad word is false and undesirable in truth and worth; the bad tree illustrates that condition.

Being uprooted and lacking stability are stated in the rest of the comparison. Khabīth should therefore not be rendered “rootless” or “unstable” in every occurrence. These features belong to this verse’s imagery, rather than the root’s lexical definition.

Nor should the bad tree be identified as one particular species without evidence. The verse itself emphasizes the tree’s condition and lack of stability.

Here, khabīth may describe false, wrongful, corrupt, or objectionable speech. Not every statement that upsets a listener is khabīth: an unpleasant truth may distress its audience while remaining true and just.

Conversely, speech may sound pleasing and enticing while its content rests on falsehood or deception. Superficial appeal and whether speech is ṭayyib or khabīth are separate questions.

تَعْمَلُ الْخَبَائِثَ (taʿmalu al-khabāʾith): foul deeds

Surah Al-Anbiyāʾ states:

وَلُوطًا آتَيْنَاهُ حُكْمًا وَعِلْمًا وَنَجَّيْنَاهُ مِنَ الْقَرْيَةِ الَّتِي كَانَتْ تَعْمَلُ الْخَبَائِثَ إِنَّهُمْ كَانُوا قَوْمَ سَوْءٍ فَاسِقِينَ

wa-lūṭan ātaynāhu ḥukman wa-ʿilman wa-najjaynāhu mina al-qaryati allatī kānat taʿmalu al-khabāʾitha innahum kānū qawma sawʾin fāsiqīna

“To Lot We gave judgment and knowledge, and We rescued him from the town that committed foul deeds. They were indeed an evil, defiantly disobedient people.” (Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:74)

Al-khabāʾith is the object of taʿmalu, “does” or “commits.” It therefore concerns actions and behavior characterized as khabīth.

The following description, قَوْمَ سَوْءٍ فَاسِقِينَ (qawma sawʾin fāsiqīn), reinforces the moral direction. Khabīth here concerns wrongful actions, rather than merely the poor quality of an object.

Other verses describe the conduct of Lot’s people, including Al-Aʿrāf 7:80, Al-Aʿrāf 7:81, Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:28, and Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:29. They help identify the condemned behavior.

Lexically, however, al-khabāʾith is not the exclusive name of one particular act. It is plural and denotes foul deeds in the construction taʿmalu al-khabāʾith. Their specific referents come from the context and related verses.

Not every use of al-khabāʾith should therefore be restricted to a single behavior. The same plural occurs in Al-Aʿrāf 7:157, but within a different grammatical construction and context.

5. Khabīth people: moral distinction and responsibility

Distinguishing khabīth from ṭayyib

In Āl ʿImrān 3:179, the Qur’an says that The God will not leave the believers in their present condition until He distinguishes the bad from the good.

Khabīth and ṭayyib concern people’s condition and the disclosure of their differences. This is no longer poor land or disagreeable food: the distinction concerns morality and faith.

The root KH B TH does not itself mean “disbeliever” or “hypocrite.” A verse may distinguish a group by particular conduct and attitudes, but that group’s identity and the grounds for distinction come from context.

The remainder of the verse also says that the unseen is not disclosed to the audience. Distinguishing khabīth from ṭayyib therefore does not justify claiming certain knowledge of everyone’s inner reality or ultimate fate.

KH B TH provides an evaluative description. Identifying particular referents and making final judgments about individuals are separate matters.

لِيَمِيزَ اللَّهُ الْخَبِيثَ مِنَ الطَّيِّبِ (li-yamīza allāhu al-khabītha mina al-ṭayyib)

Surah Al-Anfāl states:

لِيَمِيزَ اللَّهُ الْخَبِيثَ مِنَ الطَّيِّبِ وَيَجْعَلَ الْخَبِيثَ بَعْضَهُ عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ فَيَرْكُمَهُ جَمِيعًا فَيَجْعَلَهُ فِي جَهَنَّمَ أُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْخَاسِرُونَ

li-yamīza allāhu al-khabītha mina al-ṭayyibi wa-yajʿala al-khabītha baʿḍahu ʿalā baʿḍin fa-yarkumahu jamīʿan fa-yajʿalahu fī jahannama ulāʾika humu al-khāsirūna

“So that The God may separate the bad from the good, place the bad one upon another, heap it all together, and put it in Hell. Those are the losers.” (Al-Anfāl 8:37)

This distinction is connected to the outcome in the hereafter. The preceding context concerns people who spend their wealth to hinder others from the way of The God (Al-Anfāl 8:36).

Both people and their conduct and expenditure therefore matter in understanding the verse. Without examining the context, khabīth should not be confined to the name of an object or a merely outward human characteristic.

Here, khabīth relates to an attitude and action opposed to truth and ending in a condemned outcome. The word retains its undesirable, unworthy direction; the moral and eschatological significance emerges from the verse’s context.

الْخَبِيثَاتُ لِلْخَبِيثِينَ (al-khabīthātu li-l-khabīthīn): women, people, or words?

Surah Al-Nūr states:

الْخَبِيثَاتُ لِلْخَبِيثِينَ وَالْخَبِيثُونَ لِلْخَبِيثَاتِ وَالطَّيِّبَاتُ لِلطَّيِّبِينَ وَالطَّيِّبُونَ لِلطَّيِّبَاتِ أُولَٰئِكَ مُبَرَّءُونَ مِمَّا يَقُولُونَ لَهُمْ مَغْفِرَةٌ وَرِزْقٌ كَرِيمٌ

al-khabīthātu li-l-khabīthīna wa-al-khabīthūna li-l-khabīthāti wa-al-ṭayyibātu li-l-ṭayyibīna wa-al-ṭayyibūna li-l-ṭayyibāti ulāʾika mubarraʾūna mimmā yaqūlūna lahum maghfiratun wa-rizqun karīmun

“The impure are for the impure, and the impure for the impure; the pure are for the pure, and the pure for the pure. Those are innocent of what they say; for them are forgiveness and generous provision.” (Al-Nūr 24:26)

This verse calls for particular grammatical and contextual care.

Al-khabīthāt is feminine plural; al-khabīthūn and al-khabīthīn are masculine plurals. The feminine forms may refer to women, but can also describe grammatically feminine things, such as words or deeds.

Two major readings consequently arise.

One concerns pure or impure women and men. Another associates foul speech or deeds with unworthy speakers and doers, and good speech and behavior with worthy people.

The preceding verses concern accusation, slander, and speaking about others. The ending, مِمَّا يَقُولُونَ (mimmā yaqūlūn), also refers directly to speech. These clues make the reading concerning pure and impure words a serious, defensible interpretation.

The reading concerning people is also grammatically possible. Nevertheless, the feminine plural alone cannot establish a universal, exceptionless rule governing every woman’s and man’s marriage.

Al-Taḥrīm 66:10 and Al-Taḥrīm 66:11 themselves offer examples of spouses differing in faith and moral condition. Any interpretation that asserts an absolute moral correspondence between all spouses must be assessed against those verses.

For root study, khabīth here concerns impurity and unworthiness in the human and moral sphere. Whether the feminine forms specifically refer to women, words, or deeds is a contextual question, not something already settled by the root’s lexical definition.

Abundance does not turn khabīth into ṭayyib

Surah Al-Māʾidah states:

قُلْ لَا يَسْتَوِي الْخَبِيثُ وَالطَّيِّبُ وَلَوْ أَعْجَبَكَ كَثْرَةُ الْخَبِيثِ فَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ يَا أُولِي الْأَلْبَابِ لَعَلَّكُمْ تُفْلِحُونَ

qul lā yastawī al-khabīthu wa-al-ṭayyibu wa-law aʿjabaka kathratu al-khabīthi fa-ittaqū allāha yā ulī al-albābi laʿallakum tufliḥūna

“Say: the bad and the good are not equal, even if the abundance of the bad impresses you. So be mindful of The God, people of understanding, so that you may prosper.” (Al-Māʾidah 5:100)

This is particularly clear evidence that khabīth does not always mean something people immediately find repulsive.

The Qur’an says that its abundance may impress or even attract the audience, without making khabīth equal to ṭayyib.

For example, wealth obtained through corruption may be plentiful and provide outward comfort, but abundance does not erase its wrongful acquisition. Likewise, objectionable behavior may spread widely and attract many followers; its prevalence does not itself alter its moral worth.

These examples are contemporary applications of the verse’s principle, rather than lexical definitions of khabīth.

Here, the distinction between khabīth and ṭayyib concerns value, which numbers, popularity, and outward appeal do not determine.

6. Al-khabāʾith and lawfulness and prohibition

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:157, the Qur’an describes the Messenger as enjoining what is right, forbidding what is wrong, permitting good things, prohibiting foul things, and lifting the heavy burdens and restraints that had been upon people.

This verse is especially significant because al-khabāʾith is associated with يُحَرِّمُ (yuḥarrimu), “prohibits.” The relationship between khubth and prohibition is explicitly stated in this construction.

A basic distinction must nevertheless be preserved: khabīth characterizes something within the semantic field of impurity, corruption, or unworthiness; ḥarām expresses a ruling of prohibition.

Both may apply to one referent, but they are not lexical synonyms.

Is everything khabīth forbidden?

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:58, khabīth land is unsuitable for growth. In Al-Baqarah 2:267, khabīth goods may be inferior. In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:74, khabāʾith denotes foul deeds. In Ibrāhīm 14:26, khabīth describes a word and a tree.

None of these uses warrants translating khabīth throughout the Qur’an solely as “legally forbidden.”

At the same time, Al-Aʿrāf 7:157 explicitly speaks of prohibiting al-khabāʾith. That relationship must neither be ignored nor weakened without evidence.

The precise question is what falls within that ruling and what criteria the Qur’an provides for identifying it.

The lexical meaning of khabīth alone cannot fully answer this question. Verses concerning prohibition, good things, foods, actions, divine limits, and the scope of the address must also be examined.

Personal dislike and divine prohibition

Arabic may use khabīth of things disagreeable in taste, smell, or quality. This linguistic usage does not mean that everything a person dislikes eating or smelling is forbidden by The God.

People’s tastes differ. Something may be normal, accepted food in one society and disagreeable in another. An individual may also avoid a food because of experience, illness, sensitivity, health concerns, or cultural habit.

Such choices may have reasonable grounds. Personal abstention nevertheless differs from claiming a divine prohibition binding on everyone.

Someone may dislike a particular animal’s meat or avoid it over health concerns. That decision alone does not establish that The God has forbidden everyone to eat it.

Conversely, liking a food or customarily eating it does not by itself establish its lawfulness. A religious ruling must be understood from evidence relevant to that ruling.

In Al-Naḥl 16:116, the Qur’an forbids attributing lawfulness and prohibition to The God on the basis of unsupported assertions. This warning matters in both directions: people may neither forbid something in His name without evidence nor disregard a divine ruling without evidence.

Personal liking or dislike alone is therefore insufficient to establish a universal ruling.

Does al-khabāʾith in Al-Aʿrāf 7:157 mean only food?

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:157, enjoining right and forbidding wrong precede permitting good things and prohibiting foul things. This association places the verse within guidance and rulings; its entire scope should not be restricted without evidence to food’s taste or smell.

The pairing of ṭayyibāt and khabāʾith may also concern dietary rulings. Denying that connection would likewise be unwarranted.

In Qur’anic language itself, however, al-khabāʾith is not the name of a fixed list of animals. This verse alone does not name every forbidden referent.

Identifying those referents requires examining other verses and the ruling’s scope. The claim that people in a particular region dislike something cannot alone establish a divine prohibition for everyone.

Nor does the general expression al-khabāʾith immediately make one particular people’s dietary preferences the permanent, universal criterion of khabāthah. Such a generalization requires independent evidence.

This caution does not dismiss health or real harm. A food may be contaminated, spoiled, or dangerous, making avoidance necessary or reasonable. Identifying actual harm, taking personal precautions, and issuing a religious ruling in the name of The God must nevertheless remain distinct.

A lexical study of KH B TH can therefore assist understanding a ruling, but cannot replace an independent investigation of lawfulness and prohibition.

7. Comparison with related roots

KH B TH (khabutha) compared with Ṭ Y B (ṭāba)

Study of Ṭ Y B

The principal Qur’anic opposition to KH B TH is Ṭ Y B. These families directly contrast in several verses.

Ṭayyib may denote good quality, purity, agreeableness, desirability, and worthiness; khabīth expresses the opposing poor quality, impurity, corruption, or unworthiness.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:58, the contrast concerns land quality; in Al-Baqarah 2:267, choosing property to give; in Al-Māʾidah 5:100, a difference in value; and in Āl ʿImrān 3:179 and Al-Anfāl 8:37, moral distinction.

These examples show that both families depend on their referents and context. Always translating ṭayyib as “lawful” would force khabīth into “forbidden,” but that approach does not work for the verses about land, property, and speech.

KH B TH (khabutha) compared with N J S (najas)

Study of N J S

نَجَسٌ (najas) occurs in Al-Tawbah 9:28. It relates to impurity, but its contextual meaning should not be equated with every use of khabīth without examination.

Khabīth has a broader range, including poor land, corrupt speech, and unworthy behavior.

Applying najas to people in Al-Tawbah 9:28 also requires independently examining the intended impurity there. The comparison does not establish that both words always denote physical contamination alone.

KH B TH (khabutha) compared with R J S (rijs)

Study of R J S

The Qur’an uses rijs in contexts of contamination, foulness, and condemned conditions. This sometimes approaches khabīth, but the roots are not identical in use.

For example, khabīth describes unproductive land in Al-Aʿrāf 7:58, whereas rijs does not have the same use.

Rendering both words as “foul” may fit some verses, but this proximity should not conceal their different roots and referents.

KH B TH (khabutha) compared with F S D (fasada)

Study of F S D

Fasād concerns deterioration, the disruption of soundness, and the emergence of an unsound or wrongful condition. Khabīth more often characterizes an undesirable or unworthy quality.

Their meanings may overlap: corrupt behavior may also be khabīth. Yet khabīth does not necessarily describe a process of deterioration. The unproductive land in Al-Aʿrāf 7:58 may have been unsuitable for cultivation from the outset, rather than having necessarily deteriorated.

Fasād in the Qur’an may also concern the disruption of order, well-being, and social relations; it has its own range.

KH B TH (khabutha) compared with Ḥ R M (ḥaruma)

Study of Ḥ R M

Ḥaruma and its related forms concern inviolability, prohibition, and protected bounds. This field differs fundamentally from khabīth.

Khabīth describes a thing’s quality or worth; ḥarām speaks of its prohibition or inviolability.

When Al-Aʿrāf 7:157 speaks of prohibiting al-khabāʾith, these two levels occur together in one sentence. Their association does not establish identical meanings.

This distinction must be retained in translation and in understanding rulings.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

For khabutha in Al-Aʿrāf 7:58, “was poor,” “was unsuitable for growth,” or “had poor quality” fit the context. “Was ritually impure” and “was forbidden” do not.

For al-khabīth in Al-Baqarah 2:267, “inferior goods” or “unsuitable, defective things” convey the sense more clearly. In Al-Nisāʾ 4:2, translation and explanation should also preserve the contrast with ṭayyib and orphans’ property rights.

In Al-Māʾidah 5:100, “impure and pure” or “unworthy and worthy” may work, depending on the translation’s approach and accompanying explanation. The wording should make clear that value and quality are at issue, rather than necessarily physical contamination.

In Ibrāhīm 14:26, a khabīth word may be rendered “objectionable speech,” “wrongful speech,” or, in light of the comparison, “a false word.” The khabīth tree is likewise undesirable and unworthy within that comparison. The tree’s uprooting should be conveyed by the following clause, rather than absorbed into the adjective itself.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:74, al-khabāʾith is naturally “foul deeds” or “wrongful actions.” In Al-Aʿrāf 7:157, its association with prohibition allows “foul, objectionable things,” without turning the translation into a list of particular referents.

In Āl ʿImrān 3:179 and Al-Anfāl 8:37, translation should retain the moral distinction between khabīth and ṭayyib. “Impure and pure,” “bad and good,” or “unworthy and worthy” depend on context.

In Al-Nūr 24:26, until the feminine forms’ referents are finally assessed, it is preferable to avoid inserting “women” or “words” without justification. If one reading is chosen for the main translation, the other established reading should be explained.

Among non-Qur’anic lexical forms, khubth may mean impurity, badness, corruption, or unworthiness; khabāthah belongs to the same field. Khabathu al-ḥadīd means dross separated from metal. These help explain Arabic usage but must not be recorded as independent Qur’anic occurrences.

Conclusion

The Arabic root KH B TH opposes Ṭ Y B and denotes undesirability, poor quality, impurity, corruption, and unworthiness. Its meaning branches according to its referent: khabīth land is unsuitable for growth; khabīth property may be inferior or wrongfully appropriated; khabīth speech is false and unworthy; and khabīth conduct is morally condemned.

These uses show that khabīth is not always ritually impure, foul-smelling, a forbidden food, or an inherent description of a human group. Context identifies the quality or behavior being condemned.

The Qur’anic contrast between khabīth and ṭayyib concerns both the material world and human conduct and values. It cannot be restricted to a purely technical legal opposition.

On lawfulness and prohibition, the Qur’an connects khabāʾith with prohibition. This does not authorize declaring everything an individual or culture dislikes divinely forbidden without independent evidence. Permission and prohibition must be understood from the relevant ruling and evidence, rather than personal attraction or aversion.

In its basic sense, khabīth describes something undesirable and unworthy in quality, effect, conduct, or value. This undesirability may be material, practical, or moral; its precise meaning is determined by the accompanying words, sentence structure, and Qur’anic context.
