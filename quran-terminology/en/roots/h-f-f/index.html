From surroundings and edges to encircling gardens and angels around the Throne

In Arabic, the root Ḥ F F concerns something being situated around another thing and surrounding it. The verb «حَفَّ» is used when people or things are positioned around something and encompass it from its sides. «حِفَاف» denotes an edge or margin, while «حَافّ» describes someone or something situated around another.

This root also has other uses. «حَفِيف» is a sound heard from the movement of a bird’s wings, tree leaves, wind, or other things. «حَفَف» and «حُفُوف» can denote dryness, scarcity, and hardship in living. These meanings are recorded in early dictionaries, but evidence is needed before reducing them all to a single image.

The root Ḥ F F occurs only twice in the Qur’an. In Al-Kahf 18:32, date palms are placed around two vineyards. In Al-Zumar 39:75, angels are described around the Throne. Both uses concern surrounding and being situated around something, although their subjects and settings differ.

Qur’anic forms: «وَحَفَفْنَاهُمَا» in Al-Kahf 18:32 and «حَافِّينَ» in Al-Zumar 39:75.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: the two occurrences and morphology of Ḥ F F

Other forms recorded in Arabic include «حَفَّ», «يَحُفُّ», «حِفَاف», «حَفِيف», «حَفَف», «حُفُوف», «حَافّ», «مَحَفَّة», «حَفَّفَ», and «احْتَفَّ», but these forms do not occur as such in the Qur’an.

1. Semantic principles and early Arabic usage

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies three separate principles for this family: a kind of sound, something being positioned around another thing, and hardship in living. In explaining the third principle, he also discusses dryness and scarcity.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: sound, surrounding, and hardship as three principles

This division matters because an excessively broad and vague definition cannot adequately explain every use of the root. Surrounding involves a spatial relationship between two things; ḥafīf concerns a sound produced by movement; and ḥafaf and ḥufūf concern dryness and scarcity.

Ḥaffa: being situated around something

«حَفَّ الْقَوْمُ بِفُلَانٍ» means that a group has gathered around a person and surrounded him from different sides.

Al-ʿAyn records this expression for people gathering around their leader or chief.

This use involves two parties: those who occupy the surroundings, and the person or thing among them.

«حَفَّ الشَّيْءُ بِالشَّيْءِ» is also recorded for surrounding something with another thing. In some constructions, the preposition bi- introduces what occupies the surroundings.

For example, when a garden is surrounded with trees, the trees stand around it; they do not necessarily fill its entire interior.

This relationship appears clearly in Al-Kahf 18:32.

However, ḥaffa does not necessarily mean building a wall, blocking an entrance, or creating a defensive barrier. People may gather around someone without imprisoning him or placing him beyond other people’s reach.

Being situated around something should therefore be distinguished from preventing access to it.

The verb can describe a surrounding arrangement, but it does not by itself determine its exact geometry, the distances between things, or whether every route of entry is completely closed.

Ḥifāf: an edge or margin

In Arabic, «حِفَاف» denotes the edge or margin of something.

«حِفَافَا الشَّيْءِ» means its two sides. The expression is used of the head, a vessel, a mountain, land, and other things.

For example, the two sides of a vessel or the two edges of a path may be called its ḥifāf.

Here, the edges themselves are in view, rather than necessarily something besieging it from outside.

The distinction matters. Ḥifāf may denote a thing’s boundary or margin, whereas «حَفَّ بِهِ» expresses the relationship of something else being situated around it.

In expressions concerning the human head, ḥifāf also denotes hair remaining around a bald area.

The image of edges is particularly clear here: part of the head is bald, and the remaining hair lies around it.

Both uses involve the spatial meaning of edges and surroundings, but surrounding usages should not all be restricted to hair or the human head.

Kitāb al-ʿAyn: edges, sound, dryness, and surrounding

A poetic witness from Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd

The Muʿallaqah of the pre-Islamic poet Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd includes the following verse:

كَأَنَّ جَنَاحَيْ مَضْرَحِيٍّ تَكَنَّفَا
حِفَافَيْهِ شُكَّا فِي الْعَسِيبِ بِمِسْرَدِ

ka-anna janāḥay maḍraḥiyyin takannafā / ḥifāfayhi shukkā fī al-ʿasībi bi-misradi

The verse means: “As though the two wings of a long-winged bird of prey flanked the camel’s tail on either side, stitched into its tailbone with an awl.” The poet likens the hair on the two sides of the tail to those wings.

«حِفَافَيْهِ» refers to the two edges. «تَكَنَّفَا» comes from a different root and expresses being situated on the two sides of something.

The witness matters because ḥifāf occurs in pre-Islamic poetry with the meaning of edges or the two sides of a thing.

The verse is recorded in the Muʿallaqah and its early commentaries, including Ibn al-Anbārī’s commentary. Some editions differ slightly in their readings, but the use of ḥifāf for two edges is clear.

Ibn al-Anbārī’s commentary: Ṭarafah’s witness and the camel-tail image

This provides useful historical evidence for the spatial branch of the root. It does not establish that every use of Ḥ F F must involve only two particular sides: other Arabic expressions allow a broader surrounding arrangement.

Lisān al-ʿArab, Ḍ R Ḥ: maḍraḥiyy and the long-winged bird

Ḥafīf: the sound of movement

Another branch of the root is «حَفِيف».

In Al-ʿAyn, ḥafīf denotes an audible sound of movement, such as the movement of a bird’s wings or branches, or something passing through the air.

«حَفَّ جَنَاحُ الطَّائِرِ» concerns the sound of a bird’s wing moving.

«حَفَّتِ الشَّجَرَةُ» may likewise describe a sound produced by moving leaves or branches.

Lisān al-ʿArab also records ḥafīf for the sound of a swiftly moving horse and the sound of camels moving.

All these examples concern a sound of movement, but the movement and the thing producing it differ.

Thus ḥafīf cannot be restricted to the sound of a bird’s wings, just as it cannot be treated as a general name for every kind of sound.

Its familiar feature is a sound arising from movement or contact between the parts of something.

Ibn Fāris identifies this semantic field as a principle distinct from surrounding. Shared root letters therefore do not justify claiming that ḥafīf originally means the sound of something circling another thing.

Such a connection cannot be demonstrated for all the witnesses.

Ḥafaf and ḥufūf: dryness and straitened living

In Arabic, «حُفُوف» can denote dryness and an absence of moisture or fat.

«حَفَّ شَعَرُهُ» is recorded for hair that has become dry or has gone without its usual oiling and care.

«سَوِيقٌ حَافٌّ» denotes dry sawīq, a preparation of ground grain, which has not been mixed with oil or another fatty substance.

For land, «حَفَّتِ الْأَرْضُ» is recorded when its vegetation has dried out.

Within this branch, «حَفَف» and «حُفُوف» have also been used for straitened means and hardship in living.

«هُمْ فِي حَفَفٍ مِنَ الْعَيْشِ» means that they live in need and hardship.

The relationship between dryness and difficult living conditions is understandable: in everyday life, food shortages, lack of fat, and dried vegetation may accompany poverty and need.

This explanation should not become a claim that every poverty-related use of this root necessarily arose from a particular climatic condition or historical drought.

Ibn Fāris makes hardship in living the third principle and discusses dryness within that branch; he does not reduce it, sound, and surrounding to a single meaning.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: dryness and straitened living within the third principle

Al-ʿAyn attributes the following rajaz lines to Ruʾbah ibn al-ʿAjjāj:

قَالَتْ سُلَيْمَى إِذْ رَأَتْ حُفُوفِي
مَعَ اضْطِرَابِ اللَّحْمِ وَالشُّفُوفِ

qālat sulaymā idh raʾat ḥufūfī / maʿa iḍṭirābi al-laḥmi wa-l-shufūfi

Translation of the two hemistichs: “Sulaymā, when she saw my dryness and worn condition, with trembling flesh and emaciation, said…” These hemistichs begin a sentence; Sulaymā’s speech is not completed in this quotation.

Al-ʿAyn explains «حُفُوفِي» through dryness. In a report from Ibn al-Aʿrābī, the same witness illustrates loss of hearing. It should therefore not be confined to one reading without identifying the source.

Al-ʿAyn and its editors: the ḥufūf witness and reference to Ruʾbah’s collected poetry

Ruʾbah was a poet of the Islamic period, but the attribution of these particular lines is not uniform: Al-ʿAyn assigns them to him, and its editors refer to his collected poetry, whereas Al-Ṣaghānī rejects that attribution in Al-ʿUbāb. The witness should therefore not be presented with certain attribution or as pre-Islamic poetry.

Al-ʿUbāb al-Zākhir: disputed attribution and the loss-of-hearing reading

Trimming and removing hair

Lexical sources record «حَفَّ الشَّعْرَ» for trimming or removing hair.

«حَفَّتِ الْمَرْأَةُ وَجْهَهَا» is used of a woman removing her facial hair.

«احْتَفَّ النَّبْتَ» likewise concerns cutting or clipping vegetation.

These uses involve reducing or removing something. Connections with diminution, dryness, or removing a covering may be explainable, but one definite historical origin should not be invented for all of them.

«الحُفَافَة» denotes plucked hair or hair that falls away after trimming, and also remaining straw or fodder. It should not be defined generally as “food left after eating.” «احْتِفَاف», meaning eating all the contents of a pot, is a different usage.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Lisān al-ʿArab, and Tāj al-ʿArūs: ḥufāfah, fodder, and hair clippings

These words do not occur in the Qur’an, but they show the range of Arabic usage and prevent defining Ḥ F F exclusively through surrounding.

Serving and attending to another

Arabic records «يَحُفُّنَا وَيَرُفُّنَا» for attending to, caring for, and serving others.

Some lexicographers explain it through service and meeting needs.

In this use, a person attends to another, working and serving around him.

A connection with gathering around a person is conceivable, but this use should not become the principal meaning of every form of the root.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: attending to and serving another

In Al-Zumar 39:75 in particular, the angels are described as ḥāffīn. The word alone does not establish “servants” as its direct meaning. Their role in the scene should be distinguished from the word’s spatial meaning.

2. «وَحَفَفْنَاهُمَا بِنَخْلٍ»: Al-Kahf 18:32

وَاضْرِبْ لَهُمْ مَثَلًا رَجُلَيْنِ جَعَلْنَا لِأَحَدِهِمَا جَنَّتَيْنِ مِنْ أَعْنَابٍ وَحَفَفْنَاهُمَا بِنَخْلٍ وَجَعَلْنَا بَيْنَهُمَا زَرْعًا

wa-ḍrib lahum mathalan rajulayni jaʿalnā li-aḥadihimā jannatayni min aʿnābin wa-ḥafafnāhumā bi-nakhlin wa-jaʿalnā baynahumā zarʿan

Translation: And set forth for them the example of two men: We gave one of them two vineyards, surrounded them with date palms, and placed cultivated land between them.

Al-Kahf 18:32

«حَفَفْنَاهُمَا» is a first-person plural past-tense verb from the unaugmented form I verb «حَفَّ».

It is formed from the doubled root Ḥ F F. In «حَفَفْنَا», the two root letters fāʾ appear separately, and the suffix nā marks the subject.

The object pronoun humā refers to the two gardens.

Thus in «وَحَفَفْنَاهُمَا بِنَخْلٍ», the gardens are what is surrounded, and the date palms are what has been placed around them.

The two gardens and the trees around them

The verse describes two vineyards surrounded with date palms, with cultivated land between them.

Three parts can be distinguished in this image: the vineyards, the surrounding date palms, and the cultivated land between the gardens.

«حَفَفْنَاهُمَا» concerns the palms’ relationship to the gardens, rather than the gardens’ produce or the cultivated land between them.

The palms have been placed around the gardens. This arrangement can create a flourishing, productive landscape, since the agricultural holding includes several kinds of produce.

The verb ḥafafnāhumā itself, however, does not mean developing the land, making it fertile, or increasing its yield. Those features come from the overall picture and the following verses.

Al-Kahf 18:33 states that both gardens yielded their produce and that a river was made to flow between them.

This information completes the picture of the flourishing gardens, while Ḥ F F still denotes being situated around something and surrounding it.

Were the palms a wall or defensive enclosure?

Some translations convey the image through expressions such as “a fence of palms” or “a wall of date palms.”

Such expressions can provide an approximate image of trees standing around a garden, but the literary image should be distinguished from the verb’s direct meaning.

Ḥafafnāhumā does not itself state that the palms formed a continuous, impenetrable, defensive wall.

The trees may have been planted in an orderly arrangement along the gardens’ margins, but their spacing, number, height, and precise arrangement are not specified.

Nor does the verse say that the palms were placed there to prevent people or animals from entering.

“We surrounded them” or “We placed palms around them” is therefore more precise than “We built an impenetrable barrier of palms.”

Any protective function of the trees should be considered as an agricultural possibility, distinct from the lexical meaning.

The role of the preposition bi-

In «بِنَخْلٍ», the preposition bi- occurs with the word nakhl, date palms.

This construction identifies the palms as what surrounds the gardens: they have been situated around them.

Here bi- does not mean “between.” For the cultivated land between the gardens, the verse uses the separate expression «بَيْنَهُمَا».

The two relationships should therefore remain distinct: the palms are around the gardens, and the cultivated land is between them.

The verse’s own structure provides this distinction without requiring hypothetical details about the gardens’ layout.

Ibn ʿAṭiyyah, Al-Kahf 18:32: surrounding palms and ḥafafnāhumā

The garden image in the story’s context

This verse opens a parable about two men. One has been given gardens, wealth, and abundant resources.

He goes on to speak about his wealth and position, supposing that his garden will never perish.

Al-Kahf 18:42 describes the destruction of the garden’s yield and its owner’s regret over what has happened.

This context shows that the initial description of the gardens depicts the man’s worldly prosperity.

However, ḥafafnāhumā should not be translated as pride, hoarding wealth, or attachment to worldly life. The verb describes only one feature of the gardens’ physical arrangement.

The story’s moral follows from the owner’s words and actions and their outcome, rather than from the meaning of Ḥ F F itself.

3. «حَافِّينَ مِنْ حَوْلِ الْعَرْشِ»: Al-Zumar 39:75

وَتَرَى الْمَلَائِكَةَ حَافِّينَ مِنْ حَوْلِ الْعَرْشِ يُسَبِّحُونَ بِحَمْدِ رَبِّهِمْ وَقُضِيَ بَيْنَهُمْ بِالْحَقِّ وَقِيلَ الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ رَبِّ الْعَالَمِينَ

wa-tarā al-malāʾikata ḥāffīna min ḥawli al-ʿarshi yusabbiḥūna bi-ḥamdi rabbihim wa-quḍiya baynahum bi-l-ḥaqqi wa-qīla al-ḥamdu li-llāhi rabbi al-ʿālamīna

Translation: And you see the angels gathered around the Throne, glorifying their Lord with praise. Judgment is passed among them in truth, and it is said: “Praise belongs to The God, Lord of the worlds.”

Al-Zumar 39:75

«حَافِّينَ» is the sound masculine plural of «حَافّ», the active participle of the unaugmented form I verb «حَفَّ».

It is accusative here and describes the angels’ state when they are seen.

In «وَتَرَى الْمَلَائِكَةَ حَافِّينَ», the angels are the object of seeing, and ḥāffīn states their condition.

«مِنْ حَوْلِ الْعَرْشِ» identifies what they are situated around.

Surrounding and being situated around something

In this context, ḥāffīn means those situated around something.

Al-ʿAyn cites this very verse to explain «حَفَّ الْقَوْمُ بِسَيِّدِهِمْ»: a group gathering around its leader.

This lexical witness shows that ḥāffīn does not by itself mean worshipping or glorifying. It describes the angels’ position and arrangement.

The following expression, «يُسَبِّحُونَ بِحَمْدِ رَبِّهِمْ», explains their glorification.

Two matters are therefore expressed separately: the angels being around the Throne, and their glorifying their Lord with praise.

Translating ḥāffīn as “those glorifying” or “worshippers” merges the following verb’s meaning with this word’s meaning.

Does ḥāffīn mean a complete ring?

Ḥāffīn and min ḥawli al-ʿarsh present an image of angels around the Throne.

The Persian expression ḥalqah zadah-and, “have formed a ring,” is natural and conveys gathering around something.

Nevertheless, the expression alone does not determine the number of rings, their exact geometry, the angels’ distance from the Throne, or whether a circle is completely closed.

Arabic can describe people surrounding something or standing around it without detailing their exact formation.

Min ḥawl likewise denotes the surroundings; it does not explain the Throne’s physical structure or directions in familiar terrestrial coordinates.

Accordingly, “situated around the Throne” preserves the direct meaning without imposing further details on the verse.

Ḥāffīn and bearing the Throne

Ghāfir 40:7 mentions those who bear the Throne and those who are around it.

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:17 also describes bearing the Throne in a scene of the hereafter.

These verses matter for studying the Qur’anic uses of ʿarsh, but bearing should not be inserted into the meaning of ḥāffīn in Al-Zumar 39:75.

Ḥāffīn concerns being situated around something; «يَحْمِلُونَ» comes from Ḥ M L and concerns bearing or carrying.

Both groups or roles may appear in a Qur’anic scene, but ḥāffīn alone does not establish that the angels in question must be the bearers of the Throne.

The distinction should be preserved.

The Throne and the limits of the root’s meaning

ʿArsh has its own uses in Arabic and the Qur’an, and its meaning requires a separate study of ʿ R Sh.

In Al-Zumar 39:75, Ḥ F F contributes the surrounding relationship between the angels and the Throne.

This root does not determine the Throne’s shape, material, size, physical location, or manner of existence.

Nor does its use in a scene of the hereafter by itself establish that angels have bodies like human bodies or stand in a space governed by familiar earthly laws.

The verse presents a scene of judgment, angels, and their glorification. The direct meaning of ḥāffīn should be preserved, while details of the unseen require independent evidence.

Ḥāffīn at the surah’s conclusion

Al-Zumar 39:71 describes the fate of those who disbelieved. In Al-Zumar 39:73, those mindful of their Lord are led toward the Garden.

Al-Zumar 39:74 then presents the speech of the Garden’s inhabitants and their praise.

Al-Zumar 39:75 closes with the image of angels around the Throne and their glorification, and speaks of judgment in truth and praise of the Lord of the worlds.

This sequence shows that the angels’ presence at the surah’s conclusion belongs to the broader scene of divine judgment.

Ḥāffīn itself, however, does not mean judging. Judgment is expressed separately through «وَقُضِيَ بَيْنَهُمْ بِالْحَقِّ».

Nor should the pronoun in «بَيْنَهُمْ» necessarily be restricted to the angels without examining the context: the preceding verses also discuss human groups and their destinies.

The contribution of Ḥ F F is therefore clear: describing the angels around the Throne. Other words and constructions express the scene’s other features.

Al-Ṭabarī, Al-Zumar 39:75: surrounding angels and judgment among creation

4. The relationship between the two Qur’anic uses

Comparing Al-Kahf 18:32 with Al-Zumar 39:75 shows that both uses depend on a spatial relationship despite their different subjects.

In Al-Kahf, palms have been placed around two gardens. In Al-Zumar, angels are described around the Throne.

The first use employs the verb «حَفَفْنَاهُمَا», expressing an action in which the palms have been placed around the gardens.

In the second, «حَافِّينَ» describes the angels and states their surrounding position in the scene.

The distinction between verb and active participle matters here. In Al-Kahf, the verb concerns placing the palms around the gardens; in Al-Zumar, the participle concerns the angels’ state.

In both cases, something is around something else. What surrounds and what lies among the surroundings, however, differ.

A single fixed referent such as “wall,” “guard,” or “closed formation” cannot therefore be assigned to both uses.

In neither verse does the root itself mean protecting or preventing access. Palms may sometimes offer protection, and angels have various duties in other verses, but those features do not follow from Ḥ F F itself.

Brief root meaning: In Qur’anic usage, being situated or placing something around another thing.

This definition is specific enough to explain both verses, but it should not define every Arabic use of the root: the branches of ḥafīf and ḥufūf also exist.

5. Distinctions from related roots

Ḥ F F (ḥaffa) and Ḥ W Ṭ (ḥāṭa)

Ḥ W Ṭ and «إحاطة» concern encompassing and surrounding.

In its spatial branch, Ḥ F F also denotes being situated around something.

In the Qur’an, however, iḥāṭah can extend beyond spatial arrangement, as in divine knowledge encompassing things or a condition encompassing people.

The two Qur’anic uses of Ḥ F F instead express a surrounding relationship in two particular images.

This does not mean that Ḥ F F can have no broader uses in Arabic; it identifies the field of surroundings within which its Qur’anic uses occur.

Surrounding is therefore an appropriate equivalent in Al-Kahf and Al-Zumar, but not every meaning of Ḥ W Ṭ should be transferred to Ḥ F F.

Study of Ḥ W Ṭ

Ḥ F F (ḥaffa) and Ṭ W F (ṭāfa)

Ṭawāf concerns going round something and moving around it.

Ḥ F F can describe simply being situated around something and does not necessarily imply circular movement.

For example, palms around two gardens do not move around them, yet ḥafafnāhumā is used of them.

This demonstrates that ḥaffa in its Qur’anic meaning does not require circular movement.

In Al-Zumar 39:75 as well, ḥāffīn denotes the angels being around the Throne. The word alone does not establish that they are continuously circling it.

Being around something and going round it are related but distinct concepts.

Study of Ṭ W F

Ḥ F F (ḥaffa) and Ḥ D Q (ḥadaqa)

In Arabic, Ḥ D Q also concerns surrounding and something being situated around another thing.

«أحدق به» can mean encompassing or surrounding it.

This use is close in meaning to «حفّ به», but the roots should not be treated as one.

The similarity can help explain surrounding in Ḥ F F, but features of the eye, its socket, or other derivatives of Ḥ D Q should not be transferred to it.

Study of Ḥ D Q

Ḥ F F (ḥaffa) and Kh F F (khaffa)

These roots look similar in writing, but their first letters differ.

Ḥ F F begins with ḥāʾ and concerns surrounding in its Qur’anic branch.

Kh F F begins with khāʾ and concerns lightness, becoming lighter, and reducing heaviness.

Qur’anic forms such as «خَفِيف» and «تَخْفِيف» belong to the latter root.

Ḥāffīn in Al-Zumar 39:75 should therefore not be confused with khafīf or lightness.

Nor should dryness and scarcity in some uses of Ḥ F F automatically be equated with lightness in Kh F F.

Study of Kh F F

Ḥ F F (ḥaffa) and Ḥ F Ẓ (ḥafiẓa)

Ḥ F Ẓ concerns keeping and protecting.

In Qur’anic usage, Ḥ F F concerns being situated around something.

Someone may guard a valuable thing from a surrounding position, but that situation involves two separate concepts: being around it and performing protection.

Ḥāffīn therefore does not by itself mean guards.

Nor does ḥafafnāhumā bi-nakhlin necessarily mean complete protection of the gardens.

A barrier or protection may result from some surrounding arrangements, but it should not be inserted into the root’s lexical definition.

Study of Ḥ F Ẓ

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

In Al-Kahf 18:32, «وَحَفَفْنَاهُمَا بِنَخْلٍ» can be translated:

“And We surrounded both of them with date palms.”

This rendering preserves the verb’s direct meaning and fits the Arabic construction.

“And We placed date palms around both of them” is also clear and natural, although the first rendering is closer to the verb’s structure.

Either rendering should make clear that humā refers to the two gardens.

“We protected the two gardens with a wall of palms” adds details not expressed by the word itself. The word concerns palms around the gardens, rather than necessarily constructing a defensive barrier.

In Al-Zumar 39:75, «حَافِّينَ مِنْ حَوْلِ الْعَرْشِ» can be rendered:

“Situated around the Throne.”

“Gathered in a ring around the Throne” is also natural, provided that forming a ring conveys gathering around something without implying an exact, fixed geometry.

“The angels around the Throne” also works in a fluent translation, but “the angels situated around the Throne” makes the active participle’s contribution clearer.

Ḥāffīn should not be changed into “those circumambulating,” “bearers of the Throne,” or “guards of the Throne”: none of these meanings follows from this derivative alone.

Outside the Qur’an, ḥifāf can be translated as “edge” or “margin,” ḥafīf as “sound of movement” or, where appropriate, “rustling,” and ḥafaf and ḥufūf as “dryness” or “straitened living.”

In the hair-related use, «حَفَّ الشَّعْرَ» means “trimmed the hair” or “removed the hair.”

These differences show that translation must follow the derivative and context, rather than selecting one fixed equivalent for the entire lexical family.

Conclusion

Ḥ F F has three familiar semantic branches in Arabic: the sound of movement, surrounding and being situated around something, and dryness and hardship in living.

In the first, ḥafīf denotes a sound produced by the movement of a bird’s wings, branches, wind, or other things.

In the second, ḥaffa concerns being around something, and ḥifāf concerns its edges and margins. This use is also attested in pre-Islamic poetry.

In the third, ḥafaf and ḥufūf concern dryness, scarcity, and straitened living. Uses concerning dry hair, food without added fat, and difficult living conditions belong to this field.

Only the surrounding branch occurs in the Qur’an.

In Al-Kahf 18:32, «وَحَفَفْنَاهُمَا» states that date palms have been placed around two vineyards. In Al-Zumar 39:75, «حَافِّينَ» describes angels situated around the Throne.

The shared meaning is a relationship in which something is situated or placed around another thing.

This meaning does not itself express circular movement, protection, wall-building, completely closed access routes, or a particular geometry.

“Surrounding” and “being situated around” are therefore the most appropriate Qur’anic equivalents. Studying the two occurrences shows that the surrounding relationship remains the same while its subjects and contexts differ.

This definition preserves the verses’ direct meaning while preventing other Arabic branches, such as ḥafīf and ḥufūf, from being merged into the Qur’anic meaning of Ḥ F F without evidence.
