Miserliness, greed, and withholding: from clinging to personal advantage to refusing to share good or respect others' rights

In early Arabic, SH Ḥ Ḥ denotes withholding something to whose retention or acquisition a person is strongly attached. It connects two features: holding back or refusing to give, and eagerness to retain or possess something.

In pre-Islamic Arabic, shaḥīḥ described someone reluctant to part with wealth or personal advantage. Shuḥḥ named this disposition. Its scope nevertheless extends beyond money: people may withhold opportunities, privileges, shares, or other benefits they desire.

Dictionaries sometimes equate shuḥḥ with bukhl, and sometimes define it as miserliness combined with greed, or more intense miserliness. These definitions overlap, but shuḥḥ can emphasize attachment to retaining or acquiring a benefit as well as refusal to give.

The root occurs five times in four Quranic verses: An-Nisa 4:128, Al-Ahzab 33:19, Al-Hashr 59:9, and At-Taghabun 64:16. Its Quranic forms are shuḥḥ and ashiḥḥah.

Quranic usage extends beyond refusing to give money. The disposition can emerge in marriage, sharing benefits, helping others, responding to need, and preferring personal desires over goodness and justice.

1. Shuḥḥ in early Arabic

A witness from the Muʿallaqah of Amr ibn Kulthum

In his Muʿallaqah, the pre-Islamic poet Amr ibn Kulthum describes wine's effect on a person:

تَرَى اللَّحِزَ الشَّحِيحَ إِذَا أُمِرَّتْ
عَلَيْهِ لِمَالِهِ فِيهَا مُهِينَا

tarā al-laḥiza al-shaḥīḥa idhā umirrat / ʿalayhi li-mālihi fīhā muhīnā

The verse conveys:

“You see the grasping miser, when wine is passed around to him, treating his wealth as of little account for its sake.”

Here al-shaḥīḥ describes someone who clings to wealth and is reluctant to spend it.

Al-laḥiz likewise describes a grasping, ungenerous person in this context. Together, the descriptions depict someone unwilling to part readily with wealth.

The poet describes this person's changed attitude under the influence of wine: he comes to treat his wealth as expendable. The verse should not be reduced to a general claim about spending on anything one strongly desires.

The witness offers two semantic observations.

First, shaḥīḥ denotes miserliness and withholding, rather than poverty or financial inability. A miser may possess wealth but resist parting with it.

Second, shuḥḥ does not mean an absolute inability to spend. The poem depicts a change under wine's influence. Spending generously on one's own desires while withholding help from others is a possible separate example, rather than the poem's exact assertion.

The amount spent alone therefore does not identify shuḥḥ. A person's relationship to wealth and benefit, and conduct when personal desires conflict with others' needs or rights, also matter.

This verse, attributed to a pre-Islamic poet, supports the sense of miserliness and attachment to wealth. Attribution alone does not establish its precise date. Extension to rights and social relations must be assessed through other usage, especially Quranic context.

Competition over an advantage

The attested expression tashāḥḥa al-rajulāni ʿalā al-amr describes two people contesting something that neither wishes to relinquish.

The issue is not merely refusing to give existing possessions: each party seeks to retain or acquire the benefit or privilege concerned.

This expression illustrates the competitive branch. In disputes over a right, opportunity, or benefit, eagerness to retain or acquire it can produce unwillingness to yield to another.

Its recording in later dictionaries does not itself establish when the expression became current before Islam. The poem attributed to Amr ibn Kulthum offers a more direct witness for early shaḥīḥ; tashāḥḥa helps establish the family's attested semantic range.

Figurative uses

Arabic also records zand shaḥāḥ. A zand is a stick used in traditional fire-making; this description refers to one that does not readily produce fire.

The stick is portrayed as though withholding fire.

Māʾ shaḥāḥ is also recorded for scanty or difficult-to-obtain water. Accounts of related land descriptions differ and should not be treated as identical without examination.

Such uses show how a human disposition of withholding can be extended to other things.

They do not establish that the root historically began with fire sticks, scarce water, or a closed object. The evidence is insufficient for that origin claim, and some poetic examples of these extensions come from poets after Islam.

A defensible account of the family is withholding coupled with strong attachment to retaining or acquiring something. Figurative uses illuminate this meaning but do not replace its definition or historical evidence.

2. Forms and semantic core

SH Ḥ Ḥ is a doubled Arabic root: its final two consonants are identical.

Its principal forms include:

  • Shuḥḥ: miserliness and withholding combined with eagerness to retain or acquire something; three Quranic occurrences.
  • Shaḥḥa / yashiḥḥu: to withhold or resist giving or relinquishing. Other imperfect vocalizations are recorded in Arabic dialects.
  • Shaḥīḥ: miserly or strongly withholding; a person with that disposition.
  • Shaḥāḥ: a description close to shaḥīḥ, also used figuratively.
  • Ashiḥḥah: a plural of shaḥīḥ; miserly or withholding people; two Quranic occurrences.
  • Ashiḥḥāʾ and shiḥāḥ: other recorded plurals of shaḥīḥ.
  • Tashāḥḥa: to contest something with another, resisting relinquishment.
  • Mushāḥḥah: contention or mutual insistence over a right or advantage.

Only the noun shuḥḥ and plural description ashiḥḥah occur in the Quran. The family's verbs and the forms shaḥīḥ and tashāḥḥ do not occur there in those forms.

The semantic core can be expressed as follows:

Shuḥḥ is an insistent attachment to retaining or acquiring something, accompanied by withholding and resistance to relinquishing it.

This distinguishes it from simply valuing possessions or benefits. One can love something, work to preserve it, or defend a legitimate right without shuḥḥ.

Shuḥḥ emerges when attachment becomes grasping, self-centered withholding, especially resistance to goodness, fairness, or others' rights.

Each Quranic context must identify the object of attachment and resulting conduct. Its instances cannot all be restricted in advance to money or charitable giving.

3. Quranic uses

An-Nisa 4:128: marriage and competing interests

وَإِنِ امْرَأَةٌ خَافَتْ مِن بَعْلِهَا نُشُوزًا أَوْ إِعْرَاضًا فَلَا جُنَاحَ عَلَيْهِمَا أَن يُصْلِحَا بَيْنَهُمَا صُلْحًا ۚ وَالصُّلْحُ خَيْرٌ ۗ وَأُحْضِرَتِ الْأَنفُسُ الشُّحَّ ۚ وَإِن تُحْسِنُوا وَتَتَّقُوا فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ كَانَ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ خَبِيرًا

wa-in imraʾatun khāfat min baʿlihā nushūzan aw iʿrāḍan fa-lā junāḥa ʿalayhimā an yuṣliḥā baynahumā ṣulḥan wa-al-ṣulḥu khayrun wa-uḥḍirati al-anfusu al-shuḥḥa wa-in tuḥsinū wa-tattaqū fa-inna Allāha kāna bi-mā taʿmalūna khabīrā

Al-Nisāʾ 4:128

“If a woman fears estrangement or aversion from her husband, there is no blame on them for reaching a settlement between themselves; settlement is better. Grasping selfishness has been brought before souls. If you act well and remain mindful, The God is fully aware of what you do.”

The verse concerns a woman fearing her husband's estrangement or aversion and the possibility of settlement between them.

It then states:

وَأُحْضِرَتِ الْأَنفُسُ الشُّحَّ

wa-uḥḍirati al-anfusu al-shuḥḥ

Al-Nisāʾ 4:128

“Souls have been confronted with grasping selfishness.”

Here shuḥḥ is not confined to retaining money. Marriage can involve strong attachments concerning affection, attention, companionship, financial resources, family rights, privileges, or one's share in a common life.

Such attachments can produce conflict, especially when two people disagree over something each desires.

Yet having a right and claiming it are not inherently shuḥḥ.

A woman's demand that her husband respect her legitimate rights cannot simply be called miserliness or greed. Likewise, a man's claim to a legitimate right is not in itself shuḥḥ.

The description becomes relevant when attachment to personal desires or advantages overrides fairness, kindness, and the other party's rights.

The verse uses the plural al-anfus, souls, rather than “the woman's soul” or “the man's soul.” Its general statement cannot be made an exclusively female or male trait without evidence.

Both spouses may be susceptible, though its expression can differ.

A husband might seek the benefits of marriage while withholding his responsibilities to his wife. Either party might also cling to a desire so strongly as to exclude any fair settlement.

Insistence on a right must be distinguished from selfish insistence. Freely and knowingly relinquishing a right must likewise be distinguished from concessions extracted through pressure, threats, or fear of deprivation.

“Settlement is better” does not license an oppressive settlement. The verse follows its reminder of shuḥḥ with a call to goodness and mindfulness; settlement must be understood alongside kindness, responsibility, and mindfulness of The God.

Uḥḍirat is a passive Form IV verb. In the common grammatical analysis, al-anfus is the passive subject and al-shuḥḥ the second object.

This construction describes the presence of shuḥḥ before souls. It does not itself establish that people are condemned to this disposition throughout life or unable to reform it.

Al-Hashr 59:9 and At-Taghabun 64:16 speak of protection from one's own shuḥḥ, showing that freedom from its dominance is possible and commendable.

Al-Ahzab 33:19: withholding assistance and pursuing benefits

Al-Ahzab describes a group who avoid genuine participation in danger.

Al-Ahzab 33:18 says they discourage others and participate in battle only a little.

Al-Ahzab 33:19 begins:

أَشِحَّةً عَلَيْكُمْ

ashiḥḥatan ʿalaykum

Al-Aḥzāb 33:19

“Withholding toward you.”

It then describes their fear:

فَإِذَا جَاءَ الْخَوْفُ رَأَيْتَهُمْ يَنظُرُونَ إِلَيْكَ تَدُورُ أَعْيُنُهُمْ كَالَّذِي يُغْشَىٰ عَلَيْهِ مِنَ الْمَوْتِ

fa-idhā jāʾa al-khawfu raʾaytahum yanẓurūna ilayka tadūru aʿyunuhum ka-alladhī yughshā ʿalayhi mina al-mawt

Al-Aḥzāb 33:19

“When fear comes, you see them looking at you, their eyes rolling like someone fainting from the pangs of death.”

It continues:

فَإِذَا ذَهَبَ الْخَوْفُ سَلَقُوكُم بِأَلْسِنَةٍ حِدَادٍ أَشِحَّةً عَلَى الْخَيْرِ

fa-idhā dhahaba al-khawfu salaqūkum bi-alsinatin ḥidādin ashiḥḥatan ʿalā al-khayr

Al-Aḥzāb 33:19

“When fear departs, they assail you with sharp tongues, grasping over good.”

Ashiḥḥatan appears twice, with different complements.

The first phrase concerns their conduct toward believers, in a context of discouragement and avoidance of danger.

Withholding can here involve assistance, companionship, effort, and a fair share of responsibility.

In the second phrase, ashiḥḥatan ʿalā al-khayr, good or benefit is the object concerned.

Some early explanations understand al-khayr here as wealth or spoils. This fits people who avoid danger but aggressively demand benefits afterward.

Al-khayr is not, however, restricted to spoils throughout the Quran. The phrase can describe withholding good from others or grasping eagerness for benefits. Battle and the group's conduct make seeking advantages while avoiding participation prominent instances.

Both features can coexist: someone refuses a share of responsibility but demands a larger share of benefit.

For example, a person might withhold time, money, or effort from a collective undertaking, then insist on its profits or privileges after success.

This fits the semantic field because the person wants the benefit while withholding a fair contribution or share.

Such examples are applications, not lexical definitions. Nor should everyone absent from battle be classified under the verse without considering circumstances and actual responsibilities. The Quran describes a particular group amid discouragement, fear, harsh speech, and withholding.

Ashiḥḥah thus reaches beyond individual financial behavior into social relations and the distribution of responsibilities and benefits.

Al-Hashr 59:9: preferring others and protection from shuḥḥ

Al-Hashr describes people who love the emigrants, feel no inward desire over what they have been given, and prefer others to themselves.

Part of the verse states:

وَيُؤْثِرُونَ عَلَىٰ أَنفُسِهِمْ وَلَوْ كَانَ بِهِمْ خَصَاصَةٌ

wa-yuʾthirūna ʿalā anfusihim wa-law kāna bihim khaṣāṣah

Al-Ḥashr 59:9

“They prefer others to themselves, even though they themselves are in need.”

It then states:

وَمَن يُوقَ شُحَّ نَفْسِهِ فَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْمُفْلِحُونَ

wa-man yūqa shuḥḥa nafsihi fa-ulāʾika humu al-mufliḥūn

Al-Ḥashr 59:9

“Whoever is protected from the grasping selfishness of their own soul—those are the successful.”

This is a particularly clear use, juxtaposing shuḥḥ with willingness to relinquish personal benefits or resources to help others.

Yuʾthirūna belongs to ʾ TH R and here means preferring others to oneself.

By contrast, one's own shuḥḥ can produce attachment to personal advantage that resists sharing even when others are genuinely in need.

The verse does not describe only giving from abundance: “even though they themselves are in need” makes the givers' own need explicit.

The significance lies in recognizing another's need and preferring that person on a particular occasion despite one's own need.

This does not establish a universal requirement to sacrifice all one's possessions or the necessities of one's family. The verse praises altruism without calling preservation of legitimate needs shuḥḥ.

Shuḥḥ is not identical to possessing wealth. A wealthy person can respect rights, assist the needy, and avoid monopolizing advantages. Someone with few possessions may nevertheless exhibit shuḥḥ over a desired opportunity, privilege, or benefit.

The preceding context also matters. Al-Hashr 59:7 says concerning wealth and its distribution:

كَيْ لَا يَكُونَ دُولَةً بَيْنَ الْأَغْنِيَاءِ مِنكُمْ

kay lā yakūna dūlatan bayna al-aghniyāʾi minkum

Al-Ḥashr 59:7

“So that it does not circulate only among the wealthy among you.”

That verse concerns wealth's circulation and distribution; Al-Hashr 59:9 describes people responding to others' needs with love, generosity, and altruism.

Freedom from shuḥḥ thus matters personally, economically, and socially, though the word itself names neither an economic system nor detailed property rules.

Shuḥḥa nafsihi concerns one's own soul. The subject is not merely condemning other misers: each person should watch for this disposition within themselves.

Yūqa is passive, from W Q Y, and denotes being protected or preserved.

The verse does not say that success belongs only to someone who never desires personal advantage. It speaks of someone protected from their own shuḥḥ.

Having desires and interests therefore differs from being dominated by this disposition.

At-Taghabun 64:16: giving and freedom from shuḥḥ within one's capacity

At-Taghabun states:

فَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ مَا اسْتَطَعْتُمْ وَاسْمَعُوا وَأَطِيعُوا وَأَنفِقُوا خَيْرًا لِأَنفُسِكُمْ ۗ وَمَن يُوقَ شُحَّ نَفْسِهِ فَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْمُفْلِحُونَ

fa-ittaqū Allāha mā istaṭaʿtum wa-ismaʿū wa-aṭīʿū wa-anfiqū khayran li-anfusikum wa-man yūqa shuḥḥa nafsihi fa-ulāʾika humu al-mufliḥūn

Al-Taghābun 64:16

“Be mindful of The God as far as you are able; listen, obey, and give—it is better for yourselves. Whoever is protected from the grasping selfishness of their own soul—those are the successful.”

The ending of Al-Hashr 59:9 recurs here in a different setting.

In Al-Hashr it accompanies altruism and preferring the needy. In At-Taghabun it follows commands to mindfulness, listening, obedience, and giving.

Here shuḥḥ is particularly connected with withholding expenditure and resisting the relinquishment of wealth or resources.

The commands occur alongside mā istaṭaʿtum: mindfulness of The God as far as one's ability permits.

Grammatically, this qualification directly modifies fa-ittaqū. Its place at the beginning of the commands nevertheless situates the discussion within responsibility and actual ability.

Freedom from shuḥḥ does not mean disregarding necessities, genuine inability, or responsibilities toward family and other rights-holders.

Someone with limited income who must provide food, housing, treatment, and other family necessities is not a miser merely for being unable to give as much as a wealthy person.

Conversely, someone with abundant resources may strongly resist appropriate financial responsibility or assistance.

The issue is not the absolute amount given, but conduct in relation to capacity, need, responsibility, and others' rights.

At-Taghabun 64:17 then speaks of a good loan. This connection confirms the importance of giving and directing wealth toward good in this passage.

Freedom from one's own shuḥḥ here means release from attachment that obstructs good and financial responsibility.

Both passages associate this protection with success. Quranic success is thus not confined to professions of faith or outward conduct: reform of inward tendencies also matters.

Study of F L H

4. Distinctions from related concepts

SH Ḥ Ḥ and B KH L: miserliness

Bukhl denotes withholding something one could give but is reluctant to relinquish.

The Quran uses this family especially concerning wealth, giving, and others' rights.

For example, Aal Imran 3:180 describes:

الَّذِينَ يَبْخَلُونَ بِمَا آتَاهُمُ اللَّهُ مِن فَضْلِهِ

alladhīna yabkhalūna bi-mā ātāhumu Allāhu min faḍlih

Āl ʿImrān 3:180

“Those who withhold what The God has given them from His bounty.”

Here bukhl concerns withholding something already possessed.

Shuḥḥ can convey this too, but many lexical explanations emphasize greed and strong attachment more prominently.

Some define it as miserliness with greed, while others consider it more intense than bukhl.

Another proposed distinction associates bukhl chiefly with retaining existing possessions, whereas shuḥḥ can extend to coveting something not yet possessed.

This helps explain some uses but should not become an absolute rule: the semantic fields overlap.

Al-Ahzab 33:19 can involve both withholding assistance and attachment to benefits. An-Nisa 4:128 likewise extends beyond financial possessions.

Bukhl tends to emphasize withholding behavior; shuḥḥ can also emphasize the inward attachment and greed accompanying it.

This distinction is not equally present in every sentence, but helps explain the families' range.

SH Ḥ Ḥ and Ḥ R Ṣ: eagerness

Ḥirṣ denotes intense desire and pursuit. Its object can be good or bad.

At-Tawbah 9:128 describes the messenger:

حَرِيصٌ عَلَيْكُمْ

ḥarīṣun ʿalaykum

Al-Tawbah 9:128

“Deeply concerned for you.”

Here eagerness concerns others' welfare and is commendable.

An-Nisa 4:129 likewise uses “even if you eagerly strive” in discussing the desire and effort to be equitable among wives.

Ḥ R Ṣ cannot therefore always be translated as blameworthy greed.

In shuḥḥ, eagerness is coupled with withholding and retaining or acquiring advantage. The person not only desires something strongly but resists relinquishing it.

Eagerness can thus contribute to shuḥḥ without every instance of eagerness being shuḥḥ.

A physician's determination to save a patient or a researcher's dedication to truth is not shuḥḥ merely because it is intense.

By contrast, disregarding others' rights or withholding good because of attachment to personal gain can fall within this field.

SH Ḥ Ḥ and Ḍ N N: withholding

Ḍann also denotes withholding and holding something tightly.

At-Takwir 81:24 has bi-ḍanīn in the reading transmitted by Hafs. Ḍanīn means withholding or miserly; the statement that he does not withhold comes from the sentence's negation, not from this adjective alone. Another reading has ẓanīn, meaning accused or suspect; the two readings must be distinguished.

Ḍann overlaps with shuḥḥ in withholding, but this does not make them identical in every use.

Shuḥḥ prominently connects withholding with strong eagerness to retain or acquire a benefit. In ḍann, refusal to relinquish or communicate something can be central.

This is a semantic tendency to examine, not an absolute boundary imposed on every occurrence.

SH Ḥ Ḥ and ʾ TH R: altruistic preference

Al-Hashr 59:9 juxtaposes preferring others with protection from one's own shuḥḥ.

This contrast illuminates the difference between clinging to one's advantage and willingly preferring another.

Yet not every failure to prefer others is shuḥḥ.

People may retain something to meet their own or their family's necessities. That is not inherently shuḥḥ.

Altruistic preference becomes particularly evident when someone knowingly and generously prefers another despite having a genuine right or need themselves.

In Al-Hashr, these dispositions point in different directions; between them are varied situations requiring consideration of justice, capacity, and responsibility.

5. From personal disposition to social consequences

The four Quranic verses show that shuḥḥ can emerge in different areas of life.

In families, attachment to a demand or privilege can eclipse fairness and mutual responsibility.

In collective undertakings, someone may avoid costs and risks but demand the largest share of the results.

Economically, individuals or groups may retain advantages while resisting others' receipt of their due.

Socially, it can appear as monopolizing opportunities, withholding help, or demanding privileges without responsibility.

Consider an official reserving public resources or jobs for associates while denying others equal opportunity. If driven by grasping attachment to the group's advantages and withholding from rightful recipients, this can exemplify social shuḥḥ.

Shuḥḥ names a disposition. Judging a particular person requires examining behavior, the right at issue, and evidence of motive and awareness. Disagreement over benefits or refusal of a request alone does not prove it.

This matters especially for rights. People should not surrender their rights or permit exploitation under the names of generosity and altruism simply to avoid being called miserly.

Conversely, appeals to rights and ownership should not conceal greed, monopoly, or withholding others' due.

The crucial distinction is: protecting a legitimate right is not shuḥḥ; protecting personal advantage at the expense of others' rights can be a clear instance of it.

By associating protection from shuḥḥ with success, the Quran gives its reform significance beyond personal virtue. Such conduct affects families, social relations, resource distribution, and economic justice.

Nevertheless, not every corruption or inequality should be attributed to shuḥḥ without investigation. It can contribute to such conduct; it does not define every kind of corruption.

6. Morphology, syntax, and translation proposals for Forqan

Shuḥḥ occurs three times as a noun in the Quran.

In An-Nisa 4:128, al-shuḥḥa is definite and accusative. Uḥḍirat is passive; in the common analysis, al-anfus is its passive subject and al-shuḥḥ its second object.

In Al-Hashr 59:9 and At-Taghabun 64:16, shuḥḥa is annexed to nafsihi in yūqa shuḥḥa nafsihi.

Yūqa belongs to W Q Y and means being protected. Shuḥḥ of the soul is consequently a disposition from whose dominance and consequences one is protected.

Ashiḥḥatan in Al-Ahzab 33:19 is an accusative plural of shaḥīḥ. A common analysis takes both occurrences as circumstantial descriptions of the group. Other analyses exist, including an accusative of censure for the first occurrence; the grammatical analysis should not be presented as exclusive.

The complements differ: ashiḥḥatan ʿalaykum concerns withholding toward the people addressed; ashiḥḥatan ʿalā al-khayr concerns good or benefit.

The preposition ʿalā alone does not decide between withholding and eager acquisition. Context and the complement establish that distinction.

Translation proposals

Shuḥḥ: “grasping selfishness,” “intense miserliness,” or “greed coupled with withholding.”

Shaḥīḥ: “miserly,” “grasping,” or “strongly withholding.”

Ashiḥḥah: “miserly people,” “grasping people,” or “those who withhold.”

Tashāḥḥ: “contending over a benefit or right,” “mutual withholding,” or “resisting relinquishment of a share.”

Mushāḥḥah: “contention and insistence over a benefit or right.”

The following renderings suit the Quranic uses:

An-Nisa 4:128

وَأُحْضِرَتِ الْأَنْفُسُ الشُّحَّ

wa-uḥḍirati al-anfusu al-shuḥḥ

Al-Nisāʾ 4:128

“Grasping selfishness has been brought before souls.”

Explanation should acknowledge attachment to retaining or acquiring rights and benefits without automatically calling legitimate claims shuḥḥ.

Al-Ahzab 33:19

أَشِحَّةً عَلَيْكُمْ

ashiḥḥatan ʿalaykum

Al-Aḥzāb 33:19

“Withholding toward you.”

And:

أَشِحَّةً عَلَى الْخَيْرِ

ashiḥḥatan ʿalā al-khayr

Al-Aḥzāb 33:19

“Grasping over good.”

Contextual explanation can address eager acquisition and withholding good. “Miserly” is familiar as a basic equivalent, but must not suggest that the verse concerns only money or charitable expenditure.

Al-Hashr 59:9 and At-Taghabun 64:16

وَمَن يُوقَ شُحَّ نَفْسِهِ فَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْمُفْلِحُونَ

wa-man yūqa shuḥḥa nafsihi fa-ulāʾika humu al-mufliḥūn

Al-Ḥashr 59:9

“Whoever is protected from the grasping selfishness of their own soul—those are the successful.”

The expression encompasses both miserliness and greed. Lexical explanation should clarify that it is more than giving little money: it is attachment that resists relinquishing good, benefit, or a fair share.

The basic translation should not pack all these explanations into one long phrase. Use a natural equivalent, leaving its fuller range to the root study and reflection on the verse.

7. Conclusion

Early Arabic associates SH Ḥ Ḥ with miserliness, withholding, and greed. Poetry attributed to the pre-Islamic period uses shaḥīḥ for someone who clings to wealth. Expressions of contention over benefits show that the family extends beyond refusal to give possessions.

The Quran uses shuḥḥ and ashiḥḥah.

An-Nisa 4:128 concerns marriage, settlement, and attachment to rights and benefits. Al-Ahzab 33:19 describes withholding participation and assistance while grasping over good. Al-Hashr 59:9 places protection from shuḥḥ beside preferring others. At-Taghabun 64:16 connects that protection with giving and mindfulness.

Together these uses extend beyond simply refusing to spend. A person may cling to acquiring or retaining wealth, privilege, opportunity, or advantage so strongly as to disregard others' welfare and rights.

Valuing possessions, earning a livelihood, safeguarding legitimate rights, and meeting necessities are not inherently shuḥḥ. Its prominent feature is strong attachment coupled with withholding and self-centeredness.

The defensible semantic core is strong attachment and eagerness to retain or acquire something, coupled with reluctance to relinquish it.

“Grasping selfishness,” “intense miserliness,” and “greed coupled with withholding” explain its Quranic uses. Translation must identify what is desired or withheld and the conduct in each context.

The Quran places protection from one's own shuḥḥ among the qualities of the successful. This does not mean losing one's rights, neglecting legitimate needs, or giving without measure; it means freedom from attachment that obstructs fairness, goodness, assistance, and respect for others' rights.
