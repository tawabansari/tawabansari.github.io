The Arabic root S K T concerns becoming silent and ceasing to speak. Sakata means “fell silent” or “stopped speaking,” sukūt means silence, and sākit describes someone who is not speaking.

In broader Arabic usage, this family also describes excitement subsiding, movement stopping, or an activity ceasing after it had been underway. Silence is therefore not restricted to a person closing their mouth; it may describe the cessation of a state previously expressed through intensity and agitation.

The root S K T occurs only once in the Quran, in Al-Aʿrāf 7:154. After Moses returns to his people and confronts their calf worship, the text says: “wa-lammā sakata ʿan Mūsā al-ghaḍab.”

Here anger is pictured as something that had been affecting Moses and whose activity and intensity have now subsided. The Quran does not say that Moses stopped speaking: al-ghaḍab itself is the subject of sakata.

This usage reveals an important distinction between a person's silence and an inner state subsiding. In the first, speech stops; in the second, the agitation and influence of a state subside.

1. The root in early Arabic

Silence: Ceasing to speak

Arabic lexicons define al-sakt and al-sukūt as khilāf al-nuṭq: the opposite of speaking.

Sakata al-rajulu describes someone becoming silent or ceasing to speak.

The person may be capable of speech while saying nothing at a particular time.

They may previously have been speaking and now have stopped. They may also have remained silent from the beginning of a gathering or event.

Both situations involve silence, though the verb sakata often highlights speech stopping or a previously audible sound ceasing.

One lexical definition states the root's central meaning as follows:

«السِّينُ وَالْكَافُ وَالتَّاءُ يَدُلُّ عَلَى خِلَافِ الْكَلَامِ»

al-sīnu wa-al-kāfu wa-al-tāʾu yadullu ʿalā khilāfi al-kalām

“Sīn, kāf, and tāʾ indicate the opposite of speech.”

This definition clarifies the family's basic meaning without determining the cause of silence.

Someone may fall silent to hear another person, refrain from speaking while thinking, remain silent out of fear, tire of talking, or deliberately withhold an answer.

All these situations may involve silence, but fear, reflection, consent, and indifference are not fixed components of the word's meaning.

A direct definition is therefore:

Being silent or ceasing to speak or make a sound, without the cause, duration, or purpose of that silence necessarily being specified by the word itself.

A poetic witness attributed to ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād says:

سَكَتُّ فَغَرَّ أَعْدَائِي السُّكُوتُ
وَظَنُّونِي لِأَهْلِي قَدْ نَسِيتُ

sakattu fa-gharra aʿdāʾiya al-sukūtu / wa-ẓannūnī li-ahlī qad nasītu

“I remained silent, and my silence deceived my enemies; they thought I had forgotten my family and people.”

Sakattu here refers to the poet's remaining silent.

He has not spoken or shown an overt reaction, and his enemies infer something about his intentions and loyalty from that silence.

The verse nevertheless distinguishes silence itself from other people's interpretation of it.

The poet's silence is a behavior; the belief that he has forgotten his people is his enemies' supposition.

The poem continues by expressing his readiness to help his people in danger. His earlier silence therefore does not mean that he has abandoned his loyalty or cannot act.

The witness matters because silence alone does not establish a person's thoughts, intentions, or abilities.

The poem is transmitted under ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād's name. Its attribution and precise date are not independently established in this study.

It illustrates a recorded usage in the Arabic poetic tradition, but its composition cannot confidently be dated before Islam on this basis alone.

This article presents no other independently dated pre-Quranic witness for establishing the meaning of sakata.

Nevertheless, the meaning of silence is clear and stable in early Arabic lexicons, and the Quranic occurrence itself attests the root in the language of revelation.

Sakata and askata: Becoming silent and silencing

Sakata is a basic triliteral verb in Arabic.

Sakata yaskutu can mean “fell silent” or “ceased speaking.”

Transitive askata is also recorded with the meaning “silenced” or “caused another to fall silent.”

Form II sakkata likewise means to silence.

For example, when a person's words or actions cause someone else to stop talking, a verb meaning “silence” may be used.

Becoming silent and silencing someone are distinct.

The first describes the state of the person who falls silent; the second identifies an agent causing silence.

Some lexical accounts also record intransitive askata for someone whose speech has ceased and who no longer speaks.

Form IV alone therefore does not prove that every historical use describes someone forcibly silencing another.

Morphology must be considered alongside the subject, object, and context.

Silence and the cessation of activity

Arabic sources record sakata al-ghaḍab with the meaning “anger subsided” or “became calm.”

Anger here is not a speaking person closing their mouth; it is an agitated state whose intensity abates.

The Arabic expression sāla al-wādī thumma sakata is also recorded: water flowed through the valley, and then its flow subsided.

Here sakata is attributed to a flow or activity stopping.

These uses show that becoming silent can extend beyond human speech in Arabic.

This extension does not make sakata fully synonymous with sakana in every sentence.

Sakana has a broader range involving calm, stability, and dwelling, whereas sakata primarily concerns silence and the cessation of sound or overt activity.

This distinction is especially relevant to Al-Aʿrāf 7:154.

2. Forms and derivatives

The root S K T occurs only once in the Quran:

Sakata — Al-Aʿrāf 7:154

It is a basic triliteral perfect verb, third-person masculine singular.

Its known Arabic verbal nouns include sukūt, sakt, and sukāt.

Other forms of this family are also recorded in Arabic.

Sakata: Fell silent or stopped speaking.

Yaskutu: Falls silent or stops speaking.

Sukūt: Silence; not speaking.

Sakt: Ceasing to speak or briefly interrupting sound.

Saktah: An instance of silence or a pause. Early lexicons also use it for an illness involving loss of speech or ordinary activity; this historical usage should not automatically be equated with a specific modern diagnosis.

Sākit: Silent; someone who is not speaking.

Sakūt and sākūt: Very silent or sparing of speech.

Sikkīt: A person of few words or someone who is usually silent.

Askata: Silenced; in certain constructions, became silent.

Sakkata: Silenced another.

Muskit: Silencing; in some uses, a statement or argument that leaves the other party without a reply.

Musākatah: Mutual silence or refraining from speaking to another.

Of these forms, only sakata occurs in the Quran.

Early lexicons also record al-sukkayt and al-sukayt for the horse coming last in a specified group of racehorses. One lexical explanation connects the name to its owner's falling silent rather than boasting because the horse did not take a leading place.

This explanation is semantically intelligible, but it does not establish the name's historical origin with certainty.

In another usage, al-saktah denotes a brief pause between portions of speech or vocal performance.

Silence in Arabic can therefore be temporary, with speech or sound resuming afterward.

None of these derivatives alone determines a silent person's intention, virtue, or weakness.

3. Wa-lammā sakata ʿan Mūsā al-ghaḍab: Moses' anger subsides

Al-Aʿrāf 7:154 says:

وَلَمَّا سَكَتَ عَنْ مُوسَى الْغَضَبُ أَخَذَ الْأَلْوَاحَ وَفِي نُسْخَتِهَا هُدًى وَرَحْمَةٌ لِلَّذِينَ هُمْ لِرَبِّهِمْ يَرْهَبُونَ

wa-lammā sakata ʿan Mūsā al-ghaḍabu akhadha al-alwāḥa wa-fī nuskhatiha hudan wa-raḥmatun li-alladhīna hum li-rabbihim yarhabūn

Al-Aʿrāf 7:154

“When anger had subsided from Moses, he took up the tablets; in their inscription were guidance and mercy for those who fear their Lord.”

This is the root's only Quranic occurrence.

Silence is not attributed to a speaking human: al-ghaḍab, anger, is the subject of sakata.

The grammatical structure is clear.

Sakata is a perfect verb.

ʿAn Mūsā specifies the relationship between the state of anger and Moses.

Al-ghaḍab is the verb's subject.

The sentence therefore does not mean that Moses stopped speaking about anger.

Nor does it say that he dispelled his anger by speaking.

What it directly states is that anger subsided from Moses.

The background to Moses' anger

Understanding this expression requires attention to Moses' return to his people.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:150 says:

وَلَمَّا رَجَعَ مُوسَىٰ إِلَىٰ قَوْمِهِ غَضْبَانَ أَسِفًا قَالَ بِئْسَمَا خَلَفْتُمُونِي مِنْ بَعْدِي أَعَجِلْتُمْ أَمْرَ رَبِّكُمْ وَأَلْقَى الْأَلْوَاحَ وَأَخَذَ بِرَأْسِ أَخِيهِ يَجُرُّهُ إِلَيْهِ قَالَ ابْنَ أُمَّ إِنَّ الْقَوْمَ اسْتَضْعَفُونِي وَكَادُوا يَقْتُلُونَنِي فَلَا تُشْمِتْ بِيَ الْأَعْدَاءَ وَلَا تَجْعَلْنِي مَعَ الْقَوْمِ الظَّالِمِينَ

wa-lammā rajaʿa Mūsā ilā qawmihi ghaḍbāna asifan qāla biʾsamā khalaftumūnī min baʿdī a-ʿajiltum amra rabbikum wa-alqā al-alwāḥa wa-akhadha bi-raʾsi akhīhi yajurruhu ilayhi qāla ibna umma inna al-qawma istaḍʿafūnī wa-kādū yaqtulūnanī fa-lā tushmit biya al-aʿdāʾa wa-lā tajʿalnī maʿa al-qawmi al-ẓālimīn

Al-Aʿrāf 7:150

“When Moses returned to his people, angry and grieved, he said: How badly you succeeded me after my departure! Did you hasten ahead of your Lord's command? He cast down the tablets and seized his brother by the head, pulling him toward himself. He said: Son of my mother, the people overpowered me and nearly killed me; do not let my enemies rejoice over me, and do not place me with the wrongdoing people.”

Moses is described here as ghaḍbān and asif.

Ghaḍbān concerns anger and intense agitation.

Asaf in this context also highlights grief and deep distress.

His response is thus not described as a single simple, fleeting emotion.

After seeing his people's conduct, he speaks, casts down the tablets, and seizes his brother's head, pulling him toward himself.

These actions show the intensity of his state.

The story also makes the cause of his anger clear: his people had turned to calf worship during his absence.

His anger therefore arises from a real and serious matter; the Quran does not present it as groundless or merely the result of a harsh temperament.

Aaron's response and the request for forgiveness

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:150, Aaron explains that the people overpowered him and nearly killed him.

Ṭā Hā 20:92, Ṭā Hā 20:93, and Ṭā Hā 20:94 give further details of the exchange between Moses and Aaron.

Aaron speaks of his concern about division and disagreement among the Children of Israel.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:151, Moses asks forgiveness for himself and his brother and asks that both be admitted into divine mercy.

Anger, objection, hearing an explanation, and seeking forgiveness thus appear together in the narrative.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:154 then describes anger subsiding and the tablets being taken up.

This sequence shows that Moses' anger is not presented as permanent or unchangeable.

The verb sakata alone does not specify how long the anger took to subside, what bodily changes Moses experienced, or which single statement brought it to an end.

The Quran does not provide these details.

4. Why does the Quran say that anger “fell silent”?

Ordinarily, the one who falls silent is someone capable of speech.

Anger, however, is neither a human nor a speaking being.

Attributing sakata to anger is therefore figurative and rhetorical.

Anger as an agitated force

When someone experiences intense anger, that state may become evident in their words and actions.

They may raise their voice, speak sharply, react hastily, or act against the cause of their anger.

Anger is then not merely hidden within the person; its effects are visible in outward behavior.

In Moses' story, anger accompanies speech and action.

Thus, when the Quran says sakata ʿan Mūsā al-ghaḍab, anger can be understood as a force that had been prompting Moses to react and whose agitating influence has now subsided.

Within this image, anger had seemingly been speaking or calling the person to act, but has now become silent.

The expression retains a connection to literal silence. Cessation of overt activity remains present, but the subject is the agitation of anger rather than human speech.

Anger subsiding is different from speech ending

Saying only that Moses stopped speaking would leave open the possibility that he remained angry.

A person can remain silent at the height of anger.

Conversely, someone's anger may subside while they continue discussing the matter and acting to put it right.

Silence of the tongue and anger subsiding are therefore distinct.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:154, anger itself is the subject of sakata; the sentence accordingly concerns the calming of anger.

This distinction follows from the verse's structure and should be preserved in translation.

Why sakata rather than sakana?

S K N and S K T are close in this usage.

Sakana can denote becoming calm, movement subsiding, or reaching a settled state.

Sakata likewise expresses agitation subsiding in its Quranic use.

Their imagery nevertheless differs in a meaningful way.

Sakana more readily emphasizes movement, disturbance, or an unsettled state becoming calm.

Sakata primarily means becoming silent and ceasing to speak. Applied to anger, it evokes a force ceasing after previously making its influence evident in behavior.

Sakata al-ghaḍab can thus express anger subsiding while giving a more vivid image of its falling silent.

This is a tendency, not an absolute boundary excluding all overlap between sakata and sakana in Arabic.

The choice of sakata also does not imply that anger was literally an independent speaking being.

A figurative verb does not establish a new bodily nature or mode of existence for its subject.

Anger subsiding does not mean approving wrongdoing

Moses' anger subsides in Al-Aʿrāf 7:154, but his people's calf worship does not thereby become right.

The wrong that caused his anger does not change in truth or responsibility when he becomes calm.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:152 states the consequences for the calf worshippers.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:153 also speaks of those who did wrong, then repented and believed.

This sequence shows that anger subsiding is not equivalent to disregarding responsibility or treating wrongdoing as insignificant.

A person can cease to be intensely angry while continuing to recognize wrong conduct as wrong.

After becoming calm, they may also act with greater care and justice to remedy the situation.

In Moses' story, the tablets are taken up again after anger subsides.

This action occurs in a context of renewed attention to the guidance and mercy expressed in the tablets' inscription.

The verb sakata alone does not establish that Moses resolved every disagreement, forgave everyone, or never again became angry about that conduct.

The word describes only the ending or subsiding of that state on that occasion.

Anger's silence and taking up the tablets

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:150, Moses casts down the tablets.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:154, he takes them up when anger subsides from him.

This contrast matters to the narrative's structure.

In the first situation, Moses' behavior is described under the influence of intense anger.

In the second, he takes up the tablets again after that state subsides.

The Quran then speaks of guidance and mercy in their inscription.

The verse does not say that casting the tablets down broke them or that a new set was made to replace them.

Such details cannot be extracted from akhadha al-alwāḥ.

For the study of S K T, the clear conclusion is that anger subsiding marks a change in the narrative. The tablets' physical fate is a separate question from this root's meaning.

5. Comparison with related roots

S K T and Ṣ M T: Becoming silent and remaining silent

Ṣ M T is among the closest roots to S K T.

Both can describe a person's not speaking and being silent.

Arabic lexicons sometimes explain ṣamata through sakata.

In some uses, however, sakata highlights the cessation of earlier speech or sound, while ṣamata more readily describes silence itself and the absence of speech.

This is not absolute. Both may describe the same silent person in Arabic.

The Quran uses Ṣ M T once, as ṣāmitūn in Al-Aʿrāf 7:193.

That verse concerns the identical outcome of calling upon false deities and the addressees' remaining silent.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:154, by contrast, sakata describes Moses' anger.

These two verses show that although both roots concern silence, their applications here differ.

Sakata al-ghaḍab is an established Arabic expression for anger subsiding, but the same meaning cannot be transferred without evidence to every construction involving Ṣ M T.

Forqan's separate study of Ṣ M T examines silence in humans and soundless objects.

Study of Ṣ M T

S K T and N Ṣ T: Silence and listening

N Ṣ T in the Quran concerns becoming silent in order to listen.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:204, the addressees are commanded to listen and observe inṣāt when the Quran is recited.

In Al-Aḥqāf 46:29, a group of jinn present to hear the Quran likewise call on one another to observe inṣāt.

In these uses, inṣāt is more than the absence of speech: it is silence accompanied by listening.

Sakata does not itself include that purpose.

A person may become silent without listening to anything. Their silence may arise from fatigue, fear, reflection, or another cause.

Inṣāt, in its clear uses, is associated with listening to speech or sound.

Sakata should therefore not always be translated “listened attentively.”

Nor does inṣāt necessarily mean accepting and affirming what is heard.

Someone may listen carefully and afterward judge whether the statement is true or false.

S K T and S K N: Silence and becoming calm

S K N has a wide range involving calm, settledness, dwelling, and the subsiding of motion.

The Quran uses this family concerning human rest, dwelling in homes, night, the relationship of spouses, and sakīnah.

Sakata primarily concerns becoming silent, but can also describe anger subsiding.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:154, sakata al-ghaḍab is consequently close in outcome to sakana al-ghaḍab.

Dwelling in a house or finding repose with a spouse cannot, however, be expressed with sakata in the same sense.

Closeness in one usage does not make every derivative of two roots equivalent.

Forqan's study of S K N separately examines settledness, calm, sakīnah, and dwelling.

Study of S K N

S K T and K Ẓ M: Anger subsiding and anger being restrained

K Ẓ M in Arabic concerns holding something back and containing it.

Āl ʿImrān 3:134 includes restraining rage among the qualities of those who do good.

Ghayẓ and ghaḍab belong to related families involving intense agitation and displeasure.

Kaẓm emphasizes restraint and preventing a reaction from appearing or spreading, whereas sakata al-ghaḍab in Al-Aʿrāf 7:154 describes the agitated state itself subsiding.

A person may feel angry but restrain it and avoid harming another.

Such restraint does not mean that the feeling of anger has completely disappeared.

By contrast, saying anger subsided concerns its intensity diminishing or ending.

These processes may be related in human life, but they are distinct.

Al-Shūrā 42:37 also praises people who forgive when angry.

The verse shows that feeling anger and forgiving are not necessarily incompatible.

The ethical value of conduct is therefore not determined solely by anger's presence or absence; how a person handles it and responds afterward also matters.

S K T and Ṣ W M: Silence and abstention

Ṣ W M in Arabic concerns abstaining and withholding oneself from an action.

In Maryam 19:26, Mary speaks of ṣawm and then declares that she will not speak to any human that day.

Refraining from speech is a manifestation of ṣawm in this context.

This usage does not make ṣawm and silence synonymous throughout Arabic.

Silence concerns being quiet and ceasing to speak.

Ṣawm has a broader range of abstention, whose object must be identified from the sentence and context.

Silence can thus be an instance of abstention, but not all abstention is silence, and not every silence necessarily takes place within ṣawm.

This distinction matters in Quranic research on ṣawm: one particular instance should not become the root's entire meaning.

6. Silence and the ethical value of conduct

Silence is, in itself, neither virtue nor sin.

Someone may remain silent to think, listen to another, or refrain from saying something wrong.

Silence can be judicious conduct in these circumstances.

Another person may know the truth but withhold it to protect their own interests.

The ethical judgment then follows not merely from their silence but from their responsibility, knowledge, intention, and the consequences of remaining silent.

Similarly, a person's silence does not establish that they have accepted another's statement.

They may disagree while deciding not to respond at that moment.

The poetic witness attributed to ʿAntarah illustrates this distinction: his enemies took his silence as evidence that he had forgotten his people, although that inference was not necessarily correct.

The Quranic use concerns something beyond human silence.

Moses' anger subsides, but his responsibility regarding his people's calf worship does not end.

Anger becoming silent is therefore distinct from silencing truth or abandoning reform.

This point follows from the story's context; the root itself expresses only the cessation or subsiding of the preceding state.

Sakata should not be turned into a rule that one must always remain silent before wrongdoing, just as not speaking should not invariably be taken as weakness, fear, or indifference.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

For human uses of S K T, the basic equivalents are “fall silent” and “be silent.”

Where someone stops after speaking, “ceased speaking” is also appropriate.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:154, however, anger is the subject. A word-for-word rendering such as “anger became silent” can sound unfamiliar in Persian.

A more natural, accurate rendering is:

“When anger had subsided from Moses, he took up the tablets.”

This preserves the Quranic sense without forcing the Arabic imagery into an artificial Persian expression.

“When Moses' anger became calm” is also defensible in meaning.

“When Moses fell silent” is inaccurate because Moses is not the subject of sakata in the verse.

Likewise, “when Moses stopped speaking” restricts the sentence to verbal silence and overlooks anger subsiding.

Suitable equivalents for other Arabic forms in the family include:

Sakata: Fell silent or ceased speaking.

Sakata ʿan al-kalām: Stopped speaking.

Sukūt: Silence or not speaking.

Sākit: Silent; someone who is not speaking.

Sakūt: Very silent or sparing of speech.

Saktah: A pause or brief silence; also an illness term in early lexicons, without necessarily corresponding to one specific modern diagnosis.

Askatahu: Silenced him.

Sakkatahu: Made him quiet.

Sakata al-ghaḍab: Anger subsided or became calm.

Muskit: Silencing; of an argument, an answer that closes off further objection.

In Moses' story, sukūt should not be translated as accepting his people's calf worship, forgetting the wrong, or relinquishing responsibility for guidance.

The rhetorical image of anger's silence should not turn anger into an independent speaking entity.

The basic translation need only convey anger subsiding. The Arabic expression's imagery can be explained in the study.

Conclusion

S K T in Arabic concerns becoming silent and ceasing to speak.

In the primary use, a sākit person does not speak or has stopped speaking.

The family also extends to an activity ceasing or an agitated state subsiding. Sakata al-ghaḍab is accordingly an established expression for anger becoming calm.

In the poem attributed to ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād, silence concerns a human and others' interpretation of it. The witness shows that silence alone does not establish a person's intention or position.

The root occurs only once in the Quran.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:154 describes anger subsiding from Moses after his return and intense response to his people's calf worship.

The expression matters grammatically and semantically because al-ghaḍab is the subject of sakata. The Quran reports anger subsiding, not Moses' tongue falling silent.

Anger is pictured as a force that previously influenced behavior and whose intensity has now subsided.

Moses then takes up the tablets, and the Quran speaks of guidance and mercy in their inscription.

This sequence shows that anger's intensity ending is distinct from responsibility ending, wrongdoing being overlooked, or misconduct being accepted.

The final definition is therefore:

S K T primarily denotes becoming silent or ceasing to speak. In extended uses, it can express overt activity stopping or an agitated state subsiding. In Al-Aʿrāf 7:154, it means Moses' anger subsiding, not his ceasing to speak.

This root overlaps with ṣamt in silence, inṣāt in refraining from speech, and sukūn in becoming calm, but none is fully synonymous with the others in all uses.

For Forqan, “becoming silent” is the family's basic equivalent, while “anger subsiding” suits its Quranic occurrence.

The expression highlights that anger, however intense, need not be permanent or unchangeable. A person can emerge from it, continue to recognize its cause as wrong, and act calmly to remedy the situation.

In Moses' story, anger subsiding does not mean truth falling silent. It marks the end of an agitated state and a return to the situation in which the tablets of guidance and mercy are taken up again.
