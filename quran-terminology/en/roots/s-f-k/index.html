Foundational Meaning

The root S F K points originally to pouring a liquid out from its place, especially when a liquid leaves a vessel, body, or place of containment. The central image is “pouring out,” not simply gentle flowing. Something that belonged in its place leaves that place and spreads over a surface.

In classical Arabic, the best-known and dominant use of this root is with blood. The expression سَفْكُ الدَّم (safk al-dam) means shedding blood, usually referring to bloodshed and killing.

Material Image

In the linguistic image, instead of only saying that someone was killed, Arabic may say that his blood was poured out. It highlights the visible result of the act: blood has left the body's protected boundary and has been spilled onto the ground.

This root can be understood originally for pouring liquid in general, like a liquid poured out of a vessel. But the severity and violence of the image become clearest when the liquid is blood. In سَفْكُ الدَّم (safk al-dam), the body is seen as the place that contains blood, and bloodshed means pouring out what should have remained protected within the body's sanctuary.

Qur'anic Use

In the Qur'an, this root appears only twice, and both times it is joined with دِمَاء (dimā'), bloods. This shows that the Qur'anic use of S F K is specifically in the field of bloodshed, not in the general meaning of pouring any liquid.

In Al-Baqarah 2:30: وَيَسْفِكُ الدِّمَاءَ (wa-yasfiku al-dimā'), and he sheds bloods.

In Al-Baqarah 2:84: لَا تَسْفِكُونَ دِمَاءَكُمْ (lā tasfikūna dimā'akum), do not shed your own bloods.

In both uses, the object is dimā'. Therefore, in systematic Qur'anic translation, safk in these two places should be rendered as “shedding blood” or “bloodshed,” not with the general word “pouring,” because the Qur'an itself specifies the semantic field through blood.

Wa-Yasfiku Al-Dimā'

In the scene of the angels' speech about the human being's khilāfah on earth, the phrase وَيَسْفِكُ الدِّمَاءَ (wa-yasfiku al-dimā') appears. It does not only speak of the ending of life; it places the image of bloodshed before the eyes.

Bloodshed here signals violence leaving its proper limit. The sanctuary of life is broken, blood leaves its protected place, and the earth becomes a scene of assault upon life.

Lā Tasfikūna Dimā'akum

In the covenant with the Children of Israel, the Qur'an says: لَا تَسْفِكُونَ دِمَاءَكُمْ (lā tasfikūna dimā'akum). Its outward wording is “do not shed your own bloods.” This is a prohibition against killing within the community and breaking the sanctity of life among people who should stand in responsible relation.

The Qur'an does not treat the blood of another member within the same collective as separate from one's own blood. When a person sheds the blood of another member of the community, he is in effect shedding his own blood, because he breaks the sanctity of shared life from within.

The Point of Dimā'akum

The expression دِمَاءَكُمْ (dimā'akum) carries an important linguistic point. Its outward wording is “your own bloods.” In Arabic, such an expression can speak of the inner bond of a community: the blood of another person within that community is not viewed as separate from one's own blood.

Therefore, in explanatory translation one may say “do not shed one another's blood.” But in a more exact translation, “do not shed your own bloods” preserves the main force of the phrase better.

Difference From Qatl

The difference between safk and qatl matters. Qatl focuses on taking life, the ending of living existence. Safk focuses on blood being poured out, the outward and material display of violence.

Qatl and safk may occur in one event, but their angles are not the same. Qatl names the final result; safk highlights the scene of bloodshed.

Difference From Ṣabb

Ṣabb is pouring liquid and can be used for water, rain, punishment, or anything poured down. Its ethical weight depends on the context.

Safk, however, in Qur'anic use appears with blood and carries a heavier, more violent force from the beginning. Ṣabb may be ordinary, natural, or even merciful; سَفْكُ الدِّمَاء (safk al-dimā') is an expression of bloodshed and assault upon life.

Difference From Safḥ

Safk must also be distinguished from safḥ. Both are connected with pouring, but safḥ appears in the Qur'an in the phrase دَمًا مَّسْفُوحًا (daman masfūḥan), blood poured and flowing.

Safḥ points more to the state of blood that has been poured and is flowing, while safk highlights the act of shedding blood. Briefly: safk is the act of pouring blood out; safḥ is the state of blood poured and flowing.

Difference From Sayl

Sayl points more to flowing or running naturally or in volume, as in a flood. Safk is the act of pouring something out: an agent removes something from its place.

In Qur'anic use, that something is blood. Safk is therefore ethically and humanly heavier than the natural image of flow or streaming.

The Social Weight of Bloodshed

In pre-Qur'anic Arabic and the older Arab social world, blood and blood-vengeance carried major social weight. Expressions about blood were not only bodily descriptions; they were tied to tribe, revenge, sanctity, war, and collective responsibility.

When it was said that someone's blood had been shed, the matter was not only the death of an individual. A social event had occurred, one that could begin conflict, retaliation, and a new cycle of violence. For this reason, سَفْكُ الدَّم (safk al-dam) in Arabic carries a harsher image than simple “killing”: it shows the blood and evokes the social consequences of bloodshed.

Translation Suggestions for Forqan

For وَيَسْفِكُ الدِّمَاءَ (wa-yasfiku al-dimā'): “and he sheds bloods,” or “and he commits bloodshed.”

For لَا تَسْفِكُونَ دِمَاءَكُمْ (lā tasfikūna dimā'akum): “do not shed your own bloods”; and in explanation, “do not shed one another's blood.”

For S F K in Qur'anic root-level translation: “to shed blood,” “to commit bloodshed,” or “to pour blood out from the sanctuary of life.”

Summary

S F K is a root with a clear material image: pouring liquid out from its place. In the Qur'an, however, this image is used specifically for blood. The root does not appear in the Qur'an bare and without an object; it always appears with dimā'.

Its difference from qatl is that qatl speaks of the ending of life, while safk speaks of blood being poured out and the visible display of violence. For that reason, the most fitting Qur'anic equivalent is “shedding blood” or “bloodshed.”
