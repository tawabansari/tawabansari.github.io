Moisture, softness, and freshness: from moist and dry to ripe dates

The Arabic root R Ṭ B denotes moisture, dampness, and a condition opposed to dryness. This meaning appears in descriptions of a fresh plant shoot, damp cloth, soft feathers, green vegetation, and fruit that retains its moisture and softness.

Within this family, raṭb describes something moist or damp, raṭīb something soft and fresh, and ruṭab dates that have ripened and softened but have not yet reached the dried-date stage.

Two principal uses must therefore be distinguished: a general description of moisture as opposed to dryness, and the name of a particular stage in the ripening of date-palm fruit.

The connection is clear: fresh, ripe dates are softer and moister than dried dates. Yet ruṭab in the context of palms does not name anything wet; it identifies a particular fruit at a particular stage of ripening.

The root occurs only twice in the Qur’an.

The first occurrence is in Al-Anʿām 6:59, in wa-lā raṭbin wa-lā yābisin, where moist and dry appear together in the context of The God’s encompassing knowledge.

The second is in Maryam 19:25, in ruṭaban janiyyan, describing fresh, ripe dates provided as food for Maryam beside the palm trunk.

Qur’anic forms: raṭbin in Al-Anʿām 6:59 and ruṭaban in Maryam 19:25.

Other recorded Arabic forms include raṭuba, raṭiba, yarṭubu, ruṭūbah, raṭābah, raṭb, raṭīb, ruṭab, ruṭb, ruṭub, ruṭabah, raṭbah, raṭṭaba, tarṭīb, arṭaba, irṭāb, taraṭṭaba, marṭūb, murṭib, and muraṭṭib.

These forms differ in meaning and use. The vowels of both rāʾ and ṭāʾ in raṭb, ruṭab, and ruṭb are particularly significant and must not be overlooked in Qur’anic study.

1. Core meaning and early Arabic usage

Raṭb: the opposite of yābis

The lexical core of this root has been expressed as follows:

الرَّاءُ وَالطَّاءُ وَالْبَاءُ أَصْلٌ وَاحِدٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى خِلَافِ الْيُبْسِ

al-rāʾu wa-al-ṭāʾu wa-al-bāʾu aṣlun wāḥidun yadullu ʿalā khilāfi al-yubsi

“Rāʾ, ṭāʾ, and bāʾ have one underlying sense, indicating the opposite of dryness.”

This definition clarifies the family’s central meaning.

In general usage, raṭb describes something that is not dry and possesses some moisture or softness.

Yābis, by contrast, describes something dry or something that has lost its moisture and suppleness.

A fresh branch, for example, may be soft and flexible, whereas a dry branch may have become brittle and less flexible.

Cloth may likewise become damp through contact with water and lose that moisture when it dries.

This contrast does not mean that everything described as raṭb has necessarily been immersed in water or has a completely wet surface.

In Arabic, moistness can also concern internal moisture, freshness, and softness.

A fresh plant shoot, for example, may be called raṭb even when its surface is not wet.

The root’s core meaning therefore extends beyond a water-covered surface.

Raṭīb: softness and freshness

A lexical explanation states:

الرَّطْبُ بِالْفَتْحِ: ضِدُّ الْيَابِسِ، وَالرَّطْبُ: النَّاعِمُ

al-raṭbu bi-al-fatḥi ḍiddu al-yābisi wa-al-raṭbu al-nāʿimu

“Raṭb, with fatḥah on rāʾ, is the opposite of dry; raṭb also means soft.”

The forms raṭb and raṭīb are further identified as descriptions of things possessing moisture and softness.

In Arabic, ghuṣn raṭīb means a soft, fresh branch.

Rīsh raṭīb is also used for soft feathers.

These usages show the close association between moisture and softness within this family.

Yet raṭīb should not always be translated as “wet.”

Soft feathers may be dry but still be called raṭīb because of their delicacy.

Freshness in a plant can likewise concern softness and retained moisture in its tissues, without any external wetting.

Moistness, dampness, freshness, and softness should therefore be distinguished, although they overlap in this root’s uses.

Raṭuba: becoming moist and soft

The expression raṭuba al-shayʾu yarṭubu ruṭūbatan describes something becoming moist or damp.

Raṭābah is also recorded for the condition of moisture and softness.

The form raṭiba is recorded as well.

This difference in the verb’s vowel should not by itself be used to construct two wholly independent meanings.

The clear sense concerns the presence or development of moisture.

The verb itself does not specify how that moisture came about.

A plant may naturally be fresh and moist, cloth may be wetted with water, or fruit may soften as it ripens.

These are different situations describable with words from this family.

Raṭṭaba: moistening or feeding someone fresh dates

Raṭṭaba is a Form II verb.

In one usage, raṭṭaba al-thawba means to dampen or wet cloth.

In another, raṭṭaba al-qawma means to feed a group of people fresh dates.

This distinction matters.

The first usage focuses on changing an object’s condition by adding moisture.

The second concerns providing and feeding people the fruit called ruṭab.

Raṭṭaba cannot therefore receive one fixed translation in every sentence.

The object and context determine whether it means moistening something or feeding someone fresh dates.

Modern tarṭīb also describes moisturizing skin and other materials. This agrees with the root’s lexical sense, but modern technical concepts should not all be projected onto its early forms.

2. Ruṭab: fresh, ripe dates

One of this family’s best-known uses names a stage of date ripening.

The following definition is given for ruṭab:

الرُّطَبُ، وَالْوَاحِدَةُ رُطَبَةٌ: النَّضِيجُ مِنَ الْبُسْرِ قَبْلَ إِتْمَارِهِ

al-ruṭabu wa-al-wāḥidatu ruṭabatun al-naḍīju mina al-busri qabla itmārihi

“Ruṭab, of which one fruit is a ruṭabah, is busr that has ripened but has not yet become tamr.”

This definition is central to the Qur’anic usage: ruṭaban in Sūrat Maryam names this fruit.

Distinguishing busr, ruṭab, and tamr

Arabic has different names for date-palm fruit at successive stages of growth and ripening.

Busr names a stage before the fruit softens into ruṭab. It is generally firmer at this stage, but sweetness and edibility vary with the date variety.

Ruṭab concerns the stage at which dates soften and ripen while retaining their freshness.

In ordinary usage, tamr refers to ripe dates that have lost some moisture and become more suitable for storage.

This sequence expresses a familiar distinction among the stages of date fruit.

It should not, however, be made into an exceptionless biological scheme.

Palms, date varieties, and climatic conditions can differ in the speed and course of ripening.

Arabic may also use tamr more broadly for dates without the speaker distinguishing every technical stage.

The principal lexical distinction is clear: ruṭab names fresh, ripe dates, not every soft fruit or every damp object.

Arṭaba al-busru

Arṭaba al-busru means that busr dates have reached the ruṭab stage.

Arṭabat al-nakhlatu likewise describes a palm whose fruit has entered that stage.

This usage relates to raṭuba, becoming moist, but takes on a more specific meaning in the context of date palms.

Saying that a branch has become raṭb may mean that it has become moist or soft.

Saying arṭaba al-busru, however, identifies the fruit’s arrival at a particular stage.

A root’s general meaning can thus acquire a specialized use in naming a plant or fruit.

Ruṭab as a collective generic noun

Ruṭab has an important grammatical characteristic in Arabic.

In morphological analysis, ruṭab should not simply be treated as a broken plural of ruṭabah.

The distinction is explained as follows:

لَيْسَ رُطَبٌ بِتَكْسِيرِ رُطَبَةٍ، وَإِنَّمَا الرُّطَبُ كَالتَّمْرِ

laysa ruṭabun bi-taksīri ruṭabatin wa-innamā al-ruṭabu ka-al-tamri

“Ruṭab is not a broken plural of ruṭabah; rather, ruṭab is like tamr.”

Ruṭab can name the fruit collectively, while ruṭabah identifies one individual fruit.

This distinction matters for grammatical structure.

The Qur’an uses ruṭaban janiyyan.

Janiyyan accompanies ruṭaban in the masculine singular.

This construction accords with ruṭab being a generic noun.

Collective meaning does not require a plural adjective. Nonhuman plurals also commonly take a feminine singular adjective; ruṭab here, however, functions as a masculine generic noun.

Arabic collective generic nouns can differ from count plurals in their usage and agreement.

Does ruṭab mean unripe dates?

No.

In established lexical usage, ruṭab denotes fruit that has ripened and softened.

This stage should be distinguished from busr before softening, and from dried dates.

Nor should ruṭab be restricted to dates that have just been picked.

Ruṭab dates may still be on the palm and ready for gathering.

We must therefore distinguish the fruit’s stage of ripeness from the act of picking or harvesting it.

The presence of janiyyan in Maryam 19:25 makes this distinction especially relevant.

3. Other Arabic uses: green vegetation, animal fodder, and gentle speech

Ruṭb: fresh grass and vegetation

Ruṭb, with ḍammah on rāʾ and sukūn on ṭāʾ, and ruṭub, with ḍammah on both, are also recorded for fresh grass and green plants.

These usages concern vegetation that has not dried and retains its freshness.

Ruṭb al-kalaʾ refers to fresh, green pasture.

Arḍ murṭibah describes land with abundant fresh vegetation and grass.

These usages clearly relate to the core ideas of moisture and freshness.

Ruṭb meaning fresh herbage should nevertheless not be confused with ruṭab meaning ripe dates.

The vowel of ṭāʾ, together with context, helps distinguish them.

In the Qur’an, ruṭaban occurs alongside a palm: the reference is to fresh dates, not grass or green pasture.

Raṭbah: fresh animal fodder

Raṭbah, with fatḥah on rāʾ, is recorded for fiṣfiṣah, fresh lucerne or alfalfa, or its growing patch while it remains green.

Raṭaba al-dābbata is also used for feeding fresh herbage to an animal.

This shows that the R Ṭ B family was familiar in the vocabulary of animal husbandry.

Raṭbah meaning fodder must be distinguished from ruṭabah meaning one fresh date.

Without vowel marks the two look alike, but their meanings and domains differ.

That distinction must remain clear in lexical research.

Raṭīb as a description of softness

Early usage applies raṭīb to branches, feathers, and soft things.

This reflects a recognized association between moisture and softness in Arabic.

Softness and moisture are not, however, identical concepts.

Something may be naturally soft even when its surface is not wet.

Conversely, a hard object may have a surface dampened by water.

The referent must therefore guide the definition of raṭīb.

For a plant shoot, “fresh and soft” is appropriate.

For cloth that has absorbed water, “damp” is more natural.

For feathers, “soft and delicate” may convey the intended meaning.

Not all usages should consequently be reduced to “soaked with water.”

A moist tongue: usages concerning speech

Lexical records also preserve uses of this family concerning the tongue and speech.

A moist tongue in Arabic expressions can evoke gentle speech or continuity in speaking.

Another expression, raṭiba al-rajulu bi-mā ʿindahu, describes someone voicing whatever he has or whatever occurs to him, whether right or wrong.

Here the issue is speaking; the root itself does not determine whether the utterance is correct or incorrect.

In literary language, a moist tongue can also evoke fluent speech or continually mentioning and remembering someone.

These uses have an intelligible connection with softness and fluency.

They do not establish that R Ṭ B means speaking, eloquence, or remembrance of The God in the Qur’an.

The two Qur’anic contexts are clear: moist and dry within encompassing divine knowledge, and ripe dates in Maryam’s story.

Speech-related meanings thus remain non-Qur’anic uses of the family.

4. Early poetic witnesses: raṭban wa-yābisan

A clear witness occurs in a verse attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays, a pre-Islamic poet:

كَأَنَّ قُلُوبَ الطَّيْرِ رَطْبًا وَيَابِسًا
لَدَىٰ وَكْرِهَا الْعُنَّابُ وَالْحَشَفُ الْبَالِي

ka-anna qulūba al-ṭayri raṭban wa-yābisan / ladā wakrihā al-ʿunnābu wa-al-ḥashafu al-bālī

“The birds’ hearts, moist and dry, beside the bird of prey’s nest resemble fresh jujubes and shriveled, worn-out dates.”

The poet describes the hearts of birds lying beside a bird of prey’s nest.

Some remain fresh and soft; others have dried over time.

He compares the fresh hearts to ʿunnāb and the dry hearts to ḥashaf bālin.

ʿUnnāb is jujube fruit, whose freshness and color are compared here with the fresh birds’ hearts.

Ḥashaf refers to poor-quality, dried dates; bālin emphasizes their worn condition.

The verse thus pairs fresh and dry hearts with fresh jujubes and shriveled dates.

Raṭban here does not name dates

This witness has particular lexical value.

The poet says:

قُلُوبَ الطَّيْرِ رَطْبًا وَيَابِسًا

qulūba al-ṭayri raṭban wa-yābisan

He is describing birds’ hearts in two conditions, moist and dry.

Raṭban here does not mean ruṭab dates: its referent is the birds’ hearts.

The word expresses the general qualities of freshness and moisture.

This is close to raṭbin in Al-Anʿām 6:59.

In both, raṭb contrasts with yābis.

Seeing the three letters R Ṭ B is therefore not enough to conclude that the word always names dates.

Form and context determine the meaning.

The witness’s historical value

This verse appears in transmissions of Imruʾ al-Qays’s poetry and in rhetorical discussions of simile.

Rhetorically, it compares one thing in two conditions with two different things: moist hearts with jujubes, and dry hearts with shriveled dates.

It shows that the contrast between raṭb and yābis was familiar in literary Arabic attributed to the period before the Qur’an.

It does not establish that Al-Anʿām 6:59 necessarily alludes to this particular verse of poetry.

A shared linguistic contrast alone proves neither borrowing nor direct allusion.

What can be stated securely is that the contrast had a history in Arabic and was intelligible to Arabic speakers.

Sāʿidah ibn Juʾayyah: becoming wet in a natural setting

A poem by Sāʿidah ibn Juʾayyah al-Hudhalī contains the following:

بِشَرَبَّةٍ دَمِثِ الْكَثِيبِ بِدُورِهِ
أَرْطَىٰ يَعُوذُ بِهِ إِذَا مَا يُرْطَبُ

bi-sharabbatin damithi al-kathībi bi-dūrihi / arṭā yaʿūdhu bihi idhā mā yurṭabu

“In a place with a soft sand dune, with arṭā shrubs around it, where it seeks shelter when it becomes wet.”

The verse describes a place with a soft sand dune and arṭā shrubs around it, where an animal seeks shelter when it becomes wet. This line alone does not securely identify the ground itself as what becomes damp.

The final verb, vocalized yurṭabu, means to become wet; yarṭabu is also transmitted. Both concern acquiring moisture, while the surrounding poem is needed to identify the verb’s referent precisely.

The witness illustrates a verbal use concerning moisture.

Sāʿidah ibn Juʾayyah was a mukhaḍram poet who lived across the pre-Islamic and Islamic periods. The precise date of this verse is uncertain.

It is therefore useful evidence for early Arabic, but should not be assigned to a time before the Qur’an without firm grounds.

Dhū al-Rummah: vegetation losing its moisture

Dhū al-Rummah’s poetry contains the following:

حَتَّىٰ إِذَا مَعْمَعَانُ الصَّيْفِ هَبَّ لَهُ
بِأَجَّةٍ نَشَّ عَنْهَا الْمَاءُ وَالرُّطُبُ

ḥattā idhā maʿmaʿānu al-ṣayfi habba lahu / bi-ajjatin nashsha ʿanhā al-māʾu wa-al-ruṭubu

“Until the fiercest summer blew upon it with scorching heat, drying up its water and fresh vegetation.”

The verse describes intense summer heat and the disappearance of water and fresh vegetation.

Al-ruṭub here concerns fresh herbage, not ripe dates.

It demonstrates the use of closely related forms in descriptions of vegetation and pasture.

Dhū al-Rummah belongs to the Umayyad period, however; his poetry is not pre-Islamic evidence.

The verse attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays is thus more suitable for studying the moist–dry contrast before the Qur’an, while still attending to how poetic witnesses were transmitted.

5. Wa-lā raṭbin wa-lā yābisin: the root in Al-Anʿām

The first Qur’anic occurrence is in Al-Anʿām:

وَعِنْدَهُ مَفَاتِحُ الْغَيْبِ لَا يَعْلَمُهَا إِلَّا هُوَ وَيَعْلَمُ مَا فِي الْبَرِّ وَالْبَحْرِ وَمَا تَسْقُطُ مِنْ وَرَقَةٍ إِلَّا يَعْلَمُهَا وَلَا حَبَّةٍ فِي ظُلُمَاتِ الْأَرْضِ وَلَا رَطْبٍ وَلَا يَابِسٍ إِلَّا فِي كِتَابٍ مُبِينٍ

wa-ʿindahu mafātiḥu al-ghaybi lā yaʿlamuhā illā huwa wa-yaʿlamu mā fī al-barri wa-al-baḥri wa-mā tasquṭu min waraqatin illā yaʿlamuhā wa-lā ḥabbatin fī ẓulumāti al-arḍi wa-lā raṭbin wa-lā yābisin illā fī kitābin mubīnin

Al-Anʿām 6:59

“With Him are the keys of the unseen; none knows them but He. He knows whatever is on land and in the sea. No leaf falls without His knowing it, nor is there a grain in the darknesses of the earth, nor anything moist or dry, except in a clear record.”

Here raṭbin stands beside yābisin.

The contrast is a particularly clear example of the root’s general meaning.

The form raṭbin

Al-Anʿām 6:59 uses raṭbin, with fatḥah on rāʾ and sukūn on ṭāʾ.

This differs from ruṭaban in Maryam 19:25.

Raṭb here is a general description of something moist or damp.

Yābis, its opposite, refers to something dry.

In the familiar Ḥafṣ reading, raṭbin is genitive and coordinated with preceding words.

Grammatical explanations differ over its relation to wa-mā tasquṭu min waraqatin and to the final clause.

A reading with ḥabbah, raṭb, and yābis in the nominative is also reported; it more readily allows an analysis as an independent clause.

These differences in case do not alter the word’s basic meaning.

In all these analyses, raṭb stands opposite yābis.

An encompassing contrast

The verse first mentions mafātiḥ al-ghayb, the keys of the unseen.

It then describes The God’s knowledge of what is on land and in the sea.

Next come a falling leaf and a grain in the earth’s darknesses.

Finally comes wa-lā raṭbin wa-lā yābisin.

This sequence shows that the verse concerns the breadth and comprehensiveness of divine knowledge.

Moist and dry are two familiar, opposing conditions placed together.

Arabic can use both sides of a contrast to express comprehensiveness.

Saying that nothing moist or dry is hidden from view, for example, need not scientifically divide all beings into two wholly separate categories.

Rather, two contrasting conditions express inclusiveness.

Raṭb in Al-Anʿām 6:59 should therefore not be restricted to fresh vegetation or ripe dates.

The context is much broader.

Does raṭb here mean only a living being?

Some interpretations associate raṭb with living beings and yābis with dead or inanimate things.

Lexically, however, that association is not absolute.

An inanimate object can be damp.

A living thing can also have dry parts.

Without independent evidence, the moist–dry contrast therefore does not establish that raṭb here means precisely “living” and yābis precisely “dead.”

Such a reading may be figurative or an extension of meaning, but should not replace the direct lexical sense.

The verse also mentions leaves, grain, land, and sea: its account of divine awareness is not limited to living beings.

Does raṭb here name dates?

No.

In Maryam 19:25, ruṭaban accompanies the mention of a palm and refers to fresh dates.

In Al-Anʿām 6:59, raṭbin contrasts with yābisin in a context of encompassing divine knowledge.

The distinction is apparent in both form and context.

Rendering the phrase as “neither fresh dates nor anything dry” would therefore narrow and distort it.

“Nothing moist or dry” is more appropriate here.

6. Divine knowledge and kitābin mubīn

Al-Anʿām 6:59 ends with:

إِلَّا فِي كِتَابٍ مُبِينٍ

illā fī kitābin mubīnin

Al-Anʿām 6:59

“Except in a clear record.”

The phrase matters for interpreting the verse, but should be distinguished from the lexical meaning of R Ṭ B.

Kitāb comes from K T B, and mubīn from B Y N.

Neither belongs to the derivatives of R Ṭ B.

A clear record and encompassing knowledge

The opening attributes knowledge of the unseen to The God.

It then describes His awareness of land and sea, a falling leaf, and grain hidden in the earth.

Finally, all these are placed in relation to kitābin mubīn.

The relation between divine knowledge and this clear record should be examined within that context.

Some interpretations explain kitāb mubīn as the Preserved Tablet.

Another reading understands it as expressing the recording and encompassing reach of divine knowledge.

A final judgment on the expression requires examining its other Qur’anic uses as well.

For example, Sūrat Yūnus states:

وَمَا يَعْزُبُ عَنْ رَبِّكَ مِنْ مِثْقَالِ ذَرَّةٍ فِي الْأَرْضِ وَلَا فِي السَّمَاءِ وَلَا أَصْغَرَ مِنْ ذَٰلِكَ وَلَا أَكْبَرَ إِلَّا فِي كِتَابٍ مُبِينٍ

wa-mā yaʿzubu ʿan rabbika min mithqāli dharratin fī al-arḍi wa-lā fī al-samāʾi wa-lā aṣghara min dhālika wa-lā akbara illā fī kitābin mubīnin

Yūnus 10:61

“Nothing of an atom’s weight on earth or in heaven is hidden from your Lord, nor anything smaller or greater, except in a clear record.”

This agrees with Al-Anʿām 6:59 in expressing comprehensive divine knowledge.

Yet raṭbin wa-lā yābisin alone does not establish that the Qur’an available to human beings necessarily contains every technical detail of natural science, history, medicine, and other fields.

Such a conclusion needs a separate argument about kitāb mubīn.

Nor does comprehensive divine knowledge by itself amount to a denial of human choice.

Knowing an event and compelling someone to perform it are distinct concepts.

A complete theory of compulsion, choice, or destiny should therefore not be drawn from the words “moist and dry.”

For root study, the clear point is raṭb’s general meaning of moistness in contrast to dryness, within a statement of encompassing divine knowledge.

7. Ruṭaban janiyyan: fresh dates in Maryam’s story

The second Qur’anic occurrence is in Sūrat Maryam:

وَهُزِّي إِلَيْكِ بِجِذْعِ النَّخْلَةِ تُسَاقِطْ عَلَيْكِ رُطَبًا جَنِيًّا

wa-huzzī ilayki bi-jidhʿi al-nakhlati tusāqiṭ ʿalayki ruṭaban janiyyan

Maryam 19:25

“Shake the trunk of the palm toward yourself, so that it drops fresh, ripe dates upon you.”

Ruṭaban here names date-palm fruit at the fresh, ripe stage.

A precise reading requires examining the sentence and the accompanying adjective.

Maryam’s situation in the story

Two verses earlier, Maryam 19:23 describes her labor pains:

فَأَجَاءَهَا الْمَخَاضُ إِلَىٰ جِذْعِ النَّخْلَةِ

fa-ajāʾahā al-makhāḍu ilā jidhʿi al-nakhlati

Maryam 19:23

“Then the pains of childbirth drove her to the trunk of the palm.”

Her distress and words are then mentioned.

In Maryam 19:24 she is told not to grieve. In the reading that takes sariyyan as a stream, her Lord has provided water within reach; that word’s meaning is a separate subject of study.

The command to shake the palm trunk and the falling of fresh dates follow.

The next verse also says:

فَكُلِي وَاشْرَبِي وَقَرِّي عَيْنًا

fa-kulī wa-ishrabī wa-qarrī ʿaynan

Maryam 19:26

“So eat, drink, and let your eyes be comforted.”

Taken together, these passages show ruṭab as real food for Maryam.

The meaning of ruṭab should not, however, be changed into consolation, relief from grief, or a treatment for labor pains.

Those matters arise from the narrative situation and other expressions.

Ruṭab itself names the fruit.

Wa-huzzī: shaking

Huzzī is an imperative from H Z Z.

Maryam is asked to shake the palm trunk toward herself.

The verb has no derivational connection with R Ṭ B.

Huzzī expresses the action of shaking.

Ruṭaban identifies the fruit that falls.

Shaking should therefore not be included in the lexical meaning of ruṭab.

Nor does the verse specify the required force or the precise process by which the fruit detaches.

The story describes a particular event; without further evidence it should not become a complete account of the tree’s physical mechanics.

Tusāqiṭ: the falling fruit

In the Ḥafṣ reading, tusāqiṭ belongs morphologically to Form III and conveys dropping or causing to fall.

Other recognized readings include forms of tassāqaṭ.

Grammatical and exegetical discussions address the differences between these readings and the verb’s subject.

Across the familiar readings, however, the principal result is clear: ruṭab dates fall upon Maryam.

In this reading, ruṭaban is the verb’s accusative object, identifying the fruit that the palm drops.

Janiyyan is an accusative adjective describing that same fruit.

The verb of falling and the fruit’s name express different concepts.

Tusāqiṭ expresses the event.

Ruṭaban identifies the kind of fruit.

Janiyyan describes its quality.

These meanings should not all be merged into the definition of R Ṭ B.

8. Distinguishing ruṭaban from janiyyan

Maryam 19:25 places two related words together:

رُطَبًا جَنِيًّا

ruṭaban janiyyan

Maryam 19:25

“Fresh, ripe dates.”

They do not, however, share one root.

Ruṭab comes from R Ṭ B.

Janiyy comes from J N Y.

Ruṭab: the fruit’s stage of ripeness

As the lexical discussion established, ruṭab names fresh, ripe dates that retain their freshness and softness.

The word identifies the fruit and its condition.

Ruṭaban alone would therefore still identify fresh, softened date-palm fruit.

Janiyy: fruit ready for picking

In one analysis, janiyy means fruit gathered while fresh; another explanation applies it to ripe fruit ready to be picked.

The first explanation states:

جَنِيًّا يَعْنِي مَجْنِيًّا

janiyyan yaʿnī majniyyan

“Janiyyan means gathered.”

In this explanation, fruit is taken from its place while fresh.

Another explanation applies janiyy to fruit that has ripened and is ready for picking.

The verse’s context must be considered.

The Qur’an says the dates fall upon Maryam.

Janiyyan should not be translated as though someone had previously picked all the dates by hand and placed them in a container.

Both “freshly gathered fruit” and “ripe fruit ready for gathering” deserve consideration here. Neither alone establishes prior picking by human hands.

Does ruṭaban janiyyan repeat one meaning?

No.

The words are related but do not express precisely the same thing.

Ruṭaban identifies the fruit and its ripening stage.

Janiyyan, in one explanation, emphasizes gathering while fresh; in another, readiness for gathering.

Together they complete the image of fresh, soft fruit suitable for eating.

In Persian, “fresh, ripe dates” can naturally convey the combined description.

Lexical study should nevertheless make clear that “freshly picked” is not fully contained within R Ṭ B itself.

The picking-related meaning belongs to J N Y.

9. Can ruṭaban janiyyan establish the date of Jesus’s birth?

One proposed inference is that, because ruṭab dates ripen in particular seasons, Jesus must have been born during that season.

Lexical and Qur’anic analysis does not make this conclusion certain.

The verse itself names no date, month, or season.

It simply says that fresh, ripe dates fall upon Maryam.

Exegetical accounts differ concerning the palm’s condition.

Some reports say the palm was dry.

Others describe it bearing fruit at an unusual time.

Still other explanations take it as a living, fruit-bearing tree.

The verse itself does not explicitly say that the palm was dry, bore fruit in winter, or was in its ordinary harvest season.

None of these reports can therefore be imposed on the Qur’anic text solely through ruṭab.

Even knowing the usual ripening season in a region would not by itself establish the exact date of a historical event.

The precise location, palm variety, climate, and other factors would also need to be known.

Ruṭaban janiyyan identifies the food provided, not a calendar date for Jesus’s birth.

Is this a general medical instruction concerning childbirth?

Exegetical and nutritional writings sometimes invoke the verse when discussing the benefits of dates for women who have recently given birth.

Two matters must be distinguished.

First, the Qur’an speaks of food and drink for Maryam in a particular event.

Second, determining dates’ medical effects on mothers and infants requires scientific research and attention to individual circumstances.

Ruṭab itself does not establish that dates are necessary for every woman in every childbirth situation, or that they replace medical care.

The verse also specifies no dosage, dietary formula, or treatment method.

For lexical study, the clear information is the fruit’s name and its fresh condition.

Its role in Maryam’s story belongs to the context of those verses; independent medical claims should not be imposed on the word’s lexical meaning.

10. R Ṭ B and related roots and words

R Ṭ B (ruṭūbah) and Y B S (yubs)

Yabisa concerns becoming dry in Arabic.

Raṭb and yābis explicitly contrast in Al-Anʿām 6:59.

Sūrat Yūsuf also mentions dry ears of grain:

وَأُخَرَ يَابِسَاتٍ

wa-ukhara yābisātin

Yūsuf 12:43

“And others that are dry.”

Yābisāt here describes ears of grain.

It belongs to Y B S, not R Ṭ B.

Moisture and dryness are opposing conditions.

That opposition does not always amount to good versus bad.

A fresh plant may suit one purpose and, after drying, become useful for storage or another purpose.

Fresh and dried dates can likewise both be suitable foods.

Raṭb should therefore not be assigned an absolute meaning of goodness, or yābis one of badness.

The contrast in Al-Anʿām 6:59 expresses comprehensiveness, not a moral judgment about the two conditions.

Study of Y B S

R Ṭ B (ruṭūbah) and Ṭ R W (ṭarāwah)

Ṭariyy describes something fresh and tender in Arabic.

The Qur’an says:

لِتَأْكُلُوا مِنْهُ لَحْمًا طَرِيًّا

li-taʾkulū minhu laḥman ṭariyyan

Al-Naḥl 16:14

“So that you may eat fresh meat from it.”

Ṭariyyan here describes meat.

It does not derive from R Ṭ B.

Freshness and softness can nonetheless bring some uses of ṭariyy and raṭīb close together.

They should not be treated as identical in form or throughout their usage.

Ruṭab names fresh date-palm fruit, whereas laḥman ṭariyyan refers to fresh meat.

Freshness is thus shared in some usages, but each word has its own range.

Study of Ṭ R W

R Ṭ B (ruṭūbah) and N Ḍ J (nuḍj)

Naḍija concerns becoming cooked or ripe.

The Qur’an says:

كُلَّمَا نَضِجَتْ جُلُودُهُمْ

kullamā naḍijat julūduhum

Al-Nisāʾ 4:56

“Whenever their skins are cooked through.”

Naḍijat here describes heat’s effect upon skin.

In Arabic, the same family can also describe fruit ripening.

Ruṭab is ripe fruit, but the two should not be equated.

Nuḍj concerns the process or condition of cooking, ripening, or reaching maturity.

Ruṭab names a particular stage of date ripening involving softness and retained moisture.

Not every ripe fruit is therefore called ruṭab.

Study of N Ḍ J

R Ṭ B (ruṭūbah) and J N Y (jany)

Janā concerns picking and harvesting fruit.

In Maryam 19:25, janiyyan qualifies ruṭaban.

Sūrat Al-Raḥmān says:

وَجَنَى الْجَنَّتَيْنِ دَانٍ

wa-janā al-jannatayni dānin

Al-Raḥmān 55:54

“And the fruit to be gathered from both gardens is near.”

This illustrates janā’s connection with fruit and its gathering.

R Ṭ B, however, concerns moisture, freshness, and, in palm-related usage, fresh dates.

Fruit can be ready for picking without being dates.

Ruṭab dates may also remain on the palm.

Picking fruit and dates ripening are therefore related but distinct concepts.

Study of J N Y

R Ṭ B (ruṭūbah) and L Y N (līn)

Līn concerns softness and flexibility.

Raṭīb also means soft or delicate in some usages.

Softness and dampness are not always identical.

Something can be soft yet contain little moisture.

A hard object can also become damp.

In descriptions of branches, feathers, or speech, raṭīb may therefore emphasize softness.

In Al-Anʿām 6:59, by contrast, raṭb’s opposition to yābis foregrounds moisture and dryness.

The Persian equivalent should consequently fit the referent and context.

R Ṭ B (ruṭūbah) and R B Ṭ (rabṭ)

These roots contain similar letters but differ in their order.

R Ṭ B consists of rāʾ, ṭāʾ, and bāʾ.

R B Ṭ consists of rāʾ, bāʾ, and ṭāʾ.

Rabṭ concerns binding and joining in Arabic.

The Qur’an says:

وَرَبَطْنَا عَلَىٰ قُلُوبِهِمْ

wa-rabaṭnā ʿalā qulūbihim

Al-Kahf 18:14

“And We made their hearts firm.”

This usage has no direct lexical connection with moisture.

Similarity of letters does not justify transferring “strengthening” into R Ṭ B or deriving binding and joining from moisture.

11. Translation proposals for Forqan

Translation should preserve the distinction between a general description of moistness and the specific name of fresh dates.

Raṭuba / raṭiba: became moist, damp, or soft, depending on the referent.

Yarṭubu: becomes moist; acquires moisture.

Ruṭūbah: moisture, dampness; the condition of being moist.

Raṭābah: moisture and softness; freshness and delicacy.

Raṭb: moist or damp; in some usages soft and fresh.

Raṭīb: soft, fresh, or damp, according to context.

Ruṭab: fresh, ripe dates; softened date-palm fruit before complete drying.

Ruṭabah: one fresh, ripe date.

Ruṭb / ruṭub: fresh, green grass and vegetation in pasture-related usages.

Raṭbah: fresh lucerne or alfalfa, or its growing patch while green.

Raṭṭaba: dampened, softened, or moistened; in a food-related usage, fed people fresh dates.

Tarṭīb: wetting or moistening; in another usage, feeding someone ruṭab dates.

Arṭaba al-busru: the busr dates reached the ruṭab stage.

Arṭabat al-nakhlatu: the palm’s fruit reached the ruṭab stage.

Irṭāb: dates reaching the ruṭab stage; in another usage, causing moisture.

Taraṭṭaba: became moist; acquired moisture.

Marṭūb: dampened; possessing moisture.

Murṭib: in palm-related usage, a tree or fruit whose dates have reached the ruṭab stage; in another usage, something that produces moisture.

Al-Anʿām 6:59

وَلَا رَطْبٍ وَلَا يَابِسٍ إِلَّا فِي كِتَابٍ مُبِينٍ

wa-lā raṭbin wa-lā yābisin illā fī kitābin mubīnin

Al-Anʿām 6:59

A suitable rendering:

“Nothing moist or dry exists except in a clear record.”

Raṭb here has its general sense of moist or damp.

“No fresh dates and nothing dry” would be unsuitable, since the verse uses moist and dry comprehensively.

Nor should “living and dead” replace raṭb and yābis in a direct translation.

If proposed as a figurative reading, that meaning should be examined separately.

Maryam 19:25

تُسَاقِطْ عَلَيْكِ رُطَبًا جَنِيًّا

tusāqiṭ ʿalayki ruṭaban janiyyan

Maryam 19:25

A suitable rendering:

“So that it drops fresh, ripe dates upon you.”

Ruṭaban here refers to fresh date-palm fruit.

Lexical explanations of janiyyan concern gathering while fresh or readiness for gathering.

Ruṭaban should therefore not be rendered merely as “moist fruit”: in the palm context it names dates at a specific stage of ripening.

Nor should the translation of janiyyan imply that the verse itself establishes prior picking by human hands or a specific harvest time.

The proposed Persian rendering, “fresh, ripe dates,” is natural and fits the verse. The word explanation should still preserve janiyy’s connection with gathering.

12. Conclusion

R Ṭ B in Arabic denotes moisture, dampness, and a condition opposed to dryness.

This appears in descriptions of fresh shoots, soft feathers, damp cloth, green grass, and other things possessing moisture or softness.

Within the same family, ruṭab names dates that have ripened and softened but have not yet become dried dates.

Raṭb, moist or damp, must therefore be distinguished from ruṭab, fresh dates, and ruṭb in its herbage-related usage.

These distinctions are substantive: they help determine the meaning of Qur’anic passages.

Imruʾ al-Qays’s poetry offers a significant witness in which raṭban wa-yābisan describes fresh and dry birds’ hearts.

It shows that the moist–dry contrast was familiar in early Arabic and was not restricted to dates.

R Ṭ B occurs twice in the Qur’an.

In Al-Anʿām 6:59, raṭbin opposes yābisin within the context of The God’s encompassing knowledge.

The reference is not merely to fresh vegetation or ripe dates: moist and dry appear together in an inclusive expression.

Without independently examining kitāb mubīn, however, the phrase cannot yield a complete theory of the Qur’an’s contents, compulsion and choice, or the details of divine knowledge.

In Maryam 19:25, ruṭaban names the fresh, ripe dates that fall upon Maryam as food.

Janiyyan belongs to another root; its lexical explanations concern gathering while fresh or readiness for gathering.

The dates’ ripeness, their readiness for picking, and their falling must therefore be distinguished.

Nor does the verse establish the exact date of Jesus’s birth, a specific season for Maryam’s childbirth, or a general medical instruction for all women.

Those questions require independent evidence.

Distinctions must also be maintained when comparing related roots.

Y B S concerns dryness, Ṭ R W freshness and tenderness, N Ḍ J ripening or cooking, and J N Y picking and harvesting fruit.

These meanings may coincide in a fresh, ripe fruit, but they do not constitute one root or one lexical meaning.

Final definition: R Ṭ B principally denotes moisture, dampness, and softness opposed to dryness. In general usage, raṭb describes something moist or damp; in palm-related usage, ruṭab names fresh, ripe dates that retain their freshness. The Qur’an uses the first sense in raṭbin wa-lā yābisin to express comprehensive divine knowledge, and the second in ruṭaban janiyyan for the fresh dates provided for Maryam. The kind of moisture, ripening stage, freshness of gathering, and outcome of each event must be understood through the word’s form and the verse’s context, rather than one fixed equivalent for every derivative.
