Shares and portions, drawing lots, arrows, and changes in complexion

Arabic S H M has several semantic branches. The main ones concern shares and portions, arrows and lot sticks, participation in drawing lots, and a separate field of altered color, pallor, and a worn appearance.

In division, sahm is a portion received by a person or group from a whole. In drawing lots, it can denote a stick or token used to determine a portion or select someone. Sāhama is consequently known in the sense of participating in drawing lots.

The root occurs only once in the Quran, as fa-sāhama in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:141. After going to the laden ship, Jonah participated in the drawing of lots and was among those who lost.

Sāhama directly means drawing lots or participating in the draw. Jonah’s unfavorable result is expressed separately by fa-kāna mina al-mudḥaḍīn.

Participation in allocation, the result of the draw, and its consequences must therefore be distinguished. They are connected in Jonah’s story but are not lexically identical.

1. Early Arabic usage and poetic evidence

Sahm: a portion of a whole

In early Arabic, sahm denoted a portion someone received or in which they had a claim.

Suhmah is also recorded for a share or portion.

Where several people share property, produce, or another matter, each person’s sahm can be the amount or entitlement belonging to them.

It does not necessarily imply equal portions. Shares may differ; the word relates someone to a portion, while the division determines its size, value, and basis.

The sense belongs to Arabic social and economic life: distributing property or produce, allocating portions, and dividing things claimed by several people.

Al-sahm is recorded as a portion or allotment, with plurals including as-hum, sihām, and suhmān.

Not every share is financial. Kinship, opportunities, and social situations can also involve a portion or stake.

Pre-Islamic poetry: a share in kinship

The pre-Islamic poet ʿUbayd ibn al-Abraṣ says in his well-known poem:

سَاعِدْ بِأَرْضٍ إِذَا كُنْتَ بِهَا
وَلَا تَقُلْ إِنَّنِي غَرِيبُ

قَدْ يُوصَلُ النَّازِحُ النَّائِي وَقَدْ
يُقْطَعُ ذُو السُّهْمَةِ الْقَرِيبُ

sāʿid bi-arḍin idhā kunta bihā / wa-lā taqul innanī gharību / qad yūṣalu al-nāziḥu al-nāʾī wa-qad / yuqṭaʿu dhū al-suhmati al-qarību

“Cooperate in whatever land you inhabit, and do not say, ‘I am a stranger.’ A distant outsider may receive affection, while a close relative with a bond of kinship may be cut off.”

Dhū al-suhmah identifies someone connected to another through kinship.

Early lexical explanations interpret al-suhmah here as kinship or relatedness.

The concept was therefore not confined to property division or lot arrows. It could describe the relationship giving someone a place within a family or human community.

The poet contrasts welcoming an outsider with severing a relative’s ties. Suhmah means the connection arising from kinship, rather than winning a draw.

The poem survives in the collection attributed to ʿUbayd and in early commentaries. Its attribution is familiar, although individual lines cannot generally be independently dated within the transmission of pre-Islamic poetry.

It supports the early sense of a share or family bond without establishing the origins of every S H M meaning.

Sahm: an arrow and an instrument for drawing lots

Sahm also denotes an arrow shot from a bow.

An arrow’s shaft, head, and other components are distinguishable. Qidḥ can denote the shaft, particularly before the arrow is finished.

Such sticks were also used for drawing lots in some social practices.

They could bear different markings to allocate portions or select one person among several.

The semantic connection is clear: a participant may have a token whose selection determines their allocation or position.

A fighting arrow and a lot stick nevertheless differ.

An arrow can be used for hunting or war; a lot stick for selection or allocation. Similarity in shape or name does not make their functions identical.

Early lexical accounts give different explanations of direction: from a portion to its token, or from a lot stick to the share it yields.

The connection is clear, but the historical sequence should not be treated as certain without independent evidence.

Early poetry: arrow-like patterns on cloth

The pre-Islamic poet Aws ibn Ḥajar says:

فَإِنَّا رَأَيْنَا الْعِرْضَ أَحْوَجَ سَاعَةً
إِلَى الصَّوْنِ مِنْ رَيْطٍ يَمَانٍ مُسَهَّمِ

fa-innā raʾaynā al-ʿirḍa aḥwaja sāʿatan / ilā al-ṣawni min rayṭin yamānin musahhami

“We have found honor, in a critical moment, more in need of protection than fine Yemeni cloth patterned like arrows.”

Transmitted versions differ, including raʾaynā and wajadnā and variants in the expression for the cloth.

Musahham describes cloth bearing arrow-like lines or patterns.

The familiar shape of an arrow could thus provide an image for textile decoration.

Musahham here means neither owning a share nor participating in a draw, but resemblance between cloth patterns and arrows.

The family served both allocation and the naming of physical objects and shapes.

2. Another branch: altered complexion and a worn appearance

Alongside shares, arrows, and lots, S H M has another semantic branch.

Sahama wajhu al-rajul is recorded for a man’s face changing color or appearance, including becoming pale.

Suhūm and suhām are used for altered color, leanness, and bodily wear.

Such a condition may arise from extreme heat, difficult travel, illness, grief, or other hardship.

Poetry attributed to ʿAntarah

A poem attributed to ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād says:

وَالْخَيْلُ سَاهِمَةُ الْوُجُوهِ كَأَنَّمَا
تُسْقَى فَوَارِسُهَا نَقِيعَ الْحَنْظَلِ

wa-al-khaylu sāhimatu al-wujūhi ka-annamā / tusqā fawārisuhā naqīʿa al-ḥanẓali

“The horses have drawn, altered faces, as though their riders were being given an infusion of bitter colocynth.”

Sāhimat al-wujūh concerns a changed facial appearance under hardship.

Lexical explanations discuss the horses’ appearance and the riders’ condition conveyed through the image.

The riders’ imagined bitter drink emphasizes the severity and unpleasantness of the situation.

The relevant lexical point is the change in facial color and appearance.

Sāhim here does not mean a shareholder, archer, or participant in a draw.

The line is transmitted in ʿAntarah’s attributed collection and early lexicons. Attribution to a pre-Islamic poet supports this branch’s presence in the early literary tradition, though the line’s exact date is not independently established.

Is changed complexion related to arrows and portions?

Some early analyses recognize two distinct semantic bases: a share or portion, and a change in color or appearance.

Within the second branch, sahām, with an initial a vowel, is recorded for summer heat and the heat of a scorching wind. Suhām and sahām also have uses involving leanness and altered color.

Suhām is also recorded for an ailment affecting camels. Ibil sawāhim describes camels whose bodies or appearance have been altered by travel and hardship.

These meanings concern bodily wear and change.

No clear historical evidence supplied here establishes that portions and lots originated in changed complexion, or that complexion originated in arrows or allocation.

The two attested branches should therefore remain distinct:

First, portions, arrows, and lots.

Second, altered color and bodily or facial appearance.

An invented image such as “time’s arrow striking the face” cannot replace historical etymology.

3. The Quranic form fa-sāhama

S H M occurs only once in the Quran:

Fa-sāhama — Al-Ṣāffāt 37:141

The word has two components:

Fa is a connective linking the event to the preceding narrative.

Sāhama is a third-person masculine singular past-tense verb in Form III.

Its subject is Jonah, mentioned in the preceding verses.

Here sāhama means drawing lots or taking part in the draw.

Arabic has related uses of istahama, tasāhama, and as-hama in the field of drawing lots.

Those particular forms do not occur in the Quran.

The noun sahm and plurals sihām and as-hum likewise do not occur as derivatives of this root in the Quran.

The role of Form III

Form III can express a relationship or action involving more than one party.

Sāhama in the context of lots has such a use: participants share a situation involving selection or allocation.

Its form alone does not establish equal shares, equal freedom, or identical responsibility among all participants.

Form III does not impose an absolute rule of reciprocity or equality on every Arabic verb.

“Participating in a draw” follows from this verb’s established usage, rather than its morphological pattern alone.

Sāhama by itself also means neither winning nor losing.

A participant may receive a favorable or unfavorable result.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:141 states the result in the next phrase.

4. Jonah’s draw: fa-sāhama fa-kāna mina al-mudḥaḍīn

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:139 says:

وَإِنَّ يُونُسَ لَمِنَ الْمُرْسَلِينَ

wa-inna yūnusa la-mina al-mursalīn

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:139

“Jonah was indeed one of the messengers.”

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:140 says:

إِذْ أَبَقَ إِلَى الْفُلْكِ الْمَشْحُونِ

idh abaqa ilā al-fulki al-mashḥūn

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:140

“When he fled to the laden ship.”

Then Al-Ṣāffāt 37:141 says:

فَسَاهَمَ فَكَانَ مِنَ الْمُدْحَضِينَ

fa-sāhama fa-kāna mina al-mudḥaḍīn

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:141

“Then he participated in drawing lots and was among those who lost.”

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:142 continues:

فَالْتَقَمَهُ الْحُوتُ وَهُوَ مُلِيمٌ

fa-iltaqamahu al-ḥūtu wa-huwa mulīm

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:142

“Then the fish swallowed him while he was blameworthy.”

These four verses supply the immediate context of fa-sāhama.

Drawing lots aboard the ship

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:140 describes the ship as al-mashḥūn.

Mashḥūn concerns being filled or laden.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:141, Jonah participates in the draw and loses.

Together, these verses connect the draw with the ship and its passengers.

This passage does not give the procedure’s details.

It does not specify the number of participants, what bore their names, how often the draw occurred, or who conducted it.

Some commentarial reports describe a ship endangered by its load or sea conditions, whose passengers decided to cast someone overboard.

These reports offer possible narrative circumstances, but their details are not all contained in the verse’s wording.

Lexically, fa-sāhama states participation in drawing lots.

Al-mudḥaḍīn: those who lost the draw

The verse continues:

فَكَانَ مِنَ الْمُدْحَضِينَ

fa-kāna mina al-mudḥaḍīn

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:141

“And was among those who lost.”

Al-mudḥaḍīn belongs to D Ḥ Ḍ, not S H M.

That family concerns slipping, loss of firmness, and, in extended uses, defeat or invalidation.

Here mudḥaḍīn describes those defeated or unsuccessful in the draw.

The action and its result are expressed separately:

Sāhama describes participation in the draw.

Kāna mina al-mudḥaḍīn specifies its result.

Translating fa-sāhama directly as “lost the draw” inserts the second phrase’s result into the first verb.

A more accurate rendering says he drew lots, or participated in the draw, and was among those who lost.

Does losing a draw establish guilt?

A draw is a way of selecting an outcome among possibilities.

Losing alone does not establish sin or moral wrongdoing.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:142 calls Jonah mulīm, a description associated with blameworthiness.

That description comes from another root and must be considered in relation to Jonah’s conduct and the whole story.

Neither sāhama nor mudḥaḍīn establishes that everyone who loses a draw is morally guilty.

Other verses illuminate Jonah’s conduct and its outcome, including Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:87 and Al-Qalam 68:48.

They help explain the story’s significance without changing the lexical meaning of sahm.

Did Jonah voluntarily cast himself into the sea?

After reporting his unfavorable draw, the Quran says the fish swallowed him.

The draw, its result, and his being swallowed form a narrative sequence.

The passage does not expressly state whether he jumped, others cast him overboard, or exactly what intervened.

These details should therefore not replace the meaning of sāhama in the study.

The verb concerns drawing lots; how Jonah entered the water is a separate question it does not determine.

5. Drawing lots among related Quranic passages

Casting pens to determine Mary’s guardian

Āl ʿImrān 3:44 says:

ذَٰلِكَ مِنْ أَنْبَاءِ الْغَيْبِ نُوحِيهِ إِلَيْكَ وَمَا كُنْتَ لَدَيْهِمْ إِذْ يُلْقُونَ أَقْلَامَهُمْ أَيُّهُمْ يَكْفُلُ مَرْيَمَ وَمَا كُنْتَ لَدَيْهِمْ إِذْ يَخْتَصِمُونَ

dhālika min anbāʾi al-ghaybi nūḥīhi ilayka wa-mā kunta ladayhim idh yulqūna aqlāmahum ayyuhum yakfulu maryama wa-mā kunta ladayhim idh yakhtaṣimūn

Āl ʿImrān 3:44

“This is news of the unseen that We reveal to you. You were not with them when they cast their pens to determine which of them would care for Mary, nor were you with them when they disputed.”

This verse contains no derivative of S H M.

The described action nevertheless relates to drawing lots.

A group disputed Mary’s guardianship and cast their pens to determine the guardian.

Lexical and exegetical explanations describe this as drawing lots.

Jonah’s story uses sāhama itself, whereas Mary’s story uses yulqūna and aqlāmahum.

The Quran thus uses different linguistic constructions for two events involving selection.

The comparison shows drawing lots within Quranic narratives without establishing an identical instrument or procedure for every draw.

Is every draw maysir or gambling?

In early Arabic practice, sticks allocating shares also served in some forms of maysir.

Similar tools and names do not make every use of lots gambling.

In maysir, participants can expose property or benefits to winning and losing, with one gaining at another’s expense.

A draw may instead simply select someone, determine a turn, or allocate a shared matter.

Where entitlements are already established, it can assign particular portions or turns without wagering the underlying rights.

Three matters should be distinguished: selecting by lot, wagering property, and using signs to claim knowledge of the unseen.

Historical practices may share instruments while differing in object and function.

Al-Māʾidah 5:3 mentions istiqsām bi-al-azlām among prohibited practices.

Al-Baqarah 2:219 and Al-Māʾidah 5:90 also address maysir and its implications.

Those passages do not make every social use of lots identical to maysir or the prohibited divinatory practice.

Conversely, Jonah’s draw does not itself authorize every wager or arbitrary decision about human lives and rights.

The act, what is allocated, people’s rights, and the consequences require separate examination.

Lots, rights, and justice

A draw may be used where several people have equal claims or qualifications and no other clear reason determines the choice.

It may then help avoid arbitrary preference.

Drawing lots does not automatically create an entitlement.

If something belongs to a particular person, another name’s selection alone cannot establish the loss of that right.

A decision about life, liberty, or fundamental rights is likewise not justified merely by a draw.

The Quran reports a draw in Jonah’s story; that report does not establish a general license for groups to endanger a member by lot.

Lexical meaning, a reported event, and a general ruling are distinct matters.

This distinction is essential to Quranic study.

6. Shares and participation in later usage

Modern Arabic and Persian widely use “share” in economics, ownership, and participation.

Sihām can denote company ownership shares, and musāhim someone holding a share or participating.

Musāhamah also describes contributions of work, money, or cooperation.

These uses relate to the older field of portions and allotments.

They should not be transferred uncritically to Quranic sāhama.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:141, Jonah participates in a draw. The verse does not describe buying an investment share or financing the ship’s construction.

Modern participation, in turn, need not involve drawing lots.

Someone may contribute to a school, research, investment, or social activity without any draw.

Economic and social participation belong to the family’s usage history, but Quranic translation must preserve the verb’s historically supported contextual sense.

Having a share is not identical to having a right

Someone may say, “I have a share in this work.”

This could express participation, responsibility, benefit, or influence on the result.

The word alone does not specify the share’s size or nature.

Participation also does not necessarily confer a right to perform the act.

A person may contribute to wrongdoing; their share then indicates responsibility rather than legitimacy.

Conversely, someone may benefit from a good act without participating directly.

Shares and portions relate a person to a whole or result; the ethical and legal significance depends on the subject and other evidence.

7. Comparison with related roots

S H M (sahm) and Q S M (qasm): shares and division

Q S M concerns dividing and determining portions.

The allotment branch of S H M can denote what someone receives in a division.

Dividing and having a share are related but distinct.

Division concerns separating or defining parts; a share emphasizes the resulting portion.

Lots can determine an allocation, but not every division requires them.

S H M (sahm) and N Ṣ B (naṣīb): shares and portions

Naṣīb denotes a portion allotted to someone.

Sahm also belongs to this field.

The words can be close in ordinary usage.

Sahm additionally names an arrow or lot instrument, whereas naṣīb is not ordinarily an arrow’s name.

They may be very close in property division, but their distinction becomes clearer when discussing arrows or lots.

S H M (sahm) and Q R ʿ (qurʿah): lots and shares

Physical qarʿ concerns striking or impact; qurʿah, in the same family, denotes drawing lots.

Quranic sāhama denotes participation in a draw.

They overlap in selection by lot without making all their derivatives synonymous.

Sahm can be the token or portion, while qurʿah more directly denotes the selection procedure.

“Drew lots” is therefore natural for sāhama in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:141, but not every sahm means a draw.

S H M (sahm) and R M Y (ramy): arrows and throwing

Ramy concerns throwing something.

One sense of sahm names an arrow that can be shot.

Sahm is the object or instrument; ramy is the action.

An arrow may be made for shooting, but not every act of throwing uses an arrow.

A lot stick may be cast or drawn, but that is not the same as shooting in battle.

S H M (sahm) and D Ḥ Ḍ (daḥḍ): participation and losing

The two roots appear together in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:141.

Sāhama describes entering the draw.

Mudḥaḍīn describes the unfavorable result.

The first verb concerns the procedure; the second expression concerns its outcome for the person.

Translation must retain this distinction.

Otherwise, the two parts are collapsed and mudḥaḍīn loses its independent function.

S H M (sahm) and S M M (samm): distinct roots

Quranic S M M concerns an opening and scorching wind; Arabic also records the sense of poison.

One branch of S H M concerns facial changes associated with heat and hardship.

They can approach a similar field when describing heat’s effects but remain distinct roots.

Related descriptions of heat and the body do not by themselves establish historical kinship.

Sahām for scorching heat should not be equated in every usage with samūm, a very hot wind.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

The only Quranic form is fa-sāhama in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:141.

The verse reads:

فَسَاهَمَ فَكَانَ مِنَ الْمُدْحَضِينَ

fa-sāhama fa-kāna mina al-mudḥaḍīn

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:141

Suggested translation:

“Then he participated in drawing lots and was among those who lost.”

A shorter natural rendering is:

“Then he drew lots and was among those who lost.”

This agrees with Forqan’s current translation.

The first version makes participation in a shared procedure more explicit.

Both should distinguish sāhama from the result expressed by mudḥaḍīn.

“Then he received his share” is unsuitable because it replaces drawing lots with receiving an allotment.

“Then he participated in the ship” does not convey the verb’s established contextual sense.

“Then he lost the draw” conveys the overall outcome but merges the verb with the following phrase.

For direct translation, “drew lots” or “participated in drawing lots” is preferable.

Important forms in this family

Sahm: an arrow; in another usage, a portion or share.

Sihām: a plural of sahm, for arrows or shares according to context.

As-hum: another plural of sahm.

Suhmah: a portion and, in some uses, kinship.

Sāhama: drew lots or participated with others in a draw.

Istahama: participated in a draw or drew lots.

Tasāhama: drew lots with others; more broadly, participated in allocation or a shared activity.

As-hama: allotted a share or, in another usage, conducted a draw among people.

Musahham: bearing arrow-like patterns or lines.

Sāhim al-wajh: someone with a pale or altered complexion.

Suhūm: a change in facial or bodily appearance.

Sahām: scorching heat; suhām, in recorded uses, an ailment or condition involving bodily leanness and altered color.

These forms illustrate the family, but only fa-sāhama occurs in the Quran.

Conclusion

Arabic S H M is a family with several branches.

One concerns shares, arrows, and lot sticks. Sāhama and istahama are known for participation in drawing lots.

Poetry attributed to ʿUbayd uses suhmah for a share and kinship; Aws’s poetry describes arrow-like cloth patterns.

Another branch concerns changes in facial or bodily color and condition. Poetry attributed to ʿAntarah provides early evidence.

The branches are lexically attested, but there is insufficient evidence here to assert a single historical origin for both.

The root appears only once in the Quran, as fa-sāhama in Al-Ṣāffāt 37:141.

After going to the laden ship, Jonah participated in the draw and lost.

Sāhama states the participation; mudḥaḍīn states the unfavorable outcome.

Sahm does not itself mean defeat, participation alone does not imply sin, and winning cannot automatically establish every decision’s legitimacy.

The resulting definition is:

In its allocation branch, S H M concerns a share of a whole and instruments associated with determining it, such as arrows or lot sticks. Sāhama emphasizes participating in drawing lots. Another branch concerns altered color, pallor, and bodily wear; it should not be merged with the first without historical evidence.

In Al-Ṣāffāt 37:141, fa-sāhama specifically means drawing lots or participating in the draw.

The passage also calls for distinguishing an action, its outcome, and moral judgment about a person. Drawing lots selects a result; losing is its outcome; responsibility depends on the person’s conduct and relevant evidence.

This keeps lexical meaning distinct from narrative details and unsupported ethical or legal conclusions.
