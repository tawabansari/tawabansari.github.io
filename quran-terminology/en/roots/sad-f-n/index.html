Standing in a particular posture: from the position of a horse’s legs to other uses in early Arabic

1. Introducing the root and its forms

The Arabic root Ṣ F N is best known for a kind of standing. Its prominent use describes a horse resting its weight on three legs and bending the fourth so that only the tip of its hoof touches the ground.

Arabic calls this posture ṣufūn, and a horse standing this way ṣāfin.

Ṣ F N occurs only once in the Qur’an, as al-ṣāfinātu in Ṣād 38:31.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: one active-participle occurrence

Familiar Arabic forms include:

  • صَفَنَ (ṣafana): of a horse, stood in a particular posture; of a person, stood with the feet in a certain position.
  • يَصْفِنُ (yaṣfinu): stands in ṣufūn.
  • صُفُون (ṣufūn): a standing posture, particularly a horse resting on three legs with the fourth bent.
  • صَافِن (ṣāfin): a horse or other being standing in ṣufūn.
  • صَافِنَات (ṣāfināt): the sound feminine plural of ṣāfin or ṣāfinah; the only form of this root in the Qur’an.
  • صُفْن (ṣufn): a vessel or bag for keeping equipment or water.
  • صَفَن (ṣafan): in one lexical use, the scrotal covering or sac.
  • تَصَافَنَ (taṣāfana): of water, shared it in measured portions.

These forms do not all share one narrow meaning. The standing branch must particularly be distinguished from the branch concerning a vessel or container.

2. The semantic core and early Arabic usage

Standing on three legs

The best-known meaning of ṣafana concerns a horse.

A horse can normally support its weight on four legs. When it bends one leg and places only the hoof’s tip on the ground, Arabic uses ṣafana al-faras.

Lexicographers explain this through qāma ʿalā thalāthi qawāʾim: it stood on three legs.

They then mention the tip of the fourth hoof resting on the ground.

This describes a standing posture, rather than the horse’s movement. Ṣāfin alone therefore means neither swift nor running.

Human standing

Ṣufūn is not restricted to horses.

Early sources also record ṣāfin for a person standing with the feet in a particular position. Some lexicographers explain it as standing with the feet side by side; others also accept standing more generally.

The horse’s highly specific posture is therefore not the word’s only known use throughout Arabic.

The broader human use must nevertheless not imply that Qur’anic ṣāfināt has no feature beyond standing. Each use must be examined with its own subject and context.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Miṣbāḥ al-Munīr, and Lisān al-ʿArab: standing in horses and people

Arabic poetic witnesses

Ibn al-Aʿrābī transmits a line describing a horse:

أَلِفَ الصُّفُونَ فَلَا يَزَالُ كَأَنَّهُ
مِمَّا يَقُومُ عَلَى الثَّلَاثِ كَسِيرَا

alifa al-ṣufūna fa-lā yazālu ka-annahu / mimmā yaqūmu ʿalā al-thalāthi kasīrā

Translation:

“It has grown accustomed to ṣufūn; from standing so often on three legs, it continually appears as though its leg were injured.”

Al-ṣufūn concerns the position of the horse’s legs. The comparison with an injured creature arises from the unusual appearance of its stance.

The poet does not necessarily mean that the horse is actually injured or lame. Its way of standing evokes that image.

Lexical sources transmit the line without clearly identifying the poet or composition date in the account examined. It may attest Arabic usage, but should not be declared certainly pre-Islamic without independent evidence.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Asās al-Balāghah: the witness transmitted by Ibn al-Aʿrābī

The vessel and container branch

Alongside standing, Ṣ F N has another Arabic use.

Al-ṣufn denotes a bag or vessel in which a shepherd or traveler could keep food, equipment, or needed water.

The word is also used for a small leather water vessel.

Al-ṣafan is likewise recorded for the covering or sac containing the testicles.

This branch highlights keeping something within a covering or container.

Taṣāfana al-qawmu al-māʾa concerns sharing water among several people. Explanations describe a small vessel or measuring each person’s portion by enough water to cover a pebble.

Al-Farazdaq says:

فَلَمَّا تَصَافَنَّا الْإِدَاوَةَ أَجْهَشَتْ
إِلَيَّ غُضُونُ الْعَنْبَرِيِّ الْجُرَاضِمِ

fa-lammā taṣāfannā al-idāwata ajhashat / ilayya ghuḍūnu al-ʿanbariyyi al-jurāḍimi

A rendering close to the wording:

“When we shared the waterskin’s water, the wrinkled face of that gluttonous ʿAnbarī turned toward me and burst into tears.”

Lisān al-ʿArab, JRḌM: jurāḍim meaning gluttonous

The important feature is taṣāfannā, sharing water, rather than a horse standing.

Al-Farazdaq was an Umayyad poet. The line therefore attests early Arabic after Islam, rather than pre-Islamic usage.

Al-Khaṭṭābī’s Gharīb al-Ḥadīth: Al-Farazdaq’s witness and measuring water with a pebble

Do all these meanings share one origin?

Lexicographers differ in explaining the relationship between these uses.

Ibn Fāris records two semantic foundations for Ṣ F N in Maqāyīs al-Lughah: a kind of standing and a vessel or container.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: two foundations, standing and a vessel

In Al-Mufradāt, Al-Rāghib al-Isfahānī seeks to explain the family through bringing parts close together and gathering them. He connects the position of a horse’s legs and some container uses through this image.

The relationship can be examined as a semantic explanation: ṣufūn involves the arrangement of legs, while a vessel gathers something within a container.

Such resemblance alone, however, does not establish that all branches historically arose from one original image.

A sound definition should therefore preserve both established branches, rather than replace actual evidence of usage with their possible relationship.

3. The Qur’anic use: al-ṣāfinātu al-jiyād

The only Qur’anic occurrence belongs to Solomon’s story:

إِذْ عُرِضَ عَلَيْهِ بِالْعَشِيِّ الصَّافِنَاتُ الْجِيَادُ

idh ʿuriḍa ʿalayhi bi-al-ʿashiyyi al-ṣāfinātu al-jiyādu

“When, in the evening, the standing, swift horses were presented before him.”

Ṣād 38:31

The preceding verse describes Solomon as a good servant who repeatedly turns back. The passage then mentions the horses being presented in the evening.

The meaning of al-ṣāfināt

Al-ṣāfināt is the sound feminine plural of ṣāfin or ṣāfinah, an active participle of ṣafana.

The word applies to horses here.

The feminine plural does not by itself establish that every horse was female: Arabic nonhuman plurals can take feminine constructions.

Al-ṣāfināt therefore refers to horses standing in ṣufūn when presented.

The familiar lexical description is resting on three legs while bending the fourth.

This posture can suggest calm standing and bodily poise. In descriptions of horses, it has sometimes been regarded as a sign of beauty and a quality of good horses.

The word itself, however, does not directly mean purebred, costly, warhorse, or ready to charge. Such features require additional evidence.

Distinguishing al-ṣāfināt from al-jiyād

Two expressions occur together:

الصَّافِنَاتُ الْجِيَادُ (al-ṣāfinātu al-jiyād)

Ṣāfināt describes the horses’ standing posture, while al-jiyād concerns their quality and agility.

Jiyād is a plural of jawād applied to a horse: a good, swift horse with considerable running ability.

The descriptions thus concern different aspects: the horse’s standing posture and its quality as a good, agile horse.

This clarifies the verse’s image. The horses can be standing when presented while also being swift horses.

Running speed must not be inferred from ṣāfināt alone, nor should jiyād be reduced to standing on three legs.

Study of J W D

Does ṣāfināt mean horses ready to move?

Some exegetical explanations regard ṣufūn as a sign of a horse’s readiness to move.

That reading may arise from the description of agile horses and their appearance. The familiar lexical definition of ṣafana itself, however, concerns a kind of standing.

Readiness to move may therefore describe the horse’s possible condition, rather than the root’s direct, invariable meaning.

Nor can the word alone establish the horses’ number, whether they had wings, their origin, or a military purpose for their presentation.

The verse itself states only that horses with the two descriptions were presented.

4. Comparing related roots

Ṣ F N (ṣafana) and Ṣ F F (ṣaffa)

Ṣ F F concerns placing objects beside one another, arranging rows, and forming ranks.

The Qur’an says:

إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُحِبُّ الَّذِينَ يُقَاتِلُونَ فِي سَبِيلِهِ صَفًّا كَأَنَّهُمْ بُنْيَانٌ مَرْصُوصٌ

inna allāha yuḥibbu alladhīna yuqātilūna fī sabīlihi ṣaffan ka-annahum bunyānun marṣūṣun

“Indeed, The God loves those who fight in His way in ranks, as though they were a firm, closely joined building.”

Al-Ṣaff 61:4

Ṣaffan describes people’s arrangement in a rank.

Ṣāfin, applied to a horse, describes its leg position and manner of standing.

Although both roots can evoke parts positioned beside one another, their meanings are not identical.

Study of Ṣ F F

Distinguishing ṣawāffa from ṣawāfin

Al-Ḥajj says:

فَاذْكُرُوا اسْمَ اللَّهِ عَلَيْهَا صَوَافَّ

fa-udhkurū isma allāhi ʿalayhā ṣawāffa

“So mention The God’s name over them as they stand aligned.”

Al-Ḥajj 22:36

Ṣawāffa in the familiar Qur’anic reading comes from Ṣ F F, rather than Ṣ F N.

Some transmitted readings report ṣawāfin, which belongs to Ṣ F N. Explanations describe animals with one leg bound, standing on three legs.

This transmitted variant is not the verse’s standard form in the ten canonical readings.

The reports show that standing and leg positions can form a shared field between the expressions. That proximity must not erase the distinction between their root letters.

In counting derivatives in the familiar Qur’anic text, ṣawāffa belongs to Ṣ F F and al-ṣāfināt to Ṣ F N.

Al-Ṭabarī: ṣawāff and ṣawāfin in transmitted readings

Ṣ F N (ṣafana) and Q W M (qāma)

Qāma is a more general verb for rising, standing, and being upright.

Ṣafana, in its familiar use concerning horses, expresses a particular kind of standing.

Every horse in ṣufūn is therefore standing, but not every standing horse is in ṣufūn.

Since some human uses of ṣufūn approach standing generally, the distinction must nevertheless not become an exceptionless rule for all historical Arabic usage.

5. Translation proposals for Forqan

Ṣafana: stood in a particular posture; of a horse, rested on three legs and bent the fourth.

Ṣufūn: a particular standing posture, especially a horse standing on three legs with the tip of the fourth hoof on the ground.

Ṣāfin: standing in ṣufūn.

Al-ṣāfināt: horses standing in ṣufūn; standing horses in fluent translation, with their particular leg position explained in lexical notes.

Ṣufn: a bag or vessel for keeping equipment or water.

Taṣāfana al-qawmu al-māʾa: they shared the water in measured portions.

The current Persian Forqan rendering of Ṣād 38:31 means:

“When, in the evening, the standing, swift horses were presented before him.”

This suits the base text: standing conveys the first description naturally, while swift expresses the second.

The details of standing on three legs and bending the fourth are better explained in the word box and root article than included in the entire short verse translation.

6. Conclusion

Ṣ F N has two established Arabic branches: a kind of standing and uses concerning vessels and containers.

In the first, ṣufūn describes a horse resting its weight on three legs and bending the fourth, with its hoof’s tip possibly touching the ground. Human uses also concern standing and foot positions.

In the second, words such as ṣufn denote a vessel or bag. Connections through gathering or bringing parts close together have been suggested, but their single historical origin is not certain.

The Qur’an uses only al-ṣāfināt, referring to the horses presented before Solomon. It describes their standing posture, whereas al-jiyād describes their quality and swiftness.

Brief root meaning for Forqan:

A kind of standing, especially a horse resting on three legs with the fourth bent; in another branch, a vessel or container for keeping something.

Meaning in Ṣād 38:31:

Al-ṣāfināt: horses standing in the particular posture called ṣufūn, supported on three legs with the fourth bent.
