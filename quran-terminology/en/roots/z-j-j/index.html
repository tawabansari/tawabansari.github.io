Central meanings: in Qur’anic usage, glass and a vessel made from it; in other early Arabic branches, slenderness of form, a spear’s metal butt, and driving or throwing.

1. The root and its pre-Islamic meanings

Z J J is a doubled Arabic root: its second and third consonants are identical. Its several meanings should not be reduced to a single image without historical investigation.

In pre-Islamic Arabic, zujājah denoted a glass vessel used for holding or drinking liquids. Transparency, brilliance, and visibility of the contents were relevant to its description.

Zujāj names glass itself: a hard, generally transparent and brittle material used for vessels and other objects.

Pre-Islamic poetry directly attests this meaning; it is not merely a definition later lexicographers inferred from the Qur’an.

Alongside it, zujj denoted a narrow metal fitting at the lower end of a spear. Zajaj described slender, elongated eyebrows, while zajja could mean throwing or driving something.

An early lexical analysis describes zajj as follows:

الزَّاءُ وَالْجِيمُ أَصْلٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى رِقَّةٍ فِي شَيْءٍ

al-zāʾu wa-al-jīmu aṣlun yadullu ʿalā riqqatin fī shayʾin

Meaning: “Zāy and jīm indicate thinness or slenderness in something.”

The spear’s butt and slender eyebrows illustrate that analysis.

It illuminates zujj and zajaj, but does not independently establish that zujāj, glass, historically arose from the same image of thinness.

Attested meanings, root classification, and each word’s historical origin must therefore be distinguished.

2. Glass in pre-Islamic Arabic

First example: a glass vessel in ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād’s Muʿallaqah

The pre-Islamic poet ʿAntarah ibn Shaddād says:

بِزُجَاجَةٍ صَفْرَاءَ ذَاتِ أَسِرَّةٍ
قُرِنَتْ بِأَزْهَرَ فِي الشِّمَالِ مُفَدَّمِ

bi-zujājatin ṣafrāʾa dhāti asirratin / qurinat bi-azhara fī al-shimāli mufaddami

The verse means:

“With a yellow-tinged glass cup bearing visible lines, paired with a bright ewer in the server’s left hand, its mouth covered.”

The preceding verse describes drinking wine, so zujājah here is an actual drinking vessel.

In explanations of this verse, zujājah denotes a drinking vessel and asirrah the visible lines or markings on it.

The yellow color requires care: it may belong to the drink inside. The verse does not establish that the glass material itself was yellow.

The example shows pre-Islamic use of zujājah for a glass vessel familiar enough to be mentioned without explaining its material or manufacture.

Its pre-Qur’anic meaning can thus be expressed as follows:

In pre-Islamic Arabic, zujājah was a glass vessel used for holding or drinking liquids.

The example establishes familiarity with glass vessels, but not their place of manufacture or the tribes among whom glassmaking was practiced.

Second example: zijāj in Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā’s poetry

The pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā says:

وَمَنْ يَعْصِ أَطْرَافَ الزِّجَاجِ فَإِنَّهُ
يُطِيعُ الْعَوَالِي رُكِّبَتْ كُلَّ لَهْذَمِ

wa-man yaʿṣi aṭrāfa al-zijāji fa-innahu / yuṭīʿu al-ʿawālī rukkibat kulla lahdhami

The verse means:

“Whoever rejects an offer of peace presented with the spear-butts will be compelled to yield to their sharp spearheads.”

Zujj denoted the metal fitting at the lower end of a spear. Unlike the sinān, the piercing head at its top, the zujj was fitted to the opposite end.

Zijāj here is the plural of zujj, not a reference to glass.

Lexical explanations describe presenting spear-butts as a sign of willingness to make peace, whereas turning the spearheads toward an enemy signaled readiness for battle.

The example situates the word in pre-Islamic warfare and weaponry.

Comparing the two poets makes an important distinction clear:

Zujājah names a glass vessel, whereas zijāj, with kasrah on the zāy, can name spear-butts collectively.

Shared consonants do not make the meanings identical.

3. Principal forms and derivatives

Zujāj, zajāj, and zijāj

All three vocalizations are recorded for glass, although zujāj is particularly familiar.

Zijāj also has the distinct use as the plural of zujj, a spear’s metal butt.

Vocalization and context therefore help determine the meaning.

Zujājah

This denotes a glass vessel or an object made of glass.

It is recorded for a drinking vessel and also a glass lamp.

It is the only derivative of Z J J found in the Qur’an.

Zujj

A narrow metal fitting at the lower end of a spear.

It differs from the piercing point, or sinān.

It was also used to plant and steady a spear in the ground.

Zajja yazujju

To drive, throw, or forcefully cast something away.

Zajja bi-al-shayʾi min yadihi means throwing something from one’s hand.

This concerns motion and casting away, not the transparency or brittleness of glass.

Zajjaja al-rumḥa

To fit a spear with its metal butt.

Azajja al-rumḥa is also recorded in this sense, although some accounts differ concerning particular uses of that form.

Zajaj

Of eyebrows, slenderness and length, sometimes with a graceful curve.

Ḥājibun azajju describes a slender, elongated eyebrow with a distinct curve.

Zajjajati al-marʾatu ḥājibahā describes a woman shaping, thinning, or making her eyebrow appear longer.

Azajj and zajjāʾ

Applied to people or birds, azajj can describe different bodily features.

Of a male ostrich it can denote long legs and an extended stride. Its feminine form is zajjāʾ.

These uses concern bodily form and elongation.

Zajjāj

A person who makes glass; also used for someone who sells it.

The word functions as an occupational designation in Arabic.

It does not occur in the Qur’an and should not be confused with zujājah.

4. Qur’anic usage

Z J J occurs in the Qur’an only in Al-Nūr 24:35.

Zujājah appears twice in that verse:

First as indefinite zujājatin, then as definite al-zujājatu.

No other known derivative of the root occurs in the Qur’an.

Al-Nūr 24:35

اللَّهُ نُورُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ ۚ مَثَلُ نُورِهِ كَمِشْكَاةٍ فِيهَا مِصْبَاحٌ ۖ الْمِصْبَاحُ فِي زُجَاجَةٍ ۖ الزُّجَاجَةُ كَأَنَّهَا كَوْكَبٌ دُرِّيٌّ يُوقَدُ مِنْ شَجَرَةٍ مُبَارَكَةٍ زَيْتُونَةٍ لَا شَرْقِيَّةٍ وَلَا غَرْبِيَّةٍ يَكَادُ زَيْتُهَا يُضِيءُ وَلَوْ لَمْ تَمْسَسْهُ نَارٌ ۚ نُورٌ عَلَىٰ نُورٍ ۗ يَهْدِي اللَّهُ لِنُورِهِ مَنْ يَشَاءُ ۚ وَيَضْرِبُ اللَّهُ الْأَمْثَالَ لِلنَّاسِ ۗ وَاللَّهُ بِكُلِّ شَيْءٍ عَلِيمٌ

Allāhu nūru al-samāwāti wa-al-arḍi mathalu nūrihi ka-mishkātin fīhā miṣbāḥun al-miṣbāḥu fī zujājatin al-zujājatu ka-annahā kawkabun durriyyun yūqadu min shajaratin mubārakatin zaytūnatin lā sharqiyyatin wa-lā gharbiyyatin yakādu zaytuhā yuḍīʾu wa-law lam tamsashu nārun nūrun ʿalā nūrin yahdī Allāhu li-nūrihi man yashāʾu wa-yaḍribu Allāhu al-amthāla li-al-nāsi wa-Allāhu bi-kulli shayʾin ʿalīm

Al-Nūr 24:35

Translation:

“The God is the light of the heavens and the earth. The likeness of His light is as a niche containing a lamp; the lamp is in a glass vessel, and the glass is as though it were a radiant star. The lamp is lit from a blessed olive tree, neither eastern nor western. Its oil almost shines even though no fire has touched it. Light upon light. The God guides whom He wills to His light and sets forth examples for people; and The God knows all things.”

First occurrence: fī zujājatin

الْمِصْبَاحُ فِي زُجَاجَةٍ

al-miṣbāḥu fī zujājatin

Al-Nūr 24:35

“The lamp is in a glass vessel.”

Here zujājah is the glass vessel or enclosure containing the lamp.

The preposition fī expresses the lamp’s location inside it.

This matters because miṣbāḥ and zujājah designate different things.

Miṣbāḥ is the lamp, the lighting device; zujājah is its glass vessel or covering.

Zujājah therefore does not here denote the flame or light source itself.

Although some Arabic uses call a glass lamp a zujājah, this verse distinguishes the vessel from the lamp it contains.

Second occurrence: al-zujājatu ka-annahā kawkabun durriyyun

الزُّجَاجَةُ كَأَنَّهَا كَوْكَبٌ دُرِّيٌّ

al-zujājatu ka-annahā kawkabun durriyyun

Al-Nūr 24:35

“The glass is as though it were a radiant star.”

Al-zujājatu refers back to the glass introduced in the preceding sentence.

The verse now highlights its visible qualities within the image of light.

With the lamp, the glass appears so radiant that it is likened to a bright star.

This accords with glass transmitting light and displaying brilliance.

Zujājah itself does not lexically mean star, light, or brilliance. Those qualities come from the simile and kawkabun durriyyun.

“Glass” or “glass vessel” therefore suffices as a direct equivalent. The description of brilliance belongs in the following clause.

5. Glass within the example of light

Al-Nūr 24:35 brings several tangible elements together:

A niche, a lamp, glass, an olive tree, oil, and fire.

Each contributes to the image of illumination.

The glass is neither an independent light source nor fuel. It contains the lamp, allows its light to be seen, and itself appears brilliant.

Transparency matters here: a completely opaque vessel would not produce the same image of a shining lamp within.

The comparison to a radiant star also shows that more than the mere presence of a container is intended; its luminous appearance matters.

Nevertheless, lexical interpretation must not impose an exact vessel shape, glass thickness, cutting pattern, refractive index, or lamp engineering upon the text.

The Qur’an gives no such details.

Literal and figurative meaning

The verse presents the likeness of divine light through familiar elements of human life.

At the literal level of the image, zujājah is a glass vessel.

In figurative reflection, that vessel forms part of an image linking light, radiance, and guidance.

Some interpretations liken the glass to the human heart and the lamp to faith or guidance.

This may be examined as a figurative reading, but it does not change the lexical meaning of zujājah to “human heart.”

The verse does not say that the Arabic word for glass means heart. Any such correspondence requires support from the complete example and related passages about light, hearts, and guidance.

The literal meaning remains glass.

6. Distinguishing related words

Z J J (zujāj) and Q R R (qawārīr)

The Qur’an also uses qawārīr for glass or glasslike vessels.

Al-Naml 27:44 describes ṣarḥun mumarradun min qawārīra, a smooth glass structure in the account of Solomon and the queen of Sheba.

This shares the glass meaning of zujājah, but the two words have different roots.

Qawārīr is the plural of qārūrah, a word used for a glass vessel. Calling it simply any container would overlook the glass-related meaning attested in this word.

Qawārīr also appears in Al-Insān 76:15 and Al-Insān 76:16. The expression qawārīra min fiḍḍatin shows that the physical properties of ordinary glass vessels should not automatically be imposed on every occurrence.

Shared reference to a glass vessel does not make the roots or their full semantic ranges identical.

Z J J (zujājah) and Ṣ B Ḥ (miṣbāḥ)

Miṣbāḥ in Al-Nūr 24:35 is the lighting device.

Zujājah is the vessel containing it.

The verse explicitly distinguishes them through al-miṣbāḥu fī zujājatin.

Equating them in translation would erase part of the image’s structure.

Z J J (zujājah) and N W R (nūr)

Nūr denotes light.

Zujājah denotes the glass vessel through which light can pass and which appears radiant with the lamp.

Their meanings are distinct.

Glass is associated with light in the example, but that association does not make illumination part of the word’s lexical definition.

Z J J (zajja) and Z J W (zajā)

Zajja can also mean driving or throwing.

Z J W concerns driving, advancing, or moving something, as in driving clouds forward.

The roots resemble each other in sound and some uses, but their structures differ.

Z J J is doubled; Z J W has a final wāw.

Driving or throwing in one branch of Z J J should not be transferred to the Qur’anic glass vessel.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

The principal Qur’anic equivalent of zujājah is “glass.”

“Glass vessel” or “glass enclosure” may clarify its distinction from the lamp itself.

Other lexical forms may be rendered as follows. These proposals do not replace installed verse translations or authored word-box explanations.

Zujāj: glass, the material.

Zujājah: glass, a glass vessel, or a glass lamp, depending on context.

Zujj: a spear’s metal butt.

Zijāj: in the spear-related usage, the plural of zujj.

Zajja bi-al-shayʾi: drove or threw something.

Zajjaja al-rumḥa: fitted the spear with its metal butt.

Zajaj: slenderness, length, and curvature of eyebrows.

Azajj: slender and elongated, of eyebrows; or long-striding in certain applications to people and birds.

Zajjāj: a glassmaker or glass seller.

For al-miṣbāḥu fī zujājatin in Al-Nūr 24:35, “the lamp is in a glass vessel” is concise and faithful.

For al-zujājatu ka-annahā kawkabun durriyyun, “the glass is as though it were a radiant star” is suitable.

An explanation can identify the lamp’s glass vessel, but “glass” should not be replaced in translation by heart, faith, light, or another figurative correspondence.

8. Conclusion

Z J J has several Arabic semantic branches that must be distinguished.

In pre-Islamic Arabic, zujājah named a glass vessel, directly attested in ʿAntarah’s Muʿallaqah.

In the same period, zujj denoted a spear’s metal butt and zijāj its plural, as Zuhayr’s poem illustrates.

Other uses concern slender, elongated eyebrows, bodily features, driving, and throwing.

This shared lexical classification does not establish a single historical origin for all meanings. The analysis through slenderness illuminates zujj and zajaj; Qur’anic zujājah requires its own evidence.

Only zujājah occurs in the Qur’an, twice in Al-Nūr 24:35.

It is the vessel containing the lamp, described as so brilliant that it resembles a radiant star.

Its direct meaning remains glass or a glass vessel. Brilliance follows from the context and simile, while ethical or figurative correspondences require separate examination.

This distinction preserves the word’s familiar pre-Qur’anic meaning while preventing the figurative image from replacing its lexical definition.
