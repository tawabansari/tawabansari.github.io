The visible figure, rising, and a fixed gaze: from early Arabic usage to the Quranic image of staring eyes

In early Arabic, SH KH Ṣ is associated with emerging, rising, and being visible. شَخْص denoted the visible body or figure of a human or another being, especially at a distance. شَخَصَ could describe rising or emerging, departing from one place for another, or the lifting and fixing of the gaze.

Several uses can be distinguished: a figure visible to the eye, something protruding above a surface, a traveler setting out, and a gaze that rises and remains fixed.

These images are related, but their historical derivation from a single original movement cannot simply be assumed. The visible figure and departure belong to the same word family; that alone does not prove which sense developed first.

The root occurs twice in the Quran: تَشْخَصُ in Ibrahim 14:42 and شَاخِصَةٌ in Al-Anbiya 21:97. Both concern human eyes and gazes in scenes of divine judgment.

Here, the eyes remain open and fixed, departing from their ordinary state. Fear, bewilderment, and the shock of confronting reality come from the context; the root does not mean fear in every use.

1. The root in early Arabic

Shakhṣ: a figure seen at a distance

In early Arabic, الشَّخْص denoted a visible human or other figure. When someone was seen at a distance before their features or identity could be distinguished, what was visible was their shakhṣ.

Early lexicons explain this as سواد الإنسان وغيره: the outline or figure visible at a distance.

Sawād here need not indicate black skin; it refers to a discernible shape before the observer.

Thus, a shakhṣ can stand out from its surroundings even when the observer does not yet know who the person is.

A passage attributed to the early Quḍāʿī poet ʿAwf ibn Subayʿ describes old age and failing sight:

وَصَارَ كَفَرْخِ النَّسْرِ يَهْتَزُّ جِيدُهُ
يَرَى دُونَ شَخْصِ الْمَرْءِ شَخْصًا إِذَا رَأَى

wa-ṣāra ka-farkhi al-nasri yahtazzu jīduhu / yarā dūna shakhṣi al-marʾi shakhṣan idhā raʾā

The sense of the line is:

“He has become like a vulture chick whose neck trembles; when he looks, he sees another figure before the figure of a person.”

The poet depicts impaired sight in old age: another image appears before the single figure being viewed.

شخص المرء denotes the visible human figure, not moral character, social standing, or psychological traits.

The passage survives in older collections about exceptionally long-lived people and is attributed to ʿAwf ibn Subayʿ. Details of his life and extraordinary longevity are not all historically secure. The line illustrates an old usage, but its precise date should not be assigned without evidence.

This distinguishes the older shakhṣ from the modern concept of personality. Personality can encompass psychological, moral, and social characteristics; that concept should not be presented as the original meaning of shakhṣ in pre-Islamic Arabic.

Shukhūṣ: rising and setting out

Early Arabic also used شَخَصَ for rising and moving away from one's place.

شَخَصَ مِنْ بَلَدِهِ means that he left his town and set out. شُخُوص can describe a traveler's departure from their residence.

Al-Aʿshā, a poet of the late pre-Islamic period, says:

لَعَمْرِي لَئِنْ أَمْسَى مِنَ الْحَيِّ شَاخِصًا
لَقَدْ نَالَ خَيْصًا مِنْ عُفَيْرَةَ خَائِصًا

la-ʿamrī laʾin amsā mina al-ḥayyi shākhiṣan / la-qad nāla khayṣan min ʿUfayrata khāʾiṣan

The sense is that, if the person has departed from the community, he has gained only a little from ʿUfayrah. The verbs describe him in the third person; they do not identify the departing person as the speaker.

شاخصا describes someone leaving their residence and setting out elsewhere.

Thus, shukhūṣ can express travel and departure, as well as the visibility of a figure at a distance.

Al-Aʿshā lived close to the period of Quranic revelation, but the line's exact date is unknown. It witnesses the language of that broad period without dating the origin of this meaning.

Lexical explanations also connect shukhūṣ with ارتفاع, rising; هبوط, by contrast, denotes descent.

Yet saying that someone shakhaṣa from their town does not necessarily mean they traveled to a geographically higher location. Departure is the salient meaning here.

Travel in this usage should not always be restricted to upward movement.

Shukhūṣ al-baṣar: a raised, fixed gaze

شُخُوصُ الْبَصَر is the branch directly relevant to the Quran.

In early Arabic, شَخَصَ بَصَرُهُ described a person's eyes or gaze rising and remaining fixed. Lexical explanations also associate it with open eyes and an absence of blinking.

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Afwah al-Awdī says:

وَمَا خِلْتُ يُجْدِينِي أُسَاتِي وَقَدْ بَدَتْ
مَفَاصِلُ أَوْصَالِي وَقَدْ شَخَصَ الْبَصَرُ

wa-mā khiltu yujdīnī usātī wa-qad badat / mafāṣilu awṣālī wa-qad shakhaṣa al-baṣar

“I did not think my healers would help me, when the joints of my limbs had become visible and my gaze had become fixed.”

The poet speaks of old age, weakness, and approaching death.

شَخَصَ الْبَصَر describes a gaze that has ceased its ordinary movement and become fixed.

This witness is particularly relevant because أبصار is likewise the subject of shukhūṣ in both Quranic occurrences.

The line is attributed to al-Afwah al-Awdī in older sources, with variants in some words. وَقَدْ شَخَصَ الْبَصَرُ is shared by familiar versions. Nevertheless, neither attribution nor precise date can be established with complete certainty.

Here, shukhūṣ al-baṣar accompanies approaching death; the expression itself does not mean death.

Eyes can also remain fixed in severe illness, fear, bewilderment, or confrontation with a shocking event.

Death belongs to the poet's situation rather than the basic meaning of shakhaṣa.

Protruding and becoming visible

Older Arabic dictionaries record other uses of this family.

شَخَصَ الْجُرْحُ describes a wound protruding or swelling: part of the body rises above its ordinary surface.

شَخَصَ السَّهْمُ describes an arrow passing above its target, highlighting its trajectory relative to the target.

شَخَصَ النَّجْمُ likewise refers to a star appearing or rising.

These uses concern elevation and visibility. Their recording in later dictionaries does not establish that every one occurred with the same form and frequency in pre-Islamic poetry.

The evidence places this root in several neighboring fields: the visible figure, rising or departing, protrusion, and the fixed gaze.

The gaze branch provides the direct evidence for its Quranic use.

2. Important forms and derivatives

The SH KH Ṣ family has several established Arabic forms.

شَخْص: the visible body or figure of a human or another being; in human reference, it can also denote the individual.

أَشْخَاص، شُخُوص، شِخَاص: plurals of shakhṣ. The plural shukhūṣ, figures, must be distinguished from the verbal noun shukhūṣ, rising, movement, or departure.

شَخَصَ / يَشْخَصُ: rose, emerged, became visible, or departed. With the eye or gaze as subject, it conveys staring or remaining open and fixed.

شُخُوص: the verbal noun of shakhaṣa, expressing emergence, rising, or movement. In شخوص البصر, it denotes a fixed gaze.

شَاخِص: protruding, visible, departing, or staring; its precise meaning depends on its subject and context.

شَاخِصَة: the feminine form of shākhiṣ, occurring in the Quran.

أَشْخَصَ: caused someone to leave, sent them away, or transferred them. In archery, it can also describe raising an arrow or sending it above the target.

إِشْخَاص: moving or sending someone from one place to another.

شَخِيص: large-bodied or conspicuous in build.

شَخَاصَة: largeness or prominence of the body; older sources differ over its verbal derivation.

شَخَّصَ and تَشْخِيص: identifying or distinguishing a thing or person from others. Modern medical diagnosis belongs to this field, but its specialized meaning must not be projected onto all older uses.

Only تَشْخَصُ and شَاخِصَةٌ occur in the Quran.

The noun shakhṣ, its plurals, ashkhaṣa, and tashkhīṣ do not occur there in these forms.

Despite the family's breadth in Arabic, its Quranic use is limited to the state of the eyes and gaze in a scene of dread.

3. Quranic occurrences

Ibrahim 14:42: staring eyes on the day of judgment

The Quran says:

وَلَا تَحْسَبَنَّ اللَّهَ غَافِلًا عَمَّا يَعْمَلُ الظَّالِمُونَ ۚ إِنَّمَا يُؤَخِّرُهُمْ لِيَوْمٍ تَشْخَصُ فِيهِ الْأَبْصَارُ

wa-lā taḥsabanna allāha ghāfilan ʿammā yaʿmalu al-ẓālimūna innamā yuʾakhkhiruhum li-yawmin tashkhaṣu fīhi al-abṣār

Ibrāhīm 14:42

“Never suppose that The God is unaware of what the wrongdoers do. He only delays them until a day when eyes will stare fixedly.”

تَشْخَصُ is an imperfect verb from SH KH Ṣ; الْأَبْصَارُ is its subject.

The verse first addresses the deeds of wrongdoers, warning that their respite does not mean that The God is unaware of their conduct.

It then introduces a day when eyes will remain fixed.

The direct meaning of tashkhaṣu is this particular state of the gaze. Its terrifying cause comes from the context of divine judgment and confrontation with the consequences of wrongdoing.

For comparison, when unexpectedly confronted by a shocking reality, a person may stare at one point briefly and their ordinary eye movement may change.

This makes the lexical image intelligible. The verse, however, concerns the day of reckoning rather than an ordinary, passing daily reaction.

The Quran itself develops the image in the following verse.

Ibrahim 14:43

مُهْطِعِينَ مُقْنِعِي رُءُوسِهِمْ لَا يَرْتَدُّ إِلَيْهِمْ طَرْفُهُمْ ۖ وَأَفْئِدَتُهُمْ هَوَاءٌ

muhṭiʿīna muqniʿī ruʾūsihim lā yartaddu ilayhim ṭarfuhum wa-afʾidatuhum hawāʾ

Ibrāhīm 14:43

“Hurrying, with their heads raised; their gaze does not return to them, and their hearts are empty.”

The verse places several features together.

مُقْنِعِي رُءُوسِهِمْ describes the position of their heads.

لَا يَرْتَدُّ إِلَيْهِمْ طَرْفُهُمْ describes their gaze failing to return.

وَأَفْئِدَتُهُمْ هَوَاءٌ adds an image of their inward condition.

This helps explain tashkhaṣu. Their gaze does not readily turn away, shift, or return to its ordinary state.

The entire content of Ibrahim 14:43 must not be placed inside the root's definition.

The fixed gaze comes from tashkhaṣu; raised heads from their own phrase; the gaze not returning from لا يرتد إليهم طرفهم; and the condition of the hearts from أفئدتهم هواء.

These are components of a connected image rather than synonyms for one word.

Shukhūṣ here does not mean becoming blind. Open eyes and a fixed gaze describe a different condition from blindness.

The verse depicts eyes staring before divine judgment, without asserting a definite loss of sight.

Distinguishing shukhūṣ from fear and bewilderment

The observable state must be distinguished from its cause.

The observable state is the fixed gaze.

The context places it on the day of confrontation with divine judgment and the consequences of wrongdoing.

Fear, bewilderment, and shock are therefore appropriate contextual explanations, but tashkhaṣu should not be restricted to “fear” in a dictionary.

Wonder, intense attention, or an unexpected event can also leave a gaze fixed. The root describes the gaze; the sentence identifies the feeling and its cause.

In Ibrahim 14:42, this condition concerns the accountability of wrongdoers. They received respite in worldly life and now face a day they cannot pass through by disregarding their deeds' consequences.

The image belongs to a warning about responsibility and reckoning, rather than a description of an eye disease or neurological disorder.

Al-Anbiya 21:97: the staring eyes of those who disbelieved

The second occurrence is in Al-Anbiya:

وَاقْتَرَبَ الْوَعْدُ الْحَقُّ فَإِذَا هِيَ شَاخِصَةٌ أَبْصَارُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا يَا وَيْلَنَا قَدْ كُنَّا فِي غَفْلَةٍ مِّنْ هَٰذَا بَلْ كُنَّا ظَالِمِينَ

wa-iqtaraba al-waʿdu al-ḥaqqu fa-idhā hiya shākhiṣatun abṣāru alladhīna kafarū yā waylanā qad kunnā fī ghaflatin min hādhā bal kunnā ẓālimīn

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:97

“And the true promise draws near; suddenly the eyes of those who disbelieved stare fixedly: Woe to us! We were heedless of this; indeed, we were wrongdoers.”

This verse uses the active participle شَاخِصَةٌ rather than the verb tashkhaṣu.

The sentence depicts the state of the disbelievers' eyes becoming manifest as the true promise approaches.

فَإِذَا emphasizes the sudden appearance of this condition.

شَاخِصَةٌ أَبْصَارُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا describes eyes fixed in a stare at that moment.

Alongside the judgment against wrongdoing described in Ibrahim 14:42, this verse also quotes the group's own words:

يَا وَيْلَنَا قَدْ كُنَّا فِي غَفْلَةٍ مِّنْ هَٰذَا بَلْ كُنَّا ظَالِمِينَ

yā waylanā qad kunnā fī ghaflatin min hādhā bal kunnā ẓālimīn

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:97

“Woe to us! We were heedless of this; indeed, we were wrongdoers.”

Their words clarify how they confront the truth.

They first mention their heedlessness and then acknowledge their wrongdoing.

Their staring eyes thus accompany the disclosure of a reality they previously disregarded.

This connection reinforces fear and bewilderment in context, but shākhiṣah itself does not mean “repentant,” “sinful,” or “confessing.”

Shākhiṣah describes the eyes; acknowledgment of wrongdoing comes from their speech.

Does shākhiṣah mean raised eyes?

Older lexical explanations associate shukhūṣ al-baṣar with a raised gaze and eyes remaining open.

In Quranic use, neither raising the gaze nor staring should categorically replace the other without considering context.

Ibrahim 14:43 also mentions raised heads, supporting the image of an upward gaze.

Al-Anbiya 21:97 does not independently mention raised heads. “Their eyes are fixed on the sky” would therefore add a detail not established by that verse alone.

“Their eyes stare fixedly” retains the common core of both occurrences.

A lexical note can add that early Arabic also connects shukhūṣ with raising and fixing the gaze.

4. Morphology and syntax

The two Quranic forms have different grammatical structures.

تَشْخَصُ: an imperfect verb

Ibrahim 14:42 says:

تَشْخَصُ فِيهِ الْأَبْصَارُ

tashkhaṣu fīhi al-abṣār

Ibrāhīm 14:42

“Eyes will stare fixedly on it.”

تَشْخَصُ is a Form I imperfect verb, from شَخَصَ يَشْخَصُ, in the third-person feminine singular.

It is intransitive here, with الأبصار as subject.

Although abṣār is plural, the verb is feminine singular. This agreement is common with nonhuman plurals, including body parts.

The eyes themselves are the subject experiencing shukhūṣ.

Thus, the verb means “the eyes stare,” not “they make the eyes stare.”

شَاخِصَةٌ: an active participle

Al-Anbiya 21:97 says:

فَإِذَا هِيَ شَاخِصَةٌ أَبْصَارُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا

fa-idhā hiya shākhiṣatun abṣāru alladhīna kafarū

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:97

“Suddenly the eyes of those who disbelieved stare fixedly.”

شَاخِصَةٌ is the feminine form of the active participle شَاخِص, derived from شَخَصَ.

In a recognized syntactic analysis, هي is a pronoun of the situation, explained by the following clause. شاخِصة is the fronted predicate and أبصار the postponed subject; the whole clause serves as the predicate of هي.

Other analyses of هي exist, but the principal readings all attribute shukhūṣ to the eyes of those who disbelieved.

The feminine form shākhiṣah also accords with the nonhuman plural abṣār.

The imperfect verb in Ibrahim and the participle in Al-Anbiya do not establish a momentary gaze in one verse and an everlasting gaze in the other.

A verb may emphasize the occurrence of a state, while a participle describes that state; precise duration or permanence cannot be inferred from this morphological distinction alone.

5. Distinctions from neighboring roots

SH KH Ṣ compared with B Ṣ R

Baṣar concerns sight and seeing, whereas Quranic shukhūṣ describes a particular condition of the eyes or gaze.

Both roots occur together in the two verses:

تَشْخَصُ فِيهِ الْأَبْصَارُ

tashkhaṣu fīhi al-abṣār

Ibrāhīm 14:42

And:

شَاخِصَةٌ أَبْصَارُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا

shākhiṣatun abṣāru alladhīna kafarū

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:97

Abṣār names the subject, while tashkhaṣu or shākhiṣah describes its state.

Shukhūṣ therefore does not mean sight itself; it specifies how the gaze is held in a particular condition.

Someone can see while their gaze moves normally. In another situation, their eyes may remain fixed on something.

Both involve baṣar, but only the latter involves shukhūṣ.

SH KH Ṣ compared with Ṭ R F

Ṭarf concerns the eye and movements of the gaze, including turning or shifting it.

Ibrahim 14:43, following the image in Ibrahim 14:42, says:

لَا يَرْتَدُّ إِلَيْهِمْ طَرْفُهُمْ

lā yartaddu ilayhim ṭarfuhum

Ibrāhīm 14:43

“Their gaze does not return to them.”

This clarifies the unusual condition of the gaze.

Shukhūṣ highlights staring; لا يرتد إليهم طرفهم describes the gaze failing to return.

Blinking and ordinary eye movement also belong to the Ṭ R F family. Hence lexical explanations sometimes use لا يطرف: the eye does not blink normally.

Nevertheless, shukhūṣ and ṭarf belong to different roots; one root's entire range should not be absorbed into the other's definition.

SH KH Ṣ compared with Z Y GH

Zaygh concerns deviation from a path or direction.

Al-Ahzab 33:10 says:

وَإِذْ زَاغَتِ الْأَبْصَارُ

wa-idh zāghati al-abṣār

Al-Aḥzāb 33:10

“And when the eyes swerved.”

This occurs in a context of severe fear and pressure.

زاغت الأبصار highlights a shifting or swerving gaze; تشخص الأبصار describes a fixed stare.

Both may occur in fear, but their linguistic images differ.

Their shared context does not make shukhūṣ synonymous with zaygh.

SH KH Ṣ compared with KH SH ʿ

Quranic khushūʿ can express humility, lowered bearing, or submission.

Al-Qamar 54:7 says:

خُشَّعًا أَبْصَارُهُمْ

khushshaʿan abṣāruhum

Al-Qamar 54:7

“With their eyes downcast.”

The image emphasizes a lowered gaze.

Shukhūṣ instead concerns raising or fixing the eyes.

The Quran thus uses different words for the eyes in resurrection scenes; these do not all present the same image.

Downcast eyes in one verse and staring eyes in another should not be merged into a single physical state without evidence. Each verse's context and image deserve separate examination.

SH KH Ṣ compared with R F ʿ

Rafʿ means raising or rising and overlaps with certain uses of shakhaṣa in the field of elevation.

Yet rafʿ can concern people, things, standing, or rank, without necessarily involving a visible figure or a fixed gaze.

Shukhūṣ in a protruding wound, an overshooting arrow, or a fixed gaze expresses visibility or elevation under particular conditions.

The two are not interchangeable everywhere. Elevation may contribute to the image of shukhūṣ, but each derivative's subject and context must also be considered.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

The family's broad range prevents one fixed equivalent from serving every derivative.

شَخْص: “body,” “visible figure,” or, with human reference, “individual.”

شُخُوص, as a plural of shakhṣ: “figures” or “persons.”

شُخُوص, as a verbal noun: “rising,” “emerging,” “setting out,” or “staring fixedly,” according to context.

شَخَصَ: “rose,” “emerged,” “became visible,” or “departed”; of the eyes, “stared fixedly.”

شَاخِص: “visible,” “raised,” “departing,” or “staring,” depending on its subject.

شَاخِصَة: “staring fixedly” in its Quranic use with the eyes.

أَشْخَصَ: “sent away,” “caused to depart,” or “transferred.”

إِشْخَاص: “sending away” or “transferring.”

شَخِيص: “large-bodied” or “strongly built.”

شَخَّصَ: “identified and distinguished,” in uses involving differentiation.

Modern tashkhīṣ can mean diagnosing illness, identifying a problem, or establishing identity. These uses concern distinguishing and specifying, but must not replace the Quranic meanings of tashkhaṣu and shākhiṣah.

Translating the two Quranic occurrences

Ibrahim 14:42

إِنَّمَا يُؤَخِّرُهُمْ لِيَوْمٍ تَشْخَصُ فِيهِ الْأَبْصَارُ

innamā yuʾakhkhiruhum li-yawmin tashkhaṣu fīhi al-abṣār

Ibrāhīm 14:42

“He only delays them until a day when eyes will stare fixedly.”

Al-Anbiya 21:97

فَإِذَا هِيَ شَاخِصَةٌ أَبْصَارُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا

fa-idhā hiya shākhiṣatun abṣāru alladhīna kafarū

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:97

“Suddenly the eyes of those who disbelieved stare fixedly.”

“Stare fixedly” is a natural, precise shared rendering because it expresses the unusual, stationary gaze. These proposals do not alter the installed verse translations.

“Eyes opened wide in terror” can help explain the image, but terror comes from context and should not replace the root's direct meaning.

“Eyes fixed on the sky” is not a universal equivalent: the precise direction requires contextual evidence.

“Stunned and bewildered” evokes something close to staring, but introduces a broader psychological meaning and is less exact as the basic rendering of these verses.

7. Conclusion

In early Arabic, SH KH Ṣ concerns the visible figure, emergence, rising, and movement. Its family supplies words for the human body, departure, protrusion, and the fixed gaze.

Early poetic witnesses use shakhṣ for a visible human figure, shākhiṣ for a departing traveler, and shakhaṣa al-baṣar for a fixed gaze.

Their imagery is related, but the historical origins and sequence of all these senses remain uncertain. Modern personality or specialized diagnosis must not be assigned to every old use.

Only tashkhaṣu and shākhiṣah occur in the Quran. Both concern eyes remaining fixed in a stare.

Ibrahim 14:42 places this image within a warning about the respite of wrongdoers and the day of judgment. The following verse develops it through raised heads and a gaze that does not return.

Al-Anbiya 21:97 depicts staring eyes as the true promise approaches and those who disbelieved acknowledge their heedlessness and wrongdoing.

Together, the occurrences express a gaze raised or held open and fixed, no longer moving in its ordinary way.

Fear, bewilderment, and confrontation with truth come from the context, not from the root alone.

For Forqan, “eyes staring fixedly” is the best shared rendering. It preserves the lexical image while leaving fear, bewilderment, accountability, and the consequences of deeds to explanations supported by each verse's context.
