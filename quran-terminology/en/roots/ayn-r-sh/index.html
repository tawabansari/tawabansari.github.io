From raised structures and house roofs to royal thrones and the divine Throne

In many physical uses, the Arabic root ʿ R SH concerns erecting, raising, and constructing structures whose parts rest on supports. These may be house roofs, canopies, plant trellises, or elevated, sturdy seats. In other uses, ʿarsh denotes the seat of rule and authority. This physical account does not by itself determine the nature of the divine Throne.

In early Arabic, ʿarsh names a king's throne, a roof, a building, or a wooden structure. ʿArīsh denotes a shelter or canopy commonly made from branches, wood, and plant material. Taʿrīsh concerns erecting such structures, especially supports for the branches of plants such as grapevines.

This family has a broad Quranic range: structures erected by people, gardens provided with trellises, the collapsed roofs of settlements, thrones belonging to rulers such as Joseph and the queen of Sheba, and, in numerous verses, the Throne of The God.

ʿArsh is not confined to a single object or concept across these uses. The roofs of a settlement, a ruler's throne, and the divine Throne belong to one linguistic family, but each verse's context determines the reference.

Its application to The God should not cause us to ignore its physical meanings or translate every Quranic occurrence as “power” or “sovereignty.” The Quran also uses this root for actual structures, house roofs, and garden trellises.

The Quranic forms include the noun ʿarsh, its plural ʿurūsh, the verb «يَعْرِشُونَ», and the passive participle «مَعْرُوشَات». The nominal forms occur with different prefixes, attached pronouns, and case endings, including «عَرْشُهُ», «عَرْشَهَا», «عَرْشُكِ», and «عُرُوشِهَا». The family occurs 33 times in 32 verses: two verbs, two passive participles, and 29 nouns, including three plural occurrences.

Understanding this range requires beginning with tangible Arabic usage and then distinguishing each form's meaning in its Quranic context.

1. Historical meaning and early Arabic usage

Early Arabic attests ʿ R SH in building and erecting supported structures. A major physical branch concerns something constructed, raised, or held upon supports; it should not be made a universal definition determining the nature of every referent.

Several physical uses illustrate this branch.

ʿArsh as a roof or raised structure

«عَرْشُ الْبَيْتِ» denotes a house's roof, the part resting on walls or supports and covering the space below.

ʿArīsh also names a shelter or canopy erected from wood, branches, and plant material. Such structures provide shade and protection from the sun in hot environments.

In simple houses or shelters, posts or branches might be erected vertically, with timber and plant covering spread across them. The resulting structure rested on its supports and created a covered space.

This image accords with ʿarsh as an erected, supported structure.

ʿArsh and ʿarīsh in agriculture

In agriculture, «عَرْشُ الْكَرْمِ» names the structure supporting grapevine branches.

Grapevines can spread along the ground or climb a trellis or support. A farmer's erecting posts and timber and placing branches upon them is expressed through the family of taʿrīsh.

This use clarifies the root. It concerns more than height alone: a structure is erected so that something else can rest upon it or be held up by it.

Trellises, roofs, and canopies differ in form and purpose, yet can share this structural feature.

Well structures and timberwork

Arabic also records ʿarsh for timberwork erected at the mouth or upper part of a well.

In one lexical description, timber braces the unstable upper part of a well above its stone lining, providing a place from which water drawers can work. This is more specific than simply a cover over the well.

This use likewise concerns a structure supported by other parts.

These applications did not all have one fixed shape. ʿArsh was not the name of a single tool with standard dimensions and construction. Context determines whether a roof, canopy, trellis, throne, or another structural part is intended.

A poetic witness attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abi Sulma

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr ibn Abi Sulma states:

تَدَارَكْتُمَا الْأَحْلَافَ قَدْ ثُلَّ عَرْشُهَا
وَذُبْيَانَ إِذْ زَلَّتْ بِأَقْدَامِهَا النَّعْلُ

tadāraktumā al-aḥlāfa qad thulla ʿarshuhā / wa-dhubyāna idh zallat bi-aqdāmihā al-naʿl

“You two rescued the allies when their foundations and order had collapsed, and Dhubyan when their footing slipped.”

The verse praises people who worked to resolve disputes and restore peace between groups.

«ثُلَّ عَرْشُهَا» speaks of an ʿarsh collapsing, but does not describe a physical throne breaking. It concerns damage to the order, authority, and connections of a social group.

Here ʿarsh figuratively denotes the group's stability and organization. Just as a building rests upon its supports, a community or alliance depends on its bonds and internal order. Their collapse can be pictured as the collapse of an ʿarsh.

This witness is historically significant: it supports a use for social stability, standing, and authority that cannot simply be reduced to later interpretation of Quranic verses.

Attribution and precise wording of early poetry nevertheless require historical care. Early sources attribute this verse to Zuhayr, with variants in some words. It can inform our understanding of usage attributed to the pre-Islamic period, but cannot establish the origins of every meaning in the family.

The historical meaning of the royal throne

ʿArsh also names a ruler's throne.

In many societies, a royal throne was more than a seat: it was the official place of rule and a sign of its owner's standing and authority.

Two levels are involved: the throne as an actual manufactured object, and the royal position it represents.

These levels are related but distinct.

A ruler's throne may refer to the object that can be moved, sat upon, or altered. In another context, it can signify kingship and authority.

A major physical branch can be explained through an erected, raised, or supported structure. Roofs, canopies, trellises, and thrones are familiar examples. The use for stability and sovereignty relates to this field, but does not prove that all branches arose from one original object or building method, or determine the nature of the divine Throne.

2. Building and erecting: «يَعْرِشُونَ» in the Quran

«يَعْرِشُونَ» is a form I verb from «عَرَشَ» and occurs twice in the Quran.

Its Quranic uses concern building and erecting structures.

Structures erected by people

Al-Nahl states:

وَأَوْحَىٰ رَبُّكَ إِلَى النَّحْلِ أَنِ اتَّخِذِي مِنَ الْجِبَالِ بُيُوتًا وَمِنَ الشَّجَرِ وَمِمَّا يَعْرِشُونَ

wa-awḥā rabbuka ilā al-naḥli ani ittakhidhī mina al-jibāli buyūtan wa-mina al-shajari wa-mimmā yaʿrishūn

Al-Naḥl 16:68

“Your Lord inspired the bees: take homes in the mountains, in the trees, and in what people erect.”

Al-Nahl 16:68

The verse mentions three settings for the bees' homes: mountains, trees, and what people erect.

«يَعْرِشُونَ» here concerns human-made structures.

Such structures can provide places for bees to settle and build their homes. The verb itself does not name a specific type of hive or building.

«مِمَّا يَعْرِشُونَ» should therefore not be restricted in translation to “artificial hives built by people.” It refers to what they erect without specifying a single structure type.

The point is that bees can make use of both natural spaces and structures erected by people.

Here the root clearly concerns construction and erection, rather than royal thrones or political power.

The structures of Pharaoh and his people

Al-Araf 7:137 states:

وَدَمَّرْنَا مَا كَانَ يَصْنَعُ فِرْعَوْنُ وَقَوْمُهُ وَمَا كَانُوا يَعْرِشُونَ

wa-dammarnā mā kāna yaṣnaʿu firʿawnu wa-qawmuhu wa-mā kānū yaʿrishūn

Al-Aʿrāf 7:137

“We destroyed what Pharaoh and his people were making and what they were erecting.”

Here «يَعْرِشُونَ» appears alongside «يَصْنَعُ».

Ṣ N ʿ broadly concerns making and producing, while ʿ R SH in this context can emphasize structures being erected or raised.

These might include buildings, canopies, or trellises, but the verse does not identify the type and shape of every structure.

“What they were erecting” is therefore preferable to restricting the phrase to “thrones they were making” or “tall royal buildings.”

Nor does the verb necessarily designate religious buildings, idol temples, or structures peculiar to Pharaoh; such identifications require separate evidence.

The two Quranic occurrences clearly support the meaning of building and erecting.

3. «مَعْرُوشَات»: gardens with supporting structures

Al-Anam 6:141 states:

وَهُوَ الَّذِي أَنْشَأَ جَنَّاتٍ مَعْرُوشَاتٍ وَغَيْرَ مَعْرُوشَاتٍ وَالنَّخْلَ وَالزَّرْعَ مُخْتَلِفًا أُكُلُهُ

wa-huwa alladhī anshaʾa jannātin maʿrūshātin wa-ghayra maʿrūshātin wa-l-nakhla wa-l-zarʿa mukhtalifan ukuluh

Al-Anʿām 6:141

“He is the One who brought forth gardens, trellised and untrellised, and date palms and crops with varied produce.”

«مَعْرُوشَات» is the feminine plural of the passive participle «مَعْرُوش», from the basic verb «عَرَشَ». Here it describes gardens whose plants have been provided with structures or supports.

It contrasts with «غَيْرَ مَعْرُوشَات»: gardens without such trellises or structures.

This contrast is important for understanding the root.

Simply translating maʿrūshāt as “high gardens” would obscure the distinction. Understanding it as gardens whose plants are supported on trellises makes the contrast natural and clear.

Some plants are trained on supports or grown with trellises, while others grow without such structures.

Maʿrūshāt thus concerns cultivation and the arrangement of plants, rather than the gardens' moral value or superior rank.

Some lexical explanations identify these with vineyards whose branches are trellised. This fits Arabic usage, but the word's form does not make it an exclusive name for grapes.

Translation should therefore retain the general meaning, with vineyards offered as a clear example in the explanation.

Morphologically, maʿrūshāt directly relates to yaʿrishūn: the verb expresses erection, while the passive participle describes its result in the gardens.

4. «عُرُوشِهَا»: roofs and collapsed structures

The plural «عُرُوش» is another important Quranic form.

It appears in three verses in the expression «خَاوِيَةٌ عَلَىٰ عُرُوشِهَا».

The ruined settlement in al-Baqarah 2:259

Al-Baqarah states:

أَوْ كَالَّذِي مَرَّ عَلَىٰ قَرْيَةٍ وَهِيَ خَاوِيَةٌ عَلَىٰ عُرُوشِهَا

aw ka-alladhī marra ʿalā qaryatin wa-hiya khāwiyatun ʿalā ʿurūshihā

Al-Baqarah 2:259

“Or like the one who passed by a settlement fallen upon its roofs.”

Al-Baqarah 2:259

Here «عُرُوشِهَا» denotes the settlement's roofs or upper structures.

Khāwiyah indicates its ruined, collapsed condition.

The image can depict roofs that have fallen, with other parts of the buildings collapsing onto them. It suits structures lying upon their own fallen components.

The phrase does not require us to establish an identical, definite sequence for every building's collapse. Its main purpose is to portray the ruined settlement.

ʿUrūsh here does not name royal thrones: the subject is a ruined settlement and its buildings.

Ruined settlements in al-Hajj 22:45

Another verse states:

فَكَأَيِّنْ مِنْ قَرْيَةٍ أَهْلَكْنَاهَا وَهِيَ ظَالِمَةٌ فَهِيَ خَاوِيَةٌ عَلَىٰ عُرُوشِهَا

fa-ka-ayyin min qaryatin ahlaknāhā wa-hiya ẓālimatun fa-hiya khāwiyatun ʿalā ʿurūshihā

Al-Ḥajj 22:45

“How many settlements We destroyed while they were unjust, so they lie fallen upon their roofs.”

Al-Hajj 22:45

The same expression describes settlements whose buildings and structures have been devastated.

Here too, ʿurūsh concerns roofs and structural parts.

The noun does not itself mean destruction. Destruction is expressed by «أَهْلَكْنَاهَا» and «خَاوِيَةٌ».

ʿUrūsh identifies part of the structure now lying in ruin.

The ruined garden in al-Kahf 18:42

The story of the owner of two gardens states:

فَأَصْبَحَ يُقَلِّبُ كَفَّيْهِ عَلَىٰ مَا أَنْفَقَ فِيهَا وَهِيَ خَاوِيَةٌ عَلَىٰ عُرُوشِهَا

fa-aṣbaḥa yuqallibu kaffayhi ʿalā mā anfaqa fīhā wa-hiya khāwiyatun ʿalā ʿurūshihā

Al-Kahf 18:42

“He began turning his palms over what he had spent on it, while it lay fallen upon its trellises.”

Al-Kahf 18:42

Here ʿurūsh appears in a garden context. Trellises and structures supporting plants are natural referents.

The difference from al-Baqarah 2:259 and al-Hajj 22:45 shows how one form can refer to different things in architectural and agricultural settings.

In a settlement, the roofs of houses are prominent; in a garden, the frames and trellises supporting plants.

In all three verses, collapse is conveyed by the complete construction. ʿArsh itself does not mean falling, destruction, or punishment.

5. Human thrones: royal seats and authority

The Quran also uses ʿarsh for rulers' actual thrones.

This relates to the image of a raised structure, while also foregrounding the throne's official and governmental role.

Joseph's throne

Yusuf 12:100 states:

وَرَفَعَ أَبَوَيْهِ عَلَى الْعَرْشِ وَخَرُّوا لَهُ سُجَّدًا

wa-rafaʿa abawayhi ʿalā al-ʿarshi wa-kharrū lahu sujjadan

Yūsuf 12:100

“He raised his parents onto the throne, and they fell prostrate before him.”

Here ʿarsh names the place onto which Joseph raises his parents.

«رَفَعَ ... عَلَى الْعَرْشِ» points to a specific place upon which people can be positioned.

Joseph's role in the story also involves authority and governmental responsibility. His throne can therefore be both an actual object and a sign of his position.

Yet the word does not simply mean “government” here, since the sentence speaks of people being placed upon it.

The queen of Sheba's throne

In al-Naml 27:23, the hoopoe says of the queen of Sheba:

إِنِّي وَجَدْتُ امْرَأَةً تَمْلِكُهُمْ وَأُوتِيَتْ مِنْ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ وَلَهَا عَرْشٌ عَظِيمٌ

innī wajadtu imraʾatan tamlikuhum wa-ūtiyat min kulli shayʾin wa-lahā ʿarshun ʿaẓīm

Al-Naml 27:23

“I found a woman ruling them, who has been given of everything, and she has a great throne.”

«عَرْشٌ عَظِيمٌ» describes the queen's royal throne.

ʿAẓīm is its adjective, expressing greatness or magnificence.

ʿArsh does not itself mean “great.” The adjective supplies that additional quality.

Later in the story, Solomon says:

أَيُّكُمْ يَأْتِينِي بِعَرْشِهَا قَبْلَ أَنْ يَأْتُونِي مُسْلِمِينَ

ayyukum yaʾtīnī bi-ʿarshihā qabla an yaʾtūnī muslimīn

Al-Naml 27:38

“Which of you will bring me her throne before they come to me in submission?”

Al-Naml 27:38

«بِعَرْشِهَا» refers to that particular throne.

Solomon asks for it to be brought, so the story presents it as a movable object.

Al-Naml 27:41 states:

قَالَ نَكِّرُوا لَهَا عَرْشَهَا نَنْظُرْ أَتَهْتَدِي أَمْ تَكُونُ مِنَ الَّذِينَ لَا يَهْتَدُونَ

qāla nakkirū lahā ʿarshahā nanẓur a-tahtadī am takūnu mina alladhīna lā yahtadūn

Al-Naml 27:41

“He said: disguise her throne for her; let us see whether she recognizes it or is among those who do not.”

And al-Naml 27:42 states:

فَلَمَّا جَاءَتْ قِيلَ أَهَٰكَذَا عَرْشُكِ

fa-lammā jāʾat qīla a-hākadhā ʿarshuk

Al-Naml 27:42

“When she arrived, she was asked: is your throne like this?”

These verses show that the queen's throne had an identifiable appearance and features. Its appearance could be changed, and its owner then asked to recognize it.

This passage is among the clearest Quranic evidence for ʿarsh as an actual throne.

Since it belonged to a ruler, it could also represent her authority and standing.

An actual throne can also symbolize sovereignty. An object can have a material existence and represent its owner's social and political position.

This does not establish that every Quranic use necessarily refers to a human throne of the same shape and construction.

6. The divine Throne: sovereignty, creation, and a reality beyond direct human knowledge

A substantial portion of the Quranic uses concern The God.

In these verses, the Throne relates to creation, sovereignty, governance, knowledge, angels, and events of the Resurrection.

They should be considered together without absorbing each expression's direct meaning into a predetermined theological or cosmological theory.

«رَبُّ الْعَرْشِ»: Lord of the Throne

Al-Tawbah 9:129 states:

عَلَيْهِ تَوَكَّلْتُ وَهُوَ رَبُّ الْعَرْشِ الْعَظِيمِ

ʿalayhi tawakkaltu wa-huwa rabbu al-ʿarshi al-ʿaẓīm

Al-Tawbah 9:129

“In Him I have placed my trust, and He is Lord of the great Throne.”

Al-Muminun 23:86 states:

قُلْ مَنْ رَبُّ السَّمَاوَاتِ السَّبْعِ وَرَبُّ الْعَرْشِ الْعَظِيمِ

qul man rabbu al-samāwāti al-sabʿi wa-rabbu al-ʿarshi al-ʿaẓīm

Al-Muʾminūn 23:86

“Say: who is Lord of the seven heavens and Lord of the great Throne?”

These verses identify The God as Lord of the Throne.

Rabb concerns lordship, ownership, and governance. The expression identifies the Throne's relationship to divine lordship.

Al-Muminun 23:116 also states:

فَتَعَالَى اللَّهُ الْمَلِكُ الْحَقُّ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ رَبُّ الْعَرْشِ الْكَرِيمِ

fa-taʿālā allāhu al-maliku al-ḥaqqu lā ilāha illā huwa rabbu al-ʿarshi al-karīm

Al-Muʾminūn 23:116

“Exalted is The God, the true Sovereign. There is no deity except Him, Lord of the noble Throne.”

In the reading quoted here, «الْكَرِيمِ» is an adjective describing the Throne.

Ghafir 40:15 uses «ذُو الْعَرْشِ», and al-Buruj 85:15 likewise describes The God by this title.

These expressions show the Throne's special role in the language of divine greatness and sovereignty.

“Possessor of the Throne” and “Lord of the Throne” alone do not establish its dimensions, shape, material, or physical location.

The queen's great throne and the great divine Throne

Al-Naml presents a notable verbal connection.

The hoopoe first says of the queen:

وَلَهَا عَرْشٌ عَظِيمٌ

wa-lahā ʿarshun ʿaẓīm

Al-Naml 27:23

“And she has a great throne.”

Al-Naml 27:23

Shortly afterward, following the account of her people's sun worship, it states:

اللَّهُ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ رَبُّ الْعَرْشِ الْعَظِيمِ

allāhu lā ilāha illā huwa rabbu al-ʿarshi al-ʿaẓīm

Al-Naml 27:26

“The God: there is no deity except Him, Lord of the great Throne.”

Al-Naml 27:26

Both passages use ʿarsh with ʿaẓīm, but one concerns a human ruler's throne and the other the divine Throne.

The proximity of these expressions is significant.

The queen possessed a throne and ruled, but her authority was limited. In the same surah, The God is Lord of the great Throne, with no deity besides Him.

Their juxtaposition can thus be read as a contrast between limited human rule and divine sovereignty.

Describing both thrones as great does not make their dimensions, construction, or nature identical. They share the language of greatness; context determines what that greatness entails.

«اسْتَوَىٰ عَلَى الْعَرْشِ»: creation and governance

Several verses mention «اسْتَوَىٰ عَلَى الْعَرْشِ» following the creation of the heavens and earth.

Al-Araf 7:54 states:

إِنَّ رَبَّكُمُ اللَّهُ الَّذِي خَلَقَ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ فِي سِتَّةِ أَيَّامٍ ثُمَّ اسْتَوَىٰ عَلَى الْعَرْشِ

inna rabbakumu allāhu alladhī khalaqa al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍa fī sittati ayyāmin thumma istawā ʿalā al-ʿarsh

Al-Aʿrāf 7:54

“Your Lord is The God, who created the heavens and earth in six days, then istawā over the Throne.”

This construction also appears in Yunus 10:3, al-Rad 13:2, al-Furqan 25:59, al-Sajdah 32:4, and al-Hadid 57:4.

Ta Ha 20:5 uses a different word order:

الرَّحْمَٰنُ عَلَى الْعَرْشِ اسْتَوَىٰ

al-raḥmānu ʿalā al-ʿarshi istawā

Ṭā Hā 20:5

“The Entirely Merciful istawā over the Throne.”

ʿArsh is the word under study, while istawā belongs to the separate root S W Y. The verb is retained here as a transliterated term so its interpretation can be addressed independently.

Sitting, being positioned, or being established should not be attributed directly to ʿ R SH itself. The complete expression combines the verb istawā, the preposition ʿalā, and the noun ʿarsh.

In physical uses, istawā ʿalā can concern being positioned or established upon something. Its application to The God does not authorize transferring every bodily feature of a person sitting on a throne to Him.

Nor should the expression be disregarded as though the Quran merely said “The God governs.” It uses the particular construction istawā ʿalā al-ʿarsh and relates it to creation.

In Yunus 10:3, this is followed by:

يُدَبِّرُ الْأَمْرَ

yudabbiru al-amr

Yūnus 10:3

“He governs the affair.”

This sequence places the Throne and istiwāʾ within the context of divine creation and governance.

Al-Hadid 57:4 likewise follows creation and istiwāʾ over the Throne with divine knowledge of what enters the earth, emerges from it, descends from heaven, and ascends into it.

These verses thus associate the Throne with creation, sovereignty, knowledge, and governance.

That context deepens understanding of its role without fully specifying its reality or the manner of divine istiwāʾ.

«وَكَانَ عَرْشُهُ عَلَى الْمَاءِ»: the Throne and water

Hud 11:7 states:

وَهُوَ الَّذِي خَلَقَ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ فِي سِتَّةِ أَيَّامٍ وَكَانَ عَرْشُهُ عَلَى الْمَاءِ

wa-huwa alladhī khalaqa al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍa fī sittati ayyāmin wa-kāna ʿarshuhu ʿalā al-māʾ

Hūd 11:7

“He is the One who created the heavens and earth in six days, and His Throne was over the water.”

This is an important occurrence because it relates the Throne to water through ʿalā.

The text speaks of the Throne as having been over water.

This should not be removed from translation without reason or replaced with an entirely different statement such as “divine sovereignty extended over everything.”

The clause nevertheless does not independently explain the Throne's material, construction, or physical relationship to water.

If the Throne here is a reality within creation, its nature cannot be determined solely by analogy with a human throne or canopy.

Mention of water does not establish that it is a floating device, a particular celestial body, or a physical structure with the familiar properties of earthly objects and water.

The verse explicitly states a relationship between the Throne and water within a discussion of creation.

That relationship invites further study and reflection, but a proposed conclusion should not replace the word's lexical meaning.

Bearers of the Throne and those around it

Ghafir 40:7 states:

الَّذِينَ يَحْمِلُونَ الْعَرْشَ وَمَنْ حَوْلَهُ يُسَبِّحُونَ بِحَمْدِ رَبِّهِمْ

alladhīna yaḥmilūna al-ʿarsha wa-man ḥawlahu yusabbiḥūna bi-ḥamdi rabbihim

Ghāfir 40:7

“Those who bear the Throne and those around it glorify their Lord with praise.”

Two descriptions are important: bearing the Throne and being around it.

«يَحْمِلُونَ» belongs to Ḥ M L and expresses bearing or carrying. «حَوْلَهُ» refers to the Throne's surroundings.

The Quran here speaks of the Throne in terms of relationships with bearers and with those around it.

Al-Haqqah 69:17 states:

وَالْمَلَكُ عَلَىٰ أَرْجَائِهَا وَيَحْمِلُ عَرْشَ رَبِّكَ فَوْقَهُمْ يَوْمَئِذٍ ثَمَانِيَةٌ

wa-l-malaku ʿalā arjāʾihā wa-yaḥmilu ʿarsha rabbika fawqahum yawmaʾidhin thamāniyah

Al-Ḥāqqah 69:17

“The angels will be at its edges, and that day eight will bear your Lord's Throne above them.”

The number eight is explicit. The verse does not, however, explain the bearers' nature in detail or the manner of their bearing.

The explicit number should not arbitrarily be turned into a symbol of multitude or a coded system; nor does it allow calculation of the Throne's physical construction or dimensions.

Al-Zumar 39:75 states:

وَتَرَى الْمَلَائِكَةَ حَافِّينَ مِنْ حَوْلِ الْعَرْشِ يُسَبِّحُونَ بِحَمْدِ رَبِّهِمْ

wa-tarā al-malāʾikata ḥāffīna min ḥawli al-ʿarshi yusabbiḥūna bi-ḥamdi rabbihim

Al-Zumar 39:75

“You will see the angels surrounding the Throne, glorifying their Lord with praise.”

These verses should not be set aside by claiming that ʿarsh is always merely another name for power. The Quran also speaks of bearing it, being around it, and its relationships with other beings.

Yet these images do not prove that its material, location, and construction resemble human thrones.

Quranic study should preserve the images actually expressed while avoiding unsupported specification of their unknown manner.

The Throne and the oneness of sovereignty

Al-Anbiya 21:22 states:

لَوْ كَانَ فِيهِمَا آلِهَةٌ إِلَّا اللَّهُ لَفَسَدَتَا فَسُبْحَانَ اللَّهِ رَبِّ الْعَرْشِ عَمَّا يَصِفُونَ

law kāna fīhimā ālihatun illā allāhu la-fasadatā fa-subḥāna allāhi rabbi al-ʿarshi ʿammā yaṣifūn

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:22

“Had there been deities in them other than The God, both would have fallen into ruin. Glory be to The God, Lord of the Throne, above what they describe.”

The verse associates the oneness of divinity with lordship of the Throne.

Al-Zukhruf 43:82 also identifies The God as Lord of the Throne and exalts Him above improper descriptions.

These uses give the Throne a prominent place in expressing divine sovereignty and greatness.

They do not make its lexical meaning “monotheism” or “absolute sovereignty.” Such ideas follow from the verses together and from its relationship to The God.

7. ʿArsh and kursī: related but distinct words

Both ʿarsh and kursī relate to position and sovereignty in the Quran, but they remain different words.

ʿArsh has a broad Arabic range including raised structures, roofs, trellises, and royal thrones.

Kursī can name a seat or throne. The Quran applies it both to Solomon and to The God, while the nature of the divine kursī remains a separate question.

Sad 38:34 states:

وَلَقَدْ فَتَنَّا سُلَيْمَانَ وَأَلْقَيْنَا عَلَىٰ كُرْسِيِّهِ جَسَدًا ثُمَّ أَنَابَ

wa-la-qad fatannā sulaymāna wa-alqaynā ʿalā kursiyyihi jasadan thumma anāb

Ṣād 38:34

“We tested Solomon and cast a body upon his throne; then he turned back.”

Kursī here refers to a particular seat belonging to Solomon.

By comparison, the Quran uses ʿarsh for Joseph and the queen of Sheba.

Both words can therefore concern human royal thrones, but should not be treated as exact synonyms in every verse.

Al-Baqarah 2:255 states:

وَسِعَ كُرْسِيُّهُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ

wasiʿa kursiyyuhu al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍ

Al-Baqarah 2:255

“His kursī encompasses the heavens and earth.”

This is thematically related to the Throne passages, but the sentence does not define the exact relationship between kursī and ʿarsh.

Merely juxtaposing the words cannot establish a definite system in which the kursī is necessarily part of the Throne, beneath it, or located at a specified point in the universe's physical structure.

Their being different words does not disprove every real relationship between them either.

Study should first clarify each family's independent meaning and Quranic uses, then consider their possible relationship.

The same principle applies to comparison with saqf, bināʾ, mulk, and istiwāʾ.

Saqf names a building's covering. ʿArsh can mean a roof or erected structure, but its usage is not confined to roofs.

Bināʾ concerns buildings and construction; some uses of ʿarsh concern a raised structure.

Mulk concerns sovereignty and authority; ʿarsh can represent their seat or symbol.

Istiwāʾ belongs to S W Y and requires independent study. Its association with the Throne does not authorize merging the two roots into one lexical definition.

8. Translation recommendations for Forqan

The appropriate equivalent should be chosen according to the form and the verse's context.

عَرْش: throne, royal throne, roof, or raised structure, depending on the subject. In divine passages, Persian can retain the word ʿarsh; English “the Throne” should likewise avoid specifying an unsupported physical form.

عُرُوش: roofs or erected structures. “Roofs” is natural for ruined settlements; “trellises” can suit garden contexts.

يَعْرِشُونَ: they erect, construct, or build. In al-Nahl 16:68, “what people erect” is appropriate.

مَعْرُوشَات: trellised, or provided with supporting structures. “Trellised and untrellised gardens” clearly renders al-Anam 6:141.

عَرْشٌ عَظِيمٌ: a great or magnificent throne, in the queen of Sheba's story.

رَبُّ الْعَرْشِ الْعَظِيمِ: Lord of the great Throne.

ذُو الْعَرْشِ: Possessor of the Throne.

اسْتَوَىٰ عَلَى الْعَرْشِ: istawā over the Throne. The meaning and manner of istiwāʾ require independent study of S W Y and the context; adding “sat down” without independent evidence is inappropriate.

عُرُوشِهَا in al-Baqarah 2:259 and al-Hajj 22:45: its roofs.

عُرُوشِهَا in al-Kahf 18:42: its trellises, in the garden context.

For the divine passages, the Persian study recommends retaining ʿarsh. “Throne” is natural and clear in human contexts, but readers should not automatically limit its divine application to a human seat.

Replacing ʿarsh directly with “power,” “authority,” or “sovereignty” would remove the word's original image.

Those concepts can be explored in reflection without replacing the basic translation.

9. Summary

A major physical branch of ʿ R SH concerns building and erecting supported structures. House roofs, canopies, plant trellises, timberwork, and royal thrones are important examples; these do not by themselves determine the nature of the divine Throne.

Early Arabic evidence also supports figurative uses for authority and the stability of a social group. The verse attributed to Zuhayr about the allies' collapsed ʿarsh illustrates this.

The Quran develops this range through several clear applications.

Yaʿrishūn concerns building and erecting structures. Maʿrūshāt describes gardens provided with supports or trellises. ʿUrūsh in ruin passages names roofs or structures that have collapsed.

In the stories of Joseph and the queen of Sheba, ʿarsh is a royal throne: an actual object that also represents its owner's authority.

Divine passages relate the Throne to creation, sovereignty, governance, knowledge, angels, and the Resurrection. They speak of Lord of the Throne, istiwāʾ over it, its being over water, and its bearers and those surrounding it.

These expressions should be considered together. Reducing the Throne to sovereignty overlooks explicit imagery involving water, bearing, and surroundings. Imagining it exactly like a human throne reduces its unknown nature to limited material experience.

Lexical meaning, what may exist, and the Throne's actual nature are three distinct questions.

A semantic summary is: building and erecting; in physical applications, a raised structure such as a roof, canopy, trellis, or throne; and an extension to the seat of rule and the stability of authority. Its divine application does not by itself establish a particular material, support, or construction.

This account explains tangible Quranic uses and prepares the study of divine passages without deciding the Throne's nature in advance.

Concerning the divine Throne, the Quran neither invites disregard of its own expressions nor supplies every unknown detail. Its special relationship to lordship, sovereignty, and creation is clear; its full reality and manner remain matters requiring further understanding.
