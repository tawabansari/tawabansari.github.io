A decisive blow to the head and brain: truth crushing falsehood

1. Introduction and semantic field

The triliteral Arabic root D M GH concerns the brain and a severe blow reaching it.

Dimāgh names the brain. Damaghahu describes striking someone’s head so that the injury reaches the brain. In early uses, such a blow may be extremely severe and fatal.

This concrete image extends figuratively to decisive victory and the crushing of an opposing force.

For example, ḥujjatun dāmighatun denotes an argument that decisively refutes an opposing claim and undermines its reasoning.

No actual physical blow strikes a person’s head in this use. What collapses is the validity of the claim or argument.

D M GH occurs only once in the Qur’an, as fa-yadmaghuhu in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:18.

God speaks there of casting truth against falsehood; truth then crushes falsehood, and falsehood vanishes.

The Qur’anic use thus belongs to a forceful image: truth crushes falsehood so that it cannot withstand it.

The verbal image must nevertheless be distinguished from its actual referent.

Falsehood is not presented as an actual body possessing a head and brain. The image of a severe blow is transferred to truth overcoming falsehood.

Nor does this image establish that the Qur’an commands physical blows against opponents as a means of proving the truth.

The verse directly concerns truth and falsehood, rather than prescribing violence against people.

2. Qur’anic and Arabic forms

The Qur’anic occurrence

The root occurs only once, in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:18:

Fa-yadmaghuhu

The form consists of three parts:

Fa-: a connective linking the following action with the preceding clause.

Yadmaghu: a third-person masculine singular imperfect verb, from Form I damagha.

-Hu: a third-person masculine singular object pronoun referring to al-bāṭil, “falsehood.”

In the sentence, therefore, truth crushes falsehood.

The preceding naqdhifu is attributed to God, whereas the semantic agent of fa-yadmaghuhu is truth, cast against falsehood.

This syntactic distinction matters and should not be overlooked.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: one Form I verb in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:18

Familiar Arabic forms

Early dictionaries record the following members of this family:

Dimāgh: the brain.

Admighah: a plural of dimāgh, “brains.”

Dumugh: another recorded plural.

Damagha, yadmaghu: to strike the head so that injury reaches the brain; figuratively, to overcome decisively or discredit.

Damgh: a verbal noun: a blow reaching the brain; figuratively, decisive defeat or crushing.

Dāmigh: an active participle: delivering a decisive blow; in argument, an argument or proof that discredits the opposing claim.

Dāmighah: a blow or wound reaching the brain; also the name of certain components of tools and structures.

Madmūgh: a person or thing subjected to damgh.

Damīgh: someone whose head or brain has been severely injured.

Damghā: a recorded plural for certain descriptions of brain injury.

Admagha: in some early uses, to place someone in a difficult situation or compel them through need.

Dammagha: a non-Qur’anic derived form. Dammagha al-tharīda bi-al-dasami, for example, is recorded for mixing or coating tharīd with fat. This use requires separate treatment from Form I.

Modern Arabic also uses words such as damghah for a seal or official mark. A Turkish origin, tamgha/ṭamgha, is reported for this marking sense in sources such as Dozy’s supplement. Identical spelling alone therefore does not prove a derivational connection with damgh concerning the brain.

Modern stamping should not be imported into the Qur’anic meaning without historical evidence.

Only the Form I verb yadmaghu occurs in the Qur’an.

Al-Muḥīṭ, Al-ʿUbāb and Dozy’s supplement: derived forms and stamping

3. The semantic core in early Arabic

Ibn Fāris’s definition

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris writes:

الدَّالُ وَالْمِيمُ وَالْغَيْنُ كَلِمَةٌ وَاحِدَةٌ لَا تَتَفَرَّعُ وَلَا يُقَاسُ عَلَيْهَا

al-dālu wa-al-mīmu wa-al-ghaynu kalimatun wāḥidatun lā tatafarraʿu wa-lā yuqāsu ʿalayhā

“Dāl, mīm, and ghayn constitute a single, limited family that does not branch out or provide a basis for broad analogy.”

He continues:

فَالدِّمَاغُ مَعْرُوفٌ، وَدَمَغْتُهُ: ضَرَبْتُهُ عَلَى رَأْسِهِ حَتَّى وَصَلْتُ إِلَى الدِّمَاغِ

fa-al-dimāghu maʿrūfun wa-damaghtuhu ḍarabtuhu ʿalā raʾsihi ḥattā waṣaltu ilā al-dimāghi

“Dimāgh is familiar; damaghtuhu means: I struck him on the head until the blow reached the brain.”

This definition is especially relevant to the method of root studies.

Unlike many roots for which he identifies a broad abstract image, Ibn Fāris emphasizes a specific, limited field here.

There is therefore no need to invent a hypothetical meaning such as “any pressure,” “any penetration,” or “any victory” for D M GH.

Its clear concrete sense concerns the brain and a severe blow to the head.

The transfer of that image to victory and refutation can then be examined.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: the brain and a blow reaching it

Dimāgh: the brain

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

الدِّمَاغُ: حَشْوُ الرَّأْسِ

al-dimāghu ḥashwu al-raʾsi

“Dimāgh: the contents of the head.”

Al-Muḥkam wa-al-Muḥīṭ al-Aʿẓam records the same definition.

This linguistically describes the brain and its location within the head.

It should not be turned into a precise definition in modern anatomy.

Modern science distinguishes the brain’s different parts and complex neurological functions. The early lexical definition simply identifies the familiar substance within the head.

To understand damgh in Arabic, this relationship suffices: a blow to the head reaching a vital interior part.

Damgh: more than an ordinary blow to the head

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

دَمَغَهُ دَمْغًا: شَجَّهُ حَتَّى بَلَغَتِ الشَّجَّةُ الدِّمَاغَ

damaghahu damghan shajjahu ḥattā balaghati al-shajjatu al-dimāgha

“He damagha him: he wounded his head until the wound reached the brain.”

Concrete damgh therefore denotes more than touching or superficially striking the head.

A blow that does not produce injury reaching the brain cannot simply be equated with this specialized sense without considering its severity and result.

In its familiar use, the blow is severe and reaches a vital part of the body.

The image consequently suits the expression of decisive, devastating defeat.

This does not establish that everyone with a brain injury necessarily dies.

Early damgh may evoke a fatal blow, but the actual injury and its outcome require context or independent evidence.

Dāmighah: a severe head wound

Early lexical and medical classifications use dāmighah for a head wound reaching or damaging the brain.

It appears alongside names such as hāshimah, munaqqilah, and āmmah.

These terms identify different degrees or characteristics of head injury.

The early classification should not be equated with modern medical categories without scientific examination.

The Qur’an itself does not discuss this classification.

Its value for root study is to clarify the blow’s severity and location.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, Al-Muḥkam and Lisān al-ʿArab: brain and dāmighah wounds

4. Semantic extension: from a physical blow to decisive defeat

Damaghahu meaning “overcame him”

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

وَدَمَغَهُ يَدْمَغُهُ دَمْغًا: غَلَبَهُ وَأَخَذَهُ مِنْ فَوْقُ

wa-damaghahu yadmaghuhu damghan ghalabahu wa-akhadhahu min fawqu

“He damagha him: he overcame him and prevailed over him from above.”

The verb here extends beyond an actual blow to the brain to severe defeat.

Their relationship can be explained through imagery: just as a severe blow to the brain can remove bodily resistance, decisive defeat can break an opponent’s power to resist.

“From above” should not, however, imply actual spatial elevation in every use.

In scholarly debate, decisive evidence can refute a false claim without any physical movement from above to below.

Elevation here can therefore depict ascendancy.

Ḥujjatun dāmighatun

After explaining concrete damgh in Al-Mufradāt, Al-Rāghib al-Isfahānī writes:

وَحُجَّةٌ دَامِغَةٌ كَذَلِكَ

wa-ḥujjatun dāmighatun kadhālika

“A dāmighah argument is likewise.”

The expression denotes evidence or reasoning that demolishes the foundation of an opposing claim.

Someone might claim that something is rationally or textually necessary, for example, while clear evidence shows that the claim’s main premise was false. That evidence can discredit the claim.

The conclusiveness of an argument must nevertheless be distinguished from the loudness of its speaker.

A dāmigh argument is decisive because it refutes the opposing claim, not because of anger, threats, humiliation, or shouting.

The truth’s victory in Qur’anic fa-yadmaghuhu should consequently not be reduced to human violence.

Al-Rāghib’s Mufradāt and Asās al-Balāghah: decisive argument and truth prevailing

Refuting an argument and defeating a person

Figurative language sometimes describes someone defeating an opponent through proof.

Two different events may, however, lie behind this expression.

One is the actual refutation of a false argument.

The other is an opponent being placed in a weaker social, psychological, or political position.

These are not necessarily identical.

An argument may be false while its advocate retains social power.

Someone may also be humiliated in a debate without their reasoning being refuted.

A ḥujjah dāmighah, strictly understood, therefore concerns the force of reasoning and refutation of a claim, not merely defeating an opponent’s person or social standing.

This distinction matters when understanding truth and falsehood in the Qur’an.

5. Subsidiary Arabic uses

The sun’s heat affecting the head

Lisān al-ʿArab states:

وَدَمَغَتْهُ الشَّمْسُ دَمْغًا: آلَمَتْ دِمَاغَهُ

wa-damaghat-hu al-shamsu damghan ālamat dimāghahu

“The sun damagha him: it caused pain in his brain.”

This concerns the severe effect of solar heat on a person.

The subject is no longer one person physically striking another’s head, but discomfort or injury caused by the sun’s heat.

Its shared feature with the principal sense is a severe effect on the head or brain.

In this use the verb does not mean ordinary sunshine.

Nor does the early expression establish a specific medical diagnosis such as severe heatstroke, brain hemorrhage, or a particular neurological disease.

In ordinary language, it expresses the intensity of the effect on the head.

Dāmighah in a camel saddle

Dictionaries also record dāmighah as an iron or wooden component at the rear of a camel saddle.

It joined and secured the saddle’s parts.

Al-Azharī describes an iron piece laid across the upper ends of the two curved saddle frames and secured with nails.

Ibn Shumayl also describes wooden versions bound with leather straps.

This use does not mean striking the head.

The tool’s name may relate to its upper position on the saddle or another resemblance.

This relationship should not be presented as a proven historical origin.

Lisān al-ʿArab: the sun’s effect and saddle components

Dhū al-Rummah’s witness

The Umayyad poet Dhū al-Rummah says:

فَرُحْنَا وَقُمْنَا وَالدَّوَامِغُ تَلْتَظِي
عَلَى الْعِيسِ مِنْ شَمْسٍ بَطِيءٍ زَوَالُهَا

fa-ruḥnā wa-qumnā wa-al-dawāmighu taltaẓī / ʿalā al-ʿīsi min shamsin baṭīʾin zawāluhā

“So we set out in the day’s heat and arose, while the saddles’ upper fittings on the camels seemed to blaze beneath a sun slow to decline.” The order fa-ruḥnā wa-qumnā is retained from Lisān al-ʿArab; Al-ʿUbāb transmits the opening as fa-qumnā fa-ruḥnā.

Al-dawāmigh here is the plural of dāmighah in its saddle sense.

This dictionary-transmitted witness shows dawāmigh naming a material component of travel equipment. A printed Tāj al-ʿArūs footnote also reports al-dawāfiʿ in the Dīwān transmission. This study therefore cites the lexical transmission al-dawāmigh without claiming a uniform reading across all Dīwān copies.

Dawāmigh should not be translated here as the camels’ brains or head wounds.

The poem concerns saddles and solar heat.

Dhū al-Rummah lived after the period of Qur’anic revelation. His verse is consequently classical Arabic evidence, not an independent pre-Islamic witness.

Tāj al-ʿArūs and printed footnote: Dhū al-Rummah and transmission variants

Dāmighah in the date palm

Dictionaries also record dāmighah for a long, hard shoot emerging among the palm’s central parts and harming the tree if left in place.

Damage to the plant’s sensitive central part matters in this use.

An imagery-based connection with striking the brain is possible: both involve injury to an important part of a structure.

This alone does not prove that the plant’s name historically originated in the image of striking a human brain.

The use belongs in the root’s lexical history but not in the direct meaning of Qur’anic fa-yadmaghuhu.

Limits of pre-Qur’anic evidence

Authoritative Arabic dictionaries give explicit, consistent definitions of damgh as a severe blow reaching the brain.

This review, however, did not establish a sufficiently secure pre-Islamic poetic witness for independently quoting that particular verb.

There is no need to attribute an uncertain line to a pre-Islamic poet merely to complete the study.

Dhū al-Rummah’s useful witness is later than the beginning of Islam and concerns the specific saddle use of dawāmigh.

The principal support for the Qur’anic sense thus remains the clear lexical definitions and the verse’s own construction.

Al-ʿUbāb and Lisān al-ʿArab: the poetic transmission, saddle and palm

6. The Qur’anic use: Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:18

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:18

بَلْ نَقْذِفُ بِالْحَقِّ عَلَى الْبَاطِلِ فَيَدْمَغُهُ فَإِذَا هُوَ زَاهِقٌ وَلَكُمُ الْوَيْلُ مِمَّا تَصِفُونَ

bal naqdhifu bi-al-ḥaqqi ʿalā al-bāṭili fa-yadmaghuhu fa-idhā huwa zāhiqun wa-lakumu al-waylu mimmā taṣifūna

“Rather, We cast truth against falsehood; it crushes it, and suddenly falsehood vanishes. Woe to you for what you describe.”

This is the root’s only Qur’anic occurrence.

The image comprises three related actions or states:

First, truth is cast against falsehood.

Then truth deals falsehood a crushing blow.

Finally, falsehood vanishes.

These parts are connected, but their words and semantic roles are independent.

Bal naqdhifu bi-al-ḥaqqi ʿalā al-bāṭili

Naqdhifu belongs to Q DH F and concretely concerns throwing or casting.

Its subject in this verse is God.

Bi-al-ḥaqqi identifies what is cast against falsehood.

ʿAlā al-bāṭili identifies the object and direction of this confrontation.

Truth is thus depicted as something powerful cast against falsehood.

This does not mean that truth here is a stone or material body.

The image expresses truth overcoming falsehood.

Qadhf and damgh should also not be identified with one another.

Qadhf expresses the casting.

Damgh emphasizes the decisive blow and its effect on the opposing side.

This distinction identifies D M GH’s own contribution to the meaning.

Fa-yadmaghuhu: what crushes what?

Fa-yadmaghuhu contains an imperfect verb.

Its subject refers back to al-ḥaqq, “truth.”

Its object pronoun -hu refers to al-bāṭil, “falsehood.”

The construction is therefore: God casts truth against falsehood, and truth crushes falsehood.

This syntactic distinction must be preserved.

Saying “God crushes falsehood” is not unrelated to the verse’s overall outcome, but it makes truth’s independent role as the direct agent of crushing less apparent in the construction.

Truth itself has an active role in the verse.

The image shows that falsehood cannot withstand truth made apparent and effective.

This does not mean that anything a person calls “truth” automatically discredits others’ claims.

A person’s claim that their statement is true requires examination through evidence.

The verse concerns the truth God casts against falsehood.

Divine truth should therefore not be equated with every personal or collective claim to possess the truth.

Why damgh rather than simply ḍarb?

Arabic ḍarb can express different kinds of striking, impact, and broader uses.

The concrete image of damgh, however, is an extremely severe blow reaching the brain.

Using this verb therefore evokes decisive defeat.

Falsehood does not merely encounter superficial opposition: the image describes a blow demolishing its power to resist.

This intensity must nevertheless be understood in the sphere of truth and falsehood.

A potentially fatal blow in a verbal image does not command striking opponents on the head.

The object of damgh is falsehood, not a person disagreeing with someone over an issue.

This distinction separates rhetorical imagery from human ethical conduct.

Fa-idhā huwa zāhiqun: the outcome of crushing

Zāhiqun belongs to Z H Q.

This family concerns departing, disappearing, and passing away.

Fa-idhā presents the result as sudden and evident.

In the verse’s image, falsehood vanishes after encountering truth and being crushed.

Zuhūq and damgh are nevertheless different words.

Damgh emphasizes a decisive blow and crushing.

Zahaqa expresses vanishing or disappearing.

This distinction matters in translation.

Rendering fa-yadmaghuhu merely as “then it destroys it” reduces part of the decisive-blow image.

Treating fa-idhā huwa zāhiqun as merely repeating the same verb likewise loses the resulting disappearance of falsehood.

Their distinct contributions should therefore be retained.

Does falsehood always disappear immediately from people’s lives?

In the verse’s image, falsehood suddenly vanishes after encountering truth.

This does not imply that every revelation of a truth immediately eliminates all false beliefs and conduct in society.

The Qur’an itself describes people persisting in denial, concealment, or following falsehood after clear signs have come.

A false claim losing its validity before clear evidence must therefore be distinguished from people continuing to follow it.

A claim may have been refuted while people continue repeating it through prejudice, self-interest, or ignorance.

Continued adherence to falsehood does not make the falsehood correct.

Conversely, a claim should not be considered true merely because opponents have refused to accept it.

Evidence and truth are the criteria, rather than follower numbers or the speed of social acceptance.

Wa-lakumu al-waylu mimmā taṣifūna

The verse ends by warning its addressees concerning what they describe.

The expression concerns their speech and the attributes or claims they state.

Earlier verses discuss the creation of heaven and earth, creation not being play, and improper claims about God.

Truth overcoming falsehood thus occurs in a context criticizing false statements about God and creation.

Damgh itself does not mean describing or attributing a statement to God.

Taṣifūna belongs to another family and requires separate analysis.

Wayl likewise expresses a threatening warning, rather than the direct meaning of damgh.

Al-Ṭabarī: damgh, vanishing and the closing warning

7. Context: creation is not play

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:16 states:

وَمَا خَلَقْنَا السَّمَاءَ وَالْأَرْضَ وَمَا بَيْنَهُمَا لَاعِبِينَ

wa-mā khalaqnā al-samāʾa wa-al-arḍa wa-mā baynahumā lāʿibīna

“We did not create heaven and earth and what is between them in play.”

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:17 also raises and rejects the supposition of God taking amusement.

Verse 18 then begins with bal, “rather.”

This connection matters.

After denying that creation is play, the Qur’an speaks of casting truth against falsehood.

Damgh thus belongs to a serious discussion of truth, creation, and improper claims about God.

Why does this context matter?

Read apart from the preceding verses, verse 18 could become merely a general slogan about truth defeating falsehood.

The context, however, concerns something more specific: divine creation and governance are not founded on play or amusement.

Truth stands against such false claims.

This clarifies the direct example’s scope.

Truth and falsehood are, of course, not confined to this issue in the Qur’an. Other verses discuss following truth, judging justly, concealing truth, and making false claims.

These subjects should nevertheless not all become the direct meaning of damgh in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:18.

The root supplies the image of decisive crushing; the subject and referent of falsehood emerge from context.

8. Related verses on truth and falsehood

Al-Isrāʾ 17:81: truth arrives and falsehood departs

Al-Isrāʾ 17:81 states:

وَقُلْ جَاءَ الْحَقُّ وَزَهَقَ الْبَاطِلُ إِنَّ الْبَاطِلَ كَانَ زَهُوقًا

wa-qul jāʾa al-ḥaqqu wa-zahaqa al-bāṭilu inna al-bāṭila kāna zahūqan

“Say: Truth has come and falsehood has vanished; falsehood is indeed bound to vanish.”

This verse clearly relates to Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:18.

Both oppose truth and falsehood.

Both describe falsehood’s vanishing, zuhūq.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:81, however, does not contain damgh.

This distinction matters.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:18 additionally supplies the decisive-blow image.

Al-Isrāʾ 17:81 states truth’s arrival and falsehood’s departure more directly.

The Al-Isrāʾ verse therefore clarifies the Al-Anbiyāʾ image’s outcome without adding another D M GH occurrence.

Al-Raʿd 13:17: foam and what remains

Al-Raʿd 13:17 describes flowing water and foam forming upon it.

It then states:

فَأَمَّا الزَّبَدُ فَيَذْهَبُ جُفَاءً وَأَمَّا مَا يَنْفَعُ النَّاسَ فَيَمْكُثُ فِي الْأَرْضِ

fa-ammā al-zabadu fa-yadhhabu jufāʾan wa-ammā mā yanfaʿu al-nāsa fa-yamkuthu fī al-arḍi

“The foam passes away, while what benefits people remains in the earth.”

This verse offers another image of truth and falsehood.

Falsehood there is likened to unstable foam.

In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:18, it is pictured as something crushed by truth’s blow.

The images differ but can be compared in relation to falsehood’s instability and truth’s and benefit’s endurance.

Damgh nevertheless does not lexically mean foam disappearing from the water’s surface.

Each Qur’anic example uses its own imagery.

Al-Anfāl 8:8: establishing truth and nullifying falsehood

Al-Anfāl 8:8 states:

لِيُحِقَّ الْحَقَّ وَيُبْطِلَ الْبَاطِلَ وَلَوْ كَرِهَ الْمُجْرِمُونَ

li-yuḥiqqa al-ḥaqqa wa-yubṭila al-bāṭila wa-law kariha al-mujrimūna

“To establish truth and nullify falsehood, though the criminals dislike it.”

Yubṭila belongs to B Ṭ L.

It concerns invalidating and nullifying.

Figurative damgh can also discredit falsehood, but has a more intense, concrete image.

Their outcomes may therefore converge while their roots and verbal images differ.

Muḥammad 47:3: following truth and falsehood

Muḥammad 47:3 states that those who disbelieve follow falsehood, while believers follow the truth from their Lord.

What people follow may therefore be truth or falsehood.

Ittibāʿ belongs to T B ʿ and concerns following.

Damgh, by contrast, concerns decisive crushing.

Following falsehood and crushing falsehood are thus different matters.

People may continue following falsehood even after evidence refuting it becomes apparent.

A false belief’s social endurance therefore does not, by itself, establish its truth.

9. Distinguishing D M GH from related roots

D M GH (damagha) and Q DH F (qadhafa): casting and crushing

They occur together in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:18.

Qadhf concerns casting and throwing.

Damgh concerns a decisive blow that concretely reaches the brain.

In the image, truth is cast and then crushes falsehood.

Qadhf thus emphasizes the initial casting, and damgh the impact’s effect.

This distinction should not become a physical law about the nature of truth and falsehood.

D M GH (damagha) and Ḍ R B (ḍaraba): striking and a severe blow to the brain

Arabic ḍarb has a broad field covering different kinds of striking and figurative uses.

Concrete damgh, however, concerns a particular severe blow reaching the brain.

Every physical damgh is thus a blow, but not every blow is damgh.

Figurative damgh also emphasizes decisive victory and refutation.

Simply translating it as “struck” may therefore fail to convey the image’s full intensity.

Study of Ḍ R B

D M GH (damagha) and D Ḥ Ḍ (daḥaḍa): crushing and discrediting an argument

Arabic daḥḍ concerns slipping and loss of firmness; in argument it also concerns invalidating a proof.

Al-Kahf 18:56 and Ghāfir 40:5 describe attempts to discredit truth through daḥḍ.

Figurative damgh, by contrast, uses a severe blow to the brain to express decisive defeat.

Their outcomes may converge in refuting a claim, but their verbal images differ.

Their moral direction must also be understood contextually: someone may argue falsely to discredit truth, whereas truth crushes falsehood in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:18.

Study of D Ḥ Ḍ

D M GH (damagha) and B Ṭ L (baṭala): invalidation and crushing

B Ṭ L concerns being invalid or becoming ineffective.

Damgh can portray the decisive cause of a false claim losing its validity.

Damgh itself, however, does not mean every formal or legal annulment.

A contract may, for example, be declared void under valid rules, without every Arabic context calling that action damgh.

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:18 uses an intense image of truth defeating falsehood.

Study of B Ṭ L

D M GH (damagha) and Z H Q (zahaqa): a decisive blow and vanishing

Zahaqa expresses vanishing and disappearing.

Damgh emphasizes the decisive blow and defeat.

They occur successively in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:18.

Their contextual relationship is thus that of crushing and vanishing.

They should nevertheless not be treated as complete synonyms.

Something may vanish without a blow-to-the-brain image being applied to it.

D M GH (damagha) and Q H R (qahara): prevailing and decisively crushing

Arabic qahr concerns prevailing and overpowering.

Dictionaries also explain figurative damgh through qahr and victory.

Qahr, however, has a broader field of dominance and victory.

In its familiar use, damgh retains the image of a decisive blow.

Not every victory should therefore be called damgh.

Nor does damgh in an example about truth and falsehood establish that every exercise of power over someone is truth prevailing.

D M GH (damagha) and D M R (dammara): destruction and a decisive blow

Qur’anic tadmīr concerns the destruction of persons, societies, and structures.

Damgh, however, occurs only once, in an image concerning falsehood.

Its concrete use concerns a blow injuring the brain.

Tadmīr generally concerns destruction and severe devastation.

Despite similar outcomes, the roots’ semantic fields therefore differ.

Their shared initial D M letters do not establish derivation from a single common root.

Study of D M R

10. Translation proposals for Forqan

Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:18

The Qur’anic form is:

فَيَدْمَغُهُ

fa-yadmaghuhu

Equivalents worth considering include:

“Then it crushes it”: a natural equivalent preserving the intensity of defeat and the blow’s image.

“Then it brings it down”: conveys the outcome but less of the direct-blow image.

“Then it decisively shatters it”: clarifies intensity, although “decisively” is an added explanation.

“Then it crushes its brain”: closer to the concrete image, but potentially suggesting an actual body with a brain. This concern also applies to the corresponding Persian rendering.

“Then it nullifies it”: conveys the outcome while largely removing damgh’s intensity and principal image.

The proposed basic Forqan rendering is therefore “then it crushes it,” corresponding to the proposed Persian equivalent.

This suits the Arabic without requiring falsehood to be understood as bodily.

The complete phrase is:

بَلْ نَقْذِفُ بِالْحَقِّ عَلَى الْبَاطِلِ فَيَدْمَغُهُ فَإِذَا هُوَ زَاهِقٌ

bal naqdhifu bi-al-ḥaqqi ʿalā al-bāṭili fa-yadmaghuhu fa-idhā huwa zāhiqun

It can be rendered:

“Rather, We cast truth against falsehood; it crushes it, and suddenly falsehood vanishes.”

This retains three independent components:

Casting truth, crushing falsehood, and falsehood’s vanishing.

This proposal does not automatically change Forqan’s published translation. Any correction of approved wording requires separate review and approval.

Non-Qur’anic equivalents

Dimāgh: brain.

Damagha raʾsahu: struck his head so that the injury reached the brain.

Damgh: a severe blow reaching the brain; figuratively, decisive defeat.

Dāmighah: a severe wound reaching the brain; in other uses, the name of certain tool components.

Ḥujjatun dāmighatun: decisive evidence refuting the opposing claim.

Madmūgh: struck or injured through damgh.

Damaghat-hu al-shamsu: the sun’s heat caused pain in his head.

These meanings must be assessed according to each sentence’s subject matter.

11. Conclusion

Arabic D M GH concerns the brain and a severe blow reaching it.

Unlike many roots, Ibn Fāris describes this as a limited family with little branching.

Dictionaries name the brain dimāgh and define damagha as delivering a blow reaching it.

This concrete field extends figuratively to prevailing and decisively crushing.

A ḥujjah dāmighah likewise discredits an opposing claim.

The root occurs only once in the Qur’an, as fa-yadmaghuhu in Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:18.

God casts truth against falsehood, truth crushes falsehood, and falsehood vanishes.

Qadhf expresses casting.

Damgh emphasizes the impact’s decisive effect.

Zuhūq expresses falsehood’s disappearance.

These three expressions must remain distinct.

The blow-to-the-brain image also does not authorize physical violence against people with different beliefs or interpretations: the direct object of damgh is falsehood.

Divine truth should likewise not be equated with every human claim to possess truth.

A statement’s truth requires sound evidence and support. Mere assertion, the speaker’s social standing, or follower numbers cannot turn falsehood into truth.

A false claim may also be refuted while people continue following it. A false belief’s social persistence does not preserve its logical validity.

Proposed final definition: In its familiar core, D M GH denotes a severe blow to the head reaching and injuring the brain. The concrete image extends to prevailing and decisively crushing. In Al-Anbiyāʾ 21:18, fa-yadmaghuhu describes truth’s effect on falsehood: crushing and decisively discrediting it. Its disappearance is expressed separately by fa-idhā huwa zāhiqun. Brief root meaning: A severe blow to the head reaching the brain; figuratively, crushing and decisively overcoming.
