The Qur’anic Root Q N W

The date-palm cluster: distinguishing the cluster, its strands, and its supporting stalk

Qur’anic forms

Qinwānun

Broken plural; Al-Anʿām 6:99

1. Basic meaning and linguistic history

قِنْو (qinw) is the Arabic name for a date-palm cluster: its slender branching strands together with what they bear. Its plural is قِنْوَان (qinwān). “Clusters of dates” clearly conveys the Qur’anic plural. The word names neither the tree nor a single date, nor merely the cluster’s bare stalk.

Arabic agricultural vocabulary distinguishes parts of the date palm and stages of fruit growth and ripening. Qinw names neither the tree itself nor one date, but the cluster of fruits together with their supporting branching structure.

Arabic dictionaries sometimes discuss forms with final wāw, Q N W, and final yāʾ, Q N Y, in one entry. This overlap occurs in words for acquiring and keeping property. Here the focus is qinwān; aqnā is examined in the separate Q N Y study.

Qur’anic form examined: قِنْوَانٌ (qinwānun), clusters of dates, the sole occurrence under this entry, in Al-Anʿām 6:99. Three other verses in this article provide comparative evidence.

Qinw and qunw are recognized vocalizations for a date cluster; aqnāʾ and qinwān are its plurals. They must not be equated with qinyah, property kept for use, or qanāh, a spear or water conduit, even though dictionaries place them in related lexical families.

2. Physical referent and evidence from early Arabic

The date-palm cluster

In the transmitted text of the Muʿallaqah of the pre-Islamic poet Imruʾ al-Qays, a beloved’s long, thick hair is described as follows:

وَفَرْعٍ يَزِينُ الْمَتْنَ أَسْوَدَ فَاحِمٍ أَثِيثٍ كَقِنْوِ النَّخْلَةِ الْمُتَعَثْكِلِ

“And a tress adorning the back, coal-black and thick, like the many-stranded cluster of a date palm.”

The poet compares thick locks of hair to the strands of a date cluster. This transmitted pre-Islamic poetic witness supports the name’s earlier use. Attribution alone cannot determine the precise date of composition or original wording.

In another poem attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays, tall, fruitful palms are described:

سَوَامِقَ جَبَّارٍ أَثِيثٍ فُرُوعُهُ وَعَالَيْنَ قِنْوَانًا مِنَ الْبُسْرِ أَحْمَرَا

“Tall, robust palms with dense branches, bearing clusters of red dates high above.”

Busr denotes dates that have not yet reached the soft-ripe ruṭab stage.

The verse is transmitted with variants, but qinwān clearly denotes date clusters in those versions.

This example also shows that qinw need not mean a cluster of fully ripe dates or one close to the ground. Ripeness and position are expressed by other words and descriptions.

Lexical analysis of attachment and its distinction from word history

In an entry with a weak final radical, Ibn Fāris distinguishes attachment and intermingling from height and prominence. This classifies recorded meanings analytically; it does not conclusively establish that every wāw and yāʾ form developed from one original image.

He relates qinw to attachment because the cluster remains connected to its tree. That is his proposed semantic connection; the direct definition remains a date-palm cluster.

Lexical sources record qanāh for a spear, a water conduit, and, in qanāt al-ẓahr, the spine. These uses concern an elongated, connected form, but similarity alone does not establish a common historical origin with the name of a date cluster.

Attachment and extension alone do not define qinw: not every part attached to a palm or every elongated object is called qinw. The definition must retain the identity of the cluster.

3. Qinwān in the Qur’an

The Qur’an uses the word in Al-Anʿām while inviting people to observe plant growth and the formation and ripening of fruits:

وَهُوَ الَّذى أَنزَلَ مِنَ السَّماءِ ماءً فَأَخرَجنا بِهِ نَباتَ كُلِّ شَىءٍ فَأَخرَجنا مِنهُ خَضِرًا نُخرِجُ مِنهُ حَبًّا مُتَراكِبًا وَمِنَ النَّخلِ مِن طَلعِها قِنوانٌ دانِيَةٌ وَجَنّٰتٍ مِن أَعنابٍ وَالزَّيتونَ وَالرُّمّانَ مُشتَبِهًا وَغَيرَ مُتَشٰبِهٍ انظُروا إِلىٰ ثَمَرِهِ إِذا أَثمَرَ وَيَنعِهِ إِنَّ فى ذٰلِكُم لَـٔايٰتٍ لِقَومٍ يُؤمِنونَ

Wa-huwa alladhī anzala mina al-samāʾi māʾan fa-akhrajnā bihi nabāta kulli shayʾin fa-akhrajnā minhu khaḍiran nukhriju minhu ḥabban mutarākiban wa-mina al-nakhli min ṭalʿihā qinwānun dāniyatun wa-jannātin min aʿnābin wa-l-zaytūna wa-l-rummāna mushtabihan wa-ghayra mutashābihin unẓurū ilā thamarihi idhā athmara wa-yanʿihi inna fī dhālikum la-āyātin li-qawmin yuʾminūn.

“He is the One who sent down water from the sky, and with it We brought forth vegetation of every kind. From it We brought forth green growth, from which We bring forth overlapping grains; and from the palms, from their emerging flower clusters, [come] nearby clusters of dates; and gardens of grapes, olives, and pomegranates, alike and unlike. Look at its fruit when it bears fruit and at its ripening. Indeed, in these are signs for people who believe.”

Al-An'am 6:99

Four words occur together in the date-palm passage, each expressing a different aspect of growth and produce:

  • النَّخْل (al-nakhl): date palms.
  • طَلْع (ṭalʿ): the emerging flower and fruit structure of the palm.
  • قِنْوَان (qinwān): date clusters.
  • دَانِيَة (dāniyah): near and within reach.

Nakhl names the tree, ṭalʿ the emerging reproductive structure, and qinwān its clusters. Their nearness is expressed by dāniyah, from D N W.

Closeness to the ground, easy access, and a low position should therefore not be built into the independent meaning of qinwān.

Morphology and syntax

Qinwān is the broken plural of qinw, on the fiʿlān pattern.

One morphological feature is that its written plural resembles the dual. The two nevertheless differ grammatically.

The nominative dual is qinwāni and the accusative or genitive dual is qinwayni; the nominative plural is qinwānun. The dual loses its nūn in a construct phrase, whereas the plural’s nūn is part of the word’s structure.

In the verse, nunation and grammatical construction show that qinwān is plural, not dual. The verse therefore refers to palm clusters, not two particular clusters.

Syntactically, qinwānun is a delayed subject, preceded by its prepositional predicate. Dāniyatun qualifies qinwān.

The feminine singular dāniyah follows Arabic agreement for nonhuman plurals; it does not mean that the cluster is singular.

Observing growth and ripening

The verse draws attention not only to the existence of different fruits, but to the process by which they emerge and ripen.

Someone buying dates or other fruit in a market usually encounters the end result. The verse directs attention to the process of growth, formation, and ripening itself.

Qinwān is one element of that picture. Its lexical meaning is date clusters; the invitation to observe creation and recognize divine signs arises from the verse as a whole.

4. Comparison with related roots and words

Q N W (qinw) and Ṭ L ʿ (ṭalʿ): date clusters and emerging produce

The two words appear together in Al-Anʿām 6:99.

Ṭalʿ concerns emerging and appearing; in the palm context, it denotes the emerging flower cluster and produce.

Qāf says:

وَالنَّخلَ باسِقٰتٍ لَها طَلعٌ نَضيدٌ

Wa-l-nakhla bāsiqātin lahā ṭalʿun naḍīd.

“And tall date palms with closely arranged flower clusters.”

Qaf 50:10

Ṭalʿ and qinw refer to related aspects of palm growth, and their lexical explanations sometimes overlap. In Al-Anʿām 6:99, min ṭalʿihā identifies where the clusters emerge from. This contextual distinction should not become an absolute boundary governing every use of the two words.

Q N W (qinw) and ʿ R J N (ʿurjūn): the cluster and its supporting stalk

ʿUrjūn denotes the main supporting stalk of a date cluster. Its curved shape becomes more apparent after the fruit is harvested and the stalk dries.

Yā Sīn says:

وَالقَمَرَ قَدَّرنٰهُ مَنازِلَ حَتّىٰ عادَ كَالعُرجونِ القَديمِ

Wa-l-qamara qaddarnāhu manāzila ḥattā ʿāda ka-l-ʿurjūni al-qadīm.

“And the moon: We have determined phases for it until it returns like an old date-cluster stalk.”

Ya-Sin 36:39

Qinw concerns the cluster and what it bears, whereas ʿurjūn more particularly denotes its supporting axis or stalk.

Q N W (qinw) and Th M R (thamar): a cluster and a tree’s produce

Thamar denotes fruit and the produce of plant growth; it is not restricted to one kind of tree or a particular fruit structure.

Apples, pomegranates, grapes, and dates are examples of thamar, whereas qinw in its familiar usage specifically names a palm cluster.

Q N W (qinw) and Q Ṭ F (qiṭf): a date cluster and produce for picking

Qiṭf denotes fruit or a cluster that has been picked or is ready for picking.

Qinw specifically names a date-palm cluster and its structure. In the Q Ṭ F family, picking and harvesting are prominent.

5. The relationship between Q N W and Q N Y

Separating Q N W and Q N Y in Forqan’s index and the morphological index used here organizes Qur’anic occurrences. It does not deny the overlap of wāw and yāʾ forms in Arabic dictionaries.

For example, qanawtu al-shayʾ and qanaytu al-shayʾ are recorded for acquiring and keeping something. Iqtināʾ likewise means obtaining and retaining property for oneself.

Some early Arabic dialects used qinyān rather than qinwān as the plural of qinw.

Al-Najm says:

وَأَنَّهُ هُوَ أَغنىٰ وَأَقنىٰ

Wa-annahu huwa aghnā wa-aqnā.

“And it is He who gave sufficiency and bestowed possessions.”

An-Najm 53:48

This study translates aqnā as “bestowed possessions” and examines it under Q N Y; bringing satisfaction is another lexically supported reading. That choice concerns the verb in Al-Najm, not the definition of qinwān.

Despite the families’ historical and lexical overlap, bestowing property should not be imported into qinwān in Al-Anʿām. There it specifically names date clusters.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

For qinwānun in Al-Anʿām 6:99, “clusters of dates” is proposed.

Qinwānun dāniyatun can be rendered “nearby clusters of dates” or “date clusters within reach.” “Cluster” names the whole, with fruit-bearing strands as its parts; “nearby” comes from dāniyah.

The translation should preserve the distinction between ṭalʿ, qinwān, and dāniyah, rather than merging emergence, cluster structure, and nearness into one word.

7. Conclusion

Qinwān in Al-Anʿām 6:99 is the plural name for date-palm clusters. Transmitted poetic evidence supports this name’s early use. Deriving all wāw and yāʾ branches from attachment or extension goes beyond the direct definition and requires independent historical evidence.

Proposed definition for Forqan: Qinw: a date-palm cluster, with its slender strands and what they bear; qinwān: clusters of dates. Fruit ripeness and proximity to the ground are not necessary parts of the name’s meaning.
