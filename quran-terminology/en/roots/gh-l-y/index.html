Boiling, seething, movement caused by heat, and imagery of intense boiling

The Arabic root غ ل ي concerns liquids boiling under heat. غَلَى means that water or another liquid boiled; غَلْي and غَلَيَان name boiling itself; أَغْلَى means to bring something to the boil.

In its basic use, a liquid boils under heat and develops a distinctive churning movement. Water in a pot over a fire, for example, heats up and boils, its surface moving.

Arabic also uses this image for intense anger, excitement, and agitation. Someone whose anger is aroused may be compared to a boiling pot. This figurative use should not replace the root's basic meaning.

The root occurs twice in the Quran, both times in Dukhan: يَغْلِي in Dukhan 44:45 and غَلْي in Dukhan 44:46.

Both occurrences describe the food of punishment. Its boiling in bellies is compared to the boiling of intensely hot water.

This root differs from غ ل و, used in the Quran for exceeding bounds in religion. High prices and overcharging are likewise not the Quranic meaning of غ ل ي.

1. Meaning in early Arabic

In early Arabic, غَلَتِ الْقِدْرُ تَغْلِي described the contents of a pot boiling.

قِدْر is a vessel placed over a fire to cook or boil food.

Here the word concerns heat and the movement it causes. Water or food in the vessel heats up, boils, and loses its previous stillness.

A pot of water over a fire may initially be still. As the heat increases, the water moves more and eventually boils.

Arabic expresses this as غَلَى الْمَاءُ or غَلَتِ الْقِدْرُ.

In the first expression, the water itself is the subject that boils.

In the second, boiling is attributed to the pot, meaning its contents.

This distinction is familiar in ordinary language. Persian likewise says “the pot boils,” although it is the contents that boil, not the vessel itself.

Lexicons record two principal verbal nouns:

غَلْي and غَلَيَان.

Both concern boiling; their different forms do not justify inventing wholly independent meanings.

A witness attributed to pre-Islamic poetry: al-Nabigha's boiling pots

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Nabigha al-Dhubyani reads:

يَسِيرُ بِهَا النُّعْمَانُ تَغْلِي قُدُورُهُ

yasīru bihā al-Nuʿmānu taghlī qudūruhu

تَجِيشُ بِأَسْبَابِ الْمَنَايَا الْمَرَاجِلُ

tajīshu bi-asbābi al-manāyā al-marājilu

Translation:

“Al-Nuʿman advances with that army; his pots boil, and the great cauldrons seethe with the causes of death.”

The poet uses boiling pots to portray the intensity and terror of battle.

تَغْلِي قُدُورُهُ refers to his pots boiling.

تَجِيشُ belongs to another root and concerns seething, movement, and surging.

الْمَرَاجِلُ is the plural of مِرْجَل: large cooking pots or cauldrons.

Combining boiling pots with the causes of death, the poet depicts fierce combat and an army in motion.

The familiar meaning of boiling supplies the imagery of war.

This does not mean that غلي originally meant fighting, killing, or campaigning.

Battle's intensity is the subject of the comparison; boiling is the word's meaning.

The verse is transmitted in al-Nabigha's collected poetry and cited in commentary. Its image of boiling pots is therefore a documented poetic witness, without requiring every variant of the poem to be identical.

It shows that boiling pots and their use to portray intensity and tumult were familiar in poetry attributed to pre-Islamic times.

Another witness: the sound of boiling in Imruʾ al-Qays

The muʿallaqa attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays describes a powerful horse:

عَلَى الذَّبْلِ جَيَّاشٌ كَأَنَّ اهْتِزَامَهُ

ʿalā al-dhabli jayyāshun ka-anna ihtizāmahu

إِذَا جَاشَ فِيهِ حَمْيُهُ غَلْيُ مِرْجَلِ

idhā jāsha fīhi ḥamyuhu ghalyu mirjali

Translation:

“Though lean, he is vigorous and surging; when his inner ardour stirs, his rumbling sounds like a cauldron boiling.”

غَلْيُ مِرْجَلِ here refers to a pot boiling.

The horse's inner rumbling and surging energy are compared to a boiling cauldron.

غلي names boiling itself within a simile.

This witness is particularly useful for غَلْي, the same verbal noun found in Dukhan 44:46.

The verse has variant readings in the transmitted muʿallaqa. Some versions have عَلَى الْعَقْبِ or similar wording instead of عَلَى الذَّبْلِ.

These variants change the interpretation of the opening, while the comparison غَلْيُ مِرْجَلِ remains in familiar versions.

The witness shows that غَلْي evoked an intelligible image of boiling and vigorous liquid movement, usable to describe sound, energy, and excitement.

2. Semantic core and branches of usage

A. غَلَى: boiling under heat

The basic meaning of غَلَى is to boil.

It applies to water and other liquids and may be attributed to a pot's contents.

Water, milk, or liquid food may boil when heated.

But not all heating is boiling.

Water can become warm without boiling.

Once the churning called غليان begins, the verb غلى is appropriate.

Thus غلى concerns boiling, not merely a rise in temperature.

This matters in translation. Dukhan 44:45 uses يغلي, so “becomes warm” does not adequately convey it.

“Boils” is more precise.

B. غَلْي and غَلَيَان: the process of boiling

غَلْي and غَلَيَان are two established verbal nouns of this verb.

An Arabic verbal noun can name an action or process itself.

غَلَيَانُ الْمَاءِ, for example, refers to water boiling.

It means not merely hot water but its boiling state.

The Quran uses the shorter form غَلْي.

It should not be mistaken for the name of a substance or implement.

غَلْي names boiling; حَمِيم in the Quranic phrase is the substance whose boiling supplies the comparison.

C. أَغْلَى and غَلَّى: bringing to the boil

أَغْلَى الْمَاءَ means to bring water to the boil.

غَلَّى الْمَاءَ is likewise recorded for boiling water.

Their distinction from غَلَى الْمَاءُ is apparent in the subject and sentence structure.

In غَلَى الْمَاءُ, the water boils.

In أَغْلَى الرَّجُلُ الْمَاءَ, the person makes the water boil.

The first construction describes boiling occurring.

The second involves a person or agent causing it.

Forms IV and II can therefore have a causative meaning in these uses.

Neither أغلى nor غلّى occurs in the Quran. The Quranic يغلي belongs to the basic three-consonant verb.

D. Seething anger and excitement

Arabic sources also record غَلِيَ الرَّجُلُ for a person's anger becoming intense.

Persian likewise says “his blood boiled” or “he seethed with anger.”

This uses liquid boiling to explain an inner human state.

Someone may become so angry at injustice that their voice, movement, and reactions intensify.

Boiling imagery can describe this condition.

The figurative use does not imply that water literally boils inside the person's body.

Nor is every intense anger necessarily wrong. Its value and resulting conduct depend on the cause, purpose, and manner of response.

The Quran does not use غ ل ي for this psychological sense. Both occurrences describe boiling in the imagery of punishment.

E. غَالِيَة: a compounded perfume

Arabic sources also use غَالِيَة for a kind of blended perfume.

Some lexical explanations associate the name with boiling aromatic ingredients and oils.

Another explanation connects it with the perfume's high price and value.

These are different etymologies: boiling belongs to غ ل ي, whereas high prices belong to غ ل و.

Historical accounts of the perfume's naming largely concern periods after the Quran's revelation.

Without independent evidence, it should not be presented as an unquestionably pre-Islamic derivative of boiling.

The word does not occur in the Quran and is not decisive for understanding يغلي or غلي in Dukhan.

3. Important forms and derivatives

Established forms associated with غ ل ي include:

  • غَلَى / يَغْلِي: boiled / boils.
  • غَلْي: boiling.
  • غَلَيَان: boiling or seething.
  • غَالٍ: boiling, in the relevant sense.
  • أَغْلَى / يُغْلِي: brought / brings to the boil.
  • إِغْلَاء: bringing to the boil.
  • غَلَّى / يُغَلِّي: boiled; in other uses, perfumed with a compound scent.
  • تَغْلِيَة: boiling; in another lexical use, greeting from a distance by a gesture.
  • مُغْلًى: something that has been boiled.
  • تَغَلَّى: perfumed oneself with ghaliya.
  • غَالِيَة: a blended perfume, with differing explanations of its name.
  • غَلَّايَة: a vessel or appliance for boiling liquids, in later usage.

Only two of these derivatives occur in the Quran:

يَغْلِي: an imperfect verb in the basic form.

غَلْي: the verbal noun of that verb.

أغلى, غلّى, and تغلّى therefore have no Quranic occurrences.

غليان is also absent, although it is an established verbal noun in Arabic.

Examining Arabic derivatives helps establish the root's range, but non-Quranic forms must not be represented as words appearing in the verses.

The morphology of يَغْلِي

يَغْلِي is the imperfect of غَلَى يَغْلِي.

It is a final-weak root whose third consonant is yāʾ, appearing as alif or yāʾ in different forms.

In Dukhan 44:45, يَغْلِي is third-person masculine singular.

In context it describes boiling occurring.

What boils is determined by the verb's relationship with the preceding expressions.

The construction كَغَلْيِ الْحَمِيمِ

In Dukhan 44:46, غَلْي is a verbal noun.

The initial كَـ marks a comparison.

الْحَمِيمِ is the genitive complement of غَلْي.

The phrase therefore means “like the boiling of scalding water.”

غلي here names a process, not a food or drink.

4. يَغْلِي فِي الْبُطُونِ in the Quran

Dukhan 44:45

كَالْمُهْلِ يَغْلِي فِي الْبُطُونِ

ka-al-muhli yaghlī fī al-buṭūn

Al-Dukhān 44:45

“Like molten metal, it boils in the bellies.”

This verse continues the description of the tree of zaqqum.

Dukhan 44:43 introduces شَجَرَتَ الزَّقُّومِ.

Dukhan 44:44 calls it the food of الْأَثِيمِ, the sinful one.

Dukhan 44:45 then compares the food to الْمُهْلِ and describes it boiling in bellies.

The meaning of يغلي is inseparable here from food entering the belly.

كَالْمُهْلِ: comparison with an intensely hot substance

مُهْل belongs to another root.

Lexicons and Quranic commentaries explain it in such ways as molten metal or thick oil sediment.

Determining its exact referent is a separate question from the root غ ل ي.

The translation proposed above renders كالمهل as “like molten metal.”

For the present root, the key point is that يَغْلِي follows this comparison.

The verse thus portrays intensely hot, boiling food.

But يغلي itself does not mean molten metal or oil.

Boiling is the action or state; muhl supplies the comparison.

فِي الْبُطُونِ: where the boiling occurs

The verse includes فِي الْبُطُونِ.

بطون is the plural of بطن, belly.

The preposition في specifies where the described event occurs.

The verse does not merely say that food boils before being eaten: it describes boiling in bellies.

This distinction matters.

In ordinary life, food may boil in a pot and be eaten after cooling.

Here boiling is located inside the bellies.

Thus يغلي في البطون should not be translated merely as “was hot before it was eaten.”

Nor should the expression be made into a definitive theory of chemical reactions, exact temperatures, or the body's biological structure in the Hereafter.

The Quran portrays boiling in bellies without explaining its physical mechanism.

The antecedent of يَغْلِي

In the Hafs reading, the verb is يَغْلِي.

Grammarians have discussed two proposed antecedents for its implicit subject, but they have not accepted them equally.

The first is طَعَامُ الْأَثِيمِ in Dukhan 44:44: the sinful one's food boils in the bellies.

The second proposal connects the verb with الْمُهْلِ because it is nearby. Abu ʿAli al-Farisi rejects that analysis: muhl supplies the comparison, while what boils is the food compared to it.

This grammatical disagreement does not change the verb's meaning, boiling.

Other recognized readings have تَغْلِي.

In that reading, the implicit subject refers to the grammatically feminine شَجَرَتَ الزَّقُّومِ.

The intended image is what is eaten from the tree boiling in the bellies.

The reading variation changes the verb's grammatical reference, not the root's meaning.

Boiling remains part of the description of zaqqum and punishment.

5. كَغَلْيِ الْحَمِيمِ: boiling scalding water

Dukhan 44:46

كَغَلْيِ الْحَمِيمِ

ka-ghalyi al-ḥamīm

Al-Dukhān 44:46

“Like the boiling of scalding water.”

The verse is semantically connected to the preceding one, while each contributes its own part to the image.

Dukhan 44:45 uses the verb يَغْلِي: it boils.

Dukhan 44:46 uses the verbal noun غَلْي: boiling.

The first describes boiling occurring; the second compares its manner to the boiling of hamim.

الْحَمِيمِ: intensely hot water

حميم here means intensely hot, scalding water.

It belongs to ح م م and should not be equated with غ ل ي.

In this phrase, hamim is what boils in the comparison.

غلي is the process itself.

Water, for example, may be warm without boiling.

When it boils, it has the boiling state in addition to being hot.

The verse places these two concepts together.

حميم evokes intense heat; غلي evokes boiling.

“Like the heat of hot water” therefore does not convey the entire phrase.

“Like the boiling of scalding water” is more precise.

Why boiling rather than mere heat?

Saying only that the food was intensely hot would describe its heat.

غلي additionally denotes boiling and its active state.

In ordinary experience, boiling liquid visibly churns and moves.

The image therefore evokes both heat and the liquid's restless movement.

In the context of punishment, this emphasizes the condition's intensity and painfulness.

That severity comes from the whole image and context; غ ل ي does not mean punishment in every Arabic use.

In al-Nabigha's poem, a boiling pot depicts fierce battle.

In Imruʾ al-Qays's poem, it depicts the sound of a powerful horse.

In Dukhan, the same process describes the food of punishment.

Boiling retains its meaning, while its subject and the image's significance vary with context.

Related verses about the food of punishment

Saffat 37:66 describes filling bellies with food from the tree of zaqqum.

Saffat 37:67 mentions a mixture of scalding water.

Waqiah 56:52 describes eating from zaqqum, followed by drinking hamim in Waqiah 56:54.

These verses are thematically related to the imagery in Dukhan.

Their features should not all be included in the lexical meaning of غلي.

غلي concerns boiling.

زقوم names the tree or food under discussion.

حميم means intensely hot water.

Eating, drinking, filling the belly, and punishment are conveyed by other words and clauses.

Kahf 18:29 also uses muhl to describe a punishing drink, with the scorching of faces expressed by another verb.

Boiling, scorching, drinking, and choking are therefore not synonyms; each contributes its own meaning.

6. Distinctions from related roots

غ ل ي versus غ ل و: boiling and exceeding bounds

These roots look similar in writing but are treated separately in the Quranic corpus.

غ ل ي concerns boiling and seething.

غ ل و concerns rising, increasing, and exceeding a measure.

In Nisa 4:171 and Maidah 5:77, تَغْلُوا belongs to غ ل و and concerns excess in religion.

By contrast, يَغْلِي in Dukhan 44:45 belongs to غ ل ي and means boiling.

Their similarity, especially without vowel marks, can cause confusion.

But لَا تَغْلُوا does not mean “do not boil,” nor does يَغْلِي in Dukhan mean religious excess.

Some Arabic forms for rising prices and causing boiling look similar; root and context must be checked before assigning a meaning.

غ ل ي versus ف و ر: boiling and surging

ف و ر concerns boiling, surging, and vigorous movement.

Its semantic field is close to غلي.

A vigorously boiling liquid may foam and overflow its vessel.

Boiling and surging can thus occur together.

Yet boiling does not necessarily cause overflow.

Mulk 67:7 describes the Fire with تَفُورُ.

This concerns intensity and surging but does not belong to غ ل ي.

Not every feature of surging should be transferred to Quranic غلي.

غ ل ي versus ج ي ش: boiling and seething

جاش is used for a liquid boiling or stirring.

Its family also describes a surging sea, intensified emotion, and a crowd in motion.

Al-Nabigha places تغلي and تجيش in the same image.

Their combination shows the closeness of their imagery.

جاش can cover a broader range of tumult and vigorous movement, whereas the direct sense of غلي concerns boiling.

Their appearance in one verse does not make all their uses identical.

غ ل ي versus ح م م: boiling and heat

حميم, when used of water, denotes intense heat.

غلي names boiling itself.

They appear together in Dukhan 44:46.

The distinction is visible in the verse: غلي is boiling, and حميم the intensely hot liquid whose boiling supplies the comparison.

حميم also occurs in the Quran for a close friend. That use must be considered separately from scalding water.

غ ل ي versus ش و ي: boiling and scorching

شوي concerns roasting or scorching through heat.

In Kahf 18:29, يَشْوِي describes scorching faces.

غلي in Dukhan 44:45, however, concerns boiling.

An intensely hot substance may scorch, or a liquid may boil, but these are different processes.

Rendering يغلي as “scorches” without preserving boiling erases the distinction.

غ ل ي versus ن ض ج: boiling and becoming cooked

نضج concerns ripening and becoming cooked.

Food may cook in boiling water.

But the water boiling and the food cooking are different processes.

Nisa 4:56 uses نَضِجَتْ of skins in the context of punishment.

That belongs to ن ض ج, not غ ل ي.

The boiling of food in Dukhan does not by itself establish every feature of its cooking or structural alteration.

7. Questions of interpretation and translation

Does غلي mean only being hot?

No. Heat and boiling are related but distinct.

A liquid can be hot without yet boiling.

غلي denotes the boiling state.

“Boils” is therefore more accurate than “becomes hot” in Dukhan 44:45.

Does غلي mean becoming expensive?

Not in its Quranic use.

Rising prices belong to the family غ ل و.

Quranic غ ل ي, by contrast, concerns boiling.

Their visual similarity should not collapse the distinction.

Is غليان always associated with anger?

No.

Language can portray intense anger through boiling, but the root's basic sense is boiling under heat.

Neither Quranic occurrence of غ ل ي means becoming angry.

Is يَغْلِي فِي الْبُطُونِ merely a metaphor for sorrow?

The context describes zaqqum and its boiling in bellies.

The primary reading should preserve that image.

Its pain and suffering invite reflection, but boiling should not be removed and the expression reduced to psychological distress alone.

Conversely, the physical manner of this boiling in the Hereafter is not established by the word itself.

Does boiling here mean a specific scientific reaction?

No. غلي is a familiar word for boiling.

Modern science can describe liquid boiling through temperature, pressure, and phase transition.

A later scientific definition should not be presented as the entirety of the word's meaning in the Arabic of revelation.

The verse specifies neither temperature nor ambient pressure nor chemical composition.

Do غلي and غليان have independent meanings?

In their basic use, both name boiling.

The Quran uses غَلْي.

غليان is familiar in Arabic and Persian but does not itself occur in the Quran.

A Quranic study should preserve the actual form used in the verse.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

In Dukhan 44:45, يَغْلِي means “boils.”

A suitable translation is:

“Like molten metal, it boils in the bellies.”

In Dukhan 44:46, غَلْي means “boiling.”

A suitable translation is:

“Like the boiling of scalding water.”

These equivalents preserve the distinction between verb and verbal noun.

Other Arabic derivatives may be rendered as follows:

غَلَى: boiled.

يَغْلِي: boils.

غَلْي: boiling.

غَلَيَان: boiling or seething.

غَالٍ: boiling, in this root's relevant sense.

أَغْلَى: brought to the boil.

إِغْلَاء: bringing to the boil.

غَلَّى: caused to boil.

تَغْلِيَة: boiling; in another lexical use, greeting from a distance by a gesture.

مُغْلًى: boiled.

غَالِيَة: a compounded perfume, with disputed explanations of its name.

Translation should distinguish boiling, heating, scorching, and surging. They may occur together but retain their individual meanings.

Conclusion

The Arabic root غ ل ي concerns boiling and seething.

In its basic use, water or another liquid boils under heat, developing distinctive movement and agitation.

Poetry attributed to pre-Islamic times supports this meaning.

Al-Nabigha uses boiling pots to portray intense battle.

Imruʾ al-Qays compares a powerful horse's sound to a boiling cauldron.

These witnesses show both a familiar material meaning and literary comparisons conveying intensity, movement, and tumult.

The root occurs twice in the Quran, both times in Dukhan.

يَغْلِي in Dukhan 44:45 describes zaqqum food boiling in bellies.

غَلْي in Dukhan 44:46 names boiling itself in a comparison with scalding water.

In these verses, غلي means boiling, not punishment, scorching, or rising prices. The image's severity comes from combining it with zaqqum, muhl, hamim, and the context of punishment.

غ ل ي must also remain distinct from غ ل و. In the Quran, the former concerns boiling and the latter exceeding bounds in religion.

The semantic core of غ ل ي is liquid boiling under heat; wider usage can employ that image for intense anger, excitement, and tumult.

Its direct Quranic meaning is clear: boiling and seething, evoking intense heat and churning in the description of the food of punishment.
