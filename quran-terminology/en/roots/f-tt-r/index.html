The triliteral Arabic root ف ط ر has two important, established senses: splitting or opening something, and beginning or bringing it into being. The same family includes فَاطِر, an originator or creator; فِطْرَة, the original nature and constitution of creation; and اِنْفِطَار, splitting apart.

In material usage, فَطَرَ الشَّيْءَ means to split something, while اِنْفَطَرَ describes something splitting or cracking.

When the Qur’an calls God فَاطِرِ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ, however, it means the originator of the heavens and earth, rather than merely someone who split existing heavens and earth.

This distinction also matters in فِطْرَةَ اللَّهِ الَّتِي فَطَرَ النَّاسَ عَلَيْهَا. Fitrah concerns the condition and nature upon which humans were created, rather than social practices and beliefs learned from family or society after birth.

The root ف ط ر occurs twenty times in nineteen Qur’anic verses. Its usages cover the creation of the heavens and earth, the creation of humans, humanity’s original nature, fissures and cracks, and the sky splitting in the great events of resurrection.

These senses are linguistically related, but should not all be restricted to one physical image. Originating creation, the sky splitting, and humanity’s original nature are distinct usages, each understood in its own context.

1. The root in early Arabic

Splitting and cracking

In early Arabic, فَطَرَ الشَّيْءَ means to split something.

فَطَرَ الإِنَاءَ means to split a vessel or cause it to crack.

فُطُور is used for fissures and cracks appearing in something.

Something previously continuous and whole develops a fissure.

For example, a pottery vessel may crack after an impact. The crack marks a break in its structure.

Arabic also uses فَطَرَ نَابُ الْبَعِيرِ for a camel’s canine tooth emerging from its gum.

In this image, the tooth appears by splitting the tissue covering it.

Splitting therefore does not always mean destruction; it can accompany emergence and appearance.

Splitting and appearing remain distinguishable concepts, however. Something can split without a new being emerging from it.

Pre-Islamic poetic evidence: Antarah ibn Shaddad

The renowned pre-Islamic poet Antarah ibn Shaddad describes his sword:

وَسَيْفِي كَالْعَقِيقَةِ وَهْوَ كِمْعِي
سِلَاحِي لَا أَفَلَّ وَلَا فُطَارَا

Wa-sayfī ka-l-ʿaqīqati wa-hwa kimʿī / silāḥī lā afalla wa-lā fuṭārā.

Translation: “My sword is like a flash of lightning and is my close companion; my weapon, neither notched nor cracked.”

Here فُطَار refers to a crack or fissure in the sword’s blade.

أَفَلّ likewise concerns notching and damage to its edge.

The poet describes the strength and soundness of his sword: neither its edge nor its blade is damaged.

This usage directly relates to فُطُور in al-Mulk 67:3.

Both concern a fissure or break in something’s structure.

The couplet is preserved in the collected poetry attributed to Antarah and in early literary and lexical sources. Part of the first hemistich varies, including وهو كمعي and فهو كمعي, but فطار appears in the familiar transmission.

This evidence supports the presence of the sense “crack” in language attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

It does not establish, however, that every use of faṭir for creation originally described splitting a pre-existing body.

Antarah’s verse supports the sense of cracking, rather than every detail of originating creation.

Beginning and bringing into being

Another major branch concerns beginning something not previously done or bringing something into being that did not previously exist in that form.

Lexical sources record فَطَرَ الْأَمْرَ for beginning or initiating an affair.

In creation contexts, فَاطِر likewise means originator or creator.

A well-known account attributed to Ibn Abbas in early lexical sources says he did not fully understand فاطر السماوات والأرض until hearing two Arabs dispute over a well.

One had said:

“أَنَا فَطَرْتُهَا”

That is, “I began digging it.”

Here faṭara concerns beginning an undertaking and bringing something about.

This account is not independently dated pre-Qur’anic poetry. Nor is the reported statement about creating the universe from nothing; it concerns beginning a well.

Its value lies in the lexical sense of beginning, rather than proving a complete theory of how the universe was created.

The relationship between splitting and originating creation

An imagistic connection can be seen between these semantic branches.

A tooth emerges from the gum as its covering tissue splits.

A plant may break through the soil as it emerges from the earth.

Such images can help explain the connection between splitting and appearing.

They do not mean that God necessarily split pre-existing matter to create the universe.

فاطر السماوات والأرض concerns bringing the heavens and earth into being.

How creation began must be studied through the relevant verses, rather than constructing a definite physical process from the material sense of splitting alone.

Thus splitting is one historical meaning, and originating creation is another recognized branch of the same family; neither should replace the other without contextual evidence.

2. Linguistic forms and Qur’anic derivatives

Major Arabic forms in this family include:

  • فَطَرَ: split; in another usage, began or brought into being.
  • فَطْر: splitting; in the creation branch, originating.
  • فَاطِر: originator or creator; in other usages, one who splits.
  • فِطْرَة: the original nature and constitution upon which a being was brought into existence.
  • فُطُور: fissures and cracks.
  • تَفَطَّرَ: cracked or developed splits.
  • اِنْفَطَرَ: split apart.
  • مُنْفَطِر: splitting or in a state of splitting.
  • فَطِير: in some usages, something fresh and not fully developed; in food usage, unleavened dough.
  • أَفْطَرَ: broke or ended a fast.
  • إِفْطَار: breaking a fast through eating or drinking.

Not all these forms occur in the Qur’an.

Forms present in the Qur’an

The twenty occurrences divide morphologically as follows:

  • Eight Form I verbs, فَطَرَ, with different inflections and pronoun suffixes.
  • Two Form V verbs, يَتَفَطَّرْنَ.
  • One Form VII verb, اِنْفَطَرَتْ.
  • Six active participles, فَاطِر.
  • One noun, فِطْرَة.
  • One noun, فُطُور.
  • One Form VII active participle, مُنْفَطِر.

Together they make twenty occurrences in nineteen verses, since al-Rum 30:30 contains two derivatives of the root.

The important structure of يَتَفَطَّرْنَ

In Maryam 19:90 and al-Shura 42:5, يَتَفَطَّرْنَ belongs to Form V.

It should not be treated as identical to the unaugmented فَطَرَ.

This verbal structure concerns cracking and splitting apart.

By contrast, فَطَرَ النَّاسَ in al-Rum 30:30 is a transitive Form I verb referring to bringing people into being.

A shared root does not erase differences in morphology and contextual meaning.

3. فَاطِرُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ: originator of the heavens and earth

Fatir 35:1

الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ فَاطِرِ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ جَاعِلِ الْمَلَائِكَةِ رُسُلًا أُولِي أَجْنِحَةٍ مَثْنَىٰ وَثُلَاثَ وَرُبَاعَ ۚ يَزِيدُ فِي الْخَلْقِ مَا يَشَاءُ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ قَدِيرٌ

Al-ḥamdu li-llāhi fāṭiri s-samāwāti wa-l-arḍi jāʿili l-malāʾikati rusulan ulī ajniḥatin mathnā wa-thulātha wa-rubāʿ. Yazīdu fī l-khalqi mā yashāʾ. Inna llāha ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīr.

Translation: “Praise belongs to The God, originator of the heavens and earth, who makes the angels Messengers with wings in twos, threes, and fours. He adds to creation what He wills; indeed, The God has power over all things.”

Faṭir describes God in this verse.

It is the active participle of faṭara, meaning originator or creator in this context.

The Qur’an does not merely say that God split existing heavens and earth; it identifies Him as their originator.

The verse continues by mentioning His making angels Messengers and adding to creation.

This connection indicates that the subject is God’s creative power and ordering of creation.

Distinguishing faṭir and jaʿil

The same verse gives two descriptions:

«فَاطِرِ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ»

Fāṭiri s-samāwāti wa-l-arḍ.

and

«جَاعِلِ الْمَلَائِكَةِ رُسُلًا»

Jāʿili l-malāʾikati rusulā.

Faṭir emphasizes originating the heavens and earth.

Jaʿil here describes placing the angels in the role of Messengers.

The two words therefore do not perform identical semantic roles.

God both originates beings and assigns His creatures particular positions, tasks, and order.

This difference does not establish that faṭara always means only creation from nothing, or that jaʿala always means only changing something already present.

Each root has a wider range of usages requiring separate study.

Six occurrences of faṭir

فاطر السماوات والأرض appears in six verses:

In al-Anam 6:14, God is identified as originator of the heavens and earth, alongside the question of taking a protector other than Him.

In Yusuf 12:101, Yusuf addresses God as originator of the heavens and earth in prayer, identifying Him as his protector in this world and the hereafter.

In Ibrahim 14:10, the Messengers invoke the originator of the heavens and earth in response to their peoples’ doubts about God.

In Fatir 35:1, the description accompanies praise of God at the surah’s opening.

In al-Zumar 39:46, it is linked to God’s knowledge of the hidden and manifest and His judgment between His servants.

In al-Shura 42:11, it is followed by the creation of mates and the declaration that nothing resembles Him.

These six usages show that faṭir serves more than an introduction to the beginning of creation: it also supports arguments about God’s oneness, protection, guidance, knowledge, and judgment.

The word faṭir itself does not mean all those attributes.

Originating the heavens and earth grounds the verses’ arguments; the other attributes are expressed by their separate clauses.

4. فَطَرَ: bringing humans and the world into being

Al-Anam 6:79

إِنِّي وَجَّهْتُ وَجْهِيَ لِلَّذِي فَطَرَ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ حَنِيفًا ۖ وَمَا أَنَا مِنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ

Innī wajjahtu wajhiya li-lladhī faṭara s-samāwāti wa-l-arḍa ḥanīfan wa-mā anā mina l-mushrikīn.

Translation: “I have turned my face, inclining to truth, toward the One who originated the heavens and earth; and I am not among those who associate partners.”

These are Ibrahim’s words.

In the preceding verses, he discusses a star, the moon, and the sun, considering their passing in relation to his Lord.

In verse 79, he then declares his orientation toward the originator of the heavens and earth.

Faṭara here concerns originating creation.

Ibrahim does not turn toward one part of the universe as his ultimate deity; he turns toward the originator of the universe itself.

Faṭr thus relates here to God’s oneness and the distinction between Creator and creature.

Human creation

In al-Anbiya 21:56, Ibrahim likewise invokes the Lord of the heavens and earth who originated them. Several other verses use faṭara for humans.

In Hud 11:51, Hud says that his reward rests with the One who originated him.

In Ta Ha 20:72, those who have believed invoke the One who created them when confronting Pharaoh.

Ya Sin 36:22 speaks of worshipping the One who originated the human being and to whom the return will be.

In al-Zukhruf 43:27, Ibrahim declares himself free of his people’s objects of worship, except the One who originated him and will guide him.

In these usages, فَطَرَنِي and فَطَرَنَا concern the creation of humans.

None means splitting the human body.

For example, الَّذِي فَطَرَنِي means “the One who originated me,” rather than “the One who split my body.”

Original creation and the possibility of re-creation

Al-Isra 17:51

أَوْ خَلْقًا مِمَّا يَكْبُرُ فِي صُدُورِكُمْ ۚ فَسَيَقُولُونَ مَنْ يُعِيدُنَا قُلِ الَّذِي فَطَرَكُمْ أَوَّلَ مَرَّةٍ ۚ فَسَيُنْغِضُونَ إِلَيْكَ رُءُوسَهُمْ وَيَقُولُونَ مَتَىٰ هُوَ ۖ قُلْ عَسَىٰ أَنْ يَكُونَ قَرِيبًا

Aw khalqan mimmā yakburu fī ṣudūrikum. Fa-sayaqūlūna man yuʿīdunā? Quli lladhī faṭarakum awwala marratin. Fa-sayunghiḍūna ilayka ruʾūsahum wa-yaqūlūna matā huwa? Qul ʿasā an yakūna qarībā.

Translation: “Or a creation that seems great in your hearts. Then they will say: Who will bring us back? Say: The One who originated you the first time. They will then shake their heads at you and say: When will it be? Say: Perhaps it is near.”

Here فَطَرَكُمْ is accompanied by أَوَّلَ مَرَّةٍ.

The combination emphasizes humanity’s first creation.

The verse argues that the One who originated you has the power to bring you back and raise you again.

Faṭara again means beginning and bringing into being.

It should not be restricted to a physical account of the body splitting at birth.

5. فِطْرَةَ اللَّهِ: humanity’s original nature

Al-Rum 30:30

فَأَقِمْ وَجْهَكَ لِلدِّينِ حَنِيفًا ۚ فِطْرَتَ اللَّهِ الَّتِي فَطَرَ النَّاسَ عَلَيْهَا ۚ لَا تَبْدِيلَ لِخَلْقِ اللَّهِ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ الدِّينُ الْقَيِّمُ وَلَٰكِنَّ أَكْثَرَ النَّاسِ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ

Fa-aqim wajhaka li-d-dīni ḥanīfā. Fiṭrata llāhi llatī faṭara n-nāsa ʿalayhā. Lā tabdīla li-khalqi llāh. Dhālika d-dīnu l-qayyimu wa-lākinna akthara n-nāsi lā yaʿlamūn.

Translation: “So set your face firmly toward the din, inclining to truth—the nature established by The God upon which He created people. There is no altering the creation of The God. That is the upright din, but most people do not know.”

This is the Qur’an’s only occurrence of the noun fiṭrah.

The same verse also contains the verb faṭara.

It thus directly connects original nature and human creation:

«فِطْرَتَ اللَّهِ»

Fiṭrata llāh.

and

«الَّتِي فَطَرَ النَّاسَ عَلَيْهَا»

Allatī faṭara n-nāsa ʿalayhā.

That is, the nature upon which God created people.

What does fitrah mean?

Morphologically, fiṭrah is a noun concerning the condition and manner of original creation.

For example, saying humans were created with a capacity to learn identifies a feature of their created constitution.

Which language they later learn, what knowledge they acquire, and what choices they make are separate matters.

A capacity for learning is not identical to a ready-made body of knowledge.

Fitrah likewise requires distinguishing humanity’s original nature from beliefs, behaviors, and habits learned after birth.

The verse does not say that all people know every rule, doctrinal detail, and item of religious knowledge from the outset.

It speaks instead of the relationship between humanity’s created nature and a din oriented toward truth.

Fitrah and hanif

The verse begins:

«فَأَقِمْ وَجْهَكَ لِلدِّينِ حَنِيفًا»

Fa-aqim wajhaka li-d-dīni ḥanīfā.

That is, set your face firmly toward the din, inclining to truth.

Hanif belongs to another root and, in the Qur’an, concerns turning away from association of partners and toward God’s oneness.

The nature established by God is mentioned immediately afterward.

This association shows that fitrah here is more than a name for human bodily structure.

The verse connects people’s religious orientation with their created nature.

Fitrah here therefore concerns a foundation in human creation compatible with turning toward God and the upright din.

This does not establish that all humans already know religion’s details without learning, thought, or choice.

Is fitrah the same as inherited religion?

No. Fitrah is attributed to human creation, rather than to family, ethnicity, or society.

One child may grow up in a family holding certain religious beliefs, another in a different setting.

These differences can affect their thinking, behavior, and choices.

But if fitrah concerns the original human constitution, it cannot be restricted to inherited membership in a religious group.

The verse also says:

«فَطَرَ النَّاسَ عَلَيْهَا»

Faṭara n-nāsa ʿalayhā.

That is, He created people upon it.

Al-nas encompasses humanity, rather than only the followers of a particular people or denomination.

Fitrah here is therefore a shared foundation in human creation.

Fitrah and human choice

If humans are created upon a nature established by God, why can they turn away from truth?

The answer should not be sought by denying human choice.

Al-Rum 30:29, immediately preceding the fitrah verse, discusses those who follow their desires without knowledge.

Al-Rum 30:31 and al-Rum 30:32 call for returning to God, taqwa, and avoiding association of partners and division.

This context shows that people can depart from the right path in conduct and orientation.

If possessing fitrah compelled everyone to believe and behave rightly, commands to return, practice taqwa, and reform would have little clear meaning.

Other verses likewise call people to understanding and choice.

Al-Insan 76:3 speaks of the way being shown and the possibility of gratitude or ingratitude.

Al-Shams 91:7, al-Shams 91:8, al-Shams 91:9, and al-Shams 91:10 concern the soul’s constitution, its awareness of wrongdoing and taqwa, and human responsibility for purifying or corrupting it.

These verses are compatible with understanding fitrah as an original human capacity and foundation. The word itself in al-Rum 30:30 does not define every detail of human psychology and ethics.

لَا تَبْدِيلَ لِخَلْقِ اللَّهِ

This is an important clause in the verse.

La tabdil concerns the absence of replacement or alteration in God’s creation.

Two recognized interpretive approaches exist.

One understands it as affirming the stability of humanity’s fundamental creation and God-given nature.

The other reads the statement within a command: do not replace the creation and nature established by God with human constructions.

Grammatically, the expression is negative. In Arabic, however, a declarative sentence in a commanding context can also convey a normative meaning.

Both approaches should therefore be examined against the verse’s context.

The clause must not become an unrestricted assertion that humans never change physically, psychologically, or behaviorally.

The Qur’an itself discusses growth, aging, illness, guidance, error, repentance, and changes in conduct.

لا تبديل لخلق الله should not be made to deny every observable change in human life.

Nor does it by itself authorize calling any social trait, cultural custom, or religious interpretation “unchangeable fitrah.”

Such a claim requires establishing whether the trait actually arises from human creation or from social learning and habit.

Fitrah and the upright din

The verse continues:

«ذَٰلِكَ الدِّينُ الْقَيِّمُ»

Dhālika d-dīnu l-qayyim.

That is, this is the upright din.

The connection concerns a shared foundation in human creation and a compatible religious orientation.

Fitrah should not replace revelation, thought, or personal responsibility.

People have the capacity to understand, yet also need reminders and guidance.

They may fail to use that capacity, imitate others, or stubbornly resist a truth they have recognized.

Fitrah here is therefore neither a complete body of ready-made knowledge nor a license to attribute every personal belief to God.

6. فُطُور: fissures and breaks in the sky’s structure

Al-Mulk 67:3

الَّذِي خَلَقَ سَبْعَ سَمَاوَاتٍ طِبَاقًا ۖ مَا تَرَىٰ فِي خَلْقِ الرَّحْمَٰنِ مِنْ تَفَاوُتٍ ۖ فَارْجِعِ الْبَصَرَ هَلْ تَرَىٰ مِنْ فُطُورٍ

Alladhī khalaqa sabʿa samāwātin ṭibāqā. Mā tarā fī khalqi r-raḥmāni min tafāwut. Fa-rjiʿi l-baṣara hal tarā min fuṭūr.

Translation: “The One who created seven heavens in layers. You see no disharmony in the creation of the Entirely Merciful. Look again: do you see any fissures?”

Fuṭur here means fissures or cracks.

It directly relates to the material sense of ف ط ر.

Tafawut also appears in this verse, but belongs to ف و ت and concerns structural disharmony or incongruity.

The verse places two aspects together:

The absence of disharmony in creation, and finding no fissures or breaks in it.

Is fuṭur the same as fitrah?

No.

Fuṭur here refers to fissures and cracks.

Fiṭrah in al-Rum 30:30 concerns humanity’s original nature and constitution.

They share a root, but differ in form and meaning.

هل ترى من فطور should not be translated as “Do you see any fitrah?”

Nor should fuṭur here be understood as breakfast or the ending of a fast.

In this construction it concerns fissures; usages involving food and breaking a fast belong to another branch of the family.

Does the verse deny every physical change in the sky?

No.

It speaks of the absence of disharmony and the fissures being sought within creation’s order.

The following verse, al-Mulk 67:4, invites repeated, more careful observation.

That should not be turned into a denial of all movement, change, or natural phenomena in the sky.

The Qur’an itself discusses the motion of celestial bodies and major cosmic events.

Verses concerning resurrection also describe the sky splitting.

The condition of creation in this verse and the events of the world’s end should each be understood in context.

7. يَتَفَطَّرْنَ: the heavens approaching a split

Maryam 19:90

تَكَادُ السَّمَاوَاتُ يَتَفَطَّرْنَ مِنْهُ وَتَنْشَقُّ الْأَرْضُ وَتَخِرُّ الْجِبَالُ هَدًّا

Takādu s-samāwātu yatafaṭṭarna minhu wa-tanshaqqu l-arḍu wa-takhirru l-jibālu haddā.

Translation: “The heavens almost split apart because of it, the earth cracks open, and the mountains collapse in ruins.”

Yatafaṭṭarna here describes the heavens splitting apart.

The sentence, however, begins with تَكَادُ.

This verb denotes approaching the occurrence of an event.

The verse therefore does not say that the heavens have already fully split at that moment; it conveys the gravity of a matter that almost brings about such a rupture.

What occasioned this image?

Maryam 19:91

أَنْ دَعَوْا لِلرَّحْمَٰنِ وَلَدًا

An daʿaw li-r-raḥmāni waladā.

Translation: “Because they attribute a child to the Entirely Merciful.”

This verse identifies the cause or subject of the preceding image.

Earlier verses discuss attributing a child to God.

Here the heavens almost splitting, the earth cracking, and the mountains collapsing emphasize the enormity of that claim.

The image should not be turned into a physical theory requiring measurable cracks in the sky whenever someone makes that claim.

The verse forcefully expresses the gravity and wrongness of attributing a child to God.

Nor does the image mean that God Himself suffers damage or incapacity.

The other occurrence of يَتَفَطَّرْنَ

Al-Shura 42:5

تَكَادُ السَّمَاوَاتُ يَتَفَطَّرْنَ مِنْ فَوْقِهِنَّ ۚ وَالْمَلَائِكَةُ يُسَبِّحُونَ بِحَمْدِ رَبِّهِمْ وَيَسْتَغْفِرُونَ لِمَنْ فِي الْأَرْضِ ۗ أَلَا إِنَّ اللَّهَ هُوَ الْغَفُورُ الرَّحِيمُ

Takādu s-samāwātu yatafaṭṭarna min fawqihinna wa-l-malāʾikatu yusabbiḥūna bi-ḥamdi rabbihim wa-yastaghfirūna li-man fī l-arḍ. Alā inna llāha huwa l-ghafūru r-raḥīm.

Translation: “The heavens almost split apart from above them; and the angels glorify their Lord with praise and seek forgiveness for those on earth. Surely The God is the Forgiving, the Especially Merciful.”

Yatafaṭṭarna again describes the heavens splitting.

The context, however, differs from Maryam 19:90.

Al-Shura concerns divine greatness, the angels’ glorification, and their seeking forgiveness for earth’s inhabitants.

Maryam links the image of splitting to attributing a child to God.

Although the verb and image share a semantic field, their cause and function must be examined separately in each verse.

These usages show that yatafaṭṭarna is not restricted in every context to one event or a single cause.

8. اِنْفَطَرَتْ and مُنْفَطِر: the sky splitting at resurrection

Al-Infitar 82:1

إِذَا السَّمَاءُ انْفَطَرَتْ

Idhā s-samāʾu nfaṭarat.

Translation: “When the sky splits apart.”

Infaṭarat belongs to Form VII.

This construction attributes the splitting to the sky itself.

The following verses describe the stars scattering, the seas being transformed, and the graves being overturned.

The splitting here is therefore part of the great events of resurrection.

Distinguishing faṭara and infaṭara

In creation contexts, faṭara is transitive, as in فَطَرَ النَّاسَ.

Infaṭarat in al-Infitar 82:1 instead describes the sky itself splitting.

One concerns originating a being, the other the occurrence of a split.

Translation must preserve this distinction.

Munfaṭir in al-Muzzammil

Al-Muzzammil 73:18

السَّمَاءُ مُنْفَطِرٌ بِهِ ۚ كَانَ وَعْدُهُ مَفْعُولًا

As-samāʾu munfaṭirun bihi. Kāna waʿduhu mafʿūlā.

Translation: “The sky will split because of it; His promise is to be fulfilled.”

Munfaṭir is a Form VII active participle.

The sky’s splitting is connected to the event described in the preceding verse.

Al-Muzzammil 73:17 speaks of a day whose severity turns children gray-haired.

The pronoun in بِهِ thus relates to that day and the terror and gravity of its event.

Munfaṭir itself does not mean “the Day of Resurrection”; it expresses splitting.

Why is munfaṭir masculine?

Al-sama is usually feminine in Arabic.

In al-Infitar 82:1, the verb is feminine:

«اِنْفَطَرَتْ»

Infaṭarat.

In al-Muzzammil 73:18, however, مُنْفَطِرٌ is masculine.

Arabic grammarians offer several explanations, including masculine agreement in some uses of nouns with nonbiological feminine gender, or considering a broader sense such as a roof or a thing that splits.

The most precise grammatical explanation is disputed.

One possibility should therefore not be presented without examination as the sole certain cause.

For the root’s meaning, what matters is that munfaṭir belongs to the family of splitting and describes the sky splitting here.

9. فُطْر and إِفْطَار: ending a fast

Arabic أَفْطَرَ الصَّائِمُ means that the fasting person ended the fast.

Ifṭar likewise concerns eating or drinking after abstaining from them.

This usage is established in lexical sources.

A semantic connection can be seen between breaking a continuous period of abstention and ending it.

Without sufficient historical evidence, however, one should not confidently claim that this precise image originally produced the term.

More importantly, ifṭar and afṭara meaning the ending of a fast do not occur in the Qur’an.

Al-Baqarah 2:187 permits eating and drinking until dawn becomes distinct, then commands completing the fast until night.

It does not use afṭara for this instruction.

The name “Eid al-Fitr” likewise does not occur in the Qur’anic text.

A Qur’anic index of ف ط ر must not count expressions familiar from later religious culture as derivatives present in verses.

Nor should the resemblance between fuṭur in al-Mulk 67:3 and its food-related usage cause confusion.

In al-Mulk 67:3, it means fissures, rather than breakfast or breaking a fast.

10. Comparison with neighboring roots

ف ط ر (فَطَرَ) and خ ل ق (خَلَقَ)

The Qur’an uses khalaqa for creating, originating, and forming beings.

In creation contexts, faṭara highlights initial origination.

They share a semantic field, but not every usage.

Al-Rum 30:30 places فطر الناس and خلق الله in the same verse.

This association shows the concepts’ proximity.

Elsewhere, however, ف ط ر also conveys splitting, which cannot be transferred to every occurrence of khalaqa.

ف ط ر (فَطَرَ) and ب د ع (أَبْدَعَ)

The root ب د ع concerns bringing about something new and unprecedented.

Al-Baqarah 2:117 identifies God as بَدِيعُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ.

Verses using faṭir emphasize origination and bringing into being.

These descriptions are close, but every feature of ب د ع should not be derived from ف ط ر alone.

Neither should determine physical details of the universe’s beginning without examination of other verses.

ف ط ر (فَطَرَ) and ب ر أ (بَرَأَ)

The Qur’anic root ب ر أ also concerns originating and creating.

Al-Hashr 59:24 describes God as الْخَالِقُ الْبَارِئُ الْمُصَوِّرُ.

These words can emphasize different aspects of creation.

They should not be assigned a mandatory technical sequence—for example, faṭr necessarily being stage one, barʾ stage two, and shaping stage three of one physical process.

The roots themselves do not establish such a precise sequence.

ف ط ر (فَطَرَ) and ف ت ق (فَتَقَ)

Fatq concerns opening or separating something previously joined.

Al-Anbiya 21:30 discusses the heavens and earth being joined and then separated.

The splitting branch of ف ط ر is close to fatq.

Yet Qur’anic فاطر السماوات والأرض means the originator of the heavens and earth.

The roots’ proximity does not restrict every occurrence of faṭr to the event described in al-Anbiya 21:30.

ف ط ر (فَطَرَ) and ش ق ق (شَقَّ)

Shaqq concerns splitting and cleaving apart.

This is very close to the material branch of faṭr.

Maryam 19:90 uses يَتَفَطَّرْنَ for the heavens and تَنْشَقُّ for the earth in the same verse.

Their association demonstrates semantic proximity.

Faṭr also has the branches of originating creation and fitrah.

Not every act of splitting is called faṭr, nor does every use of faṭr mean physical splitting.

ف ط ر (فَطَرَ) and س و ي (سَوَّىٰ)

Taswiyah concerns ordering, proportioning, and placing something in an organized structure.

In human contexts, faṭr emphasizes original creation.

The Qur’an describes human creation through various words and expressions.

Their differences do not establish one definite sequence for every biological stage of human creation.

ف ط ر (فَطَرَ) and ف ت ر (فَتَرَ)

These roots resemble one another in writing but are independent.

ف ت ر concerns slackening, diminished intensity, and intervals between events.

In al-Maidah 5:19, فترة من الرسل refers to an interval between Messengers.

Qur’anic ف ط ر instead concerns creation, original nature, and splitting.

Despite their visual resemblance, fatrah and fiṭrah have different roots and meanings.

11. Challenges in understanding and translation

The first error is restricting every occurrence of faṭr to splitting.

In فاطر السماوات والأرض, the meaning of originator is clear.

Rendering it “splitter of the heavens and earth” without regard to context obscures creation.

A second error equates fitrah with a ready-made set of beliefs and rules.

Al-Rum 30:30 speaks of the nature upon which humanity was created.

It does not say every newborn knows every doctrinal and religious detail.

A third error turns fitrah into a society’s inherited religion.

فطر الناس عليها concerns people, rather than only a particular ethnicity or denomination.

A fourth error uses لا تبديل لخلق الله to deny all human bodily and psychological change.

That conclusion conflicts with verses about growth, aging, choice, learning, and behavioral change.

A fifth error identifies fuṭur with fitrah or ifṭar.

They belong to one family but differ in meaning and form.

A sixth error constructs a definite theory of the universe’s physical origin solely from the sense of splitting.

Faṭr can mean splitting, but faṭir in creation contexts means originator.

A seventh error equates the sky splitting at resurrection with disharmony in present creation.

Al-Mulk 67:3 concerns the absence of fissures in the observed structure; al-Infitar 82:1 announces another, future event.

Each must be understood in its own time and context.

Finally, fitrah must not be taken to exempt humans from responsibility for choosing.

Possessing an original foundation does not compel a particular behavior.

The Qur’an calls people to understanding, reflection, return, reform, and choosing the right path.

12. Translation proposals for Forqan

For creation-related فَطَرَ, suitable renderings include “originated,” “created,” and, in an appropriate context, “brought into being for the first time.”

For فَاطِر, “originator” or “initiator of creation” is precise.

For فِطْرَة, suitable options include “original nature,” “created nature,” or “original constitution.”

For فُطُور, use “fissures,” “cracks,” or “breaks.”

يَتَفَطَّرْنَ means “split apart” or “develop cracks”; in تَكَادُ السَّمَاوَاتُ يَتَفَطَّرْنَ, “almost” comes from تَكَادُ, rather than from يَتَفَطَّرْنَ alone.

اِنْفَطَرَتْ can be rendered “split apart” or, according to context, “splits apart.”

For مُنْفَطِر, “splitting” is suitable, or “will split apart” in al-Muzzammil’s context.

Principal examples

Fatir 35:1

«فَاطِرِ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ»

Fāṭiri s-samāwāti wa-l-arḍ.

Proposal: “Originator of the heavens and earth.”

Al-Isra 17:51

«الَّذِي فَطَرَكُمْ أَوَّلَ مَرَّةٍ»

Alladhī faṭarakum awwala marrah.

Proposal: “The One who originated you the first time.”

Al-Rum 30:30

«فِطْرَتَ اللَّهِ الَّتِي فَطَرَ النَّاسَ عَلَيْهَا»

Fiṭrata llāhi llatī faṭara n-nāsa ʿalayhā.

Proposal: “The nature established by The God upon which He created people.”

Al-Mulk 67:3

«هَلْ تَرَىٰ مِنْ فُطُورٍ»

Hal tarā min fuṭūr?

Proposal: “Do you see any fissures?”

«تَكَادُ السَّمَاوَاتُ يَتَفَطَّرْنَ مِنْهُ»

Takādu s-samāwātu yatafaṭṭarna minh.

Proposal: “The heavens almost split apart because of it.”

Al-Infitar 82:1

«إِذَا السَّمَاءُ انْفَطَرَتْ»

Idhā s-samāʾu nfaṭarat.

Proposal: “When the sky splits apart.”

Brief root meaning and contextual meaning

Brief root meaning: Splitting and opening; beginning and bringing into being; original nature and constitution.

Meaning in creation verses: Originating humanity or the heavens and earth.

Meaning in al-Rum 30:30: The original nature upon which God created people, connected to a religious orientation toward truth.

Meaning in al-Mulk 67:3: Fissures and breaks in the sky’s structure.

Meaning in resurrection verses: The sky splitting apart.

The general root meaning should be distinguished from the meaning of each derivative in its verse, without transferring every branch to every occurrence.

13. Conclusion

Early Arabic ف ط ر has two major branches: splitting and originating.

Antarah ibn Shaddad’s pre-Islamic poetic evidence uses fuṭar for cracks in a sword. Lexical accounts also record beginning and bringing into being for this family.

The root occurs twenty times in nineteen Qur’anic verses.

In creation verses, faṭir and faṭara concern God’s origination and creation.

Fitrah in al-Rum 30:30 expresses humanity’s original nature in connection with a din oriented toward truth.

Fuṭur in al-Mulk 67:3 concerns fissures and breaks.

Yatafaṭṭarna, infaṭarat, and munfaṭir describe the sky splitting in different contexts.

The family concerns splitting and opening, and, in the creation branch, beginning and bringing into being. Fitrah names the original nature and condition upon which a being was created.

These senses are related, but their distinctions are real.

Creating humans, the sky splitting, and a vessel cracking are not one event, just as human fitrah does not mean a physical fissure in humans.

The Qur’an connects human creation and the upright din through fitrah. This relationship should not be turned into inherited beliefs, ready-made knowledge, or behavioral determinism.

Humanity is created with a nature related to orientation toward truth and God; people nevertheless remain responsible for understanding, choosing, and reforming their conduct.

Understanding this root therefore requires distinguishing the historical sense of splitting, the creative sense of originating, original nature, and usages describing the sky splitting.

Preserving these boundaries prevents unsupported scientific and doctrinal interpretations being imposed upon the word, while clarifying the actual connection between faṭr, faṭir, and fitrah in Qur’anic language.
