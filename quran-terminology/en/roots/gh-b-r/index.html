Remaining, being left behind, and dustiness

The Arabic root gh–b–r has two established semantic branches: something or someone remaining after others have passed, departed, or separated; and dust, a dusty colour, and the effect of dust on surfaces or faces.

Both are known in early Arabic and occur in the Quran. Sharing a root does not itself establish that every meaning developed from one original material image.

Ghābir describes someone or something remaining, although lexical sources also record “past.” In material uses, ghubār and ghabarah refer to dust and the colour or layer that settles on something and changes its appearance.

The Quran uses the first branch in Lot's story and his wife's fate, and the second to describe faces on the Day of Resurrection.

1. Forms examined and early Arabic usage

The family's principal forms include:

  • Ghabara: remained, persisted, or passed.
  • Ghābir: remaining; in some lexical uses, past.
  • Ghubr and ghubbar: a remainder, such as milk or part of a period.
  • Ghubūr: staying and remaining.
  • Ghubār: airborne dust.
  • Ghabarah: dust, its trace, or a dusty layer.
  • Ghubrah: a dusty, greyish colour.
  • Aghbar: dusty or dust-coloured.
  • Ghabrāʾ: the earth or something dusty in colour.

Only al-ghābirīn and ghabaratun occur in the Quran. Other forms help establish the Arabic semantic field but should not be presented as Quranic derivatives.

First witness: milk remaining in pastoral life

A verse attributed to the pre-Islamic poet al-Ḥārith ibn Ḥillizah reads:

لَا تَكْسَعِ الشَّوْلَ بِأَغْبَارِهَا

lā taksaʿi al-shawla bi-aghbārihā

إِنَّكَ لَا تَدْرِي مَنِ النَّاتِجُ

innaka lā tadrī mani al-nātiju

Interpretive translation: “Do not leave the remaining milk in the she-camels' udders for future benefit; you do not know who will benefit when they give birth.” The lexical explanations differ concerning the precise action denoted by taksaʿ.

Aghbār here means milk remaining in the camels' udders. The remaining branch therefore had a concrete application in herders' everyday life, beyond people and time.

Something has been used or its main portion has gone, but a quantity remains.

Lexical sources transmit this verse under al-Ḥārith ibn Ḥillizah's name. It is evidence for the recorded meaning of a remainder; the poet's name alone does not independently establish the precise date or transmission history of this verse.

Second witness: ghabrāʾ in Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd's poetry

The famous Muʿallaqah of the pre-Islamic poet Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd includes:

رَأَيْتُ بَنِي غَبْرَاءَ لَا يُنْكِرُونَنِي

raʾaytu banī ghabrāʾa lā yunkirūnanī

وَلَا أَهْلُ هَذَاكَ الطِّرَافِ الْمُمَدَّدِ

wa-lā ahlu hādhāka al-ṭirāfi al-mumaddadi

Translation: “I found that the sons of the dusty earth do not disown me, nor do the people of that great, extended tent.”

Banū ghabrāʾ is commonly explained here as poor people associated with the dusty earth. Other explanations include travellers. The owners of the great tent stand opposite them, suggesting prosperity.

Here ghabrāʾ relates to the earth, soil, and its dusty colour. This branch therefore appears in evidence attributed to the pre-Islamic period.

These examples record two semantic fields in poetry attributed to pre-Islamic poets: a remainder and the earth or dust. Dating each individual witness requires evidence beyond the poet's name.

2. The root's semantic cores

A. Remaining after others leave or pass

This branch centres on something remaining while another part passes or departs.

Milk left in an animal's udder can be called ghubr. Classical Arabic also uses ghubr al-layl for the remaining part of the night.

Remaining does not necessarily mean being rescued, staying unharmed, or enjoying a desirable condition. Someone may remain exposed to a danger others have escaped.

This distinction is decisive for the Quranic al-ghābirīn.

B. Dust and its visible effect

The second branch concerns dust, dusty colour, and the trace that settles on something.

Ghubār generally means particles of soil dispersed in the air or stirred up by movement. Ghabarah can mean dust itself or its visible layer or trace. Ghubrah also describes a dusty, darkened colour.

These terms are close, but differences of form and usage should be preserved.

One suggested connection is that dust remains in the air after people, animals, or caravans move, just as a ghābir remains after others leave.

This image makes the relationship understandable but does not establish which meaning historically developed from the other.

C. Why can ghābir mean “past”?

Arabic lexical sources classify ghābir among words that can express the opposite senses “remaining” and “past.”

These appear contrary: what remains is still present; what has passed is no longer in the present.

The sentence and context determine each use. One construction may refer to time still remaining, another to a period already elapsed.

Recording both senses does not establish when they arose or their order of development. Dating the use “past” requires independently datable evidence.

The Quranic al-ghābirīn does not display this ambiguity: all seven contexts support remaining.

3. Al-ghābirīn in Lot's story

Al-ghābirīn is the sound masculine plural of the active participle ghābir. It occurs seven times in the Quran, always concerning Lot's story and his wife.

Remaining contrasted with being rescued

Al-Aʿrāf 7:83

فَأَنجَيْنَٰهُ وَأَهْلَهُۥٓ إِلَّا ٱمْرَأَتَهُۥ كَانَتْ مِنَ ٱلْغَٰبِرِينَ

fa-anjaynāhu wa-ahlahu illā imraʾatahu kānat mina al-ghābirīn

Al-Aʿrāf 7:83

“So We rescued him and his family, except his wife; she was one of those who remained.”

Two conditions are contrasted: Lot and the family rescued from danger, and his wife among those remaining.

Al-ghābirīn does not itself mean criminals, the destroyed, or those punished. Its semantic core is remaining.

Where, and in what condition, did they remain?

Context supplies the answer. Lot and his companions were rescued from that community, while his wife remained among those upon whom punishment fell. Al-Aʿrāf 7:84 describes the punishment and the criminals' fate.

In this story, al-ghābirīn are those who did not separate from the community and shared its fate.

Translating only “those destroyed” substitutes the outcome for the root's meaning. “Those who remained” is more precise, while contextual reflection explains that remaining did not bring rescue.

Qaddarnā and the determination of remaining

Al-Ḥijr 15:60

إِلَّا ٱمْرَأَتَهُۥ قَدَّرْنَآ إِنَّهَا لَمِنَ ٱلْغَٰبِرِينَ

illā imraʾatahu qaddarnā innahā la-mina al-ghābirīn

Al-Ḥijr 15:60

“Except his wife; We have determined that she is indeed one of those who remain.”

Qaddarnā adds determination or ordaining to the sentence, while al-ghābirīn continues to denote those remaining.

Determination, condemnation, or destruction should therefore not be transferred into the root gh–b–r itself.

Al-Ḥijr 15:65 also commands Lot and his companions to depart at night, contrasting those rescued with the person who will remain.

The expression fī al-ghābirīn

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:171

إِلَّا عَجُوزًا فِى ٱلْغَٰبِرِينَ

illā ʿajūzan fī al-ghābirīn

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:171

“Except an old woman among those who remained.”

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:172

ثُمَّ دَمَّرْنَا ٱلْـَٔاخَرِينَ

thumma dammarnā al-ākharīn

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:172

“Then We destroyed the others.”

These verses clearly distinguish the word's meaning from the people's eventual fate.

An old woman is first excepted from those rescued and placed among al-ghābirīn. The Quran then states the destruction of the others.

ʿAjūz describes the woman's age; al-ghābirīn identifies the group among whom she remained. Old age is not part of ghābir's meaning.

Fī al-ghābirīn differs structurally from min al-ghābirīn. Fī emphasizes being among a group, while min expresses membership. Both describe the same situation here, but translation should preserve the different prepositions.

Other occurrences of al-ghābirīn

Al-Naml 27:57 describes the rescue of Lot and his family and states his wife's position through qaddarnāhā mina al-ghābirīn.

In Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:32, the divine messengers tell Abraham that Lot and his family will be rescued, except his wife.

In Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:33, the message is addressed directly to Lot: he and his family will be rescued, except his wife, who is among those remaining.

Al-Ṣāffāt 37:135 repeats illā ʿajūzan fī al-ghābirīn; Al-Ṣāffāt 37:136 states the destruction of the others.

The meaning remains consistent. Differences concern presentation, addressee, narrative time, and sentence structure.

Why the masculine plural?

Lot's wife is a woman, yet the Quran places her min al-ghābirīn or fī al-ghābirīn, using a masculine plural.

The term describes a group of which she is a member, rather than a feminine singular description of her alone.

Arabic masculine plurals can designate groups containing men and women. The form therefore does not establish that all members are male.

Nor does it determine their precise number, identity, or composition.

4. Ghabarah describing faces at the Resurrection

The second branch occurs in ʿAbasa.

ʿAbasa 80:40

وَوُجُوهٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ عَلَيْهَا غَبَرَةٌ

wa-wujūhun yawmaʾidhin ʿalayhā ghabarah

ʿAbasa 80:40

“And faces that day will be dust-covered.”

ʿAbasa 80:41

تَرْهَقُهَا قَتَرَةٌ

tarhaquhā qatarah

ʿAbasa 80:41

“Darkness will cover them.”

Ghabarah is a noun, not an active participle. It denotes dust or a dust-like effect on the face.

ʿAlayhā ghabaratun depicts a face bearing dust or a dusty colour.

The following verse uses qatarah, a different root, concerning darkness covering the face.

Together the words develop the description: ghabarah evokes dustiness, while qatarah highlights enveloping darkness.

In contrast, ʿAbasa 80:38 and ʿAbasa 80:39 describe radiant, laughing, joyful faces. The passage depicts two different human conditions at the Resurrection.

This contrast makes dustiness an unfavourable image here. That evaluation comes from context: the root does not always mean condemnation or punishment.

Without evidence, the dust should not be restricted to a specific physical substance, smoke, or natural phenomenon. The Quran depicts dusty, darkened faces but does not explain their material character at the Resurrection.

5. Comparison with nearby roots

Gh–B–R and B–Q–Y: being left behind and remaining

B–q–y broadly expresses remaining and enduring, whether of property, time, beings, or long-lasting matters.

Ghābir also involves remaining, but in Lot's Quranic story it accompanies the departure of those rescued and the remaining of the other group.

The difference here comes from context, not an absolute rule that gh–b–r always signifies undesirable remaining and b–q–y desirable persistence.

Gh–B–R and Kh–L–F: remaining and being behind

Kh–l–f concerns being behind or after another, succession, and falling behind.

Remaining behind others sometimes approaches ghābir, but kh–l–f has a broader range involving position, sequence, and succession.

Ghābir in Lot's story describes those remaining without necessarily implying succession or a spatial position behind someone.

Gh–B–R and T–R–K: remaining and leaving

T–r–k concerns abandoning, leaving, or leaving something behind.

Al-ʿAnkabūt 29:35 speaks of leaving a clear sign from that community. This differs from al-ghābirīn in the same story.

T–r–k expresses an act resulting in something being left, whereas ghābir describes the condition of someone remaining.

Gh–B–R and Q–T–R: dustiness and darkness

The roots occur together in ʿAbasa 80:40 and ʿAbasa 80:41.

Ghabarah evokes dust or dusty colour on the face, while qatarah denotes darkness covering it in the same context.

Both contribute to one description without being exact lexical synonyms.

Gh–B–R and Gh–R–B: two distinct roots

Despite their similar letters, gh–b–r and gh–r–b are independent roots.

Quranic gh–r–b concerns such meanings as sunset and the west. Shared images of departure or passing do not make them one root.

6. Challenges in understanding and translation

First: do not confuse those remaining with survivors

The Persian term for someone left behind can sometimes mean a survivor of an accident.

Al-ghābirīn in Lot's story, however, refers to those who remained and were not rescued.

An expression meaning “those who remained” is therefore clearer. A word also meaning “survivors” is acceptable only if the reader will not mistake it for those saved.

Second: do not make destruction part of ghābir's meaning

Ghābir means someone remaining; destruction is the group's contextual outcome.

“Those destroyed” may communicate the story's outcome, but erases the distinction between the word's meaning and the consequence stated in the verses.

This distinction is essential for understanding the root independently.

Third: do not impose the sense “past” on the Quranic use

Arabic records ghābir for past time and uses it in expressions about elapsed periods.

In Lot's story, however, the contrast between the group's rescue and others remaining clarifies the intended sense.

The recorded sense “past” does not justify translating al-ghābirīn here as predecessors, ancestors, or people of ancient times.

Fourth: distinguish dust from all its effects

Dust may reduce visibility, change a colour, or indicate dryness and moving soil. These consequences do not all belong to ghabarah's lexical definition.

In ʿAbasa, darkness, the sorrowful image, and the contrast with joyful faces arise from context. They are not independent, constant meanings of the root.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

The following equivalents reflect form and context:

Al-ghābirīn: “those who remained”; where clarification is needed, “those left remaining.”

For min al-ghābirīn, “one of those who remained” is suitable; for fī al-ghābirīn, “among those who remained” better preserves the construction.

Ghabaratun: “dust” or “dustiness.” In ʿAbasa 80:40, “faces that day will be dust-covered” is natural and appropriate.

A lexical note may explain a layer or trace of dust on the face. Adding shame, disgrace, or condemnation to the translation exceeds the word's own meaning.

For non-Quranic uses, ghubr means a remainder, ghubār dust, aghbar dusty, and ghabrāʾ dust-coloured or the earth, depending on context.

Conclusion

Gh–b–r has two distinguishable Arabic branches: remaining or being left behind, and dust or dusty colour. Both are attested in early language and used in the Quran.

Ghābir describes someone or something remaining. Although Arabic also records “past,” remaining suits every Quranic occurrence.

In Lot's story, al-ghābirīn contrast with those rescued. His wife remained among them and shared the community's fate. Remaining is the lexical meaning; destruction is the outcome stated elsewhere in the passage.

Ghabarah in ʿAbasa depicts dusty faces that darkness covers in the following verse. The unfavourable significance comes from the Resurrection context and the contrast with radiant, joyful faces.

A fabricated single meaning should not reduce remaining, passing time, and dust to a vague definition. Accuracy requires preserving the established branches and identifying each derivative's contribution to the verse's structure and context.
