From the colour red to the donkey: a useful animal in life and Qur’anic similes

The Arabic root ح م ر has two familiar semantic fields: red and related colour characteristics, and حِمَار, the donkey, an animal used for riding, carrying loads, and transport. حُمْرَة denotes redness; أَحْمَر describes something red; and حِمَار names the animal whose plural forms include حَمِير and حُمُر.

These branches share their root consonants, but their historical relationship is not fully clear. Some lexicographers have connected حِمَار with the animal’s colouring, while others allow redness and the animal’s name to be separate principles. Shared consonants alone therefore do not establish that the donkey was originally named for being red.

ح م ر occurs six times in the Qur’an. One occurrence of حُمْرٌ concerns red colouring in mountains. The other five name donkeys, either actual animals or elements in similes. The animal is sometimes presented among useful provisions for humanity; its voice occurs in advice about measured conduct; and carrying books or fleeing provides images for criticizing human behaviour.

Qur’anic forms: حِمَارِكَ in Al-Baqarah 2:259, الْحَمِيرَ in Al-Naḥl 16:8, الْحَمِيرِ in Luqmān 31:19, حُمْرٌ in Fāṭir 35:27, الْحِمَارِ in Al-Jumuʿah 62:5, and حُمُرٌ in Al-Muddaththir 74:50.

Arabic also records حُمْرَة, أَحْمَر, حَمْرَاء, احْمَرَّ, احْمارَّ, حَمَّرَ, حَمِير, حُمُر, أَحْمِرَة, حِمَارَة, and أَتَان. أَتَان names a female donkey, however, and does not share the root ح م ر.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: six occurrences, five animal nouns and one colour adjective

1. Semantic Principles and Uses in Early Arabic

حُمْرَة: the colour red

حُمْرَة names the colour red. أَحْمَر is masculine and حَمْرَاء feminine; the adjectival plural of أَحْمَر in colour usage is حُمْر.

Al-ʿAyn presents redness as a familiar colour and records احْمَرَّ and احْمارَّ for becoming red.

Lisān al-ʿArab also records redness in animals, clothing, and other things capable of bearing colour.

This use is not confined to a particularly bright red. Arabic, like other languages, can include a range of neighbouring shades within red.

A red stone, for example, may differ in shade from a red fruit or cloth, yet all can belong within the red colour field.

In Fāṭir 35:27, حُمْرٌ is followed by مُخْتَلِفٌ أَلْوَانُهَا, expressing variation in colours.

احْمَرَّ and احْمارَّ: becoming red

احْمَرَّ الشَّيْءُ means that something became red.

احْمارَّ is also recorded for becoming red.

Lisān al-ʿArab reports a distinction: احْمَرَّ for stable redness and احْمارَّ for transient redness. Al-Jawharī’s Al-Ṣiḥāḥ, however, presents them as equivalent; that distinction should therefore not be attributed to him without qualification.

A material’s natural redness, for example, may be lasting, while environmental conditions can temporarily make something red.

The distinction is disputed and cannot be an exceptionless rule for determining a colour’s duration from verb pattern alone. Some morphological explanations regard the forms as equivalent or the longer form as intensive.

Sharḥ Durrat al-Ghawwāṣ: disagreement concerning iḥmarra and iḥmārra

حَمَّرَ الشَّيْءَ also means making something red or colouring it red.

These verbs do not occur in the Qur’an. The sole Qur’anic use of the colour branch is the adjectival حُمْرٌ.

Al-Ṣiḥāḥ: equivalent colour verbs and the adjectival plural

Extended uses of redness

Arabic also uses red in figurative expressions.

مَوْتٌ أَحْمَرُ is recorded for a harsh or terrible death.

سَنَةٌ حَمْرَاءُ can describe a difficult year marked by famine.

احْمَرَّ الْبَأْسُ also occurs for battle or fighting becoming intense.

Redness in these expressions characterizes intensity, danger, or hardship.

They do not make red inherently a sign of evil or violence.

Other expressions use red for beauty, ornaments, and desirable things. Its positive or negative associations therefore depend on the particular context and usage.

الأَحْمَرَانِ sometimes denotes gold and saffron, and elsewhere meat and wine.

Such names relate to colour or cultural and social value, but أَحْمَر in its basic sense does not mean gold, meat, or wine.

Nor do these expressions establish that Fāṭir 35:27 concerns precious metals or food. The verse describes actual mountain colours.

حِمَار: the donkey

حِمَار names a familiar animal long used for riding, carrying loads, and travelling along different routes.

Al-ʿAyn includes both domestic and wild asses within this family’s uses.

أَتَان also names the female donkey.

Lexical sources record the plural forms أَحْمِرَة, حَمِير, حُمُر, and حُمُرَات.

Their usage can differ, but all belong within the naming of this animal.

Domestic donkeys were used for loads, riding, and daily work.

Wild asses inhabit open plains and deserts; early Arabic poetry sometimes describes their speed, flight, and movement in groups.

حِمَار therefore does not always name a domestic animal. Some contexts concern a wild ass.

This matters in Al-Muddaththir 74:50, whose image concerns animals fleeing danger.

Was حِمَار named after the colour red?

Ibn Fāris initially states in Maqāyīs al-Lughah that, in his view, ح م ر has one principle: redness.

He immediately allows another possibility, however: two independent principles, one the colour red and the other the donkey’s name.

Explaining the latter, he identifies حِمَار as a familiar animal and supplies its plurals.

His caution matters.

Some donkeys may be reddish or reddish brown, but donkeys do not all have one colour.

The existence of حِمَار in Arabic does not itself show that its earliest speakers necessarily formed the name from a description of red colouring.

Such a relationship requires independent historical and etymological evidence.

This study therefore treats redness and the animal’s name as two established branches of one lexical family, without asserting a certain shared historical origin.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: one principle or two

Other uses of حِمَار

Arabic also applies حِمَار to certain manufactured objects.

Al-ʿAyn mentions a piece of wood or implement used by a metal polisher and called حِمَار.

Lexical sources also use it for part of a structure or barrier around a water trough.

حِمَارُ قَبَّانَ names a very small creature, comparable in a Persian explanation to certain small animals with many legs.

Such naming can arise from resemblance in shape, function, or behaviour; a shared name does not make all these objects and creatures identical.

In all five Qur’anic animal usages, حِمَار concerns the actual animal. Even in similes, the familiar animal supplies the image.

Al-ʿAyn and Lisān al-ʿArab: the animal, plurals, and names of objects

2. Early Witnesses: Redness and Donkeys in Arab Life

Redness in poetry attributed to Al-Aʿshā

A poem attributed to Al-Aʿshā reads:

إِنَّ الْأَحَامِرَةَ الثَّلَاثَةَ أَهْلَكَتْ
مَالِي وَكُنْتُ بِهِنَّ قِدْمًا مُولَعَا

inna al-aḥāmirata al-thalāthata ahlakat / mālī wa-kuntu bihinna qidman mūlaʿā

Meaning: Those three things named red have ruined my wealth; I have long been devoted to them.

The continuation identifies wine, fatty meat, and saffron as the three things.

أَحَامِرَة functions here as a name for things, rather than merely an adjectival plural describing their appearance.

Ibn Fāris uses this witness to explain the distinction between أَحَامِرَة and the adjectival plural حُمْر.

For colour directly, the plural of أَحْمَر, red, is حُمْر: the form found in Fāṭir 35:27.

The verse is transmitted in dictionaries and supplementary material attributed to Al-Aʿshā. Attribution and wording must nevertheless be checked against reliable editions; the poet’s name alone does not establish when each passage was composed.

Transmission of poetry attributed to Al-Aʿshā: aḥāmirah

Donkeys and hardship in Arabic poetry

A verse transmitted in Maqāyīs al-Lughah and Al-Qālī’s Amālī reads:

إِذَا غَرَّدَ الْمُكَّاءُ فِي غَيْرِ رَوْضَةٍ
فَوَيْلٌ لِأَهْلِ الشَّاءِ وَالْحُمُرَاتِ

idhā gharrada al-mukkāʾu fī ghayri rawḍatin / fa-waylun li-ahli al-shāʾi wa-l-ḥumurāti

Meaning: If the mukkāʾ bird sings somewhere other than a meadow, woe to the owners of sheep and donkeys.

مُكَّاء names a bird whose song the verse’s commentators associate with green meadows and plentiful pasture. This literary explanation does not replace independent examination of the bird’s habitat.

The commentary explains its singing outside a meadow as a sign of scarce pasture and difficulty finding grass.

Simṭ al-Laʾālī explains the difficulty as affecting sheep and donkey owners who cannot travel as far as camel herders to seek pasture and rainfall. This explains the livelihood situation in the poem, rather than establishing a general biological rule for all these animals.

حُمُرَات is a plural of the donkey’s name and concerns animals important for people’s work and transport.

The exact date and attribution of this verse are unclear in the sources; it must not confidently be assigned to the pre-Islamic period.

It nevertheless witnesses the donkey’s established name and its connection with livelihood and pasture in Arabic literary usage.

Simṭ al-Laʾālī: the mukkāʾ witness and scarce pasture

Wild asses in desert imagery

Early Arabic poetry uses حِمَارُ الْوَحْشِ for a wild ass and words such as عَانَة for a group of them.

Such descriptions depict an animal moving across open land and fleeing hunters or predators when it senses danger.

This image fits حُمُرٌ مُسْتَنْفِرَةٌ in Al-Muddaththir 74:50.

Its wild life does not, however, make حِمَار itself mean cowardly or fleeing.

Flight is expressed by مُسْتَنْفِرَة and فَرَّتْ; حُمُر simply names the animals.

3. Actual Donkeys in Qur’anic Accounts

Al-Naḥl 16:8: riding and adornment

وَالْخَيْلَ وَالْبِغَالَ وَالْحَمِيرَ لِتَرْكَبُوهَا وَزِينَةً وَيَخْلُقُ مَا لَا تَعْلَمُونَ

wa-l-khayla wa-l-bighāla wa-l-ḥamīra li-tarkabūhā wa-zīnatan wa-yakhluqu mā lā taʿlamūna

Translation: And horses, mules, and donkeys [He created] for you to ride and as adornment; and He creates what you do not know.

الْحَمِيرَ is a plural of حِمَار here.

The donkey accompanies the horse and mule: three animals familiar in people’s lives for transport and riding.

The verse mentions two uses: riding and adornment.

لِتَرْكَبُوهَا comes from ر ك ب and concerns riding.

زِينَةً comes from ز ي ن and concerns adornment.

Neither belongs inside حِمَار’s lexical meaning. It names the animal, while the verse separately expresses human uses of it.

Adornment is not confined to an animal’s colour or physical beauty. Possessing and using a mount could embellish people’s lives as well as serve practical needs.

The verse does not establish that the animal’s worth consists solely in its usefulness to humanity. It identifies familiar human benefits without defining every characteristic or the entire place of the living creature.

Mentioning riding and adornment does not by itself settle every question about whether these animals’ meat may be eaten.

Al-Naḥl 16:5 mentions livestock used for food and other benefits. The different descriptions do not, without independent evidence, establish that every animal named for riding is forbidden as food.

The meaning of حِمَار and its dietary status are separate questions.

Al-Baqarah 2:259: a donkey in an account of death and revival

أَوْ كَالَّذِي مَرَّ عَلَىٰ قَرْيَةٍ وَهِيَ خَاوِيَةٌ عَلَىٰ عُرُوشِهَا قَالَ أَنَّىٰ يُحْيِي هَٰذِهِ اللَّهُ بَعْدَ مَوْتِهَا فَأَمَاتَهُ اللَّهُ مِائَةَ عَامٍ ثُمَّ بَعَثَهُ قَالَ كَمْ لَبِثْتَ قَالَ لَبِثْتُ يَوْمًا أَوْ بَعْضَ يَوْمٍ قَالَ بَلْ لَبِثْتَ مِائَةَ عَامٍ فَانْظُرْ إِلَىٰ طَعَامِكَ وَشَرَابِكَ لَمْ يَتَسَنَّهْ وَانْظُرْ إِلَىٰ حِمَارِكَ وَلِنَجْعَلَكَ آيَةً لِلنَّاسِ وَانْظُرْ إِلَى الْعِظَامِ كَيْفَ نُنْشِزُهَا ثُمَّ نَكْسُوهَا لَحْمًا فَلَمَّا تَبَيَّنَ لَهُ قَالَ أَعْلَمُ أَنَّ اللَّهَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ قَدِيرٌ

aw ka-alladhī marra ʿalā qaryatin wa-hiya khāwiyatun ʿalā ʿurūshihā qāla annā yuḥyī hādhihi allāhu baʿda mawtihā fa-amātahu allāhu miʾata ʿāmin thumma baʿathahu qāla kam labithta qāla labithtu yawman aw baʿḍa yawmin qāla bal labithta miʾata ʿāmin fa-nẓur ilā ṭaʿāmika wa-sharābika lam yatasannah wa-nẓur ilā ḥimārika wa-li-najʿalaka āyatan li-l-nāsi wa-nẓur ilā al-ʿiẓāmi kayfa nunshizuhā thumma naksūhā laḥman fa-lammā tabayyana lahu qāla aʿlamu anna allāha ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīrun

Translation: Or like the one who passed a settlement fallen upon its roofs. He said, “How will The God revive this after its death?” The God caused him to die for a hundred years, then raised him. He said, “How long have you remained?” He said, “A day or part of a day.” He said, “Rather, you have remained a hundred years. Look at your food and drink: they have not changed; and look at your donkey; and so that We may make you a sign for people. Look at the bones, how We raise them, then clothe them with flesh.” When it became clear to him, he said, “I know that The God has power over everything.”

حِمَارِكَ is a singular noun with the pronoun كَ: your donkey.

The donkey here is an actual animal belonging to, or accompanying, the person in the account.

The verse begins with the settlement’s condition and the person’s question about its revival after death, then describes his own death and raising.

His food, drink, donkey, and bones subsequently become objects of observation.

Lexically, حِمَار has no special meaning such as bones, death, or revival. Those concepts come from other verbs and clauses.

Some exegetical reports identify the person or elaborate the donkey’s condition. The verse itself does not name the person or describe every stage of what happened to the animal.

Nor does حِمَارِكَ alone establish the animal’s original ownership or how it had been kept.

What is clear is that the animal appears among the signs the person is instructed to observe.

The donkey here is therefore part of a scene clarifying death and revival, rather than an ethical simile.

4. The Donkey’s Voice and Qur’anic Similes

Luqmān 31:19: donkeys’ voices and measured speech

وَاقْصِدْ فِي مَشْيِكَ وَاغْضُضْ مِنْ صَوْتِكَ إِنَّ أَنْكَرَ الْأَصْوَاتِ لَصَوْتُ الْحَمِيرِ

wa-qṣid fī mashyika wa-ghḍuḍ min ṣawtika inna ankara al-aṣwāti la-ṣawtu al-ḥamīri

Translation: Be moderate in your walking and lower your voice; the most disagreeable of sounds is the voice of donkeys.

الْحَمِيرِ is a plural of حِمَار.

The verse continues Luqmān’s advice to his son.

Luqmān 31:18 forbids arrogant behaviour toward people and walking proudly. The next verse discusses moderation in walking and lowering one’s voice.

This connection places the donkey’s voice within advice about human conduct.

أَنْكَرَ in أَنْكَرَ الْأَصْوَاتِ comes from ن ك ر and here characterizes the most disagreeable sound; الْأَصْوَاتِ belongs to the independent root ص و ت.

حِمَار itself does not mean an ugly, ignorant, or worthless being. It names the animal, whose voice is characterized separately in the sentence.

The donkey’s natural voice is part of its own behaviour and interaction with its surroundings. The verse does not make it ethically responsible for producing that voice.

The comparison teaches human conduct, particularly avoiding needless loudness and irritating or arrogant behaviour.

Conversely, it does not say that a person must always speak quietly, even when warning others, seeking a right, or alerting people to danger.

وَاغْضُضْ مِنْ صَوْتِكَ occurs in a context of measured conduct. Appropriate volume depends on the situation and purpose of speech.

The lexical meaning of حَمِير therefore remains donkeys; the voice’s disagreeable quality and the behavioural lesson come from the whole verse.

Al-Jumuʿah 62:5: carrying books without fulfilling responsibility

مَثَلُ الَّذِينَ حُمِّلُوا التَّوْرَاةَ ثُمَّ لَمْ يَحْمِلُوهَا كَمَثَلِ الْحِمَارِ يَحْمِلُ أَسْفَارًا بِئْسَ مَثَلُ الْقَوْمِ الَّذِينَ كَذَّبُوا بِآيَاتِ اللَّهِ وَاللَّهُ لَا يَهْدِي الْقَوْمَ الظَّالِمِينَ

mathalu alladhīna ḥummilū al-tawrāta thumma lam yaḥmilūhā ka-mathali al-ḥimāri yaḥmilu asfāran biʾsa mathalu al-qawmi alladhīna kadhdhabū bi-āyāti allāhi wa-allāhu lā yahdī al-qawma al-ẓālimīna

Translation: Those entrusted with the Torah who then failed to bear it are like a donkey carrying books. Wretched is the likeness of people who denied The God’s signs; The God does not guide wrongdoing people.

الْحِمَارِ is a definite singular noun here.

The animal enters a simile contrasting physically carrying books with accepting responsibility for their contents.

حُمِّلُوا التَّوْرَاةَ introduces the responsibility placed upon the group concerned.

لَمْ يَحْمِلُوهَا then states that they did not properly assume or discharge that responsibility.

On the simile’s other side, a donkey bears books on its back.

It carries the physical load; carrying books does not mean understanding and acting on their contents.

The simile therefore concerns the relationship between possessing or carrying a text and understanding and fulfilling its responsibilities.

This matters because merely keeping or transmitting a book does not establish commitment to its meaning.

That conclusion must not be inserted into حِمَار’s lexical definition. It remains the familiar animal capable of carrying loads; lack of understanding of the books belongs to the simile’s relationship.

Nor should the rebuke be directed at the animal itself. A donkey is not responsible for understanding revelation or following human instructions.

The rebuke concerns people who had a responsibility toward the text and failed to discharge it.

The verse also identifies the criticized group as الَّذِينَ كَذَّبُوا بِآيَاتِ اللَّهِ, those who denied The God’s signs.

That qualification matters. Without further evidence, the verse cannot judge all followers of the Torah in every period, whatever their knowledge and conduct, in the same way.

The criticism concerns the situation described by the verse itself.

Conceptually, the danger need not be confined to one community or book: anyone entrusted with an authoritative text who turns away from understanding and fulfilling its responsibilities may face a similar inconsistency. That ethical extension follows analysis of the simile, rather than حِمَار’s lexical meaning.

Study of S F R

Al-Muddaththir 74:50: fleeing donkeys

كَأَنَّهُمْ حُمُرٌ مُسْتَنْفِرَةٌ

ka-annahum ḥumurun mustanfiratun

Translation: As though they were donkeys fleeing in alarm.

حُمُرٌ is a plural of حِمَار here.

مُسْتَنْفِرَةٌ comes from ن ف ر and concerns taking fright and fleeing.

Al-Muddaththir 74:51, the following verse, reads:

فَرَّتْ مِنْ قَسْوَرَةٍ

farrat min qaswaratin

Meaning: That have fled from a qaswarah.

Early explanations interpret قَسْوَرَة as a lion, archers, or hunters. In these readings, something or someone causes the animals to flee; that role should remain distinct from each transmission’s lexical equivalent.

The two verses thus depict a group of wild asses fleeing danger in alarm.

The widespread reading has مُسْتَنْفِرَة with kasrah on fāʾ; other readings also transmit مُسْتَنْفَرَة with fatḥah.

The former emphasizes the animals’ taking fright and fleeing; the latter gives greater prominence to their being frightened or driven to flee.

Both depict a group of animals rapidly escaping danger.

Al-Ṭabarī: two readings of mustanfirah and interpretations of qaswarah

The simile occurs in a context of people turning away from the reminder.

Al-Muddaththir 74:49 asks why they turn away from the reminder; their behaviour is then likened to fleeing donkeys.

The rebuke concerns the people’s avoidance of the reminder, rather than the animal or its natural flight.

حُمُر cannot mean red things here. With ḍammah on mīm, it is a plural of حِمَار; مُسْتَنْفِرَة and فَرَّتْ also support that sense.

Conversely, حُمُر itself does not mean cowardly, ignorant, or unruly. Any such characteristics discussed in relation to human behaviour must come from the whole simile and context, rather than the animal’s name.

5. حُمْرٌ: Red Colouring in Mountains

Fāṭir 35:27

أَلَمْ تَرَ أَنَّ اللَّهَ أَنْزَلَ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً فَأَخْرَجْنَا بِهِ ثَمَرَاتٍ مُخْتَلِفًا أَلْوَانُهَا وَمِنَ الْجِبَالِ جُدَدٌ بِيضٌ وَحُمْرٌ مُخْتَلِفٌ أَلْوَانُهَا وَغَرَابِيبُ سُودٌ

a-lam tara anna allāha anzala mina al-samāʾi māʾan fa-akhrajnā bihi thamarātin mukhtalifan alwānuhā wa-mina al-jibāli judadun bīḍun wa-ḥumrun mukhtalifun alwānuhā wa-gharābību sūdun

Translation: Have you not seen that The God sent water down from the sky, and through it We brought forth fruits of varying colours? In the mountains are white and red streaks of varying colours, and intensely black ones.

حُمْرٌ is the plural of أَحْمَر and means red here.

It is the Qur’an’s only occurrence of this root’s colour branch.

The verse begins with the different colours of fruit, then discusses mountain colours and streaks.

جُدَدٌ, plural of جُدَّة, concerns streaks and paths in mountains whose colours differ from their surroundings.

بِيضٌ is the plural of أَبْيَض, white; حُمْرٌ is the plural of أَحْمَر, red.

غَرَابِيبُ سُودٌ emphasizes intense blackness.

The verse thus places white, red, and black together in describing mountain formations.

Redness and the substance of rock

حُمْرٌ expresses colour, rather than the material composing a mountain.

Natural red streaks can relate to different minerals and geological compositions, but حُمْر does not name a specific mineral or chemical compound.

The basic translation must therefore not replace it with iron, copper, iron oxide, or a particular stone.

These may cause redness in some geological formations, but that explanation requires independent scientific evidence.

The verse concerns perceptible colour.

مُخْتَلِفٌ أَلْوَانُهَا also indicates colour differences among the streaks mentioned.

It therefore does not limit mountains to three uniform, unchanging colours.

Colour and the diversity of creation

Fāṭir 35:28 follows the fruit and mountain descriptions with the different colours of people, moving creatures, and livestock.

The connection presents colour variation as a perceptible feature of creation.

Colour itself cannot determine a person’s or animal’s ethical worth.

حُمْرٌ here does not concern donkeys, unlike حُمُرٌ in Al-Muddaththir 74:50.

The different vowels on mīm in these two forms mark an important distinction:

حُمْرٌ means red things, whereas حُمُرٌ is a plural of حِمَار and means donkeys.

Without vowel marks, both are written حمر; in these two verses, vocalization and context identify each meaning. Arabic outside these passages, however, also records حُمْر with sukūn on mīm as a plural of حِمَار. Sukūn alone therefore does not exclude the donkey sense in every Arabic usage.

Lisān al-ʿArab: ḥumr with sukūn also occurs as a plural of the animal’s name

This is a clear example of the importance of vocalization and morphology in Qur’anic root studies.

6. Distinctions from Nearby Roots

ح م ر (ḥimār) and ب غ ل (baghl)

بَغْل names a mule, usually the offspring of a horse and donkey.

Al-Naḥl 16:8 places الْخَيْل, الْبِغَال, and الْحَمِير together.

Their juxtaposition uses three distinct names for three familiar animal groups.

حِمَار must therefore not be confused with a mule or horse, even when all serve riding and transport in the same setting.

Study of B GH L

ح م ر (ḥimār) and خ ي ل (khayl)

خَيْل denotes horses.

Donkeys and horses have shared uses in human life, including riding and carrying loads.

Their names and biological characteristics nevertheless differ.

The Qur’an names horses and donkeys independently; translation should preserve the distinction.

The difference itself does not establish the greater ethical or religious worth of either animal.

ح م ر (ḥimār) and ح م ل (ḥamala)

ح م ل concerns bearing and moving loads.

Al-Jumuʿah 62:5 has الْحِمَارِ يَحْمِلُ أَسْفَارًا.

حِمَار names the animal; يَحْمِلُ is the verb carrying.

The donkey enters the example through its ability to carry loads, but حِمَار itself does not mean porter or carrier.

This distinction prevents an animal’s function from replacing its lexical name.

ح م ر (ḥumrah) and ب ي ض (bayāḍ)

بَيَاض concerns the colour white.

Fāṭir 35:27 places white and red together in describing mountain streaks.

The colour fields differ, but both can describe a material’s appearance.

Some early Arabic colour names for faces or human groups extend beyond a perfectly pure colour.

Those uses should not be imposed on Fāṭir’s direct sense of red, however, since actual mountain colours are described there.

Study of B Y Ḍ

ح م ر (ḥumrah) and س و د (sawād)

سَوَاد denotes blackness.

In Fāṭir 35:27, غَرَابِيبُ سُودٌ follows حُمْرٌ.

The contrast presents the diversity of mountain colours.

Red does not mean dark or black here. Each colour has its independent form.

حُمْر should therefore be translated red, and سُود black.

ح م ر (ḥumrah) and ل و ن (lawn)

لَوْن is the general name for colour.

حُمْرَة identifies a particular colour.

Fāṭir 35:27 places red alongside مُخْتَلِفٌ أَلْوَانُهَا.

The construction allows red to appear in differing shades and forms, but حُمْرَة does not mean colour in general.

Study of L W N

ح م ر (ḥamar) and ح م ي (ḥamiya)

In its heat-related use, ح م ي concerns becoming hot and heat.

The colour branch of ح م ر concerns redness.

Metal can become red through heating, but redness and heat are different concepts.

Red mountain streaks in Fāṭir 35:27 do not imply that they are hot.

حَامِيَة in the recognized reading of Al-Kahf 18:86 likewise belongs to heat, rather than ح م ر.

ح م ر (ḥimār) and ح م د (ḥamd)

حَمْد concerns praise, whereas حِمَار names an animal and حُمْرَة a colour.

The families share their first two consonants, but differ in their final consonant and semantic field.

Formal resemblance therefore cannot transfer praise into ح م ر.

7. Translation Proposals for Forqan

حِمَارِكَ in Al-Baqarah 2:259 should be “your donkey.” It is a singular noun with a second-person pronoun; “your quadruped” or “your mount” should not replace it, except within an explanation of the animal’s role in the account.

الْحَمِيرَ in Al-Naḥl 16:8 should be “donkeys.” The verse names familiar animals for riding and adornment.

صَوْتُ الْحَمِيرِ in Luqmān 31:19 should be “the voice of donkeys.” Persian can also use an expression corresponding to “donkeys’ braying,” but صَوْت means sound or voice; braying explains the kind of sound produced.

حُمْرٌ in Fāṭir 35:27 should be “red,” or “red-coloured” where appropriate. It does not name the animal; it describes mountain streaks’ colouring.

الْحِمَارِ in Al-Jumuʿah 62:5 should be “donkey.” Carrying books without acting on their contents belongs in the explanation of the simile, rather than in the animal’s lexical definition.

حُمُرٌ in Al-Muddaththir 74:50 means “donkeys,” or “wild asses” in light of the following verse. Wildness is not an independent component of the word itself; it follows the contextual image of taking fright and fleeing.

“Donkeys fleeing in alarm” is a concise, natural rendering of حُمُرٌ مُسْتَنْفِرَةٌ.

Outside the Qur’an, حُمْرَة is “redness,” أَحْمَر “red,” حَمْرَاء the feminine “red,” احْمَرَّ “became red,” and حَمَّرَ “made red.”

حِمَار is “donkey” and أَتَان “female donkey.”

Throughout these usages, the animal’s name, colour, and the behaviour or function attributed in context must remain distinct.

Conclusion

Brief root meaning: Redness and the colour red; in another branch, ḥimār means donkey, with the plural forms ḥamīr and ḥumur.

In the first branch, حُمْرَة denotes red and أَحْمَر something bearing that colour. Verbs such as احْمَرَّ and حَمَّرَ express becoming red and making something red.

In the second, حِمَار names an animal known both domestically and in the wild; حَمِير and حُمُر belong to this branch.

Ibn Fāris considers a shared principle of redness but also allows the animal’s name to be separate from colour. Their historical relationship must therefore not be asserted without firm evidence.

The Qur’an uses the colour branch once and the animal branch five times.

حُمْرٌ in Fāṭir 35:27 concerns red mountain streaks. Al-Naḥl 16:8 names the donkey among animals familiar for riding and adornment; Al-Baqarah 2:259 places it in an account of death and revival.

Luqmān 31:19 uses the donkey’s voice in advice about measured speech. Al-Jumuʿah 62:5 makes its carrying books the basis of a simile about responsibility to understand and act upon a text. In Al-Muddaththir 74:50, fleeing donkeys picture people turning away from the reminder.

These usages show that the animal’s name does not inherently carry a negative ethical judgement. One verse presents it among useful provisions for life; others use familiar characteristics or behaviour to explain human conduct.

حِمَار’s lexical definition must therefore not become ignorant, cowardly, unruly, or worthless. Any such judgements within a simile concern the relationships among that particular example’s elements.

حُمْرٌ, red, is also distinct from حُمُرٌ, donkeys. The mīm’s vowel and the morphology, together with each verse’s context, identify the intended meaning.

This root thus has two distinct, clear Qur’anic meanings: the colour red and the donkey’s name. Neither should be altered to establish the other.

For Forqan, “red” suits the colour branch and “donkey” or “donkeys” the animal branch. The study should establish these senses through linguistic evidence and preserve the distinction between lexical meaning, an animal’s natural characteristics, and the ethical message of Qur’anic similes.
