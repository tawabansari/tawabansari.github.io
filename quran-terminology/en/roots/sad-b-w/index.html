Childhood and early life, inclination and attraction, and the east wind: three semantic branches in Arabic

The Arabic root Ṣ B W includes words used in three main domains: childhood, inclination toward someone or something, and the name of a wind blowing from the east.

This family includes ṣabiyy, a child; ṣibā, childhood; ṣabā yaṣbū, to incline or become attached; and al-ṣabā, the name of a wind.

The root occurs three times in three Qur’anic verses: aṣbu in Yūsuf 12:33, and ṣabiyyan in Maryam 19:12 and Maryam 19:29.

Childhood, inclination, and the east wind share root consonants but do not have an identical meaning. Connections between childhood and youthful attractions deserve examination, but every use cannot be reduced to one psychological state or a proven historical image.

1. The root’s semantic cores in Arabic

In Maqāyīs al-Lughah, Ibn Fāris identifies three semantic principles for this family:

First, young age; second, a particular wind; and third, inclination or tilting.

He places ṣabiyy in the branch concerning young age, and explains ṣabā ilā al-shayʾi yaṣbū as the heart’s inclination toward something.

He also mentions the east wind and ṣābā al-rumḥa, to tilt a spear.

This classification shows that early lexicographers did not explain every form in the family through a single definition without exceptions.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: three principles and the distinct hamzated root

Childhood and young age

Al-ṣibā denotes childhood or early youth in Arabic.

For example, raʾaytuhu fī ṣibāhu means “I saw him in his childhood.”

Ṣabiyy names a child; recorded plurals include ṣibyah, ṣibyān, and other forms.

Here the defining feature is a stage of life, rather than immature behavior or an attraction to amusement.

Childhood may involve play, dependence, a need for care, and the gradual development of abilities. None of these features alone fully defines ṣabiyy.

Inclination and attraction

Ṣabā ilā al-shayʾi yaṣbū means to incline or feel drawn toward something.

Lisān al-ʿArab records:

صَبَا إِلَيْهِ صَبْوَةً وَصُبُوًّا: حَنَّ

ṣabā ilayhi ṣabwatan wa-ṣubuwwan: ḥanna

That is, he inclined toward him and felt longing.

This can concern interest in a person, attachment, longing, or attraction to an activity.

In some expressions, especially when the attraction concerns excessive amusement or rash conduct, the word also relates to youthful frivolity and impulses.

Nevertheless, inclination should not invariably be equated with foolishness or sin.

Someone may feel drawn toward something without acting on that attraction or doing anything wrong.

The east wind

Al-ṣabā, with fatḥah on the ṣād, names the wind blowing from the east in the familiar Arabic classification of winds.

Its counterpart, al-dabūr, names the wind coming from the west.

This use names a natural phenomenon; it should not be translated as childhood or attachment.

Some lexical accounts connect the wind’s name with inclination or facing a direction. Such an explanation does not itself prove that its name historically derived from human emotional attraction.

Childhood, inclination, and the wind are therefore three established branches whose relationship requires care.

2. Uses in early Arabic

Ṣabiyy and early childhood

Lisān al-ʿArab also records a restricted definition of ṣabiyy:

مِنْ لَدُنْ يُولَدُ إِلَى أَنْ يُفْطَمَ

min ladun yūladu ilā an yufṭama

That is, from birth until weaning.

The same source also explains ṣabiyy through ghulām and records broader childhood uses.

The nursing period therefore cannot be treated as a fixed limit on every use of ṣabiyy.

The word can describe a young child who is no longer necessarily nursing.

A verse attributed to Ruʾbah uses ṣubayyah for children. It describes a group of children without the word itself specifying their exact ages.

A verse attributed to Suwayd ibn Kurāʿ also reads:

فَهَلْ يُعْذَرَنْ ذُو شَيْبَةٍ بِصَبَائِهِ
وَهَلْ يُحْمَدَنْ بِالصَّبْرِ إِنْ كَانَ يَصْبِرُ

fa-hal yuʿdharan dhū shaybatin bi-ṣabāʾihi / wa-hal yuḥmadan bi-al-ṣabri in kāna yaṣbiru

Translation: “Is a gray-haired man excused for his youthful folly? And, if he exercises patience, will he be praised for it?”

Here ṣabāʾ refers to youthful folly or conduct resembling that of the young, attributed to a gray-haired man. It need not refer to deeds performed earlier in his youth.

Later lexicons preserve this witness’s wording and attribution; its precise composition date is not independently established by that transmission. It should therefore not be presented unreservedly as a securely dated pre-Islamic witness.

Lisān al-ʿArab: ṣabiyy and Suwayd’s witness

The verse matters because ṣabā can concern both a stage of life and behavior associated with that stage. This witness concerns youthful folly in old age, so age and conduct are distinct.

Longing and inclination: a poetic witness with differing attributions

Lisān al-ʿArab attributes this verse to “Zayd ibn Ḍabbah.” Al-Ṭabarī also quotes it, while the notes in the consulted edition of his commentary identify the poet as “Yazīd ibn Ḍabbah al-Thaqafī.” This difference between transmissions should be retained:

Al-Ṭabarī and the edition’s notes: attribution of the Hind witness

إِلَى هِنْدٍ صَبَا قَلْبِي
وَهِنْدٌ مِثْلُهَا يُصْبِي

ilā hindin ṣabā qalbī / wa-hindun mithluhā yuṣbī

Translation: “My heart inclined toward Hind; a woman like Hind draws hearts toward her.”

In the first half, ṣabā qalbī means that my heart became inclined.

In the second, yuṣbī concerns arousing inclination or attraction in another.

One expression describes a person’s inclination; the other concerns someone or something that arouses it.

This is a clear witness to the root’s emotional use. Lexical transmission alone, however, does not establish its precise composition date, so it cannot be treated as a securely dated pre-Islamic witness.

The subject here is attraction and attachment. The verb ṣabā does not necessarily mean being deceived, committing a sin, or losing one’s reason.

Tilting and changing direction

Arabic also records physical uses involving inclination.

For example:

صَابَى الرُّمْحَ

ṣābā al-rumḥa

That is, he tilted the spear.

Ṣābaynā ʿani al-ḥamḍi is also recorded as turning away from ḥamḍ, salt-loving grazing plants. The example concerns a change from one kind of grazing or forage, rather than the name of a route.

Ṣabati al-rāʿiyatu is also recorded of a grazing animal lowering and inclining its head toward the pasture.

Inclination here is not necessarily psychological: it can be a physical change of direction or movement toward something.

This may show a semantic connection between physical inclination and inward attraction, but resemblance alone cannot establish which meaning historically came first.

Arabic lexicons: physical inclination and ḥamḍ

The east wind in early poetry

A poem attributed to the pre-Islamic poet ʿAdī ibn Zayd al-ʿIbādī reads:

ثُمَّ صَارُوا كَأَنَّهُمْ وَرَقٌ جَفَّ
فَأَلْوَتْ بِهِ الصَّبَا وَالدَّبُورُ

thumma ṣārū ka-annahum waraqun jaffa / fa-alwat bihi al-ṣabā wa-al-dabūru

Translation: “Then they became like dried leaves that the east and west winds sweep away.”

Transmissions show slight differences in the opening verb and wording about the leaves, while the contrast between al-ṣabā and al-dabūr remains in familiar versions.

Muʿjam al-Shuʿarāʾ: the thumma aḍḥaw variant in ʿAdī’s witness

The poet uses winds from different directions to depict dried leaves being scattered.

Ṣabā here names a wind and has no direct connection to human age or emotional longing.

The witness shows that ṣabā was an established wind name in Arabic poetic descriptions of nature and movement.

3. Qur’anic uses

Yūsuf 12:33: aṣbu ilayhinna

قَالَ رَبِّ السِّجْنُ أَحَبُّ إِلَيَّ مِمَّا يَدْعُونَنِي إِلَيْهِ وَإِلَّا تَصْرِفْ عَنِّي كَيْدَهُنَّ أَصْبُ إِلَيْهِنَّ وَأَكُنْ مِنَ الْجَاهِلِينَ

qāla rabbi al-sijnu aḥabbu ilayya mimmā yadʿūnanī ilayhi wa-illā taṣrif ʿannī kaydahunna aṣbu ilayhinna wa-akun mina al-jāhilīna

Translation: He said, “My Lord, prison is dearer to me than that to which they invite me. Unless You turn their scheme away from me, I shall incline toward them and become one of the ignorant.”

Aṣbu comes from ṣabā yaṣbū and means inclining or being drawn toward something.

Joseph faces the invitation and pressure of women seeking to draw him into conduct contrary to his moral choice.

He prefers prison to what they invite him to, and asks his Lord to turn their scheme away from him.

Aṣbu: inclination or accepting an invitation?

In Lisān al-ʿArab, Abū al-Haytham explains ṣabā ilā fulānah as inclining toward a woman.

Al-Ṭabarī likewise explains aṣbu ilayhinna through amīlu ilayhinna, “I incline toward them,” and goes on to describe complying with what they want.

Al-Ṭabarī: inclination and complying with the women’s demands

These two levels should be distinguished.

Directly, aṣbu means inclination. Where attraction accompanies pressure to act, that inclination can prepare the way for accepting another’s demand.

“I incline toward them” is therefore closer to the wording, while “I give in to what they want” explains a possible contextual consequence.

Feeling an inclination and acting on it are not the same semantic stage.

The conditional construction

In the expression:

وَإِلَّا تَصْرِفْ عَنِّي كَيْدَهُنَّ أَصْبُ إِلَيْهِنَّ

wa-illā taṣrif ʿannī kaydahunna aṣbu ilayhinna

Illā here combines conditional in and negative lā: “if not” or “unless.”

Taṣrif is the verb of the condition, and aṣbu its consequence.

Aṣbu is a first-person singular imperfect verb in the jussive. Its underlying form is aṣbū; the final weak consonant is omitted because of the jussive.

It should therefore not be mistaken for a past-tense verb or a different noun.

The sentence gives a conditional result: if their scheme is not turned away, he may incline toward them.

It does not say that Joseph had already yielded to their demands or committed an act.

Is inclination inherently blameworthy?

The verse continues:

وَأَكُنْ مِنَ الْجَاهِلِينَ

wa-akun mina al-jāhilīna

That is, “and I shall become one of the ignorant.”

In this particular narrative, Joseph describes an attraction that would lead to wrongful behavior if followed.

That assessment comes from the object of the attraction and its possible consequence, rather than ṣabā inherently meaning sin in every use.

Someone may feel drawn to a person or thing without that inclination itself being wrong.

Here the demand is one Joseph considers contrary to the right course.

His request for divine help does not negate his agency. The verse also states his own preference: prison is dearer to him than what they demand.

Conscious choice and seeking divine help therefore appear together in the narrative.

Maryam 19:12: wa-ātaynāhu al-ḥukma ṣabiyyan

يَا يَحْيَىٰ خُذِ الْكِتَابَ بِقُوَّةٍ وَآتَيْنَاهُ الْحُكْمَ صَبِيًّا

yā yaḥyā khudhi al-kitāba bi-quwwatin wa-ātaynāhu al-ḥukma ṣabiyyan

Translation: O John, take the Scripture firmly; and We gave him judgment while a child.

Ṣabiyyan here describes John.

It is accusative and functions grammatically as a circumstantial qualifier of the pronoun hu in ātaynāhu: it describes John’s condition when he received al-ḥukm.

John was granted judgment during childhood.

Ṣabiyyan itself does not mean judgment, wisdom, or knowledge. Those concepts concern the separate word al-ḥukm and its context.

Does ṣabiyy mean a nursing infant?

Some lexicons define ṣabiyy as a child from birth until weaning.

Arabic usage also extends beyond that restricted definition.

Maryam 19:12 gives no numerical age for John. It establishes that he was young when granted judgment.

Ṣabiyyan alone therefore cannot establish that John was necessarily a newborn or nursing infant.

Conversely, being granted judgment does not justify changing ṣabiyy into an adolescent who has reached maturity.

The verse specifically describes John receiving a gift during childhood.

The appropriate meaning here is “in childhood” or “while he was a child.”

Childhood, ability, and understanding

Ṣabiyyan describes John’s age and stage of life without itself determining the extent of his understanding or judgment.

The range of understanding, judgment, or responsibility included in al-ḥukm must be examined through that word and the Qur’anic context.

The meaning of ṣabiyy does not establish that every child has this capacity; nor can childhood invariably be equated with a complete inability to understand.

The verse describes a gift granted to John, rather than defining all children generally.

Al-Ṭabarī: judgment during childhood

Maryam 19:29: fī al-mahdi ṣabiyyan

فَأَشَارَتْ إِلَيْهِ قَالُوا كَيْفَ نُكَلِّمُ مَنْ كَانَ فِي الْمَهْدِ صَبِيًّا

fa-ashārat ilayhi qālū kayfa nukallimu man kāna fī al-mahdi ṣabiyyan

Translation: Then she pointed toward him. They said, “How can we speak to someone who is a child in the cradle?”

Ṣabiyyan here describes Jesus.

It appears alongside al-mahd: a cradle or a place where a young child is laid.

This contextual clue narrows its meaning.

Maryam 19:12 refers to John’s childhood; Maryam 19:29 describes a child in the cradle.

In the second verse, “nursing infant” or “newborn” is therefore closer to the context.

Nevertheless, ṣabiyyan belongs to the same semantic family in both verses. The second verse’s fī al-mahd supplies the difference concerning the child’s precise age and circumstances.

Study of M H D

Why were people surprised at speaking to him?

In Maryam 19:27, Mary brings her child to her people. In Maryam 19:29, she points toward him, and they ask how they can speak to a child in the cradle.

Their question reflects ordinary expectations about a very young child’s ability to speak.

In Maryam 19:30, Jesus answers:

قَالَ إِنِّي عَبْدُ اللَّهِ آتَانِيَ الْكِتَابَ وَجَعَلَنِي نَبِيًّا

qāla innī ʿabdu allāhi ātāniya al-kitāba wa-jaʿalanī nabiyyan

Translation: “He said: I am a servant of The God. He has given me the Scripture and made me a prophet.”

His answer describes an extraordinary event in the narrative.

It does not establish that ṣabiyy means someone capable of speech, or that its lexical meaning is a child with a special rank.

Ṣabiyy still means a child. His speaking is a feature of the event described in the following verses.

Al-Ṭabarī: the child in the cradle and the people’s question

4. Important forms and morphological distinctions

Ṣabiyy and ṣabiyyah

In ordinary usage, ṣabiyy names a boy or male child; its feminine is ṣabiyyah.

Both Qur’anic instances of ṣabiyyan concern males: John and Jesus.

Familiar plurals include ṣibyah and ṣibyān; Arabic also records other plural forms.

The masculine form does not restrict childhood itself to boys. Feminine forms and general expressions for childhood also exist.

Why is ṣabiyy written with yāʾ?

Ṣ B W is a final-weak root whose last radical is wāw.

Ṣabiyy, however, is written with yāʾ.

Arab lexicographers and grammarians explain the difference through rules governing weak-consonant changes and word formation.

Ṣibyān likewise contains yāʾ, while forms such as ṣibwān are also recorded.

The variation between wāw and yāʾ does not itself establish two entirely separate roots.

Morphological analysis, however, should not be turned into a definitive claim about a word’s remote historical derivation.

Arabic lexicons: wāw and yāʾ forms

Ṣabā and ṣabwah

Ṣabā yaṣbū expresses inclining or feeling drawn toward something.

Recorded verbal nouns include ṣabw, ṣubuww, and ṣabwah.

In some uses, ṣabwah concerns attraction or conduct associated with youthful excitement and frivolity.

That connotation is not present in every use of ṣabā.

Inclination toward a beloved may simply express longing. Where the attraction concerns wrongful conduct, its context can make it blameworthy.

Aṣbā and taṣābā

Aṣbā is recorded for arousing inclination or drawing someone toward something.

Taṣābā can mean behaving like children or young people; in some uses it concerns pursuing emotional attractions or amusements associated with youth.

These forms do not occur in the Qur’an.

A person’s conduct, arousing inclination in someone else, and imitating childish behavior should be distinguished.

Al-ṣabā: a wind name

Al-ṣabā names the east wind; recorded plurals include ṣabawāt.

Al-dabūr names the wind from the opposite direction.

This word does not occur in the Qur’an. Its meaning should not be transferred to aṣbu in Joseph’s narrative or ṣabiyyan in the accounts of John and Jesus.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: three occurrences in two forms

5. Comparison with related roots

Ṣ B W (ṣabā) and M Y L (māla)

M Y L concerns inclination, deviation in direction, and leaning.

Ṣabā ilā can likewise express inclination toward something.

The two families are very close in some uses; lexicographers explain ṣabā through māla.

Ṣ B W also includes the familiar ṣabiyy and ṣibā family concerning childhood, and the wind name ṣabā.

In Yūsuf 12:33, inclination is the direct meaning of aṣbu; its object and possible consequence come from the narrative.

The verse does not establish that every use of M Y L or Ṣ B W is blameworthy.

Ṣ B W (ṣabā) and H W Y (hawiya)

Some uses of H W Y concern loving, attraction, and desire.

Ṣabā can similarly express longing or inclination toward someone or something.

Hawā has its own semantic family and constructions in the Qur’an. In some contexts it denotes a desire preferred over guidance or truth.

Aṣbu in Yūsuf 12:33 concerns inclination toward the women pressuring Joseph.

This overlap does not make every human inclination morally wrong.

The value of conduct depends on the object of desire and how a person responds to it.

Study of H W Y

Ṣ B W (ṣabā) and Ṭ F L (ṭifl)

Ṭifl denotes a child in Arabic and is also used for children in the Qur’an.

Ṣabiyy likewise denotes a child, with young age particularly evident in its Qur’anic uses.

In Maryam 19:29, fī al-mahd alongside ṣabiyyan establishes that the child is very young.

Maryam 19:12 does not specify John’s exact age.

There is therefore no basis for an absolute rule that ṣabiyy always means only a nursing infant and ṭifl always an older child.

The words overlap in childhood; context clarifies their age range.

Ṣ B W (ṣabā) and GH L M (ghulām)

Ghulām occurs in the Qur’an for a boy or male offspring in various circumstances.

Sometimes it concerns a child or adolescent; elsewhere the precise stage must be established from context.

Ṣabiyy also describes a male child, whose young age is clear in both Qur’anic instances.

One cannot say categorically that a ghulām is always older than a ṣabiyy, or that the terms can never describe the same person at a particular stage.

Their distinction should be investigated through actual usage, rather than imposing a fixed age table on the Qur’an.

Ṣ B W (ṣabā) and Ṣ B ʾ (ṣabaʾa)

These roots are distinct despite their outward resemblance.

Ṣ B W concerns childhood, inclination, and the east wind.

Ṣ B ʾ concerns emerging or appearing in Arabic; al-ṣābiʾīn and related forms occur in the Qur’an.

For example, al-ṣābiʾīn occurs in Al-Baqarah 2:62 and al-ṣābiʾūn in Al-Māʾidah 5:69.

Similarity between ṣābin and ṣābiʾ does not make the Sabians lovers, children, or people who merely incline toward something.

The families require separate examination.

Study of Ṣ B ʾ

6. Challenges in understanding and translation

The main challenge is distinguishing a person’s age from behavioral qualities attributed to that age in some cultures.

Ṣabiyy means a child. It does not itself mean ignorant, irresponsible, playful, or incapable of understanding.

Likewise, ṣabā in its inclination and longing branch does not necessarily mean committing a sin.

In Yūsuf 12:33, the women’s particular invitation and pressure place inclination within a moral trial. The wrongfulness of the possible consequence emerges from the verse as a whole, rather than from the root being intrinsically bad.

In Maryam 19:12, John’s childhood accompanies his receiving judgment. This does not permit changing ṣabiyy into a mature person or adult.

In Maryam 19:29, Jesus’ presence in the cradle makes his very young age clearer. His speaking is a separate narrative event, outside the definition of ṣabiyy.

Translation should preserve these distinctions.

Nor should an unproven account of a shared origin for childhood, inclination, and the east wind be presented as certain. Historical connections may exist, but shared consonants or poetic imagery cannot establish them alone.

7. Translation proposals for Forqan

The basic equivalent of ṣabiyy is “child”; “young child” can be used where context warrants.

In Maryam 19:12:

وَآتَيْنَاهُ الْحُكْمَ صَبِيًّا

wa-ātaynāhu al-ḥukma ṣabiyyan

Preferred rendering:

“And We gave him judgment while a child.”

This retains the stage of life without adding a numerical age or nursing status.

In Maryam 19:29:

كَيْفَ نُكَلِّمُ مَنْ كَانَ فِي الْمَهْدِ صَبِيًّا

kayfa nukallimu man kāna fī al-mahdi ṣabiyyan

Preferred rendering:

“How can we speak to someone who is a child in the cradle?”

The cradle clarifies that the child is at a very early stage of life.

In Yūsuf 12:33:

أَصْبُ إِلَيْهِنَّ

aṣbu ilayhinna

Preferred rendering:

“I shall incline toward them.”

This conveys the verb’s direct meaning. Explaining how such inclination could lead to compliance belongs to contextual analysis.

Outside the Qur’an, al-ṣibā can be rendered “childhood”; ṣabwah, according to context, “inclination,” “attraction,” or “youthful impulse”; and al-ṣabā, “the east wind.”

8. Conclusion

Ṣ B W has three established Arabic branches: childhood, inclination toward someone or something, and the east wind.

A semantic connection between childhood and youthful attractions is understandable. It cannot, however, be turned into a definitive account of every form’s historical origin. The wind name has its own use, and its precise derivational relationship to the other branches remains uncertain.

Ṣabiyyan occurs twice in the Qur’an, describing John and Jesus as children. Their different circumstances emerge from context: John receives judgment during childhood, while Jesus is in the cradle.

Aṣbu in Joseph’s narrative means inclining toward the women. It occurs conditionally and describes a possible result, rather than an act already accomplished.

Short definition: In its human branch, Ṣ B W concerns childhood; its verbal branch expresses inclination or being drawn toward something. Ṣabā also names a familiar Arabic wind. Brief root meaning: Childhood and young age; inclination and attraction; ṣabā: the east wind.

Childhood should therefore not be equated with foolishness, nor inclination with inherent sin. Sharing a root does not make the wind name mean childhood or longing.

In every use, word form and context establish the meaning. Moral value, a person’s exact age, and a wind’s natural characteristics should not be added to the root’s definition without supporting evidence.
