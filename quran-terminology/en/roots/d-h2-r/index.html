Extended time, the passage of ages, and attributing events to time

Arabic dahr denotes extended time and the passage of ages. It may encompass a long period, part of a human lifetime, or the world’s temporal expanse. Literary Arabic sometimes depicts it as though it independently causes change, aging, death, and misfortune.

D H R occurs only twice in the Qur’an, both times as the noun al-dahr: in Al-Jāthiyah 45:24 and Al-Insān 76:1.

They differ significantly. Al-Insān uses dahr for extended time; Al-Jāthiyah quotes people attributing human destruction exclusively to it. Time’s lexical meaning must therefore be distinguished from conceiving time as an independent cause of events.

1. Semantic core and Arabic usage

Extended time and the passage of ages

Lisān al-ʿArab defines dahr as al-amadu al-mamdūdu: an extended duration.

Its principal use therefore denotes a temporal expanse within which events and changes occur, rather than a particular instant.

Dictionaries also explain it as al-zamānu al-ṭawīlu, “a long time.”

Some reports assign it a thousand years, but this cannot serve as a general, fixed definition. Actual Arabic usage shows that dahr need not have a specified numerical length.

For example, Arabs would say:

أَقَمْنَا عَلَى مَاءٍ دَهْرًا

aqamnā ʿalā māʾin dahran

“We stayed by a watering place for a long time.”

Here dahr means neither a thousand years nor necessarily the world’s entire duration. It denotes a period the speaker considers long and extended.

Al-Azharī explains in Tahdhīb al-Lughah that dahr can concern either part of a long stretch of time or the entire duration of worldly life. His examples from contemporary Arab speech include long residence in a place.

Length and extension thus matter, but the word alone determines neither beginning nor end.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Tahdhīb al-Lughah: extended time and the watering place

Are dahr and zamān synonymous?

Dictionaries differ concerning their relationship.

Some lexicographers, including Shamir, identify them in a general sense. Others distinguish bounded or seasonal periods from dahr’s broader extension.

After reporting the disagreement, Al-Azharī explains that dahr and zamān can coincide in some uses but cannot replace each other in every expression.

One can speak of the time of heat, cold, or ripening fruit, for example: definite seasons within the year. Dahr does not usually supply that limited seasonal division.

This should nevertheless not become an absolute rule that zamān is always short and dahr always endless. Their fields overlap, and context determines duration.

Reports of Shamir and Al-Azharī: dahr and zamān overlap

2. Dahr in early Arabic: life passing and fortunes changing

Pre-Islamic Arabic poetry frequently places dahr alongside life, death, aging, changing fortunes, and lost blessings.

These uses show that it was more than an abstract term for counting time. Human experience linked it with passing life and changing circumstances.

Dahr in Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd’s poetry

The Muʿallaqah attributed to the pre-Islamic poet Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd states:

أَرَى الْعَيْشَ كَنْزًا نَاقِصًا كُلَّ لَيْلَةٍ
وَمَا تَنْقُصِ الْأَيَّامُ وَالدَّهْرُ يَنْفَدِ

arā al-ʿaysha kanzan nāqiṣan kulla laylatin / wa-mā tanquṣi al-ayyāmu wa-al-dahru yanfadi

A sense translation is: “I see life as a treasure diminishing every night; whatever the days and time diminish will eventually run out.”

The poet likens human life to treasure depleted as nights and days pass.

Al-dahru is coordinated with al-ayyāmu, both participating in the diminishing. Mā introduces the conditional construction, with yanfadi as its response. Days and time therefore appear together in the image of life’s treasure being depleted.

Dahr can thus concern the span of a human life without denoting all the world’s time.

Al-dahru yanfadi should not be detached from the construction and read as “time itself runs out.” In the conditional analysis, the implicit subject of yanfadi refers to what days and time diminish: here, life’s treasure. The line therefore makes no independent claim that all cosmic time ends.

Al-Tabrīzī’s commentary on Ṭarafah: what time diminishes runs out

Dahr as a cause of misfortune

Ibn Fāris quotes a verse attributed to ʿAmr al-Ḍubaʿī in Maqāyīs al-Lughah:

رَمَتْنِي بَنَاتُ الدَّهْرِ مِنْ حَيْثُ لَا أَرَى
فَكَيْفَ بِمَنْ يُرْمَى وَلَيْسَ بِرَامِ

ramatnī banātu al-dahri min ḥaythu lā arā / fa-kayfa bi-man yurmā wa-laysa bi-rāmi

“The daughters of time shot at me from where I cannot see; what becomes of someone who is shot at but is not himself an archer?”

Banāt al-dahr, “daughters of time,” represents calamities and events befalling a person.

The poet pictures time as a force targeting someone from an unseen direction.

The dictionary attributes this verse to ʿAmr al-Ḍubaʿī, but its exact date and pre-Qur’anic status are less clear than the Muʿallaqah witness. Its principal value here is as recorded literary imagery concerning dahr.

Such expressions show Arabs sometimes attributing unwanted events to time. A poetic comparison alone, however, cannot establish that every poet using it believed time to be theologically independent.

Literature can say “time broke me” or “time took everything” without assigning time independent will or power.

This distinction between literary imagery and actual belief about causes matters for the Qur’anic use.

Daharahum amrun: an event befalling someone

Dictionaries record daharahum amrun: an event or affliction befell them.

Dahara here is no longer simply a name for time, but concerns an unwelcome event occurring.

Ibn Fāris explains the root through al-ghalabatu wa-al-qahru, “overpowering and prevailing.” He connects dahr’s name with time passing over and affecting things.

This can illuminate the relationship between passing time and deterioration, but does not prove the word’s historical origin.

Temporal dahr is independently established in Arabic. Ibn Fāris’s connection between the noun and a verb of overpowering is partly etymological analysis and possibility.

One therefore cannot assert with certainty that overpowering came first and time later. Two related branches are attested: extended time, and overpowering or events befalling people.

Maqāyīs al-Lughah: overpowering, ʿAmr’s witness and possible derivation

3. Dahr in the Qur’an

Al-Jāthiyah 45:24: wa-mā yuhlikunā illā al-dahru

وَقَالُوا مَا هِيَ إِلَّا حَيَاتُنَا الدُّنْيَا نَمُوتُ وَنَحْيَا وَمَا يُهْلِكُنَا إِلَّا الدَّهْرُ وَمَا لَهُمْ بِذَٰلِكَ مِنْ عِلْمٍ إِنْ هُمْ إِلَّا يَظُنُّونَ

wa-qālū mā hiya illā ḥayātunā al-dunyā namūtu wa-naḥyā wa-mā yuhlikunā illā al-dahru wa-mā lahum bi-dhālika min ʿilmin in hum illā yaẓunnūna

This study’s proposed rendering: They said: “There is nothing but our worldly life; we die and live, and nothing destroys us except time.” They have no knowledge of that; they merely conjecture.

The Qur’an quotes people limiting life to this world and attributing human destruction exclusively to dahr.

Two parts of their statement must be distinguished.

First, mā hiya illā ḥayātunā al-dunyā limits life to worldly life.

Then wa-mā yuhlikunā illā al-dahru identifies time alone as what destroys them.

Mā … illā expresses restriction in the second clause. Grammatically, al-dahru is the subject of yuhliku.

In the quoted speech, dahr thus occupies the role of destructive agent.

Being the grammatical subject of quoted speech does not mean that the Qur’an endorses that agency.

The Qur’an immediately says:

وَمَا لَهُمْ بِذَٰلِكَ مِنْ عِلْمٍ إِنْ هُمْ إِلَّا يَظُنُّونَ

wa-mā lahum bi-dhālika min ʿilmin in hum illā yaẓunnūna

They have no knowledge supporting the claim and rely only on conjecture.

In Al-Jāthiyah 45:25, the same group demands their fathers’ return in response to clear signs. Al-Jāthiyah 45:26 then states that God gives people life, causes them to die, and gathers them on the Day of Resurrection.

This therefore concerns more than people aging and dying over time. The context addresses a claim limiting life to this world, rejecting a return after death, and attributing destruction exclusively to dahr.

That goes beyond observing passing time to judging life’s source and final outcome.

The verse should consequently not be read as denying natural effects of passing time. Bodily deterioration, natural changes, and aging are observable. Its criticism concerns the unsupported exclusive claim about destruction and confinement of life to this world.

Namūtu wa-naḥyā should retain its stated order in translation: “we die and live.” Contextual analysis can examine successive generations or other readings, but those explanations should not replace the actual wording without grounds.

Dahr still lexically means time’s extended passage. Its destructive agency arises from the construction and the speakers’ belief, not because the word inherently means “destroyer.”

Al-Ṭabarī: quoted speech, death and life, and exclusive destruction

Al-Insān 76:1: ḥīnun mina al-dahri

هَلْ أَتَىٰ عَلَى الْإِنْسَانِ حِينٌ مِنَ الدَّهْرِ لَمْ يَكُنْ شَيْئًا مَذْكُورًا

hal atā ʿalā al-insāni ḥīnun mina al-dahri lam yakun shayʾan madhkūran

This study’s proposed rendering: Has there passed over the human being a period of time when he was not a thing mentioned?

Dahr denotes time’s expanse here, without independent power, will, or agency being attributed to it.

Ḥīnun mina al-dahri is especially significant.

Ḥīn names a time or period; mina al-dahri relates it to a greater temporal expanse.

Min naturally expresses a part or portion here: a period out of time.

Dahr is thus the broader temporal setting within which a ḥīn lies.

This construction alone determines neither that ḥīn’s length nor the full extent of dahr. A thousand years or any other fixed measure requires independent contextual evidence; no such number appears in the expression itself.

Lam yakun shayʾan madhkūran

The continuation describes a time when the human being was not “a thing mentioned.”

Al-Insān 76:2 mentions human creation from a mingled drop, and Al-Insān 76:3 speaks of guidance and human choice.

This sequence directs attention to human existence’s beginning and background: the present condition has not always existed.

Lam yakun shayʾan madhkūran should nevertheless not become a definitive theory of pre-birth existence without further analysis.

“Not being mentioned” and “absolute nonexistence in every respect” are not identical propositions. The negation’s scope concerns the whole verse and other creation passages.

For the root study, the directly relevant point is a period passing within time’s expanse.

Al-Ṭabarī: a period of time and not being mentioned

Distinguishing the two Qur’anic uses

Al-Jāthiyah 45:24 places dahr in people’s speech attributing destruction to it, a claim the Qur’an criticizes.

Al-Insān 76:1 uses dahr in the Qur’an’s own statement of temporal extension.

The word inherently names neither an independent power nor a doctrine denying the Hereafter.

Lexically, dahr is time and its extended passage. Attributing events to it concerns the speaker’s belief and manner of expression.

Neither Qur’anic occurrence makes al-dahr a name or description of God.

4. Lexical forms and changing usage

Only the noun al-dahr occurs in the Qur’an, but classical Arabic has other forms.

Dahr is singular; adhur and duhūr are recorded plurals.

Dahrun dāhirun is an emphatic expression also explained in the sources through time’s severity; its meaning requires context. Dahra al-dāhirīna expresses very long or perpetual duration. Neither expression independently specifies a numerical length.

Daharahu amrun means that an event, particularly an unwelcome one, befell someone.

Some dictionaries record mudāharah and dihār for dealings, contracts, or hiring over an extended period. They belong to the temporal branch, not destruction.

Dahārīr also appears in expressions such as dahra al-dahārīr and can, contextually, concern remote ages or time’s adversities and changes.

These forms illuminate Arabic’s scope, but none except al-dahr occurs in the Qur’an.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: only two definite nouns

The word dahrī

Some lexical vocalizations use duhrī, with ḍammah on dāl, for an elderly person: someone through whom much time has passed. Asās al-Balāghah and Tāj al-ʿArūs distinguish this from doctrinal dahrī, with fatḥah.

In later terminology, dahrī denotes someone attributing events to time and denying the Hereafter. Some sources also record the ḍammah vocalization for this doctrinal meaning, so the distinction is not exceptionless across all sources.

Lisān al-ʿArab calls the doctrinal use muwallad: a usage developing or becoming current later.

Lexical dahr should therefore be distinguished from the theological label Dahriyyah.

Al-Jāthiyah 45:24 quotes people attributing destruction exclusively to dahr, but does not itself call them Dahriyyah.

Their reported belief may resemble later views, without importing whole subsequent philosophical systems, classifications, or historical labels into the verse’s direct meaning.

Asās al-Balāghah, Lisān al-ʿArab and Tāj al-ʿArūs: duhrī/dahrī and later usage

5. Comparison with related roots and words

D H R (dahr) and Ḥ Y N (ḥīn)

Ḥīn denotes a time or period whose bounds depend on context.

It may be short or long; the word alone supplies no fixed length.

Al-Insān 76:1 clarifies their relationship through ḥīnun mina al-dahri: a period or segment within time’s expanse.

Here dahr is the greater expanse, and ḥīn identifies part of it.

This does not establish that ḥīn is always shorter than dahr in every Arabic use. The distinction is clear in this construction, without being a universal rule.

Study of Ḥ Y N

D H R (dahr) and ʾ B D (abad)

Qur’anic abad, particularly abadan, expresses continuation throughout the relevant future.

Dahr principally names time’s expanse. It can concern the past, present life, or an extended period, without necessarily expressing that same future continuation.

Al-Insān 76:1 concerns past time, whereas Qur’anic abadan generally describes the future continuation of an action, state, or ruling.

This does not mean that dahr never concerns very long or unlimited duration. Literary expressions can describe its endurance, with that meaning arising from construction and context.

Their temporal fields overlap without complete synonymy.

Study of ʾ B D

D H R (dahr) and ʾ M D (amad)

Amad concerns a matter’s duration or temporal limit, frequently emphasizing elapsed time or a relevant interval.

Al-Ḥadīd 57:16, for example, describes amad becoming long.

The focus is on an extended duration becoming lengthy, whereas dahr more generally names time and its expanse.

Dahr can supply events’ temporal background, while amad more often determines or describes a state’s duration or an interval.

This distinction is likewise relative and contextual, rather than an absolute separation.

Study of ʾ M D

D H R (dahr) and H L K (halaka)

Al-Jāthiyah 45:24 places dahr alongside yuhlikunā.

H L K concerns perishing, disappearance, and the end of existence or effectiveness; D H R denotes time and its expanse.

The speakers attribute destruction to dahr. Their co-occurrence does not make destruction part of dahr’s lexical meaning.

Nor does yuhlikunā establish that Arabic always conceived dahr as a destructive being.

This distinction preserves the boundaries among word meaning, sentence construction, and speakers’ beliefs.

6. Translation proposals for Forqan

Appropriate dahr equivalents include “time,” “the passage of ages,” and sometimes “a long time.”

Persian rūzgār is useful because it can concern passing time, a period of life, and its changes. It should nevertheless not automatically introduce misfortune, fate, or a ruling force into every sentence.

In Al-Jāthiyah 45:24, the expression is:

وَمَا يُهْلِكُنَا إِلَّا الدَّهْرُ

wa-mā yuhlikunā illā al-dahru

An appropriate rendering is:

“And nothing destroys us except time.”

This retains the restriction and the attribution of destruction to dahr as stated in the speakers’ words.

“Nothing destroys us except time’s natural passage and the laws of the universe” adds interpretation: “the laws of the universe” does not appear in the Arabic.

Translating dahr as God here is also incorrect. The verse quotes people attributing destruction to time rather than naming God al-dahr.

In Al-Insān 76:1, the expression is:

حِينٌ مِنَ الدَّهْرِ

ḥīnun mina al-dahri

It can be rendered “a period of time” or “an interval within time.”

Persian “a time out of the ages” retains min’s construction and avoids imposing an unspecified fixed measure.

“A thousand years,” “infinite time,” or “the whole history of the world” has no basis in the expression itself.

7. Conclusion

Arabic D H R concerns extended time and the passage of ages. Dahr may describe a long part of life, the world’s temporal expanse, or a period during which events occur.

Early Arabic places it alongside life, death, aging, and changing fortunes, sometimes personifying time as an agent. Related verbs also concern events and afflictions befalling people. These images alone do not establish overpowering or destruction as the word’s original meaning.

Al-dahr occurs twice in the Qur’an. Al-Insān 76:1 denotes temporal extension; Al-Jāthiyah 45:24 quotes people attributing human destruction exclusively to time, a claim the Qur’an presents as unsupported by knowledge.

Brief definition: D H R principally denotes extended time and the passage of ages; related uses can concern changes and events experienced over time. Brief root meaning: Time and its extended passage, sometimes a long period; in related uses, events befalling and overpowering people.

Dahr should therefore not invariably be equated with fate, death, overpowering, or a destructive force. Its basic meaning is time; assigning time power or agency is a separate matter requiring each statement’s construction and context.
