Pearls, radiance, and the expression of beauty in Arabic and the Qur’an

1. Introducing the root and its basic meaning

The four-letter root L A L A has two recognized semantic branches in Arabic: لُؤْلُؤ (luʾluʾ), meaning pearls, and verbs such as لَأْلَأَ (laʾlaʾa) and تَلَأْلَأَ (talaʾlaʾa), meaning to shine, sparkle, or radiate.

A pearl is a gem that forms in the soft tissue of certain shelled mollusks in saltwater or freshwater. It has long been known for its beauty, surface luster, and ornamental value. In Arabic, لُؤْلُؤ names this particular gem; it is not a name for every shining object or precious stone.

Gemological Institute of America: saltwater and freshwater pearls

In the Qur’an, the word appears both for adornment in the Garden and in similes expressing beauty, clarity, and an appealing appearance. One verse also mentions pearls as something that comes out of the sea.

The basic meaning of لُؤْلُؤ remains constant in all these uses: pearls. The verses differ in whether the gem itself is being discussed or its familiar qualities are used to describe something else.

Important forms of the root

لُؤْلُؤ (luʾluʾ): pearls; a generic noun naming the gem, not restricted to a single pearl. This is the only lexical form of the family that occurs in the Qur’an.

لُؤْلُؤَة (luʾluʾah): one pearl; the Arabic countable singular. This form does not occur in the Qur’an.

لَآلِئ (laʾāliʾ): pearls; a recognized Arabic plural of لُؤْلُؤَة. This form does not occur in the Qur’an either.

لَأْلَأَ (laʾlaʾa): shone, sparkled, or radiated. In some early uses it also describes moving a tail or making the eyes flash.

تَلَأْلَأَ (talaʾlaʾa): to shine or radiate, particularly of a star, the moon, lightning, fire, or a face.

تَلَأْلُؤ (talaʾluʾ): shining, sparkling, or radiance.

لَأْلَاء (laʾlāʾ): light or radiance, such as the light of a lamp.

Sultan Qaboos Encyclopedia of Arab Names: laʾlāʾ

لُؤْلُؤِيّ (luʾluʾiyy): pearly; something associated with pearls or resembling them in color and appearance.

لُؤْلُؤ occurs six times in the Qur’an: Al-Ḥajj 22:23, Fāṭir 35:33, Al-Ṭūr 52:24, Al-Raḥmān 55:22, Al-Wāqiʿah 56:23, and Al-Insān 76:19. The differences between لُؤْلُؤٌ, لُؤْلُؤًا, اللُّؤْلُؤُ, and اللُّؤْلُؤِ are grammatical and case differences of the same noun, not independent derivatives with separate meanings.

Quranic Arabic Corpus: six nominal occurrences

2. The semantic field in early Arabic

Pearls and radiance

Arabic dictionaries explain لُؤْلُؤَة through دُرَّة (durrah), a pearl. In the same lexical family, تَلَأْلَأَ النَّجْمُ means that a star shone, while تَلَأْلَأَتِ النَّارُ describes a fire shining or blazing.

These uses show that radiance is an important semantic connection within the family. Nevertheless, not all its meanings should be reduced to a single image.

For example, early lexical evidence records لَأْلَأَ الثَّوْرُ بِذَنَبِهِ as moving the tail of a wild bull, and the same usage is recorded for a gazelle. Here the subject is tail movement. Its inclusion in the same lexical family as verbs of shining does not establish that every form of the root means “the movement of light.”

Conversely, the resemblance between a pearl and a shining object does not establish that the noun لُؤْلُؤ was necessarily derived from the verb تَلَأْلَأَ. Their association within one lexical family is clear, but establishing which historically came first requires independent evidence.

Lisān al-ʿArab and Al-Muḥkam: pearls, radiance, and tail movement

A witness from pre-Islamic poetry

In the famous Muʿallaqah of Ṭarafah ibn al-ʿAbd, the poet mentions pearls alongside zabarjad when describing the beauty of his beloved:

وَفِي الْحَيِّ أَحْوَى يَنْفُضُ الْمَرْدَ شَادِنٌ
مُظَاهِرُ سِمْطَيْ لُؤْلُؤٍ وَزَبَرْجَدِ

wa-fī al-ḥayyi aḥwā yanfuḍu al-marda shādinun / muẓāhiru simṭay luʾluʾin wa-zabarjadi

Explanatory translation: “Among the tribe is a gazelle-eyed beloved, like a young gazelle shaking the fruits of the arāk tree; wearing two strings of pearls and zabarjad one over the other.”

In this poetic image, pearls are a familiar ornamental gem. The expression سِمْطَيْ لُؤْلُؤٍ وَزَبَرْجَدِ shows that pearls had a place in adornment and jewelry-making.

The significance of this witness is that pearls already appear as adornment in Arabic poetry before the Qur’an. When the Qur’an speaks of pearls as adornment in the Garden, it therefore uses neither an unknown word nor a meaning newly invented for that word.

Ibn al-Anbārī, Sharḥ al-Qaṣāʾid al-Sabʿ: Ṭarafah’s witness and two necklaces

Early dictionaries also record لَأْآء and لَأْآل for a pearl owner or dealer. Lexicographers report disagreements about their exact forms and morphological regularity. These expressions show that pearls were associated not only with literary beauty but also with trade and recognized occupations.

Lisān al-ʿArab: terms for a pearl owner or dealer

3. Qur’anic uses of the root

A. Pearls as a gift from the sea

Al-Raḥmān 55:22

يَخْرُجُ مِنْهُمَا اللُّؤْلُؤُ وَالْمَرْجَانُ

yakhruju minhumā al-luʾluʾu wa-l-marjānu

Suggested translation: “From the two of them come pearls and marjān.”

The verse follows discussion of two seas and a barrier between them. Here لُؤْلُؤ names an actual gem associated with the aquatic world.

مَرْجَان (marjān) appears alongside it. The two words are coordinated by the conjunction وَ, but this coordination alone does not determine the biological or mineral nature of either.

Some early explanations understand pearls and marjān as large and small pearls, while in other uses marjān denotes a different ornamental material. Establishing the precise range of marjān belongs to a separate study of that word. The disagreement does not imply that لُؤْلُؤ means anything other than pearls here.

مِنْهُمَا refers back to the two seas mentioned earlier. This grammatical relationship must be examined in the study of Al-Raḥmān 55:19, Al-Raḥmān 55:20, Al-Raḥmān 55:21, and Al-Raḥmān 55:22 themselves. The lexical meaning of لُؤْلُؤ alone does not establish that every kind of pearl must form in both types of water or precisely how pearls develop.

Alongside the other signs in nature, pearls appear in this verse as an observable gift of creation.

B. Pearls as adornment

Al-Ḥajj 22:23

إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُدْخِلُ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا وَعَمِلُوا الصَّالِحَاتِ جَنَّاتٍ تَجْرِي مِنْ تَحْتِهَا الْأَنْهَارُ يُحَلَّوْنَ فِيهَا مِنْ أَسَاوِرَ مِنْ ذَهَبٍ وَلُؤْلُؤًا وَلِبَاسُهُمْ فِيهَا حَرِيرٌ

inna allāha yudkhilu alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti jannātin tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhāru yuḥallawna fīhā min asāwira min dhahabin wa-luʾluʾan wa-libāsuhum fīhā ḥarīrun

Suggested translation: “Indeed, The God admits those who have believed and done righteous deeds into gardens beneath which rivers flow. There they are adorned with bracelets of gold and with pearls, and their clothing there is silk.”

The verse places pearls alongside gold and silk in an image of adornment and enjoyment in the Garden.

The verb يُحَلَّوْنَ does not belong to the root Ḥ L W, but to the family Ḥ L Y and the concept of jewelry and adornment. Being adorned therefore comes from this verb and the sentence structure, not from the word لُؤْلُؤ itself.

Al-Muḥkam: ḥaly, jewelry, and adornment

In مِنْ أَسَاوِرَ مِنْ ذَهَبٍ وَلُؤْلُؤًا, pearls are one element of adornment. The noun pearl alone does not establish how the gem is fitted into jewelry, how it is made, or the exact arrangement of the adornment.

The same construction also occurs in Fāṭir 35:33. That verse speaks of gardens of enduring residence whose entrants are adorned with bracelets of gold and pearls, and whose clothing is silk.

The repetition of this image in two surahs shows that pearls, like gold, are a valuable ornamental material in Qur’anic language. Nevertheless, لُؤْلُؤ does not become a general word for wealth or blessing; those concepts arise from the place of pearls in the context.

In the reading used by Forqan, وَلُؤْلُؤًا is accusative in both verses. The genitive reading وَلُؤْلُؤٍ is also transmitted among the recognized readings. Analyses of the accusative include coordination with the syntactic position of مِنْ أَسَاوِرَ or an understood verb, as in وَيُحَلَّوْنَ لُؤْلُؤًا. In the genitive reading too, the noun is related to the elements of the adornment phrase. The case difference does not change the noun’s meaning of pearls.

Majmaʿ al-Bayān: accusative and genitive readings of luʾluʾ

C. Protected pearls: an image of clarity and beauty

Al-Ṭūr 52:24

وَيَطُوفُ عَلَيْهِمْ غِلْمَانٌ لَهُمْ كَأَنَّهُمْ لُؤْلُؤٌ مَكْنُونٌ

wa-yaṭūfu ʿalayhim ghilmānun lahum ka-annahum luʾluʾun maknūnun

Suggested translation: “And boys of theirs circulate around them, as though they were protected pearls.”

Here pearls appear in a simile rather than as jewelry.

كَأَنَّهُمْ indicates a comparison. The boys mentioned are not pearls; in the verse’s image, they are likened to protected pearls.

The adjective مَكْنُون (maknūn) belongs to K N N and conveys being covered, kept, or protected. It makes the appearance of the pearls in the simile more specific.

A pearl kept safe from damage and dirt can exemplify clarity, outward cleanliness, and appealing beauty. These qualities are understood from the combination of pearls with maknūn and the setting of the simile.

Al-Wāqiʿah 56:22 and Al-Wāqiʿah 56:23

وَحُورٌ عِينٌ

wa-ḥūrun ʿīnun

كَأَمْثَالِ اللُّؤْلُؤِ الْمَكْنُونِ

ka-amthāli al-luʾluʾi al-maknūni

Suggested translation: “And wide-eyed ḥūr, like protected pearls.”

In these two verses too, protected pearls provide a comparison for beauty.

كَأَمْثَالِ expresses likeness. الْمَكْنُون adds the same description of being protected and kept safe.

The similarity between the uses in Al-Ṭūr and Al-Wāqiʿah is clear, although the subjects of the comparisons differ. In Al-Ṭūr, ghilmān are compared to protected pearls; in Al-Wāqiʿah, the subject is ḥūr ʿīn.

This difference shows that the Qur’anic image of pearls is not confined to a single group. Pearls provide a familiar example of beauty and clarity in different similes.

At the same time, maknūn is not the meaning of pearl itself. If this adjective were removed, لُؤْلُؤ would still mean pearls, but the resulting image would no longer contain all the qualities of اللُّؤْلُؤِ الْمَكْنُونِ.

D. Scattered pearls: a different image of beauty

Al-Insān 76:19

وَيَطُوفُ عَلَيْهِمْ وِلْدَانٌ مُخَلَّدُونَ إِذَا رَأَيْتَهُمْ حَسِبْتَهُمْ لُؤْلُؤًا مَنْثُورًا

wa-yaṭūfu ʿalayhim wildānun mukhalladūna idhā raʾaytahum ḥasibtahum luʾluʾan manthūran

Suggested translation: “And enduring boys circulate around them; when you see them, you think them scattered pearls.”

In this verse, pearls have the adjective مَنْثُور (manthūr), rather than مَكْنُون.

Manthūr belongs to N TH R and means scattered. لُؤْلُؤًا مَنْثُورًا creates an image of pearls scattered in different places, each displaying its own appeal.

The image suits the boys moving around the inhabitants of the Garden. The scattered pearls can also suggest their multiplicity and presence in different places.

Comparison with Al-Ṭūr 52:24 is significant:

In Al-Ṭūr, لُؤْلُؤٌ مَكْنُونٌ presents protected pearls.

In Al-Insān, لُؤْلُؤًا مَنْثُورًا presents scattered pearls.

Pearls remain the core of both similes, but the different adjectives create distinct images. Maknūn indicates protection, while manthūr indicates scattering.

This distinction should not be lost by translating both expressions merely as “beautiful and radiant beings.”

4. The root’s meaning and its accompanying words and descriptions

In the Qur’an, L A L A does not comprise an extensive set of different verbs and nouns. Nevertheless, studying it is important for distinguishing the meaning of the word itself from that of a phrase.

First, لُؤْلُؤ must be distinguished from تَلَأْلُؤ. Both are recorded within one lexical family, but one names a gem and the other is the verbal noun of shining. Translating Qur’anic لُؤْلُؤ as radiance would replace the gem’s name with one of its associated concepts.

Second, لُؤْلُؤ is not identical to مَرْجَان. Although some early lexical reports understand marjān as small pearls, this disagreement over the referent of marjān does not erase the clear meaning of لُؤْلُؤ. A separate study of marjān must also examine its other uses, including Al-Raḥmān 55:58.

Third, دُرّ (durr) is an Arabic word close in meaning to pearls and is sometimes used in dictionaries to explain لُؤْلُؤ. The Persian word gawhar, however, is broader and can include various precious stones and materials. For precise Qur’anic translation, marwārīd, meaning pearl, is therefore more specific than gawhar, meaning gem.

Fourth, the adjectives مَكْنُون and مَنْثُور do not belong to L A L A. They change the image without giving the noun pearl a new meaning.

Furthermore, the ornamental value of pearls does not mean that the word inherently conveys a moral judgment. Moral purity, innocence, piety, or spiritual rank cannot be derived from لُؤْلُؤ itself. If such concepts arise in a context, their basis must be sought in the structure and content of that particular verse.

5. Translation proposals for Forqan

The basic Persian and Dari equivalent of لُؤْلُؤ is marwārīd: pearl or pearls.

This equivalent can be retained in all six Qur’anic uses; there is no need to invent a separate new meaning for each verse.

Suitable translations of the Qur’anic phrases are:

  • اللُّؤْلُؤُ وَالْمَرْجَانُ: pearls and marjān.
  • وَلُؤْلُؤًا: and pearls; depending on the sentence, “and with pearls.”
  • لُؤْلُؤٌ مَكْنُونٌ: protected pearls or pearls kept safe.
  • اللُّؤْلُؤِ الْمَكْنُونِ: protected pearls.
  • لُؤْلُؤًا مَنْثُورًا: scattered pearls.

In translating the similes, a plural equivalent can be used for natural Persian, since Arabic لُؤْلُؤ is a generic noun and is not necessarily restricted to one pearl.

For example, كَأَنَّهُمْ لُؤْلُؤٌ مَكْنُونٌ can be translated as “as though they were protected pearls,” without imposing an additional meaning on the word itself.

Radiant, clear, beautiful, or precious may describe the qualities of pearls, but none is a fixed substitute for pearls in the basic translation.

6. Conclusion

The four-letter root L A L A is associated in Arabic with pearls and radiance. The noun لُؤْلُؤ names pearls, while verbs such as لَأْلَأَ and تَلَأْلَأَ have the lexical meanings of shining and sparkling.

The Qur’an uses only the noun لُؤْلُؤ from this family. One verse mentions pearls as a gift from the sea, two include them among the adornments of the Garden, and three use them in similes of beauty and appealing appearance.

The differences between these uses arise from context and accompanying words, not from a fundamental change in the meaning of لُؤْلُؤ. Pearls remain pearls in all of them: sometimes the gem itself is discussed, and sometimes its clarity and beauty provide a comparison.

A short definition suitable for Forqan is therefore:

Brief root meaning: Pearls: gems known for their beauty and luster, used in the Qur’an both as adornment and a natural gift, and in similes concerning beauty.
