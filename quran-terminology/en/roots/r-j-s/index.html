Defilement and impurity, objectionable conduct, inner corruption and its consequences: tangible and intangible uncleanness in Arabic and the Qur’an

In Arabic, R J S concerns defilement, impurity, and something regarded as unclean or objectionable. Depending on usage, this may concern tangible material, objectionable behavior, a person’s moral and religious state, or an adverse, punishing consequence.

For example, Al-Anʿām 6:145 calls pork رِجْس; Al-Māʾidah 5:90 describes khamr, maysir, anṣāb, and azlām as رِجْسٌ مِنْ عَمَلِ الشَّيْطَانِ; Al-Tawbah 9:95 describes a group engaged in wrongdoing as رِجْس; and Al-Aḥzāb 33:33 speaks of removing rijs and purifying the household.

These uses show that Qur’anic rijs is not restricted to bodily impurity. Nor can it be reduced simply to sin or spiritual defilement: its use concerning food illustrates part of the word’s tangible range.

Early Arabic R J S also has uses involving loud thunder, camels’ calls, disturbance, and intermixture. One etymological analysis treats intermixture as central, while dirt and defilement are principal established meanings of the noun رِجْس.

Three matters must therefore be distinguished: the word’s attested Arabic meaning, its particular use in each verse, and the rulings or messages conveyed by the complete verse.

1. Qur’anic frequency and forms

R J S occurs ten times in nine verses across seven surahs. Every occurrence is the noun رِجْس; no verb derived from this root occurs in the Qur’an.

VerseQur’anic formSubject
Al-Māʾidah 5:90رِجْسٌKhamr, maysir, anṣāb, and azlām
Al-Anʿām 6:125الرِّجْسَA consequence of not believing
Al-Anʿām 6:145رِجْسٌPork
Al-Aʿrāf 7:71رِجْسٌA consequence and divine anger upon ʿĀd
Al-Tawbah 9:95رِجْسٌDescribing a group engaged in wrongdoing
Al-Tawbah 9:125رِجْسًاAdded defilement
Al-Tawbah 9:125رِجْسِهِمْTheir existing defilement
Yūnus 10:100الرِّجْسَA consequence of not reasoning
Al-Ḥajj 22:30الرِّجْسَIdol-related impurity
Al-Aḥzāb 33:33الرِّجْسَDefilement to be removed

The repetition of two forms of the same root in Al-Tawbah 9:125 matters:

فَزَادَتْهُمْ رِجْسًا إِلَىٰ رِجْسِهِمْ

fa-zādathum rijsan ilā rijsihim

“So it added defilement to their defilement.”

One occurrence denotes added defilement and the other their existing defilement. This repetition contributes to the sentence’s meaning and must not be omitted when counting or explaining the verse.

All ten Qur’anic occurrences have the nominal reading رِجْس, with kasrah on rāʾ. This must be distinguished from رَجْس, with fatḥah, also attested for loud sound.

2. Basic meaning in early Arabic dictionaries

Defilement and what is regarded as objectionable

An Arabic explanation of رِجْس says:

كُلُّ شَيْءٍ يُسْتَقْذَرُ فَهُوَ رِجْسٌ

kullu shayʾin yustaqdharu fa-huwa rijsun

That is: “Everything regarded as unclean or foul is rijs.”

Another definition is:

الرِّجْسُ: الْقَذَرُ، وَقِيلَ: الشَّيْءُ الْقَذِرُ.

al-rijsu: al-qadharu, wa-qīla: al-shayʾu al-qadhir

That is: “Rijs is dirt; alternatively, the dirty thing itself.”

The definitions suggest a subtle distinction. Rijs sometimes names defilement or impurity, and sometimes the thing characterized by it.

One may speak of a contaminated substance, but also call conduct foul because it is morally objectionable.

In the second use, the behavior need not have a color, odor, moisture, or microbial contamination. The impurity concerns values and morality.

One lexical classification distinguishes what is objectionable to natural disposition, reason, or religious judgment. Examples involving food and prohibited conduct clarify the distinction.

This is an analysis of types of objectionableness; it does not mean that the Qur’an expressly names one of these three headings in each use of rijs.

Does rijs always mean tangible impurity?

No.

The Qur’an also associates rijs with idol worship, conduct involving khamr and maysir, a person’s inner state, and consequences of not believing.

In particular, Al-Tawbah 9:125 describes defilement added to defilement in a context concerning the heart’s state and responses to revelation.

This increase cannot be explained through dirty clothes, contaminated water, or the transfer of an impure substance.

Al-Anʿām 6:145, however, also shows rijs used concerning food.

A suitable definition must therefore retain the word’s actual breadth: rijs may denote tangible defilement or moral, behavioral, and religious foulness. Context determines the particular meaning.

R J S and intermixture in an etymological analysis

One etymological analysis expresses the family’s foundational meaning as follows:

الرَّاءُ وَالْجِيمُ وَالسِّينُ أَصْلٌ يَدُلُّ عَلَى اخْتِلَاطٍ.

al-rāʾu wa-al-jīmu wa-al-sīnu aṣlun yadullu ʿalā ikhtilāṭ

That is: “Rāʾ, jīm, and sīn form a root indicating intermixture.”

In this analysis, مَرْجُوسَة describes a group whose affairs have become mixed up and disordered.

Thunder and camels’ calls are also examined within the family, while rijs meaning defilement is explained through the admixture of something contaminating.

The analysis is significant because it proposes a semantic connection among disorder, confused sound, and defilement.

Caution is needed, however. Intermixture is not inherently objectionable: water and soil may mix, useful materials may combine, or people may assemble for good without the situation being called rijs.

Not every instance of mixing in Arabic is therefore rijs.

Nor can it be established without historical evidence that the meaning of defilement necessarily arose in every form from thunder or disorder.

Intermixture is best treated as one etymological analysis, while defilement and impurity are the established direct meanings of رِجْس in Qur’anic usage.

3. Principal Arabic forms and derivatives

رِجْس: defilement and impurity

رِجْس, with kasrah on rāʾ, is the established noun for defilement or something regarded as foul and objectionable.

In some settings, it also denotes objectionable conduct, disobedience, disbelief, or punishment.

These meanings are attested in early dictionaries, but they must not replace one another without contextual support in every sentence.

The recorded plural of رِجْس is أَرْجَاس.

That plural does not occur in the Qur’an.

رَجُسَ يَرْجُسُ: becoming unclean or foul

رَجُسَ describes something or someone becoming unclean or foul.

The verbal noun رَجَاسَة also denotes impurity and foulness.

رَجُسَ الشَّيْءُ is attested in Arabic with this meaning.

رَجِسَ and رَجَسَ also occur in some uses for objectionable conduct or a state of defilement.

The verb’s vowels matter: one reading may be closer to the impurity branch, another to the sound-related uses.

رَجِس and مَرْجُوس: descriptions of impurity

رَجِس describes a person or thing regarded as unclean.

مَرْجُوس is also attested within this semantic field.

In رَجُلٌ رِجْسٌ, rijs can characterize a person emphatically.

This construction does not establish that the person has physically turned into contaminated material.

Where the description concerns behavior, belief, or morality, its meaning must be understood in that setting.

رَجْس: loud sound

رَجْس, with fatḥah on rāʾ, is recorded in early dictionaries for loud thunder and camels’ calls.

رَجْس describes the loud sound of thunder and a camel’s rumbling call.

Sound and its intensity are prominent here, rather than moral or bodily impurity.

Shared root letters alone therefore do not make رِجْس in Al-Māʾidah 5:90 mean loud sound or vibration.

رَجَسَتِ السَّمَاءُ and اِرْتَجَسَتْ

An Arabic expression is:

رَجَسَتِ السَّمَاءُ

rajasati al-samāʾu

That is, the sky rumbled, or thunder sounded from it.

اِرْتَجَسَتْ is also attested within this field of sound and disturbance.

By extension, irtijās can denote shaking and strong movement accompanied by sound.

The Arabic family is thus not confined to defilement, although its sound-related branch is not directly present in the Qur’an.

رَاجِس, رَجَّاس, and رَجْسَاء

In thunder-related usage, رَاجِس describes a cloud or something that rumbles.

رَجَّاس also describes a thundering cloud or a camel with a loud rumbling call.

نَاقَةٌ رَجْسَاءُ الْحَنِينِ describes a she-camel whose yearning call is sustained and repeated.

Sound in this branch can thus be not only intense but successive.

Repetition of sound must not become an essential meaning of Qur’anic rijs, however.

مَرْجُوسَة: confusion and intermixture

مَرْجُوسَة and مَرْجُوسَاء are recorded for a group whose affairs become mixed up, confused, or disordered.

This use directly relates to analyzing the root through intermixture.

Qur’anic rijs does not simply mean confusion. Confusion may be a consequence of wrongdoing, but it is not present in every verse.

مِرْجَاس: a stone used in a well

Dictionaries identify مِرْجَاس as a stone tied to a rope and lowered into a well.

It could stir up mud and sediment at the bottom; the muddy water would then be drawn out to clean the well.

Some sources also describe using it to determine water depth.

مِرْدَاس is also recorded for this implement and has been considered the more familiar form by some lexicographers.

The historical connection of مِرْجَاس with R J S is therefore less clear than that of رِجْس, and the variant readings must be retained.

نَرْجِس: the flower’s name

نَرْجِس is the Arabic name for the familiar narcissus flower.

Lexicographers identify it as an Arabicized loanword and offer different morphological analyses.

The presence of R J S within the flower’s name does not establish that it historically shares a root with rijs meaning impurity.

The flower’s name should not be used as evidence for the Qur’anic meaning of defilement.

4. Early Arabic and poetic evidence

Thunder and rumbling clouds

This rajaz is transmitted in lexical discussions of رَجَّاس:

وَكُلُّ رَجَّاسٍ يَسُوقُ الرُّجَّسَا
مِنَ السُّيُولِ وَالسَّحَابِ الْمُرَّسَا

wa-kullu rajjāsin yasūqu al-rujjasā / mina al-suyūli wa-al-saḥābi al-murrasā

Meaning under this reading: “And every loud-rumbling one drives forward [masses] of rumbling torrents and earth-scouring clouds.” The image combines rumbling and movement with the effect of torrents and clouds upon the earth.

Rajjās and rijs here belong to the field of sound and rumbling.

This accords with lexical explanations concerning thunder and loud sound.

One transmission attributes the rajaz to al-ʿAjjāj and reverses the order of torrents and clouds. Attribution and wording should be reported cautiously; this transmission does not supply certain pre-Islamic evidence.

A camel’s rumbling in Ruʾbah ibn al-ʿAjjāj’s poetry

Ruʾbah ibn al-ʿAjjāj says:

بِرَجْسٍ بَخْبَاخِ الْهَدِيرِ الْبَهْبَهِ

bi-rajsin bakhbākhi al-hadīri al-bahbah

The phrase means approximately: “With a heavy, resonant, rumbling call.” The poet describes a camel’s rumbling.

رَجْس here belongs to the field of sound.

Its significance is that the sound-related use extends beyond thunder in the sky to an animal’s call.

Ruʾbah lived in the Umayyad and early Abbasid periods. This is therefore evidence from early post-Islamic Arabic, not pre-Islamic poetry.

Collective confusion in an anonymous rajaz

The following rajaz is transmitted under مَرْجُوسَة:

نَحْنُ صَبَّحْنَا عَسْكَرَ الْمَرْجُوسِ
بِذَاتِ خَالٍ لَيْلَةَ الْخَمِيسِ

naḥnu ṣabbaḥnā ʿaskara al-marjūsi / bi-dhāti khālin laylata al-khamīs

Meaning: “At Dhāt Khāl, on Thursday night, we attacked the army of al-Marjūs.” This transmission alone does not establish the precise identities or location.

Al-marjūs here has been connected with confusion and intermixture.

The witness is cited to explain a group whose affairs become mixed up and whose ordinary order breaks down.

The poet and date are not identified in the transmission examined. The historical details of the battle or event cannot therefore be reconstructed from this verse.

The well-stone in anonymous poetry

The following witness is transmitted for مِرْجَاس:

إِذَا رَأَوْا كَرِيهَةً يَرْمُونَ بِي
رَمْيَكَ بِالْمِرْجَاسِ فِي قَعْرِ الطَّوِي

idhā raʾaw karīhatan yarmūna bī / ramyaka bi-al-mirjāsi fī qaʿri al-ṭawī

Meaning: “When they see adversity, they cast me into it, as you cast the mirjās into the depths of a well.”

The image of throwing a stone into a well illustrates the implement-related use of mirjās.

Some versions, however, have مِرْدَاس instead of مِرْجَاس.

The witness must therefore be treated cautiously and cannot alone establish that the implement’s name originally derived from R J S.

Findings from the witnesses

These examples show that sound-related uses, intermixture, and an implement’s name are recorded in Arabic lexical tradition.

This study has not identified a pre-Islamic poetic witness for رِجْس meaning defilement with sufficiently secure attribution and wording to date that meaning’s earliest use.

That gap must not be filled by inventing poetry or assigning Islamic-period witnesses to pre-Islamic poets.

Early lexical transmission provides explicit evidence for defilement and impurity, and the Qur’an itself confirms these meanings in different settings.

5. Qur’anic رِجْس in Al-Māʾidah 5:90

Al-Māʾidah 5:90

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِنَّمَا الْخَمْرُ وَالْمَيْسِرُ وَالْأَنْصَابُ وَالْأَزْلَامُ رِجْسٌ مِنْ عَمَلِ الشَّيْطَانِ فَاجْتَنِبُوهُ لَعَلَّكُمْ تُفْلِحُونَ

yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū innamā al-khamru wa-al-maysiru wa-al-anṣābu wa-al-azlāmu rijsun min ʿamali al-shayṭāni fa-ijtanibūhu laʿallakum tufliḥūn

“You who have believed, khamr, maysir, anṣāb, and azlām are defilement from Satan’s work. So avoid it, so that you may prosper.”

Four different subjects with one description

The verse places four subjects together: khamr, maysir, anṣāb, and azlām.

It then describes all of them as رِجْسٌ مِنْ عَمَلِ الشَّيْطَانِ.

The construction matters because they are not all the same kind of material thing.

Khamr concerns the covering of reason; maysir involves gambling and gain based on chance; anṣāb and azlām belong to the ritual practices, implements, and beliefs familiar at that time.

Taking rijs here exclusively as chemical or microbial contamination, or washable impurity, would not supply a common explanation for all four subjects.

مِنْ عَمَلِ الشَّيْطَانِ clearly foregrounds the objectionable behavioral and practical aspect.

One lexical explanation likewise treats rijs here as a name for something foul and objectionable in conduct.

Does rijs alone mean “forbidden”?

“Forbidden” is one of the explanations recorded for rijs in lexical sources.

Qur’anic study should nevertheless distinguish describing something as objectionable from commanding its avoidance.

Al-Māʾidah 5:90 does not stop at the description rijs, but commands:

فَاجْتَنِبُوهُ

fa-ijtanibūhu

“So avoid it.”

The instruction to avoid it is therefore explicit in this verse; no independent ruling needs to be extracted from the lexical meaning of rijs alone.

Conversely, this method does not permit declaring anything someone finds objectionable or unclean divinely forbidden without Qur’anic evidence.

Forqan’s published article “Halal and Haram in the Qur’an: Divine Legislative Authority and the Danger of Assigning Partners in Religion” also examines this distinction.

Halal and Haram in the Qur’an: Forqan study

Connection with Al-Baqarah 2:219

Al-Baqarah 2:219 says of khamr and maysir:

قُلْ فِيهِمَا إِثْمٌ كَبِيرٌ وَمَنَافِعُ لِلنَّاسِ وَإِثْمُهُمَا أَكْبَرُ مِنْ نَفْعِهِمَا

qul fīhimā ithmun kabīrun wa-manāfiʿu li-al-nāsi wa-ithmuhumā akbaru min nafʿihimā

“Say: ‘In them are great sin and benefits for people, but their sin is greater than their benefit.’”

This verse and Al-Māʾidah 5:90 illuminate each other.

Al-Baqarah sets the morally and practically harmful aspect of khamr and maysir against their benefits.

Al-Māʾidah describes the same two subjects, alongside two others, as rijs min ʿamal al-shayṭān and commands avoidance.

The next verse, Al-Māʾidah 5:91, also connects khamr and maysir with hostility, hatred, and being diverted from remembering The God and ṣalāh.

The defilement is therefore not simply a material property; behavioral and social consequences are present in context too.

Rijs itself does not mean hostility, gambling, intoxication, or forgetting The God. Other words in the verses convey those concepts.

6. فَإِنَّهُ رِجْسٌ in Al-Anʿām 6:145: pork

Al-Anʿām 6:145

قُلْ لَا أَجِدُ فِي مَا أُوحِيَ إِلَيَّ مُحَرَّمًا عَلَىٰ طَاعِمٍ يَطْعَمُهُ إِلَّا أَنْ يَكُونَ مَيْتَةً أَوْ دَمًا مَسْفُوحًا أَوْ لَحْمَ خِنْزِيرٍ فَإِنَّهُ رِجْسٌ أَوْ فِسْقًا أُهِلَّ لِغَيْرِ اللَّهِ بِهِ

qul lā ajidu fī mā ūḥiya ilayya muḥarraman ʿalā ṭāʿimin yaṭʿamuhu illā an yakūna maytatan aw daman masfūḥan aw laḥma khinzīrin fa-innahu rijsun aw fisqan uhilla li-ghayri allāhi bih

“Say: ‘I find nothing in what has been revealed to me forbidden to someone who eats it, except carrion, blood poured out, or pork—for it is foul—or something offered in disobedience over which a name other than The God’s has been invoked.’”

Rijs in the context of food

This is particularly clear evidence of rijs applied to something tangible.

Among the foods named, the Qur’an places فَإِنَّهُ رِجْسٌ after لَحْمَ خِنْزِيرٍ.

In a common reading, the pronoun هُ in فَإِنَّهُ refers to pork. Other exegetical explanations address its referent, the description’s scope, and its relationship to the prohibited items collectively; verbal proximity alone does not settle the entire discussion.

Exegetical explanation of rijs here also includes impurity, objectionableness, and association with disobedience.

Does this verse establish that rijs means a disease agent?

No.

Scientific studies may examine diseases transmitted through contaminated or unprocessed meat. Lexically, however, rijs does not mean a parasite, virus, bacterium, or carcinogen.

The verse does not name a particular scientific cause such as a specific parasite.

The discovery of a biological agent therefore cannot be declared the certain and exclusive reason for calling pork rijs.

Nor does the presence of the same risk factor in another animal’s meat automatically transfer a divine prohibition to that animal.

Food safety and determining religious prohibitions are different subjects. The latter requires reference to the Qur’an’s legislative statements.

Does rijs here establish legal impurity through contact?

The verse concerns طَاعِمٍ يَطْعَمُهُ: someone eating something.

Its explicit subject is therefore consumption.

Calling pork rijs confirms its objectionable character in this context. That description alone does not determine every rule concerning contact, clothing, washing, cooking equipment, or other details of purity.

Such rulings require separate evidence if presented as divine obligations.

Conversely, the breadth of rijs must not be used to ignore the explicit prohibition on eating pork, expressed at the sentence’s beginning through مُحَرَّمًا عَلَىٰ طَاعِمٍ and the subsequent list.

Both the meaning of foulness and the explicit consumption-related ruling must therefore be retained without substituting one for the other.

7. فَاجْتَنِبُوا الرِّجْسَ مِنَ الْأَوْثَانِ: the defilement of idol worship

Al-Ḥajj 22:30

فَاجْتَنِبُوا الرِّجْسَ مِنَ الْأَوْثَانِ وَاجْتَنِبُوا قَوْلَ الزُّورِ

fa-ijtanibū al-rijsa mina al-awthāni wa-ijtanibū qawla al-zūr

“So avoid the defilement associated with idols, and avoid false speech.”

الرِّجْس occurs here with الْأَوْثَان.

Early interpretations offer two related explanations: the idols themselves are objectionable, or the expression concerns defilement resulting from worshipping and following them.

One explanation connects idol-related rijs with worshipping them and following Satan through that behavior.

Are idols physically impure?

The expression alone does not establish such a general ruling.

An idol may be made of stone, wood, metal, or another material. Taking the shape of an idol does not by itself change those materials into different physical substances.

Avoiding idol worship and its associated religious practices is a central contextual concern.

Rijs can therefore denote the objectionableness of idol worship and its consequences without requiring all the idol’s materials to be physically contaminated.

Rijs in relation to قَوْلَ الزُّورِ

The same sentence contains a second command:

وَاجْتَنِبُوا قَوْلَ الزُّورِ

wa-ijtanibū qawla al-zūr

“And avoid false speech.”

This proximity shows two objectionable behaviors or conditions: idol-related defilement and false speech.

Zūr and rijs are not complete synonyms, however.

Zūr belongs to the separate root Z W R and concerns falsehood and departure from truthfulness.

Rijs should therefore not simply be translated “falsehood” here, nor qawl al-zūr turned into “bodily impurity” in its place.

For Qur’anic study, the verse’s relationship to associating partners with The God, rituals, and legislative authority matters. That broader discussion belongs to studying pilgrimage and idol worship, rather than the independent meaning of R J S.

8. Rijs describing people and their inner state

Al-Tawbah 9:95: إِنَّهُمْ رِجْسٌ

Al-Tawbah 9:95

سَيَحْلِفُونَ بِاللَّهِ لَكُمْ إِذَا انْقَلَبْتُمْ إِلَيْهِمْ لِتُعْرِضُوا عَنْهُمْ ۖ فَأَعْرِضُوا عَنْهُمْ ۖ إِنَّهُمْ رِجْسٌ

sa-yaḥlifūna bi-allāhi lakum idhā inqalabtum ilayhim li-tuʿriḍū ʿanhum fa-aʿriḍū ʿanhum innahum rijs

“They will swear to you by The God when you return to them, so that you leave them alone. So turn away from them; they are indeed foul.”

Rijs here characterizes a group of people.

The context before the description concerns their oaths and behavior, while the verse’s continuation states the consequence of their deeds.

The foulness thus concerns their conduct and moral and religious state.

This fits lexical explanations of objectionable conduct and inner impurity.

Does calling people rijs mean their bodies are impure?

No; the verse itself does not establish that conclusion.

When the Qur’an calls a group rijs because of their conduct, this must not be turned without evidence into contamination transferable through physical contact.

The verse itself commands فَأَعْرِضُوا عَنْهُمْ: turn away from them.

The instruction belongs to social relations and their behavior; it should not automatically become a ban on touching their bodies or a declaration that their clothing is impure.

Nor should a description of one group’s wrongdoing become a general judgment that every person with a similar identity or affiliation is inherently foul.

The verse judges a group’s conduct and circumstances in a particular situation.

Al-Tawbah 9:125: defilement that increases

Al-Tawbah 9:125

وَأَمَّا الَّذِينَ فِي قُلُوبِهِمْ مَرَضٌ فَزَادَتْهُمْ رِجْسًا إِلَىٰ رِجْسِهِمْ وَمَاتُوا وَهُمْ كَافِرُونَ

wa-ammā alladhīna fī qulūbihim maraḍun fa-zādathum rijsan ilā rijsihim wa-mātū wa-hum kāfirūn

“But those in whose hearts is disease—it added defilement to their defilement, and they died while disbelieving.”

The preceding verse, Al-Tawbah 9:124, concerns a surah’s revelation and people’s different responses.

For believers, the surah is described as increasing faith.

For those whose hearts contain disease, however, Al-Tawbah 9:125 describes defilement added to their defilement.

The contrast matters.

One message may elicit different responses and consequences in two groups with different inner states.

One exegetical explanation connects rijs with doubt, hypocrisy, and objectionable responses to revelation added to earlier defilement.

Did the Qur’an cause rijs to increase?

Grammatically, the subject of فَزَادَتْهُمْ refers back to the surah discussed in the preceding verse.

That grammatical relationship alone does not establish that the divine message is inherently contaminating.

In the preceding verse, the same surah increases the believers’ faith.

The difference lies in the addressees’ responses and inner states.

For example, someone resisting a truth may respond to fresh evidence by persisting in error rather than revising their view. Encountering evidence can thus bring their moral choice more clearly into view.

This example explains the contextual relationship between the verses; it is not a lexical definition of rijs.

The word itself expresses defilement or impurity; the complete phrase expresses its increase within this group’s state.

9. Rijs as consequence and punishment

Al-Aʿrāf 7:71: rijs and anger upon ʿĀd

Al-Aʿrāf 7:71

قَالَ قَدْ وَقَعَ عَلَيْكُمْ مِنْ رَبِّكُمْ رِجْسٌ وَغَضَبٌ

qāla qad waqaʿa ʿalaykum min rabbikum rijsun wa-ghaḍab

“He said: ‘Rijs and anger from your Lord have indeed fallen upon you.’”

Hūd says this in reply to the people of ʿĀd.

In context, the people have opposed his call. Al-Aʿrāf 7:71 itself also mentions disputing over deities named by them and their ancestors.

Rijs here is neither a food’s name nor a description of a material act. وَقَعَ عَلَيْكُمْ مِنْ رَبِّكُمْ connects it with a consequence falling upon the people.

Early exegetical explanations connect rijs here with punishment, divine anger, and displeasure.

A grammatical point must nevertheless be retained.

The Qur’an says:

رِجْسٌ وَغَضَبٌ

rijsun wa-ghaḍabun

“Defilement or punishment, and anger.”

Two nouns occur side by side with a conjunction.

A precise translation should therefore not erase rijs and reduce both words to one “anger.”

Context can explain rijs as a foul or punishing consequence and ghaḍab as divine anger and displeasure.

The distinction preserves the verse’s construction and avoids imposing an absolute synonym.

Al-Anʿām 6:125: rijs upon those who do not believe

Al-Anʿām 6:125

كَذَٰلِكَ يَجْعَلُ اللَّهُ الرِّجْسَ عَلَى الَّذِينَ لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ

kadhālika yajʿalu allāhu al-rijsa ʿalā alladhīna lā yuʾminūn

“Thus The God places rijs upon those who do not believe.”

The preceding part of the verse concerns the breast being opened to Islam or made tight and constrained for those directed into error.

Rijs therefore belongs here to a situation involving faith, guidance, and error.

Two types of explanation are proposed: rijs as something containing no good, and rijs in connection with error and intrusive suggestion gaining control over someone who turns away from accepting faith.

The word alone cannot identify the precise psychological or external mechanism of this defilement.

Nor should the phrase become a definite theory that people are forcibly contaminated or misguided from the outset without choice, without examining the wider verses on choice, guidance, and responsibility.

This study concerns the meaning of rijs. Divine will in relation to human choice requires a separate investigation.

Yūnus 10:100: rijs and failing to use reason

Yūnus 10:100

وَمَا كَانَ لِنَفْسٍ أَنْ تُؤْمِنَ إِلَّا بِإِذْنِ اللَّهِ ۚ وَيَجْعَلُ الرِّجْسَ عَلَى الَّذِينَ لَا يَعْقِلُونَ

wa-mā kāna li-nafsin an tuʾmina illā bi-idhni allāhi wa-yajʿalu al-rijsa ʿalā alladhīna lā yaʿqilūn

“No soul can believe except by The God’s permission; and He places rijs upon those who do not reason.”

Rijs occurs here with لَا يَعْقِلُونَ.

This is particularly significant: the issue is not simply lacking information or not knowing a fact; يَعْقِلُونَ concerns using reason.

One exegetical explanation connects rijs here with punishment and divine anger.

This does not make rijs mean punishment in every Qur’anic occurrence.

For example, Al-Māʾidah 5:90 calls khamr and maysir rijs, while Al-Aḥzāb 33:33 speaks of removing rijs.

In Yūnus 10:100, a “punishing or foul consequence” is therefore understood through the word’s connection with يَجْعَلُ ... عَلَى and the context of guidance and reasoning.

لَا يَعْقِلُونَ must not be taken to censure every person with lower cognitive ability or an intellectual disability.

The context concerns responses to the signs and using reason, rather than valuing people according to medical conditions or involuntary cognitive characteristics.

10. لِيُذْهِبَ عَنْكُمُ الرِّجْسَ: purifying the household

Al-Aḥzāb 33:33

وَقَرْنَ فِي بُيُوتِكُنَّ وَلَا تَبَرَّجْنَ تَبَرُّجَ الْجَاهِلِيَّةِ الْأُولَىٰ وَأَقِمْنَ الصَّلَاةَ وَآتِينَ الزَّكَاةَ وَأَطِعْنَ اللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُ ۚ إِنَّمَا يُرِيدُ اللَّهُ لِيُذْهِبَ عَنْكُمُ الرِّجْسَ أَهْلَ الْبَيْتِ وَيُطَهِّرَكُمْ تَطْهِيرًا

wa-qarna fī buyūtikunna wa-lā tabarrajna tabarruja al-jāhiliyyati al-ūlā wa-aqimna al-ṣalāta wa-ātīna al-zakāta wa-aṭiʿna allāha wa-rasūlahu innamā yurīdu allāhu li-yudhhiba ʿankumu al-rijsa ahla al-bayti wa-yuṭahhirakum taṭhīrā

“Remain in your homes; do not display yourselves like the display of the former age of ignorance. Establish ṣalāh, give zakāh, and obey The God and His messenger. The God only wishes to remove rijs from you, people of the household, and purify you thoroughly.”

Rijs here stands in contrast with purification.

Grammatically, الرِّجْسَ is the object of يُذْهِبَ, while عَنْكُمُ identifies those from whom it is to be removed.

يُطَهِّرَكُمْ تَطْهِيرًا then emphasizes purifying.

The expressions are closely related: removing defilement and making pure.

Rijs and ṭahr nevertheless belong to different roots and must not be defined in place of one another.

What is rijs in this verse?

Its particular meaning requires attention to context.

Al-Aḥzāb 33:28, Al-Aḥzāb 33:29, Al-Aḥzāb 33:30, Al-Aḥzāb 33:31, Al-Aḥzāb 33:32, Al-Aḥzāb 33:33, and Al-Aḥzāb 33:34 address the prophet’s household and his wives’ responsibilities.

The verse itself contains instructions concerning conduct, avoiding display, establishing ṣalāh, zakāh, and obeying The God and the messenger.

At least in a direct contextual reading, the defilement therefore concerns behavior and moral and religious circumstances.

One exegetical explanation likewise connects rijs with evil, indecency, and defilement resulting from disobedience.

“Impurity” or “defilement” is therefore suitable, while restricting it to bodily dirt does not fit the context.

Does removing rijs mean permanent infallibility?

R J S itself does not supply that meaning.

Idhhāb belongs to DH H B and here denotes removing something.

Taṭhīr belongs to Ṭ H R and concerns making pure.

These words alone do not determine when, under what conditions, or how the purity is realized.

Nor does rijs itself establish that the addressees were protected from every lapse and error from their creation until the end of life.

That claim would require independent, explicit evidence.

Conversely, the emphasis on purification must not be disregarded. The Qur’an foregrounds this purpose through إِنَّمَا يُرِيدُ اللَّهُ and وَيُطَهِّرَكُمْ تَطْهِيرًا.

Lexically, rijs should retain the meaning of impurity and defilement; the full scope of purification and the identification of the household belong to a separate reflection on the verse.

Relation to the published Ṭ H R study

Forqan’s published study of Ṭ H R also examines this verse in the context of the address to the prophet’s household.

That study does not restrict purification to washing the body, but also relates it to behavioral, moral, and social defilement.

This background accords with the lexical evidence for rijs.

The present article should nevertheless not be written merely to validate that reflection. The meaning of rijs must be assessed independently of a chosen interpretation through the full range of Qur’anic and Arabic usage.

11. R J S compared with related roots

R J S and N J S: rijs and najāsah

Najas belongs to the separate root N J S.

That root occurs only once in the Qur’an, in Al-Tawbah 9:28:

إِنَّمَا الْمُشْرِكُونَ نَجَسٌ

innamā al-mushrikūna najasun

“Those who associate partners are indeed unclean.”

Arabic dictionaries treat rijs and najas as synonyms in some uses.

Synonymy in certain settings does not make every occurrence identical.

Qur’anic rijs concerns food, behavior, inner states, and consequences and punishment.

The sole Qur’anic najas describes a group of people, with the rest of the verse stating a restriction on approaching the Sacred Mosque.

Neither word should therefore become, without context, a complete system of legal rulings on bodily impurity.

Moral foulness and impurity transferable through contact are different concepts, although Arabic words may sometimes serve both.

Study of N J S

R J S and R J Z: rijs and rijz

This distinction matters greatly in lexical and Qur’anic study.

R J Z is a separate root.

Qur’anic رِجْز often concerns punishment, as in Al-Baqarah 2:59:

فَأَنْزَلْنَا عَلَى الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا رِجْزًا مِنَ السَّمَاءِ

fa-anzalnā ʿalā alladhīna ẓalamū rijzan mina al-samāʾ

“So We sent punishment down from the sky upon those who wronged.”

Al-Muddaththir 74:5 also says:

وَالرُّجْزَ فَاهْجُرْ

wa-al-rujza fa-uhjur

“And shun rujz.”

In some punishment-related uses, rijs and rijz have been treated as closely related forms or pronunciation variants with sīn and zāy interchanged.

That lexical account does not make every occurrence of the two roots identical.

R J S and R J Z each occur ten times in the Qur’an. Rijz concerns punishment and shunning impurity, while rijs also appears in settings such as food and the heart’s state.

The lexical connections with R J Z should therefore be recorded, while its full analysis belongs to its separate root study.

Study of R J Z

R J S and KH B TH: foulness and objectionableness

Khubth concerns impurity, foulness, and objectionableness in Arabic.

Al-Aʿrāf 7:157 says:

وَيُحَرِّمُ عَلَيْهِمُ الْخَبَائِثَ

wa-yuḥarrimu ʿalayhimu al-khabāʾith

“And He forbids foul things to them.”

Rijs and khabīth can be close in some settings, but neither should be treated as the other’s necessary, invariable meaning.

For example, Al-Māʾidah 5:90 contains an explicit command to avoid.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:157, الْخَبَائِث occurs with يُحَرِّمُ.

Both can express objectionableness, but the complete sentence determines the ruling.

People are not entitled to declare everything objectionable to their own culture or taste divinely forbidden as rijs or khabīth without evidence.

Study of KH B TH

R J S and Ṭ H R: defilement and purification

Many uses of Ṭ H R contrast with defilement and uncleanness.

The relationship is explicit in Al-Aḥzāb 33:33:

لِيُذْهِبَ عَنْكُمُ الرِّجْسَ ... وَيُطَهِّرَكُمْ تَطْهِيرًا

li-yudhhiba ʿankumu al-rijsa … wa-yuṭahhirakum taṭhīrā

“To remove defilement from you … and purify you thoroughly.”

Rijs is what is to be removed; taṭhīr is the act of making pure.

Ṭahārah is not confined to removing rijs. The Qur’an also speaks of the purity of water, clothing, places, people, and other states.

Conversely, rijs sometimes concerns punishment or an adverse consequence without an instruction in the same verse to wash or physically cleanse anything.

The opposition between the roots is real but should not become a mechanical relationship without exceptions.

R J S and A TH M: impurity and sin

Ithm belongs to A TH M and concerns sin.

Al-Baqarah 2:219 describes khamr and maysir as containing إِثْمٌ كَبِيرٌ.

Al-Māʾidah 5:90 calls the same two subjects, alongside anṣāb and azlām, rijs.

This association across verses shows that rijs sometimes relates to sin and objectionable conduct.

Ithm and rijs are not complete synonyms: Qur’anic rijs also describes pork and punishment.

Translating every instance of rijs as “sin” would therefore lose part of the word’s actual range.

Study of A TH M

R J S and R K S: defilement and returning to an adverse state

R K S resembles R J S in writing but is a separate root.

Al-Nisāʾ 4:88 says:

وَاللَّهُ أَرْكَسَهُمْ بِمَا كَسَبُوا

wa-allāhu arkasahum bi-mā kasabū

“And The God has returned them to an adverse state because of what they earned.”

Raks concerns returning, overturning, or plunging into an adverse condition.

Rijs, by contrast, names defilement or impurity.

Context may connect defilement with becoming caught in an adverse state, but that association does not make the roots identical.

12. Can one rendering serve every occurrence of rijs?

For direct Persian translation, palīdī, “foulness” or “impurity,” is the most suitable general equivalent.

Ālūdagī, “defilement,” is also natural and defensible in many settings, particularly those concerning the heart and conduct.

These equivalents should not, however, be treated as interchangeable in every verse regardless of context.

Al-Anʿām 6:145 concerns foulness in relation to pork.

Al-Māʾidah 5:90 and Al-Ḥajj 22:30 foreground objectionable acts and conduct.

Al-Tawbah 9:95 and Al-Tawbah 9:125 emphasize moral and religious defilement.

In Al-Aʿrāf 7:71 and Yūnus 10:100, the contextual explanation must also record the consequence- or punishment-related use.

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:33, rijs contrasts with purification.

For Forqan, the best approach is therefore to keep the brief root meaning stable while explaining each word’s contextual meaning separately.

13. Conclusion

Arabic R J S encompasses uses concerning defilement and impurity, loud sound, confusion, and intermixture.

One etymological analysis treats intermixture as the root’s center and explains defilement through it. Other explanations emphasize dirt and what is regarded as unclean or objectionable.

For Qur’anic usage, defilement and impurity have broader, direct support, but must not be restricted to bodily contamination.

The root occurs ten times in nine Qur’anic verses, always as the noun رِجْس.

Its uses cover prohibited food, objectionable conduct and idol worship, moral and religious defilement, increasing inner defilement, punishing consequences, and removing impurity in a purification setting.

This variety does not make the word wholly unrelated from one verse to another. Objectionableness and defilement remain recognizable throughout.

Food, objectionable conduct, the heart’s state, and punishment are nevertheless not a single instance of impurity. Context identifies which aspect is prominent.

Calling something rijs must also be distinguished from declaring it religiously prohibited. Al-Māʾidah 5:90 explicitly commands فَاجْتَنِبُوهُ, and Al-Anʿām 6:145 explicitly prohibits eating. Rijs alone does not authorize new rulings for everything objectionable.

In Al-Aḥzāb 33:33, removing rijs concerns purification, while identifying the household and assessing claims of infallibility must remain separate from lexical root study.

Proposed final definition for Forqan:

In its principal Qur’anic use, R J S denotes defilement and impurity, or something regarded as unclean and objectionable. This may be tangible or concern human behavior, belief, and moral state; in some settings, it also denotes a consequence or punishment. Early Arabic also records uses involving loud sound and confusion. The shared sense of impurity must not turn every occurrence into bodily contamination, sin, punishment, or a prohibition. Each verse’s context determines its particular meaning and implications.
