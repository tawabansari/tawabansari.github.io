Qur’anic Root F L Q (ف ل ق)

Falaq: splitting and opening—from germinating seeds and the parting sea to the breaking dawn

Qur’anic forms

فَالِقُ
(fāliqu)
Active participle of Form I; twice, in Al-Anʿām 6:95 and Al-Anʿām 6:96

فَانْفَلَقَ
(fa-infalaqa)
Form VII past tense; once, in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:63

الْفَلَقِ
(al-falaqi)
Noun; daybreak in the preferred reading; once, in Al-Falaq 113:1

1. The root and its Qur’anic forms

The Arabic root F L Q concerns splitting, opening apart, or a cleft appearing in something. فَلَقَ (falaqa) means “to split,” while اِنْفَلَقَ (infalaqa) means “to split open.” Within the same family, فِلْق (filq) names a portion separated through splitting. فَلَق (falaq), in an established use, denotes daybreak, when light appears on the horizon and night’s darkness begins to withdraw.

The root is not restricted to breaking hard objects. Splitting wood or stone, a seed opening during germination, clefts in the ground, and the appearance of morning light all belong to this family’s field.

The Qur’an describes The God as فَالِقُ الْحَبِّ وَالنَّوَىٰ and فَالِقُ الْإِصْبَاحِ. In Moses’ story the sea فَانْفَلَقَ, “then split apart.” Sūrat Al-Falaq begins by instructing people to seek refuge in رَبِّ الْفَلَقِ.

The connection with splitting and opening is clear, but does not make every occurrence the same kind of physical splitting. The sea parting, a seed opening, and dawn appearing differ in referent and manner.

Qur’anic forms

F L Q occurs four times in four verses, in three morphological forms:

  • فَالِقُ (fāliqu): the active participle of the simple verb falaqa, “splitter” or “opener”; twice, in Al-Anʿām 6:95 and Al-Anʿām 6:96.
  • فَانْفَلَقَ (fa-infalaqa): a past-tense Form VII verb, “then it split apart”; once, in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:63.
  • الْفَلَقِ (al-falaqi): a noun, understood in its established Qur’anic use as daybreak or the light of the beginning of day; once, in Al-Falaq 113:1.

The following citation forms and lexical uses help compare the Qur’anic forms with the wider vocabulary. They should not all be counted as Qur’anic uses: some merely introduce a verb, while some vocalizations occur in the Qur’an in a particular sense.

  • فَلَقَ، يَفْلِقُ (falaqa, yafliqu): “he split” and “he splits”; citation forms of the simple verb, neither of these verbal forms itself occurring in the Qur’an.
  • فَلْق (falq): splitting, the verbal noun, with an unvowelled lām.
  • فَلَق (falaq): daybreak; this same noun occurs with the definite article in Al-Falaq 113:1. A cleft or depression in the ground is another lexical use, but is not its attested Qur’anic meaning.
  • فِلْق / فِلْقَة (filq / filqah): a separated portion or fragment of something split.
  • فُلُوق (fulūq): clefts or cracks.
  • تَفَلَّقَ (tafallaqa): to split open or divide into several portions.
  • فَلَّقَ (fallaqa): to split, in a formation that may emphasize intensity or repetition.
  • فَلِيق (falīq): in some uses, something or a place with a cleft, including a designation for part of a camel’s neck.
  • مِفْلَاق (miflāq): in some lexical sources, a description of an impoverished or base person; it does not occur in the Qur’an.

The distinctions between falq, falaq, and filq should be preserved from the outset. They belong to one family without having the same meaning in every use.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:63 also places فِرْق beside فَانْفَلَقَ. Firq belongs to F R Q, rather than F L Q. This distinction matters when identifying Qur’anic forms.

2. The semantic core and early Arabic usage

Splitting and opening apart

Arabic فَلَقْتُ الشَّيْءَ means “I split the thing,” producing a separation in it.

Pressure or a blow may accomplish this, but the root itself specifies no single tool or method.

Lexical uses include splitting pistachios, pomegranates, eggs, wood, and stone.

Something previously continuous develops a cleft or has portions separated from one another.

Infalaqa expresses becoming split, whereas falaqa can concern the act of splitting and its agent.

The Qur’an likewise calls The God fāliq, but uses fa-infalaqa for the sea.

Filq: a portion of something split

Arabic filq or filqah can name a portion separated from something through splitting.

If a vessel breaks or wood is split lengthwise, for example, a part may be called filqah.

Early bow making also used wood split into two portions. A bow made from such wood could be called قَوْسٌ فِلْقٌ.

The family consequently names not only splitting but also its result or a portion produced by it.

It does not follow that every filqah must be an equal half. Splitting may produce unequal halves or several fragments.

Clefts in the ground and valleys

Lexical sources also record falaq for a cleft, depression, or open space between two elevations.

The shape of the ground matters here. A low space between hills or rocky formations may appear as a passage or hollow.

A line attributed to Aws ibn Ḥajar reads:

وَبِالْأُدْمِ تُحْدَىٰ عَلَيْهَا الرِّحَالُ
وَبِالشَّوْلِ فِي الْفَلَقِ الْعَاشِبِ

“And [he honors his friend] with pale camels bearing travelling saddles and being driven onward, and with she-camels yielding little milk in the grassy hollow.”

The line continues a description of Faḍālah’s generosity to a friend; يَحْبُو in the preceding line supplies the act of giving. الشَّوْل describes she-camels whose milk has diminished and whose udders have contracted. الْفَلَقِ الْعَاشِبِ here denotes a hollow or cleft in the ground where vegetation grows.

ʿĀshib supplies the vegetation covering the place. Vegetation is not part of falaq itself.

Aws ibn Ḥajar is a pre-Islamic poet, and lexical sources attribute this line to him. As with other early poetic witnesses, its wording and attribution should nevertheless remain distinct from the precise dating of a word’s use.

Falaq was thus not confined to daybreak or splitting seeds; it could also name a recognizable formation in the ground.

A use in Zuhayr’s poetry

A line attributed to Zuhayr ibn Abī Sulmā reads:

مَا زِلْتُ أَرْمُقُهُمْ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا هَبَطَتْ
أَيْدِي الرِّكَابِ بِهِمْ مِنْ رَاكِسٍ فَلَقَا

“I kept watching them until the camels’ forelegs bore them down from Rākis into a hollow.”

Falaq here concerns terrain and a caravan’s movement. أَيْدِي الرِّكَابِ literally denotes the riding camels’ forelegs, whose steps depict the caravan’s descent.

Commentaries on the poem explain Rākis as the name of a valley and falaq as low ground between elevations. A basic translation can therefore retain Rākis as a name and render falaqan as “into a hollow.” Treating Falaq as another proper place name, if proposed, requires independent evidence; the word’s form alone does not settle it.

The line is useful for spatial usage, but cannot by itself establish a valley’s precise shape or the origin of its name.

F L Q in the language of plant growth

A seed splitting during germination is a clear use of the family.

A seed or fruit stone initially has a covering that may split as a new part of the plant emerges.

Words from this root also describe the opening of a palm’s spathe around its emerging flower cluster.

This agricultural use directly informs فَالِقُ الْحَبِّ وَالنَّوَىٰ in the Qur’an.

Seed splitting should nevertheless remain distinct from every stage of growth. It may form part of growth, but F L Q itself does not mean the entire process of plant development, fruiting, or harvest ripening.

Falaq: dawn appearing

فَلَقُ الصُّبْحِ is an established use of the family.

It concerns the light of daybreak, when light appears on the horizon and night’s darkness withdraws from that region.

Lexical sources also record أَبْيَنُ مِنْ فَلَقِ الصُّبْحِ: something “clearer than the light of daybreak.”

Splitting darkness here is an image for the appearance of light.

Darkness should not be imagined as a hard object physically split with a tool. Language expresses the observable arrival of morning through opening and darkness drawing aside.

Some poetic transmissions use falaq for dawn. A line attributed to Dhū Al-Rummah, for example, is transmitted with falaq, but Ibn Barrī identifies shafaq as its correct wording.

A word’s presence in one version of a poem is therefore insufficient to establish historical usage without examining wording and context.

The sense of falaq al-ṣubḥ is nevertheless clearly recorded in the Arabic lexical tradition independently of that line.

3. فَالِقُ الْحَبِّ وَالنَّوَىٰ: opening grain and fruit stones for growth

Al-Anʿām 6:95

إِنَّ اللَّهَ فَالِقُ الْحَبِّ وَالنَّوَىٰ ۖ يُخْرِجُ الْحَيَّ مِنَ الْمَيِّتِ وَمُخْرِجُ الْمَيِّتِ مِنَ الْحَيِّ ۚ ذَٰلِكُمُ اللَّهُ ۖ فَأَنَّىٰ تُؤْفَكُونَ

“Indeed, The God is the splitter of grain and fruit stones. He brings the living out of the dead and is the one who brings the dead out of the living. That is The God; how, then, are you turned away from the truth?”

Fāliq is an active participle attributed to The God here.

الْحَبِّ is the genitive complement of فَالِقُ, and النَّوَىٰ is coordinated with it. Splitting is thus applied to two objects, grain and fruit stones.

Grain and fruit stones: two particular referents

Ḥabb here concerns grains and plant seeds.

Nawā concerns fruit stones, especially stones such as those of dates.

Both grain and fruit stones can provide the starting point for a new plant in agriculture.

When conditions for germination are present, a shoot emerges and the covering opens.

Fāliq here consequently concerns splitting grain and fruit stones in connection with a plant’s emergence.

It should not be translated only as “causing to grow,” eliminating the image of splitting.

Causing growth can convey the result or wider process, while fāliq preserves the particular image of opening and splitting.

Splitting and bringing forth

After فَالِقُ الْحَبِّ وَالنَّوَىٰ, the Qur’an says:

يُخْرِجُ الْحَيَّ مِنَ الْمَيِّتِ وَمُخْرِجُ الْمَيِّتِ مِنَ الْحَيِّ.

Alongside the splitting of grain and fruit stones, these clauses present a wider field of beings emerging or being brought forth.

Falq and ikhrāj do not, however, belong to the same root.

Falq concerns splitting and opening apart; ikhrāj concerns bringing out.

The actions can coincide within a natural event: a shoot’s emergence, for example, is connected with the opening of the seed covering.

يُخْرِجُ الْحَيَّ مِنَ الْمَيِّتِ should not be restricted entirely to seed splitting. Its own wording has a wider range and can be studied alongside other verses on life and death.

Nor does the verse establish that grain must be biologically dead. الْحَبِّ وَالنَّوَىٰ and الْحَيَّ مِنَ الْمَيِّتِ are connected expressions, but the precise referents of life and death in the second require separate examination.

Does fāliq here mean Creator?

Some lexical explanations gloss fāliq in this verse as khāliq, “Creator.”

That interpretation is understandable insofar as a seed opening and a plant appearing belong to divine creation and ordering.

Lexically, however, F L Q and KH L Q are not identical.

Khalq has a broader field involving creating, bringing into being, and determining proportions.

Falq here conveys the particular image of splitting.

Rendering فَالِقُ الْحَبِّ وَالنَّوَىٰ solely as “Creator of grain and fruit stones” therefore loses an important part of the verse’s lexical image.

“Splitter of grain and fruit stones” stays closer to the wording; its connection with growth can be explained separately.

The verse’s place in Qur’anic argument

The verse begins with إِنَّ اللَّهَ and ends with ذَٰلِكُمُ اللَّهُ فَأَنَّىٰ تُؤْفَكُونَ.

Splitting grain and fruit stones is therefore more than an agricultural observation. Within the verse, it also serves as a sign for recognizing The God and challenging turning away from truth.

That conclusion about divine oneness follows from the complete verse and context, rather than F L Q’s lexical meaning alone.

The verse does not set out the scientific details of germination, cellular structures, or the chemistry of plant growth.

Its natural image should be preserved without importing modern scientific details into fāliq itself.

4. فَالِقُ الْإِصْبَاحِ: opening morning light

Al-Anʿām 6:96

فَالِقُ الْإِصْبَاحِ وَجَعَلَ اللَّيْلَ سَكَنًا وَالشَّمْسَ وَالْقَمَرَ حُسْبَانًا ۚ ذَٰلِكَ تَقْدِيرُ الْعَزِيزِ الْعَلِيمِ

“He splits open and brings forth morning light, and has made night a time of rest, and the sun and moon a means of reckoning. That is the ordering of the Invincible, the Knowing.”

Fāliq is again attributed to The God.

Unlike the preceding verse, however, its object is الْإِصْبَاحِ, rather than grain or fruit stones.

Fāliq and iṣbāḥ

Iṣbāḥ belongs to Ṣ B Ḥ (ص ب ح), here concerning the beginning of daylight.

Fāliq belongs to F L Q and conveys splitting or opening.

فَالِقُ الْإِصْبَاحِ thus connects two semantic fields: splitting and morning appearing.

In ordinary language, daybreak is when light appears on the horizon and night’s darkness gradually withdraws.

This is expressed through the image of darkness splitting.

Fāliq itself should not be defined as the sun shining. Light and shining follow from the sentence’s subject and context, while F L Q supplies opening and splitting.

Fāliq al-ḥabb and fāliq al-iṣbāḥ

In Al-Anʿām 6:95, splitting applies to grain and fruit stones: things with material coverings that open during germination.

In Al-Anʿām 6:96, splitting applies to morning.

The root can thus occur both in a concrete physical use and as an image describing a natural occurrence.

Their connection does not make darkness a hard object like a seed’s covering.

Nor should seed splitting be made purely symbolic simply because fāliq describes morning in the next verse.

Each verse has its own referent; the shared image operates in two different settings.

Night, sun, and moon

The verse continues by presenting The God as making night a time of rest and the sun and moon a means of reckoning.

Morning is thereby placed among phenomena concerning night, day, and recognizing time.

Rest follows from سَكَنًا and reckoning from حُسْبَانًا, rather than F L Q itself.

Night’s rest or calculating celestial movements should not become part of falaq’s definition.

فَالِقُ الْإِصْبَاحِ alone also supplies no theory of the sun’s physical motion or how the earth moves.

The Qur’anic language concerns morning’s appearance, observable to someone on earth.

Fāliq as a description of The God

The two consecutive Al-Anʿām verses apply fāliq to The God.

He is presented as both splitting grain and fruit stones and opening morning light.

The form alone does not mean an independent name carrying every property assigned by a later theological system.

Qur’anic fāliq is an active participle accompanied by genitive complements: فَالِقُ الْحَبِّ وَالنَّوَىٰ and فَالِقُ الْإِصْبَاحِ.

Its actual form should be preserved in translation.

Nor does splitting imagery establish that divine action must be bodily and tool-dependent, like splitting wood with an axe.

The verse attributes these events to The God without fully explaining divine action’s manner or every event’s natural process.

5. فَانْفَلَقَ: the sea parting in Moses’ story

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:63

فَأَوْحَيْنَا إِلَىٰ مُوسَىٰ أَنِ اضْرِبْ بِعَصَاكَ الْبَحْرَ ۖ فَانْفَلَقَ فَكَانَ كُلُّ فِرْقٍ كَالطَّوْدِ الْعَظِيمِ

“We revealed to Moses: Strike the sea with your staff. Then the sea split apart, and each separated portion was like a great mountain.”

فَانْفَلَقَ is a past-tense Form VII verb.

Its grammatical subject is an implicit “it,” referring to the sea. الْبَحْرَ was the object of the preceding command اضْرِبْ. In the second clause the sea is what becomes split, rather than necessarily the agent causing the splitting.

The sea splitting apart

Infalaqa here means the sea splitting or opening apart.

This is directly connected with material splitting.

It does not mean that the sea breaks like a hard stone.

The thing split is seawater. The following expression explains the result: each portion became like a great mountain.

Splitting concerns a separation in the water and its portions moving apart.

The verse does not describe the physical manner, duration, or details of water flow.

It should consequently not be restricted to a particular theory of tides, earthquakes, wind, or other natural factors.

Conversely, the image of an actual sea parting should not be made into a merely spiritual or social division without justification.

The verse describes the command to strike the sea with a staff, followed by its splitting.

Distinguishing infalaqa and firq

فَانْفَلَقَ and فِرْق stand together in the same verse.

They belong to two different roots:

فَانْفَلَقَ belongs to F L Q and conveys splitting apart.

فِرْق belongs to F R Q and here names a portion of water separated from another.

The action and the resulting separated portion are therefore expressed through different linguistic families.

Their semantic proximity does not make them one root.

Comparison with Al-Baqarah 2:50

Al-Baqarah 2:50

وَإِذْ فَرَقْنَا بِكُمُ الْبَحْرَ فَأَنْجَيْنَاكُمْ وَأَغْرَقْنَا آلَ فِرْعَوْنَ وَأَنْتُمْ تَنْظُرُونَ

“And when We parted the sea for you, saved you, and drowned Pharaoh’s people while you watched.”

فَرَقْنَا here belongs to F R Q, rather than F L Q.

The two verses nevertheless concern the same event: the sea’s separation in the story of Moses and the Children of Israel.

The Qur’an can thus describe one event through two related linguistic families.

Falq foregrounds splitting, while farq concerns separation or separating.

No absolute boundary should be constructed in which farq never means splitting or falq never involves separation.

The families operate within a shared semantic field in this story.

Ṭā Hā 20:77 also commands Moses to make a dry path through the sea. It uses other words, however, which should not all be counted as F L Q derivatives.

The result of the sea’s splitting

كَالطَّوْدِ الْعَظِيمِ in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:63 describes the water’s portions in size and appearance.

Ṭawd denotes a mountain or great elevation.

Mountain-like greatness is therefore not the meaning of F L Q itself. It is the verse’s description of the separated portions of sea.

Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:65 describes Moses and his companions being saved later in the same story.

Infalaqa should consequently not be lexically defined as saving. The parting provides a route to safety in this story, but salvation is a contextual result.

6. رَبِّ الْفَلَقِ: the Lord of daybreak

Al-Falaq 113:1

قُلْ أَعُوذُ بِرَبِّ الْفَلَقِ

“Say: I seek refuge in the Lord of daybreak.”

This is the sole Qur’anic occurrence of the noun الْفَلَق.

Arabic falaq is established for daybreak and the light appearing at the beginning of day.

Lexical and exegetical explanations nevertheless propose other senses. A suitable translation requires distinguishing recorded Arabic meaning, the verse’s construction, and the surah’s context.

The first reading: daybreak

Arabic فَلَقُ الصُّبْحِ concerns the light of daybreak.

Its connection with splitting is clear: morning light appears on the horizon and draws night’s darkness aside.

Al-Anʿām 6:96 also calls The God فَالِقُ الْإِصْبَاحِ.

This connection within the Qur’an strengthens the daybreak reading.

“The Lord of daybreak” consequently has strong linguistic and Qur’anic support.

It should not be restricted to “the Lord of the sun.”

Daybreak concerns the appearance of early daylight; the sun itself may not yet have risen above the horizon.

Nor should falaq be made into the wholly abstract action of splitting, since al-falaq is also an established Arabic noun for daybreak.

The second reading: creation and created beings

Some early explanations understand falaq as creation or all created beings.

This relates to the breadth of splitting imagery in the natural world.

Seeds split and plants emerge; morning light appears; opening and manifestation can be expressed through the F L Q family in Arabic.

On this basis, some have understood رَبِّ الْفَلَقِ as “the Lord of all created beings.”

The reading can be examined within the lexical family’s semantic range.

Its possibility alone does not, however, establish grounds for preferring it over the established daybreak sense.

In رَبِّ الْفَلَقِ, al-falaq is a noun and the genitive complement of rabb. Daybreak is an established nominal meaning in Arabic, but the grammatical construction alone does not decide between that meaning and the broader creation reading.

Al-Anʿām 6:96 explicitly connects The God with opening morning.

“The Lord of daybreak” is therefore preferable in terms of direct meaning and the connection within the Qur’an.

This preference does not mean that The God is only Lord of morning. The Qur’an itself presents Him as Lord of all created beings.

The question here is the meaning of falaq, rather than limiting The God’s lordship.

Readings concerning a place or punishment

Some exegetical reports identify falaq as a pit, prison, or place in Hell.

Sūrat Al-Falaq itself supplies no description of such a place.

Those reports also do not erase Arabic’s established daybreak meaning.

They alone therefore cannot justify translating رَبِّ الْفَلَقِ as “the Lord of the pit of Hell.”

That identification needs independent evidence and lacks sufficient support for the basic translation.

The connection with the following verses

Al-Falaq 113:2

مِنْ شَرِّ مَا خَلَقَ

“From the evil of what He has created.”

The verse states the scope of seeking refuge.

Sharr belongs to another root and concerns evil or harm that may arise in relation to created beings.

The expression does not say that every created being is intrinsically evil.

مِنْ شَرِّ مَا خَلَقَ should therefore not be understood as making creation itself evil.

Al-Falaq 113:3

وَمِنْ شَرِّ غَاسِقٍ إِذَا وَقَبَ

“And from the evil of gathering darkness when it envelops.”

Gathering darkness is the subject here.

If al-falaq in the first verse means daybreak, a recognizable image connects light beginning with dangers that may arise in darkness.

This supports the daybreak reading: the surah begins by seeking refuge in the Lord of dawn, then describes evil associated with darkness arriving.

The correspondence does not make falaq lexically mean “salvation from darkness.”

Falaq names daybreak; seeking refuge from evil follows from the construction and subsequent verses.

Al-Falaq 113:4 concerns the evil of those who blow into knots, and Al-Falaq 113:5 the evil of an envier when he envies.

These dangers are not necessarily all confined to night’s darkness.

The whole surah should therefore not be reduced to seeking refuge only from nighttime dangers.

Does falaq mean light’s certain victory over darkness?

Naturally occurring daybreak is associated with darkness withdrawing.

Reflection on the verse may draw on this image to explore hope, relief, and seeking refuge in The God.

Such an extension should not replace the root’s direct meaning.

Falaq itself does not lexically mean victory, liberation from oppression, discovering truth, or the end of all suffering.

These ideas may be examined separately through other verses, but do not follow from the word alone.

In Sūrat Al-Falaq, the clearer and more defensible sense is seeking refuge in the Lord of daybreak, whose morning light is among His signs of creation.

7. Comparing related roots

F L Q (فَلْق) and F R Q (فَرْق): splitting and separating

The roots are closely related in meaning.

Falq concerns splitting and opening apart.

Farq concerns separating and distinguishing things.

Both appear in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:63: the sea splits, and each separated portion becomes like a great mountain.

Al-Baqarah 2:50 uses farq for the same event.

An exceptionless boundary should therefore not be constructed between them.

Forqan’s lexical study must nevertheless examine the actual Qur’anic forms separately. Semantic proximity does not make them one root.

F L Q (فَلْق) and SH Q Q (شَقّ): related fields of splitting

Arabic shaqq concerns splitting and producing a cleft.

Falq belongs to the same semantic field.

In uses concerning wood, stone, and other material objects, they may be very close.

It does not follow that falq always means complete splitting while shaqq always means partial splitting, or the reverse.

Each Qur’anic use is understood through its construction and referent.

F L Q (فَلَق) and F J R (فَجْر): dawn and the beginning of daylight

Arabic fajr also concerns splitting and opening and, in its established temporal use, the light at the beginning of day.

Falaq too is used for daybreak.

Their temporal and imagistic fields are consequently close.

They should not be treated as one root or freely substituted for one another in every use.

The Qur’an also uses F J R for opening and causing water to flow, while Qur’anic F L Q concerns seeds, morning, the sea, and daybreak.

Their precise differences require study across both families’ uses.

“Daybreak” is a natural equivalent in Sūrat Al-Falaq; the Arabic word fajr need not be inserted into the translation itself.

F L Q (فَلَق) and Ṣ B Ḥ (صُبْح): breaking dawn and morning itself

Al-Anʿām 6:96 joins fāliq with iṣbāḥ.

Ṣ B Ḥ concerns morning and the beginning of daylight.

Fāliq supplies the image of splitting.

Morning is what is opened and brought forth in this expression.

In Sūrat Al-Falaq, al-falaq itself can name daybreak.

A natural event’s name may therefore be related to an image of how it appears.

That relationship does not make every F L Q derivative mean morning.

F L Q (فَلْق) and F Ṭ R (فَطْر): opening and initiating creation

Arabic F Ṭ R also concerns splitting and beginning.

Qur’anic forms occur in relation to creating the heavens and earth.

Some lexical explanations also relate F L Q to creating or bringing into being.

The families are nevertheless not wholly synonymous.

فَالِقُ الْحَبِّ وَالنَّوَىٰ clearly pictures grain and fruit stones splitting, while فَاطِرِ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ has a broader construction and field.

The shared image of splitting should not make fāṭir and fāliq identical translations in every verse.

F L Q (فَلْق) and B Z GH (بُزُوغ): celestial bodies appearing

Arabic buzūgh concerns the appearance of the sun, moon, or stars.

Al-Anʿām 6:77 and Al-Anʿām 6:78 use this root for the moon and sun.

The daybreak branch of F L Q also concerns light appearing.

B Z GH in those verses, however, concerns the celestial body’s appearance; فَالِقُ الْإِصْبَاحِ concerns morning light opening.

These events are related but not identical.

فَالِقُ الْإِصْبَاحِ should therefore not be definitively translated as “the one who causes the sun to rise.”

F L Q (فَلَق) and F L K (فَلَك): different roots

F L Q and F L K share their initial letters but are not one root.

Qur’anic F L K concerns ships and a celestial course or orbit.

F L Q concerns splitting and opening apart.

Falaq should therefore not be translated as an orbit, planetary circulation, or the structure of the heavens.

Similar sounds and spelling do not establish historical kinship or identical meanings.

8. Translation proposals for Forqan

Translation should preserve the distinctions between material splitting, opening morning, and the established noun for daybreak.

فَالِقُ الْحَبِّ وَالنَّوَىٰ in Al-Anʿām 6:95

Principal proposal:

“Splitter of grain and fruit stones.”

For the clause:

“Indeed, The God is the splitter of grain and fruit stones.”

A contextual explanation may connect their splitting with germination and the plant’s appearance.

“Causing grain and fruit stones to grow” should preferably not replace fāliq’s principal meaning, since it removes the splitting image.

فَالِقُ الْإِصْبَاحِ in Al-Anʿām 6:96

Principal proposal:

“He who splits open and brings forth morning light.”

More fluently:

“He opens the light of morning.”

This preserves opening and morning appearing.

“Creator of morning” alone is not a precise equivalent: it is more general and conveys splitting less distinctly.

فَانْفَلَقَ in Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:63

Principal proposal:

“Then it split apart.”

For the complete clause:

“Then the sea split apart, and each portion became like a great mountain.”

Infalaqa alone should not be rendered as “they were saved” or “a dry path appeared.” These results follow from context and related verses.

الْفَلَقِ in Al-Falaq 113:1

Principal proposal:

“Daybreak.”

For the verse:

“Say: I seek refuge in the Lord of daybreak.”

This preserves established Arabic meaning and has a clear connection with فَالِقُ الْإِصْبَاحِ in Al-Anʿām 6:96.

The broader reading “Lord of created beings” can be discussed lexically, but there is insufficient reason to prefer it over daybreak in the basic translation.

Brief meanings for word-by-word boxes

  • فَالِقُ, Al-Anʿām 6:95: splitter; here, of grain and fruit stones.
  • فَالِقُ, Al-Anʿām 6:96: splitter and opener; here, bringing forth morning light.
  • فَانْفَلَقَ, Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:63: then it split apart; concerning the sea’s waters separating.
  • الْفَلَقِ, Al-Falaq 113:1: daybreak; the light appearing at the beginning of day.

None of these uses should translate falaq as victory, liberation, rebirth, or intellectual awakening.

Such ideas may arise in contextual reflection but do not constitute the root’s basic meaning.

9. Conclusion

Arabic F L Q concerns splitting, opening apart, and a cleft appearing.

Material uses concern wood, stone, seeds, fruit stones, and other objects. Falq may name the act, while filqah names a portion of something split.

In the daybreak use, the family names light appearing on the horizon and drawing night’s darkness aside.

The root occurs four times in the Qur’an.

In Al-Anʿām 6:95, The God is فَالِقُ الْحَبِّ وَالنَّوَىٰ, splitting grain and fruit stones in the process of growth.

In Al-Anʿām 6:96, He is فَالِقُ الْإِصْبَاحِ, bringing forth morning light.

In Al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:63, the sea splits in Moses’ story, and each portion becomes like a great mountain.

Al-Falaq 113:1 instructs seeking refuge in رَبِّ الْفَلَقِ, whose established and appropriate sense is “the Lord of daybreak.”

Splitting thus connects the family’s Qur’anic uses, while its manner and referent vary by verse.

A seed splitting and morning opening are not one physical event. Nor should the sea’s parting become merely figurative, since the verse explicitly describes separated portions of water.

Daybreak in Sūrat Al-Falaq is a natural meaning with lexical support. Sources propose the broader possibility of creation, but the root’s breadth alone is insufficient to eliminate the established dawn sense.

Distinctions from farq, shaqq, fajr, ṣubḥ, and faṭr should also be preserved. The families may overlap without becoming one root or freely interchangeable in translation.

For Forqan, the basic definition is: falq is splitting or opening something apart. Materially it concerns producing a cleft and separating portions; in relation to morning, it concerns dawn appearing and early daylight becoming manifest. Fāliq is a splitter or opener, infalaqa expresses becoming split, and al-falaq in Sūrat Al-Falaq names daybreak.

This definition accommodates the four Qur’anic occurrences without importing creation, salvation, guidance, or light’s victory into the root itself without justification.
